Modelling quantum measurements without superposition
Gabriele Cobucci, Alexander Bernal, Roope Uola, Armin Tavakoli

Abstract
Superposition is the core feature that sets quantum theory apart from classical physics. Here, we investigate whether sets of quantum measurements can be modelled by using only devices that are operationally classical, in the sense that they have no superposition properties. This leads us to propose classical measurement models, which we show to be stronger than commutative measurements but weaker than joint measurability. We determine both the exact depolarisation noise rate and the measurement loss rate at which the all projective measurements in d d -dimensional quantum theory admit a classical model. For finite sets of quantum measurements we develop methods both for constructing classical models and for falsifying the existence of such model via prepare-and-measure setups. Furthermore, we show that this concept also has operational implications. For that, we consider whether quantum measurements with classical side-information can be implemented in sequence without causing a disturbance and we show that classical models imply an affirmative answer. Our work sheds light on superposition as a resource for quantum measurement devices.



Introduction.—
The superposition principle is at the heart of quantum theory’s break with classical ideas. One possible way to view superposition is to have a pre-selected basis with respect to which it can arise
22
. A different and less restrictive view is to adopt no priviledged basis and ask whether there exists some basis in which superpositions vanish. This is captured by the textbook notion of commuting observables. For projective measurements, commutation is equivalent to joint measurability (JM), by which there is a single measurement from which the others are obtained by classical post-processing
12
. However, for general measurements, which are positive operator-valued measures (POVMs), commutation is a strictly stronger condition than JM. It motivates a natural question: to what extent can classical measurement devices reproduce the measurements in quantum theory?

On the one hand, one may argue that commutation no longer captures the full power of classical measurement models. For example, the noisy Pauli observables
\eta\sigma_{Z}
and
\eta\sigma_{X}
fail to commute for any
\eta>0
, but one would not expect observables that are almost exclusively noise (
\eta\approx 0
) to be useful for quantum information. On the other hand, JM addresses whether a set of measurements can be reduced to a single measurement, but not whether the latter requires superposition features. This makes JM natural for certain black-box input/output experiments
28
;
18
;
24
but it also makes it too powerful for classical notions of measurement.

Here, we introduce classical measurement models with the goal of reproducing the Hilbert space representation of any set of POVMs implemented by a given quantum measurement device. The main idea behind these models is that a random variable is used to select between a number of measurement devices whose output is subjected to a conventional post-processing. Importantly, each of these measurement devices is classical in the standard sense of commuting observables: there always exists a basis in the Hilbert space of the system in which all operations of the device are diagonal. The possibility of stochastically calling the different classical devices allows the model to account for a strictly larger class of measurements than those captured by commutation. In contrast, the classical measurement models are a subset of JM due to being prohibited from directly using superposition features. These relationships are illustrated in Fig
1
.

Figure 1
:
Classifying measurement models.
Commuting observables, joint measurability and POVMs represent increasingly broad notions of measurement. We introduce classical models based on measurement devices without superposition features. These are intermediate between commutation and joint measurability.

In what follows, we first formalise the classical measurement models. Then, we discuss to what extent they can account for all the projective measurements in
d
-dimensional quantum theory. We provide an exact solution to this problem both in terms of the depolarisation noise and the measurement loss needed to make a classical model possible. Subsequently, we consider more realistic quantum measurement devices, which only implement some finite set of well-selected measurements. For any finite set of POVMs, we show how to numerically search for a classical model. Furthermore, provided no such model exists, we show how to derive witness-type proofs of this fact. Finally, we go beyond the foundational interest in understanding the role of superposition in quantum measurements and consider the operational implications of classical measurement models. Specifically, we consider the well-known problem of whether a pair of measurements can be implemented in sequence without the former disturbing the latter
12
. We consider this problem in the presence of a classical hidden variable, shared between the devices tasked with implementing the respective measurements, and we show that a classical measurement model implies the possibility of a non-disturbing implementation. Classical models therefore operationally contrast JM, as the latter turns out only to be a necessary condition for non-disturbance.

Classical measurement models.—
Consider a set of POVMs,
\mathbf{M}\equiv\{M_{a|x}\}_{a,x}
, on a
d
-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, where
a=1,\ldots,o
labels the outcome and
x=1,\ldots,n
labels the measurement choice. We ask whether
\mathbf{M}
can be simulated in a model that uses only the following resources: (i) arbitrary pre-processing, (ii) arbitrary post-processing, and (iii) measurement devices that individually have no superposition features on the Hilbert space of the system. This model is illustrated in Fig
2
and we now proceed to formalise it.

We consider an arbitrary quantum state,
\rho
, and whether it is possible to simulate its measurement statistics for any one of the measurements appearing in the set
\mathbf{M}
. Our classical measurement model for the implementation of
\mathbf{M}
is as follows. We use a random variable,
\lambda
, sampled from a probability density function
q(\lambda)
. Depending on its value,
\rho
is directed to a measurement device named
\mathcal{M}_{\lambda}
. Since
\mathcal{M}_{\lambda}
cannot have superposition properties, it is restricted to operate in a specific orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{H}
. We call that basis
\{\ket*{e^{(\lambda)}_k}\}_{k=1}^{d}
and label by
E_{k|\lambda}=\outerproduct*{e_{k}^{(\lambda)}}{e_{k}^{(\lambda)}}
the associated projectors. The outcome of this measurement,
k
, and the device’s label
\lambda
, are then fed into a post-processing unit. This unit takes as input also the label,
x
, of the specific quantum measurement in
\mathbf{M}
that we aim to implement. Thus, the post-processing is represented by some conditional distribution
p(a|x,k,\lambda)
, where
a
is the final outcome of the simulation. Putting it all together,
\mathbf{M}
admits a classical model when it can be written on the form

M_{a|x}=\int d\lambda\,q(\lambda)\,\sum_{k=1}^{d}p(a|x,k,\lambda)E_{k|\lambda},

(1)

where
\{E_{k|\lambda}\}_{k}
is a basis measurement for every
\lambda
. Note that the model may use an (uncountably) infinite number of devices
\mathcal{M}_{\lambda}
, thereby motivating the integral. Actually, the model can be simplified without loss of generality. In Supplementary Material we show that the post-processing can be ignored at the price of allowing
\{E_{k|\lambda}\}_{k}
to be a projective measurement of arbitrary rank. Thus, the classical measurement model is equivalently written as

M_{a|x}=\int d\lambda\,q(\lambda)F_{a|x,\lambda},

(2)

where
\{F_{a|x,\lambda}\}_{a}
is a measurement satisfying
F_{a|x,\lambda}F_{a^{\prime}|x,\lambda}=F_{a|x,\lambda}\delta_{a,a^{\prime}}
due to projectivity, and
[F_{a|x,\lambda},F_{a^{\prime}|x^{\prime},\lambda}]=0
due to the classicality of the devices.

Let us now make a few observations. Firstly, the space of all sets of quantum measurements that admit a classical model is closed and convex. Secondly, if
\mathbf{M}
consists of extremal POVMs then
q(\lambda)
must be deterministic. This reduces the classical measurement model to standard commutativity. Thirdly, if
\mathbf{M}
is classical then it is also JM. By definition,
\mathbf{M}
is JM if there exists a POVM
\{G_{\mu}\}_{\mu}
, the so-called parent POVM, such that
M_{a|x}=\sum_{\mu}p(a|x,\mu)G_{\mu}

12
. To recover this from our definition (
1
), we need only to select
\mu=(\lambda,k)
and define the POVM
G_{\mu}\coloneqq G_{(\lambda,k)}=q(\lambda)E_{k|\lambda}
. In particular, classical simulability is equivalent to JM with parent POVMs which admit a projective simulation of the type defined in
16
. It is straightforward to see that the converse does not hold, i.e. JM does not imply classicality. For instance the three-outcome qubit measurement corresponding to unit Bloch vectors forming an equilateral triangle is extremal and non-commuting. Thus it has no classical model, but any single measurement is trivially JM. Finally, in the simplest setting
\mathbf{M}
has only a single POVM. In that case, the question of whether a classical measurement model exists reduces to whether the POVM admits a so-called projective simulation. The latter is an established problem
16
for which dedicated methods have been developed
15
;
6
;
4
, but unfortunately these do not directly apply when
\mathbf{M}
contains more than a single measurement. We note that a different approach to simulate sets of POVMs has been considered in
11
.

Classical model for pair of measurements.—
Let us start with a simple example of how classical models are more powerful than standard commutation. For this, we consider an arbitrary pair of non-commuting bases,
\{\ket{f_a}\}_{a=1}^{d}
and
\{\ket{h_a}\}_{a=1}^{d}
. We apply to both bases the depolarisation map
\Phi_{v}(X)=vX+(1-v)\Tr\left(X\right)\frac{\mathds{1}}{d}
, where
v\in[0,1]
is the visibility. Thus,
M_{a|1}=\Phi_{v}(\outerproduct{f_a}{f_a})
and
M_{a|2}=\Phi_{v}(\outerproduct{h_a}{h_a})
. While the two measurements commute only for
v=0
, there exists a classical model whenever
v\leq\frac{1}{2}
, independently of the dimension
d
. To show that, we use only two measurement devices:
\mathcal{M}_{1}
measures in
\{\ket{f_a}\}_{a}
and
\mathcal{M}_{2}
measures in
\{\ket{h_a}\}_{a}
. We call them with equal probability,
q(1)=q(2)=\frac{1}{2}
, and select the post-processing as
a=k
if
x=\lambda
while
a
is random if
x\neq\lambda
. Inserted into Eq (
1
), this gives the set
\mathbf{M}
when
v=\frac{1}{2}
.

This model shows that many non-commuting observables admit a classical model in arbitrary large dimension. However, the model is usually far from optimal, i.e. even larger values of
v
are typically possible with a classical description of
\mathbf{M}
. In End Matter, we present a numerical method for constructing classical measurement models for any finite choice of
\mathbf{M}
and we apply it to improve the above classical model to visibilities significantly higher than
v=\frac{1}{2}
.

The classical limit of all projective measurements.—
A conceptually appealing question is to consider the possibility of a classical model for all noisy projective quantum measurements in a given Hilbert space. Specifically, this means choosing
\mathbf{M}=\{\Phi_{v}\left(M_{a}\right)\}
, where
M
runs over all the (uncountably many) bases of
d
-dimensional Hilbert space, and to determine the necessary and sufficient condition on
v
for a classical model.

The set of all bases can equivalently be associated with the set of unitaries
U\in\text{SU}(d)
, so we can label the measurements by
M_{a|U}
, where
U
serves as the measurement index. Applying the depolarisation map therefore gives us the target set
\mathbf{M}_{v}=\{vM_{a|U}+\frac{1-v}{d}\openone\}_{U\in\text{SU}(d)}
, for some visibility
v\in[0,1]
. Our first result determines the exact threshold value,
v^{*}
, below which
\mathbf{M}_{v}
admits a classical model and above which it does not. This threshold is given by

v^{*}=\frac{H_{d}-1}{d-1},

(3)

where
H_{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{k}
is the Harmonic number. The proof is given in Supplementary Material. The argument relies on the explicit construction of a classical simulation model using techniques introduced in
27
;
14
and on the observation that the elements of
\mathbf{M}_{v}
can be viewed as quantum states. This lets us prove the result by leveraging a one-to-one connection to classical models recently proposed for quantum states
5
.

The result (
3
) provides the fundamental noise limit below which classical models are able to simulate all basis measurements in quantum theory in dimension
d
. For large
d
, it scales as
v\sim\frac{\gamma-1+\log d}{d}
, where
\gamma\approx 0.577
is the Euler-Mascheroni constant. Thus, it becomes rapidly expensive for a classical model to account for all projective measurements in high-dimensional quantum theory. Moreover, in the qubit case, the same bound,
v=\frac{1}{2}
, is also necessary and sufficient for the existence of classical models of the set of all noisy non-projective measurements
29
;
20
.

Furthermore, the classical simulation providing the threshold (
3
) admits a minimal modification to also account for losses in the measurements. This means composing the depolarisation channel with a loss channel,
L_{\eta}
, yielding the measurements
L_{\eta}\circ\Phi_{v}(M_{a})
. Here,
L_{\eta}
implements the target measurement with efficiency
\eta
, while assigning the failed outcome
M_{\varnothing}=(1-\eta)\openone
otherwise. An optimal classical model can be constructed by mimicking the joint measurability model introduced in
21
, leading to the critical efficiency
\eta^{*}=d(1-v)^{d-1}
(for
v>1/2
). We refer to
21
for a detailed derivation and to the Supplementary Material for complete expressions when
v\leq 1/2
.

Detecting superposition features.—
Real-life quantum devices do not implement all projective measurements allowed in quantum theory, but instead only the few measurements relevant for a specific quantum technology purpose. Therefore, we now consider the more realistic situation in which
\mathbf{M}
is a finite set of POVMs. How can we prove that
\mathbf{M}=\{M_{a|x}\}_{a,x}
admits no classical model? We propose a witness-based method that can robustly prove the failure of classical models in a practical experimental configuration.

Consider that we have a preparation device that can generate any one of the states
\{\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{m}\}
. Letting
z
label our choice of state, we use
\rho_{z}
to probe our measurement device for its
x
’th setting. By Born’s rule, the resulting probability distribution is
p(a|z,x)=\tr(\rho_z M_{a|x})
. We seek to determine inequalities of the form

W(\mathbf{M})=\sum_{a,z,x}c_{azx}\tr(\rho_z M_{a|x})\leq\beta,

(4)

where
c_{azx}
are some real coefficients and
\beta
is a bound satisfied by all classical measurement models. Then, a violation of the inequality falsifies classical models.

We now show how to determine
\beta
for any choice of
\mathbf{M}
,
\{\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{m}\}
and
\{c_{azx}\}
. Assume that
\mathbf{M}
admits a classical model as in (
1
). Due to the linearity of
W
, its optimal value is reachable for a deterministic choice of
\lambda
, i.e. we need only to consider a single classical device in the simulation which performs a basis measurement
\{E_{k}\}_{k}
. Hence, we have

W=\sum_{k,a,x}p(a|x,k)\tr\left(E_{k}\mathcal{O}_{ax}\right)
, where we defined
\mathcal{O}_{ax}=\sum_{z}c_{azx}\rho_{z}
. Next, we write the post-processing as a convex combination of deterministic distributions
p(a|x,k)=\sum_{\gamma}q(\gamma)D_{\gamma}(a|x,k)
, where
D_{\gamma}(a|x,k)\equiv\delta_{a,\gamma(x,k)}

9
. Selecting the deterministic strategy for which the maximum is achieved, we get

\beta=\max_{\gamma}\max_{\{E_{k}\}}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\tr(\sum_{a,x}D_{\gamma}(a|x,k) \mathcal{O}_{ax} E_{k}).

(5)

The remaining task is to address the maximisation over
\{E_{k}\}
. For qubit measurements, we can solve it exactly. In Supplementary Material we show that for
d=2
it becomes

\beta=\max_{\gamma}\ \tr(\mathcal{O}_{+}^{(\gamma)})+\sqrt{\tr(\mathcal{O}_{-}^{(\gamma)})^{2}-4\det(\mathcal{O}_{-}^{(\gamma)})},

(6)

where
\mathcal{O}_{\pm}^{(\gamma)}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{a,x}\left(D_{\gamma}(a|x,1)\pm D_{\gamma}(a|x,2)\right)\mathcal{O}_{ax}
. For higher dimensions, we determine upper bounds on
\beta
via semidefinite programming (SDP) relaxations
23
. We relax the rank-one projective measurements
\{E_{k}\}_{k}
to unit trace measurements
\{N_{k}\}_{k}
and compute the SDP

\displaystyle g_{\gamma}\equiv\max_{\{N_{k}\}}

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{d}\tr(\sum_{a,x}D_{\gamma}(a|x,k) \mathcal{O}_{ax} N_{k})

(7)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle N_{k}\succeq 0,\quad\tr(N_{k})=1,\quad\forall k,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{d}N_{k}=\openone,

for each deterministic strategy
\gamma
. The bound on
\beta
is obtained by selecting the largest value of
g_{\gamma}
over all possible
\gamma
. Our implementation of the procedure is available at
7

To illustrate the usefulness of the witness method, we analyse three different sets of qubit POVMs, for which the analytical bound (
6
) can be used. The first one,
\mathbf{M}_{1}
, is a set of five symmetric informationally complete (SIC) POVMs (where the Bloch vectors of the SICs correspond to the vertices of the compound of five tetrahedra
8
). For the second and third sets,
\mathbf{M}_{2}
and
\mathbf{M}_{3}
, we select the rank-1 measurements obtained respectively from two and three and MUBs
10
. Our choice of witness is a state-discrimination protocol corresponding to
z=(b,y)
and
c_{azx}=c_{axby}=\delta_{a,b}\delta_{x,y}
. The witness becomes

W_{\text{st.-discr.}}(\mathbf{M})=\sum_{a,x}\tr(\rho_{a|x}M_{a|x}),

(8)

and the value of
\beta
is computed from (

) to be

\displaystyle\beta_{1}=5+\frac{\sqrt{25+14\sqrt{2}}}{\sqrt{3}}\simeq 8.864,

(9)

\displaystyle\beta_{2}=2+\sqrt{2},\quad\beta_{3}=3+\sqrt{3}.

These classical bounds are indeed violated by every set
\mathbf{M}_{i}
. In particular,
W_{\text{st.-discr.}}(\mathbf{M}_{1})=10,\,W_{\text{st.-discr.}}(\mathbf{M}_{2})=4
and
W_{\text{st.-discr.}}(\mathbf{M}_{3})=6
. The visibility below which the noisy measurements
\{\Phi_{v}\left(\mathbf{M}_{i}\right)\}
no longer violate the inequality is

\displaystyle v_{1}=\frac{\sqrt{25+14\sqrt{2}}}{5\sqrt{3}}\simeq 0.7729,

(10)

\displaystyle v_{2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\simeq 0.7071,\quad v_{3}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\simeq 0.5774.

For the latter two cases, this is the necessary and sufficient condition. For the former case, the result is not far from optimal, as indicated by the numerical lower bound discussed in End Matter:
v\leq 0.7605
.

Measurement classicality and non-disturbance.—
We now go further and show that classical measurement models have operational implications that distinguish them from JM. For that, we consider the problem of whether quantum measurements can be implemented in sequence without causing a disturbance. Specifically, consider that we are given two measurements, that we for simplicity label as
A_{a}=M_{a|1}
and
B_{b}=M_{b|2}
. Our goal is to realise first
\{A_{a}\}_{a}
and then
\{B_{b}\}_{b}
on an arbitrary state
\rho
in such a way that the former does not disturb the outcome statistics of the latter. This scenario is illustrated in Fig
3
. A source of classical randomness samples
\lambda
from some distribution
q(\lambda)
and sends it to the two measurement devices. The two devices are then programmed to implement the POVM
\{A_{a|\lambda}\}_{a}
and
\{B_{b|\lambda}\}_{b}
respectively. These may be arbitrary, but their effective action must correspond to the target measurements, namely

\displaystyle A_{a}=\sum_{\lambda}q(\lambda)A_{a|\lambda},\quad\text{and}\quad B_{b}=\sum_{\lambda}q(\lambda)B_{b|\lambda}.

(11)

For a given
\lambda
, the state arriving at the second device depends not only on
\rho
and
\{A_{a|\lambda}\}_{a}
but also on the instrument that implements the latter. Denote that instrument by
\{I_{a|\lambda}\}_{a}
. Formally,
I_{a|\lambda}
is a completely positive map,
\sum_{a}I_{a|\lambda}
is trace-preserving and the dual map satisfies
I^{\dagger}_{a|\lambda}(\mathds{1})=A_{a|\lambda}
. In order to be non-disturbing, we must have that
\sum_{a,\lambda}q(\lambda)\tr\left(I_{a|\lambda}(\rho)B_{b|\lambda}\right)=\tr\left(\rho B_{b}\right)

\forall\rho
. This can equivalently be written as

\sum_{a,\lambda}q(\lambda)I_{a|\lambda}^{\dagger}(B_{b|\lambda})=B_{b}.

(12)

If there exists instruments
\{I_{a|\lambda}\}
and POVMs
\{B_{b|\lambda}\}
such that the conditions (
11
) and (
12
) are satisfied, we say that the POVMs
\{A_{a}\}_{a}
and
\{B_{b}\}_{b}
admit a non-disturbing realisation.

Let us now consider the relationship between non-disturbance, classical models and JM. Firstly, non-disturbance implies JM. To show that, consider the parent POVM
G_{ab}=\sum_{\lambda}q({\lambda})\mathcal{I}_{a|\lambda}^{\dagger}(B_{b|\lambda})
. The completeness of
\{G_{ab}\}_{ab}
follows from
\sum_{b}B_{b|\lambda}=\mathds{1}
and that the dual of a CPTP map is unital. We can now check the marginals:
\sum_{b}G_{ab}=A_{a}
due to Eq (
11
) and
\sum_{a}G_{ab}=B_{b}
due to Eq (
12
).

Secondly, JM does not imply non-disturbance. To show that, we use that if
\{A_{a}\}_{a}
or
\{B_{b}\}_{b}
is extremal our problem reduces to that studied in
13
, where no classical randomness is allowed. It was shown in
13
that for qubits commutation is necessary and sufficient for such non-disturbance. For example, if
\{A_{a}\}_{a}
and
\{B_{b}\}_{b}
are both the same extremal non-projective qubit measurement then they are trivially jointly measurable, but the former disturbs the latter if the measurement operators do not commute. Thus, JM is too powerful to guarantee non-disturbance.

Thirdly, we prove in Supplementary Material that a classical measurement model for the pair
\{A_{a}\}_{a}
and
\{B_{b}\}_{b}
implies non-disturbance. The proof is based on adopting the classical model (
1
) and selecting a Lüders instrument for device number
\lambda
employed in the classical measurement model (see Fig
2
). One can then verify that the non-disturbance conditions are fulfilled. In conclusion, we have that

\text{Classical model}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\text{Non-disturbance}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\text{JM}.

(13)

For unbiased dichotomic qubit measurements, this hierarchy collapses and all three concepts become equivalent. In general, however, the implications are strict. The fact that non-disturbance does not imply classicality is expected, because the former is not a symmetric concept. In other words, swapping the measurement order could change whether
\{A_{a}\}_{a}
and
\{B_{b}\}_{b}
admit a non-disturbing implementation. In Supplementary Material we explicitly show that non-disturbance does not imply classicality for POVMs in dimension
d\geq 5
.

Discussion.—

Quantum measurement devices routinely rely on controlling superposition properties. Here, we have proposed conceptually classical models for simulating quantum measurements, centered on the core idea that no device employed in the simulation model can have any superposition features. This extends into the domain of quantum measurements the line of research recently developed to address dimensionality
3
and superposition
5
in quantum state preparation devices without adopting a priviledged basis. Our results show both how to construct classical models, how to falsify their existence and how to reveal their operational significance.

We determined the exact noise and loss thresholds at which all projective
d
-dimensional measurements admit a classical model. Interestingly, both these thresholds coincide with those at which the set of all von Neumann measurements is jointly measurable
26
;
21
. This means that even though joint measurability is a strictly larger set, the two notions become equivalent in the limit in which all projective measurements allowed in the theory are considered. It remains an interesting open problem to identify this threshold when also including non-projective measurements. Since classical models correspond to JM with parent POVMs which are PVM-simulable, lower bounds on this threshold can be found in
2
;
1
, although they are not expected to be optimal. In addition, we have seen that classical measurements are operational distinct from joint measurability, as the former was sufficient for succeeding with sequential non-disturbance tasks, whereas the latter was only necessary. A deeper understanding of the relation between these two concepts may be unveiled by studying whether the gap between them can grow, perhaps in an unbounded way, by considering non-disturbance tasks with more than two sequential measurements.

SM 1
Simulation protocol without post-processing

In this appendix, we show that the post-processing rule in the definition of the classical model for measurement can be omitted without loss of generality if we let each device
\mathcal{M}_{\lambda}
perform higher-rank measurements, i.e.

M_{a|x}=\int d\lambda\,q(\lambda)\,\sum_{k=1}^{d}p(a|x,k,\lambda)E_{k|\lambda}\iff M_{a|x}=\int d\tilde{\lambda}\,q(\tilde{\lambda})F_{a|x,\tilde{\lambda}},\quad\forall a=1,\dots,o,\text{ and }x=1,\dots,n,

(1.1)

where
\{F_{a|x,\tilde{\lambda}}\}_{a,x}
for each
\tilde{\lambda}
are higher-rank projectors such that
F_{a|x,\tilde{\lambda}}F_{a^{\prime}|x,\tilde{\lambda}}=F_{a|x,\tilde{\lambda}}\delta_{a,a^{\prime}}
,
[F_{a|x,\tilde{\lambda}},F_{a^{\prime}|x^{\prime},\tilde{\lambda}}]=0
for
a\neq a^{\prime}
and
x\neq x^{\prime}
and
\sum_{a}F_{a|x,\tilde{\lambda}}=\openone
. Notice that the sets
\{\lambda\}_{\lambda}
and
\{\tilde{\lambda}\}_{\tilde{\lambda}}
are not necessarily the same.

We start proving that the simulation protocol on the right hand side of (
1.1
) is necessary for a classical simulation. First, consider the post-processing rule
p(a|x,k,\lambda)
and relabel the tuple
(x,k,\lambda)\rightarrow z
, where
z=1,\dots,nd|\lambda|
. Now we show that it is possible to decompose this probability distribution as a convex combination of deterministic strategies
D_{\gamma}(a|z)=\delta_{a,\gamma(z)}
,

p(a|z)=\sum_{\gamma}q(\gamma)D_{\gamma}(a|z).

(1.2)

Define a matrix
P
whose elements are
\{P_{az}=p(a|z)\}_{a,z}
. This matrix is stochastic, i.e. it is positive and the sum of all the elements for each column is
1
. Therefore, (
1.2
) follows directly from Theorem 1 in
9
.

Then, relabeling again
z\rightarrow(x,k,\lambda)
, we get

M_{a|x}=\int d\lambda\,q(\lambda)\,\sum_{k=1}^{d}\sum_{\gamma}q(\gamma)\delta_{a,\gamma(x,k,\lambda)}E_{k|\lambda}=\int d\lambda\sum_{\gamma}\,q(\lambda)q(\gamma)\sum_{k=1}^{d}\delta_{a,\gamma(x,k,\lambda)}E_{k|\lambda},

(1.3)

where we can write
q(\lambda)q(\gamma)=q(\gamma,\lambda)
and define a new index
\tilde{\lambda}\coloneqq(\gamma,\lambda)
. Call
F_{a|x,\tilde{\lambda}}\equiv\sum_{k=1}^{d}\delta_{a,\gamma(x,k,\lambda)}E_{k|\lambda}
. These are higher-rank projective measurements, since

\sum_{a}F_{a|x,\tilde{\lambda}}=\sum_{a}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\delta_{a,\gamma(x,k,\lambda)}E_{k|\lambda}=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\underbrace{\sum_{a}\delta_{a,\gamma(x,k,\lambda)}}_{=1}E_{k|\lambda}=\openone,

(1.4)

where we used that
\{E_{k|\lambda}\}_{k}
is a proper measurement for each
\lambda
. Moreover,

F_{a|x,\tilde{\lambda}}F_{a^{\prime}|x,\tilde{\lambda}}=\sum_{k,k^{\prime}=1}^{d}\delta_{a,\gamma(x,k,\lambda)}\delta_{a^{\prime},\gamma(x,k^{\prime},\lambda)}\underbrace{E_{k|\lambda}E_{k^{\prime}|\lambda}}_{=E_{k|\lambda}\delta_{k,k^{\prime}}}=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\delta_{a,\gamma(x,k,\lambda)}E_{k|\lambda}\delta_{a,a^{\prime}}=F_{a|x,\tilde{\lambda}}\delta_{a,a^{\prime}},

(1.5)

and

[F_{a|x,\tilde{\lambda}},F_{a^{\prime}|x^{\prime},\tilde{\lambda}}]=\sum_{k,k^{\prime}=1}^{d}\delta_{a,\gamma(x,k,\lambda)}\delta_{a^{\prime},\gamma(x^{\prime},k,\lambda)}\underbrace{[E_{k|\lambda},E_{k^{\prime}|\lambda}]}_{=0}=0,

(1.6)

where in both cases we used that
E_{k|\lambda}E_{k^{\prime}|\lambda}=E_{k|\lambda}\delta_{k,k^{\prime}}
. Therefore, starting from the simulation protocol with post-processing, we got the protocol on the right hand side of (
1.3
), which is equivalent to the one on the right hand side of (
1.1
).

We can now prove that the right hand side of (
1.1
) is also sufficient for the simulation protocol of measurements. Indeed, if there exist some higher-rank projectors
F_{a|x,\lambda}
for which a simulation is possible, then by spectral decomposition we find

F_{a|x,\lambda}=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\mu(a,x,k,\lambda)E_{k|a,x,\lambda}=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\mu(a,x,k,\lambda)E_{k|\lambda}=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\delta_{a,\gamma(x,k,\lambda)}E_{k|\lambda}

(1.7)

where in the second equality we have used that
[F_{a|x,\lambda},F_{a^{\prime}|x^{\prime},\lambda}]=0
, hence the eigenbasis of each
F_{a|x,\lambda}
does not depend on
(a,x)
, i.e.
E_{k|a,x,\lambda}=E_{k|\lambda}
. For the last equality, notice that since
F_{a|x,\lambda}
is a projector, its eigenvalues can only be
\{0,1\}
, i.e.
\mu(a,x,k,\lambda)\in\{0,1\}
. In addition, using that
\sum_{a}F_{a|x,\lambda}=\openone
, we get that
\sum_{a}\mu(a,x,k,\lambda)=1
. Therefore,
\mu(a,x,k,\lambda)
is a proper probability distribution over
a
, i.e.
\mu(a,x,k,\lambda)=p(a|x,k,\lambda)
. In particular, it is always possible to rewrite
\mu(a,x,k,\lambda)=\delta_{a,\gamma(x,k,\lambda)}
for some function
\gamma
which maps
(x,k,\lambda)
to values of
a
. Inserting (
1.7
) into the right hand side of (
1.1
), we find a simulation protocol with post-processing as defined in the main text.

Consider that we have an ensemble of states

Proof of equivalence 4

We can parametrize the set

The necessary condition comes directly from implication 2, since
\Tr(N_{a|U})=1

Therefore,

(v(t),\eta(t))=\left(t,d(1-t)^{(d-1)}\right).

(2.38)

SM 3
Analytical witness for qubit measurements

In this section we prove an analytical solution to the maximisation problem

\max_{\{E_{k}\}}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\tr(\sum_{a,x}D(a|x,k) \mathcal{O}_{ax} E_{k}),

(3.39)

3.39
) leads to the following optimization problem:

\max_{\{E_{1},E_{2}\}}\left\{\tr\left(\mathcal{O}_{+}\left(E_{1}+E_{2}\right)\right)+\tr\left(\mathcal{O}_{-}\left(E_{1}-E_{2}\right)\right)\right\}=\tr\left(\mathcal{O}_{+}\right)+\max_{\{E_{1},E_{2}\}}\left\{\tr\left(\mathcal{O}_{-}\left(E_{1}-E_{2}\right)\right)\right\},

(3.42)

where in the first step we have used that
E_{1}+E_{2}=\openone
. The final optimization can be performed by taking
E_{1},E_{2}
as the eigenvectors associated with the largest and smallest eigenvalues of
\mathcal{O}_{-}
,
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
, respectively. Indeed, for
E_{k}=\outerproduct*{e_k}{e_k}
:

\tr\left(\mathcal{O}_{-}\left(E_{1}-E_{2}\right)\right)=\expectationvalue{\mathcal{O}_{-}}{e_1}-\expectationvalue{\mathcal{O}_{-}}{e_2}\leq\lambda_{1}\left(\mathcal{O}_{-}\right)-\lambda_{2}\left(\mathcal{O}_{-}\right),

(3.43)

where we have used that
\lambda_{2}\left(\mathcal{O}_{-}\right)\leq\expectationvalue{\mathcal{O}_{-}}{\psi}\leq\lambda_{1}\left(\mathcal{O}_{-}\right)
for any state
\ket{\psi}
.
Therefore,

\max_{\{E_{1},E_{2}\}}\left\{\tr\left(\mathcal{O}_{-}\left(E_{1}-E_{2}\right)\right)\right\}=\lambda_{1}\left(\mathcal{O}_{-}\right)-\lambda_{2}\left(\mathcal{O}_{-}\right).

(3.44)

Notice that this expression gives the maximum independently of the signs of
\lambda_{i}\left(\mathcal{O}_{-}\right)
. In addition, since we are dealing with 2-dimensional matrices, this expression can be related to matrix invariant, namely the trace and the determinant. Indeed, it holds that

\lambda_{1}\left(\mathcal{O}_{-}\right)-\lambda_{2}\left(\mathcal{O}_{-}\right)=\sqrt{\left(\lambda_{1}\left(\mathcal{O}_{-}\right)+\lambda_{2}\left(\mathcal{O}_{-}\right)\right)^{2}-4\lambda_{1}\left(\mathcal{O}_{-}\right)\lambda_{2}\left(\mathcal{O}_{-}\right)}=\sqrt{\tr(\mathcal{O}_{-})^{2}-4\det(\mathcal{O}_{-})}.

(3.45)

In consequence, now the value of (
3.39
) reads

\max_{\{E_{k}\}}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\tr(\sum_{a,x}D(a|x,k) \mathcal{O}_{ax} E_{k})=\tr(\mathcal{O}_{+})+\sqrt{\tr(\mathcal{O}_{-})^{2}-4\det(\mathcal{O}_{-})},

(3.46)

which completes the proof.

SM 4
Classicality implies non-disturbance

In this appendix we prove that any classically simulable set of two measurements is also non-disturbing, i.e. if
A_{a}=M_{a|1}
and
B_{b}=M_{b|2}
, then

\mathbf{M}\equiv\{A_{a},B_{b}\}_{a,b}\quad\text{classically simulable}\quad\implies\quad\{A_{a},B_{b}\}_{a,b}\quad\text{average non-disturbing}.

(4.47)

To this end, recall that a set of POVMs is classically simulable if

\displaystyle A_{a}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda}q(\lambda)\sum_{k=1}^{d}p_{A}(a|k,\lambda)E_{k|\lambda},

(4.48)

\displaystyle B_{b}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda}q(\lambda)\sum_{k=1}^{d}p_{B}(b|k,\lambda)E_{k|\lambda},

for some rank-one projective measurements
\{E_{k|\lambda}\}_{k}
. For simplicity we have limited the number of measurement devices in the simulation to a finite discrete set. Let us define

\displaystyle A_{a|\lambda}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{d}p_{A}(a|k,\lambda)E_{k|\lambda},

(4.49)

\displaystyle B_{b|\lambda}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{d}p_{B}(b|k,\lambda)E_{k|\lambda}.

It is clear that
A_{a}=\sum_{\lambda}q(\lambda)A_{a|\lambda}
and
B_{b}=\sum_{\lambda}q(\lambda)B_{b|\lambda}
. To prove that
\{A_{a},B_{b}\}_{a,b}
are non-disturbing, we need to verify that there exists a collection of instruments
\{\mathcal{I}_{a|\lambda}\}_{\lambda}
implementing
A_{a|\lambda}
such that

\sum_{\lambda}q(\lambda)\,\sum_{a}\mathcal{I}_{a|\lambda}^{\dagger}\left(B_{b|\lambda}\right)=B_{b}.

(4.50)

We can prove that this is true for the specific case of Lüders instrument, where
\mathcal{I}_{a|\lambda}(\rho)=\sqrt{A_{a|\lambda}}\rho\sqrt{A_{a|\lambda}}
for any state
\rho
. Note that in this case,
\mathcal{I}_{a|\lambda}^{\dagger}(M)=\sqrt{A_{a|\lambda}}M\sqrt{A_{a|\lambda}}
for any measurement
M
.
Therefore, using (
4.49
) in the l.h.s of (
4.50
), we get

\displaystyle\sum_{\lambda}q(\lambda)\,\sum_{a}\mathcal{I}_{a|\lambda}^{\dagger}\left(B_{b|\lambda}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda}q(\lambda)\,\sum_{a}\sqrt{A_{a|\lambda}}B_{b|\lambda}\sqrt{A_{a|\lambda}}

(4.51)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda}q(\lambda)\,\sum_{a}\sqrt{\sum_{k^{{}^{\prime}}=1}^{d}p_{A}(a|k^{{}^{\prime}},\lambda)E_{k^{{}^{\prime}}|\lambda}}\sum_{k=1}^{d}p_{B}(b|k,\lambda)E_{k|\lambda}\sqrt{\sum_{k^{{}^{\prime\prime}}=1}^{d}p_{A}(a|k^{{}^{\prime\prime}},\lambda)E_{k^{{}^{\prime\prime}}|\lambda}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda}q(\lambda)\,\sum_{a}\sum_{k^{{}^{\prime}}=1}^{d}\sqrt{p_{A}(a|k^{{}^{\prime}},\lambda)}E_{k^{{}^{\prime}}|\lambda}\sum_{k=1}^{d}p_{B}(b|k,\lambda)E_{k|\lambda}\sum_{k^{{}^{\prime\prime}}=1}^{d}\sqrt{p_{A}(a|k^{{}^{\prime\prime}},\lambda)}E_{k^{{}^{\prime\prime}}|\lambda},

where in the last equality we used that, since
\{E_{k|\lambda}\}_{k}
are rank-one projective POVMs, then

\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{d}p_{A}(a|k,\lambda)E_{k|\lambda}}=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\sqrt{p_{A}(a|k,\lambda)}E_{k|\lambda}.

(4.52)

Recalling that
E_{k|\lambda}E_{k^{{}^{\prime}}|\lambda}=\delta_{k,k^{{}^{\prime}}}E_{k|\lambda}
, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{\lambda}q(\lambda)\,\sum_{a}\mathcal{I}_{a|\lambda}^{\dagger}\left(B_{b|\lambda}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda}q(\lambda)\,\sum_{a}\sum_{k,k^{\prime},k^{{}^{\prime\prime}}=1}^{d}\sqrt{p_{A}(a|k^{{}^{\prime}},\lambda)}p_{B}(b|k,\lambda)\sqrt{p_{A}(a|k^{{}^{\prime\prime}},\lambda)}\,E_{k^{{}^{\prime}}|\lambda}E_{k|\lambda}E_{k^{{}^{\prime\prime}}|\lambda}

(4.53)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda}q(\lambda)\,\sum_{a}\sum_{k,k^{\prime},k^{{}^{\prime\prime}}=1}^{d}\sqrt{p_{A}(a|k^{{}^{\prime}},\lambda)}p_{B}(b|k,\lambda)\sqrt{p_{A}(a|k^{{}^{\prime\prime}},\lambda)}\,\delta_{k^{{}^{\prime}},k}\delta_{k,k^{{}^{\prime\prime}}}E_{k|\lambda}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda}q(\lambda)\,\sum_{a}\sum_{k=1}^{d}p_{A}(a|k,\lambda)p_{B}(b|k,\lambda)E_{k|\lambda}=\sum_{\lambda}q(\lambda)\,\sum_{k=1}^{d}p_{B}(b|k,\lambda)E_{k|\lambda},

where we used that
\sum_{a}p_{A}(a|k,\lambda)=1
. Notice that the last equality is exactly the r.h.s of (
4.50
) with
B_{b|\lambda}
defined in (
4.49
).

SM 5
Non-disturbance does not implies classicality

In this Appendix we prove that non-disturbance does not imply classicality for sets of POVMs in dimension
d\geq 5
. For this purpose, we assume that non-disturbance implies classicality and we show that this leads to a contradiction.

Consider a
d
-dimensional POVM
\{\tilde{M}_{a}\}_{a=1}^{o}
that, if performed twice in sequence, disturbs itself (later we will take this POVM to be the trine measurement
17
to provide a specific case of the theorem). From the implication
\text{Classical model}\Rightarrow\text{Non-disturbance}
, it is not classically simulable. Now construct the extended POVM

\mathbf{M}\equiv\{M_{a}=\outerproduct{a}{a}\oplus\tilde{M}_{a}\}_{a},\quad a=1,\ldots,o.

(5.54)

This measurement acts on a (
o
+
d
)-dimensional Hilbert space and is non-disturbing, since we can consider the instrument
\mathcal{I}_{a}^{\dagger}(\mathcal{X})=\tr(\mathcal{X}\ketbra{a}\oplus 0_d)M_{a}
. Thus, assuming the antithesis, we must have that
\{M_{a}\}_{a=1}^{o}
is classically simulable. As we will prove later, if an extented measurement
\{M_{a}\}_{a=1}^{o}
of the form (
5.54
) is classically simulable, then the initial POVM
\{\tilde{M}_{a}\}_{a=1}^{o}
is also classically simulable. Therefore, using again that
\text{Classical model}\Rightarrow\text{Non-disturbance}
, we conclude that
\{\tilde{M}_{a}\}_{a=1}^{o}
is non-disturbing when performed twice in sequence. This last statement is in contradiction with the initial hypothesis, i.e. that
\{\tilde{M}_{a}\}_{a=1}^{o}
disturbs itself. Therefore, we get

\text{Non-disturbance}\quad\not\Rightarrow\quad\text{Classical model}

(5.55)

as long as a self-disturbing measurement
\{\tilde{M}_{a}\}_{a=1}^{o}
exists for some dimension. One example is the case of the extremal trine qubit measurement
\mathbf{M}^{\text{trine}}\equiv\{M_{a}=\frac{2}{3}\outerproduct{\psi_a}{\psi_a}\}_{a}
, where
\ket{\psi_a}=\cos(a\pi/3)\ket{0}+\sin(a\pi/3)\ket{1}
and
a=\{1,2,3\}

17
. Since
[M_{a},M_{b}]\neq 0
for
a\neq b
, the trine measurement disturbs itself (indeed, for extremal POVMs, our problem of non-disturbance reduces to the one in
13
). Therefore, using this POVM in (
5.54
), we get an example of a non-disturbing measurement in dimension
5
that is not classically simulable.

Now we will show that
\{\tilde{M}_{a}\}_{a=1}^{o}
is classically simulable if and only if
\{M_{a}\}_{a=1}^{o}
is also classically simulable. In the proof we will consider the classical simulation protocol without the post-processing introduced in Appendix
SM 1
.

For the sufficient condition, we assume that
\{\tilde{M}_{a}\}_{a=1}^{o}
is classically simulable, i.e.

\tilde{M}_{a}=\int d\lambda\,q(\lambda)\,\tilde{F}_{a|\lambda},

(5.56)

for some probability distribution
q(\lambda)
and a set of projectors
\{\tilde{F}_{a|\lambda}\}_{a,\lambda}
for which
\tilde{F}_{a|\lambda}\tilde{F}_{a^{\prime}|\lambda}=\tilde{F}_{a|\lambda}\delta_{a,a^{\prime}}
for each
\lambda
. Then, a classical simulation protocol for the measurement
\{M_{a}=\outerproduct{a}{a}\oplus\tilde{M}_{a}\}_{a=1}^{o}
can be built using a new set of projectors
\{F_{a|\lambda}\equiv\outerproduct{a}{a}\oplus\tilde{F}_{a|\lambda}\}_{a,\lambda}
. Indeed,

F_{a|\lambda}F_{a^{\prime}|\lambda}=(\outerproduct{a}{a}\oplus\tilde{F}_{a|\lambda})(\outerproduct{a'}{a'}\oplus\tilde{F}_{a^{\prime}|\lambda})=\delta_{a,a^{\prime}}\outerproduct{a}{a}\oplus\delta_{a,a^{\prime}}\tilde{F}_{a|\lambda}=\delta_{a,a^{\prime}}F_{a|\lambda},\quad\text{for each }\lambda.

(5.57)

For the necessary condition, we assume that a classical simulation model exists for
\{M_{a}=\outerproduct{a}{a}\oplus\tilde{M}_{a}\}_{a=1}^{o}
, i.e.

\outerproduct{a}{a}\oplus\tilde{M}_{a}=\int d\lambda\,q(\lambda)\,F_{a|\lambda},

(5.58)

where now the projectors
\{F_{a|\lambda}\}_{a,\lambda}
live in the
(o+d)
-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{(o+d)}
. Denote as
\{\outerproduct{a}{a}\oplus 0\}_{a}
a set of projectors acting non-trivially only on the
o
-dimensional subspace of
\mathcal{H}_{(o+d)}
and such that
\{\ket{a}\}_{a}
is a basis for this subspace. Then,

\delta_{a,a^{\prime}}\delta_{a,a^{\prime\prime}}=(\bra{a'}\oplus 0_{d})\outerproduct{a}{a}\oplus\tilde{M}_{a}(\ket{a''}\oplus 0_{d})=\int d\lambda\,q(\lambda)\,(\bra{a'}\oplus 0_{d})F_{a|\lambda}(\ket{a''}\oplus 0_{d}),\quad\forall a,a^{\prime},a^{\prime\prime},

(5.59)

from which we deduce that, since
\{F_{a|\lambda}\}_{a}
are projectors, the non-zero matrix elements on the
o
-dimensional subspace of
\mathcal{H}_{(o+d)}
are diagonal, i.e.

F_{a|\lambda}=\outerproduct{a}{a}\oplus\tilde{F}_{a|\lambda},\quad\forall a.

(5.60)

Since
F_{a|\lambda}F_{a^{\prime}|\lambda}=F_{a|\lambda}\delta_{a,a^{\prime}}
, then also
\tilde{F}_{a|\lambda}\tilde{F}_{a^{\prime}|\lambda}=\tilde{F}_{a|\lambda}\delta_{a,a^{\prime}}
. Therefore, applying in (
5.58
) the projector
\tilde{\Pi}
onto the
d
-dimensional subspace associated with
\{\tilde{M}_{a}\}_{a}
, we get

\tilde{M}_{a}=\tilde{\Pi}M_{a}\tilde{\Pi}=\int d\lambda\,q(\lambda)\tilde{\Pi}F_{a|\lambda}\tilde{\Pi}=\int d\lambda\,q(\lambda)\tilde{F}_{a,\lambda},

(5.61)

which is a classical simulation model for
\{\tilde{M}_{a}\}_{a}
.
