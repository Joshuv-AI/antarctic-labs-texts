Disparities in pathways for anaemia care and recovery among women after major abdominal surgery: secondary analysis of a prospective cohort study in 29 countries
Authors: Sivesh Kathir Kamarajah, Dharani Hapangama, Dania Badran, Dania Badran, Omar Kouli, Ieva Jakaitytė, Setthasorn Ooi Zhi Yang, Aneel Bhangu, Kariem El-Boghdadly, James Glasbey, Ana Minaya Bravo, Gregory Lip, Shakila Thangaratinam, Dharani Hangapama, Sivesh K Kamarajah, Omar Kouli, Sivesh Kamarajah, S K Kamarajah, R Blanco-Colino, N Brindl, S Brown, W A Cambridge, D Chaudhry, K Gressmann, R R Gujjuri, A Hilder, A Jaffer, I Jakaityte, M Kawka, O Kouli, E Matey, K A McLean, A Mergo, E C Mills, V Murray, S Z Y Ooi, I S Pereira, A M Riad, A Sgrò, S Q Shafi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAnaemia is a common modifiable risk factor in surgery, yet how it is recognized and managed in women across different surgical pathways is unknown. This study aimed to evaluate anaemia prevalence in women and its management across surgical pathways and life stages, examine associations with 30-day outcomes, and estimate the potential benefit of anaemia correction.MethodsA secondary analysis was conducted of a prospective cohort study from 427 hospitals in 29 countries, including women having elective major abdominal surgery. The primary exposure was surgical pathway (gynaecology versus non-gynaecology), used as a pragmatic proxy for pathway design. Preoperative anaemia and perioperative iron therapy were conceptualized as mediators; menopausal status (pre- versus postmenopausal, by age) was a prespecified effect modifier. Coprimary outcomes were 30-day unplanned readmission and days alive and out of the hospital 30 days from surgery (DAOH30). Mixed-effects logistic regression was used with a hospital random intercept for readmission, and quantile regression for DAOH30. A 2000-iteration cluster bootstrap estimated absolute risk reduction and number needed to treat (NNT = 1/absolute risk difference) for counterfactual anaemia correction in key subgroups. Natural-effects models assessed whether recorded perioperative iron therapy partly explained the pathway differences in readmission.ResultsA total of 10 114 women (median age 57.6 years (interquartile range 48.0–69.0)) were included; 3590 (35.5%) were premenopausal and 3214 (31.8%) underwent gynaecological surgery. Preoperative anaemia was present in 29.0% and was more frequent in non-gynaecology than gynaecology (31.9% versus 22.8%). Thirty-day unplanned readmission occurred in 7.2%. In adjusted models, the gynaecology pathway had lower odds of readmission than non-gynaecology (adjusted odds ratio 0.78, 95% confidence interval 0.62 to 0.97), whereas preoperative anaemia was associated with higher readmission (adjusted odds ratio 1.28, 1.06 to 1.56). Lower perioperative iron use in non-gynaecology partly mediated the pathway gap (indirect effect odds ratio 1.06, 1.02 to 1.09; proportion mediated 15.2%). The largest model-based absolute risk difference was observed among women of premenopausal age undergoing non-gynaecological surgery: predicted readmission was 11.8% with anaemia versus 6.4% without anaemia, corresponding to an absolute risk difference of 5.32% and an NNT estimand of 19. Median DAOH30 was 26 days (interquartile range 23–28). Anaemia was associated with fewer days at home, with the largest decrement among those with poorest recovery (10th percentile β −3.0 days, 95% confidence interval −4.0 to −2.0), a smaller reduction at the median (β −1.0 days, −1.3 to −0.72), and no difference at the 90th percentile.ConclusionFor women, pathway design, not only patient risk, shapes whether anaemia is recognized and treated. The greatest avoidable harm falls on premenopausal women outside gynaecology. Health systems should embed universal screening and optimization, adopt standardized iron protocols across all pathways, and track life-stage-stratified performance metrics to close this gap and increase days at home.

Introduction

Anaemia is a leading cause of disability in women. It affects over a third of those of reproductive age and remains common after menopause1. In surgical patients, anaemia is associated with more complications, more transfusions, longer hospital stays, and higher mortality2,3. Even in non-surgical patients, anaemia can impact on clinical outcomes in chronic long-term conditions, such as atrial fibrillation, where higher incidences of major bleeding, heart failure hospitalization, and mortality have been shown in association with anaemia severity4. Notably, the causes of anaemia differ by life stage: menstrual blood loss and iron deficiency before menopause; chronic disease, nutritional deficits, and occult bleeding after menopause5,6. However, in all groups, anaemia is modifiable with interventions such as oral or intravenous iron, which can improve haemoglobin levels, reduce transfusion requirements, and enhance postoperative recovery7,8.

Despite clear guidance, perioperative anaemia care is uneven. Patient blood management (PBM) guidelines3 recommend universal preoperative screening and optimization before major surgery, yet delivery varies across services. Gynaecological surgical pathways, anchored in women’s health, may include routine haemoglobin testing, iron supplementation protocols, and continuation of therapy after surgery. In contrast, non-gynaecological pathways may lack such integration, with screening and treatment delivered inconsistently or too late to influence outcomes. The result is a structural gap in pathway design: access to evidence-based anaemia care may depend more on the pathway a woman enters than on her clinical need. Whereas sex differences in surgical outcomes are well described9–11, far less is known about within-women inequities across specialties and life stages. Existing research and knowledge are based on single specialties such as obstetrics, gynaecologic oncology, or cardiac and rarely consider life stage.

This study addresses a critical evidence gap: no previous large-scale analysis has compared anaemia prevalence, management, and outcomes in women across gynaecological and non-gynaecological pathways, stratified by menopausal status, nor quantified the absolute benefit of anaemia correction in key subgroups. The aim was therefore to compare the burden of anaemia and its management by life stage and pathway among women having major abdominal surgery; examine associations with two coprimary outcomes, 30-day unplanned readmission (clinical destabilization) and days alive and out of hospital up to 30 days from surgery (DAOH30; time at home); and estimate absolute risk reduction and number needed to treat (NNT) associated with correcting anaemia in high-yield subgroups. Addressing this gap will evaluate whether disparities are structural and modifiable, and whether targeted optimization can reduce readmissions and increase time at home.

Methods

Data source, study design and population

A secondary analysis was performed of the CASCADE cohort (CArdiovaSCulAr outcomes after major abDominal surgEry)12, a prospective study of postoperative outcomes after major abdominal surgery conducted across 427 hospitals in 29 European countries. This secondary analysis was prespecified in the main study protocol12. CASCADE enrolled consecutive adults (≥ 18 years) undergoing elective or emergency major abdominal surgery, defined as abdominal or pelvic visceral resection, open vascular, or transplant surgery across five surgical specialties and any operative approach. Exclusions were trauma, day-case procedures, and operations without visceral resection. Data were collected prospectively over five predefined 2-week periods between 24 January and 3April 2022, with 30-day follow-up. Ethical approval was granted by the NHS South East Scotland Research Ethics Committee (NR/161AB6), with exemption from individual consent owing to use of anonymized routine data; local approvals were obtained per national regulations. A summary of the country-level approvals are provided in Appendix S1. A detailed description of the CASCADE cohort study is presented in Appendix S2. Reporting follows STROBE13 guidance (Appendix S3). The CASCADE protocol12 and primary results14 are published elsewhere. For this analysis, women undergoing elective major abdominal surgery were included; emergency cases were excluded.

Data collection

A prespecified case report form (CRF) was developed drawing upon templates successfully used in previous multinational cohort studies conducted within the STARSurg global collaborative research network15,16. The CRF was developed in collaboration with a Study Management Group comprising surgical, perioperative, and geriatric care experts. Several iterative rounds of expert review and refinement ensured that the CRF captured relevant patient- and operative-level data aligned with the preplanned primary and secondary analyses. This process also ensured the content validity of the CRF, as it was informed by existing literature and clinical expertise. At each participating hospital, clinical data were prospectively collected and entered into the CRF by local research teams, which consisted primarily of trainee doctors and consultants. All collaborators received online training on data definitions, and additional training or troubleshooting was available from national and study leads. The CRF was designed for use by clinicians to record routinely collected clinical data on all eligible patients. A standardized 30-day follow-up was conducted in person, via telephone or through chart review, using structured protocols. The CRF was translated into local languages by national study leads to ensure contextual appropriateness. Although formal psychometric validation of the CRF was not undertaken, translations were based on previously validated templates used in multinational studies conducted by the research network. To ensure data integrity further, the study leveraged the well established research infrastructure of the STARSurg global network. This included centralized data storage using the Research Electronic Data Capture17 web application hosted at the University of Birmingham (UK), which facilitated secure and standardized data management. Central monitoring of data completeness and logic was performed throughout the study, and only data collection periods achieving > 95% completeness were accepted for inclusion in the pooled analysis. However, no data validation was performed on ascertainment and accuracy, as previous studies conducted by the research network have highlighted robust data quality. Based on previous studies18,19, data ascertainment and accuracy are as high as 96%.

Exposures

The primary exposure was surgical pathway, defined by operating specialty (gynaecology versus non-gynaecology). Surgical pathway was treated as a pragmatic bundle exposure, capturing pathway organization alongside differences in case mix, procedure type, operative complexity, and perioperative infrastructure, rather than as a direct measure of any single pathway component. This was treated as a proxy for perioperative pathway design, reflecting systematic differences in PBM. Gynaecological services more often embed routine haemoglobin (± ferritin) testing, explicit treatment thresholds, and direct access to iron therapy20–22, whereas non-gynaecological services show later and more heterogeneous PBM adoption, with anaemia assessment variably devolved to general medicine or primary care3,23. Treating specialty as a pathway proxy therefore captures organizational design and protocolization differences plausibly upstream of screening and treatment delivery, beyond case mix. Perioperative anaemia and its management were conceptualized as mediators on the causal pathway between surgical specialty and outcomes. Preoperative anaemia was defined by World Health Organization24 criteria for non-pregnant women (haemoglobin < 120 g/l). The preoperative haemoglobin was the last value recorded before incision, typically at preassessment or on admission. For descriptive analyses, postoperative nadir haemoglobin (lowest inpatient value) and discharge haemoglobin (last inpatient value before discharge) were also defined. Perioperative iron therapy (oral or intravenous) was included as the principal treatment mediator, representing a direct, modifiable mechanism through which pathway design could alter outcomes. Menopausal status was not directly recorded in CASCADE. Age was therefore used as a pragmatic proxy for life stage, defining women aged < 51 years as being of premenopausal age and women aged ≥ 51 years as being of postmenopausal age. This threshold was selected to approximate the median age at natural menopause25,26. These cut-offs reflect median age at natural menopause in high-income settings. All pathway models were adjusted for case mix and included a random intercept for hospital. Prespecified covariates included frailty, American Society of Anesthiologists (ASA) grade, multimorbidity (≥ 2 long-term conditions)27, operative indication (benign versus malignant), and approach (open versus minimally invasive).

Study outcomes

Two coprimary outcomes were prespecified to capture complementary dimensions of recovery and health-system use: 30-day unplanned readmission and DAOH30. Unplanned readmission was any unanticipated inpatient admission (index or non-index hospital) after discharge and within 30 days of the index operation. Planned or staged readmissions (for example chemotherapy, planned stoma reversal), interhospital transfers during the index admission, and emergency department attendances without admission were excluded. Ascertainment used hospital records and structured patient contact. DAOH30was calculated as 30 minus total inpatient days (index stay plus all readmissions) within 30 days. A minimum of 1 inpatient day was counted for the index admission. Patients who died during the index admission or remained hospitalized for the full 30-day window were assigned DAOH30 = 0. For patients discharged who died within 30 days, DAOH30 equalled days alive after discharge. A detailed description of different scenarios to calculate DAOH30 is presented in Fig. S1. Readmission provides a system-level signal of adverse postoperative course, and DAOH30 captures patient-centred recovery. DAOH30 is a patient-centred metric28 integrating survival, length of stay, and readmissions into a single interpretable measure29; it is routinely derivable from administrative data and facilitates robust comparisons across hospitals and pathways29. Using both outcomes minimizes reliance on any single proxy for recovery, improves interpretability across surgical pathways, and directly serves the study objective of identifying pathway-driven, modifiable differences in perioperative care for women.

Sample size

As a secondary analysis, a precision check against expected readmission differences was conducted. Using published estimates30 (12.3% with anaemia versus 6.0% without anaemia; absolute difference 6.3% points), 3240 women per group would provide 80% power (two-sided α = 0.05). Allowing 10% missingness, approximately 7200 women would undergo surgery. The cohort of 10 114 women undergoing surgery provides ample precision for subgroup and interaction estimates.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics are reported as median (interquartile range (i.q.r.)) or counts (%). Between-group comparisons used Mann-Whitney U and χ2 tests. All tests were two-sided (α = 0.05). Primary inferential analyses used complete cases for the relevant model variables. Missingness was low for the main exposure and coprimary outcomes, but was higher for some descriptive variables, including postoperative nadir haemoglobin, discharge haemoglobin, and smoking status. Postoperative and discharge haemoglobin were therefore interpreted descriptively and were not used as core adjustment variables in the primary readmission model. Effect sizes and 95% confidence intervals (c.i.) were emphasized; no multiplicity adjustment was applied to secondary or exploratory analyses. Analyses used R 4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) using the packages finalfit, mgcv, tidyverse, and quantreg.

For the outcome of 30-day unplanned readmission, multilevel logistic regression with a random intercept for hospital to account for clustering was used. A hospital-level random intercept was included because patients were clustered within hospitals and were therefore unlikely to represent statistically independent observations. This term allowed baseline readmission risk to vary between hospitals, accounting for shared institutional factors such as perioperative protocols, discharge thresholds, readmission practices, and access to anaemia optimization services. The resulting odds ratios (ORs) should therefore be interpreted as hospital-conditional associations, estimated after accounting for between-hospital differences in baseline readmission risk. Fixed effects included multimorbidity, ASA grade, frailty, surgical approach, and operative indication. Results are presented as adjusted ORs with 95% c.i. Model assumptions were assessed including collinearity, calibration, discrimination, and random-effects variance. To evaluate whether effects differed by life stage and anaemia status, a generalized additive model with a prespecified three-way interaction (pathway × preoperative anaemia × menopausal status) was fitted. Marginal predicted risks under counterfactual anaemia states were estimated by g-computation, from which absolute risk differences were derived. An NNT-type estimand as the inverse of the absolute risk difference was also calculated. Because this was an observational analysis, this estimate should not be interpreted as a trial-derived clinical NNT or a direct treatment target. Rather, it represents a model-based counterfactual estimand reliant on standard causal assumptions, including no residual confounding, correct model specification, consistency, and adequate overlap between comparison groups. Its purpose was to aid interpretation of absolute risk differences and identify subgroups with the greatest potential yield from anaemia optimization. Uncertainty was quantified with 2000 hospital-cluster bootstrap replicates. Whether perioperative iron therapy explained part of the pathway–readmission association was tested. Iron therapy was selected as the mediator because it represents a direct, modifiable mechanism through which pathway design could alter outcomes. A natural-effects modelling approach with two logistic models (outcome and mediator) was applied31. Estimates included natural direct and indirect effects, and the proportion mediated. Identification assumptions were prespecified (no unmeasured confounding of exposure-outcome, exposure-mediator, or mediator-outcome; no mediator-outcome confounder affected by exposure).

Sensitivity analysis by operative specialty

To address heterogeneity within the non-gynaecology group, two sensitivity analyses were performed. First, anaemia burden, estimated blood loss, perioperative iron therapy, and 30-day unplanned readmission were summarized by granular operative specialty. Vascular and transplant surgery were combined because of small numbers. Second, because gastrointestinal and liver surgery was the largest non-gynaecological subgroup, the counterfactual interaction analysis was repeated restricted to women undergoing gastrointestinal and liver surgery, using the same modelling approach as the primary analysis. Predicted probabilities of unplanned readmission were estimated under anaemic and non-anaemic states, stratified by menopausal status, with absolute risk differences and numbers needed to treat derived from 2000 bootstrap replicates.

For DAOH30, quantile regression was used due to the skewed, non-normal, and zero-inflated distribution of this outcome32,33. This approach estimates effects across the outcome distribution rather than at the mean, providing insight into heterogeneity of recovery34. Models were adjusted for the same covariates as above. Effect estimates are reported at the following percentiles: 5th (patients with poorest recovery) 10th, 25th, 50th (median), 75th and the 90th percentile (patients with the best recovery). Results are expressed as β coefficients with 95% c.i.

Role of the funding source

The sponsor of the study had no role in study design, data collection, data analysis, data interpretation, or writing of the report. The corresponding author had full access to all the data in the study and had final responsibility for the decision to submit for publication.

Results

Cohort characteristics

A total of 10 114 women undergoing elective major abdominal surgery from 427 hospitals in 29 European countries were included (Fig. 1). Of these, 3214 (31.8%) underwent gynaecology surgery and 6900 (68.2%) underwent non-gynaecology surgery. Median age was 52.0 years (i.q.r. 44.0-62.0) in women undergoing gynaecology surgery and 62.0 years (i.q.r. 49.0–72.0) undergoing non-gynaecology surgery. Compared with women undergoing non-gynaecology surgery, women undergoing gynaecology surgery were more likely to be younger and consequentially premenopausal (48.8% versus 29.3%; P < 0.001), had lower ASA physical status (ASA I–II 82.5% versus 62.8%; P < 0.001), less multimorbidity (25.4% versus 40.8%), and lower frailty (frailty 4-9: 8.5% versus 15.6%). Most procedures were for benign indications (59.7%), and 61.0% used minimally invasive approaches (Table 1).

Preoperative anaemia and management

Preoperative anaemia was less common in gynaecological than non-gynaecological surgery (22.8% versus 31.9%; P < 0.001). After surgery, anaemia remained less common in patients undergoing gynaecological than non-gynaecological surgery at haemoglobin nadir (47.9% versus 57.4%; P < 0.001) and at discharge (38.0% versus 43.5%; P < 0.001). Although preoperative iron treatment was similar across the pathways, postoperative iron therapy use remained higher in gynaecological than non-gynaecological pathways (11.8% versus 6.6%; P < 0.001) (Table 1). Differences in baseline characteristics by preoperative anaemia status is presented in Table S1.

Unplanned 30-day readmission

Overall, 724 of 10 114 (7.2%) women were readmitted within 30 days. Readmission was more frequent in non-gynaecology than gynaecology pathways (7.5% versus 6.3%; P = 0.030), in women with anaemia than those without (9.2% versus 6.4%; P < 0.001) and in premenopausal than postmenopausal women (8.6% versus 6.4%; P < 0.001). Differences in patient- and operative-level characteristics by unplanned 30-day readmission are presented in Table S2. In a multilevel logistic regression, women undergoing surgery in gynaecological pathways had lower odds than those in non-gynaecological (OR 0.78, 95% c.i. 0.62 to 0.97) for 30-day readmission. However, preoperative anaemia was associated with higher 30-day unplanned readmission (OR 1.28, 1.06 to 1.56) (Fig. 2). Women of postmenopausal age were associated with lower odds of 30-day unplanned readmission (OR 0.77, 0.62 to 0.95) than those of premenopausal age. The full model is presented in Table S3.

Multilevel logistic regression of patient, and operative characteristics on unplanned readmission to hospital within 30 days from surgery in patients undergoing elective abdominal surgery. Multilevel model includes hospital and country identifier as a random effect.

To understand the influence between surgical pathways (exposure) and perioperative anaemia treatment (mediator) on 30-day unplanned readmission, a mediation analysis was performed. A total of 15.2% of the pathway difference in 30-day unplanned readmission was mediated by lower perioperative iron therapy use in non-gynaecology (natural indirect effect OR 1.06, 95% c.i. 1.02 to 1.09; P = 0.001) (Fig. S2). The remaining pathway association was not explained by perioperative iron therapy, indicating that iron treatment captured only a modest, measurable component of the pathway difference in readmission, with other pathway-level mechanisms likely contributing. Interactions between menopausal status, pathway, and anaemia were explored using a generalized additive model with cluster bootstrap. Absence of preoperative anaemia was associated with a lower predicted probability of readmission across strata (Fig. 3a). The largest absolute difference was for premenopausal women in non-gynaecological surgery: predicted readmission 11.8% with anaemia versus 6.4% without anaemia; absolute risk reduction 5.32% (95% c.i. 3.59 to 7.36; P < 0.001), corresponding to a NNT of 19 (18.8, 95% c.i. 13.6 to 27.9) to prevent one readmission by correcting anaemia (Fig. 3b; Tables S4, S5).

Sensitivity analysis by operative specialty

Specialty-stratified sensitivity analysis confirmed heterogeneity within the non-gynaecology group. Gastrointestinal and liver surgery was the largest non-gynaecological subgroup (6208), with preoperative anaemia in 1950 of 5995 women (32.5%), preoperative iron therapy in 495 of 6208 (8.0%), postoperative iron therapy in 396 of 6208 (6.4%), and 30-day unplanned readmission in 464 of 6208 (7.5%) (Table S7). By comparison, gynaecological surgery had lower preoperative anaemia prevalence (733 of 3061, 23.9%) and similar preoperative iron therapy use (268 of 3214, 8.3%), but higher postoperative iron therapy use (378 of 3214, 11.8%). In the gastrointestinal and liver surgery-only counterfactual analysis, the findings were consistent with the main non-gynaecology analysis (Table S5).

Days alive and out of hospital at 30-days

Median DAOH30 was 26 days (i.q.r. 23–28). Premenopausal women had more days alive and out of hospital than postmenopausal women (median 27 (i.q.r. 24–29) versus 25 (22–27); P < 0.001). Patients with anaemia had fewer days alive and out of hospital than patients without anaemia (Fig. 4a). In multivariable quantile regression, the association between gynaecological pathways and lower DAOH30 was greatest across all percentiles from the 5th percentile (β −4.3 days, 95% c.i. −5.1 to −3.5; P < 0.001), and to the 90th percentile (β −1.0 days, −1.9 to −0.1; P = 0.030) (Fig. 4b). The association between preoperative anaemia and lower DAOH30 was greatest at the 5th and 10th percentiles (β −3.0 days, −4.0 to −2.0; P < 0.001), smaller at the median (β −1.0 days, −1.3 to −0.72; P < 0.001), and absent at the 90th percentile (β 0.00 days, −0.85 to 0.85; P > 0.900). However, women of postmenopausal age were not associated with DAOH30 across all percentiles (Fig. 4d). The detailed model of the quantile regression is presented in Table S6.

Although gynaecological pathways were associated with lower odds of 30-day unplanned readmission, they were also associated with fewer days alive and out of hospital across the DAOH30 distribution. These findings should not be interpreted as contradictory, as the two outcomes capture different aspects of recovery. Readmission reflects postdischarge clinical destabilization, whereas DAOH30 is strongly influenced by the duration of the index admission, postoperative monitoring, discharge policy, and procedure mix. Therefore, lower readmission can coexist with lower DAOH30 where inpatient stays are longer or discharge thresholds differ across pathways.

Discussion

In this study, across 10 114 women in 427 hospitals in 29 European countries, the principal findings are as follows: perioperative anaemia care systematically varies by surgical pathway; preoperative anaemia was common, especially outside gynaecological surgical pathways, and associated with poor 30-day outcomes; and gynaecological pathways used iron therapy more consistently, whilst non-gynaecological pathways treated less despite higher need, and part of their higher readmission risk was explained by lower use of perioperative iron therapy.

To the authors’ knowledge, no studies have evaluated perioperative anaemia across surgical pathways that differ in their alignment with women’s health priorities, quantified differences in treatment, or assessed the consequences for outcomes across menopausal status. The largest, most actionable gain remains with premenopausal women in non-gynaecological surgery, where correcting anaemia could reduce readmissions by about 5.3% (NNT of 19). Findings from this study emphasize the need for health systems to embed anaemia optimization as a default process in every pathway, track it with simple metrics, and close the gap for higher-risk groups.

Existing research has examined mixed-sex populations2,35, where sex-specific and life stage-specific analyses are absent, or have been restricted to a single specialty such as obstetrics or gynaecology3,36,37. The adverse association between preoperative anaemia and surgical outcomes is well established, with meta-analyses linking anaemia to increased complications, longer hospitalization, and greater mortality2,36,38,39. In contrast, the present analyses compares gynaecological versus non-gynaecological pathways, stratifies by life stage, quantifies absolute benefit, and tests a plausible mechanism, lower perioperative iron use, behind pathway differences in readmission. The present data suggest that for women having major abdominal surgery, whether anaemia is recognized and treated depends as much on how care is organized as on clinical risk. The two coprimary outcomes point to complementary truths. Readmission signals clinical destabilization and service failure; DAOH30 captures time at home, integrating survival, length of stay, and readmissions. Anaemia raised readmission risk and reduced DAOH30, with the largest shortfall among women with poorest recovery. These patterns support PBM as a system priority, not an optional add-on.

A central finding is that perioperative anaemia care and its association with postoperative outcomes differ across heterogeneous surgical pathways. Surgical pathways should therefore be interpreted as a bundle exposure. The observed associations may reflect differences in anaemia-management infrastructure, but also residual differences in case mix, procedure type, operative complexity, blood loss, discharge practices, and other specialty-specific processes. Accordingly, these findings suggest pathway-level variation in anaemia care and recovery, but do not prove that pathway design alone caused the observed differences. Gynaecological and non-gynaecological surgeries represent distinct perioperative care models, with different degrees of integration of women’s health priorities, PBM protocols, and access to timely iron therapy. These structural differences translate into heterogeneous risk profiles and modifiability of outcomes.

Although preoperative anaemia was consistently associated with worse outcomes across pathways, its absolute impact on 30-day readmission varied substantially between gynaecological and non-gynaecological surgery, with the largest absolute benefit from correction concentrated among premenopausal women undergoing non-gynaecological procedures. This reflects both higher baseline risk and lower treatment uptake in these pathways, illustrating how the same biological exposure can have different system-level consequences depending on pathway design.

Heterogeneity was also evident in postoperative recovery, as assessed by DAOH30. Using quantile regression, anaemia was associated with markedly fewer days at home among women with the poorest recovery, with smaller effects around the median and no clear association among those with the best recovery. This distributional heterogeneity suggests that anaemia disproportionately affects vulnerable recovery trajectories, particularly within less protocolized pathways. Residual heterogeneity is likely across hospitals and countries, reflecting variation in screening thresholds, iron availability and timing, perioperative governance, and discharge or readmission practices. Although multilevel modelling accounted for clustering, such contextual differences may continue to influence both treatment delivery and outcome ascertainment. Residual heterogeneity is also likely across hospitals and countries, reflecting variation in screening thresholds, iron availability, perioperative governance, and discharge or readmission practices. The hospital random intercept accounted for clustering and allowed baseline readmission risk to vary between hospitals, but it did not identify which institutional factors explain this variation. Therefore, the readmission estimates should be interpreted as hospital-conditional associations rather than direct measures of specific hospital-level processes.

Finally, the two coprimary outcomes provided complementary, but not identical, signals. Gynaecological pathways were associated with lower odds of 30-day unplanned readmission, but also with fewer days alive and out of hospital. This distinction is important. Readmission captures postdischarge clinical destabilization and health-system use, whereas DAOH30 integrates survival, index length of stay, and readmissions. A lower DAOH30 in gynaecology therefore does not necessarily contradict more consistent anaemia management; it may reflect longer planned inpatient recovery, greater representation of gynaecological oncology procedures, pathway-specific postoperative monitoring, or more conservative discharge policies. The findings suggest that anaemia treatment may be more consistently delivered in gynaecology, whereas overall recovery time remains shaped by procedure mix, cancer pathways, inpatient care models, and institutional discharge practices.

The differences observed are likely driven by variation in the design and implementation of perioperative pathways. In gynaecological pathways, high iron-deficiency burden may have driven embedded protocols, which may include routine haemoglobin and/or ferritin testing, clear thresholds, and ready access to perioperative iron treatment. In contrast, non-gynaecological pathways may devolve anaemia to primary care or general medicine, resulting in late or inconsistent screening and treatment. The persistence of differences across menopause suggests a structural cause, not simply variation in aetiology. Lower perioperative iron therapy use in non-gynaecological pathways mediated 15.2% of the pathway difference in 30-day readmission, suggesting that iron treatment is a measurable and modifiable contributor, but not the dominant explanation for the pathway gap. The mediation analysis was consistent with partial mediation by recorded perioperative iron therapy but should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than mechanistic. Receipt of iron therapy may reflect anaemia severity, anaemia aetiology, timing of surgery, clinician decision-making, and local service availability, which may also relate to readmission. The remaining direct association should not be interpreted simply as ‘unmeasured confounding’, but rather as the component not explained by the measured mediator. This may include other pathway-level factors such as timing of anaemia screening, access to ferritin testing, preoperative assessment infrastructure, thresholds for intervention, transfusion practice, postoperative monitoring, discharge and readmission thresholds, institutional resources, surgical complexity, and residual confounding. Therefore, standardizing iron therapy is likely to reduce only part of the disparity and should be embedded within broader perioperative pathway redesign. This mechanistic interpretation is further limited by the absence of ferritin or transferrin saturation data. Perioperative iron therapy should therefore be interpreted as a measurable marker of anaemia-directed care, rather than proof that the observed pathway association was mediated specifically through correction of iron-deficiency anaemia. Further, the higher prevalence of anaemia in the premenopausal population may be linked to the high prevalence of unrecognized and untreated menstrual disorders in this group, representing a silent burden. A further caveat is that anaemia is generally associated with poor overall health and adverse socioeconomic indices.

These findings translate into clear priorities for practice and policy. First, the high prevalence of anaemia across all groups, and its disproportionate burden in non-gynaecological surgery, supports the adoption of universal perioperative screening and optimization in all surgical specialties, consistent with international PBM guidelines20. Embedding haemoglobin assessments into preoperative workflows and monitoring compliance through quality systems would operationalize this recommendation. Second, the lower use of iron therapy in non-gynaecological pathways despite greater need highlights the importance of standardized treatment protocols, covering both oral and intravenous regimens, with defined thresholds and timelines for intervention40. This is to ensure timely preoperative correction and appropriate postoperative continuation41. Third, targeted optimization for high-risk groups, particularly premenopausal women in non-gynaecological surgery, could include early identification at surgical listing, fast-track access to intravenous iron, and coordinated follow-up, offering rapid and measurable reductions in readmissions. Finally, pathway-level performance metrics stratified by menopausal status including screening rates, time to treatment, and rates of preoperative optimization should be incorporated into perioperative governance to enable benchmarking, guide resource allocation, and support continuous improvement.

This study has several strengths. These include prospective data, including a large sample size, harmonized definitions, and a focus on women with a life-stage lens. Using both a system outcome (readmission) and a recovery metric (DAOH30) increases policy relevance. Multilevel modelling accounted for clustering; interaction modelling and g-computation provided interpretable absolute effects; mediation offered mechanistic insight. Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, as an observational analysis, causality cannot be inferred, and residual confounding from unmeasured factors such as surgical complexity, intraoperative events, institutional resources, and social determinants remains possible. Although the mixed-effects model accounted for hospital clustering, the random intercept captures unmeasured between-hospital variation rather than explaining it. Residual institutional confounding may therefore persist if hospital-level factors, such as anaemia screening pathways, intravenous iron access, discharge policy, or readmission thresholds, are associated with both surgical pathway and outcome. Second, menopausal status was inferred from age, introducing inevitable misclassification. Third, anaemia definitions were standardized using the World Health Organization criteria, but variation in local laboratory thresholds, measurement timing, and treatment triggers across countries may have introduced heterogeneity. Relatedly, because iron treatment may reflect unmeasured anaemia severity, aetiology, clinician judgement, and service availability, these findings should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than definitive evidence of mechanism. Missing data may also have introduced bias. Although missingness was low for the main exposure and coprimary outcomes, postoperative nadir haemoglobin, discharge haemoglobin, and smoking status had more substantial missingness. Missing postoperative haemoglobin values are unlikely to be completely random, as testing may reflect clinical concern, postoperative complications, local monitoring protocols, and discharge practices. These variables were therefore interpreted cautiously, particularly where presented descriptively, and residual bias from non-random missingness cannot be excluded. Fourth, classification of surgery as gynaecological versus non-gynaecological was used as a pragmatic marker of pathway design, but inevitably oversimplifies variation in procedure type, expected blood loss, operative complexity, and perioperative infrastructure. Sensitivity analyses were therefore performed using granular operative specialty categories and a focused gastrointestinal and liver surgery-only analysis. These showed heterogeneity across specialties but did not materially alter the overall interpretation. Nevertheless, residual heterogeneity within specialties and between hospitals may persist despite adjustment for operative indication, approach, frailty, multimorbidity, and hospital-level clustering. Fifth, treatment classification lacked detail on timing, dose, formulation, and adherence, and it was not always possible to distinguish optimization from reactive postoperative treatment. Sixth, unplanned readmission, although widely used in quality reporting, is a composite outcome influenced by multiple factors beyond anaemia; reasons for readmission were not always available. Days alive and out of hospital can also be affected by local discharge and readmission policies. Seventh, The NNT estimates should be interpreted cautiously. They were calculated from model-based counterfactual absolute risk differences in an observational data set and therefore depend on strong assumptions, including no residual confounding, consistency, adequate overlap, and correct model specification. They should be viewed as NNT-type estimands useful for comparing the potential yield of optimization across subgroups, rather than as direct clinical treatment targets. Prospective interventional studies are needed to determine whether anaemia correction would achieve these absolute reductions in readmission. Finally, participating hospitals may be more research-active than non-participating centres, potentially underestimating the scale of disparity, and variation in intravenous iron availability across Europe may limit immediate feasibility of universal implementation.

Surgical pathways without embedded anaemia-management processes may under-recognize and undertreat perioperative anaemia, even when clinical need is high. This pathway-level variation is measurable, clinically important, and potentially modifiable. Universal screening and optimization, standardized treatment protocols across surgical pathways, and targeted strategies for high-risk groups, particularly women of premenopausal age undergoing non-gynaecological surgery, should be prioritized. Life-stage-stratified performance metrics within perioperative governance may help identify unwarranted variation, guide resource allocation, and improve consistency of surgical care and outcomes for women.
