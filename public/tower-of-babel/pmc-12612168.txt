Cancer-associated fibroblast subtype and risk signature as predictors of prognosis and treatment effectiveness in gastric cancer
Authors: Ruyue Chen, Zengwu Yao, Lixin Jiang, Jinchen Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite the persistent high incidence and mortality rates of gastric cancer, limited progress has been made in improving prognosis.cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) hold promise as novel biomarkers, yet their specific roles and mechanisms in gastric cancer remain enigmatic. We utilized the TCGA-STAD dataset for model training and the GSE84433 dataset for validation to explore potential associations between cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) and clinical outcomes in gastric cancer. Through consensus clustering, differential expression analysis, enrichment analysis, and immune infiltration assessment, Kaplan–Meier (KM) survival analysis, and TIMER database exploration, we examined the prognostic significance and underlying biological mechanisms of CAF-related genes in gastric cancer. A CAF risk model (CAF-RM) was developed to assess the correlation between risk scores and survival, drug sensitivity, and immune infiltration. The model was further validated through immunohistochemical (IHC) and immunofluorescence (IF) staining. Utilizing data from 42 CAF-related genes, consensus clustering identified two distinct patient subgroups, C1 and C2, with significant differences in survival, immune scores, and immune cell infiltration. A Cox-Lasso regression-derived CAF-RM accurately discriminated high-risk from low-risk gastric cancer patients, consistent with validation cohort results, IHC and IF staining. These findings suggest that CAFs play a role in the tumor immune microenvironment of gastric cancer and highlight the efficacy of CAF-RM in prognosis prediction. CAF subtypes associate with immune tumor microenvironment alterations in gastric cancer. Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) is a prevalent global malignancy, with a consistently high mortality rate1. Early diagnosis remains challenging due to the non-specific nature of its symptoms, often leading to delayed identification and poor prognosis2,3. Current treatment strategies primarily involve surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy, but despite technological advancements, mortality rates remain unacceptable4. Immunotherapy is also a strategy to treat gastric cancer5,6, such as CheckMate 649 trial7 and KEYNOTE 859 trial8. Therefore, the urgent need for effective biomarkers and prognostic models is pivotal in improving patient outcomes9–11. Research in early diagnosis and personalized treatment plans for GC holds the key to reducing mortality rates and enhancing patient care12.

Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) play a pivotal role in gastric cancer initiation and progression, influencing tumor microenvironment through secretion of cytokines, matrix components, and growth factors13,14. Additionally, CAFs facilitate immune evasion, suppressing anti-tumor immune responses15. Exploring their mechanisms may offer novel therapeutic targets in gastric cancer treatment16,17. CAFs suppress anti-tumor immune responses and promote immune evasion by secreting cytokines such as TGF-β and IL-6. They also interact with tumor cells, activating Wnt/β-catenin and Notch signaling pathways, thereby enhancing tumor cell invasiveness. Furthermore, CAFs remodel the tumor stroma by secreting matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), which facilitates tumor cell migration and metastasis, thus driving gastric cancer progression. CAFs also induce the polarization of M2 macrophages, enhancing the immunosuppressive environment and inhibiting the function of anti-tumor T cells, thereby exacerbating tumor immune tolerance18–20. In summary, CAFs play a multifaceted role in gastric cancer by modulating the tumor immune environment and extracellular matrix remodeling, which supports tumor growth, metastasis, and immune evasion. Therefore, targeting CAFs may offer a promising therapeutic strategy for gastric cancer treatment.

This study introduces the novel CAF risk model (CAF-RM) as a valuable tool for predicting patient outcomes and identifying individuals who may benefit most from immunotherapy in gastric cancer. By assessing the immune microenvironment and survival prognosis of GC patients, CAF-RM opens the door to discovering potential therapeutic targets and advancing personalized treatment strategies. Its development and validation highlight its potential to revolutionize cancer care by tailoring interventions to individual patient needs.

Data sources

Transcriptomic data from 407 STAD cases were collected from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) for the training set, comprising 375 STAD samples and 32 normal samples. Additionally, gene mutation data and clinical information for TCGA-STAD were obtained. For the validation set, microarray gene expression data from 357 patients were sourced from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. This dataset, with GEO accession number GSE84433 on platform GPL6947, provided valuable information for validation purposes. The combination of TCGA and GEO databases allowed for a comprehensive analysis of gene expression and mutation patterns in stomach cancer, enhancing our understanding of the disease.

Consensus clustering analysis of CAF-related subtypes

We chose 42 CAF-related genes for clustering analysis using ConsensusClusterPlus. With K-means clustering and one-logarithmic correlation distance, we resampled 80% of samples ten times. The stability of results was confirmed by determining the optimal cluster number based on cumulative distribution function curve, K value, and intra-cluster consistency. Although clustering stability was observed at both K = 2 and K = 4, survival analysis demonstrated that K = 2 provided the most significant prognostic separation and higher reproducibility across datasets.

Identification and analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs)

Using the limma method (v3.40.6), we performed differential analysis to identify DEGs between groups, with criteria of adjusted p < 0.05 and |fold change|> 1.5. GO and KEGG (https://www.kegg.jp/kegg/kegg1.html)21–23 enrichment analyses were then conducted on the identified DEGs to compare signaling pathways and biological effects among the CAF-related cluster cohorts. The enrichment analyses were significant with q-value and p value thresholds of < 0.05.

Mutation landscape analysis

We analyzed somatic mutations in STAD patients from TCGA data, visualizing mutated genes in a waterfall plot.

Construction and validation of CAF-related model (CAF-RM)

DEGs were first identified between the two CAF-related clusters. These DEGs were then used as candidate genes for prognostic model construction. We conducted a study employing the LASSO-Cox regression method with the glmnet package in R. This approach integrated survival time, outcome, and gene expression data. Our goal was to optimize the model through tenfold cross-validation. Additionally, we devised a nomogram leveraging TNM staging, age, sex, and risk characteristics to predict GC patient survival rates at 1, 3, and 5 years.

The risk score is calculated as follows: Risk score = ∑(Gene expression_i × Coefficient_i). Where Gene expression refers to the expression level of each gene, and Coefficient is the coefficient of each gene derived from the Cox regression model.

Immune cell infiltration analysis

We employed advanced algorithms to analyze the composition of 22 immune cell types in the tumor microenvironment. By using the CIBERSORT and ESTIMATE algorithms, we calculated the relative proportions of immune cells, estimated tumor purity, immune scores, and stromal scores. In addition, a Spearman correlation analysis was performed to investigate the connection between risk scores and immune cell types.

CIBERSORT, a computational method based on gene expression data, employs a support vector regression (SVR) algorithm to deconvolute bulk tissue gene expression profiles and estimate the proportion of various immune cell types in complex tissue environments. By leveraging a predefined gene signature matrix for immune cells, the algorithm infers the relative abundance of immune cell populations, such as T cells, macrophages, and dendritic cells, among others. This method allows for the detailed analysis of the immune microenvironment, providing insights into the immune landscape of gastric cancer and its potential impact on disease progression and therapy response.

To further validate the immune infiltration landscape, we also utilized the TIMER (Tumor Immune Estimation Resource) database, which provides a systematic evaluation of six major immune infiltrates (B cells, CD4⁺ T cells, CD8⁺ T cells, macrophages, neutrophils, and dendritic cells) across diverse cancer types. TIMER employs a deconvolution approach to infer immune cell abundance from RNA-seq expression profiles and has been widely applied for studying immune–tumor interactions.

Drug sensitivity analysis

We evaluated chemotherapy and immunotherapy response through IC50 values using the pRRophetic R package. We predicted IC50 values for various drugs in gastric cancer samples, examining the association between risk groups and drug sensitivity.

Validation of CAF-related prognostic genes via immunohistochemical staining

Data from HPA were accessed, including immunohistochemical staining of GC and normal tissues. CAF-related prognostic genes were validated at the protein level, with staining ranging from high to undetectable.

Kaplan–Meier plotter analysis

To further validate the prognostic value of the CAF-RM genes, we utilized the Kaplan–Meier plotter, an online tool that integrates gene expression and survival data from multiple gastric cancer cohorts. Overall survival (OS) was assessed using Kaplan–Meier survival analysis, and hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. A log-rank test was applied to evaluate statistical significance, with a threshold of p < 0.05 considered significant.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) and immunofluorescence (IF)

Paraffin-embedded gastric cancer and adjacent tissues were sectioned at 4 μm. After deparaffinization, rehydration, and antigen retrieval, sections were incubated with primary antibodies. For IHC, HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies and DAB were used for visualization with hematoxylin counterstaining. For IF, fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies and DAPI nuclear staining were applied. Images were captured using a light or fluorescence microscope.

Results

Clustering analysis identified CAF-related subtypes

An interaction network of 42 CAF genes24–26 was analyzed (Fig. 1A). Consensus clustering of 375 GC samples using the ConsensusClusterPlus algorithm revealed that both K = 2 and K = 4 produced relatively stable clusters (Fig. 1B,C). However, the cumulative distribution function (CDF) curves, intra-cluster consistency, and prognostic separation supported K = 2 as the optimal solution (Fig. 1D). The two subtypes exhibited distinct CAF gene expression patterns (Fig. 1E), as visualized in the consensus matrix heat map (Fig. 1F). Kaplan–Meier survival analysis demonstrated that subtype C2 associated with better overall survival compared to C1 [(p = 0.03; HR = 0.69; 95% CI (0.49–0.96)] (Fig. 1G) (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Identification of CAFs-related subtypes by consensus clustering. (A) Protein–protein interaction (PPI) network analysis of 42 CAFs-related genes; (B, C) Cluster analysis cumulative distribution function (CDF), showing the area under the curve and the decreasing trend of Delta when k = 2–10; (D) Example cluster consistency plot, showing that the consensus value is optimal when k = 2; (E) Heat map of CAFs-related gene expression in the two subtypes; (F) Consensus matrix with optimal k = 2; (G) Kaplan–Meier curve of overall survival of the two subtypes (p = 0.03).

Differential gene expression and enrichment analysis were conducted for CAF-related subtypes to identify key genes and pathways

The contrasting prognostic outcomes between two different subtypes of CAFs prompted an investigation into the underlying molecular mechanisms. Through a thorough analysis of the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in both subtypes, a total of 938 up-regulated DEGs and 115 down-regulated DEGs were identified (Fig. 2A,B). Notably, the expression patterns of these genes varied significantly within each subtype. Subsequent GO and KEGG enrichment analyses revealed that these DEGs play a crucial role in extracellular components and various cancer-related processes. These processes include biological adhesion, extracellular matrix formation, interactions within the collagen-containing extracellular matrix, ECM-receptor interactions, Proteoglycans in cancer regulation, Cell adhesion molecules (CAMs) modulation, and cytokine-cytokine receptor interactions (Fig. 2C,D). These findings shed light on the intricate molecular landscape underlying the divergent prognostic outcomes observed between the two CAFs subtypes.

Identification of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and biological pathways between the two CAFs subtypes. (A, B) Heatmaps and volcano plots show the DEGs of the two groups; (C, D) Bubble plots and circle plots show the enrichment of GO and KEGG (https://www.kegg.jp/kegg/kegg1.html) potential signaling pathways.

Comparison of somatic mutations and immune cell populations in the tumor microenvironment in different subtypes associated with CAFs reveals distinct characteristics

The study delved into the somatic mutations present in patients from different groups, examining the top 15 genes with the highest mutation rates through waterfall plots (Fig. 3A,B). In group C1, genes such as TTN, TP53, CSMD3, LRP1B, and MUC16 exhibited the most frequent mutations. Conversely, group C2 showcased ARID1A as the fifth most commonly mutated gene, with a mutation frequency of 30.2%, surpassing group C1’s frequency of 23.0%. Notably, MUC16 emerged as the third most frequently mutated gene in group C2 at a rate of 39.5%, outstripping the 28.7% frequency seen in group C1. Remarkably, TTN and TP53, with the highest mutation frequencies, retained their top positions in both groups, underscoring their significance in the mutation profile.

Somatic mutations and immune infiltration in two CAFs subtypes. (A, B) Gene mutation waterfall plots show that the 15 most common mutated genes in GC are different between C1 and C2 groups; (C) ESTIMATE box plots show immune score, stromal score and ESTIMATE score of different infiltration subtypes; (D) CIBERSORT box plot showing the differences in the infiltration of 22 immune cells in two CAFs subtypes. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001.

Our research delved further into the intricacies of the immune cell populations in the tumor microenvironment within two distinctive subtypes. Notably, the immune score, stromal score, and estimated score of group C2 surpassed those of group C1 (Fig. 3C). Employing the CIBERSORT method, we illustrated the variances in immune cell infiltration between these groups with detailed box plots. It was evident that group C1 exhibited a more pronounced degree of monocytes, M2 macrophages, and resting mast cells infiltration in comparison to group C2. In addition, notable differences were observed in NK memory cells and CD4⁺ memory T cells, with group C2 showing increased abundance of these subsets (Fig. 3D). This observation sheds light on the distinct characteristics and potentially different treatment responses exhibited by each subtype.

Developed and validated, CAF-RM accurately predicts risks

Integration of survival time, status, and DEGs expression data using lasso-cox analysis yielded 7 prognostic genes linked to CAFs: NRP1, SERPINE1, MATN3, APOD, RGS2, SNCG, and RAMP1 (Fig. 4). The model revealed a risk score formula where each gene held a specific weight. Noteworthy, the Lambda value was determined to be 0.0733275947510836. The optimal lambda value was determined using the minimum cross-validation error criterion, which controls model complexity and prevents overfitting. This was optimized using cross-validation. The Lambda value plays a crucial role in regularized regression models by controlling the model’s complexity, preventing overfitting, and ensuring the model’s generalizability. At this Lambda value, the coefficients (weights) of each gene are determined, reflecting the relative importance of each gene in risk prediction. This process helps in identifying the most relevant biomarkers while maintaining model stability and predictive power. The model formula is:RiskScore = 0.0807526385722191*NRP1 + 0.0786537002346875*SERPINE1 + 0.0505704829995201*MATN3 + 0.0140445996646025*APOD + 0.0084157004381075*RGS2 + 0.00293123529020579*SNCG + 0.00771956565159379*RAMP1.

Construction and validation of the CAFs-related risk model. (A, B) Lasso-Cox analysis extracted 7 CAFs-related prognostic genes; (C) Prognostic heat map showing the relationship between different risk scores, patient survival and gene expression changes of the CAFs-related risk model; (D, E) K-M curves show for Overall Survival for CAF-RM in the training dataset TCGA-STAD and validation dataset GSE84433.

This study underscores the potential impact of CAFs-related genes on prognosis. Through regression analysis, the weight of each gene was calculated. These gene weights are closely related to their functions in the tumor microenvironment. For instance, higher weights for certain genes indicate their significant role in gastric cancer progression. These genes may contribute to the development of gastric cancer by promoting tumor cell proliferation or facilitating immune evasion, thus supporting the tumor’s growth and metastasis. The weights reflect the relative importance of each gene in the overall risk model, highlighting critical biomarkers for prognosis and therapeutic targeting.

The analysis conducted on the relationship between survival status and risk score revealed a significant decline in the survival rate of patients as the risk score increased. It was observed that the seven genes identified are all considered risk factors for patient survival, as their expression levels displayed a noticeable increase in tandem with the rise in risk score. Furthermore, the prognostic significance of the CAF-risk model (CAF-RM) for GC patients was examined using Kaplan–Meier analysis. In the TCGA-STAD dataset, it was found that the low-risk group had a more favorable survival prognosis [p = 6.8 × 10–8; HR = 2.42; 95 CI%(1.74,3.36)], a trend that was also confirmed in the GSE84433 validation set [p = 4.9 × 10–4; HR = 1.70; 95 CI%(1.26,2.30)]. These findings indicate the effectiveness of the CAF-RM in accurately predicting survival outcomes for gastric cancer patients, underlining its potential as a valuable tool in clinical practice.

Association of CAF-RM with prognosis in medical research studies

Our study utilized multivariate Cox analysis to establish a nomogram incorporating TNM grade, age, sex, and other risk characteristics for predicting the survival of gastric cancer patients (Fig. 5A). Calibration curves were plotted to demonstrate the consistency between predicted and actual survival outcomes (Fig. 5B), while ROC analysis was conducted to assess the predictive performance of the nomogram (Fig. 5C). The area under the curve (AUC) values for 1, 3, and 5 years were found to be 0.70, 0.75, and 0.79, respectively, indicating the strong predictive ability of our model. These findings suggest that our nomogram based on CAF-RM provides a reliable tool for estimating survival outcomes in gastric cancer patients.

Association of CAF-RM with GC prognosis. (A–C) Nomo plots, calibration plots, and time-dependent ROC curve analysis for predicting the 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival probabilities of patients.

CAF-RM’s relationship with tumor microenvironment and drug efficacy shows potential for enhanced treatment outcomes

The study delved into the connection between CAFs-related genes and immune cell infiltration in the immune microenvironment. Findings indicated a positive correlation between risk score and Monocytes, M2 macrophages, and resting Mast cells, while a negative correlation was observed with T follicular helper cells (Fig. 6A–D). These results were consistent in the GSE84433 validation set (Fig. 6E–H). The research highlights the critical role of CAFs-related genes in shaping the immune landscape, shedding light on potential implications for immunotherapy strategies.

Association of CAF-RM with the tumor microenvironment. Scatter plots showing the correlation of the risk score with infiltration of T follicular helper cells and monocytes, M2 macrophages, and resting mast cells in the training set (A–D) and further validated by the GSE84433 cohort (E–H). *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001.

Subsequently, we investigated 16 compounds, focusing on the variations in IC50 predicted values between individuals at high and low risk. The drug sensitivity analysis revealed (refer to Fig. 7) that individuals classified in the low-risk category exhibited higher sensitivity towards several drugs including Ponatinib (AP24534), Luminespib (AUY922), Bortezomib, Bosutinib, Cisplatin, Cytarabine, Dasatinib, Docetaxel, Elesclomol, Paclitaxel, Pazopanib, Temsirolimus, and Vinblastine, in comparison to those in the high-risk group. Conversely, individuals categorized as high-risk demonstrated increased sensitivity to Afatinib (BIBW2992), Gefitinib, and Methotrexate compared to their low-risk counterparts. These findings hold promise for tailoring personalized treatment strategies for patients in both risk categories. High-risk patients are generally associated with poorer prognosis and may require more aggressive treatment strategies, such as intensified chemotherapy or targeted therapy, to improve treatment outcomes. In contrast, low-risk patients are likely to have better survival prospects and may be suitable for conventional treatment approaches, or in some cases, may not require extensive intervention.

Furthermore, the treatment differences are particularly critical when considering immunotherapy. For high-risk patients, immune checkpoint inhibitors (such as PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors) may be necessary to enhance the immune system’s anti-tumor response. These patients, due to the immune evasion characteristics of their tumor microenvironment, may rely more on immunotherapy to achieve better therapeutic results. On the other hand, low-risk patients may experience favorable outcomes through conventional chemotherapy or localized treatment regimens.

Our analysis, as depicted in Fig. 8, revealed a significant increase in APOD and SNCG expression in gastric cancer tissues. However, the expression variances of NRP1 and SERPINE1 were not pronounced. Discussion

Gastric cancer (GC) is a significant global health concern, with researchers striving to identify biological markers to enhance prognosis. Recent studies have highlighted the crucial role of cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) in the development of GC, offering a promising avenue for novel treatment approaches. These findings have shed light on potential strategies to target CAFs and their involvement in cancer progression, opening up new possibilities for improved outcomes in gastric cancer patients. Efforts to elucidate the complexities of GC continue to hold great potential for advancements in treatment.

This study aimed to identify and characterize two distinct subgroups of cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) based on the expression of 42 CAF-related genes. Surprisingly, our analysis revealed that gastric cancer patients clustered into these subgroups exhibited significant differences in survival outcomes, immune cell infiltration patterns, and overall prognosis. To further investigate these findings, we utilized the limma method to pinpoint specific genes that were differentially expressed between the two subgroups. Through the application of the cox-lasso method, we identified a set of 7 key genes that were instrumental in distinguishing these CAF subtypes. These genes have been implicated in cancer progression and inflammation, affecting immune responses in various ways. For instance, APOD has been shown to modulate oxidative stress and inflammation by binding to arachidonic acid (ARA)27. NRP1, on the other hand, has been linked to promoting cell proliferation and migration in prostate cancer28, while its downregulation has been associated with progression in bladder cancer29. Additionally, increased levels of SERPINE1 due to antiplatelet therapy have been linked to enhanced cell motility and an increased risk of metastasis in colon cancer30. MATN3, an extracellular matrix protein, may influence cell adhesion, matrix organization, and stromal-tumor interactions, thereby affecting cancer progression31. RGS2 is involved in G-protein signaling regulation, potentially modulating cell proliferation, migration, and immune responses within the tumor microenvironment32. SNCG has been reported to promote tumor cell invasion, metastasis, and stromal remodeling, contributing to a more aggressive phenotype33. RAMP1, as a receptor activity-modifying protein, may influence signaling pathways related to tumor-stroma crosstalk and CAF activation34. Collectively, these genes likely impact CAF activity, extracellular matrix composition, and immune infiltration, which in turn shape patient outcomes. Integrating these molecular insights with clinical parameters, such as TNM stage, enables stratification of patients into distinct risk groups using the CAF-RM nomogram, providing potential guidance for prognosis assessment and individualized treatment strategies.This comprehensive analysis sheds new light on the complex role of CAFs in gastric cancer progression and highlights potential therapeutic targets for further exploration.

Our comprehensive analysis of 7 specific genes revealed that patients with CAF-RM demonstrated improved survival rates within the low-risk group. This positive prognosis was consistent in both the initial and validation data sets. These findings shed light on a potential treatment approach focused on manipulating the tumor microenvironment. This study highlights a promising avenue for enhancing patient outcomes in cancer treatment.

The CAF-RM signature developed in this study demonstrates robust prognostic performance and has potential clinical utility in gastric cancer management. By integrating the CAF-RM risk score with conventional clinical parameters such as TNM stage, age, and sex, we constructed a nomogram capable of stratifying patients into distinct risk groups. High-risk patients identified by this nomogram exhibited poorer overall survival, whereas low-risk patients showed favorable outcomes, indicating its potential to complement traditional staging systems. Such stratification may inform individualized treatment decisions, including more intensive monitoring or adjuvant therapy for high-risk patients and tailored therapeutic strategies for low-risk patients. Furthermore, combining molecular signatures like CAF-RM with clinical parameters could enhance the precision of prognostic assessment, providing a more comprehensive framework for patient counseling and therapeutic planning. Overall, the CAF-RM nomogram represents a promising tool for translating molecular insights into clinically actionable guidance in gastric cancer.

In our analysis depicted in the CIBERSORT box plot (Fig. 3D), it was observed that the infiltration levels of monocytes, M2 macrophages, and resting mast cells were significantly higher in group C1 compared to group C2. Monocytes are known for their versatility, actively participating in inflammatory responses, phagocytosis of pathogens, and differentiation into macrophages and dendritic cells, thereby boosting antigen presentation abilities35–37. Additionally, monocytes contribute to tissue repair and regeneration by secreting a range of cytokines and chemokines that regulate immune responses and inflammatory processes.M1 macrophages excel at combating bacteria and pathogens by generating oxygen-free radicals and inflammatory cytokines, whereas M2 macrophages focus on anti-inflammatory activities and facilitating tissue repair and regeneration38–40. Despite their quiescent state, resting mast cells swiftly release stored mediators like histamine, cytokines, and chemokines upon encountering pathogens or stimuli, thereby instigating inflammatory responses and attracting other immune cells. Moreover, resting mast cells also aid in maintaining tissue equilibrium, participating in antimicrobial defense, and promoting tissue healing41–43.These findings suggest a possible correlation between the immune cell profiles in the two groups and the survival rates of GC patients. Group C2, with lower infiltration levels of these immune cells, may exhibit enhanced survival outcomes possibly due to a more balanced immune response that promotes tissue repair and homeostasis while effectively combating pathogens. The impact of cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) on the immune microenvironment is also crucial. Through interactions with immune cells, particularly by inducing the polarization of M2 macrophages, CAFs enhance the immunosuppressive environment and inhibit the function of anti-tumor T cells, thus exacerbating tumor immune tolerance44. Additionally, CAFs secrete immunosuppressive factors such as IL-10 and TGF-β, promoting the aggregation of Tregs and M2 macrophages, further suppressing the function of effector T cells and NK cells, thereby weakening the immune surveillance capacity of the host 45.

CAFs-related genes play a crucial role in the immune microenvironment, impacting immune cell infiltration. Our analysis revealed a positive correlation between CAF-RM and Monocytes, M2 macrophages, and resting Mast cells, while a negative correlation was observed with T follicular helper cells (Fig. 6A–D). This finding was further validated in the GSE84433 dataset (Fig. 6E–H).

Furthermore, we identified 12 compounds with varying IC50 values between high-risk and low-risk groups. Interestingly, the high-risk group showed sensitivity to 3 compounds, while the low-risk group exhibited sensitivity to 9 compounds, as illustrated in Fig. 7. This information could potentially aid in personalized medication for these distinct patient groups.

In terms of drug sensitivity, CAFs may influence the tumor’s response to chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and immunotherapy by altering the structure of the tumor microenvironment and immune responses. For instance, growth factors such as HGF and VEGF secreted by CAFs promote tumor angiogenesis, enhance tumor cell survival, and reduce the penetration of chemotherapy drugs, thereby leading to chemotherapy resistance. At the same time, CAFs secrete immunosuppressive factors that reduce the tumor’s response to immune checkpoint inhibitors (such as PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors), diminishing the efficacy of immunotherapy46,47. Therefore, targeted therapies against CAFs could not only restore tumor immune sensitivity but also potentially enhance the tumor’s response to chemotherapy and immunotherapy.

To assess the prognostic significance of CAFs-related genes in gastric cancer, we utilized HPA immunohistochemical staining data. Our results indicated that these genes may serve as important markers for gastric cancer progression. To strengthen these findings, we performed immunohistochemistry (IHC) on our own gastric cancer tissue samples, confirming the expression patterns of these genes. Additionally, Kaplan–Meier survival analysis (KM-plotter) further validated their prognostic relevance. Functional enrichment analyses using GO and KEGG highlighted potential biological pathways influenced by the CAF-RM genes, providing insights into tumor microenvironment modulation. Finally, TIMER database analyses demonstrated significant associations between these genes and immune cell infiltration, underscoring their role in shaping the tumor immune landscape. Collectively, these multi-dimensional validations suggest that targeting CAF-RM–related pathways may offer a promising strategy for improving patient outcomes in gastric cancer.

While bioinformatics analysis is certainly a valuable tool for precise data analysis and prediction, it does have its limitations. It is crucial to verify findings through experimental methods like real-time PCR and western blotting in cell and animal studies. To further investigate the underlying molecular mechanisms, gene knockout or overexpression experiments should be incorporated, both in vitro and in vivo. These experiments would help clarify the specific role of CAF-related genes in gastric cancer progression and their impact on immune modulation and drug sensitivity. By manipulating the expression levels of key genes within CAFs, we can assess their effects on tumor growth, immune infiltration, and response to therapies. Such studies will provide more detailed insights into how CAFs contribute to the tumor microenvironment and identify potential targets for therapeutic intervention. Additionally, the results of this study should be further validated in other gastric cancer cohorts. This retrospective study underscores the need for future prospective, multicenter research to confirm and expand upon our findings. By utilizing a combination of bioinformatics and experimental approaches, we can better understand the potential biomarkers and implications for gastric cancer diagnosis and treatment, ultimately leading to improved outcomes for patients.

Conclusions

Our research emphasized the link between different subtypes of cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) and alterations in the immune microenvironment within gastric cancer. A predictive model known as CAF-RM was developed and proven effective across various patient groups. This model plays a crucial role in forecasting survival rates, levels of immune cell infiltration, drug responses, and more in individuals with gastric cancer. These findings enhance our knowledge of CAFs and offer potential avenues for tailored treatment approaches.
