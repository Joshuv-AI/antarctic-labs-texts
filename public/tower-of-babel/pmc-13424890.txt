Psychodermatology in Ireland: current practices, confidence and barriers among dermatologists
Authors: Li Jie Helena Yoo, Man Ching Wong, Siona Ní Raghallaigh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In this study, we surveyed Irish dermatologists and highlight that the majority lack formal training in psychodermatology but are cognisant in recognizing mental health issues in patients with chronic dermatological diseases. Access to psychology remains significantly limited and there is an urgent need for interdisciplinary collaboration and standardization of referral pathways to bridge the gap between dermatology, psychology and psychiatry in Ireland.

Psychiatric comorbidity affects 30–40% of patients requiring dermatology services. Psychodermatology encompasses three main domains: (i) psychiatric disorders with dermatological symptoms (e.g. delusional infestation, dermatitis artefacta and trichotillomania); (ii) psychiatric disorders caused by chronic dermatological disease; and (iii) psychophysiological disorders (dermatology diseases precipitated or exacerbated by stress).1 Untreated mood disorders can lead to undesirable lifestyle behaviours that modulate the onset and progression of skin diseases.2 The National Clinical Programme for Dermatology in Ireland (2020) recommends appointing psychologists to dermatology teams, in order to provide holistic care.3 We created a survey that aimed to assess current practices, confidence and barriers among dermatologists in managing patients requiring psychodermatology services, and to evaluate access to psychological and psychiatric services in Ireland.

An online survey was distributed to members of the Irish Association of Dermatologists (IAD) in June 2024, with a reminder sent 1 month later. Data were analysed on 31 August 2024. Association between clinician grade, years of training and practices were examined using the χ2 test, with P-values ≤0.05 considered statistically significant.

Of 135 IAD members, 47 responded (34.8%): 13 consultants (28%), 26 registrars (56%), 4 senior house officers (9%), 3 general practitioners in dermatology (6%) and 1 advanced nurse practitioner (2%). The majority of respondents (n = 39; 83%) practised in Ireland, with 98% (n = 46) in public settings. The majority of respondents (n = 34; 72%) had no formal training in psychodermatology (Table S1, see Supporting Information). Where recorded, training comprised brief sessions at conferences or other courses. Psychophysiological disorders were most frequently encountered (1–3 patients weekly), followed by mental health problems due to chronic skin conditions (1–3 patients monthly). Psychiatric disorders manifesting with dermatological symptoms were the least commonly encountered (1–3 patients annually).

Most clinicians (n = 45/47; 96%) rarely encountered patients with suicidal ideation secondary to skin disease; when present, it was most often seen in patients with atopic dermatitis (n = 31/45; 69%), acne (n = 25/45; 56%), psoriasis (n = 22/45; 49%) and hidradenitis suppurativa (n = 22/45; 49%). While 45% (n = 21/47) of clinicians frequently assessed psychological burden, only 23% (n = 11/47) regularly used validated mental health scales. No significant differences were found between consultants and registrars with regard to the frequency of assessing a patient’s mental wellbeing (χ2 = 0.03, P = 0.86), use of validated tools (χ2 = 0.57, P = 0.45) or referral to community support services (χ2 = 0.31, P = 0.58), reflecting similar approaches across grades. Most clinicians (n = 35/47; 74%) felt confident in identifying urgent cases and triaging patients for psychiatric assessment. Confidence did not significantly differ by years of training (χ2 = 0.81, P = 0.67), suggesting that experience alone does not ensure greater confidence, probably reflecting the fragmented and self-directed nature of psychodermatology training.

The majority of respondents (n = 26/47; 55%) infrequently referred patients to psychology services, reflecting that 81% (n = 38/47) of respondents work in centres without dedicated psychological services. Furthermore, 77% (n = 36/47) reported no established pathways for urgent psychiatric referrals. Key challenges included limited consultation time (n = 36/47; 77%), insufficient knowledge in managing patients with primary psychiatric disease and comorbidities (n = 29/47; 62%), and inadequate mental health service support (n = 28/47; 60%). Thematic analysis further explored barriers to arranging psychology referrals (Table S2, see Supporting Information).

Although most clinicians lack formal psychodermatology training, Irish dermatologists are cognisant in recognizing mental health issues in patients with chronic skin conditions. However, this awareness does not always translate into practice, as shown by the low rate of psychology referrals, likely due to limited access and referral barriers.

The British Association of Dermatologists and European Academy of Dermatology and Venereology (EADV) have invested in advancing psychodermatology training, yet it remains informal and unstandardized.4 Training gaps identified in this survey align with those reported by UK dermatologists, highlighting similar educational challenges.5 Refining the Irish dermatology curriculum by introducing mandatory psychodermatology modules, structured clinical exposure by integrating telehealth psychiatry liaison and standardized assessment tools could enhance competence.

The EADV ‘Burden of Skin Diseases’ study highlighted significant stigmatization and reduced quality of life in patients requiring dermatology services, underscoring the psychological burden of dermatological conditions.6 Limited access to psychological services, coupled with barriers identified in this study, emphasize the need for interdisciplinary collaboration and standardized pathways linking dermatology, psychology and psychiatry in Ireland.

The 34.8% response rate may have introduced nonresponse bias as clinicians with a greater interest in psychodermatology may have been more likely to participate. The power of this study is limited by the relatively small sample size. No information was collected on respondents’ subspecialty focus, limiting generalizability. Reliance on self-reported data may introduce recall bias and the cross-sectional design does not capture changes in service or training over time.

Irish dermatologists recognize the psychological impact of chronic skin disease, yet gaps in training, limited access to services and fragmented referral pathways constrain holistic care. Addressing these barriers through structured training, standardized assessment tools and interdisciplinary collaboration could improve outcomes, reduce stigma and enhance service efficiency. Having dedicated psychologists in dermatology departments may streamline care and increase patient engagement within one multidisciplinary centre.7 It is crucial that we establish a referral pathway for urgent psychiatric referrals, a critical gap in recognizing that holistic dermatology care extends beyond skin deep.
