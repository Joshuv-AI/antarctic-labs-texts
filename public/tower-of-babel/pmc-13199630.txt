Toxicodynamics and Human Health Risks of Metal Nanoparticles: A Review of AI/ML-Based Predictive Nanotoxicology
Authors: Kumud Kant Awasthi, Garima Awasthi, Pallavi Saxena, Anjali Awasthi, Ankit Srivastava, Mahipal Singh Sankhla, Anupam Singha Roy, Aabid Hussain, Rashi Miglani, Gaurav Rawat, Ruchi Pathania
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMetal-based nanoparticles (MNPs) are the most extensively explored advanced nanostructures for biomedical applications. These MNPs, have unique intrinsic properties. These properties modify particle characteristics and functionalization, making them suitable for applications in biomedicine, cancer imaging, and other therapeutics. With the exceeding production of MNPs annually, toxic chemical and solvents used for synthesis of MNPs are causing various health implications. This review discusses MNPs effects on human exposure, biokinetics, and toxicodynamics alongside developments in artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) for predictive toxicology. In vitro and in vivo mechanistic study reports organ-specific accumulation of MNPs, particularly in liver, kidney, and brain, with potential crossing of the blood brain and placental barriers leading to oxidative stress, ROS production, mitochondrial dysfunction, and genotoxicity. The review also presents integrating omics approach and AI frameworks to attain safe nanoparticle development and human health risk assessment.Keywords: metal nanoparticles, nanotoxicology, toxicodynamics, biokinetics, artificial intelligence, human health

Introduction

Exponential usage of metal nanoparticles (MNPs) covered almost every horizon of modern life ranging from food packaging, cosmetics to precision medicine delivery to advanced diagnostics in health care. Versatile physiochemical properties of MNPs owing due to the small size and high surface area these particles are highly reactive and felicitate close contact with cells and tissues.1 However, this biological proximity presents the risk of subtle and cumulative toxic impact that might not be noticeable in traditional toxicology frameworks. In this regard, the idea of toxicodynamic the way that nanoparticles interact with biological targets over time to have negative effects has emerged as a key concept for comprehending the hazards to human health. Toxicodynamics as bridge between physicochemical properties and biological effects associated with long-term, low-dose exposures in practical situations.2

Metal and metal-oxide nanoparticles may enter the body through ingestion, inhalation, skin contact, and medical procedures. From epidemiological and experimental studies, it has been observed that they can then spread to various organs, such as the brain, heart, liver, lung, and reproductive tissues. These particles may cause oxidative stress, mitochondrial damage, inflammation, immunological dysregulation, and genotoxicity after internalization.3–7 They may also be linked to respiratory disorders, cardiovascular problems, neurotoxicity, and carcinogenesis. Physicochemical characteristics including size, shape, solubility, surface charge, and corona formation have a significant impact on the amount and pattern of these reactions. This results in a complex toxicodynamic environment that is challenging to represent using single-parameter or static models.8

Simultaneously, the artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) playing its role in generation, integration, and interpretation of nanotoxicity data which is being transmuted by large, multidimensional datasets which is beyond the capabilities of conventional statistical methods that are currently produced via high-throughput in vitro tests, omics platforms, and sophisticated imaging.9 The supervised and unsupervised machine learning offers strong framework for mapping the complex interplay between nanoparticle descriptors, adverse biological outcomes, and exposure conditions. These data-driven approaches help in prioritizing testing, helps in improving dose–response assessment, and support safer by-design ingenuities for metal nanomaterials when paired with mechanistic awareness. However, rapid adoption of these applications ie, AI/ML in nanotoxicology, introduces challenges; methodological differences, fragmented datasets, and narrow experimental scopes, which often restrict and limit generalizability.

Furthermore, in contrast to the public health context, the black-box models hinder and often lack transparency, which is required for regulatory and clinical standards. Moving forward, there is a pressing need to pair and connect toxicodynamic concepts with explainable AI, standardized reporting protocols, and validation of frameworks to ensure practical efficacy in risk assessment.10 The exposure pathway, widespread biodistribution, and toxicodynamic effects of metal nanoparticles are schematically illustrated in Figure 1.

Classification and Physicochemical Characteristics of Metal Nanoparticles

Inorganic NPs come under the sub-category of MNPs depicting distinct physiochemical properties based upon their sizes in comparison with their bulk metals. Inorganic NPs stability, reactivity and optical behavior changes significantly with the change in their composition, size, shape and surface chemistry. This unique feature enables their usage in multiple biomedical applications. Categorically, nanoparticles are divided into three based on their composition: the organic, carbon-based, and inorganic systems. The inorganic class comprises metal and metal oxide nanoparticles,11 the alterations in electronic structure and redox behavior among these groups, metal nanoparticles are often further classified into noble metals like Au, Ag, and Pt and more reactive transition or base metals like Fe, Cu, Co, Ni, and Zn. Metal nanoparticles, according to morphology and dimensionality, are categorized into zero-dimensional (spheres, cubes), one-dimensional (nanorods, nanowires), and two-dimensional (nanoplates, nanofilms) forms.12 A balancing arrangement differentiates single‑component metal nanoparticles from more complex systems such as core shell, alloy, and metal–polymer or metal–carbon nanocomposites, which demonstrate developing possessions do not present in the discrete mechanisms. When associated to bulk materials, metal nanoparticles typically have at least one dimension less than 100 nm, increasing the fraction of surface atoms and the surface-area-to-volume ratio. Many important progressions, such as biodistribution, cellular uptake and optical response, are supportively measured by its shape and size. Alterations in localized surface plasmon resonance bands result in distinctive changes in diameter or aspect ratio for noble metals which are widely used in imaging and sensing.13,14

Colloidal stability and interactions with biomolecules and cell membranes are enticingly inclined by surface charge, which is often labelled as zeta potential. While near-neutral surfaces reassure agglomeration and sedimentation, particularly in electrolyte-rich environments, high absolute zeta potential tends to stabilize metal nanoparticle dispersions. Small ligands, polymers, or biomolecules are examples of surface coatings that may modify charge, steric stabilization, and specific binding. They often regulate biological individuality through the expansion of protein corona. Surface-modified ZnO nanoparticles, for instance, demonstrated comparable core size and crystallinity but knowingly different zeta potentials, signifying that coatings may adapt interfacial features without affecting the crystalline metal core. Since they control surface energy, facet expression, and mechanical and chemical stability, crystallinity, and defect structure that are substantial physicochemical features of metal nanoparticles. While polycrystalline or twinned particles have grain boundaries and flaws that function as very reactive or unstable sites, single-crystalline particles often display well-defined facets with expectable catalytic action. Architectures like core-shell, hollow, or mesoporous metal nanoparticles are examples of inner structure that offer more control over surface accessibility, its loading capacity, and diffusion paths. To reduce toxicity, such structural complexity is increasingly employed to make the most of performance in drug administration, imaging, and heterogeneous catalysis.15 Predominantly, for more soluble systems like Ag, Cu, and Zn, where liberated ions knowingly subsidize to biological and ecological influences, dissolution and oxidation are important components of metal nanoparticle behavior. Even though agglomeration and surface passivation regularly decrease nanospecific properties in environments, decreasing primary particle size which may boost dissolving rate constants and apparent solubility. As metal nanoparticles endure aggregation, corona formation, redox reactions, and surface modifications, their physicochemical characteristics change dynamically in biological and ecological systems.

Routes and Mechanisms of Human Exposure

Human exposure to metal nanoparticles leads to toxicological risk which occurs through multiple routes, including ingestion, inhalation, and dermal contact, each associated with specific toxicological risks.

Understanding these routes is crucial for assessing the toxicological effects of nanoparticles. Breathing of MNPs may cause serious lung problems in animal models, such as fibrosis and inflammation.16 Inhalation studies are the recommended approach for assessing respiratory risks because they enable a more precise evaluation of dose-response interactions.17,18 When inhalation is not practical, alternative techniques for pulmonary exposure include intratracheal instillation and pharyngeal or laryngeal aspiration.18 The exposure routes influencing nanoparticle toxicity are outlined in Figure 2.

The gastrointestinal absorption of nanoparticles may be significant, especially depending on their size. Laboratory studies have indicated that uptake primarily occurs in the intestines, which may adversely affect beneficial bacterial populations.19 The skin serves as a primary route for potential exposure to nanoparticles, especially through products like cosmetics and textiles embedded with MNPs. Studies suggest that nanoparticles may penetrate the skin, particularly through compromised barriers such as cuts or abrasions.19 However, there is limited information regarding the absorption of nanoparticles across the stratum corneum or their potential accumulation in dermal tissues. Three-dimensional skin cell cultures have shown promise in predicting irritation but may overestimate the extent of absorption.17

Additional exposure routes include dermal uptake and injection. The systemic effects of nanoparticles may vary based on their absorption through these routes. For example, dermal exposure has been linked to the potential for nanoparticles to enter the bloodstream, particularly when skin integrity is compromised.17

Furthermore, MNP exposure may result from environmental contamination, with concentrations predicted to increase significantly in the future, heightening concerns about their ecological and health impacts.20 Understanding these various routes of exposure is essential for developing strategies to mitigate the risks associated with metal nanoparticles, especially as their prevalence in consumer products and the environment continues to rise. The toxicity of metal nanoparticles (NPs) is a complex, influenced by various physicochemical parameters such as size, shape, surface area, and chemical composition. Different metallic nanoparticles exhibit distinct toxicity mechanisms and variations in their cytotoxicity levels. Generally, an increase in the atomic number of the element corresponds with an increase in cytotoxicity, due to variations in band-gap energy.21

The toxicity of nanoparticles is the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS).3–6 While ROS play essential roles in cellular signaling and the immune response at low concentrations, excessive ROS may lead to oxidative stress (OS) and cellular damage, including lipid peroxidation and DNA damage.22,23 The mechanisms underlying ROS production depend on several factors, including the nanoparticles’ size and surface characteristics, which influence their interaction with biological systems.21 Exposure to certain metal nanoparticles, such as cobalt, silver, nanoparticles, has been linked to DNA damage through ROS generation, which activates key proteins involved in the DNA damage response.3,6,22 Research indicates that Cu nanoparticles may also affect gene expression in zebrafish embryos, leading to both up-regulation of wound healing genes and down-regulation of genes responsible for phototransduction. These interactions underscore the potential for nanoparticles to accelerate mutagenesis and disrupt normal cellular functions.22,23

The size of nanoparticles significantly impacts their toxicity. Smaller particles tend to have higher dissolution rates and greater biological reactivity, potentially leading to increased cytotoxic effects.21 NPs interaction with biological systems comprises translocation to secondary organs, phagocytosis via immune cells, and release of inflammatory mediators.17,23 These multiple responses reflected by the biological entity compelled the requirement for comprehensive understanding of various NPs impact concerning biological responses. In some cases, NPs may not directly impart cellular damage; however, they significantly alter the surroundings due to which the metabolic activities get severely affected. For example, NPs exposure induces cytokine release from lung cells and results in systemic inflammatory responses imparting toxicity to distant organs together with central nervous system and cardiovascular system.17 Interpretation and evaluation of these indirect risks impact on overall health after the NPs exposure is unpredictable and its imperative to understand these for concluding the overview of MNPs impact on human health.22 An overview of metal and metal-oxide nanoparticles, including their size or formulation characteristics, exposure models, toxicological endpoints, biological effects, and associated biomarkers, is summarized in Table 1.

Metal Nanoparticle Toxicodynamics and Human Health

The metal nanoparticles toxicodynamic tends to be complex as exposure scenario expands and the materials move from the laboratory into different fields such as medicine, agriculture, and consumer products. On the other hand, artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) nowadays offer realistic substitutions to purely experimental methods, predicting how these particles behave in biological systems, including humans. Integrating data-driven approach for toxicological effect insight is critical for protecting human health while allowing safer-by-design nanomaterials. Engineered metal and metal-oxide nanoparticles, typically 1–100 nm in size, may cross epithelial and endothelial barriers, distribute systemically, and trigger inflammation, oxidative stress, fibrosis, neurotoxicity, genotoxicity, and cell death.37,38 Their toxicodynamics differ from bulk counterparts because nanoscale properties such as core size, hydrodynamic diameter, surface area, surface charge, band-gap energy, and the number of oxygen atoms all modulate particle–cell interactions and biotransformation.39 In the dataset discussed, nanoparticles included metallic (Au, Ag), metal-oxide (ZnO, TiO2, CuO, CoFe2O4, Fe3O4, Al2O3, SiO2), and polymeric polystyrene types, with toxicity operationalized as binary cellular responses (“Triggered” vs “No effect”) across 881 experimental observations.40 Biologically, toxic responses encompassed classical endpoints such as loss of viability, ROS generation, mitochondrial damage, and apoptosis, which underlie organ-level outcomes like lung injury, cardiovascular effects, and carcinogenesis in vivo.

Nanotoxicity Prediction via Machine Learning Architectures

Machine Learning works on multivariate toxicodynamic landscape for analyzing toxicity. Machine learning-based prediction is made upon using Kaggle-sourced nanoparticle toxicity data-Random Forests, Decision Trees, XGBoost, Logistic Regression, and k Nearest Neighbors. Physicochemical descriptors (hydrodynamic size, core size, surface area and charge, NOxygen, electronic descriptors e and Ec, exposure period, dose, and particle type) were used to train all models to distinguish between toxic and non-toxic situations. The quantification of performance was carried out with accuracy, recall, F1-scores, class-specific precision, confusion matrices, and using global metrics, including area under the ROC curve (AUC), specificity, and sensitivity. With an overall accuracy of 0.94 and precision/recall/F1 of 0.91/0.98/0.95 for toxic samples and 0.97/0.90/0.93 for non-toxic samples, the Decision Tree model produced just 3 false positives and 13 false negatives in the held-out set.41

With 0.95 accuracy, toxic precision/recall/F1 of 0.94/0.98/0.96, and non-toxic precision/recall/F1 of 0.97/0.93/0.95, Random Forests aggregate several decorrelated Trees lightly improved performance, lowering false negatives to 9 while retaining just 3 false positives. In addition to it, XGBoost, with an accuracy of 0.96, a gradient boosted tree ensemble designed for sparse structured data, offered the best balance between sensitivity and precision. Toxic predictions in this model achieved 0.93 precision, perfect 1.00 recall, and 0.96 F1, whereas non-toxic predictions achieved 1.00 precision, 0.91 recall, and 0.95 F1. In practicality, XGBoost identified all toxic instances, but at the expense of 11 non-toxic samples being incorrectly labeled as toxic, as depicted in Table 2. In contrast, k Nearest Neighbors (0.91) and Logistic Regression (accuracy 0.89) provided interpretable baselines but showed reduced memory for non-toxic samples (0.78 and 0.86, respectively), suggesting a larger false positive load that could exaggerate perceived hazard. XGBoost’s 100% toxic recall shows how ensemble approaches can be tailored toward conservative risk classification while still keeping strong specificity.

Studies of importance bridges statistical predictions to mechanistic toxicology by mentioning which attributes of nanoparticles influence model results. In this research, the rankings derived from the Gini index using Decision Tree, Random Forest, and XGBoost aligned on surface area, dosage, quantity of oxygen atoms, and exposure time as the primary factors influencing toxicity, while also noting the supplementary effects of core size and hydrodynamic size. Dosage was found to be the single most influencing factor according to the impurity reduction line chart. Surface area, exposure time, NOxygen, and size descriptors come next, quantitatively supporting traditional dose–response ideas in a multidimensional descriptive environment. Boxplots, Kernel density plots, and violin plots demonstrated that non-toxic cases generally clustered at slightly elevated NOxygen levels and reduced dose ranges. However, the overlapping distributions highlighted that oxygen content influences, rather than determines, hazard, and should be analyzed in conjunction with exposure conditions and size-dependent biokinetics.

To mitigate bias stemming from dependence on a singular dataset, findings from other peer-reviewed Nano QSAR and machine learning studies have been incorporated. These studies demonstrate that model performance is contingent upon dataset quality, feature selection, and validation techniques. Ensemble approaches, including Random Forest and XGBoost, typically yield excellent accuracy and robustness, but simpler models like logistic regression provide superior interpretability but exhibit diminished performance on complex datasets.

A comparative investigation indicates that Random Forest effectively manages high-dimensional data and mitigates overfitting, whereas XGBoost enhances recall but may elevate false positive rates. Support Vector Machines perform effectively with smaller datasets but require meticulous parameter optimization. Deep learning identifies intricate patterns but suffers from a lack of interpretability and necessitates extensive datasets. Nano QSAR models offer mechanistic insights, although their efficacy is contingent upon the quality of descriptors.

Contradictory results are also documented. Certain studies designate dosage and surface area as critical parameters, whilst others emphasize the influence of surface charge and protein corona effects. Moreover, models frequently exhibit strong performance during internal validation yet have diminished accuracy in external validation. These disparities underscore the necessity for uniform datasets, rigorous validation, and the integration of mechanistic and data-driven methodologies.

These AI-derived perceptions have unswerving consequences for human health risk assessment and safer-by-design nanomaterials. First, the 96% accuracy and perfect toxic recall of XG Boost validate that ML models competent on well-curated experimental datasets may consistently flag harmful nano formulations before widespread in vivo testing, thereby reducing the animal abuse, cost, and time. Second, ML tools must be train on realistic dataset gathered with respect to surface area, exposure time and dosage concentration obtained through real exposure scenarios50 Third reactive oxygen species mediated impairment can be moderated without pacifying useful performance by managing surface chemistry. The Cutting-edge order fulfills the promise of AI-enabled Toxicodynamics in the era of metal nanoparticles. Controllers and inventers may be able to test virtual nano formulations iteratively, enhance benefit-risk profiles, and also modify safety assessments for vulnerable populations by combining these supervised models with high-throughput screening, omics-level mechanistic data, and a dynamic “digital twin” illustration of the human body.

Omics Technology in Nanotoxicology

Omics-based approaches have become a vital tool to understand how metal nanoparticles interact with human biological systems. Genomics and transcriptomics enable the early alterations and changes in gene expression, inflammation, metabolic disruption, and oxidative stress.9 These high-throughput datasets provide the first layer of evidence linking exposure conditions to biological responses, especially when nanoparticles often trigger subtle, multi-pathway responses that cannot be captured through conventional assays. After comparing exposed and unexposed cells or tissues, these platforms help recognize biomarkers that signal risk long before clinical symptoms appear.51

Proteomic and metabolomic approaches provide deeper insight into the consequences of nanoparticle interactions within cells. Proteomics enables the characteristics of metal nanoparticles change protein structure, abundance, and function, with stress-response proteins and enzymes elaborating in detoxification.52 Metabolomics complements this by capturing shifts in small-molecule profiles that reflect energy metabolism, cellular redox status, and membrane integrity. These omics layers generate molecular signatures that reveal the mechanisms behind cytotoxicity, genotoxicity, and organ-specific toxicity. Such multi-omics profiles are valuable for comparing different particle sizes, coatings, and compositions, which may lead to different biological outcomes.53

As omics datasets continue to grow larger and more complex, artificial intelligence and machine learning have become an important tool for interpreting them.54 These methods enable to identify hidden patterns in transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic datasets that are often overlooked by statistical approaches. Machine-learning models have demonstrated an effective toxic concentration, classifying nanoparticles according to its hazardous potential, and identify the molecular features associated with adverse outcomes. By effectively handling from high-dimensional dataset, AI-driven tools reduce uncertainty in toxicity assessment and support data-driven decision-making in both research and regulatory situations.9

The amalgamation of omics technologies and AI will able to generate a prevailing framework for evaluation of human health risks in advance before the mass production and usage of NPs. AI Predictive models trained using the multi-omics datasets can estimate biological retorts to novel nanoparticle formulations without demanding wide-ranging in vivo testing.55 This approach hastens risk assessment while plummeting ethical and logistical burdens associated with animal studies. As omics datasets are accessible, these AI-enabled methods are expected to evolve in future, leading to safer nanoparticle design, more reliable and accurate hazard classification, and provide stronger protection to human health. The synergy and combination between omics and predictive power from machine learning will likely to shape the future generation of nanotoxicology research.56

Use of Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning in Nanotoxicology

The algorithms of Machine learning have made considerable progress in predicting the toxicodynamics of metal nanoparticles, facilitating the combination of physicochemical descriptors with biological endpoints for the assessment of human health risks. By integrating omics characteristics, molecular fingerprints, and physical qualities like size and ζ-potential, Random Forest models achieve AUROC values up to 0.92, demonstrating their superior performance in handling high-dimensional data like as cytotoxicity, carcinogenicity, and macrophage toxicity. Extreme Gradient Boosting employs 79 descriptors, including electronegativity and surface charge, to achieve a balanced accuracy exceeding 85% and F1-scores approximately 0.88, thereby addressing class imbalance in predictions related to genotoxicity and immune cell toxicity (Springer; Elsevier). For the datasets providing chemical properties and charge and with recall rates of more than 90%, Support Vector Machines encourage strong non-linear differentiation for oxidative damage and nanotoxicity.57

Comparative Toxicity: In vitro, in vivo, and Human Studies

Metal nanoparticles exhibit size- and shape-dependent toxicological profiles that span a wide potency range across in vitro, in vivo, and human exposure scenarios, with silver (AgNPs, 10–50 nm spherical) and copper oxide (CuO NPs, 10–50 nm spherical) showing high reactivity evidenced by IC50 values of 5–40 µg/mL and 2–10 µg/mL in mammalian and epithelial cells, respectively, alongside 2–5× elevations in reactive oxygen species (ROS) and lipid peroxidation markers at sublethal doses. Undoubtedly, NPs composition plays a vital role in depicting the toxicity but exact role of shape-size and composition of each metal-based NPs concerning toxicity lacks translational coherence. As the reactive NPs like CuO and AgNPs were reported to show low in vitro IC50, but exhibited ROS driven stress responses revealing that the oxidative stress is the central mechanism of imparting toxicity. In contrast, AuNPs and Fe3O4 NPs revealed low cytotoxicity in the particle size less than <5 nm and reported biocompatibility; however, changes in size and surface alterations led to incompatibility along with enhanced reactivity. Gold (AuNPs, 10–30 nm spherical) and iron oxide (Fe3O4/SPIONs, 5–30 nm spherical) demonstrate lower cytotoxicity, maintaining >80% cell viability up to 50–200 µg/mL, though smaller <5 nm AuNPs or rod shapes increase ROS 1.5–3×; rodent studies confirm organ burdens of a few to tens of µg/g at intravenous doses of 5–20 mg/kg with enzyme elevations typically <2–3× baseline. Zinc oxide (ZnO NPs, 20–50 nm) and titanium dioxide (TiO2 NPs, 20–100 nm) fall intermediate, with keratinocyte IC50s of 10–50 µg/mL and GI cell viability drops at ≥50 µg/mL, linked to 2–5× ROS increases, while ceria (CeO2 NPs, 5–20 nm) often shows protective effects up to 100 µg/mL.73–78 These findings indicate that the data available for concluding any generalized statement about toxicity is inadequate.

In vivo and human data further highlight translation gaps, as rodent oral/inhalation doses of 1–10 mg/kg for AgNPs, ZnO, CuO, and NiO NPs (20–50 nm) induce 2–4× rises in liver/kidney enzymes, lung inflammation scores, and histopathological changes, with persistent particle retention, yet human occupational exposures remain below 0.1 mg/m3 air with minimal systemic effects from sunscreens (eg, ZnO/TiO2 at 2 mg/cm2 yielding trace blood Zn/Ti) or clinical SPIONs at 3–7 mg/kg intravenous (Maddaleno, 2025; Mo, 2024). Selenium and ceria NPs having 20–100nm reported to reduce 30–70% tumor volumes in rodents at 0.5–2 mg/kg with enzymes <2× normal. Study also reported to resist daily doses ranging from tens to hundreds of µg in humans. Whereas cobalt NPs 10–40nm induced cardiopulmonary concerns at 1–4 mg/kg which underscore the need for physicochemical tuning to balance therapeutic utility against organ-specific risks.79–83 These studies highlighted the role of ion release and dissolution kinetics in imparting toxicity concluding that the ion mediated toxicity and context-dependent redox activity can be either defensive or detrimental. Furthermore, the study explored how leaf extract of Datura stramonium may help form Co3O4 and NiO nanoparticles, along with Co3O4@NiO core-shell nanostructures in different ratios. Zinc oxide (ZnO) nanoparticles show therapeutic potential with applications ranging from drug delivery to imaging to anticancer properties. After entering the body, they are absorbed mostly as Zn2⁺ ions and partly as particles, and higher zinc levels are found in the liver, kidneys, lungs, and spleen after exposure (liver, kidney, lung, spleen accumulation). The liver knobs most of its metabolic processes, and they are removed mostly through expressions and partly through urine. At higher doses, ZnO nanoparticles may cause liver injury across various exposure routes (oral, intravenous, intraperitoneal, intratracheal) and may also affect the lungs and kidneys. Their toxicity is linked to the buildup of reactive oxygen species generated by excess Zn2⁺ release and by the semiconductor properties of the particles. Coating ZnO nanoparticles with silica reduces toxicity by limiting ion release and oxidative stress. With proper surface alteration, ZnO nanoparticles are being explored for cancer treatment, bioimaging and targeted drug delivery, showing their therapeutic value with safer design strategies. Risk Assessment and Regulatory Perspectives (GA)

Current risk assessment for metal nanoparticles is on traditional paradigms that often underestimate dynamic toxicokinetics, such as AgNP dissolution rates increasing 2–5× for particles <20 nm or CuO NP IC50 values of 2–10 µg/mL linked to 3–5× ROS elevation in lung epithelia.84 AI-driven Nano-QSAR models now integrate >79 descriptors (size, ζ-potential, electronegativity) to predict multi-endpoint toxicity with balanced accuracies >85% and F1-scores of 0.88, supporting read-across for untested variants and reducing animal use by 70–90% in regulatory screening.85,86

Conclusions

Metal nanoparticles (MNPs) possess tunable physicochemical properties that could transform biomedicine. Nonetheless, as production escalates, their toxicodynamics—comprising organ-specific accumulation, blood–brain barrier penetration, ROS-mediated oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and genotoxicity pose significant threats to human health. Consequently, there is an imperative necessity to create novel, robust instruments for assessing and comprehending the mechanisms underlying the toxicity of metal-based nanoparticles. Machine learning models, particularly XGBoost, offer robust analytical toxicology frameworks, attaining 96% accuracy and flawless hazardous recall by utilizing descriptors such as exposure time, dosage, surface area, and NOxygen. Integrating AI/ML techniques with omics data, high-throughput screening, and physiologically based pharmacokinetic models facilitates safer-by-design approaches, such as surface coatings to mitigate risk, diminishes animal testing, and supports regulatory virtual evaluations. Future ingenuities require to provide curated datasets, authenticate digital twins for chronic revelations, and align evaluations with global standards, while understanding toxicodynamics for the safe application of MNPs.
