Coincident large left atrial myxoma and severe three-vessel coronary artery disease: single-stage resection with CABG: a case report
Authors: Angie Hawat, Ahmad Al-Bitar, Mohammad Alaa Aldakak, Ahmad Badran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiac myxoma, the most common primary cardiac tumor, can present with symptoms that overlap with coronary artery disease (CAD), creating a diagnostic challenge, particularly in older patients with cardiovascular risk factors.Case presentationWe report a case of a 67-year-old Arab woman with long-standing hypertension and type 2 diabetes mellitus, who presented with typical exertional angina. Laboratory findings were notable for mild anemia and significantly elevated inflammatory markers, which had normalized by the time of surgery. Background

Primary cardiac neoplasms are rare (< 0.03% in autopsy series), and cardiac myxoma (CM) is the most frequent benign primary cardiac tumor [1, 2]. CM shows a mid-life predilection with female preponderance, and ~ 85% arise in the left atrium—typically from the interatrial septum near the fossa ovalis [1]. Clinically, CM often presents along a triad of intracardiac obstruction, systemic embolization, and constitutional symptoms, but manifestations vary widely with tumor size, location, and mobility, complicating timely diagnosis [1, 2]. Diagnostic complexity is magnified when CM coexists with major cardiovascular disease, creating overlapping presentations and therapeutic tradeoffs, as underscored by published combined myxoma–coronary artery disease (CAD) reports [3]. Coexistent CAD is clinically meaningful: a meta-analysis estimated a pooled CAD prevalence of ~ 20.7% among adults with CM, and single-center cohorts that performed routine angiography in older patients reported higher rates, reinforcing the importance of preoperative coronary assessment [4, 5]. This coexistence is particularly prominent in older patients and those with traditional cardiovascular risk factors such as hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia. Because chest pain and dyspnea are shared features of CAD and CM-related physiology, many authors advocate routine preoperative coronary angiography in patients > 40 years or with atherosclerotic risk factors to detect critical comorbidity and guide operative strategy [4, 5]. When both pathologies are present, a single-stage operation—complete tumor excision with coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG)—offers definitive management of dual life-threatening processes and is supported by surgical experience [6].

Case presentation

A 67-year-old Arab woman with a past medical history of long-standing hypertension and type 2 diabetes mellitus presented with recurrent exertional chest pain of typical anginal characteristics. She also reported a remote history of treated typhoid fever associated with weight loss and intermittent fever. On admission, her electrocardiogram revealed sinus rhythm with a normal axis and a heart rate of 72 beats per minute. It demonstrated widespread ST-segment depression in the lateral and inferior leads, accompanied by reciprocal ST-segment elevation in lead V1. Serial troponin measurements were negative, ruling out acute myocardial infarction. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed a large left atrial mass measuring approximately 4 × 3 cm, consistent with a probable atrial myxoma, in addition to preserved left ventricular systolic function with regional wall motion abnormalities (Figs. 1, 2). Coronary angiography confirmed severe three-vessel coronary artery disease, including critical lesions of the left anterior descending artery, obtuse marginal branch, and right coronary artery. No abnormal tumor neovascularization or feeding vessels from the epicardial coronaries were observed on angiography.

Laboratory investigations revealed mild normocytic anemia, with hemoglobin levels ranging from 10.8 to 11 g/dL and a hematocrit of 33%. Markers of inflammation were significantly elevated, with ESR reaching 55 mm/h and CRP levels ranging from 31 to 138 mg/L. These inflammatory markers were noted to have normalized on repeat testing 2 months later, during the preoperative workup for surgery. Following the initial diagnosis, the patient was scheduled for surgery. However, the procedure was deferred by 2 months to allow for the management of her newly diagnosed urinary tract infection and for the normalization of her inflammatory markers, optimizing her condition for surgery. During this interval, the patient remained under close clinical surveillance. A repeat transthoracic echocardiogram performed just prior to surgery confirmed the stability of the left atrial mass, with no significant change in its size (still measuring approximately 4 × 3 cm) or morphology. Importantly, the patient reported no new symptoms, and there were no clinical events suggestive of systemic embolization, such as transient ischemic attacks, cerebrovascular accidents, or peripheral arterial emboli, during this waiting period. Urinalysis demonstrated turbid urine with marked pyuria (100–110 WBCs/HPF) and microscopic hematuria (6–8 RBCs/HPF), suggestive of a urinary tract infection (UTI). This infection was treated with a course of antibiotics, and a repeat urinalysis prior to surgery confirmed complete resolution. Glycemic control was poor, as reflected by fasting blood glucose between 203 and 293 mg/dL and HbA1c up to 7.7%. Dyslipidemia was also evident, with triglycerides of 289 mg/dL and reduced HDL cholesterol of 25 mg/dL. Renal and hepatic profiles showed a borderline elevation of serum creatinine at 1.23 mg/dL and increased AST at 71 U/L, while electrolytes revealed mild hypokalemia at 3.35 mmol/L. Serological testing was positive for Widal (Typhi O 1:320), whereas Brucella serology was negative.

The patient underwent surgery via median sternotomy. Total cardiopulmonary bypass time was 100 min, with an aortic cross-clamp time of 55 min. Myocardial protection was achieved with antegrade cold blood cardioplegia. Intraoperative transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) was used to confirm the preoperative findings, guiding the surgical approach. The sequence of the procedure began with the complete excision of the atrial mass, performed en bloc along with a cuff of the interatrial septum to ensure tumor-free margins. The resulting septal defect was repaired using a Dacron patch (Fig. 3). Following the tumor excision, coronary artery bypass grafting was performed. The left internal mammary artery was anastomosed to the left anterior descending artery. In addition, a saphenous vein graft was placed to the obtuse marginal branch, and another saphenous vein graft was constructed to the right coronary artery.

Intraoperative photograph obtained during open-heart surgery via median sternotomy, showing the atrial myxoma being excised en bloc with part of the interatrial septum. The mass is marked with an asterisk (*)

Gross examination of the resected specimen revealed a smooth, lobulated, gelatinous mass measuring 4 × 3 × 2 cm. A photograph of the gross surgical specimen with a standard ruler confirmed the tumor dimensions of 4 × 3 × 2 cm (Fig. 4). Histopathological analysis (H&E stain; magnification × 100) confirmed the diagnosis of cardiac myxoma with no evidence of malignancy, showing characteristic polygonal cells embedded in a myxoid stroma (Fig. 5). The postoperative course was uneventful. The patient was followed up at 1 month and again at 10 months post-surgery. She remained asymptomatic, with excellent clinical status and no evidence of tumor recurrence on follow-up echocardiograms.

Discussion

CM is the most common benign primary cardiac neoplasm in adults, most often arising in the left atrium and producing symptoms through obstruction, embolization, or systemic inflammatory effects [1, 2]. In older or high-risk patients, CM may coexist with atherosclerotic CAD, yielding overlapping presentations that require careful mechanistic adjudication [4, 5]. The burden of coexistent CAD in CM is nontrivial: a meta-analysis estimated a pooled prevalence of ~ 20.7%, and series employing routine angiography in older cohorts reported higher frequencies, justifying liberal preoperative coronary assessment [4, 5]. Accordingly, in patients > 40 years, with conventional risk factors, or with angina, coronary angiography provides decisive information that often converts a planned isolated tumor resection into a combined procedure [4, 5].

Three mechanisms should be considered when angina accompanies CM [1, 2]. First, coronary embolization of friable tumor fragments can precipitate acute coronary syndromes and may present as myocardial infarction with non-obstructive coronary arteries (MINOCA), as contemporary case descriptions demonstrate [7, 8]. Second, highly vascular myxomas may recruit feeding vessels from epicardial coronaries, generating a coronary "steal" that produces exertional ischemia and resolves after tumor resection with ligation of feeders; angiographic and surgical observations document anomalous supply from circumflex or right coronary branches [9, 10]. Third—and most common in older patients with risk factors—angina reflects coincidental multivessel atherosclerotic CAD requiring revascularization in addition to tumor removal, as repeatedly illustrated in case literature [3, 11]. In our patient, the absence of angiographic evidence for tumor neovascularization or embolic phenomena, coupled with her significant atherosclerotic risk factors and the presence of severe fixed lesions on angiography, strongly pointed to the third mechanism: coincidental atherosclerotic CAD as the primary driver of her anginal symptoms.

Systemic inflammatory and hematologic abnormalities frequently accompany CM and contextualize laboratory findings [1, 2]. Elevated acute-phase reactants and mild normocytic anemia are attributed to tumor-driven cytokine release—particularly interleukin-6—which can mimic vasculitides or infective endocarditis and delay diagnosis [12–14]. Recognizing this paraneoplastic inflammatory milieu helps interpret results and supports the urgency of definitive therapy [12, 13]. In this case, the initial presentation with markedly elevated inflammatory markers and pyuria raised concern for an active infection. However, these findings were ultimately attributed to a combination of the myxoma's paraneoplastic effect and a concurrent, treated UTI. The decision to proceed with surgery was made only after confirming the resolution of the UTI and the normalization of inflammatory markers, thereby minimizing the risks associated with operating on an infected patient. The negative troponin levels allowed for a non-emergent, comprehensive preoperative assessment.

Echocardiography anchors the diagnostic pathway. Transthoracic echocardiography generally suffices to detect a mobile, lobulated atrial mass, define attachment, and estimate embolic/obstructive risk, while transesophageal echocardiography refines morphology when needed [1, 2]. Intraoperative TEE, as used in our case, is invaluable for confirming the diagnosis, assessing for any other cardiac pathologies, guiding de-airing, and evaluating the immediate result of the surgical repair. Beyond adjudicating chest pain, preoperative coronary angiography informs operative planning and may delineate tumor neovascularization, typically from circumflex or right coronary branches, enabling targeted intraoperative ligation where present [10, 15]. Attention to anomalous feeders is prudent because persistent or iatrogenic coronary-cavitary fistulas have been reported when the supply is not addressed [16].

Once identified, large and mobile left-atrial myxomas are treated as surgical urgencies to preempt unpredictable embolic stroke or intermittent valvular obstruction [1, 2]. In the presence of severe multivessel CAD, a staged strategy poses hazards in either sequence: performing CABG first leaves a friable intracardiac tumor with embolic potential during or after revascularization, whereas resecting the tumor first exposes a patient with critical CAD to major operative stress without revascularization [3, 11]. Consequently, a single-stage operation that combines complete tumor excision with surgical revascularization offers a coherent solution in appropriately selected patients [6, 17, 18]. In our patient, we elected to perform the tumor excision first. This approach allowed for an unobstructed view and optimal conditions for the complete resection of the myxoma before subjecting the heart to the additional manipulation and cardioplegia required for grafting.

Technical principles seek to minimize recurrence and complications. Standard practice entails en bloc excision of the mass with a cuff of interatrial septum at the stalk to ensure negative margins, followed by patch repair of the septal defect when required—an approach associated with low recurrence in sporadic CM [1, 2]. In combined procedures, using the left internal mammary artery to the left anterior descending artery with supplementary saphenous vein grafts to additional targets represents a conventional and durable revascularization strategy consistent with published experience [3, 17]. The optimal sequence of steps (grafting before versus after tumor excision) remains individualized according to coronary anatomy, tumor morphology, and myocardial protection needs, with contemporary reviews supporting tailoring to operative goals [18]. The excellent clinical and echocardiographic outcomes at the 10-month follow-up underscore the success of this combined approach in our patient.

In aggregate, several practice points emerge. Clinicians evaluating older patients with intracardiac masses and typical angina should explicitly consider embolic events and tumor-related steal while recognizing that coincidental atherosclerotic CAD is often the dominant driver when angiography demonstrates fixed, flow-limiting lesions [7–11]. Routine preoperative coronary angiography in patients with risk factors or age > 40 years is justified by the documented prevalence of coexistent CAD and its impact on operative planning [4, 5]. Furthermore, a thorough preoperative evaluation to identify and treat concurrent infections or other sources of inflammation is crucial for optimal surgical timing and patient safety. When significant CAD is present, simultaneous tumor excision and CABG provide definitive management of both life-threatening processes with acceptable safety in reports spanning early surgical series and modern case experience [6, 17, 18]. Collectively, contemporary evidence and long-standing surgical practice converge on a paradigm that prioritizes urgent resection of left-atrial myxoma while integrating comprehensive coronary revascularization when indicated [1, 2, 4–6, 17, 18].

Conclusion

Coexistent left atrial myxoma and severe atherosclerotic CAD, though uncommon, poses distinct diagnostic and therapeutic complexities. Thorough preoperative evaluation—particularly routine coronary angiography in older or high-risk patients—is pivotal to adjudicate angina mechanisms and define management. In this case, angiography established coincidental multivessel atherosclerosis as the cause of the patient's angina, and a single-stage operation combining complete myxoma excision with CABG provided definitive therapy. The decision to proceed with surgery was carefully timed after the resolution of a concurrent infection and the normalization of inflammatory markers. This strategy minimizes the hazards of staged procedures and remains the safest, most effective approach when significant CAD accompanies cardiac myxoma.
