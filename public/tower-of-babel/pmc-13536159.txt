Aligning awareness, systems and policy to increase equitable access to genomically driven cancer care
Authors: Kelly Kohut, Joshua Asaad Kuti, Sandra Balladares, Nicola Reents, Raza Sayyed, Benedito Mauro Rossi, Janina Balazar-Palacios, Bami Adenipekun, Nicolò Matteo Luca Battisti, Csaba László Dégi, Mev Dominguez-Valentin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGenomic testing has the potential to transform cancer care across the patient pathway. However, its benefits remain unevenly realised across populations and health systems. Precision oncology is characterised by a strong promissory discourse, with expectations of improved outcomes and cost-effectiveness, yet real-world implementation remains variable and context dependent. This review examines how patient and public awareness interacts with, and is constrained by, structural, organisational and political-economic factors that shape equitable access to genomically driven cancer care across five themes: (1) the power of patient and public advocacy; (2) learning from the patient perspective; (3) culturally responsive communication; (4) structural and personal barriers and facilitators and (5) political economy of health. Examples are mapped across global regions to highlight how health system, structural and societal factors continue to limit the universal realisation of genomic medicine’s benefits. We present four recommendations to strengthen the translation of awareness into equitable access and clinical impact: (1) expand and adequately power genomic studies in underserved populations; (2) improve risk communication and decision-making across the cancer pathway; (3) equitable validation and interpretation of emerging genomic technologies and (4) generate real-world evidence on access, uptake and outcomes of genome-matched therapies. Embedding awareness, trust, access and equity in future initiatives is imperative to realise the promise of precision medicine for all patients.Keywords: Biomarkers, Cancer screening, Ethnic and Racial Minorities, Genetic markers, Health Services Research

Introduction

Genomic profiling has the potential to transform cancer care across the patient pathway, but its benefits remain unevenly realised across populations and health systems.1 Precision oncology is underpinned by strong claims of improved outcomes and cost-effectiveness, yet implementation remains variable and contingent on access, workforce capacity, affordability and trust.2 In this review, genomic profiling is used as an umbrella term encompassing germline genetic testing, somatic or tumour genomic testing, liquid biopsy and circulating tumour DNA (ctDNA) assays and companion diagnostics linked to targeted therapies. These modalities differ in purpose, timing and clinical implications and are referred to explicitly where relevant.

Genomic profiling may improve diagnostic accuracy and identify pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants, structural rearrangements or biomarkers that inform tailored treatment, sometimes with a more favourable safety profile compared with chemotherapy.3 Examples of targeted therapies include EGFR inhibitors for lung cancer,4 PARP inhibitors for homologous recombination-deficient tumours including breast5 and ovarian cancers6 and immunotherapy for tumours exhibiting high mutational burden including colorectal cancer7 and melanoma.8 Serial liquid biopsies measuring ctDNA may also support longitudinal monitoring of tumour evolution, progression and treatment resistance.9

Germline genetic testing, typically performed on blood-derived or saliva-derived DNA, may be undertaken alongside or after somatic tumour testing.10,11 Identifying germline pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants is clinically important because they may be inherited, confer risk of future primary malignancy and inform cascade testing for at-risk relatives. On average, approximately three at-risk relatives are tested per proband, with the familial variant identified in 1.5.12 Additionally, comprehensive genomic screening in low- and lower-middle-income countries (LLMICs) can identify novel mutations that can lead to individualised genomic testing for identified populations.13,14 Germline testing can therefore support earlier risk detection, surveillance, individualisation of genomic tests for certain populations and risk-reducing interventions including lifestyle modifications. Cost per quality-adjusted life-year gained falls well within accepted cost-effectiveness thresholds in many settings.15–18

Communication about genomic profiling is often complex and emotionally sensitive.19 Historically, genetic counselling was delivered by specialist genetics professionals, although availability has varied across regions and remains limited in some settings. Declining sequencing costs and the growing role of genomics in time-sensitive treatment decisions have accelerated the mainstreaming of genomic testing.20,21 As a result, non-genetics clinicians increasingly handle consent, ordering and results disclosure in oncology clinics.22–24 Although genomics education is increasingly recommended within oncology training programmes,25 many clinicians report feeling underprepared and constrained by limited time, confidence and resources to integrate genomic profiling into routine practice which can critically shape the offer to patients.26,27

Genomic research remains limited in underserved populations, including LLMICs, non-White European ancestry populations, racially, ethnically and geographically minoritised groups, children, older adults and others facing barriers related to health-system inequity, low health literacy, cultural factors or socioeconomic deprivation.1,28 Interpretation of variant pathogenicity is also more difficult when populations are under-represented in sequencing databases, yielding higher rates of variants of uncertain significance (VUS) and restricting clinical application of findings.29 These disparities persist even in well-resourced health systems and contribute to poorer cancer outcomes, including survival.2

Patient and public awareness is a key determinant of uptake of health interventions and is increasingly recognised as central to the effective delivery of precision oncology.30 However, awareness alone is insufficient: its impact depends on system capacity, affordability, trust and workforce readiness.28 This is particularly important given the global burden of cancer morbidity and mortality and the need to ensure that genomically driven cancer care benefits extend beyond well-resourced settings.31 Improving understanding of the availability and value of genomic profiling may help support more appropriate use of effective, targeted and reduce avoidable disparities.32

This review synthesises evidence from researchers and patient advocates across diverse healthcare systems. Patient and public involvement (PPI) partners (n=2) are co-authors, bringing lived experience to enhance the relevance of this review.33,34 They contributed to topic prioritisation, interpretation of findings, drafting and refinement of the manuscript. Their input shaped the framing of key themes, particularly around patient experience, trust and communication. Although the PPI partners were drawn from different regions and backgrounds, their perspectives cannot represent all settings or minoritised communities. No dedicated funding was available, a common barrier for time-intensive projects,35,36 though co-authorship offered non-financial recognition.35 National guidance supports remuneration for PPI contributions, including meetings and reviews37 and this should be costed in future funding applications.

A narrative review methodology was chosen to enable flexible synthesis of multidisciplinary evidence38 across clinical, behavioural and policy domains. We conducted a non-systematic search of PubMed, Scopus and Google Scholar using combinations of terms relating to genomic medicine, cancer, public awareness, patient engagement, equity and health inequalities (table 1). The search covered January 2019 to February 2026 to capture contemporary evidence in a rapidly evolving field. Records were screened for relevance based on conceptual contribution, recency and diversity of geographic and health-system context. Selection was iterative and discussion based, with multiple authors contributing to source identification. Additional grey literature was identified through targeted searching and PPI-informed recommendations to promote inclusion of under-represented contexts and emerging initiatives. Grey literature, project outputs and unpublished materials were included only where they added context not available in peer-reviewed sources. As a narrative review, this approach prioritised conceptual depth and breadth over exhaustive inclusion.38

Aim

This review examines how patient and public awareness interacts with structural, cultural and system-level factors to shape equitable access to genomically driven cancer care.

Summary of key findings from the narrative review

Advanced genomic technologies enable characterisation of the cancer-specific genomic signatures to inform diagnosis, refine prognostication, select targeted therapies, monitor treatment response and detect resistance or tumour evolution.39 Genomic profiling is increasingly integrated into cancer care pathways for risk stratification, including hereditary cancer assessment and matched-trial enrolment, although its screening role remains most established in high-risk settings such as hereditary breast and ovarian cancer, Lynch syndrome or Li-Fraumeni syndrome and for age-based or exposure-based risk groups, such as colorectal cancer risk after age 50 years or lung cancer risk in people with a smoking history.40–42 Figure 1 illustrates the multiple time points at which a patient with cancer may be offered genomic profiling to guide care. This pathway is necessarily idealised; in practice, many patients do not reach every step because of the inequities described in this review, especially in LLMIC settings.

Despite scientific advances, substantial inequalities persist in access to genomically driven cancer care worldwide.43 Socioeconomic, geographic and demographic factors continue to shape who benefits from precision oncology, reinforcing existing disparities.44 This review identifies five overarching themes that illustrate these inequities, with examples mapped across global regions to show how health-system, structural and societal factors limit the universal realisation of the genomic medicine’s benefits. The five themes are explored in detail below.

Theme 1: the power of patient and public advocacy

Patient and public awareness does not operate independently; rather, it interacts with health-system readiness, service availability, reimbursement mechanisms, workforce capacity and trust in healthcare institutions to determine whether patients ultimately access genomically informed care.45 Advocacy emerged in this review as a central determinant of engagement with care.46 However, its impact depends on multiple factors and there is a lack of universal coverage and standardised referral pathways that enable equitable access to genomic services.45 Although some examples derive from broader cancer awareness initiatives rather than genomics-specific programmes, the same mechanisms influence genomics-specific outcomes by increasing awareness of testing opportunities, improving referral to genetics services, encouraging participation in genomic research and supporting access to genomically informed treatment pathways. The impact of awareness varies across cancer types and sociocultural contexts.47,48 Breast cancer has benefited from sustained advocacy and media visibility, whereas oral cancer, despite rising incidence and modifiable risk factors, has seen more limited success from public campaigns.49 Even where education is effective, behaviour change among vulnerable or high-risk groups often depends on trust and on broader social determinants of health.49,50 In LLMICs, barriers extend beyond cost to include limited social support, competing life priorities and structural weakness in healthcare delivery.51

In a pilot study in Pakistan, individuals aware of hereditary colorectal cancer risk still avoided screening colonoscopy because of financial burden, social circumstances and concern that a diagnosis would affect work and livelihood (Sayyed, Kohut, Ayub et al, unpublished project, presented at European Hereditary Tumour Group Meeting, Vilnius, Lithuania 2023). This highlights the need for advocacy strategies that are culturally and socially grounded rather than simply informational. Messages delivered by trusted community leaders, peers, relatable patient voices or public influencers may resonate more strongly than conventional health-system communication.52

Digital platforms also offer opportunities to extend reach.48 Targeted social media campaigns, such as a visually engaging lung cancer awareness initiative have demonstrated high engagement, improved knowledge and the ability to reach individuals previously unaware of their risk.53 However, such strategies must account for digital literacy and access, or they risk reinforcing existing inequities.54 Patient-led communication shares important features with genetic counselling, where personalised dialogue helps translate complex genomic risk into meaningful, actionable information.21

Patient and public advocacy can contribute to policy change, shape guideline development and help harmonise standards of care while remaining sensitive to national context.55 Patient organisations and charities may also provide financial support, navigation assistance and peer-led education to help individuals understand surveillance, risk-reducing options and the psychosocial implications of dealing with cancer and inherited cancer risk.55 Peer advocates may play a critical role in addressing fear, mistrust and emotional barriers that may otherwise deter patients from pursuing genomic profiling, trial participation or targeted therapies.56,57 Through these functions, advocacy can empower communities, strengthen health literacy, build trust and support more equitable uptake of genomically driven cancer care, especially for underserved communities.58

Theme 2: shared decision-making: learning from the patient perspective

This theme focuses on clinical encounters and their impact on the patient experience. After a cancer diagnosis, patients are often asked to absorb information spanning prognosis, treatment, lifestyle and emotional support.59 Their understanding may be shaped in part by their learning style and by clinicians’ communication style.60 Eliciting patient preferences and incorporating them into shared decision-making involves a collaborative process with clinicians, jointly considering evidence, uncertainty and patient values.61 Some patients may value genomic testing even when immediate therapeutic implications are limited because findings may inform future treatment opportunities, surveillance strategies or cascade testing for relatives. In practice, however, patients may find it difficult to express what matters most to them62 and clinicians may lack training in shared decision-making techniques.63 This can lead to medical paternalism or reliance on unvalidated tools or non-scientific evidence.64 ‘Expert’ consensus decisions agreed in multidisciplinary team (MDT) meetings can also exclude the individual patient perspective and acceptance of MDT recommendations may be shaped by asymmetries in knowledge and power.65 A systematic review of 25 studies found that clearly defined roles within breast cancer MDTs and transparent processes are central to effective shared decision-making, particularly when supported by infrastructure that integrates clinical, cost and patient-reported outcome considerations.66

Precision oncology offers meaningful potential to improve outcomes,67 but expectations must be managed carefully. Unrealistic hopes may lead to disappointment when patients are ineligible for targeted therapies or clinical trials.60 In a multi-institutional Australian survey of 293 patients with advanced cancer, 69% overestimated the treatment impact of genomic profiling, although 89% experienced no decisional regret about the testing performed.68 Similarly, a US single-centre telephone survey of biomarker testing in non-small-cell lung cancer (207/670 patients; 31% response rate) found substantial misunderstandings, including confusion between germline and somatic variants.69 These findings suggest that deeper research is needed, particularly in underserved groups, to clarify the origins and consequences of knowledge gaps and to identify communication strategies to support informed choices.

Patient advisory structures are increasingly used to reduce disparities in genomic care. For example, an American study convened English-speaking and Spanish-speaking patient advisory committees.70 Although participants reported mostly positive experiences, many were unsure whether their feedback influenced decision-making.70 This highlights the importance of transparent communication about the purpose of PPI, the scope of influence and how input will be used.

Direct-to-consumer genomic profiling adds further complexity to precision oncology.71 Many patients assume these tests are equivalent to clinical-grade diagnostics, despite their more limited variant coverage, lower analytic sensitivity and lack of pretest counselling, clinical interpretation or follow-up support.72 As a result, patients may arrive in oncology clinics with incomplete or misinterpreted results, creating additional communication and emotional challenges for clinicians.73 Confusion is heightened when results include VUS, which may arise from both tumour and germline testing and can be difficult to interpret or explain. Misinterpretation of VUS findings can generate anxiety or prompt inappropriate actions,74 such as unnecessary surveillance, risk-reducing surgery and cascade testing of relatives. Although VUS classifications may change as new evidence emerges, patients and clinicians may not always be aware of the need for ongoing re-evaluation.75

A stronger understanding of the probabilistic nature of genomic profiling and the limits of uncertainty tolerance may help patients and providers communicate more effectively.76 When patients better understand genomic concepts, they are better able to articulate their values and weigh risks, benefits and trade-offs.77 Strengthening genomic literacy therefore supports shared decision-making as a collaborative process in which clinical expertise and patient priorities are aligned to reach decisions that are both evidence-based and personally meaningful.62,78 Improving this requires competency-based curricula, tailored learning resources, public-facing educational materials and community workshops that simplify complex genomic information.79 Embedding genomics into routine practice may also be supported by decision support interventions, electronic health record integration, cross-disciplinary collaboration and culturally responsive codesigned pathways.80,81

Importantly, barriers to informed shared decision-making are documented even in settings where genomic profiling is formally available. In a survey of 566 HR+/HER2− breast cancer patients eligible for testing across five European countries, only 21.6% had been informed of their eligibility and 84.6% reported insufficient information to make a meaningful decision.82 This patient-reported information deficit highlights that closing this gap requires proactive, patient-centred communication embedded within clinical pathways, not simply the structural availability of a test.

Theme 3: culturally responsive communication

Whereas, theme 2 focuses on communication within individual clinical encounters and shared decision-making, this theme examines community-level communication, genomic literacy and public engagement. A major and often under-recognised barrier to genomically informed cancer care is limited genomic literacy among patients and, in some settings, healthcare professionals.77 Genomic literacy includes understanding the purpose, benefits, limitations, and implications of genomic profiling.83 Without this foundation, patients may struggle to make informed decisions about treatment and misunderstanding may foster confusion, anxiety, distrust, fear of discrimination, or the belief that genomic results are deterministic rather than probabilistic.84 These challenges emphasise that genomic literacy is not solely an individual responsibility but is influenced by how information is communicated and supported within healthcare systems.85–87

Culturally responsive communication is a critical mechanism through which awareness can be translated into meaningful engagement with genomic cancer care. This includes acknowledging where there are unfair limitations and transparently communicating this to patients. An example is artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted interpretation, which has been increasingly integrated into genomics research. However, model performance is limited by training on datasets that under-represent non-White populations. This creates predictions that are less accurate or less generalisable across diverse groups, potentially amplifying existing health disparities if diversity is not intentionally embedded during development and validation.

Culturally responsive communication refers to communication that is linguistically accessible, culturally safe and adapted to local context and values.88 It includes language concordance, plain-language explanations and recognition of the emotional and historical factors that shape trust.88 It also requires recognition that no single communication model will be appropriate across settings or communities. Qualitative studies in breast cancer suggest that some patients feel processed rather than supported, with trust undermined by rushed consultations, poorly explained results and insufficient attention to emotional concerns.87 Other communities, including Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples, remain under-represented in genomic research and the oncology workforce.89 Meaningful community engagement, co-designed communication strategies and culturally responsive genetic counselling are therefore essential if precision medicine is to strengthen trust and access rather than reproduce existing inequities.

Theme 4: structural and personal barriers and facilitators

Structural and personal barriers determine whether awareness translates into access, acting as key mediators between knowledge and uptake.90 Structural barriers include service availability, referral systems, geography, workforce constraints and reimbursement; personal barriers include transport, time, financial status, insurance availability, competing demands, beliefs and fear.91 Implementation of genomic profiling in oncology varies widely across settings. Countries with publicly funded, centralised health systems may find wider adoption easier, as illustrated by the IMPRESS-Norway trial, which reported encouraging disease-control outcomes in patients with advanced cancer receiving targeted therapies.92 At a regional level, the European Cancer Organisation’s Action Report (2024) highlighted persistent fragmentation and called for coordinated expert centres, aligned reimbursement pathways and improved information for clinicians and patients.93 In the USA, coordinated, multimodal community engagement initiatives in cancer genome sequencing research have clarified roles and strengthened partnerships throughout the research lifecycle.94 However, ethical questions persist regarding the hybrid clinical-research space occupied by genomic data, underscoring the need for inclusive PPI to guide practice.95 Broader policy analyses argue that the regulatory and translational challenges seen in oncology will extend to other disease domains as genomic profiling becomes increasingly embedded in healthcare.96

In Latin America, barriers to genomic tumour profiling are tangible and well documented.97 Resources and health facilities are unevenly distributed within and between countries and rural versus urban locations, cancer genetic counselling services remain scarce in many settings, and regulatory, workforce and funding gaps limit systematic screening for hereditary cancer.97 A multicentre real-world study of Lynch syndrome screening in 1684 colorectal cancer cases from 24 registries or centres in nine countries highlighted these disparities, showing variable uptake of screening and counselling, incomplete referral of patients with suspected Lynch syndrome to genetic services and persistent gaps between test availability and patient access.98 Nevertheless, universal tumour screening has improved identification of Lynch syndrome compared with older strategies.98 Complementing this, an online survey of genetic service providers across 13 Latin American countries found that 71% of 120 respondents reported access to multigene panels, locally or via external laboratories, but first evaluations were most often performed by medical geneticists (58%) with only 21% by genetic counsellors, and financing remained heterogeneous with around 80% reporting national funding and substantial out-of-pocket costs for testing in specific countries (Bazalar et al, unpublished study in Peru). Together, these data show both the need and the opportunity: existing registries, pilot programmes and growing professional engagement provide scalable platforms, but targeted investment in distributed reference laboratories, accredited counselling training, standardised national guidelines and reimbursement mechanisms is required to translate diagnostic capacity into equitable access and clinical benefit.99

Theme 5: political economy of health

Political economy of health refers to how power, financing, regulation and institutional priorities shape who gains access to genomically driven cancer care. Health system architecture—how care is financed, how technologies are appraised and how coverage decisions are made—is not a neutral backdrop to precision oncology but an active determinant of access.100 Even where clinical evidence is compelling and guideline recommendations are clear, political economy factors such as budget fragmentation, commercial pricing strategies, regulatory delays and insufficient patient voice in health technology assessment can systematically narrow access to genomically guided cancer care.82,100,101 A 2023 editorial in The Lancet highlighted persistent bottlenecks in the UK, including slow approval processes, the administrative burden and cost of clinical trials and the absence of a coordinated national strategy to implement innovation equitably.102 Similar challenges are reflected globally. A 2020 review of access to cancer medicines underscored stark disparities between high-income and LLMICs, driven by prohibitive drug costs, weak health systems, regulatory delays and inadequate data management and supply-chain infrastructure.103 In response, the Union for International Cancer Control established the Access to Oncology Medicines Coalition to improve affordability of WHO essential cancer medicines in LLMICs.104 Estimates suggest that only 9%–54% of LLMICs had universal availability of essential cancer drugs and 13%–68% reported substantial risk for catastrophic expenditure, represented by 40% of household income.105

Regional collaborations also offer promise. In Latin America, patient advocates and precision oncology specialists from 10 countries issued a call to action to expand genomically driven cancer care.106 Large national genomics programmes illustrate how strategic policy investment can support equity. The US All of Us Research Programme has generated a dataset with over 80% of participants from historically underserved groups, laying a foundation for more inclusive participation in future precision medicine research.107 Similarly, the UK’s Genomic Medicine Service, building on the 100 000 Genomes Project, has integrated whole-genome sequencing into routine care within the National Health Service, offering a coordinated model for population-level implementation.108

Despite these advances, genomic profiling adds complexity and cost to oncology workflows. Evidence from Belgium shows that even high-income countries face ethical, data-privacy and practical challenges beyond financial considerations.109 Nonetheless, multiple studies support the cost-effectiveness of genomic profiling across multiple cancer types, settings and health systems.15,17,110–112 A systematic review of hereditary breast and ovarian cancer concluded that germline genetic testing is likely cost-effective, particularly when targeted to high-risk individuals.112 Predictive genetic testing for at-risk relatives after identification of a familial variant further improves values by enabling surveillance and risk-reducing interventions that may prevent advanced, high-cost cancer care.110

Reimbursement fragmentation is a particularly acute problem: even within the European Union (EU), reimbursement timelines for companion diagnostics and genomic profiling assays vary by years between member states, meaning that the same tumour profile that warrants testing in one country goes untested next door.93,113 This disparity directly affects access to timely and potentially life-saving testing and reflects deliberate policy choices about health technology assessment frameworks, national budget priorities and regulatory harmonisation.93

The gap between cost-effectiveness evidence and implementation reflects more than resource limits. Budget silos, delayed reimbursement and competing policy priorities mean that proven genomic interventions can remain unavailable long after their value has been established.3 Promissory narratives about precision oncology may further inflate expectations that outpace current funding and delivery capacity. This is not primarily a knowledge deficit but a political economy challenge: upfront budget impact concerns routinely override lifetime cost savings, siloed budgets prevent diagnostic investment that would reduce downstream therapeutic spend and patient advocacy in genomics remains muted relative to drug access campaigns.3 Closing this gap requires explicitly naming these mechanisms, not simply reiterating the evidence.

In LLMIC settings, a critical distinction exists between affordability and prioritisation. In some contexts, the barrier is not cost alone but the absence of genomic profiling from national cancer control plans, rendering it invisible to health system planners regardless of price, a pattern driven by competing priorities, fragmented health systems and inadequate data infrastructure.114 Advocacy for including genomic profiling in essential cancer care packages, like the WHO Essential Medicines List, can serve as a powerful policy lever to catalyse funding and scale-up.115 At the European level, the EU Cancer Mission and the Beating Cancer Plan have explicitly prioritised genomics infrastructure, biobanking and cross-border data sharing. These initiatives offer a promising pathway that, if fully realised, could significantly reduce reimbursement and access disparities across member states.116

Future directions

Below, we outline research needs and technological strategies to improve public awareness and equitable access to genomically driven cancer care, drawing directly from recent studies and syntheses. Drawing directly from the barriers and opportunities identified across the five review themes, we present four recommendations to strengthen the translation of awareness into equitable access and clinical impact. Expand and power genomic studies in underserved populations to enable equitable awareness and access

Representative evidence is essential to equitable access. Under-representation in genomic studies limits both the clinical validity of genomic profiling and the development of culturally relevant communication and engagement strategies. Addressing these gaps is therefore critical not only for evidence generation but also for building trust, improving relevance in underserved populations and supporting culturally responsive communication.

Examples from Japan and Africa both illustrate how genomic medicine is most powerful when it is adapted to local populations, clinical contexts and systems of care rather than being imported unchanged. In Japan, evidence from adolescent and young adults (AYA) and older adults showed that targeted sequencing and comprehensive genomic profiling can identify clinically relevant alterations, but outcomes data remained limited for certain subpopulations—notably AYA and under-represented racial or ethnic groups.117 In African settings, initiatives such as the Human Heredity and Health consortium demonstrate that sustainable genomic research can be built with African-led capacity building, equitable partnerships, community engagement and infrastructure development.118 Taken together, these studies suggest that the value of genomic medicine lies not only in generating data, but in building locally grounded, ethically robust systems that support equitable implementation in resource-constrained settings.

Improve risk communication and decision-making across the cancer pathway

Uncertainty around risk prediction and disease progression creates challenges for patient understanding.119 Improving how risk is communicated, including uncertainty, is essential if awareness is to translate into meaningful engagement with prevention and treatment decisions.120 A key example is the limited ability to predict which premalignant lesions will progress, such as ductal carcinoma in situ of the breast and many screen detected prostate lesions, which contributes to both over and undertreatment.121 After diagnosis, minimal residual disease (MRD) testing can quantify residual tumour burden and provide early indication of recurrence risk.122 MRD testing supports risk stratification, informs adjuvant treatment decisions, enables dynamic response monitoring and facilitates early identification of resistant variants that may warrant intervention. Together, these challenges underscore the need for more precise predictive and monitoring tools so that patients receive appropriately tailored care while minimising unnecessary treatment and missed opportunities for early intervention.

Ensure equitable validation and implementation of emerging genomic technologies

Inclusive genomic profiling research is essential to ensure that awareness efforts do not direct patients towards technologies that are not validated for their populations. ctDNA is a promising non-invasive tool for profiling, MRD detection and monitoring, but validation and implementation have been uneven across racial and ethnic groups and resource settings.123 Many ctDNA assays and thresholds were derived in predominantly White cohorts, raising concerns about generalisability and potential to exacerbate disparities. A useful exemplar is the EU-funded PREDI-LYNCH project (https://predi-lynch.eu), a pan-European, multiomic initiative evaluating multiple non-invasive liquid-biopsy technologies for early detection of the three most common Lynch syndrome-associated cancers. This project pairs these assays with AI-driven signal detection and embeds a socioeconomic and ethical impact assessment to support acceptability, affordability and scalability across diverse health systems. Future ctDNA consortia should adopt PREDI-LYNCH-style features, including intentional recruitment of high-risk and under-represented groups, multiomic assay comparison, ancestry-aware AI model training and integrated implementation-science and ethical evaluation. The goal is that liquid biopsy innovations close rather than widen equity gaps. PREDI-LYNCH also anchors the newly launched EARLYSCAN cluster (Early Screening & Hereditary Cancer Awareness Network), uniting it with DISARM (ovarian cancer risk assessment via affordable liquid biopsies) and SHIELD (pancreatic cancer surveillance via blood-based immunoassays) under the Horizon Europe Mission on Cancer. This coordination aligns research across countries and cancer types, harmonises clinical pathways and ethics, and accelerates implementation-ready standards for heritable cancer early detection.

Generate real-world evidence on access, uptake and outcomes of genome-matched therapies

Real-world evidence is required to understand whether awareness, navigation and access to genomic interventions translate into improved uptake across diverse populations, particularly where structural barriers remain substantial.124 While genome-matched therapies can improve clinical outcomes, barriers such as insurance, cost, geography and clinician and patient awareness all influence whether actionable findings are ultimately translated into treatment.125 Existing qualitative work highlights the central role of case managers in navigating these barriers, but it remains limited in its ability to quantify impact across populations.126

Limitations

This review has several limitations. As a non-systematic narrative review, it is subject to selection bias. Although illustrative examples including from Africa were included where available, the evidence base identified through the search strategy remained weighted towards high-income settings and certain cancer types. Relevant regional programmes, non-English literature and emerging implementation initiatives in LLMIC may therefore have fallen outside the scope of this review. Evidence relating to certain cancer types, particularly breast and hereditary cancers and to some regions may be over-represented. Although inclusion of PPI co-authors was a strength and they brought perspectives from different backgrounds and settings, due to time and funding limitations there was representation from only a minority of cancer types and countries.

Conclusions

Genomic medicine has the potential to reshape cancer care, yet its benefits are inconsistently realised across populations and health systems. Compelling evidence supports the clinical utility and cost-effectiveness of genomic profiling across multiple cancer types and settings. However, disparities in awareness, access, clinician preparedness and trust limit the real-world impact of precision oncology. Patients frequently encounter complex information with insufficient emotional or navigational support, while clinicians contend with genomic literacy gaps and constrained time and resources. Structural obstacles—including fragmented reimbursement, uneven integration of genomic services and under-representation in research and clinical trials—further compound these inequities and reduce the generalisability of evidence.

Achieving equitable genomic cancer care therefore requires coordinated action across public, professional and policy domains. Culturally grounded, patient-centred communication and public awareness initiatives can improve understanding and support meaningful shared decision making, but their effects depend on parallel investment in workforce training, decision support interventions and service integration. Crucially, policy reforms to enable inclusive research, broaden trial access and harmonise reimbursement are needed so that awareness translates into timely, affordable treatment for all patients.

This review emphasises the interaction between public awareness and the political economy of health: awareness alone is insufficient unless systems are prepared and incentives aligned. Embedding awareness, trust, implementation capacity and equity into research, clinical pathways and policy design is therefore essential if genomically driven cancer care is to deliver consistent benefit across diverse populations (figure 2). While previous reviews have described barriers to precision oncology implementation, this review uniquely centres the interaction between public awareness and political economy factors in shaping equitable access.
