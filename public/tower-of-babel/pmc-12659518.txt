Semaphorin 5A modulates focal adhesion pathway and lamellipodia formation in melanoma
Authors: Matteo Brignone, Maria Concetta Cufaro, Cristiana Ercolani, Ilenia Masi, Fabio Di Ferdinando, Aldo Palange, Elisabetta Valentini, Marta Di Martile, Piero Del Boccio, Donatella Del Bufalo, Luca Tamagnone, Laura Rosanò, Simona D’Aguanno
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionMetastatic potential and rising incidence make cutaneous melanoma a major health concern worldwide. While current therapies have proven effective, they are limited by side effects arising and by the development of drug resistance. For this reason, identifying new therapeutic targets and predictive biomarkers is crucial. Semaphorin 5 A (SEMA5A) is a member of the Semaphorin family, implicated in both cancer biology and neural development. We have previously demonstrated that this protein promotes different aggressive features of melanoma cells. In this study, we conducted a more in-depth investigation into the role of SEMA5A in the development and progression of melanoma.MethodsWe performed proteomic and in silico analysis of stable knockdown SEMA5A human melanoma cells to identify the cellular pathways modulated by SEMA5A. The data used are available via ProteomeXchange with the identifier PXD065661. In vitro cell migration, clonogenic ability and cell viability assays under different adhesive substrate conditions were conducted using both human and murine melanoma SEMA5A-depleted clones. Western blotting and immunofluorescence analyses were employed to investigate focal adhesion regulation and lamellipodia formation. A Nocodazole assay was used to evaluate focal adhesion dynamics. A xenograft mouse model of melanoma was used to investigate the role of SEMA5A in tumor formation and tumor growth. Paraffin tumor sections were analyzed using immunohistochemical staining.ResultsDAVID functional annotation tool revealed significant enrichment of Focal Adhesion and Integrin pathway-associated proteins in both control and SEMA5A-silenced cells. Ingenuity pathway analysis highlighted the role of SEMA5A in modulating several cellular pathways, including focal adhesion and lamellipodia organization. We demonstrated that SEMA5A depletion reduces the migration, clonogenic capacity, and viability of melanoma cells in both low and high stiffness culture substrates. SEMA5A was found to affect FAK and paxillin phosphorylation, as well as RhoA and Integrin β1 activation. This regulates focal adhesion dynamics, and lamellipodia formation. Furthermore, SEMA5A was found to regulate Arp3 nucleation and expression. Delayed tumor formation and reduced tumor growth were observed in mice bearing melanoma xenografts expressing low levels of SEMA5A. Immunohistochemical analysis of xenograft tumor sections revealed increased level of active Integrin β1 and phosphorylated FAK in SEMA5A-silenced tumors compared to control.ConclusionsThese findings establish for the first time that SEMA5A is a novel player of melanoma aggressiveness and progression, modulating cancer cell viability and migration, focal adhesion signaling and lamellipodia formation in vitro and tumor growth in vivo.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-025-02526-z.Keywords: Semaphorin 5A, Melanoma, Focal adhesion, Lamellipodia, Integrin β1

Introduction

The incidence of cutaneous melanoma (briefly, melanoma) is increasing worldwide due to an aging population, elevated exposure to UV radiation and to teratogens [1]. The International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC, Lyon, France) estimated 325,000 new cases and 57,000 deaths from melanoma worldwide in 2020. Melanoma incidence rates vary greatly by geography, ranging from 42 cases per 100,000 persons/year for males and 31 cases per 100,000 persons/year for females in Australia/New Zealand, to 19 cases for men and women in Western Europe and 18 cases for men and 14 cases for women per 100,000 persons/year in North America [2]. Despite progress in early diagnosis and treatment, melanoma remains a major public health problem due to its aggressive and highly metastatic characteristics [1]. The introduction of BRAF inhibitors (vemurafenib, dabrafenib and encorafenib), MEK inhibitors (cobimetinib, binimetinib and trametinib) and immune checkpoint inhibitors (e.g. anti-PD-1 and anti-CTLA-4) has drastically improved the prognosis of melanoma patients. However, limitations to the use of approved therapies, such as drug-resistance and toxicity, push for continuous research and advancements in new treatment strategies [1] and in a deeper understanding of molecular mechanisms underlying melanoma aggressiveness. Furthermore, so far, there are no biomarkers predictive of response to immunotherapy approved for melanoma. In recent years, advances in Next-Generation Sequencing (NGS) technology and immunohistochemistry have led to the emergence of increasingly sophisticated panels of biomarkers. These panels consider critical factors such as tumor mutational burden, T lymphocyte infiltration, and gene expression signatures associated with treatment response [3]. While several studies represent significant progress towards personalized cancer therapy, further research is urgently needed to identify and validate biomarkers.

Semaphorins (SEMAs) are a family of highly conserved glycoproteins, both secreted and transmembrane, originally identified as pivotal factors in guiding axonal growth during the development of neural circuitry [4]. Numerous studies have highlighted the involvement of SEMAs in a wide array of biological processes, including cardiovascular development, angiogenesis, bone homeostasis, and immune responses [5]. More recently, SEMAs have attracted considerable interest in the context of cancer biology, with numerous studies demonstrating their association with tumor growth, cancer invasiveness, neoangiogenesis and metastasis, as well as their modulatory role in immune responses [6, 7]. In this regard, the effects of SEMAs are complex and depend on the cancer type and context, ranging from pro-tumor to anti-tumor activity [6]. Furthermore, preclinical studies have identified certain family members as potential biomarkers or therapeutic targets [6–8]. SEMAs largely bind to Plexins, their central high-affinity receptors, to initiate different signaling pathways [6, 9].

Some SEMAs and Plexins have been reported to play a role in the transformation of melanocytes, the progression of melanoma, and the response to therapy [9]. Among these proteins, Semaphorin 5 A (SEMA5A) is particularly notable [10]. SEMA5A is an integral membrane protein, but has also been reported to exist as both a membrane-bound molecule and a cleaved soluble extracellular domain [11]. There is evidence to suggest that circulating SEMA5A could be a potential clinical biomarker, as it is elevated in the serum of patients with inflammatory diseases [12–14]. The role of SEMA5A in cancer remains controversial, with both tumor-promoting and tumor-suppressing functions being described, depending on the tumor histotype [15–21]. Our previous findings established the SEMA5A protein as a driver of melanoma progression, by regulating cell migration and invasiveness, through Akt/ERK phosphorylation, and favoring the formation of vasculogenic structures [10]. In support of these functional findings, we also provided evidence of the relevance of SEMA5A in melanoma progression through the analysis of SEMA5A transcript expression in a public database and by immunohistochemistry analysis of human melanoma samples. The results indicated higher levels of SEMA5A in more advanced and/or metastatic forms of melanoma [10]. Here, we conducted an in-depth investigation to characterize the mechanistic role of SEMA5A in the development and progression of melanoma. Proteomic and bioinformatic analyses of human melanoma cells revealed that SEMA5A is involved in different cellular pathways, particularly concerning Focal Adhesion, Formation of Lamellipodia and Cellular protrusions.

Focal adhesions, which are formed in proximity of integrins and extracellular matrix (ECM) junctions, play a role in cell migration and invasion, and matrix remodeling, thereby contributing to various stages of tumor progression [22]. Lamellipodia are thin and sheet-like membrane protrusions, characterized by active actin polymerization and depolymerization, playing crucial roles in both cell motility and migration [23] as well as mechanosensing [24]. Recently, focal adhesion signaling has been associated with resistance to therapy and metastasis in melanoma, and lamellipodia formation has been described in the migration of melanoma cells [22, 25, 26]. Given the important roles of focal adhesions and lamellipodia in melanoma progression, we conducted in vitro and in vivo experiments to investigate the role of SEMA5A in these cellular structures.

Methods

Stable cell line construction and transient transfection

Human (A375) and murine (B16F10) melanoma cell lines were maintained in RPMI-1640 or DMEM, respectively, complete medium (Euroclone, Milan, IT), containing 10% inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS) (GIBCO, ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), 1% L-glutamine (Euroclone) and 100 µg/ml penicillin/streptomycin (Euroclone). Cell lines were routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination and authenticated.

Stable clones expressing low levels of SEMA5A protein were obtained by transducing the A375 and B16F10 cell line with a pool of commercially obtained viral particles (sc-63000-V, sc-63001-V, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Santa Cruz, CA, USA) containing 3 lentiviral plasmid constructs expressing 3 shRNA sequences of 19–25 nucleotides directed against human or murine SEMA5A mRNA sequence, capable of repressing its expression by degradation of the mRNA. Control clones were obtained using pools of commercially obtained viral particles (sc-108080, Santa Cruz Biotechnology) containing lentiviral plasmid constructs expressing shRNA sequences that do not induce specific degradation of any known cellular mRNA. A total of 1.5 × 104 A375 or 2.0 × 104 B16F10 cells were seeded in complete medium in 24-well plates. The next day, 10 µl of viral particles and 5 µg/ml of Polybrene (Santa Cruz Biotechnology) were added in 200 µl of medium. After 24 h, cells were trypsinized and cultured in the presence of 1 µg/ml for A375 and 2 µg/ml B16F10 of puromycin (Euroclone) for clone selection. SEMA5A expression at protein and mRNA levels in clones was assessed by western blot and Quantitative Real-Time PCR (qRT-PCR), respectively. All experiments were performed using melanoma clones at an early passage number.

Cell treatment

Cells were treated with Nocodazole (NDZ), a microtubule-disrupting agent, as described in literature [27–29]. Cells were grown on 100 mm plate, starved overnight in a medium containing 0.1% serum, and incubated with 10 µM NDZ (SML1665, Sigma-Aldrich) in serum-free medium for 4 h to depolymerize microtubules (MTs) on Collagen I-coated six-well plate. To allow MT reformation, the cells were washed from NDZ, incubated at 37 °C in serum-free medium and collected at different time points (0, 60, and 120 min). Whole-cell extracts were subjected to western blotting analysis.

Western blotting analysis

Cells were pelleted by gentle mechanical removal from the surfaces of dishes or multi-wells using scrapers, maintained on ice and lysed in RIPA buffer in the presence of protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Santa Cruz Biotechnology). Protein concentrations were determined by colorimetric assay (Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit, Thermo Scientific). Western blotting was performed with 35–40 µg of protein extracts using the following primary antibodies: SEMA5A (#AP12324PU-N) from OriGene (Rockville, Maryland, USA), phospho-FAK (Y576) (#ABP-0290), FAK (MAB-10157), phospho-paxillin (Y118) (ABP-0156), and paxillin (#MAB-80123) from Immunological Sciences (Rome, Italy), Arp3 (#A4514) from Abclonal (Düsseldorf, Germany), active conformation of Integrin β1 (#MAB2079Z) from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany); α-tubulin (sc- 32293) and GAPDH (#sc-32233) from Santa Cruz Biotechnology. The levels of RhoA activation were analyzed by RhoA activation assay (#BK036; Cytoskeleton, Denver CO USA) performed according to the manufacturer’s guidelines. Enhanced Chemiluminescent Substrate method (LiteAblotTURBO, Euroclone) was used to detect immunostained bands, except for the detection of phosphorylated proteins, for which Clarity Max Western ECL Substrate (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA) was used. ChemiDoc System instrument (Bio-Rad Laboratories) was used to acquire images, while ImageJ software was used for densitometric evaluation and normalization with relative controls.

Reverse transcription and qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted using a Qiagen RNeasy Mini kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Reverse transcription was performed using RevertAid Reverse Transcriptase (Thermo Scientific). Reaction conditions were: 50 °C for 60 min, 85 °C for 5 min, 4 °C until stopped. qRT-PCR was performed using a QuantStudio 6 Flex Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA), using the SYBR green dye detection method. The mRNA levels were normalized using β-actin. The primers used to analyze each gene were: human SEMA5A: 5’-ACTGTTCTAGCGACGGCACC-3′ (forward), 5’-CCCCAGAAAGCCCATCTGT-3′(reverse); human β-actin: 5’-ATTGCCGACAGGATGCAGAA-3′ (forward), 5’- GCTGATCCACATCTGCTGGAA-3′ (reverse);

murine β-actin: 5’-CATTGCTGACAGGATGCAGAA GG-3’ (forward), 5’- TGCTGGAAGGTG GACAGTGAGG-3’ (reverse); murine SEMA5A 5’- TGAGTCCCACTTCTGCTCTA-3’(forward), 5’- GAGGGTCACTGAAACCCTAAT-3’ (reverse).

The results were evaluated by the ΔΔCt method.

Transwell migration, cell adhesion, clonogenic ability and cell viability on different substrate

The transwell migration assay was performed as previously reported [10, 30]. A total of 5 × 104 cells were seeded in serum-free media into the upper chamber of 24-well ThinCert Cell Culture Inserts (Greiner Bio-One S.r.l, Cassina de Pecchi, Italy). The lower well contained medium with 10% FBS. After 5 h incubation at 37 °C, cells remaining on the top side of the membrane were removed, and migrated cells stained, photographed, and counted. From each transwell, several images were acquired using the light channel of the Bio-Rad ZOE fluorescent cell imager (Bio-Rad Laboratories).

For the cell adhesion assay, melanoma cells were stained with PKH67 dye according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Sigma) and seeded on 96-well plates coated with 50 µg/ml Collagen type I, 50 µg/ml Collagen type IV or 50 µg/ml Fibronectin. Images of adherent cells were acquired using the green channel of the Bio-Rad ZOE fluorescent cell imager and cells were counted by ImageJ.

To assess the ability to form colonies (clonogenicity), cell suspensions of different melanoma clones were seeded in 60 mm Petri dishes. After 10 days, colonies were stained with 2% methylene blue in 95% ethanol and counted (1 colony >50 cells) [31].

Cell viability in different stiffness condition was evaluated as previously described [30]. Melanoma clones were seeded in 6-well plates coated with Hydrogels bound with Collagen type I from bovine skin or rat tail at different elastic modulus (8 and 50kPA) (Softwell, Cell Guidance Systems, Cambridge, UK). After 4 and 24 h from seeding, images were acquired using the light channel of Bio-Rad ZOE fluorescent cell imager, cells were detached using gentle treatment with trypsin and stained with Trypan Blue solution. Both viable cells excluding the dye, and nonviable cells absorbing the dye and appearing blue were counted by CellDrop automated cell counter (DeNovix, Wilmington, DE, USA).

Cells were also seeded in 6-well plates with different coating: 50 µg/ml Collagen type I (Collagen I Rat Tail, #A1048301, GIBCO), 50 µg/ml Collagen type IV (sc-29010, Santa Cruz Biotechnology), 50 µg/ml Fibronectin (#sc-29011, Santa Cruz Biotechnology). After 1 h and 4 h from seeding, cell viability was evaluated as described above.

Immunofluorescence (IF) and confocal imaging analysis

Cells cultured on coverslips coated with Collagen type I, after 4 h were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature, permeabilized with 0.2% Triton-X-100 and blocked with 1% BSA and 0.1% Tween20 in PBS for 30 min at room temperature. Samples were incubated with primary antibodies in 0.5% BSA in PBS overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with secondary antibodies conjugated with suitable fluorochromes (Jackson ImmunoResearch, Cambridge House, UK) for 1 h at room temperature. Primary antibodies were: Arp3 (#A4514), FAK (#A11131) from Abclonal (Düsseldorf, Germany), phosphorylated FAK (Y567, # 2H74L24) from Invitrogen (ThermoFisher), active Integrin β1 chain (#MAB2079Z) from Merck. Coverslips were finally mounted with Fluoromount™ Aqueous Mounting Medium for fluorescence (#F4680, Merk, Sigma-Aldrich). Nuclei were stained with DAPI (#D1306, Invitrogen, ThermoFisher), F-actin with phalloidin (# A1238, Alexa Fluor 594, ThermoFisher). Images were obtained using a Zeiss LSM880 confocal laser scanning microscope, with a 63x/1.40 NA oil objective and excitation spectral laser lines at 405, 488, 543, nm. Image acquisition and processing were carried out using the Zeiss Confocal Software Zen 3.1 (Blue edition). Signals from different fluorescent probes were taken in sequential scan settings and co-localization was visualized in merge images. For quantification of images relative to pFAK, the number of positive structures of the green channel was calculated using Image J, 3D Objects Counter function, by dividing the total number of objects for each image by the total number of nuclei (at least six images for each condition were used). Arp3/F-actin and active Integrin β1/F-actin co-localization were estimated by calculating Pearson’s correlation coefficient with Coloc2 Plugin of Image J. Cells were analyzed for each labelling condition, and representative results were shown. The Pearson’s coefficients were calculated from multiple images and then averaged, and standard deviation of the mean was calculated. Arp3 Mean Fluorescence Intensity was estimated by Image J, by dividing the total intensity of each image by the total number of nuclei (at least three images for each condition were used). The resulting value for each condition was averaged, and the standard deviation of the mean was calculated.

Label-free proteomics by Liquid Chromatography tandem Mass Spectrometry (LC-MS/MS)

In order to evaluate the protein expression of SEMA5A knockdown clones, we performed a comparative shotgun proteomics analysis of A375 SEMA5A knockdown cells and and their related control cells. Cell pellets, obtained by scraping cells, were lysed by sonication on ice (Sonicator U200S control, IKA Labortechnik, Staufen, Germany) at 70% amplitude in a lysis buffer (urea 6 M in 100 mM Tris/HCl, pH = 7.5). 50 µg of proteins for each condition of lysed cells were digested with trypsin overnight at 37 °C by Filter Aided Sample Preparation (FASP) protocol. Previously proteins were quantified through a Bradford assay (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) using Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MI, USA) as the standard calibration curve.

For protein label-free identification and quantification, tryptic peptides from each sample were analyzed in triplicate with LC-MS/MS using the UltiMate™ 3000 UPLC (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) chromatographic system coupled to the Orbitrap Fusion™ Tribrid™ (Thermo Fisher Scientific) mass spectrometer.

Tryptic peptides were analyzed through a Trap Cartridge C18 (0.3 mm ID, 5 mm L, 5 μm PS, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and then separated on an EASY-spray Acclaim™ PepMap™ C18 (75 μm ID, 15 cm L, 2 μm PS, Thermo Fisher Scientific) nanoscale chromatographic column with a gradient of 65 min from 5 to 90% of ACN. Mass spectrometry parameters have been described in detail in a previous publication of ours [32, 33]. The mass spectrometry proteomics data have been deposited to the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the PRIDE [34] partner repository with the dataset identifier PXD065661.

Proteomics data processing

Proteomics MS/MS raw data were processed using MaxQuant version 1.6.10.50 (Max-Planck Institute for Biochemistry, Martinsried, Germany). Andromeda was used for picking and peptide search was performed by the UniProt database (released 2018_04, taxonomy Homo Sapiens, 20,874 entries and released 2019_11). Processing parameters were set as already listed in the previous published works [35, 36]. Briefly, carbamidomethylation of cysteines (C) was defined as fixed and quantification modification, while oxidation of methionines (M) and deamidation of asparagines (N) and glutamines (Q) were set as variable modifications as already reported in our previous works. False discovery rate (FDR) was set to 1% for both protein and peptide levels and LFQ Intensity values was used to quantify protein abundance in each sample and used for functional expression analysis [37]. Bioinformatics analysis was performed with Perseus v. 1.6.10.50 (Max-Planck Institute for Biochemistry, Martinsried, Germany) setting parameters as already reported [33, 36]. Differences in protein expression as well as presence and absence of proteins were evaluated for each comparison and, to define the differentially expressed proteins between the two clones and their controls, a univariate statistical analysis was performed with a p-value threshold of 0.05, visualizing results as a Volcano Plot. For each different comparison, through the Ingenuity Pathway Analysis tool (IPA, Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), protein ratios (A375 shSEMA5A/control) were used for the Gene Ontology and functional enrichment analysis. IPA is able to predict the activation (z-scores ≥ 2.0) or inhibition (z-scores ≤ − 2.0) of transcriptional regulators or downstreams for the loaded dataset based on prior knowledge of expected effects from published literature citations stored in the IPA system [38]. The “Database for Annotation, Visualization, and Integrated Discovery” (DAVID) (https://davidbioinformatics.nih.gov/) was used for gene ontology (GO) enrichment functional analysis, to highlight the main biological pathways and molecular functions in which protein datasets were involved, according to the tool “Functional Annotation Chart” as reported in Supplementary Files [39].

In vivo experiments

A375 control (shCTRL) and SEMA5A knockdown (shSEMA5A#4) cells in exponential growth phase were harvested from the culture by trypsinization, washed, and resuspended in PBS. 5 × 106 viable cells/mouse were subcutaneously injected into female CD-1 nude (nu/nu) mice (Charles River Laboratories, Calco, Italy) between 6 and 8 weeks of age. All procedures involving animals and their care were compliant with D.lgs 26/2014 (816/2015-PR of 11/08/2015) of the Italian Minister of Health and received the relative authorization (n° 235/2023-PR. PI Dr D’Aguanno). Institutional Review Boards of both Regina Elena National Cancer Institute and Minister of Health approved all the procedures involving animals (species, quality, number, discomfort/distress/pain, sacrifice). Under temperature-controlled, specific pathogen-free conditions, mice had sufficient access to water and standard mouse food. Animals were daily monitored for food consumption, body weight, and behaviour. No weight loss was observed. Twice a week, tumor dimensions were measured using caliper. Tumor volume was calculated using the following formula: volume (mm3) = length × width2 × 0.52. The maximal tumor size of 10% of the body weight was not exceeded. The experiments were repeated twice: 10 mice for group in the first experiment and 6 mice for group in the second experiment. The non-parametric Mann–Whitney test was employed to evaluate differences between the groups. Differences were statistically significant when p < 0.05. After assessing the reproducibility of tumor growth in the two experiments, the data were pooled (N = 16/condition). Differences in tumor formation between the two groups were evaluated applying the Fisher’s exact test and considered significant when p < 0.05.

Immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis

At the end of the second experiment (day 26; 6 mice per condition), tumors were excised, fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin, embedded in paraffin, cut and stained with Hematoxylin and Eosin (EE) to check tissue integrity. In the case of shSEMA5A condition, a total of 5 tumors were analysed, since in one mouse the tumor was not palpable. For the control condition, 6 tumors were initially stained with EE, but due to technical problems (likely in the pre-analytical phase) with one of them, only 5 were subjected to subsequent staining with the antibodies of interest.

IHC staining was performed on three distinct paraffin sections (3 μm) cut from each tumor. Immunoreactions were revealed by Bond Polymer Refine Detection in an automated autostainer (Bond-PRIME, Leica Biosystems, Milan, Italy) using the following monoclonal antibodies (mAb): anti-FAK rabbit Ab (#A11131, Abclonal), anti-phosphorylated FAK recombinant rabbit Ab (# 2H74L24, Invitrogen), anti-active Integrin β1 chain mouse Ab (#MAB2079Z, Merck). All slides were examined and analyzed by light microscopy. The score of immunoreaction intensity was evaluated for each antibody, specifying cellular localization (membrane/cytoplasm). Specifically, staining was classified as: SCORE 1+ (Low, staining that is barely detectable), SCORE 2+ (Medium, staining that is weak/moderate), SCORE 3+ (High, staining that is intense/strong). Cases with faint staining were regarded as negative (SCORE 0). Images were acquired with original magnification ×400.

Statistical analysis

In vitro experiments were replicated at least three times, unless otherwise indicated, and the data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Group differences were analyzed with a two-sided unpaired Student’s t-test. For in vivo experiments differences between groups were analyzed by Mann–Withney and Fisher’s exact tests and considered statistically significant for p < 0.05.

Results

SEMA5A silencing alters the proteome of melanoma cells

Previously, we demonstrated that SEMA5A regulates in vitro cell migration and invasion and the formation of vasculogenic structures in human M14 melanoma cells, through activating the Akt/ERK pathway [10]. Here, we generated stable clones of the human A375 and murine B16F10 melanoma cell lines, expressing low levels of SEMA5A protein, as described in Methods section. Validation of protein and mRNA knockdown is reported in Supplementary Figure S1A-D. In accordance with our previously published data [10], SEMA5A depletion reduced cell migration in vitro (Supplementary Figure S1E, F). We then characterized proteome changes induced by SEMA5A in melanoma cells. To this aim, we performed shotgun proteomics analysis in SEMA5A knockdown (sh5A#4) and control (shCTRL) A375 cells. A total of 1734 and 1664 proteins were identified and quantified in the sh5A and control conditions, respectively. Of these, 1604 proteins were common to both conditions, as reported in the Venn diagram of Fig. 1A, representing 89.4% of all identified proteins. Meanwhile, 7.25% and 3.4% of proteins were specific to SEMA5A knockdown and control cells, respectively. The complete list of the quantified proteins is provided in Supplementary Table S1 (sheet “Quantification”). Among the 1604 common proteins analysed, 482 were significantly differentially expressed, as assessed by t-test (p-value < 0.05). The Volcano Plot in Fig. 1B shows 194 significantly up-regulated (red dots), and 284 down-regulated proteins (green dots) among the common proteins in SEMA5A knockdown cells, compared to the control. All the differentially proteins shown in the Volcano Plot are listed in Supplementary Table S1, sheet “Volcano Plot”.

Proteomic analysis also revealed that SEMA5A is involved in focal adhesion and lamellipodia formation and maturation. We focused on Collagen I, because a similar response was observed in terms of cell viability on Collagen I and Collagen Type IV substrates. The results have showed that downregulating SEMA5A in A375 cells markedly increased the phosphorylation of FAK (pFAK), the main the focal adhesion pathway component, 4 h after plating cells on Collagen I coated dishes, compared to control cells (Fig. 4A). Consistent with these results, higher phosphorylation of paxillin was observed in SEMA5A knockdown cells (Fig. 4A), suggesting that SEMA5A regulates focal adhesion via FAK-dependent paxillin pathway.

To this end, two experiments were performed by subcutaneously injecting in nude mice A375 knockdown SEMA5A cells and control cells. Discussion

Recent studies have emphasized that SEMAs, including SEMA5A, play a significant role in cancer progression and their expression level may have diagnostic and prognostic value; however, melanoma-specific data is limited [6]. Using in vitro and in vivo melanoma cells, we gained insight into the intricacies of the SEMA5A-regulated cell migration and focal adhesion processes, describing a mechanism involving the Integrin β1/FAK pathway and lamellipodia formation. Consistent with previous data indicating that SEMAs regulate cell proliferation and colony formation in cancer cells [6, 47], we demonstrated that depleting SEMA5A reduced the colony formation and cell viability of melanoma cells cultured under varying substrate conditions, including those mimicking the stiffness found in the melanoma TME. These findings establish a functional link between SEMA5A and the adaptive response of tumor cells to ECM stiffness for the first time. While limited data is available in literature about the role of SEMAs in response to ECM stiffness, some of them seem to have a biological relevant link both in normal and tumor cells. The paralog family member SEMA5C has been shown to promote the collective migration of follicular epithelial cells during Drosophila development, and its activity is influenced by ECM stiffness [48]. Furthermore, the repulsive response of dorsal root ganglion sensory neurons to SEMA3A is modified by substrate stiffness, and the transcription of its receptor proteins, Neuropilin-1 and Plexin A4, is regulated by stiffness [49]. Neuropilin-1 expression has also been reported to be modulated by substrate stiffness in normal and prostate cancer cells, thereby promoting malignancy in both non-tumoral and prostate cancer cells [50]. Interactions between tumor cells and ECM components play an important role in melanoma cell adhesion and migration, thereby promoting invasion and metastasis [51]. Our results demonstrate a role played by SEMA5A in the interaction with ECM components and in focal adhesion, by the link with RhoA/FAK/paxillin pathway. RhoA, among the small GTPases, plays a crucial role in cytoskeletal reorganization and tumorigenesis [52]. In particular, RhoA affects cell‒cell or cell–ECM interactions by inducing rearrangements within the cytoskeleton [52]. FAK, a member of the protein tyrosine kinase superfamily, represents the main component of the focal adhesion signaling pathway [53]; it transmits signals from the ECM to the cell cytoplasm, regulating cellular functions such as cell survival, proliferation, migration and invasion [52]. In response to upstream stimuli, primarily through the Integrin signaling activation, FAK dimerizes, and the Y397 residue becomes autophosphorylated. Complete FAK enzymatic activation is achieved after the Y576 and Y577 residues are phosphorylated [52]. In addition to its involvement in focal adhesion, cell migration and invasion, FAK has also been described for the involvement in the crosstalk between tumor cells and the TME [52]. Indeed, a high level of FAK in solid tumors promotes TME remodeling by recruiting immunosuppressive cells and favoring cytokines secretion [52]. FAK inhibitors have been shown to suppress leucocyte and macrophage infiltration in mouse models of solid tumors [52]. Due to their potential effectiveness, FAK inhibitors are being evaluated in clinical trial for treating solid tumors [52]. Recently, focal adhesion signaling was also associated with response to therapy and metastasis in melanoma. RNA-seq analysis revealed the enrichment of ECM signaling, including focal adhesion signaling, in the transcriptomes of melanoma lesions derived from patients who were resistant to BRAF and MEK inhibitors [25]. Mutations frequently occurred in proteins associated with focal adhesion were identified in a large-scale characterization of metastases derived from melanoma patients after postmortem examination, employing a combination of RNA-seq and mass spectrometry analysis [54]. While SEMAs belonging to class 3 have been reported to rapidly alter the cytoskeleton and disassemble focal adhesions, this study provides the first evidence of SEMA5A’s role in regulating these cellular structures [55–58].

Paxillin plays a central role in the assembly and disassembly of focal adhesions and the control of cytoskeletal dynamics, which are essential for efficient cell motility [59]. After being phosphorylated at the tyrosine residues Y31 and Y118, paxillin acts as a docking site for proteins that are involved in both actin polymerization and integrin clustering [59]. In SEMA5A knockdown cells, we have observed increased RhoA activation and elevated FAK and paxillin protein phosphorylation. This regulation suggests a basal function of SEMA5A in inhibiting RhoA activation, that have not yet been previously described. Moreover, our results suggest that SEMA5A plays a crucial role in regulating focal adhesion dynamics. This is further supported by a focal adhesion turnover assay, in which SEMA5A depletion led to the accumulation of pFAK after NDZ washout, suggesting impaired or delayed focal adhesion disassembly. A higher level and/or an increased in pFAK kinetics after NDZ washout is associated with reduced cell migration after SEMA5A silencing, in agreement with our previously published data [10]. The link between a higher level and/or an increased kinetic of pFAK in an NDZ assay and decreased of ability of cells to migrate has also been reported for different tumor histotypes [28, 29]. Consistent with this, we have also observed a faster accumulation of active Integrin β1 in SEMA5A knockdown cells after NDZ washout. Furthermore, increased colocalization of active Integrin β1 and F-actin was evident in SEMA5A-depleted cells. These results suggest that the absence of SEMA5A results in more stable and less dynamic focal adhesions, thereby impairing the cellular plasticity required for efficient cell migration. The mechanisms linking SEMA-dependent signaling and Integrin-activated pathways are not fully understood and are primarily based on data regarding SEMA3A and SEMA4D. These proteins have been shown to inhibit integrin-mediated cell adhesion through the NRP1/PlexinA1 complex in endothelial cells and the PlexinB1 receptor in tumor cells, respectively [60–62]. A novel signaling pathway elicited by SEMA6C has been described in cancer cells of different histotypes. This pathway involves SEMA6C binding, and subsequent phosphorylation, of the tyrosine kinases c-Abl. This leads to FAK activation, independent of cell-matrix adhesion [63]. Other findings have suggested the existence of interaction between SEMA7A and Integrin β1 that is capable of activating FAK in glioma stem cells and increasing their motility [64]. Recently, Steinmetz and colleagues used an estrogen receptor-positive breast cancer model and found that SEMA7A binds to Integrins β1 and β4, activating AKT-mediated pro-survival signaling via the RGD domain [65].

Functional proteomic analysis revealed that SEMA5A modulated Formation of lamellipodia and Quantity of cellular protrusions among biological functions, as well as Canonical pathways including Actin Nucleation via the ARP-WASP Complex, which is essential for the lamellipodia [24]. Our findings showed that control cells exhibited sheet-like membrane protrusions and Arp3/F-actin colocalization area, which are hallmarks of active actin nucleation and lamellipodia formation, essential for cell movement. In contrast, SEMA5A knockdown disrupted these organized nucleation structures, resulting in a marked reduction in Arp3/F-actin colocalization and increased Arp3 protein expression levels. Taken together, these results suggest that SEMA5A supports efficient and dynamic lamellipodia formation, which appears to be impaired in its absence.

Research on cellular dynamics in cancer progression has revealed the intricate role of lamellipodia and filopodia [24, 66]. Lamellipodia, which are governed by complex rearrangements of the actin cytoskeleton, facilitate the migration, invasion and interaction of cancer cells with the TME [66]. In particular, in mouse and human melanoma models, increased cell migration associated with lamellipodia formation has been linked to oncogenic MAPK signaling and loss of PTEN [26]. Furthermore, the recruitment to lamellipodia of β1 and β3 integrins, two critical adhesion proteins involved in cell migration, has been documented in A375 melanoma cells [67]. Regarding this topic, it has been reported that PlexinB3 stimulation by SEMA5A resulted in the significant inhibition of cell migration and invasion, that was mediated by RAC1 inactivation and disruption of lamellipodia in glioma cells [15, 68]. The association of SEMA5A with cellular protrusions has been described in the context of the nervous system, where a mutation in the SEMA5A gene has been linked to autism spectrum disorder [69]. Recently, it was demonstrated that SEMA5A promotes axonal complexity and reduces functional connectivity in enteric neurons [70]. So far, no data have been reported on the role of SEMA5A in regulating focal adhesion and Integrin β1 signaling or lamellipodia in melanoma. There is also no indication of the possible involvement of SEMA5A receptors or other regulatory factors in these processes. The manner in which SEMA5A regulates these pathways, either directly or through interaction with receptors or other molecular partners, will be a future focus of our research. Interestingly, we also found that SEMA5A knockdown delays tumor formation and reduces tumor growth in a xenograft melanoma mouse model. Our results indicate that SEMA5A depletion results in a more stable and less dynamic focal adhesions, which impairs cell plasticity in vitro and ultimately slows tumor growth in vivo. Several studies have investigated the mechanism linking the focal adhesion formation/stability to cell migration in vitro and to tumor growth and metastasis in vivo. Some studies have reported that immature focal adhesions, with an unstable structure and few visible adhesion clusters, promoted cell migration. However, the maturation of these structures into larger and more organized ones inhibited cell migration in other cells [71–73]. Furthermore, it is impossible to identify a single mechanism that links the activation of the Integrin β1 and FAK pathways, the formation/dynamics of focal adhesions, and cell migration/invasion in vitro and with tumor growth in vivo. In this regard, different scenarios have been described in solid tumor models. Elongated and larger focal adhesions with reduced focal adhesion dynamics and increased FAK phosphorylation have been associated with a reduced cell migration and invasion in vitro, as well as reduced tumor size and lymph node metastasis formation in vivo [73]. Conversely, immature and small in size focal adhesions, associated with increased FAK and paxillin phosphorylation were proposed to enhance cell migration and invasion in vitro, and the number of liver metastasis nodules in vivo [71]. In vitro, reduced cell migration, decreased number and size of focal adhesion foci and levels of RhoA protein have been reported, without affecting focal adhesion recycling/disassembly, integrins expression and their presentation in the membrane, whereas decreased number of metastatic foci were observed in vivo [74]. Recently, it was reported that PlexinB3 is required for the induction of FAK signaling and the promotion of cell migration, invasion, and cancer stem cell specification in breast cancer cells, under hypoxia condition of growth [75]. Moreover, PlexinB3 knockdown in breast cancer cells reduced spontaneous lung metastasis and tumor initiation capacity when orthotopically implanted into female SCID mice; however, no differences in tumor growth were observed compared to control [75]. To further characterize the effects of SEMA5A depletion in vivo, tumor xenograft sections were analyzed by IHC. Consistent with in vitro data, no differences have been observed in the expression of FAK protein between the SEMA5A-silenced tumors and the control. In the tissues, total FAK protein staining was exclusive to the cytoplasm. On the contrary, the localization of active FAK has been observed to membrane in all groups. A trend of higher phosphorylated FAK protein and active Integrin β1 staining has been observed in SEMA5A-silenced tumors, partially confirming the in vitro experiments. The reduced growth of SEMA5A-silenced tumors in vivo is in line with the reduced cell viability of cells expressing low levels of SEMA5A observed in vitro, under different conditions of stiffness and substrate, and reduced clonogenic ability. We can hypothesize that SEMA5A has other, as-yet- unknown, function that support cell viability in vitro and tumor growth in vivo, which need further investigations.

Overall, our findings underscore the significant role of SEMA5A in cell-ECM communication and highlight the intricate mechanisms that govern these interactions. The unique ability of SEMA5A to regulate key cellular processes, such as cell migration, focal adhesion, and lamellipodia formation, crucial for the interaction with ECM and TME, is reflected in the in vivo murine melanoma model, where reduced SEMA5A expression was associated with delayed tumor initiation and decreased tumor growth.

Conclusion

In conclusion, SEMA5A contributes to melanoma aggressiveness, demonstrating its role by modulating focal adhesion and lamellipodia dynamics, and tumor progression. Our data reveal an unrecognized function of SEMA5A, reinforcing its importance as a tumor-promoting factor and suggesting it as a potential therapeutic target for melanoma.
