Introduction

In accordance with current guidelines,1,2 computed tomography (CT) calcium scoring is widely used to adjudicate discordant aortic stenosis (AS) severity, particularly when low-flow, low-gradient (LFLG) severe AS is suspected. However, calcium-based thresholds may be less reliable when obstruction is driven by fibrosis and commissural fusion rather than bulky calcification. We report a case of paradoxical LFLG severe AS with rheumatic morphology and unexpectedly low CT calcium burden.

Case presentation

An 82-year-old woman presented with progressive exertional dyspnoea. Nine months before referral, symptoms worsened with chest pain. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) revealed AS, and she was referred to our institution.

At presentation, the patient was classified as New York Heart Association functional class III. Physical examination revealed height 160 cm, weight 54 kg, body surface area 1.55 m2, blood pressure 130/73 mmHg, heart rate 80 beats/min, and oxygen saturation 97% on room air. A cardiac examination revealed a grade 2/6 systolic murmur and an audible mechanical valve click. There was no leg oedema.

The patient had rheumatic mitral stenosis treated with open mitral commissurotomy 50 years earlier and mechanical mitral valve (MV) replacement 20 years earlier. She also had chronic atrial fibrillation, dyslipidaemia, and a history of surgery for left breast cancer.

Laboratory tests showed a B-type natriuretic peptide level of 189.6 pg/mL. Electrocardiography revealed atrial fibrillation, incomplete right bundle branch block, left ventricular (LV) high voltage, and strain T-wave changes (Figure 1). Chest radiography showed cardiomegaly without pulmonary congestion.

Electrocardiographic findings at presentation. A 12-lead electrocardiogram showed atrial fibrillation, incomplete right bundle branch block, and left ventricular high voltage with ST-segment depression and T-wave inversion in leads II, III, aVF, V5, and V6, consistent with a strain pattern.

TTE showed concentric LV remodelling with preserved systolic function (LV mass index, 88.4 g/m2; relative wall thickness, 0.49; LV ejection fraction, 61%) and no regional wall motion abnormalities (Figure 2A, Supplementary material online, Videos S1 and S2). The aortic valve (AV) had a tricuspid configuration with mild calcification and restricted opening, although the orifice was suboptimally delineated (Figure 2B, Supplementary material online, Video S3). Blood pressure during echocardiographic assessment was 119/62 mmHg. The LV outflow tract diameter was 17.6 mm, and the stroke volume index was 30 mL/m2, consistent with a low-flow state (Figure 2C). Continuous-wave Doppler showed a peak velocity of 3.57 m/s, peak pressure gradient of 51 mmHg, and a mean pressure gradient of 27 mmHg (Figure 2D). The Doppler velocity index was 0.22, and the continuity-equation aortic valve area (AVA) was 0.50 cm2, corresponding to an indexed AVA of 0.32 cm2/m2, suggesting paradoxical LFLG severe AS. Contrast-enhanced CT showed no severe coronary artery stenosis. The AV calcium score was 889 Agatston units, below the conventional female threshold for severe calcific AS. Contrast-enhanced short-axis CT also showed leaflet tip thickening and focal calcification along the cusp edges (Figure 3). Because CT calcium burden was discordant, further haemodynamic evaluation was pursued. Simultaneous LV and aortic pressure measurements showed a mean gradient of 34.8 mmHg (Figure 4), which was below the conventional 40 mmHg threshold. However, because pressure gradients are flow-dependent and may be reduced in paradoxical low-flow physiology, this borderline invasive gradient supported the likelihood of severe AS when interpreted as part of an integrated assessment with the echocardiographic and anatomical findings. Cardiac output and invasive AVA were not measured during catheterization.

Three-dimensional transoesophageal echocardiography (TEE) showed fusion of all commissures and leaflet tip thickening of the right and left coronary cusps, further restricting cusp motion and resulting in a triangular systolic orifice (Figure 5A, Supplementary material online, Video S4). Multiplanar reconstruction yielded a planimetry-derived AVA of 0.69 cm2 (Figure 5B, C, and D). Taken together, the findings supported the final diagnosis of paradoxical LFLG severe AS despite the discordantly low CT calcium burden. The patient fulfilled the echocardiographic criteria for paradoxical LFLG severe AS, including preserved LV ejection fraction, reduced stroke volume index, low transvalvular gradient, and markedly reduced AVA by the continuity equation. Although the CT calcium score was below the conventional female threshold for severe calcific AS, this finding was not considered sufficient to exclude severe AS given the characteristic rheumatic features demonstrated on CT and TEE. In this context, the low calcium score was interpreted as reflecting the non-calcific rheumatic substrate rather than mild stenosis. In addition, although invasive haemodynamics demonstrated only a borderline severe transvalvular gradient, 3D TEE directly visualized a severely restricted anatomical orifice, supporting anatomical severe AS. On the 6-min walk test, she developed dyspnoea with a Borg scale score of 15, and her walking distance was limited to 150 m. The exertional dyspnoea and limited walking distance further supported the clinical significance of the stenosis.

Given symptomatic severe AS, advanced age, and two prior sternotomies, the heart team recommended transcatheter AV implantation (TAVI). A balloon-expandable valve was selected because its shorter frame length and relatively precise deployment were considered advantageous for avoiding interference with the prior mechanical MV prosthesis. Although valve migration was a concern because rheumatic AS may have limited calcification and the AV calcium burden was low in the present case, leaflet thickening, commissural fusion, and focal calcification were considered sufficient for anchoring. Preprocedural CT showed an annular area of 341 mm2, for which a 23-mm balloon-expandable valve was considered appropriate. As both external iliac arteries were small, elective TAVI was performed using a left carotid approach, and the valve was implanted without complications.

TTE performed on Postoperative Day 4 showed a peak AV velocity of 1.75 m/s and a mean pressure gradient of 5.3 mmHg, without paravalvular leak. On Postoperative Day 6, 6-min walk testing showed improvement in walking distance from 150 to 254 m, although mild dyspnoea remained with a Borg scale score of 13. The patient was discharged home on Postoperative Day 7. At 1 month, symptoms improved, and she was classified as New York Heart Association class II. This early haemodynamic and functional improvement after TAVI further supported the clinical significance of the stenosis despite the low CT calcium burden.

Discussion

This case illustrates a diagnostic pitfall in paradoxical LFLG severe AS: A low CT calcium burden may be misleading in rheumatic morphology.

In our patient, TTE suggested paradoxical LFLG severe AS, whereas CT calcium burden was below the guideline-stated female threshold (>1200 Agatston units).1 The key issue is that these thresholds were primarily developed for degenerative calcific AS, where calcium burden is closely linked to stenosis severity. In contrast, rheumatic AS may cause severe obstruction by commissural fusion and fibrosis, with less leaflet calcification.2

This distinction is central to the present case. The patient had an established rheumatic substrate following prior MV replacement for rheumatic mitral stenosis, supporting possible rheumatic AV involvement. In general, rheumatic AS is characterized by commissural fusion, leaflet thickening, restricted cusp motion, and variable fibrosis, often with calcification concentrated along the leaflet edges. These features result in a triangular systolic orifice. By contrast, degenerative calcific AS of a tricuspid AV is typically characterized by more bulky calcification, predominantly involving the central and basal portions of each cusp, without commissural fusion, resulting in a stellate-shaped systolic orifice. These established morphologic distinctions were consistent with the CT and TEE findings in the present case. The interpretation of these findings as rheumatic AV morphology is supported by prior descriptions of rheumatic AV disease,2 and recent evidence that the diagnostic performance of CT calcium scoring in rheumatic AS is limited compared with degenerative or bicuspid AS.3 A prior CT-based study moved beyond calcium-only assessment by quantifying fibrocalcific valve composition in AS.4 Although this method highlights the contribution of the non-calcific fibrotic tissue to stenotic valve burden, it remains investigational and does not specifically address rheumatic AS. Therefore, in suspected rheumatic AS, a discordantly low CT calcium score should not be interpreted in isolation but should be weighed against valve morphology, Doppler haemodynamics, invasive findings, and symptom burden. Thus, when rheumatic morphology is suspected and calcium burden is low, 3D TEE planimetry remains clinically important.

In the present case, 3D TEE planimetry was pivotal. It demonstrated an AVA of 0.69 cm2, confirming severe anatomical stenosis when haemodynamic findings and CT calcium burden were discordant. In such cases, TEE may allow a clearer delineation of the restricted valvular orifice than TTE,2 particularly when rheumatic morphology with limited calcification is present. Li et al. previously reported a similar case of paradoxical LFLG severe AS with only mild calcification, in which the true severity was clarified using dobutamine stress echocardiography.5 In the present case, dobutamine stress echocardiography or exercise/stress testing was considered but not performed. This patient had preserved LV ejection fraction and a small, concentrically remodelled LV, making adequate flow augmentation potentially difficult and flow-based adjudication less reliable. Therefore, we prioritized anatomy-based assessment by 3D TEE. The clearly restricted anatomical orifice, commissural fusion, leaflet tip thickening, and planimetry-derived AVA of 0.69 cm2 supported severe AS despite the discordantly low CT calcium score.

After TAVI, the patient’s 6-min walk distance improved by ∼100 m, supporting the clinical significance of the stenosis and appropriateness of the intervention.

A recent longitudinal study of rheumatic MV intervention showed that a subset of patients with baseline mild or moderate AS progressed to severe disease during follow-up, emphasizing the importance of continued surveillance for delayed AV progression.6 As rheumatic valvular disease becomes less common in high-resource settings,7 our report underscores the need to revisit its distinctive morphology, longitudinal progression, and diagnostic pitfalls in contemporary structural heart disease evaluation.

This study was based on a single case, and TEE planimetry depends on image quality and correct plane alignment. We do not suggest abandoning CT calcium scoring, but rather interpreting it in light of the valve aetiology. In rheumatic valve disease, a low calcium burden may be misleading because obstruction may be driven by commissural fusion and fibrosis.

Conclusion

This case highlights a diagnostic pitfall of relying on CT calcium score alone in paradoxical LFLG severe AS with rheumatic morphology. When rheumatic involvement is suspected, 3D TEE planimetry and careful morphologic assessment may be essential to resolve discordant grading and guide intervention.
