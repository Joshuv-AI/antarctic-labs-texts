Musculoskeletal Ultrasound Education in Hand Surgery Fellowships
Authors: Raysa G. Cabrejo, Rameez Qudsi, John R. Fowler, Diane C. Riley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe use of ultrasound in upper-extremity surgery has increased dramatically in diagnostics, therapeutics, and ultrasound-guided procedures. Despite the growing relevance, it is unclear how much structured musculoskeletal ultrasound (MSK US) education is being provided by hand surgery fellowship programs.MethodsTwo qualitative, cross-sectional online surveys were distributed to all Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education-accredited Hand Surgery Fellowship Program Directors in 2024 and 2025. Surveys contained six questions (2024) and nine questions (2025) and required approximately 1 minute to complete.ResultsThirty-one of 94 programs responded to the survey in 2024 and 40/93 in 2025. Ultrasound was included in fellowship training in 21 responding programs in 2024 and 23 in 2025. Among programs that include ultrasound, it was used for diagnostic (69% in 2024, 60% in 2025), therapeutic (59% in 2024 and 50% in 2025) and surgical applications (17% in 2024, 21% in 2025). Training under a hand surgeon with MSK US expertise increased from 69% in 2024 to 81% in 2025. Many programs were unaware of online MSK educational materials available through either AAOS ROCK (55% in 2024 vs 33% in 2025) or American Society for Surgery of the Hand Handthology (30% in 2025).ConclusionsSurvey responses in both 2024 and 2025 show a substantial interest in MSK US utilization and education among hand surgery fellowships, with >90% of responding programs reporting current or planned integration of ultrasound training. Despite this, hand surgery training lacks a standardized curriculum comparable to those established in emergency medicine, PM&R, rheumatology, and sports medicine.Clinical relevanceFurther integration of ultrasound in clinical practice and establishing a core MSK US curriculum for hand surgery fellowship training could be critical steps to ensure competency, support credentialing, and align with the evolving needs of clinical practice. Limitations of this study are response rate and potential response bias.

Ultrasound uses high-frequency sound waves to produce real-time images of internal structures. It is a noninvasive, portable, low-cost, and dynamic diagnostic modality that provides detailed anatomical information to address clinical questions.1 Multiple studies have shown that early incorporation of ultrasound education reinforces retention of physiology and anatomy and improves student confidence. Narula et al2 argued in a 2018 JAMA commentary that it was time to add ultrasound as a fifth pillar to bedside physical examination. Since 2014, the number of medical schools having an ultrasound curriculum has more than doubled. The most recent survey shows that 66% of medical schools have approved point-of-care ultrasound curriculums.3

Over the last 5 years, the number of publications in Journal of Hand Surgery, Journal of Hand Surgery Global Online, and HAND regarding ultrasound has increased over 200%. Ultrasound courses have been included in the American Society for Surgery of the Hand (ASSH) meeting annually since 2017. Ultrasound-guided procedures, such as injections, aspirations, and fracture reductions, are now integral to hand surgery practice.4, 5, 6 A survey of ASSH members in 2020 demonstrated that 51% to 68% of surgeons use ultrasound for diagnostic purposes and 33% of respondents performed US-guided injections.7

As the scope of ultrasound applications continues to expand within hand and upper-extremity surgery, there is a growing imperative to ensure that trainees achieve proficiency in musculoskeletal ultrasound (MSK US). The scientific literature is not clear in the degree of exposure and formal ultrasound education within hand surgery residency programs or in hand surgery fellowships.8 In the most recent reviews regarding hand surgery fellowship education, ultrasound education is not mentioned.8 The 2022 Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education (ACGME) Program Requirements for Graduate Medical Education in Hand Surgery does not include ultrasound education nor does the ASSH Fellowship System and Directory. In contrast, anesthesia, cardiology, critical care, emergency medicine, endocrine, obstetrics-gynecology, nonsurgical sports medicine, physiatry, and rheumatology have curriculums.9,10 Specifically, emergency medicine residencies have a core lecture series and a dedicated 2–4 week rotation, and physical medicine and rehabilitation residencies require 10 ultrasound procedures to be completed as a graduation requirement.11, 12, 13 Ninety-four percent of adult rheumatology fellowships teach ultrasound as part of their training with 41% having a formalized curririculum.14,15 In fact, the American Medical Society for Sports Medicine has published a core curriculum for MSK US education, such that graduating fellows meet core competency as outlined by the American Institute of Ultrasound in Medicine to be credentialed providers for MSK ultrasound and procedures.9,16

The objective of this study is to evaluate the state of ultrasound education among hand surgery fellowship programs via survey. By examining existing educational practices and institutional resources, this work seeks to inform the development of a standardized framework for integrating ultrasound training as a core component of hand surgery education and clinical practice.

Methods

Two online qualitative cross-sectional surveys were distributed to all ACGME-accredited hand surgery fellowship programs in the United States; the first in 2024 and the second in 2025. Contact information for programs was obtained from publicly available data through the ACGME and American Medical Association databases in 2024 and through ASSH-to-programs listed in the ASSH fellowship directory in 2025. The survey consisted of six questions in 2024 and nine questions in 2025 and was designed to be completed in approximately 1 minute.

The study received formal exemption for IRB review as it is a voluntary survey and thus a category 2 exemption of federal regulation (45CFR 46 104).

Results

The response rate of the surveys was 33% (31/94) in 2024 and 43% (40/93) in 2025; 13 programs answered both years. Sixty-eight percent of the respondents (21/31) in 2024 and 58% (23/40) in 2025 reported MSK US training as part of their fellowship (Fig. 1). Among programs that include ultrasound, it was used for diagnostic (69% in 2024, 60% in 2025), therapeutic (59% in 2024, 50% in 2025) and surgical applications (17% in 2024, 21% in 2025) (Fig. 2A,B). If ultrasound was not a part of the core curriculum, 72% of respondents in 2024 and 78% of respondents in 2025 planned to incorporate ultrasound training in the future (Fig. 3A,B). Training under a hand surgeon with MSK US expertise increased from 69% of respondents in 2024 to 81% in 2025 (Fig. 4A,B). Seventeen (55%) of programs in 2024 and 13 (32.5%) in 2025 were unaware that the AAOS ROCK curriculum includes a section on US MSK (Fig. 5A,B). Twelve programs in 2025 (30%) were unaware of the ASSH Handthology MSK educational materials. Nine programs (22%) were influenced by the ASSH Handthology MSK US online education offerings to include ultrasound in their fellowship while eight programs (6 in 2025 vs 2 in 2024) are waiting for a standardized curriculum for hand surgery fellowships (Fig. 6). Two additional questions were asked in 2025 that were not asked in the 2024 survey. The first asked if faculty had presented or published on ultrasound. Twenty-five percent of responding programs in 2025 had faculty that presented or published on ultrasound (Fig. 7). The second gave respondents a chance to express their thoughts on the utilization of ultrasound in hand fellowship (Fig. 8). Nine programs commented. The comments ranged from two that felt it should not be taught as it unnecessarily increased cost to six that felt it was an important emerging technology and one added new faculty with an interest in ultrasound. Discussion

A majority of responding hand surgery fellowship programs indicated that they have or will be adding MSK US education to their fellowship. Most programs use ultrasound for both diagnostic and therapeutic purposes. Overall, this survey demonstrates an interest in the utilization and teaching of ultrasound in hand fellowship programs. In the literature, there are multiple examples of brief education sessions, including lectures and hands-on simulations, leading to increased comfort levels of medical students and residents with ultrasound and subsequently leading to improved diagnostic and procedural abilities.17, 18, 19 Developing a core curriculum in fellowship would build a foundation to standardize ultrasound throughout the specialty; a critical element required by employers and hospital systems for credentialing and resource allocation.

Ultrasound exposure and education in medical school has doubled since 2014. Most incorporate it as part of their physical examination education.20 Hand surgeons, reconstructive surgeons, and general surgeons are using ultrasound in their practices already.21,22 Thus, we are at a crossroads where some trainees are coming into hand surgery with an exposure to ultrasound but may not be able to expand their technical competence and clinical reasoning during their hand fellowship. Many fields have made the investment to formalize ultrasound education into a core curriculum. Sports medicine proposed a core curriculum in 2010, and by 2017, all graduating fellows met American Institute of Ultrasound in Medicine core competence in MSK ultrasound.9,16 Forty-one years have passed since Hentz et al23 concluded that ultrasound would add “measurably to our understanding of the function and maladies of the hand.” Twenty years have passed since the report of the first ultrasound-guided hand surgery.24 Furthermore, ASSH has included at least one hands-on ultrasound course at the annual meeting for 9 years. These courses have been available to hand surgeons and more recently part of the Resident & Fellows Review Course in Hand Surgery. Nevertheless, a core curriculum has yet to be adopted for hand fellowships to ensure core competency in diagnostic, therapeutic, and ultrasound-guided procedures.

As with most survey studies, a major limitation of this study is response rate bias. The 2023−2024 survey was based on the ACGME database for Hand fellowships. Only 35% of the programs listed the fellowship directors’ email, and this database required crosschecking via Google search of the individual programs to find email contacts. Prior studies have shown that these websites often contain obsolete or inaccurate data.25, 26, 27, 28 A second survey was sent out in 2025 via ASSH to fellowships listed in their online fellowship directory. This directory is compiled by ASSH and is the reference standard accepted for recent papers evaluating fellowship education and leadership.8,29 Despite being housed by ASSH and accessible only via permission from ASSH council for survey use, ASSH acknowledges that the information listed has not been vetted. ASSH lists the email contact for fellowship programs and ACGME lists email for either the fellowship director, an administrator for the fellowship or a mail address. While Cohen et al28 found that 51% of the weblinks listed in the ASSH directory provided a direct link to a program, they did not provide information on the accuracy of the email addresses provided in the directory. Discrepancies in email addresses, hospital firewalls and changes in leadership can diminish response rates. Thirteen programs answered both surveys. It is possible that some programs did not answer the second survey because they had answered the first survey.

The present surveys support strong interest in MSK US utilization and education among hand surgery fellowships. Despite this, hand surgery lacks a standardized curriculum comparable to those established in related fields such as emergency medicine, PM&R, rheumatology and nonsurgical sports medicine. Establishing a core MSK US curriculum is a critical next step to ensure competency, support credentialing, and align hand surgery training with the evolving needs of clinical practice.
