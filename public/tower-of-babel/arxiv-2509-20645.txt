Look Before you Leap: Estimating LLM Benchmark Scores from Descriptions
Jungsoo Park, Ethan Mendes, Gabriel Stanovsky, Alan Ritter, Georgia Institute of Technology, The Hebrew University of Jerusalem

Abstract
Progress in large language models is constrained by an evaluation bottleneck: build a benchmark, evaluate models and settings, then iterate. We therefore ask a simple question: can we forecast outcomes before running any experiments? We study text-only performance forecasting: estimating a model’s score from a redacted task description and intended configuration, with no access to dataset instances. To support systematic study, we curate Precog , a corpus of redacted description–performance pairs spanning diverse tasks, domains, and metrics. Experiments show the task is challenging but feasible: models equipped with a retrieval module that excludes source papers achieve moderate prediction performance with well-calibrated uncertainty, reaching mean absolute error as low as 8.7 8.7 on the Accuracy subset at high-confidence thresholds. Our analysis indicates that stronger reasoning models engage in diverse, iterative querying, whereas current open-source models lag and often skip retrieval or gather evidence with limited diversity. We further test a zero-leakage setting—forecasting on newly released datasets or experiments before their papers are indexed—where GPT-5 with built-in web search still attains non-trivial prediction accuracy. Overall, our corpus and analyses offer an initial step toward open-ended anticipatory evaluation, supporting difficulty estimation and smarter experiment prioritization. 1 1 1 Our data and code and are available in https://github.com/JJumSSu/PRECOG

Introduction

Modern LLM development is evaluation-centric: define a benchmark, sweep models and configurations, iterate
(
Brown et al. 2020
;
Achiam et al. 2023
;
Touvron et al. 2023
;
Team et al. 2023
)
. As benchmarks proliferate, exhaustive curation and full sweeps demand substantial compute, researcher time, and funding
(
Mazumder et al. 2023
)
. We therefore seek an
ex ante
performance forecaster to help designers decide what to build and where to measure.
For example, consider a team proposing a binary “solver-choice” task on an ordinary differential equations benchmark (
Figure

1
;
Gaonkar et al. 2025
).
Before annotating a single example, they can ask a concrete what-if: will GPT-4.1
(
Achiam et al. 2023
)
without CoT
(
Wei et al. 2022
)
land nearer 30 or 60 Accuracy points? A forecast like this has the potential to quickly inform scope and spend—how large a pilot to run, which baselines to include, and whether the task merits full-scale annotation.

Figure 1:

Precog
pairs a redacted task/dataset description with its target metric. Given the inputs (a redacted description of dataset and an experimental configuration
(
Gaonkar et al. 2025
)
), the goal is to predict the target metric. The regressor conditions on performance-relevant details and may retrieve corroborating evidence from the literature.

Two adjacent lines of work on ML model performance prediction address related goals: (i)
missing-entry prediction
, which infers unobserved cells from observed ones
(
Xia et al. 2020
;
Ye et al. 2021
;
Ye et al. 2023
;
Zhao et al. 2025
)
; and (ii)
efficient evaluation
, which extrapolates full-benchmark performance from a small set of pilot runs
(
Maia Polo et al. 2024
;
Pacchiardi et al. 2024
;
Anugraha et al. 2025
)
. Both assume seed measurements or closely related tasks and typically rely on engineered numeric features. In contrast, we target a
zero-example, open-ended
setting in which retrieval is driven by natural-language task descriptions, supporting researchers who are creating new datasets or designing new experiments.
Prior work has also explored the capability of LLMs to forecast the outcomes of future geopolitical events in online prediction markets
(
Karger et al. 2024
;
Halawi et al. 2024
)
, however to the best of our knowledge, this is the first work to explore the capability of LLMs to predict their own performance on a new task based only on a text description.

We formulate text-only performance forecasting as regression: given a natural-language specification of the dataset and evaluation setup, including task, data provenance, prompting/decoding choices, and metric/protocol, we predict a target metric normalized to a
0
–
100
scale.
As in
Figure

1
, the input is a description of the proposed task and configuration; the output is a predicted metric value (e.g.,
32
). Unlike feature-engineered baselines, we operate directly on free-form, literature-style text and optionally use retrieval to consult related prior work, rather than interpolation from seed results.

To study this setting systematically, we curate

Precog
, a corpus of arXiv-mined experimental records
(
Park et al. 2025
)
that, for each record, pairs the dataset-source paper (introducing the benchmark) with the result-reporting paper (reporting the score).
From these sources, we synthesize redacted descriptions that preserve task and setup details while masking dataset names and numeric results.
At retrieval time, we exclude each record’s own source papers to limit leakage. To further probe residual leakage risks
(
Tian et al. 2024
)
, we also evaluate a streaming regime that ingests arXiv releases and makes predictions before they are indexed by search engines.

We then evaluate zero-shot LLM regressors, optionally augmented with literature search.
Given a redacted description, the model may iteratively issue evidence-seeking queries (ReAct-style
(
Yao et al. 2023
)
) and outputs a point estimate alongside a self-reported confidence
(
Yoon et al. 2025
)
.
We assess calibration by sweeping a confidence threshold and reporting the coverage–MAE trade-off, and we analyze search tool-use behavior.

On

Precog
, a proprietary reasoning model with literature retrieval attains MAE
14.0
across seven metric groups and
8.7
on the Accuracy subset at high-confidence thresholds, with reasonably calibrated uncertainty—indicating the task is hard but tractable.
The proprietary reasoning model employs diverse, iterative querying, whereas current open-source models underperform and exhibit shallower tool use.
In streaming prediction, GPT-5
(
OpenAI 2025
)
with built-in web search delivers competitive, though imperfect, performance on newly released datasets.

Altogether, our study shows that LLM-based regression models, which predict model performance from text descriptions, can help inform benchmark design, prioritize configurations, determine dataset sizes, and guide pilot experiment planning—while reducing the compute and time required for exploratory analysis. We release

Precog
, a redacted-description benchmark grounded in scientific literature, along with the accompanying prompts, protocols, and experimental code. We hope these resources and our evaluation methodology will support future advances in predictive model development and evaluation.

Related Work

Performance Prediction

Prior work predicts
missing table entries
from observed cells plus side information, using matrix-completion or response-surface models
(
Achille et al. 2019
;
Xia et al. 2020
;
Chen & Ritter 2021
;
Ye et al. 2021
;
Ye et al. 2023
;
Rabinovich et al. 2023
;
Shi et al. 2025
;
Zhao et al. 2025
)
.
These methods typically assume (i) seed results on related tasks, (ii) stable task identities, and (iii) overlap that enables interpolation.
A complementary line of work targets
efficient evaluation
: execute a small subset of measurements and extrapolate full-benchmark performance via learning-curve or proxy modeling
(
Maia Polo et al. 2024
;
Pacchiardi et al. 2024
;
Anugraha et al. 2025
)
.
These approaches still require running some evaluations on the target (or closely related) benchmark.

LLMs for Regression

Recent work explores LLMs as regressors in structured experimental settings, such as AutoML or black-box optimization
(
Song et al. 2024
;
Vacareanu et al. 2024
;
Nguyen et al. 2024
;
Requeima et al. 2024
;
Tang et al. 2024
)
.
OmniPred
(
Song et al. 2024
)
, for instance, trains LLMs on millions of hyperparameter optimization trajectories from Google’s internal Vizier database and demonstrates strong regression accuracy across diverse tasks.
However, these approaches rely on structured parameterizations (e.g., key–value hyperparameter strings) and assume access to seed trials or finetuning on the target task.
In contrast, we study a zero-example setting where the sole input is a free-form natural-language description of a novel dataset and evaluation protocol, supporting researchers who have not yet collected any results.

Extracting Structured Records from Litertaure

Prior work extracts structured result tuples (e.g., task, dataset, metric) from scientific articles
(
Singh et al. 2019
;
Hou et al. 2019
;
Kardas et al. 2020
;
Yang et al. 2022
;
Bai et al. 2023
;
Şahinuç et al. 2024
;
Park et al. 2025
)
.

Precog
builds on these tuples: for each experimental record, we synthesize a richer natural-language description that augments the tuple with additional context.

3
Task

Formulation

We study zero-example, text-only performance forecasting as a regression problem.
Given a self-contained, redacted description
x_{i}
of a dataset and evaluation configuration, the model performs reasoning and outputs a numeric estimate
\hat{y}_{i}\in[0,100]
.
We treat
x_{i}
as a structured yet free-form text description that captures contextual nuances of the task, dataset, and experimental configuration without requiring access to instances.
To compare heterogeneous metrics (e.g., Accuracy, F1, BLEU), we convert each paper-reported score
y_{i}^{\text{paper}}
to a common 0–100 scale via a simple normalization
(N:\mathbb{R}\!\to\![0,100])
;
y_{i}=N(y_{i}^{\text{paper}})
.
Concretely, values already on a 0–100 scale remain unchanged, while fractional scores in
([0,1])
are multiplied by 100 (e.g., Accuracy
(0.35\mapsto 35.0)
; F1
(83.2\mapsto 83.2)
.

Given a redacted description
x_{i}
, an LLM regressor
f_{\theta}
predicts the normalized target as
\hat{y}_{i}=f_{\theta}(x_{i},Z_{i})
, where
Z_{i}
is an optional set of retrieved evidence. We obtain
Z_{i}
by querying a time-bounded literature corpus
\mathcal{C}
with a top-
k
retriever
R_{k}
on a query formed from
x_{i}
, i.e.,
Z_{i}=R_{k}(q(x_{i}),\,\mathcal{C})
. Setting
k=0
yields the
description-only
variant
f_{\theta}(x_{i},\varnothing)
. At inference,
f_{\theta}
may iterate in a ReAct-style loop—reason
(
Yao et al. 2023
)
, issue
q(\cdot)
, retrieve
Z_{i}
, refine—and then output
\hat{y}_{i}
. For example, for the open-domain QA description above,
f_{\theta}
can retrieve prior exact-match (EM) ranges for similar setups and use them to refine
\hat{y}_{i}
.

Evaluation

Following prior work
(
Srinivasan et al. 2021
;
Ahuja et al. 2022
;
Jawahar et al. 2024
)
, we evaluate
f_{\theta}
with two standard criteria: mean absolute error (MAE) and the Pearson correlation
\mathrm{r}
between
y_{i}
and
\hat{y}_{i}
.
MAE (lower is better) measures absolute accuracy across heterogeneous metrics, while
\mathrm{r}
(higher is better) assesses whether predictions preserve the relative ordering of tasks/configurations.
Used together, they capture both value accuracy and trend fidelity despite differences in metric scales and variances—an evaluation protocol that is standard for performance prediction.

4
Data Curation

To evaluate at scale, we need a large number of diverse and realistic, open-ended pairs
(x_{i},y_{i})
.
Rather than synthesizing experimental configurations and running automated experiments, which is both costly and unlikely to capture the kinds of problems practitioners actually investigate, we semi-automatically curate experimental configurations and their reported results directly from the scientific literature.
Our pipeline harvests redacted task descriptions and reported scores from published papers; humans intervene only for precision-critical steps.
We follow the pipeline in
Figure

2
: (i) collect arXiv experimental records (
section

4.1
); (ii) define the record provenance and paper types (
section

4.2
); (iii) recover each record’s sources (
section

4.3
); (iv) extract a schema-aligned, redacted description (
section

4.4
); and (v) perform validation and quality control (
section

4.5
).

4.1
Source Corpus

We build off of LLMEvalDB
(
Park et al. 2025
)
, a structured arXiv-mined repository (2023–2024) dominated by non-finetunable, proprietary models—suited to isolating dataset/task effects with less model/method variance.
Each
experimental record
is a table cell with the dataset name, configuration context, and reported metric.
Values are normalized to define
y_{i}
.
We consider seven metrics with natural [0,100] scales: Accuracy, F1, Recall, Precision, ROUGE, BLEU, and EM.

4.2
Record Provenance and Paper Types

Each record is grounded in two literature sources: the result paper, which reports the score, and the dataset paper, which introduced the dataset.
These may coincide or differ.
For example, Paper A introduces a QA dataset and reports accuracy for several models (result paper = dataset paper).
Later, Paper B evaluates a new prompting/evaluation protocol on the same dataset under a revised setup (result paper
\neq
dataset paper)
(
Park et al. 2021
;
Rangapur & Rangapur 2024
)
.

4.3
Dataset Source Paper Retrieval

To compile faithful descriptions for each record, we first collect its sources. LLMEvalDB
(
Park et al. 2025
)
provides the result papers.
When the result paper differs from the dataset paper, we automatically recover the latter by inferring the citation with an LLM, resolving it to an arXiv ID, downloading the PDF, and verifying the dataset name/title.
We then run a rule-based check to confirm that the PDF explicitly mentions the dataset (string match).
Because source fidelity is critical, any remaining errors are corrected via targeted human edits.

4.4
Extracting Redacted Input Description

Given the sources for the result and dataset papers, an extractor LLM produces a schema-aligned, redacted description
x_{i}
that summarizes the
task
(problem definition and input/label format),
data collection
(provenance, domain, collection protocol),
evaluation
(metric and protocol),
difficulty cues
(number of classes, long context, reasoning depth), the relevant
subset/split
, the
prompting strategy
(zero/few-shot, CoT), and any
other record-specific settings
(context-window, tool/retrieval use), when reported; see the
Appendix

D
for illustrative examples.

We systematically remove the dataset names, performance numbers, and statistics.
To further reduce extraction-time leakage, a second LLM rewriting pass is applied and the same checks are re-enforced, yielding faithful, self-contained, anonymized inputs
x_{i}
.

5
Experiments

Baselines

We compare against three families of baselines.
First, we report the overall test-split mean across all metrics (
Section

5.1
) and the per-metric test-split means (
Section

5.2
). Because no training or validation set is available, the test-set average serves as a strong baseline for comparison.
Second, we evaluate text-encoder–based regressors.
We embed each description and train classical models—k-nearest neighbors
(
Guo et al. 2003
)
and XGBoost
(
Chen & Guestrin 2016
)
—on these embeddings.
With no dedicated training set, we use k-fold cross-validation
(
Fushiki 2011
)
: fit on k-1 folds, evaluate on the held-out fold, and average over folds.
For embeddings, we use E5-Mistral-7B
(
Wang et al. 2023
)
, a leading open-source encoder.

LLMs

We evaluate both proprietary and open-source models in reasoning and non-reasoning settings to isolate the contribution of latent chain-of-thought style reasoning from raw generation quality.
On the proprietary side we use GPT-5
(
OpenAI 2025
)
as a reasoning model.
On the open-source side we include reasoning-enabled model Qwen3-32B
(
Yang et al. 2025
)
, alongside non-reasoning models Qwen2.5-32B, Qwen2.5-72B
(
Team 2024
)
.
Using matched prompts and decoding settings across these pairs lets us attribute gains specifically to reasoning capabilities.
We prompt the model to output its final estimate in
boxed{}
with a brief rationale, then parse the boxed value during post-processing.
Predictions are produced via greedy decoding with default generation settings.

All models share a single custom retriever over the arXiv API

Consequently, despite higher processing cost, we rely on the custom retriever for GPT-5.

In the previous sections, we demonstrated that LLMs can generate accurate predictions from dataset descriptions.
A natural next question is how these predictions compare to those made by human experts.
To explore this, we asked six graduate students and one professor of computer science, all specializing in NLP and machine learning, to each predict 20 accuracies based solely on the same text descriptions provided to models.
On this 140-item subset, human predictions correlated moderately with ground truth (

) and outperformed the mean baseline in terms of MAE (19.6 vs. 21.7).
Nevertheless, GPT
-

Although human forecasters might improve on this task with additional training
(
Tetlock et al. 2014
)

7
Streaming Prediction

Using

Precog
with a leakage-mitigating custom retriever, GPT-5 outperforms the per-metric test-set mean baseline. Nevertheless, residual answer leakage cannot be entirely excluded—for instance, when subsequent papers copy baseline results from the original publication, or when models memorize results during pre- or post-training.
To further validate our experimental setup under more realistic conditions, we adopt a
streaming prediction
setting, which tests models on their ability to predict performance on newly reported arXiv results immediately upon release, before they can be indexed by search engines or incorporated into training.

Conclusion & Discussion

Conclusion.

We recast evaluation as
forecasting
and introduce

Precog
, a leakage-mitigating corpus of redacted, schema-aligned description–performance pairs. Search-augmented reasoning with GPT-5 produces accurate forecasts with calibrated uncertainty, and remains effective in a streaming setting where results are predicted for newly released papers prior to broad indexing. These findings indicate that anticipatory evaluation is feasible and practically useful for estimating task difficulty, prioritizing experiments, and reducing compute.

Discussion: limits of current open-source models.

Despite progress, open-source baselines lag behind GPT-5 in both accuracy and calibration. They show brittle tool use (inconsistent gains from retrieval), weaker representations of fine-grained cues in long descriptions, and higher variance across metric families (e.g., small-variance metrics like BLEU/ROUGE). Improved query planning, evidence selection, and uncertainty modeling are needed for reliable deployment.

Discussion: feasibility and utility.

Text-only description
\rightarrow
performance forecasting is viable “in the wild.” As a decision aid, calibrated predictions can (i) triage experiments before running them, (ii) allocate compute toward high-yield configurations, and (iii) surface risky or low-payoff settings early. In aggregate, this supports faster iteration cycles and more principled experimental design.

Outlook: toward specialized forecasting models.

To accelerate scientific discovery, we advocate purpose-built regressors that leverage LLM reasoning.
In particular,
reasoning-aware regression
trains a regression model on top of reasonig LLMs, while
better use of retreival tool
focuses on learned query planning and selective evidence use.
Developing these components on top of

Precog
and the streaming benchmark should yield more accurate, trustworthy, and cost-efficient forecasting systems.

Ethics Statement

We rely exclusively on publicly available research artifacts (e.g., arXiv PDFs and published results); no human subjects or personally identifiable data are involved, and all usage follows licenses and provider terms.
To reduce leakage, we redact dataset identities and block retrieval of each record’s table-source and dataset-source papers; because residual cues may persist, we also evaluate a time-gated, zero-leakage setting on genuinely unseen datasets.
Focusing on arXiv means some records lack full experimental details, which can introduce label noise; to aid auditing, we release venue metadata per record and document known issues.
Our strongest results use large proprietary models, raising equity and environmental concerns; we therefore report compute, provide open baselines, and encourage reproduction with smaller models.
Finally, the corpus is predominantly English, limiting generality; expanding multilingual coverage is an important direction for future work.

Appendix B
Annotation Guidelines

B.1
Description Quality

This section details the human annotation protocol used to evaluate the quality of redacted, schema-aligned descriptions against their source PDFs (
\mathrm{TSP}_{i}
,
\mathrm{DSP}_{i}
).
Annotators were instructed to verify that each description is anonymized, complete where possible, and strictly grounded in the provided sources.

Annotation Task

Annotators were given:

•

The extracted input description for a given experimental record.

•

The corresponding table-source and dataset-source PDFs.

The task was to check whether the description faithfully reflects information from the PDFs, is anonymized, and adheres to the fixed schema.

B.2
Evaluation Criteria

Three dimensions of quality were assessed:

Anonymization (Binary).

Descriptions must not include dataset or subset names, and must exclude performance numbers drawn from the main paper.
Annotators assign either
Accept
(score: 1.0) or
Reject
(score: 0.0).

Schema coverage (1–5).

Checks whether all schema fields are filled appropriately when information is available in the PDFs, and “None” is used only when the information is truly absent.

•

Score 1
: Ignores the schema; most fields missing or collapsed.

•

Score 2
: Very few fields filled; many omissions; “None” often misused.

•

Score 3
: About half of fields filled; some omissions or misplacements.

•

Score 4
: Nearly all fields correctly covered; rare omissions or minor misplacements.

•

Score 5
: Complete and consistent coverage; “None” only when truly absent; perfectly aligned with schema.

Factual grounding (1–5).

Ensures every statement in the description is directly supported by the PDFs, without speculation or outside knowledge.

•

Score 1
: Mostly unsupported or invented; contradictions with PDFs.

•

Score 2
: Frequent unsupported claims; weak sourcing.

•

Score 3
: Generally grounded, but several unsupported claims or mild over-interpretation.

•

Score 4
: Fully grounded with only minor overstatements; no contradictions.

•

Score 5
: Entirely faithful; precise paraphrases of the PDFs; every fact traceable.

B.3
Human Performance Prediction

This subsection specifies the human annotation protocol for predicting task performance from redacted, schema-aligned descriptions. Annotators produce a single accuracy estimate and a brief rationale
without consulting external sources
, enabling an assessment of human forecasting difficulty and behavior under text-only constraints.

Annotation Task

Annotators are given:

•

A redacted description of an experimental setup (model, dataset/task, evaluation protocol, and salient configuration details).

•

A fixed-time budget per item and an interactive prompt that records their identity (first name) and assigns a shard automatically.

For each item, annotators must output:

•

Predicted accuracy
(integer in 1–100).

•

Rationale
(1–3 sentences) briefly justifying the estimate.

Instructions and Constraints

•

No external search.
Rely solely on the provided description and personal prior knowledge; do not use web search, papers, or tools.

•

Timing.
Aim to complete each prediction within 2–3 minutes.

•

Batch size.
Each annotator completes 20 samples per session.
