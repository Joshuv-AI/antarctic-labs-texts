Safety and Tolerability of Ruxolitinib Cream in Adolescents with Mild-to-Moderate Atopic Dermatitis: Results From a 1-Year, Phase III, Open-Label Study
Authors: Joel C. Joyce, H. Chih-ho Hong, Dareen D. Siri, Darryl Toth, Howard Kallender, YuTzu Kuo, Diana Stefani-Hunyady, Kim A. Papp
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRuxolitinib cream (1.5%) has demonstrated efficacy and safety in patients aged ≥ 2 years with mild-to-moderate atopic dermatitis.ObjectiveWe aimed to further investigate the safety and efficacy of ruxolitinib cream in adolescents with atopic dermatitis in an open-label study.MethodsPatients aged 12–17 years with atopic dermatitis, an Investigator’s Global Assessment score of 2/3, and 3–20% affected body surface area applied twice-daily 1.5% ruxolitinib cream for an 8-week continuous treatment period, followed by a 44-week long-term safety period in which ruxolitinib cream was applied twice daily as needed. Safety was the primary endpoint. Secondary endpoints included plasma trough concentrations of ruxolitinib. Affected body surface area, ≥ 75% improvement from baseline in Eczema Area and Severity Index, achievement of Investigator’s Global Assessment score of 0/1, and ≥ 4-point improvement from baseline in the itch Numerical Rating Scale were exploratory endpoints.ResultsOf 103 patients, 59.2% had a baseline Investigator’s Global Assessment of 3. Mean baseline body surface area and Eczema Area and Severity Index scores were 8.9% and 6.4, respectively. Over 52 weeks, 42 patients (40.8%) had treatment-emergent adverse events, most commonly upper respiratory tract infection (10.7%) and nasopharyngitis (8.7%). Three patients (2.9%) had grade ≥ 3 treatment-emergent adverse events; all were considered unrelated to treatment by the investigator. Steady-state ruxolitinib plasma concentrations during the continuous treatment period were low (geometric mean [geometric coefficient of variation], 14.2 [169] nM), consistent with the lack of observed treatment-emergent adverse events associated with systemic Janus kinase inhibition. Clinical improvements occurred during the continuous treatment period and were maintained or improved with as-needed treatment through the long-term safety period to week 52 (mean affected body surface area, 1.3%; ≥ 75% improvement from baseline in Eczema Area and Severity Index, 87.0%; Investigator’s Global Assessment 0/1, 82.6%; ≥ 4-point improvement from baseline in itch Numerical Rating Scale, 41.7%).ConclusionsRuxolitinib cream (1.5%) was well tolerated and efficacious with long-term as-needed use in adolescents with mild-to-moderate atopic dermatitis, consistent with safety and efficacy findings reported in previous phase III studies in adolescents and adults.Clinical Trial RegistrationClinicaltrials.gov identifier, NCT05456529 (registered 13 July, 2022).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40257-026-01043-0.

Key Points

In this phase III, open-label study, twice-daily application of 1.5% ruxolitinib cream was well tolerated and efficacious in adolescents (aged 12–17 years) with atopic dermatitis, with continuous application for 8 weeks followed by long-term as-needed application for 44 weeks.Low steady-state plasma concentrations of ruxolitinib, no observable hematologic effects, and no treatment-emergent adverse events associated with systemic Janus kinase inhibition indicate a low likelihood of systemic Janus kinase inhibition.The safety and efficacy outcomes of this study were generally consistent with those previously observed in children (aged 2–11 years), adolescents (aged 12–17 years), and adults (aged ≥ 18 years) with mild-to-moderate atopic dermatitis.

Introduction

Atopic dermatitis (AD) is a chronic, pruritic, inflammatory skin disease that typically commences in childhood and may persist into adolescence and adulthood [1, 2]. There is an unmet need for safe and effective treatments for mild-to-moderate AD among adolescents. Topical corticosteroid alternatives, such as topical calcineurin inhibitors, topical phosphodiesterase-4 inhibitors, and tapinarof (a topical aryl hydrocarbon receptor agonist), may have limited efficacy and/or tolerability concerns in some patients [3–8]. Thus, there remains an unmet need for a corticosteroid-free treatment option for adolescents with mild-to-moderate AD that provides long-term clinical benefit with a well-tolerated safety profile.

Ruxolitinib (selective Janus kinase [JAK]1 and JAK2 inhibitor [9]) cream (1.5%) demonstrated superior anti-inflammatory and antipruritic effects versus vehicle and was well tolerated in patients with mild-to-moderate AD in the phase III TRuE-AD1/TRuE-AD2/TRuE-AD3 (NCT03745638/NCT03745651/NCT04921969) studies [10–13], supporting its approval by the US Food and Drug Administration and Health Canada for patients aged ≥ 2 years [14, 15]. In TRuE-AD1/TRuE-AD2, the efficacy and safety of ruxolitinib cream in adolescents (aged 12–17 years) were consistent with that of the overall study population (aged ≥ 12 years) [16].

Twice-daily (BID) application of ruxolitinib cream was also efficacious and well tolerated under maximum use conditions in patients aged ≥ 12 years (≥ 25% affected body surface area [BSA; NCT03920852]) [17] and in patients aged 2–11 years (≥ 35% affected BSA; NCT05034822) [18]. Additionally, ruxolitinib cream demonstrated efficacy in patients with head and neck involvement in a sub-analysis from TRuE-AD1/TRuE-AD2 [19], in patients with moderate AD (10–20% affected BSA) whose treatment with topical corticosteroids/topical calcineurin inhibitors was ineffective or not tolerated and who were otherwise eligible for systemic therapy (TRuE-AD4; NCT06238817) [20], and in a decentralized phase II study of patients with facial and/or neck AD (NCT05127421) [21]. Itch relief was demonstrated as early as 15 minutes after ruxolitinib cream application in TRuE-AD4 [20] and in the phase II SCRATCH-AD open-label trial (NCT04839380) [22]. The present study further investigated the safety, tolerability, and efficacy of the Food and Drug Administration-approved regimen of 1.5% ruxolitinib cream BID in adolescents with mild-to-moderate AD.

Methods

Patients and Study Design

This was a phase III, single-arm study conducted at 25 sites across the USA and Canada (NCT05456529; Fig. 1 of the Electronic Supplementary Material [ESM]). The study was conducted in adolescents aged 12–17 years having AD for ≥ 2 years, an Investigator’s Global Assessment (IGA) score of 2 or 3, and an affected BSA of 3–20% (excluding the scalp) whose AD was not adequately controlled with prescription topical therapies or for whom those therapies were not advised. Key exclusion criteria included patients with an unstable disease course (spontaneous improvement or rapid deterioration) in the 4 weeks prior to the baseline visit, a concurrent and/or history of an immunocompromised condition, use of biologic agents within 12 weeks or five half-lives of the baseline visit (whichever is longer), use of systemic corticosteroids within 4 weeks of the baseline visit, or use of other topical AD treatments other than bland emollients within 1 week of the baseline visit.

All patients applied 1.5% ruxolitinib cream BID for an 8-week continuous treatment (CT) period in which all areas with AD lesions at baseline were treated, regardless of whether lesions resolved. The CT period was followed by a 44-week long-term safety (LTS) period in which ruxolitinib cream BID was applied only as needed to active AD lesions (IGA ≥ 1); patients continued to apply cream BID for 3 days after lesion clearance (IGA = 0). Treatment of new or expanded AD lesions was permitted during either period with investigator approval, provided that the total affected BSA did not exceed 20%.

Assessments

The primary endpoint was long-term safety and tolerability, assessed as the incidence and severity of treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs). Treatment-emergent adverse events of special interest included those associated with systemic JAK inhibition (serious infections, malignancies, major adverse cardiovascular events, thromboses, and mortality), as well as those associated with other AD treatments (contact dermatitis, diarrhea, folliculitis, headache, nausea, skin atrophy, and striae) and AD in general (eczema herpeticum and herpes zoster).

Secondary endpoints included trough concentrations of ruxolitinib from blood samples at weeks 2 and 4 and laboratory values for hematologic parameters. Exploratory efficacy endpoints included percentage of affected BSA, the proportion of patients with a ≥ 75% improvement in Eczema Area and Severity Index (EASI) from baseline (EASI-75), the proportion of patients with a total body or facial IGA score of 0 (clear) or 1 (almost clear) at each post-baseline visit, and the proportion of patients achieving daily and by-visit ≥ 4-point improvements in the itch Numerical Rating Scale score from baseline (itch NRS4).

Statistical Analyses

The target sample size of 100 patients was selected to optimize detection of adverse events of interest (cytopenias, herpes zoster, liver function test elevations, major adverse cardiovascular events, malignancies, nonmelanoma skin neoplasms, thrombocytosis and elevated mean platelet volume, venous and arterial thromboembolic events, and viral skin infections); the study was not powered for formal hypothesis testing of efficacy endpoints. Efficacy and safety analyses included all enrolled patients who applied ruxolitinib cream at least once. Patients in the pharmacokinetic-evaluable population applied ruxolitinib cream at least once and provided one or more post-baseline pharmacokinetic sample. Patients in the hematology-evaluable population applied ruxolitinib cream at least once and provided a baseline and one or more post-baseline hematology blood sample. Treatment-emergent adverse events were described according to the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities, Version 27.0, with severity based on Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events, Version 5.0 grades 1–5. Clinical laboratory parameters (including pharmacokinetic and hematologic values) and efficacy endpoints were described using descriptive statistics. All data are reported as observed. For the itch NRS4 analysis, only patients with an itch NRS score ≥ 4 at baseline were included; if ≥ 4 daily itch scores over the past 7 days were missing, data for the study visit were considered missing.

Results

Patients

Patients (N = 103) had a median (range) age of 14 (12–17) years; 54.4% of patients were female, 44.7% were White, and 30.1% were Black (Table 1). The CT period was completed by 95 patients (92.2%). Of 92 patients who applied ruxolitinib cream in the LTS period, 67 patients (72.8%) completed the LTS period (Fig. 2 of the ESM). The most common reason for discontinuation in the CT and LTS periods was loss to follow-up (n = 4 and n = 9, respectively).

Safety

Ruxolitinib cream was well tolerated throughout the 52-week study, with a low frequency of application-site reactions and no new safety signals emerging in the LTS period relative to the CT period (Table 2). Twenty-one (20.4%) and 30 patients (32.6%) experienced a TEAE during the CT and LTS periods, respectively. The most common TEAEs throughout the full 52-week study were upper respiratory tract infection (n = 11 [10.7%]) and nasopharyngitis (n = 9 [8.7%]). Grade ≥ 3 TEAEs were reported in three patients (2.9%; exacerbation of eczema on the face, worsening depression in a patient with a history of depression, mycoplasma pneumonia and asthmatic crisis in a patient with a history of asthma) throughout the study; all were considered by the investigator to be unrelated to treatment. The worsening of pre-existing depression and mycoplasma pneumonia cases were classified as serious TEAEs; no other serious TEAEs were observed. No cases of contact dermatitis, cytopenia, eczema herpeticum, folliculitis, herpes zoster, liver function test elevation, major adverse cardiovascular events, malignancy, nonmelanoma skin neoplasms, nausea, skin atrophy, striae, thrombocytosis, or thrombosis were observed during the study, nor were there any fatalities. Diarrhea and viral skin infections (herpes simplex, n = 1; molluscum contagiosum, n = 1) each occurred in two patients (1.9%), and headache occurred in one patient (1.0%); none of these events was considered related to treatment. Of the 60 patients with facial AD (IGA > 0 at baseline), three had application-site reactions on the face (grade 1 application-site pain that resolved and did not recur [ruxolitinib cream interruption], grade 1 hyperpigmentation of the right periorbital region that was resolving [no change in dose], and grade 2 application-site acne that did not resolve [no change in dose]).

Treatment-related adverse events were reported in four patients (application-site acne, application-site pain, pustule, and staphylococcal infection; n = 1 each); all were mild or moderate in severity. One patient discontinued treatment because of a TEAE (AD flare) during the LTS period; this was recorded as withdrawal by patient (Fig. 2 of the ESM). Treatment-emergent adverse events leading to treatment interruption occurred in five patients (4.9%) during the CT period and no patients during the LTS period (Table 2). There were no clinically meaningful trends in hematologic parameters (Fig. 3 of the ESM). Transient increases in creatine phosphokinase were observed in some patients; events were considered not to be associated with clinical symptoms (may have been due to physical exercise) and resolved without intervention.

Pharmacokinetics

The geometric mean (geometric coefficient of variation) steady-state ruxolitinib plasma concentration during the CT period was 14.2 (169) nM. No single plasma concentration value for any patient exceeded the threshold associated with JAK-mediated myelosuppression (50% inhibitory concentration of 281 nM using in vitro assays with adult whole blood [9]; Fig. 1). Ruxolitinib plasma concentrations were comparable between age groups (12–15 years vs 16–17 years), baseline IGA scores (2 vs 3), and visits, with no accumulation in ruxolitinib concentration from week 2 to week 4 of the CT period. Efficacy

Application of 1.5% ruxolitinib cream resulted in a mean reduction of affected BSA from 8.9% at baseline to 4.9% at the first post-baseline visit (week 2) and 2.5% by the end of the CT period (week 8); affected BSA remained low with as-needed use throughout the 44-week LTS period, with a slight improvement by week 52 (1.3%; Fig. 2). EASI-75 was achieved by 40.2% of patients at the first post-baseline visit (week 2) and 68.1% of patients by the end of the CT period (week 8); improvement was maintained throughout the LTS period, with 87.0% of patients achieving EASI-75 at week 52 (Fig. 3). The proportion of patients who achieved facial and total body IGA 0/1 (clear/almost clear skin) was 65.3% and 51.0%, respectively, at the first post-baseline visit (week 2) and increased to 83.7% and 80.0%, respectively, by week 8; improvements were similarly maintained through the LTS period and were 91.4% and 82.6%, respectively, at week 52 (Fig. 4). With continuous application of 1.5% ruxolitinib cream, daily itch NRS4 was achieved by 34.7% of patients at day 7 and 59.3% of patients at day 28 (Fig. 5). Discussion

Ruxolitinib cream was previously evaluated in two pivotal phase III studies of adolescents and adults with mild-to-moderate AD [10, 11]. In a pooled subpopulation of 245 adolescent patients aged 12–17 years from these studies, 92 patients applied BID 1.5% ruxolitinib cream [16]. At week 8, more adolescent patients who applied 1.5% ruxolitinib cream versus vehicle achieved clear or almost clear skin (IGA 0/1; 68.8% vs 28.6%), EASI-75 (60.9% vs 34.9%), and itch NRS4 (52.1% vs 17.4%); improvements were maintained with continued as-needed use through week 52. Ruxolitinib cream was also well tolerated throughout the 52-week study period, with no serious TEAEs reported [16].

The present study further investigated the safety and efficacy of the approved 1.5% strength of ruxolitinib cream in an adolescent patient population. In adolescents with mild-to-moderate AD, 1.5% ruxolitinib cream BID was well tolerated with continuous application for 8 weeks followed by long-term as-needed application for 44 weeks. The TEAEs observed during the 44-week LTS period relative to the 8-week CT period can be attributed to the longer follow-up time and should not be considered as new safety signals. Aside from one serious infection that was judged by the investigator as unrelated to treatment (mycoplasma pneumonia in a patient with a history of asthma), no TEAEs typically associated with systemic JAK inhibition were observed. There were few or no occurrences of application-site reactions, contact dermatitis, eczema herpeticum, folliculitis, headache, nausea, skin atrophy, or striae, which may occur with some other topical therapies (e.g., tapinarof cream [8], phosphodiesterase-4 inhibitor creams [3, 6, 23–25], and topical corticosteroids/topical calcineurin inhibitors [26, 27]). The safety profile of ruxolitinib cream observed in this study was consistent with that in the adolescent subpopulation and the overall population in the TRuE-AD1/TRuE-AD2 studies [10, 11, 16] and thus provides further evidence of safety and tolerability in adolescents.

Plasma concentrations of ruxolitinib cream were generally consistent with those previously observed in adolescents and adults (aged ≥ 12 years; TRuE-AD1/TRuE-AD2) [10, 16, 28] and children (aged 2–11 years; TRuE-AD3) [12]. Low ruxolitinib plasma concentrations, the absence of observable effects on hematologic parameters, and the low frequency or absence of TEAEs associated with JAK inhibition suggest that systemic JAK inhibition is highly unlikely [10, 13].

Long-term as-needed use of ruxolitinib cream maintained good disease control. Improvements in clinical signs and symptoms (affected BSA, EASI, IGA, itch) were generally consistent with previous findings in adolescents and adults with AD [10, 11, 16]. Ruxolitinib cream was also efficacious for the 60 patients with facial involvement at baseline, with ≥ 70% of patients achieving facial IGA 0/1 throughout the LTS period. Ruxolitinib cream was generally well tolerated in these patients, which is consistent with previous studies [19, 21]. Thus, the present findings provide evidence of safety and disease control in adolescents who did not achieve adequate disease control with other prescription topical therapies or for whom those therapies are not advised. This aligns with prescribing information from the Food and Drug Administration and Health Canada for 1.5% ruxolitinib cream [14, 15].

Regarding study limitations, this was an open-label single-arm study with a small number of patients; enrollment was only in North America, which may limit the generalizability of the data. In addition, detailed analysis of as-needed use of ruxolitinib cream is not available for this study. However, as-needed use (e.g., time off treatment, time to maintain and/or recapture clear skin) among adolescent and adult patients was described in the TRuE-AD1/TRuE-AD2 studies [29].

Conclusions

In this phase III, open-label, 1-year study, ruxolitinib cream was well tolerated in adolescent patients with mild-to-moderate AD. Twice-daily application of 1.5% ruxolitinib cream demonstrated rapid improvement of signs and symptoms with continuous use. The safety and tolerability of ruxolitinib cream enabled patients to extend improvements into the longer term with as-needed use. Results from this study provide additional data in the adolescent population, confirming efficacy and safety findings reported in the phase III TRuE-AD1/TRuE-AD2 studies in adolescents and adults [10, 11, 16]. Ruxolitinib cream may therefore represent a potential corticosteroid-free option for adolescents with mild-to-moderate AD that provides sustained clinical benefit.
