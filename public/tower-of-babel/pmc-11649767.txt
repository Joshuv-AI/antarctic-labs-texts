Forceps minor control of social behaviour
Authors: Franziska Stoller, Eleanor Hinds, Tudor Ionescu, Elmira Khatamsaz, Hugh M. Marston, Bastian Hengerer
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The PRISM project, funded by the EU’s Innovative Medicines Initiative, has identified a transdiagnostic, pathophysiological relationship between the integrity of the default mode network (DMN) and social dysfunction. To explore the causal link between DMN integrity and social behaviour, we employed a preclinical back-translation approach, using focal demyelination of the forceps minor to disrupt DMN connectivity in mice. By applying advanced techniques such as functional ultrasound imaging and automated analysis of social behaviour, we demonstrated that reduced DMN connectivity leads to impaired social interactions and increased anxiety in mice. Notably, these effects were reversible, indicating that the forceps minor, a critical fibre tract connecting key DMN regions in the prefrontal cortex, plays a crucial role in social function. This study provides direct evidence for a causal relationship between DMN integrity and social dysfunction, with potential implications for developing targeted treatments in precision psychiatry.

Introduction

Social relationships, interactions, and overall social behaviour are essential for evolutionary survival, particularly in primates1,2. It has been suggested that the neocortex of primates expands during evolution due to social pressure experienced within groups3. However, the increased degree of specialization and complexity of the ‘social brain’, which has evolved to address ecological challenges at a group-level4, may result in increased vulnerability to pathogenic interventions. Interestingly, early indicators of various neuropsychiatric diseases, such as social withdrawal, loneliness, and interpersonal dysfunction, can be observed before the main symptoms appear5. These specific indicators could function as transdiagnostic markers, facilitating early detection of psychiatric diseases and enabling the provision of personalized treatment.

The EU-funded Psychiatric Ratings using Intermediate Stratified Markers project (PRISM)6 focuses on biological parameters related to Schizophrenia (SZ), Alzheimer’s Disease (AD), Major Depressive Disorder (MDD), and social functioning. Although the pathophysiology behind these diseases is heterogenous, their symptomatology in relation to social dysfunction is very similar7–10. Based on these findings, one hypothesis of PRISM states that dysfunction of the Default Mode Network (DMN)11 could potentially link social impairment with these neuropathophysiologies. The DMN primarily operates during wakeful rest12 and is also involved in social behaviour, as evidenced by its overlap with the ‘social brain’2,13. Moreover, the DMN actively contributes to various cognitive processes, including social cognition, which involves the psychological process of perceiving, interpreting, and responding to social stimuli. Additionally, mind-wandering (often described as the drift of thoughts away from the task at hand, particularly during routine or well-practiced activities11), and Theory of Mind (a cognitive ability to attribute mental states to oneself and other people14) are important examples for DMN involvement. In all these cognitive processes, the human DMN, consisting of the ventral medial prefrontal cortex (vMPFC), posterior cingulate/retrosplenial cortex (PCC/Rsp), inferior parietal lobule (IPL), lateral temporal cortex (LTC), dorsal medial prefrontal cortex (dMPFC), and the hippocampal formation (HF)12, plays a major role.

In addition to various clinical observations describing an implication of DMN in social functioning15,16, a PRISM-study7 demonstrated a correlation between altered structural DMN connectivity and social dysfunction in SZ, AD, and healthy control (HC) participants. Notably, the rostromedial prefrontal cortex exhibited an association between social malfunction and abnormal white-matter integrity, regardless of previously diagnosed pathology or presumed healthiness. Another study17 linked perceived social support with reduced mean diffusivity during diffusion magnetic resonance imaging in the forceps minor (fmi), an interhemispheric fibre tract that forms the genu of the corpus callosum (CC) (Fig. 1a), and links both orbitofrontal cortices (OFCs). As an evolutionarily conserved brain structure18, the fmi is also part of the DMN19. To substantiate the clinical findings of a correlation between social dysfunction and reduced DMN white matter integrity in a preclinical setting, we developed a mouse model of impaired fmi integrity (Fig. 1b). To this end, we focused on manipulating the fmi-forming white matter tracts and specifically targeted the myelinating oligodendrocytes.

Lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC, Lysolecithin) is an endogenous lysophospholipid21 and its levels have been shown to correlate with ageing22, obesity23, atherosclerosis, and inflammatory diseases24. Furthermore, LPC is widely used to study focal de- and remyelination in the CNS25–28. Following local injection, LPC integrates into cellular membranes and non-specifically disrupts myelin lipids29. Locally injected LPC is cleared after 24 h, and remyelination of the lesion site begins around day 10 post-injection.

Functional ultrasound30 (fUS) and radio-frequency identification (RFID)-assisted SocialScan31 were applied to investigate the functional and behavioural outcome of transient fmi demyelination. The social arena enabled us to monitor complex behaviours of individual mice within a social group during the demyelination and remyelination phases. The behavioural data were complemented by fUS imaging of functional DMN connectivity. The preclinical dataset supports the main clinical findings of PRISM by demonstrating that transient fmi demyelination impairs DMN integrity, leading to a reversible reduction of social interactions. The findings offer new options for the development of precision psychiatry treatments of social dysfunction.

Results

Effect of forceps minor demyelination on DMN functional connectivity

To investigate whether LPC-induced demyelination (Fig. 1c) in the fmi impacts functional connectivity between prefrontal brain regions, we recorded brain activity by high-resolution fUS on day seven post-LPC injection (Fig. 1d). In a three-step scan, a volume was recorded, covering the bilateral orbital area, secondary motor cortex, prelimbic area, and agranular insular area (Fig. 1e,f). These brain regions, except for the agranular insular area, are part of the DMN. A pair-wise analysis between all regions, based on Pearson’s correlation, is depicted in matrix Fig. 1g. Compared to the control group (n = 12), LPC-treated individuals (n = 14) exhibited overall reduced correlation patterns. Specifically, correlation between the left prelimbic and the right orbital area was significantly decreased in LPC-treated animals (*p < 0.05, r = Fisher’s z-transformed Pearson’s correlation coefficient), as was the interaction between the right secondary motor and left prelimbic area (*p < 0.05, r = Fisher’s z-transformed Pearson’s correlation coefficient). Interestingly, the left secondary motor area appeared to be predominantly affected by the treatment. Correlations between this region and all other scanned DMN connected areas were significantly compromised. Furthermore, correlation values between this region and the orbital area even withstood FDR-correction, while the insular region, a non-DMN region, remained unaffected. By transforming the correlation coefficients into z-scores using Fisher’s Z-transformation, a group-level connectivity matrix also revealed the effect of LPC treatment on functional connectivity (Supplementary Fig. S1 online). As already demonstrated by Pearson’s correlation, the most significant effect was observed between the left secondary motor cortex and all DMN connected regions. Overall, animals injected with LPC exhibited reduced functional connectivity between DMN sub-regions in the coronal plane through the fmi.

A more detailed correlation analysis, focusing on the global, functional DMN connectivity, including the orbital area, prelimbic area and secondary motor cortex in both hemispheres was significantly reduced after demyelination of the fmi (**p < 0.01, unpaired t-test) (Fig. 1h). Functional connectivity between left and right orbital areas through the fmi (Fig. 1i), was not significantly decreased in LPC-treated individuals (p = 0.3136, unpaired t-test).

Comparing the strength of filtered CBV signals in the left secondary motor cortex and the right orbital area revealed a strong linear relationship between these values in control animals (r = 0.64, p < 0.0001) (Fig. 1j and Supplementary Fig. S1 online). After LPC-induced DMN impairment, blood flow in both the left secondary motor cortex and the right orbital area changed and their interdependence was lost (r = 0.1, n.s.) (Fig. 1k and Supplementary Fig. S1 online).

We focused our analyses on the peak activity period of the dark phase and calculated the mean values of sniffing and approaching for each individual animal in two three-hour time blocks. The social sniff parameter did not show a significant difference between LPC-treated and control groups in the first three hours of the dark phase. In the subsequent period until midnight, the animals injected with LPC significantly reduced their sniffing time (***p < 0.001, One-way ANOVA corrected by Bonferroni post-hoc test) (Fig. 2c). Focusing on social approach, a coherent pattern compared to social sniff can be observed. In the first three hours, animals in both cohorts approached other individuals at a similar frequency. In the second time block of a further three hours again, the animals with fmi demyelination significantly reduced their approaching times (**p < 0.01, One-way ANOVA corrected by Bonferroni post-hoc test) (Fig. 2d).

To substantiate this finding, we focused on the behaviour of individual animals. Z-scores, calculated based on the control group, were analysed in 30-min intervals between 6 p.m. and midnight. The matrices (Supplementary Fig. S2 online) display the mean z-scores for each animal during the demyelination phase (days 5–10). Both matrices, representing social sniff and approach, exhibit similar patterns. In comparison to the control mean value, LPC-treated animals appeared to reduce their social interaction time, with the reduction becoming more prominent as time progressed.

Next, we calculated the location of an animal within a predefined area. Although animals often use nests for sleeping and withdrawal, they spend a significant amount of time in the food zone. However, social interactions were detected mainly in the centre and the periphery, areas that were frequently crossed during their activity phase. Time spent in the centre of the social arena revealed a significantly reduced stay of LPC-treated animals compared to controls (*p < 0.05, unpaired t-test) (Fig. 2e). In contrast, the time spent in the periphery was not significantly different (p = 0.9189, unpaired t-test) (Fig. 2f). Total nesting time, time spent in locomotion, food zone, and water area were not significantly different (Supplementary Fig. S3 online). However, nesting time with another cage mate, a behaviour referred to as ‘social stay in nest’ was reduced in LPC animals (Fig. 2g) but did not reach significant difference (p = 0.0579, unpaired t-test) which contrasts with the outcome during remyelination phase (Fig. 3d). For comparison, all analysed parameters are displayed as normalised values in a spider web plot (Fig. 2h). Re-establishment of social behaviour during subsequent remyelination

Next, we investigated whether the reduction of functional connectivity and impaired social behaviour is reversible after remyelination of the fmi. The same cohorts that had already been studied in the social arena during the demyelination phase, were monitored for five additional days (Fig. 3a). From days twelve to seventeen post-LPC injection, the decreased centre time was no longer observed (Fig. 3b,c) and all other measures of general behaviour did not differ between groups (Supplementary Fig. S3 online).

In the next step, social parameters such as sniffing and approaching were analysed during the remyelination phase. In contrast to the demyelination phase, when sniffing and approaching times in the LPC-treated group were reduced between 9 p.m. and midnight, the same animals spend a similar amount of time with their cage mates as controls (Fig. 3e,f). Furthermore, the z-scores-based detailed analysis of each individual mouse during this six-hour period revealed a constant pattern of behaviour during the 6-h observation period and no decrease in the second half anymore (Supplementary Fig. S2 online). Intriguingly, the decrease in the parameter social stay in nest (Fig. 3d) reached statistical significance (*p < 0.05, unpaired t-test), while the overall time spent in nests was comparable to controls (p = 0.8194, unpaired t-test) (Supplementary Fig. S3 online). Thus, it appears that LPC-treated animals prefer staying alone in nests. The spider web plot (Fig. 3g) displays again the analysed parameters as normalised values. This representation illustrates that all behaviours except the complex social behaviour “social stay in nests” returned to normal.

Body weights were measured throughout the experiment, but no significant differences were observed (Supplementary Fig. S3 online).

Remyelination leads to reestablishment of DMN functional connectivity

To confirm that the brain connectivity impairment after fmi demyelination is transient, a coronal fUS scan (control: n = 11, LPC: n = 12), including the orbital area, was performed for 60 min on day 20 post injection (Fig. 3a). In fact, functional connectivity of the DMN subregions in the scanned plane did not differ significantly between LPC-treated and saline treated animals. Excluding the left insular region and right secondary motor cortex, all correlation coefficients in the prefrontal volume scan shown in the matrix were not significantly different between control and LPC –treated groups (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. S1 online). A more detailed review of the correlation analysis between DMN subregions (Fig. 4b) (p = 0.5350, unpaired t-test) and orbital areas (Fig. 4c) (p = 0.3829, unpaired t-test) revealed no difference anymore. Histological confirmation of LPC-induced de-and remyelination

To confirm the effect of LPC injections on fmi myelination, we examined brain tissue slices by histological Luxol–Fast–Blue (LFB) staining (Fig. 4e, red circles illustrate analysed area in the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), black circles the fmi region) and 3D-light sheet fluorescence microscopy (Supplementary Fig. S4 online). We evaluated the percentage of myelin loss in coronal slices compared to control animals injected with 0.9% NaCl. On day seven post-surgery, in the middle of the demyelination phase, LFB staining was performed on brains previously scanned by fUS. As a control, we quantified the degree of myelination in the ACC (Fig. 4f) during the demyelination phase, revealing no significant difference between both groups (p = 0.5278, unpaired t-test). In contrast, a significant decrease in myelination was found in the fmi region during the demyelination phase (***p < 0.0001, unpaired t-test) (Fig. 4g). In a preliminary experiment, we cleared brains two days post unilateral LPC (Supplementary Fig. S4 online) or saline (Supplementary Fig. S4 online) injection and stained them with a fluorescent myelin antibody directly conjugated to a dye. Compared to controls, a large void volume can be observed in the expected location of the fibre tract. 3D-visualization of the prefrontal brain confirmed correct injection site in all brains. On day 20 post injection, no significant differences in myelination of vehicle and LPC-treated animals were found (p = 0.4367, unpaired t-test) (Fig. 4h), which agrees with previously published data (Plemel et al.29). 3D-evaluation using the light-sheet-microscope confirmed the recovery of fibre tracts (Supplementary Fig. S4 online). Additionally, we assessed immunoreactivity of recruited cells by staining for microglia (Iba1+ cells). Supplementary Fig. S5a–d online shows the recruitment of Iba1+ cells at the injection side across all four experimental conditions. During the demyelination phase, on day 7 post injection, an increased number of microglia was observed in both NaCl-injected (Supplementary Fig. S5a online) and LPC-injected animals (Supplementary Fig. S5b online) mice. Conversely, following remyelination on day 20 post injection, the number of Iba1+ cells decreased in the control condition (Supplementary Fig. S5c online), whereas a high number of microglia cells persisted in LPC-treated mice (Supplementary Fig. S5d online). A magnified view (indicated by a white rectangle) for each condition highlights cell recruitment at the injection side.

Discussion

Here we report the design of a study aimed at back-translating clinical findings into a pre-clinical animal model. Human studies, conducted as part of the PRISM project and aiming to identify biological underpinnings for social dysfunction, revealed a disruption in white matter integrity in individuals with low social functioning. This was specifically observed in the fmi, a fibre tract in the DMN7,17,32. Our approach involves the local injection into the fmi of the demyelinating phospholipid lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC), to reproduce the impairment of white matter integrity in mice observed in the clinical human study. The lipotoxin LPC targets lipid-rich myelin membranes, resulting in focal reduction of myelin thickness33. In contrast, the number of axons and axon integrity appears to be unaffected. Spontaneous remyelination, which begins with oligodendrocyte precursor cell (OPC) recruitment to the focal lesion site 72 h post-injection29, provides a foundation for studying the reestablishment of brain connectivity and behaviour. Given that various neuropsychiatric diseases exhibit microstructural white matter abnormalities7,34, we consider LPC-induced manipulation of the fmi as a relevant model for the back-translation of clinical findings. Based on our findings, we conclude that the demyelinating effect leading to oligodendrocyte toxicity is primarily driven by LPC injection. We reference two studies showing that lysophosphatidic acid (LPA) is generated through the hydrolysis of LPC by autotaxin, an enzyme expressed by oligodendrocytes. LPA then initiates microglia-induced oligodendrocyte toxicity via LPA receptors (LPAR). The addition of autotaxin inhibitors and LPAR1/3 inhibitors to microglia-oligodendrocyte co-cultures did not mitigate LPC toxicity29,35. However, we cannot rule out the possibility that microglia activation contributed to the observed effects, as suggested by the increased number of Iba1+ cells in LPC-treated animals in our experiment. Recruitment of Iba1+ cells is a common response to LPC injection, as demonstrated in several studies36–38. Notably, previous research has observed a switch in microglia types, with iNOS+ cells predominant during demyelination and Arg1+ cells increasing around day 10 post LPC injection, coinciding with the onset of remyelination39. This is consistent with our findings of persistent microglia in LPC-treated animals during the remyelination phase. In contrast, microglia recruited in the control condition due to needle insertion underwent apoptosis or were phagocytosed, as evidenced by their absence at day 20. We therefore conclude that LPC-induced demyelination is the primary driver of the observed changes in DMN connectivity, as microglia were also recruited in the control condition during the initial days without affecting DMN connectivity. Nevertheless, it is possible that the observed changes in brain connectivity and behavioural alterations result from an interplay between demyelination and microglia recruitment through LPC manipulation.

We use functional ultrasound (fUS), to investigate the consequences of LPC-induced fmi demyelination on activity and connectivity changes in the brain based on neuro-vascular coupling30. Compared to functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), fUS detects microvascular fluctuations with remarkable sensitivity, thereby providing high spatiotemporal resolution30. In our study, we scan a coronal volume covering the orbital area, secondary motor cortex, prelimbic area, and insular region in both hemispheres during both the demyelination and remyelination phases on day 7 and 20, respectively. According to29,33, myelin wrapping is restored at day 20 post LPC injection.

The primary objective of this study is to back-translate clinical PRISM data indicating abnormalities in the white matter tracts of the fmi into a mouse model. Due to the limitations of a linear fUS probe, we are restricted to scanning a coronal volume of primarily frontal areas. Consequently, global effects on whole brain dynamics, which cannot be excluded and are likely induced by the treatment, remain unexplored. Nevertheless, we are able to replicate clinical experiments and can confirm the hypothesis that focal reduction of fmi integrity leads to a reduction in functional connectivity between frontal brain regions. Delving deeper, even though no significant decrease of connectivity can be detected between the left and right orbital area, where fmi forming fibre tracts end, we observe a trend of impairment. Nevertheless, analysis of all prefrontal areas involved in the DMN network, including orbital, prelimbic, and secondary motor cortex regions, reveal a reduction in interregional correlations of hemodynamic fluctuations between these regions. Interestingly, in our case, symmetric, bilateral LPC-injection appears to affect both hemispheres differently. The most significant decrease in DMN connectivity can be detected in the left secondary motor cortex, while in the contralateral side, the effect is less pronounced. An explanation for the reproducibly different bilateral findings can be asymmetric functional connectivity as described in the human brain40. During resting state, the mean connectivity shows a symmetry of around 95%, thereby revealing higher correlations in DMN regions of the left hemisphere41. Therefore, any manipulation can affect one side more severely compared to the contralateral one. In line with a previous published study in mice42, we observe a diminishing effect on brain activity during the resting state in the demyelination phase and recovery of the system after remyelination. Unexpectedly, we observe a decrease in connectivity between the right insular and left prelimbic region at day 20 post LPC injection.However, this effect is unlikely to be a result of the LPC injection, as there is no significant decrease in functional connectivity in the insular cortex during the demyelination phase. The observed restoration of connectivity aligns with literature regarding the myelopathy of LPC-induced demyelination, which suggests complete remyelination at day 14 post injection29. The restoration of the disrupted DMN connectome to control levels makes it possible to study the recovery of social behaviour in the same animals which have been analysed during the demyelination stage. Furthermore, the lack of a significant difference between NaCl-injected animals and LPC-injected animals’ DMN connectivity after remyelination verifies that the effects observed during the demyelination phase in the fUS cohort are due to the LPC-induced disruption.

Next, we attempt to understand the impact of DMN hypoconnectivity on social functioning in mice. Unlike previous studies that used the 3-chamber or sociability test to examine social behaviour in mice, our setup provides long-term and individual monitoring in a semi-natural environment31. The data collected post-LPC induction show a significant decrease in the social parameters of sniffing and approaching. Specifically, between 3 and 6 h post switching to the dark phase of the light cycle, we observe a decrease in social interest, potentially indicating a loss of motivation to initiate social contact with their batch mates. This dysfunction is resolved once remyelination is underway or completed. According to Peleh and colleagues, mice, being nocturnal animals, are most interactive during dark phases, particularly in the first half of the night (6 p.m.-midnight)43. Focusing on this time interval, only the second half is affected, whereas in the first half social interaction of the animals is not reduced.

In mice, the potential role of the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) in social interaction was previously explored in a study by Lee and colleagues, where a subset of mPFC neurons showed increased discharge rates correlating with approaching behaviour towards an unfamiliar mouse44. Consistent with these findings, Jennings and colleagues identified distinct neuron populations in the OFC, also located in the frontal brain, that react to social stimuli45. These findings indicate that both DMN regions, the mPFC and OFC, are somehow linked to social behaviour. The impact of manipulating the fmi fibre tract with LPC on sociability has previously been studied46. However, in this study, no difference between LPC and control groups has been found using the social interaction test to measure the social interest of mice. In contrast to our study, social interaction has been only observed for 30 min on the seventh day post-surgery, which may have been too short to detect differences between the groups. In agreement with these findings, our data do not indicate significant changes in social behaviours during the first 3 h time interval of the dark phase. One explanation for this delayed effect can be attributed to the high activity levels and exploratory behaviour of the mice during the early hours of the night. This particularly active phase can potentially interfere with the detection of the consequences of fmi demyelination on social behaviour. Moreover, Xiao and colleagues directly injected into the genu of the CC, accepting the possibility of affecting non-fmi tracts46. Therefore, discrepancies between both observations can also be due to the extension of manipulated areas.

Our data are further substantiated by extending the assessment of behaviours at a group level to the behavioural analysis of individual animals. Z-score heat maps of behavioural differences of individual animals during the first 6 h of the night phase show cooler colours in the second half of the observation period. Despite inter-individual differences, the observation of decreasing social interaction is shared by all individual animals with fmi demyelination. Most importantly, after remyelination, the reduced social approach and sniffing is reversed, reinforcing our hypothesis of fmi involvement in social behaviour. Nonetheless, social behaviour is complex, and the full behavioural spectrum has not been re-established until day 17 post LPC lesion. In contrast to direct social interaction parameters sniffing and approach, the time spent in a nest together with a cage mate is reduced even after remyelination. The longer lasting effect on more complex social behaviours may be explained by thinner and probably less efficient myelin sheaths after remyelination47.

Impaired microstructure of DMN backbone fibre tracts has also been associated with anxiety, another commonly diagnosed neuropsychiatric symptom34. Young, healthy individuals displaying high trait anxiety showed specific alterations in the inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus, corpus callosum, and most importantly in this context, the fmi tract. We therefore hypothesize that fmi tract manipulation should also influence anxiety-like behaviour. Due to the large size of the social arena (80 × 60 cm), the surface area can be considered as an unconventional open field. During the long observation period, the mice build nests close to the walls and in corners, but the open centre is considered anxiogenic48. Consistent with human findings, animals with fmi demyelination spent significantly less time in the centre of the social arena compared to controls. Importantly, after remyelination, the anxiety-like behaviour of avoiding the centre is restored, suggesting that fmi manipulation was the underlying cause. In humans, constant exposure to anxiogenic situations can increase the risk for developing social anxiety49. Moreover, both symptoms, general anxiety and social anxiety can coexist. Therefore, it is plausible to speculate that these symptoms in animals, indicated by reduced time in the centre and social behaviour parameters, can influence each other, leading to a worsening circle.

In addition to social behaviour, non-social behaviour can also be analysed in the social arena. In terms of locomotion, no significant differences are observed, although a slight reduction in LPC animals compared to controls is detectable. A lack of motivation can negatively influence movement behaviour and therefore the opportunity to meet and interact with cage mates. However, as summarized in the spider web plots, both groups display similar levels of ambulation and activity in general. Also, eating and drinking behaviour, measured as time spent in the food zone or in water area, and body weight, is comparable in both groups. Unfortunately, the animals were housed in two separate specific pathogen-free animal research facilities for behavioural studies and fUS. As a consequence, it was not possible to perform a longitudinal analysis of both readouts in a single group.

The fmi circuit is also recognized for its involvement in cognitive processes. Variations in the microstructural integrity of the fmi have been reported as indicators to distinguish between two groups of patients with mild cognitive impairment, affecting domains such as memory, language, attention, and executive functions50. This study primarily aims to back-translate abnormal DMN connectivity and dysfunctional social behaviour into animal models. Consequently, we concentrate on outcomes such as functional connectivity, social interaction, and anxiety-like behaviour. However, investigating the impact of manipulated white matter tracts in the fmi region on cognition is a promising topic for future research.

Taken together, our findings consolidate the hypothesis that disturbing the structural integrity of the fmi by transient demyelination will predominantly affect outcomes of social behaviours. The findings are of transdiagnostic value for deciphering the fmi’s contribution to the malfunctioning brain circuitry in mental disorders. Following the concept of the Research Domain Criteria (RDoC) framework, disease-independent manipulation of brain circuits is a central experimental approach for elucidating the biological underpinnings of the associated symptoms. A key outcome of the clinical work package of the PRISM consortium was the identification of an association of impaired social functioning with an impaired default mode network. Back-translation of the clinical finding into a preclinical model with frontal DMN demyelination demonstrates a causal relationship between frontal DMN impairment and impaired social behaviour. This finding sets the basis for the development of transdiagnostic precision-psychiatry approaches for relieving the burden of social withdrawal.

Methods

Mice

Six weeks old C57BL/6 male mice were ordered from Charles River Research Models and Services Germany, housed in groups of four. Mice were maintained under standard conditions (12 h/12 h dark/light cycle, temperature 20–24 °C, humidity 45–65%, and ad libitum access to food and water) in IVC GM 500 open cages (39.4 cm × 19.9 cm × 17.5 cm) filled with bedding material and tissue paper as nesting material. Following a one-week acclimation period, surgeries were conducted, and mice remained in their designated groups of four throughout the experiment. We had to exclude four LPC-treated animals for the fUS scan during demyelination phase and one control animal for the fUS scan during remyelination phase due to technical issues with the fUS machine. Therefore, 14 animals injected with LPC and 12 controls were successfully scanned in the fUS demyelination experiment (total: 26). The behaviour cohort consisted of 16 LPC against 16 control animals injected with saline (total: 32). Out of those, 12 LPC and 11 control mice proceeded to be scanned using fUS to assess the remyelination effect (total: 23). Additionally, three animals were used for the light sheet fluorescence microscope (LSFM) (total: 3). All experiments and procedures were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations and with the approval of the responsible governmental animal ethics committee (Regierungspräsidium Baden-Württemberg, Tübingen, Germany). All methods are reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org) for the reporting of animal experiments.

Stereotaxic surgery and RFID-transponder implantation

1% LPC (Sigma-Aldrich, Germany) was dissolved in physiological (0.9%) NaCl solution by sonification, before being split into aliquots and stored at − 20 °C prior to surgery. Seven weeks old mice were subcutaneously injected with 10 mg/kg meloxicam (Metacam®, Boehringer Ingelheim, Germany) 20 min before being anaesthetized in an inhalation chamber with 4% isoflurane in oxygen. After shaving the scalp, animals were fixed in a stereotaxic frame (David Kopf Instruments, USA) and inhalation anaesthesia was maintained at 1.5% isoflurane in oxygen to keep animals unconscious. Heart rate, respiratory rate and blood oxygen saturation levels were monitored throughout the surgery using a biometric paw-attached modular system (PhysioSuite, Kent Scientific Corporation, USA). Temperature was maintained at approximately 37 °C using a homeothermic warming pad and rectal thermometer (PhysioSuite, Kent Scientific Corporation, USA). 0.25% bupivacaine (Carbostesin, Aspen Germany GmbH, Germany) was directly injected under the scalp as a local anaesthetic (3 mg/kg). After five minutes an incision was made using a scalpel. Based on stereotaxic mouse brain atlas51 bilateral focal lesions were induced by 1% LPC injections at the following coordinates: AP = + 1.78, ML = ± 1.00, DV = − 2.75. For unilateral lesions induced in the preliminary experiment, the respective coordinates were AP = + 1.78, ML = + 1.00, DV = − 2.75. Lysolecithin was sonicated before injection for 30 min and 500 nl of the solution was injected bilaterally using a 2 µl Hamilton syringe at a rate of 100 nl/min. To prevent solution’s spread, the syringe was lowered to DL = − 2.80 to create a pocket and finally injected at DL = − 2.75. After injection, the syringe was kept at the coordinate for five minutes, then retracted to DL = − 2.70 for an additional five minutes. Controls received physiological (0.9%) NaCl solution. Open drilling holes were closed with Bone wax (Ethicon, Germany). After stitching the skin, an ISO compliant RFID transponder (1.25 mm × 8 mm), already prepared in a sterile syringe, was subcutaneously implanted in the lower back close to the tail after disinfecting the site. For perioperative maintenance of body fluid, mice received 0.5 mL saline before and after surgery. As postoperative treatment, Meloxicam (10 mg/kg, subcutaneously) was given on three consecutive days and animals’ health was scored for an additional two days.

fUS imaging session

The major objective of the study was to back-translate clinical data indicating alterations in the structural white matter integrity of the fmi circuit into mouse experiments. Due to the limitations of using a linear fUS probe, the scans were restricted to prefrontal areas, thereby neglecting potential global effects that may occur. Despite this constraint, we selected the fmi white matter tract to closely replicate clinical experiments. Sedation in mice was induced using 4% isoflurane in oxygen in a separate inhalation chamber. After shaving the scalps of the animals, they were fixed in a stereotactic frame (David Kopf Instruments, USA) while reducing isoflurane concentration to 1% for inhalation anaesthesia. Throughout the scan, oxygen level and heart rate were constantly monitored, and body temperature was maintained at 37 °C by using PhysioSuite homeothermic warming equipment. Meloxicam (0.05 mg/kg) and a bolus injection of dexmedetomidine hydrochloride (0.067 mg/kg, Tocris, USA) were subcutaneously injected. While progressively reducing isoflurane concentration to 0%, sedation maintenance was reached by constant subcutaneous dexmedetomidine infusion (0.2 mg/kg/h, 5 mL/kg/h flow rate) keeping mice conscious during scan session. After application of ultrasonic gel on the scalp, the ultrasound probe was positioned ~ 1 mm above animal’s head. We used the same Iconeus One scanner provided by Iconeus (Paris, France), as described in previous publications30,52,53. Doppler images were recorded at 2.5 Hz frame rate. In each session, the prefrontal area of the animal was scanned by imaging a volume divided into three planes by steps of 0.2 mm. To ensure the right position of the probe, an angiographic scan of 30 coronal slices was generated (steps of 0.2 mm) in advance. Based on the Brain Positioning System54 the target area, containing bilaterally the orbital area, the prelimbic area, the infralimbic area, and the insular region was selected. Acquisition started 10 min after isoflurane concentration was decreased to 0%. Total recording time was 60 min. After completing the imaging session, animals were directly perfused. The scan was performed either on day 7 (demyelination phase, mid of social tracking) (Fig. 1c) or day 20 post injection (remyelination phase, after social tracking) (Fig. 3a). For more detailed information about fUS processing, we refer to the study of Ionescu53.

Social arena experiment

On the fifth day post-surgery (Fig. 2a), the functionality of the RFID-transponder was verified, and social arenas (RFID-assisted SocialScan) were set up as described31. Due to the construction flexibility, three nests filled with tissue paper as nesting material (one large and two small) were affixed to the arena. Additionally, the arena included two water bottles, food hoppers filled with weighted pellets, and standard bedding material, evenly distributed (Fig. 2b). Mice (16 LPC animals vs. 16 controls) in groups of four, were housed in the arena for a duration of 12 days, with 12 h light/12 h dark cycle and the dark phase beginning at 6 p.m., while their behaviour was constantly monitored. They were assigned to the boxes by an independent, blinded scientist. All results obtained between day 5 (starting time: 6 p.m.) and day 10 (end: 6 p.m.) post injection, were included into demyelination phase, all results between day 12 (starting time: 6.p.m.) and day 17 (end: 6 p.m.) into remyelination. The period of 48 h in between was determined as transition phase and was therefore excluded from analysis. Upon completion of the experiment, the body weight was recorded, and the animals were returned to standard cages in their respective groups of four.

Perfusion and Luxol Fast Blue staining

Animals were euthanised by intraperitoneally (i.p.) injecting an overdose of Narcoren (Pentobarbital 10 mg/kg). When the tail pinch reflex was absent, they were transcardially perfused and their brains taken out into 4% PFA for ~ 16 h. Those brains were transferred to 30% Sucrose and then cut into 30 µm slices on a microtome. Slices were stored in PBS containing 0.01% Thimerosal. The formalin-fixed tissue was washed in distilled water and positioned on slides. After an incubation time of 24 h at room temperature in Luxol Fast Blue (LFB) Solution, slices were again washed in distilled water and differentiated several times in Lithium Carbonate Solution (0.05%). For further differentiation, sections were dipped into ethanol (EtOH) (70%) to receive slices where grey-matter is colourless and white-matter remains blue. Afterwards, sections were washed two times in distilled water and incubated in Cresyl Echt Violet (0.1%) for 2 min. Before quickly dehydrating sections in absolute alcohol, they were washed quickly in distilled water. By using Entellan™ (Sigma-Aldrich) sections were mounted with cover slips and imaged via light microscope. Images were further processed by ImageJ, where percentage of demyelination was calculated based on ratio of black pixels in the region of interest. For demyelination phase, we analysed in total 26 animals (control: n = 10, LPC: n = 16) because one of each group was used for clearing process and the perfusion of one LPC-treated animal was unsuccessful. During remyelination phase, one LPC-treated animal (n = 15) was used for MACS Clearing and two animals of the control group (n = 14) had to be excluded from analysis due to perfusion issues (total = 29).

Perfusion, MACS clearing and immunohistochemistry

In a preliminary experiment, three animals used for Light-sheet fluorescence microscopy were injected unilaterally with the respective solution. Two animals received LPC and were killed two days or 20 days post-injection, the third one was injected with Saline and killed 2 days after surgery. They were injected i.p. with an overdose of Narcoren, perfused with ice-cold heparinised (5 U/mL, ThermoFisher Scientific, Germany) PBS at 7 mL/min for 5 min before receiving 4% PFA for a further 8 min to achieve increased tissue clarity. Whole brains were cleared according to the MACS (MXDA-based Aqueous Clearing System) clearing protocol, as this is compatible with lipophilic dyes, and allows for whole-brain rapid clearing55. Permeabilization solution, 10X Antibody Staining Solution and Clearing Solution all came pre-prepared as part of the MACS clearing kit (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany).

Brains were collected in EDTA buffer before transferral to 4% PFA for 2 h. Then they were washed in PBS before incubation for 48 h in 5 mL MACS Permeabilization Solution under slow continuous rotation at room temperature. MBP recombinant antibody (anti-human/mouse, 1:50, REA1154, Miltenyi Biotec, Germany) was diluted in Antibody Staining Solution and brains were placed in the solution and left on a horizontal shaker for 2 weeks at 37 °C. Brains were then washed twice in Antibody Staining Solution at room temperature overnight under slow, continuous rotation, then once more for 24 h.

Absolute ethanol was prepared using activated molecular sieves (3A) and a 0.22 μm filter unit before dilution to a series of 30%, 50%, 70%, and 90%, with 2% Tween® 20. 50 mL tubes were filled completely with 30% ethanol/Tween® 20 solution and a brain was placed inside each and left overnight under continuous rotation at 28 °C before transferral to the higher concentration of ethanol/Tween® 20, and this was repeated with all dilutions. To ensure complete sample dehydration, the absolute (≥ 99.8%) ethanol solution was changed once, and the 24 h incubation step repeated twice. Finally, brains were transferred to 5 mL MACS Clearing Solution and incubated 24 h at room temperature, under continuous rotation. Before imaging, brains were temporarily stored at room temperature in MACS imaging solution.

For two-dimensional immunostaining, brain sections were incubated with the primary antibody (rabbit anti-Iba1, 1:500) overnight at 4 °C with continuous shaking. Subsequently, sections were incubated with the secondary antibody (Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-rabbit, 1:1000) for 4 h at RT with agitation. Following this, the sections were mounted using a medium containing DAPI. Images were taken by using the Opera Phenix Plus High-Content Screening System.

Light sheet fluorescence microscopy

In a preliminary experiment, the cleared and immunostained whole mouse brains, injected unilaterally with the respective solution, were imaged in the horizontal plane. One brain per acquisition was mounted using two screws holding it at the frontal part and cerebellum. The brain was then immersed in the imaging chamber filled with MACS imaging solution (refraction index 1.556) of the LaVision Biotec Ultramicroscope II (Miltenyi Biotec, DE). The laser beams were aligned horizontally with the brain, using motors in the x, y, and z direction. Afterwards, an MI Plan DC57 4× objective was fitted (Miltenyi Biotec, DE) with a numerical aperture of 0.35. Images were acquired by generation of multiple z-stacks at 0.6 × magnification with laser sheets from both sides at 647 nm wavelength to give an overview of whole brain immunostaining. Acquired stacks were generated by ImSpector (Version 4.0.360, LaVision BioTec, Germany) and saved as TIFF images for each channel and plane separately. After stitching those stack images via the ImageJ macro BigSticher, a 3D visualization of the immunostained brains was generated.

Data processing and statistical analysis

fUS

Analysis of the fUS data was performed by an independent, blinded scientist. Regional Power Doppler signal was extracted from 8 different brain regions from both hemispheres51. After cleaning the raw signal of artifacts56, the Doppler signal was filtered for sequences between 0.01 and 0.2 Hz applying the second-order Butterworth filter. Afterwards, functional connectivity matrices, displaying mean values for both groups, were generated, taking correlations between all time courses of selected regions. By using Fisher’s Z-transformation, Pearson’s r correlation coefficient was further processed to z-scores, allowing group-level analysis.

Social arena

The social arena was set up as described previously31. It included three attached nests to provide withdrawal and sleeping opportunities (Fig. 2a). Centre, periphery, corners, food, and water zones were fixed. Primary behavioural data were automatically analysed by the software package RFID-Assisted SocialScan. Pre-defined parameters for velocity, angle of movement, and distances31 were applied to compute non-social and social behaviours including time spent in zone, locomotion, approach, sniffing, following, contact, and fighting. Individual animals were tracked by their implanted RFID-transponder. The primary behaviour data sets were extracted and processed in MATLAB. The total of all detected events in a three-hour time window on each day was used to extract numbers for all social parameters and locomotion. Then, means were calculated for each individual mouse during demyelination and remyelination phases. For all other parameters, including time spent in nest, food zone, water, centre, and periphery, the total of all detected events over 24 h was taken, followed by the mean for both recording sessions. The term ‘social stay in nest’ is used to denote the time spent with one or more cage mates inside nests. This duration was calculated by summing up all such events that occur within a day in a single box. The graphs in Figs. 2g and 3d display twenty values for each treatment group. These values represent the behaviour observed in the four boxes per treatment group over a period of five days during each monitoring phase.

Behavioural z-scores were calculated for individual observation (X) based on standard deviation (σ) and mean value (μ) of the control group. Those z-scores were generated as a mean value over each five days period.

The spider web plots in this study represent all analysed parameters. For behaviours such as sniffing, approaching, following, fighting, and contact, we used the values depicted in the corresponding graphs from 9 p.m. to midnight. For social stay in nest, centre, periphery, nest, food zone, and water, we used the values depicted in the corresponding graphs collected over a 24-h period. After normalising the individual values, we computed the means and displayed them including significant differences for each treatment group in the spider web plots.

Statistical analysis

All statistics were processed in GraphPad Prism or MATLAB. For fUS data, a dynamic analysis was used by applying a sliding window and the following two-sample t-tests for each time slot to achieve higher accuracy in detecting an effect56. Each t-test was corrected by False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction57. For processing the CBV signal for the fUS-example animals, a simple linear regression was performed. For the social arena task, results were displayed as mean values (± SEM) and further processed by using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) corrected via Bonferroni post-hoc test. Only significant differences between both groups in the specific time phase were displayed. To analyse differences in spending time in a specific area in 24 h, an unpaired t-test was performed, as well as for LFB-staining. Significance levels were set at p < 0.05 indicated by (*), p < 0.01 (**) and p < 0.001 (***).
