Eye banking in India: Planning for 2050
Authors: Sujata Das
Published: 2026

Visual impairment and blindness are major health problems worldwide, including in India. In India, the RAAB (Rapid Assessment of Avoidable Blindness) 2015–19 by the NPCBVI documented that corneal opacity is a major cause of blindness (8.2% and 37.5% in the population aged ≥50 years and 0–49 years, respectively).[12] The causes of corneal blindness include infectious and noninfectious diseases that lead to corneal scarring and functional blindness. Extrapolating the figures from the RAAB survey to India’s current population, the estimated number of corneal-blind individuals would be 420,000, with an additional 25,000 to 30,000 people annually.[3] Risk factors for a higher prevalence of corneal blindness include poverty, unhygienic water quality, poor sanitation, climate, and geography. The public health impact of corneal blindness is driven not only by its prevalence but also by its occurrence in younger populations, resulting in a disproportionately high burden of disability adjusted life years (DALYs). Corneal transplantation is a viable option for restoring vision in patients with corneal opacities, and keratoplasty, in any form, is the most common type of biological tissue transplantation worldwide.

Since Eye Banking began in India in 1945, we have come a long way. The roles and responsibilities of Eye Banks have expanded over the past decade.[4] The transition is not limited to upgrading eye bank facilities, such as creating clean rooms and installing a continuous temperature recording system. It comprises additional functional activities, such as driving higher utlization of harvested cornea, using intermediate storage media, and distributing precut and prestripped corneal tissues. Despite these developments, we are far behind the target of 100,000 corneas set two decades earlier.[5] These need a multipronged approach.

Currently, most eye banks are not operating at full capacity. There is a greater need for transplantation centers than for the establishment of eye banks. Given that most high-performing eye banks are located in a few states, the plan should be to develop eye banks in the states where they are not present/not working properly. The larger, more experienced eye banks should hand-hold smaller ones to help them improve. Since HCRP (Hospital Corena Retrieval Program) is a reliable source of tissue for harvesting, greater focus should be placed on engaging in HCRP activities. Simultaneously, public awareness should not be ignored to improve voluntary collection. It goes without saying that collaboration with nongovernmental organizations, religious groups, and the media is crucial for advocacy among the general population. Eye Banks face challenges due to noncooperation from HCRP centers from time to time. Changes in legislation may significantly improve the collection process, especially in medico-legal cases.

In the last few years, we have reached around one-fourth of the target. While the focus should be on improving collection, we should also adapt to enhance utilization by incorporating a few changes. Our utilization varies significantly among eye banks across the country, from 22% to 50%.[67] Optimizing corneal utilization rates may be achieved through proper training of cornea recovery technicians and donor screening and by adopting lamellar surgery among corneal surgeons. Therefore, continuous training at all levels is necessary. Although there is a significant gap between demand and supply of corneal tissue, in some cases, corneas are discarded due to a lack of recipients. Systems like the National Organ and Tissue Transplant Organisation (NOTTO), Regional Organ and Tissue Transplant Organisation (ROTTO), and State Organ and Tissue Transplant Organisation (SOTTO) can coordinate and provide tissue to recipients awaiting a corneal transplant, ensuring that no transplantable corneas are wasted. There is a strong need for a central distribution system to coordinate with the transplant centers and eye banks across the country.

A survey conducted during COVID-19 on transplant waiting times found that the waiting list was under 3 months in over 90% of cases.[8] But this may not be true nationwide as there are still no corneal surgeons in many regions. Therefore, we need epidemiological studies to understand disease patterns, better plan human resources, and establish eye banks and corneal transplant centers.

While all efforts are aimed at improving collection and utilization, we also need to improve the quality of eye banks in India. One way to maintain uniform quality is to establish a mandatory accreditation system. Currently, it is patchy and part of the hospital accreditation program. A corneal transplant registry, like those in Australia and the UK, would help us document the disease pattern and indications.

The need for basic research cannot be denied. The contraindications list is a dynamic document as new contraindications are added and some are removed, especially with the emergence of new diseases and the evolution of lamellar surgery. The eye banking community should be updated, and research should be conducted on those issues where there is ambiguity. There is further scope for improving corneal storage solutions to enhance endothelial cell viability, which is financially viable. It will help us when we have surplus tissues.

The future of eye banking rests on bioalternatives that eliminate the need for human donor tissue. Many eye banks in Europe process various tissues, including epithelial, mesenchymal stromal, and endothelial cells. India should try to establish a few similar centers over the next 5 years. Technology plays a significant role in ophthalmology, and the eye bank should not lag. Artificial intelligence has been explored for training and tissue evaluation.

With the adoption of new technologies, capacity building, and improvements in collection and utilization, India can serve as a role model for many countries where eye banking is in its nascent stage. Strategic planning and implementation are the key requisites to overcome these hurdles. We need to work in mission mode to shorten this duration.

About the author

Dr. Sujata Das

Dr. Sujata Das is a senior Cornea faculty member at LVPEI Bhubaneswar, Medical Director of Drushti Daan Eye Bank, Head of LVPEI’s Eye Bank Network, and Vice President of the Eye Bank Association of India. She completed her medical training and residency at MKCG Medical College, subspecialty corneal training at LVPEI Hyderabad, and international fellowships in Germany (University of Erlangen-Nürnberg) and Australia (Royal Victorian Eye & Ear Hospital/CERA). She also holds an Advanced Management credential from ISB Hyderabad and an honorary D.Sc. from Ravenshaw University. A recipient of the ARVO Developing Country Eye Researcher Award, the AAO Achievement Award, and multiple AIOS honours, she is a 'Member with Thesis' of the Cornea Society (USA). She has served as an ORBIS International faculty evaluator. Dr. Das serves on several editorial boards, has edited three corneal textbooks, and authored over 15 book chapters and 190+ peer-reviewed articles. Her research focuses on corneal infections, eye banking, keratoconus, and genetic analysis of corneal dystrophies.
