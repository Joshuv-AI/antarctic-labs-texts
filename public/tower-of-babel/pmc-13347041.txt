Learning-based cognitive control: developmental trajectories in children aged 5- to 14- years
Authors: Lisa Toffoli, Giulia Stefanelli, Fiorella Del Popolo Cristaldi, Gian Marco Duma, Massimiliano Pastore, Vincenza Tarantino, Agnes Blaye, Corentin Gonthier, Giovanni Mento
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLearning-based cognitive control (CC)—the ability to implicitly adapt control based on contextual regularities—has been studied in young, typically developing children, yet its developmental trajectory remains underexplored. This study examined how learning-based CC develops across childhood under increasing cognitive demands. Overall, 149 children aged 5–14 years (79 females, M = 9.1 ± 2.6) completed a modified Flanker task and a cued go-noGo task (“Addy”). Both tasks included a List-Wide Proportion Congruency (LWPC) manipulation contrasting predictable (mostly congruent/valid) and unpredictable (50%) contexts. Reaction times (RTs), accuracy, and delta scores (incongruent/invalid–congruent/valid RTs) were analyzed. In the Flanker task, LWPC effects were similar across ages, suggesting that learning-based CC emerges early and remains stable in low-demand contexts. In contrast, in the Addy task—requiring greater attentional control and motor inhibition—developmental differences emerged. Younger children adapted behaviour by favoring speed over accuracy, while from around 9 years of age children displayed a more balanced speed–accuracy trade-off and improved accuracy, indicating greater efficiency in managing competing task demands. These findings suggest that learning-based inhibitory CC efficiency under complex, multi-demand conditions continues to develop across childhood and highlight the value of ecologically valid paradigms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-51912-1.Keywords: Learning-based cognitive control, Flanker task, Go-noGo, Inhibition, Development, Speed-accuracy tradeoffSubject terms: Neuroscience, Psychology, Psychology

Introduction

Learning-based cognitive control

Adaptability to the environment is considered a crucial skill that starts developing since birth. This adaptability is largely subtended by cognitive control (CC) abilities, an umbrella concept including a wide range of cognitive functions required for goal-directed behavior, such as inhibition, working memory updating, or set shifting1–3. CC is traditionally treated as a voluntary, top-down mechanism allowing for the instantiation of sophisticated behaviors when low-level automatic processes are not sufficient4,5. However, recent research has brought a paradigm shift on this conceptualization by showing that fine-grained modulations of CC also happen implicitly as a function of contextual regularities, a phenomenon known as learning-based CC (for recent reviews, see6–9). Foundational evidence for bottom-up adjustment mechanisms in CC comes from studies on conflict-sequence effects (CSE), which show that performance dynamically adapts from one trial to the next following conflict (i.e., the Gratton effect;10). More broadly, sequential dependencies in performance have also been documented in related phenomena, such as temporal dependencies in response times11 and post-error slowing12. Beyond these sequential adjustments, bottom-up modulations of CC can also arise through the implicit learning of contextual regularities6–9.

Overall, findings regarding learning-based CC converge on the idea that CC also operates based on internal statistical models of external events, with or without explicit awareness13–16. These internal statistical models play a crucial role in allowing to optimize CC in a bottom-up fashion: predicting the level of required control based on environmental regularities makes it possible to engage control more reactively (i.e., recruiting control when needed) or more proactively (i.e., preparing and maintaining control17) depending on the situation.

Adaptive weighting of experience

Learning-based CC has largely been studied with paradigms that require adaptive weighting of experience in order to optimize the amount of control. The hallmark example of this are proportion congruency (PC) effects: in conflict tasks (such as the Stroop or the Flanker task), conflict elicited by incongruent trials compared to congruent trials is reduced when trials are mostly incongruent (MI) compared to when trials are mostly congruent (MC; e.g.,18–22). Importantly, such effects are typically induced by manipulating list-wide (LWPC) the proportion of incongruent trials across contexts—for instance, by contrasting blocks with different proportions of incongruent trials (e.g., mostly congruent, mostly incongruent, or 50% congruent blocks in list-wide PC designs). Similar effects have been observed when the manipulation is tied to specific items (ISPC: a particular color is MI or MC), or contextual features (CSPC: words presented in a certain font or a certain location are MI or MC; for a review, see23).

PC effects have been explained by the attentional control account, which states that attention is globally adjusted based on the overall probability of encountering a conflict (see24–30; for review, see23). When most trials are incongruent (MI), enhanced attention may facilitate the implementation of proactive CC, for instance by helping participants keep task goals active in working memory (e.g.18). Crucially, these PC effects have been shown to counteract or override fatigue-related performance declines, suggesting that adaptive control mechanisms can be maintained even under sustained task demands (e.g.,23). Importantly, these kinds of CC adjustments are also found in other, non-conflict CC tasks: for instance, inhibitory control tasks can also be shaped by temporal expectancy based on list-wide31–33 and item-specific manipulations34–38. For example, the study by Toffoli, Calderan & colleagues37, used a modified version of the Balloon Analogue Risk Task (BART;39) to induce self-regulation based on the capacity to implicitly grasp and update the latent association between the balloon color and its probability to explode. Adaptation is reflected in risk-taking behavior: participants pump less for riskier colors and more for safer ones, indicating adjustment based on implicitly learned contingencies.

Another example is the dynamic prediction task (DTP;33), which requires participants to adjust CC in terms of inhibitory or excitatory mechanisms as a function of both global (i.e., block-by-block) and local (i.e., trial-by-trial) implicit temporal expectancy. In this task, adaptation is reflected in a modulation of the speed–accuracy trade-off: while maintaining the same level of accuracy, participants respond faster in blocks characterized by a higher proportion of short stimulus onset asynchronies (SOAs), while they slow down in blocks with a higher proportion of long SOAs, indicating that they implicitly adjust their response strategy to match the temporal structure of the environment. In both these cases no conflict trials were presented. In short, weighting past experience within a certain environment to regulate CC seems to be a rather general phenomenon allowing the implementation of optimal cognitive strategies.

In this framework, context-dependent shifts in the speed–accuracy balance can be seen as a potential behavioral manifestation of learning-based control. For instance, participants may strategically adopt a more cautious response mode in high-conflict contexts, resulting in slower but more accurate responses10,13. Within computational models, such adjustments are often formalized as changes in decision thresholds or control allocation, directly influencing the speed–accuracy trade-off40. Nevertheless, how learning-based cognitive control and related phenomena, such as the speed–accuracy trade-off, develop across development remains poorly understood.

A developmental perspective

Few studies have investigated learning-based CC in children. CC is generally considered as a set of higher order abilities that develop over time following prolonged trajectories and that do not reach maturity before the end of adolescence2. However, because the majority of studies have relied on a classic view of CC as a top-down process implemented consciously based on explicit instructions or goals, it is unclear whether this conclusion applies to learning-based CC. Given that implicit learning of statistical regularities is a process robustly occurring even in infants, it may be the case that learning-based CC develops much earlier than voluntarily engaged CC in children40.

In line with the learning perspective on CC6, recent studies have begun investigating the developmental trajectories of this phenomenon. These studies have shown that preschool- and school- aged children can show efficient CC in list-wide, item- or context-based PC manipulations40,41, despite struggling to engage top-down control based on explicit cues42. Further evidence suggests that increases in the ability to voluntary implement CC based on explicit cues are accompanied by concurrent increases in working memory capacity and/or improved metacognitive strategies, as is the case when transitioning from a reactive to a proactive mode of explicit control around 5 years of age42–45; conversely, there is no reason to believe that working memory capacity and metacognition have a major implication in PC effects, especially given that they typically operate without participant awareness46,47, including in children40.

Taken together, the available evidence suggests that implicit, learning-based forms of control may develop earlier than explicit control, which translates into differences of developmental trajectories. However, existing studies were restricted to very specific paradigms or age ranges; there has never actually been a systematic investigation of the developmental trajectory for learning-based CC.

Rationale for the present study

Overall, the literature to date suggests that learning-based CC might represent an early ability compared to CC based on explicit cues. However, the developmental trajectory of this ability is not clear and requires further investigation with different paradigms and with a wider age range24,40. Therefore, the overarching goal for our study was to provide robust evidence for how learning-based CC develops over time, in a broad sample of children from the preschool-period until adolescence, and with more than a single task relying on PC effects. To this end, we recruited a sample of children aged 5 to 14 years (an age range approximately starting with the youngest age at which learning-based CC has been studied and ending in early adolescence where explicit CC has largely been consolidated), and we assessed learning-based CC with two different tasks.

Our first task was a modified version of the Flanker task used in a previous study by Gonthier & colleagues40. This task requires allocation of attention to a central arrow while ignoring congruent or incongruent flanking arrows. We used a LWPC manipulation to create a mostly congruent (MC, 80% congruence) and a half-congruent (50–50, 50% congruence) block.

Our second task was a novel cued go-noGo paradigm (Addy task), in which participants had to allocate attention based on a preceding cue while withholding responses to noGo trials. A LWPC manipulation was applied, creating a mostly valid (MV, 80% congruence) and a half-valid (50–50, 50% congruence) block, with the proportion of noGo trials fixed at 17% to assess response inhibition across predictive contexts. Critically, children were not informed about cue validity (contrary to similar paradigms42,44), and an arrow was used as exogenous cue to facilitate implicit attention allocation, as arrows automatically and reliably capture attention in children and adults48–51. Two advantages of this task are that (1) it is not a type of conflict task, contrary to e.g. the flanker task or Stroop task used in prior studies40 and (2) by combining attentional orienting with response inhibition demands, the task approximates real-life cognitive challenges, such as focusing on classroom instructions while ignoring distractions.

Importantly, the inclusion of the 50:50 block was motivated not only by the aim to extend previous findings on mostly congruent versus mostly incongruent manipulations (Gonthier et al., 2021; Gonthier & Blaye, 2021), but also to examine the flexible readjustment of cognitive control under reduced environmental predictability, while accommodating practical constraints related to task duration and participant fatigue in a developmental sample. Finally, in both tasks the block order was fixed (MC/MV first, 50:50 s) to maximize the modulation of controlled settings (i.e., the order which plays the most on control modulation, rather than fatigue/training effects)40,56.

Based on previous literature (Gonthier et al., 2021, Gonthier and Blaye, 2021), we expected all children to be able to draw on implicit regularities to regulate the implementation of CC both in the Flanker and Addy tasks (presumably by creating an internal model of contextual demandingness in terms of CC), regardless of their age. In both cases, this should result in a reduced congruency/validity effect (i.e., smaller difference between congruent/valid and incongruent/invalid trials) in the half-congruent block compared to the mostly congruent block.. These effects were expected to appear in all age groups, although we also expected that learning-based CC might improve with age, possibly because older children are better at detecting statistical regularities, or better at implementing control in the first place.

We did not make specific predictions as to the direction of a possible change with age, as all patterns could be in line with the literature: the modulation of control (i.e., congruency/validity effect) based on the proportion of conflicting trials could decrease with age if older children are better at implementing control even when most trials are congruent/valid, reducing the distance between the two blocks within the task; it could increase with age if older children benefit more from learning-based CC, as could be predicted from most literature highlighting CC improvements with age; or there could be no interaction with age if learning-based CC is well in place even in the youngest children, as suggested by one prior study40.

Results

As detailed in the Method section, data were analyzed using Bayesian generalized linear mixed models (GLMMs) on reaction times and accuracy, including trial type, block, age, and their interaction, with participant-level random effects and relevant covariates (sex, previous-trial RT and accuracy). Supplementary analyses on delta scores (i.e., incongruent/invalid—congruent/valid reaction times expressed in seconds) were conducted to further examine age-related effects. Results are reported using HDIs, Probability of Direction (PD), and Bayes Factors (BF), with consistent findings across Bayesian and frequentist approaches.

Overall, we observed a main effect of age, with RTs decreasing as children grew older. As expected, we also found evidence of post-error slowing and temporal correlations in RTs across consecutive trials. In addition, there was a significant main effect of trial type, with faster responses on valid Go trials. Importantly, a trial type × block interaction showed that children became faster on invalid trials when moving from the MV to the 50:50 block, while RTs on valid trials remained stable. This resulted in a reduced validity effect (i.e., the RT difference between valid and invalid trials) in the 50:50 block.

Critically, compared to the Flanker task, a trial type × age interaction revealed that the validity effect decreased with age, indicating improved CC efficiency. In addition, a block × age interaction showed that block differences (i.e., faster RTs in the 50:50 block compared to the MV block) emerged only from around 9 years of age. Before this age, no overall block effect was observed.

Finally, the three-way interaction between trial type, block and age was not significant (with a confidence interval rounding at zero and a PD of 0.96, short of the 0.975 threshold) but the supplementary analysis based on delta scores was (Table S4.2). This analysis revealed a significant interaction between block and age: the validity effect in the MV block progressively decreased with increasing age, whereas it remained stable across ages in the 50–50 block. Altogether, these results suggest that, while maintaining similar accuracy levels across blocks, younger children modulate RTs differently when engaging in learning-based CC, with older children showing a more effective modulation of this process. More specifically, the younger children did not show a block modulation of the validity effect, while exhibiting overall larger validity effects. This effect is visible in Fig. 4B.

Discussion

The goal of this study was to extend previous research on learning-based CC in children by examining a wider age range (5–14 years) and by using two different tasks tapping into different inhibitory mechanisms. We employed two experimental tasks with list-wide manipulations -a modified Flanker task 40 and a cued go/no-go task (Addy task)—to assess how learning-based inhibitory CC supports the modulation of congruency and validity effects (i.e., the expected advantage for congruent/valid over incongruent/invalid trials) based on contextual predictability. Specifically, in the Addy task the cued structure allows for advanced motor preparation, thereby placing greater demands on the inhibition of prepotent responses when expectations are violated. In contrast, the Flanker task primarily taxes interference resolution at the time of stimulus processing. Moreover, in the latter, conflict is primarily driven by automatic stimulus–response compatibility (e.g., spatial correspondence between flankers and response buttons), and is further amplified by multiple competing response mappings. In contrast, in the Addy task, conflict arises from violations of cue-based expectations rather than from direct competition between simultaneously presented stimuli.

Our findings show that even preschoolers are able to infer and use contextual regularities to adaptively engage more reactive or proactive control strategies. Indeed, in both tasks we found that children adapted their CC when facing higher contextual demands across all ages. Specifically, they responded faster on incongruent/invalid trials without accuracy costs when moving from the mostly congruent/valid (MC/MI, fewer incongruent/invalid trials) to the half-congruent/invalid (50–50, more incongruent/invalid trials) block. This pattern contrasts with the typical fatigue-related decline often observed in similar paradigms and provides strong evidence of inhibitory CC adaptation, consistent with a use of proactive control from an earlier age than is observed in tasks using explicit cues for CC40,43,45. Critically, in the Flanker task Bayesian analyses confirmed that learning-based CC did not differ as a function of age, showing that preschoolers modulated CC implementation based on implicit regularities in the same way as 14-year-olds. Note that this is despite the finding of a robust effect of learning-based CC, as reflected in the significant interaction between task block and trial type. These findings align with previous findings40, who reported a stable LWPC effect in both preschool (5–6 years) and school-aged (8–10 years) children. Our study extends this evidence up to adolescence (14 years).

Considering previous findings on CC under explicit task demands, which typically show gradual improvements across development1, our results suggest that inhibitory learning-based CC, in the context of a flanker interference control task, emerges earlier and remains stable during development. Together, these findings may suggest a potential functional dissociation between implicit and explicit inhibitory CC40, with implicit CC possibly acting as an important scaffold for the later development of explicit control. However, the present study could not directly test this hypothesis; therefore, given the implications for early screening and intervention, future studies should directly test this hypothesis using paradigms that contrast both implicit and explicit CC demands.

Critically, prior literature had mostly investigated learning-based CC through paradigms designed to isolate specific cognitive mechanisms. Yet, this is not how CC operates in everyday life. For example, in a classroom setting, children must simultaneously pay attention to the teacher, maintain and manipulate information in memory while copying from the board, and inhibit potential distractions. Our second task, the cued go-noGo (Addy task), places multiple CC demands on children: allocating visuospatial attention in response to a cue while withholding responses on noGo trials. This design reflects, to some extent, the simultaneous demands children face in real-world settings.

As in the Flanker task, we observed a LWPC effect across all age groups in the Addy task, but here the quality of this adaptation changed with age. Specifically, all groups showed faster RTs on invalid trials compared to valid trials when moving from the mostly valid block (MV) to the 50:50 block. However, delta-based analyses revealed larger block modulation of the validity effect (i.e., invalid—valid RTs) in preschool-aged children as compared to older ones. This may support the account that learning-based CC is operational even in younger children, and resembles previous findings with non-conflict CC adaptive tasks33,52. This result suggests that preschoolers achieved learning-based CC adaptation through a different speed–accuracy trade-off: younger children took advantage of the frequent occurrence of valid trials in the mostly valid block to speed up their responding on valid trials, but this led some children to make many errors (see Fig. 5A, top left panel). From around 9 years of age, children displayed a more balanced speed–accuracy profile, and average accuracy on valid trials in the mostly valid block progressively increased to ceiling, reflecting greater efficiency at balancing task demands to obtain a robust performance. An alternative interpretation of the present findings is that performance changes across blocks reflect learning about cue validity, rather than (or in addition to) adaptive adjustments in cognitive control. In the Addy task, participants may gradually acquire knowledge about the probabilistic reliability of the cue and adjust their behavior accordingly—for instance, by reducing reliance on the cue when invalid trials are relatively frequent. From this perspective, the observed developmental differences may reflect age-related changes in how cue validity information is weighted: younger children may rely more heavily on the cue despite its imperfect reliability, whereas older children may adopt a more cautious or reactive strategy. Although this account emphasizes associative or expectancy-based learning mechanisms, it is not incompatible with a learning-based control framework. Rather, it suggests that adaptive control may, at least in part, be implemented through sensitivity to environmental regularities, such as cue validity.

To note, although accuracy reached near-ceiling levels in older participants, substantial variability in RTs remained and continued to reflect systematic modulation by the experimental manipulations. This pattern is consistent with previous developmental work (Davidson et al., 2006) showing that with increasing age, performance differences shift from accuracy-based to more subtle RTs–based effects, reflecting increasing task mastery. Accordingly, interpretations of developmental changes in speed–accuracy trade-offs should be considered with caution, focusing primarily on response time data rather than accuracy alone. Moreover, we did not observe a significant interaction between block, trial type, and age. Instead, only the difference score (RT in valid—RT in invalid trials) interacted with age, suggesting caution in interpreting these findings as evidence of a clear developmental trajectory.

These findings align with our flanker task results and previous evidence suggesting that learning-based inhibitory CC is already established by preschool age. Notably, our Addy task results extend prior research by showing, for the first time, that inhibitory CC efficiency under complex, multi-demand conditions may continue to improve across development. This underscores the importance for future research to employ ecologically valid experimental paradigms to more accurately capture how CC functions in everyday contexts and provides valuable insights for adapting educational strategies to different developmental stages.

Our study design suggests a few directions for future studies. First, we were unable to include a mostly incongruent block or counterbalance block order due to testing constraints (especially in terms of time and fatigue) with young children. Incorporating these elements in future studies would help clarify how different forms of contextual predictability shape learning-based CC across development and identify potential order-related influences.

Second, the design of the cued go-noGo task (Addy task) does not allow us to disentangle whether the observed effects stem primarily from cue–target learning difficulty (in contrast to the Flanker task, which only manipulates LWPC) or from the combined demands on attentional control and response inhibition. Future research should aim to separate these contributions to gain a clearer understanding of the mechanisms supporting learning-based CC.

In conclusion, our findings corroborate previous evidence suggesting that learning-based inhibitory CC is an early-emerging feature of the human mind, already present by preschool age. Importantly, we further demonstrate for the first time that developmental trajectories may vary depending on the specific cognitive mechanisms involved. When task demands increase—requiring both attentional control and motor inhibition—older children modulate their CC in a way that makes them less vulnerable to making errors in blocks where invalid trials are rare. Future research employing more ecologically valid, multi-demand tasks will be crucial to deepen our understanding of how CC and development interact.

Methods

The present study was not pre-registered due to time constraints. However, all materials, data, and analysis scripts are openly accessible on the Open Science Framework (OSF) at the following link: https://osf.io/mw598/?view_only=e8dc3e90fa8e4e868c3c10e59bd71b92.

Ethics statement

All parents provided written consent, and children provided oral assent after procedure explanation, following Italian ethical guidelines for research53. All experimental procedures were approved by the local Ethics Committee (School of Psychology—Padova University, protocol no. 4920) and followed the principles expressed in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Participants

Participants were recruited from kindergarten, primary, and secondary schools in the Marche (“Maria Montessori” and “Raffaello Sanzio” schools, Ancona, Italy) and Sicily (“Antonio Gramsci” and “Giovanni Falcone” schools, Palermo, Italy) regions. The initial sample included 149 children and adolescents aged 5–14 without known or diagnosed sensory, neurological or neuropsychiatric disorders and normal or corrected-to-normal vision. The number of participants was not determined through a formal power analysis but rather based on a feasibility evaluation of our resources54. After excluding participants due to corrupted data files (Flanker = 18, Addy = 0), low non-verbal reasoning scores (Coloured Progressive Matrices < 5th percentile based on age-adjusted normative data55) (Flanker = 7; Addy = 7), or low overall task accuracy (< 60%) (Flanker = 4; Addy = 21), the final sample included 120 children for the Flanker task (59 females; ages 5–14, M = 8.4 years, sd = 2.6) and 121 children for the Addy task (64 females; ages 5–14, M = 8.7 years, sd = 2.6) (see Figure S1 in Supporting Information Section “Introduction”, for participants’age distribution).

Materials

Flanker task

Trial structure

After an initial fixation cross (850 ms), a visual stimulus appeared and remained on screen until participant’s response or for a maximum of 3000 ms. The stimulus depicted five identical arrows pointing left or right. In congruent trials, the target arrow (i.e., central arrow) pointed toward the same direction of the flanker arrows (i.e., lateral arrows; e.g., <<<<<), while in incongruent trials the target and the flankers pointed toward different directions (e.g., <<><<). Instructions prompted participants to press—as fast and accurately as possible—a button based on the target direction, while ignoring the distracting flankers. Specifically, children used a numerical keypad to indicate the direction of the target arrow: up = 8, down = 2, left = 4, right = 6, with arrows placed on the corresponding numbers to facilitate response selection. The trial list was randomized.

In order to motivate children, the following oral and written instructions were given: “Would you like to help Maty become an archery champion? You will see a target sign appear in the center of the screen. Then you will see five arrows, but you need to focus on the central one while ignoring the later ones: that’s the correct arrow that Maty needs to use in order to win! If the central arrow points on the left, press left, if the central arrow points on the right, press right".

List-wide proportion congruency

Two trial types were defined by target-flanker arrow correspondence: congruent (target and flankers point the same way) and incongruent (target and flankers point differently; see Fig. 5A). Using a list-wide proportion congruency (LWPC) manipulation, two blocks were created: a mostly congruent (MC) block (96 trials; 80 congruent, 16 incongruent, 83% congruence) and a half-congruent (50–50) block (64 trials; 32 congruent, 32 incongruent, 50% congruence).

To separate adaptive control from associative learning processes (irrelevant in this case), each block included two types of trials: inducer trials, used in different proportions in the two blocks to create the proportion congruency manipulation; and diagnostic trials, used with the same number in both blocks. Specifically, inducer trials were mostly congruent in the MC block (64 congruent trials) and 50% congruent in the 50–50 block (16 congruent and 16 incongruent trials). Diagnostic trials were 50% congruent in both blocks (16 congruent and 16 incongruent trials). To separate item sets, inducer trials used flankers pointing up or down whereas diagnostic trials used flankers pointing left or right; see Fig. 5B). Because congruency was balanced and the stimulus set did not overlap with the inducer trials, performance on these trials cannot be explained by simple stimulus–response associations. Instead, diagnostic trials provide a measure of whether participants generalize control adjustments induced by the block context. This approach is commonly used to isolate genuine list-wide proportion congruency effects reflecting adaptive control rather than item-specific associative learning20,37.

The blocks’ order was fixed (i.e., mostly congruent first, half congruent second) between participants in order to increase statistical power by ensuring the same experimental structure for all participants52. The total duration of the task was approximately 10 min. Children performed a first practice block (12 trials) in order to check instruction comprehension. During practice, both visual (i.e., ‘GREAT’ for correct responses, ‘OH NO… WRONG ANSWER!’ for wrong responses and ‘FASTER!’ for delayed responses) and auditory (i.e., high tone for correct responses, low tone for wrong or delayed responses) feedback was provided. During the experimental blocks only auditory feedback was given. Three pauses were delivered along the task (respectively after 32, 64, and 112 trials) to avoid excessive fatigue and maintain motivation.

Addy task

Trial structure

After an initial fixation cross (800–1200 ms), a trial started with a visual cue, which was depicted as an arrow pointing left or right, for 500 ms. A target represented by the cartoon of a male or female astronaut appeared after an interval time of 1500 ms and remained on screen until participant’s response or for a maximum of 3000 ms. Two astronaut icons were continuously displayed at the bottom of the screen on the left and right side. Instructions prompted participants to press—as fast and accurately as possible—a button (left or right) based on the correspondence between the target and the lower astronaut icons (go trials). Finally, when an alien was present in the astronaut’s spacesuit, participants had to press no button (noGo trials). The position of the astronaut icons as well as the trial list were counterbalanced between-participants.

In order to motivate children, the following oral and written instructions were given: “Would you like to help astronauts Nick and Maty find these little stars that got lost in space? You will see a space sign appearing in the center of the screen that will help Nick and Maty find the stars. Look at it carefully because the arrow can point either to the right or to the left. Then you will see an astronaut: if you see the female astronaut, press her button, while if you see the male astronaut, press his button. However, if you see the alien, do not press any button".

List-wide proportion validity

Two trial types were defined by cue-target correspondence: valid (arrow direction matches the required button) and invalid (arrow direction does not match; see Fig. 6A). Using a list-wide manipulation, two blocks were created: a mostly valid (MV) block (72 trials; 56 valid, 16 invalid, 78% validity) and a half-valid (50–50) block (72 trials; 36 valid, 36 invalid, 50% validity). Both blocks included 17% noGo trials (12 trials, equally valid and invalid) to assess inhibitory control across contexts (see Fig. 6B). As in the flanker task, the block’s order was fixed (i.e., mostly valid first, half valid second) between participants in order to increase statistical power by ensuring the same experimental structure for all participants52. The total duration of the task was approximately 10 min. Children performed a first practice block (14 trials) in order to check instruction comprehension. Visual feedback (i.e., happy face for correct responses, puzzled face for wrong or delayed responses) was given only during the practice block. Three short pauses were delivered along the task (each after 36 trials) to avoid excessive fatigue and maintain motivation.

Addy task. Panel (A) After a fixation inter-trial interval (ITI = 800–1200 ms), the cue (arrow left or right) appeared on the center of the screen (500 ms), followed after 1500 ms by the target stimulus (male or female astronaut). Participants had to press a button (left or right) based on the correspondence between the target and the icons on the bottom left (i.e., female) and right (i.e., male): if it was coherent with the cue direction the trial was valid, otherwise it was invalid. Panel (B). A List-wide manipulation was used to create a mostly valid (MV, 78% valid trials) and a half-valid (50–50, 50% valid trials) block. Moreover, a fixed percentage of noGo trials (17%) was present in both blocks to assess response inhibition in different predictive contexts. The software E-prime (version 2.08.9063) was used to create and administer the Addy task.

Experimental procedure

Data were collected between December 2022 and June 2023, involving two experimental tasks (Flanker and Addy). As an inclusion criterion, we also administered a non-verbal reasoning test (a coloured progressive matrices task not used in the current study55). Participants were tested individually in a quiet, well-lit room free from distractions, at their school. Throughout the experimental session, an experimenter was present in the room with the participant. They were seated approximately 60 cm from a laptop (12.5 inches, resolution of 1536 × 1024 pixels). Each experimental session lasted approximately 60 min, with breaks included to prevent participant fatigue. In this within-subjects design, all children performed the Addy task first and the Flanker task second. At the end of the experimental session, participants received a Young Scientist Certificate as thanks for their contribution. No other forms of compensation were provided.

Statistical analysis

The analyses were conducted using generalized linear mixed models (GLMMs) for both RTs and accuracy. RTs (expressed in seconds) were analyzed after removing premature (i.e., < 150 ms before target onset), delayed (i.e., > 2500 ms after target onset) and incorrect (i.e., omission or commission errors) responses. Regarding accuracy, we analyzed all responses provided between 150 and 2500 ms (keeping also noGo trials with RT = 0 ms after target onset). In the case of the flanker task, only diagnostic trials were analyzed.

For the Flanker task, the study had a 2 (within-subject, trialType: congruent, incongruent) × 2 (within-subject, block: MC, 50–50) × Age (in months, continuous numeric variable, scaled) design. For the Addy task, the study had a 3 (within-subject, trialType: go valid, noGo valid, go invalid) × 2 (within-subject, block: MV, 50–50) × Age design. For both RTs and accuracy, the GLMMs models (see Table 1) included a random intercept and by-participant random slopes for trialType, block and their interaction.

Sex was included in all analyses as a subject-level covariate. To deal with potential trial-by-trial sequential or fatigue effects, the model also included two trial-level covariates (note that all results were unchanged when re-estimating the statistical models without these covariates): (1) the RT in the preceding trial (preRT, continuous numeric variable, scaled) to account for the temporal dependency in response times11; and (2) the accuracy on the preceding trial (Acc(n-1); 0 = incorrect, 1 = correct) to account for post-error slowing12. Note that all results were unchanged when re-estimating the statistical models without these covariates.

In addition, to further clarify how the effect of interest (block type x trial type) interacts with age, we conducted a supplementary analysis using delta scores as the dependent variable. The delta (i.e., incongruent/invalid—congruent/valid reaction times expressed in seconds) was calculated separately for each block (MC/MV and 50–50) in both tasks. These values were entered into a linear mixed-effects model with block, age (scaled continuous variable), and their interaction as fixed effects, sex as a covariate, and random intercepts.

All statistical analyses were performed in R56 (version 4.3.2) using a Bayesian framework with the ‘brms’ package57. Weak informative and zero-centered priors were used. Full model details (chains, samples, burn-in, family, priors) can be found in the Supporting Information (see Section "Results"). Results tables report estimated parameters with 95% Highest Density Intervals (HDI) [lower, upper]. The HDI is the posterior distribution that includes the most credible values of the true parameter58,59. Hypothesis testing used Probability of Direction (PD) from the ‘bayestestR’ package58, indicating effect direction certainty (0.5 to 1 scale, with values near 1 implying strong directional evidence), correlated with frequentist p-values. PD values equal to or greater than 97.5% were considered to indicate a probable effect58. In addition, Bayes Factors (BFs) were calculated for all parameters to quantify evidence for or against the null hypothesis60. Following conventional thresholds, BF values greater than 3 indicate moderate evidence for an effect, values greater than 10 indicate strong evidence, and values greater than 100 indicate decisive evidence. Conversely, BF values below 1/3 provide moderate evidence for the null, and values below 1/10 provide strong evidence for the null61. BFs were reported alongside PD to provide complementary information on the strength and directionality of the observed effects. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons (reported in the Supporting Information) were done using estimated marginal means contrasts (‘emmeans’ package62), reporting estimates (b), standard errors (SE), and standardized beta coefficients.

All analyses were also run using frequentist methods, with those results provided in the Supporting Information (Section “Discussion”). Importantly, the main findings were consistent across the two analytical approaches.
