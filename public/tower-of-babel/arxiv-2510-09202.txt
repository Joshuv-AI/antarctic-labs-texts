Unique continuation and Hardy’s Uncertainty Principle for Hyperbolic Schrödinger Equations
Torunn S. Jensen

Abstract




2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
35B60, 35J10, 35Q41, 35Q55

Abstract

We prove unique continuation properties related to the Hardy uncertainty principle for solutions of the hyperbolic nonlinear Schrödinger equation and the hyperbolic Schrödinger equation with potential. Under suitable conditions on the nonlinearity, or the potential, we show that if
u
is a solution with Gaussian decay at two different times, then

u\equiv 0
. These results extend to the hyperbolic setting the work of Escauriaza, Kenig, Ponce, and Vega (JEMS, 10, 2008) for the classical Schrödinger equation. The proofs rely on Carleman estimates based on calculus and convexity arguments, with the main challenge being to provide a rigorous justification of these estimates. Although our approach follows the general strategy of Escauriaza, Kenig, Ponce, and Vega, several technical modifications are required to handle the hyperbolic character of the equation.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
The hyperbolic Schrödinger equation

We consider the hyperbolic nonlinear Schrödinger equation (HNLS)

(1.1)

\partial_{t}u=i(Lu+F(u,\bar{u})),

where
u=u(x,t)\in\mathbb{C}
,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
,
F(u,\bar{u})
is a complex-valued function, and, for
1\leq k<n
,

(1.2)

L=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\partial_{x_{j}}^{2}-\sum_{j=k+1}^{n}\partial_{x_{j}}^{2},

is the “hyperbolic”  Laplace operator. These equations appear as asymptotic models in different physical contexts, such as water waves, plasma waves, electromagnetic waves and nonlinear optics. We refer to
[
20
,
24
,
3
,
19
,
7
]
, and the references therein for general derivations of nonlinear hyperbolic Schrödinger-type equations. Of course, if
k=n
in (
1.2
), then
L=\Delta
, and we recover the classical nonlinear Schrödinger equation,

(1.3)

\partial_{t}u=i(\Delta u+F(u,\bar{u})).

In the case where
F(u,\bar{u})=\pm|u|^{2}u
, we obtain the cubic hyperbolic NLS,

(1.4)

\partial_{t}u=i(Lu+|u|^{2}u).

For
n=2,
the cubic hyperbolic NLS

\partial_{t}u=i(\partial_{x}^{2}-\partial_{y}^{2}+|u|^{2}u)

describes the envelope of slowly modulated wave packets around highly oscillating waves in deep water. In nonlinear optics, it also models the evolution of electromagnetic fields in planar waveguides with self-focusing and normal dispersion, see
[
16
,
26
]
and the references therein. The cubic hyperbolic NLS also appears in more complicated settings, like the Davey-Stewartson system (see for example
[
14
]
for more details), where it is coupled with an elliptic/hyperbolic potential equation:

(1.5)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}i\partial_{t}u+\delta\partial_{x}^{2}u+\partial_{y}^{2}u=\chi|u|^{2}u+bu\partial_{x}\phi,\\
\partial^{2}_{x}\phi+m\partial^{2}_{y}\phi=\partial_{x}(|u|^{2}).\end{cases}

Here, the parameters
\delta,\chi,b,m
are real, can be both positive and negative, and
|\delta|=|\chi|=1.

It was noticed in
[
15
]
that the Strichartz estimates for the linear part of (
1.1
) are similar to the ones for the classical (elliptic) Schrödinger equation, so that the local well-posedness results for NLS (see for example
[
22
]
and the references therein) also hold for (
1.1
). However, it is still a challenging open problem to understand the global behavior of the solutions to (
1.1
), see
[
24
]
for more details.

In this paper, we will derive unique continuation results for the hyperbolic nonlinear Schrödinger equation (
1.1
). In the process, we will first investigate the case of the hyperbolic Schrödinger equation with a potential

(1.6)

\partial_{t}u=i(Lu+V(x,t)u),

where the potential
V
satisfies suitable assumptions (see Theorem
3
below).

1.2.
Uncertainty principle for the Schrödinger equation

In the series of works
[
8
,
9
,
11
,
12
,
13
]
, Escauriaza, Kenig, Ponce and Vega proved several unique continuation properties for the Schrödinger equation with potential and the nonlinear Schrödinger equation. These results generalize Hardy’s Uncertainty principle from Fourier analysis:
if
f(x)=O(e^{-|x|^{2}/\beta^{2}}),\ \hat{f}(\xi)=O(e^{-4|\xi|^{2}/\alpha^{2}})
and
\alpha\beta<4,
then
f\equiv 0
. Also, if
\alpha\beta=4
then
f
is a constant multiple of
e^{-|x|^{2}/\beta^{2}}
.
There is a corresponding
L^{2}
-result, proved in
[
6
]
:
if
\|e^{|x|^{2}/\beta^{2}}f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}
and
\|e^{4|\xi|^{2}/\alpha^{2}}\hat{f}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}
are both finite and
\alpha\beta<4
, then
f\equiv 0.

The extension of this result to
n
dimensions has also been deduced using the Radon transform, see
[
25
]
.

The solutions of the free Schrödinger equation write

u(x,t)=e^{it\Delta}u_{0}=\frac{e^{i|x|^{2}/4t}}{(2it)^{n/2}}\left(e^{i|\cdot|^{2}/4t}u_{0}\right)^{\wedge}\ (x/2t).

Thus
u(x,t)
at any time
t
is related to the Fourier transform of the initial data
u_{0}
. Then, we can apply the Hardy uncertainty principle to the function
f(x)=e^{i|x|^{2}/4}u_{0}
and deduce the following unique continuation result:
If
u
is a solution of the free Schrödinger equation,
\|e^{|x|^{2}/\beta^{2}}u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
and
\|e^{|x|^{2}/\alpha^{2}}u(1)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
are finite, and
\alpha\beta<4,
then
u\equiv 0
.

For the hyperbolic case, the solution of the free equation writes

u(x,t)=e^{itL}u_{0}=C(n,k,t)e^{i(|x_{+}|^{2}-|x_{-}|^{2})/4t}*u_{0},

for
|x_{+}|^{2}=x_{1}^{2}+\dots+x_{k}^{2},
and
|x_{-}|^{2}=x_{k+1}^{2}+\dots+x_{n}^{2},
and for some constant
C
depending on
n,k
and
t.

Thus, similar to the elliptic case,

u(x,t)=C(n,k,t)e^{i(|x_{+}|^{2}-|x_{-}|)/4t^{2}}\Big(e^{i/4t(|\cdot_{+}|^{2}-|\cdot_{-}|^{2})}u_{0}\Big)^{\wedge}(\tilde{x}/2t),

where
\tilde{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{k},-x_{k+1},\dots,-x_{n})=(x_{+},-x_{-}).
Hence, applying the Hardy uncertainty principle to the function
g(x)=e^{i(|x_{+}|^{2}-|x_{-}|^{2})/4}u_{0}
yields the Hardy uncertainty principle also for the solution of the free hyperbolic Schrödinger equation:

If
u
is a solution of the free hyperbolic Schrödinger equation,
\|e^{|x|^{2}/\beta^{2}}u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
and
\|e^{|x|^{2}/\alpha^{2}}u(1)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
are finite, and
\alpha\beta<4,
then
u\equiv 0
.

In
[
10
]
, the authors extended this result to the Schrödinger equation with potential and to the nonlinear Schrödinger equation. The proofs are quite different though and rely on Carleman’s estimates. In particular, they proved the following:

Theorem 1

(
[
10
]
)
.

Let
u\in C([0,1],L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
be a solution to

\partial_{t}u=i(\Delta u+V(x,t)u)\ \ \ in\ \mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,1],

where
V
is bounded, and either
V(x,t)=V_{1}(x)+V_{2}(x,t)
with
V_{1}
real valued and

\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\|e^{\frac{|x|^{2}}{(\alpha t+\beta(1-t))^{2}}}V_{2}(t)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}<\infty,

or

\lim_{R\to\infty}\int_{0}^{1}\|V(t)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{R})}dt=0.

If there exist constants
\alpha,\beta>0
such that
\alpha\beta<2
and
\|e^{\frac{|x|^{2}}{\beta^{2}}}u(0)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
and
\|e^{\frac{|x|^{2}}{\alpha^{2}}}u(1)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
are finite, then
u\equiv 0
.

Remark 1.1
.

This result is an extension of the Hardy Uncertainty Principle for the free Schrödinger equation to the Schrödinger equation with potential. The condition on the coefficients,
\alpha\beta<2
was not sharp in
[
10
]
, and a bit weaker than the one from Hardy’s uncertainty principle. However, in
[
11
]
the result was improved to be as sharp as in the free case, with the condition
\alpha\beta<4.

As a consequence, they also proved the following result for the NLS:

Theorem 2

(
[
10
]
)
.

Let
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
be
C([0,1],H^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
solutions to

\partial_{t}u=i(\Delta u+F(u,\bar{u}))

in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,1]
, with
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{+},\ k>n/2,\ F:\mathbb{C}^{2}\to\mathbb{C},\ F\in C^{k}
and
F(0)=\partial_{u}F(0)=\partial_{\bar{u}}F(0)=0.
If there are positive constants
\alpha
and
\beta
with
\alpha\beta<2
such that
\|e^{\frac{|x|^{2}}{\beta^{2}}}(u_{1}(0)-u_{2}(0))\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
and
\|e^{\frac{|x|^{2}}{\alpha^{2}}}(u_{1}(1)-u_{2}(1))\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
are finite. Then
u_{1}\equiv u_{2}.

1.3.
Statement of the result

In this work, we extend the Hardy uncertainty principle to solutions to the hyperbolic Schrödinger equation with potential and to solutions to the hyperbolic NLS, by applying the same techniques as in
[
10
]
.
We state our first result for the hyperbolic Schrödinger equation with potential.

Theorem 3
.

Let
u\in C([0,1],L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
be a solution to the problem

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u=i(Lu+V(x,t)u)\ \ \ in\ \mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,1]

L
defined as in (
1.2
),
where
V
is bounded, and either

(1.7)

\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\|e^{\frac{|x|^{2}}{(\alpha t+\beta(1-t))^{2}}}V(t)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}<\infty,

or

(1.8)

\lim_{R\to\infty}\int_{0}^{1}\|V(t)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{R})}dt=0.

If there exist constants
\alpha,\beta>0
such that
\alpha\beta<2
and
\|e^{\frac{|x|^{2}}{\beta^{2}}}u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
and
\|e^{\frac{|x|^{2}}{\alpha^{2}}}u(1)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
are finite, then
u\equiv 0
.

Remark 1.2
.

The first condition (
1.7
) on the potential
V
is not as general as the one in Theorem
1
, which also includes a real time independent potential
V_{1}(x)
. We chose to restrain ourself for including this kind of potential to avoid technical difficulties related to the semi-group theory of the parabolic regularization of the hyperbolic Schrödinger propagator.

As a consequence of Theorem
3
, we deduce the following result for the hyperbolic nonlinear Schrödinger equation, which in particular applies to the cubic hyperbolic NLS.

Theorem 4
.

Let
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
be
C([0,1],H^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
solutions to the equation

\partial_{t}u=i(Lu+F(u,\bar{u})),

with
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{+},\ k>n/2,\ F:\mathbb{C}^{2}\to\mathbb{C},\ F\in C^{k}
and
F(0)=\partial_{u}F(0)=\partial_{\bar{u}}F(0)=0.
If there are positive constants
\alpha
and
\beta
with
\alpha\beta<2
such that
\|e^{\frac{|x|^{2}}{\beta^{2}}}(u_{1}(0)-u_{2}(0))\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})},
and
\|e^{\frac{|x|^{2}}{\alpha^{2}}}(u_{1}(1)-u_{2}(1))\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
are finite, then
u_{1}\equiv u_{2}.

The proof of Theorem
1
in
[
10
]
relies on Carleman’s estimates. The techniques use only calculus and convexity arguments. However, the main challenge is that these computations are formal and providing a rigorous justification is not trivial. Formally, the proof of Theorem
3
is almost the same as for Theorem
1
in
[
10
]
, but when it comes to making the arguments rigorous, some differences and technical difficulties occur. In the next subsection, we outline the main steps of the proof and highlight the key differences to the proof of Theorem
1
.

It is worth noting that in
[
2
]
, Theorem
1
was extended for solutions of the electromagnetic Schrödinger equation

\partial_{t}u=i(\Delta_{A}u+Vu),

where
\Delta_{A}=(\nabla-iA)^{2}
, and
A(x):\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a real valued vector potential. In
[
5
]
the result was also extended to be sharp.
In
[
1
]
, Barcelo, Cassano and Fanelli proved different unique continuation properties for solutions of the electromagnetic hyperbolic Schrödinger equation, by only assuming decay at one time instead of two. The techniques in
[
1
]
also rely on Carleman estimates and the use of the Appell transformation for the hyperbolic equation.

1.4.
Outline of the proof of Theorem
3
.

The proof is divided into several steps.

Step 1: the conformal/Appell transformation.
The first step is to reduce the problem to the case where the parameters
\alpha
and
\beta
are equal. This can be done with the conformal/Appell transformation
1
1

1

The transformation in the hyperbolic case is not exactly the same as in the elliptic case, see in particular Lemma
2.2
.
. Instead of assuming that
\|e^{\frac{|x|^{2}}{\beta^{2}}}u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
and
\|e^{\frac{|x|^{2}}{\alpha^{2}}}u(1)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
are finite for
\alpha\beta<2
, we can assume that
\|e^{\gamma|x|^{2}}u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
and
\|e^{\gamma|x|^{2}}u(1)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
are finite for some
\gamma>\frac{1}{2}.

Step 2: heuristic argument.
The heuristic argument is the same as for the elliptic case in
[
10
]
. Let
u
be a solution of the equation (
1.6
). We define
f=e^{\phi}u
for some weight function
\phi=\phi_{R},
to be chosen later, depending on a large parameter
R
. Moreover, we let
H(t)=\|f(t)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2}.
Then
f
satisfies the equation

\partial_{t}f=(\mathcal{S+A})f,

for a symmetric operator
\mathcal{S}
and a skew-symmetric operator
\mathcal{A},
both depending on the weight function
\phi.
Ideally, we would like to prove a log-convexity inequality for the function
H,
by computing
\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}\log{H(t)}.
In particular, we want to choose
\phi
such that

\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}\log{H(t)}\geq-h(R,\gamma),

where
h
is a non-negative function depending on
\gamma
and
\phi_{R}
. This will imply that the function
e^{-\frac{h(R,\gamma)}{2}t(1-t)}H(t)
is logarithmically convex. Then, it follows that, for some
0<\epsilon<1,

\|u(1/2)\|_{L^{2}(B_{R\epsilon/4})}\leq H(0)^{1/2}H(1)^{1/2}e^{-\tilde{h}(R,\gamma)},\text{ where }\tilde{h}(R,\gamma)\longrightarrow+\infty\text{ when }R\to\infty\text{ and }\gamma>\frac{1}{2}.

If we let
R\to\infty,
and
\gamma>1/2,
the left-hand side goes to
\|u(1/2)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})},
while the right-hand side goes to 0 since
H(0)^{1/2}=\|e^{\gamma|x|^{2}}u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
and
H(1)^{1/2}=\|e^{\gamma|x|^{2}}u(1)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
are finite. This implies that
u(1/2)=0,
and thus
u\equiv 0.

However, to rigorously justify this argument, we need to know in advance that
\|e^{\phi}u(t)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
is finite for all time
t\in[0,1]
and for a suitable weight
\phi
. This is not obvious in general, even though we know that the weighted norm of the solution is finite at two times
0
and
1.
In
[
10
]
, a large part of the paper is dedicated to proving this result. Then the authors are able to conclude the proof by using a slightly different convexity argument, still relying on Carleman estimates. In this work, we follow the same path as in
[
10
]
.

Step 3: rigorous justification of the weighted norms of the solution for all
0\leq t\leq 1
.

The goal is to prove a persistence property for a Gaussian weighted
L^{2}
-norm of the solution
u
of (
1.6
), as well as an average in time estimate of the Gaussian weighted
L^{2}
-norm of
\nabla u
, by assuming that the Gaussian weighted norm of
u
is finite at times
t=0
and
t=1,
and under two different assumptions on the potential
V
. This result is the counterpart of Theorem 3 and Theorem 5 in
[
10
]
for the hyperbolic Schrödinger equation.

Theorem 5
.

Let
u\in C([0,1],L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
be a solution of the hyperbolic Schrödinger equation with potential (
1.6

\gamma>0

(1.9)

\|e^{\gamma|x|^{2}}u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\text{ and
}\|e^{\gamma|x|^{2}}u(1)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}<\infty.

If
V
is a bounded potential satisfying either

(1.10)

\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\|e^{\gamma|x|^{2}}V(t)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}<\infty

or

(1.11)

\lim_{R\to\infty}\|V\|_{L^{1}([0,1],L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{R}))}=0,

then for all
t\in[0,1]
,

(1.12)

\|e^{\gamma|x|^{2}}u(t)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq N(V)\left(\|e^{\gamma|x|^{2}}u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+\|e^{\gamma|x|^{2}}u(1)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\|u(t)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\right),

and

(1.13)

\|\sqrt{t(1-t)}e^{\gamma|x|^{2}}\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,1])}\leq N(V)\left(\|e^{\gamma|x|^{2}}u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+\|e^{\gamma|x|^{2}}u(1)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\|u(t)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\right),

where

N(V)=\begin{cases}C_{1}(V)e^{N(C_{1}(V)+C_{1}(V)^{2})}&\text{if $V$ satisfies }\eqref{cond1},\\
N(\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\|V(t)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})})^{2}&\text{if $V$ satisfies }\eqref{cond2},\end{cases}

C_{1}(V)=\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\|e^{\gamma|x|^{2}}V(t)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}e^{2\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\|ImV(t)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}}
and

is a positive constant.

To prove Theorem
5
, we follow the strategy in
[
10
]
. The main idea is to perform a parabolic regularization on (
1.6
) and prove similar estimates to (
1.12
) and (
1.13
) for solutions of the regularized equation

(1.14)

\partial_{t}v=A\Delta v+iB(Lv+Vv+F),

where

In fact, if
u
is a solution to (
1.6
) we work with
u_{\epsilon}
, a solution to the regularized equation

(1.15)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u_{\epsilon}=\epsilon\Delta u_{\epsilon}+i(Lu_{\epsilon}+F_{\epsilon}),

where

so that
u_{\epsilon}(t)=e^{\epsilon\Delta t}u(t).

We will first prove energy- and Carleman estimates for solutions of the parabolic equation (
1.14

Then we will take the limit as
\epsilon\to 0

Step 4: energy estimate for the regularized equation (
1.14

We prove an energy estimate for a specific weight function
\phi(x,t)=s(t)|x|^{2},
where
s(t)=\frac{\gamma A}{A+8\gamma(A^{2}+B^{2})t}

\displaystyle\|e^{s(t)|x|^{2}}

\displaystyle u(T)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2}+A\|\nabla(e^{s(t)|x|^{2}}u)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,T])}^{2}+2A\|2s(t)|x|e^{s(t)|x|^{2}}u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,T])}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq e^{M_{V}+|B|}\left(\|e^{\gamma|x|^{2}}u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+\|e^{s(t)|x|^{2}}F(t)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,T])}^{2}\right),

for

,
T\in[0,1]
and
M_{V}=\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\|B\,\text{Im}\ V(t)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

Remark 1.3
.

For this result, we only need the weighted
L^{2}
-norm to be finite at one time, (

Remark 1.4

The estimate (

Step 5: Carleman estimate for the regularized equation (
1.14
).

Now, we use the energy estimate (
1.4
) to prove that for a solution
u
of (
1.14
) satisfying (
1.9
), we have, for any time
0\leq t\leq 1

(1.17)

\displaystyle\|e^{\gamma|x|^{2}}u(t)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq e^{N((B^{2}(M_{1}^{2}+M_{2}^{2})+|B|(M_{1}+M_{2}))}\|e^{\gamma|x|^{2}}u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{1-t}\|e^{\gamma|x|^{2}}u(1)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{t},

This is a classical Carleman argument, and the main problem is to rigorously justify that
\|e^{\gamma|x|^{2}}u(t)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
is finite for
0<t<1
. In
[
10
]
, for the elliptic Schrödinger equation, the authors achieve this by introducing the weight

\tilde{\phi}_{a}(x)=\begin{cases}|x|^{2}&|x|<1\\
\frac{2|x|^{2-a}-a}{2-a}&|x|\geq 1,\end{cases}

for
0<a<1

and define
\tilde{\phi}_{a,\rho}=\tilde{\phi}_{a}*\theta_{\rho}
, for a radial mollifier
\theta_{\rho}
, such that at infinity,
e^{\gamma\phi_{a,\rho}}u
does not grow faster than
e^{s(t)|x|^{2}}u
, where
s(t)
is the weight obtained in the energy estimate (
1.4
). However, in the hyperbolic case, some new cross terms between the parabolic regularization
A\Delta
and the hyperbolic operator
iBL
appear in the expression of the commutator
[\mathcal{S,A}]
in the Carleman estimate. It is not clear how to deal with these terms. To avoid this issue, we chose to work with a different weight function
\phi_{a}
, where these specific cross terms vanish. In particular, we introduce the function,

\displaystyle\psi_{a}(x_{j}):=\begin{cases}x_{j}^{2}&if\ |x_{j}|<1\\
\frac{2|x_{j}|^{2-a}-a}{2-a}&if\ |x_{j}|\geq 1,\end{cases}

\displaystyle\phi_{a}(x):=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\psi_{a}(x_{j}),

where
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{j},\dots,x_{n}).

For this weight function we have that
\|e^{\gamma\phi_{a,\rho}}u(t)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq\|e^{s(t)|x|^{2}}u(t)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}<\infty
by (
1.4
), and thus we can rigorously justify the Carleman argument in this case. Finally, we conclude the proof of (
1.17
) by letting
a
and
\rho
to
0
.

Step 6: Carleman estimate for the gradient of the solutions to (
1.14
).

We also need a similar result for
e^{\gamma|x|^{2}}\nabla u
. In particular, we show that

\displaystyle\|\sqrt{t(1-t)}e^{\gamma|x|^{2}}\nabla

\displaystyle u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,1])}+\|\sqrt{t(1-t)}|x|e^{\gamma|x|^{2}}u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,1])}

(1.19)

\displaystyle\leq N(A,B,\gamma,M_{1})[\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\|e^{\gamma|x|^{2}}u(t)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\|e^{\gamma|x|^{2}}F\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}],

where the constant

is bounded away from

In

. We start by proving a Carleman estimate for compactly supported functions in both space and time. In particular, for

\phi=\mu|x+Rt(1-t)\tilde{\xi}|^{2}+(1+\epsilon)R^{2}t(1-t)/16\mu,\epsilon>0,\mu>0,R>0,\ |\xi|=1,\text{ and }g\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1}),

R\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon}{8\mu}}\|e^{\phi}g\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1})}\leq\|e^{\phi}(\partial_{t}-iL)g\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1})}.

Then, we introduce the function

where
\theta_{M}
and
\eta_{R}
are compactly supported cutoff functions in space and time respectively, and satisfy

in an open ball in

depending on a large parameter
R
and some
\epsilon>0
, depending on
\gamma.
By applying the Carleman estimate, it follows that

\displaystyle R\|e^{\phi}g\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,1])}

\displaystyle\leq N(\epsilon,\mu,\gamma)\left(R\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\|e^{\gamma|x|^{2}}\tilde{u}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+\frac{1}{M}e^{\gamma R^{2}/\epsilon}\|e^{\gamma|x|^{2}}(|u|+|\nabla u|)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[\frac{1}{2R},1-\frac{1}{2R}])}\right).

is finite thanks to Theorem

we can bound

which will imply that for some constant

2.2.
The “hyperbolic”Laplacian

We fix
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
1\leq k<n
and define the “hyperbolic ”  Laplace operator

L=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial{x_{j}}^{2}}-\sum_{j=k+1}^{n}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial{x_{j}}^{2}}=\Delta_{+}-\Delta_{-}.

Moreover, we denote

\displaystyle\nabla_{+}:=\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{1}},\cdot\cdot\cdot,\frac{\partial}{\partial_{x_{k}}}\right),\quad\nabla_{-}:=\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{k+1}},\cdot\cdot\cdot,\frac{\partial}{\partial_{x_{n}}}\right)

and

\nabla_{H}:=\left(\nabla_{+},-\nabla_{-}\right).

Then it follows that

L=\nabla\cdot\nabla_{H}.

Similarly, for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
we define

x_{+}:=(x_{1},\dots,x_{k}),\quad x_{-}:=(x_{k+1},\dots,x_{n})

and

\tilde{x}=(x_{+},-x_{-})=(x_{1},\dots,x_{k},-x_{k+1},\dots,-x_{n}),

so that
|\tilde{x}|^{2}=|x|^{2}
.
It follows from the definitions that

\displaystyle\nabla f\cdot\nabla_{H}g=\nabla_{H}f\cdot\nabla g,\quad|\nabla f|^{2}=|\nabla_{H}f|^{2},\quad L(fg)=Lf+Lg+2\nabla_{H}f\cdot\nabla g.

We also define the matrix of second order derivatives

\displaystyle D^{2}_{H}f:=\begin{bmatrix}\partial^{2}_{x_{1}}f&\dots&\partial^{2}_{x_{1},x_{k}}f&-\partial^{2}_{x_{1},x_{k+1}}f&\dots&-\partial^{2}_{x_{1},x_{n}}f\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\partial^{2}_{x_{k},x_{1}}f&\dots&\partial^{2}_{x_{k}}f&-\partial^{2}_{x_{k},x_{k+1}}f&\dots&-\partial^{2}_{x_{k},x_{n}}f\\
-\partial^{2}_{x_{k+1},x_{1}}f&\dots&-\partial_{x_{k+1},x_{k}}f&\partial^{2}_{x_{k+1}}f&\dots&\partial^{2}_{x_{k+1},x_{n}}f\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
-\partial^{2}_{x_{n},x_{1}}f&\dots&-\partial^{2}_{x_{n},x_{k}}f&\partial^{2}_{x_{n},x_{k+1}}f&\dots&\partial^{2}_{x_{n}}f\end{bmatrix}

which looks like the Hessian matrix, but for
1\leq i\leq k
and
k+1\leq j\leq n
the mixed derivative
\partial^{2}_{x_{i},x_{j}}
takes a negative sign. If both
1\leq i,j\leq k
or
k+1\leq i,j\leq n
then
\partial^{2}_{x_{i},x_{j}}
takes a positive sign.

Observe that if f is a function for which
\partial^{2}_{x_{i},x_{j}}f=0
for all
i\neq j
, then
D^{2}_{H}f=D^{2}f
where
D^{2}f
is the Hessian matrix of
f.

2.3.
The orthogonal and the indefinite orthogonal group

The orthogonal group
O(n)
consists of
n\times n
invertible matrices
A
satisfying
A^{T}A=I
, where
I
is the identity matrix.

Let
J
be the non-degenerate bilinear form represented by the matrix

J=\text{diag}(\underbrace{1,\dots,1}_{k},\underbrace{-1,\dots,-1}_{n-k}).

We define the indefinite orthogonal group
O(k,n-k)
, sometimes also called pseudo orthogonal group or generalized orthogonal group, to be the group of invertible
n\times n
matrices
A
such that

(2.1)

\displaystyle A^{T}JA=J.

In particular, the group
O(1,3)
is known as the Lorentz group and is of interest in relativity. For more information, see, for example, Chapter 1 in
[
17
]
, Chapter 4 in
[
23
]
and the references therein.

For
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
we use the notation

(2.2)

\langle\lambda,\lambda\rangle_{k,n-k}=\lambda^{T}J\lambda=\lambda_{1}^{2}+\dots+\lambda_{k}^{2}-\lambda_{k+1}^{2}-\dots-\lambda_{n}^{2}.

Observe that
\langle\lambda,\lambda\rangle_{k,n-k}
can be positive, negative and
0
depending on
\lambda
.

Remark 2.1
.

Using this notation, we can also write

\displaystyle\nabla_{H}

\displaystyle=J\nabla,\quad L=\nabla\cdot J\nabla,\quad D^{2}_{H}=JD^{2}J.

We now connect these groups with the
differential operators
\Delta
and
L
. Since
\Delta
is invariant under the orthogonal group
O(n)
, it follows that if
u
is a solution to the equation

\partial_{t}u=i(\Delta u+V(x,t)u+F(x,t)),

then the function
v(x,t)=u(Ax,t)
, for
A\in O(n)
, satisfies the Schrödinger equation

\partial_{t}v=i(\Delta v+V(Ax,t)v+F(Ax,t)).

Similarly, the operator
L
is invariant under the group
O(k,n-k)
. Indeed, if
A\in O(k,n-k)
, and
y=Ax

then, we have by the chain rule

\displaystyle Lu(y)=\nabla_{y}^{T}J\nabla_{y}u(y)=\nabla_{y}^{T}AJA^{T}\nabla_{y}u(y)=(A^{T}\nabla_{y})^{T}J(A^{T}\nabla_{y})u(y)=\nabla_{x}^{T}J\nabla_{x}(u(Ax))=L(u\circ A)(x).

Recall that the action of
O(n)
on the Euclidean sphere
S^{n-1}_{c}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:|x|^{2}=c\}

O(n)\times S^{n-1}_{c}\to S^{n-1}_{c}

is transitive. This means that for every
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
, there is
A\in O(n)
such that
A\lambda=\sqrt{c}e_{1},
for
c=|\lambda|^{2}.

Consider now the hyperbolic space

H_{c}^{n-1}(k):=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:x_{1}^{2}+\dots+x_{k}^{2}-x_{k+1}^{2}-\dots-x_{n}^{2}=\langle x,x\rangle_{k,n-k}=c\}.

If
c\neq 0,
the action

O(k,n-k)\times H^{n-1}_{c}(k)\to H^{n-1}_{c}(k)

is transitive (see for example Chapter 3 in
[
21
]
). In particular, let
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, with
c=\langle\lambda,\lambda\rangle_{k,n-k}\neq 0
. Then
\lambda\in H_{c}^{n-1}(k)
, and there exist
A\in O(k,n-k)
such that

A\lambda=\begin{cases}\pm\sqrt{c}e_{1}&\text{if }c>0,\\
\pm\sqrt{-c}e_{n}&\text{if }c<0.\end{cases}

Remark 2.2
.

•

The
\pm
depends on the sign on
\lambda_{1}
in the case
c>0,
and the sign of
\lambda_{n}
if
c<0.

•

e_{1}
can be replaced by
e_{j}
for any
1\leq j\leq k
and
e_{n}
can be replaced by any
e_{j}
for
k+1\leq j\leq n.

•

In the case
c=0
the above argument does not apply.

Some basic lemmas

First we state some basic bounds in

obtained from standard energy estimate, similar to the elliptic case.

Lemma 2.1
.

Suppose

satisfies

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u=i(Lu+V(x,t)u)\ \ \ \text{in}\ \mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,1]\\
u(x,0)=u_{0},\end{cases}

then for
N_{V}=e^{\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\|ImV(t)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}}
,

(2.3)

N_{V}^{-1}\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq\|u(t)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq N_{V}\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

Next, we restate the conformal/Appell transform for the hyperbolic Schrödinger equation from
[
1
]
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let

\partial_{s}u=i(Lu+V(y,s)u+F(y,s))\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \text{in}\ \mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,1].

For
\alpha,\beta>0,

\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
, define

\tilde{u}(x,t)=\Big(\frac{\sqrt{\alpha\beta}}{\alpha(1-t)+\beta t}\Big)^{n/2}u\Big(\frac{\sqrt{\alpha\beta}x}{\alpha(1-t)+\beta t},\frac{\beta t}{\alpha(1-t)+\beta t}\Big)e^{\frac{(\alpha-\beta)(|x_{+}|^{2}-|x_{-}|^{2})}{4i(\alpha(1-t)+\beta t)}}.

Then
\tilde{u}
satisfies

\partial_{t}\tilde{u}=i(L\tilde{u}+\tilde{V}(x,t)\tilde{u}+\tilde{F}(x,t))\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \text{in }\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,1],

where

\tilde{V}(x,t)=\frac{\alpha\beta}{(\alpha(1-t)+\beta t)^{2}}V\Big(\frac{\sqrt{\alpha\beta}x}{\alpha(1-t)+\beta t},\frac{\beta t}{\alpha(1-t)+\beta t}\Big)

\tilde{F}(x,t)=\left(\frac{\sqrt{\alpha\beta}}{\alpha(1-t)+\beta t}\right)^{n/2+2}F\Big(\frac{\sqrt{\alpha\beta}x}{\alpha(1-t)+\beta t},\frac{\beta t}{\alpha(1-t)+\beta t}\Big)e^{\frac{(\alpha-\beta)(|x_{+}|^{2}-|x_{-}|^{2})}{4i(\alpha(1-t)+\beta t)}}

Moreover,

(2.4)

\|e^{\gamma|x|^{2}}\tilde{F}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=\frac{\alpha\beta}{(\alpha(1-t)+\beta t)^{2}}\|e^{\frac{\gamma\alpha\beta}{(\alpha s+\beta(1-s))^{2}}|x|^{2}}F(s)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})},

and

(2.5)

\|e^{\gamma|x|^{2}}\tilde{u}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=\|e^{\frac{\gamma\alpha\beta}{(\alpha s+\beta(1-s))^{2}}|x|^{2}}u(s)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

for

3.1.
Energy estimate

We start by proving an energy estimate for solutions of the parabolic equation (
1.14
). As we commented in the introduction, we need to expand the energy estimate in
[
10
]
to be able to justify all the computations.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
u\in C([0,1],L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))\cap L^{2}([0,1],H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
satisfy

\partial_{t}u=A\Delta u+iB(Lu+V(x,t)u+F(x,t))\ \ \ \text{in }\ \mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,1],

for
A>0
,
B\in\mathbb{R}
,
F\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,1]),\ V
bounded. Then

\displaystyle\|e^{s(t)|x|^{2}}

\displaystyle u(T)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2}+A\|\nabla(e^{s(t)|x|^{2}}u)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,T])}^{2}+2A\|2s(t)|x|e^{s(t)|x|^{2}}u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,T])}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq e^{M_{V}+|B|}\left(\|e^{\gamma|x|^{2}}u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+\|e^{s(t)|x|^{2}}F(t)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,T])}^{2}\right),

for
s(t)=\frac{\gamma A}{A+8(A^{2}+B^{2})\gamma t}
,
\gamma>0
,
T\in[0,1]
and
M_{V}=\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\|B\text{Im}\ V(t)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
.

Proof.

Let
f=e^{\phi}u,
where
\phi=\phi(x,t)
is a real-valued weight function to be chosen later.
Then

\partial_{t}f=(\mathcal{S+A})f+iBe^{\phi}F,

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}\phi+A(|\nabla\phi|^{2}+\Delta)-iB(L\phi+2\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla_{H})-BImV,

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle=iB(\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla_{H}\phi+L)-A(\Delta\phi+2\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla)+iBReV,

and

(3.1)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}}^{2})

\displaystyle=2Re\langle Sf,f\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+2Re\langle iBe^{\phi}F,f\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

Integration by parts shows that

\displaystyle Re\langle Sf,f\rangle

\displaystyle=-A\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\nabla f|^{2}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(A|\nabla\phi|^{2}+\partial_{t}\phi\right)|f|^{2}+2BIm\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\nabla_{H}\phi\cdot\nabla f\bar{f}-B\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}ImV|f|^{2}.

Cauchy Schwartz’ and Young’s inequalities show that

\displaystyle 2BIm\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla_{H}f\bar{f}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{A}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\nabla_{H}f|^{2}+\frac{2B^{2}}{A}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\nabla\phi f|^{2},

and

\displaystyle Re\langle Sf,f\rangle

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{A}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\nabla f|^{2}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}((A+\frac{2B^{2}}{A})|\nabla\phi|^{2}+\partial_{t}\phi)|f|^{2}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|BImV||f|^{2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{A}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\nabla f|^{2}-A\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\nabla\phi f|^{2}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}((2A+\frac{2B^{2}}{A})|\nabla\phi|^{2}+\partial_{t}\phi)|f|^{2}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|BImV||f|^{2}.

If
\phi
is a function that satisfies

(3.2)

\left(\frac{2B^{2}}{A}+2A\right)|\nabla\phi|^{2}+\partial_{t}\phi\leq 0\quad\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,1],

then

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq-A\|\nabla f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2}-2A\|\nabla\phi f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2}+2\|BImV(t)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2}

(3.3)

\displaystyle\quad+2|B|\|e^{\phi}F\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

Ideally, we want
\phi(x,t)=s(t)|x|^{2},
which satisfies (
3.2
) when
s
solves the IVP

(3.4)

s^{\prime}(t)+8\left(\frac{B^{2}}{A}+A\right)s(t)^{2}=0,\quad s(0)=\gamma,

This holds when
s(t)=\frac{\gamma A}{8(A^{2}+B^{2})\gamma t+A}.

Let
M_{V}=\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\|BImV(t)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
.
Integrating (
3.1
) from
0
to
t
and Young’s inequality implies that

\displaystyle\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2}\leq

\displaystyle\|f(0)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2}-A\int_{0}^{t}\|\nabla f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2}ds-2A\int_{0}^{t}\|\nabla\phi f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2}ds+|B|\int_{0}^{t}\|e^{\phi}F\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2}ds

\displaystyle+(M_{V}+|B|)\int_{0}^{t}\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2}ds.

Applying Grönwall’s integral inequality, we deduce that

\displaystyle\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2}\leq\psi(t)+\int_{0}^{t}\psi(s)(M_{V}+|B|)e^{(M_{V}+|B|)(t-s)}ds,

where

\psi(t)=-A\int_{0}^{t}\|\nabla f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2}ds-2A\int_{0}^{t}\|\nabla\phi f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2}ds+\|f(0)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+|B|\int_{0}^{t}\|e^{\phi}F\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2}ds.

Moreover, we have that

\displaystyle(M_{V}+|B|)\int_{0}^{t}\psi(s)e^{(M_{V}+|B|)(t-s)}ds

\displaystyle\leq\|f(0)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2}(e^{(M_{V}+|B|)t}-1)+|B|\int_{0}^{t}\|e^{\phi}F\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2}d\tau(e^{(M_{V}+|B|)t}-1)

\displaystyle\ \ -A\int_{0}^{t}\|\nabla f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2}d\tau(e^{(M_{V}+|B|)t}-1)-2A\int_{0}^{t}\|\nabla\phi f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2}d\tau(e^{(M_{V}+|B|)t}-1).

Thus,

\displaystyle\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq e^{(M_{V}+|B|)t}\Big(-A\int_{0}^{t}\|\nabla f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}ds-2A\int_{0}^{t}\|\nabla\phi f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}ds+\|f(0)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle\quad+|B|\int_{0}^{t}\|e^{\phi}F\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}ds\Big),

which implies

\displaystyle\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2}+A\|\nabla f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,t])}^{2}+2A\|\nabla\phi f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,t])}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq e^{(M_{V}+|B|)}\left(\|f(0)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+\|e^{\phi}F\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,t])}\right).

This proves the result formally in the case when
\phi(x,t)=s(t)|x|^{2}.

To prove the result rigorously, we follow the argument in
[
10
]
. We define a cutoff function

\displaystyle\psi_{R}(x)=\begin{cases}|x|^{2}&\text{if}\ \ \ |x|\leq R,\\
R^{2}&\text{if}\ \ \ |x|>R,\end{cases},

a radial mollifier
\theta_{\rho}
with integral 1, and set
\phi_{\rho,R}(x,t)=s(t)(\theta_{\rho}*\psi_{R})(x),
for
s(t)=\frac{\gamma A}{8(A^{2}+B^{2})t\gamma+A}.

It follows that

\displaystyle\|f_{\rho,R}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq e^{\gamma R^{2}}\|u(t)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})},

so that
f_{\rho,R}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
for all
t\in[0,1].
Similarly, since

\nabla f_{\rho,R}=e^{\phi_{\rho,R}}(\nabla\phi_{\rho,R}u+\nabla u),

and

|\nabla\phi_{\rho,R}|\leq CR,

we also have

\|\nabla f_{\rho,R}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq e^{\gamma R^{2}}\left(C\gamma R\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\right)<\infty.

Moreover,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi_{\rho,R}+\left(\frac{2B^{2}}{A}+2A\right)|\nabla\phi_{\rho,R}|^{2}

\displaystyle=s^{\prime}(t)(\theta_{\rho}*\psi_{R})+\left(\frac{2B^{2}}{A}+2A\right)|\theta_{\rho}*\nabla\psi_{R}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq s^{\prime}(t)(|x|^{2}+C(n)\rho^{2})+s(t)^{2}\left(\frac{2B^{2}}{A}+2A\right)(C(n)(|x|^{2}+\rho^{2}),

so that

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi_{\rho,R}+8\left(\frac{2B^{2}}{A}+2A\right)|\nabla\phi_{\rho,R}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq s^{\prime}(t)(|x|^{2}+C(n)\rho^{2})+8s(t)^{2}\left(\frac{B^{2}}{A}+A\right)C(n)(|x|^{2}+\rho^{2})

\displaystyle\leq\left(s^{\prime}(t)+8\left(\frac{B^{2}}{A}+A\right)s(t)^{2})\right)C(n)(|x|^{2}+\rho^{2})=0,

and
\phi_{\rho,R}
satisfies (
3.2
) uniformly in
R
and
\rho.

Thus, by applying the formal argument for
f_{\rho,R}
the argument follows as we let
\rho
to
0
and
R
to
\infty.

∎

Then we state a general Carleman estimate proven in

Lemma 3.2
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{S}
is a symmetric operator,
\mathcal{A}
is a skew-symmetric operator, both can depend on a time variable,
G
is a positive function,
f(x,t)
is a reasonable function

H(t)=\langle f,f\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})},\ \ \ \ \ D(t)=\langle\mathcal{S}f,f\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})},\ \ \ \ \ N(t)=\frac{D(t)}{H(t)}.

Then

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{2}H

\displaystyle=2\partial_{t}\text{Re}\,\langle\partial_{t}f-\mathcal{S}f-\mathcal{A}f,f\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+2\langle\partial_{t}\mathcal{S}f+[\mathcal{S,A}]f,f\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

(3.5)

\displaystyle+\|\partial_{t}f-\mathcal{A}f+\mathcal{S}f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2}-\|\partial_{t}f-\mathcal{A}f-\mathcal{S}f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2},

and

(3.6)

N^{\prime}(t)\geq\frac{\langle\partial_{t}{\mathcal{S}}f+[\mathcal{S,A}]f,f\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}}{H}-\frac{\|\partial_{t}f-\mathcal{A}f-\mathcal{S}f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2}}{2H}.

Moreover, if

(3.7)

|\partial_{t}f-\mathcal{A}f-\mathcal{S}f|\leq M_{1}|f|+G\ \ \ \text{in}\ \ \mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,1],\ \ \ \partial_{t}\mathcal{S}+[\mathcal{S,A}]\geq-M_{0},

and

M_{2}=\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\left\|\frac{G(t)}{f(t)}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}<\infty,

then
\log{H(t)}
is convex in
[0,1]
and there is a universal constant
N
s.t

(3.8)

H(t)\leq e^{N(M_{0}+M_{1}+M_{2}+M_{1}^{2}+M_{2}^{2})}H(0)^{1-t}H(1)^{t}\ \ \ \ \text{when}\ 0\leq t\leq 1.

Now, we apply this Carleman estimate to the equation (
1.14
).

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
u\in C([0,1],L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))\cap L^{2}([0,1],H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
be a solution to the equation

(3.9)

\partial_{t}u=A\Delta u+iB(Lu+Vu+F(x,t)),\ \ \ \text{in }\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,1]

for
A>0,B\in\mathbb{R}
,
V
a complex-valued potential,
\gamma>0,
and
\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\|V(t)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq M_{1}.
Set

M_{2}=\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\frac{\|e^{\gamma|x|^{2}}F(t)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}}{\|u(t)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}},

and assume that
\|e^{\gamma|x|^{2}}u(1)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})},\|e^{\gamma|x|^{2}}u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
and

are finite. Then
\|e^{\gamma|x|^{2}}u(t)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
is logarithmically convex in

and there is a universal

\displaystyle\|e^{\gamma|x|^{2}}u(t)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq e^{N(B^{2}(M_{1}^{2}+M_{2}^{2})+|B|(M_{1}+M_{2})}\|e^{\gamma|x|^{2}}u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{1-t}\|e^{\gamma|x|^{2}}u(1)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{t}.

for

Proof.

Define
f=e^{\phi}u.
Now
f
satisfies the equation

\partial_{t}f=(\mathcal{S}+\mathcal{A})f+i(Vf+e^{\phi}F),

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}\phi+A(|\nabla\phi|^{2}+\Delta)-iB(L\phi+2\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla_{H})=:\partial_{t}\phi+A{E}_{1}-iBH_{2}

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle=iB(\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla_{H}\phi+L)-A(\Delta\phi+2\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla):=iBH_{1}-AE_{2}.

It follows that

\displaystyle[\mathcal{S,A}]

\displaystyle=-A^{2}(E_{1}E_{2}-E_{2}E_{1})-B^{2}(H_{1}H_{2}-H_{2}H_{1})+iAB(E_{1}H_{1}-H_{1}E_{1}+H_{2}E_{2}-E_{2}H_{2})

\displaystyle\ \ \ +A(E_{2}\partial_{t}\phi-\partial_{t}\phi E_{2})+iB(\partial_{t}\phi H_{1}-H_{1}\partial_{t}\phi),

and by a direct computation we have

\displaystyle[\mathcal{S,A}]

\displaystyle=A^{2}(4\nabla\phi\cdot D^{2}\phi\nabla\phi-4\nabla\cdot(D^{2}\phi\nabla)-\Delta^{2}\phi)+B^{2}(4\nabla\phi\cdot D^{2}_{H}\phi\nabla\phi-4\nabla\cdot D^{2}_{H}\phi\nabla-L^{2}\phi)

\displaystyle\ \ \ +iAB(E_{1}H_{1}-H_{1}E_{1}+H_{2}E_{2}-E_{2}H_{2})+2A(\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla\partial_{t}\phi)-iB(2\nabla\partial_{t}\phi\cdot\nabla_{H}+L(\partial_{t}\phi)).

Since

\partial_{t}\mathcal{S}=\partial_{t}^{2}\phi+2A(\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla\partial_{t}\phi)-iB(2\nabla\partial_{t}\phi\cdot\nabla_{H}+L(\partial_{t}\phi)),

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathcal{S}+[\mathcal{S,A}]

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}^{2}\phi+4A(\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla\partial_{t}\phi)-2iB(2\nabla\partial_{t}\phi\cdot\nabla_{H}+L(\partial_{t}\phi))

\displaystyle\ \ \ +A^{2}(4\nabla\phi\cdot D^{2}\phi\nabla\phi-4\nabla\cdot(D^{2}\phi\nabla)-\Delta^{2}\phi)+B^{2}(4\nabla\phi\cdot D^{2}_{H}\phi\nabla\phi-4\nabla\cdot D^{2}_{H}\phi\nabla-L^{2}\phi)

(3.10)

\displaystyle\ \ \ +iAB([E_{1},H_{1}]-[E_{2},H_{2}]).

The commutator
[\mathcal{S,A}]
differs from the elliptic case, since
E_{j}\neq H_{j}
for
j=1,2.
In particular, the cross terms between
Ej
and
H_{j}
that appears here, cancels out in the elliptic case. However, in the hyperbolic case we need to compute the commutators
[E_{1},H_{1}]
and
[E_{2},H_{2}]
. We have that

(3.11)

\displaystyle[E_{1},H_{1}]f

\displaystyle=\Delta(\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla_{H}\phi)f+2\nabla(\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla_{H}\phi)\cdot\nabla f-L(|\nabla\phi|^{2})f-2\nabla_{H}(|\nabla\phi|^{2})\cdot\nabla f,

(3.12)

\displaystyle[H_{2},E_{2}]f

\displaystyle=2\nabla_{H}\phi\cdot\nabla(\Delta\phi)f+4\nabla_{H}\phi\cdot\nabla(\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla f)-2\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla(L\phi)f-4\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla(\nabla_{H}\phi\cdot\nabla f).

Observe that both commutators depend on the mixed-partial derivatives of
\phi.
For example,

\displaystyle\Delta(\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla_{H}\phi)-L(|\nabla\phi|^{2})

\displaystyle=\nabla\cdot\nabla(\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla_{H}\phi)-\nabla\cdot\nabla_{H}(\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla\phi)

\displaystyle=2\nabla\cdot(D^{2}\phi\nabla_{H}\phi)-2\nabla\cdot(D^{2}_{H}\phi\nabla_{H}\phi).

If the mixed partial derivatives of
\phi
are zero, then
D^{2}\phi=D^{2}_{H}\phi
, so that

\displaystyle\Delta(\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla_{H}\phi)f-L(|\nabla\phi|^{2})f

\displaystyle=2\nabla\cdot(D^{2}\phi\nabla_{H}\phi)-2\nabla\cdot(D^{2}\phi\nabla_{H}\phi)=0.

Similarly, if all mixed partial derivatives are zero, then

\displaystyle(2\nabla(\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla_{H}\phi)-2\nabla_{H}(\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla\phi))\cdot\nabla f

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle(\nabla_{H}\phi\cdot(\nabla\phi)-\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla(L\phi))f

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle 4\nabla_{H}\phi\cdot\nabla(\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla f)-4\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla(\nabla_{H}\phi\cdot\nabla f)

\displaystyle=0

and in this case

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathcal{S}+[\mathcal{S,A}]

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}^{2}\phi+4A(\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla\partial_{t}\phi)-2iB(2\nabla\partial_{t}\phi\cdot\nabla_{H}+L(\partial_{t}\phi))

(3.13)

\displaystyle\ \ \ +(A^{2}+B^{2})(4\nabla\phi\cdot D^{2}\phi\nabla\phi-4\nabla\cdot(D^{2}\phi\nabla))-A^{2}\Delta^{2}\phi+-B^{2}L^{2}\phi.

If
\phi=\gamma|x|^{2},
then

\langle\partial_{t}\mathcal{S}f+[\mathcal{S,A}]f,f\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=\gamma(A^{2}+B^{2})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}32\gamma^{2}|x|^{2}|f|^{2}+8|\nabla f|^{2}\geq 0.

Since also

|\partial_{t}f-\mathcal{S}f-\mathcal{A}f|\leq|B|(\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\|V\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}|f|+e^{\phi}|F|),

it follows formally by Lemma
3.2
that

\displaystyle\|e^{\gamma|x|^{2}}u(t)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq e^{N(B^{2}(M_{1}^{2}+M_{2}^{2})+|B|(M_{1}+M_{2}))}\|e^{\gamma|x|^{2}}u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{1-t}\|e^{\gamma|x|^{2}}u(1)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{t}.

However, in order to justify that
\|e^{\phi}u(t)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

we need to modify the weight, which makes the computations more involved. The argument follows the same spirit as in
[
10
]
. However, due to the presence of the commutators

we work with a different weight function, chosen so that all mixed partial derivatives vanish, and therefore

for this specific weight.

In particular, we start by defining a binary sequence
b=b_{1}b_{2},\dots b_{n}
,
b_{j}\in\{0,1\}
, and let

\displaystyle R_{b}:=\Bigg\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\ \Bigg|\begin{cases}|x_{j}|<1&if\ b_{j}=0\\
|x_{j}|\geq 1&if\ b_{j}=1,\end{cases}\Bigg\}

\displaystyle\psi_{a}(x_{j}):=\begin{cases}x_{j}^{2}&if\ |x_{j}|<1\\
\frac{2|x_{j}|^{2-a}-a}{2-a}&if\ |x_{j}|\geq 1,\end{cases}

\displaystyle\phi_{a}(x):=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\psi_{a}(x_{j}).

Then
\phi_{a}
is defined differently in the
2^{n}
regions
R_{b}
will be continuous and differentiable in all of
\mathbb{R}^{n}.
Moreover,

\displaystyle\nabla\phi_{a}=(\psi^{\prime}(x_{1}),\dots,\psi^{\prime}(x_{n})),\quad\Delta\phi_{a}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\psi^{\prime\prime}_{a}(x_{j}),\quad L\phi_{a}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\psi^{\prime\prime}(x_{j})-\sum_{j=k+1}^{n}\psi^{\prime\prime}(x_{j}),

where

\displaystyle\psi_{a}^{\prime}(x_{j})=\begin{cases}2x_{j}&if\ |x_{j}|<1,\\
2|x_{j}|^{1-a}sgn(x_{j})&if\ |x_{j}|\geq 1,\end{cases}\quad\quad\psi_{a}^{\prime\prime}(x_{j})=\begin{cases}2&if\ |x_{j}|<1,\\
2(1-a)|x_{j}|^{-a}&|x_{j}|\geq 1.\end{cases}

Since neither
\Delta\phi_{a}
or
L\phi_{a}
is continuous at the boundaries of the regions
R_{b}
, we need to compute the third derivative in the distributional sense. It follows that the distribution

\displaystyle\partial_{x_{j}}\Delta\phi_{a}

\displaystyle=\psi_{a}(x_{j})^{\prime\prime\prime}

\displaystyle=2a(\mu_{(x_{j}=-1)}-\mu_{(x_{j}=1)})-2a(a-1)|x_{j}|^{-a-1}sgn(x_{j})\mathbbm{1}_{|x_{j}|\geq 1},

where

is the distribution defined by

\langle\mu_{(x_{j}=\pm 1)},g\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}g(x_{1},\dots,x_{j-1},\pm 1,x_{j+1},x_{n})dx

for

Moreover, let
\theta_{\rho}\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be a mollifier, and let

It follows that

\Delta^{2}\phi_{a,\rho}=\gamma\sum_{j=1}^{n}\partial_{j}\Delta\phi_{a}*\partial_{j}\theta_{\rho},
so that

\displaystyle\|\Delta^{2}\phi_{a,\rho}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\sum_{j=1}^{n}\|\partial_{j}\Delta\phi_{a}*\partial_{j}\theta_{\rho}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Big(2a(1-a)\||x_{j}|^{-a-1}\mathbbm{1}_{|x_{j}|\geq 1}*\partial_{j}\theta_{\rho}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle\quad+2a\|(\mu_{(x_{j}=-1)}-\mu_{(x_{j}=1)})*\partial_{j}\theta_{\rho}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\Big).

Since

\|(\mu_{(x_{j}=-1)}-\mu_{(x_{j}=1)})*\partial_{j}\theta_{\rho}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq C(n,\rho,\theta)

and

\||x_{j}|^{-a-1}\mathbbm{1}_{|x_{j}|\geq 1}*\partial_{j}\theta_{\rho}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq\|1*\partial_{j}\theta_{\rho}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq C(n,\rho,\theta),

it follows that

(3.14)

\|\Delta^{2}\phi_{a,\rho}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq C(n,\rho,\theta)a.

A similar argument shows that

(3.15)

\|L^{2}\phi_{a,\rho}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq C(n,\rho,\theta)a.

Since the mixed partial derivatives are 0,

, and

3.2
) now becomes

(3.16)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathcal{S}_{a,\rho}+[\mathcal{S}_{a,\rho},\mathcal{A}_{a,\rho}]

\displaystyle=4(A^{2}+B^{2})(\nabla_{\phi_{a,\rho}}\cdot D^{2}\phi_{a,\rho}\nabla\phi_{a,\rho}-\nabla\cdot D^{2}\phi_{a,\rho}\nabla)-A^{2}\Delta^{2}\phi_{a,\rho}-B^{2}L^{2}\phi_{a,\rho},

\displaystyle\langle(\partial_{t}\mathcal{S}_{a,\rho}+[\mathcal{S}_{a,\rho},\mathcal{A}_{a,\rho}])f_{a,\rho},f_{a,\rho}\rangle

\displaystyle\quad-A^{2}\langle\Delta^{2}\phi_{a,\rho}f_{a,\rho},f_{a,\rho}\rangle-B^{2}\langle L^{2}\phi_{a,\rho}f_{a,\rho},f_{a,\rho}\rangle

\displaystyle=4(A^{2}+B^{2})\left(\langle\nabla\phi_{a,\rho}^{T}D^{2}\phi_{a,\rho}\nabla\phi_{a\rho}f_{a,\rho},f_{a,\rho}\rangle+\langle D^{2}\phi_{a,\rho}\nabla f_{a,\rho},\nabla f_{a,\rho}\rangle\right)

(3.17)

\displaystyle\quad-A^{2}\langle\Delta^{2}\phi_{a,\rho}f_{a,\rho},f_{a,\rho}\rangle-B^{2}\langle L^{2}\phi_{a,\rho}f_{a,\rho},f_{a,\rho}\rangle.

Since

is positive definite, and by (

) and (

\partial_{t}\mathcal{S}_{a,\rho}+[\mathcal{S}_{a,\rho},\mathcal{A}_{a,\rho}]\geq-C(\rho,\theta)a=-M_{0}(a,\rho),

where

when

Moreover,

(3.19)

|(\partial_{t}-\mathcal{S}_{a,\rho}-\mathcal{A}_{a,\rho})f_{a,\rho}|\leq|B|(M_{1}|f_{a,\rho}|+e^{\phi_{a,\rho}}F),

and by using that

we have that

(3.20)

M_{2}(a,\rho):=\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\frac{\|e^{\phi_{a,\rho}}F\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}}{\|f_{a,\rho}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}}\leq e^{C(n)\rho^{2}}M_{2}:=M_{2,\rho},

Applying Lemma
3.2

and that

\|e^{\phi_{a,\rho}}u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq e^{N(B^{2}(M_{1}^{2}+M_{2,\rho}^{2})+|B|(M_{1}+M_{2,\rho}+M_{0}(a,\rho))}\|e^{\phi_{a,\rho}}u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}}^{(1-t)}\|e^{\phi_{a,\rho}}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{t}.

By first taking the limit as

∎

Now we prove a similar result for

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
u\in C([0,1],L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))\cap L^{2}([0,1],H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))

\displaystyle\|\sqrt{t(1-t)}e^{\gamma|x|^{2}}\nabla

\displaystyle u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,1])}+\|\sqrt{t(1-t)}|x|e^{\gamma|x|^{2}}u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,1])}

\displaystyle\leq N(A,B,\gamma,M_{1})[\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\|e^{\gamma|x|^{2}}u(t)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\|e^{\gamma|x|^{2}}F\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}]

is bounded away from

.

Proof.

Let
f=e^{\phi}u.
Assuming all computations in Lemma

are justified for

) with

and integrate from 0 to 1. Integrating by parts two times, we get for the left-hand side of (

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\partial_{t}^{2}H(t)t(1-t)dt

\displaystyle\leq H(0)+H(1).

For the first part of the right-hand side of (

), we get

(3.25)

\displaystyle 2\int_{0}^{1}t(1-t)\partial_{t}Re\langle\partial_{t}f-\mathcal{S}f-\mathcal{A}f,f\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}dt

\displaystyle=-2\int_{0}^{1}(1-2t)Re\langle\partial_{t}f-\mathcal{S}f-\mathcal{A}f,f\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}dt.

It then follows from (

Lemma 4.1

For

Proof.

Let

Then

\displaystyle e^{\phi}(\partial_{t}-iL)e^{-\phi}f

\displaystyle=-\partial_{t}\phi f+\partial_{t}f-i(\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla_{H}\phi-2i\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla_{H}-L\phi+L)f

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}f-\mathcal{S}f-\mathcal{A}f,

where

is the symmetric operator

From (
3.2
) with

and for
\phi(x,t)=\mu|x+Rt(1-t)\tilde{\xi}|^{2}-(1+\epsilon)R^{2}t(1-t)/16\mu

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathcal{S}+[\mathcal{S,A}]=

\displaystyle 2\mu R^{2}(1-2t)^{2}|\xi|^{2}-4\mu R\tilde{\xi}(x+Rt(1-t)\tilde{\xi})+\frac{(1+\epsilon)R^{2}}{8\mu}+32\mu^{3}|x+Rt(1-t)\tilde{\xi}|^{2}

\displaystyle-8\mu\Delta-8i\mu R(1-2t)\xi\cdot\nabla.

Thus,

\displaystyle+\frac{(1+\epsilon)R^{2}}{8\mu}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|f|^{2}dx+32\mu^{3}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|x+Rt(1-t)\tilde{\xi}|^{2}|f|^{2}+8\mu\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\nabla f|^{2}dx

\displaystyle-8i\mu R\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(1-2t)\xi\cdot\nabla f\bar{f}dx

\displaystyle=(1)+(2)+(3)+(4)+(5)+(6).

By Cauchy Schwartz and Young’s inequalities, and using that

we can show that

\displaystyle(4)-|(2)|\geq-\frac{R^{2}}{8\mu}\|f\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})},

so that

Finally, since

it follows that
