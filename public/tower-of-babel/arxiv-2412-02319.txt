(Positive) Quadratic Determinantal Representations of Quartic Curves and the Robinson Polynomial Thanks: Both authors were supported by the DFG grant 502861109.
Clemens Brüser, Mario Kummer

Abstract
. We prove that every real nonnegative ternary quartic whose complex zero set is smooth can be represented as the determinant of a symmetric matrix with quadratic entries which is everywhere positive semidefinite. We show that the corresponding statement fails for the Robinson polynomial, answering a question by Buckley and Šivic.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary: 14P99, 14Q30

1.
Introduction

Let
F\in{\mathbb{R}}[x_{0},x_{1},x_{2}]_{2d}
be a homogeneous polynomial of degree
2d
. We say that
F
has a
positive quadratic (determinantal) representation
if there exists a symmetric
d\times d
matrix
M=M(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2})
whose entries are homogeneous real polynomials of degree two such that
F=\det(M)
and
M(a)
is positive semidefinite for all
a\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
. Since the determinant of a positive semidefinite matrix is nonnegative, the existence of such a representation implies that
F
is a
nonnegative polynomial
, meaning that
F(a)\geq 0
for all
a\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
. Already for
d=3
it has been open whether conversely every nonnegative
F\in{\mathbb{R}}[x_{0},x_{1},x_{2}]_{6}
has a positive quadratic representation
[
Qua15
, §7]
. Recently, this problem has also drawn attention in material science
[
HH22
, p. 7]
. Buckley and Šivic conjectured in
[
BŠ20
]
that the answer is negative and they suggest that the so-called
Robinson polynomial

R=x_{0}^{6}-x_{0}^{4}x_{1}^{2}-x_{0}^{2}x_{1}^{4}+x_{1}^{6}-x_{0}^{4}x_{2}^{2}+3x_{0}^{2}x_{1}^{2}x_{2}^{2}-x_{1}^{4}x_{2}^{2}-x_{0}^{2}x_{2}^{4}-x_{1}^{2}x_{2}^{4}+x_{2}^{6}.

could be such a counterexample. This article establishes two key results. First it establishes that for the Robinson polynomial such a representation does not exist.

Theorem 1.1
.

The Robinson polynomial
R
does not have a positive quadratic representation.

In earlier work by Quarez it has been shown that in degree four, too, there are polynomials that do not admit a positive quadratic representation (see
[
Qua15
, §7]
). Our second result proves that every such counter-example must necessarily define a singular plane curve.

Theorem 1.2
.

Every smooth positive ternary quartic has a positive quadratic representation.

Throughout this article we will restrict considerations to equivalence classes of determinantal representations.

Definition 1.3
.

Let
F=\det(M)=\det(M^{\prime})
be two symmetric quadratic representations of
F\in K[x_{0},x_{1},x_{2}]
(with
K\in\{{\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}}\}
). We say that
M
and
M^{\prime}
are
equivalent
quadratic representations, if there exists an invertible matrix of constants
T\in M_{d}[K]
satisfying
M^{\prime}=T^{t}MT
.

2.
The Robinson Polynomial

2.1.
General Observations

For proving
1.1
we first need to find means to deal with the singularities of the curve defined by the Robinson polynomial. To this end, in this section we lay out preliminary observations and derive necessary conditions for a positive quadratic representation to exist for the Robinson polynomial. We fix a symmetric
d\times d
matrix
M=M(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2})
the entries of which are homogeneous polynomials of degree two such that
M(a)
is positive semidefinite for all
a\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
and such that
F=\det(M)\in\textrm{P}_{2d}
is not identically zero. We further denote
\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}=\operatorname{Proj}({\mathbb{R}}[x_{0},x_{1},x_{2}])
and let
X={\mathcal{V}}(F)\subset\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}
be the zero set of
F
, which is a projective scheme of (pure) dimension one over
{\mathbb{R}}
. First we note that under some conditions one can bound the dimension of the kernel of
M
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
0\neq a\in{\mathbb{C}}^{3}
such that the kernel of the complex
d\times d
matrix
M(a)
has dimension at least two. Then
[a]\in X({\mathbb{C}})
is a singular point of
X
.

Proof.

By the chain rule, the total derivative of
\det(M)
at
a
is given by

\textrm{d}(\det\circ M)|_{a}=\textrm{d}\det|_{M(a)}\circ\textrm{d}M|_{a}.

Now

(\textrm{d}\det|_{M(a)})_{i,j}=\left(\frac{\partial\det}{\partial x_{ij}}\right)M(a)=(-1)^{i+j}\det(M^{(i,j)}(a)),

where
M^{(i,j)}
indicates that the
i
-th row and
j
-th column of
M
have been eliminated, i.e. the
(i,j)
-th cofactor of
M
. By choice of
a
, we know that the determinant of every submatrix of
M(a)
of size at least
(d-1)\times(d-1)
equals
0
and the claim follows.
∎

Lemma 2.2
.

Assume that
d=3
and that there exists
0\neq a\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
such that the kernel of
M(a)
has dimension at least two. Then
F
is a sum of squares of homogeneous polynomials of degree three.

Proof.

By
[
Qua15
, Theorem 4.4]
any positive semidefinite biquadratic form
B(x,y)
in the variables
x=(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2})^{t},y=(y_{0},y_{1},y_{2})^{t}
that has an infinite number of zeros in
\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}\times\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}
is a sum of squares. We apply this to the biquadratic form given by
B(x,y)=y^{t}M(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2})y
. By assumption,
M(a)
is positive semidefinite for arbitrary
a
, hence
B
is a positive semidefinite form. For our specific choice of
a
, the dimension of the kernel of
M(a)
being at least two yields an infinite number of zeros. This shows that
B
is a sum of squares.

We now prove that
\det(M)
is a sum of squares following ideas in
[
Qua15
, Proposition 5.6]
. First note that, since
B
is a sum of squares, we have
M=A^{t}A
for some matrix
A\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]^{k\times 3}
, where
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
and the entries of
A
are linear homogeneous polynomials in
x
. For
S\in\{1,\dots,k\}
let
A_{S}
(resp.
(A^{t})_{S}
) denote the submatrix
A
(resp.
A^{T}
) obtained by deleting all rows (resp. columns) with index not contained in
S
. Using the Cauchy–Binet formula and summing over all
S\subset\{1,\dots,k\}
with
|S|=3
, we get

\displaystyle\det(M)=\sum_{S}\det((A^{t})_{S})\det(A_{S})=\sum_{S}\det((A_{S})^{t})\det(A_{S})=\sum_{S}\det(A_{S})^{2}.

Finally, note that
\det(A_{S})
is homogeneous of degree three, since
|S|=3
.
∎

Corollary 2.3
.

Assume that
d=3
, that
F
is not a sum of squares and that all singular points of
X
are in
X({\mathbb{R}})
. Then the dimension of the kernel of
M(a)
is at most one for all
0\neq a\in{\mathbb{C}}^{3}
.

Proof.

This is clear from combining
2.1
and
2.2
.
∎

In the following, we will associate with
M
a coherent sheaf on
X
.
The matrix
M
defines an injective morphism of
{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}}
-modules:

M\colon{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}}(-d-1)^{d}\to{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}}(-d+1)^{d}.

We let
{\mathcal{F}}
be the cokernel of this morphism (of coherent sheaves) so that we obtain a short exact sequence of
{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}}
-modules:

(1)

0\to{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}}(-d-1)^{d}\to{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}}(-d+1)^{d}\to{\mathcal{F}}\to 0.

By
[
Bea00
, Theorem A]
the sheaf
{\mathcal{F}}
is
arithmetically
Cohen-Macaulay
and its support is
X
. The latter implies that it is the pushforward
\iota_{*}{\mathcal{L}}
of the coherent sheaf
{\mathcal{L}}=\iota^{*}{\mathcal{F}}
on
X
where
\iota\colon X\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}
is the embedding. The situation is particularly nice when
{\mathcal{L}}
is a line bundle.

Lemma 2.4
.

If
F
is square-free and the dimension of the kernel of
M(a)
is at most one for all
0\neq a\in{\mathbb{C}}^{3}
, then
{\mathcal{L}}
is a line bundle. If additionally
X
is integral, then
{\mathcal{L}}\otimes{\mathcal{L}}\cong{\mathcal{O}}_{X}
.

Proof.

We note that
M
induces a morphism of fibres at closed points
a
via
M(a)
. For the first part, observe that if
a\in X
then
\det(M(a))=0
. Hence the dimension of the kernel of
M(a)
is at least one. Together with our assumption, the dimension must be exactly one and therefore the dimension of the cokernel is one, too. Following
[
Har77
, Exercise II.5.8]
, semi-continuity bounds the dimension of the fibres at all (not necessarily closed) points by
1
. Since the determinant vanishes at all points of
X
, the dimension of the fibres is constant on
X
, and as
F
is assumed to be square-free,
X
is reduced and hence
{\mathcal{F}}
is locally free (again by
[
Har77
, Exercise II.5.8]
).
The second part then follows from
[
Bea00
, Theorem B]
and the fact that
M
is symmetric.
∎

Assume that we are in the situation of
2.4
. Given
F
we can then construct
M
as follows: We go through all (isomorphy classes of) line bundles
{\mathcal{L}}
on
X
such that
{\mathcal{L}}\otimes{\mathcal{L}}\cong{\mathcal{O}}_{X}
and compute their free resolution. Then we can check whether the free resolution has the correct format and, if yes, whether the occurring matrix is positive semidefinite. For this we need to understand the
2
-torsion points in the group
\operatorname{Pic}(X)
. If
X
is smooth, then
\operatorname{Pic}(X)\cong\mathbb{T}^{g}\oplus{\mathbb{Z}}
where
\mathbb{T}
is the circle group and
g=(d-1)(2d-1)
is the genus of
X
by
[
GH81
, §2 and §4]
. The zero set of the Robinson polynomial however is not smooth and we will now explain how to understand its Picard group.

2.2.
The Picard Group of the Robinson Polynomial

Recall that the Robinson polynomial is defined as

R=x_{0}^{6}-x_{0}^{4}x_{1}^{2}-x_{0}^{2}x_{1}^{4}+x_{1}^{6}-x_{0}^{4}x_{2}^{2}+3x_{0}^{2}x_{1}^{2}x_{2}^{2}-x_{1}^{4}x_{2}^{2}-x_{0}^{2}x_{2}^{4}-x_{1}^{2}x_{2}^{4}+x_{2}^{6}.

Its zero set
X={\mathcal{V}}(R)\subset\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
has exactly ten singular points
(x_{0}:x_{1}:x_{2})
, namely those points with homogeneous coordinates in
\{0,1,-1\}
such that at most one of them is
0
. In particular, all of them are real. We denote this set of points by
Y
. The normalization of
X
is a real curve of genus
0
with empty real part and thus isomorphic to
Q={\mathcal{V}}(x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2})\subset\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. The normalization map is given by

\pi\colon Q\to X,\,(x:y:z)\mapsto((2x^{2}+z^{2})z:x(x^{2}+2z^{2}):(x-z)(x+z)y).

Using that
R
is not a sum of squares, by
2.3
and
2.4
any quadratic representation of
R
must correspond to a
2
-torsion point of
\operatorname{Pic}(X)
. To determine
\operatorname{Pic}(X)
we consider the injective morphism of sheaves
{\mathcal{O}}_{X}\to\pi_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{Q}
induced by the normalization map
\pi
. We restrict this morphism to multiplicative units, and since
\pi
is an isomorphism outside the singular locus
Y
of
X
we get the following short exact sequence of sheaves on
X

(2)

0\to{\mathcal{O}}_{X}^{\times}\to(\pi_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{Q})^{\times}\to\mathbb{T}_{Y}\to 0

where
\mathbb{T}_{Y}
is the constant sheaf associated with
\mathbb{T}
supported on
Y
.

Theorem 2.5
.

There is a short exact sequence of abelian groups

0\to\mathbb{T}^{10}\to\operatorname{Pic}(X)\to{\mathbb{Z}}\to 0.

In particular the set of 2-torsion points of
\operatorname{Pic}(X)
is isomorphic to
({\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}})^{10}
.

Proof.

Applying the long exact sequence in cohomology to
Equation

2
and using that
\mathbb{T}_{Y}
is supported on a scheme of dimension zero yields

0\to{\mathbb{R}}^{\times}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{\times}\to\mathbb{T}^{10}\to H^{1}(X,{\mathcal{O}}_{X}^{\times})\to H^{1}(X,\pi_{*}({\mathcal{O}}_{Q})^{\times})\to 0.

Now
\operatorname{Pic}(X)=H^{1}(X,{\mathcal{O}}_{X}^{\times})
, and by
[
Gro67
, Corollaire 21.8.2]
we have
\operatorname{Pic}(Q)=H^{1}(Q,{\mathcal{O}}_{Q}^{\times})=H^{1}(X,\pi_{*}({\mathcal{O}}_{Q})^{\times})
. Moreover, by
[
Sta24
, Tag 0CDY]
) we have an injection
\operatorname{Pic}(Q)\hookrightarrow\operatorname{Pic}(Q_{\mathbb{C}})\cong{\mathbb{Z}}
, hence
\operatorname{Pic}(Q)\cong{\mathbb{Z}}
. Finally, the map
{\mathbb{R}}^{\times}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{\times}
is surjective, so that we get the exact sequence

0\to\mathbb{T}^{10}\to\operatorname{Pic}(X)\to{\mathbb{Z}}\to 0.

Every 2-torsion point in
\operatorname{Pic}(X)
necessarily lies in the kernel of
\operatorname{Pic}(X)\to{\mathbb{Z}}
, thus must arise as the image of a 2-torsion point in
\mathbb{T}^{10}
. But the 2-torsion subgroup of
\mathbb{T}^{10}
is (isomorphic to)
({\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}})^{10}
.
∎

It is possible to explicitly compute the image of the morphism
\mathbb{T}^{10}\to\operatorname{Pic}(X)
, i.e., for each
a\in\mathbb{T}^{10}
we obtain a concrete line bundle
{\mathcal{L}}_{a}
satisfying (in additive notation)
{\mathcal{L}}_{a}\otimes{\mathcal{L}}_{b}={\mathcal{L}}_{a+b}
as well as
{\mathcal{L}}_{0}={\mathcal{O}}_{X}
. Before describing
{\mathcal{L}}_{a}
explicitly in
2.9
we fix some notation.

(1)

By
K(Q)
we denote both the function field of
Q
as well as the constant sheaf on
Q
associated with it. We identify the set of rational functions in
K(Q)
all of whose poles lie in the complement of
U
with
{\mathcal{O}}_{Q}(U)
so that
{\mathcal{O}}_{Q}
becomes a subsheaf of
K(Q)
.

(2)

We write
\pi^{-1}(Y)=\{q_{1},\dots,q_{10}\}
. It is a set of ten closed (but not
{\mathbb{R}}
-valued) points
q_{i}\in Q\subset\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}
.

Now recall from
Equation

1
(with
d=3
) the short exact sequence

0\to{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}}(-4)^{3}\to{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}}(-2)^{3}\to{\mathcal{L}}\to 0

where
{\mathcal{L}}
is a 2-torsion point of
\operatorname{Pic}(X)
.

Lemma 2.6
.

The vector space
H^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{R}},{\mathcal{L}}(2))
has dimension 3 over
{\mathbb{R}}
.

Proof.

Tensoring the exact sequence

0\to{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}}(-4)^{3}\to{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}}(-2)^{3}\to{\mathcal{L}}\to 0

with
{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}}(2)
yields the short exact sequence

0\to{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}}(-2)^{3}\to{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}}^{3}\to{\mathcal{L}}(2)\to 0.

The long exact sequence in cohomology then yields

0\to H^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{R}},{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}}(-2)^{3})\to H^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{R}},{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}}^{3})\to H^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{R}},{\mathcal{L}}(2))\to H^{1}(\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{R}},{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}}(-2)^{3}).

Using that both the first and last vector space in this sequence are trivial we get an isomorphism

{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\cong H^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{R}},{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}}^{3})\cong H^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{R}},{\mathcal{L}}(2)).\qed

Our goal will be to construct an explicit basis of
H^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{R}},{\mathcal{L}}_{a}(2))
for
a\in\mathbb{T}^{10}
. For that we will interpret all occurring sheaves as subsheaves of
\pi_{*}K(Q)
via the normalization map
\pi
.

Lemma 2.7
.

The sheaf
{\mathcal{O}}_{X}
is a subsheaf of
\pi_{*}K(Q)
and can locally be described by the following data:

{\mathcal{O}}_{X}(U)=\left\{f\in{\mathcal{O}}_{Q}(\pi^{-1}(U))\mid f(q_{i})\in{\mathbb{R}}\text{ for all }i\text{ with }q_{i}\in\pi^{-1}(U)\right\}.

Proof.

The normalization map
\pi
yields an injective morphism of sheaves

0\to{\mathcal{O}}_{X}\to\pi_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{Q}.

Now
{\mathcal{O}}_{Q}
is a subsheaf of
K(Q)
which proves that
{\mathcal{O}}_{X}
is a subsheaf of
\pi_{*}K(Q)
.

For the explicit description we apply the results of
Appendix

A
. Fix a real zero
p
of
R
(and thus one of its singularities) and after translation in the real plane assume
p=(0,0,1)
. Let
\hat{F}(x,y)
denote the resulting polynomial after dehomogenization with respect to
x_{2}
. One easily verifies by an explicit computation that in our setting the degree two part of
\hat{F}
is irreducible, so our hypotheses satisfy the conditions of the discussion in
Appendix

A
. Applying
A.5
to the blowup of
\hat{F}
at
(x,y)
yields that the stalk of
{\mathcal{V}}(\hat{F})
in
(x,y)
is the subset of the stalk of the blow-up of
{\mathcal{V}}(\hat{F})
at
(x,y)
comprising all those germs whose value in
(x,y)
lies in
{\mathbb{R}}
.
∎

Corollary 2.8
.

Let
H
be the divisor of zeros of a linear form
h
on
X
such that
\operatorname{supp}(H)\cap Y=\emptyset
. Define

h_{\pi}(x,y,z)=h((2x^{2}+z^{2})z,x(x^{2}+2z^{2}),(x-z)(x+z)y)

and denote its divisor of zeros on
Q
by
H_{\pi}
. The sheaf
{\mathcal{O}}_{X}(t)
is isomorphic to the subsheaf of
\pi_{*}K(Q)
that can locally be described by the following data:

{\mathcal{O}}_{X}(t)(U)=\left\{f\in{\mathcal{O}}_{Q}(\pi^{-1}(U)\backslash H_{\pi})\mid\begin{array}[]{c}f(q_{i})\in{\mathbb{R}}\text{ for }i\text{ with }q_{i}\in\pi^{-1}(U)\\
\text{ and }\text{poles in }H_{\pi}\cap\pi^{-1}(U)\\
\text{ at most of order }t\end{array}\right\}.

Proof.

We tensor the sequence

0\to{\mathcal{O}}_{X}\to\pi_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{Q}

by
{\mathcal{O}}_{X}(t)
and obtain the injection

0\to{\mathcal{O}}_{X}(t)\to\pi_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{Q}\otimes{\mathcal{O}}_{X}(t).

By the projection formula (
[
Har77
, Exercise II.5.1.d]
) we get an isomorphism

\pi_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{Q}\otimes{\mathcal{O}}_{X}(t)\cong\pi_{*}({\mathcal{O}}_{Q}\otimes\pi^{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{X}(t)).

The right hand side is a subsheaf of
\pi_{*}K(Q)
by right-exactness of the pushforward functor and because all invertible sheaves on
Q
are subsheaves of
K(Q)
. The explicit description of
{\mathcal{O}}_{X}(t)
now follows from the identification of
{\mathcal{O}}_{X}
in
K(Q)
given in
2.7
.
∎

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
a\in\mathbb{T}^{10}
. Then we have the following:

(1)

The assignment

U\mapsto{\mathcal{L}}_{a}(U):=\left\{f\in{\mathcal{O}}_{Q}(\pi^{-1}(U))\mid f(q_{i})\in{\mathbb{R}}a_{i}\text{ for all }i\text{ with }q_{i}\in\pi^{-1}(U)\right\}

defines a line bundle on
X
.

(2)

The map

\mathbb{T}^{10}\to\operatorname{Pic}(C),a\mapsto{\mathcal{L}}_{a}

is an injective homomorphism of groups.

Proof.

We begin by showing that
{\mathcal{L}}_{a}
as defined is a line bundle. To do so note that by stalk-exactness of
Equation

2
for each
a\in\mathbb{T}^{10}
and for each
i
there exists an open set
U_{i}\subseteq X
such that
\pi^{-1}(U_{i})\cap Y=\{q_{i}\}
, and there exists a rational function
f_{i}\in{\mathcal{O}}_{Q}^{\times}(\pi^{-1}(U_{i}))
satisfying
f(q_{i})=a_{i}
. Then
{\mathcal{L}}_{a}(U_{i})=f_{i}{\mathcal{O}}_{X}(U_{i})
is trivial. Finally, set
U_{0}=X\backslash Y
. Then
{\mathcal{U}}=(U_{i})_{i=0}^{10}
is an open cover of
X
, which trivializes
{\mathcal{L}}_{a}
.

It follows from the definition that

\mathbb{T}^{10}\to\operatorname{Pic}(X),a\mapsto{\mathcal{L}}_{a}

is a group homomorphism and we want to prove that it is injective, i.e., no two line bundles of the form
{\mathcal{L}}_{a},{\mathcal{L}}_{b}
are isomorphic for
a\neq b
. To see this assume that
{\mathcal{L}}_{a}
has local basis
(f_{i})_{i}
(as constructed above) and
{\mathcal{L}}_{b}
has local basis
(g_{i})_{i}
. If
{\mathcal{L}}_{a}\cong{\mathcal{L}}_{b}
, then the functions
f_{i}
and
g_{i}
have to agree on their common domain (containing
q_{i}
) - up to multiplication with elements in
K(X)^{\times}
. By
2.7
this contradicts the choices of
f_{i}
and
g_{i}
.
∎

Corollary 2.10
.

Every 2-torsion point of
\operatorname{Pic}(X)
is of the form
{\mathcal{L}}_{a}
for some
a\in\mathbb{T}^{10}
.

Proof.

We already know by
2.5
that the number of 2-torsion points in
\operatorname{Pic}(X)
is
2^{10}
. Furthermore,
{\mathcal{L}}_{a}
is a 2-torsion point for each
a\in T^{10}\cap\{1,i\}^{10}
. By
2.9
the assignment
a\mapsto{\mathcal{L}}_{a}
is injective, which proves the claim.
∎

Remark 2.11
.

One can in fact show that the homomorphism
\mathbb{T}^{10}\to\operatorname{Pic}(X)
constructed in
2.9
coincides with that induced by taking cohomology in
2.5
.

Representing Types

The approach to finding determinantal representations as laid out above follows the ideas in
[
Bea00
, Theorem B]
. However, not every 2-torsion point gives rise to a symmetric quadratic representation. For necessary and sufficient conditions on the 2-torsion point
{\mathcal{L}}
for the resulting determinantal representation to be quadratic, see
[
Dol12
, Theorem 6.2.1]
or
[
Bea00
, Proposition 3.5]
. These conditions not being satisfied do not present an obstacle to finding a determinantal representation of
R
, though. It just might (and does) happen that the distribution of degrees across the representing matrix is different.

Example 2.12
.

The following two matrices are determinantal representations of the Robinson polynomial:

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}4x_{0}^{2}-x_{1}^{2}+4x_{2}^{2}&-6x_{0}^{2}-2x_{0}x_{1}+4x_{1}^{2}-6x_{2}^{2}&4x_{0}x_{1}+x_{1}^{2}\\
&4x_{0}^{2}+8x_{0}x_{1}-8x_{1}^{2}+12x_{2}^{2}&-2x_{0}^{2}-4x_{0}x_{1}-2x_{1}^{2}\\
&&x_{0}^{2}+2x_{0}x_{1}+4x_{1}^{2}-3x_{2}^{2}\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}x_{0}^{4}+6x_{0}^{2}x_{1}^{2}+5x_{1}^{4}-2x_{0}^{2}x_{2}^{2}-7x_{1}^{2}x_{2}^{2}+x_{2}^{4}&-x_{0}^{3}-x_{0}x_{1}^{2}+x_{0}x_{2}^{2}&x_{0}^{2}+x_{1}^{2}-x_{2}^{2}\\
&-x_{1}^{2}-x_{2}^{2}&-x_{0}\\
&&0\end{pmatrix}.

The first representation corresponds to the tuple

The matrices of interest for quadratic represenations are those of type

To decide if these give a positive quadratic representation, it suffices to evaluate them in an arbitrary real point, which the Robinson polynomial does not vanish in, and check if the resulting real-valued matrix is positive definite. Due to the continuity of eigenvalues, the locus in which the representation is positive definite is open. Similarly, the set of all points of evaluation in which the representation is invertible and has at least one negative eigenvalue is open. These two sets cover all of

Remark 2.13
.

The methods that we have employed to derive our result about the Robinson polynomial are applicable more generally. For example, whenever we are given a nonnegative sextic curve with ten real zeros of multiplicity one, then its normalization is isomorphic to

3.
Smooth Positive Ternary Quartics

3.1.
The Intrinsic Geometry of a Curve

We first recall some of the intrinsic geometry underlying the set of all quadratic representations of a given smooth projective
complex
curve
C={\mathcal{V}}(F)
of degree
\deg(F)=2d
and genus
g=(d-1)(2d-1)
. We denote by
J:=J(C)
its Jacobian variety (i.e. the group scheme of degree zero divisor classes on
C
). The foundation, from which our theory is developed lies in the following classical statement, which follows from
[
Bea00
, Theorem B]
. It is also established in
[
Dol12
, §6.2.2]
.

Theorem 3.1
.

There is a bijection between invertible sheaves
{\mathcal{L}}
of degree zero on
C
satisfying

h^{0}(C,{\mathcal{L}}(d-2))=0=h^{1}(C,{\mathcal{L}}(d-1))

and equivalence classes of quadratic representations of
F
over
{\mathbb{C}}
.

Remark 3.2
.

If we restrict considerations to symmetric determinantal representations, then there is a bijection to the 2-torsion points
{\mathcal{L}}
of the group
J({\mathbb{C}})
of the Jacobian
J
with the sole condition

h^{0}(C,{\mathcal{L}}(d-2))=0.

This uses
\omega_{C}\cong{\mathcal{O}}_{C}(2d-3)
and Serre duality.

The number of nontrivial 2-torsion points of
J({\mathbb{C}})
is
2^{2g}-1
, and by
[
Bea00
, Proposition 4.6]
for a general curve every nontrivial 2-torsion point
{\mathcal{L}}
of
J({\mathbb{C}})
will satisfy these conditions. We will next describe how 2-torsion points can be understood through theta characteristics on
C
.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
C
be a smooth plane projective curve of degree
2d
over
{\mathbb{C}}
. A line bundle
\theta
on
C
is called a
theta characteristic
, if
\theta^{\otimes 2}\cong\omega_{C}\cong{\mathcal{O}}_{C}(2d-3)
. We call a theta characteristic
\theta
even (resp. odd), if
h^{0}(C,\theta)
is even (resp. odd). We call a theta characteristic
\theta

vanishing
, if
h^{0}(C,\theta)>1
.

Definition 3.4
.

Let
C
and
C^{\prime}
be projective plane curves over
{\mathbb{C}}
. We say that
C^{\prime}
is a
contact curve
to
C
, if the intersection divisor

C.C^{\prime}:=\sum_{P\in C}I(P;C,C^{\prime})\in\operatorname{Div}(C)

is divisible by 2, i.e., there exists
D\in\operatorname{Div}(C)
satisfying
C.C^{\prime}=2D
. We call
D
the
contact divisor
of
C
and
C^{\prime}
. If
C={\mathcal{V}}(F),C^{\prime}={\mathcal{V}}(G)
are reduced curves, then we will also write
F.G=C.C^{\prime}
.

Geometrically, odd theta characteristics characterize contact curves of degree
2d-3
to
C
(unique ones, if the theta characteristic is non-vanishing). More precisely, let
D
be an effective divisor on
C
representing an odd theta characteristic
\theta_{-}
. Then
D
is a sum of points on
C
such that
2D\sim(2d-3)H
, the divisor of zeros on
C
of a curve of degree
2d-3
.

Non-vanishing even theta characteristics
\theta_{+}
, on the other hand, give rise to contact curves of degree
2d-1
(via effective representatives of the divisor class
[T]
satisfying
\theta_{+}(1)\cong{\mathcal{O}}(T)
). These theta characteristics are in bijection with linear determinantal representations of
C
(see, for example,
[
Vin89
, Theorem 2, Theorem 4, Theorem 5]
).

One important result linking odd theta characteristics and 2-torsion points of
J({\mathbb{C}})
is the following.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}
be two odd theta characteristics. Then
{\mathcal{L}}=\theta_{1}\otimes\theta_{2}^{\vee}
is a 2-torsion point of
J(C)
. Conversely, every 2-torsion point
{\mathcal{L}}
of
J(C)
can be expressed as
{\mathcal{L}}=\theta_{1}\otimes\theta_{2}^{\vee}
with
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}
being odd theta characteristics.

Proof.

The first statement is clear. For the converse, see the proof of
[
Dol12
, Proposition 5.4.5]
.
∎

Hence, one way to understand the quadratic representations of
F
is via contact curves of degree
2d-3
to
C={\mathcal{V}}(F)
.

A different approach links quadratic representations of
F
to contact curves of degree
2d-2
. Following
[
Dol12
, §6.2]
, given a 2-torsion point satisfying the conditions of
3.1
, a system of contact curves of degree
2d-2
is given by effective representatives of the unique divisor class
[A]
satisfying
{\mathcal{L}}(d-1)\cong{\mathcal{O}}(A)
. Precisely, one has the following lemma (
[
Dol12
, p. 236–237]
, but see also
[
PSV11
, Proposition 5.7]
for the special case of
d=2
and
[
PV13
, Proposition 3.2]
for a similar result).

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
Q
be a quadratic representation of
F
. Then the set

{\mathcal{C}}_{Q}:=\left\{\lambda^{t}Q^{\operatorname{adj}}\lambda\mid\lambda\in\mathbb{P}^{d-1}_{\mathbb{C}}\right\}

is a system of contact curves of degree
2d-2
to
C
. If
a,b\in{\mathcal{C}}_{Q}
, then their divisors of zeros on
C={\mathcal{V}}(F)
are linearly equivalent.

Conversely, if we know at least one such contact curve
a\in{\mathcal{C}}_{Q}
, then there is an algorithm that recovers the quadratic representation
Q
of
F
. This is an adaptation of Dixon’s algorithm (see
[
Dix02
]
for the first description and
[
Vin89
]
for a more modern account), which was originally designed for the construction of linear determinantal representations. An approach very similar to ours has been given in
[
PV13
, §4]
for linear determinantal representations of hyperbolic plane curves. One of its key components is the following theorem.

Theorem 3.7

(Max Noether’s Fundamental Theorem)
.

Let
F,G,H
be homogeneous polynomials such that
{\mathcal{V}}(F)
and
{\mathcal{V}}(G)
have no common component. Furthermore assume that
F.H\geq F.G
as divisors on
{\mathcal{V}}(F)
. Then there exist homogeneous polynomials
p,q
satisfying

H=pF+qG.

If
F,G,H
are all real curves, then
p,q
may be chosen real as well.

Proof.

See
[
Ful08
, §5.5]
and
[
PV13
, Theorem 4.1]
.
∎

Algorithm 3.8

(Dixon’s Algorithm for Quadratic Representations of Ternary Quartics)
.

Let
C={\mathcal{V}}(F)
be a smooth projective plane curve of degree
4
over
{\mathbb{C}}
and let
C^{\prime}=V(a_{00})
be a reduced contact curve to
C
of degree two with contact divisor
A
. Then the following algorithm returns a quadratic representation
Q
of
F
, such that
a_{00}\in{\mathcal{C}}_{Q}
.

(1)

Choose a conic
a_{10}\in{\mathcal{L}}(2H-A)
such that the pair
a_{00},a_{10}
is linearly independent.

(2)

From Max Noether’s fundamental theorem choose a conic
a_{11}
and
p\in{\mathbb{C}}
satisfying

a_{00}a_{11}-a_{10}^{2}=pF.

(3)

Define
Q
as the symmetric matrix

Q=\begin{pmatrix}a_{00}&a_{10}\\
a_{10}&a_{11}\end{pmatrix}.

Proof.

First observe that by Riemann–Roch we have

\ell(2H-A)\geq\deg(2H-A)+1-g_{C}=4+1-3=2.

In particular we may choose
a_{10}
as desired. Next we know that

a_{10}^{2}.F\geq a_{00}.F

by construction, so the hypotheses of Max Noether’s fundamental theorem (
3.7
) are satisfied. Furthermore, note that for degree reasons we have
p\in{\mathbb{C}}
, so unless
p=0
we have found a quadratic representation.

We argue that
p=0
if and only if
a=a_{00}
is non-reduced. Since
d=2
this is the case if and only if
a=l^{2}
is the square of a linear form. Indeed, if
p=0
, then
a_{00}a_{11}=a_{10}^{2}
. By unique factorization, and since
a_{00},a_{10}
are linearly independent,
a_{10}^{2}=l_{1}^{2}l_{2}^{2}
for certain linear factors
l_{1},l_{2}
. Again by linear independence without loss of generality we get
l_{1}^{2}=a_{00}
. Conversely, assume
a=l_{1}^{2}
. Then
a_{10}
vanishes at 4 colinear points, hence is a reducible conic
a_{10}=l_{1}l_{2}
itself. This implies
a_{11}=l_{2}^{2}
, so
p=0
.
∎

Remark 3.9
.

The proof of
3.8
is elementary in the case of quartic curves. A similar algorithm can be used to construct quadratic representations of arbitrary smooth curves of even degree. The proof relies on the fact that if
a_{00}
is a reduced contact curve to
C
with contact divisor
A
, then
A-(d-1)H
defines a 2-torsion point, satisfying the cohomological hypotheses of
3.1
. Hence, a determinantal representation is implicitly already contained in the divisor
A
.

Remark 3.10
.

While
3.8
is phrased for a complex curve
C
, it can be adapted to the real setting: For a real quadratic representation of a real smooth projective curve
C={\mathcal{V}}(F)
with (real) Jacobian
J
to exist it is necessary that the corresponding 2-torsion point of
J({\mathbb{C}})
has a representing divisor that is invariant under complex conjugation. Since Max Noether’s fundamental theorem holds over
{\mathbb{R}}
, Dixon’s algorithm may still be carried out.

While a 2-torsion point as in
3.10
could arguably be called a “real” 2-torsion point, there is a subtlety not to be overlooked. Namely, there are two notions of reality for 2-torsion points (and more generally divisor classes).

Definition 3.11
.

Let
C
be a smooth projective curve over
{\mathbb{R}}
, and let
D
be a divisor on
C_{\mathbb{C}}
.

(1)

If
[D]=[\overline{D}]
, then
[D]
is called a
real
divisor class.

(2)

If there exists
D_{0}\sim D
such that
\overline{D_{0}}=D_{0}
, then
[D]
is called a
strongly real
divisor class.

Note that if
D\sim D^{\prime}
and
[D]=[\overline{D}]
, then
[D-D^{\prime}]=[0]=[\overline{D-D^{\prime}}]
, so
[D^{\prime}]=[\overline{D^{\prime}]}
. Hence reality is independent of the chosen representative of
[D]
.

Real divisor classes correspond to real closed points on the Jacobian of
C
(considered as a scheme over
{\mathbb{R}}
), and alternatively they can be identified with line bundles in
\operatorname{Pic}(C_{\mathbb{C}})
that are isomorphic to their complex conjugate. Strongly real divisor classes correspond to line bundles in
\operatorname{Pic}(C)
.

If we only consider 2-torsion points, then those used in
3.10
are the strongly real ones. Obviously, strongly real implies real, and for 2-torsion points the two notions coincide if and only if the genus of
C
is even (see
[
GH81
, Proposition 3.3]
).

3.2.
Quartic Curves: Proof of Existence

We now restrict our considerations to
C=V(F)
being a smooth ternary quartic curve defined over
{\mathbb{R}}
with

. We let
J
be its Jacobian variety (considered as an
{\mathbb{R}}
-scheme). There are exactly 63 theta characteristics on

, 35 are even and 28 are odd. They are all non-vanishing, and in particular there are exactly 28 bitangent lines (exactly four of which are real). We first prove that the conditions in
3.1
are automatically satisfied.

Lemma 3.12

(
[
Dol12
, Theorem 6.2.3]
)
.

Every non-trivial 2-torsion point of
J({\mathbb{C}})
defines a (complex) symmetric quadratic representation of
F
.

Proof.

Let
D
be a 2-torsion point of
J({\mathbb{C}})
. Then it may be realized as the difference of two odd theta characteristics, which we identify as a difference of divisors
B_{1}-B_{2}
by
3.5
. Following
3.1
it now suffices to prove

\ell(D)=0.

Indeed, if
\ell(D)>0
, then
D
is a special divisor, so by Clifford’s theorem (
[
Har77
, Theorem IV.5.2]
) we get
2(\ell(D)-1)\leq\deg(D)=0
. Thus,
\ell(D)=1
and
D
must be the zero divisor, a contradiction to
D
being non-trivial.
∎

A fact that we will use repeatedly is the following.

Proposition 3.13
.

There exist real symmetric linear determinantal representations of
F
, and they are in bijection with non-vanishing strongly real even theta characteristics on
C
.

Proof.

See
[
Vin93
, p. 456]
.
∎

For a very accessible classification of all 2-torsion points we recall the definition of a Steiner complex.

.

Let
[D]

of 2-sets of odd theta characteristics

By abuse of terminology we will sometimes refer to elements of

C

[
Dol12
, §5.4.2]

Let

Proposition 3.17
.

Let
\sigma
denote the permutation
(12)(34)(56)(78)\in S_{8}
, let
D
be a divisor whose class
[D]
is a 2-torsion point, and let
\Sigma(D)
be the associated Steiner complex. Then
[D]
is real (in the sense of
3.11
) if and only if one of the following holds:

(1)

\Sigma(D)=S^{(1)}_{I}
and
\sigma(I)=I
.

(2)

\Sigma(D)=S^{(2)}_{I}
and
\sigma(I)\in\{I,I^{c}\}
.

It is strongly real if and only if in either case
\sigma(I)=I
.

Proof.

First let
\Sigma(D)
be of type 1, so
D=B_{ik}-B_{jk}
for
I=\{i,j\}
. Then
D
is real if and only if
D\sim\overline{D}
, or equivalently
B_{ik}-B_{jk}\sim B_{\sigma(i)\sigma(k)}-B_{\sigma(j)\sigma(k)}
. This is the case if and only if
\sigma(I)=I
.

Next let
\Sigma(D)
be of type 2. Then the class of
D\sim B_{ij}-B_{kl}
is real if and only if
B_{ij}-B_{kl}\sim B_{\sigma(i)\sigma(j)}-B_{\sigma(k)\sigma(l)}
. This is the case if and only if
\sigma(I)\in\{I,I^{c}\}
.

For the second part of the statement we use that it follows from

associated with

contains real contact conics, which is the case if and only if

is such that
\sigma(I)=I
.

In this setting a 2-torsion point
[D]
is real if and only if the visual representation of the associated Steiner complex

is invariant under the simultaneous action of

.

The three Steiner complexes of type 2 which are associated with

,

4.
Concluding Remarks

It is natural to ask whether our results for smooth quartic curves generalize to curves of higher degree. It is a quick observation that the set of nonnegative polynomials of degree
2d
admitting a positive quadratic representation contains an open set in the Euclidean topology. To see this, one starts with the positive quadratic representation

F=(x_{0}^{2}+x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2})^{d}=\det((x_{0}^{2}+x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2})I_{d})

and slightly perturbs this representation.
It is less apparent how large this set of polynomials is and we conclude with the following conjecture.

Conjecture 4.1
.

The set of nonnegative forms of degree
2d
admitting a positive quadratic representation is dense (with respect to the Euclidean topology) in the set of all nonnegative forms of degree
2d
.

.
∎

Let

be the ideal of

that is generated by

is a local ring with maximal ideal

.

Lemma A.3
.

A^{\prime}
is a regular local ring.

Proof.

The Jacobian matrix of the two equations

and

is

\begin{pmatrix}-z&1&-x\\
\frac{\partial F^{\prime}}{\partial x}&0&\frac{\partial F^{\prime}}{\partial z}\end{pmatrix}.

It has rank

since

is irreducible and thus separable.
∎

Corollary A.4
.

A^{\prime}
is the normalization of
A
.

Proof.

By
A.2
the ring
A^{\prime}
is contained in the normalization of
A
and by
A.3

A^{\prime}
is regular and therefore integrally closed. This implies the claim.
∎

Proposition A.5
.

We have

A=\{f\in A^{\prime}\mid f\equiv c\mod{\mathfrak{n}}\textrm{ for some }c\in{\mathbb{R}}\}.

Proof.

Since
{\mathfrak{m}}={\mathfrak{n}}\cap A
and
A/{\mathfrak{m}}={\mathbb{R}}
, we have the inclusion “
\subseteq
”.
In order to prove the other inclusion, let

an element of the right-hand side. By
A.1
there are

such that

. Reduced modulo

this equals to

Since

Now assume that we have determined our set of “final generators”. The calculation of

the determinantal representation must be the kernel of a surjection

0\to\bigoplus_{i=1}^{N}{\mathcal{O}}_{X}(-t_{i}^{\prime}+t+\text{ twist})\overset{M}{\to}\bigoplus_{i=1}^{N}{\mathcal{O}}_{X}(-t_{i}+t+\text{ twist})\overset{\text{FinalGens}}{\to}{\mathcal{L}}_{a}(t+\text{ twist})\to 0.

Since

is supported on

and

the determinant

is a multiple of

which can then be solved.

Remark B.4
.

The mentioned system might return more than one solution, which is due to the fact that it does not inherently detect whether the constructed matrix

Ultimately we may verify, which of the representations of type
M_{0}^{0}
are positive. Using a corollary of Hermite’s Theorem (
[
BPR06
, Theorem 4.13]
) we may do so symbolically.

Definition B.6
.

Let
p\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]
be a monic polynomial of degree
n
with roots
(\alpha_{i})_{i}
in
{\mathbb{C}}
and let
q\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]
be arbitraty. We call the number

\nu_{k}(p,q)=\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}^{k}q(\alpha_{i})\in{\mathbb{R}}

the
k
-th generalized Newton sum of
p
. The matrix

\mathscr{H}(p,q)=(\nu_{i+j}(p,q))_{0\leq i,j\leq n-1}

is called the generalized Hermite matrix of
p
and
q
.

Theorem B.7
.

Let
p\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]
be monic and let
q,q_{1},\dots,q_{m}\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]
. Furthermore for
e\in{1,2}^{m}
define

q^{e}=\prod_{i=1}^{m}q_{i}^{e_{i}}.

Then we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{sign}\mathscr{H}(p,q)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}},p(\alpha)=0}\operatorname{sign}q(\alpha)

\displaystyle|\{\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}\mid p(\alpha)=0,q_{1}(\alpha)>0,\dots,q_{m}(\alpha)>0\}|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{m}}\sum_{e}\operatorname{sign}\mathscr{H}(p,q^{e}).

Proof.

See
[
Sch24
, Theorem 1.3.33 and Proposition 1.3.36]
.
∎

In our setting the polynomials
q_{1},q_{2},q_{3}
will be the leading principal minors of a given quadratic representation, and if they are all positive, then the representation is positive. But positivity of the minors depends on the exact value of
\sqrt{2}+\sqrt{3}
, which we only know by its minimal polynomial, which will take the role of
p
.

To apply this for a given quadratic representation of the Robinson polynomial we first compute the evaluation in one of its points, and we map the resulting matrix into the ring
S2={\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{2}+\sqrt{3})
. Then we proceed as follows: We use that all real roots of the minimal polynomial of
\sqrt{2}+\sqrt{3}
lie between
-4
and
4
. To compute the signatures of the Hermite matrices we use the implementation
[
GMSY
]
of Sturm’s algorithm in
Macaulay2
as described in
[
GMSY24
, Theorem 3]
.

Finally, given a quadratic representation we may evaluate it in a point and the following function checks, if the resulting matrix is positive semidefinite.
