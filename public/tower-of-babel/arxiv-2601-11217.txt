Model-free policy gradient for discrete-time mean-field control
Matthieu Meunier, Huyên Pham, Christoph Reisinger

Abstract
We study model-free policy learning for discrete-time mean-field control (MFC) problems with finite state space and compact action space. In contrast to the extensive literature on value-based methods for MFC, policy-based approaches remain largely unexplored due to the intrinsic dependence of transition kernels and rewards on the evolving population state distribution, which prevents the direct use of likelihood-ratio estimators of policy gradients from classical single-agent reinforcement learning. We introduce a novel perturbation scheme on the state-distribution flow and prove that the gradient of the resulting perturbed value function converges to the true policy gradient as the perturbation magnitude vanishes. This construction yields a fully model-free estimator based solely on simulated trajectories and an auxiliary estimate of the sensitivity of the state distribution. Building on this framework, we develop MF-REINFORCE, a model-free policy gradient algorithm for MFC, and establish explicit quantitative bounds on its bias and mean-squared error. Numerical experiments on representative mean-field control tasks demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed approach.



Key words:
Mean-field control; Policy gradient methods; Model-free reinforcement learning; Markov decision process; Perturbation analysis.

AMS subject classifications:
93E20; 60J10; 68T05; 60K35; 49N80.

Introduction

Designing algorithms which optimally control large populations of cooperative agents has recently become an important problem due to the breadth and depth of applications. In the face of the complexity of modelling individual interactions between each pair of agents, mean-field control provides a rigorous model particularly suited for problems with many agents interacting in a symmetrical way: instead of considering all possible interactions between every pair of players, one lets the number of agents go to infinity and tries to solve a limiting problem for which it suffices to study the interaction between a typical agent and the probability distribution of its state.
An important and practical research direction for such decision-making problems consists in model-free reinforcement learning (RL) algorithms.
RL algorithms are based on two elementary approaches:
value-based
learning algorithms, such as
Q
-learning (
[
42
]
), where one tries to learn a map which allows one to compare the optimal expected reward for different actions and subsequently learn a policy from this map, and
policy-based
algorithms, also known as policy gradient methods, such as REINFORCE (
[
44
]
), which directly try to optimize the value function with respect to the parameters of a policy. These two approaches are of great importance and are combined in modern deep reinforcement learning algorithms.
The study of such approaches tailored to mean-field control problems has recently received more attention, with many works developing value-based algorithms.
However, there is a notable lack of study of model-free policy-based methods for this setting in the literature.

This paper fills this gap by introducing the first policy gradient algorithm for mean-field control problems in discrete time. As in the classical REINFORCE algorithm for single-agent problems, our algorithm is entirely model-free and can be performed by simply observing trajectories of the system.

1.1
Outline of Main Results

In this section, we provide an informal description of the problem at hand and present our main contributions.
We consider a mean-field control problem in discrete time without common noise and time horizon
T\in{\mathbb{N}}
. We assume that the state space
\mathcal{X}
is finite and that the action space
\mathcal{A}
is compact and Polish.
Given an admissible control
\alpha=(\alpha_{t})_{t=0}^{T-1}
, the
\mathcal{X}
-valued state follows the dynamics

X_{0}=\xi,\quad X_{t+1}\sim P(\cdot\mid X_{t},\alpha_{t},\mathbb{P}_{X_{t}}),

where
\mathbb{P}_{X_{t}}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
denotes the law of
X_{t}
. We denote by
P(\cdot\mid x,a,\mu)
the underlying probability kernel. Consider a running reward
r:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
and a terminal reward
g:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
, which are both assumed to be measurable and bounded.
The goal is to maximize the following objective, for a given initial condition
\xi

\sim

\mu_{0}
,

\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim\mu_{0}}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}r(t,X_{t},\alpha_{t},\mathbb{P}_{X_{t}})+g(X_{T},\mathbb{P}_{X_{T}})\right],

over a set of admissible controls. For now, we consider randomized controls of feedback form, meaning that the set of admissible controls is parametrized by

\Pi\coloneq\{\pi:\{0,\ldots,T-1\}\times\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{A})\text{ measurable}\}.

Hence a control
\alpha
is admissible if there exists
\pi\in\Pi
such that
\alpha_{t}\sim\pi(\cdot\mid t,X_{t},\mathbb{P}_{X_{t}})
for all
t\in\{0,\ldots,T-1\}
.
Therefore, we seek to maximize

V^{\pi}(\mu_{0})\coloneq\mathbb{E}^{\pi}_{\xi\sim\mu_{0}}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}r(X_{t},\alpha_{t},\mathbb{P}_{X_{t}})+g(X_{T},\mathbb{P}_{X_{T}})\right],

(1.1)

where
\mathbb{E}^{\pi}
refers to
\alpha=(\alpha_{t})
being generated by
\pi
.

Our goal is to develop a model-free policy gradient algorithm to solve this mean-field control problem. Consider a set of parametrized policies
\{\pi_{\theta}:\theta\in\Theta\}
with
\Theta\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{D}
given, e.g., by a neural network with trainable parameters.
Then we want to find an estimator for
\nabla_{\theta}V^{\pi_{\theta}}(\mu_{0})
that can easily be computed in a reinforcement learning setting, e.g., by generating trajectories of
X
with a simulator.
In continuous time, this problem has been studied by
[
11
]
, where the Itô formula along flows of measures is used to write a policy gradient of the value function that can be estimated in a model-free setting.
Our main contributions are as follows:

•

We rigorously derive a policy gradient formula for the value function in Proposition
2.1
. This result highlights the computational challenges stemming from the mean-field dependence of the transition kernel and the reward functions. In particular, contrary to the single-agent problem, it is not possible to directly obtain a model-free estimator of the gradient using a likelihood ratio trick.

•

We introduce a perturbation scheme, along with a perturbed value function, and show in Theorem
2.4
that the gradient of the perturbed value function converges to the true value function as the size of the perturbation goes to
0
. In particular, the gradient of the perturbation is amenable to model-free estimation, but requires an auxiliary estimation procedure of the gradient (of the logits) of the state distribution.

•

We present a model-free algorithm to estimate the gradient of the value function, which we call MF-REINFORCE, based on the formula for the gradient of the perturbed value function in Algorithm
1
. We provide a detailed analysis and quantitative bounds on the bias and mean squared error of the newly introduced estimator in Theorems
3.3
and
3.5
respectively, based on the size of the perturbation and number of trajectories sampled.

•

We present numerical experiments of MF-REINFORCE on a few problems in Section
4
.

Policy Gradient Approximation via Perturbation for Mean-Field Reinforcement Learning

In this section, we first present the logit representation of discrete measures as an important tool for dealing with the measure dependence in the subsequent computations.
Then, we state the main working assumption and present the exact policy gradient formula.
Finally, we show convergence of a perturbed, computable gradient to the true gradient.

Throughout this section and the rest of the paper, we consider a discrete-time mean-field control problem with state space
\mathcal{X}
, action space
\mathcal{A}
, running reward
r(x,a,\mu)
, finite horizon
T\in{\mathbb{N}}
, terminal reward
g(x,\mu)
and transition kernel
P({\mathrm{d}}x^{\prime}\mid x,a,\mu)
. For a given initial condition
\xi
such that
\mathcal{L}(\xi)=\mu
, the goal is to maximize the value function
V^{\pi}(\mu)
defined in (
1.1
) over
\pi\in\{\pi_{\theta}:\theta\in\Theta\}
where
\Theta\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is the set of admissible parameters.

2.1
Parametrization of State Distributions by Logits

Defining a perturbation scheme on
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
is a priori non-trivial since
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
is not a vector space.
Therefore, we choose to parametrize probability distributions by the vector of log-probabilities, also known as logits. Then, one can perturb the logits linearly and map the perturbed logits back to a probability distribution with a softmax operation.
Assume that
|\mathcal{X}|=d<\infty
. Then we can view
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
as

\{\mu\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}:\quad\mu_{i}\geq 0\>\>\forall i,\quad\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mu_{i}=1\}.

For
l\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, we can define the following probability vector

\softmax(l)\coloneq\left(\frac{e^{l_{1}}}{\sum_{i=1}^{d}e^{l_{i}}},\ldots,\frac{e^{l_{d}}}{\sum_{i=1}^{d}e^{l_{i}}}\right)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

The inverse transformation, for a probability vector
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
such that
\mu_{i}>0
for all
i
, is

\logit(\mu)=\left(\log\mu_{1},\ldots,\log\mu_{d}\right).

Hence we can parametrize any distribution on
\mathcal{X}
with non-zero mass everywhere by a vector of logits in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
Let
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})^{*}\coloneq\{\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}):\mu(x)>0,\>\forall x\in\mathcal{X}\}
.

Since we are interested in differentiating the value function, we need to make the notion of derivative in the measure argument precise. We make the following set of standing assumptions throughout the paper.

Assumption 1
.

The state space
\mathcal{X}=\{x^{(1)},\ldots,x^{(d)}\}
is finite and the action space
\mathcal{A}
is compact and Polish.
Moreover, there exist functions
\tilde{r}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
,
\tilde{g}:\mathcal{X}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
,
\tilde{P}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
,
p:\Theta\times\mathcal{A}\times\{0,\ldots,T-1\}\times\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})\to{\mathbb{R}}
,
\tilde{p}:\Theta\times\mathcal{A}\times\{0,\ldots,T-1\}\times\mathcal{X}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
such that

•

for any
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X},a\in\mathcal{A},l\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, we have

\displaystyle r(x,a,\softmax(l))=\tilde{r}(x,a,l),\quad g(x,\softmax(l))=\tilde{g}(x,l),

\displaystyle P(x^{\prime}\mid x,a,\softmax(l))=\tilde{P}(x^{\prime}\mid x,a,l).

•

(x,a,l)\mapsto\tilde{r}(x,a,l)
is bounded in
[-M_{0},M_{0}]
where
0\leq M_{0}<\infty
, continuous in
a\in\mathcal{A}
and continuously differentiable in
l\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
similarly
(x,l)\mapsto\tilde{g}(x,l)
is bounded in
[-M_{0},M_{0}]
and continuously differentiable in
l\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
;

•

the extended transition probability
(x^{\prime},x,a,l)\mapsto\tilde{P}(x^{\prime}\mid x,a,l)
is continuous in
a\in\mathcal{A}
and continuously differentiable in
l\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
;

•

the parametrized policy
\pi_{\theta}({\mathrm{d}}a\mid t,x,\mu)
admits a density
a

\mapsto

p(\theta,a,t,x,\mu)
with respect to a finite reference measure
\nu_{\mathcal{A}}
, and we denote
|\mathcal{A}|=\nu_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathcal{A})
. We have
p(\theta,a,t,x,\softmax l)=\tilde{p}(\theta,a,t,x,l)
, and
\tilde{p}
is differentiable in
\theta
, continuous in
a\in\mathcal{A}
and continuously differentiable in
l\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
Moreover,
\nabla_{\theta}\log p
is bounded, i.e., there exists
C>0
such that

\sup_{(\theta,a,x,\mu)\in\Theta\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})^{*}}\|\nabla_{\theta}\log p(\theta,a,t,x,\mu)\|_{\infty}\leq C,\qquad t\in\{0,\ldots,T-1\}.

•

the transition probability
P
and the policies
\{\pi_{\theta}:\theta\in\Theta\}
are such that, for any
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})^{*}
and any
\theta\in\Theta
, we have, for any
t

\in

\{0,\ldots,T-1\}
,
x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}

\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{\mathcal{A}}P(x^{\prime}\mid x^{(i)},a,\mu)\pi_{\theta}({\mathrm{d}}a\mid t,x^{(i)},\mu)\mu(x^{(i)})>0,

ensuring that if the initial condition
\mu_{0}
has a valid logits representation, then under any admissible policy
\pi_{\theta}
the flow of measures
(\mu_{t})_{t=0}^{T}
has a valid logits representation.

2.2
Policy Gradient Formula: from Single-Agent to Mean-Field

Let
V(\mu_{0},\theta)=V^{\pi_{\theta}}(\mu_{0})
. Under Assumption
1
, we have
\pi_{\theta}({\mathrm{d}}a\mid t,x,\mu)=p(\theta,a,t,x,\mu)\nu_{\mathcal{A}}({\mathrm{d}}a)
,
\mu_{t}^{\theta}({\mathrm{d}}x)\coloneq\mathbb{P}(X_{t}^{\theta}=x)\nu_{\mathcal{X}}({\mathrm{d}}x)
, and
l_{t}^{\theta}=\logit(\mu_{t}^{\theta})
, where
\nu_{\mathcal{X}}
is the counting measure on
\mathcal{X}
.
Consider the joint probability of the whole trajectory
\left((X_{t}^{\theta},\alpha_{t}^{\theta})_{t=0}^{T-1},X_{T}^{\theta})\right)
under
\pi_{\theta}
, given by

P_{\theta}({\mathrm{d}}x_{0},{\mathrm{d}}a_{0},\ldots,{\mathrm{d}}a_{T-1},{\mathrm{d}}x_{T})\coloneq\mu_{0}({\mathrm{d}}x_{0})\prod_{\tau=0}^{T-1}\pi_{\theta}({\mathrm{d}}a_{\tau}\mid\tau,x_{\tau},\mu_{\tau}^{\theta})P({\mathrm{d}}x_{\tau+1}\mid x_{\tau},a_{\tau},\mu_{\tau}^{\theta}).

We want to emphasize the direct dependence of the law of
X_{t}^{\theta}
, denoted by
\mu_{t}^{\theta}
, on the parameters
\theta
. In practice, this means that differentiating the value function with respect to
\theta
induces differentiating the reward functions
r,g
and transition kernel
P
with respect to the measure argument.
In the remainder of the paper, as hinted in Section
2.1
, the derivative with respect to the measure argument will be expressed in terms of the derivative with respect to the logits representation of the measure.
We denote by
l_{t}^{\theta}=\logit(\mu_{t}^{\theta})
the logits representation of the law of
X_{t}^{\theta}
, and we write
\nabla_{\theta}l_{t}^{\theta}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times D}
for the Jacobian matrix of
l_{t}^{\theta}
with respect to
\theta
.

In particular, on top of the standard REINFORCE-like term (
[
44
]
), we obtain two additional terms corresponding to the measure derivative of the rewards, and the measure derivative of the transition kernel and policy (due to the mean-field nature of the dynamics).

Proposition 2.1
.

Let Assumption
1
hold. For any
\mu_{0}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})^{*}
and
\theta\in\Theta
, we have

\nabla_{\theta}V(\mu_{0},\theta)=\mathrm{RF}(\theta)+\mathrm{MD}(\theta)+\mathrm{MFD}(\theta),

(2.1)

where

\displaystyle\mathrm{RF}(\theta)

\displaystyle\coloneq\int\left(\sum_{t^{\prime}=0}^{T-1}\nabla_{\theta}\log p(\theta,a_{t^{\prime}},t^{\prime},x_{t^{\prime}},\mu_{t^{\prime}}^{\theta})\right)\left(\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}r(x_{t},a_{t},\mu_{t}^{\theta})+g(x_{T},\mu_{T}^{\theta})\right){\mathrm{d}}P_{\theta},

(2.2)

\displaystyle\mathrm{MD}(\theta)

\displaystyle\coloneq\int\left(\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\nabla_{l}\tilde{r}(x_{t},a_{t},l_{t}^{\theta})\nabla_{\theta}l_{t}^{\theta}+\nabla_{l}\tilde{g}(x_{T},l_{T}^{\theta})\nabla_{\theta}l_{T}^{\theta}\right){\mathrm{d}}P_{\theta},

\displaystyle\mathrm{MFD}(\theta)

\displaystyle\coloneq\int\left(\sum_{t^{\prime}=0}^{T-1}\nabla_{l}\log\tilde{p}(\theta,a_{t^{\prime}},t^{\prime},x_{t^{\prime}},l_{t^{\prime}}^{\theta})\nabla_{\theta}l_{t^{\prime}}^{\theta}+\nabla_{l}\log\tilde{P}(x_{t^{\prime}+1}\mid x_{t^{\prime}},a_{t^{\prime}},l_{t^{\prime}}^{\theta})\nabla_{\theta}l_{t^{\prime}}^{\theta}\right)

\displaystyle\cdot\left(\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}r(x_{t},a_{t},\mu_{t}^{\theta})+g(x_{T},\mu_{T}^{\theta})\right){\mathrm{d}}P_{\theta},

where
\mathrm{MD}(\theta),\mathrm{MFD}(\theta)
stand for
M
easure
D
erivative and
M
ean-
F
ield
D
erivative respectively.

Remark 2.1
.

In the classical single-agent RL setting, the policy gradient formula only contains the term
\mathrm{RF}(\theta)
,
which can be estimated in a model-free setting by generating trajectories of the state process.
However, in the mean-field setting, we have two additional terms,
\mathrm{MD}(\theta)
and
\mathrm{MFD}(\theta)
, that involve the derivatives of the rewards, transition kernel and policy with respect to the measure argument.
These terms cannot be estimated directly in a model-free setting since they require knowledge of the model coefficients.

2.3
Perturbed Process and Value Function

We first give an informal presentation of the perturbation argument. For
\pi\in\Pi,\varepsilon>0,
and a sequence of perturbations
\lambda

=

(\lambda_{t})_{t=0}^{T}
, we consider processes
X^{\pi}_{t},Y^{\pi,\lambda}_{t}
such that

\displaystyle X^{\pi}_{0}

\displaystyle=\xi,\quad\alpha^{\pi}_{t}\sim\pi(\cdot\mid t,X^{\pi}_{t},\mathbb{P}_{X_{t}^{\pi}}),\quad X^{\pi}_{t+1}\sim P(\cdot\mid X^{\pi}_{t},\alpha^{\pi}_{t},\mathbb{P}_{X_{t}^{\pi}}),

(2.3)

\displaystyle Y^{\pi,\lambda}_{0}

\displaystyle=\xi,\quad\alpha_{t}^{\pi,\lambda}\sim\pi(\cdot\mid t,Y_{t}^{\pi,\lambda},\softmax(l_{t}^{\pi}+\varepsilon\lambda_{t})),

\displaystyle Y_{t+1}^{\pi,\lambda}

\displaystyle\sim P(\cdot\mid Y_{t}^{\pi,\lambda},\alpha_{t}^{\pi,\lambda},\softmax(l_{t}^{\pi}+\varepsilon\lambda_{t})),

where
l_{t}^{\pi}\coloneq\logit(\mathbb{P}_{X_{t}^{\pi}})
.
We consider an i.i.d. sequence of random perturbations
\Lambda=(\Lambda_{t})_{t=0}^{T}
of the state distribution at each time step, independent of the idiosyncratic noise, and distributed according to
\nu
.
As
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
is not a vector space, one needs to be careful about the definition of
(\Lambda_{t})
. Thanks to the logits parametrization introduced in Section
2.1
, this perturbation can easily be applied in the space of logits. We write
\tilde{\pi}({\mathrm{d}}a\mid t,x,l)=\pi({\mathrm{d}}a\mid t,x,\softmax(l))
. Then, for a fixed
\pi
,
Y^{\pi,\Lambda}
is a process for which the distributional dependences are randomized by
\Lambda
.
We consider the following approximation of the value function for a policy
\pi

V_{\varepsilon}^{\pi}(\mu_{0})\coloneq\mathbb{E}^{\pi}_{\xi\sim\mu_{0}}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}r(Y_{t}^{\pi,\Lambda},\alpha^{\pi,\Lambda}_{t},\softmax(l_{t}^{\pi}+\varepsilon\Lambda_{t}))+g(Y^{\pi,\Lambda}_{T},\softmax(l_{T}^{\pi}+\varepsilon\Lambda_{T}))\right].

(2.4)

This perturbed value function can be explicitly written as an integral

\displaystyle V_{\varepsilon}^{\pi}(\mu_{0})

\displaystyle=\int\left(\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\tilde{r}(y_{t},a_{t},l^{\pi}_{t}+\varepsilon\lambda_{t})+\tilde{g}(y_{T},l_{T}^{\pi}+\varepsilon\lambda_{T})\right)\mu_{0}({\mathrm{d}}y_{0})

\displaystyle\cdot\prod_{\tau=0}^{T-1}\nu({\mathrm{d}}\lambda_{\tau})\tilde{\pi}({\mathrm{d}}a_{\tau}\mid\tau,y_{\tau},l^{\pi}_{\tau}+\varepsilon\lambda_{\tau})\tilde{P}({\mathrm{d}}y_{\tau+1}\mid y_{\tau},a_{\tau},l^{\pi}_{\tau}+\varepsilon\lambda_{\tau}).

From this expression, the goal is to perform a change of variables in order to remove the dependence on
\theta
in the measure term of the rewards, policy and transition kernel. The gradient of this perturbed value function will not include the derivatives of the coefficients in the measure argument, which will lead to a model-free approximate policy gradient formula.

The parametrization of a probability distribution by its logits gives a way of continuously perturbing any probability distribution
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})^{*}
:
first, compute
l_{\mu}=\logit(\mu)
, then sample an
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
-valued random variable
\Lambda
, and finally define the perturbed distribution as
\softmax(l_{\mu}+\varepsilon\Lambda)\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})^{*}
. This is the main motivation for introducing this parametrization.

We now present a lemma that motivates the choice of this perturbation-via-logits scheme.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})^{*}
and
\varepsilon>0
. Denote by
\mu_{\varepsilon}
the perturbed distribution defined by

\mu_{\varepsilon}=\softmax(\logit(\mu)+\varepsilon\Lambda),\>\Lambda\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d}),

hence
\mu_{\varepsilon}
is a random variable valued in
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})^{*}
. Then, we have

\mathbb{E}[d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mu,\mu_{\varepsilon})]\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

(2.5)

In particular, Lemma
2.2
shows that the perturbation scheme leads to a controllable perturbation of the state distribution in total variation distance, of order
\varepsilon
.

2.4
Derivation and Convergence of Perturbed Gradient

We now present a policy gradient formula for the perturbed value function
V_{\varepsilon}(\mu,\theta)=V_{\varepsilon}^{\pi_{\theta}}(\mu)
in (
2.4
).
It is the foundation of our model-free estimator of
\nabla_{\theta}V(\mu,\theta)
.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let Assumption
1
hold.
Let
\Lambda_{t},t=0,\ldots,T
be i.i.d.
\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
random variables.
Assume that for any
\pi_{\theta}
,
(X_{t}^{\theta})_{t=0}^{T}
is an
\mathcal{X}
-valued process independent of
(\Lambda_{t})_{t=0}^{T}
and

(Y_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon})_{t=0}^{T}
is an
\mathcal{X}
-valued process such that, conditioned on
\Lambda=\lambda=(\lambda_{0},\ldots,\lambda_{T})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{dT}
,

X^{\theta}
and
Y^{\theta,\varepsilon}
are distributed according to (
2.3
) with
\pi=\pi_{\theta}

and initial condition
\xi
such that
\mu_{0}=\mathcal{L}(\xi)\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})^{*}
.
Let
(\alpha_{t}^{\theta})_{t=0}^{T-1}
and
(\alpha_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon})_{t=0}^{T-1}
be the corresponding controls.
Then, for
\theta\in\Theta
, the perturbed value function
V_{\varepsilon}(\mu_{0},\theta)=V_{\varepsilon}^{\pi_{\theta}}(\mu_{0})
is differentiable in
\theta
with

\nabla_{\theta}V_{\varepsilon}(\mu_{0},\theta)=\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\sum_{t=0}^{T}\left(\varepsilon^{-1}\Lambda_{t}\nabla_{\theta}l_{t}^{\theta}+1_{t<T}\nabla_{\theta}\log\tilde{p}(\theta,\alpha_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon},t,Y_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon},l_{t}^{\theta}+\varepsilon\Lambda_{t})\right)G_{t}^{\varepsilon}\Bigg],

(2.6)

where
G_{t}^{\varepsilon}=\sum_{s=t}^{T-1}\tilde{r}(Y^{\theta,\varepsilon}_{s},\alpha_{s}^{\theta,\varepsilon},l_{s}^{\theta}+\varepsilon\Lambda_{s})+\tilde{g}(Y_{T}^{\theta,\varepsilon},l_{T}^{\theta}+\varepsilon\Lambda_{T})
and
l_{t}^{\theta}=\logit(\mathbb{P}_{X_{t}^{\theta}})
.

Remark 2.2
.

Notice that, contrary to the exact policy gradient formula (
2.1
), the formula (
2.6
) only involves derivatives with respect to the trainable parameters
\theta
. However, estimating this quantity numerically is not straightforward due to the gradient of logits
\nabla_{\theta}l_{t}^{\theta}
. To overcome this challenge, one can use the same perturbation method applied to a problem with the same dynamics but with a different reward, as detailed in Section
3.1
,

Theorem 2.4
.

Assume the same conditions as in Theorem
2.3
. Furthermore, assume that
l\mapsto\tilde{P}(x^{\prime}\mid x,a,l)p(\theta,a,t,x,l)
is twice differentiable, with bounded second derivative uniformly in
t,x,x^{\prime},a,\theta
.
Then, as
\varepsilon\to 0
, we have
\nabla_{\theta}V_{\varepsilon}(\mu_{0},\theta)\to\nabla_{\theta}V(\mu_{0},\theta)
.

Remark 2.3
.

We want to highlight that both the gradient representation of Theorem
2.3
and the convergence result Theorem
2.4

hold independently of the way the processes
X^{\theta}
and
Y^{\theta,\Lambda}
are generated, as long as the assumptions of the theorems are satisfied.
In particular, we do not make any coupling assumption between
X_{t}^{\theta}
and
Y_{t}^{\theta,\Lambda}
.
In practice, using coupled samples might help reduce variance in the gradient estimates when subtracting a baseline depending on
X_{t}^{\theta}
to the cumulated rewards in (
2.6
),
as commonly done in single-agent REINFORCE implementations (
[
44
,
39
]
).

3
Policy Gradient Algorithm

In this section, we introduce a policy gradient estimator for mean-field control problems based on (
2.6
), and analyze its bias and error.

3.1
Model-Free Estimator

For the policy gradient formula (
2.6
) to be estimated in a model-free way, one needs to approximate
\nabla_{\theta}l_{t}^{\theta}
.
Recall that, under the probability–logit correspondence, we have

\nabla_{\theta}l_{t}^{\theta}=\nabla_{\theta}\left(\log\mathbb{P}(X_{t}^{\theta}=x^{(1)}),\cdots,\log\mathbb{P}(X_{t}^{\theta}=x^{(d)})\right).

Notice that

\nabla_{\theta}\log\mathbb{P}(X_{t}^{\theta}=x^{(i)})=\frac{\nabla_{\theta}\mathbb{P}(X_{t}^{\theta}=x^{(i)})}{\mathbb{P}(X_{t}^{\theta}=x^{(i)})}.

Using the same perturbation method as in Section
2.3
, we can approximate
\nabla_{\theta}\mathbb{P}(X_{t}^{\theta}=x^{(i)})
by

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\mathbb{P}(X_{t}^{\theta}=x^{(i)})

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\theta}\mathbb{E}\left[1_{X_{t}^{\theta}=x^{(i)}}\right]

\displaystyle\approx\mathbb{E}\left[1_{Y_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon}=x^{(i)}}\sum_{s=0}^{T}\varepsilon^{-1}\Lambda_{s}\nabla_{\theta}l_{s}^{\theta}+1_{Y_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon}=x^{(i)}}\sum_{s=0}^{T-1}\nabla_{\theta}\log\tilde{p}(\theta,\alpha_{s}^{\theta,\varepsilon},s,Y_{s}^{\theta,\varepsilon},l_{s}^{\theta}+\varepsilon\Lambda_{s})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[1_{Y_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon}=x^{(i)}}\sum_{s=0}^{t-1}\varepsilon^{-1}\Lambda_{s}\nabla_{\theta}l_{s}^{\theta}+1_{Y_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon}=x^{(i)}}\sum_{s=0}^{t-1}\nabla_{\theta}\log\tilde{p}(\theta,\alpha_{s}^{\theta,\varepsilon},s,Y_{s}^{\theta,\varepsilon},l_{s}^{\theta}+\varepsilon\Lambda_{s})\right].

Notice that we used the causality and log-likelihood trick: we are able to remove the terms
s\geq t
due to the ‘reward’ at time
t
not depending on the actions at time
s\geq t
. Indeed, for
s\geq t
, by denoting
\mathcal{F}_{s}
the filtration generated by
(Y_{\leq s}^{\theta,\varepsilon},\Lambda_{\leq s},\alpha_{<s}^{\theta,\varepsilon})
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[1_{Y_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon}=x^{(i)}}\left(\varepsilon^{-1}\Lambda_{s}\nabla_{\theta}l_{s}^{\theta}+\nabla_{\theta}\log\tilde{p}(\theta,\alpha_{s}^{\theta,\varepsilon},s,Y_{s}^{\theta,\varepsilon},l_{s}^{\theta}+\varepsilon\Lambda_{s})\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[1_{Y_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon}=x^{(i)}}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\varepsilon^{-1}\Lambda_{s}\nabla_{\theta}l_{s}^{\theta}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[1_{Y_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon}=x^{(i)}}\nabla_{\theta}\log\tilde{p}(\theta,\alpha_{s}^{\theta,\varepsilon},s,Y_{s}^{\theta,\varepsilon},l_{s}^{\theta}+\varepsilon\Lambda_{s})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[1_{Y_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon}=x^{(i)}}\right]\cdot 0+\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[1_{Y_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon}=x^{(i)}}\nabla_{\theta}\log\tilde{p}(\theta,\alpha_{s}^{\theta,\varepsilon},s,Y_{s}^{\theta,\varepsilon},l_{s}^{\theta}+\epsilon\Lambda_{s})\mid\mathcal{F}_{s}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=0+\mathbb{E}\left[1_{Y_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon}=x^{(i)}}\int\nabla_{\theta}\tilde{p}(\theta,a,s,Y_{s}^{\theta,\varepsilon},l_{s}^{\theta}+\epsilon\Lambda_{s})\,da\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[1_{Y_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon}=x^{(i)}}\nabla_{\theta}(1)\right]=0.

Hence, assuming we have access to
\mathbb{P}(X_{t}^{\theta}=x^{(i)})
for every
i=1,\ldots,d
(either via direct observation or by sampling many particles), we get
T\times d\times D
linear equations with unknowns
\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{k}}\mathbb{P}(X_{t}^{\theta}=x^{(i)})
, for
t=0,\ldots,T-1,i=1,\ldots,d,k=1,\ldots,D
, where the coefficients can be estimated by Monte Carlo sampling.
This system is triangular in the time variable, and it is easy to see that
\nabla_{\theta}\mathbb{P}(X_{0}^{\theta}=x^{(i)})=0
for all
i
, hence the system can be solved by forward substitution.
Still, the resulting estimator is biased due to the approximation in the perturbation method, but the bias can be made arbitrarily small by taking
\varepsilon
small enough.
However, taking
\varepsilon
too small may lead to high variance in the estimator.
The bias and variance of this estimator are studied in Sections
3.3
and
3.4
respectively.
In practice, the linear system might be easier to solve by subtracting a baseline from the first term

\mathbb{E}\left[1_{Y_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon}=x^{(i)}}\sum_{s=0}^{t-1}\varepsilon^{-1}\Lambda_{s}\nabla_{\theta}l_{s}^{\theta}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[(1_{Y_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon}=x^{(i)}}-b(X^{\theta}_{t}))\sum_{s=0}^{t-1}\varepsilon^{-1}\Lambda_{s}\nabla_{\theta}\mathbb{P}_{X_{s}^{\theta}}\right].

We leave the study of variance reduction techniques for this estimator to future work.

3.3
Bias of Estimator

We now study the bias of the estimator given by the Mean-Field REINFORCE algorithm.
The first step is to analyze the bias in the estimation of
\nabla_{\theta}l_{t}^{\theta}
given by Algorithm
2
.
As
\nabla_{\theta}l_{t}^{\theta}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times D}
, we bound the bias in the following matrix norm
\|M\|=\sup_{j=1,\ldots,D}\sum_{i=1}^{d}M_{i,j}
for
M\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times D}
. In order to provide quantitative bounds, we make the following set of assumptions.

Assumption 2
.

The parametrization of the policy
\pi_{\theta}
is such that
p
and
\nabla_{\theta}\log p
are globally Lipschitz in the measure argument, and so is the transition kernel
P
. Formally, there exist
L_{p},L^{\prime}_{p},L_{P}>0
such that for all
\theta
,
t

\in

\{0,\ldots,T-1\}
,
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X},a\in\mathcal{A}
and
\mu,\mu^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
,

\displaystyle\left|p(\theta,a,t,x,\mu)-p(\theta,a,t,x,\mu^{\prime})\right|\leq L_{p}d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mu,\mu^{\prime}),

(3.1)

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla_{\theta}\log p(\theta,a,t,x,\mu)-\nabla_{\theta}\log p(\theta,a,t,x,\mu^{\prime})\right\|_{\infty}\leq L^{\prime}_{p}d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mu,\mu^{\prime}),

\displaystyle\sup_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}\left|P(x^{\prime}\mid x,a,\mu)-P(x^{\prime}\mid x,a,\mu^{\prime})\right|\leq L_{P}d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mu,\mu^{\prime}).

Moreover,
\tilde{P}
and
\tilde{p}
are twice differentiable in
l\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, and there exist
B_{1},B_{2}\geq 0
such that
for all
\theta\in\Theta,x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X},a\in\mathcal{A},t\in\{0,\ldots,T-1\}
and
l\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial l_{i}}\left(\tilde{P}(x^{\prime}\mid x,a,l)\tilde{p}(\theta,a,t,x,l)\right)\right|\leq B_{1},\>\forall i,

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial l_{i}\partial l_{j}}\left(\tilde{P}(x^{\prime}\mid x,a,l)\tilde{p}(\theta,a,t,x,l)\right)\right|\leq B_{2},\>\forall i,j.

We first prove that the gradient of logits estimator is bounded, starting with a lemma on the true gradient of logits.

Lemma 3.1
.

Assume the same conditions as in Theorem
2.3
, and let Assumption

hold. Then for any

, we have

\left\|\nabla_{\theta}l_{t}^{\theta}\right\|\leq\begin{cases}\frac{3C(\beta^{t}-1)}{2(\beta-1)},&\text{if }B_{1}\neq 0,\\
t\left\|\nabla_{\theta}\tilde{p}\right\|_{\infty}|\mathcal{A}|,&\text{if }B_{1}=0,\end{cases}

\beta\coloneq\frac{1}{2}\left(1+B_{1}|\mathcal{A}|+\sqrt{(1+B_{1}|\mathcal{A}|)^{2}+4B_{1}|\mathcal{A}|(d-1)}\right).

(3.3)

Remark 3.2
.

As

In order to properly bound the bias of the policy gradient estimator, we need to introduce the following regularity assumption on the reward.

Assumption 3
.

The reward functions

and

are globally Lipschitz in the measure argument, i.e.,
there exist constants

\tilde{r}
and
\tilde{g}
are twice differentiable in

such that
for all

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial l_{i}}\tilde{r}(x,a,l)\right|\leq M_{1},\>\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial l_{i}}\tilde{g}(x,l)\right|\leq M_{1},\quad\forall i,

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial l_{i}\partial l_{j}}\tilde{r}(x,a,l)\right|\leq M_{2},\>\left|\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial l_{i}\partial l_{j}}\tilde{g}(x,l)\right|\leq M_{2},\quad\forall i,j.

(3.6)

where
G_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon}=\sum_{s=t}^{T-1}\tilde{r}(Y_{s}^{\theta,\varepsilon},\alpha_{s}^{\theta,\varepsilon},l^{\theta}_{s}+\varepsilon\Lambda_{s})+\tilde{g}(Y_{T}^{\theta,\varepsilon},l_{T}^{\theta}+\varepsilon\Lambda_{T})
.
Then,

\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{\nabla V}(\theta)\right]-\nabla_{\theta}V(\mu_{0},\theta)\right\|_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon\left(k_{1,\varepsilon}\beta_{\varepsilon}^{T}-k_{2,\varepsilon}\beta^{T}+k_{3,\varepsilon}+\varepsilon k_{4,\varepsilon}\right),

(3.7)

where
k_{1,\varepsilon},k_{2,\varepsilon},k_{3,\varepsilon},k_{4,\varepsilon}
are constants defined in (
5.15
) that depend on
\varepsilon
and the problem parameters, which are bounded as
\varepsilon\to 0
.

Remark 3.3
.

The bias in the gradient of logits (
3.5
) contributes transparently to the bias in the overall policy gradient estimator. As
\varepsilon\to 0
, the dominating term in (
3.7
) is of order
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
, but we still observe a ‘curse of time’.

3.4
Error of the Estimator

In this section, we provide an upper bound on the per-coordinate mean squared error of the policy gradient estimator
\widehat{\nabla V}(\theta)
defined in Theorem
3.3
,
corresponding to the output of Algorithm
1
.
We start by giving an upper bound on the second moment of the estimator
\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}l_{t}^{\theta}}
defined in (
3.4
).

Proposition 3.4
.

Consider the same setting as in Proposition
3.2
.
Define
\Sigma_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon}
as the
d\times D
matrix with entries

\left(\Sigma_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon}\right)_{i,j}\coloneq{\mathrm{Var}}\left[\left(\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}l_{t}^{\theta}}\right)_{i,j}\right],\quad i=1,\ldots,d,j=1,\ldots,D.

Then, for all
t=0,\ldots,T
,
i=1,\ldots,d
and
j=1,\ldots,D
,

\left\|\Sigma_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon}\right\|\leq\frac{\gamma_{1,\varepsilon}}{n\varepsilon^{2}}\left(1+\frac{\gamma_{2}T}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)^{t},

(3.8)

where
\gamma_{1,\varepsilon},\gamma_{2}
are constants defined in (
5.19
) that depend on
\varepsilon
and the problem parameters.

Remark 3.4
.

The
n^{-1}
factor is expected. As
\varepsilon\to 0
,
\gamma_{1,\varepsilon}
is of order
\varepsilon^{-2}
(see the definition of
\gamma_{1,\varepsilon}
in (
5.19
)). Hence the overall dependence in
\varepsilon
of the bound (
3.8
) is at worst
\varepsilon^{-2T-4}
. Here again, we observe a curse of time.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let Assumptions
1
,
2
and
3
hold.
Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,
\theta\in\Theta
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
N\in\mathbb{N}
.
Let
\widehat{\nabla V}(\theta)^{(1)},\ldots,\widehat{\nabla V}(\theta)^{(N)}
be
N
independent copies of the policy gradient estimator defined in (
3.6
),
where each estimator
\widehat{\nabla V}(\theta)^{(k)}
is computed using an independent version of the estimators
\left(\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}l_{t}^{\theta}}\right)_{t=0}^{T}
defined in (
3.4
).
Let
\widehat{\nabla V}(\theta)=N^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\widehat{\nabla V}(\theta)^{(k)}
be the averaged estimator.
Then, for all
\theta\in\Theta
,

\mathrm{MSE}(\theta,N,n,\varepsilon)\coloneq\max_{j=1,\ldots,D}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\widehat{\nabla V}(\theta)_{j}-\nabla_{\theta}V(\mu,\theta)_{j}\right|^{2}\right]\leq\mathcal{K}_{1,\varepsilon}\varepsilon^{2}+\frac{\mathcal{K}_{2,\varepsilon}}{N\varepsilon^{2}}+\frac{\mathcal{K}_{3,\varepsilon}}{nN\varepsilon^{4}}\sum_{t=0}^{T}\mathcal{C}_{t}\varepsilon^{-2t},

(3.9)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{1,\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\left(k_{1,\varepsilon}\beta_{\varepsilon}^{T}-k_{2,\varepsilon}\beta^{T}+k_{3,\varepsilon}+k_{\varepsilon}\varepsilon\right)^{2},

(3.10)

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{2,\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=2(T+1)^{3}M_{0}^{2}\sum_{t=0}^{T}\left(E_{t}(\theta,\varepsilon)+\left\|\nabla_{\theta}l_{t}^{\theta}\right\|+\varepsilon C\right),

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{3,\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=6(T+1)^{3}M_{0}^{2}d\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{s=0}^{t-1}\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{d}\left|\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}l_{s}^{\theta}}\right|_{i^{\prime},j}+\varepsilon C\right)^{2}\right]\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{P}\left(X_{t}^{\theta}=x^{(i)}\right)^{-2},

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{t}

\displaystyle=\binom{T+1}{t+1}\left(dT\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{P}\left(X_{t}^{\theta}=x^{(i)}\right)^{-2}\right)^{t}.

Remark 3.5
.

Notice that
\mathcal{K}_{2,\varepsilon},\mathcal{K}_{3,\varepsilon}
are finite due to (
3.5
) and (
3.8
) respectively.
The bound (
3.9
) provides practical insights into the influence of the hyperparameters
n,N
and
\varepsilon
.
First, one chooses
\varepsilon
small enough for the squared bias term
K_{1,\varepsilon}\varepsilon^{2}
.
Then, one chooses
N
, i.e. the number of independent trajectories for the policy gradient estimation, to control
\mathcal{K}_{2,\varepsilon}/(N\varepsilon^{2})
.
Finally, to further help control the high-order term
\varepsilon^{-2T-4}
, one can choose to increase
n
, i.e. the number of independent trajectories per time-step in the computation of
\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}}l_{t}^{\theta}
, to be large enough.
Since the total sample complexity for generating
\widehat{\nabla V}(\theta)
is
nNT^{2}
, (
3.9
) suggests that one should take
N
as large as possible and
n=1
to minimize the MSE for a fixed computational budget. In practice, if time complexity is the main constraint, one can parallelize the simulation of
n
independent trajectories for the gradient-of-logits estimation
\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}}l_{t}^{\theta}
, which allows to increase
n
to further reduce the variance without increasing the total computation time.

Two-State Two-Action Toy Problem

The first experiment is a simple two-state two-action mean-field control problem.
It was previously considered by
[
17
]
in the context of mean-field
Q
-learning.
The action space is
\mathcal{A}=\{{\mathrm{ST}},{\mathrm{MV}}\}
and the state space is
\mathcal{X}=\{0,1\}
.
The transition kernel is defined as follows: for
x\in\mathcal{X},a\in\mathcal{A}
,
P(x^{\prime}\mid x,a)=\lambda_{x}\mathbf{1}_{a={\mathrm{MV}}}
if
x^{\prime}\neq x
and

P(x^{\prime}\mid x,a)=1-\lambda_{x}\mathbf{1}_{a={\mathrm{MV}}}
if
x^{\prime}=x
, where
\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1}\in(0,1)
are fixed parameters.
The running reward function is defined as

r(x,a,\mu)=r(x,\mu)=\mathbf{1}_{x=1}-\mu(1)^{2}-\lambda W_{1}(\mu,B),

where
W_{1}
is the 1-Wasserstein distance,
\lambda>0
is a fixed scalar parameter and
B
is a Bernoulli distribution with parameter
p
such that
1-\lambda_{0}\leq p\leq\lambda_{1}
.
The terminal reward function is
g(x,\mu)=r(x,\mu)
.
Although the original problem in
[
17
]
is an infinite-horizon discounted problem, we consider here a finite-horizon version of with time horizon
T=2
.
In this setting, there exists an optimal stationary policy given by

\displaystyle\pi^{\star}(a\mid 0,\mu)

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{1-p}{\lambda_{0}}\right)\mathbf{1}_{\{a={\mathrm{ST}}\}}+\frac{1-p}{\lambda_{0}}\mathbf{1}_{\{a={\mathrm{MV}}\}},

(4.1)

\displaystyle\pi^{\star}(a\mid 1,\mu)

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{p}{\lambda_{1}}\right)\mathbf{1}_{\{a=\mathrm{ST}\}}+\frac{p}{\lambda_{1}}\mathbf{1}_{\{a=\mathrm{MV}\}}.

In our experiments, we take the following parameters:

\lambda_{0}=0.5,\lambda_{1}=0.8,\lambda=10,p=0.6
.
This policy guarantees that, no matter the initial distribution
\mu_{0}
, the distribution at all time steps
t\geq 1
is
\mu_{t}^{\star}=B
.
The training and evaluation are done as follows.
For each training episode we simulate the population starting from a random initial distribution
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})^{*}
by drawing
\mu(L)\sim\mathcal{U}([0.1,0.9])
.
Every 10 training episodes, we freeze the policy and sample a validation episode for which we compute the population reward
V(\mu_{0})
starting from a fixed initial distribution
\mu_{0}=(\mu_{0}(0),\mu_{0}(1))=(0.2,0.8)
.
During training, we compute
N=200
trajectories for MF-REINFORCE, with
n=10
trajectories for the gradient of logits estimation.
For this first example, the policy considered is a simple static policy that outputs a
|\mathcal{X}|\times|\mathcal{A}|
matrix corresponding to the probability of each action given each state.
We make this particular choice of policy to check whether MF-REINFORCE is capable of recovering the optimal static policy given in (
4.1
).
We use the Adam optimizer
[
24
]
and train for 5,000 episodes using a learning rate of
10^{-3}
.
The experiments are run for
\varepsilon
ranging in the set

.
The validation rewards for the different values of
\varepsilon
are shown in Figure

4.2
Cybersecurity Example

We consider now an example inspired by the cybersecurity model first studied in
[
25
]
. This problem has been adapted from a mean-field game to mean-field control (e.g. from the perspective of a company or state trying to decide on the best policy to protect a population of computers) in
[
6
]
, Section 6.1.
In this model, we consider a large population of computers, each of which can be in one of the following four states: defended and infected (DI), defended and susceptible (DS), undefended and infected (UI), undefended and susceptible (US).
The action space is
\mathcal{A}=\{0,1\}
, where
0
means keeping the same level of protection (D or U) for the computer, and
1
means updating the level of protection (from D to U or vice versa).
When infected, the computer recovers at rate
q_{\mathrm{rec}}^{D}
or
q_{\mathrm{rec}}^{U}
depending on the level of protection.
When susceptible, the computer might be infected either directly by a hacker or by the other infected computers, at rates depending on the level of protection of the susceptible computer and the other infected computers.
Originally, this problem was formulated in continuous time, and the infinitesimal generator matrix can be written as follows, given the current distribution of the population
\mu
and action chosen by the computer
a
,

Q^{\mu,a}=\begin{pmatrix}\ldots&q_{\mathrm{rec}^{D}}&\lambda a&0\\
Q_{DS\to DI}^{\mu,a}&\ldots&0&\lambda a\\
\lambda a&0&\ldots&q_{\mathrm{rec}}^{U}\\
0&\lambda a&Q_{US\to UI}^{\mu,a}&\ldots\end{pmatrix},

where

\displaystyle Q_{DS\to DI}^{\mu,a}

\displaystyle=v_{H}q_{\mathrm{inf}}^{D}+\beta_{DD}\mu(DI)+\beta_{UD}\mu(UI),

\displaystyle Q_{US\to UI}^{\mu,a}

\displaystyle=v_{H}q_{\mathrm{inf}}^{U}+\beta_{UU}\mu(UI)+\beta_{DU}\mu(DI),

and all the instances of
\ldots
should be replaced by values to make each row sum to
0
.
Each computer incurs a cost whenever it is being defended or infected.
The individual cost per unit of time is given by
f(x)=k_{D}\mathbf{1}_{x\in\{DI,DS\}}+k_{I}\mathbf{1}_{x\in\{DI,UI\}}
.
We adapt this model to a discrete-time setting by considering a time grid
0,\Delta t,2\Delta t,\ldots,N_{\mathrm{epi}}\Delta t
. Between two points of the time grid, the transition matrix is given by
P^{\mu,a}_{\Delta t}=\exp(\Delta tQ^{\mu,a})
, and the running reward is
r_{\Delta t}(x,a,\mu)=r_{\Delta t}(x)=-\Delta tf(x)
.
In our numerical experiments we choose the following values for the parameters:

\displaystyle\beta_{UU}

\displaystyle=0.3,\quad\beta_{UD}=0.4,\quad\beta_{DU}=0.3,\quad\beta_{DD}=0.4,

\displaystyle q_{\mathrm{rec}}^{D}

\displaystyle=0.5,\quad q_{\mathrm{rec}}^{U}=0.4,\quad q_{\mathrm{inf}}^{D}=0.4,\quad q_{\mathrm{inf}}^{U}=0.3,

\displaystyle v_{H}

\displaystyle=0.6,\quad\lambda=0.8,\quad k_{D}=0.3,\quad k_{I}=0.5,\quad\Delta t=0.2.

Note that these are the same parameter values as in the MFC version considered in
[
6
]
, Section 6.1, except for the time discretization step. Finally, the rewards are discounted by
\gamma=0.5
and the terminal reward is taken to be the same as the running reward. The training and evaluation are done as follows.
As the transition probability is stationary and the terminal reward is the same as the step reward, the length of a training episode is taken to be
T_{\mathrm{train}}=3
to mitigate the ‘curse of time’ (see Remark
3.2
), and for each training episode we simulate the population starting from a random initial distribution
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})^{*}
.
Every 10 training episodes, we freeze the policy and sample a validation episode of length
T_{\mathrm{val}}=50
, for which we compute the population reward
V(\mu_{0})
starting from a fixed initial distribution
\mu_{0}=(1/4,1/4,1/4,1/4)
.
During training, we compute
N=200
trajectories for MF-REINFORCE, with
n=1
trajectory for the gradient of logits estimation.
The policy is given by a 2-layer MLP, with 32 hidden units and
\tanh
activation; it takes as input
t,\mu
and outputs a
\mathcal{|}\mathcal{X}|\times|\mathcal{A}|
matrix corresponding to the probability of each action given each state.
We use the Adam optimizer and train for 20,000 episodes using a learning rate of
10^{-3}
.
The experiments are run for
\varepsilon
ranging in the set
\{0.2,0.5,1.0,2.0\}
.
The validation rewards for the different values of
\varepsilon
are shown in Figure
2
.
We clearly see the effect of
\varepsilon
on the variance of the overall training algorithm: the smaller the perturbation, the more erratic the gradient estimation becomes, which leads to slower convergence.
Surprisingly, taking a smaller value of epsilon does not lead to a better policy. To confirm that the policy obtained is not suboptimal, we compare the state distribution evolution under the learned policy to the results obtained in
[
6
]
with mean-field
Q
-learning.
The resulting flow of distributions is shown in Figure
3
. Clearly, we see that the qualitative behaviour of the population under the policy learned using MF-REINFORCE eventually matches the one learned using mean-field
Q
-learning in
[
6
]
.

Proof of Lemma
2.2
.

By Pinsker’s inequality, we have

d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mu,\mu_{\varepsilon})\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{KL}(\mu\|\mu_{\varepsilon})}.

We now compute the Kullback-Leibler divergence

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}(\mu\|\mu_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mu(x^{(i)})\log\left(\frac{\mu(x^{(i)})}{\mu_{\varepsilon}(x^{(i)})}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mu(x^{(i)})\log\mu(x^{(i)})-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mu(x^{(i)})\log\mu_{\varepsilon}(x^{(i)})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mu(x^{(i)})\log\mu(x^{(i)})-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mu(x^{(i)})\left(\log\mu(x^{(i)})+\varepsilon\lambda_{i}-\log\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}e^{\log\mu(x^{(j)})+\varepsilon\lambda_{j}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mu(x^{(i)})\lambda_{i}+\log\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mu(x^{(j)})e^{\varepsilon\lambda_{j}}\right).

By Jensen’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{KL}(\mu\|\mu_{\varepsilon})}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{KL}(\mu\|\mu_{\varepsilon})\right]}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[-\varepsilon\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mu(x^{(i)})\lambda_{i}+\log\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mu(x^{(j)})e^{\varepsilon\lambda_{j}}\right)\right]}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\log\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mu(x^{(j)})e^{\varepsilon\lambda_{j}}\right)\right]}.

Using Jensen’s inequality again, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\log\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mu(x^{(j)})e^{\varepsilon\lambda_{j}}\right)\right]\leq\log\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mu(x^{(j)})\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\varepsilon\lambda_{j}}\right]\right)=\log\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mu(x^{(j)})e^{\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}}\right)=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}.

Hence
d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mu,\mu_{\varepsilon})\leq\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}}=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
, which concludes the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem

.

Now, let’s consider a policy
\pi_{\theta}

\theta
and
l
and the differentiability of
\tilde{P}
in

.
Then, given the assumptions on

, one can differentiate under the integral sign to get

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}V_{\varepsilon}(\mu_{0},\theta)

\displaystyle=\int\left(\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\tilde{r}(y_{t},a_{t},\tilde{\lambda}_{t})+\tilde{g}(y_{T},\tilde{\lambda}_{t})\right)

\displaystyle\cdot\sum_{t=0}^{T}\varepsilon^{-2}(l_{t}^{\theta}-\tilde{\lambda}_{t})\nabla_{\theta}l_{t}^{\theta}\prod_{\tau=0}^{T}\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{(d-1)/2}\varepsilon^{d-1}}\exp\left(-\frac{\|\tilde{\lambda}_{\tau}-l_{\tau}^{\theta}\|^{2}}{2\varepsilon^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\cdot\mu_{0}({\mathrm{d}}y_{0})\prod_{\tau=0}^{T-1}\mathrm{Leb}({\mathrm{d}}\tilde{\lambda}_{\tau})\tilde{\pi}_{\theta}({\mathrm{d}}a_{\tau}\mid\tau,y_{\tau},\tilde{\lambda}_{\tau})\tilde{P}({\mathrm{d}}y_{\tau+1}\mid y_{\tau},a_{\tau},\tilde{\lambda}_{\tau})

\displaystyle+\int\left(\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\tilde{r}(y_{t},a_{t},\tilde{\lambda}_{t})+\tilde{g}(y_{T},\tilde{\lambda}_{T})\right)\prod_{\tau=0}^{T}\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}\varepsilon^{d}}\exp\left(-\frac{\|\tilde{\lambda}_{\tau}-\mu_{\tau}^{\theta}\|^{2}}{2\varepsilon^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\cdot\nabla_{\theta}\left(\mu_{0}({\mathrm{d}}y_{0})\prod_{\tau=0}^{T-1}\mathrm{Leb}({\mathrm{d}}\tilde{\lambda}_{\tau})\tilde{\pi}_{\theta}({\mathrm{d}}a_{\tau}\mid\tau,y_{\tau},\tilde{\lambda}_{\tau})\tilde{P}({\mathrm{d}}y_{\tau+1}\mid y_{\tau},a_{\tau},\tilde{\lambda}_{\tau})\right).

We have

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\left(\mu_{0}({\mathrm{d}}y_{0})\prod_{\tau=0}^{T-1}\mathrm{Leb}({\mathrm{d}}\tilde{\lambda}_{\tau})\tilde{\pi}_{\theta}({\mathrm{d}}a_{\tau}\mid\tau,y_{\tau},\tilde{\lambda}_{\tau})\tilde{P}({\mathrm{d}}y_{\tau+1}\mid y_{\tau},a_{\tau},\tilde{\lambda}_{\tau})\right)

\displaystyle=\mu({\mathrm{d}}y_{0})\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\nabla_{\theta}\log\tilde{p}(\theta,a_{t},t,y_{t},\tilde{\lambda}_{t})\prod_{\tau=0}^{T-1}\mathrm{Leb}({\mathrm{d}}\tilde{\lambda}_{\tau})\tilde{\pi}_{\theta}({\mathrm{d}}a_{\tau}\mid\tau,y_{\tau},\tilde{\lambda}_{\tau})\tilde{P}({\mathrm{d}}y_{\tau+1}\mid y_{\tau},a_{\tau},\tilde{\lambda}_{\tau}).

Hence

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}V_{\varepsilon}(\mu_{0},\theta)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\left(\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\tilde{r}(Y^{\theta,\varepsilon}_{t},\alpha_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon},l_{t}^{\theta}+\varepsilon\Lambda_{t})+\tilde{g}(Y_{T}^{\theta,\varepsilon},l_{T}^{\theta}+\varepsilon\Lambda_{T})\right)

\displaystyle\cdot\left(\sum_{t=0}^{T}\varepsilon^{-1}\Lambda_{t}\left(\nabla_{\theta}l_{t}^{\theta}\right)\right)\Bigg]

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\left(\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\tilde{r}(Y^{\theta,\varepsilon}_{t},\alpha_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon},l_{t}^{\theta}+\varepsilon\Lambda_{t})+\tilde{g}(Y_{T}^{\theta,\varepsilon},l_{T}^{\theta}+\varepsilon\Lambda_{T})\right)

\displaystyle\cdot\left(\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\nabla_{\theta}\log\tilde{p}(\theta,\alpha_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon},t,Y_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon},l_{t}^{\theta}+\varepsilon\Lambda_{t}\right)\Bigg].

Finally, one can apply the causality trick to only consider the rewards from time
t
to
T
in the expectations, leading to (

).
∎

Proof of Theorem
2.4
.

Let’s decompose the expression of the gradient of the perturbed value function (
2.6
),

\nabla_{\theta}V_{\varepsilon}(\mu_{0},\theta)=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T}\varepsilon^{-1}\Lambda_{t}\nabla_{\theta}l_{t}^{\theta}G_{t}^{\varepsilon}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\nabla_{\theta}\log\tilde{p}(\theta,\alpha_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon},t,Y_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon},l_{t}^{\theta}+\varepsilon\Lambda_{t})G_{t}^{\varepsilon}\right].

(5.3)

We first look at the second expectation in (
5.3
).
Clearly, since
Y_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon}\to X_{t}^{\theta}
and
\alpha_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon}\to\alpha_{t}^{\theta}
in distribution as
\varepsilon\to 0
, we have, by expanding the expression of
G_{t}^{\varepsilon}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\left(\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\tilde{r}(Y^{\theta,\varepsilon}_{t},\alpha_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon},l_{t}^{\theta}+\varepsilon\Lambda_{t})+\tilde{g}(Y_{T}^{\theta,\varepsilon},l_{T}^{\theta}+\varepsilon\Lambda_{T})\right)\left(\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\nabla_{\theta}\log\tilde{p}(\theta,\alpha_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon},t,Y_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon},l_{t}^{\theta}+\varepsilon\Lambda_{t}\right)\Bigg]

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\left(\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}r(X^{\theta}_{t},\alpha_{t}^{\theta},\mathbb{P}_{X_{t}^{\theta}})+g(X_{T}^{\theta,\varepsilon},\mathbb{P}_{X^{\theta}_{T}})\right)\left(\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\nabla_{\theta}\log p(\theta,\alpha_{t}^{\theta},t,X_{t}^{\theta},\mathbb{P}_{X^{\theta}_{t}})\right)\Bigg]=\mathrm{RF}(\theta).

We now look at the first expectation in (
5.3
) as
\varepsilon\to 0
, which we rewrite using the following lemma.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
X,Y
be random variables valued in a measurable space
E
, and let
\varphi:E\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a bounded measurable function. Assume
X,Y
have positive densities
p_{X},p_{Y}
with respect to a reference measure
q\in\mathcal{P}(E)
, and that
p_{X}
is bounded away from
0
. Then
\mathbb{E}[\varphi(Y)]=\mathbb{E}\left[\varphi(X)\frac{p_{Y}(X)}{p_{X}(X)}\right]
.

We apply Lemma
5.1
to
X=\left(X_{0}^{\theta},\alpha_{0}^{\theta},\ldots,\alpha_{T-1}^{\theta},X_{T}^{\theta}\right)
and
Y=\left(Y_{0}^{\theta,\varepsilon},\alpha_{0}^{\theta,\varepsilon},\ldots,\alpha_{T-1}^{\theta,\varepsilon},Y_{T}^{\theta,\varepsilon}\right)
to get

\displaystyle M_{\varepsilon}(\theta)

\displaystyle\coloneq\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\tilde{r}(Y^{\theta,\varepsilon}_{t},\alpha_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon},l_{t}^{\theta}+\varepsilon\Lambda_{t})+\tilde{g}(Y_{T}^{\theta,\varepsilon},l_{T}^{\theta}+\varepsilon\Lambda_{T})\right)\cdot\left(\sum_{t=0}^{T}\varepsilon^{-1}\Lambda_{t}\nabla_{\theta}l_{t}^{\theta}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\tilde{r}(X_{t}^{\theta},\alpha_{t}^{\theta},l_{t}^{\theta}+\varepsilon\Lambda_{t})+\tilde{g}(X_{T}^{\theta},l^{\theta}_{T}+\varepsilon\Lambda_{T})\right)\cdot\left(\sum_{t=0}^{T}\varepsilon^{-1}\Lambda_{t}\nabla_{\theta}l_{t}^{\theta}\right)R_{T}^{\theta,\varepsilon}\right],

where the density ratio is

R_{T}^{\theta,\varepsilon}=\prod_{\tau=0}^{T-1}\frac{\tilde{p}(\theta,\alpha_{\tau}^{\theta},\tau,X_{\tau}^{\theta},l_{\tau}^{\theta}+\varepsilon\Lambda_{\tau})\tilde{P}(X_{\tau+1}^{\theta}\mid X_{\tau}^{\theta},\alpha_{\tau}^{\theta},l_{\tau}^{\theta}+\varepsilon\Lambda_{\tau})}{p(\theta,\alpha_{\tau}^{\theta},\tau,X_{\tau}^{\theta},\mathbb{P}_{X_{\tau}^{\theta}})P(X_{\tau+1}^{\theta}\mid X_{\tau}^{\theta},\alpha_{\tau}^{\theta},\mathbb{P}_{X_{\tau}^{\theta}})}.

(5.4)

Since
l\mapsto\tilde{P}(x^{\prime}\mid x,a,l)\tilde{p}(\theta,a,t,x,l)
is twice differentiable with uniformly bounded second derivative,
using Taylor’s formula with Lagrange remainder as
\varepsilon\to 0
we have, almost-surely

\displaystyle R_{T}^{\theta,\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\prod_{\tau=0}^{T-1}\Big[\left(1+\varepsilon\nabla_{l}\log\tilde{p}(\theta,\alpha_{\tau}^{\theta},\tau,X_{\tau}^{\theta},l_{\tau}^{\theta})\cdot\Lambda_{\tau}+o(\varepsilon)\right)

\displaystyle\cdot\left(1+\varepsilon\nabla_{l}\log\tilde{P}(X_{\tau+1}^{\theta}\mid X_{\tau}^{\theta},\alpha_{\tau}^{\theta},l_{\tau}^{\theta})\cdot\Lambda_{\tau}+o(\varepsilon)\right)\Big]

\displaystyle=1+\varepsilon\sum_{\tau=0}^{T-1}\left(\nabla_{l}\log\tilde{P}(X_{\tau+1}^{\theta}\mid X_{\tau}^{\theta},\alpha_{\tau}^{\theta},l_{\tau}^{\theta})+\nabla_{l}\log\tilde{p}(\theta,\alpha_{\tau}^{\theta},\tau,X_{\tau}^{\theta},l_{\tau}^{\theta})\right)\cdot\Lambda_{\tau}+o(\varepsilon),

where
o(\varepsilon)
corresponds to the Lagrange remainder uniformly bounded in
\varepsilon
and converging to
0
almost surely as
\varepsilon\to 0
.
This means that as
\varepsilon\to 0
, by the dominated convergence theorem,
M_{\varepsilon}(\theta)
can be written as

\displaystyle M_{\varepsilon}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{MD}_{\varepsilon}(\theta)+\mathrm{MFD}_{\varepsilon}(\theta)+o(1),\quad\text{where}

\displaystyle\mathrm{MD}_{\varepsilon}(\theta)

\displaystyle\coloneq\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\tilde{r}(X_{t}^{\theta},\alpha_{t}^{\theta},l_{t}^{\theta}+\varepsilon\Lambda_{t})+\tilde{g}(X_{T}^{\theta},l^{\theta}_{T}+\varepsilon\Lambda_{T})\right)\cdot\left(\sum_{s=0}^{T}\varepsilon^{-1}\Lambda_{s}\nabla_{\theta}l_{s}^{\theta}\right)\right],

\displaystyle\mathrm{MFD}_{\varepsilon}(\theta)

\displaystyle\coloneq\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\left(\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\tilde{r}(X_{t}^{\theta},\alpha_{t}^{\theta},l_{t}^{\theta}+\varepsilon\Lambda_{t})+\tilde{g}(X_{T}^{\theta},l^{\theta}_{T}+\varepsilon\Lambda_{T})\right)\cdot\left(\sum_{s=0}^{T}\Lambda_{s}\nabla_{\theta}l_{s}^{\theta}\right)

\displaystyle\cdot\left(\sum_{\tau=0}^{T-1}\left(\nabla_{l}\log\tilde{P}(X_{\tau+1}^{\theta}\mid X_{\tau}^{\theta},\alpha_{\tau}^{\theta},l_{\tau}^{\theta})+\nabla_{l}\log\tilde{p}(\theta,\alpha_{\tau}^{\theta},\tau,X_{\tau}^{\theta},l_{\tau}^{\theta})\right)\cdot\Lambda_{\tau}\right)\Bigg].

The off-diagonal terms in
\mathrm{MD}_{\varepsilon}(\theta)
cancel by independence of
(\Lambda_{t})_{t=0}^{T}
, hence

\mathrm{MD}_{\varepsilon}(\theta)=\varepsilon^{-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}r(X_{t}^{\theta},\alpha_{t}^{\theta},l_{t}^{\theta}+\varepsilon\Lambda_{t})\Lambda_{t}\nabla_{\theta}l_{t}^{\theta}+g(X_{T}^{\theta},l_{t}^{\theta}+\varepsilon\Lambda_{T})\Lambda_{T}\nabla_{\theta}l_{t}^{\theta}\right].

We now examine the term
t
in the above sum.
By independence of
\Lambda_{s}
and
(X_{s}^{\theta},\alpha_{s}^{\theta})
we have

\mathbb{E}[r(X_{t}^{\theta},\alpha_{t}^{\theta},\mathbb{P}_{X_{t}}^{\theta})\Lambda_{t}]=\mathbb{E}[r(X_{t}^{\theta},\alpha_{t}^{\theta},\mathbb{P}_{X_{t}}^{\theta})]\mathbb{E}[\Lambda_{t}]=0.

Hence,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\varepsilon^{-1}\tilde{r}(X_{t}^{\theta},\alpha_{t}^{\theta},l_{t}^{\theta}+\varepsilon\Lambda_{t})\Lambda_{t}\nabla_{\theta}l_{t}^{\theta}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\varepsilon^{-1}(\tilde{r}(X_{t}^{\theta},\alpha_{t}^{\theta},l_{t}^{\theta}+\varepsilon\Lambda_{t})-\tilde{r}(X_{t}^{\theta},\alpha_{t}^{\theta},l_{t}^{\theta}))\Lambda_{t}\nabla_{\theta}l_{t}^{\theta}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla_{l}\tilde{r}(X_{t}^{\theta},\alpha_{t}^{\theta},l_{t}^{\theta})\cdot\Lambda_{t}\left(\Lambda_{t}\nabla_{\theta}l_{t}^{\theta}\right)\right]+o(1)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{l}\tilde{r}(X_{t}^{\theta},\alpha_{t}^{\theta},l_{t}^{\theta})]\mathbb{E}[\Lambda_{t}^{T}\Lambda_{t}]\nabla_{\theta}l_{t}^{\theta}+o(1)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{l}\tilde{r}(X_{t}^{\theta},\alpha_{t}^{\theta},l_{t}^{\theta})]\nabla_{\theta}l_{t}^{\theta}+o(1).

The same argument can be applied to the other terms in
\mathrm{MD}_{\varepsilon}(\theta)
, which proves that
\mathrm{MD}_{\varepsilon}(\theta)\to\mathrm{MD}(\theta)
as
\varepsilon\to 0
.

We now examine
\mathrm{MFD}_{\varepsilon}(\theta)
, let

\tilde{G}^{\varepsilon,\theta}_{0}=\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\tilde{r}(X_{t}^{\theta},\alpha_{t}^{\theta},l_{t}^{\theta}+\varepsilon\Lambda_{t})+\tilde{g}(X_{T}^{\theta},l^{\theta}_{T}+\varepsilon\Lambda_{T}).

Note that
\mathbb{E}[\tilde{G}^{\varepsilon,\theta}(\theta)\Lambda_{s}^{T}\Lambda_{\tau}]=0
whenever
s\neq\tau
and that
\tilde{G}^{\varepsilon,\theta}(\theta)
is a random variable converging almost surely to
G_{0}^{\theta}
, hence

\displaystyle\mathrm{MFD}_{\varepsilon}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\tilde{G}^{\varepsilon,\theta}_{0}\sum_{s=0}^{T-1}\left(\nabla_{l}\log\tilde{P}(X_{\tau+1}^{\theta}\mid X_{\tau}^{\theta},\alpha_{\tau}^{\theta},l_{\tau}^{\theta})+\nabla_{l}\log\tilde{p}(\theta,\alpha_{\tau}^{\theta},\tau,X_{\tau}^{\theta},l_{\tau}^{\theta})\right)\cdot\Lambda_{s}\left(\Lambda_{s}\nabla_{\theta}l_{t}^{\theta}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[G_{0}^{\theta}\sum_{s=0}^{T-1}\left(\nabla_{l}\log\tilde{P}(X_{\tau+1}^{\theta}\mid X_{\tau}^{\theta},\alpha_{\tau}^{\theta},l_{\tau}^{\theta})\tilde{p}(\theta,\alpha_{\tau}^{\theta},\tau,X_{\tau}^{\theta},l_{\tau}^{\theta})\right)\cdot\Lambda_{s}\left(\Lambda_{s}\nabla_{\theta}l_{t}^{\theta}\right)\right]+o(1)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[G_{0}^{\theta}\sum_{s=0}^{T-1}\left(\nabla_{l}\log\tilde{P}(X_{\tau+1}^{\theta}\mid X_{\tau}^{\theta},\alpha_{\tau}^{\theta},l_{\tau}^{\theta})\tilde{p}(\theta,\alpha_{\tau}^{\theta},\tau,X_{\tau}^{\theta},l_{\tau}^{\theta})\right)\nabla_{\theta}l_{t}^{\theta}\right]+o(1),

where we have used independence of
\Lambda_{s}
and
(X_{s}^{\theta},\alpha_{s}^{\theta})
, and
\mathbb{E}[\Lambda_{s}^{T}\Lambda_{s}]=I_{d}
.
In particular, this means that
\mathrm{MFD}_{\varepsilon}(\theta)\to\mathrm{MFD(\theta)}
as
\varepsilon\to 0
.
Hence we have shown that

\nabla_{\theta}V_{\varepsilon}(\mu,\theta)\rightarrow\mathrm{RF}(\theta)+\mathrm{MD}(\theta)+\mathrm{MFD}(\theta)=\nabla_{\theta}V(\mu,\theta)\quad\text{as }\varepsilon\to 0.

∎

Lemma 5.2

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}_{X_{t}^{\theta}=x^{(i)}}\sum_{s=0}^{t-1}\left(\nabla_{l}\log\tilde{p}(\theta,\alpha_{s}^{\theta},s,X_{s}^{\theta},l_{s}^{\theta})+\nabla_{l}\log\tilde{P}(X_{s+1}^{\theta}\mid X_{s}^{\theta},\alpha_{s}^{\theta},l_{s}^{\theta})\right)\nabla l_{s}^{\theta}\right]

(5.9)

Proof of Lemma
5.3

We examine the first bound. We have

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}_{X_{t}^{\theta}=x^{(i)}}\zeta_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon}\Lambda_{s}\right]=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}_{X_{t}^{\theta}=x^{(i)}}\Lambda_{s}\sum_{\tau=0}^{t-1}U_{\tau}^{\theta}\right]+\sum_{k=2}^{t-1}\sum_{0\leq s_{1}<s_{2}<\ldots<s_{k}\leq t-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}_{X_{t}^{\theta}=x^{(i)}}\Lambda_{s}\prod_{r=1}^{k}V^{\theta}_{s_{r}}\right],

where

\displaystyle U^{\theta}_{\tau}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{j,k=1}^{d}\xi^{\theta}_{\tau,j,k}\Lambda_{\tau}^{(j)}\Lambda_{\tau}^{(k)}}{\psi(\theta,X_{\tau+1}^{\theta},\tau,X_{\tau}^{\theta},\alpha_{\tau}^{\theta},l_{\tau}^{\theta})},

\displaystyle V^{\theta}_{\tau}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\nabla_{l}\log\psi(\theta,X^{\theta}_{\tau+1},\tau,X_{\tau}^{\theta},\alpha_{\tau}^{\theta},l_{\tau}^{\theta})\cdot\Lambda_{\tau}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}U^{\theta}_{\tau}.

For the first term, we have by independence and zero-mean of
(\Lambda_{\tau})_{\tau}
that

\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}_{X_{t}^{\theta}=x^{(i)}}\Lambda_{s}\sum_{\tau=0}^{t-1}U_{\tau}^{\theta}\right]\right\|_{\infty}=\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}_{X_{t}^{\theta}=x^{(i)}}\Lambda_{s}U_{s}^{\theta}\right]\right\|_{\infty}\leq B_{2}d^{3}|\mathcal{A}|\mathbb{E}[|Z|^{3}],

where
Z
is a standard normal random variable.
We now examine the second term.
Fix
k\in\{2,\ldots,t-1\}
and let
s
and
0\leq s_{1}<\ldots<s_{k}<t
such that
s\in\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{k}\}
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}_{X_{t}^{\theta}=x^{(i)}}\varepsilon\Lambda_{s}\prod_{r=1}^{k}V^{\theta}_{s_{r}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}_{X_{t}^{\theta}=x^{(i)}}\Lambda_{s}\left(\varepsilon\nabla_{l}\log\psi(\theta,X^{\theta}_{s+1},s,X_{s}^{\theta},\alpha_{s}^{\theta},l_{s}^{\theta})\cdot\Lambda_{s}\prod_{r=1,s_{r}\neq s}^{k}\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}U_{s_{r}}^{\theta}+\prod_{r=1}^{k}\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}U^{\theta}_{s_{r}}\right)\right],

since all the other terms cancel by independence and zero-mean of
(\Lambda_{\tau})_{\tau}
.
We have

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}_{X_{t}^{\theta}=x^{(i)}}\Lambda_{s}\varepsilon\nabla_{l}\log\psi(\theta,X^{\theta}_{s+1},s,X_{s}^{\theta},\alpha_{s}^{\theta},l_{s}^{\theta})\cdot\Lambda_{s}\prod_{r=1,s_{r}\neq s}^{k}\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}U^{\theta}_{s_{r}}\right]\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon^{2k-1}}{2^{k-1}}B_{1}B_{2}^{k-1}d^{2(k-1)}d^{k}|\mathcal{A}|^{k}\mathbb{E}[|Z|^{2k}],

and

\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}_{X_{t}^{\theta}=x^{(i)}}\Lambda_{s}\prod_{r=1}^{k}\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}U^{\theta}_{s_{r}}\right]\right\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{\varepsilon^{2k}}{2^{k}}B_{2}^{k}d^{3k}|\mathcal{A}|^{k}\mathbb{E}[|Z|^{2k+1}].

Denote by
\sigma_{k}\coloneq\mathbb{E}|Z|^{k}
. Summing over
k
and all possible choices of
s_{1},\ldots,s_{k}
including
s
, we get the following bound

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}_{X_{t}^{\theta}=x^{(i)}}\zeta_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon}\Lambda_{s}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}B_{2}d^{3}|\mathcal{A}|\sigma_{3}

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=2}^{t-1}\binom{t-1}{k-1}\left(\frac{\varepsilon^{2k-1}}{2^{k-1}}B_{1}B_{2}^{k-1}d^{2(k-1)}d^{k}|\mathcal{A}|^{k}\sigma_{2k}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2k}}{2^{k}}B_{2}^{k}d^{3k}|\mathcal{A}|^{k}\sigma_{2k+1}\right),

which proves the first bound of the lemma for a constant
A_{1,\varepsilon}
depending only on
\varepsilon,B_{1},B_{2},d,|\mathcal{A}|,T
defined by

A_{1,\varepsilon}\coloneq\frac{B_{2}d^{3}|\mathcal{A}|\sigma_{3}}{2}+\sum_{k=2}^{t-1}\binom{t-1}{k-1}\left(\frac{\varepsilon^{2k-3}}{2^{k-1}}B_{1}B_{2}^{k-1}d^{2(k-1)}d^{k}|\mathcal{A}|^{k}\sigma_{2k}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2k-2}}{2^{k}}B_{2}^{k}d^{3k}|\mathcal{A}|^{k}\sigma_{2k+1}\right).

(5.10)

We now examine the second bound. We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}_{X_{t}^{\theta}=x^{(i)}}\zeta_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon}\nabla_{\theta}\log\tilde{p}(\theta,\alpha_{s}^{\theta},s,X_{s}^{\theta},l_{s}^{\theta})\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}_{X_{t}^{\theta}=x^{(i)}}\nabla_{\theta}\log\tilde{p}(\theta,\alpha_{s}^{\theta},s,X_{s}^{\theta},l_{s}^{\theta})\sum_{\tau=0}^{t-1}U_{\tau}^{\theta}\right]

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=2}^{t-1}\sum_{0\leq s_{1}<\ldots<s_{k}<t}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}_{X_{t}^{\theta}=x^{(i)}}\nabla_{\theta}\log\tilde{p}(\theta,\alpha_{s}^{\theta},s,X_{s}^{\theta},l_{s}^{\theta})\prod_{r=1}^{k}V_{s_{r}}^{\theta}\right].

For the first term, we have

\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}_{X_{t}^{\theta}=x^{(i)}}\nabla_{\theta}\log\tilde{p}(\theta,\alpha_{s}^{\theta},s,X_{s}^{\theta},l_{s}^{\theta})\sum_{\tau=0}^{t-1}U_{\tau}^{\theta}\right]\right\|_{\infty}\leq tCB_{2}d^{3}|\mathcal{A}|\sigma_{2}.

For the second term, fix
k\in\{2,\ldots,t-1\}
and let
s
and
0\leq s_{1}<\ldots<s_{k}<t

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}_{X_{t}^{\theta}=x^{(i)}}\nabla_{\theta}\log\tilde{p}(\theta,\alpha_{s}^{\theta},s,X_{s}^{\theta},l_{s}^{\theta})\prod_{r=1}^{k}V_{s_{r}}^{\theta}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}_{X_{t}^{\theta}=x^{(i)}}\nabla_{\theta}\log\tilde{p}(\theta,\alpha_{s}^{\theta},s,X_{s}^{\theta},l_{s}^{\theta})\prod_{r=1}^{k}\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}U_{s_{r}}^{\theta}\right].

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}_{X_{t}^{\theta}=x^{(i)}}\nabla_{\theta}\log\tilde{p}(\theta,\alpha_{s}^{\theta},s,X_{s}^{\theta},l_{s}^{\theta})\prod_{r=1}^{k}V_{s_{r}}^{\theta}\right]\right\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{\varepsilon^{2k}}{2^{k}}CB_{2}^{k}d^{3k}|\mathcal{A}|^{k}\sigma_{2k}.

Summing over
k
and all possible choices of

\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}_{X_{t}^{\theta}=x^{(i)}}\zeta_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon}\nabla_{\theta}\log\tilde{p}(\theta,\alpha_{s}^{\theta},s,X_{s}^{\theta},l_{s}^{\theta})\right]\right\|_{\infty}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{t-1}\binom{t}{k}\frac{\varepsilon^{2k}}{2^{k}}CB_{2}^{k}d^{3k}|\mathcal{A}|^{k}\sigma_{2k},

which proves the second bound of the lemma for a constant

depending only on
\varepsilon
,

,

defined by

(5.11)

concluding the proof of Lemma

.

.

\displaystyle=A_{3}\varepsilon,

.
For
\Delta_{\mathrm{RF}}^{(2)}(\theta)
, we use yet again the likelihood ratio trick. Recall

\displaystyle=\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[\zeta_{T}^{\theta,\varepsilon}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\nabla_{\theta}\log\tilde{p}(\theta,\alpha_{t}^{\theta},t,X_{t}^{\theta},l_{t}^{\theta})G_{t}^{\theta}\right]\right\|_{\infty},

where
G_{t}^{\theta}=\sum_{s=t}^{T-1}\tilde{r}(X_{s}^{\theta},\alpha_{s}^{\theta},l_{s}^{\theta})+\tilde{g}(X_{T}^{\theta},l_{T}^{\theta})
. By similar arguments as in the proof of Lemma
5.3
,
we can get hold of a constant
A_{4,\varepsilon}>0
depending only on
\varepsilon,B_{2},C,M_{0},d,|\mathcal{A}|,T
, bounded as
\varepsilon\to 0
, such that

\Delta_{\mathrm{RF}}^{(2)}(\theta)=\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[\zeta_{T}^{\theta,\varepsilon}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\nabla_{\theta}\log\tilde{p}(\theta,\alpha_{t}^{\theta},t,X_{t}^{\theta},l_{t}^{\theta})G_{t}^{\theta}\right]\right\|_{\infty}\leq A_{4,\varepsilon}\varepsilon^{2}.

We now examine
\Delta_{\mathrm{M}}(\theta)
, which we decompose as

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathrm{M}}(\theta)

\displaystyle\leq\Delta_{\mathrm{MD}}(\theta)+\Delta_{\mathrm{MFD}}(\theta),

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathrm{MD}}(\theta)

\displaystyle\coloneq\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T}\varepsilon^{-1}\Lambda_{t}\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}l^{\theta}_{t}}(G_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon}-H_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon})\right]-\mathrm{MD}(\theta)\right\|_{\infty},

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathrm{MFD}}(\theta)

\displaystyle\coloneq\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T}\varepsilon^{-1}\Lambda_{t}\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}l^{\theta}_{t}}H_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon}\right]-\mathrm{MFD}(\theta)\right\|_{\infty}.

Using the likelihood ratio
R_{T}^{\theta,\varepsilon}
we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T}\varepsilon^{-1}\Lambda_{t}\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}l^{\theta}_{t}}(G_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon}-H_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon})\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T}\varepsilon^{-1}\Lambda_{t}\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}l^{\theta}_{t}}(\tilde{G}_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon}-G_{t}^{\theta})R_{T}^{\theta,\varepsilon}\right],

where
\tilde{G}_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon}=\sum_{s=t}^{T-1}\tilde{r}(X_{s}^{\theta},\alpha_{s}^{\theta},l_{s}^{\theta}+\varepsilon\Lambda_{s})+\tilde{g}(X_{T}^{\theta},l_{T}^{\theta}+\varepsilon\Lambda_{T})
.
Since
\tilde{r},\tilde{g}
are assumed to be twice differentiable by Assumption
3
, we can use a Taylor expansion to get hold of random variables

\chi^{\theta}_{s,j,k}
for
s=0,\ldots,T
and
j,k=1,\ldots,d
bounded by
M_{2}
such that
\chi^{\theta}_{t,j,k}
is
\sigma(X^{\theta}_{t},\alpha_{t}^{\theta},\Lambda_{t})
-measurable when
t\leq T-1

and
\sigma(X_{T}^{\theta},\Lambda_{T})
-measurable when
t=T
, and

\tilde{G}_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon}=G_{t}^{\theta}+\varepsilon\sum_{s=t}^{T-1}\nabla_{l}\tilde{r}(X_{s}^{\theta},\alpha_{s}^{\theta},l_{s}^{\theta})\cdot\Lambda_{s}+\varepsilon\nabla_{l}\tilde{g}(X_{T}^{\theta},\alpha_{T}^{\theta})\cdot\Lambda_{T}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\sum_{s=t}^{T}\sum_{j,k=1}^{d}\chi^{\theta}_{s,j,k}\Lambda_{s}^{(j)}\Lambda_{s}^{(k)}.

Hence,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T}\varepsilon^{-1}\Lambda_{t}\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}l^{\theta}_{t}}(G_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon}-H_{t}^{\theta})\right]

(5.14)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left\{\sum_{t=0}^{T}\Lambda_{t}\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}l^{\theta}_{t}}\left(\sum_{s=t}^{T-1}\nabla_{l}\tilde{r}(X_{s}^{\theta},\alpha_{s}^{\theta},l_{s}^{\theta})\cdot\Lambda_{s}+\nabla_{l}\tilde{g}(X_{T}^{\theta},l_{T}^{\theta})\cdot\Lambda_{T}\right)\right\}R_{T}^{\theta,\varepsilon}\right]

\displaystyle+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\{\sum_{t=0}^{T}\Lambda_{t}\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}l^{\theta}_{t}}\sum_{s=t}^{T}\sum_{j,k=1}^{d}\chi^{\theta}_{s,j,k}\Lambda_{s}^{(j)}\Lambda_{s}^{(k)}\right\}R_{T}^{\theta,\varepsilon}\right].

The second term in the right-hand side is of order
\varepsilon
since all the random variables inside the expectation are bounded.
Using similar arguments as in the proof of Lemma
5.3
, one can get hold of a constant
A_{5,\varepsilon}
depending only on
\varepsilon,M_{2},B_{1},B_{2},C,d,|\mathcal{A}|,T
, bounded as
\varepsilon\to 0
such that

\left\|\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\{\sum_{t=0}^{T}\Lambda_{t}\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}l^{\theta}_{t}}\sum_{s=t}^{T}\sum_{j,k=1}^{d}\chi^{\theta}_{s,j,k}\Lambda_{s}^{(j)}\Lambda_{s}^{(k)}\right\}R_{T}^{\theta,\varepsilon}\right]\right\|_{\infty}\leq A_{5,\varepsilon}\varepsilon.

For the first term, we plug in the expansion of
R_{T}^{\theta,\varepsilon}
from (
5.8
) to get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\{\sum_{t=0}^{T}\Lambda_{t}\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}l^{\theta}_{t}}\left(\sum_{s=t}^{T-1}\nabla_{l}\tilde{r}(X_{s}^{\theta},\alpha_{s}^{\theta},l_{s}^{\theta})\cdot\Lambda_{s}+\nabla_{l}\tilde{g}(X_{T}^{\theta},l_{T}^{\theta})\cdot\Lambda_{T}\right)\right\}R_{T}^{\theta,\varepsilon}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T}\Lambda_{t}\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}l^{\theta}_{t}}\left(\sum_{s=t}^{T-1}\nabla_{l}\tilde{r}(X_{s}^{\theta},\alpha_{s}^{\theta},l_{s}^{\theta})\cdot\Lambda_{s}+\nabla_{l}\tilde{g}(X_{T}^{\theta},l_{T}^{\theta})\cdot\Lambda_{T}\right)\right]+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon).

Using the independence of
(\Lambda_{t})_{t=0}^{T}
and
(\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}l_{t}^{\theta}},X_{t}^{\theta},\alpha_{t}^{\theta})_{t=0}^{T}
, as well as the fact that
\mathbb{E}[\Lambda_{t}^{(i)}\Lambda_{s}^{(j)}]=\delta_{t,s}\delta_{i,j}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T}\Lambda_{t}\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}l^{\theta}_{t}}\left(\sum_{s=t}^{T-1}\nabla_{l}\tilde{r}(X_{s}^{\theta},\alpha_{s}^{\theta},l_{s}^{\theta})\cdot\Lambda_{s}+\nabla_{l}\tilde{g}(X_{T}^{\theta},l_{T}^{\theta})\cdot\Lambda_{T}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla_{l}\tilde{r}(X_{t}^{\theta},\alpha_{t}^{\theta},l_{t}^{\theta})\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}l^{\theta}_{t}}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla_{l}\tilde{g}(X_{T}^{\theta},l_{T}^{\theta})\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}l^{\theta}_{T}}\right].

Once again, one can get hold of a constant
A_{6,\varepsilon}
depending only on
\varepsilon,M_{1},B_{1},B_{2},C,d,|\mathcal{A}|,T
, bounded as
\varepsilon\to 0
such that

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathrm{MD}}(\theta)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla_{l}\tilde{r}(X_{t}^{\theta},\alpha_{t}^{\theta},l_{t}^{\theta})\right]\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}l^{\theta}_{t}}\right]-\nabla_{\theta}l_{t}^{\theta}\right)\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle+\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla_{l}\tilde{g}(X_{T}^{\theta},l_{T}^{\theta})\right]\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}l^{\theta}_{T}}\right]-\nabla_{\theta}l_{T}^{\theta}\right)\right\|_{\infty}+(A_{5,\varepsilon}+A_{6,\varepsilon})\varepsilon.

Now, we examine
\Delta_{\mathrm{MFD}}(\theta)
.
Using the likelihood ratio
R_{T}^{\theta,\varepsilon}
again, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T}\varepsilon^{-1}\Lambda_{t}\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}l_{t}^{\theta}}H_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T}\varepsilon^{-1}\Lambda_{t}\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}l_{t}^{\theta}}G_{t}^{\theta}R_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T}\varepsilon^{-1}\Lambda_{t}\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}l_{t}^{\theta}}G_{t}^{\theta}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T}\Lambda_{t}\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}l_{t}^{\theta}}G_{t}^{\theta}\sum_{s=0}^{T-1}\nabla_{l}\log\psi(\theta,X_{s+1}^{\theta},s,X_{s}^{\theta},\alpha_{s}^{\theta},l_{s}^{\theta})\cdot\Lambda_{s}\right]

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T}\varepsilon^{-1}\Lambda_{t}\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}l_{t}^{\theta}}G_{t}^{\theta}\zeta_{T}^{\theta,\varepsilon}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla_{l}\log\psi(\theta,X_{t+1}^{\theta},t,X_{t}^{\theta},\alpha_{t}^{\theta},l_{t}^{\theta})G_{t}^{\theta}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}l_{t}^{\theta}}\right]+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon).

Once again, one can get hold of a constant
A_{7,\varepsilon}
depending only on
M_{0},B_{1},B_{2},C,d,|\mathcal{A}|,T
, bounded as
\varepsilon\to 0
, such that

\Delta_{\mathrm{MFD}}(\theta)\leq\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla_{l}\log\psi(\theta,X_{t+1}^{\theta},t,X_{t}^{\theta},\alpha_{t}^{\theta},l_{t}^{\theta})G_{t}^{\theta}\right]\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}l_{t}^{\theta}}\right]-\nabla_{\theta}l_{t}^{\theta}\right)\right\|_{\infty}+A_{7,\varepsilon}\varepsilon.

Hence, the total bias can be upper bounded by

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{\nabla V}(\theta)\right]-\nabla_{\theta}V(\mu,\theta)\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\left(\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla_{l}\log\psi(\theta,X_{t+1}^{\theta},t,X_{t}^{\theta},\alpha_{t}^{\theta},l_{t}^{\theta})G_{t}^{\theta}\right]\right\|_{\infty}+\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla_{l}\tilde{r}(X_{t}^{\theta},\alpha_{t}^{\theta},l_{t}^{\theta})\right]\right\|_{\infty}\right)E_{t}(\theta,\varepsilon)

\displaystyle+\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla_{l}\tilde{g}(X_{T}^{\theta},l_{T}^{\theta})\right]\right\|_{\infty}E_{T}(\theta,\varepsilon)+(A_{3}+A_{5,\varepsilon}+A_{6,\varepsilon}+A_{7,\varepsilon})\varepsilon+A_{4,\varepsilon}\varepsilon^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\left(B_{1}d|\mathcal{A}|(T-t+1)M_{0}+M_{1}\right)E_{t}(\theta,\varepsilon)+M_{1}E_{T}(\theta,\varepsilon)+\tilde{A}_{1,\varepsilon}\varepsilon+\tilde{A}_{2,\varepsilon}\varepsilon^{2},

where
\tilde{A}_{1,\varepsilon}=A_{3}+A_{5,\varepsilon}+A_{6,\varepsilon}+A_{7,\varepsilon}
and
\tilde{A}_{2,\varepsilon}=A_{4,\varepsilon}
.
Using Proposition
3.2
, we can rewrite the upper bound in the following way

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{\nabla V}(\theta)\right]-\nabla_{\theta}V(\mu,\theta)\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon\kappa_{1}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}(T-t+1)\left(\left[K_{1,\varepsilon}+K_{2}\right]\beta_{\varepsilon}^{t}-K_{1,\varepsilon}\beta^{t}-K_{2}\right)

\displaystyle+\varepsilon\kappa_{2}\sum_{t=0}^{T}\left(\left[K_{1,\varepsilon}+K_{2}\right]\beta_{\varepsilon}^{t}-K_{1,\varepsilon}\beta^{t}-K_{2}\right)+\tilde{A}_{1,\varepsilon}\varepsilon+\tilde{A}_{2,\varepsilon}\varepsilon^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon\kappa_{1}\left[(K_{1,\varepsilon}+K_{2})\beta_{\varepsilon}^{T}\frac{2\beta_{\varepsilon}-1}{(\beta_{\varepsilon}-1)^{2}}-2K_{1,\varepsilon}\beta^{T-1}-K_{2}\frac{T^{2}+3T}{2}\right]

\displaystyle+\varepsilon\kappa_{2}\left[\left(K_{1,\varepsilon}+K_{2}\right)\frac{\beta_{\varepsilon}^{T+1}-1}{\beta_{\varepsilon}-1}-K_{1,\varepsilon}\beta^{T}-(T+1)K_{2}\right]

\displaystyle+\tilde{A}_{1,\varepsilon}\varepsilon+\tilde{A}_{2,\varepsilon}\varepsilon^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon\beta_{\varepsilon}^{T}\left[\kappa_{1}(K_{1,\varepsilon}+K_{2})\frac{2\beta_{\varepsilon}-1}{(\beta_{\varepsilon}-1)^{2}}+\kappa_{2}\beta_{\varepsilon}\frac{K_{1,\varepsilon}+K_{2}}{\beta_{\varepsilon}-1}\right]

\displaystyle-\varepsilon\beta^{T}\left(2\kappa_{1}K_{1,\varepsilon}+\kappa_{2}K_{1,\varepsilon}\right)-\varepsilon T^{2}\frac{\kappa_{1}K_{2}}{2}-\varepsilon T\frac{3\kappa_{1}+2\kappa_{2}}{2}K_{2}

\displaystyle-\varepsilon\kappa_{2}K_{2}+\tilde{A}_{1,\varepsilon}\varepsilon+\tilde{A}_{2,\varepsilon}\varepsilon^{2},

where
K_{1,\varepsilon},K_{2,\varepsilon}
are defined in (
5.13
) and

\kappa_{1}=B_{1}d|\mathcal{A}|M_{0},\kappa_{2}=M_{1}.

This gives the upper bound (
3.7
) with the following constants

\displaystyle k_{1,\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\coloneq\kappa_{1}(K_{1,\varepsilon}+K_{2,\varepsilon})\frac{2\beta_{\varepsilon}-1}{(\beta_{\varepsilon}-1)^{2}}+\kappa_{2}\beta_{\varepsilon}\frac{K_{1,\varepsilon}+K_{2,\varepsilon}}{\beta_{\varepsilon}-1},

(5.15)

\displaystyle k_{2,\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\coloneq 2\kappa_{1}K_{1,\varepsilon}+\kappa_{2}K_{1,\varepsilon},

\displaystyle k_{3,\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\coloneq-\frac{\kappa_{1}}{2}K_{2,\varepsilon}T^{2}-\frac{(3\kappa_{1}+2\kappa_{2})}{2}K_{2,\varepsilon}T-\kappa_{2}K_{2,\varepsilon}+\tilde{A}_{1,\varepsilon},

\displaystyle k_{4,\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\coloneq\tilde{A}_{2,\varepsilon},

which concludes the proof.
∎

Proofs on Estimation Errors

Proof of Proposition
3.4
.

As all the terms in the definition of
\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}l_{t}^{\theta}}
(
3.4
) involve the same version of
\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}l_{s}^{\theta}}
for
s<t
, we proceed by using the law of total variance. For any square-integrable random variable
Y
, we consider

{\mathrm{Var}}(Y)=\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{Var}}_{t}(Y)\right]+{\mathrm{Var}}\left(\mathbb{E}_{t}\left[Y\right]\right),

where
{\mathrm{Var}}_{t},\mathbb{E}_{t}
are the conditional variance and conditional expectation respectively given
\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}l_{s}^{\theta}}
for
0\leq s<t
.
Fix
t\in\{0,\ldots,T\}
,
i\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
and
j\in\{1,\ldots,D\}
.
We have

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Var}}_{t}\left(\left(\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}l_{t}^{\theta}}\right)_{i,j}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n\mathbb{P}\left(X^{\theta}_{t}=x^{(i)}\right)^{2}}{\mathrm{Var}}_{t}\left(\sum_{s=0}^{t-1}Q_{s,i,j}^{\theta,\varepsilon,t}+S_{s,i,j}^{\theta,\varepsilon,t}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n\mathbb{P}\left(X^{\theta}_{t}=x^{(i)}\right)^{2}}\left(\sum_{s=0}^{t-1}\left({\mathrm{Var}}_{t}\left(Q_{s,i,j}^{\theta,\varepsilon,t}+S_{s,i,j}^{\theta,\varepsilon,t}\right)\right)^{1/2}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n\mathbb{P}\left(X^{\theta}_{t}=x^{(i)}\right)^{2}}\left(\sum_{s=0}^{t-1}\left(2{\mathrm{Var}}_{t}\left(Q_{s,i,j}^{\theta,\varepsilon,t}\right)+2{\mathrm{Var}}_{t}\left(S_{s,i,j}^{\theta,\varepsilon,t}\right)\right)^{1/2}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n\mathbb{P}\left(X^{\theta}_{t}=x^{(i)}\right)^{2}}\left(\sum_{s=0}^{t-1}{\mathrm{Var}}_{t}\left(Q_{s,i,j}^{\theta,\varepsilon,t}\right)^{1/2}+{\mathrm{Var}}_{t}\left(S_{s,i,j}^{\theta,\varepsilon,t}\right)^{1/2}\right)^{2},

where

Q_{s,i,j}^{\theta,\varepsilon,t}\coloneq\varepsilon^{-1}\mathbf{1}_{Y_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon,t}=x^{(i)}}\left(\Lambda_{s}^{t}\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}l^{\theta}_{s}}\right)_{j},\quad S_{s,i,j}^{\theta,\varepsilon,t}\coloneq\mathbf{1}_{Y_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon,t}=x^{(i)}}\nabla_{\theta}\log\tilde{p}(\theta,\alpha_{s}^{\theta,\varepsilon,t},Y_{s}^{\theta,\varepsilon,t},l_{s}^{\theta}+\varepsilon\Lambda_{s}^{t})_{j},

and we have used
{\mathrm{Var}}_{t}(\sum_{\iota}X_{\iota})\leq(\sum_{\iota}{\mathrm{Var}}_{t}(X_{\iota})^{1/2})^{2}
, and
{\mathrm{Var}}_{t}(X_{1}+X_{2})\leq 2{\mathrm{Var}}_{t}(X_{1})+2{\mathrm{Var}}_{t}(X_{2})
for any square-integrable random variables
X_{\iota}
.
We have
{\mathrm{Var}}_{t}(S_{s,i}^{\theta,\varepsilon,t})={\mathrm{Var}}(S_{s,i}^{\theta,\varepsilon,t})\leq C^{2}

since
\nabla_{\theta}\log p
is bounded by Assumption
1
.
For
{\mathrm{Var}}_{t}(Q_{s,i,j}^{\theta,\varepsilon,t})
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Var}}_{t}\left(Q_{s,i,j}^{\theta,\varepsilon,t}\right)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{-2}{\mathrm{Var}}_{t}\left(\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{d}\mathbf{1}_{Y_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon,t}=x^{(i)}}\left(\Lambda_{s}^{t}\right)_{i^{\prime}}\left(\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}l_{s}^{\theta}}\right)_{i^{\prime},j}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon^{-2}\left(\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{d}{\mathrm{Var}}_{t}\left(\mathbf{1}_{Y_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon,t}=x^{(i)}}\left(\Lambda_{s}^{t}\right)_{i^{\prime}}\left(\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}l_{s}^{\theta}}\right)_{i^{\prime},j}\right)^{1/2}\right)^{2}.

We use the independence of
\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}l_{s}^{\theta}}
and
\mathbf{1}_{Y_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon,t}=x^{(i)}}\Lambda_{s}^{t}
to write, for all
i^{\prime}=1,\ldots,d
,

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Var}}_{t}\left(\mathbf{1}_{Y_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon,t}=x^{(i)}}\left(\Lambda_{s}^{t}\right)_{i^{\prime}}\left(\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}l_{s}^{\theta}}\right)_{i^{\prime},j}\right)={\mathrm{Var}}\left(\mathbf{1}_{Y_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon,t}=x^{(i)}}\left(\Lambda_{s}^{t}\right)_{i^{\prime}}\right)\left(\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}l_{s}^{\theta}}\right)^{2}_{i^{\prime},j},

hence

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Var}}_{t}\left(Q_{s,i,j}^{\theta,\varepsilon,t}\right)\leq\varepsilon^{-2}\left(\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{d}\left|\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}l_{s}^{\theta}}\right|_{i^{\prime},j}\right)^{2},

where we have used that
{\mathrm{Var}}(\mathbf{1}_{Y_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon,t}=x^{(i)}}\left(\Lambda_{s}^{t}\right)_{i^{\prime}})\leq\mathbb{E}[(\Lambda_{s}^{t})_{i^{\prime}}^{2}]=1
.
This implies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{Var}}_{t}\left(\left(\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}l_{t}^{\theta}}\right)_{i,j}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}n\mathbb{P}\left(X_{t}^{\theta}=x^{(i)}\right)^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{s=0}^{t-1}\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{d}\left|\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}l_{s}^{\theta}}\right|_{i^{\prime},j}+\varepsilon C\right)^{2}\right].

(5.16)

Next, we have

\mathbb{E}_{t}\left[\left(\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}l_{t}^{\theta}}\right)_{i,j}\right]=\frac{1}{\mathbb{P}\left(X_{t}^{\theta}=x^{(i)}\right)}\sum_{s=0}^{t-1}\left(\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{d}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\varepsilon^{-1}\mathbf{1}_{Y_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon,k}=x^{(i)}}\left(\Lambda_{s}^{t}\right)_{i^{\prime}}\right]\left(\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}l_{s}^{\theta}}\right)_{i^{\prime},j}+{\mathbb{E}}\left[S_{s,i,j}^{\theta,\varepsilon,t}\right]\right),

hence

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}}\left(\mathbb{E}_{t}\left[\left(\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}l_{t}^{\theta}}\right)_{i,j}\right]\right)=\frac{1}{\mathbb{P}\left(X_{t}^{\theta}=x^{(i)}\right)^{2}}{\mathrm{Var}}\left(\sum_{s=0}^{t-1}\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{d}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\varepsilon^{-1}\mathbf{1}_{Y_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon,k}=x^{(i)}}\left(\Lambda_{s}^{t}\right)_{i^{\prime}}\right]\left(\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}l_{s}^{\theta}}\right)_{i^{\prime},j}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}\mathbb{P}\left(X_{t}^{\theta}=x^{(i)}\right)^{2}}\left(\sum_{s=0}^{t-1}{\mathrm{Var}}\left(\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{d}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathbf{1}_{Y_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon,k}=x^{(i)}}\left(\Lambda_{s}^{t}\right)_{i^{\prime}}\right]\left(\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}l_{s}^{\theta}}\right)_{i^{\prime},j}\right)^{1/2}\right)^{2}.

By writing
v^{\theta,\varepsilon}_{s,i^{\prime},j}=(\Sigma_{s}^{\theta,\varepsilon})_{i^{\prime},j}={\mathrm{Var}}((\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}l_{s}^{\theta}})_{i^{\prime},j})
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Var}}\left(\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{d}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathbf{1}_{Y_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon,k}=x^{(i)}}\left(\Lambda_{s}^{t}\right)_{i^{\prime}}\right]\left(\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}l_{s}^{\theta}}\right)_{i^{\prime},j}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{d}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathbf{1}_{Y_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon,k}=x^{(i)}}\left(\Lambda_{s}^{t}\right)_{i^{\prime}}\right]\left(v_{s,i^{\prime},j}^{\theta,\varepsilon}\right)^{1/2}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{d}\left(v_{s,i^{\prime},j}^{\theta,\varepsilon}\right)^{1/2}\right)^{2},

where we have used that
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_{Y_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon,t}=x^{(i)}}\left(\Lambda_{s}^{t}\right)_{i^{\prime}}]\leq 1
.
Hence

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Var}}\left(\mathbb{E}_{t}\left[\left(\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}l_{t}^{\theta}}\right)_{i,j}\right]\right)\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}\mathbb{P}\left(X_{t}^{\theta}=x^{(i)}\right)}\left(\sum_{s=0}^{t-1}\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{d}\sqrt{v^{\theta,\varepsilon}_{s,i^{\prime},j}}\right)^{2}.

(5.17)

Combining the bounds (
5.16
) and (
5.17
), we get

\displaystyle v^{\theta,\varepsilon}_{s,i,j}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}\mathbb{P}\left(X_{t}^{\theta}=x^{(i)}\right)}\left\{\left(\sum_{s=0}^{t-1}\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{d}\sqrt{v^{\theta,\varepsilon}_{s,i^{\prime},j}}\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{s=0}^{t-1}\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{d}\left|\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}l_{s}^{\theta}}\right|_{i^{\prime},j}+\varepsilon C\right)^{2}\right]\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}\mathbb{P}\left(X_{t}^{\theta}=x^{(i)}\right)}\left\{dT\sum_{s=0}^{t-1}\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{d}v^{\theta,\varepsilon}_{s,i^{\prime},j}+\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{s=0}^{t-1}\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{d}\left|\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}l_{s}^{\theta}}\right|_{i^{\prime},j}+\varepsilon C\right)^{2}\right]\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}\mathbb{P}\left(X_{t}^{\theta}=x^{(i)}\right)}\left\{dT\sum_{s=0}^{t-1}\left\|\Sigma_{s}^{\theta,\varepsilon}\right\|+\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{s=0}^{t-1}\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{d}\left|\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}l_{s}^{\theta}}\right|_{i^{\prime},j}+\varepsilon C\right)^{2}\right]\right\}.

Note that, given the definition of
\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}l_{t}^{\theta}}
(
3.4
) and Assumption
1
, it is clear that
\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}l_{t}^{\theta}}
has finite second moment for a fixed
\varepsilon>0
.
By taking a sum over
i=1,\ldots,d
, we get the recursive bound

\|\Sigma_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon}\|\leq c_{1}\sum_{s=0}^{t-1}\|\Sigma_{s}^{\theta,\varepsilon}\|+c_{6}
,
where

\displaystyle c_{1}

\displaystyle\coloneq\frac{dT}{\varepsilon^{2}}\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{P}\left(X_{t}^{\theta}=x^{(i)}\right)^{-2}>0,

(5.18)

\displaystyle c_{6}

\displaystyle\coloneq\frac{1}{n\varepsilon^{2}}\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{s=0}^{t-1}\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{d}\left|\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}l_{s}^{\theta}}\right|_{i^{\prime},j}+\varepsilon C\right)^{2}\right]\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{P}\left(X_{t}^{\theta}=x^{(i)}\right)^{-2}\in(0,\infty).

We can apply Lemma
5.2
with
c_{2}=c_{3}=c_{4}=c_{5}=0
to get

\left\|\Sigma^{\theta,\varepsilon}_{t}\right\|\leq\frac{\gamma_{1,\varepsilon}}{n\varepsilon^{2}}\left(1+\frac{\gamma_{2}T}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)^{t},

where

\displaystyle\gamma_{1,\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\frac{3}{2}\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{s=0}^{t-1}\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{d}\left|\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}l_{s}^{\theta}}\right|_{i^{\prime},j}+\varepsilon C\right)^{2}\right]\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{P}\left(X_{t}^{\theta}=x^{(i)}\right)^{-2},

(5.19)

\displaystyle\gamma_{2}

\displaystyle=d\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{P}\left(X_{t}^{\theta}=x^{(i)}\right)^{-2}.

which concludes the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.5
.

Fix
j\in\{1,\ldots,D\}
. We use the bias-variance decomposition to write

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\widehat{\nabla V}(\theta)_{j}-\nabla_{\theta}V(\mu,\theta)_{j}\right|^{2}\right]={\mathrm{Var}}\left(\widehat{\nabla V}(\theta)_{j}\right)+\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{\nabla V}(\theta)_{j}\right]-\nabla_{\theta}V(\mu,\theta)_{j}\right|^{2}.

The bias term can be upper bounded using Theorem
3.3
.
We now focus on the variance term. We have, following the same reasoning as in the proof of Proposition
3.4

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Var}}\left[\widehat{\nabla V}(\theta)_{j}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}{\mathrm{Var}}\left(\sum_{t=0}^{T}\tilde{Q}_{t,j}^{\theta,\varepsilon}+\tilde{S}_{t,j}^{\theta,\varepsilon}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{N}\left(\sum_{t=0}^{T}\left({\mathrm{Var}}\left(\tilde{Q}_{t,j}^{\theta,\varepsilon}+\tilde{S}_{t,j}^{\theta,\varepsilon}\right)\right)^{1/2}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{N}\left(\sum_{t=0}^{T}\left(2{\mathrm{Var}}\left(\tilde{Q}_{t,j}^{\theta,\varepsilon}\right)+2{\mathrm{Var}}\left(\tilde{S}_{t,j}^{\theta,\varepsilon}\right)\right)^{1/2}\right)^{2},

where

\tilde{Q}_{t,j}^{\theta,\varepsilon}\coloneq\varepsilon^{-1}G_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon}\left(\Lambda_{t}\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}l^{\theta}_{t}}\right)_{j},\quad\tilde{S}_{t,j}^{\theta,\varepsilon}\coloneq\mathbf{1}_{t<T}G_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon}\nabla_{\theta}\log\tilde{p}(\theta,\alpha_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon},t,Y_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon},l_{t}^{\theta}+\varepsilon\Lambda_{t})_{j},

We have

{\mathrm{Var}}\left(\tilde{S}_{t,j}^{\theta,\varepsilon}\right)\leq C^{2}(T+1)^{2}M_{0}^{2}

since
\nabla_{\theta}\log p
is bounded and
|G_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon}|\leq(T+1)M_{0}
by Assumption
1
.
For
{\mathrm{Var}}(\tilde{Q}_{t,j}^{\theta,\varepsilon})
, we have, for all
i^{\prime}=1,\ldots,d
,

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Var}}\left(G_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon}\left(\Lambda_{t}\right)_{i^{\prime}}\left(\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}l_{t}^{\theta}}\right)_{i^{\prime},j}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[G_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon}\left(\Lambda_{t}\right)_{i^{\prime}}\right]^{2}{\mathrm{Var}}\left[\left(\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}l_{t}^{\theta}}\right)_{i^{\prime},j}\right]

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}l_{t}^{\theta}}\right)_{i^{\prime},j}\right]^{2}{\mathrm{Var}}\left(G_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon}\left(\Lambda_{t}\right)_{i^{\prime}}\right)

\displaystyle+{\mathrm{Var}}\left[\left(\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}l_{t}^{\theta}}\right)_{i^{\prime},j}\right]{\mathrm{Var}}\left(G_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon}\left(\Lambda_{t}\right)_{i^{\prime}}\right).

Notice that

\mathbb{E}\left[G_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon}\left(\Lambda_{t}\right)_{i^{\prime}}\right]^{2}\leq(T+1)^{2}M_{0}^{2},\quad{\mathrm{Var}}\left(G_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon}\left(\Lambda_{t}\right)_{i^{\prime}}\right)\leq(T+1)^{2}M_{0}^{2}.

Recall that
v^{\theta,\varepsilon}_{t,i^{\prime},j}={\mathrm{Var}}\left[\left(\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}l_{t}^{\theta}}\right)_{i^{\prime},j}\right]
, we have thus shown that

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Var}}\left(\tilde{Q}_{t,j}^{\theta,\varepsilon,t}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon^{-2}(T+1)^{2}M_{0}^{2}\left(\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{d}\left(2v^{\theta,\varepsilon}_{t,i^{\prime},j}+\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}l_{t}^{\theta}}\right)_{i^{\prime},j}\right]^{2}\right)^{1/2}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon^{-2}(T+1)^{2}M_{0}^{2}\left(\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{d}\sqrt{v^{\theta,\varepsilon}_{t,i^{\prime},j}}+\frac{1}{2}\left(E_{t}(\theta,\varepsilon)+\left\|\nabla_{\theta}l_{t}^{\theta}\right\|\right)\right)^{2},

where
E_{t}(\theta,\varepsilon)
is defined in (
5.5
).
Therefore, we get

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Var}}\left[\widehat{\nabla V}(\theta)_{j}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2M_{0}^{2}}{N}\left(\sum_{t=0}^{T}(T+1)\left(\varepsilon^{-1}\left(\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{d}\sqrt{v^{\theta,\varepsilon}_{t,i^{\prime},j}}+E_{t}(\theta,\varepsilon)/2+\left\|\nabla_{\theta}l_{t}^{\theta}\right\|/2\right)+C\right)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2(T+1)^{3}M_{0}^{2}}{N}\left(\sum_{t=0}^{T}2\varepsilon^{-2}\left(\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{d}\sqrt{v^{\theta,\varepsilon}_{t,i^{\prime},j}}\right)^{2}+\left(\varepsilon^{-1}\left(E_{t}(\theta,\varepsilon)+\left\|\nabla_{\theta}l_{t}^{\theta}\right\|\right)+C\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2(T+1)^{3}M_{0}^{2}}{N}\left(\sum_{t=0}^{T}2\varepsilon^{-2}d\left\|\Sigma_{s}^{\theta,\varepsilon}\right\|+\left(\varepsilon^{-1}\left(E_{t}(\theta,\varepsilon)+\left\|\nabla_{\theta}l_{t}^{\theta}\right\|\right)+C\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\delta_{1}}{N\varepsilon^{2}}\sum_{t=0}^{T}\left\|\Sigma_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon}\right\|+\frac{\delta_{2,\varepsilon}}{N\varepsilon^{2}},

where

\displaystyle\delta_{1}=4(T+1)^{3}M_{0}^{2}d,\quad\delta_{2,\varepsilon}=2(T+1)^{3}M_{0}^{2}\sum_{t=0}^{T}\left(E_{t}(\theta,\varepsilon)+\left\|\nabla_{\theta}l_{t}^{\theta}\right\|+\varepsilon C\right)^{2}.

Therefore, we have the following bound on the MSE

\displaystyle\mathrm{MSE}(\theta,N,n,\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\delta_{1}}{N\varepsilon^{2}}\sum_{t=0}^{T}\left\|\Sigma_{t}^{\theta,\varepsilon}\right\|+\frac{\delta_{2,\varepsilon}}{N\varepsilon^{2}}+\varepsilon^{2}\left(k_{1,\varepsilon}\beta_{\varepsilon}^{T}-k_{2,\varepsilon}\beta^{T}+k_{3,\varepsilon}+k_{4,\varepsilon}\varepsilon\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\delta_{1}\gamma_{1,\varepsilon}}{nN\varepsilon^{4}}\sum_{t=0}^{T}\left(1+\frac{\gamma_{2}T}{\varepsilon}\right)^{t}+\frac{\delta_{2,\varepsilon}}{N\varepsilon^{2}}+\varepsilon^{2}\left(k_{1}\beta_{\varepsilon}^{T}-k_{2}\beta^{T}+k_{3}+k_{4}\varepsilon\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\delta_{1}\gamma_{1,\varepsilon}}{nN\varepsilon^{4}}\frac{\left(1+\gamma_{2}T/\varepsilon\right)^{T+1}-1}{\gamma_{2}T/\varepsilon}+\frac{\delta_{2,\varepsilon}}{N\varepsilon^{2}}+\varepsilon^{2}\left(k_{1}\beta_{\varepsilon}^{T}-k_{2}\beta^{T}+k_{3}+k_{4}\varepsilon\right)^{2}.

Notice that, for
\delta>0

\frac{(1+\delta)^{T+1}-1}{\delta}=\sum_{t=1}^{T+1}\delta^{t-1}\binom{T+1}{t}=\sum_{t=0}^{T}\delta^{t}\binom{T+1}{t+1},

Hence we get

\mathrm{MSE}(\theta,N,n,\varepsilon)\leq\mathcal{K}_{1,\varepsilon}\varepsilon^{2}+\frac{\mathcal{K}_{2,\varepsilon}}{N\varepsilon^{2}}+\frac{\mathcal{K}_{3,\varepsilon}}{nN\varepsilon^{4}}\sum_{t=0}^{T}\mathcal{C}_{t}\varepsilon^{-t},

with the following constants

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{1,\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\left(k_{1,\varepsilon}\beta_{\varepsilon}^{T}-k_{2,\varepsilon}\beta^{T}+k_{3,\varepsilon}+k_{\varepsilon}\varepsilon\right)^{2},

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{2,\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\delta_{2,\varepsilon}=2(T+1)^{3}M_{0}^{2}\sum_{t=0}^{T}\left(E_{t}(\theta,\varepsilon)+\left\|\nabla_{\theta}l_{t}^{\theta}\right\|+\varepsilon C\right),

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{3,\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\delta_{1}\gamma_{1,\varepsilon}=6(T+1)^{3}M_{0}^{2}d\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{s=0}^{t-1}\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{d}\left|\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}l_{s}^{\theta}}\right|_{i^{\prime},j}+\varepsilon C\right)^{2}\right]\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{P}\left(X_{t}^{\theta}=x^{(i)}\right)^{-2},

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{t}

\displaystyle=\binom{T+1}{t+1}\left(\gamma_{2}T\right)^{t}=\binom{T+1}{t+1}\left(dT\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{P}\left(X_{t}^{\theta}=x^{(i)}\right)^{-2}\right)^{t},

which concludes the proof.
∎
