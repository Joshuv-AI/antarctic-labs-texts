Public distrust in physicians and healthcare utilization: a nationwide study in China
Authors: Guimiao Sun, Lili Kang, M Mahmud Khan, Xinkui Liu, Changle Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPublic distrust in physicians constitutes a significant barrier to effective healthcare utilization, particularly in China, where physician-patient tensions are well-documented. This study analyzed the association between public distrust in physicians and healthcare utilization in China.MethodsUtilizing a longitudinal nationally representative dataset from the 2012–2022 China Family Panel Studies, the final sample consisted of 168,174 observations. This study employed a two-way fixed-effects logistic regression analysis within the framework of Andersen’s Behavioral Model to examine the association between distrust and healthcare utilization, while controlling for predisposing characteristics, enabling resources, and need factors.ResultsRural areas exhibited a higher outpatient service utilization rate than urban areas. Urban inpatient service utilization rates rose annually from 9.43% in 2012 to 12.36% in 2018, surpassing rural rates, before falling to 9.71% in 2022, below rural levels. Rural residents who reported higher levels of public distrust in physicians showed a lower odds of accessing outpatient care (OR: 0.98, 95% CI: 0.96–0.99). Urban residents who reported higher levels of public distrust in physicians showed a lower odds of accessing inpatient care (OR: 0.97, 95% CI: 0.95–0.99).ConclusionHigher public distrust in physicians was associated with reduced outpatient care access among rural residents and diminished inpatient care access among urban residents. Improving physician-patient trust is a critical policy priority in China. Targeted policy interventions, including strengthening physician-patient trust in rural grassroots clinics, improving the quality of urban inpatient services, and promoting the balanced allocation of medical resources, are needed so as to narrow the urban-rural trust gap and promote the equitable and rational use of medical services.Keywords: Public distrust in physicians, CFPS, Healthcare utilization, China

Introduction

Physicians serve as pivotal actors in public health outreach work [1]. Individuals typically seek medical care upon the onset of symptoms, while physicians and health service agencies deliver services as needed. Trust in physicians is the core of the physician-patient relationship, and functions as “social capital” for an effective healthcare system [2]. A low level of public distrust in physicians should correlate with greater treatment adherence and sustained care continuity. However, as the development of society, a burgeoning issue in that context is that China and the rest of the world are confronting a profound crisis of trust in patient-physician relationships [3–6].

Globally, physician trust is experiencing a downward trajectory [7], posing a threat to the therapeutic alliance and potentially precipitating severe ramifications for healthcare systems. For instance, Armstrong et al. document that skepticism toward the healthcare apparatus is prevalent among the American populace [8]. The so-called “Golden Age of Doctoring” spanned from the early twentieth century through the 1970s; however, subsequent decades witnessed a marked erosion of public confidence in the system [9]. In Lebanon, physician trust is similarly deteriorating. Arawi and colleagues observe that the Lebanese public views the physician-patient relationship as deficient, “characterized by materialistic gain and a feeling of being treated as a ‘number’ and not as a ‘person’ [10].” Concurrently, empirical research in China has documented a parallel decline in patient trust toward medical practitioners [11].

Under China’s urban-rural dual structure, disparities in physician distrust directly shape healthcare-seeking behaviors, medical resource utilization efficiency, and health equity. This variable is critical for evaluating hierarchical diagnosis and treatment implementation, regulating physician-patient interactions, and narrowing urban-rural health gaps, with significant theoretical and practical implications for optimizing resource allocation, strengthening primary care systems, and advancing Healthy China initiatives. A Chinese urban-rural comparative study indicates that rural tuberculosis patients report significantly higher physician trust scores than their urban counterparts, which directly and positively predicts treatment outcomes [12]. However, the majority of studies suggest comparable trust levels between rural and urban residents [13–15].

The degree of trust in physicians will be directly reflected in the utilization of health services. A study spanning 1991–2011 indicates that rural inpatient utilization rates were significantly lower than urban rates, with urban residents reaching 1.7% compared to merely 0.7% among rural residents in China [16]. A study of two large psychiatric hospitals in Shandong Province demonstrates that rural inpatient service utilization is comprehensively and significantly lower than urban levels, with rural residents relying more heavily on outpatient services [17].

Studies have shown that public distrust in physicians is significantly influenced by information asymmetry, where medical professionals and institutions possess a higher level of relevant information due to their expertise, leading to patients being passive recipients of care. This asymmetry may lead to patients doubting physicians’ decisions and even developing distrust [18]. That is, physicians assume the dual roles of medical service providers (treating patients and saving people) and medical system agents (controlling costs and allocating resources). Patients believe that this role conflict makes doctors give priority to the interests of the system rather than the well-being of patients.

Most published studies have identified two forms of trust in physicians: interpersonal trust and public trust [18–21]. Interpersonal distrust refers to the distrust that patients develop with a specific physician during their therapeutic relationship. Public distrust denotes the general attitude of society toward the physicians in general rather than specific physicians or healthcare providers. In terms of measurement, researchers commonly use single-dimensional scales (Likert Trust Scale) or composite indicators (patient satisfaction combined with treatment compliance) to assess trust levels [22].

Previous studies in China have utilized Anderson’s Health Behavior Model to examine factors influencing healthcare utilization. This framework posits that healthcare utilization is determined by three interacting components: predisposing characteristics (demographic and social factors), enabling resources (financial and organizational factors), and need factors (perceived and evaluated health status). Available evidence demonstrates that distrust of physicians is closely associated with reduced utilization of medical services.

Public trust in physicians is an important psychosocial factor associated with individuals’ healthcare-seeking behavior. Prior empirical studies have provided evidence that trust in physicians is related to healthcare utilization. For example, a Chinese cross-sectional study found that both cognitive trust and affective trust were positively associated with the utilization of family doctor services [23]. In addition, Katz examined trust in physicians and trust in the healthcare system in relation to healthcare utilization outcomes, including doctor’s visits, emergency room visits, and hospital admissions, and reported that trust in physicians was significantly related to hospital admissions in some analyses [24]. Beyond these utilization-focused findings, previous studies have also identified medical mistrust as an important barrier to health promotion and a contributor to inequalities in healthcare access [25–28]. In the Chinese context, greater mistrust of primary healthcare institutions has been reported to be significantly associated with lower healthcare utilization, particularly for mild and common illnesses [29]. Furthermore, research among Chinese adults suggests that internet use may also influence the utilization of primary healthcare services by shaping patients’ trust in physicians [30].

However, obvious gaps still remain in the existing research, particularly regarding the mediating mechanisms underlying the association between physician distrust and healthcare utilization disparities across urban and rural settings [20, 31–33]. First, most studies have not included the doctor distrust system into the theoretical model framework, which makes it difficult to accurately estimate the marginal impact of distrust on the utilization of medical services under the premise of controlling key covariates, leading to the lack of robustness of relevant conclusions; Secondly, although studies focusing on the urban-rural dimension have gradually emerged, the differences in the distrust of doctors between urban and rural residents and the differentiation mechanism of how it specifically affects the utilization of two types of core services, outpatient and inpatient, have not yet been explored in depth, and the trust-driven logic behind the urban-rural medical utilization gap has not been revealed [34]. In addition, the existing research on the mediating path between doctors’ distrust and medical utilization through tendency, enabling and demand variables lacks targeted analysis combining urban and rural scenarios. These gaps make the academia’s understanding of the inequality in the use of medical services still not comprehensive enough, and also leave space for follow-up research to be filled.

To fill these gaps, this study integrates public distrust of physicians into Andersen’s Behavioral Model of Health Services Use, focusing on urban-rural disparities in distrust to examine its impact and mechanisms on outpatient and inpatient utilization, thereby providing empirical evidence for addressing urban-rural inequalities in healthcare access.

Methods

Theoretical model

The present study employed the Behavioral Model of Health Services Use (BMHSU), originally developed by Andersen [32]. Within the BMHSU framework, the individual’s utilization of health services (including both outpatient and inpatient care utilization) is determined by three key dimensions: (1) predisposing characteristics, which reflect the propensity to seek healthcare; (2) enabling factors, which facilitate or hinder access to services; and (3) perceived or evaluated need for care. Figure 1 illustrates the theoretical model underpinning this study. Each component of the model contributes independently to health services utilization, while the model itself delineates a coherent explanatory process with implicit causal ordering [35].

The theoretical model of this study using Andersen’s framework

Predisposing characteristics encompass socioeconomic status, demographic attributes, and health beliefs or attitudes. Demographic factors (age and sex) correlated with the likelihood of requiring medical care, whereas socioeconomic indicators (education and income) reflect an individual’s capacity to address health-related challenges. Notably, public distrust in physicians, a critical health belief, captures subjective attitudes toward the healthcare system, precedes actual healthcare utilization, and exerts enduring effects on healthcare-seeking behavior [36]. Collectively, these predisposing traits shape subsequent perceptions of need and ultimately influence utilization patterns. Enabling resources denote the tangible and intangible assets (insurance coverage and geographic access) that determine an individual’s ability to secure medical services. Perceived need arises from self-assessed health status and self-recognized demand for care, whereas evaluated need is derived from clinical judgments by healthcare professionals. Both dimensions of need directly modulate healthcare utilization [37].

Under the 2008 revised Andersen Behavioral Model [38], residency and location constitute contextual enabling resources reflecting structural constraints on healthcare access, while age, gender, education, physician/healthcare system distrust (predisposing), personal income, household size, insurance coverage (enabling), and self-rated health, chronic conditions (need) represent individual-level factors. This classification aligns with the model’s individual-contextual distinction and provides a theoretical foundation for examining mechanisms underlying urban-rural disparities in healthcare utilization. Public distrust in physicians constitutes a predisposing characteristic within the health beliefs and attitudes domain, representing pre-existing cognitive tendencies that shape healthcare utilization propensity. This variable entails bidirectional causality: distrust may suppress service-seeking behavior, while negative prior experiences may conversely reinforce distrust perceptions [39]. Explicitly positioning distrust theoretically and acknowledging this reciprocal relationship enhances both analytical rigor and explanatory power.

Data source

The China Family Panel Studies (CFPS), conducted by the Institute of Social Science Survey at Peking University, serves as the nationally representative longitudinal dataset for this analysis [40]. The CFPS primarily conducts face-to-face interviews aided by computer-assisted personal interviewing (interviewers used tablets to record responses), with each wave spanning from July to June of the following year. When face-to-face interviews are not feasible, telephone or web-based interviews are used as substitutes. Since its inception in 2010, the China Family Panel Studies (CFPS) has successfully completed seven biennial waves of data collection, culminating in the most recent data gathered through 2022. The survey captures multidimensional indicators, including demographics, family structure, health status, healthcare and insurance, work, income and consumption, assets, and community-level information [41].

The 2010 CFPS survey did not include data on public distrust in physicians, which contrasts with subsequent studies that have utilized CFPS data to explore the factors influencing patient trust in medical professionals. Thus, the current study constructed a six-period unbalanced panel dataset spanning from 2012 to 2022. In the second wave in 2012, 32,073 adults (aged 16 years or older) answered the full-length questionnaires; in five waves of full-sample follow-up surveys in 2014, 2016, 2018, 2020, and 2022, the number of adults successfully interviewed were 31,435, 31,213, 30,290, 22,476, and 20,687, respectively. The final analytical dataset comprised of 168,174 observations.

Measures

Public distrust in physicians

Public distrust in physicians was operationalized as an ordinal outcome variable with a range from 0 to 10, 0 indicating complete trust in physicians and 10 indicating complete distrust in physicians. The CFPS measured this construct using the following survey item: “Overall, how much do you trust physicians in China? Please indicate your level of trust on a scale from 0 to 10, where 0 stands for not all and 10 for completely (11-point Likert scale)”. Following prior CFPS-based research on public trust in physicians in China, we treated this item as an ordinal indicator of respondents’ overall level of trust in physicians in general rather than trust in a specific physician [29]. In the analysis, we reverse-coded these responses so that 0 represents completely trust and 10 completely distrust. We retained the original 0–10 response format because anchored 11-point scales are commonly used in large-scale social surveys to capture generalized trust and institutional confidence. However, we do not treat this item as a composite or multidimensional measure. Existing reviews of trust-in-physician measurement indicate that most psychometrically evaluated instruments in this field are multi-item scales, and prior validation evidence for single-item measures of public distrust in physicians remains limited [42, 43].

This 11-point Likert scale has been widely adopted in major social surveys, including the European Social Survey, the Swedish National Election Survey, and the Longitudinal Internet Studies for the Social Sciences [44]. Compared to shorter Likert scales (4-, 5-, or 6-point measures), the 11-point scale offers greater sensitivity in capturing nuanced variations in trust and reduces response skewness, thereby enhancing measurement reliability [44]. Given these psychometric advantages, the study retained the original scale in our analysis to preserve granularity in public trust assessment.

The relationship between healthcare utilization rate and distrust might exhibit a threshold effect (such as a decrease in utilization only when distrust is below a certain critical value). Grouping distrust variables can help identify such patterns and facilitate subsequent testing of the robustness of the results. (To ensure the robustness of the core results, this study primarily analyzes trust values as continuous variables. The grouping approach is only used to present result trends across different levels and does not affect the validity of the core finding.) To measure any threshold effect between healthcare utilization and public trust, variables measuring distrust were further categorized into three tiers. The study conducted additional analyses by categorizing distrust scores into three tiers: low distrust (0–3 points), medium distrust (4–7 points), and high distrust (8–10 points).

Healthcare utilization

The dependent variable in this study comprised two distinct components: inpatient care utilization and outpatient care utilization. Outpatient care utilization was set as a dummy variable that coded as 1 if the individual self-reported an outpatient visit in the previous 2 weeks before the date of the interview and 0 otherwise. The CFPS question that supporting this variable is as follows: “Did you visit a doctor in the past two weeks?” In the CFPS questionnaire, inpatient care utilization was assessed through the dichotomous question: “Have you been hospitalized in the past year?” Affirmative responses to the question were coded as 1, while respondents saying “no” were coded as 0.

This two-tiered measurement approach allows for a clear differentiation between healthcare service types while minimizing potential overlap in utilization patterns. The binary operationalization of inpatient care utilization and conditional assessment of outpatient visits follow established practices in health services research, enhancing comparability with prior studies on healthcare access and utilization.

Covariates

Consistent with prior research examining the determinants of public distrust in physicians [45–47], the multiple regression analysis incorporated an extensive set of control variables to ensure robust estimation. The following variables were used to explain the covariates: Household income was divided into four equal parts (low, lower-middle, upper-middle, or high income), China’s Body Mass Index (BMI) classification standard (for adults) was: Underweight (<18.5), Normal (18.5–23.9), Overweight (24.0–27.9), and Obese (≥28.0). It was based on the Guidelines for the Prevention and Control of Overweight and Obesity in Chinese Adults [48], which is stricter than the WHO international standards.

In the CFPS, individuals were also asked to report on their perceived health status by posing the question, ‘Rate your health status today. Choose your health status from the following health status categories: excellent, very good, good, fair or poor.’ The study defined three health status categories for our empirical analysis: poor, fair and good. Additionally, year fixed effects were included to control for temporal variations. Complete variable definitions and coding schemes are presented in Table 1.

Multiple imputation of missing values

Table 2 presented the measured variables and their respective proportions of missing values. The present study adopted the missing-at-random (MAR) assumption [49, 50] and employed multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) to handle missing data [51–54]. MICE offered greater flexibility by employing a fully conditional specification approach, which iteratively imputed missing values using univariate models tailored to each variable’s distribution [49]. For this study, MICE was selected due to its ability to handle diverse data types: logistic regression for binary variables (rural residency), Poisson regression for count variables (chronic conditions), ordered logistic regression for ordinal variables (education level, self-rated health), and multinomial logistic regression for nominal variables (smoking status). Following methodological best practices [50]. The analysis proceeded through three phases: (1) generating 50 complete datasets to ensure estimation efficiency per Rubin’s rules, (2) applying the desired statistical models to each imputed dataset, and (3) pooling results across datasets using Rubin’s variance rules to account for between- and within-imputation variability. This approach ensured robust handling of missing data while preserving statistical power and minimizing bias, particularly when the missing-at-random (MAR) assumption holds.

Statistical analysis

A two-way fixed-effects logistic model was used in this study. The empirical model was based on a latent regression and is defined as follows:

is an unobserved latent variable linked to the observed ordinal response categories related to healthcare utilization (including both outpatient care utilization and inpatient care utilization) for individual at time measured as a binary indicator of service use. The key independent variable captured the public distrust in physicians, assessed through a Likert-scale rating, with the coefficient β quantifying the direction and magnitude of distrust’s effect on healthcare utilization. The model incorporated a comprehensive set of time-varying covariates , such as Age, Male, Household income, Family size, and Self-rated health status. To account for potential confounding factors and strengthen the causal interpretation of our estimates, the model specification incorporated several key elements: (1) individual fixed effects () that controlled for all time-invariant characteristics at the person-level, such as genetic predispositions and relatively stable health behaviors that might influence both distrust in physicians and healthcare utilization; (2) time fixed effects () that captured period-specific shocks and secular trends affecting all individuals simultaneously, including policy reforms or system-wide healthcare changes; and (3) an idiosyncratic error term () that represented unobserved time-varying factors.

This rigorous approach effectively eliminated bias from unmeasured time-invariant confounders while accounting for temporal variations, thereby isolating the net effect of distrust on healthcare utilization. The coefficient β represented the marginal effect of a one-unit increase public distrust in physicians on healthcare utilization, holding other variables constant. A positive β suggested higher distrust increases healthcare utilization (potentially indicating greater care-seeking behavior), while a negative β might reflect reduced utilization due to heightened distrust. To enhance the robustness of the findings, the study conducted stratified analyses by urban-rural residence. The results were reported as odds ratios (ORs) with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs), which are essential for assessing the precision of the estimates and the statistical significance of the findings. Statistical analyses were performed using STATA version 18.

Results

General characteristics

Sample sizes varied from 20,687 to 32,073 participants over the years. Figure 2 depicted the trend in public distrust in physicians from 2012 to 2022. The mean public distrust score declined modestly from 3.39 in 2012 to 3.05 in 2022. During the COVID-19 pandemic period in 2020, a temporary decline in the mean distrust score was observed, reaching 2.85.

Mean of the public distrust in physicians over six waves

When stratified by residence, the mean distrust scores in 2020 were 2.96 for urban residents and 2.73 for rural residents, with both groups showing an overall downward trend over time, and lower mean scores observed in 2020. Regarding healthcare utilization, outpatient service utilization was higher in rural areas, whereas urban inpatient service utilization increased from 9.43% in 2012 to 12.36% in 2018 before declining to 9.71% in 2022 (see Table 3).

Discussion

This study examined the association between public distrust in physicians and healthcare utilization among urban and rural residents in China from 2012 to 2022. By integrating physician distrust into Anderson’s Health Behavior Model, we addressed a critical gap in existing literature wherein doctor distrust has rarely been incorporated into theoretical frameworks, making it difficult to accurately estimate the marginal impact of distrust on healthcare utilization while controlling for key covariates. Results indicated that public distrust declined modestly overall and improved temporarily during the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020, though levels remained higher than some theoretical predictions based on health system reform trends [51]. And urban residents reporting consistently higher distrust than rural residents. This is consistent with existing studies [52]. Two-way fixed-effects analyses showed different patterns between rural and urban areas: higher distrust in physicians was associated with lower outpatient utilization in rural areas, whereas in urban areas it was associated with lower inpatient utilization.

During the study period, public distrust in physicians exhibited a gradual downward trend, indicating an overall improvement in physician-patient relationships. A transient decline in distrust occurred during the 2020 COVID-19 pandemic, potentially attributable to enhanced social recognition of medical professionals and short-term strengthened confidence in the healthcare system [53]. Compared with rural residents, urban residents have lower trust in physicians. The primary health care service system in rural China is superior to that in urban areas [54]. China has invested a lot of resources to strengthen the medical infrastructure in rural areas, so patients in rural areas are more likely to receive timely diagnosis and treatment of common diseases. Because physicians in rural areas are not as busy as physicians in urban areas, they can spend more time with patients, so as to improve the quality of physician-patient interaction [55]. The observed urban-rural differences in public distrust in physicians may have implications for the development of context-specific strategies to strengthen physician-patient trust and may help inform policy discussions on resource allocation and healthcare equity.

Two-way fixed-effects logistic regression analysis showed different patterns between rural and urban residents in the association between physician distrust and healthcare utilization. For outpatient service utilization, higher distrust was associated with lower odds of utilization among rural residents, whereas no statistically significant association was observed among urban residents. This results likely reflects differential care options: rural residents face limited channels dominated by primary outpatient care [56], where trust directly determines utilization; urban residents, despite abundant outpatient resources, may avoid non-urgent visits or frequently switch providers due to distrust (suspecting over-treatment or over-prescription), resulting in suppressed utilization [57].

Contrary to outpatient services, the negative correlation between distrust in physicians and inpatient utilization was only significant among urban residents, with no significant association among rural residents. Urban residents have sufficient hospitalization information and diverse choices [58], and distrust in physicians can lead to delayed or refused hospitalization; moreover, urban residents pay more attention to medical experience, and trust in physicians directly affects hospitalization decisions [59]. In the absence of trust in physicians, individuals may increasingly rely on preventive healthcare measures (physical examinations, health management apps) to reduce inpatient needs. The urban-rural results identified by two-way fixed-effects analyses has important practical significance. It provides empirical support for targeted interventions, guiding healthcare practitioners to focus on trust-building in rural outpatient services and urban inpatient services to mitigate the negative impact of distrust on service utilization. For policymakers, it offers insights to optimize healthcare resource allocation tailored to the urban-rural differences, thereby promoting the rational and equitable use of healthcare services.

Limitations

This study has several methodological limitations. Firstly, reliance on self-reported data regarding distrust and healthcare utilization may introduce biases, including recall inaccuracies and social desirability effects, particularly in contexts where openly criticizing physicians is culturally sensitive. Secondly, although the longitudinal design and fixed-effects models control for unobserved time-invariant confounders, residual confounding from unmeasured time-varying factors (localized healthcare policy shifts or media-driven perceptions of physician-patient tensions) remains, cautioning against definitive causal claims. Thirdly, despite employing multiple imputation to address missing data, selective attrition (disproportionate dropout of healthier participants) could skew estimates, even though the unbalanced panel framework partially mitigates this issue. Fourth, posterior analysis indicated that the wide confidence intervals obtained for the region variable primarily stem from the insufficient number of events for outpatient care utilization in the rural areas from certain regions, leading to sparse data bias in the regression model. Therefore, the effect of regional differences on outpatient service utilization should be regarded as an exploratory finding rather than a definitive conclusion. Lastly, public distrust in physicians was measured using a single-item indicator rather than a multi-item psychometrically validated scale. Although this approach may capture respondents’ overall perception, it does not allow dimensional differentiation, and prior validation evidence for this exact measure remains limited. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution.

Conclusions

This study explored the relationship between public distrust of physicians and health service utilization among urban and rural residents in China from 2012 to 2022. The results show that the distrust level of urban residents is higher than that of rural residents. Public distrust in physicians is negatively correlated with the utilization of outpatient services of rural residents, and negatively correlated with the utilization of hospitalization of urban residents. Due to the tense physician-patient relationship and the increasing number of conflicts, improving trust in physicians is an important policy issue facing China. This paper analyzes some reasons, some of which can be solved through policy adjustment.

The trust of rural populations in doctors is much higher than that of urban residents. This may mean that the patient-centered primary health care service structure investment will help to improve the trust between patients and doctors. In rural areas, doctor care in China is more integrated than that in urban areas, and the continuity of care is relatively high. China may consider establishing a primary health care service structure to strengthen the relationship between patients and doctors. Doctor-patient communication is an important aspect of enhancing trust in medical service providers. Doctors’ training should include how to respect patients and how to show sensitivity to patients’ needs in busy doctor practice. In addition, according to the heterogeneity between urban and rural areas, we should focus on strengthening the construction of doctor-patient trust in rural grass-roots clinics, improve the quality of urban inpatient service, promote the balanced allocation of medical resources, narrow the trust gap between urban and rural doctors and patients, and promote the fair and rational use of medical services.
