The efficacy and safety of per-nasal “GTS partner” assisted traction technique for gastric endoscopic submucosal dissection: a prospective single-center randomized clinical trial
Authors: Yu-Zhen Bi, Li-Min Zhou, Si-Jia Yan, Yan Sun, Jun Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSince the snare traction-assisted ESD has been proven effective in treating flat lesions of the digestive tract, we modified and innovated the process and path of the traditional snare entering the digestive tract, aiming to investigate the efficacy and safety of using the per-nasal “GTS partner” assisted traction technology in gastric ESD.MethodsPatients with superficial gastric neoplasms were prospectively enrolled between November 2022 and May 2024 and randomly assigned to a conventional ESD (C-ESD) group or per-nasal “GTS partner” traction-assisted ESD (GTS-ESD) group. The primary outcomes were procedure time and dissection speed.ResultsThe GTS-ESD and C-ESD groups included 40 patients each, and all the enrolled patients underwent the assigned treatment. The median procedure time in the GTS-ESD group was shorter than that in the C-ESD group (38 min vs. 48 min; P < 0.001), and the mean resection speed of the GTS-ESD group was faster than that of the C-ESD group (17.95 mm2/min vs. 11.86 mm2/min; P = 0.033). The median resection speed of lesions ≥ 20 mm was faster by GTS-ESD than by C-ESD (21.21 mm2/min vs. 12.83 mm2/min, P = 0.002). The en bloc resection rate (100% vs 100%) and R0 resection rate (100% vs. 97.5%) were similar between the two groups. There were no adverse events related to the per-nasal “GTS partner” assisted traction technology, and the traction technology had little interference with the endoscopist.ConclusionsThe per-nasal “GTS partner” assisted traction technique can significantly shorten the gastric ESD procedure time and has the advantages of no damage to normal mucosa and adjustable traction direction, especially in the lower 1/3 of the stomach or lesions with a diameter of ≥ 20 mm.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00464-024-11347-z.Keywords: Snare, Traction, Partner, Endoscopic submucosal dissection

Endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) is characterized by minimal trauma, good efficacy, and high technical requirements. It is widely accepted as a minimally invasive treatment for patients with superficial gastrointestinal neoplasms [1]. According to long-term clinical observation, ESD can achieve the therapeutic effect of surgery, complete dissection of lesions, and accurate pathological evaluation [2, 3]. Due to the characteristics of the endoscopic cavity, the endoscopist’s operating space is narrow, resulting in technical difficulties, time-consuming procedures, and adverse events such as bleeding and perforation [4]. The key to solving these technique difficulties is to achieve accurate dissection by maintaining adequate visualization of the submucosal dissection plane and tissue tension during the ESD procedure.

The snare traction-assisted technology can achieve multi-direction traction by pushing or pulling the snare, and the traction force can be flexibly adjusted, thus overcoming the problems of single traction direction and uncontrollable traction force of other traction technologies. It can also be loosened if necessary, and the other part of the lesion can be grabbed and pulled again, which has a high application value in gastric ESD [5, 6].

At present, the snare traction method consists of attaching the snare to the front end of the transparent cap of the endoscope and entering the gastric cavity along with the endoscope. However, conventional snare traction is generally entered through the mouth, and the head end of the snare is hard and rough, making the mucosa prone to damage when entering the digestive duct [7]. In order to solve the problem where the conventional snare cannot be sent into the vicinity of the lesion under the direct vision of the endoscope and reduce the damage to the gastrointestinal mucosa during the process of the snare entering the gastric cavity, we improved and innovated the process and path of the snare entering the gastric cavity. The head end of the snare was inserted into the side hole of the gastric tube and then into the stomach cavity through the nose under the guidance of the gastric tube, thus avoiding mucosal damage during the direct insertion of the snare. Also, the insertion of the traction device through the nasal cavity had little interference with the operation of the endoscopist.

The present study investigated the efficacy and safety of the per-nasal “GTS partner” assisted traction technique in gastric ESD. In this technique, GT refers to Gastric Tube, and S refers to Snare; therefore, it was named the per-nasal “GTS partner” assisted traction technology.

Materials and methods

Study design

This prospective, single-center randomized controlled trial was designed to compare the procedure-related outcomes between GTS-ESD and C-ESD when treating superficial gastric neoplasms. The trial was approved by the Ethics Committee of Zhejiang Provincial People’s Hospital, Chun’an branch (Ethics Approval Number: 2022-03-10-05) and registered with the National Medical Research Registration Information System (www.yxyj.org.cn; identification No.:MR-33-24-020319), conducted following the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent for this study before enrollment.

Patients

Consecutive patients who underwent ESD for superficial gastric neoplasms between November 2022 and May 2024 were enrolled in this study. The gastric ESD procedures were performed by a single endoscopist whose experience amounted to 100 procedures per year. The eligibility criteria were as follows: (1) age 20 to 90 years old; (2) normal anatomy of the upper digestive tract; (3) endoscopic diagnosis of precancerous lesion, gastric adenomas with high risk of canceration; clinical diagnosis of intramucosal cancer (cT1a), according to the Japanese guidelines for the diagnosis and treatment of gastric cancer [8]; (4) single lesion and diameter ≥ 1 cm; (5) provided informed consent. The exclusion criteria were: (1) lesion near a previous endoscopic resection scar; (2) history of surgery of the upper digestive tract; (3) patients with nasopharyngeal tumors, stenosis, surgical history, and other gastric tube insertion difficulties; (4) severe heart, liver and kidney dysfunction; (5) severe mental disorder; (6) pregnancy or breastfeeding; (7) anesthesia intolerance; (8) anticoagulants or antiplatelet drugs were not discontinued.

Randomization

Patients were randomly assigned to the conventional ESD (C-ESD) or per-nasal “GTS partner” traction-assisted ESD (GTS-ESD) group in a 1:1 ratio with the computer-based random number table method. The random sequence was stored in assignment tables, following allocation concealment before grouping. Patients were blinded to treatment group assignment, while the endoscopist and investigator were not blinded.

Sample size

The sample size was calculated based on ESD procedure time. In our preliminary pilot study, the mean procedure time was 42.7 min in the GTS-ESD group and 70.3 min in the C-ESD group, with a common standard deviation of 26.2 min for both groups. In order to ensure a power of 90% with a two-sided error rate of 5%, 40 participants needed to be enrolled. Considering a dropout rate of approximately 20% and other unpredictable factors, the final sample was to include 80 participants.

Endoscopic procedure

All patients completed ESD preoperative evaluation, and all patients were fasted for at least 6 h, along with oral administration of defoaming agent (dimethicone) and mucosal dissolving agent (pronase Granules) 20 min prior to ESD. The following operating equipments and materials were used in the study: endoscopy (GIF-Q260J, Olympus) Dual knife (MK-T-2-195, Micro-Tech), injection needles (ATE-ZSZ-C-23 × 2000 × 25 × 4, Atete), hot biopsy forceps (HBF-23-2000, Micro-Tech), clip(ROCC-D-26-195-C, Micro-Tech), snare (SD-T-2423–35, Kangjin), gastric tube (5.3 mm (Fr16), Jiangsu), high-frequency generator (ERBEVIO 200D), carbon dioxide insufflation (Olympus), and submucosal injection was prepared by mixing normal saline 100 ml, methylene blue 0.1 ml, sodium hyaluronate 8 ml, and epinephrine 1 ml.

ESD procedure

All patients underwent procedures under monitored anesthesia care with endotracheal intubation. The equipments, materials, and other conditions used in the procedure were identical in both groups except for the traction method in the dissection phase (Fig. 1).

Study flowchart

Mucosal marking was performed approximately 5 mm outside the margin of the lesion with a Dual knife. Next, submucosal injection was injected into the submucosal layer until the lesions were fully elevated, and the mucosal and submucosal layer surrounding the lesion was dissected along the marked points using a Dual knife. The traction method, underwater technique, or any other special technique [9] was not used in the C-ESD procedure group. The vision of operation was maintained only through the transparent cap during dissection. For the Per-nasal “GTS partner” assisted traction group (Fig. 3B), after a circumferential mucosal incision of the lesion, the assistant inserted the snare into the side hole of the gastric tube and near the lesion through one side nasal cavity (Figs. 2A, B and 3A). The snare was withdrawn from the side hole of the gastric tube, the gastric tube was pulled out, and the snare was opened, after which a clip was inserted into the gastroscopic biopsy hole. The clip grabbed the snare to trap the mucosal edge of the rimb of lesion, and the snare was tightened (Fig. 2G, Video 1). The endoscopist pulled the external snare cannula, which could be pushed or pulled to change the traction direction, and the surgical field was exposed after traction; the tissue tension was maintained during the dissection procedure (Fig. 2F–I). If the first traction did not provide satisfactory visualization of the submucosal layer, the snare was released to select another suitable site for traction. After dissection, hemostatic forceps were used to coagulate the exposed small blood vessels in the wound to prevent postoperative bleeding, and the clip was administered at the suspected injury or defect of the muscle layer. Finally, the gastric tube was indwelled for decompression and drainage. Any traction-related tear was considered traction-related damage to the specimen (Fig. 2).

According to the lesion location, size, and intraoperative conditions, the patients fasted for 24–48 h after ESD. Proton pump inhibitors, gastric mucosal protective agents, and nutritional support were routinely used, and prophylactic antibiotics were given if there was intraoperative injury of muscularis propria. The patients were monitored for bleeding, perforation, fever, abdominal pain, and other adverse events through observation of clinical symptoms and laboratory examinations. For patients with early gastric cancer, it was determined whether they needed to undergo additional surgery, review gastroscopy, and abdominal CT according to the Japanese “eCura system” [10].

Outcomes

The general data of ESD perioperative period were observed and compared between the two groups, including age, sex, lesion location, lesion position, lesion diameter, macroscopic type, status of Helicobacter pylori infection, procedure time, resection speed, en bloc resection rate, R0 resection rate, specimen size, delayed bleeding, perforation, other adverse events, histological evaluation, and factors associated with per-nasal “GTS partner” assisted traction (snare disengagement, snare damage to specimen). The main endpoints were ESD procedure time and dissection speed. Secondary endpoints were en bloc resection rate, R0 resection rate, and procedure adverse events rate. Subgroup analysis of gastric ESD procedure time and dissection speed was performed according to lesion location and lesion diameter.

The lesion location (i.e., the upper, middle, or lower) and position (i.e., greater curvature, lesser curvature, anterior wall, posterior wall) were defined according to the Japanese classification of gastric cancer [11]. The procedure time (minutes) was defined as the time from the first injection to the end of tumor dissection. The specimen area was calculated using the ellipse formula: specimen area [mm2] = (transverse diameter/2) × (longitudinal diameter/2) × 3.14 (π). The dissection speed was defined as the specimen area divided by the procedure time. En bloc resection was defined as endoscopic resection of an entire lesion in a single specimen without the use of snare. R0 resection was defined as endoscopic resection of an entire lesion in a single specimen with no histopathological evidence of tumor residue at the resection margins. Cases in which the lesion’s margins were unclear because of electrosurgical or mechanical damage were classified as non-R0 resections. Perforation was diagnosed if intra-abdominal organs or fat tissues were observed during ESD procedure or free air was found on post-procedure radiographs or computed tomography images. Delayed bleeding was defined as clinical symptoms and laboratory changes indicative of bleeding after ESD [12, 13].

Statistical analysis

SPSS 26.0 software was used to perform all statistical analyses. Continuous data with normal distribution are presented as means and standard deviations (SD) and analyzed using Welch’s t-test. Variables with non-normal distribution are presented as medians and interquartile ranges (IQR) and analyzed using the Wilcoxon rank sum test. Categorical data are described using percentages and analyzed using the chi-squared or Fischer’s exact test. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 80 consecutive patients who met the eligibility criteria were enrolled in the study between November 2022 and May 2024. Patients were randomly assigned to the GTS-ESD group (n = 40) or the C-ESD group (n = 40). The flowchart of patient enrollment is shown in Fig. 1. The baseline characteristics of gender, age, lesion location, lesion position, and lesion diameter were similar between the two groups, and the differences were not statistically significant (all P > 0.05) (Table 1). All endoscopic procedures were performed successfully, and none were converted to surgery.

The median procedure time was shorter in the GTS-ESD group (38.00 min; IQR 27.25–48.50 min) than in the C-ESD group (48.00 min; IQR 43.00–70.75 min) (P < 0.001). The mean dissection speed was significantly faster in GTS-ESD (17.95 ± 5.03 mm2/min) than in C-ESD (11.86 ± 3.95 mm2/min) (P = 0.033). One case of delayed bleeding occurred in the C-ESD group on the third day after ESD, which was successfully managed by endoscopic intervention. There were no perforations in any of the groups. The En bloc resection rate of the lesions in both groups was 100%. In the C-ESD group, a few low-grade atypic cells were found at the horizontal incisal margin in one patient. As the final pathology of this lesion was highly differentiated tubular adenocarcinoma, endoscopic follow-up was recommended every 6 months. In both groups, pathological evaluation after ESD revealed one lesion with infiltrated submucosa; however, all lesions were located in SM1. One specimen injury was due to traction, but this did not affect the final pathological evaluation. No per-nasal “GTS partner” assisted traction technique-related nasal and digestive tract mucosal injuries were found (Table 2).

Subgroup analysis

The results of the subgroup analysis of gastric ESD procedure time and dissection speed by lesion location and size are shown in Tables 3 and 4. Lesions in the lower third of the stomach had a significantly shorter median ESD procedure time in the GTS-ESD group than in the C-ESD group (40 min vs. 46 min P = 0.0028). The mean dissection speed of different parts in the GTS-ESD group was faster than in the C-ESD group; however, considering the small sample size, the difference was not statistically significant. The dissection speed of the lesion ≥ 20 mm in diameter was significantly faster in the GTS-ESD group than in the C-ESD group. The median dissection speed was increased by 39.5% (21.21 mm2/min vs 12.83 mm2/min, P = 0.002).

Discussion

Achieving good submucosal visualization and tissue tension is paramount to performing a safe and efficient ESD. Under poor visualization conditions, the dissection procedure of the submucosal layer is challenging, and the procedure time is extended. Moreover, maintaining the procedure under these poor conditions increases the risk of adverse events. In order to dissect safely and efficiently, various ESD-assisted traction technologies have emerged [3, 9]. According to the source of traction, traction methods can be divided into in vivo traction and in vitro traction. According to the composition of traction devices, it can be divided into clip-line traction [14], clip-snare traction [15], spring-and-loop with clip traction [16], external grasping forceps [17], double endoscope assisted traction technology [18], robotic arm-assisted traction [19], etc. However, many traction methods have the disadvantages of being invasive, complicated operations that can greatly interfere with ESD, which is why they have not been widely used in clinical practice.

Currently, the clip-line traction technique is one of the most widely used methods that has been applied in esophageal, stomach, duodenum, and colon ESD procedures [20]. The main disadvantage is that the traction direction is fixed, and it is difficult to apply force to the contralateral (anal) direction of the lesion. In recent years, snare traction assistance technology has gradually been applied in endoscopic therapy because of certain advantages, such as low price, simple equipment, and easy operation [21, 22]. The snare traction assistance technology can achieve traction in multiple directions by pushing or pulling the snare, and the traction force can be flexibly adjusted, thus overcoming the problems of single traction direction and uncontrollable traction force of other traction technologies. In addition, in the process of snare traction-assisted resection, due to its good support and hardness, the strength of traction can be maintained, thus allowing the endoscopist to complete the dissection and traction independently, without the need for additional traction assistants, achieving a single person operation. Currently, the reported methods of external traction of the snare enter the stomach cavity through the mouth. After completing the circumferential cutting and trimming of the submucosa layer, the endoscope is withdrawn, and the snare is tightened on the front end of the transparent cap, after which the snare is brought into the gastric cavity by a gastroscope. Finally, the snare is tightened to the edge of the lesion with the help of a metallic clip [15].

Due to the hardness of the snare and the sharp head of most domestic snares, the risk of pharynx and esophagus injury increases when the snare is sent into the gastric cavity without direct vision [23]. In addition, if the snare is placed on the improper part of the transparent cap, it may increase the difficulty of release. The snare and endoscope are in the same channel, they may interfere with each other to some extent [15]. Baldaque-Silva et al. [24] reported on the “yo-yo technique”, in which soft transparent tape was wrapped around the tip of the snare to prevent damage to the digestive tract mucosa; however, this is not optimal for the release of the snare. In the pre-experimental study, we used three snares of different diameters (FG-D-18, Kangjin; SD-T-2423-35, kangjin and MTN-PFS-E-24/23, Micro-Tech) and found that thick snare is more suitable for the traction method. Therefore, we chose the SD-T-2423-35 snare with a maximum external diameter of 2.4 mm as the traction device in this study. Most gastric tubes used in China are made of silica gel with a soft texture, circular blind head end, and lateral holes in the front segment. The head end of the snare was inserted into the gastric tube’s lateral hole and brought into the gastric cavity through the gastric tube. This helps avoid mucosal damage and release difficulties during the direct insertion of the snare through the nose, which is safer and more effective. In addition, since gastrointestinal decompression was routinely used after ESD in China to achieve decompression, drainage, and promote wound healing, etc., the gastric tubes withdrawn after the snare is delivered to the destination continue to be used after ESD to avoid waste of consumables.

In this study, the median procedure time in the GTS-ESD group was 38.00 min, which was similar to the previously reported time of 38.5 min for per-oral snare-assisted ESD [15]. Indeed, in a study of snare tract-assisted ESD [15], some extent of interference between the endoscope and snare during transoral traction may have occurred despite using a thin snare with a maximum outer diameter of 1.8 mm. When the endoscope is manipulated roughly, the snare may detach from the lesion along with the clip. By contrast, the endoscopist in the present study believed that the per-nasal snare-assisted traction operation was more convenient, as the snare and endoscope do not enter the stomach cavity through the same channel, and there is little interference between endoscope and traction equipment, which facilitates the independent movement of the endoscope and the snare and improves the accuracy of ESD. Because the snare is guided by the gastric tube through the nasal cavity, all patients had no nasal discomfort, nasal cavity or oropharyngeal mucosa injury after awakening. In this study, the premature separation of the gastric tube and the snare occurred twice during the process of the snare entering the gastric cavity, due to the improper operation of the assistant. In order to reduce the occurrence of such incidents, we made a small hole in the front part of the gastric tube suitable for accompanying the snare to avoid the premature separation of the snare caused by improper operation. Considering it is challenging for the snare to grasp the lesion edge directly after mucosal circumferential incision, a clip was placed in advance at the appropriate lesion margin, and the snare could grasp the clip for traction (Fig. 2E). The process from preparation to grasping requires close cooperation between the endoscopists and their assistants. In terms of safety, the incidence of adverse events was not significantly different between the two groups. There was only one patient from the C-ESD group with delayed bleeding, which was successfully stopped after endoscopic treatment. There was one case of specimen injury due to snare traction caused by less gastric mucosa clamped by the snare and excessive traction force; however, this did not affect the final histopathological evaluation. The potential risks may arise from incorrect operation of the gastric tube, which could lead to local mucosal injury and bleeding. Before the procedure, it is essential to check whether the nasal cavity is unobstructed, and the insertion of the gastric tube should be performed gently. Nasopharyngeal injury is often mild and transient, and it will not lead to prolonged hospital stay.

Effective traction methods can reduce the difficulty of endoscopic operation, the procedure time, and the risk of adverse events to a certain extent. In terms of sedation/anesthesia, shortening the procedure time is also conducive to the safety of patients. Meanwhile, the efficacy and safety of ESD are also affected by the endoscopists’ experience and the size and location of the lesions. According to subgroup analysis, the median procedure time in the GTS-ESD group was significantly shorter than that in the C-ESD group (40 min vs 46 min P = 0.003). The mean dissection speed was also faster in the GTS-ESD group than in the C-ESD group (14.71 mm2/min vs 9.07 mm2/min, P = 0.062). These results are related to the fact that ESD in the lower third of the stomach is easier to manipulate than ESD in the upper and middle third of the stomach [25]. According to previous studies that evaluated the difficulty of gastric ESD techniques, tumor location in the upper or middle third of the stomach is an important factor affecting the time of the ESD procedure [26]. Although few lesions were located in the upper third of the stomach in the randomized patients included in this study, GTS-ESD showed an effective tendency in both dissection speed and procedure time. For lesions with a diameter ≥ 20 mm, the procedure time of GTS-ESD was reduced by 38.8%, and the dissection speed was increased by 37.1%; however, the number of cases was also small, so future studies are needed to further verify the credibility of the results by expanding the sample size.

The present study has several limitations. First, this was a single-center study with a small sample size, so the results need to be further verified by multicenter studies. Second, considering the sample size, we did not carry out a stratified random design according to the lesion location. Additionally, the lesions included in this study did not involve the fornix of the stomach, and the efficacy and safety of the per-nasal “GTS partner” assisted traction in this position cannot be fully proved. Third, the control group in this study was conventional ESD, so it was impossible to determine whether per-nasal “GTS partner” assisted traction technology is more useful than other traction methods. Fourth, there are also some pitfalls in per-nasal “GTS partner” assisted traction. Excessive traction force or clamping too little gastric mucosa will easily cause the titanium clip to fall off from the mucosa, consuming the operation time and increasing mucosal damage.

In conclusion, per-nasal “GTS partner” assisted traction seems to be a relatively safe and effective traction method for the treatment of gastric epithelial lesions, which can shorten the procedure time to a certain extent without increasing the occurrence of complications. Considering the results of the preliminary subgroup analysis, we recommend the per-nasal “GTS partner” assisted traction technique for lesions in the lower third of the stomach and ≥ 20 mm in diameter. If possible, in the future, per-nasal “GTS partner” assisted traction technique should be applied for endoscopic gastric submucosal tumor resection, esophageal ESD, or to create new assisted traction in the form of a partner method.
