Reply to Rohrer and Wenz and Arslan: The association between income and emotional well-being
Authors: Matthew A. Killingsworth, Daniel Kahneman, Barbara Mellers
Published: 2024

Killingsworth, Kahneman and Mellers (2023)—hereafter KKM (1)—was an adversarial collaboration that reconciled two conflicting findings about the association between Log(income) and emotional well-being (hereafter “happiness”). To summarize the original conflict, Kahneman and Deaton (2) found that happiness plateaued beyond $75,000/y (the midpoint of a $60 to 90K income band), whereas Killingsworth (3) found that the slope above $75,000/y was just as steep as the slope below. We labeled the pattern reported by Kahneman and Deaton the “flattening pattern.”

KKM assumed that both studies were valid, but recognized that happiness was measured differently. Our critical insight was that Kahneman and Deaton’s measure of happiness was only sensitive to variation at the lower end of the happiness distribution due to the fact that, in the critical region of higher incomes, most responses (~85%) were at the scale maximum. Therefore, variation in responses could only reflect differences at the lower end of the happiness distribution (the bottom 15%, approximately). This insight meant that the studies were not necessarily incompatible. The apparent conflict could be resolved if there was a flattening pattern in Killingsworth’s data at the lower (“unhappy”) end of the happiness distribution.

We examined Kahneman and Deaton’s original threshold beyond which further increases in income were no longer associated with a significant rise in happiness and realized that the upper bound of that income category ($90K) was a more accurate estimate than its midpoint ($75K). Adjusting for inflation, the predicted threshold beyond which a flattened pattern should be detectable in Killingsworth’s data was $100K. We then used quantile regression to compute slopes above and below $100K for different quantiles, as shown in Fig. 1. Rohrer and Wenz (4) discuss conditions under which causality could be inferred from quantile regression. While causality is undoubtedly interesting, it was not the point of our paper. Our goal was to reconcile conflicting associations—not causal relationships—between happiness and income.

We speculated about the reasons for the different patterns we observed, but as our paper acknowledges, the evidence was strictly correlational, as was also true for both studies we sought to reconcile. In the handful of studies that can draw causal inferences between income and happiness, evidence suggests that having more money does, on average, lead to greater happiness (5 –8).

Rohrer and Wenz also question the feasibility of precisely identifying an “unhappy group.” Precise identification is not required for our claim. We were looking for Kahneman and Deaton’s flattening pattern in Killingsworth’s data and found evidence of it in the lowest quantiles of the happiness distribution. We never claimed that this group was precisely invariant. Indeed, it is impossible to know where in the conditional distribution specific people would fall if their incomes varied. Furthermore, there is no guarantee that an average trend in happiness applies to any particular person. We would, however, guess that many people at the “unhappy” end of the conditional happiness distribution would probably remain at the “unhappy” if their income changed. To expect otherwise would seem to require individualized income effects that are implausibly large, given the modest relationship between income and happiness.

Arslan questions the evidence for a flattening pattern and argues that a purely linear pattern is just as plausible (9). To us, this seems unlikely. The strongest evidence against linearity is visible in the overall variation of slopes shown in Fig. 1. There are major interactions underlying this variation, above and below $100K. Below $100K, the lower the decile (calculated within each income category), the steeper the slope (P < 0.00001). Above $100K, the lower the decile, the flatter the slope (P < 0.00001). A purely linear model cannot accommodate this variation, and the accelerating slope at the high end of the happiness distribution is the opposite of what a ceiling effect would produce.

This overall pattern of slope variation entails a flattening pattern on the low end of the happiness distribution above $100K. As we reported in KKM, the flattening above versus below $100K in the quantile regression for the 15th percentile is statistically significant, as is the acceleration of the 85th percentile. This does not prove that Kahneman and Deaton’s pattern is correct, but it does show that it cannot be rejected at the low end of the happiness distribution.

The goal of KKM was straightforward. We sought to understand whether a single underlying reality could explain the results of both Kahneman and Deaton and Killingsworth. After recognizing the ceiling effect in Kahneman and Deaton, it was clear that the results were not necessarily incompatible. As it turned out, there was heterogeneity in the association between income and happiness in different regions of the happiness distribution. At the lowest level of the happiness distribution, happiness first rises steeply but then exhibits little improvement beyond $100K (an income threshold not derived from Killingsworth but predicted from the point in Kahneman and Deaton beyond which happiness no longer significantly increased). A steady, approximately linear trend occurs in the middle of the happiness distribution, and an accelerating trend appears at the higher end of the distribution. These subtle but systematic variations demonstrate the complexity of the relationships between happiness and income and offer an explanation for why the average trends reported in the two original studies diverged.
