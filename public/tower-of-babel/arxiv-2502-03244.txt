Analysis of Value Iteration Through Absolute Probability Sequences
Arsenii Mustafin *, Sebastien Colla *, Alex Olshevsky, Ioannis Ch. Paschalidis

Abstract
Value Iteration is a widely used algorithm for solving Markov Decision Processes (MDPs). While previous studies have extensively analyzed its convergence properties, they primarily focus on convergence with respect to the infinity norm. In this work, we use absolute probability sequences to develop a new line of analysis and examine the algorithm’s convergence in terms of the L 2 L^{2} norm, offering a new perspective on its behavior and performance.



1
Introduction

Markov Decision Processes (MDPs) are a foundational mathematical framework for sequential decision-making and have become a cornerstone of modern AI. They can be viewed as an extension of standard Markov Processes (MPs) designed to model sequential decision-making. MDPs were first introduced in the late 1950s, along with a key algorithm for solving them—Value Iteration
(
Bellman, 1957
)
. The algorithm’s convergence in terms of the infinity norm, at a rate determined by the discount factor
\gamma
(introduced below), was established in
(
Howard, 1960
)
, where it was also shown that this convergence rate is achievable. Subsequent analyses have focused on particular cases
(
Puterman, 1990
;
Feinberg and Huang, 2014
)
.

In this work, we continue this line of research by analyzing the convergence of the Value Iteration algorithm. The key novelty of our approach lies in the analytical framework: we use absolute probability sequences to establish the convergence of Value Iteration in terms of the L² norm. Additionally, we characterize the convergence rate in the case where a unique optimal policy induces a strongly connected graph.

2
Mathematical setting

We follow the general discounted infinite-horizon MDP setting from
Puterman (2014)
and
Sutton and Barto (2018)
. We consider an MDP with a finite set of states, denoted as
\mathcal{S}
, with
|\mathcal{S}|=n
, and a finite set of actions, denoted as
\mathcal{A}
, with
|\mathcal{A}|=m
. We assume that each action in this set is available in every state. The expected reward for taking action
a
in state
s
is denoted as
r(s,a)
, and the transition probability distribution over the state set is given by
P(\cdot|s,a)
. The discount factor of the MDP is denoted as
\gamma\in(0,1)
.

A policy
\pi
defines a choice rule for selecting action
a_{s}
in each state
s
, written as
\pi(s)=a_{s}
. If a policy selects a single action with probability 1, it is called a deterministic policy. In this paper, we consider only deterministic policies. We say that a policy
\pi
is implied by a values
V
if

\pi(s)=\argmax_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\left(r(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s^{\prime}}P(s^{\prime}|s,a)V(s^{\prime})\right).

Given a policy
\pi
, we define its value in state
s
, denoted as
V^{\pi}(s)
, to be the expected sum of the discounted rewards over an infinite trajectory starting from state
s
:

V^{\pi}(s)=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}r_{t}\right],

where
r_{t}
is the reward received at time step
t
, starting from state
s
, and actions in each state are chosen according to policy
\pi
.

We are interested in finding the optimal policy
\pi^{*}
such that:

V^{\pi^{*}}(s)\geq V^{\pi}(s),\quad\forall\pi,s.

The value vector produced by
\pi^{*}
is denoted as
V^{*}
and satisfies:

V^{*}(s)=r(s,\pi^{*}(s))+\gamma\sum_{s^{\prime}}P(s^{\prime}|s,\pi^{*}(s))V^{*}(s^{\prime}).

The Value Iteration algorithm solves this problem by computing a sequence
\{V_{t}\}
with an arbitrarily chosen initial value
V_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The sequence is generated by the update:

V_{t+1}=(1-\alpha)V_{t}+\alpha\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\left(r(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s^{\prime}}P(s^{\prime}|s,a)V_{t}(s^{\prime})\right),

(1)

where
\alpha\in(0,1)
is a chosen learning rate. Here and throughout, the subscript
\square_{t}
denotes quantities obtained after
t
updates using (
1
). In this case. vector or matrix elements, indices are given in square brackets (
[i]
or
[i,j]
); otherwise when timestep is not important, they are written as subscripts (
\square_{i}
or
\square_{i,j}
).

It was shown in
Howard (1960)
that the update (
1
) is a contraction, with
V^{*}
as its unique stationary point, implying that the sequence
\{V_{t}\}
converges to
V^{*}
in terms of the infinity norm.

In this paper, we propose a different proof technique and show that this sequence converges to
V^{*}
in terms of the weighted
L^{2}
norm. To avoid the worst-case scenario, we make the following key assumption:

Assumption 2.1
.

The unique optimal policy
\pi^{*}
induces an irreducible and aperiodic Markov process.

3
Our Approach

Our approach focuses on the analysis of error vectors:

e_{t}=V_{t}-V^{*}.

The first simple observation we make is that any consensus vector
e_{t}
,
i.e.
,
e_{t}=c\mathbf{1}
, implies the optimal policy. This allows us to focus on analyzing the convergence of
e_{t}
to consensus, which does not necessarily imply convergence to zero.

The update (
1
) can be written in vector form as:

V_{t+1}=(1-\alpha)V_{t}+\alpha(r_{t}+\gamma P_{t}V_{t}),

where the choices made by the maximization operator are encoded in the reward vector
r_{t}
and the transition matrix
P_{t}
. We denote the rewards and transitions associated with the optimal value vector
V^{*}
as
r^{*}
and
P^{*}
. Using the maximization property of these choices, we derive the following bounds on
e_{t}
.

Upper bound:

\displaystyle e_{t+1}

\displaystyle=V_{t+1}-V^{*}=(1-\alpha)e_{t}+\alpha\left[r_{t}+\gamma P_{t}V_{t}-r^{*}-\gamma P^{*}V^{*}\right]

\displaystyle\leq(1-\alpha)e_{t}+\alpha\left[r_{t}+\gamma P_{t}V_{t}-r_{t}-\gamma P_{t}V^{*}\right]=\left[(1-\alpha)I+\alpha\gamma P_{t}\right]e_{t}.

(2)

Lower bound:

\displaystyle e_{t+1}

\displaystyle=V_{t+1}-V^{*}=(1-\alpha)e_{t}+\alpha\left[r_{t}+\gamma P_{t}V_{t}-r^{*}-\gamma P^{*}V^{*}\right]

\displaystyle\geq(1-\alpha)e_{t}+\alpha\left[r^{*}+\gamma P^{*}V^{*}-r^{*}-\gamma P^{*}V^{*}\right]=\left[(1-\alpha)I+\alpha\gamma P^{*}\right]e_{t}.

(3)

We normalize the expression in (
2
) to obtain a stochastic matrix:

\left[(1-\alpha)I+\gamma P_{t}\right]=[(1-\alpha)+\alpha\gamma]P_{t\alpha}=\gamma_{\alpha}P_{t\alpha},

where
P_{t\alpha}
is a stochastic matrix with diagonal entries of at least
(1-\alpha)/((1-\alpha)+\alpha\gamma)>(1-\alpha)
.

Normalizing the matrix in the lower bound (
2
) in the same way and combining both bounds, we obtain:

\gamma_{\alpha}P^{*}_{\alpha}e_{t}\leq e_{t+1}\leq\gamma_{\alpha}P_{t\alpha}e_{t}.

(4)

This inequality implies that each element of
e_{t+1}
can be represented as a convex combination of the left- and right-hand side expressions. By introducing a diagonal matrix
D_{t}
to combine these expressions, the error vector dynamics can be expressed as:

e_{t+1}=\gamma_{\alpha}\left[D_{t}P^{*}_{\alpha}+(I-D_{t})P_{t\alpha}\right]e_{t}=\gamma_{\alpha}M_{t}e_{t}.

(5)

By construction, all matrices
M_{t}
are row stochastic with diagonal entries of at least
1-\alpha
. Additionally, by Assumption
2.1
, the underlying graph of
M_{t}
is strongly connected. The stochasticity of
M_{t}
implies the existence of a reversible Markov chain, which serves as our main analysis tool:

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\{M_{t}\}
be a sequence of row stochastic matrices and
\{p_{t}\}
a sequence of probability distribution vectors. Then,
\{p_{t}\}
is called an
absolute probability sequence
of
\{M_{t}\}
if

p_{t+1}^{T}M_{t}=p_{t}^{T},\quad\forall t\geq 0.

(6)

The existence of an absolute probability sequence for a sequence of stochastic matrices
\{M_{t}\}
was established by Kolmogorov
(
Kolmogoroff, 1936
)
. This sequence allows us to define a corresponding norm, which will be used to show convergence:

Definition 3.2
.

Let
\{p_{t}\}
be an absolute probability sequence. We define the corresponding
\{p_{t}\}
-weighted scalar product between vectors
x
and
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
as

\langle x,y\rangle_{p_{t}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{t}[i]x[i]y[i],

and the corresponding
p_{t}
-weighted squared norm as:

||x||^{2}_{p_{t}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{t}[i]x^{2}[i].

Now, we decompose the error vector
e_{t}
into two components: one in the consensus subspace and the other in its orthogonal complement, with respect to this new scalar product:

e_{t}=c_{t}\mathbf{1}+\Delta_{t},\,\text{where }\langle\mathbf{1},\Delta_{t}\rangle_{p_{t}}=p_{t}^{T}\Delta_{t}=0.

(7)

We then have that

c_{t}=\frac{\langle e_{t},\mathbf{1}\rangle_{p_{t}}}{\langle\mathbf{1},\mathbf{1}\rangle_{p_{t}}},

which corresponds to the
p_{t}
-weighted average of
e_{t}
, providing insight into the choice of
c_{t}
.

4
Main result

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
e_{t}
be a bounded initial error vector that is not at consensus (i.e.,
\Delta_{t}\neq 0
),
\{M_{t}\}
a sequence of row-stochastic matrices,
\gamma\in(0,1)
a discount factor, and
\{p_{t}\}
an absolute probability sequence for
\{M_{t}\}
. If, for any
t
, the matrix
M_{t}
has diagonal entries of at least
(1-\alpha)\in(0,1)
and a strongly connected underlying graph, then the sequence
e_{t}
generated by Update
1
satisfies

||\Delta_{t+1}||_{p_{t+1}}^{2}\leq\gamma_{\alpha}^{2}(1-R_{t})||\Delta_{t}||_{p_{t}}^{2},

(8)

where
R_{t}>0
for any
t\geq 0
.

This theorem implies the following corollary on the convergence of the Value Iteration algorithm with a rate higher than
\gamma
, which constitutes the main result of this paper.

Corollary 4.2
.

Suppose an MDP
\mathcal{M}
and let
\{V_{t}\}
be a sequence produced by the Value Iteration algorithm, starting with values
V_{0}=e_{0}+V^{*}
. Then, if Assumption
2.1
holds, Value Iteration induces a sequence of row-stochastic matrices
\{M_{t}\}
with diagonal entries
(1-\alpha)\in(0,1)
and an absolute probability sequence
\{p_{t}\}
. The resulting error vector sequence
e_{t}=c_{t}\mathbf{1}+\Delta_{t}
satisfies:

||\Delta_{T}||_{p_{T}}^{2}\leq(\gamma_{\alpha}^{2}(1-\lambda))^{T}||\Delta_{0}||_{p_{0}}^{2},

(9)

where
0<\lambda\leq R_{t}
for all
0\leq t<T
.

5
Proofs

This proof uses several standard lemmas that we state and prove here for completeness.

Definition 5.1
.

Laplacian matrix
A matrix
L\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is a Laplacian matrix if it is a symmetric
matrix with zero row sums and non-positive off-diagonal entries.

Lemma 5.2
.

If
L\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is a Laplacian matrix, then, for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

x^{T}Lx=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\sum_{j\neq i}L[i,j](x_{i}-x_{j})^{2}.

(10)

Proof.

We expand the sum in Equation
10
:

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\sum_{j\neq i}L[i,j](x_{i}-x_{j})^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}L[i,j](x_{i}-x_{j})^{2}=\sum_{i,j}L[i,j]x_{i}^{2}-2L[i,j]x_{i}x_{j}+L[i,j]x_{j}^{2}=

\displaystyle=-2\sum_{i,j}L[i,j]x_{i}x_{j}=-2x^{t}Lx,

where the third equality uses that
\sum_{j}L[i,j]=\sum_{i}L[i,j]=0

∎

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
p\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
be a non negative vector with sum of elements equal to
1
. Then, for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

\left(\sum_{i}p[i]x_{i}\right)^{2}=\sum_{i}p[i]x_{i}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}p[i]p[j](x_{i}-x_{j})^{2}.

Proof.

After rearranging terms we can write

\sum_{i}p[i]x_{i}^{2}-\left(\sum_{i}p[i]x_{i}\right)^{2}=x^{T}(\text{diag}(p)-pp^{T})x

The matrix
(\text{diag}(p)-pp^{T})
is a Laplacian matrix since it is symmetric, has rows aadding to
0
and non-positive off-diagonal entries. We then can apply Lemma
5.2
:

x^{T}(\text{diag}(p)-pp^{T})x=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\sum_{i\neq j}(-p[i]p[j])(x_{i}-x_{j})^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\sum_{i\neq j}p[i]p[j](x_{i}-x_{j})^{2}

∎

The following lemma starts the analysis of the convergence of sequence
\{\Delta_{t}\}
.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
e_{t}
be the sequence of errors described by the update
5
. Then, its decomposition
7
satisfies

\displaystyle c_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{\alpha}c_{t}

(11)

\displaystyle\Delta_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{\alpha}M_{t}\Delta_{t}

(12)

Proof.

To obtain
11
we need to multiply Equation
5
by
p_{t+1}^{T}
”

\displaystyle p_{t+1}^{T}e_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{\alpha}p_{t+1}^{T}M_{t}e_{t}=\gamma_{\alpha}p_{t}^{T}e_{t}\implies c_{t+1}=\gamma_{\alpha}c_{t},

where the equality in the first line is obtained by using the definition of
p_{t}

6
and the equality in the second line by definition of
c_{t}
.
Then, this result can be used to obtain
12
:

\displaystyle\Delta_{t+1}

\displaystyle=e_{t+1}-c_{t+1}\mathbf{1}=\gamma_{\alpha}M_{t}e_{t}-\gamma_{\alpha}c_{t}\mathbf{1}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{\alpha}M_{t}(e_{t}-c_{t}\mathbf{1})=\gamma_{\alpha}M_{t}\Delta_{t},

where the second equality uses stochasticity of

M_{t}\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{1}

We are now ready to proof the main theorem:

Proof of Theorem

.

\displaystyle||\Delta_{t+1}||^{2}_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}p_{t+1}[i]\Delta_{t+1}^{2}[i]

\displaystyle=\gamma_{\alpha}^{2}\sum_{i}p_{t+1}[i]\left(\sum_{j}M_{t}[i,j]\Delta_{t}^{2}[j]-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k,l}M_{t}[i,k]M_{t}[i,l](\Delta_{t}[k]-\Delta_{t}[l])^{2}\right)

6
Characterization of the convergence rate

Let us define the following Laplacian matrix:

L_{t}[k,l]=\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} &-\sum_{i}p_{t+1}[i]M_{t}[i,k]M_{t}[i,l],&k\neq l\\
&\sum_{i}\sum_{k\neq l}p_{t+1}[i]M_{t}[i,k]M_{t}[i,l],&k=l\\
\end{aligned}\end{cases}

Or in the matrix notation:

L_{t}=-M_{t}^{T}\Pi M_{t}+\text{diag}(M_{t}^{T}\Pi M_{t}\mathbf{1}),

where
\Pi_{t}=\text{diag}(p_{t})
. Matrix
L_{t}
is symmetric. By definition of its diagonal entries, its rows sum to zero. By non-negativity of
M
and
p
, the off-diagonal entries are well non-positive. The matrix
L_{t}
is thus well a Laplacian matrix.

Using these definitions, the term
13
can be written as a generalized Rayleigh quotient:

R_{t}=\frac{\Delta^{T}L_{t}\Delta}{\Delta^{T}\Pi_{t}\Delta}.

(14)

The generalized Rayleigh quotient is presented in
Pattabhiraman (1974)
.
For clarity, below we do not use iteration indices
t
. the bounds on the generalized Rayleigh quotient are related to the generalized eigenvalue problem:

Lv=\lambda\Pi v.

(15)

The matrix
\Pi
is diagonal and therefore its invert
\Pi^{-1}
exist sand is diagonal, as well as its factorization
\Pi=\Pi^{1/2}\Pi^{1/2}
. Thus, the pairs of eigenvalues and eigenvectors
(\lambda_{i},v_{i})
associated with the problem
15
can be seen as the classical pairs of eigenvalues and eigenvectors associated with the matrix
\Pi^{-1}L
. One of this pairs is
(0,1)
, by definition of
L
. We can also relate these pairs
(\lambda_{i},v_{i})
of eigenvalues and eigenvectors to the ones of the symmetric matrix
\tilde{L}=\Pi^{-1/2}L\Pi^{-1/2}
, which are
(\lambda_{i},\Pi^{1/2}v_{i})
.

Lv=\lambda\Pi v\Leftrightarrow\Pi^{-1}Lv=\lambda v\Leftrightarrow(\Pi^{-1/2}L\Pi^{-1/2})(\Pi^{1/2}v)=\lambda(\Pi^{1/2}v).

Since
\tilde{L}
is symmetric, its matrix of eigenvectors
\Pi^{1/2}v
is orthogonal, in the Euclidean scalar product
(\Pi^{1/2}v)^{T}(\Pi^{1/2}v)=I
, meaning that the matrix
V
of eigenvectors of
\Pi^{-1}L
are orthogonal, with respect to the
p
-weighted scalar product
V^{T}\Pi V=I
. Also, we have that
\Pi^{-1/2}
is diagonal and then matrices
\tilde{L}
and
L
are congruent, meaning that they have the same number of positive, zero and negative eigenvalues.

Since
V
contains
n
orthogonal vectors, we can decompose any vector
\Delta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
into a combination of such vectors:

\Delta=\sigma_{i}\mathbf{1}+\sum_{i=2}^{n}\sigma_{i}v_{i}

Let us plug this into
14

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta^{T}L\Delta}{\Delta^{T}\Pi\Delta}=\frac{\Delta^{T}L(\sigma_{i}\mathbf{1}+\sum_{i=2}^{n}\sigma_{i}v_{i})}{\Delta^{T}\Pi(\sigma_{i}\mathbf{1}+\sum_{i=2}^{n}\sigma_{i}v_{i})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta^{T}L(\sum_{i=2}^{n}\sigma_{i}v_{i})}{\Delta^{T}\Pi(\sum_{i=2}^{n}\sigma_{i}v_{i})}

(Using
15
)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta^{T}(\sum_{i=2}^{n}\sigma_{i}\lambda_{i}\Pi v_{i})}{\Delta^{T}\Pi(\sum_{i=2}^{n}\sigma_{i}v_{i})}

\displaystyle=\frac{(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sigma_{j}v_{j})^{T}\Pi(\sum_{i=2}^{n}\sigma_{i}\lambda_{i}v_{i})}{(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sigma_{j}v_{j})^{T}\Pi(\sum_{i=2}^{n}\sigma_{i}v_{i})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{i=2}^{n}\sigma_{j}\sigma_{i}\lambda_{i}v_{j}^{T}\Pi v_{i}}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{i=2}^{n}\sigma_{j}\sigma_{i}v_{j}^{T}\Pi v_{i}}

(using orthogonality of
V
)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{i=2}^{n}\sigma_{i}^{2}\lambda_{i}^{2}\lambda_{i}}{\sum_{i=2}^{n}\sigma_{i}^{2}}\geq\lambda_{2}(\tilde{L}),

where the first line uses the facts
L\mathbf{1}=0
and
\Delta^{T}\Pi\mathbf{1}=0
. The value
\lambda_{2}(\tilde{L})
denotes the second smallest eigenvalue of
\tilde{L}
, which is our lower bound of the Raleigh quotient:

R=\frac{\Delta^{T}L\Delta}{\Delta^{T}\Pi\Delta}\geq\lambda_{2}(\tilde{L}).

To pursue the characterization of the bound, we link
\lambda_{2}(\tilde{L})
and
\lambda_{2}(L)
in the following Lemma.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
\Pi=\text{diag}(p)
and

. We consider a Laplacian matrix

and a modified matrix

their respective second smallest eigenvalues. Than,

\lambda_{2}(\tilde{L})\geq p_{\rm max}^{-1}\lambda_{2}(L).

Proof.

Both eigenvalues can be characterized as solutions of the following optimization problems

\displaystyle\lambda_{2}(\tilde{L})=

\displaystyle\min_{x}x^{T}\tilde{L}x

(16)

\displaystyle\text{s.t. }||x||_{2}=1\text{ and }x^{T}\mathbf{1}=0.

and

\displaystyle\lambda_{2}(L)=

\displaystyle\min_{y}y^{T}Ly

(17)

\displaystyle\text{s.t. }||y||_{2}=1\text{ and }y^{T}\mathbf{1}=0.

Let

and

. Then, we have that

\displaystyle\lambda_{2}(\tilde{L})=

(18)

\displaystyle\text{s.t. }||\Pi^{1/2}y||_{2}^{2}=1\text{ and }(\Pi^{1/2}y)^{T}\mathbf{1}=0.

We also have that

, meaning that

\displaystyle p_{\rm max}^{-1}\lambda_{2}(L)=

\displaystyle\min_{y}y^{T}Ly

(19)

\displaystyle\text{s.t. }||y||_{2}=p_{\rm max}^{-1/2}\text{ and }y^{T}\mathbf{1}=0.

Finally, it is left to show that any feasible solution of

, denoted
y_{1}
, we can build a feasible solution of
18
as

that achieves a higher objective values. Let us verify that

for a well chosen values of

. We know that

because

||\Pi^{1/2}y_{1}||\leq p_{\rm max}^{-1/2}||y_{1}||_{2}=1,\quad\text{since }||y_{1}||_{2}=p_{\rm max}^{-1/2}.

The objective values achieved by the feasible solution

is
d^{2}y_{1}^{T}Lt_{1}
. Dince

.
∎

7
Conclusion

In this work, we analyzed the convergence properties of the Value Iteration algorithm using a new line of analysis based on absolute probability sequences. While previous studies have primarily focused on convergence in terms of the infinity norm, we established convergence in the weighted
L^{2}
norm, providing a refined perspective on the algorithm’s behavior.

This analysis offers a complementary viewpoint on the convergence of Value Iteration, which may be useful in further studies on the geometric properties of MDP algorithms. Future work may explore extensions to other iterative methods or examine how these findings apply in broader reinforcement learning settings.
