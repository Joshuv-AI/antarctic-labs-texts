On compromising freedom of choice and subjective value.
Nicolas Fayard † , Marc Pirlot ‡ , Alexis Tsoukiàs † † \dagger CNRS-LAMSADE, PSL, Université Paris Dauphine ‡ \ddagger MATHRO, Faculté Polytechnique, Université de Mons

Abstract
This paper proposes a new framework for evaluating capability sets by incorporating individual preferences over the diversity of accessible options. Building on the Capability Approach, we introduce a compromise method that balances between the notions of negative and positive freedom, effectively capturing the intrinsic and instrumental values of diverse choices within capability sets.

Introduction

The Capability Approach, introduced by Amartya
22
;
23
;
26
;
27
;
28
, provides a framework for evaluating individual well-being and social justice by shifting the focus from material resources to the real freedoms people have to achieve the lives they value. Central to this approach is the concept of capability set, which represents the set of achievable functionings (seen as beings and doings) that individuals can choose from, given their resources, personal characteristics, and the surrounding environment. This perspective ties freedom directly to the availability and quality of opportunities, emphasizing that freedom of choice is not only about the range of options but also about their ability to make individuals lead meaningful lives.

The link between opportunity set theory and the Capability Approach lies in their shared focus on freedom as a central metric for well-being. Opportunity set theory provides a formal framework for comparing sets of choices available to individuals, capturing the idea that freedom can be understood as a range of opportunities. This aligns directly with the Capability Approach, which interprets these sets as reflecting the real opportunities individuals have to achieve valued outcomes. By analyzing the structure, size, and relevance of opportunity sets, one can formalize and measure the extent of freedom in a way that resonates with the normative goals of the Capability Approach
(
15
;
31
;
13
;
2
)
.

In the Capability Approach, the emphasis is not on the range of nominal alternatives, but on the real opportunities to achieve valuable states of being. Each option is conceived as a vector of functionings across several welfare dimensions, and a capability set gathers the attainable vectors.
From this perspective, adding a dominated element does not enlarge what a person can truly achieve, which makes it natural to focus on the set of undominated elements (the Pareto frontier).
This stance is normatively stronger than in some opportunity set models
(
15
;
8
)
, where dominated options may still be considered relevant. For this reason, we will use the term
capability sets
rather than
opportunity sets
.

Within the broader discussion of freedom, scholars have highlighted two interconnected aspects: interpretations of freedom and methods to measure it. The interpretations, such as negative freedom, positive freedom, and preference for flexibility (see below for explanations and references), offer conceptual foundations for understanding the value of capability sets. These perspectives emphasize distinct but overlapping ways in which freedom manifests in capability sets. Meanwhile, the development of measurement approaches aims to operationalize these interpretations by providing quantitative tools to evaluate the extent and quality of freedom. Together, these two aspects form the backbone of opportunity set theory and its practical applications.

Freedom interpretations have been categorized into three main perspectives. Negative freedom, rooted in the absence of external constraints, views freedom as the ability to act without interference, focusing on the size of opportunity sets
(
1
;
19
;
14
)
. Negative freedom is closely linked to the intrinsic value of freedom, as it emphasizes the existence of options and the autonomy they represent, regardless of whether these options are utilized. Positive freedom emphasizes the availability and conversion of meaningful opportunities, highlighting the quality of options
(
23
;
3
)
. Positive freedom is inherently tied to the instrumental value of freedom, as it focuses on how opportunities enable individuals to achieve valuable outcomes and lead fulfilling lives. Finally, preference for flexibility, introduced by
13
, stresses the adaptability provided by larger sets in the face of future preference uncertainty
(
2
;
29
)
.

The methods for measuring those sets of options have evolved to capture these varying interpretations. Quantity based methods
(
15
;
18
)
, with the simplest approach, counts the number of options in a set, aligning closely with negative freedom. Weighted cardinality refines this by assigning value to each option based on its significance, connecting with positive freedom
(
31
;
3
;
16
;
31
;
32
)
. Utility-based measures evaluate sets by considering the maximum utility achievable, often linked to both positive freedom and preference for flexibility
(
13
;
20
)
. Finally, measures of diversity focus on the range and variation of choices, addressing elements of all three interpretations by highlighting the richness and adaptability of the set
(
2
;
31
)
.

In this paper, we position our work in between negative and positive freedom. Our proposed measure is situated between utility-based approaches and diversity-based approaches. To the authors’ knowledge, the only works that have attempted to measure freedom as conjointly reflecting the diversity and the valuation of options are
10
;
7
;
8
;
9
. However, a key limitation of their methods is that they do not satisfy the Strong Monotonicity Property, meaning that adding an alternative that appears relevant to an individual does not necessarily increase their freedom.

Section 2 introduces the mathematical setting and the notation. Section 3 delves into the necessity of developing a function to assess capabilities, focusing on the role of freedom of choice and exploring the Gaertner and Xu methods and the volume based method proposed by
33
. Section 4 defines desirable properties of methods for assessing capability sets. Section 5 presents our approach, the compromise approach, which respects those properties. Our approach will be compared to two methods that represent opposing perspectives on the value of diversity in capability sets. The
instrumental extreme
evaluates an opportunity set only by its best-valued option, reflecting a purely outcome-oriented view of freedom. In contrast, the
intrinsic extreme
values freedom through the availability of all equally desirable alternatives, emphasizing the richness of choice. These two serve as normative extremes between which our proposed compromise approach can be flexibly parameterized. Proofs of the main results are provided in the appendix.

A formal framework for Capability sets

An individual’s being is represented by a vector
\vec{a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{h},\ldots,a_{h^{*}})\in\mathbb{R}^{h^{*}}_{\geq 0}=\{\vec{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{h^{*}}|x_{h}\geq 0,\forall h=1,\ldots h^{*}\}
, where the coordinates
a_{h}
assess the performance levels attained on a set of
h^{*}
welfare dimensions (or functionings), such as life expectancy, level of nourishment, mobility, etc. It is assumed that the evaluations on the
h^{*}
dimensions of the space
\mathbb{R}^{h^{*}}_{\geq 0}
provide a complete description of the welfare aspects that are relevant for the individual. We also assume, w.l.o.g., that, for all welfare dimensions, the larger the evaluation the better.

We recall the definition of the weak (resp. strict) dominance relation on
\mathbb{R}^{h^{*}}_{\geq 0}
. For
\vec{a},\vec{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{h^{*}}_{\geq 0}
, we have that
\vec{a}
weakly dominates
\vec{b}
(denoted
\vec{a}\geq\vec{b}
) if
a_{h}\geq b_{h}
, for all
h=1,\ldots,h^{*}
. We say that
\vec{a}
strictly dominates
\vec{b}
(denoted
\vec{a}>\vec{b}
) if
\vec{a}
weakly dominates
\vec{b}
and
a_{h}>b_{h}
, for some
h\in\{1,\ldots,h^{*}\}

We abuse notation by using the same symbol
\geq
for denoting the usual order relation on the real numbers set
\mathbb{R}

>

In practice, a capability set can take different forms depending on the problem under study.
It may be a finite collection of attainable beings, for example when an empirical dataset enumerates distinct combinations of functionings that a person can realistically achieve
(
5
)
.
In other contexts, it may be an infinite set defined implicitly by constraints, such as the individual’s limited resources, together with a model describing how these resources can be transformed into feasible beings
(
4
)
.
In a spatial accessibility setting, for instance, the capability set could be the set of all functionings reachable within a given travel budget.
This flexibility allows the framework to encompass both discrete choice situations and continuous opportunity spaces.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{h^{*}}_{\geq 0}
, called the
capability space
.
We denote by boldface letters such as
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B},\mathbf{C}
subsets of
\mathcal{C}
.
When these subsets are nonempty and compact, we call them
capability sets
.
Compactness expresses that feasible beings are both limited and well-defined: boundedness reflects the finiteness of available resources, and closure the idea that opportunity structures include all their attainable limits. Individuals are assumed to reason over attainable beings rather than over sequences that merely approach them.
We exclude the empty set, which would correspond to the absence of any viable being, in the Capability Approach, a situation equivalent to the loss of all functionings,
i.e.
, death.
In some cases, it will be convenient to consider subsets of the larger space
\mathbb{R}^{h^{*}}_{\geq 0}
,
whenever we do so, we will make this explicit, and such sets are not referred to as capability sets.

For any subset
\mathbf{A}\subseteq\mathcal{C}
, we define its
Positive Domination Closure
(PDC) as

\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{D}}=\{\vec{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{h^{*}}_{\geq 0}\mid\exists\vec{a}\in\mathbf{A}\text{ such that }\vec{a}\geq\vec{x}\}.

(1)

That is,
\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{D}}
consists of all beings that are weakly dominated by at least one element of
\mathbf{A}

We now extend the weak and strict dominance relations to subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{h^{*}}_{\geq 0}

Definition 2.1

Let
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}

\mathbb{R}^{h^{*}}_{\geq 0}

We say that
\mathbf{A}

\vec{b}\in\mathbf{B}

We say that
\mathbf{A}

\mathbf{A}>\mathbf{B}

\mathbf{A}\geq\mathbf{B}
and there exists at least one being
\vec{a}\in\mathbf{A}
such that no being
\vec{b}\in\mathbf{B}
satisfies
\vec{b}\geq\vec{a}
.

We say that
\mathbf{A}

strongly dominates

\mathbf{B}
, denoted
\mathbf{A}\gg\mathbf{B}

if
\mathbf{A}\geq\mathbf{B}
and there exist a being
\vec{a}\in\mathbf{A}
and
\varepsilon>0

the hyper-rectangle
[\vec{a}-\varepsilon\cdot\vec{1},\vec{a}]
is contained in
\mathbb{R}^{h^{*}}_{\geq 0}

and does not intersect the dominated region of
\mathbf{B}
, that is,

[\vec{a}-\varepsilon\cdot\vec{1},\vec{a}]\cap\mathbf{B}^{\mathcal{D}}=\emptyset,

where
\vec{1}=(1,\ldots,1)
denotes the unit vector in
\mathbb{R}^{h^{*}}

2

[\vec{a}-\varepsilon\cdot\vec{1},\vec{a}]

.

If
\mathbf{A}

Similarly,

The distinction between strict and strong dominance lies in the notion of a
perceptible expansion of freedom
.
While strict dominance (
\mathbf{A}>\mathbf{B}
) only requires that
\mathbf{A}
includes at least one being not dominated by any element of

\mathbf{A}\gg\mathbf{B}

\mathbf{A}

\mathbf{B}
.
The existence of an
\varepsilon

(for instance, adding an isolated irrational point among rationals)
but corresponds to an actual, perceivable enlargement of the capability space.
This interpretation is consistent with the Capability Approach,
where differences in freedom should reflect tangible opportunities
that agents can realistically experience or value.

If
\mathbf{A}
is a capability set, the set of its non-strictly dominated points plays an important role in the sequel.

Definition 2.2

Let
\mathbf{A}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{h^{*}}_{\geq 0}
and
\vec{a}\in\mathbf{A}
.
The point
\vec{a}
is
non-strictly dominated
in
\mathbf{A}

\vec{b}\in\mathbf{A}
such that
\vec{b}>\vec{a}
.
The set of all non-strictly dominated points of
\mathbf{A}

Pareto frontier

P(\mathbf{A})

In general, if we do not assume that
\mathbf{A}

P(\mathbf{A})

If
\mathbf{A}

\mathcal{C}

\mathbf{A}
is weakly dominated by a point in
P(\mathbf{A})

Proposition 1

If
\mathbf{A}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{h^{*}}_{\geq 0}
is compact, then for all
\vec{a}\in\mathbf{A}
, there exists
\vec{b}\in P(\mathbf{A})
such that
\vec{b}\geq\vec{a}
.

Proof.
For any
\vec{x}\in\mathbf{A}
, let
\vec{x}^{\geq}
denote the set of points in
\mathbb{R}^{h^{*}}_{\geq 0}
that weakly dominate
\vec{x}
, i.e.,
\vec{x}^{\geq}=\{\vec{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{h^{*}}_{\geq 0}|\vec{y}\geq\vec{x}\}
. Consider the set
\mathbf{A}\cap\vec{x}^{\geq}
. It is a bounded and closed subset of
\mathbb{R}^{h^{*}}_{\geq 0}
, since
\mathbf{A}
is bounded and closed, and
\vec{x}^{\geq}
is closed. Let
\vec{y}^{*}\in\arg\max\{\sum_{i=1}^{h^{*}}y_{i},\vec{y}\in\mathbf{A}\cap\vec{x}^{\geq}\}
. We have
\vec{y}^{*}\in A\cap x^{\geq}
since the continuous function
\sum_{i=1}^{h^{*}}y_{i}
on the compact set
\mathbf{A}\cap\vec{x}^{\geq}
reaches its maximum in this set. The point
\vec{y}^{*}
weakly dominates
\vec{x}
and is non-strictly dominated in
\mathbf{A}\cap\vec{x}^{\geq}
. To prove the latter, assume by contradiction that there is
\vec{z}\in\mathbf{A}\cap\vec{x}^{\geq}
with
\vec{z}\neq\vec{y}
and
\vec{z}>\vec{y}^{*}
. This would imply
\sum_{i=1}^{h^{*}}z_{i}>\sum_{i=1}^{h^{*}}y^{*}_{i}
, a contradiction. We just showed that
\vec{y}^{*}
is non-strictly dominated in
\mathbf{A}\cap\vec{x}^{\geq}
. It is not either in
\mathbf{A}
because no point in
\mathbf{A}\cap\vec{x}^{\geq}
is dominated by a point in
\mathbf{A}\setminus\vec{x}^{\geq}
.

\blacksquare

An immediate consequence of this result is the following.

Corollary 1

If
\mathbf{A}
is a capability set, we have that
\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{D}}=P(\mathbf{A})^{\mathcal{D}}
.

Proof.
By Proposition
1
and the transitivity of the weak dominance relation, any point weakly dominated by a point of
\mathbf{A}
is weakly dominated by a point in
P(\mathbf{A})
.

\blacksquare

Note that the Pareto frontier
P(\mathbf{A})
of a capability set
\mathbf{A}
is not always a capability set. This is because the set
P(\mathbf{A})
may fail to be closed.

We consider a set of valuation functions
v\in V
such that each
v:\mathcal{C}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
maps the capability space
\mathcal{C}
to non-negative real numbers. The considered functions
v(\cdot)
are both increasing and continuous, the former meaning that:

\forall v\in V,\forall(\vec{c},\vec{c}^{\,\prime})\in\mathcal{C}^{2},\;\;\text{if }\vec{c}>\vec{c}^{\,\prime},\text{ then }v(\vec{c})>v(\vec{c}^{\,\prime}).

The requirement for
v
being defined on
\mathcal{C}
is strong because it requires the ability to compare all the options in
\mathcal{C}
, which may not always be possible.
Nonetheless, the monotonicity of
v
is a reasonable demand, given the objective of maximizing welfare across all dimensions.

3
Freedom of choice

As highlighted in the introduction, we want to consider two crucial aspects when comparing capability sets: their overall level of welfare (valuation of beings) and their distribution across different dimensions (diversity).

One important aspect that is often overlooked in the measurement of capability sets
is the value assigned to the freedom of choosing an option valued at a certain level of well-being
.
In the example given by
23
, it may be reasonable to argue that a person who assigns no value to choice would be indifferent between a capability set where only
\vec{a}
is achievable and a capability set where
\vec{a}
is achievable along with another option
\vec{a^{\prime}}
such that
v(\vec{a^{\prime}})<v(\vec{a})
.
However, it can also be argued that a person who values choice would prefer the second capability set over the first.
This consideration highlights the importance of incorporating individual preferences over freedom and values over beings within the measurement framework.

The assessment of capability sets is a complex task that requires careful consideration.
One way to do so is to count the number of options in the capability set, as proposed by
15
in an axiomatic characterization.
However, it should be noted that the value of choice cannot be reduced to the cardinality of the capability set due to several important reasons:

Similarly, duplicating an existing opportunity, such as adding to

We now introduce a function
\Phi
that evaluates capability sets.
Formally, for a capability set
\mathbf{A}\subseteq\mathcal{C}
, a value function
v\in V
, and a
freedom-sensitivity function

\phi:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, assumed to be continuous and strictly positive except perhaps in 0,
we denote by
\Phi_{v}^{\phi}(\mathbf{A})
the value assigned to
\mathbf{A}
according to
v
, once transformed by
\phi
.
We denote by
\Phi_{v}^{\phi}(\mathbf{A})
the value assigned to
\mathbf{A}
according to
v
, after transforming
v
by
\phi
.
This evaluation takes a non-negative real value:
\Phi_{v}^{\phi}(\mathbf{A})\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
.

Intuitively, the freedom-sensitivity function
\phi
determines how the individual values differences in attainable well-being levels.
It transforms the valuation
v(\vec{a})
of each attainable being
\vec{a}\in\mathbf{A}
into a modified contribution
\phi(v(\vec{a}))
,
thereby shaping how freedom responds to the distribution of values within the capability set.
A convex
\phi
amplifies higher valuations of
v
, approximating an
instrumental
view of freedom where the best options dominate the assessment.
Conversely, a concave
\phi
gives relatively greater importance to lower-valued beings, capturing an
intrinsic
or egalitarian view of freedom.

In view of the above discussion, we assume that
\Phi_{v}^{\phi}
satisfies the following property.

Axiom 1
(Indifference of insignificant beings)

For all value function
v\in V
, all freedom-sensitivity
function
\phi
and all capability sets
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}
such that
\mathbf{A}\geq\mathbf{B}
, we have

\Phi^{\phi}_{v}(\mathbf{A})=\Phi^{\phi}_{v}(\mathbf{A}\cup\mathbf{B})
.

Axiom
1
entails the following.

Proposition 2

If
\mathbf{A}
is a capability set,
\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{D}}
is also a capability set. Under Axiom
1
,
\Phi^{\phi}_{v}(\mathbf{A})=\Phi^{\phi}_{v}(\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{D}})
. In case
P(\mathbf{A})
is a capability set, we also have
\Phi^{\phi}_{v}(\mathbf{A})=\Phi^{\phi}_{v}(P(\mathbf{A}))
.

Proof.
Although it seems intuitively clear that
\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{D}}
is compact as soon as
\mathbf{A}
is compact, we could not find a proof of this result in literature. We thus provide a – not straightforward – proof in Appendix
A
(Lemma
1
).

Assuming that
\mathbf{A}
is a capability set, we have
\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{D}}\geq\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{A}\geq\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{D}}
. Applying Axiom
1
, we get
\Phi^{\phi}_{v}(\mathbf{A})=\Phi^{\phi}_{v}(\mathbf{A}\cup\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{D}})=\Phi^{\phi}_{v}(A^{\mathcal{D}})
.

Similarly, if
P(\mathbf{A})
is closed, hence a capability set, we have
\Phi^{\phi}_{v}(P(\mathbf{A}))=\Phi^{\phi}_{v}(\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{D}})=\Phi^{\phi}_{v}(\mathbf{A})
, since
P(\mathbf{A})^{\mathcal{D}}=\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{D}}
.
\blacksquare

\mathbf{A}

\mathbf{B}

3.1
Gaertner and Xu method

To the authors’ knowledge, the only works that have tried to measure freedom as jointly involving the diversity and the valuation of options are

It is worth noting that these methods have been primarily proposed for assessing human development, whereas the current study addresses capabilities of all types.
The fundamental principles of their model are the following (with adaptations made to facilitate understanding in the context of our problem):

First, a reference beings vector
\vec{k^{0}}
in the capability space
\mathcal{C}
is defined, which represents the direction of societal development.
Typically, this vector indicates a boundary of deprivation below which an individual is considered poor. Alternatively, it can be the existing average level of development.

Next, a distance function
d(x,y)
between two points
x
and
y
is defined.
3
3

3

The distance function need to respect some properties (see
7
).

For instance, the Euclidean distance can be used, given by

d(x,y)=\sqrt{\sum^{h^{*}}_{h=1}(x_{h}-y_{h})^{2}},

where
h^{*}
denotes the number of dimensions in the capability space.

Then, in an adapted version of
the Gaertner-Xu method, a capability set
\mathbf{A}
is assessed as follows:

1.

if
\vec{k^{0}}\not\in\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{D}}
, the assessment is a negative number; it is equal to minus the radius of the smallest sphere centered in
\vec{k^{0}}
that intersects the set
\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{D}}
in a point
\vec{a}
dominated by
\vec{k^{0}}
;

2.

if
\vec{k^{0}}\in\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{D}}
, the assessment is a positive number; it is equal to the radius of the largest sphere centered in
\vec{k^{0}}
satisfying the following condition: the part of this sphere contained in the positive orthant centered in
\vec{k^{0}}
is included in
\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{D}}
.

Mathematically, the assessment
r(\mathbf{A},d)
of
\mathbf{A}
is computed as follows:

r(\mathbf{A},d)=\begin{cases}-\min_{t}\{t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}:\{\vec{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{h^{*}}_{\geq 0}:\vec{a}\leq\vec{k^{0}},d(\vec{a},\vec{k^{0}})\leq t\}\cap\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{D}}\neq\emptyset\}\>\mbox{if}\>\vec{k^{0}}\notin\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{D}},\\
\max_{t}\{t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}:\{\vec{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{h^{*}}_{\geq 0}:\vec{a}\geq\vec{k^{0}},d(\vec{a},\vec{k^{0}})\leq t\}\subseteq\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{D}}\}\>\mbox{if}\>\vec{k^{0}}\in\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{D}}.\end{cases}

\vec{k^{0}}

\max_{t}\{t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}:\{\vec{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{h^{*}}_{\geq 0}:\vec{a}\geq\vec{k^{0}},d(\vec{a},\vec{k^{0}})\leq t\}\subseteq\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{D}}\}\>\mbox{if}\>\vec{k^{0}}\in\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{D}}.\end{cases}

3.2
On ranking linear budget sets in terms
of freedom of choice

Another interesting approach for assessing opportunity sets is the method proposed by

\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{D}}

\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{D}}
and
\mathbf{B}^{\mathcal{D}}

8
, if a person places greater value on functioning 1 relative to functioning 2, they would typically prefer set
\mathbf{A}
over
\mathbf{B}

4
Extremes of freedom valuation and desirable properties

We begin by presenting two conceptual extremes in the evaluation of freedom. The first, which we refer to as the
instrumental extreme

(
31
;
32
)
, assesses a capability set only based on its best-valued element, reflecting a view of freedom grounded in outcomes. In contrast, the
intrinsic extreme
values freedom for its own sake, and considers that all equally desirable beings be accessible to achieve a given level of well-being. These two extremes define the normative upper and lower bounds within which our proposed compromise measure of freedom is situated (see Section
5
).

4.1
Instrumental extreme

When individuals view freedom as having a purely
instrumental value
, their concern is limited to the most favorable achievable outcome. In this perspective, the individual evaluates a capability set
\mathbf{A}
solely based on the best element it contains. Consequently, they are indifferent between a set that contains only the best-valued solution and one that contains that solution along with additional, less desirable beings. The diversity or variety of options in the set does not matter; only the top performer does.

This behavior is captured by the following valuation function:

\Phi^{\max}_{v}(\mathbf{A})=\max_{\vec{a}\in\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{D}}}v(\vec{a}).

We can associate to each capability set
\mathbf{A}
a corresponding
level set
of the valuation function
v
, which collects all elements in the capability space
\mathcal{C}
that achieve the same value as the best element of
\mathbf{A}
:

\mathbf{A}_{\max}=\left\{\vec{c}\in\mathcal{C}\mid v(\vec{c})=\Phi^{\max}_{v}(\mathbf{A})\right\}.

This level set captures the frontier of best-valued beings, according to the max criterion.
It can be interpreted as a capability threshold: any capability set
\mathbf{B}
such that

\Phi^{\mathrm{max}}_{v}(\mathbf{B})\leq\Phi^{\mathrm{max}}_{v}(\mathbf{A}_{\mathrm{max}})

will be contained in
\mathbf{A}_{\mathrm{max}}^{\mathcal{D}}
.

Finally, the instrumental extreme approach can be seen as an “optimistic” interpretation of the Gaertner–Xu method. Specifically, when we take

4.2
Intrinsic extreme

An individual who values
diversity
as an essential aspect of freedom may adopt a different perspective. From this point of view, a person considers themselves free to achieve a well-being level
y
only if they can access
all beings

\vec{c}\in\mathcal{C}
such that
v(\vec{c})=y
, or beings that are
objectively better
in the sense that
\vec{c^{\prime}}\geq\vec{c}
and
v(\vec{c})=y
.

In other words, they do not consider themselves free to reach level
y
unless their capability set includes all relevant solutions that attain (or surpass) that value.

This behavior is captured by the following valuation function:

\Phi^{\min}_{v}(\mathbf{A})=\inf_{\vec{c}\in\mathcal{C}\setminus\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{D}}}v(\vec{c}),

which corresponds to the lowest well-being level that
cannot
be achieved by the capability set
\mathbf{A}
.

As with the extreme instrumental approach, we can define a level set representing all beings in
\mathcal{C}
that attain the boundary value of
\Phi^{\min}_{v}(\mathbf{A})
:

\mathbf{A}_{\min}=\left\{\vec{c}\in\mathcal{C}\mid v(\vec{c})=\Phi^{\min}_{v}(\mathbf{A})\right\}.

This set captures the threshold beyond which the individual no longer considers themselves free to attain that level of well-being.
For all capability sets
\mathbf{B}
, if we have
\Phi^{\min}_{v}(\mathbf{A}_{\min})\leq\Phi^{\min}_{v}(\mathbf{B})
, then
\mathbf{A}_{\min}
is a set contained in
\mathbf{B}^{\mathcal{D}}
.

4.3
Desirable properties

To ensure that our proposed freedom measure
\Phi
is both theoretically sound and aligned with our intuitive understanding of freedom, it must satisfy a number of desirable properties. In the following, we outline a set of axioms that formalize these requirements over the capability sets and the valuation functions in
V
.

Axiom 2
(Strong Monotonicity)

For all
v\in V
, all freedom-sensitivity
function
\phi
, and capability sets
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}
, if
\mathbf{A}\geq\mathbf{B}
then
\Phi^{\phi}_{v}(\mathbf{A})\geq\Phi^{\phi}_{v}(\mathbf{B})
, and if
\mathbf{A}\gg\mathbf{B}
then
\Phi^{\phi}_{v}(\mathbf{A})>\Phi^{\phi}_{v}(\mathbf{B})
.

Axiom 3
(Continuity)

For all
v\in V
all freedom-sensitivity
function
\phi
, and capability sets
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{C}
, such that
\Phi^{\phi}_{v}(\mathbf{A})>\Phi^{\phi}_{v}(\mathbf{C})
, for all
\lambda\in[\Phi^{\phi}_{v}(\mathbf{C}),\Phi^{\phi}_{v}(\mathbf{A})]
, there exists a capability set
\mathbf{B}
such that
\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{D}}\cap\mathbf{C}^{\mathcal{D}}\subseteq\mathbf{B}^{\mathcal{D}}\subseteq\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{D}}\cup\mathbf{C}^{\mathcal{D}}
and
\Phi^{\phi}_{v}(\mathbf{B})=\lambda
.

Axiom 4
(Invariance of Scaling Effects)

For all
v\in V
, all freedom-sensitivity
function
\phi
, and capability sets
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}
, if
\Phi^{\phi}_{v}(\mathbf{A})\geq\Phi^{\phi}_{v}(\mathbf{B})
then, for any
\vec{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{h^{*}}_{>0}
, letting
\mathbf{A}^{\prime}=\vec{\alpha}\cdot\mathbf{A}
,
\mathbf{B}^{\prime}=\vec{\alpha}\cdot\mathbf{B}
, and defining
v^{\prime}(\vec{\alpha}\cdot\vec{a})=v(\vec{a})
, we have

\Phi^{\phi}_{v^{\prime}}(\mathbf{A}^{\prime})\geq\Phi^{\phi}_{v^{\prime}}(\mathbf{B}^{\prime}).

Axiom 5
(Bounded Freedom Principle)

For all
v\in V
, all freedom-sensitivity
function
\phi
, and for any capability set
\mathbf{A}
,

\Phi^{\phi}_{v}(\mathbf{A}_{\min})\leq\Phi^{\phi}_{v}(\mathbf{A})\leq\Phi^{\phi}_{v}(\mathbf{A}_{\max}).

The
Strong Monotonicity
axiom requires that the valuation function
\Phi_{v}
respects the dominance relationships between capability sets in a way that reflects meaningful expansions of freedom.
If one set
\mathbf{A}
weakly dominates another set
\mathbf{B}
(
\mathbf{A}\geq\mathbf{B}
), then its valuation should not be lower.
If
\mathbf{A}
strongly dominates
\mathbf{B}
(
\mathbf{A}\gg\mathbf{B}
), then its valuation must be strictly higher.
This axiom captures the fundamental intuition that freedom should increase whenever a capability set expands in a tangible and measurable manner, rather than by the mere addition of limit or redundant options.
Variations of this principle, such as strict monotonicity
(
33
)
and other forms of monotonicity
(
13
;
21
;
2
)
, are common in the literature.

(

)
and other forms of monotonicity
(

;

;

, are common in the literature.

It is worth noting that the volume-based method of
33
is defined for linear budget sets, where the dominance relation
\mathbf{A}>\mathbf{B}
directly implies
\mathbf{A}\gg\mathbf{B}
.
In the more general non-linear setting considered here, this implication no longer holds:
\mathbf{A}>\mathbf{B}
does not necessarily entail that
\mathrm{vol}(\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{D}})>\mathrm{vol}(\mathbf{B}^{\mathcal{D}})
.
For instance, consider
\mathbf{A}=\{(1,1)\}
and
\mathbf{B}=\{(1,1),(2,0)\}
.
Here,
\mathbf{A}<\mathbf{B}
in the dominance sense, yet
\mathrm{vol}(\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{D}})=\mathrm{vol}(\mathbf{B}^{\mathcal{D}})
.

The
Continuity
axiom ensures that the valuation function
\Phi_{v}
behaves in a smooth and predictable manner when capability sets are modified. It reflects the intuition that freedom assessment should not change in a discontinuous or abrupt manner when capability sets evolve gradually.
In particular, this condition implies a stronger version of the
Solvability
property,
a standard requirement in Decision Analysis and measurement theory

(
12
)
. A weaker variant of this axiom appears in
8
under the name
Betweenness
.

The
Invariance of Scaling Effects
axiom guarantees that the ordering of capability sets is invariant under uniform scaling of the underlying dimensions. That is, if every welfare dimension measure of a capability set is multiplied by a positive factor, the relative ranking of the freedom measures remains unchanged. This property is adapted from
33
and is vital because the absolute levels of capabilities can depend on the units of measurement; however, the perceived degree of freedom should be independent of such scaling. Without this invariance, the freedom measure could be unduly influenced by arbitrary choices of scale, detracting from its consistency and applicability.

Finally, the
Bounded Freedom Principle
establishes that the freedom measure
\Phi^{\phi}_{v}(\mathbf{A})
is confined between two extremes: an intrinsic (min) and an instrumental (max) view of freedom. Specifically, it requires that

\Phi^{\phi}_{v}(\mathbf{A}_{\min})\leq\Phi^{\phi}_{v}(\mathbf{A})\leq\Phi^{\phi}_{v}(\mathbf{A}_{\max})

for every capability set
\mathbf{A}
, every
v\in V
and every
\phi
. The intrinsic extreme represents the lower bound, reflecting a perspective that values beings for their inherent worth, while the instrumental extreme represents the upper bound, reducing the set to its best option. This axiom ensures that our measure of freedom remains within an interval that is rationally motivated .

Together, these axioms form the core properties that any reasonable measure of freedom should satisfy, thereby ensuring both theoretical rigor and practical relevance.

5
Compromise approach

In the previous approaches (instrumental and intrinsic), the evaluation of a capability set
\mathbf{A}
did not account for the contribution of all efficient solutions in its Pareto frontier.
To overcome this limitation, we introduce a
compromise approach
that integrates the contribution of every point in the Pareto frontier, ensuring that any addition of a non-dominated option expanding the measurable domain of attainable beings has a strictly positive impact on

\Phi^{\phi}_{v}(\mathbf{A})
.
This approach satisfies the previously stated axioms, remains sensitive to the chosen value function
v
, accommodates different perspectives on freedom, and offers a balanced and reasonable way to capture the value of capability sets.

5.1
Definition

We define the compromise method as follows:

\displaystyle\Phi^{\phi}_{v}(\mathbf{A})=\int\limits_{\vec{a}\in\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{D}}}\phi(v(\vec{a}))\,d\vec{a}

where
\phi
is a continuous function
\phi:[0,\max_{\vec{a}\in\mathcal{C}}v(\vec{a})]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, with
\phi
strictly positive except perhaps in
0
.

Proofs that
\Phi^{\phi}_{v}
satisfies the desirable properties listed in Section
4.3
are presented in Appendix
B
.

\phi:[0,\max_{\vec{a}\in\mathcal{C}}v(\vec{a})]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}

\phi(v(\vec{a}))=c
for all
\vec{a}\in\mathbf{A}

\Phi^{\phi}_{v}(\mathbf{A})=\int_{\vec{a}\in\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{D}}}\phi(v(\vec{a}))\,d\vec{a}=c\cdot\mathrm{vol}(\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{D}}).

See proof in Appendix

5.2
Algorithms and complexity

From a computational point of view, calculating
\Phi^{\phi}_{v}(\mathbf{A})
presents a complex challenge that is beyond the scope of this paper. Nevertheless, we introduce Algorithm
1
as a theoretical method to compute
\Phi^{\phi}_{v}(\mathbf{A})
when
P(\mathbf{A})
is known and finite, despite practical limitations in its application.

Considering
\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{D}}=P(\mathbf{A})^{\mathcal{D}}
, we can express
\Phi^{\phi}_{v}(\mathbf{A})
as

\Phi^{\phi}_{v}(\mathbf{A})=\Phi^{\phi}_{v}\left(\bigcup_{\vec{a}\in P(\mathbf{A})}\vec{a}\right).

Applying the Principle of Inclusion-Exclusion further refines this expression to

\Phi^{\phi}_{v}\left(\bigcup_{\vec{a}\in P(\mathbf{A})}\vec{a}\right)=\sum_{Z\subseteq\mathcal{P}(P(\mathbf{A}))\setminus\{\emptyset\}}(-1)^{|Z|+1}\cdot\Phi^{\phi}_{v}\left(\bigcap_{\vec{a}\in Z}\vec{a}^{\mathcal{D}}\right).

This equation underpins the generation of all subsets of the power set of the Pareto frontier of
\mathbf{A}
(line 3 of Algorithm
1
).

We note that for any set
Z
within the power set
\mathcal{P}(P(\mathbf{A}))
, the expression
\bigcap_{\vec{a}\in Z}\vec{a}^{\mathcal{D}}
is equivalent to
(\min_{\vec{a}\in Z}a_{h}\,|\,\forall h\in\{1,\ldots,h^{*}\})^{\mathcal{D}}
. This equivalence forms the basis for deriving lines 4-6 of Algorithm
1
. Furthermore, line 7 is developed based on the Principle of Inclusion-Exclusion.
A crucial observation is that we transform
P(\mathbf{A})
into
P(\mathbf{A})\cap\mathbb{R}^{h^{*}}_{>}
(line 1) so that for any function
v\in V
, the function
\phi(v())
maintains strict positivity over
\mathcal{C}
. Consequently,
\Phi^{\phi}_{v}(\bigcap_{\vec{a}\in Z}\vec{a}^{\mathcal{D}})
always has a non-null value.

To compute the integral indicated in line 6, we can utilize numerical methods, facilitated by the SciPy 1.0 Python package
(
30
)
. While this approach is computationally intensive, it offers a viable solution for performing the required calculations in the algorithm.
It is important to note that the complexity of the integration methods in SciPy 1.0, especially the ‘dblquad‘ function, is variable and cannot be expressed using a standard fixed-complexity notation. Consequently, it is advisable to be aware that complex functions, precise requirements, and larger ranges are computationally demanding.

Furthermore, the practical feasibility of the algorithm is limited due to the exponential complexity involved in generating the power set
\mathcal{P}(P(\mathbf{A}))
, which scales as
O(2^{|P(\mathbf{A})|})
. This exponential growth in complexity renders the algorithm impractical for large Pareto sets.

Conclusion

This study proposed a new approach to evaluating capability sets by integrating individual preferences over diverse options, thereby bridging the gap between negative and positive freedom within the Capability Approach.
Our methodological contribution lies in the introduction of the
compromise approach
, which preserves several desirable properties of volume-based measures
(
33
)
while relaxing their symmetry requirement, thus allowing individual values and perspectives on freedom to be explicitly incorporated.
Compared to the framework of
8
, our approach additionally satisfies
strong monotonicity
, ensuring that every addition of a non-dominated strictly positive being is valued.
Future work will focus on the elicitation of the function
\phi
, its operationalization in empirical contexts, and the development of more efficient algorithms for computing these valuations.

Appendix B
Proof of axioms for the compromise approach

B.1
Strict monotonicity

First, assume that
\mathbf{A}\geq\mathbf{B}
. By definition of weak set dominance, for every solution in
\mathbf{B}
there is a solution in
\mathbf{A}
that weakly dominates it. This implies that the PDC of
\mathbf{B}
is a subset of the PDC of
\mathbf{A}
, i.e.,

\mathbf{B}^{\mathcal{D}}\subseteq\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{D}}.

Since the integral used to define
\Phi^{\phi}_{v}
is monotonic with respect to set inclusion, it follows that

\Phi^{\phi}_{v}(\mathbf{A})=\int_{\vec{a}\in\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{D}}}\phi\Big(v(\vec{a})\Big)\,d\vec{a}\geq\int_{\vec{b}\in\mathbf{B}^{\mathcal{D}}}\phi\Big(v(\vec{b})\Big)\,d\vec{b}=\Phi^{\phi}_{v}(\mathbf{B}).

Now assume that
\mathbf{A}\gg\mathbf{B}
.
By definition of strong set dominance (Definition
2.1
), there exist a being
\vec{a}_{0}\in\mathbf{A}
and
\varepsilon>0
such that the hyper-rectangle

[\vec{a}_{0}-\varepsilon\cdot\vec{1},\vec{a}_{0}]
is contained in
\mathbb{R}^{h^{*}}_{\geq 0}
and does not intersect
\mathbf{B}^{\mathcal{D}}
, i.e.,

[\vec{a}_{0}-\varepsilon\cdot\vec{1},\vec{a}_{0}]\cap\mathbf{B}^{\mathcal{D}}=\emptyset.

Hence, this
\varepsilon
-cube is a measurable subset of

\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{D}}\setminus\mathbf{B}^{\mathcal{D}}
with strictly positive Lebesgue measure.

We can thus decompose the integral as follows:

\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{D}}=\mathbf{B}^{\mathcal{D}}\cup(\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{D}}\setminus\mathbf{B}^{\mathcal{D}}),

and by the additivity of the integral we obtain

\Phi^{\phi}_{v}(\mathbf{A})=\int_{\vec{b}\in\mathbf{B}^{\mathcal{D}}}\phi\!\big(v(\vec{b})\big)\,d\vec{b}+\int_{\vec{a}\in\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{D}}\setminus\mathbf{B}^{\mathcal{D}}}\phi\!\big(v(\vec{a})\big)\,d\vec{a}.

Since
\phi(v(\vec{a}))
is strictly positive on its domain
and the set
\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{D}}\setminus\mathbf{B}^{\mathcal{D}}
has positive measure
(because it contains the nonempty cube
[\vec{a}_{0}-\varepsilon\cdot\vec{1},\vec{a}_{0}]
),
the second integral is strictly positive:

\int_{\vec{a}\in\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{D}}\setminus\mathbf{B}^{\mathcal{D}}}\phi\!\big(v(\vec{a})\big)\,d\vec{a}>0.

Therefore,

\Phi^{\phi}_{v}(\mathbf{A})>\Phi^{\phi}_{v}(\mathbf{B}).

\blacksquare

B.2
Invariance of Scaling Effects

When we scale the set
\mathbf{A}
by
\vec{\alpha}
, each vector
\vec{a}
in
\mathbf{A}
is mapped to
\vec{a}^{\prime}=\vec{\alpha}\cdot\vec{a}
. A standard property of Lebesgue measure in
\mathbb{R}^{h^{*}}_{>}
is that scaling every coordinate by a positive factor multiplies the measure of any measurable set by a constant. In particular, there exists a constant

c=\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\cdots\alpha_{h^{*}}>0,

such that for any integrable function
f
and measurable set
\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{D}}
we have

\int_{\vec{a^{\prime}}\in\vec{\alpha}\cdot\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{D}}}f(\vec{a}^{\prime})\,d\vec{a}^{\prime}=c\int_{\vec{a}\in\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{D}}}f(\vec{a})\,d\vec{a}.

In our setting, the definition of
v^{\prime}
ensures that

\phi\Big(v^{\prime}\bigl(\vec{\alpha}\cdot\vec{a}\bigr)\Big)=\phi\Big(v(\vec{a})\Big),

so that the evaluation function for the scaled set becomes

\Phi^{\phi}_{v^{\prime}}(\mathbf{A}^{\prime})=c\,\Phi^{\phi}_{v}(\mathbf{A}),

and similarly,

\Phi^{\phi}_{v^{\prime}}(\mathbf{B}^{\prime})=c\,\Phi^{\phi}_{v}(\mathbf{B}).

Since
c
is a positive constant and by hypothesis
\Phi^{\phi}_{v}(\mathbf{A})\geq\Phi^{\phi}_{v}(\mathbf{B})
, it follows immediately that

\Phi^{\phi}_{v^{\prime}}(\mathbf{A}^{\prime})\geq\Phi^{\phi}_{v^{\prime}}(\mathbf{B}^{\prime}).

\hfill\blacksquare

B.3
Continuity

By monotonicity of the integral, we have

\Phi^{\phi}_{v}(\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{D}}\cap\mathbf{C}^{\mathcal{D}})\leq\Phi^{\phi}_{v}(\mathbf{C})<\Phi^{\phi}_{v}(\mathbf{A})\leq\Phi^{\phi}_{v}(\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{D}}\cup\mathbf{C}^{\mathcal{D}}).

For
t
in the interval

\Bigl[0,\;\max_{\vec{x}\in\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{D}}\cup\mathbf{C}^{\mathcal{D}}}v(\vec{x})\Bigr],

define the function

G(t)=\int_{\vec{x}\in\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{D}}\cap\mathbf{C}^{\mathcal{D}}}\phi\bigl(v(\vec{x})\bigr)d\vec{x}\;+\;\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}\vec{x}\in(\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{D}}\cup\mathbf{C}^{\mathcal{D}})\setminus(\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{D}}\cap\mathbf{C}^{\mathcal{D}})\\
v(\vec{x})\leq t\end{subarray}}\phi\bigl(v(\vec{x})\bigr)d\vec{x}.

Because
\phi
is strictly positive,
\phi
and
v
are continuous, and the integral is monotone w.r.t. the integration domain,
G(t)
is continuous and non decreasing with respect to
t
. In particular, when
t
is minimal (i.e.,
t=0
) we have

G(0)=\int_{\vec{x}\in\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{D}}\cap\mathbf{C}^{\mathcal{D}}}\phi\bigl(v(\vec{x})\bigr)d\vec{x}=\Phi^{\phi}_{v}(\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{D}}\cap\mathbf{C}^{\mathcal{D}}),

and when
t
is maximal (i.e.,
t=\max_{\vec{x}\in\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{D}}\cup\mathbf{C}^{\mathcal{D}}}v(\vec{x})
)

G\Bigl(\max_{\vec{x}\in\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{D}}\cup\mathbf{C}^{\mathcal{D}}}v(\vec{x})\Bigr)=\int_{\vec{x}\in\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{D}}\cup\mathbf{C}^{\mathcal{D}}}\phi\bigl(v(\vec{x})\bigr)d\vec{x}=\Phi^{\phi}_{v}(\mathbf{A}\cup\mathbf{C}).

Since

\Phi^{\phi}_{v}(\mathbf{A}\cap\mathbf{C})\leq\Phi^{\phi}_{v}(\mathbf{C})<\lambda<\Phi^{\phi}_{v}(\mathbf{A})\leq\Phi^{\phi}_{v}(\mathbf{A}\cup\mathbf{C}),

the Intermediate Value Theorem guarantees that there exists a
t^{*}
in

\Bigl[0,\;\max_{\vec{x}\in\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{D}}\cup\mathbf{C}^{\mathcal{D}}}v(\vec{x})\Bigr]

such that

G(t^{*})=\lambda.

Now, define the set

\mathbf{B}=\Bigl\{\vec{x}\in\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{D}}\cup\mathbf{C}^{\mathcal{D}}:\;\vec{x}\in\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{D}}\cap\mathbf{C}^{\mathcal{D}}\;\text{ or }\;v(\vec{x})\leq t^{*}\Bigr\}.

Clearly,
\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{D}}\cap\mathbf{C}^{\mathcal{D}}\subseteq\mathbf{B}\subseteq\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{D}}\cup\mathbf{C}^{\mathcal{D}}
. By construction, the evaluation of
\mathbf{B}
is given by

\Phi^{\phi}_{v}(\mathbf{B})=G(t^{*})=\lambda.

This completes the proof.
\hfill\blacksquare
