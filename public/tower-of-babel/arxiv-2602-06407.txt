Primordial Black Hole signatures from femtolensing and spectral fringe of Gamma-Ray Bursts
Chang-Yu Dai 1 , and Po-Yan Tseng 1,2

Abstract
Femtolensing of gamma-ray bursts (GRBs) are vastly studied to constrain the primordial black hole (PBHs) lighter than 10 − 13 10^{-13} solar mass and may close the window for PBH dark matter. In this case, wave optics formalism is required and carefully implemented in our analysis. Incorporating the GRB observational data from Swift XRT, we perform the statistic analysis of PBH lensing, comparing with null hypothesis where BAND model is used to parametrize the GRB spectrum. We found few GRB data manifest the spectral fringe which characterize the feature of femtolensing by PBHs, and the analysis shows moderate statistical preference in terms of goodness of fit. Conversely, since most of the fitting to GRB spectral data do not improved with PBH lensing, we utilize to obtain upper bound on the PBH fractional abundance with respect to dark matter. However, the robust constraint cannot be achieved, unless the size of GRBs are smaller than 5 × 10 7 5\times 10^{7} m for PBH mass around 5 × 10 − 15 5\times 10^{-15} solar mass.

Formalism of Femtolensing

We review the femtolensing formalism by following Ref.
Katz:2018zrn
;
Tamta:2024pow
;
Hogg:1999ad
.
We will start with the geometric optics approximation and then introduce the wave optics corrections. In the end, we discuss the physics condition that the geometric approach deviates from the wave optic prediction.

We can set an axis from the observer to the lens, then
\beta
is the angle between the line from the observer to the source’s real position and the axis. The
\theta
is the angle between the line from the observer to the source’s lensing image and the axis. From Fermat’s principle,
\nabla_{\theta}(\Delta t)=0
in geometric optics limit where
\Delta t
denotes the delayed time which will be discussed later.
Blandford:1986zz
And one can use the lensing equation

\vec{\theta}-\vec{\beta}=\nabla\psi(\vec{\theta}).

(1)

to determine the positions of the images. The vector form means those angle are projected to the lens plane which is perpendicular to observer-lens axis. The
\psi(\vec{\theta})
is the lensing potential given by

\psi(\vec{\theta})=\frac{D_{LS}}{D_{L}D_{S}}\frac{2}{c^{2}}\int dz\,\Phi(D_{L}\vec{\theta},z)

(2)

where
\Phi
is the gravitational potential, and
D_{L}

D_{S}

D_{LS}
are angular diameter distances from the observer to the lens, from the source to the lens, and from the lens to the source respectively
Hogg:1999ad
. One can relate
\psi
with lens mass distribution
\rho
by the Poisson equation

\nabla^{2}\psi(\vec{\theta})=\frac{8\pi G}{c^{2}}\frac{D_{LS}D_{L}}{D_{S}}\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}dz\,\rho(\sqrt{(D_{L}\theta)^{2}+z^{2}}).

(3)

If a gamma-ray encounters a lens at redshift
z_{L}
, then it will be split into different rays. These rays will go through different paths lead to a geometric time delay
Bartelmann:2010fz
;
Katz:2018zrn

\Delta t_{geom}=\frac{1}{c}\frac{D_{L}D_{S}}{D_{LS}}(1+z_{L})\frac{|\vec{\theta}-\vec{\beta}|^{2}}{2}.

(4)

In addition, the gravitational potential will cause another time delay compare to non-lensing gamma ray. Because the distances of source, lens, and observer are far more than lensing region, we can use thin lens approximation to write the time delay as
Bartelmann:2010fz
;
Katz:2018zrn

\Delta t_{grav}=\frac{1}{c}\frac{D_{L}D_{S}}{D_{LS}}(1+z_{L})\psi(\vec{\theta})

(5)

So they will have a total time difference

\Delta t=\Delta t_{geom}+\Delta t_{grav}=\frac{1}{c}\frac{D_{L}D_{S}}{D_{LS}}(1+z_{L})\left(\frac{|\vec{\theta}-\vec{\beta}|^{2}}{2}-\psi(\vec{\theta})\right)

(6)

which cause a phase shift
\Delta\phi=\omega\Delta t
where
\omega
is the angular frequency of photon. If the travel time of the split rays can’t be distinguished by the detector, then the detector will get interfered image. So the intensity of the signal received by the detector will be magnified. This phenomenon will manifested in spectrum.

We assume point-like lens, so lens potential is spherically symmetric

\psi(\vec{\theta})\rightarrow\psi(\theta)=\theta^{2}_{E}\text{ln}(\theta),

(7)

where
\theta_{E}
is Einstein angle

\theta_{E}\equiv\sqrt{\frac{4GM}{c^{2}}\frac{D_{LS}}{D_{L}D_{S}}},

(8)

where
G
is gravitational constant and
M_{\rm{PBH}}
is the PBH mass. We normalize
\vec{\theta}
and
\vec{\beta}
by Einstein angle by define
\vec{x}

\vec{y}
as
\vec{x}\equiv\vec{\theta}/\theta_{E}

\vec{y}\equiv\vec{\beta}/\theta_{E}
respectively. So the lensing equation Eq.
1
becomes

x-y=\frac{1}{x}.

(9)

This equation has two solution

x_{\pm}=\frac{1}{2}(y\pm\sqrt{y^{2}+4})

(10)

which means we can observe two images. These two images lead the intensity

\mu_{\pm}=\frac{y^{2}+2}{2y\sqrt{y^{2}+4}}\pm\frac{1}{2}.

(11)

If they can produce the interference pattern, then the total magnification will become

\mu=\frac{y^{2}+2}{y\sqrt{y^{2}+4}}+\frac{2}{y\sqrt{y^{2}+4}}\text{sin}\left[\Omega\left(\frac{y\sqrt{y^{2}+4}}{2}+\text{ln}\left|\frac{y+\sqrt{y^{2}+4}}{y-\sqrt{y^{2}+4}}\right|\right)\right]

(12)

Here we introduce the dimensionless frequency
\Omega
defined as

\Omega\equiv\frac{4GM_{\rm{PBH}}(1+z_{L})}{c^{3}}\omega.

(13)

Because the energy range of GRB from Swift XRT is
10^{0}\sim 10^{1}\text{keV}
, and the mass of PBH of interest is
10^{-17}\sim 10^{-12}M_{\odot}
. The wavelength of GRB is comparable with the Schwarzschild radius of PBH, so we need consider the wave optics. The magnification for wave optics can be describe by
\mu=|F|^{2}
. Here
F
is the ratio of the wave amplitude
\phi_{L}/\phi_{0}
(
\phi_{L}
is the lensing amplitude and
\phi_{0}
is the amplitude without lensing) and is given by
Tamta:2024pow
;
Nakamura:1999uwi

Again, we consider a point-like, spherically symmetric lens. So Eq.(
14

GRB data of Swift XRT

The Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory (Swift) was launched on November 20, 2004

,

So the real process should be

Here

4
Conclusion

We used 106 GRBs data observed by Swift XRT to examine PBH gravitational femtolensing, which occurs when light propagates through the vicinity of a PBH, the gravitational field of PBH causes light to deflect and induces phase shifts along different paths. The interference among different paths causes an oscillating pattern, which is imprinted on the energy spectrum of detected photon data.

According to our analysis, some of the GRBs display the feature of lensing oscillation caused by PBHs, rather than the simple exponential power-law spectrum predicted from the BAND model. Those potentially PBHs are inferred to be localized within the Milky Way. This is because most of GRB have relatively large
a_{s}
by assuming
a_{s}=c\times T_{90}
. Under the constraint of
\sigma_{y}\ll 1
, they only be lensed by PBHs at small lens redshifts
z_{L}
.

Whether there is an oscillation pattern can not be determined directly from the GRB data. Therefore, we define the oscillation feature using
\bar{\mu}
as function of energy, in which the first local minimum is less than one.
Some of the GRB data display the oscillation, but not eligible for our oscillation requirement with
\bar{\mu}
. The oscillation pattern occurs in
\bar{\mu}
when the source, the lens, and the observer are not collinear. So
y_{0}
is critical for oscillation; in particular, the oscillation cannot occur when
y_{0}
is smaller than one. We highlight two GRBs, GRB091029 and GRB101219B, which satisfy the aforementioned oscillation requirement and prefer the PBH lensing interpretation.

Finally, we use the
84
GRBs from Table
2

\sim
Table
7
to calculate the constrain on
f_{\rm{PBH}}
. The constraint of
f_{\text{PBH}}\leq 1
can only be achieved if we set the
a_{\text{s}}
smaller than
10^{7}
m. For
a_{\rm{s}}=c\times T_{90}
, more GRB observation data are needed to reach a constraint with
f_{\text{PBH}}\leq 1
.
We assume the monochromatic mass of PBHs when calculating the constraint on
f_{\text{PBH}}
. The mass interval with
f_{\text{PBH}}\leq 1
extends from
5\times 10^{-14}M_{\odot}
to
10^{-16}M_{\odot}
. The quantities
f_{\rm{PBH}}
and
\tau
are scaling and inversely proportional under the requirement
P_{\text{null}}=0.05
.
\tau
is proportional to the number of GRB observation data. For projection sensitivity, we assume that GRB observed in the future has the same distribution as our current sample of
84
GRBs. Then a sample of
10^{7}
GRBs would be sufficient to achieve
f_{\text{PBH}}\leq 1
for
a_{\rm{s}}=c\times T_{90}
.
