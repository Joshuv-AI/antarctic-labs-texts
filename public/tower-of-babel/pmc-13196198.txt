Adverse childhood experiences and learning engagement in medical students: the mediating role of negative emotions
Authors: Weihong Xu, Yi Xia, Ruoshui Yu, Wei Wei, Shanshan Li, Zhaoxun Liu, Xingcun Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveLearning engagement has become a pivotal indicator in higher medical education, garnering substantial research attention. However, the negative impact of adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) on learning engagement has rarely been discussed, and the underlying psychological mechanisms remain unclear. This study investigated the associations between ACEs and learning engagement among medical students and the mediating role of negative emotions (NEs).MethodsParticipants were selected via random cluster sampling, using a cross-sectional study design. A total of 1004 medical students completed questionnaires about their adverse childhood experiences, learning engagement, perceived stress, depression, and anxiety. To examine the mediating role of NEs in the relationship between ACEs and learning engagement, a multiple mediation model was utilized.ResultsA total of 43.2% of the study participants self-reported experiencing ACEs. Significant pairwise correlations were observed among ACEs, NEs (depression, perceived stress, and anxiety), and learning engagement. According to the mediation analysis, perceived stress (-0.444, 95% CI [-0.706, -0.208]), depression ( -0.855, 95% CI [-1.197, -0.322]), and anxiety (0.294, 95% CI [0.004, 0.600]) mediated the relationship between ACEs and learning engagement after adjusting for covariates, with a total mediating effect of 85.8% of the total effect.ConclusionThese findings underscore the critical need to examine the interactions among ACEs, learning engagement, and NEs and extend existing research on the psychological determinants of medical students’ academic engagement. Medical universities and educators should implement systematic ACEs and NEs screening and provide timely monitoring and evidence-based psychological interventions to enhance students’ learning engagement.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09224-5.Keywords: Adverse childhood experiences, Learning engagement, Stress, Depression, Anxiety

Introduction

Higher medical education serves as the cornerstone of health-care system development and is charged with the responsibility of medical professionals. It directly impacts population health outcomes and societal well-being, playing a pivotal role in achieving the “Healthy China 2030” strategic goals [1]. As a result, there are growing expectations for excellence and examination of the quality of higher medical education. With the emergence of the theory of the “student-centered” approach to education, learning engagement has gained increasing prominence in national policy discussions [1]. Medical students, as the primary recipients of medical education, their learning engagement offers a pivotal perspective on the efficacy of educational programs [2, 3]. Furthermore, given some unique traits of the medical field, medical students face heightened learning challenges and pressures from professional responsibilities [4]. Most critically, the consequences of diminished learning engagement in medical education extend beyond individual academic failure; it directly threatens the long-term stability of the healthcare workforce and the quality of future patient care [5, 6]. Therefore, an investigation into the mechanisms underlying medical student learning engagement is warranted.

Learning engagement include three dimensions—energy, devotion, and absorption—representing a positive and fulfilling psychological state experienced during learning. Higher learning engagement predicts enhanced academic performance and fosters essential personal and professional competencies in medical students [7, 8]. It plays an important role in building learning environments and advancing professional education development [9]. Previous research has indicated that learning engagement may be affected by personal factors (such as motivation, self-efficacy and educational satisfaction) and outside environmental elements (such as social support, peer relationships and a sense of belonging) [10–12]. However, the majority of study has concentrated on the influence of the current environment and individual characteristics, with less research on individuals’ early adverse environmental characteristics and the mechanisms they currently produce [13].

Adverse childhood experiences (ACEs), a crucial subtype of early adverse environments, encompass possibly harmful incidents that take place ahead of 18. These consist of all forms of abuse, neglect, and dysfunction in the home [14, 15]. In a landmark longitudinal study, 67.0% of the approximately 10,000 participants tracked reported at least one ACE [14]. ACEs not only have acute short-term effects on childhood but also have lasting impacts on developmental trajectories, increasing the risk for poor physical/mental health, impaired socialization, and adverse educational outcomes [16, 17]. Study among middle school students indicates that ACEs in the household can result in the deterioration of the parent‒child bond and thus a lack of sufficient energy to engage in learning [18]. These studies establish ACEs as a significant risk factor for students’ learning engagement, potentially lowering academic satisfaction and motivation [5]. Particularly the students in the context of higher medical education, a field renowned for its arduous programs, rigorous curricula, intense competition, and intricate doctor-patient dynamics, which act as significant environmental stressors that may exacerbate the trauma embedded by ACEs [1, 6]. Therefore, it is essential to employ a trauma-informed perspective to investigate the specific mechanisms underlying medical student learning engagement, providing a robust rationale for targeted educational interventions and support systems.

Our study follows Mehrabian’s S-O-R theoretical framework [19]: environmental stimuli do not directly trigger behavior; rather, they influence the organism’s internal state, which then drives observable behavior. Previous studies have shown that individuals experiencing ACEs are at risk for long-term mental disorders during the process of adaptation [20]. In accordance with the theory of “latent vulnerability”, such adaptations may correlated with neurocognitive functioning, manifesting in symptoms like negative emotions (NEs), which may less suited to react to normative settings such as schools [21, 22]. NEs refer to subjective, unpleasant affective states encompassing diverse emotional experiences [23]. These not only correlate with increased maladaptive behaviors but also constitute risk factors for emotional disorders [24]. Critically, the interrelated states of depression, anxiety, and stress represent core manifestations of NEs [25, 26]. In this study, perceived stress, depression, and anxiety were treated as parallel mediators. Although these emotional states often co-occur, they are conceptually and structural distinct. Examining these constructs as separate parallel pathways enables more targeted implications for psychological intervention within medical education [27, 28]. Recent evidence indicates that ACEs increase susceptibility to negative affect while diminishing positive affect in adulthood, including anxiety, depression, and posttraumatic stress disorder, which represent the most prevalent psychological sequelae observed following ACEs exposure [29]. The correlation between NEs and learning has been repeatedly substantiated [30]. The limited resource theory of self-control elucidates how chronic exposure to NEs depletes essential executive resources. In an academic setting, this resource depletion manifests as diminished attentional control and impaired persistence, further undermining learning engagement [31, 32]. Therefore, based on above theories and empirical researches, we regard ACES as the distal environmental stimulus, learning engagement as the response, and consider whether NEs—as the organism’s internal state—serve as the mediating variable in this relationship.

Previous studies on the relationship between ACEs and learning engagement have focused predominantly on external factors and internal protective factors, with critically limited examinations of internal risk factors. Although the relationships between ACEs and NEs and learning engagement are robust, research elucidating the mechanisms underlying these relationships remains scarce. In addition, most studies on ACEs and learning engagement has concentrated on high school students or normal college students, with fewer studies on medical university students. However, understanding these mechanisms in medical students is not merely an educational concern but a public health necessity, as these students are navigating the transition from trauma survivors to professional healers. Therefore, this study’s goal was to analyze the mediating functions of NEs and explore the association between ACEs and medical students’ learning engagement (Fig. 1). Based on extant theoretical frameworks and evidence, we hypothesized that (1) Medical students’ learning engagement is significantly correlated negatively with ACEs and that (2) The association between ACEs and learning engagement is mediated by NEs (depression, anxiety, and perceived stress).

Method

Participants and study design

The data came from a cross-sectional survey given to medical students in Guangzhou, China. Research participants were recruited from two different campuses (Panyu district and Yuexiu district) of a medical university in Guangzhou between May 12 and November 20, 2024 and selected by random cluster sampling. Students were grouped by grade, with one or two classes randomly selected from each grade. This study utilized paper-based questionnaires to ensure a higher level of participant engagement and data quality. Researchers visited classrooms during breaks or independent study sessions and distributed/collected questionnaires on-site. A brief oral presentation was delivered to explain the study’s objectives, the voluntary nature of participation, and the strict measures taken to ensure anonymity.

In total, 1,051 questionnaires were initially collected. Following the identification and elimination of missing values and outliers exhibiting logical inconsistencies. After removing 47 cases invalid cases (32 for missing values and 15 for logical inconsistencies), the final analytical sample included 1,004 participants, yielding a valid response rate of 95.5%. Inclusion criteria were : (1) voluntary participation in the study, (2) comprehension of all questionnaire items. Exclusion criteria were: (1) inconsistent responses (e.g., identical responses across all items and attention checks with incorrect answers), (2) refusal by participants, and (3) Number of missing values ≥ 1.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and all participants were informed about the purpose of the study and provided written informed consent prior to participation. The Guangzhou Medical University Ethics Committee granted ethical permission for this study (No: 202406003). All datasets were pseudonymized via identifier replacement prior to analysis.

Measure

The survey was measured using a structured paper questionnaire in Chinese, which consisted of three sections: Introduction, Demographics, and several validated scales. The complete questionnaire is available in the supplementary materials.

Adverse childhood experiences

ACEs were assessed via the validated Chinese adaptation of the 14-item Revise ACEQ-R [33]. Compared with the original 10-item ACEQ scale, the ACEQ-R scale adds peer victimization, social exclusion, community violence exposure, and socioeconomic deprivation to the original three dimensions (abuse, neglect, and household dysfunction), which can more comprehensively measure ACEs. The participants answer ‘yes’ or ‘no’ to indicate whether they were exposed to each event category prior to the age of 18. A cumulative ACE score (range: 0–14) was computed by summing affirmative responses. This approach is consistent with the widely adopted cumulative risk framework in ACE research, as it provides a parsimonious way to capture the overall burden of ACEs and its dose - response relationship with later outcomes. Higher scores indicate a higher frequency of early adversity. In this investigation, the scale had a standardized α coefficient of 0.718.

Negative emotions

To perform a granulated analysis of NEs (depression, anxiety, and perceived stress), we treat these three emotions as independent mediators, thereby identify the unique and incremental contribution of each emotional facet in explaining the relationship between ACEs and learning engagement. The following three validated scales were employed.

Depression

The 9-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) was used to measure depression [34], which was a validated instrument aligned with DSM-IV diagnostic criteria. Each question was answered on a 4-point Likert scale: ‘Not at all’, ‘Several days’, ‘More than half the days’, and ‘Nearly every day’ and scored 0–3 points (sum scores rang 0–27). More severe symptoms are indicated by higher scores.The scale’s Cronbach’s α coefficient in this study was 0.854.

Anxiety

Anxiety were test via the 7-item Generalized Anxiety Disorder scale (GAD-7), a self-report instrument assessing symptoms on the basis of the DSM-IV diagnostic criteria [35]. A 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (Never) to 3 (Always) is used to rate each item (sum scores rang 0–21). Higher scores indicate more severe anxiety. The scale had a standardized α coefficient of 0.913 in this study .

Perceived stress

Perceived stress was measured via the Chinese Perceived Stress Scale (CPSS, a validated adaptation of Cohen’s 14-item Perceived Stress Scale [36, 37]. The questions were answered on a 5-point Likert frequency scale ranging from ‘Never’ to ‘Always’, with 7 items being positively assigned and 7 items negatively assigned. Greater perceived stress is indicated by higher total scores. In this investigation, the scale had a standardized α coefficient of 0.862.

Learning engagement

Learning engagement was assessed via the Utrecht Work Engagement Scale-Student (UWES-S) [38], which was validated for Chinese adaptation by Li & Huang [39]. Three dimensions—vigor, motivation, and concentration—are included in the 17 items on the scale. The scale used a 7-point Likert, with 1 denoting never and 7 denoting practically daily, was used for the response alternatives. We utilized the composite score to measure learning engagement which provides a more parsimonious and reliable representation of overall learning engagement in educational settings. Higher composite scores were indicative of more learning engagement. In this investigation, the scale had a standardized α coefficient of 0.963.

Demographic variables

Potential covariates influencing learning engagement were identified through a literature review [4, 40]. Demographic factors covered gender, residence (urban, rural), only child status (yes or no), family per capita monthly income and family type (core family, single parent family, reorganized family, intergenerational family). Academic factors include grade and major satisfaction.

Statistical analysis

The analysis was using IBM SPSS 25.0 and Mplus. Given the large sample size (n = 1,004), the normality was assessed each dependent variable by examining skewness and kurtosis values, alongside visual inspection of Q-Q plots. The absolute values of skewness and kurtosis for all key variables were within the acceptable range (< 2 and < 7, respectively), suggesting an approximately normal distribution [41]. The descriptive statistics were computed as frequencies and percentages. The t-test was used for binary variables and the one-way ANOVA were used for multi-category variables to compare the differences. Prior to hypothesis testing, collinearity diagnostics confirmed that all variance inflation factors (VIFs) did not exceed 5, and the tolerance values were all above 0.40, indicating multicollinearity did not severely affect the subsequent analyses. Moreover, common method bias was assessed via Harman’s single-factor test, revealing that the first unrotated factor explained 24.8% of the variance (< 30.0% threshold), did not indicate severe bias [42]. To rigorously assess the potential for common method bias inherent in self-reported data, we supplemented the Unmeasured Latent Method Construct (ULMC) approach. Specifically, a confirmatory factor analysis measurement model that does not include method factor was first established, then an unmeasured common method factor was added to the model, and compare the fit indices of the two models. The change in fit indices was minimal: △CFI= -0.008, △TLI= -0.009, △RMSEA = 0.015, and △SRMR= -0.452. The results indicated that the inclusion of the method factor did not lead to a substantial improvement in model fit and did not indicate severe bias in the current study [43]. Finally, bivariate associations between ACEs, NEs (anxiety, depression and perceived stress), and learning engagement were analyzed using Pearson correlation analysis.

To examine the effects of ACEs on learning engagement and the mediation function of NEs among medical students, demographic variables were controlled for as covariates. Using PROCESS macro 4.0 (Model 4), we conducted a multiple parallel mediation analysis with 5,000 bias-corrected bootstrap resamples. When the indirect effects’ 95% BCa CIs did not include zero, mediation significance was established. Ultimately, we were able to determine each variable’s total, direct, and indirect mediating effects.

Results of the mediation effect analysis

Prior to the analyses, the assumption of homoscedasticity was assessed for each regression model. Visual inspection of the standardized residual plots showed that the residuals were randomly and evenly distributed across all levels of the predicted values, indicating no evidence of heteroscedasticity. The demographic variables that showed significant from Table 1 were included as covariates (grade, family type, and satisfaction of speciality). Table 3 presents regression specifications for all the mediation models, and Fig. 2 displays the specific path coefficients. The regression findings revealed that ACEs was negatively associated with learning engagement (B = -1.171) and positively associated with perceived stress (B = 0.710), depression (B = 0.791) and anxiety (B = 0.623) (All, P < 0.001). After controlling NEs, ACEs no longer associated with learning engagement (B = -0.166, P = 0.634), indicating complete mediation. Moreover, when ACEs were controlled, perceived stress (B = -0.626) and depression (B = -1.080) (All P < 0.001) had a negative impact on learning engagement, but anxiety (B = 0.472, P < 0.05) served as an unusual positive predictor of learning engagement.These results demonstrate that the association between ACEs and learning engagement is mediated by NEs collectively. Importantly, despite the inter-correlation, all three mediators retained significant unique predictive power for learning engagement, and the large sample size ( n = 1004) provided sufficient statistical power to yield stable and reliable for the mediation paths.

The parallel mediation effects of ACEs on learning engagement

Table 4 shows the total, direct, and indirect effects of ACEs on learning engagement via NEs. The entire total effect was − 1.171 (95% CI: -1.871, -0.472), indicating that ACEs significantly associated with reduced learning engagement. After controlling for mediators, the overall direct effect was − 0.166 (95% CI: -0.851, 0.519), suggesting that ACEs had no direct impact on learning engagement. With a mediating impact of 85.8%, NEs were associated with learning engagement, as indicated by the 95% BCa CI of the total indirect effect, which was − 1.005 (95% CI: -1.413, -0.610). In particular, there were significant relative mediating effects of perceived stress [ -0.444 (95% CI: -0.706, -0.208)], depression [ -0.855 (95% CI: -1.197, -0.322)], and anxiety [ 0.294 (95% CI: 0.004, 0.600)] on the influence of ACEs on learning engagement. These effects accounted for 37.9%, 73.0%, and 25.1% of the total indirect effect, respectively. In the current multiple parallel mediation model, the sum of the absolute proportions of the specific indirect effects (37.9% for stress, 73.0% for depression, and 25.1% for anxiety) exceeds 100%, which indicates a suppression effect may be present. This occurs because the indirect effect through anxiety (0.294) has a different sign than the other indirect effects and total effect (-1.171). All proportions were calculated as the ratio of each specific indirect effect to the total effect.

Discussion

As far as we know, our investigation is the first thorough examination of the emotional processes that connect ACEs to medical students’ poor learning engagement. Our cross-sectional analysis of 1,004 students from medical universities revealed that 43.2% reported having experienced ACEs. Given that ACEs play a role in disrupting neurodevelopmental trajectories and health problem in adulthood, medical students with such encounters are more prone to act negatively and show cognitive and emotional problem [44, 45]. According to the findings, the average learning engagement score was 80.284 ± 19.0, indicating generally positive academic behaviors among medical students [1]. This aligns with prior studies attributing sustained engagement to rigorous training demands and frequent high-stakes assessments in medical education. Additionally, our samples form a prominent medical university in a developed coastal province, which maintains high academic standards. These hyperbaric environments may foster higher baseline learning engagement and NEs among students compared to general universities. So compared to a survey of 11,796 college students in China using the same measurement technique, the students of our study’s mean scores for anxiety (3.871 ± 3.831) and depression (4.904 ± 3.908) were greater [46]. This result suggests that the phenomenon of NEs is more pronounced among medical students. This study, therefore, examined negative emotions (NEs) as potential mediators, investigating an underlying pathway between ACEs and variations in learning engagement. This would be highly important for strengthening social attention to the psychological health of students and improving learning engagement.

According to the current study, ACEs and learning engagement are strongly and negatively correlated. In keeping with other research findings, this supported Hypothesis 1, which states that medical students who have had less exposure to ACEs exhibit greater levels of learning engagement [47]. Although the correlation between ACEs and learning engagement was relatively small(r = − 0.139), ACEs represent distal and cumulative exposures, which actually masks a significant, multifaceted and lasting impact on the mechanism that is critical for medical students’ learning engagement. This could be related to the cumulative impact of ACEs, which may be linked to long-term influence on college students throughout their academic careers. The helplessness theory also hold that individuals with ACE exposure often internalize self-worth deficits and attribute adversity to personal inadequacies. This psychological state creates formidable barriers to learning, leading to a decline in proactive learning engagement [38].

Based on the findings of the extant literature, ACEs was significantly associated with elevated NEs [48, 49]. Individuals exposed to ACEs develop maladaptive emotional schema wherein negative experiences activate dysfunctional meta-cognitive processes, heightening sensitivity to adverse stimuli and perpetuating frequent negative affective states [50]. Specifically, this process initiates a toxic stress response that chronically activates stress systems, thereby amplifying subjective stress perception [51]. Simultaneously, according to the neural diathesis-stress mechanisms, long-term exposure to ACEs may trigger excessive release of cortisol, epinephrine, and norepinephrine [52]. Ultimately, by upsetting emotion regulatory networks, this endocrine dysregulation makes people more susceptible to pathological stress perception and the emergence of anxious and depression symptoms [53–55].

Our study result also consistent with prior research that elevated perceived stress and depression significantly associated with reduced learning engagement [11]. Specifically, perceived stress and depression may be associated with subjective reports of cognitive difficulties, manifesting as attentional deficits, memory consolidation difficulties, and slowed information processing [56, 57]. These factors diminish learning efficiency and intrinsic motivation, consequently affecting learning engagement. Additionally, emotions not only correlate with the acquisition and encoding of memories, as well as their storage and reactivation. According to limited resource theory of self-control and process efficacy theory, the production of NEs consumes working control and memory resources, which leads to impaired learning persistence and learning memories, thus affecting learning engagement [58]. Notably, our result indicated a positive indirect effect through the anxiety pathway [ 0.294 (95% CI: 0.004, 0.600)]. The finding suggests that when shared variance with depression and stress is controlled, anxiety may capture a distinct component that is positively associated with learning engagement. However, considering the negative correlation between anxiety and learning engagement observed in the correlation analysis, we cautiously suggest that this may reflect a suppression effect or inconsistent mediation pattern [59]. Although traditional views emphasize the debilitating role of anxiety in academic settings [60], emerging evidence suggests a more complex relationship, where moderate levels of anxiety might occasionally serve as a motivational force or facilitate cognitive processes under specific conditions [61, 62]. This underscores the importance of distinguishing between different types of emotional distress. The focus of intervention should be on alleviating students’ depressive state and excessive psychological pressure. As for moderate and dynamic anxiety, it can be guided to transform it into positive learning strategies rather than blindly eliminating it. Given the contradictory findings in existing literature, future longitudinal research is warranted to further disentangle these complex dynamics.

Hypothesis 2 was supported by the mediation analysis, which indicates that NEs play a potential mediating role in the association between ACEs and learning engagement Specifically, medical students with higher ACE exposure tended to report elevated perceived stress and depression, which were negatively associated with learning engagement. This pathway is consistent with Mehrabian’s S-O-R theoretical framework. The ACE-related neuroendocrine sensitization and diminished emotion regulation, which may be linked to the manifestation of NEs [63]. Prolonged NEs may further trigger hippocampal–amygdala neurotoxicity [64], disrupting memory consolidation and attentional control processes essential for learning engagement. When controlling for covariates and NEs (perceived stress, depression, and anxiety), the direct path between ACEs and learning engagement became nonsignificant, suggesting that NEs may played a indirect mediating role. Thus, it is crucial to focus on the early adverse experiences of medical students and improve their maladaptive emotions, which may increase their learning engagement and promote medical education.

Limitations

The following are the main limitations of the study. First, although having strong theoretical underpinnings and validated instruments, this study’s cross-sectional methodology limits drawing conclusions about causality. Establishing a causal relationship requires experimental or longitudinal designs. Second, self-reported measures of ACEs, perceived stress, anxiety, and depression may introduce recall bias and social desirability effects or simplify the complexity of ACEs and NEs without clinical diagnostic verification. Third, considering possible bidirectional interactions, simultaneity bias results from the feedback effect between variables [65]. For example, while perceived stress and depression predict low learning engagement, heightened learning engagement may also ameliorate stress and depression [66]. Finally, the sample is drawn from a single institution, which limits generalizability to other Chinese contexts with varying learning engagement and NES. Future multicenter studies should incorporate stratified national sampling to enhance external validity.

Implications

From a scholarly viewpoint, our research advances the ACEs-engagement discourse by establishing NEs as pivotal mediators, underscoring the necessity of integrating neuroaffective mechanisms into medical education research and providing additional insight for improving students’ learning engagement. Future investigations could employ longitudinal designs to determine causal pathways among ACEs, NEs, and learning engagement, particularly tracking clinical training transitions. From a practical point of view, we advocate University Counseling and Psychological Services should incorporate a brief assessment of ACEs into existing psychological screening protocols. Identifying students with high ACE scores can help universities proactively offer support to those at greater risk of maladjustment. Second, interventions should prioritize enhancing students’ emotional regulation skills. For instance, universities could offer vidence-based workshops focusing on stress/depression-coping strategies, peer-mentoring programs, and regular emotional feedback. Finally, family-school cooperation should foster a supportive current environment that reinforces students’ resilience and self-regulation. ultimately improving their in learning engagement.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that ACEs significantly associated with reduced learning engagement in medical students and that NEs (anxiety, perceived stress, and depression) are associated with increased ACEs. Parallel mediation analyses revealed distinct pathways in which perceived stress and depression function as conventional negative mediators, whereas the relationship between ACEs and learning engagement is positively impacted by anxiety. These findings suggest that medical students with higher ACEs exposure tend to exhibit greater emotional dysregulation, which is negatively associated with learning engagement as mediated by NEs. This study emphasizes how crucial mental health is to learning engagement and demonstrates the connection between NEs in ACEs and learning engagement. Consequently, targeting these domains and implementing trauma-responsive interventions could enhance medical students’ learning engagement, ultimately increasing the quality of medical education.
