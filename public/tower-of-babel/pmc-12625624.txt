Prenatal psychosocial factors and changes in marital satisfaction after childbirth: an online cohort study of pregnant women
Authors: Chika Kubota, Chika Yokoyama, Haruna Irino, Yasue Mitamura, Aiko Okatsu, Satoko Sasagawa, Ayako Kanie, Aiichiro Nakajima, Miyuki Makino, Sayaka Aoyama, Yaeko Kataoka, Masaru Horikoshi, Hironori Kuga, Masaya Ito
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMarital satisfaction is a key determinant of maternal mental health during the perinatal period. Identifying psychosocial factors associated with changes in marital satisfaction can help inform early preventive interventions.MethodsThis longitudinal study examined associations between psychosocial factors measured during pregnancy and changes in marital satisfaction from pregnancy to 52 weeks postpartum. Participants were 752 pregnant women in Japan (mean age = 32.0 ± 4.3 years) who completed online surveys during pregnancy and at 52 weeks postpartum. Marital satisfaction was assessed using the Quality of Marriage Index (QMI), a validated measure of relationship satisfaction, and categorized into three groups based on QMI score differences: improved (≥ 1-point increase), unchanged (0-point difference), and worsened (≥ 1-point decrease). Psychosocial variables included depressive symptoms assessed by the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS), household income, perceived burden of household chores and childcare, parity, and perceived family support. Multinomial logistic regression was used to examine associations between these variables and changes in marital satisfaction.ResultsHigher levels of depressive symptoms during pregnancy, as measured by the EPDS, were significantly associated with worsening marital satisfaction after childbirth (RRR = 2.38, 95% CI: 1.30–4.37, p = 0.005). Lower household income also predicted a decline in satisfaction (RRR = 0.999, 95% CI: 0.999–1.00, p = 0.017). Participants who reported extremely long hours of household chores and childcare were less likely to experience improved satisfaction (RRR = 0.15, 95% CI: 0.04–0.65, p = 0.011). First-time childbirth was negatively associated with improvement (RRR = 0.41, 95% CI: 0.20–0.87, p = 0.019). Higher levels of perceived family support were also associated with less improvement in marital satisfaction (RRR = 0.89, 95% CI: 0.80–0.98, p = 0.017).ConclusionPsychosocial factors during pregnancy, including depressive symptoms, economic hardship, and perceived domestic burden, were significantly associated with subsequent changes in marital satisfaction. These findings underscore the importance of early mental health screening and supportive interventions for expectant couples.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-025-08370-w.Keywords: Postpartum period, Pregnancy, Postpartum depression, Psychosocial support, Marital relations, Parity

Introduction

Pregnancy and childbirth are significant life events that bring profound physical, psychological, and social changes. During this transition, marital relationships often undergo substantial shifts, particularly among primiparous women. Previous research has shown that marital satisfaction tends to decline during the transition to parenthood [1, 2]. The potential impact of marital changes on maternal mental health, quality of life, child development, and overall family functioning has received increasing scholarly attention in recent years.

An expanding body of research has explored the relationship between marital satisfaction and postpartum depression [3]. Given the challenges in objectively assessing marital relationships, many studies rely on self-reported measures of marital satisfaction or related constructs. For example, Małus et al. (2016) reported an association between decreased marital quality and postpartum depression [4]. Boda et al. (2023), in a study of 35 Japanese couples one month postpartum, found that higher Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS) scores were associated with insufficient support from husbands and poor communication skills [5]. Similarly, Yakupova and Liutsko (2021) reported that lower Couple Satisfaction Index scores during the perinatal period predicted higher rates of perinatal depression [6]. Other studies have emphasized the bidirectional nature of these associations: depression in one partner can affect the mental health of the other [7, 8], highlighting the interdependence of partner well-being. These findings suggest that marital satisfaction is critical to perinatal mental health.

Various psychosocial factors have been identified as contributors to changes in marital satisfaction. For example, subjective burden related to housework and childcare [6] and perceived social support [9, 10] have emerged as key predictors. Additionally, research has shown that partners actively participating in housework and childcare tend to help maintain or improve marital satisfaction [11]. Economic conditions also play a role; studies have demonstrated that household income and financial stress can affect marital quality and mental health outcomes [5, 10]. A large-scale longitudinal study of over 80,000 Norwegian mothers found that self-esteem typically declines during pregnancy, increases after childbirth, and gradually decreases again. At the same time, relationship satisfaction drops sharply with the first child and continues to fall more slowly thereafter. That study also revealed a moderate correlation between changes in self-esteem and relationship satisfaction [12]. In the Maternal Anxiety about Infant Development (MARI) Study, both low self-esteem and reduced partner satisfaction were identified as key psychosocial and interpersonal correlates of peripartum anxiety and depressive disorders [13].

Despite these findings, few longitudinal studies have examined changes in marital satisfaction from pregnancy through postpartum, using large, population-based samples. Most existing studies have been conducted in Western countries, with limited evidence from Asia, particularly Japan. Understanding how prenatal psychosocial factors affect postpartum marital satisfaction across different cultural contexts is essential for designing effective and culturally appropriate interventions to support family relationships.

Given this context, the present study aimed to fill this gap by using data from a prospective cohort of pregnant women in Japan. Specifically, we examined changes in marital satisfaction from pregnancy to 52 weeks postpartum and explored how psychosocial factors during pregnancy predicted those changes. By identifying key predictors of postpartum marital satisfaction, this study seeks to inform the development of targeted, evidence-based interventions to support perinatal couples and promote family well-being.

Materials and methods

Study design

This was a longitudinal observational study targeting pregnant women. Participants were recruited from an online panel managed by a commercial internet research company (Rakuten Insight), and psychosocial factors were assessed using self-administered questionnaires at four time points: during pregnancy, and at 4, 26, and 52 weeks postpartum. The present analysis used data collected during pregnancy and at 52 weeks postpartum.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the Ethical Guidelines for Medical and Biological Research Involving Human Subjects issued by the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare of Japan. The protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the National Center of Neurology and Psychiatry (initial approval: January 14, 2021; approval number: A2020-119; final approval: May 12, 2023; approval number: B2023-024).

The study was registered with the UMIN Clinical Trials Registry, which aligns with ICMJE and WHO ICTRP standards (UMIN ID: UMIN000044079; registration date: April 30, 2021).

Rakuten Insight identified eligible participants through screening questions from their online panel. Informed consent was obtained electronically: a study description was displayed on the screen, and participants could proceed to the questionnaire only after clicking the consent checkbox. The explanation included assurances of privacy protection and the right to withdraw.

Data collection was outsourced to an independent research agency following our study protocol. All personal identifiers were removed before the data were transferred to the research team, and the investigators had no access to personally identifiable information. Anonymized data were managed in compliance with applicable laws and regulations and the aforementioned ethical guidelines, with full consideration for participant privacy.

Participants

Recruitment began on July 1, 2021, and ended on March 31, 2024. Eligible participants were pregnant Japanese-speaking women who provided informed consent via the online panel. No exclusion criteria were applied. As of September 2022, Rakuten Insight maintained approximately 2.2 million active panelists, who were required to update their profiles within 12 months of their last login. The company also implements quality control measures by excluding fraudulent respondents through four annual trap-question checks.

Measures

Demographic variables

Demographic data included marital status, household income, educational attainment, and pregnancy and delivery characteristics (e.g., delivery date and method, infant sex, birth weight, NICU admission). Participants also reported the daily hours spent by themselves and their partners on childcare, housework, and paid employment.

The Edinburgh postnatal depression scale (EPDS)

The EPDS is a 10-item self-report scale developed by Cox et al. in 1987 to screen for postpartum depression, with each item rated from 0 to 3 and total scores ranging from 0 to 30. In the original validation study, the cut-off score of 12/13 yielded a sensitivity of 86%, specificity of 78%, and positive predictive value of 73% [14].

The Japanese version, developed by Okano et al. in 1996, demonstrated good reliability and validity, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.78 and significant differences in scores between postpartum depressed and non-depressed women [15]. The suggested cut-off score for postpartum use was 8/9 (sensitivity = 75%, specificity = 93%, PPV = 50%). For use during pregnancy, a study involving 210 women recommended a cut-off of 12/13, with sensitivity and specificity of 90.0% and 92.1%, respectively [16].

In the present study, Cronbach’s alpha (α) during pregnancy was 0.84.

Generalized anxiety disorder 7-item scale (GAD-7)

The GAD-7, developed by Spitzer et al. in 2006, includes seven items rated on a 4-point scale, with total scores ranging from 0 to 21. The original cut-off of 10 yielded a sensitivity of 89% and specificity of 82% [17].

The Japanese version, validated by Doi et al., showed high internal consistency (α = 0.92 in psychiatric patients; α = 0.91 in general adults). Using a cut-off of 10, sensitivity and specificity were 82.1% and 79.2%, respectively, in psychiatric samples [18].

Internal consistency in this study was good (α = 0.88).

Quality marriage index (QMI)

The QMI, developed by Norton in 1983, consists of six items assessing overall marital satisfaction [19]. Five items use a 7-point scale, and one uses a 10-point scale.

In Japan, the scale was translated by Moroi, with each item rated on a 4-point scale from 1 to 4, resulting in a total score ranging from 6 to 24 points [20]. In Japanese perinatal samples, the QMI has demonstrated good to excellent internal consistency (α = 0.87–0.94 for fathers; α = 0.90–0.96 for mothers) [21].

In the present study, internal consistency during pregnancy was excellent (α = 0.97).

Multidimensional scale of perceived social support (MSPSS)

The MSPSS, developed by Zimet et al. in 1988, assesses perceived support from family, friends, and significant others [22]. The subscales consist of three factors: significant other, family, and friends. Higher scores indicate a higher level of perceived social support.

Iwasa created a seven-item version in Japan in 2007 as a shortened version [23]. In a study targeting middle-aged and older adults, the Japanese version of the 12-item MSPSS showed a high positive correlation with the original scale and high consistency in score distribution and gender and age differences, suggesting its reliability and validity.

In the present study, internal consistency during pregnancy was excellent (α = 0.96).

Sense of authenticity scale (SOAS)

Ito and Kodama developed the SOAS in 2008 to measure the sense of authenticity, defined as the feeling of being true to oneself [24]. It consists of seven items rated on a five-point scale, with higher total scores indicating a stronger sense of authenticity. Regarding reliability, the internal consistency among college students was acceptable (α = 0.79). The test-retest reliability was also adequate (r = 0.70, p < 0.01). They confirmed a one-factor structure, supporting the scale’s construct validity. They also reported acceptable reliability (r = 0.70, p < 0.01). The scale correlated positively with self-esteem (r = 0.73, p < 0.01).

Given its reported associations with reduced anxiety and depression, enhanced purpose in life, personal growth, positive social connections, and autonomy, we considered the sense of authenticity measured by this scale to represent a core facet of self-esteem in our analysis.

In this study, internal consistency for data from pregnant women was good (α = 0.86).

Statistical methods

To assess potential attrition bias, we compared baseline characteristics between the 768 participants who completed the 52-week postpartum survey and the 971 participants who dropped out after the pregnancy survey.

We then conducted a multinomial logistic regression analysis to examine factors associated with changes in marital satisfaction (QMI scores) from pregnancy to 52 weeks postpartum. Of the 768 participants who responded at both time points, we excluded 16 individuals (three who started a new relationship, six who experienced separation, and seven who consistently had no partner), as tracking changes in marital satisfaction was not feasible for these cases. As a result, 752 participants with continuous relationships were included in the final analysis.

All questions were mandatory within each survey wave; thus, no item-level missing values were missing. The dependent variable was categorized into three groups based on the difference in total QMI scores between pregnancy and 52 weeks postpartum: a deterioration group (≥ 1-point decrease), an unchanged group (0-point difference), and an improvement group (≥ 1-point increase). The unchanged group served as the reference category. We selected 52 weeks postpartum as the endpoint because, by that time, children’s daily routines are typically stabilized and maternal physical recovery is complete, making it a more valid indicator of sustainable marital satisfaction.

Independent variables were selected based on previous studies of perinatal marital dynamics, as referenced in the Introduction. Variables with variance inflation factor (VIF) values below five were included to avoid multicollinearity. We ensured an event-per-variable (EPV) ratio of ≥ 10 to maintain model stability.

Categorical predictors included parity (primiparous vs. multiparous), the respondent’s time spent on household chores and childcare (categorized as follows: <4 h [low], 4–<8 h [moderate], 8–<16 h [high], 16–<24 h [very high], and ≥ 24 h [extremely high/abnormal]), presence of cohabitants (individual and children, nuclear family, extended family), and child’s gender. Continuous variables included respondent age, household income, MSPSS subscale scores (family and friends), SOAS total score, the respondent’s and her partner’s working hours and educational attainment, and total scores on the EPDS and GAD-7. Partner-related variables (e.g., education and working hours) had slightly smaller sample sizes (N = 1719) than maternal variables (N = 1739), because some participants either did not have a cohabiting partner, were separated, or did not provide information on their partner.

Among these, log transformation was applied only to the EPDS and GAD total scores during pregnancy, as these variables showed positively skewed distributions (EPDS skewness = 1.23, kurtosis = 1.03; GAD skewness = 2.23, kurtosis = 5.53). Although the EPDS distribution was not as extreme as the GAD distribution, both were transformed to maintain analytic consistency and improve model stability. For other continuous measures, the distributions were not perfectly normal but were considered sufficiently close to normality, and thus, raw scores were used. Descriptive statistics, including skewness and kurtosis for all study variables, are presented in Supplementary Table S1.

Time spent on housework and childcare exceeding 24 h per day was treated categorically as an indicator of psychological distress. All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 30.0 (IBM Corp., Tokyo, Japan). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Discussion

Main findings

This study investigated the relationship between psychosocial factors during pregnancy and subsequent changes in marital satisfaction up to 52 weeks postpartum. Our analysis revealed that depressive symptoms during pregnancy significantly predicted a deterioration in marital satisfaction. Additionally, the likelihood ratio tests (Table 3) identified higher depressive symptoms, lower perceived family support, first-time childbirth, and infant sex as significant predictors. In contrast, the multinomial logistic regression analysis (Table 4) indicated that higher depressive symptoms and lower household income predicted deterioration, while primiparity, extreme housework/childcare, and high perceived family support were associated with a lower likelihood of improvement.

Depression and marital satisfaction

Our findings demonstrated that higher levels of depressive symptoms during pregnancy (as measured by the EPDS) significantly predicted a decline in marital satisfaction after childbirth. Previous research has indicated that depression can persist or worsen during the transition to postpartum, with partner conflict identified as a key contributing factor, particularly among women whose depressive symptoms intensify after childbirth [26]. Furthermore, meta-analyses have identified prenatal depression, anxiety, and poor marital quality, exposure to violence, and adverse economic conditions as significant risk factors for postpartum depression [7]. Another meta-analysis has shown a robust association between poor marital quality and postpartum depression [3]. While these studies underscore the impact of marital quality on maternal mental health, our findings suggest the reverse may also be true: depressive symptoms during pregnancy may erode marital satisfaction. These results support the importance of early mental health assessment during pregnancy and the implementation of relationship-focused interventions as part of perinatal care.

Sociodemographic and obstetric factors

Lower household income was associated with decreased marital satisfaction, consistent with previous findings that socioeconomic disadvantage adversely affects both depressive symptoms and relationship quality [27]. These results emphasize the need to account for economic context in perinatal psychosocial support.

Additionally, first-time mothers were significantly less likely to report improvement in marital satisfaction. Previous research has highlighted that becoming parents often alters relationship dynamics [28]. A meta-analysis by Bogdan et al. (2022) reported that marital satisfaction typically declines after the first child’s birth [2]. Our findings align with this pattern, underscoring the importance of supporting first-time parents navigating these relational transitions.

Psychosocial burdens and support systems

In the multinomial logistic regression analysis (Table 4.), participants who reported spending 24 hours daily on housework and childcare were significantly less likely to experience improved marital satisfaction. This finding suggests that the perceived burden may be more significant than the actual time spent. One possible explanation, particularly in Japan, is the influence of internalized beliefs, such as “mothers may hold a sense of mission and accept the role of providing for their family [29]” or prevailing gender role norms, which have been shown in previous research to affect household labor division and relationship satisfaction [30–32]. Further studies are needed to examine these mechanisms directly.

The multinomial logistic regression also showed that participants who reported high levels of perceived family support were less likely to show improvement. One possible explanation is a ceiling effect—those who reported high family support during pregnancy may have already had high marital satisfaction, leaving less room for improvement. Future studies should adjust for baseline marital satisfaction and define clinically meaningful thresholds for change.

Unmeasured and qualitative factors

In the likelihood ratio tests (Table 3), infant sex was significantly associated with marital satisfaction (p = 0.045). However, this association did not remain significant in the multinomial logistic regression analysis (Table 4.). Although we initially considered whether raising a child of the opposite sex might contribute to stress, the adjusted analysis did not support this interpretation.

The model’s explanatory power (Nagelkerke R² = 0.170) was moderate, indicating that multiple factors influence marital satisfaction. Partner-related variables (e.g., education, working hours, childcare time) were insignificant, suggesting that unmeasured qualitative aspects—such as empathy and communication quality—may play essential roles in relational dynamics.

Limitations

First, this study did not account for specific potential confounders, such as the partner’s history of mental illness, changes in employment status after childbirth, and fluctuations in household economic conditions—all of which may influence marital satisfaction.

Second, significant differences in baseline characteristics were observed between the 768 participants who completed the 52-week postpartum survey and the 971 who dropped out, raising concerns about attrition bias. Respondents differed from non-respondents in terms of marital satisfaction during pregnancy, age, working hours, and partner’s educational attainment. These discrepancies may have introduced selection bias and limited the generalizability of the findings. Future research should consider applying statistical methods, such as multiple imputation and propensity score matching, to address attrition bias better.

Third, categorizing participants based on a 1-point change in QMI scores may not capture clinically meaningful differences. Future studies should consider using minimal clinically significant differences (MCID) to define groups or analyzing QMI scores as a continuous variable.

Fourth, the analysis was limited to participants who remained in a relationship with the same partner from pregnancy through 52 weeks postpartum. Six participants who reported separation during this period were excluded. Because the reasons for separation were not assessed, the degree of marital dissatisfaction may have been underestimated, particularly in cases where relationship breakdown resulted from significant conflict or distress.

Finally, this study focused on psychosocial factors during pregnancy as predictors of subsequent changes in marital satisfaction. However, it is also possible that declining marital satisfaction influences the onset or course of postpartum depression, suggesting a bidirectional relationship. As we did not conduct a path analysis or cross-lagged model to examine this possibility, our findings should be interpreted with caution. Future studies should employ structural equation modeling with longitudinal data to clarify these reciprocal associations.

Conclusion

This study identified several psychosocial predictors of changes in marital satisfaction after childbirth. Depression during pregnancy, low household income, primiparity, and high subjective burden from housework and childcare were associated with deterioration or limited improvement in marital satisfaction. These findings highlight the need for integrated perinatal mental health care that incorporates both psychological and relational support for pregnant women and their partners.
