The Strength of Flow Refueling Location Problem Formulations and an Extension to Cyclic Routing
Nagisa Sugishita, Margarida Carvalho, Ribal Atallah

Abstract
The Flow Refueling Location Problem (FRLP) is a stylized model for determining the optimal placement of refueling stations for vehicles with limited travel ranges, such as hydrogen fuel cell vehicles and electric vehicles. A notable extension, the deviation FRLP, accounts for the possibility that drivers may deviate from their preferred routes to refuel or recharge. While solution techniques based on various mathematical programming formulations have been thoroughly explored for this extension, there is a lack of theoretical insights into the relationships and strengths of these formulations. In this work, for the deviation extension, we study two prominent FRLP formulations from the literature and compare their strengths in terms of linear programming (LP) relaxations. We show that the LP relaxation of one formulation yields a bound that is at least as tight as that of the other, which may explain its observed superior performance. Building on these insights, we address a common modeling assumption in the FRLP that requires drivers to use the same paths for their outbound and inbound trips. Specifically, we relax this assumption and introduce the cyclic FRLP, where drivers may use different paths in each direction. We show how existing formulations can be naturally extended to accommodate this setting and describe a branch-and-cut algorithm to solve the problem. We provide numerical experiments highlighting the benefits of such asymmetric routing. For example, in an instance based on the Californian network, the cyclic FRLP serves all demands using 30% fewer facilities than the original FRLP.

Introduction

The Flow Refueling Location Problem (FRLP), introduced by
Kuby and Lim (2005)
, extends flow-capturing location models by
Hodgson (1990)
to address the optimal placement of refueling facilities for vehicles with limited range.
In the model by
Kuby and Lim (2005)
, each demand is associated with an origin, a destination and a flow volume, and is considered served if there are enough refueling facilities to ensure that the drivers can complete their journey with limited-range vehicles. The goal of the FRLP is to maximize the demands that are served.

On the practical level, the growing popularity of hydrogen-powered vehicles and electric vehicles (EVs), as well as governmental incentives in some countries to support their adoption, underscores the relevance of the FRLP (see
Arslan and Karaşan (2016)
and
Kadri et al. (2020)
). Indeed, given the current insufficiency of refueling and charging infrastructure, it is critical to deploy these facilities effectively. In what follows, we use the EVs application to unify our terminology around the FRLP, referring to charging stations for refueling facilities and EVs for limited-range vehicles.

On the methodological side, the literature on the FRLP has mostly focused on integer programming-based approaches.

Kuby and Lim (2005)
introduced a mixed-integer programming (MIP) formulation for the FRLP.
However, its size grows rapidly with the problem scale. To address this,
Capar and Kuby (2012)
proposed a more scalable formulation, which was further improved by
Capar et al. (2013)
. Building on this,
Arslan and Karaşan (2016)
applied Benders decomposition to accelerate the solution process.
Kadri et al. (2020)
extended the FRLP to a multi-stage stochastic setting, using a decomposition approach closely related to that of
Arslan and Karaşan (2016)
, with an identical structure for the separation subproblem.

The previously discussed works focus on the classical FRLP, where drivers are assumed to strictly follow predetermined (e.g., shortest) paths without deviation. However, in practice, drivers may deviate slightly from their usual routes to access charging stations. To capture this behavior,
Kim and Kuby (2012)
introduced the deviation FRLP, which allows for multiple feasible paths per origin-destination (OD) pair. Their formulation requires enumerating all paths and, for each path, all combinations of station placements that ensure traversability. This leads to rapid growth in model size as the instance scale increases.

Yıldız et al. (2016)
proposed an improved formulation of the deviation FRLP that eliminates the need to enumerate combinations of station placements.
Leveraging techniques from
Capar et al. (2013)
, they express path traversability using set-covering constraints.
Additionally, they introduced a path-segment-based formulation, solved via a branch-and-price algorithm.
While this formulation, together with a branch-and-price approach, yields better performance, the set-covering formulation offers greater flexibility and modeling capabilities for incorporating additional constraints, as it directly uses binary variables indicating station presence at each node.
Independently,
Arslan et al. (2019)
and
Göpfert and Bock (2019)
proposed another MIP formulation, using set-covering constraints to express the condition that each demand is served, rather than expressing the condition that each path is traversable, as in
Yıldız et al. (2016)
.
This approach eliminates the need for path enumeration and, when solved with a branch-and-cut algorithm, significantly outperforms previous methods, successfully handling instances of practical size.

On the theoretical front, the comparative strengths of the formulations for the deviation FRLP remain underexplored.
We aim to address that gap.
To be more specific, we compare the MIP formulation developed independently by
Arslan et al. (2019)
and
Göpfert and Bock (2019)
with the formulation by
Yıldız et al. (2016)
based on the set-covering constraints.
By analyzing these formulations, we aim to understand the reasons behind the superior computational performance of the state-of-the-art formulation.
We note that although
Yıldız et al. (2016)
proposed two formulations, the set-covering formulation and path-segment formulation, we concentrate on the set-covering one, excluding the path-segment formulation due to its limited flexibility for integrating additional constraints.

In addition, we propose an extension of the deviation FRLP, which we call the cyclic FRLP, to support more flexible routing.
All prior work mentioned above assumes route symmetry, i.e., drivers must use the same path in both directions.
While simplifying the problem, this assumption may lead to suboptimal outcomes in more realistic settings.
Furthermore, this assumption requires the underlying transportation network to be undirected.
In the cyclic FRLP, drivers are allowed to choose different outbound and inbound routes, enabling more efficient utilization of transportation infrastructure. Notably, the formulation of
Arslan et al. (2019)
and
Göpfert and Bock (2019)
can be naturally extended to accommodate this generalization.
We develop a solution method for the cyclic FRLP by introducing a labeling algorithm that searches for a cycle (round-trip route) that can be repeatedly traversed by vehicles with limited range.
A summary of the literature and our contribution to the FRLP—aside from the theoretical analysis of formulation strength—is provided in Table
1
.

Using the Californian network developed by
Arslan et al. (2014)
, we demonstrate the difference between the deviation and cyclic FRLP.
Furthermore, to complement our experiments and highlight the value of the cyclic FRLP, we consider a variant that minimizes the number of charging stations while covering a specified portion or all of the demand. This variant has been studied in the literature under the symmetric routing assumption (e.g.,
Capar et al. (2013)
and
Kınay et al. (2021)
) and can be readily addressed by adapting formulations such as those by
Arslan et al. (2019)
and
Göpfert and Bock (2019)
. Including this variant allows us to directly compare the added benefit of incorporating cyclic routes against the classical FRLP with symmetric assumptions.
For example, on one of the instances based on the Californian network by
Arslan et al. (2014)
, the cyclic FRLP is able to serve all demands with 30% fewer charging stations than the deviation FRLP.

Section

In the classical and deviation FRLP, a demand is served if there is a path that is traversable repeatedly without a vehicle running out of battery. Drivers are required to use the same path for both outbound and inbound trips.

As discussed by
Capar et al. (2013)
, given a path and the travel range of a vehicle, one can construct a family
C\subset\mathcal{P}(N)
of node sets such that the path is traversable repeatedly with the vehicle if and only if
\sum_{j\in S}x_{j}\geq 1
for all
S\in C
, where
\mathcal{P}(N)
is the power set of
N
.
In words, the path is traversable if and only if each
S\in C
contains a node with a charging station.
Thus, for each
q\in Q
and

, we can find a family
C_{r}^{\text{disagg}}\subset\mathcal{P}(N)
of node sets such that
r
is traversable if and only if
\sum_{j\in S}x_{j}\geq 1
for all
S\in C_{r}^{\text{disagg}}
.
Demand
q\in Q
is served if and only if there exists
r\in R_{q}
such that
\sum_{j\in S}x_{j}\geq 1
for all
S\in C_{r}^{\text{disagg}}
.
Thus, we can obtain the following formulation of the deviation FRLP:

\displaystyle\max_{x\in X,z\in\{0,1\}^{|R|}}\

\displaystyle\sum_{q\in Q}\sum_{r\in R_{q}}f_{q}z_{r}

(1)

s.t.

\displaystyle\sum_{r\in R_{q}}z_{r}\leq 1,

\displaystyle\qquad\forall q\in Q,

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in S}x_{j}\geq z_{r},

\displaystyle\qquad\forall q\in Q,r\in R_{q},S\in C_{r}^{\text{disagg}},

where
R=\cup_{q\in Q}R_{q}
and, for each
q\in Q
, the auxiliary variable

is 1 if path

is used and 0 otherwise.

1

As discussed in the introduction,

We do not consider the path-segment formulation since it is less flexible than those considered in this section.

Proposition 4
.

Let
C\subset\mathcal{P}(N)
be a family of node sets and

C^{\prime}=\{S^{\prime}\in C:S=S^{\prime}\ \text{or}\ S\not\subset S^{\prime},\ \forall S\in C\}.

(i)

It holds
\bar{P}(C)=\bar{P}(C^{\prime})
.

(ii)

Let
S\in C^{\prime}
, then
there exists
(x,y)\in\{0,1\}^{|N|+1}
such that
(x,y)\in\bar{P}(C\backslash\{S\})\backslash\bar{P}(C)
.

(iii)

Let

\sum_{j\in S}x_{j}\geq y_{q}
is facet-defining for
\mathrm{conv}(\bar{P}(C)\cap\{0,1\}^{|N|+1})
.

Proof.

(i)

It is clear that
\bar{P}(C)\subset\bar{P}(C^{\prime})

, so we will show
\bar{P}(C)\supset\bar{P}(C^{\prime})

To derive a contradiction, suppose that
\bar{P}(C)\not\supset\bar{P}(C^{\prime})

(x,y)\in\bar{P}(C^{\prime})\backslash\bar{P}(C)

Since

and
0\leq y\leq 1
.
Furthermore, since
(x,y)\not\in\bar{P}(C)
, there exists
S\in C
such that
\sum_{j\in S}x_{j}<y
.
However, we can find
S^{\prime}\in C^{\prime}
such that
S^{\prime}\subset S
, for which we have
\sum_{j\in S^{\prime}}x_{j}\leq\sum_{j\in S}x_{j}<y
.
This contradicts the assumption that
(x,y)\in\bar{P}(C^{\prime})
.
Thus, it follows that
\bar{P}(C)\supset\bar{P}(C^{\prime})
.

(ii)

Let
S
be any node set in

.
Define

1&\text{otherwise.}\\
\end{cases}

Then, for any

3
Strength of Formulations of the Deviation FRLP

In Section
2
, two formulations of the deviation FRLP were presented.
In this section, we compare their strengths in terms of the tightness of the bounds given by their LP relaxations.

To facilitate the discussion, we rewrite formulations (
1
) and (
2
).
Following the arguments of
Kuby and Lim (2005)
, it can be shown that the optimal objective value of (
1
) remains unchanged if the integrality constraints on

are relaxed.
Thus, we can rewrite formulation (
1
) as

\max_{x\in X}\sum_{q\in Q}v^{\text{disagg}}_{q}(x),

(5)

where for each
q\in Q
and
x\in[0,1]^{|N|}
, we have

1
1

1

The vector
z_{q}\in[0,1]^{|R_{q}|}
in

is not the same as

in (
1
), but both reflect the traversability of the corresponding paths.

v^{\text{disagg}}_{q}(x)=\max_{z_{q}\in[0,1]^{|R_{q}|}}\left\{\sum_{r\in R_{q}}f_{q}z_{qr}:\sum_{r\in R_{q}}z_{qr}\leq 1,\sum_{j\in S}x_{j}\geq z_{qr},\ \ \forall r\in R_{q},S\in C_{r}^{\text{disagg}}\right\}.

(6)

We have
x\in X
is optimal for (
1
) if and only if it is optimal for (
5
).
Note that being the value function of an LP,
v^{\text{disagg}}_{q}
is concave for each
q\in Q
.

Similarly, it can be shown that the optimal objective value does not change if we relax the integrality constraint of
y_{q}
for
q\in Q
in formulation (

Therefore, we can rewrite formulation (
2
) as

\max_{x\in X}\sum_{q\in Q}v^{\text{agg}}_{q}(x),

q\in Q
and

, we have

v^{\text{agg}}_{q}(x)=\max_{y_{q}\in[0,1]}\left\{f_{q}y_{q}:\sum_{j\in S}x_{j}\geq y_{q},\ \ \forall S\in C_{q}^{\text{agg}}\right\}.

Again,
x\in X
is optimal for (
2
) if and only if it is optimal for (
7
).
Note that
v^{\text{agg}}_{q}
is a concave function for each
q\in Q
.

For any

and

, we have

However, functions

Let us define

K_{q}=\left\{v:\text{$v$ is a concave function on $[0,1]^{|N|}$}\ \text{and}\ v^{\text{agg}}_{q}(x^{\prime})=v(x^{\prime}),\ \ \forall x^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{|N|}\right\}.

.
Now, let

Being the point-wise infimum of concave functions,

is concave and thus

.
The following lemma gives an alternative definition of

.

Lemma 8
.

Let us denote
\{0,1\}^{|N|}=\{\beta_{i}:i=1,2,\ldots,2^{|N|}\}
, that is,
\beta_{i}\in\{0,1\}^{|N|}
for each
i
and
\beta_{i}\not=\beta_{j}
for any
i\not=j
.
Then, we have

v^{\text{tight}}_{q}(x)=\max_{\alpha}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{2^{|N|}}\alpha_{i}v^{\text{agg}}_{q}(\beta_{i}):\sum_{i=1}^{2^{|N|}}\alpha_{i}\beta_{i}=x,\sum_{i=1}^{2^{|N|}}\alpha_{i}=1,\alpha\geq 0\right\}.

Proof.

Let us write

v_{q}(x)=\max_{\alpha}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{2^{|N|}}\alpha_{i}v^{\text{agg}}_{q}(\beta_{i}):\sum_{i=1}^{2^{|N|}}\alpha_{i}\beta_{i}=x,\sum_{i=1}^{2^{|N|}}\alpha_{i}=1,\alpha\geq 0\right\},

where
v_{q}
is polyhedral and concave since it is the value function of an LP.
Furthermore,
v_{q}(x)=v^{\text{agg}}_{q}(x)
for any
x\in\{0,1\}^{|N|}
.
We show that for any
v_{q}^{\prime}\in K_{q}
, we have
v_{q}\leq v_{q}^{\prime}
, which implies that
v^{\text{tight}}_{q}=v_{q}
.
Fix any

.
To derive a contradiction, suppose there exists
x^{\prime}\in[0,1]^{|N|}
such that
v_{q}(x^{\prime})>v_{q}^{\prime}(x^{\prime})
.
Let
\alpha^{\prime}
be the solution of the LP in the definition in

given

.
In particular, it follows that

\sum_{i=1}^{2^{|N|}}\alpha_{i}^{\prime}\beta_{i}=x^{\prime}.

We have

\displaystyle v_{q}^{\prime}(x^{\prime})<v_{q}(x^{\prime})=\sum_{i=1}^{2^{|N|}}\alpha_{i}^{\prime}v^{\text{agg}}_{q}(\beta_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{2^{|N|}}\alpha_{i}^{\prime}v_{q}^{\prime}(\beta_{i}),

Therefore, we have

In particular, Lemma

.

The set

consists of concave functions that model the amount of served demand corresponding to

The tightest function in

Proposition 9

Let
q\in Q
and

(11)

(i)

\displaystyle\max_{\alpha}\left\{f_{1}\sum_{i\in I_{2}}\alpha_{i}:\sum_{i\in I_{2}}\alpha_{i}\left\|\beta_{i}\right\|_{1}\leq\|x\|_{1},\sum_{i=1}^{2^{|N|}}\alpha_{i}=1,\alpha\geq 0\right\}

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\max_{\alpha}\left\{f_{1}\sum_{i\in I_{2}}\alpha_{i}:n\sum_{i\in I_{2}}\alpha_{i}\leq 6,\sum_{i=1}^{2^{|N|}}\alpha_{i}=1,\alpha\geq 0\right\}.

Therefore, it follows that for
x\in\bar{X}

v^{\text{tight}}_{1}(x)\leq\frac{6}{n}f_{1}.

Thus,

\gamma\max_{x\in\bar{X}}\sum_{q\in Q}v^{\text{tight}}_{q}(x)\leq\frac{n}{6}\max_{x\in\bar{X}}\sum_{q\in Q}v^{\text{tight}}_{q}(x)\leq f_{1}=\max_{x\in\bar{X}}\sum_{q\in Q}v^{\text{agg}}_{q}(x).

Cyclic FRLP

4.1
Formulations

In Sections
2
and
3
, we studied the FRLP under the standard setting considered in the literature (e.g.,
Arslan et al. (2019)
and
Göpfert and Bock (2019)
), where it is assumed that drivers use the same path in both directions, from the origin to the destination and back.
An OD pair is thus considered served if drivers can traverse the path repeatedly.
Furthermore, this symmetric routing requires all roads to be undirected.
However, in many practical situations, it may be natural to consider the possibility that drivers use different paths for their outbound and return trips.
In this setting, an OD path is considered served if one can repeat a cycle from the origin to the destination and back, without running out of battery.
We call this extension of the FRLP, which allows such flexibility, as the
cyclic FRLP
;
see Figure
5
for an illustration.
In contrast, we refer to the deviation FRLP (including the classic FRLP), where paths are symmetric, as the
original FRLP
.
In this section, we develop a formulation and solution method for the cyclic FRLP.

4.2
Solution Method

Arslan et al. (2019)
and
Göpfert and Bock (2019)
solve formulation (
2
) for the original FRLP using branch-and-cut algorithms.
In this section, we demonstrate how to adapt the method proposed by
Arslan et al. (2019)
to solve the cyclic FRLP using the same formulation.
Among the variants proposed in their work, we focus on the one that yields the best performance in their experiments.

In a branch-and-cut algorithm, formulation (
2
) is initially considered without constraint (
2b
), and a standard branch-and-bound algorithm is applied.
Each time the branch-and-bound algorithm finds a candidate integer solution
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
, the algorithm checks whether any constraint (
2b
) is violated.
Specifically, it searches for
q\in Q
and
S\in C_{q}^{\text{agg}}
such that the corresponding constraint is not satisfied at
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
.
If a violated constraint is found, it is added to the model, and the current solution is discarded.
Otherwise, the candidate solution is feasible for the full problem.

While the scheme of the branch-and-cut algorithm outlined above is quite generic, the task of finding violated constraints for a given integer solution is typically problem-specific, as it must be done efficiently.
We now describe this separation procedure.
Let
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
be a candidate integer solution for which we aim to identify a violated constraint (
2b
) or its feasibility.
Fix
q\in Q
such that
y_{q}^{\prime}=1

(if
y_{q}^{\prime}=0
, constraint (
2b
) is satisfied for all
S\in C_{q}^{\text{agg}}
).
Define
S^{\prime}=\{j:x_{j}^{\prime}=0\}
, the set of nodes where no charging station is installed in the candidate solution.
We first check if there exists a cycle in
R_{q}
traversable repeatedly, assuming we only install charging stations at nodes in
N\setminus S^{\prime}
.
If such a cycle exists, then the constraint is satisfied for any
S\in C_{q}^{\text{agg}}
.
Otherwise, the constraint with
S=S^{\prime}
is violated.

In light of Proposition
4
, it is desirable to find a minimal node set that violates constraint (
2b
).
To this end, we iteratively attempt to remove nodes from
S^{\prime}
.
For each
j\in S^{\prime}
, we check whether there exists a traversable cycle in
R_{q}
using the modified node set
S^{\prime}\setminus\{j\}
(i.e., we search for a traversable cycle assuming we install charging stations at nodes in
N\setminus(S^{\prime}\setminus\{j\})
).
If no such cycle exists, we can replace
S^{\prime}
with
S^{\prime}\setminus\{j\}
.
The final (reduced)

The branch-and-cut approach and the separation procedure described above follow those of
Arslan et al. (2019)
.
However, in the deviation FRLP, identifying a violated constraint corresponds to finding a traversable path, whereas in the cyclic FRLP, it involves finding a traversable cycle.
The symmetric path assumption in the deviation FRLP (i.e., that drivers must use the same path for both outbound and inbound trips) considerably simplifies the search for a traversable path.
It can be shown that a path is repeatedly traversable if and only if a driver can leave the origin with a half-charged battery and reach the destination with at least 50% of the battery remaining.
This condition reduces the search for a feasible path to a shortest path problem on a transformed network (see
Arslan et al. (2014)
for details).
In contrast, it is more involved to find a traversable cycle from the origin to the destination and back.
For instance, it is not known in advance how much energy can be assumed at the origin or how much must remain upon arrival at the destination. Hence, we propose a new procedure to find a traversable cycle.

In the cyclic FRLP, the existence of a traversable cycle in
R_{q}
can be tested using a labeling algorithm (see
Irnich and Desaulniers (2005)
).
For example, suppose
R_{q}
includes all cycles of length at most
\tau
that start at the origin, visit the destination, and return to the origin.
The graph is first modified by adding a sink node
s
connected from the origin via a zero-length edge.
Each label has the form
(\delta_{\text{charge}},\delta_{\text{dest}},l_{\text{start}},l_{\text{charge}},\gamma_{\text{end}})
, where

•

\delta_{\text{charge}}
is 1 if and only if we have visited at least one charging station and 0 otherwise,

•

\delta_{\text{dest}}
is 1 if and only if we have visited the destination and 0 otherwise,

•

l_{\text{start}}
is the total distance traveled from the origin,

•

l_{\text{charge}}
is the distance since the last charging station (equal to
l_{\text{start}}
if
\delta_{\text{charge}}=0
),

•

\gamma_{\text{end}}
is the amount of charge (in distance units) needed at the origin before completing the cycle (equal to
\infty
if
\delta_{\text{charge}}=0
).

We initialize the origin with a label
(0,0,0,0,\infty)
if there are no charging stations at the origin or otherwise
(1,0,0,0,0)
.
A label
(\delta_{\text{charge}},\delta_{\text{dest}},l_{\text{start}},l_{\text{charge}},\gamma_{\text{end}})
at node
j_{1}
can be extended to node
j_{2}\not=s
if and only if
l_{\text{start}}+l_{j_{1}j_{2}}\leq\tau
and
l_{\text{charge}}+l_{j_{1}j_{2}}\leq d
where
l_{j_{1}j_{2}}
is the length of the edge from node
j_{1}
to node
j_{2}
and
d
is the vehicle’s travel range.
Similarly, a label
(\delta_{\text{charge}},\delta_{\text{dest}},l_{\text{start}},l_{\text{charge}},\gamma_{\text{end}})
at the origin can be extended to the sink
s
if and only if
l_{\text{charge}}+\gamma_{\text{end}}\leq d
.
The label at node
j_{2}
resulting from an extension is given by the resource extension function
f
.
It is defined by
f:(\delta_{\text{charge}},\delta_{\text{dest}},l_{\text{start}},l_{\text{charge}},\gamma_{\text{end}})\mapsto(\delta_{\text{charge}}^{\prime},\delta_{\text{dest}}^{\prime},l_{\text{start}}^{\prime},l_{\text{charge}}^{\prime},\gamma_{\text{end}}^{\prime})
, where

\displaystyle\delta_{\text{charge}}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0,&\delta_{\text{charge}}=0\ \text{and}\ x_{j_{2}}=0\ (\text{i.e., there is no charging stations at $j_{2}$}),\\
1,&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

\displaystyle\delta_{\text{dest}}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0,&\delta_{\text{dest}}=0\ \text{and}\ j_{2}\text{ is not the destination},\\
1,&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

\displaystyle l_{\text{start}}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=l_{\text{start}}+l_{j_{1}j_{2}},

\displaystyle l_{\text{charge}}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}l_{\text{charge}}+l_{j_{1}j_{2}},&x_{j_{2}}=0,\\
0,&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

\displaystyle\gamma_{\text{end}}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}l_{\text{start}}+l_{j_{1}j_{2}},&\delta_{\text{charge}}=0\ \text{and}\ x_{j_{2}}=1,\\
\gamma_{\text{end}},&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

To determine the order in which labels are extended, we use a scoring rule.
Let
\text{dist}(j_{1},j_{2})
denote the shortest distance from node
j_{1}
to node
j_{2}
, and let
o
and
d
represent the origin and destination, respectively.
Given a label
(\delta_{\text{charge}},\delta_{\text{dest}},l_{\text{start}},l_{\text{charge}},\gamma_{\text{end}})
attached to a node
j
, its score is computed as
\text{dist}(j,d)+\text{dist}(d,o)
if
\delta_{\text{dest}}=0
, and as
\text{dist}(j,o)
otherwise.
In other words, the score represents the length of the shortest cycle that could be formed by extending the label, ignoring travel range or distance limits.
The label with the smallest score is selected for extension.

A traversable cycle in
R_{q}
exists if and only if the sink node
s
receives a label during the labeling process. When
s
is labeled, the condition
l_{\text{charge}}+\gamma_{\text{end}}\leq d
ensures that the sum of the distance from the first charger after departure and the distance from the last charger before returning is at most the vehicle’s travel range, thereby guaranteeing that the cycle can be repeated. Conversely, if a traversable cycle exists, there must be a corresponding sequence of labels that will be identified during the execution of the labeling algorithm.

Conclusions

We studied the FRLP, an optimization problem for determining the optimal placement of refueling stations for vehicles with limited travel ranges, such as EV charging stations.
Specifically, we examined two formulations from the literature, one developed independently by
Arslan et al. (2019)
and
Göpfert and Bock (2019)
, and another one proposed by
Göpfert and Bock (2019)
.
The former formulation is used in the state-of-the-art algorithm, which is based on a branch-and-cut approach.
We showed that this formulation is tighter than the latter and that the relative difference in bounds obtained from their LP relaxation can be arbitrarily large.
This tightness may partially explain the superior performance of the current state-of-the-art solution method.

We also addressed a common routing assumption used in the literature of the FRLP, which requires drivers to use the same path for their outbound and inbound trips.
To relax this, we introduced a variant called the cyclic FRLP, where drivers may take different paths on their outbound and return trips.
We demonstrated how existing formulations for the original FRLP can be naturally extended to model this variant and presented its solution method.
Furthermore, we presented a labeling algorithm for finding a traversable cycle, enabling the use of a branch-and-cut approach to solve the cyclic FRLP.
Through a small example and numerical experiments on the Californian network from
Arslan et al. (2014)
, we highlighted the significant impact of correctly modeling driver behavior on solution quality.

The cyclic FRLP is more flexible than the original FRLP, which requires drivers to use the same path for both outbound and return trips.
It relaxes this assumption by allowing different paths in each direction, but still assumes that the same cycle is used for every round trip.
A natural extension is to allow drivers to repeat an
n
-sequence of cycles with some predetermined number
n
, which could model medium-distance travel where refueling or recharging is not needed after every trip.
The solution method, including the separation procedure based on the labeling algorithm, can be readily adapted to handle this extension.
However, evaluating its practical relevance is left for future work.
