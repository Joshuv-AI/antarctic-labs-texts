PlQC based highly sensitive and reproducible novel SERS active substrate for biomolecule detection with high specificity
Authors: Sibashish Chakraborty, Rishabh Vij, Richa Goel, Bhanu Pratap Singh, Kunaal Dhingra, Venu Gopal Achanta, Satish Kumar Dubey
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractSurface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS) is a powerful tool for biomolecule sensing. When combined with a broadband plasmonic structure, label-free, highly sensitive detection of specific molecules is possible. It is non-invasive, sensitive, fast, and can be used for in-situ analysis, unlike enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay, fluorescence immunoassay, and radioimmunoassay. However, one of the challenges is to have an active SERS substrate that is uniform, sensitive, and specific to molecules of interest. In this work, we report plasmonic quasicrystal (PlQC) as a highly sensitive (enhancement factor ≈ 1014), uniform, reproducible, and stable (concerning time and ambient conditions) SERS active substrate. Herein, we present the label-free sensing of standard cotinine (up to 1 ng/mL), the ideal biomarker for nicotine exposure due to its long lifetime compared to nicotine. In addition, up to 1 nanogram level of cotinine has also been detected in synthetic urine and saliva employing PlQC as a SERS-active substrate. Introduction

Raman scattering is an inelastic process where the scattered photon (wL) frequency gets shifted by a frequency (wM) corresponding to the molecular vibrational energies1. Although Raman spectroscopy has a small scattering cross-section, it is commonly utilized for molecular characterization since these vibrational states are like fingerprints of the molecules. Additional applications of Raman spectroscopy emerged as methods to improve Raman signals became available2. For example, Plasmon-mediated hot spots and local field enhancement led to the development of surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS) and tip-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (TERS)3. SERS is defined as a significant increase in the Raman cross-section of a molecule when put near metallic surfaces4. Supplemented by non-invasive sampling, low sample preparation time and cost, high spectral resolution, and the capacity of Raman spectroscopy to identify minute structural changes, SERS is a very sensitive, effective, and selective technique. This resulted in applications that showed great potential in a variety of sectors, such as chemistry5, food safety6, drug detection7, narco-analysis8, explosive detection9, medical diagnostics, and health care10.

Enhancement in the Raman signal is fundamentally explained using two mechanisms: chemical (CM) and electromagnetic (EM) enhancements. Resonances in the SERS cross-section are caused by the charge transfer states, often referred to as hybridized states, that arise from the chemical interaction of molecules with the metal surface. EM is a consequence of the strong localization of light to sub-wavelength dimensions (hot spots) caused by plasmonic nanostructures/nanoparticles. The EM enhancement factor (EF) of the Raman signal in a plasmonic system is a product of enhancement in local field intensity of excitation source at wl frequency (MLF =|ELF(wl)/Ein(wl)|2) and the enhancement in the Raman scattering cross-section at Raman frequency of wR (MLF =|ER(wR)/Ein(wR)|2). Thus, EF is maximum when the local field enhancement is high and resonant Raman excitation is carried out (wl = wR). A SERS probe that enhances the Raman signal at multiple vibrational levels, retaining both the resonance positions and signal strengths (peak heights), is best for label-free detection of molecules through EM, CM, or a combination of both. Much research has been done on SERS spectroscopy, leading to the development of many SERS-active substrates with regular and ordered nanostructures or chemically produced nanoparticles/composites using various materials and manufacturing techniques11–17. The focus is to develop a sensitive, stable, reproducible, and uniform SERS platform to develop cost-effective SERS-based sensors capable of in-field rapid detection by handheld Raman devices18–20. SERS is a potential single-molecule detection technique that has advantages over other techniques and has applications like in human DNA sequence studies, pharmacokinetics to monitor drug molecules in living cells, and where averaging in an ensemble is to be avoided16,21,22.

An essential requirement of health diagnostics is to provide a rapid, sensitive, and precise method. Various analytical tools, such as mass spectrometry, fluorescence spectroscopy, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), fluorescence immunoassay (FIA), and radioimmunoassay (RIA), have been developed to characterize the structure of biomolecules, as well as for biosensing, bio-diagnosis, and biomedical imaging. These tools are based on different physical, chemical, and biological phenomena. Nevertheless, none of these methods currently can meet all the demands of contemporary biomedicine due to their time-consuming nature, relatively poor sensitivity, and/or the need for specialized conditions that are far from the field setting. SERS has become a versatile technique providing sensitivity and structural information needed due to the notable advancements in optics, laser technology, portable Raman spectrometers, and nanofabrication. The motivation for this work is to create a SERS platform that can accurately detect cotinine in smokers, passive smokers, and non-smokers. Additionally, we intend to construct a prototype that can be easily transitioned from the laboratory to the market or field (Lab to fab) for practical use.

While the localized plasmon resonances in nanoparticles and propagating plasmons in periodic structures like 1-D or 2-D gratings host discrete resonances, a reproducible broadband plasmonic structure is possible by randomly distributing different scattering elements in a periodic lattice23 or a quasi-periodic lattice24. A plasmonic quasicrystal (PlQC) conforming to 5-fold rotation symmetry was demonstrated for broadband, dispersionless, and incident polarization-independent transmission enhancement24 and harmonic generation due to strong local field enhancement25. PlQCs were also shown to enhance the magneto-optical properties over multiple bands26. Along with these unique properties, the dense near-field hotspots of PlQC (Supplementary information Fig. S1 under S1.1.) would be helpful in uniform, large area, launch angle and polarization-independent SERS substrate. An 8-fold PlQC was demonstrated as a SERS-based sensor for molecular monolayer of pMA (p-mercaptoaniline) as a probe molecule and achieved a SERS enhancement factor of 2.10 × 10727. Rippa et al.28, demonstrated the potential of a dodecagonal PlQC in detecting femtomolar chemisorbed bacteriophages. In this study, we present a label-free, highly sensitive, and reproducible 5-fold PlQC that can detect up to attomolar concentrations of Rhodamine 6G (R6G) and does not require functionalization of the substrate for biomolecules like Cotinine (a residue of nicotine used as a biomarker for tobacco abstinence) and urea. Our study would be very useful in developing epidemiological studies on nicotine addiction among smokers, non-smokers, passive smokers, and tobacco consumers.

We make use of the principal component analysis to demonstrate label-free detection of Cotinine (down to 1 ng/mL concentration) in both synthetic urine and synthetic saliva (Sigma Aldrich). We also establish reproducibility of Raman spectra, uniformity over large area, specificity, and sensitivity of PlQC. Thus the results reported in this work may open up new avenues for the development of label-free, broadband, dispersion-less single biomolecule detection. It can also be applied to clinical samples in an efficient manner to enable quick and precise bio-diagnostics of various diseases.

Methods

Fabrication of quasi-periodic PlQC

Materials and chemicals

Sourced from Sigma-Aldrich, the following materials were utilized: deionized water (DI water), cotinine, R6G, synthetic urine, and synthetic saliva. All the solutions, i.e., the different concentrations of R6G and cotinine, were made with DI water by serial dilution.

Design and fabrication of PlQC

The PlQC structure is designed by a modified dual grid method in which the lattice coordinates corresponding to the 5-fold rotation symmetry are identified from the intersection points of five p/5 rotation and superposition steps of an array of lines separated by a constant distance (inherent lattice constant of 600 nm)24. The GDSII file for the electron beam lithography was generated with the coordinates of the 5-fold rotation symmetry.

To fabricate PlQC, a 100 nm gold film was deposited on a 0.5 mm thick quartz substrate using direct current (DC) magnetron sputtering. We used controlled Argon flow with 25 sccm and 8 × 10−3 mbar pressure. An applied voltage of 0.40 kV, resulting in a current of 0.12 A, was used to generate plasma. The deposition rate with these parameters was about 0.8 nm per second. PMMA AR632.12 electron beam resist from ALLResist GmbH was spin-coated on top of the gold film at 6000 rpm for 60 s. Raith eLine was used to pattern the designed quasicrystal for electron beam lithography (EBL). The subsequent development process included employing a mixture of MIBK and IPA at a ratio of 1:3 for 60 s, followed by a separate treatment with IPA for 30 s. Subsequently, Reactive Ion Etching (RIE) was used to transfer the pattern from the resist material to the gold surface. This was achieved by using argon (Ar) plasma, generated by applying 138 W of RF power under a pressure of 0.5 Pa. In 14 min, 100 nm of gold layer was etched. Then, oxygen plasma, generated using 80 W RF power at 1 Pa pressure, was used to etch the remaining resist over the sample surface. The fabricated PlQC has a lattice parameter of 600 nm, and the average hole diameter is 92 ± 6.7 nm. The morphology of PlQC was characterized by Field-Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy (FESEM, TESCAN), Atomic Force Microscope (AFM, Park Systems, Korea). Figure 1a represents the FESEM micrographs of well-structured and highly uniform distribution of air holes in gold film conforming to 5-fold PlQC. The presence of several concentric circles indicates that the structure has a hierarchical framework. Figure 1b represents the 3D AFM image of PlQC. AFM measurement has been performed in tapping mode within a scan area of 10 μm× 10 μm. The chemical composition of the PlQC was examined using Energy Dispersive Spectroscopy (EDS) technology from Oxford Instruments, UK to verify the film’s purity (Supplementary information S1.2).

(a) FESEM images of PlQC at different magnifications, and (b) 3D AFM image of PlQC.

SERS sample preparation

To prepare cotinine samples in synthetic urine, we started with standard synthetic urine and tagged it with cotinine at 1 µg/mL and 1 ng/mL. This solution was mechanically stirred and kept in ambient conditions. A 20 µL solution was dropcasted on the PlQC sample and then air-dried at room temperature to form a thin layer (to facilitate adsorption).

The Raman spectra of cotinine in synthetic saliva were collected from samples prepared using the same procedure as synthetic urine. The Renishaw Raman setup with 785 nm wavelength laser source, a 50X objective, and a spectrometer with 1200 grooves per millimeter grating was used for recording Raman spectra. Further details of the setup are in supplementary information S1.3.

Results and discussions

The plasmon-mediated enhanced transmission is measured by normalizing the spectrum recorded through patterned and unpatterned metal samples. Around 15 to 100 times enhancement in transmission was observed depending on the fabrication optimization, especially the RIE process to get vertical air holes in metal film.

The acceptance of a SERS substrate depends on three main factors: sensitivity measured in terms of enhancement factor and the smallest concentration that can be measured, uniformity over the entire sample surface and in time, and specificity for molecules. In the following, we present results to show sensitivity, uniformity, and specificity.

Ultra-trace detection

For ultra-trace detection, we have used 40 µL each of different concentrations of Rhodamine 6G dye (R6G). R6G was drop cast on the PlQC structure, and SERS spectra (Fig. 2a) were recorded for different concentrations varying between 10−6 M to 10−18 M (in steps of 10−3 M concentration). The characteristic peaks of R6G, such as 1195 cm−1 (C-H in-plane bending), 1314 cm−1 (N-H in-plane vibration), 1366 cm−1 (aromatic C-C stretching), 1511 cm−1 (aromatic C-C stretching) are distinctly visible, which establishes PlQC as highly sensitive SERS substrate. Figure 2b-d show the principal component analysis of the four peaks in terms of the relative peak heights (ratio of the ith peak height divided by the height of the dominant peak at 1366 cm−1). The presence of multiple SERS peaks within the broad plasmon band offers specificity to any molecule with sensitivity down to a single molecule. Intensities of different Raman lines of R6G are denoted I1 (1195 cm−1), I2 (1314 cm−1), I3 (1366 cm−1), and I4 (1511 cm−1), respectively.

(a) SERS spectra of R6G recorded at concentrations ranging from µM to aM on PlQC along with blank SERS spectra of PlQC, (b-d) the peak height ratio of different Raman lines with respect to the dominant peak at 1366 cm−1 for different concentrations. Straight lines in red, blue, and green represent the mean values, respectively. This shows the robustness of the PlQC substrate towards specificity and sensitivity down to single molecule level (aM concentration).

Sensitivity

The enhancement factor (EF) is defined as EF=, where IRS and ISERS are the intensities of Raman signal and SERS signal of the same Raman mode; NRS and NSERS are the numbers of probe molecules contributing to Raman and SERS scattering, respectively. While the SERS signal for R6G was recorded down to aM concentrations, thus reaching a single-molecule regime, the EF is calculated for 1366 cm-1 Raman mode for the concentration of 10–18 M of R6G to be ≈ 1014 (details provided in Supplementary, S1.4). The high sensitivity observed is due to the enhanced local field on top of the PlQC structure. Detailed finite difference time domain (FDTD) based numerical simulations with 785 nm wavelength are presented in Supplement S1.1.

The enhancement factor, specificity from the multiple fingerprint peaks within plasmon enhanced wavelength region, and sensitivity down to aM concentrations make PlQCs interesting for sensing. Uniformity of the SERS substrate and specificity are also important for biosensing applications. In the following we show the detection of biomolecule cotinine using PlQC as a SERS substrate.

Detection of cotinine

After establishing the capability of PlQC as a SERS substrate with high selectivity and sensitivity (to single molecule), we will present its use in biomolecule detection with no labels and no functionalization. Nicotine addiction is a matter of public health concern and has been recognized as a contributing factor for several illnesses, such as cardiovascular diseases, lung and other malignancies, chronic respiratory disorders, stroke, and pregnancy-related difficulties29.

Nicotine, an alkaloid mostly found in tobacco, is an amine consisting of rings of pyridine and pyrrolidine. Cotinine is a key result of nicotine metabolism after it has been adsorbed, along with a few additional byproducts, such as 3 HC, etc., Cotinine is identified as an important biomarker of tobacco exposure, as it provides the most reliable information on the consumption of other tobacco products. The level of cotinine in different bodily fluids, such as urine, saliva, or serum, is closely correlated with the extent of nicotine consumption, which can be used for quantification of tobacco exposure in both active and passive smokers. The standard cut-off concentration that distinguishes a smoker from a non-smoker is 2.47 ng/mL of urine30,31.

Cotinine is a superior biomarker of tobacco smoke compared to nicotine due to two main reasons. Firstly, 72% of nicotine is metabolized into cotinine, making it a more reliable indicator. Secondly, cotinine has a much longer half-life of 17 h, in contrast to nicotine’s half-life of just 3 h31. The Raman spectra of Cotinine (Fig. 3) show two intense peaks centered at 1032 cm−1 and 1052 cm−1, attributed to the symmetrical “breathing” and the trigonal deformation of the pyridine moiety, respectively30. Detection of nano-gram concentrations of cotinine was reported by Han et al.32. using silver nanoparticles as a SERS-active substrate, while Yang et al.33. in artificial saliva by a multivariate curve resolution approach. Furthermore, Huang et al.34 used Ag nanoparticles to detect cotinine at 1.76 ng/mL in synthetic urine samples. Using a 3D hybrid nanostructure as a SERS-active substrate, Bei et al.35. identified 40 µg/mL quantity of cotinine in an aqueous solution. By employing SERS for simultaneous multiplexed cotinine measurement, Alharbi et al.36 reported the detection of 10−8 M cotinine concentration. We have used the PlQC structure as a SERS substrate for accurate determination of cotinine in saliva and urine at two different concentrations: 1 ng/mL and 1 µg/mL.

SERS spectra of cotinine (1 ng/mL) on PlQC. The inset shows a zoomed-in view of the Cotinine peaks.

Detection of cotinine in synthetic urine and synthetic saliva

The liver oxidizes cotinine, which is found in blood, saliva, and urine, among other body fluids37. During tobacco smoking, its level fluctuates relatively, to a lesser extent than other tobacco products38, which makes it a more reliable biomarker for measuring the extent of tobacco exposure39. The collection procedure involving either urine/saliva is non-invasive, which implies that it can be used to develop do-it-yourself (DIY) kits available off-the-shelf40,41. In addition, due to the higher solubility of cotinine in water, the concentration of cotinine in the saliva is higher by 15–40%, making detecting cotinine easier and more reliable in saliva than in blood. This offers an additional advantage of non-invasive testing, an easy and well-tolerated collection procedure when multiple samples are required over a limited period.

Figure 4a and b show the two characteristic Raman peaks of Cotinine at 1032 cm−1 and 1052 cm−1 in synthetic urine. Figure 4c shows the Raman spectrum of synthetic urine for reference taken on the same sample. Figure 4d and e show the two prominent cotinine peaks at 1032 cm−1 and 1052 cm−1 for ng and µg concentrations in synthetic saliva. Figure 4f shows the characteristic Raman peaks of saliva at 1020 cm−1, 1071 cm−1, and 1134 cm−1. These results show that sensitivity even below 1 ng is possible for biomolecule cotinine with PlQC as a SERS active substrate.

Uniformity

The uniformity of the SERS signal across the substrate surface is essential as it is used to assess the reproducibility and repeatability of the SERS signal in the fabricated substrate.

In this investigation, cotinine solution at 1 ng/mL concentration was drop-casted on PlQC and left to dry. The SERS signal was then collected after 3 h at 15 randomly selected positions throughout the substrate (1 × 1 cm2) to evaluate the uniformity of PlQC as an efficient SERS-active substrate. Figure 5a shows the SERS signal at randomly chosen locations, showing characteristic peaks at 1032 cm−1 and 1052 cm−1.

(a) SERS spectra of cotinine (1 ng/mL concentration) at 15 different spatial locations over the PlQC, (b) peak height of the 1032 cm−1 Raman line at different spatial positions, (c) peak height of the 1052 cm−1 Raman line at different spatial positions, and (d) The distribution of I1/I2 for standard cotinine with mean value marked as a red line.

The intensities of the two Raman lines at 1032 cm−1 and 1052 cm−1 are plotted for the 15 different spatial positions on the sample in Fig. 5b and c, respectively. The relative standard deviation (RSD) of 8.11% and 7.39% for the two peaks shows an excellent uniformity of the PlQC as SERS substrate agreeing with the near-field profile and simulated electric field profile of PlQC shown in Supplement Figure S1 and Figure S2. In addition, 2D Raman mapping of PlQC has been performed by targeting the 1366 cm−1 Raman mode of picomolar concentration of R6G, explained in supplementary information S.1.5.

Multiple resonances corresponding to the fingerprints of the analyte could be studied to achieve specificity for a specific analyte among a larger matrix of analytes. In this work, we calculated the ratio of the two prominent cotinine peaks (i.e., 1032 cm−1 and 1052 cm−1) to alleviate this issue. The intensities have been marked as I1 and I2 for peaks 1032 cm−1 and 1052 cm−1, respectively. The distribution of I1/I2 for standard cotinine is 1.1 ± 0.06 from the measurement data at 15 locations on the surface. Figure 5d illustrates the ratio I1/I2 distribution for standard cotinine, with a mean value of 1.1. The mean value is shown by a red line.

This ratio is found to be higher for cotinine in synthetic urine/saliva than standard cotinine because of the contribution of the urine/saliva signal to the 1032 cm−1 peak. Without any correction, the ratio was 1.83 ± 0.13. However, by subtracting the baseline and the standard synthetic urine/saliva spectrum from the compound samples of cotinine in synthetic urine/saliva, the ratios of I1/I2 were found to be in the range of 1.5 ± 0.24 and using inbuilt Renishaw software, the ratios of I1/I2 were found to be in the range of 1.3 ± 0.17 (detailed description has been shown in supplementary information table S1). Thus, cotinine (at any concentration) in any analyte can be confirmed if I1/I2 ~ 1.3 ± 0.24. This is especially relevant for detecting cotinine in clinical samples (such as blood, urine, and saliva) and avoiding false-positive or false-negative results.

The high sensitivity of PlQC makes it a suitable candidate for use in ultra-high-sensitivity sensors. Most of such high-sensitivity SERS active substrates in literature constitute chemically synthesized plasmonic nanoparticles/nanostructures or lithographically fabricated structures with ~ 10–20 nm nanogaps. These require stringent fabrication requirements, and only a fraction of the substrate (hot spot region) contributes to the signal. However, the PlQC structure, in the form of an array of air holes in a gold film, is prepared with standard EBL procedure and can be replicated easily using nanoimprint lithography. This ensures that such patterns can be mass-produced and used for commercial applications.

Conclusion

To summarize, PlQC is shown to be a highly sensitive, uniform, reproducible SERS substrate (described in supplementary information S.1.6.), and stable SERS probe. Detection of biomolecules like cotinine makes it suitable for various applications including narco-analysis, bio-diagnostics, estimation of adulteration of food/water, etc., in trace quantities. PlQC samples are reproducible, which ensures the repeatability of Raman spectra of the analyte under investigation. The proposed substrate detected up to aM concentration of R6G with an enhancement factor ≈ 1014. We have also demonstrated the detection of cotinine in both synthetic urine and saliva (down to 1 ng/mL) and that the ratio of the intensity of two characteristic peaks of cotinine, if in the range of 1.3 ± 0.24, indicates the presence of cotinine in a matrix. The limit of detection of cotinine has also been investigated in this study, and it is found to be 1.27 ng/mL (details have been explained in supplementary information S.1.7.), which is lower than the clinical range of cotinine in saliva/urine. The data collected with synthetic saliva and urine suggest that the PlQC can be effectively used for clinical samples for rapid and accurate bio-diagnostics of different diseases. In the process of producing epidemiological studies on nicotine addiction among smokers, non-smokers, passive smokers, and tobacco consumers, our research would be of great assistance.
