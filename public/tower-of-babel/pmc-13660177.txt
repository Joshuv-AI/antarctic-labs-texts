Computed tomography findings of a retroperitoneal chronic expanding hematoma with multiple hemangiomas: A case report and literature review
Authors: Takayoshi Shinya, Kanako Yoshida, Takeshi Iwasa, Mana Shimomura, Yoichi Otomi, Ryoei Minato, Junya Furukawa, Yoshimi Bando, Masafumi Harada
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chronic expanding hematoma (CEH) is an uncommon lesion that gradually increases in size over time. A hemangioma is a benign vascular tumor that may involve the skin, mucosa, or visceral organs; however, involvement of retroperitoneal organs is rare. Herein, we describe the case of a 56-year-old woman with a CEH adjacent to the right ovary and multiple congenital hemangiomas involving the skin, subcutaneous tissue, oral cavity, and liver. CEH was completely resected because it was suspected to be the cause of anemia and because an ovarian tumor or soft tissue sarcoma could not be excluded. In this patient, multiple hemangiomas demonstrated various enhancement patterns and calcifications on multiphasic contrast-enhanced computed tomography, and tumor size fluctuations were observed over an 8-year follow-up period. We discuss the imaging features of CEH in a patient with multiple congenital hemangiomas, with a brief review of the literature.

Introduction

In general, hematomas are spontaneously reabsorbed and rarely cause serious problems. However, in rare cases, they may progressively enlarge and become encapsulated and fibrotic, resulting in chronic expanding hematoma (CEH) [1]. It may be misdiagnosed as a malignant soft tissue tumor owing to its large size, progressive enlargement, and heterogeneous enhancement on imaging [2]. A hemangioma is a benign vascular tumor that may involve the skin, mucosa, or visceral organs, such as the liver and spleen. Involvement of retroperitoneal organs, including the kidneys, pancreas, and adrenal glands, is rare [[4], [5], [6], [7], [8], [9], [10]]. Multiple congenital hemangiomas involving the abdominal cavity and retroperitoneum are extremely rare, and only limited reports have been published in the English literature [11,12].

The imaging characteristics of CEH associated with retroperitoneal hemangioma have not been well described, particularly on multiphasic multidetector computed tomography (MDCT), in the English literature [9]. Moreover, no previous studies have reported an 8-year radiological follow-up of multiple hemangiomas.

This case report describes a retroperitoneal hemangioma presenting as a CEH and characterizes its CT findings over an 8-year follow-up period, along with a review of the relevant literature.

Case Report

A 56-year-old woman was referred to our hospital because multiple tumors were suspected to be the source of hemorrhage. She had been diagnosed with subcutaneous cavernous hemangiomas involving the skin and soft tissues throughout her body since birth. At a previous hospital, CT revealed multiple tumors with heterogeneous density in the abdominal cavity and retroperitoneum, and the patient had been under observation for 3 years. Three years after the initial CT examination, follow-up abdominopelvic CT was performed at the previous hospital to evaluate iron deficiency anemia. Slight interval changes in tumor size were observed, with both enlargement and reduction raising suspicion of intratumoral hemorrhage. The patient was subsequently referred to our hospital for further evaluation and management. At the initial visit to our hospital, the anemia had already improved following treatment with oral iron supplementation and blood transfusions. No symptoms such as abdominal pain, diarrhea, or fever, were reported. Clinical laboratory test results and tumor marker levels, including carcinoembryonic antigen, carbohydrate antigen 19-9, and cancer antigen 125, were within normal limits. Post-contrast MDCT revealed multiple abdominal and retroperitoneal tumors comprising heterogeneous components, including solid components, cystic areas, and calcifications (Fig. 1). The tumors showed various enhancement patterns on MDCT. The largest tumor was a 170 mm circumscribed cystic tumor adjacent to the right ovary, and the mean CT values of the solid component with calcification were 40, 53, 58, and 78 HU on the unenhanced scan and in the arterial, portal, and late phases, respectively (Fig. 2). No lymph node involvement or distant metastases were identified. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) could not be performed because of claustrophobia. Multiple hemangiomas with various morphologies were considered the primary diagnosis; however, neurinomas, lymphangiomas, ovarian tumors, and soft tissue sarcomas were also included in the differential diagnosis. Anemia recurred 3 months later, suggesting repeated intratumoral hemorrhage. At that time, we retrospectively recognized a change in the density of the largest lesion adjacent to the right ovary during the 3-year follow-up period, which was suspected to reflect subclinical repeated intratumoral bleeding (Fig. 3). Moreover, the tumor measured 160 mm in diameter on CT images obtained at the previous hospital 3 years before the patient’s first visit to our hospital (not shown) and had gradually increased in size to 170 mm. Therefore, ovarian tumors and soft tissue sarcomas could not be excluded. Macroscopically, the mass was located adjacent to the right ovary and exhibited a smooth, whitish surface. On sectioning, it was identified as a cystic lesion containing brown, friable material. Histopathological examination revealed dense fibrosis with calcification of the cystic wall. The cystic contents consisted of old blood components with fibrin deposition and degenerative changes. Clusters of irregularly structured blood vessels were observed surrounding the lesion, suggesting an underlying hemangioma. No abnormal findings were identified in the right ovary or fallopian tubes. The final diagnosis was CEH associated with a hemangioma.

The patient was followed up with CT and laboratory tests for 5 years after surgery. No recurrence of the CEH adjacent to the right ovary or severe anemia was observed. Most of the hemangiomas decreased in size, whereas only the cystic lesion adjacent to the pancreas increased in size. We plan to continue follow-up with unenhanced low-dose CT because the multiple tumors have the potential to increase in size and cause intratumoral hemorrhage in the future.

Discussion

Although multiple imaging modalities are available for characterizing CEH, an optimal diagnostic method has yet to be established. Dynamic CT typically demonstrates rim enhancement in the arterial phase, along with a heterogeneous distribution of enhancement, reflecting the presence of granulation tissue with vascular channels predominantly located within the hematoma capsule [2,3,13,14]. In some clinical settings, differentiating CEH from sarcomas using MDCT can be difficult. In cases of CEH [13,14], dynamic CT typically shows heterogeneous contrast enhancement in the arterial phase and a heterogeneous distribution of strongly enhanced areas in the delayed phase. Sarcomas may present as heterogeneous cystic masses with hypervascularity [15]. In addition, calcification in CEH tends to be located at the periphery of the mass, although both sarcomas and CEH may exhibit faint, coarse calcifications within the tumors [[14], [15], [16]]. Enhancement patterns and the distribution of intratumoral calcification may be important findings for differentiating CEH from sarcomas. However, the detailed enhancement patterns of CEH and malignant retroperitoneal tumors, including sarcomas, have not yet been established, and further MDCT studies are needed to assess the diagnostic value of quantification of CT measurements in these tumors. Other differential diagnoses include ovarian tumors, schwannomas, cavernous hemangiomas, lymphangiomas, and lymphatic-venous malformations (LVMs). Although accurate differentiation among CEH, ovarian tumors, schwannomas with intratumoral hemorrhage, and cavernous hemangiomas can be difficult, the characteristic contrast enhancement pattern of CEH may provide an important clue to its diagnosis on MDCT. Cystic lymphangiomas and LVMs are benign neoplasms characterized by prominent thin-walled cystic lesions and rarely contain calcified walls [[17], [18], [19]]. They can also demonstrate increased attenuation on unenhanced CT in cases of intratumoral infection or hemorrhage. In the present patient, the CEH adjacent to the right ovary did not exhibit rim enhancement of the thickening wall containing calcifications in the arterial phase. However, multiphasic CT revealed irregular peripheral enhancement with weak progressive enhancement. This finding was considered to reflect a hemangioma surrounding the CEH. In a previous report of intrathoracic CEH [20], signal intensity within the lesion changed over time, reflecting temporal variations in hemoglobin levels caused by repeated intralesional hemorrhage. A mixed signal intensity on T2-weighted images, referred to as the mosaic sign, has been described [3,20]. MRI revealed a mass containing fluid and solid components in a patient with CEH in the left thorax [16]. In a patient with CEH involving the musculoskeletal system, MRI showed a mass with heterogeneous signal intensity on both T1- and T2-weighted images and a peripheral rim of low signal intensity, reflecting central zones of fluid collection containing fresh and altered blood and a wall composed of collagenous fibrous tissue [21]. On diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), intrapelvic CEH showed two different signal intensities, which corresponded to the pathological features of fresh and altered blood components [22]. Therefore, MRI may be useful for characterizing the internal components of CEH in various organs. Because MRI was not performed preoperatively in this patient, changes in signal intensity within the lesion over time, could not be evaluated; such changes may be caused by repeated intralesional hemorrhage. However, non-contrast CT demonstrated changes in attenuation within the CEH adjacent to the right ovary during the follow-up period, and these findings may also reflect repeated episodes of intratumoral hemorrhage. The self-perpetuating expansion of CEH is thought to result from an inflammatory response to the fibrin matrix and the cellular breakdown products of leukocytes, erythrocytes, and hemoglobin. These factors activate the inflammatory process within the hematoma and cause repeated exudation or bleeding from capillaries in the granulation tissue within hematoma [2,23]. This mechanism can result in repeated hemorrhage, organization of hematoma, fibrosis, the development of calcifications, fluctuations in CT attenuation, and temporal changes in contrast enhancement. Because the tumor adjacent to the right ovary exhibited an atypical enhancement pattern for CEH, it was difficult to establish an accurate preoperative diagnosis of CEH and rule out hemorrhagic neoplasms. However, we were able to identify the absence of a tumor-feeding artery and the hypovascularity of the tumor, which helped us plan the operative strategy through multidisciplinary collaboration. In addition, a recent study demonstrated that preoperative vascular phenotyping with Doppler US and dual-energy CT (DECT) is a noninvasive method for assessing CD31-associated angiogenic activity, an endothelial marker that provides a histological correlate of tumor vascularity [24]. In patients with CEH, the diagnostic utility of Doppler US and DECT remains unclear, and further investigation is needed to establish their clinical value in the diagnosis of CEH.

Retroperitoneal hemangiomas are typically well-defined lesions with round or lobular margins [8]. Phleboliths are calcified nodules that can be regarded as a suggestive feature of hemangiomas [25]. Matsui et al. reported that hemangiomas typically demonstrate significant enhancement during the arterial phase on CT imaging [9]. By contrast, Khazaal et al. reported that nonparenchymal hemangiomas in the abdomen more commonly demonstrate progressive enhancement [8]. In the present patient, the pathologically confirmed hemangioma adjacent to the CEH demonstrated weak progressive enhancement on postcontrast multiphasic CT. By contrast, multiple suspected hemangiomas located in the pleura, mesentery, and retroperitoneal space exhibited variable enhancement patterns, including hypervascular enhancement. Variations in the proportions of neovascularity, arteriovenous shunting, thrombosis, hemorrhage, fibrous tissue, and inflammatory cell infiltration within the solid component may result in diverse enhancement patterns on postcontrast multiphasic CT [5,26]. High vascularity, arteriovenous shunting, and inflammatory cell infiltration within the solid component may result in a more hypervascular appearance of the hemangiomas. In contrast, intratumoral thrombosis, hemorrhage, and fibrous tissue may result in a more hypovascular appearance than that of parenchymal hemangiomas in the abdomen.

Conclusion

CEH remains difficult to diagnose radiologically. In the present patient, malignancy could not be excluded, and surgical resection was performed before a definitive diagnosis of the retroperitoneal mass was established. Recognition of changes in attenuation within the retroperitoneal mass, which may reflect repeated hemorrhage, may assist radiologists in suggesting a diagnosis of CEH. Furthermore, the characteristic CT features of retroperitoneal cavernous hemangiomas have not been fully characterized. In the present patient, cavernous hemangiomas in the retroperitoneum and peritoneum demonstrated various enhancement patterns on multiphasic postcontrast CT, with CEH formation within a tumor during the follow-up period.
