Deep learning-based CT model for non-invasive prediction of tertiary lymphoid structures in pancreatic cancer: a multicenter study with prospective validation in an immunochemotherapy cohort
Authors: Haopeng Yu, Xiaoying Li, Xuan Cheng, Yan Deng, Yuqi Wang, Yu Li, Bingjie Liu, Zixing Huang, Dan Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) remains one of the most lethal malignancies. Tertiary lymphoid structures (TLS) have emerged as key components of the tumor immune microenvironment, associated with improved survival and enhanced immunotherapy response. However, non-invasive detection of TLS remains a significant clinical challenge.ObjectiveThis multicenter study aimed to develop and validate a deep learning-based CT model to noninvasively predict TLS status and evaluate its prognostic and predictive value across surgical, non-surgical, and prospective immunochemotherapy cohorts.Materials and methodsWe enrolled 223 surgically resected patients with PDAC from two centers for model development and 82 non-surgical patients for clinical validation. A ResNet50-based deep learning network (DLN) was trained to predict TLS status from preoperative contrast-enhanced CT. The locked model was evaluated in 44 patients from a prospective immunochemotherapy trial (ChiCTR2000032293), with pathology-anchored verification, outcome assessment, and spatial immune profiling via cyclic multiplex tyramide signal amplification.ResultsThe DLN achieved area under the curves (AUCs) of 0.987, 0.937, and 0.853 in training, internal, and external validation cohorts. In the non-surgical cohort (n=82), DLN-predicted TLS-positive patients had significantly longer overall survival (OS; median 19 vs 7 months; HR=3.611, p<0.0001). In the prospective cohort, pathology-anchored verification yielded an AUC of 0.929 (specificity=1.000). DLN-high patients demonstrated superior objective response rate (90.0% vs 26.5%, p=0.001), progression-free survival (PFS) (median 13.1 vs 5.3 months, p<0.001), and OS (median 17.6 vs 8.7 months, p<0.001). The DLN score independently predicted OS (HR=0.052, p<0.001) and PFS (HR=0.079, p<0.001) in multivariable analysis. The DLN score was orthogonal to programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression (rho=0.011, p=0.946) but correlated with dendritic cell-T helper cell spatial interactions (p=0.037) and T helper-B cell spatial co-localization (rho=0.307, p=0.043)—two hallmarks of functional TLS architecture—and inversely with tumor cell density (rho=−0.399, p=0.007).ConclusionThis DLN-based CT model reliably predicts TLS status in PDAC with prospectively validated prognostic and predictive value in immunochemotherapy, biological grounding in TLS-associated spatial immune architecture, and orthogonal predictive value to PD-L1, supporting a complementary biomarker strategy for immunotherapy patient selection.Keywords: Adenocarcinoma, Immunotherapy, Tumor microenvironment - TME, Computed tomography, Biomarker

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

We developed a deep learning model using standard contrast-enhanced CT that accurately predicts TLS status and prospectively validated it in a registered immunochemotherapy trial (ChiCTR2000032293), demonstrating for the first time that this non-invasive imaging biomarker independently predicts therapeutic response (objective response rate: 90.0% vs 26.5%), survival (overall survival HR=0.052), and correlates with TLS-associated dendritic cell-T helper cell spatial immune interactions and reduced tumor cell density, while being orthogonal to programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression.

This model offers a scalable, non-invasive method to assess TLS-associated immune architecture from routine clinical CT scans, enabling immunotherapy patient selection and clinical trial stratification for all patients with pancreatic cancer regardless of surgical eligibility. Introduction

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) continues to rank among the most lethal malignancies, with a dismal 5-year survival rate of approximately 13%.1 This unfavorable prognosis stems primarily from late diagnosis, aggressive disease progression, and inherent resistance to conventional therapeutic approaches.2 Recent advancements in tumor immunology have illuminated the critical role of tertiary lymphoid structures (TLS)—ectopic lymphoid aggregates developing within non-lymphoid tissue—in modulating the tumor immune microenvironment.3 4 These structures consistently associate with enhanced antitumor immunity and improved clinical outcomes across various cancer types.5–8 In PDAC, mature TLS containing germinal centers and high endothelial venules correlate with prolonged survival and increased responsiveness to immune checkpoint inhibitors.9–11 These findings underscore TLS significance as critical niches for adaptive immunity, though their preoperative evaluation remains an unmet clinical need.

Currently, TLS evaluation relies predominantly on histopathological analysis of resected specimens.12 This approach is invasive, time-consuming, and unsuitable for guiding preoperative therapeutic strategies.13 Crucially, approximately 80–85% of patients with PDAC present with locally advanced or metastatic disease at diagnosis and are ineligible for surgical resection, meaning that histopathology-based TLS assessment is unavailable for the vast majority of patients. Postoperative immunohistochemistry or multiplex fluorescence staining, while informative, cannot be applied to inoperable patients or those undergoing neoadjuvant therapy. Moreover, the spatial heterogeneity of TLSs—such as the differential prognostic impacts of intratumoral versus stromal TLSs—complicates standardized quantification.14 15 For instance, TLSs in inflammatory zones of hepatocellular carcinoma have been paradoxically linked to increased recurrence risk, highlighting the need for non-invasive, whole-tumor characterization.16 17 These limitations emphasize the urgency of developing preoperative imaging biomarkers to assess TLS status and optimize treatment planning.

Recent artificial intelligence advancements, particularly deep learning methodologies, have revolutionized medical imaging data analysis. Unlike traditional radiomics approaches that primarily depend on handcrafted feature extraction, deep learning-based methods can automatically identify complex, high-dimensional image patterns without predefined human assumptions. This automation not only reduces observer dependency compared with subjective radiological assessments but also enables a more comprehensive and reproducible analysis of imaging data. Artificial intelligence-driven approaches have demonstrated remarkable potential across a wide range of clinical applications. For example, deep learning models have been successfully implemented in lung cancer screening, breast cancer detection, and colorectal cancer risk stratification, achieving diagnostic accuracies comparable to or exceeding those of experienced radiologists.18–23 In pancreatic cancer, deep learning has started to gain attention, with studies exploring its use in tumor detection, prognosis prediction, and treatment response evaluation.24–29 While prior studies have linked TLS to specific imaging features across multiple cancer types—including CT-based radiomics predicting TLS in hepatocellular carcinoma,30–32 MRI radiomics for TLS in breast cancer33 and intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma,34 and CT radiomics for TLS in lung adenocarcinoma35 36—similar research in PDAC is notably lacking.

The current study aimed to develop and validate a deep learning-based CT imaging model for the non-invasive prediction of TLS status in patients with PDAC and to evaluate its prognostic value across surgical and non-surgical cohorts. Critically, we further sought to prospectively validate the locked model in an independent cohort of patients with advanced or recurrent PDAC receiving anti-programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1)-based immunochemotherapy, integrating pathology-anchored verification, clinical outcome assessment, and spatial immune profiling to establish a closed-loop evidence framework linking imaging features to immune architecture to clinical outcomes.

Materials and methods

Study design and data collection

This study comprised two phases: (1) retrospective model development and validation using surgical cohorts and (2) prospective validation in an independent immunochemotherapy cohort.

Retrospective surgical cohorts (model development)

We enrolled 163 patients from Center 1 (West China Hospital) and 60 patients from Center 2 (West China Tianfu Hospital) between January 2018 and March 2022. Eligibility for inclusion required: (1) histopathological confirmation of PDAC via surgically resected specimens and (2) availability of preoperative contrast-enhanced CT (CECT) scans within 1-month prior to surgery. Exclusion criteria included: (1) previous antitumor therapy (eg, neoadjuvant treatment) before surgery, (2) poor-quality CECT images (due to motion artifacts or incomplete tumor coverage), (3) unavailability or insufficiency of tissue samples for TLS evaluation, or (4) incomplete clinical records. Subjects from Center 1 were allocated randomly to either the training set (TS, n=114) or the internal validation set (IVS, n=49) in a proportion of 7:3, whereas the external validation set (EVS) was composed entirely of patients recruited from Center 2 (n=60).

Clinical validation set

For the clinical validation set (CVS), 82 patients with pathologically confirmed PDAC were enrolled from Center 3 (Shangjin Nanfu Hospital) between May 2020 and December 2022. Eligibility included: (1) pathological confirmation of PDAC via biopsy and (2) availability of pretreatment CECT scans performed within 1 month of starting treatment. Exclusion criteria included: (1) prior radical surgery, (2) poor-quality CECT images, and (3) incomplete clinical records. Because these patients did not undergo surgical resection, histopathological TLS status could not be determined; instead, deep learning network (DLN)-based TLS prediction was applied to stratify patients for survival analysis.

Prospective immunochemotherapy cohort

To prospectively validate the clinical utility of the DLN model in the context of immunotherapy, we applied the locked model to a subset of patients enrolled in a registered phase Ib/II clinical trial (ChiCTR2000032293) evaluating an anti-PD-1 antibody combined with gemcitabine plus nab-paclitaxel as first-line treatment for locally advanced or metastatic PDAC at Center 1 (West China Hospital). Key findings of the parent trial included a confirmed objective response rate (ORR) of 33.3%, median progression-free survival (PFS) of 5.6 months, and median overall survival (OS) of 8.9 months, with a manageable safety profile consistent with the known toxicity profiles of the individual agents. Full details have been reported separately.37 Between April 2020 and September 2022, 72 patients were enrolled in the trial. Among them, 44 had evaluable pretreatment CECT scans acquired within 1-month prior to treatment initiation and constituted the full analysis set (FAS) for imaging-based validation.

The retrospective and prospective cohorts were entirely non-overlapping populations, as confirmed by cross-referencing unique patient identifiers (details in online supplemental text 4).

Among the 44 patients in the FAS, 20 had undergone prior curative pancreatectomy before disease recurrence and had available formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) surgical resection specimens with histopathologically confirmed TLS status, constituting the pathology-anchored set (PAS, n=20). The remaining 24 patients were diagnosed via biopsy without prior surgical resection. All 44 patients underwent comprehensive clinical outcome assessment and cyclic multiplex tyramide signal amplification (CmTSA)-based spatial immune profiling on baseline pretreatment FFPE tissue specimens (surgical resection specimens for PAS patients; core needle biopsy specimens for non-surgical patients).

The application of the DLN model to this immunochemotherapy cohort was designed as an exploratory post hoc analysis; the original trial protocol did not prespecify CT-based TLS prediction as a study endpoint. However, the model and its cut-off were locked prior to application, and no parameter optimization was performed on the prospective data. The cut-off of 0.570 was determined by maximizing Youden’s Index for discrimination of pathologically confirmed TLS status and was fixed before application to the prospective cohort. The same cut-off was used across the TS, IVS, EVS, PAS, and FAS. Specifically, “locked” indicates that: (1) model weights were frozen after retrospective model development and were not fine-tuned, retrained, or recalibrated using prospective data; (2) the fixed cut-off of 0.570 was applied without modification; and (3) no post hoc threshold optimization was performed in the PAS or FAS. This locked-model approach ensures the integrity of prospective validation.

Histological staining and TLS identification

TLS identification in surgical cohorts (TS, IVS, EVS, and PAS)

TLS presence was assessed on paired whole-slide H&E and CmTSA images by two senior pathologists blinded to clinical outcomes (detailed staining and identification protocols in online supplemental text 1). TLS calls required organized lymphoid aggregates with spatially co-localized CD3+T cells and CD20+B cells.10 38–40 All TLSs were classified as intratumoral TLS (iTLS) when located within the panCK-defined tumor parenchyma. Representative images are shown in online supplemental figure 2.

TLS identification in the pathology-anchored set

In the PAS (n=20), TLS status was determined using the same protocol applied to FFPE surgical resection specimens obtained prior to disease recurrence, with a customized seven-marker CmTSA panel (CD4, CD8, CD20, CD11c, CD68, PD-L1, panCK). 6 patients were classified as TLS-positive and 14 as TLS-negative.

Image acquisition, preprocessing, and tumor segmentation

CECT scans were acquired using different scanner models at multiple participating institutions. To maintain consistency across the multicenter datasets, a uniform preprocessing protocol was applied to all CECT images. Two radiologists (YL and YD), with 5 and 7 years of experience in abdominal imaging, respectively, manually performed three-dimensional segmentation of the tumor regions using ITK-SNAP software (V.3.8.0, http://www.itksnap.org/). Subsequently, the segmented volumes of interest (VOIs) were evaluated by two senior radiologists (HY and ZH), who reached a consensus to resolve any inconsistencies. These segmentation and quality assurance steps were carried out on a center-by-center basis. CECT images were acquired using three scanner models across centers: Brilliance 64 (Philips Medical Systems), SOMATOM Definition AS+ (128-slice; Siemens Healthcare), and Somatom Definition Flash (dual-source; Siemens Healthcare). Key acquisition parameters were harmonized (tube voltage 120 kV, slice thickness 2–5 mm, reconstruction interval 2 mm). Portal venous phase images were used for analysis. Preprocessing included abdominal windowing (level 50 Hounsfield units (HU), width 400 HU), resampling to isotropic voxel spacing, and intensity normalization to ImageNet mean and SD. A peritumoral margin of approximately 3.5–4.0 mm (5-pixel expansion from the tumor boundary) was included in the input patches. Scanner distribution across centers is reported in online supplemental table 4. Additional methodological details can be found in online supplemental text 2. The reporting of this study followed the STARD 2015 guidelines for diagnostic accuracy studies. In a post hoc timing assessment of 20 randomly selected cases conducted during manuscript revision, the median total end-to-end processing time was 19.0 min per case (IQR 16.8–21.9), of which manual segmentation accounted for a median of 14.4 min (IQR 12.9–16.7). The sampling and timing procedures are detailed in online supplemental text 2.3.

Development of a deep learning model

We developed a DLN model based on the ResNet50 architecture to predict TLS status in PDAC. The model incorporated 50 convolutional layers with residual connections (skip connections), facilitating efficient feature extraction from CT images and mitigating gradient vanishing during training. The ResNet50 backbone was chosen for its residual connections that facilitate effective gradient flow in deep networks, its robust transferable representations from ImageNet pretraining, and its moderate depth, which is well-suited to the sample size constraints of clinical imaging datasets. Tumor VOIs, extracted from preoperative CECT scans, were fed into the model, which output the probability of TLS positivity for each patient. The resulting imaging features were passed through fully connected layers for final classification. Additional details on the model’s implementation, training strategy, and hyperparameters are presented in online supplemental text 3.

Construction of a clinical model and nomogram

In the TS, clinical predictors were selected using univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses, with ORs and 95% CIs computed (online supplemental table 2). Variables with p<0.05 in univariate analysis were entered into multivariate analysis. Carbohydrate antigen 125 (CA125) was confirmed as the sole independent clinical predictor of TLS status and was used to construct a clinical model as a baseline comparator. To further enhance predictive performance, a nomogram was created by integrating the DLN model output with CA125.

Model performance assessment and interpretability

The discrimination ability of individual models in predicting TLS status was evaluated by calculating the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC). Further performance indicators, such as accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV), were also computed. Moreover, decision curve analysis (DCA) was used to analyze the clinical usefulness of each model by quantifying the net benefit across a range of probability thresholds.

Prospective validation: pathology-anchored verification

In the PAS (n=20), the DLN model’s discriminative performance was evaluated using ROC analysis, with AUC and 95% CIs calculated via bootstrapping. The prespecified cut-off of 0.570 was applied to generate a confusion matrix, from which sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, and overall accuracy were computed. The Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare DLN scores between histopathologically confirmed TLS-positive and TLS-negative groups.

Prospective validation: clinical outcome assessment

In the FAS (n=44), patients were stratified into DLN-high (≥0.570) and DLN-low (<0.570) groups based on the prespecified cut-off. Clinical endpoints included: (1) ORR, defined as the proportion of patients achieving complete response or partial response (PR) per Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors V.1.1 criteria; (2) PFS, defined as the time from enrollment to disease progression or death from any cause; and (3) OS, defined as the time from enrollment to death from any cause. Between-group comparisons used Fisher’s exact test for ORR, Kaplan-Meier analysis with log-rank test for PFS and OS, and Cox proportional hazards regression (univariable and multivariable) for survival analyses. Multivariable models adjusted for age, neutrophil-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), and albumin level—variables that were either imbalanced between groups or had established prognostic significance in PDAC. In an exploratory incremental prognostic analysis, the continuous DLN score was first evaluated alone and then combined with NLR and log-transformed carbohydrate antigen 19-9 (CA19-9). Model discrimination was summarized using Harrell’s C-index with 95% CIs. Nested models were compared using likelihood-ratio tests. Because of the limited sample size and number of events, no new risk-score cut-off was derived, and these analyses were considered hypothesis-generating.

Prospective validation: spatial immune profiling

To explore the biological basis of DLN predictions, we examined correlations between DLN scores and spatial immune cell interactions from CmTSA staining in the FAS (n=44). CmTSA was performed on baseline pretreatment FFPE specimens (surgical resection for PAS patients; core needle biopsy for non-surgical patients) using a seven-marker panel (CD4, CD8, CD20, CD11c, CD68, PD-L1, panCK). Spatial networks linking cells within 15 µm were constructed to quantify dendritic cell (DC)-T helper cell (Th), DC-cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL), and Th-CTL interaction scores. Programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) tumor proportion score (TPS) was assessed using the 22C3 antibody (n=42). Spearman correlations and Mann-Whitney U tests were used for analysis.

Statistical analysis

All statistical evaluations were performed with IBM SPSS (V.26.0) and R (V.4.5.2). For continuous variables, group comparisons were made by applying either Student’s t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test, depending on data distribution. Categorical variables were evaluated using either the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test. Differences in the AUC among various models were examined via the DeLong test. Youden’s Index was employed to identify optimal cut-off points for stratifying patients into high-risk and low-risk categories. To evaluate the prognostic significance of the DLN in OS, Kaplan-Meier survival curves were generated and compared using the log-rank test. The sample size calculation was based on an expected TLS-positive rate of 45%. To ensure a statistical power of 90% (β=0.10) at a significance level of α=0.05, and to accommodate up to 15 potential predictor variables, a minimum of 265 participants was required for the retrospective component. For multiple comparisons, adjusted p values were calculated by means of the Bonferroni method. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p value below 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics of retrospective cohorts

A total of 305 patients with pathologically confirmed PDAC were included across three centers for model development and validation. The TS consisted of 114 patients from Center 1, while the IVS included 49 patients from the same center. The EVS comprised 60 patients from Center 2. An independent CVS, consisting of 82 patients with biopsy-confirmed PDAC, was enrolled from Center 3. Baseline clinical and demographic characteristics were generally balanced among the cohorts (p>0.05 for all comparisons). Among the 223 surgical patients, 100 (44.84%) were histologically classified as TLS-positive based on immunohistochemical assessment. The prospective immunochemotherapy cohort comprised 44 patients in the FAS, including 22 females (50.0%) with a mean age of 57.2±7.3 years. Patients were stratified into DLN-high (n=10) and DLN-low (n=34) groups using the prespecified cut-off of 0.570. The two groups were well balanced for all baseline characteristics, including age (p=0.602), sex (p=0.280), hemoglobin (p=0.973), NLR (p=0.585), albumin (p=0.231), platelet count (p=0.343), CA125 (p=0.363), and CA19-9 (p=0.989; online supplemental table 1). Notably, DLN-high patients achieved a significantly higher objective response rate than DLN-low patients (ORR: 90.0% vs 26.5%, p=0.001).

Performance of the clinical model

Univariate logistic regression analysis identified CA125 (OR: 0.988, 95% CI 0.976 to 0.997, p=0.019) and NLR (OR: 0.946, 95% CI 0.894 to 0.991, p=0.032) as variables significantly associated with TLS positivity. Multivariate analysis confirmed CA125 as an independent predictor. Based on these results, a clinical model was developed and tested, yielding an AUC of 0.656 in the IVS and 0.674 in the EVS. Full regression results are presented in online supplemental table 2.

Performance of the deep learning model in TLS prediction

The DLN model demonstrated excellent performance in predicting TLS status using preoperative CECT images (figure 2a–c). In the TS, the model achieved an AUC of 0.987 (95% CI 0.973 to 1.000). The performance remained robust in the IVS, with an AUC of 0.937 (95% CI 0.851 to 1.000), and was validated externally (EVS) with an AUC of 0.853 (95% CI 0.747 to 0.959). In the EVS, the DLN achieved a sensitivity of 0.625 and a specificity of 1.000, using the fixed cut-off of 0.570 derived by maximizing Youden’s Index for pathological TLS discrimination. Representative ROC curves are illustrated in figure 2, and the corresponding performance metrics are detailed in table 2. Despite statistical significance, the DLN model alone showed superb discriminative capacity compared with the clinical model (p<0.05 for AUC comparison via DeLong test across three datasets). DCA (figure 2d) confirmed a greater net clinical benefit for the combined model across a range of threshold probabilities compared with using the DLN or clinical model alone.

Construction and validation of the combined nomogram model

A nomogram integrating the DLN model output with selected clinical variables was constructed to improve TLS prediction. The corresponding nomogram is presented in online supplemental figure 3. This combined model achieved significantly enhanced performance, with an AUC of 0.937 (95% CI 0.851 to 1.000) in the internal validation cohort and an AUC of 0.851 (95% CI 0.743 to 0.958) in the external validation cohort. The DeLong test indicated no significant difference between the combined nomogram and the DLN model alone (p=0.308 for EVS), suggesting that the addition of clinical variables did not statistically outperform the DLN model, but provided a clinically useful integrated tool.

Prognostic value of pathological and predicted TLS status across cohorts

We assessed the prognostic relevance of both pathologically confirmed and model-predicted TLS status in patients with PDAC by analyzing OS in different cohorts. In Center 1, patients with pathologically confirmed TLS-positive tumors (n=76) had significantly longer OS compared with TLS-negative patients (n=87), with a median OS of 29.3 months versus 13.1 months (log-rank p<0.0001; HR=8.553, 95% CI 5.640 to 12.970). Similar trends were observed in Center 2, where TLS-positive patients (n=24) had a median OS of 25.9 months compared with 13 months in TLS-negative patients (log-rank p<0.0001; HR=5.694, 95% CI 3.066 to 10.572). Prospective validation: pathology-anchored verification

To rigorously verify the DLN model’s diagnostic accuracy against a histopathological gold standard in a prospective setting, we evaluated its performance in the PAS (n=20), comprising patients who had undergone prior curative pancreatectomy before disease recurrence and had available FFPE surgical resection specimens with CmTSA-confirmed TLS status.

The locked DLN model achieved an AUC of 0.929 (95% CI 0.815 to 1, p=0.002) for discriminating histopathologically confirmed TLS status (figure 4a). At the prespecified cut-off of 0.570, the model demonstrated a sensitivity of 0.833 (5/6), specificity of 1.000 (14/14), PPV of 1.000 (5/5), NPV of 0.933 (14/15), and overall accuracy of 0.950 (19/20).

Prospective validation: clinical outcomes in the immunochemotherapy cohort

In the FAS (n=44), patients were stratified into DLN-high (n=10, DLN score ≥0.570) and DLN-low (n=34, DLN score <0.570) groups for clinical outcome analysis.

DLN-high patients achieved a significantly higher ORR than DLN-low patients (90.0% (9/10) vs 26.5% (9/34), Fisher’s exact test p=0.001) (figure 4c). Specifically, 9 of 10 DLN-high patients achieved PR and 1 achieved stable disease (SD), whereas in the DLN-low group, 9 achieved PR, 21 achieved SD, and 4 experienced progressive disease (PD) as best response. No patient in the DLN-high group experienced PD.

Kaplan-Meier analysis revealed significantly longer PFS in the DLN-high group compared with the DLN-low group (median 13.1 months vs 5.3 months; log-rank p<0.001) (figure 4d). In univariable Cox regression, DLN-high status was associated with a 71.5% reduction in progression risk (HR=0.285, 95% CI 0.129 to 0.627, p=0.002). In multivariable Cox regression adjusting for age, NLR, and albumin, the DLN score (analyzed as a continuous variable) remained an independent predictor of PFS (HR=0.079, 95% CI 0.017 to 0.355, p<0.001).

Similarly, OS was significantly longer in the DLN-high group (median 17.6 months vs 8.7 months; log-rank p<0.001) (figure 4e). In univariable Cox regression, DLN-high status was associated with a significant reduction in mortality risk (HR=0.237, 95% CI 0.102 to 0.551, p<0.001). In multivariable Cox regression adjusting for age, NLR, and albumin, the DLN score remained the strongest independent predictor of OS (HR=0.052, 95% CI 0.010 to 0.275, p<0.001), while NLR was also independently prognostic (HR=1.135, 95% CI 1.014 to 1.272, p=0.028). Albumin and age did not reach statistical significance in the multivariable model. Full Cox regression results are presented in table 3.

In exploratory incremental analyses, the DLN score alone yielded a Harrell C-index of 0.639 (95% CI 0.537 to 0.719) for OS and 0.670 (95% CI 0.567 to 0.748) for PFS. Adding NLR changed the C-index to 0.656 for OS and 0.674 for PFS, while further inclusion of log-transformed CA19-9 resulted in C-indices of 0.659 and 0.685, respectively. Prospective validation: correlation between DLN score and spatial immune microenvironment

To explore the biological underpinnings of DLN-predicted TLS status, we examined the relationship between DLN scores and spatial immune cell interactions derived from CmTSA profiling in the FAS (n=44).

No correlation was observed between DLN scores and PD-L1 TPS (Spearman rho=0.011, p=0.946; n=42 with available data) (figure 4f), indicating that the DLN model captures immune features that are fundamentally distinct from PD-L1 expression and suggesting complementary biomarker utility. Among 42 patients with available PD-L1 TPS data, an exploratory 2×2 stratification combining PD-L1 status (TPS ≥1% vs <1%) and DLN group (high vs low) was performed (online supplemental table 5). DLN-high patients showed favorable observed outcomes in both PD-L1 strata (ORR: 85.7% (6/7) in PD-L1-negative patients and 100% (2/2) in PD-L1-positive patients), whereas among DLN-low patients, the observed ORR was 62.5% (5/8) in the PD-L1-positive subgroup and 16.0% (4/25) in the PD-L1-negative subgroup. Given the limited sample size in individual quadrants, formal statistical comparisons were not performed; these descriptive results are presented as hypothesis-generating.

Among the spatial interaction scores examined, two TLS-associated immune axes showed significant associations with DLN predictions. First, DC-Th interaction intensity was significantly higher in the DLN-high group compared with the DLN-low group (median 0.143 vs 0.008; Mann-Whitney U test p=0.037) (figure 4h), consistent with the role of DC-Th crosstalk as a driver of antigen presentation and T-cell priming within organized immune niches.37 Second, the DLN score showed a significant positive correlation with Th-B cell (CD4+–CD20+) spatial interaction intensity (Spearman rho=0.307, p=0.043) (figure 4g). This latter finding is of particular biological significance, as the spatial co-localization of T helper cells and B cells—forming organized T/B zonation—is a defining architectural hallmark of functional TLS.3 4 8 The enrichment of Th-B spatial interactions in DLN-high tumors therefore provides direct evidence that the imaging biomarker captures the core structural feature of TLS, namely the organized lymphoid niche where T-cell help drives B-cell activation, affinity maturation, and class-switch recombination.

In contrast, DC-CTL interaction scores (p=0.29) and Th-CTL interaction scores (p=0.60) did not differ significantly between groups, suggesting that the DLN model preferentially captures the antigen-presentation and lymphoid-organization axes of TLS function rather than the downstream effector arm.

DLN scores showed a significant inverse correlation with tumor cell density (Spearman rho=−0.399, p=0.007) (figure 4i), consistent with the established observation that TLS-rich tumors tend to have lower tumor cellularity and a more immune-permissive microenvironment.

Discussion

Our study developed and validated a DLN-based model for non-invasive TLS prediction in PDAC using routine CECT images, demonstrating robust performance across internal and external validation cohorts. A critical advance is the multilayered prospective validation of the locked model within a registered phase Ib/II immunochemotherapy trial (ChiCTR2000032293), providing three independent lines of evidence: pathology-anchored diagnostic verification, clinical outcome prediction, and spatial immune mechanistic grounding.

First, in the pathology-anchored subset comprising patients with available surgical resection specimens, the DLN achieved an AUC of 0.929 (95% CI 0.815 to 1.000) with a specificity of 1.000 (14/14), directly demonstrating its diagnostic accuracy against a CmTSA-confirmed histopathological gold standard—a substantially more rigorous validation than conventional immunohistochemistry-based TLS assessment. Second, DLN-predicted TLS status was strongly associated with objective therapeutic response (ORR: 90.0% vs 26.5%, p=0.001), PFS (median 13.1 vs 5.3 months, p<0.001), and OS (median 17.6 vs 8.7 months, p<0.001), with the DLN score confirmed as an independent predictor of OS in multivariable analysis (HR=0.052, p<0.001) adjusting for age, NLR, and albumin. Third, the biological bridging analysis revealed that DLN scores were orthogonal to PD-L1 expression (rho=0.011, p=0.946) but correlated with both DC-Th spatial interaction intensity (p=0.037) and Th-B cell spatial co-localization (rho=0.307, p=0.043)—two complementary hallmarks of functional TLS architecture reflecting antigen presentation and lymphoid organization, respectively. NLR remained independently associated with OS in the primary multivariable analysis, raising the possibility that systemic inflammation may complement the imaging-derived immune phenotype. However, in exploratory incremental models, adding NLR and log-transformed CA19-9 produced only modest changes in discrimination beyond the DLN score alone (OS C-index, 0.639 to 0.659; PFS C-index, 0.670 to 0.685), and the fully adjusted models did not significantly improve model fit (likelihood-ratio p=0.125 for OS and p=0.370 for PFS). Given the limited cohort size and the post hoc nature of the analysis, these findings were not used to derive a composite risk score. Larger cohorts are required to determine whether systemic inflammatory and serological markers provide reproducible incremental value beyond the DLN score.

Prior studies have used CT-based and MRI-based radiomics to predict TLS status in hepatocellular carcinoma, lung adenocarcinoma, breast cancer, and intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma.30–36 Radiomics quantifies predefined image features, whereas deep learning learns hierarchical representations directly from images and may capture spatial patterns not fully represented by handcrafted features. However, this theoretical advantage does not establish empirical superiority, and a head-to-head comparison using identical cohorts is required. CT and MRI also provide complementary information for TLS prediction. Contrast-enhanced CT is rapid, widely available, relatively robust to motion, and routinely used for PDAC staging and response assessment, facilitating multicenter analysis and longitudinal evaluation. CT-based radiomics has also shown value in assessing treatment response in PDAC.38 MRI offers superior soft-tissue contrast and multiparametric sequences, including diffusion-weighted and dynamic contrast-enhanced imaging, which may capture additional features related to cellularity, perfusion, and stromal composition.39 Nevertheless, MRI requires longer acquisition times, is more susceptible to motion and protocol variability, and is less uniformly available across centers. Thus, our findings should not be interpreted as demonstrating the intrinsic superiority of CT. Future studies should directly compare radiomics and deep learning using matched CT and MRI examinations and investigate whether multimodal integration provides incremental value.

The observed association between TLS positivity and improved OS in our surgical cohorts aligns with a growing body of evidence positioning TLS as a cornerstone of antitumor immunity.41 42 By serving as specialized niches for lymphocyte activation and maturation, TLS enhance local immune surveillance and are linked to improved outcomes for patients with PDAC receiving both immunotherapy and conventional chemotherapy.8 9 43 44 A key innovation in our study is the way it addresses a critical gap in existing TLS research. Most studies to date have derived knowledge of TLS in PDAC exclusively from histopathological analysis of surgically resected specimens,45 a method that excludes a large, clinically significant population—those with locally advanced or metastatic disease who are not candidates for surgery. Consequently, the true prevalence and prognostic impact of TLS in this non-surgical majority have remained largely unknown and difficult to assess. Our study bridges this gap through two complementary approaches: the CVS (n=82) from Center 3 demonstrates that DLN-predicted TLS status stratifies prognosis in entirely non-surgical patients (median OS: 19 vs 7 months, p<0.0001), while the prospective immunochemotherapy cohort from Center 1 provides pathology-anchored verification and mechanistic depth.

The orthogonality between DLN scores and PD-L1 expression deserves particular emphasis. PD-L1 remains the most widely used biomarker for immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy, yet its predictive value in PDAC is limited and inconsistent.37 Our finding that the DLN model captures immune features entirely independent of PD-L1 suggests that these two biomarkers reflect fundamentally different aspects of antitumor immunity: PD-L1 reflects the adaptive immune evasion axis, whereas DLN-predicted TLS status reflects the organized immune activation axis characterized by DC-Th spatial interactions. This complementarity supports a dual-biomarker strategy that could enable more refined patient stratification for immunotherapy. An exploratory 2×2 stratification combining PD-L1 and DLN status (online supplemental table 5) revealed a complementary pattern: DLN-high patients showed favorable outcomes in both PD-L1 strata, while among DLN-low patients, those with PD-L1-positive tumors showed a higher response rate (62.5% vs 16.0%), suggesting that PD-L1 may provide additional response stratification within the DLN-low subgroup. Although the small quadrant sample sizes preclude definitive conclusions, this complementary pattern suggests that combined PD-L1 and DLN status may provides more granular risk stratification than either marker alone. Prospective validation in a biomarker-stratified trial design is warranted.

An important question is how routine CECT captures signals reflective of microscopic TLS. We propose that TLS-associated high endothelial venules,3 4 which exhibit distinct vascular morphology and increased permeability, may alter local contrast enhancement patterns. Furthermore, TLS formation is associated with reduced desmoplastic stromal density and lower tumor cellularity—directly supported by our finding of an inverse DLN-tumor cell density correlation (rho=−0.399, p=0.007)—producing measurable differences in tissue attenuation detectable by deep learning.

Regarding the mechanistic basis for the association between DC-Th spatial interactions and immunochemotherapy response, our data are consistent with an emerging paradigm in which organized immune niches—rather than diffuse immune infiltration—determine the efficacy of checkpoint blockade. Within TLS, DCs present tumor-associated antigens via MHC-II to CD4+T helper cells, which in turn provide “licensing” signals to DCs through CD40L-CD40 interactions enhancing cross-presentation to CTLs,46 and secrete cytokines (eg, IL-21, CXCL13) that promote B-cell maturation and germinal center reactions. When anti-PD-1 therapy is combined with chemotherapy, immunogenic cell death releases neoantigens efficiently captured by TLS-resident DCs, while PD-1 blockade reinvigorates exhausted T cells within these organized niches—explaining why patients with higher DC-Th interaction scores derive greater benefit.

Notably, the significant correlation between DLN scores and Th-B cell spatial interaction intensity provides arguably more direct evidence linking the imaging biomarker to TLS biology. While DC-Th interactions reflect the antigen-presentation axis, Th-B cell co-localization represents the lymphoid-organizational axis—the very architectural feature that distinguishes TLS from disorganized immune infiltrates. Within TLS, cognate CD4+ T helper–CD20+ B-cell interactions drive germinal center–like reactions including affinity maturation and generation of tumor-specific antibodies.3 8 Recent studies have demonstrated that mature TLS in PDAC are structurally homologous to secondary lymphoid organs.43 The dual enrichment of both DC-Th and Th-B spatial interactions in DLN-high patients suggests that the imaging model captures tumors harboring structurally complete and functionally active TLS.

When comparing our work with existing non-invasive TLS detection methods, our CECT-based model offers distinct advantages over the ¹⁸F-FDG PET/CT approach recently explored by Yan et al.47 CECT is the universally recommended imaging modality for pancreatic cancer management, is more widely available, lower in cost, and involves less radiation. Moreover, our deep learning model captures spatial and textural patterns linked to the immune microenvironment, enabling a more direct evaluation of TLS-associated tissue architecture independent of glucose metabolism.

Looking ahead, the clinical utility of our model is expected to grow as therapeutic strategies aimed at inducing TLS formation in situ become more widely adopted.40 44 48 49 Our CECT-based DLN model is ideally suited for non-invasive, serial monitoring of TLS status during such treatments, enabling real-time assessment of therapeutic efficacy and providing a quantifiable imaging endpoint for clinical trials evaluating next-generation immunotherapies.

The post hoc workflow assessment confirmed that manual segmentation was the principal bottleneck, accounting for a median of 14.4 min within a total end-to-end processing time of 19.0 min per case. The current pipeline should therefore not be considered a fully deployable clinical system. A practical implementation would require automated or weakly supervised tumor localization, followed by DLN inference and structured output within the picture archiving and communication system (PACS) environment. Potential alternatives include radiologist-defined bounding boxes, two-dimensional key-slice annotation, and automated pancreas and tumor segmentation using pancreas-specific nnU-Net-based models. These approaches could substantially reduce operator time, but may introduce boundary errors in infiltrative tumors and lesions adjacent to vessels, ducts, or inflammatory tissue. Because the classifier was trained using manually curated tumor masks, the effect of substituting automated or coarse annotations cannot be assumed to be negligible and should be evaluated through segmentation-perturbation experiments and external workflow validation. PACS integration would require automated DICOM routing, phase selection, preprocessing, segmentation, inference, quality-control flags, and return of the DLN score with an uncertainty indicator to the radiology workstation. Thus, the present study establishes clinical validity under a research workflow rather than readiness for routine deployment.

Several limitations of this study warrant discussion. First, the application of the DLN model to the prospective immunochemotherapy cohort was an exploratory post hoc analysis rather than a prespecified endpoint of the original trial; although the model and cut-off were locked prior to application, prospective trials with DLN-based stratification as a prespecified endpoint are needed to confirm its predictive utility. Second, the prospective validation cohort was relatively small (n=44), and the DLN-high group comprised only 10 patients; the resulting effect size estimates should therefore be interpreted with appropriate caution, as reflected in their wide CIs (eg, OS HR 95% CI 0.010 to 0.275), and larger prospective studies are needed to confirm these findings and establish more precise effect estimates. Third, the PAS included only 20 patients with available surgical specimens, and the iTLS prevalence was relatively low (6/20), which may limit the precision of diagnostic performance estimates. Fourth, the DLN model was trained on surgical resection specimens from patients with resectable disease and subsequently applied to patients with locally advanced or metastatic PDAC in the CVS and prospective cohorts. Although the inverse association between the DLN score and tumor cell density provides biological plausibility, it does not exclude stage-related spectrum effects or differences in tumor morphology, prior treatment exposure, and disease distribution between resectable and advanced PDAC. The model therefore requires systematic validation across disease stages and treatment settings, ideally with stage-stratified performance estimates and contemporaneous tissue reference standards. Fifth, spatial immune profiling in 24 non-surgical FAS patients relied on core needle biopsy specimens, which may not fully represent TLS spatial heterogeneity; our analysis was restricted to intratumoral TLS and stromal TLS were not separately evaluated; additionally, the CmTSA panel lacked germinal center markers (eg, BCL6, Ki-67), precluding assessment of secondary follicle-type TLS maturation. Sixth, the post hoc timing assessment was conducted in 20 randomly selected cases during manuscript revision rather than being prospectively embedded in routine clinical care. Although it provides an empirical estimate of the current workflow burden, the measured time may vary by operator experience, tumor conspicuity, and local infrastructure. Moreover, the present performance estimates were obtained using manually curated masks and should not be assumed to transfer unchanged to bounding-box, key-slice, or automated segmentation workflows. Prospective evaluation of automated segmentation, processing time, failure modes, robustness to mask perturbation, and end-to-end PACS integration is required before clinical deployment.

In conclusion, our study establishes a DLN-based CT model as a reliable, non-invasive tool for predicting TLS status in PDAC. The prospective validation within a registered immunochemotherapy trial demonstrates that DLN-predicted TLS status independently predicts therapeutic response and survival, correlates with TLS-associated spatial immune interactions—particularly DC-Th and Th-B cell crosstalk—and is orthogonal to PD-L1 expression, offering complementary and non-redundant predictive value to the most widely used immunotherapy biomarker. These findings support the clinical translation of this imaging biomarker for immunotherapy stratification in PDAC, offering a scalable approach applicable to all patients regardless of surgical eligibility.

All patients in the prospective cohort provided written informed consent prior to enrollment, which included consent for the use of clinical data, imaging data, and tissue specimens for biomarker research and publication of anonymized results.
