Increased Levels of Serum Eosinophil-Derived Neurotoxin in Patients With Chronic Spontaneous Urticaria
Authors: Eun-Mi Yang, Hyun-Mi Kim, Jieun Seo, Young-Min Ye, Chang-Keun Kim, Hae-Sim Park
Published: 2026

Mast cells are the primary effector cells in the pathogenesis of chronic spontaneous urticaria (CSU), whereas the role of eosinophils, which are key effector cells in T2-inflammatory diseases, remains not fully understood.123 A recent study has suggested serum eosinophil-derived neurotoxin (EDN), a biomarker for eosinophil activation, is significantly elevated in patients with CSU and correlates positively with disease severity.4 We speculated that activated eosinophils may contribute to the inflammatory processes in CSU. Therefore, this study analyzed the association between serum EDN levels, and clinical and laboratory findings in patients with CSU.

Ninety-six patients with CSU and 67 healthy controls (HCs) were enrolled. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and the study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Ajou University Medical Center, Suwon, Korea (AJOUIRB-SMP-2013-108). Serum EDN and free immunoglobulin E (IgE) levels were measured by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) (SKIMS-BIO Co., Seoul, Korea) and homemade IgE ELISA using YH35324 (IgETRAP),5 respectively. Serum levels of total IgE and specific IgE to toxic shock syndrome toxin-1 (TSST-1) and eosinophil cationic protein (ECP) were measured by ImmunoCAP (ThermoFisher Science, Waltham, MA, USA) (Supplementary Table S1). Clinical (including urticaria activity [UAS], urticaria control test scores) and immunologic parameters were compared. For statistical analyses, continuous variables were compared using Student’s t-test and the Mann-Whitney U test, depending on their distribution (parametric or non-parametric). In addition, the χ2 test was used for categorical clinical parameters and Spearman rank correlation analysis were applied for correlation studies.

A meta-analysis reviewing 38 studies found a significantly higher prevalence and odds ratio of atopic disorders (nearly a 3-fold) in patients with CSU than in controls.6 The present study demonstrated a significantly higher rate of atopy (58.1% vs. 43.4%, P < 0.05), serum levels of total IgE (264.61 ± 296.51 vs. 53.81 ± 49.44, P < 0.001) and those of free IgE (585.50 ± 560.36 vs. 111.30 ± 113.33, P < 0.001) in CSU patients than in HCs (Supplementary Table S1), suggesting the contribution of T2- and IgE-mediated mechanisms in the pathogenesis of CSU. In addition, the present study shows serum levels of EDN tended to be higher in CSU patients than in HCs (P = 0.153); CSU patients with higher symptom score (UAS7 ≥ 28) had significantly higher EDN levels than those with lower UAS7 (P = 0.022, Figure A). Significant correlations were found between serum EDN levels, and ECP/total IgE levels/specific IgE to TSST-1/blood eosinophil counts (P < 0.001, P = 0.009, P = 0.014 and P < 0.001, respectively, Figure B-E). Serum EDN and ECP levels were significantly higher in CSU patients with high total eosinophil counts (blood eosinophils ≥ 150/uL) than in those with low eosinophils (< 150/uL; P = 0.001 and P < 0.001, respectively). This suggests that blood eosinophils ≥ 150/uL may predict increased eosinophil activation status in CSU patients. Our previous study showed that serum specific IgE to TSST-1 was significantly associated with the phenotype of CSU,7 suggesting a possible contribution of specific IgE to TSST-1 to T2/eosinophilic inflammation in the pathogenesis of CSU. Taken together, T2/eosinophilic inflammation could contribute to the pathogenesis of CSU, especially in severe patients with CSU, where anti-IgE therapy may be beneficial.
