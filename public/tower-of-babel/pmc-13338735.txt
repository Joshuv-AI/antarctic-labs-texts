Ultrasound radiomics features for identifying the masseter muscle in patients with masticatory muscle tendon–aponeurosis hyperplasia: A pilot study
Authors: Chiaki Kuwada, Tsutomu Kuwada, Masako Nishiyama, Yukiko Takashi, Michihito Nozawa, Tetsushi Oguma, Shigemitsu Sakuma, Arihiro Nakamura, Atsushi Abe, Eiichiro Ariji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo investigate whether quantitative radiomics features derived from ultrasound images of the masseter muscle, combined with machine learning, can identify masticatory muscle tendon–aponeurosis hyperplasia (MMTAH).Materials and MethodsSeven patients clinically diagnosed with MMTAH (3 men, 4 women; mean age, 52.7 ± 14.3 years) and 7 age- and sex-matched healthy controls were included. A total of 84 ultrasound image slices (42 MMTAH and 42 controls) were analysed. The masseter muscle was manually segmented using 3D Slicer, and radiomics features were extracted with the SlicerRadiomics extension. Logistic regression and random forest models were constructed, and their performance was evaluated using internal validation.ResultsA total of 107 radiomics features were extracted, from which informative features were selected for model development. The logistic regression model showed a sensitivity of 0.83, specificity of 0.90, accuracy of 0.87 and an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.95. The random forest model showed a sensitivity of 0.83, specificity of 0.93, accuracy of 0.88 and an AUC of 0.92. There was no significant difference in AUC between the two models (P=0.33).ConclusionRadiomics-based analysis of ultrasound images may provide a quantitative and reliable approach for differentiating MMTAH from normal masseter muscles.Keywords: Radiomics, Masticatory Muscles, Masseter Muscle, Hypertrophy, Ultrasonography

Introduction

Masticatory muscle tendon–aponeurosis hyperplasia (MMTAH) is characterised by hyperplasia of the tendinous and aponeurotic components of the masticatory muscles and has increasingly been recognised as an important cause of slowly progressive trismus. The condition often begins in adolescence and is frequently associated with a square mandibular configuration and a hard cord-like structure palpable along the anterior border of the masseter muscle on maximal opening.1,2 Clinically, MMTAH often shows limited response to conservative management, and many patients require surgical intervention to achieve satisfactory improvement in mouth opening.1,2,3 Accordingly, the development of reliable and objective imaging biomarkers that enable early and accurate diagnosis is of considerable clinical importance.

Although the imaging literature on MMTAH is sparse, a few studies have used magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and demonstrated its utility for depicting tendinous and aponeurotic elements.2,3,4,5,6 Reported MRI findings include bilateral enlargement of the masseter muscles and thickening of the aponeurosis at the anterior margin of the masseter. In addition, CT has been reported to demonstrate bilateral masseter enlargement with areas of increased density in the anterior and lateral regions.4 These findings underscore the value of cross-sectional imaging in the assessment of this disorder. In comparison, ultrasonography (US) represents a complementary modality that offers several practical advantages: it is noninvasive, nonionising, and widely accessible, and it permits real-time dynamic evaluation from multiple insonation angles.7,8 Previous ultrasonographic studies have demonstrated that US can detect disease-related changes in the masseter, such as alterations in thickness and internal echogenic pattern, in various pathological conditions, including myofascial pain, temporomandibular disorders and bruxism.8,9,10,11 These findings suggest that US may also be applicable to the evaluation of MMTAH by visualising morphological or textural changes within the muscle. However, despite its potential, no prior studies have directly compared the ultrasonographic characteristics of the masseter between patients with MMTAH and healthy controls. Furthermore, current diagnostic assessments of MMTAH rely primarily on qualitative visual interpretation, which is subject to observer variability and limits reproducibility. Therefore, the introduction of quantitative image analysis techniques may enhance diagnostic accuracy and objectivity.

Recently, radiomics has rapidly emerged as an advanced analytical approach in medical imaging. By extracting a large number of quantitative features from medical images and analysing them using statistical or machine-learning-based methods, radiomics enables the identification of image-based information that cannot be captured by conventional visual assessment. Radiomics analysis has been applied to various imaging modalities, and reports on ultrasound-based radiomics have suggested its potential usefulness in the diagnosis of diseases in the head and neck region.12,13,14,15

In the present study, radiomics analysis and machine learning techniques were applied to masseter ultrasound images to investigate whether quantitative radiomics features can distinguish MMTAH from healthy masseter muscle. This study hypothesised that radiomics-derived parameters can capture the underlying tissue heterogeneity characteristics of MMTAH and may therefore provide objective and reproducible imaging biomarkers to support its diagnosis.

Materials and Methods

The study design was approved by the Ethics Committee of our University (approval number 723) and was planned in accordance with the ethical standards of the Helsinki Declaration.

Participants in the present study comprised 7 patients clinically diagnosed with MMTAH (3 men and 4 women; mean age, 52.7 ± 14.3 years) and 7 age- and sex-matched healthy controls. In this study, MMTAH was diagnosed according to previously reported clinical criteria, including slowly progressive limitation of mouth opening beginning in adolescence, absence of restriction in lateral or protrusive mandibular movement, palpation of a firm cord-like structure along the anterior border of the masseter muscle at maximal mouth opening, and a square mandibular configuration.1,2 A total of 84 ultrasound image slices were analysed, consisting of 42 slices from the MMTAH group and 42 slices from the control group.

Ultrasound image acquisition

All ultrasound images of the masseter muscles were taken in B-mode using a Logiq E9 system (GE Healthcare, Tokyo, Japan) with a centre frequency of 12 MHz. The ultrasonographic examinations were performed by an oral and maxillofacial radiologist with 9 years’ experience performing musculoskeletal and maxillofacial imaging.

Ultrasound images were acquired with participants seated in a resting position. Because MMTAH is generally regarded as a bilateral condition, scans were obtained from both the left and right masseter muscles at three anatomical levels: just below the zygomatic arch, at the midpoint of the masseter muscle, and immediately above the inferior border of the mandible. Thus, six ultrasound image slices were obtained from each participant. The probe was positioned perpendicular to the muscle contraction direction and the mandibular surface to ensure consistent orientation across all participants.

Masseter muscle segmentation and radiomics feature extraction

Image analysis was performed using 3D Slicer (https://www.slicer.org/). Because the ultrasound images were originally acquired in colour (red/green/blue) format, they were first converted into scalar volumes (greyscale images) to ensure compatibility with radiomics analysis. The masseter muscle region was then manually segmented using 3D Slicer. In this study, the region of interest was defined over the analysable masseter muscle within the selected ultrasound section, rather than being restricted to visually identifiable tendinous or aponeurotic components alone. To ensure accurate and consistent delineation, the region of interest was defined within the area located superior to the masseter’s mandibular contact area (Fig. 1). When the ultrasound probe was positioned perpendicular to the mandibular surface, the anterior portion of the muscle (i.e., the region located anterior to its mandibular contact area) was unlikely to be scanned perpendicular to the muscle fibres, which could have resulted in unreliable echogenic intensity measurements. Therefore, this anterior region was excluded from the analysis.

Radiomic features were computed from the manually segmented masseter muscle regions using the SlicerRadiomics extension for 3D Slicer, which provides a Python-based module for extracting quantitative imaging features.

Creation of the prediction model and analysis

In the present study, radiomic features were used to construct two types of models: a logistic regression (LR) model and a random forest (RF) model.

For the LR Model with L1 Regularisation (LASSO), all radiomic feature values were standardised using z-score normalisation within each training fold prior to model fitting. The LR model with L1 regularisation was employed to encourage sparsity of the regression coefficients. This regularisation penalises less informative features by shrinking their coefficients towards zero, thereby enabling implicit feature selection during model training.

Model performance was evaluated using 5-fold stratified cross-validation as an internal validation procedure. In each fold, the model was trained on 80% of the dataset and validated on the remaining 20%, while preserving the class distribution. Predictions from all validation folds were aggregated to construct a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, and the area under the ROC curve (AUC) was calculated as the primary performance metric. The optimal probability threshold was determined using the Youden index of the ROC curve and was applied to compute accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, and F1-score, as well as to generate the confusion matrix.

To assess the stability and interpretability of the LR model, logistic regression coefficients were extracted from each cross-validation fold. The mean and standard deviation of the regression coefficients across folds were obtained, and the top 10 features with the highest absolute mean coefficient values were visualised as horizontal bar plots.

For the RF Model, all radiomic features were used without prior feature selection. Model performance was evaluated using the same 5-fold stratified cross-validation procedure as applied for the LR model, and predictions from all validation folds were aggregated for performance assessment. Following the same procedure as for the LR model, the ROC curve and confusion matrix for the RF model were generated, and performance metrics (AUC, accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, and F1-score) were calculated. Feature importance values derived from the RF model were averaged across folds, and the top 10 features with the highest mean importance scores were visualised as horizontal bar plots.

Differences in the AUCs between the LR and RF models were assessed using DeLong’s test, with significance set at P<0.05.

Results

A total of 107 radiomic features were extracted, comprising 18 first-order, 14 shape-related and 75 texture features. In the present study, only a single slice per level was analysed; therefore, shape features, which are typically derived from three-dimensional volumetric data, were excluded. Instead, the model was constructed using features that are meaningful within a single slice, such as texture and first-order features.

Discussion

In the present study, we performed radiomics analysis using ultrasound images of the masseter muscle and constructed both LR and RF models to classify the masseter muscles of MMTAH patients and healthy controls. The AUCs of the LR and RF models were high, at 0.95 and 0.92, respectively, with no significant difference between the models.

Among the top 10 features selected, three features were common to both models: GLRLM_GrayLevelNonUniformity, GLCM_IMC2 and firstorder_Skewness. Of these, GLRLM_GrayLevelNonUniformity and GLCM_IMC2 achieved high AUC values of 0.86 and 0.81, respectively, when evaluated independently using univariate ROC analysis rather than as part of a multivariable machine-learning model.

The grey-level run-length matrix (GLRLM) captures information about runs of consecutive pixels that share the same grey-level value in a specified direction, providing insight into intensity homogeneity along that direction.16 Among its features, GrayLevelNonUniformity quantifies the distribution of grey levels among the runs within a region of interest, with higher values indicating that certain grey levels dominate less evenly, reflecting greater heterogeneity or non-uniformity in the texture of the image.17 This feature was higher in a substantial proportion of MMTAH patients than in healthy controls. Previous reports have suggested that MMTAH may be associated with structural alterations in the masseter muscle in addition to tendon–aponeurotic hyperplasia. Specifically, fatty infiltration, hyalinisation, and microcalcification have been observed in affected tissues using electron microscopy.18 These alterations are similar to those seen in tendinosis, which is characterised by tendon rupture, fibrinogen accumulation and structural degeneration.19,20,21 In younger individuals, fatty infiltration of the masseter muscle bundles may indicate disuse atrophy, while in middle-aged patients, mild degenerative changes, such as tendon calcification, are often present.22 These findings collectively imply that the masseter muscle in MMTAH may undergo fatty and other forms of degeneration, resembling chronic tendinopathic conditions.

In addition, GLCM_IMC2 (informational measure of correlation 2) is a grey level co-occurrence matrix (GLCM)–derived texture feature that quantifies texture complexity. In the present study, this feature showed generally higher values in patients with MMTAH than in healthy controls. Higher IMC2 values indicate increased complexity in local grey-level patterns within the masseter muscle, suggesting that the tissue exhibits not only heterogeneous intensity distribution but also intricate spatial arrangements of muscle fibres and degenerative or fibrotic changes. When considered alongside the increased GLRLM_GrayLevelNonUniformity observed in MMTAH, these findings support the notion that the masseter muscle texture is characterised by both heterogeneous intensity and complex local organisation, potentially reflecting subtle structural alterations rather than purely random texture disruption. The radiomic features of the masseter muscle on ultrasound images identified in the present study may therefore reflect these underlying histopathological characteristics. While no single radiomic feature can fully capture the complexity of tissue alterations, GLRLM_GrayLevelNonUniformity and GLCM_IMC2 appear to provide complementary information, reflecting heterogeneous intensity distribution and complex local structural organisation, respectively. Together, these features may serve as promising quantitative imaging biomarkers for distinguishing pathological changes in MMTAH, and their discriminative performance could be further enhanced when combined with other complementary radiomic features in multivariable models.

In comparison, although firstorder_Skewness demonstrated limited discriminative performance when evaluated independently (AUC=0.68 in univariate ROC analysis), it may still provide complementary information in multivariable machine-learning models when combined with other radiomic features. The RF algorithm constructs an ensemble of decision trees and determines the final classification based on the majority vote of these trees, allowing the model to capture nonlinear interactions among features and model complex relationships.12,23 Similarly, LASSO performs feature selection by shrinking less informative coefficients towards zero, resulting in a sparse set of predictors in a multivariable setting. As a result, radiomic features with limited discriminative performance in univariate analyses may nonetheless have contributed to the high overall AUC observed for both models in the present study.

The present study evaluated imaging changes in the analysable masseter muscle within the selected ultrasound section rather than focusing solely on visually identifiable tendinous or aponeurotic components. This approach was chosen because MMTAH is clinically related to tendon–aponeurotic hyperplasia, but previous imaging and histopathological studies have also suggested associated changes involving the masseter muscle more broadly, including bilateral enlargement and intramuscular structural alterations. Accordingly, our radiomics model should be interpreted as capturing a disease-associated masseter imaging phenotype related to clinically diagnosed MMTAH, rather than as a direct replacement for the established diagnostic criteria or as a simple ultrasound-based measurement of tendon thickness alone.

Although the two radiomics models developed in the present study showed relatively high performance, this study has several limitations. First, the region of interest was limited to the mandibular contact area of the masseter muscle. The anterior portion of the masseter, where tendinous structures are particularly well-developed, was not adequately scanned perpendicular to the muscle fibres and was therefore excluded from the analysis. Consequently, tendinous features in the MMTAH and control groups may not have been fully captured in the models, and future studies should construct models with perpendicular scanning of the anterior region. Second, the sample size was relatively small, and only internal validation could be performed. Increasing the number of cases and performing external validation in future studies will be necessary to better assess the generalisability of the models. Third, the study population was relatively older, and age-related changes in the masseter muscle may have influenced the results. Fourth, the models were developed using data from a single centre, and future work should incorporate multi-centre data to enhance the robustness and applicability of the models. Finally, the diagnosis of MMTAH in this study was based on clinical criteria, without histopathological confirmation for the analysed cohort. Therefore, the relationship between the ultrasound radiomics findings and the underlying tissue pathology could not be directly established, and the biological meaning of these imaging features should be interpreted with caution.

In conclusion, the findings of the present study suggest that radiomics features derived from masseter ultrasound images may discriminate MMTAH from healthy masseter muscle, providing tentative evidence to reject the null hypothesis. Both LR and RF models demonstrated relatively high performance, indicating that radiomics analysis has the potential to offer objective and reproducible imaging biomarkers to support the diagnosis of MMTAH.
