Clinicopathological features and outcomes of glomerular microangiopathy: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Peng Xia, Hanshu Liu, Yubing Wen, Wei Ye, Wenling Ye, Xixi Zheng, Xiaoxiao Shi, Lu Zhang, Ying Wang, Limeng Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlomerular microangiopathy (GMA) is a group of diseases characterized pathologically by thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) changes in the glomerulus without detectable microthrombi or intima edema of small arteries on renal biopsy. Few studies have focused on the clinicopathological characteristics and prognosis of patients with GMA. In this single-center retrospective cohort study, we summarized the clinical and pathological data of patients diagnosed with GMA between January 2005 and July 2023 at the Peking Union Medical College Hospital. Treatment and prognosis were also analyzed. A total of 32 patients diagnosed with GMA were included in this study. They were 62.5% male, with a mean age of 46 ± 17 years. The most common primary diseases were idiopathic multicenter Castleman disease (iMCD) (31.2%), POEMS (polyneuropathy, organomegaly, endocrinopathy, monoclonal plasma cell disorder, and skin changes) syndrome (21.9%), and exposure to anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) agents (21.9%). All the patients exhibited various degrees of proteinuria (2.22 g/d (IQR 0.94, 3.50)). Acute kidney injury (AKI) complicated by significantly elevated inflammatory markers was observed in 13 patients, mostly iMCD patients (n = 8). In renal pathology, patients with iMCD showed marked endothelial cell proliferation, whereas patients with POEMS syndrome displayed prominent mesangial cell proliferation. Treatments targeting primary diseases could improve proteinuria and renal function. Patients with GMA primarily presented with proteinuria and AKI. The common primary diseases included iMCD, POEMS syndrome, and exposure to anti-VEGF agents. GMA usually responds well to treatment of primary diseases. Renal pathology is essential for diagnosis. It is important to distinguish GMA from TMA that may respond to anti-C5 therapy.

Introduction

Thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) is a group of diseases characterized by microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia, and organ dysfunction due to platelet (PLT) thrombosis in the microcirculation [1]. The hallmark of renal histological changes in TMA lies in the blood vessels, presenting with intimal edema, endothelial cell swelling, microthrombi in the capillary lumen, and stenosis or occlusion of the capillary lumen, leading to renal ischemia [2–4]. Meanwhile, the glomeruli of TMA patients are often affected, and the typical changes include proliferation and swelling of glomerular endothelial cells, separation of endothelial cells from the basement membrane, thickening of the basement membrane, and expanded subendothelial space [2,3,5].

Glomerular microangiopathy (GMA) is a distinct group of patients with pathologic glomerular lesions of TMA without detectable microthrombi or intima edema of small arteries on renal biopsy [6]. The clinical manifestations of GMA are heterogeneous, typically presenting with various levels of proteinuria and renal dysfunction [6–12]. GMA was often observed in patients receiving anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) agents, POEMS (polyneuropathy, organomegaly, endocrinopathy, monoclonal plasma cell disorder, and skin changes) syndrome, and idiopathic multicenter Castleman’s disease-TAFRO (iMCD-TAFRO) [6–12]. Different from atypical hemolytic-uremic syndrome (aHUS), GMA patients usually showed kidney injury in the absence of renal microthrombi or peripheral schistocytes. This distinction is of therapeutic significance because the latter usually responds to C5 treatment (using eculizumab or ravulizumab) [13–15]. However, due to the rarity of these cases, there is still a lack of reports regarding comprehensive clinical data and prognosis of GMA.

In this study, we conducted a retrospective cohort study to describe the clinicopathological characteristics, treatment, and prognosis of patients with GMA.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This single-center retrospective cohort study included patients with GMA diagnosed by kidney biopsy between January 2005 and July 2023 at the Peking Union Medical College Hospital (PUMCH). GMA is characterized by TMA-like changes in glomeruli, including proliferation and swelling of glomerular endothelial cells, fibrin cap or hyalinosis in the capillary lumen (sometimes reported as pseudothrombi), expanded subendothelial space, stenosis or occlusion of the capillary lumen, without typical TMA changes in small arteries, including intimal edema, endothelial cell swelling, fragmented red blood cells, and microthrombi.

Informed consent

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Peking Union Medical College Hospital (No. K4717). The Ethics Committee of Peking Union Medical College Hospital has approved a waiver of informed consent.

Data collection and definitions

We thoroughly reviewed the medical records of the patients diagnosed with GMA to collect demographic data, clinical information, and laboratory test results. Nephrotic syndrome (NS) was defined as urinary protein ≥3.5 g/day and serum albumin level ≤3.0 g/dL. The diagnosis of acute kidney injury (AKI) and chronic kidney disease (CKD) were in line with the Kidney Disease Outcomes Quality Initiative (KDIGO) criteria [16,17]. The diagnostic criteria of idiopathic multicenter Castleman disease (iMCD) and iMCD-TAFRO were based on the consensus proposed by the Castleman Disease Collaborative Network, while the POEMS syndrome was diagnosed following the Chinese guidelines [18,19]. For the treatment response, complete remission (CR) in proteinuria was defined as 24 h urinary protein (24hUp) <0.3 g and serum albumin > 35 g/L and partial remission (PR) was defined as 24hUp between 0.3 g and 3.5 g and serum albumin >30 g/L. No remission (NR) was defined when the 24hUp change did not meet the CR or PR definition or when the patient entered end-stage renal disease (ESRD) or death. AKI recovery was defined as return of sCr to normal.

Pathological studies

Kidney biopsy specimens were prepared for immunofluorescence, light microscopy (LM), and electron microscopy (EM) following the laboratory routine described before [20]. Two senior pathologists independently viewed the images and made diagnoses. The pathological changes of GMA were divided into three groups: (1) endocapillary proliferative glomerulonephritis (ECPGN)-like group, (2) membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis (MPGN)-like group, and (3) simple GMA group. The ECPGN-like group presented with GMA accompanied by diffuse and prominent endothelial cell proliferation, excluding secondary autoimmune diseases or infections. The MPGN-like group presented with GMA accompanied by significant proliferation of mesangial cells with interposition and GBM duplication, but the fluorescence and EM tests failed to support immunocomplex-mediated diagnosis. Tubular atrophy and interstitial fibrosis were evaluated using the Banff lesion score i-IFTA system. Lesions were scored from 0 to 3, with corresponding percentages of the cortical area affected: 0 for less than 10%, 1 for 10–25%, 2 for 26–50%, and 3 for more than 50% [21]. Renal arteriolar sclerosing changes such as hyalinization and intimal fibroelastosis were also described.

Follow-up and outcomes

The treatment regimens and responses of the patients were evaluated and documented during the follow-up period, which extended to the onset of ESRD, death, or 31 August 2024.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), median, and interquartile range, depending on their distribution. These variables were analyzed using the Student’s t-test, Mann–Whitney’s U-test, and one-way ANOVA. Categorical variables were tested using chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS, version 26.0, SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL) and GraphPad Prism 9 (version 9.01, GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Clinical characteristics of GMA patients

A total of 32 GMA patients were included in the study. The patients were mainly male (62.5%), and their mean age was 46 ± 17 years. The most common clinical manifestations were edema (84.4%), pleural effusion (59.4%), hypertension (56.3%), pericardial effusion (56.3%), ascites (46.9%), and fever (37.5%) (Table 1). All patients showed various degrees of proteinuria, with an average 24hUP of 2.40 ± 1.66 g/d. Microscopic hematuria was observed in 68.8% (22/32) of patients. The median sCr level was 136 μmol/L (IQR 107.3, 166.0). Following the KDIGO definition, AKI occurred in 40.6% (13/32) of the patients, and 30.8% (4/13) of the AKI patients reported a decrease in urine volume. Another 15 patients showed elevation of sCr (median sCr 149 μmol/L, IQR (119, 171)) but without baseline sCr reference. One of the patients underwent dialysis. These patients exhibited no clinical evidence of microangiopathic hemolytic anemia. They showed no sign of peripheral schistocytes and the median lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) level was 183 (142, 242) U/L. The primary diseases of the GMA included iMCD (31.2%, 10/32), POEMS syndrome (21.9%, 7/32), and cancer patients receiving anti-VEGF agents (21.9%. 7/32), and miscellaneous (25.0%, 8/32) (Table 1).

Patients with iMCD, including iMCD-TAFRO, exhibited ECPGN-like manifestations in renal pathology, with extensive endothelial cell proliferation and mild mesangial cell proliferation without significant inflammatory cell infiltration (Figure 1(A,B)). Electron microscopy revealed prominent swelling and proliferation of endothelial cells with a significantly expanded subendothelial space (Figure 1(C)). In contrast, patients with POEMS syndrome displayed MPGN-like manifestations, with extensive and significant mesangial cell proliferation, mesangial interposition, double contour formation, and mild segmental endothelial cell proliferation (Figure 1(D,E)). Electron microscopy showed considerable proliferation of mesangial cells with interposition, significantly expanded subendothelial space, and red blood cells within the subendothelial area (Figure 1(F)).

Patients exposed to VEGF agents exhibited only simple GMA. Five patients presented with fibrin caps (Table 2) in the subendothelial and mesangial regions (Figure 1(H)), with slight endothelial cell proliferation (Figure 1(G)), and the expanded subendothelial space showed a double contour sign (Figure 1(H)). Under EM, the endothelial cells appeared swollen, with further widened subendothelial space and electron-dense materials in both the subendothelial and mesangial areas (Figure 1(I)). Among patients with other etiologies, two patients exhibited MPGN-like GMA (patients nos. 25 and 30), while the other patients showed simple GMA (Table 2).

The tubulointerstitial injuries were relatively mild (tubulointerstitial injury score 0–1 in 24/32 patients). Vascular lesions, such as atherosclerosis and hyalinization, were observed in 21 patients (65.6%) without any sign of intimal edema, endothelial cell swelling, or microthrombi. Immunofluorescent staining revealed mostly unremarkable findings. Only three patients with fibrin caps in the glomeruli exhibited weakly positive deposits of IgM, C4, and C1q (Figure S1). Renal pathology description is essential to differentiate GMA patients from aHUS-type of TMA, since the treatment strategy might be quite different.

Clinical features of GMA patients with different primary diseases

We did not observe significant differences in sCr and proteinuria among the patients with different primary diseases (Table 1). Hematuria and AKI were more prevalent in the patients with iMCD. Several inflammatory markers were elevated in iMCD patients, including white blood cell (WBC) count (7.23 ± 2.53 × 109/L, p = 0.018), neutrophil (NEUT) count (4.72 ± 2.08 × 109/L, p = 0.037), high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) levels of 63.99 mg/L (IQR 16.09, 96.61), and D-dimer levels of 3.79 mg/L FEU (IQR 2.47, 15.36) (Figure S2). Additionally, we noted a lower PLT count in patients with iMCD (Table 1). Elevated serum VEGF levels were observed in patients with POEMS syndrome, iMCD, and MRGS (Table S1).

Clinicopathological correlation

Patients with simple GMA had significantly higher 24hUp levels than those with MPGN-like GMA (Figure S3). Most AKI cases occurred in the ECPGN-like group (mostly iMCD-TAFRO patients, Table 1). No significant correlation was found between sCr levels and the degree of tubulointerstitial injury (Table S2).

Clinical features in GMA patients with and without AKI

Of the patients with AKI (n = 13), 69.2% male with a mean age of 40 ± 20 years. Their average 24hUp of 3.20 g/d (IQR 1.67, 4.19) and hematuria were present in 69.2% of the patients (Table 3). Their median sCr of 135.5 μmol/L (IQR 92, 170) and average urine volume in the 24hUp of 1,681.1 ± 749.0 mL. Patients with AKI had fever (69.2%), edema (84.6%), pleural effusion (76.9%), and ascites (61.5%) (Table 3). Compared with non-AKI patients, the AKI patients had lower serum albumin levels (29.8 ± 4.3 g/L, p < 0.001), PLT counts (146.5 ± 93.5 × 109/L, p = 0.030), and higher hs-CRP (65.18 mg/L, IQR 12.58, 97.38, p = 0.025) and D-dimer levels (2.96 mg/L FEU, IQR 1.21, 11.45) (Figure 2).

Treatment and outcomes

The median follow-up time for patients (n = 25) was 29 months (IQR 15, 57). One patient died of lung adenocarcinoma one year after admission, and one patient had ESRD on dialysis.

GMA treatment mostly targeted the primary diseases (Table 4). Proteinuria achieved remission in 22/25 patients (CR, n = 14; PR, n = 8). The renal function showed full recovery in 8/10 AKI patients after treatment. Another 8/14 patients with elevated sCr showed improvement of renal function during the follow-up (Table 4). The main treatment for cancer patients receiving anti-VEGF agents is the discontinuation of the drug. Patients with GMA and hematologic disorders receive various therapies, including glucocorticoids, glucocorticoid in combination with immunosuppressants, and therapies targeting plasma cells. Some patients (28.1%) received supportive treatment only. Two iMCD patients and the MRGS patient showed disease relapse (no. 9 in 25 months, no. 13 in 39 months, and no. 31 in 3 months). For the eight patients with other etiologies, treatment options included supportive therapy (50%, 4/8) and prednisone combined with cyclophosphamide (50%, 4/8). However, only two showed CR in proteinuria (Table 4).

Discussion

GMA is a rare group of diseases whose renal histopathology shows TMA-like changes in glomeruli without detectable microthrombi or intima edema of small arteries on renal biopsy [6–12]. As a rare group of diseases, this might be the largest single-center retrospective cohort of GMA. Three main causes of GMA were identified and the treatment targeting primary disease, or identification of an aHUS-type of TMA among this rare group of diseases, could improve patient prognosis.

Consistent with previous reports, iMCD (including iMCD-TAFRO), POEMS syndrome, and the use of anti-VEGF agents were the main causes of GMA [6–12]. However, we must notice that these rare diseases may involve true aHUS-type of TMA requiring use of anti-C5 therapy [13–15]. In addition, various hematological diseases (MM, MDS, and MGRS) and vasculitis were observed. No GMA were clearly reported in these diseases. Tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) have been reported to induce classical TMA while no documented cases of TKI-associated GMA have been published [22–25]. Given that TKIs also modulate the VEGF signaling pathway, clinicians should consider GMA as differential diagnosis when patients receiving TKI therapy develop proteinuria or renal dysfunction [22–25]. In our case series, primary disorders were not identified in four patients. These findings suggest that the etiology and pathophysiology of GMA are still not well understood and require further study.

Although proteinuria and renal insufficiency were commonly seen in patients with GMA, the renal manifestations varied depending on the primary diseases [5]. Different to other diseases, 80% of GMA patients with iMCD presented with AKI, characterized by decreased urine output and moderately elevated sCr levels. One patient even required dialysis support. Significantly elevated inflammatory markers and lower PLT counts were also observed in iMCD patients, which could be attributed to iMCD-TAFRO (80% of the included iMCD patients).

As a rare subset of specific iMCD, TAFRO syndrome patients usually show shared manifestations, including thrombocytopenia, anasarca, ascites, reticulin fibrosis in the bone marrow, renal dysfunction, and organomegaly (TAFRO) with significantly elevated IL-6, VEGF, tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), IL-1, and IL-8 [26–28].

In terms of renal pathology, in addition to the typical features of GMA reported previously, we observed subtle differences in the degree of proliferation of endothelial and mesangial cells, which were closely related to the underlying diseases (Table 2) [6]. Patients with iMCD exhibited GMA resembling ECPGN, whereas patients with POEMS syndrome exhibited GMA similar to MPGN. In previous reports, the renal pathology in iMCD patients revealed endothelial cell swelling, mesangial cell proliferation, and signs of double contour formation, typically described as TMA-like or MPGN-like changes [10,12,26,29–32]. However, after carefully reviewing the images published, we found significant endothelial cell proliferation in some cases [6,7,9,10]. The most common glomerular changes observed in POEMS syndrome included mesangial and endothelial cell proliferation [33]. Additional findings featured thickening of the glomerular basement membrane and double contour formation [33]. These observations align with data from 20 other cases of POEMS-associated renal disease, where 70% exhibited MPGN-like glomerulopathy and 10% showed signs of GMA [26,34].

There is some evidence of a relationship between clinical and pathological manifestations. For example, in patients, ECPGN-like (iMCD-TAFRO patients) was common with AKI, which often presented with decreased urine volume and decline in eGFR, which might be the result of occlusion of the loop lumen in the glomerulus due to endothelial changes, combined with volume redistribution throughout the body and volume depletion due to poor appetite and fever [27,35]. Meanwhile, proteinuria was significantly lower in MPGN-like GMA patients (mainly POEMS syndrome) than in patients who were exposed to anti-VEGF treatment.

The pathogenesis of GMA remains unclear. It has been suggested that an imbalance in VEGF in the kidneys is related to GMA in patients receiving anti-VEGF agents [6,7,17]. However, this link is questionable in patients with iMCD and POEMS syndrome. Although we observed elevated serum VEGF levels in patients with iMCD and POEMS syndrome, which was also found in previous studies, inconsistent patterns of intra-renal VEGF expression in these individuals were reported [10,26,36,37]. The discrepancy between systemic and local renal VEGF levels might stem from several proposed mechanisms [8,9]. First, the local renal VEGF is secreted by podocytes and renal tubular epithelial cells, which could be injured in iMCD and TMA [38]. Second, increased levels of serum VEGF could affect the glomerular capillary loops, which may inhibit VEGF production in podocytes through a negative feedback regulation [39]. Soluble fms-like tyrosine kinase 1 (sFlt-1), a receptor for VEGF, inhibits the binding of VEGF to its receptors on endothelial cells and podocytes, ultimately compromising the filtration barrier [40]. Additionally, elevated IL-6 might promote the VEGF pathway and mesangiolysis [37,41]. Although the role of complement in TMA is well-established, its role in GMA is unknown. In this way, in the context of anti-VEGF therapy, MCD, POEMS, etc., it is crucial to accurately distinguish between GMA and aHUS-type TMA. It is noteworthy that GMA only affects the glomerular capillaries rather than the peritubular capillaries (PTCs) [2]. This distinction may arise from differences in the expression of VEGF receptors, variations in microenvironments, and differences in hemodynamics among the glomeruli capillaries and PTC, which needs intensive study to disclose the underlying mechanisms involved [42,43].

The treatment of patients with GMA should target the primary disease, which has also been concluded in the literature [6–12]. For patients receiving anti-VEGF agents, drug discontinuation is the most important treatment. Therapies targeting primary diseases are usually used for GMA patients with hematological disorders (Table 4). The combination of siltuximab and high-dose corticosteroids is recommended as first-line therapy for patients with severe iMCD, including iMCD-TAFRO [18,44]. We have reported a case of iMCD-TAFRO successfully treated with the bortezomib–cyclophosphamide–dexamethasone (BCD) regimen before [45]. In this retrospective cohort, the patients received quite different regimens (Table 4 and Table S1) from the latest first-line treatment [18,46]. We speculated that this was partially due to the failure to obtain correct diagnosis during the initial hospitalization. Therefore, improvement in the understanding of GMA might be helpful to guide the diagnostic direction when we are uncertain about the primary diseases. Treatment of POEMS syndrome consists of plasma cell-directed therapy, including melphalan–dexamethasone (MD), cyclophosphamide–dexamethasone (CD), peripheral blood stem cell transplantation, lenalidomide–dexamethasone (RD), thalidomide–dexamethasone, and bortezomib [19,47]. The POEMS syndrome patients in this study received similar treatment and responded well (Table 4). However, since some of the diseases causing GMA may also trigger aHUS-type TMA, when the treatment targeting the primary disease fails to achieve the expected outcome, additional tests such as C4d or C5b-9 stains, complement-related genetic tests, or even repeated kidney biopsy should be considered to determine if there are indications for anti-complement therapy [47–51].

Our study had several limitations. As a retrospective cohort, some included patients were not correctly diagnosed at the initial stage, resulting in a lack of crucial data, such as serum VEGF and insufficient follow-up. Owing to the long time-span, some patients did not receive the latest recommended treatment, which might have affected their response to the therapeutic regimens.

Conclusions

We reported the largest cohort of patients with GMA without detectable microthrombi or intima edema of small arteries on renal biopsy. The iMCD, POEMS syndrome, and anti-VEGF therapy were the most common causes. The difference in clinicopathologic characteristics was highly correlated with etiology. The patients responded well to treatment targeting primary diseases.
