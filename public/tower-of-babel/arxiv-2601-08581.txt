Entanglement-swapping measurements for deterministic entanglement distribution
Mir Alimuddin, Jaemin Kim, Antonio Acín, Leonardo Zambrano

Abstract
Entanglement swapping is a key primitive for distributing entanglement across nodes in quantum networks. In standard protocols, the outcome of the intermediate measurement determines the resulting state, making the process inherently probabilistic and requiring postselection. In this work, we fully characterize those measurements under which entanglement swapping becomes deterministic: for arbitrary pure inputs, every measurement outcome produces local-unitarily equivalent states. We also show that an optimal measurement, maximizing a concurrence-type entanglement measure, is built from complex Hadamard matrices. For this optimal protocol, we provide a complete, dimension-dependent classification of deterministic entanglement-swapping measurements: unique in dimensions d = 2 , 3 d=2,3 , infinite for d = 4 d=4 , and comprising 72 72 inequivalent classes for d = 5 d=5 . We further consider a general network with multiple swapping nodes and show that, for d = 2 , 3 d=2,3 the resulting end-to-end state is independent of the order in which the repeaters perform the optimal measurements. Our results establish optimal entanglement-swapping schemes that are post-selection free, in the sense that they distribute entanglement across generic quantum network architectures without unfavorable measurement outcomes.

Noise robustness.

While our main results are stated for pure inputs, LU-deterministic swapping remains robust under independent bipartite depolarizing noise on the input links, provided the swapping measurement are related by only diagonal unitaries. Concretely, if the node performs a maximally entangled measurement with operator basis
\{E_{i}\}
satisfying
E_{i}=D_{L}^{(i)}E_{1}D_{R}^{(i)}
for diagonal unitaries
D_{L}^{(i)},D_{R}^{(i)}

VI

This robustness relies on the strong symmetry of depolarizing noise. For general mixed inputs, characterizing LU-equivalence is substantially more intricate: even for two qubits it typically requires evaluating complete sets of LU invariants

53

Entanglement distribution on generic quantum networks.

Bipartite measurements constitute the operational core of many practical quantum network architectures

;
16
;
45
;
44
;
41
;
40
. They enable the extension of entanglement across multiple nodes by performing local joint measurements on two subsystems at a time. Our
building-block
swapping module can thus be directly embedded into arbitrary network topologies, such as linear chains, two-dimensional lattices, or more complex networks.

A key property of this construction is its
universal LU-determinism
. Once each repeater implements a bipartite measurement satisfying the property, all possible network outputs are guaranteed to be mutually LU-equivalent, irrespective of the specific entanglement spectra of the elementary links. Furthermore, when each repeater implements a complex-Hadamard–based measurement, the per-swap performance becomes
optimal
(in terms of G-concurrence), and the resulting end-to-end entanglement along any chosen path is correspondingly maximized. Operationally, the protocol is
postselection-free
: all measurement outcomes are LU-equivalent and can be mapped to the computational basis via local unitary updates, so swaps are never retried due to “unlucky” results.

In a generic network scheme, a natural question follows: does the sequence in which intermediate nodes perform their swapping operations affect the final entangled state shared between the end users? We find that in low local dimensions the protocol exhibits a strong robustness with respect to the swapping order:

Remark 1

(Order independence in low dimensions)
.

Consider a linear chain

\mathsf{A}\!-\!\mathsf{N}_{1}\!-\!\cdots\!-\!\mathsf{N}_{L}\!-\!\mathsf{B}

in which each swapping node implements a maximally entangled measurement satisfying universal LU-determinism and optimality. For
d\in\{2,3\}
, the corrected end-to-end state shared by
\mathsf{A}
and
\mathsf{B}
is order-independent: the final LU-equivalence class does not depend on which node initiates the swapping process or in which order swaps are performed. For
d>3
, each swap remains optimal with respect to G-concurrence, but different swap orders can lead to final states with different Schmidt vectors.

Discussion & Outlook.

We identified the precise structural conditions that a bipartite swapping measurement must satisfy to ensure that all measurement outcomes yield LU–equivalent states for arbitrary pure input states. We then showed that these conditions are satisfied by measurements constructed from unbiased operators and that, when optimality is imposed, they correspond to complex Hadamard matrices. This, in turn, allowed us to provide a complete, dimension-dependent classification of optimal, deterministic, and universal entanglement-swapping measurements. We find a finite number of classes for dimensions
d=2,3,5
, while, surprisingly, an infinite family appears for
d=4k
, where
k
is positive integer. This nontrivial dimension dependence arises from the rich structure of complex Hadamard matrices. Measurements belonging to different classes yield output states that are not only locally unitary equivalent, but also the entangled states they generate cannot be transformed into one another by deterministic LOCC operations.
Generalization of the above classification of complex Hadamard matrices to arbitrary dimensions is, in general, highly nontrivial.

Our results have several practical implications for quantum networks. The first advantage is that, since all measurement outcomes within a given class produce LU–equivalent entangled states, the amount of entanglement generated in each round is known and identical. Consequently, the protocol is post-selection free, as all the measurement outcomes produce the same amount of entanglement. In contrast, a generic swapping protocol on an
N
-node network may yield up to
d^{2N}
distinct outcome branches, each potentially with a different amount of entanglement. For tasks requiring a fixed target state, this would necessitate independent post-processing for each branch, something that our deterministic protocol completely avoids. A second advantage of our framework is that it guarantees optimal entanglement distribution with respect to the G-concurrence, not only on average but for every outcome individually. Finally, we have experimental practicality. Fixed measurement devices combined with outcome-dependent local corrections constitute a natural and convenient setup for most experiments, and our universal swapping scheme is explicitly compatible with this architecture. Moreover, for low dimensions (
d=2,3
), the protocol is order independent: the distributed state is the same regardless of which node performs the measurement first (discussed in Appendix
VII
). This feature avoids global synchronization, allowing the protocol to start at any node as soon as a neighboring link becomes available, and thereby reducing the coherence-time demands on the physical systems.

Looking forward, several directions naturally emerge. First, extending the framework to mixed or noisy input states could provide insight into realistic implementations, including error tolerance and robustness against imperfect operations (see Appendix
VI
for depolarizing-noise robustness). Second, exploring multipartite and higher-dimensional swaps could generalize the protocol to networks beyond simple bipartite links, enabling scalable entanglement distribution in large, complex topologies. Finally, the connection to complex Hadamard matrices suggests potential links to other areas of quantum information and communication theory.

-dimensional maximally entangled state.

The initial states of the protocol are

\displaystyle\ket{\tilde{\psi}}

\displaystyle=(\tilde{A}\otimes\mathds{1})\ket{\Phi}_{\mathsf{AN_{A}}}=(U_{1}AV_{1}\otimes\mathds{1})\ket{\Phi}_{\mathsf{AN_{A}}},

(13)

\displaystyle\ket{\tilde{\phi}}

\displaystyle=(\tilde{B}\otimes\mathds{1})\ket{\Phi}_{\mathsf{N_{B}B}}=(V_{2}BU_{2}\otimes\mathds{1})\ket{\Phi}_{\mathsf{N_{B}B}},

(14)

where we have used the singular value decompositions of
\tilde{A}
and
\tilde{B}
. The matrices
A
and
B
are real and diagonal, and the normalization
\operatorname{tr}(\tilde{A}^{\dagger}\tilde{A})=\operatorname{tr}(\tilde{B}^{\dagger}\tilde{B})=1
implies
\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}=\|B\|_{\mathrm{F}}=1
.

In the first step of the protocol, Alice, Bob, and the node apply the unitary
U_{1}^{\dagger}\otimes V_{1}^{\ast}\otimes V_{2}^{\dagger}\otimes U_{2}^{\ast}
to the shared state
\ket{\tilde{\psi}}\otimes\ket{\tilde{\phi}}
on the system
\mathsf{AN_{A}N_{B}B}
. The full state of the system then becomes

\displaystyle(U_{1}^{\dagger}\otimes V_{1}^{\ast}\otimes V_{2}^{\dagger}\otimes U_{2}^{\ast})\ket{\tilde{\psi}}\otimes\ket{\tilde{\phi}}

\displaystyle=(U_{1}^{\dagger}\otimes V_{1}^{\ast}\otimes V_{2}^{\dagger}\otimes U_{2}^{\ast})(U_{1}AV_{2}\otimes\mathds{1}\otimes V_{2}BU_{2}\otimes\mathds{1})\ket{\Phi}_{\mathsf{AN_{A}}}\otimes\ket{\Phi}_{\mathsf{N_{B}B}}

\displaystyle=(A\otimes\mathds{1}\otimes B\otimes\mathds{1})\ket{\Phi}_{\mathsf{AN_{A}}}\otimes\ket{\Phi}_{\mathsf{N_{B}B}}

\displaystyle=\ket{\psi}\otimes\ket{\phi}

(15)

where we have used the identity
\mathds{1}\otimes X\ket{\Phi}=X^{T}\otimes\mathds{1}\ket{\Phi}
.

Thus, we can reduce the problem to the study of the protocol with initial states

\displaystyle\ket{\psi}=(A\otimes\mathds{1})\ket{\Phi},\qquad\ket{\phi}=(B\otimes\mathds{1})\ket{\Phi},

(16)

for diagonal and positive semidefinite
A
and
B
satisfying
\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}=\|B\|_{\mathrm{F}}=1

In the next step, the node performs a projective measurement
M_{\mathsf{N}}=\{M_{i}\}_{i=1}^{d^{2}}
with elements

\displaystyle M_{i}=\mathds{1}_{\mathsf{A}}\otimes\ket{\Gamma_{i}}\bra{\Gamma_{i}}_{\mathsf{N_{A}N_{B}}}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathsf{B}},\quad\mathrm{where}\penalty\ \ket{\Gamma_{i}}=(E_{i}^{*}\otimes\mathds{1})\ket{\Phi}_{\mathsf{N_{A}N_{B}}},

(17)

normalized such that
\operatorname{tr}(E_{i}^{T}E_{j}^{*})=\delta_{ij}
. After obtaining outcome
i
at the node, the (unnormalized) state shared by Alice and Bob is

\displaystyle\ket{\hat{\eta}_{i}}

\displaystyle=\bra{\Gamma_{i}}_{\mathsf{N_{A}N_{B}}}\left(A\otimes\mathds{1}\otimes B\otimes\mathds{1}\right)\ket{\Phi}_{\mathsf{AN_{A}}}\otimes\ket{\Phi}_{\mathsf{N_{B}B}}

\displaystyle=\bra{\Phi}_{\mathsf{N_{A}N_{B}}}\left(A\otimes E_{i}^{T}\otimes B\otimes\mathds{1}\right)\ket{\Phi}_{\mathsf{AN_{A}}}\otimes\ket{\Phi}_{\mathsf{N_{B}B}}

\displaystyle=\bra{\Phi}_{\mathsf{N_{A}N_{B}}}({AE_{i}B}\otimes\mathds{1}\otimes\mathds{1}\otimes\mathds{1})\ket{\Phi}_{\mathsf{AN_{A}}}\otimes\ket{\Phi}_{\mathsf{N_{B}B}}

\displaystyle=({AE_{i}B}\otimes\mathds{1})\ket{\Phi}_{\mathsf{AB}},

(18)

with probability

\displaystyle p_{i}=\braket{\hat{\eta}_{i}|\hat{\eta}_{i}}=\operatorname{tr}\big(B^{2}E_{i}^{\dagger}A^{2}E_{i}\big).

(19)

We study the requirements on the operator basis of the measurement
\mathcal{O}(M_{\mathsf{N}})=\{E_{i}^{*}\}_{i=1}^{d^{2}}
such that the protocol is
universally LU-deterministic
, meaning that for every pure input pair
\{\ket{\tilde{\psi}},\ket{\tilde{\phi}}\}
, the normalized output states
\ket{\eta_{i}}
on
\mathsf{AB}
conditioned on different measurement outcomes are mutually local unitarily equivalent (LU-equivalent).

II
The components of
E_{i}
have the same modulus
\tfrac{1}{d}

Assuming that
A
and
B
are full-rank operators, equality of the ranks of
AE_{i}B
and
AE_{j}B
is a necessary condition for these operators to have the same singular values. Since multiplication by full-rank operators preserves rank, this implies that
E_{i}
and
E_{j}
must have the same rank.
Having operators
E_{i}
with deficient rank would mean that the resulting states
\ket{\eta_{i}}=\ket{\hat{\eta}_{i}}/\sqrt{p_{i}}
would immediately have Schmidt rank smaller than
d
, even if the initial states
\ket{\psi}
and
\ket{\phi}
have Schmidt rank equal to
d
. Therefore, from now on, we consider only full-rank measurements
\{E_{i}^{*}\}_{i=1}^{d^{2}}
.

Here we show that if the normalized output states
\{\ket{\eta_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{d^{2}}
are all LU-equivalent, then the probabilities
p_{i}
are uniform and all
E_{i}^{*}
have entries of equal modulus
\tfrac{1}{d}
.

Theorem 3
.

If
\ket{\eta_{i}}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{p_{i}}}\ket{\hat{\eta}_{i}}
is LU-equivalent to
\ket{\eta_{j}}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{p_{j}}}\ket{\hat{\eta}_{j}}
for all
i,j
, and all
\{E_{i}^{*}\}_{i=1}^{d^{2}}
are non-singular, then the probabilities
p_{i}
satisfy

p_{i}=\frac{\left|\det E_{i}\right|^{2/d}}{\sum_{j}\left|\det E_{j}\right|^{2/d}}.

Proof.

If two states are LU-equivalent, their singular values must be equal. If the singular values of
X
are equal to those of
Y
, then
\left|\det X\right|=\left|\det Y\right|
. Using this, we have

\displaystyle\left|\det\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{p_{i}}}AE_{i}B\right)\right|^{2/d}

\displaystyle=\left|\det\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{p_{j}}}AE_{j}B\right)\right|^{2/d},

(20)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p_{i}}\left|\det A\right|^{2/d}\left|\det E_{i}\right|^{2/d}\left|\det B\right|^{2/d}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p_{j}}\left|\det A\right|^{2/d}\left|\det E_{j}\right|^{2/d}\left|\det B\right|^{2/d}.

(21)

Hence,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p_{i}}\left|\det E_{i}\right|^{2/d}=\frac{1}{p_{j}}\left|\det E_{j}\right|^{2/d}=C,

(22)

for some constant
C
. From
\sum_{i}p_{i}=1
, we obtain
C=\sum_{i}\left|\det E_{i}\right|^{2/d}
. Since
\left|\det(E_{i})\right|\neq 0
, each probability is therefore

\displaystyle p_{i}=\frac{\left|\det E_{i}\right|^{2/d}}{\sum_{j}\left|\det E_{j}\right|^{2/d}}.

(23)

∎

This shows that the probabilities must be independent of the initial states if the resulting states are to be unitarily equivalent and all
\{E^{\ast}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{d^{2}}
are full rank. However, the set
\{E^{\ast}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{d^{2}}
itself may still depend on the initial states, as it must be chosen so that the output states of the protocol are LU-equivalent.

Theorem 4
.

Let
\ket{\eta_{i}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{p_{i}}}(AE_{i}B\otimes\mathds{1})\ket{\Phi}
be a normalized quantum state, where each
E_{i}
is non-singular and satisfies
\|E_{i}\|_{F}=1
, and
A
and
B
are real diagonal matrices such that
\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}=\|B\|_{\mathrm{F}}=1
.
If, for any choice of
A
and
B
, all states in the set
\{\ket{\eta_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{d^{2}}
are unitarily equivalent to one another, then necessarily
p_{i}=\tfrac{1}{d^{2}}
and all matrix elements
\langle m|E_{i}^{\ast}|n\rangle
satisfy
\left|\langle m|E_{i}^{\ast}|n\rangle\right|=\tfrac{1}{d}
for all
i,m,n
.

Proof.

From the previous theorem, we have

\displaystyle p_{j}\left|\det E_{i}\right|^{2/d}=p_{i}\left|\det E_{j}\right|^{2/d},

(24)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}[BE_{j}^{\dagger}A^{2}E_{j}B]\left|\det E_{i}\right|^{2/d}=\operatorname{Tr}[BE_{i}^{\dagger}A^{2}E_{i}B]\left|\det E_{j}\right|^{2/d},

(25)

\displaystyle\sum_{mn}b_{m}^{2}a_{n}^{2}\left(|\langle m|E_{j}|n\rangle|^{2}\left|\det E_{i}\right|^{2/d}-|\langle m|E_{i}|n\rangle|^{2}\left|\det E_{j}\right|^{2/d}\right)=0.

(26)

This implies that
|\langle m|E_{j}|n\rangle|^{2}\left|\det E_{i}\right|^{2/d}=|\langle m|E_{i}|n\rangle|^{2}\left|\det E_{j}\right|^{2/d}
. Since
\|E_{j}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=\sum_{mn}|\langle m|E_{j}|n\rangle|^{2}=1
, it follows that
\left|\det E_{i}\right|=\left|\det E_{j}\right|
for all
i,j
. This in turn implies that

p_{i}=\frac{1}{d^{2}},

since
p_{i}=\frac{\left|\det E_{i}\right|^{2/d}}{\sum_{j}\left|\det E_{j}\right|^{2/d}}
.
We then have

\displaystyle p_{i}=\sum_{mn}b_{m}^{2}a_{n}^{2}|\langle m|E_{i}|n\rangle|^{2}=\frac{1}{d^{2}}.

(27)

Because this equality must hold for all
A
and
B
, and since
\sum_{mn}b_{m}^{2}a_{n}^{2}=1
, it follows that for all
i,m,n
,

\left|\langle m|E_{i}|n\rangle\right|=\left|\langle m|E_{i}^{\ast}|n\rangle\right|=\frac{1}{d}.

∎

III
Characterization of LU-equivalent measurement operators

Now, we derive the necessary and sufficient conditions under which two measurement operators
E_{i}
and
E_{j}
yield unitarily equivalent states for all input pairs. We show that this equivalence restricts the relationship between operators to the
phase-conjugation
classes defined in the main text. We begin with two technical lemmas.

Lemma 3
.

Let
\varphi_{i}
,
\varphi_{r}
,
\theta_{i}
,
\theta_{r}
,
\delta
and
\alpha
be six phases such that

\displaystyle\varphi_{i}-\varphi_{r}=s^{\prime}(\theta_{i}-\theta_{r})+\alpha\pmod{2\pi}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\theta_{i}-\theta_{r}=\delta+\pi\epsilon\pmod{2\pi},

(28)

where
\epsilon\in\{0,1\}
and
s^{\prime}\in\{-1,1\}
. Then,
\varphi_{i}
is independent of
\epsilon
,

\displaystyle\varphi_{i}=s\theta_{i}+(\varphi_{r}+s\theta_{r})+\delta(s^{\prime}-s)+\alpha\pmod{2\pi}

(29)

for
s\in\{-1,1\}
.

Proof.

From Eq. (
28
), we obtain

\displaystyle\varphi_{i}-\varphi_{r}=s^{\prime}(\delta+\pi\epsilon)+\alpha

(30)

Subtracting
s(\theta_{i}-\theta_{r})=s(\delta+\pi\epsilon)\pmod{2\pi}
for
s\in\{-1,1\}
to the equation above, we obtain

\displaystyle\varphi_{i}=s\theta_{i}+(\varphi_{r}+s\theta_{r})+(s^{\prime}-s)\delta+(s^{\prime}-s)\pi\epsilon+\alpha\pmod{2\pi}.

(31)

If
s^{\prime}
and
s
have the same sign, then
(s^{\prime}-s)\pi\epsilon=0
. If they have different sign, then
(s^{\prime}-s)\pi\epsilon=\pm 2\pi\epsilon=0\pmod{2\pi}
. Then, the equation is independent of
\epsilon
.
∎

Lemma 4
.

Let
\{\theta_{k}\}_{k=1}^{d}
and
\{\varphi_{k}\}_{k=1}^{d}
be real numbers. Assume that for all
a_{k}\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{k=1}^{d}a_{k}e^{i\theta_{k}}\right|=\left|\sum_{k=1}^{d}a_{k}e^{i\varphi_{k}}\right|.

(32)

Then there exists
s\in\{-1,+1\}
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
such that for all
k
,

\displaystyle\varphi_{k}=s\theta_{k}+\alpha\pmod{2\pi}.

(33)

Proof.

For all
a_{k}\geq 0
, squaring both sides and expanding gives

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{k=1}^{d}a_{k}e^{i\theta_{k}}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}a_{k}^{2}+2\sum_{k<j}a_{k}a_{j}\mathrm{Re}\left(e^{i(\theta_{k}-\theta_{j})}\right),

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{k=1}^{d}a_{k}e^{i\varphi_{k}}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}a_{k}^{2}+2\sum_{k<j}a_{k}a_{j}\mathrm{Re}\left(e^{i(\varphi_{k}-\varphi_{j})}\right).

Since this must hold for all
a_{k}\geq 0
, it follows that
\cos(\theta_{k}-\theta_{j})=\cos(\varphi_{k}-\varphi_{j})
for all
j,k
. This in turn implies that for any
j,k
,

\varphi_{k}-\varphi_{j}=\pm(\theta_{k}-\theta_{j})\pmod{2\pi}.

(34)

We now show that this sign is constant across all pairs of angles. Fix an index
1\leq r\leq d
and, for each
k
, choose
s_{k}\in\{\pm 1\}
such that

\varphi_{k}-\varphi_{r}=s_{k}\,(\theta_{k}-\theta_{r})\pmod{2\pi}.

(35)

Assume there exist indices
j,k
with
s_{j}=+1
and
s_{k}=-1
. Using Eq. (
35
), we obtain:

\displaystyle\varphi_{j}-\varphi_{k}=(\varphi_{j}-\varphi_{r})-(\varphi_{k}-\varphi_{r})=(\theta_{j}-\theta_{r})+(\theta_{k}-\theta_{r})=\theta_{j}+\theta_{k}-2\theta_{r}\pmod{2\pi}.

(36)

Substituting Eq. (
34
) gives

\displaystyle\theta_{j}+\theta_{k}-2\theta_{r}=(\theta_{j}-\theta_{k})\pmod{2\pi}\qquad\text{or}\qquad\theta_{j}+\theta_{k}-2\theta_{r}=-(\theta_{j}-\theta_{k})\pmod{2\pi}.

(37)

Simplifying, we obtain

\displaystyle\theta_{k}-\theta_{r}=0\pmod{\pi}\qquad\mathrm{or}\qquad\theta_{j}-\theta_{r}=0\pmod{\pi}.

(38)

Therefore, if a sign mismatch
s_{j}\neq s_{k}
occurs, at least one of
\theta_{j}
or
\theta_{k}
is congruent to
\theta_{r}
modulo
\pi
. By this argument, if there exist indices
j,k
such that
(\theta_{j}-\theta_{r})\not\equiv 0\pmod{\pi}
and
(\theta_{k}-\theta_{r})\not\equiv 0\pmod{\pi}
, then
s_{j}=s_{k}
. Hence all indices with
\theta_{k}-\theta_{r}\not\equiv 0\pmod{\pi}
share the same sign
s
. For these indices,
s_{k}=s
constant, and setting
\alpha=\varphi_{r}-s\,\theta_{r}
, Eq. (
35
) gives

\displaystyle\varphi_{k}=s\,\theta_{k}+\alpha\pmod{2\pi},

(39)

as required.

For an index
q
with
\theta_{q}-\theta_{r}=0\pmod{\pi}
we have

\displaystyle\theta_{q}-\theta_{r}=\epsilon\pi\pmod{2\pi},

(40)

for
\epsilon\in\{0,1\}
. Then, using Thm.
3
and fixing
s
as the one that share the non-degenerate indices (if no one exists, pick it arbitrarily) we obtain

\displaystyle\varphi_{q}=s\theta_{q}+\alpha,

(41)

for
\alpha=\varphi_{r}-s\theta_{r}
. This means that for all
k
,
\varphi_{k}=s\theta_{k}+\alpha
.

∎

For two states, equality of the eigenvalues of their reduced density matrices is equivalent to being related by local unitaries. The following theorem characterizes the measurements
{E_{i}}
that generate unitarily connected states.

Theorem 5
.

Let
\rho=AVB^{2}V^{\dagger}A
and
\sigma=AUB^{2}U^{\dagger}A
be density matrices, with
U
and
V
non-singular matrices such that
|u_{ij}|=|v_{ij}|\neq 0
for all
i,j
, and
A
,
B
real diagonal matrices such that
\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}=\|B\|_{\mathrm{F}}=1
. Then, the eigenvalues of
\rho
and
\sigma
coincide for all
A,B
if and only if
V=D_{L}UD_{R}
or
V=D_{L}U^{*}D_{R}
for some diagonal unitary matrices
D_{L},D_{R}
.

Proof.

Let
A
and
B
be arbitrary diagonal real matrices, and suppose that

\rho=AVB^{2}V^{\dagger}A
and
\sigma=AUB^{2}U^{\dagger}A

have the same eigenvalues. Then their Frobenius norms must coincide:
\|\rho\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=\|\sigma\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}
. This implies that

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}a_{i}^{2}a_{j}^{2}\left(\Big\lvert\sum_{k}b_{k}^{2}u_{ik}u_{jk}^{*}\Big\rvert^{2}-\Big\lvert\sum_{k}b_{k}^{2}v_{ik}v_{jk}^{*}\Big\rvert^{2}\right)=0.

(42)

Because
A
is arbitrary, this implies that for all
i,j
,

\displaystyle\Big\lvert\sum_{k}b_{k}^{2}u_{ik}u_{jk}^{*}\Big\rvert=\Big\lvert\sum_{k}b_{k}^{2}v_{ik}v_{jk}^{*}\Big\rvert.

(43)

Writing
u_{ik}=ce^{i\theta_{ik}}
and
v_{ik}=ce^{i\varphi_{ik}}
for constant
c>0
, and using Lemma
4
, this condition translates into the phase relation

\displaystyle\varphi_{ik}-\varphi_{jk}\equiv s_{ij}(\theta_{ik}-\theta_{jk})+\alpha_{ij}\pmod{2\pi}\qquad\text{for all }k,

(44)

where
s_{ij}\in\{\pm 1\}
encodes a possible global conjugation and

\alpha_{ij}
is a phase depending on the pair
(i,j)
.

Now we want to find a relation between
\varphi_{ij}
and
\theta_{ij}
. Fix a reference row
r
. For each
j
we can rewrite Eq. (
44
) as

\varphi_{ik}-\varphi_{rk}\equiv s_{ir}\,(\theta_{ik}-\theta_{rk})+\alpha_{ir}\pmod{2\pi}\qquad\text{for all }k.

(45)

Suppose there exist two rows
i,j
with opposite signs relative to
r
, say
s_{ir}=+1
and
s_{jr}=-1
. Then, using Eq. (
45
) and subtracting the two relations gives

\displaystyle\varphi_{ik}-\varphi_{jk}

\displaystyle\equiv(\theta_{ik}+\theta_{jk}-2\theta_{rk})+(\alpha_{ir}-\alpha_{jr})\pmod{2\pi}.

(46)

On the other hand, applying Eq. (
44
) directly to rows
i,j
gives

\displaystyle\varphi_{ik}-\varphi_{jk}\equiv s_{ij}(\theta_{ik}-\theta_{jk})+\alpha_{ij}\pmod{2\pi}.

(47)

Comparing the two expressions, we find that for all
k

(1-s_{ij})\theta_{ik}+(1+s_{ij})\theta_{jk}-2\theta_{rk}\equiv\alpha_{ij}-(\alpha_{ir}-\alpha_{jr})\pmod{2\pi},

(48)

Now we have two cases:

1.

If
s_{ij}=+1
, then Eq. (
48
) reduces to

\displaystyle 2(\theta_{jk}-\theta_{rk})\equiv\alpha_{ij}-(\alpha_{ir}-\alpha_{jr})\equiv\text{constant}\pmod{2\pi}.

(49)

Thus
\theta_{jk}-\theta_{rk}
is independent of
k
modulo
\pi
, which implies that the phases of row
j
differ from those of
r
by a column-independent shift modulo
\pi
.

2.

If
s_{ij}=-1
, then (
48
) gives

\displaystyle 2(\theta_{ik}-\theta_{rk})\equiv\alpha_{ij}-(\alpha_{ir}-\alpha_{jr})\equiv\text{constant}\pmod{2\pi},

(50)

so row
i
is proportional to row
r
.

Hence, if there exist two rows
i,j
with
s_{ir}\neq s_{jr}
, at least one of them must be degenerate with respect to
r
. Consequently, for all the rows that are non-degenerate with respect to
r
, all
s_{ir}
must equal a common sign
s
.
Defining phases
\alpha_{i}=\alpha_{ir}
and
\beta_{k}=\varphi_{rk}-s\theta_{rk}
, from Eq. (
45
) we obtain

\displaystyle\varphi_{ik}\equiv s\,\theta_{ik}+\alpha_{i}+\beta_{k}\pmod{2\pi},

(51)

valid for all rows
i
and columns
k
.

Now, suppose row
i
is degenerate. Then there exist
\epsilon_{ik}\in\{0,1\}
such that

\theta_{ik}-\theta_{rk}\equiv\delta_{i}+\pi\epsilon_{ik}\pmod{2\pi}\qquad(\forall k)

(52)

where
\delta_{i}=(\alpha_{ij}-\alpha_{ir}+\alpha_{jr})/2
. Fix the global sign
s
selected by the non-degenerate rows (if none exist, choose
s
arbitrarily). Using Eq. (
45
) and Theorem
3
, with
\alpha=\alpha_{ir}
,
\delta=\delta_{i}
and
\epsilon=\epsilon_{ik}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\varphi_{ik}=s\theta_{ik}+(\varphi_{rk}+s\theta_{rk})+\delta_{i}(s_{ir}-s)+\alpha_{ir}

(53)

Defining
\beta_{k}=\varphi_{rk}+s\theta_{rk}
and
\alpha_{i}=\delta_{i}(s_{ir}-s)+\alpha_{ir}
, we obtain for every degenerate row
i
,

\varphi_{ik}\equiv s\,\theta_{ik}+\alpha_{i}+\beta_{k}\pmod{2\pi}\qquad(\forall k).

(54)

Thus we conclude that there exists a global
s\in\{\pm 1\}
, row-dependent phases
\alpha_{i}
, and column-dependent phases
\beta_{k}
such that

\displaystyle\varphi_{ik}\equiv s\,\theta_{ik}+\alpha_{i}+\beta_{k}\pmod{2\pi}\qquad\text{for all }i,k,

(55)

which is equivalent to
V=D_{L}UD_{R}
if
s=+1
, and
V=D_{L}U^{*}D_{R}
if
s=-1
.

The converse direction is immediate.
∎

IV
Optimality in terms of G-concurrence

Recall that the (unnormalized) output state shared by Alice and Bob after obtaining measurement outcome
i
at the node is written as
\ket{\hat{\eta}_{i}}=(AE_{i}B\otimes\mathds{1})\ket{\Phi}_{\mathsf{AB}}
with outcome probability
p_{i}=\braket{\hat{\eta}_{i}|\hat{\eta}_{i}}
and the normalized state
\ket{\eta_{i}}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{p_{i}}}\ket{\hat{\eta}_{i}}
.
Define

\displaystyle\hat{G}_{i}=AE_{i}B,\penalty\ \penalty\ G_{i}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{p_{i}}}\hat{G}_{i}=p_{i}^{-1/2}AE_{i}B,

(59)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\ket{\hat{\eta}_{i}}=\sqrt{p_{i}}\ket{\eta_{i}}

\displaystyle=(\sqrt{p_{i}}G_{i}\otimes\mathds{1})\ket{\Phi}

\displaystyle=(\hat{G}_{i}\otimes\mathds{1})\ket{\Phi}

\displaystyle=(AE_{i}B\otimes\mathds{1})\ket{\Phi}.

(60)

Note that
G_{i}
is normalized, while
\hat{G}_{i}
is not:

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}[G_{i}^{\dagger}G_{i}]=1,\penalty\ \penalty\ \operatorname{Tr}[\hat{G}_{i}^{\dagger}\hat{G}_{i}]=p_{i}.

(61)

The G-concurrence of a pure state in dimension
d
is defined as follows.

Definition 3

(G-concurrence)
.

Consider a normalized pure state
\ket{\psi}
in dimension
d
with the coefficient matrix
M
:
\ket{\psi}=(M\otimes\mathds{1})\ket{\Phi}
.
Its G-concurrence, denoted as
\mathcal{C}_{d}(\ket{\psi})
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{d}(\ket{\psi})=d\left|\det M\right|^{\tfrac{2}{d}}.

(62)

Lemma 5

(Determinant Arithmetic Mean-Geometric Mean inequality)
.

Let
\operatorname{sv}(M)=(\sqrt{\lambda_{1}},\ldots,\sqrt{\lambda_{d}})
, where
\operatorname{Tr}[M^{\dagger}M]=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}=1
. From the Arithmetic Mean-Geometric Mean inequality, we have

\displaystyle\left|\det M\right|^{2/d}=(\prod_{i}\lambda_{i})^{1/d}\leq\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}=\frac{1}{d}\operatorname{Tr}[M^{\dagger}M],

(63)

where the equality holds when
\lambda_{i}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{d}}
for all
i
, i.e.,
(M\otimes\mathds{1})\ket{\Phi}=(U\otimes\mathds{1})\ket{\phi^{+}_{d}}
for the normalized maximally entangled state
\ket{\phi^{+}_{d}}
and some unitary
U
.

We utilize Lemma
5
and the fact that
\det(pM)=p^{d}\det(M)

k\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}

Lemma 10
.

Let
d=4k
with
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
. Then the set of complex Hadamard matrices of order
d

contains infinitely many distinct equivalence classes under row/column phase multiplications and complex conjugation.

Proof.

We say
H\sim_{\mathrm{PC}}K
if either
K=D_{1}HD_{2}
or
K=D_{1}H^{\ast}D_{2}
for some
diagonal unitaries
D_{1},D_{2}
.

Step 1: a PC-invariant.

For any matrix
H=(h_{ij})
with nonzero entries, define the cross-ratio

\chi(H):=\frac{h_{1,2}\,h_{2,3}}{h_{1,3}\,h_{2,2}}.

If
H^{\prime}=D_{1}HD_{2}
, then
h^{\prime}_{ij}=a_{i}\,h_{ij}\,b_{j}
and the diagonal phases cancel in the
ratio, hence
\chi(H^{\prime})=\chi(H)
. If
H^{\prime}=H^{\ast}
, then
\chi(H^{\prime})=\chi(H)^{\ast}
.
Therefore, the unordered pair
\{\chi(H),\chi(H)^{\ast}\}
is invariant under
\sim_{\mathrm{PC}}
.

Step 2: the
4\times 4
family.

For the family
U(\alpha)
in (
83
), a direct computation gives

\chi(U(\alpha))=\frac{U(\alpha)_{1,2}\,U(\alpha)_{2,3}}{U(\alpha)_{1,3}\,U(\alpha)_{2,2}}=\frac{\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)}{\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)\left(\tfrac{ie^{i\alpha}}{2}\right)}=ie^{-i\alpha}.

Hence, if
U(\alpha)\sim_{\mathrm{PC}}U(\beta)
then either

ie^{-i\alpha}=ie^{-i\beta}
or
ie^{-i\alpha}=(ie^{-i\beta})^{\ast}=-ie^{i\beta}
.
Equivalently,

\alpha\equiv\beta\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2\pi)\qquad\text{or}\qquad\alpha\equiv\pi-\beta\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2\pi).

Restricting to
\alpha,\beta\in[0,\pi/2)
forces
\alpha=\beta
. Thus the set

\{U(\alpha):\alpha\in[0,\pi/2)\}
contains infinitely many PC-inequivalent matrices.

Step 3: multiply by a Fourier matrix to reach
d=4k
.

Let
F_{k}
be the
k\times k
Fourier complex Hadamard matrix, and define

V_{k}(\alpha):=F_{k}\otimes U(\alpha).

Then
V_{k}(\alpha)
is a complex Hadamard matrix of order
4k
.
Now pick the same four positions in the
U(\alpha)
part while keeping the Fourier indices
fixed (e.g. using the first row/column block). The Fourier factors cancel in the cross-ratio,
so

\chi\!\left(V_{k}(\alpha)\right)=\chi\!\left(U(\alpha)\right)=ie^{-i\alpha}.

Thus
V_{k}(\alpha)\sim_{\mathrm{PC}}V_{k}(\beta)
with
\alpha,\beta\in[0,\pi/2)
implies

\alpha=\beta
. Since
[0,\pi/2)
is infinite (indeed uncountable), there are infinitely many
PC-inequivalent complex Hadamard matrices in dimension
d=4k
.
∎

V.4

Lemma 11
.

The equivalence relations

(phase and permutation) and

We exhibit the two required counterexamples in different dimensions.

(i)

but

(ii)

does not imply

In dimension

in (

\overline{U(\alpha)}=U(\pi-\alpha)

However, under

VI
Noise Robustness under Depolarizing Noise

We consider mixed inputs obtained by applying bipartite depolarizing noise to each link:

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathsf{AN_{A}}}

\displaystyle=(1-p)\,\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi}+p\,\frac{\mathds{1}}{d^{2}},\qquad\rho_{\mathsf{N_{B}B}}=(1-q)\,\ket{\phi}\bra{\phi}+q\,\frac{\mathds{1}}{d^{2}},

(84)

where the ideal pure states are

\displaystyle\ket{\psi}=(A\otimes\mathds{1})\ket{\Phi},\qquad\ket{\phi}=(B\otimes\mathds{1})\ket{\Phi},

(85)

with
A,B
diagonal, positive semidefinite, and normalized as
\|A\|_{F}=\|B\|_{F}=1
.
Accordingly, the reduced states are diagonal in the computational basis:

\displaystyle\rho_{\psi}:=\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathsf{N_{A}}}[\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi}]=A^{2},\qquad\rho_{\phi}:=\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathsf{N_{B}}}[\ket{\phi}\bra{\phi}]=B^{2}.

(86)

The node performs a projective measurement
\{M_{i}\}_{i=1}^{d^{2}}
on
\mathsf{N_{A}N_{B}}
:

\displaystyle M_{i}=\mathds{1}_{\mathsf{A}}\otimes\ket{\Gamma_{i}}\bra{\Gamma_{i}}_{\mathsf{N_{A}N_{B}}}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathsf{B}},\qquad\ket{\Gamma_{i}}=(E_{i}^{\ast}\otimes\mathds{1})\ket{\Phi}_{\mathsf{N_{A}N_{B}}},

(87)

and we assume that
\{E_{i}^{\ast}\}_{i=1}^{d^{2}}
is an operator basis of a maximally entangled measurement.

Diagonal PC-orbit assumption.

For robustness, we impose the following
sufficient
structural condition: all measurement operators
lie in the same
phase-multiplication orbit
(i.e., the non-conjugate part of a PC-class),

\displaystyle E_{i}=D_{L}^{(i)}\,E_{1}\,D_{R}^{(i)}\qquad\text{for all $i$},

(88)

where
D_{L}^{(i)},D_{R}^{(i)}
are diagonal unitaries in the computational basis.
(We discuss the conjugation branch
E_{i}=D_{L}^{(i)}E^{\ast}_{1}D_{R}^{(i)}
in Remark
5
below.)

Conditional output states.

For outcome
i
, the unnormalized post-measurement state on
\mathsf{AB}
is

\displaystyle\tilde{\eta}_{i,\mathsf{AB}}:=\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathsf{N_{A}N_{B}}}\!\left[M_{i}\,\bigl(\rho_{\mathsf{AN_{A}}}\otimes\rho_{\mathsf{N_{B}B}}\bigr)\right].

(89)

A direct expansion using Eq. (
84
) yields

\displaystyle\tilde{\eta}_{i,\mathsf{AB}}=\frac{1}{d^{2}}\Bigl[(1-p)(1-q)\,\ket{\eta_{i}}\bra{\eta_{i}}+(1-p)q\,\rho_{\psi}\otimes\frac{\mathds{1}}{d}+p(1-q)\,\frac{\mathds{1}}{d}\otimes\rho_{\phi}+pq\,\frac{\mathds{1}}{d}\otimes\frac{\mathds{1}}{d}\Bigr],

(90)

where the pure-output vectors are

\displaystyle\ket{\eta_{i}}=\bigl(d\,AE_{i}B\otimes\mathds{1}\bigr)\ket{\Phi}.

(91)

From Lemma
1
, all outcome probabilities are given by
p_{i}=\tfrac{1}{d^{2}}
. Hence the normalized conditional output state is simply

\displaystyle\eta_{i,\mathsf{AB}}=d^{2}\,\tilde{\eta}_{i,\mathsf{AB}},

(92)

i.e., the bracketed expression in Eq. (
90
).

LU-determinism under depolarizing noise (sufficient condition).

By the phase-multiplication assumption (
88
) and the diagonality of
A,B
, we have

\displaystyle AE_{i}B=D_{L}^{(i)}\,(AE_{1}B)\,D_{R}^{(i)}.

(93)

Using
(U\otimes V)(C\otimes\mathds{1})\ket{\Phi}=(UCV^{T}\otimes\mathds{1})\ket{\Phi}
and the fact that diagonal matrices satisfy
D^{T}=D
,
it follows that

\displaystyle\ket{\eta_{i}}=(D_{L}^{(i)}\otimes D_{R}^{(i)})\,\ket{\eta_{1}}.

(94)

Since
\rho_{\psi}
and
\rho_{\phi}
are diagonal in the same basis by Eq. (
86
),
the same diagonal unitaries commute with the noise terms:

\displaystyle D_{L}^{(i)}\,\rho_{\psi}\,D_{L}^{(i)\dagger}=\rho_{\psi},\qquad D_{R}^{(i)}\,\rho_{\phi}\,D_{R}^{(i)\dagger}=\rho_{\phi}.

(95)

Combining this with Eq. (
90
) shows that the mixed outputs are mutually LU-equivalent:
for all
i
,

\displaystyle\eta_{i,\mathsf{AB}}=(D_{L}^{(i)}\otimes D_{R}^{(i)})\,\eta_{1,\mathsf{AB}}\,(D_{L}^{(i)}\otimes D_{R}^{(i)})^{\dagger}.

(96)

Therefore, the universal LU-determinism of the swapping measurement is robust to bipartite depolarizing noise
under the sufficient condition (
88
).

Remark 5

(Obstruction from the conjugation branch)
.

If some outcomes satisfy
E_{i}=D_{L}^{(i)}E^{\ast}_{1}D_{R}^{(i)}
with
E_{1}\not\sim D_{L}E^{\ast}_{1}D_{R}
(i.e.,
E_{1}
is not
self-conjugate up to diagonal phases), then the local unitaries mapping
\ket{\eta_{1}}\mapsto\ket{\eta_{i}}
in the pure case
need not be diagonal. In that situation, for generic inputs with nondegenerate
\rho_{\psi}
and
\rho_{\phi}
,
such non-diagonal corrections do not preserve the noise terms
\rho_{\psi}\otimes\mathds{1}/d
and
\mathds{1}/d\otimes\rho_{\phi}
,
and mixed-state LU-determinism can fail.

F_{\mathbf{s}_{\mathrm{L}}}=\mathrm{L}(A_{0},\ldots,A_{k}),\qquad F_{\mathbf{s}_{\mathrm{R}}}=\mathrm{L}(A_{k+1},\ldots,A_{n-1}).

Therefore

F_{\mathbf{s}}=F_{\mathbf{s}_{\mathrm{L}}}\star F_{\mathbf{s}_{\mathrm{R}}}=\mathrm{L}(A_{0},\ldots,A_{k})\star\mathrm{L}(A_{k+1},\ldots,A_{n-1})=\mathrm{L}(A_{0},\ldots,A_{n-1}),

where the last equality is exactly the lemma (
135
) with
X=\mathrm{L}(A_{0},\ldots,A_{k})
and

(B_{1},\ldots,B_{m})=(A_{k+1},\ldots,A_{n-1})
.
Thus (
136
) holds for length
n
, completing the induction.

Consequently,
F_{\mathbf{s}}
is independent of
\mathbf{s}
, and every swapping strategy yields the same final corrected diagonal coefficient matrix (hence the same LU class between
\mathsf{A}
and
\mathsf{B}
).
∎
