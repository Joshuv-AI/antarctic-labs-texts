Downregulation of p120 catenin in the mouse hippocampus contributes to the development of depression-like phenotypes
Authors: Chen Chen, Lai-peng Feng, Pei-ling Shi, Qing Liu, Lu-wen Zhang, Qi Wang, Rui-fang Hua, Xi Su, Wen-qiang Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMajor depressive disorder (MDD) is a highly prevalent psychiatric disorder and one of the leading causes of disability worldwide. Neuroinflammation is strongly implicated in the pathophysiology of MDD, suggesting that regulators of neuroinflammatory signaling are feasible therapeutic targets. The CTNND1 gene encodes a member of the armadillo protein family termed p120 (or catenin delta) that functions in cell–cell adhesion and signal transduction, including among immune cells, suggesting immunomodulatory activity.ResultsWe report that systemic injection of the bacterial toxin lipopolysaccharide (LPS) induced depression-like behaviors in mice while concomitantly activating the neuroinflammatory NF-κB signaling pathway, upregulating interleukin (IL)-1β expression, and reducing p120 expression in the hippocampus. Moreover, shRNA-mediated knockdown of hippocampal p120 expression also activated the NF-κB signaling pathway, enhanced IL-1β expression, and induced the same depression-like behaviors. Similarly, both LPS treatment and p120 knockdown upregulated pNF-κB and IL-1β levels in pheochromocytoma-12 cells.ConclusionsThese findings suggest that p120 may function to prevent the development or expression of depressive symptoms by suppressing proinflammatory NF-κB-IL-1β signaling in the hippocampus. Targeting p120 may be a feasible therapeutic strategy to treat MDD.Graphical AbstractKnockdown of p120 serve as a negative regulator of neuroinflammation via NF-κB signalling. Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a disabling psychiatric disease that currently afflicts ~264 million individuals worldwide [1, 2]. Moreover, the overall lifetime risk is currently 15–18%, suggesting that MDD will remain a severe public health burden. In fact, the prevalence of MDD in China alone is estimated at ~3.4% or roughly 50 million current cases [1]. In addition to personal struggles, MDD places a heavy burden on families and the nation as a whole [3]. Despite decades of research, a substantial fraction of patients (~40%) do not respond to currently available treatments, suggesting an inadequate understanding of pathogenic mechanisms [4–6]. There is emerging evidence that neuroinflammation contributes to the pathogenesis of MDD [7, 8], as proinflammatory cytokines such as interleukin (IL)-1β are elevated in the serum and brain of many depressed patients [9, 10]. However, the upstream mechanisms activating and regulating neuroinflammatory processes in the MDD brain are largely unknown.

Consistent with a major contribution of neuroinflammation to MDD pathogenesis, injection of the bacterial toxin lipopolysaccharide (LPS) induces a depression-like state in rodents [11] that is associated with blood and brain elevations in proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α, and IL-6 [12]. Moreover, the concentrations of these cytokines are often related to the severity of depression-like behavioral symptoms [13, 14]. At the molecular level, stress activates the nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) signaling pathway [12], which involves phosphorylation and detachment of a regulatory subunit and subsequent formation of NF-κB subunit heterodimers in the cytoplasm, translocation of these dimers to the nucleus (permitted by the presence of nuclear localization sequences [NLSs]), binding to specific DNA sequences, and transcription of stress-related genes [15, 16].

The gene p120 consists of 21 exons, and generates 22 distinct transcripts (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). The encoded protein p120 (catenin delta) contains an armadillo repeat domain that binds directly to the intracellular domain of E-cadherin, and through this interaction regulates cell–cell adhesion, cell polarity, and signal transduction between cells [17, 18]. Consistent with a broad spectrum of physiological functions, p120 protein is localized within the inner leaflet of the plasma membrane as well as in the cytosol and nucleus of endothelial cells, epithelial cells, and immune cells, including macrophages [19]. Expression in immune cells implies involvement in the regulation of inflammation. Indeed, recent evidence suggests that p120 contributes to the anti-inflammatory response in both infectious and noninfectious models, at least in part by attenuating toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) signaling and NF-кB activation. For instance, p120 overexpression reduced LPS-induced lung inflammatory injury [20] by inhibiting NF-кB activation [21]. These findings raise the possibility that p120 regulates innate immunity. However, it is unknown if p120 expression is altered in MDD or if p120 can serve to mitigate MDD symptoms through effects on neuroinflammation.

We speculated that the LPS-induced depression-like phenotype would be associated with p120 downregulation, resulting in enhanced IL-1β expression via NF-κB signaling pathway activation, and that both this depression-like phenotype and enhanced inflammatory signaling would be recapitulated by p120 knockdown in the hippocampus. In this study, we report that p120 expression was indeed downregulated and expression levels of pNF-κB and IL-1β upregulated in the hippocampus of LPS-treated mice exhibiting a depression-like behavioral phenotype. Further, stereotactic injection of a p120 knockdown vector recapitulated this depression-like phenotype and activation of proinflammatory NF-κB-IL-1β signaling. These findings identify p120 suppression as a potential pathogenic mechanism for MDD.

Methods

Animals

Adult male C57BL/6J mice (7–8 weeks old, 30 ± 3 g) were purchased from Beijing HFK Bioscience Co., Ltd., Beijing, China (license No. SCXK (Jing) 2024-0003) and raised under standard laboratory conditions (22 ± 2 °C ambient temperature, 40–60% relative humidity, 12 h/12 h light/dark cycle, lights on from 07:30 to 19:30) with ad libitum access to food and water. Mice were housed in a standard individually ventilated cage system. Female mice were excluded from testing to reduce data variability. Moreover, MDD is twice as prevalent in adult females than adult males, and females tend to exhibit greater symptom severity and higher rates of comorbid anxiety, suggesting distinct underlying molecular mechanisms [22–24].

Mice were randomly divided into the following four groups of 10 each (all 10 for the behavioral tests, 6 for the subsequent western blot assays, and 4 for immunofluorescence staining): a Control group receiving vehicle injection, and three experimental groups receiving LPS, shRNA-Ctrl, or shRNA-p120 as detailed in the next sections.

LPS treatment

Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) was purchased from Servicebio (Wuhan, China, Cat# GC205009-10 mg) dissolved in 1× phosphate buffered saline (PBS) (Servicebio, Wuhan, China, Cat# G4202-500ML), and administered at 2 mg/kg/day as previously reported [25]. Mice were randomly divided into two groups of 10 and injected intraperitoneally (i.p.) with either LPS (LPS group) daily or equal-volume 1× PBS (Ctrl group) for 3 consecutive days. Behavioral tests for depression-like phenotypes were performed 24 h after the last injection of LPS or vehicle alone. At completion, mice were euthanized by pentobarbital sodium and perfused through the heart with sterile normal saline. Hippocampi were quickly removed and stored at −80 °C.

Stereotaxic injection of vectors into the CA3 region of mouse hippocampus

A separate mouse cohort (9 weeks old) received stereotactic injections of vector into the hippocampal CA3 region to examine the effects of p120 depletion on behavior and immune status. Briefly, mice were deeply anesthetized with pentobarbital sodium (10 mL/kg; Beijing Dingguo Changsheng Biotechnology Co. Ltd., Beijing, China) and injected with 1 μL adeno-associated virus vector (1 × 1012 genome copies/mL) at coordinates −2.5 mm anterior–posterior relative to the bregma, ±3.5 mm lateral to the midline, and −3.5 to −2.8 mm deep using a stereotaxic frame (Ruiwode Life Science, Shenzhen, China). Half of the mice were injected with pAAV-mCherry (empty vector) and the other half with an AAV expressing shRNA targeting p120 (pAAV-mCherry-shRNA) (OBiO Technology Co., Shanghai, China). Behavioral testing and other experiments were performed 3 weeks after viral injection.

Behavioral testing

All behavioral tests were conducted by experimenters blinded to treatment group.

The open field test (OFT) was used to assess anxiety/depression-like behaviors as previously described [26]. In brief, the mice were gently placed individually into an open field arena (50 × 50 × 40 cm) divided into 9 equal squares (each 3 × 3 cm) to define a central region and a peripheral region. Movement was recorded with an overhead video camera for 5 min. Total movement distance and exploration tracks were analyzed using SMART software (V3.0.06, Panlab, Spain). The open field was wiped with 40% ethanol and dried between sessions.

The light–dark transition test was conducted to measure anxiety-like behaviors as previously described [26]. The test is based on the innate preference of mice to avoid brightly lit areas but also to explore new environments. The test equipment consists of a chamber divided into a light area (19 × 18 × 20 cm) and a dark area (19 × 18 × 20 cm). The mice were placed in the light area and allowed to move freely between the two areas for 5 min. Time spent in the light area and number of transitions from dark to light were recorded using Pan Lab SMART 3.0 software. The light and dark areas were cleaned thoroughly with 40% ethanol after each test.

The tail suspension test (TST) was used to measure despair-like behavior in mice following the original reported procedure with modifications [27]. Mice were suspended by a hook within a 20 × 20 × 32 cm box for 5 min and immobility (a sign of helplessness/behavioral despair) recorded using Pan Lab SMART 3.0 software. The equipment was wiped with 40% ethanol and dried thoroughly before the next test.

The forced swim test (FST) was also conducted to measure despair-like behaviors according to a previously described method [28]. Mice were placed gently in a 21-cm diameter plastic cylinder filled to a depth of 60 cm with room temperature (25 ± 1 °C) water and passive floating (immobility) was measured over a 5-min period using Pan Lab SMART 3.0 software. Immobility was defined as floating motionless in the water or movement just sufficient to maintain the nose above the water surface.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Blood samples were collected from the orbit of mice into anticoagulant tubes, which were gently reversed several times to mix the blood with anticoagulant and then centrifuged at 3000 g for 10 min at 4 °C. The plasma was carefully separated and stored at −80 °C until use. The IL-1β concentration was measured using a commercial kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions (ELK Biotechnology, Wuhan, China, Cat# ELK1271). Finally, the optical density of the samples was read on a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA, Cat# VARIOSKAN LUX) at 450 nm.

Cell culture and treatment

Pheochromocytoma (PC)-12 cells (Procell, Wuhan, Hubei, China, Cat# CL-0481) were cultured in a special PC-12 cell culture medium (Procell, Wuhan, Hubei, China, Cat# CM-0481) at 37 °C under a humified 5% CO2 atmosphere. Cells were stimulated with 1000 ng/mL LPS (10 mg; Cat# GC205009, Servicebio, Wuhan, China) for 6 h to establish an in vitro neuroinflammation model. Some PC-12 cultures were first infected with p120-targeted shRNA lentiviral vector (shRNA-p120) or control lentivirus vector (shRNA-Ctrl) at 1.5 × 108 TU/mL according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Hanbio Company). After infection for 24 h, cells were harvested and lysed for qRT-PCR or western blot assays, or fixed for immunofluorescence staining as detailed in the following sections.

Real-time reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from hippocampi and cells using a HiPure Universal RNA Mini Kit (Tiangen, Beijing, China, Cat# DP451) according to the manufacturer’s protocols and reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the NovoScript Plus All-in-One 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (Novoprotein, Suzhou, Jiangsu, China, Cat# E047-01B). The cDNA was amplified by PowerUp™ SYBR™ Green Mix (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA, Cat# A25742) using the 7500 real-time fluorescent qPCR system (ThermoFisher Scientific) and the following thermocycle settings: 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s, 60 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 1 min. Relative target gene expression was normalized to glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) mRNA expression by the 2ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Western blot assays

Hippocampi were harvested and homogenized in RIPA buffer. Lysate proteins (30 μg per gel lane) were separated on 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gels (Epizyme Biotech, Shanghai, China, Cat# PG212) and transferred to 0.45 µm pore size polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (Merck Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA, Cat# IPFL00010). Membranes were blocked in protein-free rapid blocking solution (Servicebio, Wuhan, China, Cat# G2052) for 1 h at room temperature, and then incubated with the following primary antibodies at room temperature for 2 h: anti-GAPDH (mouse, 1:5000; Proteintech, Wuhan, China, Cat# 60004-1-Ig, RRID: AB_2107436), anti-CTNND1 (rabbit, 1:1000; Proteintech, Wuhan, China, Cat# 12180-1-AP, RRID: AB_2086267), anti-NF-κB (rabbit; 1:2000; Servicebio, Wuhan, China, Cat# GB11997-100), anti-phospho-NF-κB (rabbit; 1:2000; Servicebio, Wuhan, China, Cat# GB113882-100), and anti-IL-1β (mouse, 1:2000; Immunoway, Plano, TX, USA, Cat# YM4682). Immunoblotted membranes were washed three times in tris-buffer saline with Tween 20 (TBST) and then incubated in horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG (1:5000; Proteintech, Wuhan, China, Cat# SA00001-2, RRID: AB_2722564) or anti-mouse IgG (1:5000; Proteintech, Cat# SA00001-1, RRID: AB_2722565) for 1 h at room temperature with shaking. Immunolabeling of target protein bands was visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence solution (Merck Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA, Cat# WBKLS0500) and captured using the AI600 Ultra-sensitive Multifunctional Imaging System (GE, USA). Band densities were quantified using ImageJ 1.50i software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) and normalized to GAPDH density. All assays were performed at least three times with independently prepared lysate samples from different mice.

Immunofluorescence staining

Mice were anesthetized with pentobarbital sodium (100 mg/kg, i.p.) and sacrificed by intracardial perfusion of saline followed by 4% paraformaldehyde solution. Brains were harvested, submerged successively in 0.1 M phosphate buffer with 15%, 20%, and 30% sucrose until loss of buoyancy, and sectioned on the sample holder at 10-μm thickness using a cryotome (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany, Cat# CM1950). Immunofluorescence staining was performed according to a previously reported protocol [29]. Briefly, slices were incubated with Immunol Staining Primary Antibody Dilution Buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China, Cat# P0103) containing primary antibodies against anti-p120 (rabbit, 1:1000; Proteintech, Wuhan, China, Cat# 12180-1-AP, RRID: AB_2086267), anti-NF-kB (rabbit; 1:2000; Servicebio, Wuhan, China, Cat# GB11997-100) overnight at 4 °C, and then with FITC-labeled goat anti-mouse IgG(H + L) (1:500; Beyotime, Shanghai, China, Cat# A0568), Cy3-labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG(H + L) (1:500; Beyotime, Shanghai, China, Cat# A0516), Cy3-labeled goat anti-mouse IgG(H + L) (1:500; Beyotime, Shanghai, China, Cat# A0521), and (or) Alexa Fluor 647-labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG(H + L) (1:500; Beyotime, Shanghai, China, Cat# A0468) at 37 °C for 1 h. Slices were mounted on slides and immunofluorescence captured using a laser scanning confocal microscope (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany, Cat# Stellaris5SR). Identical staining protocols were applied to PC-12 cell cultures. All images were initially captured using a 40× objective lens, with subsequent single-cell morphological analysis performed through digital zoom (scale bars indicate actual dimensions).

Statistical analyses

Sample sizes were not predetermined by statistical analysis as there are no previous measures for reliable extrapolation of effect magnitude. However, the sample sizes were in accord with previous publications [30, 31]. All statistical analyses and graphing were conducted using GraphPad Prism software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA, www.graphpad.com). The statistical results are presented as mean ± standard error of mean (SEM) based on results of the Shapiro–Wilk test for normality and compared by independent samples Student’s t tests. A P value <0.05 (two-tailed) was considered statistically significant for all tests.

Results

Hippocampal p120 modulates LPS-induced neuroinflammation and depression-like behaviors

Systemic injection of LPS (2 mg/kg/day) induced depression-like behaviors in the open field test (OFT), light–dark transition test, tail suspension test (TST), and forced swimming test (FST). In the OFT, total distance traveled (P < 0.001, Fig. 1A), time in the arena center (P < 0.05, Fig. 1B), and number of entries into the center area (P < 0.01, Fig. 1C) were all lower in the LPS group compared to the vehicle-treated (Ctrl) group, suggesting greater anxiety and depression-like motor hypoactivity. In the light–dark transition test as well, LPS group mice made fewer transitions (P < 0.001, Fig. 1D) and spent less time in the light area (P < 0.001, Fig. 1E) compared to the Ctrl group, again indicative of greater behavioral anxiety. Additionally, immobility times in both the TST (P < 0.001, Fig. 1F) and FST (P < 0.01, Fig. 1G) were longer among LPS-treated mice than Ctrl mice, a behavioral pattern indicative of depression-like behavioral despair/helplessness. Collectively, these findings indicate that LPS injection can establish a robust behavioral model of depression.

We then examined if these behavioral phenotypes were associated with expression levels of p120 and the neuroinflammatory markers pNF-κB, NF-κB, and IL-1β, as the NF-κB pathway is a central mediator of proinflammatory gene regulation in both innate and adaptive immunity [32]. Treatment with LPS significantly enhanced NF-κB phosphorylation and IL-1β expression in the hippocampus, but reduced hippocampal p120 expression, suggesting that p120 may serve as a negative regulator of neuroinflammation in the hippocampus (Fig. 1H, I). Moreover, LPS treatment reduced p120 mRNA expression (P < 0.01, Fig. 1G) and enhanced IL-1β mRNA expression (P < 0.001, Fig. 1K). In addition, LPS injection increased the plasma concentration of IL-1β (P < 0.001, Fig. 1L), indicating a strong systemic proinflammatory response.

Downstream targets of the NF-κB signaling pathway can be upregulated through enhanced phosphorylation of subunit dimers, which augments transactivation activity [16], and by increased translocation of NF-κB dimers into the nucleus [33]. To distinguish between these mechanisms, we also conducted immunofluorescence staining of brain slices using anti-p120 and anti-NF-κB antibodies, and found increased nuclear NF-κB concomitant with reduced p120 expression (Fig. 1M–O).

Downregulation of hippocampal p120 expression induced depression-like behaviors and neuroinflammation

To provide further evidence that p120 may negatively regulate LPS-induced neuroinflammation and prevent depression-like behaviors, we tested the effects of hippocampus-specific p120 knockdown. Injection of an AAV vector encoding shRNA-p120 into the CA3 enhanced depression-like behaviors compared to control AAV (shRNA-Ctrl) vector injection, including reduced total distance traveled (P < 0.05, Fig. 2A), center time (P < 0.05, Fig. 2B), and center area entries (P < 0.05, Fig. 2C) in the OFT. In contrast, no abnormalities were observed in dark–light transition number (P > 0.05, Fig. 2D), time spent in the light area (P > 0.05, Fig. 2E), or immobility times in the TST (P > 0.05, Fig. 2F) and FST (P > 0.05, Fig. 2G). Nonetheless, direct downregulation induced anxiety and motor retardation, strongly suggesting that p120 underexpression contributes directly to the behavioral phenotype. Western blotting also revealed that p120 knockdown enhanced NF-κB phosphorylation and IL-1β expression (Fig. 2H, I). Similarly, both the plasma concentration of IL-1β (P < 0.001, Fig. 2L) and IL-1β mRNA expression of hippocampus(P < 0.001, Fig. 2K) were enhanced by p120 knockdown (P < 0.001, Fig. 2G), suggesting that emergence of these depression-like behaviors is due to neuroinflammation and that p120 normally serves as a negative regulator of neuroinflammation in the hippocampus.

Immunofluorescence staining of brain slices with anti-p120 and anti-NF-κB antibodies confirmed that the shRNA-p120 vector significantly reduced p120 protein expression in hippocampus and concomitantly increased nuclear NF-κB (Fig. 2M–O). Taken together, these results suggest that underexpression of p120 in the mouse hippocampus increases susceptibility to anxiety and depression through activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway.

p120 negatively regulates LPS-induced inflammatory injury of PC-12 cells

As numerous uncontrolled factors may contribute to the LPS response in mouse brain, we examined the influence of LPS (1000 ng/mL) on neuroinflammatory signaling in PC-12 cells. As expected, exposure to LPS significantly increased NF-κB phosphorylation and IL-1β expression, but also downregulated p120 expression compared to vehicle-treated Ctrl cells (Fig. 3A, B). We then examined the effect of LPS (1000 ng/mL) on expression of p120 and IL-1β at the mRNA level by qRT-PCR, and found that LPS decreased p120 mRNA (P < 0.001, Fig. 3C) but increased IL-1β mRNA compared to Ctrl cells (P < 0.001, Fig. 3D). These findings were further supported by immunofluorescence staining. Treatment of PC-12 cells with LPS (1000 ng/mL) for 6 h significantly reduced p120 immunoexpression but increased NF-κB immunoexpression in the nucleus (Fig. 3E). These results strongly suggest that p120 negatively regulates LPS-induced inflammatory injury in PC-12 cells.

Downregulation of p120 expression induced inflammatory signaling in PC-12 cells

We then examined the influence of p120 expression on neuroinflammatory signaling in PC-12 cells. Consistent with mouse results, downregulation of p120 by shRNA-p120 lentivirus infection significantly increased NF-κB phosphorylation and IL-1β protein expression compared to shRNA-Ctrl lentivirus infection (Fig. 4A, B). We further confirmed that shRNA-p120 decreased the mRNA expression level of p120 (P < 0.001, Fig. 4C) and increased the mRNA expression of IL-1β (P < 0.001, Fig. 4D) compared to shRNA-Ctrl-infected cells. Immunostaining also revealed that NF-κB was increased in the nucleus of PC-12 cells, while p120 was downregulated (Fig. 4E). These data indicate that downregulation of p120 expression can activate the NF-κB signaling pathway directly in isolated neuron-like cells.

Discussion

The present results strongly suggest that p120 negatively regulates the NF-κB neuroinflammatory signaling pathway in mouse hippocampus and that this regulatory mechanism acts to protect against depression-like disease. Systemic administration of the bacterial toxin LPS reduced hippocampal p120 expression, induced greater NF-κB signaling in the hippocampus, and led to the emergence of depression-like behaviors (motor retardation, fear/anxiety, and despair) in mice. Further, downregulation of hippocampal p120 by infusion of an AAV vector induced depression-like behaviors (motor hypoactivity and anxiety), and aggravated inflammation in the hippocampus. The pathological manifestations of MDD are associated with elevated IL-1β expression and NF-κB phosphorylation [34, 35], and expression of both were significantly increased by p120 downregulation. Moreover, p120 knockdown led to the emergence of depression-like behaviors (motor retardation and fear/anxiety). Thus, p120 knockdown mice recapitulated some of the core behavioral features of the depression-like phenotype induced by LPS. Pheochromocytoma-12 cells, which originate from the neural crest of the rat adrenal medulla, are widely used to establish cellular models of neurological disorders [36, 37]. In accord with findings in mice, stimulation with LPS or p120 knockdown by transfection of a lentivirus vector encoding shRNA-p120 activated the NF-κB signaling pathway and upregulated IL-1β expression, confirming a direct intracellular pathway involving p120 that negatively regulates NF-κB signaling. Taken together, our results provide clear evidence that p120 is a novel regulator of neuroinflammation and promoter of mood stabilization.

Injection of LPS is a widely used method to induce anxiety/depression-like behaviors in mice [38, 39]. Further, these behaviors are reliably measured in the OFT [26]. Treatment with LPS resulted in downregulation of p120 expression in the hippocampus, motor hypoactivity as evidenced by reduced total distance traveled in the OFT, and anxiety as evidenced by reduced time in the center area and fewer entries into the center area of the OFT. In addition to the OFT, the light–dark transition test was conducted to measure anxiety-like behaviors as previously described [26]. The test is based on the innate preference of mice to avoid brightly lit areas but also to explore new environments. In our experiments, LPS mice made fewer transitions and spent less time in the light box; however, no abnormalities were observed in the light–dark transition test between shRNA-Ctrl and shRNA-p120 mice. The tail suspension test (TST) and forced swim test (FST) were also conducted to measure despair-like behavior in mice following the original reported procedure with modifications [27, 28]. The LPS-treated group exhibited longer immobility times than the Ctrl group, but again p120 knockdown did not induce such abnormal behaviors. We speculate that LPS not only alters NF-κB signaling, but also MAPK signaling [40–42]. We suggest that the depression-like condition induced by LPS is more severe than that induced by p120 knockdown because LPS alters both NF-κB and MAPK signaling pathways in microglia, while p120 knockdown induced only abnormal NF-κB signaling. Nonetheless, these and previous findings suggest that p120 underexpression can induce a behavioral anxiety/depression-like phenotype similar but less severe than that induced by LPS.

Depression is a heterogeneous disorder, and epidemiological, genetic, and physiological studies indicate that pathogenesis involves the dysregulation of multiple signaling systems [43] including neuroinflammatory signaling [44]. Neuroinflammation is a response to various forms of stress, and both clinical and preclinical findings have consistently found that the hippocampus is highly vulnerable to stress [25, 45]. Furthermore, multiple lines of evidence implicate hippocampal stress and inflammation in the pathophysiology of MDD [39]. Microglia cells are the major resident immune cells of the central nervous system and are closely related to macrophages, which are activated in response to tissue damage and infectious stimuli as part of the initial reparative and host antimicrobial response pathways. Upon activation, microglia are polarized into a pro-inflammatory phenotype M1 or an anti-inflammatory phenotype M2 [46]. Both NF-κB signaling and upregulated IL-1β expression are implicated in the polarization of microglia [47, 48]. Consistent with this notion, induction of neuroinflammation by LPS treatment induced a behavioral depression-like phenotype in addition to activating the NF-κB signaling pathway as evidenced by enhanced NF-κB phosphorylation and nuclear translocation. In addition, this LPS response was associated with reduced p120 expression, while knockdown of p120 in the hippocampus of mice not administered LPS resulted in a similar depression-like behavioral phenotype as well as induction of the NF-κB signaling pathway. These results suggest that p120 hypofunction in the hippocampus may contribute to the pathophysiology of MDD and further that p120 is a critical negative regulator of hippocampal neuroinflammation and polarization of microglia into the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype.

Increased cytokine expression has been observed both in the peripheral blood of MDD patients and depressive rodent models [9, 10]. Nuclear factor-κB is considered a core initiator of the cellular stress response, and IL-1β is a well-known downstream target gene upregulated by NF-κB activation and nuclear translocation [49]. Notably, the magnitude of the increase in IL-1β was directly associated with MDD symptom severity [50]. In our study, both LPS administration and hippocampal p120 knockdown significantly increased plasma IL-1β concomitant with the development of depression-like behavioral phenotypes. To our knowledge, this is the first study reporting that downregulation of p120 can induce depression-like behaviors, likely in part by inducing microglial polarization into the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype ensuing neuroinflammation.

In accord with findings in mice, both LPS stimulation and p120 knockdown by transfection of a lentivirus vector encoding shRNA-p120 activated the NF-κB signaling pathway and upregulated IL-1β expression, confirming a direct intracellular pathway involving p120 that negatively regulates NF-κB signaling.

The major findings of this study are as follows: (1) LPS-induced neuroinflammation in mice was associated with reduced p120 expression in the hippocampus and the emergence of a depression-like behavioral phenotype; (2) p120 knockdown also activated the NF-κB signaling pathway and induced depression-like behaviors; (3) p120 knockdown activated the NF-κB signaling pathway in PC-12 cells. Taken together, our results provide evidence that p120 negatively regulates neuroinflammation in the hippocampus and that this effect can prevent the emergence of a depression-like behavioral phenotype characterized by motor retardation, anxiety, and despair/helplessness. These findings suggest that neuroinflammation in the hippocampus strongly contributes to the onset and behavioral expression of depression and that this effect is exacerbated by p120 downregulation, which normally serves to suppress neuroinflammation. Further experiments with transgenic mice overexpressing p120 are necessary to confirm these conclusions. In addition, such experiments could identify p120 as a clinically important suppressor of neuroinflammation and protective factor against stress-induced depression. Modulation of the p120 and NF-κB pathways may have therapeutic potential for depression.

Our study has limitations. Since depressive disorders arise from multi-regional brain dysfunction [51, 52], analyzing hippocampal function alone presents inherent limitations. Notably, emerging evidence indicates that p120 is expressed beyond the hippocampus in other brain regions [53, 54]. Therefore, investigating whether p120 contributes to the pathogenesis of depression in these additional brain areas holds significant future research value. Another limitation of this study is that the depression-like phenotype induced by LPS was partially recapitulated by p120 knockdown in the mouse hippocampus, suggesting that p120 overexpression in hippocampus may provide protection against LPS-induced neuroinflammation and depression-like behaviors. However, due to the cost and time, we did not examine the effects of p120 expression vectors. Finally, our sample size was set relatively small to reduce the number of mice. Nonetheless, the results provide justification for larger-scale studies. To summarize, our study provides further evidence for the potential pathogenic and therapeutic importance of p120 in MDD. Regulation of p120 will assist in developing therapeutic strategies for this progressive disease.
