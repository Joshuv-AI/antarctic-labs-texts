Introduction

Globally, the incidence of oral diseases such as dental caries, periodontitis, oral mucosal lesions, and oral cancer remains high, making them a major public health burden worldwide. According to statistics, oral diseases have affected approximately 3.7 billion people worldwide. Research predicts that over the next 30 years, especially the prevalence of periodontal disease and dental caries, will continue to rise.1, 2 As an important gateway connecting the external environment and the respiratory system, the oral cavity is a potential entry point for microorganisms that can trigger systemic diseases. Its health status is closely linked to various systemic conditions.3, 4 For example, a study on patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD) showed that more than half of dialysis patients had periodontitis, nearly half experienced dry mouth symptoms, and most had poor oral self-care abilities. Oral diseases are not only associated with local inflammation and infection, but also elevate the risk of cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality via mechanisms such as systemic inflammation and malnutrition, suggesting that oral health interventions play a significant role in the management of systemic diseases like CKD.5

Beyond physiological effects, oral health is also closely related to psychological well-being. Unattractive dental appearance may lead to social withdrawal and feelings of inferiority, and can even impact speech and eating abilities, severely affecting patients’ well-being. Global burden of disease studies indicate that severe tooth loss impacts individual functioning to a degree comparable to moderate heart failure.6 In addition, oral diseases impose a heavy socioeconomic burden. The high cost of dental care increases financial strain on families and continually drains public healthcare resources. Particularly concerning is that economically disadvantaged groups, who struggle to afford treatment, face a higher risk of tooth loss, further exacerbating health inequalities.7 Therefore, seeking effective and accessible strategies for the prevention and treatment of oral diseases has become an urgent priority.

With the rapid development of nanobiomaterials science, nanozymes, a class of artificial enzymes with enzyme-like catalytic activity, have demonstrated broad application potential in the field of biomedicine. Not only can they mimic the activity of natural enzymes such as superoxide dismutase (SOD), peroxidase (POD), and catalase (CAT) to provide antioxidant therapy for inflammation, neurodegenerative diseases, and organ damage. They can also catalyse the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) for tumour treatment as well as antibacterial and anti-biofilm applications, thereby achieving diversified functions from therapy to diagnosis.8,9 In the field of dental medicine, compared with the mechanical properties and biological inertness of traditional dental materials, nanozymes enable precise regulation of ROS within the complex oral microenvironment, efficiently degrade pathogenic biofilms, and even promote tissue remineralization and defect repair.10 Compared with natural enzymes, nanozymes possess distinct advantages, including tunable catalytic activity, excellent stability, and cost-effectiveness.11 These inherent merits further consolidate their potential in clinical applications, making them a promising alternative to natural enzymes in various biomedical scenarios.

Therefore, the objective of this review is to comprehensively collate the latest research progress of nanozymes in oral health management, systematically analyse their core mechanisms in anti-biofilm, oxidative stress regulation and tissue repair, and summarize the design and application characteristics of different nanozyme loading platforms. The rationale of this study is to address the clinical bottlenecks of traditional oral disease treatment, clarify the application potential and existing challenges of nanozymes in precision oral medicine, and provide a clear research direction for the subsequent development and clinical translation of nanozyme-based oral therapeutic systems.

Primary factors contributing to oral diseases

Infectious oral diseases are the most common in clinical practice. Investigation into their causes reveals that they are closely related to external factors such as differences in tooth morphology and daily dietary habits, as well as the combined influence of intrinsic host factors like genetic defects and environmental factors. In-depth studies show that their core pathological process all begins with the formation of a microbial biofilm,12 subsequently triggering excessive oxidative stress responses in the host.13 This process is simultaneously influenced by intrinsic host factors, including genetic defects and environmental conditions.

Formation of biofilms

In nature, bacteria frequently exist as multispecies communities.14 The oral cavity, with its unique environmental conditions conducive to microbial adhesion and growth, fosters highly diverse microbial communities, commonly referred to as biofilms.15 The development of biofilms can be divided into four stages: the initial adhesion phase, the lag phase, the rapid growth phase, and the steady-state phase. Formation initiates with the colonization of oral microorganisms on surfaces such as teeth, soft tissues, dental implants, and restorative materials, transitioning bacteria from a planktonic to a sessile state. This transition shifts bacteria from a planktonic to a sessile state, followed by a lag phase characterized by upregulation of gene expression. Subsequently, a rapid growth phase occurs where large amounts of extracellular polysaccharide (EPS) matrix are produced, ultimately reaching a steady-state phase of dynamic equilibrium. This multidimensional, complex pathogenic bacterial complex not only forms a physical barrier but also serves as a persistent source of inflammatory stimulation, inducing a series of oral diseases such as dental caries, gingivitis, and periodontitis.16

It is worth emphasizing that tooth surface-associated biofilms, especially subgingival plaque in periodontitis patients, exhibit a strong correlation with various systemic diseases, such as cardiovascular disease, diabetes, respiratory ailments, and adverse pregnancy outcomes.17 This further underscores the critical importance of eliminating pathogenic biofilms. Currently, traditional treatment methods such as mechanical debridement often fail to eradicate pathogenic bacteria and may even lead to subsequent infections. Antibiotic therapy, as a routine clinical treatment, can lead to the emergence of multidrug-resistant strains when overused. Conventional antimicrobial agents, including chlorhexidine (CHX), hydrogen peroxide, and other chemical disinfectants, are ineffective at degrading the EPS matrix. While CHX can kill free-floating bacterial pathogens, it is significantly less effective against dental plaque biofilms and may contribute to tartar formation and tooth discoloration.18 Although probiotics have been extensively investigated as an emerging therapeutic strategy and can modulate microbial homeostasis to a certain extent, the stability of their therapeutic efficacy and their long-term safety remain controversial.19 Simultaneously, the drawbacks of traditional dental materials are increasingly evident. For instance, titanium ions in dental implants may confer growth advantages to peri-implant pathogens, facilitating the covert proliferation of pathogenic microorganisms.20

Oxidative stress response

Bacterial colonization in the oral cavity not only triggers infections but also induces chronic immune and inflammatory responses, such as periodontitis caused by subgingival plaque and peri-implantitis caused by soft tissue biofilms around dental implants.21 Persistent inflammation stimulates excessive production of ROS, highly reactive oxygen-containing chemicals including hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), singlet oxygen (1O2), superoxide anion (O2−), and hydroxyl radical (•OH).22 The sources of ROS include exogenous and endogenous factors. Exogenous sources are more widespread, encompassing common daily exposures such as ethanol, cigarette smoke, medications, and high-fat, high-protein diets, as well as dental-related interventions including ozone therapy, ultrasonic scaling, fluoride application, and specific restorative materials. In contrast, endogenous sources represent the primary pathways of ROS generation, including mitochondrial respiratory chain activity, cytochrome P450 metabolism, POD activity, and inflammatory cell activation.23

ROS is a double-edged sword. At optimal concentrations, it exerts beneficial effects such as tissue repair, cell differentiation, angiogenesis, and VEGF-induced cell migration. In oral antimicrobial defense, neutrophils also generate large amounts of ROS through respiratory bursts when pathogens invade, oxidizing bacterial structures to inhibit their growth and reproduction.24 However, when biofilms persist and cause chronic inflammation, excessive ROS production occurs. Elevated ROS levels induce oxidative damage to cell membranes, DNA, and proteins, further exacerbating the inflammatory response,25 and ultimately damaging host tissues.

Host factors

Genetic defects leading to dental developmental abnormalities also constitute significant risk factors for infectious oral diseases. The normal structure and function of teeth depend on the precise regulation of multiple genes.26 Any gene mutation or abnormal expression may affect tooth morphology, mineralization levels, or antimicrobial capacity, thereby increasing susceptibility to oral infections. Taking dental hard tissue as an example, it is primarily composed of enamel, dentin, and cementum.27 Enamel hypoplasia and dentin hypoplasia are two common types of hereditary dental developmental disorders, primarily caused by mutations in enamel-related genes and dentin-related genes, respectively. These disorders result in abnormal enamel or dentin structure, manifesting as enamel thinning or absence, inadequate mineralization, or reduced dentin strength.28 This makes teeth more prone to mechanical injury while markedly weakening their ability to resist microbial invasion, indirectly increasing the likelihood of infection.

Classification of nanozyme loading platforms

Nanozymes, as a class of nanomaterials possessing enzyme-mimetic catalytic activity, have demonstrated significant potential in treating oral diseases through antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, and tissue regenerative effects. However, their practical application remains constrained by critical bottlenecks such as poor stability, insufficient targeting, and short local retention time, limiting their therapeutic efficacy. Therefore, selecting an appropriate loading platform is crucial for optimizing nanozyme delivery efficiency, achieving controlled release regulation, and enabling efficient functional expression. Different loading platforms can significantly enhance therapeutic outcomes through multiple dimensions by precisely regulating nanozyme release kinetics, increasing local drug concentration at the lesion site, and enhancing specific interactions with target tissues. Applications of nanozyme-loaded platforms in oral diseases.

Nanoparticles

Nanoparticles are a term used by IUPAC to refer to any particles with dimensions between 1 and 100 nm.55 However, the scope of nanoparticles is broader in practical applications, encompassing structures with dimensions reaching several hundred nanometres. Their core value resides in conferring distinctive physicochemical properties and functions via nanoscale structural design, which are unavailable in conventional macroscopic materials.

Nanozymes are a class of catalytic nanoparticles that combine the high specific surface area and precise controllability of nanoparticles with the highly efficient catalytic activity of natural enzymes.56 Ma et al57 successfully constructed a single-atom nanozyme exhibiting both CAT and SOD activities by anchoring individual iron atoms within an N-doped amorphous carbon substrate (Figure 1A). This dual-functional nanozyme efficiently scavenges ROS, offering a novel strategy for regulating oral oxidative stress.

Among various nanomaterials for fabricating nanozymes, metal nanoparticles are particularly prominent owing to their excellent catalytic and antibacterial properties. Their nanoscale size allows effective penetration into bacterial cell walls and biofilm matrices, overcoming delivery barriers of conventional antimicrobials. Meanwhile, the large specific surface area provides abundant active sites, supporting superior enzyme-mimetic performance.58 Further investigation has revealed that metal nanoparticles, metal oxide nanoparticles and inorganic non-metallic nanoparticles have also been demonstrated to mimic the activities of multiple enzymes,59 including SOD, POD and OXD, playing a crucial role as highly effective antibacterial agents in the treatment of oral diseases.60

Nanoparticles serve as loading platforms for nanozymes, with their core advantages lying in ease of functionalization and strong structural diversity. Among these, titanium dioxide nanotubes (TNT) offer several advantageous properties for drug delivery, including excellent biocompatibility, structural extensibility, and highly tunable drug release properties.61 Zhu et al62 constructed an array of titanium dioxide nanotubes loaded with iron-nitrogen-doped carbon single-atom nanozymes (Fe-NC nanozymes) on the surface of medical-grade titanium (Fe-NC@TNT, Figure 1B). This composite system fully exposes the catalytic active sites of Fe-NC nanozymes through the high specific surface area of TNT. It not only significantly enhances their multi-enzyme mimetic activity but also demonstrates outstanding efficacy in alleviating peri-implant inflammation and promoting soft tissue integration, providing a practical solution for addressing post-surgical infections in dental implants. Notably, recent advances in dental nanotechnology have highlighted that nano-composite coatings and nanocluster-based carriers can further improve the mechanical interlocking and biocompatibility of nanozyme-loaded platforms. For instance, nanosilica-zirconia composites have been applied in dental restorative materials to enhance wear resistance and esthetic appeal, which provides a valuable reference for optimizing the structural stability of nanozyme carriers in the oral cavity.63

Drug delivery systems based on two-dimensional (2D) nanomaterials are rapidly advancing. Compared to traditional zero-dimensional (0D) and one-dimensional (1D) nanomaterials, 2D nanomaterials possess higher specific surface area and drug loading capacity.64 Liao et al65 employed a coordination strategy driven by L-cysteine and graphitylene (GDY) to anchor low-dose silver nanoparticles/silver ions onto GDY nanosheets, successfully constructing the GDY/L-cys/Ag (GLA) nanozyme (Figure 1C). This nanozyme specifically activates POD-like activity within the acidic microenvironment of oral pathogenic biofilms. Its synergistic antibacterial effect, combined with controlled silver ion release, enables targeted and precise biofilm clearance, significantly enhancing the specificity and safety of anti-biofilm therapy.

Metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) and their nanoscale derivatives (NMOFs) are porous hybrid materials constructed from polyvalent bridging ligands and metallic linkers. With outstanding porosity, structural tunability, and catalytic activity, these materials serve as ideal carrier platforms for nanozymes.66 Liu et al67 synthesized a series of MOF/Ce-based nanozymes exhibiting dual-enzyme mimicry of deoxyribonuclease and POD activities (Figure 1D). The ultrahigh specific surface area of MOFs effectively supports the loading of Ce ions, enabling the assembly of multiple active catalytic centres and thus exhibiting enhanced catalytic activity.

These nanoplatforms – TNT with tubular structural extensibility, 2D materials with lamellar high loading capacity, and MOFs with porous tunability – each leverage their unique structural features to optimize nanozyme delivery, loading, and catalytic efficiency. The innovative application of these multifunctional nanoplatforms not only achieves precise nanocatalyst delivery and efficient loading but also enables precise regulation of the oral microenvironment by mimicking natural enzyme activity. This marks a significant shift in oral disease treatment strategies from traditional passive antibacterial approaches toward active, precision biological regulation paradigms, offering novel insights for overcoming clinical treatment bottlenecks.

Hydrogel

The nanocatalyst-hydrogel system combines the highly efficient catalytic performance of nanocatalysts with the advantages of the three-dimensional network structure. It not only enables stable encapsulation and activity protection of nanocatalysts while reducing potential biotoxicity, but also precisely regulates drug release rhythms and optimizes the oral microenvironment.68 To achieve the goal of controlled zero-order drug release kinetics, environmentally responsive smart hydrogels have demonstrated significant application potential as novel drug delivery systems in recent years.69

Light-responsive hydrogels stand as exemplary smart carriers, with their core mechanism involving the conversion of light energy into thermal energy via photosensitive components embedded within thermosensitive materials. This raises the temperature of material and triggers structural changes in the hydrogel, enabling on-demand drug release.70 Cheng et al71 developed a multifunctional oral hydrogel (MPCST) for oral ulcer healing. Based on a tannic acid-thioctic acid supramolecular hydrogel, the system exhibits both excellent wound exudate absorption capacity and tissue adhesion, enabling secure fixation at the ulcer site. The MPCS complex loaded in MPCST comprises polydopamine-coated molybdenum disulfide nanosheets (MoS₂@PDA), nitrous oxide precursor sodium nitroprusside (SNP), and cerium oxide (CeO₂). Under near-infrared light irradiation, MoS₂ nanosheets rapidly generate heat, triggering a photothermal effect that enhances bactericidal efficacy by altering bacterial cell membrane permeability. Concurrently, SNP decomposes to release NO, efficiently promoting angiogenesis and accelerating wound healing. Furthermore, this process synergistically enhances the antioxidant enzyme activity of CeO₂, thereby efficiently scavenging excess ROS (Figure 2A). This synergistic mechanism of photothermal heating, gas release, and antioxidant effects endows the hydrogel with multifunctional therapeutic capabilities, significantly improving oral ulcer healing efficiency.

Electric current can also serve as an environmental signal to induce hydrogel responses.72 Zhou et al73 specifically designed an electro-responsive hydrogel for oral wound healing, composed of polyacrylamide (PAA), sodium alginate (SA), and reduced graphene oxide (rGO). In this system, rGO serves as the conductive component, establishing efficient electronic pathways and reconstructing the local electric field at the wound site. This guides repair cells to migrate directionally toward the injury, providing propulsion for tissue regeneration. Concurrently, rGO exhibits excellent antioxidant enzyme activity, effectively mitigating oxidative stress damage to tissues (Figure 2B).

In recent years, multifunctional smart hydrogels with multiple response capabilities have further transcended the limitations of single-response systems, expanding the application boundaries of this field. A multifunctional composite hydrogel was constructed by means of the self-assembly between copper tannate nanostructures (CuTA NSs) and glycerol monostearate/2, 6-di-tert-butyl-4-methylphenol hydrogel.44 This material exhibits triple intelligent responsiveness, precisely adapting to the complex pathological environment of periodontitis. Firstly, the negative charge of the hydrogel enables long-term retention at positively charged inflammatory sites via electrostatic adsorption, enhancing local drug concentration. Secondly, the enzyme-sensitive ester bonds within the hydrogel are specifically hydrolysed by matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9), which is highly expressed in periodontal inflammation sites, enabling on-demand release of CuTA nanozymes. Finally, ROS-responsive CuTA nanozymes further respond to excessive ROS in the inflammatory microenvironment. By mimicking the cascade reactions of SOD and CAT, they efficiently scavenge multiple ROS species. This multi-response mechanism endows the hydrogel with broad-spectrum antibacterial and anti-biofilm capabilities. It also promotes macrophage polarization from pro-inflammatory M1 to anti-inflammatory M2 types by modulating the Nrf2/NF-κB pathway, while upregulating osteogenic gene expression. This achieves synergistic treatment by suppressing inflammation, regulating immunity, and promoting bone regeneration (Figure 2C). Furthermore, the injectable properties and excellent biocompatibility of hydrogel enhance its clinical applicability, offering a novel smart solution for comprehensive periodontitis treatment.

In summary, integrating multiple smart response mechanisms, including photo-responsive, electro-responsive, and enzyme-responsive capabilities, significantly enhances the synergistic efficacy of the nanozyme-hydrogel system in drug controlled release, antibacterial/anti-inflammatory effects, and tissue regeneration, advancing oral disease treatment toward precision and intelligence. Future research should focus on overcoming challenges such as insufficient system stability and limited biocompatibility in complex oral environments to accelerate the transition from laboratory studies to clinical applications.

Nanofibers

Nanofibers have emerged as highly promising drug delivery carriers in the biomedical field due to their unique advantages including large specific surface area, high porosity, uniform fibre dimensions, excellent biocompatibility, and the ability to mimic the structure of natural extracellular matrices.74

Recent studies have revealed that certain nanofiber materials inherently possess enzyme-like catalytic activity. For instance, polyaniline nanofibers exhibit both oxidase and POD activity, enabling in situ catalysis of ROS generation. They also enhance targeted bacterial clearance through electrostatic adsorption while avoiding the potential cytotoxicity associated with metal-based nanozymes, thereby ensuring clinical safety (Figure 3A).47

In clinical applications, electrospinning is widely adopted due to its simplicity and versatility, enabling efficient production of structurally uniform nanofiber membranes. The superior properties of these nanofiber membranes stem not only from their high porosity, which enables efficient drug loading and sustained release capabilities, but also from the extensive polymer selection and diverse post-modification strategies available. This provides flexible opportunities to incorporate smart responsive features into the fibre membranes, allowing for the precise construction of responsive nanofiber carriers tailored to the microenvironment of oral pathologies.75 Huang et al76 employed radial electrospinning to fabricate directionally aligned fibre structures that mimic the natural process of cell migration from periphery to centre during wound healing. This design effectively guides fibroblast migration along specific orientations, providing an ideal repair scaffold for diseases requiring centripetal healing, such as oral mucosal ulcers and periodontal pockets. The system, simultaneously loaded with copper peroxide nanodots possessing POD-like catalytic activity, features a self-catalytic mechanism that achieves H₂O₂ self-supply. Furthermore, Cu²⁺ catalyses H₂O₂ to generate highly cytotoxic •OH radicals via a Fenton-like reaction, conferring dual functions of antibacterial activity and wound repair (Figure 3B).

The oral microenvironment is constantly fluctuating due to influences from food, dental plaque, saliva, and other factors. Nanozymes typically exhibit low catalytic activity when subjected to environmental fluctuations, particularly pH changes. Therefore, Xu et al49 developed a copper-tetra (4 -carboxyphenyl) porphyrin (Cu-TCPP) nanozyme composite fibre membrane, which autonomously regulates the local pH. By proton dissociation, the composite fibre membrane forms a localized acidic microenvironment, which enables Cu-TCPP to sustain high POD activity within the complex oral milieu. This innovative advancement overcomes the inherent dependency of traditional nanozymes on stable pH conditions (Figure 3C).

A dual-enzyme system designed by Zhang et al50 exhibits intelligent pH-switching properties. In acidic environments, the system exhibits highly efficient POD-like activity, while in neutral or alkaline environments, it switches to HPO-like activity, catalysing the production of •OH and bromine ions, respectively (Figure 3D). These two active species synergistically eliminate bacteria and disrupt biofilm structures, enabling precise adaptation to diverse oral infection sites.

An increasing number of composite carriers are emerging to further address clinical requirements. Exemplified by hydrogel nanofiber membrane composite carriers, these advanced systems integrate the structural guidance of nanofibers with the moist physiological microenvironment of hydrogels, enabling the formation of a ‘bionic ecosystem’ that closely recapitulates the natural extracellular matrix. This system not only facilitates the efficient loading of active ingredients but also achieves active adaptation to and precise regulation of the oral microenvironment.77 Wu et al78 combined electrospinning with photopolymerization to transform physically stacked fibres into chemically crosslinked hydrogel networks. This enables the membrane material to maintain structural integrity in the aqueous oral environment. The loaded iridium nanozyme exhibits multiple enzymatic activities, including CAT, POD, and SOD, precisely scavenging various ROS to accelerate healing of oral ulcers, extraction sockets, and other wounds (Figure 4A).

Notably, the ZIF-8 nanozyme composite carrier proposed by Fang et al79 possesses glucose-responsive properties, mediating cascade catalysis to yield antibacterial Zn²⁺ and angiogenic NO, thereby synergistically enhancing therapeutic efficacy. This makes it particularly suitable for treating diabetic oral ulcers, demonstrating promising potential for personalized clinical therapy (Figure 4B).79 Currently, nanofiber carrier systems are transitioning from laboratory research to clinical translation. Future efforts should focus on developing standardized preparation processes and optimizing catalytic unit stability to address quality control challenges in large-scale production, thereby accelerating their widespread adoption in dental clinical practice.

Applications of nanozymes in oral diseases

Dental caries

Globally, the prevalence of dental caries remains persistently high, posing a significant challenge to public oral health. Research indicates that oral diseases affect approximately 3.9 billion people worldwide, with dental caries maintaining high incidence rates across all age groups.80 Its pathogenesis is complex, fundamentally driven by the synergistic interactions of the host, oral microbiota, and diet. When the oral microbial environment becomes imbalanced, cariogenic bacteria such as Streptococcus mutans (S. mutans) form structurally complex biofilms on tooth surfaces. Notably, carbohydrate metabolism in these bacteria leads to the production of acidic byproducts and extracellular EPS, which chronically disrupt the mineralization homeostasis of tooth hard tissues, ultimately resulting in enhanced demineralization and the development of carious lesions.81, 82, 83, 84

Notably, in the field of caries prevention and treatment, antimicrobial strategies relying on POD-like activity have emerged as a cutting-edge research hotspot, owing to their exceptional efficiency and remarkable specificity for pathogenic bacteria. These nanozymes catalyse the decomposition of low-concentration hydrogen peroxide, generating highly reactive •OH that directly disrupt the structure of cariogenic bacteria and disintegrate biofilms.85 As previously noted, biofilm formation results from the synergistic action of cariogenic bacteria. However, achieving targeted elimination of these bacteria and their biofilms without harming oral commensal flora remains a critical challenge. To address this issue, Duan et al86 designed Cu- and Fe-doped chitosan-based nanozymes. These nanozymes achieve precise enrichment of cariogenic bacteria through electrostatic adsorption between their surface positive charges and the negative charges on bacterial cell walls. Simultaneously, in the acidic microenvironment of carious lesions, the synergistic effect of Cu and Fe bimetals remarkably augments POD activity, which allows for efficient eradication of cariogenic bacteria with negligible impact on beneficial symbiotic bacteria such as Streptococcus oralis, ultimately achieving highly specific antibacterial efficacy.

Furthermore, the susceptibility of H₂O₂ to degradation and its short duration of action in the oral environment have constrained the application of POD-like nanozymes. Researchers have successfully surmounted this key limitation through innovative endogenous replenishment approaches.87 Wang et al88 innovatively integrated H₂O₂-producing Streptococcus gordonii with iron-based POD-mimetic nanozymes. This synergistic system enables sustained in situ generation of H₂O₂ in the oral cavity, furnishing a stable substrate for nanozyme catalysis and ultimately achieving long-term, targeted ablation of cariogenic biofilms. More complex multi-enzyme cascade systems further enhance removal efficiency. Gao et al89 designed MoSe₂ nanoflowers that, under ultrasonic excitation, accelerate the cascade reaction between GOx-like and POD-like enzymes via piezoelectric effects. This approach achieves self-sustained H₂O₂ supply while efficiently catalysing the generation of •OH radicals, demonstrating potent degradation of multidrug-resistant cariogenic bacterial biofilms.

Compared to POD-like nanozymes, oxidase-like nanozymes possess unique advantages. Importantly, these advanced nanozymes can directly catalyse oxygen to produce ROS without relying on exogenous H₂O₂, which endows them with superior adaptability to the complex oral microenvironment. Consequently, their application scope has been extended to the precision diagnosis and targeted treatment of dental caries. In caries diagnosis, Shen et al90 innovatively engineered a colorimetric sensor array based on Fe-N-C single-atom nanozymes (Fe-N-C SANs). These Fe-N-C SANs possess efficient TMB oxidation catalytic activity, which induces distinct colour development signals upon interaction with different cariogenic bacteria. Combined with machine learning algorithms for signal analysis, this system achieves rapid and specific differentiation of various cariogenic bacterial strains, thereby providing a reliable, accurate, and user-friendly detection tool for the early diagnosis of dental caries. This sensor can also be extended for diagnostic monitoring of other oral diseases, demonstrating broad clinical applicability.

In the therapeutic domain, the unique moist and dynamic environment of the oral cavity demands high adhesion and stability from treatment materials. To address this challenge, researchers incorporated the thermosensitive polymer PNIPAM into zinc porphyrin covalent organic frameworks (Zn-COF), creating a smart nanogel (Zn-COFNHG) with oxidase-like activity. This material not only retains the photosensitivity of zinc porphyrin, enabling efficient catalysis of ¹O₂ generation under external stimulation, but also significantly enhances the accessibility of active sites and interaction efficiency with bacteria through its nanogel structure. Consequently, temperature-controlled, oxidase-mimetic catalysis of TMB under visible light conditions was achieved.91

Nevertheless, the therapeutic potential of single nanozyme systems is still constrained by several inherent drawbacks, including insufficient targeting accuracy and limited remineralization capacity in deep dentin lesions; to overcome these bottlenecks and further elevate the precision and restorative efficiency of anti-caries therapy, the integration of nanozymes with other functional biomaterials has become a promising research direction, among which nanozyme–antimicrobial peptide (AMP) composite systems have attracted extensive attention based on recent advances in antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) for oral biofilm control, offering a promising solution.92 Specifically, AMPs can specifically recognize S. mutans to enhance targeting of cariogenic biofilms, while nanozymes degrade the EPS matrix to improve AMP penetration, and together they regulate oral flora and prevent pathogen overgrowth, with future designs needing to integrate these dual functions while ensuring compatibility with the oral microbiome to advance caries therapy toward a precise and restorative paradigm.

Periodontitis

Periodontitis is a chronic inflammatory disease triggered by dental plaque biofilm, with its core pathological feature being a vicious cycle of dysbiosis in the microbial community and the host immune response. Pathogens dominated by anaerobic bacteria such as Porphyromonas gingivalis and Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans, along with their metabolic byproducts, activate the host immune system. This triggers the release of numerous inflammatory mediators, causing gingival inflammation to spread to deeper tissues like the periodontal ligament and alveolar bone. Ultimately, this cascade of pathological events culminates in irreversible periodontal tissue damage, encompassing periodontal pocket formation, progressive alveolar bone resorption, and functional impairment of the periodontal ligament.93 Importantly, its pathology involves multiple interconnected pathological factors, including bacterial infection, aberrant oxidative stress, irreversible tissue destruction, and hypoxic microenvironmental conditions, making nanozymes with monofunctional enzyme-mimetic activity inadequate to address the requirements of multi-targeted therapy. Thus, nanozymes with integrated multi-enzyme synergistic activity that can comprehensively address antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, and tissue regenerative demands throughout the disease course have emerged as a pivotal research direction in periodontitis therapy.

In this field, nanozymes with ROS scavenging capabilities represent the earliest category applied to anti-inflammatory treatment of periodontitis. Importantly, cerium oxide nanoparticles (CeO₂ NPs) stand out as a typical multi-functional nanozyme with both SOD-like and CAT-like activities. These nanozymes efficiently scavenge excessive ROS, thus alleviating oxidative stress-induced tissue damage in periodontitis.94 Furthermore, Yu et al95 provided compelling in vivo evidence that CeO₂ NPs remarkably mitigate periodontal inflammation and structural impairment in rats by suppressing the MAPK–NF-κB signalling pathway, a key cascade regulating inflammatory responses (Figure 5A).

However, antioxidant therapy alone cannot fully control complex inflammation. Researchers have optimized therapeutic efficacy through composite strategies combining nanocatalysts with carriers. Polydopamine (PDA) emerged as an ideal carrier due to its excellent biocompatibility, superior radical scavenging capacity, and unique mesoporous structure.96 Zhang et al97 constructed the mPDA@CeO₂ nanozyme (Figure 5B), a composite system that combines the SOD-like and CAT-like activities of CeO₂ NPs with the hydroxyl radical scavenging capability of mesoporous polydopamine (mPDA), forming a triple synergistic antioxidant effect. Notably, the nanozyme’s inherent mesoporous structure enables simultaneous loading of two drugs with different solubilities, including hydrophobic alkanin and hydrophilic metformin. Through its sustained release property, this system maintains stable and effective drug concentrations in vivo, extending the therapeutic window. This overcomes the pharmacokinetic differences between the two drugs, enabling long-term intervention against periodontal inflammation. Furthermore, Tan et al98 introduced an Ag–TiO₂ system encapsulated within alginate (ATA) microspheres (Figure 5C). Leveraging ATA’s biocompatibility and sustained-release properties, this system significantly enhances POD-like and CAT-like activities under infrared light excitation. This approach simultaneously achieves efficient catalytic antibacterial effects and oxidative stress regulation, further expanding the application scenarios of composite nanozymes.

To further boost therapeutic precision and bioavailability, vesicular delivery systems have been developed as advanced carriers for nanozymes. Featuring high loading efficiency and stimuli-responsive release, these systems avoid poor targeting and sudden drug release seen in conventional microspheres. pH-sensitive chitosan-liposomes enable targeted delivery into periodontal pockets and improve EPS penetration.99 Meanwhile, mesenchymal stem cell-derived apoptotic vesicles (MSC-ApoVs) show strong immunomodulatory and regenerative functions.100 Loading nanozymes into MSC-ApoVs synergistically promotes the polarization of macrophages toward the M2 phenotype, inhibits osteoclasts, and promotes the proliferation and osteogenic differentiation of periodontal ligament stem cells. This composite platform enhances tissue repair capacity while simultaneously improving the stability and bioavailability of the nanozymes.

The hypoxic environment within periodontal pockets is a critical condition for anaerobic bacteria colonization and proliferation. Therefore, combining catalytic antibacterial action with hypoxia alleviation represents an innovative strategy targeting the pathological characteristics of periodontitis. Gao et al101 innovatively engineered a triple-enzyme-active ion gel incorporating copper nanoparticles (Cu-NDs) (Figure 5D). Leveraging its prominent POD-like activity, the gel efficiently catalyses H₂O₂ in the bacterial microenvironment to produce •OH, a potent reactive oxygen species, thereby achieving direct and efficient eradication of pathogenic bacteria. Concurrently, CAT-like activity decomposes H₂O₂ into oxygen, effectively alleviating hypoxia within periodontal pockets and inhibiting anaerobic bacterial growth. SOD-like activity catalyses the dismutation of superoxide anions to reduce cellular damage. Importantly, the ionic liquid (IL) component within the system enables efficient penetration of Cu-NDs into gingival tissue and gingival crevices, substantially boosting local nanozyme concentration and synergistically augmenting its antibacterial and antioxidant therapeutic efficacy, critical for addressing the localized pathological features of periodontitis. This addresses the challenge of traditional drugs struggling to penetrate deep periodontal tissues.

Recent advancements in smart, multifunctional nanozymes have propelled periodontitis treatment from static, single interventions toward dynamic, multifaceted regulation. The near-infrared light-responsive copper-cerium bimetallic oxide nanozyme (CuCeO₄) developed by Li et al37 not only exhibits photothermal effects to efficiently eliminate periodontal pathogens under external illumination but also, in the absence of light, utilizes its inherent nanozyme activity to scavenge excess ROS. It further modulates the Nrf2/HO-1 signalling pathway to synergistically promote anti-inflammatory, antioxidant effects, and alveolar bone regeneration (Figure 5E). Current nanocatalysts in periodontitis treatment have transitioned from single-function to multi-effect synergism and from passive intervention to intelligent response.

Despite recent progress in the development of nanozyme-based periodontal therapy, the field remains at the mechanistic validation stage with several critical bottlenecks. Major limitations include insufficient stability of nanocatalysts within the complex periodontal microenvironment, inadequate spatiotemporal control for alveolar bone regeneration, low therapeutic reproducibility, limited in vivo evidence, and oversimplified disease models. Moreover, the lack of standardized synthesis and biosafety evaluation protocols significantly hinders the clinical translation of next-generation nanocatalytic therapeutics.

Oral mucositis

Radiation-induced oral mucositis (RIOM) is a prevalent oral complication in clinical practice, particularly among patients undergoing radiotherapy. Its core pathological mechanism stems from epithelial cell damage caused by radiation, which further triggers a persistent vicious cycle of oxidative stress and excessive inflammatory responses, ultimately exacerbating mucosal damage. Radiation and chemotherapy directly destroy oral mucosal epithelial cells and the basement membrane, leading to DNA breaks. This damage promotes the massive generation of free radicals such as ROS within cells, causing severe oxidative stress and disrupting cellular redox balance.102 Concurrently, DNA damage activates transcription factors such as p53 and NF-κB, thereby upregulating the expression of inflammatory cytokines including TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6. These inflammatory cytokines further induce apoptosis, increase vascular permeability, and activate inflammation-related signalling pathways, exacerbating tissue damage. Ultimately, the combined effects of oxidative stress and inflammation create a vicious cycle, leading to disruption of the mucosal barrier function and promoting the onset and progression of oral mucositis.103,104

Given the complex pathological environment of RIOM, nanozymes capable of simultaneously scavenging ROS and modulating cell death pathways demonstrate unique therapeutic value. To meet this unmet clinical need, Xiao et al105 innovatively engineered a detachable and soluble microneedle patch (DC@MNs) system that synergistically co-loads multi-enzyme-active cerium dioxide nanoparticles and the iron chelator DFO. This dual-component design is tailored to simultaneously intervene in the pathological cascade of RIOM. Through the physical penetration of microneedles, active components are precisely delivered to the site of mucosal injury.97 Study demonstrates that DC@MNs mitigate inflammatory responses by synergistically eliminating ROS and inhibiting ferroptosis, thereby promoting oral mucosal repair and alleviating RIOM symptoms.

The triggers of oral mucositis extend beyond chemotherapy and radiotherapy. Various factors such as local infections, autoimmune diseases, and nutritional deficiencies may disrupt the normal metabolism and immune function of the oral mucosa, leading to oral ulcers that share similar morphology but differ in their pathological microenvironments.106 Therefore, broad-spectrum nanozyme platforms capable of adapting to the microenvironments of ulcers caused by different etiologies hold greater application potential. Research targeting refractory diabetic oral ulcers is particularly illustrative, as their mucosal healing environment exhibits characteristic features of high glucose, high ROS, and hypoxia.107 Zheng et al108 developed a GOx -catalysed H₂O₂ system by constructing a nanogel complex. Importantly, the GOx-CAT cascade catalytic system confers a synergistic therapeutic advantage for diabetic oral ulcers. GOx initiates the cascade by catalysing the oxidation of glucose at the ulcer bed, which not only reduces pathological hyperglycaemia but also ameliorates the inherent glucose metabolism impairment of diabetic wounds. Critically, the H₂O₂ byproduct is immediately converted into oxygen via CAT-mediated catalysis. This dual outcome alleviates the hypoxic microenvironment and abrogates H₂O₂-induced secondary oxidative stress. This integrated, multi-targeted strategy fundamentally optimizes the ulcer healing niche, successfully disrupting the intractable vicious cycle that perpetuates non-healing in diabetic oral ulcers.

In addition to constructing composite systems, simple surface modifications of existing nanozymes can also significantly enhance their therapeutic efficacy. Gu et al confirmed that vitamin B2-modified iron oxide nanoparticles exhibit significantly enhanced POD-like, CAT-like, and SOD-like activities compared to unmodified counterparts.45 The elevated enzymatic activities enable more efficient scavenging of ROS at the ulcer site and reduce the secretion of inflammatory mediators, thereby accelerating mucosal repair. This study further demonstrates that simple nanozyme engineering can optimize catalytic performance, offering a low-cost, easily implementable technical pathway for treating oral ulcers.

Oral cancer

Oral cancer ranks as the sixth most common malignant tumour globally, impacting public health worldwide. Statistics indicate that Asia has the highest incidence rate of oral cancer, accounting for 65.8% of all global cases. Among these, oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) constitutes the predominant histological type of oral malignancy, representing over 90% of cases.109 The pathogenesis of oral cancer is complex, resulting from the combined effects of multiple factors and steps. Internal and external factors such as smoking, alcohol consumption, high-risk HPV infection, and genetic susceptibility can induce genetic and epigenetic alterations in normal oral epithelial cells. This leads to a progressive transformation from a normal state to abnormal proliferation and development, ultimately progressing to invasive carcinoma. During this process, oncogene activation and tumour suppressor gene inactivation disrupt normal cellular regulatory mechanisms. This leads to disordered interactions between cancer cells and the tumour microenvironment (TME), with multiple dysregulated signalling pathways intertwining to collectively drive tumorigenesis and progression.110

Traditional clinical treatments for oral cancer include surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and immunotherapy. However, these approaches all have limitations.111 Surgery often compromises oral function and aesthetics, while chemotherapy and radiotherapy carry significant systemic toxicity. Immunotherapy exhibits low response rates in some patients. Against this backdrop, nanocatalytic therapy, capable of precisely responding to the TME and delivering targeted strikes, offers a novel direction for oral cancer treatment.

The oral cancer TME exhibits unique characteristics, including hypoxia, elevated GSH levels, and high H₂O₂ levels, features that serve as ideal targets for nanozyme-catalysed reactions.112 Early studies focused on enhancing oral cancer treatment efficacy through the synergistic combination of photothermal therapy (PTT) and chemokinetics-driven therapy (CDT). PTT operates by utilizing photothermal materials to generate localized high temperatures under near-infrared irradiation, directly killing cancer cells. CDT, meanwhile, relies on nanozymes to catalyse the conversion of tumour-endogenous H₂O₂ into highly toxic •OH, achieving targeted destruction.113 Qian et al114 demonstrated that molybdenum diphosphide nanorods (MoP₂ NRs) irradiated with 808 nm laser not only exhibit potent photothermal effects, elevating local tumour temperatures to eliminate cancer cells, but also simultaneously enhance their POD-like activity. This efficiently catalyses the decomposition of tumour-derived H₂O₂ into •OH radicals, further killing cancer cells through the CDT mechanism and laying the foundation for subsequent research (Figure 6A).

Although MoP₂ NRs performed excellently in a single nanozyme system, the complexity of the tumour TME, particularly its severe hypoxic state, significantly limits the efficacy of oxygen-dependent photodynamic therapy. To overcome this limitation, research has shifted toward multi-enzymatic nanoplatforms. Li et al115 constructed a sea urchin-shaped Au@Pt-Ce6-HN-1 nanoplatform system. This platform exhibits not only CAT-like activity, decomposing H₂O₂ to produce O₂ that alleviates tumour hypoxia and enhances photodynamic efficacy, but also POD-like activity that catalyses •OH generation. Under NIR-II laser excitation, its photothermal effect further accelerates catalytic reaction kinetics, achieving triple synergistic therapy combining PTT, PDT, and CDT (Figure 6B).

In addition to regulating oxygen levels, directly targeting the antioxidant defense system of tumours has also proven to be an effective strategy for enhancing therapeutic efficacy. Chan et al116 reported lipid-coated single-atom gold nanocatalysts (m-SACDs) that specifically target the over-expressed GSH in tumour cells. This system disrupts intracellular redox balance by efficiently depleting GSH and blocking ROS scavenging pathways. Simultaneously, synergistic interaction with carbon quantum dots under light irradiation significantly enhances ROS generation, effectively inducing tumour cell apoptosis (Figure 6C). Building upon the aforementioned research, more comprehensive therapeutic modalities have been developed to achieve highly efficient tumour targeting. The Cu₂Se nanoplate-composite hydrogel platform recently developed by Zhong et al117 further expands the dimensions of combination therapy. This system exhibits outstanding photothermal properties and enables precise release of NO under NIR-II laser control while efficiently depleting GSH within tumours. It effectively integrates photothermal therapy with gas therapy and chemokinetics (Figure 6D), offering a more comprehensive and robust solution for the clinical treatment of OSCC.

Notably, a systematic review of the research progress on nanozyme-based therapies for oral cancer uncovers a distinct evolutionary trend. Initially, research focused on single-agent nanozyme treatments. It has since shifted toward multi-mechanism synergistic approaches. Concurrently, therapeutic modalities have advanced from PTT or PDT to integrated regimens that combine catalytic therapy, PDT, and gas-based therapy, fully leveraging the unique pathological features of the oral cancer TME. However, despite these significant advancements, nanozyme-based therapies for oral cancer still face three core challenges hindering clinical translation and therapeutic efficacy: insufficient tumour-targeting ability of nanozymes leading to off-target toxicity, poor biocompatibility of metal-based nanozymes causing cytotoxicity and immune responses, and imprecise controlled release resulting in reduced efficacy and increased side effects. To promote the clinical translation of these nanozyme systems, future studies should prioritize three core directions: further enhancing targeting capabilities to improve tumour-specific accumulation, optimizing biocompatibility to reduce off-target toxicities, and refining controlled release properties to achieve precise temporal and spatial regulation of therapeutic effects.

Others

Beyond caries, periodontitis, oral mucositis, and oral cancer, nanozymes also play a significant role in the prevention and treatment of other oral diseases. Take peri-implantitis as an example, a common complication following implant restoration. Its pathological mechanism resembles that of periodontitis, triggered by biofilm formation around the implant. This biofilm damages peri-implant tissues, stimulates the host immune system, and induces an inflammatory response.118 The unique surface structure of implants makes effective debridement challenging with traditional instruments, and excessive debridement may damage the implant surface. Therefore, nanozymes with POD activity can catalyse the production of highly oxidative free radicals, effectively eliminating bacteria causing peri-implantitis and degrading biofilm matrix.

Furthermore, nanozymes such as MnO₂ and RuO₂ can scavenge excess ROS to mitigate inflammation and hold significant potential for promoting peri-implant bone repair and regeneration by modulating relevant osteogenic signalling pathways. This is crucial for ensuring long-term implant stability.35,40 To further optimize therapeutic efficacy and address biosafety concerns of chemically synthesized nanomaterials, green-synthesized metal and non-metal nanoparticles have been integrated with nanozymes as a promising auxiliary strategy for peri-implantitis treatment, leveraging their inherent biocompatibility and synergistic antibacterial-osteogenic effects. For instance, green-organic synthesized graphene oxide nanoparticles derived from onion extract, when combined with nanozymes, not only enhance antibacterial activity against peri-implant pathogenic bacteria but also promote osteoblast adhesion and proliferation by mimicking natural bone mineral composition.119

Nanozymes demonstrate significant potential in preventing and treating maxillofacial infections. Postoperative infections frequently occur following oral and maxillofacial surgeries, leading to delayed healing or even tissue defects. Systematic reviews indicate that restricting antibiotic use in maxillofacial trauma surgeries does not significantly increase infection risk, whereas smoking is a confirmed risk factor.120 This evidence suggests that clinical practice requires localized treatment strategies capable of precisely controlling infections without relying on systemic administration. Many nanozymes extend far beyond antibacterial properties. For instance, the aforementioned Cu-NDs ion gel not only penetrates the gingival crevice to exert antibacterial effects but also activates the hypoxia-inducible factor pathway by releasing copper ions, thereby promoting epithelial cell proliferation and matrix remodeling.101 Numerous nanozymes have been found to fundamentally improve repair outcomes by regulating immune cell polarization, such as promoting macrophage M2 phenotype conversion, activating fibroblast migration, accelerating collagen deposition, and enhancing angiogenesis.121

Oral candidiasis, a common fungal infection, also responds to nanozyme therapy. Nanozymes with oxidase-like activity catalyse the production of reactive oxygen species like superoxide anion, disrupting the cellular structure of Candida albicans. To further improve clearance efficiency, early studies attempted to combine nanoenzymes with photothermal therapy. For instance, Pd nanoparticles loaded onto metal-phenolic networks generate localized hyperthermia under near-infrared irradiation, achieving efficient physical clearance of fungi and offering novel strategies for treating conditions like denture stomatitis.122

Furthermore, leveraging nanocatalyst activity enables the development of a series of oral care products. Zhang et al123 engineered Au@AMP, which achieved nearly 100% removal of S. mutans biofilms under low-concentration H₂O₂ and near-infrared irradiation. This formulation effectively decomposed both endogenous and exogenous dental stains, demonstrating significant whitening effects while SEM analysis confirmed no enamel damage. In summary, whether in treating peri-implant infections, preventing postoperative complications, or combating fungal infections, nanozymes demonstrate unique advantages through their tunable catalytic functions and multifaceted biological effects. They elevate traditional passive antimicrobial approaches or single-modality repairs into an active, multidimensional, synergistic therapeutic strategy that regulates the biological microenvironment. This establishes a robust foundation for their broader application across oral medicine.

Clinical applications and challenges

Although nanozymes show great promise in treating oral diseases, their potential toxicity remains a significant challenge for clinical application. First, the small size and high surface activity of nanozymes may cause cytotoxicity, such as cell membrane damage and mitochondrial dysfunction. Long-term exposure may lead to DNA damage or metabolic abnormalities.124 Secondly, the release of ions from certain metal-based nanozymes may disrupt oral microbial balance and even induce the emergence of drug-resistant strains.125 In addition to toxicity, the lack of standardized preparation processes and quality control systems for nanozymes is another important factor restricting their clinical translation: the catalytic activity and biocompatibility of nanozymes are highly dependent on their preparation methods and particle size distribution, and the current lack of unified preparation standards leads to poor repeatability of nanozyme research results, which is difficult to meet the requirements of clinical application.

Nevertheless, relying on rational material design and optimized formulations, significant progress has been achieved in advancing the clinical translation of nanozymes. A prominent case in point is the successful application of Ferumoxytol, ultra-small superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles, in combating dental caries-associated biofilms. Initially approved by the United States Food and Drug Administration as an injectable iron supplement for the clinical treatment of iron-deficiency anaemia, this nanomaterial exhibits exceptional biosafety profiles, which have laid a solid foundation for its repurposing in the field of dentistry. Notably, a randomized, crossover, single-blind clinical trial has yielded the first compelling human-derived evidence confirming the efficacy of nanozymes in targeting and eradicating oral biofilms.126 The study confirmed that topical application of Ferumoxytol, through its pH-dependent POD-like activity, efficiently catalyses the in situ generation of highly toxic hydroxyl radicals from low-concentration hydrogen peroxide within the acidic biofilm microenvironment of dental caries. This process precisely degrades the extracellular polymeric matrix and disrupts the cell membranes of pathogenic bacteria. More importantly, Ferumoxytol specifically targets and eradicates S. mutans via its glucan-binding mechanism while enabling rapid detection of pathogenic biofilms through a simple colorimetric reaction. This work achieves integrated catalytic, therapeutic, and diagnostic functions also possesses unparalleled advantages in biosafety and clinical translation pathways due to its primary component being an approved drug. It establishes a critical milestone for the clinical advancement of the entire nanozyme field.

Conclusion and future outlook

This paper provides a systematic review of the latest research advances in nanozymes for oral health management. Covering their core mechanisms of action, diverse loading platforms, and specific therapeutic applications in a range of oral diseases, including dental caries, periodontitis, oral mucositis, and OSCC, this review comprehensively demonstrates the enormous potential of nanozymes as an emerging and versatile biomaterial in the field of oral medicine. The core advantage of nanozymes lies in their ability to actively respond to and regulate the complex oral microenvironment. Unlike the passive effects of traditional materials, nanozymes achieve precise targeting and dynamic intervention against oral diseases by mimicking the catalytic functions of natural enzymes. Whether catalysing antibacterial free radicals at acidic caries sites through POD-like activity, scavenging excess ROS at inflammatory sites via SOD/CAT activity, or achieving synergistic treatment through cascade reactions in tumour microenvironments, nanozymes demonstrate intelligence and efficiency surpassing conventional therapies. Notably, the strategic integration of nanozymes with versatile delivery platforms has markedly optimized their stability, tumour/tissue-targeting precision, and in-situ retention duration. This innovative engineering strategy effectively overcomes critical limitations of free nanozymes, providing a robust basis for advancing their clinical translation.

Looking forward, nanozyme research and development will embrace a new phase centred on diversification and clinical applicability, with several pivotal directions and challenges emerging. Firstly, future nanozymes will evolve beyond single catalysts into closed-loop therapeutic systems integrating diagnostic, therapeutic, restorative, and real-time monitoring functions. Leveraging AI-assisted design, this approach promises rapid screening for highly efficient, specific nanozymes targeting specific oral pathogens or pathological processes. Secondly, single-enzyme activity alone is insufficient for treating complex oral diseases. Future research will emphasize constructing multi-enzyme synergistic systems and deep integration with other therapeutic modalities. For instance, an ideal nanozyme platform for periodontitis treatment should simultaneously possess multiple capabilities: degrading biofilms, alleviating hypoxia, scavenging ROS, promoting osteogenesis, and modulating immune macrophage polarization. In addition, developing novel, low-toxicity, biodegradable nanozyme materials is fundamental to advancing their clinical application. Non-metallic nanozymes and single-atom enzymes will emerge as research focal points due to their higher catalytic efficiency and potentially superior biocompatibility. Concurrently, more comprehensive and long-term biosafety evaluation systems must be established, particularly in the specialized oral environment, to systematically investigate the profound impacts of nanozymes on oral soft tissue, hard tissue, nerves, and long-term microbial balance. Lastly, combining nanocatalysts with 3D printing to incorporate them into customized dental implants, periodontal tissue-guided regeneration membranes, or drug-delivery scaffolds enables patient-specific treatment plans. This marks the dawn of precision medicine in oral healthcare.
