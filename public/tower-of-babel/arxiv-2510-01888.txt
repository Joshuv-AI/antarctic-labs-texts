Counterfactual quantum measurements
Ingita Banerjee, Kiarn T. Laverick, Howard M. Wiseman

Abstract
Counterfactual reasoning plays a crucial role in exploring hypothetical scenarios, by comparing some consequent under conditions identical except as results from a differing antecedent. David Lewis’ well-known analysis evaluates counterfactuals using a hierarchy of desiderata. These were, however, built upon a deterministic classical framework, and whether it could be generalized to indeterministic quantum theory has been an open question. In this Letter, we propose a formalism for quantum counterfactuals in which antecedents are measurement settings. Unlike other approaches, it non-trivially answers questions like: “Given that my photon-detector, observing an atom’s fluorescence, clicked at a certain time, what would I have seen using a field-quadrature detector instead?”



Counterfactual reasoning has applications in diverse fields of study including policy making
Tetlock and Belkin 1996
, game theory
Epstude and Roese 2008
, weather and climate
Otto 2017
, artificial intelligence
Celar and Byrne 2023
, philosophy
Lewis 1979
;
Butterfield 1992
;
Pearl and Mackenzie 2018
and causal discovery
Pearl 2009
;
Imbens and Rubin 2015
;
Wang and Zhang 2025
;
Committee 2021
.
Counterfactual statements also played an important role in development of theories of physics. In quantum theory,
the 1935 debate between Einstein-Podolsky-Rosen (EPR)
Einstein et al. 1935
and Bohr
Bohr 1935

famously centred around a counterfactual: the result of a measurement that could have been performed instead of the one that actually was.
Subsequently, there have been many debates over whether ‘counterfactual definiteness’ (that counterfactual propositions have definite truth values) was an implicit assumption in the derivation of Bell’s theorem
Skyrms 1982
;
Stapp 1971
;
Lambare and Franco 2021
;
Żukowski and Brukner 2014
.

In this Letter, we are not concerned with concepts like ‘counterfactual definiteness’, which make sense only in the context of deterministic hidden variables
Bell 1966
;
Bohm and Aharonov 1957
.
Rather, we aim to understand counterfactuals within the framework of orthodox quantum theory (OQT),
without hidden variables. That is, we must grapple with the indeterminacy, and violation of classical causality
Bell 1964
;
Wood and Spekkens 2015
;
Wiseman and Cavalcanti 2016
;
Kochen and Specker 1990
;
Bell 1985
, in OQT. This is challenging
because the best-developed philosophical analyses of counterfactuals assume classical systems with deterministic causation. Nevertheless, building on these analyses, we propose a counterfactual quantum calculus
that allows us to give
non-trivial answers even to complicated questions like
“Given that my photon-detector, observing an atom’s fluorescence, clicked at a certain time, what would I have seen had I been using a field-quadrature detector instead?”

The structure of this Letter is as follows. We begin by briefly presenting the most useful classical framework for us, that of Lewis
Lewis 1979
.
Then we discuss how to extend it to the quantum domain, including evaluating counterfactual probabilities rather than counterfactual propositions. Next, we use the example of a Bell-CHSH scenario to introduce our key ideas for evaluating non-trivial counterfactual probabilities in OQT by
keeping fixed certain elements across both the actual and the counterfactual worlds. We then formalize these ideas into a general methodology for counterfactual settings in OQT. We demonstrate its effectiveness by applying it in a highly non-trivial situation involving continuous monitoring of a resonantly driven atom by two observers. Our formalism bridges the gap between the power of quantum theory and the intuitive counterfactual reasoning used in scientific explanation and prediction.

Classical Counterfactuals
.— Of the many approaches to evaluating counterfactuals in the classical realm, one of the most influential is that of Lewis
Lewis 1979
. His approach is based on similarity analysis of possible worlds, where
Lewis 1979

“a counterfactual ‘If it were that A, then it would be that C’ is (non-vacuously) true if and only if some (accessible) world where both A and C are true is more similar to our actual world, overall, than is any world where A is true but C is false.”
Here A is the antecedent and C is the consequent. Lewis claims that the world most similar to the actual one can be found by following a hierarchy of desiderata, of which only the top two concern us:

1.

Avoid big, widespread, diverse violations of law.

2.

Maximize the spatio-temporal region throughout which perfect match of particular fact prevails.

This approach, has, faced criticism
Percival 1999
;
Elga 2001
;
Horwich 1987
;
Suresh et al. 2023
, particularly that it is not always clear as to what should remain “matched” when proposing the antecedent.
In addition to this, Lewis’s analysis faces two major hurdles when it comes to applying it to OQT
Butterfield 1992
. First, it assumes a classical ontology, where there is a matter of fact about the physical state of the world at any point in space-time. Second, it assumes deterministic laws of time-evolution for those facts. This is why, for Lewis, a counterfactual proposition is either true or false. In OQT, on the other hand, most physical quantities do not have determinate properties, and the theory is inherently probabilistic when it comes to those that do (measurement results).

Extending Lewis’s analysis for OQT
.—We now present four steps (restrictions, a generalisation and an interpretation) that takes us from Lewis’s approach to a formalism for counterfactuals in OQT.
i) We restrict our description to the classical elements of the theory: measurement settings, measurement outcomes, and uncontrolled classical variables (noise). ii) We generalize from counterfactual propositions to counterfactual probabilities.
That is, given the antecedent, rather than asking
whether the consequent is true, we ask what is the probability (Pr) of the consequent.
To avoid the potential misunderstanding that
this counterfactual probability be straightforwardly empirically observable, we will instead call it a
supposability
(Su).

We note that steps (i)
and (ii) above (but not the new terminology) were also adopted in another recent work on “a semantics for quantum counterfactuals”
Suresh et al. 2023
. Our remaining steps are quite different, leading to radically different results; see End Matter. iii) We address Lewis’s first desideratum by restricting the antecedent to be measurement settings. In the context of OQT, settings are free choices external to the quantum systems under study, so there is no violation of law to consider a different choice. This type of counterfactual is also one of the most common and interesting types of speculative thinking by humans.
iv) We interpret Lewis’s second desideratum as requiring us to keep fixed (in actual and counterfactual worlds)
any classical variables uninfluenced by the counterfactual antecedent. This means other measurement settings and outcomes, even if unknown to the person posing the counterfactual (or, to be more formal, even if unspecified by the theory or as “evidence”
Pearl and Mackenzie 2018
in the counterfactual).

Step (iv) of course raises the question of what outcomes are influenced by a setting in OQT. One could give a general answer in the framework of quantum causal models
Costa and Shrapnel 2016
;
Allen et al. 2017
;
Chaves et al. 2015
;
Leifer and Spekkens 2013
, which fits well with the ontology of OQT. Adopting the appropriate quantum DAG (directed acyclic graph) for a given quantum experiment, a classical variable would be uninfluenced by a counterfactual setting if it is not amongst its descendants in the DAG. In this Letter, we avoid that complication by considering experiments with a space-time arrangement such that the variables uninfluenced by a counterfactual setting are so by virtue of being outside its future light cone.

The general counterfactual setting calculus
.—
Now we formalize the rules to compute a supposability for counterfactual settings in any scenario.
We begin by defining the set of all experimentally relevant events,
\mathbf{\Omega}=\{(\Omega_{\alpha},\alpha)\}_{\alpha}
. Here
\alpha
is a unique index for an event, including its space-time location, and
\Omega_{\alpha}
is the classical random variable describing it. (Not all variables need be random, but we allow it.)
This set contains a subset

\bm{Z}
of measurement settings,
and a disjoint subset
\bm{O}
of corresponding outcomes (so
|\bm{Z}|=|\bm{O}|
). The remaining disjoint subset
\mathbf{\Lambda}
comprises exogenous independent classical noise variables. Next, we need a physical theory defining probability distributions for the outcomes given the settings and noise,
\wp(\bm{o}|\bm{z},\bm{\lambda})
, and for the noises themselves,
\mathbf{p}=(\ldots,p_{l}(\lambda_{l}),\ldots)
.
We also need a causal structure
\mathfrak{C}
(such as a quantum DAG, or just space-time relations) on
\left\{{\alpha}\right\}
, which will be reflected in the no-signalling properties of
\wp
.
For the setting choice, we allow an arbitrary
(even adaptive
Wiseman and Milburn 2009
) strategy
\bm{\mathcal{S}}
, a separate set of non deterministic functions (also constrained by
\mathfrak{C}
) that specify each setting by
Z_{j}=\mathcal{S}_{j}(\bm{\Omega})
(requiring
|\bm{\mathcal{S}}|=|\bm{Z}|
).

To define the counterfactual, we need the following. There is some evidence,
\bm{E}\subseteq\bm{Z}\cup\bm{O}
with value
\bm{e}
. A subset
\bm{A}\subseteq\bm{Z}
, of settings, defines the counterfactual antecedent,
\bm{A}^{\prime}=\bm{a}^{\prime}
. That is, the counterfactual strategy is
\bm{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}=\overline{\bm{{\mathcal{S}}}}\,\cup\,\bm{a}^{\prime}
, where
\overline{\bm{{\mathcal{S}}}}
is a subset of
\bm{\mathcal{S}}

which is disjoint to
\bm{a}^{\prime}
, where the latter is here being treated as a set of trivial functions. Define also
\bm{D}\subset\bm{\Omega}
, the inclusive
descendants, relative to the causal structure
{\mathfrak{C}}
, of
\bm{A}
, and
\bm{D}^{\prime}
similarly for
\bm{A}^{\prime}
.
This lets us define the fixtures, the set of events such that
\bm{F}\equiv\bm{F}^{\prime}
, by

\bm{F}\equiv\bm{\Omega}\backslash\bm{D}
.
Finally, there is some consequent
\bm{C}^{\prime}\subseteq\bm{D}^{\prime}
,
for which we can consider any possible value,
\bm{C}^{\prime}=\bm{c}^{\prime}
.

The counterfactual question can now be stated. With, as background,
\wp
,
\mathbf{p}
, and

\bm{\mathcal{S}}
, what is the
supposability that
\bm{C}^{\prime}
would equal
\bm{c}^{\prime}
, had
\bm{A}^{\prime}
taken value
\bm{{a}}^{\prime}
, given
\bm{E}=\bm{e}
?
As above, we notate and define this as

\displaystyle{\rm Su}(\bm{C}^{\prime}=\bm{c}^{\prime}|\bm{A}^{\prime}=\bm{a}^{\prime}||\bm{E}=\bm{e})\coloneqq

(4)

\displaystyle\sum_{\bm{f}}\text{Pr}(\bm{C}^{\prime}=\bm{c}^{\prime}|\bm{A}^{\prime}=\bm{a}^{\prime},\bm{F}=\bm{f})\,\text{Pr}(\bm{F}=\bm{f}|\bm{E}=\bm{e}).

Note that the second Pr is evaluated (using
\wp
and
{\bf p}
) for strategy
\bm{\mathcal{S}}
, while the first
is for strategy
\bm{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}=\overline{\bm{{\mathcal{S}}}}\,\cup\,\bm{a}^{\prime}
.

Counterfactuals with continuous monitoring
.— Here we show the power of our formalism by applying it in a scenario with two parties continuously monitoring a quantum system. A resonantly driven two-level atom radiates, say via a leaky cavity, into two independent electromagnetic baths, which separately undergo continuous-in-time measurements by two observers, Alice and Bob, as in Fig.

In the interaction frame, the atom’s unconditioned state obeys the Lindblad master equation

\displaystyle\rho_{t+\text{d}t}

\displaystyle=(1+\mathcal{L}\text{d}t)\rho_{t}

\displaystyle=\gamma\text{d}t\mathcal{J}[\hat{\sigma}_{-}]\rho_{t}+\mathcal{J}[(1-i\hat{H}_{\text{eff}}\,\text{d}t)]\rho_{t},

(5)

We assume that a fraction

Conclusion
.— We have introduced a general counterfactual calculus for non-deterministic theories, replacing Lewis’s truth-valued counterfactuals
Lewis 1979
with probability-valued ones, which we call supposabilities. A key aspect of Lewis’s approach we build upon is to maximize the fixtures across actual and counterfactual worlds. Our calculus is applicable to any probabilistic theory having free measurement choices with classical outcomes, and a causal (in the no-signalling sense) structure. In particular, we have applied it to quantum scenarios, from simple projective measurements on a Bell pair to continuous measurements of fields radiated by a driven atom.

In both of the examples we analysed, there are two parties with no communication. Our calculus allows for scenarios with multiple parties and/or communication, which we will explore in future work. We can also generalize from calculating the suspected value of a photocurrent
Y_{t}
at each time
t
to calculating the suspectation of more interesting summary statistics of the whole record. A greater challenge is to relax the restriction that the antecedent of the counterfactual be a measurement setting.
Finally, we pose the question: can the supposabilities in our theory be measured experimentally, beyond merely using empirical probabilities in place of theoretical ones?

Comparison with other Work
.—
As noted in the main text, a recent work, by Suresh
et. al.

Suresh et al. 2023
, previously introduced counterfactual probabilities in a similar context to our own work. The crucial points of difference with our work start with the fact that they take inspiration from Pearl
Pearl 2000
rather than Lewis. The former uses a classical causal model, represented by a DAG, which was generalized to a quantum causal model in
Suresh et al. 2023
.
Following Pearl, their formalism proceeds in three main steps: abduction, action, and prediction.

In the abduction step, Bayesian inference is used to update the probabilities of the unknown (exogenous) variables given the evidence. For us, the evidence consists of the all the settings and only a subset of the outcomes, while they ‘canonically’ take the evidence to be
all
results and
all
settings (in our notation,
\bm{e}=(\bm{a},\bm{z})
). Also, their semantics does not allow adaptive measurement schemes. A more crucial difference is that the only variables they perform this Bayesian updating on are the classical noise variables
\bm{\Lambda}
. Thus, in the scenarios we consider in this Letter, with no classical noise, they would obtain only trivial counterfactual probabilities, in which the evidence from the actual world is irrelevant.

In the action step of Pearl’s formalism, the antecedent of the counterfactual is determined by actively intervening in the DAG and fixing the value of the corresponding random variable, thereby severing all causal arrows that lead to that variable. Their antecedent includes
all
the measurement settings
\bm{z}^{\prime}
and a sublist of the corresponding outcomes
\bm{b}^{\prime}
obtained at a set of nodes
\bm{B}
. It is this choice of antecedent that entails that only the exogenous variables
\bm{\Lambda}
are fixed across the actual and counterfactual worlds, because all other variables are descendents of
\bm{z}^{\prime}
in the DAG. Lewis, by contrast, avoided this kind of active-intervention step, treating the antecedent as a plausible alternative within the laws of the theory. This is most easily satisfied by restricting to settings for the antecedent as we have done — meaning our formalism is strictly different from, rather than more general than, the semantics of Ref.
Suresh et al. 2023
— but it is not out of the question to also consider outcomes as antecedents in Lewis’s framework, as noted in our conclusion.

The third and final step in Pearl, and in Ref.
Suresh et al. 2023
, is prediction, where the antecedent and the updated information about the classical noise
\bm{\Lambda}
is used to compute the counterfactual probabilities of the measurement outcomes
\bm{c}^{\prime}
from nodes
\bm{C}
. Here
\bm{C}
is the set of outcome nodes that are disjoint from those (
\bm{B}
) forming part of antecedent. This step is analogous to our evaluation of the supposabilities of consequents (also denoted
\bm{C}
).

Very recently, another interesting paper considering counterfactuals in quantum mechanics has appeared
Wang and Zhang 2025
. This one is more in the spirit of counterfactual definiteness as discussed in the Introduction, but Wang and Zhang consider the novel assumption of joint exogeneity (that the collection of all potential outcomes in any randomized experiment is statistically independent of the treatment assignment). They show that this assumption, interpreted in a particular way, can be falsified by OQT in the so-called instrumental scenario. Here we wish to make clear that the counterfactual question we ask does not rely on any joint exogeneity assumption, even implicitly. We do not postulate a joint distribution over the outcomes of different measurements of Alice. Critically, our supposability (not a probability) of the consequent has a type of conditioning (
||
) on events in the actual world that is different from its conditioning (
|
) on those in the counterfactual world.

Appendix A
Counterfactual Probabilities for the Bell-CHSH scenario

In this section we provide a detailed step by step comprehensive explanation of how we evaluate the result
\frac{3}{4}
for the counterfactual probability (supposability) in Eq. (3) in the main text. We start by computing the individual probabilities occuring in the supposability

{\rm Su}(A^{\prime}=\,\rightarrow|X^{\prime}=\,\leftrightarrow||A=\uparrow,X=\updownarrow,Y=\rotatebox[origin]{135.0}{$\updownarrow$})=\sum_{b}{\rm Pr}(A^{\prime}=\,\rightarrow|B=b,X^{\prime}=\,\leftrightarrow,Y=\rotatebox[origin]{135.0}{$\updownarrow$}){\rm Pr}(B=b|A=\uparrow,X=\updownarrow,Y=\rotatebox[origin]{135.0}{$\updownarrow$}).

(S.1)

Recall the actual scenario: Alice and Bob share a singlet state

|{\Psi_{-}}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|{\uparrow\downarrow}\rangle-|{\downarrow\uparrow}\rangle)
and perform a Bell-CHSH experiment on it. The joint probability distribution of the outcomes given the settings is

\wp(a,b|x,y)=\text{Pr}(A=a,B=b|X=x,Y=y)\,.

(S.2)

Although Alice does not know Bob’s outcomes, she can estimate the probabilities of his possible outcomes (the second factor in Eq. (
S.1
)) using her evidence (
A,X,Y
) and the conditional probability

{}\text{Pr}(B=b|A=a,X=x,Y=y)=\frac{\wp(a,b|x,y)}{\sum_{\beta}\wp(a,\beta|x,y)}\,.

(S.3)

Alice’s actual measurement setting and outcomes is taken to be
X=\updownarrow
and
A=\uparrow
. Since they share a singlet state, this collapses Bob’s state to
|{\downarrow}\rangle
on which he measures along the rotated basis
Y=

\updownarrow

.
Using Eq. (
S.3
), we have

\text{Pr}(B=\rotatebox[origin]{135.0}{$\uparrow$}|A=\uparrow,X=\updownarrow,Y=\rotatebox[origin]{135.0}{$\updownarrow$})=\frac{1}{2}(1+\cos{\phi})=\frac{\sqrt{2}+1}{2\sqrt{2}}\,,

(S.4)

where
\phi
is the angle between the direction of the state and the direction along which it is measured. For Bob’s measurement choice,
\phi=\pi/4
. Similarly

\text{Pr}(B=\rotatebox[origin]{135.0}{$\downarrow$}|A=\uparrow,X=\updownarrow,Y=\rotatebox[origin]{135.0}{$\updownarrow$})=\frac{1}{2}(1-\cos{\phi})=\frac{\sqrt{2}-1}{2\sqrt{2}}\,.

(S.5)

In this particular example, Bob’s outcomes constitute the classical events that are not in the future light-cone of the event
X^{\prime}
(the antecedent), therefore the probabilities of these outcomes serve as probabilities of the unknown fixtures which can now be used in the counterfactual scenario by adding them to the conditional. The probability of her consequent (
A^{\prime}
) (the first factor in Eq. (
S.1
)) given her antecedent (
X^{\prime}
) and for the different possible outcomes of Bob using the conditional probability is thus given by

{}\text{Pr}(A^{\prime}=a^{\prime}|B=b,X^{\prime}=x,Y=y)=\frac{\wp(a^{\prime},b|x,y)}{\sum_{\alpha}\wp(\alpha,b|x,y)}\,.

(S.6)

Using Eq. (
S.6
), we have

\text{Pr}(A^{\prime}=\,\rightarrow|B=\rotatebox[origin]{135.0}{$\uparrow$},X^{\prime}=\,\leftrightarrow,Y=\rotatebox[origin]{135.0}{$\updownarrow$})=\frac{\sqrt{2}+1}{2\sqrt{2}}\,,

(S.7)

and

\text{Pr}(A^{\prime}=\,\rightarrow|B=\rotatebox[origin]{135.0}{$\downarrow$},X^{\prime}=\,\leftrightarrow,Y=\rotatebox[origin]{135.0}{$\updownarrow$})=\frac{\sqrt{2}-1}{2\sqrt{2}}\,.

(S.8)

Summing over all the possible outcomes of Bob, Alice finally computes the probability of her particular consequent
A^{\prime}=\rightarrow
in the counterfactual scenario if she had chosen her measurement settings to be
X^{\prime}=\leftrightarrow

given
that she has actually measured in
X=\updownarrow
and recorded the outcome
A=\uparrow
to be

\displaystyle{\rm Su}(A^{\prime}=\,\rightarrow|X^{\prime}=\,\leftrightarrow||A=\uparrow,X=\updownarrow,Y=\rotatebox[origin]{135.0}{$\updownarrow$})

\displaystyle=\sum_{b}{\rm Pr}(A^{\prime}=\,\rightarrow|B=b,X^{\prime}=\,\leftrightarrow,Y=\rotatebox[origin]{135.0}{$\updownarrow$}){\rm Pr}(B=b|A=\uparrow,X=\updownarrow,Y=\rotatebox[origin]{135.0}{$\updownarrow$})

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{2}+1}{2\sqrt{2}}\times\frac{\sqrt{2}+1}{2\sqrt{2}}+\frac{\sqrt{2}-1}{2\sqrt{2}}\times\frac{\sqrt{2}-1}{2\sqrt{2}}=\frac{3}{4}\,.

(S.9)

Appendix C
Calculating the average over Bob’s possible records

In order to compute the final suspectation value in Eq. (12) in the main text, one needs to average over
\overleftrightarrow{m}
with the appropriate probability (second factor), i.e., the conditional probability
\text{Pr}(\overleftrightarrow{M}=\overleftrightarrow{m}|\overleftrightarrow{N}=\overleftrightarrow{n})
. This distribution is obtained from the unnormalized state,
\tilde{\rho}^{\overleftarrow{M},\overleftarrow{n}}_{T}
, by using methods developed in quantum state smoothing
Guevara and Wiseman 2015
;
Chantasri et al. 2021
.

C.1
Unnormalized states and ostensible distribution

Estimation theory aims to describe the system by conditioning upon measurements. The system takes
different quantum trajectories
or paths, depending on the measurement records
\bm{R}_{t}
obtained in each infinitesimal time
[t+\text{d}t)
. This process can be described by a set of measurement operations (completely positive maps)
\mathcal{M}_{\bm{R}_{t}}
, that evolve the (unnormalized) state forward in time via

\tilde{\rho}_{t+\text{d}t}^{\overleftarrow{\bm{R}}_{t+\text{d}t}}=\mathcal{M}_{\bm{R}_{t}}\tilde{\rho}_{t}^{\overleftarrow{\bm{R}}_{t}}\,,

(S.10)

where we introduce the following notation for the records:

\displaystyle\overleftarrow{\bm{R}}_{t}=\{\bm{R}_{s}:s\in[t_{0},t)\}\,,

(S.11)

\displaystyle\overrightarrow{\bm{R}}_{t}=\{\bm{R}_{s}:s\in[t,T)\}\,,

(S.12)

\displaystyle\overleftrightarrow{\bm{R}}_{t}=\{\bm{R}_{s}:s\in[t_{0},T)\}\,.

(S.13)

Also note that, when the record has perfect efficiency (no portion of the environment is unobserved), the completely positive map translates to a purity-preserving (mapping pure states to pure states) measurement operation defined as
\mathcal{M}_{\bm{R}_{t}}\bullet=\hat{C}_{\bm{R}_{t}}\bullet\hat{C}_{\bm{R}_{t}}^{\dagger}
.

In our case there are two observers, Alice and Bob, and we are considering the problem from Alice’s perspective. That is,
\bm{R}=(U,O)
, and we can write
\mathcal{M}_{\bm{R}_{t}}\bullet=\mathcal{M}_{U_{t}}\mathcal{M}_{O_{t}}\bullet
or
equiv.

\mathcal{M}_{\bm{R}_{t}}\bullet=\hat{C}_{\bm{U}_{t}}\hat{C}_{\bm{O}_{t}}\bullet\hat{C}_{\bm{O}_{t}}^{\dagger}\hat{C}_{\bm{U}_{t}}^{\dagger}
. Here
U
is the record unknown to Alice (but observed by Bob), while
O
is the record observed by Alice.
The unnormalized state conditioned on both measurement records (unknown and observed) can be calculated by applying a series of measurement operations to the system’s initial state
\rho_{0}
as

\tilde{\rho}_{t}^{\overleftarrow{U},\overleftarrow{O}}=\mathcal{M}_{U_{t-\text{d}t}}\mathcal{M}_{O_{t-\text{d}t}}\ldots\mathcal{M}_{U_{0}}\mathcal{M}_{O_{0}}\rho_{0}.

(S.14)

Note that we have omitted the time subscript on
\overleftarrow{U},\overleftarrow{O}
when the context makes it clear.

Now, given a past observed record
\overleftarrow{O}
, the correct statistics for the hypothetical past unobserved records
\overleftarrow{U}
cannot be obtained by the standard quantum trajectory technique. Since the
\overleftarrow{U}
and
\overleftarrow{O}
are both ‘past records’ on the total interval
[0,T)
, parts of
\overleftarrow{O}
are in the future of parts of
\overleftarrow{U}
and so in order to generate
\overleftarrow{U}
with the correct statistics given a single
\overleftarrow{O}
, one needs to consider the later parts of
\overleftarrow{O}
that affect the likelihood of the earlier parts of
\overleftarrow{U}
. This can only be obtained via the unnormalized states, with the unobserved records generated according to an ostensible probability
\wp_{\text{ost}}
, with
U_{t}
independent of
U_{s}
for
t\neq s
. (See Section 3.3.3 of Ref.
Chantasri et al. 2021
for a more detailed discussion.) Note that this ostensible distribution is something that can be chosen almost arbitrarily, because in the end the evolution of the unnormalized state captures the correct statistics of the unknown records
\overleftarrow{U}
obtained from its trace
\text{Tr}[\tilde{\rho}^{\overleftarrow{U},\overleftarrow{O}}]
.
In our case the unobserved and the observed records are Bob and Alice’s records, respectively, with

\text{actual}:\overleftrightarrow{U},\overleftrightarrow{O}=\overleftrightarrow{M},\overleftrightarrow{N}\,,\,\,\,\,\,\text{counterfactual}:\overleftrightarrow{U},\overleftrightarrow{O}=\overleftrightarrow{M},\overleftrightarrow{Y}\,.

(S.15)

C.2
The choice of ostensible probabilities for Bob’s jump

Given a record of Alice’s photon-detections
\overleftrightarrow{N}=\overleftrightarrow{n}
(which in our simulations is just a single jump at time
t_{A}\equiv 6.25\gamma^{-1}
), we generate Bob-jumps randomly at a rate
\lambda(t)
. This corresponds to choosing the following ostensible probabilities for the two possible measurement results, i.e., a photon is detected or not, at any time
t

Wiseman 1996
,

\wp_{\text{ost}}(M_{t}:=1)=\lambda(t)\text{d}t\;\;\wp_{\text{ost}}(M_{t}:=0)=1-\lambda(t)\text{d}t\,.

(S.16)

It helps to choose the ostensible distribution to be close to the actual distribution in order to maximize the effective ensemble size (and hence minimize the statistical errors)
Chantasri et al. 2021
. In this case, the most obvious choice, given Alice’s actual record, is the rate that would be generated, at each instant, by Alice’s filtered state, namely

\lambda(t)=\gamma\eta_{\rm B}\text{Tr}[\hat{\sigma}_{+}\hat{\sigma}_{-}\rho^{\overleftarrow{n}}_{t}],

(S.17)

and that is what we choose. Note that the technique would not be valid were
\lambda(t)
to depend on the randomly generated
\overleftarrow{M}
in a given run; we cannot replace
\rho^{\overleftarrow{n}}_{t}
in Eq. (
S.17
) by
\rho^{\overleftarrow{M},\overleftarrow{n}}_{t}
.

C.3
Generating an
ostensible
-weighted ensemble of Bob’s records given a single record of Alice

Alice samples Bob’s records independently in intervals that are determined by her own jump times. This also includes the interval between the initial time
t_{0}
and her first recorded jump, and the interval between her final recorded jump and the final time
T
. Restricting Bob’s sampling to these intervals ensures that inconsistencies such as simultaneous jumps recorded by Alice and Bob do not occur.

With the appropriate choice of the ostensible probabilities, Alice can now generate an ensemble for Bob’s photon-detections weighted by this distribution.
Starting with the initial time
t_{0}=\tau_{0}
, Alice can use the cumulative (
ostensible
) probability of a no-jump for Bob to generate a random number
r
such that it satisfies the equation

r=\exp\left({-\int_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau}\lambda(s)ds}\right).

(S.18)

The solution of this equation defines the first time
\tau=s_{1}
, at which Bob records a jump. Alice repeats the process but this time resetting the initial time
\tau_{0}=s_{1}
, to obtain a new solution
s_{2}
, which corresponds to the second time for Bob to record a jump. This iteration continues, with each step producing an independent jump time within the current interval. In this way Alice generates a random record of jumps for Bob
\{s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{n}\}
, in each interval,
according to the ostensible rate of jumps
\lambda
.

In the particular case we study, there are only two time intervals:
[t_{0},t_{A})
and
[t_{A},T)
determined by her recorded time of jump
t_{A}=6.25\gamma^{-1}
.
By repeating the entire process again, each time from
t_{0}=0
to
T=10\gamma^{-1}
, Alice generates an
ostensible
-weighted ensemble of Bob’s records on the interval
[t_{0},T)
, which we notate as
\tilde{\mathfrak{E}}=\{\overleftrightarrow{M}|\overleftrightarrow{N}=\delta_{t,t_{A}}\}
. Here the tilde emphasises that the elements of the ensemble appear with frequencies given by the ostensible distribution, not the actual distribution. This ensemble comprises two parts:
{\tilde{\mathfrak{E}}_{1}}=\{\overleftarrow{M}_{t_{A}}|{\overleftrightarrow{N}}=\delta_{t,t_{A}}\}
and
{\tilde{\mathfrak{E}}_{2}}=\{{\overrightarrow{M}_{t_{A}}}|{\overleftrightarrow{N}}=\delta_{t,t_{A}}\}
with 20,000 Bob’s records in each part. Since every record in
\tilde{\mathfrak{E}}_{1}
can be combined with every other record in
\tilde{\mathfrak{E}}_{2}
the total ensemble size is given by the Cartesian product
|\tilde{\mathfrak{E}}|=|\tilde{\mathfrak{E}}_{1}|\times|\tilde{\mathfrak{E}}_{2}|
which yields an ensemble of size
|\tilde{\mathfrak{E}}|=4\times 10^{8}
.

C.4
Obtaining the actual probability of Bob’s records from the unnormalized state

The actual probability for each generated record
\overleftrightarrow{M}=\overleftrightarrow{m}
in the ensemble
\tilde{\mathfrak{E}}
, is
{\rm Pr}[\overleftrightarrow{M}=\overleftrightarrow{m}|\rho_{0},\overleftrightarrow{n}]\propto\text{Tr}[\tilde{\rho}^{\overleftarrow{m},\overleftarrow{n}}_{T}]
, where

\tilde{\rho}_{T}^{{\overleftarrow{m}},\overleftarrow{n}}
is the unnormalized filtered state at the final time
T
,
conditioned on both Alice’s (fixed) and Bob’s (varying) past record, corresponding to the above ostensible probabilities for Bob’s
Chantasri et al. 2021
. The measurement operators for Alice (photon-detections) and Bob (photon-detections) that constitute the map for the evolution of the above mentioned unnormalized filtered state corresponding to the chosen ostensible probabilities are given by

\begin{cases}\hat{C}_{N}\propto 1-\frac{1}{2}(\gamma\eta_{\rm A}\hat{\sigma}_{+}\hat{\sigma}_{-})\text{d}t&\text{if no-jumps for Alice }(N=0)\\
\hat{C}_{N}\propto\hat{\sigma}_{-}&\text{if an Alice click }(N=1)\end{cases}\,,

(S.19)

and

\begin{cases}\hat{C}_{M}=1-\left(i\hat{H}+\frac{1}{2}(\gamma\eta_{\rm B}\hat{\sigma}_{+}\hat{\sigma}_{-}-\lambda)\right)\text{d}t&\text{if no-jumps for Bob }(M=0)\\
\hat{C}_{M}=\sqrt{\gamma\eta_{\rm B}/\lambda}\,\hat{\sigma}_{-}&\text{if a Bob click }(M=1)\end{cases},

(S.20)

respectively, where
\hat{H}=(\Omega/2)\hat{\sigma}_{x}
is the system Hamiltonian.

Evolving under these measurement operators via

\tilde{\rho}^{\overleftarrow{M},\overleftarrow{n}}_{t+\text{d}t}=\hat{C}_{M}\hat{C}_{n}\tilde{\rho}^{\overleftarrow{M},\overleftarrow{n}}_{t}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\hat{C}^{\dagger}_{n}\hat{C}_{M}^{\dagger}\,,

(S.21)

leads to the (unnormalized) state update

\tilde{\rho}^{\overleftarrow{M},\overleftarrow{n}}_{t+\text{d}t}=\begin{cases}\left\{{1+\text{d}t\left({{\cal L}+\lambda-\gamma\eta_{\rm B}{\cal J}[\hat{\sigma}_{-}]-\gamma\eta_{\rm A}{\cal J}[\hat{\sigma}_{-}]}\right)}\right\}\tilde{\rho}^{\overleftarrow{M},\overleftarrow{n}}_{t}&\text{if no clicks\,,}\\
(\gamma\eta_{\rm B}/\lambda){\cal J}[\hat{\sigma}_{-}]\tilde{\rho}^{\overleftarrow{M},\overleftarrow{n}}_{t}&\text{if a Bob click\,,}\\
\text{arbitrary constant}\times{\cal J}[\hat{\sigma}_{-}]\tilde{\rho}_{t}^{\overleftarrow{M},\overleftarrow{n}}&\text{when Alice click}\;(t=t_{A})\,.\par\end{cases}

(S.22)

with
\tilde{\rho}_{0}=\rho_{0}
, which we choose to be the ground state as in the main text. Note that averaging Eq. (
S.22
) over Bob’s photon-detections yields Eq. (6) in the main text.

Recall that the records for Bob’s jumps in our case were generated independently in the two intervals defined by the jumps of Alice. For a record
\{s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{n}\}
in each interval, Eq. (
S.22
) has a piecewise analytical solution given by

\tilde{\rho}_{t}^{\overleftarrow{M},\overleftarrow{n}}=\begin{cases}{\rm exp}\left[\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\lambda(s)\text{d}s\right]{\rm exp}\left[{-i\hat{H}_{\text{eff}}(t-t_{0})}\right]\rho_{0}\,{\rm exp}\left[{i\hat{H}_{\text{eff}}^{\dagger}(t-t_{0})}\right]&\text{if }t<s_{1},\\
\frac{{\rm exp}\left[{\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\lambda(s)\text{d}s}\right]}{\prod_{k=1}^{n}\lambda(s_{k})}\prod_{i=1}^{n}A(s_{i}-s_{i-1}){\rm exp}\left[{-i\hat{H}_{\text{eff}}(t-s_{n})}\right]\rho_{0}\,{\rm exp}\left[{i\hat{H}_{\text{eff}}^{\dagger}(t-s_{n})}\right]&\text{if }t\geq s_{1},\end{cases}

(S.23)

where

A(t)={\rm exp}\left[{-\frac{\gamma t}{2}}\right]\sin^{2}\left(\Omega^{\prime}t/2\right)\left(\frac{\Omega}{\Omega^{\prime}}\right)^{2}\eta_{\rm B}\gamma\,\,\,\text{and}\,\,\,\hat{H}_{\text{eff}}(t)=\hat{H}-\frac{i}{2}(\gamma\hat{\sigma}_{+}\hat{\sigma}_{-}-\lambda(t))

(S.24)

and
\Omega^{\prime}=\sqrt{\Omega^{2}-(\gamma/2)^{2}}
. The effective Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{\text{eff}}(t)
defined above governs the evolution of the atom when there is no jump recorded by either of the observers. Note that it differs from the time-independent
\hat{H}_{\text{eff}}
defined in the main text by the addition of
i\lambda(t)/2
.

C.5

The records for Bob, generated according to the ostensible distribution, were sampled independently over the two intervals determined by Alice’s recorded jump time
t_{A}
,
[t_{0}=0,t_{A})
and
[t_{A},T=10\gamma^{-1})
. In each interval the unnormalized state
\tilde{\rho}_{t}^{\overleftarrow{M},\overleftarrow{n}}
was evolved until the final time of the interval (using Eq. (
S.23
)) for all the 20,000 records in the corresponding part of
\tilde{\mathfrak{E}}

Each record was then assigned a weight equal to trace of the unnormalized state at the end of the interval. It is important to note here that the correct weight in the first interval
[t_{0},t_{A})

\text{Tr}\left[\tilde{\rho}_{t_{A}}^{\overleftarrow{M},\overleftarrow{n}}|{e}\rangle\langle{e}|\right]

\overleftrightarrow{n}

The weights were then normalized

\sum_{j=1}^{20{,}000}w_{j}=1

W_{k}

denoted by
\overleftrightarrow{M}

C.6
Using the Ensemble to calculate the Suspectation value of
Y_{t}

In the counterfactual scenario, Alice’s choice of measurement settings is homodyne detection while Bob’s setting is fixed as photon-detection. Given we can generate an equiprobable ensemble of Bob’s detections
{\mathfrak{E}}=\{\overleftrightarrow{M}|\overleftrightarrow{n}\}
, we can replace the probability in any average, like the second factor in Eq. (12) in the main text, by

\sum_{\overleftrightarrow{m}}f(\overleftrightarrow{m})\times\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\text{Pr}(\overleftrightarrow{M}=\overleftrightarrow{m}|\overleftrightarrow{N}=\overleftrightarrow{n})=\lim_{|{\mathfrak{E}}|\to\infty}\sum_{\overleftrightarrow{m}\in\mathfrak{E}}f(\overleftrightarrow{m})\times\frac{1}{|{\mathfrak{E}}|}\,,

(S.25)

where
f(\overleftrightarrow{m})
can be any expression depending on
\overleftrightarrow{m}
. Thus, given
|{\mathfrak{E}}|\gg 1
, we can approximate the final suspectation value using the equiprobable ensemble as

\mathbb{S}(Y_{t}|\text{Y-homodyne}\,\|\,\overleftrightarrow{N}=\delta_{t,6.25})\approx\frac{1}{|{\mathfrak{E}}|}\sum_{\overleftrightarrow{m}\in\,{\mathfrak{E}}}{\text{Re}}\;\frac{\text{Tr}[\hat{E}^{\overrightarrow{m}}_{t}(i\hat{\sigma}_{-})\hat{\rho}^{\overleftarrow{m}}_{t}]}{{\text{Tr}[\hat{E}_{t}^{\overrightarrow{m}}}\hat{\rho}_{t}^{\overleftarrow{m}}]}\,.

(S.26)

For our calculations of the suspectation value, we do not need such a large ensemble as used to generate Fig.
S1
(b). We find that taking
|{\mathfrak{E}}|=1000
is sufficient to obtain a curve with negligible noise.

Appendix D
Generating Fig. 5 in the End Matter

D.1
General considerations

In this section we detail the steps to generate Fig. (5) in the End Matter. That figure was used to help explain the shape of the curve obtained for the suspected value in Fig. (3) of the main text. In particular we should explain why, for times
t
close to
t_{A}
, the suspected value for Alice’s homodyne current in the counterfactual world, conditioned on her detection at
t_{A}
in the actual world, is larger than the unconditioned suspectation. [From Eq. (8), the unconditioned suspected value equals
\text{Tr}[\rho_{t}\hat{\sigma}_{y}]
, where
\rho_{t}
is the solution to the master equation (5). That is, it is simply an expected value, since a suspectation in a counterfactual world with no evidence from the actual world is simply an expectation in an alternate actual world.] Our explanation for this feature, based on Eq. (15) in the End Matter, works if it is the case that
\left\langle{\hat{\sigma}_{y}}\right\rangle^{\overleftarrow{Y}}
is usually positive. But this raises the question of what we mean by
\left\langle{\hat{\sigma}_{y}}\right\rangle^{\overleftarrow{Y}}
and by “usually”.

In saying “usually” we are recognizing that there is an ensemble of possible values of
\left\langle{\hat{\sigma}_{y}}\right\rangle^{\overleftarrow{Y}}
in the counterfactual world. At the most fine-grained level, we could consider all possible jump records
\overleftrightarrow{M}
for Bob, and, for each of them, all possible Y-homodyne records
\overleftrightarrow{Y}
for Alice. At a more coarse-grained level we could consider only conditioning on
\overleftarrow{Y}_{t}
. But these both leave open the question of what distribution of these records to use to define “usually”. If we were to consider only
\overleftarrow{Y}_{t}
, then we could consider
\overleftarrow{Y}_{t}
for any
t
to be distributed according to the supposability
{\rm Su}(\overleftarrow{Y}_{t}||\overleftrightarrow{N}=\overleftrightarrow{n})
, where
\overleftrightarrow{n}
is the jump at time
t_{A}
. However, this would seem to be presuming the very thing we are aiming at explaining — the supposable-statistical properties of
Y_{t}
in Fig. 3. An alternative would be to use an unconditioned distribution for
\overleftarrow{Y}_{t}
, but this runs the risk of giving a distribution for
\left\langle{\hat{\sigma}_{y}}\right\rangle^{\overleftarrow{Y}}
which is too far removed from what is likely in the counterfactual world to allow valid conclusions to be drawn.

For these reasons, we here adopt a relatively simple compromise solution in defining the distribution for
\overleftarrow{Y}_{t}
. We consider the state conditioned on both
\overleftrightarrow{M}
and
\overleftrightarrow{Y}
. But we do not consider all possible
\overleftrightarrow{M}
according to the distribution

{\rm Pr}(\overleftrightarrow{M}=\overleftrightarrow{m}|\overleftrightarrow{N}=\overleftrightarrow{n})
, and to compute the distribution for
\overleftrightarrow{Y}
in each case, as that would again lead to a distribution for
\overleftrightarrow{Y}
that we are trying to explain. Instead, we pick a single
\overleftrightarrow{m}^{\chi}
— a characteristic one given
\overleftrightarrow{N}=\overleftrightarrow{n}
— and from that directly compute the distribution of the filtered-state mean
\text{Tr}[{\rho}_{t}^{\overleftarrow{m}^{\chi},\overleftarrow{Y}}\hat{\sigma}_{y}]
. Note that, unlike in the computation of the suspectation for
Y_{t}
, at time
t
this depends only on
\overleftarrow{m}^{\chi}_{t}
, not
\overleftrightarrow{m}^{\chi}
(still less an ensemble of
\overleftrightarrow{m}
).

In the first subsection below we explain how we generate the distribution (over
\overleftarrow{Y}_{t}
) of
\text{Tr}[{\rho}_{t}^{\overleftarrow{m},\overleftarrow{Y}}\hat{\sigma}_{y}]
, given
\overleftrightarrow{m}
. In the second subsection we explain how we choose
\overleftrightarrow{m}^{\chi}
, the most characteristic
\overleftrightarrow{m}
.

D.2
Generating the ensemble by solving a Fokker–Planck equation

As just discussed, what we want, given a possible record
\overleftrightarrow{m}
for Bob, is an ensemble of possible Y-homodyne records for Alice such that at any time
t
,
\overleftarrow{Y}_{t}
appears with the correct probability given
\overleftarrow{m}_{t}
.
This ensemble can again be obtained again by using techniques of linear quantum trajectories for an unnormalized state
Chantasri et al. 2021
. This unnormalized state,
\tilde{\rho}_{t}^{\overleftarrow{m},\overleftarrow{Y}}
, is conditioned on both the Y-homodyne measurement (
\overleftarrow{Y}
) and fixed photon-detections for Bob (
\overleftarrow{m}
). Specifically, it is the solution of a stochastic master equation that, between two consecutive jumps for Bob, has the form

\text{d}\tilde{\rho}_{t}^{\overleftarrow{M},\overleftarrow{Y}}=-i\,\text{d}t\left[\hat{H},\tilde{\rho}_{t}^{\overleftarrow{M},\overleftarrow{Y}}\right]+\gamma\eta_{\rm A}\text{d}t\,\mathcal{D}[i\hat{\sigma}_{-}]\tilde{\rho}_{t}^{\overleftarrow{M},\overleftarrow{Y}}+\sqrt{\gamma\eta_{\rm A}}\,\text{d}W_{t}\overline{\mathcal{H}}[i\hat{\sigma}_{-}]\tilde{\rho}_{t}^{\overleftarrow{M},\overleftarrow{Y}}-\gamma\eta_{\rm B}\,\text{d}t\,\overline{\mathcal{H}}\left[\frac{1}{2}\hat{\sigma}_{+}\hat{\sigma}_{-}\right]\tilde{\rho}_{t}^{\overleftarrow{M},\overleftarrow{Y}},

(S.27)

where
\overline{\mathcal{H}}[\hat{c}]\bullet=\hat{c}\bullet+\bullet\hat{c}^{\dagger}

represents the linearized version of the operator
\mathcal{H}[\hat{c}]\bullet=\hat{c}\bullet+\bullet\hat{c}^{\dagger}-\text{Tr}(\hat{c}\bullet+\bullet\hat{c}^{\dagger})\bullet
, and
\mathcal{D}[\hat{c}]=\hat{c}\bullet\hat{c}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}(\hat{c}^{\dagger}\hat{c}\bullet+\bullet\hat{c}^{\dagger}\hat{c})
. Note that averaging Eq. (
S.27
) over Bob’s photon-detections yields Eq. (7) in the main text, while averaging it over Alice’s homodyne detection leads to the no click evolution of Eq. (13) in the End Matter.

To be consistent with the actual scenario, the initial state is taken to be the ground state. Since this (pure) unnormalized state is conditioned on all possible records, it will remain pure at all times. In addition to that, the state of the atom is being driven about the
x
-axis, which limits it to the
y
–
z
plane of the Bloch sphere and therefore we can reparametrize it by the Bloch angle
\theta
, such that

\tilde{\rho}_{t}^{\overleftarrow{M},\overleftarrow{Y}}=(\Lambda_{t}/2)(\hat{I}+\sin{\theta}\,\hat{\sigma}_{y}+\cos{\theta}\,\hat{\sigma}_{z})\,,

(S.28)

where,
\Lambda_{t}\equiv\text{Tr}[\tilde{\rho}_{t}^{\overleftarrow{M},\overleftarrow{Y}}]
takes care of the state’s norm.

As shown in Section 5.2 of Ref.
Chantasri et al. 2021
, one can use this parametrized form of the unnormalized state in Eq. (
S.27
) to obtain an unnormalized probability density function (PDF)
\tilde{\wp}_{\overleftarrow{M},\overleftarrow{Y}}(\theta)
for
\theta
given the past records, which gives the normalized PDF simply by

\wp_{\overleftarrow{M},\overleftarrow{Y}}(\theta)=\frac{\tilde{\wp}_{\overleftarrow{M},\overleftarrow{Y}}(\theta)}{\int\text{d}\theta\tilde{\wp}_{\overleftarrow{M},\overleftarrow{Y}}(\theta)}.

(S.29)

Specifically, one can obtain
\tilde{\wp}_{\overleftarrow{M},\overleftarrow{Y}}(\theta)
by solving the partial differential equation (PDE)

\partial_{t}\tilde{\wp}_{\overleftarrow{M},\overleftarrow{Y}}(\theta)=-\partial_{\theta}\!\big[A_{\theta}(\theta)\,\tilde{\wp}_{\overleftarrow{M},\overleftarrow{Y}}(\theta)\big]-\frac{\gamma\eta_{\rm B}}{2}\,(1+\cos\theta)\,\tilde{\wp}_{\overleftarrow{M},\overleftarrow{Y}}(\theta)+\frac{1}{2}\,\partial_{\theta}^{2}\!\Big[B_{\theta}(\theta)^{2}\,\tilde{\wp}_{\overleftarrow{M},\overleftarrow{Y}}(\theta)\Big]+\partial_{\theta}\!\Big[B_{\theta}(\theta)\,\sqrt{\gamma\eta_{\rm A}}\,\sin\theta\,\tilde{\wp}_{\overleftarrow{M},\overleftarrow{Y}}(\theta)\Big],

(S.30)

where

A_{\theta}(\theta)=-\Bigg(\Omega\;-\;\frac{\gamma\eta_{\rm B}}{2}\,\sin\theta\;+\;\frac{\gamma\eta_{\rm A}}{2}\,\cos\theta\,\sin\theta\Bigg)\,,\ B_{\theta}(\theta)=-\sqrt{\gamma\eta_{\rm A}}\,\big(1+\cos\theta\big).

This can be solved numerically (using Mathematica’s ‘NDSolve’ package) with the delta-function initial state approximated by a suitably narrow-width Gaussian function
\wp_{t=0}(\theta)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}\exp\,[-\frac{(\theta-\mu)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}]
. For the initial ground state, we have chosen
\theta_{0}=\pi
and
\sigma=0.01
, which gives the peak to be
\wp_{t=0}(\pi)\approx 39.89
instead of infinity and appropriate boundary conditions
\wp_{\overleftarrow{M},\overleftarrow{Y}}(\theta=0)=\wp_{\overleftarrow{M},\overleftarrow{Y}}(\theta=2\pi)\,\forall\,t
.

D.3
The choice of
\overleftrightarrow{m}^{\chi}

Recall from Fig.
S1
(b) that the rate of jumps for Bob, given
\overleftrightarrow{N}=\delta_{t,t_{A}}

0.6\gamma

1.

3.

Other photon-detections can occur at any other time. This is allowed because jumps after
t_{A}

t_{A}

{\rho}_{t_{A}}^{\overleftarrow{m}^{\chi},\overleftarrow{Y}}

With the above assumptions we can find the distribution

t=0
state in the preceding subsection up to a time

. Then we normalize it according to Eq. (
S.29

Finally, in order to obtain the sinusoidal curve of the expectation value of
\langle\sigma_{y}\rangle
, one can use the normalized state
\rho_{t}^{\overleftarrow{M},\overleftarrow{Y}}

\theta

S.28

\langle\sigma_{y}\rangle=\text{Tr}\left[\rho_{t}^{\overleftarrow{m}^{\chi},\overleftarrow{Y}}\,\hat{\sigma}_{y}\right]=\sin{\theta}.

(S.31)
