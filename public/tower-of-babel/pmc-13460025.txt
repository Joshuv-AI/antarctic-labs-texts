Venetoclax plus azacitidine, low‐dose cytarabine, aclarubicin, and G‐CSF (VA‐CAG regimen) for newly diagnosed young patients with acute myeloid leukemia: A prospective, multicenter, single‐arm Phase II clinical trial
Authors: Xiaoping Li, Qiurong Zhang, Xiaoyan Li, Lin Liu, Yonglin Shen, Yu Li, Lihui Peng, Chaoyang Zhou, Chie Qiu, Zhongtao Yuan, Shiqian Hou, Youcheng Wang, Lihua Zhang, Jingqiong Li, Liang Zhao, Qiuyuan Tang, Zucong Chen, Jing Feng, Xuejuan Zhang, Guoxiu Bao, Kui Zhang, Renpeng Zhang, Shiqi Li, Yingnian Chen, Jianhua Rao, Yancheng Dong, Dingsong Zhang, Yueyuan Wang, Aifang Li, Xi Ye, Lei Lei, Jiao Chen, Sufen Xu, Yanling Jin, Chaoen Zheng, Yang Liang, Sanbin Wang

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe conducted a prospective, multicenter, Phase II study to evaluate the safety and efficacy of venetoclax/azacitidine/low‐dose cytarabine/aclarubicin/granulocyte colony‐stimulating factor (G‐CSF) (VA‐CAG) in young patients with newly diagnosed acute myeloid leukemia (ND‐AML). The VA‐CAG regimen included venetoclax (100 mg/day, Days 1–2; 200 mg, Day 3; 400 mg/day, Days 4–21), azacitidine (75 mg/m2, Days 1–7), cytarabine (10 mg/m2/12 h, Days 1–7), aclarubicin (12 mg/m2, Days 1, 3, 5, and 7), and G‐CSF (5 μg/kg/day, Days 0–8). The primary endpoint was the complete remission (CR) rate after Cycle 1; the secondary endpoints included measurable residual disease (MRD)‐negative remission rate, adverse events, and duration of remission (DOR). A total of 120 subjects were enrolled. The median age was 50 years (interquartile range, IQR, 36–57). The CR and composite complete response (CRc) rates were 91% (95% CI 86%–96%) and 95% (95% CI 91%–99%), respectively; 84% (95% CI 81%–88%) of the CRc patients achieved MRD‐negative remission. Common Grade ≥4 adverse events included neutropenia (96%), thrombocytopenia (86%), febrile neutropenia (24%), pneumonia (4%), and sepsis (4%). The median times to recovery of absolute neutrophil count (ANC) ≥ 0.5 × 109/L and platelet count ≥ 20 × 109/L for responding patients were 14 days (IQR, 9–19) and 10 days (IQR, 4–16), and the 60‐day mortality rate was 0%. With a median follow‐up of 22.1 months (95% CI 20.4–23.7 months), 34 subjects subsequently received hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT). The median DOR values of all patients and the patients who did not receive HSCT were not reached. Hence, the VA‐CAG regimen is a safe and effective first‐line induction chemotherapy for ND‐AML patients.

INTRODUCTION

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a type of hematological malignancy with a relatively low 5‐year survival rate of less than 30%. 1 The 3 days of anthracycline + 7 days of cytarabine (3 + 7) regimen is the classical first‐line treatment for “fit” AML patients, with a complete remission (CR) rate of 50%–80%.2, 3, 4 However, in real‐world studies, fewer than 50% of AML patients receive intensive chemotherapy (IC) as a result of multiple organ dysfunction, poor physical condition, older age, or other reasons. 5 , 6 The CR rates for various low‐dose regimens, such as venetoclax plus azacitidine (VA), homoharringtonine/aclarubicin/granulocyte colony‐stimulating factor (G‐CSF) (HAG), and cytarabine/aclarubicin/G‐CSF (CAG), have been reported to be between 40% and 60%, with a median survival of 10–15 months.7, 8, 9, 10 These outcomes necessitate further improvement compared with those of other types of leukemias.

Venetoclax, a B‐cell lymphoma‐2 (BCL‐2) inhibitor, targets the antiapoptotic protein BCL‐2, inducing apoptosis in tumor cells independent of genetic mutations. 11 The combination of venetoclax and hypomethylating agents (HMAs) has been approved as a new standard treatment for older or unfit newly diagnosed AML (ND‐AML) patients, 9 resulting in a low incidence of adverse events (AEs) but a relatively low CR rate. Recent studies revealed that venetoclax combined with IC regimens, such as venetoclax plus 3 + 7, cladribine/high‐dose cytarabine/idarubin (CLIA) or fludarabine/cytarabine/G‐CSF/idarubicin (FLAG‐IDA), can further increase the composite complete response (CRc) rate to 70%–91% for ND‐AML patients.12, 13, 14 However, prolonged myelosuppression and severe AEs remain significant concerns. Ensuring a high remission rate while spontaneously reducing AEs is essential for improving induction regimens. CAG is a reduced‐intensity chemotherapy regimen designed for unfit AML patients, achieving a CR rate of approximately 40%–60%. 15 , 16 The combination of CAG and HMAs has been shown to effectively increase the CR rate to approximately 60%–80%. 17 , 18 On the basis of the improved outcomes with the addition of HMAs to CAG, we hypothesize that the combination of VA and low‐dose CAG may result in an increased remission rate and a reduction in AEs.

This prospective, multicenter, single‐arm, Phase II clinical trial was designed to validate the activity and safety of the venetoclax/azacitidine/low‐dose cytarabine/aclarubicin/G‐CSF (VA‐CAG) regimen as a first‐line induction chemotherapy for young ND‐AML patients.

METHODS

Study design and participants

We conducted a prospective, multicenter, single‐arm, Phase II clinical trial at 16 hospitals across China. Patients aged 18–65 years who were diagnosed with ND‐AML were enrolled. Key eligibility criteria included Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status of 0–2, total bilirubin < 2 × upper limit of normal (ULN), aspartic aminotransferase and/or alanine aminotransferase < 3 ULN, creatinine clearance rate ≥ 30 mL/min, and left ventricular ejection fraction ≥ 45% as determined by echocardiography. The exclusion criteria included acute promyelocytic leukemia, other hematological malignancies (patients with myelodysplastic changes in bone marrow at the time of diagnosis were included), uncontrolled active infection and bleeding, severe cardiopulmonary insufficiency, inability to sign the informed consent form, and a life expectancy of less than 1 month. Withdrawal criteria included the emergence of intolerable adverse effects related to trial treatment, patients withdrawing informed consent, or patients lost to follow‐up during the evaluation period.

Sample size

This study uses a one‐sample proportion test design. The primary endpoint was the CR rate. The sample size was calculated to establish superiority, n=[Z1−αP0(1−P0)+Z1−βPT(1−PT)]2(PT−P0)2

The CR rate was 90% for the VA‐CAG regimen (PT) and 80% for the 3 + 7 regimen (P0). A sample size of 108 patients provided 80% power (1 − β = 0.80), with a one‐sided Type 1 error of 2.5% (α = 0.025). Allowing for a 10% dropout rate, the final target was 120 patients in the population.

Procedure

All patients were hospitalized throughout the course of therapy. The VA‐CAG regimen included the following: venetoclax administered orally once daily (100 mg on Days 1–2; 200 mg on Day 3; 400 mg on Days 4–21), azacitidine (75 mg/m2 on Days 1–7), cytarabine (10 mg/m2 every 12 h on Days 1–7), aclarubicin (12 mg/m2 on Days 1, 3, 5, and 7), and G‐CSF (5 μg/kg on Days 0–8). Note: The venetoclax dose was adjusted on the basis of concomitant CYP3A inhibitors; patients with hyperleukocytosis received pretreatment with 100 mg/m2/day cytarabine until the peripheral white blood cell (WBC) count fell below 20 × 109/L. G‐CSF administration was discontinued if the WBC count exceeded 20 × 109/L (Supporting Information S2: Figure 1). Drugs (including FLT3 inhibitors, IDH1/2 inhibitors, etc.) were not permitted to be used spontaneously.

On the basis of the physician's preference and the patient's specific circumstances, patients were administered at least one antifungal drug for prevention or treatment. Broad‐spectrum antibacterial drugs were utilized for patients who presented agranulocytosis accompanied by fever. Throughout the agranulocytosis phase, patients received subcutaneous injections of G‐CSF at dosages ranging from 5 to 10 μg/kg/day. Transfusions of red blood cells (RBCs) or platelets were permitted when the hemoglobin concentration decreased to less than 60 g/L or when the platelet count was less than 20 × 109/L, respectively.

Patients who achieved partial remission (PR) were eligible to receive a second cycle of the VA‐CAG regimen. Those who showed no response (NR) were administered a 3 + 7 regimen as salvage reinduction chemotherapy. Patients who achieved CRc and had no ongoing complications from previous treatments were considered for consolidation chemotherapy or hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) according to their prognosis risk (refer to the European Leukaemia Network [ELN] 2022 risk stratification system 19 ), physical conditions, and physicians' judgments and preferences. ELN favorable‐risk patients generally received four cycles of high‐dose cytarabine (3 g/m2 q12h on Days 1, 3, and 5). Intermediate or adverse patients, or those who remain with measurable residual disease (MRD) after two cycles of chemotherapy, need to consider a bridge to HSCT. Patients without a suitable donor or who rejected HSCT were offered consolidation chemotherapy (Supporting Information S2: Figure 1).

MICM analysis and MRD assessments

Leukemia‐associated immunophenotyping analysis was performed by 6‐color multiparameter flow cytometry (MFC) at study enrollment. Molecular analysis was performed via real‐time polymerase chain reaction (RT‐PCR) and a 56‐gene next‐generation sequencing platform. Cytogenetic analysis was performed with a standard metaphase karyotype and fluorescence in situ hybridization analysis.

For response assessments, bone marrow biopsies were performed at 28–35 days or 1 week after hematologic recovery, and spontaneous MRD was tested by MFC with a minimum sensitivity of 10−3 to 10−4 (0.1%–0.01%). MRD < 0.1% was defined as MRD‐negative.

Endpoint and definition

The primary endpoint was the CR rate after Cycle 1, and the secondary endpoints were the MRD‐negative rate, AEs, and duration of remission (DOR); the other endpoints included relapse‐free survival (RFS) and overall survival (OS).

AEs were documented and categorized by the treating physician according to the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 5.0. 20 Complete remission with incomplete hematologic recovery (CRi) was defined as a CR with an absolute neutrophil count (ANC) < 1 × 109/L or a platelet count < 100 × 109/L independent from RBCs transfusion. CRc was defined as CR plus CRi, and a PR was defined as having MRD between 5% and 25% and a leukemic blast percentage reduction of more than 50%. NR was defined as the failure to achieve CRc or PR. The MRD‐negative rate was defined as the proportion of patients who converted to MRD < 10−3 by flow cytometry among the population who achieved CRc. DOR was calculated as the period from the first documented CR or CRi to disease progression or death in CR patients whose data were censored at the time of death. OS was calculated from the time of induction to death with censoring of patients at the point of last follow‐up. RFS was calculated as the time from achievement of a CR or CRi to hematologic relapse or death, with censoring of patients at the last follow‐up. The durations of Grade 4 neutrocytopenia and thrombocytopenia were calculated from the end of chemotherapy (Day 7) when ANC was <0.5 × 109/L or the platelet count was <20 × 109/L to the recovery of ANC ≥ 0.5 × 109/L or the platelet count was ≥20 × 109/L. Other clinical responses were defined according to modified International Working Group response criteria for AML. 21

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 8.0.2) and SPSS (version 27.0). The median follow‐up period was calculated via the reverse Kaplan–Meier method. Time‐to‐event outcomes were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and the differences were compared using log‐rank tests. The survival outcomes between those who underwent HSCT after the landmark (Day 90 after the initiation of the VA‐CAG therapy) and those who did not undergo HSCT were compared using landmark analysis with R (version 4.0.5). The cut‐off for clinical data collection was December 30, 2025. Confidence intervals (CIs) were computed using the Clopper–Pearson method. All the statistical tests were two‐tailed with the significance threshold set at 0.05.

RESULTS

Demographics and baseline characteristics

From December 20, 2022, to December 30, 2024, a total of 124 ND‐AML patients from 16 hospitals were evaluated for study eligibility, 120 of whom were enrolled in the study. Four patients were excluded from this study because they did not meet the eligibility criteria or withdrew consent (Figure 1). The study cohort consisted of 69 males and 51 females, with a median age of 50 years (interquartile range, IQR, 36–57) at the time of enrollment. According to the 2022 ELN risk stratification system, 51 (42%), 31 (26%), and 38 (32%) patients were classified as adverse risk, intermediate risk, and favorable risk, respectively. The baseline characteristics of the patients are summarized in Table 1. The common molecular mutations and gene rearrangements observed included WT1 (33%), FLT3‐ITD (23%), IDH1/2 (23%), NRAS/KRAS (22%), DNMT3A (18%), NPM1 (18%), CEBPA‐bZIP (13%), RUNX1::RUNX1T1 (12%), KIT (12%), ASXL1 (11%), TET2 (11%), and CBFβ::MYH11 (10%). Twenty‐seven patients had FLT3‐ITD mutations, 15 of whom had a high allelic ratio. Two patients exhibited detectable TP53 mutations. More than half (62%) of all patients received documented administration of CYP3A inhibitors during the induction window for antifungal prophylaxis or treatment.

Clinical response

All patients enrolled in the study received at least one cycle of VA‐CAG treatment. Following a single cycle, the ORR of the entire population was 98% (95% CI 95%–100%), with a CR rate of 91% (95% CI 86%–96%), a CRi rate of 4% (95% CI 1%–8%), and a PR rate of 3% (95% CI 0%–12%). Furthermore, 92 patients (84%, 95% CI 81%–88%) out of 110 who achieved CRc achieved MRD‐negative remission as assessed by MFC (4 patients did not receive MRD assessment). The CR rates of the ELN favorable, intermediate, and unfavorable risk groups at Cycle 1 were 97% (95% CI 91%–99%), 94% (95% CI 77%–100%), and 84% (95% CI 73%–96%), respectively. Similarly, the MRD‐negative remission rates of the CRc patients of these groups were 86% (95% CI 80%–92%), 90% (95% CI 79%–100%), and 78% (95% CI 66%–90%), respectively (Table 2). Three patients who achieved a PR underwent a second cycle of the VA‐CAG regimen, with two of them achieving a CR. Additionally, three NR patients were treated with a 3 + 7 regimen, two of whom achieved CR (Figure 1). The cumulative CRc rate after two cycles of VA‐CAG was 97% (95% CI 93%–100%).

In the evaluation of the gene mutation or gene rearrangement subgroup (Figure 2A), we observed 100% CR rates in AML patients with CEBPA‐bZIPmut, KITmut, RUNX1::RUNX1T1, and CBFβ::MYH11 subgroups. However, the t(8;21) AML patients had a low MRD‐negative rate of 77% assessed by MFC and 31% assessed by RT‐PCR (Supporting Information S2: Table 1). Satisfactory CR rates were also noted for AML patients with mutations of active signaling‐related genes (NRAS/KRAS/PTPN11, 93% and FLT3‐ITD, 89%), myelodysplasia‐related genes (ASXL1, 92% and RUNX1, 90%), and methylation genes (TET2, 92%; DNMT3A, 91%; and IDH1/2, 89%) (Supporting Information S2: Table 1). Among the 27 AML patients with FLT3‐ITD mutation, 9 of the 10 patients with coexisting NPM1mut achieved CR, and the median OS of patients with FLT3‐ITDmut was 26.8 months (Supporting Information S2: Table 2, Supporting Information S2: Figure 2). However, AML patients with KMT2A‐rearrangement achieved a relatively inferior CR rate of 80%, with MRD‐negative rates of 89% (as assessed by MFC) and 50% (as determined using RT‐PCR). In addition, 12 of the 14 AML patients with complex karyotypes achieved CR, but only 62% achieved MRD‐negative CR (Supporting Information S2: Table 1), with a median RFS of 11.7 months (Supporting Information S2: Table 2, Supporting Information S2: Figure 2). Although two AML patients with baseline TP53 mutations achieved CR, both deceased during the observation period, resulting in median OS and RFS of 6.9 and 4.6 months, respectively (Supporting Information S2: Table 2, Supporting Information S2: Figure 2). Safety

Frequent AEs of any grade are displayed in Table 3. Hematological AEs were most common. Grade ≥4 neutropenia and thrombocytopenia were noted in 96% (n = 116) and 86% (n = 102) of the patients, respectively, whereas 57% (n = 70) of the patients developed Grade ≥3 anemia. Severe myelosuppression resulted in venetoclax withdrawal on Day 14 in two patients who presented with severe bleeding. The median times to recovery of ANC ≥ 0.5 × 109/L and platelet count ≥ 20 × 109/L were 14 days (IQR, 9–19) and 10 days (IQR, 4–16), respectively, and the median times to recovery of ANC ≥ 1.0 × 109/L and platelet count ≥ 50 × 109/L were 15 days (IQR, 10–19) and 12 days (IQR, 6–17), respectively. During the induction period, the median RBC volume and platelet transfusion were 7 units (IQR, 3.5–9.5) and 40 units (IQR, 20–70), respectively.

Non‐hematologic AEs of any grade occurring in ≥10% of patients predominantly included fatigue (100%), infections (43%), nausea (36%), transaminase elevation (23%), decreased appetite (22%), headache (19%), bleeding (18%), constipation (14%), and vomiting (10%). Infectious complications included pneumonia (23%), bacteraemia (5%), skin and soft tissue infections (10%), viral infections (8%), and sepsis (4%). Grade 4 fungal pneumonia was suspected or confirmed in 4% (n = 5) of patients with radiographic or clinical evidence. All infections were effectively controlled following anti‐infective therapy. With adequate hydration and the administration of 100 mg/m2 per day of cytarabine to reduce the WBC count to less than 20 × 109/L prior to VA‐CAG treatment, tumor lysis syndrome was not observed. Cardiac events, severe mucositis and fatal bleeding events did not occur during the induction phase. Early death within 60 days was recorded as 0.

Survival outcomes

After one or two cycles of induction/reinduction therapy, 116 out of 120 patients advanced to the consolidation phase. Two patients received salvage therapy due to disease progression, and another two achieved CR after the 3 + 7 regimen and subsequently received consolidative chemotherapy and HSCT (Figure 1). Regarding post‐remission consolidation treatment, 33 patients in the CRc state (20 were MRD‐negative) transitioned to HSCT after a median of 2 consolidation cycles (range, 0–10) and a median of 3 months from the first time of achieving CR to transplantation, whereas 83 patients who did not undergo HSCT continued with sustained chemotherapy. The swimmer plot is displayed in Figure 3. Over the median follow‐up period of 22.1 months (95% CI 20.4–23.7 months), 5 patients were lost to follow‐up, and 35 deaths occurred. The predominant cause of death was disease progression (n = 22), followed by infection (n = 5), bleeding events (n = 5), and HSCT (n = 3). The median OS and RFS for the entire cohort were not reached. The 12‐month OS and RFS rates of all patients were 86% (95% CI 79%–91%) and 76% (95% CI 66%–83%), respectively. The estimated 24‐month OS and RFS were 69% (95% CI 59–77) and 63% (95% CI 53–72), respectively (Figure 4A,B).

The survival outcomes were compared between patient we transitioned to HSCT (HSCT group) and patients who received consolidative chemotherapy (No‐HSCT group) (Supporting Information S2: Table 3). Over the median follow‐up period of 22.4 months (95% CI 15.3–29.5 months) after HSCT, the median OS (P = 0.018) and RFS (P = 0.028) significantly improved in patients of the HSCT group when compared with those of the No‐HSCT group in the landmark analysis (Figure 4C,D). Only one patient relapsed following HSCT (at 21.8 months), whereas 25 patients in the No‐HSCT group experienced relapse at a median of 8.6 months (range, 2.0–18.9 months). We also analyzed the survival outcomes across ELN risk stratification. Patients in the intermediate‐risk group seemed to have the best OS outcomes, whereas patients in the adverse‐risk group exhibited the worst OS, with a median OS of 27.7 months. However, no group difference was noted (P = 0.133). Moreover, no significant differences were noted in RFS among the three groups (P = 0.337) (Figure 4E). Given the short follow‐up time and the limited sample size, any ELN‐risk group did not achieve the median DOR (Supporting Information S2: Table 2). In the gene subgroup analysis, compared with TP53‐negative patients, patients with TP53 mutations had obviously shorter OS (P < 0.001) and RFS (P < 0.001). Relatively inferior RFS was observed in patients with RUNX1::RUNX1T1 fusion (P = 0.095) or complex karyotype (P = 0.081), although the results were not statistically different (Supporting Information S2: Figure 2).

DISCUSSION

Achieving deep remission and ensuring safety are two crucial goals of a front‐line induction regimen. The advent of venetoclax has swiftly established itself as a pivotal component in AML treatment regimens. The VIALE‐A trial demonstrated the efficacy of venetoclax combined with azacitidine, achieving a remarkable CR rate of 66% and extending the median OS to 16.6 months in unfit patients. 11 The CAG regimen, initially reported by a Japanese group for AML treatment, is a relatively low‐intensity chemotherapy with manageable toxicity. 15 Approximately 40%–60% of patients achieve CR following a single course of CAG, 15 , 16 leading to its widespread adoption in Japan and China, particularly in elderly patients diagnosed with AML or myelodysplastic syndrome. Various modified CAG‐based protocols have been implemented, such as CAG plus decitabine 17 , 18 and CAG plus PD‐1 inhibitor. 22 Inspired by the concept of integrating venetoclax with less intense chemotherapy, our study was meticulously designed to combine a short‐term VA regimen with a low‐dose CAG approach. This innovative regimen offers a highly effective and well‐tolerated treatment paradigm for ND‐AML patients, characterized by an outstanding remission rate, a reduced duration of myelosuppression, and a decreased incidence of AEs.

Wang et al. demonstrated the efficacy of combining venetoclax with the 3 + 7 regimen as a first‐line treatment for fit ND‐AML patients, reporting CR and MRD‐negative remission rates of 91% (95% CI 76%–98%, n = 33) and 97% (95% CI 83%–100%), 12 respectively. Another two studies have reported that a sustained course of venetoclax plus CLIA and venetoclax plus FLAG‐IDA for ND‐AML patients reached CR rates of 84% (n = 50) and 70% (n = 29), respectively. Moreover, 82% and 96% of patients achieved MRD negativity, respectively. 13 , 14 In our study, the VA‐CAG regimen resulted in favorable outcomes, with a CR rate of 91% and CRc rate of 96% after Cycle 1, although the MRD remission rate (84%) of the entire cohort did not appear superior to that of those receiving the venetoclax + IC regimens mentioned above. Although older adults (age > 65 years) were excluded from this study, more than half of the patients were over 50 years old. Consequently, further investigations should be conducted in older and unfit ND‐AML patient populations.

In subgroup analyses, patients with core binding factor (CBF) AML and myelodysplasia‐related AML demonstrated sensitivity to VA‐CAG therapy, which was in agreement with some reports. 23 , 24 Conversely, patients harboring KMT2A‐rearrangements exhibited inferior remission outcomes. It has been reported that FLT3‐ITD mutation is associated with adverse clinical outcomes and has limited efficacy with venetoclax. 25 In this study, patients with FLT3‐ITDmut achieved a high CR rate (89%) and a high 12‐month OS (86%) without the use of an FLT3 inhibitor. Large‐sample randomized‐controlled trials are expected for validating whether the VA‐CAG regimen can overcome the poor prognosis of FLT3‐ITD in the future. Interestingly, t(8;21) AML patients achieved a 100% CR rate but showed low MRD‐negative remission rates (77% by FCM, 31% by PCR) and shorter RFS. Relatively poor results for patients with t(8;21) AML have also been reported after the administration of venetoclax‐based therapies. 20 Some studies have reported a low response rate in patients with t(8;21) AML who received venetoclax + HMAs, because RUNX1::RUNX1T1 is associated with low expression of BCL‐2 and high expression of BCL‐XL. 26 , 27 Therefore, on the basis of our observations, we hypothesized that the incorporation of low‐dose CAG into the VA regimen may help to ameliorate the reduced responsiveness of t(8;21) AML patients to venetoclax to some extent. However, due to persistent MRD, patients do not derive significant survival clinical benefits from these induction and low‐intensity consolidation approaches.

In this study, the most common severe AEs were myelosuppression and infection, which is in accordance with the expectations and the results of previous studies on AML.12, 13, 14, 15 Although nearly all the patients experienced neutropenia and approximately half developed infectious complications of any grade, only 8% of the patients developed Grade 4 sepsis or pneumonia. Thus, we speculated that the life‐threatening infections were prevented through efficacious antimicrobial prophylaxis and because of the short neutropenic duration. Despite a highly common severe thrombocytopenia, no fatal bleeding events were reported due to the brief period of thrombocytopenia. Venetoclax was discontinued in only two patients due to persistent Grade 4 thrombocytopenia and severe bleeding, and both patients achieved CR. Therefore, this regimen is an efficient option for patients who are not suitable for IC, and it is more feasible as a bridging therapy for HSCT. Bazinet et al. are exploring the reduction in venetoclax dosage in AML therapies. 28 Based on our findings and the findings of the study by Bazinet et al., we propose adjusting the dose of venetoclax as a focus of the next phase of exploration. Possibly due to the reduced dosage of alkylating agents, the relatively young average age of the patients, and the short follow‐up period, no treatment‐related heart failure was observed during the follow‐up in this study. Most of the patients exhibited favorable tolerance in the induction phase, with mild mucositis and vomiting. Improved safety decreased patients' apprehension towards induction, which facilitated their seamless transition to the subsequent treatment stages.

The median times to recovery of patients with ANC ≥ 0.5 × 109/L and platelet count ≥ 20 × 109/L were 14 days and 10 days after the completion of chemotherapy, respectively. These recovery durations are favorable compared to those with venetoclax plus 3 + 7 (19 days and 20 days) 12 and venetoclax+CLAD/LDAC (26 days and 21 days) regimens. 29 Consequently, VA‐CAG may result in significant reductions in infection‐ and treatment‐related mortality. Notably, approximately half of the patients experienced rapid megakaryocyte recovery between Day 14 and Day 21 during venetoclax administration, and approximately one‐quarter of the patients did not require platelet transfusion. Given the abbreviated period of myelosuppression and lower demand for blood transfusion, VA‐CAG treatment could alleviate the supply pressure for blood products and potentially decrease the production of anti‐RBC/anti‐platelet antibodies as well as the risk of blood‐borne diseases.

In this study, most of the patients did not undergo HSCT. Patients who underwent HSCT seemingly had better survival outcomes than patients who received only consolidation or maintenance chemotherapy. Consequently, VA‐CAG has emerged as an effective non‐intensive inducer of ND‐AMLs and is particularly useful as a bridge to HSCT in patients with poor prognosis. However, the short follow‐up periods limit our ability to draw definitive conclusions regarding the outcomes in the analysis stratified by ELN risk stratification. To date, no significant differences have been observed among the three ELN groups.

This study has several limitations. This was a single‐arm trial without direct evidence for comparing the optimal efficacy of this regimen against traditional chemotherapy, venetoclax plus IC, or venetoclax combined with small molecular targeted drugs. The short follow‐up duration, low number of patients with no events, and small sample size precluded definitive conclusions regarding the survival outcomes among the different ELN stratification groups. Furthermore, the lack of standardized post‐induction consolidation and maintenance protocols may have introduced variability in the long‐term results. Therefore, a large‐sample randomized clinical trial comparing VA‐CAG with venetoclax plus IC is warranted to confirm the long‐term survival benefits of VA‐CAG and to clarify the potential to achieve higher remission rates and lower adverse effects.

In conclusion, VA‐CAG is a promising chemotherapy for AML patients with a high CR rate and well‐tolerated AEs. The VA‐CAG regimen could be considered a first‐line induction therapy for suitable AML patients.
