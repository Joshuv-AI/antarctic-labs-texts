AI utilization, enjoyment, trust and instructor competence as predictors of intercultural communication competence among Chinese students
Authors: Huayi Wang, Caiyun Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractInternational students in China navigate both linguistic and cultural adaptation demands in classrooms increasingly infused with artificial intelligence (AI). Guided by the broaden-and-build theory of positive emotions, this study tested whether AI-mediated learning enjoyment channels the impact of AI-mediated interaction utilization on communication competence in intercultural contexts, and whether trust in AI recommendations and instructor AI pedagogical competence strengthen this indirect effect. Survey data from 427 students in Wuhan, Xi’an, and Hangzhou were analyzed using PLS-SEM. Results revealed a significant direct effect of AI utilization on intercultural competence (β = 0.292) and a complementary enjoyment-mediated pathway (β = 0.051). Both trust and instructor competence amplified the indirect link. The model explained 34.7% of the variance in intercultural competence, offering empirical guidance for AI-rich, globally connected campuses.Keywords: AI-mediated interaction utilization, AI-mediated learning enjoyment, Communication competence in intercultural contexts, Trust in AI recommendations, Instructor AI pedagogical competence, Broaden-and-build theorySubject terms: Cultural and media studies; Cultural and media studies; Education; Information systems and information technology; Language and linguistics; Language and linguistics; Science, technology and society

Introduction

The convergence of artificial intelligence (AI) and the digital economy is faster than ever, changing the landscape in which nations are developing human capital. In the last decade, information and communication technology sectors have expanded at three times the average rate of growth of the rest of the economy in the OECD area, reflecting a structural shift in productivity growth to work based on the use of technology1. With the digital transformation becoming a key prerequisite for an inclusive growth, the development institutions associate national competitiveness and citizens’ ability to engage with AI ecosystems2. This technology trend is undeniable within higher education: 92% of students now incorporate generative AI in their studies, up from 66% only a year ago3, and institutional leaders are beginning to define AI strategy as an academic orphan. From chatbot tutors to virtual writing partners to automated feedback engines, tutoring technology is no longer an experiment but a mainstream intervention with clear and proven benefits for understanding and persistence especially for multilingual and international student populations4.

China is the center of this transition. With Belt and Road scholarships and policy support to internationalize their campuses, Chinese universities are aggressively attracting students from Asia, Africa and beyond to enjoy tech-enabled learning spaces. As a result of this influx, China is increasingly becoming a laboratory and a melting pot for intercultural contact, as there is a mixing of learners with diverse language repertoires, cultural conventions and digital practices in the classroom. The advantages and disadvantages of international students in China are also special in the development of intercultural communication abilities, because the use of AI has been increasingly integrated into education in Chinese universities5. The Chinese government has prioritized AI literacy at the national level, for instance Beijing mandated AI training of all students by 2025, as a part of a much wider initiative to drive innovation6. Usage of AI is growing rapidly among the students: there is currently a survey showing up to 92% of university students are already using AI tools in their studies, a huge increase from 66% a year ago7. These trends make it harder to understand the effects of AI-mediated learning experiences on important outcomes such as intercultural communication competence in higher education in China.

A survey of engineering students in some of the Chinese provinces recently has indicated that 20.95% of the students use generative AI tools on a daily basis and 41.89% use them at least more than once a week, but 4.73% never use generative AI8. These statistics show that AI has become part of the studying process of students. On the other hand, more foreign students enter Chinese universities under scholarships of national initiatives such as the BRI; a research on cross-cultural communication shows that international students are more susceptible to cultural shock and thus need to be guided to intercultural communicative competence9. Drawing on a confluence of these tendencies, China is a convenient setting to examine the relationships between the enjoyment of AI-mediated learning, the application of AI-mediated interaction, and intercultural communication competence.

Employers echo this urgency. Global recruiter surveys put the intercultural competency and facility with AI among the most important generic skills required by graduate hires - directly related to organizational innovation and market reach10. Yet the challenge for universities at scale remains finding pedagogical structures that can bring together fluency in the technical and intercultural sensibility. Against this backdrop, Communication Competence in Intercultural Contexts - the ability to negotiate, co-create and control meaning with culturally diverse others - emerges as a critical graduate quality, impacting on employability in transnational labour markets as well as social cohesion on increasingly diverse campuses. This study makes the case that the development of such competence at this stage is dependent on the degree to which students use AI to engage in dialogue, explore, and reflect. Past research has shown that AI-mediated technologies, from translation engines to conversational bots, can replicate real-life intercultural interaction, offering low-stakes environments to learn perspective-taking and flexible messaging behavior11. We therefore identify the AI-mediated interaction Utilization as a key achiever to the intercultural communication development of Chinese universities, leading to an empirical research that seeks to investigate the direct effect of AI-mediated interaction on the communicative Resonance of students.

Recent scholarship on technology-enhanced learning teaches us to consider outcomes not so much in terms of the technologies used, but rather in terms of the reasons underlying an effect and the conditions under which it is promoted or inhibited12. In the context of the above reasoning, AI-Mediated Learning Enjoyment can be considered the mediator as it has been able to trap the affective mechanism of how AI-Mediated Interaction Utilization may result in more engagement and skill development, whereas empirical research has indicated that class online enjoyment consistently channels digital exposure into more significant achievement and participation, particularly on language and intercultural front13,14. We also have found that positive emotions have important psychological features: positive emotions broaden attention, enhance intrinsic motivation and promote exploratory behavior, and these are all key antecedents to practicing complex intercultural communication. Employers echo this urgency. Global recruiter surveys have rated cross-cultural capability and comfort with artificial intelligence as one of the most important general capabilities among graduate hires - directly correlated to organizational innovation and market reach10. Yet at scale, universities continue to fail to create pedagogical architectures able to marry cultural humility with technical sophistication. Against this background, Communication Competence in an Intercultural Context - the ability to negotiate, co-create and control meaning with culturally diverse others - emerges as a critical graduate competence, impacting on employability in transnational labour markets and social cohesion in increasingly diverse campuses. This research concludes that the development of such competence at this time is dependent on the degree to which students utilize AI to interact, investigate and reflect. Emerging evidence is showing that AI-mediated technologies, including translation engines and conversational bots, can simulate real-life intercultural interactions, and offer low-stakes settings to practice perspective-taking and flexible messaging behaviors11. We thus identify the AI-mediated interaction utilization as the key motivator to the intercultural communication of Chinese universities and we open up a research avenue for an empirical study to explore the direct influence of the AI-mediated interaction on the communication achievements of Chinese learners.

Contemporary scholarship about technology-enhanced learning emphasizes that to account for outcomes, it is important to focus on the reasons why an effect occurs, and on the circumstances in which it is enhanced or hindered12. In the framework of the above-mentioned logic, AI-Mediated Learning Enjoyment can be seen as the mediator, which has been able to capture the affective mechanism by which AI-Mediated Interaction Utilization can lead to greater engagement and skill development, while empirical research has pointed out that the enjoyment related to a class online has been consistently shown to channel digital exposure into more substantial achievement and participation, especially in language and intercultural settings13,14. We have also found that positive emotions have important psychological properties: positive emotions widen attention, increase intrinsic motivation and promote exploratory behavior, which are all essential antecedents to practicing complex intercultural communication. At the same time, there are two moderators for the boundaries of this pathway.

First, Trust in AI Recommendations influences whether students actually follow the guidance and feedback generated by chatbots or writing agents; studies find that higher trust magnifies learning gains, whereas insufficient trust suppresses them, leading to superficial or skeptical tool use15. Second, Instructor AI Pedagogical Competence shapes the classroom micro-culture in which AI is deployed; teachers who can seamlessly integrate AI and model culturally responsive strategies substantially boost the effectiveness of digital interventions, whereas low competence can trigger confusion or reinforce bias16. These considerations are especially salient in Chinese higher education, where policy mandates accelerate AI adoption across curricula and international enrolments are rising sharply17. The success of this national push ultimately hinges on whether global learners experience sufficient enjoyment, trust the algorithmic advice they receive, and encounter instructors capable of scaffolding AI use toward intercultural aims. Economic analyses from the OECD further emphasize the stakes: AI fluency and cross-cultural competence now rank among the skills most strongly correlated with employability and national competitiveness18. By embedding AI-Mediated Learning Enjoyment as the psychological engine and juxtaposing Trust in AI Recommendations and Instructor AI Pedagogical Competence as contextual gatekeepers, our framework acknowledges the intertwined roles of emotion, attitude and instructional design in converting AI use into authentic Communication Competence in Intercultural Contexts.

As the world’s largest emerging digital market, China has become both a source and a magnet for mobile talent, now ranking third globally in inbound higher-education enrollment19. Government initiatives such as the Belt and Road Scholarship fully fund overseas learners, drawing cohorts from more than 200 countries each year. Chinese universities are responding by embedding AI across curricula, launching large-scale efforts, like DeepSeek-based courses, to build an innovation workforce20. Yet international students still report language barriers, limited intercultural support, and adaptation stress in local academic cultures21. Hybrid and AI-driven teaching models promise flexibility, but their payoff hinges on how effectively learners can deploy AI tools to bridge cultural and communicative gaps22. By examining AI-mediated interaction utilization as a pathway to enhanced communication competence, our study offers evidence-based strategies for turning ubiquitous classroom AI into practical intercultural gains. Such insights align regionally: Chinese degrees increasingly supply talent pipelines across Asia and Africa, so strengthening students’ cross-cultural skills bolsters both campus cohesion and wider Belt and Road labor markets.

So far, however, there is scant research analyzing the nexus of AI use and intercultural competence in the context of the close to half a million international students studying in China23. The current research fills this gap by exploring how and whether AI-mediated instructional usage can improve cross-cultural intercultural communication competence of foreign students in Chinese universities. Our research is based on broaden-and-build theory, according to which positive emotions expand the momentary mindset and accumulates lasting personal resources24. We suggest that positive AI-based learning experiences can expand the scope of student engagement and eventually develop their intercultural communication capacity. We have identified four gaps in the literature that are addressed by this study, as we outline them below, and we indicate our contributions.

To begin with, AI-mediated learning enjoyment is an empirical test that has not been conducted using previous research to establish a relationship between the use of AI tools and intercultural competence. Although the general literature on educational research proposes that positive emotional responses to learning might enhance student engagement25, the issue of whether enjoyment of AI-based learning can result in increased intercultural communication competence in a multicultural classroom is not clear. Second, the current literature on AI applications in education has concentrated on academic performance or uptake of technology but has not given much consideration to other socio-cultural results such as cross-cultural intercultural communication competence22. We are reacting to the recent appeals to investigate the role of AI beyond academic success, its promising applications to intercultural competence of students in a Chinese setting. Third, despite the fact that personal dispositions (e.g. openness) and context (e.g. teaching quality) are known to affect intercultural learning, this has not been included in models of AI-mediated learning. We present trust in AI recommendations and instructor AI pedagogical competence as mediating variables, since our study is based on the fact that the use of technology does not take place in a vacuum, and is influenced by the attitudes of students, as well as instructor expertise. Fourth, there is a dearth of studies that take place within the Chinese context of higher-education that study these dynamics. We have positioned our research in China to tackle the necessity to comprehend AI-enhanced learning in the cultural and institutional context of a nation that has spearheaded AI-in-education efforts.

In summary, this work makes several novel contributions. It is among the first to link AI-mediated instructional usage with intercultural communication competence outcomes for international students, a timely topic given China’s push for “Internationalization at Home.” We develop and test a dual moderated mediation model, grounded in broaden-and-build theory, to explain how enjoyment from AI-enhanced learning can broaden students’ intercultural skills and under what conditions this effect is amplified. The findings offer fresh theoretical insight by extending positive emotion theory into the intercultural education domain, and provide practical guidance for universities and policymakers on leveraging AI to foster global competence. Ultimately, our study sheds light on “what’s new” (AI as a tool for intercultural development) and “why it matters” (informing policy on AI integration and international education in China).

Literature and hypotheses

Broaden-and-Build theory of positive emotions

First articulated by Barbara Fredrickson, the broaden-and-build theory proposes that discrete positive emotions, joy, interest, contentment, and love temporarily broaden individuals’ thought-action repertoires and, over time, build durable cognitive, social, and psychological resources26. By widening attentional scope, positive affect sparks play, exploration, and creative problem solving, laying the groundwork for complex skills that persist beyond the emotional episode27. Within educational psychology, evidence shows that such emotional broadening increases cognitive flexibility and learning persistence, especially when digital tools invite curiosity and experimentation28. In our framework, AI-mediated learning enjoyment embodies this affective catalyst: when students experience genuine enjoyment while interacting with AI tutors or chatbots, their widened attentional bandwidth encourages deeper linguistic play, risk-taking, and reflection—behaviors essential for practicing nuanced, context-sensitive discourse.

Critically, the resources amassed through repeated positive encounters, knowledge, perspective-taking ability, and social confidence map directly onto communication competence in intercultural contexts. Studies of intercultural sensitivity confirm that positive emotions facilitate empathy, message adaptation, and the decoding of culturally novel cues. In Chinese universities, where AI applications increasingly mediate multilingual exchanges, positive affect becomes a lever for overcoming technostress and cultural adjustment barriers28. Thus, by framing AI-mediated learning enjoyment as the psychological engine that links AI-mediated interaction utilization to heightened intercultural communication competence, the broaden-and-build theory provides a coherent explanation of why technology use can translate into globally valued communicative skills.

AI-mediated interaction utilization and communication competence in intercultural contexts

Artificial intelligence is no longer a fringe experiment in campus IT; institutional leaders now map AI onto every layer of education management, from enrollment analytics to personalized student support, creating a taxonomy of administrative and pedagogical applications that drive strategic planning and budgeting decisions29. Surveys across North America, Europe, and Asia report that a majority of universities are embedding generative AI into teaching and assessment workflows, viewing it as a lever for scalable quality and learner retention30. Beyond operational efficiency, AI’s interactive features, chatbots, dialogue agents, and adaptive feedback systems offer continuous, low-stakes practice spaces that can model culturally nuanced discourse, thereby positioning AI as a catalyst for intercultural competence development31.

Empirical studies confirm that AI-facilitated dialogues improve students’ ability to decode cultural cues and negotiate meaning with peers from different backgrounds32. In Chinese classrooms, conversational bots have boosted international students’ engagement and willingness to communicate, signaling the technology’s potential to bridge linguistic and cultural divides33. From an education-management standpoint, such gains align with universities’ mandates to internationalize curricula and support diverse cohorts—objectives that hinge on scalable, technology-mediated interventions rather than labor-intensive tutoring models. Therefore, AI-mediated interaction utilization surfaces as a strategic input that can enhance communication competence in intercultural contexts, satisfying both managerial efficiency and learner development goals. The broaden-and-build theory provides a psychological lens on this link: frequent, enjoyable AI interactions widen students’ attentional scope, encouraging exploration of culturally varied expressions and gradually building durable intercultural skills. Synthesizing organizational imperatives with affective science yields our first hypothesis.

H1

AI-mediated interaction utilization is positively associated with greater communication competence in intercultural contexts among international students in Chinese higher education.

AI-mediated interaction utilization, AI-mediated learning enjoyment, and communication competence in intercultural contexts

Interactive AI tools, chatbots, adaptive feedback systems, and conversational agents, are consistently reported to boost students’ curiosity, playfulness, and overall learning enjoyment when embedded in coursework34. Global surveys further reveal that more than 90% of undergraduates now use generative AI, citing “fun” and “engagement” as leading reasons for continued adoption3. In Chinese EMI programs, ChatGPT pilots reduced language anxiety while heightening enjoyment, confirming that purposeful AI-mediated interaction utilization naturally cultivates AI-mediated learning enjoyment among international learners. Positive affect, in turn, has long been linked to superior intercultural outcomes: broaden-and-build theory posits that emotions like enjoyment widen attentional scope and foster exploratory communication behaviors35. Drawing on broaden-and-build theory, we posit that positive emotional experiences during learning, such as enjoyment derived from AI-mediated activities, can broaden students’ cognitive and social engagement, thereby building resources like intercultural communication competence24. Enjoyment is a key positive emotion that expands one’s willingness to explore, create, and interact. In an intercultural learning context, students who enjoy AI-assisted learning may be more open and attentive to cultural content and communication practice, leading to improved competence. Prior research in language and intercultural communication supports this connection: for example, students who report higher classroom enjoyment are more willing to communicate in a second language and engage with peers from other cultures36.

Meta-analyses of virtual exchanges show that students who enjoy technology-rich tasks demonstrate stronger perspective-taking and adaptive messaging skills across cultures35. A recent systematic review of AI learning environments echoes these findings, associating elevated enjoyment with measurable gains in intercultural communication competence37. Chinese university studies corroborate that enjoyment during AI-facilitated dialogues predicts higher self-assessed competence in multicultural groups. Thus, we expect that AI usage that enhances learning enjoyment will indirectly promote Intercultural Competence by motivating students to broaden their participation in intercultural interactions.

Sequential-mediation models suggest that enjoyment often serves as the psychological engine translating technology use into performance gains38. Yet no published work has tested whether AI-mediated learning enjoyment channels the effect of AI-mediated interaction utilization on communication competence in intercultural contexts for international students in China, where AI adoption and cross-border enrollment are both surging. We focus on AI-mediated learning enjoyment as the central mediating mechanism, given its strong theoretical grounding. Enjoyment captures an affective state directly addressed by broaden-and-build theory and is immediately relevant to learning interactions with AI. While other affective or motivational factors (e.g., curiosity, self-efficacy) could conceivably form additional links in the chain, our model isolates enjoyment to delineate its unique contribution. This parsimonious approach highlights the affective pathway; however, we acknowledge that in reality, multiple processes may operate in tandem. We return to this point in the limitations, noting that future research could explore sequential mediators beyond enjoyment. By embedding enjoyment as a mediator, our study closes this gap and operationalizes the full broaden-and-build mechanism: AI use (utilization) evokes positive emotion (enjoyment), which broadens attention and builds enduring intercultural communication resources.

H2

AI-mediated interaction utilization is positively associated with AI-mediated learning enjoyment.

H3

AI-mediated learning enjoyment is positively associated with communication competence in intercultural contexts.

H4

AI-mediated learning enjoyment mediates the positive relationship between AI-mediated interaction utilization and communication competence in intercultural contexts.

Moderating roles of trust in AI recommendations and instructor AI pedagogical competence

Trust remains a decisive filter through which learners assess algorithmic feedback, influencing both their emotional response and subsequent learning behaviors15. Cross-national surveys show that high-trust students engage more deeply with AI-powered study aids and report stronger positive emotions, whereas low-trust peers dismiss or minimally consult the same recommendations39. Experimental studies in college settings confirm that trust not only boosts immediate AI usage but also moderates the indirect effect of AI use on positive emotion, yielding a significant moderated-mediation pattern40. Similar dynamics appear in language learning, where belief in chatbot credibility strengthens the path from conversational practice to enjoyment gains. In Chinese higher education, perceived reliability of machine feedback predicts whether international students integrate AI prompts into daily communication drills, thereby shaping the affective payoff of those drills22. Taken together, this evidence implies that strong trust in AI recommendations amplifies the positive link between AI-mediated interaction utilization and AI-mediated learning enjoyment, and by extension, heightens the enjoyment-driven boost to communication competence in intercultural contexts.

All students do not have the same pleasure in using AI tools - a key contingency is how much they trust such AI suggestions. Trust in AI is confidence of the student that the suggestions or feedback provided by the AI is right and helpful. Our hypothesis is that trust will moderate the impact of AI usage on enjoyment. High trust would make students less anxious to follow AI guidance and make the usage of this tool more engaging learning. In case of low trust, students might treat AI recommendations with distrust or mistrust, which diminishes the enjoyment. It should also be noted that trust and reliance are different: trust is an attitude towards the credibility and good intentions of the AI, and reliance is an action of accepting AI recommendations. According to research, reliance has less strong connotations than trust, and trust is a prerequisite that leads to proper reliance on AI systems41. In our research, we focus on trust to measure a general trust attitude (e.g., confidence in and readiness to follow AI recommendations). We consider trust to be a unidimensional construct that described the perception of users regarding the credibility of AI. We anticipate higher AI-mediated interaction utilization-AI-mediated learning enjoyment gains among those students that believe in the AI suggestions because they might have more positive engagements with the AI inputs.

Instructor capacity constitutes a second contextual gatekeeper. Studies demonstrate that teachers’ digital competence moderates the relationship between technology adoption and student engagement, with high-competence instructors translating tool features into clearer learning benefits42. Systematic reviews of AI integration highlight that instructors who master intelligent TPACK frameworks create richer, affect-laden learning experiences than those who simply plug tools into existing routines43. Evidence from online courses during COVID-19 shows that teacher competence intensifies the association between instructional technology and perceived learning outcomes through heightened student motivation, a moderated-mediation effect44. Additional research links teachers digital skills to students’ willingness to experiment with AI and the enjoyment they derive from such experimentation16. Early trials in Chinese universities reveal that faculty trained in AI-supported intercultural pedagogy can double international students’ interaction frequency and self-rated competence compared with untrained counterparts44. These findings suggest that instructor AI pedagogical competence strengthens both the utilization-to-enjoyment path and the downstream effect of enjoyment on intercultural communication skills. Consistent with broaden-and-build theory, high trust and high instructor competence create an affect-supportive climate that enlarges the cognitive broadening triggered by enjoyable AI interactions, thereby magnifying the indirect effect of AI-mediated interaction utilization on communication competence.

We further propose that the broader learning environment, particularly the instructor’s ability to integrate AI into teaching – will shape outcomes. Instructor AI pedagogical competence refers to the instructor’s skill in using AI tools to augment instruction (e.g., designing AI-supported activities, addressing AI-related queries). An instructor proficient in AI can create more effective and enjoyable AI-enhanced learning experiences, and help students translate those experiences into skill gains. We hypothesize that Instructor AI Pedagogical Competence moderates the link between AI-mediated learning enjoyment and intercultural communication, i.e. a second-stage moderation. When instructors are highly competent with educational AI, a student’s enjoyment is more likely to convert into meaningful intercultural learning (for example, through well-crafted assignments that encourage cultural exchange via AI platforms). Conversely, if instructor competence is low, even if a student enjoys using AI, the learning may not be structured in a way that fosters intercultural growth. In essence, a capable instructor acts as a catalyst that amplifies the benefits of AI-facilitated enjoyment on building intercultural competence. This moderator reflects China’s educational context, where teachers are traditionally viewed as key facilitators of student learning; when they leverage AI effectively, student outcomes should improve45.

Combining these elements, our model is a dual-moderated mediation. We predict that AI-mediated interaction utilization has an indirect effect on intercultural communication via AI-mediated learning enjoyment and that this indirect effect is contingent on trust in AI recommendations and instructor AI pedagogical competence. Specifically, we posits a positive moderation of the AI-mediated interaction utilization → AI-mediated learning enjoyment path by trust (first-stage moderation), and we further posits a positive moderation of the AI-mediated learning enjoyment → intercultural communication path by instructor AI pedagogical competence (second-stage moderation). Finally, we state that the mediated effect of AI-mediated interaction utilization on communication competence in intercultural contexts (through AI-mediated interaction utilization) is stronger when both trust in AI and instructor competence are high, and weaker (or non-significant) when they are low. This comprehensive model allows us to examine not only whether AI usage can build intercultural competence, but under what conditions this process is most effective. Therefore:

H5

The positive indirect effect of AI-mediated interaction utilization on communication competence in intercultural contexts, through AI-mediated learning enjoyment, is stronger when trust in AI recommendations is high rather than low.

H6

The positive indirect effect of AI-mediated interaction utilization on communication competence in intercultural contexts, through AI-mediated learning enjoyment, is stronger when instructor AI pedagogical competence is high rather than low.

Figure 1 (conceptual model) represents the hypotheses of our research in accordance with broaden and build theory. We assume that there exists a mediation process in which AI-mediated instructional use enhances AI-mediated learning enjoyment, which subsequently leads to greater cross-cultural intercultural communication competence. This sequence indicates dynamics of broaden-and-build: first positive experiences (enjoyment) broaden thought-action repertoires of students, which results in the formation of long-term abilities and resource (intercultural competence). Our conceptualization of cross-cultural intercultural communication competence is the ability of the student to successfully communicate and adjust in Chinese intercultural contexts, which is a vital outcome to international students. When we base our model on theory, all the constructs map onto to broaden-and-build component (e.g., AI-mediated instructional usage is the new stimulus, AI-mediated learning enjoyment is the positive emotion, cross-cultural intercultural communication competence is the acquired resource).

Methods

Participants and data-collection procedure

Data were gathered from international students enrolled at universities in Wuhan, Xi’an, and Hangzhou, three innovation-intensive cities that collectively host more than 10,000 degree-seeking foreign learners and represent China’s central, western, and eastern development corridors, respectively. Wuhan’s “Optics Valley” anchors national AI R&D; Hangzhou, home to Alibaba Cloud, ranks among China’s top digital-economy hubs; Xi’an, a Silk Road gateway, is a strategic node for the Belt and Road education initiative. The sample was 427 international students attending universities. All participants had at least one AI-assisted course (e.g. a class where AI tools like intelligent tutoring systems or AI-driven translation apps were used) in the past year. We used local coordinators to administer paper-based questionnaires during breaks and study classes. Additionally, the use of paper questionnaires was chosen to encourage the highest participation (practical, given some Chinese universities’ online settings may result in a high response rate from in-person recruitment46,47. Each respondent gave informed consent and was guaranteed anonymity. We verified the data for completeness, i.e., less than 2% of the responses contained any missing values. Given this low rate, we addressed missing data by listwise deletion (that is, removing cases with high amounts of missing data) resulting in an effective sample size of N = 421 (final) for analysis. Requiring previous experience with AI course ensured respondents could make meaningful reports about AI use and enjoyment, however, we recognize this criterion may bias the sample towards students more tech-engaged. We point out this as a limitation (potentially inflating mean AI usage/enjoyment), but it was necessary to identify relevant participants. In line with institutional review board approval at the lead author’s university, participation was voluntary, anonymity was assured, and no identifying information was recorded. Data were encrypted and stored on password-protected drives.

Approval for human experiments

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Guizhou University of Engineering Science (Ref. no. GUES/EDU/EA/2024/0926 − 47; approval date: September 26, 2024), and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Sample-size estimation

An a priori power analysis for a first-stage moderated-mediation model (PROCESS Model 14) was conducted in G*Power 3.1 with α = 0.05, power = 0.80, and a medium effect size (f² = 0.15); the calculation indicated a minimum of ≈ 380 participants. Because online or classroom surveys of mobile international students routinely achieve 20–30% response rates, we distributed 650 questionnaires. A total of 469 were returned (72.2%); after excluding cases with > 10% missing data or uniform (“straight-line”) answers across all Likert items, 427 responses remained for analysis. The final sample exceeds rules-of-thumb for structural equation modeling that recommend 10 participants per estimated parameter or a minimum N = 20048.

Measures

All constructs were assessed with established multi-item instruments on a five-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree). AI-mediated interaction utilization was measured with four items adapted from Stöhr, Ou49. A representative statement is “Using AI tools improves my intercultural communication skills.” AI-mediated learning enjoyment employed the 21-item Foreign Language Enjoyment scale50, reworded for AI contexts (e.g., “Using AI in class is fun”). Communication competence in intercultural contexts relies on six items taken from Liu, Ma51, including items such as “I can successfully converse with peers from diverse Chinese cultural backgrounds.” Trust in AI recommendations consisted of six items based on the work of Amoozadeh, Daniels52 ; a sample item reads “I rely on the AI’s recommendations even if I’m not absolutely certain they’re correct.” Finally, the instructor’s AI pedagogical competence was captured with five items based Hanaysha, Shriedeh53, including “My instructor highlights key intercultural strategies during AI-enhanced sessions.” Cronbach’s alpha values from prior studies exceed 0.70 for each scale, and reliabilities were re-evaluated with the current sample.

AI-mediated interaction utilization was adapted from prior work (four items). No item was removed from this scale. AI-mediated learning enjoyment retained all five items from the original scale. Intercultural communication competence was measured with six items. We did not remove any item from this scale. Trust in AI recommendations originally included five items, and we did not remove any item. Instructor AI pedagogical competence was measured with five items. AI-mediated learning enjoyment originally employed the 21-item. However, we deleted three items due to poor factor loading and used 18 items in our final measurement model for this construct.

Pilot testing and expert review

The English questionnaire was forward-translated into simplified Chinese, back-translated, and piloted with 30 international students; feedback led to minor wording adjustments54,55. Three experts in AI-supported language education evaluated content validity, confirming item relevance and clarity.

Common-method bias control

Procedural remedies included anonymity, randomized item order, and psychological separation of predictors and outcomes56,57. Statistically, Harman’s single-factor test showed the first factor accounted for 26% of variance, below the 50% threshold, and a latent marker-variable technique produced non-significant CMV estimates.

Ethical considerations

The study followed APA ethical standards. Participants read an informed-consent statement, were free to withdraw, and received no compensation58,59. Data handling complied with Chinese Ministry of Education guidelines for foreign-student research and GDPR-aligned storage protocols. Collectively, the sampling design, rigorous measurement, and bias-control steps ensure that subsequent analyses of moderated-mediation effects rest on robust and ethically collected evidence.

Measurement model and structural model assessment

All factor loadings exceeded 0.70 (p < .001), indicating strong convergent validity. Composite reliabilities ranged from 0.88 to 0.93, and average variance extracted values were above the 0.50 threshold. Discriminant validity was confirmed using the Heterotrait–Monotrait ratio (HTMT), with all values below 0.85. The measurement model exhibited good fit (SRMR = 0.043, NFI = 0.93, RMS_θ = 0.106), and a one-factor model fit significantly worse than the five-factor model. In the structural model, AMIU had a direct positive effect on CCIC (β = 0.29, p < .001) and an indirect effect via AMLE (β = 0.05, 5% CI [0.02, 0.08]). The interactions AMIU × TAIR and AMIU × IAPC were significant (β = 0.11 and 0.08, respectively), indicating that high trust in AI and instructor competence strengthen the AMIU → AMLE path. Model explanatory power was moderate (R²_AMLE = 0.28; R²_CCIC = 0.35), and Q² values indicated predictive relevance.

Results

Data were analyzed with SmartPLS (version 3.28), a graphical software designed for partial least-squares structural-equation modeling (PLS-SEM). PLS-SEM is preferred when the model is predictive, contains second-order or higher-order paths, and uses latent variables measured with many indicators60–62. These circumstances mirror the present study’s dual-moderated mediation framework. SmartPLS accommodates complex models with modest distributional assumptions and performs bootstrapping to generate robust standard errors, features that outperform covariance-based packages when sample sizes are in the 200–500 range. Moreover, its assessment panels automate reliability, convergent, and discriminant validity diagnostics recommended by Hair, Hult63, making it especially suitable for our reflective measurement model.

Discussion

Our data show that AI-mediated interaction utilization exerts a direct, medium-sized positive effect on communication competence in intercultural contexts. This aligns with recent classroom interventions in which extended chatbot dialogues increased students’ sensitivity to culturally nuanced discourse patterns30. Prior studies typically examined short tasks or single-culture cohorts; by capturing semester-long engagement among a heterogeneous sample of international learners in China, our work broadens the geographical and demographic scope of AI-supported intercultural research. The finding is especially salient given that more than 90% of undergraduates worldwide now report using generative AI, a surge documented in consecutive higher-education surveys. Second, AI-mediated interaction utilization significantly predicts AI-mediated learning enjoyment, replicating evidence that interactive algorithms transform routine study into intrinsically rewarding experiences35. In our model, AI-mediated learning enjoyment also enhances communication competence in intercultural contexts, confirming virtual-exchange meta-analyses that link positive affect to perspective-taking, adaptive messaging, and mutual understanding across cultures. The indirect path connecting utilization to competence through enjoyment validates the psychological engine suggested, but rarely tested, by language-education scholars72, and it does so in a large-sample PLS-SEM design, an analytic approach recommended when models emphasize prediction with multiple latent constructs73.

Third, the magnitude of this indirect effect depends on two contextual levers. High trust in AI recommendations amplifies the utilization → enjoyment → competence chain, echoing experiments showing that credibility perceptions moderate both affective and behavioral responses to AI tutors73. Equally important, the pathway is strengthened under high instructor AI pedagogical competence, reinforcing reviews that identify digitally proficient teachers as catalysts for technology-driven intercultural learning. Notably, the moderating influence of instructor competence is larger than that of trust, suggesting that skilled pedagogical design can partially offset student skepticism, a critical insight for campuses negotiating AI governance and academic-integrity debates. Finally, the model explains 34.7% of the variance in intercultural competence, surpassing many earlier technology-enhanced language-learning studies and underscoring the practical leverage of the combined variables. Together, the results position China’s AI-rich classrooms as fertile ground for testing nuanced psychological and contextual dynamics that have been theorized but seldom integrated within a single predictive framework.

On a further note, guided by the broaden-and-build theory of positive emotions, our findings illustrate a full causal chain: engaging AI tools triggers enjoyment, which widens learners’ communicative repertoire, and the enlarged repertoire builds lasting intercultural competence. Trust in the technology and instructors’ AI pedagogical competence act as climate factors that intensify or dampen this broadening process, confirming that emotions flourish when attitudes and instructional scaffolding align. In sum, the study demonstrates how strategically orchestrated AI interactions can convert algorithms into affinity, equipping global learners with the communicative agility demanded by an interconnected, AI-driven era.

Theoretical implications

Our findings show a clear path through which AI in education can promote intercultural development and contributes to both the field of technology in education literature and intercultural communication theory. We demonstrated that AI mediated usage of the instruction indirectly contributes to intercultural communication competence through increased enjoyment of learning, and this is consistent with Fredrickson’s broaden-and-build theory24. This study is one of the first to empirically validate that positive emotions in an AI-supported learning context can “broaden” the engagement of students in ways that “build” durable intercultural skills. The result showing that enjoyment fully mediated the link between AI usage and intercultural communication suggests that it is in fact the affective experience - the joy and interest triggered by AI, that drives the growth of competence, not AI usage per se. This highlights the often underestimated role of affect in intercultural adaptation: having a fun experience with new tools such as AI may help to reduce anxiety, boost openness, and facilitate more interaction with host culture content and peers. Our work extends intercultural communication models by identifying technology-enabled enjoyment as a facilitator of competence development. We add to this prior research on international students by pointing out the role of positive affect and curiosity in fostering intercultural adjustment36, and specifically how ITs can be used to induce these positive states in order to accelerate the learning of cultures.

Importantly, our results from a dual-moderation study provide insights into when and for whom AI mediated learning is most beneficial. Trust in AI recommendations turned out to be a critical personal factor. This finding rings true with theories about technology acceptance, if students perceive the AI to be credible and helpful, they are more likely to engage deeply & reap emotional rewards. Conversely, distrust may result in the shallow or cautious use, blunting the potential enjoyment and learning gains. In practical terms, increasing students’ trust in educational AI (e.g., through increasing AI transparency or teaching students how to effectively use AI) could magnify the positive outcomes. Meanwhile instructor AI pedagogical competence proved to be an important context factor. This corresponds to the idea that in the educational culture of China, the role of the teacher in the learning experiences of the students is very influential. When instructors are knowledgeable about AI integration, they can set up a structured but dynamic learning environment in which AI tools actively support intercultural discussions, simulations, or learning tasks based on problems. Our results are consistent with the larger literature that indicates that effective teacher use of technology can improve student performance and engagement45. We add a new contribution in that even this applies to intercultural skills outcomes: a competent instructor can bridge the gap between AI technology and intercultural content in ways that seriously enhance student competence gains. Overall, these findings on moderation help address calls for more nuanced looks at AI in education - moving beyond “does AI help learning?” to “under what conditions and how?“. The finding of significant moderated mediation means that theories of learning supported by AI need to consider issues of user trust and pedagogical implementation to fully explain the results.

The results should be interpreted in the context of the Chinese study. China has quickly emerged as a global center for AI innovation and international education. On the one hand, governmental policies (e.g., the Next Generation AI Development Plan) are driving universities toward the use of AI tools, while on the other hand, China has a large and diverse international student population5. This cluster of priorities provides a fertile ground for AI-mediated intercultural learning. “Our findings indicate that AI tools - including intelligent tutoring systems, translation apps or AI-driven discussion platforms - have the potential to contribute positively to mitigating some of the well-known challenges international students in China overcome.” For example, foreign students often face Chinese language barriers or classroom culture, which makes the support of interactive AI technology more interesting and inclusive (translating, explaining) in real-time. When students are happy rather than fearful in the classroom, they are likely to socialize more with local peers and teachers which speeds the process of learning the language and understanding of the culture. In essence, AI can represent a cultural interconnect: by increasing the pleasure and sense of security, it helps students to break out of their comfort zone and practice communication in the host culture. This trend is in line with China’s “Internationalization at Home” strategy, which aims to integrate international students and to enhance the intercultural experiences of domestic students. Our discussion also acknowledges the impact of teaching practices in China, for example, respect for teacher authority and the emphasis on teacher-led learning. Our findings are intuitive, given the high perceived control of instructors in Chinese classrooms, to find that instructor-level AI competency has a significant impact on outcomes for our sample. Therefore, if the instructor incorporates AI well into group assignments or cross-cultural exchanges, students will respond accordingly by becoming involved more deeply, taking pleasure in the process, and ultimately enhancing their intercultural competency. Conversely, if the use of AI is haphazard or the instructor is disengaged, then students may perceive the AI as an insignificant add-on and reap less value. These subtleties emphasize the reality that the influence of technology in education is culturally mediated. In conclusion, our study validates that China’s emphasis on AI, even if complemented by the increase in education, can come to fruition in terms of development of intercultural competence, but the human factor (trusting students, competent teachers) is still key to unlocking this potential.

Practical implications

The findings from this study have timely implications for universities and policy makers, particularly in China and other similar settings. First, it’s important to build student trust in AI. Institutions may include orientations or workshops on AI tools at the beginning of the courses where students learn the functionality, accuracy, and best practices of the tools. By making AI recommendations more transparent and understandable, students may feel more comfortable and trusting of AI recommendations. For instance, a university could conduct a one-week “AI literacy boot camp” for incoming international students to demystify the popular AI learning platforms (explain what the AI can and cannot do). This can help to overcome skepticism and showcase success stories of AI-assisted learning, which can help to build trust. Second, you need to invest in training instructors on how to integrate AI. Our findings indicate that even the most sophisticated of AI tools will be of limited educational impact if teachers are not capable of using them pedagogically. Universities could create faculty development programs to teach AI pedagogy - similar to how teaching centers create programs to teach online pedagogy. Facing the government policy, China’s universities are already taking steps in this direction: for example, some universities have started special courses and summer schools devoted to AI for students and teachers. One such example is the International Program on Machine Learning at Peking University, which provides an opportunity for foreign students and domestic faculty to learn and practice AI together and use it to study research projects. Such programs portray an emerging understanding that technical and cultural skills can be learnt together. We invite further collaborations between international offices, computer science departments and language/culture centers in order to develop AI-enabled intercultural learning activities.

From the policy point of view, our study strengthens the argument that AI in education should not be pursued only for better academic results, but also for global citizenship development. Governments and educational bodies in China and other regions could incorporate intercultural competence indicators in their assessment of the success of AI integration programs. For example, along with exam scores or job placement rates, universities could measure growth in students’ self-reported intercultural skills or abilities working in cross-cultural teams after using AI-based curricula. Guidelines on education informatization (education informatization 2.0) recently released by the Chinese Ministry of Education could be expanded to explicitly include intercultural outcomes as an objective. Additionally, universities hosting international students can use our findings to justify providing AI-enabled resources for intercultural support, such as AI translators or chatbots that help bridge language gaps in campus life. Some Chinese universities have even started deploying AI chatbots for student counseling and cross-cultural communication practice. However, it’s crucial to monitor and ensure these tools are used in a way that genuinely engages students (producing enjoyment, not frustration). In sum, the big takeaway for institutional leaders is that AI is not just a tech trend, it can be a strategic asset in international education. By deliberately cultivating positive, trust-rich AI learning environments, universities can accelerate intercultural learning, helping foreign students integrate more smoothly and preparing all students to thrive in a technologically advanced, culturally diverse world.

Limitations and future research

While our model was confirmed to be supported, other factors could explain the observed relationships. One potential explanation is reverse causality; students who are already more interculturally competent or motivated may be more likely to use and enjoy AI. In this case, high cross-cultural communication may be a cause, not an effect. We attempted to ameliorate this with a course-based usage (to ensure that all students are exposed), but this is not able to eliminate pre-existing differences. Similarly, previous AI or language experience may have confounding effects: a tech-savvy student may be using AI successfully while adapting better interculturally - while native Chinese language ability could facilitate engagement with AI. We did not include in-depth prior AI experience, and did not include Chinese proficiency or academic major as controls. Self-rated Chinese proficiency is less susceptible to changes in key paths, but future studies should use objective measures or stratified sampling. We also have a cross-sectional design, so endogeneity is an issue. Studies that longitudinally measure cross cultural communication growth while accounting for the amount of AI use, or that use experimental interventions to actively change the amount of exposure to AI, or that use instrumental variable strategies (e.g., variation in campus IT infrastructure) may better identify causality.

Another line of research is the consideration of boundary conditions. Disciplines can vary in their susceptibility: AI tools are likely to have less impact in less culturally embedded fields (e.g., mathematics, engineering) than in social sciences and humanities. Institutional resources may also moderate outcomes - low-resource universities might have smaller effects than higher resource ones with strong AI support, for example. Cultural distance also may be at play: AI has the potential to benefit students with culturally distant backgrounds more. In future work, measurement invariance across groups (e.g. male vs. female, high vs. low Chinese proficiency) also needs to be established through methods such as MICOM in PLS-SEM to ensure that the observed differences are not caused by measurement bias. Such refinements will increase generalizability and ensure that findings can be generalized to varied settings.

Other limitations must be noted. First, all data were self-reported at a single time point, raising concerns about common method bias and subjectivity. Although we used anonymity, established scales, and Harman’s test (which indicated no dominant factor), future research should incorporate peer or instructor ratings or task-based measures of intercultural competence. Second, our requirement that participants had taken AI-assisted courses may bias results, limiting generalizability. Broader samples, including students unfamiliar with AI, should be tested to examine whether effects require a baseline of tech readiness. Third, while the sample was diverse, subgroup analyses (e.g., by region of origin) were not performed, which could reveal important cultural differences in AI acceptance and communication styles. Finally, the fast pace of AI development means our findings, rooted in the 2024–2025 university context, may quickly become outdated as new generative tools reshape engagement. Ongoing research is needed to keep conclusions relevant.

Conclusion

This study shows that AI-mediated interaction utilization directly and indirectly, through AI-mediated learning enjoyment, enhances communication competence in intercultural contexts among international students in China. High trust in AI recommendations and strong instructor AI pedagogical competence intensify this enjoyment-driven mechanism, underscoring the joint importance of user attitudes and pedagogical design. The findings extend the broaden-and-build theory of positive emotions beyond traditional classrooms, illustrating how positive affect triggered by AI broadens communicative repertoires and builds enduring intercultural skills. As Chinese universities accelerate AI adoption to meet national innovation targets, strategically cultivating enjoyment, trust, and faculty expertise can convert algorithms into genuine intercultural affinity, a priority echoed by global surveys that rank cross-cultural competence and AI literacy among the top graduate capabilities.
