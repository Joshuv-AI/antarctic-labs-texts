Verlinde rings and cluster algebras arising from quantum affine algebras
Chul-hee Lee, Jian-Rong Li, Euiyong Park

Abstract
. We formulate a positivity conjecture relating the Verlinde ring associated with an untwisted affine Lie algebra at a positive integer level and a subcategory of finite-dimensional representations over the corresponding quantum affine algebra with a cluster algebra structure. Specifically, we consider a ring homomorphism from the Grothendieck ring of this representation category to the Verlinde ring and conjecture that every object in the category has a positive image under this map. We prove this conjecture in certain cases where the underlying simple Lie algebra is simply-laced with level 2 or of type A 1 A_{1} at an arbitrary level. The proof employs the close connection between this category and cluster algebras of finite cluster type. As further evidence for the conjecture, we show that for any level, all objects have positive quantum dimensions under the assumption that some Kirillov-Reshetikhin modules have positive quantum dimensions.



1.
Introduction

The dilogarithm function is defined as

\operatorname{Li}_{2}(z)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{z^{n}}{n^{2}},\quad 0\leq z\leq 1.

Though seemingly simple, it is remarkable that the dilogarithm plays a significant role in various fields, including algebraic
K
-theory, number theory, mathematical physics, and hyperbolic geometry.
A
dilogarithm identity
takes the form

(1.1)

\sum_{j\in J}L(x_{j})=\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}c,

where
L(z)
is the Rogers dilogarithm function, given by

L(z)=\operatorname{Li}_{2}(z)+\frac{1}{2}\log(z)\log(1-z),

(x_{j})_{j\in J}
are real algebraic numbers satisfying
0\leq x_{j}\leq 1
, and
c
is a rational number;
see, for example,
[
28
,
38
]
for a more detailed discussion.

Dilogarithm identities play a crucial role in the study of certain integrable statistical models that are believed to be connected to conformal field theories (CFTs). In CFTs, the central charge of a model is a fundamental parameter that reflects the algebraic structure of the theory. By employing the Thermodynamic Bethe Ansatz (TBA) approach, physicists can link quantities from the statistical models to quantities from the related CFTs; see
[
39
]
for example.
This often leads to the effective central charge
c
being computed as a sum of dilogarithms evaluated at certain algebraic numbers, as in (
1.1
).
While providing a rigorous mathematical justification for the TBA approach is challenging, these dilogarithm identities offer robust consistency checks, thereby supporting the framework’s validity.

Building on studies inspired by the TBA framework, Kirillov formulated a family of conjectural dilogarithm identities associated with a simple Lie algebra
{{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}}
over
{\mathbb{C}}
, and an integer
\mathsf{k}\geq 2

[
27
]
. The positive real arguments of the dilogarithm identity are given as a solution to a certain system of algebraic equations.
Beyond the dilogarithm identity itself, an interesting aspect of this construction is the fact that these positive arguments could be also derived from the conjecturally positive numbers
\mathcal{D}^{(i)}_{m}
, obtained by evaluating the characters
Q^{(i)}_{m}
of certain finite-dimensional representations
\operatorname{res}W^{(i)}_{m}
of
{{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}}
, indexed by
(i,m)
, where
i
corresponds to a vertex in the Dynkin diagram of
{{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}}
, and
m=0,1,\ldots,t_{i}\mathsf{k}
:

\mathcal{D}^{(i)}_{m}:=Q^{(i)}_{m}\left(\frac{\rho}{\mathsf{k}+h^{\vee}}\right).

Here,
\rho
is the Weyl vector, and
h^{\vee}
is the dual Coxeter number.
The value
t_{i}\in\{1,2,3\}
is the ratio of the norm of the highest root to the norm of the simple root
\alpha_{i}
.
The number obtained by specializing the character of a finite-dimensional
{{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}}
-module
V
as above is also known as its quantum dimension at level
\mathsf{k}
, as these values approach the actual dimension of
V
as
\mathsf{k}\to\infty
.
This quantum dimension is a real number, though it is not necessarily positive.

The development of cluster algebras
[
8
]
led to the eventual proof of the conjectural dilogarithm identity for all types
[
36
,
3
,
20
,
21
]
.
Despite these advancements, expressing the positive arguments in the dilogarithm identity in terms of the character evaluations persisted as an open challenge.
In the meantime, building on Kirillov’s work, Kuniba, Nakanishi, and Suzuki explored this problem, and introduced additional conjectures that extended his original formulation
[
30
,
31
,
32
,
33
]
.

In
[
34
]
, the problem was reformulated as one concerning the image of the isomorphism classes of Kirillov-Reshetikhin (KR) modules
W^{(i)}_{m}
over the quantum affine algebra
U_{q}^{\prime}(\widehat{{\mathfrak{g}}}_{0})
in the Verlinde ring
K_{0}(\operatorname{Rep}(\widehat{{\mathfrak{g}}}_{0}^{\prime},\mathsf{k}))
associated with the affine Lie algebra
\widehat{{\mathfrak{g}}}_{0}
and
\mathsf{k}
, under a certain ring homomorphism

\varphi_{\mathsf{k}}:K_{0}(\operatorname{Rep}{U_{q}^{\prime}(\widehat{{\mathfrak{g}}}_{0})})\to K_{0}(\operatorname{Rep}(\widehat{{\mathfrak{g}}}_{0}^{\prime},\mathsf{k})).

See (
4.1
) for its description.
The Verlinde ring
K_{0}(\operatorname{Rep}(\widehat{{\mathfrak{g}}}_{0}^{\prime},\mathsf{k}))
is a
{\mathbb{Z}}
-ring with a distinguished basis, allowing for a well-defined notion of positivity; see Definition
2.5
.
In particular, an important question was whether
\varphi_{\mathsf{k}}([W^{(i)}_{m}])
is positive for
0\leq m\leq t_{i}\mathsf{k}
.
This formulation has made it possible to prove the conjectures proposed by Kirillov and further developed by Kuniba, Nakanishi, and Suzuki for all classical types.

Although the above formulation has resolved some long-standing questions in certain cases, the new challenge of determining the image of all KR modules in
K_{0}(\operatorname{Rep}(\widehat{{\mathfrak{g}}}_{0}^{\prime},\mathsf{k}))
under the map

remains an open problem; see Conjecture
4.1
and related references for a more precise and general statement.
Especially, Conjecture
4.1
introduces an intriguing positivity question concerning
K_{0}(\operatorname{Rep}{U_{q}^{\prime}(\widehat{{\mathfrak{g}}}_{0})})
and
K_{0}(\operatorname{Rep}(\widehat{{\mathfrak{g}}}_{0}^{\prime},\mathsf{k}))
, leading to the following natural questions:

•

First, is there a natural subring or subset of
K_{0}(\operatorname{Rep}{U_{q}^{\prime}(\widehat{{\mathfrak{g}}}_{0})})
where the map
\varphi_{\mathsf{k}}
consistently produces a positive image? In general,
\varphi_{\mathsf{k}}([V])
may be zero or even negative for an object

in
\operatorname{Rep}{U_{q}^{\prime}(\widehat{{\mathfrak{g}}}_{0})}
.

•

Second, does this issue have a more direct connection to cluster algebras? Although the original dilogarithm identity is strongly linked to cluster algebras, particularly through the
Y
-systems
[
39
,
10
,
26
]
, it has remained an open problem to place Conjecture
4.1
within the broader context of cluster algebras.

Meanwhile, in their work on the monoidal categorification of cluster algebras, Hernandez and Leclerc introduced certain monoidal subcategories
\mathscr{C}_{\ell}
of
\operatorname{Rep}{U_{q}^{\prime}(\widehat{{\mathfrak{g}}}_{0})}
, parametrized by positive integers
\ell

[
16
,
17
]
.
They showed that the Grothendieck ring of each of these subcategories has a cluster algebra structure.
In particular,
\mathscr{C}_{1}
provides a monoidal categorification of a cluster algebra of finite cluster type, whose classification is given in
[

]
.

In response to the questions above, we propose the following conjecture in this paper:

Conjecture

(Conjecture
4.4
)
.

Let
\mathsf{k}\geqslant 2
be a positive integer,
\ell(\mathsf{k})=\mathsf{k}-1
and let
\varphi_{\mathsf{k}}:K_{0}(\mathscr{C}_{\ell(\mathsf{k})})\to K_{0}(\operatorname{Rep}(\widehat{{\mathfrak{g}}}_{0}^{\prime},\mathsf{k}))

on

For every

in
\mathscr{C}_{\ell(\mathsf{k})}

For a precise formulation of the conjecture, we make a slight adjustment of the definition of

; see Section

and Remark

.

Main results.

The main results of this paper are as follows:
We prove that if the KR modules in the initial seed of
K_{0}(\mathscr{C}_{\ell(\mathsf{k})})
have positive quantum dimensions at level
\mathsf{k}
, then all objects in
\mathscr{C}_{\ell(\mathsf{k})}
also have positive quantum dimensions at level
\mathsf{k}
; see Theorem
4.6
.
This is consistent with a quantum dimension version of our conjecture, and provides a strong evidence to our main conjecture.
We also establish our main conjecture, the Verlinde positivity, in certain special cases, specifically for

\mathscr{C}_{\ell}
when the underlying simple Lie algebra
{{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}}
is of type
A_{1}
, and for
\mathscr{C}_{1}
when
{{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}}
is of type
A_{n}
or one of the exceptional types
E_{6}
,
E_{7}
, or
E_{8}
for certain choices of height functions:
see Theorem
5.1
.
In addition, we provide a detailed conjectural description for type
D_{n}
.

This paper is organized as follows.
In Section
2
, we review the basic concepts of cluster algebras, Kac-Moody algebras, and the quantum Kac-Moody algebras. We also provide a brief introduction to the Verlinde ring associated with an affine Lie algebra and a positive integer
\mathsf{k}\geqslant 1
.
In Section
3
, we define the subcategories

, parametrized by positive integers

, of the category of finite-dimensional representations over quantum affine algebras, equipped with a cluster algebra structure.
In Section
4
, we present our main conjecture concerning positivity in the Verlinde ring under a certain ring homomorphism from

. As supporting evidence, we prove its quantum dimension version under the assumption that the conjecture of Kirillov, Kuniba, Nakanishi and Suzuki regarding the positivity of the quantum dimension of certain KR modules holds.
In Section
5
, we establish our main conjecture for

in some special cases.
In Section

Background

2.1.
Cluster algebras

Let
\mathsf{K}={\mathsf{K}^{\mathrm{ex}}}\sqcup{\mathsf{K}^{\mathrm{fr}}}
be a (finite or countably many) index set that decomposes into the set
{\mathsf{K}^{\mathrm{ex}}}
of
exchangeable
indices and the set
{\mathsf{K}^{\mathrm{fr}}}
of
frozen
indices. An
exchange matrix

\mathsf{B}=(b_{i,j})_{i\in\mathsf{K},j\in{\mathsf{K}^{\mathrm{ex}}}}
is an integer-valued matrix such that

(a)

for each
j\in{\mathsf{K}^{\mathrm{ex}}}
, there are finitely many
i\in\mathsf{K}
such that
b_{i,j}
are nonzero,

(b)

the
principal part

(b_{i,j})_{i\in{\mathsf{K}^{\mathrm{ex}}},j\in{\mathsf{K}^{\mathrm{ex}}}}
of
\mathsf{B}
is skew-symmetric.

For convenience, when
i\in\mathsf{K}
and
j\in{\mathsf{K}^{\mathrm{fr}}}
, we define

b_{i,j}=\begin{cases}-b_{j,i}&\text{ if $i\in{\mathsf{K}^{\mathrm{ex}}}$ and $j\in{\mathsf{K}^{\mathrm{fr}}}$},\\
0&\text{ if $i,j\in{\mathsf{K}^{\mathrm{fr}}}$}.\end{cases}

Note that, to an exchange matrix
\mathsf{B}
, one can associate a quiver
Q_{\mathsf{B}}
as follows: the set of vertices of
Q_{\mathsf{B}}
is
\mathsf{K}
and the number of arrows from
i\in\mathsf{K}
to
j\in\mathsf{K}
is defined to be the number
{\mathop{\mathrm{max}}}\{0,b_{i,j}\}
.
The quiver
Q_{\mathsf{B}}
also determines the matrix
\mathsf{B}
by taking

b_{i,j}:=\text{(the number of arrows $i\rightarrow j$)}-\text{(the number of arrows $j\rightarrow i$)}.

Let
A
be a commutative algebra which is contained in a field
F
. A pair
\mathscr{S}=(\{x_{i}\}_{i\in\mathsf{K}},\mathsf{B})
is called a
seed
in
A
if the set
\{x_{i}\}_{i\in\mathsf{K}}
is contained in
A
and the homomorphism from a polynomial ring
{\mathbb{Z}}[\mathrm{X}_{i}\ ;i\in\mathsf{K}]
to
A
defined by
\mathrm{X}_{i}\mapsto x_{i}
(
i\in\mathsf{K}
) is injective. The set
\{x_{i}\}_{i\in\mathsf{K}}
is called a
cluster
of
\mathscr{S}
and the elements
x_{i}
are called
cluster variables
. For any
\mathbf{a}=(a_{i})_{i\in\mathsf{K}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{\mathsf{K}}
, the monomial

x^{\mathbf{a}}:=\prod_{i\in\mathsf{K}}x_{i}^{a_{i}}

is called a
cluster monomial
.

For each
k\in{\mathsf{K}^{\mathrm{ex}}}
, we define a set
\mu_{k}(\{x_{i}\}_{i\in\mathsf{K}})=\{y_{i}\}_{i\in\mathsf{K}}
and a matrix
\mu_{k}(\mathsf{B})=(c_{i,j})_{i\in\mathsf{K},j\in{\mathsf{K}^{\mathrm{ex}}}}
by

\displaystyle y_{i}

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}(x^{\mathbf{a}^{\prime}}+x^{\mathbf{a}^{\prime\prime}})/x_{k}&\text{ if $i=k$,}\\
x_{i}&\text{ if $i\neq k$,}\end{cases}

\displaystyle c_{i,j}

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}-b_{i,j}&\text{ if $i=k$ or $j=k$},\\
b_{i,j}+(-1)^{\delta(b_{i,k}<0)}{\mathop{\mathrm{max}}}\{b_{i,k}b_{k,j},0\}&\text{ otherwise,}\end{cases}

where
\mathbf{a}^{\prime}=(a_{i}^{\prime})_{i\in\mathsf{K}}
and
\mathbf{a}^{\prime\prime}=(a_{i}^{\prime\prime})_{i\in\mathsf{K}}
are given by

a_{i}^{\prime}=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if $i=k$,}\\
{\mathop{\mathrm{max}}}\{0,b_{i,k}\}&\text{ if $i\neq k$,}\end{cases}\qquad a_{i}^{\prime\prime}=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if $i=k$,}\\
{\mathop{\mathrm{max}}}\{0,-b_{i,k}\}&\text{ if $i\neq k$.}\end{cases}

The pair
\mu_{k}(\mathscr{S}):=(\mu_{k}(\{x_{i}\}_{i\in\mathsf{K}}),\mu_{k}(\mathsf{B}))
is called the
mutation
of
\mathscr{S}
at
k
,
and the following equality in
F
is called an
exchange relation
:

x_{k}y_{k}=x^{\mathbf{a}^{\prime}}+x^{\mathbf{a}^{\prime\prime}}.

The
cluster algebra

\mathcal{A}(\mathscr{S})
, associated to an initial seed
\mathscr{S}
, is the
{\mathbb{Z}}
-subalgebra of the field
F
generated by all cluster variables obtained from
\mathscr{S}
by all possible successive mutations.
A commutative algebra
A
has a
cluster algebra structure
associated to a seed
\mathscr{S}
in
A
if there exists a family
\mathfrak{F}
of seeds in
A
such that

(a)

\mathcal{A}(\mathscr{S})
coincides with
A
,

(b)

\mathscr{S}\in\mathfrak{F}
, and any mutation of a seed in
\mathfrak{F}
is contained in
\mathfrak{F}
,

(c)

for any
\mathscr{S}^{\prime},\mathscr{S}^{\prime\prime}\in\mathfrak{F}
,
\mathscr{S}^{\prime}
can be obtained from
\mathscr{S}^{\prime\prime}
by a finite sequence of mutations.

A cluster algebra is said to be of
finite cluster type
if it has a finite number of cluster variables.
The
Cartan counterpart

C(\mathsf{B})=(c_{i,j})_{i,j\in{\mathsf{K}^{\mathrm{ex}}}}
associated with an exchange matrix
\mathsf{B}
is the square matrix defined by

c_{i,j}=\begin{cases}2&\text{ if }i=j,\\
-|b_{i,j}|&\text{ if }i\neq j.\end{cases}

We then have a couple of results on cluster algebras of finite cluster types, which will be needed later.

Theorem 2.1

(Finite type classification
[
9
, Theorem 1.4]
)
.

The cluster algebra
A
is of finite cluster type if and only if there is an exchange matrix
\mathsf{B}
of

such that the Cartan counterpart

is a block diagonal matrix of Cartan matrices of finite types, up to conjugation by a permutation matrix.

Theorem 2.2

(
[
19
, Theorem 4.3.1]
)
.

Let
A
be a cluster algebra of finite cluster type. The cluster monomials of
A
form a linear basis of
A
.

2.2.
Affine Lie algebras and quantum affine algebras

This subsection provides a brief overview of Kac-Moody algebras and their quantum counterparts, with a particular focus on the affine case.
For detailed information on Kac-Moody algebras, refer to
[
22
]
.
Let
({\mathsf{A}},\mathsf{P},\Pi,\mathsf{P}^{\vee},\Pi^{\vee})
be a
Cartan datum
consisting of a
symmetrizable generalized Cartan matrix

{\mathsf{A}}=(a_{i,j})_{i,j\in I}
, a free abelian group
\mathsf{P}
, called the
weight lattice
, its dual
\mathsf{P}^{\vee}:=\operatorname{Hom}(\mathsf{P},{\mathbb{Z}})
, the set
\Pi=\{\alpha_{i}\}_{i\in I}\subset\mathsf{P}
of
simple roots
and the set
\Pi^{\vee}=\{h_{i}\}_{i\in I}\subset\mathsf{P}^{\vee}
of
simple coroots
.
They meet the following requirements:

•

Both
\Pi
and
\Pi^{\vee}
are linearly independent.

•

The condition
\langle h_{i},\alpha_{j}\rangle=a_{i,j}
holds for all
i,j\in I
.

•

|I|-\operatorname{rank}{\mathsf{A}}=\operatorname{rank}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}\mathsf{P}-|I|.

Here,
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the canonical pairing
\mathsf{P}\times\mathsf{P}^{\vee}\to{\mathbb{Z}}
and
\mathsf{P}^{\vee}\times\mathsf{P}\to{\mathbb{Z}}
.
We define
\mathsf{Q}:=\bigoplus_{i\in I}{\mathbb{Z}}\alpha_{i}
, known as the
root lattice
.
Additionally, we introduce the complex vector spaces
\mathfrak{h}:=\mathsf{P}^{\vee}\otimes\mathbb{C}
and
\mathfrak{h}^{*}:=\mathsf{P}\otimes\mathbb{C}
.
We call
\mathsf{P}_{+}:=\{\lambda\in\mathsf{P}\mid\langle\lambda,h_{i}\rangle\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}\text{ for all }i\in I\}
the set of
dominant integral weights
.

With this datum, we define an associated Lie algebra
{\mathfrak{g}}
, known as the
Kac-Moody algebra
, which is denoted by
{\mathfrak{g}}({\mathsf{A}})
in
[
22
]
. Under the assumption that
{\mathsf{A}}
is symmetrizable,
{\mathfrak{g}}
can be described by the following presentation; see
[
22
, Theorem 9.11]
.

Definition 2.3
.

The Kac-Moody algebra
{\mathfrak{g}}
associated with
({\mathsf{A}},\mathsf{P},\Pi,\mathsf{P}^{\vee},\Pi^{\vee})
is the Lie algebra
over
{\mathbb{C}}
generated by
e_{i},f_{i}

(i\in I)
and
h\in\mathsf{P}^{\vee}
satisfying following relations:

(i)

[h,h^{\prime}]=0\quad
for
h,h^{\prime}\in\mathsf{P}^{\vee},

(ii)

[e_{i},f_{j}]=\delta_{i,j}h_{i}\quad\text{for }i,j\in I,

(iii)

[h,e_{i}]=\langle h,\alpha_{i}\rangle e_{i},\quad[h,f_{i}]=-\langle h,\alpha_{i}\rangle f_{i}\quad\text{for }i\in I,h\in\mathsf{P}^{\vee},

(iv)

(\operatorname{ad}e_{i})^{1-a_{i,j}}e_{j}=(\operatorname{ad}f_{i})^{1-a_{i,j}}f_{j}=0\quad\text{for }i\neq j.

There exists a
\mathbb{Q}
-valued, non-degenerate symmetric bilinear form
(\cdot|\cdot)
on
\mathsf{P}
satisfying

(\alpha_{i}|\alpha_{i})>0
for all
i\in I
, and for any

,

\langle h_{i},\lambda\rangle=\frac{2(\alpha_{i}|\lambda)}{(\alpha_{i}|\alpha_{i})}.

The
Weyl group

W
of
{\mathfrak{g}}
is the subgroup of
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathsf{P})
generated by the
fundamental reflections

for

, where

(2.1)

s_{i}(\lambda)=\lambda-\left\langle h_{i},\lambda\right\rangle\alpha_{i}\quad\text{for }\lambda\in\mathsf{P}.

It turns out that

is a Coxeter group, and the bilinear form
(\cdot|\cdot)
on
\mathsf{P}
is

-invariant.

Now we turn to a quantum deformation of the universal enveloping algebra
U({\mathfrak{g}})
of a Kac-Moody algebra
{\mathfrak{g}}
.
Let
q
be an indeterminate and
\Bbbk
the algebraic closure of the subfield

in
\widehat{\Bbbk}\mathbin{:=}\bigcup_{m>0}{\mathbb{C}}((q^{1/m}))
.
For each

, set

, and for any

, define

\displaystyle[n]_{i}=\frac{q^{n}_{i}-q^{-n}_{i}}{q_{i}-q^{-1}_{i}},\quad\

\displaystyle[n]_{i}!=\prod^{n}_{k=1}[k]_{i},

\displaystyle\left[\begin{matrix}m\\
n\\
\end{matrix}\right]_{i}=\frac{[m]_{i}!}{[m-n]_{i}![n]_{i}!}.

We set

.
Note that

Definition 2.4
.

The quantum group

\displaystyle\sum^{1-a_{ij}}_{k=0}(-1)^{k}e^{(1-a_{ij}-k)}_{i}e_{j}e^{(k)}_{i}=\sum^{1-a_{ij}}_{k=0}(-1)^{k}f^{(1-a_{ij}-k)}_{i}f_{j}f^{(k)}_{i}=0\quad\text{ for }i\neq j,

.
The numbers
h=\sum_{i\in I}\mathsf{a}_{i}
and
h^{\vee}=\sum_{i\in I}\mathsf{a}^{\vee}_{i}
are called the
Coxeter number
and the
dual Coxeter number
, respectively.

Let us fix a Cartan datum as follows.
Let
\mathsf{P}^{\vee}
be a free
{\mathbb{Z}}
-module with basis
\{h_{0},\dots,h_{N},\mathsf{d}\}
, and then
define the dual lattice
\mathsf{P}:=\operatorname{Hom}(\mathsf{P}^{\vee},{\mathbb{Z}})
.
For each
i\in I
, we define the
fundamental weights

\Lambda_{i}\in\mathsf{P}
as the linear functionals satisfying

\langle\Lambda_{i},h_{j}\rangle=\delta_{i,j}
for
j\in I
and
\langle\Lambda_{i},\mathsf{d}\rangle=0
.
We then choose
\mathrm{\delta}\in\mathsf{P}
such that

(2.2)

\langle\mathrm{\delta},h_{i}\rangle=0\quad\text{for all }i\in I,

and
\langle\mathrm{\delta},\mathsf{d}\rangle=1
.
In other words, the ordered set
\{\Lambda_{0},\dots,\Lambda_{N},\mathrm{\delta}\}
is a
{\mathbb{Z}}
-basis of
\mathsf{P}
, dual to the ordered basis
\{h_{0},\dots,h_{N},\mathsf{d}\}
of
\mathsf{P}^{\vee}
.
For each
i\in I
, define
\alpha_{i}\in\mathsf{P}
so that
\langle\alpha_{j},h_{i}\rangle=a_{i,j}
for any
j\in I
, along with
\langle\alpha_{0},\mathsf{d}\rangle=1
and
\langle\alpha_{i},\mathsf{d}\rangle=0
for
i\in I_{0}
.
Specifically, we have

(2.3)

\alpha_{0}=\mathrm{\delta}+\sum_{i\in I}a_{i,0}\Lambda_{i},\quad\alpha_{j}=\sum_{i\in I}a_{i,j}\Lambda_{i}\quad\text{for }j\in I_{0}.

With these choices,
({\mathsf{A}},\mathsf{P},\Pi,\mathsf{P}^{\vee},\Pi^{\vee})
forms a Cartan datum.
The relation
\mathrm{\delta}=\sum_{i\in I}\mathsf{a}_{i}\alpha_{i}\in\mathsf{P}
holds, and
\mathrm{\delta}
is referred to as the
imaginary root
.
Additionally, we define the
canonical central element

\mathsf{c}:=\sum_{i\in I}\mathsf{a}^{\vee}_{i}h_{i}\in\mathsf{P}^{\vee}
.

Let
{\mathfrak{g}}:={\mathfrak{g}}({\mathsf{A}})
denote the affine Kac-Moody algebra associated with the Cartan datum
({\mathsf{A}},\mathsf{P},\Pi,\mathsf{P}^{\vee},\Pi^{\vee})
, referred to as the
affine Lie algebra
.
This datum also gives rise to a corresponding
quantum affine algebra

U_{q}({\mathfrak{g}})
.
In this paper, we will also use the notation
\widehat{{\mathfrak{g}}}_{0}
for
{\mathfrak{g}}
, which is common in the literature, while
{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}
will be discussed in the subsequent paragraphs.

We define the
level
of
\Lambda\in\mathsf{P}
as the number

(2.4)

\mathsf{k}:=\langle\Lambda,\mathsf{c}\rangle=\sum_{i\in I}\mathsf{a}^{\vee}_{i}\langle\Lambda,h_{i}\rangle.

The canonical central element
\mathsf{c}\in{\mathfrak{g}}
acts on
L(\Lambda)
for
\Lambda\in\mathsf{P}_{+}
as a scalar equal to the level of
\Lambda
. This value is also referred to as the level of
L(\Lambda)
.

The set
I_{0}=I\setminus\{0\}
serves as the index set for the underlying simple finite-dimensional Lie algebra
{{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}}
of type
X_{N}
.
Specifically,
{{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}}
is the Lie subalgebra of
{\mathfrak{g}}
generated by the Chevalley generators
e_{i}
,
f_{i}
and
h_{i}
for
i\in I_{0}
.
Analogously,
U_{q}({{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}})
is the
\Bbbk
-subalgebra of
U_{q}({{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}})
generated by
e_{i},f_{i},K^{\pm 1}_{i}
for
i\in I_{0}
.
They can also be described as a Kac-Moody algebra and its quantum counterpart as follows.
Let
\mathsf{P}^{\vee}_{0}
denote the
{\mathbb{Z}}
-submodule of
\mathsf{P}
with basis
\{h_{i}\}_{i\in I_{0}}
.
For
\lambda\in\mathsf{P}=\operatorname{Hom}(\mathsf{P}^{\vee},{\mathbb{Z}})
, let
\overline{\lambda}\in\operatorname{Hom}(\mathsf{P}^{\vee}_{0},{\mathbb{Z}})
be the restriction of

\lambda
to
\mathsf{P}^{\vee}_{0}
.
Define

(2.5)

\mathsf{P}_{0}:=\oplus_{i\in I_{0}}{\mathbb{Z}}\overline{\Lambda}_{i},\quad\Pi_{0}:=\{\overline{\alpha}_{i}\}_{i\in I_{0}},\quad\text{and}\quad\Pi_{0}^{\vee}:=\{h_{i}\}_{i\in I_{0}}.

Then
{{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}}
and
U_{q}({{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}})
are the Kac-Moody algebra and the corresponding quantum group associated with the Cartan datum
({\mathsf{A}}_{0},\mathsf{P}_{0},\Pi_{0},\mathsf{P}^{\vee}_{0},\Pi_{0}^{\vee})
, respectively.
Throughout paper, we will also use the notation
\varpi_{i}:=\overline{\Lambda}_{i}
for
i\in I_{0}
.

Let
\operatorname{Rep}{{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}}
and
\operatorname{Rep}{U_{q}({{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}})}
denote the categories of integrable representations for
{{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}}
and
U_{q}({{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}})
, respectively. The objects in those categories are finite-dimensional.
The Grothendieck rings
K_{0}(\operatorname{Rep}{{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}})
and
K_{0}(\operatorname{Rep}{U_{q}({{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}})})
are both
{\mathbb{Z}}
-rings, with bases
\{[L(\lambda)]\mid\lambda\in P_{0}\}
, and
\{[L^{q}(\lambda)]\mid\lambda\in P_{0}\}
, respectively.
There exists a ring isomorphism that maps
[L^{q}(\lambda)]
to
[L(\lambda)]
:

(2.6)

K_{0}(\operatorname{Rep}{U_{q}({\mathfrak{g}})})\xrightarrow{\cong}K_{0}(\operatorname{Rep}{{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}}).

Throughout the paper, we will also denote the simple
U_{q}({{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}})
-module
L^{q}(\lambda)
as
L(\lambda)
whenever the context is clear.

Let
{\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime}:=[{\mathfrak{g}},{\mathfrak{g}}]
denote the derived subalgebra of
{\mathfrak{g}}
.
It is a subalgebra generated by
e_{i},f_{i},h_{i}
for
i\in I
, and is isomorphic to

({\mathbb{C}}[t,t^{-1}]\otimes_{{\mathbb{C}}}{{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}})\oplus{\mathbb{C}}\mathsf{c},

a central extension of the loop algebra, equipped with a certain Lie bracket.
This is also called the affine Lie algebra.
Analogously, we define
U^{\prime}_{q}({\mathfrak{g}})
to be the
\Bbbk
-subalgebra of
U_{q}({\mathfrak{g}})
generated by
e_{i},f_{i},K^{\pm 1}_{i}
for
i\in I
.
The key distinction between the algebras
U_{q}({\mathfrak{g}})
and
U^{\prime}_{q}({\mathfrak{g}})
is that
U^{\prime}_{q}({\mathfrak{g}})
has a rich theory of finite-dimensional representations, whereas any non-trivial representation of
U_{q}({\mathfrak{g}})
is infinite-dimensional.
In the literature,
U^{\prime}_{q}({\mathfrak{g}})
is also known as the quantum affine algebra.
This paper focuses on certain finite-dimensional representations of
U^{\prime}_{q}({\mathfrak{g}})
; see Section
3
for additional details.

We now turn to the action of Weyl groups. We define
\mathsf{P}_{{\mathrm{cl}}}:=\mathsf{P}/(\mathsf{P}\cap\mathbb{Z}\mathrm{\delta})
.
When there is no risk of confusion, we will simply write
\Lambda_{i}
for the image of
\Lambda_{i}\in\mathsf{P}
under the quotient map
\mathsf{P}\to\mathsf{P}_{{\mathrm{cl}}}
.
The level of an element in
\mathsf{P}_{{\mathrm{cl}}}
is defined in the same way as in (
2.4
).
Note that, from (
2.1
) and (
2.2
), we have
w(\mathrm{\delta})=\mathrm{\delta}
for all
w\in W
.
Then, the
affine Weyl group

W
acts on both
\mathsf{P}
and
\mathsf{P}_{{\mathrm{cl}}}
.
In particular, the fundamental reflection
s_{i}
for
i\in I
acts linearly on
\mathsf{P}_{{\mathrm{cl}}}
by

(2.7)

s_{i}\Lambda_{j}=\Lambda_{j}-\delta_{i,j}\alpha_{i}\pmod{{\mathbb{Z}}\mathrm{\delta}},\quad j\in I.

Using (
2.3
), we can express this action entirely in terms of
{\mathsf{A}}
and the fundamental weights.
Let
\rho:=\sum_{i\in I}\Lambda_{i}\in\mathsf{P}_{{\mathrm{cl}}}
be the
affine Weyl vector
.
The
shifted Weyl group action
of
W
on
\mathsf{P}_{{\mathrm{cl}}}
is given by

(2.8)

w\cdot\lambda:=w(\lambda+\rho)-\rho,

for
\lambda\in\mathsf{P}_{{\mathrm{cl}}}
.
Finally, the
finite Weyl group

W_{0}
, generated by
\{s_{i}\}_{i\in I_{0}}
, acts on the weight lattice
\mathsf{P}_{0}
(
2.5
) of
{{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}}
. The fundamental reflection
s_{i}
for
i\in I_{0}
acts on
\mathsf{P}_{0}
by

(2.9)

s_{i}\varpi_{j}=\varpi_{j}-\delta_{i,j}\overline{\alpha}_{i},\quad j\in I_{0}

which can be expressed explicitly in terms of
{\mathsf{A}}_{0}
and
\{\varpi_{i}\}_{i\in I_{0}}
.

2.3.
Verlinde rings

This subsection provides a brief introduction to the Verlinde ring associated to an affine Lie algebra
{\mathfrak{g}}
and an integer
\mathsf{k}\geqslant 1
.
For a detailed systematic account, refer to
[
29
]
.
For additional insight from a conformal field theory perspective, see
[
7
, Chapters 14 and 16]
.

Let
\mathsf{P}_{{\mathrm{cl}}}^{\mathsf{k}}
denote the subset of
\mathsf{P}_{{\mathrm{cl}}}
consisting of elements with level
\mathsf{k}
.
We define

\mathsf{P}^{\mathsf{k}}_{{\mathrm{cl}},+}:=\{\lambda\in\mathsf{P}_{{\mathrm{cl}}}^{\mathsf{k}}\mid\lambda=\sum_{i\in I}c_{i}\Lambda_{i},\,c_{i}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}\}.

An element
\lambda=\sum_{i\in I}c_{i}\Lambda_{i}\in\mathsf{P}_{{\mathrm{cl}}}
belongs to
\mathsf{P}^{\mathsf{k}}_{{\mathrm{cl}},+}
if and only if

\sum_{i\in I}\mathsf{a}^{\vee}_{i}c_{i}=\mathsf{k},\quad c_{i}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}.

Hence,
\mathsf{P}^{\mathsf{k}}_{{\mathrm{cl}},+}
is a finite set.
Note that there is a bijection
\mathsf{P}_{{\mathrm{cl}}}^{\mathsf{k}}\to\mathsf{P}_{0}
given by the restriction
\lambda\mapsto\overline{\lambda}
:

(2.10)

\lambda=\sum_{i\in I}c_{i}\Lambda_{i}\mapsto\overline{\lambda}=\sum_{i\in I_{0}}c_{i}\varpi_{i}.

For a given
\varpi\in\mathsf{P}_{0}
, we can find a unique element
\lambda\in\mathsf{P}_{{\mathrm{cl}}}^{\mathsf{k}}
such that
\overline{\lambda}=\varpi
.
Under this bijection,
\mathsf{P}^{\mathsf{k}}_{{\mathrm{cl}},+}
corresponds to the dominant integral weights in the fundamental alcove:

\{\varpi\in\mathsf{P}_{0}\mid\langle h_{i},\varpi\rangle\geqslant 0\text{ for each }i\in I_{0}\text{ and }\langle\theta^{\vee},\varpi\rangle\leqslant\mathsf{k}\},

where
\theta^{\vee}:=\sum_{i\in I_{0}}\mathsf{a}^{\vee}_{i}h_{i}
.
In this subsection, we adopt a specific normalization for a non-degenerate symmetric bilinear form
(\cdot|\cdot)_{0}
on
\mathsf{P}_{0}
so that
(\theta|\theta)_{0}=2
, where
\theta=\sum_{i\in I_{0}}\mathsf{a}_{i}\overline{\alpha}_{i}
is the highest root of
{{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}}
.

Given a pair
\lambda,\mu\in\mathsf{P}_{{\mathrm{cl}}}^{\mathsf{k}}
, define

(2.11)

S_{\lambda,\mu}:=C({\mathsf{A}},\mathsf{k})\cdot\sum_{w\in W_{0}}(-1)^{\ell(w)}\exp\left(-{\frac{2\pi\sqrt{-1}(w(\overline{\lambda}+\overline{\rho})|\overline{\mu}+\overline{\rho})_{0}}{\mathsf{k}+h^{\vee}}}\right),

where
C({\mathsf{A}},\mathsf{k})
is a certain normalizing constant that depends only on the Cartan datum and not on
\lambda
and
\mu
; see
[
22
, Theorem 13.8]
for an explicit expression.
Note that the sum is taken over the finite Weyl group
W_{0}
.
The matrix
S=(S_{\lambda,\mu})_{\lambda,\mu\in\mathsf{P}^{\mathsf{k}}_{{\mathrm{cl}},+}}
is referred to as the
modular
S
-matrix
.
This quantity plays a role, for example, in describing the modular transformation properties of characters of integrable highest weight representations at level
\mathsf{k}
for affine Lie algebras; see
[
23
]
and
[
22
, Theorem 13.8]
. This connection explains its name.

Here, we provide a summary of several key properties of the modular
S
-matrix.

(1). Symmetry: The modular
S
-matrix is symmetric, so for
\lambda,\mu\in\mathsf{P}_{{\mathrm{cl}}}^{\mathsf{k}}
,

S_{\lambda,\mu}=S_{\mu,\lambda}.

(2). Unitarity: The modular
S
-matrix is unitary:

(2.12)

SS^{\dagger}=I_{n},

where
S^{\dagger}
denotes the transpose of the complex conjugate of
S
, and
I_{n}
is the identity matrix of size
n=|\mathsf{P}^{\mathsf{k}}_{{\mathrm{cl}},+}|
.

(3). Weyl group symmetry: For
w\in W
and
\lambda,\mu\in\mathsf{P}_{{\mathrm{cl}}}^{\mathsf{k}}
, the following relations holds:

(2.13)

S_{w\cdot\lambda,\mu}=(-1)^{\ell(w)}S_{\lambda,\mu}.

Here, the shifted action of
W
defined in (
2.8
) is used.

(4). Conjugation symmetry: For
\lambda,\mu\in\mathsf{P}_{{\mathrm{cl}}}^{\mathsf{k}}
, we have

(2.14)

S_{\lambda^{*},\mu}=S^{*}_{\lambda,\mu},

where
S^{*}_{\lambda,\mu}
is the complex conjugate of
S_{\lambda,\mu}
and
\lambda^{*}\in\mathsf{P}_{{\mathrm{cl}}}^{\mathsf{k}}
is defined as follows:
For
\varpi\in\mathsf{P}_{0}
, let
\varpi^{*}:=-w_{0}\varpi\in\mathsf{P}_{0}
, where
w_{0}
is the longest element of the finite Weyl group
W_{0}
.
For an affine weight
\lambda\in\mathsf{P}_{{\mathrm{cl}}}^{\mathsf{k}}
, its conjugate
\lambda^{*}\in\mathsf{P}_{{\mathrm{cl}}}^{\mathsf{k}}
is the unique element of
\mathsf{P}_{{\mathrm{cl}}}^{\mathsf{k}}
such that
\overline{\lambda^{*}}=\overline{\lambda}^{*}
.

The simple objects in the category
\operatorname{Rep}(\widehat{{\mathfrak{g}}}_{0}^{\prime},\mathsf{k})
of integrable representations of level
\mathsf{k}
over the affine Lie algebra
\widehat{{\mathfrak{g}}}_{0}^{\prime}
are indexed by
\mathsf{P}^{\mathsf{k}}_{{\mathrm{cl}},+}
, and any object can be written as a direct sum of these simple objects (
[
22
, Theorem 10.7]
).
However, the tensor product of two objects in
\operatorname{Rep}(\widehat{{\mathfrak{g}}}_{0}^{\prime},\mathsf{k})
, considered as representations of the Lie algebra, does not belong to
\operatorname{Rep}(\widehat{{\mathfrak{g}}}_{0}^{\prime},\mathsf{k})
because its level becomes
2\mathsf{k}
.

Surprisingly, there exists a different kind of product on
\operatorname{Rep}(\widehat{{\mathfrak{g}}}_{0}^{\prime},\mathsf{k})
, called the
fusion product
.
The
Verlinde ring

K_{0}(\operatorname{Rep}(\widehat{{\mathfrak{g}}}_{0}^{\prime},\mathsf{k}))
, also known as the
WZW fusion ring
or simply the
fusion ring
, is the Grothendieck ring of the category
\operatorname{Rep}(\widehat{{\mathfrak{g}}}_{0}^{\prime},\mathsf{k})
of integrable representations
\mathsf{k}
over
\widehat{{\mathfrak{g}}}_{0}^{\prime}
.
Specifically, it is a free
{\mathbb{Z}}
-module with basis
\{[L(\lambda)]:\lambda\in\mathsf{P}^{\mathsf{k}}_{{\mathrm{cl}},+}\}
, endowed with the product

[L(\lambda)]\cdot[L(\mu)]=\sum_{\nu\in\mathsf{P}^{\mathsf{k}}_{{\mathrm{cl}},+}}N_{\lambda\mu}^{\nu}[L(\nu)],

where
N_{\lambda\mu}^{\nu}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
denotes the dimension of the
conformal block

V_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(\lambda,\mu,\nu^{*})
on
\mathbb{P}^{1}
; see
[
1
,
29
]
.
The coefficients
N_{\lambda\mu}^{\nu}
are called
fusion coefficients
.

It is a commutative, associative ring with unity
[L(\mathsf{k}\Lambda_{0})]
.
The associativity is closely related to the factorization property of the space of conformal blocks; see
[
29
, Chapters 3 and 4]
.
The fusion coefficients can be written in terms of the modular
S
-matrix via the
Verlinde formula
:

(2.15)

N_{\lambda\mu}^{\nu}=\sum_{\omega\in\mathsf{P}^{\mathsf{k}}_{{\mathrm{cl}},+}}\frac{S_{\lambda,\omega}S_{\mu,\omega}S^{*}_{\nu,\omega}}{S_{\mathsf{k}\Lambda_{0},\omega}}.

Note that although the normalizing factor in (
2.11
) has no effect on (
2.18
) below, it is necessary for the validity of (
2.15
).

Definition 2.5
.

An element
\sum_{\lambda\in\mathsf{P}^{\mathsf{k}}_{{\mathrm{cl}},+}}c_{\lambda}[L(\lambda)]\in K_{0}(\operatorname{Rep}(\widehat{{\mathfrak{g}}}_{0}^{\prime},\mathsf{k}))
is
non-negative
if
c_{\lambda}\geqslant 0
for all
\lambda\in\mathsf{P}^{\mathsf{k}}_{{\mathrm{cl}},+}
.
A non-zero element of
K_{0}(\operatorname{Rep}(\widehat{{\mathfrak{g}}}_{0}^{\prime},\mathsf{k}))
is
positive
if it is non-negative.

Although the fusion product has its origins in conformal field theory
[
37
]
and is mathematically defined in a rather complicated algebro-geometric way, there is a way to handle it using only elements from Lie theory, while avoiding the need for explicit computation of the
S
-matrix, which is computationally demanding.
Specifically, there exists a surjective ring homomorphism
\pi_{\mathsf{k}}:K_{0}(\operatorname{Rep}{{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}})\to K_{0}(\operatorname{Rep}(\widehat{{\mathfrak{g}}}_{0}^{\prime},\mathsf{k}))
.
We provide a description of this map below.
The Grothendieck ring
K_{0}(\operatorname{Rep}{{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}})
of finite-dimensional representations of
{{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}}
is a free
{\mathbb{Z}}
-module with basis
\{[L(\varpi)]\mid\varpi\in\mathsf{P}_{0,+}\}
.
Thus, it is sufficient to specify

\pi_{\mathsf{k}}([L(\varpi)])
to define a ring homomorphism.

For a given
\lambda\in\mathsf{P}_{{\mathrm{cl}}}^{\mathsf{k}}
, either:

•

There is no non-trivial element
w\in W
such that
w\cdot\lambda=\lambda
,

•

or there exists a non-trivial element
w\in W
such that
w\cdot\lambda=\lambda
.

We define
\lambda\in\mathsf{P}_{{\mathrm{cl}}}^{\mathsf{k}}
as
null
if the latter condition holds.
If the first condition holds, then there exists a unique
\lambda^{\prime}\in\mathsf{P}^{\mathsf{k}}_{{\mathrm{cl}},+}
such that

(2.16)

\lambda^{\prime}=w\cdot\lambda

for some unique
w\in W
.
Then
\pi_{\mathsf{k}}:K_{0}(\operatorname{Rep}{{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}})\to K_{0}(\operatorname{Rep}(\widehat{{\mathfrak{g}}}_{0}^{\prime},\mathsf{k}))
can be defined as follows:

(2.17)

\pi_{\mathsf{k}}([L(\varpi)]):=\begin{cases}0&\text{if $\lambda$ is null,}\\
(-1)^{\ell(w)}[L(\lambda^{\prime})]&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

where
\varpi\in\mathsf{P}_{0,+}
, and
\lambda\in\mathsf{P}_{{\mathrm{cl}}}^{\mathsf{k}}
is the unique affine weight of level
\mathsf{k}
such that
\overline{\lambda}=\varpi
, which is not necessarily in
\mathsf{P}^{\mathsf{k}}_{{\mathrm{cl}},+}
.
In case
\lambda
is not null,
\lambda^{\prime}\in\mathsf{P}^{\mathsf{k}}_{{\mathrm{cl}},+}
and
w\in W
are given as in (
2.16
).
The existence of this homomorphism is established in
[
1
]
;
see also
[
29
, Theorem 4.2.9]
and the references therein for further details.
This enables expressing the fusion product in terms of the tensor product in
\operatorname{Rep}{{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}}
.
For
\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2}\in\mathsf{P}^{\mathsf{k}}_{{\mathrm{cl}},+}
, the product
[L(\Lambda_{1})]\cdot[L(\Lambda_{2})]
is given by:

[L(\Lambda_{1})]\cdot[L(\Lambda_{2})]=\pi_{\mathsf{k}}\left([L(\overline{\Lambda_{1}})]\right)\cdot\pi_{\mathsf{k}}\left([L(\overline{\Lambda_{2}})]\right)=\pi_{\mathsf{k}}\left([L(\overline{\Lambda_{1}})\otimes L(\overline{\Lambda_{2}})]\right).

Simply put, determining the image of
\pi_{\mathsf{k}}
involves moving a weight from the fundamental chamber to the fundamental alcove via the generators of
W
while keeping track of the number of simple reflections applied.
This procedure is demonstrated in Example
2.6
and illustrated in Figure
1
.

\pi_{\mathsf{k}}
; see Example
2.6

Example 2.6
.

Let
{{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}}
be of type
A_{2}
.
The elements of
\mathsf{P}_{0}
can be represented as points in a hexagonal lattice in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, with basis vectors
\varpi_{1}=(1,0)
and
\varpi_{2}=\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)
.

Consider now
\mathsf{k}=3
.
Each element of
\mathsf{P}_{{\mathrm{cl}}}^{\mathsf{k}}
is uniquely associated with a point in the hexagonal lattice through the bijection given in (
2.10
).
Among these, 10 elements belong to
\mathsf{P}^{\mathsf{k}}_{{\mathrm{cl}},+}
, with each
\lambda\in\mathsf{P}^{\mathsf{k}}_{{\mathrm{cl}},+}
corresponding to a bottom-left lattice point marked in red in Figure
1
.

For
\varpi=c_{1}\varpi_{1}+c_{2}\varpi_{2}
with
c_{1},c_{2}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geqslant 0}
and the corresponding
\lambda=(\mathsf{k}-c_{1}-c_{2})\Lambda_{0}+c_{1}\Lambda_{1}+c_{2}\Lambda_{2}
,
\lambda
is null if
\varpi
is located at a lattice point crossed by
\times
.
Otherwise,
\varpi
is assigned a specific numbering, and
\overline{\lambda^{\prime}}
in (
2.16
) corresponds to a red point with the same numbering as
\varpi
.
If
\varpi
is marked in blue, then
\pi_{\mathsf{k}}(L(\varpi))=-L(\lambda^{\prime})
; otherwise,
\pi_{\mathsf{k}}(L(\varpi))=L(\lambda^{\prime})
.

For
\lambda\in\mathsf{P}_{{\mathrm{cl}}}^{\mathsf{k}}
, we define

(2.18)

{\mathcal{D}}_{\lambda}:=\frac{S_{\lambda,\mathsf{k}\Lambda_{0}}}{S_{\mathsf{k}\Lambda_{0},\mathsf{k}\Lambda_{0}}}=\frac{\prod_{\alpha\in\Delta_{+}}\sin\frac{\pi(\overline{\lambda}+\overline{\rho}|\alpha)_{0}}{\mathsf{k}+h^{\vee}}}{\prod_{\alpha\in\Delta_{+}}\sin\frac{\pi(\overline{\rho}|\alpha)_{0}}{\mathsf{k}+h^{\vee}}},

where
\Delta_{+}
denotes the set of positive roots for
{{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}}
, and the last equality is a consequence of the Weyl denominator formula.
Especially, for
\lambda\in\mathsf{P}^{\mathsf{k}}_{{\mathrm{cl}},+}
, we have
{\mathcal{D}}_{\lambda}>0
, referring to it as the
quantum dimension
of
L(\lambda)
, which we denote by

\operatorname{qdim}_{\mathsf{k}}L(\lambda)
.
If
\lambda\in\mathsf{P}_{{\mathrm{cl}}}^{\mathsf{k}}
is null, then
{\mathcal{D}}_{\lambda}=0
.
It turns out that
\operatorname{qdim}_{\mathsf{k}}:K_{0}(\operatorname{Rep}(\widehat{{\mathfrak{g}}}_{0}^{\prime},\mathsf{k}))\to{\mathbb{R}}
, extended linearly from the values of
\operatorname{qdim}_{\mathsf{k}}L(\lambda)
for
\lambda\in\mathsf{P}^{\mathsf{k}}_{{\mathrm{cl}},+}
, is a ring homomorphism.
This can be seen from the Verlinde formula (
2.15
), and the unitarity (
2.12
) of the
S
-matrix.
Furthermore, any positive element in
K_{0}(\operatorname{Rep}(\widehat{{\mathfrak{g}}}_{0}^{\prime},\mathsf{k}))
has a positive quantum dimension.

3.
The categories
\mathscr{C}_{\ell}
over quantum affine algebras

Let
{\mathfrak{g}}
be an affine Lie algebra of untwisted type.
In this subsection, we consider a certain subcategory of the category of finite-dimensional integrable
U^{\prime}_{q}({\mathfrak{g}})
-modules, which was introduced by Hernandez and Leclerc in
[
16
,
17
]
from the viewpoint of cluster algebras.

Let
{{\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathrm{fin}}}
be the simple Lie algebra of finite simply-laced type associated with
U^{\prime}_{q}({\mathfrak{g}})
(see
[
24
]
and
[
25
, Section 6.1]
),
{\mathsf{A}}_{{\mathrm{fin}}}
the Cartan matrix of
{{\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathrm{fin}}}
and
I_{\mathrm{fin}}
the index set of
{\mathsf{A}}_{{\mathrm{fin}}}
.
A
Q
-datum
\mathcal{Q}=(\mathsf{D},\sigma,\xi)
is a triple consisting of the
Dynkin diagram

\mathsf{D}
of
{{\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathrm{fin}}}
, the
folding automorphism

\sigma
between
{{\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathrm{fin}}}
and
{{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}}
, and a

The folding automorphism

induces a surjection

. Define

\sigma^{\circ}({\mathfrak{g}})\mathbin{:=}\{(\imath,p)\in{I_{\mathrm{fin}}}\times{\mathbb{Z}}\mid p-\xi(\imath)\in 2d_{\imath}{\mathbb{Z}}\},

We denote by

-modules.
It is known that the isomorphism classes of simple modules in

Because

Let

be the Laurent polynomial ring in formal variables

We write a monomial

For a dominant monomial

and

We define

4.
Verlinde rings and cluster algebras

4.1.
Positivity conjecture

Let
{{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}}
be a simple Lie algebra, and
\mathsf{k}\geqslant 2
be a positive integer.
From now on, we write
\ell(\mathsf{k}):=\mathsf{k}-1
.
We consider the ring homomorphism
\varphi_{\mathsf{k}}:K_{0}(\operatorname{Rep}{U_{q}^{\prime}(\widehat{{\mathfrak{g}}}_{0})})\to K_{0}(\operatorname{Rep}(\widehat{{\mathfrak{g}}}_{0}^{\prime},\mathsf{k}))
, defined as the composition of the homomorphisms
(
3.2
), (
2.6
), and (
2.17
):

(4.1)

\varphi_{\mathsf{k}}:K_{0}(\operatorname{Rep}{U_{q}^{\prime}(\widehat{{\mathfrak{g}}}_{0})})\xrightarrow{\operatorname{res}}K_{0}(\operatorname{Rep}{U_{q}({{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}})})\xrightarrow{\cong}K_{0}(\operatorname{Rep}{{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}})\xrightarrow{\pi_{\mathsf{k}}}K_{0}(\operatorname{Rep}(\widehat{{\mathfrak{g}}}_{0}^{\prime},\mathsf{k})).

Note that one can define the
{\mathbb{R}}
-valued map
\operatorname{qdim}_{\mathsf{k}}
on each of the rings
K_{0}(\operatorname{Rep}{U_{q}^{\prime}(\widehat{{\mathfrak{g}}}_{0})})
,
K_{0}(\operatorname{Rep}{U_{q}({{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}})})
, and
K_{0}(\operatorname{Rep}{{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}})

by pulling back
\operatorname{qdim}_{\mathsf{k}}
from
K_{0}(\operatorname{Rep}(\widehat{{\mathfrak{g}}}_{0}^{\prime},\mathsf{k}))
.

In this subsection, we present our main conjecture regarding the positivity in the Verlinde ring
K_{0}(\operatorname{Rep}(\widehat{{\mathfrak{g}}}_{0}^{\prime},\mathsf{k}))
under the ring homomorphism
\varphi_{\mathsf{k}}
on the Grothendieck ring of the monoidal subcategory
\mathscr{C}_{\ell(\mathsf{k})}
of
\operatorname{Rep}{U_{q}^{\prime}(\widehat{{\mathfrak{g}}}_{0})}
.

In
[
34
]
, we studied the image of the KR modules under this map
\varphi_{\mathsf{k}}
.
Let us briefly review a conjecture formulated there.
For each
(i,m,p)\in I_{0}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}\times{\mathbb{Z}}
, let
W^{(i)}_{m,p}
be the corresponding KR module.
By restriction, we get a finite-dimensional
U_{q}({\mathfrak{g}})
-module
\operatorname{res}W^{(i)}_{m,p}
, whose isomorphism class is independent of
p
, and
for simplicity, we use the notation
\varphi_{\mathsf{k}}([W^{(i)}_{m}])
.

For
i\in I_{0}
, let
t_{i}:=d/d_{i}\in\{1,d\}
, where
d_{i}
is given in (
3.1
), and
d={\mathop{\mathrm{max}}}_{i\in I_{0}}\{d_{i}\}\in\{1,2,3\}
.
Alternatively, as noted in the Introduction,
t_{i}
is the ratio of the norm of the highest root to that of the simple root
\alpha_{i}
.
Thus,
t_{i}=2/(\alpha_{i}\mid\alpha_{i})_{0}
, with the bilinear form
(\cdot\mid\cdot)_{0}
normalized such that
(\theta\mid\theta)_{0}=2
.
We have the following conjecture:

Conjecture 4.1
.

[
34
, Conjecture 3.10]

For
i\in I_{0}
, the following properties hold :

(1)

\varphi_{\mathsf{k}}([W^{(i)}_{m}])
is positive if
0\leqslant m\leqslant t_{i}\mathsf{k}
.

(2)

\varphi_{\mathsf{k}}([W^{(i)}_{m}])
is a unit if
m=0
or
m=t_{i}\mathsf{k}
.

(3)

\varphi_{\mathsf{k}}([W^{(i)}_{m}])=0
if
m=t_{i}\mathsf{k}+1
.

For a more precise version of the conjecture, including a detailed description of the units and the values of
\varphi_{\mathsf{k}}([W^{(i)}_{m}])
for
m>t_{i}\mathsf{k}+1
, see
[
34
]
.
Especially, the conjecture above implies the following conjecture, originally proposed by Kirillov
[
27
]
and later expanded by Kuniba, Nakanishi and Suzuki
[
33
, Conjecture 14.2]
.

Conjecture 4.2

(Theorem in some cases stated below)
.

For
i\in I_{0}
, the following properties hold :

(1)

\operatorname{qdim}_{\mathsf{k}}W^{(i)}_{m}
is positive if
0\leqslant m\leqslant t_{i}\mathsf{k}
.

(2)

\operatorname{qdim}_{\mathsf{k}}W^{(i)}_{m}=1
if
m=0
or
m=t_{i}\mathsf{k}
.

(3)

\operatorname{qdim}_{\mathsf{k}}W^{(i)}_{m}=0
if
m=t_{i}\mathsf{k}+1
.

This quantum dimension version of the conjecture is known to hold for classical types
[
34
]
, as well as for type
E_{6}
(for all
i\in I_{0}
) and types
E_{7}
and
E_{8}
(for certain nodes depending on
i\in I_{0}
)
[
13
]
.
See also
[
35
]
for a discussion on the case of
\mathsf{k}=1
for the exceptional types
E_{6}
,
E_{7}
, and
E_{8}
and its applications.

Proposition 4.3
.

Assume that Conjecture
4.1
or Conjecture
4.2
holds for all
i\in I_{0}
.
Then
\operatorname{qdim}_{\mathsf{k}}W^{(i)}_{m}>1
for
i\in I_{0}
and
1\leqslant m\leqslant t_{i}\mathsf{k}-1
.

Proof.

For
0\leqslant m\leqslant t_{i}\mathsf{k}
,
let
\mathcal{D}^{(i)}_{m}=\operatorname{qdim}_{\mathsf{k}}W^{(i)}_{m}
be the quantum dimension of
W^{(i)}_{m}
at level
\mathsf{k}
, which must be positive under the given assumption.
Then the
Q
-system
[
15
]

(4.2)

(Q^{(i)}_{m})^{2}-Q^{(i)}_{m+1}Q^{(i)}_{m-1}=\prod_{j:a_{i,j}<0}\prod_{n=0}^{-a_{i,j}-1}Q^{(j)}_{\bigl\lfloor\frac{a_{j,i}m-n}{a_{i,j}}\bigr\rfloor},\quad i\in I_{0},m\geqslant 1

satisfied by the characters of KR modules, implies that the finite sequence
(\mathcal{D}^{(i)}_{m})_{0\leqslant m\leqslant t_{i}\mathsf{k}}

Since the ring

has a cluster algebra structure of finite cluster type, it contains only finitely many cluster variables and finitely many exchange relations. We begin by briefly outlining the strategy for proving Theorem

, noting that this approach can also be applied to other types, as long as that they possess a cluster algebra structure of finite cluster type.

Let

denote the set of cluster variables of
K_{0}(\mathscr{C}_{\ell(\mathsf{k})})
, where
J
is a finite set. Then,

\varphi_{\mathsf{k}}(x_{j})=\sum_{\lambda\in\mathsf{P}^{\mathsf{k}}_{{\mathrm{cl}},+}}c(j,\lambda)[L(\lambda)]\in\operatorname{Rep}(\widehat{{\mathfrak{g}}}_{0}^{\prime},\mathsf{k})

for some integers

. Our goal is to show that
c(j,\lambda)
is non-negative for every pair
(j,\lambda)\in J\times\mathsf{P}^{\mathsf{k}}_{{\mathrm{cl}},+}
and that for a fixed
j
, not all
c(j,\lambda)
are zero.

If a cluster variable is attached to a KR module, we can explicitly determine its image in the Verlinde ring under the map
\varphi_{\mathsf{k}}
. Specifically, the decomposition of a KR module into a direct sum of irreducible
U_{q}({{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}})
-modules can be obtained using the Fermionic formula
[
14
]
.
Moreover, the image under the homomorphism
\pi_{\mathsf{k}}:K_{0}(\operatorname{Rep}{{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}})\to K_{0}(\operatorname{Rep}(\widehat{{\mathfrak{g}}}_{0}^{\prime},\mathsf{k}))
can be algorithmically determined by employing the affine Weyl group as explained in subsection
2.3
.

In general, the main challenge in computing the image under
\varphi_{\mathsf{k}}
lies in the absence of an efficient method for decomposing a
U_{q}^{\prime}(\widehat{{\mathfrak{g}}}_{0})
-module into a direct sum of irreducible
U_{q}({\mathfrak{g}})
-modules when it is not a KR module. In some cases, we may compute the
q
-character, truncated
q
-character, or classical character as a
U_{q}({\mathfrak{g}})
-module
using the Frenkel-Mukhin algorithm
[
11
]
, or by exploiting the cluster algebra structure as in
[
17
]
.
However, these methods are often impractical, particularly because the modules involved, especially in exceptional types, tend to have very large dimensions. This leads to considerable computational complexity, making character calculations and module decompositions inefficient and difficult to execute in practice.

Instead, we can impose the exchange relations as constraints on the coefficients
\{c(j,\lambda)\}
. Since
\varphi_{\mathsf{k}}
is a ring homomorphism, applying it to the exchange relations yields a finite system of polynomial equations involving
\{c(j,\lambda)\}
.
The coefficients associated with a KR module can be determined directly. From there, it is sufficient to verify that this partial integral solution can be uniquely extended to an integral solution for the entire system of equations. Afterward, we can confirm whether all
c(j,\lambda)
are non-negative.

The exchange relations generally lead to a large system of non-linear Diophantine equations for
c(j,\lambda)
. Instead of solving the entire system at once, we iteratively solve only the linear equations that arise after substituting the known values of
c(j,\lambda)
into all exchange relations at each step.
Remarkably, only two iterations are needed for all types
E_{6}
,
E_{7}
, and
E_{8}
.
A Mathematica implementation of this approach, together with all cluster variables and exchange relations, is accessible at
