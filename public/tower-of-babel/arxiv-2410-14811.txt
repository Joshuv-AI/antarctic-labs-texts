Performance Estimation for Smooth and Strongly Convex Sets
Alan Luner, Benjamin Grimmer

Abstract
We extend recent computer-assisted design and analysis techniques for first-order optimization over structured functions—known as performance estimation—to apply to structured sets. We prove “interpolation theorems” for smooth and strongly convex sets with interior point conditions and bounded diameter, showing a wide range of extremal questions amount to structured mathematical programs. Prior function interpolation theorems are recovered as a limit of our set interpolation theory. Our theory provides finite-dimensional formulations of performance estimation problems for algorithms utilizing separating hyperplane oracles and linear optimization oracles of smooth/strongly convex sets. As applications of this computer-assisted machinery, we identify a minimax optimal separating hyperplane method and areas for improvement in the theory of Frank-Wolfe and non-Lipschitz Smooth Optimization.

Introduction

Given a proposed algorithm, the study of its worst-case convergence guarantees over some family of problem instances can be framed as a meta-optimization problem. In this work, we will consider examples of first-order optimization methods applied to problems from a structured family of objective functions and constraint sets. Given some measure of performance (e.g., final objective gap), the exact worst-case performance of the method corresponds to computing the maximum of this measure at the algorithm’s output over all problem instances. This is known as the Performance Estimation Problem (PEP). In general, optimizing over a family of functions and sets is an infinite-dimensional problem, typically beyond direct approach. Surprisingly, recent advances have shown that PEPs can often be computer-solved and have led to several state-of-the-art results in the design and analysis of optimization algorithms.

Performance estimation was first proposed by Drori and Teboulle in
[
1
]
, where tractable relaxations of these worst-case PEPs were considered. Shortly afterward, the “interpolation theorems” of Taylor, Hendrickx, and Glineur
[
2

Specifically, their function interpolation results showed that the infinite-dimensional optimization problem of finding a worst-case problem instance for

[

2.1
Preliminaries on Smooth and Strongly Convex Functions

Consider a closed convex function
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
. We denote
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
and
\|\cdot\|
for the Euclidean inner product and associated norm. We denote the domain of
f
by
\mathrm{dom\ }f
and its subdifferential at some
x\in\mathrm{dom\ }f
by
\partial f(x)=\{g\mid f(y)\geq f(x)+\langle g,y-x\rangle\quad\forall y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}
. Each element
g\in\partial f(x)
is referred to as a subgradient and must be the gradient
\nabla f(x)
when
f
is differentiable.

Typically,
L
-smoothness of a function is defined as
\nabla f(x)
being
L
-Lipschitz. For the sake of developing other symmetries, here we instead (equivalently) define a closed convex
f
as being
L
-smooth
if for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
g\in\partial f(x)
,

f(y)\leq f(x)+\langle g,y-x\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\|y-x\|^{2}\ .

(2.1)

That is, each subgradient provides a quadratic upper bound on
f
. Mirroring this, we say
f
is
\mu
-strongly convex
if for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
g\in\partial f(x)
,

f(y)\geq f(x)+\langle g,y-x\rangle+\frac{\mu}{2}\|y-x\|^{2}\ .

(2.2)

That is, each subgradient provides a quadratic lower bound on
f
.
We allow the extreme cases of
L,\mu\in\{0,\infty\}
, taking limits above. Every convex
f
is vacuously
\infty
-smooth and
0
-strongly convex. The only
0
-smooth functions are linear and the only
\infty
-strongly convex functions are indicators of points.
Denote the set of all
\mu
-strongly convex and
L
-smooth functions
f
by
\mathcal{F}_{\mu,L}
.

As a motivating example of the performance estimation problem, consider minimizing some
f\in\mathcal{F}_{0,L}
via
N
steps of a gradient method, defined as

x_{i+1}=x_{i}-\frac{1}{L}\sum_{j=0}^{i}H_{i,j}\nabla f(x_{j})

(GM)

with initial point
x_{0}
and a lower triangular matrix
H
. Note this model captures simple methods like gradient descent via a diagonal
H
and more complex momentum methods like Nesterov’s accelerated method
[
27
]
and the optimal gradient method of
[
16
]
.
Suppose as an initial condition, we are guaranteed the initial distance to a minimizer
\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\|
is bounded above by
R
. Then the infinite-dimensional PEP of seeking a problem instance with objective in
\mathcal{F}_{0,L}
achieving the largest final objective gap can be formulated as

p_{\mathrm{S}}(L,R):=\begin{cases}\max\limits_{x_{i},f}&f(x_{N})-f(x_{\star})\\
\mathrm{s.t.}&f\in\mathcal{F}_{0,L}\\
&x_{i+1}=x_{i}-\frac{1}{L}\sum_{j=0}^{i}H_{i,j}\nabla f(x_{j})\\
&\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\|\leq R,\quad\nabla f(x_{\star})=0\ .\end{cases}

(2.3)

Note that aside from the constraint
f\in\mathcal{F}_{0,L}
, this optimization problem only depends on
f
via its function value and gradient at finitely many points, namely
\{x_{0},\dots,x_{N},x_{\star}\}
.
Denote these values by
f_{i}=f(x_{i})
and
g_{i}=\nabla f(x_{i})
for each
i\in\{0,\dots,N,\star\}
.
The following definition of function interpolation from
[
2
]
allows us to reformulate into these more limited quantities:

Definition 2.1
.

Consider a set of observations
S=\{(x_{i},g_{i},f_{i})\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
where
x_{i},g_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
f_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
for all
i\in\mathcal{I}
. The set
S
is
\mathcal{F}_{\mu,L}
-interpolable
if there exists a function
f\in\mathcal{F}_{\mu,L}
such that
g_{i}\in\partial f(x_{i})
and
f(x_{i})=f_{i}
for all
i\in\mathcal{I}
.

Function interpolability describes whether there exists an appropriate function that matches the given finite set of function values and subgradients and interpolates between them (See
Figure

2
). In terms of the working example (
2.3
), this can then be expressed finitely as

p_{\mathrm{S}}(L,R)=\begin{cases}\max\limits_{x_{i},g_{i},f_{i}}&f_{N}-f_{\star}\\
\mathrm{s.t.}&\{(x_{i},g_{i},f_{i})\}_{i\in\{0,\dots,N,\star\}}\text{ is }\mathcal{F}_{0,L}\text{-interpolable}\\
&x_{i+1}=x_{i}-\frac{1}{L}\sum_{j=0}^{i}H_{i,j}g_{j}\\
&\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\|\leq R,\quad g_{\star}=0\ .\end{cases}

(2.4)

Although finite, this formulation is still not particularly tractable as the interpolability constraint is, at first glance, not approachable. The critical insight of Taylor et al.
[
2
, Theorem 4]
was establishing explicit necessary and sufficient conditions for function interpolability.

Theorem 2.2
.

(​​
[
2
, Theorem 4]
)

A set
S=\{(x_{i},g_{i},f_{i})\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
is
\mathcal{F}_{\mu,L}
-interpolable if and only if the following quadratic condition holds for all
i,j\in\mathcal{I}
:

f_{i}-f_{j}+\langle g_{i},x_{j}-x_{i}\rangle+\frac{1}{2(1-\mu/L)}\left(\frac{1}{L}\|g_{i}-g_{j}\|^{2}+\mu\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|^{2}-\frac{2\mu}{L}\langle g_{i}-g_{j},x_{i}-x_{j}\rangle\right)\leq 0\ .

(2.5)

Plugging this into (
2.4
) yields an explicit QCQP exactly describing gradient descent’s worst-case behavior over the considered family of structured functions. Semidefinite programming lifting techniques (and duality) can be further used to gain insights from this new formulation.

Remark 1
.

Throughout the literature and in this work, the
equivalent
reformulation of the QCQP into a semidefinite program depends on an assumption of high dimension (e.g.,
d\geq N+2
). This assumption is only required for the SDP to be an equivalent problem, rather than a relaxation. For any
d
, the resulting SDP provides a valid, dimension-independent upper bound.

It is worth noting that there are many collections of necessary constraints on the set of observation data
S
. Any standard inequality for
\mu
-strongly convex,
L
-smooth functions must necessarily hold: for example, the definitions (
2.1
) and (
2.2
). However, as Taylor et al.
[
2
]
highlight, these other inequalities prove not to be sufficient for interpolation. Hence, identification of the right inequalities, e.g., (
2.5
), is key for the “if and only if” nature of interpolation theory and, consequently, its power in enabling computer assistance. Similarly, in
Section

3
, we find that while there are many necessary constraints for set interpolability, a careful selection is needed for sufficiency.

2.2
Preliminaries on Smooth and Strongly Convex Sets

We now switch our focus to smoothness and strong convexity of sets. These notions mirror their function counterparts; see
[
28
,
29
,
30
]
as classic references. These properties are, however, quite under-explored in the contemporary first-order method literature compared to their function counterparts, which are the backbone of much modern convergence theory. In part, this work aims to provide tools to eventually alleviate this discrepancy.

Consider a nonempty closed convex set
C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
We denote the interior of a set
C
by
\mathrm{int\ }C
and its boundary by
\mathrm{bdry\ }C
. We denote the normal cone of
C
at some
z\in\mathrm{bdry\ }C
by
N_{C}(z)=\{v\mid\langle v,x-z\rangle\leq 0\quad\forall x\in C\}
and refer to individual elements as normal vectors. Let
B(x,r)
be the closed ball of radius
r
centered at
x
. We define
\mathrm{diam}(C)=\sup\{\|x-y\|\mid x,y\in C\}
and the
\delta
-interior of
C
by
\mathrm{int}_{\delta}C=\{x\mid B(x,\delta)\subseteq C\}
. Note
\mathrm{int}_{0}C=C
.
Lastly, we define the Minkowski sum of two sets
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
as

C_{1}+C_{2}=\{x+y\mid x\in C_{1},y\in C_{2}\}\ .

We now define smoothness and strong convexity with respect to sets. For a more full discussion of the equivalent definitions of these properties and their analogs with smooth and strongly convex functions, we refer readers to
[
31
, Section 2]
. Overall, one can recover the analogous definitions for functions by replacing unit normal vectors with gradients, bounding balls with bounding quadratics, and sets with epigraphs.
A set
C
is
\beta
-smooth
if for any
z\in\mathrm{bdry\ }C
and unit vector
n\in N_{C}(z)
,

B(z-\frac{1}{\beta}n,\frac{1}{\beta})\subseteq C\ .

A set
C
is
\alpha
-strongly convex
if for any
z\in\mathrm{bdry\ }C
and unit vector
n\in N_{C}(z)
,

B(z-\frac{1}{\alpha}n,\frac{1}{\alpha})\supseteq C\ .

Again, we allow the limiting cases of
\alpha,\beta\in\{0,\infty\}
. Every convex set is
0
-strongly convex and
\infty
-smooth. The only
\infty
-strongly convex sets are singletons and the only
0
-smooth sets are halfspaces.
Let
\mathcal{C}_{\alpha,\beta,D}
denote the set of all closed convex sets
C
that are
\alpha
-strongly convex,
\beta
-smooth, and have
\mathrm{diam}(C)\leq D
. See an illustration of smooth and strongly convex sets in
Figure

.

Our discussion of set smoothness and strong convexity throughout will focus on these properties at full dimension. One could consider smoothness/strong convexity with respect to a fixed subspace. Through a simple change of variables, our definitions and theory could be applied.

3
Main Result: Set Interpolation Theory

This section presents and proves our main results on set interpolability.

We first define a set of conditions that will be necessary throughout our theory. Consider a set of observations
S=(\{(z_{i},v_{i})\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}},\{(x_{k},\delta_{k})\}_{k\in\mathcal{K}})
and denote the associated unit normal vectors by
n_{i}=\frac{v_{i}}{{}\|v_{i}\|}
for all
i\in\mathcal{I}
.
Due to the added complexity of our set interpolation model, our conditions involve the introduction of auxiliary parameters
\{w_{k}\}_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
along with a constant factor
\lambda\in[\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}},1]
, typically equal to one, enabling strengthened versions of these conditions.
We find that for any
\mathcal{C}_{\alpha,\beta,D}
-interpolable observation data
S=(\{(z_{i},v_{i})\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}},\{(x_{k},\delta_{k})\}_{k\in\mathcal{K}})
, there must exist auxiliary parameters
\{w_{k}\}_{k\in\mathcal{K}}
and a choice of
\lambda
such that for all
i,j\in\mathcal{I}
and
k,l\in\mathcal{K}
,

\displaystyle\|z_{i}-\frac{1}{\alpha}n_{i}-(z_{j}-\frac{1}{\beta}n_{j})\|\leq\frac{1}{\gamma}

(Interp1)

\displaystyle\|z_{i}-\frac{1}{\alpha}n_{i}-w_{k}\|\leq\frac{1}{\gamma}-s_{k}

(Interp2)

\displaystyle\|x_{k}-w_{k}\|\leq\frac{1}{\beta}-\delta_{k}+s_{k}

(Interp3)

\displaystyle\|z_{i}-\frac{1}{\beta}n_{i}-(z_{j}-\frac{1}{\beta}n_{j})\|\leq\lambda(D-\frac{2}{\beta})

(Interp4)

\displaystyle\|z_{i}-\frac{1}{\beta}n_{i}-w_{k}\|\leq\lambda(D-\frac{2}{\beta})-s_{k}

(Interp5)

\displaystyle\|w_{k}-w_{l}\|\leq\lambda(D-\frac{2}{\beta})-s_{k}-s_{l}

(Interp6)

where
\gamma=(\frac{1}{\alpha}-\frac{1}{\beta})^{-1}
and
s_{k}=\max\{0,\delta_{k}-\frac{1}{\beta}\}
for all
k\in\mathcal{K}
. In the case
\alpha=\beta
, we will use the convention that
\frac{1}{\gamma}=0
.
As a shorthand, we write
S\in\mathrm{Interp}(\alpha,\beta,D;\lambda)
if
\{w_{k}\}_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
exist satisfying all six conditions.

We can briefly motivate our auxiliary parameters

and
\lambda
. The values

arise from interpolating a smooth set, which can be decomposed into

represents a corresponding point in

\lambda

and

results in more computationally difficult PEP formulations.

Remark 2

(On Indicator Function Formulation)
.

Instead of using boundary points and normal vectors, one can equivalently define set interpolation in terms of indicator functions. For any set
C
and indicator
\iota_{C}=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }x\in C\\
\infty&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

one has that
v_{i}\in N_{C}(z_{i})
if and only if
v_{i}\in\partial\iota_{C}(z_{i})
. Similarly, one can define a
\delta
-interior point as some
x
such that
\iota_{C}(x+\delta\zeta)=0
for all
\|\zeta\|\leq 1
. We focus on the normal-vector formulation to align with our later applications.

Remark 3

(On the Computational Cost of Auxiliary Variables)
.

The function interpolation result of
Theorem

2.2
provides a direct means to algebraically check if a collection of observation data
\{(x_{i},f_{i},g_{i})\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
is interpolable. One must check
O(|\mathcal{I}|^{2})
quadratic inequalities. Our set interpolation theory does not present such an easy algebraic check. One must determine whether
\{w_{k}\}_{k\in\mathcal{K}}
exist satisfying the needed conditions. This corresponds to solving a second-order cone program with
O(|\mathcal{K}|)
variables and
O(|\mathcal{I}|^{2}+|\mathcal{K}|^{2})
constraints. However, in cases with no or very few interior point conditions (
|\mathcal{K}|=0
or small), or where
\beta=\infty
, this cost reduces back to checking
O(|\mathcal{I}|^{2})
inequalities.

Remark 5

(On the Special Case of Interpolating with Strongly Convex Sets)
.

If we take
\beta
to
\infty
, that is, entirely relaxing the requirement of smoothness, then our interpolation conditions reduce to

\displaystyle\|z_{i}-\frac{1}{\alpha}n_{i}-z_{j}\|\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}

(3.1)

\displaystyle\|z_{i}-\frac{1}{\alpha}n_{i}-x_{k}\|\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}-\delta_{k}

(3.2)

\displaystyle\|z_{i}-z_{j}\|\leq\lambda D

(3.3)

\displaystyle\|z_{i}-x_{k}\|\leq\lambda D-\delta_{k}

(3.4)

\displaystyle\|x_{k}-x_{l}\|\leq\lambda D-\delta_{k}-\delta_{l}

(3.5)

with no more dependence on auxiliary parameters
\{w_{k}\}_{k\in\mathcal{K}}
.
As an immediate corollary, we have the following result for strongly convex sets.

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
S=(\{(z_{i},v_{i})\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}},\{(x_{k},\delta_{k})\}_{k\in\mathcal{K}})
. If
S
is
\mathcal{C}_{\alpha,\infty,D}
-interpolable, then for all
i,j\in\mathcal{I}
and
k,l\in\mathcal{K}
, (
3.1
-
3.5
) are satisfied for
\lambda=1
. Conversely, if for all
i,j\in\mathcal{I}
and
k,l\in\mathcal{K}
, (
3.1
-
3.5
) are satisfied for
\lambda=1/\sqrt{2}
, then
S
is
\mathcal{C}_{\alpha,\infty,D}
-interpolable.

Remark 6

(On the Special Case of Interpolating with Smooth, Convex Sets)
.

We can similarly relax the strong convexity condition, taking
\alpha
to zero and deriving simpler conditions for smooth convex interpolation from
Theorem

3.3
. Suppose that
\|z_{i}-\frac{1}{\alpha}n_{i}-(z_{j}-\frac{1}{\beta}n_{j})\|\leq\frac{1}{\gamma}
. Then

\displaystyle\langle z_{i}-\frac{1}{\alpha}n_{i}-z_{j}+\frac{1}{\beta}n_{j},z_{i}-\frac{1}{\alpha}n_{i}-z_{j}+\frac{1}{\beta}n_{j}\rangle\leq\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\quad

\displaystyle\|z_{i}-\frac{1}{\beta}n_{i}-z_{j}+\frac{1}{\beta}n_{j}\|^{2}-2\langle\frac{1}{\gamma}n_{i},z_{i}-\frac{1}{\beta}n_{i}-z_{j}+\frac{1}{\beta}n_{j}\rangle+\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\quad

\displaystyle\langle n_{i},z_{j}-\frac{1}{\beta}n_{j}-z_{i}+\frac{1}{\beta}n_{i}\rangle\leq-\frac{\gamma}{2}\|z_{i}-\frac{1}{\beta}n_{i}-z_{j}+\frac{1}{\beta}n_{j}\|^{2}\ .

Taking the limit as
\alpha\to 0
and correspondingly
\gamma\to 0
, this inequality becomes

\langle n_{i},z_{j}-\frac{1}{\beta}n_{j}-z_{i}+\frac{1}{\beta}n_{i}\rangle\leq 0

(3.6)

in place of (
Interp1
).
Similarly, for
w_{k}
, we obtain

\langle n_{i},w_{k}+s_{k}n_{i}-z_{i}+\frac{1}{\beta}n_{i}\rangle\leq 0

(3.7)

in place of (
Interp2
). We can then state a corollary for smooth sets.

Corollary 3.5
.

Let
S=(\{(z_{i},v_{i})\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}},\{(x_{k},\delta_{k})\}_{k\in\mathcal{K}})
.
S
is
\mathcal{C}_{0,\beta,D}
-interpolable if and only if there exist
\{w_{k}\}_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that for all
i,j\in\mathcal{I}
and
k,l\in\mathcal{K}
, (
3.6
) and (
3.7
) along with (
Interp3
)-(
Interp6
) are satisfied for
\lambda=1
.

Taking the limit as
\beta
tends to
\infty

yields a final result for nonsmooth, non-strongly convex interpolation.

Corollary 3.6
.

A set
S=(\{(z_{i},v_{i})\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}},\{(x_{k},\delta_{k})\}_{k\in\mathcal{K}})
is
\mathcal{C}_{0,\infty,D}
-interpolable if and only if for all
i,j\in\mathcal{I}
and
k,l\in\mathcal{K}
,

\displaystyle\langle n_{i},z_{j}-z_{i}\rangle\leq 0

\displaystyle\langle n_{i},x_{k}+\delta_{k}n_{i}-z_{i}\rangle\leq 0

\displaystyle\|x_{k}-x_{l}\|\leq D-\delta_{k}-\delta_{l}

\displaystyle\|z_{i}-x_{k}\|\leq D-\delta_{k}

\displaystyle\|z_{i}-z_{j}\|\leq D

where
n_{i}=\frac{v_{i}}{{}\|v_{i}\|}
for all
i\in\mathcal{I}
.

3.1
Proof of Theorem
3.1

Suppose that
S
is
\mathcal{C}_{\alpha,\beta,D}
-interpolable. Then there exists some
C\in\mathcal{C}_{\alpha,\beta,D}
such that
v_{i}\in N_{C}(z_{i})
,
z_{i}\in C
, and
x_{k}\in\mathrm{int}_{\delta_{k}}C
for all
i\in\mathcal{I},k\in\mathcal{K}
. From
Lemma

2.6
, we know that
C=C_{0}+B(0,\frac{1}{\beta})
for some
\gamma
-strongly convex set
C_{0}
, where
\frac{1}{\gamma}+\frac{1}{\beta}=\frac{1}{\alpha}
. In particular, as shown in
[
31
]
, we can write
C_{0}
explicitly with the Minkowski difference
C_{0}=C-B(0,\frac{1}{\beta}):=\{x\mid x+B(0,\frac{1}{\beta})\subseteq C\}
.

As a first intermediate result, we claim for each
i\in\mathcal{I}
,

v_{i}\in N_{C_{0}}(z_{i}-\frac{v_{i}}{{\beta}\|v_{i}\|})\ .

(3.8)

This follows since
v_{i}\in N_{C}(z_{i})
implies for all
y\in C
,
\langle v_{i},y-z_{i}\rangle\leq 0
. So noting that every
p\in C_{0}
has
p+\frac{v_{i}}{{\beta}\|v_{i}\|}\in C
, we have

\langle v_{i},p-(z_{i}-\frac{v_{i}}{{\beta}\|v_{i}\|})\rangle\leq 0
for all
p\in C_{0}
. From the definition of
C_{0}
,
z_{i}-\frac{v_{i}}{{\beta}\|v_{i}\|}\in C_{0}
. Together these yield (
3.8
).

Since
C_{0}
is
\gamma
-strongly convex, (
3.8
) implies that
C_{0}\subseteq B(z_{i}-\frac{v_{i}}{{\beta}\|v_{i}\|}-\frac{v_{i}}{{\gamma}\|v_{i}\|},\frac{1}{\gamma})
. Since
z_{j}-\frac{v_{j}}{{\beta}\|v_{j}\|}\in C_{0}
for all
j\in\mathcal{I}
, we have
z_{j}-\frac{v_{j}}{{\beta}\|v_{j}\|}\in B(z_{i}-\frac{v_{i}}{{\beta}\|v_{i}\|}-\frac{v_{i}}{{\gamma}\|v_{i}\|},\frac{1}{\gamma})
. So (
Interp1
) holds as

\|z_{i}-\frac{v_{i}}{{\alpha}\|v_{i}\|}-(z_{j}-\frac{v_{j}}{{\beta}\|v_{j}\|})\|=\|z_{i}-\frac{v_{i}}{{\beta}\|v_{i}\|}-\frac{v_{i}}{{\gamma}\|v_{i}\|}-(z_{j}-\frac{v_{j}}{{\beta}\|v_{j}\|})\|\leq\frac{1}{\gamma}\ .

Now, we verify (
Interp2
) and (
Interp3
) for each
k\in\mathcal{K}
through two cases:

Case 1:
x_{k}\in C_{0}
.
In this case, we let
w_{k}=x_{k}
. Suppose that
\delta_{k}>\frac{1}{\beta}
, so
s_{k}:=\max\{0,\delta_{k}-\frac{1}{\beta}\}=\delta_{k}-\frac{1}{\beta}
. We claim that
x_{k}\in\mathrm{int}_{s_{k}}C_{0}
. Suppose that for some
\zeta
,
x_{k}+(\delta_{k}-\frac{1}{\beta})\frac{{\zeta}}{\|{\zeta}\|}\notin C_{0}
. Then
x_{k}+(\delta_{k}-\frac{1}{\beta})\frac{{\zeta}}{\|{\zeta}\|}+\frac{1}{\beta}\frac{{\zeta}}{\|{\zeta}\|}=x_{k}+\delta_{k}\frac{{\zeta}}{\|{\zeta}\|}\notin C
. But since
x_{k}\in\mathrm{int}_{\delta_{k}}C
, this is a contradiction, so we must have
x_{k}\in\mathrm{int}_{s_{k}}C_{0}
. Now suppose
\delta_{k}\leq\frac{1}{\beta}
. Then
s_{k}=0
, so
x_{k}\in\mathrm{int}_{s_{k}}C_{0}
is true by assumption. Since
x_{k}=w_{k}
, we have shown that
w_{k}+B(0,s_{k})\subseteq C_{0}
. When combined with the previous fact that
C_{0}\subseteq B(z_{i}-\frac{v_{i}}{{\beta}\|v_{i}\|}-\frac{v_{i}}{{\gamma}\|v_{i}\|},\frac{1}{\gamma})
, it follows that

\|z_{i}-\frac{v_{i}}{{\alpha}\|v_{i}\|}-w_{k}\|\leq\frac{1}{\gamma}-s_{k}\ .

This is equivalent to (
Interp2
).
Finally, noting
\|w_{k}-x_{k}\|=0\leq\max\{0,\frac{1}{\beta}-\delta_{k}\}=\frac{1}{\beta}-\delta_{k}+s_{k}
, we conclude (
Interp3
) is satisfied.

Case 2:
x_{k}\notin C_{0}
.
Since
C=C_{0}+B(0,\frac{1}{\beta})
and
x_{k}\notin C_{0}
, we must have
\delta_{k}<\frac{1}{\beta}
. We therefore have
s_{k}=0
, and we set
w_{k}
as the orthogonal projection of
x_{k}
onto
C_{0}
and
\zeta=\frac{x_{k}-w_{k}}{\|x_{k}-w_{k}\|}
as the corresponding normal vector to
C_{0}
. Since
w_{k}\in C_{0}
, the same reasoning as above leveraging (
3.8
) implies (
Interp2
). We know
x_{k}\in\mathrm{int}_{\delta_{k}}C
, so it follows that
w_{k}+\zeta(\|w_{k}-x_{k}\|+\delta_{k})\in C
. However, since
\zeta\in N_{C_{0}}(w_{k})
, it follows that
\|w_{k}-x_{k}\|+\delta_{k}\leq\frac{1}{\beta}
. Recalling

, that is exactly (
Interp3
).

Finally, we verify the diameter conditions (
Interp4

Interp5
), and (
Interp6
). For all

and in either case above, we have that

. Hence the diameter bound of

from

2.5
yields the remaining three conditions for
\lambda=1

Verification that

Next, we consider

Verification of diameter bound

By

Jung’s theorem

Applied to the set

4
Separating Hyperplane Algorithms as an SDP

In the remainder of this paper, we provide three applications of our interpolation theorems. In this section, we consider a family of simple algorithms computing an element of a convex set by iteratively using a separating hyperplane oracle. Our interpolation theorems allow us to quantify the worst-case stopping time of such a method over any smooth and/or strongly convex set with an interior point via semidefinite programming. Motivated by and confirming our numerical results, we identify a simple minimax optimal separating hyperplane algorithm.

For any closed convex set
C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we denote the set of (unit) separating hyperplanes of
C
at some
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
by

SH_{C}(x)=\{n\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mid\|n\|=1,\ \langle n,y-x\rangle\leq 0\quad\forall y\in C\}\ .

Note this set is nonempty exactly when
x\notin\mathrm{int\ }C
. Here, our primary interest is in designing algorithms constructing a member of
\mathrm{int\ }C
using a sequence of separating hyperplane oracle queries. For example, this generalizes convex minimization, seeking an
\epsilon
-minimizer where each subgradient provides such a separating hyperplane to the
\epsilon
-level set. As a general form of method with
N
queries, consider any iteration producing points
x_{i}
via

x_{i+1}=x_{i}-\sum_{j=0}^{i}H_{i,j}n_{j},\qquad n_{i}\in SH_{C}(x_{i})\ ,\qquad\forall i=0,\dots,N-1

(4.1)

parameterized by the lower triangular matrix of stepsizes
H
. Note this method must halt once
x_{i}\in\mathrm{int\ }C
as
SH_{C}(x_{i})
is empty.

Given fixed
\alpha,\beta,\delta,R
, we consider the problem finding a feasible point to any set
C\in\mathcal{C}_{\alpha,\beta,\infty}
containing some
q\in\mathrm{int}_{\delta}C
and any initialization
x_{0}
with
\|x_{0}-q\|\leq R
. Since no diameter bound is enforced on
C
, our interpolation conditions are necessary and sufficient (i.e.,
Theorem

3.3
applies).

The question of whether a problem instance exists where an algorithm
H
can fail to construct some
x_{i}\in\mathrm{int\ }C
with
i=0,\dots,N
corresponds to

\exists x_{i},n_{i},q,C\qquad\mathrm{s.t.}\qquad\begin{cases}&C\in\mathcal{C}_{\alpha,\beta,\infty}\\
&B(q,\delta)\subseteq C\\
&n_{i}\in SH_{C}(x_{i})\\
&\|x_{0}-q\|\leq R\\
&x_{i+1}=x_{i}-\sum_{j=0}^{i}H_{i,j}n_{j}\ .\end{cases}

A successful algorithm design would be a selection of
H
such that no solution to the above system exists, as a failure to have
n_{k}\in SH_{C}(x_{k})
exist for some
k
implies
x_{k}\in\mathrm{int\ }C
. Noting that every separating hyperplane
n_{i}
must be normal to the set
C
at some
z_{i}
, this system can be rewritten in terms of interpolation with
\mathcal{I}=\{0,\dots,N\}
.
Theorem

3.3
enables this decision problem to be described as a system of quadratic inequalities using our first three interpolation conditions (
Interp1
)-(
Interp3
):

\exists x_{i},z_{i},n_{i},q,w\qquad\mathrm{s.t.}\qquad\begin{cases}&\|z_{i}-\frac{1}{\alpha}n_{i}-(z_{j}-\frac{1}{\beta}n_{j})\|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}}\\
&\|z_{i}-\frac{1}{\alpha}n_{i}-w\|^{2}\leq(\frac{1}{\gamma}-s)^{2}\\
&\|q-w\|^{2}\leq(\frac{1}{\beta}-\delta+s)^{2}\\
&\langle n_{i},z_{i}-x_{i}\rangle\leq 0\\
&\|n_{i}\|^{2}=1\\
&\|x_{0}-q\|^{2}\leq R^{2}\\
&x_{i+1}=x_{i}-\sum_{j=0}^{i}H_{i,j}n_{j}\end{cases}

(4.2)

where
\gamma=(\frac{1}{\alpha}-\frac{1}{\beta})^{-1}
and
s=\max\{0,\delta-\frac{1}{\beta}\}
.
Without loss of generality, we fix
q=0
.
Assuming
d\geq 2N+4
, a Gram matrix reformulation of this quadratic system yields an equivalent SDP feasibility problem with variable
G
capturing every quadratic term in the original variables
x_{0},z_{i},n_{i},w
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\Lambda=[x_{0}|z_{0}|z_{1}|\dots|z_{N}|n_{0}|n_{1}|\dots|n_{N}|w]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times(2N+4)}\ ,

\displaystyle G=\Lambda^{T}\Lambda\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{2N+4}\ .

In the case
d<2N+4
, this reformulation still represents a valid upper bound on our performance estimation problem, but it is no longer guaranteed to be tight (See
Remark

1
).
Section

B.1
presents this reformulation in full for the sake of completeness. Such reformulations are widespread in the existing PEP literature. Hence, we can efficiently certify whether a proposed separating hyperplane algorithm is guaranteed to construct a feasible point.

Consider the simple separating hyperplane method fixing
H
to be diagonal with constant value:

x_{i+1}=x_{i}-hn_{i}\ ,\qquad n_{i}\in SH_{C}(x_{i})\ ,\qquad\forall i=0,\dots,N-1\ .

(4.3)

For a given family of problem instances fixing
\alpha,\beta,\delta,R
, we can compute the number of iterations needed to guarantee a strictly feasible point by growing
N
until the corresponding SDP becomes infeasible. Denote this maximal number of steps needed to ensure an interior point is found by
N_{\mathrm{max}}
. Fixing
h=\max\{\delta,\frac{1}{\beta}\}
,
Figure

5
shows
N_{\mathrm{max}}
as
\delta
and
R
vary under this stepsize. From these numerical results, one can readily identify a formula
N_{\mathrm{max}}=\lfloor\frac{(R+h-\delta)^{2}}{h^{2}}\rfloor
.

Figure 5:
Numerical PEP result for
N_{\mathrm{max}}
given a constant stepsize
h=\max\{\delta,\frac{1}{\beta}\}
as
\delta
varies on the left and
R
on the right. In the second plot, the line for
\beta=10
is covered by
\beta=5
.

This simple method and numerically observed rate turn out to be the minimax optimal method and guarantee for any separating hyperplane method of the general form (
4.1
).
Section

B.2
presents an elementary proof of this fact.

Theorem 4.1
.

For any
C\in\mathcal{C}_{\alpha,\beta,D}
with
q\in\mathrm{int}_{\delta}C
and
\|x_{0}-q\|\leq R
, the iteration (
4.3
) with stepsize
h=\max\{\delta,\frac{1}{\beta}\}
must halt with
x_{i}\in\mathrm{int\ }C
by iteration
i\leq N_{\mathrm{max}}:=\lfloor\frac{(R+h-\delta)^{2}}{h^{2}}\rfloor
.
Moreover, there exists
C,q,x_{0}
and separating hyperplane oracle such that for any method (
4.1
) has
x_{0},\dots,x_{N_{\mathrm{max}}-1}\notin\mathrm{int\ }C
.

In this case, the proof of this minimax optimality theorem is sufficiently simple that one could have reached these conclusions without computer-assistance. Nevertheless, these results demonstrate the new possibilities of our set interpolation theory. The numerical insights generated in the following two sections escalate in complexity beyond what is reasonable to do “by-hand”.

5
Frank-Wolfe as an SDP with Nonconvex Constraints

In this section, we consider performance estimation of the Frank-Wolfe (conditional gradient) method on smooth and strongly convex sets, extending the prior analysis of Taylor, Hendrickx, and Glineur
[
3
]
in the case of convex sets.
This method applies to constrained optimization problems

\min_{x\in C}f(x)

(5.1)

where
f
is at least convex and
L
-smooth and
C
is at least a convex set with diameter at most
D
. At various points, we will consider problem classes with additional structures like
\mu
-strong convexity of
f
and
\alpha
-strong convexity and
\beta
-smoothness of
C
. We will let
x_{\star}\in C
denote a minimizer of (
5.1
) which exists by compactness.

Given a gradient oracle for
f
and a linear minimization oracle for
C
, the Frank-Wolfe method generates iterates
x_{1},\dots,x_{N}\in C
via

\displaystyle z_{k}\in\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{y\in C}\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),y\rangle

(FW)

\displaystyle x_{k+1}=(1-h_{k})x_{k}+h_{k}z_{k}

for some stepsize schedule
h=(h_{0},\dots,h_{N-1})
. Such stepsizes are sometimes called “open-loop”. Typically, stepsizes follow
h_{k}=\frac{2}{k+2}
or more generally
h_{k}=\frac{\ell}{k+\ell}
for some
\ell>0
. In all cases, we will assume that
0\leq h_{k}\leq 1
for all
k
, ensuring
x_{k+1}
remains a convex combination of
x_{0}
and all
z_{i}
.

5.1
Frank-Wolfe PEP Formulation and Computational Approaches

For a fixed algorithm, determined by fixing
h
above, its worst-case performance after
N
steps can be formulated as a performance estimation problem.
The worst-case minimum objective gap seen is given by

\displaystyle p_{\mathrm{FW}}(N,h;\mu,L,\alpha,\beta,D)=\begin{cases}\max\limits_{f,C,x_{0}}&\min\limits_{k=0,\dots,N}f(x_{k})-f(x_{\star})\\
\text{s.t.}\quad&x_{i+1}=(1-h_{i})x_{i}+h_{i}z_{i}\\
&z_{i}\in\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{i\in C}\langle\nabla f(x_{i}),y\rangle\\
&x_{0}\in C\\
&C\in\mathcal{C}_{\alpha,\beta,D}\\
&f\in\mathcal{F}_{\mu,L}\\
&x_{\star}\in\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{y\in C}f(y)\ .\end{cases}

(5.2)

From first-order optimality,
z_{i}\in\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{y\in C}\langle\nabla f(x_{i}),y\rangle
can be replaced by the equivalent condition
-\nabla f(x_{i})\in N_{C}(z_{i})
. Then our interpolation theory provides a reformulation as a solvable finite problem. See
Appendix

C
for further details supporting this reformulation. For ease of exposition, in what follows, we consider the specific case of optimizing
L
-smooth functions over
\beta
-smooth sets, but analogous derivations follow for any combination of smoothness and strong convexity of functions and sets. We will separately consider the cases
\nabla f(x_{\star})=0
and
\nabla f(x_{\star})\neq 0
, here focusing on
\nabla f(x_{\star})\neq 0
. This separation is further motivated by classic results in the literature
[
33
,
34
,
35
,
36
]
where the behavior of the Frank-Wolfe algorithm depends significantly on whether
x_{\star}\in\mathrm{int\ }C
.

Using both set and function interpolation theory, we can reformulate (
5.2
) as the following QCQP with additional separable nonconvex equality constraints. Letting
\theta=(h,\mu,L,\alpha,\beta,D)
, with
\alpha=0
for ease of presentation,

p_{\mathrm{FW}}(N;\theta)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\max\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{k},g_{k},f_{k}\\
w_{k},z_{i},n_{i}\end{subarray}}&f_{\mathrm{min}}-f_{\star}\\
\text{s.t.}&f_{\mathrm{min}}-f_{k}\leq 0&\forall k\in\mathcal{K}\\
&f_{k}-f_{l}+\langle g_{k},x_{l}-x_{k}\rangle+\frac{1}{2L}\|g_{k}-g_{l}\|^{2}\leq 0&\forall k,l\in\mathcal{K}_{\star}\\
&x_{i+1}=(1-h_{i})x_{i}+h_{i}z_{i}&\forall i\in\mathcal{I}\\
&\langle-g_{i},z_{j}-\frac{1}{\beta}n_{j}-(z_{i}-\frac{1}{\beta}n_{i})\rangle\leq 0&\forall i,j\in\mathcal{I}_{\star}\\
&\langle-g_{i},w_{k}-(z_{i}-\frac{1}{\beta}n_{i})\rangle\leq 0&\forall i\in\mathcal{I}_{\star},k\in\mathcal{K}_{\star}\\
&\|x_{k}-w_{k}\|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\beta^{2}}&\forall k\in\mathcal{K}_{\star}\\
&\|z_{i}-\frac{1}{\beta}n_{i}-(z_{j}-\frac{1}{\beta}n_{j})\|^{2}\leq(D-\frac{2}{\beta})^{2}&\forall i,j\in\mathcal{I}_{\star}\\
&\|z_{i}-\frac{1}{\beta}n_{i}-w_{k}\|^{2}\leq(D-\frac{2}{\beta})^{2}&\forall i\in\mathcal{I}_{\star},k\in\mathcal{K}_{\star}\\
&\|w_{k}-w_{l}\|^{2}\leq(D-\frac{2}{\beta})^{2}&\forall k,l\in\mathcal{K}_{\star}\\
&\langle g_{i},n_{i}\rangle\leq 0&\forall i\in\mathcal{I}_{\star}\\
&\|n_{i}\|^{2}=1&\forall i\in\mathcal{I}_{\star}\\
&\langle g_{i},n_{i}\rangle^{2}=\|g_{i}\|^{2}&\forall i\in\mathcal{I}_{\star}\ .\end{array}\right.

(5.3)

Note that the last three constraints above are equivalent to the condition
n_{i}=\frac{-g_{i}}{\|g_{i}\|}
.
In the case that
g_{\star}=0
, these constraints must be removed as
n_{\star}
is undefined, requiring a branch in our formulation based on
g_{\star}
.

One can repeat the “standard” Gram matrix reformulation approach discussed in
Section

4
. Assuming
d\geq 4N+6
, one can derive an equivalent problem with variables
G
and
F
as

\displaystyle F=[f_{0}|f_{1}|\dots|f_{N}|f_{\mathrm{min}}]\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times(N+2)}

\displaystyle\Lambda=[x_{0}|g_{\star}|g_{0}|g_{1}|\dots|g_{N}|z_{0}|\dots|z_{N-1}|n_{\star}|n_{0}|\dots|n_{N-1}|w_{\star}|w_{0}|\dots|w_{N}]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times(4N+6)}

\displaystyle G=\Lambda^{T}\Lambda\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{4N+6}\ .

We omit the restatement of this program after this standard change of variables. This Grammian change-of-variables reformulates all of the constraints of (
5.3
) as linear constraints in our new variables, with the exception of the equality constraint
\langle g_{i},n_{i}\rangle^{2}=\|g_{i}\|^{2}
which is quadratic in
G
. This constraint is nonconvex, and therefore cannot be expressed through a convex SDP.
We present three methods of solving or bounding the PEP (
5.3
), in order of increasing computational difficulty. In any case, we introduce the redundant constraints
\langle-g_{i},n_{j}\rangle\leq\langle-g_{i},n_{i}\rangle
for all
i,j\in\mathcal{I}_{\star}
, which prove useful in our relaxations below.

As a simplest approach, one can relax the constraint
\langle g_{i},n_{i}\rangle^{2}=\|g_{i}\|^{2}
to
\langle g_{i},n_{i}\rangle^{2}\leq\|g_{i}\|^{2}
, which is convex in
G
, providing a upper bound for (
5.2
). This is similar to the original performance estimation approach of Drori and Teboulle
[
1
]
. While excluded from our numerics, this relaxation enables computations of performance upper bounds up to larger values of
N
(
\sim 50
).

Alternatively, one can reformulate (
5.3
) by introducing new variables
\psi_{i}
to parameterize the nonconvex constraint.
Given
\psi=(\psi_{\star},\psi_{0},\dots,\psi_{N-1})\in\mathbb{R}^{N+1}_{\geq 0}
, one can define the parameterized problem
\hat{p}_{\mathrm{FW}}(\psi;\theta)
by adding the constraints
\|g_{i}\|^{2}=\psi_{i}
and
\langle-g_{i},n_{i}\rangle=\sqrt{\psi_{i}}
, which are linear in
G
. Finding a local solution to (
5.3
) then amounts to zeroth-order local maximization of

\hat{p}_{\mathrm{FW}}(\psi;\theta)
with respect to
\psi
, yielding a lower bound for (
5.3
). This approach was computationally feasible to run up to
N\leq 15
.

Finally, complementing the above local computation of lower bounds, one can globally optimize
(
5.3
)
via a simple branch-and-bound-type procedure. By Cauchy-Schwarz,
\langle-g_{i},n_{i}\rangle\leq\|g_{i}\|
. Then one can apply piecewise McCormick relaxations
[
37
,
38
]
to create a partition of convex relaxations of our equality condition
\langle-g_{i},n_{i}\rangle=\|g_{i}\|
. One can then globally optimize by iteratively refining this partition for each
i
. Given this procedure’s computational cost scales exponentially in
N
, our computations of such global upper bounds were limited to
N\leq 5
. When tractable, these numerical upper bounds (denoted by dots in our figures) always closely aligned with our numerical lower bounds, together certifying tight bounds on the true optimal value.

5.2
Survey of Smooth/Strongly Convex Settings for Acceleration

Bounds on Frank-Wolfe’s worst-case convergence rates are well-studied. For the most general setting of convex bounded
C
and
L
-smooth convex
f
, the best known upper bound on convergence rates in this setting is due to Jaggi
[
39

6
Gradient Methods for Epismooth Functions

As a final application, we show that our set interpolation theory can provide novel insights even in the context of unconstrained minimization. In particular, consider a gradient method (
GM
) applied to minimize some differentiable, convex function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
.
Much of the modern theory for gradient methods assumes
f
is
L
-smooth. This is a major restriction from the family of all differentiable functions, ruling out, for instance, all polynomials with degree greater than two.

Here, we consider applying first-order methods to a larger class of differentiable functions, namely functions with a smooth epigraph.
We say
f
is
L
-epigraphically smooth
(
L
-epismooth
) if its epigraph
\mathrm{epi\ }f=\{(x,t)\mid f(x)\leq t\}
is an
L
-smooth set.
Theorem

3.7
with
\eta=1
establishes that all
L
-smooth functions are
L
-epismooth, so this is a strictly broader class of functions.
Note epismoothness of
f
does not guarantee that
f
has a full domain of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(consider a simple U-shaped function). Consequently, one cannot guarantee the iteration (
GM
) is well-defined in general, so additional care is needed. To handle this, we consider modified gradient methods of the form

x_{i+1}=x_{i}-\frac{1}{L}\sum_{j=0}^{i}H_{i,j}\frac{\nabla f(x_{j})}{\sqrt{{\|\nabla f(x_{j})\|^{2}}+1}}\ .

(Epi-GM)

Normalizing our stepsizes above allows methods to keep
x_{i+1}-x_{i}
bounded even as
\nabla f(x_{k})
becomes arbitrarily large (as shown in
Figure

8
). This property can then be used to ensure the iterates do not leave the domain of
f
: For example, fixing
H
to be the identity matrix, the above iteration becomes the gradient descent iteration
x_{i+1}=x_{i}-\frac{\nabla f(x_{i})}{L\sqrt{{\|\nabla f(x_{i})\|^{2}}+1}}
, which repeatedly moves to the minimizer of the ball majorizing
f
, ensuring progress every iteration.

(x_{k},f_{k})
\frac{(\nabla f(x_{k}),-1)}{L\ \|(\nabla f(x_{k}),-1)\|}
\left(x_{k}-\frac{h_{k}\nabla f(x_{k})}{L\sqrt{{\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{2}}+1}},\ f_{k}+\frac{h_{k}}{L\sqrt{{\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{2}}+1}}\right)
(x_{k+1},\ f_{k+1})

Modified gradient method for epismooth functions (
GM
).

To illustrate the breadth of this class of functions, the following proposition provides a useful means of checking if a function is
locally
epismooth.

Proposition 6.1
.

For any
f\in\mathcal{C}^{2}
and
x
in the domain of
f
, let

M(x)=\frac{(I+\nabla f(x)\nabla f(x)^{T})^{-1/2}(\nabla^{2}f(x))(I+\nabla f(x)\nabla f(x)^{T})^{-1/2}}{\sqrt{{\|\nabla f(x)\|^{2}}+1}}\ .

(6.1)

Then
f
is locally
L
-epismooth at
x
if and only if
L\geq\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(M)
.

Proof.

We define a local ball upper bound of
f
at
x
by

f(y)\leq\mathfrak{b}_{x}(y;L):=f(x)+\frac{1}{L\sqrt{{\|\nabla f(x)\|^{2}}+1}}-\sqrt{\frac{1}{L^{2}}-\|y-x+\frac{\nabla f(x)}{L\sqrt{{\|\nabla f(x)\|^{2}}+1}}\|^{2}}

(6.2)

where we say
\mathfrak{b}_{x}(y;L)=\infty
outside of its domain.

From the definition of smoothness,
f
is
L
-epismooth if and only if for all
x,y
,
f(y)\leq\mathfrak{b}_{x}(y;L)
.
Observe that
\mathfrak{b}_{x}(x;L)=f(x)
and
\nabla\mathfrak{b}_{x}(x;L)=\nabla f(x)
. Therefore, the upper bound
\mathfrak{b}_{x}(y;L)
holds locally if and only if
\nabla^{2}f(x)\preceq\nabla^{2}\mathfrak{b}_{x}(x;L)
. We can compute
\nabla^{2}\mathfrak{b}_{x}(x;L)=L\sqrt{{\|\nabla f(x)\|^{2}}+1}(I+\nabla f(x)\nabla f(x)^{T})
. Then rearranging and taking the maximum eigenvalue, we obtain our result. ∎

Using this result, we can identify simple functions that are not smooth in the function sense of uniformly Lipschitz gradient but satisfy local epismoothness on their domain. For example, consider

f(x)=\begin{cases}-\log x\quad&\mathrm{if\ }x>0\\
\infty&\mathrm{if\ }x\leq 0\ .\end{cases}

Using (
6.1
), we can calculate
\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(M(x))=\frac{\frac{1}{x^{2}}}{(1+\frac{1}{x^{2}})^{3/2}}\leq\frac{2}{3\sqrt{3}}
for all
x>0
.
So
f
is locally
\frac{2}{3\sqrt{3}}
-epismooth everywhere on its domain. As a second, nontrivial example, one can verify for example
\|Ax-b\|^{4}_{4}
is epismooth.

6.1
Worst-Case Performance of Epismooth Gradient Methods

The worst-case performance of a gradient method defined by a predetermined stepsize matrix
H
on a smooth function and on an epismooth function are closely related. In
Section

2
, we introduced the PEP for smooth functions (
2.3
), and we now present an analogous PEP for the epismooth case.
In both cases, we assume the initial point
x_{0}
satisfies
\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\|\leq R
for some minimizer
x_{\star}
of
f
. Lastly, to ensure that
x_{0}
is in the domain of
f
(i.e.,
f(x_{0})<\infty
), we further require that
R\leq\frac{1}{L}
. Then the worst-case performance of (
Epi-GM
) for a given matrix
H
on an
L
-epismooth function is

p_{\mathrm{ES}}(L,R)=\begin{cases}\max\limits_{x_{i},f}\quad&f(x_{N})-f(x_{\star})\\
\text{s.t.}&\mathrm{epi}f\in\mathcal{C}_{0,L,\infty}\\
&x_{i+1}=x_{i}-\frac{1}{L}\sum_{j=0}^{i}H_{i,j}\frac{\nabla f(x_{j})}{\sqrt{{\|\nabla f(x_{j})\|^{2}}+1}}\quad\quad\forall i\in[0\mathrm{\,:\,}N-1]\\
&\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\|\leq R,\qquad\nabla f(x_{\star})=0\ .\end{cases}

(6.3)

For simplicity, we have suppressed the parameters
N
and
H
from our notation for
p_{\mathrm{ES}}
.
We again denote the PEP for performance of (
GM
) given
H
on an
L
-smooth function by
p_{\mathrm{S}}(L,R)
.
Both of these settings possess useful rescaling properties, proven in
Section

D.1
.

Lemma 6.2
.

For all
\eta>0
and any choice of
N,L,R
, one has that

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{ll}\begin{array}[]{l}p_{\mathrm{S}}(L,\eta R)=\eta^{2}p_{\mathrm{S}}(L,R)\ ,\\
p_{\mathrm{S}}(\eta L,R)=\eta p_{\mathrm{S}}(L,R)\ ,\end{array}&\qquad\qquad p_{\mathrm{ES}}(L,\eta R)=\eta p_{\mathrm{ES}}(\eta L,R)\ .\end{array}

Observe that together the rescaling properties for
p_{\mathrm{S}}
imply
p_{\mathrm{S}}(L,\eta R)=\eta p_{\mathrm{S}}(\eta L,R)
, matching that of the epismooth setting. However, notably, the individual rescaling properties for
p_{\mathrm{S}}
do not hold for
p_{\mathrm{ES}}
. These individual rescaling properties of
p_{\mathrm{S}}
establish that it suffices to characterize
p_{\mathrm{S}}(1,1)
to fully understand
p_{\mathrm{S}}
. Since this does not hold for
p_{\mathrm{ES}}
, any future characterizations of epismooth minimization methods must depend on the ratio of
L
and
R
.

Under an appropriate regularity condition, we find that the convergence of any epismooth minimization method converges to its classic smooth convergence rate as the ratio between
L
and
R
grows. That is, if initialized sufficiently close to
x_{\star}
, perhaps from some initial “burn-in” procedure, guarantees proven for
L
-smooth minimization can be lifted to
L
-epismooth minimization.

Formally, this result relies on the following regularity condition: A gradient method defined by
H
is
eventually-epismooth-stable
if for any
L
, there exist constants
\bar{R},C>0
such that applying (
Epi-GM
) to any
L
-epismooth function with
\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\|\leq R<\bar{R}
must have
\|x_{i}-x_{\star}\|\leq CR
,
\|g_{i}\|\leq CLR
, and
|f_{i}|\leq CLR^{2}
for all
i=0,\dots,N
.
This condition ensures the following asymptotic relation, proven in
Section

D.3
.

Theorem 6.3
.

For any eventually-epismooth-stable method defined by
H
,

\lim_{\eta\to 0}\frac{p_{\mathrm{ES}}(L,\eta R)}{\eta^{2}}=\lim_{\eta\to 0}\frac{p_{\mathrm{S}}(L,\eta R)}{\eta^{2}}=p_{\mathrm{S}}(L,R)\ .

Hence, after an initial burn-in producing
x_{0}
with
\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\|
sufficiently small, optimization strategies for smooth functions such as momentum
[
27
,
16
]
or recent long-step gradient descent techniques
[
21
,
23
]
may be applicable to epismooth functions. One simple example of an eventually-epismooth-stable algorithm is applying (
Epi-GM
) with stepsizes
h\in(0,2)
(i.e.,
H=\mathrm{diag}(h,\dots,h)
). One can directly show this iteration guarantees descent and a nonincreasing distance to
x_{\star}
, as illustrated in
Figure

8
, ensuring the needed bounds.

Applying our interpolation theory, we verify this convergence of epismooth and smooth worst-case performances.
Following
Theorem

3.3
, the same Grammian reformulation presented in previous sections provides a reformulation of
p_{\mathrm{ES}}(L,R)
as an SDP with an additional rank-1 constraint. We show a brief derivation in
Section

D.2
.
Unfortunately, the rank-1 constraint makes this problem nonconvex. Hence we approach it directly as a QCQP using black-box optimization software
[
26
,
25
]
.

Proof of Theorem

Proof of Claimed Stopping Time.
Suppose that by iteration

Using this fact, every iteration

\displaystyle\|x_{k+1}-\hat{q}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|x_{k}-\hat{q}\|^{2}+h^{2}-2h\langle n_{k},x_{k}-\hat{q}\rangle\leq\|x_{k}-\hat{q}\|^{2}-h^{2}\ .

Inductively applying this ensures that if all

Proof of Matching Lower Bound.
Finally, we establish an exactly matching lower bound on any method of the form (
