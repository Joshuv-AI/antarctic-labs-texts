Amitriptyline inhibits EAG1 channels by binding to their PAS domains and exerts EAG1-dependent anti-tumorigenic effects
Authors: Joos Berghausen, Clementine A D Thomas, Ze-Jun Wang, Kyle Kihn, Eric Glasgow, Tinatin I Brelidze
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEther-a-go-go 1 (EAG1) potassium channels are overexpressed in the majority of cancers, and inhibition of their activity decreases cancer growth and migration. Here, we show that amitriptyline (AmiT), an antidepressant that is also used for pain management in cancer patients, inhibits EAG1 by binding to their PAS domains and exerts EAG1-dependent anti-tumorigenic effects. AmiT inhibited the growth of MDA-MB-231 and SH-SY5Y cells expressing EAG1 at high levels, while having a smaller effect on the growth of A375 cells expressing EAG1 at lower levels. Knocking in EAG1 in HEK293 cells increased the inhibition of cell growth by AmiT, while knocking out EAG1 in MDA-MB-231 cells decreased the inhibition of cell growth by the drug. AmiT also inhibited cell migration in MDA-MB-231 and SH-SY5Y cells, while having no effect in A375 cells. Knocking out EAG1 in MDA-MB-231 cells completely removed the effect of AmiT on cell migration. Similar to the in vitro results, AmiT inhibited the growth of MDA-MB-231 and SH-SY5Y zebrafish xenografts, while having no effect on A375 xenografts. Taken together, these results indicate that EAG1 channels are a functional target of AmiT and suggest a potential repurposing of this FDA-approved drug as an anti-cancer agent for tumors with high EAG1 expression.Subject terms: Cancer therapy, Cell biology

Introduction

The ether-à-go-go (EAG) 1 channel, also called Kv10.1, is a voltage-dependent potassium channel, encoded by the KCNH1 gene [1]. It is part of the KCNH voltage-gated potassium channel family that additionally includes EAG-related (ERG) channels, and EAG-like K+ (ELK) channels [1]. EAG1 channels are tetramers, with each subunit comprised of six transmembrane segments (S1-S6). The S1-S4 segments form the voltage-sensing (VS) domain, and the S5 and S6 segments from all four subunits form the centrally located potassium channel pore. Intracellularly, the EAG1 channel possesses a Per-ARNT-Sim (PAS) domain at the N-terminus and a cyclic nucleotide-binding homology domain (CNBHD) at the C-terminus connected to the S6 segment via the C-linker [2, 3].

EAG1 channels are almost exclusively expressed in the central nervous system (CNS) [4]. Their main function has been linked to the regulation of neural excitability by governing presynaptic Ca2+ influx and neurotransmitter release during high-frequency firing [5]. While EAG1 channels are scarcely expressed in the periphery, this changes drastically in cancer, with over 70% of cancers found to be overexpressing EAG1 channels [6–10]. Moreover, transfection of non-cancerous Chinese hamster ovary (CHO) and NIH3T3 mouse fibroblast cells with EAG1 channels induced a malignant phenotype [11]. Importantly, silencing of EAG1 channel expression decreases cancerous properties [12]. Despite their anti-tumorigenic potential, there are no FDA-approved EAG1 channel inhibitors.

The cellular pathways affected by EAG1 channels in cancer are not fully understood and involve multiple mechanisms [13]. In hepatocarcinoma cells, EAG1 has been shown to affect cell proliferation by inhibiting the degradation of SKP2, a protein involved in the degradation of cell-dependent kinase (CDK) inhibitors and ubiquitination [14, 15]. EAG1 channels are also proposed to regulate cancer progression through vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), an important regulator of angiogenesis in cancer. Silencing EAG1 in osteosarcoma cells inhibits cell proliferation and migration via the VEGF pathway [16–18]. Furthermore, the EAG1 channel is upregulated, via the ERK1/2 pathway, in serum-deprived conditions, in A549 lung cancer cells, suggesting it may facilitate survival in nutrient-deprived environments that surround most solid tumors [19]. These studies establish the involvement of EAG1 channels in the regulation of cell growth and migration.

The oncogenic properties of EAG1 channels and their mostly CNS-limited expression pattern make them an attractive target for cancer therapeutics. Furthermore, it has been shown that EAG1 knockout mice exhibited anatomically normal cortex, hippocampus, and cerebellum in adulthood and displayed no alteration in general health screens, sensorimotor functioning, social behavior, and learning and memory [20]. This suggests that cancer treatment involving selective inhibition of EAG1 channels should be well-tolerated.

We have previously shown that the tricyclic drugs chlorpromazine and imipramine bind to the PAS domain of the EAG1 channel and inhibit its currents [21, 22]. Here we demonstrate that amitriptyline (AmiT), another tricyclic drug commonly used as an antidepressant and for the treatment of neuropathic pain caused by chemotherapy [23, 24], also inhibits EAG1 currents by binding to their PAS domains. AmiT inhibited growth and migration of MDA-MB-231 triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) and SH-SY5Y neuroblastoma cells, known to overexpress EAG1 channels at high levels. In contrast, AmiT inhibited growth of A375 melanoma cells, which express EAG1 channels at a lower level, significantly less, and had no statistically significant effect on the migration of A375 cells. Overexpression of EAG1 channels in non-cancerous HEK293 cells significantly increased the cell sensitivity to AmiT and decreased the IC50 by a factor of three, while knocking out EAG1 channel expression in MDA-MB-231 cells decreased the effect of AmiT on cell growth and led to a loss of the anti-migratory effect of AmiT. Finally, zebrafish xenograft experiments indicate that AmiT inhibits the tumor growth of MDA-MB-231 and SH-SY5Y xenografts expressing EAG1 channels at high levels, while having no statistically significant effect on the A375 xenograft growth. Taken together, our in vivo and in vitro results indicate that AmiT inhibits cancer growth in an EAG1-expression-dependent manner and suggest that, in addition to the known therapeutic application in pain and depression management, AmiT can have benefits for cancer treatment.

Methods

Protein purification

The PAS domain of mouse EAG1 (mEAG1) was purified using Ni2+-NTA and size-exclusion chromatography, as described before [25, 26]. Briefly, the PAS domain of mEAG1 channels (residues 7-136) was subcloned into the pETM11 bacterial expression vector that contains an N-terminal 6-Histidine tag and expressed in BL21 (DE3) cells. The protein expression was induced using isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyronoside (IPTG). The cells were spun down and collected by centrifuging at 5000 rpm for 15 min before storing at −80 °C. The cells were then resuspended in Buffer A (30 mM HEPES, 150 mM KCl, 10% glycerol, 1 mM TCEP, pH 7.5) supplemented with 0.8 mM ABSF and 2.5 mg/ml DNAse and lysed using the C-5 Emulsiflex. The lysate was spun down by centrifugation in the Beckman 45 Ti rotor at 30,000 rpm for 1 h at 4 °C. The protein was then purified from the supernatant with Ni2+-NTA chromatography using a HisTrap HP column (Cytiva), Buffer A as the running buffer and Buffer B (Buffer A + 500 mM imidazole) as the elution buffer. The eluted protein fractions were analyzed with the Coomassie Blue-stained gels. Fractions with the proteins with the molecular weight corresponding to the PAS domain were concentrated down and further purified with the size-exclusion chromatography using a Superdex 200 Increase column (Cytiva) and Buffer A as the running buffer. The purity and molecular weight of the eluted protein was verified using a Coomassie Blue-stained gel and mass spectrometry at the Proteomics and Metabolomics Core Facility at Georgetown University Medical Center. Both Ni2+-NTA and size-exclusion chromatography experiments were performed using AKTA Pure (Cytiva). The concentration of the purified PAS domain was calculated using the Bradford Protein Assay Kit (Pierce). The purified protein was aliquoted and stored at −80 °C before use.

Surface-plasmon resonance experiments

The surface plasmon resonance (SPR) experiments were conducted using the Biacore 4000 (GE Healthcare) at 25 °C. The protein was immobilized on an NTA chip (Cytiva), as previously described [25, 26]. Briefly, the chip sensor surface was activated with a 1-min injection of 0.5 mM NiCl2. The purified PAS protein was diluted to ~ 200 nM concentration with the HBS-P buffer (150 mM NaCl, 10 mM HEPES, 0.05% (v/v) P20, pH 7.4) and injected onto the sensor chip surface. Once captured, the protein was cross-linked with 20 s injections of NHS-EDC cross-linkers to prevent protein loss during the experiment, followed by 20 s injections of 1 M ethanolamine. The protein was captured at approximately 2000–3000 resonance units (RU), where 1 RU corresponds to 1 pg of protein/mm2.

For the SPR experiments, 10 mM AmiT and 10 mM cAMP stock in DMSO was diluted to 100 μM concentration with Buffer C (Buffer A without glycerol) to the final DMSO content of 3% (v/v) to precisely match the SPR running buffer (Buffer C + 3% DMSO). The subsequent dilutions for the concentration-dependence experiments were prepared in the SPR running buffer. AmiT and cAMP were injected over the immobilized protein for 60 s at a flow rate of 30 μl/min, followed by the buffer only injections for 180 s. The chip surface was regenerated with 20 s injections of 1 M NaCl between the subsequent analyte injections. The experiments were done in duplicates for each analyte injection. The data were corrected for nonspecific binding by subtracting the SPR response for buffer only injections (blank) and the response from the flow cell spot with no immobilized protein.

Ligand docking and contact analysis

For the computational docking experiments a 3D structure of AmiT was obtained from PubChem [27], hydrogens were added and 3D orientation optimized by the Avogadro software [28]. The ligand was then docked into the PAS domain binding pocket with the Rosetta Ligand Docking using the ROSIE server [29–32]. Contact analysis was performed using an in-house Python script, available on GitHub (https://github.com/kkihnphd/AmiT_Docking), utilizing the PDB module of Biopython [33]. Ligand contacts for each docking model PDB generated with the Rosetta Ligand Docking protocol were determined by measuring the distances between the nitrogen (N), oxygen (O), and hydrogen (H) atoms of the ligand and the N, O, and H atoms of residues within a 4 Å radius. A contact was counted if a ligand’s N or O atoms were within 3.3 Å of a residue’s H atoms. Residues with significantly higher ligand contact frequencies (z-scores > 1.96, corresponding to a 95% confidence interval) were deemed as critical for the ligand binding to the binding pocket.

Two-electrode voltage-clamp experiments

The cDNA encoding the full-length mEAG1 channel, which is 99% identical to the human EAG1 channel, in the pGH19 vector was generously provided by Dr. G. Robertson (University of Wisconsin–Madison, Madison, WI). An EAG1 deletion mutant (ΔPAS) lacking the PAS domain (Δ2–130) in the pGH19 vector was generated by Bio Basic Inc. (Canada) and sequence-verified by Genewiz (South Plainfield, NJ). Complementary RNA (cRNA) was transcribed using the T7 mMessage mMachine kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA).

Defolliculated Xenopus laevis oocytes were obtained from Ecocyte Bioscience (Austin, TX) and injected with mEAG1 cRNA (approximately 50 nL per oocyte) using a Nanoinject II oocyte injector (Drummond Scientific, Broomall, PA). Two-electrode voltage-clamp (TEVC) recordings were conducted 2–3 days post-injection, as previously described [21]. Briefly, oocytes were placed in an RC-3Z recording chamber (Warner Instruments), and currents were recorded using an OC-725C amplifier (Warner Instruments) controlled by the pClamp11 software (Molecular Devices). Signals were digitized with a Digidata 1550 data acquisition system (Molecular Devices).

Recording electrodes were pulled from borosilicate glass (Sutter Instrument) and had a resistance of 0.7–1.5 MΩ. The bath solution contained (in mM): 96 NaCl, 4 KCl, 0.1 CaCl₂, 1.8 MgCl₂, and 5 HEPES (pH 7.5). 10 mM AmiT stock was prepared in high purity water and then diluted with the bath solution to obtain the range of concentrations used for the concentration-dependence experiments. AmiT was added to the bath solution using a gravity-fed solution changer and was always changed from low to high concentration. Currents were recorded 4–5 min after changing the bath solution to allow AmiT to diffuse inside the oocytes. The pipette (intracellular) solution contained 3 M KCl. EAG1 currents were elicited by a series of 1000 ms depolarizing voltage steps ( − 100 to +70 mV, in 10 mV increments) from a holding potential of −80 mV, followed by a 150 ms step to −100 mV. Currents were recorded without leak subtraction. To determine the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC₅₀) for AmiT, the percentage of current inhibition at each concentration was plotted and fitted to the Hill equation, as described before [21].

Cell culture

Stably GFP-expressing MDA-MB-231 (P20117), SH-SY5Y (P20103), and A375 (P20122) cells were purchased from Innoprot and cultured according to the manufacturer’s protocol. MDA-MB-231 cell line with knocked out EAG1 expression and the corresponding Cas9 expressing MDA-MB-231 control cell line were generated by Applied Biological Systems (ABM, Canada) using CRISPR/Cas9 technology and cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Media (DMEM), supplemented with 10% (v/v) heat-inactivated FBS and 1% (v/v) penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco), and 0.3 µg/mL puromycin (ABM Cat. No. G264). HEK-293 cells were cultured in DMEM, supplemented with 10% (v/v) heat-inactivated FBS and 1% (v/v) penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco). HEK-293 cells stably overexpressing human EAG1 channels (HEK293-EAG1) were generously donated by Dr. Luis Pardo (Max Planck Institute for Multidisciplinary Sciences, Göttingen, Germany) and cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% (v/v) heat-inactivated FBS and 1% (v/v) penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco), and 300 µg/mL Zeocin (Gibco). Trypsin-free PBS-EDTA was used for cell detachment from the tissue culture dishes to avoid potential cleavage of EAG1 channels. Cells were cultured at 37 °C and 5% CO2.

Reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction experiments

EAG1 mRNA levels were quantified using the reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR). Total RNA was extracted from the tested cells using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen GmbH). RNA concentration and purity were assessed by Nanophotometer P330 (Implen). First-strand cDNA was synthesized from 1 µg of total RNA using random hexamer primers and a commercial reverse transcription kit (High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit from Applied Biosystems). The resulting cDNA was diluted to a working concentration of 10 ng/µl. RT-qPCR was performed using the CFX Connect Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad) and SYBR Green chemistry. Each 25 µl reaction contained 12.5 µl iQ SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad), 2 µl cDNA template, 1 µl of forward and reverse primers each at 10 µM concentration, and nuclease-free water to volume. The thermal cycling protocol consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 40 amplification cycles (95 °C for 15 s, 57.5 °C for 1 min). Forward and reverse primer sequences for EAG1 were 5′-TCTGTCCTGTTTGCCATATGATGT-3′ and 5′-CGGAGCAGCCGGACAA-3′, respectively. Forward and reverse primer sequences for hERG1 were 5′-ACGTCTCTCCCAACACCAAC-′3 and 5′-GAGTACAGCCGCTGGATGAT-3′, respectively.

Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was used as the internal reference gene. Forward and reverse primer sequences for GAPDH were 5′-GGAGCGAGATCCCTCCAAAAT-3′ and 5′-GGCTGTTGTCATACTTCTCATGG-3′, respectively. Fluorescence signal was detected via SYBR Green intercalation. Each sample was run in triplicate, and non-template controls were included to monitor for contamination. Relative gene expression levels were quantified by normalizing to GAPDH, and the comparative threshold cycle method (2−ΔΔCq) was used to calculate EAG1 expression differences between cancer cell lines [34]. Student’s t-test was performed to analyze differences in gene expression. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Cell viability assay

For the cell viability assays, cells were seeded at a density of 10,000 cells per well in a 96-well plate. Following 24 h of culture, cells were treated with AmiT for 48 h applied at up to 12 different concentrations, ranging from 0 µM to 1000 µM. Each concentration was applied in triplicate. The 48 h treatment timeline has been previously determined to be the optimal duration, as it allows for maximal drug exposure time, yet the cells do not reach confluency in the control group. CellTiter-Blue assay (Promega) was used to determine cell viability. The CellTiter-Blue assay is based on the conversion of non-fluorescent resazurin into fluorescent resorufin by living cells. The resazurin fluorescence upon excitation at 560 nm was recorded at 590 nm with the Synergy H4 Plate Reader. The experiments were repeated three times. All data were normalized to the control group (untreated) and fitted with the Hill equation to calculate IC50 values. Comparison between IC50 values was performed using one-way ANOVA. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

For experiments to compare the growth rate of MDA-MB-231/Cas9 and MDA-MB-231/EAG1-KO cells, the Cas9 and EAG1-KO MDA-MB-231 cells were plated at a density of 4000 cells per well into three 96-well plates, with each plate containing 9 wells with Cas9 and 9 wells with EAG1-KO cells. Viability of cells for one of the plates was measured using the CellTiter-Blue assay 24 h after plating, for another plate 48 h after plating, and for the last plate 72 h after plating. For experiments to determine the effect of 10 µM AmiT on the growth rate of either MDA-MB-231/Cas9 or MDA-MB-231/EAG1-KO cells, corresponding cells were plated at a density of 4000 cells per well into 18 wells of three 96-well plates. 24 h after plating, cells in 9 of the 18 wells on a plate were treated with 10 µM AmiT. Viability of cells was measured using the CellTiter-Blue assay at 0 h, 24 h, and 48 h after the treatment with AmiT.

Wound healing assay

For the wound healing assay, the cancer cells were seeded at a density of 50,000 cells per well into 24-well plates. Once the wells reached 80–90% confluency, a wound was introduced by scratching across the bottom of the well with a 200 μL pipette tip. Following the wound introduction, the wells were washed three times with PBS to remove the debris and were treated with either media alone (control group) or media containing 10 μM AmiT (treatment group). A concentration of 10 μM AmiT, approximately 4–6 times lower than the IC50 values, was selected for the wound healing experiments since at this concentration, AmiT does not affect cell growth (Supplementary Fig. 3B). For each well, three representative images were collected along the wound at 0, and 48 h after the wound introduction using an Olympus IX-71 inverted microscope. The wound size was calculated for each day using the ImageJ Wound Healing Size Tool plugin [35]. The experiments were repeated three times. Changes from 0 h to 48 h were calculated for each well individually, and a comparison between the treatment and control groups was performed using two-way ANOVA. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Zebrafish xenografts

All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with NIH guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals and approved by the Georgetown University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (Protocol 2017-0078).

For the zebrafish xenograft experiments, 100 – 200 cancer cells were injected into the yolk-sac of 2 days post-fertilization (dpf) zebrafish embryos, as described before [36, 37]. The zebrafish line Tg(kdrl:mCherry)y206 that expresses fluorescent protein mCherry in the endothelial cells, was used. Around 200 embryos were randomly divided into treatment and control groups. The animals in the treatment group were treated daily with AmiT added to the fish water at the maximal tolerable dose (MTD) of 15 µM. For the MTD assay, 2 dpf zebrafish larvae were placed in 1 mL fish water solution with increasing concentrations of AmiT, starting at 5 µM and increasing in 5 µM increments. Five fish were placed per well in a 24-well plate. The larvae were monitored over 5 days. The highest concentration at which no mortality and minimal effects on zebrafish development and general behavior were observed was designated as the MTD [38]. Changes in tumor sizes were monitored daily for four days post-injection using an Olympus IX-71 inverted microscope, as previously described [36]. Tumor sizes were calculated blinded using ImageJ by measuring tumor area [37]. Animals lacking measurable tumors were excluded from the study. Changes from day 0 to day 4 were calculated for each individual fish, and comparisons between treatment and control groups were performed using the unpaired Student’s T-test. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Sex of zebrafish embryos becomes clear only at the age of 3 months, well after the first week, to which we limit our experiments. Therefore, sex as a biological variable does not need to be considered for the xenograft experiments described in this study.

Results

AmiT binds in a concentration-dependent manner to the PAS domain of EAG1 channels

We have shown that the PAS domain of EAG1 channels binds tricyclic compounds such as chlorpromazine and imipramine [21, 39]. To test if the PAS domain of mEAG1 channels also binds AmiT, we immobilized the isolated PAS domains on the NTA sensor chip and determined SPR response over the range of AmiT concentrations (Fig. 1A). The SPR response increased with the increase in the AmiT concentration (Fig. 1B–C). Due to a nonspecific binding of AmiT to the NTA sensor chip surface at concentrations higher than 100 µM, we were unable to determine the full concentration dependence necessary for the binding affinity calculations. However, the concentration-dependent increase in the SPR response for the tested AmiT concentrations clearly demonstrates that AmiT directly binds to the PAS domain of EAG1 channels.

D SPR sensorgrams recorded for the immobilized PAS domain in response to the application of cAMP at the indicated concentrations. Residues found to be in contact with AmiT in a statistically significant manner across the generated docked models are shown as green sticks.

As a negative control for the SPR experiments, we tested cAMP binding to the immobilized PAS domains of EAG1 channels. cAMP has no effect on current from EAG1 channels and, therefore, would be expected not to bind to the PAS domains [40]. In agreement with this, application of cAMP over the range of concentrations had no effect on the SPR response (Fig. 1D). These results indicate that cAMP does not bind to the isolated PAS domains of EAG1 channels and suggest that AmiT binding to the PAS domains is specific.

To further investigate AmiT binding to the PAS domains, we performed a computational docking of AmiT into the PAS domain of EAG1 channels with the Rosetta Ligand Docking using the ROSIE server [29–32]. For the ligand docking, we first generated a tetrameric structural model of the full-length mouse EAG1 (mEAG1) channel using the rat EAG1 (rEAG1) cryo-EM structure, which was determined in the complex with calmodulin (PDB ID: 5K7L) [2]. From this structure, a mouse EAG1 model was generated to directly correlate the computational docking results with our electrophysiology studies, as they are based on current recordings from mEAG1 channels. Importantly, human and mouse EAG1 channels share 99% identity in their amino acid sequence, therefore, we expect that our findings for mouse EAG1 will be relevant to human EAG1 channels as well. To dock AmiT into the PAS domain of the full-length mEAG1 channels, the calmodulin was removed from the rEAG1 structure, and the sequence of rEAG1 was mutated using the CHARMM-GUI to match the mEAG1 sequence [41–44]. Since AmiT is structurally similar to the tricyclic compounds imipramine and chlorpromazine, binding of which to the PAS domain and effect on EAG1 currents was investigated in detail with electrophysiology and mutagenesis [21, 22], we initially docked AmiT to the same binding pocket in the PAS domain as the two tricyclic compounds were shown to bind. 500 different binding conformers of AmiT were generated, with 100 representative poses chosen by the ROSIE server. One of these poses is shown in Fig. 1E. We also analyzed residues that are important for interactions with AmiT by evaluating H-bonds from both ligand and pocket perspectives, by measuring distances between nitrogen (N) and oxygen (O) atoms of AmiT to hydrogen (H) atoms of residues in the PAS domain and vice versa. Distances ≤3.3 Å were counted as H-bond contacts [45]. To identify the most important residues facilitating ligand binding, a z-score analysis was conducted on the distribution of contact counts for each residue. Residues with z-scores >1.96, representing contacts outside the 95% confidence interval, were selected as critical for binding. These analysis identified residues D39, V80, V83, R84, F87, F130, and Y71 as the key residues for interactions with AmiT (Fig. 1E). Not all of these residues are forming interactions with AmiT at the same time, however, based on the analysis of the generated poses, they form a network of interactions that is important for AmiT binding. Noteworthy, these residues were also shown to form a network of interactions between the PAS domain and imipramine, with the double mutation D39G/R84G decreasing the inhibition of EAG1 currents to the same level as for the deletion of the entire PAS domain and Y71G mutation increasing the EAG1 current inhibition by making it easier for the ligand to access the binding pocket in the PAS domain. Taken together, the docking experiments indicate that the PAS domain of EAG1 channels can accommodate AmiT and suggest that the residues that contribute to the binding of imipramine are also involved in interactions with AmiT.

AmiT inhibits EAG1 currents in a concentration-dependent manner in part by binding to the PAS domain

To assess the functional impact of AmiT binding to the PAS domain of EAG1 channels, we expressed full-length wild-type (WT) and mutant (ΔPAS) mEAG1 channels that lack the PAS domain in Xenopus laevis oocytes. Whole-cell currents were recorded using TEVC in the absence and presence of AmiT applied over the range of concentrations in the bath solution. AmiT inhibited currents from WT mEAG1 channels expressed in oocytes with the IC50 of 61.5 ± 2.0 µM (Fig. 2A and C). This is consistent with the previous report of hEAG1 current inhibition by AmiT, with the IC50 of 15.2 ± 0.2 μM based on the whole-cell current recordings from HEK293-EAG1 cells [46]. Since mEAG1 and hEAG1 channels are 99% identical in their amino acid sequence, the difference in the IC50 is unlikely to be due to the sequence differences between mEAG1 and hEAG1 and most likely is due to the much larger size of oocytes compared to mammalian HEK293 cells. Stage V-VI oocytes used in our electrophysiology experiments typically vary from 1 to 1.3 mm in diameter [47], while HEK293 cells are about 14 μm in diameter [48]. Due to such a large difference in size, oocytes have a larger capacity to bind drugs to various molecular targets and intracellular structures. This decreases the effective concentration of unbound drugs in oocytes in comparison with HEK293 cells, leading to substantially increased IC50. We expect that the IC50 determined using the excised inside-out patch-clamp recording technique, which allows application of AmiT directly to the intracellular side of EAG1 channels containing the PAS domains, will be substantially lower than the one determined with TEVC. Indeed, we have shown that for chlorpromazine, the IC50 of EAG1 current inhibition is ~4 µM when determined using currents recorded with the excised inside-out patch-clamp technique and ~30 µM using TEVC [21]. Importantly, due to the reported accumulation of AmiT in tissues and long-term administration to the patients, the effective cellular concentrations of AmiT can reach high micromolar ranges, well above the IC50 of EAG1 inhibition by AmiT reported here and in the previous study [49–51].

The lines represent fits of the data with Hill equation with the IC50 of 61.5 + 2.0 µM (n of 6) for WT (black) and 110.9 + 13.2 µM (n of 5) for ΔPAS (red). The data are presented as mean ± SD.

Deletion of the PAS domain substantially decreased EAG1 current inhibition, with a doubling of the IC50 of inhibition to 110.9 ± 13.2 µM (Fig. 2B–C). These results indicate that binding of AmiT to the PAS domain causes EAG1 current inhibition. The residual inhibition observed in the absence of the PAS domain indicates that AmiT acts through binding to an additional site(s) besides the PAS domain. The most likely secondary site would be inside the pore domain, as was suggested for another tricyclic drug imipramine [22]. However, an extracellular location for the secondary site(s) could also be a possibility. For instance, many potassium channels are inhibited by tetraethylammonium (TEA) binding to their extracellular pore entrance near the selectivity filter [52–54]. Also, mibefradil is thought to inhibit currents through EAG1 channels by binding to the voltage sensor extracellularly [55].

AmiT inhibits cancer cell growth in an EAG1-expression-dependent manner

Inhibition of EAG1 currents is expected to have an anti-tumorigenic effect [12, 16]. Therefore, we tested whether AmiT inhibits cell growth in an EAG1-expression-dependent manner. For these experiments, we selected the MDA-MB-231 human breast cancer cell line and the SH-SY5Y human neuroblastoma cell line, known to overexpress EAG1 channels at high levels, and the A375 human melanoma cell line, known to express EAG1 channels at a lower level compared to the other two cell lines [56–58]. This expression pattern was confirmed using RT-PCR experiments that indicated both MDA-MB-231 and SH-SY5Y cells express EAG1 channels at significantly higher levels than A375 cells (Fig. 3A–B). Importantly, endogenous EAG1 currents have been recorded from MDA-MB-231 and SH-SY5Y cells [59, 60], indicating that the mRNA expression reported with the RT-PCR experiments corresponds to the presence of functional EAG1 channels at the cell membrane that generate ionic currents. In addition to the three cancer cell lines, HEK293 cells were also tested to evaluate the cytotoxicity of AmiT for non-cancerous cells. The effect of AmiT on cell growth was investigated using the CellTiter-Blue cell viability assay (Promega). The CellTiterBlue assay measures cell viability based on the ability of living cells to convert a non-fluorescent dye (resazurin) into a fluorescent product (resorufin). Since nonviable cells do not generate a fluorescent signal, and only viable cells can divide, and since confluency was not reached during the experiments, the assay can be used to assess cell growth. AmiT caused a concentration-dependent effect on cell growth that was strongest for MDA-MB-231 cells with the IC₅₀ value of 33.34 ± 1.8 μM (Fig. 3C). This was closely followed by SH-SY5Y cells with the IC₅₀ value of 39.11 ± 6.4 μM. The difference between the IC50 values for the two cell lines was not statistically significant. The effect of AmiT on cell growth was weaker for A375 cells with an IC₅₀ value of 57.38 ± 17.5 μM (Fig. 3C) and the weakest for the non-cancerous HEK293 cells with the IC₅₀ value of 93.36 ± 11.9 μM. The RT-PCR experiments indicate that the EAG1 expression is substantially higher for SH-SY5Y cells compared to MDA-MB-231 cells (Fig. 3A–B), yet the effects of AmiT on cell growth are similar for these two cell lines. This suggests that the inhibitory effect of AmiT on cell growth saturates at a certain EAG1 channel expression level and does not increase further with the increase in the EAG1 expression level. Taken together, our findings indicate that AmiT has an inhibitory effect on cell growth in vitro, and the effect is dependent on the EAG1 channel expression levels.

AmiT inhibits cancer cell migration in an EAG1-expression-dependent manner

Ion channels, and specifically EAG1 channels, have been suggested to play a role in metastasis [14, 56]. Inhibition of EAG1 channels has been shown to decrease cell migration [16]. It has been estimated that 90% of cancer-related deaths are due to metastasis [61]. Therefore, to fully characterize a drug’s anti-tumorigenic potential, the analysis of its ability to inhibit metastasis is of utmost importance. To further investigate the anti-tumorigenic potential of AmiT, we tested its effect on cancer cell migration and the dependence of the effect on EAG1 channel expression levels. Cancer cell migration was evaluated using a commonly used wound healing assay, where a scratch wound healing was analyzed in the absence and presence of 10 μM AmiT 0 and 48 h after the wound introduction (Fig. 4A–C). In the absence of AmiT, the wound was almost entirely healed at 48 h post scratch for MDA-MB-231 and SH-SY5Y cells, and only 28.29 ± 10.4% healed for A375 cells (Fig. 4D). Application of AmiT significantly inhibited wound healing in MDA-MB-231 and SH-SY5Y cells, with only 61.03 ± 7.5% and 63.42 ± 17.9% of the wound healed, respectively, at 48 h after scratch (Fig. 4A–B). Application of AmiT did not cause a statistically significant decrease in the wound healing in A375 cells at 48 h post scratch (Fig. 4D). To test if the effect of AmiT on the A375 cell migration requires a longer time period to manifest, we monitored wound healing in A375 cells for a longer time. Even at 120 h post-scratch, the wound healing was incomplete (66.39 ± 9.4%) in the absence of AmiT. Importantly, the wound healing was not statistically different in the presence of AmiT with 62.46 ± 6.0% wound healed (Supplementary Fig. 1). Taken together, our results indicate that AmiT inhibits migration of MDA-MB-231 and SH-SY5Y cells expressing EAG1 channels at high levels, while having no statistically significant effect on the migration of A375 cells that express EAG1 channel at a lower level. This suggests that AmiT inhibits cancer cell migration in an EAG1-expression-dependent manner.

Overexpression of EAG1 increases the inhibitory effect of AmiT on HEK293 cell growth

Our results suggest that AmiT inhibits cell growth, at least in part, by inhibiting EAG1 channels. To further investigate this possibility, we tested the effect of AmiT on growth of HEK293 cells constitutively overexpressing EAG1 channels (HEK293-EAG1) [62]. We first confirmed the overexpression of EAG1 mRNA in HEK293-EAG1 cells using RT-qPCR (Fig. 5A). The effect of AmiT on the growth of HEK293-EAG1 cells was investigated using the CellTiter-Blue cell viability assay and compared to the effect of AmiT on WT HEK293 cells, reported in Fig. 3. AmiT decreased HEK293-EAG1 cell viability in a concentration-dependent manner with the IC₅₀ of 28.84 ± 3.15 μM (Fig. 5B). This IC50 was significantly lower than the IC50 of 93.36 ± 11.9 μM for WT HEK cells (Fig. 5B). Therefore, overexpression of EAG1 channels in HEK293 cells increased the inhibitory effect of AmiT on cell growth 3-fold, consistent with our hypothesis that the anti-growth effect of AmiT is, at least in part, mediated via EAG1 current inhibition.

Knocking out EAG1 expression in MDA-MB-231 cells decreases the effect of AmiT on the cell growth and migration

To further demonstrate that AmiT exerts its anti-tumorigenic action via EAG1 channels, we examined the effect of AmiT on the growth and migration of MDA-MB-231 cells with knocked out expression of the EAG1 channel (MDA-MB-231/EAG1-KO). The genetically modified cells were generated using CRISPR/Cas9 technology by targeting the PAS domain sequence of human EAG1 channels (Accession No. NM_172362.3) with sgRNAs (5’-AATATTGTTCGGCGGTCCAA 3’; 5’- GGAATGCTCAGATAGTGGAC-3’; 5’-CAAGCTGTCTGGCTATCACA-3’). This strategy aimed at introducing a frameshift mutation resulting in a premature stop codon in the PAS domain of EAG1 channels. Successful EAG1 gene knockout was confirmed via Sanger sequencing of cDNA, which was converted from the isolated RNA (Supplementary Fig. S2). A low Phred Quality score due to the overlapping peaks in the sequence chromatogram after the expected cleavage site indicates missing nucleotides base due to the genetic modification. Cas9-expressing MDA-MB-231 cells (MDA-MB-231/Cas9) were used as a control for these experiments. Comparison of the cell viability in the absence and presence of various concentrations of AmiT indicated that the effect of AmiT on the cell growth was significantly smaller for the MDA-MB-231/EAG1-KO cells (IC50 of 60.78 ± 5.2 μM) compared to the MDA-MB-231/Cas9 control cells (IC50 of 46.42 ± 16.4 μM) (Fig. 6A). Importantly, in addition to the effect on IC50, knocking out EAG1 changed the shape of the concentration dependence plot for the cell viability, revealing a virtual absence of an effect at concentrations up to 30 μM, followed by a sharp drop in the cell viability to the levels seen in the Cas9 control cells. This reflects a biphasic nature of the effect, where at low concentrations AmiT shows an EAG1-dependent inhibition of the cell growth, while at high concentrations the EAG1-dependent effect gets saturated and/or is overshadowed by an off-target or cytotoxic effects of AmiT. Therefore, knocking out EAG1 channels removes the effect of AmiT mediated by EAG1 channel inhibition, while leaving the off-target/cytotoxic effect intact. Taken together, the decrease of the effect of AmiT on the cell growth of EAG1-KO cells in comparison to the Cas9 control indicates that AmiT inhibits MDA-MB-231 cell growth, at least in part, due to EAG1 inhibition.

To investigate the effect of EAG1 channel knock out on the migration of MDA-MB-231 cells, we evaluated cancer cell migration using the wound healing assay in the absence and presence of 10 μM AmiT 48 h after scratch introduction. In the absence of AmiT, MDA-MB-231/EAG1-KO cells showed only 56.24 ± 11.3% wound healing, while the Cas9 control cells were almost completely healed (93.61 ± 6.2%) (Fig. 6B–C). This indicates that knocking out EAG1 channels significantly decreases the migratory ability of MDA-MB-231 cells. Application of AmiT did not have a statistically significant effect on wound healing in MDA-MB-231/EAG1-KO cells, with only 50.19 ± 14.8% wound healed, while the wound healing in the Cas9 control cells was substantially slowed down from 93.61 ± 6.2% to 54.63 ± 8.5% when adding AmiT. The loss of the anti-migratory effect of AmiT in the MDA-MB-231/EAG1-KO cells demonstrates that AmiT inhibits the migration of MDA-MB-231 cells solely by inhibiting EAG1 channels. Moreover, the almost complete equality of % wound healing for the control cell line in the presence of AmiT and the knocked-out cell line in the absence and presence of AmiT indicates that EAG1 is the main contributor to the migratory cellular pathway in MDA-MB-231 cells.

AmiT inhibits cancer growth in zebrafish xenografts in an EAG1-expression-dependent manner

Our experiments indicate that AmiT decreases cancer growth in a concentration- and EAG1-expression-dependent manner in cell culture experiments. To investigate the effect of AmiT on cell growth in vivo, we employed zebrafish xenografts. EAG1 channels are crucial for zebrafish development [63]. Therefore, prior to xenografts, we conducted experiments to identify the maximal tolerable dose (MTD) for AmiT that had no detectable effect on zebrafish mortality, development, and general behavior. The MTD for AmiT was determined to be 15 μM. For the xenograft experiments, 2 dpf zebrafish larvae were implanted with SH-SY5Y, MDA-MB-231, and A375 cells constitutively expressing GFP. The larvae were either left untreated or treated with 15 μM of AmiT added to the fish water. Tumor size was determined immediately post-implantation (Day 0) and 96 h post-implantation (Day 4). After four days of treatment, AmiT significantly decreased tumor size in larvae engrafted with MDA-MB-231 and SH-SY5Y cells, while having no statistically significant effect on the implanted tumor size for A375 cells (Fig. 7A–C). For MDA-MB-231 cells, averaged tumor size increased on Day 4 by 56.08% in the absence of AmiT and decreased by 14.87% in the presence of AmiT (Fig. 7A and 7D). For SH-SY5Y cells, averaged tumor size increased on Day 4 by 48.94% in the absence of AmiT and decreased by 20.02% in the presence of AmiT (Fig. 7B and 7D). In contrast, for A375 cells, averaged tumor size increased on Day 4 by 38.27% in the absence of AmiT and increased by about the same 34.86% in the presence of AmiT (Fig. 7C and 7D). These findings are in alignment with the in vitro results and suggest that the effect of AmiT on cancer growth in vivo is EAG1-expression-dependent.

Discussion

Here, we show that AmiT inhibits EAG1 channels by binding to their intracellular regulatory PAS domains and exerts anti-tumorigenic effects, both in vitro and in vivo, in an EAG1-expression-dependent manner. Our SPR-based experiments indicate that AmiT directly binds to the isolated PAS domains of EAG1 channels. Computational docking further showed that the EAG1 PAS domain can accommodate AmiT. The electrophysiology experiments indicate that AmiT inhibits currents recorded from EAG1 channels with TEVC, and the inhibition is substantially decreased in the mutant channels lacking the PAS domain. In vitro experiments demonstrate that AmiT significantly inhibits cell growth and migration of MDA-MB-231 triple-negative breast cancer cells and SH-SYS5 neuroblastoma cells, both of which express EAG1 channels at a high level, while having significantly smaller effect on the growth of A375 melanoma cells, which express EAG1 channels at a lower level than MDA-MB-231 and SH-SY5Y cells, and has no statistically significant effect on the migration of A375 cells. These results suggest that the magnitude of the anti-tumorigenic effects of AmiT correlates with the EAG1 channel expression levels. Consistent with this, overexpression of EAG1 channels in HEK cells substantially increased the inhibitory effect of AmiT on cell growth, and knocking out EAG1 channel expression in MDA-MB-231 cells decreased the inhibitory effect of AmiT on cell growth and completely eliminated AmiT’s effect on cell migration. Our in vivo experiments indicate that AmiT substantially decreases the growth of MDA-MB-231 and SH-SY5Y zebrafish xenografts while having no statistically significant effect on the growth of A375 cell xenografts. These results further support the EAG1-expression-dependence of the anti-tumorigenic effect of AmiT observed in the in vitro experiments. Taken together, these findings indicate that AmiT can elicit anti-tumorigenic effects, and these effects are, at least in part, mediated by EAG1 channel inhibition.

Our results indicate that AmiT inhibits MDA-MB-231 triple-negative breast cancer, SH-SY5Y neuroblastoma, and A375 melanoma cell growth with the IC50 of ~ 33, 40, and 57 μM, correspondingly (Fig. 3). Our RT-PCR results (Fig. 3) and studies from other research groups indicate that these three cell lines express EAG1 channels [56–58], albeit at different levels. Moreover, endogenous EAG1 currents from MDA-MB-231 and SH-SY5Y cells had been reported before [59, 60]. Since our electrophysiology results indicate that EAG1 currents are inhibited by AmiT, we expect that the effect of AmiT on the cell growth of the three cell lines is, at least in part, mediated via the inhibition of the ionic currents from EAG1 channels. Consistent with this, we find that the potency of AmiT effect on the cell growth is higher for MDA-MB-231 and SH-SY5Y cells expressing EAG1 channels at higher levels than for A375 cells expressing EAG1 at lower levels, suggesting that overall AmiT affects the growth of the examined cell lines in an EAG1-expression dependent manner. However, the IC50 for AmiT was similar for MDA-MB-231 cells and SH-SY5Y cells, despite substantially higher EAG1 expression levels in SH-SY5Y cells, suggesting either a saturation of the AmiT effect on the cell growth or a substantial EAG1-independent mechanism of AmiT action in SH-SY5Y cells. There also might be differences in the efficacy, the maximal effect for AmiT, among the three examined cell lines. However, the variability in the cell viability assay complicates the comparison of the efficacies of the AmiT effect for the examined cell lines. For instance, the maximal effect of AmiT is seemingly highest for SH-SY5Y cells (Fig. 3C), however, this might be due to the higher baseline at low AmiT concentrations. The differences in the cell viability levels at low AmiT concentrations most likely reflect a variability of the fluorescence signal and do not have a biological significance. If the baseline at low AmiT concentrations is adjusted for SH-SY5Y cells to match the other examined cell lines, then there will not be a substantial difference in the efficacy.

To our knowledge, the effect of AmiT on MDA-MB-231 and A375 cells has not been examined before. However, it has been reported that AmiT does not affect cell growth of MCF-7 breast cancer cells [64]. MDA-MB-231 are more aggressive cancer cells compared to MCF-7. EAG1 channel overexpression seems to be especially important in aggressive cancers [65]. The preferential inhibition by AmiT of MDA-MB-231 cell growth over that of MCF-7 cells supports the role of EAG1 channels in aggressive cancers. The previously reported IC50 for AmiT inhibition of SH-SY5Y cell growth of ~60 μM is higher than the IC50 of ~40 μM determined in our study [66]. This difference could be potentially due to the differences in the viability assays used in the studies. While we measured cell viability by incubating the cells for 2 h with CellTiter-Blue, in the other study MTT assay was used, and cells were incubated for 1 h. In addition to breast cancer and neuroblastoma, the anti-tumorigenic effect of AmiT has been reported in glioblastoma, lung cancer, hepatocellular carcinoma, and leiomyosarcoma [66, 67]. Therefore, previous reports and our findings strongly indicate the anti-tumorigenic potential of AmiT.

Metastasis caused by cancer cell migration is a major contributor to cancer-related mortality, and it has been estimated that 90% of cancer-related deaths are due to metastasis [61]. EAG1 channels have been suggested to play a role in metastasis, including in TNBC, where expression of EAG1 channels was found to be significantly higher compared to cancer patients with non-TNBC [14, 56, 65]. Consistent with this, knocking out EAG1 channels in MDA-MB-231 cells significantly slowed down wound healing from ~94% to ~56% (Fig. 6), indicating that EAG1 channels are one of the major contributors to the migration of MDA-MB-231 cells. Interestingly, knocking out EAG1 channels did not affect the growth rate of MDA-MB-231 cells in a statistically significant manner (Supplementary Fig. 3A), indicating that EAG1 may not be a key driver of MDA-MB-231 cell growth. Knocking out EAG1 channels also completely eliminated the inhibitory effect of AmiT on the cell migration, suggesting that the effect of AmiT on the migratory pathway is primarily mediated by EAG1 channel inhibition in TNBC cells. Importantly, 10 μM AmiT did not alter the growth rate of MDA-MB-231 cells (Supplementary Fig. 3B), indicating that the observed differences in wound healing describe anti-migratory rather than anti-proliferative effects of AmiT. Notably, A375 cells that express EAG1 channels at a lower level than MDA-MB-231 and SH-SY5Y cells were substantially slower at wound healing, which was unaffected by AmiT. This further highlights the importance of EAG1 channels in cell migration and the anti-tumorigenic effect of AmiT. Although the dependence of both anti-growth and anti-migratory effects of AmiT on the EAG1 channel expression levels in our in vitro and in vivo experiments is apparent, it is nonlinear. Indeed, SH-SY5Y cells express EAG1 several hundred-fold more than MDA-MB-231 cells, according to our RT-qPCR results (Fig. 3A and 3B), yet the inhibitory effect of AmiT on the growth, and migration of these two cell lines was similar (Figs. 3C and 4D). This suggests that either the maximal anti-tumorigenic effect of AmiT or the maximal anti-tumorigenic effect of EAG1 channel inhibition has been reached in MDA-MB-231 cells, and increased EAG1 channel expression in SH-SY5Y cells has no additional anti-tumorigenic effect. Heterogeneity in genetics and functional characteristics of cancer cells is well established [68–71]. Therefore, it is possible that AmiT might act via additional or different mechanisms, and the maximal anti-tumorigenic effect of AmiT might be different in other cancer types, or even vary among subgroups within the same type of cancer. Examination of additional cancer types, such as liver and lung cancer known to overexpress EAG1 channels [72], would be important to further investigate EAG1-expression dependent mechanism of AmiT action. Importantly, it has been shown that EAG1 channels can contribute to cancer progression independent of their ion-conducting function. For instance, the effect of EAG1 channels on the epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition in lung cancer cells was not altered by EAG1 channel inhibitor astemizole [72], and eliminating ion permeation through EAG1 channels by introducing a point mutation in the channel pore did not abolish EAG1-dependent malignant transformation of NIH/3T3 mouse embryonic fibroblast cells [73]. Since AmiT would be expected to affect the ionic conduction through EAG1 channels but not necessarily the ion conduction-independent mechanisms, which could be different in different cell types, these could further contribute to the differences in the functional link between AmiT and EAG1 channel expression levels in different types of cancers and will need to be investigated in future studies.

Human eag-related gene (hERG) channels, encoded by the KCNH2 gene, belong to the same family of potassium-selective channels as EAG1 and are also known to be involved in cancer [74]. Moreover, hERG channels are also inhibited by amitriptyline [75]. To test if the expression levels of hERG channels in the tumor cells examined in our study follow the same pattern as EAG1 channels and, therefore, might be contributing to the seemingly EAG1-expression-dependent effect of amitriptyline, we analyzed the expression pattern of hERG channels with RT-PCR (Supplementary Fig. 4). Our analysis revealed a distinct expression profile for hERG and EAG1 channels across the examined cell lines. EAG1 channels were expressed at higher levels than hERG in MDA-MB-231 cells, at lower levels than hERG1 in SH-SY5Y cells, and at similar levels as hERG in A375 cells (Supplementary Fig. 4A). While EAG1 channels were expressed in MDA-MB-231 and SH-SY5Y cells at higher levels than in A375 cells (Fig. 3B), hERG expression levels were similar in MDA-MB-231 and A375 cells (Supplementary Fig. 3C) and only upregulated in SH-SY5Y cells relative to A375 cells (Supplementary Fig. 3B). These results suggest that the magnitude of the anti-tumorigenic effects of AmiT reported in our study correlates with the EAG1 channel expression levels rather than hERG expression.

Zebrafish have emerged as a powerful model organism for studies of cancer [76]. Zebrafish share high genetic similarity to humans, with approximately 70% of human genes and 80% of human disease-associated genes represented by at least one zebrafish orthologue [77]. The transparency of zebrafish embryos allows high-resolution imaging of cancer growth and migration [37]. The cost of zebrafish housing is a fraction of that for mice, a frequently used mammalian model for xenografts. Additionally, rapid development and high fecundity make it possible to test high numbers of zebrafish for each experiment, increasing the rigor of findings. Zebrafish embryos lack a mature immune system, minimizing the rejection of implanted cancer cells and avoiding the necessity of generating immunodeficient or humanized animal models [78–80]. However, the absence of a mature immune system hinders the use of zebrafish embryos for studies involving the adaptive immune system. Therefore, studies using mammalian (mice) xenografts will be important to investigate the effect of AmiT on the EAG1-dependent cross-talk with adaptive immune cells within the tumor microenvironment.

AmiT is an FDA-approved tricyclic antidepressant (TCA) that exerts its antidepressant effect by primarily inhibiting the reuptake of serotonin and norepinephrine [81]. Consistent with our findings, treatment of patients with brain metastasis and glioblastoma multiforme with TCAs, including AmiT, improved the outcome, and it was suggested that the inhibition of EAG1 channels by TCAs is one of the mechanisms responsible for this improvement [82]. TCAs use has also been reported to reduce the risk of developing glioma and colorectal cancer, which are known to overexpress EAG1 channels [83–85]. Depressive disorder is common among cancer patients. A 2018 cross-sectional study in Thailand revealed that of cervical cancer patients, 13.5% were diagnosed with depression compared with only 0.3-2.4% nationwide [86]. Another study in Germany concluded that out of 4020 cancer patients, a quarter were diagnosed with depression, which was 5 times higher than in the general population [87]. Amitriptyline can ameliorate the depression common in cancer patients. Amitriptyline is also frequently used for cancer pain, specifically neuropathic pain, management [88–90]. Therefore, AmiT could provide a triple benefit in cancer treatment: alleviate the symptoms of depressive disorder, help with pain management, and also slow down cancer growth.

Clinically observed concentrations of AmiT in plasma are in the low micromolar range (∼ 0.8 μM) [91, 92]. However, due to the well-documented accumulation of the tricyclic drugs in tissues and long-term administration to the patients, the effective cellular concentrations are expected to be 10 to 1000-fold higher [49–51]. Therefore, the IC50 values for the anti-tumorigenic effect of AmiT on cell growth, and migration reported in our study are within the clinically relevant doses of the drug. Combination therapy of AmiT with other commonly used chemotherapeutics could further amplify its benefits and warrants further investigation in future studies.

In summary, our study suggests a novel mechanism of anti-tumorigenic action of amitriptyline by binding to the PAS domain of EAG1 and inhibiting EAG1 currents. The EAG1-expression-dependent inhibition of cancer cell growth, and migration underscores the potential of EAG1 as a therapeutic target and amitriptyline as a promising candidate for cancer treatment.
