Topological and Purely Topological Alignment Dynamics
Trevor M. Leslie, Jan Peszek

Abstract
. We study the Euler Alignment system of collective behavior, equipped with ‘topological’ interaction protocols, which were introduced to the mathematical literature by Shvydkoy and Tadmor. Interactions subject to these protocols may depend on both the Euclidean distance between agents and on the mass distribution between them—the ‘topological’ component. When the interaction protocol is regular, we prove sufficient conditions for the existence of global-in-time classical solutions, related to the initial nonnegativity of a conserved quantity of the system. The remainder of our results explore the case where the interactions are ‘purely’ topological and the interactions do not depend on the Euclidean distance. We show that in this case, the system decouples into an autonomous velocity equation in mass coordinates together with a scalar conservation law with time-dependent flux determined by the velocity. We analyze the long-time behavior for the dynamics associated to both regular and singular protocols.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
The Euler Alignment System

This paper is concerned with the Euler Alignment system, which when posed on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
takes the following general form.

(1)

\left\{\begin{split}\partial_{t}\rho+\nabla\cdot(\rho u)&=0,\qquad(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,\infty),\\
\partial_{t}(\rho u)+\nabla\cdot(\rho u\otimes u)&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi(x,y,t)(u(y,t)-u(x,t))\rho(x,t)\rho(y,t)\,\mathrm{d}y\end{split}\right.

Here
\rho=\rho(x,t)\geq 0
and
u=u(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denote the density and macroscopic velocity of a continuum of agents which interact according to the ‘communication protocol’
\phi\geq 0
. We make the global assumption that
\rho
is normalized to have mass
1
:

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho(x,\cdot)\,\mathrm{d}x=1.

The character of the system (
1
) and evolution of the associated dynamics depend strongly on the properties of
\phi
. Most of the literature on (
1
) treats the case where
\phi=\phi(|x-y|)
is a function of the Euclidean distance between
x
and
y
only (possibly singular at zero, in which case the integral in (
1
) may be understood in a principal value sense if necessary).
But
\phi
may have other dependencies as well. In particular, this paper is concerned with the case where
\phi(x,y,t)
may depend on the mass distribution between
x
and
y
, a paradigm that was first advocated for in the mathematical literature by Shvydkoy and Tadmor
[
34
]
, who referred to such
\phi
as ‘topological protocols.’ In fact, the majority of our work is focused on a ‘purely topological’ variant of the protocols introduced in
[
34
]
, where
\phi
depends
only
on the mass distribution between points and not on their Euclidean distance. Interestingly, the use of purely topological protocols leads to a complete decoupling of the velocity equation in mass coordinates, which enables the study of the long-time behavior in terms of an autonomous velocity equation.

The Euler Alignment system was first derived by Ha and Tadmor in
[
16
]
as a hydrodynamic version of the celebrated Cucker–Smale system of ODEs
[
7
,
8
]
, which reads as follows. For
N
agents with masses, positions, and velocities given by
(m_{i},x_{i},v_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
,

(2)

\left\{\begin{split}\dot{x}_{i}&=v_{i},\qquad i=1,\ldots,N,\\
\dot{v}_{i}&=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{N}m_{j}\phi(x_{i},x_{j},t)(v_{j}-v_{i}).\end{split}\right.

System (
1
) is the pressureless (monokinetic) variant of macroscopic alignment dynamics derived from the Cucker–Smale model cf.
[
12
,
11
]
(alternative non-monokinetic approaches can be found e.g. in
[
17
]
).

The study of (
1
) and (
2
) falls under the umbrella of
collective dynamics
, which is concerned with understanding how large-scale macroscopic structures emerge from local interactions in systems of many agents. Formulating and analyzing mathematical models that capture such phenomena is in general highly nontrivial. The Cucker–Smale and Euler Alignment systems have received copious attention because of their compatibility with ‘flocking’ dynamics (terminology intentionally reminiscent of a flock of birds) and their relative mathematical tractability; analysis of these systems is nonetheless quite subtle, and a good deal remains unknown. We will define precisely what we mean by ‘flocking’ below and discuss a select few references, but it is not our purpose here to review the already vast literature on Cucker–Smale-type systems. We refer instead to the survey papers
[
25
,
32
,
35
]
, the book
[
31
]
, and references therein, for more on these systems and some of their applications.

1.2.
Flocking Dynamics and the Role of Topological Distance

The communication protocols
\phi(x,y,t)
we consider will be symmetric in
x
and
y
, which will guarantee conservation of the total momentum of the system:

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho u(t)\,\mathrm{d}x=\overline{u}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho_{0}u_{0}\,\mathrm{d}x,\qquad t\geq 0.

For our version of the system, then, it makes sense to say that the system (
1
) undergoes ‘velocity alignment’ in the time-asymptotic limit if
u(t)
tends in some sense to its density-weighted average
\overline{u}
as
t\to\infty
. (In the absence of guaranteed momentum conservation, a suitable replacement would be to require that
\diam u(t)\to 0
.) We say that ‘flocking’ occurs if the density profile converges (in some appropriate topology) to a traveling wave in the time-asymptotic limit:
\rho(\cdot-\overline{u}t,t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle t\to+\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}\rho_{\infty}
. There is a robust literature detailing and proving various conditions which are sufficient to guarantee that velocity alignment and flocking occur. Many of them assume that the communication protocol is radial,
\phi=\phi(|x-y|)
, and ‘heavy-tailed’, i.e., nonintegrable at infinity, and establish results of the form ‘smooth solutions must flock’ under this heavy-tail assumption, cf.
[
15
]
and many later references. It is more realistic, however, to assume that distant agents do not communicate directly with one another—i.e., that the protocol is ‘local’—and thus that
\phi(x,y,t)
should be zero if
x
and
y
are sufficiently far apart. For such local protocols, the only mechanism through which information can propagate longer distances is through
in
direct communication, which requires a ‘chain’ of communicating agents, or, in the context of (
1
), some sort of control from below on the density or a related quantity.

Establishing alignment through chain-connectivity arguments is a delicate matter; we mention two papers where such arguments appear. One is the work
[
24
]
by Morales, the second author, and Tadmor, where it is assumed that the communication protocol is extremely strong (relative to features of the initial configuration
(\rho_{0},u_{0})
) for small
|x-y|
. The other work, which is more relevant to our present considerations, is due to Shvydkoy and Tadmor
[
34
]
, and it takes a complementary approach. Rather than assuming once and for all that the communication is sufficiently strong, the authors build into their protocol a mechanism that ‘boosts’
\phi
in regions where
\rho
is small—precisely where it is needed most. More concretely,
[
34
]
introduces a dependence of
\phi(x,y,t)
on the so-called
topological distance

\,\mathrm{d}_{\rho(t)}(x,y)
between
x
and
y
at time
t
, which they define to be the integral of
\rho(t)
over the ‘communication domain’
\Omega(x,y)
that lies between them. There is not a canonical choice for the exact shape of
\Omega(x,y)
(cf.
[
30
]
), but one natural approach is to take
\Omega(x,y)
to be an American football-shaped region whose two ends are located at
x
and
y
. The relevance of the topological distance is motivated by field studies, e.g., the StarFlag project (cf.
[
1
]
), and the principle that it encodes is that information travels more slowly in regions of higher density. In other words, intermediate agents cause interference in communication, and thus agents with more mass between them should be considered ‘farther apart’. On the other hand, the information flows undisturbed through regions with low density, similar to the case of Cucker–Dong model with communication reaching
q
-closest neighbors
[
6
,
27
,
23
]
.

With respect to the principles outlined above,
[
34
]
offers a compelling narrative. It provides a criterion on
\rho(t)
that is sufficient to guarantee that alignment occurs, and then demonstrates how that criterion relaxes when topological distance is incorporated into the communication protocol. Thus not only is the topological distance physically relevant, but furthermore, its inclusion in the model actively serves to promote flocking behavior. Our analysis below will showcase some additional features of the topological dynamics, including the extreme case where the communication protocol depends
only
on the topological distance between points.

1.3.
Well-posedness and the Role of Dimension 1

It is well known that systems with the left-hand side like that of (
1
) are dramatically harder to analyze in higher dimensions than they are when the spatial dimension is 1 (at least, from the point of view of well-posedness). The Euler Alignment system is no exception. Suppose for a moment that
\phi=\phi(|x-y|)
is radial. Then (
1
) admits an additional conserved quantity (call it
e
) satisfying, in 1D, a pure continuity equation
\partial_{t}e+\partial_{x}(ue)=0
, cf.
[
38
,
4
,
33
,
10
]
, etc. This quantity
e
is the cornerstone of essentially all existing well-posedness theory for (
1
) in one spatial dimension. However, no natural analog of
e
is known to satisfy a similarly simple equation in higher dimensions, which is part of why the state of the art in 1D is much more developed than the corresponding theory in higher dimensions for this specific system.

In 1D, the communication domain
\Omega(x,y)
in Shvydkoy and Tadmor’s definition of the topological distance degenerates to an interval
(x,y)
, so that for
\rho\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
we have

\,\mathrm{d}_{\rho}(x,y)=\bigg|\int_{x}^{y}\rho(z)\,\mathrm{d}z\bigg|=|M(y)-M(x)|,\qquad M(z)=\int_{-\infty}^{z}\rho(z^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}z^{\prime},

and a ‘topological kernel’ will take the form

(3)

\phi(x,y,t)=\varphi(\!\,\mathrm{d}_{\rho(t)}(x,y),x-y)

for some
\varphi:[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)
. In
[
34
]
, it is shown that, perhaps surprisingly, the Euler Alignment system equipped with
\varphi(\!\,\mathrm{d},z)=\frac{h(|z|)}{\,\mathrm{d}^{\tau}|z|^{1+\alpha-\tau}}
(with
h
smooth,
0<\alpha<2
, and
\tau>0
) still admits an ‘
e
-quantity’; this quantity is crucial for their theory of existence and uniqueness of 1D strong solutions in the periodic, vacuum-free setting.

In Section
2
below, we demonstrate that the Euler Alignment system corresponding to any
\varphi(\!\,\mathrm{d},z)
satisfying some mild regularity properties (including boundedness) also admits an
e
-quantity. We will establish that global-in-time classical solutions exist when this quantity is nonnegative at time zero; this is essentially an extension of the positive direction of the so-called ‘critical threshold condition’ of Carrillo, Choi, Tadmor, and Tan
[
4
]
, which applies to bounded, Euclidean kernels
\phi=\phi(|x-y|)
. Unlike the case considered in
[
4
]
, it is not clear whether
e_{0}\geq 0
is a
sharp
condition in our case; the topological contribution seems to promote well-posedness, but it is difficult to quantify exactly by how much.

1.4.
Purely Topological Protocols and Reformulation of the System

After establishing global-in-time existence of classical solutions under the threshold condition
e_{0}\geq 0
, we will concern ourselves with an additional simplification in 1D of the system, which arises in the very special but instructive case where
\phi
is ‘purely topological’, i.e.,
\varphi(\!\,\mathrm{d},z)=\varphi(\!\,\mathrm{d})
. More precisely, we assume that
\varphi:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
is nonnegative, possibly singular at zero (but nowhere else), and that it is at least locally Lipschitz continuous away from
0
. The communication protocol
\phi(x,y,t)
then takes the form

(4)

\phi(x,y,t)=\varphi(\!\,\mathrm{d}_{\rho(t)}(x,y)).

For reference, we explicitly record the system (
1
) specialized to the case of dimension 1 and
\phi
as in (
4
):

(5)

\left\{\begin{split}\partial_{t}\rho+\partial_{x}(\rho u)&=0,\qquad(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}\times(0,\infty),\\
\partial_{t}(\rho u)+\partial_{x}(\rho u^{2})&=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(\!\,\mathrm{d}_{\rho(t)}(x,y))(u(x,t)-u(y,t))\rho(x,t)\rho(y,t)\,\mathrm{d}y.\end{split}\right.

Let us reformulate it formally in mass coordinates (see e.g.
[
22
,
26
]
). Suppose
(\rho,u)
is a non-vacuous and sufficiently smooth solution of (
5
); let
M(\cdot,t)
be a cumulative distribution of
\rho(\cdot,t)
, and define
v:(0,1)\to\mathbb{R}
via
v(M(x,t),t)=u(x,t)
. Then we have

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v(M(x,t),t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}v(M(x,t),t)-\partial_{t}M(x,t)\partial_{m}v(M(x,t),t)

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}u(x,t)+u(x,t)\partial_{x}M(x,t)\partial_{m}v(M(x,t),t)=(\partial_{t}u+u\partial_{x}u)(x,t)

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(\!\,\mathrm{d}_{\rho(t)}(x,y))(u(x,t)-u(y,t))\rho(y,t)\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(|M(x,t)-M(y,t)|)(v(M(x,t),t)-v(M(y,t),t))\partial_{y}M(y,t)\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{1}\varphi(|M(x,t)-m^{\prime}|)(v(M(x,t),t)-v(m^{\prime},t))\,\mathrm{d}m^{\prime},

which leads to

(6)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}v(m,t)=-\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\varphi(|m-m^{\prime}|)(v(m,t)-v(m^{\prime},t))\,\mathrm{d}m^{\prime},\qquad(m,t)\in(0,1)\times(0,\infty),\\
\partial_{t}M(x,t)+\partial_{x}(A(M(x,t),t))=0,\qquad A(m,t)=\int_{0}^{m}v(m^{\prime},t)\,\mathrm{d}m^{\prime}\end{cases}

The point of this reformulation is that the unknown
v
decouples completely. Once a solution for (
6
)
1
is found, the flux
A
can be computed and (
6
)
2
solved, using the theory of scalar conservation laws.
We perform a rigorous analysis of equivalence between (
5
) and (
6
) in Section
3
.

1.5.
Outline of the Paper and Statement of Results

This paper initiates the study of four main questions, the last three of which concern the case of purely topological kernels.

(i)

Which initial data
(\rho_{0},u_{0})
lead to global-in-time classical solutions for the Euler Alignment system with bounded topological protocols?

(ii)

In what sense is the system (
6
) well-posed, and how does the well-posedness depend on
\varphi
?

(iii)

When exactly does a solution
(v,M)
of (

(\rho,u)
that satisfies (

(iv)

Finally, how do solutions
(v,M)
of (

(\rho,u)
? In particular, for which kernels
\varphi

These questions are of course intertwined, but we basically address question (i) in Section

3

, and question (iv) in Section

. We give a brief summary.

1.5.1.
A Threshold Condition for Global Existence of Classical Solutions

As mentioned above, our first result concerns classical solutions of the system with regular topological protocols.

Theorem 1.1
.

Consider the Euler Alignment system (
1
) in one spatial dimension, with communication protocol of the form (
3
), where
\varphi=\varphi(\!\,\mathrm{d},z)
is Lipschitz and nonincreasing in
\,\mathrm{d}
, as well as even and bounded in
z
.
Assume
\rho_{0},u_{0}\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
and define
\psi_{0}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
via

(7)

\psi_{0}(\alpha):=u_{0}(\alpha)+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Phi\big(\!\,\mathrm{d}_{\rho_{0}}(\alpha,\gamma),\alpha-\gamma\big)\rho_{0}(\gamma)\,\mathrm{d}\gamma,\qquad\Phi(\!\,\mathrm{d},z)=\int_{0}^{z}\varphi(\!\,\mathrm{d},\zeta)\,\mathrm{d}\zeta

If
\psi_{0}
is nondecreasing, then there exists a unique global-in-time classical solution associated to the initial data
(\rho_{0},u_{0})
.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
is our first goal and can be found in Section
2
.

Remark 1.2
.

Interestingly, the threshold for global-in-time classical solutions of purely topological dynamics can be formulated in mass coordinates in a way that is completely independent of the initial density
\rho_{0}
; see Remark
1.4
. This feature sharply distinguishes the purely topological setting from most known critical threshold results for Euler–alignment and porous medium–type dynamics, where the density plays a fundamental role.

We remark that the uniqueness part of the assertion above should be understood to mean that
\rho
is uniquely determined, and that
u(t)
is uniquely determined in
\supp\rho(t)
for each
t\geq 0
. This version of the Theorem is stated in slightly simplified form; in fact, we really only need
\psi_{0}
to be nondecreasing inside the set
U:=\{x:\rho_{0}(x)>0\}
. It is also possible to include, for instance, the case where
U
consists of finitely many open intervals and
\rho_{0}
and
u_{0}
are not required to be
C^{1}
(or even defined) outside
U
. Our proof of Theorem
1.1
follows the approach of the first author in
[
21
]
, see also
[
20
]
. The ‘
e
-quantity’ emerges as the spatial derivative of a time-dependent version of
\psi_{0}
, and indeed, our criterion for global-in-time existence can be written as
e_{0}:=\partial_{x}\psi_{0}\geq 0
. We will give a precise formula for
\psi
in Section
2
, and we will also discuss a partial converse of Theorem
1.1
in Remark
2.2
.

In the microscopic and mesoscopic (kinetic) level, transformations akin to (
7
) serve as a singularity reduction of the kernel
\varphi
in kinetic descriptions of alignment dynamics. In
[
5
,
28
]
(see also
[
14
,
18
]
) it is shown that the second-order Cucker–Smale-type dynamics with communication
\widetilde{\varphi}(s)=s^{-\alpha}
with
\alpha\in(0,1)
can be equivalently reformulated as first-order dynamics with communication
\widetilde{\Phi}(s)\approx s^{1-\alpha}
. Article
[
29
]
reveals a gradient flow structure of such models.

1.5.3.
Well-posedness and Focus on the
v
-equation

Our requirements on a ‘weak solution’ of (
6
) include the stipulation that
(m,t)\longmapsto v(m,t)
is essentially bounded, which guarantees that
A
is Lipschitz in
m
, uniformly with respect to time. The standard Kruzhkov theory
[
19
]
then guarantees the existence of a unique entropy solution
M
of (
6
)
2
associated to any initial condition in our classes of interest. Therefore, a mass-distributional solution will exist on a time interval that is as long as is allowed by
v
, and this solution will be admissible for as long as
M
remains absolutely continuous.

The hypotheses of Theorem
1.1
, suitably translated to the
(v,M)
framework, provide a natural criterion for
M
to remain absolutely continuous for all time. On the other hand, a more comprehensive study of conditions guaranteeing absolute continuity of
M
lie outside the scope of this paper. Rather, we focus on understanding the
v
equation, which will provide us with weak solutions of (
6
) and carries most information related to stability and long-time behavior. We consider two main cases: bounded
\varphi
and
\varphi(r)=C_{s}r^{-1-2s}
for some

(and some constant
C_{s}
). These two cases must be treated in different ways. Indeed, let us denote the right side of (
6

; that is, define

(Lf)(m):=\int_{0}^{1}\varphi(|m-m^{\prime}|)\big(f(m)-f(m^{\prime})\big)\,\mathrm{d}m^{\prime}.

If
\varphi
is bounded, then

is a bounded operator from

to itself, and

-valued solutions of (
6
)

(which remain in

\varphi(r)=C_{s}r^{-1-2s}

(which makes (

is unbounded, and one must prescribe its domain in order to complete the definition. Two possible choices for the domain (spelled out in Section

and

, denoted
\Lambda_{\mathsf{D}}
and

We compile the main takeaways regarding existence of solutions in the following Theorem; more complete statements will be provided below.

Theorem 1.5
.

(a)

Suppose that

2.
Global-in-Time Classical Solutions for Regular Topological Protocols

This section is dedicated to the discussion of classical solutions related to topological protocols in 1D. In particular, we shall prove Theorem
1.1
.

The initial value problem for the system (
1
), written in Lagrangian coordinates (with reference domain
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and reference density
\rho_{0}
) is given by

(10)

\begin{cases}\dot{X}(\alpha,t)=V(\alpha,t),\\
\dot{V}(\alpha,t)=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi(X(\alpha,t),X(\gamma,t),t)(V(\alpha,t)-V(\gamma,t))\rho_{0}(\gamma)\,\mathrm{d}\gamma\\
X(\alpha,0)=\alpha,\quad V(\alpha,0)=u_{0}(\alpha).\end{cases}

Given a classical solution
(X,V)
of (
10
), it is clear that one obtains a classical solution
(\rho,u)
of (
1
) through the relationships
\rho(X(\alpha,t),t)\det\nabla_{\alpha}X(\alpha,t)=\rho_{0}(\alpha)
and
u(X(\alpha,t),t)=V(\alpha,t)
, on any time interval
[0,T)
on which the following conditions are satisfied:

(a)

\alpha\longmapsto X(\alpha,t)
remains invertible.

(b)

\alpha\longmapsto\det\nabla_{\alpha}X(\alpha,t)
remains bounded from below.

When
d=1
, conservation of mass implies that

\,\mathrm{d}_{\rho(t)}(X(\alpha,t),X(\gamma,t))=\,\mathrm{d}_{\rho_{0}}(\alpha,\gamma),

so that under the assumption (
3
), we have

\phi(X(\alpha,t),X(\gamma,t),t)=\varphi(\!\,\mathrm{d}_{\rho_{0}}(\alpha,\gamma),X(\alpha,t)-X(\gamma,t)).

Consequently, when
\varphi(\!\,\mathrm{d},z)
is Lipschitz in
\,\mathrm{d}
and bounded in
z
, equation (
10
)
2
can be rewritten (still under the assumption that
d=1
) as

\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\bigg[V(\alpha,t)+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Phi(\!\,\mathrm{d}_{\rho_{0}}(\alpha,\gamma),X(\alpha,t)-X(\gamma,t))\rho_{0}(\gamma)\,\mathrm{d}\gamma\bigg]=0,

where
\Phi(\!\,\mathrm{d},z)=\int_{0}^{z}\varphi(\!\,\mathrm{d},\zeta)\,\mathrm{d}\zeta
, as in (
7
). Therefore, under the hypotheses of Theorem
1.1
, the system (
10
) reduces to

(11)

\begin{cases}\dot{X}(\alpha,t)=\psi_{0}(\alpha)-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Phi(\!\,\mathrm{d}_{\rho_{0}}(\alpha,\gamma),X(\alpha,t)-X(\gamma,t))\rho_{0}(\gamma)\,\mathrm{d}\gamma,\\
X(\alpha,0)=\alpha.\end{cases}

It is clear (e.g. from the Picard Theorem) that this system has a global-in-time classical solution
X(\alpha,t)
under our working hypotheses. We show that it translates to a global-in-time solution
(\rho,u)
of the Euler Alignment system, whenever
\psi_{0}
is nondecreasing. It suffices to verify the conditions (a) and (b) on the flow map
X(\cdot,t)
that were mentioned above. We do both simultaneously by providing a lower bound on the difference quotient
\frac{X(\beta,t)-X(\alpha,t)}{\beta-\alpha}
, for
\alpha<\beta
. We have

\displaystyle\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\big[X(\beta,t)-X(\alpha,t)\big]

\displaystyle=\psi_{0}(\beta)-\psi_{0}(\alpha)-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigg(\Phi\big(\!\,\mathrm{d}_{\rho_{0}}(\beta,\gamma),X(\beta,t)-X(\gamma,t)\big)

\displaystyle\hskip 122.34685pt-\Phi\big(\!\,\mathrm{d}_{\rho_{0}}(\alpha,\gamma),X(\alpha,t)-X(\gamma,t)\big)\bigg)\rho_{0}(\gamma)\,\mathrm{d}\gamma

\displaystyle=\psi_{0}(\beta)-\psi_{0}(\alpha)-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}\xi}\Phi(\!\,\mathrm{d}_{\rho_{0}}(\xi,\gamma),X(\xi,t)-X(\gamma,t))\,\mathrm{d}\xi\,\rho_{0}(\gamma)\,\mathrm{d}\gamma.

Furthermore,

\displaystyle\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}\xi}\Phi(\!\,\mathrm{d}_{\rho_{0}}(\xi,\gamma),X(\xi,t)-X(\gamma,t))

\displaystyle=\partial_{1}\Phi(\!\,\mathrm{d}_{\rho_{0}}(\xi,\gamma),X(\xi,t)-X(\gamma,t))\cdot\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}\xi}\bigg|\int_{\gamma}^{\xi}\rho_{0}\bigg|

\displaystyle\qquad+\varphi(\!\,\mathrm{d}_{\rho_{0}}(\xi,\gamma),X(\xi,t)-X(\gamma,t))\partial_{\xi}X(\xi,t)

\displaystyle=\bigg(\int_{0}^{X(\xi,t)-X(\gamma,t)}\partial_{1}\varphi(\!\,\mathrm{d}_{\rho_{0}}(\xi,\gamma),\zeta)\,\mathrm{d}\zeta\bigg)\rho_{0}(\xi)\mathrm{sgn}(\xi-\gamma)

\displaystyle\qquad+\varphi(\!\,\mathrm{d}_{\rho(t)}(X(\xi,t),X(\gamma,t)),X(\xi,t)-X(\gamma,t))\partial_{\xi}X(\xi,t).

Since
\varphi
is nonincreasing in its first component by assumption, it follows that the first term on the right is nonpositive, so that, putting everything together, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\big[X(\beta,t)-X(\alpha,t)\big]

\displaystyle=\psi_{0}(\beta)-\psi_{0}(\alpha)+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}\bigg|\int_{0}^{X(\xi,t)-X(\gamma,t)}\partial_{1}\varphi(\,\mathrm{d}_{\rho_{0}}(\xi,\gamma),\zeta)\,\mathrm{d}\zeta\bigg|\rho_{0}(\xi)\rho_{0}(\gamma)\,\mathrm{d}\xi\,\mathrm{d}\gamma

\displaystyle\qquad-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{X(\alpha,t)}^{X(\beta,t)}\varphi(\!\,\mathrm{d}_{\rho(t)}(y,X(\gamma,t)),y-X(\gamma,t))\,\mathrm{d}y\,\rho_{0}(\gamma)\,\mathrm{d}\gamma

\displaystyle\geq\psi_{0}(\beta)-\psi_{0}(\alpha)-\|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}}\big[X(\beta,t)-X(\alpha,t)\big],

where we have dropped the second term entirely. Grönwall’s inequality then implies that

\frac{X(\beta,t)-X(\alpha,t)}{\beta-\alpha}\geq\exp(-\|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}}t)+\frac{\psi_{0}(\beta)-\psi_{0}(\alpha)}{\beta-\alpha}\cdot\frac{1-\exp(-\|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}}t)}{\|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}}}.

This proves Theorem
1.1
.

Remark 2.1
.

Defining

\psi(x,t)=u(x,t)+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Phi(\!\,\mathrm{d}_{\rho(t)}(x,y),x-y)\rho(y,t)\,\mathrm{d}y,

we have

\partial_{t}\psi+u\partial_{x}\psi=0.

Consequently,
e=\partial_{x}\psi
satisfies

\partial_{t}e+\partial_{x}(eu)=0,

provided that
\rho
and
u
are sufficiently regular. The analysis of threshold conditions in Euler Alignment systems is often formulated in terms of
e
(and sometimes
q=\frac{e}{\rho}
, which satisfies
\partial_{t}q+u\partial_{x}q=0
) rather than
\psi
.

Remark 2.2
.

For protocols of the form
\phi=\phi(|x-y|)
, the second term in the expansion of
\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\big[X(\beta,t)-X(\alpha,t)\big]
(which we dropped) is absent, so that
\psi_{0}(\beta)-\psi_{0}(\alpha)
serves as an
upper
bound on this time derivative. If
\psi_{0}(\beta)-\psi_{0}(\alpha)
is negative, then the solution must eventually lose regularity in this case, at or before time
T=\frac{\psi_{0}(\alpha)-\psi_{0}(\beta)}{\beta-\alpha}
. This gives rise to a clean, critical threshold condition: regularity persists if
\psi_{0}
is everywhere nondecreasing, but not if it decreases somewhere. In the case where
\phi
has a topological component, the sign-definite term that we dropped complicates the picture. A better understanding of this term would allow us to make more precise statements involving sufficient conditions for finite-time loss of regularity. For instance, if we know
a priori
that
\diam\supp(\rho(t))\leq\mathcal{D}
for all
t
in the time interval of regularity, then the term in question is bounded above by
\|\partial_{1}\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}}\mathcal{D}\,\mathrm{d}_{\rho_{0}}(\alpha,\beta)
, and a sufficient condition for finite-time loss of regularity is

\psi_{0}(\beta)-\psi_{0}(\alpha)<-\|\partial_{1}\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}}\mathcal{D}\,\mathrm{d}_{\rho_{0}}(\alpha,\beta),

for some
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, with
\alpha<\beta
.
Up to minor technical details, this condition can be reformulated as

q_{0}(\alpha)<-\|\partial_{1}\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}}\mathcal{D},

for some
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
.

Remark 3.4

(Existence of weak solutions)
.

4.1.
The Dirichlet and Neumann Regional Fractional Laplacian Operators

To proceed, we must choose a domain for

m\in(0,1)
we have
v(m)-\bar{v}=\int_{0}^{1}v(m)-v(m^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}m^{\prime}
and thus

\displaystyle|v(m)-\bar{v}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left|\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{\varphi(|m-m^{\prime}|)}(v(m)-v(m^{\prime}))\ \frac{1}{\sqrt{\varphi(|m-m^{\prime}|)}}\,\mathrm{d}m^{\prime}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{\varphi(|m-m^{\prime}|)}\,\mathrm{d}m^{\prime}\int_{0}^{1}\varphi(|m-m^{\prime}|)|v(m)-v(m^{\prime})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}m^{\prime}.

Taking the supremum in the former integral on the right-hand side above and integrating the latter with respect to
m
yields the desired inequality.
∎

Remark 5.2
.

The constant
c_{\varphi}
is finite in many natural cases. For instance:

1.

If
\varphi(r)\geq\varepsilon>0
on
[0,1]
, then

\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{\varphi(|m-m^{\prime}|)}\,\mathrm{d}m^{\prime}\leq\varepsilon^{-1},

hence
c_{\varphi}<\infty
.

2.

Condition
c_{\varphi}<\infty
does not require
\varphi
to be
bounded away from zero, Indeed, if
\varphi(r)=(1-r)^{\alpha}
with
\alpha\in(0,1)
, then

\varphi(1)=0
, yet

\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{(1-|m-m^{\prime}|)^{\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}m^{\prime}<\infty

for every
m\in[0,1]
(the only singularity is integrable for

\alpha<1
). Thus
c_{\varphi}<\infty
even though
\varphi
vanishes at
r=1
.

3.

If
\varphi=0
on a set of positive measure, then

1/\varphi=\infty
on that set and
c_{\varphi}=+\infty
; in particular,
short-range kernels in the mass variable are excluded here.

4.

In the singular case of
\varphi(r)=C_{s}r^{-1-2s}
with
s\in(0,1)
we have

c_{\varphi}\leq 2C_{s}\int_{0}^{1}|m^{\prime}|^{1+2s}\,\mathrm{d}m^{\prime}<\infty.

Suppose that

Remark 5.6
.

The statement above and proof below are formulated for the Neumann regional fractional Laplacian, but the Dirichlet case follows in a similar fashion.

Proof.

The proof follows by a standard asymptotic estimate

\|v(t)\|_{C^{0,\alpha}}\xrightarrow{t\to+\infty}0,

for the fractional heat equation coupled with a direct change of variables performed
for all

m\in(0,1)
. We sketch the proof for the reader’s convenience, referring to
[
2
]
for details.
By linearity,
\omega:=v_{1}-v_{2}
is a solution of (
6
)
1
in the sense of Definition
4.3
.
The inverse of the regional fractional Laplacian

in

is a compact and self-adjoint operator on

of

consisting of eigenvectors of

(with respective eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}
associated with

). One can also show that the eigenvalue associated with constant functions is
\lambda_{0}=0
, while all others are positive, with the smallest determined by the Rayleigh quotient

0<\lambda_{1}:=\inf\left\{\frac{{\mathcal{E}}_{\varphi}(v,v)}{\|v\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}^{2}}\;:\;v\in W^{s,2}(0,1)\setminus\{0\},\ \int_{0}^{1}v=0\right\}.

In this basis,

\omega(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda_{i}t}\omega_{i}e_{i},\quad t>0,

and for

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}_{\varphi}(\omega(t),\omega(t))

\displaystyle=(\Lambda^{s}_{{\mathsf{N}}}\omega(t),\omega(t))=\sum_{i=1}^{+\infty}\lambda_{i}e^{-2\lambda_{i}t}|\omega_{i}|^{2}

{\mathcal{E}}_{\varphi}(\omega(t),\omega(t))\leq e^{-2\lambda_{1}(t-\tau)}{\mathcal{E}}_{\varphi}(\omega(\tau),\omega(\tau))\leq e^{-\lambda_{1}(t-\tau)}\|\omega(\tau)\|_{W^{s,2}}^{2}.

In particular, since, by Remark

∎

6.
Conclusions and Outlook

The main goal of this paper was to investigate the relationship between purely topological alignment dynamics and its reformulation in mass coordinates, which leads to a decoupling of the velocity equation. We focused on well-posedness and long-time behavior of solutions corresponding to both bounded and singular communication kernels. In the latter case, the velocity equation reduces to a regional fractional heat equation.

Part of the appeal of purely topological alignment dynamics lies in the simplicity of the arguments used to establish stability and alignment estimates, as demonstrated in Section
5
. This naturally raises the question of long-time behavior in the case of short-range purely topological communication protocols, where the support of the interaction kernel
\varphi
is contained in some interval
[0,\rho]\subset[0,1)
. In such a setting, the condition
c_{\varphi}<+\infty
, required for the Poincaré inequality in Lemma
5.1
, is no longer satisfied.

Nevertheless, alternative approaches based on chain connectivity arguments (see, for instance,
[
24
,
32
]
and related works of Tadmor
[
36
,
37
]
) have been successfully employed to propagate interactions from short to long ranges through regions of positive density. Interestingly, in the purely topological setting, interactions appear to propagate even through regions of very low density,
\rho\ll\varepsilon
, suggesting a more accessible mechanism that merits further investigation.

Another natural direction for future research concerns the mean-field limit. On the one hand, the velocity equation is fully decoupled from the rest of the system and, consequently, its evolution is insensitive to whether it transports an atomic measure or a continuum limit of empirical measures. Moreover, the scalar conservation law governing the cumulative distribution function
M
admits approximations by piecewise constant functions
M^{N}
(for instance, via a wave-front tracking argument), which are naturally associated with empirical measures
\rho^{N}=\partial_{x}M^{N}
. On the other hand, even with this structural information, it remains highly non-trivial to determine whether the corresponding particle system describing the evolution of the atoms of
\rho^{N}
satisfies a closed system of ODEs of the form (
2
).

These considerations lead to the following questions:

Q1

Is it possible to employ chain connectivity arguments to propagate interactions induced by short-range communication protocols without imposing assumptions on the magnitude of the agents’ density?

Q2

Can mass-distributional solutions be rigorously constructed as mean-field limits of solutions to ODE systems of the form (
2
)?

We believe that addressing these questions will contribute to a deeper understanding of purely topological alignment dynamics and help bridge the gap between particle-based models and their continuum descriptions.
