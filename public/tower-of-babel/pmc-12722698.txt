Development and optimisation of green flame retardant properties of cotton fabric treated with chitosan nanoparticles and phosphate using Taguchi method
Authors: Ismail Hossain, Md. Abdul Alim, Nayeem Uddin, Suprio Shantanu Saha, Md. Shohan Parvez, Md. Maniruzzaman, Mohammad Salman Haque, Anirban Mallick
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The inherent flammability of cotton poses a significant fire risk during combustion. In this work, an eco-friendly and sustainable flame-retardant (FR) cotton woven fabric was fabricated using bio-based chitosan nanoparticles (ChNPs) and diammonium phosphate (DAP). The Taguchi method was employed to optimise the FR treatment process, utilising an L9 orthogonal array design comprising nine experiments with three replications each. Chemical characterization of the treated fabrics was performed using Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR). The successful attachment of ChNPs and DAP to the cotton fabric surface was confirmed through Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) and Energy-Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDX). The size and morphology of ChNPs were analysed using Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM). Thermogravimetric Analysis (TGA) was conducted to assess the thermal stability and percentage of weight loss in the fabric samples. The treated fabrics were further evaluated for flame spread time and tensile strength. The study successfully identified the optimal treatment conditions as a DAP concentration of 6%, a ChNP concentration of 5 g/L, and a curing temperature of 160 °C. The results showed that flame retardancy improved significantly with increasing DAP concentration and curing temperature, while ChNPs contributed moderately to the FR performance. TGA indicated that untreated cotton completely degraded around 510 °C, whereas treated samples remained thermally stable up to 700 °C, with a residue of 34%. In conclusion, the application of the Taguchi method proved to be an effective approach for optimising flame-retardant treatment parameters, enhancing the thermal and fire-resistant properties of cotton fabric in an eco-friendly and sustainable manner.

Introduction

Nowadays, cotton textile fabrics are used in a wide range of applications, including apparel and fashion textiles, home textiles, medical textiles, and industrial and technical textiles, due to their vibrant color shades, hydrophilic properties, breathability, softness, comfort, biodegradability, renewability, and eco-friendliness compared to synthetic fibre fabrics1–7. Nevertheless, their intrinsic flammability limits their range of applications in certain areas, such as home furnishings, hospitals, railways, and the oil, gas, and fossil fuel sectors8. During a fire, cotton materials can cause tragic losses to both human life and property2,3,9,10. Therefore, there is a pressing need for the development of flame-retardant (FR) textiles2,8. The demand for fire-safe materials is growing, with an increasing emphasis on durability, recyclability, environmental sustainability, and commercial feasibility8,11–15. A few works have been reported in the past study regarding FR properties are presented as follows:

Basak et al. developed an eco-friendly FR cotton textile using extracted natural spinach leaf juice13. Repon et al. applied three different fire retardant chemicals, including Borax, Di-ammonium phosphate (DAP), and Thiourea, to improve the fire retardancy of Jute fabric by the padding method14. Younis investigated the effect of non-durable coating, namely DAP, Boric acid, Urea, and Sodium borate, on the flammability and thermal properties of polyester fabric15. Patel examined the influence of household softeners on the FR and thermal behaviour of textile products16. Mayer-Gall et al. developed FR cotton, cotton/polyester, and cotton/nylon fabrics using a green water-soluble Cyclophosphazene (CPZ)17. Li et al. made eco-friendly FR cotton fabrics by using reactive products of methyldimethoxysilane and phytic acid18. Samanta et al. analysed the effect of ammonium sulfamate and & urea on the FR performance of the jute fabric19. Karim-nejad et al. applied response surface methodology (RSM) to optimize the effect of sodium hypophosphite (SHP), citric acid (CA), and nano-ZnO in FR properties of mercerized cotton fabric20. Qutab et al. developed novel phosphorus-based flame-retardant cotton fabrics using the non-toxic ingredient diammonium hydrogen phosphate (DAHP) and citric acid under normal, inert, and nitrogen atmospheres21. Veerappagounder et al. investigated the flame-retardant properties of cotton fabric treated with diammonium phosphate, cyclodextrin, and butane tetracarboxylic acid (BTCA)10. Gao et al. produced bio-based flame-retardant cotton fabrics using a reactive phosphorus–nitrogen-based intumescent flame retardant (IFR) system comprising diethyl phosphite, urea, and glutaraldehyde through the Kabachnik–Fields reaction22. Wu et al. modified cotton fabric using a novel reactive phosphorus–nitrogen (P–N) intumescent flame retardant composed of pentaerythritol, cyanuric chloride, and triethyl phosphite to produce durable flame-retardant cotton fabric2. Fang et al. constructed a fully bio-based and eco-friendly coating for polyester/cotton blend fabric using chitosan and phytic acid. Zhang et al. developed biomass-assisted flame retardants for cotton fabrics and attributed the acceptable value of limiting oxygen index (LOI) of ≈ 27 to the char-forming character of the used flame retardant23. Wang et al. produced bio-based flame-retardant cotton fabrics using a phosphorus–nitrogen flame retardant and evaluated their performance through vertical flammability and limiting oxygen index (LOI) tests24. Attia et al. developed scalable flame-retardant, antibacterial, and UV-protective spherical nanoparticles using green precursors derived from Molokhia leaves25. Emam et al. employed a green synthesis approach to fabricate environmentally friendly and effective nanocoatings for textile fabrics, imparting flame-retardant, antibacterial, and UV-protective properties, using upcycled shrimp shells, rice husks, and Molokhia stems8.

However, due to health and environmental concerns, the use of synthetic and conventional halogenated flame retardants in the fabrication of FR textiles has been globally restricted. In this context, researchers and industries have been striving to develop flame-retardant materials that are more environmentally friendly and sustainable2,11,26,27. Chitosan, on the other hand, is a biocompatible, biodegradable, nontoxic, and environmentally friendly and carbon-rich natural flame retardant that can be used to develop FR cotton fabrics9,27,29. Moreover, chitosan nanoparticles exhibit superior performance compared to their bulk form due to their large surface area-to-volume ratio11. Nevertheless, chitosan is not used alone as a flame retardant for achieving effective and durable flame retardancy on cotton fabrics due to its poor fire-retardant performance, low adhesion, lack of strong intumescent behaviour, and insufficient release of non-flammable gases, such as ammonia and water, to suppress flames21,26. In this context, effective and durable flame-retardant textiles can be produced using chitosan in combination with phosphorus-based compounds such as diammonium phosphate(DAP) and citric acid a cross-linking agent26,30. However, traditional methods for fabrication of bio-based flame-retardant textiles rely on a trial-and-error approach, which is time-consuming, resource-intensive, and costly31. Moreover, various factors influence the flame-retardant efficiency of treated textile materials, including fibre content, fabric weight and structure, types of flame retardant agents, processing conditions, and garment design. Therefore, it is essential to control and optimise all these factors using a systematic approach to identify the key contributors and achieve optimal flame-retardant (FR) performance of the treated material. Conventional optimisation methods based on single-parameter variation or full factorial experimental designs have proven inadequate in this context, as they are resource-intensive, costly, time-consuming, and difficult to control32,33.

In this case, the Taguchi Method (TM) is a practical approach for optimisation and prediction in engineering quality. It employs a unique orthogonal array design to investigate the entire parameter space using only a small number of trials and provides a practical, simple, and structured method for optimisation that is faster and more cost-effective, while also enabling the identification of key factors influencing the process32,34,35. To the best of our knowledge, no work has been reported on optimising the process variables affecting the flame-retardant (FR) performance of cotton woven fabric treated with DAP and ChNPs using the Taguchi approach.

Therefore, the key motivation behind this work is to develop an eco-friendly and sustainable flame-retardant cotton woven fabric based on DAP, ChNP, and citric acid concentrations and curing temperature, and to optimise the interactive process variables using Taguchi methodology. By adopting an appropriate optimisation approach, a production engineer can improve resource efficiency, reduce reliance on trial-and-error methods, minimise production costs and waste, enhance productivity and quality, mitigate environmental impact, and foster innovation in smart manufacturing — all of which support the Sustainable Development Goals of the United Nations. The treated fabrics were evaluated for flame spread time and tensile strength. The chemical structures of the untreated and treated cotton fabrics were analysed using FTIR. The size and morphology of ChNPs were examined using TEM. The surface morphology and elemental composition were studied using SEM and EDX, respectively. Additionally, TGA was employed to assess degradation and combustion behaviours of the fabric samples.

Experimental section

Materials

Pre-treated plain woven cotton fabric with an areal density of 140 g/m², 40 ends per inch (EPI), 40 picks per inch (PPI), warp count of 120, and weft count of 72 was sourced from a local factory in Dhaka. Low molecular weight chitosan (degree of deacetylation: 90%), sodium tripolyphosphate (TPP), and citric acid were purchased from SRL Private Limited (India) and used without further purification. Polysilgen, a silicon-based softener, was supplied by CHT, Bangladesh.

Experimental methods

Taguchi statistical method

The Taguchi methods, developed by Prof. Genichi Taguchi in the 1950 s, emphasize the practical application of engineering principles rather than relying on a complex statistical framework. The Taguchi technique for efficient optimization consists of six main steps: (i) identifying control parameters and their influence on overall quality performance, (ii) designing an appropriate experimental layout, (iii) conducting the experiments, (iv) analysing the test results, (v) performing variance analysis, and (vi) conducting a validation experiment32,36,37. The objective of the Taguchi optimization method is to determine the optimal combination of process control parameters that minimizes response variation in the presence of noise factors. Taguchi introduced the Signal-to-Noise (S/N) ratio, measured in decibels (dB), as a key quality metric and performance indicator. An increase in the S/N ratio indicates improved product quality. By minimizing the influence of external noise factors that may adversely impact process performance, this method enhances the S/N ratio. The three most commonly used types of S/N ratios are: (i) Smaller-the-Better, (ii) Larger-the-Better, and (iii) Nominal-the-Best32,38, which can be mathematically represented as follows:

Preparation of ChNPs

The ionic gelation method was employed to produce chitosan nanoparticles (ChNPs), with sodium tripolyphosphate (TPP) serving as the crosslinking agent. Chitosan solutions of 0.2% and 0.6% (w/v) were prepared by dissolving chitosan in 1% (v/v) acetic acid. The pH of the solution was carefully adjusted to 5 by gradually adding 10 N NaOH. TPP, at concentrations ranging from 0.2% to 0.3% (w/v), was then added to the chitosan solution under continuous stirring, resulting in the immediate formation of ChNPs. The nanoparticles were collected by centrifuging the mixture at 6500 rpm for 30 min at 4 °C. The ChNPs were thoroughly rinsed with distilled water to remove any remaining alkaline residues. The preparation process of the chitosan nanoparticles is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Treatment of fabric

After the experimental setup, a total of nine cotton woven samples were chemically treated in three replicates according to the Taguchi L9 OA (Table 2), using a laboratory exhaust system, as per the specified concentrations of DAP (2%, 6%, 10%), ChNP (1 g/l, 3 g/l, 5 g/l), Citric acid 4%, and softener 2 g/l at 40 °C for 20 min, with a material-to-liquor ratio (M: L) of 1:15. The chemically coated samples were then dried at 80 °C for 10 min. Finally, the dried samples were cured at 150 °C, 160 °C, and 170 °C for 3 min. The flame spreading time (FST) of each fabric sample was then calculated using the 45° Flammability test procedure.

Fabric characterization and testing

Before testing, the samples were dried and conditioned at (65 ± 2) % RH and (25 ± 2) °C.

Flammability test

45° angle test (ASTM D1230-94)

A 45° angle flammability test (Fig. 3) is a recognized technique for determining the flammability of materials, particularly fabrics and textiles. This test calculates the length of time it takes for a flame to travel a specific distance along a fabric sample positioned at a 45° angle.

FTIR analysis

The specific chemical bonds formed between the ChNPs, DAP, and cotton cellulose were identified using Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy (Shimadzu IR Tracer-100, Japan).

SEM and EDX study

The surface structure of both unprocessed and processed cotton fabric samples was frequently studied using the SEM (ZEISS Gemini Sigma 300, Germany). Additionally, an energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) spectrometer was used for elemental analysis.

TEM analysis

A transmission electron microscope (TEM) (JEM-1400, JEOL, Japan) operating at an accelerating voltage of 100 kV was used to examine the size and morphology of the ChNPs. The ChNP suspension was ultrasonically dispersed in deionized water, and a small drop of the diluted suspension was placed onto a 300-mesh copper grid coated with a holey carbon film.

TGA analysis

The thermal degradation of chitosan phosphate-treated and untreated cellulose fabrics was evaluated using a thermogravimetric (TG) analyzer. The samples were heated at a rate of 5 °C/min with a PerkinElmer Pyris 1 analyzer to obtain thermogravimetric (TG) and differential thermogravimetric (DTG) curves. For each sample, the maximum degradation rate point (MDRP) was determined as the temperature corresponding to the peak of the DTG curve. Similarly, the thermal degradation onset point (TDOP) was defined as the temperature at which a tangent drawn from the initial weight-loss region of the TG curve intersects with the extrapolated baseline.

Testing of tensile strength (TS)

The TS of fabric samples, both untreated and treated with ChNP and DAP, was assessed using a universal tensile testing equipment (Testometric Win, UK) following the ASTM D5035-95 standard1. The sample’s measurements were 20 cm by 5 cm.

Results and discussion

Fire-retardant mechanism of chitosan, DAP, and citric acid on cotton fabric

The fire-retardant mechanism of chitosan and DAP on cotton fabric works through a synergistic effect involving both condensed and gas phase actions:

During combustion, chitosan releases non-flammable gases like NH₃ and H₂O (Scheme 1) and forms a protective char layer on the cotton surface (Scheme 2) due to its high nitrogen content. The NH₃ and H₂O dilute the oxygen and fuel, and the char layer insulates the fabric, inhibiting heat and mass transfer. Additionally, chitosan and cellulose form a covalent bond and a thermally more stable, three-dimensional cross-linked network in the presence of citric acid (Scheme 3), promoting intumescence where the material swells upon heating to form a foamed char barrier11,26.

Cellulose–OH + Chitosan–OH/NH₂ → Cell–(= O) C–N/O–Chitosan + H₂O.

Scheme 3.

Cell–OH + NH2/OH–Chitosan + Citric Acid → Cell–(= O) C–N/O–Chitosan–Citric–O–Cell.

(Cross-linked network).

DAP decomposes on heating to produce phosphoric acid (H₃PO₄) and ammonia (NH₃) (Scheme 4). H₃PO₄ is a dehydrating agent, and NH₃ is a non-flammable gas that dilutes combustible vapors (oxygen and fuel). Phosphoric acid reacts with the –OH in cellulose and chitosan to form phosphate esters (Schemes 5 and 6). These esters are thermally less reactive and help promote the formation of char. Once phosphate esters are formed, heating continues to cause acid-catalyzed dehydration of cellulose and chitosan (Scheme 7), thereby promoting carbonization rather than the release of flammable gases. The char layer insulates the fabric, blocks oxygen, and slows combustion. Furthermore, citric acid forms a cross-linked network with phosphorylated cellulose (Scheme 8), enhancing thermal stability, durability, and efficient char formation during combustion10,21,22,27,28,39. Due to its cationic nature, chitosan helps bind DAP onto the cotton fabric, improving wash durability. DAP catalyzes the charring of both cellulose and chitosan, increasing thermal stability. The combined system forms a stable, intumescent char barrier, reducing heat release and smoke generation.

Interpretation of the Taguchi experimental data for flame retardancy

The effect of DAP concentration on flame spread time (FST) is illustrated in Fig. 2(a). As shown, FST increases with growing DAP concentration. Notably, FST rises by approximately 4.5% as the DAP concentration increases from 2% to 6%. The underlying reason is that DAP decomposes upon heating to produce phosphoric acid (H₃PO₄) and ammonia (NH₃). The released NH₃, a non-flammable gas, dilutes combustible vapours, while the phosphoric acid promotes the formation of a thermally stable char layer on the fabric surface. This char insulates the fabric, restricts oxygen access, and slows down flame spread across the surface, resulting in an increased flame spread time (FST)10,21,22,27,39. Beyond 6% DAP, the FST decreases as the system becomes oversaturated, resulting in inefficient bonding, reduced stability, and poor retention of flame-retardant components. This weakens the integrity of the protective char layer, allowing flames to spread more rapidly.

Figure 2(b) shows the relationship between average flame spread time (FST) and ChNP concentration. As observed in Fig. 2(b), FST increases from 32.37 s to 32.81 s as the ChNP concentration rises from 1 g/L to 5 g/L. This is because, during combustion, a higher concentration of ChNP releases a large amount of non-flammable NH₃ and H₂O gases, forming a thermally stable protective barrier on the fabric surface. The released NH₃ and H₂O dilute the available oxygen and combustible gases, while the stable char layer insulates the fabric, inhibiting heat and mass transfer. This prevents flame propagation, thereby increasing the flame spread time (FST)11,26.

Figure 2(c) clearly shows that the effect of curing temperature on flame spread time is not linear. The flame spread time (FST) increases as the curing temperature rises from 150 °C to 160 °C. FST increases from 150 °C to 160 °C because the flame-retardant system based on chitosan and DAP becomes more effective at this temperature range due to enhanced chemical bonding, improved char formation, and the optimal release of flame-inhibiting gases, resulting in slower flame propagation and enhanced flame-retardant performance. Conversely, the FST decreases as the curing temperature increases from 160 °C to 170 °C. This can be explained as follows: beyond 160 °C, the amino and hydroxyl groups of chitosan begin to degrade, reducing its ability to bond effectively with phosphate from DAP. This leads to reduced char formation during burning, resulting in a decrease in FST. Similarly, at temperatures above 160 °C, DAP decomposes rapidly, releasing ammonia and phosphoric acid prematurely, before forming strong bonds with chitosan or cellulose. As a result, some phosphorus may be lost through evaporation, leaving less available during combustion to catalyse char formation, ultimately leading to a lower FST.

Tensile strength analysis

Effect of DAP and CT on tensile strength (TS)

Figure 5 shows a 35% increase in TS as DAP concentration rises from 2% to 10% at a curing temperature (CT) of 150 °C. This is because DAP can form chemical bonds with the functional groups in the fibres. These chemical bonds provide strong interfacial adhesion between different phases in the material, resulting in better load transfer and, consequently, higher tensile strength. In contrast, increasing the CT from 160 °C to 170 °C results in an 87% decrease in tensile strength at certain DAP concentrations.

Fig. 5Effect of DAP and CT on tensile strength.

Effect of DAP and CT on tensile strength.

Effect of ChNP and CT on tensile strength

It is clear from Fig. 6 that an increase in ChNP concentration from 1 g/L to 3 g/L at all curing temperatures leads to a 14% reduction in tensile strength (TS). This decrease is likely due to higher ChNP levels disrupting the material’s structure. Conversely, TS improved by 18% when the curing temperature increased from 150 °C to 170 °C at a ChNP concentration of 1 g/L, which can be attributed to the formation of strong covalent bonds between ChNP molecules and the fibre at elevated temperatures.

Characterization of treated fabric

FTIR analysis

Figure 7 depicts the characteristics FTIR absorption peak at different wavelengths. The intensity of the chitosan-coated cotton fabric is lower than that of raw cotton fabric, probably due to the overlap between cellulose and chitosan5,40. The broad absorption band observed at 3421 cm⁻¹ and 2903 cm⁻¹ (Figure Treated) corresponds to the collective absorption of N–H and O–H groups in the polymer, indicating hydrogen bonding between chitosan and the cotton fibres41. The characteristic peak of 1652 cm⁻¹, associated with N–H bending, is present only in treated samples, confirming the presence of chitosan5,42. The characteristic peak at 1070 cm⁻¹ is associated with the P = O stretching vibration, while the peak at 560 cm⁻¹ is attributed to the P-O bending of the phosphate group, confirming the manifestation of phosphate in the treated fabric. A distinctive peak at 1720 cm⁻¹ in the treated sample, attributed to the > C = O stretching vibration of the carbonyl group, also confirms the existence of citric acid, which cannot be found in untreated cotton fabric43.

SEM and EDX analysis

Figure 8 displays the SEM pictures of the cotton fabric samples that were treated (b) and untreated (a). The control sample (a) is clean and free of any chemical coating or deposition. Nevertheless, after being treated with DAP and ChNPs, the surfaces become rougher with multiple chemical coatings or deposits, as clearly visible in Fig. 8(b). TEM analysis

TEM analysis was employed to confirm the formation, shape, and size of the ChNPs. The TEM images revealed that the nanoparticles were predominantly spherical in morphology (Fig. 9a). The size distribution of these nanoparticles was analysed using ImageJ software (ImageJ, Version: 1.54 m, URL Link: https://imagej.net/ij/features.html). The size distribution of ChNPs ranged from 10 to 45 nm, with the average particle size calculated to be 21.5 nm (Fig. 9b).

TGA analysis

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was conducted to evaluate the impact of ChNP and DAP on the thermal stability of the fabric. The results showed that the untreated fabric began to degrade at 228 °C, while the fabric treated with ChNP and DAP exhibited an increased onset thermal degradation temperature of 324 °C (Fig. 10), indicating significantly enhanced thermal stability. Furthermore, the untreated fabric underwent complete thermal degradation at approximately 510 °C. In contrast, the treated fabric remained thermally stable up to around 700 °C, with about 34% of the sample residue remaining at this temperature. This clearly demonstrates the improved thermal resistance imparted by ChNP and DAP. The incorporation of ChNP and DAP not only enhanced the thermal stability of the fabric but also improved its flame-retardant properties, making it highly suitable for applications that demand resistance to elevated temperatures.
