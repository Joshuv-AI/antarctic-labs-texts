Dissecting molecular heterogeneity in primary gastric cancer by IGFBP7-related analysis
Authors: Yuan-jie Liu, Qian-wen Ye, Wei Zhang, Jie-pin Li, Ling-ping Ji, Shen-lin Liu, Xi Zou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGastric cancer (GC) subtypes, particularly diffuse-type GC (DGC) and intestinal-type GC (IGC), exhibit distinct histopathological and microenvironmental features. The molecular mechanisms driving these differences are not fully understood.MethodsWe integrated bulk transcriptomic data, single-cell RNA sequencing, and spatial transcriptomics to compare IGC and DGC. In vitro and in vivo functional assays were used to investigate the role of the EGR1–IGFBP7/VCAM1 axis.ResultsInsulin-like Growth Factor Binding Protein 7 (IGFBP7) was predominantly expressed in stromal fibroblasts and endothelial cells in IGC, but additionally in malignant epithelial cells in DGC. Early Growth Response 1 (EGR1) was identified as a key transcriptional co-activator of IGFBP7 and Vascular Cell Adhesion Molecule 1 (VCAM1), defining the EGR1–IGFBP7/VCAM1 axis. This pathway induced endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EndMT) and abnormal angiogenesis, generating Cancer-Associated Fibroblast (CAF)-like endothelial derivatives that enhanced epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) in tumor cells. Functional assays showed subtype-specific effects—strongly promoting tumor growth in DGC but potentially inhibiting growth in IGC. In DGC, high axis activity promoted invasion, peritoneal dissemination, resistance to epidermal growth factor receptor–tyrosine kinase inhibitors (EGFR-TKIs), and induced immune exclusion, with expansion of CAFs and M2-like macrophages and reduced CD8⁺T-cell infiltration.ConclusionsThis study systematically explored the heterogeneity between IGC and DGC, and identified EGR1 as a key participant in EndMT within DGC. Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) is a major global health issue, being the fifth most common cancer and the fourth leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide [1]. The incidence of GC varies greatly across regions, with particularly high rates in Eastern Asia, Eastern Europe, and parts of Latin America [2]. Although improvements in diagnostic techniques and treatments, such as endoscopic resection, surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and targeted therapies, have helped clinical outcomes, the prognosis for advanced GC is still poor, with a five-year survival rate under 30% [3, 4]. The differences in tumor biology and the complexity of the tumor microenvironment (TME) make existing therapies less effective and lead to frequent treatment resistance [5].

Tumor heterogeneity, particularly between diffuse-type gastric cancer (DGC) and intestinal-type gastric cancer (IGC), plays a significant role in treatment resistance. These subtypes exhibit distinct TME, which influence drug response, immune evasion, and metastatic potential [6]. IGC is typically associated with chronic Helicobacter pylori infection, and its TME often has organized gland-like structures with a higher density of activated stromal and immune cells [7, 8]. In contrast, DGC is characterized by poorly cohesive cells, a desmoplastic stroma, and a more immunosuppressive TME [9, 10]. Recent studies have highlighted distinct stromal-vascular interactions in IGC and DGC [11]. In IGC, VEGF-A drives angiogenesis and correlates with vessel count, promoting tumor progression. In DGC, elevated FGF2 expression induces abnormal vascularization and a hypoxic microenvironment, contributing to immune evasion and resistance to therapy. These differences in vascular remodeling affect immune infiltration and drug delivery, underscoring the importance of stromal-vascular crosstalk in subtype-specific therapy development.

Consequently, understanding the specific ecosystems of each subtype is essential for developing precision oncology strategies. Our team has shown that Insulin-like Growth Factor Binding Protein 7 (IGFBP7) plays an important role in controlling the aggressive traits of GC, such as increased invasiveness and metastasis [12]. Recent studies show that IGFBP7 functions context-dependently, acting as both a tumor suppressor and promoter, depending on the TME and its signaling pathways [13–15]. Notably, studies have reported its involvement in regulating cancer cell proliferation, angiogenesis, and therapeutic resistance through complex interactions with IGF axis components and other oncogenic signaling networks [16–18]. These findings highlight the need for detailed studies to understand the molecular factors that control IGFBP7’s behavior in various cancer types, which is crucial for developing targeted treatment strategies.

Recent technological advances in single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and spatially resolved transcriptomics have revolutionized our ability to dissect tumor heterogeneity at an unprecedented resolution [19]. Unlike traditional bulk sequencing, scRNA-seq allows for the identification of rare cell populations and transcriptional states within the TME [20, 21], and spatial transcriptomics helps to map gene expression while maintaining tissue architecture [22]. Together, these tools help us understand the cellular and molecular differences in GC subtypes, find new biomarkers, and guide the development of better treatments.

We previously found that IGFBP7 is an epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) marker in DGC, which strengthens the link between poor cohesion characteristics (PCC) and the mesenchymal phenotype [12]. In this study, by integrating bioinformatics analysis, clinical specimens, and in vitro and in vivo models, we established the EGR1–IGFBP7/VCAM1 signaling in GC. This signaling controls tumor-associated endothelial cell (TEC) Endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EndMT) and tumor cell EMT, and subsequently facilitated an immunosuppressive TME and induced resistance to immunotherapy. Our data suggest that the EGR1–IGFBP7/VCAM1 signaling pathway plays an important role in the malignant traits of GC, offering a new way to understand its heterogeneity.

Bioinformatics analysis

Bulk-seq datasets and corresponding processing

For pan-cancer analysis, we obtained the uniformly processed pan-cancer dataset from the University of California Santa Cruz (UCSC) database [23]. For the GC sub-analysis, RNA sequencing data (normalized as FPKM) were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) via the TCGAbiolinks R package [24] and converted to Transcripts Per Million (TPM) for better cross-sample comparison. We also retrieved histopathological images from the Genomic Data Commons (GDC) repository. At the same time, GC cohorts from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) were analyzed using the Biomarker Exploration of Solid Tumors (BEST) web tool [25], employing its default parameters, to investigate gene expression, pathway enrichment, drug sensitivity, and immune infiltration.

For drug sensitivity analysis, we included three additional datasets from the GEO database: GSE101491 (afatinib), GSE61676 (erlotinib), and GSE33658 (gefitinib), for validation. Microarray raw data were processed using the Robust Multiarray Average (RMA) normalization method implemented in the affy R package (v1.88.0) [26]. Probe IDs were annotated and converted to gene symbols using the respective platform files (GPL14951 for GSE101491 [27], GPL5188 for GSE61676 [28], and GPL570 for GSE33658 [29]). Probes that did not map uniquely to a single gene symbol were removed. For genes mapped by multiple probes, the mean expression value was calculated to represent the gene’s expression level for subsequent analysis. Box plots of gene expression were drawn by the ggplot2 package (v4.0.1) [30].

All data processing and statistical comparisons were done using standard bioinformatic pipelines to ensure reproducibility and accuracy.

scRNA-seq datasets and corresponding processing

This study utilized three curated public single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) datasets (GSE134520 [31], GSE183904 [32], and GSE228598 [33]), covering the progression from gastritis to early IGC, from DGC to peritoneal metastasis, and peritoneal cells collected from malignant ascites or peritoneal washing. These resources allowed a comprehensive assessment of IGFBP7 expression heterogeneity across anatomical regions and pathological stages. Processed count matrices generated by CellRanger were downloaded from the GEO database.

In addition to public datasets, we also generated an in-house scRNA-seq dataset representing three major histological subtypes of GC: mixed-type (MGC), IGC, and DGC. Pathological information is provided in Table S2. Tissue dissociation, single-cell suspension preparation, and library construction were performed based on a previously established protocol [34], with minor optimizations for the current sample types. All steps, including quality control, sequencing, and downstream processing, were done under standardized conditions to ensure consistency and comparability with external datasets.

scRNA-seq data processing was conducted using the Seurat package [35] (v5.0.1) under stringent quality control criteria: cells with fewer than 200 detected genes or exceeding 10% mitochondrial gene content were excluded, and genes detected in fewer than three cells were filtered out. Inter-batch effects were corrected using Harmony integration prior to standardization, normalization, and identification of highly variable features. Dimensionality reduction was performed based on the top 25 principal components, which were used to construct a neighborhood graph (via the “FindNeighbors” function). Clustering was performed at a resolution of 0.7 for initial cell grouping, and subclustering of stromal and epithelial cells was conducted at a resolution of 0.1 (“FindClusters” function).

The R packages irGSEA (v2.1.5) [36] was employed for functional enrichment. Gene sets sourced from the MSigDB were set as the background gene sets [37]. In addition, the Nebulosa package (v1.18.0) [38] was also used to calculate the comprehensive expression of multiple genes. Cellular trajectory analysis was performed using Monocle3 (v1.3.7) [39] to infer differentiation paths among endothelial cell subtypes without pre-defined temporal or directional cues [40]. To this end, pseudotime values were calculated to order cells along a continuum of developmental states. Furthermore, to complement this analysis and independently assess the direction of differentiation, we applied CytoTRACE (v1.1.0) [41], which predicts the ordering of cell states and their relative stemness based on transcriptional diversity [41]. Intercellular communication analysis was performed using LIANA (v0.1.12) [42], a computational framework that aggregates predictions from a suite of independent ligand-receptor inference methods. The specific algorithms utilized in this study included “natmi”, “connectome”, “logfc”, “sca”, and “cellphonedb”. Each method provides complementary evidence to robustly identify and quantify potential communication events between all defined cell types in the dataset. The visualization related with scRNA-seq analyses was carried out using the scPlotter (v1.4) package [43], and the default settings were used.

Spatial transcriptome (ST) datasets and corresponding processing

We obtained the spatially resolved transcriptomics dataset GSE251950 [44] from the GEO database. Preprocessing and visualization of the spatial transcriptomics (ST) data were performed following the standard workflow implemented in the Semla package [45] (v1.3.2). To initiate the analysis of spatially resolved transcriptomics data, a Seurat object integrated with spatial coordinates and histological images is constructed using the Semla R package [45]. The process begins by importing the standard outputs from the 10x Genomics Visium platform, which comprise (i) a gene-barcode expression matrix, (ii) a tissue position list file that maps spatial barcodes to their respective coordinates in the tissue, and (iii) a high-resolution histological image in JPEG or PNG format. Subsequently, the “ReadVisiumData” function is employed to simultaneously read these files and automatically generate a comprehensive Seurat object. This object is structured to contain a count matrix with genes as rows and spatial spots as columns. Crucially, spatial coordinates for each spot are stored within the object’s metadata, and the accompanying histological image is integrated for subsequent visualization. Finally, the “MapFeatures” function can be applied to this object to visualize the spatial distribution of features like the total number of RNA counts or the count of unique genes per spot, allowing for an initial quality assessment of the loaded data directly on the tissue image.

The spatial organization of cell types was annotated using the SPACET package [46] (v1.3.0) with its core “SpaCET.deconvolution” function under default settings. This reference-free framework infers cellular composition by applying non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) to pan-cancer gene expression signatures, thereby obviating the need for a paired scRNA-seq reference dataset. The method estimates the relative abundance of constituent cell types for each visium spot, ultimately generating a quantitative cell proportion matrix that reflects the tissue’s spatial heterogeneity. ST-related visualizations were achieved through the “MapFeatures” (single numeric feature), “MapMultipleFeatures” (multiple numeric features), and “MapLabels” (categorical features) functions. Additionally, the “crop_area” parameter was used to capture representative fields of view.

Transcription factor prediction

The Transcription Factor Target Finder (TFTF) package [47] is a computational tool designed to predict both target genes of transcription factors and upstream regulators of genes of interest. It integrates prediction outputs from several widely recognized databases—including hTFtarget [48], KnockTF [49], CHEA [50], GTRD [51], and ChIP Atlas [52]—and combines them with correlation analysis to enhance the reliability of inferred transcription factor–target gene regulatory interactions. In this study, TFTF identified EGR1 as a potential upstream regulator of IGFBP7, with predicted binding sites found in the IGFBP7 promoter (Table S3) and the VCAM1 promoter (Table S4).

Tissue sample collection

A total of 60 specimens were collected for each pathological category, including non-atrophic gastritis (NAG), atrophic gastritis (AG), intestinal metaplasia (IM), early IGC, advanced IGC, early DGC, and advanced DGC. Detailed clinicopathological information for all enrolled cases is shown in Table S5. Samples were obtained through endoscopic biopsy or surgical resection, and none of the patients had received chemotherapy, targeted therapy, or other antitumor treatments before specimen collection. All pathological diagnoses were reviewed and confirmed independently by two certified pathologists. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing University of Chinese Medicine (Ethics approval number: 2022NL-162-02, approval date: 2022-09-16), and written informed consent was obtained from both the patients and their attending clinicians. Tumors were staged and graded according to the 8th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) Tumor-Node-Metastasis (TNM) classification system [53]. Immediately after excision, tissues were rinsed in cold phosphate-buffered saline and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde to maintain morphological integrity.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining

Gastric tissue samples were obtained either by surgical resection or endoscopic biopsy. After fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde, the samples were embedded in paraffin and sectioned to a thickness of 4 μm. The sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated through graded ethanol, and subjected to heat-induced antigen retrieval. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% hydrogen peroxide, and the slides were incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. Detection was performed using a horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody and diaminobenzidine (DAB) as the chromogen, followed by hematoxylin counterstaining. The staining patterns were evaluated independently by two experienced pathologists who were blinded to the clinical information. Both the staining intensity and the proportion of positive cells were recorded, and a semi-quantitative H-score was calculated by multiplying the intensity score (0–3) by the percentage of stained cells (0–100). For subsequent analyses, patients were stratified based on their H-scores.

Cell lines and culture conditions

Primary human gastric fibroblasts (HGFs, Cat. No. PSHX-C205) and the corresponding complete culture medium (Cat. No. PSHX-G205) were obtained from HyCyte, Suzhou, China. These cells represent normal fibroblasts (NFs) derived from human gastric tissue. HGFs were maintained under standard conditions and only passages ≤ 5 were used for experiments. Human embryonic kidney 293T cells (HEK-293T) were purchased from Procell, Wuhan, China, and cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (P/S). Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were also obtained from Procell and grown in endothelial cell medium. Gastric cancer cell lines MKN45 (Cat. No. CL-0292) and MKN74 (Cat. No. CL-0730) were purchased from Procell, while MKN45-luc cells (Cat. No. LZQ0076) were obtained from Zhongqiao Xinzhou, Shanghai, China; all three were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute 1640 Medium (RPMI-1640) medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% P/S. The human monocytic leukemia cell line THP-1 (Cat. No. CL-0233, Procell) was maintained in RPMI-1640 medium containing 10% FBS, 0.05 mM β-mercaptoethanol, and 1% P/S. Unless otherwise specified, all cells were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere of 95% air and 5% CO₂. All cell lines were authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling and routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination.

Multiplex immunofluorescence via tyramide signal amplification (TSA)

Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tumor tissues were sectioned at 4 μm, dewaxed in xylene, and rehydrated through graded ethanol. Heat-induced epitope retrieval (HIER) was performed in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) containing 3% H₂O₂ to quench endogenous peroxidase activity. After cooling, sections were blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 30 min at room temperature (RT, 22–25℃). Slides were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies and fluorophore-labeled tyramide. Multiplex staining was achieved by repeating cycles of HIER, BSA blocking, and antibody incubation, with each round detecting a different antigen. Nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) for 5 min, and slides were mounted with anti-fade medium. Whole-slide fluorescence images were acquired using the Pannoramic SCAN system (3DHISTECH Ltd., Budapest, Hungary) at high resolution under identical acquisition settings for all samples. Multiplex signal quantification and spatial phenotyping analyses were performed using the HALO image analysis platform (Indica Labs, Albuquerque, NM, USA).

Generation and characterization of tumor-associated endothelial-like cells (TEC-like HUVECs)

MKN45 gastric cancer cells were cultured to 70–80% confluence and then maintained for an additional 48 h in basal medium containing 2% FBS. The conditioned medium (CM) was collected, filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane, and mixed in a 1:1 ratio with endothelial basal medium for the subsequent culture of HUVECs under hypoxic conditions (5% O₂, 5% CO₂) for 48 h After induction, HUVECs exhibited elevated expression of angiogenesis/activation markers, including CD105 and VEGF-A (Figure S1A), alongside significant morphological changes: control HUVEC demonstrated a spindle-shaped, tightly clustered morphology, whereas induced cells became elongated and dispersed (Figure S1B). RNA sequencing further revealed that TEC-like HUVECs showed substantial upregulation of genes associated with cell survival and angiogenesis (Figure S1C), confirming their tumor-associated, pro-angiogenic phenotype. These conditioned HUVECs are hereafter referred to as tumor endothelial cells (TECs) and were used in subsequent experiments as an in vitro model of tumor-associated endothelium.

Previous studies, including several published papers, have confirmed that EGR1, an EMT-associated transcription factor, strongly promotes mesenchymal transition [40, 41]. In our system, TEC-like HUVECs overexpressing EGR1 acquired mesenchymal features, including an elongated spindle-like morphology (Figure S1D), and showed increased expression of cancer-associated fibroblast (CAF) markers. These changes suggest that the cells adopt a fibroblast-like state derived from endothelial cells; in this study we refer to them as endothelial-derived CAF-like cells (EC-CAFs). However, CAFs are highly heterogeneous [42], and in vivo lineage-tracing studies indicate that only a subset of CAFs arise from endothelial cells [43]. Thus, our model captures an endothelial-derived CAF-like population but does not reproduce the full spectrum of CAF states present in human tumors.

Lentiviral vector construction and transfection

Lentiviral vectors were custom-designed and generated in collaboration with GeneChem (Shanghai, China). A detailed description of plasmid backbones, viral packaging systems, and vector components is summarized in Tables S6-S8. For transduction, cells were seeded at densities optimized for each cell line and exposed to viral supernatant containing 8 µg/mL polybrene to facilitate viral entry. Cultures were maintained for 24–48 h to ensure efficient genomic integration, after which the medium was replaced with fresh growth medium to remove residual viral particles. Successful transduction, as well as the efficiency of gene silencing or overexpression, was confirmed by Western blot analysis (Figure S2).

Western blotting (WB)

Proteins were extracted using Radioimmunoprecipitation Assay (RIPA) buffer, and concentrations were determined using the Bradford assay. Equal amounts of protein (20 µg per sample) were separated on SurePAGE Bis–Tris gradient gels (10% or 12%) and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. Membranes were blocked with blocking solution at RT for 1 h, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary antibodies. After washing, membranes were incubated with fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies for 1 h at RT. Protein bands were visualized using the Licor Odyssey fluorescence imaging system (CLX, Licor, USA), with β-tubulin, β-actin, or GAPDH as loading controls for normalization.

Immunofluorescence

Cells were seeded on sterilized glass coverslips placed in 12-well plates and allowed to adhere overnight. After the indicated treatments, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at RT and permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 in Phosphate-Buffered Saline (PBS) for 10 min. Following three washes with PBS, nonspecific binding sites were blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h. Cells were then incubated with the indicated primary antibodies diluted in blocking buffer at 4 °C overnight. After additional PBS washes, samples were incubated with fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at RT in the dark. Nuclear counterstaining was performed with DAPI for 5 min. Coverslips were mounted using an anti-fade mounting medium, and fluorescent images were captured using an upright fluorescence microscope (BX53, Olympus, Japan) under identical acquisition parameters.

Luciferase reporter assay

HEK293T cells were co-transfected with a firefly luciferase reporter plasmid carrying the target promoter sequence and a Renilla luciferase plasmid as an internal control using Lipofectamine 3000. After 48 h, luciferase activity was determined with the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay and normalized to Renilla signals. All assays were performed in triplicate.

ChIP-qPCR assay

HEK293T cells (2 × 10⁷) were cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde for 10 min at RT, and the reaction was quenched with 0.125 M glycine for 5 min. Cells were washed with PBS, pelleted, and lysed on ice for 5 min in lysis buffer (10 mM HEPES, pH 7.5; 0.1 mM EDTA; 0.5% NP-40; protease inhibitors). Nuclei were collected by centrifugation (2000 × g, 10 min, 4 °C), and chromatin was fragmented by sonication to 100–500 bp. 10% of the sheared chromatin was saved as input, while the remainder was subjected to immunoprecipitation with anti-EGR1 antibody or rabbit IgG (negative control). Immunocomplexes were washed, eluted, and reverse cross-linked. DNA was purified by phenol–chloroform extraction, quantified with a Qubit 3 fluorometer, and fragmentation was confirmed by agarose gel electrophoresis. Enrichment of specific genomic regions was assessed by quantitative PCR using SYBR Green Master Mix on a real-time PCR system. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Colony formation assay

Colony formation was assessed under both co-culture and monoculture conditions to evaluate the impact of gene-transfected TECs on tumor cell proliferation, as well as the intrinsic effect of gene transfection on tumor cells. In the co-culture assay, TECs (500 cells/well), transfected with specific genes, were seeded in the upper chamber of 6-well plates, while tumor cells (MKN74 or MKN45, 500 cells/well) were seeded in the lower chamber at a 1:1 ratio. TECs were transfected prior to co-culture. For the monoculture assay, tumor cells (500 cells/well), transfected with the respective genes, were seeded in separate wells of the 6-well plates. After a 14-day incubation period at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO₂ atmosphere, colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.5% crystal violet in 25% methanol. Colonies were counted, and the results were used to evaluate the effects of TECs on tumor cell colony formation and the intrinsic effect of gene transfection on tumor cell proliferation. Each condition was performed in triplicate, and the assay was repeated at least three independent times to ensure reproducibility.

Tumor sphere formation assay

Tumor sphere formation was assessed under both co-culture and monoculture conditions to evaluate the impact of gene-transfected TECs on tumor cell sphere formation, as well as the intrinsic effect of gene transfection on tumor cells (MKN74 and MKN45). In the co-culture assay, TECs (1 × 10⁴ cells/well) that had undergone specific gene transfection were seeded alongside 1 × 10⁴ tumor cells in a 1:1 ratio in 6-well ultra-low attachment plates, with serum-free RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 1× B27 Supplement (B27), 20 ng/mL Epidermal Growth Factor (EGF), and 20 ng/mL basic Fibroblast Growth Factor (bFGF). In the monoculture assay, tumor cells (1 × 10⁴ cells/well), transfected with the desired genes, were seeded in the same medium in separate wells of the 6-well ultra-low attachment plates. Both co-culture and monoculture conditions were incubated for 7 days at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere. Tumor spheres greater than 50 μm in diameter were visualized under a phase-contrast microscope and quantified by counting the number of spheres formed in each well. Each condition was performed in triplicate, and the assay was repeated at least three independent times to evaluate the effect of TECs on tumor sphere formation and to assess the intrinsic impact of gene transfection on tumor cell sphere formation.

Tumor cell invasion assay

Tumor cell invasion was assessed using Matrigel-coated Transwell chambers (24-well, 8-µm pore size). For the co-culture assay, tumor cells (MKN74 and MKN45, 2 × 10⁴ cells/well) were seeded in the upper chamber with serum-free RPMI-1640 medium, while TECs, pretreated as indicated, were seeded in the lower chamber at a 1:1 ratio (2 × 10⁴ cells/well) with RPMI-1640 medium containing 10% FBS as a chemoattractant. After 24 h of incubation at 37 °C in 5% CO₂, non-invading cells were removed, and invading cells on the lower membrane were fixed, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and visualized under a phase-contrast microscope (CKX41, Olympus, Japan). The number of invaded cells was quantified by counting six random fields per membrane. Each condition was tested in triplicate and repeated in at least three independent experiments. In the tumor cell-only assay, tumor cells, transfected with the indicated genes, were seeded in the upper chamber of a Matrigel-coated Transwell insert (8 μm pore size) at 2 × 10⁴ cells/well with serum-free RPMI-1640 medium. The lower chamber contained RPMI-1640 medium with 10% FBS. After 24 h of incubation, non-invading cells were removed, and invading cells were processed and quantified as above.

Wound healing assay

The wound healing assay was performed using a Transwell-based non-contact co-culture system to evaluate the effect of TECs on tumor cells migration. Tumor cells (4 × 10⁵ cells/well) were seeded into the lower chamber of a 6-well Transwell system (0.4-µm pore size) and cultured in serum-free medium for 24 h to form a confluent monolayer. A linear wound was created using a sterile 200-µL pipette tip, and TECs, pretreated as indicated, were seeded into the upper chamber at a 1:1 ratio (4 × 10⁵ cells/insert). The co-culture system was incubated at 37 °C in 5% CO₂, and wound closure was recorded at 0, 12, and 24 h using a phase-contrast microscope (CKX41, Olympus, Japan). Wound areas were quantified with ImageJ software. Each condition was tested in triplicate and repeated in at least three independent experiments.

For the tumor cell-only assay, tumor cells were transfected with the indicated genes prior to seeding (4 × 10⁵ cells/well) into the lower chamber of a 6-well Transwell system. After creating a linear wound, wound closure was monitored at 0, 12, and 24 h, and wound areas were quantified as described above.

Tube formation assay

Growth factor–reduced Matrigel was thawed on ice and dispensed into pre-chilled 24-well plates (250 µL per well). The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 30–45 min to allow gel polymerization. TECs (2 × 10⁴ cells/well) subjected to different viral transfections—including EGR1 overexpression, or IGFBP7/VCAM1 knockdown—were seeded into the upper chambers of Transwell inserts placed in the 24-well plates. In parallel, untransfected TECs (2 × 10⁴ cells/well) were seeded in the lower chamber on Matrigel-coated wells. After 12 h of incubation at 37 °C, tube-like network structures formed by the TECs were visualized with an inverted phase-contrast microscope. Network formation was quantified using ImageJ (Angiogenesis Analyzer). Each condition was tested in triplicate and reproduced in at least three independent experiments to evaluate the effects of EC-CAF–like transformation of TECs on angiogenesis.

Recruitment of TECs (Transwell assay)

In the 24-well Transwell system, TECs (1 × 10⁴ cells/well) transfected with different genes, such as EGR1 overexpression or IGFBP7/VCAM1 knockdown, were seeded in the lower chamber. Untransfected TECs (1 × 10⁴ cells/well) were placed in the upper chamber of the Transwell insert (8 μm pore size). After 24 h of incubation at 37 °C, migrated TECs were counted to assess recruitment ability. Each condition was tested in triplicate and repeated in at least three independent experiments to evaluate the impact of EC-CAF–like transformation on TEC migration.

Subcutaneous xenograft tumor model

All experimental procedures involving animals were conducted in accordance with the Basel Declaration and were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Jiangsu Province Hospital of Chinese Medicine (Ethics approval number: 2024DW-102-01, approval date: 2024-12-12). Male BALB/c nude mice (4–6 weeks old, n = 6 per group) were used in all xenograft experiments.

Experiment 1: Tumor formation from co-injected transfected TECs and MKN45 cells.

Mice were subcutaneously injected in the right flank with a mixed suspension of transfected TECs and MKN45 cells at a 1:1 ratio (1 × 10⁶ MKN45 cells + 1 × 10⁶ TECs per mouse) in 100 µL PBS/Matrigel (1:1, v/v). Cells in each group were subjected to the indicated genetic interventions prior to injection.

Experiment 2: Tumor formation from transfected MKN45 cells alone.

In parallel, mice were subcutaneously injected with transfected MKN45 cells [2 × 10⁶ cells in 100 µL PBS/Matrigel (1:1, v/v)], also subjected to the indicated genetic interventions.

For both experiments, tumor growth was monitored every two days using vernier calipers to measure tumor length (a) and width (b). Tumor volume was calculated as V = 1/2 × a × b² (a = length, b = width), and growth curves of the subcutaneous xenografts were plotted accordingly. Animal welfare was closely monitored, and humane endpoints were enforced such that tumor weight did not exceed 10% of body weight and maximum tumor diameter did not exceed 20 mm. At the experimental endpoint or when humane endpoints were reached, mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation following anesthesia with isoflurane, in accordance with institutional animal care guidelines. Tumors were excised, weighed, photographed.

Peritoneal metastasis xenograft model

Male BALB/c nude mice (4–6 weeks old, n = 6 per group) were intraperitoneally injected with a mixed suspension containing MKN45-luc cells and TECs at a 1:1 ratio. Specifically, each mouse received 1 × 10⁶ MKN45-luc cells and 1 × 10⁶ TECs suspended in 100 µL of PBS. The TECs underwent preconditioning according to the study’s requirements. Tumor progression was monitored by bioluminescence imaging using the BLT multimodal imaging system (AniView100). Prior to imaging, mice were intraperitoneally injected with 100 mg/kg d-luciferin and anesthetized with 2% isoflurane. Bioluminescence intensity (p/s/cm²/sr) was measured to assess tumor progression. Imaging was performed on day 28 post-injection. At the experimental endpoint or upon reaching humane endpoints, mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation following anesthesia with isoflurane, in accordance with institutional animal care guidelines. Experiment 2: Injection of transfected MKN45-luc Cells Alone.

In a parallel experiment, mice received an intraperitoneal injection of transfected MKN45-luc cells (2 × 10⁶ cells in 100 µL PBS) subjected to the indicated genetic modifications. Tumor progression was monitored in the same manner as in Experiment 1.

IC50 assay

The IC50 assay was employed to assess the impact of gene-transfected TECs on the sensitivity of tumor cells (MKN74 and MKN45) to gefitinib, a tyrosine kinase inhibitor. Two experimental conditions were evaluated: co-culture of gene-transfected TECs with tumor cells and monoculture of tumor cells after gene transfection. In the co-culture assay, TECs transfected with different genes were co-cultured with tumor cells (MKN74 or MKN45) for 48 h. After the co-culture, the tumor cells were trypsinized and seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁴ cells/well. Tumor cells were then treated with gefitinib at a series of concentrations (0, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.4, 0.8, 1.6, 3.2 µM) for 24 h. Cell viability was assessed using the CCK-8 assay, and the IC50 value was calculated based on the concentration of gefitinib that inhibited 50% of the cell viability. For the monoculture assay, tumor cells (MKN74 or MKN45), transfected with the respective genes, were seeded directly in 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁴ cells/well. After 24 h of incubation, tumor cells were treated with the same range of gefitinib concentrations (0, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.4, 0.8, 1.6, 3.2 µM) for 24 h. Cell viability was assessed using the CCK-8 assay, and the IC50 value was determined for each condition to assess the intrinsic effect of gene transfection on the gefitinib sensitivity of the tumor cells.

In vivo assessment of gefitinib sensitivity

To evaluate the sensitivity of tumors to gefitinib in vivo, a subcutaneous xenograft model was used. All experimental procedures involving animals were conducted in accordance with the Basel Declaration and approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Jiangsu Province Hospital of Chinese Medicine (Ethics approval number: 2024DW-102-01, approval date: 2024-12-12). Male BALB/c nude mice (4–6 weeks old, n = 6 per group) were employed in the experiments.

Experiment 1: Tumor formation from co-injected transfected TECs and MKN45 cells.

Mice were subcutaneously injected with a 1:1 mixture of MKN45 cells and TECs (1 × 10⁶ cells each) in 100 µL PBS/Matrigel (1:1, v/v). Prior to injection, TECs in each group underwent the indicated genetic interventions.

Experiment 2: Tumor formation from transfected MKN45 cells alone.

Mice were also subcutaneously injected with MKN45 cells (2 × 10⁶ cells) in 100 µL PBS/Matrigel (1:1, v/v), with the indicated genetic interventions applied.

Starting from day 7 post-injection, when tumors were palpable and measurable (≥ 5 mm in diameter), mice were treated with gefitinib via oral gavage at a dose of 50 mg/kg/day for 21 consecutive days. Tumor growth was monitored every two days using a vernier caliper to measure tumor length (a) and width (b). Tumor volume was calculated as V = 1/2 × a × b², and growth curves were plotted. In addition to tumor growth, general health and behavior were closely monitored for drug-induced side effects. Signs of toxicity, such as significant body weight loss (> 15%), fur condition changes, reduced activity, and signs of distress (e.g., labored breathing, lethargy), were recorded daily. Mice exhibiting severe symptoms were removed from the study and humanely euthanized.

Macrophage differentiation and co-culture system

Sterile glass coverslips were first placed in the lower chambers of a 6-well transwell system (0.4 μm pore size). THP-1 cells (1 × 10⁵ cells/well) were then seeded onto the coverslips and treated with 10 ng/mL phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) for 48 h to induce macrophage differentiation. The PMA-containing medium was subsequently replaced with serum-free medium, and the cells were further cultured for 24 h before co-culture. For transwell co-culture experiments, either TECs (with different genetic modifications) or gastric cancer cells (MKN74 or MKN45) were seeded in the upper chambers, while differentiated macrophages adhered to coverslips were maintained in the lower chambers. After 24 h of co-culture, macrophages on the coverslips were collected and subjected to immunofluorescence analysis.

Isolation and co-culture of CD8⁺T cells

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from human peripheral blood samples using Ficoll density gradient centrifugation. CD8⁺T lymphocytes were subsequently enriched with CD8 MicroBeads in combination with MS columns and a MACS Separator (Miltenyi Biotec), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The purity of the isolated CD8⁺T-cell population consistently exceeded 90%, as confirmed by flow cytometry. For flow cytometric staining, CD8⁺T cells were resuspended in 50 µL PBS and incubated with antibody panels at 4 °C in the dark for 30 min, followed by washing with PBS supplemented with 5% FBS. Data acquisition was performed using a multiparameter flow cytometer (URIT bf-730, Guilin Ulead Medical Electronics, China), and analyses were conducted with Kaluza software, version 2.1 (Beckman Coulter, USA). For transwell co-culture experiments, TECs or GC cells (MKN74 or MKN45), each transfected with different viruses, were seeded into the upper chambers (2 × 10⁵ cells per well) of a 6-well transwell system (0.4 μm pore size), while freshly isolated CD8⁺T cells (1 × 10⁶ cells per well) were plated in the lower chambers. After 24 h of co-culture, T cell activation was assessed by quantifying the percentage of CD69⁺ cells via flow cytometry. CD69 was selected as an activation marker of T cells.

Patient cohort and treatment regimen

To evaluate the association between the average H-score of EGR1, IGFBP7, and VCAM1 and immunotherapy efficacy, we conducted a retrospective observational cohort study including 60 patients with unresectable locally advanced, recurrent, or metastatic DGC treated at the Jiangsu Province Hospital of Chinese Medicine between 1 October 2022 and 31 December 2024. Specimens were collected and stored in accordance with institutional protocols, and all patient records were anonymized and de-identified prior to analysis. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Inclusion criteria were: (1) newly diagnosed GC confirmed by clinical examination, gastroscopy, MRI, PET-CT/CT, and hematological tests; (2) complete baseline pathological and radiological information, with pathology slides independently validated by two gastrointestinal pathologists; and (3) availability of comprehensive clinical data. Treatment protocol: All enrolled patients received first-line systemic therapy with sintilimab in combination with platinum-based doublet chemotherapy. Immunotherapy: Sintilimab was administered at a fixed dose of 200 mg intravenously on Day 1 of each 21-day cycle.

Chemotherapy backbone: Selected at the discretion of the treating physician and consisted of one of the following standard regimens: XELOX (Capecitabine + Oxaliplatin): Oxaliplatin 130 mg/m² i.v. on Day 1; Capecitabine 1000 mg/m² orally twice daily, Days 1–14. SOX (S-1 + Oxaliplatin): Oxaliplatin 130 mg/m² i.v. on Day 1; S-1 40–60 mg (based on body surface area) orally twice daily, Days 1–14.

Treatment cycles: Repeated every 21 days. Tumor response was assessed radiologically every 6–8 weeks using contrast-enhanced CT or PET-CT, according to RECIST 1.1 criteria [54]. Treatment continued until disease progression, development of intolerable adverse events, withdrawal of consent, or completion of a maximum of 2 years of sintilimab therapy. Exclusion criteria included: (1) pathological diagnosis other than GC (e.g., gastrointestinal stromal tumor); (2) death during treatment; (3) incomplete clinical or follow-up data; (4) active infection, immune system disorder, or hematological disease; (5) prior local radiotherapy or radiofrequency ablation; (6) HIV positivity; or (7) inability to tolerate treatment-related toxicities. The detailed pathological characteristics of the enrolled patients are provided in Table S9.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The concentration of IGFBP7 secreted into cell culture supernatants was measured using a commercial ELISA kit, following the manufacturer’s protocol. Culture media were collected without replenishment to prevent dilution effects, then transferred to clean tubes and centrifuged at 3,500 rpm for 10 min at RT to remove cellular debris. The clarified supernatants were added to ELISA plates, and the absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (ELX800, Bio-Tek, USA). Each sample was assayed in triplicate to ensure reproducibility.

In the retrospective study, pre-treatment serum samples were collected from 60 advanced DGC patients prior to treatment. Blood samples were centrifuged at 1,500 × g for 10 min at RT, and the serum was separated and stored at -80 °C until analysis. The serum cytokine profile was measured using a commercial multiplex cytokine assay (according to the manufacturer’s protocol). We specifically examined cytokines related to CD8⁺T cells activation (IFN-γ, TNF-α, IL-2), immunosuppressive cytokines (IL-10, TGF-β1, IL-6), and M2/TAM-associated chemokines (CCL2, CCL5, CXCL12). These cytokine levels were compared between the high and low EGR1–IGFBP7/VCAM1 axis groups. The concentrations of these cytokines were measured in triplicate to ensure reproducibility and accuracy.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 10.1.2) and R (version 4.3.2). Data are presented as mean ± SD. For comparisons between two groups, Student’s t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test was used as appropriate. For multiple group comparisons, one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test was applied. Correlations were assessed using Pearson’s or Spearman’s correlation coefficient. Categorical data were analyzed using the Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test. Survival analysis was performed with Kaplan-Meier curves and the log-rank test. A P-value of < 0.05 was considered significant. Specific tests are indicated in figure legends.

The expression pattern of IGFBP7 differs between IGC and DGC

Our team’s previous work demonstrated that IGFBP7 is highly expressed in tumor cells within the deep layers of the GC and promotes an invasive tumor phenotype [12]. Furthermore, recent studies indicate that IGFBP7 influences the immune microenvironment of GC tumors and the response to immunotherapy [55]. These findings highlight IGFBP7 as an emerging therapeutic target in GC and highlight its research value.

We first examined the expression characteristics of IGFBP7. Figure 1A shows that, based on data from the Human Protein Atlas (HPA), IGFBP7 is primarily localized in the Golgi apparatus, suggesting that it may be a secretory protein. Next, we used data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) to compare IGFBP7 expression levels in tumor and paired normal samples across 33 cancer types (Fig. 1B). Excluding cancer types with limited or no normal tissue data, we found that IGFBP7 levels were upregulated in breast invasive carcinoma (BRCA), colon adenocarcinoma (COAD), esophageal carcinoma (ESCA), head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSC), liver hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC), and stomach adenocarcinoma (STAD). In contrast, IGFBP7 levels were lower in tumors compared to normal tissues in bladder urothelial carcinoma (BLCA), kidney chromophobe (KICH), kidney renal papillary cell carcinoma (KIRP), lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC), prostate adenocarcinoma (PRAD), thyroid carcinoma (THCA), and uterine corpus endometrial carcinoma (UCEC). These results indicate that IGFBP7 may play different roles in various types of cancer. Consistent with this, several studies indicate that IGFBP7 has context-dependent roles, acting as a tumor suppressor in some cancers but as a tumor promoter in others, in a manner influenced by cell type and microenvironment [13, 17, 56]. Because GC shows marked heterogeneity and is broadly classified into intestinal-type (IGC) and diffuse-type (DGC), we next compared IGFBP7 expression between these subtypes. This analysis aimed to clarify how IGFBP7 contributes to their distinct molecular and microenvironmental features. As shown in Fig. 1C, results from five independent datasets indicate that IGFBP7 levels are significantly higher in DGC compared to other subtypes. To further explore the biological implications of this subtype-specific upregulation, we performed co-expression network and functional enrichment analyses, which identified an IGFBP7-associated module more abundant in the DGC network (Figure S3). This module was linked to extracellular matrix organization, angiogenesis, and EMT, highlighting a potential role of IGFBP7 in promoting invasive and stromal-interactive phenotypes. In line with these observations, analysis across additional cohorts showed that IGFBP7 expression was particularly elevated in the invasive subtype and tumors with perineural invasion. High IGFBP7 expression was consistently associated with poor survival outcomes across several datasets (Figure S4). Taken together, these data suggest that in DGC, IGFBP7 is tied to matrix remodeling, angiogenesis and EMT, which is in line with a more invasive phenotype. In IGC, IGFBP7 is higher in earlier stages but lower in advanced disease, showing a different pattern and hinting that its contribution to late aggressive behavior may be more limited.

To capture the distinct pathogenic trajectories of IGC and DGC, we assembled a cohort comprising 60 cases each of non-atrophic gastritis (NAG), atrophic gastritis (AG), intestinal metaplasia (IM), early IGC, advanced IGC, early DGC, and advanced DGC (Fig. 1D). This cohort recapitulates both the Correa cascade for IGC and the more abrupt, non-metaplastic progression characteristic of DGC. We performed immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining for IGFBP7, further confirming the upregulation of IGFBP7 in DGC at the protein level. Interestingly, while IGFBP7 expression gradually increased from NAG to early IGC, it notably decreased from early to late-stage IGC. This unexpected decline prompted us to examine IGFBP7 expression at a single-cell resolution. As shown in Fig. 1E, pan-cancer scRNA data indicate that IGFBP7 is mainly expressed in stromal cells, with lower but detectable levels in malignant cells. Analysis of GC scRNA-seq data (Fig. 1F-H, in house data) confirmed strong expression in fibroblasts and endothelial cells, and revealed higher levels in epithelial cells from DGC samples. Consistently, the broader single-cell landscape (GSE134520) demonstrated stromal enrichment of IGFBP7 along the progression from normal mucosa through gastritis and metaplasia to early GC, with focal induction in epithelial subsets (Figure S5).

Spatial transcriptomic (ST) data further mapped IGFBP7 distribution in IGC and DGC (Fig. 1I-J). In IGC, expression was largely confined to stromal regions enriched with endothelial cells and CAFs, whereas in DGC it extended into malignant epithelial areas. Additional samples from the same dataset showed consistent compartmental organization and confirmed focal IGFBP7 enrichment in malignant clusters of DGC (Figure S6).

To validate these findings experimentally, we performed multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF) staining on formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) sections from IGC and DGC samples (Fig. 1K-L). Segmentation analysis using the HALO platform identified tumor cells (EPCAM⁺), endothelial cells (ENG⁺), and cancer-associated fibroblasts (FAP⁺). The results showed that in IGC, IGFBP7 was predominantly localized to endothelial cells and CAFs, with a clear distinction between stromal and epithelial regions. In contrast, DGC samples exhibited IGFBP7 expression across all three cell types, with a more intermixed pattern of stromal and epithelial cells. These results suggest that the upregulation of IGFBP7 in DGC is primarily due to its expression in malignant epithelial cells. Thus, IGFBP7 shifts from being mainly expressed in stromal cells in premalignant lesions and IGC to being expressed in both stromal and epithelial cells in DGC. As a secreted protein, IGFBP7 can act on the cells that produce it and on nearby cells [17]. When the main source of IGFBP7 changes, the set of target cells and the signals it drives are also likely to change. This shift helps explain why the same molecule looks relatively limited in intestinal-type tumors but is closely linked to strong EMT and invasive behavior in diffuse-type tumors.

IGFBP7 is associated with DGC-specific EMT and angiogenesis phenotypes

DGC shows poorer differentiation and a more pronounced EMT program than the intestinal-type, contributing to its aggressive clinical behavior [57]. We examined four defined EMT programs from the 3CA database [58] and compared their expression profiles between IGC and DGC. As shown in Fig. 2A and Figure S7, EMT-Ⅰ and EMT-Ⅳ exhibited significantly higher expression levels in DGC. scRNA-seq data further revealed that EMT-Ⅰ and EMT-Ⅳ were predominantly expressed in epithelial cells and fibroblasts derived from DGC and MGC (Fig. 2B). Correlation analysis confirmed strong positive correlations between IGFBP7 and the DGC-specific EMT-Ⅰ/Ⅳ programs, further reinforcing the notion that IGFBP7 is a distinct feature of DGC (Fig. 2C). Next, we characterized the spatial distribution of IGFBP7 and the four EMT programs using ST. The results showed that IGFBP7 expression consistently co-localized with EMT-I and EMT-IV programs in both IGC and DGC subtypes (Fig. 2D and Figure S8A). Additionally, regions with high IGFBP7 expression were associated with increased levels of partial EMT (pEMT), complete EMT (cEMT), and angiogenesis (Fig. 2E and Figure S8B).

Considering that IGFBP7 also exhibits a strong correlation with angiogenesis [17], we further explored its potential role in this process. We calculated the angiogenesis score in the HALLMARK background across three independent datasets. The Kruskal-Wallis test results revealed significantly higher angiogenesis scores in DGC (Fig. 2F). This aligns with the current consensus that DGC exhibits more pronounced angiogenic activity than IGC [59]. Fibroblasts and endothelial cells are two major stromal components of the tumor microenvironment, and their bidirectional signaling plays a crucial role in tumor progression and stromal remodeling [60]. Specifically, fibroblasts secrete Vascular Endothelial Growth Factor (VEGF), which promotes the recruitment and activation of endothelial cells, thereby inducing the formation of a structurally and functionally aberrant vasculature [61, 62]. This pathological angiogenesis contributes to the establishment and maintenance of a hypoxic TME, fostering conditions conducive to tumor survival, invasion, and resistance to therapy [63, 64]. To map these interactions, we constructed a comprehensive cell-cell communication (CCC) network using the LIANA framework, as shown in Fig. 2G-H. We observed more frequent communication between fibroblasts and endothelial cells in DGC than in IGC. Figure 2I and J show the top 20 downregulated and upregulated ligand-receptor interactions in DGC, respectively. These findings suggest that enhanced angiogenesis in DGC may be partly driven by crosstalk between fibroblasts and endothelial cells. IGFBP7 has been reported to bind the endothelial receptor CD93 and to regulate tumor vessels in a microenvironment-dependent way, often supporting abnormal, leaky vasculature, while blockade of the IGFBP7-CD93 axis can normalize vessels and improve drug and immune cell delivery [65, 66]. Taken together with our CCC data, this supports the idea that IGFBP7-high fibroblasts and endothelial cells in DGC help build a pro-angiogenic, hypoxic microenvironment that favors EMT and peritoneal spread, whereas in IGC IGFBP7 expression is more restricted in the stroma and its impact on vessel structure may be more limited. The above results suggest that IGFBP7 may be a key factor contributing to the phenotypic differences between DGC and IGC, warranting further investigation into its molecular mechanisms; to this end, an IGFBP7-associated interaction network was constructed using the STRING database, which revealed a close association with VCAM1—a gene known to enhance tumor-endothelial adhesion and promote metastatic spread (Fig. 2K). Given the lack of evidence for a direct regulatory relationship between IGFBP7 and VCAM1, we postulated a common upstream regulator and used the TFTF tool to predict transcription factors binding to the IGFBP7 promoter, identifying three candidates (Fig. 2L). Notably, among these, only EGR1 has been demonstrated to directly promote VCAM1 transcription, upregulating both its mRNA and protein levels [67]. Pan-cancer data confirmed a broad positive correlation between EGR1 and IGFBP7 (Fig. 2M), and JASPAR analysis predicted EGR1 binding sites in the IGFBP7 promoter (Fig. 2N, Table S3), which was validated by dual-luciferase assays and ChIP-qPCR (Fig. 2O-P). Previous studies have shown that EGR1 positively regulates VCAM1 expression [68, 69]. Consistent with this, EGR1 overexpression in HEK293T cells increased VCAM1 promoter activity, and ChIP-qPCR confirmed EGR1 binding at the VCAM1 promoter (Fig. 2Q-S, Table S4). In GC biopsies, IGFBP7, EGR1, and VCAM1 protein levels were strongly correlated by IHC (Fig. 2T). These findings establish the EGR1–IGFBP7/VCAM1 signaling axis, with EGR1 regulating both IGFBP7 and VCAM1.

Transcription factor EGR1 promotes endothelial to mesenchymal transition (EndMT) in GC

Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) projection of single-cell transcriptomes from IGC (GSE134520) (Fig. 3A) and from our DGC cohort (Fig. 3B) revealed markedly different stromal architectures. In IGC, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and myofibroblasts occupied distinct clusters; in DGC, fibroblasts and endothelial cells predominated. Focusing on the endothelial compartment, pseudotime trajectory analysis delineated two divergent routes: endothelial cells in IGC appeared transcriptionally arrested midway, whereas in DGC they advanced along a continuum toward fibroblast-like phenotypes (Fig. 3C). Along this DGC trajectory, EGR1, IGFBP7, and VCAM1 were upregulated in a distinct temporal sequence (Fig. 3C). To strengthen the connection between these changes and fibroblast identity, correlation analysis showed that EGR1 expression was strongly associated with fibroblast-enriched transcriptional programs (Fig. 3D), suggesting EGR1 as a central regulator of stromal activation and motivating further functional studies. Thus, we hypothesized that EGR1-related signaling could regulate EndMT. EndMT is a process in which endothelial cells transition into a mesenchymal state, exhibiting typical mesenchymal morphology and functional properties, such as enhanced motility and contractility [70]. To validate this hypothesis, we first examined EGR1’s effect in primary gastric fibroblasts (NFs), which are inherently mesenchymal and thus unsuitable for direct EndMT assessment. Instead, we assessed myofibroblast activation markers: EGR1 overexpression markedly increased COL1A1 and α-SMA, confirmed by western blotting and immunofluorescence (Fig. 3E-F), indicating a shift toward the activated myofibroblast phenotype.

Next, we focused on the endothelial compartment. Specifically, we examined tumor endothelial cells (TECs), where EndMT is a critical pathway for fibroblast generation, fibrosis, and stromal mesenchymal transition [71]. Gene set enrichment analysis revealed robust activation of the Notch and TGF-β pathways in DGC endothelium (Fig. 3G), both known drivers of endothelial plasticity. This was accompanied by elevated EGR1 expression [72, 73]. Western blotting confirmed higher levels of Notch and TGF-β1 proteins in TECs compared to normal endothelial cells (Fig. 3H). Functionally, EGR1 overexpression in TECs triggered hallmark features of EndMT: a loss of endothelial markers (CD31, VE-cadherin) and induction of mesenchymal markers (α-SMA, vimentin) (Fig. 3I-J). However, this same perturbation failed to induce EndMT in HUVECs, highlighting the tumor-specific context dependency. Importantly, pharmacological inhibition of either Notch or TGF-β signaling substantially reduced EGR1-driven EndMT in TECs (Fig. 3K-L), restoring endothelial identity and dampening mesenchymal transitions. These findings reveal a cooperative axis between EGR1 and these pathways in remodeling the tumor endothelium. Finally, mIF across patient samples spanning premalignant lesions, IGC, and DGC showed a pronounced enrichment of EGR1⁺IGFBP7⁺FAP⁺ stromal cells in DGC (Fig. 3M). These cells were rare in IGC and nearly absent in precancerous stages, underscoring the importance of EGR1-driven stromal reprogramming as a key pathogenic feature in DGC progression.

EGR1–IGFBP7/VCAM1 signaling promotes EMT in GC cells

The endothelial-derived CAF-like cells (EC-CAFs) transition in TECs is a key process that promotes tumor progression through dynamic changes in cellular phenotypes [74]. Recent evidence suggests that EC-CAFs secrete various pro-tumorigenic factors and extracellular matrix components, collectively establishing a favorable microenvironment that drives EMT in cancer cells, thereby enhancing their invasiveness and metastatic potential [75]. Motivated by these findings, we explored how EGR1-mediated EndMT impacts EMT in GC cells.

We began by investigating the role of the EGR1–IGFBP7/VCAM1 signaling axis in TEC mesenchymal transition. IF staining revealed that EGR1 overexpression in TECs induced a mesenchymal-like phenotype, characterized by increased α-SMA and FAP levels, and a morphological shift typical of EC-CAFs (Fig. 4A). Silencing either IGFBP7 or VCAM1 reversed these changes, underscoring the critical role of the EGR1–IGFBP7/VCAM1 axis. Western blotting confirmed a significant upregulation of CAF markers, including FAP and GJA4, in TECs upon EGR1 overexpression, which was attenuated by knockdown of either VCAM1 or IGFBP7 (Fig. 4B).

We next examined the effects of EC-CAFs on GC cells. Co-culture and functional assays demonstrated that the EGR1–IGFBP7/VCAM1 signaling pathway in TECs significantly enhanced the growth and invasiveness of GC cells (Fig. 4C). In both colony formation (Fig. 4D) and tumor sphere assays (Fig. 4E), MKN74 and MKN45 GC cells co-cultured with EGR1-transfected TECs showed increased colony numbers and sizes. Notably, these pro-tumorigenic effects were reversed when either IGFBP7 or VCAM1 was silenced, highlighting the critical role of the EGR1–IGFBP7/VCAM1 axis in driving these effects. Transwell invasion (Fig. 4F) and wound healing assays (Fig. 4G) confirmed increased migration and invasion of GC cells in the presence of EGR1-overexpressing TECs. Western blot analysis of GC cells co-cultured with EGR1-overexpressing TECs showed upregulation of EMT markers, including TNC, N-cadherin, MMP9, and MMP2, alongside downregulation of E-cadherin (Fig. 4H). Silencing either IGFBP7 or VCAM1 reversed these changes, further supporting the role of the EGR1–IGFBP7/VCAM1 axis in promoting EMT. In vivo, EGR1-overexpressing TECs accelerated tumor growth, confirming the importance of this signaling axis in tumor progression (Fig. 4I). We then investigated the role of the EGR1–IGFBP7/VCAM1 pathway in angiogenesis. Immunofluorescence analysis of GC tissue samples confirmed its relevance in vivo, showing co-localization of IGFBP7, CD93 (a receptor for IGFBP7), and ENG in DGC tissues, with a Pearson correlation coefficient of 0.683 (Fig. 4J). This suggests that the EGR1–IGFBP7/VCAM1 axis is involved in tumor-associated vascular remodeling. Tube formation assays further supported this, demonstrating that this axis enhances endothelial cell function in angiogenesis (Fig. 4K). Recruitment assays revealed that TECs overexpressing EGR1 secreted higher levels of IGFBP7, promoting TEC recruitment, while VCAM1 knockdown reduced IGFBP7 secretion (Fig. 4L-M). Additionally, IF for CD93 showed increased CD93 expression on EGR1-overexpressing TECs, which was attenuated upon silencing IGFBP7 or VCAM1 (Fig. 4N), supporting activation of the CD93–IGFBP7 axis.

Building on these findings, we next analyzed DGC tissues via mIF, showing significant co-localization of IGFBP7, EGR1, and TNC in epithelial regions, with a Mander’s Overlap Coefficient (MOC) of 0.785, further highlighting the close association between these markers in DGC cells (Fig. 4O). Public dataset analyses (GSE163813 and GSE207373) reinforced the subtype-specific heterogeneity of EGR1 signaling in GC. Notably, IGFBP7 knockdown in AGS cells (intestinal-type) led to an upregulation of EMT markers, while in SGC7901 cells (poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma), EMT markers were suppressed (Fig. 4P). This opposing regulatory pattern underscores the functional divergence of the EGR1–IGFBP7/VCAM1 axis in different GC subtypes. Western blotting results from MKN74 (intestinal-type) and MKN45 (diffuse-type) cells confirmed distinct expression patterns (Fig. 4Q). In line with this, CCLE database analysis showed higher IGFBP7 expression in MKN45 compared to MKN74 cells (Figure S9), supporting the presence of subtype-specific differences. Additionally, pseudotime analysis of epithelial compartments (GSE134520) showed stable IGFBP7 expression throughout epithelial evolution in IGC, suggesting a limited role in intestinal-type progression (Figure S10). Functional assays further demonstrated that EGR1 promoted tumor progression in MKN45 but inhibited growth in MKN74 cells. These findings were corroborated by in vivo xenograft experiments, which confirmed the subtype-specific effects of the EGR1–IGFBP7/VCAM1 axis (Fig. 4R-W). These subtype-dependent effects show that the same signaling axis can drive very different outcomes depending on tumor context and point to the EGR1–IGFBP7/VCAM1 pathway as a promising therapeutic target, particularly in DGC.

EGR1–IGFBP7/VCAM1 signaling facilitates the peritoneal metastatic dissemination of GC cells

GC commonly metastasizes to the peritoneal cavity, with EMT playing a critical role in enhancing tumor cell invasiveness and accelerating metastatic progression [76]. Given our previous findings that IGFBP7 promotes EMT, we investigated its role in the peritoneal metastasis of GC. We used the GSE183904 dataset from the GEO database, which includes primary GC and GC peritoneal metastasis (GCPM) samples.

The scRNA-seq workflow is summarized in Fig. 5A, UMAP plots and marker-based bubble plots in Fig. 5B show the main cell types. IGFBP7 expression was higher in fibroblasts, endothelial cells and epithelial cells from GCPM samples (Fig. 5C), and epithelial cells in GCPM showed strong activation of the EGR1–IGFBP7/VCAM1 axis (Fig. 5D-E). Since tumor cells in peritoneal metastasis are more aggressive than those in primary tumors [77], we performed pathway analysis on epithelial cells from GCPM with high IGFBP7 expression. The results revealed enhanced EMT signaling (Fig. 5F). Trajectory analysis showed that endothelial cells in GCPM transdifferentiate into CAF-like fibroblasts, with IGFBP7 playing a role in this transition (Fig. 5G-I). Similarly, mesothelial cells in GC ascites exhibited enriched expression of CAF-associated markers, and co-localization of mesothelial marker UPK3B with FAP and IGFBP7 confirmed CAF-like properties in mesothelial clusters (Figure S11). Additionally, analysis of cell-cell communication (CCC) showed marked differences between primary GC and GCPM, with fibroblast-epithelial interactions significantly strengthened in GCPM (Fig. 5J-M). ST analysis further confirmed the co-localization of EGR1–IGFBP7/VCAM1 signaling with both EMT and angiogenesis processes (Fig. 5N-O). mIF analysis confirmed that epithelial regions with high EGR1-IGFBP7 expression exhibited pronounced EMT characteristics (Fig. 5P).

To validate the functional role of EGR1–IGFBP7/VCAM1 signaling in peritoneal metastasis, xenograft models were employed. A mixture of treated TECs and tumor cells was injected into nude mice (Fig. 5Q). EGR1–IGFBP7/VCAM1 signaling mediates gefitinib resistance in GC

Epidermal growth factor receptor tyrosine kinase inhibitors (EGFR-TKIs) generally have limited efficacy in GC, largely due to tumor heterogeneity, compensatory angiogenic bypass mechanisms, and an immunosuppressive TME [78]. Given our previous findings that EGR1–IGFBP7/VCAM1 signaling promotes angiogenesis and EMT, we hypothesized that this pathway contributes to the poor response of GC, especially diffuse-type gastric cancer (DGC), to EGFR-TKI therapy. To test this hypothesis, we assessed drug sensitivity across different levels of EGR1–IGFBP7/VCAM1 signaling using the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) database. A positive correlation was observed between higher EGR1–IGFBP7/VCAM1 levels and the IC50 values for EGFR-TKIs, including Gefitinib, Afatinib, and Erlotinib (Fig. 6A). To validate these findings, we downloaded three additional drug resistance datasets from the GEO database and compared EGR1/IGFBP7/VCAM1 expression between responders and non-responders. Figure 6B shows significantly higher expression of EGR1, IGFBP7, and VCAM1 in the gefitinib non-responder group. Spatial transcriptomic (ST) analysis revealed the colocalization of EGFR signaling with IGFBP7, suggesting a potential relationship (Fig. 6C). CCK-8 assays showed that the IC50 of gefitinib was significantly increased in MKN74 and MKN45 cells co-cultured with EGR1-overexpressing TECs, and this effect was reduced when IGFBP7 or VCAM1 was silenced (Fig. 6D).

We then evaluated the impact of EGR1–IGFBP7/VCAM1 signaling on gefitinib-mediated anti-angiogenesis. TECs were divided into eight experimental groups, with four groups treated with gefitinib. Gefitinib treatment significantly suppressed tubule formation across all groups. However, in the gefitinib-treated groups, overexpression of EGR1 reduced the anti-angiogenic effect, as indicated by partial restoration of tubule formation. This attenuation was reversed by silencing IGFBP7 or VCAM1 (Fig. 6E), confirming the involvement of both proteins in modulating endothelial cell responses to gefitinib.

Finally, we tested this pathway in vivo (Fig. 6F). In the first model, we co-injected TECs and MKN45 cells into nude mice. EGR1 overexpression in TECs reduced the antitumor effect of gefitinib and increased tumor volume, while knockdown of IGFBP7 or VCAM1 in TECs partly restored drug sensitivity (Fig. 6G). In the second model, we implanted MKN45 cells alone. EGR1 overexpression again impaired gefitinib efficacy, and IGFBP7 silencing in tumor cells partly reversed this effect (Fig. 6H). The role of EGR1–VCAM1 signaling in tumor growth has been reported in other systems [79–81], we did not further investigate it here. Together, these results suggest that EGR1–IGFBP7/VCAM1 signaling may play an important role in gefitinib resistance in GC.

EGR1–IGFBP7/VCAM1 signaling is associated with immune exclusion features of GC

A comprehensive analysis across multiple GC cohorts revealed a strong correlation between EGR1 expression and immune architecture within the TME. In almost every dataset examined, higher EGR1–IGFBP7/VCAM1 signaling levels were positively associated with stromal programs enriched in CAFs and M2-polarized macrophages, while signatures of cytotoxic lymphocytes were either diminished or inversely correlated (Fig. 7A-B). These patterns, observed across different computational algorithms, reinforce previous findings that stromal expansion and macrophage reprogramming can suppress antitumor immunity [82].

To further explain these results, we analyzed spatial transcriptomic maps from both intestinal-type and diffuse-type GC (Fig. 7C-D and Figure S12). EGR1 emerged as a central upstream regulator, with its expression aligning closely with IGFBP7 and VCAM1 in stromal regions enriched in CAFs and immunosuppressive macrophages. These areas also exhibited high levels of myeloid-associated chemokines and immune mediators such as HLA-DPA1, CX3CR1, CXCL12, CSF1R, and CCL2, known to recruit and sustain myeloid cells within the TME [83–85]. UMAP projection and Area Under Curve (AUC)-based ranking further revealed significant differences in cellular composition between intestinal-type and diffuse-type tumors (Figure S13). We identified specific areas where the EGR1–IGFBP7/VCAM1 axis overlapped with cytokine-driven immunosuppressive signals, forming a stromal barrier that physically and functionally hindered T cell entry into the tumor core.

In line with these findings, mIF performed on DGC specimens, analyzed quantitatively using the HALO platform, showed that regions with high EGR1 expression had dense infiltrates of CAFs and tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), which were often in close proximity. In contrast, areas with low EGR1 expression showed a marked reduction in both macrophage infiltration and cellular proximity (Fig. 7E).

The functional impact of this axis was further demonstrated in co-culture experiments. TECs or MKN45 cells overexpressing EGR1 induced macrophages derived from THP-1 cells to adopt a TAM-like phenotype, with increased expression of CD206 and SPP1. Silencing IGFBP7 or VCAM1 significantly reduced this effect, confirming their role in mediating EGR1-driven macrophage polarization (Fig. 7F-I). Interestingly, the opposite result was observed in MKN74 cells (Fig. 7J-K). These functional assays were supported by computational immune dysfunction estimates, where tumors with high expression of EGR1, IGFBP7, and VCAM1 exhibited elevated Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) scores, an established indicator of immune evasion and resistance to checkpoint blockade (Fig. 7L).

At the tissue level, mIF analysis revealed distinct patterns in EGR1-high and EGR1-low DGC tumors (Fig. 7M). EGR1-high tumors were characterized by abundant SPP1⁺ macrophages and FAP⁺ fibroblasts, with few CD8⁺T cells present. Conversely, EGR1-low tumors had more accessible tumor cells and substantial T cell infiltration. Spatial quantification using HALO confirmed these observations: in EGR1-low tumors, CD8⁺T cells were located closer to malignant cells, whereas in EGR1-high tumors, CAFs and TAMs formed a dense barrier at the tumor front, restricting T cells to the periphery. Proximity histograms further confirmed that CD8⁺T cells within 20 μm of tumor cells were more prevalent in EGR1-low tumors than in EGR1-high tumors (Fig. 7N). Taken together, these results demonstrate that activation of the EGR1–IGFBP7/VCAM1 signaling axis triggers stromal expansion and macrophage reprogramming, creating both physical and biochemical barriers that inhibit T cell infiltration, thereby promoting immune exclusion in GC.

EGR1–IGFBP7/VCAM1 signaling is associated with immunotherapy resistance of GC

We explored how the EGR1–IGFBP7/VCAM1 pathway affects the immune context and immunotherapy response in GC. CD8⁺T cell infiltration is a key determinant of response to immune checkpoint blockade [86] and is influenced by tumor mutational burden and driver mutations [87, 88]. To assess whether our pathway is merely a marker of genomic instability, we compared genetic features between axis-high and axis-low tumors. As shown in Fig. 8A, mutation load and copy-number variations (CNVs) did not differ significantly between the two groups. A genomic instability score also indicated lower genomic instability in epithelial cells with high EGR1–IGFBP7/VCAM1 signaling (Fig. 8B). In contrast, histopathological analysis showed reduced immune cell infiltration in axis-high tumors (Fig. 8C), suggesting that this pathway is more closely linked to immune exclusion than mutational burden. Spatial transcriptomic analysis revealed that DGC exhibit stronger immune exclusion than IGC (Figure S14). In DGC, regions with high EGR1–IGFBP7/VCAM1 expression were enriched in CAF and TAM signatures, consistent with a stromal barrier that limits lymphocyte access. In our cohort, CD8 protein expression was inversely correlated with both EGR1 and IGFBP7 (Fig. 8D-E). Next, we examined whether serum cytokine levels showed a similar pattern. In the retrospective set of patients with pre-treatment serum, the axis-high group had lower levels of CD8⁺ T cell activation-related cytokines (IFN-γ, TNF-α) and higher levels of immunosuppressive cytokines (IL-10, TGF-β1), together with increased M2/TAM-associated chemokines (CCL2, CXCL12), compared with the axis-low group (Fig. 8F). Consistently, ST analysis showed PD-1 and PD-L1 signals located close to EGR1–IGFBP7/VCAM1-high regions (Fig. 8G). Taken together, these findings support an immune context in axis-high patients that is more immunosuppressive and macrophage-rich, and may be more prone to T-cell exhaustion and weaker responses to immunotherapy. We then tested how EGR1–IGFBP7/VCAM1 signaling affects T cell function. In co-culture experiments, EGR1 overexpression reduced CD8⁺T cell activation, an effect partially reversed by silencing IGFBP7 or VCAM1 (Fig. 8H-I). Additionally, tumor-derived EGR1–IGFBP7/VCAM1 signaling modulated T cell function in a manner dependent on tumor type. In MKN45 (DGC), the axis suppressed T cell activity, whereas in MKN74 (IGC), the opposite effect was observed (Fig. 8J-K). We investigated the relationship between EGR1/IGFBP7/VCAM1 expression and immunotherapy response. In the IMvigor210 cohort treated with anti-PD-L1 agents, patients with high expression of this axis had lower response rates compared to those with low expression (Fig. 8L). Similarly, in our independent cohort, patients with high combined EGR1–IGFBP7/VCAM1 IHC expression showed a lower response rate to immunotherapy (Fig. 8M). These findings suggest that high EGR1–IGFBP7/VCAM1 signaling contributes to immune evasion, promoting resistance to immunotherapy in GC.

Discussion

This study describes the role of IGFBP7 in GC across different histological subtypes and cell compartments. We show subtype-specific expression patterns, a dual role in tumor and stromal cells, and links with resistance to targeted therapy and immunotherapy. Together, these data support a model in which IGFBP7 helps shape GC heterogeneity and remodeling of the TME.

We first confirmed that IGFBP7 expression is significantly higher in DGC than in IGC, a difference that is partly explained by its additional expression in poorly differentiated tumor cells in DGC. In line with the stronger mesenchymal traits of DGC, and with the high expression of IGFBP7 in fibroblasts and endothelial cells, our data suggest that IGFBP7 is preferentially enriched in cell states with mesenchymal features. Previous studies have linked IGFBP7 to poor differentiation and EMT, showing that its overexpression is associated with loss of E-cadherin and gain of N-cadherin and vimentin and with more aggressive tumor behavior in several cancer types [89–91]. Consistent with this, recent work in GC has shown that CAF-derived IGFBP7 promotes tumor progression by enhancing TAM/M2 macrophage polarization through the FGF2/FGFR1/PI3K/AKT axis and is associated with poor prognosis. These observations support the view that IGFBP7 acts as a microenvironment-dependent regulator whose impact can shift from subtle to strongly pro-tumorigenic depending on histology, cellular source and stromal context.

Through integrative multi-omics analyses, we identified a context-dependent EGR1–IGFBP7/VCAM1 signaling axis. EGR1 is a transcription factor that can promote both angiogenesis and EMT by inducing pro-angiogenic mediators such as VEGF, repressing epithelial markers like E-cadherin, and activating mesenchymal markers including N-cadherin and vimentin, often through PI3K/Akt and MAPK signaling [73, 92, 93].

In our study, EGR1-regulated IGFBP7/VCAM1 signaling mediated EndMT in tumor endothelial cells and generated EC-CAFs that enhanced angiogenesis and EMT in GC cells. At the same time, the pathway showed opposite effects in tumor cells depending on differentiation status: it augmented EMT programs and malignant traits in poorly differentiated MKN45 cells but had more limited or even restraining effects in well-differentiated MKN74 cells. The interaction between IGFBP7 and the endothelial receptor CD93, which has been shown to support disorganized tumor vasculature [66], provides a link between this axis and vascular remodeling. Taken together, these findings suggest that the effect of the EGR1–IGFBP7/VCAM1 pathway varies by subtype and is shaped by the epithelial program, surrounding stroma and local vessels.

This kind of subtype-dependent reversal is in line with observations for other regulators within single cancer types. In gastric cancer, Lee, et al. showed that the transcription factor SP1 plays opposite roles in intestinal-type and diffuse-type tumors: loss of SP1 enhances migration and invasion in intestinal-type cells but decreases motility in diffuse-type cells [94].In breast cancer, SPDEF has been reported to behave as a tumor promoter in luminal subtypes but as a tumor suppressor in other molecular contexts [95], and NOS1AP has been described as having a subtype-specific dual role as tumor suppressor and oncogene [96]. These examples support the idea that a single pathway can switch from restraining to promoting malignancy depending on histology, molecular subtype and microenvironment, which is consistent with the opposite functions of the EGR1–IGFBP7/VCAM1 axis that we observe in intestinal-type versus diffuse-type GC.

The clinical efficacy of EGFR-TKIs in GC remains limited, and the underlying mechanisms are not fully understood. We found that higher IGFBP7 and EGR1–IGFBP7/VCAM1 signaling were associated with resistance to gefitinib and other EGFR-TKIs in pharmacogenomic datasets and in GC drug-response cohorts, and that activation of this axis in TECs and poorly differentiated tumor cells reduced gefitinib sensitivity in vitro and in vivo. By contrast, in well-differentiated cells the same pathway showed a more sensitizing profile. This paradoxical behavior provides a plausible explanation for the inconsistent clinical responses to anti-EGFR therapies in GC and underscores the need for patient stratification based on both molecular subtype and microenvironmental features. Recent studies have shown that high EGFR gene copy number alone may not predict response, and alternative pathways—such as EGFR/GRP78 signaling—may modulate therapeutic outcomes [97]. Our data suggest that EGR1–IGFBP7/VCAM1 signaling represents one additional mechanism contributing to primary or acquired resistance, particularly in DGC, and that targeting this axis might help refine EGFR-TKI use in selected patients. We also provide evidence that the EGR1–IGFBP7/VCAM1 pathway contributes to an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. Across multiple GC cohorts, high axis activity was linked to expansion of CAF-rich stroma, accumulation of SPP1⁺ and M2-like macrophages, high TIDE scores, and reduced CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration, with spatial analyses showing that CAFs and TAMs form a physical barrier around tumor nests in EGR1-high regions. In line with independent reports that CAF-derived IGFBP7 promotes TAM/M2 polarization in GC, our co-culture experiments showed that EGR1–IGFBP7/VCAM1 signaling drives macrophages toward a TAM-like phenotype and impairs CD8⁺ T-cell activation. Notably, these effects were again subtype-dependent, with diffuse-type models showing stronger immune exclusion, while intestinal-type models exhibited weaker or even opposite trends. Together with the observation that high EGR1/IGFBP7/VCAM1 expression is associated with poor response to anti–PD-L1/PD-1 checkpoint blockade in both public and independent cohorts, our data point to this axis as a potential stromal and immune checkpoint that limits the effectiveness of immunotherapy in GC.

These findings point to several potential therapeutic strategies that can be tested. As IGFBP7 signals through CD93 and VCAM1 at the tumor-vascular and stromal interface, agents targeting IGFBP7-CD93 signaling or VCAM1-mediated adhesion could be evaluated as partners for existing treatments [98]. Preclinical studies of CD93 blockade have shown that interfering with the CD93-IGFBP7 axis can normalize tumor vessels, enhance CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration and improve responses to PD-1–based immunotherapy, and early-phase trials are already testing anti-CD93 antibodies in combination with PD-1 inhibitors in patients with advanced solid tumors [65, 99, 100]. Together with our observation that high EGR1–IGFBP7–VCAM1 activity is linked to gefitinib resistance and immune exclusion, these findings suggest that inhibitors of this axis might be combined with EGFR-TKIs or immune checkpoint inhibitors to overcome resistance, particularly in DGC. Given the subtype-dependent behavior of the pathway, any such approaches will need to stratify patients by histology and by EGR1–IGFBP7–VCAM1 status when evaluating these combination strategies.

Limitations

Several limitations should be noted. The number of clinical specimens, especially for rare subtypes and metastatic lesions, is limited and may limit how broadly our findings can be generalized. Our evidence that tumor endothelial cells can acquire CAF-like properties is based on EndMT features in vitro and in xenograft models without lineage tracing in spontaneous GC, so their prevalence and impact in human tumors remain unclear. In addition, the mechanisms linking EGR1–IGFBP7/VCAM1 to EMT, vascular remodeling and immune escape, and the therapeutic value of targeting this axis and its crosstalk with other stromal and immune pathways, still need to be tested in more complex in vivo systems and in clinical studies.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study establishes the EGR1–IGFBP7/VCAM1 axis as a critical regulator of GC progression, therapy resistance, and immune evasion. Its context-dependent functions highlight the complexity of GC heterogeneity and underscore the need for subtype-specific therapeutic strategies. Future studies focusing on targeting this pathway—potentially in combination with EGFR inhibition or immunotherapy—may offer new avenues for improving outcomes in advanced GC patients.
