Elucidating the Pro‐Tumor Role of LncRNA CROCCP2 in Glioma via the miR‐5584‐5p/HOXD11/Autophagy Pathway
Authors: Feng Wang, Ganni Lei, Yuan Peng, Maoqiao Liu, Tianqi Jiang, Minghui Wang, Zhe Wang, Jun Pu, Jia Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTGlioma is the most common primary malignant tumor of the central nervous system, characterized by high invasiveness and a high recurrence rate. The clinical therapeutic efficacy remains limited, necessitating the exploration of novel molecular mechanisms and therapeutic targets. Long non‐coding RNAs (lncRNAs), as key epigenetic regulators in tumor progression, can influence tumorigenesis and development through lncRNA–miRNA–mRNA regulatory axes. Here, we reveal the critical role of the lncRNA CROCCP2/miR‐5584‐5p/HOXD11 axis in glioma. LncRNA CROCCP2 was significantly upregulated in glioma tissues and cell lines, in vitro, markedly promoting the proliferation, migration, and invasion of glioma cells. Mechanistically, LncRNACROCCP2 acts as a competing endogenous RNA to sponge miR‐5584‐5p, thereby relieving its suppression of the target gene HOXD11, leading to HOXD11 upregulation. HOXD11 negatively regulated autophagy, as reflected by reduced LC3B‐II and Beclin1 levels and increased p62 expression. Conversely, CROCCP2 or HOXD11 knockdown activated autophagy and provided supportive evidence for ongoing autophagic flux based on the addition of LC3B‐II and p62 after bafilomycin A1 treatment. Knockdown of either lncRNA CROCCP2 or HOXD11 suppressed glioma cell proliferation, migration, and invasion in vitro. In addition, HOXD11 silencing markedly inhibited subcutaneous and intracranial tumor growth in nude mice; furthermore, HOXD11 knockout can even halt the growth of subcutaneous tumors. Collectively, our findings suggest that the lncRNA CROCCP2/miR‐5584‐5p/HOXD11 axis contributes to glioma cell malignant phenotypes in vitro and tumor growth in vivo, at least partly through negative regulation of autophagy, providing a potential molecular target for glioma therapy.

Introduction

Glioma is the most prevalent and highly aggressive primary malignant tumor of the central nervous system, constituting approximately 81% of all intracranial tumors [1]. Despite significant advances in multimodal treatment strategies, including maximal surgical resection, radiotherapy, and temozolomide (TMZ)‐based chemotherapy, the prognosis for patients with high‐grade gliomas remains dismal, with median overall survival typically less than two years [2, 3, 4]. The 2016 World Health Organization (WHO) classification of central nervous system tumors incorporated key molecular markers, such as isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH) mutation and 1p/19q co‐deletion, underscoring the critical role of molecular subtyping in guiding precise therapeutic approaches [5]. Consequently, elucidation of the molecular mechanisms underlying glioma pathogenesis and identification of novel therapeutic targets remain pressing objectives.

Advances in high‐throughput sequencing (HTS) and bioinformatics have revealed numerous genes, signaling pathways, and non‐coding RNAs intimately involved in tumorigenesis and progression [6]. Among these, long non‐coding RNAs have emerged as pivotal epigenetic regulators that can act as competing endogenous RNAs to sequester microRNAs, thereby modulating downstream gene expression. This regulatory axis profoundly influences various tumor‐associated biological processes, including proliferation, migration, invasion, apoptosis, and resistance to therapy [7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12]. Nonetheless, a substantial number of novel lncRNAs with uncharacterized functions remain to be elucidated.

We identified a novel lncRNA, CROCCP2, exhibiting significant upregulation in glioma tissues. Computational predictions suggested that lncRNA CROCCP2 serves as a ceRNA by sponging miR‐5584‐5p, thereby regulating the expression of HOXD11. Elevated HOXD11 expression was correlated with poorer patient prognosis, implying a putative oncogenic role [13, 14]. The HOX gene family, recognized for its key functions in embryonic development, is frequently aberrantly expressed in various cancers, contributing to malignant phenotypes such as enhanced proliferation, invasion, and metastasis [15, 16, 17, 18]. However, the precise mechanistic role of HOXD11 in glioma remains unclear. While previous studies have demonstrated that lncRNA–miRNA–mRNA networks modulate tumor malignancy through diverse pathways, whether the lncRNACROCCP2/miR‐5584‐5p/HOXD11 axis modulates glioma via autophagy regulation remains to be determined.

In this article, we investigated that LncRNACROCCP2 promotes glioma cell proliferation, migration, and invasion by sponging miR‐5584‐5p to upregulate HOXD11 and suppress autophagy in vitro. Furthermore, in vivo knockdown of HOXD11 significantly inhibited tumor growth in nude mice, whereas HOXD11 overexpression partially reversed the tumor‐suppressive effects elicited by LncRNACROCCP2 silencing. Collectively, our findings reveal a novel mechanistic pathway in which the LncRNACROCCP2/miR‐5584‐5p/HOXD11 axis promotes glioma cell malignant phenotypes in vitro and tumor growth in vivo, at least partly through negative regulation of autophagy, highlighting its potential as a molecular target for glioma therapy [19, 20, 21].

Materials and Methods

Ethics Approval and Consent to Participate

All procedures were performed in compliance with relevant laws and institutional guidelines, and the animal experiments were authorized and approved by the Ethics Committee of Kunming Medical University (KMMU2025MEC106 and KMMUX2025020031).

Glioma Tissue Sample

This study included 12 glioma tissue samples and 6 paired adjacent normal brain tissues, all obtained from glioma patients undergoing surgical resection at the First Affiliated Hospital of Kunming Medical University. All methods involving human participants and the use of human tissue samples were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. The experimental protocols were reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Kunming Medical University (approval number: KMMU2025MEC106). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians prior to inclusion in the study.

Cell Lines

Human normal astrocyte cell line (HEB) and human glioma cell lines (U251, LN229, U‐118‐MG, U87) were obtained from Yunnan JemBiotech Co. Ltd. HEB, U251, LN229, and U87 cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (PS). U‐118‐MG cells were cultured in RPMI‐1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% PS. All cells were maintained at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2.

Experimental Animals and Grouping

All animal experimental procedures were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. The study is reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines. 36 nude mice aged 6–8 weeks, weighing 18–22 g, were provided by the Department of Laboratory Animal Science, Kunming Medical University. Mice were randomly assigned into six groups: U251 control group (NC), U251 si‐HOXD11 group, U251 HOXD11 knockout group (KO), LN229 control group (NC), LN229 si‐HOXD11 group, and LN229 HOXD11 knockout group (KO). All mice were housed under specific pathogen‐free (SPF) conditions. Animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Kunming Medical University (approval number: KMMUX2025020031). At the endpoint of the study, mice were euthanized by CO2 inhalation followed by cervical dislocation, in accordance with institutional guidelines.

Microarray Data Acquisition and Preprocessing

RNA sequencing data from 666 glioma samples and 5 normal brain tissues were downloaded from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database (https://www.cancer.gov/ccg/research/genome‐sequencing/tcga). Differential expression analysis of lncRNAs, miRNAs, and mRNAs was performed using the R package “edgeR” with selection criteria of |log2 fold change| > 2 and p < 0.05. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were conducted using the “clusterProfiler” R package. lncRNA–miRNA–mRNA regulatory interactions were predicted via ENCORI, DIANA, TargetScan v7.2, and miRcode databases.

Quantitative Real‐Time PCR (qRT‐PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from tumor cells using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA). Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using a cDNA reverse transcription kit (Invitrogen, USA). RT‐PCR was performed with SYBR Green qPCR Mix (Invitrogen) in a 20 μL reaction system to quantify the expression levels of lncRNA CROCCP2, miR‐5584‐5p, and HOXD11. β‐actin served as the internal reference gene. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences are listed in Table 1.

Western Blot

Total protein was extracted from cells using RIPA lysis buffer. Protein concentration was determined by BCA assay. Equal amounts of protein were mixed with 10% SDS‐PAGE loading buffer and denatured at 100°C for 5 min, then separated by SDS‐PAGE. Proteins were transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore). Membranes were blocked with 5% non‐fat milk at room temperature for 1 h and incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies. After washing with TBST, membranes were incubated with appropriate secondary antibodies at room temperature. Protein bands were detected using the Odyssey infrared imaging system (LI‐COR Biosciences, USA). β‐actin was used as the loading control. ImageJ was used for density analysis of each band. For each sample, we first measured the grayscale value of the target protein band, where LC3B was used to quantify the LC3‐I and LC3‐II bands separately, and the relative grayscale value was evaluated using the LC3‐II/LC3‐I ratio. This was then normalized to the grayscale value of the corresponding internal control band to correct for differences in protein loading and transfer efficiency. After completing the initial normalization step, we used the normalized value of the control group as a reference value and set it to 1.0. Other groups are expressed as multiples of change relative to the control group (set as 1).

Plasmid Construction and Transfection

Wild‐type and mutant plasmids for CROCCP2 and HOXD11, as well as shRNA constructs, were synthesized by JemBiotech (Yunnan, China). Plasmid transfection was performed using TransIntro EL reagent (TransGen Biotech, China). miRNA mimics and inhibitors were transfected using riboFECT CP reagent (RiboBio, China) following the manufacturer's protocols.

Colony Formation Assay

U251 and LN229 cells were plated in 6‐well plates at 500 cells per well. After 14 days of incubation, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.5% crystal violet. Colonies were imaged and quantified using ImageJ software.

Cell Counting Kit‐8 Assay

Cells were seeded in 96‐well plates at 3 × 103 cells per well. After 48 h of transfection, 10 μL of CCK‐8 reagent (Dojindo, CK04) was added to each well and incubated for 2 h. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured to calculate cell proliferation rates using the formula: Proliferation rate = [(OD_sh‐CROCCP2—OD_blank)/ (OD_negative control—OD_blank)] × 100%.

Wound Healing Assay

U251 and LN229 cells were seeded in 6‐well plates and grown to 80% confluence. A sterile 10 μL pipette tip was used to create a “scratch” wound. Cells were washed with PBS and incubated with complete medium. Images were taken at 0, 24, and 48 h post‐scratch. Wound closure rate was quantified using ImageJ.

Transwell Migration and Invasion Assay

Transwell chambers with 8 μm pore size (Corning) were precoated with Matrigel (BD Biosciences) for invasion assays. 5 × 104 cells were seeded in the upper chamber with serum‐free medium, while medium containing 10% FBS was added to the lower chamber. After 48 h of incubation, cells on the lower membrane surface were fixed, stained, and counted.

Dual‐Luciferase Reporter Assay

Wild‐type and mutant CROCCP2 and HOXD11 sequences were cloned into the pEZX‐MT05 vector. U251 cells were co‐transfected with these constructs and miR‐5584‐5p mimics or negative control using the Secrete‐Pair Dual Luminescence Assay Kit (GeneCopoeia, SPDA‐D010). After 48 h, GLuc and SEAP luciferase activities were measured to assess miRNA‐target interactions.

CRISPR/Cas9‐Mediated HOXD11 Knockout

Three sgRNAs targeting the coding sequence of HOXD11 were designed and synthesized (https://zlab.bio). After annealing, sgRNAs were cloned into linearized RNA‐puro vectors to generate recombinant plasmids, which were transformed into E. coli for selection and sequencing validation. Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher) was used to transfect plasmids into glioma cells. Knockout efficiency was confirmed by RT‐PCR and Western blot.

Nude Mouse Subcutaneous Tumor Model and siRNA Injection

A total of 0.2 mL of glioma cells or HOXD11 knockout cells (5 × 107 cells/mL) was subcutaneously injected into the right axilla of nude mice. Once tumors formed, si‐HOXD11 or negative control siRNA was administered intratumorally once weekly. Tumor volume was measured weekly using the formula: V = π/6 × length × width [2]. Survival status was monitored.

Nude Mouse Orthotopic Tumor Model

Log‐phase U251 cells stably expressing luciferase were suspended in sterile PBS at 1 × 107 cells/mL, then mixed with precooled Matrigel at a 1:8 volume ratio. Under aseptic conditions, a cranial burr hole was drilled 2 mm posterior and 1.5 mm lateral to the bregma. Using a 10 μL microsyringe, 10 μL of the cell suspension was injected vertically 3 mm deep into the right striatum. The needle was left in place for 2 min to prevent reflux before withdrawal. The burr hole was sealed with bone wax, and the scalp was sutured.

In Vivo Bioluminescence Imaging

Thirty days after orthotopic tumor implantation, mice were anesthetized and injected intraperitoneally with luciferin sodium (15 mg/kg). Mice were positioned supine on an imaging platform (Olympus, Japan), and bioluminescence signals in the brain were captured. Tumor burden was assessed by relative luminescence intensity.

Hematoxylin and Eosin Staining

At the experiment endpoint, tumor tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 5 μm thickness. Sections were deparaffinized, hydrated, stained with hematoxylin for 5 min and eosin for 2 min, dehydrated through graded alcohols, cleared with xylene, and mounted with neutral resin. Histopathological morphology was examined by tissue slide scanning.

HOXD11 Expression Characteristics and Functional Enrichment Analysis

HOXD11 expression levels in various tumor types and corresponding normal tissues, as well as its prognostic value, were analyzed via the GEPIA database. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated using the “survival” R package. Gene Ontology and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses of HOXD11‐related genes were performed using the “clusterProfiler” R package.

Statistical Analysis

All data analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 21.0. All experiments were performed with at least three independent biological replicates. Comparisons between two groups were conducted by an independent samples t‐test; comparisons among three or more groups were done using one‐way ANOVA. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD). p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Upregulation of lncRNA CROCCP2 Promotes Glioma Cell Proliferation

To screen for functionally relevant lncRNAs in glioma, differential expression analysis was performed using RNA sequencing data from the TCGA database, identifying 53 significantly differentially expressed lncRNAs, including 12 upregulated and 41 downregulated lncRNAs (Figure 1A,B). Among them, lncRNA CROCCP2 was significantly upregulated (Figure 1C). To validate this finding, qPCR was performed to detect the expression levels of CROCCP2 in clinical glioma tissues and adjacent normal brain tissues, showing consistent results with the TCGA data: CROCCP2 expression was significantly increased in glioma tissues (Figure 1D). Additionally, compared to the normal human astrocyte cell line HEB, CROCCP2 expression in glioma cell lines U251, LN229, U‐118‐MG, and U87 was consistent with the tissue results (Figure 1E).

To further clarify the role of CROCCP2 in glioma proliferation, shRNA was designed to knock down CROCCP2 expression in U251 and LN229 cells. Knockdown of CROCCP2 significantly inhibited cell proliferation (Figure 1H), as evidenced by a reduction in colony formation (Figure 1F,G). These results indicate that CROCCP2 is aberrantly highly expressed in glioma cells and significantly promotes cell proliferation.

LncRNA CROCCP2 Promotes Glioma Proliferation and Invasion by Targeting miR‐5584‐5p

To identify the miRNAs targeted by CROCCP2, differential expression analysis of miRNAs was performed using the TCGA database, revealing 125 significantly dysregulated miRNAs. Among them, miR‐5584‐5p was markedly downregulated in glioma tissues (Figure 2A,C) and showed a negative correlation with CROCCP2 expression (Figure 2B), suggesting a potential regulatory relationship. To further validate the expression of miR‐5584‐5p in glioma, RT‐PCR was conducted to detect its levels in glioma tissues, paired adjacent normal tissues, and several glioma cell lines. Consistent with the bioinformatics analysis, miR‐5584‐5p was significantly downregulated in both glioma tissues and cells (Figure 2D,E). Moreover, knockdown of CROCCP2 in U251 and LN229 cells significantly increased miR‐5584‐5p expression (Figure 2F), further supporting the conclusion that miR‐5584‐5p is negatively regulated by CROCCP2. The miRcode database predicted complementary binding sites between CROCCP2 and miR‐5584‐5p (Figure 2H). To verify this interaction, dual‐luciferase reporter assays showed that miR‐5584‐5p mimics significantly decreased luciferase activity of the CROCCP2‐WT construct but had no obvious effect on the mutant plasmid (Figure 2G), indicating a direct targeting relationship.

To investigate the functional role of miR‐5584‐5p in glioma proliferation and invasion, miR‐5584‐5p mimics and inhibitors were transfected into U251 and LN229 cells. Results showed that miR‐5584‐5p mimics significantly inhibited colony formation and cell viability (Figure 2I–M), whereas the inhibitor had no significant effect. Transwell assays demonstrated that overexpression of miR‐5584‐5p effectively suppressed cell invasion (Figure 2N–P). These findings indicate that miR‐5584‐5p is downregulated in glioma and negatively regulated by lncRNA CROCCP2, and its overexpression can effectively inhibit glioma cell proliferation and invasion.

miR‐5584‐5p Targets HOXD11 and Negatively Regulates Its Aberrant Overexpression in Glioma

Given that the lncRNA–miRNA–mRNA regulatory network plays a crucial role in the progression of various tumors, we sought to further identify key downstream mRNAs within this axis. Transcriptomic data of glioma from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database were subjected to differential expression analysis, which revealed 191 differentially expressed mRNAs, including 185 upregulated and 6 downregulated genes (Figure 3A,B). By integrating the differentially expressed miRNA profile, correlation analysis was performed between these mRNAs and miR‐5584‐5p. The results demonstrated a significant negative correlation between HOXD11 and miR‐5584‐5p (Figure 3C). Subsequent large‐scale dataset analysis confirmed that HOXD11 expression was markedly elevated in glioma (Figure 3D), suggesting it may be a potential target gene of miR‐5584‐5p.

RT‐PCR analysis further showed that HOXD11 was significantly overexpressed in glioma tissue specimens and multiple glioma cell lines (Figure 3E,F), indicating its potential biological function in glioma. To investigate whether miR‐5584‐5p regulates HOXD11 expression, U251 and LN229 cells were transfected with either miR‐5584‐5p mimics or inhibitors. The results revealed that overexpression of miR‐5584‐5p significantly downregulated HOXD11 mRNA levels, whereas inhibition of miR‐5584‐5p led to a marked increase in HOXD11 expression (Figure 3G,H), supporting a negative regulatory relationship between the two. TargetScan and miRcode analyses were then employed to predict potential binding sites between miR‐5584‐5p and HOXD11 (Figure 3J). Dual‐luciferase reporter assays were performed following transfection of HOXD11 WT or MT plasmids into glioma cells. The results demonstrated that luciferase activity was significantly reduced in the miR‐5584‐5p mimics + HOXD11 WT group, whereas no significant change was observed in the MT group (Figure 3I), further confirming the direct targeting relationship. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that miR‐5584‐5p functions as an upstream regulator of HOXD11, directly targeting and modulating its expression.

Knockdown of HOXD11 Significantly Inhibits the Malignant Biological Behavior of Glioma Cells

RT‐PCR screening identified siRNA‐887 as the most effective interference fragment targeting HOXD11 (Figure 4A). Transfection of this fragment into U251 and LN229 cells confirmed its silencing efficiency, showing a significant reduction in HOXD11 mRNA levels in both cell lines (Figure 4B). Furthermore, Western blot analysis demonstrated that siRNA‐887 markedly suppressed HOXD11 protein expression in U251 and LN229 cells (Figure 4C,D). These results indicate that the selected interference fragment exhibits high silencing efficiency and can effectively inhibit HOXD11 expression. To investigate the biological effects of HOXD11 knockdown, we silenced HOXD11 in U251 and LN229 cells and performed functional assays. Colony formation assays revealed that the number of colonies in the HOXD11 knockdown group was significantly lower than that in the negative control (NC) group (Figure 4E,F), indicating reduced proliferative capacity. Consistent with this, CCK‐8 assays also showed decreased cell viability following HOXD11 silencing (Figure 4G).

Wound healing assays demonstrated that at both 24 and 48 h post‐scratch, the migration rate of si‐HOXD11–treated cells was markedly reduced, and wound closure rates were significantly decreased compared with the NC group (Figure 4H–J), suggesting that HOXD11 promotes glioma cell migration. Similarly, Transwell invasion assays showed that the number of U251 and LN229 cells traversing the Matrigel‐coated membrane to the lower chamber was markedly decreased after HOXD11 knockdown (Figure 4K–M), indicating that HOXD11 facilitates glioma cell invasion. These findings demonstrate that HOXD11 contributes to glioma progression in vitro, and its aberrant overexpression promotes the malignant biological behavior of glioma cells, including proliferation, migration, and invasion.

Knockdown or Knockout of HOXD11 Suppresses Subcutaneous and Orthotopic Glioma Growth in Nude Mice

To investigate the in vivo role of HOXD11 in regulating glioma growth, subcutaneous xenograft models were established in nude mice using U251 and LN229 cells, followed by intratumoral injection of si‐HOXD11. After 7 weeks of treatment, gross anatomical examination revealed that tumor volumes in the si‐HOXD11‐treated group were markedly smaller than those in the NC group, in both U251‐ and LN229‐derived tumors (Figure 5A). In the U251 model, tumor volumes in the treatment group began to decrease compared with controls from week 3, with an average difference exceeding 500 mm3 by week 7 (Figure 5B). In the LN229 model, differences became apparent from week 4, reaching 400 mm3 by week 7 (Figure 5C). These findings indicate that HOXD11 silencing significantly inhibits the growth rate of gliomas in vivo.

To further validate the function of HOXD11, CRISPR/Cas9 technology was used to construct HOXD11 KO plasmids (Figure 5D,E). Western blot analysis showed that all three plasmids reduced HOXD11 expression in U251 cells, with plasmid No. 2 achieving over 90% knockout efficiency; a significant inhibitory effect was also observed in LN229 cells (Figure 5F,G). These results confirmed the successful generation of high‐efficiency KO‐HOXD11 cell lines. Then we conducted in vitro experiments, including plate colony assays, wound healing assays, and Transwell assays, to evaluate the changes in proliferation, migration, and invasion capabilities of HOXD11 knockout cell lines. The results showed that after HOXD11 knockout, both U251 and LN229 cell lines exhibited significantly lower numbers and sizes of cell colonies compared to the NC group (Figure 5M,N), significantly reduced cell migration rates (Figure 5O–Q), and almost no cells invaded the outer membrane (Figure 5R,S). This indicates that HOXD11 knockout can almost completely block the proliferation, migration, and invasion of glioma cells. When KO‐HOXD11 cells were injected subcutaneously into nude mice, tumor growth was more profoundly restricted than in the siRNA‐treated group; in some mice, no palpable tumor mass was detected from week 3 onward (Figure 5K,L). Gross anatomical examination revealed that tumor volumes in the KO group were significantly smaller than in the NC group (Figure 5H). Dynamic tumor volume monitoring showed that, in the U251 model, KO tumors began to shrink significantly from week 4, with continued reduction until week 7 (Figure 5I), while in the LN229 model, rapid shrinkage was evident from week 5, with only minimal residual tumors in a few mice by week 7 (Figure 5J).

To recapitulate the primary tumor environment of gliomas, orthotopic xenograft models were generated in nude mice using luciferase‐labeled cells. In vivo bioluminescence imaging demonstrated markedly reduced luminescent signals in the KO‐HOXD11 and si‐HOXD11 groups compared with the NC group, indicating substantial tumor shrinkage, with the knockout group showing the smallest tumor burden (Figure 6A,B). HE staining of brain sections revealed minimal residual tumor tissue in the KO and si‐HOXD11 groups, with loosely arranged cells and reduced nuclear atypia (Figure 6C,D). To further assess tumor proliferative activity, we performed Ki‐67 immunohistochemical staining. Compared with the NC group, the proportion of Ki‐67 positive cells in the si‐HOXD11 group was significantly reduced. Quantitative analysis confirmed that the average optical density (AOD) index of the si‐HOXD11 group was significantly reduced (Figure 6E–G), indicating that tumor proliferative capacity was impaired after HOXD11 silencing. Notably, the number of positive cells in the KO‐HOXD11 group was significantly reduced, with only sporadic or almost undetectable Ki‐67 positive cell nuclei observed, showing a clear staining background and well‐defined nuclear morphology (Figure 6H,I). Collectively, these results demonstrate that HOXD11 not only regulates glioma cell behavior in vitro but also markedly promotes tumor progression in vivo. Its knockout significantly suppresses glioma growth, highlighting HOXD11 as a key gene in glioma development.

HOXD11 Promotes Glioma Progression by Suppressing Autophagy

Pan‐cancer expression analysis using the GEPIA database revealed that HOXD11 was significantly upregulated in glioma, esophageal carcinoma, head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, and lung squamous cell carcinoma, while being downregulated in six other cancer types (Figure 7A). Survival analysis further showed that the three‐year overall survival rate was 0% in the high HOXD11 expression group, compared to approximately 25% in the low‐expression group; notably, after 2,000 days of follow‐up, about 10% of patients in the low‐expression group remained alive (Figure 7B). These results suggest that high HOXD11 expression is closely associated with poor prognosis in glioma and may act as a potential oncogene in its pathogenesis. To investigate the potential mechanism by which HOXD11 contributes to glioma progression, we performed GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses. KEGG analysis indicated that HOXD11 was enriched in the p53 signaling pathway (Figure 7C), whereas GO analysis revealed its involvement in biological processes such as the regulation of autophagy (Figure 7D). These findings suggest that HOXD11 may influence glioma progression by modulating autophagy levels. To elucidate the regulatory role of HOXD11 in autophagy activity, we examined the expression of key autophagy‐related proteins in U251 and LN229 cells. As shown in Figure 7E–H, compared to the NC group, HOXD11 knockdown resulted in a significantly increased LC3B‐II/I ratio and Beclin1 protein levels (Figure 7F,G). Simultaneously, we observed a significant decrease in the protein level of p62, a specific substrate for autophagy degradation (Figure 7H). The simultaneous accumulation of LC3B‐II and degradation of p62 preliminarily suggests that HOXD11 deficiency stimulates autophagy activation rather than simple impairment of autophagic degradation. A key challenge in interpreting the elevated LC3B‐II levels lies in distinguishing whether it is due to accelerated autophagosome synthesis or impaired lysosomal clearance. To rigorously address this fundamental question, we performed autophagy flux analysis using the lysosomal inhibitor bafilomycin A1 (Baf‐A1), as Baf‐A1 blocks V‐ATPase and prevents lysosomal acidification. We hypothesized that if HOXD11 knockdown enhanced autophagy initiation, then inhibition of downstream degradation would lead to further accumulation of autophagy markers. The results were consistent with this hypothesis: In cells transfected with si‐HOXD11, the addition of Baf‐A1 significantly increased the levels of LC3B‐II and Beclin1 compared to cells treated with si‐HOXD11 alone (Figure 7F,G). More importantly, the si‐HOXD11‐induced p62 depletion was not only eliminated but effectively reversed to a state of abundant p62 accumulation after Baf‐A1 treatment (Figure 7H). This rescue effect of Baf‐A1 on p62 levels supports the interpretation that the decrease in p62 was at least partly dependent on lysosome‐mediated autophagic degradation. In summary, these findings suggest that knocking down HOXD11 triggers enhanced autophagy initiation and is accompanied by ongoing autophagic flux in glioma cells.

To validate the impact of the lncRNA CROCCP2/miR‐5584‐5p/HOXD11 axis on autophagy, CROCCP2 expression was silenced in U251 and LN229 cells. qPCR results showed that knocking down CROCCP2 significantly reduced the mRNA expression of HOXD11 and SQSTM1, while significantly increasing the expression of LC3 and Beclin1. Subsequently, overexpression of HOXD11 in CROCCP2‐silenced cells showed that LC3 and Beclin1 expression levels were reversed, and p62 was also significantly upregulated (Figure 7I,J). Furthermore, the expression detection results of HOXD11, LC3B, Beclin1, and p62 proteins were consistent with the qPCR results (Figure 7K–M), indicating that HOXD11 plays a critical role in CROCCP2‐mediated autophagy regulation. Collectively, these results demonstrate that CROCCP2, as the upstream component of the ceRNA regulatory network, modulates autophagy in glioma cells via HOXD11 expression.

CROCCP2 Regulates the Malignant Biological Behavior of Glioma by Suppressing Autophagy

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was used to examine intracellular autophagosomes. The results showed that knockdown of CROCCP2 in U251 and LN229 cells markedly increased the number of autophagosomes, with some fusing with lysosomes to form autolysosomes. In contrast, subsequent HOXD11 overexpression significantly reduced the number of autophagosomes (Figure 8A–C). Together with previous findings, these results suggest that CROCCP2 acts as a ceRNA to bind and deplete miR‐5584‐5p, thereby relieving its inhibitory effect on HOXD11. This leads to HOXD11 upregulation and the development of autophagy resistance in glioma cells.

To determine whether CROCCP2 regulates the malignant biological behavior of glioma via this mechanism, we silenced CROCCP2 in U251 and LN229 cells. Wound‐healing assays revealed that, at 48 h post‐knockdown, cell coverage in the scratch area was markedly lower than in the NC group, indicating a significant reduction in migration speed. Reintroduction of HOXD11 expression restored migration capacity (Figure 8D,E). Transwell invasion assays further demonstrated that CROCCP2 knockdown significantly decreased the number of cells invading the lower chamber in both U251 and LN229 lines compared to the NC group; HOXD11 overexpression reversed this inhibitory effect (Figure 8F,G). These findings indicate that CROCCP2 promotes glioma cell migration and invasion by relieving miR‐5584‐5p–mediated suppression of HOXD11, thereby enabling glioma cells to resist autophagy and maintain malignant phenotypes.

To further comprehensively evaluate the impact of CROCCP2 on tumor progression and enhance the translational significance of this study, we constructed the sh‐CROCCP2 cell line and established an orthotopic intracranial transplantation model. In vivo imaging results showed that the tumor volume in the sh‐CROCCP2 group was significantly smaller than that in the NC and sh‐CROCCP2 + OE‐HOXD11 groups (Figure 9A,B). HE staining results showed that the tumor tissue in the sh‐CROCCP2 group was smaller, the tumor cells were less densely packed, and some cell nuclei became more regular in morphology (Figure 9C,D). Survival analysis showed that sh‐CROCCP2 significantly improved the survival of nude mice, reaching 66% on day 15, while the survival rate was 0% in the NC and CROCCP2 + OE‐HOXD11 groups (Figure 9E). This indicates that CROCCP2 not only maintains the malignant biological behavior of glioma cells in vitro but also promotes glioma progression in vivo.

Discussion

In recent years, the lncRNA–miRNA–mRNA regulatory axis, as a core component of non‐coding RNA regulatory networks, has emerged as a pivotal mechanism in the initiation and progression of various malignancies [8, 28, 29]. LncRNAs act as competing endogenous RNAs to “sponge” miRNAs, thereby alleviating their inhibitory effects on target mRNAs and modulating multiple oncogenic pathways. This ceRNA mechanism has become a critical focus for exploring novel tumor mechanisms and developing therapeutic interventions [30, 31, 32]. In this study, we concentrated on glioma, a highly aggressive central nervous system malignancy with poor prognosis, and systematically elucidated for the first time the role of the lncRNA CROCCP2/miR‐5584‐5p/HOXD11 regulatory axis in mediating malignant phenotypes via autophagy modulation (Figure 10). This provides novel insights into the epigenetic regulatory network of glioma and offers potential targets for therapy.

The mechanism of lncRNA CROCCP2/miR‐5584‐5p/HOXD11 axis regulates glioma progression through autophagy.

Through analysis of TCGA datasets, we found that lncRNACROCCP2 was significantly upregulated in glioma tissues and cell lines, correlating closely with poor patient prognosis. Functional assays demonstrated that silencing lncRNA CROCCP2 markedly inhibited glioma cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, supporting its oncogenic role in glioma progression [33, 34]. Bioinformatics predictions coupled with dual‐luciferase reporter assays confirmed that lncRNA CROCCP2 contains complementary binding sites to miR‐5584‐5p and can directly bind to this miRNA. Mechanistic studies revealed that lncRNA CROCCP2 negatively regulates miR‐5584‐5p expression, while the downstream target gene HOXD11 is concomitantly upregulated, consistent with classical ceRNA network behavior [35, 36]. HOXD11, a transcription factor aberrantly activated in various solid tumors [14, 16, 37], was found here to be highly expressed in glioma. Prognostic analyses indicated a dismal 3‐year overall survival rate of 0% in glioma patients with high HOXD11 expression. However, its functional role in glioma had remained unclear until this study, which demonstrated that HOXD11 knockdown not only suppressed the malignant behaviors of glioma cells but also significantly delayed the growth of xenograft and orthotopic tumors, underscoring its tumor‐promoting function. It should be noted that the orthotopic U251 intracranial model used in this study has inherent limitations in evaluating diffuse tumor invasion. Since U251 cell intracranial tumors mainly present as dense tumor masses and no typical invasive phenotype has been observed in orthotopic transplantation models, current in vivo models cannot provide conclusive evidence to support the pro‐invasive role of HOXD11 compared to the diffuse invasive behavior common in human gliomas. This difference is mainly due to the limited survival of tumor‐bearing mice and the difficulty in capturing more extensive diffuse infiltration before the animals reach their humanitarian endpoint. Therefore, our in vivo data more strongly support the growth‐promoting role of HOXD11, while its role in diffuse brain invasion needs further validation in the future with the successful construction of more invasive glioma models.

Importantly, bioinformatics enrichment analyses and subsequent mechanistic experiments suggested that HOXD11 may facilitate glioma progression via regulation of autophagy pathways. Autophagy has been reported to exert tumor‐suppressive effects by enhancing protein degradation and reducing synthesis, thereby inhibiting sustained tumor cell growth [4, 38, 39]. GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses revealed significant enrichment of autophagy‐ and p53‐related signaling pathways. Consistently, interference of lncRNA CROCCP2 or HOXD11 in glioma cells resulted in marked upregulation of autophagy marker LC3B and downregulation of the autophagy substrate p62, indicating enhanced autophagic activity. The role of autophagy in tumors is complex and context‐dependent; in glioma, reduced autophagy levels have been correlated with malignant transformation and progression [40]. Decreased autophagy leads to proteostasis imbalance, facilitating clonal proliferation of pre‐malignant cells [41]. Moreover, previous studies have shown that autophagy activation, lipotoxicity, and lysosomal membrane permeability synergistically promote the death of glioma cells induced by pharmacological agents [42]. Our findings further corroborate that lncRNA CROCCP2 promotes glioma malignancy by upregulating HOXD11 to suppress autophagy.

Despite these systematic findings delineating the regulatory axis and its biological functions, limitations remain. First, the precise molecular mechanisms by which HOXD11 mediates autophagy remain unclear. Whether HOXD11 regulates key downstream genes or modulates the p53 pathway warrants further investigation through integrated approaches such as ChIP‐seq and RNA‐seq. Second, most mechanistic insights were derived from cellular and small animal models; validation in clinical specimens and organoid models is needed to enhance translational relevance. Additionally, RNA‐based therapeutics still face challenges in clinical application, including tissue‐specific delivery, stability, and immunogenicity, necessitating multidisciplinary integration with materials science and pharmaceutics for effective clinical translation. Another question worth exploring is the mechanism of CROCCP2 upregulation in gliomas. Although CROCCP2 is located on chromosome 1p, its increased expression is unlikely to be solely due to a copy number increase. Large‐scale 1p alterations are not typical genomic events in glioblastomas, and previous studies have shown that lncRNA dysregulation can often occur in a manner independent of gene dosage. Other possible mechanisms include transcriptional activation by upstream oncogenic transcription factors, promoter hypomethylation, or broader epigenetic/chromatin remodeling events [43]. Studies have shown that some lncRNAs can be overexpressed independently of copy number through the synergistic regulation of DNA methylation and transcription factors [22, 23]. Therefore, we hypothesize that aberrant CROCCP2 expression may not be a simple genomic dosage effect, but rather driven by transcriptional or epigenetic mechanisms. However, this study focused on the mechanism of the downstream target protein HOXD11 and did not directly explore the upstream regulatory mechanism of CROCCP2 expression. Future analyses should integrate factors such as copy number, promoter methylation, chromatin state, and transcription factor binding to elucidate this mechanism.

In summary, this study uncovers the lncRNA CROCCP2/miR‐5584‐5p/HOXD11 axis as a novel regulatory mechanism contributing to glioma cell malignant phenotypes in vitro and tumor growth in vivo, at least partly through autophagy regulation. It not only broadens the understanding of glioma molecular pathogenesis but also provides potential therapeutic targets. Notably, our previous study had already implicated the CROCCP2/miR‐5584‐5p axis in apoptosis‐related regulation in glioma through BIRC5, suggesting that this upstream ceRNA module may influence glioma biology via more than one downstream effector. In the present study, we further extend these findings by identifying HOXD11 as another functional target downstream of the same axis and demonstrating that CROCCP2 promotes glioma cell proliferation, migration, and invasion in vitro, in association with negative regulation of autophagy. Combined with the results of this study, it suggests that CROCCP2/miR‐5584‐5p may exert pleiotropic biological effects based on its downstream targets. Future studies integrating this axis with other key signaling pathways could construct a more comprehensive regulatory network, facilitating the development of multi‐targeted, personalized therapies to improve treatment efficacy and patient survival in glioma.

In conclusion, we systematically revealed the critical role of the lncRNA CROCCP2/miR‐5584‐5p/HOXD11 regulatory axis in regulating glioma cell proliferation, migration, and invasion in vitro and tumor growth in vivo. We demonstrated for the first time that lncRNA CROCCP2 acts as a ceRNA to sponge miR‐5584‐5p, thereby releasing HOXD11 from miRNA‐mediated repression. HOXD11 subsequently regulates autophagy, which may partly contribute to glioma cell malignant phenotypes in vitro. This regulatory axis holds not only biological significance but also represents a promising therapeutic target with high clinical translational potential. Further validation in more clinically relevant invasive glioma models is required before its translational significance can be fully established.
