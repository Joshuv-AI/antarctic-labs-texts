Exploring listeners’ perceptions of AI-generated and human-composed music for functional emotional applications
Kimaya Lecamwasam, Tishya Ray Chaudhuri

Abstract




This work investigates how listeners perceive and evaluate AI-generated as compared to human-composed music in the context of emotional resonance and regulation. Across a mixed-methods design, participants were exposed to both AI and human music under various labeling conditions (music
correctly
labeled as AI- or human-origin, music
incorrectly
labeled as AI- or human-origin, and
unlabeled
music) and emotion cases (
Calm
and
Upbeat
), and were asked to rate preference, efficacy of target emotion elicitation, and emotional impact. Participants were significantly more likely to rate human-composed music, regardless of labeling, as more effective at eliciting target emotional states, though quantitative analyses revealed no significant differences in emotional response. However, participants were significantly more likely to indicate preference for AI-generated music, yielding further questions regarding the impact of emotional authenticity and perceived authorship on musical appraisal. Qualitative data underscored this, with participants associating humanness with qualities such as imperfection, flow, and “soul.” These findings challenge the assumption that preference alone signals success in generative music systems. Rather than positioning AI tools as replacements for human creativity or emotional expression, they point toward a more careful design ethos that acknowledges the limits of replication and prioritizes human values such as authenticity, individuality, and emotion regulation in wellness and affective technologies.

Introduction

Generative AI is revolutionizing music creation, offering new possibilities for applications ranging from personalized soundtracks to therapeutic interventions. Despite their increasing integration into everyday music listening experiences, it is not yet clear whether the emotional impact of AI-generated music on human listeners matches that of human-composed music, and what mismatches, if any, arise. Without evaluating the emotional impact of AI-generated music, deploying music generation systems might inadvertently undermine one of music’s most important functions.

Our findings reveal a diverse range of features influencing listener classifications, including repetition, beat, melodic timing, and perceived “humanness”. However, subjective interpretations of features, music preferences, and determinations of maximal emotional resonance varied. These results highlight the complexity of designing generative systems that align with listener expectations while fostering emotional resonance and highlight the nuanced ways listeners evaluate generative music, revealing how perceptions of authenticity, emotional resonance, and prior experiences interact to shape judgments. Furthermore, these results underscore ethical concerns, including cultural biases in datasets and the subjective nature of authenticity.

Related Work

2.1
Emotion-Sensitive AI and Ethics

Given the well-established application of music as a tool to impact human perception, cognition, and emotional expression
[
4
,
5
]
, there is growing interest in assessments of the use of
generative
music in similar contexts, including for mental health interventions
[
6
,
7
]
, adaptive soundtracks for games
[
8
,
9
]
, and personalized music recommendations
[
10
,
11
]
. This interest reflects a broader trend in AI research: developing systems that better understand and respond to human emotions. There are several ethical concerns that must be addressed as the field progresses. At a minimum, there is ongoing debate surrounding the universality of emotional expression, as illustrated through the ways facial expressions may differ along culture-dependent, nature-nurture lines, which has notable implications for applications of emotion-centered facial recognition algorithms
[
12
,
13
,
14
]
. In health-specific contexts, this debate is especially relevant since any clinical applications of algorithms must (1) treat users equally, thus requiring the minimization of algorithmic bias, (2) promote user autonomy and confidentiality, which calls for increased awareness of the ways that “digital markers” are used to profile emotional and psychological states, and (3) be clinically efficacious and accountable, minimizing cases of discrimination or misidentification
[
15
,
16
]
. Proper emotion sensitivity in these use cases requires “intimate” applications of AI techniques, as well as Machine Learning and Deep Learning algorithms
[
17
]
, to ultimately provide responses to detected emotion at a “human” level with, ideally, appropriate sensitivity and contextual awareness. This work seeks to contribute to this larger ethical conversation through the lens of music for health, by investigating the features of music that are highlighted as “human” by human listeners as a way of understanding how authenticity, emotional resonance, and cultural context shape interactions with generative systems, to ultimately inform the design of more ethical emotion-sensitive music generation.

2.2
Ethical Considerations of Generative Music

Current perspectives on the use of AI in music creation and production raise questions surrounding copyright law
[
18
]
, training bias
[
19
]
, and practical application
[
20
]
. Algorithmic recommendation is already in use across many mainstream music streaming services, though recent work suggests that algorithmically-driven listening reduces consumption diversity, which ultimately negatively impacts long-term user conversion and retention
[
21
]
. In a multi-subject case study of “a classical/jazz music composer, a film and video game music composer, an interactive media composer, an electroacoustic composer, a sound artist, and a DJ,” researchers found that the threat that AI poses to job opportunities and artistic integrity is especially relevant to working artists and musicians, in part due to concerns related to a lack of transparency and creative control within AI tools
[
22
]
.

One way to promote transparency in this space is through providing methods for easy detection of AI-generated music to listeners. SONICS and the SpecTTTra model
[
23
]
provide a comprehensive framework for Synthetic Song Detection (SSD) by introducing a diverse dataset that includes a wide range of synthetic and human-composed music, addressing gaps in prior datasets by incorporating greater diversity in music styles and lyrics while enabling robust analyses of full-length compositions. The SpecTTTra model, designed to capture long-range temporal dependencies, allows for deeper insights into musical structure and authenticity. In a different approach, Spotify’s LLark
[
24
]
combines generative music modeling with natural language processing to create a multimodal system for enhanced music analysis by unifying annotations from diverse datasets, enabling the system to reason about and describe music in a way that is both flexible and interpretable. Moreover, LLark’s open-source approach promotes transparency and may encourage further research into music authenticity detection. However, though both SpecTTTra and LLark demonstrate strong potential for identifying AI-generated music through evaluation of features such as structural, timbral, and emotional nuance, there is still growing need in the field to “distinguish between human-composed and synthetic songs to safeguard artistic integrity and protect human musical artistry”
[
23
]
. In addition to this, many existing systems rely heavily on Western tonal music datasets, which reduces their cross-cultural applicability since different approaches to music convey different emotions in different cultures
[
25
]
. This parallels current emotion-sensitive systems’ tendencies to oversimplify emotional states, mapping them to a culturally limited set of categories or dimensions, thereby failing to capture the complexity and nuance of human emotional experiences
[
26
,
19
]
, all of which are considerations that will be assessed over the course of our study.

2.3
Applications of Emotion-Sensitive Music Generation for Health

This work also aims to contribute to the current debate surrounding the efficacy of generative music as a mental health intervention, as compared to human-composed music. In recent years, there has been a shift in psychotherapy towards flexibility, responsiveness, and “mutuality”, where therapists and clients work together as equal partners, given evidence of the statistically significant relationship shown between mutuality and therapeutic outcomes
[
27
,
28
]
. Given the current global mental health crisis
[
29
,
30
]
, there has been increasing focus on the use of “digital psychiatry” tools, in terms of psychiatrist-anchored telehealth and app-enabled care
[
30
]
and AI-powered intervention
[
31
]
. Despite the notable benefits of AI-based approaches, concerns about the role of algorithmic bias, as well as the potential for inadequate support and exploitation, remain
[
32
]
. Additionally, a shift to AI could potentially reduce true mutuality in the therapeutic process
[
33
,
34
]
, since emotional “understanding” is based on pattern recognition and language modeling instead of prior emotional experience and reciprocation, despite demonstrated ability of Large Language Models (LLMs) such as ChatGPT to, at times, outperform the general public on metrics such as the Levels of Emotional Awareness Scale
[
31
,
35
]
.

Though a detailed analysis of the broad debate for and against AI-powered therapeutic interventions is out of scope for this paper, several of these more general concerns are especially relevant for applications of generative music as a therapeutic intervention. Generative music offers a level of creative flexibility to music therapists that has the potential to be especially sensitive to patients’ needs
[
36
]
. However, as the field stands, it is still unclear which elements of purely human-composed music most impact human emotion, thus introducing a confounding factor to this approach. Moreover, prior work has shown that participants reportedly “enjoyed music less” when they believed it to be AI-generated through tests of “‘human-sounding’ classical music excerpts”
[
37
]
, though more work in this space investigating genre and listener diversity is needed, especially since musical enjoyment plays a significant role in the efficacy of music-based health interventions, and holds demonstrated physiological
[
38
]
and psychological impact
[
39
]
.

While work such as the EmotionBox
[
40
]
sought to account for limitations in emotion-sensitive AI by developing systems that did not rely upon pre-labeled emotion datasets, so as to not incorporate potentially biased emotion labels, the evaluation of human preference in their work was not intensive, though it does indicate promising results. Systems like MusicRL
[
41
]
, which rely on reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), provide valuable insights, offering a method to make AI-generated music more emotionally responsive. While MusicRL improves music generation quality by learning from large-scale user feedback
[
41
]
, the validation process in MusicRL, which uses a simplified one-to-five rating system that accounts for factors like text adherence, acoustic quality, and overall appeal all in one scale, lacks the depth necessary to explore which specific features are hallmarks of AI-generated or human-composed music
[
41
]
. It is also important to account for the role that
human
preference may play towards biasing models, with respect to dataset labels and human feedback validation steps. There is established precedent demonstrating that “human essence” is more preferentially attributed to those perceived as in-group individuals
[
42
]
. As such, the way we define “humanness” when assessing AI-generated music becomes especially relevant, since culturally biased definitions could accidentally dehumanize those in a model’s “out-group” due to implicit biases, especially with regards to the inherently individual nature of emotional reaction and resonance, as well as the presence of musical instruments and techniques that may be unfamiliar to those who are not a part of the associated culture.

3
Methods

This study, which compared the emotional resonance of human-composed and AI-generated music (Appendix
A
), was conducted using Prolific via Qualtrics survey (
N=152
). There were two cases of music that participants listened to: “Calming and Soothing” (
Calm
) and “Upbeat and Invigorating” (
Upbeat
). Within both of these cases, participants were provided with two one-minute-long songs to listen to, one that was generated by SunoAI and the other that was composed in-house by Myndstream
1
1

1

More information about Myndstream can be found at https://myndstream.com/
. Both the Suno system and the producer were provided with the same two, 200-word prompts/briefs:

•

Calm
: “Compose a relaxing instrumental piece to eliminate anxiety. Use soothing melodies, gentle harmonies, and calm tempo. Include soft piano, ambient synths and ethereal effects to evoke peace and relaxation.”

•

Upbeat
: “Compose an invigorating instrumental piece to boost mood. Use uplifting melodies, dynamic rhythms, and a lively tempo. Include electronic drums, vibrant synths, and ethereal effects to evoke joy.”

Both prompts were written with input from Myndstream’s Head of Music based on how he would brief human composers to compose music for the two emotion categories. Both the producer and the research team member who generated the AI-generated music did not have access to the music of the other case to prevent bias. The AI-generated song was chosen after one round of generation.

In total, participants listened to four one-minute long songs, two AI-generated and two human-composed. At the start of the study, all participants completed a brief demographic questionnaire, which also gathered data about music training, music preference, and experience with generative AI. Next, they completed the State Anxiety Scale from the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI-S)
[
1
]
, and were then randomly assigned to one of three experimental groups: (1)
Unlabeled
music (Group
U
), where participants were asked to label which music they thought was AI-generated and which music they thought was human-composed; (2)
Correct
ly labeled AI-generated and human-composed music (Group
C
); and (3)
Incorrect
ly labeled AI-generated and human-composed music (Group
I
), where participants were told that AI-generated music was actually human-composed and vice versa (Fig.
2
-I). At the end of the full study, participants in Group
I
were informed that the music they listened to was incorrectly labeled and were asked to reflect. The STAI-S was specifically used due to its focus on participants’ self-reported anxiety at a particular moment in time and, therefore, was thought to be more sensitive to the impact of a short-term intervention such as this, though we note that the STAI-S data collected in this study were purely exploratory.

Within each music block, all participants listened to one song at a time (randomly assigned to either AI-generated or human-composed first) and, immediately following this, completed the Geneva Music-Induced Affect Checklist (GEMIAC), which assessed the emotional resonance of each of the pieces
[
2
]
. The GEMIAC survey builds upon the Geneva Music Emotion Scale (GEMS) by incorporating a broader range of genres and identified emotions, both positive and negative
[
2

. We elected to only use the intensity rating of the GEMIAC (Appendix

[
2
]
.

Once they listened to and completed the GEMIAC for both songs within the block, participants completed a comparison exercise. All participants reported which song they
preferred
and which song
more effectively conveyed the emotion case targeted in that music block
(referred to as “efficacy” in this work). Participants in Group
U

labeled which song they believed was human-composed and which they believed was AI-generated
, and provided detail regarding their reasoning. Participants in Groups
C
and
I
were asked to
reflect on the musical features they identified that influenced their choices
. Finally, participants completed the STAI-S again, and self-reported if any of the four songs changed their mood. This study was randomized and included repeated measures, using a mixed design to compare both between- and within-subject data. We embedded two attention checks into the Qualtrics survey, one after the first STAI-S measure and one randomly during the intervention task.

3.1
Data Analysis

3.1.1
Quantitative Analysis

To analyze participants’ music
preference
and the music that was the most
effective
in eliciting the target
Calm
or
Upbeat
emotional states across experimental groups, we initially fit generalized linear mixed-effects models (GLMM) to account for possible variation across musical condition. However, model diagnostics indicated a singular fit, with the variance of the random effect for condition estimated at or near zero. Given the absence of repeated measures and the limited number of levels in the grouping variable, we proceeded with Poisson generalized linear models (GLMs), which provided a more parsimonious and stable model for the count data, implemented via the lme4 and lmerTest packages in R
[
43
,
44
]
.

For
preference
, model comparison using Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) favored a model including main effects of experimental group, participants’ indicated musical preference, and emotion case, along with an interaction between preference and emotion case (
AIC=143.23
), over both a main-effects-only model (
AIC=152.04
) and a model including an interaction between experimental group and preference (
AIC=153.64
). The selected model provided good overall fit to the data (Residual Deviance = 29.31 on 22 degrees of freedom). Similarly, for
efficacy
, the best-fitting model included main effects of experimental group, efficacy, and musical condition, along with an interaction between efficacy and emotion case (
AIC=145.86
). A model including an interaction between experimental group and efficacy did not improve fit (
AIC=167.45
), suggesting that labeling condition had no meaningful effect on perceived efficacy.

We also used Poisson GLMs to compare the total counts for preference and efficacy across emotion cases and experimental groups, and to compare preference and efficacy ratings to each other. We used a multinomial logistic regression to examine how participants in the
Unlabeled
conditions’ labeling accuracy (reverse, correct, or unclear) varied as a function of musical emotion (
Calm
vs.
Upbeat
) and actual human or AI music origin (Table
3
). The labeling accuracy model included all two- and three-way interactions and was fit using a log link function. We also assessed whether labeling behavior aligned with musical preferences across six labeling categories: correctly labeled AI, correctly labeled human-composed music, incorrectly labeled AI as human, incorrectly labeled human as AI, and unclear responses (Table
4
). We conducted Fisher’s Exact Tests to determine whether labeling accuracy related to musical preference.

To assess the impact of all tested features on GEMIAC scores, we fit a linear mixed-effects (LME) model that included fixed effects for experimental group, emotion class, music origin (AI-generated vs. human-composed), and GEMIAC category, along with their interactions. The linear mixed-effects model included a random intercept for participants to account for individual differences in baseline emotional responses.

Given the factorial design and the inclusion of multiple predictors, we applied corrections for multiple comparisons (estimated marginal means with Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise contrasts) to our models to reduce the risk of Type I error. This approach is especially important given the moderate sample size, which may limit statistical power for detecting small effects and increase the risk of overinterpretation.

3.2
Qualitative Data Analysis

Free-text responses were analyzed across the three experimental groups, spanning participants’ preferences, efficacy ratings, and feature assessments. Responses were analyzed for thematic groupings using Braun and Clarke’s guidelines for thematic analysis
[
45
]
. We generated initial codes inductively, tagging meaningful segments of text with short descriptive labels. These codes ranged from surface-level observations (e.g., “mentions rhythm”, “describes vocals”) to latent meanings (e.g., “prefers human imperfection”, “distrusts AI”). We then sorted the codes into potential themes based on shared conceptual meaning. For example, codes such as “organic,” “real,” and “alive” were clustered under a broader theme of
Naturalness and Humanity
, while codes like “emotionally moving,” “soothing,” and “vibe” supported a theme of
Emotional Resonance
. In reviewing these themes, we examined internal coherence and ensured distinction between themes. Some themes were merged, refined, or relabeled to better reflect their content.

4.2
Unlabeled Group Labeling Accuracy

We conducted a multinomial logistic regression to examine whether emotion condition (
Calm
vs.
Upbeat
), music origin (Human vs. AI), and their interaction predicted labeling behavior: “Reverse” labeling (e.g., labeling AI as human or human as AI), “Correct” labeling (e.g., labeling AI as AI or human as human), and “Unclear” (e.g., labeling as "unclear" or "equal") (Table
3

Upbeat
condition than in the
Calm
condition (
\beta=2.15,SE=0.47,z=4.54,p<0.001,OR=8.63
). However, music origin and its interaction with emotion condition were not significant predictors of correct labeling (
p>0.58
), indicating that participants’ labeling accuracy did not differ substantially between human- and AI-composed pieces. This result serves as a useful sanity check: if participants were frequently mislabeling human-composed music as AI-generated, it follows that they would also misattribute AI-generated pieces as human-composed at a similar rate, yielding no significant overall difference in accuracy by origin.

For the “Unclear” response category, no predictors reached significance (
p>0.79

4.4.1
Impact of Demographics

We also assessed the impact of demographic factors on GEMIAC scores in order to determine whether differences in participants’ levels of prior experience would yield differences in emotional impact. A linear mixed-effects model examined how age, prior experience with AI-generated music, general music experience, and opinions on AI in the music industry influenced emotional responses to music. Random intercepts were included for participant ID to account for repeated measures across observations. Prior experience with generative music was a significant predictor: participants who reported excellent prior experience with generated music showed significantly higher overall GEMIAC scores (
\beta=1.58,SE=0.56,p=0.005
), while other levels of experience (e.g., average, good, poor) were not significantly different from the reference, which could serve as an indication of confirmation bias due to prior positive exposure
[
46
]
, though further investigations into this relationship are needed. This effect was moderated by age, though age on its own did not yield significant effect: for example, those aged 34–41 with “good” prior AI music experience reported significantly higher ratings (
\beta=1.963,SE=0.895,p=0.031
) than those aged 18–25 with no prior AI music experience. Additionally, participants who reported actively seeking out music for wellness reported higher emotional responses (
\beta=0.55,SE=0.24,p=.026
) relative to those who did not. No significant main effects were found for general music experience or for participants’ opinions about the role of AI in the music industry. Post-hoc comparisons using the emmeans package confirmed robust differences in emotion intensity ratings between GEMIAC categories across both emotion conditions and participant-level covariates, suggesting that these emotional responses are consistent regardless of prior musical experience or AI attitudes. Overall, the results indicate that both participant background and specific emotional targets substantially shape emotional impact, and that categories associated with calmness and connection drive the strongest responses. Random effects indicated modest variability across participants (
\sigma^{2}=0.24
), suggesting that individual differences contributed to emotional response patterns beyond fixed demographic predictors.

4.5.1
Thematic Trends by Emotion Case

In the
Calm

Unlabeled participants (

In the
Upbeat

4.5.2
Thematic Frequencies and Cues

Across the approximately 1,700 analyzed responses, we constructed eight main themes: “Naturalness and Humanity” (252 responses),“Perceptual Ambiguity or Uncertainty” (226 responses), “Emotional Resonance” (225 responses), “Mechanical or Synthetic Quality” (177 responses), “Technical Features” (176 responses), “Listener Agency or Subjectivity” (121 responses), “Genre or Context Association” (54 responses), “Creativity, Novelty, or Usefulness” (51 responses), and “Indifference or Detachment” (50 responses).

The most common reasoning theme was
Naturalness and Humanity
, which appeared most frequently in the
C
and
I

C

Upbeat

Perceptual Ambiguity or Uncertainty
was most common in the
I

Participants often admitted confusion with phrases such as “not sure,” “couldn’t tell,” and “hard to say.” As one participant explained, “I would not have known it was AI unless you told me—it felt like a guess.” Another noted, “I had to just choose based on a hunch.” In some cases, participants explicitly noted that the pieces blended qualities or lacked distinguishing features. Interestingly, in the

The AI piece [that was actually human] doesn’t have nearly as much emotion as it’s human counterpart [that was actually AI].”

Emotional Resonance
was also widespread, appearing across all experimental groups. Participants described how the music made them feel using affective language such as “calming,” “uplifting,” “enchanting,” or “joyful.” These responses sometimes stood alone (“I just liked how it made me feel”) or appeared alongside technical descriptors, indicating that emotional interpretation often coexisted with more analytical reasoning. One participant remarked, “It gave me a good, enchanting type of feeling,” while another noted, “It was slower and gave an overall calm feeling.”
Technical Features
, were particularly mentioned in the
U

The most frequently mentioned elements were “beat”, which centers around tempo and rhythm (e.g., “The beat felt natural and more human-made.”); melody (e.g., “I liked the melody more. It had a nicer flow.”); rhythm (e.g., “The rhythm helped build the emotion.”); tone (e.g., “The tone was softer and made it feel more organic.”); and tempo (e.g., “It had a slower tempo that helped me relax.”) Another participant mentioned that they were “Not sure about the composer, but the instrumental was well layered.” These specific elements often served as proxies for either emotional effect or perceived authorship, blending technical and affective reasoning. However, participants generally did not provide detailed insight into
how

Listener Agency or Subjectivity
captured responses that emphasized personal interpretation and taste. Participants frequently qualified their statements with phrases like “for me,” “I think,” or “depends on the person,” indicating awareness of subjectivity in musical evaluation. This theme underscores how participants situated their responses within personal frameworks rather than making universal claims. For instance, one participant said, “It just felt better to me—I know others might disagree,” signaling an openness to multiple interpretations.
Genre or Context Association
emerged when participants compared the music to other functional or cultural reference points. These included ads, video games, ambient background music, or specific genres. Such comparisons helped participants make inferences about authenticity or intention. For example, one participant wrote that the
Upbeat
AI-generated music, “sounded like it was made for an advertisement,” implying both polish and emotional neutrality. These associations often shaped perceptions of purpose, complexity, or emotional depth.
Creativity, Novelty, or Usefulness
captured a small but distinct group of responses in which participants positively evaluated the music as “interesting,” “novel,” or “useful.” These responses often reframed the AI–human distinction as less relevant than the generative potential of the technology. For instance, one participant described the AI-generated pieces as “a tool for creativity,” suggesting that even synthetic-sounding outputs could have artistic value depending on use context. Finally,
Indifference or Detachment
was expressed in participants that indicated that they “…felt the same” about all pieces, “…felt indifferent”, or “…didn’t care either way.” Such responses may reflect low emotional impact, confusion, or lack of familiarity with music analysis, and tended to appear across conditions.

Importantly, these findings
raise broader questions about the evaluative frameworks participants used when judging emotional effectiveness and musical preference
. Whether participants focused on humanness, authenticity, or technical features likely shaped how they interpreted the emotional impact of the music. If listeners were primarily attending to perceived humanness or authenticity, this suggests that social and relational dimensions of music, such as a sense of connection to a creator or recognition of intentionality, remain central to how music is emotionally processed and valued. Alternatively, if judgments were guided by technical or aesthetic qualities (e.g., melodic structure, production polish, or novelty), this indicates a more evaluative or formal approach to emotional engagement.
Understanding which features listeners emphasize not only helps explain variance in responses to AI- versus human-generated music but also has implications for the design and training of future generative systems.
Foundation models intended for music generation should account for the enduring importance of human imperfection, emotional expressivity, and cultural context, not simply technical fluency, if they are to resonate meaningfully with listeners. This insight is also critical for efforts to uplift and protect human musical creativity in an era where automated systems are increasingly capable of producing emotionally persuasive outputs.

5
Discussion

This study investigated how listeners emotionally respond to human- and AI-generated music under different labeling conditions. Using the Geneva Music-Induced Affect Checklist (GEMIAC), the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI-S), and a series of comparison tasks, we measured both aesthetic emotional responses and momentary emotion regulation across three experimental conditions:
Correct
ly labeled,
Incorrect
ly labeled, and
Unlabeled
music (human or AI), spanning both
Calm
and
Upbeat
emotion cases. While quantitative measures showed that participants were not uniformly better at identifying authorship, and that emotional responses, as measured by the GEMIAC, did not differ significantly by label, they did illustrate a disconnect between self-reported musical preference and emotional efficacy. Qualitative data revealed persistent associations between human compositions and certain musical and affective characteristics.

5.1
Perceptions of Humanness in Music

Participants commonly associated music labeled as human-composed with emotional nuance, uniqueness, and intentionality. Free response data emphasized qualities like “flow,” “realness,” “organic[ness],” “soul,” and “imperfection” as indicators of humanity, though we note that a general, standardized definition of what makes music “human” is hotly debated. These descriptors echo core ideas in music performance literature, where micro-expressive timing, interpretive variability, and dynamic shaping are seen as central to human musicality
[
47
,
48
,
49
,
50
]
. However, participants also tended to assign human characteristics to AI-generated music when they believed that the AI music was of human origin. This may reflect both the nature of the data used to train generative models and the effects of anthropomorphism, or the attribution of human characteristics to non-human agents
[
51
]
, which is an area of continued interest and debate in AI research. However, further study is required to fully assess the nuances of these interactions.

Discussions of inauthenticity or "fake" elements also shaped perceptions of humanity, including perceived “robotic[ism]” and artificiality. Perfection also signified a lack of humanity to some: listeners interpreted irregularities as hallmarks of human authorship as opposed to flaws. One participant remarked, “It felt like a real person was playing—there were tiny flaws that made it feel alive.” Such responses suggest that listeners intuitively link imperfection with expressiveness, a perspective aligned with theories of musical expressivity in both classical and jazz traditions. Similarly, participants who referenced “soul” or “emotion” as key features of humanness in music were drawing on deeply rooted aesthetic frameworks that value originality, distinctiveness, and personal voice in composition. These findings underscore the importance of preserving traces of human imperfection and nuance in the development of future foundation models for music. The persistent indication of the emotional resonance of human-composed pieces, even when participants were unaware of the source, suggests that listeners remain attuned to subtle expressive cues that may be difficult for generative systems to fully replicate. This not only highlights a critical direction for model training and evaluation, but also affirms the ongoing cultural and emotional significance of human-made music. As generative technologies become more capable, it becomes all the more vital to support and protect the creative labor and artistry of human musicians. However, specifications about what made or did not make our musical stimuli appear human generally did not extend beyond surface-level reflections, thus providing an interesting avenue for future study.

5.2
Preference, Efficacy, and Labeling Effects

We identified a complex interplay between musical preference and perceived efficacy since participants did not always prefer the music they found most emotionally effective, and vice versa. This divergence was especially apparent in the free response data, where several respondents described preferring a track for its perceived authenticity or “feel,” even when another track was rated as more effective for mood modulation. For instance, participants frequently preferred music they believed to be human-composed, regardless of whether or not it actually was. In the
Incorrect
ly labeled group, 36 participants indicated a preference for AI-generated music that they believed was human. In the
Unlabeled

Calm
group, a majority of respondents who selected the AI piece for preference (33 out of 35) assumed it was human-composed. These findings suggest that preference may be more deeply tied to perceived artistic origin and emotional authenticity than actual musical function, though further exploration is needed.

Labeling further influenced this dynamic. When labels aligned with expectations, participants expressed stronger preferences and clearer justifications. When labels were misleading, confusion increased, and participants often rationalized their responses by focusing on surface-level traits such as polish, instrumentation, or clarity. Importantly, this work raises questions about the validity of using preference as a primary metric in systems that rely on user feedback to refine AI music. If participants prefer a piece because they believe it is human, even when it is not, preference alone may not capture the functional success of music in therapeutic or mood-modulating settings.

5.3
Implications for Generative Music Systems

Our results suggest that listeners’ judgments of musical humanness are tied to subtle affective and compositional features, not merely audio quality or stylistic fidelity. For developers of generative systems, these findings imply the importance of incorporating:

•

Micro-expressive variation (e.g., rubato, dynamic shaping)

•

Idiosyncratic or “imperfect” phrasing

•

Structural ambiguity or unexpected turns

•

Framing cues or metadata to influence listener reception

However, further work is necessary to assess whether the emotional resonance of generative models might be better received if they better emulate the expressive irregularities and idiosyncratic voice found in human composition and performance.

5.4
Limitations and Future Directions

It is important to note that this study is limited by stimulus variety and sample size. While this work offers insights into listener interpretations of ambient and mood-based music, further research should explore genre-specific reactions (e.g., classical, jazz, or pop) and cultural variability. Beyond this, however, even within our target genres, there are countless potential genres, as well as prompts, that could elicit completely different sets of labels, preferences, and efficacies. Additionally, we found the lack of concrete discussion of auditory artifacts interesting, which we hypothesize is due to a nonspecialist audience. We plan to conduct future studies with expert audiences, in an effort to assess the impact of musical and AI/ML expertise on emotional resonance ratings and free response reflection.

It is also important to continue to explore the preference-efficacy dichotomy, and to gain deeper understanding of (1) how listeners determine “humanity” in music and (2) what impact this has on emotional outcomes and music preference. One possible way to do so could be through a more fine-grained musicological analysis comparing AI-generated and human-composed excerpts. This approach, however, may be limited by genre: comparisons of electronic and acoustic music, regardless of the point of origin, could lead to participant labels and determinations that are solely based upon stylistic characteristics. Controlling for this, in the event of systematic differences in, for instance, harmony, phrasing, or gesture, we would be better able to piece apart which musical features impact preference and effective emotional resonance. However, even if we do not identify significant differences between the two origin sources, it would still be useful to dig deeper into the depth of emotional impact, which we began to explore through quantitative comparisons of average GEMIAC scores for human and AI-generated music.

Additionally, although the STAI-S data in this study (Appendix
C
) was exploratory, it provides a foundation for future work examining how music generated by humans versus AI affects short-term anxiety and emotional regulation. Such studies could incorporate clinical populations or real-time physiological markers to evaluate the therapeutic impact of different musical origins and framing. This has particular relevance for the use of generative AI in wellness contexts, where trust, emotional resonance, and perceived authenticity may mediate efficacy.

Finally, integrating physiological data (e.g., heart rate, skin conductance) or longitudinal exposure could deepen our understanding of how listeners internalize and adapt to AI music over time.

5.5
Conclusion

In sum, participants’ judgments of humanness in music were shaped less by technical quality than by perceived emotional authenticity, uniqueness, perceived authorship, and expressive nuance. While there was overarching preference for AI-generated music, emotional efficacy was most often attributed to human-composed works. These findings challenge common metrics for generative music evaluation and highlight the need to distinguish aesthetic appreciation from functional outcomes. The exploratory STAI results further support the emotional potential of music, setting the stage for future research on trust, framing, and affect regulation in AI-supported wellness settings. By acknowledging and modeling these subtleties, AI-generated music may become not only more convincing, but more meaningfully expressive and therapeutically relevant.
