Trajectories and associated factors of life-space in older patients following percutaneous coronary intervention: a mixed-methods study
Authors: Yanyan Zhang, Bei Yang, Yin Wang, Kangyao Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOlder patients following percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) face long-term health risks such as in-stent restenosis. Life-space serves as a valuable clinical indicator for assessing and predicting the overall health of patients. Monitoring changes in life-space can facilitate early detection of health status changes. This study aimed to identify the trajectories and associated factors of life-space among older patients following PCI, thereby prompting timely intervention and improving cardiac rehabilitation outcomes.MethodsA total of 236 patients were enrolled. During hospitalization, patients’ general information was assessed. At 1, 3, and 6 months after discharge, the patients’ life-space was assessed firstly, followed by semi-structured interviews with voluntary participants via outpatient or telephone. Purposive sampling selected 10 patients from the low-to-medium trajectory groups for qualitative analysis based on information sufficiency. Latent class growth model was used to analyze life-space trajectories, and multinomial logistic regression analysis was conducted to examine their associated factors. Conventional content analysis method was used to provide additional possible factors hindering patients from achieving high-level life-space trajectory.ResultsThe life-space of older patients following PCI showed an upward trend within 6 months of discharge, with an initial rapid increase followed by a slower progression. Three trajectory subgroups of life-space were identified: low-level persistently restricted group (18.14%), medium-level gradually improving group (47.55%), and high-level unrestricted group (34.31%). Factors associated with trajectory membership included times of PCI, single stent implantation, outings due to family affairs, exercise endurance, depression, and self-efficacy. Additionally, qualitative analysis revealed 4 categories of factors associated with achieving a high-level life-space: physical symptom fluctuation factors, psychosocial adaptation factors, information-cognitive coordination factors, and external environmental interaction factors.ConclusionThere were three distinct heterogeneous trajectories, including one with persistently restricted life-space. Factors associated with life-space trajectories include physiological, psychosocial, disease cognition, and environmental factors. Targeted interventions such as symptom management, psychological counseling, health education, and supportive external environment may promote life-space expansion.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s41043-026-01447-y.

Introduction

Coronary heart disease (CHD) is characterized by a high incidence, disability rate, and mortality rate. As of 2021, the global number of heart failure cases caused by CHD has reached 19.16 million and continues to rise [1], imposing a heavy burden on healthcare systems. Furthermore, the occurrence of CHD shows an increasing trend with age. The Chinese Cardiovascular Disease Institute reported that its prevalence among individuals aged 60 and above was as high as 27.8‰ [2]. Percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) is typically recommended as the first-line treatment for CHD thanks to its minimal invasiveness and shorter hospital stays. In 2021, the total number of registered cases of coronary intervention in China was over 1.16 million, making it the country with the highest volume of coronary intervention performed globally [2]. Although PCI can significantly revascularize the coronary arteries of patients, it does not signify the end of the disease, as patients still face long-term health risks postoperatively. Previous studies had reported that the restenosis rate within 6 months following PCI remains at 5–15% [3] and the incidence of adverse cardiovascular events gradually increases over time [4]. The recurrence rate and long-term benefits are often less favorable in the older adults. Firstly, older patients commonly present with multimorbidity, including reduced physiological functions and frequent comorbidities such as hypertension [5]. More importantly, the occurrence and progression of adverse cardiovascular events in older patients are often atypical or even asymptomatic, making timely detection difficult [6]. Secondly, older patients often exhibit insufficient cognitive awareness and low self-management capabilities, frequently accompanied by significant psychological burdens and poor adherence to cardiac rehabilitation, which further diminishes the long-term efficacy of PCI [7]. Thus, the long-term rehabilitation management of older patients following PCI remains challenging. How to rapidly predict and identify postoperative health changes in these patients, thereby providing targets for intervention strategies, is a critical issue that requires current attention.

Life-space describes the spatial area of an individual’s daily activities, encompassing both the physical and social environments extending beyond the bedroom [8]. Unlike traditional measures such as the 6-minute walk test, life-space emphasizes a comprehensive understanding of the integrated physical, psychological, and social experience that emerges following mobility behavior, rather than merely the capacity for physical activity. In recent years, studies across a range of disease conditions have demonstrated that the patient experience reflected in life-space, is closely associated with disease progression [9], recurrence risk [10], and clinical treatment outcomes [11]. For instance, among older adults with low back pain, those without restricted life-space showed a 58% lower risk of progressing to severe disability compared to those with restrictions [12]. This may be attributable to greater pain sensitivity and attentional focus on pain among individuals with restricted life-space, which amplifies pain catastrophizing experiences, leading to further reductions in physical activity and sustained functional decline. A systematic review showed that approximately 42% of older individuals worldwide experience restricted life-space [13]. Matsuda et al. [14]further revealed that individuals with cardiovascular diseases or related risk factors are more likely to exhibit reduced life-space. Therefore, the life-space levels of older people with cardiovascular disease warrants particular attention. In the context of post-PCI recovery, most patients require a prolonged period to achieve hemodynamic stability [15]. In the context of post-PCI recovery, most patients require a prolonged period to achieve hemodynamic stability, and their physical functions, emotional states, and rehabilitation needs vary over time [16]. This makes it critical to dynamically assess the life-space level and its related factors in this population [17]. However, studies focusing on life-space in older people following PCI remain scarce, and the current evidence is predominantly derived from cross-sectional surveys [13]. For instance, Dan et al. [18] found that 51.8% of the patients were classified as life-space constriction, and identified social support, health literacy, and other influencing factors. Single-time-point assessments cannot capture the patterns of change in life-space, and the variables considered are insufficient, making it difficult to provide targeted intervention strategies.

Therefore, this study adopts an explanatory sequential mixed methods design, aiming to explore the trajectories and heterogeneous patterns of life-space in older patients following PCI, and to comprehensively analyze the factors associated with trajectory membership. The findings are expected to provide new insights for clinicians to accurately determine intervention timing and develop targeted plans, thereby improving the benefits of cardiac rehabilitation.

Methods

Study design

This study employed an explanatory sequential mixed methods design, concurrently collecting quantitative and qualitative data via outpatient or telephone follow-ups at 1 month after discharge (T1), 3 months after discharge (T2), and 6 months after discharge (T3). The heterogeneity of life-space trajectories was identified and the quantitative analysis was used to explore if the included independent variables were associated with life-space trajectories grouping. Then using qualitative analysis of patient follow-up texts to explain quantitative results, and to provide additional potentially contributing factors. Finally, the findings were integrated and interpreted in the discussion section to comprehensively understand the associated factors of life-space trajectories in older patients following PCI.

Study population

A convenience sampling method was utilized to recruit participants from the cardiovascular departments of three tertiary hospitals located in the southeastern coastal region of China, from March 2024 to January 2025. These hospitals are affiliated with the National Heart Disease Research Center and treat patients from across the country. The patient inclusion criteria were: (1) aged 60 to 79 years [19]; (2) underwent PCI with post-procedural thrombolysis in myocardial infarction flow grade 3; (3) normal cognitive and comprehension abilities; (4) provided informed consent. Exclusion criteria were: (1) severe organic diseases, such as heart failure, malignant tumors; (2) mobility-impairing conditions, such as visual impairments, hemiplegia.

The sample size for quantitative survey was determined using two approaches: based on the single-group repeated measures estimation Table [20], with three measurements per case, an assumed average correlation coefficient (ρ) of 0.50, an effect size (f) of 0.14 (low effect), α = 0.05, and a desired power of (1-β) = 0.80, a sample size of 165 participants was required. Additionally, as suggested by Muthén et al. [21], a minimum of 50 participants per latent class is recommended. With three trajectory groups commonly identified in comparable studies [22], the sample size should be no less than 150. After accounting for a 20% attrition rate, at least 206 participants were targeted. The qualitative interview was conducted concurrently with quantitative survey, and the sampling followed the information sufficiency principle, as detailed in the Data Collection section.

[24] examined factors influencing life-space among community-dwelling older people, suggesting that it provided systematic and scientific guidance for variable selection. Accordingly, this study adopted Webber’s Conical Model for variable screening based on the characteristics of older people following PCI. Qualitative instruments

The initial semi-structured interview outline was developed by the researchers based on the research objectives and literature review. It was subsequently revised by three experts in clinical nursing and qualitative research. The final interview content focused on the patients’ experiences regarding home-based and outdoor activities at T1, T2, and T3, and factors influencing their progression toward higher levels of daily mobility. (The finalized interview outline is provided in Supplementary Table S2).

Data collection

Quantitative data collection

After obtaining informed consent, the researcher collected candidate factors of life-space during the patient’ s hospitalization (T0), when their condition was stable and they were able to cooperate with the survey. Upon completion of data collection, the researcher immediately checked the questionnaires for completeness, clarifying and supplementing any omitted items or logical inconsistencies. At the end of T0 survey, the researcher recorded at least two contact methods for the patient, explained the schedule and importance of subsequent follow-ups, and tentatively scheduled the approximate time window for T1 follow-up. Subsequently, at T1, T2, and T3, the researcher assessed patients’ life-space levels via outpatient or telephone follow-ups.

Qualitative data collection

The researchers conducted qualitative interviews with each participant following their quantitative life-space assessment at three time points: T1, T2, and T3. Interviews were terminated if a participant withdrew consent at any point. Each interview focused on factors related to change or stability in participants’ life-space during the corresponding period. The researcher simultaneously wrote interview notes to record key information and points of uncertainty during the interviews, and facilitated in-depth responses through active listening, timely clarification, and probing. All interviews were audio-recorded to objectively record the raw data. After distinct life-space trajectories were identified, participants were first classified accordingly. Within each group, a purposive sampling strategy was employed based on interview duration, content richness, completeness, and relevance to the research topic. Using the high-level group as a reference, textual data were sampled from the medium- and low-level trajectory groups, and participants with the greatest diversity in characteristics (e.g., sex and number of surgeries) were selected for qualitative analysis. Sampling followed the information sufficiency principle, meaning that sample size should be based on research objectives, the complexity of research questions, data diversity, and the researcher’s capacity to interpret the data [33]. Data collection continued until core research questions were sufficiently addressed and the complexity of phenomenon was reflected [34]. After reaching sufficient information, this study also cross-checked the extracted codes against the remaining interview data from the sample, and ultimately no new codes were generated.

Data analysis

Quantitative data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS v26.0 and Mplus version 8.3, with a two-sided P-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Descriptive statistics were presented as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables and as means and standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables. Independent samples t-tests and chi-square tests compared characteristics between included participants and those lost to follow-up. Latent Class Growth Modeling (LCGM) was used to identify distinct trajectories of life-space. The optimal number of latent growth classes was selected based on the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and adjusted Bayesian information criterion (aBIC), with lower values indicating better model fit. The Lo-Mendell-Rubin likelihood Ratio Test (LMR) and the Bootstrap Likelihood Ratio Test (BLRT) were used to evaluate the classification quality of the latent classes; a significant result (P < 0.05) suggests that the k class model provides a better fit than the (k-1) class model. Entropy was used to assess the accuracy of class assignment, with values above 0.8 considered desirable [35]. Differences across the identified trajectory groups were compared using one-way ANOVA for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables. To explore potential predictors of different trajectory groups, variables showing significant differences in univariate analyzes were entered into a multinomial logistic regression model.

Qualitative data were analyzed using conventional content analysis [36]. The anonymised transcripts were imported into NVivo for data management. Firstly, two researchers repeatedly read the transcripts and listened to audio recordings to become thoroughly familiar with the data and to mark key text segments. Based on the research objective, open coding rules were jointly developed, including criteria for segmenting meaning units and protocols for handling ambiguous statements. Secondly, two transcripts were randomly selected and independently coded by the two researchers. They compared their coding results one by one and developed strategies to resolve coding discrepancies during the process, such as re-examining transcript context and inviting a third researcher with extensive qualitative research experience to participate in discussions [37]. Thirdly, the two researchers independently coded the remaining interview transcripts and held regular consensus meetings to resolve any discrepancies. Finally, by reviewing the codes and considering how they interrelated, the researchers grouped similar codes into subthemes, which were ultimately synthesized into themes.

Trustworthiness and rigor

This study followed the trustworthiness criteria proposed by Lincoln and Guba [38]. In terms of credibility, a preliminary analytical summary of each round of interview data was returned to the participants during the subsequent round of interviews for member checking. For dependability, pre-coding tests were conducted. Moreover, the coding results of two randomly selected transcripts were used to assess inter-coder reliability, and the calculated Cohen’s Kappa coefficient was 0.72, indicating good agreement [39]. For confirmability, participants’ original statements were cited as supporting evidence.

Results

Quantitative results

General characteristics of participants

Of the 236 older patients following PCI screened, 32 patients (13.6%) were excluded due to loss to follow-up or repeat revascularization, and full data were collected from 204 participants. Participants had an average age of 68.96 (SD = 5.81) years and were predominantly male (70.1%). Most patients underwent elective PCI (57.3%) and received a single stent (62.3%). Over 70% of participants reported the highest levels of accessibility and safety in their neighborhoods. There were no significant differences between the follow-up group (n = 204) and the lost to follow-up group (n = 32) in terms of baseline characteristics. (Details are shown in Supplementary Table S3).

Classification of life-space trajectories

As shown in Fig. 1, the total life-space score of the overall sample showed an upward trend, with most participants remaining in a restricted state within one month of discharge (Mean = 54.17, SD = 16.32). A total of six models were fitted successively (Details are shown in Supplementary Table S4). According to the fitting indicators, model parsimony and interpretability of results, three groups were ultimately selected. Based on slope and intercept, the three trajectories were labeled as follows: the low-level persistently restricted group (Class 1, n = 97, 47.55%) had the consistently lowest level of life-space, and its mean scores remained in the restricted range at all time points. In contrast, the medium-level gradually improving group (Class 2, n = 37, 18.14%) started with a restricted life-space score but gradually increased over time and successfully surpassed the threshold for the restricted state. The high-level unrestricted group (Class 3, n = 70, 34.31%) exhibited the highest life-space level, with its mean score staying unrestricted throughout the entire observation period.

Associated factors of trajectory subgroups

The results showed statistically significant differences among the three life-space trajectory groups in terms of variables such as age, education level, times of PCI, exercise endurance level, and PHQ-9 scores, as presented in Table 1. Using the three trajectory groups as the dependent variable (with Class 3 as the reference), variables with a P < 0.05 in the univariate analysis were entered into a multinomial logistic regression model. The variance inflation factor (VIF) values for independent variables ranged from 1.045 to 1.981, which were below the cut-off value of 10, demonstrating no multicollinearity [40]. Nagelkerke pseudo-R2 was 0.67, suggesting that the model exhibited good explanatory power. As shown in Table 2, compared to Class 3, patients with fewer than three PCIs and higher depression scores had a higher possibility of entering Class 2. Conversely, patients who received single stent implantedand those with higher self-efficacy scores were less likely to belong to Class 2 (P < 0.05). Compared to Class 3, patients with poor exercise endurance and higher depression scores had a higher possibility of entering Class 1. In contrast, those who went out due to family affairs and with higher self-efficacy scores were less likely to belong to Class 1 (P < 0.05).

Qualitative results

During the qualitative phase, 46 individuals declined to participate in interviews after completing the questionnaire, and 69 withdrew during the interview process, resulting in 103 patients who completed all three interviews. Following the identification of life-space trajectory classes, we found 52 patients belonged to “low- and medium-level trajectories”. Among them, 13 patients were excluded due to relatively brief interviews or a lack of richness and depth in the information they provided. Ultimately, 10 patients were included, comprising 7 males and 3 females, aged between 63 and 78 years. The characteristics of the included interview participants are presented in Supplementary Table S5, wherein 7 patients exhibited a persistently restricted life-space level after discharge. Through the analysis and synthesis of the follow-up transcripts, the associated factors that prevented patients’ life-space from progressing into the “high-level unrestricted group” were identified. These factors were categorized into 4 themes and 14 sub-themes, namely: physical symptom fluctuation factors, psychosocial adaptation factors, information-cognitive coordination factors, and external environmental interaction factors (as detailed in Table 3).

Discussion

This study found that, overall, the life-space score of older patients following PCI was 54.17 (SD = 16.32) points one month after discharge (T1), a limited level per the scale. Subsequently, the life-space score continued to rise to over 60 points at three months (T2) and six months (T3) of discharge, but the rate of increase from T2 to T3 was slower than that from T1 to T2. This trend is consistent with the cardiac rehabilitation process [41]. The first month of discharge represents the initial phase of outpatient cardiac rehabilitation, during which myocardial perfusion has often not yet been fully restored [42]. It aligns with the qualitative findings, which showed that patients still experienced distressing symptoms such as shortness of breath and chest tightness, as well as reduced cardiopulmonary function at T1, thereby restricting their mobility range and diminishing their independent activities [43]. Furthermore, the qualitative findings suggested that the effects of sleep disturbances and complications post-discharge may persist. Within three months of discharge, Charman et al. [16] reported that patients typically received ongoing cardiac rehabilitation team input, including exercise prescriptions modification, cognitive-behavioral interventions and lifestyle adjustments. These measures have increased patients’ physical activity levels to a certain extent, which may lay a foundation for remarkable improvements in life-space. Six months after discharge, patients had generally adapted to the new lifestyle and exercise habits, and the cardiac rehabilitation program entered the maintenance period, typically with few significant adjustments [44]. In addition, at this time, the life-space was also approaching the plateau period [45], and the growth rate naturally tended to plateau. These findings further suggest that the trend in life-space changes among older adults within six months following PCI are closely associated with their physiological characteristics and behavioral transition features at different cardiac rehabilitation phases.

This study identified three unique life-space trajectories through LCGM: low-level persistently restricted group (Class 1), medium-level gradually improving group (Class 2), and high-level unrestricted group (Class 3). 34% of the participants were found to belong to the high-level unrestricted group. Their life-space level was already unrestricted at discharge and remained high throughout the rehabilitation process. The medium-level gradually improving group accounted for the highest proportion (48%). At one month following PCI, their life-space level was significantly restricted, and they experienced a transition from a restricted to an unrestricted life-space at T2. However, they subsequently remained at a level slightly above the restriction cut-off, suggesting that a risk of restriction may still exist. During the entire observation period, the life-space level of low-level persistently restricted group was the lowest and consistently restricted, with the weakest growth trend. Therefore, clinical personnel should identify patients in Class 1 and Class 2 as early as possible, and employ Class 3 as a reference to analyze associated factors in order to develop targeted intervention measures.

Quantitative results showed that, compared to Class 3 (high-level unrestricted group), common factors associated with patients belonging to Class 1 and Class 2 (low- and medium-level trajectories) included high depression scores and low self-efficacy scores. Pérez-Trujillo et al. [46] developed a two-year risk prediction model for life-space confinement, in which psychological variables accounted for 21.9% of the explanatory power. The qualitative findings pointed to the potential underlying reasons. On the one hand, insufficient self-efficacy may be reflected in qualitative subthemes where participants exhibited “internal fear of disease recurrence” and “external risk avoidance behavior.” During interviews, participants expressed distress about disease relapse and uncertainty regarding sudden risks, which may have led them to adopt a more conservative approach to their life-space out of safety concerns. These findings align with the study by He et al. [47], which indicated that reduced self-efficacy leads to excessive anxiety regarding the risk of cardiovascular adverse events triggered by post-PCI activities, potentially resulting in active avoidance of going out. The qualitative subthemes also revealed the negative impact of “stress from peer bereavement”. Driggers et al. [48]. found that events such as the death of patients’ peers or relatives may negatively affect their sense of social well-being, thereby reducing their frequency of going out. The above findings suggest that for older patients following PCI, attention should be paid not only to their physical rehabilitation progress but also to their psychological well-being. On the other hand, the qualitative analysis showed that the psychological manifestations of patients in the Class 1 and Class 2 groups may not be entirely identical. For instance, regarding the subtheme of “lack of proactive motivation for outings” under self-efficacy perspective, data from Class 1 group suggested that participants tended to stay close to home because of concerns about burdening others. This characteristic may reflect a potential self-perceived burden among patients. Prolonged immersion in such emotions may foster depression and exacerbate social withdrawal [47]. In contrast, participants in Class 2 group tended to abandon long-distance travel due to a lack of interest in outdoor activities because theyconfined themselves to older people or patient roles. Therefore, healthcare professionals should not only assess patients’ mental health levels but also identify the sources of their psychological distress and develop intervention plans accordingly. For instance, for patients whose life-space is only slightly above the cut-off for restriction, strategies such as sharing successful cases and organizing peer exchange meetings may be effective approaches to inspire them to enjoy outdoor activities and proactively expand their activity boundaries.

Quantitative results showed that, compared to Class 3 (high-level unrestricted group), independent factors associated with patients belonging to Class 2 (medium-level gradually improving group) included PCI times and the number of stents implanted. In this study, patients with higher times of PCI were more likely to be categorized into Class 3 group. This may be related to their more frequent exposure to health education from medical staff. Qualitative analysis also reflected that participants in Class 2 group mentioned information–cognition coordination factors more frequently, particularly emphasizing that insufficient rehabilitation information led to difficulties in making decisions about going out. Patients with higher times of PCI may have received more education and access to health information from healthcare professionals, which reduced uncertainty about disease prognosis and enhanced their ability to control risk factors [49]. However, Kampka et al. [50] noted that as the PCI times increase, patients face a higher possibility of disease recurrence and progression. They may become more reluctant to engage in moderate exercise and social activities, thus hindering the expansion of their life-space. These findings suggest that future studies should further explore the correlation between these two variables. The number of implanted stents often reflects the complexity of the patient’ s vascular disease. For patients with multiple stent implantations, it usually takes several months for myocardial perfusion to return to its previous state, thereby delaying the process of regaining functional capacity [49].

Quantitative results show that, compared to Class 3 (high-level unrestricted group), independent factors associated with patients belonging to Class 1 (low-level persistently restricted group) included exercise endurance and family caregiving tasks. The instability of physiological functions, forcing individuals to prefer activity venues close to home or within walking distance [51]. In contrast, the qualitative analysis showed that participants in Class 2 mentioned symptom fluctuations at significantly fewer time points and with lower frequency than Class 1 (17 vs. 33). This is consistent with the finding that poor exercise endurance did not enter the regression model for Class 2 group. Nevertheless, participants in Class 2 group still mentioned the impact of physiological instability during interviews as physical function is the foundation of mobility. Therefore, it is recommended to implement continuous exercise training incorporating aerobic and resistance components for patients in both Class 1 and Class 2 groups [52]. Additionally, compared to Class 3, patients without household affairs necessitating outings had a higher possibility of entering Class 1. This aligns with the findings of Berg et al. [53]. Out-of-home household affairs, such as intergenerational caregiving and shopping for supplies, may encourage patients to maintain a relatively regular frequency of going out and to expand their daily activity radius. Qualitative analysis supplemented the influence of home-based household tasks on life-space changes. Participants reported that time spent on caring for sick relatives or doing housework may crowd out opportunities to go out. Integrating the quantitative and qualitative findings suggested that interventions should be tailored according to the type and intensity of family responsibilities undertaken by patients. For instance, for those primarily engaged in physically demanding household chores, their burden may be mitigated through family support systems and community care resources, thereby granting them opportunities for autonomous outdoor mobility.

In addition, qualitative findings provided insights not captured by quantitative results. Firstly, social support was not included in the regression model, but qualitative analysis revealed that patients commonly perceived multidimensional support from family members, peers and medical staff after discharge, and they expected more support to expand their activity range. However, some family members’ support was about advising patients to limit their activities due to concerns about the risk of recurrence. This coexistence of positive and negative effects, to some extent, explained the non-significant association between social support and the heterogeneous life-space trajectories. This finding contrasts with the study by Taylor et al. [52], which identified social support as a key protective factor for life-space, and such differences may be due to variations in the study populations. Taylor et al.’s research subjects were cancer patients who were physically weak but required frequent medical attention, and objectively required assistance and support from their families and healthcare providers. While patients following PCI required less objective physical assistance, and social support mainly influenced their willingness to travel. This suggests that healthcare professionals should also pay attention to the rationality of social support for older patients, rather than only focusing on their level. Secondly, qualitative analysis suggested that patients in Class 1 and Class 2 may be affected by the same factors at different time points. For instance, regarding the cognitive bias, patients in Class 2 mentioned higher time points and frequencies compared to those in Class 1. This difference may be related to the different causes of cognitive bias. Patients in Class 2 were mainly affected by online health information interference. Internet expansion broadened older adults’ knowledge channels, yet diverse, uneven information often caused misunderstanding of activity scope, duration and mode, reducing willingness to travel afar [54]. Moreover, they may be repeatedly exposed to inaccurate content because such information is readily accessible, which may correspondingly lead to more frequent reporting. In contrast, patients in Class 1, with higher age and lower education, had relatively limited internet usage, thereby avoiding interference from online information [55]. Their cognitive bias was mainly reflected in an erroneous perception of outdoor activities, which was usually corrected during the outpatient follow-up one month after discharge, so the factor would not be mentioned after that. This finding may provide a potential avenue for differentiating between patients following Class 1 or Class 2 and for implementing precisely targeted interventions.

Limitations

This study employed a mixed-methods design to explore life-space trajectories among older patients after PCI. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. The first limitation is related to the convenience sampling strategy, participants with higher health awareness and socioeconomic status may be overrepresented, as the sample were enrolled from easily accessible groups. This may lead to selection bias and limit the representativeness and generalizability of the findings. Secondly, cognitive function measures were used solely as exclusion criteria rather than as independent variables. Future research could incorporate these measures to fully understand the factors associated with life-space trajectories. Thirdly, the sample size for the qualitative component was determined based on informational adequacy rather than achieving full data saturation. Finally, the ORs obtained derived from logistic regression models in this study may overestimate the effect size. Future research could employ alternative analytical methods with higher statistical power, based on expanding sample size, to more comprehensively validate the results.

Conclusion

This study identified three distinct trajectories of life-space among older patients following PCI: low-level persistently restricted group, medium-level gradually improving group, and high-level unrestricted group. By integrating quantitative and qualitative analysis, it was found that factors associated with life-space trajectories include physiological factors, psychosocial factors, disease cognition status, and environmental factors. These findings highlight the need for dynamic assessment and multidimensional management of life-space in older patients following PCI. Interventions tailored to these factors such as psychological counseling and community integration programs may be beneficial in promoting patients’ life-space and overall quality of life.
