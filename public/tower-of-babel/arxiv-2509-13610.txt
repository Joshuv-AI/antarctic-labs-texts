Volatile-bearing mineral atmospheres of hot rocky exoplanets as probes of interior state and composition
Fabian L. Seidler, Paolo A. Sossi, Dan J. Bower, Brice-Olivier Demory

Abstract
Context. The atmospheres of hot rocky exoplanets (HREs) are a product of the conditions of thermochemical equilibrium with underlying magma oceans. It follows that the relative abundances of the prevailing species in such atmospheres, determined spectroscopically, can be harnessed to infer the pressure-temperature-composition ( P − T − X P-T-X ) of equilibration. Spectra collected by the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) indicate a potential CO/CO 2 -rich atmosphere on the HRE 55 Cancri e, a possible result of such interactions. Aims. Here, we aim to identify diagnostic atmospheric spectral features that can be used to infer the chemical composition, pressure and temperature of HREs, and to apply them to existing observations of 55 Cancri e and other objects. Methods. We construct a coupled atmosphere-interior model that computes the equilibrium gas speciation and stables condensates in the atmosphere in the system Si-Mg-Fe-O-C-H-S-N-He. The model accounts for both the equilibrium vaporisation of mineral gases (e.g., SiO, Fe) and the partitioning of volatile species (e.g., H 2 O, CH 4 ) between the magma ocean and atmosphere. Emission and transmission spectra and hence the (observable) radius of the planet are computed self-consistently. We apply the model to a fiducial, fully molten magma ocean planet with 8 Earth masses (mimicking 55 Cancri e) and explore a parameter space that spans volatile mass fractions ( V ​ M ​ F VMF , the sum of H, C, N, S and He) from 0.1 to 10 × \times that of the bulk silicate Earth (BSE), solar- ( z z = 0) to BSE-like metallicities ( z = 1 z=1 ), and oxygen fugacities ( f O 2 ) from -6 to +6 log 10 units relative to the iron-wüstite (IW) buffer. Results. We find f O 2 to be the major control of the shape of emission and transmission features. At low f O 2 ( < Δ <\Delta IW - 3) mineral gases, notably SiO, become important and, particularly at low V ​ M ​ F VMF , induce a thermal inversion, whereas high V ​ M ​ F VMF cases stabilise CH 4 (at mid- to high z z ) and SiH 4 (at low z z ). At weakly reducing- to neutral f O 2 (-3 < < Δ \Delta IW < < + 3), either He ± \pm H 2 (low z z ) or CO/CO 2 (high z z ) predominate, causing upper atmospheric cooling and weak- to moderate absorption features that scale with V ​ M ​ F VMF . At high f O 2 ( ∼ Δ ​ I ​ W \sim\Delta IW + 3), CO 2 in high z z atmospheres is joined by SO 2 , producing strong absorption at 9 μ \mu m. Condensation of silicates and iron oxides can also occur in the upper atmospheres of such planets. Inflated HREs, whose densities scaled to that of an Earth-like planet of the same mass ( ρ / ρ ⊕ \rho/\rho_{\oplus} ) fall below 0.5 are rare, but require either low z z at Earth-like V ​ M ​ F VMF (=1) or moderate z z at higher-than-terrestrial V ​ M ​ F VMF . The majority of HREs, including 55 Cancri e, have ρ / ρ ⊕ \rho/\rho_{\oplus} between 0.6 – 0.9, implying modest atmospheres of mixed heritage (0 < < z z < < 1), degenerate with f O 2 and V ​ M ​ F VMF . This degeneracy can be broken by spectral data, which, for 55 Cancri e, hint at either low- z z /neutral f O 2 or moderate- z z /low f O 2 . Future observations at wavelengths beyond 8 μ \mu m are key to discerning between these scenarios.



1
Introduction

Hot rocky exoplanets (HREs) denote a class of ultra-short period exoplanets that are similar in density to those of the terrestrial planets of the Solar System, implying an interior structure dominated by a rocky mantle and a core of iron-nickel alloy. Unlike the terrestrial planets, they have dayside temperatures (
T_{day}
) in excess of the melting point of silicates (
\gtrsim
1000 K).
They usually exceed Earth in mass and radius
(
Luque & Pallé 2022
)
, thus forming a subset of the Super-Earth population, and revolve on tight orbits around their host stars (few stellar radii,
d_{orb}\sim 2-10R_{\star}
). Consequently, also owing to the age of the stellar system in which they occur, these planets are likely to be tidally locked
(
Léger et al. 2011
)
, resulting in the day side likely showing extreme hot spots even in the presence of atmospheres
(
Hammond & Pierrehumbert 2017
;
Zhang & Showman 2017
)
.
They are also numerous; today, roughly 1000 candidates with substellar temperatures above 1000 K have been identified
(
Zilinskas et al. 2022
)
.

Though their densities are broadly consistent with an interior structure similar to that of Earth
(
Zeng et al. 2016
)
, the range permits planets with large metallic cores akin to Mercury
(
Adibekyan et al. 2021
)
, while others have lower uncompressed densities than Earth and thus must either harbour a smaller core
(
Elkins-Tanton & Seager 2008
)
or a (comparatively) thick atmosphere
(
Peng & Valencia 2024
)
; for some, only the latter option is consistent with their low density.

Therefore, and despite the likelihood for atmospheric escape owing to the high degree of stellar irradiation experience by HREs (e.g.
Salz et al. 2016
;
Cherubim et al. 2025
), volatile-rich atmospheres appear to persist.
Indeed, recent observations with the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) of the old
(
Bourrier et al. 2018
, 8.6
\pm
1 Gyr,)
HRE 55 Cancri e indicate the possible existence of an atmosphere
(
Hu et al. 2024
;
Patel et al. 2024
)
, which appears compatible with a CO/CO
2
composition
(
Hu et al. 2024
)
, supporting earlier claims based on data from the Spitzer Space Telescope
(
Demory et al. 2016a
;
Demory et al. 2016b
)
. Another potential candidate, TOI-431 b, also showed lower than expected emission flux at 15
\mu
m, consistent with heat-redistribution by an atmosphere
(
Monaghan et al. 2025
)
.

If present, the atmosphere may act as a thermal blanket for the interior, sustaining a deep, molten magma ocean over its lifetime
(
Nicholls et al. 2024
;
Nicholls et al. 2025
)
.
The high temperatures further facilitate short chemical reaction timescales
(
Arrhenius 1889
)
, implying that the chemistry of the atmosphere should adjust to the thermochemical conditions of the mantle, provided the mass of the atmosphere is small compared to the reservoir of chemically active melt (
Seidler et al. 2024
; otherwise, the mantle and atmosphere tend to a common equilibrium, see e.g.
Schlichting & Young 2022
).
Physically, this equilibrium manifests as the mutual solubility of volatile- and silicate liquid components at the magma-ocean-atmosphere interface (MAI). Volatile species that show particularly high solubilities in silicate liquids are water
(
Sossi et al. 2023
)
, S-bearing gases
(
Boulliung & Wood 2022
;
Boulliung & Wood 2023
)
and N
2
provided N
3-
is stable in the liquid
(
Libourel et al. 2003
;
Dasgupta et al. 2022
)
. As volatile-bearing gases dissolve in the magma, the atmospheric composition must change as a result
(
Sossi et al. 2020
;
Kite et al. 2020
;
Seo et al. 2024
)
.
In addition, evaporation of the underlying silicate melt releases gases such as SiO(g), Mg(g) or Fe(g), colloquially known as mineral gases. On dry (volatile-free) HREs, the mineral vapour may form an atmosphere on its own
(
Zilinskas et al. 2022
;
Seidler et al. 2024
, e.g.)
, but if volatiles are present, a mixed atmosphere emerges
(
Zilinskas et al. 2023
;
Charnoz et al. 2023
;
Piette et al. 2023
;
Van Buchem et al. 2025
)
.
Therefore, interactions at the MAI, which is likely not directly observable by telescopes, imposes the speciation and mass of the atmosphere at the interface, thereby permitting the nature of the underlying magma ocean to be inferred from observations of atmospheric spectra.

A handful of earlier studies have attempted to model mixed mineral-volatile atmospheres of HREs. Typically, they assume injection of pre-formed silicate vapour into a volatile background gas, closing the chemical network
a posteriori

(
Zilinskas et al. 2023
;
Piette et al. 2023
)
. This approach, though instructive, is not thermodynamically self-consistent because the
f
O
2
of the system after the addition of volatile elements (such as H and C) decreases, owing to consumption of O
2
in the initial mineral atmosphere by H
2
O and CO/CO
2
. Therefore, the final
f
O
2
will no longer correspond to the initial guess, which was used to determine the mass and composition of the mineral atmosphere. This is important because the equilibrium partial pressures of mineral gas species, such as SiO(g), depend on
f
O
2

(
Wolf et al. 2023
;
Seidler et al. 2024
)
.
As the mantle is a virtually infinite mass reservoir relative to the atmosphere, the conserved quantity for mineral species in the gas phase is therefore not their amount, but their chemical potential
\mu=\mu^{0}+RTlnf
and thus their fugacity,
f
at a given temperature (
\mu^{0}
is the chemical potential at the arbitrary reference state, typically 1 bar and the
T
of interest,
T
is the temperature and

As shown in
Seidler et al. 2024

a
of any melt component containing that element
1
1

1

e.g., SiO
2
(l) for SiO(g). However, the melt does not contain SiO
2
as species

4+

tetrahedrons that may or many not polymerize to form a network, with the metal ions (Mg
2+

2

and the oxygen fugacity:

f\propto a^{\alpha}(P,T,\mathbf{x}_{melt})\cdot f\text{O}_{2}^{\beta}

(1)

where
\alpha
and
\beta
are constants depending solely on the stoichiometry of the vaporisation reaction.

The importance of
f
O
2
is further demonstrated in pure volatile atmospheres, wherein the speciation shifts from reduced components such as CO and H

to oxidised CO
2
and H

(
Sossi et al. 2020

and the solubility of N

Boulliung & Wood 2023

(
Hu et al. 2024
;
Bello-Arufe et al. 2025
;
Nicholls et al. 2025
)
. However, the degree to which its intensity varies as a function of the partial pressures of other gases has yet to be explored. Moreover, depending on the extent to which mineral gases evaporate, the production of new species occurs, such as silane (SiH
4
) in atmospheres of solar-like composition under reducing conditions
(
Charnoz et al. 2023
)
.

The mantle redox state - as expressed by
f
O
2
- is expected to exert crucial controls on the atmospheric composition. Inversion thereof can be used to constrain information about the hidden interior.
Yet, a systematic and self-consistent treatment of how the presence of volatile elements interact with mineral species in the presence of a magma ocean have not been performed with self-consistent thermodynamics. Such models are crucial for identifying degeneracies in atmospheric retrievals of
f
O
2
, volatile mass fractions and metallicities of HREs, as well as diagnostic signals thereof.

Here, we present a coupled interior-atmosphere model that computes the key observable properties of volatile-bearing HREs of a given mass - their radius
R_{p}
, the emission and transmission spectra as function of the
f
O
2
at the magma ocean-atmosphere interface, volatile mass fraction (
VMF
), and the metallicity of volatile elements (
Z
).
In Section
2
, we introduce this model, and investigate the main results in Section
3
; there, we present a systematic investigation of the atmospheric mass and speciation as function of
f
O
2
,
VMF
and
Z
, as well as the structure and emission and transmission spectra of these atmospheres. We relate our models the observed properties of real HREs in
4
. Finally we conclude and summarise in Section
5

Methods

2.1
Model pipeline

The coupled atmosphere-interior model is composed of two main components,
a.)
the (independent) model for computing the speciation and mass of gaseous species and dissolved components at the MAI,

Atmodeller

2
2

2

https://github.com/ExPlanetology/atmodeller

(
Bower et al. 2025
)
, and
b.)
the (dependent) radiative transfer pipeline
phaethon
3
3

3

https://github.com/ExPlanetology/phaethon

(
Seidler et al. 2024
)
that computes the speciation and structure of the overlying atmosphere layer-by-layer based on the input boundary conditions at the MAI (step a.) and the top of the atmosphere.
In order to ensure consistency, steps a.) and b.) have to be brought into equilibrium, that is, the temperature and pressure at the MAI must match those imposed by the radiative transfer calculation for a given
T_{irr}
, for which an iterative computation is required.
The components of the pipeline are explained in more detail below.

2.1.1
Atmosphere-interior equilibrium model

The fundamental assumption of the atmosphere-interior model is that the melt and gas are at chemical equilibrium.
A precise statement of this condition is that the chemical potential (
\mu
) of any species (
i
) in the melt must equal that in the gas

\mu_{i,\text{melt}}=\mu_{i,\text{gas}}

(2)

enabling the partitioning of species between mantle and atmosphere to be derived by linking their fugacities (effective pressures) in the gas phase with their activities (effective concentrations) in the melt, performed by
Atmodeller
via the extended law of mass action
4
4

4

This law simultaneously enforces the law of mass action, which relates the free energies of compounds to their activities and fugacities and subsequently their mole fractions and partial pressures, and mass balance, which ensures mass is conserved.
.

We impose two sets of constraints on the system of equations
2
.
First, we ensure mass conservation for each volatile element H, He, C, N, and S and thus their combined total mass.
The individual masses are determined as described in Sec.
2.3
.

The second set of constraints are the fugacities of species supplied by the magma ocean. The mantle, composed of molten silicates, is the largest reservoir of O in the system, and can effectively act as infinite reserve thereof.
Hence, the masses of O
2
, the mineral gases or any gas containing oxygen, as well as the total atmospheric mass are not conserved. Instead, we impose the oxygen fugacity (
f
O
2
) as an independent variable, which is reported here relative to the iron-wüstite buffer IW,
\Delta
IW
:=f\text{O}_{2}(P,T)-IW(P,T)
, for which we use the formulation of
Hirschmann 2021
.
The fugacities of the mineral species, here
f
SiO,
f
Mg and
f
Fe (one per included element), are set by the melt composition,
f
O
2
and
T_{MAI}
, and were computed using a modified version of the
MAGMA
code
(
Fegley Jr & Cameron 1987
;
Schaefer & Fegley Jr 2004
;
Seidler et al. 2024
)
. The melt is assumed to be of identical composition to the bulk silicate Earth for all simulations
(
Palme & O’Neill 2013
)
.

To evaluate volatile partitioning between mantle and atmosphere, the radius of the planet at the interface (i.e.,
R_{p,MAI}
), the surface gravity (
g_{MAI}
) as well as the mantle melt mass fraction (
x_{melt}
) and the temperature at the MAI,
T_{MAI}
, are required (see
Bower et al. 2019
, their Eq. 2).

R_{p,MAI}
is computed from the mass of the planet,
M_{p}
, and its core-mass fraction CMF by interior structure models
(
Zeng et al. 2016
)
.
Unless mentioned otherwise, the mass is kept constant at
M_{p}=8M_{\oplus}
to mimic that of 55 Cancri e
(
Bourrier et al. 2018
)
.
To study Earth-like interiors only, we keep CMF=0.325 throughout. With these parameters we obtain
R_{p,MAI}=1.74R_{\oplus}
.
The melt mass is internally computed by
Atmodeller
from the mantle melt fraction
x_{\text{melt}}
and the total mantle mass,
M_{melt}=x_{melt}\cdot M_{p}(1-CMF)
. Because our model ignores any volatile species that can be incorporated into solids,
M_{\text{melt}}
defines the effective mass into which volatile elements may dissolve.
Throughout this study, we fix
x_{melt}=1
, i.e., fully molten.
Lower melt fractions are not expected to substantially modify the atmospheric composition
(
Bower et al. 2022
;
Maurice et al. 2024
)
, unless
x_{melt}\lesssim 0.3
.
The melt fraction of volatile rich HREs are expected to be
\gtrsim 0.75
, as the planets struggle to cool under the intense irradiation
(
Nicholls et al. 2024
)
.

The set of desired target gas species (including species of both mineral and volatile gases) is added to
Atmodeller
together with their respective solubility laws, if available
(
Bower et al. 2025
)
. The full list of gas species, including their solubility laws, can be found in Table
2

We assume an ideal equations of state for all gas species.

Atmodeller
solves for the mass distribution and partial pressures of all species given the aforementioned constraints.
Once all the partial pressures (and thus the total pressure), together with the dissolved quantities in the melt is known, the elemental fractions are passed on to the radiative model (see next Sec.
2.1.2
). If
Atmodeller
detects the formation of condensates (here, only graphite is considered due to the high temperature at the MAI), the condensate mass is removed and the atmospheric composition is calculated from the remaining gas mass.

2.1.2
Radiative transfer model

An HREs atmospheric spectrum is expected to reflect its composition and thermal structure, which depend on the outgassed composition at the MAI. This, in turn, is influenced by temperature and pressure set at the MAI.
In the equilibrium solution, the temperature at the base of the atmosphere has to match that of the melt.
This non-straightforward problem has to be solved iteratively, for which we use the radiative transfer model
phaethon
.
Its pipeline operates as follows:

1.

The energy budget of the planet is determined from the irradiation temperature
T_{irr}
, which can be related to the orbital distance
d
via:

d=\sqrt{\mathfrak{f}\cdot(1-A_{B})}R_{\star}\left(\frac{T_{\star}}{T_{\text{irr}}}\right)^{2}

(3)

where
A_{B}
is the bond albedo of the melt, which is assumed to be zero
(
Essack et al. 2020
)
and
\mathfrak{f}=2/3
is the dilution factor; neither choice is relevant as no observable quantity (e.g., P-T-structure, spectrum) depends on
d
.
From
d
and the stellar spectrum,
HELIOS
determines the wavelength-dependent energy input to the atmosphere from the stellar parameters, i.e. its radius
R_{\star}
and its spectrum, for which we use the Sun’s
(
Gueymard 2004
)
.
The internal temperature of the planet is set to 0 K (i.e., no internal heat sources).

2.

Initially, we set
T_{MAI}
to
T_{\text{irr}}
. Alternatively, an informed initial guess can be given to accelerate convergence.

3.

The atmospheric elemental abundances as well as the pressure at the MAI are computed by the atmosphere-interior model (Sec.
2.1.1
) using the current
T_{MAI}
.

4.

The elemental ratios are assumed to remain constant throughout the atmospheric column (i.e., infinitely well mixed). Look-up tables for the gas speciation as function of pressure and temperature are computed by
FastChem COND

(
Kitzmann et al. 2024
)
. The grid ranges from the pressure at the MAI (variable) to the top of the atmosphere (
10^{-8}
bar, constant for all models). Equilibrium condensation is allowed. If condensates form, they are removed from the gas and do not influence the radiative transfer computation. All relevant reactions are listed in Table
3
.

5.

HELIOS

(
Malik et al. 2017
;
Malik et al. 2019
)
solves the equations of radiative transfer using the opacities of the individual gas species for an atmosphere confined between the top-of-the-atmosphere (TOA, fixed at
10^{-8}
bar) and bottom-of-the-atmosphere (MAI) pressures given the energy input based on the energy budget of the planet (step 1), and derives the P-T-structure of the atmosphere, including a new value for
T_{MAI}
. Convective adjustment is enabled with an adiabatic exponent for diatomic gases,
\kappa=2/7
(from the adiabatic equation of an ideal gas, see
Malik et al. 2019

\mathchoice{\frac{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mu\ln T}{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mu\ln P}}{\displaystyle{\frac{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mu\ln T}{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mu\ln P}}}{\scriptstyle{\frac{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mu\ln T}{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mu\ln P}}}{\scriptstyle{\frac{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mu\ln T}{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mu\ln P}}}=\kappa:=-\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}

(4)

where

, using the mixing ratios of each layer inferred from the

6.

If the new

is within 35 K of the prior guess, the model is taken to have converged, though many cases reach a temperature convergence of better than 2 K. Otherwise, a new

is estimated via root-finding (see Appendix

7.

Once converged,
phaethon

phaethon

B.2

8.

Transmission spectra were computed from the P-T-structure and chemistry with

(
Mollière et al. 2019

2.3
Volatile budget

2.3.1
Total volatile mass

To determine the total amount of H-He-C-N-S volatiles in the planet, we first define the volatile mass fraction (VMF) as the mass of volatiles
M_{V}
relative to the mass of the entire planet,
M_{P}
:

VMF:=\frac{M_{V}}{M_{P}}

(5)

For the Earth, we find
VMF_{\oplus}=0.00028485

(
Palme & O’Neill 2013
)
. We further define the volatile mass factor of any model planet by:

f_{VMF}:=\frac{VMF}{VMF_{\oplus}};

(6)

this equation represents the volatile enrichment of a planet of any mass,
M_{P}
, compared to an Earth scaled up to
M_{P}
, i.e.
f_{VMF}=1
if the planet has the same mass fraction of volatile elements as Earth (but these are not necessarily present in the same relative abundances - see section
2.3.2
, below). We caution that
f_{VMF}
is not identical to the atmospheric mass fraction since oxygen is not included in this calculation. Oxygen is a special case as it is instead set by a combination of
VMF
,
Z
and the
f
O
2
of the mantle (for the same reason, we cannot use the atmospheric mass fraction as free parameter).
Because
f_{VMF}
may vary by orders of magnitude, we will work with its logarithm,
\log f_{VMF}
, instead. By definition,
\log f_{VMF}
=0 corresponds to a planet with an identical fraction of volatiles as Earth,
\log f_{VMF}
=1 to ten times as much and
\log f_{VMF}
=-1 to a tenth of Earth’s volatile mass.

3.2
Thermal structure and opacity

3.2.1
Phenomenology

In Fig.
3

Many of the dominant gas species, namely CO

(
Ledley et al. 1999
)
, but cooling in the upper layers due to efficient emission in the same wavelength bands
(

Thus, most of the simulated atmospheres show a notable greenhouse effect up to 500 K in conjunction with an upper atmosphere (

, C2, D2).
The major gas species maintain a roughly constant mixing ratio throughout the atmospheric column, in spite of changing temperatures (
\Delta T\sim 1000
K) as discussed above.
Only when the atmosphere shows a thermal inversion (i.e., when the upper layers are hotter than lower layers), is the speciation of the major gases tangibly impacted; this occurs mainly by dissociation into atoms or ions, which predominantly affects H
2
O (Fig.
3
, A3, B3).
Therefore, an inversion can weaken the radiative activity of the atmosphere, Fig.
3

-3

Mainly responsible for the formation of an inversion is SiO(g), a strong UVIS absorber (see Fig.
14
); other (atomic) metal species that have abundant lines in the visible and the UV, namely Mg, Fe and Si, contribute as well
(e.g.
Zilinskas et al. 2022
;
Zilinskas et al. 2023
;
Seidler et al. 2024
, also see Fig.

These gases are prevalent in atmospheres of low

O
2
and high temperatures due to enhanced silicate vaporisation
(
Seidler et al. 2024

, particularly when

is (comparatively) low to prevent the major volatile species (CO, H

Indeed, the UV range (

\mu

3

The VIBSE cases exhibit clear peaks in optical depth, owing to the presence of CO(g) in the reducing case (4.5

The cooler upper atmospheres of the BSE cases leads to a positive feedback loop, wherein the mineral gases condense (see Sec.

Below the photosphere (indicated by the white bands in Fig.

4.2.3
Future observations

In Fig.
12
, we show five distinct scenarios for the atmosphere of 55 Cancri e, together with predicted measurements using the MIRI medium resolution spectroscopy (MRS) mode.
In contrast to the low-resolution (LRS) mode used by
Hu et al. 2024
, the detector does not saturate despite of the star’s brightness and can thus retrieve more robust results.
The MRS mode offers 4 different channels with 3 subbands (SHORT, MEDIUM, and LONG) each.
To capture important features (CO
2
at 15
\mu
m, SO
2
and SiO at 9
\mu
m), the medium channel is required.
It becomes apparent that the predicted observations - when the spectrum in each channel is binned over the whole bandpass - is sufficient to distinguish between the endmember scenarios of gas dwarfs with primordial atmospheres (
\log f_{VMF}
=1,
\Delta
IW-6,
z=0
), steam planets (
\log f_{VMF}
=1,
\Delta
IW0,
z=0
), VIBSE-like planets and their redox states(
\log f_{VMF}
=0,
\Delta
IW-3 (reduced) /
\Delta
IW+3 (oxidised),
z=1
) and mineral gas enriched atmospheres (
\log f_{VMF}
=-1,
\Delta
IW-6,
z=0
).
A future observation of 55 Cancri e was approved in Cylce 4 GO, proposal 7875,
Zhang et al. 2025
).
However, it must be noted that a significant degree of information hinges on the total flux, and any inference will be degenerate with the size of the condensed interior (Sec.
4.1
) and the temperature/heat redistribution of the atmosphere
(
Zilinskas et al. 2025
)
.
This information can be constrained by obtaining phase curves (which constrain the heat redistribution) and the primary transit, which determines the size of the atmosphere (Sec.

).

4.3
Limitations and improvements

1.

Incomplete or missing line lists represent a major limitation of this study. Some species that we found to potentially occur in appreciable abundances in HRE atmospheres, yet lack short-wave opacities, are acetylene (C
2
H
2
) and methane (CH
4
). Both gases occur exclusively under highly reducing conditions (
\Delta
IW
\lesssim
-3), with the latter emerging only in cooler atmospheres (
T_{\text{irr}}

\lesssim
1500 K). Acetylene may, however, become a dominant absorber in heavy atmospheres (
\log f_{VMF}

\geq
1), but opacity data below 1
\mu
m are missing (Fig.
14
), meaning its inclusion would result in a spurious greenhouse effect (see Appendix
H
for more details). Complete opacities of important species in heavy, reduced atmospheres is required.
Additionally, S
2
, achieving mixing ratios of up to
10^{-2}
in oxidised and sulphur-rich atmospheres (see Fig.
3
, C3), has no available line lists; the implications of its absence are therefore challenging to estimate. However, it is usually superseded in abundance by the strong absorbers CO
2
and SO
2
, and thus its influence may be only of secondary order.
Many species are only available for lower temperatures on DACE, most importantly SO
2
which only extends up to 1900 K, and H
2
O only up to 2900 K.
To eliminate these biases, the available line lists have to be extended via experimental and theoretical means, and evaluated over the whole pressure-temperature range of HRE atmospheres.

2.

The solubility laws used in this work are described according to Henry’s or Sieverts’ law approximations, which may break down when the concentration of the solute increases (see Appendix
A
) and pressures, temperatures and
f
O
2
diverge from those at which the laws were calibrated (see Table
2
or
Bower et al. 2025
, their Table 1). This is particularly relevant for the hotter HREs, for which surface temperatures can approach 3000 K. By contrast, experimental constraints are typically half that temperature; only H
2
O was measured to applicable temperatures (
\sim
2173 K,
Sossi et al. 2023
). Further, the Henry’s law constants for most species were obtained for basaltic compositions of the melt, which, though reasonable approximations, are unlikely to hold for magma oceans on exoplanets. This may pose problems for the solubility of S
2
, which is a complex function of melt FeO content
(
O’Neill & Mavrogenes 2002
;
Boulliung & Wood 2023
)
, while the solubilities of gases that dissolve by physical incorporation (H
2
, N
2
, the noble gases) depend strongly on melt composition
(
Carroll & Draper 1994
)
. Only H
2
O solubility laws are available for an Earth’s mantle-like composition
(
Sossi et al. 2023
)
. Furthermore, all solubility laws are derived for either pure gases or simple mixtures thereof, and thus the true solubilities may deviate from those predicted (see Appendix
A
for more detail). More experimental measurements have to be obtained.

3.

All the radiative transfer codes used here (
HELIOS
and
petitRADTRANS
) as well as
FastChem
assume an ideal gas equation of state, which is violated by heavy atmospheres (
\gtrsim
1000 bar). This issue is fundamental and goes beyond the scope of this study (cf. Sec.
2.1.2
), but only affects the layers below the photosphere (
P_{photo}\lesssim
1 bar in all cases).
Likely implications are
i.)
miscalculation of atmospheric elemental ratios (e.g., C/O),
ii.)
underestimation of dissolved content (since fugacity coefficients for major volatiles tend to exceed 1 at high pressures),
iii.)
differences in speciation in the atmospheric column of the lower atmosphere (as computed by
FastChem
), and
iv.)
an underestimation of the planets radius, as non-ideal species of the major volatiles are less compressible than ideal gases
(
Holland & Powell 1991
;
Shi & Saxena 1992
;
Bower et al. 2025
)
. Issues
i
and
ii
can in principle be corrected for by passing a non-ideal equation of state to
Atmodeller
. Issues
iii
and
iv
cannot be mended without major adaptions to the other underlying codes.

4.

While we included the effect of condensation on the removal of a gas phase, we did not include the formation of clouds or hazes. Within the model presented here, this is a valid approach: we consider only static, time-independent 1D atmospheric models at full equilibrium, at which high instellation promotes convective stability. Therefore, any condensates should settle, rendering the final equilibrium condensate-free but with an upper atmosphere depleted in the condensible species. It should be noted that this neglects diffusion, which, based on the Chapman-Enskog formalism, scales by
T^{3/2}/P
and could thus deliver silicate species into the upper atmospheric layers where condensation occurs. In a realistic HRE atmosphere with their strong thermal gradient
(
Hammond & Pierrehumbert 2017
)
, condensation may occur as gas is advected to the nightside
(
Nguyen et al. 2024
)
. More detailed studies on the influence of P-T-structure and condensation in both 1D and 2D/3D models are required
(
Hammond & Pierrehumbert 2017
;
Zhang & Showman 2017
, cf.)
.

5.

The heat redistribution factor
\mathfrak{f}
, despite being assumed to equal 2/3, is subject to change. It exerts a strong influence on the secondary eclipse spectra, with cooler temperatures lowering the emission flux by
\sim
20-50 ppm when changing the irradiation temperature from 2500 K to 2000 K (dayside average and equilibrium temperature of 55 Cnc e, respectively).
This difference is crucial to consider when fitting the observed spectra, as they result in different valid scenarios
(
Zilinskas et al. 2025
)
.
To combat degeneracies (as well as variability between visits, as seen in 55 Cancri e,
Patel et al. 2024
), multi-wavelength full phase curve observations are required.

5
Summary

1.

Despite varying bulk chemistry, volatile-rich mineral gas atmospheres are generally dominated by He, CO, CO
2
, H
2
, and H
2
O. The
f
O
2
controls the ratios of CO/CO
2
and H
2
/H
2
O, where the former are favoured in more reducing and the latter in oxidising atmospheres. The
p
SO
2
reaches a maximum near
\Delta
IW+3, owing to its dissolution as S
2-
at lower
f
O
2
and SO
{}_{4}^{2-}
at higher
f
O
2
rendering it the dominant gas in oxidising, high-
z
atmospheres. H
2
O is never the prevailing gas due to its high solubility, while He, when present, is present almost exclusively in the atmosphere, as are CO and CO
2
. Nitrogen is also insoluble (as N
2
), except at low
f
O
2
at which N
3-
is stable in the melt. Mineral gases, such as SiO, Mg, MgO and Fe evaporate preferentially in highly reduced and hot atmospheres. Cooler, higher
\log f_{VMF}
and reducing atmospheres (
T<2000
K) are more prone to form methane instead of CO, with the occurrence of CH
4
, C
2
H
2
, HCN. In reduced atmospheres at the highest temperatures and
\log f_{VMF}
SiH
4
becomes abundant, but the ideal gas approximation is no longer valid in such cases.

2.

The atmospheric structures of HREs are typified by a strong greenhouse effect in the lower atmosphere, induced by CO, CO
2
, H
2
O and SO
2
, and thermal inversions in the upper atmosphere, caused mainly by SiO.
The former is nearly universally present but exacerbated in oxidised atmospheres (
\Delta
IW
\gtrsim 0
). In these cases, the surface of the planet is up to 800 K hotter than its irradiation temperature, allowing for magma oceans on planets with irradiation temperatures as low as 1000 K.
On the other hand, the upper atmosphere thermal inversion appears in atmospheres whose opacity structure is dominated by mineral gases (i.e., SiO).

3.

Emission spectra are mostly sensitive to oxygen fugacity (
f
O
2
), metallicity (
z
) and temperature, and to a lower degree to the volatile content (
\log f_{VMF}
). Key molecules with strong absorption features include SO
2
, CO
2
and H
2
O, and when the atmosphere is inverted, CO and SiO appear as emission features. SO
2
is the most robust tracer of
f
O
2
in the range
\Delta
IW +3
\pm
3, except at
z\sim 0
, where the feature appears weak due to low total sulfur abundances. CO
2
and H
2
O are present across a wide range of conditions, making them poor stand-alone redox indicators, but allow for constraints on metallicity (
z
). SiO emission at 9
\mu
m identifies hot, highly reducing, low-metallicity atmospheres. Cooler reduced atmospheres, provided they have high
\log f_{VMF}
, show CH
4
or HCN emission instead, which may be replaced by C
2
H
2
features in a more realistic setting. The 4.5
\mu
m region (JWST NIRCam) is spectrally degenerate in CO
2
, CO, SO
2
and SiO, while JWSTs MIRI instrument can distinguish between redox regimes and temperatures by targeting distinct CO
2
, SO
2
, and SiO features in the 7–20
\mu
m band.

4.

Transmission spectra show H
2
O, CO
2
, and SO
2
features in the mid-infrared to the same effect as emission spectra, but their high mean-molecular weights compress the atmosphere, limiting detectability. H
2
O bands between 1-2
\mu
m become tracers of atmospheric mass (
\log f_{VMF}
) for oxidised low-
z
atmospheres, while planets with
z\sim 0
show strong mid-IR SiO and CO features. The UVIS to NIR (300–1000 nm) shows a minimum in transit depth but hosts lines from SO, OH, Fe, Mg, MgO, SiO, and H
2
O that vary with
f
O
2
, offering a promising window for ground-based detection of magma oceans, and retrievals of the
f
O
2
.

5.

In the cooler upper layers, condensation of silicates, oxides and carbides might occur. For ultra-high irradiation temperatures (
\geq 2500
K), the condensation front is pushed to very high altitude (or is even non-existent), while in colder (
T_{\text{irr}}

\leq 2000
K) or more reduced (
\Delta
IW
<
-3) it can occur in lower atmospheric layers.

6.

While the majority of mass-radius relationships of the hottest HREs are consistent with atmospheres with a limited scale height (favoured at high
z
and low
VMF
), some, such as 55 Cancri e and TOI-1408 c, despite
T_{irr}\sim 2500
K, have densities that are too low to be explicable by a bulk silicate Earth-like volatile budget (i.e.,
z
= 1 and log
VMF
= 0). Hence, these HREs are consistent with i) low
z
(solar-like) atmospheres at similar scaled volatile mass fractions (
VMF
) to the BSE for 55 Cancri e, or ii) in the case of TOI-1408 c, have
both
low
z
and high
VMF
to explain their extended atmospheres. We find that mass-radius relations are degenerate with respect to
z
and
\log f_{VMF}
, which can only be resolved by spectroscopic means.

7.

The MIRI spectra of 55 Cancri e obtained by
Hu et al. 2024
preclude oxidised VIBSE-scenarios at any
\log f_{VMF}
and highly reduced (
\Delta
IW-6) SOLAR cases at
\log f_{VMF}

\geq 1
, indicating that the planet is unlikely to be a heavier Earth-twin nor hosts a captured nebular atmosphere. Intermediate scenarios remain possible. This conclusion aligns with the constraints from the mass-radius data at shorter wavelength. The NIRCam observations by
Hu et al. 2024
and
Patel et al. 2024
do not paint a consistent picture and remain in conflict among themselves or the constraints from MIRI and mass-radius, but generally prefer reduced atmospheres of low
z
.

Appendix B
More details on
phaethon

B.1
root finding

As seen in Sec.
2.1
, the melt temperature (
T_{\mathrm{melt}}
) sets the atmospheric composition, which in turn affects the P–T profile and thus
T_{\mathrm{BOA}}
, which must equal
T_{\mathrm{melt}}
in equilibrium. This circular dependency is resolved by finding the root of the residual
\Delta T=T_{\mathrm{BOA}}-T_{\mathrm{melt}}
via iterative P–T profile computations. Because radiative transfer calculations are computationally expensive, minimizing the number of iterations is essential.

We find heuristically that
\Delta T
, to first order, behaves approximately linearly with
T_{\mathrm{melt}}
, which we exploit in the root-finding algorithm. During each iteration
n
,
phaethon
records the pair
(T_{\mathrm{melt},n},\Delta T_{n})
, where
\Delta T_{n}:=T_{\mathrm{BOA},n}-T_{\mathrm{melt},n}
. The initial guess
T_{\mathrm{melt},1}
is typically the planets average dayside temperature (or a user-defined value, if desired). The second estimate is set to
T_{\mathrm{melt},2}=T_{\mathrm{BOA},1}
.

In subsequent iterations, if a sign change in
\Delta T_{n}
is detected (relative to any previous
\Delta T_{k}
,
k<n
), the next
T_{\mathrm{melt},n+1}
is computed by finding the root of a linear interpolation between
(T_{\mathrm{melt},n},\Delta T_{n})
and
(T_{\mathrm{melt},m^{\ast}},\Delta T_{m^{\ast}})
, where
m^{\ast}
is defined such that
T_{\mathrm{melt},m^{\ast}}
is closest to
T_{\mathrm{melt},n}
, i.e.
m^{\ast}=\underset{m\neq n}{\arg\min}\left|T_{\mathrm{melt},n}-T_{\mathrm{melt},m}\right|
and
\operatorname{sgn}(\Delta T_{n})=-\operatorname{sgn}(\Delta T_{m^{\ast}})
.
If no sign change occurs, the algorithm proceeds with a secant-like method by linearly extrapolation the last two residuals,
(T_{\mathrm{melt},n-1},\Delta T_{n-1})
and
(T_{\mathrm{melt},n},\Delta T_{n})
, and defining the root of this extrapolation as
T_{\mathrm{melt},n+1}
.
If the algorithm detects cycling or fails to converge within a predefined number of iterations, it switches to Bayesian optimization
(
Nogueira 2014–
)
to escape the local region, and resumes the linear search once a new viable estimate is found.
Convergence is reached when
|\Delta T_{n}|\leq\Delta T_{\mathrm{tol}}
, typically within 3–5 iterations. Final residuals are often below 10 K, and in many cases below 5 K.

B.2
Wavelength dependent radius

The occultation depth is written as

\frac{F_{p}}{F_{\star}}=\left(\frac{R_{p}}{R_{\star}}\right)^{2}\frac{\varepsilon_{p}(\lambda)}{\varepsilon_{\star}(\lambda)}

(14)

where
F
is the flux (
J/m^{2}
) of the respective body emitted at its surface,
R
its radius and
\varepsilon
the emissivity, in
J/m^{-3}
. Most often, the assumption of
R_{p}=\text{const}
is made, which is valid under the assumption that the atmosphere is significantly smaller than the planet itself. However, in many models shown in this study, we find that the atmospheric extension presents itself as a significant portion of the planets size, especially in the hydrogen-rich and reduced cases (see Fig.
7
). Further, as we see in the opacity structure (Fig.
3
), the photosphere changes its height with wavelength; for extended atmospheres, this effect can be drastic.
As a result, the planet will appear bigger in wavebands where the photosphere is higher up, and smaller in cases were it lies at depth. This will have an effect on the spectral features based on Eq.
14
. To account for this, we directly find
F_{p}(\lambda)
by integrating the contribution function times the altitude in each wavelength:

F_{p}(\lambda)=4\pi\int_{0}^{z_{max}}c(z,\lambda)\cdot(R_{c}+x)^{2}dz

(15)

where
z
is the altitude above the surface (
z=0
corresponds to the radius of the condensed parts)
c(z,\lambda)
is the contribution function in
J/m^{2}
per wavelength (total units
J/m^{3}
), and
z_{max}
is the maximal vertical extend of the atmosphere (here, the height of the top layer, which always corresponds to
P_{TOA}=10^{-8}
bar).
Note that the altitude obtained with HELIOS depends on the local gravitational acceleration, which is held constant at the surface value and thus may lead to more compressed atmospheres, further underestimating its true radius.
The effect of using a fixed planetary radius is shown in Fig.
13
.
Generally, only atmospheres with low
z
are affected, as the expansion effect is strongly correlated to the mean molecular weight (see Sec.
3.3.2
).

Appendix D
Collision induced absorption (CIA)

Here, we assess the effect of collision-induced absorption (CIA) relative to the opacities of dominant molecular species. The available CIA pairs relevant for the high-temperature conditions in HRE atmospheres are H
2
–H
2
, H
2
–H, and H
2
–He.
The collision line-lists of other dominant gases, e.g. CO
2
-CO
2
, have no experimental or theoretical support in the temperature range of interest.
CIA scales primarily with pressure, in contrast to the opacities of triatomic molecules such as H
2
O, CO
2
, and SO
2
that show stronger response to temperature.

As shown in Sec.
3.2
, the photospheres are located at low pressure (typically in the
\sim 10^{-1}
–
10^{-6}
bar range) for any combination of
z
,
\log f_{VMF}
, and
f
O
2
. These altitudes are well above the pressure levels (
\sim 1-100
bar) at which CIA opacities become comparable to molecular opacities. At such high pressures, HRE atmospheres can reach temperatures of
\sim 3000
K, for which even the high-temperature CIA line lists are currently unavailable. As a result, our model cannot self-consistently include CIA effects under these conditions. However, given the relatively grey nature of CIA opacity in the infrared, we expect their primary effect to be a general greying of the spectrum in the wavelength range where most energy transport occurs (i.e., at infrared wavelengths corresponding to
T\sim 3000
K). Consequently, CIAs are unlikely to significantly affect the radiative transfer in atmospheres that are already optically thick due to strong absorbers like CO
2
, SO
2
, and especially H
2
O.

To test this hypothesis, we performed simulations for a
\log f_{VMF}
= 0,
\Delta
IW-6 atmosphere at both
z=0
and
z=1
, including CIA contributions from H
2
–H
2
and H
2
–He. The results (Fig.
15
) confirm that the effect of CIA is negligible at both
T_{\text{irr}}
= 2500 K and 1500 K, with no appreciable difference from previous runs. This specific choice of tested parameters corresponds to the part of the parameter space were we expect CIAs to be most pronounced, i.e. H
2
-rich atmospheres.
