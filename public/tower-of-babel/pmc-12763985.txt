Background

Early psychosis represents a critical period in the developmental trajectory of serious mental illness, with first-episode psychosis (FEP) typically emerging in adolescence or early adulthood [1]. Characterized by hallucinations, delusions, disorganized thinking, and behavioral changes, FEP often marks the beginning of disorders such as schizophrenia, schizoaffective disorder, or bipolar disorder with psychotic features [2]. Research consistently shows that early intervention during this period significantly improves long-term outcomes, reducing disability and enhancing recovery prospects [3, 4]. The global pooled incidence of psychotic disorders is estimated at approximately 26 cases per 100,000 person-years, with reported rates ranging from 6 to 90 per 100,000 across studies [5]. High-income countries have established specialized early intervention services that have improved clinical outcomes, reduced hospitalization rates, and enhanced quality of life for individuals experiencing FEP [3, 6].

In India, this scenario presents unique challenges. With a population exceeding 1.4 billion and limited mental healthcare resources, the estimated prevalence of psychotic disorders is approximately 3.5 per 1000 persons [7]. However, treatment gaps remain substantial, with studies indicating that up to 70–80% of individuals with serious mental illnesses in India do not receive adequate care [8, 9]. Cultural factors, stigma, limited awareness, and centralized mental health services further compound these challenges, particularly in rural areas where access to specialized psychiatric care is severely limited [10, 11].

The importance of involving people with experience (PWLE) in research and clinical practice has gained recognition as a fundamental aspect of patient-centered care [12]. The experiential knowledge possessed by individuals who have navigated psychosis and their caregivers offers invaluable insights that complement professional expertise [13]. When people with lived experience (PWLE) participate in research design, service planning, and implementation, they help ensure that interventions are relevant, acceptable, and responsive to real needs [14, 15]. This approach fosters recovery-oriented practices, reduces stigma, and promotes empowerment [16, 17].

Advisory boards incorporating diverse stakeholders, particularly those with lived experience, have emerged as effective structures for meaningful involvement in mental health services [18]. In countries such as Australia, the United Kingdom, Canada, and the United States, early psychosis advisory boards have demonstrated significant impacts on service development [19, 20]. Programs such as ORYGEN in Australia, the PIER Program in Maine, USA [21], and the EPIP in Singapore [22] have institutionalized service user involvement through formal advisory mechanisms, resulting in more responsive and effective interventions.

For example, the International First-Episode Vocational Recovery (iFEVR) group established a global advisory board including service users and caregivers to guide vocational support in early psychosis services [23]. These boards have contributed to the development of recovery-focused interventions, strengthened community linkages, and enhanced service accessibility [24].

In India, while there has been growth in early intervention services for psychosis in recent years, formal advisory structures incorporating experience remain limited (Rangaswamy et al., 2019). Programs such as the SCARF in Chennai and the NIMHANS in Bangalore have pioneered some participatory approaches [25, 26], but the systematic involvement of people with lived experience (PWLE) and caregivers in advisory capacities has not been widely implemented. The few existing initiatives operate primarily within academic or research settings rather than being integrated into routine clinical care systems.

This gap highlights a critical need for developing culturally appropriate advisory structures that can effectively incorporate the voices of people with lived experience (PWLE) and caregivers into the Indian mental health care system [27]. Traditional hierarchical healthcare models in India often sideline experiential knowledge, focusing instead on expert-led approaches [28]. Moreover, contextual factors such as stigma, family-centered care patterns, and diverse sociocultural influences necessitate a tailored approach to advisory board development [29, 30].

The present study addresses this gap by developing and evaluating a first-episode psychosis advisory board that meaningfully integrates people with lived experience (PWLE), caregivers, and diverse stakeholders. By creating a structured manual and piloting the advisory board, this research aims to establish a replicable model for inclusive participation in early psychosis services across India. This initiative is designed to bridge the disconnect between service providers and recipients, ultimately enhancing the relevance, acceptability, and effectiveness of early psychosis interventions in the Indian context [31, 32].

Methods

Objectives

To develop and pilot test an early psychosis advisory board involving people with lived experience (PWLE), caregivers, and different stakeholders.

Study design

The present study used an exploratory research design and mixed-method approach to better understand the development and piloting of the advisory board.

Study population

To achieve the study objectives, three sets of participants were involved in two phases:

Phase 1 involves 2 sets of participants, one set in stage-1 professionals from different backgrounds who were part of the in-depth interviews, and the second set were experts from different organizations in the field who created or were part of an exciting advisory board of members around the world.

To establish an advisory board and subsequently develop a manual, 19 participants were recruited for phase one using purposive sampling. The group consisted of people with lived experience (over 18 years old), their caregivers, lawyers, faith healers, college representatives, and mental health professionals from the Schizophrenia Research Foundation, India. In-depth interviews were conducted with all 19 participants, following a semi-structured interview guide created specifically for this study (see Supplementary-4) after obtaining consent. Additionally, four experts from various organizations each having created or served on notable advisory boards internationally participated in discussions (see Supplementary-1).

A total of 13 members were recruited to the advisory board through a formal interview and selection process. These individuals were selected from a pool of over 75 applicants who applied for this position based on specific inclusion criteria.

The criteria required that members represent diverse perspectives, including persons with lived experience (PWLE), caregivers, and various professional stakeholders (such as social workers, psychologists, psychiatrists, general physicians, human resources professionals, professors, and college students). Furthermore, all participants were required to be over 18 years of age. In possession of general knowledge regarding early psychosis. Able to commit time for regular meetings.

Data analysis

This study used a mixed-methods approach to data analysis, integrating both qualitative and quantitative methods. The analysis was conducted in distinct stages, focusing on qualitative thematic analysis of interviews and focus groups, and a quantitative content validity assessment of the manual evaluation.

Qualitative analysis

Qualitative data were drawn from two phases of data collection: in-depth interviews (Phase 1) and focus group discussions (FGDs) (Phase 2). With participant consent, all interviews and FGDs were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim. The resulting transcripts were systematically analyzed using thematic analysis [34] to identify, analyze, and report patterns (themes) within the data.

Quantitative analysis: manual evaluation

A quantitative evaluation was conducted to assess the manual’s content validity. This was achieved by administering a structured survey, adapted from established manual evaluation criteria [33] to a panel of 10 experts.

The survey was divided into three distinct sections:

Section 1: General Feedback: Consisted of 3 questions rated on a 5-point Likert scale (where 1 = Strongly disagree and 5 = Strongly agree).

Phase 2: piloting and evaluate the advisory board

Building upon the findings from Phase 1, Phase 2 focused on piloting and evaluating the advisory board. This stage involved establishing a piloting advisory board for early psychosis and monitoring its operation over a period of six months.

Stage 1: recruitment of members

An advisory board recruitment campaign was launched utilizing various channels such as social media platforms, flyers distributed through WhatsApp, email, and posters and flyers displayed in the SCARF(I) Outpatient Department (OPD) area.

This resulted in 75 applications from diverse stakeholders, including general physicians, psychologists, psychiatrists, students, lawyers, professors, living experience persons, caregivers, psychiatric nurses, human resource personnel, and media personnel. For the selection process, the applications were evaluated on the basis of predefined criteria designed to ensure a well-rounded board that demonstrated interest in mental health and wellness, the ability to represent the needs of individuals with early psychosis effectively, the availability of time and commitment to board activities, the people lived experience (PWLE) of mental health illness (individuals or family members/caregivers) and professional expertise in working with individuals experiencing psychosis.

A total of 25 applicants meeting these criteria were shortlisted for individual interviews. From the interviews, 13 members were selected for the final board: 1- general physician (works in local government hospital), psychologist-1, psychiatrists-2, students-1, professors-1, people with lived experience-2 (PWLE), caregivers-3, human resource personnel-1 and 1- community social worker. All their professional backgrounds of these selected board members were documented.

Stage 2: training and piloting of the advisory board

A formal inauguration ceremony marked the establishment of the advisory board. All the members signed an agreement outlining their commitment to the six-month pilot phase.

Over a period of 6 months, twelve group sessions were conducted that combined both skill development and board meetings. The initial sessions focused on orientation to SCARF(I), its first-episode psychosis (FEP) program, and the research project. Following this phase, a codesign session was conducted to identify the skill training needs of board members.

Following the successful six-month pilot operation of the advisory board, a focus group discussion was conducted to evaluate and gain deeper insights into their experiences. The information, suggestions, recommendations, and guidance gathered throughout the previous stages were meticulously reviewed and integrated into the final manual draft. The content of Draft 01 was updated on the basis of the feedback received.

The overall evaluation results demonstrate a high level of content validity for the manual across all three assessment sections. The manual received its highest rating in Sect. 1 (General Feedback), with a validity score of 86.0%, followed closely by Sect. 2 (Content Assessment), at 84.7%. Section 3 (Understanding of Commonality and Other Aspects) received a slightly lower but still substantial validity score of 82.0%.

The overall content validity achievement level of 83.8% indicates that experts generally find the manual comprehensive, well-structured, and effective in addressing its intended purpose. This score exceeds the commonly accepted threshold of 80% for content validity, suggesting that the manual meets professional standards and is suitable for its intended audience. The table of overall scores is attached in supplementary 2.

The strong performance across all sections demonstrates that the manual effectively covers the key topics related to identifying people with lived experience (PWLE), engaging stakeholders, defining advisory board roles, developing recruitment strategies, facilitating meetings, and evaluating performance. The slightly lower score in Sect. 3 may indicate areas for potential refinement in how the manual addresses commonality aspects.

Discussion

This study focused on a critical gap in the literature by developing an advisory board that specifically incorporates people with lived experience (PWLE) and other key stakeholders. The findings indicate the significance of a multipronged approach, integrating critical evaluation of existing manuals, in-depth stakeholder interviews (Table 1) and pilot testing of the board.

By adapting existing frameworks through critical analysis, this study addresses a gap in the current knowledge base and establishes a foundation for developing a comprehensive manual for building advisory boards with diverse stakeholder representations.

The evaluation (supplementry-2) strongly validates the manual across all three assessment domains. With an overall content validity achievement level of 83.8%, the manual effectively addresses its intended purpose of guiding the establishment and operation of advisory boards that incorporate lived experience perspectives. These findings support the implementation of the manual in real-world settings while highlighting specific areas for future iterations.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to use multipronged approach to develop an advisory board and evaluate it by creating a manual for an advisory board for first-episode psychosis services, pilot testing the board using the developed manual, and refining the manual via the codesign methodology. However, the lack of an explicit implementation framework was considered to be a key limitation. Future studies can benefit from incorporating existing frameworks, such as the RE-AIM framework (reach, effectiveness, adoption, implementation, maintenance) [35] or the consolidated framework for implementation research (CFIR) [36].

For example, multiple aspects of the study, such as reach (proportion of the target population reached), effectiveness (impact of the board on stakeholders), adoption (uptake of the board model by organizations), implementation (fidelity of the board functioning as outlined in the manual), and maintenance (sustainability of the board over time), could be assessed using the RE-AIM framework. Similarly, various factors influencing the implementation process, such as intervention characteristics (developing a manual involving different stakeholders as advisory board members), the outer setting (organisational structure and the process involving the advisory board), the inner setting (the FEP program was already established to create an advisory board for the program), and the characteristics of the individuals involved (different professionals, people with lived experience and caregivers), could be identified with the help of the CFIR framework [36]. Hence, incorporating an implementation framework during the development process would have provided a structured approach for operationalizing the manual and anticipating potential challenges in real-world settings. This could have informed the content of the manual itself, offering guidance for overcoming these challenges.

In addition to that, integrating people with lived experience (PWLE) with health professionals on advisory boards offers the benefit of authentic expertise but risks creating power imbalances and tokenism. In our study, we found these potential challenges were effectively mitigated.

Several factors contributed to this. First, proactive power dynamic training was provided to all members, establishing equality between experiential and professional knowledge. Second, the board was unified by a strong, shared motive to improve early psychosis outcomes, which fostered a collaborative environment.

To ensure PWLE members had an equal impact, specific structural mechanisms were implemented, such as consensus-based decision-making, and equal voting rights were implemented during the board meetings.

Valuable insights into perceptions and preferences regarding board composition, recruitment, involvement, and functions were gained from the stakeholder interviews (Table 1). These themes align with recommendations for building inclusive and effective advisory boards. In addition, the focus group discussions (Table 2) further highlighted the importance of training, a clear structure, communication, and fostering relationships among board members. These findings resonate with previous research on the importance of ongoing support and development for advisory boards [37].

Limitation

Several limitations warrant acknowledgement in this study. First, the research was conducted exclusively at a single tertiary mental health care institution in Chennai, India, rather than across multiple sites. This single-site approach potentially restricts the generalizability of our findings, as the specific institutional context, including pre-existing relationships, infrastructure, and organisational culture, may have shaped both the implementation process and outcomes in ways that may not be representative of other healthcare settings. Adding to that the manual developed through the critical evaluation process was not done by a systematic search, as this was done through targeting websites and databases.

The duration of our follow-up period was relatively brief, meaning our findings capture only the initial stages of integrating people with experience (PWLE) into management structures. Consequently, we cannot draw conclusions about longer-term outcomes or assess the sustained engagement and participation of these stakeholders over time.

A significant limitation was the absence of formal process and outcome evaluation measures. While the involvement of people with lived experience (PWLE) formed the cornerstone of our initiative, we did not implement structured assessments to measure the impact on governance processes, decision-making quality, or service delivery improvements. This gap restricts our ability to make definitive claims about the effectiveness or broader influence of this particular model of stakeholder engagement.

Furthermore, the study lacked an explicit implementation framework to guide our approach. The absence of a structured, theoretically informed methodology for planning, integrating, and evaluating stakeholder involvement may have compromised both the consistency and replicability of our process. This limitation also hindered our capacity to identify specific mechanisms through which the advisory board model creates impact.

These limitations are reflective of the exploratory and pilot nature of our work, which prioritised establishing foundational participatory structures rather than conducting a comprehensive evaluation at this preliminary stage. Future iterations of this work should address these methodological gaps to strengthen the evidence base for inclusive advisory board models in mental health settings.

Conclusion

This study employed a comprehensive, multi-faceted approach to establish an advisory board that included people with lived experience (PWLE) alongside various stakeholders. Throughout this process, we developed a practical manual that mental health organisations and institutions in lower- and middle-income countries can utilise to create their own inclusive advisory boards (manual will be available on request from the corresponding author). Future research should build upon this foundation by integrating implementation frameworks to assess the reach, effectiveness, and long-term sustainability of this board model across diverse contexts and settings. Such research would provide invaluable insights for both researchers and practitioners, thereby enhancing the overall impact of advisory boards in supporting individuals experiencing first-episode psychosis and potentially extending benefits to other vulnerable populations.
