Metastatic Leydig cell tumor: A case report and review of the literature
Authors: Faisl Alslimah, Mohammed Radwan, Mutasim Alkhalifah, Fawaz Alkeraithe, Ali Alzughbi, Mofarej Alhogbani, Naif Aldhaam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLeydig cell tumors are rare sex cord-stromal neoplasms, mostly benign, with about 10% malignant. A 42-year-old man presented with a 6 cm right testicular mass and sclerotic bone lesions, normal tumor markers, and no lymphadenopathy. Orchidectomy showed a Leydig cell tumor with vascular invasion but no marked atypia. Follow-up was irregular. After 24 months, imaging revealed an 8 × 7.5 cm retroperitoneal mass; biopsy confirmed metastasis. RPLND removed a single involved lymph node. Testosterone fell from >120 nmol/L to 2.30 nmol/L postoperatively. Despite limited risk factors, metastatic disease occurred, highlighting unpredictability, chemoresistance, and importance of surveillance and aggressive surgical management.

Introduction

Leydig cell tumors (LCTs) comprise 75–80% of sex cord-stromal tumors. Adults may exhibit symptoms such as painless testicular mass, testicular pain, gynecomastia (caused by androgen excess and peripheral estrogen conversion), impotence, decreased libido, and infertility. This tumor type is most common among 30- to 60-year-old men. Testicular ultrasonography and serum tumor markers should be included in the diagnostic workup. As these tumors can appear variable on ultrasonography, with no discernible difference from germ cell tumor (GCT). Luteinizing hormone, follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), testosterone, estrogen, and estradiol levels should also be measured in cases of gynecomastia, infertility, low libido, or precocious puberty. Upon confirmation of the diagnosis, patients should undergo chest, abdominal, and pelvic CT for staging.1

In 2005, Conkey et al. reported on 16 patients with Leydig cell tumors, mostly with low malignant potential. A malignant clinical course was noted in two cases; however, these cases showed obvious pathological features including lymphatic invasion, larger size, invasion of the testicular capsule and spermatic cord, numerous mitoses, cellular pleomorphism, nuclear atypia and areas of necrosis. Three of the remaining 14 patients demonstrated mild to moderate cellular pleomorphism. Two of these showed focal capsular invasion, and one exhibited occasional mitoses1

In 2017, the Nason Study reported 2755 cases of testicular cancer; 22 (0.79%) of these were Leydig cell tumors, 19 of which were invasive (stage T1), three were in situ, and one patient developed local recurrence following an organ-preserving procedure and underwent a completion orchidectomy. None of the patients had metastatic disease or experienced disease-related mortality.2

Case presentation

A healthy 42-year-old man with a right testicular mass and multiple bony sclerotic lesions was referred to our hospital in 2020 for further management. Physical examination revealed a right testicular mass measuring 6 × 4 × 4 cm3 with no other signs, including gynecomastia or palpable lymph nodes, and negative tumor markers.

Computed tomography (CT) of the abdomen revealed multiple scattered focal sclerotic lesions throughout the axial and appendicular osseous structures that represented enostosis, with no signs of abdominal or pelvic metastasis. The chest CT scan was negative for metastasis, and right inguinal radical orchidectomy was performed. Pathological examination revealed a Leydig cell tumor measuring 6 cm with focal vascular invasion (Fig. 1-A, Fig. 1-BA and B). No other pathological features suggestive of malignancy seen in the radical orchidectomy specimen, such as cytological atypia, increased mitotic activity, necrosis, and infiltrative margins.

20X magnification showing the focal vascular invasion.

Despite a six-month follow-up being scheduled, the patient only returned 12 months later. At follow-up, abdominal CT was negative, apart from previously sclerotic bony lesions. The patient was kept under surveillance, although he did not adhere to regular follow-up After 18 months of surveillance, a full endocrine panel was obtained: testosterone was measured at 35 nmol/L (mildly elevated above the upper limit of the normal reference range of 10–35 nmol/L), and other tumor marker levels were unremarkable. A bone scan showed three small focal increased uptakes within the left posterior skull, and diffuse heterogeneous increased tracer uptake was noted on multiple ribs. Further characterization with a Positron Emission Tomography (PET) scan was advised and showed multiple sclerotic lesions that did not demonstrate significant FDG activity, suggesting a benign etiology (Fig. 2-A, Fig. 2-B, Fig. 2-CA,B,2C). The right external iliac lymph node had moderate-intense hypermetabolism and measured 1.3 × 0.9 cm2(Fig. 2D). No other suspicious hypermetabolic status was noted. A multidisciplinary team decided on imaging surveillance and follow-up.

After 24 months of surveillance, the testosterone level was >120 nmol/L and tumor markers were negative. Abdominal CT showed interval development of a large heterogeneously enhancing right retroperitoneal mass abutting the inferior vena cava, aorta, right iliac vessels, and right psoas muscle (8 × 7.5 cm) (Fig. 3-A, Fig. 3-BA,B). The mass displaced the adjacent structures without invasion. A PET scan was repeated and showed the interval development of a large right retroperitoneal hypermetabolic mass highly suspicious for nodal metastasis with a persistent residual hypermetabolic right external iliac lymph node (Fig. 3C). A Tumor Board Meeting was held and a biopsy decision was made. Retroperitoneal biopsy was performed using interventional radiology. Pathological examination revealed a metastatic malignant Leydig cell tumor, following which a multidisciplinary team decided to perform RPLND.

Coronal view of the abdomen computed tomography (CT) showed a large heterogeneous enhancing right retroperitoneal mass abutting the IVC, measuring 8 × 7.5 cm2, and containing internal necrosis. The mass had displaced the adjacent structure without invasion.

Axial view of the abdomen computed tomography (CT) showed a large heterogeneous enhancing right retroperitoneal mass abutting the IVC measuring 8 × 7.5 cm2 and containing internal necrosis.

Positron emission tomography (PET) showed a large right retroperitoneal hypermetabolic mass highly suspicious for nodal metastasis.

Open RPLND was performed, and a large right retroperitoneal capsulated mass measuring 8 × 7.5 × 6.5 cm3 was found to be tightly adhered to the vena cava wall. The mass was carefully dissected free from the IVC wall without local spillage, with the bilateral ureters and vessel loops retracted laterally (Fig. 4-A, Fig. 4-BA and B). The final pathology of the mass was a metastatic Leydig cell tumor limited to a fibrous capsule of one retroperitoneal lymph node and negative 21 retroperitoneal lymph nodes for metastasis (Fig. 5-A, Fig. 5-BA–B). One week after the RPLND, the patient's condition improved, and his testosterone levels dropped steeply to 2.30 nmoL/l. The patient was instructed to regularly follow-up every 3 months to monitor abdominal CT, chest radiography, and hormone levels.

Discussion

The lung and retroperitoneum are the most common sites of metastatic disease. The prognosis is poor for metastatic Leydig cell tumors that are resistant to radiation and chemotherapy.3 Patients should undergo routine clinical evaluations, CT imaging of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis; and regular monitoring of their hormone profile. Excision of the primary tumor may result in hypogonadism and persistent Leydig cell dysfunction; 40% of men may need postoperative testosterone supplementation.4 For histological differences between metastasizing and non-metastasizing forms of Leydig cell tumors, the former exhibited larger sizes, higher mitotic index, atypical mitotic activity, nuclear variation, necrosis, angiolymphatic invasion, infiltrative margins, high grade, and invasion into the rete testis, epididymis, or tunica.3 RPLND is a prognostic, palliative, and potentially therapeutic procedure that can be performed in patients who have both organ-confined and metastatic disease after orchidectomy.

LCTs are uncommon sex-cord stromal neoplasms that contribute to only a small minority of testicular malignancies and commonly have normal germ-cell tumor markers; endocrine manifestations (e.g., gynecomastia) are inconsistent and may be absent, as in our case.5

The index orchiectomy specimen contained two histopathologic predictors of malignant conduct—tumor size ≥5 cm, vascular invasion—features that have repeatedly correlated with metastatic potential as well as unfavorable prognoses in modern series as well as reviews.6 Our case thus demonstrates how “bland” cytology may accompany high risk phenotype when architectural as well as proliferative criteria are balanced.

The imaging discordance observed during surveillance warrants specific comment for clarity. At 18 months, bone scintigraphy demonstrated focal increased tracer uptake in the left posterior skull and multiple ribs, while FDG-PET/CT showed no significant metabolic activity in the corresponding sclerotic foci. This apparent contradiction reflects a well-recognized limitation of FDG-PET in osteoblastic (sclerotic) bone lesions: such lesions have low glucose metabolic activity relative to osteolytic metastases and may fall below the spatial or metabolic resolution threshold of FDG-PET, whereas bone scintigraphy (which detects osteoblastic activity via technetium-99m uptake) retains sensitivity in this setting. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of bone may further complement both modalities when discordance exists.7 Our patient's later testosterone surge, in the setting of previously “quiet” bone PET, became the impetus to intensify evaluation, eventually uncovering the retroperitoneal mass.

For malignant LCT with retroperitoneal nodal disease, various cohorts underpin primary surgery centrality in the form of template-based or full RPLND for durable regional control and potential long-term disease remission in carefully selected individuals.8 Conversely, sex-cord stromal malignancies (including malignant LCT) respond poorly to platinum chemotherapy as well as radiotherapy, thus limiting the usefulness of systemic therapy in all contexts short of select palliative situations.9 Current institutional guidelines reaffirm surgery-first management models with personalized surveillance due to these biologic constraints.10 Our own case fits this model: RPLND obtained complete macroscopic clearance with biochemical normalization in the operative period. About one third of LCTs are hormonally active; even when classical markers are normal, androgen or estrogen in excess can reflect tumor burden and relapse.11 Here, the spike in testosterone (>120 nmol/L) preceded imaging-confirmed progression and then reverted after cytoreduction, highlighting the value of serial sex-hormone panels in surveillance algorithms.5 The inclusion of structured endocrine monitoring in conjunction with imaging may advance detection of biologically active recurrence. This case clarifies risk-stratified surveillance in LCT by: (i) combining histologic risk parameters (size, necrosis, mitotic activity, vascular/infiltrative growth) with (ii) endocrine kinetics as a sensitive, individualized signal, and (iii) recognizing modality-specific blind spots (FDG-PET insensitivity to specific sclerotic bone lesions). Each, individually, underpins the concept of a surveillance “triangle” (pathology-endocrine-multimodality imaging) instead of depending on any one axis.5, 6, 7

For LCTs ≥5 cm and/or with malignant histologic features, we suggest: (a) upfront multimodality staging (CT/MRI ± bone scan, with PET used judiciously); (b) risk-adapted interval that places most intensive imaging in the first 24–36 months; (c) endocrine panels every 3 months (testosterone, estradiol, LH/FSH) in the first year, given their prognostic significance; and (d) early referrals to high-volume centers for RPLND consideration in isolated nodal metastases8, 9, 10 For dissemination disease, few therapies are available; small modern case reports address targeted therapy or endocrine-moderating therapy, but evidence remains preliminary.12

Due to single-patient study with subideal early adherence, generalizability is limited. Without centralized pathology review in Ki-67/DNA ploidy and molecular profiling, biologic inferences are limited. There was no protocolization in imaging through time points, which can impair detection sensitivity. Despite this, the convergence between the biochemical and clinicopathologic signals, as well as the unequivocal response to surgical debulking, underlines causal interpretation. Closing synthesis. Histology in malignant-potential LCT is relevant; endocrine patterns can be as informative as radiology; and in the emergence of nodal disease, RPLND provides the most dependable route to regional control. Our case brings these principles to life and identifies traps in interpreting FDG-PET sclerotic bone disease. Future multi-institutional series should challenge endocrine-anchored surveillance algorithms and determine indications for adjunctive systemic therapy in this uncommon tumor. 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12.

Conclusion

Most Leydig cell tumors are benign. However, 10% of cases are malignant, and such conversion to malignancy requires close follow-up given the extremely variable presentation and relative rarity of the tumor. Our case presented an aggressive and rapidly growing large retroperitoneal mass with a known pathology of a Leydig cell tumor and two features that suggested a malignant phenotype with a size of >5 cm and focal vascular invasion only. Urologists should closely monitor this issue, and patients should be informed about the possibility of more aggressive therapy during follow-up.
