Morphological discrimination of human lice (Anoplura: Pediculidae) by eggs’ cap-like operculum
Authors: Mohammad Akhoundi, Hantatiana Juliana Heriniaina, Sophie Brun, Emma Benamari, Arezki Izri
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractHuman lice have long been parasites of humans and are responsible for several epidemics in human medical history. However, their morphology, particularly head and body lice, remains very similar. In this study, we discriminate human louse species based on the number, shape and size of aeropyles (respiratory pores) of the egg operculum as well as the size of the respiratory orifice in the aeropyles. For this purpose, lice and eggs were collected from infested patients referring to Parasitology Department of Avicenne Hospital (Bobigny, France) and analyzed morphologically. The microscopic examination of the aeropyles revealed a large variation in the number ranging from 5 to 12 for head lice, 10 to 16 for body lice and 13 to 18 for pubic lice with a statistically distinct average of 8.3, 12.9 and 15.7, respectively. Although the length of the head and body louse eggs were almost similar, the width of body louse eggs were bigger than those of head louse (412.9 μm and 363.5 μm, respectively), while pubic louse eggs had the smallest length (692.9 μm) and width (286.1 μm). In addition, pubic louse eggs had a greater operculum length and width (253.7 μm length and 220.7 μm width) than head (230.6 μm length and 210.2 μm width) and body lice (227.9 μm length and 199.9 μm width). Moreover, pubic lice had the smallest average aeropyle size (41.2 μm) compared to head (51.1 μm) and body lice (49.1 μm). The respiratory orifice on the aeropyles of head and body lice (9.9 μm) were almost twice as big as those of pubic lice (4.9 μm). There was an inverse correlation between the number of aeropyles and their size in head, body and pubic lice. These findings are helpful in accurate identification of human louse species using their eggs, which could be useful for control management strategies against these ectoparasites.Keywords: Human lice, Morphological discrimination, Eggs, Operculum, Aeropyle, Respiratory orificeSubject terms: Microbiology, Medical research

Introduction

Human lice (Anoplura: Pediculidae) are wingless, obligatory hematophagous ectoparasites with a long history of cohabitation with humans1–3. There are two species: (a) two subspecies of the human louse (Pediculus humanus), the head louse (Pediculus humanus capitis), and the body louse (Pediculus humanus humanus); and (b) pubic louse (Pthirus pubis).

Head lice are widespread worldwide, particularly among schoolchildren, affecting all socio-economic and ethnic groups4. They reside on the scalp and lay eggs at the base of hair shafts. Intense pruritus of the scalp, neck, ears, and shoulders are the common symptoms of infestation5. While the role of head lice in disease transmission is debatable, no formal evidence certifying its vectorial role is available6. Head lice are spread through hair-to-hair contact or more rarely contact with infested items like clothing (such as hats, scarves, coats) or other personal items (such as combs, brushes or towels)7. Body lice are mainly associated with people living in poor hygienic conditions. They are known vectors of at least three pathogenic bacteria to humans: Rickettsia prowazekii (causative agent of louse-borne typhus), Borrelia recurrentis (responsible of louse-borne relapsing fever) and Bartonella quintana (etiologic agent of trench fever)8. With exception of blood-feeding, they spend most of their time on clothes where they also lay eggs9. Pubic lice, or crabs, typically inhabit pubic hair10, but can also be found in other areas like eyebrows, eyelashes, beard, mustache, chest, and armpits11,12. They are mainly transmitted through sexual contact, but can also spread via infested bedding, clothing and towels, especially in crowded places13. Intense itching in the pubic area and groin, particularly at night, is the most commonly reported symptom14.

Human lice undergo hemimetabolic development including egg, three nymphal stages, and the adults lasting around 35 days15. After mating, females lay 3 to 8 eggs per day for almost 16 days16, attaching them to hair shafts near the scalp.

The eggs in human lice are small, transparent, yellow to light brown, oval or teardrop-shaped, measuring 0.3 to 0.8 millimeters17,18. The eggs possess an operculum, an oval-shaped lid-like structure that covers the developing embryo inside. Nymphs hatch from eggs within 6–9 days. When hatching, the operculum will be opened allowing the nymph to hatch, leaving behind the empty eggshell (nit) which lacks an operculum, appears collapsed, and exhibits a matte, whitish color, rendering it non-translucent. Accordingly, the absence of an operculum indicates that the nymph has emerged from the egg17. The eggs are firmly attached to hair by a durable glue-like (cement) substance secreted by female louse accessory gland. This glue hardens quickly, securely fixing and protecting the egg during development19–21. The only part of the egg free of glue is a vault-like operculum at distal end which prevents the embryo from asphyxiation during development22,23. The operculum is made from a slightly thinner and more porous part of the egg’s outer shell (chorion) containing several aeropyles. Aeropyles are the respiratory pores found in many terrestrial insect eggs24. Human louse eggs have a distinct operculum, with a row of pores near the operculated end. This porous operculum allows gas and humidity exchange and fluid retention during embryo development25–27. These structures consist of a system of apertures that converge down from a larger opening to a smaller one. The aeropyles and their adjacent reticulum network can be fractured to reveal the smaller openings that make up the substructure25,26.

Human head and body lice share very similar morphological criteria in which it is difficult to discriminate them morphologically despite ecological separation. Additionally, body lice are larger in size than head lice, particularly in the length of the tibia of the second pair of legs28–30 and they also have longer antennae31. However, these morphological differences are not entirely obvious and, in some cases, depending on the physiological stages, can be confusing.

In this study, we aimed to differentiate human louse species based on the external morphological criteria of their eggs.

Materials and methods

Lice collection

Human lice specimens were obtained by parasitological examination of suspected patients referred to the Parasitology Department of the Avicenne Hospital (Bobigny, France) from October 2023 to February 2024. Head lice and their eggs were collected by combing the hair of infested patients. For the body lice, the clothes of suspected individual were subjected to parasitological examinations, while the pubic area of possible infested individuals were examined clinically. The collected louse specimens and the eggs were placed in sterile Petri dishes and examined morphologically under a stereomicroscope (Stemi 508, Carl Zeiss, Germany). Adult specimens of head, body and pubic lice were identified separately according to the morphological identification key of Ewing32,33. Thereafter, the eggs were morphologically examined to determine their shape, size and external ornamentation.

Microscopic examination of louse eggs and their morphological differentiations

The eggs were dissected under a stereomicroscope and the associated operculum was individually mounted on sterile microscopic slides containing a drop of sterile Eukitt solution (composed of 5% acrylic resin and 55% xylene) and were analyzed morphologically. The number, shape and size of the eggs, the number and size of the aeropyles present on the operculum of the eggs, as well as the size of the respiratory orifices were recorded.

Statistical analysis

In order to compare the morphometric measurements of egg length, operculum, and aeropyles of head, body, and pubic lice, statistical analysis was performed using the Chi-square (χ²) test with R software.

Results

A total of 66 adult specimens (34 head lice, 21 body lice and 11 pubic lice, respectively), and 40 eggs (20 head lice, 10 body lice and 10 pubic lice, respectively) were collected from seven infested individuals, (three with head lice, two with body lice and two with pubic lice) (Fig. 1). The morphological analysis of the eggs revealed a large variation in the number, shape and size of the aeropyles on the operculum. The eggs of head lice had 5–12 aeropyles on their operculum (Fig. 2), while those of the body and pubic lice had 10–16 and 13–18 aeropyles, respectively (Figs. 3 and 4; Table 1). A significant difference in the average number of aeropyles was observed between head lice (8.3 aeropyles), body lice (12.9), and pubic lice (15.7), which allows for their discrimination despite some individual overlaps (Table 1).

Head and body lice had almost similar average length of eggs (810.1 μm and 808.7 μm, respectively), while body louse eggs were significantly wider than those of head lice (412.9 μm and 363.5 μm, respectively). Pubic louse eggs measured in average 692.9 μm in length and 286.1 μm in width, and were significantly smaller than head and body louse eggs.

On the other hand, the operculum of head and body lice had a nearly similar average length (230.6 μm versus 227.9 μm) and width (210.2 μm versus 199.9 μm), while the average length (253.7 μm) and width (220.7 μm) of pubic lice operculum was bigger than those of head and body lice.

Furthermore, head lice had the largest aeropyle size (52.1 μm diameter), followed by body lice (49.1 μm) and pubic lice (41.2 μm). Finally, head and body louse aeropyles had a similar average length of respiratory orifice (9.9 μm) while this of the pubic louse was 4.9 μm (Table 1).

Discussion and conclusions

Traditionally, distinguishing between head and body lice relied on ecological isolation (scalp versus clothes) or morphological criteria like body length, tibia length or antenna length28–31,13. However, most of these criteria are not clearly defined as they depend on the lice developmental stage, or are hardly achievable.

The eggs were the subject of some investigations providing detailed information on the morphological structure (including size, shape, and surface texture), development, and hatching conditions of lice eggs along with observations on their respective viability and adaptations to different environmental conditions21,27. In this study, despite some individual overlapping, a distinct difference in egg and operculum shapes and measurements as well as in the size and number of aeropyles on the egg’s operculum was observed which made it possible to discriminate between head, body and pubic lice (Table 1). Regarding phenotypically similar species of head and body lice, the criteria such as egg width and the number of aeropyles allow for differentiation between them (Table 1). Additionally, pubic lice eggs had the smallest length and width among human lice. The latter is consistent with the size of pubic lice compared to head lice and body lice. On the other hand, pubic lice surprisingly had a greater average of operculum length and width than head and body lice. This may be explained by the fact that pubic lice have a shorter but distinctly broader body than head and body lice which require a larger operculum for the embryo to exit from the egg34. Additionally, pubic lice had the smallest average aeropyle size compared to head and body lice. Moreover, there was an inverse correlation between the number of aeropyles and their size in the head, body and pubic lice. Pubic louse eggs with aeropyle numbers ranging from 13 to 18, had the smallest aeropyle size compared to head lice with 5 to 12 but larger aeropyles. Furthermore, head and body lice had a similar respiratory orifice size but both of them were two times larger than those pubic lice. Finally, the eggs had a varied shape in head and body lice compared to pubic lice with a triangle-like shape allowing to discriminate them morphologically (Fig. 1). The latter is consistent with a previous study reporting that ventral side of the embryo in the head and body lice is located at the level of the hair, whereas the embryo of pubic lice is orientated laterally to the hair35. These findings confirm previous preliminary investigations about the morphological differences in eggs of human lice36 and highlight the significance of these criteria for distinguishing between these species in the absence of adult specimens. This discrimination between species is crucial especially for assigning the vectorial role of human pathogens and for subsequent control management strategies adopted for the treatment of these species.

On the other hand, although human lice have demonstrated a low oxygen requirement37, variation in the number of aeropyles in head (5–12 aeropyles), body (10–16) and pubic (13–18) lice could be evaluated in relation to the ecological isolation of human lice to explore whether there is a possible correlation between the aeropyles number and the amount of air circulation in different regions of the body (scalp, clothes and pubic area) ? For instance, is there a correlation between limited air circulation in the pubic area and higher number of aeropyles in the eggs, while in head lice more exposed to air, the number of aeropyles is lower than those of body and pubic lice. Similarly, could there be a potential association between the ecological isolation of human lice and the size of the respiratory orifice?

Due to restricted number of specimens with limited geographic diversity addressed in this study, further investigations with a broader range of human lice specimens from various geographic locations may further clarify and expand upon these findings. Additionally, considering the function of aeropyles in air and humidity exchange of embryos, they have been the subject of treatments with pediculicides e.g., based on dimethicone by blocking gas exchanges, leading to the death of the louse egg38. This underscores the importance of aeropyles not only in species discrimination but also in the treatment of louse eggs.
