Associations between social determinants of health and COVID-19 outcomes by variants of concern: a Bayesian spatiotemporal analysis
Authors: Fanyu Xiu, Michael A Irvine, Natalie Prystajecky, Linda Hoang, Linwei Wang, Sharmistha Mishra, Beate Sander, Stefan Baral, Naveed Z Janjua, Hind Sbihi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSocially and economically disadvantaged populations were disproportionately affected by COVID-19, but it remains unclear whether and how variants of concern moderated the associations between social determinants of health and COVID-19 outcomes.MethodsWe aggregated cases by week and health region (n=231) from 1 January 2021 to 27 February 2022, covering four major variants of concern waves–Alpha, Gamma, Delta and Omicron–in British Columbia, the third most populated province in Canada. We constructed a region-specific, continuous socioeconomic index (SI) in which a one-unit increase represents a 10% increase in socioeconomic status across the health region. We fitted Bayesian spatiotemporal models on four COVID-19 outcomes separately: cases, hospitalisations, intensive care unit admissions and deaths, adjusting for interaction between SI and variants of concern, percentage male and vaccine coverage. We estimated the marginal rate ratio (RR) between SI and the outcomes by variants of concern.ResultsOverall, Alpha, Gamma and Delta heightened the associations between SI and all outcomes. The marginal RR between SI and cases was 0.96 (95% credible interval (CrI): 0.93, 1.00) in the absence of variants of concern, 0.93 (95% CrI: 0.88, 0.97) in the presence of Alpha, 0.93 (95% CrI: 0.88, 0.98) for Gamma and 0.90 (95% CrI: 0.87, 0.93) for Delta. Omicron reversed the direction of SI’s association with cases (RR: 1.03, 95% CrI: 0.99, 1.07) and dampened the associations with other outcomes.ConclusionsVariants of concern generally widened the socioeconomic disparities in COVID-19 outcomes. Tailoring and optimising pandemic preparedness and response measures to the specific needs of disadvantaged populations is vital for reducing the additional disease burden experienced by some communities in British Columbia and across Canada.Keywords: COVID-19, Sociodemographic Factors, Communicable Disease Control, SARS-CoV-2, Space-Time Clustering

Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic unfolded in December 2019 and led to over 770 million cases and seven million deaths globally,1 placing significant strain on health systems worldwide. In late 2020, the SARS-CoV-2 rapidly evolved into variants of concern (VOC), exhibiting increased infectivity and severity compared with the initially dominant wild-type strain.2 From the start of 2021 to early 2022, in British Columbia (BC), the third most populated province in Canada, laboratory testing has identified five VOC: Alpha (Pangolin Lineage B.1.1.7 and Q.*), Beta (B.1.351), Gamma (P.1), Delta (B.1.617.2 and AY.*) and Omicron (B.1.1.529 and BA.*).3 Notably, Alpha and Gamma concurrently spread among the population from March to June 2021, succeeded by two bigger waves dominated by Delta (June–December 2021) and Omicron (December 2021–February 2022), respectively4 5 (figure 2A).

In response to the fast-changing pandemic, public health in BC responded swiftly, adopting routine laboratory screening/sequencing and vaccination strategies combined with policy measures, such as social distancing and travel restrictions.6 Whole genome sequencing (WGS) and targeted VOC qPCR single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) screening were concurrently used for variant typing among all COVID-19 positive tests (henceforth ‘cases’) between January and May 2021.7 Only WGS was used thereafter and periodically limited to prioritised cases and at-risk populations depending on public health capacity and needs.7 Furthermore, a four-phase immunisation programme was enacted, with the target population expanded from high-risk individuals (December 2020–March 2021) to the broader population (April 2021–September 2021).8 From September 2021 to May 2022, booster shots were available for at-risk individuals 6–8 months after the second doses.9

In addition to the rapid evolution of the pathogen, COVID-19 outcomes were also strongly patterned across socioeconomic strata.10 Social determinants of health (SDoH) have been highlighted as non-medical factors that shape health outcomes for various diseases11 and COVID-19 is no exception. Previous studies in BC and elsewhere in Canada have underscored the heightened infection risks among communities with higher proportions of visible minorities, low-income households and certain essential services.12,14 Moreover, the socioeconomic patterning of COVID-19 acquisition and hospitalisation risk varied by the prominent VOC in BC.5

Understanding the interplay between VOC and SDoH on COVID-19 cases and severity offers critical insight into public health strategies targeting specific populations. Although previous research established the mediating role of VOC between income and hospitalisation in BC,15 few studies have examined the role of VOC on the associations between a broad SDoH indicator and COVID-19 cases and severity. We hypothesised that VOC moderates the associations between SDoH and COVID-19 cases, hospitalisations, intensive care unit (ICU) admissions and deaths.

Methods

Study design

We conducted an ecological study using weekly COVID-19 surveillance data from 1 January 2021 to 27 February 2022 (60 weeks) in BC.16 The geographic unit of analysis was the Community Health Service Area (CHSA), which are 231 contiguous (2022 boundary configuration), mutually exclusive and exhaustive regions for community-level health planning and service provision in BC.16 The study period was chosen to cover VOC-based waves affecting the province at the early course of the pandemic, and to ensure adequate sample size for the Omicron-dominated phase.

Patient and public involvement

None.

Data sources

We calculated the number of COVID-19 cases, hospitalisations, ICU admissions and deaths per week and CHSA by aggregating individual-level data from the BC COVID-19 Cohort (BCC19C, 2019–present), a large population-based, dynamic provincial health database.17 Datasets integrated within the BCC19C are summarised in online supplemental table S1. We included positive laboratory test samples from BC residents with available WGS or SNP results (ie, those with known VOC). The WGS/SNP (henceforth ‘variant typed’) samples were drawn from laboratory positive tests conducted by the BC Centre for Disease Control Public Health Laboratory, which should reflect the overall case profiles in BC, as self-administered antigen test kits were largely unavailable during the study period.18 In addition, we excluded all those who were infected by the Beta VOC due to the low number of cases that occurred in BC (0.1% of all positive samples), had missing sex assigned at birth, age, CHSA or hospital admission date (if hospitalised), lived outside of province, were in high-risk settings (lived in long term care or aged ≤2 years) and reinfections. Finally, demographic characteristics and socioeconomic variables for CHSA were retrieved from the 2021 Census of Population.19

Measure of variables

Four COVID-19 outcome measures were considered: the number of cases, hospitalisations, ICU admissions and deaths. The outcomes were standardised by age and sex assigned at birth (as detailed in Statistical analysis).

To obtain a single measurement of the exposure variable, SDoH, we computed a single socioeconomic index (SI) for each CHSA by conducting a factor analysis,20 using the following 12 area-level variables represented by percentage (except for the median income): visible minorities, individuals without high school education among the population aged 25–64 years, dwellings needing major repair, low-income population, single-parent families, dwellings that are apartments, population living alone, recent movers, single/divorced/widowed population, unemployment ratio, dependency ratio and median income per year (in CAD, reversely coded). The variables were informed by two commonly used SDoH metrics in the study region, the BC’s Index of Multiple Deprivation21 and the Pampalon Index.22 This approach, further explained in online supplemental methods, allowed us to incorporate diverse axes of marginalisation and avoid overfitting issues from including multiple SDoH variables at once. The SI was continuous and scaled such that a one-unit increase represents a 10% increase in socioeconomic status across all CHSA to be included in the study.

The VOC variables were the presence/absence of Alpha, Gamma, Delta and Omicron among COVID-19 cases in each week and CHSA. This measure was adopted as opposed to the dominant VOC, due to the high concurrency between the Alpha and Gamma cases at the beginning of the study period.

To adjust for other potential confounders, we included the percentage male (%) and the coverage of complete COVID-19 primary vaccine series (henceforth ‘full vaccine coverage’).23 We specified full multivariable models rather than adopting stepwise covariate inclusion, based on prior evidence showing differences in COVID-19 outcomes between sex and vaccine coverage in the same study settings.12 14 The percentage male was fixed across time, since the population distribution remained roughly unchanged throughout the study period. The proportion of fully vaccinated individuals was defined as having either a single dose of the Janssen vaccine or 2 doses of any other mRNA or vector vaccines,9 adjusted for a 14-day period for the vaccines to take effect.24

Statistical analysis

We summarised each of the outcomes using standardised incidence ratio (SIR) specific to each week and CHSA.23 The expected outcomes were calculated by indirectly standardising the observed outcomes, based on 10 age-sex strata.12 The SIR per week and CHSA was then calculated as the ratio of the observed to the expected outcome.

Oij is the observed outcome of interest and Eij is the expected number of the outcome specific to the ith CHSA and jth week. μij is the expected SIR, modelled using a log-linear model. Xij is a vector containing the values of the SI and the covariates. Additionally, interactions between SI and VOC were included in Xij to investigate the hypothetically different associations between SI and the outcomes by VOC. We used INLA as a fast and practical alternative to Markov Chain Monte Carlo for fitting spatiotemporal models, which allows for further exploration of model structures and priors with minimal loss of accuracy, as demonstrated in previous studies.23 25

For easier interpretation, we computed the marginal rate ratios (RR) for SI by VOC, which was achieved through creating linear combinations between the fixed effect of SI and the interaction terms using the built-in function inla.make.lincombs in R-INLA.26 ψ is the overdispersion parameter.

We multiplied the expected rate of an outcome with an estimated probability of infections tested positive and variant typed (pij to adjust for the varying testing and variant typing rate, following the approach described in Adhikari et al).12 This probability was modelled using a fixed intercept and testing rate parameter multiplied by the time-varying, area-specific testing and variant-typing percentage. This latent model was incorporated using an INLA built-in function, inla.rgeneric.define().27

To account for correlation and unobserved heterogeneity in outcomes between proximate space and time, the model incorporated a spatial random effect, vij, and a temporal random effect, uij.28 29 The spatial random effect vij was implemented using the Besag-York-Mollié 2 (BYM2) parameterisation for higher model identifiability.29 We examined several distributions for the temporal effect u, including random walk of order 1 (RW1) and 2 (RW2) and an auto-regressive distribution with order 1 (AR1). The model performance was evaluated using the Watanabe–Akaike information criterion (WAIC) and the Conditional Predictive Ordinate (CPO).30 31 Standard normal priors were given for all fixed coefficients and the coefficients for testing; a weakly informative Gamma prior (with shape and rate both equal to 0.001) was set for the precision of the temporal random effect. Finally, the precision and mixing parameter of the spatial random effect vij were given using penalised complexity priors, where P(1τv>0.50.31)=0.01 and P(ϕ<0.5)=23, respectively.32 33

In total, we conducted four sensitivity analyses. First, we explored the effect of using vaguer priors for the precision of the fixed coefficients (normal(0, 0.1)) and the mixing parameter for the spatial random effect (P(ϕ<0.5)=12), and a prior that favours smoothness over time for the precision of the temporal random effect (Gamma(1,0.01)). Second, we examined the impact of adjusting for partial vaccine coverage, instead of full vaccine coverage, on the associations between SI and the outcomes. Third, to investigate the effect of including the probability of infections tested positive and variant typed on the results, we repeated the analysis using a model unadjusted for testing. Finally, to explore the correlation between the Alpha and Gamma waves, we fitted models that included only one of two VOCs along with Delta and Omicron.

All analyses were conducted using the statistical software R (V.4.4.1).34 Models were fitted using the package R-INLA (V.24.06.27).35 All implemented codes can be accessed in the study’s GitHub repository.

Results

There were 312 993 COVID-19 cases confirmed by laboratory tests from 1 January 2021 to 27 February 2022 in BC (figure 1). Among these samples, 172 200 were excluded if there were no typed variant or if the infection was due to Beta VOC. An additional 13 065 samples were excluded for exclusion criteria described in Data sources. A total of 127 728 samples were included in the study, which were distributed across all 231 CHSA in BC.

Figure 2A presents the distribution of COVID-19 cases by VOC across the study period. At the beginning of the study period (4 January 2021–21 March 2021), where most cases were non-VOC, the proportion of variant typed positive tests was 29%. During the concurrent phase of Alpha and Gamma (22 March 2021–4 July 2021), 78% of the positive tests were variant typed, followed by 71% variant typed in the Delta wave (5 July 2021–12 December 2021), and decreased to 24% from the start of the Omicron wave (13 December 2021) to the end of the study period (27 February 2022). Additionally, there were a total of 10.9 million doses of vaccines administered in the province (figure 2B). Throughout the study period, full and partial vaccine coverage rose from 0% to over 82% and 87%, respectively (figure 2B).

Online supplemental table S2 presents the model diagnostic statistics for various spatial models. We chose the BYM2-RW2 as the base model as it has overall better fit (lower WAIC and CPO). The cumulative observed number of the outcomes across weeks is almost always within the 95% credible intervals (CrI) of the model estimates (online supplemental figure S1), indicating the posterior predictive distribution well describes the data.

Table 1 shows the estimated fixed effects and online supplemental table S3 summarises the posterior distribution of the hyperparameters. A 10% increase in full vaccine coverage was associated with 12% (95% CrI: 10%, 14%) reduced rate of cases and 15% (95% CrI: 9%, 20%) reduced rate of deaths. Most interactions between the SI and VOC included 1 within the 95% CrI, patterns in the estimated effects suggest that the impact of SI may differ by VOC. Table 2 presents the marginal RR of SI by presence of a given VOC. When VOC was absent, SI had a negative association with all four outcomes: a one unit increase of SI (higher socioeconomic status) was associated with 4% (95% CrI: 0%, 7%) reduction in cases, 8% (95% CrI: 2%, 13%) reduction in hospitalisations, 13% (95% CrI: 5%, 20%) reduction in ICU admissions and 13% (95% CrI: 7%, 18%) reduction in deaths. In the presence of all VOCs except Omicron, the associations between SI and cases were in the same direction and their protective effect generally increased. For example, in the presence of Delta, a one unit increase of SI corresponded to an 18% (95% CrI: 12%, 24%) decrease in ICU admissions, as compared with 13% (95% CrI: 5%, 20%) in the absence of VOC. We report the low precision observed for the association between SI and deaths in the presence of Gamma, with a one unit increase of SI associated with 7% (95% CrI: −10%, 21%) reduction in deaths.

The association between SI and cases changed direction in the presence of Omicron: a one-unit increase of SI corresponded to a 3% (95% CrI: –1%, 7%) increase in cases. For hospitalisations, ICU admissions and deaths, the associations remained in the same direction as when VOC was absent, but the magnitude decreased. For example, in the presence of Omicron, a one unit increase of SI was associated with a 10% (95% CrI: 1%, 18%) reduction in ICU admissions, as compared with a 13% (95% CrI: 5%, 20%) in the absence of VOC.

The sensitivity analysis using different priors provided very similar results to the main analysis (online supplemental table S4). Including partial vaccine coverage, instead of the full vaccine coverage, as a covariate produced similar results as the main analysis (online supplemental file 1). The model unadjusted for testing generally led to smaller differences in marginal RR in the presence and absence of VOC, but results remained generally the same quantitatively (online supplemental table S6). Notably, the association between SI and cases was negative in the presence of Omicron, as compared with being positive in the main analysis. When either Alpha or Gamma was excluded, the marginal RR was slightly lower than the main analysis (online supplemental tables S7 and S8).

Discussion

We fitted Bayesian spatiotemporal models separately to COVID-19 cases, hospitalisations, ICU admissions and deaths within a large, retrospective cohort in BC, Canada. This study constitutes a strong, robust investigation of the associations between SDoH and COVID-19 outcomes by VOC over space and time. We found that the associations between SDoH and COVID-19 outcomes could be moderated by VOC. Specifically, the socioeconomic disparities in disease burden were heightened by the presence of Alpha, Gamma and Delta, but were generally dampened by the presence of Omicron. In the aftermath of the COVID-19 pandemic in BC, our findings highlight the need for continuing surveillance of VOC, especially among socially and economically disadvantaged groups. In case of seasonal upticks or pandemic resurgence, public health strategies should be adapted to consider both structural-level drivers of inequalities and circulating VOC among the population. The observed protective effect of increased vaccine coverage reinforces the critical role of vaccination programmes and supports continued investment in improving booster dose coverage.

The differential associations between SDoH and disease burden by VOC could be due to differing mode of transmission, housing and labour conditions, structural barriers to health, public policies, vaccine-mediated and infection-derived immunity across epidemic waves driven by each VOC.36 37 For instance, during highly transmissive VOC waves, public-facing roles, which are generally linked to lower socioeconomic status,12 38 might experience more pronounced outcomes compared with the rest of the population, especially when distancing measures are in place. Similarly, comorbidities prevalent among socially and economically disadvantaged groups might exacerbate the severity of VOC with certain pathogenetic profiles, leading to higher hospitalisation and death rates.39 Immunity acquired through vaccine and/or prior infection also varied across VOC waves. The high vaccine coverage (~80%) at the beginning of the Omicron wave, combined with Omicron’s broader community transmission and changes in clinical presentation,4 might explain, to some degree, the reverse direction of the association between SDoH and cases in the presence of Omicron. The shift from laboratory-based testing towards self-administered rapid antigen testing also resulted in the underreporting of variant-typed cases during this period (only 24% typed), which may have introduced selection bias.

Compared with the main analysis, the model unadjusted for the number of laboratory tests produced lower associations between SDoH and all four outcomes by VOC. This potentially reflects the changing testing strategies during the study period,7 as well as the disproportionate testing rates across diverse sociodemographic populations. Previous analysis on the cohort has shown that a higher odds of testing was associated with females, younger ages and lower material deprivation.17 The over-representation of individuals with higher socioeconomic status in the unadjusted model, who had better access to testing and vaccination programmes,40 41 could have resulted in underestimating the socioeconomic disparities in the outcomes.

This study has several limitations. First, we did not account for policy measures and case importations in the study region, meaning there could be misclassification of cases or uncertainty in the outcomes. However, since strong restrictions—including a ban on dine-in services and indoor fitness—only lasted for approximately 2 months at the start of the pandemic in the study region and most of the major social distancing orders and travel restrictions were issued at the provincial or federal level,6 these factors were expected to have impacted CHSA uniformly. Second, the ecological study design inherently limits our ability to draw individual-level inference from the observed area-level associations. The use of aggregate data also prevents adjustment for individual-level confounders, such as mobility between CHSA and waning vaccine-mediated and infection-induced immunity, which could result in spatial misclassification and residual confounding. While we attempted to mitigate this by adjusting for key region-level covariates and incorporating detailed spatial random effects, the residual spatial autocorrelation in certain areas and the persistence of temporal trends indicate that important unadjusted factors likely remain, warranting further investigation into unmeasured and unadjusted spatially and temporally correlated factors. Third, although we adjusted for the probability of infections tested positive and variant typed through WGS or qPCR SNP screening, there could be remaining bias considering VOCs’ different pathogenicity and highly varying testing and variant typing strategies across the study period.7 42 The bias was also likely to affect the four outcomes differently, with cases being the most common outcome and more sensitive to changes in the strategies and immunity2 as compared with less reported and more severe outcomes such as hospitalisations, ICU admission and deaths, which might be more comparable over time.

The strengths of this study include using a large, population-based data platform with high quality. The WGS/SNP technologies for variant typing were highly sensitive and robust, resulting in little misclassification of VOC.43 44 Additionally, the spatiotemporal modelling approach accounted for geographical heterogeneity of cases, SDoH characteristics, public health interventions and access to care,23 a crucial step towards enhancing model accuracy and health equity.37 Finally, constructing a SI allowed us to incorporate diverse axes of SDoH, while avoiding multicollinearity and overfitting issues.

Conclusions

This study provides robust evidence of the moderating role of VOC on the associations between SDoH and COVID-19 outcomes. In comparison to non-VOC, VOC generally led to a more disproportionate burden of cases and severe outcomes on socially and economically disadvantaged populations. Our findings underscore the necessity for tailoring and optimising pandemic preparedness and response strategies to structural-level drivers of inequalities and pathogenetic profiles in BC and across Canada.
