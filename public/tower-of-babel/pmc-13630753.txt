Thirty years of paediatric colonic motility assessment modalities: a systematic review
Authors: Assia Comella, Hannah Evans-Barns, Phil Dinning, Mark Safe, Misel Trajanovska, Sebastian K. King
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionDisorders of paediatric colonic motility may arise following operative repair of congenital colorectal conditions, as well as spontaneously. This may lead to symptoms of faecal incontinence, in addition to constipation. Management of such typically requires several investigations, clinic appointments and treatment strategies. Different investigation modalities are available to objectively and accurately describe gastrointestinal transit time. In the paediatric setting, most of these investigations are invasive, involve radiation, or require patient hospital admission. This systematic review summarises paediatric colonic motility investigations by compiling an assessment of modalities available over the last 31 years, their uses, advantages, and limitations.MethodsThe following databases (Cochrane, EMBASE, MEDLINE) were interrogated. The keywords used were “paediatric”, “pediatric”, “colonic motility”, and “investigation”, searched as keywords and Medical Subject Heading/Emtree subject heading. Studies that were included were case reports, prospective and retrospective cohort studies, case series, and reviews discussing and comparing modalities to investigate paediatric (< 18 years old) colonic motility. Inclusion criteria included papers published in the last 31 years, including data about paediatric colonic motility investigations indications, rationale for investigation of choice, advantages and limitations. The protocol was registered with PROSPERO (ID 1034832). Study selection was conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) protocol.ResultsThe initial search yielded 1061 papers. After removing duplicates and screening abstracts and full texts, 122 papers were included for analysis. The most widely-used motility investigation types were radiopaque marker studies, scintigraphy and colonic manometry. However, these had limitations, including radiation exposure, the need for an experienced-centre, and invasiveness. Hydrocolonic ultrasonography was radiation-free, not expensive, and safely repeatable for serial monitoring or assessment, but results were operator-dependent. Newer non-invasive tests that aim to limit radiation exposure have been emerging, such as cine-MRI, wireless capsule and the electromagnetic capsule tracking system. Assessment of anorectal function was often combined with colonic motility assessment to provide a full assessment, depending upon the clinical picture and diagnostic objectives.DiscussionOver the last 31 years numerous investigations have been utilised to study bowel anatomy, function, and neuromuscular ability. Each of these modalities have advantages, but also limitations. It is crucial to know the intrinsic characteristics of each modality to help decide which is most appropriate for the clinical indication in question. Radiopaque marker studies, scintigraphy and colonic manometry were the three most widely reported colonic motility studies methods. Evidence regarding colonic motility modalities in paediatrics was limited compared with the adult population. This lack of evidence is likely due to the invasive nature of some of these investigations, costs, radiation exposure, and limited availability at specific centres. It is crucial for clinicians involved in paediatric patient care to be familiar with available tests, their properties, availabilities, and to use the most appropriate investigation to answer each specific clinical question.ConclusionsThis systematic review has shown that, to date, there are several colonic motility assessment modalities that all have their advantages, limitations and different indications. It is imperative for clinicians to base their investigative plan on the specific patient, clinical/centre expertise and clinical question. In the future, there is a need to develop a non-invasive, objective, world-wide reproducible and ambulatory method that may provide information on actual day-to-day physiological colonic motility.Systematic Review Registrationhttps://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/view/CRD420251034832, PROSPERO CRD420251034832.

Introduction

Faecal incontinence in the paediatric population may have several aetiologies. Some of these are post-surgical repair of congenital colonic conditions, such a Hirschsprung disease (HD) or anorectal malformation (ARM). About 50% of children post-surgical repair for HD experience faecal incontinence (1). Patients post-ARM repair are reported to experience defective bowel control in 20%–40% of cases (2). Faecal incontinence may also present as constipation without any prior surgical intervention. This is a common presenting complaint for paediatric patients to see their general practitioner, representing 3% of presentations (3). This percentage is even higher when focusing upon paediatric gastroenterologists visits, with 25% of presentations (4–6). As the causes of constipation vary, management strategies may be equally diverse, often requiring multiple investigations and clinic appointments. To optimise targeted therapies, evaluating colonic motility may be crucial, especially in constipated patients that fail to respond to conventional medical therapies (3).

Over the years, several investigations have been developed to study bowel anatomy, function, and neuromuscular capability. Modalities vary globally, as different countries prioritise specific tools to objectively measure gastrointestinal transit time and inform personalised therapy. These investigations are chosen based on practicality, availability, invasiveness and the clinical questions to be answered (3). While each modality offers distinct advantages, they also possess limitations. Ideally, an investigation should be specific, sensitive, and capable of guiding management, with minimal costs or side effects. However, no current investigation is perfect, and availability varies by centre. Furthermore, several well-established methods involve radiation, which must be minimised in the paediatric population (5). It is, therefore, vital to understand the characteristics of each modality to determine which is most appropriate for a given clinical indication.

The primary aim of this systematic review was to catalogue and critically appraise the diagnostic modalities, developed over the past 31 years, for assessing paediatric colonic motility. Over the years, colonic manometry in particular has become the gold standard for accurate colonic motility assessment. This tool has been constantly evolving and developing in the academic domain since the 1980s. It has then been further applied to clinical use, and in particular, in the paediatric population in the 1990s. Therefore, our systematic review explored colonic motility tools utilised since the 1990s, detailing the available assessments, focusing on their diagnostic yield, practical advantages, and current constraints (7, 8).

Methods

Study selection was conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) protocol (Figure 1) and registered with PROSPERO (ID 1034832) (9). Our search encompassed Cochrane, EMBASE and MEDLINE. The searched keywords were “paediatric”, “pediatric”, “colonic motility”, “investigation”. Medical Subject Heading/Emtree subject heading searches were exploded including the following terms: “paediatric”, “pediatric”; “colonic motility”, “gastrointestinal motility”, “gastrointestinal transit”, “myoelectric complex migrating”, “peristalsis”; “investigation” (Appendix 1). Reference lists were also assessed and relevant articles included (10).

-Ultrasonography (US) is a motility assessment tool that involves saline filling of the colon during transabdominal US assessment, providing real-time details about bowel anatomy and morphology that may affect patient's motility (ie. faecal impaction, colonic diameter). The test is radiation-free, less expensive compared with scintigraphy or colonic manometry, and provides information about volume and width of bowel loops, evaluating volume of fluid movement to clean the colon and haustra movement to assess colonic peristalsis. It is, though, limited in function-evaluation as it provides a small window of activity, with the risk of a too-short spatial and timeframe assessment to assess motility in order to capture High Amplitude Propagating Contractions (HAPC). The test is safely repeatable for serial monitoring/assessment, but it is operator-dependent with variable accuracy depending on the indications and study population. The percentage of articles reporting about this investigation in this review is 5% (14, 19, 47, 57, 71, 74, 77).

-Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) has been largely utilised to provide radiation-free motility assessment and to determine colonic length. This modality has had different applications and outcomes of interest, being able to be used as a transit study as well as dynamic contractility and anatomical assessment tool. Cine-MRIs are dynamic investigations that may assess whole gut peristalsis, as well as identify areas of stasis and abnormal contractility (112, 114). Images acquired by MRI have also been used as transit assessment tools looking at movement of ingested markers capsules filled with water and a gadolinium-based contrast agent or 19F-fluorine-labelled capsules (50, 130, 131). These investigations have the added benefit of being able to define colonic length and anatomical structures/abnormalities (132–134). These investigations are expensive, require specialised skills and the patients are required to be stationary for prolonged imaging times. The percentage of articles reporting about this investigation in this review is 2%.

Discussion

This review explored advantages and limitations of various motility investigations used in the last 31 years. Radiopaque marker studies, scintigraphy and colonic manometry are the three most widely used colonic motility investigations (3). Radiopaque marker studies involve ingestion over a day or consecutive days of one or more capsules. Abdominal radiographs are then performed, usually on day 3 and/or day 7 post-capsule ingestion, to assess the colonic course of the makers. In Australia an abdominal radiograph costs AUD$54.70 with a package of sitz markers averaging a cost of AUD$200–1,500, while scintigraphy has a cost of AUD$728.85 (135–140). Historically, radiopaque markers testing is a widely-used investigation that has paediatric evidence supporting its validity (132, 141). This investigation though exposes children to radiation, is at risk of observer error and has poor definition of segmental colonic transit as a limited number of serial x-rays are taken (3, 46). Rybak et al. describe this investigation as an initial platform to ascertain which children might require further, potentially more invasive, assessment (142).

Scintigraphy provides useful information about whole gastrointestinal and segmental colonic transit, as well as gastric and small intestine function, while still being non-invasive as it involves swallowing of a radioactive capsule and monitoring of its passage throughout the whole intestine. Carmo et al. (21), through scintigraphy, showed that different portions of the colon have different functions, with the transverse colon working as reservoir and the descending colon as conduit. However, scintigraphy also exposes children to radiation, is expensive, is only provided at specialised centres for the paediatric population, and lacks age-specific normal values (140, 143). Limitations for radiopaque markers studies and scintigraphy studies also involve variation in protocols, which makes comparison of data difficult across different centres. For example, Popescu et al. presented a clear summary of the different ROM protocols that are available, and Rajundrajith et al. discussed variation in protocols and investigation reporting (3, 137, 144). Low compliance with the test and risk of loss-to-follow up are present with both radiopaque studies and scintigraphy. Papadopulos et al. (137) reported the ESPGHAN/NASPGHAN 2011 evidence-based recommendations, which discouraged the use of scintigraphy as a routine investigation for children with functional constipation.

Colonic manometry in a paediatric setting involves a general anaesthetic and hospital admission for placement of a catheter per rectum or per stoma in the colon. The catheter may be water-perfused or a solid-catheter (142). Since the early 2000s the advance of high-resolution colonic manometry, with sensors placed at only 1–2 cm distance from each other, has become the gold-standard for assessing colonic neuromuscular function in children (8, 145, 146). Colonic manometry provides evaluation of segmental colonic motor function, potentially discriminating between functional and neuromuscular causes of constipation. This is a very useful investigation, but it has some limitations: it is invasive and expensive and therefore of limited availability, requiring a general anaesthetic and hospital admission, as well as specialised equipment and staff. Acquisition of the equipment and software to perform high resolution colonic manometry may range from AUD$20,000 to AUD$100,000 (147). Bowel preparation is required prior to the test, raising the question if the data obtained are truly representative of the daily physiological state of the colon.

Most importantly, there is a lack of paediatric control data with colonic manometry as a healthy paediatric cohort has never been investigated due to the invasiveness of the test and the unethical nature of the potential study. Tipnis et al. and Dranove et al. compared the use of ROM and CM in the paediatric population. They concluded that that ROM has a role as a screening test to rule out colonic inertia though with limitations regarding segmental information. CM can then be used and become useful in further describing to drive specific management potentially distinguishing between functional and neuromuscular disorders (34, 101). Additionally, Rybak et al. agreed with Papadopulos et al. that, although there is accordance between scintigraphy and colonic manometry regarding constipation categorisation, scintigraphy suggestive of colonic inertia should undertake further assessment (with colonic manometry) prior to proceeding with medical or surgical management, as scintigraphy provides information about bulk transit without detailed assessment of motor patterns which may be crucial for targeted treatment (137, 142). Another limitation of colonic manometry is the lack of agreement on definition of “normal colonic motility”. American and European Consensuses have been drawn in the past to attempt to describe this, but there is still lack of standardised data reporting. This makes international comparison of findings challenging for an investigation that already has limitations in terms of number of centres being able to perform it and interpret it (87, 142, 148–150). Over the last 30 years, high-resolution colonic manometry (HRCM) has revolutionised the understanding of colonic motility, being able to directly assess bowel contractility and providing insight in colonic myogenic and neurogenic function (145, 151, 152). With sensors being placed just a few cm from each other, it has finally been possible to describe the complex interactions of different contractions (propagating vs. non propagating, anterograde vs. retrograde). This has then become the gold-standard investigation to assess colonic motility.

Over the years, attempts have been made to develop more specific and less invasive modalities to describe colonic motility. These have mainly been studied in the adult population, with only minimal data available in the paediatric population and predominantly in the research field. Cine-MRI is a non-invasive, radiation-free, dynamic high-resolution spatiotemporal investigation that allows visualisation of bowel diameter as well as information about colonic motility (153). However, this is an expensive modality that has limited clinical use and applicability currently as its data yield is still emerging and being defined with further studies needed to prove its clinical validity (142, 144, 148, 149, 154). Another assessment modality was the Wireless Capsule (SmartPill Corporation, Buffalo, NY), which was discontinued in 2023 due to ongoing challenges accessing its manufacturing components (41, 142, 155). Haase et al. first described the 3D-Transit system, which was compared with ROM and found to have better estimation of segmental transit time and provide details about colonic motility patterns (50, 156, 157). The 3D electromagnetic Capsule Tracking system is an ambulatory radiation-free system and has the ability to precisely assess the progression and tracking of multiple swallowed capsules, providing information about the whole gastrointestinal motility transit time with high spatial and temporal resolution (13). On the other hand, this device has mainly been used in the academic field, with limited paediatric data available (13, 50). In addition, the capsules have a standardised size with the potential to be difficult to swallow or to be retained in children (142). Lastly, surface electroenterography is a new emerging non-invasive tool that assesses colonic motility via US-guided placement of electrodes on the abdomen. This has mainly been used in the academic area and is yet to be validated in the paediatric population (89). All these emerging newer investigations strive to be non-invasive, artefact free and limit patient distress, while accurately assessing colonic physiology. However, there are limitations to their clinical use. They are costly and require specialised equipment and trained staff. Moreover, the large amount of data generated from these new investigations also poses a challenge in terms of length of data analysis. Therefore, the use of artificial intelligence (AI) to support analysis in this space is fast expanding (158).

In conjunction with colonic motility, anorectal function assessment is also crucial in patients that present with refractory constipation, as rectal distension may cause inhibition of colonic motor activity (5, 159, 160). Indeed the rectosigmoid function is crucial for stool evacuation, with this anatomical area having been identified as the physiological gatekeeper for stool evacuation through a physiological feedback regulatory mechanism (161). Anorectal manometry is a useful test to acquire information about anal sphincter pressure, sensory function, recto-anal inhibitory reflex (RAIR) and rectum-external sphincter defecation dynamics. If a 3D catheter is used, 3D dynamic representation of pressure changes in the anal canal may also be graphically represented. This modality often requires general anaesthesia in the younger paediatric population due to its invasive nature. As a result, only limited information may be gained as patient cooperation is needed for sensory testing and some dynamic components of the test. Defecography may provide information about morphological and dynamic defecation whilst the patient is in a physiological position. Similarly, contrast enema may provide evaluation of colonic morphology and dimensions, as well as having a therapeutic effect to empty the colon, but this provides little information about colonic motility.

This systematic review has limitations. Several of the articles lacked comparability as they are mainly case series or single arm cohort studies with small cohort of studied patients. Some of the articles also lacked details about length of patient follow-up and a comparator cohort, but most of them had clear and structured population selection and assessment of outcome, as highlighted by the Modified Newcastle-Ottawa scale assessment results. This systematic review only includes three randomised controlled trials, with a range of retrospective or prospective studies, case series or cohort or case control studies Due to the heterogeneity of included studies, patient populations, diagnostic protocols and outcome measures, only a qualitative narrative analysis could be performed with no meta-analysis. This reflects the lack of standardised protocols for these investigations, which poses a challenge to comparing international data and drawing conclusions to optimise patient management. Despite these limitations, this systematic review includes a wide range of articles, including 122 publications from multiple countries and centres across the world.

In summary, evidence regarding colonic motility modalities in paediatrics is scattered compared with the adult population. This lack of evidence is likely due to the invasive nature of some of these investigations, as well as costs, radiation exposure and limited availability at specific centres. Comparison and reproducibility of existing data is also complicated due to high variability in protocols and reporting. Based on the evidence, the authors conclude that site investigation availability and staff expertise pose the first limitations to the clinical use of each of these. Colonic manometry is currently the gold-standard to assess colonic motility. The emerging use of surface mapping provides a newer non-invasive alternative that can use electrical frequencies as surrogate markers to measure colonic motility. This has the potential to become an alternative to colonic manometry. If the clinical question requires transit information, scintigraphy provides important whole gut and segmental information. This review highlights that it is crucial for clinicians involved in patient care to be familiar with available tests, their properties, availabilities, and to use these investigations in conjunction with each other. This will ensure they have all relevant information to answer the clinical question and provide a targeted care plan for the specific patient

Conclusions

This systematic review showed that to date there are several colonic motility assessment modalities that all have their advantages, limitations and different indications. It is imperative for clinicians to base their investigative plan on the specific patient, clinical/centre expertise and clinical question. Clinicians should make patients and their families aware that this can be a lengthy process that might require a combination of different investigations in order to have the most optimal answer and individualised management. In the future, there is a need to develop a non-invasive, objective, world-wide reproducible and ambulatory method that may provide information on actual day-to-day physiological colonic motility. If such a modality was achieved, this could allow measurement in control healthy cohorts to produce normative data for comparison and optimally target differences.
