Introduction

Leukemia is a heterogeneous group of hematologic malignancies that disproportionately affect the elderly, with incidence and mortality rates rising sharply with age. As global populations continue to age, the burden of leukemia among older adults is expected to increase substantially. While therapeutic advances have improved outcomes for many leukemia patients, these benefits remain unevenly distributed. Elderly patients residing in underserved regions face unique challenges related to diagnostic delays, limited access to novel therapies, and systemic health inequities. These factors contribute to poorer prognoses and highlight the urgent need for tailored strategies to improve care equity [[1], [2]]. Geriatric oncology, a specialized field that addresses the complexities of cancer care in older adults, must reconcile the biological heterogeneity of aging with the diverse social and economic barriers prevalent in underserved settings. Aging is accompanied by physiological changes such as decreased organ function, immunosenescence, and increased comorbidity burden, which complicate leukemia diagnosis and treatment. Furthermore, psychosocial factors, including cognitive decline, social isolation, and financial constraints, disproportionately affect elderly patients, influencing treatment decisions and adherence. These age-related factors necessitate a personalized approach that extends beyond disease biology to incorporate holistic patient assessment [[3], [4]].

Diagnostic accuracy is the cornerstone of effective leukemia management but remains a significant hurdle in underserved regions. The gold standard diagnosis of leukemia often relies on advanced laboratory techniques including immunophenotyping, cytogenetics, and molecular profiling, which are frequently unavailable or centralized in distant urban centers. Elderly patients may present with atypical symptoms or concurrent illnesses that mask leukemia, causing delays in referral and diagnosis. These barriers are compounded by inadequate healthcare infrastructure and a shortage of trained hematologists and pathologists, particularly in low- and middle-income countries [[5], [6]]. Therapeutic disparities further compound the challenges faced by elderly leukemia patients in resource-poor areas. Although novel agents such as hypomethylating agents, targeted oral therapies (e.g., BCL-2 and BTK inhibitors), and reduced-intensity stem cell transplantation regimens have transformed leukemia care globally, their accessibility in underserved regions is limited by cost, supply chain issues, and regulatory hurdles. Additionally, clinical management is complicated by the frequent exclusion of elderly patients from clinical trials, resulting in a lack of age-specific efficacy and safety data. As a result, many elderly patients receive suboptimal or palliative care instead of potentially life-prolonging therapies [[7], [8]].

The integration of comprehensive geriatric assessment (CGA) into leukemia care represents a paradigm shift essential for optimizing treatment in older adults. CGA evaluates functional status, comorbidities, cognition, psychological health, nutrition, and social support, enabling individualized treatment plans that balance efficacy and tolerability. Despite its proven value, CGA is rarely implemented in underserved regions due to resource constraints and lack of trained personnel. Bridging this gap requires innovative approaches such as training non-specialist healthcare workers in geriatric principles and leveraging technology for remote assessments [[9], [10], [11]]. Emerging models of care that decentralize diagnostics and treatment to community settings show promise in enhancing access for elderly leukemia patients in underserved areas. Telemedicine platforms can facilitate specialist consultations and follow-up, overcoming geographic and transportation barriers. Mobile diagnostic units and sample referral networks help bring timely diagnostic services closer to patients’ homes. In parallel, expanding supportive care services—including psychosocial support, nutritional counseling, and caregiver education—can improve treatment adherence and quality of life, which are crucial determinants of outcomes in older patients [[11], [12], [13], [14]].

Aim

This review aims to critically analyze the current challenges and emerging strategies in geriatric oncology with a focus on elderly leukemia patients in underserved regions. It highlights barriers to diagnosis, treatment, and survival equity while discussing innovations and policy interventions necessary to advance access and improve outcomes.

Revised methods

This study was conducted as a narrative review with a structured and transparent literature search approach, designed to synthesize and critically appraise existing evidence on geriatric oncology, with a specific focus on leukemia care in underserved regions. While not a formal systematic review or meta-analysis, methodological rigor was enhanced through clearly defined search strategies, eligibility criteria, and study selection processes to improve reproducibility and minimize bias. A comprehensive literature search was undertaken across multiple electronic databases, including PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Web of Science, Scopus, the Cochrane Library, Global Health, and LILACS, covering publications from database inception through March 2024. To ensure breadth and contextual relevance, additional sources were identified through manual reference list screening, grey literature searches, and review of policy documents and organizational reports. The search strategy combined controlled vocabulary (e.g., MeSH and Emtree terms) with free-text keywords related to “leukemia,” “acute myeloid leukemia,” “chronic lymphocytic leukemia,” “acute lymphoblastic leukemia,” “older adults,” “geriatric,” “low- and middle-income countries,” “underserved,” “health equity,” and “access to care.” No strict date limits were imposed; however, emphasis was placed on studies published from 2000 onward to reflect contemporary clinical practice.

Eligibility criteria were defined a priori. Studies were included if they involved adult populations aged ≥60 years with any form of leukemia and were conducted in or relevant to low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), as classified by the World Bank. Both quantitative and qualitative study designs were considered, including observational studies, clinical trials, systematic reviews, and program evaluations. Articles addressing epidemiology, diagnostic access, treatment patterns, clinical outcomes (e.g., survival, response rates, toxicity), health system interventions, or cost considerations were eligible. Exclusion criteria comprised studies with predominantly younger populations without age-stratified analysis, studies conducted exclusively in high-income settings without contextual relevance, small case series, editorials, and conference abstracts lacking full data. All identified records were imported into a reference management system, and duplicate entries were removed prior to screening. Titles and abstracts were then reviewed for relevance, followed by full-text assessment of potentially eligible studies. Discrepancies in study selection were resolved through discussion and consensus. The study selection process is summarized in a PRISMA-style flow diagram, which enhances transparency in reporting. In total, 104 studies were included in the final synthesis after sequential of identification, screening, eligibility assessment, and exclusion based on predefined criteria.

Data extraction was conducted using a standardized framework capturing study characteristics (design, setting, population), leukemia subtype, diagnostic approaches, treatment modalities, outcomes, and key findings. Given the heterogeneity in study designs, populations, and reported outcomes, a quantitative meta-analysis was not feasible. Instead, findings were synthesized using a thematic and comparative narrative approach, enabling integration of quantitative results where available alongside qualitative insights. To strengthen interpretive rigor, a qualitative appraisal of study quality was undertaken using appropriate tools aligned with study design (e.g., Newcastle–Ottawa Scale for observational studies, AMSTAR 2 for systematic reviews, and Joanna Briggs Institute tools for qualitative research). While formal grading of evidence was not performed, variability in methodological quality, risk of bias, and contextual limitations was explicitly considered in the synthesis and discussion. The final synthesis was organized across key domains, including epidemiology and disease burden, diagnostic access, treatment strategies, clinical outcomes, health system constraints, and policy implications. Quantitative data—such as survival estimates, treatment response rates, and regional disparities—were incorporated wherever available to support evidence-based conclusions. This structured yet flexible methodology ensures that the review provides both analytical depth and contextual relevance, aligning with the study’s objective of advancing equitable geriatric leukemia care in underserved settings (Fig. 1).

Diagnostic challenges in elderly leukemia patients in underserved regions

Accurate and timely diagnosis of leukemia in elderly patients is fundamental to effective treatment and improved outcomes. However, in underserved regions, diagnostic challenges are particularly pronounced due to limitations in healthcare infrastructure, scarcity of specialized laboratory services, and a shortage of trained hematologists and pathologists. These deficits often lead to delayed or inaccurate diagnoses, which can significantly worsen prognosis in a disease that requires rapid intervention [[15], [16]]. Leukemia diagnosis relies heavily on sophisticated laboratory techniques such as flow cytometry, cytogenetic analysis, and molecular profiling to differentiate leukemia subtypes, determine prognosis, and guide targeted therapies. In many resource-limited settings, these technologies are either unavailable or centralized in distant urban hospitals, resulting in prolonged turnaround times for test results or the necessity for patients to travel long distances. For elderly patients, who may have limited mobility and social support, such barriers often translate into missed appointments, delayed care initiation, and disease progression [[17], [18]]. In addition to infrastructural constraints, the clinical presentation of leukemia in older adults can be atypical or subtle, further complicating diagnosis. Symptoms such as fatigue, anemia, and infections are common in aging and may be mistaken for other chronic illnesses or attributed to normal aging. Comorbidities prevalent in the elderly, including cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and chronic kidney disease, can mask hematologic abnormalities or complicate laboratory interpretations. This diagnostic ambiguity often leads to under-recognition or misclassification of leukemia in the elderly population [19].

Furthermore, limited awareness among primary care providers and community health workers regarding the unique presentation of leukemia in older adults contributes to diagnostic delays. In underserved regions, where specialized oncology services are sparse, frontline healthcare workers are frequently the first point of contact. Without adequate training in recognizing hematologic malignancies, especially in the elderly, opportunities for early referral and diagnosis are missed [20]. Innovative approaches are needed to overcome these challenges. Developing decentralized diagnostic networks, including mobile laboratory units and sample transport systems, can bring essential testing closer to patients. Telepathology and telehematology services enable remote expert consultation, reducing dependence on local expertise. Additionally, simplified, low-cost diagnostic assays adapted for low-resource environments are being explored to facilitate faster, point-of-care leukemia screening [21]. Training programs aimed at improving the recognition of leukemia symptoms in older adults among primary healthcare providers are also critical. Such programs can promote earlier suspicion, prompt referral, and improved diagnostic timelines. Together, these strategies can help mitigate the diagnostic barriers faced by elderly leukemia patients in underserved regions and lay the groundwork for timely, appropriate treatment interventions (Table 1) [22].

Therapeutic barriers and treatment disparities

Treatment of leukemia in elderly patients presents a complex clinical challenge that is further intensified in underserved regions by multiple therapeutic barriers and systemic disparities. Elderly patients often have diminished organ reserve, increased comorbidities, and frailty, which limit their ability to tolerate intensive chemotherapy or hematopoietic stem cell transplantation. In resource-poor settings, these inherent clinical complexities are compounded by limited access to modern treatment modalities, lack of supportive care infrastructure, and socioeconomic constraints, leading to suboptimal therapy and poorer outcomes [23]. One of the foremost barriers to effective treatment in underserved regions is the limited availability and affordability of newer, less toxic therapies that have revolutionized leukemia care globally. Hypomethylating agents (HMAs), targeted oral therapies such as tyrosine kinase inhibitors, BCL-2 inhibitors, and Bruton’s tyrosine kinase inhibitors, as well as reduced-intensity conditioning regimens for transplant, offer improved efficacy with reduced toxicity profiles compared to conventional chemotherapy. However, high costs, inconsistent drug supply chains, and regulatory challenges often restrict their availability in low- and middle-income countries, preventing elderly patients from receiving these potentially life-prolonging treatments [7].

Furthermore, treatment decision-making for elderly leukemia patients in underserved regions is frequently hampered by the lack of comprehensive geriatric assessment (CGA) tools and expertise. CGA helps stratify patients based on functional status, comorbidities, cognitive function, and psychosocial factors, guiding personalized therapy that balances efficacy with quality of life. In many resource-limited settings, such assessments are rarely performed, resulting in therapeutic nihilism or blanket treatment approaches based solely on chronological age rather than physiological fitness. Consequently, many older adults are either undertreated with supportive care alone or exposed to unnecessarily aggressive regimens that increase toxicity risk [[7], [24]]. Supportive care services critical to treatment success—including transfusion support, antimicrobial prophylaxis, and management of treatment-related side effects—are often underdeveloped or unavailable in underserved regions. This gap exacerbates treatment-related morbidity and mortality, discouraging healthcare providers and patients from pursuing curative-intent therapies. Moreover, cultural beliefs, health literacy, and financial toxicity influence treatment acceptance and adherence, further widening disparities [24].

Efforts to mitigate these therapeutic barriers include implementing cost-reduction strategies such as generic drug production, government subsidy programs, and international aid partnerships to improve access to essential medications. Training healthcare providers in geriatric oncology principles and establishing multidisciplinary care teams can optimize treatment planning and toxicity management. Additionally, expanding infrastructure for supportive care and palliative services enhances patient quality of life and facilitates adherence (Table 2) [[25], [26]].

Innovations and emerging solutions

Addressing the multifaceted challenges faced by elderly leukemia patients in underserved regions requires innovative approaches that transcend traditional healthcare models. Recent advances in diagnostics, treatment delivery, and care coordination offer promising avenues to improve equity and outcomes for this vulnerable population. By leveraging technology, adapting clinical protocols, and fostering collaborative frameworks, these emerging solutions aim to overcome systemic barriers while accommodating the unique needs of older adults [27]. One significant innovation is the decentralization of diagnostic services through mobile laboratories, point-of-care testing, and telemedicine platforms. Mobile diagnostic units equipped with essential hematology and molecular testing capabilities can reach remote or resource-limited areas, reducing delays caused by transportation and sample referral. Telepathology and telehematology enable remote expert interpretation of diagnostic specimens, allowing for timely and accurate leukemia subtyping critical for tailored therapy. Such technologies also facilitate remote multidisciplinary tumor boards, improving decision-making despite limited local specialist availability [28].

In the therapeutic domain, oral targeted therapies and hypomethylating agents have transformed the treatment landscape for elderly leukemia patients by offering effective, less intensive options compatible with outpatient administration. Efforts to improve affordability and availability of these agents in underserved regions include partnerships with generic drug manufacturers, patient assistance programs, and advocacy for inclusion in national essential medicines lists. Additionally, novel dosing schedules and treatment protocols tailored for resource-limited settings are being explored to maximize benefits while minimizing toxicity and healthcare resource use [29]. Comprehensive geriatric assessment (CGA) is increasingly recognized as a cornerstone of personalized oncology care. Emerging solutions involve integrating simplified CGA tools into primary healthcare workflows and training non-specialist providers to perform basic geriatric screening. Digital health platforms and mobile applications can support remote CGA data collection and monitoring, enabling dynamic assessment of functional status and treatment tolerance. These tools facilitate shared decision-making, ensuring that treatment choices reflect patients’ physiological fitness, preferences, and social context [30].

Another promising approach is the use of telemedicine to bridge geographic and specialist gaps. Virtual consultations allow elderly patients to access expert hematology and geriatric oncology care without the burdens of travel, while remote monitoring supports early detection and management of treatment complications. Telehealth also enhances caregiver education and psychosocial support, which are essential components of comprehensive cancer care for older adults [31]. Collaborative international consortia and clinical research networks focused on underserved populations are expanding opportunities for elderly patients to participate in clinical trials. Inclusion of diverse demographic groups ensures that evidence-based treatment guidelines better reflect the heterogeneity of real-world populations. Additionally, research efforts aimed at understanding the biology of leukemia in the elderly and identifying biomarkers of treatment response are critical for developing age-appropriate therapies [32].

Community engagement and health system strengthening are vital for sustainable progress. Educational initiatives targeting healthcare workers and the public can raise awareness of leukemia symptoms and the importance of early diagnosis. Health policy reforms to improve financing, drug procurement, and workforce capacity are necessary to institutionalize access improvements. Together, these innovations and emerging solutions offer a hopeful path toward closing the gaps in care for elderly leukemia patients in underserved regions (Fig. 2) [33].

Innovations and Emerging Solutions.

Policy and health system strategies to enhance equity

Achieving equitable leukemia care for elderly patients in underserved regions requires robust policy frameworks and health system reforms that address structural barriers and resource limitations. Effective policies must prioritize the unique needs of geriatric oncology patients by ensuring sustainable financing, strengthening healthcare infrastructure, and fostering inclusive health governance. Such strategies are critical to bridging the persistent gaps in diagnosis, treatment access, and supportive care [34]. One pivotal approach is the integration of geriatric oncology into national cancer control plans and non-communicable disease strategies. By explicitly recognizing the aging population’s cancer burden, governments can allocate dedicated funding and resources toward age-specific cancer care programs. This includes expanding access to essential diagnostics, novel therapeutics, and supportive services tailored to older adults. Policy frameworks should also promote equitable distribution of healthcare facilities and specialists to reduce urban-rural disparities that disproportionately affect elderly patients in remote areas [[35], [36]].

Universal health coverage (UHC) models play a central role in reducing financial barriers to leukemia care. Policymakers must work to include essential leukemia treatments—such as hypomethylating agents, targeted therapies, and supportive medications—within publicly funded health insurance schemes. Subsidizing or negotiating lower prices for high-cost drugs through pooled procurement mechanisms and partnerships with pharmaceutical companies can enhance affordability. Furthermore, reducing out-of-pocket expenses for elderly patients and their families mitigates treatment abandonment and improves adherence [37]. Strengthening the health workforce is another cornerstone of system-level equity strategies. Training programs focused on geriatric oncology should be incorporated into medical education curricula and continuing professional development to expand the pool of healthcare providers equipped to care for elderly leukemia patients. Task-shifting initiatives, which empower nurses and community health workers to conduct basic geriatric assessments and follow-up care, can extend service reach in resource-limited settings. Support for multidisciplinary care teams facilitates comprehensive management addressing medical, functional, and psychosocial needs [38].

Health information systems must be enhanced to capture data on cancer incidence, treatment outcomes, and patient demographics stratified by age and region. Robust surveillance enables identification of disparities and monitoring of intervention impact, informing evidence-based policy adjustments. Digital health technologies can streamline data collection and support decision-making at all health system levels [39]. International collaborations and public-private partnerships are essential for mobilizing resources, sharing expertise, and scaling successful models of geriatric leukemia care. Global initiatives should advocate for the inclusion of elderly patients from underserved regions in clinical research to generate relevant evidence and guide guidelines. Additionally, leveraging philanthropic funding and innovative financing mechanisms can supplement government efforts to improve access to diagnostics and treatments [40].

Conceptual framework for equitable geriatric leukemia care in underserved settings

Equitable care for older adults with leukemia in underserved settings requires a shift from fragmented, disease-centered approaches toward an integrated model that simultaneously accounts for biological aging, disease heterogeneity, and health system constraints. The proposed conceptual framework is anchored on three interdependent domains—patient-level vulnerability, disease-level complexity, and system-level capacity—operating within a broader socio-economic and policy environment that ultimately determines access, quality, and outcomes [41]. At the patient level, the framework recognizes that chronological age is an insufficient determinant of treatment decision-making. Instead, biological age, frailty status, functional reserve, cognitive capacity, comorbidity burden, and social support systems define an individual’s therapeutic window. In underserved regions, these dimensions are often further shaped by late presentation, malnutrition, and limited caregiver resources. Embedding geriatric assessment—whether comprehensive or in adapted, resource-sensitive formats— into routine leukemia care allows clinicians to stratify patients into fit, vulnerable, or frail categories. This stratification is critical for aligning treatment intensity with tolerance, thereby minimizing toxicity while preserving efficacy. Importantly, the framework emphasizes that geriatric assessment should not be viewed as an optional add-on but as a core clinical tool guiding equitable decision-making [[42], [43]].

The second domain, disease-level complexity, reflects the biological and clinical heterogeneity of leukemias in older adults. Acute myeloid leukemia, chronic lymphocytic leukemia, and other hematologic malignancies exhibit distinct molecular profiles, risk stratification schemas, and therapeutic pathways, all of which are further modified by aging biology. In high-resource settings, management increasingly relies on cytogenetics, molecular diagnostics, and targeted therapies. However, in underserved environments, limited access to these technologies constrains risk-adapted care. The framework therefore introduces a tiered diagnostic and therapeutic approach, wherein essential, intermediate, and advanced levels of care are defined based on available resources. At the essential level, emphasis is placed on accurate morphological diagnosis and clinically guided treatment selection; at higher tiers, integration of molecular diagnostics and targeted therapies is pursued as capacity allows. This stratified model aims to optimize clinical benefit within existing constraints rather than imposing unattainable standards [[20], [44]]. The third domain, health system capacity, represents the most decisive determinant of equitable care. This includes the availability of trained personnel, diagnostic infrastructure, essential medicines, referral systems, and financial protection mechanisms. In many underserved regions, leukemia care is hindered by workforce shortages, centralized services, and high out-of-pocket costs. The framework conceptualizes health systems not as static limitations but as modifiable platforms for intervention. Strategies such as task-shifting, decentralization of services through regional hubs, telemedicine support, and integration of oncology into primary healthcare are positioned as practical levers to expand access. Additionally, the framework underscores the importance of supply chain resilience and inclusion of key leukemia therapies in national essential medicines lists, ensuring that therapeutic advances translate into real-world impact [[45], [46]].

These three domains are embedded within a cross-cutting layer of socioeconomic and cultural determinants, which shape health-seeking behavior, treatment adherence, and decision-making. In many contexts, older adults rely heavily on family support, and treatment choices are influenced by financial considerations, cultural perceptions of aging, and trust in healthcare systems. The framework therefore incorporates shared decision-making models adapted to local realities, ensuring that care remains patient-centered while acknowledging contextual constraints [47]. At the outermost level, the framework is supported by policy and governance structures, which define the extent to which equitable care can be realized. National cancer control plans, health financing policies, regulatory environments, and international collaborations collectively determine resource allocation and system priorities. The framework advocates for the explicit inclusion of geriatric oncology within national and global cancer strategies, coupled with investments in data systems, research capacity, and implementation science. Without policy alignment, even well-designed clinical interventions are unlikely to achieve scale or sustainability [48]. A defining feature of this framework is its dynamic and iterative nature. Improvements in one domain—such as expanded diagnostic capacity—can shift the balance across others, enabling more precise treatment and better outcomes. Conversely, weaknesses in any single domain can undermine the entire continuum of care. As such, the framework encourages continuous evaluation, context-specific adaptation, and integration of emerging innovations, including digital health tools and real-world data systems [49].

Older adults with leukemia face intersecting challenges: biologically aggressive disease, frailty, comorbidities, and major barriers to accessing high-quality care, especially in underserved regions. Research across leukemia types and care settings points to recurring building blocks that can be assembled into a practical framework for equitable geriatric leukemia care. Comprehensive geriatric assessment (CGA) to evaluate frailty, cognition, comorbidities, function, and social support, guiding individualized therapy and supportive care [[41], [42]]. Multidisciplinary teams linking hematology, geriatrics, palliative care, nursing, rehab, pharmacy, and social work, with shared decision-making centered on quality of life and patient goals [[41], [42]]. Early, integrated palliative and supportive care to manage high symptom burden and maintain dignity in late/refractory disease. Outpatient, home care, telemedicine, and psychosocial programs to reduce travel, hospitalizations, and costs while improving access in rural/low-resource areas [[20], [43], [44]]. Telemedicine can widen access but must be evaluated for equity impacts (who can connect, what quality of care is delivered) [45].

Older adults in rural/poor areas and those using EDs report worse access and experiences, highlighting gaps in urgent and ambulatory care coordination [50]. Patient-reported experience (timeliness, communication, trust) varies by race/ethnicity and should be used as a quality and equity indicator in leukemia programs [51]. Conceptual access models and health-equity frameworks emphasize care as a sequence of people–system interactions over time, sensitive to innovations like telemedicine [48]. Resource-stratified geriatric hematology programs in low-resource settings; financing for multidisciplinary, community, and home-based services; and national guidelines embedding CGA and palliative care [52]. Targeted QI and provider training to address racial disparities, shared decision-making, and clinical trial access in community practices [52]. Research frameworks and mapping tools to identify stakeholders, stages, and gaps in geriatric/long-term care research, enabling context-specific solutions [53]. An equitable geriatric leukemia care framework in underserved settings rests on: geriatric-informed, multidisciplinary clinical care; community- and home-based delivery; systematic attention to access and patient experience; and supportive policy, financing, and research infrastructure. Together, these elements can move care from hospital-centric and disease-focused to person-centered, community-anchored, and equity-driven for older adults with leukemia (Table 3).

Different Barriers and Levers for Equity.

A critical examination of existing strategies to improve geriatric leukemia care in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs)

A critical examination of existing strategies to improve geriatric leukemia care in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) reveals a mixed landscape in which pragmatic innovations show promise, but their impact is often constrained by context-specific limitations and variable implementation fidelity. Among these, task-shifting, essential medicines policies, and the development of regional diagnostic hubs have emerged as frequently cited approaches to bridge gaps in access and delivery. However, their feasibility, scalability, and real-world effectiveness warrant closer scrutiny [55]. Task-shifting, defined as the redistribution of clinical responsibilities from highly specialized providers to trained non-specialist health workers, has demonstrated operational feasibility in several LMIC settings, particularly in HIV and primary care programs. Its application in oncology—and more specifically in geriatric leukemia care—is gaining traction as a response to severe workforce shortages [56]. Evidence suggests that with structured training and protocol-driven care pathways, nurses and general practitioners can effectively support components of leukemia management, including symptom monitoring, treatment adherence, and palliative care delivery. In geriatric populations, task-shifting also facilitates the incorporation of simplified geriatric assessment tools at lower levels of care. However, scalability remains uneven. Oncology care, unlike many chronic disease programs, involves higher clinical complexity, rapid decision-making, and management of treatment-related toxicities, which limits the extent to which responsibilities can be safely decentralized. Furthermore, regulatory constraints, professional resistance, and insufficient supervision frameworks often impede large-scale adoption. While pilot programs report improved access and patient satisfaction, robust outcome data—particularly survival benefits in leukemia—remain limited, underscoring the need for more rigorous evaluation [[57], [58]].

Essential medicines policies represent another cornerstone strategy aimed at improving affordability and availability of cancer therapeutics. The inclusion of key leukemia drugs—such as cytotoxic agents, corticosteroids, and selected targeted therapies—on national essential medicines lists (EMLs) has been associated with improved procurement prioritization and, in some contexts, reduced costs through pooled purchasing or generic substitution. In practice, however, the translation of policy into consistent access is far from uniform [59]. Many LMICs face persistent supply chain disruptions, limited fiscal space, and dependence on external donors, which compromise drug availability at the point of care. Moreover, while EML inclusion may cover older, off-patent agents, access to newer targeted therapies—critical for conditions such as FLT3-mutated AML or relapsed CLL—remains highly restricted [60]. Cost-effectiveness analyses, where available, suggest that certain low-intensity regimens and generics offer favorable value in older populations, particularly when aligned with geriatric assessment–guided treatment selection. Nonetheless, the broader impact of essential medicines policies on survival outcomes in elderly leukemia patients is difficult to quantify due to fragmented data systems and heterogeneity in implementation. Thus, while policy frameworks are necessary, they are insufficient without parallel investments in financing, procurement systems, and regulatory efficiency [16].

The establishment of regional diagnostic hubs has been proposed as a means to overcome the high capital and technical requirements of advanced leukemia diagnostics, including flow cytometry, cytogenetics, and molecular testing. Centralizing these services at strategically located centers can, in theory, improve diagnostic accuracy and enable risk-adapted treatment while maintaining cost efficiency through economies of scale. In several LMIC contexts, hub-and-spoke models have demonstrated feasibility, particularly when supported by public–private partnerships or international collaborations [61]. Turnaround times and diagnostic yield have improved in such settings, contributing to more appropriate treatment selection. However, scalability is contingent on reliable specimen transport systems, digital connectivity, and sustained technical expertise—all of which are variably developed across regions. Delays in sample referral, logistical bottlenecks, and inequities between urban and rural areas can attenuate the benefits of centralized diagnostics. Additionally, the clinical utility of advanced diagnostics is inherently linked to the availability of corresponding therapies; without access to targeted treatments, the incremental value of molecular stratification may be diminished in some settings. Outcome data remain limited, though early reports suggest improvements in diagnostic precision rather than clear survival gains, highlighting a gap between diagnostic advancement and therapeutic access (Table 4) [[62], [63]].

Selected Strategies and Their Feasibility in Low-Resource Settings.

Geriatric leukemia care in LMICs sits at the intersection of aging, cancer, and fragile health systems. Available research is mostly general to cancer or leukemia, not specific to older adults in LMICs, but it highlights several strategies and their limitations. Health-systems strengthening models using WHO “building blocks” improved pediatric leukemia survival in a Mexican public hospital by staffing a dedicated leukemia service, stabilizing drug supply, and adding supportive and psychosocial care; this is proposed as a transferable model for other LMIC cancers [55]. A national survey in Mexico showed major geographic and level-of-care inequities in access to core diagnostics (flow cytometry, karyotyping, NGS). Physicians ranked diagnostic and risk-stratification tests as the top barrier and top priority for improvement, highlighting the need for decentralized, subsidized diagnostics [56]. Reviews on cancer control and survivorship in LMICs emphasize strong primary care platforms, referral networks, and survivorship follow-up, with research focused on access, affordability, and quality improvement rather than “state-of-the-art” technologies [[57], [58], [59]].

For older AML, geriatric assessment (GA) predicts survival and tolerance and can personalize treatment intensity, but implementation is limited by workflow and training needs [63]. Global GA implementation work confirms multiple practical barriers and calls for tailored, context-specific strategies [62]. In LMIC AML, intensive chemotherapy is often unsafe due to weak supportive care (ICU, antimicrobials, transfusion), leading to high early mortality; authors recommend tailoring intensity to local capacity and expanding access to lower-intensity or oral regimens and cost-saving transplant strategies (e.g., outpatient transplants, biosimilars, haploidentical donors) [16]. For chronic leukemias in older adults, public-health-oriented strategies include risk-based screening, community-based interventions, subsidized access to TKIs/monoclonal antibodies, and national registries, judged generally feasible and impactful in low-resource settings [60]. Across LMIC cancers, supportive care interventions (psychosocial support, symptom management, patient navigation) improved quality of life despite fragile systems, suggesting high relevance for frail older leukemia patients [61].

Late-stage and refractory leukemias in older adults are more frequent and more lethal in LMICs because of underdiagnosis, delayed presentation, and limited targeted therapies and supportive care; region-specific strategies must strengthen diagnostics, equitable drug access, and age-adapted supportive care [[16], [60], [61]]. For refractory disease, early, integrated palliative care and community-based programs, addressing social determinants (transport, caregiver support, housing, nutrition), are highlighted to improve quality of life and reduce hospitalizations. Cancer research priority papers for LMICs stress implementation research, quality improvement, and value-based care, not high-cost technologies. There is almost no LMIC-specific empirical work on geriatric leukemia, representing a major evidence gap [63]. Existing strategies relevant to geriatric leukemia in LMICs cluster around health-system strengthening, decentralizing and subsidizing diagnostics, adapting treatment intensity to local capacity and patient frailty, and embedding robust supportive and palliative care. Multidisciplinary and geriatric-informed models are repeatedly endorsed but under-implemented due to workforce and workflow barriers. Evidence is largely indirect (from broader cancer or mixed-age leukemia populations), underscoring the urgent need for implementation and outcomes research specifically focused on older adults with leukemia in LMICs.

Evidence-based vs. aspirational interventions in equitable geriatric (Leukemia) cancer care in LMICs

Designing an equitable framework for older adults with leukemia in underserved settings requires distinguishing what already shows measurable benefit from what is conceptually promising but under-evaluated. The papers below collectively emphasize that most LMIC cancer interventions are still in pilot or early implementation phases, with limited robust outcome data. Health-systems strengthening programs for leukemia with workforce training, standardized protocols, and financing improved 5-year survival for pediatric ALL in Mexico (e.g., standard-risk 73%→100%) and multiple sustainability indicators [55]. Supportive care interventions (psychosocial support, symptom management, education, navigation) in LMICs improved mental health, physical symptoms, and quality of life in randomized/quasi-experimental studies [61]. Education and training of health workers in LMIC implementation projects consistently changed clinical processes and practice patterns, using low-cost, context-specific strategies [[64], [65]]. Some early-diagnosis interventions combining public campaigns and workforce training downstaged certain cancers and improved diagnostic intervals, though only in a minority of studies [65].

Patient navigation increased follow-up, adherence, screening, and retention, but no studies showed improved survival or long-term health outcomes [66]. Patient education programs improved knowledge and sometimes screening or adherence, but rarely used validated tools or tracked long-term clinical outcomes or cost-effectiveness [67]. Task-shifting, primary-care–oncology integration, and financing mechanisms are highlighted as evidence-based system strategies for cancer control in LMICs, though not geriatric- or leukemia-specific [[7], [68]]. Across NCD and cancer implementation research in LMICs, most studies focus on feasibility and adoption, with few evaluating sustainability, cost, or population-level effects [[69], [70], [71]]. Reviews urge adding implementation science outcomes and clinically relevant endpoints (survival, stage shift, toxicity, QoL) to future trials and scale-up efforts [71]. Use of frameworks such as RE-AIM, CFIR, and transferability models is recommended to choose context-appropriate strategies and define which local data are needed before spreading interventions to new settings [68]. In LMIC and underserved contexts, interventions with the clearest measurable impact for equitable leukemia/cancer care include health-systems strengthening programs, supportive care packages, and targeted workforce education. Patient navigation, education, and many geriatric-adapted, integrated models remain largely aspirational, supported by process but not robust clinical outcomes. Future work should embed strong implementation science and clinical endpoints to move these promising concepts into proven, scalable solutions for older adults with leukemia.

Global and regional quantitative patterns in (Older) leukemia populations

Several large burden-of-disease and registry studies provide quantitative and regional comparisons relevant to leukemia in older adults, including LMICs, though few are exclusively geriatric.

Global & LMIC burden in older adults

Among people 60–89 years, AML caused 61,559 new cases, 53,620 deaths, and 990,656 DALYs in 2019; incidence, mortality and DALYs in this age group have all risen over 30 years, especially in high-SDI regions [80]. For leukemia overall, incidence and mortality rise steeply with age and are higher in men globally [[81], [82]]. A leukemia quality of care index (QCI) shows strong positive association with SDI; QCI falls sharply at older ages, with the lowest values in 75–79-year old and in low-SDI countries [[83], [84]].

Country- and region-specific older leukemia data

Latin America (older AML): In a single-center Mexican cohort ≥55 years (IC vs low-dose cytarabine vs best supportive care), median OS was 4.9 vs 1.7 vs 1.2 months; intensive chemotherapy improved survival even in patients ≥70, while age and Charlson index were not independent predictors [85].

•LMIC CML (GIPAP): Among 33,985 patients in 94 LMICs, only 4.7% were ≥65; 3-year OS 89.4%. Age ≥65, delayed treatment >1-year, advanced phase, and being from Latin America or Africa were associated with worse survival [86].

Access, equity, and underserved older populations

In Mexico, access to key diagnostics (flow cytometry, karyotype, NGS) shows sharp urban–rural and tertiary–secondary gaps, with heavy out-of-pocket reliance outside major cities; physicians rank diagnostic access as the top barrier [56]. In British Columbia, older AML patients (≥60) living rurally were less likely to see a hematologist/oncologist and to receive azacitidine; among non-intensive-therapy patients, rural residence predicted worse overall survival (HR ∼1.2) [87]. In US Medicare data, older adults with leukemia/lymphoma who used the ED in year 1 reported significantly worse perceived access to care, and ED use was more common in rural and high-poverty areas [20]. A US AML cohort ≥70 showed substantial regional variation in intensive chemotherapy use (36–57%) and survival; differences were larger in older patients, with ∼10% survival variation unexplained after adjustment, suggesting practice-pattern inequity [88].

Financial and system-level inequalities (Broader Leukemia)

In 832 formerly poor Chinese counties, age-standardized leukemia incidence and mortality fell ∼60–70% from 2019–2024, but financial toxicity was severe: median out-of-pocket ratios reached ∼40% in Eastern China, near WHO catastrophic thresholds, with marked spatial clustering [89]. Global analyses confirm leukemia burden and QCI track closely with economic and educational development, and estimates are least reliable in low-SDI regions, underscoring data gaps for older adults in LMICs [90].

Quantitative studies show that older adults, particularly in LMICs and underserved regions, experience rising AML burden, lower quality-of-care indices, substantial regional variation in treatment intensity and diagnostics, and significant financial toxicity. High-SDI regions have better care quality but still marked age-related declines, while LMIC data (Latin America, Africa, parts of Asia) reveal younger presentation, delayed treatment, and worse survival. Evidence highlights diagnostics access, rural residence, socioeconomic status, and health-system capacity as key drivers of regional inequities in geriatric leukemia outcomes.

Differentiation of leukemia subtypes and treatment details

Leukemia is first divided into four main subtypes based on how fast it progresses (acute vs chronic) and which cell line is affected (myeloid vs lymphoid): AML, ALL, CML, CLL [[91], [92]]. Each has specific diagnostic criteria and increasingly subtype-tailored treatments.

Core subtype differentiation

Clinically, acute leukemias present rapidly with marrow failure (anemia, infections, bleeding), whereas chronic forms evolve more slowly and may be found incidentally [[63], [93]].

Finer subtyping within AML and ALL

•AML: Traditional FAB subtypes (M1–M7) are based on morphology; M3 (acute promyelocytic leukemia) is common in some cohorts (Singh et al., 2016). Modern systems integrate recurrent mutations/translocations (e.g. t(8;21), inv(16), t(15;17), NPM1, FLT3, IDH1/2) and even RNA-seq–defined expression groups (G1–G8) with distinct prognosis and drug sensitivity [[94], [95], [96]].

Conclusion

Elderly leukemia patients in underserved regions face a complex interplay of clinical, social, and systemic challenges that hinder timely diagnosis, equitable access to effective therapies, and optimal treatment outcomes. These barriers are compounded by the unique vulnerabilities of aging, including frailty, comorbidities, and atypical disease presentation, which require tailored approaches that go beyond standard oncology care. Without deliberate and coordinated efforts, disparities in leukemia outcomes among older adults will persist and widen, undermining global health equity goals.

Promising innovations in diagnostics, therapeutics, and care delivery, coupled with the integration of geriatric principles and digital health technologies, offer pathways to overcome many of these challenges. However, sustainable progress depends on comprehensive policy and health system reforms that prioritize geriatric oncology within national cancer control strategies, expand universal health coverage, strengthen the healthcare workforce, and enhance data-driven decision-making. Engaging stakeholders across sectors, from local communities to international organizations, is critical to mobilizing the resources and expertise needed to transform care.
