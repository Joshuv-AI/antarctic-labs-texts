Radiomics models identifying dementia in patients with normal age-related hippocampal atrophy: a study based on machine learning
Authors: Xiao-Li Yang, Yu-Fan Luo, Sheng-Wen Huang, Chen Li, Yuan-Yuan Wang, Huang-Huang Huang, Meng-Xiang Wang, Wen-Mei Lu, Ping-Ping Zong, Hu-Lie Zeng, Dan-Hong Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMethodsOur study aimed to employ a machine learning models based on radiomics for automated dementia identification in cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD) patients with normal age-related hippocampal atrophy (HA).MethodsCSVD patients were divided randomly into training and validation sets in a 6:4 ratio. Bilateral hippocampus segmented based on T2 and FLAIR were used for radiomics. Dementia radiomics models (T2 model, FLAIR model, T2-FLAIR model) were developed in the training sets and was further verified in the validation sets. The rad_score from the best models, along with clinical factors, was integrated to construct clinic-radiomics models for dementia through multifactor logistic regression analysis.Results116 CSVD patients were recruited, 30 patients were demented and 86 patients were non-demented. In predicting dementia in the training and validation sets, the area under curves (AUC) were 0.811 and 0.779 for the T2 model, 0.807 and 0.755 for the FLAIR model, and 0.864 and 0.801 for the T2-FLAIR model. In the overall 116 patients, the AUCs were 0.801 for the T2 model, 0.783 for the FLAIR model, and 0.840 for the T2-FLAIR model. No statistical differences were found among the three models. Given the highest AUC value in the T2-FLAIR model, converting the rad-score of it into binary T2-FLAIR (No, Yes) revealed a correlation between a 'Yes' response and dementia (OR = 13.21, 95% CI 3.487−59.922, p < 0.001) after adjusting for risk factors.ConclusionRadiomic analysis of the hippocampus in T2-FLAIR images can effectively identify dementia in CSVD patients with normal age-related HA.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12877-025-06526-z.Keywords: Hippocampus, Radiomic, Machine learning, Dementia, Cerebral small vessel disease

Introduction

Dementia is a clinical diagnosis characterized by cognitive symptoms that interfere with the ability to function at usual activities [1] and has been established as a major cause of disability and dependency in older adults [2]. As an acquired syndrome, dementia has diverse causes, such as Alzheimer’s disease (AD), Parkinson’s disease (PD), and cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD), reflecting specific changes in the brain [3]. Current epidemiological evidence points to several major strategies that may prevent or delay the onset of dementia [2], but early identification is still crucial as timely treatment can significantly prevent disease progression and improve prognosis.

Hippocampal atrophy (HA) has been associated with dementia in AD [4, 5], PD [6, 7], and post-stroke [8, 9]. However, little is known about its association in CSVD patients, despite atrophy has been recognized as a neuroimaging manifestation of CSVD [10]. Previous studies indicated that not everyone with a small hippocampus develops dementia [11, 12]. A 10-year follow-up study demonstrated that changes in hippocampal volume do not correlate with cognitive performance [11], because the HA rates was increased with age in healthy human aging [13] and in non-demented elderly individuals [12].The Medial Temporal Atrophy (MTA) score isa visual rating scale widely used for assessing HA [4, 14, 15]. In clinical settings, the normal age-related HA is defined as for individuals aged < 75 years with an MTA score < 2, and for those aged ≥ 75 years with an MTA score < 3 [15]. Given that recognizing dementia at the first visit is crucial for delaying disease progression, however, the clinical diagnosis of dementia is primarily based on time-consuming neuropsychological scale testing, presenting a major challenge in achieving accurate, objective, and timely diagnosis [16].

Radiomics, a method of texture analysis, provides information about first-, second-, and higher-order morphological features [17, 18]. Texture analysis includes a variety of image analysis techniques that quantify the variations in surface intensity or patterns, including some that are imperceptible to the human visual system [19], and are a useful way to extract detailed information from brain images, increase the precision of diagnosis, and assess disease prognosis. For AD, radiomics can early predict from mild cognitive impairment (MCI) to AD [20–22]. For PD, radiomics analysis has served as a potential tool for early prediction PD dementia [23], and for identifying the subtypes of PD [24]. In recent years, machine learning has used statistical techniques to grant computer systems the capability to “learn” from data to promote performance on an exact task without being explicitly programmed [25]. Although the combination of radiomics and machine learning has been explored in various medical contexts, there is a dearth of studies focusing on the hippocampus of CSVD, an age-related whole-brain disorder affecting cerebral arterioles, capillaries, and venules, leading to HA and dementia in older individuals [26].

The primary challenge in clinical practice is accurately identifying dementia in CSVD patients with normal age-related HA to prevent further deterioration. Radiomics, by extracting imperceptible details from conventional images, can offer valuable insights. Therefore, the current study aims to build a machine learning model based on quantitative, clinically empirical, and hippocampal radiomics features for the automated identification of dementia.

Materials and methods

Participant selection

‘Investigation on the Status of Cerebrovascular Diseases and Establishing Cohort in Shanghai Aging Population (ISCDECSHAP)’ is a prospective, population-based, and cohort study of stroke incidence and risk factors in an aging population from Shang Hai City. The ISCDECSHAP study aimed to establish a Chinese CSVD community cohort, and was approved by the ethics committee of Hua Shan Hospital and the Fifth People’s Hospital of Shanghai. Written informed consent was obtained from all the patients or their representatives before data collection. According to the protocol, all subjects, at least 60 years old, performed Cerebral Multimodal Structural Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI), neuropsychological tests Chinese Mini-Mental State Examination (C-MMSE) [27], Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (IADL), Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) and blood tests. All the examinations for this study were completed within a week. CSVD patients were defined as moderate-to-severe cerebral White Matter Hyperintensities (deep Fazekas > 1 point or paraventricular > 2 points); or mild cerebral White Matter Hyperintensities (deep Fazekas = 1 point or paraventricular 2 points) in combination with lacunar or 3 microhemorrhages. We only recruited normal age-related HA in CSVD patients from this population into our study. Patients meeting any of the following criteria were excluded: history of malignancies, dementia caused by other brain diseases (such as AD confirmed by lumbar puncture or PETCT, Lewy body dementia, frontotemporal lobe dementia, and PD confirmed by PETCT), alcohol and drug abuse, and nutritional metabolic disorder and endocrine system diseases.

Definition of dementia and normal age-related MTA evaluation

Dementia was diagnosed using the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders 5 definition of major neurocognitive disorder and was present if individuals who met one of the following: (1) A clinical diagnosis of dementia. (2) C-MMSE<24 indicative of cognitive impairment. Reduced capabilities in daily living as measured by a score ≤ 7 on the IADL [28], CDR ≥ 1.0 [29]. A 5-point MTA scale [30] was used for HA evaluation. An MTA<2 was considered normal for individuals aged < 75 years, and MTA < 3 for those aged ≥ 75 years [15].

MRI acquisition and image processing

The maximum interval between clinical assessments and imaging scans was 7 days. Neuroimaging examinations were performed on a 3.0 T MRI scanner (GE, USA) with a standard 20-channel head coil. The full sequence of cranial MRI included axial T1-weighted sequences, T2-weighted sequences, fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR), diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), and axial susceptibility-weighted imaging (SWI). But only T2 and FLAIR were used for hippocampal radiomics study with the following scan parameters: repetition time (TR) = 3500 ms, echo time (TE) = 99 ms, inversion time (TI) = 2500 ms, field of view (FOV) = 240 mm, acquisition matrix = 256 × 256, slice thickness = 6 mm, The obtained three-dimensional images had a resolution of 0.5 mm ×0.5 mm ×0.5 mm.

Hippocampal segmentation and radiomics feature extraction

All images, stored in Digital Medical Imaging and Communication (DICOM) format, were anonymized. Bilateral hippocampal segmentation was performed using 3D Slicer software by two radiologists (Dr. Yufan-Luo and Dr. Chen Li). On the axial layer of cerebral images of each patient, bilateral hippocampal was outlined to depict the entire manually. Examples of image segmentation are shown in Fig. 1. Using the R package “icc” for interobserver correlation coefficient (ICC) calculation. The radiomics features with an ICC > 0.7 were first screened out in the training set. Then, the features with a variance less than 1.0 were excluded, which was followed by replacing the missing values with a median value. The correlation analysis with a cut-off value set to 0.7 was then applied to remove the redundant features and reduce the collinearity of the features [31]. Finally, 2060 radiomics features, including 93 initial first order and texture features, 14 shape features, 744 wavelet features, 465 log features, 186 square and squareroot features, 93 exponential features, 93 logistic features, 279 lbp features, and 93 gradient features were extracted.

Workflow for model construction

Feature selection and modeling

The parameters of the 2060 features extracted from the bilateral hippocampal FLAIR and T2 sequences of 116 CSVD patients were standardized. The data were randomly divided into a training set and a validation set in a 6:4 ratio. Features were screened through Max-Relevance and Min-Redundancy (mRMR), and Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE). Selected radiomics features were used to construct a clinical model with the Logistic regression algorithm to predict dementia (family="binomial”, link="logit”). Rad_score formula = Feature * Corresponding coefficient (Estimate) + Intercept value (Estimate).

Model performance evaluation

The radiomics model’s effectiveness in the training and validation set was assessed using accuracy, specificity, sensitivity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value. The x-axis of the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve represented the false-positive rate, while the y-axis represented the true-positive rate. The calibration degree of the radiomics prediction model was evaluated by constructing the calibration-curve and using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit testing method. The comprehensive performance of the image ensemble prediction model was quantified using the Brier Score. The clinical benefit of the radiomics prediction model was displayed via a decision curve analysis (DCA). The area under the curve (AUC) values of the training and validation sets were compared using the DeLong test to verify fitting. The workflow for model construction is shown in Fig. 1.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.1.0, https://www.rproject.org). For demographic and clinical features, continuous variables with normal distribution were presented as mean ± standard deviation, and those with abnormal distribution were presented as median (interquartile range). Student t-test or ANOVA for normally distributed continuous parameters and Wilcoxon or Kruskal–Wallis test for abnormal distribution continuous parametric variables were used when appropriate. Categorical variables were expressed as frequency (percentage) and the χ2 test or Fisher exact test was used. By using ROC analysis, the optimal cutoffs of preoperative risk factors with continuous values were determined at the maximum Youden index. All the AUC values and ORs were present with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and the AUCs were compared by DeLong test. The radiomic score (rad_score) of the best models and clinical factors were combined to construct clinic-radiomics models for dementia using multifactor logistic regression analysis. The following R packages were mainly used: “irr” for ICC calculation by setting “twoway” and type of “agreement”, “findCorrelation” for correlation analysis, “mRMRe” and “caret” for feature selection, “glmnet” for Logistic regression algorithm, “pROC” for ROC analyses, “rms” for calibration curve, “rmda” for decision curve analysis, and “ggplot2” for graphical visualisation. A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Development and validation of the models to identify dementia

After adjusting for traditional risk factors such as age, gender, and education level, yes of T2-FLAIR Rad-score was positive with dementia (adjusted odds ratio, 10.736; 95% CI, 3.45–38.11; p < 0.01) (Fig. 5b). The relationship still existed after adjusting all related confounding factors, with OR 13.21, 95% CI3.487 − 59.922, p < 0.001. Meanwhile, education level was a robust independent risk factor for dementia with OR 0.748, 95%CI 0.569–0.941 (Fig. 5c).

Discussion

Utilizing a combination of radiomics and machine learning to comprehensively investigate dementia in CSVD patients with age-related normal HA, we had several main findings. First, in this group of population, although mild HA is normal for the elderly, 25.86% (30/116) of elderly CSVD patients still had dementia. Second, the hippocampus radiomic of T2-FLAIR can identify dementia. This finding holds significant implications for early dementia detection in CSVD patients.

CSVD is the second most common cause of dementia and the main cause of mixed dementia [10]. As an age-related disease, the prevalence of CSVD in elderly people aged 65 and above is as high as 80–90% [10]. HA is one of a marker of CSVD imaging feature [10], overlapping with other neurodegenerative processes commonly [7, 32, 33]. In our study, we found 25.86% (30/116) of elderly CSVD patients with normal age-related HA had dementia, consistent with the results of previous studies that not everyone with a small hippocampus develops dementia [11, 12], and the result was also confirmed by a study from 10-year follow-up of hippocampal volume on MRI in 518 non-demented elderly in the Netherlands, they found that during the 6 years of follow-up, the absolute volume decline was 1.7% per year for the left hippocampus and 1.6% per year for the right hippocampus, but only 50 people developed incident dementia [12]. On the other hand, not everyone with dementia has a small hippocampus, because pathological studies suggested that part of AD patients have no atrophy over time [34, 35], and 44% of AD patients with amyloid-positive were deemed to have age-appropriate levels of MTA [4]. Our research also confirms this point, because 3 dementia patients in our study were diagnosed AD clearly by the PETCT examinations. From this, it can be seen that HA is not specific to dementia, especially for patients with CSVD.

HA has a variety of pathologies, such as β-amyloid deposition, α-synuclein protein deposition [32, 33], and vascular brain injuries [8, 9]. In our study, even though the cut-offs for the MTA score excluded maximally HA caused by degenerative diseases [15], we still found 3 dementia CSVD patients were diagnosed as AD, which confirmed further the results of the 1362 autopsy pathology study, they suggest that the majority of patients with AD have mixed dementia, with both classic AD neurodegenerative pathology as well as cerebrovascular pathology [36]. But no matter which one pathological mechanism for HA, microvascular dysfunction or CSVD, may have a “double hit” effect on brain health by accelerating neurodegeneration (hit two) in addition to impairing brain perfusion, function, and pathways mediated by multiple focal ischemic or hypoxic micro-injuries (hit one) [37, 38]. Although the “two-hit hypothesis” requires testing in future studies, CSVD plays a crucial role in cognitive impairment caused by various factors. After all, there were links between vascular brain injuries and a differential vulnerability of the subiculum within the hippocampal loop, unbiased of the effect of neurodegenerative diseases, particularly when vascular injuries affect deep/subcortical structures [8].

Precious studies just focused on the relationship between CSVD common neuroimaging features (such as lacunes, white matter hyperintensity, perivascular space, and recent small subcortical infarct) and cognitive impairment [39], neglecting the impact of hippocampus on cognitive function. A study from Singapore demonstrated that the presence of CSVD could result in a unique pattern of hippocampal subfield atrophy in patients with cognitive impairment [40]. A longitudinal study based on HA using serial MRI in participants with hippocampal sclerosis of aging at autopsy suggested that the disproportionate HA seen may begin early before dementia [41]. Therefore, it is speculated that the subtle changes in the histology and structures of the hippocampus have occurred prior to the onset of cognitive impairment, whereas the conventional cerebral MRI just only can detect the obvious morphological changes. A combination of radiomics and machine learning is the trend of precision medicine, and is widely used in clinical practice. Particularly increasing the precision of diagnosis, and assessing disease prognosis [7, 22–24]. Recently, using this method, Betrouni et al. proved that the texture features of the hippocampus and entorhinal cortex can help diagnose early cognitive impairment after stroke [42]. Tozer et al. reported that the texture parameters of white matter lesions could help diagnose cognitive impairment in CSVD and correlated with the global function and executive function significantly [43]. However, until now, there is little study of the automatic diagnosis of dementia by using radiomics and machine learning in CSVD patients. In our study, we found that The AUC of the T2 radiomics model for predicting dementia was 0.801, of the FLAIR model was 0.783, and of the T2-FLAIR model was 0.840, all the radiomics models showed a higher predicted value.

The combination of radiomics and machine learning is a trending trend in the development of clinical medicine [44]. Although machine learning algorithms for comparison were various, such as Logistic Regression (LR), Support Vector Machine (SVM), and Gradient Boosting Machines (GBM) [44]. While by comparing multiple machine learning models to find the most suitable machine learning model. In our study, we used the most common GBM and SVM algorithms to reconstructe the radiomics models, and the results showed that the GBM model performs good just in the training set but the test set. While the SVM and LR models perform similarly, but the LR model was slightly better than the SVM model, so we finally use the LR model in our study. The results of this additional analysis were placed in the supplementary material Figure S1. Similarly, we have performed SHAP analysis to screen the radiomics features, and the results were shown in the supplementary material Figure S2.

In conclusion, we confirmed that texture features that can be obtained from routine clinical images, are abnormal in CSVD, and the degree of abnormality correlates with global cognition, and this is consistent with previous research results [43]. Therefore, radiomics may be useful to assess disease severity in clinically collected data, like our study, although the hippocampus did not have atrophy or even have normal age-related atrophy, we still found that hippocampal radiomics may distinguish dementia perfectly in CSVD, because the single-level features such as gray matter volume or the shape of the hippocampus were not insufficient to characterize it as a generalizable biomarker for cognitive level.

Strengths of this study include the use of a deep learning method involving parameter tuning, feature selection, and combination, which can enhance accuracy [45]. However, there are several limitations to consider. Firstly, radiomic features were extracted based on the entire hippocampus, while the hippocampus is divided into subfields with distinctive histological characteristics and specialized functions [46, 47]. Radiomic feature extraction may not be suitable for such small regions. Secondly, as a cross-sectional study, we demonstrated that deep learning hippocampal radiomics may be used to distinguish dementia in CSVD patients with normal age-related HA, but longitudinal studies are needed for prediction.

Conclusions

Collectively, our study results suggest that hippocampal radiomics from T2-FLAIR images have the potential to be clinically used to differentiate cognitive function levels in CSVD patients with normal age-related HA.
