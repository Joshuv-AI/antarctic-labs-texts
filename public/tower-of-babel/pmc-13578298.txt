PFAS-defined liquid crystal monomers: An overlooked class of organofluorine contaminants
Authors: Xu Li, Huixian Li, Xuneng Tong, Qi Wang, Kenneth M. Y. Leung, Yuhe He
Published: 2026

Sun et al. (1) report a >60% decline in extractable organofluorine (EOF) in subarctic pilot whales since 2011, with four legacy per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) accounting for ~75% of the measured organofluorine burden. Their findings provide a timely context for considering fluorinated contaminants outside conventional PFAS monitoring. Earlier work found 30 to 75% of EOF unexplained by target PFAS in 7 of 25 Northern Hemisphere marine-mammal liver samples, while suspect screening revealed additional PFAS classes (2), highlighting overlooked organofluorines.

Liquid crystal monomers (LCMs), functional constituents of liquid-crystal formulations used across smart electronics, provide one such example. Su et al. (3) identified 87 of 362 LCMs as potentially persistent and bioaccumulative, while Liu and Abbatt (4) showed that operating LCD screens directly emit LCMs. LCMs and many PFAS are associated with the electronics lifecycle and may share release pathways during product use, disposal, and e-waste recycling, yet they have largely been studied within separate frameworks. LCM research emphasizes structures governing material performance, whereas PFAS research applies fluorinated structural criteria. Accordingly, LCMs containing an OECD-qualifying fully fluorinated methyl or methylene carbon are also PFAS by structure. We propose the term PFAS-defined liquid crystal monomers (PFAS-LCMs) (Fig. 1A). Structure-based screening of two published inventories (5, 6) identified 228 nonredundant PFAS-LCM structures (Fig. 1B).

Structural definition and inventory-based identification of PFAS-defined liquid crystal monomers (PFAS-LCMs). (A) Representative LCM structures containing OECD-qualifying fully fluorinated methyl carbons in –OCF3, –CF3 and –CF2– motifs. The qualifying motifs are highlighted. (B) Structure-based screening of two published inventories containing 1,173 and 1,606 LCM entries. Structure harmonization and deduplication yielded 1,666 nonredundant LCM structures, including 1,108 fluorine-containing LCMs and 228 PFAS-LCMs under the OECD 2021 structural definition.

The marine relevance of this intersection is evident. Tao et al. (7) detected LCMs across multiple tissues, including brain, in South China Sea dolphins and porpoises, with several compounds meeting the OECD PFAS definition, although their PFAS identity was not recognized. In porpoises, temporal trends in LCM concentrations paralleled global LCD production, consistent with a production-linked pattern. These findings do not imply that LCMs offset the EOF decline reported by Sun et al. (1). Rather, viewed alongside declining legacy-PFAS burdens, they suggest that electronics-associated PFAS-LCMs may follow distinct source profiles, temporal trends, and tissue distributions, thereby broadening the picture of marine organofluorine contamination.

Beyond marine mammals, although mostly reported in China, LCMs have been globally detected in household dust (8, 9). LCMs also present in e-waste-worker serum and maternal–fetal samples, with evidence of placental transfer and biological effects (9, 10). Yet LCM studies have documented these compounds without generally recognizing their PFAS identity, whereas PFAS-focused research has rarely incorporated LCMs into target or suspect screening. PFAS-LCMs have therefore fallen between the two fields rather than remained undocumented. Reevaluating existing datasets alongside expanded PFAS screening could clarify their occurrence and determine whether they contribute to unresolved EOF or generate persistent transformation products such as trifluoroacetic acid.

The declining legacy-PFAS burdens reported by Sun et al. (1) demonstrate the environmental benefits associated with regulatory phase-outs. Building on this progress, recognizing PFAS-LCMs provides a complementary impetus to broaden PFAS and EOF assessments, because their omission from both LCM- and PFAS-focused frameworks may leave part of the organofluorine chemical space unresolved. Priorities include structure-resolved environmental and biological monitoring, EOF mass-balance analysis, and studies of transformation, bioaccumulation, and toxicity. More broadly, PFAS-LCMs should serve as an entry point for systematic investigation and disclosure of the wider LCM chemical space across the electronics lifecycle.
