Global minimizers for a two-sided biharmonic Alt-Caffarelli problem
Hans-Christoph Grunau, Marius Müller

Abstract
We study global minimizers of biharmonic analogues of the Alt-Caffarelli functional. It turns out that half-space solutions are global minimizers for the two-sided Alt-Caffarelli functional, but not in the one-sided case. In addition, we identify a further class of global minimizers, all of which have constant Laplacian. Recent work by J. Lamboley and M. Nahon reduces potential global minimizers in dimension two to four possible categories. Our work shows that three of these categories persist in any dimension and are in fact global minimizers. Moreover, we show that minimizers of the two-sided biharmonic Alt-Caffarelli problem do in general not satisfy a partial differential equation, not even with a signed measure as right-hand-side. This is in sharp contrast to the corresponding one-sided problem.

Introduction

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be open and bounded. For
u\in H^{2}(\Omega)
we define the
two-sided biharmonic Alt-Caffarelli functional

\mathcal{F}(u,\Omega):=\int_{\Omega}(\Delta u)^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x+|\{x\in\Omega:u(x)\neq 0\}|.

(1)

In the following we will write simply
|\{u\neq 0\}|
instead of
|\{x\in\Omega:u(x)\neq 0\}|
if
\Omega
is clear from the context. Minimizers of the functional have recently been studied in
[
9
]
under Dirichlet and Navier boundary conditions. It turned out that for any
boundary datum
u_{0}
and any
C^{2}
-domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, minimizers of
\mathcal{F}(\cdot,\Omega)
lie in
C^{1,\alpha}(\Omega)
for any
\alpha\in(0,1)
, but in general not in
C^{2}(\Omega)
.
The question of
C^{1,1}
-regularity remains open, but symmetry results and explicit study of radial minimizers suggest a positive answer. In dimension
n=2
the behaviour of minimizers at the free boundary
\partial(\{u\not=0\}\cup\{\nabla u\not=0\})
has been addressed in the recent work
[
11
]
.

The main goal of this article is to study
global minimizers
of the above functional in arbitrary dimensions. These are defined as follows.

Definition 1
.

We say that
u\in H^{2}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

minimizes
\mathcal{F}
globally

if for all bounded open sets
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and all
v\in H^{2}(\Omega)
such that
v-u\in H_{0}^{2}(\Omega)
one has
\mathcal{F}(u,\Omega)\leq\mathcal{F}(v,\Omega)
.

This definition may be equivalently reformulated as follows:

We say that
u\in H^{2}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

minimizes
\mathcal{F}
globally

if for all bounded open sets
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and all
v\in H^{2}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that
v=u
outside
\Omega
one has
\mathcal{F}(u,\Omega)\leq\mathcal{F}(v,\Omega)
.

We find examples of global minimizers and in particular obtain some

two-homogeneous
global
minimizers.
Throughout this paper, “homogeneous” always means “positive homogeneous”.
The absence of a maximum principle imposes a challenge in this context, especially since uniqueness and symmetry results are not readily available. Examples of global minimizers that we find fall under two categories

(I)

Half-space minimizers of the form
u(x)=\pm\frac{1}{2}((x-x_{0})\cdot\nu)^{2}\chi_{(0,\infty)}((x-x_{0})\cdot\nu)
for some unit vector
\nu\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
See Theorem
1
in Section
3
.

(II)

Functions
u
with constant Laplacian, i.e
\Delta u=c
for some
c\in\mathbb{R}
. However, global minimality of such functions depends on the value of the constant
c
. The condition
|c|\geq 1
ensures global minimality, see Theorem
2
in Section
4
.
For other constants, global minimiality is in general not
true, as we shall show in Lemma
9
.

Both categories contain two-homogeneous functions and hence give rise to two-homogeneous global minimizers. The above list is not necessarily an exhaustive list of global minimizers. The question whether more global minimizers exist arises naturally. To this end one may see e.g. the recent work
[
11
]
by Lamboley and Nahon, where a potential “angular minimizer” shows up in dimension
n=2
.

The present work also pursues two ideas how the above list could be extended. Firstly, we investigate whether the condition
|c|\geq 1
in Type (II) can be relaxed or removed, revealing that it can not be removed completely. Secondly, we show that the prefactor
\pm\frac{1}{2}
in Type (I) cannot be replaced by any other number. To this end note that in the special case
\nu=e_{n}:=(0,0,...,1),x_{0}=0
in Type-(I) minimizers take the form
u(x)=\pm\frac{1}{2}x_{n}^{2}\chi_{(0,\infty)}(x_{n})
. Inspired by this structure we compute all
d_{1},d_{2}\in\mathbb{R},d_{1}\neq d_{2}
such that the functions

w_{d_{1},d_{2}}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R},\quad w_{d_{1},d_{2}}(x):=d_{1}\tfrac{x_{n}^{2}}{2}\chi_{(0,\infty)}(x_{n})+d_{2}\tfrac{x_{n}^{2}}{2}\chi_{(-\infty,0)}(x_{n})

(2)

yield global minimizers and find that necessarily
d_{1}=\pm 1,d_{2}=0
or
d_{1}=0,d_{2}=\pm 1
(cf. Section
5
). Notice that there we also study the omitted case
d_{1}=d_{2}
, but minimizers that arise in this case would fall under Category (II) rather than Category (I).

Explicit knowledge of global minimizers provides
more insights into the

blow-up limits
of the minimizers found in
[
9
]
around their free boundary (despite the fact that the absence of a maximum principle makes a rigorous result in this situation
difficult). Such blow-up limits are used in the classical Alt-Caffarelli problem (see e.g.
[
19
, Theorem 1.2 and the outline of its proof]
)
to study regularity of the free boundary. We will elaborate on this connection more precisely below (cf. Section
1.1
).

The lecture notes
[
19
]
provide a broad survey on the regularity theory of free boundaries of the classical Alt-Caffarelli problem
[
1
]
.
One studies for a given smooth domain
G\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
minimizers of

\mathcal{F}_{AC}(u):=\int_{G}|\nabla u|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x+|\{x\in G:u(x)\neq 0\}|\qquad\textrm{among all $u\in W^{1,2}(G):u|_{\partial G}=1$}

(3)

and seeks to understand properties of
the free boundary
\partial\{u\neq 0\}
. Notice that due to the maximum principle, minimizers of
\mathcal{F}_{AC}
satisfy
u\geq 0
and hence one may replace
\{u\neq 0\}
by
\{u>0\}
. For the higher order functional in (
1
), replacing
\{u\neq 0\}
by
\{u>0\}
makes a significant difference, as we shall see below. This is why we distinguish between the
two-sided problem
(
1
) and the
one-sided problem
, where the functional

\tilde{\mathcal{F}}(u,\Omega)=\int_{\Omega}(\Delta u)^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x+|\{x\in\Omega:u(x)>0\}|.

is investigated.
Our study here is focussed on global minimizers of
the two-sided problem, but we also prove in Lemma
8
that these are not global minimizers of the one-sided problem.

Recently, the functionals
\mathcal{F}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}

have raised a lot of interest. In
[
6
,
7
]
, the one-sided problem is
studied in arbitrary dimensions with Navier boundary conditions. The authors show existence and first regularity results for minimizers and give a dichotomy result for free boundary points. Not only the pure one-sided functional is studied, but also the one-sided functional with the additional obstacle constraint
u\geq 0
. Notice that under such an
obstacle constraint, the one-sided and two-sided functionals coincide. Such
adhesive obstacle problems
have been studied before in one dimension for curves
[
12
,
13
]
and are of interest in many biological applications, such as
gecko adhesion
, cf.
[
17
]
.

For the one-sided functional without the obstacle constraint it has been shown in
[
14
,
15
]
that in two dimensions minimizers of the one-sided problem enjoy optimal
C^{2,1}
-regularity in two dimensions. The same regularity result applies to the free boundary
\partial\{u\neq 0\}
since it is observed that the gradient of minimizers can not have zeroes on the free boundary (which intertwines the regularity of minimizers and free boundary). These observations do not change if one replaces the Laplacian by an anisotropic elliptic operator of the form
\mathrm{div}(A\nabla)
, as
[
16
]
shows. In
[
9
]
, the two-sided problem is studied for the first time. The different gradient behaviour yields a different optimal regularity result (under Navier boundary conditions):
C^{2}
-regularity is impossible. The origin of this discrepancy is also discussed in this article: In contrast to the one-sided problem, minimizers of the two-sided functional may in general not satisfy any measure-valued PDE, not even with a signed measure. This is also a remarkable difference to the two-sided biharmonic obstacle problem
[
3
]
, where a measure-valued PDE is indeed satisfied with a signed measure. Notice that for one-sided problems, measure-valued PDEs arise naturally from variational inequalities, as e.g. observed in the classical biharmonic obstacle problem
[
2
]
.

1.1
Application: Blow-Up-Analysis

To understand the regularity of the free boundaries of minimizers
\tilde{u}\in W^{1,2}(G)
of (
3
), a classical approach discussed in
[
19
]
works via a
blow-up analysis
. More precisely, for some point
x_{0}
on the free boundary
\partial\{\tilde{u}\neq 0\}
one forms

\tilde{u}_{x_{0},r}(x):=\frac{\tilde{u}(x_{0}+rx)}{r}

(4)

and studies
the limit as
r\searrow 0
.
Limits of these functions have (if existent in a suitable sense) naturally the following properties:

•

They are global minimizers of the classical Alt-Caffarelli functional and

•

they are one-homogeneous functions.

Regularity results of the free boundary are then obtained by studying the zero level set of such one-homogeneous global minimizers. In dimension two and assuming
x_{0}=0

nonnegative one-homogeneous global minimizers are exhaustively classified by half-plane solutions of the form

\tilde{u}_{\nu}(x):=(x\cdot\nu)\chi_{(0,\infty)}(x\cdot\nu),

where
\nu\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
is an arbitrary unit vector (cf.
[
19
, Proposition 9.13]
). An inspection of this proof reveals that if one drops the prerequisite of nonnegativity, also affine linear functions can be one-homogeneous global minimizers of
\mathcal{F}_{AC}
.
The free boundary
\partial\{\tilde{u}_{\nu}>0\}
is always a straight line
of the form
\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:x\cdot\nu=0\}
i.e. a smooth one-dimensional manifold. This smoothness of the free boundary of blow-ups is decisive for the regularity of the free boundary of the original problem. The same approach works also in higher dimensions, and it turns out that the regularity of the free boundary of one-homogeneous global minimizers is decisive for regularity results. This is why
[
19
]
defines the critical dimension
d^{*}
of the Alt-Caffarelli problem as follows

Definition 2

(
[
19
, Definition 1.5]
)
.

d^{*}
is the smallest dimension such that there exists a function
z:\mathbb{R}^{d^{*}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that

(i)

z
is nontrivial, non-negative and one-homogeneous,

(ii)

z
is a global minimizer of the classical Alt-Caffarelli functional,

(iii)

the
free boundary
\partial\{z>0\}
is not a
(d^{*}-1)
-dimensional
C^{1}
-submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{d^{*}}
.

It is now known that
d^{*}\in\{5,6\}
(cf.
[
19
,
4
,
5
,
10
]
).
It seems meaningful to think about a generalization of this approach to the biharmonic Alt-Caffarelli problem for
\mathcal{F}(\cdot,\Omega)
. Notice first that if
x_{0}
is a free boundary point, then in view of Lemma
1
one has
u(x_{0})=0,\nabla u(x_{0})=0
. Hence it seems more meaningful to study the following blow-up of a minimizer
u

u_{x_{0},r}(x):=\frac{u(x_{0}+rx)}{r^{2}}.

(5)

Due to the lack of the maximum principle for a higher order problem it is generally not easy to show that
u_{x_{0},r}
possesses a limit as
r\searrow 0
. In dimension
n=2
an existence result for limits has been achieved by J. Lamboley and M. Nahon in
[
11
]
, by means of a monotonicity formula based on the one in
[
7
]
.
The authors use this to describe the free boundary and to classify its potential singular points, if
n=2
.
In higher dimensions the existence question of such limits seems challenging and goes beyond the scope of this article. However, if existent, such limits are naturally two-homogeneous.

Moreover, cut-and-paste-arguments suggest again to expect that blow-up limits are global minimizers of
\mathcal{F}
. Observe that the condition
v-u\in H_{0}^{2}(\Omega)
in Definition
1
appears here naturally, since it is needed for cut-and-paste procedures. As a result, blow-up limits are indeed expected to be two-homogeneous global minimizers.

Definition
2
only admits nonnegative functions, which is not meaningful in the biharmonic context due to the lack of a maximum principle.
We leave out this nonnegativity requirement but remark at this point that
the decisive counterexample is still nonnegative, cf. Corollary
2
.

Based on the reasoning above, one may define
the critical dimension
d^{**}
for the biharmonic Alt-Caffarelli problem as follows.

Definition 3
.

d^{**}
is the smallest dimension such that there exists a function
z:\mathbb{R}^{d^{**}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that

(i)

z
is nontrivial and two-homogeneous,

(ii)

z
is a global minimizer of
\mathcal{F}
,

(iii)

the
free boundary
\partial\{z=0,\nabla z=0\}
is not a
(d^{**}-1)
-dimensional
C^{1}
-submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{d^{**}}
.

We will study the value of
d^{**}
and find in Corollary
2
that
d^{**}=2.
The reason for this is readily understood: The class of Type-(II)-minimizers contains many quadratic forms and the free boundaries of quadratic forms (in the sense of Definition
3
(iii)) can be more complicated than just a
C^{1}
-hypersurface. For example they can consist only of a singleton or of a subspace whose codimension is higher than one.
An interesting question for future research is whether even more complicated free boundaries than singletons or lower-dimensional subspaces can arise as free boundaries of two-homogeneous global minimizers. It is conjectured that even in dimension two they do arise. Indeed, in
[
11
, Eq. (1.8)]
the authors find a candidate for a two-homogeneous global minimizer whose free boundary consists of a cone, whose opening angle
t_{1}
is the unique solution of
\tan(t_{1})=t_{1}
in
(\pi,2\pi)
. Its global minimality is conjectured and not proven so far.

As a consequence, the study of the regularity of the free boundary via blow-up in the biharmonic setting is more involved than in the first order
setting. This is not just due to unsettled convergence questions in dimension
n\geq 3
(e.g. since homogeneity results of blow-ups are more involved than the celebrated approach in
[
20
]
) but also due to different qualitative behaviour of blow-up limits.

Structure of the article

In Section

In Section

2.1
Properties of global minimizers

In the following we will frequently use a regularity result for minimizers of
\mathcal{F}(\cdot,\Omega)
from
[
9
, Theorem 2]
.

Lemma 1

(
[
9
, Theorem 2]
)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be open and
u_{0}\in C^{\infty}(\Omega)
. Further let
u\in H^{2}(\Omega)
be a minimizer of
\mathcal{F}(\cdot,\Omega)
in
\mathscr{D}(u_{0}):=\{v\in H^{2}(\Omega):v-u_{0}\in H_{0}^{2}(\Omega)\}.
Then
u\in C^{1,\alpha}(\Omega)
and
u
is smooth and biharmonic on
\Omega\setminus\{u=0,\nabla u=0\}
.

Note in particular that regularity can only break down at points where
u=0
and
\nabla u=0
, i.e. non-regular points of the zero level set. As a consequence it seems more suitable to think of the free boundary as
\partial\{u=0,\nabla u=0\}
and not as
\partial\{u=0\}
.
Next we deal with symmetries of global minimizers. To this end we first observe the following symmetries of the functional
\mathcal{F}(\cdot,\Omega)
, which can be verified by direct computation:

•

For each
u\in H^{2}(\Omega)
one has
\mathcal{F}(-u,\Omega)=\mathcal{F}(u,\Omega)
.

•

For each
u\in H^{2}(\Omega)
and
O\in O(n,\mathbb{R})
one has
\mathcal{F}(u\circ O,\Omega)=\mathcal{F}(u,\Omega)
.

Lemma 2
.

Let
u\in H^{2}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be a global minimizer of
\mathcal{F}
. Then it holds:

(a)

-u\in H^{2}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is also a global minimizer of
\mathcal{F}
.

(b)

For each
O\in O_{n}(\mathbb{R})
the function
u\circ O\in H^{2}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is also global minimizer of
\mathcal{F}
.

(c)

For each
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
u(\,\cdot\,+x_{0})
is also a global minimizer of
\mathcal{F}
.

Proof.

We only prove (a). Assertions (b) and (c) follow along the same lines. Suppose that
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}

is open and bounded and
v\in H^{2}(\Omega)
is such that
v-(-u)\in H_{0}^{2}(\Omega)
. One readily checks that then
-v\in H^{2}(\Omega)
and
(-v)-u\in H_{0}^{2}(\Omega)
. Since
u
is a global minimizer this implies
\mathcal{F}(-v,\Omega)\geq\mathcal{F}(u,\Omega)
. As a result we have

\mathcal{F}(v,\Omega)=\mathcal{F}(-v,\Omega)\geq\mathcal{F}(u,\Omega)=\mathcal{F}(-u,\Omega).

∎

These symmetry results allow us to reduce the functions in Type I to one
prototype
. Indeed by a sign change we may assume that
\pm=+
and by a rotation we may achieve
\nu=e_{n}
. As a result each function in Type I coincides up to rotation, translation,
and sign change with

u:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},\quad u(x):=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}x_{n}^{2}&x_{n}>0,\\
0&x_{n}\leq 0.\end{cases}

(6)

Hence it is sufficient to show that this function yields a global minimizer of
\mathcal{F}.
We next simplify the problem further. In Definition
1
, minimality must be checked for all bounded open sets
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We claim next, that it is sufficient to check minimality in the special case that
\Omega
is a ball centered in the origin. In order to show this we need the following extension lemma.

Lemma 3
.

Suppose that
\Omega,\tilde{\Omega}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
are bounded open sets such that
\tilde{\Omega}\supset\Omega
. Let
u\in H^{2}(\tilde{\Omega})
and
v\in H^{2}(\Omega)
be such that
v-u\in H_{0}^{2}(\Omega)
. Define
\tilde{v}:\tilde{\Omega}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
via

\tilde{v}(x)=\begin{cases}v(x)&x\in\Omega\\
u(x)&x\in\tilde{\Omega}\setminus\Omega\end{cases}

Then
\tilde{v}\in H^{2}(\tilde{\Omega})
. Moreover
\tilde{v}-u\in H_{0}^{2}(\tilde{\Omega})
.

Proof.

The proof is immediate, observing that
C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)\subset H_{0}^{2}(\Omega)
is dense.
∎

Now we claim that minimality in balls centered at zero is sufficient for global minimality.

Lemma 4
.

Suppose that
u\in H^{2}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is such that for all
R>0
the following is satisfied: For each
v\in H^{2}(B_{R}(0))
such that
v-u\in H_{0}^{2}(B_{R}(0))
one has
\mathcal{F}(v,B_{R}(0))\geq\mathcal{F}(u,B_{R}(0)).
Then
u
is a global minimizer of
\mathcal{F}
.

Proof.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be open and bounded. Then there exists
R>0
such that
\Omega\subset B_{R}(0)
. Now let
v\in H^{2}(\Omega)
be such that
v-u\in H_{0}^{2}(\Omega)
.
We can define
\tilde{v}:B_{R}(0)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
to be

\tilde{v}(x)=\begin{cases}v(x)&x\in\Omega\\
u(x)&x\in B_{R}(0)\setminus\Omega\end{cases}

and conclude by Lemma
3
that
\tilde{v}\in H^{2}(B_{R}(0))
and
\tilde{v}-u\in H_{0}^{2}(B_{R}(0))
. By assumption we conclude

\mathcal{F}(\tilde{v},B_{R}(0))-\mathcal{F}(u,B_{R}(0))\geq 0.

(7)

Since
\tilde{v}=u
on

we have
\mathcal{F}(\tilde{v},B_{R}(0)\setminus\Omega)=\mathcal{F}(u,B_{R}(0)\setminus\Omega)

This and (

) yield

Type I: Half-space minimizers

The main result of this section as follows.

Theorem 1
.

Let
u\in H^{2}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

):

Recall that by Lemma
4

3.1
Proof of Minimality

Let
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be any ball centered at
0
. We define
B^{+}:=B\cap\{x_{n}>0\}
,
B^{-}:=B\cap\{x_{n}<0\}.
Before we start the proof we need several preparatory lemmas. The first one here is a higher order version of an inequality in
[
19
, Proof of Lemma 2.13]
. We note that
[
19
, Lemma 2.13]
uses the maximum principle, which is why it is not straightforward to generalize it to our higher order setting.

Lemma 5
.

Let
v\in H^{2}(B)
be such that
v=0
and
\nabla v=0

\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
a.e. on
\partial B\cap\{x_{n}<0\}
. Then

2\int_{B\cap\{x_{n}=0\}}\partial_{n}v(x^{\prime},0)\;\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}\leq\mathcal{F}(v,B^{-}).

(8)

Equality holds if and only if
\Delta v|_{B^{-}}=\chi_{\{v\neq 0\}\cap B^{-}}
a.e..

Proof.

Using that
\partial B^{-}=(B\cap\{x_{n}=0\})\cup(\partial B\cap\{x_{n}<0\})
and
\nabla v=0

\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
almost everywhere on
\partial B\cap\{x_{n}<0\}
we find that

\displaystyle\int_{B\cap\{x_{n}=0\}}\partial_{n}v(x^{\prime},0)\;\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}=\int_{\partial B^{-}}(\nabla v\cdot\nu)\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}.

Applying the Gauss divergence theorem yields

\displaystyle\int_{B\cap\{x_{n}=0\}}\partial_{n}v(x^{\prime},0)\;\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}=\int_{B^{-}}\Delta v(x)\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\{v\neq 0\}\cap B^{-}}\Delta v(x)\;\mathrm{d}x,

where we used that by
[
9
, Lemma 4]

\Delta v=0
a.e. on
\{v=0\}
. Using that
\Delta v(x)\leq\frac{1+(\Delta v(x))^{2}}{2}
we estimate

\displaystyle\int_{B\cap\{x_{n}=0\}}\partial_{n}v(x^{\prime},0)\;\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}=\int_{\{v\neq 0\}\cap B^{-}}\Delta v(x)\;\mathrm{d}x\leq\int_{\{v\neq 0\}\cap B^{-}}\frac{1+(\Delta v(x))^{2}}{2}\;\mathrm{d}x

(9)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}|\{v\neq 0\}\cap B^{-}|+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\{v\neq 0\}\cap B^{-}}|\Delta v(x)|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x\leq\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{F}(v,B^{-}).

Inequality
8
follows. It remains to study the equality case. Notice that the estimate
\Delta v(x)\leq\frac{1+(\Delta v(x))^{2}}{2}
is satisfied with equality if and only if
\Delta v(x)=1
. Equality in the estimate (
9
) would therefore imply that
\Delta v=1
a.e. on
\{v\neq 0\}\cap B^{-}
. Since (again by
[

3.2
Nonminimality for the one-sided functional

In literature the following

[

One fundamental difference between

and

As was shown in

[

\{u=0\}

Since minimizers with positive boundary data lie in

(cf.
[

, Theorem 2]

[

, Examples 2 and 4]
for radial minimizers of

with vanishing gradient on

. Notice also that the half-plane minimizer in (

Lemma 8
.

Let

In this section we discuss

Theorem 2
.

Let
n\geq 1

be such that
\Delta w\equiv C
for some

Proof.

If
\Delta w\equiv C

|\{w=0\}|=0

[
9

\Delta w=0

By Lemma
4

such that
v-w\in H_{0}^{2}(B)

\mathcal{F}(v,B)\geq\mathcal{F}(w,B)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(v,B)-\mathcal{F}(w,B)

\displaystyle=\int_{B}(\Delta v)^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x-\int_{B}(\Delta w)^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x+|\{v\neq 0\}\cap B|-|\{w\neq 0\}\cap B|

\displaystyle=\int_{B}(\Delta v)^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x-\int_{B}(\Delta w)^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x+|\{v\neq 0\}\cap B|-|B|

\displaystyle=\int_{B}(\Delta v)^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x-\int_{B}(\Delta w)^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x-|\{v=0\}\cap B|

\displaystyle=\int_{B}[(\Delta v-w)^{2}+2\Delta v\Delta w-(\Delta w)^{2}]\;\mathrm{d}x-\int_{B}(\Delta w)^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x-|\{v=0\}\cap B|

\displaystyle=\int_{B}(\Delta(v-w))^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x+2\int_{B}\Delta w\Delta(v-w)\;\mathrm{d}x-|\{v=0\}\cap B|.

(17)

Now

2\int_{B}\Delta w\Delta(v-w)\;\mathrm{d}x=2C\int_{B}\Delta(v-w)\;\mathrm{d}x=2\int_{\partial B}\partial_{\nu}(v-w)\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}=0,

since

Moreover, by

\displaystyle\int_{B}(\Delta(v-w))^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{B\cap\{v\neq 0\}}(\Delta(v-w))^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x+\int_{B\cap\{v=0\}}(\Delta(v-w))^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{B\cap\{v\neq 0\}}(\Delta(v-w))^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x+C^{2}|B\cap\{v=0\}|.

Using the previous two computations in (

\mathcal{F}(v,B)-\mathcal{F}(w,B)=(C^{2}-1)|B\cap\{v=0\}|+\int_{B\cap\{v\neq 0\}}(\Delta(v-w))^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x\geq 0,

due to the fact that

5
Nonextendability of categories (I) and (II)

We have now studied two types of minimizers. It is natural to ask the question whether there are more minimizers that have a
similar form
to these two types. A natural question to ask is e.g. whether
linear combinations
of global minimizers yield global minimizers again. This is not true for Type (II) minimizers. Indeed, Lemma
9
shows that multiplying the Type-(II) global minimizer
w(x)=|x|^{2}
by a small constant yields a function that is not a global minimizer anymore. We can however also study linear combinations of Type-(I) minimizers.
For example, two global minimizers of Type (I) are given by
u_{1}(x):=\frac{1}{2}x_{n}^{2}\chi_{(0,\infty)}(x_{n})
and
u_{2}(x)=\frac{1}{2}(-x_{n})^{2}\chi_{(0,\infty)}(-x_{n})=\frac{1}{2}x_{n}^{2}\chi_{(-\infty,0)}(x_{n})
(which is a global minimizer as an orthogonal transformation of
u_{1}
, cf. also Lemma
2
). The following theorem examines global minimality properties of linear combinations of the form
d_{1}u_{1}+d_{2}u_{2}
,
d_{1},d_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
and finds that Type (I) is very rigid with respect to linear combinations. This justifies that Category (I) can not be extended.

.

For
d_{1},d_{2}\in\mathbb{R}

is a nontrivial global minimizer, then one out of the following two cases occurs:

or
d_{1}=0,d_{2}=\pm 1
, i.e.
w_{d_{1},d_{2}}
is a Type-
\mathrm{(I)}
-minimizer.

\mathrm{(II)}

d_{1},d_{2}\neq 0
and
d_{1}=d_{2}
, i.e.
w_{d_{1},d_{2}}
is a Type-
\mathrm{(II)}
-minimizer.

Proof.

Of course,
w_{0,0}(x)\equiv 0
is a global minimizer but is not to be considered by assumption.
So, we have to distinguish the cases whether none or one of the
d_{j}
is equal to
0
.

(a)

Case
d_{1}\not=0
,
d_{2}=0
.

Let
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a ball centered at
0
and let
\psi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(B;\mathbb{R}^{n})
be arbitrary. Since
w_{d_{1},d_{2}}
is assumed to be a global minimizer we take from
[
7
, Lemma 4.4]
by obviously changing
\chi_{\{u>0\}}
into
\chi_{\{u\not=0\}}
:

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle-\int_{B\cap\{w_{d_{1},d_{2}}\neq 0\}}\mathrm{div}(\psi)\;\mathrm{d}x-\int_{B}(\Delta w_{d_{1},d_{2}})^{2}\;\mathrm{div}(\psi)\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle+2\int_{B}\Delta w_{d_{1},d_{2}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(2\partial_{i}\psi\cdot\partial_{i}(\nabla w_{d_{1},d_{2}})+\partial^{2}_{ii}\psi\cdot\nabla w_{d_{1},d_{2}}\right)\right)\;\mathrm{d}x.

(20)

Here “
\,\cdot\,
” denotes the canonical scalar
product in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
We compute the terms in (
20
) and find

\int_{B\cap\{w_{d_{1},d_{2}}\neq 0\}}\mathrm{div}(\psi)\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{B^{+}}\mathrm{div}(\psi)\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\partial B^{+}}\psi\cdot\nu\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{1}=-\int_{B\cap\{x_{n}=0\}}\psi_{n}\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}

and

\int_{B}(\Delta w_{d_{1},d_{2}})^{2}\;\mathrm{div}(\psi)\;\mathrm{d}x=d_{1}^{2}\int_{B^{+}}\mathrm{div}(\psi)\;\mathrm{d}x=-d_{1}^{2}\int_{B\cap\{x_{n}=0\}}\psi_{n}\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{1}.

For the third term notice that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}(2\partial_{i}\psi\cdot\partial_{i}(\nabla w_{d_{1},d_{2}})+

\displaystyle\partial^{2}_{ii}\psi\cdot\nabla w_{d_{1},d_{2}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\partial_{i}\psi\cdot\partial_{i}(\nabla w_{d_{1},d_{2}})+[\partial_{i}\psi\cdot\partial_{i}(\nabla w_{d_{1},d_{2}})+\partial^{2}_{ii}\psi\cdot\nabla w_{d_{1},d_{2}}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\partial_{i}\psi\cdot\partial_{i}(\nabla w_{d_{1},d_{2}})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\partial_{i}(\partial_{i}\psi\cdot\nabla w_{d_{1},d_{2}}).

This decomposes the third term into two further summands. For the last summand we compute

\displaystyle 2\int_{B}\Delta w_{d_{1},d_{2}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\partial_{i}(\partial_{i}\psi\cdot\nabla w_{d_{1},d_{2}})\right)\;\mathrm{d}x=

\displaystyle 2d_{1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{B^{+}}\partial_{i}(\partial_{i}\psi\cdot\nabla w_{d_{1},d_{2}})\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2d_{1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\partial B^{+}}(\partial_{i}\psi\cdot\nabla w_{d_{1},d_{2}})\nu_{i}\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}=0,

where we have used that for
i=1,...,n
one has
\partial_{i}\psi=0
on
\partial B
and
\nabla w_{d_{1},d_{2}}=0
on
B\cap\{x_{n}=0\}
. Moreover, for the first (additional) summand we have

\displaystyle 2\int_{B}\Delta w_{d_{1},d_{2}}

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\partial_{i}\psi\cdot\partial_{i}\nabla w_{d_{1},d_{2}}\right)\;\mathrm{d}x=2d_{1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{B^{+}}\partial_{i}\psi\cdot\partial_{i}\nabla w_{d_{1},d_{2}}\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2d_{1}^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{B^{+}}\partial_{i}\psi\cdot\partial_{i}\left(x_{n}e_{n}\right)\;\mathrm{d}x=2d_{1}^{2}\int_{B^{+}}\partial_{n}\psi_{n}\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2d_{1}^{2}\int_{B\cap\{x_{n}=0\}}\psi_{n}\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}.

(21)

Using all the previous computations in (
20
) we obtain

0=(1+d_{1}^{2}-2d_{1}^{2})\int_{B\cap\{x_{n}=0\}}\psi_{n}\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}=(1-d_{1}^{2})\int_{B\cap\{x_{n}=0\}}\psi_{n}\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}.

Choosing a test vector field
\psi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(B;\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that
\int_{B\cap\{x_{n}=0\}}\psi_{n}\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\neq 0
we find
d_{1}^{2}=1
, that is
d_{1}=\pm 1
.

(b)

Case
d_{1}=0
,
d_{2}\not=0
.

Applying Lemma
2
to the previous case yields that
d_{2}=\pm 1
.

(c)

Case
d_{1}\not=0
,
d_{2}\not=0
.

Let
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a ball centered at
0
and let
\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(B;\mathbb{R})
be arbitrary.
Then for
t\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
(but close to
0
) we have

\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}}(w_{d_{1},d_{2}})=

\displaystyle\int_{B}(\Delta w_{d_{1},d_{2}})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+|B|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}}(w_{d_{1},d_{2}}+t\varphi)=\int_{B}(\Delta w_{d_{1},d_{2}}+t\varphi)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+|\{x\in B:\,w_{d_{1},d_{2}}+t\varphi\not=0\}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{B}(\Delta w_{d_{1},d_{2}}+t\varphi)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+|B|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{B}(\Delta w_{d_{1},d_{2}})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+2t\int_{B}\Delta w_{d_{1},d_{2}}\cdot\Delta\varphi\,\mathrm{d}x+t^{2}\int_{B}(\Delta\varphi)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+|B|

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\quad 0\leq

\displaystyle 2t\int_{B}\Delta w_{d_{1},d_{2}}\cdot\Delta\varphi\,\mathrm{d}x+t^{2}\int_{B}(\Delta\varphi)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.

Since the sign of
t
may be arbitrary this gives

\forall\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(B;\mathbb{R}):\quad\int_{B}\Delta w_{d_{1},d_{2}}\cdot\Delta\varphi\,\mathrm{d}x=0.

This means that
w_{d_{1},d_{2}}
is weakly biharmonic and hence, by elliptic regularity, strongly biharmonic in
B
. In particular,
w_{d_{1},d_{2}}\in C^{\infty}(B)
. This implies that
d_{1}=d_{2}
.

∎

. In

6
No measure-valued equation for minimizers

In one-sided biharmonic free boundary problems such as the one-sided Alt-Caffrelli problem
[
14
]
or the biharmonic obstacle problem
[
2
]
, minimizers
v\in H^{2}(\Omega)
satisfy a measure-valued PDE of the form

\int_{\Omega}\Delta v\Delta\varphi\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}\varphi\;\mathrm{d}\mu\qquad\textrm{for all $\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)$},

where
\mu
is a Radon measure. The reason for this is that imposing a sign constraint on admissible perturbations gives rise to a (distributional)
differential inequality.

The well-known Riesz-Markov-Kakutani representation theorem
[
8
, Theorem 1.39]
converts such inequalities to a measure-valued PDE as the one stated above.

For two-sided problems it seems reasonable to expect a signed (!) Radon measure
\mu
such that

\int_{\Omega}\Delta v\Delta\varphi\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}\varphi\;\mathrm{d}\mu\qquad\textrm{for all $\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)$}.

(22)

Such equation is for example satisfied in the biharmonic two-obstacle problem
[
3
]
. We will show in the following that (surprisingly) for the two-sided Alt-Caffarelli problem, such a measure-valued equation may in general not
hold. We will actually present two reasons for this.

(i)

We will investigate the properties of explicit minimizers in 1-d found in
[
9
]
. These are explicitly computed (under Navier boundary conditions). We will characterize the distribution
\varphi\mapsto\int_{\Omega}u^{\prime\prime}(x)\varphi^{\prime\prime}(x)\;\mathrm{d}x
for any such explicit minimizer
u
and show that this distribution can never be given by a measure.

(ii)

We will argue that solutions of (
22
) must necessarily have
W^{3,1}_{loc}
-Sobolev regularity. However, the explicit radial minimizers in
[
9
, Example 2, Example 4]
and the half-space minimizers found in Theorem
1
do not enjoy
W^{3,1}_{loc}
-regularity. The regularity observation is independent of the boundary conditions and the explicit minimizers whose regularity we investigate satisfy both Dirichlet and Navier boundary conditions.

6.1
Distributional equations for explicit solutions

In
[
9
, Example 1]
it has been found that for
\Omega=(-R,R)
,
R>\sqrt{3}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}
and
u_{0}\equiv 1
the minimizer of
\mathcal{F}
in

\mathscr{N}:=\{u\in H^{2}(\Omega):u-u_{0}\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\}

is uniquely determined by

u(x)=\begin{cases}0&|x|\leq R-\sqrt{3},\\
\frac{(|x|-R+\sqrt{3})^{2}(R+2\sqrt{3}-|x|)}{6\sqrt{3}}&|x|>R-\sqrt{3}.\end{cases}

We compute the derivatives of
u
and obtain

\displaystyle u^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0&|x|\leq R-\sqrt{3}\\
\frac{1}{6\sqrt{3}}[2(|x|-R+\sqrt{3})(R+2\sqrt{3}-|x|)\mathrm{sgn}(x)+(|x|-R+\sqrt{3})^{2}(-\mathrm{sgn}(x))]&|x|>R-\sqrt{3}\end{cases},

\displaystyle u^{\prime\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0&|x|\leq R-\sqrt{3}\\
\frac{1}{6\sqrt{3}}[2(R+2\sqrt{3}-|x|)-4(|x|-R+\sqrt{3})]&|x|>R-\sqrt{3}\end{cases}=\begin{cases}0&|x|\leq R-\sqrt{3}\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}[R-|x|]&|x|>R-\sqrt{3}\end{cases}.

Now define
h_{1},h_{2}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

h_{1}(x):=R\chi_{(R-\sqrt{3},R)}(|x|),\quad h_{2}(x):=|x|\chi_{(R-\sqrt{3},R)}(|x|).

For any
\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
we compute further

\int_{\Omega}h_{1}(x)\varphi^{\prime\prime}(x)\;\mathrm{d}x=R\varphi^{\prime}(-R+\sqrt{3})-R\varphi^{\prime}(R-\sqrt{3})

and

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}h_{2}(x)\varphi^{\prime\prime}(x)\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{-R}^{-R+\sqrt{3}}-x\varphi^{\prime\prime}(x)\;\mathrm{d}x+\int_{R-\sqrt{3}}^{R}x\varphi^{\prime\prime}(x)\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=-(-R+\sqrt{3})\varphi^{\prime}(-R+\sqrt{3})-(R-\sqrt{3})\varphi^{\prime}(R-\sqrt{3})+\int_{-R}^{-R+\sqrt{3}}\varphi^{\prime}(x)\;\mathrm{d}x-\int_{R-\sqrt{3}}^{R}\varphi^{\prime}(x)\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=-(-R+\sqrt{3})\varphi^{\prime}(-R+\sqrt{3})-(R-\sqrt{3})\varphi^{\prime}(R-\sqrt{3})+\varphi(-R+\sqrt{3})+\varphi(R-\sqrt{3}).

Using the formula for
u^{\prime\prime}
above we find

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u^{\prime\prime}(x)\varphi^{\prime\prime}(x)\;\mathrm{d}x=(\varphi^{\prime}(-R+\sqrt{3})-\varphi^{\prime}(R-\sqrt{3}))-\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}(\varphi(R-\sqrt{3})+\varphi(-R+\sqrt{3})).

The distribution
\varphi\mapsto\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}(\varphi(R-\sqrt{3})+\varphi(-R+\sqrt{3}))
can clearly be represented by a Radon measure on
\Omega
since for each
\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
one has

\varphi(R-\sqrt{3})+\varphi(-R+\sqrt{3})=\int_{\Omega}\varphi\;\mathrm{d}(\delta_{R-\sqrt{3}}+\delta_{-R+\sqrt{3}}).

However, the distibution
\varphi\mapsto\varphi^{\prime}(-R+\sqrt{3})-\varphi^{\prime}(R-\sqrt{3})
is not represented by a Radon measure as the following lemma shows. In view of this the distribtion

u^{(4)}:\varphi\mapsto\int_{\Omega}u^{\prime\prime}(x)\varphi^{\prime\prime}(x)\;\mathrm{d}x

cannot be represented by a Radon measure.

Lemma 10
.

Let
\Omega=(-R,R)
for any
R>0
,
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}\in\Omega
mutually distinct and
c_{1},\ldots,c_{m}\in\mathbb{R}
. If there exists a signed Radon measure
\mu
on
\Omega
such that the distribution
\varphi\mapsto\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}\varphi^{\prime}(a_{i})
is represented by
\mu
, that is

\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}\varphi^{\prime}(a_{i})=\int_{\Omega}\varphi\;\mathrm{d}\mu\qquad\textrm{for all $\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)$,}

(23)

then
c_{1}=...=c_{m}=0
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality we may assume that
a_{1}<a_{2}<...<a_{m}
.
Suppose that
a_{1},...,a_{m}\in\Omega
and
c_{1},...,c_{m}\in\mathbb{R}
are chosen such that (
23
) holds for a Radon measure
\mu
. Let
\delta>0
be such that
a_{1},...,a_{m}\in(-R+\delta,R-\delta)
. Define
\Omega^{\prime}:=(-R+\delta,R-\delta)
and observe that
\mu
is a finite signed measure on
\Omega^{\prime}
. Then one can use Fubini’s theorem as follows: For each
\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega^{\prime})
one has

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\varphi\;\mathrm{d}\mu

\displaystyle=\int_{(-R+\delta,R-\delta)}\left(\int_{(-R+\delta,x)}\varphi^{\prime}(s)\;\mathrm{d}s\right)\;\mathrm{d}\mu(x)=\int_{(-R+\delta,R-\delta)}\left(\int_{(s,R-\delta)}\;\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\right)\varphi^{\prime}(s)\;\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\int_{(-R+\delta,R-\delta)}\mu((s,R-\delta))\varphi^{\prime}(s)\;\mathrm{d}s.

This and (
23
) imply that for each
\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega^{\prime})
one has

\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}\varphi^{\prime}(a_{i})=\int_{(-R+\delta,R-\delta)}\mu((s,R-\delta))\varphi^{\prime}(s)\;\mathrm{d}s=\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}f(s)\varphi^{\prime}(s)\;\mathrm{d}s,

(24)

where
f(s):=\mu((s,R-\delta))
defines a function in
L^{\infty}(\Omega^{\prime}).

Now choose any function
\eta\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega^{\prime})
such that
\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\eta=1
. Notice that for each
\psi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega^{\prime})
the function
\varphi:\Omega^{\prime}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},

\varphi(x):=\int_{-R+\delta}^{x}\psi(s)\;\mathrm{d}s-\int_{-R+\delta}^{x}\eta(s)\;\mathrm{d}s\left(\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\psi(y)\;\mathrm{d}y\right)
lies in
C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega^{\prime})
. Using this as a test function in (
24
) we find for any
\psi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega^{\prime})

\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}\left(\psi(a_{i})-\eta(a_{i})\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\psi(y)\;\mathrm{d}y\right)=\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}f(s)\left(\psi(s)-\eta(s)\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\psi(y)\;\mathrm{d}y\right)\;\mathrm{d}s.

Rearranging terms we find

\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}\psi(a_{i})-\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}\eta(a_{i})\right)\psi(y)\;\mathrm{d}y=\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}f(s)\psi(s)\;\mathrm{d}s-\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\left(\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}f(s)\eta(s)\;\mathrm{d}s\right)\psi(y)\;\mathrm{d}y.

Defining
g(s):=f(s)+\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}\eta(a_{i})-\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}f(y)\eta(y)\;\mathrm{d}y
(switching the names of the variables
y
and
s
in the last summand) we obtain that
g\in L^{\infty}(\Omega^{\prime})
and

\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}\psi(a_{i})=\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}g(s)\psi(s)\;\mathrm{d}s\qquad\textrm{for all $\psi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega^{\prime})$.}

(25)

Considering test functions
\psi\in C_{0}^{\infty}((-R+\delta,a_{1}))
we infer that
g|_{(-R+\delta,a_{1})}=0
almost everywhere. Next, for
i=1,...,m-1
, using test functions
\psi\in C_{0}^{\infty}((a_{i},a_{i+1}))
yields
g|_{(a_{i},a_{i+1})}=0
almost everywhere. Finally, considering test functions
\psi\in C_{0}^{\infty}((a_{m},R-\delta))
we infer that
g|_{(a_{m},R-\delta)}=0
almost everywhere. Since
\{a_{1},...,a_{m}\}
is finite we conclude that
g\equiv 0
almost everywhere and in view of (
25
) we find

\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}\psi(a_{i})=0\qquad\textrm{for all $\psi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega^{\prime})$}.

As a result one readily obtains
c_{1}=...=c_{m}=0
as stated.
∎

6.2
Regularity of measure-valued solutions

Lemma 11
.

Suppose that
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a smooth domain and
v\in H^{2}(\Omega)
satisfies (
22
). Then
v\in W^{3,q}_{loc}(\Omega)
for any
q\in(1,\frac{n}{n-1})
.

Proof.

Let
\Omega^{\prime}\subset\subset\Omega
be a smooth subdomain and fix
q\in(1,\frac{n}{n-1})
. Then
\mu
is a finite signed measure on
\Omega^{\prime}
. By
[
18
, Proposition 5.1]
there exists some
w\in W^{1,q}_{0}(\Omega^{\prime})
such that

\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\nabla w\cdot\nabla\varphi\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\varphi\;\mathrm{d}\mu\qquad\textrm{for all $\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega^{\prime})$.}

In particular, integration by parts implies

\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}w\Delta\varphi\;\mathrm{d}x=-\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\varphi\;\mathrm{d}\mu\qquad\textrm{for all $\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega^{\prime})$.}

Notice that by (
22
) we have

\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\Delta v\Delta\varphi\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\varphi\;\mathrm{d}\mu\qquad\textrm{for all $\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega^{\prime})$},

Adding the previous two equations we find

\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}(\Delta v+w)\Delta\varphi\;\mathrm{d}x=0\qquad\textrm{for all $\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega^{\prime})$},

that is,
\Delta v+w
is harmonic on
\Omega^{\prime}
and therefore lies in
C^{\infty}(\Omega^{\prime})
. Since
w\in W^{1,q}(\Omega^{\prime})
we infer that
\Delta v\in W^{1,q}_{loc}(\Omega^{\prime})
. Elliptic regularity yields
v\in W^{3,q}_{loc}(\Omega^{\prime})
. Since
\Omega
can be exhausted by domains with smooth boundary we have
v\in W^{3,q}_{loc}(\Omega)
.
∎

Now we show that our global minimizer found defined in (
6
) does not lie in
W^{3,q}_{loc}(\Omega)
for any
q\geq 1
.

Lemma 12
.

Let
u
be defined as in (
6
) and
\Omega=B:=B_{1}(0)
. Then
u\in W^{2,\infty}(\Omega)
but
u\not\in W^{3,q}_{loc}(\Omega)
for any
q\geq 1
.

Proof.

One readily checks that
u\in W^{2,\infty}(\Omega)
and for a.e.
x\in B_{1}(0)
we have

D^{2}u(x)=\mathrm{diag}(0,...,0,\chi_{B^{+}}(x)).

Assume now that
u\in W^{3,q}_{loc}(\Omega)
for some
q\geq 1
. Then, in particular
\partial_{nn}u=\chi_{B^{+}}\in W^{1,q}_{loc}(\Omega)
for some
q\geq 1
. This cannot be true (e.g. since Sobolev functions must be continuous along almost every ray
\{x_{1}=s\}\cap\Omega
, cf.
[
8
, Theorem 4.21]
).
∎

In
[
9
, Example 2 and 4]
it was shown for
n=2
that for a suitably small constant boundary datum
u_{0}>0
minimizers
u
of
\mathcal{F}(\cdot,B_{1}(0))
under Dirichlet (respectively Navier) boundary conditions are unique and satisfy for almost every
x\in B_{1}(0)

\Delta u(x)=\begin{cases}0&|x|<\rho\\
C\log|x|+D&|x|>\rho\end{cases},

(26)

for some
C,D\in\mathbb{R}
and
\rho\in(0,1)
.
Moreover,
[
9
, Remark 9 and Remark 11]
show that
\Delta u\not\in C^{0}(B_{1}(0)),
that is
C\log(\rho)+D\neq 0
. The following lemma shows that in this case
u\not\in W^{3,1}_{loc}(B_{1}(0))
.

Lemma 13
.

Suppose that
u\in H^{2}(B_{1}(0))
satisfies (
26
) for some
C,D\in\mathbb{R}
and
\rho\in(0,1)
. Then
u\in W^{3,1}_{loc}(B_{1}(0))
if and only if
C\log(\rho)+D=0
.

Proof.

It is an immediate consequence of
[
8
, Theorem 4.21]
that Sobolev functions must also be continuous along almost every ray
\{r\sigma:r\in(0,1)\}

(\sigma\in\partial B_{1}(0))
. If we pick one such ray and use (
26
) we infer that
C\log\rho+D=0
. The opposite direction is an easy computation and left to the reader.
∎
