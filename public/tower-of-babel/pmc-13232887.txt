Dectin-1 signaling promotes Galectin-3 shedding and expansion of immunosuppressive CD71+ erythroid cells in breast cancer
Authors: Ako Azimi, Amirhossein Rahmati, Shima Shahbaz, Zahra Elahi, Alireza Zahiriabyaneh, Junyan Cao, Ernesto Luisito Padua, Chloe Church, Emmanuel T. Alimo, Afsaneh Lavasanifar, John Walker, Shokrollah Elahi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTImmunotherapy with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) has transformed cancer treatment, yet many patients fail to respond, underscoring the need to better understand mechanisms of resistance. One emerging mechanism involves the recruitment of erythroid progenitors and precursors, known as CD71+ erythroid cells (CECs), to the tumor microenvironment. Using murine models of triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), we investigated the therapeutic effects of curdlan, a β-glucan. We found that TNBC was associated with extramedullary erythropoiesis and expansion of CECs. Unexpectedly, curdlan worsened tumor progression by further increasing CEC abundance. Immunophenotypic analysis revealed a marked expansion of Galectin-3 (Gal-3)+ CECs in both spleen and tumor. Mechanistically, our findings suggest that curdlan-Dectin-1ligation induces Gal-3 shedding from CD11b+cells, enabling its deposition onto CECs. Notably, we discovered that Gal-3 is co-expressed and colocalized with PD-L1 and Dectin-1 on CECs. Importantly, PD-L1 blockade substantially reduced immunosuppressive Gal-3+PD-L1+ CECs, suppressed extramedullary erythropoiesis, and induced pronounced tumor regression. Re-analysis of publicly available scRNAseq datasets further confirmed the abundance of Gal-3+ CECs in human bone marrow. Collectively, these findings identify Gal-3+PD-L1+ CECs as key drivers of an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment and reveal a previously unrecognized axis through which PD-L1 blockade exerts therapeutic benefit in breast cancer.

Introduction

The incidence of breast cancer has continued to rise and remains the leading cause of cancer-related death in women globally. 1 Breast cancers are heterogeneous and typically classified into seven subtypes. Among them, triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is the most aggressive subtype of breast cancer. 2 It accounts for 15–20% of cases of breast cancer with frequent metastasis to the lungs, brain, and bones. TNBC is a subtype of breast cancer that clinically lacks the expression of estrogen and progesterone receptors (ER/PR) and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2), which does not respond to common hormone therapies or HER2-targeted therapies, leaving patients with limited treatment options. 3 Immunotherapy, particularly immune checkpoint blockade (ICB), has revolutionized cancer treatment. However, it shows limited effectiveness in poorly immunogenic solid tumors, which are characterized by low mutation burden, reduced T cell infiltration, and an abundance of immunosuppressive cells. 4 , 5 Therefore, identifying novel therapeutic pathways in the tumor microenvironment (TME) is becoming the main goal of immunotherapy. 5 Although current immunotherapy strategies primarily target adaptive immune cells, immunophenotyping of solid tumor TMEs shows that myeloid cells are the predominant infiltrating immune cells. 6 , 7 Therefore, reprogramming of myeloid cells via the pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) in the TME from a pro-tumor to an anti-tumor phenotype has received substantial interest. For example, dendritic cell-associated C-type lectin-1 (Dectin-1), one of well-described members of C-type lectin receptors (CLRs) has emerged recently as a potential target to modulate myeloid cells. Dectin-1 interacts with various ligands, including β-glucans, mannans, proteins, and lipids, found on pathogens and cancer cells. 8 A limited number of studies have reported that targeting Dectin-1 by cognate agonists, such as curdlan, a yeast derived β-1,3-glucan polysaccharide modulates anti-tumor responses. 9 , 10

Under physiological conditions, Dectin-1 ligation activates a signaling cascade involving Syk kinase 11 and the CARD9 adaptor protein, 12 which in turn activate pathways such as MAPK and NFκB. 13 , 14 This activation triggers the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS). 15 , 16 However, the ultimate outcomes of Dectin-1 activation depend on the type of ligands and microenvironment milieu. For this reason, the application of curdlan to modulate myeloid cells through Dectin-1 has been the subject of debate and controversy. For example, in cancer models like B16-F10 melanoma and MDA-MB-231 mammary cancer, Dectin-1-mediated trained immunity has been shown to enhance immune responses and promote anti-tumor immunity. 17 In the B16-F10 melanoma model, ligation of Dectin-1 on dendritic cells (DCs) by N-glycans on B16 cells leads to the upregulation of INAM, which subsequently enhances the tumor-killing activity of NK cells. 17 Furthermore, Dectin-1 ligation promotes Th9 differentiation by upregulating TNFSF15 and OX40L on DCs, boosting overall immune responses. 18 Similarly, targeting Dectin-1 results in a substantial downregulation of PD-L1 and VISTA expressing myeloid cells, which results in tumor regression in the B16-F10 melanoma model. 9

In contrast, in the colorectal cancer (CRC) model, Dectin-1 signaling promotes tumor progression by inducing PGE2-synthesizing enzymes, which subsequently suppress IL22RA2 expression. Administration of the Dectin-1 antagonist laminarin reduces tumor development in this mouse model. 19 Additionally, in DSS-induced colorectal cancer, Dectin-1 signaling drives the expansion of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) within intestinal tumors through cytokines, such as IL-6 and IL-1β. This effect is inhibited by treatment with the Dectin-1 antagonist laminarin. 19 Similar observations were made in the CT26 colorectal model, where curdlan treatment promoted tumor progression. 9 Dectin-1 plays a tumor-promoting role in pancreatic cancer by binding to Galectin-9 (Gal-9) on macrophages and ductal tumor cells, inhibiting macrophage differentiation into anti-tumor M1 phenotypes. 10 However, the effects of targeting Dectin-1 on erythropoiesis and subsequently tumor progression remain unexplored.

Erythroid progenitors and precursors, defined as CD71+ erythroid cells (CECs), co-expression CD71 and TER119 in mice but CD71 and CD235a in humans. 20 CECs mediate their immunosuppressive properties through the release of soluble factors, including TGF-β, arginase-1, arginase-2, and reactive oxygen species (ROS) or via cell-cell interactions through the expression of co-inhibitory molecules, such as PD-L1/PD-L2, VISTA, Galectin-3 (Gal-3), and Gal-9. 21-24

Although the role of galectins in erythropoiesis is not well understood, a recent study has highlighted the role of Gal-9 in promoting the expression of mTOR and AMPK in erythroid progenitors. 25 Similarly, we have reported that Gal-3 is predominantly expressed on erythroid progenitors in the spleen, liver, bone marrow (BM), and other tissues during development. Additionally, we found that the presence of Gal-3 on erythroid progenitors promotes the activation of pathways, such as phospho-LCK, MAPK, and STAT5 that associated with cell proliferation, differentiation and survival. 26 CECs during the process of maturation downregulate CD45 and as such mature red blood cells (RBCs) lack this glycoprotein. 27 Therefore, CECs can be classified into CD45+ and CD45-subsets, with CD45+ exhibiting greater immunosuppressive potency compared to their negative counterparts. 28 , 29 Although CECs are scarce in the periphery of human adults and in low frequency in the spleen of adult mice, they are abundant in the peripheral blood of human neonates, newborn mice, fetal liver, and get expanded during pregnancy. 20 , 28 , 30 , 31 Additionally, CECs are expanded in the periphery under pathological conditions, including stress hematopoiesis, tumor-induced anemia, and chronic viral infections. 23 , 32 The severity of anemia has been shown to correlate with an increase in the frequency of CECs and subsequently the lack of avelumab efficacy in viral-associated solid tumors. 33 This resistance to immunotherapy is likely attributable to the potent immunosuppressive mechanisms of CECs, which present a major challenge for mono-target immunotherapies. While the expression and function of targeting Dectin-1 in myeloid cells have been extensively studied, its presence and function in CECs remain unknown.

Therefore, this study was designed to evaluate the expression of Dectin-1 in CECs and investigate the effects of curdlan-induced Dectin-1 signaling in erythropoiesis. Additionally, we explored phenotypical characteristics and immunosuppressive properties of curdlan-induced CECs in a TNBC model. Finally, we investigated whether CECs can be targeted by anti-PD-L1 therapy.

Materials and methods

In vivo experiments

The experimental study protocols were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committees (ACUCs) at the University of Alberta (Protocol # AUP00002737) in agreement with the guidelines outlined in the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals established by The Canadian Council for Animal Care. All animal studies were conducted under AAALAC-accredited ethical standards and are reported in accordance with ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines. We used the minimum number of animals necessary for statistical rigor. Housing, handling, and experimental procedures met all requirements of the Canadian Council on Animal Care. Isoflurane was used for the induction and maintenance of anesthesia during tumor inoculation. The animals were euthanized using carbon dioxide (CO₂) in accordance with institutional and regulatory guidelines.

Wild-type C57BL/6, BALB/c, and C57BL/6 Dectin-1 knockout (Clec7a^-/-) mice were purchased from Charles River Laboratories and Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME), respectively. To ensure experimental consistency, age- and sex-matched mice (6–8 weeks old females) were utilized in all studies. The EO771 and 4T1 breast cancer cell lines were originally obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA). EO771/4T1 breast cancer cells were cultured under standard conditions and suspended in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) prior to injection. Each mouse was inoculated with 5 × 105 EO771 or 4T1 cells in a total volume of 100 μL into the fourth mammary fat pad. Following tumor cell injection, mice were monitored regularly for tumor growth, overall health status, and any signs of distress.

In some studies, intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of curdlan (# C7821, MilliporeSigma) was administered (300 μg per mouse) twice per week, 9 starting 3 d after EO771 or 4T1 cell inoculation and continuing until the experimental endpoint at day 24. The control mice received an equal volume of PBS. For the tumor-free experiments, mice were injected with curdlan at the same concentration (300 μg per mouse) as in the tumor-bearing model. The control mice were injected with PBS. After three curdlan or PBS injections, mice were euthanized using CO2 for tissue harvesting and immune cell isolation. For the anti-PD-L1 (Cat#BE0101, Bio X cell) treatment, mice in the treated group received 200 μg of anti-PD-L1 in 50 μL of PBS via i.p. twice a week, beginning on day 8 when the tumor mass was palpable. The control mice were administered 50 μL of PBS. Similarly, for the anti-CD71 (8D3) treatment (10 μg/mouse), 28 mice were administered via i.p. twice a week for a total of 5 injections, while controls received an isotype antibody.

Tissue processing and cell isolation

To generate a single-cell suspension, spleens were gently dissociated between sterile frosted glass slides in 5 mL of 1 × RBC lysis buffer, followed by dilution with an equal volume (5 mL) of RPMI 1640 culture medium according to our protocol. 34 The resulting cell suspension was passed through a nylon mesh to remove debris. Cells were subsequently resuspended in RPMI 1640 medium (MilliporeSigma) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; MilliporeSigma), 1% penicillin-streptomycin (MilliporeSigma), and nonessential amino acids (MilliporeSigma) to support cell viability.

For tumor tissue processing, tumors were aseptically dissected and washed twice with Hanks’ Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS, MilliporeSigma). The tissues were then finely minced using a sterile scalpel and enzymatically digested in a lysis buffer containing DNase (20 U, MilliporeSigma) and Collagenase Type IV (2000 U, MilliporeSigma). The digested samples were transferred to 15-mL conical tubes and incubated at 37 °C for 25 min in a shaking incubator to facilitate tissue dissociation. Following enzymatic digestion, the tumor suspensions were washed with RPMI 1640 medium and sequentially filtered through 70 and 40 μm nylon meshes to remove residual aggregates. To eliminate excess tissue fragments and debris, the cell suspensions were subjected to density gradient centrifugation using Ficoll®-Paque PREMIUM 1.084. Finally, the isolated cells were thoroughly washed and prepared for further analyses.

Human study

Studies involving human subjects were approved by the Health Research Ethics Board of the Alberta Cancer Committee (HREBA; protocol #CC-17-0307). The study participant provided written informed consent prior to enrollment.

Flow cytometry analysis

Antibodies conjugated to fluorophores and targeting mouse and human cell antigens were sourced from BD Biosciences, Thermo Fisher Scientific, or BioLegend. A complete list of antibodies is provided in Supplementary Table 1. The LIVE/DEAD staining kit (Invitrogen) was used to exclude dead cells. Surface and intracellular staining were performed according to our protocols. 30 , 35 , 36 Intracellular levels of TGF-β and arginase-I were measured following the intracellular staining protocol. 22 Paraformaldehyde-fixed cells were examined using a BD LSR Fortessa-SORP or Fortessa-X20 flow cytometer (BD Biosciences). Flow cytometry data were analyzed using FlowJo software (version 10).

ELISA assays

Galectin-3 and TGF-β concentrations were quantified using DuoSet ELISA kit (R&D Systems, Cat#DY1197 and Cat#DY1679, respectively) following the manufacturer's protocols.

Image stream analysis

Surface-stained murine splenocytes (1 × 10⁶ cells) were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde prior to image acquisition. Imaging flow cytometry was performed using the Amnis ImageStream Mark II (EMD Millipore, ON, Canada), with a minimum of 10,000 images collected per sample, as previously described. 37 , 38 Data analysis was conducted using the IDEAS software package, where single-cell events in focus were selected for further evaluation. Sequential gating strategies were applied to identify CECs (TER119+ CD71+ cells), followed by the selection of cells co-expressing Gal-3 and PD-L1. The colocalization of Gal-3 with PD-L1 was assessed using the IDEAS colocalization wizard.

Proliferation assay

For the proliferation assay, CECs or Gal-3+ CECs were isolated from the spleens of both untreated and curdlan-treated tumor-bearing mice using anti-Gal-3 and anti-TER119 (TER-119) antibodies and a magnetic column. T cells were also isolated (STEMCELL Technologies), labeled with Cell Trace violet dye (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and cultured in 96‐well plates in the absence or presence of CECs at 1:1 ratio. T cell stimulation was performed using anti‐CD3 (1 μg mL−1) and mitomycin‐treated whole splenocytes as we have reported elsewhere. 39 , 40

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq)

Data from the NCBI dataset PRJNA492227 were analyzed in R (v4.4.3) using Seurat (v5.1.0). 41 A Seurat object was generated with genes expressed in at least three cells and cells expressing a minimum of 200 genes. Doublets (>4,000 genes) and cells with high mitochondrial gene content (>10%) were excluded using subset. Data from 25 patients were combined using merge, log-normalized, and scaled. Dimensionality reduction was conducted using RunPCA, FindNeighbours, and FindClusters (30 principal components, resolution 0.3). Batch effects were corrected with IntegrateLayers. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified with FindAllMarkers (min. pct = 0.25, logFC threshold = 0.25, P < 0.05). Clusters were annotated with singleR using the celldex NovershternHematopoieticData reference. 42 , 43 We used subset to select erythroid cells and reclustered them for further analysis. Dot plots, feature plots, and heatmaps were generated with DotPlot, featurePlot, and DoHeatmap functions, respectively.

Statistical analysis

To assess statistical differences between groups, the Mann-Whitney U-test or Kruskal–Wallis analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for non-normally distributed data. Post hoc tests include Dunn’s multiple comparisons for (Kruskal–Wallis analysis) and Tukey-Kramer test (one-way ANOVA with multiple comparisons). All statistical analyses were performed using Prism 10.2.3 (GraphPad Software, version 347). Data are presented as mean ± SD, with a p-value of < 0.05 considered statistically significant. In violin plots, the middle line represents the median, the bottom line 1st quartile, and the top line 3rd quartile.

Results

Curdlan treatment expands CECs and promotes breast cancer tumor progression

To investigate the role of Dectin-1 in the EO771 murine model, we administered curdlan twice a week into tumor-bearing mice starting 3 d after the cell line transplantation. Mice were euthanized on day 24 (Figure 1A). Our results revealed a significant increase in tumor weight and volume in curdlan-treated tumor-bearing mice (T-TB) compared to untreated tumor-bearing mice (U-TB) (Figure 1B–D). Similarly, spleen weight in the U-TB group was significantly higher than in the tumor-free spleen (TF) group, with an even greater increase in the T-TB group (Figure 1E,F). We found a significant increase in the frequency of splenic CECs in the U-TB group compared to the TF group, which was further elevated after curdlan treatment (Figure 1G,H and Supplementary Figure 1A). Notably, we observed a significant increase in the frequency of CECs in the TME after curdlan administration (Figure 1I,J).

We next evaluated whether this effect was specific to the E0771 model or could be observed in other breast tumor models. Using the 4T1 model, we found that curdlan treatment, administered using the same regimen as in E0771 (Figure 1A), significantly promoted tumor progression, as evidenced by increased tumor weight and volume (Supplementary Figure 1B–D) and splenomegaly (Supplementary Figure 1E,F). Consistently, we observed expansion of splenic CECs (Supplementary Figure 1G,H) and their presence, especially in the TME of curdlan-treated mice (Supplementary Figure 1I,J). Together, these findings indicate that curdlan treatment expands CECs in both the spleen and TME, promotes splenomegaly, and enhances tumor progression across both breast cancer models.

Erythroid progenitors express Dectin-1

To explore the potential impact of curdlan treatment on erythropoiesis, we assessed the expression of Dectin-1 in CECs obtained from the spleen, liver, and BM of young tumor-free mice. We found that a portion of CD45+ CECs constitutively express Dectin-1, which was more prominent in those residing in the liver and BM (Figure 1K,L). These observations prompted us to examine whether this population is modulated under tumor conditions in response to curdlan treatment. We found that the frequency of splenic Dectin-1⁺ CECs was significantly increased in tumor-bearing mice compared to controls, and this expansion was further enhanced following curdlan treatment (Figure 1M,N). Phenotypic analysis revealed that Dectin-1⁺ CECs were enriched for CD45 expression (Supplementary Figure 1K), indicative of a more immature erythroid progenitor phenotype. 44 Functionally, Dectin-1⁺ CECs exhibited higher levels of ROS and arginase I compared to their Dectin-1⁻ counterparts (Supplementary Figure 1L,M), indicating a more pronounced immunosuppressive profile. Notably, Gal-3+ CECs were also expanded in curdlan-treated mice (Supplementary Figure 1N,O) and showed strong co-expression with Dectin-1 (Supplementary Figure 1P,Q). Moreover, co-localization of Gal-3 and Dectin-1 was observed within a subset of these cells (Figure 1O and Supplementary Figure 1R). These results identify a functionally distinct subset of Dectin-1⁺ CECs that expands in tumor-bearing hosts and is further enriched by curdlan treatment.

Tumor modulates the frequency of splenic CECs in the presence and absence of curdlan treatment

We have previously reported that CECs possess immunoregulatory properties and exert their suppressive effects through several mechanisms, including the expression of co-inhibitory receptors (e.g. PD-L1/PD-L2, and VISTA). 21 , 22 To better delineate the impact of curdlan on the effector functions of CECs, we quantified the expression of PD-L1, PD-L2, VISTA, Gal-3, and Gal-9 in splenic CECs following curdlan treatment. Our results showed a significant increase in the percentages of PD-L1+ CECs in TB groups compared to the TF group, while no significant difference was observed between U-TB and T-TB groups (Figure 2A–C). However, the intensity of PD-L1 expression remained unchanged in splenic CECs between groups (Supplementary Figure 2A,B). Similarly, we observed a significant increase in the frequency of PD-L2+ CECs after curdlan treatment compared to the U-TB and TF groups (Figure 2D–F), without any changes in PD-L2 intensity (Supplementary Figure 2C,D). Considering that VISTA+ CECs promote the induction of regulatory T cells (Tregs), 22 we evaluated the impact of curdlan on VISTA expression. We noted that although tumor-bearing mice had an elevated frequency of VISTA+ CECs, curdlan treatment did not alter this effect (Figure 2G–I). A similar observation was made for the intensity of VISTA expression in splenic CECs (Supplementary Figure 2E,F). Additionally, we found that curdlan treatment neither changed the proportion of Gal-9-expressing CECs (Figure 2J–L) nor its intensity in these cells (Supplementary Figure 2G,H). However, we observed a dramatic increase in both the frequency of Gal-3+ CECs and its intensity (Figure 2M–Q). Notably, this alteration was absent in tumor-bearing mice, supporting the potential role of curdlan and Dectin-1 ligation in the expansion of Gal-3+ CECs. Considering that CD45-expressing CECs exhibit heightened immunosuppressive properties, 23 , 28 we assessed the frequency of Gal-3+ in relation to CD45 expression. Our findings revealed that, in contrast to Gal-3- CECs, the majority of Gal-3+ CECs express CD45 (Figure 2R,S). Finally, we evaluated the co-expression of the aforementioned markers in splenic CECs in TF and TB mice in the absence and presence of curdlan treatment. We found a significant increase in the frequency of Gal-3+PD-L1+ (Figure 2T,U), Gal-3+PD-L2+ (Figure 2V,W), and Gal-3+VISTA+ (Figure 2X,Y) co-expressing CECs. However, no significant differences were observed in the co-expression of other markers across the different groups except PD-L2+VISTA+CECs (Supplementary Figure 2I–O). Our findings indicate that curdlan modulates the expression of regulatory molecules in splenic CECs.

Curdlan treatment increases the frequency of CECs co-expressing multiple co-inhibitory receptors in the TME

Considering the substantial expansion of CECs in the TME (Figure 1I,J), we phenotyped them based on the expression of various co-inhibitory molecules. Our results revealed that while the frequency of PD-L1+ CECs remained unchanged in curdlan-treated tumor-bearing mice (Supplementary Figure 3A), the intensity of its expression was significantly increased in the treated group (Figure 3A,B). Additionally, we did not observe any significant difference in the frequency or intensity of expression of PD-L2, VISTA, and Gal-9-expressing CECs in the TME of curdlan-treated mice (Supplementary Figure 3B–J). Consistent with our findings in the spleen, we observed a significant increase in both the frequency of Gal-3+CECs and the intensity of Gal-3 expression in CECs from the TME of curdlan-treated tumor-bearing mice (Figure 3C–F). Next, we compared the expression of these inhibitory molecules in splenic and TME CECs from tumor-bearing mice. We found that CECs within the TME exhibited a significantly higher proportion of PD-L1+, PD-L2+, Gal-9+ (Supplementary Figure 3K–M), and Gal-3+ (Figure 3G) expressing cells, regardless of curdlan treatment, compared to their splenic counterparts. However, we did not observe any difference in the frequency of VISTA+CECs between the spleen and TME of both groups. These observations suggest differential phenotypic characteristics of CECs in the spleen and TME.

We then analyzed the co-expression of these inhibitory molecules in TME CECs following curdlan treatment. We found a significant increase in the percentages of CECs co-expressing Gal-3/PD-L1, PD-L2/PD-L1, and PD-L2/Gal-3 in curdlan-treated group compared to the untreated group (Figure 3H–M). However, there was no significant difference in the frequency of CECs co-expressing Gal-3/VISTA and Gal-9/PD-L2 between the two groups (Supplementary Figure 3N,O). Furthermore, we observed a significant increase in the frequency of CECs co-expressing Gal-9/Gal-3, VISTA/PD-L1, Gal-9/PD-L1, VISTA/Gal-9, and VISTA/PD-L2 in the curdlan-treated group (Supplementary Figure 3P–Y).

These observations indicate that the TME promotes the expression of different inhibitory molecules in CECs, which is further amplified by curdlan, potentially enhancing their immunosuppressive properties.

Gal-3 co-localizes with PD-L1 on CECs

Considering the identical expression pattern of PD-L1 and Gal-3 in tSNE plots (Figure 2C,O), we utilized the ImageStream approach to determine the interaction of these molecules, as reported for other molecules. 9 , 32 These analyses confirmed the co-localization of Gal-3 with PD-L1 in CECs (Figure 3N and Supplementary Figure 4).

Expansion of Gal-3+ CECs in the spleen and BM of tumor-free mice following curdlan treatment

Our results so far have shown that curdlan treatment leads to the expansion of Gal-3+ CECs in both the spleen and TME of TB mice. To investigate whether curdlan has the same effect on CECs in tumor-free mice, we treated mice with curdlan in the same manner as tumor-bearing mice. Our results revealed a significant increase in spleen weight in curdlan-treated tumor-free mice (Figure 4A,B). We also observed a significant expansion of CECs following curdlan treatment, which was enriched with CD45+ CECs (Figure 4C–F). Additionally, we found a significant increase in the frequency of Gal-3+ CECs and the intensity of Gal-3 expression in CECs in curdlan-treated mice compared to the control group (Figure 4G–J). As observed in tumor-bearing mice (Figure 2R,S), we noted that the majority of Gal-3+ CECs were erythroid progenitors expressing CD45 (Figure 4K,L). These observations suggest that curdlan-mediated Dectin-1 signaling induces extramedullary erythropoiesis, supported by a significant increase in CD45+ CECs in the treated group. 27 To investigate whether curdlan influences the medullary erythropoiesis, leading to extramedullary hematopoiesis, we examined the frequency of CECs in the BM of curdlan-treated mice. Our analysis revealed a significant reduction in the frequency of CECs in the BM of treated mice compared to the control group (Figure 4M,N). Despite the reduction in the frequency of CECs following curdlan treatment, the proportion of Gal-3-expressing CECs was significantly increased (Figure 4O,P). Similar to the spleen, Gal-3+ CECs were highly enriched in the CD45+ CEC subset in the BM (Figure 4Q,R). Considering that Gal-3⁺ CECs, despite a greater proliferative capacity, exhibit a more apoptotic phenotype under physiological conditions, 26 we subjected curdlan-induced CECs to further analysis. We found that Gal-3⁺ CECs express higher levels of p38-MAPK and STAT-5 than their negative counterparts, without any significant difference between curdlan-treated and control groups (Supplementary Figure 5A,B). In support of the apoptotic feature of Gal-3+ CECs, our data further revealed that the frequency of Fas/FasL-expressing CECs within the Gal-3+ subset was significantly increased following curdlan treatment (Figure 4S–U). However, while the intensity of Fas remained unchanged between both groups, treated mice had elevated levels of FasL (Supplementary Figure 5C,D), leading to enhanced apoptosis of Gal-3+ CECs in the treated versus the control group (Figure 4V,W). Collectively, these findings indicate that curdlan treatment facilitates the expansion of Gal-3+ CECs, which exhibit more apoptotic potential.

Curdlan treatment upregulates the expression of arginase-I, TGF-β-LAP, and enhances the production of ROS in splenic CECs

Given that CECs, in addition to cell-cell interactions, mediate their immunosuppressive function through the generation of ROS, arginase-I, arginase-II, and TGF-β, 20 , 23 , 28 , 44 we assessed these regulatory properties in splenic CECs after curdlan treatment. Our results show a significant elevation in ROS production, arginase-I, and TGF-β expression in total and CD45+ CECs in the treated group compared to the control group (Figure 5A–F and Supplementary Figure 5E–G). Additionally, we found that Gal-3+ CECs expressed higher levels of ROS, arginase-I, and TGF-β after curdlan treatment (Figure 5G–L). To determine the immunosuppressive properties of curdlan-induced CECs, we performed a T cell proliferation assay. We found that curdlan-induced CECs exhibited significantly more pronounced suppression of both CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cell proliferation than CECs obtained from the control group (Figure 5M–P). Taken together, these data indicate that curdlan treatment promotes the expansion of CECs and Gal-3+ CECs with enhanced immunosuppressive properties.

Curdlan mediates its effects through Dectin-1 signaling

β-glucans, such as curdlan, have been shown to interact with receptors, such as Dectin-1 and Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4). 45 To determine whether the observed effects of curdlan are mediated exclusively through Dectin-1 and its subsequent signaling cascade, we utilized the Clec7a Knockout (Dectin-1 KO, DKO) mice.

Considering that a subset of CECs express Dectin-1 under physiological conditions (Figure 1K,L), we evaluated the effects of curdlan treatment on the frequency of splenic Dectin-1+ CECs in wild-type mice. We found that curdlan treatment promotes the expansion of Dectin-1+ CECs (Supplementary Figure 5H,I). In contrast to the wild-type mice (Figure 4C–I), the frequencies of both CECs, Gal-3+ CECs, and spleen weight remained unchanged in DKO mice after curdlan treatment (Supplementary Figure 5J–O). The same observations were made in the BM (Supplementary Figure 5P–S). These observations suggest that the effects of curdlan on CECs are mediated through Dectin-1 and its downstream signaling pathways.

Curdlan promotes the expansion of CECs with more potent characteristics

Considering the expansion of CECs co-expressing PD-L1 and Gal-3 (Figure 2T,U), we conducted additional studies to evaluate their properties. We found that Gal-3+PD-L1+ CECs express significantly higher levels of ROS, arginase-I, and TGF-β compared to other subsets (Supplementary Figure 6A–F). Additionally, the Gal-3+PD-L1+ CECs sub-population showed significantly greater surface co-expression of TGF-β/LAP and glycoprotein A repetitions predominant (GARP: a docking receptor for TGF-β-LAP) compared to the other sub-populations, highlighting their potential for releasing the active form of TGF-β (Supplementary Figure 6G–I). Moreover, we found that the Gal-3+PD-L1+ CEC subset was enriched with a higher frequency of TGF-β/LAP+GARP+ cells and showed greater expression of TGF-β once mice were treated with curdlan (Supplementary Figure 6J–O). These observations indicate that curdlan promotes the expansion of PD-L1+Gal-3+ CECs with a more potent phenotype. We next tested a mechanistic hypothesis suggested by our earlier findings that Gal-3 enhances the activation and release of TGF-β through GARP-LAP-TGF-β complexes on the surface of CECs. 26 To determine whether Gal-3 promotes TGF-β activation, isolated CECs were treated with recombinant Gal-3 (rGal-3: 1 μg), and TGF-β levels in the supernatant were measured after 4 h. CECs treated with rGal-3 released significantly higher levels of TGF-β compared to untreated CECs (Supplementary Figure 6P). Consistent with this, TGF-β levels were elevated in supernatants from T-TB tumor homogenates relative to U-TB tumor homogenates (Supplementary Figure 6Q), as well as in plasma from T-TF compared to U-TF (Supplementary Figure 6R). Together, these findings support a potential pathway in which Gal-3+ CECs contribute to tumor progression by promoting Gal-3-mediated activation of TGF-β following curdlan-induced Dectin-1 trained immunity.

Curdlan–Dectin-1 ligation promotes Gal-3 shedding from activated myeloid cells, leading to its deposition on CECs

The activation of neutrophils results in the shedding of CD44-bound Gal-9. 37 , 46 We hypothesized that curdlan-induced CD11b activation may result in Gal-3 shedding. Therefore, CD11b⁺ cells were stimulated with LPS, zymosan (TLR2/Dectin-1 agonist), or curdlan. After 24 hours, neither LPS nor zymosan changed Gal-3 expression; however, curdlan reduced Gal-3 intensity in CD11b⁺ cells (Figure 6A,B). Consistent with this reduction, ELISA analysis showed significantly elevated Gal-3 levels in the supernatant from T-TB tumor homogenates compared with the U-TB group (Figure 6C). Similarly, plasma from tumor-free mice exhibited increased Gal-3 following curdlan treatment, indicating that this effect occurs independently of tumor presence (Figure 6D). Moreover, supernatants collected after 4 h from cultured CD11b⁺ cells showed that cells isolated from treated mice released higher levels of Gal-3 than those from untreated mice (Figure 6E).

Notably, we observed the co-expression (Figure 6F) and colocalization of Dectin-1 and Gal-3 on CD11b⁺ cells (Figure 6G). To assess the interaction with curdlan, CD11b⁺ cells were incubated with calcofluor white (CFW)-labeled curdlan at 4 °C. Under these non-phagocytic conditions, CFW-curdlan associated with both Dectin-1 and Gal-3, showing clear co-localization (Figure 6H). In contrast, this association was not observed at 37 °C, a permissive temperature for phagocytosis. Instead, CFW-curdlan and Dectin-1 co-localized intracellularly, while surface Gal-3 intensity was markedly reduced (Figure 6H). These observations suggest that Gal-3 is associated with Dectin-1 on CD11b⁺ cells, which following Curdlan–Dectin-1 engagement, Gal-3 is shed rather than internalized.

We next hypothesized that shed Gal-3 may bind to other cells, including CECs. Supporting this, CECs cultured for 4 h with supernatants from CD11b⁺ cells isolated from curdlan-treated mice exhibited a significantly higher proportion of Gal-3⁺ CECs compared with CECs cultured with supernatants from untreated mice (Figure 6I,J).

To explore how Gal-3 directly modulates CEC signaling, CECs isolated from tumor-free mice were cultured in vitro with recombinant Gal-3 (1 µg/ml) for 6 h. We observed that Gal-3 treatment increased ERK1/2, LCK, p38-MAPK, and STAT5 signaling in CECs, suggesting that Gal-3 deposition actively induces intracellular pathways associated with CEC activation and immunoregulatory programming (Supplementary Figure 6S–Z). Together, these findings provide evidence that Curdlan–Dectin-1 activation triggers the shedding, rather than internalization, of Galectin-3, thereby increasing its extracellular availability. This mechanism offers important insight into how Curdlan-induced trained immunity increases the presence of Gal-3⁺ on CECs, a highly immunosuppressive CEC subset.

Dectin-1 activation promotes CEC expansion and CEC-dependent tumor progression

To determine whether the effects of curdlan are dependent on Dectin-1 signaling, Dectin-1-deficient mice were treated with curdlan and compared to wild-type controls. We found that curdlan treatment in DKO mice did not result in any significant changes in CEC frequency (Supplementary Figure 7A,B). Consistently, no differences were observed in tumor size or volume (Supplementary Figure 7C,D), nor in spleen size and weight (Supplementary Figure 7E,F), between curdlan-treated and control Dectin-1-deficient mice. These findings demonstrate that the expansion of CECs and the associated tumor-promoting effects induced by curdlan are dependent on Dectin-1 signaling. Next, to directly assess the functional contribution of CECs to tumor growth under Dectin-1 activation, we performed CD71 blockade (anti-CD71 antibody) in the 4T1 tumor model. While curdlan treatment significantly promoted tumor progression, CD71 blockade markedly attenuated tumor growth under the same conditions (Supplementary Figure 7G–I). These findings support a central role for CECs in mediating the pro-tumoral effects induced by Dectin-1 activation.

Anti-PD-L1 treatment results in a substantial depletion of PD-LI+Gal-3+ CECs

Considering the high abundance of CECs co-expressing Gal-3 and PD-L1, we decided to evaluate the therapeutic potential of an anti-PD-L1 antibody. Therefore, we treated tumor-bearing mice with the anti-PD-L1 antibody when tumors were palpable (day 8) twice per week, as illustrated in Figure 7A. We found that the anti-PD-L1 therapy resulted in a significant decrease in both tumor weight and volume (Figure 7B–D). We also observed a significant decrease in the frequency of CECs in the TME in the treated versus the control group (Figure 7E,F). As expected, we observed a substantial decrease in both the frequency of Gal-3+PD-L1+ CECs (Figure 7G,H) and their intensity of expression within the TME of treated mice (Figure 7I–L). Next, we assessed the effect of anti-PD-L1 therapy on the frequency of splenic CECs. Interestingly, we noted that anti-PD-L1 prevented splenomegaly as evidenced by the lack of changes in the spleen weight between tumor-free and anti-PD-L1-treated mice (Figure 7M,N). However, the frequency of splenic CEC was reduced in the treated compared to the control group (Figure 7O,P). Consistent with our findings in the TME, there was a significant decrease both in the frequency of splenic PD-L1+Gal-3+ CECs (Figure 7Q,R) and their intensity of expression following treatment with the anti-PD-L1 antibody (Figure 7S–V). Our observations support the therapeutic effects of anti-PD-L1 via the depletion of CECs in the TME and spleen of tumor-bearing mice.

The impact of anti-PD-L1 therapy on the BM resident CECs

We then investigated whether this reduction in splenic CECs results in enhanced compensatory erythropoiesis in the BM. Notably, we observed a significant increase in the frequency of CECs in the BM of anti-PD-L1-treated compared to the control group (Supplementary Figure 8A,B). However, the frequency of PD-L1+Gal-3+ CECs was markedly reduced in the BM of the treated group (Supplementary Figure 8C,D), along with a decrease in their intensity of expression (Supplementary Figure 8E–H). Furthermore, we observed the promotion of CECs towards maturation in the BM, evidenced by a significant reduction in the frequency of CD45+ CECs in the BM of anti-PD-L1-treated mice (Supplementary Figure 8I,J). Similarly, the intensity of CD45 in BM CECs was significantly reduced following treatment (Supplementary Figure 8K,L). In contrast, we found significantly higher TER119 expression in CECs in the treated compared to the untreated group, suggesting that anti-PD-L1 promotes the maturation of CECs in the BM (Supplementary Figure 8M,N). To evaluate the immunoregulatory properties of CECs following anti-PD-L1 treatment, we analyzed ROS, arginase-I, and TGF-β/LAP expression. Our results showed significantly lower expression of ROS, arginase-I, and TGF-β/LAP in CECs following treatment with the anti-PD-L1 therapy (Supplementary Figure 8O–T).

Collectively, our findings demonstrate that targeting PD-L1 leads to a reduction in PD-L1+Gal-3+ CECs in the TME, and the depletion of these cells could be an additional mechanism by which anti-PD-L1 exerts its therapeutic function.

The majority of human BM erythroid cells (CECs) express Gal-3

To determine whether our observations in mice are applicable to humans, we reanalyzed a publicly available scRNA-seq dataset(41) to investigate the gene expression of Gal-3+ in human BM CECs. Following the filtering of low-quality cells, a total of 90,153 bone marrow cells from 25 patients were clustered into 19 distinct populations (Figure 8A). Annotation of these clusters (Figure 8B) allowed us to identify erythroid cells, which were subsequently sub-setted and re-clustered for further analysis. This analysis delineated 11 erythroid sub-clusters (Figure 8C). Analysis of Gal-3 expression across these clusters revealed widespread expression in all clusters, with notably lower levels in cluster 8 (Figure 8D). Erythroid cells were further stratified into Gal-3- and Gal-3+ subsets (Figure 8E), revealing that more than 70% of erythroid cells express Gal-3 (Figure 8F). Gal-3+ erythroid cells exhibited elevated expression of CD235a, CD71, and CD45, particularly in clusters 3, 4, and 6 (Figure 8G), which aligns with our observations in mice. Analysis of differentially expressed genes identified distinct genes upregulated in Gal-3+ erythroid cells associated with metabolic regulation and stress response (Figure 8H). Of note, TMCC2, ARL4A, and KRT1 were enriched in Gal-3+ erythroid cells, potentially indicating differences in cellular organization and structural integrity. Additionally, BPGM and SLC2A1, genes involved in erythroid metabolism, were notably upregulated in Gal-3+ cells, potentially reflecting altered metabolic processes in these cells. Furthermore, genes related to oxidative stress response, such as BNIP3L and SELENBP1, suggesting a potential role in oxidative stress regulation. Finally, in concordance with our observation in mice, arginase-I (ARG1) expression was higher in Gal-3+ erythroid cells compared to their Gal-3- counterparts, supporting the immunoregulatory roles of human Gal-3+ CECs (Figure 8I). Although we have previously shown that human BM CECs express Gal-3, 26 limited access to human BM specimens has constrained more extensive analyses. Nonetheless, as a proof of concept, we observed that a subset of human BM CECs co-expresses Gal-3 and PD-L1 (Figure 8J). In line with these observations, we have also reported the expression of PD-L1, PD-L2, and VISTA on human CECs in patients with viral-associated solid tumors, 33 supporting a conserved immunoregulatory phenotype across species and disease contexts.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the role of curdlan, a β-glucan, in modulating CECs during tumor progression using the EO771 murine model of TNBC. Our results provide compelling evidence that curdlan treatment promotes the expansion of CECs, alters their immunophenotype, and enhances their immunosuppressive properties in both the spleen and TME. These observations were further validated in the 4T1 breast cancer model.

Notably, CEC expansion was accompanied by splenomegaly, indicative of an extramedullary hematopoietic response induced by curdlan. This observation aligns with previous studies showing that Dectin-1 ligation can enhance hematopoietic activity. 47 We found that curdlan treatment further exacerbated this expansion, especially in the spleen, where both the frequency of CECs and their immunosuppressive potential were significantly increased. Importantly, curdlan treatment led to a marked expansion of Dectin-1⁺ CECs, accompanied by co-localization of Gal-3 with Dectin-1. This finding suggests that Dectin-1-expressing CECs may represent a functionally specialized subset with enhanced immunoregulatory capacity. Although the precise mechanism underlying the expansion of this subset remains unclear, it is plausible that Dectin-1 signaling indirectly shapes the erythroid niche through modulation of the inflammatory microenvironment. 48 Further studies will be required to delineate whether this expansion is cell-intrinsic or mediated via myeloid or stromal intermediates.

Consistent with this, curdlan-treated mice exhibited enhanced frequencies of CECs expressing co-inhibitory molecules, including PD-L1, PD-L2, and Gal-3, markers associated with immunosuppressive function as previously reported. 21 , 26 , 33 While the intensity of PD-L1, PD-L2, and VISTA expression remained unchanged, we observed a striking increase in both the frequency and intensity of Gal-3 expression in splenic CECs. This effect was particularly pronounced within the Gal-3+ subset, which was enriched for CD45+ cells, an early erythroid population known to exhibit potent immunosuppressive activity through elevated production of ROS, TGF-β, arginase-I, and arginase-II. 23 , 27 , 28

The expansion of Gal-3+ CECs likely imposes significant pressure on medullary erythropoiesis, leading to a reduction in BM-resident CECs. This is supported by the greater apoptotic potential of Gal-3+ CECs. Consequently, this shift induces extramedullary erythropoiesis, albeit with a diminished capacity for terminal erythroid maturation, thereby potentially impacting overall erythropoietic homeostasis. Gal-3, a known immunomodulatory molecule, has been shown to play a role in various biological functions, such as cell growth, apoptosis, differentiation, angiogenesis, and inflammation. 49-51 Although Gal-3 is broadly expressed both intra-and extracellularly in immune and non-immune cells, 52 , 53 our findings suggest that activated CD11b+ cells shed Gal-3 following curdlan-Dectin-1 ligation, similar to the shedding reported for Gal-9 in activated neutrophils. 37 Moreover, Gal-3 appears to exhibit a higher affinity for CD45+ CECs, and our observations support the deposition onto these cells. This could be attributed to the increased expression of Gal-3 receptors (e.g. CD71 and GARP) on CD45+ CECs. 26 Based on our observations, the presence of Gal-3 on CECs following curdlan treatment points to a potential mechanism by which curdlan modulates erythropoiesis and, subsequently, immune responses. In line with this, the co-expression of Gal-3 with PD-L1 and other inhibitory receptors in curdlan-treated CECs suggests a heightened immunosuppressive profile, which may facilitate tumor progression by preventing effective anti-tumor immunity. Additionally, our study supports the notion that curdlan-induced CECs exhibit enhanced capacity to suppress T cell proliferation, further underlining their immunosuppressive role.

Mechanistically, this heightened immunoregulatory properties of Gal-3+ CECs could be explained by elevated levels of PD-L1/2 expression and soluble factors, such as ROS, TGF-β, and arginase-I. Whether the reported role of Gal-3 in sustaining TGF-β1 signaling by selectively binding to N-glycan residues on the TGF-β1 receptor on other cells, 54 , 55 is the case for CECs remains to be explored. Additionally, Gal-3 interacts with surface glycans and integrins, facilitating their association with the TGF-β/LAP complex, leading to increased TGF-β receptor activation and downstream signaling. 56-58 In line with these findings, we have previously reported that Gal-3 interacts with GARP on CECs. 26

The TME is known to influence the phenotypic and functional characteristics of various immune cells, 59 and our data demonstrate that curdlan treatment amplifies this effect by modulating CECs. We observed a greater expression of PD-L1, PD-L2, and Gal-3 in the TME as compared to the spleen, even in the absence of curdlan treatment. This suggests that the TME inherently favors the accumulation of CECs with immunosuppressive properties, 24 , 33 which is further augmented by curdlan. The observed interaction between Gal-3 and PD-L1 is one of our novel findings in this study. Notably, we found that Gal-3 not only co-expresses but also colocalizes with PD-L1 on CECs. Therefore, this partnership likely contributes to a more potent immunosuppressive phenotype, capable of further dampening anti-tumor immunity.

To delineate the mechanism(s) underlying CEC expansion following curdlan treatment, we utilized DKO mice. Notably, curdlan failed to induce CEC expansion or increase Gal-3 expression and had no effect on tumor growth in the absence of Dectin-1. These findings indicate that Dectin-1 is a critical receptor mediating the effects of curdlan. This conclusion is further supported by CEC depletion experiments, where abrogation of CECs eliminated the effects of curdlan treatment and resulted in reduced tumor progression. This finding aligns with previous research demonstrating that β-glucans like curdlan interact with Dectin-1, activating downstream signaling pathways that modulate immune cell function. 9 , 17 , 60 Therefore, our study clearly implicates Dectin-1 signaling in mediating the effects of curdlan on erythropoiesis and the expansion of CECs.

An intriguing aspect of our study is the observed shift in erythropoiesis induced by curdlan treatment. In tumor-free mice, curdlan promoted extramedullary erythropoiesis, as evidenced by the expansion of CECs in the spleen and the decrease in CECs within the BM. This shift suggests that curdlan-induced Dectin-1 signaling not only affects myeloid cell subsets(9) but also influences erythropoiesis processes, leading to changes in erythroid progenitor distribution and function. Interestingly, curdlan treatment resulted in a reduction of CECs in the BM, which could be due to the enhanced immunosuppressive properties of Gal-3+ CECs that disrupt normal erythropoietic homeostasis.

Given the prominent co-expression/co-localization of PD-L1+Gal-3+ CECs and their abundance in the TME, we explored the potential therapeutic effects of anti-PD-L1 treatment. We observed that anti-PD-L1 therapy significantly reduced the proportion of CECs co-expressing PD-L1 and Gal-3, in both the TME and spleen. The reduction in Gal-3+/PD-L1+ CECs was associated with significant tumor regression. Moreover, anti-PD-L1 treatment prevented splenomegaly and decreased the expression of markers associated with immunosuppressive properties of CECs. These findings highlight the potential of targeting PD-L1+Gal-3+ CECs as an additional therapeutic strategy to enhance anti-tumor immunity. Finally, our scRNA seq analysis revealed that the majority of human BM CECs express the Gal-3 gene (LGALS3) and, similar to their counterparts in mice, express higher levels of TFRC (CD71), PTPRC (CD45), and ARG1 (arginase-I) genes. However, further studies are required to delineate the role of Gal-3+ CECs in tumor progression in human subjects across different cancer types. Our findings are consistent with the pro-tumor effects of Dectin-1 signaling in colorectal and pancreatic cancers, 9 , 10 but contrast with the anti-tumor effects observed in the melanoma model. 9 Therefore, our study adds another layer of complexity to the previously contradicting therapeutic effects of targeting Dectin-1 across different tumor models.

A key limitation of this study is the absence of RNA-seq analysis comparing Gal-3⁺ and Gal-3⁻ CECs, which would provide important mechanistic insights into the role of Gal-3 and Dectin-1 in CECs effector function. Furthermore, the lack of assessment of Gal-3 expression in human-derived CECs from breast cancer patients limits the translational interpretation of our findings, particularly in the context of immune checkpoint blockade.

In conclusion, our study provides novel insights into the impact of curdlan-induced Dectin-1 signaling on erythropoiesis. Curdlan treatment not only promotes the expansion of CECs but also enhances their immunosuppressive potential, contributing to tumor progression. The therapeutic depletion of PD-L1+Gal-3+ CECs using anti-PD-L1 antibody demonstrates the potential for targeting these cells as part of immune-based cancer therapies. Further studies are needed to explore the full therapeutic potential of modulating CECs in cancer and other diseases associated with dysregulated immune responses.
