Introduction of Vacuum Fields to Cavity with Diffraction Loss


Abstract
The space-based gravitational wave detector DECIGO employs a 1000 km-long Fabry-Perot cavity, which introduces significant diffraction loss. Previous studies accounted or diffraction loss by modeling it as a reduction in laser power for sensitivity calculations. In this research, we take an optical approach by explicitly considering the impact of vacuum fields introduced through diffraction loss, including leakage loss and higher-order mode loss, as well as mirror optical loss. By formulating input-output relations that incorporate these vacuum fields in a realistic cavity with diffraction loss and an optical spring, we achieve a more precise evaluation of DECIGO’s optical response. This analysis provides new insights into how diffraction loss affects quantum-locking techniques, offering a refined framework for improving DECIGO’s optical design and sensitivity estimation.

The method of mixing vacuum fields

Generally, when there is a loss in laser interferometers, a vacuum field is correspondingly mixed in and increases the quantum noise.
In this section,
vacuum fields mixed in response to three types of losses are discussed.
The first is optical loss occurred on a mirror. The second is leakage loss caused by the diameter of the mirror being smaller than that of the laser beam spot. For sake of simplicity, laser beam that spills out from the mirror is blocked.
The third is higher-order mode loss that occurs
when the input laser beam’s spatial mode and the cavity geometry do not match.
The leakage loss and higher-order mode loss occur by diffraction when the cavity length is large and the diameter of the laser beam is significantly expanded.
When leakage loss occurs, the edge of TEM
00
is cut off
and higher-order components of the laser spatial mode are introduced. Assuming a high finesse cavity, we will treat only the fundamental mode (TEM
00
) of Gaussian beam inside cavity in this paper.
The effect of diffraction loss is given by following coefficient
D

[
6
]

D\equiv\sqrt{1-\exp\left(-\frac{2\pi z_{\rm{R}}}{\lambda(l^{2}+z_{\rm{R}}^{2})}R^{2}\right)},

(1)

where,
z_{\rm{R}}
is Rayleigh length,
\lambda
is a wavelength of the laser beam,
l
is the distance away from beam waist, and

R
is the mirror radius.

Discussion

Figure 4.1
:
Comparison to our previous research. While the previous research calculated the sensitivity with laser power, this research calculated the sensitivity from quantum fluctuations.

The simulation result is shown in Fig.
4.1
.
The quantum noise level using our new formulae is compared with our previous study. Note that this simulation assumes
d\phi=0
.
Whereas the previous research calculated SN and radiation pressure noise (RP) only from the laser source, this study calculates
them from vacuum fields mixed through the diffraction loss. The RP is different because only the fluctuation of the laser light was calculated as the pushing force on the mirrors in the previous research. In this case,
the other vacuum fields are also calculated as the pushing force on the mirrors.
Therefore, the RP noise was underestimated in previous studies and is now treated appropriately.

Figure 4.2
:
Sensitivity curves in the case of the diffraction loss and optimizing the detuning angle. The top-left figure represents the case where there is no diffraction loss, while the figures moving toward the bottom-right show sensitivity as diffraction loss increases.

Vacuum fields derived from the leakage loss and higher-order mode loss are all plotted together in Fig.
4.2
with different levels of the diffraction loss.
In all cases, the frequencies of the dips are not exactly the same: the frequency dependence of the dip is different
because the RP and SN enter at different locations in the cavity, which correspond to different points on the block diagram.
For example, the factor that creates the dip in the phase fluctuation of the laser light is the reflected light at the input mirror,
which is not related to the other fluctuations.
Also, as diffraction loss increases, the level of loss-derived vacuum field increases, limiting sensitivity.
By adding more cavity parameters to search, it may be possible to align the dips of RP and SN and optimize sensitivity even in the presence of the diffraction loss.

5
Summary

Due to recent CMB research, which has lowered the upper limit of the normalized gravitational wave energy density for PGW, improving DECIGO’s target sensitivity has become essential. To achieve this, previous researches considered incorporating an optical spring into DECIGO’s sub cavity, combined with a homodyne detection readout — a technique known as quantum locking. When considering the integration of an optical spring in the main cavity of the DECIGO for further sensitivity improvement, the effect of optical spring is reduced because diffraction loss occurs in the main cavity with the long cavity length of 1000 km.
To quantitatively evaluate the effect of the diffraction loss, we investigated how the vacuum field was mixed in a general cavity with the diffraction loss.
Considering vacuum fields mixed in through diffraction loss (leakage loss and higher-order mode loss) as well as optical loss
