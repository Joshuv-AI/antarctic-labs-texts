Watermarking Discrete Diffusion Language Models
Avi Bagchi, Akhil Bhimaraju, Moulik Choraria, Daniel Alabi, Lav R. Varshney

Abstract
Watermarking has emerged as a promising technique to track AI-generated content and differentiate it from authentic human creations. While prior work extensively studies watermarking for autoregressive large language models (LLMs) and image diffusion models, none address discrete diffusion language models, which are becoming popular due to their high inference throughput. In this paper, we introduce the first watermarking method for discrete diffusion models by applying the distribution-preserving Gumbel-max trick at every diffusion step and seeding the randomness with the sequence index to enable reliable detection. We experimentally demonstrate that our scheme is reliably detectable on state-of-the-art diffusion language models and analytically prove that it is distortion-free with an exponentially decaying probability of false detection in the token sequence length.

Introduction

The rapid development and widespread deployment of artificial intelligence (AI) agents necessitate guarantees of authenticity and traceability
(
Fairoze et al. 2025
;
Biden, Jr. 2023
;
Biden, Jr. and Harris 2023
)
. A particularly serious security concern is distinguishing AI-generated text from human-written content
(
Dathathri et al. 2024
)
, as nefarious actors exploit generative AI to create deceptive content
(
Tarsney 2025
;
Tang, Chuang, and Hu 2023
)
. Such misinformation pollutes the information environment and compromises the integrity of data used to train future AI models.
Since the reliability of post-hoc automated detectors degrades as model quality improves
(
Fairoze et al. 2025
;
Jawahar, Abdul-Mageed, and Lakshmanan 2020
;
Varshney, Keskar, and Socher 2020
)
, watermarking generative models has surfaced as a novel solution
(
Aaronson and Kirchner 2022
;
Kirchenbauer et al. 2024
)
.

A watermark subtly shifts the output distribution of a generative model so that it remains invisible to humans yet can be detected algorithmically
(
Fairoze et al. 2025
;
Kirchenbauer et al. 2024
;
Alabi and Varshney 2025
)
. For instance,

Kirchenbauer et al. 2024
propose partitioning the vocabulary into a red and green list with the model favoring the latter in sampling. Strongly biasing the sampling procedure, however, creates a precarious tradeoff between detectability and distortion
(
Christ, Gunn, and Zamir 2023
;
Giboulot and Furon 2024
)
. A watermark signal that is too strong, for example, can raise the perplexity, indicating that the watermark unduly increases the entropy of the distribution of tokens relative to a baseline language model. Ideally, a watermark will satisfy the following properties.

•

Soundness
: The detection scheme reliably identifies unwatermarked content as unwatermarked
(
Fairoze et al. 2025
;
Christ, Gunn, and Zamir 2023
;
Fan et al. 2025
)
.

•

Completeness
: The detection scheme reliably identifies watermarked content as watermarked
(
Fairoze et al. 2025
;
Christ, Gunn, and Zamir 2023
)
.

•

Distortion-Freeness
: The watermark does not significantly reduce the quality of the text
(
Fairoze et al. 2025
;
Christ, Gunn, and Zamir 2023
;
Giboulot and Furon 2024
)
. Completeness and distortion-freeness are often inversely related.

•

Robustness
: The watermark is still detectable following bounded modifications to the text
(
Fairoze et al. 2025
;
Giboulot and Furon 2024
;
Li et al. 2025b
;
Kuditipudi et al. 2024
;
Fan et al. 2025
)
.

A watermark may satisfy the aforementioned properties for one architecture yet fail miserably for another, as successful schemes often exploit idiosyncrasies in the sampling procedure
(
Fernandez 2025
)
. Thus,
as architectures evolve, so should watermarks
. Researchers have proposed watermarks for autoregressive large language models (LLMs)
(
Aaronson and Kirchner 2022
;
Kirchenbauer et al. 2024
)
and image diffusion models
(
Wen et al. 2023
)
, but none for discrete diffusion—a notable gap given their rapid growth in both research and commercial use
(
Google DeepMind 2025
;
Inception Labs et al. 2025
)
. Our contributions are as follows.

1.

We introduce the first watermarking method for discrete diffusion models by applying the distribution-preserving Gumbel-max trick at every diffusion step and seeding the randomness with the sequence index to enable reliable detection.

2.

We analytically prove that the false detection probability decays exponentially with the number of generated tokens and that our watermark leaves the token sampling distribution unchanged.

3.

We implement our watermark on the state-of-the-art Language Diffusion Model LLaDA
(
Nie et al. 2025a
)
and achieve high completeness and soundness.
While green-list methods significantly decrease LLaDA performance on math and logic benchmarks, our Gumbel-max
technique entirely preserves both benchmark scores and perplexity.

Related Work

Discrete Diffusion Models:

Diffusion models gradually corrupt data into a simple distribution and then learn a reverse process to reconstruct the original data
(
Sohl-Dickstein et al. 2015
;
Ho, Jain, and Abbeel 2020
)
. Although earlier work focused on learning continuous data, diffusion model methods have been adapted for categorical domains. Unlike their autoregressive counterparts that capture left-to-right dependencies, discrete diffusion models generate tokens in parallel, enabling faster inference
(
Wang et al. 2025
)
, greater controllability
(
Schiff et al. 2025
)
, and enhanced comprehension of global patterns
(
Hu et al. 2021
)
. While the first discrete diffusion models mapped discrete tokens into continuous space before applying traditional continuous diffusion
(
Li et al. 2022
;
Gong et al. 2023
;
Han, Kumar, and Tsvetkov 2023
)
, more recent models operate directly on the discrete state space
(
Hoogeboom et al. 2021
;
Austin et al. 2021
;
Meng et al. 2023
;
Lou, Meng, and Ermon 2024
;
Nie et al. 2025a
)
. Inception Labs’ models empirically validate discrete diffusion at scale across text and multimodal domains
(
Inception Labs et al. 2025
;
Li et al. 2025a
)
.

Watermarking Generative Models:
In continuous settings (e.g. images, audio, etc.), watermarking is well-studied as one can exploit known signal processing properties to achieve a robust fingerprint
(
Wen et al. 2023
;
Katzenbeisser and Petitcolas 2000
)
. For discrete diffusion, however, modifications similar to
Wen et al. 2023
not only yield completely incomprehensible outputs but are also undetectable.
Kirchenbauer et al. 2024
introduce a watermark for models generating discrete data (i.e., autoregressive LLMs) which biases the sampling procedure to favor a subset of the vocabulary (i.e., the “green list”) with the hope that there is a statistically greater number of tokens from the green list in the output. While
Kirchenbauer et al. 2024
propose an arbitrary threshold for watermark detection,
Li et al. 2025c
introduce a hypothesis-testing framework to systematically evaluate and compare different watermark detection methods.
He et al. 2025
extends this perspective by jointly optimizing both watermark embedding and detection, detailing the trade-off between detectability and distortion—a balance we also empirically reveal in this paper.

Watermarking Scheme

To motivate our scheme, consider the soft watermark for autoregressive LLMs proposed by
Kirchenbauer et al. 2024
which applies a bias
\delta
, favoring a fraction of the vocabulary
\gamma
(called the “green list”) during sampling. Note that the scheme does not directly translate from autoregressive LLMs to discrete diffusion models.

•

Unlike autoregressive LLMs, in discrete diffusion, there is sampling across a set of steps
S
. We must choose an optimal
S_{W}\subseteq S
to watermark, complicating our completeness / distortion tradeoff. Specifically, we observe empirically that similar to
\delta

|S_{W}|
increases, completeness and distortion are both non-decreasing. We present two variations of this green-list scheme adapted for discrete diffusion in Appendix
A
and Appendix
B

•

Kirchenbauer et al. 2024
use the previous token(s) as the seed for generating the red and green lists—this is not possible in diffusion models where the tokens are all generated concurrently.

The first bullet motivates crafting a watermark that is unbiased at every step in the sampling process. In this case,
S_{W}=S
is optimal—if accumulating bias that distorts the text is no longer a concern, we should watermark every step to maximize completeness. To do so, we take inspiration from
Aaronson and Kirchner 2022
that sample the token with the maximum value of
r^{\frac{1}{p}}
such that
r\sim\text{Unif}[0,1]
and
p
is the probability of that token. In Alg.
1
, we repeat this custom sampling at every sampling step.

Addressing the second bullet, we seed the random number generator that generates
r_{i}
by the position in the sequence so that during detection (Alg.
2
), we can regenerate
r_{i}
at each position
i
. We directly follow
Aaronson and Kirchner 2022
in computing a score
\frac{1}{L}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\ln(\frac{1}{1-r_{i}})
where
L
is the length of the generated sequence. For unwatermarked text, the normalized score is centered around 1 whereas for watermarked text, it is expected to be centered around a value exceeding some threshold that is greater than 1. To thwart prefix deletions that cause all of the seeding to be off by some offset, we follow
Kuditipudi et al. 2024
and instead seed by
(s+i)\bmod m
for some parameter
m\ll L
and offset
s
. In detection (Alg.
2
), we iterate through all possible offsets and choose the alignment with the maximum normalized score.

Conclusion and Future Work

This paper introduced the first watermark for discrete diffusion language models. We demonstrate its completeness empirically, and its soundness and distortion-freeness both theoretically and empirically. Future work aims to implement our framework for additional models beyond LLaDA. As discrete diffusion models improve at coding, one can evaluate our scheme against such benchmarks. In addition, although we propose a preliminary scheme to achieve prefix-deletion robustness, it is inherently limited and at the expense of our theoretical guarantees. Following the edit distance algorithms of
Kuditipudi et al. 2024
may yield improvements.

Appendix B
Red-Green List Scheme: Scaling the Score

In this section, we take a slightly different perspective on the red-green list scheme for discrete diffusion models. Rather than biasing the logits upon sampling, we can instead gradually tilt the transition kernel towards a subset of the vocabulary. To demonstrate this approach, we use the score entropy discrete diffusion (SEDD) model introduced by
Lou, Meng, and Ermon 2024
. To motivate SEDD, consider a language model with vocabulary
\mathcal{V}
that generates sequences of maximum length
d
where
\mathcal{X}
denotes all possible sequences.
|\mathcal{X}|
is exponential in
d
. While auto-regressive models address this problem by conditioning on prior tokens via the chain rule, SEDD learns the concrete score, representing the relative likelihood of transitioning from some state
x
to other states at time
t
under the PMF
p_{t}:\mathcal{X}\to\displaystyle[0,1]
.

Let
s_{\theta}:\mathcal{X}\times\displaystyle\mathbb{R}\to\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{X}|}
be a network that approximates the concrete score:

s_{\theta}(x,t)\approx\left[\frac{p_{t}(y)}{p_{t}(x)}\right]_{y\neq x}\mbox{.}

By minimizing the score entropy loss, the model learns token-level changes where a transition
x\to y
is such that
x=(x^{1},\ldots,x^{i},\ldots,x^{d})
and
y=(x^{1},\ldots,\hat{x}^{i},\ldots,x^{d})
. They are expressed as a continuous-time Markov chain where
x_{t}\in\mathcal{X}
is the full sequence of tokens at time
t
. We seek the transition probabilities
\mathbb{P}(x_{t+\Delta t}=y|x_{t}=x)
for a small
\Delta t
such that
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
and
Q(y,x)
defines the rate at which the system transitions from state
x
to state
y
.

\mathbb{P}(x_{t+\Delta t}=y|x_{t}=x)=\begin{cases}Q(y,x)\Delta t\quad\text{for $y\neq x$}\\
1-\sum_{y\neq x}Q(y,x)\Delta t\quad\text{for $y=x$}\end{cases}

Reformulating the PMF
p_{t}
as a column vector
p_{t}\in\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{X}|}
where
[p_{t}]_{i}
is the probability of being in state
i
at time
t
, we describe the stochastic process as a first-order ordinary differential equation
\frac{dp_{t}}{dt}=Qp_{t}
having solutions:

•

Forward:
p_{t+\Delta t}=e^{Q\Delta t}p_{t}

,

•

Backwards:
p_{t-\Delta t}=e^{-Q\Delta t}p_{t}

.

To perform denoising in the reverse process, we apply Bayes’ rule.

p_{t-\Delta t|t}(x_{t-\Delta t}|x_{t})=\frac{[e^{Q\Delta t}]_{x_{t},x_{t-\Delta t}}[e^{-Q\Delta t}p_{t}(\cdot)]_{x_{t-\Delta t}}}{p_{t}(x_{t})}\mbox{.}

We can simplify by noting that the score function
s_{\theta}
approximates
\frac{p_{t}(\cdot)}{p_{t}(x_{t})}
. Since the model is trained only to estimate probability ratios between sequences that differ by a single token (i.e. Hamming distance
1
), we structure the score function output as
s_{\theta}(x_{t},t)\in\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{d\times|\mathcal{V}|}
, where each entry
(i,j)
represents the relative likelihood of replacing the token at at position
i
in
x_{t}
with token
j\in\mathcal{V}
. These transitions depend on the noise schedule, where
\overline{\sigma}
denotes the cumulative noise.

\begin{split}p_{t-\Delta t\mid t}(x_{t-\Delta t}^{i}\mid x_{t}^{i})&=\big(\exp(-\sigma_{t}^{\Delta t}Q)s_{\theta}(x_{t},t)_{i}\big)_{x_{t-\Delta t}^{i}}\\
&\quad\times\exp(\sigma_{t}^{\Delta t}Q)(x_{t}^{i},x_{t-\Delta t}^{i})\end{split}

(4)

where

\sigma_{t}^{\Delta t}=(\overline{\sigma}(t)-\overline{\sigma}(t-\Delta t))\mbox{.}

Here,
Q
is defined so that the forward diffusion process converges to a distribution favorable for sampling.
Lou, Meng, and Ermon 2024
introduces two varieties: absorbing (uses an extra “MASK” token
M
) and uniform.

To watermark SEDD, we scale entries in the score
s_{\theta}
in (
4
) corresponding to green-list tokens by a factor
\delta>1
to increase their likelihood during generation (Fig.
4
).

s^{\prime}_{\theta}(x_{t},t)_{i}=\begin{cases}s_{\theta}(x_{t},t)_{ij},&\text{if }j\notin G,\\[4.0pt]
\delta\,s_{\theta}(x_{t},t)_{ij},&\text{if }j\in G.\end{cases}

(5)

Appendix D
Discussion: Absorbing vs. Uniform

Recall that
Lou, Meng, and Ermon 2024
propose two strains of initial noise vectors: absorbing and uniform. The choice of initial noise affects the watermark detectability (Figure
4
provides a visual intuition).

Using an initial noise vector sampled from the uniform distribution necessitates a larger
\delta
than that of its absorbing state initial noise vector counterpart. In the former case, the system can transition out of a watermarked token state while in the latter case, it cannot. In addition, for the absorbing case, if
\delta
is applied at a set of steps very late in the diffusion process, most tokens have already collapsed into their final tokens, so the watermark will not be detectable. Such a property does not exist in uniform state reverse diffusion. Other peculiarities in this vein arise with the various re-masking strategies of LLaDA. Unfortunately, as we describe in the model details section, we were not able to train a quality SEDD model for the uniform state and thus could not evaluate these hypotheses.
