Default Hospice Consultation in Comfort Measures Orders and Hospice Engagement
Authors: L. Scott Sussman, John M. Henderson, Nancy Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This quality improvement study examines the association of having a hospice consultation as default for patients given comfort measures orders with hospice engagement.

Introduction

Hospice improves end-of-life care quality yet remains underutilized, with persistent racial and ethnic access disparities.1,2,3,4 In hospitals, hospice enrollment requires clinician-initiated consultation, creating workflow barriers and referral practice variability. A key driver to decrease in-hospital deaths includes the ability to connect patients with hospice care. We evaluated whether embedding default-based hospice consultation within comfort measures only (CMO) orders, as a form of choice architecture, was associated with hospice engagement.

Methods

This quality improvement study was determined not to constitute human participants research per Yale University institutional policy; therefore, institutional review board approval and informed consent were not required. This study follows the SQUIRE reporting guideline. We conducted a 4-year, longitudinal cross-sectional evaluation of inpatients with CMO orders across a 7-campus academic health system (2680 beds; >175 000 annual discharges) from fiscal years (FYs) 2022 through 2025. In FY 2023, an electronic health record (EHR) redesign linked hospice consultation with the CMO order using a preselected, opt-out default with brief educational text. Historically, hospice consults and CMO required separate orders. Outcomes included hospice consultation, hospice disposition (defined as discharge to hospice), and inpatient deaths among nonhospice patients with CMO orders. Race and ethnicity (categorized as Asian, Hispanic, non-Hispanic Black, non-Hispanic White, and other [American Indian or Native American, Middle Eastern or North African, Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander, unknown, not listed, prefer not to share, or no response]) were self-reported. Annual proportions were summarized descriptively. Interrupted time series (ITS) analyses of quarterly hospice consultation and disposition proportions were performed using segmented linear regression with terms for time, postintervention period, and time after intervention. Newey-West standard errors were used to account for autocorrelation. Subgroup-specific 95% CIs were calculated using Wilson score binomial intervals for FY 2025 consultation and disposition proportions. All statistical tests were 2-sided, with statistical significance defined as P < .05. Analyses used R version 4.2.1 (R Project for Statistical Computing).

Results

During the study, 14 614 patients (11 801 aged ≥65 years [81%]; 7219 female [49%]; 1552 non-Hispanic Black [11%]; 1136 Hispanic [8%]; 11 269 non-Hispanic White [77%]) had CMO orders; most had Medicare (Table). Hospice consults increased from 44% to 84% after implementation (Figure, A), and disposition increased from 37% to 56% (Figure, B). In ITS analyses, implementation was associated with an immediate increase in consultation of 21 (95% CI, 10-33) percentage points (P = .001) and an increase by nearly 3 percentage points per quarter postimplementation. Disposition increased by 13 (95% CI, 7-19) percentage points (P < .001), with an increase of 2 percentage points per quarter postimplementation. Concurrently, inpatient deaths among nonhospice patients with CMO orders decreased by 16 (95% CI, 14-18) percentage points.

At baseline, both consult and disposition rates were lower among Black and Hispanic patients compared with White patients, consistent with national patterns.3,4 Following implementation, consult rates increased across all groups and the absolute consult-rate gap narrowed. By FY 2025, consultation had converged across groups (Asian: 80.0% [95% CI, 69.2%-87.7%]; Black: 82.6% [95% CI, 78.5%-86.1%]; Hispanic: 81.5% [95% CI, 76.4%-85.7%]; White: 84.4% [95% CI, 83.0%-85.7%]; other: 82.6% [95% CI, 74.9%-88.4%]). However, disposition remained somewhat lower in racial and ethnic minority groups (Asian: 50.0% [95% CI, 38.6%-61.4%]; Black: 52.9% [95% CI, 47.9%-57.9%]; Hispanic: 50.9% [95% CI, 45.0% to 56.9%]; White: 56.9% [95% CI, 55.1%-58.8%]; other: 53.7% [95% CI, 44.9%-62.4%]).

Discussion

In this quality improvement study, embedding hospice consultation within CMO orders was associated with substantial and sustained increases in hospice engagement. Because hospice enrollment cannot occur without consultation, improving consult reliability represents a meaningful intervention. Prior studies demonstrate that EHR nudges can influence clinicians.5 This project embedded hospice consideration directly into an existing clinical transition rather than relying on interruptive alerts or educational efforts alone. The magnitude and durability of change suggest that workflow-aligned defaults may reduce unwarranted variation in referrals. These findings contribute to evidence that EHR-based behavioral nudges can guide clinicians in time-sensitive, high-stakes settings. The ITS findings strengthen the temporal inference by showing an immediate post-implementation increase in both consultation and disposition.

Connecting patients with hospice care may explain the reduction in hospital deaths among non-hospice CMO patients, which likely represents a shift in death from hospital to hospice rather than a true mortality decrease; this aligns with evidence that hospice enrollment enables patients to die outside the hospital and improves end-of-life care quality.6 The narrowing of consult-rate gaps suggests that standardizing referral processes may reduce variability in consults. However, the persistently lower point estimates for disposition in racial and ethnic minority groups, despite overlapping CIs and wider intervals, suggest that downstream differences may reflect barriers beyond consultation, which may require additional structural and culturally responsive strategies.

This evaluation has limitations. Secular changes in hospice capacity, patient preferences, or broader end-of-life care patterns may have contributed to these trends, and the observational design may be limited in establishing causality. Results from a single health system may not generalize to others. However, the large sample size and immediate temporal association support the plausibility of an intervention effect. These findings suggest that embedding evidence-based defaults within clinical workflows may be a scalable informatics strategy to standardize hospice consideration and improve equitable access to end-of-life care.
