STDiff: A State Transition Diffusion Framework for Time Series Imputation in Industrial Systems
Gary Simethy, Daniel Ortiz-Arroyo, Petar Durdevic

Abstract
Most deep learning methods for imputing missing values treat the task as completing patterns within a fixed time window. This assumption often fails in industrial systems, where dynamics are driven by control actions, are highly non-stationary, and can experience long, uninterrupted gaps. We propose STDiff , which reframes imputation as learning how the system evolves from one state to the next. STDiff uses a conditional denoising diffusion model with a causal bias aligned to control theory, generating missing values step-by-step based on the most recent known state and relevant control or environmental inputs. On a public wastewater treatment dataset with simulated missing blocks, STDiff consistently achieves the lowest errors, with its advantage increasing for longer gaps. On a raw industrial dataset with substantial real gaps, it produces trajectories that remain dynamically plausible, in contrast to window-based models that tend to flatten or over-smooth. These results support dynamics-aware, explicitly conditioned imputation as a robust approach for industrial time series, and we discuss computational trade-offs and extensions to broader domains.

1
Introduction

Multivariate time series are central to decision-making in finance, healthcare, and industrial process control. Missing values - caused by sensor faults, network outages, or maintenance - jeopardize forecasting, anomaly detection, and closed-loop control
(
lin2020
)
. Much recent progress is driven by models that excel on public benchmarks (Electricity, Traffic, ETT, Exchange, M4)
(
Dua:2019
;
zhou2021
;
wu2021
;
makridakis2018m4
)
. Yet simple linear baselines can match or outperform sophisticated Transformers on some datasets
(
zeng2023are
)
, calling into question their robustness.

Wastewater Treatment Plants (WWTPs) are cyber-physical systems governed by nonlinear biochemical processes, influenced by non-stationary disturbances, and manipulated by discrete control actions
(
alex2008
;
copp2002
;
he2012
;
kadlec2009
)
. Measurements are often indirect and noisy
(
kadlec2009
)
. Missingness frequently occurs in long, contiguous chunks - conditions under which window-based imputers become brittle
(
wen2022
;
zhang2023domain
)
.

A unifying limitation of many state-of-the-art methods is their
window-based
design: the model infers missing values from a fixed-size slice of past/future data. When the gap exceeds the window, usable context becomes sparse or absent. We argue that, for dynamical systems, imputation should model how the system evolves, i.e., a probabilistic “world model” of state transitions
(
sutton2018
;
oksendal2003
)
.

We introduce
STDiff
, which learns a discrete-time conditional transition model using a denoising diffusion approach. This design embeds a causal bias, integrates control and external inputs, and enables robust recursive imputation across extended gaps. We evaluate STDiff against RNN-, attention-, Transformer-, diffusion-, LLM-, and dynamics-based baselines on WWTP datasets, showing consistent gains and analyzing failure modes of window-based approaches.

Our main contributions are:

•

Formulation of imputation as probabilistic state transition modeling
- reframing the task to explicitly capture how system states evolve over time, rather than relying solely on fixed-window context.

•

STDiff framework
- a conditional diffusion model aligned with discrete-time, control-driven industrial systems, incorporating both control inputs and exogenous variables.

•

Comprehensive validation
- extensive experiments on wastewater treatment plant (WWTP) datasets, showing robustness to long contiguous gaps and improved accuracy over strong baselines, including Latent ODE.

We next review each category in turn and discuss how they motivate the design of STDiff.

2
Related Work

Research in multivariate time series imputation spans a spectrum from simple statistical heuristics to deep generative and dynamics-aware models. We group the literature into three major categories:

•

Window-based pattern completion
- methods that rely on fixed-size local context to fill gaps, including classical statistics, matrix factorization, recurrent networks, attention-based transformers, and generative models.

•

Dynamics-based system modeling
- approaches that model temporal evolution explicitly, often in continuous time, using neural ODEs, SDEs, or controlled differential equations to capture system dynamics.

•

Hybrid approaches
- models that combine short-term feature extraction with long-term dynamics modeling, sometimes incorporating domain-specific physics or constraints.

2.1
Window-based pattern completion

Classical statistical techniques such as mean/median substitution, last observation carried forward, and linear interpolation remain widely used due to their speed and simplicity
(
lin2020
)
. However, these methods fail to model nonlinear dependencies or variable-specific temporal patterns, often introducing bias when the missing segments are long or structured.
Matrix factorization and low-rank tensor completion methods improve upon simple statistics by exploiting correlations across time and features
(
bahadori2014
;
yu2016temporal
)
, but they are still fundamentally limited to linear relationships.

With the rise of deep learning, recurrent models have become prominent. BRITS
(
cao2018
)
integrates imputation directly into recurrent updates, using temporal decay to weight older observations
(
pascanu2013
)
. Attention-based models such as SAITS
(
du2023saits
)
extend this by capturing temporal and feature-wise dependencies jointly, improving flexibility over purely sequential RNNs.
Transformer variants - including Informer
(
zhou2021
)
, Autoformer
(
wu2021
)
, and iTransformer
(
liu2024
)
- push scalability and periodicity modeling further, with iTransformer treating variates as tokens for improved feature interactions.

Generative approaches represent another branch of window-based methods. GAN-based imputation (GAIN)
(
yoon2018
)
frames the task as a minimax game between a generator and a discriminator, producing diverse but sometimes unstable results. More recently, diffusion models such as CSDI
(
tashiro2021
)
have shown strong performance in structured missingness scenarios, benefiting from denoising objectives
(
ho2020
;
song2019
;
dhariwal2021
)
.
Despite their strengths, all of these window-based methods rely heavily on the availability of recent local context. When long, contiguous gaps occur, performance degrades sharply because no mechanism enforces physically plausible long-horizon evolution.

2.2
Dynamics-based system modeling

An alternative to fixed-window approaches is to model the temporal evolution of the system explicitly. Neural ordinary differential equations (Neural ODEs)
(
chen2018
;
rubanova2019
)
parameterize continuous-time dynamics, naturally handling irregular sampling. Probabilistic extensions, such as Neural stochastic differential equations (Neural SDEs)
(
li2020
;
tzen2019
)
and controlled differential equations (CDEs)
(
kidger2020
)
, further account for uncertainty and exogenous influences.
These methods are conceptually elegant and can capture fine-grained temporal continuity, but their application to high-frequency SCADA-like datasets can be computationally demanding. Injecting discrete control actions into continuous solvers remains challenging, often requiring expensive event handling
(
ainsworth2021ctpg
;
li2025neural
)
. Furthermore, over-smoothing is a common issue, particularly when models prioritize stability over reproducing high-frequency transients.

2.3
Hybrid approaches

Several recent works have sought to combine the strengths of window-based feature extraction with the robustness of dynamics-aware modeling. For instance, short-term convolutional encoders fused with latent ODE dynamics to capture both local variability and long-term structure
(
de2023
)
. Others use physics-informed neural networks (PINNs)
(
raissi2019pinn
)
to embed domain-specific constraints into data-driven models, improving extrapolation beyond the observed horizon. These hybrids, however, often require domain-specific priors or carefully engineered architectures, limiting their general applicability.

2.4
Motivation for STDiff

There remains a clear gap between strong local pattern recognizers and models that encode causal, control-driven evolution over long horizons. STDiff addresses this by learning a discrete-time conditional transition kernel with a denoising diffusion model. This design combines the robustness of dynamics modeling with explicit conditioning on control inputs and exogenous variables, enabling plausible imputation even in extended gaps where purely local-context models fail.

3
The State Transition Diffusion (STDiff) Framework

This section introduces STDiff, a framework for robust imputation in control-driven industrial systems. We frame imputation as probabilistic state transition modeling, instantiate the transition kernel via conditional diffusion, present the model architecture and algorithms, and conclude with a discussion of advantages.

3.1
Problem formulation: imputation as state transition modeling

Let
x_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{D_{x}}
denote the system state at time
t
, influenced by control inputs
u_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{D_{u}}
and exogenous variables
w_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{D_{w}}
.
Under a first-order Markov assumption
(
sutton2018
)
, the conditional transition distribution is given by

p_{\theta}(x_{t}\mid x_{t-1},u_{t},w_{t}).

Imputation across a gap of length
H
becomes recursive conditional generation:

\displaystyle\hat{x}_{k+h}

\displaystyle\sim p_{\theta}\bigl(\,\cdot\,\big|\,\hat{x}_{k+h-1},u_{k+h},w_{k+h}\bigr),

\displaystyle h=1,\dots,H,\quad\hat{x}_{k}=x_{k},

which depends only on the most recent state and known inputs, avoiding the context starvation of fixed windows.

\hat{\epsilon}=\epsilon_{\theta}(\,x_{t,\tau},\tau,c\,)

.
During reverse sampling we also inject fresh noise
z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
when

\sigma_{\tau}=\sqrt{\beta_{\tau}}

Unless stated otherwise, scalar–vector operations act elementwise across state dimensions; we also use
a_{\tau}=(1-\alpha_{\tau})/\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{\tau}}
for brevity in the algorithms.

3.2
Realizing the kernel with conditional diffusion

We use a denoising diffusion probabilistic model (DDPM)
(
ho2020
)
. The forward process is:

q(x_{t,\tau}\mid x_{t,\tau-1})=\mathcal{N}\!\left(\sqrt{1-\beta_{\tau}}\,x_{t,\tau-1},\;\beta_{\tau}\,I\right),

q(x_{t,\tau}\mid x_{t,0})=\mathcal{N}\!\left(\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{\tau}}\,x_{t,0},\;(1-\bar{\alpha}_{\tau})\,I\right),

where

\alpha_{\tau}=1-\beta_{\tau},\quad\bar{\alpha}_{\tau}=\prod_{s=1}^{\tau}\alpha_{s}.

The reverse model predicts the added noise via:

L(\theta)=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\big\|\epsilon-\epsilon_{\theta}(x_{t,\tau},\tau,c)\big\|^{2}\right],\quad c=\{x_{t-1},u_{t},w_{t}\}.

3.3
Model architecture

The STDiff framework employs a conditional denoising diffusion model that explicitly integrates the most recent valid system state along with known control and exogenous inputs.
A lightweight multi-layer perceptron (MLP) encodes the tuple
(x_{t-1},u_{t},w_{t})
into a fixed-dimensional embedding vector
c_{\text{emb}}
, which serves as the conditioning signal throughout the reverse diffusion process.

The noise predictor
\epsilon_{\theta}
is parameterized as a 1D U-Net
(
ronneberger2015
)
, chosen for its ability to capture both local temporal correlations and multi-scale dependencies in industrial time-series data. The U-Net operates on the noisy state
x_{t,\tau}
and receives two conditioning inputs:

1.

The encoded control/exogenous context
c_{\text{emb}}
(added to intermediate feature maps via channel-wise concatenation), and

2.

A sinusoidal timestep embedding of
\tau
, allowing the network to learn time-dependent denoising behaviours across the diffusion trajectory.

This design enables the model to adapt the reverse process to the specific operational regime and control context at each step. Figure
1
illustrates the overall training architecture, highlighting how the conditioning signal is injected into the network layers.

Algorithms

The STDiff framework consists of two core procedures:
(1) a
training phase
(
Algorithm
1
), adapted from the DDPM objective
(
ho2020
)
with explicit state-transition conditioning; and
(2) a
recursive imputation phase
(
Algorithm
2
), which performs conditional reverse sampling for each missing time step in sequence.

3.5
Imputation (recursive sampling)

Building on the conditional reverse diffusion kernel, the imputation process starts from the last available valid state
x_{k}
and generates
\hat{x}_{k+1},\dots,\hat{x}_{k+H}
one step at a time, executing a complete reverse diffusion process for each missing time step.

At step
h
, the model conditions on the previously imputed state
\hat{x}_{k+h-1}
along with the known future control inputs
u_{k+h}
and exogenous variables
w_{k+h}
. This conditioning ensures that each imputed point is dynamically consistent with both the evolving system trajectory and the planned or observed external influences.

The reverse process is initialized from Gaussian noise and proceeds through
T
denoising steps, progressively refining the state estimate until a clean sample is obtained. This sample becomes
\hat{x}_{k+h}
and is fed into the next step, allowing information to propagate sequentially across the imputation horizon.

By recomputing the context embedding at each step, the model maintains sensitivity to both the evolving state and the sequence of inputs. Figure
2
visualizes this recursive sampling procedure, and the precise algorithmic steps are given in
Algorithm
2
.

3.6
Theoretical advantages

This formulation offers several benefits for industrial time-series imputation:

1.

Robustness to long gaps:
Dependence on the last valid state and known inputs mitigates context starvation.

2.

Causal inductive bias:
Modeling the transition
p(x_{t}\mid x_{t-1},u_{t},w_{t})
aligns with the causal direction of control-driven systems - effects follow from the previous state and inputs - so the model responds correctly to interventions in
u_{t}
and avoids spurious window-based correlations
(
battaglia2018
;
peters2017
)
.

3.

Explicit control/exogenous handling:
Directly models how external drivers influence state evolution.

4.

Probabilistic regularization:
Sampling from learned conditionals reduces runaway error accumulation across recursive steps.

We have introduced STDiff and motivated its advantages for dynamics-aware imputation. Next, we empirically test these claims on wastewater datasets, detailing datasets, baselines and metrics.

Figure
4

2

•

STDiff
preserves both spike timing and amplitude without overshoot or excessive smoothing, indicating that recursive state-transition conditioning anchors bursts even in high-variance intervals.

•

CSDI
retains spikes but attenuates peak amplitudes during volatile periods.

•

heavily smooth the series, removing operationally significant transients.

•

Latent ODE
produces occasional bursts but its baseline drifts during long gaps, suggesting long-horizon predictions are not well anchored.

Preserving such perturbations is critical: they often signal process instability or control interventions; suppressing them risks operationally misleading imputations.

5.2.2
NH
4
- extended gaps, slow drift

Figure
5
focuses on NH
4
, a slow-moving water-quality parameter that drifts seasonally and occasionally exhibits sharp transients.
During extended gaps there is no ground truth, so structure that appears within a gap cannot be ruled out a priori.
Our qualitative assessment therefore asks whether imputations (i) carry the underlying drift across the gap, (ii) allow occasional bursts consistent with the surrounding context, and (iii) reconnect smoothly at the gap boundaries.

•

STDiff
maintains slow drift while allowing intermittent high-amplitude deviations consistent with pre- and post-gap behavior.

•

Latent ODE
generates lower-amplitude spikes with smoother transitions, which may underrepresent genuine rapid changes.

•

CSDI
flatlines mid-gap (e.g., early September), symptomatic of context starvation; variability resumes abruptly at observed-data boundaries.

Avoiding both excessive smoothing and unrealistic temporal stationarity is crucial for slow-varying variables like NH
4
, where gradual drift informs early warnings.

5.3
Computational considerations and limitations

STDiff requires
T
reverse-diffusion steps for each missing time step, so inference scales as
\mathcal{O}(H\,T)
with the gap length
H
(i.e.,
\mathcal{O}(T)
per step). In our experiments we set
T{=}1000
; using fewer steps or accelerated samplers/distillation can reduce latency at a small loss in fidelity, whereas larger
T
improves accuracy at higher cost.

Because the model conditions on control/exogenous inputs
(u_{t},w_{t})
, its performance depends on their quality. We pass binary masks alongside values and apply random input dropouts during training to make the model tolerant to occasional corruption. When long stretches of these inputs are missing, two practical fallbacks are: (i) imputing the covariates with a
simple
method (e.g., forward-fill/back-fill with decay, linear interpolation, or a low-order Kalman filter fitted on past data), and (ii) running a history-only variant that conditions only on the last valid state together with the input masks.

The present kernel assumes first-order Markov dynamics. This keeps sampling simple but can miss higher-order effects such as actuator delays or slow seasonal components. A short history encoder (e.g., a small TCN/RNN over a few past steps) can be added to the conditioning path to capture these dependencies without changing the diffusion objective; we leave this as a straightforward extension.

Finally, deployment in industrial streams must account for concept drift
(
gama2014
)
. We recommend rolling, time-based validation; lightweight drift monitoring on summary statistics; versioned checkpoints; and periodic retraining or scheduled refreshes when monitored drift or evaluation metrics indicate degradation.

6
Conclusion

In this work, we reframed time series imputation for industrial systems as the task of learning a conditional state transition model, rather than relying solely on fixed-window pattern completion. Our proposed method, STDiff, uses a denoising diffusion approach to generate missing values across long and irregular gaps, explicitly modelling how the system evolves over time rather than simply inferring values from short local context.

By conditioning on the last observed state and known control or external inputs, STDiff embeds a causal bias aligned with the physical and control-driven nature of industrial processes. This design avoids the context starvation that plagues fixed-window models, maintains robustness under severe block-missingness, and preserves important dynamic behaviours in both high-variance and slow-drift variables.

We validated STDiff on two wastewater treatment plant datasets - one with controlled synthetic gaps and one with naturally occurring operational gaps. In both settings, STDiff consistently outperformed strong window-based baselines and a continuous-time dynamics model, demonstrating that explicitly modelling system transitions yields more accurate and operationally plausible imputations. These results suggest that dynamics-aware, conditionally driven imputation is a principled and practical solution for cyber-physical systems, where the temporal coherence of reconstructed signals can be as critical as numerical error reduction.

The broader implication of this work is that industrial time series imputation benefits greatly from models that respect the causal structure of the system being modelled. By combining data-driven learning with explicit conditioning on system inputs, it is possible to achieve reconstructions that not only score well on error metrics but also align with the physical realities of the process - supporting better forecasting, monitoring, and decision-making.

7
Future Work

While this study establishes the potential of STDiff, further development could make it more powerful, efficient, and broadly applicable. The experiments revealed areas where the method’s strengths such as preserving dynamics under scarce context - could be extended, and where current limitations, like handling concept drift or scaling to extremely long gaps, could be addressed. Improvements in these directions would not only enhance accuracy but also strengthen deployment readiness in real-world industrial settings.

Promising directions for future research include:

•

Richer conditioning:
Incorporate short historical encoders, such as recurrent or temporal convolutional networks, alongside step-wise transitions to capture higher-order dependencies without sacrificing interpretability.

•

Cross-domain validation:
Apply STDiff to other control-driven domains such as manufacturing, HVAC, and power grid operations to assess portability and generalisability.

•

Downstream impact:
Quantify the benefits of improved imputation on forecasting accuracy, anomaly detection, and control performance, including integration with model-based reinforcement learning.

•

Adaptive retraining:
Develop strategies for handling concept drift in evolving industrial environments through periodic or online model updates.

•

Computational optimisation:
Explore faster reverse-diffusion sampling methods to reduce inference time for very long gaps while maintaining accuracy.
