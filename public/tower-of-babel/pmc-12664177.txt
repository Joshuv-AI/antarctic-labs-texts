Global burden of early-onset mental disorders in adolescents and young adults: a systematic analysis based on the Global Burden of Disease study 2021
Authors: Shi-Nan Wu, Rongqiang Liu, Lin Chen, Shao-Pan Wang, Xiao-Dong Chen, Dan-Yi Qin, Bing Yan, Shu-Jia Guo, Cai-Hong Huang, Jiaoyue Hu, Zuguo Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMental disorders represent a major global health challenge. This study aims to quantify the burden of mental disorders among adolescents and young adults (AYAs, aged 15–39) from 1990 to 2021 at global, regional, and national levels, to examine temporal trends and regional disparities, and to assess changes relative to 2019, providing evidence to inform targeted prevention and intervention strategies.MethodsWe analyzed Global Burden of Disease (GBD) data from 1990 to 2021 to assess the incidence, disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), and age-standardized rates of mental disorders (including anxiety disorders, autism spectrum disorders, conduct disorder, eating disorders, schizophrenia, attention−deficit/hyperactivity disorder, bipolar disorder, depressive disorders, idiopathic developmental intellectual disability and other mental disorders) among AYA, stratified by age, gender, sociodemographic index and region.ResultsIn 1990, the number of incident cases of mental disorders was 120.4 million (94.1 to 153.9 million), increasing to 156.8 million (121.5 to 202.1 million) in 2019, and reaching 195.3 million (149.9 to 253.0 million) in 2021. The age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR) was 5512.73 (4310.87 to 7039.93) in 1990, 5332.9 (4129.93 to 6875.31) in 2019, and 6561.63 (5034.48 to 8502.24) in 2021. The average annual percentage change (AAPC) in ASIR from 1990 to 2019 was −0.12 (−0.13 to −0.1), while from 2019 to 2021, the AAPC sharply increased to 12.15 (7.91 to 15.18). Globally, depressive disorders accounted for the highest number of incident mental disorder cases, while depressive disorders and anxiety disorders were the leading contributors to DALYs.ConclusionsSince 2019, the incidence of mental disorders among AYAs has risen markedly, with anxiety and depressive disorders accounting for the largest share of the burden. These findings highlight the urgent need for targeted, evidence-based public health interventions and proactive mental health strategies to address emerging challenges and reduce the long-term burden on this vulnerable population.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07581-x.

Introduction

Mental disorders have been shown to be one of the major causes of the global disease burden in the Global Burden of Disease Study (GBD) 2019 [1]. In 2019, the global prevalence of mental disorders across all age groups reached 970.1 million population, while the disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) attributed to mental disorders amounted to 125.3 million years [2]. Additionally, the Lancet Commission on Global Mental Health and Sustainable Development has emphasized that mental health is a fundamental human right and is essential for the development of all countries and regions. It is crucial to expand personalized services for individuals with mental disorders and ensure that they have access to their basic human rights [3]. The Coronavirus Disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic has been linked to a substantial increase in depression, anxiety, and substance use disorders, especially among adolescents and young adults [4]. Recent studies and the GBD 2021 update further indicate that the global burden of mental disorders has continued to rise in the post-pandemic period, largely driven by the psychological and social impacts of COVID-19 [5].

Adolescents and young adults (AYAs), aged 15–39 years, are particularly susceptible to the onset and impact of mental disorders, as this developmental stage is critical for educational achievement, career establishment, and the formation of social and emotional relationships [6–9]. Recent systematic reviews have revealed a global increase in the prevalence of depression and anxiety among AYAs, with pronounced disparities across regions and socio-demographic groups [10, 11]. The long-term consequences of mental disorders may be closely associated with low economic productivity, incarceration, suicide, and homelessness [12]. Moreover, our project team also found that mental disorders are closely related to dry eye, and good mental health is just as important as medication for treatment [13]. Therefore, mental disorders not only represent major psychological concerns but also interfere with the treatment of other diseases, resulting in both psychological and physical harm to patients. Moreover, they impose a substantial burden on public healthcare systems and reduce overall economic productivity. In response, several global initiatives and national policy frameworks have identified adolescent mental health as a critical priority for early intervention and prevention [14, 15].

Although previous studies have investigated the global burden of mental disorders and certain subtypes such as schizophrenia, as well as the epidemiological distribution among children and adolescents to support early intervention across the life course [16], these studies remain limited in several aspects. Most analyses are based on outdated datasets (e.g., GBD 2019 or earlier), focus narrowly on specific age groups or disorders, and lack comprehensive coverage of AYAs—a population experiencing the highest incidence of mental disorders and undergoing rapid physical, emotional, and psychosocial changes [17]. To address these gaps, we utilized the most recent GBD 2021 dataset to extract data on ten categories of mental disorders (including anxiety disorders, autism spectrum disorders, conduct disorder, eating disorders, schizophrenia, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, bipolar disorder, depressive disorders, idiopathic developmental intellectual disability, and other mental disorders) among AYAs at global, regional, and national levels across 204 countries and territories. We standardized and estimated the age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR) and age-standardized DALY rate (ASDR). This study aims to quantify the global incidence and DALY burden of multiple mental disorders among AYAs using GBD 2021 data and to identify demographic and regional disparities by age, sex, region, and Socio-Demographic Index (SDI) level. To our knowledge, this is the first comprehensive and up-to-date global analysis to systematically assess the burden of multiple mental disorders among AYAs, providing essential evidence to guide early intervention, resource allocation, and mental health policy development.

Methods

Data sources

The burden data for mental disorders among AYAs were obtained from GBD 2021. GBD 2021 provides the latest comprehensive analysis of 371 diseases and 87 risk factors across 204 countries and regions worldwide [18]. This study used GBD 2021 data to obtain the incidence, and DALYs of mental disorders among AYAs from 1990 to 2021, with subgroup analyses conducted by gender, age, region, and country. Data were acquired using the Global Health Data Exchange (GHDx) querytool maintained by the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (http://ghdx.healthdata.org/gbd-results-tool). This tool allows users to access annual frequency and age-standardized rates (ASR) for diseases’ incidence,prevalence, DALYs, deaths, and other data, stratified by gender, age, region, and country [19]. The mental disorders included in this study and their corresponding GBD codes or classification criteria are detailed in Supplementary Table 1 [20]. Owing to data unavailability for this age group, autism spectrum disorder, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, and related developmental disorders were excluded from the analysis. By leveraging the GBD database’s data on mental disorders among AYAs, this study was able to analyze trends and patterns in the burden of mental disorders across different demographic and geographic factors, providing valuable insights for the global prevention and control of mental disorders in specific periods and age groups. This was a non-clinical study and therefore did not require a clinical trial registration number. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. As the GBD database is publicly available and contains only de-identified data, ethical approval and informed consent were not required.

Estimation of the burden of mental disorders among AYAs

Descriptive analysis is used to assess the burden of mental disorders among AYAs at global, regional, and national levels. The analysis categorizes global regions based on the SDI, which incorporates total fertility rate, average education level, and per capita income, into low SDI ( < 0.466), lower-middle SDI (0.466–0.619), middle SDI (0.619–0.712), high-middle SDI (0.712–0.810), and high SDI (≥0.810) [21, 22]. In this study, we utilized disease maps to visualize the ASIR, and ASDR per 100,000 people at the country level. We also evaluated the trends in mental disorders among AYAs from 1990 to 2021 using estimated annual percent change (EAPC), and presented the distribution on a world map. In addition, Joinpoint analysis was conducted to assess the overall and segmental epidemiological trends of ASIR and ASDR among sex subgroups from 1990 to 2021.

Epidemiological trends of mental disorders across different regions

Area charts were generated to illustrate the cause-specific distribution of mental disorders from 1990 to 2021, based on ASIR and ASDR, across global and five SDI regions, as well as by specific causes of mental disorders. Spearman correlation analyses were conducted to assess the relationship between ASIR and ASDR of mental disorders and SDI levels across 21 global regions from 1990 to 2021. The analysis was based on the correlation between the annual SDI values from 1990 to 2021 across 21 global regions and the corresponding ASIR and ASDR values. Additionally, we evaluated mental disorders and their specific causes in AYAs by age and sex subgroups.

Statistic analysis

The reported estimates for incidence, and DALYs in the study are accompanied by 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs) [23]. These intervals are determined by identifying the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of all 1,000 samples in the posterior distribution at each step of the burden estimation process [2]. We calculated the annual percentage change (APC) and its 95% confidence interval (CI), as well as the average annual percentage change (AAPC) and its 95% CI. For the calculation of ASR among AYAs aged 15 to 39 years, the following formula was used: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ASR = {{\mathop \sum \nolimits_{i = 1}^A {a_i}{w_i}} \over {\mathop \sum \nolimits_{I = 1}^A {W_I}}}$$\end{document}

In this formula, ai represents the incidence rate for a specific age group, wi denotes the weight of the same age group in the selected reference standard population (with i indicating the ith age group), and A represents the upper age limit. Age standardization aims to eliminate the influence of differences in population age structures across countries and regions, ensuring the comparability of research metrics [24]. For the calculation of the AAPC and APC in Joinpoint, we used the overall mental disorders in the 15–39 age group. Additionally, we set the maximum number of joinpoints to 5, and model selection was based on the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) [25]. Joinpoint regression analysis was performed on age-standardized data globally, with stratification by sex. At the same time, we also provided a distribution of the increasing and decreasing trends of the different specific causes that constitute mental disorders from 1990 to 2021, presented as an area chart. Additionally, we compared the trend distributions of the specific causes of mental disorders across different age groups. In the above calculations, the positive and negative values of EAPC and AAPC respectively represent an increasing trend in the indicator over the study period, meaning the values of the indicator rise over time, or a decreasing trend, meaning the values of the indicator decline over time. These metrics are based on the GBD 2021 standard population structure data. The world maps were developed using the ggplot2 package (version 3.4.2), sf package (version 1.0–15). All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.3.2), and Joinpoint (version 4.9.0.0). A P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. In this study, apart from the aforementioned regression analyses, statistical significance was determined based on non-overlapping 95% UIs, in accordance with the standard GBD analytical framework.

Results

The global burden of mental disorders among AYAs

In 1990, the number of newly diagnosed mental disorders reached 120.4 million (94.1 to 153.9 million), increasing to 156.8 million (121.5 to 202.1 million) in 2019 and 195.3 million (149.9 to 253.0 million) in 2021. Correspondingly, the ASIR was 5512.73 (4310.87 to 7039.93) in 1990, 5332.9 (4129.93 to 6875.31) in 2019, and 6561.63 (5034.48 to 8502.24) in 2021. The AAPC of ASR from 1990 to 2019 was −0.12 (−0.13 to −0.10), while from 2019 to 2021 it was 12.15 (7.91 to 15.18). In 2021, the number of newly diagnosed mental disorders was higher among females than males, reaching 115.5 million (88.2 to 150.4 million) in females and 79.8 million (61.7 to 102.4 million) in males. Among different age subgroups within the 15 to 39 year range, the 20 to 24 age group had the highest incidence in 2021, with 40.7 million cases (30.9 to 54.9 million). Among the five SDI regions, the low-middle SDI region reported the highest number of new cases, reaching 55.9 million (42.4 to 73.4 million). Among the 21 global regions, South Asia recorded the highest incidence of newly diagnosed mental disorders in 2021, with 52.6 million cases (40.4 to 68.5 million). Distribution of mental disorders across 204 countries and territories globally

In 2021, among the 204 countries and territories, Greenland, Palestine, and the United States of America had the highest ASIR of mental disorders among AYAs, reaching 18,965.26 (13,464.6 to 26,157.01), 13,513.55 (9,223.32 to 19,680.95), and 12,622.43 (10,050.15 to 15,711.94), respectively. Regarding ASDR, Greenland, Australia, and New Zealand ranked highest, with values of 4,734.92 (3,315.7 to 6,648.19), 4,171.94 (3,004.37 to 5,527.16), and 3,972.84 (2,832.69 to 5,289.38), respectively. Based on the EAPC from 1990 to 2021, the three countries with the fastest-growing ASIR were Mexico, Spain, and Malaysia, with EAPCs of 1.5 (1.23 to 1.76), 1.04 (0.65 to 1.43), and 0.91 (0.72 to 1.11), respectively. In contrast, the countries with the fastest-declining ASIR were China, Cuba, and Singapore, with EAPCs of −1.32 (−1.51 to −1.12), −1.24 (−1.56 to −0.92), and −1.19 (−1.45 to −0.93), respectively. For ASDR, the highest increases were observed in Mexico, Spain, and Malaysia, with EAPCs of 0.84 (0.7 to 0.97), 0.62 (0.43 to 0.82), and 0.44 (0.37 to 0.51), respectively. The most decreases in ASDR were found in China, Cuba, and Estonia, with EAPCs of −0.47 (−0.55 to −0.38), −0.41 (−0.57 to −0.25), and −0.33 (−0.49 to −0.18), respectively. Trend distribution of mental disorders

Globally, the AAPC of ASIR and ASDR from 1990 to 2021 was 0.62 and 0.41, respectively. An APC greater than 0 indicates an upward trend in the disease indicator, whereas an APC less than 0 indicates a downward trend. For ASIR in both sexes, the APC was 0.01 during 1990–2005, −1.56 during 2005–2010, 0.43 during 2010–2019, and 12.15 during 2019–2021. For ASDR, the APC was −0.07 during 1990–2019 and 7.52 during 2019–2021. Further details on sex subgroups are provided in Fig. 2. In terms of age- and sex-specific trends, the overall disease burden of mental disorders among AYAs was higher in females than in males, with the highest ASIR observed in the 20–24-year age group and the highest DALYs in the 30–34-year group. Among specific disorders, anxiety and depressive disorders contributed most substantially to the total burden, both showing markedly higher rates in females. Anxiety disorders peaked in the 15–19-year age group, whereas depressive disorders peaked in the 35–39-year group. In contrast, bipolar disorder showed no notable sex difference, with its highest incidence in the 15–19-year group. For conduct disorder, eating disorders, and schizophrenia, incidence rates were generally higher in males. Eating disorders peaked in the 15–19-year group, and schizophrenia in the 20–24-year group. Due to data unavailability for this age range, autism spectrum disorder, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, idiopathic developmental intellectual disability, and other mental disorders were not included in this analysis. Regional distribution and correlation of mental disorders and specific causes

Globally, from 1990 to 2021, the leading causes of mental disorders based on the ASIR were depressive disorders and anxiety disorders, with ASIRs in 2021 of 5320.85 (95% UI: 3876.77 to 7250.69) and 846.14 (564.21 to 1168.66), respectively. Regarding the ASDR, the primary contributors were depressive disorders, anxiety disorders, autism spectrum disorders, bipolar disorder, and schizophrenia, with 2021 ASDRs of 840.24 (537.51 to 1240.50), 679.61 (431.65 to 993.49), 153.00 (103.77 to 215.64), and 138.71 (86.71 to 210.23), respectively. These distributions showed no distinct variation across the five global SDI regions, as detailed in Fig. 3. A Spearman correlation analysis between ASIR/ASDR and SDI across 21 global regions from 1990 to 2021 revealed that for ASIR, no significant association was observed between mental disorders and SDI among AYAs (ρ = 0.02; p = 0.604). However, for ASDR, a significant positive correlation was found (ρ = 0.26; p < 0.001). In 2021, the region with the highest ASIR was High-income North America, reaching 12,275.18 (9726.11 to 15,289.73), while the highest ASDR was observed in Australasia, at 4137.82 (3002.90 to 5469.73). Discussion

The number of new cases of mental disorders globally reached 195.3 million in 2021, with an ASIR of 6,561.63 per 100,000. Moreover, this study confirmed a notable inflection point in the distribution of both incidence and DALY indicators around the year 2019. Previous studies have confirmed that 80.6% of the burden of mental disorders primarily occurs in the working-age population (ages 16–65), with the age group of 15–39 being a peak age range for the occurrence of mental disorders [2]. Therefore, based on the latest GBD 2021 data on mental disorders, we conducted a comprehensive analysis at the global, regional, and national levels. This study is the first to examine the burden of mental disorders in high-incidence age groups of AYAs within the GBD 2021 framework.

The analysis of mental disorders among AYAs based on GBD 2021 revealed that depressive and anxiety disorders remain the predominant contributors to the overall burden. Specifically, depressive disorders exhibit a markedly higher ASIR than other mental disorders, while anxiety and depressive disorders together account for the largest proportion of the ASDR. These findings are consistent with prior evidence highlighting the significant psychological and systemic impacts of these conditions on brain health, behavior, and lifestyle [26, 27]. Unlike previous studies that primarily focused on mental disorders in childhood and adolescence or on specific regions such as China [1, 28], as well as on particular conditions like anxiety and depressive disorders [29], our study is the first to comprehensively examine the epidemiological characteristics of mental disorders among individuals aged 15–39 years—a critical transitional period of life—at the global level. However, our study extends previous GBD analyses by providing a comprehensive and up-to-date evaluation specifically focused on AYAs, a population segment often underrepresented in global mental health assessments.

Impact of COVID-19 on mental health trends

Moreover, by leveraging data from GBD 2021, our research identifies a distinct epidemiological inflection point around 2019, which has not been clearly characterized in earlier GBD-based or systematic studies. This turning point coincides with the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, suggesting that the pandemic served as a catalyst for the rapid escalation of mental health burden worldwide [28, 30]. The global spread of COVID-19 introduced multiple psychosocial stressors—fear of infection, economic insecurity, unemployment, social isolation, and uncertainty about the future—that collectively intensified anxiety and depressive symptoms across populations [31–34]. Furthermore, the constant exposure to pandemic-related misinformation and the worldwide economic downturn further compounded these mental health challenges [29, 35]. Taken together, these findings not only document temporal changes in the global burden of mental disorders but also provide evidence that global crises can notably alter mental health trends among AYAs. This observation contributes to a deeper understanding of the temporal dynamics of mental disorders and highlights the importance of resilient and adaptive mental health systems capable of responding to global emergencies.

The findings of this study suggest several targeted strategies to mitigate the burden of mental disorders among AYAs. First, given the disproportionately high incidence and burden of depressive and anxiety disorders, priority should be given to strengthening early identification and intervention programs specifically for these conditions in AYAs. Screening initiatives could be integrated into schools, universities, and primary care settings to facilitate timely detection and referral [36]. Second, the identification of a distinct inflection point around 2019, coinciding with the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, highlights the need for rapid response mechanisms in mental health systems to address emerging crises. Policy measures could include contingency plans for scaling up mental health services during periods of increased psychosocial stress, such as pandemics or economic downturns. Third, given the influence of social isolation, misinformation, and economic stressors on mental health, interventions should prioritize digital mental health platforms [37], online counseling [38], and community-based outreach programs to maintain support during times when traditional services are disrupted. Finally, cross-sector collaboration should be directed and measurable, for example by linking education, health, and social services to address the specific risk factors identified in this study, rather than proposing generic coordination. These targeted and evidence-linked measures ensure that policy recommendations are directly informed by the observed trends and risk factors in AYAs, increasing the likelihood of effective mitigation of mental health burden.

Sex and age differences

On the other hand, our study found that the incidence and DALY rates of mental disorders among females aged 15–39 are notably higher than those among males. This gender difference is evident worldwide, particularly for anxiety disorders and depressive disorders, where females show a markedly higher burden than males. However, it is noteworthy that for conditions such as conduct disorder, eating disorders, and schizophrenia, males have notably higher incidence rates than females. In contrast, for bipolar disorder, there is no notable difference between sexes. For anxiety and depressive disorders, females in this age group experience significant hormonal fluctuations due to puberty, menstrual cycles, pregnancy, and postpartum changes, particularly involving estrogen and progesterone. These hormonal fluctuations can affect the neurotransmitter systems in the brain, increasing the risk of mental disorders such as anxiety and depression among females during this age period [39, 40]. Additionally, females aged 15–39 often bear more social responsibilities, such as balancing academic, work, marital, and family roles. The burden of these multiple roles can lead to increased stress and anxiety, especially in modern society, where females are expected to fulfill traditional family roles while also facing challenges in their career development. Societal and cultural expectations regarding appearance, body image, and gender roles place significant pressure on females, particularly during adolescence and early adulthood. This pressure often leads to psychological issues, such as appearance anxiety, eating disorders, and body image concerns, which can increase the risk of mental disorders. Considering these complex factors, it is crucial for governments at all levels worldwide to prioritize anxiety and depressive disorders among females, providing targeted support and implementing effective interventions. Additionally, it is worth noting that in the AYAs population, anxiety disorders are slightly more prevalent in the 15 to 19 age group compared to other age groups. For bipolar disorders and eating disorders, the 15 to 19 age group also shows a notably higher prevalence. In contrast, schizophrenia is most prevalent in the 20 to 24 age group within the AYAs population. The 15 to 19 age range corresponds to adolescence, a period marked by significant emotional fluctuations, during which the brain’s emotional regulation mechanisms are not yet fully matured [41, 42]. Due to the dual psychological and physiological changes during adolescence, anxiety, bipolar, and eating disorders show a tendency to be more prevalent. On the other hand, the higher incidence of schizophrenia in the 20 to 24 age group may be related to brain development and structural changes during this period, particularly the maturation of the cerebral cortex, as schizophrenia typically emerges during critical periods of neurodevelopment [43, 44]. We should pay more attention to the mental health of the AYAs population, especially the impact of physiological and social changes during adolescence and young adulthood on mental health.

Regional disparities and policy implications

The incidence and DALY rates of mental disorders exhibit diverse patterns across different countries. In 2021, Greenland had the highest ASIR and ASDR among 204 countries worldwide. From 1990 to 2021, Mexico experienced the fastest growth in mental disorders among the AYAs population, while China saw the fastest decline. Additionally, studies suggest that the cultural adaptation pressure in Greenland is one of the key factors contributing to the high prevalence of mental disorders, with adolescents being the primary affected group [45]. Research has reported that in Mexico, 87.4% of patients with mild mental disorders, 77.9% of those with moderate disorders, and 76.2% of patients with severe mental disorders (such as schizophrenia or bipolar disorder) did not receive treatment [46, 47]. To address the high prevalence and rapid increase of mental disorders in Mexico, it is essential to enhance the coverage of mental health services and increase resource investment, particularly for comprehensive interventions targeting socioeconomic pressures, traumatic violence, and the youth population. In contrast, China has shown the greatest decline in mental disorders among the AYA population in recent years. These epidemiological changes may be associated with increased national attention to mental health, including the implementation of the first Mental Health Law in 2012, greater financial investment, expanded mental health training programs, and the development of community-based services [48, 49]. Therefore, the epidemiological trends of mental disorders vary across different countries and regions, and governments may need to formulate relevant policies tailored to local conditions to closely monitor and prevent mental disorders in the AYAs population. Moreover, we also observed that while there was no significant correlation between the ASIR of mental disorders among AYAs and the SDI levels across the 21 global regions, the ASDR showed a significant positive correlation with SDI. Studies have shown that the prevalence of depression, anxiety, and stress-related disorders is significantly higher in high-SDI or high-income countries than in low- and middle-income countries [50]. This trend is believed to be associated with modernization-related factors such as fast-paced lifestyles, intense social competition, feelings of loneliness, reduced social support, heavy workloads, and increased dependence on digital technology [51, 52]. Recent literature suggests that social determinants, such as socioeconomic status, education, and access to healthcare, notably contribute to mental health disparities among adolescents [29]. At the same time, the COVID-19 pandemic has further exacerbated these issues, with social isolation, economic uncertainty, and prolonged screen exposure having a apparent impact on young people’s mental health, driving the rise of digital mental health interventions [53]. Digital tools, including mobile applications and online counseling services, have become effective means to bridge the gap in mental health services, especially in resource-limited areas [54]. By improving social support systems, such as career counseling, psychological support, and digital mental health services, it is possible to alleviate stress and enhance the mental well-being of adolescents and young adults. However, it is important to note that while the region with the highest ASIR was High-income North America, the highest ASDR was observed in Australasia. The rapid increase in these areas may be attributed to improvements in medical diagnosis and public health systems, socioeconomic changes and stressors, rapid population growth and an increasing number of young people, cultural and societal shifts, and the impact of natural disasters and conflicts. Understanding these factors is crucial for formulating appropriate public health interventions.

Limitations

However, several limitations should be acknowledged in this study. First, the GBD data are derived from integrated meta-regression analyses, and the quality and availability of data vary across countries, geographical regions, and disease types, which may affect the accuracy of the findings [55]. In economically underdeveloped regions, due to outdated diagnostic techniques, reports of mental disorders may be underreported. Second, the occurrence of mental disorders is influenced by factors such as race and genetics, which the GBD data cannot specifically analyze in terms of their impact on the burden of mental disorders among AYAs. In addition, this study did not account for the different severity levels of mental disorders, yet such information is critical for implementing targeted interventions in different regions. Moreover, variations in mental health surveillance systems across regions may lead to data heterogeneity and misclassification, affecting the comparability of results and the interpretation for policy-making. Finally, these limitations imply that caution is needed when interpreting the findings and formulating policy recommendations, particularly in low-resource settings and regions with incomplete data, as the actual disease burden may be underestimated or overestimated.

Conclusion

This study systematically analyzed the global burden of mental disorders among AYAs using data from GBD 2021 across global, regional, and national levels. We identified 2019 as a key turning point in the epidemiological trends of mental disorders from 1990 to 2021, with a notable increase thereafter. Depressive and anxiety disorders accounted for the largest proportion of the overall burden, and females consistently showed higher rates than males. Globally, Mexico exhibited the fastest increase in mental disorders among AYAs, whereas China experienced the greatest decline. These findings highlight the urgent need for early intervention and prevention strategies targeting the AYA population. Integrating mental health education into school curricula, implementing nationwide awareness campaigns, and leveraging digital mental health platforms could help reduce the growing burden. Future research should focus on longitudinal analyses to clarify causal pathways, explore differences by disorder severity, and evaluate the effectiveness of targeted policy and intervention approaches across regions.
