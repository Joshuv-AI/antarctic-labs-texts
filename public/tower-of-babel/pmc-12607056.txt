Amygdala functional connectivity and response to aerobic exercise in subthreshold depression-an exploratory fMRI study
Authors: Lina Huang, Wen Zhang, Jun Zhang, Qin Li, Wenbin Shen, Hongqiang Zhang, Xiaowei Yin, Juyun Zhou, Qingguo Ding
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAerobic exercise (AE) has emerged as a promising non-pharmacological intervention for depression. However, the extent to which AE can alleviate depressive symptoms varies across individuals, and the neural or clinical factors that relate to treatment response remain incompletely understood. This study aimed to investigate whether baseline amygdala-based functional connectivity (FC) is associated with symptom improvement following AE and identify neural correlates of treatment responsiveness.MethodsForty-three participants with subthreshold depression (StD) completed an AE intervention and were classified as remitters (n = 21) or non-remitters (n = 22) based on post-AE Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) scores. Resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) data were acquired at baseline and after the intervention. Group comparisons and partial correlation analyses were conducted to assess associations between amygdala-based FC and depressive symptom outcomes.ResultsBaseline left amygdala FC was positively correlated with post-AE PHQ-9 scores in the right precuneus and bilateral middle frontal gyrus (MFG), and negatively correlated with changes in PHQ-9 (ΔPHQ-9) scores in the left precuneus and left MFG. Additionally, remitters showed reduced FC between the left amygdala and left supplementary motor area (SMA) compared to non-remitters. Baseline right amygdala FC was positively correlated with post-AE PHQ-9 scores in the left inferior parietal lobe (IPL), right middle temporal gyrus (MTG), left superior medial frontal gyrus (mSFG) and left MTG, but there were no significant findings for ΔPHQ-9 or group differences. An exploratory analysis combining bilateral amygdala FC and clinical variables yielded high classification accuracy within the same sample (AUC = 0.93). A significant group effect was also observed in the right MTG for left amygdala FC, though no group × time interaction emerged.ConclusionsBaseline amygdala FC is associated with symptom improvement during AE in StD. Introduction

Depression is a prevalent and debilitating mental disorder that imposes significant burdens on individuals and society [1]. Subthreshold depression (StD), characterized by clinically relevant depressive symptoms that do not meet the full diagnostic criteria for major depressive disorder (MDD) [2], represents a critical mental health concern due to its high risk of progression to full-blown MDD [3, 4]. Early identification and intervention in individuals with StD are therefore essential to prevent symptom escalation and chronicity.

The pathogenesis of depression involves complex interactions among genetic, environmental, and neurobiological factors [5]. Among these, the amygdala - an essential hub for emotional processing and regulation - has been consistently implicated in both StD and MDD. Aberrant amygdala function and its disrupted connectivity with prefrontal regulatory regions have been observed in neuroimaging studies and are believed to underlie the emotional dysregulation central to depressive symptoms [6–8]. Notably, baseline amygdala functional connectivity (FC) has been shown to be associated with post-intervention changes in depressive symptoms [9, 10], highlighting its relevance for understanding individual differences in emotion regulation and treatment responsiveness.

Building on this framework, aerobic exercise (AE) has gained increasing recognition as a promising non-pharmacological intervention for mood disorders [11, 12]. It is recommended as one of the treatment options for mild to moderate depression in many evidence-based guidelines [13]. Its antidepressant effects have been attributed to mechanisms such as enhanced neurogenesis, modulation of monoamine neurotransmitters, and anti-inflammatory actions [12, 14]. Recent evidence further indicates that AE can modulate fronto-limbic circuits involved in emotion regulation, with the amygdala playing a central role in stress and affective processing [15]. However, it remains unclear to what extent AE can normalize amygdala-related dysfunction in StD, and whether baseline amygdala FC relates to subsequent symptom improvement.

Therefore, the present study aimed to investigate amygdala-based FC in individuals with StD and to evaluate the neural effects of AE. Specifically, we examined whether baseline amygdala FC were related to differential clinical outcomes following AE. By elucidating these neural correlates, our aim was to explore potential neurobiologically informed strategies for personalized intervention to prevent the progression of StD.

Methods

Participants enrollment

Forty-three adults aged 18–50 with StD and no prior treatment were enrolled. All participants engaged in a moderate-intensity AE program for eight weeks. Detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria are provided in our prior study [16] and in the Supplemental Material 1. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Changshu Hospital Affiliated to Nantong University (Approval No. 2022-KY-SK06), and all participants provided written informed consent.

Clinical outcome measures

Depressive symptom severity was assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) [17–20]. Assessments were conducted at baseline and at the end of the 8-week AE intervention. We categorized participants into “remitters” and “non-remitters” to focus on clinically meaningful outcomes, i.e., achieving a post-intervention PHQ-9 score < 5, which represents no depressive symptoms [21].

Aerobic exercise protocol

Participants underwent a structured 8-week moderate-intensity AE regimen, consistent with the American College of Sports Medicine guidelines [22, 23]. Supervised sessions primarily foucsed on closed-skill activities [24], performed at 60–75% of maximum heart rate and scheduled on nonconsecutive days (further details provided in Supplemental Material 2). Prior researches have demonstrated the efficacy of this protocol in alleviating depressive symptoms [16, 25].

The AE intervention was discontinued under the following conditions: (1) prolonged absence or failure to comply with trial requirements for more than two consecutive weeks; (2) emergence of new psychiatric or physical conditions during the study period; or (3) significant life events that could jeopardize participant well-being. These criteria were established to ensure participant safety and study integrity. In addition, participants were also instructed to maintain a stable diet during the intervention.

MRI acquisition

Eligible participants underwent MRI scanning on a 3.0T GE Discovery 750 W MRI scanner (General Electric Medical Systems, Waukesha, WI, USA), equipped with a 16-channel head-neck combined array coil (GE Healthcare). The same acquisition parameters were used throughout the study. During the resting-state scan, participants were instructed to lie in a supine position, stay awake, breathe calmly and evenly, and refrain from focusing on any particular thoughts. Foam cushions were utilized to limit head motion, and malleable earplugs were provided to reduce scanner noise. Resting-state blood oxygen level-dependent (BOLD) imaging was acquired using T2-weighted single-shot gradient-echo planar imaging (EPI), with the following parameters: repetition time (TR) = 2000 ms, echo time (TE) = 30 ms, flip angle = 90°, field of view (FOV) = 240 mm × 240 mm, matrix = 64 × 64, slice spacing = 3.8 mm (0.2 mm gap), pixel bandwidth = 7812.5 Hz/pixel, voxel size = 3.75 × 3.75 × 3.6; volumes = 150, and 33 slices acquired in ascending order without full coverage of the cerebellum. Additionally, a sagittal three-dimensional T1-weighted (3D-T1WI) structural image was obtained using the Bravo sequence, with parameters: TR = 8.464 ms, TE = 3.248 ms, flip angle = 12°, FOV = 256 mm × 256 mm, matrix = 64 × 64, and slice thickness / spacing = 1 mm / 0 mm. All images underwent quality control by a board-certified radiologist before anonymizing participants’ identities. To minimize acute exercise effects on neural activity, participants were instructed to refrain from any form of exercise for 24 h prior to each scan.

Data preprocessing

Data preprocessing was conducted using the DPARSF 5.0 toolkit in Matlab 2022b. The processing steps were as follows: (1) converting image data from DICOM to NIFTI format; (2) discarding the first 10 volumes to allow signal stabilization; (3) performing slice timing and head motion correction, with participants exhibiting head motion exceeding 3 mm translation or 3° rotation being excluded; (4) normalizing the data to Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space [26] and resampling to a 3 × 3 × 3 mm resolution; (5) applying spatial smoothing with a 4 mm full-width at half-maximum (FWHM) Gaussian kernel; (6) removing linear trends; (7) regressing out motion parameters (Friston 24-parameter model), cerebrospinal fluid, and white matter signals; global signal regression was excluded; (8) identifying high-motion time points using framewise displacement (FD), and participants exhibiting head motion with mean FD >0.5 mm being excluded; (9) applying band-pass filtering (0.01–0.08 Hz) to retain low-frequency fluctuations relevant for FC analyses.

Seed points-based FC analysis

Regions of interest (ROIs) for bilateral amygdala were defined using the automated anatomical labeling (AAL3) atlas. For each ROI, the average time series was extracted and correlated with all other brain voxels to generate individual FC maps. Pearson’s correlation coefficients were transformed to z-values using Fisher’s r-to-z transformation to improve normality [27].

Statistical analysis

Partial correlation analyses were performed to examine the associations between baseline FC and both post-AE PHQ-9 scores and changes in PHQ-9 (i.e., pre-post intervention, ΔPHQ-9) scores, while controlling for age, sex, years of education, head motion, and body mass index (BMI) as covariates. Multiple comparisons were corrected using Gaussian Random Field (GRF) correction, with a voxel-wise threshold of p < 0.01 and a cluster-level threshold of p < 0.05 (two-tailed). Subsequently, Baseline FC maps were first compared between remission and non-remission groups. In addition, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was conducted to examine the discriminative ability of bilateral amygdala-based FC metrics for differentiating treatment responders from non-responders. Clinical variables were defined as age, sex, education, and BMI, reflecting demographic and general health background rather than symptom severity. Baseline PHQ-9 was not included in the ROC analysis to avoid symptom severity disproportionately influencing the evaluation of intervention response.

To assess group × time interactions, a two-way analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was performed to evaluate the main effects of group (remission vs. non-remision) and time (baseline vs. follow-up) on bilateral amygdala-based FC. Age, sex, educational level, head motion, and BMI were included as nuisance covariates. ANCOVA results were corrected for multiple comparisons using GRF correction, with a voxel-wise threshold of p < 0.01 and a cluster-level threshold of p < 0.05.

Results

Participant demographics

The 43 participants’ mean age was 30.67 years (range: 19-49 years, standard deviation: 7.45), and included 44.19% males, 55.81% females. There were no significant differences between remitters (n = 21) and non-remitters (n = 22) in terms of age, sex, educational level, BMI, and baseline level of physical activity (Table 1). Although these variables were not significant, they were included as covariates of no interest in the group-level analyses. Participants demonstrated good adherence to the AE intervention, with attendance and effective training rates exceeding 80% (4 participants dropped out).

Effect of AE on the severity psychological symptom

Overall, participants with StD exhibited a significant reduction in PHQ-9 scores following the 8-week AE intervention (p < 0.001). There was no significant difference in baseline PHQ-9 scores between participants who would later achieve remission (remitters) and those who would not (non-remitters). After the intervention, both groups exhibited significant reductions relative to their baselines. Notably, remitters demonstrated greater reductions in PHQ-9 scores (ΔPHQ-9: 5.00 vs. 0.00, p < 0.001, Table 2), reflecting a stronger improvement in depressive symptoms.

ROC curve analysis demonstrated that the inclusion of FC metrics alongside clinical variables (age, sex, education, and BMI) significantly improved discriminative performance compared with clinical variables alone. Specifically, bilateral amygdala FC achieved an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.85 (95% confidence interval [CI], 0.73–0.97) in differentiating remitters from non-remitters, outperforming models based on unilateral FC measures. Notably, the integration of bilateral FC metrics with clinical variables further improved classification performance, yielding an AUC of 0.93 (95% CI, 0.86–1.00) (Fig. 2).

Discussion

This study investigated the neural correlates of amygdala-based FC and their association with response to AE among individuals with StD. Our findings demonstrate that AE intervention significantly reduced depressive symptoms, with remitters showing greater improvements compared to non-remitters. Importantly, baseline amygdala FC patterns were associated with intervention outcomes, suggesting their potential relevance for understanding individual differences in responsiveness to AE.

Consistent with prior literature, AE exerted a beneficial effect on depressive symptoms in individuals with StD [16, 28]. While there were no significant demographic or clinical differences between remitters and non-remitters at baseline, marked differences emerged in post-AE PHQ-9 scores and symptom reduction, suggesting individual variability in treatment response. Although the non-remission group did not achieve full remission (PHQ-9 score < 5), they also showed notable symptom improvement. A potential explanation for this outcome is that the duration of the AE intervention was relatively short [29], suggesting that a longer intervention period may be necessary in future studies to achieve more substantial benefits.

At the neural level, baseline left amygdala FC was significantly correlated with post-AE PHQ-9 scores in the overall StD participants, particularly in the right precuneus and bilateral MFG. These areas are involved in self-referential processing and cognitive control [30, 31], suggesting that stronger connectivity between the amygdala and these regions may reflect enhanced emotional regulation capacity, which in turn supports symptom improvement. In addition, negative associations between baseline left amygdala FC and ΔPHQ-9 scores were observed in the left precuneus and left MFG. This suggests that stronger FC between the amygdala and these regions at baseline was associated with less degree symptom improvement following AE.

Interestingly, the findings revealed a nuanced pattern in the relationship between baseline amygdala-MFG connectivity and treatment outcome. Baseline left amygdala FC with the right precuneus and bilateral MFG showed a positive correlation with post-AE PHQ-9 scores and a negative correlation with ΔPHQ-9. That is, stronger baseline FC was associated with higher residual depressive symptoms after AE, and with smaller symptom improvement. This pattern suggests that high left amygdala-MFG connectivity may serve as a marker of non-response risk rather than a pro-response signature. Strong connectivity (especially bilateral MFG) can be regarded as a higher reserve of cognitive control ability [32, 33]. However, in the context of emotion regulation, heightened amygdala–prefrontal coupling may instead reflect a more rigid or compensatory network: individuals may have relatively intact circuits that help maintain current symptom levels but offer limited plasticity for further improvement. This can also be interpreted as a ceiling effect, where participants with stronger connectivity already have moderately regulated emotional networks, leaving less room for AE-induced gains. These findings emphasize the need to differentiate neural markers of final symptom state from those linked to subsequent symptom improvement, as overlapping but distinct network pathways may contribute to each.

Notably, the left MFG emerged as a convergent hub in our analyses, showing significant associations with both left and right amygdala FC. Specifically, stronger baseline amygdala-left MFG connectivity was associated with higher post-AE PHQ-9 scores for both hemispheric seeds, and for the left amygdala, with smaller ΔPHQ-9. This convergence underscores the potential importance of the left MFG as a key node linking emotional reactivity and cognitive control. The left MFG is involved in higher-order cognitive processing, including verbal and non-verbal reasoning, and has been implicated in maladaptive processes such as rumination [34, 35]. Stronger coupling with the amygdala may therefore represent a rigid or over-engaged regulatory mechanism that sustains depressive symptoms and limits intervention-induced plasticity. These findings suggest that left MFG connectivity with the amygdala may serve as a critical neural marker of non-response risk in StD.

Furthermore, remitters exhibited significantly weaker FC between the left amygdala and the SMA compared to non-remitters. The SMA is involved in motor planning and is increasingly recognized for its role in integrating affective and behavioral response [36, 37]. The reduced connectivity between the amygdala and the SMA in the remission group may reflect a normalization of emotion–motor integration. In depressive states, hyperconnectivity between these regions might reflect increased emotional interference with motor planning and execution(such as symptoms like lack of motivation and slow movement) [38]. Following AE, the decoupling of these circuits, observed in the remission group, could indicate reduced emotional dysregulation and improved autonomy of motor and executive functions, contributing to symptom remission.

Similarly, baseline right amygdala FC showed positive correlations with post-AE PHQ-9 scores in the left IPL, right MTG, left mSFG, and left MTG. This indicates that stronger right amygdala-cortical coupling was associated with higher residual depressive symptoms following AE, again suggesting a potential risk marker of non-response. However, no significant associations were observed with ΔPHQ-9, and no group differences emerged between remitters and non-remitters. These findings suggest that right amygdala connectivity may be more related to the persistence of depressive symptoms than to changes following treatment. These regions are key components of the default mode and salience networks, which are often dysregulated in depression [39–42]. However, right amygdala FC did not correlate with changes in PHQ-9 scores nor differ significantly between remitters and non-remitters, suggesting a more state-dependent rather than trait-level association. Moreover, the absence of significant group differences in right amygdala FC also suggests a potential lateralization effect, with the left amygdala playing a more critical role in relation to treatment response. Previous studies have noted that the left amygdala may be more involved in conscious, cognitive aspects of emotional evaluation, while the right may be more automatic and reactive [43, 44]. Our results are consistent with this functional specialization and suggest that left amygdala networks are more sensitive to behavioral interventions such as AE.

Importantly, the ROC analysis further reinforced the clinical utility of neuroimaging biomarkers. Bilateral amygdala FC metrics, especially when combined with demographic and clinical information, showed strong ability to discriminate remitters from non-remitters (AUC = 0.93). This supported a precision psychiatry framework, where neurofunctional data are leveraged to identify individuals most likely to benefit from behavioral interventions such as AE. In this study, the clinical variable set included age, sex, education, and BMI, reflecting demographic and general health background rather than symptom severity. Baseline PHQ-9, although clinically relevant, was not included because remitters and non-remitters did not differ significantly at baseline, and our aim was to assess the contribution of FC without symptom severity dominating the analysis. Head-motion effects, though not explicitly added as covariates, were minimized through rigorous preprocessing and quality control procedures.

A significant main effect of group was observed in the right MTG for left amygdala FC, independent of time, indicating that some functional differences may be trait-like and not fully normalized with symptom remission. Instead, these enduring connectivity patterns may represent trait-like neurobiological markers associated with individual differences in treatment responsiveness. The persistence of altered amygdala–MTG connectivity may reflect a pre-dispositional or compensatory mechanism, suggesting that baseline FC features are linked to subsequent clinical outcomes.

The current study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the relatively small sample size restricts the generalizability of the findings and limits the strength of causal inferences. Second, a placebo or control group was not included, as the study was designed to investigate FC changes following AE by comparing differences between remitters and non-remitters, and by examining baseline FC as an effective indicator in differentiating treatment response. Third, our study population consisted primarily of young adults with subclinical depressive symptoms, it remains uncertain whether bilateral amygdala FC could serve as a neuroimaging marker of treatment response in individuals with MDD, older adults, or patients receiving usual care. Future research should address these limitations by incorporating larger and more diverse samples, appropriate control conditions, and participants from clinical populations receiving standard treatments.

In conclusion, this study provides preliminary evidence that amygdala FC is associated with differential responses to AE in individuals with StD. By pointing to neural patterns linked to intervention responsiveness, our findings suggest the potential utility of neuroimaging biomarkers in informing personalized approaches to mental health care.
