Functional and quality of life outcomes after tibialis posterior transfer for foot drop from peroneal nerve injury

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo identify which functional domains show the greatest improvement in patients with foot drop (FD) due to common peroneal nerve (CPN) injury treated with tibialis posterior tendon transfer (TPTT).DesignAmbispective, longitudinal, nonrandomized study.SettingTertiary academic hospital.ParticipantsNineteen patients (15 men; mean age 29.5 ± 13.3 years) with FD secondary to CPN injury who underwent TPTT between 2018 and 2022.InterventionsTPTT performed via circumtibial route with standard postoperative rehabilitation protocol.Main outcome measuresFunctional outcomes assessed using the American Orthopaedic Foot and Ankle Society (AOFAS) score at baseline and at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months postoperatively. Repeated measures ANOVA and effect sizes (Cohen’s d) were calculated (α = 0.05, β = 0.01).ResultsMean AOFAS scores significantly increased from 46.58 ± 10.2 preoperatively to 87.1 ± 9.94 at 12 months postoperatively (p= 1.10 x 10⁻²², d = 3.739). The most notable functional gains are seen in surface walking ability, likely reflecting improved foot alignment and decreased claw toe formation. These findings support TPTT as an effective intervention for enhancing mobility and quality of life in this population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00590-026-04919-3.Keywords: Common peroneal nerve injury, Foot drop, Tendon transfer, Tibialis posterior, Functional outcomes

Introduction

Common peroneal nerve (CPN) is a lesion that consists of the paralysis of the dorsiflexors of the foot and ankle, the loss of the extension of the toes, and the loss of the eversion of the subtalar joint Krishnamurthy et al. [1, 2]. When the damage presents on this nerve, the principal clinical repercussion observed is foot drop (FD) by losing the ability to walk. The lesion of the CPN can be developed by cancer, iatrogenic lesions, or traumatic events of the lumbar plexus Head et al. [3], where traumatic lesions are the most common etiology.

Surgical management is the first option of treatment, and the options include tenodesis, arthrodesis, and tendon transfers by being the last one the most successful of them (87.4% of patients recover function) Vigasio et al. [4]. Tendon transfers are the most popular and successful techniques reported in the literature with a wide diversity: tendon-to-tendon transfers, tendon-to-bone fixation, and both. However, the tendon-to-tendon transfer is the most used in the literature Stevoska et al. [5]. Tibialis posterior tendon transfer (TPTT) is the technique widely accepted for FD e Özkan et al. [6].

Lower limb lesions are particularly implicated in potential harm to both physical and emotional health as well as the preclusion of work, social, and leisure activities Steinau et al. [7]. Most of the patients that suffer from an injury that affects their movement independence, as the FD does, are vulnerable to suffering depression and anxiety Awale et al. [8].

Furthermore, limitations in mobility may lead to secondary musculoskeletal complications due to altered gait mechanics, muscle atrophy, and joint instability, further exacerbating disability and reducing quality of life [8]. Despite the well-documented effectiveness of tendon transfers in restoring function, there remains a gap in understanding which specific functional components are most positively impacted by these procedures.

This study highlights two novel aspects compared to previous literature. First, It provides a specific breakdown of each component of the American Orthopedic Foot and Ankle Society Score (AOFAS) scale., which has not been previously reported, providing a more comprehensive understanding of which functional aspects are most affected and improved following surgery [9, 10]. Second, this study incorporates a qualitative analysis by including patients’ subjective experiences regarding their post-surgical impact on their quality of life. This qualitative dimension offers valuable insights that cannot be quantified, play a crucial role in assessing the overall success of the intervention. This approach represents a novel an unconventional contribution in medical studies that has gained popularity for defining the functional impact of therapies, as reported in previous studies in other fields [5, 10].

Materials and methods

An ambispective single-center quasi-experimental study with preoperative and postoperative comparison was conducted according to the PROCESS (Preferred Reporting of Case Series in Surgery) guidelines [11]. The study evaluated functional recovery in patients with foot drop (FD) secondary to common peroneal nerve (CPN) injury after tibialis posterior tendon transfer (TPTT). Measurements were taken preoperatively and at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months postoperatively. Patient recruitment occurred from April 1, 2019, to July 30, 2022, at the Peripheral Nerve Clinic, Health Medical Unit of High Speciality. Ethics approval was granted (R-2022-3401-029), and informed consent for surgery, data collection, and publication was obtained from all participants.The estimation of the sample size was based on the postoperative functional outcomes reported by Cho et al. who described a mean AOFAS score of 86.2 ± 11.4 in patients with foot drop secondary to common peroneal nerve injury treated with tibialis posterior tendon transfer. Considering statistical significance α = 0.05, β = 0.2 and effect size dz = 0.75, resulting in a minimum required sample size of 16 patients.

Eligible patients were 15–65 years old, with FD due to CPN injury. Exclusion criteria included clinical improvement with rehabilitation, refusal of surgery, non-traumatic etiology (e.g., tumor, infection, vascular), severe ankle arthrosis, or loss to follow-up. Data collection

Demographic data (age, gender), injury characteristics (pattern, side, interval injury-surgery), and occupation were recorded. Functional recovery was assessed using the AOFAS score [12], analyzing both the total and individual components (activity limitations, walking distance, walking surfaces, gait abnormality, sagittal motion, hindfoot motion, ankle stability) to determine the most responsive aspects to surgery.

Surgical technique

TPTT involved a medial malleolus incision to release the tibialis posterior tendon (TPT), followed by two additional incisions to reroute and divide the tendon. The inner TPT slit was attached to the tibialis anterior (TA) tendon and the external slit to the extensor hallucis longus (EHL) tendon and extensor digitorum longus (EDL) tendon using the Pulvertaft technique and nylon 2−0, following the technique by Steinau et al. [7, 13] (Fig. 2). A partial percutaneous Achilles tenotomy was performed prior to transfer to enhance postoperative dorsiflexion. All surgeries were performed by the same surgical team.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics (mean, SD, frequencies) were used for demographic and clinical data. Functional outcome changes were analyzed using repeated measures ANOVA after confirming homocedasticity through Levene’s test, and sphericity was assessed using Mauchly’s test. When the assumption of sphericity did not occur, Greenhouse–Geisser-corrected degrees of freedom were used. followed by appropriate post-hoc tests (DMS or C-Dunnett). Effect size (Cohen’s d) was calculated between baseline and final follow-up. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 25.0 (SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL), with significance set at p < 0.05.

Results

From 41 patients evaluated for elegibility, only 19 patients met the inclusion criteria for the analysis. According to gender, the percentage of males predominated (79%). The mean age of the population at the time of the injury was 29.52 ± 13.3 years. According to the lesion pattern, 73% presented an associated injury, among which involvement of the anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) predominates in 26% of cases, followed by involvement of the posterior cruciate ligament (PCL) in 16% of patients. The main mechanism of injury was motorcycle accidents (47.3%), followed by sports injuries (26.3%). The injuries were predominantly located on the left side (63%). The most frequent occupation was student (31%), and there was an average interval between injury and surgery of 16.26 ± 10.56 months. Outcomes

Clinical status was assessed at five different times: preoperatively and at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months after surgery. The evaluations were made using the AOFAS questionnaire [12]; for analysis purposes, both the global score and the broken down scores by individual items were considered. The scores of the questionnaire at the different evaluations are shown in Fig. 3. Complete AOFAS assessments were available for all 19 patients at each evaluation.

Scores obtained using the American Orthopedic Foot and Ankle Society Score (AOFAS) questionnaire at different times of clinical evaluation before and after surgery

In order to establish the effectiveness of the technique in functional restoration, the global AOFAS scores at the different evaluations were compared (Fig. 4A). The mean total AOFAS score increased progressively from 46.58 ± 10.21 points preoperatively to 50.32 ± 10.38 at 1 month, 68.16 ± 12.40 at 3 months, 82.16 ± 10.76 at 6 months, and 87.11 ± 9.94 at 12 months. Significant differences were observed between the preoperative status and postoperative follow-up evaluations through the repeated-measures analysis (Greenhouse–Geisser-corrected F[2.625, 47.246] = 142.140, p = 1.10 × 10 − 22, partial η² = 0.888).

B–I. Functional components of the AOFAS broken down by items and ranked according to the size of the effect (Cohen's d test) of the intervention on the functional component, noting that walking on surfaces was predominantly improved (p = 1.33 × 10⁻¹⁹, d = 10.668). The values shown in the graph correspond to means and standard deviations. Inferential statistical analysis was performed using a one-factor ANOVA (post-hoc analysis with a DMS (Duncan Multiple Range Test))

In Holm-adjusted paired post hoc comparisons, the mean total AOFAS score increased by 3.74 points at 1 month compared with the preoperative assessment (95% CI, 0.17–7.30; p = 0.0410; Cohen’s dz = 0.505), by 21.58 points at 3 months (95% CI, 15.94–27.22; p = 4.60 × 10 − 7; dz = 1.843), by 35.58 points at 6 months (95% CI, 30.61–40.54; p = 3.65 × 10 − 11; dz = 3.453), and by 40.53 points at 12 months (95% CI, 35.30–45.75; p = 1.28 × 10 − 11; dz = 3.739). Thus, a considerable effect size was observed between the preoperative evaluation and the 12-month evaluation (Cohen’s dz = 3.739), supporting a substantial improvement in functional status after surgery. The group trajectory showed a modest increase during the first postoperative month, followed by a marked improvement between 1 and 6 months and an additional increase through the 12-month assessment.

On the other hand, in order to define the clinical component that improved the most after the intervention, an analysis was performed by calculating the effect sizes of each of the AOFAS questionnaire items between the preoperative and 12-month evaluations (Fig. 4B–I). The components showing the largest standardized improvements after surgery were walking on different surfaces (Holm-adjusted p = 1.33 × 10 − 19, Cohen’s dz = 10.668), alignment (Holm-adjusted p = 3.50 × 10 − 15, dz = 6.009), and decreased limitation of daily activities (Holm-adjusted p = 1.29 × 10 − 12, dz = 4.277).

Walking-on-different-surfaces scores increased from 0.00 ± 0.00 preoperatively to 4.89 ± 0.46 at 12 months, representing a mean improvement of 4.89 points (95% CI, 4.67–5.12). Alignment increased from 0.00 ± 0.00 to 9.47 ± 1.58 points, with a mean improvement of 9.47 points (95% CI, 8.71–10.23), whereas activity-limitation scores increased from 0.42 ± 1.26 to 8.26 ± 1.82 points, representing a mean improvement of 7.84 points (95% CI, 6.96–8.73).

Significant improvements between the preoperative and 12-month evaluations were also observed in gait abnormality (Holm-adjusted p = 3.75 × 10 − 9, dz = 2.601), sagittal motion (p = 3.31 × 10 − 6, dz = 1.682), walking distance (p = 4.08 × 10 − 4, dz = 1.107), and hindfoot motion (p = 0.0162, dz = 0.755). Although pain showed a significant overall effect of time in the repeated-measures analysis (p = 0.0447), the difference between the preoperative and 12-month assessments was not statistically significant after adjustment for multiple comparisons (Holm-adjusted p = 0.2271, dz = 0.467). Ankle-stability scores remained unchanged throughout follow-up and were therefore not subjected to inferential post hoc testing.

The particularly large standardized effect sizes for walking on different surfaces and alignment should be interpreted cautiously because both items showed no preoperative variability and approached their maximum possible scores at 12 months, and in this follow-up period, no postoperative complications were observed.

Discussion

Regarding the surgical alternatives to manage the FD, there is a systematic review reported by Stevoska et al. (2021) that summarizes the clinical outcomes in three of the most common types of fixation techniques [5]. Among the surgical techniques, tendon-to-tendon transfer is the most commonly reported (67%), followed by tendon to bone (33%), and the combination of both in the last place (7%) [5]. Relative to tendon-to-tendon transfer technique, there is a wide diversity of tendons used to perform the surgery, where the most common given tendon to align the FD is the TPT, which is divided in two tails where the insertion sites depend on the preference of the surgeon.

Based on the surgical intervention, there is a great variety of techniques to perform the tendon-to-tendon transfer, which has been described mainly for traumatic etiologies that are the most common cause of FD in the population Krishnamurthy et al. [1, 3, 5–7, 13, 14]. The author, Movahedi-Yeganeh [2], used the same tendons that we used in this study, demonstrating elimination of claw toes as well as improvement in balance on the ankle range of motion Movahedi [2]. It is also important to mention that in other etiologies different than traumatic ones such as those caused by infectious, vascular and neurological lesions Shah et al. [15–17].

Functional outcomes

Regarding functional results and quality of life, there is a lack of evidence. Cho et al. [17] reported a retrospective observational study considering the daily activities and gait ability, showing satisfactory results after three years of follow-up Cho et al. [18, 21]. On the other hand, Steinau et al. (2011) reported the changes in different fields of quality life after the tendon transfer (e.g., social life, fitness for work, recreational activity, among others) observing that the items that improved the most after surgery were the symptoms related to quality of life, recreation activities, and the ability to walk at home Steinau et al. [7, 19]. Unfortunately, these studies did not use standardized tools and the evaluations were performed retrospectively, attributing a recall bias.

In our study we observed that walking on surfaces was the functional component that improved the most (p =dz = 10.668) after 12 months of follow-up, because on our surgical technique one of the slits of TPT was given to EHL and EDL tendons. This configuration may contribute to improved toe alignment and gait mechanics. However, claw toe deformity was not objectively evaluated in the present study, and therefore this proposed mechanism should be considered a plausible hypothesis rather than a robust and demonstrated finding. A phenomenon explained by the extensor compensation of EHL and EDL tendon´s muscles on the loss of the flexor component secondary to the FD, and its repercussion on the biomechanics of gait Kilic et al. [20]. Although the split attachment to the TA, EHL, and EDL has been proposed to improve the biomechanical balance of the foot and potentially reduce the tendency toward digital flexion deformities, our study did not include objective clinical or radiographic assessment of claw toe deformity.

Steinau et al. (2011) performs a similar surgical technique omitting the fixation of the tibial posterior slit to EHL and EDL tendons as a first instance, they considered the need to perform an extra maneuver in which fixes the extensor pollicis and EDL tendon to the retinaculum to prevent the claw toes and plantar flexion of the foot Steinau [7]. However, we consider that it is possible to omit this added maneuver if the fixation of EHL and EDL tendons is carried out in the first instance.

It is interesting to observe that despite the fact that most of the articles reported on this technique focus on the anatomical condition of the foot (alignment) Vigasio et al. [4, 6, 14]. We observed that the magnitude of improvement was greater in walking on surfaces, a finding that was made by the use of AOFAS as a measurement instrument and that could be explained by the compensation of the technique on the flexor components of the toes Movahedi-Yeganeh [2, 7, 13]. On the other hand, it is important to mention that pain is not among the main improvements (p = 0.044, dz = 0.467), because the patients presented no or moderate pain prior to the intervention. However, a significant decrease in pain was observed from the first month to 12-month follow-up (Fig. 4), a phenomenon probably explained by the decrease in pain associated with gait disturbances.

Measure instrument

Kitaoka et al. [12] described the rating systems developed by de AOFAS with the propose of standardized the method of reporting clinical status of the ankle and foot. The result was four grading scales, each with 100 possible points. Cho et al. [17] used the AOFAS score, FAOS and FAAM to grade the improvement of their patients, AOFAS improve from 65.1 to 86.2 (21% of the AOFAS scale) Cho et al. [18]. Unfortunately, the authors who report their results using various measurement scales do not report which item is the one that mainly improves in their studies, since they only describe the improvement of their patients as a global score.

It is known that the Stanmore system is used more frequently in this type of surgeries but compared to the AOFAS there is a few differences between the scales that may be important to report on the clinical changes after the surgery Kiliç et al. [20]. The main improvement in our study measured with AOFAS score was the ability of walking on surfaces (p = 1.33 × 10⁻¹⁹, dz = 10.668) after 12 months of the surgery and rehabilitation which cannot be measured if we only used the Stanmore because there is no similar item on this.

Analyzing the phrases shown in Table 2, we observe that phrases related to the use of footwear (patient number 3,6,15), loss of autonomy (patient number 4,5,18) and labor repercussion (patient number 6,8,10) are frequently exposed, above other aspects such as limitation of activities (patient number 13,19), social repercussion (patient number 2), and emotional condition (patient number 9). The most standardized clinical tools to evaluate these patients, such as the AOFAS and the STANMORE, consider some of these aspects mentioned by the patients in Table 2, such as limitation of activities, loss of autonomy or use of footwear Kitaoka et al. [12, 13, 20, 22]. However, components such as occupational, emotional, or social repercussions are omitted. Therefore, we believe that integrating these qualitative aspects into the evaluation could lead to the development of a more comprehensive assessment tool. This tool would not only address the limitations in current clinical scales, but also improve our understanding of the broader impacts on the patient’s quality of life [5, 21, 23].

Qualitative studies in medicine are essential for understanding aspects of the health process that are not captured by quantitative results. Analyzing patients’ experiences and statements after surgery helps identify perceptions of recovery, quality of life, and daily challenges that clinical scales may overlook. This approach enhances the doctor-patient relationship, identifies unmeasured outcomes, evaluates surgical success from the patient’s perspective, and generates new research hypotheses. Thus, it contributes to more humanized care focused on the patient’s overall well-being [11, 24]. The qualitative analysis of patient statements following surgery reveals that the intervention not only improved motor function but also significantly enhanced independence, quality of life, and emotional well-being. Patients highlighted the recovery of basic mobility, such as walking unaided and using regular footwear, which allowed them to regain autonomy and reduce dependence on others. The surgery also facilitated a return to work-related activities and social participation, with some expressing hope of resuming professional roles. Emotionally, the procedure restored a sense of control and hope, alleviating feelings of despair and frustration. However, some patients acknowledged ongoing challenges and residual limitations, underscoring the complexity of the recovery process. Overall, these insights highlight the importance of considering both objective functional outcomes and subjective patient experiences to comprehensively evaluate the success of surgical interventions.

Qualitative analyses can pave the way for the development of new functional assessment scales. In this case, we consider it important to incorporate the key aspects identified in our study (recovery of basic mobility, independence and autonomy, return to work and social participation, emotional well-being, and adaptation to residual limitations), to design a tool that effectively evaluates these components, which could be based on the items related to functional repercussion reported by Steinau et al. [7, 10], in search of a standardized tool that considers these aspects related to the quality of life of a person.

Clinical considerations

Based on our experience, we consider that the definitive removal of immobilization with a suropodalic splint encompassing the phalanges should be carried out at the sixth post-surgical week, at which time gait support begins with crutches. After the immobilization removal, the plantigrade position stability is reduced, an expected outcome due to factors such as: decrease in muscle strength, and the antagonism of the Achilles tendon over dorsiflexion, for which we highly recommend performing a partial Achilles tendon tenotomy to achieve a plantigrade position. On the other hand, it is essential to warn the patient about the expectations of the procedure, especially about the fact that it will require a period of 3–4 months for muscle strengthening through physical therapy, before the patient starts walking normally at 6–7 months after surgery (Fig. 4), when the patient will be able to perform a dorsiflexion between 5 and 15°.

Limitations

Although the AOFAS Ankle-Hindfoot Score has been widely used for decades and remains one of the most frequently reported instruments for evaluating functional outcomes after foot and ankle surgery, its limitations should be acknowledged. The AOFAS includes physician-derived components, has recognized ceiling effects, and is not a purely patient-reported outcome measure (PROM). Nevertheless, the AOFAS score was selected because it has been the standardized functional assessment routinely used in our institution, allowing consistent longitudinal evaluation of patients and facilitating comparison with previous studies on tibialis posterior tendon transfer. Although more recent and validated PROMs, such as the Foot and Ankle Ability Measure (FAAM), the Foot and Ankle Outcome Score (FAOS), and PROMIS Physical Function, may provide a more comprehensive and integrative assessment of patient-perceived function and quality of life, these instruments were not routinely implemented in our clinical practice during the study period. Future studies should incorporate these PROMs to complement clinician-based assessments and provide a more patient-centered evaluation of surgical outcomes.

Conclusion

TPTT is an effective technique for functional restoration in patients with FD secondary to a CPN injury. It was shown that the main clinical benefit was observed in walking on surfaces, a phenomenon which may be related to improved balance of the extensor mechanism and more favorable gait biomechanics. Because claw toe deformity was not objectively assessed, this explanation should be interpreted as a hypothesis requiring further investigation, and the gait improvement through the compensation of the tendons selected for the surgery (EHL and EDL tendons), on the loss of the flexor component secondary to the CPN injury. In addition, it is necessary to develop tools that allow the evaluation of other commonly omitted functional components such as occupational, emotional or social repercussions.
