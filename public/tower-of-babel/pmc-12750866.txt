Alternative splicing patterns in genetic subtypes of diffuse large B-cell lymphoma
Authors: Sanyeowool An, Junshik Hong, Dong-Yeop Shin, Ja Min Byun, Youngil Koh, Sung-Soo Yoon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) is a heterogeneous disease with distinct genetic subtypes, each exhibiting unique molecular characteristics. Alternative splicing (AS) plays a critical role in hematologic malignancies, but its role in DLBCL progression remains less understood. Recent studies have focused extensively on genetic variants, but systematic exploration of AS in DLBCL is limited.MethodsWe analyzed whole-exome and RNA sequencing data from 620 DLBCL patient samples obtained from the European Genome-phenome Archive. RNA-seq data were aligned using STAR and quantified with RSEM to calculate TPM-normalized expression values. Subtype classification was performed using the LymphGen framework, integrating somatic variants identified with GATK Mutect2, annotated with Annovar, and copy number variations, as well as BCL2 and BCL6 fusion events detected using Arriba and validated with FISH. Alternative splicing events were identified with rMATS, and isoform-level differences were analyzed using DRIMSeq. Splicing-related pathway activity was assessed using MSigDB gene sets, and splicing factor expression was evaluated across subtypes.ResultsThe BN2 and MCD subtypes demonstrated higher activity in AS-related pathways, including spliceosome assembly and mRNA splice site recognition, accompanied by a significant increase in non-canonical isoforms. In the BN2 subtype, differential splicing was observed, especially in genes like ACTG1, RPLP0, CD74, and ACTB. These genes showed significant changes in their isoforms, suggesting that alternative splicing may play a role in driving the aggressive behavior associated with this subtype. Notably, these genes include essential housekeeping components, suggesting that targeting their splicing variants could disrupt critical cellular processes in cancer cells while sparing normal tissues. In addition, splicing factor analysis identified RBM39 as significantly downregulated in MCD, suggesting its role in aberrant splicing regulation. RBM39, an RNA-binding protein involved in the regulation of splicing, may contribute to DLBCL pathogenesis through its reduced expression, potentially leading to abnormal splicing patterns that promote malignancy in the MCD subtype.ConclusionsIntegrated analysis using rMATS and DRIMSeq consistently identified subtype-specific AS patterns, consolidating our findings. Future research should focus on validating the functional impact of these splicing alterations and assessing their potential as biomarkers for more tailored therapeutic approaches in DLBCL.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-14967-3.Keywords: Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, Alternative splicing, Genetic subtypes, Isoforms

Background

Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) is the most prevalent subtype of B-cell lymphomas, comprising approximately 30% of all non-Hodgkin lymphoma cases [1]. This aggressive malignancy primarily affects B-lymphocytes, making rapid and effective therapeutic intervention crucial due to its fast growth rate. Despite initial treatment success, approximately 40% of DLBCL patients experience refractory or relapsed (R/R) disease, and even with emergence of recent therapeutics including CAR-T and bispecifics, large number of R/R DLBCL patients eventually die of the disease [1]. Therefore, there still exists an urgent need for the development of novel therapeutic strategies [2]. In this context, understanding the pathobiology of DLBCL is essential for developing effective therapeutic strategies to address treatment resistant and relapsed disease.

Classification of DLBCL based on the cell of origin (COO) using RNA expression profile into Activated B-cell-like (ABC) and Germinal Center B-cell-like (GCB) has been utilized for more than 20 years [3]. With advances in next-generation sequencing (NGS) have enabled DNA-based subtyping through the LymphGen classification, which integrates variants, gene fusions, and copy number variations (CNVs) to offer a more detailed view of DLBCL heterogeneity than the COO based model. The LymphGen classification divides DLBCL into at least five subtypes based on distinct genetic alterations, each linked to specific molecular pathways. For example, the MCD subtype is characterized by MYD88 and CD79B variants, promoting NF-κB pathway activation and B-cell receptor signaling. On the other hand, BN2 harbors BCL6 and NOTCH2 variants, influencing B-cell development and differentiation [4].

Alternative splicing (AS) is a crucial regulatory mechanism in cancer, enabling the generation of protein diversity that drives tumor progression, immune evasion, and therapy resistance [5]. In DLBCL, distinct AS in specific genes has been shown to influence survival outcomes significantly [6]. It is known that AS changes in pathways such as NOTCH signaling, the circadian rhythm, and the ABC transport pathway, which play crucial roles in B cell maturation and immune regulation (6). These changes may drive tumor aggressiveness, modulate immune responses, and promote drug resistance [7].

As such, AS might be an important mechanism in DLBCL progression. In addition, AS could offer opportunities for targeted therapeutic strategies in DLBCL. Therapeutics targeting AS have shown promise in both hematologic malignancies and solid tumors. For example, H3B-8800, a splicing modulator, disrupts spliceosome activity and has been effective in promoting cell death in cohesin-mutant myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS) and acute myeloid leukemia (AML) [8]. Splice-switching oligonucleotides (SSOs) have also been utilized to redirect splicing events, altering gene expression and enhancing immune responses, making them a potential strategy for overcoming immune-resistant cancers [9]. While the role of AS in DLBCL pathogenesis is increasingly recognized, comprehensive investigations into the variation of AS patterns across genetic subtypes are still lacking.

Given AS’s significant role in cancer pathology and its therapeutic potential, it is essential to investigate its impact on DLBCL subtypes. By integrating AS profiling into the classification of DLBCL, we can better capture the disease's complexity and identify novel therapeutic targets. To investigate subtype-specific AS patterns and identify genes influenced by AS in DLBCL pathogenesis, we utilized publicly available whole-exome sequencing (WES) and RNA-seq data. Through this approach, we aim to identify subtype-specific AS patterns and understand their biological impact, with the ultimate goal of improving diagnostic and therapeutic strategies for DLBCL.

Methods

Data acquisition

Whole-exome sequencing (WES) and bulk RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) data for 775 DLBCL patient samples were obtained from the European Genome-phenome Archive (EGA) under accession number [EGAD00001003600]. Originally published by Reddy et al. [10], this dataset provided paired genomic and transcriptomic data for comprehensive molecular characterization of DLBCL. We selected 620 samples that met the RNA-seq quality criteria, defined as having over 12,000 expressed genes and retaining feature-filtered variants for further analysis.

Variant calling for lymphGen classification

To identify somatic variants, we employed GATK v4.2.0.0 with the Mutect2 algorithm, analyzing WES BAM files sourced from the dataset by Reddy et al. [10], followed by functional annotation using Annovar [11] and VEP [12]. To ensure subtype-specific variant identification, we applied stringent filtering criteria, including gnomAD MAF < 0.1% to exclude non-pathogenic germline variants, and depth thresholds (depth ≥ 10 and alt allele depth ≥ 3) to retain high-confidence variants. To minimize potential artifacts, variants with a VAF between 0.4 and 0.6 were filtered out, and those displaying strand bias greater than 8:2 were excluded from further analysis. Inclusion criteria were Limited to genes recurrently mutated in at least 5% of samples, except MYD88, NOTCH1, NOTCH2, CD79B, and EZH2, given their established roles in DLBCL biology and significance in the LymphGen framework. Somatic variants in splicing factor genes were annotated and classified using the COSMIC Cancer Gene Census (CGC) tier system, following recommendations from the AMP/ASCO/CAP guidelines [13]. Each variant was assigned to a tier (Tier 1, 2) reflecting its relevance in cancer, as defined in COSMIC. Detailed tier assignments and annotations are provided in Supplementary Table 1.CNV data from the original study were processed by classifying log-transformed ratios, where values of log2(1/2) or lower were categorized as heterozygous loss and values of log2(3/2) or higher were categorized as gain, to ensure consistency with CNV classification criteria. All filtered variants and CNV data were then integrated into the LymphGen classification framework (https://llmpp.nih.gov/lymphgen/lymphgendataportal.php?version=2.0) for subtyping, following Wright et al.'s guidelines [4]. The variant status of feature genes in each DLBCL subtype was visualized using the oncoplot function from the maftools R package (v2.18.0) within the R software environment (v4.3.3).

Fusion analysis of BCL2 and BCL6 translocations

To analyze the translocation status of BCL2 and BCL6, we performed a comprehensive fusion analysis on the RNA-Seq data from all 620 patient samples, supplementing the available FISH data. Fusion detection was carried out using the Arriba (v2.2.1) tool, which identifies fusion transcripts by detecting chimeric reads and discordant read pairs from RNA-Seq data [14]. The results obtained from Arriba were then compared with the FISH findings in the subset of 76 samples with FISH data. In cases where discrepancies between the two methods were observed—such as when Arriba detected a fusion that was not captured by FISH or vice versa—we adopted a conservative approach. If either method suggested the presence of a translocation event, we classified it as a positive fusion case. A Venn diagram was generated using VennDiagram (v1.7.3) to visualize the number of samples with BCL2 and BCL6 fusions, as well as the overlap of samples harboring both fusion events.

RNA-Seq alignment and quantification

RNA-Seq data for this study was sourced from the Reddy et al. publication [10], which provided paired RNA-Seq data for 775 out of 1,001 DLBCL samples While the study by Reddy et al. utilized TopHat for alignment and Cufflinks for quantification, we converted the BAM files to FASTQ format using samtools (v1.14) and realigned the reads with STAR (v2.7.7a) [15]. For quantification at both gene and isoform levels, we utilized RSEM (v1.3.3) to calculate transcripts per million (TPM) normalized expression values directly from the BAM files generated by STAR.

Analysis of splicing-related pathways with MSigDB and GOSemSim

Gene signature analysis was conducted using MSigDB C5 ontology gene sets, focusing on splicing-related pathways. Only gene sets with a set size of ≥ 5 were included, resulting in 36 selected pathways. Expression values were normalized as log2(TPM + 0.01), and for each sample, the mean expression value of genes within each gene set was calculated. The resulting mean values were z-scaled on a sample-wise basis, and the averaged z-scores for each DLBCL subtype were visualized using bar plots to represent the relative expression of AS-related pathways. Semantic similarities between pathways were analyzed using GOSemSim to identify functional overlaps between AS-related pathways [16]. To evaluate the association between splicing-related pathway activation and clinical outcomes, we quantified the activation level of AS-related pathways using gene set expression. The mean expression value of genes within all splicing-related gene sets was calculated. The samples were then divided into three groups based on activation levels: High (top 25%), Medium (middle 50%), and Low (bottom 25%). Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated for overall survival using the R package survival, and ggsurvplot from the survminer package was used for visualization. Statistical significance was assessed using the log-rank test.

Alternative splicing and differential transcript usage analysis

AS events were quantified using the rMATS (replicate Multivariate Analysis of Transcript Splicing) tool (v4.3), which identifies differentially spliced events across subtypes by comparing each group against a combination of the other four subtypes [17]. In this analysis, the five groups—MCD, BN2, EZB, ST2, and A53—were each assessed separately against a pooled set of the remaining four subtypes, ensuring a comprehensive evaluation of subtype-specific splicing events. The types of AS events detected include Skipped Exon (SE), Alternative 5' Splice Site (A5SS), Alternative 3' Splice Site (A3SS), Mutually Exclusive Exons (MXE), and Retained Intron (RI). The results were filtered based on a p-value threshold of < 0.05. The number of AS events per subtype and event type was visualized using bar plots, and the overall distribution of PSI values was represented through PCA plots, both generated with ggplot2 (v3.5.1). To compare the proportions of different isoforms within each gene to identify significant differences in isoform usage between BN2 and Non-BN2 subtypes, we performed DTU analysis using the DRIMSeq R package (v1.32.0) [18]. Genes with significant isoform-level changes (p < 0.05) identified by DRIMSeq that also exhibited significant alternative splicing (p < 0.05) in rMATS were visualized using the plotProportions function of DRIMSeq.

Splicing factor expression analysis

Splicing factor (SF) expression was quantified using log2 (TPM + 0.01) transformed values for SFs identified from a large-scale cancer study [19]. Among the 404 SFs reported in this study, 395 remained after filtering out those with minimal expression in our dataset. To assess overall differences in SF expression across DLBCL subtypes, the Kruskal–Wallis test was first applied as a non-parametric method. Following this, Dunn’s post-hoc test was conducted to evaluate pairwise differences in SF expression levels between subtypes, with adjusted p-values (p.adj < 0.05) used to identify significant comparisons. The box plot for specific gene expression across DLBCL subtypes was generated using ggplot2 (v3.5.1). Additionally, hnRNP and SR protein expression patterns were visualized through heatmaps created using the pheatmap package (v1.0.12). To validate our findings in an independent dataset, we analyzed RBM39 expression and alternative splicing patterns using the TCGA-DLBC (N = 48) cohort (https://tcga-data.nci.nih.gov/tcga/). We referenced the classification provided in a prior study by Li et al. for LymphGen subtype assignments [20].

Results

LymphGen-based classification and splicing factor variant analysis in DLBCL

The oncoplot illustrating variants by subtype across 620 DLBCL cases is presented in Fig. 1A. Genetic analysis revealed KMT2D as the most frequently mutated gene (53%), followed by subtype-specific alterations including MYD88 L265P in MCD (36%), EZH2 in EZB (35%), and TET2 in ST2 (51%), while all A53 samples harbored TP53 variants. The N1 subtype was excluded from further classification as no samples met the criteria for assignment. Among the 620 patient samples analyzed, 76 had FISH data for BCL2 and BCL6. Fusion analysis using Arriba and FISH results identified BCL2 translocations in 61 samples and BCL6 translocations in 63 samples (Fig. 1B, Supplementary Table 1). LymphGen analysis successfully classified genetic subtypes in 53.1% of cases (329/620), with predominant subtypes being ST2 and EZB, while 46.9% remained unclassified, consistent with previously reported ranges (30–50%) (Fig. 1C). Fifty samples (8.1%) exhibited overlapping genetic profiles, classifying them as Genetically Composite (Fig. 1C). Among the SFs evaluated, variants were identified in ZRSR2 and SF1. ZRSR2 variants were present in 14 of the 279 DLBCL samples classified into subtypes, while SF1 variants were detected in 28 samples (Table 1).

Alternative splicing mechanisms and spliceosome-related pathways are relatively activated in BN2 and MCD subtypes

To assess the differential activation of AS and spliceosome-related pathways among DLBCL genetic subtypes classified by the LymphGen algorithm, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of pathway activity across the subtypes (Fig. 2, Supplementary Table 2). BN2 and MCD showed the highest activity in key AS pathways, such as “mRNA 5'-splice site recognition” and “spliceosomal tri-snRNP complex assembly,” highlighting their subtype-specific splicing profiles. In contrast, A53 exhibited minimal pathway activation. To investigate whether the observed consistency in activation across these splicing-related pathways could be explained by inherent relationships between the pathways themselves (e.g., hierarchical or functional overlap), we conducted a pathway similarity analysis using the GOSemSim algorithm. This analysis evaluates the degree of similarity between pathways based on the gene ontology terms associated with their components. The results, visualized in Supplementary Fig. 1, revealed that splicing-related pathways do not uniformly exhibit high similarity. Instead, notable variations in similarity were observed between different pathways, indicating that the pathways analyzed are not simply redundant or overlapping in their function. Interestingly, even among pathways with distinct gene compositions and lower similarity scores, the trend of heightened activation in the BN2 and MCD subtypes persisted. This suggests that the increased activity in these subtypes is not solely due to overlapping pathway components but may instead reflect subtype-specific regulation of distinct splicing mechanisms.

We also assessed genetically composite cases, which accounted for 8.1% (50/620) of samples. These cases exhibited AS activation patterns similar to BN2 and MCD subtypes (Fig. 2). The most prevalent composite subtypes were EZB/ST2 (68%), followed by BN2/ST2 (10%) and BN2/EZB (8%) (Supplementary Table 1). EZB and ST2 subtypes harbored the highest frequency of splicing factor variants (Table 1), suggesting that genetic alterations in splicing regulators could play a role in AS pathway activation.

To assess the clinical impact of AS-related pathways, we stratified samples into three groups based on pathway activation levels: High (top 25%), Medium (middle 50%), and Low (bottom 25%). Kaplan–Meier survival analysis revealed a trend where samples with high AS pathway activation exhibited worse overall survival compared to those with low activation levels (Supplementary Fig. 2). Although the difference did not reach statistical significance (log-rank p = 0.16), the survival curve for the high activation group was consistently positioned below that of the low activation group. These findings indicate a potential link between increased AS pathway activity and poor prognosis, highlighting the need for further investigation in larger cohorts.

Distinct alternative splicing events across the genetic subtypes

Using the rMATS tool, we examined AS patterns across DLBCL subtypes (Fig. 3A, Supplementary Table 3). A total of five AS types were identified, with MXE events being the most frequent, followed by SE, RI, A3SS, and A5SS. Notably, only a single A5SS event was detected across all subtypes, emphasizing its uncommon occurrence.

BN2 and MCD, in particular, showed high numbers of subtype-specific AS events. MXE events were particularly prominent in BN2, while RI events were present across both BN2 and MCD. ST2 displayed moderate splicing activity, with an even distribution across event types, while A53 exhibited limited AS involvement. EZB showed minimal AS activity, indicating limited splicing involvement. To explore the differential AS patterns between subtypes that showed relatively high AS-related pathway activation, we performed a PCA of Percent Spliced In (PSI) values comparing MCD, BN2, and Other subtype. As shown in the PCA plot (Fig. 3B), the BN2 samples are clearly separated from Other subtype, which are thought to have fewer unique AS events. In contrast, MCD samples appear more overlapped with Other subtype, indicating less divergence in overall AS patterns. To validate the robustness of these patterns, we performed same analysis in the TCGA-DLBC cohort (N = 48). Despite the smaller sample size, BN2 cases again formed a distinct cluster, separated from MCD and other subtypes (Supplementary Fig. 4 A). This suggests that the BN2-specific splicing landscape is reproducible across independent cohorts, even when individual AS events vary.

Specific AS events were identified in the BN2 and MCD subtypes, with most involving non-canonical isoforms (Table 2). In BN2, ACTG1, which encodes gamma-actin, a key component of the cytoskeleton, showed A5SS and RI events. RPLP0, encoding a ribosomal protein critical for maintaining ribosome stability, exhibited RI events. Additionally, RACK1, a versatile scaffolding protein that mediates various cellular signaling pathways, displayed MXE and SE events. ACTB, which encodes beta-actin, another essential cytoskeletal protein, exhibited RI events shared between BN2 and MCD subtypes. Similarly, CD74, a protein central to antigen presentation in immune cells like macrophages, dendritic cells, and B cells, was commonly differentially spliced in BN2 and MCD subtypes and also exhibited SE events.

Differential transcript usage reveals subtype-specific isoform patterns in BN2

Given the distinct AS patterns and elevated number of AS events and pathway activations in BN2, we conducted a Differential Transcript Usage (DTU) analysis using DRIMSeq to explore how isoform diversity shapes BN2's unique molecular characteristics. By comparing BN2 with Non-BN2 subtypes, we identified genes with significant DTU among those previously showing BN2-specific AS events (p-value < 0.05), with detailed results presented in Supplementary Table 4. The DTU plot (Fig. 4) presents only those genes where significant DTU was observed between BN2 and Non-BN2 subtypes, highlighting genes with isoforms that showed relatively higher expression compared to other isoforms within the same gene, thus effectively illustrating group-specific differences. DRIMSeq revealed that RPLP0, ACTB, CD74, and ACTG1 displayed significant DTU specifically in the BN2 subtype compared to Non-BN2 subtypes (p-value < 0.05), consistent with their BN2-specific AS patterns identified by rMATS.

Analysis of splicing factor expression and subtype-specific variations

To investigate whether the differential activation of splicing-related pathways across DLBCL subtypes and the observed PSI profile differences could be associated with variations in the expression of specific SFs, we utilized a List of 404 SFs identified from study by Seiler et al. (2018) [19]. We examined the expression patterns of two critical families of SFs: heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoproteins (hnRNPs) and serine/arginine-rich (SR) proteins, both of which play central roles in the regulation of AS. Out of the 404 SFs analyzed, 31 hnRNPs and 11 SR proteins were expressed across subtypes; however, no significant differences were observed, indicating that hnRNP and SR protein expression levels are relatively consistent among DLBCL subtypes (Supplementary Fig. 3, Supplementary Table 5). Given the potential influence of SFs on global splicing patterns, we conducted a gene-by-gene analysis to identify any SFs showing significant differential expression between DLBCL subtypes. Through this analysis, we identified RBM39, a key SF and transcriptional coactivator involved in regulating AS and mRNA stability, as significantly underexpressed in the MCD group compared to other subtypes (Fig. 5). To further validate our findings, we analyzed an independent validation cohort from the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) (TCGA-DLBC cohort). While RBM39 expression remained lower in MCD compared to other subtypes, the difference was not statistically significant. However, a consistently lower trend was observed in both cohorts, reinforcing the potential subtype-specific role of RBM39. (Supplementary Fig. 4B).

Discussion

In this study, we examined the AS landscape across 620 DLBCL patient samples, focusing on the subtype-specific patterns and their biological relevance. By integrating genomic, transcriptomic, and fusion data, distinct AS profiles were identified among LymphGen-classified genetic subtypes. Notably, the BN2 and MCD subtypes exhibited significant splicing-related alterations. BN2, marked by BCL6 translocations and NOTCH2 variants, showed a substantial increase in non-canonical isoforms and elevated activity in spliceosome-associated pathways, emphasizing the critical role of splicing dysregulation in its disease mechanism. Meanwhile, MCD exhibited downregulation of RBM39, revealing unique splicing regulatory mechanisms. Our findings provide deeper insights into the molecular heterogeneity of DLBCL and suggest potential therapeutic opportunities through the targeting of aberrant splicing events specific for molecular subtypes. Despite relying on a single dataset, the integration of multiple analytical approaches, including rMATS, DRIMSeq, provided consistent and reproducible results. The convergence of these methodologies strengthens the validity of our conclusions regarding the subtype-specific splicing alterations in DLBCL. To enhance the accuracy of LymphGen classification, we employed a combined approach using FISH and Arriba analyses. Arriba’s transcript-level sensitivity complemented FISH’s ability to confirm structural rearrangements, minimizing false negatives. This integrated strategy provided a robust framework for detecting BCL2 and BCL6 fusions and investigating subtype-specific splicing mechanism.

We examined variants in SFs with well-established implications in hematologic malignancies. Among the SFs evaluated, variants were identified in ZRSR2 and SF1. SF variants, such as those in ZRSR2 and SF1, are critical in hematologic malignancies but appear less frequent in DLBCL compared to other malignancies like acute myeloid leukemia (AML) and myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS), where SF variants can occur in up to 50% of MDS cases and 10% of AML cases [21]. This suggests that SF variants may play a more context-specific role in DLBCL, potentially influenced by other unique genetic alterations. To explore their potential functional impact, we compared alternative splicing patterns between patients with and without ZRSR2 or SF1 variants. However, no statistically significant differences in splicing were observed. Notably, all of ZRSR2 and SF1 variants in our cohort were not assigned any tier in the COSMIC-CGC classification, indicating that they currently lack sufficient evidence for clinical or pathogenic significance. This suggests that these variants may not result in functional impairment of splicing. In addition, functional redundancy and compensation by other splicing regulators or pathways might mitigate any detectable effects on global splicing profiles. Finally, the relatively small number of mutated cases further limits the statistical power to detect subtype-specific splicing alterations.

The genetically composite group, which exhibited activation patterns in AS-related pathways similar to BN2 and MCD, contained a significant proportion of EZB/ST2 cases (68%). Given that EZB and ST2 harbored the highest frequency of splicing factor variants, it is plausible that these variants contribute to the observed AS pathway activation. However, due to the genetic heterogeneity within these composite subtypes, establishing a clear mechanistic link remains challenging. Future research should focus on defining how specific splicing factor variants influence AS regulation in composite subtypes and their potential clinical implications.

The most notable finding in our study is the marked enrichment of AS-related pathways, such as "mRNA 5'-splice site recognition," in the BN2 and MCD subtypes, suggesting a higher splicing burden or altered regulation. Pathway similarity analysis using GOSemSim indicated that these patterns were driven by subtype-specific mechanisms rather than shared pathway overlap. Although SF expression levels did not significantly differ between subtypes, the observed pathway activation may be attributed to post-transcriptional modifications, such as phosphorylation or ubiquitination, which can modulate SF activity and enhance splicing efficiency. For instance, previous research has shown that phosphorylation of serine/arginine-rich (SR) proteins enhances their binding affinity to the spliceosome, thereby facilitating splice site recognition and influencing exon inclusion [22].

Furthermore, we investigated the association between AS pathway activation and clinical outcomes, finding that samples with higher AS pathway activity demonstrated a trend toward worse survival outcomes. Although the survival difference did not reach statistical significance, the consistent downward shift in survival probability for the high activation group suggests a potential role of AS pathway dysregulation in disease severity. This underscores the need for future studies with larger, longitudinally followed cohorts to validate the prognostic impact of splicing activity using more robust survival models. Furthermore, due to the absence of detailed clinical information, including treatment regimens and response outcomes, we were unable to determine whether AS activity correlates with therapeutic responsiveness to specific chemotherapeutic or targeted agents. Nevertheless, given the mechanistic role of AS in modulating gene expression, immune escape, and drug resistance, it is theoretically plausible that subtype-specific AS alterations may influence responses to certain therapies. In particular, integrating splicing metrics with time-to-event data and multivariate clinical predictors could help establish AS activation as an independent prognostic biomarker. Future studies integrating splicing profiles with comprehensive clinical data, including treatment history and response, will be necessary to clarify the predictive utility of AS in guiding DLBCL therapy.

Among these subtypes, the PCA of PSI values across all AS events revealed that BN2 samples form a distinctly separated cluster from the other subtype. Splicing dysregulation in BN2 may compound the effects of disrupted transcriptional programs governed by BCL6 [23], intensifying molecular dysfunction. As a key transcriptional repressor, BCL6 is critical for regulating pathways involved in lymphocyte development and immune function [24]. Variants or translocations affecting BCL6 in BN2 may influence splicing by altering splicing machinery expression or directly targeting mRNAs regulated by BCL6 or other pathways. This interplay between transcriptional dysregulation and splicing abnormalities adds complexity to BN2 pathogenesis, with aberrant splicing significantly contributing to its disrupted cellular architecture.

The relative upregulation of splicing-related pathways in the BN2 subtype is accompanied by a higher frequency of AS events, many of which involve non-canonical isoforms. Notably, RPLP0 and ACTB, widely used as normalization controls due to their presumed stable expression, exhibited significant splicing changes in BN2. RPLP0 showed RI events, potentially compromising ribosomal stability and protein synthesis, while ACTB exhibited isoform-level shifts, indicating possible disruptions in actin dynamics and cellular structure. Similarly, ACTG1, critical for cytoskeletal remodeling, showed splicing changes, including A5SS and RI events, further highlighting alterations in cytoskeletal integrity and cellular motility specific to BN2. These findings emphasize the vulnerability of housekeeping genes to disease-specific splicing changes, underscoring their potential as biomarkers or therapeutic targets in BN2-associated malignancies. Additionally, CD74, a key player in immune regulation, showed distinct splicing changes in BN2, reinforcing its role as a candidate biomarker in DLBCL. To support the robustness of our findings, we performed external validation using the TCGA-DLBC dataset (N = 48). Although this analysis reproduced subtype-specific trends, the limited sample size restricts definitive conclusions. Future research incorporating larger, independent cohorts with comprehensive molecular and clinical annotation will be crucial to confirm the functional relevance of the identified splicing alterations. While our isoform-level validation using DRIMSeq in the TCGA cohort did not consistently reproduce the BN2-specific differential transcript usage, PSI-based PCA confirmed that BN2 cases remain molecularly distinct in terms of global splicing patterns. This observation reinforces the idea that PSI-based clustering provides a broader view of AS-driven transcriptomic architecture, which may be more resilient to technical and biological variability than individual exon- or isoform-level events. As such, integrating both event-level and global AS metrics could enhance the reliability of subtype-specific splicing biomarker discovery.

The MCD subtype demonstrates a distinct splicing landscape characterized by the significant downregulation of RBM39, a key regulator of splicing that interacts with spliceosome components such as SF3B1 and U2AF2 [25]. Downregulation of RBM39 disrupts normal splicing regulation, leading to widespread splicing abnormalities, including intron retention and exon skipping. This primarily affects genes involved in RNA processing and splicing machinery, altering the overall transcriptome profile [26]. RBM39 downregulation in the MCD subtype may interact synergistically with the hallmark MYD88 and CD79B variants, which activate NF-κB signaling. Loss of RBM39 could exacerbate splicing dysfunction and promote oncogenic programs, potentially making this subtype more vulnerable to spliceosome-targeting therapies. Inhibiting RBM39 has shown efficacy in inducing cell death in myeloid leukemias with SF variants, revealing subtype-specific vulnerabilities that could inform new therapeutic strategies [27]. Splicing modulators, such as H3B-8800, which disrupt spliceosome activity, could be explored as a treatment strategy to selectively impair the aberrant splicing machinery in MCD. One additional note is that A53 group also had a downregulation of RBM39. Due to the limited sample size of the A53 cohort (N = 6), further research with a larger A53 sample set is warranted to validate these findings. Our findings were further examined using the TCGA-DLBC cohort. The trend of lower RBM39 expression in MCD remained consistent, suggesting that RBM39 downregulation may be a recurrent feature of MCD, albeit with variability across datasets. Future studies with larger validation cohorts will be essential to precisely delineate the functional impact of RBM39 and its role in alternative splicing regulation in MCD.

Although WHO 2022 and ICC classifications represent current clinical standards for DLBCL, the dataset used in our study lacked the pathological metadata required for such classification. Consequently, our analyses were limited to molecular subtypes defined by the LymphGen algorithm.

While our study sheds Light on the splicing landscapes of LymphGen subtypes, there are several Limitations to consider. 1. In our study, 46.9% of cases remained unclassified using the LymphGen framework, likely due to DLBCL heterogeneity, insufficient genomic alterations, or technical issues like incomplete sequencing depth. Nevertheless, BN2-specific splicing patterns and pathway enrichment were consistently observed across independent methods, supporting the reliability of our findings. 2. In processing CNV data, we applied fixed log2 ratio thresholds to define copy number gains and losses. This approach does not account for mosaic copy number alterations, which may exist at intermediate signal levels and be present only in a subset of tumor cells. As a result, some subclonal CNVs may have been missed. Incorporating B-allele frequency, tumor purity estimates, or continuous CNV modeling may improve sensitivity in future studies. 3. Detecting statistically significant splicing events is challenging due to subtle exon-level variations between subtypes. To address this, we used DRIMSeq for isoform-level analysis, which showed minimal overlap with rMATS. While the distinct statistical models—likelihood-ratio test in rMATS and Dirichlet-multinomial modeling in DRIMSeq—resulted in some differences, the overall consistency in splicing trends supports the robustness of these findings. 4. When conducting DTU analysis, defining canonical transcripts accurately is crucial. Our study used Ensembl annotations, but these definitions can vary by tissue, complicating isoform interpretation. Nonetheless, adopting these established annotations represents the current best practice for ensuring consistency and reliability in transcript-level analyses.

In conclusion, this study investigated the role of AS in shaping the distinct molecular landscapes of DLBCL subtypes. The BN2 subtype, enriched with non-canonical isoforms and elevated splicing activity, offers potential for splicing alterations to serve as biomarkers or therapeutic targets for disrupting aggressive molecular pathways. Subtype-specific splicing events and isoform shifts in key genes like RPLP0, ACTB, ACTG1, and CD74 suggest promising avenues for AS-targeted therapies Moreover, the distinct expression patterns of RBM39 in the MCD subtype highlight the role of splicing regulators in defining subtype-specific profiles. Future studies should validate these splicing events’ functional impact and integrate AS analysis with epigenomic data to uncover regulatory mechanisms. Advances in long-read sequencing are expected to further clarify isoform-level alterations and identify new therapeutic targets [28]. Future research should validate the functional impact of these splicing events. Integrating AS analysis with proteomic and epigenomic data will help uncover novel regulatory mechanisms and support the development of precise, subtype-specific therapies. Our findings suggest that alternative splicing alterations may serve not only as biomarkers but also as potential therapeutic targets in DLBCL. Subtype-specific splicing events—especially in BN2 and MCD—highlight the opportunity to explore spliceosome modulators or splice-switching oligonucleotides for therapeutic development. These approaches may offer complementary strategies alongside existing targeted therapies.
