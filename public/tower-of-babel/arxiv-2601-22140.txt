Quantum fluctuations in hydrodynamics and quantum long-time tails
Akash Jain

Abstract
We construct a quantum Schwinger-Keldysh (SK) effective field theory for the diffusive hydrodynamics of a conserved scalar field. Quantum corrections within the SK framework are guided by fluctuation-dissipation relations, enforced via a dynamical Kubo-Martin-Schwinger (KMS) symmetry. We find that the KMS symmetry necessarily generates fluctuation contributions in the SK effective action at all orders in the noise field, thereby giving rise to intrinsically non-Gaussian noise. We use our results to compute one-loop quantum corrections to the two-point density-density retarded correlation function, leading to a quantum generalization of hydrodynamic long-time tails. Our results apply at arbitrarily high orders in ℏ \hbar . The one-loop results for retarded correlation functions have been expressed in terms of a family of polynomials. We also provide a closed-form expression for the one-loop results at leading order in the wavevector expansion.

Introduction

Fluctuation-dissipation theorem (FDT) requires that a physical system with dissipation must also exhibit fluctuations. Hydrodynamics describes the long-wavelength dissipative dynamics of many-body systems close to thermal equilibrium. However, the traditional framework of hydrodynamics—built on conservation laws and deterministic constitutive relations—does not account for fluctuations mandated by FDT
[
1
]
. Fluctuations at finite temperature persist even in the classical limit, arising as stochastic uncertainty due to coarse-graining over microscopic degrees of freedom. Stochastic hydrodynamics accounts for these classical thermal fluctuations by incorporating random noise into the constitutive relations
[
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
]
. However it still fails to account for the full quantum nature of fluctuations as systems depart from the classical limit.

Hydrodynamics is organised as a perturbative expansion near thermal equilibrium. Given the frequency
\omega
of fluctuations near equilibrium, hydrodynamics requires that
\omega\ll 1/t_{\text{micro}}
, where
t_{\text{micro}}
is largest characteristic timescale of the microscopic theory, like the mean free time between collisions of constituent particles. On the other hand, quantum fluctuations operate at the time scale
\hbar\beta
, where
{\text{k}}_{\resizebox{}{}{\scalebox{0.8}[1]{{\hbox{{\scriptsize B}}}}}}\beta=1/T
is the inverse temperature of the system. For theories where there is a sensible separation of scales between between
t_{\text{micro}}
and
\hbar\beta
, we may systematically ignore the quantum fluctuations. This is the regime of validity of stochastic hydrodynamics.
However for systems where all intrinsic timescales are either comparable to or smaller than
\hbar\beta
, there is no meaningful way to separate quantum and stochastic fluctuations.
An important example are conformal field theories (CFTs), which lack any characteristic scale of their own and thus
t_{\text{micro}}
is set precisely by the thermal scale
\hbar\beta
.

N
. For large-
N
CFTs, the microscopic time-scale is instead controlled by
t_{\text{micro}}\sim N\hbar\beta\gg\hbar\beta
. I thank Shiraz Minwalla for pointing this out.

Schwinger-Keldysh (SK) hydrodynamics is an action-based effective field theory (EFT) framework for hydrodynamics
[
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
]
, which enables us to systematically include the effects of thermal as well as quantum fluctuations. In particular, the framework features a symmetry-based realisation of the FDT, known as the dynamical Kubo-Martin-Schwinger (KMS) symmetry. This symmetry acts as a guiding principle for introducing fluctuations in hydrodynamics, both thermal and quantum, consistent with the FDT requirements at the full non-linear level.

The action of the KMS symmetry on SK-EFTs is non-local in time. SK-EFTs are set up on a closed-time contour that goes back-and-forth in time
[
14
]
, with independent degrees of freedom and background fields living on both parts of the contour. We will collectively denote these as
f_{1}(t)
and
f_{2}(t)
. The KMS symmetry acts on these fields with a relative phase-shift
i\hbar\beta
in imaginary time, i.e.

\displaystyle f_{1}(t)\to\pm f_{1}(-t+i\theta),\qquad f_{2}(t)\to\pm f_{2}(-t-i\hbar\beta+i\theta).

(1.1)

where
\theta
is a constant parameter and
\pm
denotes the time-reversal eigenvalue of the fields. As a result, any coupling terms in the effective action between
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
transform non-locally in time under KMS symmetry. This makes the KMS symmetry quite hard to implement in SK-EFTs.

Interestingly, the KMS symmetry becomes local in the classical limit
\hbar\to 0
. To this end, we identify the average “physical” combination of the fields
f_{r}=(f_{1}+f_{2})/2
and the difference “noise” combination
f_{a}=(f_{1}-f_{2})/\hbar
. These transform under the KMS symmetry at leading order in
\hbar
as

\displaystyle f_{r}(t)\to\pm f_{r}(-t)+\mathcal{O}(\hbar),\qquad f_{a}(t)\to\pm f_{a}(-t)\pm i\beta\partial_{t}f_{r}(-t)+\mathcal{O}(\hbar).

(1.2)

This classical version of the KMS symmetry is relatively easier to implement in SK-EFTs and has been successfully applied to derive an EFT for stochastic hydrodynamics.

In this work, we present a systematic mechanism to implement the full quantum version of the KMS symmetry in the SK effective action. This construction enables us to derive the quantum-complete SK-EFT for hydrodynamic models, consistent with FDT requirements at finite
\hbar
. We focus on a simple hydrodynamic model of a single conserved scalar field for concreteness, though extensions to more general hydrodynamic models is straight-forward. As an application of our framework, we derive one-loop quantum corrections to the two-point retarded correlation funtion of density. This enables us to compute the quantum correction to hydrodynamic long-time tails in the theory of diffusion.

section

appendix

1.1
Overview of results

The classical diffusion model:

For concreteness, we will work with the simplest hydrodynamic model for a single conserved density
n
with diffusive dynamics. This simple model can describe conserved particle dynamics, conserved U(1) charges, or conserved energy in the absense of momentum conservation. The extension to full hydrodynamic models with conserved momenta is conceptually straightforward.
The classical conservation equation for
n
is given as

\displaystyle\partial_{t}n+\partial_{i}J^{i}=0,

(1.3)

where
J^{i}
denotes the flux. Instead of working directly with
n
, it is convenient to instead express the theory in terms of the associated thermodynamic chemical potential
\mu
, such that
n(\mu)
is a function of
\mu
. The flux
J^{i}
admits diffusive constitutive relations that take the form

\displaystyle J^{i}=-\sigma(\mu)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\partial^{i}\mu-E^{i}}}\right),

(1.4)

where
\sigma(\mu)
is the conductivity transport coefficient and
E_{i}=\partial_{i}A_{t}-\partial_{t}A_{i}
denotes an applied electric field. Note that the response of
J^{i}
to applied electric fields as well as chemical potential gradients is controlled by the same transport coefficient
\sigma
. This follows as a consequence of the second law of thermodynamics; see e.g.
[
15
]
for a quick derivation.

Substituting the constitutive relations into the conservation equation and linearising in fluctuations
\delta n=n-n(\mu_{0})
, we see that the model features diffusive dynamics
\partial_{t}\delta n=D\,\partial_{i}\partial^{i}\delta n-\sigma\,\partial_{i}E^{i}
, where
D=\sigma/\chi
is the diffusion constant. In Fourier-space parametrised by frequency
\omega
and wavevector
k_{i}
, this leads to the standard diffusion pole
\omega=-iD{\bf k}^{2}
. We can also use the hydrodynamic model to obtain the classical retarded correlation functions (or response function). For instance, the two-point classical retarded correlation function of density is given as

\displaystyle G^{\mathrm{R}}_{nn}(\omega,{\bf k})_{\text{cl}}=\frac{\delta n_{\text{onshell}}[A]}{\delta A_{t}}\bigg|_{A=0}=\frac{\sigma\,{\bf k}^{2}}{D\,{\bf k}^{2}-i\omega},

(1.5)

which admits a diffusive pole. See
[
16
]
for more details.

Classical SK-EFT for diffusion:

The degrees of freedom in the SK-EFT for diffusion are a pair of phase fields
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
. The average field
\varphi_{r}=(\varphi_{1}+\varphi_{2})/2
is related to the chemical potential via
\mu=\partial_{t}\varphi_{r}+A_{t}
. Whereas, the difference combination
\varphi_{a}=(\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2})/\hbar
is understood as the noise field parametrising fluctuations. The SK effective action for diffusion takes the form

\displaystyle S=\int\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}^{d}{\bf x}\Big(n(\mu)\partial_{t}\varphi_{a}-\sigma(\mu)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\partial^{i}\mu-E^{i}}}\right)\partial_{i}\varphi_{a}+\mathcal{O}(\varphi_{a}^{2})\Big),

(1.6)

where we have suppressed the quadratic and higher-order terms in the noise field. Here
d
denotes the number of spatial dimensions.
Extremising the effective action with respect to
\varphi_{r}
or
\mu
sets the noise field
\varphi_{a}
to zero onshell. Whereas, extremising it with respect to
\varphi_{a}
leads the the classical conservation equation in
eq.

1.3
with the constitutive relations in
eq.

1.4
.

To progress beyond the classical equations of motion and account for fluctuations, we need to specify the higher-order noise terms in the effective action (
1.6
). At the classical level, the KMS symmetry determines these to be
[
10
,
17
,
18
]

\displaystyle S=\int\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}^{d}{\bf x}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n(\mu)\partial_{t}\varphi_{a}-\sigma(\mu)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\partial^{i}\mu-E^{i}}}\right)\partial_{i}\varphi_{a}+\frac{i}{\beta}\sigma(\mu)\,\partial_{i}\varphi_{a}\partial^{i}\varphi_{a}+\mathcal{O}(\hbar)}}\right).

(1.7)

Note that the noise part of the effective action is imaginary, which concerns the dissipative nature of SK-EFT models. We can use this model to obtain the retarded as well as symmetric correlation functions in the classical limit. To this end, we need to also couple the effective action to noise-type gauge fields
A_{at}
,
A_{ai}
, which is achieved by making the substitutions
\partial_{t}\varphi_{a}\to\partial_{t}\varphi_{a}+A_{at}
and
\partial_{i}\varphi_{a}\to\partial_{i}\varphi_{a}+A_{ai}
in
eq.

1.7
. For uniformity, in the SK-EFT language it is customary to denote the original gauge fields
A_{t}
,
A_{i}
as
A_{rt}
,
A_{ri}
.

At tree-level, i.e. ignoring interactions in the SK-EFT,
eq.

1.7
leads to two-point retarded and symmetric correlation functions of density

\displaystyle G^{\mathrm{R}}_{nn}(\omega,{\bf k})

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\delta}{\delta A_{rt}}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{-i\delta}{\delta A_{at}}}}\right)\ln{\cal Z}\bigg|_{A_{r},A_{a}=0}

\displaystyle=\frac{{\bf k}^{2}\sigma}{D\,{\bf k}^{2}-i\omega}+\mathcal{O}(\hbar)+\ldots,

\displaystyle G^{\mathrm{S}}_{nn}(\omega,{\bf k})

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{-i\delta}{\delta A_{at}}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{-i\delta}{\delta A_{at}}}}\right)\ln{\cal Z}\bigg|_{A_{r},A_{a}=0}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\beta}\frac{{\bf k}^{2}\sigma}{|D\,{\bf k}^{2}-i\omega|^{2}}+\mathcal{O}(\hbar)+\ldots,

(1.8)

where ellipses denote loop-corrections due to interactions. For the retarded correlation function, we have recovered the classical result in
eq.

2.6
at treee level. Whereas, flucutations in the model also lead to a non-trivial symmetric correlation function, which parametrises the probabilistic variance of the density,
\langle n^{2}\rangle-\langle n\rangle^{2}
. Note that the symmetric correlation function has poles at
\omega=\pm iD{\bf k}^{2}
. The correlation functions satisfy the classical FDT
[
19
]

G^{\mathrm{S}}_{nn}(\omega,{\bf k})=\frac{2}{\beta\omega}\Im G^{\mathrm{R}}_{nn}(\omega,{\bf k})+\mathcal{O}(\hbar),

(1.9)

which is ensured by the KMS symmetry of
eq.

1.7
.

We can also use
eq.

1.7
to calculate the corrections to the correlation functions due to interactions with classical fluctuations. For example, the leading correction to the two-point retarded correlation function of density is given as
[
17
,
18
]

\displaystyle G^{\mathrm{R}}_{nn}(\omega,{\bf k})=\frac{{\bf k}^{2}\sigma}{D\,{\bf k}^{2}-i\omega}+\frac{i\omega\,{\bf k}^{4}}{(D\,{\bf k}^{2}-i\omega)^{2}}\frac{\chi^{2}\lambda^{2}}{\beta D}\frac{\Gamma\bigl(1-\tfrac{d}{2}\bigr)}{(16\pi)^{d/2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\bf k}^{2}-\frac{2i\omega}{D}}}\right)^{\tfrac{d}{2}-1}+\mathcal{O}(\hbar)+\ldots.

(1.10)

In particular, note that the correction piece is non-analytic and gives rise to a branch-cut in the complex-
\omega
plane starting at
\omega=-iD{\bf k}^{2}/2
. In real-space, this leads to a power-law fall-off of the correlation function at late times, known as long-time tails
[
20
,
21
]
, instead of the exponential decay as predicted by classical deterministic hydrodynamics. Note that in
d=2
, the non-analyticity behaves as
\ln\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\bf k}^{2}-2i\omega/D}}\right)
instead.

Quantum-complete SK-EFT for diffusion:

The main result of this paper is to derive the quantum-completion of the SK effective action in
eq.

1.7
, guided by the finite-
\hbar
version of the KMS symmetry. We find that the KMS symmetry generates terms in the effective action at all orders in the noise field. Truncating to quartic order in the noise field, we find

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}^{d}{\bf x}\bigg[n(\mu)\partial_{t}\varphi_{a}-\sigma(\mu)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\partial^{i}\mu-E^{i}}}\right)\partial_{i}\varphi_{a}

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{i}{\beta}\sigma(\mu)\partial_{i}\varphi_{a}\mathfrak{D}\partial^{i}\varphi_{a}-\frac{i}{\beta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\partial^{i}\mu-E^{i}}}\right)\,\mathfrak{D}_{2}\Big(\partial_{i}\varphi_{a},\sigma^{\prime}(\mu)\partial_{t}\varphi_{a}\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{\hbar^{2}}{24}n^{\prime\prime}(\mu)(\partial_{t}\varphi_{a})^{3}-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{8}\sigma^{\prime\prime}(\mu)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\partial^{i}\mu-E^{i}}}\right)\partial_{i}\varphi_{a}(\partial_{t}\varphi_{a})^{2}-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{8}\partial_{t}\Big(\sigma^{\prime}(\mu)\partial_{t}\varphi_{a}\Big)\partial_{i}\varphi_{a}\partial^{i}\varphi_{a}

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{i\hbar^{2}}{8\beta}\sigma^{\prime\prime}(\mu)(\partial_{t}\varphi_{a})^{2}\partial_{i}\varphi_{a}\mathfrak{D}\partial^{i}\varphi_{a}-\frac{i\hbar^{2}}{24\beta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\partial^{i}\mu-E^{i}}}\right)\mathfrak{D}_{2}\Big(\partial_{i}\varphi_{a},\sigma^{\prime\prime\prime}(\mu)(\partial_{t}\varphi_{a})^{3}\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathcal{O}(\varphi_{a}^{5})\bigg],

(1.11)

where
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{D}_{2}
are differential operators defined as

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}=\frac{\hbar\beta\partial_{t}}{2}\cot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\hbar\beta\partial_{t}}{2}}}\right)

\displaystyle=1-2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\zeta(2n)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\hbar\beta\partial_{t}}{2\pi}}}\right)^{2n},

\displaystyle=1-\frac{\hbar^{2}\beta^{2}}{12}\partial_{t}^{2}-\frac{\hbar^{4}\beta^{4}}{720}\partial_{t}^{4}+\ldots,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}_{2}(f,g)=\frac{1}{\partial_{t}}\Big(g\mathfrak{D}f-f\mathfrak{D}g\Big)

\displaystyle=2\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\sum_{n=1}^{2m}(-1)^{n+1}\,\zeta(2m)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\hbar\beta}{2\pi}}}\right)^{2m}\partial_{t}^{n-1}f\,\partial_{t}^{2m-n}g

\displaystyle=\frac{\hbar^{2}\beta^{2}}{12}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(f\,\partial_{t}g-\partial_{t}f\,g}}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{\hbar^{4}\beta^{4}}{720}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(f\partial_{t}^{3}g-\partial_{t}f\partial_{t}^{2}g+\partial_{t}^{2}f\partial_{t}g-\partial_{t}^{3}g}}\right)+\ldots,

(1.12)

where
\zeta
denotes the Riemann Zeta function. Note that both these operators are perfectly local in time within a derivative expansion. Note that the quantum corrections leave the linear piece in
\varphi_{a}
untouched. Therefore they do not affect the classical conservation equation of diffusion. However, they do affect the quadratic and all higher-order terms in
\varphi_{a}

The SK effective action (

) leaves the retarded correlation function untouched at the tree-level. However, the symmetric correlation function modifies as

\displaystyle G^{\mathrm{R}}_{nn}(\omega,{\bf k})

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\delta}{\delta A_{rt}}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{-i\delta}{\delta A_{at}}}}\right)\ln{\cal Z}\bigg|_{A_{r},A_{a}=0}

\displaystyle=\frac{{\bf k}^{2}\sigma}{D\,{\bf k}^{2}-i\omega}+\ldots,

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{-i\delta}{\delta A_{at}}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{-i\delta}{\delta A_{at}}}}\right)\ln{\cal Z}\bigg|_{A_{r},A_{a}=0}

\displaystyle=\hbar\omega\coth\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\hbar\beta\omega}{2}}}\right)\frac{{\bf k}^{2}\sigma}{|D\,{\bf k}^{2}-i\omega|^{2}}+\ldots,

As a consequence of the KMS symmetry, these expressions satisfy the quantum FDT

G^{\mathrm{S}}_{nn}(\omega,{\bf k})=\hbar\coth\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\hbar\beta\omega}{2}}}\right)\Im G^{\mathrm{R}}_{nn}(\omega,{\bf k}).

(1.14)

In addition to diffusive poles at

Quantum long-time tails:

As an application of the quantum-complete SK-EFT in

, we can compute the one-loop quantum corrections to the two-point retarded correlation function of density in

\displaystyle G^{\mathrm{R}}_{nn}(\omega,{\bf k})

\displaystyle=\frac{{\bf k}^{2}\sigma}{D\,{\bf k}^{2}-i\omega}+\frac{i\omega\,{\bf k}^{4}}{(D\,{\bf k}^{2}-i\omega)^{2}}\frac{\chi^{2}\lambda^{2}}{\beta D}\frac{\Gamma\bigl(1-\tfrac{d}{2}\bigr)}{(16\pi)^{d/2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\bf k}^{2}-\frac{2i\omega}{D}}}\right)^{\tfrac{d}{2}-1}

\displaystyle\hskip 80.00012pt\times\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\zeta(2n)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\hbar\beta}{8\pi}}}\right)^{2n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(D{\bf k}^{2}-2i\omega}}\right)^{2n}\mathcal{P}_{d,2n}(z)}}\right)+\ldots,

where

and
\mathcal{P}_{d,2n}(z)

Note that the individual polynomials do not give any additional non-analytic structure to the correlation function. However, the entire resummed expression does give rise to new Matsubara-like pole structures. While we have not been able to perform the resummation for arbitrary values of

We begin with a brief review of the Schwinger–Keldysh effective field theory (SK-EFT) formalism

2.1
Schwinger-Keldysh formalism

Dynamical fields and global symmetries:

SK-EFTs are set-up on a closed-time contour, with leg “1” going forward in time and leg “2” returning backward in time to the initial state. While the conventional single-time contour from textbook quantum field theory can only access correlation functions at zero temperature, this exotic closed-time contour can be used to compute symmetric, retarded, and advanced correlators at finite temperature.
Each leg of the contour is equipped with its own set of degrees of freedom and background fields. For the theory of diffusion, the dynamical field content consists of a pair of phase fields
\varphi_{1,2}
, with the subscripts labelling the respective legs of the contour.
The theory is required to obey independent global U(1) symmetries on the two legs, i.e.

\varphi_{1,2}\to\varphi_{1,2}-\Lambda_{1,2}.

(2.1a)

It is useful to introduce a set of background gauge fields
A_{1,2\mu}
associated with these symmetries, with transformation rules

A_{1,2\mu}\to A_{1,2\mu}+\partial_{\mu}\Lambda_{1,2}.

(2.1b)

Using these, we can define the global symmetry invariants

B_{1,2\mu}=\partial_{\mu}\varphi_{1,2}+A_{1,2\mu},

(2.2)

which are invariant under the background-gauged versions of the global U(1) symmetries.

It is also useful to introduce an average-difference basis

\displaystyle f_{r}=\frac{f_{1}+f_{2}}{2},\qquad f_{a}=\frac{f_{1}-f_{2}}{\hbar},

(2.3)

for various dynamical and background fields. The average “
r
” combinations are understood as the “physical” macroscopic fields, while the difference “
a
” combinations as the stochastic noise associated with them. The SK effective action
S[B_{r},B_{a}]
for diffusion can be expressed in terms of the global symmetry invariants
B_{r,a\mu}
.

Diagonal shift symmetry:

In addition to the global U(1) symmetries above, we impose a local time-independent diagonal shift symmetry between the two phase fields

\varphi_{1,2}\to\varphi_{1,2}+\lambda,\qquad\partial_{t}\lambda=0,

(2.4)

which acts uniformly on the two legs of the SK contour. This symmetry ensures that the SK-EFT describes the phase where the global U(1) symmetry of the microscopic theory is spontaneously unbroken; see
[
22
]
.
It is easy to see that the difference combination
B_{a\mu}
is invariant under the diagonal shift symmetry, while the average combination
B_{r\mu}
transforms as

B_{r\mu}\to B_{r\mu}+\partial_{\mu}\lambda.

(2.5)

Furthermore, due to the time-independent nature of this symmetry, the time-component
B_{rt}
of the average combination is also invariant. This is identified as the local chemical potential
\mu\equiv B_{rt}
in the theory of diffusion.

Schwinger-Keldysh generating functional:

The fundamental object of interest in non-equilibrium field theory is the SK generating functional
\mathcal{Z}[A_{r},A_{a}]
, prescribed as a functional of the two sets of background fields
A_{r,a\mu}
. It can be used to compute the retarded and symmetric correlation functions of
J^{\mu}
using the variational formulae

\displaystyle G^{\mathrm{R}}_{J^{\mu}J^{\nu}}

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\delta}{\delta A_{r\nu}}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{-i\delta}{\delta A_{a\mu}}}}\right)\ln{\cal Z}[A_{r},A_{a}]\bigg|_{A_{r},A_{a}=0},

\displaystyle G^{\mathrm{S}}_{J^{\mu}J^{\nu}}

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{-i\delta}{\delta A_{a\mu}}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{-i\delta}{\delta A_{a\nu}}}}\right)\ln{\cal Z}[A_{r},A_{a}]\bigg|_{A_{r},A_{a}=0},

(2.6)

and similarly for higher-point functions.
The generating functional is obtained by performing a path integral of the effective action
S[B_{r},B_{a}]
over the dynamical fields
\varphi_{r,a}
, as in
2
2

2

Note that we have absorbed the conventional factor of
1/\hbar
in the exponent of the path integral into the definition of
S
. This enables the path integral to have a smooth
\hbar\to 0
limit.

{\cal Z}[A_{r},A_{a}]=\int\mathcal{D}\varphi_{r}\,\mathcal{D}\varphi_{a}\exp\Big(iS[B_{r},B_{a}]\Big).

(2.7)

Unitarity constraints:

The SK generating functional is required to satisfy the following three unitarity constraints

\mathcal{Z}[A_{r},A_{a}=0]=1,\qquad\mathcal{Z}[A_{r},-A_{a}]=\mathcal{Z}^{*}[A_{r},A_{a}],\qquad\Re\mathcal{Z}[A_{r},A_{a}]\leq 0,

(2.8)

arising from generic properties of quantum field theories defined on a closed-time contour.
More details regarding the underlying physics can be found in the review of
[
22
]
. These constraints can naturally be extended to the effective action as

S[B_{r},B_{a}=0]=0,\qquad S[B_{r},-B_{a}]=-S^{*}[B_{r},B_{a}],\qquad\Im S[B_{r},B_{a}]\geq 0.

(2.9)

We can arrange
S
as a series in powers of
B_{a}
, in which case the three conditions mean that:
S
must at least be linear in
B_{a}
, the terms with even-powers of
B_{a}
must be imaginary, and the total imaginary part of the effective action must be non-negative.

Classical equation of motion:

The classical equation of motion for diffusion can be obtained by extremising the effective action with respect to the dynamical fields

\displaystyle\frac{\delta S}{\delta\varphi_{a}}\bigg|_{A_{a}=0,A_{r}=A}=0,\qquad\frac{\delta S}{\delta\varphi_{r}}\bigg|_{A_{a}=0,A_{r}=A}=0.

(2.10)

Here we have turned off the difference/noise combination of the background gauge fields
A_{a\mu}
, while identified the average combination
A_{r\mu}
with the classical background gauge field
A_{\mu}
.
Since the effective action is at least linear in noise fields, it follows that the second equation above identically sets the dynamical noise field
\varphi_{a}
to zero. On the other hand, the first equation leads to the U(1) conservation equation

\displaystyle\partial_{\mu}J^{\mu}=0,\qquad J^{\mu}=\frac{\delta S}{\delta A_{a\mu}}\bigg|_{A_{a}=0,A_{r}=A}.

(2.11)

The SK conditions in
eq.

need to be satisfied for arbitrary non-equilibrium field theories defined on a closed-time contour. However, to describe correlation functions in a thermal state, the SK generating functional
\mathcal{Z}[A_{r},A_{a}]
is further required to satisfy a discrete dynamical KMS (Kubo-Martin-Schwinger) symmetry. The KMS symmetry is responsible for implementing the fluctuation-dissipation relations between symmetric and retarded correlation functions

G^{\mathrm{S}}_{J^{\mu}J^{\nu}}(\omega,{\bf k})=\hbar\coth\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\hbar\beta\omega}{2}}}\right)\Im G^{\mathrm{R}}_{J^{\mu}J^{\nu}}(\omega,{\bf k}),

(2.12)

as well as their higher-point generalisations
[
23
]
. The KMS symmetry is defined through a transformation of the background fields

\displaystyle\mathrm{K}A_{1\mu}(t,{\bf x})

\displaystyle=\Theta A_{1\mu}(t-i\theta,{\bf x}),

\displaystyle\mathrm{K}A_{2\mu}(t,{\bf x})

\displaystyle=\Theta A_{2\mu}(t+i\hbar\beta-i\theta,{\bf x}),

(2.13)

where
\theta
is an arbitrary constant parametrising a translations in imaginary time. Whereas,
\Theta
represents some discrete anti-unitary symmetry of the microscopic theory involving time-reversal transformation (such as time-reversal T, spacetime reversal PT, or variants involving charge conjugation, CT or CPT). See
table

1
. We can check that KMS is a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
transformation,
\mathrm{K}^{2}=1
. More details can be found in textbook treatments of thermal field theory, e.g. in
[
14
]
, or in the more recent review of
[
22
]
.

In the SK-EFT for diffusion, the action of the KMS symmetry is naturally extended to the dynamical fields as

\displaystyle\mathrm{K}\varphi_{1}(t,{\bf x})

\displaystyle=\Theta\varphi_{1}(t-i\theta,{\bf x}),

\displaystyle\mathrm{K}\varphi_{2}(t,{\bf x})

\displaystyle=\Theta\varphi_{2}(t+i\hbar\beta-i\theta,{\bf x}),

(2.14)

This is often called the dynamical KMS symmetry. The action of the KMS symmetry on the background fields in
eq.

2.13
is universal and can be derived from general principles of thermal field theory. On the other hand, the dynamical KMS symmetry in
eq.

2.14
, i.e. how KMS transformations act on the dynamical fields, is part of the specifications of the model. In particular, there may be other consistent SK-EFT models where the KMS symmetry acts differently on the dynamical fields; see e.g.
[
15
]
.

Since the KMS symmetry acts differently on the “1” and “2” copies of the fields, its action on the SK-EFT—in particular, on the average-difference combinations of fields—is highly non-local in time. However, working perturbatively in derivatives or
\hbar
, the action of the KMS symmetry can be made local. To this end, let us employ a Taylor expansion in time to represent the KMS transformation in
eqs.

2.13
and
2.14
as

\displaystyle\mathrm{K}f_{1}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\Theta}\Big(\mathrm{e}^{i\hbar\beta/2\partial_{t}}f_{1}\Big),

\displaystyle\mathrm{K}f_{2}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\Theta}\Big(\mathrm{e}^{-i\hbar\beta/2\partial_{t}}f_{2}\Big),

(2.15)

where
f
is a placeholder for
A_{\mu}
,
\varphi
, or
B_{\mu}
, while
\tilde{\Theta}\equiv\mathrm{e}^{i(\hbar\beta/2-\theta)}\Theta=\Theta\mathrm{e}^{-i(\hbar\beta/2-\theta)}
. Passing onto the average-difference basis, this leads to

\displaystyle\mathrm{K}f_{r}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\Theta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\cos\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\hbar\beta\partial_{t}}{2}}}\right)f_{r}+\frac{i\hbar}{2}\sin\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\hbar\beta\partial_{t}}{2}}}\right)f_{a}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\tilde{\Theta}f_{r}+\frac{\hbar^{2}}{4}\tilde{\Theta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i\beta\partial_{t}f_{a}-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\partial_{t}^{2}f_{r}}}\right)+\mathcal{O}(\hbar^{4}),

\displaystyle\mathrm{K}f_{a}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\Theta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\cos\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\hbar\beta\partial_{t}}{2}}}\right)f_{a}+\frac{2i}{\hbar}\sin\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\hbar\beta\partial_{t}}{2}}}\right)f_{r}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\tilde{\Theta}\Big(f_{a}+i\beta\partial_{t}f_{r}\Big)-\frac{\hbar^{2}\beta^{2}}{8}\tilde{\Theta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\partial_{t}^{2}f_{a}+\frac{i\beta}{3}\partial_{t}^{3}f_{r}}}\right)+\mathcal{O}(\hbar^{4}).

(2.16)

This representation will be extremely useful in the next section while constructing the effective action for quantum fluctuations in hydrodynamics.

2.3
Effective action in
\hbar\to 0
limit

The dynamical KMS transformations in
eq.

2.16
become quite simple in
\hbar\to 0
limit. This “classical” version of the KMS symmetry is relevant for classical stochastic field theories valid at small frequencies
\omega\ll T_{0}/\hbar
, where quantum effects are suppressed. This condition precisely yields the classical 2-point fluctuation-dissipation theorem

G^{\mathrm{S}}_{J^{\mu}J^{\nu}}(\omega,{\bf k})=\frac{2}{\beta\omega}\Im G^{\mathrm{R}}_{J^{\mu}J^{\nu}}(\omega,{\bf k})+\mathcal{O}(\hbar).

(2.17)

Similar higher-point relations can be derived by using this symmetry at higher non-linear orders in background fields.

As an example, the SK effective action for diffusion in the limit
\hbar\to 0
is given as
[
10
,
17
,
18
]

\displaystyle S_{\text{cl}}

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}^{d}{\bf x}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(B_{at}n(B_{rt})-\sigma(B_{rt})B_{a}^{i}\partial_{t}B_{ri}+\frac{i}{\beta}\sigma(B_{rt})B_{a}^{i}B_{ai}+\mathcal{O}(\hbar)}}\right),

(2.18)

where we have identified the charge density
n(\mu)
and the conductivity
\sigma(\mu)
as functions of
\mu=B_{rt}
.
The action is manifestly invariant under the global U(1) symmetries and the time-independent diagonal shift symmetry. It trivially obeys the first two SK unitarity constraints in
eq.

2.9
. The third condition imposes that the conductivity
\sigma
is a non-negative function of
\mu
, i.e.

\displaystyle\sigma(\mu)\geq 0.

(2.19)

Finally, let us look at the dynamical KMS symmetry. The KMS transformation of the thermodynamic density term in
eq.

2.18
gives

\displaystyle\mathrm{K}\Big(B_{at}n(B_{rt})\Big)

\displaystyle=\tilde{\Theta}\Big(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(B_{at}+i\beta\partial_{t}B_{rt}}}\right)n(B_{rt})\Big)+\mathcal{O}(\hbar)

\displaystyle=\tilde{\Theta}\Big(B_{at}n(B_{rt})\Big)-\partial_{t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i\beta\,\tilde{\Theta}p(B_{rt})}}\right)+\mathcal{O}(\hbar),

(2.20)

where
p
is the thermodynamic pressure defined such that
p^{\prime}(\mu)=n(\mu)
. For
\Theta=\rm{T,PT}
, the
\tilde{\Theta}
operator merely implements a change of coordinates
t,{\bf x}
in the action. We thus infer that the density term in
eq.

2.18
is invariant under KMS transformations up to a total-derivative boundary contribution that can be ignored. The same argument also applies for
\Theta=\rm{CT,CPT}
, except that
n(\mu)
must be an odd function of
\mu
.

On the other hand, for the conductivity terms in
eq.

2.18
, we note that

\displaystyle\mathrm{K}\Big({-}\sigma(B_{rt})B_{a}^{i}\partial_{t}B_{ri}\Big)

\displaystyle=\tilde{\Theta}\Big(\sigma(B_{rt})\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(B_{a}^{i}+i\beta\partial_{t}B_{r}^{i}}}\right)\partial_{t}B_{ri}\Big)+\mathcal{O}(\hbar)

\displaystyle=\tilde{\Theta}\Big(\sigma(B_{rt})B_{a}^{i}\partial_{t}B_{ri}\Big)+i\beta\,\tilde{\Theta}\Big(\sigma(B_{rt})\partial_{t}B_{r}^{i}\partial_{t}B_{ri}\Big)+\mathcal{O}(\hbar),

\displaystyle\mathrm{K}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{i}{\beta}\sigma(B_{rt})B_{a}^{i}B_{ai}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\tilde{\Theta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{i}{\beta}\sigma(B_{rt})\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(B_{a}^{i}+i\beta\partial_{t}B_{r}^{i}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(B_{ai}+i\beta\partial_{t}B_{ri}}}\right)}}\right)+\mathcal{O}(\hbar)

\displaystyle=\tilde{\Theta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{i}{\beta}\sigma(B_{rt})B_{a}^{i}B_{ai}}}\right)-2\tilde{\Theta}\Big(\sigma(B_{rt})B_{a}^{i}\partial_{t}B_{ri}\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad-i\beta\tilde{\Theta}\Big(\sigma(B_{rt})\partial_{t}B_{r}^{i}\partial_{t}B_{ri}\Big)+\mathcal{O}(\hbar).

(2.21)

Therefore, these terms also map to themselves up to the
\tilde{\Theta}
transformation. As before, the conductivity terms are identically KMS-invariant for
\Theta=\rm{T,PT}
, while for
\Theta=\rm{CT,CPT}
we further require that
\sigma(\mu)
is an even function of
\mu
.

It is often useful to cast the SK effective action explicitly in terms of
\mu
. We find

\displaystyle S_{\text{cl}}

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}^{d}{\bf x}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(B_{at}n(\mu)-\sigma(\mu)B_{a}^{i}\Big(\partial_{i}\mu-E_{i}\Big)+\frac{i}{\beta}\sigma(\mu)B_{a}^{i}B_{ai}+\mathcal{O}(\hbar)}}\right),

(2.22)

where
E_{i}=\partial_{i}A_{rt}-\partial_{t}A_{ri}
is the background electric field.

Quantum-complete Schwinger-Keldysh effective action

In
section

2
, we obtained the SK effective action for diffusion in
\hbar\to 0
limit. Our aim in this section is to complete this effective action with appropriate quantum corrections such that it satisfies the full KMS symmetry in
eq.

2.16
up to arbitrary orders in
\hbar
. We will start with the free quadratic version of the theory in
section

3.1
, where it is easy to guess the correct form of the effective action based on FDT. We then derive the complete interacting non-linear effective action in
section

3.2
.

3.1
Free effective action

To obtain the quantum-complete version of SK effective action, it is easiest to start with the free theory, with
n(\mu)=\chi_{0}\mu
and
\sigma(\mu)=\sigma_{0}
, where
\chi_{0}
is the constant thermodynamic charge susceptibility and
\sigma_{0}\geq 0
is the constant conductivity. In this case, the classical effective action reduces to

\displaystyle S_{\text{cl,free}}

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}^{d}{\bf x}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\chi_{0}B_{rt}B_{at}-\sigma_{0}B^{i}_{a}\partial_{t}B_{ri}+\frac{i}{\beta}\sigma_{0}B^{i}_{a}B_{ai}+\mathcal{O}(\hbar)}}\right).

(3.1)

At the level of two-point functions, obtaining the quantum version of FDT in
eq.

2.12
from the classical one in
eq.

2.17
merely corresponds to promoting the noise strength from
2/(\beta\omega)
to
\hbar\coth(\hbar\beta\omega/2)
. Correspondingly, we can guess the quantum-complete version of the free effective action by appropriately promoting the noise term

\displaystyle S_{\text{free}}

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}^{d}{\bf x}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n_{0}B_{at}+\chi_{0}B_{rt}B_{at}-\sigma_{0}B^{i}_{a}\partial_{t}B_{ri}+\frac{i}{\beta}\sigma_{0}B^{i}_{a}\mathfrak{D}B_{ai}+\mathcal{O}(\hbar)}}\right),

(3.2)

where we have defined the differential operator
\mathfrak{D}
as

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}

\displaystyle=\frac{\hbar\beta\partial_{t}}{2}\cot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\hbar\beta\partial_{t}}{2}}}\right)

\displaystyle=1-\frac{\hbar^{2}\beta^{2}}{12}\partial_{t}^{2}-\frac{\hbar^{4}\beta^{4}}{720}\partial_{t}^{4}+\ldots.

(3.3)

Note that the operator
\mathfrak{D}
, and thus the effective action, is completely local within the derivative expansion of effective field theories. One may check that
eq.

3.2
continues to obey all the SK requirements: global U(1) symmetries, diagonal shift symmetry, as well as the three SK unitarity constraints.

Verifying the KMS symmetry:

Let us verify that the effective action in
eq.

3.2
is indeed invariant under full KMS transformations in
eq.

2.16
. To this end, it is useful to introduce a short-hand notation

\displaystyle\mathsf{C}=\cos\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\hbar\beta\partial_{t}}{2}}}\right),\qquad\mathsf{S}=\sin\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\hbar\beta\partial_{t}}{2}}}\right).

(3.4)

In terms of these,
\mathfrak{D}=\hbar\beta/2\,\partial_{t}\mathsf{C}/\mathsf{S}
.
Further, note that
\mathrm{e}^{\lambda\partial_{t}}\big(fg\big)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{e}^{\lambda\partial_{t}}f}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{e}^{\lambda\partial_{t}}g}}\right)
for arbitrary functions
f
and
g
.
It follows that

\displaystyle\mathsf{C}\big(fg\big)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{C}f\,\mathsf{C}g-\mathsf{S}f\,\mathsf{S}g,

\displaystyle\mathsf{S}\big(fg\big)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{C}f\,\mathsf{S}g+\mathsf{S}f\,\mathsf{C}g.

(3.5)

The KMS transformation of the
\chi_{0}
term in
eq.

3.2
gives

\displaystyle\mathrm{K}\Big(B_{rt}B_{at}\Big)

\displaystyle=\tilde{\Theta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathsf{C}B_{rt}+\frac{i\hbar}{2}\mathsf{S}B_{at}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathsf{C}B_{at}+\frac{2i}{\hbar}\mathsf{S}B_{rt}}}\right)}}\right]

\displaystyle=\tilde{\Theta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathsf{C}B_{rt}\mathsf{C}B_{at}+\frac{2i}{\hbar}\mathsf{C}B_{rt}\mathsf{S}B_{rt}+\frac{i\hbar}{2}\mathsf{S}B_{at}\mathsf{C}B_{at}-\mathsf{S}B_{at}\mathsf{S}B_{rt}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\tilde{\Theta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathsf{C}\Big(B_{rt}B_{at}\Big)+\mathsf{S}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{i}{\hbar}B_{rt}^{2}}}\right)+\mathsf{S}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{i\hbar}{4}B_{at}^{2}}}\right)}}\right].

(3.6)

Since the Taylor expansion of
\mathsf{S}
starts from
\hbar\beta/2\,\partial_{t}+\ldots
, it follows that
\mathsf{S}(f)=\partial_{t}(\ldots)
. Therefore, the last two terms above drop out as total-derivative boundary contributions in the effective action. On the other hand, the Taylor expansion of
\mathsf{C}
starts with
1-\hbar^{2}\beta^{2}/8\,\partial_{t}^{2}+\ldots
, therefore we have
\mathsf{C}(f)=f+\partial_{t}(\ldots)
. Using this we find immediately see that

\displaystyle\mathrm{K}\Big(B_{rt}B_{at}\Big)

\displaystyle=\tilde{\Theta}\Big(B_{rt}B_{at}\Big)+\partial_{t}(\ldots).

(3.7)

Since
B_{rt}B_{at}
is even under charge conjugation, it follows that the
\chi_{0}
term in
eq.

3.2
is invariant under KMS for any choice of
\Theta
.

Let us now look at the two
\sigma_{0}
terms. In this case, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{K}\Big({-}B^{i}_{a}\partial_{t}B_{ri}\Big)

\displaystyle=\tilde{\Theta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathsf{C}B_{a}^{i}+\frac{2i}{\hbar}\mathsf{S}B_{r}^{i}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathsf{C}\partial_{t}B_{ri}+\frac{i\hbar}{2}\mathsf{S}\partial_{t}B_{ai}}}\right)}}\right]

\displaystyle=\tilde{\Theta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathsf{C}B_{a}^{i}\mathsf{C}\partial_{t}B_{ri}-\mathsf{S}B_{r}^{i}\mathsf{S}\partial_{t}B_{ai}+\frac{i\hbar}{2}\mathsf{C}B_{a}^{i}\mathsf{S}\partial_{t}B_{ai}+\frac{2i}{\hbar}\mathsf{S}B_{r}^{i}\mathsf{C}\partial_{t}B_{ri}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\tilde{\Theta}\bigg[\mathsf{C}B_{a}^{i}\mathsf{C}\partial_{t}B_{ri}-\mathsf{S}B_{r}^{i}\mathsf{S}\partial_{t}B_{ai}-\frac{i\hbar}{2}\mathsf{S}B_{a}^{i}\mathsf{C}\partial_{t}B_{ai}+\frac{2i}{\hbar}\mathsf{S}B_{r}^{i}\mathsf{C}\partial_{t}B_{ri}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\mathsf{S}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{i\hbar}{2}B_{a}^{i}\partial_{t}B_{ai}}}\right)\bigg],

\displaystyle\mathrm{K}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{i}{\beta}B^{i}_{a}\mathfrak{D}B_{ai}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\tilde{\Theta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{i\hbar}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathsf{C}B_{a}^{i}+\frac{2i}{\hbar}\mathsf{S}B^{i}_{r}}}\right)\frac{\mathsf{C}}{\mathsf{S}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathsf{C}\partial_{t}B_{ai}+\frac{2i}{\hbar}\mathsf{S}\partial_{t}B_{ri}}}\right)}}\right]

\displaystyle=\tilde{\Theta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{i\hbar}{2}\mathsf{C}B_{a}^{i}\frac{\mathsf{C}^{2}}{\mathsf{S}}\partial_{t}B_{ai}-\mathsf{C}B_{a}^{i}\mathsf{C}\partial_{t}B_{ri}-\mathsf{S}B^{i}_{r}\frac{\mathsf{C}^{2}}{\mathsf{S}}\partial_{t}B_{ai}-\frac{2i}{\hbar}\mathsf{S}B^{i}_{r}\mathsf{C}\partial_{t}B_{ri}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\tilde{\Theta}\bigg[\frac{i\hbar}{2}\mathsf{C}B_{a}^{i}\frac{\mathsf{C}^{2}}{\mathsf{S}}\partial_{t}B_{ai}-\mathsf{C}B_{a}^{i}\mathsf{C}\partial_{t}B_{ri}+\mathsf{C}B^{i}_{r}\mathsf{C}\partial_{t}B_{ai}-\frac{2i}{\hbar}\mathsf{S}B^{i}_{r}\mathsf{C}\partial_{t}B_{ri}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\mathsf{S}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(B^{i}_{r}\frac{\mathsf{C}}{\mathsf{S}}\partial_{t}B_{ai}}}\right)\bigg].

(3.8)

Adding the two results, we find

\displaystyle\mathrm{K}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({-}B^{i}_{a}\partial_{t}B_{ri}+\frac{i}{\beta}B^{i}_{a}\mathfrak{D}B_{ai}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\tilde{\Theta}\bigg[\mathsf{C}\Big(B^{i}_{r}\partial_{t}B_{ai}\Big)+\mathsf{C}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{i\hbar}{2}B_{a}^{i}\frac{\mathsf{C}}{\mathsf{S}}\partial_{t}B_{ai}}}\right)\bigg]+\partial_{t}(\ldots)

\displaystyle=\tilde{\Theta}\bigg[B^{i}_{r}\partial_{t}B_{ai}+\frac{i}{\beta}B_{a}^{i}\mathfrak{D}B_{ai}\bigg]+\partial_{t}(\ldots)

\displaystyle=\tilde{\Theta}\bigg[{-}B_{ai}\partial_{t}B^{i}_{r}+\frac{i}{\beta}B_{a}^{i}\mathfrak{D}B_{ai}\bigg]+\partial_{t}(\ldots).

(3.9)

Therefore, the
\sigma_{0}
terms are also invariant under KMS for any choice of
\Theta
.

3.2
Non-linear effective action

The full interacting version of the action is slightly trickier to guess. One can convince themselves that simply promoting the coefficients in
eq.

3.2
to be generic functions of
B_{rt}
does not work.
However, we can use the following trick. For some function
F(f)
defined over a single copy of fields, let us define the average-difference combinations

\displaystyle F(f)_{r}=\frac{F(f_{1})+F(f_{2})}{2}

\displaystyle=\cosh\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\hbar}{2}f_{a}\frac{\partial}{\partial f_{r}}}}\right)F(f_{r})

\displaystyle=F(f_{r})+\frac{\hbar^{2}}{8}f_{a}^{2}F^{\prime\prime}(f_{r})+\ldots,

\displaystyle F(f)_{a}=\frac{F(f_{1})-F(f_{2})}{\hbar}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\hbar}\sinh\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\hbar}{2}f_{a}\frac{\partial}{\partial f_{r}}}}\right)F(f_{r})

\displaystyle=f_{a}F^{\prime}(f_{r})+\frac{\hbar^{2}}{24}f_{a}^{3}F^{\prime\prime\prime}(f_{r})+\ldots.

(3.10)

Note also that

\displaystyle\Big(F(f)G(f)\Big)_{r}

\displaystyle=F(f)_{r}G(f)_{r}+\frac{\hbar^{2}}{4}F(f)_{a}G(f)_{a},

\displaystyle\Big(F(f)G(f)\Big)_{a}

\displaystyle=F(f)_{r}G(f)_{a}+F(f)_{a}G(f)_{r}.

(3.11)

It is easy to check that the KMS transformations of
F(f)_{r,a}
take the same form in
eq.

2.16
, i.e.

\displaystyle\mathrm{K}F(f)_{r}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\Theta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\cos\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\hbar\beta\partial_{t}}{2}}}\right)F(f)_{r}+\frac{i\hbar}{2}\sin\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\hbar\beta\partial_{t}}{2}}}\right)F(f)_{a}}}\right],

\displaystyle\mathrm{K}F(f)_{a}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\Theta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\cos\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\hbar\beta\partial_{t}}{2}}}\right)F(f)_{a}+\frac{2i}{\hbar}\sin\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\hbar\beta\partial_{t}}{2}}}\right)F(f)_{r}}}\right].

(3.12)

Therefore, we can recycle the derivations in
section

3.1
to show that the combinations

\displaystyle\mathfrak{L}_{1}[F(B)]

\displaystyle=F(B)_{a},

\displaystyle\mathfrak{L}_{2}[F(B),G(B)]

\displaystyle=iF(B)_{a}G(B)_{a}-\frac{1}{\hbar}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(F(B)_{a}\frac{\mathsf{S}}{\mathsf{C}}G(B)_{r}+G(B)_{a}\frac{\mathsf{S}}{\mathsf{C}}F(B)_{r}}}\right),

(3.13)

is invariant under KMS for any functions
F
and
G
.
In fact, the free terms in the previous subsection can be viewed as special cases of these combinations

\displaystyle\mathfrak{L}_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{1}{2}\chi_{0}B_{t}^{2}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\chi_{0}B_{at}B_{rt},

\displaystyle\mathfrak{L}_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{1}{\beta}\sigma_{0}B^{i},\mathfrak{D}B_{i}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{\beta}\sigma_{0}B_{a}^{i}\mathfrak{D}B_{ai}-\frac{1}{\hbar\beta}\sigma_{0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(B_{a}^{i}\frac{\mathsf{S}}{\mathsf{C}}\mathfrak{D}B_{ri}+\mathfrak{D}B^{i}_{a}\frac{\mathsf{S}}{\mathsf{C}}B_{ai}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{0}B_{a}^{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{i}{\beta}\mathfrak{D}B_{ai}-\partial_{t}B_{ri}}}\right)+\partial_{t}(\ldots).

(3.14)

Note that
g(\mathfrak{D}f)=(\mathfrak{D}g)f+\partial_{t}(\ldots)
. Having been constructed in terms of the invariants
B_{r,a\mu}
, the combinations in
eq.

3.13
are manifestly invariant the global U(1) symmetries. They also trivially satisfy the first two unitarity constraints in
eq.

2.9
.
However, we will need to verify the third unitarity constraint and the diagonal shift symmetry on case-by-case basis.

Thermodynamic density term:

Having identified the invariants in
eq.

3.13
, lifting the thermodynamic density term to the quantum level is quite straightforward. We can simply take

GGL regularisation:

Note that the interaction vertices outlined above carry factors of frequencies. Consequently, the frequency integrals inside loops typically diverge. As such, this is typical feature of interacting field theories and warrants a careful regularisation prescription to tame such integrals. However, the issue is more subtle in SK-EFTs because improper regularisation may lead to misleading unphysical results like unstable poles in the retarded correlation functions and nonzero expectation values of noise correlation functions
[
10
]
(see also
[
24
,
13
]
).

The authors in
[
25
]
proposed a specific regularisation scheme to circumvent these issues, which we will call GGL (Gao-Glorioso-Liu) regularisation. Therein, the denominator
\Delta(p)
in the “
ra
” propagator in
eq.

4.3
is replaced by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Delta(p)}\to\frac{1}{\Delta_{\text{GGL}}(p)}=\frac{1}{\Delta(p)(1-i\omega/\Lambda_{\omega})^{N}},

(4.12)

for some positive integer
N
and a large non-negative frequency cutoff
\Lambda_{\omega}
. All the frequency loop integrals can be rendered finite by choosing a large enough
N
. We can remove the cutoff at the end of our calculations by sending
\Lambda_{\omega}\to\infty
.
To be consistent with the KMS symmetry, we need to also simultaneously regularise the “
rr
” propagator.
We find that the respective denominator
\Delta_{\mathrm{S}}(p)
in
eq.

4.3
get regularised as

\displaystyle\frac{{\bf k}^{2}\sigma_{0}}{\Delta_{\mathrm{S}}(p)}\to\frac{{\bf k}^{2}\sigma_{0}}{\Delta_{\mathrm{S},\text{GGL}}(p)}=\frac{{\bf k}^{2}\sigma_{0}\Re(1-i\omega/\Lambda_{\omega})^{p}+\omega\chi\Im(1-i\omega/\Lambda_{\omega})^{p}}{\big|\Delta(p)(1-i\omega/\Lambda_{\omega})^{p}\big|^{2}}.

(4.13)

Note that the GGL regularisation preserves the following splitting property of the
\Delta_{\mathrm{S}}(p)

\displaystyle G^{\mathrm{R}}_{nn}(p)

Outlook

In this work, we have derived a quantum-complete SK-EFT for the hydrodynamics of a single scalar charge, consistent with quantum FDT requirements, by enforcing the KMS symmetry at nonzero
\hbar
. In the classical limit, the KMS symmetry only enforces fluctuations at quadratic order in the noise fields in the SK effective action. However, upon including quantum corrections, we find that the KMS symmetry generates fluctuation terms at all orders in the noise fields. We have used our theory to calculate one-loop quantum corrections to the two-point retarded correlation function of the charge density at arbitrary order in
\hbar
. In doing so, we have evaluated quantum corrections to hydrodynamic long-time tails in the diffusion model at the one-loop level. While our general result is expressed as a sum over a set of polynomials, we have also been able to find a closed-form expression for the quantum corrections at small wavevector.

The polynomials
\mathcal{P}_{d,2n}(z)
in our results are defined through specific wavevector integrals. However, at the time of writing, we have not been able to identify these polynomials in terms of standard families of polynomials in the literature. It would be quite interesting to make progress on this front. Such an identification may also help us to resum the results in
eq.

1.15
for finite wavevector and thus probe the full analytic structure of the quantum corrections. Furthermore, it will be useful to extend these computations to higher-loop orders and to include higher-derivative corrections in the diffusion equation.

We have focused on a simple hydrodynamic model of a diffusive scalar conserved charge. An immediate generalization of this work would be to derive the quantum-complete SK effective action for full hydrodynamics, including fluid velocity and momentum fluctuations. For classical long-time tails, it is known that the non-analytic corrections arising from shear viscosity enter the retarded correlation functions earlier in the derivative expansion as compared to the diffusion model
[
21
]
. In view of this, it will be interesting to explore the analytic structure of quantum corrections in full hydrodynamics including momentum modes, based on the works of
[
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
]
, as well as extensions to non-relativistic hydrodynamics
[
26
,
27
]
. In this regard, it will also be interesting to investigate the stability and causality issues of relativistic hydrodynamics in the quantum-complete SK-EFT paradigm
[
15
]
.

We mentioned in the main text that the SK effective action we have derived is likely not unique as a quantum completion of the diffusion model. More generally, SK-EFTs may admit non-universal fluctuation terms that are not constrained by the KMS symmetry
[
18
]
. It will be of interest to explore how this non-universality behaves in the presence of quantum fluctuations. Regardless, it is known that FDTs, or equivalently the KMS symmetry, are sufficiently constraining to entirely fix the SK-EFT effective action up to cubic interactions. Since our result for quantum corrections to the retarded correlation function and quantum long-time tails is only sensitive to cubic couplings, our conclusions are therefore robust under such non-universalities.

As mentioned in the introduction, quantum fluctuations are particularly important in theories or models that lack any intrinsic scale of their own, such as conformal field theories. In this context, holography, or the AdS/CFT correspondence
[
28
]
, offers an interesting paradigm for testing the predictions of our model. In particular, it may be possible to derive the quantum-complete version of the SK effective action using holography, following the work of
[
29
,
30
]
.

As a final remark, we note that we have used the GGL prescription to regulate frequency loop integrals in the SK-EFT
[
25
]
. A more systematic approach to regularization in SK-EFTs involves introducing additional ghost fields to counter the divergences. This approach also enables us to read off interesting cutoff-dependent results needed to study non-equilibrium RG flows, which are identically set to zero under GGL regularization. In the classical limit, the ghost part of the SK effective action is determined by an emergent BRST supersymmetry
[
31
,
32
]
. However, it is not clear at the moment whether such a symmetry principle extends to the quantum level. As a future direction, it will be interesting to identify the ghost contributions to the quantum-complete SK effective action, which may be used to regulate frequency integrals instead of the GGL prescription. We leave these investigations for future work.
