Insights into the genetic basis of natural selection and domestication from Sorghum
Authors: Hui Guo, Dong Zhang, Xiyin Wang, Tae-ho Lee, Andrew H Paterson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAnalysis of gene sequences showing signatures of selection can provide insights into the evolution and domestication of an organism and can suggest ways to accelerate crop improvement. Using whole-genome resequencing and RNA-seq-derived SNP data from 69 diverse sorghum accessions, we characterized genetic variation and identified genes showing evidence of potentially important roles in sorghum domestication and adaptation. Sorghum genes showing evidence of recent selection based on fixation index (Fst) include Sb02g037735, a protease inhibitor functioning in seed storage; and Sb02g029450, with a double-stranded RNA binding motif. Ten genes showing evidence of selective sweeps include 4 ion transporters that all are located in likelihood intervals for QTLs related to drought resilience of sorghum. Of 15 genes containing 16 deleterious SNPs fixed for different alleles between domesticated and wild sorghums, significantly low Tajima’s D indicates recent selection in two apoptotic ATPase-containing NB-ARC domain genes, signaling proteins involved in disease resistance and regulation of cell death. A group of 22 genes show evidence of convergent selection in sorghum, maize and rice. Integration of selection signatures, large-effect mutations, gene functional annotations, and positional (QTL) information suggests a small number of strong candidate genes involved in sorghum domestication and adaptation, while also identifying a broader set of plausible candidates. Comparison of genes under selection in sorghum, rice, and maize supports both convergent and divergent selection patterns during cereal evolution and domestication.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55629-z.Keywords: Sorghum, Natural selection, Domestication, SNP, QTL, CerealSubject terms: Biotechnology, Genetics, Molecular biology, Plant sciences

Introduction

Sorghum, Sorghum bicolor (L.) Moench, is one of the world’s leading cereal crops. It serves as a staple food in Africa and India, and major livestock feed in America. The grains of sorghum have been used for making wine and gluten-free food. Quick-growing and high-biomass sorghum is under study as a possible energy crop. A particularly desirable feature about sorghum for both food and energy production is its ability to grow in warm climates with less water than many other crops, which makes it a priority for research into agricultural resilience to global climate change.

Sorghum was domesticated in northeast Africa at least 8000 years ago1–4. It has been spread to the Middle East, India, China and America. With adaptation to new geographic areas, novel morphological and physiological traits can arise, such as the change of flowering time in temperate germplasm to day length neutrality.

Domesticated sorghum is considered to include five botanical races: Caudatum, Guinea, Bicolor, Durra and Kafir. The five races differ from each other in morphological features5. Race caudatum is widely dispersed in the region where sorghum is thought to have first been domesticated. Race bicolor is the most widely dispersed in Africa and retains primitive morphological features resembling wild sorghum. The diffusion pattern of the races have been extensively studied morphologically6 and genetically3,7,8.

The domestication of sorghum led to drastic changes in its morphology from its wild ancestors. Major quantitative trait loci (QTLs) for important agronomical traits have been extensively studied9–13. However, only a few genes have been cloned to date. Recently, whole genome association study by reduced representation sequencing of genomic DNA has been used to discover novel QTLs3,14. Deep whole genome sequencing of a population is a powerful tool for studying evolutionary history of a population, identifying selective sweeps in the genome and elucidating genomic evolution.

This study integrates whole-genome resequencing data from 26 accessions, RNA-seq-based SNP data from 40 accessions, and sequence data from three additional published accessions from Asia. Together, these 69 accessions broadly sample the genetic diversity of Sorghum bicolor, including 61 cultivated accessions from the five botanical races, five wild accessions from two subspecies (S. drummondii and S. verticilliflorum), and three outgroup species (S. propinquum, S. timorense, and S. versicolor). We systematically searched these data for genes and other genomic features associated with sorghum adaptation and domestication. Combined with previously published whole-genome association results, this framework was used to prioritize candidate genes for several domestication-related traits.

Results

Population and haplotype structure of sorghum

The consensus sequences of single copy genes from each accession were used to construct phylogenetic trees of the sorghum genotypes studied (Fig. 1A). Three outgroup species and two wild accessions mark the root of the tree. Another three wild accessions are closely related to one Caudatum, two Guinea and one Guinea-Caudatum accession(s), perhaps suggesting the ancestral origin of Caudatum and Guinea races, strong gene flow between the two races and wild ancestors, or misidentification. Principal component analysis (PCA) shows that most Caudatum and Guinea races do not cluster with the wild accessions (Fig. 1B). The analysis of population structure confirms that there is strong genetic admixture not only between Caudatum, Guinea and wild races, but also among the five domesticated races (Fig. 1C). Based on the inference from Fig. 1C that there are 7 subpopulations (k = 7), wild type (blue), Caudatum (red) and Guinea (magenta) are frequently found together in mosaic genotypes. Races Guinea and Kafir show little admixture, but races Bicolor and Durra show a high level of genetic admixture with other races. The complex history of diffusion and selection in sorghum may confound phylogenetic inference.

Pairwise Fst was calculated among the five sorghum races (Fig. 1D). There are 595 genes that have Fst larger than 0.8 between at least one pair of races (Supplemental Table S1). The most diverged races are Kafir and Guinea based on the median Fst values of all genes. Indeed, Kafir is relatively distant from any other races genetically, consistent with its geographical distribution. Genes with singularly large Fst include Sb02g037735, a protease inhibitor functioning in seed storage that is extensively diverged between Guinea and at least three other races; and Sb02g029450, with a double-stranded RNA binding motif that is diverged between durra and three other races. Such large divergence (Fst) between one race and three other races suggests that these genes might have been under selection in that single race (Sb02g037735 in Guinea, Sb02g029450 in Durra).

Linkage disequilibrium (LD) measures the level of non-random association between alleles, which can be influenced by natural selection, effective population size, geographic subdivision and mating patterns15. We characterized LD in sorghum using sliding windows of 200 kb in size to characterize each region in the genome (Fig. 2A). In each window, pairwise single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were used to train the LD function proposed by Hill and Weir16. LD in each window is represented by markers that are separated by 3000 bp. Regions with the highest LD mostly reside in heterochromatin. Continuous windows with large LD are observed in several regions with known domestication loci: 59 Mb on chr9 (Dw1), 12 Mb on chr6 (Dw4), 42 Mb on chr6 (Dw2), 54 Mb on chr7 (Dw3) and 33 Mb on chr6 (Ma1). Several novel loci with large LD are also identified: 28 Mb on chr3, 13 Mb on chr7 and 23 Mb on chr10.

Using the Hill and Weir model, genome wide LD decreases to < 0.1 for physical distances larger than 3000 bp (Fig. 2B). This level of LD is two times larger than what is found in its close relative, maize17, perhaps due to a generally higher rate of inbreeding in sorghum than maize. In Fig. 2A, genomic regions with LD significantly higher than 0.1 are more likely to contain genes or other regulatory elements that are subject to selection, as explored below.

Unusual genetic features of drought resistance genes

Drought resistance is a widespread feature of Sorghum bicolor, implying that the genetic loci controlling this trait may have been fixed for associated alleles long before domestication. To identify genes showing evidence consistent with drought-related adaptation, we focused on regions that combined strong non-neutrality statistics (Tajima’s D and Fu and Li’s statistics) with elevated LD. We identified 62 LD regions significantly larger than could be explained by random factors (LD > 0.30, FDR = 0.05) as shown in Fig. 2A, containing 330 genes (Supplemental Table S2). Tajima’s D, Fu & Li’s D* and Fu & Li’s F* of each annotated gene in the sorghum genome were calculated, with P-values for each gene determined by coalescent simulation of a neutrally evolved population 10000 times using the ms program18. We aimed to match simulations to the real sorghum population as closely as possible by using values calculated from our empirical dataset as simulation parameters. The significance threshold of Tajima’s D was estimated at -1.729 (FDR = 0.05), with 21 of the 330 genes in the large LD regions showing genetic diversity lower than this threshold. Among these 21 genes, 10 have putative functions, including 4 ion transporters, 4 Zinc finger related DNA binding genes, one cellulose synthesis related gene (1,3-beta-glucan synthase) and one branching related gene (Table 1). Ion transporters are involved in salt and drought tolerance signal pathways19, and all four that we found are located in likelihood intervals for drought-related QTLs, albeit which collectively account for ~ 50% of the sorghum genome20. One of them, Sb03g034980 (chr3:~63.15 Mb), locates in one (Stg1, chr3:61-66 Mb, covering ~ 0.6% of the genome) of the four ‘stay green’ QTLs reported by three independent studies21–23, which explains 13–20% of phenotypic variation (Stg1). Figure 3A shows detailed pairwise LD in a 100Kb region around one transporter gene (Sb03g014130). Five other transporter genes are located in large LD regions and some shows evidence of recent selection by Tajima’s D, Fu&Li’ D* and Fu&Li’s F* statistics (Supplemental Table S3). For one of them, Sb04g018050, a vacuolar sorting associated protein, flanking SNPs show LD that extends ~ 50Kb up- and down-stream (Fig. 3B). The LD decay near both Sb03g014130 and Sb04g018050 is much slower than the genome-wide average (Fig. 3C). LD and Tajima’s D for all annotated genes in the sorghum genome were calculated and the ones with large LD (> 0.3) are shown in Supplemental Table S4.

Domestication genes

We identified 3289 SNPs that are fixed in a sample of 61 domesticated sorghums but not in wild sorghum accessions (Supplemental Table S6). Among these, 1442 are in intergenic regions. Among the 1847 fixed SNPs in 1311 genes, 575 are in CDS (318 nonsynonymous, 257 synonymous), 58 in 3’-UTR, 14 in 5’-UTR, and 1197 in introns. Function enrichment analysis of the 1311 genes shows that kinase activity (P = 0.0097), cell organization and biogenesis (P = 0.0098) and function in plastids (P = 0.0005) are significantly over-represented. Sb03g030460, a transcription factor with a nuclear LIM domain, contains the largest number of SNPs9 differentiating domesticated and wild types, however all are intronic. Sb08g000920, an FDB-associated F-box gene, contains 8 SNPs in the coding region, five of which change amino acids. Sb07g020170, a protein disulfide isomerase, contains 2 SNPs in the 3’ CDS region (both nonsynonymous) and 5 in the 3’-UTR. One nonsynonymous SNP causes premature protein termination in the wild type, indicating gain of function of the domesticated allele or very recent (post-domestication) loss of function in the wild type. Sb04g034610, a multi-copper oxidase, contains 7 SNPs in the coding region -- although 6 are nonsynonymous, significantly low Tajima’s D (-1.80) indicates recent selection on this gene.

There are 16 deleterious SNPs in 15 genes that are fixed for different alleles between domesticated and wild sorghums including the three outgroups (Supplemental Table S7). Significant low Tajima’s D provides further evidence that two of the genes have been under recent selection. Both of the genes are apoptotic ATPase-containing NB-ARC domain genes, signaling proteins involved in disease resistance and regulation of cell death. In the 2937 bp coding region of Sb05g026180, an “A” to “T” mutation in the 945th nucleotide leads to a stop codon at 315 amino acids in wild accessions. Similarly, the Sb03g014426 protein has 1619 amino acids in length while the wild type has premature termination at 1047 amino acids. Sb04g022890, a putative voltage gated chloride channel important for maintaining safe ion concentrations within cells, shows marginally significant Tajima’s D, with a G to T mutation that leads to a stop codon in domesticated S. bicolor.

We identified 1863 high frequency ( > = 90%) derived SNPs in the domesticated sorghums using monomorphic alleles among the five wild S. bicolor genotypes and three outgroup species as ancestral (Supplemental Table S8), with 1161 (62%) located within genes. A total of 518 are in coding regions (252 synonymous, 266 nonsynonymous), 73 in 3’-UTRs, 12 in 5’-UTRs and 702 in introns. The gene that contains the largest number10 of high frequency derived SNPs is Sb05g026180, the apoptotic ATPase with an NB-ARC domain showing significantly low Tajima’s D (-2.49739). Sb10g022490, an endosomal transmembrane protein, contains 6 high frequency derived SNPs (2 CDS, 4 intronic) and shows significantly reduced nucleotide diversity (Tajimas’ D = -2.45786). Sb10g002500 also contains 6 high frequency derived SNPs (5 in CDS), one causing extension of the gene in wild types (Tajima’s D = -2.23646). Also interesting is Sb03g014426, another apoptotic ATPase with NB-ARC domain, with significantly reduced nucleotide diversity (Tajima’s D = -2.35893), and which contains the largest number6 of high frequency SNPs in the CDS region. One of them leads to a premature stop codon at the 840th amino acid.

There are 14 genes containing high frequency derived SNPs that are deleterious (Supplemental Table S8), three of which show reduced nucleotide diversity. Two genes (Sb03g014426 and Sb10g002500) contain relatively large numbers of high-frequency SNPs (6 each) and significantly low Tajima’s D (-2.36 and − 2.24, respectively). The other one, Sb02g038050, contains 3 high-frequency derived SNPs and shows significantly reduced level of nucleotide diversity. It is a Ras-gap SH3 domain binding protein, which is an ubiquitin-specific protease.

Deleterious mutations and their origin

Deleterious mutation is enriched in the diversification of genes during the domestication process24–28. In total, we found 28,457 deleterious SNPs in 13,090 genes. These SNPs fall into in four categories: premature stop codon (13928, 48.9%), translation extension (9324, 32.8%), alternative-splicing site (4819, 16.9%) and change of translation initiation site (386, 1.4%). A total of 14,510 deleterious SNPs in 9066 genes are polymorphic in the domesticated sorghum genotypes, including 6963 (48%) premature stop codons, 4199 (28.9%) alternative splicings, 3068 (21.1%) translation extensions and 280 (1.9%) changes of translation initiation site. A remarkable 87% of the SNPs that disrupt alternative splicing sites are polymorphic in the domesticated populations, which is much higher than other types of striking mutations.

Genes with deleterious SNPs that are polymorphic in the domesticated sorghum population show non-random associations with functional groups, using GO-slim annotation from Arabidopsis. A significant number of genes with nonsense mutations (stop codon and stop codon extension) are located in the cell membrane, implicated in response to stress and have kinase activity (P < 0.01). Genes with SNPs that alter splicing motifs are significantly enriched in structural and transporter function categories (Supplemental Table S9). Genes that respond to stress have high turnover rates due to the ongoing ‘arms race’ with pathogens and change of environment. It is surprising to see many SNPs that disrupt splicing motifs enriched in structural and transporter genes in the domesticated sorghum genotypes.

Domestication genes with phenotypes

Previously published GWAS results from 354 accessions in a sorghum association panel (SAP), genotyped for 265,487 SNPs3,29,30, were integrated with the resequencing-based candidate analyses to identify genomic regions that contribute to inflorescence morphology (seed size and awn presence), plant height and flowering time. Since reduced-representation genotyping-by-sequencing was used to genotype this population, the 265,487 SNPs have limited power to pinpoint causative gene candidates directly (about 72% of annotated genes contain > = 1 SNP site). We therefore used prior functional hotspots identified by GWAS and QTL mapping together with the high-density genotype information in this study to prioritize candidate genes for important domestication-related traits. Supplemental Table S10 shows a list of candidate genes in strong linkage with the SNPs that are significantly associated with a number of domestication traits, as described below.

Seed size

Seed size is a key trait in most plants31, which is closely related to fitness, ecology and domestication. Larger seeds contain more nutrition and are easier to harvest and process for human uses, but in the wild are harder to disperse, sometimes germinate later, and penetrate soil poorly. Small seed size is sometimes associated with weediness32.

A total of 98 genes in large LD regions that also show significant GWAS markers for seed size had reduced nucleotide diversity. We further narrowed the 98 genes based on slow LD decay within the 200 kb flanking region, containing SNPs that only differ between domesticated and wild types and containing high-frequency derived SNPs in the domesticated genotypes, finding five genes (Supplemental Table S11). Sb06g002950 is Subtilisin/kexin protease, which is related to development and protein turnover. The gene has extended LD, low nucleotide diversity (Tajima’s D = -2.0251, FDR = 0.01) and is in a large LD region as a significant marker related to seed mass. Sb06g014960, a K+ transporter, has four SNPs segregating between domesticated and wild types and one high frequency derived SNPs in the protein-coding region. This gene is associated with both seed mass and flowering time. Sb06g014970 encodes a DNA repair protein showing significantly reduced nucleotide diversity (Tajima’s D = -2.0273, FDR = 0.01). Sb04g012680, a HAT transposase, contains 3 high-frequency derived SNPs in the protein-coding region and is related to seed mass. The only gene related to seed length, Sb07g022890, is a carbonic anhydrase, which is a key factor for fixing CO2 in photosynthesis.

Flowering time & plant height

The new genome sequence data permitted us to build upon prior findings using reduced-representation genotyping by sequencing, to identify a strong candidate for a sorghum height gene. Morris et al. identified four markers significantly associated with plant height and three regions significantly associated with flowering time3,20. There are 2030 genes in the regions associated with plant height and 1144 in the regions associated with flowering. We ordered phenotypic values for each trait in our resequenced sorghum genotypes and chose extreme phenotypes for identification of causal SNPs. For flowering time, we chose 5 accessions that flower in less than 55 days (SC55, SC301, SC855, SC265, Ajabsido (SAP-16) and 4 accessions that flower at least after 80 days since sowing (Cowley (SAP-16), P898012 (SAP-93), M35-1 (IS 1054), SC35). For plant height, we chose 5 accessions that are shorter than 80 cm (SC55, SC121, SC110, Tx399, SC704) and 5 accessions that are taller than 145 cm (CSM63E, Cowley (SAP-48), SC52, M35-1 (IS 1054), P898012 (SAP-93)). Phenotypic data are from a US sorghum association panel (SAP) from three different growouts29,30. We identified nonsynonymous SNPs (nsSNPs) that differ between the two groups of accessions, finding 130 nsSNPs in 71 genes for flowering time and 1060 nsSNPs in 418 genes for plant height. Five genes that are closest to the significance peak and contain the largest number of causal nsSNPs are highlighted in yellow and blue, respectively, in Supplemental Table S11. One gene (Sb09g028330) that is 3494 bp from the peak GWAS association with plant height on chromosome 9 (dw1) is a tubby family protein and contains 3 nsSNPs in the conserved tub domain that differ between short and tall accessions (Fig. 5). Tajima’s D of this gene (2.53) indicates balancing selection or divergence between subpopulations. Arabidopsis tubby mutant lines (AtTLP9) show abscisic (ABA) acid insensitivity, which suggests that Sb09g028330 may participate in the ABA signaling pathway.

Awn presence

Awns can aid in dispersing seed to a germination site, and the morphology of awns depends on the soil structure where the plant grows33. Specifically, awnless small seeds are prevalent in light sandy soils, while hairy seeded types tend to grow in structural soils, a mixture of gap-graded gravels, clay loam, and a hydrogel stabilizing agent that prevents the mixture from separating. Both awned and awnless types are found in the sorghum cultivated races. For example, durra has long awns as wild type, while caudatum and kafir have no awns. GWAS studies show a single peak (~ 72.7 Mb) on chromosome 330. Based on pairwise LD measurement (r2), the association region can extend from 68.9 Mb to 74.2 Mb on chromosome 3 and contains 670 genes. Genes involved in carbohydrate metabolism are significantly enriched6 in the 50 kb region flanking the peak, perhaps related to the observation that awns contribute significantly to the carbohydrate supply for seeds in awned inflorescences34.

We found 216 nsSNPs in 92 genes that differentiated wild-type durra (awned) from awnless kafir, and caudatum. Sb03g45420, a hexokinase that phosphorylates hexoses, is ~ 21 kb from the peak and contains three nsSNPs, two located in the hexokinase 1 domain. Three genes have been identified as regulating awn length in rice35,36 including An-1, a bHLH transcription factor; DROOPING LEAF (DL), a YABBY family member; and OsETT2, an auxin responsive factor. However, we found different enzymes in the carbohydrate metabolism pathway to be enriched in the < 50 kb region flanking the significant peak in sorghum. This suggests diverged major effectors regulating development of awns in sorghum and rice.

Comparison of domestication genes in sorghum, rice and maize

Correspondence of QTL data originally suggested the hypothesis that independent domestication of sorghum, rice, maize and other cereals may have involved parallel (convergent) changes in some common gene families11. To place the sorghum results in a broader cereal context, we also examined previously published SNP datasets representing diverse domesticated and wild maize and rice accessions37,38. We found 3289 SNPs (1311 genes) in sorghum, 8947 SNPs (104 genes) in rice and 53,257 SNPs (1585 genes) in maize that have fixed different alleles between wild and domesticated accessions, including 22 homologous groups of genes (Supplemental Table S12). Disease resistance genes and kinases that mediate signal transduction are the most abundant functional groups in the 22 homologous groups, followed by enzymes involved in carbohydrate metabolism. For example, a gene (AC195587.4_FGT004) in maize producing stripe rust resistance protein Yr10, its homologs in sorghum (Sb05g021460) and in rice (Os11g0676050), all contain fixed SNPs between domesticated and wild genotypes.

Genes showing significantly small Tajima’s D (as compared to neutral evolution) may have experienced recent positive selection in domesticated populations of the three species. We identified 1904, 1056, and 1910 such genes in sorghum, rice and maize, respectively. Analysis of GO slim terms found significant function enrichment for cell organization and biogenesis in all three species; electron transport or energy pathways in sorghum and rice; nucleotide binding in maize and rice; and transferase in sorghum (Supplemental Table S13). Ten orthogroups that contain at least one gene in each of sorghum, maize and rice showing significantly reduced nucleotide diversity (Tajima’s D < -1.8) are identified (Supplemental Table S14). Five of the ten orthogroups are signaling proteins. Genes in Orthogroup 23 encode apoptotic ATPase-containing NB-ARC domain proteins, involved in disease resistance and regulation of cell death. A gene in the group, Sb05g026180, also shows several lines of evidence that it might be a target for sorghum domestication, including significantly reduced nucleotide diversity (Tajima’s D = -2.5) and the largest number10 of genic high frequency ( > = 90%) derived SNPs found in domesticated sorghums. Other functions that are potentially under selection in sorghum, maize and rice are Potassium transporter, Apoptotic ATPase, Vesicle coat complex COPI, alpha subunit, Transferring glycosyl groups and Galactosyltransferases.

Domestication of starch metabolism

Large carbohydrate-rich seeds are a common feature selected during domestication of sorghum, rice and maize. We investigated starch synthesis genes for parallel signatures of selection among the three species. Important genes in the pathway have been well studied in maize39, and we identified orthologs based on sequence similarity and colinearity in sorghum and rice. Genes in the pathway show large variation of selection pressure among the three species as reflected by Tajima’s D, Fu & Li’s D* and Fu & Li’s F* statistics (Supplemental Table S15), with little correspondence among the species. For example, the sh2 and agp2 genes implicated in domestication and natural selection39,40 show evidence of recent positive selection in sorghum but not in maize and rice. While the sbe139 gene has been suggested to be under selection pressure in maize, its orthologs in sorghum and rice show no evidence of selection. Fixed SNPs between wild and domesticated accessions showed little relationship among the starch genes of the three species, with one in the intron of the sbe2b gene in sorghum, one in the intron of the zpu1 gene in maize, and none in rice starch synthesis genes. These lines evidence suggest selection of diverse genes in the starch metabolism pathway during the domestication of different cereal crops.

Discussion

Integration of selection signatures, large-effect mutations, gene annotation and positional (QTL) information strongly implicates a small number of genes in domestication and adaptation of sorghum, and also identifies a larger number of plausible candidates.

Our study adopted a bottom-up approach that identifies nucleotide differences between domesticated and wild accessions, with different results than prior studies. We identified 1847 SNPs that are fixed between domesticated and wild sorghum genotypes (Supplemental Table S6), located in 1311 genes with 575 in CDS (318 nonsynonymous and 257 synonymous). Genes with kinase activity (P = 0.0097), protein metabolism (P = 0.0086) and function in plastid (P = 0.0005) were significantly over-represented among the 1311 candidates. In contrast, 62% of an earlier sampling of 53 domestication genes were transcription factors, 23% were enzymes and 10% were transporters and ubiquitin ligases26, most of which were identified through QTL mapping. QTL mapping often samples only a small subset of alleles in a gene pool, and can miss many small effect loci or even large effect loci depending on sample size and population structure. Our bottom-up approach may mitigate these biases. In turn, however, some genes under domestication and diversification might be neutral or polymorphic in wild populations and would be missed by our analysis.

Sorghum was domesticated in northeast Africa at least 8000 years ago1–4 and its characteristic durability makes it considered the most drought resistant of the major cereal crops. Plant response to drought has been extensively studied, with ion transporter genes strongly associated with drought resistance19. We sought evidence of natural selection for drought resilience in sorghum by identifying genes showing evidence of selective sweeps, specifically with significant Tajima’s D and which also were located in genomic regions of large LD. Among the top 10 such genes, four are ion transporters (Table 1) and all four are located in likelihood intervals of drought-related QTLs20. One of them, Sb03g034980, a voltage and ligand gated potassium channel, locates in a QTL for Stg3 reported by three independent studies of the stay green phenotype21–23. Complementary to the analysis of selection features for single genes, a whole-genome scan identified 13 large regions showing reduced nucleotide divergence. The transporter genes in those regions (Supplemental Fig. 1) are good candidates for further experimental validation.

Several lines of evidence point to apoptotic ATPase-containing NB-ARC domain genes, signaling proteins involved in disease resistance and regulation of cell death, as being closely associated with sorghum domestication. Of 15 genes containing 16 deleterious SNPs fixed for different alleles between domesticated and wild sorghums, two apoptotic ATPase-containing NB-ARC domain genes show significant low Tajima’s D indicating recent selection (Supplemental Table S7). One of these, Sb05g026180, has significantly reduced nucleotide diversity (Tajima’s D = -2.5) and contains the largest number10 of 1161 genic high frequency ( > = 90%) derived SNPs found in domesticated sorghums. All 10 derived SNPs located in the protein-coding region. Sb03g014426, another apoptotic ATPase, has significantly reduced nucleotide diversity (Tajima’s D = -2.36) and the largest number6 of derived high frequency SNPs in the CDS region during domestication. Significantly reduced nucleotide diversity and large numbers of derived high frequency alleles strongly suggest that the two genes have been a target of sorghum domestication. Both genes contain a fixed mutation that elongates the open reading frame suggesting a gain of function in comparison to the five wild sorghum species. The recently fixed alleles during sorghum domestication may be a result of an arms-race between domesticated sorghum genotypes and pathogens. For example, new alleles for powdery mildew resistance genes (NBS-LRR) are merged after wheat domestication41, similar to the rice blast resistance gene (Pik)42.

Whole genome resequencing of modest numbers of diverse individuals coupled with reduced representation sequencing of large mapping populations that have been phenotyped, facilitates the identification of candidate genes. For plant height, we identified a candidate gene (Sb09g028330) for dw1. Sb09g028330 locates 3494 bp from the peak of GWAS-based association with plant height on chromosome 9 (dw1). Three nsSNPs differing between short and tall genotypes locate in the conserved tub domain of Sb09g028330 (Fig. 5). For awn presence, six genes that belong to the carbohydrate metabolism pathway are within 50Kb of the most significant marker. Sb03g45420, a hexokinase that is ~ 21 kb from the peak, contains three nsSNPs, two located in the hexokinase 1 domain. Meta-analysis of two different datesets and integrating multiple sources of information can provide insights to genes and/or pathways underlying a trait.

The comprehensive analysis of candidate genes for natural selection, domestication and drought resistance in sorghum produced a prioritized gene list based on the character of the SNPs within gene. These analyses provide a valuable source of candidates for trait discovery in sorghum. The genes list can be combined with QTL mapping or GWAS studies to prioritize candidate genes that are in a region associated with a trait.

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. First, the analysis relies on a set of high-confidence gene models from the reference annotation; individual gene structures should therefore be confirmed before targeted experimental validation. Second, the relatively small number of wild sorghum accessions may affect inference of fixed differences and candidate functional SNPs. Third, the combined use of resequencing and RNA-seq-derived SNP data improves genic variant discovery but also introduces dataset-specific biases, particularly because RNA-seq provides uneven coverage across expressed loci and was analyzed here for SNP discovery rather than transcript abundance. Finally, Fst analysis and neutrality statistics are sensitive to population structure, so candidates supported by multiple lines of evidence should be prioritized.

Conclusion

Integration of selection signatures, large-effect mutations, gene annotations and positional (QTL) information suggests a small number of strong candidate genes involved in sorghum domestication and adaptation, while also identifying a larger number of plausible candidates. Comparison of genes under selection in sorghum, rice and maize suggests both convergent and divergent selection patterns in the evolution and domestication of cereal crops. Functional validation and broader wild sampling will be required to confirm the specific roles of these candidate genes and pathways.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and sequence resources

Because this study integrates previously generated public datasets, a single experimental growth or sampling protocol does not apply to all accessions. Instead, the accession panel and accession-level archive identifiers are summarized in the Data Availability section, and the original studies are cited where the sequencing resources were first reported. The present study focuses on reanalysis of these sequence data for population-genetic inference rather than on new wet-lab generation of DNA, RNA, or expression measurements.

Read alignment and SNP calling

Reads were aligned to the Sorghum bicolor reference genome43 using BWA version 0.5.944. Multiple-sample SNP calling was performed using the mpileup program in the samtools package together with bcftools45. Reads with mapping quality score > = 25 and base quality > = 20 were used for SNP calling. Raw SNPs were further filtered according to read depth distribution to reduce paralog contamination and low-coverage artifacts. Each accession’s genotype was assigned using maximum-likelihood estimation from reads with coverage between 4x and 30x, and SNPs with minor allele frequency > = 0.01 were retained for downstream analysis.

RNA-seq datasets were included only for SNP discovery in expressed regions and not for gene-expression analysis. We recognize that RNA-seq differs from whole-genome resequencing in genomic coverage and that BWA is not splice-aware. Accordingly, RNA-seq-derived variants were interpreted conservatively and used to expand genic SNP discovery across the diversity panel rather than to make transcript-level inferences. The joint analysis therefore prioritizes candidate loci for follow-up validation, but does not eliminate platform-specific ascertainment bias.

We analyzed 69 accessions representing multiple sequence resources: whole-genome resequencing data for 26 sorghum accessions, RNA-seq data for 40 accessions, and sequence data for three previously published Asian accessions46. The panel included 61 cultivated S. bicolor accessions spanning the five botanical races, five wild S. bicolor accessions, and three outgroup species (Sorghum propinquum, Sorghum timorense, Sorghum versicolor). For publicly available datasets, plant growth, tissue collection, nucleic acid extraction, and library preparation followed the protocols reported in the original studies associated with each accession. In the present work, we reanalyzed the deposited reads within a unified SNP-discovery framework to enable comparative population-genetic analyses across the combined panel.

SNP annotation

A customized script is used to map all SNPs to the Sorghum bicolor gene model version 1.4. Striking SNPs are identified as those mapped to coding regions, splicing sites, stop codons and transcription initiation sites. The functional impact of nsSNPs is assessed based on the evolutionary conservation profile of amino acids. Orthologous groups of protein sequences from 30 plant species are constructed using OrthoMCL47. Protein sequences from each orthologous group are aligned using Clustalw248. Population structure, PCA, Fst, and candidate prioritization

Population parameters and neutrality statistics (Tajima’s D, Fu & Li’s D*, and Fu & Li’s F*) were calculated using libsequence C + + library v1.7.450. To determine significance thresholds, coalescent simulations of populations under neutral evolution were performed using the ms program18. Population size and number of segregating sites used in the simulation were matched to the empirical data as closely as possible, and the process was repeated 10,000 times. Tajima’s D significance thresholds were determined at the stated false discovery rates. PCA was performed on the filtered SNP genotype matrix to summarize major axes of genetic variation across accessions. Pairwise Fst among sorghum races was calculated at the gene level using SNPs located within annotated genes, and summary distributions were compared across all race pairs. Candidate genes and regions were prioritized by integrating multiple lines of evidence, including elevated LD, significant neutrality statistics, fixed or high-frequency derived SNPs, gene annotation, and overlap with previously reported QTL or GWAS intervals.

Inference of population structure and admixture

Population admixture was inferred using Structure, which assigns ancestry proportions for each accession across a predefined number of genetic clusters (K). We evaluated a series of K values and used the most interpretable solution, with the fewest mosaic genotypes and the clearest biological correspondence to the sampled accessions, for presentation in Fig. 1C. The five botanical races were retained as the primary biological classification, whereas the Structure analysis was used to summarize genetic admixture among them.

Linkage disequilibrium, GWAS integration, and comparative analysis

LD was characterized in 200 kb sliding windows across the genome. In each window, pairwise SNPs were used to fit the Hill and Weir16 model of LD decay, and LD values were summarized using markers separated by 3 kb. Previously published GWAS results from the sorghum association panel3,29,30 were integrated with the resequencing-based analyses to prioritize candidate genes within regions associated with seed size, awn presence, flowering time, and plant height. Published SNP data representing diverse maize and rice genotypes were analyzed in parallel for cross-species comparison of fixed differences, genes with reduced nucleotide diversity, and candidate pathways under selection37,38.

Construction of phylogenetic trees

The coding sequence of a gene from small gene families having < 6 copies and having no tandem copies was used to search the genome sequence of each involved lineage and the best BLAST hit region at stringent criteria was retrieved (E-value<1e-10). The retrieved orthologous regions of a gene in all lineages were aligned using CLUSTALW and the alignments of all genes were used to construct a concatenated alignment. At the mapping depth of at least 65, that is > = 65 lineages have nucleotide at a specific site in the alignment, 1721 genes contributed to produce the alignment of 306,267 bp. The concatenated alignment was used to construct phylogenetic trees using PhyML v3.051.

Function enrichment test

Function enrichment analysis was performed using custom scripts based on Arabidopsis GO-slim annotations assigned to sorghum genes by best BLASTP hit. Binomial tests were used with the proportion of each GO-slim term among all annotated sorghum genes as the null expectation. Test significant threshold is defined as P < 0.01, specified otherwise.
