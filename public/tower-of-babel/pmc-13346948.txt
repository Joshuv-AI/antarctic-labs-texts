Research on configuration path modeling of ESG performance in Chinese state-owned listed companies based on fsQCA
Authors: Jiayan Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBased on the perspective of institutional logic, this study explores the formation mechanism of environmental, social, and governance (ESG) performance of Chinese state-owned listed companies. Existing research often analyzes the influencing factors of ESG from a single dimension, failing to fully reveal the synergistic and conflicting effects of multiple institutional logics. This paper adopts the fsQCA method to analyze data from 387 A-share state-owned listed companies from 2015 to 2023, innovatively incorporating political logic as a structural embedded element into the analytical framework, and systematically examining the configurational effects of the four major logics: political, market, governance, and social. The study finds that enterprises can achieve high-level ESG performance through multiple paths such as “regulation-governance synergy” and “market-social drive,” while the absence or mismatch of institutional logics can lead to poor ESG performance. The research further reveals the differentiated performance of these configurational paths among central and local enterprises, as well as different industries. This study provides a new theoretical perspective for understanding the complex mechanisms of ESG governance in state-owned enterprises and has important implications for promoting ESG practices in state-owned enterprises in a classified manner.Keywords: State-owned enterprises, ESG performance, Institutional logic, FSQCA, Sustainable developmentSubject terms: Complex networks, Complex networks, Economics, Economics

Introduction

Environmental, social, and governance (ESG) performance has become an important indicator of corporate competitiveness and long-term resilience in the context of sustainable development and economic restructuring1. This issue is especially significant for Chinese state-owned enterprises (SOEs), which are expected not only to preserve and increase the value of state-owned assets, but also to support broader national goals in environmental protection, social stability, and governance modernization2. As a result, ESG practices in SOEs are shaped by multiple institutional demands rather than by a single market-oriented logic. Prior research has shown that ESG outcomes are closely related to institutional embeddedness, organizational capabilities, and external pressures, yet these influences do not operate in isolation3.

The complexity of ESG governance in Chinese SOEs can be better understood within the broader discussion of varieties of capitalism. While classic work distinguishes between liberal and coordinated market economies4, recent studies on state capitalism emphasize that SOEs often function simultaneously as market actors, policy implementers, and social stabilizers5. In China’s state-led market system, this hybrid role creates a distinctive configuration of political, market, governance, and social logics. Such logics may reinforce one another, but they may also conflict, leading firms facing similar external conditions to exhibit very different ESG outcomes6.

Existing studies have offered useful insights, but most rely on linear approaches that estimate the net effect of individual variables. This makes it difficult to explain why the same institutional condition may support strong ESG performance in one setting but correspond to weak performance in another. It also limits our ability to identify the combinations of conditions through which SOEs achieve high ESG performance, or fail to do so. To address this gap, this study draws on institutional logic theory and adopts fuzzy-set Qualitative Comparative Analysis (fsQCA), a method well suited to examining conjunctural causation and equifinality7.

Using data from 387 Chinese A-share state-owned listed companies from 2015 to 2023, this study addresses two questions: which combinations of institutional logics are associated with high ESG performance, and which combinations are associated with non-high ESG performance? It further examines whether these configurations vary across different ownership levels and industry contexts. By doing so, the study aims to provide a more concise and context-sensitive explanation of ESG governance in SOEs, while clarifying the structurally embedded role of political logic in China’s state-owned sector.

Literature review

Institutional logics and ESG in state-owned enterprises

Institutional logic theory provides a framework for understanding how competing institutional demands shape organizational behavior3. For Chinese state-owned enterprises, which operate under both state mandates and market pressures, this perspective is particularly relevant for analyzing their environmental, social, and governance performance8,9.

Prior research has examined the individual effects of specific institutional pressures. Political logic studies emphasize government regulation as a key driver of corporate environmental responsibility10. Market logic research focuses on how competition and financial constraints influence green innovation. Social logic analyses investigate stakeholder and media pressure on sustainability disclosure. While these studies offer valuable insights, they typically employ linear regression methods that cannot capture the complex interactions among multiple institutional forces11,12.

This methodological limitation creates a significant theoretical gap. Linear approaches fail to explain why state-owned enterprises operating under similar institutional conditions exhibit divergent ESG performance outcomes. More importantly, they cannot identify the specific combinations of conditions that enable some enterprises to achieve superior ESG results while others struggle.

The configurational approach in sustainability research

To address this limitation, management scholars have increasingly adopted configurational methods such as fuzzy-set Qualitative Comparative Analysis13,14. This approach recognizes that organizational outcomes often result from specific combinations of conditions rather than the independent effects of individual factors. Unlike traditional statistical methods that identify average net effects, fsQCA helps uncover multiple causal pathways to the same outcome, a concept known as equifinality15.

Recent applications of fsQCA in sustainability research have demonstrated its value. Studies have identified different configurations of external pressures, internal capabilities, and governance structures that lead to high corporate sustainability performance. For example, research on Chinese manufacturing firms has shown that both strong regulatory pressure combined with good governance, and strong market signals combined with stakeholder pressure, can lead to good ESG outcomes16,17.

However, existing configurational studies in the ESG domain have important limitations when applied to state-owned enterprises. First, they often treat government influence as one factor among many, rather than recognizing political logic as a fundamental, structurally embedded feature of state-owned enterprise governance. Second, few studies systematically compare configurations associated with both high and non-high performance, limiting our understanding of why similar conditions can lead to different outcomes. Third, there is limited research examining how these configurational patterns might vary across different types of state-owned enterprises.

Research gap and this study’s contribution

This study addresses these gaps by developing an integrated configurational framework that explicitly models four key institutional logics, political, market, governance, and social, in analyzing state-owned enterprise ESG performance18. We ask two specific research questions: What combinations of these institutional logics lead to high ESG performance in state-owned enterprises, and what combinations are associated with non-high performance? As a supplementary robustness and contextual applicability check, this study also examines whether the identified configurations remain broadly meaningful across enterprises with different ownership structures and industry contexts.

Although existing research has begun to utilize configurational methods to analyze corporate ESG performance, there are still three significant deficiencies. At the theoretical level, most studies simplify political logic into external environmental factors, failing to deeply explain its embedded characteristics within the governance structure of state-owned enterprises. At the methodological level, existing research primarily focuses on identifying high-performance paths, lacking comparative configurational analysis of high and non-high ESG performance. At the research object level, differentiated research on state-owned enterprises, as a special organizational form, remains weak. This study aims to address these deficiencies by constructing a structural embedding model that incorporates political logic and adopting a dual-path comparative research method, focusing on the configurational mechanisms of ESG performance in state-owned enterprises.

Our research makes several important contributions. First, we move beyond single-logic explanations to provide a more comprehensive understanding of how multiple institutional forces interact to shape sustainability outcomes. Second, by comparing both high and non-high performance configurations, we reveal the conditional nature of institutional influences, showing how the same logic can have different effects depending on its combination with other conditions. Third, our findings offer practical insights for developing more targeted, context-sensitive ESG governance strategies for different types of state-owned enterprises, as shown in Fig. 1.

By comparing the configurational patterns of high and non-high ESG performance, this paper provides a more comprehensive theoretical perspective for understanding the synergistic and conflicting effects of institutional logics.

Theoretical framework

This study develops a configurational theoretical framework to explain ESG performance in state-owned enterprises (SOEs) through the interaction of four institutional logics: political logic, market logic, governance logic, and social logic. Political logic emphasizes legitimacy and policy compliance; market logic stresses efficiency and competitive performance; governance logic concerns internal accountability and control; and social logic reflects reputational expectations and external scrutiny. In SOEs, these logics coexist because firms simultaneously bear state mandates and market responsibilities.

A central feature of this framework is the explicit treatment of political logic as a distinct and structurally embedded dimension of ESG governance. In SOEs, political logic is not simply an external background condition. Rather, it is embedded in ownership arrangements, party governance, and policy implementation mechanisms, which makes it qualitatively different from the other logics. For this reason, political logic should be analyzed directly within ESG configurations, rather than being treated only as a contextual influence. This approach allows the study to examine whether political logic operates as a source of support, complementarity, or tension in different causal combinations19,20.

Social logic originates from expectations and reputational pressures imposed by stakeholders such as the public and the media. In this study, it is operationalized through social supervision pressure, which reflects the intensity of external ESG-related scrutiny21. However, the four logics do not always align. Institutional complexity theory suggests that organizations facing multiple and potentially inconsistent logics must continually adapt to competing legitimacy demands. This is particularly relevant for SOEs as hybrid organizations positioned between state objectives, market discipline, and social expectations.

Accordingly, the interaction among logics should not be reduced to a simple opposition between synergy and conflict. Instead, it should be understood as a spectrum ranging from complementarity and balance to contradiction and decoupling. Some combinations may support effective ESG governance through mutually reinforcing arrangements, such as political-market coordination or governance-social alignment22,23. Other combinations may produce weak or symbolic ESG responses when political compliance, market efficiency, and organizational capabilities are poorly matched, resulting in strategic drift or decoupling24.

From this perspective, ESG performance in SOEs is best understood as the outcome of configurations rather than the net effect of isolated factors. This configurational view is especially useful because it can explain both the combinations associated with high ESG performance and those associated with non-high performance, thereby offering a more precise basis for the comparative analysis conducted in this study25.

Research hypothesis

A key theoretical contribution of this study is to distinguish political logic from generalized external context and to define it as a structurally embedded element of state-owned enterprises (SOEs). Through state ownership, party governance, and policy implementation mechanisms, political logic becomes an internal force shaping strategic decisions rather than a mere background condition. On this basis, the study focuses on how political logic may interact with other institutional logics in either complementary or conflicting ways, thereby influencing ESG performance.

Prior research suggests that different institutional logics may reinforce one another, such as the alignment between political pressure and market incentives26–28. However, fsQCA emphasizes conjunctural causation, meaning that outcomes result from specific combinations of conditions rather than the independent effect of a single factor28,29. Accordingly, this study proposes three configurational hypotheses. H1 posits that at least one configuration combining strong political logic with specific market conditions is associated with high ESG performance, reflecting a political–market synergy29. H2 proposes that effective governance and strong social supervision may jointly form a governance–society balance associated with high ESG performance30,31. H3 suggests that multiple distinct configurations of the four institutional logics may each lead to high ESG performance, and that these paths may vary across ownership levels and industries (Fig. 2)16.

Research methods

Study design

This study employs fuzzy-set Qualitative Comparative Analysis (fsQCA), a set-theoretic method designed to examine sufficiency relationships between combinations of antecedent conditions and outcomes, rather than strict causal effects35. Unlike conventional statistical approaches that estimate the net effect of individual variables, fsQCA identifies multiple equivalent configurations associated with the same outcome, making it well suited to the analysis of complex institutional interactions and organizational performance.

The initial sample includes Chinese state-owned listed companies on the Shanghai and Shenzhen A-share markets from 2015 to 2023. Following standard screening procedures, ST/*ST firms, financial firms, and cases with missing key variables were excluded. The final sample consists of an unbalanced panel of 387 state-owned enterprises, covering major sectors such as manufacturing, energy, and information technology.

To meet the cross-case comparison requirement of fsQCA, the panel data were converted into cross-sectional data by calculating the arithmetic mean of each variable for each firm over 2015–2023. This treatment captures the relatively stable, medium- to long-term characteristics of institutional logic and ESG orientation, while reducing the influence of short-term fluctuations. Accordingly, the results should be interpreted as reflecting structural configurational patterns rather than annual dynamic changes. Financial and governance data were obtained mainly from Wind, CNRDS, and CSMAR, while ESG data were based on Huazheng ESG ratings. Missing values were manually supplemented and cross-checked using annual and CSR reports (Table 1).

Selection of variables

The outcome variable in this study is enterprise ESG performance, measured using the Huazheng ESG rating. This rating integrates publicly disclosed information on environmental management, social responsibility, and governance quality into a composite index.

The conditional variables correspond to the four institutional logics. Political logic is proxied by government regulation intensity, measured through a composite index based on the number of provincial environmental policies and corporate environmental penalties. Market logic is captured by the degree of market competition, combining the industry Herfindahl index and firm market-share volatility. Social logic is represented by social supervision pressure, measured through negative media coverage and the number of public complaints. Governance logic is proxied by governance structure, with the proportion of independent directors as the core indicator reflecting internal oversight and balance.

In addition, this study includes corporate social responsibility (CSR) culture as a normative condition rather than an independent institutional logic. It is measured through a composite index based on CSR report quality and the number of social responsibility awards. This treatment reflects the extent to which social responsibility norms are internalized within the firm and allows the analysis to capture how external institutional pressures are translated into organizational practices.

To ensure measurement quality, all composite indicators were subjected to validity and reliability tests. Expert review and context-based design support content validity, factor analysis indicates acceptable construct validity, and reliability tests show satisfactory internal consistency. Alternative weighting tests also confirm the robustness of the measures. The full variable definitions are reported in Table 2.

fsQCA method and procedures

This study employs fuzzy-set Qualitative Comparative Analysis (fsQCA) to examine how multiple institutional logics combine to shape the ESG performance of state-owned enterprises. Unlike methods that test the net effect of a single variable, fsQCA is designed to identify configurations of conditions associated with an outcome and is therefore well suited to questions implied by H1 and H2 concerning conjunctural causation and equifinality. Accordingly, the theoretical expectations derived from H1 and H2 are used to specify the directional expectations for the intermediate solution.

Following the standard fsQCA procedure, the analysis includes data calibration, necessity analysis, truth table construction, Boolean minimization, and robustness checks. All analyses were conducted using fsQCA 3.0. In addition to identifying configurations associated with high ESG performance, this study also examines configurations linked to non-high ESG performance. This comparative design helps reveal not only multiple effective paths, but also how the same institutional logic may support or constrain ESG performance under different combinations of conditions.

For calibration, raw variables were transformed into fuzzy-set membership scores from 0 to 1 using the direct method, with anchor points for full non-membership, crossover, and full membership. The specific calibration thresholds are reported in Table 3.

For calibration, continuous variables were transformed using the direct method, with the 5th percentile as full non-membership, the 50th percentile as the crossover point, and the 95th percentile as full membership. For Governance, the crossover was set at 0.33, reflecting China’s statutory minimum requirement for board independence. This design combines empirical distribution with theoretical meaning and facilitates comparison across variables and industries. The appropriateness of these anchors was further examined through sensitivity tests.

Before configurational analysis, a necessary condition analysis was conducted to assess whether any single condition was necessary for high ESG or non-high ESG performance. Consistency and coverage were calculated for each condition, and a consistency value above 0.90 was used as the benchmark for necessity. The results are reported in Sect. 4.2.

The core fsQCA procedure then involved truth table construction and Boolean minimization. A consistency threshold of 0.80 and a frequency threshold of 3 cases were adopted to identify substantively meaningful configurations. The intermediate solution was reported to distinguish core from contributing conditions. Solution consistency and coverage were used to evaluate the empirical relevance of each path. Robustness was assessed through alternative calibration settings, replacement of Huazheng ESG with Wind ESG, and sub-sample analyses by ownership level and industry type. To improve transparency, the study also reports the fuzzy membership matrix for all cases.

Empirical analysis

Descriptive statistics and correlation

As the core entity of China’s economic system, state-owned enterprises (SOEs) not only have their own sustainable development but also carry national strategic goals and social public values in their environmental, social, and governance performance. Against the backdrop of accelerating global sustainable development agenda, analyzing the impact mechanism of SOEs’ ESG performance requires a complex interaction based on multidimensional institutional logic. This chapter takes state-owned listed companies on the Shanghai and Shenzhen A-share markets from 2015 to 2023 as research samples, and systematically constructs an institutional logic analysis framework by integrating core variables such as political regulation, market competition, social supervision, governance structure, and responsibility culture.

Analysis of necessary conditions

In fuzzy set qualitative comparative analysis, consistency measures the necessity strength of antecedent conditions for the outcome variable, with a value range of [0,1]. If consistency is greater than 0.9, it indicates that the condition is a potentially necessary condition for the outcome to occur. Coverage reflects the explanatory range of necessary conditions for the outcome, with a higher value indicating that the condition supports the outcome in more cases.

In fsQCA, consistency measures the necessity of a condition for the outcome, and coverage indicates the empirical relevance of that necessity. The results of the necessary condition analysis for high/non-high ESG performance are shown in Table 6.

Analysis of configuration path

By constructing and analyzing the calibrated truth table, this study identifies configurations associated with both high and non-high ESG performance among state-owned enterprises. For high ESG performance, the intermediate solution reports an overall consistency of 0.85 and an overall coverage of 0.82. For non-high ESG performance, the corresponding intermediate solution shows an overall consistency of 0.81 and a coverage of 0.78. The necessity analysis further indicates that no single institutional logic constitutes a necessary condition for high ESG performance. This finding suggests that ESG performance in state-owned enterprises is better understood as a configurational outcome shaped by the joint alignment of multiple institutional logics, rather than by the isolated effect of any single condition.

As shown in Table 7, four equivalent configurations are associated with high ESG performance: Regulation-Governance Synergy, Market-Society Driven, Culture-Structure Integration, and Political-Resource Complement. These paths differ in their internal composition, but all indicate that high ESG performance emerges when institutional logics are combined in a mutually reinforcing manner. The results therefore support the equifinality argument of fsQCA: similar outcomes may be associated with different but internally coherent combinations of conditions.

The Regulation-Governance Synergy path has the highest raw coverage (0.72) and a consistency of 0.89, indicating that it is the broadest and most empirically representative configuration. Its analytical implication is not simply that government regulation or governance structure is individually important, but that regulatory pressure becomes more effectively translated into ESG practices when accompanied by a stronger internal governance structure. The representative case of China State Construction Engineering Corp. illustrates this logic: high regulatory embeddedness combined with relatively strong board-level governance is associated with more substantive rather than symbolic ESG implementation.

Table 8 reports three configurations associated with non-high ESG performance, with an overall solution consistency of 0.81 and coverage of 0.78. These configurations should not be interpreted as simple mirror images of the high-ESG paths. Instead, they represent different forms of institutional misalignment. The first path, logic deficiency, has a consistency of 0.82 and raw coverage of 0.67, suggesting that the coexistence of regulatory pressure and market pressure does not necessarily produce ESG improvement when internal coordination remains weak. The second path, logic conflict, reports a consistency of 0.79 and raw coverage of 0.58, indicating that political regulation without sufficient governance capacity may lead only to procedural or symbolic responses. The third path, cultural vacuum, has the highest coverage at 0.73 and a consistency of 0.85, pointing to the risks of weak internal CSR commitment combined with insufficient external social supervision. Taken together, the three paths indicate that non-high ESG performance is associated less with the absence of one specific condition than with the absence of effective alignment across multiple institutional logics.

Discussion

Discussion of results

The findings indicate that ESG performance in state-owned enterprises (SOEs) is better understood as a configurational outcome shaped by the interaction of multiple institutional logics, rather than as the result of any single factor acting independently. In particular, political logic appears as a structurally embedded force within SOEs, and its role varies across different combinations with market, governance, and social logics. This helps explain why firms exposed to similar external pressures may still display markedly different ESG outcomes. Compared with conventional linear approaches, the configurational perspective adopted in this study provides a more suitable framework for interpreting institutional complexity, because it captures conjunctural causation and equifinality rather than isolated net effects.

The four high-ESG paths further clarify this point. The Regulation–Governance Synergy path suggests that political pressure is more likely to correspond to strong ESG performance when it is matched by effective internal governance. The Market–Society Driven path shows that external market discipline and social supervision may jointly support ESG improvement even when political or governance conditions are less dominant. The Culture–Structure Integration path highlights the importance of embedding social responsibility values into organizational routines and governance mechanisms. The Political–Resource Complementarity path, by contrast, indicates that political support may compensate for weaker market incentives under specific conditions. Taken together, the coexistence of these paths supports the argument of multiple equifinal routes to high ESG performance.

At the same time, the comparison between high and non-high ESG configurations is theoretically important. It shows that the same institutional logic may operate as either a support or a constraint depending on the broader configuration in which it is embedded. This finding extends existing ESG research by suggesting that the relationships among institutional logics may matter as much as the strength of individual logics. It should also be emphasized that fsQCA identifies sufficiency relationships rather than strict causal effects; the results therefore indicate which combinations are associated with the outcome, not deterministic causal laws.

Recent reviews have emphasized both the analytical value and the limitations of ESG research, including conceptual overlap, measurement divergence, and rating inconsistency across providers36,37. This treatment is also consistent with the political-economy view of corporate governance, which highlights the institutional and political origins of governance arrangements and their relevance for firm-level outcomes38.

Practical implications

The findings offer a differentiated basis for ESG governance in SOEs by showing that there is no single universally effective governance model. Instead, the practical value of the study lies in identifying distinct combinations of institutional conditions associated with high ESG performance. This implies that policy makers and managers should begin with a diagnosis of the enterprise’s dominant institutional environment and internal governance characteristics, and then adopt strategies that fit the relevant configuration rather than applying uniform policy tools across all firms.

For enterprises corresponding to the Regulation–Governance Synergy path, especially central SOEs or firms in more regulated industries, ESG governance may be strengthened by linking regulatory incentives more closely to governance quality. In such cases, policy pressure is likely to be more effective when board independence, supervisory mechanisms, and internal accountability are also strengthened. For firms closer to the Market–Society Driven path, typically local SOEs in more competitive sectors, ESG improvement may depend more on transparency, stakeholder scrutiny, and reputational discipline. Public disclosure systems, media visibility, and mechanisms that translate social supervision into managerial incentives are therefore likely to be especially relevant.

The Culture–Structure Integration path suggests that firms with stronger existing responsibility norms should move beyond symbolic commitment and formalize these values within governance arrangements. Embedding ESG indicators into executive evaluation and board accountability may help turn cultural commitment into more stable organizational practice. For firms corresponding to the Political–Resource Complementarity path, often in strategic or monopolistic sectors, the key issue is whether political support is translated into measurable ESG outcomes rather than remaining a source of passive protection. In such settings, subsidies, approvals, or policy resources should be tied more clearly to observable ESG benchmarks.

These implications should be understood as configuration-sensitive suggestions, not universal prescriptions. Their effectiveness depends on whether the institutional and organizational conditions of a given SOE match the relevant path. In this sense, the study contributes to more targeted and context-aware ESG governance, rather than advocating a single “best practice” model.

Limitations and future research

This study has several limitations that define its scope and suggest directions for future research. First, although the use of multi-year averages is consistent with the cross-case logic of fsQCA, it captures relatively stable patterns rather than short-term changes. The analysis therefore cannot fully reveal how institutional logic configurations evolve over time or how firms adjust ESG strategies in response to policy, market, or governance shocks. Second, while fsQCA is effective for identifying condition combinations associated with outcomes, it does not fully explain the internal processes through which these configurations operate. The present study therefore cannot unpack managerial responses, organizational capabilities, or other micro-level mechanisms in detail. Third, the analysis focuses on Chinese SOEs and uses domestic ESG ratings, which is appropriate for the research question but limits broader comparability. Future research could incorporate longitudinal designs, mixed methods, cross-ownership comparisons, alternative ESG ratings, and cross-national evidence to further extend the configurational understanding of ESG governance.

Conclusion

From an institutional logic perspective, this study applies fsQCA to examine how combinations of political, market, governance, and social logics are associated with ESG performance in Chinese state-owned listed enterprises. The results show that high ESG performance is linked to multiple configurational paths, including Regulation–Governance Synergy, Market–Society Driven, Culture–Structure Integration, and Political–Resource Complementarity. This suggests that ESG governance in SOEs is shaped by the alignment of several institutional forces rather than by any single factor alone. A key finding is that political logic should be understood as a structurally embedded element of SOEs, not merely an external contextual condition. The comparison between high and non-high ESG paths further shows that the same logic may support or constrain ESG performance depending on its combination with other conditions. Overall, the study contributes to institutional logic research by offering a configurational explanation of ESG variation and provides a more differentiated basis for ESG governance in state-owned enterprises.
