The coordinated actions of metformin in the intestine and liver improves hyperglycemia
Authors: Ling He, Hongying An, Karina Ramirez, Sally Radovick, Fredric E. Wondisford
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYMetformin is the first-line oral anti-diabetic agent. Metformin concentrations in the intestine can reach up to 1.3 mM, while those in the portal vein are approximately 0.075 mM. It is unclear whether this metformin concentration difference contributes to metformin’s antidiabetic effects. Here, we showed that high metformin concentrations upregulate G6PC expression through AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) activation to prevent glucose release in intestinal epithelial cells (IECs). The inhibition of mitochondrial activity by high metformin concentrations leads to drastically increased glucose utilization through glycolysis, along with lactate overproduction in the IECs. Subsequently, glycolytic metabolite lactate is released from IECs into portal vein and delivered to the liver. In the liver, low concentrations of metformin activate AMPK to promote mitochondrial fission and mitophagy to maintain a healthy mitochondrial population, resulting in increased lactate utilization in the mitochondria. These coordinated actions of metformin in the intestine and liver improve hyperglycemia in diabetes and obesity.

INTRODUCTION

Metformin is the first-line anti-diabetic agent and is the second most prescribed drug in the USA. Intensive studies have been conducted to uncover the molecular mechanisms of metformin action.1 Human and animal studies showed that metformin can inhibit liver glucose production, a major cause of hyperglycemia in patients with type 2 diabetes (T2D),2–4 and metformin can directly inhibit glucose production in primary hepatocytes;5,6 therefore, it has been proposed that metformin acts mainly through inhibiting gluconeogenesis in the liver.7 In recent years, the intestine is increasingly recognized as an important organ for metformin action.8 Some studies suggested that the intestine may be the primary site of metformin action.9–11 Metformin is an oral medication, and the bioavailability of metformin in the intestine is around 40%–60%, thus about 50% of oral metformin would remain in the intestine.12 Therefore, metformin concentrations in the intestinal mucosa are up to 300 times higher than in the circulation13,14 and can reach high concentrations ~1.3 mM.1,13–15 In contrast, metformin concentrations in the portal vein are around 0.040–0.075 mM.16,17 In patients with T2D and obese animal models, metformin treatment significantly increased glucose uptake in the intestine;9,18–20 strikingly, the intestine is the highest 18F-fluorodexoyglucose (18F-FDG) uptake site after intravenous administration in PET-CT imaging. Intestinal 18F-FDG uptake is positively correlated with metformin’s improvement of hyperglycemia in patients with T2D.19 These data suggest that the intestine is a critical metformin-targeting site.

Compared with healthy individuals, patients with obesity and T2D have a changed microbiota in the intestine, this microbial dysbiosis is correlated to the occurrence of obesity and T2D.21,22 Intestinal barrier dysfunction, with an increased intestinal permeability, has been observed in several pathophysiological conditions, including obesity, T2D, and aging,23–25 that allows the translocation of bacteria, their fragments or products, such as lipopolysaccharides (LPSs), from the intestine into the bloodstream,25–28 resulting in endotoxemia and low-grade inflammation.29–31 We found that increased levels of liver LPS and LPS-induced P300 protein in obese mice, and LPS-induced P300 acetylates insulin receptor substrate1/2 (critical mediators in insulin signaling) to impair insulin signaling, consequently contributing to increased liver glucose production and hyperglycemia.32 Considering the close relationship of ‘the leaky gut’ and metabolic diseases,33–36 intestinal barrier dysfunction with increased intestinal permeability is therefore a therapeutic target for metabolic diseases.

AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) is a master regulator of metabolism.37 Activation of AMPK by metformin can inhibit gluconeogenesis that contributes to metformin’s glucose-lowering effects.1,3,5 Moreover, intestinal glucose production via gluconeogenesis contributes to 5%–7% of systemic glucose production,38 and metformin treatment can increase glucose utilization and lactate production in the intestine without elevating lactate levels in the circulation.2,39,40 Our previous study showed that metformin has biphasic effects on mitochondrial activity, with low concentrations activating mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation and high concentrations inhibiting mitochondrial respiration.41 Metformin-stimulated overproduction of lactate in the intestine is likely used in the liver. However, it is unclear how these metformin actions in the intestine and liver contribute to metformin’s antidiabetic effects and the significance of intestinal AMPK in metformin action, and whether different metformin concentrations in the intestine and liver are important for metformin action.

RESULTS

Increased intestinal permeability and liver LPS levels are associated with insulin resistance in obese mice and elderly mice

Insulin resistance is a hallmark of patients with obesity and T2D.42,43 Low-grade inflammation plays an important role in the development of insulin resistance32,44 and increased intestinal LPS leakage can cause systemic insulin resistance.45,46 We found that high-fat diet (HFD) induced obese mice exhibited insulin resistance (Figure 1A). We determined the intestinal permeability by orally giving FITC-dextran to mice47 and found that plasma FITC-dextran levels increased 2.4-fold in HFD-fed mice compared with mice fed a regular diet (Figure 1B), indicating an increased intestinal permeability. There is a 4.58-fold increase in LPS levels in the liver of HFD-fed mice than that in the liver of mice fed a regular diet (Figure 1C). Furthermore, genetically modified obese db/db mice exhibited severe insulin resistance (Figure 1D), these obese mice had significantly increased intestinal permeability (Figure 1E) and LPS levels (Figure 1F) in the liver compared with that of control mice.

Aging is a major risk factor for contribution to diabetic epidemic, and there is a high prevalence of T2D in elderly individuals.48 The preservation of insulin sensitivity is critical for healthy aging.49–51 Unfortunately, insulin sensitivity is naturally reduced with age,52,53 leading to dramatic increases in the number of people with metabolic diseases.54,55 We found that aged mice (78 weeks of age) exhibited insulin resistance compared with younger mice (12 weeks of age) (Figure 1G). Older mice (52 and 78 weeks of age) had 10 times higher internal permeability than younger mice (12 weeks of age) (Figure 1H). The 78-week-old mice also had 4 times higher liver LPS levels (Figure 1I).

The intestine is an important organ for metformin action

Intestinal barrier dysfunction results in low-grade inflammation and contributes to the development of insulin resistance in obesity.32,56 Our study demonstrated that increased intestinal permeability and liver LPS levels are associated with insulin resistance (Figure 1). We determined antidiabetic effects of clinically relevant metformin dose (50 mg/kg/day) in HFD-fed mice.41 In agreement with the clinical studies,57,58 we found that metformin treatment through drinking water significantly reduced body weight gain (Figure 2A) and daily food intake in HFD-fed mice (Figure 2B). HFD-fed mice had significantly increased intestinal permeability and LPS levels in the liver (Figures 1B and 1C); however, metformin treatment significantly reduced intestinal permeability and LPS levels in both the portal vein and liver (Figures 2C–2E). Since metformin can modulate the composition of microbiota to reduce LPS production in the intestine,59 this metformin’s effect can also contribute to the reduction of LPS levels in the portal vein and liver. Metformin treatment significantly reduced serum levels of insulin and leptin (Figure 2F), and improved systemic insulin sensitivity and decreased glucose production in the liver (Figures 2G and 2H). These data indicate that metformin treatment can improve systemic insulin sensitivity and reduce body weight gain in HFD-fed mice.

To verify the importance of the intestine in metformin action, HFD-fed mice were given 50 mg/kg/day of metformin through the oral route in drinking water or comparable 37.5 mg/kg/day of metformin through intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection. The relatively lower metformin dose was given through i.p. injection because of near 100% metformin’s bioavailability through i.p. injection60 compared with 40%–60% metformin’s bioavailability in the intestine.12 Treatment through i.p. injection would avoid metformin action in the intestine. Interestingly, we observed reduced body weight gain of HFD-fed mice treated with metformin through i.p. injection during the first 4 weeks of treatment (Figure 2I), even though this did not reach statistical significance. However, HFD-fed mice treated with metformin orally exhibited significantly less body weight gain and reduced food intake after prolonged treatment compared with those treated with metformin through i.p. injection (Figures 2I and 2J). Treatment with metformin through the oral route significantly decreased intestinal permeability and LPS levels in both the portal vein and liver (Figures 2K–2M). Mice treated with metformin orally also exhibited reduced serum levels of leptin (Figure 2N) and improved insulin sensitivity and decreased glucose production in the liver (Figures 2O and 2P). These data indicate that the intestine is indeed an important organ for metformin action.

To address whether metformin has any effect on metabolic parameters of young and healthy mice fed a regular diet, 6-month-old mice were given 50 mg/kg/day of metformin through the oral route in drinking water for 12 weeks. We found that metformin treatment had no significant impact on the body weight gain, food intake, intestinal permeability, systematic insulin sensitivity, and liver glucose production (Figures 2Q–2X). These data are consistent with our finding that intestinal permeability remains intact in young mice (Figure 1H).

The loss of AMPKα1/2 subunits increases intestinal permeability and causes insulin resistance

Previous reports showed that AMPK activity was decreased in intestinal epithelial cells (IECs) of HFD-fed mice61 and obese patients with T2D,62 and activation of AMPK can promote the assembly of tight junction in IECs.63 We determined the phosphorylation levels of AMPKα at T172 in IECs isolated from young and older mice, and found that elderly mice had significantly reduced AMPK activity (Figures 3A and 3B). To test the importance of AMPK in regulating intestinal permeability, we bred double floxed AMPKα1/2 mice32 with Villin-Cre transgenic mice and generated IEC-specific AMPKα1/2 knockout (Gut-α1/2 KO) mice (Figure 3C). We found that both male and female Gut-α1/2 KO mice consumed more food than their floxed AMPKα1/2 littermates (Figures 3D and 3C) and have higher body weight compared with age-matched double floxed male and female AMPKα1/2 mice (Figures 3F and 3G). The loss of AMPKα1/2 subunits led to significantly increased intestinal permeability (Figure 3H), leading to elevated liver LPS levels (Figure 3I). These data suggest that AMPK plays an important role in maintaining intestinal barrier integrity. Since leaky gut can cause low-grade inflammation and insulin resistance,44 we found that Gut-α1/2 KO mice exhibited insulin resistance (Figure 3J) along with increased glucose production in the liver (Figure 3K). Our previous study showed that LPS can induce acetyltransferase P300 via IRE1-XBP1s signaling pathway and elevated P300 acetylates insulin receptor substrate1/2 (critical mediators in insulin signaling) to impair insulin signaling, consequently contributing to increased liver glucose production and hyperglycemia.32 In the liver of Gut-α1/2 KO mice, there were increased protein levels of P300 and XBP1s (Figures 3L and 3M). Furthermore, we observed increased phosphorylation of IκB and NF-κB and protein levels of TNF-α and IL-1β (Figures 3L and 3M). These results substantiate further that increased liver LPS can cause insulin resistance by activating IRE1-XBP1s-P300 signaling and NF-κB inflammatory pathways.

The loss of intestinal AMPKα1/2 led to significantly increased food intake and body weight in both male and female mice (Figures 3D–3G). Since GDF-15 can reduce food intake and body weight gain, and activation of AMPK stimulates GDF-15 expression and release,11,64,65 we determined GDF-15 levels in the portal vein of floxed AMPKα1/2 mice and Gut-α1/2 KO mice and found that the loss of AMPKα1/2 led to a significant reduction of GDF-15 levels in the portal vein (Figure 3N). A previous study showed that metformin can increase GDF-15 levels in the circulation,11 we found that treatment with metformin led to 3-fold increase in GDF-15 mRNA levels in primary IECs and inhibition of AMPK by Compound C negated metformin-stimulated GDF-15 expression (Figures 3O and 3P). These data suggest that the reduction of GFD-15 levels could play a role in increased food intake and body weight gain in Gut-α1/2 KO mice.

The loss of intestinal AMPKα1/2 subunits decreases metformin’s efficacy

To assess the effects of intestinal AMPK on systemic insulin sensitivity, we challenged Gut-α1/2 KO mice and floxed AMPKα1/2 littermates with an HFD. Both male and female Gut-α1/2 KO mice had higher body weight compared with age-matched floxed male and female AMPKα1/2 mice (Figures 4A and 4B) and consumed more HFD food (Figures 4C and 4D). Male Gut-α1/2 KO mice exhibited insulin resistance after being fed an HFD (Figure 4E) and had significantly higher mRNA levels of rate-limited gluconeogenic genes G6pc and Pck1 in the liver (Figure 4F). Activation of duodenal AMPK by metformin can reduce liver glucose production,10 we examined whether the loss of intestinal AMPKα1/2 subunits had any impact on metformin’s efficacy. After 4 weeks of HFD feeding, mice were treated with metformin (50 mg/kg/day) through drinking water for 6 weeks,41 then we conducted pyruvate challenge experiment to examine the liver glucose production and found that Gut-α1/2 KO mice had significantly increased blood glucose levels (Figure 4G). After 8 weeks of metformin treatment, Gut-α1/2 KO mice exhibited insulin resistance (Figure 4H).

Activation of AMPK by metformin decreases intestinal permeability and stimulates lactate overproduction in the intestine

The loss of AMPKα1/2 subunits led to significantly increased intestinal permeability and liver LPS levels (Figures 3H and 3I). We then tested whether metformin treatment could change the expression of tight junction proteins to affect intestinal epithelial barrier function. Primary IECs were treated with metformin, and we found that metformin treatment significantly increased AMPK activity (increased T172 phosphorylation), along with significantly increased tight junction protein levels (ZO-1, claudin-1, and occludin) (Figures 4I and 4J). Furthermore, in human intestinal epithelial Caco2 cells, treatment with an AMPK specific activating-peptide Pa496h,66,67 significantly increased AMPK activity and tight junction protein levels (ZO-1, claudin-1, and occludin) (Figures 4K and 4L), and decreased the permeability (Figure 4M), indicating that activation of AMPK upregulates the expression of tight junction proteins to strengthen intestinal epithelial barrier function.

Interestingly, in HFD-fed mice, metformin treatment through drinking water significantly increased lactate levels in the portal vein (Figure 4N); however, it had no significant impact on lactate levels in the circulation (Figure 4O). These data are consistent with results from clinical trials showing that oral metformin ingestion significantly increased lactate levels in the portal vein, not in the circulation.40,68–70 To examine whether metformin could stimulate lactate production in enterocytes, we treated primary IECs with high concentrations of metformin observed in the intestine after oral metformin administration.1,13–15 As shown in Figure 4P, treatment with high concentrations of metformin increased lactate production in a concentration-dependent manner (Figure 4P). Importantly, the loss of AMPK in the IECs significantly decreased lactate levels in the portal vein of HFD-fed mice treated with metformin (Figure 4Q), suggesting that AMPK plays a role in metformin-mediated lactate overproduction in the intestine.

Inhibition of mitochondrial respiration leads to increased glucose uptake and glycolysis in IECs

After intravenous administration of 18F-FDG, the intestine is the highest 18F-FDG uptake site in both patients with T2D and obese animal models.9,18–20 Indeed, treatment with high concentration of metformin led to more than 5-fold 2-NDBG (fluorescent glucose analog) uptake in primary IECs (Figures 5A and 5B). Metformin treatment significantly increased the protein levels of Glut1 and 2 in primary IECs (Figures 5C and 5D) and in IECs of HFD-fed mice (Figures 5E and 5F). Increased Glut1 and 2 would augment glucose uptake in IECs from circulation, thus resulting in 18F-FDG accumulation in the intestine observed in patients with T2D and obese animal models.9,18–20 However, it is important to note that we found that metformin treatment significantly reduced G6PC protein levels in IECs of HFD-fed mice (Figures 5E and 5G). Intestinal glucose production via gluconeogenesis contributes to 5%–7% of systemic glucose production.38 In primary IECs, metformin treatment significantly decreased glucose production in a concentration-dependent manner (Figure 5H). The above data demonstrate that high metformin concentrations can inhibit glucose production and release in the IECs.

High concentrations of metformin can reduce mitochondrial oxygen consumption by inhibition of mitochondrial complex 1 in hepatocytes,71,72 so we tested the effects of high concentrations of metformin on mitochondrial respiration in primary IECs. We found that high concentrations of metformin significantly inhibited the oxygen consumption rate (OCR) in primary IECs (Figure 5I), but it resulted in significantly augmented extracellular acidification rate (Figure 5J), which reflects increased glycolytic flux and lactate efflux.73 To verify the inhibition of mitochondrial respiration by high concentrations of metformin, we used pyruvate or lactate as substrates in Seahorse assays and found that treatment with high concentrations of metformin inhibited mitochondrial respiration in primary IECs as well (Figures 5K and 5L). Treatment with 0.5 mM of metformin had a greater inhibition of mitochondrial respiration when lactate was the substrate (compare Figures 5K and 5L). This is due to the production of extra NADH by conversion of lactate into pyruvate, and NADH cannot be used when mitochondrial complex 1 is inhibited. Therefore, when mitochondrial complex 1 is inhibited by high concentrations of metformin, and lactate cannot be used to produce ATP via oxidative phosphorylation. Under this condition, IECs will increase glycolysis to maintain cellular energy homeostasis, along with the overproduction of lactate, which was observed in HFD-fed mice treated with metformin (Figure 4N).

Metformin-stimulated lactate production is AMPK-dependent in IECs

Having seen that HFD-fed Gut-α1/2 KO mice treated with metformin had significantly lower lactate levels in the portal vein (Figure 4Q), we assessed the importance of AMPK in metformin-stimulated lactate production. First, we overexpressed functional AMPKα1β1γ1 complex in primary IECs (Figure 5M), followed by treatment with high concentrations of metformin (2 mM). Overexpression of functional AMPKα1β1γ1 complex significantly increased metformin-stimulated lactate production (Figure 5N). Second, we employed Compound C to inhibit AMPK activity; this treatment led to significantly decreased metformin-stimulated lactate production in primary IECs (Figure 5O). Third, overexpression of AMPKα2K45R mutant with dead kinase activity74 also significantly decreased metformin-stimulated lactate production in primary IECs compared with cells expression with wild type AMPKα2 subunit (Figures 5P and 5Q). These data reveal that metformin-stimulated lactate is AMPK-dependent in IECs.

Upregulation of G6PC expression by metformin increases glycolysis in IECs

Even though metformin treatment significantly increased the protein levels of Glut1 and 2 (Figures 5C–5F), these changes could not explain the increased glucose uptake in the intestine because of the bidirectional function of glucose transporters. Metformin treatment decreased G6PC protein levels in IECs of HFD-fed mice (Figures 5E and 5G), and treatment with high metformin concentration (1 mM) activated AMPK or overexpression of functional AMPKα1β1γ1 complex significantly decreased G6PC protein levels in primary IECs (Figures 5M and 6A–6C). Metformin treatment suppressed the mRNA levels of G6pc and Pck1 (Figure 6D). To test the importance of G6PC protein in metformin’s effect on glycolysis and lactate overproduction, we generated adenoviral FLAG-tagged G6PC expression vector to express G6PC in primary IECs (Figure 6E). Compared to IECs with expression of adenoviral GFP controls, overexpression of G6PC led to more than 6-fold reduction of metformin-stimulated accumulation of fluorescent glucose analog 2-NDBG in IECs (Figures 6F and 6G). Overexpression of G6PC also led to a 75% reduction of lactate levels in metformin-treated IECs (Figure 6H). Next, we sought to determine whether metformin treatment could affect glucose and glucose 6-phosphate in IECs, and found that metformin treatment led to a 5-fold increase in glucose 6-phosphate levels; however, with decreased glucose levels in IECs (Figures 6I and 6J), resulting in drastically increased ratio of glucose 6-phosphate/glucose (Figure 6K). Collectively, decreased G6PC expression by metformin leads to the accumulation of glucose 6-phosphate and augmentation of glycolysis in IECs. In the meantime, the inhibition of the expression of G6pc and Pck1 genes by metformin would block the use of lactate/pyruvate as substrates for gluconeogenesis. When lactate/pyruvate cannot be used in the mitochondria because of the inhibition of mitochondrial activity by high concentrations of metformin, lactate (pyruvate needs to be converted to lactate to regenerate the limited NAD+ and to sustain glycolysis) will be released in the intestine and delivered to the liver through the portal vein.

Low concentrations of metformin promote mitochondrial dynamics and lactate utilization in hepatocyte

In our animal study, metformin treatment significantly increased lactate levels in the portal vein (Figure 4N); however, there was no significant change of lactate levels in the circulation of HFD-fed mice (Figure 4O). Similar findings were observed in metformin clinical trials.40,68–70 Liver can use lactate to make glucose through gluconeogenesis. However, low metformin concentrations found in the portal vein can inhibit gluconeogenesis using lactate and pyruvate as substrate (Figures 7A and 7B). In addition, in HFD-fed mice, metformin treatment significantly decreased blood glucose levels in pyruvate tolerance test (Figure 7C), reflecting metformin inhibition of glucose production from pyruvate via gluconeogenesis in the liver. These data suggest that metformin may promote the use of lactate in mitochondria of hepatocytes. Our studies, along with others, have demonstrated that hepatocytes’ mitochondria in obese mice and obese patients exhibit increased size, forming reticula or “megamitochondria,” with decreased oxidative activity compared to young or healthy counterparts.66,75–77 AMPK can stimulate mitochondrial fission through phosphorylating Mff to recruit Drp1 onto the mitochondrial membrane and driving mitochondrial fission78 or phosphorylating MTFR1L to inhibit mitochondrial fusion.79 We found that low metformin activated AMPK, increased MFF phosphorylation, and activated mitophagy, leading to the increased mitochondrial fission (Figures 7D–7F) to maintain a healthy mitochondrial population. In primary hepatocytes, low metformin concentration significantly increased mitochondrial respiration with either lactate or pyruvate as substrate (Figures 7G and 7H).

DISCUSSION

The IECs provide a physical barrier that prevents the entry of bacterial toxins and pathogens.80,81 Intestinal barrier dysfunction, with an increased intestinal permeability, has been observed in several pathophysiological conditions, including obesity/T2D and aging,23–25 that allows the translocation of bacteria, their fragments or products, such as LPS, from the intestine into the bloodstream,25–28 resulting in endotoxemia and low-grade inflammation.29–32 We reported that LPS-induced P300 acetylates insulin receptor substrate1/2 to impair insulin signaling, consequently contributing to increased liver glucose production and hyperglycemia.32 Activation of AMPK can augment the intestinal epithelial barrier function.82–85 However, AMPK activity was impaired in IECs of HFD-fed mice61 and obese patients with T2D,62 and we found a decreased AMPK activity in IECs of aged mice. Our study revealed that the loss of AMPKα1/2 in IECs led to significantly increased intestinal permeability; in contrast, activation of AMPK significantly increased tight junction protein levels and reduced the permeability of cultured IECs, suggesting that AMPK plays an important role in maintaining intestinal barrier integrity. Furthermore, intestinal AMPKα1/2 KO mice fed either a regular diet or HFD had higher body weight compared to age-matched floxed AMPKα1/2 mice and consumed more food. We also found that intestinal AMPKα1/2 KO mice had decreased GDF-15 levels in the portal vein. This offers an explanation for increased body weight and food intake in intestinal AMPKα1/2 KO mice because GDF-15 can reduce food intake and body weight, and activation of AMPK can stimulate GDF-15 expression and release.11,64,65

Metformin is an oral anti-diabetic agent. There is up to 300 times higher concentration of metformin accumulated in the intestine than in the circulation. High concentrations of metformin reduce mitochondrial oxygen consumption by inhibition of mitochondrial complex 1 in hepatocytes71,72; we found that low metformin concentration (75 μM) can improve mitochondrial respiration in hepatocytes.41 In addition, in human studies, low concentrations of metformin can activate mitochondrial respiratory activity.86,87 Thus, metformin has concentration-dependent biphasic effects on mitochondrial respiration. To test whether high concentrations of metformin can inhibit mitochondrial respiratory activity in IECs, we treated primary IECs with high concentrations of metformin (1 or 2 mM) within the concentration ranges found in the intestine after oral administration. These high concentrations of metformin significantly reduced mitochondrial respiration, leading to increased glycolysis. To verify these results, we then determined mitochondrial respiratory activity in Seahorse assays using lactate or pyruvate as substrates; high metformin concentrations also significantly inhibited mitochondrial respiration. The above results reveal that inhibition of mitochondrial activity by high concentrations of metformin will lead to increased glucose utilization through glycolysis to maintain cellular energy homeostasis along with the overproduction of lactate. This is the exact phenomenon observed in human and animal studies with metformin treatment because the intestine is the highest 18F-FDG uptake site in both patients with T2D and obese animal models after intravenous administration of 18F-FDG. In agreement with these studies, our results showed that treatment with high concentrations of metformin led to more than a 5-fold increase in uptake of fluorescent glucose analog 2-NDBG in primary IECs, resulting in increased lactate production and the elevation of lactate levels in the portal vein.9,18–20

In agreement with the findings that intestinal AMPK plays an important role in metformin effects,62 the loss of AMPK in the IECs significantly decreased lactate levels in the portal vein of HFD-fed mice treated with metformin. These data suggest that AMPK plays an important role in metformin-mediated lactate production in the intestine. To validate the importance of AMPK in metformin-mediated lactate production, we overexpressed functional AMPKα1β1γ1 complex in IECs and found that overexpression of AMPKα1β1γ1 complex significantly increased metformin-stimulated lactate production. In contrast, inhibition of AMPK activity by Compound C or overexpression of AMPKα2K45R mutant with dead kinase activity significantly decreased metformin-stimulated lactate production in primary IECs. These data show that metformin-stimulated lactate production is AMPK-dependent. Intestinal glucose production via gluconeogenesis contributes to 5%–7% of systemic glucose production,38 and we found that metformin treatment significantly decreased G6PC protein levels in the IECs of HFD-fed mice and in primary IECs; this will decrease glucose release from IECs because G6PC removes the phosphate group from glucose 6-phosphate and glucose will be then released from the cells with the bidirectional function of glucose transporters. Indeed, overexpression of G6PC led to more than 6-fold reduction of metformin-stimulated glucose accumulation of fluorescent glucose analog 2-NDBG and a 75% reduction of lactate in IECs. These data indicate that a decrease in G6PC protein by metformin leads to increased glycolysis in IECs.

In our animal study, we found that metformin treatment significantly increased lactate levels in the portal vein; however, there was no significant change in lactate levels in the circulation of HFD-fed mice. This result pinpoints that the liver may utilize the lactate as either a substrate to make glucose or enter the mitochondria for oxidative phosphorylation. If lactate is converted to glucose in hepatocytes, this process will cost 4 ATP and 2 GTP to make one molecule of glucose from lactate, creating a futile intestinal-liver cycle88 and contributing to metformin’s glycemic control. Considering that metformin can significantly inhibit gluconeogenesis in the liver,3,5,6 the use of lactate to make glucose cannot be the primary process of lactate clearance in the liver. Since activated AMPK can phosphorylate Mff to promote mitochondrial fission,78 and activated AMPK can phosphorylate MTFR1L to inhibit mitochondrial fusion.79 Our study showed that low metformin concentrations found in the portal vein can stimulate mitochondrial fission in hepatocytes. This has important physiological relevance for metformin action because mitochondrial fission can alleviate reductive stress, reactive oxygen species, toxic metabolites, and promote the removal of damaged mitochondria by mitophagy, thus maintaining a healthy mitochondrial population, which is especially important when there are excessive nutrients (obesity/T2D).89 In hepatocytes, low metformin concentrations can increase mitochondrial respiratory activity when either lactate or pyruvate was used as substrate.

In summary, metformin is an oral antidiabetic agent, with about 50% bioavailability, generating up to 300 times higher metformin concentrations in the intestine than in the circula tion.13,14 High metformin concentrations in the intestine downregulate G6PC expression to stimulate glycolysis inside IECs, and with inhibited mitochondrial complex 1 activity, leading to increased glucose utilization through glycolysis and lactate release. Activation of AMPK by low concentration metformin promotes mitochondrial dynamics in hepatocytes to utilize lactate delivered from IECs. The coordinated actions of metformin in the intestine and liver improve hyperglycemia in patients with T2D and obesity.

Limitations of the study

In this report, our provided data shows that HFD-fed mice treated with metformin orally exhibited significantly less body weight gain compared to metformin treatment through i.p. injection, and the loss of AMPK catalytic α1/2 subunits in IECs led to increased body weight gain. GDF-15 can reduce food intake and body weight gain, and activation of AMPK stimulates GDF-15 expression and release. Even though we found that the loss of AMPK in the intestine decreased GDF-15 levels in the portal vein, there is a high expression level of GDF-15 in the kidney and metformin is excreted unchanged in the kidney, and metformin can increase GDF-15 expression in the kidney. The relative importance of metformin in upregulating GDF-15 expression in the intestine and kidney need to be determined in the future study. Our results show that high concentrations of metformin in the intestine can prevent glucose release and stimulate glycolysis, with the inhibition of mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, this leads to increased glucose utilization in the IECs. Metformin can also activate AMPK to increase glucose use in muscle and adipose tissues. Considering that the intestine is the highest 18F-FDG uptake site after intravenous administration in PET-CT imaging in patients with T2D, suggesting that the intestine maybe the primary site of metformin action to improve hyperglycemia. This needs to be tested in follow-up studies.

STAR★METHODS

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND STUDY PARTICIPANT DETAILS

All animal protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Johns Hopkins University (Protocol#: MO20M100) and the University of Arizona (Protocol#: 2024–1197). We bred double floxed AMPKα1/2 mice with transgenic Villi-Cre mice (Jackson laboratory) to generate the intestinal epithelial cell-specific AMPKα1/2 knockout (Gut-α1/2 KO) mice. Floxed AMPKa1/2 mice and Gut-α1/2 KO mice were fed on an HFD for 4 weeks, then treated with metformin (50 mg/kg/day). Mice weight gain and water consumption were measured every 7 days, and metformin concentrations in drinking water were adjusted accordingly. HFD-fed mice or healthy mice were given metformin (50 mg/kg/day) in drinking water. In animal experiment to test the importance of the intestine in metformin action, one group of HFD-fed mice were treated with metformin 50 mg/kg/day by drinking water (Oral) and a daily sham intraperitoneal injection; another group of HFD-fed mice were given 37.5 mg/kg/day through intraperitoneal injection. All mouse age and gender are indicated in individual animal studies.

Cell culture

Cell cultures were conducted using mouse primary hepatocytes and primary epithelial cells or cryopreserved Hepa1-6 cells, Caco2, Hek293 (ATCC) and these cell lines were authenticated and we received consistent results. We did not test for mycoplasma; however, primary cells were cultured in growth medium supplemented with Gentamicin/Amphotericin B (R-015–10, GIBCO). Please refer to individual sections below and Figure Legends for the specific stimulation condition used for each assay. Cells were incubated in standard conditions: 37°C with 5% CO2.

METHOD DETAILS

Adenoviruses and adeno-associated viruses (AAV)

Adenovirus FLAG-tagged AMPKα1, α2, β1, γ1 and AMPKα2-K45R mutant were generated as we described previously.6,90 Adenoviral GFP expression vector was purchased from Vector Core at University of Pennsylvania. Adenovirus FLAG-tagged human G6PC1 expression vector was generated by Welgen Inc (Worcester, MA).

Determination of gut permeability and tolerance tests

Mice were orally given FITC-dextran (400 mg/kg), 4 h later, blood samples were collected and plasma FITC-dextran levels were determined using BioTek Synergy H1 plate reader. For insulin tolerance test, mice were injected intraperitoneally (ip) with 0.5–0.8 unit/kg insulin after fasting for 4–6 h. For pyruvate tolerance test, mice were injected intraperitoneally (ip) with 1.5–2 g/kg pyruvate after fasting for 16 h.

Measurement of endotoxin (LPS) levels, lactate, glucose 6-phosphate, and GDF-15 levels

Liver tissues were homogenized in an ice-cold water bath and centrifuged for 10 min at 2500 × g to remove connective tissue. The Limulus Amebocyte Lysate Endotoxin Detection Assay kit (Lonza, Walkersville, MD) was used to determine endotoxin levels in the supernatant of liver homogenized tissue or plasma of portal vein following the steps recommended by the manufacturer. Lactate levels in the blood and medium were determined using Lactate-Glo Assay kit (Promega). Primary IECs were washed with PBS, then cultured in glucose, pyruvate, and glutamine free DMEM supplemented with 25 mM glucose and 2 mM metformin for 6 h. Cells were washed with PBS 6 times before harvested, and cellular levels of G-6-phosphate were determined using Colorimetric Glucose-6-Phosphate Assay Kit (AAT Bioquest). Serum GDF-15 levels were determined using mouse GDF-15 ELISA Kit (AVIVA).

Preparation of primary hepatocytes, intestinal epithelial cells, and glucose production assay

Mouse primary hepatocytes were prepared as we described previously.6 Mouse intestinal epithelial cells were isolated from mice fed an HFD using a published protocol.91 Mouse primary hepatocytes were cultured in William’s medium E supplemented with ITS (BD Biosciences) and dexamethasone. Primary hepatocytes were treated with vehicle or 80 μM metformin for 16 h, then cells were washed with PBS twice, and the medium was changed to FBS-free DMEM supplemented with vehicle and 80 μM metformin. After 3h of serum starvation, cells were washed twice with PBS, and the 1 mL glucose production medium (lactate and pyruvate) was supplemented with vehicle and 80 μM metformin and/or10 nM glucagon. After 3h incubation with glucose production medium, both the medium and cells were collected. The medium was used to determine glucose concentrations with EnzyChrom Glucose Assay Kit.92 For glucose production in primary mouse intestinal epithelial cells (Cell Biologics) were treated as described in primary hepatocytes without treatment with glucagon.

Determination of cellular glucose levels in IECs and mitochondrial size

A fluorescence labeled 2-deoxy-glucose: 2-NBDG (2-(N-(7-nitrobenz-2-oxa-l,3-diazol-4-yl) amino)-2-deoxyglucose) (2-NBDG) (Cat# 72987, Sigma), that cannot undergo glycolysis,93,94 was used as an indicator of cellular glucose concentrations. After the treatment of cells, medium was changed to glucose-free DMEM containing 200–400μM of fluorescence labeled 2-NBDG for 20–30 min, then washed with no color DMEM, and the fluorescence intensities were determined using.

Leica Stellaris 8 Confocal Microscope. Primary hepatocytes were cultured in DMEM or treated with indicated concentrations of vehicle or metformin, followed by the addition of 50 nM MitoTracker™ Red FM (M22425, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Fluorescent images were acquired via Leica Stellaris 8 Confocal Microscope.

Determination of mitochondrial respiratory activity

Primary hepatocytes and primary IECs were seeded in an XF 96 well plate coated with 0.01% collagen type I. Cells were treated as indicated, followed by the determination of mitochondrial respiratory chain activity using Seahorse XF96 Extracellular Flux Analyzers in Seahorse assay medium containing 10 mM glucose or 10 mM lactate or 10 mM pyruvate (pH7.4). After determination of basal oxygen consumption rates, cells were sequentially treated with oligomycin A (1 μM), FCCP (1 μM), and rotenone (1 μM) along with antimycin A (1 μM). Viable cell numbers were counted and used to normalize the oxygen consumption rate.

QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

We used Prism 9.4.1 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA) to plot the graphs and conduct statistical analysis. At least 3 samples per group were chosen for statistically meaningful interpretation of results and differences in the studies using the Student’s t test and analysis of variation. All n values represent biological replicates (different mice, cultured cells, or in vitro experiments). Differences were considered significant when p < 0.05 that are indicated as *, p < 0.05, or are indicated as ns when not significant. Statistical details of experiments and statistical tests used can be found in the main text and figure legends.
