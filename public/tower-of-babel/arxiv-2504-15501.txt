Mapping molecular polariton transport via pump-probe microscopy
Piper Fowler-Wright, Michael Reitz, Joel Yuen-Zhou

Abstract
We demonstrate how the transport properties of molecular polaritons in optical cavities can be extracted from a microscopic modeling of pump-probe spectroscopy. Our approach combines a mean-field treatment of the light-matter Hamiltonian with a perturbative expansion of both light and matter components, along with spatial coarse-graining. This approach extends semiclassical cavity spectroscopy to multimode light–matter interactions, providing full access to spatially resolved transient spectra. By simulating a microscopy experiment with counter-propagating pump and probe pulses, we compute the differential transmission and show how molecular dephasing and persistent dark exciton populations drive sub-group-velocity transport of the root-mean-square displacement. We analyze transport across the polariton dispersion, showing how velocity renormalization correlates with excitonic weight, consistent with experimental observations, and further its dependence on the rate of molecular dephasing, exciton hopping, and exciton-exciton annihilation. Our results highlight the need to consider measured spectroscopic observables when characterizing transport in polaritonic systems. Keywords: light–matter interaction, strong coupling, organic microcavities, polariton transport, ultrafast spectroscopy, group velocity renormalization



Exciton polaritons are hybrid light-matter quasiparticles formed through strong
coupling of confined electromagnetic modes with electronic excitations. Their

In this Letter, we present a microscopic model of a pump-probe transport
experiment in an organic microcavity (

displacement, emerges in a simple model of molecular polariton transport with
pure dephasing. In particular, we assess the role of dark excitonic populations
in renormalizing the velocity and the dependence of transport properties on
in-plane cavity momentum and dephasing rate.

In pump-probe microscopy experiments, two excitation schemes are commonly
used
[

]

populate the polariton branches indiscriminately to a large degree. A probe
pulse is then tuned to a target energy and momentum. In the second, the pump is
itself tuned to a specific point on the polariton dispersion, generating
coherent polariton populations. This second, resonant excitation scheme, can be
more naturally incorporated in the mean-field approach.

We now identify an appropriate spectroscopic observable. Since pump-probe
experiments are inherently nonlinear optical measurements, they capture
differential signals—specifically, the change in cavity output between pump-on
and pump-off conditions. Accordingly, we consider the differential transmission,

\Delta T=T^{p\text{-on}}-T^{p\text{-off}}

As explained in Ref.

conjugation. Extending this to the multimode model, we define the

\displaystyle\Delta T_{n}(\omega)=\eta_{p}^{2}(\kappa^{2}/2)\,\real[\alpha^{(0,1)}_{n}(\omega)\overline{\alpha}^{(2,1)}_{n}(\omega)],

(11)

which scales with the intensity of the pump field and is independent of the probe field amplitude.
In words, this expression constitutes the heterodyning of the
third-order signal
\alpha_{n}^{(2,1)}
by the probe
\alpha_{n}^{(0,1)}

with counter-propagating pulses provides a simple way to separate out pump and
probe signals, which is a practical challenge in experiments.

In conclusion, we developed a framework for molecular polariton transport in multimode cavities
by calculating spatiotemporally-resolved spectra, providing a clear
understanding of how dark excitonic populations can renormalize the transport
properties in a simplified model of a pump-probe experiment. By analyzing the
nonlinear differential transmission, we demonstrated how irreversible transfer
to dark exciton states, induced by molecular dephasing, creates stationary
excitonic populations that slow net transport. Moreover, that this
renormalization becomes more pronounced with increased excitonic weight

X_{k}^{2}
and molecular dephasing
\gamma_{\phi}
. Similar dependence on

X_{k}^{2}
has been widely reported in experiments, while that on
\gamma_{\phi}

could be investigated, e.g., using materials with different electron-phonon
coupling strengths.

We considered the lowest-order nonlinear signal for a counter-propagating
pump-probe experimental setup, which has the advantage of providing clear
separation of the pump and probe signals. While we used a simple model of pure
dephasing for molecular emitters, our approach readily extends to multi-level
systems, including coupling to vibrational modes directly
[
45
]
.
An accurate description of vibronic coupling may be essential for a complete
understanding of real experiments where these interactions dominate the
dynamics
[

]
.

Furthermore, this method could be applied to calculate higher-order nonlinear
responses and explore alternative experimental schemes not limited to
microcavity exciton-polaritons. For example, one may consider setups involving
local probe fields that are scanned to construct a real-space
image
[
15
]
.

Future work may also incorporate higher-order correlations beyond the mean-field
description to understand analogous microscopy experiments based on
photoluminescence measurements
[
39
]
, or integrate our
approach with FDTD electromagnetic simulations
[

,

to
account for arbitrary photonic environments.

S2
Derivation of mean-field equations

In this section, we outline the derivation of the mean-field equations of motion
for the model introduced in Eq. (3) of the Letter. These steps may be applied to
other many-body models with unitary evolution and incoherent processes to
calculate spatially resolved dynamics in extended systems.

For reference, we repeat here
the TC Hamiltonian introduced in Eq. (1)

\displaystyle\begin{split}H&=\sum_{k=1}^{N_{k}}{\omega}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{k}a^{\dagger}_{k}a^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{k}+\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{\omega_{0}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}}{2}\sigma^{z}_{n}\\
&+\sum_{k=1}^{N_{k}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}g(a^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{k}\sigma^{+}_{n}e^{ikr_{n}}+\text{H.c.}),\end{split}

(S1)

and Master Equation
[Eq. (3) in the main text]:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho=

\displaystyle-i\bigl[H,\rho\bigr]+\sum_{k=1}^{N_{k}}\kappa\mathcal{L}[a^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{k}]+\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{\gamma_{\phi}}{4}\mathcal{L}[\sigma^{z}_{n}].

(S2)

S2.1
Spatial Coarse Graining

The first step is to coarse grain the one-dimensional
molecular system by assigning molecules into sites at

r_{n}=\Delta r,2\Delta r,\ldots,N_{k}\Delta r

such that the spatial resolution
\Delta r=L/N_{k}
is
set by the number of sites
N_{k}
. For this purpose, it
is helpful to introduce a two-index notation for the spin
variables (Pauli matrices),

\sigma^{\alpha}_{n{\rm x}}
where
n=1,\ldots,N_{k}

identifies the site and
{\rm x}=1,\ldots,N_{s}
the molecule
at that site. Here
N_{s}=N/N_{k}
is the number of molecules
per site:

\displaystyle\begin{split}H&=\sum_{k=1}^{N_{k}}{\omega}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{k}a^{\dagger}_{k}a^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{k}+\sum_{n=1}^{N_{k}}\sum_{{\rm x}=1}^{N_{s}}\frac{\omega_{0}}{2}\sigma^{z}_{n{\rm x}}\\
&+\sum_{k=1}^{N_{k}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{k}}\sum_{{\rm x}=1}^{N_{s}}g(a^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{k}\sigma^{+}_{n{\rm x}}e^{ikr_{n}}+\text{H.c.}),\end{split}

(S3)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho=

\displaystyle-i\bigl[H,\rho\bigr]+\sum_{k=1}^{N_{k}}\kappa\mathcal{L}[a^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{k}]+\sum_{n=1}^{N_{k}}\sum_{{\rm x}=1}^{N_{s}}\frac{\gamma_{\phi}}{4}\mathcal{L}[\sigma^{z}_{n{\rm x}}].

(S4)

S2.2
Mean-field Approximation

We next write down equations of motion for the operators

a^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{k}
,
\sigma^{\alpha}_{n{\rm x}}
. Later,
we will realize that we only need to keep a single representative
spin variable for
n=1
,…,
N_{k}
, since molecules
at the same site have the same on-site properties
(i.e., operator expectation values).

The Heisenberg equations of motion for the expectation
of an operator
X
is obtained from
Eq.

S4

via
\partial_{t}\langle X\rangle=\text{Tr}[X\partial_{t}\rho]
. This yields

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{t}\langle a^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{k}\rangle&=-\bigl(i{\omega}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{k}+\kappa/2\bigr)\langle a^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{k}\rangle\\
&-ig\sum_{n=1}^{N_{k}}\sum_{{\rm x}=1}^{N_{s}}e^{-ikr_{n}}\langle\sigma^{-}_{n{\rm x}}\rangle,\end{split}

(S5a)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{t}\langle\sigma_{n{\rm x}}^{-}\rangle&=-\bigl(i\omega^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{0}+\gamma_{\phi}/2\bigr)\langle\sigma_{n{\rm x}}^{-}\rangle\\
&+ig\sum_{k=1}^{N_{k}}e^{ikr_{n}}\langle\sigma^{z}_{n{\rm x}}a^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{k}\rangle,\end{split}

(S5b)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\langle\sigma_{n{\rm x}}^{z}\rangle

\displaystyle=-4g\sum_{k=1}^{N_{k}}\imaginary[\langle\sigma_{n{\rm x}}^{-}a^{\dagger}_{k}e^{-ikr_{n}}\rangle].

(S5c)

Note that sums over the spin variables do not appear in

Eqs.

S5b
to
S5c
, since

\sigma^{\alpha}_{n{\rm x}}
refers to a single species
and so commutes with
\sigma^{\alpha}_{n{\rm y}}

unless
m=n

and

{\rm y}={\rm x}
. Additionally,
these equations contain expectations of pairs of operators
and so
Eqs.

S5a
to
S5c
does not form
a closed set of equations.

To close
Eqs.

S5a
to
S5c
, we apply the
mean-field approximation by factorizing the expectations

\langle\sigma^{z}_{n{\rm x}}a^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{k}\rangle\approx\langle\sigma^{z}_{n{\rm x}}\rangle\langle a^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{k}\rangle
,

\langle\sigma_{n{\rm x}}^{-}a^{\dagger}_{k}\rangle\approx\langle\sigma_{n{\rm x}}^{-}\rangle\langle a^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{k}\rangle^{*}
. As explained in
the Letter, this follows directly from a product
state ansatz for the total many-body state. Specifically,
it is assumed

\displaystyle\rho=\bigotimes_{k=1}^{N_{k}}\rho_{k}\bigotimes_{n=1}^{N_{k}}\bigotimes_{{\rm x}=1}^{N_{s}}\rho_{n{\rm x}},

(S6)

where
\rho_{k}
is a density matrix for the

k^{\text{th}}
photon mode and
\rho_{n{\rm x}}
are the
density matrices for each molecule. The equations become

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{t}\langle a^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{k}\rangle&=-\bigl(i{\omega}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{k}+\kappa/2\bigr)\langle a^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{k}\rangle\\
&-ig\sum_{n=1}^{N_{k}}\sum_{{\rm x}=1}^{N_{s}}e^{-ikr_{n}}\langle\sigma^{-}_{n{\rm x}}\rangle,\end{split}

(S7a)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{t}\langle\sigma_{n{\rm x}}^{-}\rangle&=-\bigl(i\omega^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{0}+\gamma_{\phi}/2\bigr)\langle\sigma_{n{\rm x}}^{-}\rangle\\
&+ig\langle\sigma^{z}_{n{\rm x}}\rangle\sum_{k=1}^{N_{k}}e^{ikr_{n}}\langle a^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{k}\rangle,\end{split}

(S7b)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\langle\sigma_{n{\rm x}}^{z}\rangle

\displaystyle=-4g\sum_{k=1}^{N_{k}}\imaginary[\langle\sigma_{n{\rm x}}^{-}\rangle\langle a^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{k}\rangle^{*}e^{-ikr_{n}}].

(S7c)

Finally, since
the on-site properties
\langle\sigma_{n{\rm x}}^{\alpha}\rangle

are the same for any
{\rm x}=1,\ldots,N_{s}
at fixed

n
(i.e., molecules at the same location), we can
perform the sum over
{\rm x}
in
Eq.

S7a
and write

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{t}\langle a^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{k}\rangle&=-\bigl(i{\omega}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{k}+\kappa/2\bigr)\langle a^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{k}\rangle\\
&-igN_{s}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{k}}e^{-ikr_{n}}\langle\sigma^{-}_{n}\rangle,\end{split}

(S8a)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{t}\langle\sigma_{n}^{-}\rangle&=-\bigl(i\omega^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{0}+\gamma_{\phi}/2\bigr)\langle\sigma_{n}^{-}\rangle\\
&+ig\langle\sigma^{z}_{n}\rangle\sum_{k=1}^{N_{k}}e^{ikr_{n}}\langle a^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{k}\rangle,\end{split}

(S8b)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\langle\sigma_{n}^{z}\rangle

\displaystyle=-4g\sum_{k=1}^{N_{k}}\imaginary[\langle\sigma_{n}^{-}\rangle\langle a^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{k}\rangle^{*}e^{-ikr_{n}}],

(S8c)

where now
\langle\sigma_{n}^{\alpha}\rangle
denotes
the expectation for
any one
of the
identical spin variables at
r_{n}
.

S2.3
Photonic Variables in Real Space

It is convenient to evaluate the photon variables in
real space by introducing the transformed
operators,

\displaystyle a^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{n}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{k}}}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{k}}e^{ikr_{n}}a^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{k},

(S9)

from which

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{t}\langle a^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{n}\rangle&=-\frac{i}{\sqrt{N_{k}}}\sum_{k}e^{ikr_{n}}{\omega}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{k}\langle a^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{k}\rangle-\bigl(\kappa/2\bigr)\langle a^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{n}\rangle\\
&-igN_{s}\sqrt{N_{k}}\langle\sigma^{-}_{n}\rangle,\end{split}

(S10a)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{t}\langle\sigma_{n}^{-}\rangle&=-\bigl(i\omega^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{0}+\gamma_{\phi}/2\bigr)\langle\sigma_{n}^{-}\rangle\\
&+ig\sqrt{N_{k}}\langle\sigma^{z}_{n}\rangle\langle a^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{n}\rangle,\end{split}

(S10b)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\langle\sigma_{n}^{z}\rangle

\displaystyle=-4g\sqrt{N_{k}}\imaginary[\langle\sigma_{n}^{-}\rangle\langle a^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{n}\rangle^{*}],

(S10c)

having used

\sum_{m=1}^{N_{k}}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{k}}e^{-ikr_{m}}=N_{k}\delta_{nm}

to obtain the second line in
Eq.

S10a
. To resolve
the remaining sum in this equation, we note that, for

{\omega}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{k}={\omega}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{c}+k^{2}c^{2}/2{\omega}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{c}
,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{k}}}&\sum_{k=1}^{N_{k}}e^{ikr_{n}}{\omega}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{k}a^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{k}}}{\omega}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{c}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{k}}e^{ikr_{n}}a^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{k}\\
&+\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{k}}}\frac{c^{2}}{2{\omega}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{c}}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{k}}e^{ikr_{n}}k^{2}a^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{k}\end{split}

\displaystyle={\omega}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{c}a^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{n}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{k}}}\frac{c^{2}}{2{\omega}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{c}}\partial_{r_{n}}^{2}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N_{k}}e^{ikr_{n}}a^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{k}\right)

(S11)

\displaystyle={\omega}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{c}a^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{n}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{k}}}\frac{c^{2}}{2{\omega}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{c}\Delta r^{2}}\partial_{n}^{2}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N_{k}}e^{ikr_{n}}a^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{k}\right)

\displaystyle={\omega}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{c}a^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{n}+\frac{c^{2}}{{\omega}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{c}\Delta r^{2}}\partial_{n}^{2}a^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{n},

where the second last line follows from the chain rule.
Hence a quadratic dispersion maps to a second spatial
derivative, and the equations in real space read

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{t}\langle a^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{n}\rangle&=-\bigl(i\omega^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{c}+\kappa/2\bigr)\langle a^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{n}\rangle-igN_{s}\sqrt{N_{k}}\langle\sigma^{-}_{n}\rangle\\
&+\frac{ic^{2}}{2\omega^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{c}\Delta r^{2}}\langle a^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{n}\rangle,\end{split}

(S12a)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{t}\langle\sigma_{n}^{-}\rangle&=-\bigl(i\omega^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{0}+\gamma_{\phi}/2\bigr)\langle\sigma_{n}^{-}\rangle\\
&+ig\sqrt{N_{k}}\langle\sigma^{z}_{n}\rangle\langle a^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{n}\rangle,\end{split}

(S12b)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\langle\sigma_{n}^{z}\rangle

\displaystyle=-4g\sqrt{N_{k}}\imaginary[\langle\sigma_{n}^{-}\rangle\langle a^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{n}\rangle^{*}].

(S12c)

To obtain Eqs. (4a)-(4c) in the Letter
it remains to identify

\Omega=g\sqrt{N}
,
C=c^{2}/2\omega^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{c}

and use the rescaled photon variable

\tilde{a}_{n}^{\phantom{\dagger}}=\sqrt{N_{k}/N}a^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{n}
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{t}\langle\tilde{a}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{n}\rangle&=-\bigl(i{\omega}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{c}+\kappa/2\bigr)\langle\tilde{a}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{n}\rangle-i\Omega\langle\sigma^{-}_{n}\rangle\\
&\phantom{=}\ +iC\partial_{n}^{2}\langle\tilde{a}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{n}\rangle+\sqrt{\kappa}\langle\tilde{a}_{n}^{\text{in}}\rangle,\end{split}

(S13a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\langle\sigma^{-}_{n}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\bigl(i\omega_{0}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}+\gamma_{\phi}/2\bigr)\langle\sigma^{-}_{n}\rangle+i\Omega\langle\sigma^{z}_{n}\rangle\langle\tilde{a}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{n}\rangle,

(S13b)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\langle\sigma^{z}_{n}\rangle

\displaystyle=-4\Omega\imaginary\left[\langle\sigma^{-}_{n}\rangle\langle\tilde{a}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{n}\rangle^{*}\right].

(S13c)

S3
Polaritonic Beer-Lambert law

To obtain an analytical expression for the propagation of photonic modes in the
cavity, we linearize the mean-field equations by writing
\langle\sigma^{z}_{n}\rangle=z_{n}^{(0,0)}+\delta z_{n}
, where
z_{n}^{(0,0)}
is
time-independent. In a leading-order approximation, we assume that changes to
the molecular population are small,
|\delta z_{n}|\ll|z_{n}^{(0,0)}|
, such
that
Eqs.

S13a
and
S13b

for the photon and coherence expectations
form a closed set:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{t}\langle\tilde{a}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{n}\rangle&=-(i{\omega}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{c}+\kappa/2)\langle\tilde{a}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{n}\rangle-i\Omega\langle\sigma^{-}_{n}\rangle\\
&\phantom{=}\ +iC\partial_{n}^{2}\langle\tilde{a}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{n}\rangle,\end{split}

(S14a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\langle\sigma^{-}_{n}\rangle

\displaystyle=-(i\omega_{0}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}+\gamma_{\phi}/2)\langle\sigma^{-}_{n}\rangle+i\Omega z^{(0,0)}_{n}\langle\tilde{a}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{n}\rangle.

(S14b)

For simplicity we set
\langle\tilde{a}^{\text{in}}_{n}\rangle=0
, to derive
an expression after the driving fields have switched off, but the following is
readily generalized to include non-zero input.

Equations

S14a
and
S14b
can be solved by taking Fourier transforms.
Defining
\alpha_{n}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}(\omega)=\int dt\,e^{i\omega t}\langle\tilde{a}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{n}\rangle(t)
,
s_{n}(\omega)=\int dt\,e^{i\omega t}\langle{\sigma}^{-}_{n}\rangle(t)
, we find

\displaystyle-i\omega s_{n}(\omega)

\displaystyle=-(i\omega_{0}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}+\gamma_{\phi}/2)s_{n}(\omega)+i\Omega z^{(0,0)}_{n}\alpha_{n}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}(\omega)

(S15)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow s_{n}(\omega)

\displaystyle=-\Omega\chi(\omega)\alpha_{n}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}(\omega),

(S16)

where the molecular susceptibility is defined as

\displaystyle\chi(\omega)=\frac{z_{n}^{(0,0)}}{(\omega-\omega_{0}^{\vphantom{\dagger}})+i\gamma_{\phi}/2}.

(S17)

For a uniform molecular population (e.g., all molecules in the ground state,

z_{n}^{(0,0)}\equiv-1
),
\chi(\omega)
is independent of position
n
.

Substituting
Eq.

S16
into the equation for

\alpha_{n}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}(\omega)
yields

\displaystyle\begin{split}-i\omega\alpha_{n}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}(\omega)&=-(i{\omega}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{c}+\kappa/2)\alpha_{n}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}(\omega)+iC\partial_{n}^{2}\alpha_{n}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}(\omega)\\
&\phantom{=}\ -i\Omega s_{n}(\omega)\end{split}

(S18)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\partial_{n}^{2}\alpha_{n}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\lambda^{2}(\omega)\alpha_{n}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}(\omega),

(S19)

where
\lambda^{2}(\omega)=-(1/C)\left[(\omega-{\omega}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{c})+i\kappa/2+\Omega^{2}\chi(\omega)\right]
relates to the retarded photon Green’s function, as described in the
Letter.

Equation

S19
is a (1D) Helmholtz equation, which arises commonly, e.g.,
in electromagnetism
[
28
]
and quantum scattering
problems
[
41
]
. It admits two solutions:

\displaystyle\alpha_{n}^{\rightarrow}(\omega)=Ae^{\lambda(\omega)n},\quad\alpha_{n}^{\leftarrow}(\omega)=Ae^{-\lambda(\omega)n},

(S20)

corresponding to right- and left-propagating waves when
\imaginary\lambda(\omega)>0
. Accordingly, the real part of the propagation constant,
\real\lambda(\omega)=\real[D^{R}(\omega)]^{-1/2}
governs the decay of the
intracavity field at frequency
\omega
, as expressed in Eq. (6) of the Letter.
We note further that, whilst derived for our simplified
model comprising two-level systems, this result can be naturally generalized to
complex multi-level systems where the susceptibility
\chi(\omega)
is
replaced by the full molecular self-energy
[
54
]
.

S4
Bright and dark exciton populations

Bright excitonic states are symmetric superpositions of all emitter states that
couple to a cavity mode
[
56
]
. From the interaction in
Eq.

S1
, we
define the bright-state operator for the
k^{\text{th}}
mode as:

\displaystyle\sigma^{+}_{k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}e^{-ikr_{n}}\sigma^{+}_{n}.

(S21)

Applying our spatial coarse-graining to
N_{k}
positions, we rewrite the sum
over all molecules as a sum over positions
r_{n}=n\Delta r

(
n=1,\ldots,N_{k}
) and the
N_{s}
molecules at each position:

\displaystyle\sigma^{+}_{k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{k}}\sum_{{\rm x}=1}^{\text{$N_{s}$}}e^{-ikr_{n}}\sigma^{+}_{n{\rm x}}.

(S22)

The total bright excitonic population as a function of position is then
obtained from

\displaystyle p^{B\text{-tot}}_{n}=\frac{1}{N_{k}}\sum_{k,k^{\prime}}\langle\sigma^{+}_{k}\sigma^{-}_{k^{\prime}}\rangle e^{i(k^{\prime}-k)r_{n}}.

(S23)

When evaluating
Eq.

S23
, care must be taken when calculating
coherences
\langle\sigma^{+}_{n{\rm x}}\sigma^{-}_{m{\rm y}}\rangle
for
n=m
and

{\rm x}={\rm y}
, i.e., when the indices refer to the same molecule. In this case,

\langle\sigma^{+}_{n{\rm x}}\sigma^{-}_{n{\rm x}}\rangle\equiv(1+\langle\sigma^{z}_{n{\rm x}}\rangle)/2
, and a mean-field approximation should not be applied since the operators are acting on the same molecule. With this
in mind, from
Eq.

S23
it follows that

\displaystyle p^{B\text{-tot}}_{n}

\displaystyle=(\text{$N_{s}$}-1)|\langle\sigma^{+}_{n{\rm x}}\rangle|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\langle\sigma^{z}_{n{\rm x}}\rangle+1\right).

(S24)

Since molecules at the same position share identical on-site properties, we
write
\langle\sigma_{n{\rm x}}^{+}\rangle=\langle\sigma_{n}^{+}\rangle
, where

\langle\sigma_{n}^{+}\rangle
is the expectation for any molecule at
n
. In
the large-
N
limit, where
\text{$N_{s}$}=N/N_{k}\gg 1
, we obtain

\displaystyle p^{B\text{-tot}}_{n}

\displaystyle\approx N_{s}|\langle\sigma^{+}_{n}\rangle|^{2}.

(S25)

The dark state population
p^{D\text{-tot}}_{n}
is given by the total
molecular population at
n
,

p^{m\text{-tot}}_{n}=\text{$N_{s}$}(1+\langle\sigma^{z}_{n}\rangle)
,
minus the bright-state contribution:

\displaystyle p^{D\text{-tot}}_{n}

\displaystyle=p^{m\text{-tot}}_{n}-p^{B\text{-tot}}_{n}

\displaystyle\approx\text{$N_{s}$}\left[\left(1+\langle\sigma^{z}_{n}\rangle\right)/2-|\langle\sigma^{+}_{n}\rangle|^{2}\right].

(S26)

Eq. (8a) and (8b) of the Letter for the dynamics of the scaled populations

p^{B}_{n}=p^{B\text{-tot}}_{n}/N_{E}
and
p^{D}_{n}=p^{D\text{-tot}}_{n}/\text{$N_{s}$}
follow
straightforwardly from the mean-field
Eqs.

S13b
and
S13c
for

\langle\sigma^{-}_{n}\rangle
and
\langle\sigma^{z}_{n}\rangle
.

S5
Derivation of Spectroscopic Observables

In this section, we provide steps for calculating spectroscopic
observables within the mean-field approach.
We focus on the differential transmission in a pump-probe
experiment as considered in the Letter, but our framework may
be applied in principle to determine linear and non-linear
observables in pump-probe spectroscopy configurations
in cavity-QED systems.

The derivation proceeds in two main steps,
firstly establishing the perturbative treatment of multiple input
pulses, then connecting the resulting field amplitudes to
measurable observables.

S5.1
Input Pulses and Perturbative Expansion

Dynamical information in a non-linear spectroscopic experiment is
obtained by interacting the system with
multiple
light pulses.
The fact that there is more than one input pulse means that
it is not possible to think simply in terms of evolution from
a fixed initial state, which is the most common approach when obtaining quantum
dynamics in theoretical calculations. Instead, one must consider
these pulses as driving terms of the photonic (cavity) field in the
evolution, hence adding the input field
\langle\tilde{a}^{\text{in}}_{n}(t)\rangle
to

Eq.

S12a
,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{t}\langle\tilde{a}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{n}\rangle&=-\bigl(i{\omega}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{c}+\kappa/2\bigr)\langle\tilde{a}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{n}\rangle-i\Omega\langle\sigma^{-}_{n}\rangle\\
&\phantom{=}\ +iC\partial_{n}^{2}\langle\tilde{a}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{n}\rangle+\sqrt{\kappa}\langle\tilde{a}_{n}^{\text{in}}\rangle,\end{split}

(S27)

which is Eq. (4a) of the Letter.

For a pump-probe experiment, we consider two input pulses.
Each input pulse (
\beta
)
is characterized by its magnitude,
\eta_{\beta}
, phase

e^{-i\omega^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\beta}t}

(frequency
\omega^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\beta}
),
and shape in both time
f_{\beta}(t)

and

space
D_{n}^{\beta}
(equivalently, time and momentum space).
Writing these components in setting

\displaystyle\sqrt{\kappa}\langle\tilde{a}^{\text{in}}_{n}(t)\rangle=\eta_{p}f_{p}(t)e^{-i{\omega}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{p}t}D_{n}^{p}+\eta_{p^{\prime}}f_{p^{\prime}}(t)e^{-i{\omega}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{p^{\prime}}t}D_{n}^{p^{\prime}},

(S28)

allows one to specify
two pulses—i.e., pump (
p
) and probe (
p^{\prime}
)—of
any possible shape. In the Letter we consider
Gaussian pulses in the form

\displaystyle f_{\beta}(t)e^{-i\omega_{\beta}^{\phantom{\dagger}}t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma_{t}^{2}}}e^{-(t-t_{0})^{2}/(2\sigma_{t}^{2})}e^{-i\omega_{k_{\beta}}^{\text{LP}}t},

(S29a)

\displaystyle D_{n}^{\beta}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}e^{-(n-n_{0})^{2}/(2\sigma_{n}^{2})}e^{ik_{\beta}n\Delta r},

(S29b)

where the normalization
\mathcal{N}
is chosen such that

\sum_{n}\absolutevalue*{D_n^\beta}^{2}=1
and
\sigma_{n}=\sigma_{r}/\Delta r
defines the pulse width in space.
As can be seen, information of the target input wavevector
k_{\beta}

is imprinted on the spatial profile

D_{n}^{\beta}
as a modulation
e^{ik_{\beta}r_{n}}
.

The key insight underlying our method is that these input pulses are nominally

weak
in the sense that one may consider them as
perturbations to the system’s normal state

\langle a^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{n}\rangle=\langle\sigma^{-}_{n}\rangle=0
,

\langle\sigma^{z}_{n}\rangle=-1
, i.e., an empty cavity. This permits
a perturbative expansion of the system state in powers of
the pulse magnitudes,

\displaystyle\begin{gathered}\langle a^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{n}\rangle=\sum_{a,b}\eta_{p}^{a}\eta_{p^{\prime}}^{b}\alpha^{(a,b)}_{n},\\
\langle\sigma^{-}_{n}\rangle=\sum_{a,b}\eta_{p}^{a}\eta_{p^{\prime}}^{b}s^{(a,b)}_{n},\quad\langle\sigma^{z}_{n}\rangle=\sum_{a,b}\eta_{p}^{a}\eta_{p^{\prime}}^{b}z^{(a,b)}_{n},\end{gathered}

where
\alpha^{(0,0)}_{n}=s^{(0,0)}_{n}=0
,
z_{n}^{(0,0)}=-1

correspond to the normal state.

S5.2
Differential Transmission
\Delta T_{n}

We define the differential transmission as the
normalized transmission
T_{n}(\omega)
of the probe pulse
with and without the pump pulse
[
35
]
:

\displaystyle\Delta T_{n}(\omega)=T_{n}^{p-\mathrm{on}}(\omega)-T_{n}^{p-\mathrm{off}}(\omega).

(S32)

In particular, we omit from
T_{n}^{p-\mathrm{on}}
any terms involving the pump only
(e.g.,
\alpha^{(1,0)}_{n}
below), to correctly obtain the probe transmission.

From the input-output relations for cavity transmission
[
44
]
,
the output field at each position
n

is directly proportional to the internal cavity field
\langle\tilde{a}_{n}^{\phantom{\dagger}}\rangle
. The
transmitted intensities are proportional to the square of the output field
and so, keeping terms up to third order as well as only linear contributions in the probe field,

\displaystyle T_{n}^{p-\mathrm{off}}(\omega)

\displaystyle\propto(\kappa/2)^{2}|\alpha_{n}^{(0,1)}(\omega)|^{2},

(S33a)

\displaystyle T_{n}^{p-\mathrm{on}}(\omega)

\displaystyle\propto(\kappa/2)^{2}|\alpha_{n}^{(0,1)}(\omega)+\eta_{p}^{2}\alpha_{n}^{(2,1)}(\omega)|^{2}.

(S33b)

Since
\eta_{p^{\prime}}\ll\eta_{p}
, we neglect, in particular, the
(1,2)
contribution, which is suppressed relative to the
(2,1)
term by an additional power of the weak probe amplitude. Moreover, because the detected signal is selected along the probe propagation direction, higher-order contributions generally fulfill different phase-matching conditions and radiate into different momentum channels. They therefore do not contribute to the measured response considered here, as illustrated in Fig.
S1
, which schematically shows the
k
-space distributions of the linear and third-order cavity fields as obtained from a Fourier space analysis of Eqs. (
S35
).

Figure S1:
Momentum-space distributions of linear (left) and third-order cavity fields (right).
The linear pump-induced cavity field
\alpha^{(1,0)}
is centered around
k_{p}
, while the linear probe-induced cavity field
\alpha^{(0,1)}
is centered around
k_{p^{\prime}}
. At third order, the
(2,1)
contribution contains the phase-matching channels

k_{p}-k_{p}+k_{p^{\prime}}=k_{p^{\prime}}
and
k_{p}+k_{p}-k_{p^{\prime}}
, where the first combination corresponds to the standard pump-probe signal,
and the second to the so-called double-quantum-coherence signal, which is not of interest in this work.
Since the detected signal is selected along the probe direction
k_{p^{\prime}}
, only the pump-probe contribution of
(2,1)
contributes to the measured response. Note for the experiment considered in the Letter,

k_{p}=-k_{p^{\prime}}=\pi/2

\mu\text{m}^{-1}
and
\absolutevalue{2k_p-k_\pp}=\absolutevalue{2k_\pp-k_p}=3k_{p}
.

We therefore obtain (keeping only the leading third-order correction to the differential transmission)

\displaystyle\Delta T_{n}(\omega)\propto\eta_{p}^{2}(\kappa^{2}/2)\mathrm{Re}[\alpha_{n}^{(0,1)}(\omega)\overline{\alpha}_{n}^{(2,1)}(\omega)],

(S34)

which is Eq. (10) of the Letter. Note we do not
normalize this quantity.

S6
Perturbative pump-probe equations

As explained above, the leading contribution to the nonlinear
differential transmission
\Delta T_{n}
arises from terms second order in the
pump and first order in the probe
[
35
]
:
\alpha^{(0,1)}_{n}
and

\alpha^{(2,1)}_{n}
. The equations of motion for variables at a given order
depend on those of equal or lower order. Thus, in principle, one requires the
dynamics of all
\alpha^{(a,b)}_{n}
,
\sigma^{(a,b)}_{n}
, and

z^{(a,b)}_{n}
for
a\leq 2
,
b\leq 1
.

However, many of these variables remain trivial for the initial conditions we
consider,
\langle\tilde{a}^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{n}\rangle=\langle\sigma^{-}_{n}\rangle=0
,
\langle\sigma^{z}_{n}\rangle=-1
at
t=0
.
This ensures all
\alpha^{(a,b)}_{n}
,
\sigma^{(a,b)}_{n}
, and

z^{(a,b)}_{n}
are initially zero, except for
z^{(0,0)}_{n}\equiv-1
. The
resulting nontrivial set of equations is:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\alpha_{n}^{(1,0)}

\displaystyle=-\left(i{\omega}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{c}+\kappa/2-iC\partial^{2}_{n}\right)\alpha_{n}^{(1,0)}-i\Omega\sigma_{n}^{(1,0)}+D^{p}_{n}f_{p}(t)e^{-i{\omega}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{p}t},

(S35)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\sigma_{n}^{(1,0)}

\displaystyle=-(i{\omega}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{0}+\gamma_{\phi}/2)\sigma_{n}^{(1,0)}+i\Omega z_{n}^{(0,0)}\alpha_{n}^{(1,0)},

\displaystyle\partial_{t}z_{n}^{(2,0)}

\displaystyle=-4\Omega\imaginary\left[\sigma_{n}^{(1,0)}\overline{\alpha}_{n}^{(1,0)}\right],

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\alpha_{n}^{(0,1)}

\displaystyle=-\left(i{\omega}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{c}+\kappa/2-iC\partial^{2}_{n}\right)\alpha_{n}^{(0,1)}-i\Omega\sigma_{n}^{(0,1)}+D^{p^{\prime}}_{n}f_{p^{\prime}}(t)e^{-i{\omega}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{{p^{\prime}}}t},

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\sigma_{n}^{(0,1)}

\displaystyle=-(i{\omega}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{0}+\gamma_{\phi}/2)\sigma_{n}^{(0,1)}+i\Omega z_{n}^{(0,0)}\alpha_{n}^{(0,1)},

\displaystyle\partial_{t}z_{n}^{(1,1)}

\displaystyle=-4\Omega\imaginary\left[\sigma_{n}^{(1,0)}\overline{\alpha}_{n}^{(0,1)}+\sigma_{n}^{(0,1)}\overline{\alpha}_{n}^{(1,0)}\right],

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\alpha_{n}^{(2,1)}

\displaystyle=-\left(i{\omega}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{c}+\kappa/2-iC\partial^{2}_{n}\right)\alpha_{n}^{(2,1)}-i\Omega\sigma_{n}^{(2,1)},

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\sigma_{n}^{(2,1)}

\displaystyle=-(i{\omega}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{0}+\gamma_{\phi}/2)\sigma_{n}^{(2,1)}+i\Omega\left(z_{n}^{(0,0)}\alpha_{n}^{(2,1)}+z_{n}^{(1,1)}\alpha_{n}^{(1,0)}+z_{n}^{(2,0)}\alpha_{n}^{(0,1)}\right).

Here, we have explicitly included the pump and probe
driving terms from
Eq.

S28
. As in the Letter, an overbar
is used to denote a complex conjugate. These equations were
obtained by first deriving the Heisenberg equations of motion
for the single-operator expectations

\langle\tilde{a}^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{n}\rangle
,

\langle\sigma^{-}_{n}\rangle
, and

\langle\sigma^{z}_{n}\rangle
from the master equation
(
S2
). The mean-field approximation,

\langle\tilde{a}^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{n}\sigma^{z}_{n}\rangle\approx\langle\tilde{a}^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{n}\rangle\langle\sigma^{z}_{n}\rangle,\ \langle\tilde{a}^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{n}\sigma^{+}_{n}\rangle\approx\langle\tilde{a}^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{n}\rangle\langle\sigma^{-}_{n}\rangle^{*},

was applied before making the expansion described by

Section

S5.1
and
collecting terms of identical order in
\eta_{p}
and
\eta_{{p^{\prime}}}
.

S7
Transport Velocity from Counter-Propagating Pulses

Figure S2:
Calculating transport velocities in the counter-propagating experiment. Red and green arrows mark the initial spatial locations of the pump and probe spots, respectively, which
are initially separated a distance

2r_{0}=100\ \mu\text{m}
.

In this section, we show how polariton transport velocities are extracted from
the motion of the differential signal
\Delta T_{n}
in Fig. 3 of the Letter
arising from counter-propagating pump and probe pulses.

Suppose the probe is delayed by
\Delta\tau
and that after a time
t
the
pump has advanced to a position
x
to the right of the origin
(see sketch in
Fig.

S2
). The total distance traversed by the pump is
r_{0}+x

over the time interval
t
, giving a transport velocity of

v_{k_{p}}^{\phantom{\dagger}}=(r_{0}+x)/t
>0. The probe, on the other hand,
traveled a distance
r_{0}-x
in a time
t-\Delta\tau
reduced by the delay,
giving
v_{k_{p^{\prime}}}^{\phantom{\dagger}}=-(r_{0}-x)/(t-\Delta\tau)<0
. Here, we used the symmetry of the polariton dispersion about
k=0
, which implies that the
group velocities have equal magnitude and opposite sign for
k_{p^{\prime}}=-k_{p}
, i.e.,

v_{k_{p^{\prime}}}=-v_{k_{p}}
.

From the probe equation,

\displaystyle v_{k_{p}}^{\phantom{\dagger}}(t-\Delta\tau)=r_{0}-x.

(S36)

Using the pump equation

v_{k_{p}}^{\phantom{\dagger}}t=r_{0}+x
, we obtain:

\displaystyle(r_{0}+x)-v_{k_{p}}^{\phantom{\dagger}}\Delta\tau

\displaystyle=r_{0}-x

(S37)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow v_{k_{p}}^{\phantom{\dagger}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2x}{\Delta\tau}.

Thus, the transport velocity is determined as
twice
the speed

dx/d(\Delta\tau)
of the signal arising from the counter-propagating pulses.

To connect this result to the velocities
v_{k_{p}}^{\text{peak}}
and
v_{k_{p}}^{\text{rms}}

reported in Fig. 4 of the Letter, note that the differential transmission
signal is an extended object, and therefore a choice must be made to
define
x
during the experiment. Choosing the maximum absolute value of
the differential signal gives the peak velocity
v_{k_{p}}^{\text{peak}}
,
whilst choosing the root-mean-square displacement of the signal yields

v_{k_{p}}^{\text{rms}}
.

S8
Transport with Exciton Hopping and Exciton-Exciton
Annihilation

In this section, we provide results for the polariton transport in the presence of additional processes that may become important in realistic materials such as organic semiconductors: exciton hopping and exciton–exciton pair annihilation
[
17
,
26
]
. Exciton hopping enables energy transfer between neighboring sites, while exciton–exciton annihilation introduces nonlinear dissipation.

S8.1
Derivation of Perturbative Equations

These processes may be readily included in our framework
with the extended model (cf.
Eq.

S38
)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{t}\rho=&-i\bigl[H+H_{\text{hop}},\rho\bigr]+\sum_{k=1}^{N_{k}}\kappa\mathcal{L}[a^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{k}]\\
&+\sum_{n=1}^{N_{k}}\sum_{{\rm x}=1}^{N_{s}}\frac{\gamma_{\phi}}{4}\mathcal{L}[\sigma^{z}_{n{\rm x}}]\\
&+\sum_{n=1}^{N_{k}}\sum_{{\rm x},{\rm y}=1}^{N_{s}}\gamma^{\text{ee}}\mathcal{L}[\sigma^{-}_{n{\rm x}}\sigma^{-}_{n{\rm y}}].\end{split}

(S38)

The parameter
\gamma^{\text{ee}}
sets the rate of exciton-exciton
annihilation, an incoherent process which becomes relevant for many organic
materials at high optical intensities under intense pulse excitation
[
1
]
, while

H_{\text{hop}}
describes coherent hopping between neighboring coarse-grained sites,

\displaystyle H_{\text{hop}}=-J\sum_{n=1}^{N_{k}}\sum_{{\rm x},{\rm y}=1}^{N_{s}}\bigl(\sigma^{+}_{n{\rm x}}\sigma^{-}_{n+1,{\rm y}}+\text{H.c}\bigr),

(S39)

with hopping amplitude
J
. The importance of inter-site hopping is set by whether the exciton diffusion length is comparable to the coarse-graining scale
\Delta r
.

Since the additional terms involve only molecular operators, the equation
of motion for the photonic variable, Eq. (4a) of the main text, remains unchanged.
Those for the spin variables acquire additional terms proportional to
J
and

\gamma^{\text{ee}}
. Using the same coarse-graining described
above, under the mean-field approximation, one has

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{t}\langle\sigma^{-}_{n}\rangle&=-\bigl(i\omega_{0}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}+\gamma_{\phi}/2\bigr)\langle\sigma^{-}_{n}\rangle+i\Omega\langle\sigma^{z}_{n}\rangle\langle\tilde{a}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{n}\rangle\\
&\phantom{=}\ -iJN_{s}\langle\sigma^{z}_{n}\rangle\left(\langle\sigma_{n+1}^{-}\rangle+\langle\sigma_{n-1}^{-}\rangle\right)\\
&\phantom{=}\ -(\gamma^{\text{ee}}/2)(N_{s}-1)\langle\sigma_{n}^{-}\rangle\left(1+\langle\sigma^{z}_{n}\rangle\right),\end{split}

(S40a)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{t}\langle\sigma^{z}_{n}\rangle&=-4\Omega\imaginary\left[\langle\sigma^{-}_{n}\rangle\langle\tilde{a}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{n}\rangle^{*}\right]\\
&\phantom{=}\ -4JN_{s}\imaginary\left[\langle\sigma_{n}^{-}\rangle^{\ast}\left(\langle\sigma_{n+1}^{-}\rangle+\langle\sigma_{n-1}^{-}\rangle\right)\right]\\
&\phantom{=}\ -\gamma^{\text{ee}}(N_{s}-1)\left(1+2\langle\sigma^{z}_{n}\rangle+\langle\sigma^{z}_{n}\rangle^{2}\right).\end{split}

(S40b)

It remains to determine revised equations for the coefficients

\alpha_{n}^{(a,b)}
,
\sigma^{(a,b)}
,
z^{(a,b)}_{n}
in the
perturbation expansion. Those for the photonic variable are unchanged
from
Eq.

S35
, whilst those for the spin variables acquire
new local and non-local terms. Noting
z_{n}^{(0,0)}\equiv-1
still,
the resulting modified equations read

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\sigma_{n}^{(1,0)}

\displaystyle=-(i{\omega}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{0}+\gamma_{\phi}/2)\sigma_{n}^{(1,0)}-i\Omega\alpha_{n}^{(1,0)}+iJN_{s}\left(\sigma^{(1,0)}_{n-1}+\sigma^{(1,0)}_{n+1}\right),

(S41)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}z_{n}^{(2,0)}

\displaystyle=-4\Omega\imaginary\left[\sigma_{n}^{(1,0)}\overline{\alpha}_{n}^{(1,0)}\right]-4JN_{s}\imaginary\left[\overline{\sigma}_{n}^{(1,0)}\left(\sigma^{(1,0)}_{n-1}+\sigma^{(1,0)}_{n+1}\right)\right]-2\gamma^{\text{ee}}(N_{s}-1)z^{(2,0)}_{n},

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\sigma_{n}^{(0,1)}

\displaystyle=-(i{\omega}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{0}+\gamma_{\phi}/2)\sigma_{n}^{(0,1)}-i\Omega\alpha_{n}^{(0,1)}+iJN_{s}\left(\sigma^{(0,1)}_{n-1}+\sigma^{(0,1)}_{n+1}\right),

\displaystyle\partial_{t}z_{n}^{(1,1)}

\displaystyle=-4\Omega\imaginary\left[\sigma_{n}^{(1,0)}\overline{\alpha}_{n}^{(0,1)}+\sigma_{n}^{(0,1)}\overline{\alpha}_{n}^{(1,0)}\right]

\displaystyle-4JN_{s}\imaginary\left[\overline{\sigma}_{n}^{(1,0)}\left(\sigma^{(0,1)}_{n-1}+\sigma^{(0,1)}_{n+1}\right)+\overline{\sigma}_{n}^{(0,1)}\left(\sigma^{(1,0)}_{n-1}+\sigma^{(1,0)}_{n+1}\right)\right]

\displaystyle-2\gamma^{\text{ee}}(N_{s}-1)z_{n}^{(1,1)},

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\sigma_{n}^{(2,1)}

\displaystyle=-(i{\omega}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{0}+\gamma_{\phi}/2)\sigma_{n}^{(2,1)}+i\Omega\left(z_{n}^{(1,1)}\alpha_{n}^{(1,0)}+z_{n}^{(2,0)}\alpha_{n}^{(0,1)}-\alpha_{n}^{(2,1)}\right)

\displaystyle+iJN_{s}\left[\left(\sigma^{(2,1)}_{n-1}+\sigma^{(2,1)}_{n+1}\right)-z^{(2,0)}_{n}\left(\sigma^{(0,1)}_{n-1}+\sigma^{(0,1)}_{n+1}\right)-z^{(1,1)}_{n}\left(\sigma^{(1,0)}_{n-1}+\sigma^{(1,0)}_{n+1}\right)\right]

\displaystyle-(\gamma^{\text{ee}}/2)(N_{s}-1)\left(z_{n}^{(1,1)}s^{(1,0)}_{n}+z_{n}^{(2,0)}s^{(0,1)}_{n}\right).

Hopping processes introduce non-local terms in the spin variables, enabling transport between neighboring sites at an effective rate
JN_{s}
proportional to the number of emitters per site
N_{s}
. However, note that the
site spacing
\Delta r=L/N_{k}\sim 0.3\ \mu
m is such that this transport may not be
discernible on the scale of the polariton-assisted transport,
even when
JN_{s}
is comparable to the collective light-matter coupling.
On the other hand, exciton-exciton annihilation processes introduce additional loss terms for second-order and higher-order spin variables, with rates that scale similarly with
N_{s}
.

k_{p}=\pi/2

\mu\text{m}^{-1}
. The
results, presented in
Figs.

S7(a)
to
S7(b)
for
0\leq JN_{s}\leq\Omega
, show that, while increasing
J
decreases the group velocity

\tilde{v}^{\text{grp}}
at this
k_{p}
, the transport velocity

v^{\text{obs}}
decreases more than can be accounted for by the decrease in

\tilde{v}^{\text{grp}}
alone, i.e., the renormalization increases. This
trend can be explained by the increasing exciton content (i.e., changing
Hopfield coefficients) of the propagating excitation with increasing hopping.
The subsequent decrease at large
JN_{s}\gtrsim 2\Omega/3
in
Fig.

S7(b)

is likely due to the probe pulse failing to cover the full extent of the signal
(
\Delta\tau=1.2
ps in
Fig.

S6
), as well as by the breakdown of the
perturbation theory when
JN_{s}
is no longer small.

Finally, the addition of exciton-exciton annihilation has a surprising effect on
transport: by providing a loss mechanism for dark, dephased molecular excitations, the
ballistic proportion of the propagating excitation increases,
and hence does the
effective (i.e., rms)
transport velocity. The presence of this effect, i.e., the
depletion of
the ‘dragged’ molecular tail, is highly sensitive to the value of

\gamma_{\text{ee}}
. In
Fig.

S7(c)
, we see a sudden decrease
in renormalization as
\gamma_{\text{ee}}\ll\kappa
is switched on from zero,
then a steady decrease as
\gamma_{\text{ee}}\to\kappa
.
This further demonstrates the complex range of
behaviors in organic materials, where observed transport is not simply determined
by group velocities. Instead, one must also consider the spectroscopic
observable being measured, the nature of the excitation, and relevant loss
processes. We believe the effect of exciton-exciton annihilation is an
under-explored aspect in models of organic polariton transport, as a process
that is often unavoidable in real experiments
[
1
]
.

S9
Molecular Disorder

In this section, we show how static molecular disorder can be included in the model,
allowing for more realistic descriptions of microcavity experiments
involving disordered organic materials.

To incorporate molecular disorder, we consider the generalized Tavis-Cummings
Hamiltonian,

\displaystyle\begin{split}H&=\sum_{k=1}^{N_{k}}{\omega}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{k}a^{\dagger}_{k}a^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{k}+\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{\omega_{n}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}}{2}\sigma^{z}_{n}\\
&+\sum_{k=1}^{N_{k}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}g_{n}(a^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{k}\sigma^{+}_{n}e^{ikr_{n}}+\text{H.c.}),\end{split}

(S42)

in which the energy
{\omega}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{0}\to{\omega}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{n}
and
coupling
g\to g_{n}
became emitter-dependent (note here
n=1,\ldots,N

enumerates the entire molecular ensemble, not spatial position).

To coarse-grain the one-dimensional molecular system, we now
assign three indices to the spin variables:
n
, for the
site position
r_{n}
(
n=1,\ldots,N_{k}
);
i
, for the
class of molecule with energy and coupling

{\omega}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{i},g_{i}
(
i=1,\ldots,M
);
and
{\rm x}_{i}
, for individual molecules within
class
i
. Here
{\rm x}_{i}=1,\ldots,\mu_{i}N_{s}
where
N_{s}
is the number of molecules
per site (as before) and
\mu_{i}
is the fraction of molecules with energies
and couplings within a range about
{\omega}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{i}
and
g_{i}
.
The Hamiltonian is then expressed as (cf.
Eq.

S3
):

\displaystyle\begin{split}H&=\sum_{k=1}^{N_{k}}{\omega}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{k}a^{\dagger}_{k}a^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{k}+\sum_{n=1}^{N_{k}}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\sum_{{\rm x}_{i}=1}^{\mu_{i}N_{s}}\frac{\omega_{i}}{2}\sigma^{z}_{ni{\rm x}_{i}}\\
&+\sum_{k=1}^{N_{k}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{k}}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\sum_{{\rm x}_{i}=1}^{\mu_{i}N_{s}}g_{i}(a^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{k}\sigma^{+}_{ni{\rm x}_{i}}e^{ikr_{n}}+\text{H.c.}),\end{split}

(S43)

and the master equation,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{t}\rho=&-i\bigl[H,\rho\bigr]+\sum_{k=1}^{N_{k}}\kappa\mathcal{L}[a^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{k}]\\
&+\sum_{n=1}^{N_{k}}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\sum_{{\rm x}_{i}=1}^{\mu_{i}N_{s}}\frac{\gamma_{\phi}}{4}\mathcal{L}[\sigma^{z}_{ni{\rm x}_{i}}].\end{split}

(S44)

In the following, we set
g_{i}=g
to obtain results for the case of energetic disorder only, but
results with inhomogeneous couplings can be derived in the same way.

The derivation of the mean-field equations is identical to before up to evaluation
of sums over the molecular indices
{\rm x}_{i}
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{t}\langle a^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{k}\rangle&=-\bigl(i{\omega}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{k}+\kappa/2\bigr)\langle a^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{k}\rangle\\
&-ig\sum_{n=1}^{N_{k}}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\sum_{{\rm x}_{i}=1}^{\mu_{i}N_{s}}e^{-ikr_{n}}\langle\sigma^{-}_{ni{\rm x}_{i}}\rangle,\end{split}

(S45a)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{t}\langle\sigma_{ni{\rm x}_{i}}^{-}\rangle&=-\bigl(i\omega^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{i}+\gamma_{\phi}/2\bigr)\langle\sigma_{ni{\rm x}_{i}}^{-}\rangle\\
&+ig\langle\sigma^{z}_{ni{\rm x}_{i}}\rangle\sum_{k=1}^{N_{k}}e^{ikr_{n}}\langle a^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{k}\rangle,\end{split}

(S45b)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\langle\sigma_{ni{\rm x}_{i}}^{z}\rangle

\displaystyle=-4g\sum_{k=1}^{N_{k}}\imaginary[\langle\sigma_{ni{\rm x}_{i}}^{-}\rangle\langle a^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{k}\rangle^{*}e^{-ikr_{n}}].

(S45c)

It is no longer the case that all molecules on a given site have the same properties. However,
one can still sum over all molecules of the same
class
within a site in
Eq.

S45a
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{t}\langle a^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{k}\rangle&=-\bigl(i{\omega}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{k}+\kappa/2\bigr)\langle a^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{k}\rangle\\
&-igN_{s}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{k}}e^{-ikr_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\mu_{i}\langle\sigma^{-}_{ni}\rangle,\end{split}

(S46a)

where
\langle\sigma_{ni}^{\alpha}\rangle
denotes
the expectation for any one of the
spin variables at
r_{n}
with energy
{\omega}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{i}
.
After transforming the photon variables to real space and rescaling
(
\tilde{a}_{n}^{\phantom{\dagger}}=\sqrt{N_{k}/N}a^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{n}
),
the final equations are

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{t}\langle\tilde{a}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{n}\rangle&=-\bigl(i{\omega}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{c}+\kappa/2\bigr)\langle\tilde{a}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{n}\rangle-i\Omega\sum_{\smash{i=1}}^{M}\mu_{i}\langle\sigma^{-}_{ni}\rangle\\
&\phantom{=}\ +iC\partial_{n}^{2}\langle\tilde{a}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{n}\rangle+\sqrt{\kappa}\langle\tilde{a}_{n}^{\text{in}}\rangle,\end{split}

(S47a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\langle\sigma^{-}_{ni}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\bigl(i\omega_{i}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}+\gamma_{\phi}/2\bigr)\langle\sigma^{-}_{ni}\rangle+i\Omega\langle\sigma^{z}_{ni}\rangle\langle\tilde{a}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{n}\rangle,

(S47b)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\langle\sigma^{z}_{ni}\rangle

\displaystyle=-4\Omega\imaginary\left[\langle\sigma^{-}_{ni}\rangle\langle\tilde{a}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{n}\rangle^{*}\right],

(S47c)

with
\Omega=g\sqrt{N}
and
C=c^{2}/2\omega^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{c}
.

Therefore, the number of mean-field equations scales with the number of classes one considers
when grouping the molecular energies into bins. Still, the low complexity of
Eqs.

S47a
to
S47c

makes is possible to calculate dynamics for tens or even hundreds
of bins and hence achieve a fine resolution of molecular energy.

To illustrate the above, we consider
M=11
bins sampling a Gaussian distribution of molecular energies centered around
\omega_{0}=1.0
eV with standard deviation
\sigma_{m}
. Introducing
energetic disorder modifies the polariton group velocities in a non-trivial way. To mitigate this effect
and so isolate any disorder-induced renormalization of the transport properties,
we select a slightly lower pump and probe wavevector than that used
in the Letter,
k_{p}=-k_{p^{\prime}}=2\pi/5

\mu\text{m}^{-1}
. At this wavevector,
the theoretical lower polariton group velocity
\partial_{k}\omega^{\text{LP}}(k_{p};\omega_{m})
is more
symmetrical about changes in molecular energy
\omega_{0}
, thereby reducing artifacts arising from non-symmetric effects.

These results are consistent with low temperature measurements of exciton-polariton transport

to apply our framework to explore localization effects

]
arising from static
disorder—and their interplay with exciton hopping—as a route to diffusive transport in future work.
