Dietary, Lifestyle, and Psychological Factors Associated with Premenstrual Syndrome Among Female University Students in Saudi Arabia: A Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Mashael Huwaikem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPremenstrual syndrome (PMS) is common among women of reproductive age and has been associated with dietary, lifestyle, anthropometric, and psychological factors. However, evidence regarding the combined associations of these factors with PMS among young women in Saudi Arabia remains limited. This study examined dietary, lifestyle, anthropometric, psychological, and sleep-related factors associated with PMS among female university students.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 502 female students aged 18–29 years at King Faisal University, Saudi Arabia. Premenstrual symptoms were assessed using the Shortened Premenstrual Assessment Form (SPAF), dietary characteristics using a semi-quantitative Food Frequency Questionnaire (FFQ) and the Adolescent Food Habits Checklist (AFHC), and psychological distress using the Depression Anxiety Stress Scale-21 (DASS-21). Lifestyle, menstrual, anthropometric, and sleep-related characteristics were also assessed. Group differences were examined using non-parametric and categorical tests, and multivariable logistic regression was used to identify factors independently associated with PMS.ResultsParticipants with PMS had significantly higher stress, anxiety, depression, DASS-21 total, and sleep scores than those without PMS (all p < 0.001). In the multivariable analysis, stress score (AOR = 1.05, 95% CI: 1.02–1.10; p = 0.005) and sleep score (AOR = 1.03, 95% CI: 1.02–1.05; p < 0.001) remained significantly associated with PMS. Anxiety, depression, body mass index, and healthy dietary behaviour were not independently associated with PMS after adjustment.ConclusionStress and sleep-related characteristics showed the clearest independent associations with PMS. Dietary and anthropometric measures were not independently associated with PMS after multivariable adjustment. Given the cross-sectional design, these findings should be interpreted as associations rather than causal relationships. Prospective studies are needed to clarify temporal relationships among psychological, sleep-related, dietary, lifestyle, and premenstrual health factors.

Introduction

Premenstrual syndrome (PMS) is a common condition characterized by recurrent physical, emotional, and behavioral symptoms that occur during the luteal phase of the menstrual cycle and usually resolve shortly after the onset of menstruation.1 Although symptoms are generally mild, they may become sufficiently severe to interfere with daily activities, academic performance, and quality of life. Premenstrual dysphoric disorder (PMDD) represents a more severe clinical condition characterized particularly by prominent affective symptoms and substantial impairment in daily, social, academic, or occupational functioning. Although PMS and PMDD share a cyclical premenstrual pattern, they differ in symptom severity and diagnostic requirements and should not be used interchangeably.1–3 Approximately 20–30% of women of reproductive age experience moderate-to-severe PMS, while 3–8% meets diagnostic criteria for PMDD.1–3

The underlying mechanisms of PMS remain incompletely understood, and current management strategies primarily focus on symptom relief rather than the underlying pathophysiology.4 Pharmacological treatment may not be appropriate for all women because of potential adverse effects and contraindications.5 Consequently, increasing attention has been directed toward modifiable lifestyle factors, including dietary habits, nutritional status, physical activity, sleep, and psychological well-being, as potential approaches for reducing symptom severity and improving quality of life. PMS is characterized by a wide range of physical symptoms, including fatigue, abdominal bloating, breast tenderness, headache, and generalized body pain, together with psychological symptoms such as irritability, anxiety, depressed mood, impaired concentration, sleep disturbances, and social withdrawal.2–4

Dietary modification has been proposed as a practical non-pharmacological strategy for PMS management; however, evidence regarding the relationship between dietary intake and PMS remains inconsistent. Previous studies have primarily focused on individual food groups or nutrients, including dairy products, vegetables, seafood, cereals, calcium, and vitamin D, with inconsistent findings across different populations.6–12 Current clinical recommendations advocate diets rich in complex carbohydrates, calcium, fruits, vegetables, and whole grains while limiting foods high in saturated fat, sugar, salt, and caffeine.13 Nevertheless, observational studies have shown that women with PMS often consume greater amounts of processed foods, caffeinated beverages, refined carbohydrates, and sugar-sweetened foods while consuming fewer fruits and vegetables and exhibiting poorer overall dietary quality than women without PMS.14–16

Beyond dietary intake, increasing evidence suggests that psychological distress and lifestyle factors may also contribute to PMS. Elevated levels of stress, anxiety, depression, and poor sleep quality have been associated with increased symptom severity and reduced quality of life among women with PMS.17,18 These findings indicate that PMS is a multifactorial condition influenced by interactions among dietary behaviors, nutritional status, psychological well-being, and lifestyle factors rather than by individual dietary components alone.

In Saudi Arabia, PMS represents an important health concern among young women, particularly university students. Rasheed and Al-Sowielem reported a high premenstrual symptom severity score in 37.5% of college-aged women in Saudi Arabia, while Rafique and Al-Sheikh reported premenstrual symptoms in 46.7% of 738 female health-sciences students and identified a significant association with psychological stress.19,20 These Saudi data demonstrate a substantial local burden and support further investigation of potentially modifiable dietary, lifestyle, psychological, and sleep-related factors associated with premenstrual health.

Despite growing interest in modifiable factors associated with premenstrual health, much of the existing literature has examined dietary, psychological, sleep-related, or lifestyle factors separately. Evidence evaluating these factors concurrently within the same analytical framework remains comparatively limited, particularly among female university students in Saudi Arabia. In addition, relatively few studies have considered dietary characteristics together with psychological distress, sleep, anthropometric indicators, menstrual characteristics, and lifestyle factors while accounting for potential confounding variables. The present study therefore extends the available evidence by examining these domains simultaneously in a relatively large sample of Saudi female university students and by using multivariable analysis to identify factors independently associated with PMS.

Accordingly, this study aimed to examine dietary, lifestyle, anthropometric, psychological, and sleep-related factors associated with premenstrual syndrome among female university students at King Faisal University, Saudi Arabia.

Methods

Study Setting and Participants

A cross-sectional study was conducted at King Faisal University (KFU), Al Ahsa, Saudi Arabia, between February and May 2026 using a self-administered structured questionnaire. Female students aged 18–29 years who were enrolled at KFU during the study period, had experienced regular menstrual cycles for at least three consecutive months, and provided written informed consent were eligible to participate. Participants with chronic medical conditions, including sickle cell disease, diabetes mellitus, or diagnosed psychological disorders, and those outside the specified age range were excluded from the study.

Participation was voluntary, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants before data collection. Participant anonymity and confidentiality were maintained by collecting no personally identifiable information, such as names or addresses. No financial or non-financial incentives were provided for participation.

Participants were recruited using a non-probability convenience sampling technique during the predefined study period. A formal priori power analysis was not performed in the original study protocol. All eligible students who met the inclusion criteria and agreed to participate during the study period were therefore invited to participate. A total of 502 eligible participants completed the questionnaire and were included in the final analysis.

Data Collection

Data were collected using a modified, pretested, self-administered structured questionnaire that included demographic characteristics, menstrual health, dietary intake, anthropometric characteristics, lifestyle factors, psychological distress, and sleep-related information.

Before the main survey, a pilot study involving 50 female students at King Faisal University was conducted to assess the clarity, applicability, and comprehensibility of the study instrument. The pilot testing also helped determine whether the wording and structure of the questionnaire were understandable and suitable for the target university population. Data obtained from the pilot study were excluded from the final analysis.

Participants were recruited during student activities and health-related events organized or supported by the Deanship of Student Affairs. Female students from both health and non-health colleges were invited to participate. Eligibility was confirmed before questionnaire administration, and only students who met the inclusion criteria and provided written informed consent were enrolled. The questionnaires were completed during the scheduled activities under the supervision of the research team.

Study Instruments

Premenstrual Symptoms

Premenstrual symptoms were assessed using the Shortened Premenstrual Assessment Form (SPAF) developed by Allen et al18 The SPAF comprises 10 items assessing symptom severity, with each item rated from 1 (none or no change from normal) to 6 (maximum change), yielding a total score of 10–60; higher scores indicate greater premenstrual symptom severity. A SPAF score of ≥27 was used as the predefined threshold for classification in the present analysis. Because PMS and PMDD are related but clinically distinct conditions, this SPAF-based threshold was interpreted as an indicator of premenstrual symptom status rather than as a formal clinical diagnosis of PMDD. Previous validation studies reported high internal consistency for the SPAF, including Cronbach’s alpha of 0.91;21 this value refers to published validation data and was not calculated from the present study sample.

Dietary Assessment

Dietary intake was assessed using a semi-quantitative Food Frequency Questionnaire (FFQ) comprising commonly consumed foods from major dietary categories, including meat and poultry, fish, eggs, dairy products, fruits, vegetables, grains, snack foods, beverages, oils and nuts.22,23 Participants reported habitual consumption frequency for each listed item, and frequency responses were coded numerically for descriptive analysis. The questionnaire was used to characterize relative food-consumption frequency rather than to estimate absolute energy or nutrient intake. Item-level food-frequency data were retained for descriptive interpretation. The available study documentation did not specify a fixed recall period or a standardized portion-size estimation procedure; consequently, these aspects could not be reconstructed retrospectively and are acknowledged as limitations. Any aggregate food-frequency score was treated as exploratory and was not interpreted as a validated measure of overall dietary quality. The complete questionnaire was pilot tested among 50 female students at King Faisal University for clarity, comprehensibility, and applicability; this pilot assessment was not a formal psychometric validation of the FFQ in the Saudi population.

Healthy Dietary Behavior

Healthy dietary behavior was assessed using the Adolescent Food Habits Checklist (AFHC). Item responses were scored according to the checklist procedure used in the study and combined to obtain an overall AFHC score, with higher scores representing healthier food-choice behavior.24 Because the available study documentation did not contain a separate scoring manual specifying item-level coding and the full theoretical score range, the AFHC score is interpreted as the study-derived indicator of healthy dietary behavior rather than as a newly validated scale. The term “Adolescent Food Habits Checklist (AFHC)” is used consistently throughout the manuscript.

Psychological Distress

Psychological distress was assessed using the Depression Anxiety Stress Scale-21 (DASS-21), comprising 21 self-report items distributed across three seven-item subscales assessing depression, anxiety, and stress. Higher subscale scores indicate greater levels of the corresponding symptoms. Depression, anxiety, and stress scores were examined separately in the inferential analyses, whereas the DASS-21 total score was summarized descriptively and was not entered simultaneously with the three component subscales in the multivariable model.

Sleep Assessment

Sleep-related characteristics were assessed using self-reported items within the structured study questionnaire rather than a separate objective monitoring method. Responses to the sleep-related items were combined to derive the sleep score used in the analyses, with higher scores indicating greater sleep-related difficulty. The available study documentation did not identify a separate validated sleep instrument or provide an independent published scoring algorithm; the sleep score is therefore interpreted as a study-derived self-reported measure. This measure was evaluated in both bivariate and multivariable analyses, and its measurement limitations are acknowledged below.

Physical Activity

Physical activity was assessed by self-report through the study questionnaire. Participants reported whether they engaged in regular physical activity and were classified accordingly for descriptive and analytical purposes. Because physical activity was based on self-report rather than objective monitoring, it was treated as a self-reported lifestyle characteristic.

Menstrual Characteristics and Pain Assessment

Menstrual characteristics assessed through the questionnaire included age at menarche, average menstrual cycle length, menstrual duration, menstrual flow, and menstrual pain.

Menstrual pain intensity was assessed using a self-reported Numerical Rating Scale (NRS). Pain severity was categorized as mild (NRS 1–3), moderate (NRS 4–6), or severe (NRS 7–10), with higher scores indicating greater pain intensity.25

Age at menarche was categorized as <12 years, 12–14 years, and ≥15 years as prespecified descriptive groupings to distinguish earlier, typical, and later menarche within the study population.

Anthropometric Indicator

Body mass index (BMI) was used as an anthropometric indicator rather than as a comprehensive measure of nutritional status. BMI was calculated as body weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in meters (kg/m2), using height and weight values recorded in the study questionnaire. The available study documentation did not specify standardized equipment or a direct researcher-measurement protocol; therefore, these questionnaire-recorded anthropometric values are interpreted cautiously, and this limitation is acknowledged in the manuscript.

Data Analysis

Data was analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Before statistical analysis, the dataset was screened for completeness, consistency, and accuracy. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic, menstrual, dietary, anthropometric, lifestyle, sleep-related, and psychological characteristics. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR), depending on distribution, whereas categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages.

Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Because several variables were not normally distributed, non-parametric statistical methods were applied where appropriate. Differences between participants with and without PMS were evaluated using the Mann–Whitney U-test for continuous variables and the Chi-square test for categorical variables.

Variables considered clinically relevant or associated with PMS in preliminary analyses were evaluated in a multivariable logistic regression model. Because the DASS-21 total score is mathematically derived from the depression, anxiety, and stress subscales, the total score was not entered simultaneously with the individual subscales, thereby avoiding deterministic collinearity between the total score and its components. The three DASS-21 subscales were retained as separate candidate variables; because formal variance-inflation-factor or tolerance statistics were not available in the study documentation, their adjusted coefficients are interpreted cautiously in view of possible intercorrelation. Adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were used to quantify associations. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Given the cross-sectional design, regression estimates were interpreted as associations and not as evidence of temporal or causal relationships.

Ethical Considerations

The study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of King Faisal University, Al Ahsa, Saudi Arabia (Reference No. KFU-REC-2024-JAN-ETHICS1965). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrolment. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional research committee and the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Participant confidentiality and anonymity were maintained throughout the study.

After multivariable adjustment, stress and sleep scores remained significantly associated with PMS. Each one-unit increase in stress score was associated with a 5% increase in the odds of PMS (AOR = 1.05, 95% CI: 1.02–1.10; p = 0.005), whereas each one-unit increase in sleep score was associated with a 3% increase in the odds of PMS (AOR = 1.03, 95% CI: 1.02–1.05; p < 0.001).

The associations observed for anxiety and depression in the crude analyses were attenuated and were no longer statistically significant after adjustment (anxiety: AOR = 1.00, 95% CI: 0.97–1.04, p = 0.786; depression: AOR = 1.01, 95% CI: 0.98–1.04, p = 0.568). BMI, the AFHC score, and the exploratory aggregate food-frequency measure were also not significantly associated with PMS in the adjusted model. Overall, the multivariable findings indicate that stress and sleep scores were independently associated with PMS in this study population, whereas the dietary and anthropometric measures included in the model were not independently associated with PMS.

Discussion

Premenstrual syndrome (PMS) is a common health condition among women of reproductive age and can adversely affect daily functioning and quality of life. The present study examined dietary, anthropometric, psychological, sleep-related, menstrual, and lifestyle factors associated with PMS among female university students in Saudi Arabia. The study contributes context-specific evidence by evaluating these domains concurrently within a multivariable framework.

The dietary findings should be interpreted cautiously. Overall healthy dietary behavior, as assessed using the Adolescent Food Habits Checklist (AFHC), did not differ significantly between participants with and without PMS. Item-level FFQ results describe habitual consumption frequencies within the study population but do not establish independent associations between specific foods and PMS. The exploratory aggregate food-frequency measure was not treated as a validated measure of dietary quality and was not statistically significant in the adjusted model. Accordingly, the present findings do not support conclusions that particular foods or overall dietary patterns independently contribute to PMS. Previous studies have reported associations between processed foods, refined carbohydrates, caffeinated beverages, and PMS, but differences in dietary assessment, population characteristics, cultural eating practices, and confounder adjustment may account for variation across studies.12,15,17

Psychological and sleep-related factors showed clearer associations with PMS. Participants with PMS had significantly higher stress, anxiety, depression, DASS-21 total, and sleep scores in the bivariate analyses. In the multivariable model, stress and sleep remained significantly associated with PMS, whereas the associations for anxiety and depression were attenuated and were no longer statistically significant. These results indicate that stress and sleep-related characteristics had stronger independent associations with PMS than the dietary and anthropometric measures examined. Similar associations between psychological distress, sleep disturbance, and premenstrual symptoms have been reported previously.26–28 Because the study is cross-sectional, however, the temporal direction of these relationships cannot be established.

BMI and healthy dietary behavior were not independently associated with PMS after adjustment.29,30 BMI should therefore be interpreted as an anthropometric proxy rather than as a comprehensive measure of nutritional status. The absence of significant adjusted associations for BMI and AFHC supports a multifactorial interpretation of PMS and cautions against attributing premenstrual symptoms primarily to body size or individual dietary components.31,32

Although associations between psychological distress, sleep disturbances, and PMS are not entirely novel, the present study adds evidence from a relatively large sample of Saudi female university students by examining psychological, sleep-related, dietary, anthropometric, menstrual, and lifestyle factors within the same analytical framework. The principal contribution therefore lies in this integrated, context-specific assessment rather than in identifying entirely new individual risk factors.

From a practical perspective, stress and sleep may warrant particular attention in university-based menstrual-health education and support for female students in Saudi Arabia. General healthy-eating guidance remains appropriate as part of broader health promotion,33,34 but the present findings do not justify specific dietary prescriptions for PMS prevention or treatment. Future prospective cohort studies and well-designed intervention studies using validated dietary assessments, standardized sleep measures, and repeated assessment of premenstrual symptoms are needed to clarify temporal relationships and determine whether modifiable psychological, sleep-related, or lifestyle factors can reduce the burden of PMS.

Conclusions

This study examined dietary, anthropometric, psychological, sleep-related, and lifestyle factors associated with PMS among female university students in Saudi Arabia. In the multivariable analysis, stress and sleep scores remained significantly associated with PMS, whereas BMI and healthy dietary behavior were not independently associated after adjustment.

The dietary findings should be interpreted cautiously. Item-level food-frequency data were descriptive, and the exploratory aggregate food-frequency measure was not treated as a validated measure of dietary quality. The study therefore does not provide evidence that specific foods or dietary patterns were independently associated with PMS, and the findings should not be interpreted as demonstrating a direct or causal dietary effect.

Interpretation is limited by the cross-sectional design, convenience sampling from a single university, self-reported physical activity, menstrual pain, sleep-related characteristics and dietary intake, and potential recall and reporting bias associated with the FFQ. The available study documentation did not specify a fixed FFQ recall period, standardized portion-size estimation, a separately validated sleep-scoring algorithm, or standardized direct anthropometric measurement equipment. BMI was used only as an anthropometric proxy and does not constitute a comprehensive assessment of nutritional status.

Within these limitations, psychological well-being and sleep-related health may warrant particular attention in menstrual-health support for female university students.35,36 General dietary counselling may remain part of healthy-lifestyle promotion, but the present results do not support specific dietary recommendations for PMS prevention or treatment. Prospective multicenter studies using validated dietary and sleep measures, objective or standardized anthropometric assessment, and broader nutritional indicators are needed to clarify temporal relationships.

Strengths and Limitations

The present study has several strengths. It included a relatively large sample of female university students (n = 502) and evaluated demographic, menstrual, dietary, anthropometric, lifestyle, psychological, and sleep-related characteristics within a common analytical framework. A pilot study involving 50 participants was conducted before the main survey to improve questionnaire clarity and applicability. The use of the SPAF and multivariable logistic regression strengthened the structured assessment and allowed adjustment for several potential confounding variables.

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. Participants were recruited from a single university using non-probability convenience sampling, which limits generalizability. The cross-sectional design precludes temporal or causal inference. Dietary intake, physical activity, menstrual pain, sleep-related characteristics, height and weight information, and other questionnaire data were self-reported or questionnaire-recorded and may be affected by recall or reporting bias. The FFQ was not formally validated in the present Saudi population, and the available study documentation did not specify a fixed recall period or standardized portion-size estimation procedure; the aggregate food-frequency measure should therefore be regarded as exploratory. The available documentation also did not identify a separately validated sleep instrument or formal sleep-scoring algorithm, and formal collinearity diagnostics for the DASS-21 subscales were not available. BMI represents an anthropometric proxy rather than a comprehensive nutritional assessment. Residual confounding from unmeasured variables cannot be excluded. Future multicenter prospective studies using representative sampling and validated, objective or standardized dietary, sleep, and anthropometric assessments are recommended.
