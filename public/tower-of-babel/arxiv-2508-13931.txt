Random positive linear operators and their applications to nonparametric statistics Accepted for publication in Test
José A. Adell, J. T. Alcaláand C. Sangüesa

Abstract
We outline a general procedure on how to apply random positive linear operators in nonparametric estimation. As a consequence, we give explicit confidence bands and intervals for a distribution function F F concentrated on [ 0 , 1 ] [0,1] by means of random Bernstein polynomials, and for the derivatives of F F by using random Bernstein-Kantorovich type operators. In each case, the lengths of such bands and intervals depend upon the degree of smoothness of F F or its corresponding derivatives, measured in terms of appropriate moduli of smoothness. In particular, we estimate the uniform distribution function by means of a random polynomial of second order. This estimator is much simpler and performs better than the classical uniform empirical process used in the celebrated Dvoretzky-Kiefer-Wolfowitz inequality.

, Bouezmarni and Rolin

]
, Leblanc
[

,

]
, Dutta
[

]
, Ouimet
[

]
, and Wang and Lu
[
37
]
), conditional distribution functions and densities (cf. Janssen et al.
[
19
]
and Belalia et al.
[

]
), copula functions and densities (cf. Janssen et al.
[

,
18
]
), regression functions (cf. Tenbusch
[

]
and Slaoui
[

]
), regression quantile functions (Janssen et al.
[

]
and Khardani
[

The usual approach in nonparametric estimation by means of random Bernstein polynomials can be illustrated by considering the estimation of distribution functions (see, for instance, Leblanc
[
24
]
, Ouimet
[
27
]
, and Wang and Lu
[
37
]
). Suppose that
X
is a
[0,1]
-valued random variable having an unknown continuous distribution function
F
, and that
\mathbb{Y}_{n}=(Y_{n}(x),\ x\in[0,1])
is its corresponding empirical distribution function of size
n
. The stochastic process
\mathbb{Y}_{n}
, the natural estimator of
F
, has discontinuous paths. For this reason, we construct the random Bernstein polynomial of degree
m
, namely,
B_{m}(Y_{n};x)
acting on
Y_{n}
, having infinitely differentiable paths (see Section
2
for precise definitions). Under the assumption that
F
is twice continuously differentiable and that
0<F(x)<1,\ x\in(0,1)
, we have

n^{1/2}\left(B_{m}(Y_{n};x)-F(x)\right)\overset{({\cal L})}{\longrightarrow}N(0,F(x)(1-F(x))),\quad x\in(0,1),

whenever
m/\sqrt{n}\rightarrow\infty
, as
m,n\rightarrow\infty
, where
\overset{({\cal L})}{\longrightarrow}
denotes convergence in law. This allows us to provide asymptotic confidence intervals for
F(x),\ x\in(0,1)
. The optimal degree
m=m(n)
of the Bernstein polynomial is that minimizing the mean square error (MSE) or the mean integrated square error (MISE), respectively defined as

MSE=\mathbb{E}\left(B_{m}(Y_{n};x)-F(x)\right)^{2},\quad\hbox{and}\quad MISE=\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{1}\left(B_{m}(Y_{n};x)-F(x)\right)^{2}dx.

It turns out that, in both cases, the order of magnitude of
m
is
m\sim n^{2/3}
(a random degree is chosen in Dutta
[
15
]
).

In other words, for a given confidence level
1-\alpha
, with
\alpha\in(0,1)
and under the preceding assumptions on
F
, we asymptotically have for any
x\in(0,1)

P\left(\left|B_{m}(Y_{n};x)-F(x)\right|\geq z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sqrt{F(x)(1-F(x))}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\sim\alpha,\quad m\sim n^{2/3},\quad n\rightarrow\infty.

(1)

In this paper, we assume that
F
is simply continuous and that
0<F(x)<1
,
x\in(0,1)
. For any
x\in(0,1)
, we construct explicit confidence intervals of the form

P\left(\left|B_{m}(Y_{n};x)-F(x)\right|\geq r_{\alpha}\frac{\sqrt{F(x)(1-F(x))}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\leq\alpha,\quad n\geq n_{0}(\alpha,x),

(2)

as well as confidence bands

P\left(\left\|B_{m}(Y_{n};x)-F(x)\right\|>\frac{\widetilde{r}_{\alpha}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\leq\alpha,

(3)

where
r_{\alpha}
and
\widetilde{r}_{\alpha}
are positive constants only depending upon
\alpha
(see Corollary
58
and Corollary
45
, respectively). Then, we determine the minimum degree
m
of the Bernstein polynomial, i.e., the simplest Bernstein estimator
B_{m}(Y_{n};x)
satisfying (
2
) and (
3
). In each case, this degree
m
essentially depends upon the rate of convergence of
\left|B_{m}(F;x)-F(x)\right|
or
\left\|B_{m}(F;x)-F(x)\right\|
towards
0
. In turn, such rates depend on the degree of smoothness of
F
, measured in terms of appropriate moduli of smoothness (see Section
3
).

In the particular case of the uniform distribution, the minimum degree
m
of the Bernstein polynomial is
m=2
. This leads us to the following striking results (see Corollary
5.2
and Corollary
50
, respectively)

P\left(\left|B_{2}(Y_{n};x)-x\right|\geq 4x(1-x)\delta\right)\leq 2exp\left(-8n\delta^{2}\left(1-\frac{8}{3}\delta\right)\right),\ x\in(0,1),\ 0<\delta\leq 1/8,

as well as

P\left(\left\|B_{2}(Y_{n};x)-x\right\|>\delta\right)\leq 2e^{-8n\delta^{2}},\quad\delta>0.

In other words,
B_{2}(Y_{n};x)
is a random polynomial of degree
2
only involving a random variable having the binomial distribution with parameters
n
and
1/2
. This estimator is much simpler and performs in a more efficient way than the classical uniform empirical process used in the Dvoretzky-Kiefer-Wolfowitz (D-K-W) inequality (cf. Dvoretzky et al.
[
16
]
and Massart
[
25
]
).

Formulas (
2
) and (
3
) are also extended to the derivatives
F^{(k)}
of the distribution function
F
(see Sections
4
-
6
). In fact, we use random Bernstein polynomials to estimate
F
, and random Bernstein-Kantorovich type operators to estimate
F^{(k)},\ k\geq 1
. This kind of results has several applications in statistical data analysis, such as local minima/maxima, optimal bandwidth in kernel density estimation and so on (see, for instance, Sasaki et al.
[
32
]
or, in a multivariate setting, Chacón and Duong
[
13
, Chap. 5 and 6]
and the references therein).

To keep the paper in a moderate size, we only consider Bernstein estimators of
F
and its derivatives. However, in Section
2
, we outline a unified procedure on how to apply general random positive linear operators in nonparametric estimation. In this regard, classical kernel estimators can be viewed as estimators built up from random Weierstrass type operators.

In Section
7
, we illustrate some of the results in Sections
4
-
6
by considering simulations referring to a family of specific distribution functions with different degrees of smoothness. The proofs of the results in Sections
4
-
7
have been moved to Appendix
A

A.1
in the Appendix. Finally, a summary of the contributions of ths paper is provided in Section
8

2
Deterministic and random positive linear operators

Throughout this paper, the following notations and assumptions will be used. Let
\mathbb{N}
be the set of positive integers and
\mathbb{N}_{0}=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
. We assume that
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
, with
n,m\geq 2
. The usual supremum norm is denoted by
\|\cdotp\|
. Given a closed real interval
I
, we denote by
C^{k}(I)
the set of all
k
times continously differentiable functions defined on
I
,
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, and set
C(I)=C^{0}(I)
. Any stochastic process considered has right-continuous paths. Finally, for the sake of clarity, we consider two probability spaces
(\Omega,{\cal F},P)
and
(\widetilde{\Omega},\widetilde{\cal F},\widetilde{P})
, together with their product
(\Omega\times\widetilde{\Omega},{\cal F}\otimes\widetilde{\cal F},P\otimes\widetilde{P})
. Expectations in these three spaces are denoted by
\mathbb{E}
,
\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}
and
\mathbb{E}\times\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}
, respectively. Suppose that
Y
(resp.
\widetilde{Z}
) is a real-valued random variable defined on
\Omega
(resp.
\widetilde{\Omega}
). We can always assume that these two random variables are defined on
\Omega\times\widetilde{\Omega}
via the formulas

Y_{1}(\omega,\widetilde{\omega})=Y(\omega),\quad\widetilde{Z}_{1}(\omega,\widetilde{\omega})=\widetilde{Z}(\widetilde{\omega}),\quad(\omega,\widetilde{\omega})\in\Omega\times\widetilde{\Omega}.

(4)

It turns out that
Y_{1}
and
\widetilde{Z}_{1}
are independent. Actually, for any Borel sets
A
and
B
, we have
Y_{1}^{-1}(A)=Y^{-1}(A)\times\widetilde{\Omega}
and
\widetilde{Z}_{1}^{-1}(B)=\Omega\times\widetilde{Z}^{-1}(B)
, thus implying that

\displaystyle P\times\widetilde{P}\left(Y_{1}^{-1}(A)\cap\widetilde{Z}_{1}^{-1}(B)\right)=P\times\widetilde{P}\left(Y^{-1}(A)\times\widetilde{Z}^{-1}(B)\right)

\displaystyle=P\left(Y^{-1}(A)\right)\widetilde{P}\left(\widetilde{Z}^{-1}(B)\right)=P\times\widetilde{P}\left(Y_{1}^{-1}(A)\right)P\times\widetilde{P}\left(\widetilde{Z}_{1}^{-1}(B)\right).

(5)

Let
\widetilde{\mathbb{Z}}_{m}=(\widetilde{Z}_{m}(x),\ x\in I)
be a stochastic process such that
\widetilde{Z}_{m}(x)
takes values in
I
, for any
x\in I
, and satisfies

\widetilde{Z}_{m}(x)\xrightarrow{({\cal L})}x,\quad x\in I,\quad m\rightarrow\infty.

(6)

Associated to
\widetilde{\mathbb{Z}}_{m}
, we define the positive linear operator

L_{m}(f;x)=\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}f(\widetilde{Z}_{m}(x))=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(\theta)d\widetilde{F}_{m,x}(\theta),\quad x\in I,\quad f\in{\cal M}(I),

(7)

where
\widetilde{F}_{m,x}
is the distribution function of
\widetilde{Z}_{m}(x)
and
{\cal M}(I)
is the set of all real measurable functions defined on
I
for which the preceding expectations exist. We assume that

L_{m}({\cal M}(I))\subseteq C^{k}(I),\quad k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

(8)

The Continuous Mapping Theorem and assumption (
6
) guarantees that
L_{m}(f;x)\rightarrow f(x)
, as
m\rightarrow\infty
, for any bounded function
f\in C(I)
. By (
8
),
L_{m}(f;x)
is a smooth approximant of
f(x)
.

Before going further, we consider two well known examples of operators
L_{m}
satisfying (
6
)-(
8
) (see, for instance
[
4
]
, for further examples).

Example 1

(
Bernstein polynomials.

I=[0,1]
)
.

Let
x\in[0,1]
. Let
(\widetilde{U}_{j})_{j\geq 1}
be a sequence of independent copies of a random variable
\widetilde{U}
uniformly distributed on
[0,1]
and consider the uniform empirical process of size
m

\widetilde{Z}_{m}(x)=\frac{\widetilde{S}_{m}(x)}{m},\qquad\widetilde{S}_{m}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}1_{[0,x]}(\widetilde{U}_{j}),

(9)

where
1_{A}
stands for the indicator function of the set
A
. The
m
-th Bernstein polynomial of a function
f\in{\cal M}([0,1])
is defined by

B_{m}(f;x)=\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}f\left(\frac{\widetilde{S}_{m}(x)}{m}\right)=\sum_{k=0}^{m}f\left(\frac{k}{m}\right)p_{m,k}(x),\quad p_{m,k}(x)={m\choose k}x^{k}(1-x)^{m-k}.

(10)

Example 2

(
Weierstrass-type operators
.
I=\mathbb{R}
)
.

Let
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Let
\widetilde{W}
be an arbitrary real valued random variable with distribution function
\widetilde{F}
and let
\left(h_{m}\right)_{m\geq 1}
be a sequence of positive real numbers converging to
0
. The Weierstrass-type operators are defined by

W_{m}(f;x)=\mathbb{\widetilde{E}}f(x+h_{m}\widetilde{W})=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x+h_{m}\theta)d\widetilde{F}(\theta),\quad f\in{\cal M}(\mathbb{R}).

If
\widetilde{W}
has the standard normal distribution and
h_{m}=1/\sqrt{m}
,
W_{m}
is called the
m
-th Weierstrass operator. In such a case, denote by
\eta(\theta)
the standard normal density. Recall that the Hermite polynomials
(H_{k}(\theta))_{k\geq 0}
are defined via the Rodrigues formula

H_{k}(\theta)=(-1)^{k}\frac{\eta^{(k)}(\theta)}{\eta(\theta)},\quad\theta\in\mathbb{R},\quad k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
. The derivatives of the Weierstrass operator have the form

W_{m}^{(k)}(f;x)=\frac{1}{h_{m}^{k}}\widetilde{E}f\left(x+h_{m}\widetilde{W}\right)H_{k}(\widetilde{W}),\quad f\in{\cal M}(\mathbb{R}),

(11)

or

W_{m}^{(k)}(f;x)=\widetilde{E}f^{(k)}\left(x+h_{m}\widetilde{W}\right),\quad f\in C^{k}(\mathbb{R}).

Such formulas are analogous to the expressions given in (
30
) and (
31
) below for the Bernstein polynomials. As a consequence of (
11
), note that we have the Wiener expansion

W_{m}(f;x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k!}\mathbb{\widetilde{E}}f(h_{m}\widetilde{W})H_{k}(\widetilde{W})\left(\frac{x}{h_{m}}\right)^{k},\quad|x|<h_{m},

whenever
\mathbb{\widetilde{E}}f^{2}(h_{m}\widetilde{W})<\infty.

Roughly speaking, if in formula (
7
), we choose a random function
f
independent of
\widetilde{Z}_{m}
, we obtain a random positive linear operator. More precisely, let
\mathbb{Y}_{n}=\left(Y_{n}(x),\ x\in I\right)
be a stochastic process. As follows from (
4
) and (
5
), no generality is lost if we assume that the stochastic processes
\mathbb{Y}_{n}
and
\mathbb{\widetilde{Z}}_{m}
, as defined on
\Omega\times\widetilde{\Omega}
, are independent. We then consider the subordinated stochastic process
\left(Y_{n}(\mathbb{\widetilde{Z}}_{m}(x)),\ x\in I\right)
and define the random positive linear operator

L_{m}(Y_{n};x)=\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}Y_{n}(\widetilde{Z}_{m}(x))=\int_{\mathbb{R}}Y_{n}(\theta)d\widetilde{F}_{m,x}(\theta),\quad x\in I,

(12)

where
\widetilde{F}_{m,x}(\theta)
is the distribution function of
\widetilde{Z}_{m}(x)
. In other words, we have replaced in (
7
) the deterministic function
f(\theta)
by the random function
Y_{n}(\theta)
. Note that the paths of
L_{m}(Y_{n};x)
are in
C^{k}(I)
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
, as follows from (
8
).

One of the main motivations of definition (
12
) comes from the following estimation problem. Assume that the process
\mathbb{Y}_{n}
is integrable. and that

\mathbb{E}Y_{n}(x)=\mu(x)\quad x\in I,

(13)

as well as

Y_{n}(x)\xrightarrow{({\cal L})}\mu(x),\quad x\in I,\quad n\rightarrow\infty,

(14)

where
\mu\in C(I)
is unknown. Instead of using the process
\mathbb{Y}_{n}
as an estimator of
\mu
, we consider the following approximation

\displaystyle L_{m}(Y_{n};x)-\mu(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}Y_{n}(\theta)d\widetilde{F}_{m,x}(\theta)-\mu(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(Y_{n}(\theta)-\mu(\theta)\right)d\widetilde{F}_{m,x}(\theta)-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\mu(\theta)-\mu(x)\right)d\widetilde{F}_{m,x}(\theta)

\displaystyle=L_{m}(Y_{n}-\mu;x)+L_{m}(\mu;x)-\mu(x),\quad x\in I.

(15)

The term
L_{m}(\mu;x)-\mu(x)
is deterministic and can be estimated using approximation-theoretic techniques based on appropriate moduli of smoothness. By assumption (
8
), the random term
L_{m}(Y_{n}-\mu;x)
has paths in
C^{k}(I)
. This implies, under the assumption that
\mu\in C^{k}(I)
, for some
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, that we can differentiate in (
15
) to obtain the approximation

L_{m}^{(k)}(Y_{n};x)-\mu^{(k)}(x)=L_{m}^{(k)}(Y_{n}-\mu;x)+L_{m}^{(k)}(\mu;x)-\mu^{(k)}(x),\quad x\in I,

(16)

thus giving a procedure to estimate
\mu^{(k)}
by using the smooth estimator
L_{m}^{(k)}(Y_{n};x)
.

In this paper, attention is focused on the case in which
\mu=F\in C[0,1]
is the distribution function of a certain random variable
X
taking values in
[0,1]
, and
\mathbb{Y}_{n}
is its corresponding empirical process. Specifically, let
\left(X_{r}\right)_{r\geq 1}
be a sequence of independent copies of
X
and define

Y_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{r=1}^{n}1_{(-\infty,x]}(X_{r}),\quad x\in[0,1].

(17)

To rewrite this process, let
\left(U_{r}\right)_{r\geq 1}
be a sequence of independent copies of a random variable
U
uniformly distributed on
[0,1]
and denote

S_{n}(x)=\sum_{r=1}^{n}1_{[0,x]}(U_{r}),\quad x\in[0,1].

(18)

Let

F^{\star}(u)=\inf\{x\in\mathbb{R},\ F(x)\geq u\},\quad u\in(0,1),

be the quantile function of
X
. Since
F^{\star}(U)\overset{({\cal L})}{=}X
, where
\overset{({\cal L})}{=}
stands for equality in law, we can write

Y_{n}(x)\overset{({\cal L})}{=}\frac{S_{n}(F(x))}{n},\quad x\in[0,1].

(19)

By Example
10
and (
12
), the random Bernstein polynomials associated to
\mathbb{Y}_{n}
are

B_{m}(Y_{n};x)=\frac{1}{n}\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}S_{n}\left(F(\widetilde{S}_{m}(x)/m)\right)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{m}S_{n}\left(F(k/m)\right)p_{m,k}(x),\quad x\in[0,1].

(20)

If the random variable
X
takes values in
[0,\infty)
or
\mathbb{R}
, we should consider the random Szàsz-Mirakyan operator (as used in Ouimet
[
26
]
and Hanebeck and Klar
[
17
]
) or the random Weierstrass-type operator associated to
\mathbb{Y}_{n}
, respectively. For instance, in this last case, let
\mathbb{Y}_{n}
be as in (
17
), with
x\in\mathbb{R}
. As in Example
2
, let
\widetilde{F}(\theta)
be the distribution function of
\widetilde{W}
. By (
12
), the associated random Weierstrass-type operator applied to
-\widetilde{W}
takes on the form

\displaystyle W_{m}(Y_{n};x)=\widetilde{E}Y_{n}(x-h_{m}\widetilde{W})=\int_{\mathbb{R}}Y_{n}(x-h_{m}\theta)d\widetilde{F}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{r=1}^{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}}1_{(-\infty,x-h_{m}\theta]}(X_{r})d\widetilde{F}(\theta)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{r=1}^{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}}1_{(-\infty,\left(x-X_{r}\right)/h_{m}]}(\theta)d\widetilde{F}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{r=1}^{n}\widetilde{F}\left(\frac{x-X_{r}}{h_{m}}\right),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

(21)

This expression gives us the classical kernel estimator of
F
, the distribution function of
X
(cf. Tsybakov
[
35
]
).

Many positive linear operators, such as those considered in the examples above, preserve monotonicity, that is,
L_{m}(f,x)
is nondecreasing if
f(x)
is so. By (
7
), this is equivalent to the fact that the stochastic process
(\widetilde{Z}_{m}(x),\ x\in I)
has nondecreasing paths (cf. Shaked and Shanthikumar
[
33
, Chap. 1]
). If this is the case, and whenever
\mathbb{Y}_{n}
is an empirical process, and
\mu=F
is a distribution function, then formulas (
15
) and (
16
) approximate
F
and
F^{(1)}
by smooth random distribution functions and densities, respectively.

3
Bernstein-Kantorovich type operators

In this section, we consider some approximation properties of Bernstein and Bernstein-Kantorovich type operators, which will be used in estimating distribution functions and their derivatives, respectively. From now on, we assume that all of the random variables appearing under the same expectation sign are mutually independent. Also, we denote by
\sigma^{2}(x)=x(1-x),\ x\in[0,1]
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
h\geq 0
, and
0\leq x\leq x+kh\leq 1
. Recall that the
k
-th forward differences of a function
f
defined on
[0,1]
at step
h
are recursively defined as

\Delta_{h}^{0}f(x)=f(x),\ \Delta_{h}^{1}f(x)=f(x+h)-f(x),\ \Delta_{h}^{k}f(x)=\Delta_{h}^{k-1}\left(\Delta_{h}^{1}f\right)(x),\ k\geq 2,

(22)

or, equivalently, by

\Delta_{h}^{k}f(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{k}{k\choose j}(-1)^{k-j}f(x+jh).

(23)

Let
(\widetilde{V}_{j})_{j\geq 1}
be a sequence of independent copies of a random variable
\widetilde{V}
uniformly distributed on
[0,1]
. It turns out (cf.
[
5
]
) that

\Delta_{h}^{k}f(x)=h^{k}\widetilde{E}f^{(k)}\left(x+h(\widetilde{V}_{1}+\dots+\widetilde{V}_{k})\right),\quad f\in C^{k}[0,1],\quad x\in[0,1].

(24)

On the other hand, the usual first modulus of continuity of
f\in C[0,1]
is defined as

\omega(f;\delta)=\sup\left\{\left|f(x)-f(y)\right|:\ x,y\in[0,1],\ |x-y|\leq\delta\right\},\quad\delta\geq 0,

(25)

whereas the Ditzian-Totik second modulus of smoothness of
f
with weight function
\phi\geq 0
is defined as

\omega_{2}^{\phi}(f;\delta)=\sup\left\{|\Delta_{h\phi(x)}^{2}f(x)|,\ x\pm h\phi(x)\in[0,1],\ 0\leq h\leq\delta\right\},\quad\delta\geq 0.

(26)

If
\phi\equiv 1
,
\omega_{2}(f;\cdotp):=\omega_{2}^{1}(f;\cdotp)
is the usual second modulus of continuity of
f
. With regard to the Bernstein polynomials, we take from Păltănea
[
29
]
and
[
28
, pp. 94-96]
the following approximation result.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
f\in C[0,1]
. Then,

\left|B_{m}(f;x)-f(x)\right|\leq{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{3}{2}}\omega_{2}\left(f;\frac{\sigma(x)}{\sqrt{m}}\right),\quad x\in[0,1],

(27)

and

\left\|B_{m}(f;x)-f(x)\right\|\leq\frac{5}{2}\omega_{2}^{\sigma}\left(f;\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\right).

(28)

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
m>k
. Let
\widetilde{T}_{k}
be a random variable taking values in
[0,k]
. We define the positive linear operators

L_{m,k}(f;x)=\widetilde{E}f\left(\frac{\widetilde{S}_{m-k}(x)+\widetilde{T}_{k}}{m}\right),\quad f\in{\cal M}([0,1]),\quad x\in[0,1],

(29)

where the random variable
\widetilde{S}_{m-k}(x)
is defined in (
9
). In the particular case in which
\widetilde{T}_{k}=\widetilde{V}_{1}+\dots+\widetilde{V}_{k}
, (
\widetilde{T}_{0}=0
), where the sequence of random variables
(\widetilde{V}_{j})_{j\geq 1}
is defined as in (
24
), the operator
L_{m,k}
is called the Bernstein-Kantorovich operator (see, for instance
[
3
]
,
[
7
]
, and the references therein). In such a case, we denote
B_{m,k}:=L_{m,k}
. Note that
B_{m,0}=B_{m}
.

The Bernstein-Kantorovich operators describe the derivatives of Bernstein polynomials when acting on smooth functions. More precisely, we have (cf.
[
6
]
)

B_{m}^{(k)}(f;x)=(m)_{k}\widetilde{E}\Delta^{k}_{1/m}f\left(\frac{\widetilde{S}_{m-k}(x)}{m}\right),\ \quad f\in{\cal M}([0,1]),\quad x\in[0,1],

(30)

where
(m)_{k}=m(m-1)\dots(m-k+1),\ k\in\mathbb{N}
,
((m)_{0}=1)
is the falling factorial, as well as

B_{m}^{(k)}(f;x)=\frac{(m)_{k}}{m^{k}}B_{m,k}(f^{(k)};x),\ \quad f\in C^{k}[0,1],\quad x\in[0,1].

(31)

The approximation properties of the Bernstein-Kantorovich type operators defined in (
29
) are given in the following result.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
m>k
. For any
f\in C[0,1]
, we have

|L_{m,k}(f;x)-f(x)|\leq 2k\omega\left(f;\frac{1}{m}\right)+\frac{3}{2}\omega_{2}\left(f;\frac{\sigma(x)}{\sqrt{m}}\right),\quad x\in[0,1],

(32)

and

\|L_{m,k}(f;x)-f(x)\|\leq 2k\omega\left(f;\frac{1}{m}\right)+\frac{5}{2}\omega_{2}^{\sigma}\left(f;\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\right).

(33)

Proof.

Consider the function
f_{a}(y)=f(ay),\ y\in[0,1]
,
0<a\leq 1
. It turns out that

\omega_{2}(f_{a};\delta)\leq\omega_{2}(f;a\delta),\quad\omega_{2}^{\sigma}(f_{a};\delta)\leq\omega_{2}^{\sigma}(f;\delta\sqrt{a}),\quad\delta\geq 0.

(34)

Actually, the first inequality readily follows from definition (
26
), whereas the second one also follows from (
26
) and the fact that

\frac{a\sigma(x)}{\sigma(ax)}=\sqrt{\frac{a(1-x)}{1-ax}}\leq\sqrt{a}.

Let
a=1-\frac{k}{m}
. By (
29
), we can write

\displaystyle L_{m,k}(f;x)-f(x)=\widetilde{E}f\left(a\frac{\widetilde{S}_{m-k}(x)}{m-k}+\frac{\widetilde{T}_{k}}{m}\right)-\widetilde{E}f\left(a\frac{\widetilde{S}_{m-k}(x)}{m-k}\right)

\displaystyle+\widetilde{E}f\left(a\frac{\widetilde{S}_{m-k}(x)}{m-k}\right)-f\left(ax\right)+f\left(ax\right)-f(x).

By (
25
), this implies that

|L_{m,k}(f;x)-f(x)|\leq 2\omega\left(f;\frac{k}{m}\right)+|B_{m-k}(f_{a};x)-f_{a}(x)|.

Therefore, (
32
) follows from Theorem
28
, (
34
), and the subadditivity property of
\omega\left(f;\cdotp\right)
. Inequality (
33
) is shown in a similar way.
∎

The rate of uniform convergence in (
33
) for
\|L_{m,k}(f;x)-f(x)\|
cannot be better than
m^{-1}
(see
[
31
]
,
[
6
]
, and the references therein). Such a rate is attained when
f\in C^{2}[0,1]
, since

\omega_{2}^{\sigma}\left(f;\delta\right)\sim\|\sigma^{2}f^{(2)}\|\delta^{2},\quad\delta\rightarrow 0.

Similarly, the order of magnitude of the upper bound in (
32
) is
m^{-1}
at most. However, this order of magnitude can be improved if
f\in C^{k}[0,1],\ k>2
, and some further assumptions on the derivatives of
f
are made, as follows by considering the Taylor’s expansion of
f
(cf.
[
7
]
). Even more, if
f
is locally constant, the rates of convergence become exponential. Indeed, for a fixed
x\in(0,1)
, consider the nonnegative convex function

r(x,\theta)=\theta\log\frac{\theta}{x}+(1-\theta)\log\frac{1-\theta}{1-x},\quad 0\leq\theta\leq 1.

We take from
[
7
]
the following result.

Theorem 3.3
.

In the setting of Theorem
3.2
, assume further that
f(t)=c
,
t\in(a,b)
,
0<a<b<1
, for some constant
c
. For any
x\in\left(ma/(m-k),{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}(mb-k)/(m-k)}\right)
, we have

\displaystyle|L_{m,k}(f;x)-f(x)|\leq

\displaystyle\|f-c\|\left(\exp\left(-(m-k)r\kern-2.0pt\left(x,\frac{ma}{m-k}\right)\right)+\exp\left(-(m-k)r\kern-2.0pt\left(x,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{mb-k}{m-k}}\right)\right)\right).

4
Confidence bands

Unless otherwise specified, we assume from now on that
x\in(0,1)
and that
F\in C[0,1]
is the distribution function of a certain random variable
X
. We start from identity (
15
) for the Bernstein polynomials when
\mu=F
, that is,

B_{m}(Y_{n};x)-F(x)=B_{m}(Y_{n}-F;x)+B_{m}(F;x)-F(x),

(35)

where
\mathbb{Y}_{n}
is the empirical process defined in (
17
). Differentiating this formula, we obtain from (
31
)

B_{m}^{(k)}(Y_{n};x)-F^{(k)}(x)=B_{m}^{(k)}(Y_{n}-F;x)+\frac{(m)_{k}}{m^{k}}B_{m,k}(F^{(k)};x)-F^{(k)}(x),

(36)

whenever
F\in C^{k}[0,1]
,
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, and
m>k
.

To give confidence intervals or bands, we need to estimate the tail probabilities of
B_{m}^{(k)}(Y_{n}-F;x)
in both (
35
) and (
36
). In the case
k=0
, we see from (
20
) that this random part is given by

B_{m}(Y_{n}-F:x)=\frac{1}{n}\widetilde{E}\left(S_{n}(F(\widetilde{S}_{m}(x)/m))-nF(\widetilde{S}_{m}(x)/m)\right).

(37)

Suppose that
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
m>k
. To deal with the derivatives of
F
, we see from (
17
) that

\Delta^{1}_{1/m}Y_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{r=1}^{n}1_{(x,x+1/m]}(X_{r}),

thus implying, by virtue of (
22
) and (
23
), that

\Delta^{k}_{1/m}Y_{n}(x)=\Delta^{k-1}_{1/m}\left(\Delta^{1}_{1/m}Y_{n}\right)(x)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}{k-1\choose j}(-1)^{k-1-j}\sum_{r=1}^{n}1_{\left(x+\frac{j}{m},x+\frac{j+1}{m}\right]}(X_{r}).

We thus have from (
30
) that the estimator of
F^{(k)}(x)
is given by

\displaystyle B_{m}^{(k)}(Y_{n};x)=(m)_{k}\widetilde{E}\Delta^{k}_{1/m}Y_{n}\left(\frac{\widetilde{S}_{m-k}(x)}{m}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{(m)_{k}}{n}\sum_{l=0}^{m-k}p_{m-k,l}(x)\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}{k-1\choose j}(-1)^{k-1-j}\sum_{r=1}^{n}1_{\left(\frac{l+j}{m},\frac{l+j+1}{m}\right]}(X_{r}).

(38)

We point out that, for
k=1
, this estimator was considered by many authors (see, for instance, Vitale
[
36
]
, Babu et al.
[
8
]
, Bouezmarni and Rollin
[
11
]
, Ouimet
[
27
]
, and Wang and Lu
[
37
]
, among others). On the other hand, using representation (
19
), the random term on the right-hand side in (
36
) can be written as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(m)_{k}}B_{m}^{(k)}(Y_{n}-F;x)=\widetilde{E}\Delta^{k}_{1/m}\left(Y_{n}-F\right)\left(\frac{\widetilde{S}_{m-k}(x)}{m}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}{k-1\choose j}(-1)^{k-1-j}\widetilde{E}\left(S_{n}\left(F\left(\frac{\widetilde{S}_{m-k}(x)+j+1}{m}\right)\right)-S_{n}\left(F\left(\frac{\widetilde{S}_{m-k}(x)+j}{m}\right)\right)\right.

\displaystyle\phantom{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}{k-1\choose j}{k-1\choose j}(-1)^{k-1-j}}\left.-n\left(F\left(\frac{\widetilde{S}_{m-k}(x)+j+1}{m}\right)-F\left(\frac{\widetilde{S}_{m-k}(x)+j}{m}\right)\right)\right).

(39)

To give confidence bands for
F^{(k)}
, we use the celebrated D-K-W inequality (cf.
[
16
]
) in the final form shown by Massart
[
25
]
, which states that

P\left(\left\|\frac{S_{n}(x)}{n}-x\right\|>\delta\right)\leq 2e^{-2n\delta^{2}},\quad\delta>0.

(40)

Our first main result is the following.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Assume that
F\in C^{k}[0,1]
. Then,

P\left(\left\|B_{m}^{(k)}(Y_{n};x)-F^{(k)}(x)\right\|>\frac{l_{k,\alpha}(m)}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\leq\alpha,

(41)

where
m>\max(k,1)
is the first positive integer such that

\left\|\frac{(m)_{k}}{m^{k}}B_{m,k}(F^{(k)};x)-F^{(k)}(x)\right\|\leq\frac{1}{2}l_{k,\alpha}(m)\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}},

(42)

and

l_{k,\alpha}(m)=2^{k}(m)_{k}\sqrt{2\log(2/\alpha)}.

(43)

We see from (
41
) and (
43
) that the length of the confidence band increases as
k
increases. As a counterpart, the degree
m
of the Bernstein estimator decreases as
k
increases. This will be clear after the comments following Corollaries
45
and
47
below.

Setting
k=0,1
in Theorem
43
, we obtain confidence bands for
F
and
F^{(1)}
, respectively.

Corollary 4.2
.

If
F\in C[0,1]
, then

P\left(\left\|B_{m}(Y_{n};x)-F(x)\right\|>\frac{\sqrt{2\log(2/\alpha)}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\leq\alpha,

(44)

where
m>1
is the fist positive integer such that

\left\|B_{m}(F;x)-F(x)\right\|\leq\frac{\sqrt{2\log(2/\alpha)}}{2\sqrt{n}}.

(45)

Suppose that the Ditzian-Totik modulus of
F
fulfills the Lipschitz condition

\omega_{2}^{\sigma}(F;h)\leq Ch^{\beta},\quad h\geq 0,\quad 0<\beta\leq 2,

for some constant
C>0
. By (
28
), condition (
45
) is satisfied if

\frac{5C}{2}\frac{1}{m^{\beta/2}}\leq\frac{\sqrt{2\log(2/\alpha)}}{2\sqrt{n}}.

In other words, the degree
m
of the Bernstein polynomial has the order of magnitude
m\sim n^{1/\beta}
, thus heavily depending on the degree of smoothness of
F
.

Corollary 4.3
.

If
F\in C^{1}[0,1]
, then

P\left(\left\|B_{m}^{(1)}(Y_{n};x)-F^{(1)}(x)\right\|>\frac{2m\sqrt{2\log(2/\alpha)}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\leq\alpha,

(46)

where
m>1
is the fist positive integer such that

\left\|B_{m,1}(F^{(1)};x)-F^{(1)}(x)\right\|\leq\frac{m\sqrt{2\log(2/\alpha)}}{\sqrt{n}}.

(47)

In view of (
32
), suppose that

\left\|B_{m,1}(F^{(1)};x)-F^{(1)}(x)\right\|\leq\frac{C}{m^{\beta/2}},\quad 0<\beta\leq 2,

for some constant
C>0
. Then, condition (
47
) is satisfied if

\frac{C}{m^{\beta/2}}\leq\frac{m\sqrt{2\log(2/\alpha)}}{\sqrt{n}}.

This implies that the degree
m
of the Bernstein polynomial has the order of magnitude
m\sim n^{1/(\beta+2)}
. However, the length
l
of the confidence band has the order of magnitude
l\sim n^{-\beta/(2\beta+4)}
, as follows from (
46
). As in Corollary
45
, this length
l
strongly depends on the degree of smoothness of
F^{(1)}
.

Finally, assume that
F(x)=x,\ x\in[0,1]
. Then, we have from (
10
) and (
31
)

\left\|\frac{(m)_{k}}{m^{k}}B_{m,k}(F^{(k)};x)-F^{(k)}(x)\right\|=0,\quad k=0,1,\quad m\geq 2.

(48)

In other words, condition (
42
) in Theorem
43
holds for
m\geq 2
. This leads us to state the following simple result for the uniform distribution.

Corollary 4.4
.

If
F(x)=x,\ 0\leq x\leq 1
, then

P\left(\left\|B_{2}(Y_{n};x)-x\right\|>\delta\right)\leq 2e^{-8n\delta^{2}},\quad\delta>0.

(49)

and

P\left(\left\|B_{2}^{(1)}(Y_{n};x)-1\right\|>\delta\right)\leq 2e^{-n\delta^{2}/8},\quad\delta>0.

(50)

By (
19
) and (
20
), the estimator

B_{2}(Y_{n};x)=2\sigma^{2}(x)Y_{n}(1/2)+x^{2}=2\sigma^{2}(x)\frac{S_{n}(1/2)}{n}+x^{2}

is a random polynomial of degree
2
which only involves the random variable
S_{n}(1/2)
having the binomial distribution with parameters
n
and
1/2
. To estimate the uniform distribution, this estimator is much simpler and much more efficient than the uniform empirical process used in the D-K-W inequality (compare inequalities (
40
) and (
49
)). In addition, the
MSE and MISE of the estimator
B_{2}(Y_{n};x)
are easily computed, since

MSE=4\sigma^{4}(x)Var(Y_{n}(1/2))=\frac{\sigma^{4}(x)}{n},\quad MISE=\frac{1}{30n}.

5
Confidence intervals for
F

In this section, we give confidence intervals for
F(x)
, for any
x\in(0,1)
. For the sake of simplicity, we assume from now on that
0<F(x)<1,\ x\in(0,1)
.

To this end, we consider the nonnegative, increasing, and convex function

\tau(\epsilon)=(1+\epsilon)\log(1+\epsilon)-\epsilon,\ \epsilon\geq 0;\quad\tau(\epsilon)\geq\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2}-\frac{\epsilon^{3}}{6}\geq\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{3},\quad 0\leq\epsilon\leq 1.

(51)

We state the basic result of this section.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
x\in(0,1)
and
\epsilon>0
. Then,

\displaystyle P\left(\left|B_{m}(Y_{n};x)-F(x)\right|\geq\epsilon B_{m}(\sigma^{2}(F);x)+\left|B_{m}(F;x)-F(x)\right|\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2exp\left(-\frac{n}{q_{m}(x)}B_{m}\left(\sigma^{2}(F);x\right)\tau(\epsilon)\right),

(52)

where

q_{m}(x)=1-x^{m}-(1-x)^{m}.

(53)

As a first application, we consider the case of the uniform distribution, which has interest in itself.

Corollary 5.2
.

Let
F(x)=x,\ 0\leq x\leq 1
, and
\delta>0
. Then,

P\left(\left|B_{2}(Y_{n};x)-x\right|\geq 4\sigma^{2}(x)\delta\right)\leq 2e^{-n\tau(8\delta)/4}\leq 2exp\left(-8n\delta^{2}\left(1-\frac{8}{3}\delta\right)\right),

(54)

where the last inequality holds whenever
\delta\leq 1/8
.

Observe that the upper bound in (
54
) is uniform in
x\in(0,1)
. This means that the same confidence level works for any
x\in(0,1)
. On the other hand, the length of the confidence interval in (
54
) is shorter than that of the confidence band in Corollary
50
, except for
x=1/2
, case in which both lengths are the same. In fact, near the endpoints of
[0,1]
, the length of the confidence interval is very short.

Let

l_{\alpha}=\sqrt{3\log(2e^{1/3}/\alpha)}.

(55)

For a general distribution function
F
, we give the following result.

Corollary 5.3
.

Let
x\in(0,1)
. Assume that
n\geq l_{\alpha}^{2}/\sigma^{2}(F(x))
. Then,

P\left(\left|B_{m}(Y_{n};x)-F(x)\right|\geq\left(2l_{\alpha}+\frac{1}{l_{\alpha}}\right)\frac{\sigma(F(x))}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\leq\alpha,

(56)

where
m>1
is the first positive integer such that

\left|B_{m}(\sigma^{2}(F);x)-\sigma^{2}(F(x))\right|\leq\frac{\sigma^{2}(F(x))}{l_{\alpha}^{2}},

(57)

and

\left|B_{m}(F;x)-F(x)\right|\leq\frac{\sigma\left(F(x)\right)l_{\alpha}}{\sqrt{n}}.

(58)

Comparing this result with Corollary
45
, we see that the length of the confidence intervals and bands for
F
have the same order of magnitude
1/\sqrt{n}
. On the other hand, assume that
F
and
\sigma^{2}(F)
fulfil the Lipschitz condition

\max\left(\omega_{2}(F;h),\omega_{2}(\sigma^{2}\left(F\right);h)\right)\leq Ch^{\beta},\quad 0<\beta\leq 2,

(59)

for some constant
C>0
. By (
27
), conditions (
57
) and (
58
) are satisfied if

\frac{3}{2}C\frac{\sigma^{\beta}(x)}{m^{\beta/2}}\leq\frac{\sigma^{2}(F(x))}{l_{\alpha}^{2}}\quad\hbox{and}\quad\frac{3}{2}C\frac{\sigma^{\beta}(x)}{m^{\beta/2}}\leq\frac{\sigma\left(F(x)\right)l_{\alpha}}{\sqrt{n}},

(60)

respectively. This means that the order of magnitude of the degree
m
of the Bernstein polynomial is
m\sim n^{1/\beta}
, again heavily depending on the degree of smoothness of
F
. In this respect, suppose that
F(x)=c
,
x\in(a,b)
,
0<a<b<1
. For
x\in(a,b)
, Theorem (
3.3
) implies that condition (
58
) is satisfied if

\|F-c\|\left(e^{-mr(x,a)}+e^{-mr(x,b)}\right)\leq\frac{\sigma\left(F(c)\right)l_{\alpha}}{\sqrt{n}},

and therefore, the order of magnitude of
m
is
m\sim\log n
. Similar considerations can be made with regard to condition (
57
).

No proper comparisons between expressions (
56
) and (
1
) can be made, because the confidence interval in (
56
) is explicit, whereas that in (
1
) is asymptotic. This notwithstanding, the length of the confidence interval in (
56
) is a little worse than that in (
1
). For instance, if
\alpha=0.05
, then

2l_{\alpha}+\frac{1}{l_{\alpha}}=7.2353,\quad z_{\alpha/2}=1.96,

as follows from (
55
). However, in both cases, the lengths of the confidence intervals have the same order of magnitude. On the other hand, formula (
56
) holds for any
F\in C[0,1]
, whereas (
1
) only holds for
F\in C^{2}[0,1]
. In this last case, the size
m
of the Bernstein polynomial has order of magnitude
n^{2/3}
in (
1
), and
n^{1/2}
in (
56
), as follows from (
59
) and (
60
).

6
Confidence intervals for
F^{(k)}

In this section, we fix
k\in\mathbb{N}
and assume that
m>k
. We suppose that
F\in C^{k}[0,1]
and denote by
\rho(x)=F^{(1)}(x)
. We also assume that
\rho(x)>0,\ x\in(0,1)
.

Denote by
\lfloor y\rfloor
the integer part of
y\in\mathbb{R}
. Let

r_{k}(m)=\frac{md_{k}}{(m)_{k}{2(k-1)\choose k-1}},

(61)

where

d_{k}={k-1\choose\lfloor k/2\rfloor}.

(62)

On the other hand, consider the following particular case of the positive linear operator defined in (
29
)

L_{m,k}(\rho;x)=\widetilde{E}\rho\left(\frac{\widetilde{S}_{m-k}(x)+\widetilde{R}_{k-1}+\widetilde{V}}{m}\right),

(63)

where
\widetilde{V}
is uniformly distributed on
[0,1]
and

\widetilde{P}\left(\widetilde{R}_{k-1}=j\right)=\frac{\displaystyle{k-1\choose j}^{2}}{\displaystyle{2(k-1)\choose k-1}},\quad j=0,1,\dots,k-1.

(64)

The following result is analogous to Theorem
53
.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
x\in(0,1)
and
\delta>0
. Then,

\displaystyle P\left(\left|B_{m}^{(k)}(Y_{n};x)-F^{(k)}(x)\right|\geq\delta L_{m,k}(\rho,x)+\left|\frac{(m)_{k}}{m^{k}}B_{m,k}(F^{(k)};x)-F^{(k)}(x)\right|\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2exp\left(-\frac{n}{(m)_{k}d_{k}r_{k}(m)}L_{m,k}(\rho;x)\tau(r_{k}(m)\delta)\right).

(65)

The following result is the main result of this section.

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
x\in(0,1)
. Set

s_{k}(m)=\frac{(m)_{k}}{\sqrt{m}}\sqrt{{2(k-1)\choose k-1}}.

(66)

Assume that

\frac{m}{n}\leq\frac{\rho(x)}{l_{\alpha}^{2}d_{k}^{2}}{2(k-1)\choose k-1},

(67)

where
l_{\alpha}
and
d_{k}
are defined in (
55
) and (
62
), respectively. Then,

P\left(\left|B_{m}^{(k)}(Y_{n};x)-F^{(k)}(x)\right|\geq\left(2l_{\alpha}+\frac{1}{l_{\alpha}}\right)\sqrt{\frac{\rho(x)}{n}}s_{k}(m)\right)\leq\alpha,

(68)

where
m>1
is the first postive integer such that

|L_{m,k}(\rho;x)-\rho(x)|\leq\frac{\rho(x)}{l_{\alpha}^{2}},

(69)

and

\left|\frac{(m)_{k}}{m^{k}}B_{m,k}(F^{(k)};x)-F^{(k)}(x)\right|\leq l_{\alpha}\sqrt{\frac{\rho(x)}{n}}s_{k}(m).

(70)

The comparison of this result with Theorem
43
reveals the following. For
k\geq 1
, the length of the confidence interval for
F^{(k)}
is that of the confidence band for
F^{(k)}
reduced by a factor
1/\sqrt{m}
, provided that the constants are dismissed. This follows from (
43
) and (
66
).

If
k=1
, then
d_{1}=1
and
s_{1}(m)=\sqrt{m}
, as follows from (
62
) and (
66
), respectively. On the other hand, we see from (

) and the comments following (

) that
L_{m,1}=B_{m,1}
. Therefore, the following result on density estimation is an immediate consequence of Theorem
70
.

Corollary 6.3
.

Let
x\in(0,1)
. Assume that

\frac{m}{n}\leq\frac{\rho(x)}{l_{\alpha}^{2}}.

Then,

P\left(\left|B_{m}^{(1)}(Y_{n};x)-\rho(x)\right|\geq\left(2l_{\alpha}+\frac{1}{l_{\alpha}}\right)\sqrt{\frac{m\rho(x)}{n}}\right)\leq\alpha,

(71)

where
m>1
is the first positive integer such that

\left|B_{m,1}(\rho;x)-\rho(x)\right|\leq\min\left(\frac{\rho(x)}{l_{\alpha}^{2}},l_{\alpha}\sqrt{\frac{m\rho(x)}{n}}\right).

(72)

In view of (
32
), suppose that

\left|B_{m,1}(\rho;x)-\rho(x)\right|\leq\frac{C(x)}{m^{\beta/2}},\quad 0<\beta\leq 2,

for some positive constant
C(x)
. Then, condition (
72
) is satisfied if

\frac{C(x)}{m^{\beta/2}}\leq\min\left(\frac{\rho(x)}{l_{\alpha}^{2}},l_{\alpha}\sqrt{\frac{m\rho(x)}{n}}\right).

This implies that the degree
m
of the Bernstein polynomial has the order of magnitude
m\sim n^{1/(\beta+1)}
. As a consequence, the length
l
of the confidence interval in (
71
) has the order of magnitude
l\sim n^{-\beta/(2\beta+2)}
.

Recall that, under analogous smoothness assumptions on
\rho(x)
, the length
l
of the confidence band for
\rho(x)
has the order of magnitude
l\sim n^{-\beta/(2\beta+4)}
, as seen in the comments following Corollary
47
.

Finally, suppose that
\rho(x)=c>0,\ x\in(a,b)
, with
0<a<b<1
. Then, it follows from Theorem
3.3
that

\left|B_{m,1}(\rho;x)-\rho(x)\right|\leq\|\rho-c\|\left(\exp\left(-(m-1)r\kern-2.0pt\left(x,\frac{ma}{m-1}\right)\right)+\exp\left(-(m-1)r\kern-2.0pt\left(x,\frac{mb-1}{m-1}\right)\right)\right),

whenever
x\in\left(ma/(m-1),(mb-1)/(m-1)\right)
. Therefore, for any
x
in the previous interval, we can take
m
such that
m+\log m\sim\log n

8
Conclusions

The main contributions of this paper can be summarized as follows.

(a)

We give explicit confidence bands and intervals for a distribution function
F
concentrated on
[0,1]
by means of random Bernstein polynomials, and for the derivatives
F^{(k)},\ k\geq 1
, by using random Bernstein-Kantorovich type operators. The only smoothness assumption on
F
or
F^{(k)},\ k\geq 1
, is continuity.

(b)

In each case, we choose the simplest estimator, that is, the minimum degreee
m
of the Bernstein estimator fulfilling the requirements to construct the confidence band or interval, for a given confidence level
1-\alpha
.

(c)

The lengths of the confidence bands and intervals for
F^{(k)},\ k\geq 0
, essentially depend on the degree of smoothness of
F^{(k)}
. In any case, such lengths increase as
k
increases. As a counterpart, the degree
m
of the Bernstein estimator decreases as
k
increases.

(d)

As expected, the order of magnitude of the length of the confidence band for
F^{(k)}
is bigger than that of the confidence interval for
F^{(k)}
, excepting for
k=0
, case in which both orders are
1/\sqrt{n}
.

(e)

Suppose that
F
is constant in a certain subinterval
(a,b)
of
[0,1]
. To construct the confidence interval for
F(x)
,
x\in(a,b)
, the degree
m
of the Bernstein estimator can be chosen as
m\sim\log n
. A similar property holds true for
F^{(1)}
. In such a case, the length of the corresponding confidence interval has the order of
(\log n/n)^{1/2}
.

(f)

In the particular case of the uniform distribution, we consider a second-order Bernstein polynomial. This estimator is much simpler and outperforms the classical uniform empirical process used in the D-K-W inequality.
