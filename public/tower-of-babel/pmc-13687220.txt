Clinical features and global epidemiological analysis of sparganosis: Based on 5 pediatric cases and a systematic review of 135 literature reports
Authors: Jia Zhang, Yingyi Zhang, Helin Yan, Hongye Wang, Yangmengqian Zhou, Bo Zhao, Mifeng Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective Sparganosis is a rare parasitic disease caused by the larvae of Spirometra mansoni, with pediatric cases being particularly uncommon and prone to misdiagnosis. This study aims to analyze the clinical features, diagnostic strategies, and treatment responses in pediatric sparganosis, and to investigate its global epidemiological trends and diagnostic and therapeutic advancements through a systematic review. Methods We conducted a retrospective analysis of 5 confirmed pediatric sparganosis cases admitted to our center between 2014 and 2023. A systematic search of the PubMed database (1980–2025) for relevant literature was performed, leading to the screening and data extraction of 135 global case reports. Descriptive statistics, correlation analysis, and trend visualization were conducted using R software (version 4.5.1). Results Among the 5 children in our center, the median age was 7 years, and all were from high-parasitic-endemicity areas in Yunnan Province, China. All cases (100%) presented with marked eosinophilia (median value 4.42 × 10⁹/L). Neurological involvement was predominant (60%, 3/5), manifesting as epilepsy, myelitis, etc. All patients received praziquantel treatment, with four also receiving concomitant corticosteroids, and all showed symptom improvement. Analysis of global cases revealed a geographic clustering in East Asia (primarily China, South Korea, Japan, and Thailand), with the central nervous system and subcutaneous tissue being the two major infection sites. Diagnosis relied mainly on MRI (“tunnel sign”), serological testing, and metagenomic Next-Generation Sequencing (mNGS). The highest improvement rate (92.2%) was observed with combination therapy (surgery plus medication), which yielded comparably high improvement rates to single-modality treatments. Conclusion Pediatric sparganosis predominantly involves the nervous system. Diagnosis requires integrating epidemiological history, imaging, and molecular testing. Treatment should be individualized, with a recommended regimen of surgery combined with long-term, weight-based praziquantel therapy, supplemented by corticosteroids when necessary. Strengthening public health education remains the fundamental measure for preventing this disease.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00436-026-08746-7.

Introduction

Sparganosis is a zoonotic parasitic disease caused by the larvae of Spirometra species, predominantly Spirometra mansoni. Humans become infected primarily through accidental ingestion of infected copepods (first intermediate host) harboring infective larvae, or by consuming raw or undercooked meat from secondary intermediate hosts such as frogs and snakes (Liu et al. 2015, Wiwanitkit 2005). Although cases are sporadically reported worldwide, over 80% are concentrated in East and Southeast Asia (Li et al. 2010). Our systematic review further confirms this East Asian predominance, with China, South Korea, Japan, and Thailand constituting the major endemic foci, while sporadic cases have emerged across North America, Europe, and Oceania over recent decades, suggesting a trend towards wider geographical dissemination. The sparganum larvae exhibit tissue-migratory capability, enabling them to invade subcutaneous tissues, muscle, the central nervous system (CNS), eyes, and visceral organs. Among these, CNS involvement is the most severe manifestation, frequently resulting in epilepsy, neurological dysfunction, and even death (Iampreechakul et al. 2025, Nobayashi et al. 2006). Children are a high-risk population due to behavioral patterns (e.g., frequent outdoor activities, consumption of untreated water) and their relatively immature immune systems. However, systematic clinical summaries of pediatric cases remain limited, and their nonspecific presentations often lead to delayed diagnosis. By analyzing five pediatric cases from our center and integrating a systematic review of 135 global cases reported from 1980 to 2025, this study aims to comprehensively delineate the clinical features, diagnostic methods, and therapeutic strategies for pediatric sparganosis, thereby providing evidence-based insights for clinical practice.

Materials and methods

Case review

A retrospective collection was conducted for 5 confirmed pediatric cases (≤ 14 years old) of sparganosis at our center from January 2014 to December 2023. Data collected included demographic characteristics, epidemiological history, clinical manifestations, laboratory findings, imaging results, treatment regimens, and prognosis.

Case definition and diagnostic criteria

In this study, the diagnosis of sparganosis was established based on one or a combination of the following criteria, which were synthesized by the authors based on a comprehensive review of the published literature and our institutional clinical experience. Etiological diagnosis: Detection of Spirometra mansoni larvae (sparganum) in surgical or biopsy specimens. Imaging diagnosis: Presence of typical “tunnel sign” or “beaded enhancement” on MRI or CT scans, combined with epidemiological history. Serological diagnosis: Positive result for anti-sparganum specific IgG antibodies in serum detected by ELISA. However, attention should be paid to potential cross-reactivity with other helminth infections (e.g., paragonimiasis, cysticercosis). Confirmation via imaging or mNGS is recommended in such cases. Molecular biological diagnosis: Detection of specific nucleic acid sequences of Spirometra mansoni by mNGS or PCR. Clinical diagnosis: In the context of epidemiological exposure, a presumptive diagnosis of sparganosis can be made when typical clinical manifestations (e.g., subcutaneous nodules, neurological symptoms) are present and accompanied by significantly elevated eosinophil counts, after excluding other potential etiologies (Liu et al. 2015; Iampreechakul et al. 2025; Song et al. 2007; Hu et al. 2022).

Systematic literature review and data analysis

A systematic search of the PubMed database (1980–2025) was performed using the keywords “sparganosis,” “Spirometra,” “child,” and “pediatric.” Inclusion criteria comprised case reports, case series, or epidemiological studies providing complete clinical information. Studies with incomplete data or duplicate reports were excluded. PubMed was selected as the primary database due to its comprehensive coverage of peer-reviewed biomedical literature and its common use in systematic reviews of rare infectious diseases. The year 1980 was chosen as the starting point because it marks the earliest decade with well-documented pediatric sparganosis cases and the widespread application of modern diagnostic techniques such as computed tomography (CT) and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). The exclusion of other databases (e.g., Embase, Web of Science, CNKI) is acknowledged as a limitation of this study. Extracted variables included publication year, country, age, infection site, diagnostic methods, treatment regimens, and outcomes.

It should be noted that the identification of pediatric patients relied primarily on the keywords “child” and “pediatric” during the literature search. Since age and sex data were not uniformly reported across all source literature, we cannot independently verify that all 135 included cases strictly represent pediatric patients; some may have been captured due to discussion of pediatric differential diagnoses or inclusion in mixed-age case series. This constitutes a inherent limitation of the systematic review.

Inclusion criteria: case reports, case series, or epidemiological studies reporting confirmed sparganosis cases; studies providing complete clinical information including at least three of the following: demographic data, clinical manifestations, diagnostic methods, treatment regimens, or clinical outcomes; full-text articles available in English. Exclusion criteria: studies with incomplete or insufficient clinical data; duplicate reports of the same patient from different publications; reviews, editorials, letters, or conference abstracts without original case data; animal studies or experimental studies.

Clinical data of five pediatric cases from our center

This study reports five pediatric cases of sparganosis from Yunnan Province, China. Their clinical manifestations encompass various types, including hepatobiliary, neurological, serosal, and disseminated forms, aiming to complement the existing literature on the clinical spectrum of this disease in the pediatric population. Concurrently, we review relevant domestic and international literature to offer insights for its early recognition, precise diagnosis, and integrated management. Case 1

Hepatobiliary Type -Portal Hypertension and Hypersplenism.

Demographics and Chief Complaint: Female, 7 years old, “discovered splenomegaly for over 4 years, hematemesis and melena for 3 days.”

History and Examination: The disease course spanned 4 years, featuring progressive splenomegaly culminating in upper gastrointestinal bleeding upon admission. Physical examination revealed severe anemia, abdominal distension, and massive splenomegaly. Laboratory tests indicated thrombocytopenia (51 × 10⁹/L), anemia (Hb 60 g/L), and weakly positive serum anti-sparganum IgG antibody detected by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). Imaging confirmed moderate-to-severe splenomegaly, hepatomegaly, portal hypertension, and collateral circulation formation.

Treatment and Outcome: Following supportive therapy, hemostasis, and hepatoprotective treatment, the patient’s condition stabilized. The discharge diagnosis considered portal hypertension possibly secondary to sparganum infection. Parasitization of the liver or portal venous system by sparganum can induce chronic granulomatous inflammation, leading to hepatic fibrosis, portal vein obstruction, and portal hypertension. Such reports are relatively rare but highly misleading. This case, with its prolonged course starting as isolated splenomegaly and progressing to life-threatening gastrointestinal bleeding, suggests that chronic parasitic infections should be considered in the differential diagnosis of unexplained portal hypertension, even in low-prevalence areas.

Case 2

Cerebrospinal Type (Epileptic) -Refractory Epilepsy.

Demographics and Chief Complaint: Male, 9 years old, “recurrent convulsions for over 7 years.”

History and Examination: The child’s illness began in early childhood, initially as febrile seizures, later progressing to afebrile seizures of various types. Brain MRI showed a small cystic lesion near the right lateral ventricle frontal horn. Electroencephalogram (EEG) was abnormal. Sparganum antibody was previously positive but later turned negative.

Treatment and Outcome: Diagnosed with “epilepsy and sparganosis,” the patient was treated with levetiracetam for seizure control. Praziquantel had been administered in the past. During this admission, the infectious disease department determined repeated anthelmintic treatment was unnecessary, focusing primarily on seizure management. Cerebral sparganosis is the most common form of central nervous system involvement, often presenting as refractory epilepsy (Kim et al. 1996). Characteristic MRI findings include the “tunnel sign,” beaded enhancement, or migratory linear enhancement, although atypical cystic lesions are also not uncommon (Song et al. 2007). Antibody titers may become negative following treatment or as the disease progresses, thus active infection cannot be ruled out based solely on serological results (Liu et al. 2015). This case illustrates the chronic nature and recurrent seizures typical of cerebral sparganosis, highlighting the need for long-term antiepileptic therapy combined with timely anthelmintic treatment.

Sparganum antibody was previously positive but later turned negative. This serological conversion suggests that the initial infection may have occurred in early childhood, with epilepsy emerging as a late neurological sequela of chronic cerebral sparganosis. The negative conversion of antibodies over time does not exclude the possibility of prior infection, particularly when characteristic imaging findings and compatible clinical manifestations are present (Liu et al. 2015).

Case 3

Serosal Type - Polyserositis.

Demographics and Chief Complaint: Female, 9 years old, “discovered pericardial effusion for 1 day.”

History and Examination: Acute onset, manifesting as massive pericardial, pleural, and peritoneal effusions. A key laboratory finding was extreme eosinophilia (absolute count 4.42 × 10⁹/L, peak proportion 51.3%). Parasite antibody testing showed multiple positives, including sparganum and Paragonimus. Post-pericardial window surgery pathology revealed inflammatory necrotic material.

Treatment and Outcome: Emergency pericardial window surgery was performed, followed by comprehensive treatment including praziquantel (three courses), methylprednisolone, and cefuroxime. The effusions resolved, eosinophil counts decreased, and the patient was discharged in stable condition. The markedly elevated eosinophil count in this case served as a crucial diagnostic clue. Multiple positive parasite antibodies suggest possible co-infection, which should be considered in endemic areas. Therapeutically, urgent pericardial decompression combined with antiparasitic drugs and corticosteroids (to mitigate inflammatory reactions from parasite death) was key to successful management.

Parasite antibody testing showed multiple positives, including sparganum and Paragonimus. It remains uncertain whether this represents concurrent infection or serological cross-reactivity, as both parasites are helminths with potentially shared antigenic epitopes, and their transmission routes overlap in endemic areas (e.g., consumption of untreated water or undercooked crustaceans). In the absence of confirmatory microbiological evidence (e.g., mNGS or pathological identification of both parasites), the possibility of either scenario cannot be definitively excluded (Li et al. 2010, Nawa et al. 2024).

Case 4

Disseminated Type – Multisystem Involvement and Psychological Disturbance.

Demographics and Core Issues: Male, 7 years old, suffering from recurrent abdominal pain, cough, liver impairment, foot swelling, lower limb weakness, fever, and psychological/behavioral problems triggered by sparganum infection. The child was hospitalized four times within three months, with the disease course exhibiting classic features of “larva migrans.” Symptoms progressed from respiratory and gastrointestinal to hepatic and soft tissue involvement, finally manifesting as neurological symptoms (lower limb weakness) and conversion disorder (hysteria).

Treatment and Outcome: After multidisciplinary consultation, the diagnosis pointed towards “psychological and behavioral disorders secondary to somatic illness.” Treatment included multiple courses of praziquantel, anti-infective therapy, and a shift towards rehabilitation and psychological intervention. This case presents the most complex picture of systemic sparganum dissemination. More importantly, it highlights how the prolonged, recurrent torment of the disease, hospitalization experiences, and drug side effects can significantly induce anxiety, depression, and conversion disorders in children, demonstrating the comprehensive impact of chronic illness on the “biopsychosocial” health model in children (Compas et al. 2011). This case alerts clinicians to the equal importance of addressing patients’ mental health alongside treating the physical disease in managing complex parasitic infections.

Case 5

Cerebrospinal Type (Myelitic) - Acute Flaccid Paralysis.

Demographics and Chief Complaint: Female, 6 years old, “left hypochondriac pain for 4 days, limping for 2 hours.”

History and Examination: Acute onset, rapidly progressing to bilateral lower limb weakness and inability to walk. MRI showed multiple abnormal signals in the thoracic spinal cord. Cerebrospinal fluid leukocyte count was elevated (27 × 10⁹/L). Eosinophil proportion was increased, sparganum antibody was positive, and antinuclear antibody (ANA) was concurrently positive (1:320).

Treatment and Outcome: Diagnosed with “acute flaccid myelitis and sparganum infection,” the patient received praziquantel, immediate high-dose methylprednisolone pulse therapy, and intravenous immunoglobulin. Following treatment with praziquantel and high-dose methylprednisolone pulse therapy, the sparganum antibody turned negative at the 4-week follow-up (from positive at admission to negative at week 4 post-treatment); spinal cord abnormalities resolved on imaging at the 3-month follow-up, and muscle strength gradually recovered over the subsequent 6 months. Direct invasion of the spinal cord by sparganum causing acute transverse myelitis or spinal cord compression has been reported both domestically and internationally (Iampreechakul et al. 2025, Lo et al. 1987, Kwon and Kim 2004), but presentation primarily as acute flaccid paralysis is less common. The pathogenesis may involve direct mechanical damage by the larvae, toxin secretion, and the induction of intense local immune-inflammatory responses. Therapeutically, while effective anthelmintic treatment is essential, early and adequate immunosuppressive therapy is decisive in mitigating neurological damage and improving prognosis.

In summary, among the five pediatric sparganosis cases at our center, the median age was 7 years, with a male-to-female ratio of 2:3 (2 males, 3 females). All children had a clear epidemiological history (contact with frogs/snakes or consumption of untreated water). Clinical manifestations were predominantly central nervous system (CNS) infections (60%), followed by ocular and subcutaneous infections. Laboratory investigations revealed markedly elevated eosinophil counts in all patients (median 4.42 × 10⁹/L), with positive serum-specific antibodies. MRI demonstrated characteristic “tunnel signs” or “migratory signs.” Regarding treatment, one patient underwent surgery combined with praziquantel, four received medical conservative treatment only, and four received corticosteroids in combination due to inflammatory reactions. All patients showed symptom improvement, with no recurrence on follow-up. Based on these cases and our systematic review, we have developed a diagnostic and therapeutic algorithm (Fig. 8) to guide clinical practice for pediatric sparganosis.

Global geographical distribution of cases

Figure 1 illustrates the global geographical distribution of sparganosis cases. Cases were highly concentrated in East Asia, with China reporting the highest number of cases (n = 44), followed by South Korea (n = 28), Japan (n = 13), and Thailand (n = 10). Additionally, cases were also reported in India, the United States, Italy, Australia, and other countries, spanning 24 countries/regions worldwide.

Spatiotemporal evolution trends

Figure 2 presents the spatiotemporal evolution of reported cases by decade (1980–1989, 1990–1999, 2000–2009, 2010–2019, 2020–2025). Between 1980 and 1999, cases were primarily confined to East Asia. From 2000 to 2009, the distribution began to expand into Southeast Asia, Europe, and North America. During 2010–2019, there was a notable increase in the number of cases, accompanied by a broader geographical spread. In the period from 2020 to 2025, while East Asia remained the primary endemic focus, sporadic cases continued to emerge in Europe, North America, and Oceania, indicating a trend towards global dissemination.

Distribution of clinical manifestations

Figure 3 displays the distribution of clinical manifestations among the 135 cases. Central nervous system (CNS) involvement was the most common clinical manifestation, accounting for 41.5% of cases. This was followed by superficial masses (37.8%), fever (17.0%), respiratory symptoms (13.3%), digestive system symptoms (10.4%), and urinary system symptoms (5.9%). This distribution suggests that sparganosis has the capacity for multi-system involvement, with the nervous system and subcutaneous tissue being the primary target organs.

Treatment modalities and outcomes

Figure 4 compares the clinical improvement rates associated with different treatment modalities. Among the 135 global cases, 50 patients (37.0%) received surgery alone, 30 (22.2%) received medication alone, 51 (37.8%) received combined therapy (surgery plus medication), and 4 (3.0%) received no specific antiparasitic treatment. As detailed in Table 3, the improvement rate was 96.7% (29/30) in the medication alone group, 96.0% (48/50) in the surgery alone group, and 92.2% (47/51) in the combined therapy group. The no treatment group showed a 100.0% improvement rate (4/4); however, these cases were excluded from statistical comparison due to the small sample size and uncertainty regarding whether spontaneous resolution or undocumented treatment contributed to the outcomes. To compare the efficacy among the three active treatment groups, the Pearson Chi-square test was performed on the binary outcome variable (improved vs. not improved). The results showed no statistically significant difference in improvement rates among the three groups (χ² = 1.02, df = 2, p = 0.60), indicating comparable clinical efficacy across surgery alone, medication alone, and combined therapy. This finding suggests that the choice of treatment modality may be guided by factors such as lesion accessibility, disease extent, and patient condition, rather than by differences in overall efficacy.

Temporal trends in case numbers and clinical characteristics

Figure 5 illustrates the temporal trends in the number of sparganosis cases, clinical improvement rates, and the proportion of neurological symptoms from 1980 to 2025. Case numbers: There has been a continuous increase since the 1980s, with 10 cases in 1980–1989, 15 cases in 1990–1999, 18 cases in 2000–2009, reaching a peak of 55 cases in 2010–2019, followed by 30 cases in 2020–2025. Clinical improvement rate: Improvement rates remained high across all decades, at 88% in 1980–1989, reaching 100% in 1990–2009, 98% in 2010–2019, and 90% in 2020–2025. The proportion of neurological symptoms was relatively high during 1980–1999 (66.7%-75.0%), then dropped to 40.0% in 2000–2009, and further declined to 31.7%-37.5% during 2010–2025.

Correlation analysis of clinical symptoms

Pearson correlation analysis of the six major clinical symptoms (Fig. 6) revealed overall weak to moderate correlations among the symptoms. Notably, a moderate negative correlation was observed between neurological symptoms and superficial masses (r = − 0.59, 95% CI: −0.69 to − 0.47, p < 0.001), suggesting a certain clinical “mutual exclusivity,” whereby Spirometra infection tends to predominantly involve a single system, supporting the rationale for classifying clinical subtypes of the disease. Fever showed a moderate positive correlation with digestive system symptoms (r = 0.52, 95% CI: 0.38 to 0.64, p < 0.001), reflecting that systemic inflammatory responses during the acute phase are often accompanied by gastrointestinal involvement. Correlations among the remaining symptoms were weak (|r| < 0.3), indicating a low probability of co-occurrence across symptoms within the system, which corroborates the highly heterogeneous nature of the clinical manifestations of sparganosis.

Discussion

Clinical specificity of pediatric sparganosis

It should be noted that while the five cases presented in this series are all from our center in Yunnan, China, sporadic pediatric cases have been documented in other regions including South Korea, Japan, Thailand, and India (Li et al. 2010, Kim et al. 1996, Kwon and Kim 2004). Our series contributes additional clinical subtypes—particularly the hepatobiliary and serosal forms—that have been less frequently characterized in the pediatric literature. Therefore, the value of our case series lies not in being the first or only report of pediatric sparganosis, but in enriching the current understanding of the diverse clinical phenotypes and diagnostic challenges of this disease in children.

The cases in this series suggest that sparganosis in children exhibits the following characteristics. First, children demonstrate high CNS susceptibility. Due to the incomplete development of the nervous system in children, the immune response may be more intense, predisposing them to neurological manifestations. However, the high frequency of CNS involvement in adults suggests that the neurotropic nature of the parasite itself remains the key determinant (Wiwanitkit 2005, Li et al. 2010). Second, significant elevation of eosinophils serves as an important indicative laboratory marker, as observed in all five of our cases (median 4.42 × 10⁹/L) (Iampreechakul et al. 2025). Third, a thorough epidemiological history is a crucial clue for initiating the diagnostic workup, as all of our patients had documented exposure to frogs, snakes, or untreated water sources (Nobayashi et al. 2006).

Notably, one of the reported pediatric cases (Case 5) presented with ANA positivity (1:320) in the context of confirmed sparganosis and acute flaccid myelitis. Although direct evidence linking sparganosis to ANA positivity is rarely documented in previous literature, this case provides an important clinical clue for such an association, potentially implicating complex post-infectious immunopathological mechanisms. Several hypotheses may explain this phenomenon. First, molecular mimicry is a central hypothesis, where structural similarities between parasite antigens and host self-tissues (e.g., neural myelin or nuclear components) may lead to a specific immune response erroneously attacking host tissues (Rojas et al. 2025). Second, the extensive tissue destruction caused by sparganum infection may expose hidden self-antigens (e.g., nuclear antigens from damaged cells), thereby initiating an autoimmune response. In this case, larval invasion of the spinal cord or skeletal muscle, with the consequent release of intracellular contents, could provide sustained stimulation to the immune system, ultimately leading to the production of autoantibodies such as ANA. Furthermore, the migration of sparganum larvae within tissues can cause direct mechanical damage and release excretory-secretory antigens, triggering a robust local Th2-type immune response, which may explain the marked peripheral eosinophilia observed in this patient (Liu et al. 2015). More importantly, this chronic, persistent antigenic stimulation may further disrupt immune tolerance, potentially initiating a cross-reactive immune response against self-antigens.

The successful treatment strategy in this case—concurrent anti-parasitic and potent immunosuppressive therapy—provides indirect support for the above mechanisms. Anthelmintic treatment alone may lead to the sudden release of a large amount of parasite antigen, exacerbating the immune-inflammatory response. Corticosteroids can effectively suppress this process, protecting neurological function. A randomized controlled trial on cerebral sparganosis confirmed that the combination of praziquantel and dexamethasone was significantly superior to praziquantel alone in improving neurological outcomes (Wang et al. 2019). Follow-up to determine whether the ANA ultimately seroconverted in this patient would be valuable, aiding in further clarifying the causal relationship between infection and autoimmunity.

This case suggests that for children from endemic areas presenting with acute neurological symptoms accompanied by eosinophilia and autoantibody positivity, sparganosis should be considered in the differential diagnosis. Infection may trigger transient or sustained autoimmune reactions through mechanisms such as molecular mimicry or antigen exposure. Early recognition and a combined therapeutic approach consisting of antiparasitic agents and immunomodulators are crucial. Future basic and clinical research is needed to elucidate the direct molecular mechanisms linking sparganum infection to the production of specific autoantibodies.

Evolution of diagnostic strategies

Traditional diagnosis relied on pathological biopsy. Modern diagnosis has evolved into a multimodal approach: Imaging: The MRI “tunnel sign” offers relatively high specificity. Serology: ELISA is commonly used to detect specific IgG antibodies against Spirometra antigens. However, cross-reactivity with other helminth infections (e.g., paragonimiasis, cysticercosis) has been reported; therefore, positive serological results should be interpreted in conjunction with imaging and molecular diagnostics. Molecular Diagnostics: Metagenomic Next-Generation Sequencing (mNGS) technology has significantly enhanced diagnostic capability for challenging cases (Song et al. 2007). The development of molecular biological methods (such as PCR, DNA sequencing and omics) offers a novel strategy for the precise identification of infectious disorders in clinical practice.

Optimization of treatment strategies

Surgical Excision: Indicated for focal, accessible lesions (Liu et al. 2014). Pharmacotherapy: Praziquantel is the first-line drug, recommended in adequate doses and for extended courses (Hu et al. 2022). Combination Therapy: Surgery combined with medication achieves the highest improvement rate. Corticosteroid Adjunct: Applicable for cases with severe inflammatory reactions (Li et al. 2010, Nawa et al. 2024). The algorithm further guides treatment stratification as follows: surgical excision is indicated for focal, accessible lesions (Liu et al. 2014); praziquantel at 25–50 mg/kg/day for 10–14 days is recommended as first-line pharmacotherapy, with extended courses for refractory or disseminated cases (Hu et al. 2022, Wang et al. 2019); combined surgery and praziquantel is preferred for operable cases; corticosteroids are adjunctively indicated for patients with severe inflammatory reactions or CNS/spinal cord edema (Li et al. 2010, Wang et al. 2019); and antiepileptics are used for seizure control (Kim et al. 1996). Post-treatment follow-up at 3 to 6 months, including clinical assessment, laboratory tests (eosinophil counts and antibody titers), and MRI, is recommended to monitor treatment response and detect potential recurrence.

Public health implications

Sparganosis is closely associated with dietary habits and sanitary conditions. Based on our findings and the existing literature, the following public health measures are recommended. First, health education and behavioral modification should be prioritized: communities in endemic areas should be educated on the risks of consuming raw or undercooked meat from frogs, snakes, and other secondary intermediate hosts, as well as the dangers of applying raw frog or snake flesh as poultices in traditional medicine (Zeng et al. 2023, Vasconcelos Miranda et al. 2023). Second, water sanitation improvement is essential: ensuring access to safe drinking water and preventing contamination of water sources with infected copepods are critical to interrupt transmission (Zeng et al. 2023). Third, surveillance and reporting systems should be strengthened: given the expanding geographical distribution observed in our systematic review, enhanced surveillance and increased clinical awareness in non-endemic regions are needed to facilitate early diagnosis and prevent further spread.

Conclusion

This study, based on 5 pediatric cases from our center and 135 global cases, reveals that pediatric sparganosis predominantly involves the CNS (41.5% of global cases, 60% in our series), with neurological manifestations including refractory epilepsy and acute flaccid myelitis. Marked eosinophilia (median 4.42 × 10⁹/L in our cases) serves as a key laboratory clue, while MRI findings such as the “tunnel sign” aid in radiological identification. Notably, our analysis of global cases demonstrated that the proportion of neurological symptoms has declined over recent decades, while the proportion of superficial masses has gradually increased, suggesting a potential shift in clinical presentation patterns. Furthermore, our correlation analysis revealed a moderate negative correlation between neurological symptoms and superficial masses (r = − 0.59, p < 0.001), supporting the clinical subtyping of sparganosis based on predominant system involvement. In terms of treatment, combination therapy (surgery plus medication) yielded comparably high improvement rates to surgery or medication alone, with no statistically significant difference among the three modalities (χ² = 1.02, p = 0.60).

Diagnosis requires a comprehensive approach integrating epidemiological history, imaging studies (particularly MRI), and laboratory testing (including serology and, when available, mNGS). Treatment should be individualized, with surgical intervention combined with long-term, adequate-dose praziquantel therapy recommended as a primary strategy, supplemented by corticosteroids when necessary. Strengthening public health education—including promoting food safety, improving water sanitation, and discouraging traditional poultice practices—constitutes the fundamental measure for preventing this disease.
