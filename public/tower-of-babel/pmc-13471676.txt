Bilateral acute mastoiditis during Epstein–Barr virus infection in a child with alagille syndrome: a case report and scoping review
Authors: Marco Guida, Piero Farruggia, Cataldo Pellegrino, Federica Mescolo, Giovanni Francesco Saia, Chiara Marino, Tiziana Guida Rutilio, Samentha Menager, Barbara Torrente, Sara Imparato, Flavia Volpe, Caterina Dalila Fernandez, Michelangelo Sciortino, Giovanni Corsello
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBilateral acute mastoiditis is an uncommon complication of acute otitis media and is often associated with systemic infections or underlying predisposing conditions. Viral infections, particularly Epstein–Barr virus (EBV), may facilitate secondary bacterial complications in susceptible pediatric patients. Alagille syndrome, a multisystem genetic disorder, may further increase vulnerability to severe infections due to immune and anatomical factors.MethodsWe report a case of bilateral acute mastoiditis occurring in a child with Alagille syndrome during acute EBV infection: clinical, laboratory, microbiological, and radiological findings were collected and analysed. Furthermore, a scoping review was conducted to contextualize diagnostic and therapeutic strategies in pediatric mastoiditis.ResultsA 12-year-old boy with known Alagille syndrome presented with persistent fever during confirmed EBV infection. During hospitalization, he developed clinical signs of bilateral mastoiditis. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography confirmed bilateral mastoid involvement. Blood cultures yielded Streptococcus pneumoniae. The patient was treated with intravenous broad-spectrum antibiotics (ceftriaxone and vancomycin) and underwent bilateral myringotomy with drainage. Clinical conditions improved rapidly, allowing step-down to oral antibiotic therapy and complete recovery without complications.ConclusionsThis case underscores the rare association between bilateral mastoiditis, EBV infection, and Alagille syndrome and highlights the potential interplay between viral-induced immune dysregulation and genetic susceptibility in promoting severe bacterial complications. Early recognition, appropriate antimicrobial therapy, and timely surgical intervention are essential to ensure favourable outcomes. Children with underlying genetic conditions may require closer monitoring during systemic viral infections to prevent severe complications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13052-026-02290-x.Keywords: Mastoiditis, Epstein-Barr virus, Alagille syndrome, Secondary bacterial infection, Pediatrics

Introduction

Acute mastoiditis is a serious, although relatively uncommon, complication of acute otitis media and upper respiratory tract infections [1, 2]. Bilateral acute mastoiditis is exceedingly rare, with only isolated cases and small case series reported in the literature; consequently, its true incidence remains unknown.

Increasing evidence suggests that viral infections —particularly EBV— may predispose immunologically vulnerable children to secondary bacterial complications [3–6]. Alagille syndrome, a genetic disorder with multisystem involvement, notably affecting the liver, heart, and skeletal system, may heighten susceptibility to severe infections due to underlying immune dysregulation [7, 8]. Prompt diagnosis and appropriate therapeutic interventions are essential in preventing long-term sequelae [2, 9].

Methods

A scoping review was conducted to map the available evidence on acute mastoiditis in pediatric patients. Literature searches were performed in major databases (PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, Embase, and the Cochrane Library) with particular focus on studies published in the last decade. The search strategy combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text terms related to mastoiditis. Search terms included combinations of the following keywords: “mastoiditis” and “children”; “mastoiditis” and “pediatric”; “mastoiditis” and “EBV”; “mastoiditis” and “bilateral”; “mastoiditis” and “Alagille”; “mastoiditis” and “immune”; “Alagille” and “immune”; “Alagille” and “EBV”; “mastoiditis” and “immunological deficiency”; “mastoiditis” and “immune deficiency”; “mastoiditis” and “management”; “mastoiditis” and “surgery”; “mastoid and surgery”; “mastoiditis” and “surgical”; “mastoid and surgical”; “mastoiditis” and “medical”; “mastoiditis” and “antibiotic”’; “mastoiditis” and “myringotomy”; “mastoiditis” and “tympanostomy”; “mastoiditis” and “grommet”; “mastoiditis” and “tube”; “mastoiditis” and “drainage”; “mastoiditis” and “mastoidectomy”; “mastoiditis” and “prognosis”. The scoping review was conducted and reported in accordance with the PRISMA Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines. No language restrictions were applied to the database searches. A search of references was performed to identify additional relevant articles. Eligible studies included case reports, case series, literature reviews, systematic reviews, retrospective analyses, clinical trials, randomised controlled trials, observational studies, clinical guidelines, and previous scoping or narrative reviews addressing diagnostic and therapeutic strategies. Titles and abstracts were screened independently by two authors, and potentially eligible articles underwent full-text review. In accordance with scoping review methodology, no formal risk-of-bias or quality assessment was performed. The results were synthesized narratively, with particular attention to antibiotic regimens, indications for surgery, and outcomes. Case presentation

A 12-year-old male was referred to the emergency department following a four-day history of persistent fever unresponsive to initial pharmacological treatment, accompanied by malaise. The patient had a known diagnosis of Alagille syndrome and was under regular follow-up at another local healthcare facility. Seven days prior, serological testing confirmed Epstein-Barr Virus (EBV) IgM positivity.

On clinical examination, the patient appeared moderately ill but hemodynamically stable. The oropharynx appeared normal, although the tonsils were hypertrophic. Tender, left-sided cervical lymphadenopathy was present. Cardiac auscultation revealed a grade 2/3 systolic murmur over the mid-precordial area, with no other significant findings. Abdominal palpation showed splenomegaly (13.8 cm in the longitudinal diameter), consistent with the patient’s known baseline condition related to Alagille syndrome, although EBV infection may have contributed to its persistence.

Laboratory results demonstrated a raised white cell count (9,600/µL), elevated C-reactive protein (12 mg/L; normal value < 5 mg/L), and slightly increased gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), in line with the hepatic involvement typically seen in Alagille syndrome. Serological assays were positive for both EBV and Mycoplasma pneumoniae, which was also detected by nasopharyngeal swab PCR. No additional formal immunological evaluation was performed during hospitalization beyond routine laboratory investigations, as the patient had already been evaluated at the reference center where he was being followed for Alagille syndrome, with no evidence of immunodeficiency.

Initial management included anti-inflammatory therapy and azithromycin, initiated on the basis of positive Mycoplasma PCR detection on a nasopharyngeal swab and supportive serological findings in the context of compatible clinical features. By day three of hospitalization, persistent fever and trace pericardial effusion led to the addition of systemic corticosteroids (prednisone, 25 mg twice daily, for 5 days). Otolaryngological assessment revealed bilateral tympanic membrane opacification with mild erythema (Fig. 2A/B). Nasal irrigation and nebulized corticosteroids were initiated. On day six, the patient’s condition deteriorated, with the appearance of retroauricular erythema, cervical pain, and increasing local tenderness, raising suspicion of mastoid involvement. A contrast-enhanced Computed Tomography (CT) scan of the temporal bones showed bilateral mastoid air cell opacification, consistent with acute bacterial mastoiditis. Empirical antibiotic therapy with ceftriaxone and vancomycin was initiated immediately, followed by rapid defervescence; shortly afterward, a blood culture and a nasal swab performed on the sixth day of hospitalization confirmed Streptococcus pneumoniae as the causative agent. On day eight, bilateral myringotomy with drainage of mucopurulent material was performed, leading to marked clinical improvement. Parenteral antibiotic therapy was continued for 10 days and subsequently de-escalated to oral amoxicillin-clavulanate for a further two weeks following negative follow-up cultures.

Discussion

Acute mastoiditis continues to represent a serious complication of acute otitis media (AOM) in children, predominantly caused by bacterial pathogens such as Streptococcus pneumoniae, Staphylococcus aureus, and Haemophilus influenzae [1, 2, 9–12]. However, bilateral mastoiditis is exceptional in pediatric patients and is often associated with underlying predisposing conditions or systemic infections [13]. Increasing evidence suggests that viral infections—particularly EBV—may predispose immunologically vulnerable children to secondary bacterial complications [3–6, 10–12]. To better contextualize our findings, we conducted a scoping review of the literature focusing on acute mastoiditis in children. Recent reviews have highlighted significant variability in diagnostic criteria, antibiotic regimens, and surgical indications, reflecting the lack of universally standardized protocols [12, 14, 15]. Many studies consistently identify Streptococcus pneumoniae as the leading pathogen, while also emphasizing the role of polymicrobial infections and the contribution of viral co-infections in modulating disease severity and progression [1, 12, 14–18]. In our patient, the coexistence of EBV infection, detection of Mycoplasma pneumoniae, and subsequent pneumococcal mastoiditis may reflect a transient alteration of mucosal and immune defences, potentially facilitating sequential infections.

From a diagnostic perspective, the literature supports early use of imaging—particularly contrast-enhanced CT—in cases of clinical deterioration or suspected complications, as performed in our patient [1, 10, 11, 14, 16, 18]. Complications of mastoiditis include deep neck abscess, subperiosteal and intracranial abscess, suppurative labyrinthitis, facial nerve palsy, meningitis/encephalitis, Bezold’s abscess, Gradenigo’s syndrome, and venous sinus thrombosis [11–18]. Magnetic Resonance Imaging must be considered in cases of suspected intracranial complications, especially extra-axial fluid collections and vascular lesions [14].

In this context, this case highlights several clinically relevant aspects:

Diagnostic complexity in EBV-associated mastoiditis. EBV infection, which rarely can cause mastoiditis even in children without any underlying disease [19], often confounds clinical assessment due to its systemic effects, including splenomegaly, fatigue, and immune dysregulation [3, 6].

Empirical antimicrobial therapy and escalation. The initial use of azithromycin was appropriate given the detection of Mycoplasma pneumoniae. However, clinical deterioration necessitated escalation to ceftriaxone and vancomycin following the identification of S. pneumoniae. Cohort studies, reviews, and meta-analyses consistently confirm that third-generation cephalosporins, particularly ceftriaxone administered at 50–100 mg/kg/day IV, represent the cornerstone of empirical therapy [11, 12, 14–18]. In cases of severe clinical presentation, failure of first-line treatment, suspected methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), or high local MRSA prevalence, intravenous vancomycin (60 mg/kg/day divided every 6 h) should be added [11, 12, 14–18]. Adjustment of the antibiotic regimen, especially in polymicrobial infections, should subsequently be guided by culture results and antibiogram findings. Intravenous antibiotic therapy is generally recommended for at least 7–10 days, followed by oral step-down therapy, most commonly with amoxicillin–clavulanate, for a total treatment duration of approximately 3–4 weeks [14–18]. Patients treated with exclusive medical therapy have a cure rate of 95.9% [20].

The role of corticosteroids. Systemic corticosteroids such as prednisone may attenuate inflammatory responses but remain controversial in complex patients with immunocompromise or co-infection [4, 5, 15, 17]. In our patient, corticosteroids, prescribed before the onset of mastoiditis, alleviated systemic symptoms, though the immunosuppressive risk could be non-negligible in a child with Alagille syndrome [7, 8].

Surgical management. Current literature [2, 11, 12, 14–17] recommends “conservative” surgery—primarily myringotomy with tympanostomy tube placement—in patients who fail to show clinical improvement after 48–72 h of intravenous antibiotic therapy or who present with complications, some authors advocating this approach in all patients without spontaneous tympanic membrane perforation [17]; in selected cases, retroauricular incision or needle aspiration may be effective for drainage of subperiosteal abscesses. Patients who are treated with myringotomy or abscess drainage recover in 97.5% and 94.4% respectively [20]. “Extensive” surgery refers to mastoidectomy (simple/cortical or radical), and may be required in more severe or refractory cases [14–17] with reported recovery rates of 89.1% [20]. Neurosurgical interventions are generally reserved for patients with intratemporal, intracranial, or vascular complications [14–18, 20]. Sequelae, much more frequent after extensive surgery than medical treatment with or without conservative surgery, include recurrent acute mastoiditis, recurrent AOM, cholesteatoma, persistent tympanic membrane perforation, and hearing impairment [20].

Alagille syndrome and susceptibility to infectious complications. This case is of particular interest due to the presence of Alagille syndrome, a multisystem disorder characterized by hepatic, cardiovascular, and skeletal abnormalities [7, 8]. Alagille syndrome is associated with immune abnormalities including impaired lymphocyte function, potentially contributing to an increased risk of severe infection [7, 21, 22]. Moreover, structural craniofacial anomalies and Eustachian tube dysfunction reported in patients with Alagille syndrome may further predispose these children to middle ear disease and related complications [23–25]. To date, no direct link between Alagille syndrome and mastoiditis has been conclusively established; however, this case may suggest a possible association between EBV infection, genetic susceptibility, and severe bacterial complications such as bilateral mastoiditis. It underscores the need for vigilant otological monitoring in Alagille syndrome patients, particularly in the setting of systemic viral infections [3–6, 26].

Key takeaways

Empirical antibiotic therapy: current clinical guidelines consistently recommend the use of broad-spectrum antibiotics, such as ceftriaxone, targeting the most frequent pathogens, including Streptococcus pneumoniae, Staphylococcus aureus (notably methicillin-resistant strains, MRSA), and Streptococcus pyogenes in patients with mastoiditis. The empirical addition of vancomycin is advised in cases of suspected MRSA involvement or severe clinical presentation [11, 12, 14–18, 20].

The empirical addition of vancomycin is advised in cases of suspected MRSA involvement or severe clinical presentation [11, 12, 14–18, 20].

Surgical intervention: Early surgical management is warranted when there is no clinical improvement within 48–72 h of intravenous antibiotic therapy. Recommended procedures include myringotomy with or without tympanostomy tube placement. In more complicated presentations, retroauricular incision, needle aspiration, and mastoidectomy may be required [14–18, 20].

Considerations for Alagille syndrome: although not specifically addressed in standard guidelines, underlying genetic conditions such as Alagille syndrome should be taken into account, given their potential to increase infection susceptibility. In such cases, therapeutic regimens may require individualized adjustment, and closer clinical monitoring is advised throughout the course of illness [7, 8, 14–18, 20, 21].

Conclusion

Although this report presents some limitations, including the single-patient design and the absence of a comprehensive immunological reassessment during the acute phase, it highlights the complex interplay between genetic predisposition, viral infection, and secondary bacterial complications in the evolution of bilateral acute mastoiditis. The presence of Alagille syndrome may have contributed to the atypical clinical course and increased susceptibility to severe infection [7, 8, 20–25]. Early imaging, prompt initiation of broad-spectrum antimicrobial therapy, and timely surgical intervention were crucial to achieving clinical resolution [11, 12, 14–18, 20]. In light of the immunological and anatomical peculiarities associated with Alagille syndrome, children with this condition may warrant enhanced surveillance for ear, nose and throat complications, especially during or after systemic viral illnesses such as EBV infection [3–6, 26]. Further studies are needed to better define the potential association between Alagille syndrome and otological complications such as mastoiditis.
