Integrated transcriptome, metabolome and activity-guided fractionation of Parnassia wightiana Wall. reveal its insecticidal potential
Authors: Gaijuan Tang, Yucun Yang, Rong An, Zhenlan Zhang, Jianchang Li, Yonghong Li, Yonghong Wang, Chao Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundParnassia wightiana Wall. (P. wightiana) is a traditional medicinal herb with demonstrated potential for developing eco-friendly botanical insecticides, but its insecticidal properties remain underexplored.MethodsHere, we systematically evaluated the insecticidal activities of different plant tissues through integrated bioactivity assays and metabolomic profiling. Combining bioactivity-guided fractionation with transcriptomic analysis, we identified bioactive insecticidal compounds and elucidated key genes involved in secondary metabolite biosynthesis.ResultsBioassays revealed that root extracts exhibited the highest insecticidal efficacy against third-instar Mythimna separata (M. separata) larvae, with a median lethal concentration (LC50) of 19.659 mg/mL. Introduction

The identification of active substances or lead compounds from plant sources represents a critical strategy in the development of novel eco-friendly pesticides. Plants within the Saxifragaceae family are widely recognized for their substantial medicinal value, with several species having been successfully developed into pharmaceutical products [1, 2]. Members of this family exhibit diverse biological activities, including antiviral [3], antibacterial [4], antioxidant [5], and antitumor effects [6]. Parnassia wightiana Wall. (P. wightiana), a perennial herb belonging to the Saxifragaceae family [7], is commonly known as Ji-mei-hua-cao, Cang-er-qi, Qiao-mai-ye, or Ding-chuang-cao in traditional Chinese medicine, primarily distributed in the Qinling Mountains of China [8]. It has been traditionally used to treat cough, hemoptysis, urinary calculi, cholelithiasis, traumatic injuries, subcutaneous infections [9], eczema, furuncles, and poisoning [10]. Pharmacological studies have revealed its potent toxicity against Tetranysthus cinnabarinus [11]. Despite its therapeutic potential, the chemical constituents of P. wightiana and their potential agricultural applications remain inadequately explored, warranting further investigation.

Terpenoids, as one of the major classes of plant secondary metabolites, possess remarkable structural diversity and broad-spectrum biological activities [12]. These compounds demonstrate significant potential for applications in pharmaceuticals, agriculture, cosmetics, and the food industry [13, 14]. Terpenoids play a pivotal role in plant defense and stress responses [15–17]. Particularly noteworthy is their insecticidal efficacy against economically important lepidopteran pests, such as the Mythimna separata and the Peridroma saucia [18, 19]. Toosendanin, for instance, has been documented to exhibit potent antifeedant and growth-inhibitory effects against various agricultural pests [20, 21]. Previous phytochemical investigations have revealed that P. wightiana produces terpenoids with marked insecticidal properties [22, 23]. Structure-activity relationship studies further indicate that chemical modifications, particularly of the C-ring moiety, can significantly enhance the pesticidal activity of these compounds [21, 24, 25].

Dihydro-β-agarofuran sesquiterpenoids, a structurally diverse class of natural products, are predominantly sourced from plants of the Celastraceae family [26]. Researchers have extracted dihydro-β-agarofuran compounds from diverse plant sources and confirmed their insecticidal and antifeedant activities against multiple pest species [27–30]. Multiple studies have successfully isolated dihydro-β-agarofuran compounds from P. wightiana [11, 31, 32]. P. wightiana extracts demonstrate broad-spectrum insecticidal activity against lepidopteran and dipteran pests, particularly M. separata and Plutella xylostella [33]. In addition, the plant extract exhibited acaricidal activity against Psoroptes cuniculi, whereas the isolated dihydro-β-agarofuran derivatives demonstrated significant cytotoxic effects [31]. Previous investigations have revealed that sesquiterpenes and monoterpenes from Carpesium abrotanoides possess both insecticidal and antifeedant activities [34]. The sesquiterpenoid Celahin B demonstrates significant toxicity against T. cinnabarinus, with a mortality rate reaching 47.3% at 1.0 mg/mL concentration [11]. Particularly noteworthy is a dihydro-β-agarofuran sesquiterpene containing a C-2 ketone moiety, along with its derivatives, which exhibited remarkable antifeedant activity against third-instar M. separata larvae [23]. Interestingly, while dihydro-β-agarofuran sesquiterpene polyol esters isolated from P. wightiana showed potent antifungal activity against Cytospora sp., they displayed no insecticidal effects on M. separata [22]. Therefore, the specific bioactive compounds of P. wightiana and their mechanisms of action remain to be elucidated.

The biosynthesis of terpenoids in plants primarily occurs through two distinct metabolic pathways: the mevalonic acid (MVA) pathway in the cytosol and the 2-C-methyl-D-erythritol 4-phosphate (MEP) pathway in plastids [35]. These two pathways operate coordinately through crosstalk mechanisms to synergistically regulate terpenoid biosynthesis [36, 37]. The process is catalyzed by terpene synthase (TPS), a key enzyme encoded by TPS genes that converts isoprenoid precursors into diverse terpenoid skeletons [38]. TPS enzymes utilize five-carbon building blocks, such as isopentenyl pyrophosphate (IPP) and dimethylallyl pyrophosphate (DMAPP), to generate intermediate substrates, including geranyl pyrophosphate (GPP) for monoterpenes and farnesyl pyrophosphate (FPP) for sesquiterpenes [39, 40]. The TPS gene family exhibits functional diversification: TPS-a, TPS-b, and TPS-g subfamilies are primarily responsible for the synthesis of monoterpenes and sesquiterpenes, whereas TPS-c and TPS-e/f subfamilies mainly contribute to diterpene biosynthesis [41, 42]. Beyond their biosynthetic roles, TPS gene family members are involved in regulating plant stress responses, highlighting their ecological significance [43, 44].

Metabolomic profiling established preliminary correlations between insecticidal activity and metabolite composition. Bioassay-directed isolation identified the terpenoid compound Celahin B as a potent insecticidal agent. Subsequent transcriptomic and metabolic network analyses uncovered critical TPS genes involved in terpenoid biosynthesis, highlighting the TPS-a subfamily’s potential role in sesquiterpene synthesis. These results elucidate the insecticidal mechanisms of P. wightiana and provide a foundation for developing biopesticides via metabolic engineering and synthetic biology.

Materials and methods

Plant material

The whole plants of Parnassia wightiana Wall. (P. wightiana) were collected from the Huoditang Experimental Forest Station in the Qinling Mountains, from 33°18′ to 33°28′ N and 108°21′ to 108°39′ E, during the flowering stage of the plant (Fig. 1A, B). The plant material was authenticated by Associate Professor Yan Li of Northwest A&F University. Dried specimens of whole plants, roots, shoots, and flowers were separately ground into powders for subsequent experimental procedures. The voucher specimen associated with this study was deposited in the Chinese Virtual Herbarium (CVH) under the accession number XBGH008561.

Extraction and isolation

The air-dried and powdered plant material of P. wightiana (10 kg) was extracted with 95% ethanol (C2H5OH) (100 L) across three consecutive 7-day intervals at 20–25 °C. After solvent removal via rotary evaporation (Shanghai SENCO Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) at 45 °C, a dark residue (2.6 kg) was obtained and suspended in distilled water (5 L). This was then partitioned successively with petroleum ether (boiling range 60–90 °C, 5 L × 3), ethyl acetate (5 L × 3), and n-butanol (5 L × 3) to yield fractions A (petroleum ether phase, 402 g), B (ethyl acetate phase, 867 g), and C (n-butanol phase, 642 g). The methanol-soluble fraction was subjected to silica gel column chromatography (200–300 mesh, Qingdao Marine Chemical Plant, China) and eluted with a stepwise gradient of petroleum ether–ethyl acetate (from 1:0 to 0:1, v/v), and finally with pure methanol. Eleven fractions (A1–A11) were obtained based on thin-layer chromatography (TLC; plates from Shanghai Spectral Instrument Co., Ltd., China) profiles. The active fractions were further purified by column chromatography over Sephadex LH-20 (Walkman Company, China). All solvents, including ethanol, acetone, methanol, petroleum ether, chloroform, ethyl acetate, and n-butanol, were purchased from Adamas Company (China). The isolated compounds were dissolved in chloroform and stored in sealed amber vials at 4 °C to prevent photodegradation and volatilization.

Construction and analyses of metabolite profiles

Widely targeted metabolomic profiling was conducted on root, shoot, and flower tissues (Fig. 1D). 25 mg of lyophilized and finely ground plant tissue was homogenized in liquid nitrogen and subsequently transferred to a pre-chilled Eppendorf tube. Subsequently, 800 µL of ice-cold 50% aqueous methanol was added, and the mixture was meticulously homogenized and incubated at − 20 °C to facilitate optimal extraction. The homogenate was then centrifuged at 25,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. A 550 µL aliquot of the supernatant was transferred to a new tube, and 50 µL was reserved for quality control in mass spectrometry-based. A solid-phase extraction (SPE) column was sequentially preconditioned with acetonitrile, methanol, and ultrapure water, followed by elution using acetonitrile. The eluate was collected and stored at − 80 °C prior to analysis. Chromatographic separation was performed on an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (50 °C) with a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min. Each sample was injected at a volume of 10 µL and analyzed using a Xevo G2 XS QTOF mass spectrometer operated in both positive and negative ion modes. Raw data were processed to identify differentially accumulated metabolites, followed by pathway enrichment analysis. To ensure the stability of the LC-MS system throughout the analytical sequence, a pooled quality control sample was analyzed after every 10 experimental samples [45–47].

Isolation and identification of insecticidal compounds

Separation of active compounds: The collected P. wightiana powder was dried and extracted with 90% ethanol at 20–25 °C over seven extraction cycles. The first three extractions (3 × 72 h) were followed by four 48 h extractions with intermittent stirring every 3 h. The combined extracts were concentrated under reduced pressure and sequentially partitioned using petroleum ether, chloroform, ethyl acetate, and n-butanol. For the chloroform extract (100 g), gradient elution was performed using a petroleum ether/acetone system. The elution ratios of petroleum ether to acetone were varied stepwise. Fractions were combined based on TLC analysis (stained with 5% mercaptoethanol), resulting in 11 combined fractions.

Identification of active compounds: the purity of isolated compounds was assessed by TLC, and structural characterization was performed using a Bruker DRX-500 MHz NMR spectrometer (AVANCEIII, Bruker, Switzerland) complemented by ultraviolet (UV) spectroscopy. Comprehensive structural analysis involved 1H-NMR and 13C-NMR experiments, with structural assignments verified through comparison with published spectral data from relevant literature.

Insect bioassays

The leaf disk method [48, 49] was used to measure the stomach toxicity of different plant extracts and compounds on third-instar Mythimna separata (M. separata) larvae. Corn leaves were cut into 2 cm × 2 cm squares and treated with 0.5 mL of each test solution. Following solvent evaporation, a second leaf of identical size was placed on top using homemade starch paste to create a toxic leaf dish. The dishes were placed in 9 cm Petri dishes containing moistened filter paper to maintain humidity. Thirty third-instar larvae, starved for 4 h, were introduced into each Petri dish. Each treatment was repeated three times. Mortality rates were recorded at 24, 48, and 72 h, and corrected mortality was calculated. LC50 values of the test plant extracts and compounds were determined using acetone solutions at five different concentrations (50, 100, 200, 400 and 500 mg/L). Toosendanin was used as the positive control, while solvent-only and untreated groups served as negative controls.

RNA sequencing

As a perennial herb, P. wightiana presents significant challenges for obtaining high-quality RNA from its root tissues due to their high content of secondary metabolites and fibrous structure. Consequently, RNA isolation and subsequent sequencing were performed exclusively on shoot tissues (Fig. 1D). Total RNA was purified from plant tissues using Trizol, and its quality was assessed with a NanoDrop ND-1000, Bioanalyzer 2100, and agarose gel electrophoresis. RNA samples with concentrations > 50 ng/µL, RNA integrity number (RIN) > 7.0, and A260/A280 > 1.8, with a total yield > 1 µg, were used for library preparation. mRNA was enriched using oligo (dT) beads, fragmented at 94 °C, and used to synthesize cDNA. The resulting cDNA was purified, size-selected to 300 bp ± 50 bp, and amplified by PCR. Sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Raw FASTQ data were cleaned using cutadapt, aligned using HISat2, and assembled with StringTie. Gene expression was quantified using the ballgown package, and differential expression was analyzed with edgeR and DESeq2. Genes with a fold change > 2 or < 0.5 and p-value < 0.05 were considered differentially expressed. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed on these genes.

Data processing

Statistical analysis and graphical representation in this study were conducted using GraphPad Prism, SPSS and Excel software. The chi-square goodness-of-fit test was employed to analyze the data distribution. The significance of intergroup differences was assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). If the ANOVA results were significant, post hoc comparisons were further conducted using Duncan’s multiple range test. The bioassay was conducted with three biological replicates, and the results are expressed as mean and standard deviation.

Bioassay results revealed that crude extracts from all tested tissues exhibited significant insecticidal activity against third-instar M. separata larvae (Fig. 1C; Table 1). Metabolite profiles of different tissues

Metabolite profiles of roots, shoots, and flowers were generated using six biological replicates per tissue type. Principal component analysis (PCA) demonstrated significant divergence in differentially expressed ion (DEI) patterns between roots and shoots, as well as between roots and flowers. In contrast, shoot and flower metabolite profiles showed comparatively minor differences (Fig. 2A, B). These results were consistent with the insecticidal activity assays (Table 1), indicating that root metabolites of P. wightiana possess enhanced insecticidal activity compared to other tissues.

In both positive (Pos) and negative (Neg) ion modes, we identified 10,093 and 8,729 differentially expressed ions (DEIs; RSD ≤ 30%), respectively. Pathway enrichment of differentially expressed compounds

To elucidate the metabolic pathways associated with insecticidal activity in different tissues, we performed integrated pathway enrichment analysis of all differentially expressed ions (DEIs). The analysis identified significant enrichment of several terpenoid-related pathways in root tissues, including diterpenoid biosynthesis, sesquiterpenoid and triterpenoid biosynthesis, terpenoid backbone biosynthesis, and monoterpenoid biosynthesis, along with arachidonic acid metabolism (Fig. 3G). These findings strongly suggest that terpenoid metabolism plays a central role in the production of insecticidal compounds in P. wightiana.

Metabolic network of terpenoids in P. wightiana

Plant terpenoid biosynthesis involves two pathways: the MEP (C11434, 2-C-methyl-D-erythritol 4-phosphate) pathway and the MVA (C00418, (R)-mevalonate) pathway (Fig. 4) [16, 17, 36, 37]. In the MEP pathway, upstream intermediates including D-glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate (G3P; C00118), 1-deoxy-D-xylulose 5-phosphate (DXP; C11437), 4-(cytidine 5’-diphospho)−2-C-methyl-D-erythritol (CDP-ME; C11435), dimethylallyl diphosphate (DMAPP; C00235), and isopentenyl diphosphate (IPP; C00129) all exhibited low enrichment levels in root tissues (Fig. 4; Table S2). However, we observed marked enrichment of downstream terpenoid synthase (TPS) substrates, particularly (2Z,6Z)-farnesyl diphosphate (Z, Z-FPP; C19760) (Fig. 4; Table S2). Similarly, in the MVA pathway, upstream compounds were not enriched in roots, but the downstream TPS substrates were significantly enriched, including 2-cis,6-trans-farnesyl diphosphate (Z, E-FPP, C16826) and trans, trans-farnesyl diphosphate (E, E-FPP, C00448) (Fig. 4; Table S2). These findings highlight the critical role of TPS substrates (Z, Z-FPP, Z,E-FPP, and E, E-FPP) in root terpenoid metabolism, with their downstream products showing significant accumulation. The downstream products of FPP enzymes include sesquiterpenes and triterpenes. We identified four significantly enriched acyclic sesquiterpenes: (E, E)-farnesal (C03461), nivalenol (C06080), albaflavenol (C17953), and solavetivone (C09737). Additionally, two chair-chair-chair-boat configured triterpenes 24-hydroxy-β-amyrin (C19732) and arabidiol (C19828) were markedly enriched in root tissues (Fig. 4; Table S2). We infer that the sesquiterpenes, triterpenes, and other unidentified terpenoid compounds likely contribute to the high insecticidal activity observed in the roots of P. wightiana.

Insecticidal activities of identified compounds

Through activity-guided fractionation of P. wightiana extracts, we isolated insecticidal compounds and evaluated their efficacy against M. separata. Bioassay results demonstrated significant variation in toxicity among different extracts. At 50 mg/mL concentration, the 72 h corrected mortality was 45.12% for the ethanol extract and 30% for the petroleum ether extract, while the chloroform (CHCl₃) extract exhibited complete lethality (100% corrected mortality). Even at the lower concentration of 10 mg/mL, the chloroform extract maintained strong insecticidal activity (76.67% corrected mortality), significantly outperforming other extracts (Table 2). These findings indicate that the active insecticidal compounds are primarily concentrated in the chloroform extract.

Celahin B demonstrated potent insecticidal activity against third-instar M. separata larvae. At a concentration of 500 µg/mL, the compound achieved 91.67% corrected mortality, significantly surpassing the positive control toosendanin (75% corrected mortality) and the insecticidal compounds C13 and C15 isolated from P. wightiana (Fig. 6A). Dose-response analysis through five concentration gradients revealed an LC50 value of 116.642 µg/mL (95% confidence interval: 93.705–140.297 µg/mL; Fig. 6B). At this LC50 concentration, symptoms of poisoning were observed in M. separata after ingestion, including body shortening, curling, lateral rolling and paralysis, as well as gut swelling. Under 500 μm magnification using a stereomicroscope, the intestines were found to be swollen and deformed, with the internal structure appearing blurred, indicating severe toxic effects (Fig. 6C-J).

Identification and phylogenetic analysis of novel genes involved in sesquiterpene metabolism in P. wightiana

To elucidate the terpenoid biosynthetic pathways in P. wightiana, we conducted RNA-seq analysis on shoot and flower tissues. Root tissues were excluded due to technical challenges in RNA extraction caused by complex secondary metabolites. The sequencing data showed that the average percentage of clean reads in the transcriptome reached 94.82%, with Q20 at 97.58% and Q30 above 90%, and 86.88% of the reads could be mapped to the reference genome of P. wightiana, indicating high sequencing and alignment quality (Table S5). High-throughput sequencing generated 154,227 transcripts, of which 96,701 were annotated in the NCBI nr database and 71,251 in the SwissProt database. Additionally, 77,297, 78,639, and 52,310 transcripts were annotated in the KEGG, Pfam, and GO databases, respectively. In total, 107,197 transcripts were annotated, accounting for 69.51% of all transcripts (Table S6).

Phylogenetic analysis classified the 87 identified TPS genes of P. wightiana into five distinct subfamilies (Fig. 7A). The TPS-a subfamily was most predominant, representing 49.4% of all TPS genes (Table 3). Transcriptomic analysis revealed distinct expression patterns among TPS subfamilies in P. wightiana. Notably, although the PwTPS-e subfamily contained numerous members, its expression remained consistently low across aerial tissues, suggesting minimal involvement in terpenoid biosynthesis in these organs (Fig. 7A and B). Discussion

The potential of P. wightiana as a botanical pesticide source

The development of environmentally friendly botanical pesticides represents a critical pathway toward achieving sustainable agricultural development. P. wightiana, a traditional medicinal plant, has been extensively utilized in pharmaceutical applications owing to its well-documented anti-inflammatory and antimicrobial properties. A variety of dihydro-β-agarofuran-type compounds have been isolated from P. wightiana, and these compounds possess multiple biological activities, including cytotoxic effects, insect antifeedant activity, and insecticidal efficacy [8, 9, 11, 22, 23, 32]. Among them, several dihydro-β-agarofuran-type compounds have been confirmed to exhibit significant toxicity against Psoroptes cuniculi, while some compounds only show fungicidal activity with no significant insecticidal effects [22, 31]. Notably, Hu isolated the sesquiterpenoid Celahin B from this plant, showing 47.3% corrected mortality against T. cinnabarinus at 1.0 mg/mL [11]. Additionally, the dihydro-β-agarofuran-type sesquiterpene with a C-2 ketone group from P. wightiana could be structurally modified through acylation and reduction at C-8 and C-2 positions to enhance antifeedant activity against M. separata [23]. In the present study, the extract of P. wightiana was found to exhibit potent toxicity and antifeedant activities against M. separata. Further analysis revealed that Celahin B, a dihydro-β-agarofuran compound isolated from the extract, showed significant insecticidal efficacy. To our knowledge, this study represents the first report on the insecticidal activity of Celahin B (isolated from P. wightiana) against M. separata, a major grain crop pest that causes substantial agricultural losses [51]. These results demonstrate the potential of P. wightiana for developing environmentally benign botanical pesticides and controlling this agriculturally important pest of significant economic value.

Insecticidal activity of root extracts and tissue-specific distribution of secondary metabolites

The present study demonstrated that the root extracts of P. wightiana exhibited the most potent insecticidal activity. Furthermore, metabolic pathway enrichment analysis revealed that terpenoid biosynthesis pathways were significantly enriched in the roots (Fig. 3). This tissue-specific distribution of bioactive compounds is a common phenomenon. The accumulation sites of plant defensive secondary metabolites are variable, determined by both the chemical characteristics of the compounds and plant species, reflecting an ecological adaptation strategy developed through long-term evolution [52]. Significant differences in compound concentrations were observed across different plant tissues [53], and the extracts from these tissues exhibit varying insecticidal activities [54–56]. Our observation that root extracts were most potent is consistent with this pattern. This variation may also be associated with solvent polarity, which influences the solubility of different compounds during extraction [57]. While some studies report that the contents of total phenolics, total flavonoids, and terpenoids in leaf tissues are significantly higher than those in root tissues [58], our results highlight the root as a critical accumulation site for insecticidal terpenoids in P. wightiana. The biosynthesis of phenylpropanoids and terpenoids in stems as well as the expression of their related genes have further indicated the tissue-specific distribution of these compounds [59].

Given their diverse biological activities [60–65], the tissue-specific distribution of terpenoids in plants is likely a result of multiple factors, including species-specific traits, defense mechanisms, ecological adaptability, and perennial root accumulation.

Terpene synthase genes are critically involved in the synthesis of insecticidal metabolites

Currently, the genome of P. wightiana remains unsequenced, but de novo transcriptome analysis can be employed to identify terpenoid biosynthesis-related genes, providing a foundation for artificial synthesis of bioactive compounds.

As key catalysts in terpenoid diversification, TPS genes play pivotal roles in plant terpenoid metabolism. Specifically, the TPS-a subfamily primarily catalyzes sesquiterpenoid biosynthesis, while TPS-b drives monoterpenoid production—both of which are crucial for plant defense [42, 66]. Consistent with their defensive function, our transcriptomic analysis revealed that TPS-a and TPS-b genes (particularly TPS-a) exhibited relatively high expression levels in shoot and flower tissues (Fig. 7). This high expression likely promotes the accumulation of downstream defensive compounds [41, 67–71], suggesting an active role of these genes in chemical defense.

Phylogenetic reconstruction classified these TPS genes into five distinct subfamilies (TPS-a, b, c, e, and f). Notably, the TPS-a, b, and e subfamilies exhibited significantly higher gene copy numbers in P. wightiana compared to the model plants Arabidopsis thaliana and rice (Table 3; Fig. 7A), suggesting a genomic basis for terpenoid diversification. Although this quantitative abundance does not necessarily correlate directly with terpenoid diversity (as individual terpene synthases can catalyze the formation of multiple products from a single substrate) [70, 72], the expansion of these subfamilies remains functionally significant. The functional significance of these subfamily expansions may be attributed to the versatile roles of TPS genes, which are potentially involved in phenolic compound biosynthesis [73], flavonoid metabolism, and abiotic stress responses [74], while their expression has been demonstrated to enhance insect resistance in crops [75–77]. In summary, the numerically expanded TPS-a and TPS-b subfamilies are likely to function as critical catalytic mediators in the biosynthesis of insecticidal compounds (particularly sesquiterpenoids and monoterpenoids) in P. wightiana, thereby establishing a foundational framework for future functional characterization and synthetic biology applications.

However, given the lack of root-specific transcriptomic data in the current study, we cannot exclude the possibility that root-localized TPS gene expression may drive the biosynthesis of these bioactive metabolites—a hypothesis that requires experimental validation. Furthermore, the 87 TPS transcripts identified in this study may not represent the complete repertoire of TPS gene family members in this species. Future whole-genome sequencing efforts will be essential to fully characterize the TPS gene complement (particularly those genes not expressed under our experimental conditions), thereby providing a comprehensive understanding of the genetic basis for terpenoid biosynthesis in P. wightiana.

Conclusion

In this study, we systematically investigated the insecticidal activity of P. wightiana using an integrated approach combining metabolomics, transcriptomics, bioactivity-guided fractionation, and bioassays. The results demonstrated that the crude root extract exhibited significantly higher insecticidal activity compared to shoot and flower extracts in P. wightiana. Distinct differences in ion abundance were observed between roots and aerial tissues (shoots and flowers), with terpenoid metabolites being particularly enriched in the roots. We identified Celahin B as a key bioactive compound responsible for the observed insecticidal activity. Furthermore, transcriptomic analysis revealed that the TPS-a subfamily of terpene synthase genes may be involved in the biosynthesis of these active terpenoid metabolites. These findings provide a scientific foundation for the development of novel plant-derived pesticides.
