Cluster scattering diagrams via quiver moduli and tight gradings
Amanda Burcroff, Kyungyong Lee, Lang Mou, Gregg Musiker, Markus Reineke

Abstract
. We study rank-2 cluster scattering diagrams through moduli spaces of quiver representations and a recently developed combinatorial framework of tight gradings. Combining quiver-theoretic and combinatorial methods, we prove and extend a collection of conjectures posed by Elgin–Reading–Stella concerning the structural and enumerative properties of the wall-function coefficients. The tight grading perspective also provides a new proof of the Weyl group symmetry of the scattering diagram.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Background and motivation

Scattering diagrams
[
16
,
14
]
are combinatorial structures encoding wall-crossing phenomena arising in various sources of mathematics. Cluster scattering diagrams of Gross–Hacking–Keel–Kontsevich
[
11
]
are a special class attached to cluster algebras/varieties
[
9
,
8
]
, where the wall-functions reflect their mutation structure and positivity properties. In rank 2, they are closely related to the
tropical vertex
of Gross–Pandharipande–Siebert, where the wall-functions are expressed through generating series of relative Gromov-Witten invariants of toric surfaces
[
13
]
and Euler characteristics of quiver moduli
[
22
,
23
]
.

From the cluster algebra perspective, the coefficients of rank-2 wall-functions lead to
the Laurent positivity in rank-2 cluster algebras
\mathcal{A}(b,c)
of Fomin–Zelevinsky
[
27
]
. This positivity admits both combinatorial descriptions via compatible gradings using edges in Dyck paths
[
18
,
17
]
and more geometric ones via scattering diagrams
[
11
,
15
]
.

Based on extensive computational evidence, Elgin, Reading, and Stella
[
7
]
formulated a collection of conjectures regarding the wall-function coefficients
\tau(i,j)
of the cluster scattering diagram
\mathfrak{D}_{(b,c)}
associated with
\mathcal{A}(b,c)
. These conjectures, which primarily concern the dependence of
\tau(i,j)
on the positive integers
b
and
c
, form the starting point of the present work. Tight gradings, recently introduced in
[
4
,
5
]
, refine the framework of compatible gradings
[
17
,
24
]
, and provide a unified combinatorial model for these coefficients.
Our approach uses both quiver representation techniques and the combinatorics of tight gradings to confirm many of these predictions and to derive further generalizations.

Quiver moduli are algebraic varieties parametrizing isomorphism classes of stable representations of quivers. Despite being interesting in their own right for encoding hard representation-theoretic classification problems, it turned out that their topological invariants encode enumerative invariants such as Gromov-Witten and Donaldson-Thomas invariants. 2.
Rank 2 scattering diagrams and the change-of-lattice

We briefly recall some basics of rank-2 (generalized) cluster scattering diagrams.

Fix a base field
\Bbbk
of characteristic zero. For
i=1,2
, take power series

P_{i}(x_{i})=1+\sum_{k\geq 1}p_{i,k}x_{i}^{k}\in\Bbbk\llbracket x_{i}\rrbracket\quad\text{where}\quad p_{i,k}\in\Bbbk.

By a result of Kontsevich–Soibelman
[
16
]
, the two power series
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
uniquely determine a
consistent scattering diagram

(2.1)

\mathfrak{D}(P_{1},P_{2})=\{(\mathbb{R}(1,0),P_{1}),(\mathbb{R}(0,1),P_{2})\}\\
\cup\{(\mathbb{R}_{\leq 0}(v_{1},v_{2}),\mathfrak{f}_{\mathbb{R}_{\leq 0}(v_{1},v_{2})})\mid(v_{1},v_{2})\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}^{2},\gcd(v_{1},v_{2})=1\}.

Every element in
\mathfrak{D}(P_{1},P_{2})
, referred to as a
wall
, is a pair
(\mathfrak{d},\mathfrak{f})
where
\mathfrak{d}
is either a line or a ray in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
\mathfrak{f}
(the
wall-function
), when
\mathfrak{f}=\mathfrak{f}_{\mathbb{R}_{\leq 0}(v_{1},v_{2})}
, is a power series in
\Bbbk\llbracket x_{1}^{v_{1}}x_{2}^{v_{2}}\rrbracket
with constant term
1
. We refer the reader to
[
13
, Section 1.3]
for the algebraic details of the
consistency
of scattering diagrams and to
[
4
, Section 4.1]
for a presentation closer to the current setup.

The primary focus of this paper is the case where

P_{1}(x_{1})=1+x_{1}^{b}\quad\text{and}\quad P_{2}(x_{2})=1+x_{2}^{c}

for positive integers
b
and
c
. We denote this scattering diagram by
\mathfrak{D}_{(b,c)}
. They are known as the rank-2 special class of the
cluster scattering diagrams
of Gross–Hacking–Keel–Kontsevich
[
11
]
, and are closely tied with Fomin–Zelevinsky’s cluster algebras
\mathcal{A}(b,c)
in rank 2
[
27
]
.

For
\mathfrak{D}_{(b,c)}
, it is also known that each
\mathfrak{f}_{\mathbb{R}_{\leq 0}(v_{1},v_{2})}
lives in
\Bbbk\llbracket x_{1}^{db}x_{2}^{ec}\rrbracket
for the unique pair

(d,e)\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}^{2}\quad\text{such that}\quad\gcd(d,e)=1\quad\text{and}\quad v_{1}ec=v_{2}db.

Therefore we can write

(2.2)

\mathfrak{f}_{\mathbb{R}_{\leq 0}(v_{1},v_{2})}=1+\sum_{k\geq 1}\tau^{(b,c)}(kd,ke)x_{1}^{kdb}x_{2}^{kec},

defining the coefficients
\tau(kd,ke)=\tau^{(b,c)}(kd,ke)
, viewed as functions of
b
and
c
.

Example 2.3
.

Let
b=2
and
c=3
.

If
(v_{1},v_{2})=(1,1),

then
(d,e)=(3,2)
and

\mathfrak{f}_{\mathbb{R}_{\leq 0}(1,1)}=1+\tau(3,2)x_{1}^{6}x_{2}^{6}+\tau(6,4)x_{1}^{12}x_{2}^{12}+\cdots.

On the other hand, if
(v_{1},v_{2})=(2,3),
then
(d,e)=(1,1)
, and

\mathfrak{f}_{\mathbb{R}_{\leq 0}(2,3)}=1+\tau(1,1)x_{1}^{2}x_{2}^{3}+\tau(2,2)x_{1}^{4}x_{2}^{6}+\cdots.

As another example, if
(v_{1},v_{2})=(3,5),

then
(d,e)=(9,10)
, and

\mathfrak{f}_{\mathbb{R}_{\leq 0}(3,5)}=1+\tau(9,10)x_{1}^{18}x_{2}^{30}+\tau(18,20)x_{1}^{36}x_{2}^{60}+\cdots.

In all three of these cases, note that both
(v_{1},v_{2})
and
(d,e)
are primitive such that

.

A closely related scattering diagram is

\mathfrak{D}^{\circ}_{(c,b)}\coloneqq\mathfrak{D}((1+x_{1})^{c},(1+x_{2})^{b}).

(Notice the swap between
b
and
c
.) For positive

and

with
\gcd(d,e)=1

by

defining the coefficients

As pointed out in

4.
Tight gradings

Tight gradings were introduced in
[
4
]
to give a combinatorial interpretation of the wall-function coefficients in generalized cluster scattering diagrams. In this section, we recall the definition of tight gradings and establish some of their structural properties.

4.1.
Maximal Dyck paths

Fix
d_{1},d_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
. Consider a rectangle with vertices
(0,0)
,
(0,d_{2})
,
(d_{1},0)
, and
(d_{1},d_{2})
with a main diagonal from
(0,0)
to
(d_{1},d_{2})
.

We first require various notions of paths.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
be nonnegative integers.

•

A
NE path
is a lattice path in
(\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{R})\cup(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{Z})\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
starting at
(0,0)
and ending at
(d_{1},d_{2})
, proceeding by only unit north and east steps.

•

A
Dyck path
is a NE path that never passes strictly above the main diagonal from
(0,0)
to
(d_{1},d_{2})
.

•

The
maximal Dyck path

\mathcal{P}(d_{1},d_{2})
is the Dyck path proceeding from
(0,0)
to
(d_{1},d_{2})
that is closest to the main diagonal.

Given a subset
C
of steps (also known as
edges
) in a NE path
\mathcal{P}
, we denote the set of east (resp. north) steps by
C_{\mathbf{E}}
(resp.
C_{\mathbf{N}}
) and the total number of steps by
|C|
. For edges
e_{1}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{E}}
and
e_{2}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{N}}
, let
\overrightarrow{e_{1}e_{2}}
denote the subpath proceeding east from the left endpoint of
e_{1}
to the top endpoint of
e_{2}
, continuing cyclically around
\mathcal{P}
if
e_{1}
is east of
e_{2}
.

The Dyck paths from
(0,0)
to
(d_{1},d_{2})
form a partially ordered set by comparing the heights at all vertices. The
maximal Dyck path

\mathcal{P}(d_{1},d_{2})
is the maximal element under this partial order. We label the horizontal edges in
\mathcal{P}(d_{1},d_{2})
from left to right by
u_{1},u_{2},\dots,u_{d_{1}}
and the vertical edges from bottom to top by
v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{d_{2}}
.

Definition 4.2
.

We define the function
\pi:\mathcal{P}(d_{1},d_{2})\to[0,d_{1}+d_{2})
by
\rd(e)\coloneqq d_{2}x-d_{1}y
for each edge
e
, where
(x,y)
is the location of the south or west vertex of
e
.

When
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
are coprime, the maximal Dyck path
\mathcal{P}(d_{1},d_{2})
corresponds to the lower Christoffel word of slope
d_{2}/d_{1}
; see
[
3
, Sec. 1.2]
for further details. The following properties follow from this identification.

Remark 4.3
.

The quantity
\rd(e)
is the relative distance of the left (resp. bottom) vertex to the main diagonal among all vertices of
\mathcal{P}(d_{1},d_{2})
. In particular, we have
\rd(e)=\rd(e^{\prime})
if their distances to the diagonal are equal, and we have
\rd(e)=0
if the corresponding vertex is on the main diagonal. Moreover, as in
[
3
, Lemma 1.3]
, if
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
are coprime, then
\rd(e)
is distinct for all edges of
\mathcal{P}(d_{1},d_{2})
.

More specifically, for each edge
\alpha
of the maximal Dyck path
\mathcal{P}(d_{1},d_{2})
, with lattice point
(x,y)
as its western or southern vertex, the value

\pi(\alpha)/d_{1}=\frac{d_{2}x-d_{1}y}{d_{1}}=\frac{d_{2}}{d_{1}}x-y\geq 0
since
(x,y)
lies on or below the line of slope
\frac{d_{2}}{d_{1}}
. The fractional value is the exact distance to the diagonal. If we label the edges of
\mathcal{P}(d_{1},d_{2})
in order from
(0,0)
to
(d_{1},d_{2})
as
\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{d_{1}+d_{2}-1}
, this allows us to interpret
\pi(\alpha_{k})
as the modular residue of
k\cdot d_{2}\mod(d_{1}+d_{2})
.

Example 4.4
.

Consider the maximal Dyck path
\mathcal{P}(5,3)
that goes through the sequence of lattice points
(0,0),(1,0),(2,0),(2,1),(3,1),(4,1),(4,2),(5,2),(5,3)
using the edges given by
u_{1},u_{2},v_{1},u_{3},u_{4},v_{2},u_{5},v_{3}
. Then we get all distinct integer values in
[0,d_{1}+d_{2})
, for
d_{1}=5
,
d_{2}=3
, as expected:

\pi(\alpha_{0})=\pi(u_{1})=3\cdot 0-5\cdot 0=0

\pi(\alpha_{4})=\pi(u_{4})=3\cdot 3-5\cdot 1=4

\pi(\alpha_{1})=\pi(u_{2})=3\cdot 1-5\cdot 0=3

\pi(\alpha_{5})=\pi(v_{2})=3\cdot 4-5\cdot 1=7

\pi(\alpha_{2})=\pi(v_{1})=3\cdot 2-5\cdot 0=6

\pi(\alpha_{6})=\pi(u_{5})=3\cdot 4-5\cdot 2=2

\pi(\alpha_{3})=\pi(u_{3})=3\cdot 2-5\cdot 1=1

\pi(\alpha_{7})=\pi(v_{3})=3\cdot 5-5\cdot 2=5

4.2.
Definition of tight gradings

Motivated by Lee–Schiffler
[
18
]
,
Lee, Li, and Zelevinsky
[
17
]
introduced combinatorial objects called
compatible pairs
to construct the
greedy basis
for rank-
2
cluster algebras, consisting of indecomposable positive elements including the cluster monomials. Rupel
[
24
,
25
]
extended this construction to the setting of
generalized
rank-
2
cluster algebras by defining
compatible gradings
.

A function from the set of edges on
\mathcal{P}(d_{1},d_{2})
to
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
is called a
grading
. Given a grading
\omega:\mathcal{P}(d_{1},d_{2})\to\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
and a set of edges
C\subset\mathcal{P}(d_{1},d_{2})
, we set
\omega(C)\coloneqq\sum_{e\in C}\omega(e)
.

Definition 4.5
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be an NE path. A grading
\omega:\mathcal{P}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
is called
compatible
if for every pair of
u\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{E}}
and
v\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{N}}
with
\omega(u)\omega(v)>0
, there exists an edge
e
along the subpath
\overrightarrow{uv}
so that at least one of the following holds:

(4.6)

\displaystyle e\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{N}}\setminus\{v\}\quad\text{and}\quad|\overrightarrow{ue}_{\mathbf{N}}|=\omega\left(\overrightarrow{ue}_{\mathbf{E}}\right);

\displaystyle e\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{E}}\setminus\{u\}\quad\text{and}\quad|\overrightarrow{ev}_{\mathbf{E}}|=\omega\left(\overrightarrow{ev}_{\mathbf{N}}\right).

We denote the set of compatible gradings on
\mathcal{P}(d_{1},d_{2})
with
\omega(\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{N}})=p
,
\omega(\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{E}})=q
, by
\CG(d_{1},d_{2},p,q)
.
In the compatible pairs setting of
[
17
]
,
\omega
takes at most one distinct nonzero value
c
on the horizontal edges and at most one distinct nonzero value
b
on the vertical edges. This is the setting relevant for the cluster algebra
\mathcal{A}(b,c)
and the cluster scattering diagram
\mathfrak{D}_{(b,c)}
.
Thus we define
\mathrm{CG}_{\mathit{b},\mathit{c}}(d_{1},d_{2},i,j)
to be the subset of gradings
\omega
in
\CG(d_{1},d_{2},bi,cj)
such that
\omega
is either
b
(resp.
c
) or
0
on each edge of
\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{N}}
(resp.
\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{E}}
).

In their study of compatible pairs, Lee, Li, and Zelevinsky
[
17
]
introduced the notion of the “shadow” of a set of horizontal (or vertical) edges, which Rupel
[
25
]
extended to the setting of gradings.

Definition 4.7
.

Fix an NE path
\mathcal{P}
and a grading
\omega\colon\mathcal{P}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
.
For each edge
e
in
\mathcal{P}
, we define its
shadow
, denoted by
\sh(e)
or
\sh(e;\omega)
, as follows.

•

If
e
is horizontal, then its shadow is
\overrightarrow{ev}_{\mathbf{N}}
, where
v\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{N}}
is chosen such that
|\overrightarrow{ev}_{\mathbf{N}}|=\omega(\overrightarrow{ev}_{\mathbf{E}})
and
\overrightarrow{ev}
has minimal length under this condition. If no such
v
exists, let
\sh(e)=\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{N}}
.

•

If
e
is vertical, then its shadow is
\overrightarrow{ue}_{\mathbf{E}}
, where
u\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{E}}
is chosen such that
|\overrightarrow{ue}_{\mathbf{E}}|=\omega(\overrightarrow{ue}_{\mathbf{N}})
and
\overrightarrow{ue}
has minimal length under this condition. If no such
u
exists, let
\sh(e)=\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{E}}
.

For
C\subset\mathcal{P}
, let the
shadow
of
C
be
\sh(C)=\bigcup_{e\in C}\sh(e)
.

Let
S_{\mathbf{E}}=S_{\mathbf{E}}(\omega)
denote the set of horizontal edges
u\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{E}}
such that
\omega(u)>0
. Similarly, we define
S_{\mathbf{N}}
to be the set of vertical edges with nonzero value in the grading.

Definition 4.8

(tight grading)
.

A compatible grading
\omega
in
\CG(d_{1},d_{2},p,q)
is called

tight
if

•

S_{\mathbf{E}}\subseteq\sh(S_{\mathbf{N}})
or
S_{\mathbf{N}}\subseteq\sh(S_{\mathbf{E}})
and

•

pd_{2}-qd_{1}=\epsilon\gcd(p,q)
for
\epsilon=1
or
-1
.

Remark 4.9
.

The two bulleted conditions in
4.8
each have two possibilities, but the choice of one determines the other. In particular, given a tight grading, we have
S_{\mathbf{E}}\subseteq\sh(S_{\mathbf{N}})
if and only if
\epsilon=1
, and otherwise we have
S_{\mathbf{N}}\subseteq\sh(S_{\mathbf{E}})
when
\epsilon=-1
(see, for example,
[
5
, Lemma 5.23]
).

We now explain how tight gradings are related to wall-functions. Returning to the scattering diagram
\mathfrak{D}(P_{1},P_{2})
at the beginning of
Section

2
where

P_{i}=1+\sum_{k\geq 1}p_{i,k}x_{i}^{k}\in\Bbbk\llbracket x_{i}\rrbracket.

A main result of
[
5
]
is the following integrality and positivity statement for
\mathfrak{D}(P_{1},P_{2})
: any wall-function coefficient behaves as a polynomial in
\{p_{i,k}\mid i\in\{1,2\},k\geq 1\}
with nonnegative integer coefficients given by counts of tight gradings.

Theorem 4.10

(
[
5
, Theorem 5.17 and (5.35)]
)
.

(1)

There are nonnegative integers
\lambda(\mathbf{P}_{1},\mathbf{P}_{2})
for pairs of partitions
(\mathbf{P}_{1},\mathbf{P}_{2})
such that for any positive integers
d
and
e
with
\gcd(d,e)=1
, the wall-function on
\mathbb{R}_{\leq 0}(d,e)
is given by

\mathfrak{f}_{\mathbb{R}_{\leq 0}(d,e)}=1+\sum_{k\geq 1}\sum_{\mathbf{P}_{1}\vdash kd}\sum_{\mathbf{P}_{2}\vdash ke}\lambda(\mathbf{P}_{1},\mathbf{P}_{2})\prod_{i\in\mathbf{P}_{1}}p_{1,i}\prod_{j\in\mathbf{P}_{2}}p_{2,j}\cdot x_{1}^{kd}x_{2}^{ke}.

(2)

For partitions
\mathbf{P}_{1}\vdash p
and
\mathbf{P}_{2}\vdash q
, the number
\lambda(\mathbf{P}_{1},\mathbf{P}_{2})
counts tight gradings
\omega
on
\mathcal{P}(d_{1},d_{2})
with
pd_{2}-qd_{1}=\pm\gcd(p,q)
,
d_{1}\geq p
,
d_{2}\geq q
such that

\mathbf{P}_{1}=(\omega(v)\mid v\in S_{\mathbf{N}}(\omega))\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{P}_{2}=(\omega(u)\mid u\in S_{\mathbf{E}}(\omega)),

independent of the choice of
(d_{1},d_{2})
. Here, we reorder the non-zero elements of the tuples
(\omega(v)\mid v\in S_{\mathbf{N}}(\omega))
and
(\omega(u)\mid u\in S_{\mathbf{E}}(\omega))
in weakly decreasing order so that they are integer partitions.

Remark 4.11
.

In the cluster setting,

states that

\tau^{b,c}(i,j)=\left|\left\{\omega\in\mathrm{CG}_{\mathit{b},\mathit{c}}(d_{1},d_{2},i,j)\mid\omega\text{ is a tight grading}\right\}\right|\,,

whenever
d_{1}\geq bi
,
d_{2}\geq cj
, and
|bid_{2}-cjd_{1}|=\gcd(bi,cj)
.

4.3.
Structure of Tight Gradings

We now prove several structural results about tight gradings, which are needed for the later combinatorial constructions.

We begin by establishing that tight gradings have only one “component”, in the sense that the horizontal and vertical shadows have no gaps.

Lemma 4.12
.

Let
\omega
be a tight grading on
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}(d_{1},d_{2})
with
\omega(\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{N}})=p
and
\omega(\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{E}})=q
. Then there exists a unique
u_{\max}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{E}}
and
v_{\max}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{N}}
such that

•

\overrightarrow{u_{\max}v_{\max}}=\sh(S_{\mathbf{E}})\cup\sh(S_{\mathbf{N}})\cup\{v_{\max}\}
if
pd_{2}-qd_{1}>0
, or

•

\overrightarrow{u_{\max}v_{\max}}=\{u_{\max}\}\cup\sh(S_{\mathbf{E}})\cup\sh(S_{\mathbf{N}})
if
pd_{2}-qd_{1}<0
.

Proof.

Let
\gamma\coloneqq\gcd(p,q)=|pd_{2}-qd_{1}|
. Without loss of generality, assume
pd_{2}-qd_{1}<0
. The case where
pd_{2}-qd_{1}>0
is analogous as we can swap the roles of horizontal and vertical edges. Pick
u^{(i)}\in S_{\mathbf{E}}
for
i=1,2,\dots,\ell
such that
\sh(S_{\mathbf{E}})
is the disjoint union of the shadows
\sh(u^{(i)})
. Note that this is possible since edge shadows are either nested or disjoint. For each
i
, let
v^{(i)}
be the unique vertical edge such that
\displaystyle\overrightarrow{u^{(i)}v^{(i)}}_{\mathbf{N}}=\sh(u^{(i)})
. Let
\pi_{i}\coloneqq\pi\left(u^{(i)}\right)
,
q_{i}\coloneqq\left|\overrightarrow{u^{(i)}v^{(i)}}_{\mathbf{N}}\right|
, and
p_{i}\coloneqq\left|\overrightarrow{u^{(i)}v^{(i)}}_{\mathbf{E}}\right|-1
.

By the definition of compatibility for the grading
\omega
, we have
q_{i}d_{1}>p_{i}d_{2}+\pi_{i}\geq p_{i}d_{2}
. Moreover, since
\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}q_{i}=q
and
qd_{1}-pd_{2}=\gcd(p,q)
, we can choose a partition
(\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{\ell})\vdash\gamma
such that
p_{i}=\frac{\eta_{i}}{\gamma}p
and
q_{i}=\frac{\eta_{i}}{\gamma}q
. In particular, this implies
\pi_{i}<q_{i}d_{1}-p_{i}d_{2}=\eta_{i}
.

We now aim to show that
u_{1}\in\overrightarrow{u^{(i)}v^{(i)}}
for all
i
. Consider the horizontal edge
e
that is
\pi_{i}\frac{p}{\gamma}
steps to the right of
u^{(i)}
(continuing cyclically around
\mathcal{P}
if necessary). We then have

\pi(e)=\pi_{i}+\frac{\pi_{i}p}{\gamma}d_{2}-\frac{\pi_{i}q}{\gamma}d_{1}=\pi_{i}+\pi_{i}\cdot\frac{pd_{2}-qd_{1}}{\gamma}=\pi_{i}-\pi_{i}=0\,.

So indeed, since
\pi_{i}\frac{p}{\gamma}<p_{i}
and
u_{1}
is the unique edge with
\pi(e)=0
, the edge
u_{1}
is included in the path
\overrightarrow{u^{(i)}v^{(i)}}
.

As we assumed the paths
\overrightarrow{u^{(i)}v^{(i)}}
were disjoint, there can be only one such path, so
\ell=1
. We can then let
u_{\max}=u^{(1)}
. We can also see that
|\sh(u_{\max})|=p+1
, so we can let
v_{\max}=v^{(1)}
.
∎

Next, we show that the tight gradings can only be located at certain positions along the Dyck path; this result is developed pictorially in

5
.

Lemma 4.13
.

Let
\omega
be a tight grading on
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}(d_{1},d_{2})
with
\omega(\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{N}})=p
and
\omega(\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{E}})=q
, and set
\gamma=\gcd(p,q)
. Let
u_{\max}
and

•

If

•

Proof.

By definition, since
\omega
is a tight grading, either

or

holds. Assume

, so by

, we are in the case where
pd_{2}-qd_{1}>0
.

The subpath
\mathfrak{s}=\overrightarrow{u_{\max}v_{\max}}
contains all edges in
\sh(u_{\max})
, and its set of horizontal edges is precisely
\sh(v_{\max})
. Moreover, note that
v_{\max}\notin\sh(S_{\mathbf{E}})
. Therefore
\mathfrak{s}
contains exactly
p
horizontal edges and contains at least
q+1
vertical edges. Hence we have

(4.14)

\pi(v_{\max})-\pi(u_{\max})=|\mathfrak{s}_{\mathbf{E}}|d_{2}-(|\mathfrak{s}_{\mathbf{N}}|-1)d_{1}=pd_{2}-(|\mathfrak{s}_{\mathbf{N}}|-1)d_{1}\leq pd_{2}-qd_{1}=\gamma\,.

As the edge
v_{\max}
is vertical, we have
\pi(v_{\max})\geq d_{1}
. So

\pi(u_{\max})\geq d_{1}-\gamma
. Moreover, since
\pi(v_{\max})>\pi(u_{\max})
, we have

pd_{2}-(|\mathfrak{s}_{\mathbf{N}}|-1)d_{1}=\gamma-(|\mathfrak{s}_{\mathbf{N}}|-(q+1))d_{1}>0\,.

Then
\gamma\leq d_{1}
implies that
|\mathfrak{s}_{\mathbf{N}}|=q+1
. So the inequality in
Equation

4.14
is actually an equality, hence
\pi(u_{\max})=\pi(v_{\max})-\gamma
.

Since
\pi(u_{\max})\geq d_{1}-\gamma\geq d_{1}-d_{2}
, the edge
e
immediately to the right of
u_{\max}
must be vertical, as
\pi(e)\geq d_{1}
. Hence
v_{\min}
is immediately to the right of
u_{\max}
. In order for
\omega
to be compatible, we must have
\omega(v_{\min})=0

\omega(u_{\max})>0
.

The case

proceeds similarly, where we still consider the subpath
\mathfrak{s}=\overrightarrow{u_{\max}v_{\max}}
. Now
\mathfrak{s}
contains exactly
q
vertical edges and at least

horizontal edges, so we have

(4.15)

\pi(v_{\max})-\pi(u_{\max})=|\mathfrak{s}_{\mathbf{E}}|d_{2}-(q-1)d_{1}\geq(p+1)d_{2}-(q-1)d_{1}=d_{1}+d_{2}-\gamma\,.

Since
\pi(v_{\max})<d_{1}+d_{2}
, we have
\pi(u_{\max})<\gamma
. As before,
Equation

4.15
also implies that
|\mathfrak{s}_{\mathbf{E}}|=p+1
, so
\pi(v_{\max})=\pi(u_{\max})+d_{1}+d_{2}-\gamma

e
immediately preceding
v_{\max}
has
\pi(e)=d_{1}-\gamma<d_{1}
, so

. We then have
\omega(u_{\min})=0
since
\omega(v_{\max})>0

Corollary 4.16
.

If
\mathbf{P}_{1}=(p)
and
\mathbf{P}_{2}=(q)

7.
Weyl group symmetry

In this section, using tight gradings, we give a bijective proof of the Weyl group symmetry for cluster scattering diagrams, namely

(7.1)

\tau^{b,c}(i,j)=\tau^{b,c}(i,bi-j)\text{ and }\tau^{b,c}(i,j)=\tau^{b,c}(cj-i,j)\,.

This resolves
1.1
posed by Elgin, Reading, and Stella
[
7
]
.

7.1.
Mutation and Retraction

One of the main tools we use is the Lee–Li–Zelevinsky mutation map
[
17
]
. Combining this mutation map with a new “retraction” map yields a bijection between collections of tight gradings that exhibit the Weyl group symmetries.

We start by recalling some of the notions from
[
17
]
.

The
remote shadow of
S_{\mathbf{N}}
, denoted by
\rsh(S_{\mathbf{N}})
, is the subset of
\sh(S_{\mathbf{N}})
obtained by removing all horizontal edges of height

whenever

. We say that

is

by

if

and

is minimal with respect to this property. Note that this is well defined because any pair of shadows are either disjoint or nested. For

and
0<s\leq d_{2}
, let
\rsh(S_{\mathbf{N}})_{h;s}
denote the set of edges in
\rsh(S_{\mathbf{N}})
of height

that are directly shadowed by

. For

let
\rsh(S_{\mathbf{N}})_{h;s;r}
denote the

element of
\rsh(S_{\mathbf{N}})_{h;s}
, ordered left-to-right.

Since a grading
\omega\in\mathrm{CG}_{\mathit{b},\mathit{c}}(d_{1},d_{2},i,j)
is completely determined by the edge sets
S_{\mathbf{E}}
and
S_{\mathbf{N}}
where

is nonzero, from now on we identify such
\omega
with the pair

. This is precisely the setting of

in
[
17
]
; see also the discussion below

.

Definition 7.2

(
[

)
.

The

\theta:\mathrm{CG}_{\mathit{b},\mathit{c}}(d_{1},d_{2},i,j)\to\mathrm{CG}_{\mathit{b},\mathit{c}}(bd_{2}-d_{1},d_{2})

is defined for compatible gradings follows.
Fix

The fact that the image of

Remark 7.3
.

Our definition of the Lee–Li–Zelevinsky map
\theta
differs from their original formulation in
[
17
]
. We apply
\theta
to compatible gradings, rather than sets of vertical edges. It is straightforward to show that the two definitions are equivalent up to specifying a choice of horizontal edges. Note that we use the notation

in place of their parameters

,

In the definition of tight gradings, we have the condition that

\displaystyle\mathrm{CG}_{\mathit{b},\mathit{c}}^{\top}(d_{1},d_{2},i,j)

\displaystyle\mathrm{CG}_{\mathit{b},\mathit{c}}^{\vdash}(d_{1},d_{2},i,j)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{\omega\in\mathrm{CG}_{\mathit{b},\mathit{c}}(d_{1},d_{2},i,j):S_{\mathbf{N}}\subseteq\sh(S_{\mathbf{E}})\}\,.

Proposition 7.4
.

from

Proof.

When

Definition 7.5
.

The retraction map

\varrho:\mathrm{CG}_{\mathit{b},\mathit{c}}(d_{1},d_{2},i,j)\to\coprod_{0\leq k\leq i}\mathrm{CG}_{\mathit{b},\mathit{c}}^{\vdash}(d_{1},d_{2},k,j)

.

8.
Counting tight gradings

Recall from the statement of Theorem
4.10
(2) that for partitions
\mathbf{P}_{1}\vdash p
and
\mathbf{P}_{2}\vdash q
,
\lambda(\mathbf{P}_{1},\mathbf{P}_{2})
equals the number of tight gradings
\omega
on
\mathcal{P}(d_{1},d_{2})
with

\mathbf{P}_{1}=(\omega(v)\mid v\in S_{\mathbf{N}}(\omega))
and
\mathbf{P}_{2}=(\omega(u)\mid u\in S_{\mathbf{E}}(\omega)),

as long as
d_{1}\geq p
and
d_{2}\geq q
are chosen so that
|pd_{2}-qd_{1}|=\gcd(p,q)
.
Nonetheless, determining
\lambda(\mathbf{P}_{1},\mathbf{P}_{2})
by counting tight gradings in general seems to be a very hard combinatorial problem. We first give a closed formula for
\lambda(\mathbf{P}_{1},\mathbf{P}_{2})
in the case where one of the partitions is of length one, which generalizes
4.16
. Then we derive a total count of tight gradings with
|\mathbf{P}_{1}|=|\mathbf{P}_{2}|=k
. Both proofs make use, in a sense, of wall-crossing formulas, namely, the additional information that the counts are encoded in consistent scattering diagrams. We conclude this section with a combinatorial argument in a special case.

For a partition
\mathbf{P}
, write
\mu(\mathbf{P})
for its tuple of multiplicities and denote

\binom{z}{\mu(\mathbf{P})}=\dfrac{z(z-1)\cdots(z-\ell(\mathbf{P})+1)}{\prod\limits_{m\in\mu(\mathbf{P})}m!}.

Proposition 8.1
.

For any
\mathbf{P}\vdash j
, we have

(8.2)

\lambda((i),\mathbf{P})=\frac{\gcd(i,j)}{i}\binom{i}{\mu(\mathbf{P})}.

Proof.

Consider the scattering diagrams
\mathfrak{D}(1+x^{i},P_{2})
and
\mathfrak{D}(1+x,P_{2}^{i})
, where
P_{2}=1+\sum_{k\geq 1}p_{2,k}y^{k}
. Note that we have the expansion

P_{2}^{i}=1+\sum_{k\geq 1}\sum_{\mathbf{Q}\vdash k}\binom{i}{\mu(\mathbf{Q})}p_{2}^{\mathbf{Q}}y^{k},

where
p_{2}^{\mathbf{Q}}
denotes the monomial
\prod_{k\in\mathbf{Q}}p_{2,k}
.

The two scattering diagrams are related by a change-of-lattice transformation (slightly more general than
2.5
but can be proven similarly): the wall-function
\mathfrak{f}(x^{i}y^{j})
on
\mathbb{R}_{\leq 0}(i,j)
of
\mathfrak{D}(1+x^{i},P_{2})
and the wall-function
\mathfrak{f}^{\prime}(xy^{j})
on
\mathbb{R}_{\leq 0}(1,j)
of
\mathfrak{D}(1+x,P_{2}^{i})
are related by

\mathfrak{f}(xy^{j})=\mathfrak{f}^{\prime}(xy^{j})^{\frac{\gcd(i,j)}{i}}.

Comparing the
xy^{j}
-coefficients on both sides yields

\sum_{\mathbf{P}\vdash j}\lambda((i),\mathbf{P})p_{2}^{\mathbf{P}}=\frac{\gcd(i,j)}{i}\sum_{\mathbf{Q}\vdash j}\lambda((1),\mathbf{Q})\prod_{p\in\mathbf{Q}}\sum_{\mathbf{P}\vdash p}\binom{i}{\mu(\mathbf{P})}p_{2}^{\mathbf{P}}.

However, for any partition
\mathbf{Q}
with
\ell(\mathbf{Q})>1
,
\lambda((1),\mathbf{Q})=0
by its tight grading interpretation. As a special case of
4.16
, we have
\lambda((1),(j))=1
. Then the above equality reduces to

\sum_{\mathbf{P}\vdash j}\lambda((i),\mathbf{P})p_{2}^{\mathbf{P}}=\frac{\gcd(i,j)}{i}\sum_{\mathbf{P}\vdash j}\binom{i}{\mu(\mathbf{P})}p_{2}^{\mathbf{P}}.

The desired formula follows from comparing the
p_{2}^{\mathbf{P}}
-coefficients of each
\mathbf{P}
on both sides.
∎

For example, if
\ell(\mathbf{P})>i
, then
\lambda((i),\mathbf{P})
vanishes; if
\ell(\mathbf{P})=1
, then
\lambda((i),(j))=\gcd(i,j)
. It is yet unclear how to show the formula (
8.2
) purely by tight grading combinatorics. One may allude to the analysis in the proof of
4.16
, but notice that in general
\gcd(i,j)
does not necessarily divide
\lambda((i),\mathbf{P})
. See the end of this section for an example of tight grading combinatorics when
\ell(\mathbf{P})=i
.

Further, we can derive one total count of tight gradings using the quiver methods of Section
3
. We consider tight gradings of total weights
(k,k)
on the maximal Dyck path
\mathcal{P}(k+1,k)
for
k\geq 1
, which proceeds from
(0,0)
to
(k+1,k)
in steps

E(EN)^{k}=u_{1}u_{2}v_{1}u_{3}v_{2}\ldots u_{k+1}v_{k},

where
E
(resp.
N
) denotes an east (resp. north) step.
Our aim is to prove that they are counted by the series
[
20
, A002293]
:

Proposition 8.3
.

The number of tight gradings of total weights
(k,k)
on
\mathcal{P}(k+1,k)
equals

\sum_{\mathbf{P}_{1}\vdash k}\sum_{\mathbf{P}_{2}\vdash k}\lambda(\mathbf{P}_{1},\mathbf{P}_{2})=\frac{1}{4k+1}\binom{4k+1}{k}=\frac{1}{3k+1}\binom{4k}{k}.

Proof.

Let
\ell_{1}
,
\ell_{2}
be arbitrary positive integers. We consider the tropical vertex as in
[
4
, Section 3]
, with variables
p_{i,j}
specialized to

p_{i,j}=\binom{\ell_{i}}{j},\;i=1,2,\,j\geq 1.

Then
[
4
, Theorem 3.5]
(see also
4.10
) reads as follows:

f_{(1,1)}=1+\sum_{k\geq 1}\sum_{\omega}{\rm wt}(\omega)(xy)^{k},

where the inner sum ranges over all tight gradings
\omega
on
\mathcal{P}(k+1,k)
as above, and the weight is defined as

{\rm wt}(\omega)=\prod_{i=1}^{k+1}\binom{\ell_{1}}{\omega(u_{i})}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\binom{\ell_{2}}{\omega(v_{j})}.

On the other hand, we consider again the refined GW/Kronecker correspondence
[
23
]
. Namely, vanishing results for Euler characteristic of quiver moduli yield by
[
23
, Section 11]
:

f_{(1,1)}=H(xy)^{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}},

where the series
H(z)
is determined by the functional equation

H(z)=(1-zH(z)^{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}-\ell_{1}-\ell_{2}})^{-1}.

Applying the Lagrange inversion formula (similarly to
[
23
, Corollary 11.2]
) we find

f_{(1,1)}=1+\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}}{(\ell_{1}-1)(\ell_{2}-1)k+\ell_{1}\ell_{2}}\binom{(\ell_{1}-1)(\ell_{2}-1)k+\ell_{1}\ell_{2}}{k}(xy)^{k}.

Comparing coefficients, we find

\sum_{\omega}\prod_{i=1}^{k+1}\binom{\ell_{1}}{\omega(u_{i})}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\binom{\ell_{2}}{\omega(v_{j})}=\frac{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}}{(\ell_{1}-1)(\ell_{2}-1)k+\ell_{1}\ell_{2}}\binom{{(\ell_{1}-1)(\ell_{2}-1)k+\ell_{1}\ell_{2}}}{k}.

We note that both sides behave polynomially in
\ell_{1}
,
\ell_{2}
. Since the identity holds for all nonnegative
\ell_{1},\ell_{2}
, it holds as an identity of (degree
k
) polynomials in
\ell_{1},\ell_{2}
, considered as variables. We can therefore specialize
\ell_{1},\ell_{2}
to
-1
. Using

\binom{-1}{k}=(-1)^{k},

we find

\sum_{\omega}(-1)^{k}(-1)^{k}=\frac{1}{4k+1}\binom{4k+1}{k}.

But the left-hand side is just the number of all tight gradings on
\mathcal{P}(k+1,k)
, finishing the proof.∎

Finally, to give a better sense concerning the combinatorics of tight gradings, we close this section with a second proof of Proposition
8.1
restricted to the special case that
i=j
.

Combinatorial Proof of Proposition
8.1
when
i=j
.

When

\mathbf{P}\vdash i
, we have
\lambda((i),\mathbf{P})=\binom{i}{\mu(\mathbf{P})}
, which can be rewritten as

\binom{i}{\mu(\mathbf{P})}=\frac{i(i-1)\cdots(i-\ell(\mathbf{P})+1)}{\prod_{m\in\mu(\mathbf{P})}m!}=\frac{i!}{\prod_{m\in\mu(\overline{\mathbf{P}})}m!}

where
\overline{\mathbf{P}}
is the partition
\mathbf{P}
extended to length
i
by appending
(i-\ell)
zeroes. Consequently,
\lambda((i),\mathbf{P})=\binom{i}{\mu(\mathbf{P})}
counts the number of rearrangements of the extended partition
\overline{\mathbf{P}}
of length
i
(including zeroes).

We demonstrate the following bijection between the set of tight gradings on
\mathcal{P}(i+1,i)
with
\mathbf{P}_{1}=(i)
and
\mathbf{P}_{2}\vdash i
and the set of rearrangements of
\overline{\mathbf{P}_{2}}
:

1) The Maximal Dyck path
\mathcal{P}(i+1,i)
has the form
E(EN)^{i}
. Given the composition
(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{i})
which is a rearrangement of
\overline{\mathbf{P}_{2}}
, such that
0\leq a_{j}\leq i
and
a_{1}+a_{2}+\dots+a_{i}=i
, we show there is a unique tight grading with
\omega_{2}(v_{j})=a_{j}
where
v_{j}
is the
j
th
N
step of
\mathcal{P}(i+1,i)
.

2) We claim by use of the Cycle Lemma
[
6
]
, there is a unique choice of
0\leq h\leq i-1
such that we have the following inequalities satisfied by partial sums:

\mathrm{For~}1\leq g\leq h,~~\sum_{m=1-h+i}^{g-h+i}a_{m}\leq(g-1),~~~\mathrm{and~for~}h<g\leq i,~~\left(\sum_{m=1-h+i}^{i}a_{m}+\sum_{m=1}^{g-h}a_{m}\right)\leq g.

To see this, we first extend the composition
(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{i})
to one of length
i+1
by assigning
a_{0}=0
, and then turn
(a_{0},a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{i})
into a lattice path from
(0,0)
to
(i+1,i)
of the form

E(N^{a_{0}})E(N^{a_{1}})E(N^{a_{2}})\cdots E(N^{a_{i}})
, i.e. for
0\leq g\leq i
, alternate between a single
E
step and a sequence of
a_{g}

N
steps. Since
a_{0}+a_{1}+a_{2}+\dots+a_{i}=i
, we reach
(i+1,i)
as desired. The Cycle Lemma states that there is a unique cyclic rotation of the steps of this lattice path that yields a Dyck path. That rotation yielding the Dyck path equivalently yields a rotated tuple

(a_{j},a_{j+1},\dots,a_{i-1},a_{i},a_{0},a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{j-1})
satisfying the system of inequalities
a_{j}\leq 0
,
a_{j}+a_{j+1}\leq 1
,
a_{j}+a_{j+1}+a_{j+2}\leq 2
, and continuing so that each length
g
partial sum is less or equal to
g-1
. Removing the entry for
a_{0}=0
, we obtain the above system of inequalities after letting
j=i+1-h
.

3) The points closest to the diagonal of
\mathcal{P}(i+1,i)
are
p_{0}=(0,0)
,
p_{1}=(i,i-1),p_{2}=(i-1,i-2),\dots,p_{i-1}=(2,1)
. Starting with a composition
(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{i})
as above, we identify the unique choice of
h
(in the range
0\leq h\leq i-1
) such that the above system of inequalities is satisfied. Then we work in the frame starting at point
p_{h}
but for
1\leq j\leq i
, let
\omega_{2}(v_{j})=a_{j}
where
v_{j}
is the
j
th
N
step of
\mathcal{P}(i+1,i)
, so that
v_{1}
is the
N
step after the unique
EE
subsequence of
\mathcal{P}(i+1,i)
. If
\ell(\mathbf{P}_{2})=\rho
, we get
\rho(\rho+1)/2
tiles as part of the horizontal footprint attached to these vertical edges.

By construction of this tight grading, starting the frame from
p_{h}
, this means we have tiles of length
a_{i+1-h}
in the bottom row, length
a_{i+1-h}+a_{i+2-h}
in the row
2
nd from the bottom, and continuing so we have tiles of length
a_{i+1-h}+a_{i+2-h}+\dots+a_{i+j-h}
in the row
j
th from the bottom, and length
a_{1}+a_{2}+\dots+a_{i}=i
on the top row.

By the corresponding system of inequalities for this choice of
h
, we have exactly enough room for the vertical tile of length
i
corresponding to partition
\mathbf{P}_{1}=(i)
on the leftmost horizontal edge of this frame.
∎

9.
Final remarks

9.1.
Combinatorics of tight gradings

In
Section

5
, we described a process for generating a tiling associated to each tight grading. This tiling is only a partial tiling, in that there can be empty spaces in the region between the Dyck path and the frame. It could be interesting to characterize the number of such spaces, and in particular study the case of “tightest gradings” when no such spaces appear. Note that this tiling construction is different from that described in
[
4
]
and
[
5
]
, in that the footprints contain a superset of the tiles in earlier constructions.

As discussed in
7.8
, the composition of mutation and retractions gives a bijection between the tight gradings in
\CG_{3,2}^{\top}(3i+2,2i+1,i,i)
and in
\CG_{3,2}^{\vdash}(3i+1,2i+1,i,i)
. Each set of tight gradings gives a combinatorial interpretation of
\tau^{3,2}(i,i)
. The two sets of tight gradings correspond to a choice of positive or negative angular momentum broken line in the scattering diagram
\mathfrak{D}_{(3,2)}
, as described in
[
5
]
. It would be interesting to construct a general bijection between the sets of tight gradings in
\mathrm{CG}_{\mathit{b},\mathit{c}}^{\vdash}
and
\mathrm{CG}_{\mathit{b},\mathit{c}}^{\top}
that each give a combinatorial interpretation to the same wall-function coefficient.

9.2.
Potential relation with stable trees

There are several situations where Euler characteristics of quiver moduli spaces can be computed by counting so-called stable trees
[
26
]
. In fact, this is one of the main techniques in
[
23
]
for obtaining the formulas which are used in Section
3
. It is thus natural to ask for bijections between sets of stable trees and sets of tight gradings (maybe suitably weighted), but no general pattern emerged yet. One obstacle for such constructions is the fact that the notion of tight gradings is well-adapted to the natural Weyl group symmetry, whereas stable trees are not (the Weyl group symmetry corresponds to applying reflection functors to quiver representations, which does not preserve the class of tree representations naturally).

9.3.
Algebraicity and growth rate

The conjectural wall-function in
Item

C13
has the first few terms

1+2x_{1}^{2}x_{2}^{5}+5x_{1}^{4}x_{2}^{10}+17x_{1}^{6}x_{2}^{15}+64x_{1}^{8}x_{2}^{20}+259x_{1}^{10}x_{2}^{25}+\cdots.

Write the generating function as

S(x)=1+2x+5x^{2}+17x^{3}+\cdots.

Let
A(x)=1-xS(x)
. According to
[
20
, A217596]
, it satisfies the algebraic equation

A(x)^{3}=A(x)^{2}-xA(x)-x^{2}.

Then it follows that the coefficients have asymptotic exponential growth rate
27/5
.

Notice that according to the proof of
3.5
, the wall-function on
\mathbb{R}_{\leq 0}(b,c)
is algebraic over
\mathbb{Q}(x_{1}^{ib}x_{2}^{jc})
. The coefficients have asymptotic growth rate
\frac{m^{m}}{(m-1)^{m-1}}
where
m=(b-1)(c-1)
, assuming
b,c>1
.

It is an interesting question whether every wall-function
\mathfrak{f}_{\mathbb{R}_{\leq 0}(db,ec)}
with of a cluster scattering diagram is algebraic (first asked by Kontsevich
[
12
, Section 1.4]
) and whether the coefficients asymptotically always have an exponential growth rate when
bc>4
and
\mathbb{R}_{\leq 0}(db,ec)
is in the “Badlands”, i.e.,

(9.1)

\frac{e}{d}\in\left(\frac{bc-\sqrt{bc(bc-4)}}{2c},\frac{bc+\sqrt{bc(bc-4)}}{2c}\right).

Based on numerical experiments, we propose the following conjecture.

Conjecture 9.2
.

For any
r
in the open interval (
9.1
), the limit

f(r)\coloneqq\lim_{\frac{j}{i}\to r,\,i\to\infty}\frac{\ln\tau(i,j)}{\sqrt{bcij-bi^{2}-cj^{2}}}

exists and is continuous in
r
.

By taking
r=e/d
and the sequence
(i,j)=(kd,ke)
for
k\geq 1
,
9.2
implies an exponential asymptotic growth of the coefficients along the wall
\mathbb{R}_{\leq 0}(db,ec)
. Using the Weyl group symmetry (
7.1
), one can further show that
f(r)
is bounded provided continuity.

In the case where
b=c
,
9.2
implies a conjecture of Michael Douglas stated in
[
26
, Conjecture 6.1]
and
[
12
, Section 6 (iii)]
, where
\tau(d,e)
equals the Euler characteristic
\chi(M^{s}_{d,e}(b))
defined therein. We remark, however, unlike
[
26
, Conjecture 6.1]
that we do not expect
f(r)
to extend continuously to the endpoints of the interval (
9.1
).
