The power of “thank you”: gratitude, recognition, and employee well-being
Authors: Mohamed Mohiya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Informal expressions of gratitude — particularly the phrase ‘thank you’ — remain underexamined in organizational and HR research despite their widely reported impact on employee well-being. This study investigates how employees in the Saudi energy sector perceive and experience expressions of gratitude in their workplaces. Drawing on 238 naturalistic employee comments extracted from an electronic internal organizational system used across four large multinational energy organizations, and guided by Social Exchange Theory, the study employs thematic analysis to examine the mechanisms through which gratitude expression is associated with employee psychological well-being. The analysis yielded four themes: (1) gratitude as a psychological resource; (2) sincerity and relational authenticity; (3) hierarchical asymmetry in gratitude expression; and (4) cultural and organizational embeddedness. The findings suggest that informal gratitude functions as a symbolic social exchange resource, with perceived sincerity, hierarchical source, and cultural context moderating its psychological impact. The study extends Social Exchange Theory by theorizing the non-material dimensions of recognition. It provides empirical evidence from an underexamined non-Western organizational context and has direct implications for human resource management practice in Vision 2030-aligned organizations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53613-1.

Introduction and research background

This study investigates the perceived impact of expressing gratitude — specifically, the phrase ‘thank you’ — on employee well-being in large multinational energy organizations in Saudi Arabia. Despite growing evidence of the motivational significance of informal appreciation1–3, the relational and psychological mechanisms through which such expressions shape organizational outcomes remain poorly understood, particularly in non-Western, hierarchically structured, and culturally diverse industrial settings. The Saudi energy sector — characterized by multinational workforces, hierarchical authority structures, and the strategic imperatives of Vision 2030 (Saudi Vision 2030, 2016) — represents an organizational context in which these mechanisms operate with particular intensity, yet one that has been almost absent from the gratitude and recognition literature. This study is grounded in Social Exchange Theory4, which conceptualizes informal gratitude as a symbolic, non-material exchange resource whose value derives from its perceived voluntariness, sincerity, and relational specificity.

The problem is particularly salient in high-pressure, hierarchically structured environments such as large energy-sector organizations, where performance expectations are intense, supervisory cultures are often transactional, and informal appreciation may be systematically underutilized. Research consistently shows that perceived lack of appreciation is among the most frequently cited reasons for voluntary turnover5,6, yet the psychological and relational mechanisms through which gratitude expression influences employee well-being have not been adequately examined through qualitative approaches grounded in authentic employee voice—particularly in non-Western organizational contexts.

The Saudi energy sector provides a uniquely compelling setting for such an investigation. Saudi Arabia’s energy industry underpins both national economic output and the strategic ambitions of Vision 2030, a national transformation program that places human capital development, employee engagement, and organizational excellence at the forefront of economic diversification7; Saudi Vision 2030, 2016). Within this sector, multinational energy organizations employ highly diverse workforces drawn from dozens of nationalities, creating an organizational environment in which the expression and interpretation of gratitude intersects with cultural heterogeneity, professional identity, and hierarchical authority. Despite the strategic and social significance of this context, there is a notable absence of empirical research on how informal recognition practices—including expressions of gratitude—shape employee well-being in Saudi energy organizations.

This study addresses that absence through an exploratory qualitative investigation of 238 firsthand, computerized employee comments collected from an internal organizational electronic system used by four large multinational energy organizations in Saudi Arabia. The comments were generated in response to a facilitated organizational discussion on “the power of thank you” and constitute an unusually rich, unprompted, and ecologically valid dataset. Guided by Social Exchange Theory4, the study employs thematic analysis8 to examine how employees perceive the psychological, relational, and organizational significance of gratitude in their workplaces.

This study makes four contributions to the organizational behavior and human resource management literatures. Theoretically, it extends Social Exchange Theory by foregrounding the non-material, symbolic dimensions of social exchange in supervisor-subordinate relationships. Methodologically, it introduces naturalistic organizational documentary comments as an ecologically valid data source for qualitative workplace research. Empirically, it provides contextually rich evidence from an underexamined non-Western organizational context. Practically, it offers actionable implications for human resource management practitioners and senior leaders in Saudi energy organizations operating within the Vision 2030 framework.

Literature review

Conceptualization and theoretical foundation

Conceptualizing gratitude and informal recognition

Gratitude, as a psychological construct, has been defined as “a moral affect that is hypothesized to function as a moral barometer, a moral motivator, and a moral reinforcer”9, p. 250). Within organizational contexts, workplace gratitude encompasses a range of behaviors, including verbal acknowledgment, written appreciation, and physical gestures of recognition, all of which signal to the recipient that their contribution has been noticed and valued. This conceptualization distinguishes workplace gratitude from formal recognition—the latter being institutionally mandated and often financially mediated—and aligns it instead with the domain of discretionary, socially embedded communicative behavior1.

The relationship between gratitude and employee well-being has attracted growing scholarly attention since Emmons and McCullough’s10 seminal work, which demonstrated that gratitude-induction interventions produce measurable improvements in subjective well-being, affect, and even physical health. Subsequent studies in organizational psychology have confirmed that employees who feel appreciated report higher levels of job satisfaction11, greater organizational commitment12, and reduced turnover intention13. However, the majority of this research has been conducted in North American and Western European contexts, using quantitative survey instruments that capture general perceptions of organizational support rather than the immediate, relational experience of gratitude exchange in naturalistic settings.

Recent scholarship has refined the conceptualization of workplace gratitude by distinguishing between state gratitude — a momentary affective response to a specific beneficial act — and dispositional gratitude — a stable individual tendency to notice and appreciate positive contributions3,14. This distinction carries important implications for organizational research. While dispositional gratitude may shape how employees habitually attend to and interpret their work environment, state gratitude is more directly responsive to specific managerial behaviors such as verbal expressions of appreciation. The present study focuses primarily on state gratitude as a relational and organizational phenomenon — specifically, the affective and motivational responses generated in employees by specific expressions of appreciation from supervisors and senior leaders. This focus is consistent with the conceptual framework of informal recognition as a form of discretionary, socially embedded communicative behavior1 and with the broader framing of gratitude as both a psychological resource and a social exchange event.

Theoretical underpinning: social exchange theory

Social Exchange Theory (SET4,15; provides the theoretical foundation for this study. SET holds that social behavior is the product of an exchange process in which actors seek to maximize benefits and minimize costs through relationships governed by norms of reciprocity16. In organizational settings, SET has been applied extensively to understand the dynamics of employee-organization relationships, particularly as these pertain to perceived organizational support (POS17;, leader-member exchange (LMX18;, and psychological contract theory19.

Five interrelated considerations justify selecting SET as the theoretical framework for this study. First, SET provides a robust account of reciprocity as the foundational norm governing workplace relationships, enabling the study to theorize gratitude not as a unilateral gesture but as a constitutive element of an ongoing relational exchange between supervisors and subordinates. Second, SET’s distinction between economic exchange—characterized by calculable, contractual, and monetized forms of return—and social exchange—characterized by diffuse, non-contractual, and symbolically mediated forms of return4—offers a precise theoretical vocabulary for capturing the distinctive value of informal gratitude relative to formal, monetized recognition. Third, SET’s emphasis on the norm of reciprocity16 enables the study to theorize the motivational consequences of gratitude as an obligation-generating act: when a supervisor expresses sincere appreciation, the employee is not only gratified but is also motivated to reciprocate through heightened effort, loyalty, and performance. Fourth, SET has been empirically validated as a predictive framework for understanding the relationship between perceived organizational support—of which informal recognition is a key component—and employee outcomes, including organizational citizenship behavior, job satisfaction, and commitment11,20. Fifth, and most pertinently for this exploratory study, SET is well-suited to the analysis of naturalistic employee discourse because its core concepts—exchange, reciprocity, obligation, and social capital—map naturally onto the language that employees themselves use to describe their experiences of recognition and appreciation, as evidenced in the dataset analyzed here.

Notwithstanding its theoretical utility, SET has been criticized for its rationalistic assumptions about human motivation and for its relative inattention to the affective and cultural dimensions of exchange21. This study addresses these limitations by combining SET with a qualitative, thematically grounded analytical approach that allows affective, cultural, and relational nuances to surface from the data, thereby extending the theory’s application to the non-Western organizational context of the Saudi energy sector.

Locklear et al.23, conducted the most comprehensive integrative review of organizational gratitude research to date, which was published in the Journal of Organizational Behavior. Proposing a unified process model that integrates three previously siloed outcome domains — moral outcomes (prosocial behavior), wellness outcomes (well-being), and relational outcomes (interpersonal relationships) — the review identifies a critical gap: gratitude research in organizational behavior has failed to develop a unified theoretical account crossing all three domains simultaneously. This is directly relevant to the present study, which, by examining gratitude through Social Exchange Theory in a naturalistic organizational context, simultaneously addresses all three domains: employees report well-being effects (wellness), development of relational trust (relational), and a sense of moral obligation to reciprocate (moral). However, Locklear et al.,’s23 review draws almost exclusively on Western, quantitative, laboratory- or survey-based research and explicitly calls for qualitative investigation in real organizational contexts — a gap that the present study directly addresses through its naturalistic documentary dataset. Neither this review nor the studies it surveys examines non-Western, Islamic, or hierarchically structured industrial contexts, further underscoring the contextual contribution of the present study.

Zhao et al.22,, in a three-wave longitudinal study published in the Journal of Career Assessment, examined how dispositional gratitude shapes employee well-being and organizational commitment, with leader-member exchange (LMX) and coworker exchange (CWX) mediating the effects among 300 employees. Their finding that gratitude-linked well-being effects are mediated by LMX quality provides quantitative support for the hierarchical exchange dynamics central to Theme 3 of the present study. However, Zhao et al.22, focus on dispositional trait gratitude rather than expressed or received state gratitude, and their quantitative design cannot capture the contextual, relational, and cultural nuances through which employees interpret specific acts of appreciation. Furthermore, their Chinese organizational context, while offering cross-cultural resonance, does not engage with the Islamic religious frameworks that shape the moral meaning of gratitude in the Saudi energy sector. These gaps — the absence of qualitative evidence, the focus on trait rather than expressed gratitude, and the neglect of Islamic cultural context — represent the specific empirical spaces the present study fills.

Within the psychology of gratitude, Grant and Gino2 conducted a series of randomized experiments demonstrating that supervisors’ expressions of gratitude increased employees’ helping behavior, which was mediated by feelings of social worth. This finding—that gratitude communicates information about the expresser’s perception of the recipient’s value—is directly relevant to the present study, which finds that employees in the Saudi energy sector consistently interpret expressions of gratitude as evidence that their contributions have been seen and valued. However, Grant and Gino’s2 experimental design, while methodologically rigorous, necessarily strips gratitude expression of its organizational, cultural, and relational context—precisely the context that this study seeks to examine.

In a more recent contribution, Fehr et al.1, developed a multilevel model of gratitude in organizations, distinguishing between individual-, dyadic-, and collective-level processes. Their model suggests that organizational gratitude climates—shared perceptions of the prevalence and legitimacy of gratitude expression—affect collective well-being and performance beyond those of individual exchanges. This multilevel perspective is germane to the present study, as several employees in the dataset comment on the degree to which gratitude culture is (or is not) institutionalized in their organizations, suggesting that the effects of individual exchanges are embedded in a broader organizational context.

Rhoades and Eisenberger’s11 meta-analytic review of perceived organizational support (POS) found that favorable treatment from supervisors—a category that encompasses informal recognition and verbal appreciation—was among the strongest predictors of POS, which in turn predicted affective commitment, job satisfaction, and performance. This finding provides empirical support for the SET-based theoretical framework adopted in this study. It suggests that the psychological mechanisms by which gratitude influences well-being operate, at least in part, via the mediation of perceived organizational support.

Within the broader literature on informal recognition, Buckingham and Coffman’s5 practitioner-oriented research—drawing on Gallup data from over a million employees—identified “recognition in the last seven days” as one of the twelve key drivers of employee engagement. While methodologically distinct from academic research, this finding has been influential in establishing the practical significance of informal appreciation and has prompted several subsequent academic studies examining the mechanisms of its effects20,26.

Turning to the Saudi and Gulf context, the literature is markedly thinner, reflecting a broader pattern of relative neglect in organizational behavior research7,27. Studies have examined topics such as job satisfaction, organizational commitment, and leadership styles in Saudi organizations28,29, but the specific dynamics of informal recognition and gratitude remain largely unexamined. The few studies that address related themes suggest that hierarchical authority structures, paternalistic leadership norms, and cultural values associated with collectivism and religious obligation (including Islamic teachings on gratitude, as referenced in the dataset analyzed here) shape the experience of appreciation in distinctly non-Western ways30. This study contributes to addressing this gap.

Methodology

This study adopts an exploratory qualitative research design, consistent with generating an in-depth understanding of a relatively underexamined phenomenon in a specific organizational context31,32. The exploratory orientation is appropriate given the limited existing knowledge of how employees in Saudi energy-sector organizations experience informal gratitude, and the study’s goal is to generate theoretically grounded propositions rather than to test pre-specified hypotheses.

Research setting

Data were collected from four large multinational organizations operating in the Saudi energy sector, collectively employing tens of thousands of full-time employees drawn from numerous national and professional backgrounds. These organizations are significant actors in Saudi Arabia’s energy economy and are subject to the strategic and workforce transformation imperatives associated with Vision 2030. Their multinational character introduces a degree of cultural and professional diversity that enriches the dataset and enables examination of gratitude across varying cultural frameworks. The organizations are anonymized throughout this report to protect participant confidentiality and organizational identity.

Data collection

The dataset consists of 238 firsthand, computerized employee comments extracted from an internal organizational electronic system used across the four organizations. This system served as a document repository and internal communication platform, through which employees were invited to contribute to an organizational discussion on the impact of expressing gratitude on employee well-being. Data were collected over a defined period, and all comments submitted during that period were extracted in full after the discussion closed. No selection criteria were applied at the extraction stage: all 238 comments were retained for analysis, with no exclusions based on content, length, or perceived relevance. The comments are characterized here as ‘naturalistic’ rather than ‘unprompted’: while the overarching discussion topic was organizationally framed, no specific questions, response templates, or thematic prompts were provided to participants, who were free to respond in any manner and length they chose. This naturalistic character is methodologically significant: unlike interview data, which is necessarily co-constructed through the researcher-participant relationship, these comments represent unmediated employee expression. The resulting dataset is therefore ecologically valid — reflecting genuine organizational experience rather than research-reactive performance.

Data were collected over a defined three-month period, during which employees contributed voluntarily to the organizational discussion forum. At the conclusion of this period, all comments submitted to the system were extracted in their entirety by the researcher with institutional authorization. No selection criteria were applied at the extraction stage: all 238 comments posted during the discussion period were retained for analysis. No comments were excluded based on content, length, or perceived relevance, consistent with the principle of information richness that guided the purposive sampling strategy33.

The study commenced following the granting of ethical approval on 26 September 2025. Participant recruitment — operationalized as opening the organizational discussion forum to employee contributions — began in October 2025, with data collection occurring over a defined period during which employees voluntarily contributed comments.

As documentary data, the comments were extracted and treated as primary source material consistent with the document analysis tradition in qualitative research34. All identifying information—including employee names, organizational identifiers, and role titles—was removed prior to analysis. The documents were imported into a qualitative data analysis framework for systematic coding and theme development.

Sampling strategy

The study employed purposive sampling33, guided by the criterion of information richness. The guiding principle was to ensure that all data were maximally relevant to the research question and reflective of the organizational context under investigation. Three criteria guided the sampling: sector relevance (only comments from full-time employees of Saudi energy-sector organizations were included), organizational diversity (multiple organizations were sampled to introduce sub-sectoral variation), and voluntary participation (the opt-in nature of the discussion forum ensured ecological validity). No comments were excluded based on content or length; all 238 contributions meeting the sector-relevance criterion were retained. Analytical approach

The study employs thematic analysis as developed by Braun and Clarke (835,), a method well suited to exploratory qualitative research in organizational and social science contexts. Thematic analysis enables the identification, analysis, and reporting of meaning-level patterns in qualitative data and is theoretically flexible—compatible with the inductive-deductive hybrid approach adopted in this study36. Initial coding proceeded inductively, allowing themes to emerge from the data without prior categorization; subsequent interpretation was deductive, engaging the emergent themes with Social Exchange Theory as the sensitizing theoretical lens. It is important to distinguish thematic analysis from content analysis: whereas content analysis prioritizes the systematic frequency-based categorization of manifest textual units37, thematic analysis focuses on latent, interpretive patterns — what participants mean, not merely what they say. This distinction is epistemologically significant given that the present study is concerned with the meaning that employees attribute to expressions of gratitude rather than with counting the occurrence of particular words or phrases.

The analysis followed an inductive-deductive hybrid approach. In the inductive phase, initial coding was conducted without prior categorization, allowing codes to emerge directly from the data. In the deductive phase, emergent themes were subsequently interpreted through Social Exchange Theory as the sensitizing theoretical lens, enabling the analysis to connect participant meanings to established theoretical constructs without forcing the data into pre-determined categories. The analysis proceeded through six sequential phases: data familiarization, initial coding, theme generation, theme review, theme naming and definition, and theoretical mapping. Each phase built upon the previous, ensuring systematic and rigorous progression from raw data to theory-grounded thematic interpretation. Thematic analysis process and coding technique.

To strengthen the consistency and credibility of the coding process, a 20% sample of comments (n = 48, selected through systematic interval sampling) was independently reviewed and coded (see Appendix A). Subsequent comparison of coding decisions confirmed convergent interpretations across all four major themes. Where minor discrepancies arose, these were resolved through discussion and iterative refinement of code definitions, resulting in a strengthened and consensually validated thematic structure.

It is necessary to clarify that this study employs thematic analysis — not content analysis — as its primary analytical method. Whereas content analysis privileges systematic, frequency-based categorization of manifest textual content37, thematic analysis, as operationalized by Braun and Clarke (835,), prioritizes identifying latent, meaning-level patterns in qualitative data, enabling interpretive engagement with what participants say and what those accounts mean. This distinction is epistemologically significant: the present study is concerned not with counting the occurrence of particular words or phrases but with understanding the meanings that employees attach to expressions of gratitude and the relational and cultural dynamics those meanings reflect. Thematic analysis is therefore the more appropriate analytical tool, given the study’s exploratory and interpretive aims.

The analytical process was conducted manually without the use of dedicated qualitative data analysis software (such as NVivo or Atlas.ti). This decision was deliberate: given the dataset’s volume (238 comments) and the interpretive, meaning-focused character of the analysis, manual coding was deemed to offer greater analytical transparency and closer researcher proximity to the data. The comments were imported into a structured spreadsheet environment for systematic organization, initial coding, and iterative theme development, enabling flexible movement between individual comments and the evolving thematic structure throughout all six analytical phases.

Researcher reflexivity

Reflexivity is a core quality criterion in qualitative research35, requiring the researcher to critically examine how positionality, prior assumptions, and relationships to the research context may shape the analytical process and its outcomes. I have professional experience in the Saudi energy sector, which confers both an advantage — familiarity with the organizational, cultural, and hierarchical dynamics that shape the data — and a potential limitation: the risk of pre-theoretical assumptions influencing interpretive judgments in ways that are not fully transparent. To mitigate this risk, I adopted three reflexive strategies throughout the analytical process. First, I maintained a reflexive research journal during data familiarisation and coding, documenting emerging interpretations and flagging instances in which prior organizational experience may have predisposed me toward particular readings of the data. Second, initial codes and candidate themes were reviewed against the full dataset at multiple stages to ensure that analytical decisions were data-driven rather than assumption-led. Third, Social Exchange Theory was applied as a sensitizing theoretical lens rather than as a deductive template that pre-specified findings, thereby preserving the inductive integrity of the initial coding phase. These measures do not eliminate the subjective dimension of qualitative interpretation but ensure that the analytical process was conducted with appropriate reflective scrutiny.

Findings

The thematic analysis of 238 employee comments yielded four major themes, each comprising between two and four sub-themes. Together, these themes provide a rich, multi-layered account of how employees in the Saudi energy sector perceive, experience, and attribute significance to expressions of gratitude in the workplace. Frequencies and percentages reported throughout this section are employed descriptively to convey the relative prevalence of themes within this dataset; they are not intended to imply statistical generalisability or inferential significance, and should be read as qualitative indicators of thematic salience rather than as quantitatively robust findings8. Thematic structure with comment frequency and percentage.

Frequencies and percentages reported throughout this section are employed descriptively to convey the relative prevalence of themes within the dataset of 238 comments. They are not intended to imply statistical generalizability or inferential significance. Their purpose is to indicate the salience of each theme — that is, how widely it was represented across the participant group — without constituting a basis for probabilistic inference or population-level claims. All interpretations are qualitative in character and should be read accordingly8.

Theme 1: Gratitude as a Psychological Resource

The most pervasive theme across the dataset—evident in 79.4% of comments (n = 189)—concerned gratitude’s function as a psychological resource. Employees consistently described expressions of ‘thank you’ as transformative experiences that generated positive affect, renewed energy, and enhanced motivation. This theme aligns with Hobfoll’s38 Conservation of Resources (COR) theory. It is theoretically interpretable through SET as evidence that symbolic exchange resources—here, the expression of appreciation—fulfill employees’ psychological needs in ways that monetary compensation alone cannot.

Within this theme, the most prominent sub-theme concerned motivation and performance enhancement (n = 147, 61.8%). Employees described receiving a “thank you” as producing immediate motivational effects: increased willingness to exert effort, heightened engagement with tasks, and a subjective sense of capability and worth. One employee wrote that a supervisor’s expression of gratitude made them “want to come to work every day,” a formulation that succinctly captures the SET dynamic: the perceived social exchange of appreciation for effort generates an obligation-fulfilling reciprocal response in the form of continued effort and engagement.

“When my supervisor says ‘thank you,’ it gives me the power to work more efficiently. It makes my full day nice and leads to more productivity and performing all tasks in the right manner.” (Comment 38).

A second prominent sub-theme concerned emotional well-being and positive affect (n = 112, 47.1%). Employees described gratitude expressions as producing feelings of being valued, seen, and respected—psychological states associated with higher levels of subjective well-being in the organizational psychology literature39. The affective dimension of gratitude reception was not incidental but central: employees drew explicit connections between feeling appreciated and feeling psychologically well.

A third, somewhat less prevalent sub-theme concerned stress reduction and pressure management (n = 78, 32.8%). Several employees described the psychological buffering effect of gratitude in high-pressure work environments, noting that a sincere “thank you” provided psychological resilience against the demands of demanding organizational contexts. This finding resonates with Grant and Gino’s2 demonstration that expressing gratitude serves as a social resource that buffers against the psychological costs of effortful work.

Theme 2: sincerity and relational authenticity

The second theme—present in 59.7% of comments (n = 142)—concerned the critical importance of sincerity and authenticity in shaping the psychological impact of expressing gratitude. Employees drew clear distinctions between genuine appreciation, which they described as emotionally resonant and motivationally powerful, and performative or habitual gratitude, which they characterized as hollow and potentially counterproductive. This finding extends SET by highlighting the importance of perceived authenticity in the exchange: it is not merely the formal expression of gratitude that generates positive reciprocal responses, but the perception that the expresser genuinely values the recipient’s contribution.

The sub-theme of genuine versus performative gratitude (n = 118, 49.6%) was articulated consistently across the dataset. Employees were sensitive to contextual cues—tone, eye contact, sincerity of expression—that enabled them to distinguish authentic appreciation from its simulation. One employee’s observation that “appreciation must be sincere and coming from one’s heart” captured a position expressed, in various formulations, by the majority of respondents who addressed this dimension.

“Thank you is powerful when it comes from the heart and not said robotically. Thanking employees does not cost any money, yet it has real power to reinforce excellent achievements.” (Comment 187).

The sub-theme of relational trust (n = 89, 37.4%) concerned the broader relational context in which gratitude is exchanged. Several employees drew a connection between expressing gratitude and developing trust, noting that supervisors who consistently expressed appreciation were perceived as more trustworthy, more invested in their teams, and more effective as leaders. This finding resonates with Graen and Uhl-Bien’s18 LMX theory, which suggests that expressing gratitude is one of the relational practices through which high-quality leader-member exchange relationships are built and maintained.

A smaller but analytically significant sub-theme concerned non-verbal and embodied forms of recognition (n = 53, 22.3%). One employee’s account of an executive director who arrived at the employee’s workstation unaccompanied and offered a silent “pat on the hand” described lasting motivational effects “felt daily since a year ago.” This finding, while qualitatively distinctive, underscores the importance of embodied, relational presence as a component of meaningful appreciation—a dimension largely absent from quantitative survey-based research on recognition.

Theme 3: hierarchical asymmetry in gratitude expression

A third theme—evident in 49.6% of comments (n = 118)—concerned the hierarchical dimensions of gratitude in organizational settings. Employees consistently framed the expression of gratitude as a hierarchically asymmetrical practice. While peer-to-peer expressions of appreciation were valued, the motivational and well-being effects of gratitude were described as substantially amplified when the source was a supervisor or senior leader. This finding is theoretically significant within a SET framework, as it suggests that the exchange value of symbolic resources—including expressions of appreciation—is calibrated against the social and organizational status of the exchanger.

The sub-theme of supervisor-employee dynamics (n = 98, 41.2%) was the most frequently occurring. Many employees expressed frustration at supervisors’ failure to acknowledge their contributions, framing this failure as a betrayal of reciprocal obligations that constitutes a violation of the implicit social contract between employer and employee. The language of obligation and fairness—core constructs in both SET and psychological contract theory—was prominent.

“Only a few bosses could thank their team. My hat is off to these few bosses. Whereas the majority of bosses do not even know how to win the hearts of their employees, as they are simply in pursuit of their own selfish desires.” (Comment 7).

The sub-theme of senior leadership expressions (n = 67, 28.2%) highlighted the particularly potent motivational effects of recognition from executive leadership. Employees reported that these experiences were memorable, rare, and disproportionately impactful relative to recognition from immediate supervisors, suggesting a hierarchical gradient in the exchange value of gratitude. From a SET perspective, this gradient can be interpreted as reflecting the greater access to organizational resources—including the power to shape career trajectories, performance evaluations, and recognition—that senior leaders possess.

A counterintuitive sub-theme concerned the fear of recognition as weakness (n = 41, 17.2%). Several employees noted that some supervisors appeared reluctant to express gratitude, concerned that doing so would signal dependence on subordinates or weaken their perceived authority. One employee identified a specific cultural-organizational dynamic: supervisors feared that expressing appreciation would be interpreted by employees as acknowledging that the supervisor “owes” them a high-performance rating.

Theme 4: cultural and organizational embeddedness

The fourth theme—present in 40.8% of comments (n = 97)—concerned the cultural and organizational contexts that shape the expression, interpretation, and impact of gratitude in the Saudi energy sector. This theme was analytically distinct from the preceding themes in that it foregrounded gratitude’s embeddedness in social structures, cultural values, and organizational climates rather than in individual psychological dynamics.

A particularly prominent sub-theme concerned cultural values and religious framing (n = 68, 28.6%). A significant proportion of employees drew on Islamic teachings and cultural values as frameworks for understanding gratitude, citing the Prophetic hadith, “one who does not thank people does not thank God,” as a moral foundation for appreciation practices. This religious dimension of gratitude expression is specific to the Saudi and broader Islamic organizational contexts and represents an important cultural nuance absent from Western organizational literature on the subject.

The sub-theme of multicultural workplace dynamics (n = 52, 21.8%) was particularly relevant given the multinational character of the participating organizations. Employees from diverse national backgrounds navigated different cultural norms around expressing and receiving appreciation. Several noted that the absence of gratitude could be misinterpreted through a cultural lens—particularly by Asian expatriate employees, who perceived failures of appreciation as expressions of disrespect or status-based discrimination.

“I have especially seen this attitude towards Asian expatriates. Do they feel that if they say thank you, they will lose their credibility? People need to understand that respect is gained by being given and taken.” (Comment 193).

The sub-theme of organizational recognition culture (n = 49, 20.6%) concerned the degree to which gratitude and appreciation were institutionalized as organizational values. Employees in organizations perceived as having stronger recognition cultures reported more positive experiences of gratitude exchange and higher levels of motivation and belonging. This sub-theme resonates with Fehr et al.,’s1 concept of organizational gratitude climate and suggests that individual exchanges of appreciation are embedded in and shaped by broader organizational norms.

Case study: a pat on the hand—the disproportionate impact of senior leadership recognition

Among the 238 comments, one account stood out for its vividness, specificity, and analytic richness. An employee described an unannounced visit to their workstation by an Executive Director, who offered no verbal expression but instead communicated appreciation through a simple physical gesture—a pat on the hand. The employee reported that this gesture continued to influence their daily experience of work “daily” more than a year after the event. This case illustrates several theoretical propositions simultaneously: the amplified exchange value of senior leadership recognition (P3), the role of non-verbal and embodied forms of appreciation as carriers of social exchange (Theme 2), and the enduring motivational effects of perceived genuine recognition relative to more formulaic expressions (P2). From a SET perspective, the case suggests that the symbolic capital embedded in an act of recognition is calibrated not only by its content but also by the organizational position of the expresser, with recognition from senior leadership carrying a density of social exchange value that substantially exceeds that of equivalent expressions from peers or immediate supervisors.

Discussion

The findings of this study reveal a coherent and theoretically meaningful account of how employees in large multinational energy organizations in Saudi Arabia perceive and experience expressions of gratitude in the workplace. Interpreted through the lens of Social Exchange Theory (SET4;), the four themes that emerged from the thematic analysis — gratitude as a psychological resource, sincerity and relational authenticity, hierarchical asymmetry in gratitude expression, and cultural and organizational embeddedness — collectively illuminate the mechanisms through which a simple, cost-free communicative act generates substantive and durable organizational consequences. This discussion critically engages each of the four research propositions with relevant scholarship, identifying both points of convergence and divergence with the existing literature.

P1: Gratitude as a symbolic social exchange resource

The high prevalence of Theme 1 across the dataset — evident in 79.4% of comments — is consistent with P1 and closely aligns with SET’s foundational claims. Blau’s4 distinction between economic exchange, governed by calculable and contractual returns, and social exchange, characterized by diffuse, symbolically mediated reciprocity, is directly instantiated in the data. Employees consistently described the motivational and well-being effects of a “thank you” in terms that far exceed what its economic value would predict, attributing transformative effects on effort, engagement, and daily experience to what is, strictly speaking, a zero-cost communicative gesture. This finding resonates with Grant and Gino’s2 experimental evidence that gratitude expression elevates recipients’ sense of social worth and drives subsequent prosocial behavior — a relationship the present study corroborates in a naturalistic, non-laboratory context and extends to a cultural setting that Grant and Gino’s North American samples do not represent. However, whereas Grant and Gino2 focus primarily on downstream behavioral consequences, the present data illuminate the prior psychological mechanisms — specifically, the experience of being seen, valued, and regarded as a meaningful contributor — suggesting that perceived social worth is the more proximal psychological variable, with behavioral outcomes following as a consequence of this perceptual shift.

This finding stands in productive tension with Rhoades and Eisenberger’s11 meta-analytic POS account, which treats supervisory approval as an undifferentiated category. The present study suggests that informal verbal appreciation is a qualitatively distinct and particularly potent form of that approval, whose exchange value derives from its discretionary, non-institutionalized character — a distinction that aggregate POS measures do not capture.

P2: Sincerity as a moderating condition

The substantial prevalence of Theme 2 (59.7% of comments) and its specific emphasis on the distinction between genuine and performative gratitude represents one of the study’s most theoretically generative findings, and one that challenges some of the simpler formulations in the recognition literature. Buckingham and Coffman’s5 widely cited claim that recognition “in the last seven days” drives engagement implies a relatively mechanical, frequency-based model of appreciation effects; the present data significantly complicate this picture. Employees were sophisticated and sensitive interpreters of gratitude, capable of identifying hollow expressions and reporting that these generated not motivation but cynicism, distrust, and — in some cases — a sense of insult. This finding is consistent with Fehr et al.,’s1 multilevel model of organizational gratitude, which argues that gratitude expression must be perceived as voluntary, costly, and personally directed in order to generate social exchange value. The present study extends this argument by suggesting that, in the organizational contexts examined, employees use a rich array of contextual and relational cues — tone, eye contact, specificity, unsolicited delivery — to assess authenticity and calibrate their psychological and behavioral responses accordingly.

This dimension of the findings also speaks to a tension in the practitioner-oriented literature on employee recognition, which has increasingly advocated institutionalizing appreciation through recognition programs, scheduled acknowledgment rituals, and performance management frameworks13,20. The present data suggest that the institutionalization of gratitude may generate a paradox: by making appreciation obligatory and predictable, formal recognition programs may inadvertently undermine the very quality — voluntariness — that gives informal gratitude its social exchange value. This is a theoretically important implication that the recognition literature has not adequately confronted, and one that the present study brings into sharp relief.

P3: Hierarchical asymmetry and the gradient of exchange value

The prominence of Theme 3 (49.6% of comments), and specifically its finding that gratitude from senior leaders generates disproportionately strong and durable motivational effects, is consistent with SET’s proposition that the value of a social exchange resource is calibrated to the exchanger’s resource endowments4. Supervisors and senior leaders control access to the organizational resources that matter most to employees — performance evaluations, promotion decisions, public recognition, and career development opportunities — and their expressions of appreciation therefore carry information not only about current valuation but about future exchange prospects. This interpretation is supported by Graen and Uhl-Bien’s18 leader-member exchange (LMX) theory, which demonstrates that the quality of the dyadic relationship between supervisor and subordinate — of which gratitude expression is a constitutive practice — is among the strongest predictors of employee performance, satisfaction, and organizational commitment. The present study contributes to the LMX literature by identifying gratitude expression as one of the specific relational behaviors through which high-quality LMX relationships are built and maintained, a mechanism that LMX research has theorized but rarely examined through qualitative, employee-centered methods.

Critically, however, the data also reveal a finding that is at odds with mainstream LMX and POS research: a substantial minority of employees (17.2%) reported that supervisors actively withheld gratitude for fear that expressing appreciation would be interpreted as weakening their authority or creating performance entitlements. This phenomenon has received almost no systematic attention in the organizational behavior literature. However, it poses a significant structural barrier to realizing the well-being benefits attributed to informal recognition in the broader literature. It suggests that the absence of gratitude in organizations may not always reflect indifference or incompetence but rather a specific — if misguided — managerial logic, one that prioritizes the performance of authority over the cultivation of relational social exchange.

P4: Cultural and organizational embeddedness

The findings relating to Theme 4 (40.8% of comments) are consistent with and extend existing scholarship on the contextual determinants of gratitude expression, while introducing a dimension — the role of Islamic religious teaching as a moral framework for organizational appreciation — that is absent from the Western-centric literature. McCullough et al.9, foundational account of gratitude as a moral affect is germane here: the present data suggest that in the Saudi context, the moral status of gratitude is not merely a psychological disposition but a religiously grounded obligation, anchored in the Prophetic hadith that “one who does not thank people does not thank God.” This framing transforms appreciation from a managerial technique into a moral imperative, one that employees invoke not only to describe their own behavior but to evaluate and critique the behavior of supervisors who withhold it. This represents a theoretically significant extension of McCullough et al.,’s9 framework, which situates gratitude’s moral character in a secular psychological theory of prosocial motivation; the present data suggest that in religious organizational contexts, the moral character of gratitude derives from an altogether different source — divine obligation — and carries correspondingly different normative weight.

The sub-theme of multicultural workplace dynamics further complicates the picture by revealing that appreciation norms are not uniformly shared even within a single organizational context. The experiences reported by Asian expatriate employees — who described feeling systematically excluded from expressions of appreciation in ways they interpreted as disrespectful and discriminatory — point to the operation of status hierarchies within the exchange relationship that SET, in its most general formulations, does not adequately theorize. Cropanzano and Mitchell’s21 critique of SET’s rationalistic assumptions is relevant here: the theory’s core exchange logic assumes relatively symmetrical actors oriented toward reciprocal benefit, but the data reveal significant asymmetries in who has access to the social exchange of appreciation and whose contributions are deemed worthy of acknowledgment. This finding calls for a more structurally sensitive application of SET that attends to the power dimensions of exchange relationships — a theoretical development that the present study both motivates and begins to sketch.

Research contributions, limitations, and future research

Theoretical contributions

This study makes four substantive theoretical contributions to the organizational behavior and human resource management literatures, each of which extends, refines, or challenges existing theoretical frameworks in ways that have implications beyond the specific empirical context examined.

First, the study extends Social Exchange Theory4 by theorizing informal gratitude expression as a non-material symbolic resource that fulfills the social exchange obligations of the supervisor-subordinate relationship in ways that formal, monetized recognition cannot replicate. While SET has been applied extensively to the study of perceived organizational support11,17 and leader-member exchange18, these applications have largely treated recognition as an undifferentiated category. The present study advances a more differentiated account, demonstrating that the symbolic density of gratitude expression — its capacity to communicate personal regard, voluntary acknowledgment, and relational investment — constitutes a qualitatively distinct form of social exchange resource whose effects cannot be captured by aggregate measures of organizational support. This distinction has important theoretical implications: it suggests that SET-based models of employee behavior need to attend not only to the quantity of recognition received but to its qualitative character — specifically, its perceived voluntariness, sincerity, and relational specificity.

Second, the study extends SET by introducing quality calibration as a theoretical construct that mediates the relationship between gratitude expression and exchange value. The existing SET literature largely assumes that the value of an exchange resource is determined by its scarcity and the exchanger’s resource endowments4,21. The present findings complicate this account by suggesting that the perceived authenticity and sincerity of the expression are equally — and in some cases more — determinative of its exchange value. An insincere expression of gratitude from a resource-rich senior leader may have lower exchange value than a genuine expression from a peer, because it fails to meet the authenticity threshold employees apply when evaluating social exchange transactions. This finding calls for a revision of SET’s account of exchange value that incorporates the subjective quality-assessment processes through which recipients evaluate and respond to incoming social exchange resources.

Third, the study contributes to the emerging multilevel literature on organizational gratitude climates1 by providing the first qualitative, employee-centered evidence of how individual-level exchange dynamics are embedded in and shaped by organizational-level climates of appreciation. Fehr et al.1multilevel model postulates that organizational gratitude climates influence collective well-being and performance through mechanisms that exceed those of individual exchanges; the present study is consistent with and enriches this account by illustrating the specific ways in which organizational climate — manifested in supervisory role modelling, cultural norms, and institutional recognition practices — shapes the frequency, form, and perceived legitimacy of individual gratitude exchanges. Critically, the study also identifies a previously untheorized organizational barrier to gratitude expression — supervisory fear of appreciation as a performance entitlement signal — that has significant implications for the multilevel model’s account of organizational climate formation40–43.

Fourth, the study makes a novel theoretical contribution by integrating SET with Islamic organizational ethics as a cultural-contextual framework for understanding the moral dimensions of gratitude in non-Western organizational settings. McCullough et al.9, foundational account of gratitude as a moral affect is grounded in a secular psychological tradition; the present study demonstrates that in the Saudi energy-sector context, the moral character of gratitude is constituted through an entirely different normative framework — one that derives its obligatory force from religious teaching rather than from secular prosocial psychology. This integration represents a genuine theoretical extension: it challenges the implicit cultural universalism of Western-centric gratitude research. It opens a productive space for cross-cultural theoretical dialogue about the normative foundations of organizational appreciation.

Methodological contributions

Methodologically, the study makes a significant contribution by using unprompted, computerized documentary comments as primary data for qualitative analysis. This data source—derived from an electronic internal organizational system and extracted as a documentary corpus—is unusual in the organizational behavior literature, which has tended to rely on interview data, survey instruments, or experimental paradigms. The use of documentary comments offers a degree of ecological validity that interview data cannot match. Because participants were unaware that their comments would be treated as research data, their contributions are free from the response biases associated with researcher-participant interactions. This methodological novelty is relevant not only to the present study but to organizational researchers more broadly, suggesting the potential of internal organizational discourse—captured through digital platforms—as a rich and underutilized source of authentic employee experience data.

Empirical and contextual contributions

Empirically, this study provides the most extensive and contextually rich qualitative evidence to date on the perceived impact of gratitude expression in an organizational setting. The dataset of 238 unprompted employee comments, collected across four large multinational energy organizations, represents a qualitatively distinctive form of evidence — naturalistic, ecologically valid, and free from the researcher-reactivity that characterizes interview-based research. The richness and consistency of the themes emerging from this dataset lend considerable empirical weight to the study’s central argument: that informal gratitude expression is not a peripheral or trivial organizational phenomenon but a key determinant of employees’ psychological well-being, motivation, and relational quality.

The study’s contextual contribution is equally significant. Saudi Arabia’s energy sector occupies a structurally unique position in both the national economy and the broader Gulf regional economic landscape. However, it has been almost absent from the organizational behavior research that has shaped current understanding of recognition, well-being, and human resource management. The present study addresses this absence directly, generating empirical evidence that is both context-specific and theoretically generative for the broader field. Several contextual features of the Saudi energy-sector environment — the dominance of multinational organizations employing highly diverse workforces, the intersection of Islamic cultural values with international corporate governance norms, the hierarchical authority structures characteristic of the energy industry, and the strategic imperatives of Vision 2030 — create a set of organizational conditions that shape the dynamics of gratitude expression in distinctive ways. By documenting these conditions empirically and theorizing their effects, the study contributes to contextual organizational behavior research that extends well beyond the boundaries of a single sector or country.

Contextually, the findings are also directly relevant to the Vision 2030 agenda, which positions human capital development, employee engagement, and organizational excellence as central pillars of Saudi Arabia’s economic diversification strategy. The evidence that simple, cost-free acts of informal recognition generate measurable improvements in employee motivation, well-being, and commitment provides an empirical foundation for organizational development and human resource policy initiatives aimed at building more appreciative and psychologically safe workplace cultures — outcomes that are not only intrinsically valuable but strategically aligned with the national transformation program.

Practical implications

The findings have several concrete, actionable implications for human resource professionals, organizational leaders, and policymakers in the Saudi energy sector and comparable contexts.

For HR leadership and management development, the most fundamental practical implication concerns the cost-effectiveness of informal recognition as an organizational investment. The evidence that a sincere expression of “thank you” generates motivational, well-being, and relational effects that rival — and in some cases exceed — those of formal recognition programs and financial incentives challenges the prevailing organizational logic that prioritizes monetized rewards as the primary mechanism for driving engagement and performance. HR professionals should recognize informal gratitude as a high-return, zero-cost organizational resource and design leadership development programs that treat the capacity to express genuine appreciation as a core managerial competency rather than an optional interpersonal skill.

For organizational recognition program design: the sincerity findings have direct implications for how such programs are designed and implemented. The data suggest that institutionalizing appreciation through scheduled, obligatory recognition rituals risks generating the performative, hollow expressions that employees identify as psychologically counterproductive. HR professionals should resist the temptation to over-systematize gratitude and instead focus on creating organizational conditions — psychological safety, leader role modeling, and cultural permission — that support genuine, discretionary expressions of appreciation. Training interventions should focus less on the mechanics of recognition and more on developing managers’ capacity for authentic relational engagement: attending to individual contributions, expressing appreciation in specific, personal ways, and resisting the cultural and organizational pressures that inhibit supervisors from expressing gratitude.

For senior leadership engagement

The hierarchical asymmetry findings carry clear implications for executive-level practice. The evidence that expressions of appreciation from executive-level leaders generate disproportionately strong and durable motivational effects — including the case of a wordless physical gesture from an executive director that an employee described as shaping their daily experience of work more than a year later — suggests that senior leaders hold an underutilized and frequently neglected organizational resource in their capacity for direct, personal recognition of employee contributions. Organizations should create structured opportunities for senior leaders to engage directly with frontline and middle-level employees in ways that communicate genuine appreciation, recognizing that these interactions carry social exchange value far exceeding that of equivalent expressions from immediate supervisors.

For multinational HRM in the Saudi context and Vision 2030

The cultural embeddedness findings have direct implications for human resource management in Saudi multinational organizations. The intersection of Islamic values of gratitude with international corporate recognition norms creates a distinctive motivational landscape in which appreciation carries both secular organizational and religious-moral significance. HR professionals and organizational leaders operating in this context should be aware that the normative framework through which employees evaluate and respond to expressions of appreciation is not purely organizational but is deeply embedded in cultural and religious values, and that recognition practices that are misaligned with these values — or that are perceived as insincere tokens of compliance with corporate diversity norms rather than genuine expressions of regard — may generate not motivation but resistance. Particular attention should be paid to ensuring that multicultural recognition practices are genuinely inclusive and address the pattern identified in the data, whereby Asian expatriate employees reported feeling systematically excluded from expressions of appreciation, which damaged their relational engagement and well-being.

Limitations

The study is subject to several limitations that qualify the generalizability of its findings and inform directions for future research. First, the data are cross-sectional and documentary. While they capture authentic employee expression, they do not permit longitudinal analysis of gratitude dynamics or causal inference about the relationships between variables. Second, the dataset, while unusually rich for qualitative research, is confined to employees who chose to contribute to the organizational discussion, raising the possibility of self-selection bias—employees with stronger positive or negative experiences of recognition may be disproportionately represented. Third, the anonymized nature of the data precludes analysis of demographic variables—including nationality, professional level, and tenure—that are likely to moderate the relationships examined in the study. Fourth, while the study draws on four organizations, all are located in the Saudi energy sector and share important structural and contextual features; findings may not generalize to other sectors, countries, or organizational types.

A further limitation pertains to the inferential boundaries of the study design. As an exploratory qualitative investigation, this study does not permit causal inference about the relationships between gratitude expression and employee outcomes. All findings reflect participants’ perceptions and interpretive accounts of their own experience, and should be understood as theoretically grounded propositions warranting further confirmatory investigation rather than as established causal claims. Readers are therefore encouraged to interpret the study’s conclusions as providing a contextually rich basis for theory development rather than as empirical proof of the identified relationships.

Future research directions

The present study opens several productive avenues for future research. Longitudinal designs employing repeated measurements would enable investigation of whether the motivational effects of gratitude are sustained over time or subject to satiation. Comparative studies examining gratitude dynamics across multiple national and cultural contexts—within and beyond the Gulf region—would enable theorization of the cultural contingencies of SET-based gratitude exchange. The development and validation of quantitative instruments grounded in the qualitatively generated theoretical framework presented here would enable large-sample, confirmatory testing of the model’s propositions. Finally, multi-level research designs incorporating organizational climate measures alongside individual-level data would enable empirical examination of the interaction between individual exchange dynamics and collective gratitude climates theorized by Fehr et al.1.
