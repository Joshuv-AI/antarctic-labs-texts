Aqueous Accumulation and Co-Accumulation of Multiple Cationic Porphyrins within a Polyaromatic Capsule
Authors: Kazuki Toyama, Yuya Tanaka, Yohei Haketa, Hiroki Horita, Hiromitsu Maeda, Michito Yoshizawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In contrast to stable cation–anion assemblies in nonpolar solvents, neither cations nor anions assemble themselves due to electrostatic repulsion and/or hydration in bulk solution. Here we report the multiple accumulation of cationic porphyrins in water using a polyaromatic capsule formed from bent amphiphiles. Simple stirring of the aqueous capsule with cationic Au­(III)-tetraphenylporphyrins in water leads to the efficient formation of new host–guest composites, much more efficiently than that with nonionic Zn­(II) derivatives under the same conditions. The detailed UV–visible, NMR, DLS, AFM, TEM, and molecular modeling studies reveal that the accumulated multication composite is a ∼2 nm-sized spherical particle, consisting of stacked, three porphyrin cations in the capsule as a major product, through effective host–guest π–π and CH−π interactions. The theoretical analysis indicates the importance of large dispersion force-based stabilization between the cationic porphyrins. Notably, the cationic Au­(III)-porphyrins and nonionic polyaromatic compounds such as Cu­(II)-phthalocyanine and fullerene are co-accumulated efficiently in the cavity through a stepwise grinding-stirring protocol. Otherwise unstable multicationic accumulation as well as unusual cationic-nonionic co-accumulation are demonstrated in the adaptable cavity of the polyaromatic capsule even under hydratable aqueous conditions.

Introduction

Assembly/disassembly of molecules and ions largely depends on their electric charge. , In general, cations and anions are apt to assemble with each other in nonpolar media yet cations (or anions) hardly assemble themselves, due to attractive and repulsive electrostatic interactions, respectively. Embedding of an ionic core into polyaromatic frameworks generates charged aromatic compounds, capable of forming infinite, multistacked assemblies in a ··CACA·· fashion (C: cation, A: anion) through donor–acceptor-type π–π interactions (Figure a). However, the homogeneous assembly of such polyaromatic cations again has seldom been demonstrated in nonpolar as well as polar solvents (Figure b), despite their high application potential as novel multi-ionic materials, e.g., with unusual charge transport, semiconducting, and photophysical properties. Both the rational synthesis and spectroscopic studies of multicationic assemblies thus remain unaccomplished to date. Here we report the successful accumulation of multiple porphyrin cations (n = up to 3) within a polyaromatic capsule in water (Figure c). Au­(III)-tetraphenylporphyrin chloride (AuP; Figure d) was employed herein as an ionic polyaromatic with a monocationic framework and a counterion. Stacking of AuPs has hardly been detected in solution, owing to its cationic character, unlike common M­(II)-tetraphenylporphyrins (e.g., M = Zn and Ni) with nonionic frameworks. Using aqueous capsule 1 formed from bent polyaromatic amphiphiles AA (Figure e), , here we demonstrate (i) the multiple accumulation of cationic porphyrins AuP upon encapsulation in water and (ii) the structural, spectroscopic, and electrochemical analyses of the resultant (AuP) n species. In addition, the adaptable capsule cavity enables the co-accumulation of AuPs with (iii) its fluorinated analogues as well as (iv) nonionic polyaromatic compounds in water. A new synthetic tool for novel multi-ionic assemblies is thus developed in this study. The present, unusual multiaccumulation of the polyaromatic cations is anticipated for the development of novel supramolecular electronic modules.

There have been several reports on coordination cages and boxes, capable of accumulating multiple polyaromatic or porphyrin molecules (n = up to 5) in their cavities. , Nevertheless, these accumulations are severely limited to nonionic compounds (e.g., porphine, coronene, and Cu­(II)-azaporphine). Aqueous polyaromatic capsule 1, composed of (AA) m (m = 5 on average), and its derivatives provide a size- and shape-changeable cavity, effectively surrounded by the polyaromatic frameworks. , The cavity can also incorporate the stacks of nonionic polyaromatic compounds (e.g., coronene, Zn­(II)-tetraphenylporphyrin, and Cu­(II)-phthalocyanine) in water, through the hydrophobic effect and multiple π–π/CH−π interactions. , To the best of our knowledge, however, there has been no report on previous host–guest composites, possessing ionic polyaromatic stacks, from other and our groups. − We herein employed amphiphilic compound AA (Figure e), composed of a bent anthracene dimer with two ammonium-based hydrophilic groups, as an optimized host subunit. We envisioned that the following are key features in the present system: (i) multiple aromatic–aromatic interactions between the nonionic host and cationic guest frameworks in the cavity and (ii) efficient dissociation of the counterions (Cl–) from the bound porphyrin cations within the capsule, through their hydration-based stabilization in the outer aqueous phase.

Results and Discussion

Accumulation of Multiple Cationic Porphyrins

Multiple porphyrin cations AuP were quantitatively encapsulated by polyaromatic capsule 1 through simple stirring, even with the large polyaromatic framework (1.8 nm in diameter). When dark purple solid AuP (0.9 mg, 1.0 μmol; Figures S1–S3) was stirred in a colorless aqueous solution (1.0 mL) of 1 (1.0 mM based on AA) at 70 °C for 1 h, host–guest composite 1·(AuP) n was obtained as a clear red solution after filtration to remove excess suspended AuP (Figure a). The same product was obtained from AA and AuP (1.0 μmol each) via a sequential, manual grinding (5 min), water addition (1.0 mL), centrifugation, and filtration protocol (Figure b). The quantitative formation of 1·(AuP) n based on AA was revealed by detailed UV–visible and NMR studies. The UV–visible spectra of the resulting solutions exhibited new characteristic bands at 408 and 527 nm, corresponding to the Soret and Q bands of encapsulated (AuP) n , respectively (Figure c). The Soret and Q bands were red-shifted by Δλ = +2 and +6 nm, respectively, relative to those of AuP in methanol, implying the accumulation of AuP cations within the capsule. The encapsulation efficiency of the stirring method was comparable (∼1.0-fold) to that of the grinding one (Figures d, top, S4, and S5). The 1H NMR spectrum of 1·(AuP) n in D2O showed significantly broadened, aromatic signals at 6.19, 7.01, and 7.34 ppm at room temperature (Figure S7), in contrast to the sharp signals of 1 in D2O and AuP in CD3OD (Figure S2). This phenomenon indicates strong host–guest and guest–guest interactions in the capsule cavity, where the host and guest motion is largely restricted on the NMR time scale. , It is worthy of note that isostructural yet nonionic Zn­(II)-tetraphenylporphyrin (ZnP), with a strong self-stacking character, was poorly encapsulated by capsule 1 by the stirring method (0.2-fold), as compared with the grinding method (Figures d, bottom, and S10–S12). , These results clarified that the ionic properties of AuP facilitate the encapsulation of the polyaromatic frameworks, most probably due to the weakening of the self-stacking/aggregation via electrostatic repulsion. The obtained aqueous solution of 1·(AuP) n was stable enough under ambient conditions for at least 3 d as well as under high dilution conditions of up to ∼4 μM based on AuP (Figure S13).

The encapsulation efficiency of capsule 1 toward AuP was higher than those of other host compounds in water. To compare the efficiency, bent aromatic amphiphiles AS bearing anionic hydrophilic groups and PBA/PBS with pentamethylbenzene panels, bent cycloalkyl amphiphile MA, and linear alkyl amphiphiles DA/DS (Figure e, right) were employed under the same stirring conditions. The AuP-based UV-band intensities of the resultant host–guest composites at 527 nm revealed that capsules (PBS) n and (MA) n as well as typical micelles (DA) n and (DS) n provide poor encapsulation abilities yet capsules (AS) n and (PBA) n show moderate ones (0.5–0.6-fold), relative to that of 1 (Figures e, left, and S8). These results indicated that the polyaromatic cavity of 1 is essential to efficiently encapsulate (AuP) n through multiple host–guest π–π and CH−π interactions (Figure S9). The efficiency difference between cationic 1 and anionic (AS) n is most probably derived from the formation of water-insoluble salts from cationic AuPs and anionic ASs.

Detailed Analyses of the Accumulation Structure

The accumulation structure and quantity of AuPs within capsule 1 were estimated by the combination of UV–visible, 1H NMR, DOSY, DLS, AFM, TEM, and molecular modeling analyses. , In the UV–visible-based titration studies using capsule 1 (1.0 mM based on AA) and AuP in water, the Soret band of AuP within 1 was gradually blue-shifted (up to Δλ = −8 nm) at room temperature, upon addition of AuP (0.2, 0.5, 0.7, and 1.0 equiv. based on AA; Figures a and S15), in the absence of free AuP bands. The observed blue shifts are characteristic of H-type aggregation, indicating that multiple AuPs accumulate in a stacked manner within 1 as the guest loading increases. The 1H NMR-based titration experiments of 1 with AuP were conducted under the same conditions, and the resultant signal shifts and integrals were estimated at 70 °C (Figure d–3f), owing to their broadening at room temperature (Figures S19–S35). At the elevated temperature, the sharpened aromatic signals of 1·(AuP) n , formed from 1 and AuP (0.2 equiv. based on AA) in D2O, were observed at 5.33–7.32 ppm (Figure d) and upfield shifted relative to the corresponding signals of 1 in D2O and AuP in DMSO-d 6 (Figure b and c, respectively). The observed shifts are derived from the host–guest aromatic shielding effect in the cavity of 1. The high host–guest stability of 1·(AuP) n against heat was indicated by the absence of proton signals from free AuP at 70 °C. The stability was also supported by the 1H DOSY NMR analysis, where all of the host and guest aromatic signals of 1·(AuP) n display the same diffusion coefficient (logD = −9.20, d av. = 2.2 nm; Figure g), which is smaller than that of 1 under the same conditions. The aromatic-based proton signals of 1·(AuP) n , prepared from 1 and AuP (1.0 equiv. based on AA), were shifted further upfield (Figure f), owing to the additional encapsulation and enhanced aromatic shielding effect. Particularly, the significant shifts of the inner protons H b on 1 and the phenyl protons H B on AuP (Δδ = −0.95 and −0.52 ppm, respectively; Figure d and f) suggested the multiple accumulation of AuPs inside 1. Due to the absence of free AuP signals, the host subunit-to-guest (= AA:AuP) ratio was evaluated to be approximately 4:3 by their NMR signal integration. The DOSY and DLS studies clarified the formation of a single host–guest composite with an average core diameter of 2.2 nm (Figure g and h). The product size and shape were further supported by the AFM analysis of 1·(AuP) n on mica under dry conditions (Figures i, j, and S37a). The TEM image of 1·(AuP) n on a carbon grid under vacuum conditions also showed its spherical structure with an average diameter of 2 nm and a narrow size distribution (Figures k, l, and S37b).

On the basis of the characteristic shifts of the absorption band and proton signals as well as the size analyses of 1·(AuP) n (prepared from a 1:1 AA/AuP ratio), molecular modeling studies indicated the formation of a spherical core–shell structure of (AA)4 encapsulating the stack of (AuP)3, with a core diameter of 2.2 nm, as a major product among other possible stacks (AuP) n (e.g., n = 2 and 4; Figures m and S36). The optimized structure implies the presence of efficient π–π and CH−π interactions in the hydrophobic cavity, which were also supported by NOE correlations between 1 and AuP (Figure S31). Since the present capsule has a polyaromatic shell with multicationic (∼10+) outer surfaces, cationic AuPs can interact with the inner hydrophobic cavity, much more effectively than the outer hydrophilic surfaces. The chloride counterions of encapsulated AuPs exist most likely outside the capsule in water, in a manner similar to those of 1, due to the effective hydration.

Interaction Analyses within the Cationic Accumulation

To gain insights into the detailed interactions in the cationic accumulation of (AuP)3, generated preferentially within capsule 1, its optimized structure was obtained by DFT calculations (Figure n). The energy decomposition analysis was examined using the FMO2-MP2 method. In the calculated structure, the three AuPs were arranged in a slightly offset stacked geometry, with an average Au···Au distance of 4.8 Å. These structural features agreed with the blue shift in the UV–visible spectra and the upfield shifts in the NMR spectra observed upon accumulation. On the basis of the theoretical analyses, dispersion interactions between AuPs were estimated to be the highest stabilization factor (−502 kcal/mol; Figure o). The interactions were much stronger than its electrostatic (+35 kcal/mol) and exchange interactions (+141 kcal/mol), which destabilize the accumulated structure. The weak electrostatic repulsions were explained by the delocalization of the positive charge in the porphyrin framework, as shown in the electrostatic potential map of AuP obtained by DFT calculations (Figure S40a). The charge transfer interactions (−49 kcal/mol) contributed weakly to the structural stabilization. Whereas the overall interactions in the accumulated (AuP)3 structure were energetically favorable (−375 kcal/mol), the total energy of the cationic stack was much less favorable than that of the nonionic stack (ZnP)3 (ΔE = +197 kcal/mol, Figures p and S40b), highlighting the essential contribution of encapsulation within the capsule to the accumulation process.

Electrochemical Properties of the Accumulated Cations

The electrochemical properties of AuP were markedly altered upon accumulation within capsule 1. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) of 1·(AuP) n showed two reduction peaks at −0.34 and −0.68 V (vs Ag/AgCl) in water in the presence of KCl as the supporting electrolyte under an inert atmosphere (Figures q and S41a). In contrast, only a single reduction peak at −0.57 V was observed for free AuP in methanol. These peaks are assignable to the Au­(III)/Au­(II) redox process based on related Au­(III)-porphyrins. The positive shift of the first AuP-based potential (ΔV = +0.23) indicates the enhanced electron-accepting character of AuP within 1, most likely due to the cationic accumulation in the isolated cavity. This character was supported by DFT-based vertical electron affinity (VEA) calculations, whose values are 77.6, 80.4, and 81.9 kcal/mol for the AuP monomer, stacked dimer, and stacked trimer, respectively (Table S1), in the order of low to high electron-accepting abilities. In addition, the redox reversibility of 1·(AuP) n was higher than that of free AuP and maintained at least for three cycles without apparent change (Figure S41b). The electrochemical performance of the cationic species was thus improved within the capsule via efficient accumulation.

Co-Accumulation with Fluorinated Cationic Porphyrins

The present capsule-induced accumulation method was applied to two kinds of cationic porphyrins, such as AuP and its fluorinated analog AuP F (Figure a, top). A mixture of AuP and AuP F (0.5 and 0.25 equiv. based on AA, respectively) was stirred in an aqueous solution of 1 (1.0 mM based on AA) at 70 °C for 1 h (Figure a). The centrifugation and filtration of the resultant suspension gave rise to a clear red solution containing ternary host–guest composite 1·(AuP) n ·(AuP F ) m . In a manner similar to 1·(AuP) n , the selective co-accumulation of AuP and AuP F in the cavity with their random order was clarified by the combined analytical studies. Besides the UV–visible spectral changes (Figure S42), the 1H NMR spectrum of 1·(AuP) n ·(AuP F ) m at 70 °C showed new aromatic signals derived from 1, AuP, and AuP F at 4.39–6.25, 6.67–7.31, and 8.30 ppm, respectively (Figure e). These proton signals were inconsistent with those of AuP F (in DMSO-d 6), 1·(AuP F ) n , and 1·(AuP) n under the same conditions (Figure b–d). The large and small chemical shifts of proton H A’ based on free AuP F indicated the direct/indirect anthracene-based shielding effect toward AuP F within 1 and 1·(AuP) n (Figure c and e), respectively. The 1H, 19F, NOESY, DOSY NMR, and DLS analyses also supported the ternary composite formation (n = 2 and m = 1 on average) through the one-pot stirring protocol (Figures f and S43–S58).

Co-Accumulation with Nonionic Polyaromatic Compounds

Finally, the unusual co-accumulation of cationic and nonionic polyaromatic compounds was efficiently achieved using adaptable capsule 1 in water through a stepwise grinding-stirring protocol. Using Cu­(II)-phthalocyanine (CuPc) as a nonionic representative of the porphyrin family, an aqueous solution of host–guest composite 1·(CuPc) n was prepared from AA (2.0 μmol), CuPc (1.0 μmol), and water (2.0 mL; Figure a), via the grinding protocol in the same manner as 1·(AuP) n (Figure b). When the resultant clear blue solution (1.0 mL) was subjected to the stirring protocol with AuP (0.5 μmol, 0.5 equiv. based on AA) at room temperature for 1 d, host–guest composite 1·(CuPc) n ·(AuP) m was obtained as a clear dark green solution with high efficiency and selectivity (Figure b). The new ternary structure was elucidated by the combination of UV–visible, NMR, DLS, and molecular modeling studies (Figures c–5e and S60–S69). Whereas the UV–visible spectrum of precursor 1·(CuPc) n showed absorption bands at 607 and 688 nm derived from encapsulated (CuPc) n (blue line, Figure c), that of 1·(CuPc) n ·(AuP) m exhibited additional bands at 413 and 529 nm from co-encapsulated (AuP) m , accompanying the CuPc-based bands with nearly the same intensity (red line, Figure c).

The DLS analysis of the product indicated that the size of 1·(CuPc) n ·(AuP) m is larger than that of 1·(CuPc) n (d = 2.5 and 1.9 nm, respectively; Figure d). The UV–visible and DLS data of 1·(CuPc) n were consistent with those of (AA)6·(CuPc)2 reported previously. On the basis of these results and the 1H NMR integral-based ratio (6:3 AA/AuP; Figure S67), molecular modeling studies suggested the formation of a spherical (AA)6 capsule, accommodating a (CuPc)2·(AuP)3 core, with a core–shell diameter of 2.5 nm (Figure e), as a major product. The minor shifts of the CuPc-based absorption bands at ∼600 nm after the co-accumulation indicated heterogeneous (CuPc)2 and (AuP)3 accumulation rather than homogeneous one in the cavity.

The present stepwise protocol is essential for the efficient co-accumulation as well as the preparation of other cationic-nonionic host–guest composites in water. The one-pot stirring of three components, 1, CuPc, and AuP, afforded a mixture of host–guest composites in water with poor efficiency (up to 0.2-fold based on CuPc; Figure S70), relative to those of the stepwise protocol. A similar host–guest mixture was obtained with moderate efficiency (up to 0.7-fold based on AuP) from the one-pot grinding of the three components. In a similar manner to 1·(CuPc) n ·(AuP) m , the combination of AuP with nonionic perylene (Per) and fullerene C60 (C 60 ) gave rise to cationic/nonionic host–guest composites 1·(Per) n ·(AuP) m and 1·(C 60 ) n ·(AuP) m , respectively (Figures f, g, and S71–S78). The formation of the ternary composites from 1·(Per) n and 1·(C 60 ) n was indicated by the UV–visible, NMR, DLS, and molecular modeling studies. In the UV–visible spectra, the intense absorption band of AuP additionally appeared at ∼410 nm, besides the bands of encapsulated (Per) n at 400–500 nm (Figure f) and (C 60 ) n at 400–550 nm (Figure g) remaining nearly intact, indicating successful co-accumulation in the cavity. The DLS-based size analysis and1H NMR-based composition ratio studies suggested the formation of (AA)4·(Per)4·(AuP)2 and (AA)5·C 60 ·(AuP)2 as major co-accumulated structures (Figures S72-74, S77, and S78).

Conclusion

We have accomplished the accumulation and co-accumulation of multiple cationic porphyrins within a polyaromatic capsule in water. Cationic Au­(III)-tetraphenylporphyrins themselves were hardly assembled and accumulated in homogeneous solution, unlike common Zn­(II) derivative with a nonionic polyaromatic framework, owing to repulsive electrostatic interactions. In contrast, the simple mixing of the capsule, composed of bent polyaromatic amphiphiles, with the Au­(III)-porphyrins led to the selective formation of a host–guest composite in water. The detailed experimental and theoretical analyses revealed the triple accumulation of the porphyrin cations within the capsule as a major product through effective host–guest π–π and CH−π interactions, with dispersion force-based stabilization between the cations. The co-accumulation of the cationic porphyrins with its fluorinated ones as well as nonionic polyaromatic compounds was also demonstrated in the cavity, through a simple stirring and stepwise grinding-stirring protocol, respectively. On the basis of the present method and achievements, we will further develop co-accumulating structures, not only from multiple cations /anions and nonionic compounds but also from multiple cations and anions, with unique physicochemical properties for novel molecular multi-ionic materials.

Regarding potential future applications, this system contributes to the development of novel supramolecular electronic modules. The present electrochemical studies indicated that the multiaccumulated Au­(III)-porphyrin cations provide enhanced electron-accepting abilities, which are likely to be tuned through co-accumulation. The high stability of the accumulated cations in dilute solutions and the solid state on surfaces, as confirmed by the experimental studies, encourages their broad processing. The use of photoresponsive polyaromatic capsules might further facilitate the release of the accumulated cations onto electrodes without any additives or changes in conditions. Such supramolecular electronics will become an exciting field of nanoscience.
