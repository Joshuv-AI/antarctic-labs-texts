How much do we know about the parasites of great white sharks (Carcharodon carcharias) and why they matter?
Authors: Shokoofeh Shamsi, Diane P. Barton
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
There is growing recognition that excluding parasites from ecological and behavioural studies is a significant oversight, given their crucial roles in host health, energy allocation, and ecosystem function. Yet, the parasitic fauna of the great white shark (Carcharodon carcharias), a globally distributed apex predator, remains poorly understood. This review consolidates current knowledge of parasites infecting the great white shark and explores their potential ecological and physiological impacts. A synthesis of published literature and selected museum records revealed 116 parasite records, predominantly cestodes and copepods, with almost all studies being taxonomic in focus. Cestodes, known for their high reproductive output and metabolic demands, may influence growth, reproduction, and energy allocation, in their host, including great white sharks. Additionally, given that great white sharks acquire cestodes via prey infected with larval or infectious stages, parasite-induced changes in prey behaviour or vulnerability could bias dietary studies, an aspect largely overlooked in great white shark trophic ecology. Copepods, another frequently reported group, are known to cause tissue damage, respiratory inefficiency, and erratic swimming in fish, often requiring veterinary intervention in aquaculture. In sharks, behaviours such as breaching and tail-slapping have been speculatively linked to parasite removal, yet these assumptions overlook copepod biology and attachment mechanisms. Unsubstantiated interpretations risk perpetuating misconceptions and may hinder integration of parasitology into shark conservation strategies. This review also highlights critical knowledge gaps. For instance, several prey species consumed by great white sharks are known intermediate hosts of Toxoplasma gondii, a parasite capable of altering host behaviour and transmitting between intermediate hosts. Although unprovoked aggression has occasionally been reported in sharks, there are currently no data on the presence or potential effects of T. gondii in these apex predators as a potential intermediate/paratenic host. Clarifying such potential associations, alongside neglected areas such as embryonic infections (e.g., trebiid copepods), remains an important direction for future research. By identifying these critical knowledge gaps, we advocate for the integration of parasitological research into ecological and conservation frameworks to better understand apex predator health and ensure marine biodiversity protection.

Introduction

The great white shark, Carcharodon carcharias (Linnaeus, 1758), is a globally distributed apex predator with a critical role in maintaining the balance of marine ecosystems (Martin et al., 2005; Froese and Pauly, 2023). Despite their ecological significance, populations are declining due to a combination of factors, including overfishing, bycatch, habitat loss, climate change, and targeted killings (Myers et al., 2007), leading to great white sharks being listed as Vulnerable by the International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN) (Rigby et al., 2019; Froese and Pauly, 2023). Conservation efforts have focused on protective measures such as hunting bans, marine protected areas, and international agreements like the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species (CITES), which regulates their trade (Compagno, 1991; Haneman and Glazer, 1993; Compagno et al., 1997). However, limited information is available on aspects of the health and disease status of great white sharks, including the impact of parasites (Randhawa, 2011).

Parasites are fundamental, yet often underappreciated, components of marine ecosystems, influencing host health, behaviour, and population dynamics (Lafferty et al., 2008). They are ubiquitous across all ecosystems, both in terms of species richness (Dobson et al., 2008) and total biomass (Kuris et al., 2008). Beyond their direct effects on hosts, parasites contribute to the maintenance of ecosystem function through complex direct and indirect interactions with free-living organisms (Preston et al., 2016; Lymbery and Smit, 2023). Recognising these ecological services underscores their integral positive role in biodiversity and ecosystem stability, rather than viewing them solely as agents of disease (Lymbery and Smit, 2023). Parasites populations are changing overtime (Shamsi, 2021) with some studies suggesting that populations of particular marine parasites have increased under changing environmental conditions (e.g., Fiorenza et al., 2020), highlighting their potential role in host mortality and ecological dynamics. Growing evidence from diverse marine hosts shows that parasites can act as important drivers of acute disease outbreaks. For instance, a protozoan parasite, Miamiensis avidus was identified as the causative agent of meningoencephalitis responsible for mass mortality events in leopard sharks (Triakis semifasciata) in San Francisco Bay (Retallack et al., 2019). Despite such evidence from other species, the potential role of parasites in the health and behaviour of great white sharks has received little attention.

Parasites also can modify host behaviour (Poulin, 1995, 2013; Ruehle and Poulin, 2021), often to enhance their own transmission and survival. Changes in mating capacity, foraging ability, and levels of aggression have been documented in various hosts due to parasitic infections (Poulin, 1994, 2013; Demandt et al., 2020; Rogers et al., 2020; Shamsi et al., 2021, 2023; Freire et al., 2024). Although great white sharks exhibit complex behaviours, including migratory patterns, social interactions, and opportunistic feeding strategies (Martin et al., 2005; Bruce et al., 2006; Weng et al., 2007; Domeier and Nasby-Lucas, 2008; Fallows et al., 2013; Williams et al., 2022), no studies have explored the possible influence of parasites on these behaviours.

Research on parasites in great white sharks is difficult due to the logistics of access to host specimens, but knowledge of the parasite fauna could provide valuable insights into their health, migration patterns, and trophic interactions, as well as their role in broader ecosystem dynamics. These apex predators, with their long lifespans and migratory nature, are likely hosts to a wide range of parasites, including protozoans, helminths, crustaceans, and copepods. In addition, community attitudes toward sharks, ranging from fear and negative stereotyping to admiration and conservation concern (Philpott, 2002; Macdonald et al., 2023), can influence research priorities and conservation strategies. Understanding the parasitic fauna of great white sharks is therefore not only crucial for assessing their ecological health, but also for situating parasitology within broader conservation and societal contexts.

The main goal of this review is to consolidate the current knowledge of the parasites of great white sharks, identifying gaps in understanding, and exploring the ecological and conservation relevance of these host-parasite relationships. By advancing parasitological research in this iconic marine species, we aim to contribute to its conservation and the health of marine ecosystems.

Methodology

To compile a comprehensive list of parasites reported from the great white shark, a search was conducted across multiple data sources, including Web of Science, Scopus, PubMed, and Google Scholar. In addition, relevant museum databases (Australian Museum, South Australian Museum, and the USNM Parasitology Collection) were consulted to maximise coverage. The methodology involved combining published and unpublished records, as well as cross-referencing sources to minimize omissions, and removing duplicate entries where the same specimen or record appeared in both published reports and museum databases. Only literature published in English was reviewed and included in the dataset. Below are steps followed:

A combination of search terms was used to maximise retrieval, including “Carcharodon carcharias,” “parasite,” “parasit∗,” “helminth,” “protist∗,” “crustac∗,” “monogen∗,” “digen∗,” “nemat∗,” “cestod∗,” and “acanthocephal∗.” Additionally, Google Scholar was utilised with the same search terms to capture potentially relevant publications. Articles were thoroughly reviewed, and additional relevant studies cited within these articles, which were not initially retrieved through the databases, were identified. These supplementary articles were sourced via the Charles Sturt University Library and included in the dataset.

Unpublished Data: Data from unpublished sources were also incorporated to ensure completeness. These included records from the following collections: Ecology and Evolutionary Biology (EEB) Biodiversity Research Collections and Australian Helminthological Collection. The United States National Parasite Collection, housed at the Smithsonian National Museum of Natural History (https://collections.nmnh.si.edu/search/iz/) was also searched using “Carcharodon carcharias” as the host search term. All information was systematically reviewed and added to this article to create a global inventory of parasites reported from great white sharks. The nomenclature of all parasite taxa was updated and verified using the World Register of Marine Species (WoRMS; https://www.marinespecies.org/accessed 08/11/2025).

Microsoft Excel was used to generate the graph illustrating the number of records for each parasite group. The geographical map showing the distribution of parasite records was created by the Spatial Data Analysis Network (SPAN) at Charles Sturt University.

Discussion

This review reveals that, despite the global distribution of the great white shark, its parasitic fauna has been studied only sporadically. The present study provides an overview of the current state of knowledge and highlights important gaps, particularly parasites that remain unreported or unstudied. The following section considers the importance of the reported parasites.

Cestoda: Despite their ecological significance, our understanding of tapeworm species in great white sharks remains incomplete, with only a few species documented to date (Linton, 1922; Love and Moser, 1983; Dailey and Vogelbein, 1990; Randhawa, 2011; Caira et al., 2020). Expanding this knowledge is essential for both parasitological research and the conservation management of this iconic apex predator. All existing reports of cestodes in great white sharks are primarily taxonomic in nature. While such reports are crucial as they establish the foundational knowledge of species diversity and their classification, the most critical step toward understanding their ecological and biological roles, it appears there is little attempt among researchers or conservation authorities to build upon these findings. Specifically, there is a notable absence of studies reporting the prevalence, intensity, or health impacts of these cestodes on great white shark populations. The overall lack of research on cestodes in great white sharks contrasts sharply with the substantial body of work available for other animals, such as carp, cattle, humans, and even other shark species (for example see Carniado and González, 2013). For instance, a simple search of the Web of Science database (accessed 22 December 2024) returned 11,017 publications on Echinococcus granulosus, a tapeworm species infecting dogs and other hosts, reflecting the disproportionate attention given to taxa of veterinary or zoonotic importance.

Cestodes have an indirect life cycles (Taylor et al., 2015). Each life cycle stage uses a host at a different trophic level. The intermediate hosts for the immature stages of the cestodes for which great white sharks serve as definitive hosts usually include crustaceans, cephalopods, small fishes, or cetaceans. The adult stages, however, reside in predators like great white sharks, which serve as definitive hosts. Cestodes of elasmobranchs appear to exhibit high levels of host specificity (Caira et al., 2014), thus improvements in molecular study of cestode larval stages will enable postulation of food chain linkages (Randhawa, 2011).

Shark tapeworms can range in size from a few millimetres to over 1 m in length (Palm, 2004) and can occur in abundant number. For example, Randhawa (2011) reported 2536 tapeworms from the spiral valve of a single great white shark. Cestodes exhibit remarkable reproductive capacity within their definitive hosts, which demands substantial energetic resources. As parasites that rely entirely on their hosts for nourishment (Roberts et al., 2013), the energetic demand of their reproduction is a burden borne entirely by the host. This raises critical questions about the physiological cost to great white sharks and the potential long-term impacts on their health and fitness at individual and population levels. Given the scarcity of research on the prevalence and health impacts of cestodes in great white sharks, this gap becomes particularly concerning when viewed alongside their importance as apex predators in marine ecosystems. Could parasitic infections impair their growth, energy allocation, and reproductive success, ultimately influencing their ecological roles? Could the energy diverted to sustaining high fecundity in cestodes compromise the shark's energy reserves for essential activities such as hunting, or reproduction? Addressing these questions must be done without jeopardising the conservation status of great white sharks. Non-lethal approaches offer promising avenues, including the examination of stranded or incidentally caught individuals, collection of faecal material or regurgitates, and the application of molecular methods such as eDNA and metabarcoding to detect parasite DNA from water samples or host-derived materials.

Cestodes are significant not only for their health impacts on great white sharks but also, like many parasites, for their potential to influence predator–prey interactions (Timi and Poulin, 2020) and our interpretation of dietary patterns of the sharks. Parasites impose energetic costs, affect host physiology and behaviour, and can increase mortality or reduce fitness in infected individuals (Minchella and Scott, 1991). These effects may alter prey vulnerability, thereby shaping predator feeding ecology and food-web dynamics (Mouritsen and Poulin, 2005a, 2005b; Hatcher et al., 2014). In this context, ‘parasite-boosted predation’ (as termed by Timi and Poulin, 2020) does not negate active prey selection by predators; rather, it modifies the conditions under which that selection can occur. Great white sharks, as active hunters, will choose their prey, but the relative abundance, health, or behaviour of parasitized individuals may make certain animals more likely to be predated upon. Consequently, the consumption of parasitized prey may reflect a natural component of ecosystem functioning, contributing simultaneously to predator nutrition, parasite transmission, and energy flow through the food web (Hudson et al., 2006; Johnson et al., 2010; DL Preston et al., 2013). If parasitized prey is disproportionately represented in the diet of great white sharks due to altered behaviour or physical incapacity, it may distort our perception of their natural prey preferences. The possibility that higher frequency of parasitized fish, cephalopods, or other prey in the stomach contents of great white sharks could reflect prey vulnerability rather than active dietary selection has been overlooked by researchers. This highlights the need for caution when interpreting dietary studies of apex predators, where both behavioural ecology and parasite infection dynamics may obscure genuine ecological relationships, which should be interpreted within an integrated ecological framework.

Copepoda: Our results show that compared to cestodes, copepods have been reported more frequently in great white sharks, likely due to their external location, which allows for easier observation and non-invasive collection methods. The great white shark hosts several parasitic copepods, although little is known about their pathogenic effects. However, studies on other shark species can provide insights into the potential impacts of copepod infections on great white sharks. For instance, copepods of the genus Achtheinus are known to attach to fins, gill slits, and areas around the nostrils of infected sharks (Dippenaar and Jordaan, 2015). These copepods insert their antennae deep into the dermis of the shark's skin and use their maxillipeds to anchor themselves to the placoid scales covering the shark's skin. This anchoring, particularly in heavy infestations, can cause significant damage to the infected tissue with subsequent impact on shark's health and behaviour.

Among the parasites listed in Table 1, the parasitic copepod Anthosoma crissum, a parasite reported from a variety of shark hosts from all over the world, is commonly found attached to the jaw and gill arches of its hosts, where it causes lesions feeding on blood and host cells (Benz et al., 2002). Heavy infestations can result in significant pathological effects, including loss of scales and teeth (Barnard, 1955b), as well as deep inflammation that may extend into nervous tissue, potentially leading to host morbidity or even mortality (Benz et al., 2002).

Another example involves Nemesis lamna, a copepod reported to cause gill tissue proliferation in shortfin makos (Isurus oxyrinchus). Benz (1980) documented infections with an average of 12 (±4) mainly female Nemesis lamna per host, leading to tissue proliferation of such magnitude that respiratory efficiency was likely compromised. Benz (1980) also mentioned about repeated infestations resulting in cumulative and irreversible branchial tissue damage, which can have dire hydrodynamic consequences for the host shark. Other members of the genus Nemesis have been associated with similar pathologies, including swollen and pale gill filaments, further emphasizing their potential to significantly impact the health of infected sharks (Cressey, 1967c).

Copepods, as external parasites, infest various areas of the shark's body, including the skin, fins, gill slits, mouth, surface of the eyes, cloaca and claspers of their hosts. The presence of high number of copepods on these areas may have notable consequences on the function of these organs. For instance, great white sharks, which are powerful swimmers capable of reaching speeds of 50 kph (35 mph), may experience reduced respiratory ion exchange due to heavy copepod infestations, as reported in other sharks (Benz and Adamson, 1990). Such impairments could hinder the shark's overall efficiency in swimming and hunting. Severe ocular lesions caused by copepod infections have been documented in various shark species (Benz and Adamson, 1990) with impact on its functionality.

External parasites like copepods are equipped with rigid structures like hooks, spikes, or dents, which cause significant irritation to their hosts causing an abnormal behaviour known as flashing. Flashing and death due to infection with copepods such as those belonging to the genus Lethrinus has been reported in aquarium held sharks (Kik et al., 2011). These observations suggest that similar disruptions to natural behaviour and energetics may occur. In the great white shark, the copepod Nemesis lamna has been reported with a prevalence of 100 % and a mean intensity of 74 individuals per shark (Dippenaar et al., 2008). This high prevalence highlights the importance of exploring the potential health and behavioural impacts of copepod infections. While breaches and tail slapping are well-documented behaviours in the great white shark (Klimley et al., 2024), there is currently no direct evidence linking these behaviours to parasite irritation. Although direct observations in the great white shark are lacking, such cases (see Kik et al., 2011) indicate that copepod infections can have serious consequences for shark health and behaviour. Recognising and testing such hypotheses could offer valuable insights into the interplay between parasitism, behaviour, and energetics in great white sharks, with important implications for conservation planning.

The hypothesis that breaches or tail slapping might serve to remove parasites (Klimley et al., 2024) is problematic. Copepods are equipped with robust attachment organs that make them difficult to dislodge once affixed to their hosts. Even in controlled aquaculture environments, parasitized aquatic animals require prolonged treatments to either kill the parasites or cause them to release their grip (e.g., Aldrin et al., 2023). Unless evidence is provided, suggesting that these natural shark behaviours effectively remove parasites is misleading and risks promoting the misconception among policymakers that these behaviours negate the need for intervention. Such assumptions could contribute to the ongoing neglect of parasitological research and health considerations in the conservation strategies for great white sharks. Addressing this oversight is crucial for ensuring that conservation efforts are based on accurate scientific understanding rather than anecdotal interpretations. Investigating whether parasite-induced irritation contributes to breaches, tail slapping, or other behavioural displays in great white sharks could provide valuable insights into the interplay between parasitism and predator behaviour, as well as its implications for shark ecology and energetics.

Unreported and potential parasites of the great white shark

The great white shark is a long-lived apex predator, capable of surviving up to 36 years, with a global presence in diverse environments and a varied diet that includes fish, elasmobranchs, marine mammals, seabirds, and even carrion (Rigby et al., 2019; Froese and Pauly, 2023). Ontogenetic dietary shifts have been observed, with juveniles primarily feeding on fish and rays, while adults incorporate marine mammals such as cetaceans (Cliff et al., 1989; Bruce, 1992; Cortés, 1999; Hussey et al., 2012). Despite this dietary and ecological diversity, the limited reports of internal parasites in the great white shark, restricted to cestodes as shown in the present study, are interesting compared to the broader diversity of helminths reported in other shark species, and highlight the need to investigate potential unreported parasites in this host.

Helminths, such as nematodes, trematodes, and acanthocephalans, are well-documented in other shark species (Bullard et al., 2006; Weaver and Smales, 2014; Shamsi et al., 2019a, 2020), yet remain unreported in the great white shark. Chapter 18 of Biology of Sharks and Their Relatives (Caira and Healy, 2004) provides an overview of parasites recorded in elasmobranchs, including copepods, monogeneans, cestodes, nematodes, and other helminths, with examples drawn from a wide range of shark species. These parasites are reported to affect various organ systems, from external attachment on gills and fins (copepods and monogeneans) to internal infections of the gastrointestinal tract (cestodes and nematodes). The chapter emphasises that parasites can have significant effects on host physiology, nutrition, and behaviour, while also serving as important indicators of shark diet, trophic interactions, and movement patterns. Notably, despite its comprehensive scope, the chapter does not list any parasites from the great white shark (C. carcharias). This absence is striking given the shark's global distribution, longevity, and diverse diet, which should make it a suitable host for a range of parasite taxa. The lack of records in such a standard reference work highlights the paucity of parasitological research on this species and underscores the need for targeted surveys. Recognising these gaps is important not only for parasitology but also for conservation, as understanding the parasite fauna of apex predators contributes to broader assessments of marine ecosystem health.

The absence of other helminths the in great white shark reports most likely reflects the limited scope of parasitological investigations rather than true absence. Identifying these parasites is crucial, as their presence could have significant implications for host health, nutrient absorption, and energy allocation and also could provide insights into their health challenges and their role in marine parasite transmission networks. For example, nematodes with indirect life cycles are among the common helminths in several shark and ray species (Shamsi et al., 2019a, b, 2020), and the lack of records in great white sharks likely most likely reflects insufficient surveys. Digenean trematodes have complex life cycles involving several intermediate hosts and are the third major group occurring in sharks (Caira and Healy, 2004). Moreover some are known to occur in sites more typical of ectoparasites (Bray and Cribb, 2003), therefore, one might expect them to be more easily detected during shark examinations. Their absence in reports from great white sharks may therefore point to genuine host–parasite incompatibility or ecological barriers, rather than simply a lack of observation. This contrast further emphasises the need to investigate why some parasite groups are consistently “missing” from great white sharks compared to other elasmobranchs. Acanthocephala seems to be among the least found parasitic helminths in sharks followed by annelids (Caira and Healy, 2004). This rarity is thought to reflect physiological incompatibility between acanthocephalans and elasmobranchs (Smales et al., 2019), as these hosts maintain high concentrations of urea in their tissues and intestinal fluids for osmoregulation. Such conditions are likely inimical to acanthocephalans, which cannot tolerate elevated urea levels (Williams et al., 1970; Cislo and Caira, 1993; Smales et al., 2019). Consequently, infections observed in marine or estuarine elasmobranchs are probably accidental, whereas acanthocephalan infections may establish more readily in freshwater species that rely less on urea for osmoregulation (Tricas et al., 1997). However, these assumptions remain largely untested, and further investigation into the physiological and ecological mechanisms limiting acanthocephalan infections in elasmobranchs is warranted.

Understanding these “missing” groups from great white shark parasite list is important because their presence could have significant implications for host health, nutrient absorption, and energy allocation. Moreover, parasites can provide valuable insights into host diet, habitat use, and ecological interactions, offering indirect windows into great white shark biology that are otherwise difficult to study. Sharks, and particularly great whites, are often viewed through a lens of fear or fascination, which can overshadow consideration of their health and disease ecology. Yet recognising the role of parasites in sharks is essential, not only for understanding host health but also for conservation. As apex predators, great whites influence food web structure and ecosystem balance, and overlooking parasitic disease in their populations risks neglecting a key element of marine biodiversity and ecosystem health.

Notably, historical observations hint at additional biotic associates that have received almost no attention. In a footnote, Hewitt (1967) remarked that “many of these specimens were heavily infested with the thecate hydrozoan Phialella quadrata”. While hydrozoans are epibionts rather than parasites, their presence could plausibly influence hydrodynamics, abrasion, or local tissue responses. Such records underscore how little systematic effort has been devoted to documenting the full community of symbionts, parasites and epibionts associated with great whites, and how much information may be sitting unexamined in field notes, museum accessions and grey literature.

Another parasite of interest in marine ecosystems is Toxoplasma gondii, a globally distributed protozoan capable of infecting a wide range of cold and warm blooded animals, including marine mammals and fish (Wallace, 1972; Hill and Dubey, 2002; Massie et al., 2010). Its complex life cycle involves felids as only known definitive hosts, which shed oocysts into the environment. These oocysts can be ingested by a variety of intermediate/paratenic hosts, often leading to behavioural alterations such as reduced fear responses, increased boldness, and enhanced risk-taking and even promote aggressive tendencies in intermediate hosts, such as rodents and humans (Berdoy et al., 2000; House, 2014). Intriguingly, T. gondii has been identified in several marine animals at the top of the food chain, still as intermediate/paratenic hosts, including sea otters and dolphins (Cruickshank et al., 1990; Conrad et al., 2005; Chen et al., 2025) and also in fishes (Zhang et al., 2014; Marino et al., 2019; Merks et al., 2024), suggesting that marine transmission route exists. For example, Miller et al. (2020) found that sea otters bitten by great white sharks were frequently infected with T. gondii and other sarcocystids, raising the possibility that infected prey may be more vulnerable to shark predation. Interestingly, their Figure 3 shows that shark bite incidents were more prevalent in regions with higher T. gondii infection rates. While the authors did not discuss this pattern, it raises intriguing questions about whether infections in otters may increase their vulnerability to shark predation, and whether sharks feeding on infected prey could themselves be exposed. Emerging evidence that T. gondii can alter behaviour in its hosts suggests a more nuanced ecological dynamic, whereby infections may increase risk-taking and boldness in prey such as sea otters while potentially heightening aggression or altering responsiveness in predators such as sharks, thereby shaping predator–prey interactions in subtle but consequential ways. Great white sharks could, like other apex predators such as cetaceans, act as incidental or intermediate hosts of T. gondii, through trophic exposure. If so, even subclinical infections could have physiological or behavioural effects with potential consequences for predator–prey dynamics. While this remains speculative, it underscores the importance of investigating the potential for T. gondii to influence apex marine predators and the ecological processes they mediate.

Another extraordinary parasitic interaction in sharks involves trebiid copepods, which have been observed parasitizing shark embryos within the uterus (Roberts et al., 2013). These copepods penetrate the uterine wall and attach to the surface of developing embryos, becoming endosymbiotic ectoparasites. While this phenomenon has been documented in other shark species, its absence in great white shark reports warrants further investigation. The physiological and developmental implications of such parasitism could be profound, potentially affecting embryo survival rates and fitness.

Ecosystem functions and services of parasites

Beyond their impacts on host health and behaviour, the parasites of great white sharks should also be viewed through the broader lens of their role in ecosystem function. Parasites can provide key ecosystem services by regulating host and/or prey populations, mediating energy transfer across trophic levels, and maintaining biodiversity and food-web structure (Lymbery and Smit, 2023). This reframes parasites from the negative perspective of being merely agents of disease to positive contributors integral to healthy marine systems. For a wide-ranging apex predator, such as the great white shark, parasite assemblages can also serve as indicators of ecosystem connectivity and condition, complementing conventional ecological monitoring focused on abundance or movement. Incorporating parasite diversity, prevalence, and trophic roles into conservation and management frameworks may therefore enhance understanding of both shark health and ecosystem resilience.

Limitations

Our synthesis is constrained by the age and accessibility of many records. A substantial proportion of reports are historical, and recent systematic surveys are rare. It is highly likely that additional data exist in museum collections (unpublished accessions, field notes, slide series) and in older or local publications that were difficult to locate or access within an unfunded project. Geographic and methodological biases in the available literature (e.g., opportunistic sampling of stranded/bycaught individuals, focus on conspicuous ectoparasites) further limit inference about true prevalence, intensity and impacts, particularly for internal helminths. These factors reinforce the need for renewed, standardised, and museum-enabled sampling coupled with modern molecular diagnostics.

Conclusion

This review highlights the significant gaps in our understanding of the parasitic fauna associated with great white sharks (Carcharodon carcharias), an apex predator critical to marine ecosystem balance. While reported parasites include cestodes and copepods, their prevalence, intensity, and impacts on shark health and behaviour remain underexplored. These knowledge gaps stand in stark contrast to the extensive research on parasites in other marine and terrestrial species, underscoring the need for focused parasitological studies on great white sharks. Addressing these gaps is essential for understanding the complex interactions between great white shark, its parasites, and the marine ecosystems they inhabit. Parasites not only influence host health and behaviour but also reflect broader ecosystem dynamics, including food web relationships and environmental health. Incorporating parasitological research into shark conservation strategies will provide a more comprehensive understanding of the challenges faced by this vulnerable species and help inform efforts to preserve the role of apex predators in maintaining healthy marine ecosystems. By advancing our knowledge of these interactions, we can better protect great white sharks and the marine ecosystems they help sustain. It is important to emphasise that deliberate euthanasia of great white sharks for parasitological research is neither feasible nor ethically acceptable. However, when opportunities arise to examine stranded, incidentally bycaught, or otherwise deceased sharks, it is critical that comprehensive parasitological surveys are undertaken. Such opportunistic sampling, coupled with advances in molecular diagnostics and the use of existing museum collections, offers a realistic and non-destructive pathway to improve our understanding of cestode prevalence, intensity, and impacts in this species.
