Crosslink augmentation in posterior fixation of unstable thoracic and thoracolumbar fractures: A comparative cohort study of mechanical failure and sagittal alignment
Authors: Jorge Tabilo, Filadelfo Feliciano Norambuena, Cristian Valdez, César Padilla, Alejandro Barria, Cristobal Bravo, Marcos Diaz, Andrei Fernandes Joaquim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:The value of crosslink augmentation in posterior thoracolumbar instrumentation for traumatic fractures remains uncertain. Although biomechanical studies suggest improved rotational stability, clinical evidence is limited.Objective:To compare mechanical failure and sagittal alignment in posterior pedicle-screw constructs with and without crosslink augmentation for traumatic thoracic, thoracolumbar, and lumbar fractures.Methods:A retrospective single-center comparative cohort study included patients treated between January 2018 and March 2026 for traumatic thoracic, thoracolumbar, or lumbar fractures. Patients were grouped according to crosslink use. The primary outcome was construct-related mechanical failure, including rod fracture, screw loosening, implant disconnection, crosslink failure, progressive loss of correction, or revision for deformity. Local kyphosis was measured preoperatively, postoperatively, and at final follow-up.Results:Twenty-eight patients were analyzed: 13 with crosslink augmentation and 15 without crosslinks. Crosslink constructs were longer, with more instrumented levels (4.9 vs. 3.9; P = 0.008) and screws (8.2 vs. 6.7; P = 0.013). Index-level screw omission was more frequent with crosslinks (61.5% vs. 20.0%; P = 0.051), as was neurological deficit (84.6% vs. 0%; P < 0.001). AO type B/C morphology and confirmed or suspected posterior ligamentous complex injury were similarly distributed. No mechanical failures occurred in the crosslink group, whereas one noncrosslink patient developed screw loosening associated with chronic infection/fistula. Sagittal differences were not statistically significant, although final kyphosis and late kyphotic progression numerically favored crosslink augmentation.Conclusion:Crosslink-augmented constructs showed no mechanical failures despite greater baseline complexity. These findings suggest potential mechanical benefit, but larger studies are required.

INTRODUCTION

Thoracolumbar fractures are clinically relevant injuries because the thoracolumbar junction lies between the rigid thoracic spine and the mobile lumbar spine, making it vulnerable to combined compression, flexion-distraction, and rotational forces. When instability is insufficiently controlled, these injuries may progress to posttraumatic kyphosis, persistent pain, sagittal imbalance, and functional impairment.[1] Modern classification systems have therefore moved beyond morphology alone and incorporate neurological status and posterior ligamentous complex (PLC) integrity as key determinants of instability and treatment strategy.[234]

Treatment selection remains controversial, particularly in burst fractures without neurological deficit and in morphologically complex injuries with variable instability. Comparative evidence has shown that not all thoracolumbar burst fractures benefit uniformly from surgery, emphasizing the need to identify truly unstable patterns.[56] Among surgically treated patients, loss of correction, implant failure, and posttraumatic kyphosis remain important unresolved concerns in selected high-risk subgroups.[7]

Posterior pedicle-screw instrumentation is widely used to restore alignment, stabilize the injured segment, and allow early mobilization. However, the optimal construct configuration remains debated. Posterolateral fusion has not consistently improved outcomes after short-segment fixation,[8] whereas construct optimization strategies such as longer fixation or fractured-level screw placement have been associated with improved radiographic correction, reduced loss of correction, and lower implant failure in selected cases.[91011]

The PLC functions as the posterior tension band of the thoracolumbar spine. Its disruption reduces resistance to flexion-distraction and rotational forces, increases the risk of progressive kyphosis, and places greater mechanical demand on the posterior implant. However, PLC assessment remains imperfect, as radiographic findings and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) do not always correlate directly with ligamentous injury.[1213] Because deformity progression depends not only on vertebral collapse but also on injury morphology, junctional location, posterior soft-tissue competence, and construct behavior, fixation strategies should be adapted to biomechanical risk rather than fracture morphology alone.[14]

Crosslinks, or transverse connectors, may provide a simple method of construct reinforcement. Biomechanical studies have shown that crosslinks increase coupling between bilateral rods and improve rotational stiffness, while their effect on flexion-extension, axial compression, and lateral bending is less pronounced.[1516] This is relevant because unstable thoracolumbar fractures, particularly those with PLC disruption, asymmetric fixation, pedicle compromise, long constructs, or omission of fractured-level screws, are often exposed to rotational and posterior tension-band failure forces.

Despite this biomechanical rationale, the clinical value of crosslink augmentation in traumatic thoracolumbar fixation remains insufficiently defined. The present study compares posterior pedicle-screw constructs with and without crosslink augmentation in traumatic thoracic, thoracolumbar, and lumbar fractures. We specifically analyze construct-related mechanical failure, sagittal angular behavior, and PLC integrity. We hypothesized that crosslink-augmented constructs would show reliable mechanical behavior in higher-risk fracture patterns and may demonstrate favorable trends in sagittal alignment maintenance when PLC disruption or incomplete segmental fixation increases construct demand.

METHODS

Study design and reporting framework

We conducted a retrospective, single-center comparative cohort study of patients surgically treated for traumatic thoracic, thoracolumbar, and lumbar fractures using posterior pedicle-screw instrumentation, with or without crosslink augmentation. The primary objective was to compare construct-related mechanical outcomes and maintenance of local sagittal alignment according to crosslink use, with particular emphasis on PLC integrity.

The study was reported according to the STROBE recommendations for observational studies.[17] Given the retrospective design, limited cohort size, and heterogeneity of traumatic fracture patterns, the analysis was considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating.

Patient selection

All consecutive patients treated surgically for traumatic spinal fractures during the study period from January 2018 to March 2026 were screened from the institutional operative database, electronic medical records, operative reports, and imaging archives.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: acute traumatic thoracic, thoracolumbar, or lumbar fracture; treated with posterior pedicle-based instrumentation performed at the index surgery; available preoperative imaging sufficient to classify fracture morphology and assess PLC status; and available postoperative clinical and/or radiographic follow-up.

Patients were excluded if they had cervical-only injuries, nontraumatic spinal pathology, isolated decompression without instrumentation, revision surgery for nontraumatic disease, or insufficient documentation to determine fracture morphology, construct configuration, or postoperative evolution.

Patients were divided into two groups according to the index construct: Group 1 – posterior instrumentation with crosslink augmentation and Group 2 – posterior instrumentation without crosslink augmentation.

Crosslink exposure

Crosslink augmentation was defined as the placement of at least one transverse connector joining the bilateral longitudinal rods of the posterior construct. Crosslink use was analyzed as a construct-level variable rather than as an isolated intervention.

For patients in the crosslink group, the number and position of transverse connectors were recorded when available and categorized as cranial, mid-construct, or caudal. When documented, the operative rationale for crosslink use was extracted, including posterior element disruption, pedicle compromise, omission of index-level screws, asymmetric fixation, multisegmental instability, or the perceived need for additional rotational stability.

The choice of implanting a cross-link was based on the treating surgeon’s preferences.

Clinical and injury variables

Baseline variables included age, sex, body mass index, smoking status, osteoporosis, American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status, trauma mechanism, polytrauma, time from injury to surgery, and neurological status.

Neurological status was recorded using the American Spinal Injury Association Impairment Scale (AIS) when available, according to the International Standards for Neurological Classification of Spinal Cord Injury.[18] Postoperative neurological deterioration was defined as any new or worsened motor, sensory, or sphincter deficit after surgery.

Fractures were classified according to the AO Spine thoracolumbar injury classification system by two evaluators, and a third was consulted in case of nonconsensus.[19] PLC status was categorized as intact, suspected injury, or disrupted based on MRI, computed tomography, radiographic findings, and operative documentation. Because PLC integrity is a major determinant of thoracolumbar instability, confirmed and suspected PLC injuries were grouped for subgroup analysis.[220]

Surgical variables

Operative variables included surgical approach, number of instrumented levels, construct length, total number of pedicle screws, use or omission of screws at the fractured vertebra, crosslink use and position, decompression, cement augmentation, operative time, estimated blood loss, and transfusion requirement.

Construct length was defined as the number of vertebral levels included in the posterior instrumentation. Index-level screw placement was defined as insertion of one or more pedicle screws into the fractured vertebra. Omission of index-level screws was recorded when the fractured level was bypassed because of pedicle disruption, comminution, anatomical limitation, severe canal compromise, or surgeon preference.

Radiographic assessment

Radiographic variables were extracted from preoperative imaging, immediate postoperative studies when available, and the last available follow-up radiograph or computed tomography scan.

The principal radiographic parameter was local kyphosis at the injured segment. Preoperative local kyphosis was measured across the fractured vertebra or injured motion segment. Postoperative and final local kyphosis were measured on the earliest postoperative image and last available follow-up image, respectively.

Sagittal correction was defined as the difference between preoperative and postoperative local kyphosis. Loss of correction was defined as the difference between immediate postoperative and final follow-up local kyphosis. When paired measurements were unavailable, angular evolution was reported descriptively.

Additional radiographic variables included canal compromise, vertebral body height loss, fusion status, pseudoarthrosis, adjacent segment disease, screw loosening, rod fracture, implant disconnection, and crosslink integrity.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was construct-related mechanical failure, defined as rod fracture, screw loosening or pullout, implant disconnection, crosslink failure, progressive loss of correction attributable to construct insufficiency, or revision surgery for implant failure or progressive deformity.

Secondary outcomes included maintenance of sagittal alignment, postoperative neurological deterioration, infection, thromboembolic events, delirium, intensive care unit stay, hospital length of stay, discharge destination, readmission, reintervention, fusion status, pseudoarthrosis, adjacent segment disease, implant removal, and mortality.

Reinterventions were classified as mechanical or nonmechanical. Mechanical reintervention referred to surgery for implant failure, progressive deformity, pseudoarthrosis, or loss of correction. Nonmechanical reintervention included decompression for residual neural compression, staged anterior reconstruction, wound revision, or procedures unrelated to posterior construct failure.

Comparative and subgroup analyses

The main analysis compared baseline characteristics, fracture morphology, construct configuration, radiographic parameters, and outcomes between the crosslink and noncrosslink groups.

A predefined subgroup analysis was performed according to PLC status, because posterior tension-band disruption was considered the most relevant biomechanical marker of instability and a potential indication for transverse construct reinforcement. Additional exploratory comparisons were considered for high-risk construct features, including omission of index-level screws, long-segment fixation, pedicle compromise, asymmetric fixation, and high-energy trauma.

Missing data and statistical analysis

Missing data were not imputed. Patients with incomplete radiographic follow-up were retained in the clinical and mechanical outcome analysis but excluded from calculations requiring paired angular measurements. Denominators were reported according to available data for each variable.

Continuous variables were reported as mean with range or median with interquartile range, as appropriate. Categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages. Comparisons between groups were performed using Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables and the Mann–Whitney U-test for continuous variables when appropriate.

Because of the limited sample size and low number of mechanical events, P values were interpreted as exploratory. No multivariable regression was performed because the cohort was insufficient to support adjusted modeling without overfitting. Interpretation emphasized effect direction, clinical relevance, and biomechanical plausibility rather than isolated statistical significance.

RESULTS

Study cohort

A total of 33 surgically treated traumatic spinal fracture cases were screened from the institutional database. After exclusion of cervical-only injuries, 28 patients with thoracic, thoracolumbar, or lumbar traumatic fractures constituted the final analytical cohort. Thirteen patients (group 1) were treated with posterior instrumentation reinforced with at least one crosslink, whereas 15 patients underwent posterior instrumentation without crosslink augmentation (group 2).

The overall cohort had a mean age of 47.2 years and included patients treated from January 2018 to March 2026, with a predominance of high-energy trauma and a high burden of mechanically unstable injury patterns. Confirmed or suspected PLC injury was present in 17 patients, representing 60.7% of the cohort. The crosslink and noncrosslink groups had comparable age, AIS distribution, high-energy trauma rate, and PLC injury prevalence; however, crosslink-augmented constructs were more frequently used in patients with neurological deficit, open or combined approaches, longer constructs, greater screw density, and omission of index-level screws [Table 1].

Baseline characteristics

Baseline demographics were broadly balanced between groups. Mean age was 46.5 years in the crosslink group and 47.8 years in the noncrosslink group. The proportion of ASA III–IV patients was also similar between groups, affecting 38.5% of patients treated with crosslinks and 33.3% of those treated without crosslinks.

High-energy trauma was the dominant mechanism in both groups, accounting for 76.9% of cases in the crosslink group and 80.0% in the noncrosslink group. Polytrauma was numerically more frequent among patients treated with crosslink augmentation, although this difference did not reach statistical significance.

A major imbalance was observed in neurological status. Neurological deficit at presentation, defined as ASIA A–D, was present in 84.6% of patients in the crosslink group and in none of the patients in the noncrosslink group (<0.001). This suggests that crosslink augmentation was more frequently selected in clinically severe cases, rather than being randomly distributed across the cohort.

Injury morphology and posterior ligamentous complex integrity

Fracture morphology showed a high proportion of unstable patterns in the overall cohort [Table 2]. AO type B or C injuries were present in 19 of 28 patients (67.9%). In the crosslink group, AO type C injuries were observed in 5 of 13 patients (38.5%), AO type B injuries in 4 of 13 (30.8%), and AO type A injuries in 4 of 13 (30.8%). In the noncrosslink group, AO type B injuries were most frequent, occurring in 8 of 15 patients (53.3%), followed by AO type A injuries in 5 of 15 (33.3%) and AO type C injuries in 2 of 15 (13.3%). The overall AO distribution did not differ significantly between groups (P = 0.277). When unstable AO type B/C patterns were grouped, their frequency was similar between the crosslink and noncrosslink groups: 9 of 13 patients (69.2%) versus 10 of 15 patients (66.7%), respectively (P = 1.000).

Operative characteristics

Crosslink augmentation was associated with more demanding construct configurations [Table 3]. All patients in the crosslink group were treated through open or combined approaches, compared with 26.7% in the noncrosslink group. Mean construct length was greater in the crosslink group, with 4.9 instrumented levels compared with 3.9 levels in the noncrosslink group. The mean number of screws was also higher in the crosslink group.

Surgical variables according to crosslink use

Index-level screw omission was more frequent in crosslink-augmented constructs, occurring in 8 of 13 patients (61.5%) compared with 3 of 15 patients (20.0%) in the noncrosslink group. This difference approached, but did not reach, conventional statistical significance (P = 0.051). Clinically, this imbalance is relevant because omission of fractured-level screws may reduce segmental load sharing and increase the mechanical demand on the posterior construct, providing a plausible rationale for transverse reinforcement.

Operative time was longer in the crosslink group than in the noncrosslink group, with a mean duration of 282.8 min (range, 185–460) in 13 patients compared with 148.9 min (range, 75–325) in 15 patients, respectively (P < 0.001). This difference should not be interpreted as the isolated effect of crosslink placement. Rather, it likely reflects the greater surgical complexity of the crosslink group, including more frequent open or combined approaches, longer constructs, greater screw number, neurological involvement, and higher construct-related mechanical demand.

Construct-related mechanical outcomes

No rod fracture, screw loosening, implant disconnection, crosslink failure, or revision for progressive deformity was observed in the crosslink group during available follow-up. This finding is clinically relevant because crosslink augmentation was preferentially used in more demanding constructs, including longer fixation, greater screw number, omission of index-level screws, AO type B/C morphology, neurological deficit, posterior element compromise, and confirmed or suspected PLC injury.

In the noncrosslink group, no rod fracture was documented. One patient developed screw loosening in the setting of chronic infection/fistula and required additional surgical treatment. Because this occurred in association with infection, it was not classified as an isolated delayed mechanical collapse, although it remains a clinically relevant implant-related complication.

Radiographic angular analysis showed no statistically significant differences between groups for preoperative kyphosis, postoperative kyphosis, early correction, final kyphosis, late kyphotic progression, or net preoperative-to-final change. Nevertheless, the direction of effect consistently favored crosslink augmentation, with numerically lower final kyphosis, less late kyphotic progression, and better net maintenance of sagittal alignment. These findings should be interpreted as a clinically coherent but underpowered signal, rather than definitive evidence of superiority.

Taken together, crosslink-augmented constructs demonstrated a low implant-failure profile despite being used in higher-risk surgical and biomechanical scenarios.

Complications and reinterventions

Postoperative complications appeared to reflect the severity of the trauma population rather than a crosslink-specific morbidity pattern. In the crosslink group, two superficial wound infections were recorded, with no documented deep infection. Isolated systemic complications included one systemic infection, one thromboembolic event, and one episode of delirium. No postoperative neurological deterioration was observed.

Two patients in the crosslink group required reintervention. Neither was related to posterior construct failure: one patient underwent an anterior-stage procedure for residual anterior compression, and another required extension of decompression by laminectomy. No patient required reoperation for rod breakage, screw loosening, crosslink failure, progressive kyphosis, or loss of sagittal correction.

In the noncrosslink group, two reinterventions were identified, including one chronic fistula/deep infection associated with screw-related complications and one screw repositioning. These events were not classified as deformity-driven revisions. No mortality was attributed to implant failure or progressive spinal deformity in either group.

Posterior ligamentous complex subgroup interpretation

Confirmed or suspected PLC injury was frequent and remained the main biomechanical modifier of interest. Among crosslink-treated patients, PLC injury was associated with greater construct complexity, including unstable AO morphology, longer fixation, and frequent omission of index-level screws. Despite these risk factors, no crosslink-treated patient with confirmed or suspected PLC injury developed rod fracture, screw loosening, implant disconnection, crosslink failure, or revision for progressive deformity.

Although angular differences were not statistically significant, crosslink-treated patients showed numerically greater early correction, lower final kyphosis, less late kyphotic progression, and better net maintenance of sagittal alignment. This pattern is biomechanically plausible in PLC disruption, where posterior tension-band failure may increase rotational and flexion-distraction forces.

These findings do not prove that crosslinks independently prevented deformity progression. Rather, they suggest that crosslink augmentation was associated with reliable construct behavior and favorable nonsignificant radiographic trends in selected high-risk fractures.

Fusion, follow-up, and late events

Fusion assessment was limited by heterogeneous follow-up and incomplete long-term imaging. Among patients with available radiographs or computed tomography, no clear pseudoarthrosis requiring revision was identified in the crosslink group. Some patients demonstrated radiographic evidence of fusion, whereas others had incomplete or indeterminate assessment.

DISCUSSION

Principal findings

This retrospective comparative cohort evaluated the mechanical and radiographic behavior of posterior pedicle-screw constructs for traumatic thoracic, thoracolumbar, and lumbar fractures according to the use of crosslink augmentation. The principal finding was that crosslink-augmented constructs showed no rod fracture, screw loosening, implant disconnection, crosslink failure, or revision for progressive sagittal deformity during the available follow-up, despite being preferentially used in more complex and mechanically demanding scenarios.

Patients treated with crosslinks had longer constructs, greater screw number, more frequent omission of index-level screws, higher neurological severity, unstable AO type B/C morphology, posterior element compromise, and confirmed or suspected PLC injury. Therefore, the absence of mechanical failure in this subgroup is clinically relevant. However, because crosslinks were not randomly assigned, this finding should be interpreted as a signal of construct reliability in selected high-risk cases rather than definitive evidence of superiority.

The angular analysis did not identify statistically significant differences between crosslink and noncrosslink constructs for preoperative kyphosis, early postoperative kyphosis, early correction, final kyphosis, late kyphotic progression, or net preoperative-to-final change. Nevertheless, late radiographic trends consistently favored crosslink augmentation, including lower final kyphosis, less late kyphotic progression, and better net maintenance of sagittal alignment. These findings are biomechanically coherent but underpowered, and should therefore be considered hypothesis-generating.

Interpretation in the context of current evidence

The role of crosslinks in posterior spinal instrumentation remains debated. Transverse connectors were designed to increase the coupling between bilateral rod systems, particularly under rotational loads. However, clinical evidence supporting their routine use remains limited. A systematic review by Cornaz et al. concluded that crosslinks may improve construct stability under selected mechanical conditions, especially axial rotation, but that current evidence is insufficient to define universal indications for their use.[21]

Our findings align with this interpretation. The present cohort does not support routine crosslink placement in every thoracolumbar trauma construct. Instead, it supports a selective strategy in which crosslinks are considered when the posterior construct is exposed to higher mechanical demand, such as PLC disruption, AO type B/C morphology, omission of index-level screws, pedicle compromise, asymmetric fixation, posterior element disruption, or long-segment fixation.

This interpretation is also consistent with the proposed decision algorithm, which should be maintained as a figure. The figure is useful because it translates the study findings into a clinically applicable framework: crosslink augmentation is not presented as a universal implant accessory, but as a risk-adapted reinforcement for constructs with identifiable instability modifiers.

Posterior ligamentous complex injury and construct demand

PLC integrity is central to thoracolumbar trauma decision-making because it determines the competence of the posterior tension band. When the PLC is disrupted, the injured segment becomes more susceptible to flexion-distraction and rotational forces, increasing the mechanical demand placed on the posterior implant.

Recent meta-analytic evidence reinforces the importance of accurate PLC assessment. Aly et al. showed that MRI can modify thoracolumbar fracture classification and treatment decisions, mainly by identifying injuries that are upgraded from apparently compressive patterns to more unstable posterior tension-band injuries.[22] This is directly relevant to the present study, because confirmed or suspected PLC injury was frequent and represented the main biomechanical modifier of interest.

In crosslink-treated patients with confirmed or suspected PLC injury, no rod fracture, screw loosening, crosslink failure, implant disconnection, or revision for progressive deformity was observed. Although this does not prove that crosslinks independently prevented failure, it supports the concept that transverse reinforcement may be useful when posterior soft-tissue restraints are compromised, and the implant must compensate for increased rotational or flexion-distraction stress.

Sagittal alignment and angular behavior

Maintenance of sagittal alignment is one of the most relevant radiographic goals after fixation of unstable thoracolumbar fractures. In this cohort, baseline kyphosis was comparable between groups, which strengthens the interpretability of the angular comparison. Crosslink-treated patients showed numerically greater early correction, lower final kyphosis, less late kyphotic progression, and better net preoperative-to-final angular behavior.

None of these differences reached statistical significance. This likely reflects limited sample size, heterogeneous trauma patterns, incomplete long-term radiographic follow-up, and nonrandomized treatment allocation. However, the consistency of the direction of effect across several sagittal parameters is clinically meaningful. The correct interpretation is therefore cautious: crosslink augmentation was associated with favorable nonsignificant sagittal trends in a higher-risk construct population, providing a rationale for larger comparative studies with standardized imaging follow-up.

Construct configuration and index-level screw omission

Construct configuration is a major determinant of mechanical performance in thoracolumbar fracture fixation. The use of screws at the fractured vertebra has been associated with better radiological outcomes, less postoperative pain, and lower implant failure in systematic review and meta-analysis data, although at the cost of longer operative time and greater blood loss.[11]

This is relevant because omission of index-level screws was more frequent in the crosslink group. When the fractured vertebra is bypassed, load sharing through the injured level is reduced, potentially increasing stress across the rods and terminal screws. In this scenario, crosslink augmentation may be particularly rational because it increases transverse coupling of the construct and may reduce asymmetric or rotational deformation.

The absence of rod fracture, screw loosening, implant disconnection, and deformity-driven revision in the crosslink group is therefore notable. It suggests that transverse reinforcement may help maintain construct reliability when fractured-level fixation is not feasible or when posterior tension-band failure increases mechanical demand [Figure 1].

Suggested clinical decision algorithm for crosslink augmentation in thoracolumbar trauma

This algorithm illustrates a selective strategy for the use of crosslink augmentation in posterior thoracolumbar instrumentation. In patients with unstable fractures, the integrity of the PLC should be assessed as a primary determinant of mechanical stability. In cases of confirmed or suspected PLC disruption, further evaluation of construct-related risk factors is recommended, including absence of an index-level screw, pedicle compromise, asymmetric fixation, or multisegmental instability. When these high-risk features are present, crosslink augmentation may be considered to enhance construct rigidity and reduce the risk of mechanical failure. In contrast, in the absence of PLC disruption or high-risk construct characteristics, standard posterior fixation is typically sufficient, and routine crosslink use may not be necessary. This risk-adapted approach supports the selective, rather than systematic, use of crosslinks in thoracolumbar trauma.

This algorithm emphasizes that crosslink use should be risk-adapted rather than routine, targeting constructs with increased mechanical demand.

Complications and reinterventions

The complication profile did not suggest a specific morbidity signal attributable to crosslink use. In the crosslink group, two superficial wound infections were recorded, with no documented deep infection. Isolated systemic complications included one systemic infection, one thromboembolic event, and one episode of delirium. No postoperative neurological deterioration was observed.

Two patients in the crosslink group required reintervention. Neither was related to posterior construct failure: one underwent an anterior-stage procedure for residual anterior compression, and another required extension of decompression by laminectomy. No patient required revision for rod breakage, screw loosening, crosslink failure, progressive kyphosis, or loss of sagittal correction.

In the noncrosslink group, one patient developed screw loosening in the setting of chronic fistula/deep infection, and another underwent screw repositioning. These events were not classified as deformity-driven revisions, but they remain clinically relevant implant-related complications.

Clinical implications

The study supports a selective, risk-adapted role for crosslink augmentation in traumatic thoracic, thoracolumbar, and lumbar fixation. Crosslinks may be considered when one or more of the following factors are present: confirmed or suspected PLC injury, AO type B/C morphology, omission of index-level screws, pedicle compromise, asymmetric fixation, posterior element disruption, long constructs, or substantial baseline kyphotic deformity.

Conversely, routine crosslink placement in all posterior constructs is not supported. In lower-risk fractures with preserved PLC, symmetrical fixation, adequate screw density, and stable anterior column support, standard posterior fixation may be sufficient.

Limitations

This study has important limitations. Its retrospective design introduces selection bias, as crosslinks were likely used in cases perceived by the surgeon to be more unstable or mechanically demanding. The sample size was limited, reducing statistical power and increasing the risk of type II error. Radiographic follow-up was heterogeneous and incomplete, limiting definitive conclusions regarding late kyphotic progression and fusion. In addition, no multivariable adjustment was performed because the cohort size and number of events were insufficient to support reliable modeling.

Despite these limitations, the study provides clinically relevant real-world data from a high-risk trauma cohort. The absence of mechanical failure in the crosslink group, combined with favorable but nonsignificant angular trends, supports further investigation of transverse reinforcement in selected unstable thoracolumbar fractures.

CONCLUSION

In this retrospective comparative cohort, crosslink augmentation was preferentially used in more complex and mechanically demanding posterior constructs for traumatic thoracic, thoracolumbar, and lumbar fractures. Despite this higher-risk profile, no crosslink-treated patient developed rod fracture, screw loosening, implant disconnection, crosslink failure, or revision for progressive sagittal deformity during the available follow-up.

Although sagittal angular differences between crosslink and noncrosslink constructs did not reach statistical significance, late radiographic trends favored crosslink augmentation, including lower final kyphosis, less late kyphotic progression, and better net maintenance of sagittal alignment. These findings should therefore be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive evidence of superiority.

The main clinical implication is that crosslinks should not be considered routine accessories, but rather selective reinforcements in high-risk constructs, particularly when PLC disruption, omission of index-level screws, pedicle compromise, asymmetric fixation, or long-segment instrumentation increases mechanical demand. Larger prospective comparative studies with standardized radiographic follow-up are required to determine whether these favorable mechanical and sagittal trends translate into a measurable reduction in construct failure or posttraumatic deformity progression.
