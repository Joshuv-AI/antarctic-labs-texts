Unilateral biportal endoscopic decompression for two-level adjacent lumbar spinal stenosis through a single sliding approach: technical report and early follow-up
Authors: Shuang Xu, Heng Wu, Hao Liu, Junwei Xiang, Jin Yang, Chuanen Wang, Song Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTraditional unilateral biportal endoscopic (UBE) decompression for two-level adjacent lumbar spinal stenosis (LSS) often requires a third incision, potentially leading to increased blood loss, longer operative time, and greater reliance on fluoroscopy. To address these limitations, we propose a novel sliding approach UBE (S-UBE) for treating two-level adjacent LSS. This study presents the technique and its preliminary clinical outcomes.MethodsIn this retrospective study, 41 patients with two-level adjacent LSS were treated with S-UBE between April 2021 and October 2023. Treatment outcomes were evaluated based on operative time, blood loss, surgical complications, hospital stay duration, visual analog scale (VAS) scores, Oswestry Disability Index (ODI), and modified MacNab criteria. Radiological evaluations were performed at regular intervals during follow-up.ResultsAll patients successfully underwent surgery with the S-UBE technique. The mean operative time was 118.27 ± 11.79 min, average blood loss was 92.46 ± 11.75 ml, and the mean hospital stay was 5.20 ± 1.45 days. No major surgical complications occurred, though two patients experienced transient numbness and pain in their lower limbs, which recovered from conservative treatment. Postoperative VAS scores were significantly lower (P < 0.05), while ODI scores improved significantly compared to preoperative values (P < 0.05). The excellent-to-good outcome rate based on the modified MacNab criteria was 95.12%. Facet joint preservation rates were 84.76% at the L3-L4-L5 segment and 88.38% at the L4-L5-S1 segment. No cases of lumbar instability were observed during follow-up.ConclusionThe S-UBE technique is an effective alternative for treating two-level adjacent LSS, offering a minimally invasive solution with only two skin incisions. This approach demonstrates promising clinical outcomes, including reduced operative time, minimal blood loss, and high facet joint preservation rates.Keywords: Unilateral biportal endoscopy, Lumbar spinal stenosis, Surgical technique, Clinical outcomes

Introduction

Lumbar spinal stenosis (LSS) is a prevalent degenerative condition characterized by intervertebral disc herniation, hypertrophy of the ligamentum flavum, and degeneration of the facet joints. These pathological changes lead to narrowing of the spinal canal and compression of neurovascular structures, resulting in symptoms such as back pain, leg pain, neurogenic intermittent claudication, muscle atrophy, and in severe cases, urinary and fecal dysfunction [1]. With the aging population, LSS affects over 103 million people globally, presenting significant healthcare and economic challenges [2] It primarily affects individuals over 60 and often involves multiple segments, particularly in cases of two-level adjacent stenosis [3]. Conservative treatments are frequently ineffective, necessitating surgical intervention [4, 5]. Traditional laminectomy decompression, entailing resection of posterior structures along with facet joints, can effectively alleviate symptoms but carries inherent risks such as infection and iatrogenic instability [6, 7]. Although posterior lumbar interbody fusion can address postoperative instability, it may accelerate degeneration in adjacent segments, leading to adjacent segment disease [8, 9]. In severe LSS involving both central canal and lateral recess stenosis, conventional laminotomy and minimally invasive procedures like percutaneous endoscopic lumbar discectomy face technological limitations, often failing to achieve adequate decompression [10, 11].

Unilateral biportal endoscopy (UBE), introduced by Choi et al. in 2016 for LSS treatment, is a flexible, minimally invasive technique that enhances visualization, reduces surgical trauma, and improves safety, yielding favorable clinical outcomes [12, 13]. However, traditional UBE for multi-level LSS frequently requires multiple small incisions, leading to increased blood loss, longer operative times, and greater reliance on fluoroscopy [14]. Heo et al. demonstrated that biportal endoscopic posterior cervical foraminotomy effectively treats contiguous two-level adjacent foraminal lesions, enabling multiple decompressions through a single approach [15]. Given the lumbar spine’s substantial lordotic angle, we propose the sliding UBE decompression technique (S-UBE) for two-level adjacent LSS, which can be efficiently performed with a single sliding approach.

In this study, we developed the S-UBE technique for addressing two-level adjacent LSS. This article aims to describe the surgical technique and report it preliminary clinical outcomes.

Materials and methods

Study population

This single-center retrospective study received approval from the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Southwest Medical University (KY2025200). All methods and procedures were conducted in strict accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations, with informed consent obtained from all participants. From January 2023 to November 2023, a total of 41 patients diagnosed with two-level adjacent LSS underwent surgery performed by the same surgeon at our institution. Patients were selected based on stringent inclusion and exclusion criteria. The eligibility criteria included: (a) patients presenting with two-level adjacent LSS of L3-L4-L5 or L4-L5-S1, exhibiting intermittent neurologic claudication or radicular leg pain, (b) inadequate response to conservative treatment for a duration of 3 months, (c) Lumbar lordosis > 35 degrees [16], (d) surgical intervention carried out by the same surgeon, and (e) follow-up for a minimum period of 12 months. The exclusion criteria comprised: (a) patients diagnosed with single-level spinal stenosis, (b) presence of segmental instability [17], (c) bilateral radiculopathy symptoms, and (d) prior history of lumbar surgery or infectious disease.

Surgical technique

Positioning and incision design

Taking the right-side decompression of the L4-L5-S1 adjacent LSS as an example. Based on the preoperative imaging data, plan the sliding angle and the trajectory of the observation and working channels (Fig. 1A). The procedure was performed under general anesthesia with the patient in a prone position, supported by a Jackson table or radiolucent Wilson frame to reduce abdominal pressure. The target decompression level was identified and marked under fluoroscopy. The surgical region was disinfected, draped with sterile covers, and a waterproof surgical film applied. The endoscope, drill, plasma radiofrequency electroknife, and irrigation drainage system were prepared and connected. Decompression was typically performed on the symptomatic side. Under C-arm fluoroscopy, two syringe needles were inserted parallel to the intervertebral spaces of L4-L5 and L5-S1, 1 cm lateral to the midline of the spinous process, with punctures made 0.5–1 cm cranially and caudally to create a 1 cm working channel and a 0.8 cm observation channel (Fig. 1B, C). For left-side decompression, the channel orientation was reversed. The incision site may be slightly adjusted cranially to optimize access to the disc herniation, depending on the patient's body type and lumbosacral angle. For highly up-migrated lumbar disc herniations, we also recommend a slight cranial adjustment of the incision to further optimize surgical access.

Decompression of the first level

After selecting the incision site, local injection of epinephrine saline was injected locally to minimize bleeding. Taking the right side of the L4-L5-S1 segment as an example (Fig. 2A), a 0.8 cm transverse incision was made on the caudal skin. Subsequent dissection of the skin, subcutaneous tissue, and deep fascia was performed, followed by stepwise dilation to introduce a 30° endoscope. A 1 cm cranial incision was made, and after dilation, a plasma radiofrequency electroknife was introduced through the channel (Fig. 2B). According to trigonometric principles, the endoscope and surgical instruments converged at the spino-laminar junction of L4. Soft tissues around the spino-laminar junction were dissected and removed using a plasma radiofrequency electroknife, disc forceps, and Kerrison punch, creating the working space. The inferior edge of the L4 lamina, the medial margin of the L4 superior articular process, and the superior edge of the L5 lamina were identified, followed by C-arm re-positioning to confirm the decompression segment. Laminectomy was performed at the spino-laminar junction using a diamond drill, bone chisel, and Kerrison punch (Fig. 2C). However, only partial resection of the L4 lamina was carried out, specifically up to the point where the lamina meets the ligamentum flavum, in order to achieve adequate spinal canal decompression without destroying the lumbar stability. Meanwhile, a partial laminotomy of superior lamina of L5 were removed using diamond drill. The ligamentum flavum was excised to enlarge the lateral recess, exposing the dural sac and ipsilateral nerve root (Fig. 2D, E). For cases with severe lateral recess stenosis, the Kerrison punch can be employed to enlarge the lateral recess. Adhesions around the nerve root were separated, and hemostasis was achieved using electrocoagulation. In cases of disc herniation, protruding or free nucleus pulposus tissue was excised, and loose tissue in the intervertebral space was cleared (Fig. 2F). The annulus and nucleus pulposus were reshaped using an electrode.

Sliding to the second level for decompression

According the planed results of sliding angle and trajectory, the endoscopic and operative channels were slid caudally along the surface of the L5 lamina and converged at the spino-laminar junction (Fig. 3A, B). A plasma electrode was used to dissect the multifidus muscle on the caudal side, exposing the L5-S1 interlaminar space. A laminotomy was performed, the ligamentum flavum was excised, and the dural sac and intervertebral disc were explored. Upon slight withdrawal of the observation endoscope, the L5 lamina was visualized after decompression, demonstrating the sliding trajectory during the procedure (Fig. 3C). Following thorough decompression, hemostasis was achieved using an electrode (Fig. 3D). During the caudal laminotomy, some bone fragments or nucleus pulposus may have drifted into the cranial nerve root canal. Upon further withdrawal of the observation endoscope, the L5-S1 segment was confirmed to be adequately decompressed, with good nerve root mobility (Fig. 3E). As the partially resected L5 inferior lamina was visualized, the endoscope was slid cranially along the surface of the L5 inferior lamina (Fig. 3F, G). A subsequent inspection of the L4-L5 segment confirmed the absence of bone fragments or nucleus pulposus. A drainage tube was then placed, and the incision was sutured.

Postoperative management and clinical evaluation

Postoperatively, patients were administered oral celecoxib and eperisone hydrochloride to alleviate pain. On the first postoperative day, patients were instructed to rest in bed and perform straight leg elevation exercises to prevent nerve root adhesion and thrombosis. They were then encouraged to gradually resume daily activities and functional exercises under supervision, while wearing a back brace, prior to discharge. For one month, patients were advised to avoid excessive lumbar flexion, rotation, and heavy lifting, and to consistently perform lumbar and back muscle exercises.

Clinical data, including age, sex, surgical level, operation time, estimated blood loss, fluoroscopy time, lumbar lordosis, hospital stay, and postoperative complications, were recorded and analyzed. Clinical outcomes were assessed using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) for lower back and leg pain, and the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) for function recovery. Patient satisfaction with postoperative efficacy was evaluated using the modified MacNab criteria at the final follow-up. The modified MacNab grading system classifies postoperative outcomes as: excellent (complete symptom resolution and return to preoperative activity levels), good (mild symptoms with minimal activity limitations, no impact on work or daily life), acceptable (symptom reduction with activity limitations affecting normal work and life), and poor (no improvement or worsening of symptoms) [14]. Postoperative three-dimensional CT scans and dynamic lumbar X-rays were performed on the third day following surgery to evaluate decompression efficacy and lumbar stability. Follow-up imaging at 1 month, 3 months, and at the final follow-up included dynamic lumbar X-rays, which were used to assess lumbar stability [17]. Given the economic burden on local patients, MRI was only conducted during follow-up if new symptoms of lower limb pain or numbness emerged, to investigate potential recurrence. The facet joint preservation rate, reflecting lumbar stability, was calculated as the ratio of facet length after and before surgery on coronal CT scans [18] (Fig. 4).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 29.0. Normally distributed continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation, while categorical variables are presented as frequencies or proportions. Paired-sample t-tests were used to compare continuous variables before and after surgery. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Demographic data results

A total of 41 patients with two-level lumbar spinal stenosis (LSS) underwent successful S-UBE surgery with no major complications. The cohort included 26 males and 15 females, with a mean age of 74.00 ± 5.98 years. Surgical levels involved L3-L4-L5 (11 cases) and L4-L5-S1 (30 cases). The mean lumbar lordosis was 41.64 ± 4.83 degrees. The mean operative time was 118.27 ± 11.79 min, with an intraoperative blood loss of 92.46 ± 11.75 mL and fluoroscopy time of 5.32 ± 1.44 exposures. The mean postoperative hospitalization duration was 5.20 ± 1.45 days (Table 1).

Clinical and radiographical outcomes

All patients showed significant clinical improvement without compromising spinal integrity badly. Compared to preoperative conditions, the VAS for back pain decreased from 4.95 ± 0.77 to 2.72 ± 0.45 at 3 days, 2.12 ± 0.40 at 1 month, 1.83 ± 0.63 at 3 months, and 1.63 ± 0.62 at final follow-up. For leg pain, the VAS improved from 6.86 ± 0.85 to 2.76 ± 0.43 at 3 days, 2.25 ± 0.25 at 1 month, 1.96 ± 0.23 at 3 months, and 1.66 ± 0.48 at final follow-up. Functional recovery, as measured by the ODI, improved from 74.94 ± 3.08 to 33.76 ± 3.89 at 3 days, 21.43 ± 2.49 at 1 month, 15.22 ± 3.05 at 3 months, and 11.14 ± 1.79 at final follow-up. At the final follow-up, the modified MacNab criteria showed excellent results in 32 patients (78.05%), good in 7 (17.07%), and acceptable in 2 (4.88%), with a combined excellent and good rate of 95.12%. In terms of facet joint preservation, the mean preservation rate was 84.76% for L3-L4-L5 adjacent LSS and 88.38% for L4-L5-S1 adjacent LSS, with minimal disruption to spinal stability (Tables 2 and 3). Complications

Only two patients experienced transient leg numbness and pain, which recovered within 1 month after treatment using low-dose methylprednisolone, pregabalin, and methycobal. Regarding the management of dural tears, for tears less than 4mm in size, no suturing is required, and the patient is managed with bed rest and close monitoring. For tears between 4 and 12mm, a fibrin sealant patch is applied to close the tear, with continued bed rest and observation. For tears greater than 12mm, open surgical repair is recommended.

Discussion

This present study introduced a novel spinal endoscopic technique, termed S-UBE, for the treatment of two-level adjacent LSS. Technically, this approach is feasible and achieved decompression of two-level adjacent LSS through only two incisions, thereby avoiding the need for an additional third incision. Furthermore, by eliminating the requirement for a third surgical incision, this technique significantly reduced both operation time and intraoperative bleeding, consequently minimizing surgical trauma. During the follow-up period, patients experienced significant relief from low back pain and leg pain, with notable recovery in their daily functions. The rate of excellent to good surgical improvement reached as high as 95%. Additionally, this technique effectively preserved facet joints and minimized the occurrence of postoperative iatrogenic lumbar instability to the greatest extent possible.

As the population ages, the incidence of LSS is on the rise. In severe cases, LSS can cause lower limb muscle weakness, sensory deficits, and bladder or bowel dysfunction, significantly impairing daily activities [19, 20]. Surgical intervention is recommended for patients with persistent symptoms after 3–6 months of conservative treatment or those with progressive neurological deficits, cauda equina syndrome, or debilitating neurogenic claudication that affects quality of life [21]. LSS predominantly affects elderly patients with osteoporosis, and surgical treatment has been shown to reduce the risk of falls compared to conservative management, thereby decreasing the occurrence of complications related to osteoporosis-associated fractures [22, 23]. Moreover, previous researched demonstrated that the surgical treatment is prior to conservative treatment [24–27].The primary goal of surgery is to achieve complete decompression at the affected level, relieving pressure on the spinal cord and nerves while preserving spinal integrity to minimize damage. Decompression alone and decompression combined with fusion are the two most common surgical approaches for LSS. Compared to simple decompression, decompression combined with fusion decompression accompanied by fusion presents several limitations including increased blood loss, longer operative time, adjacent segment disease risks, and non-fusion complications [28–30]. For LSS patients without spinal instability, decompression without fusion demonstrates superior improvement in terms of leg pain VAS score, ODI score, and reoperation rates [31]. Decompression with fusion is only necessary in cases involving spondylolisthesis and spinal instability, while most patients are effectively treated with decompression alone [32].Traditional decompressive laminectomy provides thorough decompression, but carries well reported drawbacks involving back pain from muscle denervation and secondary lumbar instability, leading to a poor outcome in up to 50% of patients [33, 34].

In recent years, there has been a monumental shift in the realm of spinal surgery, primarily driven by technological advancements that have facilitated the development and widespread adoption of minimally invasive techniques [35–39]. This shift towards minimally invasive surgical strategies has revolutionized the treatment paradigm for LSS, motivated by the necessity to minimize tissue damage, enhance postoperative recovery, and improve outcomes for elderly patients [21]. These techniques include a variety of methodologies such as micro-endoscopic discectomy (MED), percutaneous endoscopy transforaminal discectomy (PETD), and UBE, which are extensively utilized in the treatment of LSS [40–43]. Compared to traditional decompressive laminectomy, these techniques not only achieve neural decompression but also minimize damage to the paraspinal muscles during surgery. They offer several advantages, including reduced postoperative pain, shorter hospital stays, faster functional recovery, less disruption to stability, and a shorter learning curve. Consequently, these methods are gradually replacing open surgical approaches and are emerging as safe and effective alternatives for LSS management [44]. Among these, UBE has emerged as one of the most promising techniques for LSS, combining the benefits of MED and PETD [45]. UBE employs two independent working portals, allowing for triangulated manipulation and panoramic visualization, which facilitates precise dissection and safe removal of hypertrophied ligamentum flavum, facet overgrowth, and laminar spurs [3, 46–49]. A meta-analysis by Meng et al. demonstrated that UBE effectively alleviates lumbar and leg pain, with superior VAS scores compared to other techniques than MED [44]. Additionally, UBE provides better visualization and versatility than PETD, making it the preferred option for many surgeons. A study by Kaike et al. confirmed that biportal endoscopic decompression resulted in significantly reduced operation time, improved postoperative outcomes, and higher dural sac area expansion than uniportal endoscopic decompression [50].

While UBE technique provides significant technical advantages and clinical efficacy in treating LSS, it presents challenges in cases of multilevel LSS, such as the need for additional incisions, prolonged operative times, and increased trauma, ultimately compromising surgical efficiency. To overcome this technical defect, we developed the S-UBE technique, sliding the working and operation channel to manage two-level adjacent LSS. Compared to Jia et al.'s traditional UBE technique for multi-level LSS [14], which reported an operative time of 145 ± 40.11 min and blood loss of 156.25 ± 44.32 ml, our S-UBE technique requires only two incisions, with an operative time of 118.27 ± 11.79 min and intraoperative blood loss of 92.46 ± 11.75 ml. Moreover, this technique significantly reduced the frequency of intraoperative fluoroscopy (5.32 ± 1.44 times) and the length of hospital stay (5.20 ± 1.45 days), aligning with the principles of enhanced recovery after surgery [51]. The S-UBE technique effectively removes thickened ligamentum flavum and joint hyperplasia without the need for additional incisions, resulting in thorough decompression of nerve roots and dural sacs. Meanwhile, continuous irrigation with normal saline can eliminate the inflammatory factors secreted locally during the surgical stimulation, reduce the occurrence of local inflammatory responses, and alleviate the patient's pain [52]. Follow-up assessments revealed that patients treated with S-UBE exhibited significant improvements in VAS scores and ODI index for lumbar and leg pain compared to preoperative values (P < 0.05). At the final follow-up, the rate of excellent and good outcomes reached 95.12%, demonstrating the efficacy of the S-UBE technique for the treatment of multilevel LSS. Previous research has demonstrated that the sensations of pain and numbness are transmitted by two distinct types of sensory nerves [53]. The nerve associated with numbness is comparatively thicker and has a myelin sheath, while the nerve responsible for pain is thinner and lacks myelination. After nerve decompression, unmyelinated nerve fibers typically recover more rapidly than myelinated fibers, which explains why pain often resolves faster than numbness [54]. Additionally, the common risk factors associated with numbness include the use of high-dosage radiofrequency near the nerve root, manipulation and traction of the traversing nerve root, and aggressive adhesion release. These factors can lead to edema of the nerve root, which may result in recurrent pain [55]. The present study noted transient numbness and pain in two cases, attributable to these factors. Lumbar stability is essential during surgical procedures, as the lumbar facet joints are integral to the three-joint complex that ensures stability. Excessive trauma to these joints during surgery can result in postoperative instability during flexion and extension. Abumi et al. found in vitro that resection of over half of the lumbar facet joints may lead to segmental instability [56]. Pao et al. reported a facet joint preservation rate of approximately 84.2 ± 9.3% in 81 LSS patients treated with the UBE technique, with no instances of postoperative lumbar instability [57]. During UBE operations, the laminar space at the distal segment is relatively large, resulting in less need for resection of the facet joint structure. Consequently, this leads to a higher degree of facet joint retention. In our study, the retention rates of facet joints for the L3-L4-L5 and L4-L5-S1 segments averaged approximately 84.76% and 88.38%, respectively, with no cases of lumbar instability observed postoperatively. This outcome can be attributed to our surgical technique, which involved decompression of traversing nerve roots performed at the inner edge of the upper articular process, which allows for latent decompression while minimizing excessive resection of the articular process.

For successful application of the S-UBE technique, several key factors must be considered: (1) Patient selection: The technique is appropriate for two-level adjacent LSS but not for jumping LSS, as decompression of non-contiguous segments cannot be achieved. (2) Lumbar lordosis: A lumbar lordosis angle of 35–40° is recommended to facilitate smooth sliding between adjacent segments. (3) Preoperative planning: Plan the sliding angle and trajectory based on radiographical results. (4) 30-degree endoscope: This 30-degree endoscope is crucial for expanding the visual field and facilitating decompression of the lateral recess during the sliding procedure. (5) Latent decompression: Decompression of the traversing nerve roots should be performed at the inner edge of the upper articular process to minimize excessive resection and preserve facet integrity.

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, it is a single-center retrospective analysis designed primarily to introduce an innovative surgical technique, without inclusion of a control group. Second, most procedures were performed at L3–L4 to L5–S1, limiting generalizability to other segments. Third, the S-UBE technique has thus far been applied only for decompression and lacks experience in fusion. The technique of fusion using the S-UBE needs to be explored and summarized in the future.

Conclusion

The S-UBE technique is an effective alternative for treating two-level adjacent LSS, offering a minimally invasive solution with only two skin incisions. This approach demonstrates promising clinical outcomes, including reduced operative time, minimal blood loss, and high facet joint preservation rates.
