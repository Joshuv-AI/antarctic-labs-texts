Taming Transformer without using learning rate warmup
Xianbiao Qi, Yelin He, Jiaquan Ye, Chun-Guang Li, Bojia Zi, Xili Dai, Qin Zou, Rong Xiao

Abstract
Scaling Transformer to a large scale without using some technical tricks such as learning rate warump and using an obviously lower learning rate is an extremely challenging task, and is increasingly gaining more attention. In this paper, we provide a theoretical analysis for the process of training Transformer and reveal the rationale behind the model crash phenomenon in the training process, termed spectral energy concentration of 𝑾 q ⊤ ​ 𝑾 k {\bm{W}_{q}}^{\top}\bm{W}_{k} , which is the reason for a malignant entropy collapse, where 𝑾 q {\bm{W}_{q}} and 𝑾 k \bm{W}_{k} are the projection matrices for the query and the key in Transformer, respectively. To remedy this problem, motivated by Weyl’s Inequality , we present a novel optimization strategy, i . e ., making the weight updating in successive steps smooth—if the ratio σ 1 ​ ( ∇ 𝑾 t ) σ 1 ​ ( 𝑾 t − 1 ) \frac{\sigma_{1}(\nabla\bm{W}_{t})}{\sigma_{1}(\bm{W}_{t-1})} is larger than a threshold, we will automatically bound the learning rate to a weighted multiple of σ 1 ​ ( 𝑾 t − 1 ) σ 1 ​ ( ∇ 𝑾 t ) \frac{\sigma_{1}(\bm{W}_{t-1})}{\sigma_{1}(\nabla\bm{W}_{t})} , where ∇ 𝑾 t \nabla\bm{W}_{t} is the updating quantity in step t t . Such an optimization strategy can prevent spectral energy concentration to only a few directions, and thus can avoid malignant entropy collapse which will trigger the model crash. We conduct extensive experiments using ViT, Swin-Transformer and GPT, showing that our optimization strategy can effectively and stably train these Transformers without using learning rate warmup.

Transformer

;

, owning to their ability to capture long-range dependencies through self-attention mechanisms.
However, despite their widespread application and empirical success, training deep Transformer models remains quite challenging. Practitioners
frequently encounter variant issues, such as gradient explosion

, rank collapse

, entropy collapse

)

(

)

To address these challenges, researchers have proposed various modifications to the original Transformer architecture, including altering the placement of Layer Normalization

;
Xiong et al., 2020
)
(
e
.
g

)

Similarly, DeepNet

More recent approaches
(

;

)
have focused on examining and enforcing Lipschitz continuity properties of Transformer components, which can provide insights into the network behavior and the training stability.

Although there are a few works
(

)
that can avoid using learning rate warmup to train Transformer successfully, all of them require significant modifications of the network structure.

Learning rate warmup

;

;

;
Chowdhery et al., 2023
;
Touvron et al., 2023
)
. Without the learning rate warmup stage, the Transformer training will
prone to diverge.

Although it is usual to train a Transformer by
modifying the network structure as mentioned above or using the learning rate warmup,
two natural and interesting questions remain:

1.

What are
the training dynamics of a Transformer model when its training fails or succeeds?

2.

Can we successfully tame
a Transformer without changing the network structure or without using learning rate warmup?

This paper aims to answer these questions.
To answer the first question, we examine
the training processes of three types of Transformers,
by visualizing the changing trajectories along with the training process of 15 (or 13) quantities about the parameters, activations, and attention maps.
By doing so, we observe that the model crash is accompanied by a weird phenomenon that the entropy of the attention map is almost 0 and the spectral norm of
{\bm{W}_{q}}^{\top}\bm{W}_{k}
increases to a very large value.
By conducting mathematical analysis for the Transformer training, we identify that the
Spectral Energy Concentration (SEC) of
{\bm{W}_{q}}^{\top}\bm{W}_{k}
is the key problem leading to the model crash.
To answer the second question,
motivated by Weyl’ Inequality, we present a novel optimization strategy,
i
.
e
., making weight updating smooth, and verify empirically that our optimization strategy can prevent
spectral energy concentration and thus achieving a stable convergence in training.

Paper Contributions.
The contributions of the paper are highlighted as follows.

•

We visualize the training dynamics of Transformers that train successfully or unsuccessfully and summarize two important observations from unsuccessful training that:
a) the rank of the attention map matrix tends to very low and the entropy of attention probability matrix tends to 0; and
b)
\sigma_{1}({\bm{W}_{q}}^{\top}\bm{W}_{k})
increases rapidly to a very large value.

•

We present theoretical analysis for the Transformer training,
finding that the Jacobian matrix

\bm{P}=\bm{X}^{\top}{\bm{W}_{q}}^{\top}{\bm{W}_{k}}{\bm{X}}
. It implies that the gradient of
{\bm{W}_{q}}^{\top}{\bm{W}_{k}}
is largely
dominated by the rank of
\bm{X}^{\top}\otimes\bm{X}^{\top}
.

•

We reveal that
SEC of
\bm{W}_{q}^{\top}\bm{W}_{k}
makes the attention map matrix to be sparse yet low-rank and it is the reason leading to model crash.

•

Motivated by the Weyl’s inequality, we introduce a novel strategy to address the problem of
SEC of
{\bm{W}_{q}}^{\top}\bm{W}_{k}
by controlling the rapid growth of singular values, and verify that our strategy leads to a stable training process.

Matrix norm.
Given a matrix
\bm{W}

\ell_{p}
-norm
is defined as:
\|\bm{W}\|_{p}=\sup_{\bm{x}\neq 0}\frac{\|\bm{W}\bm{x}\|_{p}}{\|\bm{x}\|_{p}}
.
When
p=2
, the induced matrix norm is the
spectral norm
,
which is defined as the largest singular value of
\bm{W}
and
is also expressed as the square root of the largest eigenvalue of the Gram matrix
\bm{W}^{\top}\bm{W}
.
The spectral norm of a matrix
\bm{W}
can be calculated as:
\|\bm{W}\|_{2}=\max_{\bm{x}\in S^{n-1}}\|\bm{A}\bm{x}\|_{2}=\sqrt{\lambda_{\max}(\bm{W}^{\top}\bm{W})}=\sigma_{1}(\bm{W})
,
where
\sigma_{1}(\bm{W})
denotes the largest singular value of matrix
\bm{W}
and
\lambda_{\max}(\bm{W}^{\top}\bm{W})
denotes the largest eigenvalue of
\bm{W}^{\top}\bm{W}
,
and
S^{n-1}
denotes a unit sphere in
\mathcal{R}^{n}
.

Power iteration to compute matrix spectral norm.
The power iteration algorithm starts with a vector
\bm{x}_{0}
of unit
\ell_{2}
-norm.
The entire iteration process is as follows:

\bm{x}_{k+1}=\frac{\bm{W}\bm{x}_{k}}{\|\bm{W}\bm{x}_{k}\|_{2}}
for
k=0,\cdots,K-1
.
At every iteration,
\bm{x}_{k}
is multiplied by matrix
\bm{W}
and normalized.
After
K
iterations,

\|\bm{x}_{K}\|_{2}
is
used as the
estimated spectral norm.
Usually, it takes 3 to 5 iterations to converge, and thus the computation cost is cheap.

Taming Transformer Requires to Revisit the Training Dynamics

To begin with, we first give some basic notions in a Transformer, which includes an attention module, an FFN module and two normalization modules that
are used before the attention module and the FFN module.
For the attention module, we usually use a multi-head attention which
allows the model to jointly attend to information from different representations with different heads. Here, for the convenience,
without losing generality, we only use a single-head attention.
To be precise, we define each of them as follows:

\displaystyle\operatorname{Attn}(\bm{X};\bm{W}_{q},\bm{W}_{k},\bm{W}_{v},\bm{W}_{o})\quad

\displaystyle=\quad\bm{W}_{o}{\bm{W}_{v}}\bm{X}\operatorname{softmax}\left(\frac{\bm{X}^{\top}{\bm{W}_{q}}^{\top}{\bm{W}_{k}}{\bm{X}}}{\sqrt{d_{q}}}\right),

\displaystyle\operatorname{FFN}(\bm{x};\bm{W}_{1},\bm{W}_{2})\quad

\displaystyle=\quad\bm{W}_{2}\operatorname{ReLU}(\bm{W}_{1}\bm{x}),

\displaystyle\operatorname{LN}(\bm{x})\quad

\displaystyle=\quad\bm{\gamma}\odot\bm{z}+\bm{\beta},\quad\text{where }\bm{z}=\frac{\bm{y}}{\operatorname{std}(\bm{y})}\text{ and}\quad\bm{y}=\left(\bm{I}-\frac{1}{D}\mathbf{11}^{\top}\right)\bm{x},

where
\bm{X}\in\mathcal{R}^{d\times n}
,
\bm{W}_{q}\in\mathcal{R}^{d_{q}\times d}
,
\bm{W}_{k}\in\mathcal{R}^{d_{q}\times d}
,
\bm{W}_{v}\in\mathcal{R}^{d_{v}\times d}
,
\bm{W}_{o}\in\mathcal{R}^{d\times d}
,
\bm{W}_{1}\in\mathcal{R}^{4d\times d}
,
\bm{W}_{2}\in\mathcal{R}^{d\times 4d}
,
\bm{\gamma}\in\mathcal{R}^{d}
and
\bm{\beta}\in\mathcal{R}^{d}

\bm{W}_{v}

3.2
Theoretical Analysis: Matrix Calculus of Transformer

To understand the training dynamics of Transformer, we should investigate the process of back-propagation
(
Rumelhart et al., 1986
;
LeCun et al., 2002
;
LeCun et al., 1989
;
LeCun et al., 1998
)
.
In the attention mechanism, however, the input and the output are both
matrices, we cannot directly use vector calculus. Instead, we need to use
Vectorization

†
†

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vectorization_(mathematics)

(
Graham, 2018
;
Petersen et al., 2008
)
and
Kronecker Product

†
†

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kronecker_product

(
Graham, 2018
;
Petersen et al., 2008
)
.
In matrix calculus, the vectorization of a matrix is a linear transformation
that converts a matrix into a vector.
Specifically, the vectorization of a matrix
\bm{M}\in\mathcal{R}^{m\times n}
, denoted as
\text{vec}(\bm{M})
, is a column vector, obtained by an ordered stacking of the columns of the matrix
\bm{M}
,
i
.
e
.,
\text{vec}(\bm{M})\in\mathcal{R}^{mn}
.
For example, for a
2\times 3
matrix
\bm{M}=\begin{bmatrix}a&b&c\\
d&e&f\end{bmatrix}
, the vectorization of
\bm{M}
is
\text{vec}(\bm{M})=[a\ d\ b\ e\ c\ f]^{\top}
.
For the attention module of Transformer, we have the following proposition about the Jacobian matrix of the output
\bm{P}
with respect to the input
\bm{X}
and the parameters.

Proposition 1

(Matrix Calculus for Self-Attention)
.

Let
\bm{P}={\bm{X}^{\top}{\bm{W}_{q}}^{\top}{\bm{W}_{k}}{\bm{X}}}
, where
\bm{X}\in\mathcal{R}^{d\times n},\bm{W}_{q}\in\mathcal{R}^{d_{q}\times d},\bm{W}_{k}\in\mathcal{R}^{d_{q}\times d}
, according to vectorization and matrix calculus, we have the following derivations:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\operatorname{vec}(\bm{P})}{\partial\operatorname{vec}({\bm{W}_{q}}^{\top}{\bm{W}_{k}})}

\displaystyle=\bm{X}^{\top}\otimes\bm{X}^{\top},\quad\frac{\partial\operatorname{vec}(\bm{P})}{\partial\operatorname{vec}(\bm{X})}=(\bm{X}^{\top}{\bm{W}_{k}}^{\top}{\bm{W}_{q}}\otimes\bm{I}_{n})\bm{K}+(\bm{I}_{n}\otimes\bm{X}^{\top}{\bm{W}_{q}}^{\top}{\bm{W}_{k}}),

(2)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\operatorname{vec}(\bm{P})}{\partial\operatorname{vec}(\bm{W}_{q}^{\top})}

\displaystyle=(\bm{W}_{k}\bm{X})^{\top}\otimes\bm{X}^{\top},\quad\frac{\partial\operatorname{vec}(\bm{P})}{\partial\operatorname{vec}(\bm{W}_{k})}=\bm{X}^{\top}\otimes(\bm{W}_{q}\bm{X})^{\top},

(3)

where
\otimes
denotes the Kronecker product,
\bm{I}_{n}\in\mathcal{R}^{n\times n}
denotes an
identity matrix with shape
n\times n
,
\bm{K}
is the commutation matrix, which depends on the dimensions of
\bm{X}
. Since
\bm{X}\in\mathcal{R}^{d\times n}
, then we know
\bm{K}\in\mathcal{R}^{nd\times nd}
. The commutation matrix
\bm{K}
has the property that
\operatorname{vec}(\bm{X}^{\top})=\bm{K}\operatorname{vec}(\bm{X})
for any matrix
\bm{X}
.

In Appendices
A
and
B
, we supply some elementary

We have the following observations from Proposition
1
.

•

We have
\frac{\partial\operatorname{vec}(\bm{P})}{\partial\operatorname{vec}({\bm{W}_{q}}^{\top}{\bm{W}_{k}})}=\bm{X}^{\top}\otimes\bm{X}^{\top}

2
, and we know

\operatorname{rank}(\bm{X}^{\top}\otimes\bm{X}^{\top})={\operatorname{rank}(\bm{X}^{\top})}^{2}

will also be very low.
Note that
\bm{X}
being low rank means that the features across different timestep are highly correlated or coherent.
If all
\bm{x}_{i}
in
\bm{X}
collapses to a single

{\bm{X}^{\top}\otimes\bm{X}^{\top}}
will only have a large singular value, and the rest are 0.

•

The Jacobian matrix
\frac{\partial\operatorname{vec}(\bm{P})}{\partial\operatorname{vec}(\bm{X})}
in Equation
2
, is in direct proportion to
\bm{X}
and
{\bm{W}_{q}}^{\top}\bm{W}_{k}

\sigma_{1}({\bm{W}_{q}}^{\top}\bm{W}_{k})

\frac{\partial L}{\partial\bm{X}}

to be magnified a lot.

•

suggests that changes in the query weights

are related to both the input
\bm{X}
and the key representation
\bm{W}_{k}\bm{X}
. Equation
3
suggests that changes in the key weights
\bm{W}_{k}
are related to both the input
\bm{X}
and the query representation
\bm{W}_{q}\bm{X}
.

•

All these relationships are interconnected, with changes in one variable potentially affecting the others.
For instance, if
\bm{W}_{k}

,
\frac{\partial\operatorname{vec}(\bm{P})}{\partial\operatorname{vec}(\bm{W}_{q}^{\top})}

3.3
Rationale in Model Crash: Spectral Energy Concentration

Before we reveal the rationale in model crash, let us first discuss a bit on the entropy collapse.
In
(
Zhai et al., 2023
)
, the entropy of an attention map
\bm{A}
is defined as
\mathcal{E}(\bm{A})=-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i,j}\log\left(A_{i,j}\right)
.
The so-called
entropy collapse
refers to the fact that the entropy
\mathcal{E}(\bm{A})
of the attention probability matrix is almost 0.

Two Entropy Collapse Modes.
In experiments, we observe two types of attention entropy collapse modes. Note that when attention collapse happens, the attention map tends to a sparse matrix (
i
.
e
., there are a few dominate nonzero coefficients in attention map), and thus the entropy of the attention map is vanishing. To be more specific, when the attention map is sparse but not low-rank, we call it a
benign collapse
; whereas if the attention map is sparse and simultaneously low-rank,
†
†

For a sparse and simultaneously low-rank matrix, we refer the reader to a recent textbook
(
Wright & Ma, 2022
)
.
we call it a
malignant collapse
.
When benign collapse occurs, the attention map is shown in the right panel of Figure
3
that, there
is almost an identity matrix. In this way, the diagonal elements are almost 1, and the non-diagonal elements have values around 0.
Unfortunately, when malignant collapse happens, the attention map becomes a sparse and simultaneously low-rank matrix as shown in the middle panel of
Figure
3
.
Furthermore, we observe that the distribution of the spectral energy of
{\bm{W}_{q}}^{\top}\bm{W}_{k}
for the benign collapse is relatively uniform; whereas the spectral energy of the attention matrix for the malignant collapse tends to concentrate on a few dominate singular values.

By analyzing the matrix

in the benign collapse when it happens in the experiments, we find that it has the following property:
{\bm{W}_{q}}^{\top}\bm{W}_{k}

When the malignant collapse happens, the model will usually crash. We identify that the rationale behind the model crash is a phenomenon called
spectral energy concentration
(
SEC
).
Before we present our theorem about SEC, let us first introduce an index to
quantify it.
Recall that
\bm{W}_{q}\in\mathcal{R}^{d_{q}\times d}
and
\bm{W}_{k}\in\mathcal{R}^{d_{q}\times d}
, where
d_{q}<d
. We have that
{\bm{W}_{q}}^{\top}\bm{W}_{k}\in\mathcal{R}^{d\times d}
, but its rank is less than or equal to
d_{q}
.
Precisely, we define a
SEC index
as follows:

\operatorname{SEC}(d_{q},s)=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{s}{\sigma_{i}^{2}({{\bm{W}_{q}}^{\top}\bm{W}_{k}})}}{\sum_{i=1}^{d_{q}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}({{\bm{W}_{q}}^{\top}\bm{W}_{k}})}},

(4)

where
d_{q}
is the head dimension and
s\leq d_{q}
.
For instance, if we have
d_{q}=64
and
s=4
, and if at this time,
\operatorname{SEC}(64,4)>99\%
, we could say the spectral energy of
{\bm{W}_{q}}^{\top}\bm{W}_{k}
highly concentrates on only four dominant singular values.

To be precise, we have the following theorem for the reason to cause a malignant collapse.

Theorem 1

(Malignant Entropy Collapse)
.

Let

\bm{P}=\bm{X}^{T}\bm{W}\bm{X}\quad\text{and}\quad\bm{A}=\operatorname{softmax}\left(\frac{\bm{P}}{\sqrt{d_{q}}}\right),\quad\bm{W}=\bm{W}_{q}^{T}\bm{W}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}.

Suppose that the following two conditions are simultaneously satisfied:

•

\bm{X}
is a low-rank matrix;

•

\bm{W}
is a low-rank matrix with only a few dominant singular values (e.g., the singular values are greater than
C_{0}\cdot\sqrt{d_{q}}
where
C_{0}\gg 1
is a constant).

Then, the attention map
\bm{A}
will be sparse
and simultaneously low-rank in high probability.

When the malignant entropy collapse happens, the training process will crash. We provide a proof for Theorem
1
in Appendix
D
.

According to Theorem
1
, we know that the model crash is caused by the spectral energy concentration.

3.4
Our Solution: Taming Transformer via Weyl’s Inequality

The analysis above reveals that spectral energy concentration is the key reason
leading to unstable training.
One manifestation of spectral energy concentration is the rapid growth of the singular values of of the weight matrices.
Thus, our
basic idea to prevent malignant collapse is to suppress
the fast growth of the singular values.
Fortunately, Weyl’s inequality provides us a simple but effective tool.

Theorem 2

(Weyl’s Inequality on Singular Values)
.

Let
\bm{W}_{1},\bm{W}_{2}\in\mathcal{R}^{m\times n}
where
m\geq n
,
\sigma_{1}(\bm{W}_{1})\geq\sigma_{2}(\bm{W}_{1})\geq...\geq\sigma_{n}(\bm{W}_{1})
be the ordered singular values of
\bm{W}_{1}
, and
\sigma_{1}(\bm{W}_{2}\geq\sigma_{2}(\bm{W}_{2})\geq...\geq\sigma_{n}(\bm{W}_{2})
be the ordered singular values of
\bm{W}_{2}
.
Then we have:

\displaystyle\sigma_{i+j-1}(\bm{W}_{1}+\bm{W}_{2})\leq\sigma_{i}(\bm{W}_{1})+\sigma_{j}(\bm{W}_{2}).

The proof for Theorem
2
is provided in Appendix
E
.
From Theorem
2
, it is easy to see that
\sigma_{1}(\bm{W}_{1}+\bm{W}_{2})\leq\sigma_{1}(\bm{W}_{1})+\sigma_{1}(\bm{W}_{2})
.
Let
\bm{W}_{t}
be the weight matrix at time step
t
,
\nabla\bm{W}_{t}
be the quantity computed from the gradients and their derivations (where
\nabla\bm{W}_{t}
can be obtained by SGD
(
Robbins & Monro, 1951
)
, Adagrad
(
Duchi et al., 2011
)
, or Adam
(
Kingma & Ba, 2014
)
),

\alpha_{t}
is the learning rate at time step
t
. Usually, our update equation is
\bm{W}_{t}=\bm{W}_{t-1}-\alpha_{t}\nabla\bm{W}_{t}
.
According to Weyl’s Inequality, we have,

\sigma_{1}(\bm{W}_{t})=\sigma_{1}(\bm{W}_{t-1}-\alpha_{t}\nabla\bm{W}_{t})\leq\sigma_{1}(\bm{W}_{t-1})+\alpha_{t}\sigma_{1}(\nabla\bm{W}_{t}).

(5)

An important observation from Equation
5
is that if
\sigma_{1}(\nabla\bm{W})
is very large, then
\bm{W}_{t}
will be significantly different from
\bm{W}_{t-1}
. It means that the successive updates of
\bm{W}_{t}
at time step
t
from time step
t-1
would “
jump
” too much. A “
smoother
” updating should satisfy the following rule.

Rule 1

(Steady Weights Updating Rule)
.

Given weights matrix
\bm{W}_{t-1}
and the updating quantity
\nabla\bm{W}_{t}
at step
t
, with the learning rate
\alpha_{t}
, a steady weights should satisfy the following inequality:

\|\bm{W}_{t-1}-\alpha_{t}\nabla\bm{W}_{t}\|_{2}\leq(1+\tau)\|\bm{W}_{t-1}\|_{2},
where
\tau>0
is a small factor.

To meet Rule
1
, what we need is that
\sigma_{1}(\bm{W}_{t-1})+\alpha_{t}\sigma_{1}(\nabla\bm{W}_{t})\leq(1+\tau)\|\bm{W}_{t-1}\|_{2}=(1+\tau)\sigma_{1}(\bm{W}_{t-1})
. It is easy to see that:

\alpha_{t}\leq\tau\frac{\sigma_{1}(\bm{W}_{t-1})}{\sigma_{1}(\nabla\bm{W}_{t})}.

(6)

This inequality
tell us that the learning rate
\alpha_{t}
should be bounded by a ratio of singular values

{\sigma_{1}(\bm{W}_{t-1})}
and
{\sigma_{1}(\nabla\bm{W}_{t})}
.
Generally,
\tau
is a small value,
e
.
g
., 0.004 or 0.005.
The intuition behind is that if the spectral norm of
\nabla\bm{W}_{t}
is
significantly larger than that of
\bm{W}_{t}
, then the model is potentially undergoing rapid changes. In such cases, a large learning rate could lead to training instability. Therefore, our
strategy is that if

\alpha_{t}>\tau\frac{\sigma_{1}(\bm{W}_{t-1})}{\sigma_{1}(\nabla\bm{W}_{t})}

then we truncate
\alpha_{t}

to
\tau\frac{\sigma_{1}(\bm{W}_{t-1})}{\sigma_{1}(\nabla\bm{W}_{t})}
. Since our

and our algorithm is motivated by Weyl’s Inequality,

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we revisited the training dynamics of the Transformers by visualizing the spectral norm of weight matrices, the activations and the attention map, presented a theoretical analysis for the Transformer training and identified two modes of attention entropy collapse,
i
.
e
., the benign collapse and the malignant collapse, in which the malignant collapse accompanies model crash. Moreover, we revealed that the
spectral energy concentration
of
{\bm{W}_{q}}^{\top}\bm{W}_{k}
is the reason
behind the model crash, which causes the attention map to be sparse and simultaneously low-rank. Furthermore, we proposed a steady updating
rule to resolve the problem of spectral energy concentration of
{\bm{W}_{q}}^{\top}\bm{W}_{k}
by controlling the rapid growth of singular values, which can prevent the fast spectral energy concentration to a few directions and thus avoid the malignant entropy collapse. We conducted extensive experiments to verify the proposed strategy with ViT, Swin Transformer, and GPT, and demonstrated that the proposed strategy could effectively and stably train a model without using any learning rate warmup.

Appendix A
Kronecker Product and Vectorization

Kronecker product
(
Graham, 2018
;
Petersen et al., 2008
)
, also called as matrix direct product, is an operation defined on two matrices of arbitrary size. The specific definition is as follows.

Definition 1

(Kronecker Product)
.

Let
\bm{A}
be an
n\times p
matrix and
\bm{B}
an
m\times q

matrix. The
mn\times pq
matrix

\bm{A}\otimes\bm{B}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}a_{1,1}\bm{B}&a_{1,2}\bm{B}&\cdots&a_{1,p}\bm{B}\\
a_{2,1}\bm{B}&a_{2,2}\bm{B}&\cdots&a_{2,p}\bm{B}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
a_{n,1}\bm{B}&a_{n,2}\bm{B}&\cdots&a_{n,p}\bm{B}\end{array}\right]

is called the Kronecker product of
\bm{A}
and
\bm{B}
. It is also called
the direct product or the tensor product.

For instance, if
\bm{A}=\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\
3&4\end{bmatrix}
, and
\bm{B}=\begin{bmatrix}1&2&3\\
3&4&5\end{bmatrix}
, then
\bm{A}\otimes\bm{B}=\begin{bmatrix}1&2&3&2&4&6\\
3&4&5&6&8&10\\
3&6&9&4&8&12\\
9&12&15&12&16&20\end{bmatrix}
.

Some basic properties of the Kronecker product include:

\displaystyle\centering\bm{A}\otimes(\bm{B}\otimes\bm{C})\@add@centering

\displaystyle=(\bm{A}\otimes\bm{B})\otimes\bm{C},

\displaystyle\bm{A}\otimes(\bm{B}+\bm{C})

\displaystyle=(\bm{A}\otimes\bm{B})+(\bm{A}\otimes\bm{C}),\quad(\bm{A}+\bm{B})\otimes\bm{C}=(\bm{A}\otimes\bm{C})+(\bm{B}\otimes\bm{C}),

\displaystyle(\bm{A}\otimes\bm{B})^{T}

\displaystyle=\bm{A}^{T}\otimes\bm{B}^{T}.

For a matrix
\bm{A}
, the rank of
\bm{A}\otimes\bm{A}
can be computed as,

\operatorname{rank}(\bm{A}\otimes\bm{A})=\operatorname{rank}(\bm{A})\cdot\operatorname{rank}(\bm{A}).

It means if the rank of the matrix
\bm{A}
is small, then the rank of
\bm{A}\otimes\bm{A}
will also be very small.

In mathematics,
Vectorization

(
Graham, 2018
;
Petersen et al., 2008
)
is usually used together with the Kronecker product to express matrix multiplication as a linear transformation on matrices.
After vectorization, we can calculate the Jacobian matrix of the matrix product more conveniently. A property of vectorization for the matrix product is defined below.

Proposition 2

(Property of Vectorization for Matrix Product)
.

Let
\bm{A}\in\mathcal{R}^{m\times n},\bm{B}\in\mathcal{R}^{n\times k},\bm{C}\in\mathcal{R}^{k\times l}
, then we have

\operatorname{vec}(\bm{A}\bm{B}\bm{C})=(\bm{C}^{\top}\otimes\bm{A})\operatorname{vec}(\bm{B}).

Proof.

Let
\bm{C}_{i}
be the
i
-th row of
\bm{C}
. Then we have:

\displaystyle\operatorname{vec}(\bm{A}\bm{B}\bm{C})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{k}b_{ij}\operatorname{vec}(\bm{a}_{i}\bm{C}_{j})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{k}b_{ij}(\bm{C}_{j}^{\top}\otimes\bm{a}_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k}(\bm{C}_{j}^{\top}\otimes\bm{A})\bm{b}_{j}

\displaystyle=(\bm{C}^{\top}\otimes\bm{A})\operatorname{vec}(\bm{B}).

∎

Furthermore, we have the following properties:

\displaystyle\centering\operatorname{vec}(\bm{A}\bm{B})\@add@centering

\displaystyle=(\bm{I}_{k}\otimes\bm{A})\operatorname{vec}(\bm{B})=\left(\bm{B}^{\top}\otimes\bm{I}_{m}\right)\operatorname{vec}(\bm{A}),

\displaystyle\operatorname{vec}(\bm{A}\bm{B}\bm{C})

\displaystyle=\left(\bm{C}^{\top}\bm{B}^{\top}\otimes\bm{I}_{m}\right)\operatorname{vec}(\bm{A})

\displaystyle=\left(\bm{C}^{\top}\otimes\bm{A}\right)\operatorname{vec}(\bm{B})

\displaystyle=\left(\bm{I}_{l}\otimes\bm{A}\bm{B}\right)\operatorname{vec}(\bm{C})

where
\bm{I}_{k}\in\mathcal{R}^{k\times k},\bm{I}_{l}\in\mathcal{R}^{l\times l},\bm{I}_{m}\in\mathcal{R}^{m\times m}
are all identity matrices.

Together with the Kronecker product, vectorization is an effective tool to compute matrix calculus. We can see the following two examples.

Let
\bm{P}=\bm{A}\bm{B}
where
\bm{A}\in\mathcal{R}^{m\times n},\bm{B}\in\mathcal{R}^{n\times k}
, we have:

\displaystyle\centering\frac{\partial\operatorname{vec}(\bm{P})}{\partial\operatorname{vec}(\bm{A})}=\bm{B}^{\top}\otimes\bm{I}_{m},\quad\frac{\partial\operatorname{vec}(\bm{P})}{\partial\operatorname{vec}(\bm{B})}=\bm{I}_{k}\otimes\bm{A}.\@add@centering

Let
\bm{P}=\bm{A}\bm{B}\bm{C}
where
\bm{A}\in\mathcal{R}^{m\times n},\bm{B}\in\mathcal{R}^{n\times k},\bm{C}\in\mathcal{R}^{k\times l}
, we have,

\displaystyle\centering\frac{\partial\operatorname{vec}(\bm{P})}{\partial\operatorname{vec}(\bm{A})}=\bm{C}^{\top}\bm{B}^{\top}\otimes\bm{I}_{m},\quad\frac{\partial\operatorname{vec}(\bm{P})}{\partial\operatorname{vec}(\bm{B})}=\bm{C}^{\top}\otimes\bm{A},\quad\frac{\partial\operatorname{vec}(\bm{P})}{\partial\operatorname{vec}(\bm{C})}=\bm{I}_{l}\otimes\bm{A}\bm{B}.\@add@centering

Vectorization and Kronecker product provide us a convenient way to analyze the self-attention module. We can compute the Jacobian matrix of the output with respect to the input or the weight matrix more conveniently.
For more introduction to Kronecker product and vectorization, the readers can refer to
(
Petersen et al., 2008
;
Graham, 2018
)

Appendix B
Derivation of Jacobian Matrix for Single-head Self-Attention

A single-head self-attention can be defined as

\bm{Y}=\bm{W}_{v}\bm{X}\bm{A},

where
\bm{A}=\operatorname{softmax}(\frac{\bm{P}}{\sqrt{d_{q}}})
in which
\bm{P}=\bm{X}^{\top}{\bm{W}_{q}}^{\top}{\bm{W}_{k}}{\bm{X}}
.
The matrix
\bm{A}
is called the attention matrix and
\frac{\bm{P}}{\sqrt{d_{q}}}
is called the logit,
\bm{A}\in\mathcal{R}^{n\times n},\bm{X}\in\mathcal{R}^{d\times n},\bm{W}_{v}\in\mathcal{R}^{d_{v}\times d}
. Here, our goal is to calculate
\frac{\partial\text{vec}(\bm{Y})}{\partial\text{vec}(\bm{X})}
.

In the main body, we have derived
\frac{\partial\operatorname{vec}(\bm{P})}{\partial\operatorname{vec}(\bm{X})}
.
Here, let us calculate the matrix calculus of
\bm{A}=\operatorname{softmax}(\frac{\bm{P}}{\sqrt{d_{q}}})
with respect to
\bm{P}
using Kronecker products and vectorization. We can rewrite it as
\bm{A}=\exp(\frac{\bm{P}}{\sqrt{d_{q}}})\oslash(\bm{1}_{n}\bm{1}_{n}^{\top}\exp(\frac{\bm{P}}{\sqrt{d_{q}}}))
, where
\bm{1}_{n}
denotes an
n
-dimensional vector of 1’s
in
\mathcal{R}^{n}
.
Note that
\bm{A}
is obtained by conducting a softmax operation in each column individually.

First, let us define two intermediate variables:

\bm{B}=\exp(\frac{\bm{P}}{\sqrt{d_{q}}}),\quad\bm{C}=\bm{1}\bm{1}^{\top}\exp(\frac{\bm{P}}{\sqrt{d_{q}}})=\bm{1}\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{B}.

In this way, we can represent the attention matrix
\bm{A}
as
\bm{A}=\bm{B}\oslash\bm{C}
.

Then, we can vectorize the equation
\bm{A}=\bm{B}\oslash\bm{C}
as follows:

\text{vec}(\bm{A})=\text{vec}(\bm{B}\oslash\bm{C})=\text{vec}(\bm{B})\oslash\text{vec}(\bm{C}).

According to the chain rule, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\text{vec}(\bm{A})}{\partial\text{vec}(\bm{C})}

Appendix C
Proof of Benign Entropy Collapse

Recall that
\bm{A}=\operatorname{softmax}(\frac{\bm{P}}{\sqrt{d_{q}}})
where
\bm{P}=\bm{X}^{\top}{\bm{W}_{q}}^{\top}{\bm{W}_{k}}{\bm{X}}
. Here, let
\bm{W}={\bm{W}_{q}}^{\top}{\bm{W}_{k}}
and
\bm{W}\in\mathcal{R}^{d\times d}
. In this way, we have that
\bm{A}=\operatorname{softmax}({\frac{\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{W}{\bm{X}}}{\sqrt{d_{q}}}})
. We know that
\operatorname{rank}(\bm{W})\leq d_{q}
and
d_{q}<d
.
To prove
\bm{A}
will always collapse to an identity matrix when
\bm{W}
is a non-symmetric positive quasi-definite square matrix, it is equivalent to prove
\mathbb{E}\left[{\bm{x}_{i}}^{\top}\bm{W}\bm{x}_{i}\right]\gg\mathbb{E}\left[{\bm{x}_{i}}^{\top}\bm{W}\bm{x}_{j}\right]
for any
i\neq j
.
It will be very hard to prove it mathematically if
\bm{W}
is a form of a non-symmetric positive quasi-definite square matrix. Therefore, let us make some simplification assumptions.
Assume
\bm{W}
is a real symmetric positive semi-definite square matrix and its trace is in direct proportion to the dimension
d_{q}
, and any
\bm{x}_{i}
is a high-dimension random vector and each element in
x_{i,j}\mathrel{\overset{\text{iid}}{\scalebox{2}[1]{$\sim$}}}\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.

We break our proof into two sub-problems.

Proposition 3

(Expectation of
{\bm{x}_{i}}^{\top}\bm{W}\bm{x}_{i}
)
.

Let
\bm{W}
be a real symmetric positive semi-definite matrix, and any
\bm{x}_{i}
be a high-dimensional random vector. Then, we have
\mathbb{E}\left[{\bm{x}_{i}}^{\top}\bm{W}\bm{x}_{i}\right]=\operatorname{trace}(\bm{W})
.

Proof.

Let
\bm{W}
be a real symmetric positive semi-definite, thus it can be decomposed into
\bm{W}=\bm{U}\Sigma\bm{U}^{\top}
. In this way, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\bm{x}_{i}}^{\top}\bm{W}\bm{x}_{i}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[{\bm{x}_{i}}^{\top}\bm{U}\Sigma\bm{U}^{\top}\bm{x}_{i}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{z}^{\top}\Sigma\bm{z}\right]\quad\text{(let $\bm{z}=\bm{U}^{\top}\bm{x}_{i}$)}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sigma_{i}z_{i}^{2}\right]\quad\text{($\Sigma$ is a diagonal matrix)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d_{q}}\sigma_{i}\times(0+1)\quad\text{(by independence, mean 0)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d_{q}}\sigma_{i}=\operatorname{trace}(\bm{W}).

For a real symmetric positive semi-definite, all its singular values are larger or equal to 0. Thus, we know
\sum_{i=1}^{d_{q}}\sigma_{i}>0
considering
\bm{W}
is not a matrix of all zeros.
∎

Proposition 4

(Expectation of
{\bm{x}_{i}}^{\top}\bm{W}\bm{x}_{j}
for
i\neq j
)
.

Let
\bm{W}
be a real symmetric positive semi-definite square matrix, and any
\bm{x}_{i}
is a high-dimension random vector,
\mathbb{E}_{i\neq j}\left[{\bm{x}_{i}}^{\top}\bm{W}\bm{x}_{j}\right]=0
.

Proof.

Let
\bm{W}
be a real symmetric positive semi-definite. Thus, it can be decomposed into
\bm{W}=\bm{U}\Sigma\bm{U}^{\top}
. In this way, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{i\neq j}\left[{\bm{x}_{i}}^{\top}\bm{W}\bm{x}_{j}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{i\neq j}\left[{\bm{x}_{i}}^{\top}\bm{U}\Sigma\bm{U}^{\top}\bm{x}_{j}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{z}^{\top}\Sigma\bm{v}\right]\quad\text{(let $\bm{z}=\bm{U}^{\top}\bm{x}_{i}$, and $\bm{v}=\bm{U}^{\top}\bm{x}_{j}$)}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{d_{q}}\sigma_{ij}z_{i}v_{j}\right]\quad\text{($\Sigma$ is a diagonal matrix. $\bm{z}$ and $\bm{v}$ are independent)}

\displaystyle=0.

∎

According to Proposition
3
and Proposition
4
, we can have that
\mathbb{E}\left[{\bm{x}_{i}}^{\top}\bm{W}\bm{x}_{i}\right]>\mathbb{E}\left[{\bm{x}_{i}}^{\top}\bm{W}\bm{x}_{j}\right]
for any
i\neq j
. Considering that
\bm{W}
is usually a high-dimensional matrix and some of its singular values are significantly larger than 0. Thus, after the softmax operation,
\bm{A}
will always collapse to an identity matrix. In this way, the self-attention module degenerates into a linear projection module. The model fitting ability will decline, but model training will not crash. Our proof is based on matrix computations
(
Golub & Van Loan, 2013
)
and high-dimensional probability
(
Vershynin, 2018
)
.

Appendix D
Proof of Malignant Entropy Collapse

Proof.

Step 1
. To prove the sparsity of
\bm{A}
, we must show that the number of non-zero elements in each column of
\bm{A}
is small.

Now, let’s consider the properties of the matrix
\bm{P}=\bm{X}^{T}\bm{W}\bm{X}
. Since
\bm{X}
and
\bm{W}
are low-rank matrices, the matrix
\bm{P}
will also be low-rank. Specifically, the rank of
\bm{P}
is bounded by the rank of
\bm{X}
and
\bm{W}
, which is much smaller than the dimension. In particular,
\bm{P}
has only a small number of significant singular values. This implies that the entries in
\bm{P}
are concentrated in a lower-dimensional subspace.

When we apply the softmax function to the rows of
\bm{P}
, the function concentrates most of the probability mass on a few components of each column. This is because the softmax function is sharply peaked around the largest values in each row. The smaller values in each row contribute less to the sum in the denominator of the softmax function, and therefore, their corresponding entries in
\bm{A}
will be small.

Thus, in each column of
\bm{A}
, only a small number of entries will be non-zero with high probability, and the rest will be close to zero. This establishes the sparsity of
\bm{A}
.

Step 2
. To prove the low-rankness of
\bm{A}
, we turn to proving that
\bm{A}
is approximately low-rank.

As noted earlier, the matrix
\bm{P}=\bm{X}^{T}\bm{W}\bm{X}
is low-rank. Specifically, the rank of
\bm{P}
is bounded by the ranks of
\bm{X}
and
\bm{W}
, which are both small. Therefore,
\bm{P}
has only a few dominant singular values. Assume that the softmax function does not significantly change the rank of the matrix. The rank of
\bm{A}
is controlled by the rank of
\bm{P}
, as the softmax operation only introduces nonlinearities that do not increase the rank.

Thus, since
\bm{P}
has a small number of dominant singular values,
\bm{A}
, formed by the softmax of
\bm{P}
, will also have a small number of significant singular values. This implies that
\bm{A}
is approximately low-rank.

In conclusion, under the assumptions that
\bm{X}
and
\bm{W}
are low-rank matrices with sufficiently large singular values, the matrix
\bm{A}
formed by applying the softmax function to
\bm{P}
will exhibit the properties of both sparsity and low-rankness with high probability.

∎

Appendix E
Proof of Weyl’s Inequality on Singular Values

Our derivation depends on
Horn & Johnson (1991)
;
Horn & Johnson (2012)
. Readers can refer to these material for more background information.

Proof.

Before we prove Weyl’s Inequality on singular values, let us review Courant-Fischer min-max principle that is
important for analyzing the singular values of matrix.

Theorem 3

(Courant-Fischer Min-max Principle for Singular Values)
.

Let

m\geq n
.
\bm{W}
has ordered singular values

\sigma_{1}\geq\sigma_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\sigma_{n}\geq 0
.
Then, for

\sigma_{i}(\bm{W})=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subset\mathcal{R}^{n}\\
\dim(S)=i\end{subarray}}\ \min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{x}\in S\\
\|\bm{x}\|=1\end{subarray}}\|\bm{W}\bm{x}\|=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}S^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{R}^{n}\\
\dim(S^{\prime})=n-i+1\end{subarray}}\ \max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{x}\in S^{\prime}\\
\|\bm{x}\|=1\end{subarray}}\|\bm{W}\bm{x}\|

where the maximum is taken over all
i
-dimensional subspaces
S
of
\mathcal{R}^{n}
,
and the minimum is taken over all unit vectors
\bm{x}
in
S
.

Let
\bm{W}_{1}=\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}_{1}\bm{V}^{\top}
and
\bm{W}_{2}=\bm{\mathcal{U}}\bm{\Sigma}_{2}\bm{\mathcal{V}}^{\top}
be singular value decompositions
of
\bm{W}_{1}
and
\bm{W}_{2}
with unitary matrix
\bm{V}=[\bm{v}_{1},\ldots,\bm{v}_{n}],\bm{\mathcal{V}}=[\bm{\mathfrak{v}}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\mathfrak{v}}_{n}]
where
\bm{v}_{i},\bm{\mathfrak{v}}_{i}\in\mathcal{R}^{n}
and unitary matrix

\bm{U}=[\bm{u}_{1},\ldots,\bm{u}_{m}],\bm{\mathcal{U}}=[\bm{\mathfrak{u}}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\mathfrak{u}}_{m}]
, where
\bm{u}_{j},\bm{\mathfrak{u}}_{j}\in\mathcal{R}^{m}
.

Let
i
and
j
be positive integers with

1\leq i,j\leq n
and
i+j\leq n+1
.
Let
S_{1}\equiv\text{Span}\{\bm{v}_{i},\ldots,\bm{v}_{n}\}
and
S_{2}\equiv\text{Span}\{\bm{\mathfrak{v}}_{j},\ldots,\bm{\mathfrak{v}}_{n}\}
; notice that
\dim(S_{1})=n-i+1
and
\dim(S_{2})=n-j+1

k\equiv\dim(S_{1}\cap S_{2})
, then we have

\displaystyle\dim(S_{1}\cap S_{2})=\dim(S_{1})+\dim(S_{2})-\dim(S_{1}+S_{2})

\displaystyle=(n-i+1)+(n-j+1)-\dim(S_{1}+S_{2})

\displaystyle\geq(n-i+1)+(n-j+1)-n=n-(i+j-1)+1\geq 1.

Because of the bounds assumed for
i
and
j
. Thus, the subspace
S_{1}\cap S_{2}
has
positive dimension
k
,
n-k+1\leq i+j-1
, and we have

\displaystyle\sigma_{i+j-1}(\bm{W}_{1}+\bm{W}_{2})

\displaystyle\leq\sigma_{n-k+1}(\bm{W}_{1}+\bm{W}_{2})

\displaystyle=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subset\mathcal{R}^{n}\\
\dim(S)=k\end{subarray}}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{x}\in S\\
\|\bm{x}\|_{2}=1\end{subarray}}\|(\bm{W}_{1}+\bm{W}_{2})\bm{x}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{x}\in S_{1}\cap S_{2}\\
\|\bm{x}\|_{2}=1\end{subarray}}\|(\bm{W}_{1}+\bm{W}_{2})\bm{x}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{x}\in S_{1}\cap S_{2}\\
\|\bm{x}\|_{2}=1\end{subarray}}\|\bm{W}_{1}\bm{x}\|_{2}+\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{x}\in S_{1}\cap S_{2}\\

Appendix F
Related Works

Training Dynamics of Transformer.
Previous works have delved into understanding the training dynamics of Transformers from two different perspectives: a high-level perspective and a low-level perspective. From a high-level perspective, Scan&Snap
(
Tian et al., 2023a
)
unveiled complex phenomena, particularly in single-layer architectures, relating to frequency and discriminative bias. These studies linked sparse attention patterns to token co-occurrence frequencies and observed two-stage behaviors in attention logits.
JoMA
(
Tian et al., 2023b
)
further improved upon previous models by incorporating residual connections and MLP nonlinearity, analyzing joint training of MLP and self-attention layers, and offering qualitative explanations for multilayer Transformer dynamics. From a low-level perspective, two critical challenges in Transformer training have been identified: rank collapse
(
Dong et al., 2021
;
Noci et al., 2022
)
, where attention output converges to a rank 1 matrix, potentially causing vanishing gradients; and entropy collapse
(
Zhai et al., 2023
)
, which denotes pathologically low attention entropy, corresponding to highly concentrated attention scores.
In this work, we analyze and prove two different entropy collapse modes and identify the key reason for model failure is spectral energy
concentration. Finally, we introduce a simple but effective solution to address this problem.

Training Stability of Transformer.

ReZero
(
Bachlechner et al., 2021
)
introduces a simple yet effective mechanism for improving training stability. The key innovation lies in initializing residual connections to zero, which allows networks to learn identity mappings more easily.
Admin
(
Liu et al., 2020
)
introduces a new network initialization strategy tailored for Transformers to make the network train stable.
DeepNorm
(
Wang et al., 2022
)
extends the concept of normalization to accommodate increasingly deeper networks. By dynamically adjusting normalization parameters, DeepNorm ensures stability even as network depth increases.
LipsFormer
Qi et al. (2023a)
addresses the specific challenge of stability in transformer networks. By introducing a Lipschitz continuity constraint, Lipsformer effectively mitigates the issue of exploding gradients - a common problem in deep transformer architectures. This approach ensures that the network’s output changes smoothly with respect to its input, promoting overall stability. ReZero, Admin, and DeepNorm can all be considered as an approach to control the Lipschitz constant of the network in the initial stage.

In this work, by revisiting the training dynamics of Transformer, we can achieve a stable training only by modifying the optimizer instead of using learning rate warmup or changing the network structures as LipsFormer
(
Qi et al., 2023a
)
and QKNorm
Henry et al. (2020)
;
Dehghani et al. (2023)
.

Learning Rate Schedule.
Warmup
(
Loshchilov & Hutter, 2016
)
has emerged as a must-have technique for ensuring a stable network training, especially in the initial phases of the optimization process. This method involves gradually increasing the learning rate from a small value to the desired initial learning rate over a certain number of training steps or epochs.
The cosine learning rate scheduler
(
Loshchilov & Hutter, 2016
)
has gained popularity due to its smooth annealing properties. This schedule decreases the learning rate following a cosine curve, starting from an initial value and decaying to a minimum value over a set number of epochs or iterations.
Cyclic learning rates
(
Smith, 2017
)
involve systematically varying the learning rate between boundary values. The learning rate oscillates between a lower and upper bound, either linearly or following other patterns (e.g., triangular, cosine).
The above-mentioned learning rate schedules require specification of a stopping time step
T
,
Defazio et al. (2024)
introduces a Schedule-Free approach that avoids the need for this stopping time by eschewing the use of schedules entirely.

Compared to the up-mentioned works, the core novel contributions of our work lie on follows.

1.

We present a theoretical analysis for Transformer training and point out two entropy collapse modes,
i
.
e
.the benign collapse and the malignant collapse.

2.

We reveal that
spectral energy concentration (SEC) of
\ {\bm{W}_{q}}^{\top}\bm{W}_{k}
is the main reason of model crash.

3.

We introduce AdamW
2
, a new optimization strategy motivated by Weyl’s Inequality.

We also observe there are two works
(
Kosson et al., 2023
;
Kosson et al., 2024
)
discussing the needs of learning rate warmup by explicitly controlling the angular
updates via Rotational Optimizer Variants and by limiting the Frobenius norm of the update relative to that of the weights. They provided some different perspectives on the necessity
of the learning rate warmup.
