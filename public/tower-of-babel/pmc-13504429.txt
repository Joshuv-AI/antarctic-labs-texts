Primary Cardioprotection in Frontline Anthracycline Therapy for Adult Hematologic Cancers
Authors: Alexis Chen Boulter, Yimei Li, Xin Wang, Daniel J. Zheng, Keith Pratz, Selina Luger, Richard Aplenc, Kelly D. Getz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This cohort study investigates uptake of cardioprotection among patients with adult hematologic malignant neoplasms receiving anthracycline.

Introduction

Anthracyclines remain a cornerstone of frontline cytotoxic therapy for many adult hematologic malignant neoplasms at cumulative doses conferring substantial risk for cardiotoxic effects.1 Two cardioprotective strategies have demonstrated efficacy in mitigating this risk: liposomal anthracycline formulations and dexrazoxane.2,3 Consensus recommendations for these strategies remain limited. The exception is CPX-351, recommended for therapy-related acute myeloid leukemia (AML) and AML with myelodysplasia-related changes4; however, this is based on improved disease response, not cardioprotection. No guidance exists for primary dexrazoxane use in adult hematologic malignant neoplasms, with prospective trials limited to adult solid tumors.5 In contrast, dexrazoxane is used near universally in pediatric AML, based on evidence of reduced cardiotoxic effects without compromise of disease response.6 Despite this, uptake of cardioprotection strategies in adult hematologic malignant neoplasms and their association with overall survival (OS) remain poorly characterized. We aimed to address these data gaps.

Methods

This cohort study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline (eAppendix in Supplement 1). The University of Pennsylvania institutional review board approved the protocol as exempt from review and consent on February 17, 2025, because this study was determined to be secondary research using deidentified data (No. 25-0224).

We leveraged the US-based, electronic health record (EHR)–derived, deidentified Flatiron Health Research Database, containing demographic, clinical, disease, and treatment information (eMethods in Supplement 1).7 Adults (aged ≥18 years) diagnosed with AML, diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL), follicular lymphoma, or mantle cell lymphoma between January 1, 2014, and January 1, 2024, who received at least 1 line of systemic therapy were included. Cardioprotection was defined as receipt of liposomal anthracyclines or dexrazoxane during frontline therapy. Because cardioprotection was exceedingly rare in follicular lymphoma and mantle cell lymphoma, subsequent analyses were restricted to AML and DLBCL. Given that patients with secondary AML likely received CPX-351 for differing disease-directed indications, the primary analysis was limited to de novo AML.

OS was measured from frontline therapy initiation to death, with follow-up administratively censored at the cohort-specific median follow-up time to mitigate differential follow-up and attrition inherent to clinical EHR-derived data. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards models estimated hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs for the association between cardioprotection and OS. Additional details are in the eMethods in Supplement 1. Statistical analyses were performed using Stata/BE version 18.0 (StataCorp). All statistical tests were 2-sided, and P < .05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Among 13 331 adults treated with frontline anthracycline-containing regimens, 3661 had AML, including 2125 with de novo AML (median [IQR] age, 57 [45-66] years; 53.1% male; 7.3% Black, 7.1% Hispanic, 65.5% White, and 10.4% missing) (Figure 1); 7241 had DLBCL (median [IQR] age, 68 [58-76] years; 56.3% male; 6.3% Black, 7.7% Hispanic, 65.5% White, and 11.4% missing) (Figure 2); 1154 had mantle cell lymphoma; and 1275 had follicular lymphoma. Public insurance was used among 15.1% of patients with de novo AML and 31.4% of those with DLBCL. Use of cardioprotective strategies was infrequent. Cardioprotection was used in 2.4% of patients with de novo AML, almost exclusively with CPX-351. In DLBCL, cardioprotection was used by 1.1% of patients; among them, 67.1% received liposomal formulations and 32.9% received dexrazoxane. Among eligible patients with DLBCL, 1.5% received R-CEOP, a frontline nonanthracycline regimen. Patients were more likely to receive cardioprotection if they were older, male, publicly insured, and had unfavorable cytomolecular risk features. Cardioprotection use was highly concentrated within a small number of practices. In adjusted analyses, liposomal anthracycline use was not associated with worse OS in de novo AML (HR, 1.45; 95% CI, 0.95-2.21). In DLBCL, neither liposomal anthracyclines (HR, 1.00; 95% CI, 0.60-1.66) nor dexrazoxane (HR, 1.18; 95% CI, 0.61-2.29) use was associated with a difference in OS.

Discussion

In this large national clinical practice cohort study, cardioprotective strategies were rarely used in adults receiving frontline anthracycline-based therapy for hematologic malignant neoplasm. Use primarily involved liposomal formulations; dexrazoxane uptake was nearly absent. Cardioprotection was not associated with worse OS in de novo AML or DLBCL. Limitations included a lack of information on baseline cardiac function, frailty, and intensive care use, factors which are poorly captured in EHR data. Importantly, the number of patients who received cardioprotective strategies was exceedingly small, particularly in AML. This limited the statistical power to detect survival differences in this study and our ability to make conclusions regarding treatment safety.

Given that a frontline nonanthracycline regimen option exists for DLBCL with R-CEOP, which substitutes etoposide for doxorubicin, we additionally evaluated the use of R-CEOP within the cohort. Among eligible patients with DLBCL, 1.5% received R-CEOP, suggesting that patients perceived to be at higher baseline risk for cardiotoxic effects may preferentially receive nonanthracycline containing frontline regimens rather than cardioprotection strategies.

Overall, cardioprotective strategies remain understudied in adult hematologic malignant neoplasms. The lack of prospective data in adult hematologic malignant neoplasms, logistical barriers, and the historic concerns for reduced antitumor efficacy likely contribute to this gap.8 In pediatric AML, near-universal dexrazoxane adoption has been associated with reduced acute and long-term cardiotoxic effects without compromised antileukemic efficacy.6 Given that adults have higher rates of cardiovascular comorbidities and lower baseline cardiac reserve, cardioprotective strategies warrant further prospective evaluation in this population. Prospective randomized studies are needed to evaluate the safety and efficacy of broader cardioprotection use, particularly dexrazoxane, in adult hematologic malignant neoplasms.
