Three-dimensional infection network analysis in maize reveals variation in fungal colonization associated with lesion phenotypes
Authors: Alexander Nedo, Yael Lupu-Haber, Lilach Pnueli, Neta Holland, Nitsan Dahan, B. Gillian Turgeon, Benjamin A. Horwitz, Randall J. Wisser, Jeffrey L. Caplan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Quantitative disease resistance in plants emerges from complex interactions between host tissues and pathogen growth dynamics, producing a spectrum of phenotypic responses. In plant-fungal interactions, disease is most visibly expressed through lesions that vary in number, size, shape, and color, collectively defining a lesion profile. For Cochliobolus heterostrophus, a fungus causing Southern Corn Leaf Blight of maize (Zea mays ssp. mays), we show that infection on different maize genotypes produces strikingly different lesion profiles. However, it remains unclear whether such macroscopic variation in lesion profiles corresponds to consistent differences in the three-dimensional organization of pathogen colonization within host tissue. We therefore examined variation in the three-dimensional structure of C. heterostrophus-infection networks across host genotypes representing four lesion-profile classes. Using light-sheet microscopy and filament-tracing methods adapted from neuroscience, we developed quantitative metrics to characterize infection network organization, including depth, density, shape, and spatial association with host vascular tissue. In this dataset, network depth was similar across genotypes, whereas network morphology (shape and density), spatial association with vascular bundles, hyphal segment length, and branching frequency varied. Notably, genotypes with similar quantitative resistance levels sometimes exhibited distinct patterns of fungal colonization, suggesting that comparable resistance can arise from different underlying infection dynamics. These findings indicate that lesion profiles may not uniquely predict infection network structure and highlight the utility of three-dimensional network metrics for describing variation that likely reflects multiple underlying host and pathogen processes. This multi-scale framework provides tools for linking macroscopic disease phenotypes with microscopic infection processes in quantitative disease resistance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-52604-6.

Introduction

Genetically determined resistance is the most widespread and environmentally benign approach to mitigate diseases in crop plants1,2. Two predominant forms of genetic resistance exist: qualitative and quantitative. Qualitative, all-or-none resistance is controlled by a so-called “gene-for-gene” interaction that functions via a generally conserved innate immune system3,4. In the case of resistance, plants typically encode a protein that perceives the presence of a pathogen protein, which elicits a rapid, localized hypersensitive defense response in the host that prevents disease development5. The large effect of this monogenic mechanism lends itself to breeding disease resistant varieties (introgressing a single gene is relatively straightforward), but it imposes intense selection pressure on the pathogen, increasing the likelihood for a breakdown in host resistance6,7. In contrast, quantitative disease resistance (QDR) confers broad and durable resistance through polygenic control, which produces continuous phenotypic variation8,9. Advances in quantitative genetics have helped to dissect the genetic architecture and pinpoint some of the genes that underlie variation in QDR10–15. These findings have uncovered genes with diverse mechanisms of action11,16,17 which may impede different stages of pathogenesis. For instance, for the fungal pathogen Setosphaeria turcica that infects maize, one quantitative trait locus (QTL) conditions partial resistance to fungal penetration into the leaf while another QTL conditions partial resistance to invasion of the vascular tissue18. Still, due in large part to its complexity, our understanding of the plant-pathogen interactions underlying QDR has only begun to emerge. A working hypothesis is that the biological complexity of QDR, including the number of QTL and their apparent mechanistic diversity, confers durability at the cropping scale19.

Southern Corn Leaf Blight (SCLB) of maize (Zea mays subsp. mays; Zm) is caused by the fungal pathogen Cochliobolus heterostrophus (Ch). Spores of Ch germinate on the leaf surface; within hours, hyphae penetrate the tissue and grow intercellularly around initially viable host cells, followed by host cell death and reproduction on necrotic tissue20,21. Natural variation in resistance to Ch on maize is predominantly quantitative, although some major, qualitative-type resistance and susceptibility loci are known for SCLB. For example, T-urf13 is a unique susceptibility allele encoded by the host mitochondrion that results in sensitivity to T-toxin-producing strains (race T) of Ch22–25. On T-toxin sensitive maize genotypes (carrying T-urf13), widely planted in the 1960’s, SCLB caused a major epidemic in the 1970s in the USA26,27. While the identification of cms-T-associated susceptibility rapidly mitigated the SCLB epidemic28, Ch strains remain broadly virulent on maize and breeding for QDR is the primary strategy for deploying genetically determined resistance. Moreover, SCLB is one of the targets of multiple disease resistance in maize, defined as a host plant (as opposed to non-host) resistance to two or more diseases29.

The phenotypic variation in QDR reflects multiple components of the host-pathogen interaction, including infection efficiency, latent period, lesion size, and spore production as described in the rust-wheat pathosystem30. Lesion traits—number, size, shape, and color—also capture variation in host response. For SCLB, we define a lesion profile as the combination of size, shape, and color, where color serves as a proxy for host cell death due to loss of pigmentation or redox reactions forming new pigments in chlorotic and necrotic tissue31. These traits are rarely quantified in an integrated manner or linked directly to pathogen development within host tissue, due in part to limited approaches for bridging macroscopic and microscopic observations. Imaging advances are beginning to address this gap. At the cellular scale, to give two examples, methods such as two-photon microscopy have enabled three-dimensional visualization of haustorial development in the Puccinia striiformis - wheat pathosystem32, and imaging of the Magnaporthe oryzae (grisea) - rice interaction identified the biotrophic interfacial complex33. At larger scales, symptom imaging has been associated with disease progression in Zymoseptoria tritici blotch and leaf rusts34,35, while recent pipelines enable time-resolved quantification of host cell death and fungal colonization in both compatible and incompatible interactions36. In prior work on the Zm-Ch pathosystem, we developed a platform for confocal microscopic imaging of fungal infection networks37. Registration of microscopic and macroscopic images of the lesions showed that the extent of fungal infection was more closely reflected in lesion size in one maize genotype (B73) than another (Mo17), suggesting genotype-dependent links between infection dynamics and lesion development. Indeed, infection of different maize lines with the same Ch wild type strain can produce lesion types so different that they might appear to represent different diseases (Fig. 1).

If distinct lesion profiles reflect different defense mechanisms, they could provide new phenotyping tools for investigating QDR. Therefore, this study aimed to characterize variation in Zm-Ch interactions across both macroscopic (whole-leaf) and microscopic (leaf tissue) scales. We observed wide variation in lesion types across a diverse panel of 27 maize genotypes (founders of a maize nested association panel38,39). From this panel, a subset of eight genotypes was selected for microscopy analysis, representing distinct lesion profiles and spanning the broad spectrum of QDR to SCLB. Light-sheet imaging of fungal infection networks enabled the development of novel metrics to characterize infection network morphology and to assess associations between macroscopic and microscopic variation.

Results

Starting at the macroscopic scale, we observed that lesions formed in response to Ch infection varied considerably across maize genotypes, defining four lesion-profile classes. Then, proceeding to light-sheet microscopy, several new metrics were used to quantify properties of Ch-infection networks. These are described in a separate section below. Most metrics are based on fundamental descriptors of networks that were developed for neurons; however, the last, vascular overlap, addresses a key feature of infection by Ch, which in early infection stages grows between adjacent cells and later is often observed to associate with the leaf vascular bundles of maize.

Maize genotypes can be distinguished by four major classes of SCLB lesion profiles

In three repeated growth chamber experiments (E1-E3) of Ch-inoculations on a reference panel of 27 maize genotypes, multiple lesion types were observed ranging from small chlorotic spots or sharply defined lesions, to more prominent lesions including light- or dark-centered eyespot-like lesions and larger necrotic spots (Supplementary Fig. 1). For some genotypes, a uniform lesion type was formed (e.g., Ki3), while for others multiple types of lesions were formed on the same plant (e.g., B73). Based on the variation in lesions observed among the 27 genotypes, four major classes of “lesion profiles” were defined (Supplementary Fig. 1; Fig. 1A). Class I was defined by very small, chlorotic spots. Class II was defined by small well-focused lesions that tended to be symmetrical. Class III was defined by the presence of eyespot-like lesions considered typical of the Zm-Ch interaction, but also included finer-scale differences leading to subclasses according to whether edges or centers of the lesions tended to be darker or lighter. Class IV was defined by large and necrotic type lesions.

For further investigation, we selected eight of the 27 genotypes with lesion profiles from each class (Fig. 1A). These included Mo17 (Class I, mild chlorotic); Ki3 and CML247 (Class II, mostly small, sharp outline, with some larger and necrotic spots on CML247); B73, Tx303 (Class III, mostly light center), and M37W (Class III, light and dark centers); Hp301 and Il14H (Class IV, necrotic). They also encompassed a wide range of quantitative resistance to SCLB, spanning the 1st to 94th percentiles of adult-plant disease severity measured in field plots for a previously described panel of 270 genotypes (File S1 in Zhong et al.40; Supplementary Dataset 1 of this study). To characterize the macroscopic lesions and examine the underlying Ch infection networks by microscopy analysis, a fourth experiment (E4) with the eight genotypes was performed using the same Ch-strain and similar growth conditions as the former experiments. Supplementary Fig. 2 shows images of Ch infections on the second leaf at four days post-inoculation for three separate plants. The lesion types observed on each genotype were consistent across experiments, with one exception: necrotic lesions on several maize genotypes in E1-E2 performed at Cornell appeared as light-colored spots without well-defined edges, while in E3-E4 performed at Technion, the lesions were dark. This difference was likely due to experimental conditions: at Cornell, inoculated plants were incubated in a mist chamber and grown openly thereafter, while at Technion plants were kept in sealed bags following inoculation (see Methods; cf. NC358 and Hp301, Supplementary Fig. 1).

A modal peak in lesion size at 2 mm2 marks differences in disease progression across genotypes

The number and size of lesions produced on a host provide a first level of quantitation for resistance. The whole-leaf leaf images from E4 shown in Supplementary Fig. 2 were processed to segment lesions and quantify the total number and size distribution, using a calibrated color cutoff to differentiate visibly diseased from healthy tissue. Across genotypes, the total number of lesions detected ranged from 173 to 519 (Fig. 1B), with approximately 4-fold variation in lesion density (1 lesion per 8 to 31 mm2 leaf area) and 100-fold variation in lesion size (0.1 to 10 mm or more). However, lesion densities varied considerably across plants for some genotypes (e.g., Tx303; Supplementary Fig. 2), so we focused on the distribution of lesion size (independent of leaf area), which was more consistent across replicate plants. The proportion of lesions per size class showed a general trend from a large number of small-sized lesions to a small number of large-sized lesions, with a second maximum of varying extent at 2 mm2. We chose this second maximum as a diagnostic measure of successful disease development: the smallest lesions included non-green spots in the leaf images that might not all reliably reflect disease development, while the largest ones were, typically, coalesced spots formed from multiple smaller lesions.

Ranking the genotypes based on their proportion of 2 mm2 lesions (Fig. 1C) was moderately correlated (Kendall’s τ = 0.5; p = 0.1) with the field resistance rankings (SCLB resistance values obtained from40). This indicated that normalized 2 mm2 lesion proportions measured on individual leaves in our experiments partially reflect field resistance rankings based on SCLB severity measurement41, particularly at the extremes of resistance and susceptibility, despite differences between young plants measured in growth chamber conditions and adult plants evaluated in field environments.

Salient features of Ch-infection networks are revealed by light-sheet microscopy

Differences in lesion type and size are likely to arise through various mechanisms that underlie QDR19. We questioned if the macroscopic variation in lesions we observed would be reflected by differences in the morphology of Ch-infection networks. To examine this, light-sheet microscopy was used to capture the three-dimensional structure of fungal infection networks from 56 separate lesions sampled across genotypes (n = 4–11 per genotype) in additional experiments (E5 series) using the same conditions as E3-E4. Figure 2A illustrates the workflow, which began by fixing, clearing, and labeling maize leaf punches as we previously described37. Our prior approach used laser scanning confocal microscopy, but here, we chose light-sheet microscopy for its fast volumetric imaging capabilities.

We observed considerable variation in the patterns formed by separate infection networks across samples. Nevertheless, some salient features could be identified. These are illustrated in Fig. 2B and include: small infection networks with a fluorescent halo, indicating a strong plant immune response as seen on M37W (and Mo17 – shown in Figure 7A); relatively small condensed or loosely expanded networks both seen on Ki3, networks strongly associated with the vascular bundles as detected on CML247; and substantially dense and expanded infection networks typical of necrotic lesions as seen on Hp301 (shown) and Il14H. Multiple types often coexisted on the same infected leaf as well. In retrospect, we should have tracked the lesion type associated with each tissue sample used for microscopy. We cannot distinguish whether salient features are a property of lesion type or due to other sources of variation affecting separate infections by Ch within the same plant.

To examine the three-dimensional structure of the infection networks of Ch, we reconstructed them using the commercially available Imaris Filament Tracer originally designed for structurally similar vascular/neuronal networks. Figure 3 illustrates the workflow for Imaris fungal network analysis. Segmented networks were manually curated to distinguish independent infections by confirming a single point-of-origin of the infection site at the leaf surface (marked by an appressorium). For each infection network, we used Imaris to extract whole-network metrics for shape characteristics and filament metrics for branching characteristics (Fig. 3; Supplementary Tables 2 and 3). Not every metric could be obtained for the full set of 57 networks we reconstructed, and the field of view imaged was sometimes insufficient to encompass entire networks, which were found to be larger than initially apparent from the visible lesion on the leaf surface. Therefore, depending on the specific metric used, analyses of infection networks included a maximum of 56 and a minimum of 47 reconstructions (sample sizes labeled in each figure). Whole network metrics are expected to be more sensitive to incomplete networks, so for these we excluded nine of the network reconstructions with major cutoff in the image. In contrast, individual filament metrics and vascular overlap (presented in a later section) are expected to be largely independent of total infection network size, so for these we retained all network reconstructions.

Before presenting the analyses, we note that the number of genotypes (n = 8) and genotype-level sample sizes were small (n = 4–11 among the 56 reconstructions; n = 3–10 among the filtered set of 47). To guard against false positives when comparing genotypes under these conditions, we adopted a uniformly conservative statistical approach: rank-based, non-parametric tests (Kendall’s τ and Kruskal-Wallis), a significance threshold of α = 0.01, and correction for multiple comparisons using the Conover-Iman procedure. This approach was applied consistently across all analyses reported below, with two exceptions for filament metrics analyzed across all networks. Nonetheless, even statistically significant results between genotypes should be interpreted as findings specific to the results obtained here, rather than as grounds for broad generalization. We view them as a basis for targeted hypotheses and as leads for future work with larger and more diverse samples.

Ch shows adaptive growth of infection networks, apparently independent of maize resistance

For the full set of 58 Ch-infection networks, we characterized each network’s branching structure and the length of every single non-branched segment (we refer to these as filaments since they usually include multiple hyphal cells). Both filament metrics showed significant differences among maize genotypes, but like whole-network metrics, neither one was significantly correlated with field resistance, lesion profile classifications, or the proportion of 2 mm2 lesions (described earlier).

To quantify branching structure, we used a metric that assigns each filament a hierarchical number indicating how many branching events separate it from the root (origin) filament. The final number of branching levels for infection networks ranged from 2 to 41. Since multiple branches can emerge from a single filament, the distribution of branch level frequencies across levels reflects how infection networks spread by branching through the host tissue. Across infection networks, individual filaments formed between 1 and 278 branches, with branch frequency increasing with hierarchical level up to level four and declining thereafter (Fig. 5A shows levels 1 to 22). For each Ch infection network, we identified the specific level with maximal branching frequency and tested for its association with the final number of hierarchical levels. Overall, peak levels were positively correlated with final levels of branching (Pearson’s r = 0.84, p = 3e-15), regardless of resistance level. This pattern was evident even among the three highly resistant genotypes (Ki3, Mo17, and CML247), which differed significantly in both their peak (Fig. 5B) and final branch levels (not shown), yet exhibited a range of variation comparable to that observed across intermediate and susceptible genotypes. This suggests that peak branching is adaptive, shifting proportionally according to a network’s eventual branching depth.

To analyze variation in filament lengths, we excluded 749 of 20,759 filaments with lengths less than 5 μm, which was near the threshold of our image resolution and may represent filaments corresponding to immature hyphae. Filaments averaged 44 μm in length. Within individual networks, the difference between maximum and minimum filament lengths ranged from 133 μm to 1.4 mm, with one infection network from CML247 capturing essentially the full extent of variation (from 6 μm to 1.4 mm filament lengths). Significant differences in filament lengths were detected between the two most susceptible genotypes, B73 and Hp301, which exhibited the largest and smallest median filament lengths (79 μm and 45 μm, respectively) among all genotypes (Fig. 5C; Supplementary Fig. 3).

Overall, filament lengths across hierarchical levels exhibited a distribution similar to branching frequencies, but with an average local maximum in filament length hierarchically shifted to level seven—approximately three levels deeper into the infection networks than peak branching frequency. While peak filament length showed a strong association with peak branching level overall (R2 = 0.58, p = 7e-11), the relationship was not 1:1. Instead, the separation between these peaks increased by approximately two levels for each unit increase in peak branch level (Fig. 5D). For example, for infection networks with a peak branch level of five, the longest filaments occurred around level 10. This suggests additional scaling relations may exist between filament size and branching structure in the growth of infection networks.

From individual filament lengths, total network length was also calculated for each infection network (Supplementary Table 1). Network size was not consistently related to filament length. For example, B73 had the longest filament lengths but overall had median sized infection networks. Differences were also observed among genotypes with contrasting lesion sizes (proportion of 2 mm² lesions), such as the smaller networks detected in the larger lesion-forming genotype M37W compared to Ki3.

Ch invades vascular bundle proximal space with genotype-specific modalities

In previous research, we found that as Ch invades the intercellular spaces of maize tissue it often grows proximal to vascular bundles 37. Here, since the vascular tissue was heavily stained by calcofluor white (Fig. 2; cyan lines), we measured the extent of Ch association with the vasculature using an ordinal score (Fig. 6A). Vascular overlap scores differed significantly among genotypes (Fig. 6B), but showed no significant rank association with field resistance overall (similar to traits in the previous section). However, examining genotypes by resistance level revealed distinct patterns: genotypes with the highest field resistance (Ki3 and Mo17) had the lowest vascular overlap scores, while genotypes with the lowest field resistance (B73 and Hp301) had the highest scores. Intermediate-resistance genotypes showed the opposite pattern, with higher vascular overlap scores associated with greater field resistance. For example, CML247, which has high field resistance (ranking 35th in the 270-panel) and forms mostly small, focused lesions (Fig. 1), had a relatively high vascular overlap score. These results offer further evidence that the cellular space surrounding the vasculature is a target of Ch growth in maize leaves, with suggestive evidence that genotypes differ in the way Ch colonizes this region of the leaf tissue.

Discussion

The genetic dissection of QDR to SCLB has largely been enabled by whole-plant or plot-level scoring of disease severity, which is feasible for large-scale phenotyping (i.e., hundreds to thousands of genotypes40,42. Nevertheless, measuring overall severity can conflate different manifestations of lesion formation and disease. This is suggested by research on the Botrytis cinerea-Arabidopsis interaction, in which different lesion properties appeared as genetically separable traits43. Here, we documented an array of lesion types formed by the Zm-Ch interaction across a small but diverse panel of maize genotypes (Supplementary Fig. 1), which, to our knowledge has not been reported previously. Although individual genotypes did not always produce a uniform lesion type, each exhibited a generally consistent set of lesion types within a leaf, serving as a basis for distinguishing four lesion-profile classes (Fig. 1; Supplementary Fig. 1). These lesion types and profile classifications provide a foundation for further characterization of disease development in the Zm-Ch pathosystem. Combined with microscopy and genomic tools, such finer-scale phenotypes may enable new approaches for dissecting QDR and could be integrated with macroscopic lesion parameters derived from image analysis34,35. However, because we did not maintain a direct correspondence between individual lesion types and their associated infection networks, our study was limited in its ability to test macroscopic–microscopic associations, particularly since some of the genotypes exhibited multiple lesion types (Fig. 1). Future work integrating these scales will be needed to resolve these relationships.

Microscopic imaging of a fungal infection network in three dimensions can provide information on the depth of infection in addition to the shape and structure of the network. Interactions with host tissues such as the vasculature can also be examined in three dimensions. For example, this approach can distinguish between fungal growth over a vascular bundle versus growth surrounding a vascular bundle. Here, we combined light-sheet microscopy with a filament tracing software (Imaris) that is used in neurosciencee.g.,44,45. Imaris has been previously used to visualize and quantify fungal infection volume in wheat, barley, and sugar beet in confocal images36 and our approach uses the filament tracer to obtain novel metrics for investigating fungal infection in plant tissue. A similar approach was used to examine in vitro growth of the fungus Aspergillus niger46 , and to quantify hyphal segment length and branching level of the human pathogen Aspergillus fumigatus47. The metrics used here included those segment and branching measurements and additional geometrical (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{R}_{p}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{R}_{o}$$\end{document}) and structural (hierarchical) features of the infection network, as well as vascular overlap (Fig. 6) which may be relevant to other pathogens like C. heterostrophus that appear to target their growth around the leaf vasculature. The goal of using the Imaris filament tracer, rather than other custom filament tracing methods we have developed previously, was to adapt a more readily accessible approach that does not require programming or coding expertise. These helped to expand examination of infection beyond the overall volume of an infection network36, revealing both adaptive development of fungal infection networks within a host, and genotype-specific modalities in resistance.

Maize genotypes exhibited substantial differences in Ch infection (Figs. 2B and 7A). This was evident even when comparing the three lines with the highest field resistance rankings and lowest proportions of 2 mm² lesions, Mo17, Ki3 and CML247. Resistance modes include one where infection is severely limited with very mild, mostly non-necrotic symptoms (Mo17); while in another pattern, resistance blocks infection from spreading by an HR-like response (Ki3, CML247). CML247 showed additional complexity, with some larger necrotic areas (Fig. 1A). We also observed genotypes that supported extensive infection networks with minimal visible lesions on the leaf and conversely, prominent lesions with relatively little fungal biomass. For example, for Ki3 compared to M37W, despite M37W having a lesion size distribution with greater proportions of large lesions (Fig. 1B-C) and a Type III macroscopic lesion profile (Fig. 1A, Supplementary Fig. 1), its infection networks tended to be smaller (Fig. 2B). This is quite striking because Ki3 has higher field resistance: Ki3 ranks 5th and M37W ranks 43rd among 270 genotypes, reported previously40. In another comparison, two genotypes with strongly contrasting field resistance (Mo17 ranking 14th and IL14H ranking 145th among 270 genotypes) exhibited similar median hyphal lengths (Fig. 5C), median total network lengths (Supplementary Table 1), and vascular overlap (Fig. 6B), yet differed markedly in lesion phenotype: Mo17 produced the Type I profile and IL14H produced the Type IV profile of large necrotic lesions. Together, these observations suggest that lesion profiles or field resistance do not uniquely reflect underlying infection dynamics and may not be neatly categorized across macroscopic and micscopic scales, but instead may represent distinct combinations of host defense responses.

This study examined variation in the morphology and spatial distribution of infection networks formed by Ch in maize leaf tissue and its association with macroscopic variation across genotypes with contrasting quantitative resistance to SCLB. A key contribution is the development of quantitative metrics for characterizing infection network geometry, structure, and spatial relationships with host tissue. While our study included many scanning hours, it was underpowered for testing general macroscopic-microscopic associations (only eight genotypes, limited samples per genotype), such that our biological findings should be considered leads for future investigation rather than evidence of broadly generalizable macroscopic-microscopic associations. With this caveat in mind, our results suggest that infection network morphology and tissue-specific growth patterns can differ among genotypes with similar levels of macroscopic resistance (or susceptibility), supporting a multi-mechanistic basis for QDR. For example, Fig. 7 illustrates that highly resistant genotypes may exhibit distinct infection trait profiles, differing in network shape, density, branching structure, and vascular association. This supports the idea that comparable resistance phenotypes can arise from different underlying infection dynamics and highlight the value of multi-scale approaches for dissecting the biological basis of QDR.

Materials and methods

Growth chamber experiments

Multiple growth chamber experiments of Ch inoculations on approximately 3-week-old maize plants were performed at both Cornell University and Technion - Israel Institute of Technology. For all experiments, the same pathogen strain C4 (MAT1-2, Tox+; Cornell University collection) was used for inoculations.

At Cornell, two experiments (E1, E2) were carried out with 26 inbred genotypes of maize (parents of a nested association mapping population plus Mo1738,39). In both experiments, three maize plants were inoculated by spraying with conidial suspensions prepared at a concentration of 6 × 104 conidia/mL in 0.02% Tween 20 (2 mL per plant). Plants were incubated in a mist chamber for 24 h after inoculation, then moved to a growth chamber for an additional three days at 24 °C under a daily photoperiod of 16 h light and 8 h dark. For each plant, the fourth leaf was photographed at four days post inoculation. These experiments provided initial observations that maize genotypes form different lesion profiles in response to Ch infection (Supplementary Fig. 1).

At Technion, additional inoculation experiments were performed. A third experiment (E3) on the complete set of 27 genotypes was used together with E1 and E2 to define lesion profile classes (Supplementary Fig. 1), from which we selected eight genotypes with different lesion profiles and levels of field resistance40. for subsequent experiments. Then, another experiment (E4) was used to scan the whole second leaf of individual plants for image analysis of the lesions. Last, a series of experiments (E5) were used to obtain tissues samples from separate lesions for microscopy (also from the second leaf). Stocks of the eight focal genotypes were provided by the USDA-ARS National Plant Germplasm System: B73 (PI 550473); CML247 (PI 595541); Hp301 (PI 587131); Il14H (PI 690323); Ki3 (PI 690324); M37W (PI 690327); Mo17 (PI 558532); and Tx303 (PI 690334). For all experiments at Technion, seeds were sown directly in plastic trays with a standard horticultural soil mix and grown for two weeks in a growth chamber at 25 °C under 100–150 µmol m− 2 s− 1 photosynthetically active radiation (cool white fluorescent tubes) and a daily photoperiod of 16 h light and 8 h dark. Conidia were suspended in 0.02% Tween 20 (Sigma) from 10 to 14 day old plates of complete medium containing xylose (CMX48 cultured in the same growth chamber as maize. For inoculation, three plants of each genotype were sprayed with about 2 mL/plant of 1 × 105 conidia/mL, the tray enclosed in a biohazard bag, and the plants grown for four days (after one day, the bag was opened at the top). For E5, about 10 × 10 mm tissue was sampled from the second leaf in regions carrying well-separated lesions, increasing the chance of observing single networks. The entire excised section was cleared, fixed, and imaged as described below.

Microscopy procedures

Sample preparation

Infected leaves were fixed using fixation buffer, which contained 2% paraformaldehyde, 2% glutaraldehyde, and 0.05% Triton x-100 in PBS, under 30 mmHg vacuum for 3–18 h. Clearing was done using 10% KOH in water for 32 h at room temperature followed by 0.2 M glycine in PBS for 18 h at 4 °C. Staining was done using Calcofluor White MR2 (CWMR) 1 mg/ml (Sigma F3543) in PBS and Wheat Germ Agglutinin conjugated with Alexa Fluor 488 (WGA-AF488) 5 µg/mL in DDW; Thermofisher Scientific W11261) 1:1000 in PBS for 2–5 days, 4 °C in the dark. Additional clearing was done using ScaleP solution containing 6 M urea, 30% glycerol, 0.1% Triton X-100 for 48 h, RT, dark37.

Image acquisition

Light-sheet microscopy was performed using a Zeiss Lightsheet 7 microscope. The light-sheet beam was generated by an LSFM 5X Foc illumination objective (NA = 0.1), captured through an EC Plan-APO 5X detection objective (NA = 0.16), and recorded with two PCO.edge 5.5 CMOS cameras. WGA-AF488 was excited using 20% power of a 50 mW 488 nm laser, while CWMR was excited using 15% power of a 20 mW 405 nm (UV) laser.

Lesion samples were embedded in 1.5% low-melting-point agarose gel (Bio-Rad, Cat #1613114) and positioned close to the gel’s surface. The embedded samples were secured to the Zeiss light-sheet holder using Bondic UV Light Liquid Plastic Welder (Bondic UK, Cambridge, UK), a UV-activated adhesive. The imaging chamber was filled with a 20% glycerol solution to match the refractive index (RI-1.36) of the cleared samples.

During imaging, samples were rotated to a 45° angle relative to the illumination light sheet. 3D image stacks were acquired with single-sided illumination, capturing data from the top to the bottom of the lesion. All acquisitions were performed at room temperature, with z-intervals of 3.5 μm. Blue (420–470 nm) and green (505–545 nm) emission filters were used during imaging, and the “pivot scan” mode was applied to reduce streaking artifacts caused by the illumination direction. However, some residual streaking artifacts may still be visible in the final images.

Filament tracing of infection networks

Whole leaf images captured by a flatbed scanner were analyzed using Fiji/ImageJ macros. First, the image scale was set, next, the single leaf regions were identified using a threshold, and finally, lesions were identified using a color threshold for each leaf. The number of lesions and their size were quantified using the ‘Analyze Particles’ command.

Light-sheet images of leaf tissues were analyzed using the Imaris analysis software 10 (Oxford Instruments). Fungal networks were segmented using the Filament Tracer module. To adapt Imaris for fungal networks, neuron-specific functions were disabled, including dendritic spine detection, autopath for dendrite tracing, and soma modeling. The starting point diameter was set to 10.2 μm. The start-point thresholding was automatically determined, and the low threshold cut-off was manually adjusted per image. Segment seed point diameter (hyphal width) was set between 4.0 and 6.0 μm, with an intensity threshold of 400. To prevent multiple start points from the same hyphal structure, seed points around the starting point were removed (diameter 10.2 μm). The hyphal filaments were moderately smoothed based on the estimated diameter of the filament (filter strength 2). Following filament segmentation, the generated network was manually corrected.

Separate networks were individually labeled, and metrics were obtained for segment branch depth level and filament segment length. Individual filament segment lengths were obtained for all filaments between branching points (including primary and terminal filaments of the network). At a branch point there are two connected segments to a parent segment. Average diameter and angle were used to determine which one is a branch. If a connected segment had a smaller diameter and a different angle than the parent segment, then it is bending away and was considered a new branch at the next branch level. If a connected segment had approximately the same diameter and angle as the parent segment, it is not a branching event and was considered to be at the same branch level as the parent segment.

Metrics for infection network depth, shape, and density

Imaris was used to fit a bounding box to the entire filament network using the three principal axes (X, Y, Z). The Z-axis that corresponds to depth was identified, and the top XY surface plane (Z = 0) was specified in Imaris. The maximum Z-axis value was used as the maximum network depth.

The Convex Hull Matlab plugin for Imaris was used to generate a convex hull surface object for each filament network. The fungal network density was calculated as the total volume of each traced fungal network divided by the volume of its convex hull. Statistical analysis

Seven infection network traits obtained from light-sheet images were used for statistical analysis: CH-prolate (convex hull prolate measure), CH-oblate (convex hull oblate measure), network density (calculated as the proportion of filament network volume to volume of the convex hull), infection depth (maximum depth of fungal infection in leaf tissue), peak branch level (hierarchical level of infection network with highest percentage of branching), mean filament length (average length of unbranched mycelial segments), and vascular overlap (ordinal score for degree of association with vascular bundles).

Tests for linear association between infection network traits and field resistance levels, lesion profile classifications (treated as numerical values), or normalized frequencies of 2 mm2 lesions were performed using simple linear regression. The measurements of resistance were not tied to individual lesions or infection networks. Therefore, the infection network values fit as predictor variables were aggregated to the genotype level using the median value across separate infection networks. This was done to avoid pseudo-replication arising from separate infection networks sharing the same genotypic resistance measure.

Statistical differences among genotypes were assessed using the Kruskal-Wallis test for each trait. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons among genotypes were performed using the Conover-Iman test with Bonferroni correction (α = 0.01). Results were visualized as box plots showing median values with interquartile ranges, with figures showing genotypes ordered from their highest to lowest ranking in field resistance to SCLB as reported by 40, based on their analysis of a diversity panel with 270 genotypes (ranks: Ki3 = 5, Mo17 = 14, CML247 = 35, M37W = 43, Il14H = 145, Tx303 = 201, B73 = 233, Hp301 = 253). All analyses were performed in R49 using the conover.test50 and multcompView51 packages.
