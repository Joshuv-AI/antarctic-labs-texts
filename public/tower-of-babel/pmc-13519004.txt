Research on deformation and seepage properties of Larssen pile reinforcing cofferdam based on numerical analysis, Tianjin, China
Authors: Shejie Lin, Xinjian Wang, Xingsheng Zhang, Shipeng Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The construction of cofferdams poses significant challenges in hydraulic engineering, where optimized design is essential for risk mitigation and construction safety. This study investigates the performance of a Larssen pile-reinforced cofferdam in a field ridge remediation project through integrated numerical modeling and limit equilibrium analysis. A coupled hydro-mechanical model was established, incorporating saturated-unsaturated seepage theory and an elastic-plastic constitutive model to simulate groundwater movement and slope stability. The results demonstrate that the Larssen sheet piles serve as an effective subsurface barrier, significantly impeding both soil and water flow. The implemented seepage cutoff measures induced a substantial hydraulic head difference across the cofferdam, leading to a marked reduction in both hydraulic gradient and water flux. Notably, simulated vertical displacements at pile tops during groundwater drawdown showed strong agreement with field measurements. The magnitude and variation trend of vertical displacements at the pile tops closely match field measurements during groundwater drawdown. Underwater side filling causes minimal pile-top settlement, while top filling results in greater settlement. Additionally, the zone of maximum ground surface settlement migrated from the cofferdam top toward the downstream slope-face. These results suggest that well-engineered Larssen sheet pile reinforcement can effectively control seepage, enhance the slope stability, and improve the structural integrity of earth-rock cofferdams.

Introduction

Cofferdams serve as critical temporary structures in large-scale hydraulic engineering projects, particularly for water diversion and containment during the construction of dams, bridges, and other waterfront infrastructure1–3. The primary function of the cofferdam is to provide anti-seepage capability, resisting both external water pressure and groundwater infiltration, thereby ensuring a relatively dry and accessible working area for construction and maintenance activities. Additionally, cofferdams must maintain sufficient structural stability and safety, especially against subsurface erosion and internal seepage failure.

Cofferdams, commonly constructed from rock, gravel, and earth, frequently exhibit limited impermeability and inadequate stability, which can complicate dewatering operations in foundation pits. As a result, such structures are prone to deformation and potential failure. To address these challenges, various reinforcement methods have been developed to enhance stability and impermeability. These improvements require comprehensive performance analysis to inform project design, making the design process more complex than that for unreinforced.

Over the past several decades, extensive research has been conducted on various cofferdam foundation types, construction methods, and reinforcement techniques4. Common geotechnical challenges associated with cofferdams include slope instability, excessive settlement or deformation, and seepage failures5. Many cofferdams are constructed on soft sediments, making the underlying foundation a critical factor in controlling both seepage and deformation. In particular, research has focused on the stress distribution and displacement behavior in cofferdam built over loose deposits, with an emphasis on the role of concrete cut-off walls within the foundation6.

The rate of water pumping from foundation pits also significantly affects cofferdam stability7–9. To understand this, numerical simulations have been performed to analyze the seepage field characteristics of cofferdams and foundations with cut-off walls10. By simulating different dewatering rates, these studies aim to identify optimal dewatering strategies and ensure structural safety11. Numerical methods have proven particularly effective for handling irregular geometries and complex engineering conditions, which is why they are now widely used in cofferdam design and analysis4,7–10,12,13. For instance, the finite element method has been applied to investigate the influence of dewatering velocity on the stability factor of cofferdam foundations equipped with high-pressure jet grouting cutoff walls12.

Reinforcement measures alter the deformation mechanisms of cofferdams, making it essential to evaluate their final stability to ensure both safety and cost-effectiveness14–16. For example, studies have examined the slope stability of earth-rock cofferdams constructed on deep overburden foundations, along with the influence of various reinforcement measures on their performance. In some projects, a composite an anti-seepage system, incorporating plastic concrete cutoff walls and geomembranes, has been implemented to enhance stability17,18.

Extreme historical floods pose significant threats to cofferdam stability. Numerical analyses have been employed to examine the impact of dynamic super-historical floods on surface deformation and slope stability. This is especially important for design where impermeable walls fully penetrate all loose deposits in the cofferdam body and its foundation. Ultimately, slope geometry and water table fluctuations have been identified as critical factors influencing cofferdam stability18.

The stability and safety of cofferdam reinforcement elements are essential for ensuring overall structural integrity19–21. Finite difference analyses have been conducted to evaluate the performance of double-row wood piles reinforced with cemented steel pipe piles. These analyses typically assess horizontal deformation and bending moments in the piles, as well as axial stresses in tension bars at the pile heads. To simplify the model, the wood and steel pipe piles are modeled as sheet piles using the equivalent stiffness principle22.

Centrifugal model tests have been performed to study the spatial distribution of stress and deformation in composite geomembranes with different installation and connection configurations within cofferdams. The effectiveness of these configurations is evaluated in terms of reinforcement performance and their influence on cofferdam settlement23. Furthermore, the mechanical behavior of membrane-bag sand cofferdams during construction has been investigated, along with theoretical derivations of their enhancement mechanisms. Studies have established relationships among membrane sandbag height, sandbag shape, mechanical parameters, and vertical settlement of the sand cofferdam24.

Steel piles, offering multiple advantages, are widely used in cofferdam reinforcement16. Numerical simulations have been conducted to analyze the stress field in cofferdams reinforced with interlocking steel pipe piles arranged in a rectangular layout, supported by four internal layers and a 3-meter-thick C30 underwater concrete base. Simulation results are often compared with design values to verify structural safety25. The double-row pile cofferdam, known for its high structural stiffness, reduced deformation and lower overall project costs, has attracted considerable research interest. Comparisons between numerical simulations and monitoring data for a double-row pile cofferdam constructed on deep silty soil demonstrate that numerical simulation can effectively evaluate and optimize design schemes, with the equivalent stiffness principle often applied to improve computational efficiency13.

Displacement and contact pressure distributions in double-row steel sheet pile cofferdams have been analyzed by comparing numerical simulations of steady-state seepage fields with empirical engineering calculations. The results indicate that the empirical method may overestimate the maximum bending moment on the inner sides of the piles, potentially leading to overly conservative designs4.

The construction phase of cofferdam reinforcement is often accompanied by changes in geotechnical conditions that can adversely affect stability. Numerical studies have investigated the influence of cut-off wall construction stages on seepage and slope stability during rapid drawdown of foundation-pit water tables, particularly for projects featuring anti-seepage systems composed of geomembranes and impermeable walls extending through all loose deposit layers26.

Despite considerable research, the deformation and seepage characteristics of cofferdams formed from existing farmland embankments and reinforced with Larssen sheet piles remain insufficiently studied. This knowledge gap poses a significant challenge in practical engineering applications. To address this issue, the present study investigates a river dredging project in a coastal province, where interlocking Larssen steel sheet piles were installed into existing farmland embankments and backfilled to form a cofferdam, preventing water ingress into the foundation pit during riverbank construction. Emphasis is placed on analyzing the settlement behavior of the Larssen sheet piles and slope stability of the cofferdam across various construction stages. The novelty of this research lies in the use of Larssen sheet piles to reinforce a cofferdam retrofitted from an earth embankment. To validate the reliability of the proposed methodology, displacement monitoring was conducted at the tops of the Larssen sheet piles during construction. Additionally, a comparison of the displacement and seepage fields and safety factors was performed with a scenario without any piles, demonstrating the effectiveness of this reinforcement scheme.

Case study

Project overview

The Jiyun River regulation project is located in the Ninghe District of Tianjin, at the border between Baoning and Ninghan. It involves the rehabilitation and reinforcement of the right embankment extending from Luoboting to Xiguan village, covering a total length of 12.478 km.

An earth-rock cofferdam, reinforced with Larssen sheet piles, was constructed to enclose the work area for regulating the right embankment between Luoboting and Liucun (engineering mileage from BLL4 + 952 to BLL5 + 664). This cofferdam has a total length of 612 m and is arranged in a U-shaped layout, forming a closed excavation zone together with the existing right embankment of the Jiyun River.

The cofferdam consists of a riverside section and the upstream and downstream sections (as shown in Fig. 1). The upstream and downstream sections are constructed as double-row sheet piles walls with soil backfilled between them, oriented at approximately 45° to the riverbank. The riverside section, running roughly parallel to the riverbank, is partially founded on an existing farmland embankment. Larssen steel sheet piles, 9 m in length, were driven into the original embankment. This embankment was then raised and shaped using locally available earth and rockfill to form the final cofferdam structure.

The finite element method (FEM) is one of the most widely used numerical techniques in geotechnical and civil engineering for stress and deformation analysis. Although FEM analysis can be used to evaluate safety via the strength reduction method, the results may lack precision because this approach requires qualitative interpretation of inflection points on load-displacement or stress-strain curves. Consequently, different evaluators may obtain varying values under the same conditions.

In contrast, the limit equilibrium method is a classic analytical approach commonly employed to assess the stability of slopes, retaining structures, embankments, cofferdams, and foundations. It is based on force and moment equilibrium of a potential failure mass and is commonly combined with numerical techniques for complex problems.

In this study, a finite element model of a typical cross-section of earth cofferdam reinforced with Larssen steel sheet piles was established using GeoStudio. Figure 2 illustrates the mesh discretization and geological conditions of the model. A plane-strain element employing a Mohr-Coulomb constitutive model is assigned to the soil, while an isotropic elastic model is used for the piles. The mesh consists of quadrilateral and triangular elements. The global element size is 0.5 m, with integration orders of 4 for quadrilaterals and 3 for triangles. The model comprises 2241 nodes, and 2147 elements. The hydraulic model accounts for both saturated and unsaturated conditions. The interface between the piles and the soil is treated as an impermeable boundary.

Numerical computation

The in-situ stress within the original farmland embankment had reached equilibrium prior to repair. All subsequent construction stages are simulated starting from this equilibrium state, as the initial stress condition significantly influences the deformation and safety of the later works, especially in zones 5 to 14 shown in Fig. 2. Therefore, initial geostatic equilibrium was established in the numerical model as described in Fig. 4(a).

To examine the influence of the Larssen sheet piles on cofferdam stability, a limit equilibrium analysis was performed on the downstream slope. Because multiple methods exist for calculating the safety factor, it is standard practice to adopt the minimum value obtained from different slice schemes as the overall safety factor. The numerical simulation follows the six sequential steps as shown in Fig. 4.

To evaluate the impact of steel sheet piles on cofferdam stability and safety, two construction scenarios—with and without piles—were simulated for comparative analysis. The study primarily focuses on pile settlement, cofferdam deformation, and the distribution characteristics of the seepage field. Following pile installation, monitoring instruments were mounted on the pile heads to record settlement throughout construction. After dewatering was completed, the downstream slope safety factor was used for comparative assessment.

Results

Settlement of Larssen piles

In the numerical simulation, the groundwater table is gradually lowered by pumping and controlled at a depth of 0.5 m below the bottom of the foundation pit (Fig. 4(e)). This groundwater table is then maintained for 169 days to meet construction requirements. The initial pumping flow from the pit is set to increase linearly up to 0.07 m³/d over a period of about 13 days to lower the groundwater table, matching the actual duration.

Field monitoring of pile-head settlement began concurrently with dewatering. In the numerical simulation, settlement was calculated starting from the initial geostatic equilibrium. To compare the two datasets, the numerical settlement was offset so that its initial value coincided with the monitoring point. As shown in Fig. 5, settlement initially decreases throughout the 13-day drawdown period, then followed a gentler, downward-curving trend during the sustained low-water-table phase. The statistical metrics of RMSE, R² and MAE between the observed and calculated data are 0.5627, 0.921 and 0.3867. R² is close to 1, and all error metrics are very low, which quantitatively demonstrates the rationality and high accuracy of the computational model.

Calculation and monitoring of Larssen pile head settlement during dewatering.

To ensure the stability of the downstream slope of the cofferdam, intermittent groundwater pumping was performed during actual dewatering, with the pumping depth increased incrementally in a stepwise manner in practice. When pumping ceases, the groundwater table stabilizes and may even begin to rise. Consequently, the initial portion of the monitoring curve exhibits periods of stagnation, characterized by quasi-stable platforms corresponding to intervals of groundwater table stabilization. In contrast, the later section shows a generally smooth decline with minor fluctuations due to variability in pumping rates.

Overall, the close match in trends and magnitude between the two curves confirms the validity of the numerical setup and its ability to reflect actual engineering conditions. Hence, further results from this model can be considered representative of the real project response.

Since the pile top settlement was not monitored during the early stages of construction, the results of the numerical calculations are utilized to illustrate the effects of repairs on settlement in various sections of the embankment. The insertion of the Larssen piles into the farmland ridge is simulated by modeling the steel material in the designated pile area. Initially, the pile top settlement is recorded at 2 mm, attributed to the impact of pile installation into the farmland ridge. As the side filling process is conducted underwater, the effective stress generated by the buoyant unit weight, 9.2 kN/m3, acts on the upper portions of both sides of the farmland ridge. The total load from this filling is 49.68 kN/m with the area of 5.4 m2 in the cross-section. The resulting settlement of the pile top is small, approximately 0.54 mm (as shown in Fig. 6), due to the relatively low buoyant density and greater distance from the pile location. The additional stress induced by the buoyant unit weight of the side-filling soil is maximal directly beneath the fill and decreases with increasing lateral distance. Owing to the relatively small magnitude of this load and its attenuation over distance, the stress transmitted to the soil adjacent to the pile is substantially reduced. Consequently, the resulting negative skin friction mobilized on the pile is significantly lower.

Settlement of the Larssen pile head during cofferdam filling.

In the second filling phase, soil is placed on top of the cofferdam and the Larssen piles. The unit weight, 19.01 kN/m3, of the filled soil is relatively high due to its position above the water level. The total load from this filling is 155.42 kN/m with the area of 8.175 m2 in the cross-section. As shown in Fig. 6, the resulting settlement of the pile top caused by this filling is more pronounced, ranging from 2.9 mm to 3.6 mm. The filling soil at the top of the cofferdam, located above the water surface, directly imposes a substantial self-weight stress on the surrounding soil of the pile. As a result, the negative skin friction generated along the pile shaft is significantly large, leading to considerable settlement at the pile top.

Settlement of the cofferdam

The vertical displacement field of the soil mass in the cofferdam reinforced with Larssen piles (Fig. 7a) differs significantly from that in an unreinforced condition (Fig. 7b).

Fig. 7Vertical displacement in the cofferdam soil at last calculation step (169 days after the first day of pumping from the foundation pit).

Vertical displacement in the cofferdam soil at last calculation step (169 days after the first day of pumping from the foundation pit).

A notable increase in vertical displacement occurs in the shallow upper portion of the downstream slope, whereas a significant decrease is observed in the shallow upper portion of the upstream slope. Therefore, the zone of maximum ground-surface settlement shifted from the cofferdam top toward the downstream slope face. In contrast, the lower portion (below the pile tip) generally experiences only a slight increase. This behavior can be attributed to two factors. First, due to seepage interception by the Larssen piles, when the groundwater table drops below the foundation-pit bottom, the groundwater table in the downstream soil is lower compared to that in unreinforced conditions. Consequently, the soil above the water table is in an unsaturated state and has a higher effective unit weight than in a saturated condition. This increase in effective consolidation pressure results in a greater overall consolidation settlement. Second, the upper part of the Larssen piles may be subjected to downward drag forces from the surrounding soil, resulting in slightly greater effective stress being transferred to the underlying soil via the pile tip.

The vertical displacement of the soil mass near the pile differs significantly from areas without piles (the locations of displacement data on nodes near the pile in Fig. 8 are shown as orange-red dashed lines numbered in Fig. 7). The piles exhibit a higher elastic modulus, which results in the compression amount on the adjacent soil mass being much less than that in cases without Larssen piles. Since continuous contact between the soil and the piles, the soil around the piles is influenced by the lower deformability of the piles during consolidation settlement or subjected to vertical loads. This leads to a reduction in vertical settlement of the soil around the upper portion of the piles, while the vertical displacement of the lower or bottom portions of the soil mass increases. Based on the variation in vertical displacement gradient of the soil near the pile, the displacement curves (denoted as I and II in Fig. 8) can be divided into an upper section and a lower section. Although the displacement generally increases from lower to higher elevations, the rate of increase in the upper section is significantly smaller than that in the lower. In contrast, gradients farther from the pile (IV) and in the no-pile case (III) are approximately constant, with the smallest displacement increase observed in the upper section in the upstream slope. These characteristics clearly result from the low deformability of the Larssen piles. The gradients for case IV, which is not directly influenced by the distant Larssen sheet pile deformation, and for case III without piles remain largely consistent along depth, although the gradient for IV is generally higher.

The Larssen piles, functioning as an underground barrier, resist soil movement through soil-structure interaction, a mechanism referred to as soil-blocking effect39–41. This phenomenon is reflected in the soil deformation patterns described above and is further illustrated by comparing the ground-surface settlements shown in Fig. 7 (a) and 7(b). At the vicinity of the pile top (from 20 m to 24 m), ground settlement is lower than that observed in the corresponding area without piles, with the maximum reduction occurring at the pile top location. Conversely, between 24 m and 40 m, settlements in the piled case exceed those in the unpiled case, with the greatest difference observed at 28 m.

The engineering significance of these findings lies in the ability to purposefully control settlement, whether in localized zones or across the ground surface of the cofferdam, through targeted pile reinforcement.

Seepage field

The anti-seepage performance of the Larssen steel piles used for suspended seepage interception in this project is significant, as demonstrated by the comparative results shown in Fig. 9.

In the absence of seepage control measures (Fig. 9b), the water pressure head contour lines in the downstream slope are positioned at higher elevations, especially adjacent to the pile. These lines display a more uniform vertical increase while maintaining horizontal continuity. This distribution pattern results in steeper hydraulic gradient especially in the middle section and reduces the minimum distance between the groundwater table and the downstream slope surface (near the downstream toe of the cofferdam), thereby adversely affecting the stability of the earth-rock cofferdam. Additionally, the elevated seepage flux (shown in Fig. 10) further complicates drainage management.

Fig. 9Seepage fields and downstream slope stability of cofferdam: (a) result with Larssen piles, (b) result without Larssen piles. (The vertical red dashed line at the location of 40 m is where the flow data in Fig. 10 was extracted).

Seepage fields and downstream slope stability of cofferdam: (a) result with Larssen piles, (b) result without Larssen piles. (The vertical red dashed line at the location of 40 m is where the flow data in Fig. 10 was extracted).

Conversely, with the strategic implementation of a cut-off wall composed of Larssen steel piles, the groundwater tables in the upstream and downstream slopes remain predominantly horizontal and exhibit very low hydraulic gradients in their respective zones. However, a distinct discontinuity in the water-pressure contours occurs across the Larssen pile wall, resulting in a water-table difference of up to 2.32 m between the two sides of the pile. The corresponding water pressure contours in the shallow downstream slope are observed at considerably lower elevations compared with the no-pile case. Notably, the minimum distance between the groundwater table and the downstream slope face is markedly increased, thereby substantially improving the stability of the downstream slope due to the smaller seepage force.

Upward groundwater seepage often undermines the stability of the downstream slope and cofferdam toes, potentially causing geotechnical failures such as soil flow or piping. It is therefore essential to evaluate the effectiveness of Larssen piles in reducing vertical seepage velocity within the downstream slope. A comparison of vertical seepage flux curves (Fig. 10) for scenarios with and without Larssen sheet piles reveals the associated flux characteristics and the seepage interception effect. In both cases, the vertical flux follows a consistent trend, increasing exponentially from 1 × 10⁻⁵ m/d at the base to 4.9 × 10⁻2 m/d near the top of the domain (along the orange dashed line in Fig. 9a). At elevations approximately 1 to 2.5 m above the base, the vertical seepage rates for the two scenarios are very similar, with a maximum absolute difference of 6.44 × 10⁻⁵ m/d. This similarity is likely attributable to the distance of this section from the reservoir bottom and the influence of the drainage well pumping boundary. Furthermore, the 3 m elevation marks a stratigraphic interface, below this depth, the soil layer exhibits lower hydraulic conductivity. Beyond this interface, the flux difference between the two scenarios gradually widens. Near the 6–7 m elevation, the difference becomes more pronounced. This elevation corresponds with the bottom of the Larssen sheet piles, where their seepage interception effect significantly alters the seepage field by reducing vertical flow flux. In the absence of Larssen sheet piles, seepage is neither intercepted nor diverted, allowing direct recharge from the upstream side. Consequently, seepage paths become shorter and the hydraulic gradient is steeper, resulting in higher seepage rates in the shallow zone. At an elevation of 8.5 m, the difference in vertical seepage flux reaches approximately 0.017 m/d.

Larssen piles also impact the water flux distribution within the cofferdam. At the upstream saturated zone of the cofferdam, the water flux across the vertical section at y = 20 m is 0.36 m3/d/m2, while at y = 30 m downstream, it decreases to 0.29 m3/d/m2. In contrast, without Larssen piles, the water fluxes at the corresponding positions are 0.42 m3/d/m2 and 0.43 m3/d/m2 respectively. These observations indicate that the Larssen piles not only alter the spatial distribution of water flux within the cofferdam body, but also significantly reduce the water flux across the vertical section downstream.

As mentioned in previous studies39–41, the Larssen piles in this cofferdam serve as a barrier to groundwater flow, an effect commonly referred to as the water-blocking effect. The piles impede the rapid drainage of upstream water into the downstream slope or pit, which reduces the hydraulic gradient of groundwater on both sides of the cutoff structure. This leads to a pronounced difference in water level elevations between upstream and downstream, ultimately resulting in a substantial decrease in flow rate and flux across the vertical sections on both sides. The modification of the seepage field enhances the cofferdam slope stability during foundation pit construction, as indicated by an increase in the factor of safety from 1.9 to 2.7. This improvement results from a reduced hydraulic gradient, which diminishes seepage forces in accordance with Darcy’s law. In the presence of the Larssen piles, the potential slip surface develops entirely within the downstream slope and exhibits a shallower maximum depth. In contrast, in scenarios without piles, the slip surface enters from the upstream slope face.

The engineering significance of these findings is the ability to control seepage and water flux deliberately. By strategically positioning anti-seepage piles, this approach minimizes the risk of geological hazards, such as subsurface seepage erosion.

Discussion

It should be noted that the present study simplifies Larssen piles using an equivalent stiffness method and neglects local soil-pile interaction. Future work should address these limitations by developing a three-dimensional model that captures the spatial geometry of Larssen piles and incorporates frictional contact at the pile-soil interface, thereby enabling a more realistic simulation. Centrifugation experiments are also recommended to validate the model’s reliability.

The stratigraphic profile comprises three soil layers under conditions of saturated seepage. From top to bottom, the horizontal permeability coefficients of these layers are as follows: Q43al, 0.021 m/d; Q42m, 0.159 m/d; and Q41al, 0.010 m/d. Based on statistical results from laboratory soil sample tests, the vertical permeability coefficients adopted in the numerical simulation are taken as half of the corresponding horizontal values. According to previous studies42,43, settlement lag tends to occur under conditions involving relatively high horizontal permeability, relatively low vertical permeability, and significant permeability contrasts between adjacent soil layers. However, the numerical simulation results suggest that settlement lag is not pronounced in present case.

While the top and bottom layers exhibit low permeability, the middle layer, characterized by the highest permeability and greatest thickest, acts as the primary pathway for seepage and, notably, for groundwater discharge. As a result, despite the low permeability of the upper and lower layers, they do not create an effective water-resisting barrier during drainage from the middle layer. It is this water-resisting effect that is critical to the stabilization of the seepage field and the development of settlement lag, as noted in the literature44.

Conclusion

This research presents a case investigation of a cofferdam reinforcement project in a river environment, in which Larssen-piles were installed to improve seepage control and slope stability. Compared with conventional cofferdam techniques, such as concrete cut-off walls or grouting curtains, the proposed Larssen-pile scheme offers distinct advantages in terms of construction flexibility, material reusability, and reduced site disturbance.

The numerical simulation, based on an elastic-plastic finite element model incorporating limit equilibrium analysis and saturated and unsaturated seepage-stress coupling, demonstrates the superior performance of the Larssen sheet piles reinforcement. In terms of anti-seepage effectiveness, the installation of Larssen sheet piles lowered the downstream groundwater table by approximately 8.5 m, and reduced the hydraulic gradient by 37%, substantially mitigating seepage-induced risks. Regarding slope stability, the factor of safety against downstream slope failure increased from 1.9 to 2.7, representing an improvement of 42% compared to the unreinforced condition. These quantified benefits highlight the engineering significance of the proposed technique.

The vertical displacements obtained from the numerical simulation show strong agreement with field measurements at the pile head during groundwater drawdown. Our simulation further indicates that lateral soil filling adjacent to the underwater cofferdam has a negligible effect on pile head settlement, whereas vertical filling atop the cofferdam induces considerably larger settlement. The vertical displacement field, shaped by the seepage interception and mechanical properties of the Larssen sheet piles, exhibits four district patterns: a significant increase in the upper downstream zone, a decrease in the upper upstream zone, a slight increase in the lower zone, and a relatively uniform zone adjacent to the pile sides.

Overall, the result demonstrates that the Larssen steel sheet piles reinforcement effectively enhances both anti-seepage performance and slope stability, offering a viable and efficient alternative to conventional cofferdam reinforcement methods. The findings provide a preliminary robust assessment of the waterproof behavior of Larssen steel sheet piles and their contribution to cofferdam stability, with implications for similar riverine engineering applicaitons.
