Optimization of crossing point temperature for predicting coal spontaneous combustion
Authors: Hossein Khakshour, Nafiseh Ayeen, Ramin Rafiee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Spontaneous combustion of coal poses a significant safety and operational risk in mining, highlighting the need for reliable and industry-oriented predictive indicators. The Crossing Point Temperature (CPT) is widely used to assess coal self-heating tendency, where lower CPT values indicate higher spontaneous combustion risk. CPT is primarily influenced by coal compositional parameters, including moisture, ash, volatile matter, and fixed carbon, which can be readily determined through proximate analysis. In this study, CPT values were experimentally measured for 32 coal samples, and a regression-based model was developed to quantify the relationship between coal composition and CPT. This model was subsequently employed as the objective function in a constrained optimization framework using two metaheuristic algorithms: Black Widow Optimization (BWO) and Giza Pyramids Construction (GPC). Optimization was performed under physically meaningful constraints, including compositional summation and parameter bounds relevant to industrial practice. The results indicate that the maximum CPT obtained by both algorithms was 204.224 °C, corresponding to low moisture (2.05%) and volatile matter (18.68%) contents and high ash content (67.36%), representing a low-risk spontaneous combustion scenario. Conversely, the minimum CPT was 146.83 °C, associated with higher moisture (8.99%) and volatile matter (30.37%) and lower ash content (54.41%), indicating increased combustion risk. Both algorithms produced consistent results, demonstrating stable convergence and computational efficiency .It should be noted that the analysis is limited to the investigated dataset and coal samples, and the findings should not be generalized to all coal types without further validation. Within these limitations, the proposed approach provides a practical tool for identifying critical CPT conditions based on standard coal properties, supporting risk assessment and decision-making in coal mining and handling operations.

Introduction

Coal remains a natural, affordable, and widely available energy resource and continues to play a major role in global electricity generation. Despite the increasing share of renewable energy sources, coal still accounts for a substantial portion of worldwide energy capacity. However, coal mining and utilization are accompanied by several technical, environmental, and safety challenges, among which spontaneous combustion is one of the most critical. Spontaneous combustion refers to the self-ignition of coal in the presence of oxygen without an external ignition source, resulting from exothermic oxidation reactions and heat accumulation. This phenomenon can lead to fires, explosions, production interruptions, environmental pollution, loss of valuable resources, and increased greenhouse gas emissions. Therefore, effective prediction and control of coal spontaneous combustion are essential to ensure operational safety, economic efficiency, and environmental protection1. Extensive research has been conducted to investigate coal spontaneous combustion using experimental, analytical, and numerical approaches. Laboratory-based studies have focused on identifying key coal properties and environmental factors that influence spontaneous combustion. For instance, Saleh et al.2 analyzed the effects of initial temperature, oxygen concentration, gas flow rate, and porosity on Crossing Point Temperature (CPT) behavior using numerical simulations, highlighting the dominant roles of porosity and oxygen availability. Wang et al.3 proposed a modified experimental framework based on the R70 oxidation method to quantify the heat recovery effect on low-rank coal combustion propensity. Onifade and Genc1 reviewed spontaneous combustion studies in the South African context, emphasizing the importance of intrinsic coal properties in evaluating self-heating tendencies.

Several experimental investigations have examined intrinsic coal characteristics and environmental conditions affecting spontaneous combustion, including moisture, ash, volatile matter, pyrite content, and ventilation4–6. Yong et al.4 introduced the constant temperature difference guidance method to determine spontaneous combustion characteristics, while Beamish and Theiler5 explored the self-heating incubation process and the interactive effects of moisture and reactive pyrite. Kursunoglu and Gogebakan6 employed polynomial regression models to predict spontaneous coal combustion tendencies under varying operational conditions.

Among the various indicators used to assess coal spontaneous combustion propensity, the Crossing Point Temperature (CPT) has been widely adopted due to its simplicity, reproducibility, and industrial relevance. CPT represents the temperature at which the coal temperature exceeds the surrounding furnace temperature during controlled oxidation, with lower CPT values indicating a higher tendency toward spontaneous combustion. Yernaidu and Tripathi7 experimentally demonstrated that coal samples with higher moisture and ash contents exhibit lower CPT values. Laxman and Patel8 confirmed the applicability of CPT testing for industrial coal assessment, particularly when combined with complementary thermal analysis techniques.

More recent studies have expanded the understanding of spontaneous combustion mechanisms through advanced analytical tools. Zhang et al.9 systematically investigated the effect of particle size on low-rank coal spontaneous combustion under various extrinsic conditions. Qiao et al.10 studied the catalytic action of inherent metals in coal, revealing their significant role in accelerating spontaneous combustion. Hemeng11 numerically simulated self-heating behavior of coal piles, considering aging effects and exothermic oxidation kinetics.

Although these studies have significantly improved the understanding of coal oxidation and CPT-related mechanisms, most existing research primarily focuses on experimental characterization or regression-based prediction of CPT values. Despite the growing use of data-driven and optimization-based methods in mining and energy engineering, the application of metaheuristic optimization algorithms to CPT analysis remains limited. In particular, advanced metaheuristic techniques such as Black Widow Optimization (BWO) and Giza Pyramids Construction (GPC) algorithms have received little attention in identifying critical CPT conditions. Current CPT-related studies rarely attempt to determine optimal combinations of coal compositional parameters that correspond to extreme CPT values (i.e., minimum and maximum risk conditions) under physically meaningful constraints.

To address this research gap, the present study proposes a regression-based optimization framework for CPT analysis. First, the statistical relationship between coal compositional parameters (ash, moisture, volatile matter, and fixed carbon) and CPT is established using experimental data obtained from 32 coal samples. Subsequently, the derived CPT regression equation is employed as an objective function and optimized using BWO and GPC algorithms to identify coal compositions associated with minimum and maximum CPT values. This approach aims to provide a practical and computationally efficient tool for CPT-based spontaneous combustion risk assessment, with particular relevance to industrial coal handling and mining applications.

The set of methods for assessing the risk of coal spontaneous combustion can be classified as shown in Fig. 4.Fig. 4Classification of techniques for evaluating coal spontaneous combustion18.

Several methods have been developed to predict the spontaneous combustion propensity of coal, including Cross-Point Temperature (CPT), Average Heating Rate (AHR), Feng’s Spontaneity Index, the FCC Index, DSC, DTA, R70, ignition temperature, Wet Oxidation Potential (WOP), TGA, Wits-Ehac and Wits-CT tests, and the slope of the time–temperature curve in XPT. Predicting coal spontaneous combustion helps prevent accidents such as fires and explosions and allows managers and engineers to optimize storage conditions and mitigate uncontrolled oxidation risks19. Failure to predict spontaneous combustion can lead to safety hazards, economic losses, environmental pollution, and reduced efficiency. In this study, the Cross-Point Temperature (CPT) method is employed to evaluate the spontaneous combustion propensity of coal samples.

Crossing point temperature (CPT)

Crossing point measurements determine the temperature at which significant self-heating begins in a coal sample, known as the “crossing point” or "relative ignition temperature." The method typically involves preparing the sample according to standard procedures, placing it in a controlled environment, gradually increasing the furnace temperature, and recording the point at which the sample temperature exceeds that of the furnace, indicating the onset of ignition20.

According to Kaymaki and Didari (2002)20, the CPT method involves heating a coal sample, prepared to a standard size, in an oven at a constant rate of 1 °C/min with an airflow of 80 ml/min. Both sample and oven temperatures are recorded every 5 min to generate a temperature–time curve. The crossing point temperature is identified as the point where the sample temperature intersects with the oven temperature, as shown in Fig. 521.Fig. 5Schematic diagram of the crossing point temperature22.

Parameters affecting spontaneous combustion and analysis results

Four parameters, namely moisture, ash, volatile matter, and fixed carbon, were measured for 32 coal samples in the laboratory. The samples included both coking and non-coking coals collected from selected seam sections with minimal contamination using standardized channel sampling procedures in accordance with IS:436 (Part I/Section I–1964). After removal of the weathered surface, representative channel samples were extracted, reduced by coning and quartering, sealed in airtight containers to prevent oxidation, and crushed and sieved to the required particle size prior to testing. Proximate analysis was performed under controlled laboratory conditions following IS:1350 (Part I–1984). These parameters were used as input (independent) variables in the model, and their statistical indices are reported in Table 1. The selection of these parameters was based on both physical relevance and practical applicability, as they are routinely determined through standard proximate analysis and have a direct influence on coal spontaneous combustion behavior. From a physical perspective, moisture affects heat retention and ignition temperature, volatile matter controls flammability and combustion rate, ash contributes to heat dissipation and influences the overall combustion process, and fixed carbon is directly related to the fuel value and combustion potential of coal. Objective function optimizationtests

The CPT objective function was used to determine its optimal values, which represents a nonlinear, composition-constrained optimization problem. Such problems can be approached through numerical methods, mathematical approaches, metaheuristic algorithms, or artificial intelligence techniques. Traditional mathematical methods require derivatives of the objective function and the solution of systems of ordinary differential equations. However, these methods are limited to linear systems, prone to trapping in local optima, and often computationally expensive. Numerical methods, including elimination and interpolation techniques, simplify nonlinear models into linear forms, which may reduce accuracy despite potential effectiveness in certain cases. To overcome these limitations, metaheuristic or intelligent optimization algorithms inspired by natural phenomena are particularly advantageous, as they do not require derivatives, are computationally efficient, and are relatively easy to implement25. In this study, a novel hybrid optimization framework was developed by combining the Black Widow Optimization (BWO) algorithm with the Giza Pyramid Construction (GPC) algorithm to optimize the Crossing Point Temperature (CPT) of coal. While hybrid approaches integrating regression and metaheuristic algorithms have been used in various domains, the proposed BWO–GPC framework introduces problem-oriented modifications specifically tailored for coal self-heating analysis. In particular, the reproductive and elimination mechanisms of BWO were redefined to work synergistically with the geometric search principles of GPC, enabling a more efficient exploration of the solution space. Both algorithms are well-suited for this problem because they can handle nonlinear, composition-constrained search spaces efficiently without requiring derivative information. BWO leverages a biological-inspired selection and cannibalism mechanism to converge rapidly toward global optima, while GPC simulates systematic adjustments of workers in ancient pyramid construction to explore the solution space effectively. Together, these algorithms provide complementary strategies for identifying both maximum and minimum CPT conditions.

The optimization process focuses on fine-tuning the key coal composition parameters moisture, ash, volatile matter, and fixed carbon to minimize the likelihood of spontaneous combustion. This CPT-focused adaptation allows the model to accurately capture the nonlinear and coupled interactions among coal constituents, leading to enhanced accuracy, stability, and interpretability compared with conventional hybrid optimization techniques. This study therefore advances the existing literature by applying these advanced metaheuristics specifically to CPT optimization, addressing the gap in previous studies where such techniques were rarely utilized.

Black widow optimization (BWO) algorithm

The Black Widow Optimization (BWO) algorithm, introduced by Hayyolalam and Pourhaj Kazem (2020), is a population-based metaheuristic inspired by the reproductive behavior and cannibalistic characteristics of black widow spiders26. In nature, female black widows attract males using pheromones, and sexual and sibling cannibalism ensure that only the strongest individuals survive27–30. These mechanisms, illustrated in Figs. 8 and 9, form the conceptual basis for strong selection pressure in the BWO algorithm.Fig. 8Female black widow species on her web26.Fig. 9Female black widow with egg sac on her web26.

Sexual cannibalism is commonly observed among invertebrates such as spiders and scorpions and is influenced by female condition and mating traits31–33. A female may produce multiple egg sacs containing hundreds of eggs34,35, and spiderlings typically emerge within several days, remain briefly on the web, and may engage in sibling cannibalism before dispersal (Fig. 10)36–38. Discussion

Objective function optimization with BWO algorithm

The higher the CPT value, the lower the likelihood of spontaneous combustion. Therefore, the CPT objective function was optimized using two metaheuristic algorithms, Black Widow Optimization (BWO) and GPC, to determine the coal property combinations associated with reduced or increased spontaneous combustion tendency. The optimization problem involved four decision variables moisture, ash, volatile matter, and fixed carbon subject to a linear constraint in which their sum equals 100. This constraint reduces the feasible search space and produces a well-structured low-dimensional optimization landscape. The optimization was implemented in Spyder 5 using Python-based implementations of both algorithms. The stopping criteria included a maximum of 1000 iterations and a precision threshold of 0.0001. Algorithm control parameters such as population size and variation operators were selected based on preliminary sensitivity testing to ensure stable convergence behavior. The optimization was performed on the trained CPT predictive model rather than directly on raw experimental samples. Therefore, the algorithms explore the continuous response surface of the model, which reduces the likelihood of classical data overfitting during the optimization stage. In the CPT maximization scenario, the BWO algorithm reached a stable plateau after 10 iterations with a CPT value of 204.224. Although this convergence speed is faster than typically expected for metaheuristic optimizers, repeated independent runs with different random initial populations and random seeds produced identical or near-identical optimal solutions, confirming stable and repeatable convergence. The rapid convergence can be explained by the combination of low search-space dimensionality, the linear composition constraint, and the exploitation-oriented operators of BWO, which intensify selection pressure and quickly eliminate weak candidate solutions. Because the CPT model response surface is smooth and continuous, these operators enable fast movement toward dominant fitness regions. The corresponding optimal composition was obtained with 2.06% moisture, 67.36% ash, 18.68% volatile matter, and 11.90% fixed carbon. According to the CPT model, this parameter combination corresponds to a lower predicted spontaneous combustion tendency. The convergence behavior is shown in Fig. 13. These results reflect optimizer performance for the present model and dataset and do not imply absolute superiority over other metaheuristic approaches.Fig. 13Convergence plot of the BWO algorithm in maximizing CPT.

In the CPT minimization scenario, the BWO algorithm converged after 410 iterations to a CPT value of 146.83. The longer convergence time compared with the maximization case suggests that the CPT response surface is flatter or more locally complex in the low-CPT region, resulting in weaker fitness gradients and slower optimizer guidance. The optimal composition in this case was obtained with 8.99% moisture, 54.41% ash, 30.37% volatile matter, and 6.21% fixed carbon, corresponding to higher predicted spontaneous combustion tendency and therefore requiring stricter preventive control measures. Objective function optimization with GPC algorithm

To further verify the robustness of the optimization results and reduce algorithm-dependent bias, the CPT objective function was also optimized using the GPC algorithm under the same variable bounds and constraint structure. The optimization was implemented in Spyder 5 using a Python-based GPC code and identical stopping criteria to enable a fair comparison with BWO. As in the BWO case, the optimization was performed on the trained CPT predictive model rather than directly on raw experimental data. In the CPT maximization scenario, the GPC algorithm converged after 405 iterations to a CPT value of 204.89. Repeated runs with different random initial populations produced nearly identical final values, indicating stable convergence behavior. The optimal composition was obtained with 2.03% moisture, 66.99% ash, 19.13% volatile matter, and 11.85% fixed carbon. The convergence curve is presented in Fig. 15. The close agreement between the GPC and BWO optima indicates that the solution region is stable and that the optimization outcome is not strongly dependent on a specific metaheuristic structure.Fig. 15Convergence plot of the GPC algorithm in maximizing CPT.

Conclusion

Metaheuristic optimization algorithms, due to their simplicity and flexibility, are capable of solving various engineering and scientific problems. The application of these metaheuristic algorithms does not require structural modification of the optimization problems. In this study, the authors used the Black Widow Optimization (BWO) and GPC metaheuristic algorithms to compute the optimal values of parameters such as moisture, ash, volatile matter, and fixed carbon to predict the likelihood of coal self-heating. The goal was to maximize and minimize the crossing point temperature (CPT), which serves as a key indicator for evaluating the probability of coal self-heating. The results indicate that metaheuristic algorithms like BWO and GPC are powerful and efficient tools for solving complex optimization problems in engineering domains, especially for predicting and managing the risk of coal self-heating. Tables 3 and 4 present the outputs of the two algorithms. The maximum CPT value corresponds to the lowest probability of coal self-heating, which is crucial for safe management and reducing storage risks. Conversely, the minimum CPT value represents the highest probability of self-heating, and identifying this scenario enables the implementation of necessary preventive and control measures. The optimized CPT compositions reveal physically meaningful trends consistent with established combustion theory. Higher CPT values, associated with reduced moisture and volatile matter contents, limit low-temperature oxidation and volatile release, thereby lowering spontaneous combustion risk. Elevated ash content further contributes to heat dissipation and dilution of reactive organic matter. In contrast, the minimum CPT conditions exhibit higher moisture and volatile matter levels, promoting heat retention and rapid oxidation, which increases susceptibility to spontaneous combustion. These trends confirm that the optimization results are not merely numerical artifacts but reflect realistic coal combustion behavior. The results from both algorithms are very similar. The BWO algorithm indicates that coal with 2.058% moisture, 67.36% ash, 18.68% volatile matter, and 11.9% fixed carbon has the lowest probability of self-heating. Similarly, the GPC algorithm shows that coal with 2.03% moisture, 66.99% ash, 19.13% volatile matter, and 11.85% fixed carbon has the lowest probability of self-heating. For the highest probability of self-heating, the BWO algorithm suggests coal with 8.99% moisture, 54.406% ash, 30.37% volatile matter, and 6.21% fixed carbon, while the GPC algorithm indicates coal with 8.99% moisture, 53.78% ash, 31.03% volatile matter, and 6.2% fixed carbon. These results align with the physical trends observed in the optimized CPT compositions, reinforcing the reliability and practical relevance of the optimization.
