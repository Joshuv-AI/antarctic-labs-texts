Immunoprophylactic potential against pneumonia caused by indigenous isolates of Klebsiella pneumoniae and Streptococcus pneumoniae in experimental mice
Authors: Iram Waqar, Imran Altaf, Rasheeda Bashir, Kunza Latif, Mehraj A Abbasov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPneumonia is a life threatening public health concern particularly due to multidrug resistant (MDR) bacterial pathogens. The rising threat of these resistant bacterial strains has exacerbated the challenge of treating bacterial pneumonia, emphasizing the urgent need of alternative therapeutic strategies such as vaccine development by targeting combined localized MDR bacterial pneumonia pathogens. In this study, clinical samples of bacterial pneumonia pathogens were collected from tertiary care hospitals of Lahore, Punjab, Pakistan. Strains were isolated and characterized on biochemical and molecular basis. The localized vaccines, including inactivated whole-cell vaccines, bivalent vaccine (KP + SP), combined vaccine (SP + LPS) and lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-based vaccines by targeting the most dominant bacterial pathogens K. pneumoniae and S. pneumoniae were prepared. The experimental trial was conducted on 7th, 14th, 48th, 60th and 90th days interval. Mice were immunized and immune responses were assessed using complement fixation test (CFT). Mice were challenged with a lethal dose (LD50) of pathogenic strain of K. pneumoniae and S. pneumoniae. Then, survival rate of each group of mice were monitored over time. indicated that the mean antibody titers peaked on 48th day for all vaccine groups, followed by a decline by 90th day of post vaccination. Among all vaccine groups, the bivalent vaccine (KP + SP) exhibited the most significant results (p < 0.05), with 98–99% survival rate. It may be concluded from this study that bivalent vaccine (KP + SP) significantly enhanced the protective humoral immune response against MDR bacterial pneumonia pathogens. Introduction

Vaccination has emerged and become most impactful public health strategies to overcome the prevalence and global burden of multi-drug resistance (MDR) against infectious diseases1. Pneumonia which leads to increased morbidity and mortality in older individuals and children worldwide particularly in low income countries South Asia, Central Africa and China despite medical treatments2. It is caused by variety of bacterial pathogens i.e. S. pneumoniae, K. pneumoniae, Acinetobacter baumannii and Pseudomonas aeruginosa3,4. Almost, 20 million cases of pneumonia are reported annually, with an estimated rate 10 million cases of childhood pneumonia in Pakistani population5. Effective treatment strategies have been severely caused by the rapid emergence of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) with multidrug-resistant forms of these bacteria making pneumonia more challenging. Therefore, to address these growing issue of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) in bacterial pneumonia pathogens and reduce the incidence of pneumonia in developing countries, it is essential to develop innovative vaccine strategies.

Among many bacterial pathogens that causing pneumonia, K. pneumoniae and S. pneumoniae, are the most dominant ones, due to the growing antimicrobial resistance (AMR) that complicate the treatment strategies thus, making the development of effective vaccines is an urgent priority6. Historically, β-lactam antibiotics, penicillin and cephalosporins were the mainstay to treat S. pneumoniae and K. pneumoniae infections, but these pathogens acquired resistance mainly by β-lactamase resistance genes blaTEM, blaCTX−M15, blaOXA−48, and blaSHV, now the trend of emergence of resistance shifts towards macrolide drugs that is mediated by the genes erm (B), erm (A), mef (A) that further complicates the treatment process7,8.

Heat inactivated whole-cell vaccines utilizes inactivated bacteria to stimulate the enhance humoral immune response against MDR bacterial pathogens of pneumonia. The vaccine was earlier administered to gold miners in South Africa9. Modern whole-cell vaccine formulations have focused on improving safety and efficacy through advanced inactivation methods, such as beta-propiolactone, aluminum hydroxide and chitosan adjuvants that can induces humoral immune response by increased IgG2 production, IL-17 A, IL-4 and IgA in various animal models10,11. Animal models including BALB/c mice, Swiss albino mice and rabbits are commonly used in preclinical studies to evaluate the immunogenic potential of pneumococcal vaccines injected by different administration routes i.e. subcutaneous, intraperitoneal, intradermal and intranasal that can effectively prevent pneumococcal colonization, provides both humoral and cell-mediated immune responses against various resistant pathogens of pneumonia K. pneumoniae, S. pneumoniae and P. aeruginosa12,13.

Different studies conducted on bivalent and conjugated vaccines that showed that capsular polysaccharides are an important virulence factor and protective antigen. The Vi capsular polysaccharide from Salmonella typhi, when conjugated with a non-toxic recombinant form of P. aeruginosa exotoxin induce the anti-IgG antibodies in infants and young children14. Additionally, preclinical studies have demonstrated that conjugating the Vi antigen with various carrier proteins such as CRM197, tetanus toxoid, diphtheria toxoid, cholera toxin subunits, the B subunit of Escherichia coli heat-labile toxin, recombinant outer membrane proteins of K. pneumoniae, and outer membrane proteins of S. typhi can significantly enhance the immune response15,16.

Developing a vaccine that targets lipopolysaccharide (LPS), a component of the outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria, holds significant promise to induce immune responses. LPS consists of an external O-antigen polysaccharide and a conserved lipid A region linked to a core oligosaccharide. It activates the immune system via the TLR-4 signaling pathway, promoting the maturation and activation of dendritic cells that initiating both humoral and cellular immune responses. Such a vaccine could potentially provide cross-protection against different strains of bacterial pneumonia, thereby reducing the risk of co-infections17. Different methods were used for extraction, purification and characterization of lipopolysaccharides (LPS) in which hot phenol extraction method was mainly used to extract LPS from Gram-negative bacteria18. Different analytical techniques like FTIR, HPLC, and GC-MS are being used to detect, quantify and analyze the structural and functional properties of LPS that gives better understanding about the purity and structural information’s for safe and effective vaccine development against AMR pathogens of pneumonia19.

Humoral immunity is the 1 st line defense to combat bacterial infections. It involved the production of specific antibodies that recognize and neutralize pathogens. After vaccination, these antibodies trigger complement activation, leading to bacterial cell lysis and clearance20. Different approaches were used to evaluate the vaccine efficacy depending upon intervals of clinical trials and antibody titer measurements. In different studies inactivated whole cell vaccines were used to immunized mice against MDR pathogens of pneumonia at different days intervals 0, 14th and 48th and booster dose was given at 14th day after last immunization. It provides 80% survival rate with high serum IgG level that was measured by Enzyme Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA) (p < 0.001), Serum Bactericidal Assay (SBA) and Complement fixation test (CFT)12,21. But CFT is considered better than ELISA and SBA in certain situations because it directly measures the functional activation of the complement system, which plays a crucial role in immune defense. Unlike ELISA, which only detects antibodies, CFT measures the ability of antibodies to trigger complement system and contribute in pathogen clearance. This makes CFT more sensitive in detecting low levels of functional antibodies, especially in diagnosing infections and evaluating vaccine responses22.

In Asian countries like in Pakistan and India, the growing threat of antimicrobial resistance (AMR), in pneumonia-causing bacteria is quiet alarming, thus indicates the need of novel solutions for localized vaccine development. Therefore, the study was aimed to develop the indigenous vaccines by targeting bacterial pneumonia stains to combat pneumonia in Pakistani population. This showed the systematic initiative approach to combine vaccine and its immunoprophylaxis on mice (BALB/c) was checked. In future, the data can be used to explore T-cell immunity and cytokine responses to predict infection outcomes that elicit robust immune protection against MDR pneumonia pathogens in developing countries.

Methods

Instituational review board (IRB) Approval

All experimental protocol was carried out according to ethical guidelines of Ethical Review Board (ERB) committee of the Department of Biotechnology, LCWU, Lahore, Pakistan (approval date: 10/01/2022; approval Registration# ORIC/LCWU/22/08). This study was conducted in compliance with the ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org).

Experimental Animals

A total of 70 BALB/c mice, each weighing between 18 and 20 g, along with two guinea pigs and four rabbits, were purchased from the Veterinary Research Institute (VRI) in Lahore, Pakistan. All mice were housed in the animal facility at the University of Veterinary and Animal Sciences (UVAS) in Lahore, Pakistan, where they received the recommended feed and water under standardized conditions.

Isolation and Characterization of Bacterial strains

Total 261 pneumonia causing bacterial strains were isolated from clinical samples e.g. sputum, tracheal aspirates, pleural fluids and nasal swabs of pneumonia suspected patients by reviewing their medical history and clinical investigations. All bacterial strains were grown onto Blood and MacConkey agar which then incubated at 37 °C for 24–48 hours. DNA was extracted using the phenol-chloroform method23. And quantity of DNA was assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (ThermoScientific, USA). The bacterial isolates were characterized by16S rRNA primer sequence Uni F: 5’AAACTTAAAGGAATTGACG3’ and Uni R: 5’ ACGGGCGGTGTGTGC3’. While the most dominant bacterial pathogens K. pneumoniae and S. pneuomniae were characterized by specie-specific primers KP-27F3: 5’GGATATCTGACCAGTCGG3 and KP27B3 5’ GGGTTTTGCGTAATGATCTG3’, cpsA-382 F: 5’ACGCAACTGACGAGTGTGAC3’ cpsA-735R: 5’GATCGCGACACCGAACTAAT3’ respectively24,25. The antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) was assessed according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) 2021, guidelines by using Kirby-Bauer disk diffusion method.

K. pneumoniae and S. pneuomniae isolates were further screened for antimicrobial resistance genes, including β-lactamase–encoding genes of K. pneumoniae isolates (bla TEM, bla OXA, and bla CTX-M) and macrolide resistance determinants for S. pneuomniae isolates (erm(B) and mef (A)). The bla TEM gene was amplified using primers sequence 5′-ATGGGGGATCATGTAACTCG-3′ and 5′-TTGCCGGGAAGCTAGAGTAA-3′, blaOXA was detected using primers 5′-TTTGGCTCGATGGTGGTATT-3′ and 5′-CCCGTTTTAGCCCGAATAAT-3′ while blaCTX-M was performed using primers 5′-TCTTCCAGAATAAGGATCCC-3′ and 5′-CCGGTTTCCGCTATTACAAAC-3′. While for S. pneumoniae, erm(B) and mef(A) genes were amplified using primers 5′-ACGAGTGAAAAAGTACTCAACCA-3′/5′-ACTTGCTCATAAGTAACGGTACT-3′ and 5′-AGTGGATCGTCATGATAGGAAGA-3′/5′-ACACGTCCTAAATATTCAGGCT-3′ respectively.

Preparation of indigenous vaccines

Different types of vaccines of most prevalent bacterial pathogens of pneumonia (K. pneumoniae and S. pneumoniae) were prepared e.g. whole cell inactivated vaccine of S. pneumoniae and K. pneumoniae, the bivalent vaccine (KP + SP), combined vaccine S. pneumoniae and lipopolysaccharide (SP + LPS) and Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) based vaccine.

Formulation of alum adjuvant

The preparation of the alum adjuvant for vaccines was prepared by dissolving the 250 gram of Na2 CO3 into 500 ml of the distilled water through proper mixing. A separate solution was prepared by dissolving 200 g of potassium aluminum sulfate (potassium alum) in 500 ml of distilled water. The solutions were mixed and stirred to get a gel like consistency. Over the course of three days, the distilled water was slightly poured over the gel to eliminate carbonate ions, and any excess water was removed carefully, resulting 30% gel utilized for vaccine formulation.

Preparation of inactivated whole cell vaccine

For the preparation of a formalin-inactivated whole-cell vaccine, S. pneumoniae was cultured on blood agar, and K. pneumoniae on MacConkey agar. Pure bacterial isolates were grown in nutrient broth and incubated at 37 °C for 24–48 h, followed by tenfold serial dilutions (10⁻¹ to 10⁻¹⁰). As 1 ml aliquot from each dilution was plated on respective media plates were incubated at 37 °C, 24–48 h. Bacterial suspensions were used for colony-forming unit (CFU) determination via the pour plate method, yielding CFUs 4.2 × 10¹¹/ml of K. pneumoniae and 5.6 × 10¹¹/ml of S. pneumoniae. That was calculated by using following formula:

CFU/ml = no. of the colonies x dilution factor/volume of Culture transferred/milliliter in the media plate.

To inactivate the bacterial culture, 0.5% formalin was added, and the mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. To confirm inactivation, 1 ml of the culture was spread onto nutrient agar plates and incubated again at 37 °C for 24 h. The culture was then transferred to nutrient broth and incubated for an additional 24 h. After incubation, bacterial growth on the nutrient agar was examined and recorded.

Preparation of Lipopolysaccharide based vaccine (LPS)

For the preparation of LPS- based vaccine, firstly, the lipopolysaccharide was extracted from the indigenous strain of K. pneumoniae by hot phenol-water extraction method. The purity and quantity of LPS was assessed by Limulus Amebocyte Lysate (LAL) assay (Chemico group limited, London) and high performance liquid chromatography (Waters, Co, Ireland) was performed18. SDS-PAGE (BioRad, USA) was done to visualize the extracted LPS with silver staining according to the standard protocol26. While active compounds and functional groups were detected by FTIR (Shimadzu, Co, Japan) and identification of vital components was determined by GC/MS (Shimadzu, Co, Japan)27.

Immunization trial in BALB/c Mice

All mice were distributed into eight groups in which two were the control groups (one was preliminary experimental control group and another was the control group after lethal challenge) and six were experimental groups (A + B+C + D+E + F). 10 mice were selected per group based on commonly accepted practices12,28. Group A and Group B was immunized with the formulation prepared whole cell inactivated vaccine of K. pneumoniae and S. pneumoniae with concentration used 4.2 and 5.6 × 1011CFU/ml respectively. 0.1 ml per dose was administered subcutaneously with formulation prepared 63 µl of Phosphate buffer saline, 10 µl formalin, 7 µl Alum gel and 20 µl of respective bacterial culture. While bivalent vaccine of Group C (KP + SP) was prepared by combining both whole cell vaccines of K. pneumoniae and S. pneumoniae with same bacterial load. And, the Combine vaccine of D group was formulated by combining whole cell inactivated vaccine S. pneumoniae with 5.6 × 1011 CFU/ml conc. and LPS with concentration 31.5EU/dose. While LPS vaccine was prepared by took 1 ml of the pure, extracted lipopolysaccharide with concentration 31.5 endotoxins units/dose and subcutaneously administered to 0.1 ml per dose to each group E mice. The booster dose of respective vaccine to each group of mice was administered on 14th day after the 1 st dose. While in control groups, PBS was injected containing the adjuvant without any antigen and amount of adjuvant was same as an experimental group. The all mice groups were housed in same standard conditions and provided same feed and water.

Preparation of dosage of commercial vaccine of pneumonia (Prevenar 13)

A 0.5 ml dose of the Prevenar13 vaccine (Pneumococcal polysaccharide conjugate vaccine 13 valent) was combined with 0.5 ml of PBS to get a total 1 ml volume, from which 0.1 ml per dose was administered to immunize each group of F of the BALB/c mice.

Collection of blood samples

Before collection of the blood samples, the mice were anesthetized with ketamine (80–100 mg/kg) combined with xylazine (5–10 mg/kg), dose was given intramuscularly. Blood samples from both the control and experimental groups were taken on the 7th, 14th, 48th, 60th, and 90th days. The serum from blood samples were separated and stored at −40 °C for subsequent analysis. Antibody titer from each group of vaccine was assessed by using the complement fixation test (CFT)29.

Challenge study

For challenge study, the total count of bacterial isolates was assessed by the method Pour plate technique. 0.5% formalin was used for inactivation of the bacterial culture and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The vaccinated and control group of mice were challenged with LD50 dose of bacterial strain of K. pneumoniae and S. pneumoniae. The number of diseased mice was observed after seven days.

Complement fixation test (CFT)

A total of 50 µl of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) was poured to each well (1 to 12) of a 96-well microtiter plate. In the first well, 50 µl of mice serum was added and mixed thoroughly with the PBS. Serial two-fold dilutions of the serum were performed through well 9. Next, 50 µl of bacterial antigen was added to wells 1 through 10, and the plate was incubated at 25 °C for 20 min to allow antigen–antibody binding. After this step, 50 µl of 4 hemolytic units (HU) of complement was added to wells 1 to 11. Sensitized sheep red blood cells (RBCs), 50 µl per well, were then added to all wells (1 to 12), followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C. The highest dilution of serum that completely prevented hemolysis was recorded. This complement fixation test (CFT) procedure was repeated for all serum samples collected from the different groups of mice.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism version 4.0 (California, USA). For each vaccination group, the mean, standard deviation (SD), and standard error of the mean (SEM) were calculated. Statistical differences between groups on different days were evaluated using One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by multiple comparison tests. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Characterization of bacterial strains

Out of 261 clinical samples of pneumonia, 128 were positive isolates in which most frequently occurring isolates were K. pneumoniae and S. pneumoniae 43 (33.6%) and 28 (21.8) respectively while distribution of other isolates was less i.e. P. aeruginosa 25(19.5%), Staphylococcus aureus 14 (10.9%), E.coli 13 (10.6%) and A. baumannii 5 (3.90%). All bacterial strains were streaked on selective media plates i.e. Blood and MacConkey agar and characterized on morphological and biochemical basis. The Streptococcus was optochin sensitive and showed hemolysis on blood agar while other gram negative strains were characterized by API 20E Kit. Positive pneumonia samples were further confirmed by 16 S rRNA primers yielded PCR product size 500 bps. Whereas characterization of most prevalent bacterial pathogens with specie-specific primers K. pneumoniae and S. pneumoniae showed the PCR product size 176 bps and 353 bps. Moreover, sequence analysis through nBLAST program of most prevalent bacterial pneumonia isolate of K. pneumoniae showed 100% sequence similarity to target gene sequences, confirming identification at the subspecies level revealed highest homology with reference sequences in GenBank whereas S. pneumoniae isolates exhibited up to 92% similarity with reference sequences. Whereas antimicrobial resistance genes analysis demonstrated a high prevalence of β-lactamase genes bla CTX-M and bla TEM in K. pneumoniae, while frequency of bla OXA was less. In contrast, macrolide resistance was more dominant in S. pneumoniae isolates.

Inactivation of the bacterial culture

After inactivation with 0.5% formalin, the bacterial culture was grown overnight on nutrient agar plates. No colonies were observed on the plate; the inactivated culture was utilized as a control.

This gel clot formation occurred at a 1:10 dilution of the sample, with 104 dilutions corresponding to endotoxin units (EU) equivalent to a lysate sensitivity of 0.125 λ. Survival of Mice (BALB/c) after lethal dose challenge

In the preliminary experiment, the control group (PBS along with adjuvant) showed 100% survival (10/10 mice alive), confirming normal health condition and there are no environmental factors related to mortality. While on the 90th post-priming day, mice in different vaccine groups were monitored to check the survival rates following a lethal challenge with LD50 1011 CFU per ml of pathogenic bacterial strains of K. pneumoniae and S. pneumoniae. Survival rate of mice in different group were as follows: Group A showed 80% survival of mice in whole-cell vaccine group of K. pneumoniae, Group B, 70% in whole-cell vaccine group of S. pneumoniae, 98–99% of Group C (KP + SP Bivalent vaccine), 90% of Group D (SP + LPS, combined vaccine), 60% Group E (LPS-based vaccine) and 70% of group F (commercial Pneumococcal vaccine, Prevenar, 13). Whereas all mice in the control group after lethal challenge died showed 100% mortality.

Comparative cross protective efficacy of bivalent vaccine

The whole-cell vaccine against K. pneumoniae (Group A) and S. pneumoniae (Group B) elicited pathogen-specific antibody responses with moderate peak titers at day 48, followed by a decline on day 90 demonstrated partial protection following lethal challenge, with survival rates of 80% and 70% respectively. In contrast, the bivalent vaccine (Group C) induced significantly higher and more sustained antibody titers against both pathogens, with peak antibody titer at day 48, Mean ± SD (KP: 106.7 ± 36.9; SP: 85.3 ± 36.9) as shown in Supplementary Table 1. Importantly, antibody levels against each pathogen in the bivalent group exceeded those observed compared to monovalent groups, indicating a synergistic immune response with 98–99% survival rate. While combined vaccine SP + LPS (Group D) induced intermediate antibody responses, higher than monovalent LPS vaccination but consistently lower than the bivalent whole-cell vaccine. While LPS-based vaccine elicited detectable antibody responses, however, protection was comparatively reduced, with survival rates of mice group 60%, indicating that antibody quantity alone was insufficient to confer optimal protection. Whereas, the commercial conjugate vaccine (Group F, Prevenar 13) induced detectable but lower antibody titers as compared to the experimental bivalent vaccine. Whereas, statistical analysis using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test further confirmed that survival and immune responses in the bivalent vaccine group were significantly higher than those observed in monovalent and LPS-based groups (p < 0.0001) (Supplementary Table 4).These findings demonstrate a synergistic enhancement of protective immunity achieved by inclusion of whole-cell antigens from both pathogens within the bivalent vaccine, resulting in superior cross-protective efficacy as shown in Fig. 1.

Discussion

Pneumonia is a foremost public health challenge, particularly due to the emergence of antimicrobial-resistant (AMR) bacterial pathogens which contribute to high morbidity and mortality rates in developing countries30. Inspite, the global availability of vaccines to prevent pneumonia, the social and healthcare burden associated with the disease still persists, largely due to the variability of bacterial strains and the complex interplay of virulence factors. These factors complicate the management strategies to prevent pneumonia, particularly in low-income countries like Pakistan and India where access to healthcare and vaccination coverage remains suboptimal31,32. Thus, there is need to develop the localized vaccine of pneumonia against most prevalent bacterial pneumonia pathogens that must be cost-effective.

In different studies, prevalence of pneumonia was reported due to β-lactamase resistance 90–100% that was more or less consistent to our results28,33. Similar findings were also observed in different countries such as in China, Turkey and South Korea whereas lower resistance rates have been reported in Germany, Japan and Saudi Arabia. The variations in resistance rates may be due to distribution and diversity of resistance genes across regions28. Recently, macrolide drugs, Azithromycin and Erythromycin may be considered as first and second-line treatments to prevent pneumonia in low-income countries34.

In a study, the inactivated whole cell vaccine of S. pneumoniae and K. pneumoniae with concentration 5.6 and 4.2 × 1011CFU/ml respectively were prepared. These observations were in line with the previously reported studies that used same bacterial load 107 to 1011CFU/ml12,35. In a study, the adjuvant, Potassium Aluminum Sulphate gel was utilized to increase the efficacy of developed vaccine. In different studies, alum adjuvant was used for vaccine preparations12,36. The reason is that subcutaneous injection of alum gel facilitates antigen retention, enabling slow release and sustained stimulation of the immune system. Due to its minimal tissue reactivity, alum gel is commonly used in vaccine formulations. Also, it effectively triggers a primary immune response while secondary immune response remains limited36.

In the current study, BALB/c mice were used to subcutaneously inject vaccines, and a booster dose was administered on the 14th day after the first immunization. Antibody titers was measured on 7th, 14th, 48th, 60th, and 90th days by using the complement fixation test. CFT is a more sensitive approach for detecting antibodies capable of activating the complement system and given protection in vivo than ELISA, which only measures antibody levels11. In different studies, several experiments conducted by using various strains of BALB/c mice and Swiss albino mice with varied injection routes, such as intraperitoneal, subcutaneous, intradermal and intranasal11,21. Also, in various studies, clinical experiment on various days were conducted on 0, 14th, 28th, 48th, 60th, and 90th day, or 0, 14th, 28th day, and serum antibody titer was tested by Enzyme Linked Immuno Sorbent Assay (ELISA) and Complement Fixation Test (CFT)11,12,21.

In a study, it was observed that the mean antibody titer of inactivated whole cell vaccine of K. pneumoniae (53.3 ± 18.5) was highly significant on 48th day. Similar observations were also observed on immune response of outer membrane protein (rSpaO) recombinant vaccine develop against S. typhi that showed highest antibody titer at 48th day and these levels were significantly high up-to 60th day than decline at 90th day while in another study conducted in China in which formaldehyde inactivated bacteria P. aeruginosa showed highest IgG titer at 56th day after 1 st immunization11,37. The reason in the variation of peak antibody titer depends how the immune system reacts over time after vaccination. Early after exposure to the vaccine antigen, the immune system develops temporary antibody response, known as the extra-follicular response. While later on, the more stable and long-term immune response develops which involved formation of memory cells and long-lived antibody-producing cells. Furthermore, the initial exposure to the vaccine antigen activates B-lymphocytes, leading to the production of diverse antibodies and triggering the classical complement pathway, as reported in several studies38,39. As these B cells multiply and differentiate into plasma cells, antibody levels typically peak around day 48th. However, due to the short lifespan of plasma cells, antibody concentrations significantly decline by day 90th. A subsequent booster dose reactivates the immune system, prompting a secondary immune response and increase the immunoglobulin levels11,39. These findings align with earlier studies demonstrating elevated levels of anti-K. pneumoniae IgG1 and IgG2a antibodies in the serum of immunized BALB/c mice following intradermal administration of a heat-killed K. pneumoniae vaccine. The titer was measured using ELISA test, confirming the vaccine’s ability to stimulate a strong humoral immune response12. Differences in antibody titers between S. pneumoniae and K. pneumoniae whole-cell vaccines may be due to variations in mice strains or differences in immunization methods. Similar observations have been reported in other studies, where intranasal administration of heat-inactivated S. pneumoniae to inbred female BALB/c mice led to the production of serum IgM but not IgG antibodies. In contrast, other mice strains developed IgG responses following parenteral immunization40.

In the study, when a lethal challenge with LD50 dose of 1011 CFU per ml of bacterial pathogenic strains of K. pneumoniae and S. pneumoniae was done, the Group A and B showed 80% and 70% survival rate. The study was conducted in Bangladesh in which survival rate of experimental mice was 80–100% after 14th days post challenge immunization with heat killed K. pneumoniae12. While in other studies showed challenged with lethal dose of S. pneumoniae, exhibited long term immunity41. Different studies indicated that effective protection against colonization in mice, following whole-cell inactivated vaccination, determined by the production of Interleukin-7 cytokine by CD4 + T-cells while lack of these immune components, the protection is lost41,42.

Protective immunity against bacterial pneumonia involves multiple immune components and cannot be explained solely by the magnitude of antigen-specific antibody responses. Evidence from murine studies, particularly in BALB/c mice, indicates that IgG subclass distribution serves as an important indicator of immune polarization. Th1-skewed responses characterized by IgG2a and IgG2b production are associated with enhanced opsonization and Fcγ receptor–dependent phagocytosis, whereas IgG1 is more commonly linked to Th2-based immunity with comparatively reduced antibacterial efficacy43. Previous investigations have further demonstrated that chitosan-based pneumococcal nano-vaccines significantly enhances the IgG responses by stabilizing antigens, facilitating uptake by antigen-presenting cells, and promoting sustained immune activation44. Similarly, bivalent vaccination strategies have been shown to induce strong systemic and mucosal antibody responses, resulting in broad protection against both influenza virus and S. pneumoniae in co-infection models. The associated reductions in bacterial burden and prolonged survival observed in vaccinated mice emphasize the importance of antibody functionality in achieving durable and cross-protective immunity45. Therefore, beyond humoral responses, cell-mediated immunity plays a pivotal role in host defense against bacterial pathogens. Quantitative and qualitative assessment of vaccine-induced T-cell responses can be achieved through flow cytometric analysis of CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T lymphocytes, including the evaluation of activation markers such as CD69 and CD44. Among these responses, Th1- and Th17-mediated immunity is particularly critical for protection against extracellular bacteria, as IFN-γ enhances macrophage bactericidal activity, while IL-17 drives neutrophil recruitment and strengthens mucosal defenses46,47. Profiling cytokines such as IFN-γ, IL-17, IL-4, and IL-10 using multiplex assays or ELISA enables detailed characterization of Th1/Th2/Th17 of immune polarization. A predominance of Th1- and Th17-associated cytokines has been linked to improved bacterial clearance and survival, whereas Th2-dominant or regulatory cytokine environments may compromise protection despite sufficient antibody production48. Notably, Th17-driven immune responses have been shown to confer antibody-independent protection against pneumococcal colonization and invasive disease in mouse models47. In addition, functional assessment using opsonophagocytic killing assays (OPKA) and other cellular responses are widely regarded as a robust correlate of protection, as it directly measures the capacity of vaccine-induced antibodies to promote phagocyte-mediated bacterial killing, offering greater predictive value than antibody titers alone measured through complement fixation test49. Although, these methods are important to measure the cellular immune responses especially, to measure the ELISA based IgG class analysis and interleukin quantification that provide insights about cellular immunity, however due to financial constraints and inaccessibility to advanced immunological techniques, these assays were not feasible in the current study.

In present study, the results of bivalent vaccine (KP + SP) was statistically analyzed it showed that the antibody titer at 48th day of post vaccination day (106.7 ± 36.9) was greater as compared to individual whole cell vaccines of pneumonia. The protection rate of mice was observed 98–99%. The enhanced immune response observed with the KP + SP bivalent vaccine may be attributed to the synergistic interaction between the two antigens, which enhances their overall immunogenicity. Consequently, this combined formulation elicited a stronger immune reaction compared to individual vaccines. Similar results were reported in a study from Iran, where a bivalent vaccine containing rPspA proteins and the pneumococcal virulence factor PhtD was developed to protect BALB/c mice from pneumococcal sepsis. The study demonstrated that the combined antigens produced a synergistic effect, resulting in a 100% survival rate in the immunized mice. In addition, co-immunization with the novel rPspA led to the elevated levels of antigen-specific IgG antibodies against both rPspA and PhtD. The combination of these two antigens produced a stronger IgG response compared to each antigen administered individually10. Our observations are in line with previous research findings10,41. Another study demonstrated that intramuscular administration of a bivalent vaccine formulated with an alum adjuvant in rabbits, guinea pigs, and mice stimulated a protective antibody response against S.typhi and Paratyphi. This bivalent vaccine, developed using MAPS (Multiple Antigen Presenting System) technology, was shown to induce a strong memory immune response and sustained levels of circulating antibodies10,42.

In current study, the combine vaccine (SP + LPS) was also prepared by the combination of whole cell vaccine of S. pneumoniae (SP) and lipopolysacchide (LPS) of K. pneumoniae. The results of combine vaccine of (SP + LPS) were statistically analyzed that indicated that the antibody titer was highly significant as compared to respective whole cell vaccines having (P-value ≤ 0.05). These results align with earlier studies showing that B cells, as antigen-presenting cells (APCs), produce protective immunoglobulins in response to specific antigens. Moreover, vaccines formulated with lipopolysaccharide containing highly immunogenic components that can enhance B-cell activation, improve the presentation of S. pneumoniae antigens in the bloodstream, thus, significantly increase antibody production, thereby amplifying the overall immune response42. Furthermore, lipopolysaccharide based (LPS) promote the proliferation and differentiation of B-lymphocytes, led to the production of non-specific polyclonal IgM antibodies. During the immune response to the S. pneumoniae vaccine, cytokines are released that enhance both humoral and cellular immunity, not only against the S. pneumoniae antigens but also in response to LPS. This dual stimulation results in more sustained immunoprophylaxis and elevated antibody levels against both targets50. In present study following a lethal challenge, BALB/c mice immunized with the combined SP + LPS vaccine showed a 90% survival rate. This outcome is consistent with earlier findings demonstrating that bivalent, conjugated, and combination vaccines offers enhanced immunoprophylactic response and broad-spectrum defense against pneumonia-causing pathogens42,50.

The difference in immune responses observed between K. pneumoniae and S. pneumoniae vaccine components are consistent with well-established species-specific immunological properties. K. pneumoniae, as a Gram-negative bacterium, expresses lipopolysaccharide and immunogenic outer membrane proteins that efficiently engage innate immune receptors, particularly TLR4, leading to strong antigen-presenting cell activation and sustained humoral responses51. In contrast, S. pneumoniae is characterized by a thick capsular polysaccharide that can limit antigen accessibility and predominantly elicits T-cell–independent antibody responses, which are often less durable and less functionally protective. This immunological distinction may explain the comparatively reduced antibody titers and protection observed with pneumococcal components alone and supports the need for adjuvant strategies that enhance T-cell help and Th17-mediated immunity for optimal pneumococcal clearance52. Indeed, adjuvants that promote cellular and mucosal immune responses have been shown to significantly improve protection against S. pneumoniae, whereas the intrinsic innate stimulatory capacity of K. pneumoniae antigens may reduce reliance on additional adjuvant signaling53,54.

In a study, Hot-Phenol method was used for LPS extraction from the K. pneumoniae and it characterized by different techniques HPLC, FTIR and GC-MS. These results were in line with other reported studies which used the same techniques for the extraction and purification of LPS18,55. Furthermore, in a study, SDS PAGE was used to determine the molecular weight of LPS. In other studies, LPS extracted from E-coli and S. typhi, also showed the stair case ladder like pattern18,56. The FTIR spectrum of the extracted K. pneumoniae LPS, measured in the range of 4000 cm⁻¹ to 500 cm⁻¹, displayed distinct absorption peaks. These spectral features were interpreted based on the reference data provided by the Reynolds Research Group (2012)57. Mainly O-polysaccharide (1645.28 cm− 1) and Pyranose ring structure (667.37 cm− 1) was identified in extracted LPS that was also detected in other studies57,58.

In a study, the GC-MS analysis detected the vital components i.e. Phenylephrine, 1-Adamantane methylamine and Alpha-bisabolol’s. These compounds showed anti-inflammatory responses in different animal models. Whereas Alpha-bisabolol does not act directly on LPS but influences its release indirectly by inhibiting bacterial growth, thereby reducing LPS levels during infection and modulating the associated immune response59. When immune response of lipopolysaccharide (LPS) vaccine and its antibody titer were statistically analyzed that showed peak antibody titer on 48th day that was less as compared to whole cell and bivalent vaccines and exhibited 60% survival rate in mice after lethal dose (1011cfu). Whereas, in contrast to another study, it was observed that the recombinant lipopolysaccharide export protein (LptH) significantly protects mice against P. aeruginosa infections that induced IgG1 and IgG 2 levels and enhanced IFN-γ and IL-17 responses in the spleen60. Hence, LPS was used as a standard compound in clinical trials to explore various aspects of endotoxemia, inflammation, septic shock, and organ dysfunction, as well as its potential role in the development of future vaccines.

Therefore, the study showed that among all vaccines prepared bivalent vaccine (KP + SP) elicited enhances immunoprophylactic response and highest survival rate in BALB/c mice against resistant bacterial pneumonia pathogens when compared with commercial vaccine of pneumonia (Prevenar 13). Also, whole cell inactivated and lipopolysaccharides (LPS) based vaccines showed potential in inducing protective immunity. Thus, the combined vaccine commercially cost-effective and may reduce the frequency of immunizations and associated handling stress in animal models as reported in different studies61,62.

Conclusion

It is concluded from this study that the bivalent vaccine (KP + SP) developed from local clinical strains of K. pneumoniae and S. pneumoniae significantly enhances humoral response in murine (BALB/c) model, producing 98–99% survival rate. Additionally, both whole cell, combined vaccine (SP + LPS) and LPS based vaccines developed from indigenous strains are promising as a potent immunogens, for addressing the growing challenge of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) bacterial pneumonia infections in Pakistani population.

Limitations

The present study was primarily focused on humoral immune responses elicited by vaccines prepared from indigenous clinical isolates of K. pneumoniae and S. pneumoniae. However, comprehensive assessment of T-cell–mediated immunity, cytokine profiling, advanced immunological assays, genetic diversity, and serotype distribution among local isolates could not be performed due to resource and funding constraints. Although, these factors are essential for understanding immune regulation, cross- protection, and strain-specific variability. Future investigations will address these limitations to support rational vaccine design, dose optimization studies for the development of regionally tailored pneumonia vaccines.
