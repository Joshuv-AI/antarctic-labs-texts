Perioperative Changes in Blood Volume and Fluid Distribution in Total Hip Arthroplasty—A Protocol for a Prospective Single‐Center Observational Study
Authors: Puk Kristiansen, Nicolai Bang Foss, Kirill Gromov, Anders Troelsen, Ana‐Marija Hristovska
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundSurgical stress response initiates a complex pathophysiological cascade that alters fluid distribution and intravascular volume. In elective total hip arthroplasty (THA), the perioperative blood loss is potentially substantial, a significant part of the loss being hidden caused by tissue extravasation, residual joint blood, and hemolysis. This far conventional methods to quantify blood loss rely on either visual estimation of bleeding or formulas based on hematological and/or anthropometric parameters, both of which are considered imprecise. Accurate assessment of perioperative blood volume changes may inform more individualized postoperative fluid therapy and improve postoperative outcomes.MethodsThe study design is a single‐center prospective observational cohort study with enrollment of 25 patients undergoing elective THA in a standardized fast‐track setting. Perioperative blood volume will be assessed using the carbon monoxide (CO)‐rebreathing method, enabling quantification of total hemoglobin mass, red cell‐, plasma‐, and total blood volume. Bioimpedance spectroscopy will be used to evaluate fluid distribution between intra‐ and extracellular compartments, as well as total body water. Measurements will be performed preoperatively before surgery (baseline), at 6 h postoperatively, and at 24 h postoperatively when feasible. Demographic data, inflammatory markers, and data on perioperative fluid administration will also be collected. The primary outcome is change in total blood volume from baseline to 6 h postoperatively. Secondary outcomes include changes in hemoglobin mass, fluid compartment shifts, and their associations with postoperative orthostatic intolerance.DiscussionBy combining CO rebreathing and bioimpedance, our study applies accurate, minimally invasive methods not previously used in surgical patients to characterize blood loss and perioperative fluid shifts. Strengths include the standardized surgical setting, detailed protocol, and transparent statistical plan, whereas limitations include the observational design, minor sample size, and exclusion of patients with major intraoperative bleeding.ConclusionThe study will provide detailed information on the changes in perioperative blood volume and its association with orthostatic intolerance following THA. CO rebreathing may offer a precise alternative to conventional estimations of blood loss and contribute to the development of more individualized perioperative fluid management strategies in major orthopedic surgery.Keywords: CO rebreathing, hidden blood loss, orthostatic intolerance, surgical stress response, THA

Background

Surgical trauma induces a complex physiological stress response involving immunological, metabolic, and hormonal mechanisms [1]. Immunological activities trigger inflammation, which may impair capillary permeability and can cause extravasation of fluids and macromolecules into the interstitial space [2]. This can lead to inadequate tissue perfusion and, in severe cases, organ dysfunction [3]. Understanding and managing the surgical stress response is therefore crucial to achieving postoperative recovery [4].

Understanding perioperative blood loss is essential for administering appropriate fluid volumes to ensure adequate perfusion [5, 6]. However, accurately measuring blood loss remains challenging due to the lack of a “gold standard” [7]. Total perioperative blood loss consists of both visible and hidden losses [8, 9]. Conventional estimates of blood loss remain imprecise [10, 11]. Several formulas based on anthropometric and hematologic variables have been developed, but their accuracy for absolute blood loss [7, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17] estimation remains limited, particularly during substantial blood volume changes [7].

THA is widely performed for decades and is generally safe despite involving substantial blood loss, especially hidden loss [18, 19, 20]. Previous studies report wide variations in total blood loss during THA, ranging from 1.188 to 1.651 L [12, 21, 22]. Perioperative bleeding can result in allogeneic transfusions which are associated with serious adverse events and increased morbidity and mortality [23, 24]. Postoperative orthostatic intolerance (OI) is also a complication after alloplastic surgery that is very common (~20%) [25, 26, 27] and can be due to hypovolemia, with a potential risk of prolonging postoperative mobilization and affecting recovery [28].

The carbon monoxide (CO)‐rebreathing method allows quantification of total hemoglobin mass (tHgb) and thus the calculation of red blood cell‐, plasma‐, and total blood volume (TBV) [29, 30]. It is safe, rapid, and accurate, capable of detecting volume changes as small as 2% [30, 31].

Bioimpedance analysis (BIA) complements these measurements by assessing total body water (TBW), intra (ICF)‐ and extracellular fluid (ECF) distribution, and detects changes in fluid shifts [32].

Together these measurements offer a more precise evaluation of perioperative blood volume changes and fluid distribution that may improve preoperative strategies for fluid therapy.

We hypothesize that the CO‐rebreathing method offers a precise and simple perioperative measurement of the changes in blood volume, as an alternative to the current inaccurate estimations of perioperative blood loss. When combined with fluid distribution data, it may allow for more individualized, rational fluid therapy and contribute to improvement in patient‐related outcomes.

The aim of the study is to explore and describe perioperative change in blood volume and fluid distribution in patients undergoing THA by CO‐rebreathing and BIA. Furthermore, we want to investigate the link between these changes and orthostatic intolerance.

Methods

Study Design

This is a single‐center prospective observational cohort study conducted at the Department of Anesthesiology and Intensive Care Medicine and Department of Orthopedic Surgery, both Copenhagen University Hospital, Hvidovre.

Patient enrollment began in March 2025 and is expected to conclude in December 2025. Data cleaning will be performed once enrollment is complete and the required sample size is secured.

Study Population

Eligibility Criteria

All patients scheduled for an elective total hip replacement in the arthroplasty section at The Orthopedic Department will be screened according to the in‐ and exclusion criteria. If none of the exclusion criteria are met, the patient will be informed about the study. Total hip arthroplasty (THA) will be performed using a posterolateral approach under spinal anesthesia, with either an uncemented or hybrid (cemented femur) selected at the surgeon's discretion. Tranexamic acid IV is administered preoperative as well as 3 h intraoperative.

Methodology

Carbon‐Monoxide Rebreathing

It is performed using (OpCO Detalo Health, Denmark). By dilution principles, the total mass of hemoglobin (Hb) can be measured. A small known amount of CO is inhaled in a closed circle. The amount is determined by age, sex, height, and weight and is added to O2; thereby, the exposure of CO for the patient breathing in the closed circuit is very low. The exposure lasts for 4 min and allows most of the CO to bind to Hb. From the dilutions principles, the total amount of Hb molecules is determined based on the known number of CO molecules and change in fractions of carboxyHb in the blood (%HbCO). The total amount of Hb mass is calculated by multiplying Hb molar mass. Intravascular volumes (plasma volume [PV], erythrocyte volume [EBV], and TBV) can be determined from the mentioned calculation alongside measurement of hematocrit (Ht) and the circulating Hb concentration. The blood samples are analyzed by a blood gas apparatus (ABL 800, Radiometer, Denmark).

BIA

It is body composition measurement and assessment of fluid distribution. The apparatus sends a low‐frequency high‐amplitude current through electrodes placed on an arm as well as on the foot. In this study it will be used for determination of fluid status, shift in intra‐ and ECFs as well as TBW.

Simple Size and Power

The aim is to measure the 95th percentile of expected total blood loss during THA.

In a previous study a total blood loss in relation to a THA was calculated by Gross equation to be 1155 ± 377 mL [16]. We chose to estimate an average blood loss to be 1100 ± 200 mL and SD for 350 mL. With a power of 0.8 (1 − β) and an α value of 0.05 the calculated sample‐size is 25 participants.

As this is an observational study, any dropout will be replaced to ensure adequate data completeness.

Statistical Plan

All statistical analyses will be conducted using R software.

Data distribution will be assessed using Q–Q plots or histograms. Depending on distribution, appropriate parametric or nonparametric tests will be applied.

Continuous data will be presented as means with standard deviation (SD) or 95% confidence intervals or as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR). Categoric variables will be presented as counts and percentages.

Differences between pre‐ and postoperative measurements will be analyzed using a paired t‐test or Wilcoxon rank‐sum test.

Logistic regression analysis will be used to evaluate the association between changes in TBW and postoperative orthostatic intolerance. Linear regression will be used to analyze associations between changes in CRP and blood loss.

A p value of < 0.05 will be considered statistically significant.

Missing Data

All missing data will be described. As the study is not a randomized controlled trial, participants with substantial missing data will be replaced to ensure completeness, as outlined in the sample size section.

Discussion

Although changes in blood volume are influenced by blood loss, they also reflect the physiological responses to surgery stress response including fluid administration, inflammation, and capillary leakage [1, 4].

The CO‐rebreathing method to measure tHgb and TBV while BIA to evaluate body fluid compartments. Together, these methods of measurement aim to address the limitations of conventional evaluation of perioperative blood loss and change in blood volume.

Previous studies have estimated total blood loss related to a THA to be approximately 1100 mL based on Hb dilution [21]. This volume includes intraoperative‐ as well as postoperative blood loss through, for example, hemolysis and extravasation [9, 34]. Visible loss includes blood collected in suctions and absorbed in surgical sponges, and so forth [10, 11] is recognized as highly imprecise [10]. In orthopedic surgery hidden blood loss happens mainly postoperatively due to tissue extravasation, residual joint blood, and hemolysis [9, 34].

By applying CO‐rebreathing and BIA, in a standardized fast‐track surgical setting, the study pursues to provide novel insight about total‐ and hidden blood loss, fluid shifts and their association with surgical stress and postoperative recovery. Current fluid therapy strategies are guided by indirect clinical parameters. By understanding the pathophysiological redistribution in fluid compartments, the knowledge can optimize existing guidelines about individualized perioperative fluid therapy management. This may help avoidance of both hypo‐ and hypervolemia. Ultimate support further optimization of perioperative care and postoperative recovery following THA.

Limitations and Strengths

The protocol contains both strengths and limitations.

The limitations that must be acknowledged; First the observational design limits the ability to establish causal relationships and introduces a potential residual confounding, for example, due to variation in perioperative management.

Second, although it is a standardized procedure itself, differences in the surgeons' technical skills potentially impact the amount of tissue damage and intraoperative bleeding. To minimize this, all participating surgeons must have a minimum experience level of a senior registrar or higher.

Third intraoperative bleedings extending 750 mL or need of blood transfusion are excluded. These patients will likely have a greater physiological response and may undergo greater surgical stress and fluid shift. However, they are rare in the setting of an elective THA and would constitute statistical outliers [21, 35].

Fourth, due to the availability of only one CO‐rebreathing machine, it is only possible to include one patient per day. This introduces a potential selection bias as this logistical constraint can lead to exclusion of eligible patients for nonclinical reasons. This was accounted for by randomly informing the patients about the study rather than in a specific order.

Fifth, as the study is the first of its kind, a small sample size was chosen in order to explore if the methods of choice can confirm the stated hypothesis as expected.

The study also has a lot of strengths. First, the study investigates perioperative blood volume changes and fluid distribution using advanced measurement techniques that have not previously been applied to a surgical cohort before.

Second, the surgical procedure is standardized and performed in a fast‐track setting with limited variations in the perioperative pathways. This may minimalize procedural variability and enhances internal validity of the study. Optimal perioperative fluid therapy is essential to maintain intravascular volume and perfusion [36]. Both hypovolemia and hypervolemia are associated with increased morbidity [36, 37], yet accurate assessment of fluid needs remains challenging due to individual variability, for example, fasting time or type of surgery and limitations of standard clinical monitoring [37].

Third, both CO‐rebreathing and BIA are minimally invasive, safe, and well‐tolerated, causing very little or no harm or inconvenience for the patients. This makes the methods feasible for use on elective procedures.

Fourth, the publication of the protocol and statistical analysis plan improves transparency and creates reproducibility, which aligns with international recommendations for research reproducibility and data integrity.

We hope the insights from this study may contribute to a more nuanced understanding of surgical stress responses and support the continuous development of perioperative fluid management strategies.

Conclusion

The study will provide detailed insight into perioperative blood loss and its association with orthostatic intolerance. The CO‐rebreathing method, known for its high accuracy in detecting small changes in blood volume, may contribute valuable knowledge toward more individualized fluid management in patients undergoing major orthopedic surgery.
