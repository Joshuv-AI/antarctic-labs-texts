Optic nerve hypoplasia – Clinical profile and co-relation with vision and neuro-radiological features
Authors: Sampada Kulkarni, Rohan Nalawade, Manjushree Bhate
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Purpose:To study the features and co-relation of optic nerve hypoplasia (ONH) with severity of visual impairment and associated neuro-radiologic features.Methods:Of 297 ONH medical records screened, 57 patients with photographic documentation of the optic nerves and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain and orbits were included in the study. Patient demographics, vision, ocular examination findings, and optic disc photographs for disc diameter: macula distance (DD: DM), size of the disc, and double ring size along with MRI were analyzed by a single observer. The disc to macula ratio was used to grade ONH as <3 as partial/mild ONH, 3.12–4 as complete ONH, and >4.12 severe ONH.Results:The mean age at presentation was 11.38 (±8.87) years. The best corrected visual acuity in the better eye ranged from 20/20 (n = 2; 3.5%) to severe to profound vision loss (<20/200 – no light perception) (n = 25; 43.85%). Optic disc findings suggestive of mild ONH in n= 31eyes (39.2%); and severe in n =17eyes (21.5%). Bilateral ONH was noted in 22 patients (38.6%), and unilateral was noted in 35 patients (59.65%). Neuroimaging findings were reported normal (n = 10, 17.54%), absent septum pellucidum and corpus callosal thinning (n = 19, 33.33%), and chiasmal atrophy (n = 9, 15.8%). Correlation analysis confirmed that as the DD: DM distance increases, poorer vision and abnormal neuro-imaging findings were noted.Conclusion:Severity of ONH as indicated by DD: DM ratio is a surrogate indicator of neuro-radiological abnormalities and poor vision. Neuro-imaging and a multidisciplinary approach are warranted.

Optic nerve hypoplasia (ONH) is a congenital disc anomaly and is one of the leading causes of childhood blindness.[1] It is a nonprogressive, underdevelopment of the optic nerve, accounting for profound visual impairment in approximately 15%–25% of infants.[2] ONH is proposed to be caused by an insult to a developing ocular system between the 6th and 16th weeks of gestation.[3] ONH can be unilateral or bilateral and further divided into diffuse and segmental with and without tilt and into three clinical subtypes: optic nerve hypoplasia simplex, septo-optic dysplasia, and septo-optic-pituitary dysplasia.[4]

The clinical spectrum of symptoms in ONH ranges from good vision to significant visual, neurological, and systemic impairment.[5] It has been associated with multiple neurological, ocular, and nonocular abnormalities.[6] The neurological anomalies occur as isolated defects, including developmental delay, hypoglycemic seizures or epilepsy, ocular abnormalities (microphthalmos, aniridia, coloboma, nystagmus, strabismus), and cranial abnormalities (agenesis of septum pellucidum, anencephaly, midline abnormalities of brain), or facial anomalies. ONH may present as a component of septo‐optic dysplasia (de Morsier’s syndrome), which presents with midline brain malformations and hypopituitarism.[2]

The incidence of clinico-neurologic abnormalities in patients with bilateral ONH (65%) is higher compared to unilateral ONH. The current study evaluates the characteristics of optic disc hypoplasia and its association with the vision and neuro-radiological findings.

Methods

This is a retrospective observational case series conducted at LV Prasad Eye Institute, Hyderabad, India. It adhered to the declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the institutional review board (LEC-BHR-R-12-21-774). Electronic medical record databases for patients with ONH who presented to the institute between January 2015 and December 2020 using the ICD code (ICD code: H47.03) of ONH diagnosis were evaluated. Patients having photographic documentation of the optic nerves demonstrating either unilateral or bilateral ONH and those with MRI imaging of the brain and orbits were included in the study.

Patient demographics including age, gender, and best corrected visual acuity were assessed using age-appropriate guidelines for vision assessment, refractive error in terms of spherical equivalent, strabismus, and nystagmus evaluation. Chief presenting complaints, pregnancy and family history, and other general examinations were obtained from the institute medical records for analysis.

Vision: The patients were classified into mild vision impairment (20/20–20/60 or LOGMAR up to 0.50), moderate vision loss (<20/60–20/200 or LOGMAR 0.52–1), severe vision loss (<20/200–20/400 or LOGMAR 1.02–1.30), and profound vision impairment (<20/400–20/1200 with LOGMAR worse than 1.32) groups. Blindness was regarded as vision <20/1200 to no light perception.

Strabismus and Nystagmus: The alternate prism cover test or modified Krinsky test was performed wherever possible. The findings were recorded as exotropia (XT), esotropia (ET), dissociated vertical deviation (DVD), and complex strabismus. Nystagmus was documented as pendular, jerky, uniplanar, multiplanar, or rotatory type.

Fundus and Optic disc examination: Examination was performed with a binocular indirect ophthalmoscope using a +20 D lens. Optic disc photographs were captured using a TOPCON TRC-NW8 nonmydriatic retinal camera with a 30-degree field optic disc protocol. The optic disc photographs (colored and red free) were analyzed by a single observer for size, peri-papillary pigmentation (hypo or hyper pigmentation), double ring sign, and emergence of blood vessels from the optic disc. The disc-to-macula ratio was calculated using a standard scale. The disc size was graded as small (<1.6 mm2), average (1.65–2.65 mm2), and large (>2.65 mm2) according to previously published classification by Rao et al.[7] In all cases, the horizontal and transverse diameters were measured and divided by two. A single observer calculated the temporal margin of the disc-to-macula distance (DM/DD distance) using a standard scale. Optic disc hypoplasia was graded as normal (no signs of double ring sign, PPRCA, blood vessel changes, increased DM/DD distance), mild (DM/DD distance 2.65–3 with one of the changes like PPRCA, double ring sign, increased tortuosity of blood vessel), moderate (3.12–4 DM/DD with or without other changes), and severe (>4.12 DM/DD distance) [Fig. 1].

The DM: DD distance was graded as follows: 2.65–3.00 with other signs of ONH as partial/mild ONH, 3.12-4 as complete ONH, and >4.12 severe ONH.

Neuroimaging: All patients underwent neuroimaging either magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or computed tomography (CT) of the brain. Clinical notes and/or images of the neuroimaging results were documented [Fig. 2].

Patients were referred for the endocrine pediatrician/physician for endocrine and neurological workup.

Statistical analysis was performed using Microsoft excel 2007. Continuous data were summarized using mean, median, and interquartile ranges, and categorical data were summarized using percentages. Univariate and multivariate analyses were done to assess correlation of the best correction visual acuity, DD: DM distance, and neuro-imaging findings.

Visual acuity: The best corrected visual acuity (BCVA) ranged from 20/20 to no perception of light. In the better eye, normal vision was noted in n = 2, 3.5%; mild vision loss was observed in n = 25, 43.85%; moderate in n = 5, 8.77%; and severe in n = 4, 7%, and profound vision loss in 21 (36.84%) patients, of which 20 (35%) patients were regarded as blind [Table 1].

Refraction: The refractive error ranged from +3 D to -15 D. Table 1 shows the distribution of refractive error in the right and left eyes with range and standard deviation. Thirty-eight (66.67%) patients had astigmatism (range -0.50 to -4.5). With the rule, against the rule, and oblique astigmatism were observed in 32 (56.14%), four (0.07%), and two (0.03%) patients, respectively. Compound myopic astigmatism was present in n = 13, 22.80%, and three (0.05%) had mixed astigmatism.

Nystagmus: Jerky or multiplanar nystagmus was noted in 26 (45.61%) patients, and 20 (35.1%) patients were normal. The remaining 11 (19.29%) patients did not report abnormal eye movement [Table 1].

Strabismus: Strabismus associated with ONH was noted in n = 50, 87.71% patients. Exotropia was more commonly observed (42%), followed by esotropia (32%) [Table 1].

Pupil: Relative afferent pupillary defect RAPD was observed in 33 (57.9%) eyes, including 11 (19.3%) patients with bilateral ONH as shown in Table 1.

Optic disc findings: They were analyzed using photographs of the optic discs. Mild, moderate, and severe ONH were observed in n = 21 (36.84%), n = 22 (38.6%), and n = 14 (22.80%) eyes, respectively. Myelinated nerve fibers with unilateral optic nerve hypoplasia were noted in n = 1, 0.02%.

Laterality: Bilateral ONH was documented in (n = 22 patients, 44 eyes, 38.6%), and unilateral ONH in n = 35 patients, 59.65%. In unilateral cases, the right eye was involved in 15 (42.85%) and the left eye in 20 (57.14%) patients [Table 2].

Discussion

ONH is a congenital anomaly characterized by the underdevelopment of the optic nerve which presents with variations in visual impairment and clinical and radiological features and is often associated with neurological and/or endocrinological abnormalities.[8]

Our cohort had a male predominance with the mean age at presentation as 11.38 (±8.77) years. Similar demographics were observed by either reported studies.[59] Some studies suggest presentation earlier in life in cases of ONH with more severe visual impairment.[10] A direct co-relation between the severity of optic disc hypoplasia as measured by DD: DM ratio and the level of visual impairment and presence of midline neurological abnormalities on MRI (P value 0.03) was observed. Patients with midline abnormalities had associated endocrine abnormalities (n = 8) such as mild hypothyroidism and low levels of testosterone, so also, an increased retinal vessel tortuosity was documented in these, as has been previously reported.[1112]

Visual acuity (VA) outcomes in ONH patients can be highly variable, as observed in our cohort wherein almost half had mild visual impairment (43.85% in the better eye), while profound vision loss was noted in 36.8% in the better eye. These findings were comparable to previous studies where a wide range of visual impairments, from near-normal vision to blindness, were reported.[13] Additionally, Brodsky and colleagues mention the role of optic disc appearance in predicting the potential for vision recovery.[14]

Our cohort demonstrated exotropia in 21.05% (n = 13), which was higher as compared to the study by Garcia,[13] who observed myopia in 10–15% of ONH patients. This disparity may reflect regional differences in the study populations; however, it underscores the importance of carefully evaluating refractive errors in ONH patients to optimize visual outcomes.

Neuro-Radiological Findings: Associations with Brain Malformations.

Our findings showed a high prevalence of some brain malformations, such as agenesis of the septum pellucidum (30%) and chiasmal atrophy (22%), particularly in patients with bilateral ONH. These findings are consistent with previous literature,[1415] who reported similar associations between bilateral ONH and brain anomalies, especially midline malformations. The strong correlation between an increased DM: DD ratio and the presence of midline brain abnormalities in our study (P value 0.03) further strengthens the argument that ONH is not merely an isolated ocular condition but is part of a broader developmental disorder involving both the optic nerve and central nervous system. One patient of bilateral ONH reported the finding of opticochiasmatic glioma on MRI. This is likely an incidental finding and has not been previously reported in the literature. Additionally, among the patients who demonstrated T2 hyperintensities on MRI, clinical findings of developmental delay and seizure disorder were noted.

A higher incidence of strabismus was noted in our cohort (n = 50, 87.71%), of which n = 21, 42% had manifest exotropia patients, while 45.61% presented nystagmus and/or nystagmoid eye movements. Garcia et al.[13] similarly reported a higher incidence of exotropia and nystagmus. Brodsky has shown a more balanced distribution of strabismus types.[10] The presence of nystagmus, along with refractive errors and strabismus, is thought to reflect the dysfunction of the visual pathways and abnormal ocular motor control, further supporting the notion that ONH is a disorder of both the visual and neural systems.

Our study had several limitations; a longitudinal follow-up could not be obtained to decipher if trends of visual impairment changed with vision rehabilitation. A significant number of patients did not follow up with the endocrinology services being situated at a different hospital.

Conclusion

There is strong correlation between severity of optic disc hypoplasia as quantified by increased DD: DM distance and the presence of midline brain malformations, which highlights the importance of comprehensive neuro-radiological evaluation in all cases of ONH, facilitating early detection of associated neurological anomalies. Recognizing that bilateral ONH is predominantly associated with profound visual and neurological deficits mandates prompt, multidisciplinary intervention to optimize developmental and functional outcomes. Unilateral ONH cases present with neuro-radiological findings warranting the need for neuroimaging in bilateral and unilateral ONH. Early diagnosis with individualized rehabilitative management maximizes residual vision and quality of life. These insights reinforce that ONH is not an isolated ocular anomaly but a complex neuro-ophthalmic disorder requiring a multidisciplinary approach that addresses ocular, neurological, and endocrine components to improve patient outcomes.
