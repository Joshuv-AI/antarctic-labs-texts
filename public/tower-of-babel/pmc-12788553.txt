Comparative Evaluation of Salivary and Serum Levels of Uric Acid (UA) and Lactate Dehydrogenase (LDH) among Patients with Periodontal Disease: A Biochemical Study
Authors: Arindam Das, B.J Janardhana Amaranath, Koushik Mukherjee, Kumari Pallavi, Tammana Bhardwaj, Neebha Kumari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Periodontitis is initiated by the formation of plaque biofilm leading to loss of equilibrium between microbial organisms and host response, resulting in disease progression. Antioxidants such as uric acid (UA) found in saliva and serum provide protection against free radical. Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) is an intracellular enzyme detected in cellular cytoplasm of the cells in human body, which becomes extracellular on cell death.Aim:The aim of the study was to evaluate and compare salivary and serum levels of uric acid (UA) and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) as specific biomarkers for periodontal tissue destruction among patients with periodontal disease.Materials and Methods:Ninety subjects were selected and placed in three groups comprising thirty subjects in each group. Group 1: Comprised of clinically healthy periodontium patients. Group 2: Comprised of patients with chronic generalized gingivitis. Group 3: Comprised of patients with generalized chronic periodontitis. Serum and salivary samples were collected and subjected to laboratory analysis to asses UA and LDH levels.Results:Serum and salivary levels of LDH and UA were found to be within normal limits in healthy subjects while LDH level increased in patients with gingivitis and periodontitis. UA level decreased in periodontitis patients.Conclusion:LDH and UA may be considered as biochemical and diagnostic markers for periodontal diseases.

INTRODUCTION

Periodontal diseases comprise a wide range of inflammatory conditions that affect the supporting structures of the teeth (the gingiva, bone, and periodontal ligament), which could lead to tooth loss and contribute to systemic inflammation.[1] Plaque has been identified as the most common etiologic factor in the development and progression of periodontal diseases.

The course of the disease is determined by the interaction between the periodontopathogenic bacteria and the host immune response. Reactive oxygen species play a role in these interactions in favor of tissue destruction.[2] Oxidative stress plays an important role and it has been reported to affect the pathogenesis of periodontal diseases.[3] In case of periodontal disease, increased PMN count and activity causes a high rate of ROS release, leading to increased oxidative stress in periodontal tissues.[4] ROS produced on the surfaces of osteoclasts may play an important role in alveolar bone resorption.[5] Periodontal tissues require adequate levels of antioxidants to prevent tissue damage caused by reactive oxygen species.

Uric acid is the end product of purine catabolism and contributes to the antioxidant capacity of blood and saliva.[6]

Lactate dehydrogenase is a cytoplasmic enzyme that can be found in the cells of almost all body tissues; it could be released in the extracellular environment from necrotic cells, as a result of periodontal destruction leading to cell injury and cell death.

The clinical diagnosis of periodontal disease is traditionally based on clinical examination of the detectable parameters such as probing depth, attachment level, plaque index, bleeding on probing and radiographic assessment of alveolar bone loss.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

A total of 90 participants, aged between 35–45 years and comprising both genders, were selected from the OPD section of the Department of Periodontology. The study included 30 individuals each with clinically healthy periodontium (Group 1), chronic generalized gingivitis (Group 2), and generalized chronic periodontitis (Group 3). Inclusion criteria required participants to have at least 20 teeth and meet the clinical diagnosis criteria for one of the three periodontal conditions. Patients with immunocompromised status, recent periodontal or antibiotic/anti-inflammatory therapy (within 3–6 months), pregnant or lactating women, and those with a history of alcohol, tobacco, or drug abuse were excluded.

After group assignment, participants were asked to provide both blood and saliva samples. They were instructed to avoid food and drink for 2 hours before saliva collection, which took place between 9 and 10 a.m. after rinsing the mouth with water. Unstimulated saliva (minimum 1 cc) was collected using the spitting method and sent to the laboratory within 24 hours. Simultaneously, 2 ml of venous blood was drawn from the antecubital fossa to assess serum levels of uric acid (UA) and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH). Both saliva and serum samples were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 5 minutes.

The biochemical markers analyzed in this study included salivary uric acid, salivary LDH, serum uric acid, and serum LDH. These were measured using a semiautoanalyzer. Data analysis were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 20.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA).

DISCUSSION

In this study, LDH levels in both saliva and serum were significantly elevated in Group 3 (periodontitis), followed by Group 2 and Group 1. Salivary LDH was highest in Group 3 (1245.92 ± 434.18 IU/L), while serum LDH was 474.75 ± 139.90 IU/L in the same group. These findings support previous studies linking elevated LDH with periodontal tissue destruction. Ghalaut et al. reported increased serum LDH in chronic periodontitis, suggesting its potential as a disease severity biomarker.[7] Similarly, Sudhakar et al. demonstrated raised salivary LDH in periodontitis patients, advocating for its use as a noninvasive diagnostic marker.[8]

A moderate positive correlation (r = 0.582, P < 0.001) was found between serum and salivary LDH levels, aligning with findings from Havle et al., who reported a significant correlation between the two fluids.[9] However, some studies by Peña et al.[10] found inconsistent associations between serum and salivary LDH levels, likely due to variations in study designs or populations.[4]

Uric acid levels in saliva showed a decreasing trend from Group 1 to Group 3, while serum UA levels were highest in Group 1. This variability reflects the complex relationship between UA and periodontitis. Bai et al.[11] observed reduced serum UA in severe periodontitis, attributing it to oxidative stress and lower antioxidant capacity, whereas Chen et al.[12] suggested that higher UA levels could be a compensatory antioxidant response to inflammation. Salivary UA showed a weak correlation with serum UA (r = 0.263, P = 0.012), and while Ye et al.[13] found significant salivary UA differences between groups, others like Ali et al.[14] did not observe such differences. These inconsistencies highlight the need for further research to clarify UA’s role in periodontal disease.

CONCLUSION

This study supports the potential use of LDH as a reliable biomarker for monitoring periodontal disease severity, particularly in serum and saliva. The moderate correlation between serum and salivary LDH levels suggests that saliva could serve as a noninvasive alternative to serum for measuring LDH in clinical settings. However, the role of UA as a biomarker for periodontal disease remains unclear.
