Selenium-induced curvature control enables polymorphism in borabuckybowls
Authors: Atsuhiro Ikeno, Masahiro Hayakawa, Nonoka Masunaga, Akira Ueda, Takuji Hatakeyama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Selenium, a heavier congener of sulfur, has a larger atomic radius and higher polarizability, leading to distinct chemical reactivity and bonding. While these differences have been exploited in biological redox chemistry and materials science, their structural implications for molecular geometry and conformational adaptability remain underexplored. Such effects are particularly important for tuning solid-state properties of curved π-conjugated systems. Here we report a selenium-embedded borabuckybowl with shallow bowl geometry and enhanced shape adaptability. This structural flexibility enhances the redox stability and enables three distinct polymorphs depending on crystallization temperature. Differential scanning calorimetry revealed clear thermodynamic differences among these polymorphs in enthalpic and entropic contributions. The influence of crystal polymorphism on microscopic charge-transport properties was investigated computationally, and the structural adaptability and stability toward oxidation enabled the formation of suitable single crystals under I2 doping conditions, allowing experimental evaluation of electrical conductivity. This study highlights sulfur-to-selenium substitution as an effective strategy for controlling molecular shape adaptability and solid-state properties of curved π-conjugated molecules.

Introduction

Selenium is a heavier congener of sulfur and possesses a larger atomic radius and higher polarizability, leading to distinct chemical reactivity and bonding characteristics. Nature selectively exploits these differences between sulfur and selenium.1–4 For example, the amino acid selenocysteine is incorporated into enzymes that require high redox activity, where the higher reactivity of selenium enables efficient catalytic cycles compared with its sulfur analogue cysteine (Fig. 1(a)).5 Inspired by these intrinsic differences, sulfur-to-selenium substitution has also been widely utilized in materials science. For instance, substitution of sulfur with selenium in II–VI semiconductors such as ZnS and ZnSe leads to pronounced differences in optical properties, typically resulting in smaller band gaps and red-shifted absorption (Fig. 1(b)).6–8 Similar trends are observed in layered transition-metal dichalcogenides, where replacement of sulfur with selenium in materials such as MoS2 and MoSe2 modifies carrier transport and excitonic properties.9–11

In molecular chemistry, the larger polarizability of selenium can significantly influence intermolecular interactions and solid-state organization.12,13 For example, selenium atoms can participate in chalcogen bonding interactions that affect molecular organization and crystal packing.14,15 In π-conjugated molecular systems, replacement of sulfur with selenium has been shown to significantly modify intermolecular interactions and solid-state packing structures, as exemplified by the enhanced electronic properties observed in selenophene-containing π-systems and in tetrathiafulvalene (TTF) derivatives such as tetramethyltetraselenafulvalene (TMTSF) (Fig. 1(c)).16 Despite these well-recognized effects, the structural implications of sulfur-to-selenium substitution for controlling molecular shape and conformational adaptability have received far less attention. This issue is particularly relevant in heteroatom-containing curved π-conjugated molecules, which have recently attracted considerable attention.17–23 In such systems, even subtle structural perturbations can lead to pronounced changes in supramolecular organization and solid-state properties.24–28

Here we report a selenium-embedded borabuckybowl (BSe3-bb) that exhibits pronounced shape adaptability arising from sulfur-to-selenium substitution. In contrast to the previously reported sulfur analogue,29 which adopts a relatively rigid bowl geometry, the selenium-containing system displays substantial structural flexibility (Fig. 1(d)). The elongated C–Se bonds reduce the bowl-to-bowl inversion barrier while maintaining a moderate bowl depth, enabling substantial molecular shape variation in the solid state. As a result, the molecule forms three distinct crystal polymorphs depending on crystallization temperature. Thermodynamic analyses reveal that these polymorphs differ in enthalpic and entropic contributions, and their distinct packing structures give rise to different charge-transport characteristic characteristics in the solid state. Notably, the molecular shape adaptability and resulting polymorphism enable the formation of robust π-stacking crystals that remain suitable even under iodine doping conditions, allowing experimental evaluation of electrical conductivity. These findings establish heteroatom substitution as an effective strategy for engineering molecular curvature, structural adaptability, and polymorphism in curved π-conjugated systems.

Results and discussion

Synthetic procedures

BSe3-bb was synthesized in two steps from TBC-Br (Scheme 1). The three bromine groups of TBC-Br were converted to methylseleno groups through lithium–bromine exchange and nucleophilic substitution with dimethyl diselenide, producing the borylation precursor TBC-SeMe in 89% yield. Subsequently, triple selenoboration of TBC-SeMe was carried out using boron triiodide (BI3) and 2,6-di-tert-butylpyridine as base in o-dichlorobenzene. After stirring at 90 °C for 24 h, BSe3-bb was obtained in 35% yield as a brown solid. BSe3-bb exhibited no fluorescence emission owing to the symmetry-forbidden S0–S1 transitions with a small oscillator strength (f = 0.0002) (Fig. S5). Additionally, its Lewis acidity was reduced compared with that of the sulfur analog, which can be attributed to the shallower bowl structure (Fig. S6).

Electrochemical property

The redox properties of BSe3-bb were revealed using cyclic voltammetry (CV) measurements in o-dichlorobenzene (Fig. 2(a)). In the reductive region, BSe3-bb exhibited two reversible one-electron redox processes with the half-wave potentials at Ered1/2 (vs. Fc/Fc+) of −1.45 V and −2.02 V. In contrast, a single pseudo-reversible two-electron redox wave was observed in the oxidative region at Eox1/2 (vs. Fc/Fc+) of 0.90 V, which is lower than that of BS3-bb (0.92 V). This two-electron process was not observed for the structurally similar sulfur analogue which instead shows two sequential one-electron oxidative waves; therefore, we performed theoretical calculations to elucidate the origin of this phenomenon. According to the calculations, the energy gap between the HOMO and HOMO−1 of BSe3-bb is approximately 0.10 eV at the B3LYP/6-311+G(d,p) level of theory with the SMD solvation model for o-dichlorobenzene, indicating that these act as nearly degenerated molecular orbitals. Moreover, the NICS and ACID calculations corroborated that this process was accelerated by the acquisition of aromaticity in the dicationic state. While only outer peripheral benzene rings contribute to the aromatic stabilization in the neutral species, the central boratricyclic unit exhibited the large negative NICS(1)zz values in the dicationic species (Fig. 2(b)), and the diatropic ring current distributed across the entire molecule was observed (Fig. 2(c)). These calculations substantiate the simultaneous occurrence of the two-electron oxidation process in the cyclic voltammogram.

Additionally, to elucidate the impact of structural flexibility on the redox properties, the optimized geometries of BSe3-bb in neutral, dicationic, and dianionic states were obtained by DFT calculations at the B3LYP/6-31+G(d,p) level of theory (Table S3). Importantly, both the dicationic and dianionic species adopted the shallower bowl-shaped structure (0.380 and 0.354 Å, respectively) compared to the neutral form (0.498 Å). These structural changes can be rationalized in terms of aromatic stabilization, as supported by NICS and ACID calculations. A reduction in bowl depth enhances the orbital overlap and promotes the efficient π-electron delocalization, thereby maximizing the stabilization effect. Furthermore, computational studies revealed that such structural changes were more pronounced in BSe3-bb than in BS3-bb, which exhibits a more rigid framework (1.057, 1.001, and 1.001 Å in neutral, dicationic, and dianionic states, respectively). Consistent with these results, the destabilization energy associated with two-electron oxidation of BSe3-bb was 32.3 kJ mol−1 lower than that of BS3-bb based on the Gibbs free energy change calculated with the SMD solvation model for o-dichlorobenzene, while the energy differences upon two-electron reduction was much smaller (5.1 kJ mol−1). These results indicated that the enhanced structural flexibility reduced the energy penalty associated with the conformational change upon two-electron redox processes, thereby facilitating the formation of stable oxidized and reduced species.

X-ray crystallography

To elucidate the solid-state structure of BSe3-bb, single crystals were grown by slow diffusion of acetonitrile into chlorobenzene solution of BSe3-bb and analyzed using X-ray diffraction. Notably, temperature-controlled crystallization afforded three racemic polymorphs, each comprising two enantiomers, (A)-BSe3-bb and (C)-BSe3-bb, stemming from bowl inversion. The crystal phases obtained at −30 °C, 4 °C, and room temperature are hereafter abbreviated as the α-, β-, and γ-phases, respectively. A striking difference among the three polymorphs was the bowl depth, defined as the distance between the boron atom and the plane formed by the three selenium atoms. While the α-phase adopted a distinctly curved bowl-shaped structure (0.915 Å), the β- and γ-phases featured nearly planar structures with much shallower bowl depths (0.155 Å and 0.214 Å, respectively) (Fig. 3(b)). This structural variation in bowl depth (0.760 Å between α-phase and β-phase) is prominent compared to the values reported for typical buckybowls exhibiting polymorphism (0.01–0.54 Å).30–32 These geometric changes affected the electronic polarization of BSe3-bb. The calculated dipole moments at the M062X/6-31+G(d,p) level of theory followed the order of α > γ > β (1.57, 0.35, and 0.28 D, respectively). Furthermore, the local structural parameters around the central boron atom also differed. The sum of the C–B–C angle was nearly 360° in the β- and γ-phases (359.8° and 359.7°, respectively), but in the α-phase, it was a little smaller (355.3°). Likewise, the average C–B bond length was marginally elongated in the α-phase (1.49 Å) compared to those in the β- and γ-phases (1.47 Å). Consequently, the boron atom in the α-phase exhibited larger out-of-plane displacement from the adjacent carbon atoms plane. To further clarify the degree of curvature around the boron center, the pyramidalization angles θp (θp = θσπ − 90°), where θσπ is the angle between the in-plane σ bonds and out-of-plane π axis, were calculated based on the C–B–C angles. Indeed, θp for the α-phase (7.3°) was larger than those for the β- and γ-phases (1.4° and 1.6°), which confirms the pyramidal-like structure of the boron center (Table 1).

Structural variations of the molecules also significantly affected the packing motifs. In the α-phase, the moderately curved molecules were stacked in a slipped-stacking polar columnar with a slipping angle of 9.1°. The intermolecular distance in a single columnar structure was determined to be approximately 3.55 Å based on the distance between two parallel planes defined by selenium atoms every other molecule (7.09 Å) (Fig. 3(c), α). In contrast, the nearly planar structure in the β- and γ-phases assembled in a nonpolar pitched π-stacking structure, displaying slightly larger π–π distances (3.56 Å and 3.59 Å) than that observed in the α-phase (ca. 3.55 Å) (Fig. 3(c), β and γ). Although both the β- and γ-phases exhibited similar π-layered structures, the stacking arrangement of these structures differed along the vertical direction (along the a- and c-axes, respectively). While the β-phase consists of two alternated layered patterns along the a-axis, the γ-phase featured four sequential patterns (1, 2, 3, and 4 in Fig. 3(c), γ), resulting in a more complicated packing structure. This diversity of the packing motifs stands in sharp contrast to the previously reported sulfur-incorporated analogue (BS3-bb), which exhibits no crystal polymorphism despite its structural similarity. To rationalize this difference, we examined the bowl-to-bowl dynamics of these molecules. Theoretical calculations at the M062X/6-31+G(d,p) level of theory revealed that BSe3-bb possesses a low inversion barrier of 2.2 kcal mol−1, which is approximately one-third of that for BS3-bb (6.1 kcal mol−1) (Fig. S9 and Table S2). Despite its low inversion barrier, BSe3-bb retains a moderate bowl depth (0.783 Å) in the DFT-optimized structure, allowing substantial molecular shape variation in the crystal phase. These computational results indicate that this enhanced structural flexibility induced by selenium substitution enables molecular assembly in a nearly planar transition-state-like form, which results in the pitched π-stacking structure observed in the β- and γ-phases above room temperature. In addition, crystallographic analysis revealed clear differences in packing symmetry among the polymorphs (Fig. S1–S3). The α-phase crystallizes in a higher-symmetry orthorhombic space group (Pca21), whereas the β- and γ-phases adopt lower-symmetry monoclinic space groups (P21/c and I2/a, respectively). These differences in crystallographic symmetry and packing complexity suggest that the β- and γ-phases possess greater configurational freedom in the crystal lattice than the α-phase, which may contribute to their entropic preference.33

Thermodynamic origin of the polymorphism

To gain further insight into the thermodynamic origin of the temperature-dependent polymorphism, the α-, β-, and γ-phases of BSe3-bb were investigated using differential scanning calorimetry (DSC). Since these phases originate from the same monomer in the identical solvent condition, the relative contribution of the enthalpic and entropic effects governing their formation are reflected in their thermodynamic properties. Although these phases can be selectively obtained by the temperature-controlled solution method described in the X-ray crystallography section, modified preparation protocols were employed to produce sufficient quantities of each crystal phase in high purity (details are described in SI). For the DSC measurements, microcrystals of BSe3-bb were prepared by sublimation for the γ-phase, whereas those for the α- and β-phases were obtained by solution-based crystallization.

The DSC traces of all three polymorphs displayed one endothermic solid–solid transition peak at different temperatures, followed by a sharp melting peak at nearly identical temperatures within the experimental uncertainty (358 ± 1.3 °C) (Fig. 4(b)). The near consistency of the melting behaviors among the α-, β-, and γ-phases, including the temperatures, endothermic amounts, and peak shapes, indicated that all of them transferred to the same solid phase after the first endothermic transition. Here, PXRD and additional DSC measurements suggest that this transformation proceeds irreversibly to a crystalline phase distinct from these three phases (Fig. S21 and S22). Thus, in this context, the enthalpy of fusion was assumed to be identical for the α-, β-, and γ-phases. While the DSC trace for the γ-phase microcrystals represented the pure-phase pattern, those for the α- and β-phases contained small amounts of heat absorption attributed to other polymorphs. To accurately determine their transition enthalpy and entropy, the heat-flow contributions from these minor components were evaluated and corrected based on the estimated mass ratio of each phase (see SI for details). In the case of the α-phase, an additional small heat absorption was observed around T = 300–305 °C, tentatively attributed to a minor unidentified phase (hereafter referred to as the δ-phase). As this δ-phase had no contribution to the melting endotherm (Fig. S19(a)), the transition enthalpy and entropy of the α-phase were evaluated using the melting enthalpy that was considered common to all polymorphs (32.6 mJ mg−1). Based on the corrected DSC data, the transition enthalpy was found to decrease in the order α > γ > β (8.3, 6.9, and 5.7 kJ mol−1, respectively), indicating that the α-phase is enthalpically favored among the three phases. Moreover, the transition entropy of the β- and γ-phases (12 and 9.6 J mol−1 K−1, respectively) was smaller than that of the α-phase (17 J mol−1 K−1), suggesting that β- and γ-phases are entropically favored. Hence, the relative free-energy stabilization of enthalpically unfavorable β- and γ-phases is expected to increase at elevated temperatures where entropic contribution becomes more pronounced. Here, we emphasize that the present discussion is based on experimentally accessible transition enthalpy and entropy rather than Gibbs free energy differences because no dynamic equilibrium exists among these polymorphs. These thermodynamic tendencies are consistent with the experimentally observed temperature-dependent polymorphism, in which the preferential motif shifts from α to β and then to γ with increasing temperature.

To further elucidate the origin of this behavior, the lattice energies of each crystal phase were calculated based on the CE-B3LYP level of theory employed in CrystalExplorer. Notably, the magnitude of lattice energy slightly decreased in the order β > γ > α (45.4, 44.7, and 43.2 kcal mol−1, respectively), which contrasted with the trend of the molecular geometric distortion from α to β. Moreover, the intermolecular interaction energies between neighboring molecular pairs were evaluated by symmetry-adapted perturbation theory (SAPT) calculations using the Psi4 program package at SAPT0/jun-cc-pVDZ level of theory (Fig. S14–S17). The results revealed that the interaction energies between adjacent columns differed significantly among the three crystalline phases, while those within each column were very similar. Notably, the β-phase showed substantially larger dispersion and electrostatic stabilization between adjacent columns, leading to the stronger intercolumn interaction than those in the α- and γ-phases. These results suggest that the polymorphism of BSe3-bb is governed by a balance between intermolecular interactions and intramolecular strain. Specifically, the β-phase is stabilized by enhanced intercolumn interactions arising from its denser crystal packing, despite the increased intramolecular strain associated with the nearly planar molecular geometry.

Electrical conductive property

The change in intermolecular interactions induced by polymorphism leads to modulation of the solid-state properties.34–36 To elucidate how the structural variation affects charge-transport properties of borabuckybowls, we evaluated the electron coupling (V) between neighboring molecules, a key factor for the charge mobility, based on the obtained X-ray crystal structures using the Amsterdam Density Functional (ADF) program (Fig. 5 and S10–S12). In this context, electron couplings for HOMO–HOMO and LUMO–LUMO interactions are described as VHOMO and VLUMO, respectively. ADF calculations revealed that, within a single columnar structure, VHOMO values for β- and γ-phases (34.4 and 28.1 meV, respectively) were reduced compared to that for α-phase (44.4 and 46.9 meV), reflecting the increased π–π surface distance. In contrast, corresponding VLUMO values did not exhibit significant changes (55.5 and 55.3 meV for α-phase, 52.9 and 61.4 meV for β- and γ-phases, respectively) due to the large contribution of diffused p orbitals of selenium atoms in LUMO. Importantly, both VHOMO and VLUMO between adjacent columns were improved in the pitched π-stacking structure of β- and γ- phase compared to the α-phase, indicating the more isotropic charge transport. These computational results demonstrated that the anisotropic features of charge transport are strongly modulated by packing structures originating from the molecular curvatures and their potential utility as organic semiconducting materials.

Considering the stable multielectron redox behavior of BSe3-bb demonstrated in electrochemical measurements, the generation of charge carriers via oxidant doping was an efficient way to elucidate the electronic basis for charge transport in the solid state. Therefore, we experimentally assessed the charge-transport properties of this buckybowl using I2 doping. Specifically, BSe3-bb was mixed with I2 in dichloromethane, followed by slow evaporation of the solvent under air at room temperature, to afford dark red crystals, and the electrical resistivity of these crystals was measured by a two-probe method (for experimental details, see the SI). As a result, a significant electrical conductivity of approximately 10−3 S cm−1 was observed at room temperature (Fig. 6(a) and S24), and in addition, SEM-EDS analysis confirmed that iodine is incorporated into these dark red crystals (0.06 and 0.03 I atom for sample 1 and 2, respectively) (Fig. S25). From X-ray diffraction studies, the crystal structure of these dark red crystals was found to be similar to that of the undoped γ-phase shown above (Fig. S23). Thus, the observed conductivity originates from the transport of charge carriers (holes) generated by I2 doping through the π-stacking columnar structure of the γ-phase. These results demonstrate that selenium incorporation simultaneously provides enhanced redox accessibility and structural flexibility, thereby enabling both chemical doping and the formation of robust π-stacking crystals suitable for electrical measurements in the solid state, consistent with the theoretical calculations described above. Investigations of detailed structure–property relationships, such as the anisotropy of carrier transport are currently underway in our group.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we establish that a selenium-embedded borabuckybowl (BSe3-bb) enables precise control of molecular curvature and conformational adaptability, leading to crystallization-temperature-dependent polymorphism with distinct packing motifs. Thermodynamic analyses revealed that the formation of the α-, β-, and γ-phases is governed by a balance between enthalpic stabilization and entropic contributions arising from packing symmetry and structural complexity, while lattice energy calculations and SAPT calculations indicate that polymorphism originates from the formation of densely packed structures with enhanced intermolecular interactions between adjacent columns despite increased molecular distortion. Furthermore, electronic coupling calculations demonstrated that these packing motifs give rise to distinct charge-transport characteristics, and electrochemical studies revealed that selenium incorporation lowers the oxidation potential and enables efficient generation and stabilization of charge carriers. As a result, oxidative doping during crystallization enables experimental evaluation of electrical conductivity, highlighting the semiconducting potential of BSe3-bb in the solid state, while the molecular shape adaptability and resulting polymorphism allow the formation of robust π-stacking crystals even under doping conditions. These findings demonstrate that sulfur-to-selenium substitution simultaneously governs structural and electronic factors, providing a general design principle for coupling molecular curvature, polymorphism, and solid-state functionality in curved π-conjugated systems.
