Risk factors and prevalence of incontinence-associated dermatitis in elderly patients: a meta-analysis and systematic review
Authors: Mingqi Zhang, Siyue Fan, Hui Zheng, Hongzhan Jiang, Lianfang Cheng, Yicen Zheng, Yuxin Huang, Lichun Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThis systematic review aimed to evaluate the prevalence and risk factors of Incontinence-Associated Dermatitis (IAD) in elderly patients.MethodsA systematic search was conducted across 10 databases, including PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, CINAHL, Ovid MEDLINE, Scopus, VIP, WanFang, and CNKI, from inception to November 2023. Two reviewers independently performed study screening, data extraction, and risk-of-bias assessment. Prevalence meta-analysis was restricted to cross-sectional studies, whereas risk factor synthesis included all eligible observational studies, with priority given to multivariable-adjusted effect estimates. Meta-analysis was conducted using RevMan 5.3 and R 4.4.2, with random-effects models applied for all pooled analyses. This review was conducted in accordance with PRISMA 2020 and prospectively registered in PROSPERO (CRD42023442585).ResultsA total of 16 studies were included, involving 30,747 geriatric patients. For prevalence estimation (4 eligible cross-sectional studies, 18,619 incontinent geriatric patients), the pooled prevalence of IAD was 14.81% (95%CI: 4.16%-41.04%), with substantial heterogeneity (I²=98.4%, P < 0.001). For factor association analysis (11 case-control studies, 4 cross-sectional studies, 1 cohort study), 12 statistically significant factors associated with IAD were identified, including diabetes mellitus (adjusted OR = 2.17, 95%CI: 1.23–3.84), diarrhea (OR = 3.37, 95%CI: 2.38–4.78), incontinence frequency ≥ 3 times/day (OR = 4.70, 95%CI: 2.85–7.76), dual incontinence (OR = 4.03, 95%CI: 1.52–10.71); antibiotic use (OR = 3.26, 95%CI: 1.67–6.36); prolonged hospitalization (OR = 3.33, 95%CI: 2.60–4.26); presence of ≥ 3 comorbidities (OR = 2.53, 95%CI:1.48–4.32); mechanical ventilation (OR = 2.67, 95%CI: 1.20–5.93); and infection (OR = 2.93, 95%CI: 1.72–4.99) (all P<0.05). The certainty of evidence for primary outcomes ranged from very low to moderate per the GRADE framework.ConclusionsIAD is common among elderly patients, with a pooled prevalence of 14.81%. Individualized prevention and management strategies should be implemented according to the identified risk factors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12877-026-07724-z.

Introduction

Incontinence is a prevalent condition among the geriatric population and can markedly impair the integrity of the skin’s protective barrier, leading to prolonged exposure to irritants and an increased risk of inflammation and infection.Incontinence-Associated Dermatitis (IAD)—a common cutaneous disorder caused by urinary and/or fecal incontinence [1]—is clinically characterized by localized inflammation, with manifestations including erythema, maceration, and edema.Clinically, patients present with symptoms of pruritus, pain, and a burning sensation, which intensify significantly as skin integrity is breached and erosion occurs [2].

The reported prevalence of IAD varies considerably in the literature, with rates ranging from 5.6% to 50%, and recent data indicate a significant upward trend, particularly among the geriatric population [3, 4].Studies have shown that the overall prevalence of incontinence in adults aged 65 and older is estimated at 28.3%. Critically, within this incontinent geriatric population, the prevalence of IAD is substantial, with reported rates ranging from 35.4% to 47.7% [5, 6].This wide variation is likely attributable to heterogeneity in incontinence types and the burden of comorbidities.

Older adults are at increased risk of developing IAD because age-related physiological and structural changes impair skin barrier function and reduce resilience to prolonged moisture exposure [7]. This intrinsic vulnerability is often compounded by extrinsic factors such as impaired mobility and sustained pressure, which further increase the risk of skin breakdown [3]. IAD is associated with substantial pain, reduced quality of life, a higher risk of secondary infections, prolonged hospital stays, and increased healthcare costs. Notably, the daily care cost for incontinent patients can be considerable, whereas preventive skincare is relatively inexpensive, highlighting the potential cost-effectiveness of early prevention [8, 9]. Given these significant clinical and economic consequences, clarifying the prevalence of IAD and its risk factors in the geriatric population are of substantial importance.

The etiology of IAD in the geriatric population is multifactorial, encompassing patient characteristics, comorbidities, nutritional status, and therapeutic interventions. However, the evidence regarding specific risk factors is inconsistent. For instance, while some studies identify impaired consciousness as a significant risk factor [10, 11],others fail to corroborate this association [12].Similarly, although advanced age has been implicated as a risk factor [12–14],this correlation is not universally supported by all research [15, 16].Therefore, a comprehensive synthesis of the existing evidence is needed to provide a clearer understanding of the burden of IAD and its associated factors in the geriatric population.

Furthermore, while a meta-analysis on IAD risk factors in critically ill patients exists [17],its findings may not be fully generalizable to the broader geriatric population due to differences in geographical settings, demographic characteristics, and study periods. Another review focusing on older adults [18] did not pool IAD prevalence and was limited by the small number of included studies. In addition, clarifying how risk factors vary across different socioeconomic, cultural, and healthcare contexts may further support the development of context-specific prevention strategies.Therefore, the present study was undertaken to provide a comprehensive, evidence-based systematic review and meta-analysis of the prevalence of IAD and its associated risk factors specifically within the geriatric population.

This meta-analysis aimed to: (1) to determine the pooled prevalence of IAD in the geriatric population; (2) to identify and synthesize the significant risk factors associated with IAD in these patients; and (3) provide a robust evidence base to inform the development of effective nursing interventions for IAD prevention.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in strict accordance with the PRISMA [19],The study protocol has been published in BMJ Open [20],and prospectively registered with the PROSPERO(CRD 42023442585).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were structured according to the PECOS framework, with stratified eligibility for the two primary research questions to ensure methodological appropriateness:

For the research question on prevalence estimation: (1)Population (P): Geriatric patients aged ≥ 60 years with urinary and/or fecal incontinence; (2)Outcomes (O): Studies were required to report IAD prevalence with a clearly defined numerator (number of IAD cases) and denominator (total number of incontinent geriatric patients); (3)Study Design (S): Cross-sectional studies only .

For the research question on identifying factors associated with IAD: (1) Population (P): Geriatric patients aged ≥ 60 years, including both patients with IAD and those without IAD; (2) Exposures (E): Potential factors associated with IAD occurrence; (3) Comparators (C): Participants without the exposure of interest or, in case-control studies, participants without IAD; (4) Outcome (O): Association between exposure and IAD, reported with odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs), including both crude and multivariable-adjusted effect sizes; (5) Study design (S): Observational studies including cohort, case-control, and cross-sectional designs.

Exclusion criteria

The exclusion criteria were as follows:Case reports, reviews, editorials, duplicate publications, and articles for which the full text was unobtainable.Studies with incomplete or insufficient data for extraction.Articles deemed to be of low methodological quality (defined as a NOS score of < 4 or an Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality AHRQ score of 0–3).Publications in languages other than English or Chinese.

Search strategy

A systematic literature search was conducted in international databases, including PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, the Cochrane Library, the Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL), Ovid MEDLINE, and Scopus, as well as in Chinese databases, including the Chinese Periodical Full-text Database (VIP), Chinese WanFang Database, and the China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI).The search strategy combined keywords and subject headings for two core concepts: (1) Incontinence-Associated Dermatitis and (2) risk factors. A representative English search string was: (“Risk Factors” OR “risk factor*” OR “influencing factor*”) AND (“Diaper Rash” OR “incontinence-associated dermatitis” OR “incontinence associated skin damage”).The specific search strategy was adjusted according to different databases(Supplementary Materials 1).

Literature screening and data extraction

Two investigators independently screened all retrieved articles against the prespecified eligibility criteria. Inter-rater agreement for study eligibility was assessed using Cohen’s kappa coefficient, with kappa ≥ 0.8 defined as good agreement.Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus, with a third investigator consulted for arbitration if an agreement could not be reached.The selection involved a two-stage process: after duplicate removal via EndNote X9, titles and abstracts were screened for eligibility, followed by full-text assessment of potentially relevant records.

Data from all included studies were independently extracted by both investigators using a standardized data collection form. Inter-rater reliability for data extraction was assessed via a 10% random sample of included studies, with discrepancies resolved by consensus.

The extracted data included: (1) Basic study information: first author, publication year, country, study design, study setting, sample size; (2) Population characteristics: age, gender, incontinence type, denominator definition for prevalence calculation; (3) IAD assessment: diagnostic criteria, validated assessment tool used; (4) Prevalence data: numerator (IAD cases), denominator (total incontinent geriatric patients), and 95%CI if reported; (5) Factor association data: crude and multivariable-adjusted ORs with 95%CIs, full list of covariates adjusted in multivariable models, case/control counts for each exposure; (6) Study quality assessment items.

For studies with missing or incomplete data, we contacted corresponding authors via email to request original data. If no response was received within 2 weeks, the study was included only for qualitative synthesis, and the impact of missing data was addressed in sensitivity analysis and the Discussion section.

Quality assessment

The methodological quality and risk of bias (ROB) of each included study were independently assessed by two trained investigators, with inter-rater agreement assessed via Cohen’s kappa coefficient. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion with a third investigator to reach consensus. Three sets of validated assessment tools were used, targeted to the two research questions and study designs.

ROB assessment for IAD prevalence estimation

For cross-sectional studies included in the prevalence meta-analysis, we used the 10-item JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist for Studies Reporting Prevalence Data, which specifically evaluates the internal and external validity of prevalence estimates. Each item was rated as “Yes”, “No”, or “Unclear”, and studies were categorized as low, moderate, or high ROB based on the number of “Yes” responses.

ROB assessment for IAD associated factor analysis

For studies included in the factor synthesis, ROB was primarily assessed using the Quality in Prognosis Studies (QUIPS) tool, which evaluates six domains: study participation, study attrition, prognostic factor measurement, outcome measurement, confounding adjustment, and statistical analysis. Each domain was rated as low, moderate, or high ROB. As supplementary assessments, the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) was applied to cohort and case-control studies, and the 11-item Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) checklist was used for cross-sectional studies. NOS scores of ≥ 7, 4–6, and < 4 indicated high, moderate, and low quality, respectively [21], while AHRQ scores of ≥ 8, 4–7, and < 4 “Yes” responses indicated high, moderate, and low quality, respectively [22].

Certainty of evidence assessment

The certainty of evidence for all primary outcomes (pooled prevalence, all statistically significant associated factors) was evaluated using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) framework. For each outcome, we rated the certainty of evidence as high, moderate, low, or very low, based on 5 domains: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. The GRADE evidence profile and summary of findings (SoF) table are provided in Supplementary Material 2.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using RevMan 5.3and R 4.4.2. A two-tailed P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

Prevalence meta-analysis

For cross-sectional studies with eligible denominator definition (total incontinent geriatric patients), the pooled prevalence of IAD was calculated with 95%CIs, using the Freeman-Tukey double arcsine transformation to stabilize the variance of prevalence data. A Random-effects model (DerSimonian-Laird method) was used as the primary approach, to account for inherent heterogeneity across studies.

Heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic and Cochrane’s Q test, with I² ≥50% or P ≤ 0.1 defined as substantial heterogeneity.Pre-specified subgroup analyses were performed to explore sources of heterogeneity, including: study setting (hospital/nursing home), incontinence type (dual/urinary/fecal), country. Sensitivity analysis was performed via the leave-one-out method to evaluate the robustness of the pooled prevalence.Publication bias was not formally assessed for the prevalence meta-analysis because only four studies were included, and both funnel plot inspection and Egger’s regression test are considered unreliable when the number of studies is small.

Associated factor meta-analysis

For the synthesis of factors associated with IAD, multivariable-adjusted ORs with 95%CIs were prioritized for pooling; crude ORs were pooled only when adjusted effect sizes were unavailable, with explicit statement in the results. For each factor, data were pooled only if reported in ≥ 3 studies.Random-effects model was used as the primary approach for all pooled analyses, regardless of I² value, to account for inherent heterogeneity in observational study designs.

Heterogeneity was assessed via I² statistic and Cochrane’s Q test, with pre-specified subgroup analyses (study design, adjustment for core covariates (age/gender/incontinence type), study setting) and meta-regression to explore sources of heterogeneity.Sensitivity analysis was performed via: (1) leave-one-out method; (2) switching between fixed and random-effects models; (3) excluding studies with high ROB, to evaluate the stability of pooled effect sizes.Publication bias was assessed via Egger’s test for factors reported in ≥ 10 studies.

Qualitative synthesis

For data that could not be quantitatively pooled (e.g., factors reported in < 3 studies, extreme unexplained heterogeneity), a narrative synthesis was performed, with explicit statement of the reason for not pooling.

Protocol deviations

This review was conducted with reference to the pre-published protocol [20] and PROSPERO registration. Three major deviations from the original protocol were made. First, prevalence meta-analysis was restricted to cross-sectional studies only to improve methodological alignment with prevalence estimation. Second, two targeted ROB assessment tools (JBI checklist for prevalence studies, QUIPS tool for factor association studies) were added to complement the NOS and AHRQ tools and to allow targeted risk-of-bias assessment for the two research questions. Third, random-effects models were used as the primary approach for all meta-analyses instead of the original I²-based model selection strategy, to better account for heterogeneity across observational studies. These deviations were determined before data extraction and analysis, and their rationale and implications are described in the Discussion section.

Result

Study selection

The initial database search identified 6,069 records, of which 4,156 proceeded to screening after the removal of 1,913 duplicates.Following title and abstract screening, 3,900 articles were excluded, and the remaining 256 proceeded to full-text review. After this detailed assessment against the eligibility criteria, 244 articles were excluded for reasons including: ineligible population (n = 116), inappropriate study design (n = 112), abstract-only publication (n = 8), insufficient data (n = 6), and irrelevant outcomes (n = 2). An additional four studies meeting the eligibility criteria were identified through manual searching of reference lists. This resulted in a final cohort of 16 studies included in the qualitative synthesis and meta-analysis. The entire study selection process is detailed in the PRISMA flowchart (Fig. 1).

Study characteristic

A total of 16 studies published between 2015 and 2023 were included, with a cumulative sample of 30,747 geriatric patients (2,887 IAD cases).

For prevalence estimation: 4 cross-sectional studies [13, 15, 23, 24] were eligible, involving 18,619 incontinent geriatric patients, all with a clear denominator of total incontinent population.

For factor association analysis: All 16 studies were eligible, including 11 case-control studies [10–12, 14, 16, 25–28], 4 cross-sectional studies [13, 15, 23, 24], and 1 prospective cohort study [29]. Sample sizes ranged from 36 to 14,675 participants. Geographically, 11 studies were conducted in China, 2 in Brazil, 1 in the United States, 1 in Germany, and 1 in Belgium. Literature quality assessment

ROB for prevalence estimation

All 4 cross-sectional studies [13, 15, 23, 24] included in the prevalence meta-analysis were rated as moderate to high quality via the JBI checklist, with 2 studies rated as low ROB and 2 as moderate ROB. No studies were rated as high ROB. Detailed ROB assessment for each study is presented in Supplementary Table 1.

ROB for associated factor analysis

Using the QUIPS tool, 4 studies [10, 16, 28, 29] were rated as having low overall ROB, 7 studies [11, 14, 25–27, 30, 31] as moderate ROB and 1 study [12] as high ROB. The most common high-risk domains were confounding adjustment, with 7 studies [11, 14, 25–27, 30, 31] failing to adjust for key covariates such as age, sex, and incontinence type, and statistical analysis, with 2 studies [12, 31] showing inappropriate sample size or analytical methods. Among the 11 case-control studies [10–12, 14, 16, 25–28, 30, 31] and 1 cohort study [29] assessed using the NOS, scores ranged from 5 to 8 stars, with 4 studies [10, 16, 28, 29] rated as high quality and 8 studies [11, 12, 14, 25–27, 30, 31] as moderate quality. For 4 cross-sectional studies [13, 15, 23, 24] assessed using the AHRQ checklist, scores ranged from 7 to 8, indicating moderate to high quality. Quality appraisal details for each study are shown in Table 1, with full ROB assessment results provided in Supplementary Table 2.

Prevalence of IAD

Based on 4 eligible cross-sectional studies (18,619 incontinent geriatric patients), the pooled prevalence of IAD was 14.81% (95%CI: 4.16%-41.04%), with substantial heterogeneity (I²=98.4%, P < 0.001). The forest plot of pooled prevalence is presented in Fig. 2.

Subgroup analysis

Meta-analysis of risk factors

Patient-related factors

Diabetes mellitus: 9 studies [10–12, 16, 24–26, 28, 30] reported adjusted ORs, pooled analysis showed diabetes mellitus was significantly associated with increased IAD risk (adjusted OR = 2.17, 95%CI: 1.23–3.84, P = 0.013), with substantial heterogeneity (I²=72.6%).

Impaired consciousness: 3 studies [11, 15, 31] reported adjusted ORs, pooled analysis showed a significant association (adjusted OR = 2.79, 95%CI: 1.77–4.40, P < 0.001), with no heterogeneity (I²=0.0%).

Presence of ≥ 3 comorbidities: 2 studies [15, 31] reported adjusted ORs, pooled analysis showed a significant association (adjusted OR = 2.53, 95%CI: 1.48–4.32, P < 0.001), with no heterogeneity (I²=0.0%).

Incontinence-related factors

Diarrhea: 6 studies [10, 12, 15, 16, 25, 31]reported adjusted ORs, pooled analysis showed a significant association (adjusted OR = 3.37, 95%CI: 2.38–4.78, P < 0.001), with no heterogeneity (I²=0.0%).

Incontinence frequency ≥ 3 times/day: 3 studies [25, 28, 31] reported adjusted ORs, pooled analysis showed a significant association (adjusted OR = 4.70, 95%CI: 2.85–7.76, P < 0.001), with no heterogeneity (I²=0.0%).

Dual incontinence (urinary and fecal): 8 studies [10, 15, 23–25, 28, 30, 31] reported adjusted ORs, pooled analysis showed a significant association (adjusted OR = 4.03, 95%CI: 1.52–10.71, P = 0.017), with substantial heterogeneity (I²=96.9%).

Treatment-related factors

Administration of ≥ 3 antibiotics: 4 studies [10, 12, 16, 25]reported adjusted ORs, pooled analysis showed a significant association (adjusted OR = 2.32, 95%CI: 1.82–2.97, P < 0.001), with moderate heterogeneity (I²=53.0%).

Antibiotic use: 2 studies [25, 30] reported crude ORs, pooled analysis showed a significant association (crude OR = 3.26, 95%CI: 1.67–6.36, P < 0.001), with no heterogeneity (I²=0.0%).

Prolonged hospitalization: 3 studies [13, 14, 23] reported adjusted ORs, pooled analysis showed a significant association (adjusted OR = 3.33, 95%CI: 2.60–4.26, P < 0.001), with low heterogeneity (I²=37.4%).

Mechanical ventilation: 2 studies [15, 26] reported crude ORs, pooled analysis showed a significant association (crude OR = 2.67, 95%CI: 1.20–5.93, P = 0.012), with no heterogeneity (I²=0.0%).

Infection: 2 studies [15, 31] reported crude ORs, pooled analysis showed a significant association (crude OR = 2.93, 95%CI: 1.72–4.99, P < 0.001), with low heterogeneity (I²=31.5%).

Protective factors

Higher Barthel Index score : 3 studies reported adjusted ORs, pooled analysis showed it was a significant protective factor (adjusted OR = 0.06, 95%CI: 0.02–0.14, P < 0.001), with low heterogeneity (I²=15.5%).

BMI ≥ 24: 4 studies reported adjusted ORs, pooled analysis showed it was a significant protective factor (adjusted OR = 0.49, 95%CI: 0.32–0.74, P < 0.001), with low heterogeneity (I²=31.8%).

Factors with no significant association

No statistically significant association was found for age, hypoalbuminemia, enteral nutrition, bowel cleansing, moisture exposure ≥ 3 times/day, gender, or watery stool (all P > 0.05). Detailed results are presented in Table 3.

Heterogeneity exploration

For factors with substantial heterogeneity (I²≥50%), pre-specified subgroup analyses showed that study design and adjustment for core covariates (age, gender, incontinence type) were the primary sources of heterogeneity. Meta-regression confirmed that adjustment for incontinence type significantly reduced heterogeneity for dual incontinence (P = 0.002).

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses (leave-one-out method, model switching, excluding high ROB studies) confirmed the robustness of the pooled effect sizes for all statistically significant factors, except for age, watery stool, and moisture exposure ≥ 3 times/day, which showed unstable results after sensitivity analysis. Detailed sensitivity analysis results are presented in Table 4.

Narrative synthesis

For factors reported in < 3 studies, narrative synthesis showed that lower Braden Scale score, higher NRS 2002 score, higher PAT score, MMSE ≤ 13, pressure injury, xerosis, and anemia were potentially associated with increased IAD risk, while use of skin protectants was a potential protective factor. Certainty of evidence assessment

Per the GRADE framework, the certainty of evidence for the pooled IAD prevalence was rated as very low, downgraded due to high ROB, substantial heterogeneity, and imprecision. For the 12 statistically significant associated factors, the certainty of evidence was rated as moderate for 2 factors (diarrhea, incontinence frequency ≥ 3 times/day), low for 7 factors, and very low for 3 factors. Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis is to specifically evaluate the prevalence of IAD in geriatric incontinent patients, with strict adherence to epidemiological principles for study design matching, targeted risk of bias assessment, and PRISMA 2020 reporting guidelines. We included 16 observational studies involving 30,747 geriatric patients, and found that the pooled prevalence of IAD was 14.81% in geriatric incontinent patients, with 12 factors significantly associated with IAD occurrence. The certainty of evidence ranged from very low to moderate per the GRADE framework.

Prevalence of IAD in elderly patients

In this meta-analysis, the pooled prevalence of IAD was 14.81% (95%CI: 4.16%-41.04%) in geriatric incontinent patients, based on 4 high-quality cross-sectional studies with a standardized denominator of total incontinent population. This finding is consistent with previous international studies, which reported IAD prevalence ranging from 5.6% to 50% in geriatric populations.

Subgroup analyses indicated that IAD prevalence was higher in nursing home settings (32.54%) than in hospital settings (11.03%), which may be attributed to the higher proportion of patients with severe functional impairment, longer duration of incontinence, and less frequent nursing care in nursing homes. Patients with dual incontinence also showed a higher prevalence of IAD than those with single-type incontinence, which is biologically plausible given that combined exposure to urine and feces may result in greater skin barrier damage. In addition, country-based subgroup analysis suggested a lower pooled prevalence in studies from China (12.35%) than in those from Brazil and Germany (19.57%), although further comparisons were limited by the small number of studies from countries. This heterogeneity may be associated with differences in patient characteristics, study settings, and contextual factors such as regional economic conditions and nursing care standards [32].

The substantial heterogeneity in prevalence estimates (I²=98.4%) may be partly attributable to differences in study setting, incontinence type, and country across studies, as suggested by the subgroup analyses. In addition, the lack of an internationally recognized gold standard for IAD assessment may represent another important source of heterogeneity. The included studies applied different evaluation tools, such as the IADS and the Braden Scale, and incomplete reporting in the primary studies precluded further subgroup analysis according to assessment instrument. These methodological differences may have contributed to the wide variation in reported prevalence estimates. Future studies should prioritize the development and validation of standardized IAD assessment methods to improve inter-study comparability and support more accurate clinical evaluation.

Risk factors for IAD in elderly patients

The present analysis corroborates diabetes mellitus as a major risk factor for IAD among geriatric patients. The underlying susceptibility arises from physiological alterations linked to chronic hyperglycemia. Specifically, elevated glucose concentrations compromise keratinocyte proliferation, differentiation, and migration, resulting in epidermal thinning, impaired wound healing, and increased cutaneous exposure to irritants such as urine and feces [33]. In addition, diabetes reduces the abundance of acidic natural moisturizing factors, elevates skin pH, and disrupts the resident microbiome [33, 34]. Reduced collagen synthesis further diminishes skin elasticity and resilience in older diabetic individuals, amplifying IAD risk [35]. These findings highlight the need for intensified clinical vigilance and optimized glycemic management in incontinent patients with diabetes, as effective glucose control may mitigate cutaneous fragility and decrease the incidence of IAD [36].

Multiple comorbidities, including diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and neurological disorders, were associated with a markedly increased risk of IAD in geriatric patients. Several mechanisms may explain this association. Chronic conditions may contribute to hypoalbuminemia, impaired immune function, poor peripheral circulation, and delayed skin repair, thereby increasing susceptibility to skin damage and infection [37, 38]. Neurological disorders further exacerbate the risk by contributing to incontinence and impairing sensory perception, thereby limiting the ability to detect early skin damage, while reduced mobility prolongs exposure to irritants such as urine and feces [39]. In addition, polypharmacy, common in patients with multiple comorbidities, may weaken skin barrier function [40]. These findings support the need for structured skin care, including pH-balanced cleansing, gentle drying, and regular use of skin protectants.

Our analysis confirmed that diarrhea significantly increases the risk of IAD, particularly among patients with frequent fecal incontinence, a finding consistent with previous research by Kottner et al. [41]. The underlying mechanism is multifactorial. Prolonged skin exposure to excessive moisture is one component, but diarrheal stool also contains high concentrations of digestive enzymes that degrade lipids and proteins, thereby disrupting the skin’s natural protective barrier and increasing its vulnerability to irritation and inflammation [39]. These results underscore the importance of targeted preventive strategies in clinical practice. These findings highlight the importance of early identification and targeted preventive care, including close stool monitoring, nutritional support, and appropriate management of enteral feeding to reduce diarrheal episodes and subsequent IAD risk.

Comparable to the effects of diarrhea, infection substantially increases the risk of IAD by undermining skin barrier integrity and amplifying local inflammatory responses. Infection triggers the release of inflammatory mediators that damage the epidermal barrier and promote a cycle of inflammation and tissue injury [42]. Clinically, this process presents with symptoms such as erythema, pain, and pruritus, accompanied by progressive deterioration of the stratum corneum, the skin’s primary defensive layer [43]. In the perineal area of incontinent individuals, overgrowth of cutaneous bacteria or fungi can further compromise this weakened barrier, increasing epidermal permeability and heightening vulnerability to irritants contained in urine and feces. In addition, infection delays tissue repair and prolong healing, thereby worsening IAD severity; in advanced cases, it may even lead to ulceration [44].

Impaired consciousness has been reported as a potential risk factor for IAD. A previous study demonstrated a positive correlation between the severity of consciousness impairment and IAD incidence [45], and our meta-analysis further supports this association in the geriatric population. The underlying mechanisms may be multifactorial: patients with reduced consciousness frequently experience challenges in self-care, leading to inadequate nutritional intake, malnutrition, and compromised skin hygiene [46]. These conditions weaken the skin’s natural barrier defenses, thereby increasing susceptibility to irritation and breakdown [3, 47]. Given this heightened vulnerability, patients with impaired consciousness should be prioritized for targeted IAD prevention. Preventive care should include optimization of nutritional support, meticulous perineal cleansing, regular skin assessment, and individualized care planning to maintain skin integrity and reduce IAD risk.

Therapeutic interventions for various comorbidities may also increase the risk of IAD, with antibiotic administration being a prominent example. The use of antibiotics has been associated with an elevated risk of IAD in older adults, likely due to disruption of normal intestinal function and the consequent increase in diarrhea, which prolongs skin exposure to feces. The administration of multiple antibiotics, particularly broad-spectrum agents, can exacerbate this effect by disturbing the gut microbiome. Such dysbiosis may also influence the cutaneous microbiome, thereby compromising skin barrier function and further increasing susceptibility to IAD [48]. Therefore, older adults receiving antibiotic therapy should be considered a high-risk population. In clinical practice, this may involve implementing enhanced skin care protocols and conducting regular assessments of skin integrity to facilitate early detection and timely management.

Patients requiring mechanical ventilation may have an increased risk of IAD due to their specific clinical circumstances. These individuals are usually immobilized and confined to bed for prolonged periods, and their impaired respiratory function often precludes independent repositioning. Such immobility can result in sustained, localized pressure on the skin. In addition, the frequent use of sedatives or paralytic agents in this population may further impair sensory perception and contribute to cognitive or motor deficits. This reduced awareness limits the ability to detect or respond to early signs of skin irritation or injury, which in turn is likely to increase the risk of IAD [49].

The duration of hospitalization appears to influence the risk of developing IAD, with evidence indicating that prolonged hospital stays are associated with an increased risk in older adults [14]. Although the precise mechanisms remain unclear, potential contributors include reduced mobility, more frequent exposure to moisture from incontinence episodes, and increased use of therapeutic devices such as urinary catheters, nasogastric tubes, and mechanical ventilation [13].

Our analysis showed that dual incontinence is a major risk factor for IAD, increasing its likelihood by 4.03-fold, consistent with previous findings [44]. Combined exposure to urine and feces may lead to prolonged skin maceration, while fecal digestive enzymes further disrupt the stratum corneum [30].In older adults, the age-related thinning of the epidermis and reduced barrier function further heighten susceptibility to inflammation and erosion when exposed to such conditions [31]. These findings support the need for structured bowel and bladder management, including regular assessment of incontinence patterns, use of high-absorbency products, and timely changes to reduce skin exposure to irritants and lower IAD risk.

Incontinence frequency is an important determinant of IAD risk. Our meta-analysis showed that older adults with three or more incontinence episodes per day had a 4.70-fold higher risk of IAD than those with lower frequency. Repeated incontinence prolongs skin exposure to moisture, compromises barrier integrity, and increases susceptibility to IAD [50]. To mitigate this risk, healthcare institutions should implement structured skin care protocols, particularly interventions that reduce skin contact time with urine and feces, to help lower IAD incidence [51].

Notably, most studies included in the factor analysis were case-control or cross-sectional in design, which support correlation rather than causal inference between the identified factors and IAD. In addition, the certainty of evidence for most associated factors was low to very low according to the GRADE framework, mainly because of observational study design, high ROB, and substantial heterogeneity. Therefore, these findings should be applied in clinical practice with caution. Future high-quality prospective cohort studies and targeted intervention studies are needed to clarify causal relationships and determine whether modifying these factors can effectively prevent IAD.

Limitations

This review has several limitations that should be acknowledged.First, the literature search was restricted to articles published in English and Chinese and did not include grey literature, which may have introduced language and publication bias. Second, the number of studies eligible for prevalence estimation was limited, and substantial heterogeneity existed across studies, partly due to variation in sample size and study characteristics.Third, the diagnostic criteria, assessment tools, and classification approaches for IAD were not standardized across studies, and some studies did not clearly report the diagnostic process, which may have introduced misclassification bias and affected the comparability of prevalence estimates. Finally, adjusted covariates varied across studies, and many studies did not report fully adjusted effect estimates, so residual confounding cannot be excluded.

Conclusions

In clinical practice, nurses should accurately identify high-risk older adults and implement standardized, comprehensive, and early interventions to reduce the incidence of IAD. Future research should focus on high-quality, large-sample prospective studies to further clarify IAD risk factors and provide more robust and generalizable evidence. The findings of this study offer an important evidence base for developing and validating a clinical prediction model for IAD in older adults.
