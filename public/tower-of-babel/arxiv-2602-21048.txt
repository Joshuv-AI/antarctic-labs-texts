t t -Hermitian Forms of Arbitrary Degree, Their Spectral Structure, and Positivity
Isaac Dobes

Abstract
We introduce t t -Hermitian forms of arbitrary degree k k , a natural extension of classical degree k k Hermitian forms obtained through a synthesis of the tensor transformation law and the t t -product of third-order tensors. We show that degree k k t t -Hermitian forms uniquely correspond to order 2 ​ k + 1 2k+1 Hermitian tensors arising as canonical representatives within the ∗ -algebra of Hermitian tensors equipped with the t t -product/ t t -Einstein product. Applying the discrete Fourier transform, their corresponding t t -Hermitian forms decompose into collections of classical degree k k Hermitian forms. This decomposition yields a universal lifting property, allowing arbitrary collections of degree k k Hermitian forms to be viewed as a single structured object which preserves fundamental properties such as Hermitian positive-definiteness. For a distinguished class of t t -Hermitian forms, which we refer to as commutant t t -Hermitian forms, we establish a spectral theory extending the classical spectral characterization of Hermitian tensors. In particular, we relate Hermitian positive-definiteness to two distinct classes of eigenvalues: the tensor eigenvalues of each frontal slice of the form’s corresponding tensor, and matrix eigenvalues obtained after unfolding each of these slices into matrices. Indeed, we prove that the tensor eigenvalues characterize Hermitian positive-definiteness; moreover, positivity of the matrix eigenvalues implies Hermitian positive-definiteness, however the converse fails in general. Together, these results provide a unified framework for structured interactions among higher-degree Hermitian forms and lay the groundwork for further study, especially with regards to the consequences of the spectral hierarchy of positivity inherent in this setting.

Introduction

The
t
-product, a relatively new multiplicative operation on third order tensors, was first developed by M.E. Kilmer, C.D. Martin, and L. Perrone in
[
10
, 2000]
. It’s properties were then soon further investigated in
[
1
, 2009]
,
[
9
, 2011]
,
[
8
, 2013]
and numerous other papers in the following years. The tensor transformation law applied to hypermatrices (which can be thought of as tensors in a fixed basis) consists of a product between a hypermatrix
\mathcal{A}
and a tuple of matrices
(X_{1},...,X_{d})
, which L.H. Lim refers to as multilinear matrix multiplication
[
12
]
. With these two operations, a rich structure emerges on sets/spaces of hypermatrices. In this paper, we develop and generalize a synthesis of the
t
-product with multilinear matrix multiplication, which we refer to as
t
-multilinear hypermatrix multiplication.

In particular, after reviewing the notion of tensor unfolding
[
2
]
and the theory of Hermitian forms (which in a fixed basis uniquely correspond to Hermitian partially symmetric hypermatrices), we apply
t
-multilinear hypermatrix multiplication to such (in general higher order) hypermatrices and third order hypermatrices of indeterminates referred to as tubal vectors. We then demonstrate that in the frequency domain, after applying the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) along the tubal mode of these hypermatrices, such a product yields a collection of Hermitian forms. We then prove that the result of this product, which we refer to as a
t
-Hermitian form, satisfies a natural lifting property, enabling collections of Hermitian forms to be encoded in a single structured object.

With this framework in mind, we introduce the notion of Joint MTU Diagonalizability, which generalizes the matricized eigendecomposition of Hermitian hypermatrices (which generalizes the the eigendecomposition of Hermitian matrices, see
[
15
, Theorem 5.2]
) to a distinguished class of hypermatrices. For hypermatrices which are joint MTU diagonalizable, we prove that they posses a generalized spectral decomposition, and when applied to their corresponding
t
-Hermitian forms we obtain a sufficient condition for positivity. Interestingly, in our investigation we discover that there is a spectral hierarchy of positivity, with tensor eigenvalues characterizing positivity, and matrix eigenvalues (obtain via tensor unfoldings in the joint MTU diagonalization) implying positivity, but not conversely. Thus, assuming positivity of the matrix eigenvalues is a stronger assumption than assuming positivity of the the tensor eigenvalues. After considering the computational implications of this, we conclude the paper and briefly discuss potential avenues for further study of this framework.

Definition 2.1
.

Let

\displaystyle M^{\mathbf{m}\times\mathbf{n}}:\mathbb{C}^{m_{1}\times...\times m_{M}\times n_{1}\times...\times n_{N}}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}^{|\mathbf{m}|\times|\mathbf{n}|}

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle\mapsto M_{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathbf{m}\times\mathbf{n}}

Lemma 2.3
.

Suppose
\mathcal{A}=u_{1}\circ...\circ u_{M}\circ v_{1}\circ...\circ v_{N}\in\mathbb{C}^{m_{1}\times...\times m_{M}\times n_{1}\times...\times n_{N}}
. Then

M_{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathbf{m}\times\mathbf{n}}=\mathrm{hvec}(\mathcal{U})\mathrm{hvec}(\mathcal{V})^{T}

with
\mathcal{U}:=u_{1}\circ...\circ u_{M}
and
\mathcal{V}:=v_{1}\circ...\circ v_{N}
.

Proof.

By definition of the outer product, for any
\mathbf{i}=(i_{1},...,i_{M})\in[m_{1}]\times...\times[m_{M}]
and
\mathbf{j}=(j_{1},...,j_{N})\in[n_{1}]\times...\times[n_{N}]

\left(M_{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathbf{m}\times\mathbf{n}}\right)_{\psi(\mathbf{i},\mathbf{m}),\psi(\mathbf{j},\mathbf{n})}=\mathcal{A}_{\mathbf{i}\mathbf{j}}=\prod\limits_{l=1}^{M}(u_{l})_{i_{l}}\prod\limits_{k=1}^{N}(v_{k})_{j_{k}}.

On the other hand, it also follows by the definition of the outer product that

\mathrm{hvec}(\mathcal{U})=\sum\limits_{i_{1},...,i_{M}}^{m_{1},...,m_{M}}\prod\limits_{l=1}^{M}(u_{l})_{i_{l}}\underbrace{e_{i_{M}}\otimes...\otimes e_{i_{1}}}_{e_{\psi(\mathbf{i},\mathbf{m})}\in\mathbb{C}^{|\mathbf{m}|}}

and

\mathrm{hvec}(\mathcal{V})=\sum\limits_{j_{1},...,j_{N}}^{n_{1},...,n_{N}}\prod\limits_{k=1}^{N}(v_{k})_{j_{k}}\underbrace{e_{j_{N}}\otimes...\otimes e_{j_{1}}}_{e_{\psi(\mathbf{j},\mathbf{n})}\in\mathbb{C}^{|\mathbf{n}|}}.

Therefore, for any
\mathbf{i}=(i_{1},...,i_{M})\in[m_{1}]\times...\times[m_{M}]
and
\mathbf{j}=(j_{1},...,j_{N})\in[n_{1}]\times...\times[n_{N}]
, the
\big(\psi(\mathbf{i}),\psi(\mathbf{j})\big)
-entry of the
|\mathbf{m}|\times|\mathbf{n}|

\mathrm{hvec}(\mathcal{U})\mathrm{hvec}(\mathcal{V})^{T}
is precisely given by

\left(\mathrm{hvec}(\mathcal{U})\mathrm{hvec}(\mathcal{V})^{T}\right)_{\psi(\mathbf{i},\mathbf{m}),\psi(\mathbf{j},\mathbf{n})}=\prod\limits_{l=1}^{M}(u_{l})_{i_{l}}\prod\limits_{k=1}^{N}(v_{k})_{j_{k}}=\left(M_{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathbf{m}\times\mathbf{n}}\right)_{\psi(\mathbf{i},\mathbf{m}),\psi(\mathbf{j},\mathbf{n})},

proving that
M_{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathbf{m}\times\mathbf{n}}=\mathrm{hvec}(\mathcal{U})\mathrm{hvec}(\mathcal{V})^{T}
.
∎

Corollary 2.4
.

Suppose
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{U}\circ\mathcal{V}\in\mathbb{C}^{m_{1}\times...\times m_{M}\times n_{1}\times...\times n_{N}}
, with
\mathcal{U}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}u_{1}^{i}\circ...\circ u_{M}^{i}
and
\mathcal{V}=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{s}\mu_{j}v_{1}^{j}\circ...\circ v_{N}^{j}
. Then

M_{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathbf{m}\times\mathbf{n}}=\mathrm{hvec}(\mathcal{U})\mathrm{hvec}(\mathcal{V})^{T}.

Proof.

By linearity of
M^{\mathbf{m}\times\mathbf{n}}
and Lemma
2.3
, we have that

M_{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathbf{m}\times\mathbf{n}}=\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{r,s}\lambda_{i}\mu_{j}\mathrm{hvec}(u_{1}^{i}\circ...\circ u_{M}^{i})\mathrm{hvec}(v_{1}^{j}\circ...\circ v_{N}^{j})^{T},

and by linearity of
\mathrm{hvec}
we have that

M_{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathbf{m}\times\mathbf{n}}=\mathrm{hvec}\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}u_{1}^{i}\circ...\circ u_{M}^{i}\right)\mathrm{hvec}\left(\sum\limits_{j=1}^{s}\mu_{j}v_{1}^{j}\circ...\circ v_{N}^{j}\right)^{T}=\mathrm{hvec}(\mathcal{U})\mathrm{hvec}(\mathcal{V})^{T}.

∎

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{m_{1}\times...\times m_{M}\times n_{1}\times...\times n_{N}}
. Then
M_{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathbf{m}\times\mathbf{n}}
has singular value decomposition

M_{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathbf{m}\times\mathbf{n}}=\sum\limits_{\alpha=1}^{r}\sigma_{\alpha}\phi_{\alpha}\varphi_{\alpha}^{\dagger}

if and only if

\mathcal{A}=\sum\limits_{\alpha=1}^{r}\sigma_{\alpha}\mathcal{U}_{\alpha}\circ\overline{\mathcal{V}_{\alpha}}

where the
\mathcal{U}_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{C}^{m_{1}\times...\times m_{M}}
are such that
\{\mathrm{hvec}(\mathcal{U}_{\alpha})\}_{\alpha=1}^{r}
forms an orthonormal set, and the
\mathcal{V}_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{1}\times...\times n_{N}}
are such that
\{\mathrm{hvec}(\mathcal{V}_{\alpha})\}_{\alpha=1}^{r}
forms an orthonormal set.

Proof.

Suppose
M_{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathbf{m}\times\mathbf{n}}
has singular value decomposition as given in the proposition statement, and let
\mathcal{U}_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{C}^{m_{1}\times...\times m_{M}}
be the unique hypermatrix such that
\mathrm{hvec}(\mathcal{U}_{\alpha})=\phi_{\alpha}
and
\mathcal{V}_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{1}\times...\times n_{N}}
be the unique hypermatrix such that
\mathrm{hvec}(\mathcal{V}_{\alpha})=\varphi_{\alpha}
for each
\alpha=1,...,r
. Then,

\displaystyle M_{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathbf{m}\times\mathbf{n}}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\alpha=1}^{r}\sigma_{\alpha}\phi_{\alpha}\varphi_{\alpha}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\alpha=1}^{r}\sigma_{\alpha}\mathrm{hvec}(\mathcal{U}_{\alpha})\mathrm{hvec}(\mathcal{V}_{\alpha})^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\alpha=1}^{r}\sigma_{\alpha}M_{\mathcal{U}_{\alpha}\circ\overline{\mathcal{V}_{\alpha}}}^{\mathbf{m}\times\mathbf{n}},\quad\text{by Corollary \ref{Matricization of Arbitrary Product}.}

Since
M^{\mathbf{m}\times\mathbf{n}}
is a linear isomorphism, the previous equality is true if and only if

\mathcal{A}=\sum\limits_{\alpha=1}^{r}\sigma_{\alpha}\mathcal{U}_{\alpha}\circ\overline{\mathcal{V}_{\alpha}}

with
\{\mathrm{hvec}(\mathcal{U}_{\alpha})\}_{\alpha=1}^{r}
and
\{\mathrm{hvec}(\mathcal{V}_{\alpha})\}_{\alpha=1}^{r}
orthonormal sets. Since all of the steps above are equalities, the converse follows by reading the proof in reverse.
∎

Z\in(\mathbb{C}^{n})^{1}\cong\mathbb{C}^{n}
with
z\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
, and write
\mathrm{Ev}_{z}
rather than
\mathrm{Ev}_{Z}
.

Remark 2.11
.

Note also that by viewing order
2
hypermatrices as matrices, in particular if
A
is an
m\times n
matrix,
B
is an
m\times p
matrix, and
C
is a
n\times q
matrix, then

A*(B,C)=B^{T}AC.

(2)

Therefore by the above definition if
H\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
and
x=[x_{1},...,x_{n}]^{T}
is a vector of indeterminates, then we have that

H*(\overline{x},x)=x^{\dagger}Hx.

(3)

Thus, if more generally we view the Hermitian matrix
H=[h_{ij}]\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
as a cubical hypermatrix of order
2
with side length
n
, then we can equivalently express the corresponding degree
1
Hermitian
h
in the variables
x_{1},...,x_{n}
as the formal multilinear matrix product:

h(x_{1},...,x_{n})=H*(\overline{x},x).

Indeed, for any degree
k
Hermitian form
h
in the variables
x_{1},...,x_{n}
we may express it as the formal multilinear matrix product:

h(x_{1},...,x_{n})=\mathcal{A}*(\underbrace{\overline{x},...,\overline{x}}_{k\text{ times}},\overbrace{x,...,x}^{k\text{ times}})

for some cubical hypermatrix
\mathcal{A}=[a_{i_{1}...i_{k}j_{1}...j_{k}}]\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times...\times n}
such that
a_{i_{1}...i_{k}j_{1}...j_{k}}=\overline{a_{j_{1}...j_{k}i_{1}...i_{k}}}
for each
i_{1},...,i_{k},j_{1},...,j_{k}\in[n]
. In summary, we have the following definition:

Definition 2.12
.

A
hypermatrix representation
of a degree
k
Hermitian form in the variables
x_{1},...,x_{n}
is a cubical hypermatrix
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times...\times n}
of even order
d=2k
such that

\mathcal{A}*(\underbrace{\overline{x},...,\overline{x}}_{k\text{ times}},\overbrace{x,...,x}^{k\text{ times}})=h(x_{1},...,x_{n})

where
x:=[x_{1},...,x_{n}]^{T}
and
*
denotes right multilinear matrix multiplication.

Note that in general the hypermatrix representation of
h
is not unique, however under certain symmetry conditions imposed on
\mathcal{A}
it is; that is, there is a one-to-one correspondence between degree
k
Hermitian forms and certain types of symmetric hypermatrices, as we explain below.

Definition 2.13
.

An even order
d=2k
hypermatrix
\mathcal{A}=[a_{i_{1}...i_{k}j_{1}...j_{k}}]\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{1}\times...\times n_{k}\times n_{1}\times...\times n_{k}}
is called
Hermitian
if

a_{i_{1}...i_{k}j_{1}...j_{k}}=\overline{a_{j_{1}...j_{k}i_{1}...i_{k}}}

for every
1\leq i_{l},j_{l}\leq n_{l}
,
l=1,...,k
.

Definition 2.14
.

An even order
d=2k
cubical hypermatrix
\mathcal{A}=[a_{i_{1}...,i_{k}j_{1},...j_{k}}]\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times...\times n}
is said to be
partially symmetric
if

a_{i_{1}...i_{k}j_{1}...j_{k}}=a_{i_{\pi(1)}...i_{\pi(k)}j_{\tau(1)}...j_{\tau(k)}},\qquad\forall\pi,\tau\in S_{k}.

If, additionally,
\mathcal{A}
is also Hermitian, then we say that
\mathcal{A}
is
Hermitian partially symmetric
.

Remark 2.15
.

Note that while non-cubical hypermatrices may be Hermitian, by definition only cubical hypermatrices can be Hermitian partially symmetric.

It was proven in
[
7
, Proposition 3.8]
that there is a bijection between degree
k
Hermitian forms in the indeterminates
x_{1},...,x_{n}
and even order Hermitian partially symmetric hypermatrices. Explicitly, this correspondence is given by the map

\begin{split}\varphi:\{\text{Hermitian partially symmetric }\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times...\times n}\}&\longrightarrow\{\text{degree }k\text{ Hermitian forms in }x_{1},...,x_{n}\}\\
\mathcal{A}&\mapsto h_{\mathcal{A}}(x)\end{split}

(4)

with

h_{\mathcal{A}}(x):=\mathcal{A}*(\overline{x},...,\overline{x},x,...,x),

or equivalently at the coordinate level,

h_{\mathcal{A}}(x_{1},...,x_{n})=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1},...,i_{k}=1\\
j_{1},...,j_{k}=1\end{subarray}}^{n}a_{i_{1}...i_{k}j_{1}...j_{k}}\overline{x_{i_{1}}}...\overline{x_{i_{k}}}x_{j_{1}}...x_{j_{k}}

with
\mathcal{A}=[a_{i_{1}...i_{k}j_{1}...j_{k}}]_{n\times...\times n}
. In particular, the Hermitian and partial symmetry conditions of
\mathcal{A}
ensure that
\varphi
bijective; its inverse sends a degree
k
Hermitian form to the unique Hermitian partially hypermatrix whose entries coincide with the form’s coefficients. Thus, we may unambiguously associate any degree
k
Hermitian form with a unique Hermitian partially symmetric hypermatrix, and vice versa.

2.2.2
Positive Definite Tensors

Definition 2.16

(
[
3
]
)
.

Let
R
be the commutative unital
∗
-algebra as given in Definition
2.9
, the evaluation map
\mathrm{ev}_{z}:R\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
be as given in Remark
2.10
, and consider the (
p=1
) extension
\mathrm{Ev}_{z}:R^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{n}
, noting in particular that

.

Suppose also that
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times...\times n}
is an even order
d=2k
Hermitian partially symmetric hypermatrix. Then we say that
\mathcal{A}
is
Hermitian positive definite (semi-definite)
if and only if for every

such that

, we have that

h_{\mathcal{A}}(w)>0\text{ }(\geq 0).

Equivalently,
\mathcal{A}
is
Hermitian positive definite (semi-definite)
if and only if

\mathcal{A}*(\underbrace{\overline{w},...,\overline{w}}_{k\text{ times}},\overbrace{w,...,w}^{k\text{ times}})>0\text{ }(\geq 0)

for every nonzero
w\in\mathbb{C}^{n}

The theory of tensor eigenvalues was developed independently by both L.H. Lim and L. Qi in
[
11
, 2005]
and
[
17
, 2005]
respectively, however in both of their work the base field is
\mathbb{R}
. In
[
14
, 2007]
, G. Ni, L. Qi, F. Wang, and Y. Wang introduce the notion of

-eigenvalues of tensors, where the base field is
\mathbb{C}
, and in
[
18

, X. Zhang and L. Qi introduces the notion of

Definition 2.17
.

Let
\mathcal{A}=[a_{i_{1}...i_{k}j_{1}...j_{k}}]\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times...\times n}
be an even order
d=2k
cubical hypermatrix. In
[
7

, Jiang et al. say that
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
is an
\bm{\widehat{C}}
-eigenvalue
of
\mathcal{A}
if there exists a vector

such that

z^{\dagger}z=1

and

\mathcal{A}*(I_{n},\underbrace{\overline{z},...,\overline{z}}_{k-1\text{ times}},\overbrace{z,...,z}^{k\text{ times}})=\lambda z.

In
[
3
]
, Chen & Yang consider a slightly more general notion than that of
\widehat{C}
-eigenvalues, and say that
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
is an
\bm{\widehat{H}}
-eigenvalue
of
\mathcal{A}
if there exists a vector

such that

\sum\limits_{i_{2},...,i_{k},j_{1},...,j_{k}=1}^{n}a_{ii_{2}...i_{k}j_{1}...j_{k}}\overline{z}_{i_{2}}...\overline{z}_{i_{k}}z_{j_{1}}...z_{j_{k}}=\lambda|z_{i}|^{2(k-1)}z_{i}\quad\forall i=1,...,n,

or equivalently

\mathcal{A}*(I_{n},\underbrace{\overline{z},...,\overline{z}}_{k-1\text{ times}},\overbrace{z,...,z}^{k\text{ times}})=\lambda|z|z.

Chen & Yang then prove the following result, providing a spectral characterization of Hermitian positive definite hypermatrices.

Theorem 2.18

(Spectral Theorem of Hermitian Forms
[
3
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times...\times n}
be a Hermitian partially symmetric hypermatrix of even order
d=2k
. Then there always exists
\widehat{H}
-eigenvalues (and hence
\widehat{C}
-eigenvalues) of
\mathcal{A}
. Moreover,
\mathcal{A}
is Hermitian positive definite (semi-definite) if and only if all of the
\widehat{H}
-eigenvalues of
\mathcal{A}
are positive (nonnegative).

In the next section, we will generalize this theorem to certain types of higher order
d+1
hypermatrices in terms of the
t
-product (which we discuss in the following subsection).

2.3
The t-product for Order 3 Tensors

2.3.1
Definition/Basic Properties

Definition 2.19
.

For an order 3 hypermatrix
\mathcal{A}=[a_{ijk}]\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n\times p}
, the
\bm{k^{th}}
frontal slice
of
\mathcal{A}
as the matrix

\mathcal{A}_{(k)}:=\mathcal{A}[:,:,k]=[a_{ijk}]_{m\times n\times 1}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}

(i.e. the index
k
is fixed). The
block-circulant matrix
of
\mathcal{A}
is then defined to be unique matrix

\mathrm{bcirc}(\mathcal{A}):=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}\mathcal{A}_{(1)}&\mathcal{A}_{(p)}&\dots&\mathcal{A}_{(2)}\\
\mathcal{A}_{(2)}&\mathcal{A}_{(1)}&\dots&\mathcal{A}_{(3)}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\mathcal{A}_{(p)}&\mathcal{A}_{(p-1)}&\dots&\mathcal{A}_{(1)}\end{array}\right]\in\mathbb{C}^{mp\times np}.

The
unfolding
of
\mathcal{A}
as the block matrix

\mathrm{unfold}(\mathcal{A}):=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\mathcal{A}_{(1)}\\
\mathcal{A}_{(2)}\\
\vdots\\
\mathcal{A}_{(p)}\end{array}\right]\in\mathbb{C}^{mp\times n}

Note that the unfolding of
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n\times p}
is precisely the
\Big((m,p),n\Big)
-matricization of
\mathcal{A}^{(23)}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times p\times n}
, where
\mathcal{A}^{(23)}[i_{1}i_{2}i_{3}]:=\mathcal{A}[i_{1}i_{3}i_{2}]
. Since
\psi_{\mathbf{m}}
is invertible for any fixed
\mathbf{m}
, it follows that for any
mp\times n
matrix
M
, there is a unique hypermatrix
\mathcal{A}(M)\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times p\times n}
such that

\Big(\mathcal{A}(M)\Big)_{ijk}=M_{\psi^{-1}\big((i,j),(m,p)\big),k}

and hence a unique
m\times n\times p
hypermatrix given by
\mathcal{A}(M)^{(23)}
. Thus, the unfolding operator of an order 3 hypermatrix
\mathcal{A}
is invertible, and so we formally define its inverse as follows:

Definition 2.20
.

For an order 3 hypermatrix
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n\times p}
, the
folding
of
\mathcal{A}
is the inverse of
\mathrm{unfold}(\mathcal{A})
, i.e.

\mathrm{fold}(\mathrm{unfold}(\mathcal{A}))=\mathcal{A}.

With this notation out of the way, we may now define the
t
-product for order
3
hypermatrices:

Definition 2.21

(t-product)
.

For order 3 hypermatrices
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n\times p}
and
\mathcal{B}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times q\times p}
, the
t-product
of
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
,
\mathcal{A}*_{t}\mathcal{B}
, is the an order
3
hypermatrix given by

A*_{t}B:=\mathrm{fold}\big(\mathrm{bcirc}(A)\mathrm{unfold}(B)\big)\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times q\times p}

Note that indeed this product is well-defined because
\mathrm{bcirc}(A)\in\mathbb{C}^{mp\times np}
,
\mathrm{unfold}(B)\in\mathbb{C}^{np\times q}
, and so
\mathrm{bcirc}(A)\mathrm{unfold}(B)\in\mathbb{C}^{mp\times q}
, hence folding we obtain a
m\times q\times p
hypermatrix. Note also that the computing this directly has a run time cost of
\mathcal{O}(mnp^{2}q)
.

Proposition 2.22

(t-Product Algebra
[
9
]
)
.

The vector space of hypermatrices
\mathbb{C}^{n\times n\times p}
is an associative unital algebra under the
t
-product
*_{t}
, with unit given by
\mathcal{I}_{1}
whose first frontal slice is the identity matrix
I_{n}
, and whose other frontal slices are the zero matrix
0_{n\times n}
; that is,

\mathrm{unfold}(\mathcal{I}_{1})=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}I_{n}\\
0_{n\times n}\\
\vdots\\
0_{n\times n}\end{array}\right].

For convenience we will denote this algebra as
(\mathcal{T}_{1}^{(n,p)},*_{t})
.

Remark 2.23
.

The subscript ”
1
” in
\mathcal{I}_{1}
and
\mathcal{T}_{1}^{(n,p)}
will make sense after reading section 2 of this paper.

Definition 2.24

(t-Conjugate Transpose)
.

Let
R
be the commutative unital
∗
-algebra as given in Definition
2.9
, and let
\mathcal{A}\in R^{m\times n\times p}
, and extend conjugation
\overline{(\cdot)}:R\rightarrow R
entrywise to hypermatrices over
R
; i.e., if
\mathcal{A}=[a_{ijk}]\in R^{m\times n\times p}
then
\overline{\mathcal{A}}:=[\overline{a_{ijk}}]\in R^{m\times n\times p}
. Then the
\bm{t}
-conjugate transpose
of
\mathcal{A}
is the hypermatrix
\mathcal{A}^{H}\in R^{n\times m\times p}
obtained by taking the conjugate transpose of each of the frontal slices
\mathcal{A}_{(k)}
and then reversing their order from
k=2
to
k=p
; that is,

\mathcal{A}^{H}=\mathrm{fold}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}(\mathcal{A}_{(1)})^{H}\\
(\mathcal{A}_{(p)})^{H}\\
\vdots\\
(\mathcal{A}_{(2)})^{H}\end{array}\right]

Remark 2.25
.

Note that in
[
9
]
the
t
-conjugate transpose is defined for order
3
hypermatrices over
\mathbb{C}
, but for the subsequent sections it will be necessary for us to extend the setting of this t-conjugate transpose operator. Therefore, we recast this definition over
R
, but remark that upon specializing
R\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
via the evaluation homomorphism defined in Remark
2.10
our definition reduces to that given by Kilmer and Martin in
[
9
]
.

Note also that over
\mathbb{C}
, the
\bm{t}
-transpose
is identically defined as the
t
-conjugate transpose, except with
H
replaced by
T
and ”conjugate transpose” replaced with ”transpose.”

Lastly, note that over
\mathbb{C}
the definition above ensures that
(\mathcal{A}*\mathcal{B})^{H}=\mathcal{B}^{H}*\mathcal{A}^{H}
(this equation also holds when replacing
H
with
T
), making
(\mathcal{T}_{1}^{(n,p)},*_{t})
a
∗
-algebra via
H
.

Definition 2.26

(t-Hermitian
[
9
]
)
.

A hypermatrix
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{T}_{1}^{(n,p)}
is
t-Hermitian
if
\mathcal{A}^{H}=\mathcal{A}
.

The subspace of all
t
-Hermitian hypermatrices is in fact a subalgebra of
\mathcal{T}_{1}^{(n,p)}
, and in particular we will denote this subalgebra as
\mathcal{H}_{1}^{(n,p)}
.

2.3.2
Tubewise t-Product

Definition 2.27
.

For an order 3 hypermatrix
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n\times p}
, the
(i,j)
-tube/fiber
of
\mathcal{A}
is the vector

\mathcal{A}_{i,j}:=\mathcal{A}[i,j,:]=[a_{ijk}]_{1\times 1\times p}\in\mathbb{C}^{p}

(i.e. the indices
(i,j)
are fixed).

The
t
-product of hypermatrices
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n\times p}
and
\mathcal{B}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times q\times p}
may then be re-defined tubewise as follows:

(\mathcal{A}*_{t}\mathcal{B})[i,j,:]=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}\mathcal{A}[i,k,:]\circledast\mathcal{B}[k,j,:]

(5)

where
\circledast
denotes the discrete circular convolution operator. Therefore, for each fixed
i\in[m]
and
j\in[q]
, if we apply the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) to the tube
(\mathcal{A}*_{t}\mathcal{B})[i,j,:]
,
then we have that

\displaystyle\mathrm{FFT}\big((\mathcal{A}*_{t}\mathcal{B})[i,j,:]\big)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{FFT}\left(\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}\mathcal{A}[i,k,:]\circledast\mathcal{B}[k,j,:]\right),\quad\text{by equation }\eqref{Tubewise t-product}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}\mathrm{FFT}\big(\mathcal{A}[i,k,:]\circledast\mathcal{B}[k,j,:]\big),\quad\text{by linearity}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}\mathrm{FFT}\big(\mathcal{A}[i,k,:]\big)\odot\mathrm{FFT}\big(\mathcal{B}[k,j,:]\big),\quad\text{by properties of FFT},

where
\odot
denotes the Hadamard product.

Now, let
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}
and
\widehat{\mathcal{B}}
denote the respective resulting hypermatrices after applying the FFT to each tube of
A
and
B
along their third mode; in other words,
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}:=\mathrm{fft}_{3}(\mathcal{A})
and
\widehat{\mathcal{B}}:=\mathrm{fft}_{3}(\mathcal{B})
. Furthermore, denote
\widehat{\mathbf{a}}_{ik}:=\mathrm{FFT}\big(\mathcal{A}[i,k,:]\big)
,
\widehat{\mathbf{b}}_{kj}:=\mathrm{FFT}\big(\mathcal{B}[k,j,:]\big)
, and
\widehat{\mathbf{c}}_{ij}:=\mathrm{FFT}\big((\mathcal{A}*_{t}\mathcal{B})[i,j,:]\big)
. Then the equations above are equivalent to

\widehat{\mathbf{c}}_{ij}=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}\widehat{\mathbf{a}}_{ik}\odot\widehat{\mathbf{b}}_{kj}

(6)

Hence, for each
l\in[p]
we have that

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{c}}_{ij}[l]

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}\widehat{\mathbf{a}}_{ik}[l]\widehat{\mathbf{b}}_{kj}[l]

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}\widehat{\mathcal{A}}[i,k,l]\widehat{\mathcal{B}}[k,j,l]

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{(l)}[i,k]\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{(l)}[k,j]

\displaystyle=\big(\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{(l)}\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{(l)}\big)[i,j].

That is,

\mathrm{FFT}\big((\mathcal{A}*_{t}\mathcal{B})[i,j,l]\big)=\big(\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{(l)}\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{(l)}\big)[i,j]

for each
i\in[m]
and
j\in[q]
; consequently,

\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{(l)}:=\mathrm{FFT}\big((\mathcal{A}*_{t}\mathcal{B})[:,:,l]\big)=\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{(l)}\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{(l)}

(7)

for each
l\in[p]
. It then follows that
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}=\mathrm{fft}_{3}(\mathcal{A}*_{t}\mathcal{B})
; that is,
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
is what we obtain after applying the FFT along the
3^{rd}
mode of
\mathcal{A}*_{t}\mathcal{B}
, or to put it more succinctly,
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
is
\mathcal{A}*_{t}\mathcal{B}
in the frequency domain. To then get back
\mathcal{A}*_{t}\mathcal{B}
in the original basis (i.e. the spatial domain), we need to apply the inverse FFT to
\widehat{C}
to each tube along its third mode; that is,

\mathrm{ifft}_{3}(\widehat{\mathcal{C}})=\mathcal{C}

where
\mathcal{C}
is precisely the
t
-product
\mathcal{A}*_{t}\mathcal{B}
. In summary, we have the following algorithm -first mentioned in
[
9
]
- which computes the
t
-product for order 3 hypermatrices in terms of the FFT:

Algorithm 1

t-Product via FFT
[
9
]

0:

\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n\times p}
and
\mathcal{B}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times q\times p}

0:

\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{A}*_{t}\mathcal{B}

1:

\widehat{\mathcal{A}}\leftarrow\texttt{fft}(\mathcal{A},[],3)

2:

\widehat{\mathcal{B}}\leftarrow\texttt{fft}(\mathcal{B},[],3)

3:

for

l=1,...,p

do

4:

\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{(l)}\leftarrow\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{(l)}\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{(l)}

5:

end

for

6:

\widehat{\mathcal{C}}\leftarrow\mathrm{fold}\left(\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{(1)}\\
\vdots\\
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{(p)}\end{array}\right]\right)

7:

\mathcal{C}\leftarrow\texttt{ifft}(\widehat{\mathcal{C}},[],3)

8:

return

\mathcal{C}

Since
\mathcal{A}
has
mn
-many tubes and
\mathcal{B}
has
nq
-many tubes along their third mode (which are vectors of length
p
), and the cost of applying the FFT to a vector of length
p
is
\mathcal{O}(p\log(p))
, the cost of applying
\mathrm{fft}_{3}
to
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
is
\mathcal{O}\big((mn+nq)p\log(p)\big)
. Additionally, since the algorithm above requires the calculation of
p
-many matrix products of
m\times n
matrices with
n\times q
matrices, it incurs a cost of
mnpq
. Lastly, since
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
has
mq
-many tubes along its third mode and the cost of applying the
\mathrm{FFT}^{-1}
to a vector of length
p
is
\mathcal{O}(p\log(p))
, the cost of applying
\mathrm{ifft}_{3}
to
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
is
\mathcal{O}(mpq\log(p))
. Thus, the total run time cost of Algorithm 1 is
\mathcal{O}\big((mnp+npq+mpq)\log(p)+mnpq\big)
. Taking
p\rightarrow\infty
,
mnp^{2}q
(the cost of the
t
-product without utilizing the FFT) will dominate both
(mnp+npq+mpq)\log(p)
and
mnpq
, so this is a significant improvement in computational efficiency for computing the
t
-product. We summarize formally below with the proposition:

Proposition 2.28
.

[
9
]

The complexity of Algorithm 1 is
\mathcal{O}\big((mnp+npq+mpq)\log(p)+mnpq\big)
.

In addition to being more computationally efficient, the fact that the
t
-product in the frequency domain slice-wise reduces to matrix multiplication is both an elegant and powerful consequence of the FFT. We will make extensive use of this fact, in particular recalling equations (
7
) when defining
t
-Hermitian forms, and in particular using that equation to prove that a collection of Hermitian forms lifts to a unique
t
-Hermitian form. Another important property which we will make use of, and which follows form Algorithm 1, is the following:

Proposition 2.29
.

[
9
]

For an order 3 hypermatrix
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n\times p}
,

\mathrm{bcirc}(\mathcal{A})=(\mathrm{DFT}_{p}^{H}\otimes I_{m})\mathrm{diag}\big(\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{(1)},...,\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{(p)}\big)(\mathrm{DFT}_{p}\otimes I_{n}),

where
\mathrm{DFT}_{p}
is the
p\times p
normalized discrete Fourier transform given by

\mathrm{DFT}_{p}[i+1,j+1]=\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}\omega_{p}^{ij}

with
\omega_{p}=e^{\frac{2\pi i}{p}}
and
i,j=0,...,p-1
, and
H
here denoting the normal matrix conjugate transpose.

2.3.3
t-Conjugate Transpose in Frequency Domain and Fourier Conjugation

In this article, it will be particularly important to consider how the
t
-conjugate transpose transforms order
3
hypermatrices
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n\times p}
in the frequency domain. In particular, we note the following observation:

Proposition 2.30
.

For any hypermatrix
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n\times p}
,

\mathcal{A}^{H}=\mathrm{ifft}_{3}\Big(\mathrm{fft}_{3}(\mathcal{A})^{\widetilde{H}}\Big)

where
\widetilde{H}
means we take the conjugate transpose of each frontal slice in the frequency domain (after applying
\mathrm{fft}_{3}
) without reversing their order, i.e.

\mathrm{fft}_{3}(\mathcal{A})^{\widetilde{H}}:=\mathrm{fold}\left(\left[\begin{array}[]{c}(\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{(1)})^{H}\\
(\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{(2)})^{H}\\
\vdots\\
(\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{(p)})^{H}\end{array}\right]\right)

Consequently, while in the spatial domain we have

\left(\mathcal{A}^{H}\right)_{(k)}=\left(\mathcal{A}_{(p-k+2)}\right)^{H}

for each
k=2,...,p
, in the frequency domain we have

\widehat{\mathcal{A}^{H}}_{(k)}=(\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{(k)})^{H}

(8)

for each
k=1,2,...,p
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n\times p}
and define

\mathcal{A}^{H_{F}}:=\mathrm{ifft}_{3}\Big(\mathrm{fft}_{3}(\mathcal{A})^{\widetilde{H}}\Big).

We will prove that

\mathcal{A}^{H_{F}}=\mathcal{A}^{H}.

By our definition for
H_{F}
and Proposition
2.29
we have that

\displaystyle\mathrm{bcirc}(\mathcal{A}^{H_{F}})

\displaystyle=(DFT_{p}^{H}\otimes I_{n})\mathrm{diag}\Big(\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{(1)}^{H},\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{(2)}^{H}....,\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{(p)}^{H}\Big)(DFT_{p}\otimes I_{n})

\displaystyle=(DFT_{p}^{H}\otimes I_{n})\mathrm{diag}\big(\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{(1)},\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{(2)}...,\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{(p)}\big)^{H}(DFT_{p}\otimes I_{n})

\displaystyle=\Big((DFT_{p}^{H}\otimes I_{n})\mathrm{diag}\big(\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{(1)},\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{(2)},...,\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{(p)}\big)(DFT_{p}\otimes I_{n})\Big)^{H}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{bcirc}(\mathcal{A})^{H}.

On the other hand, note that

\mathrm{bcirc}(\mathcal{A}^{H})=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}\mathcal{A}_{(1)}^{H}&\mathcal{A}_{(2)}^{H}&...&\mathcal{A}_{(p)}^{H}\\
\mathcal{A}_{(p)}^{H}&\mathcal{A}_{(1)}^{H}&...&\mathcal{A}_{(p-1)}^{H}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\mathcal{A}_{(2)}^{H}&\mathcal{A}_{(3)}^{H}&...&\mathcal{A}_{(p)}^{H}\end{array}\right]

and so from Proposition
2.29
we have that

\displaystyle\mathrm{bcirc}(\mathcal{A}^{H})

\displaystyle=(DFT_{p}^{H}\otimes I_{n})\mathrm{diag}\Big(\widehat{\mathcal{A}^{H}}_{(1)},\widehat{\mathcal{A}^{H}}_{(p)},...,\widehat{\mathcal{A}^{H}}_{(2)}\Big)(DFT_{p}\otimes I_{n})

\displaystyle=(DFT_{p}^{H}\otimes I_{n})\mathrm{diag}\big(\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{(1)}^{H},\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{(2)}^{H},...,\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{(p)}^{H}\big)(DFT_{p}\otimes I_{n}),\quad\text{by definition of $t$-conjugate transpose}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{bcirc}(\mathcal{A})^{H},\quad\text{from derivations above}.

Thus,

\mathrm{bcirc}(\mathcal{A}^{H_{F}})=\mathrm{bcirc}(\mathcal{A}^{H}),

which implies that
\mathcal{A}^{H_{F}}=\mathcal{A}^{H}
since in general
\mathrm{bcirc}:\mathbb{C}^{m\times n\times p}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{mp\times np}
is injective.
∎

With this better understanding of how the conjugate transpose transforms in the frequency domain, we may prove the following result which serves as groundwork and reinforces one of the overarching theme of this article which is: collections of matrices or objects defined by matrices lift to hypermatrices in the t-product algebra via the FFT along tubes.

Proposition 2.31

(Matrices Lift to Hypermatrix Algebra via t-Product)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{T}_{1}^{(n,p)}
and define
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}:=\mathrm{fft}_{3}(\mathcal{A})
. Then the map

\displaystyle\Phi:\mathcal{T}_{1}^{(n,p)}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\bigoplus\limits_{k=1}^{p}\mathcal{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle\mapsto(\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{(1)},...,\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{(p)})

is an involutive algebra isomorphism with respect to the
t
-conjugate transpose.

Proof.

First, note that since addition and scalar multiplication is defined componentwise, for any
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{C}
and
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\in\mathcal{T}_{1}^{(n,p)}
we have

\alpha\mathcal{A}+\beta\mathcal{B}=\mathrm{fold}\left(\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\alpha\mathcal{A}_{(1)}+\beta\mathcal{B}_{(1)}\\
\vdots\\
\alpha\mathcal{A}_{(p)}+\beta\mathcal{B}_{(p)}\end{array}\right]\right),

from which it follows that
\Phi
is linear; moreover, since the FFT is merely a change of basis transformation, the map
\Phi
is injective, hence a linear isomorphism. Next, recall that by equation (
7
), the
t
-product
\mathcal{A}*_{t}\mathcal{B}
slice-wise reduces to matrix multiplication in the frequency domain; consequently,

\Phi(\mathcal{A}*_{t}\mathcal{B})=(\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{(1)}\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{(1)},...,\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{(p)}\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{(p)})=(\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{(1)},...,\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{(p)})(\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{(1)},...,\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{(p)})=\Phi(\mathcal{A})\Phi(\mathcal{B}),

proving that
\Phi
is an algebra isomorphism. Lastly, by Proposition
2.30
we have that

\Phi(\mathcal{A}^{H})=\Phi(\widehat{\mathcal{A}^{H}}_{(1)},...,\widehat{\mathcal{A}^{H}}_{(p)})=\Phi\left((\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{(1)})^{H},...,(\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{(p)})^{H}\right)=\Phi(\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{(1)},...,\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{(p)})^{H}=\Phi(\mathcal{A})^{H},

proving that
\Phi
is an involutive algebra isomorphism.
∎

Corollary 2.32
.

The identity
\mathcal{I}
in
(\mathcal{T}_{1}^{(n,p)},*_{t})
has the following form:

\widehat{\mathcal{I}}=\mathrm{fold}\left(\left[\begin{array}[]{c}I_{n}\\
\vdots\\
I_{n}\end{array}\right]\right).

Proof.

This result immediately follows from Theorem
2.31
, however alternatively we may prove this directly using the discrete Fourier transform. In particular for
\mathbf{e}_{1}\in\mathbb{C}^{p}
,

\mathrm{unfold}(\mathcal{I})=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}I_{n}\\
0_{n}\\
\vdots\\
0_{n}\end{array}\right]=\mathbf{e}_{1}\otimes I_{n}

and so consequently

(\mathrm{DFT}_{p}\otimes I_{n})\mathrm{unfold}(\mathcal{I})=\mathrm{DFT}_{p}\mathbf{e}_{1}\otimes I_{n}=\mathbf{1}\otimes I_{n}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}I_{n}\\
I_{n}\\
\vdots\\
I_{n}\end{array}\right],

proving that

\widehat{\mathcal{I}}=\mathrm{fold}\left(\left[\begin{array}[]{c}I_{n}\\
\vdots\\
I_{n}\end{array}\right]\right)

∎

Remark 2.33
.

Note also that with Proposition
2.31
one can naturally define ”
t
-inverses,” and additionally a ”
t
-determinant” function on
(\mathcal{T}_{1}^{(n,p)},*_{t})
equal to the product of the determinants on each summand under
\Phi
; that is,

\det_{t}(\mathcal{A}):=\prod\limits_{l=1}^{p}\det(\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{(l)}).

This idea of a determinant function on third order tensors defined in terms of the determinant of the frontal slices in the frequency domain was first given in
[
4
]
. Note that such a ”
t
-determinant” function would then be a locus of singularity and satisfy the same algebraic properties as the classical matrix determinant (making it quite natural indeed).

Lastly, we introduce the notion of ”Fourier conjugation,” which essentially reduces to conjugation slice-wise in the frequency domain.

Definition 2.34
.

Let
R
be the commutative unital
∗
-algebra given in Definition
2.9
, and let
\mathcal{A}\in R^{m\times n\times p}
. We extend the involution
\overline{(\cdot)}:R\rightarrow R
entrywise to hypermatrices over
R
; i.e., if
\mathcal{A}=[a_{ijk}]\in R^{m\times n\times p}
, then
\overline{\mathcal{A}}:=[\overline{a_{ijk}}]\in R^{m\times n\times p}
. Then the
Fourier conjugation
of
\mathcal{A}
is the hypermatrix
J(\mathcal{A})\in R^{m\times n\times p}
such that

J(\mathcal{A})_{(l+1)}:=\overline{\mathcal{A}_{\big((-l\bmod p)+1\big)}}

for
l=0,1,...,p-1
.

Let
a=(a_{0},a_{1},...,a_{p-1})
with
a_{l}:=\mathcal{A}[i,j,l+1]
for any fixed choice of
(i,j)
. Applying the DFT to the tuple
b=(b_{0},b_{1},...,b_{p-1})
with
b_{m}:=\overline{a_{-m}}
for each
m=0,1,...,p-1
, then we have that

\displaystyle\widehat{b}_{m}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{l=0}^{p-1}\overline{a_{-l}}e^{\frac{2\pi iml}{p}}

\displaystyle=\overline{a_{0}}+\sum\limits_{l^{\prime}=-(p-1)}^{-1}\overline{a_{l^{\prime}}}e^{-\frac{2\pi iml^{\prime}}{p}},\quad\text{setting }l^{\prime}:=-l

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{l=0}^{p-1}\overline{a_{l}}e^{-\frac{2\pi iml}{p}},\quad\text{by cyclicity}

\displaystyle=\overline{\sum\limits_{l=0}^{p-1}a_{l}e^{\frac{2\pi iml}{p}}}

\displaystyle=\overline{\widehat{a}_{m}}

for each
m=0,1,...,p-1
. Consequently,

\mathrm{fft}_{3}\big(J(\mathcal{A})\big)_{(k)}=\overline{\mathrm{fft}_{3}(\mathcal{A})_{(k)}}

(9)

for each
k=1,...,p
. From this observation we have the following fact:

Fact 2.35
.

\mathrm{fft}_{3}\circ J=\overline{(\cdot)}\circ\mathrm{fft}_{3},

or equivalently

J=\mathrm{ifft}_{3}\circ\overline{(\cdot)}\circ\mathrm{fft}_{3},

from which it follows that
J
is a conjugate-linear involution on
R^{m\times n\times p}
.

To summarize, upon specializing
R\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
via the evaluation homomorphism,
(\mathcal{T}_{1}^{(n,p)},*_{t})
is a complex
∗
-algebra via
H
equipped with compatible real structure
J
, and is
∗
-isomorphic to
\bigoplus\limits_{k=1}^{p}\mathcal{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
.

3
Degree

t-Hermitian Forms

We are now ready to introduce the central object of study: degree
k
t-Hermitian forms, which generalize classical degree
k
Hermitian forms via the t-product algebra; in particular, degree

Hermitian forms lifted to one singular object through the synthesis of the

Before we establish the general theory of degree

t-Hermitian forms, we first consider the special case of degree

t-Hermitian forms (i.e. t-Hermitian quadratic forms). Additionally, we will review a result on the spectral characterization of order 3 Hermitian positive-definite hypermatrices due to
[
19
]
, which we will then show straightforwardly generalizes to degree
k

t
-Hermitian forms.

3.1
Degree 1 t-Hermitian Forms

In this subsection we generalize the matrix representation of a degree
1
Hermitian forms to order 3 hypermatrices, with multilinear matrix multiplication replaced with the
t
-multilinear hypermatrix product, which we define below:

Definition 3.1

(t-Multilinear Hypermatrix Product (
k=1
))
.

Let
R
be the same commutative unital
∗
-algebra as given in Definition
2.9
,
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n\times p}
,
\mathcal{X}^{(1)}\in R^{m\times q\times p}
, and
\mathcal{X}^{n\times r\times p}
. Then the
(right) t-multilinear hypermatrix product
of
\mathcal{A}
with the tuple
(\mathcal{X}^{(1)},\mathcal{X}^{(2)})
is given by the hypermatrix

\mathcal{A}^{\prime}:=\mathcal{A}*_{t}(\mathcal{X}^{(1)},\mathcal{X}^{(2)})\in R^{q\times r\times p}

such that each of its
(i,j,l)
-entry in the frequency domain satisfies

\widehat{\mathcal{A}^{\prime}}[i,j,l]=\sum\limits_{k_{1},k_{2}=1}^{m,n}\widehat{\mathcal{A}}[k_{1},k_{2},l]\widehat{\mathcal{X}^{(1)}}[k_{1},i,l]\widehat{\mathcal{X}^{(2)}}[k_{2},j,l],

where in particular
\widehat{(\bullet)}:=\mathrm{fft}_{3}(\bullet)
for each hypermatrix
\bullet
.

Note that in particular that we use
*_{t}
to denote both the
t
-product and the
t
-multilinear hypermatrix product, however since
t
-multilinear hypermatrix is only defined when we are multiplying a hypermatrix by a tuple of hypermatrices, context should make it clear which product we are referring to when we write
*_{t}
. Nonetheless, there is a nice relationship between the
t
-product and
t
-multilinear hypermatrix multiplication amongst order
3
hypermatrices, analogous to the relationship between matrix multiplication and classical multilinear matrix multiplication amongst order
2
hypermatrices (see equations (
2
) and (
3
)):

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
R
be the same commutative unital
∗
-algebra as given in Definition
2.9
,
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n\times p}
,
\mathcal{X}^{(1)}\in R^{m\times q\times p}
, and
\mathcal{X}^{(2)}\in R^{n\times r\times p}
. Then

\mathcal{A}*_{t}\left(J(\mathcal{X}^{(1)}),\mathcal{X}^{(2)}\right)=(\mathcal{X}^{(1)})^{H}*_{t}\mathcal{A}*_{t}\mathcal{X}^{(2)}.

Proof.

First recall that in general if
A
is an
m\times n
matrix,
B
is an
n\times p
matrix, and
C
is an
p\times q
matrix, then in particular

(ABC)_{ij}=\sum\limits_{k_{1},k_{2}=1}^{n,p}A_{ik_{1}}B_{k_{1}k_{2}}C_{k_{2}j}

(10)

for each
i,j
. Therefore, setting
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}:=\mathcal{A}*_{t}\left(J(\mathcal{X}^{(1)}),\mathcal{X}^{(2)}\right)
, then by the definition of t-multilinear hypermatrix multiplication, for each triple
(i,j,l)
we have that

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{A}^{\prime}}[i,j,l]

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k_{1},k_{2}=1}^{m,n}\widehat{\mathcal{A}}[k_{1},k_{2},l]\widehat{J(\mathcal{X}^{(1)})}[k_{1},i,l]\widehat{\mathcal{X}^{(2)}}[k_{2},j,l]

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k_{1},k_{2}=1}^{m,n}\overline{\widehat{\mathcal{X}^{(1)}}[k_{1},i,l]}\widehat{\mathcal{A}}[k_{1},k_{2},l]\widehat{\mathcal{X}^{(2)}}[k_{2},j,l],\quad\text{by equation }\eqref{Fourier conjugation}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k_{1},k_{2}=1}^{m,n}\left(\widehat{\mathcal{X}^{(1)}}\right)^{H}[i,k_{1},l]\widehat{\mathcal{A}}[k_{1},k_{2},l]\widehat{\mathcal{X}^{(2)}}[k_{2},j,l]

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k_{1},k_{2}=1}^{m,n}\widehat{(\mathcal{X}^{(1)})^{H}}[i,k_{1},l]\widehat{\mathcal{A}}[k_{1},k_{2},l]\widehat{\mathcal{X}^{(2)}}[k_{2},j,l],\quad\text{by equation }\eqref{conjugate-transpose in frequency domain}.

Therefore, by equation (
10
), for each
l\in[p]
we have that

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{A}^{\prime}}_{(l)}

\displaystyle=\widehat{(\mathcal{X}^{(1)})^{H}}_{(l)}\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{(l)}\widehat{\mathcal{X}^{(2)}}_{(l)}

\displaystyle=\widehat{\left((\mathcal{X}^{(1)})^{H}*_{t}\mathcal{A}\right)}_{(l)}\widehat{\mathcal{X}^{(2)}}_{(l)},\quad\text{by equation }\eqref{t-product to matrix product via FFT}

\displaystyle=\widehat{\left((\mathcal{X}^{(1)})^{H}*_{t}\mathcal{A}*_{t}\mathcal{X}^{(2)}\right)}_{(l)},\quad\text{by equation }\eqref{t-product to matrix product via FFT}\text{ again.}

Thus,

\widehat{\mathcal{A}^{\prime}}=\widehat{\left((\mathcal{X}^{(1)})^{H}*_{t}\mathcal{A}*_{t}\mathcal{X}^{(2)}\right)}

and so by applying
\mathrm{ifft}_{3}
on both sides we obtain

\mathcal{A}^{\prime}=(\mathcal{X}^{(1)})^{H}*_{t}\mathcal{A}*_{t}\mathcal{X}^{(2)}.

∎

The above proposition motivates the following definition:

Definition 3.3
.

Let
R
be the same commutative unital
∗
-algebra as given in Definition
2.9
,
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{H}_{1}^{(n,p)}
(i.e.
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n\times p}
and
\mathcal{A}
is
t
-Hermitian), and
\mathcal{X}\in R^{n\times 1\times p}
be a
tubal vector of indeterminates
satisfying:

\widehat{\mathcal{X}}[i,1,l]=x_{i}

for each
l\in[p]
. Then the
degree
\bm{1}
t-Hermitian form associated with
\bm{\mathcal{A}}
is defined as the t-multilinear hypermatrix product

h_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathcal{X}):=\mathcal{A}*_{t}\left(J(\mathcal{X}),\mathcal{X}\right)=\mathcal{X}^{H}*_{t}\mathcal{A}*_{t}\mathcal{X}\in R^{1\times 1\times p}\cong R^{p}.

Looking at the proof of Proposition
3.2
, we see that a degree
1

t
-Hermitian form is in fact a collection of classical Hermitian forms in the frequency domain; that is, we have the following fact:

Fact 3.4
.

In the frequency domain (after applying
\mathrm{fft}_{3}
to
\mathcal{A}
,
\mathcal{X}
, and
J(\mathcal{X})
), a degree
1

t
-Hermitian form decomposes into the collection of classical degree
1
Hermitian forms (aka Hermitian quadratic forms):

\widehat{h_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathcal{X})}[l]=\widehat{\mathcal{X}^{H}}[:,1,l]\widehat{\mathcal{A}}[:,:,l]\widehat{\mathcal{X}}[:,1,l]=\underbrace{\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{(l)}*\left(\overline{\widehat{\mathcal{X}}_{(l)}},\widehat{\mathcal{X}}_{(l)}\right)}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{same as the left-hand }\\
\text{side of equation }\eqref{MM and MMM relationship applied to forms}\end{subarray}}\qquad\forall l\in[p].

Note in particular that since
\mathcal{A}
is t-Hermitian, each frontal slice
\mathcal{A}
in the frequency domain (appying FFT along the
3^{rd}
mode) is Hermitian and thus induces a unique degree
1
Hermitian form. Therefore, when we say that in the frequency domain a degree
1
t-Hermitian forms decomposes into a collection of degree
1
Hermitian forms, this decomposition is well-defined. Moreover, note that since degree
1
Hermitian forms uniquely correspond to Hermitian matrices, it then follows that degree
1

t
-Hermitian forms uniquely correspond to
t
-Hermitian hypermatrices (of order
3
).

Remark 3.5
.

For clarity and convenience, we will denote the Hermitian form

\widehat{h_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathcal{X})}[l]:=h_{\mathcal{A}}^{(l)}(\widehat{\mathcal{X}}_{(l)}).

We emphasize that the two occurrences of the index
l
are necessary, serving two distinct roles and therefore not an abuse of notation. The superscript
(l)
indexes the Hermitian form associated
l^{th}
frontal slice of
\mathcal{A}
in the frequency domain, whereas the subscript
(l)
indicates that the input vector of this form lies in the
l^{th}
slice of
\mathcal{X}
in the frequency domain. The necessity of this distinguishing these roles will become more apparent when we characterize the notion of
t
-Hermitian positive definiteness in terms of these slicewise Hermitian forms.

Remark 3.6
.

In
[
16
]
, L. Qi and X. Zhang define the t-quadratic form of a t-symmetric matrix
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n\times p}
(t-symmetric meaning
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}^{T}
, where

is the
t
-transpose mentioned in section 1.3.1) as the product

\mathcal{X}^{T}*_{t}\mathcal{A}*_{t}\mathcal{X}

with
\mathcal{X}
a tubal vector of indeterminates. In the frequency domain, the expression above decomposes into the equations:

\widehat{\mathcal{X}}[:,:,l]^{T}\widehat{\mathcal{A}}[:,:,l]\widehat{\mathcal{X}}[:,:,l]

for each

. In general
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}
will be complex, and so the equations are not guaranteed to yield real-valued output (even given real input for
\mathcal{X}
).

In contrast, we focus on degree
1

-Hermitian forms (which may be viewed as
t
-Hermitian quadratic forms), as this framework ensures real-valued output (given real or complex input for

) in the frequency domain and admits a natural extension to higher-degree Hermitian forms, which we will explain in the next subsection. Indeed, this extensibility will play a central role in the developments of the rest of this paper.

3.1.1
Hermitian Positive Definite Degree 1 t-Hermitian Forms

In
[
19
]
, M. Zheng et al. define the notion of t-positive definite for
t
-symmetric order
3
hypermatrices
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n\times p}
, which we recast over
\mathbb{C}
:

Definition 3.7
.

Let
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{H}_{1}^{(n,p)}
(i.e.
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n\times p}
and is
t
-Hermitian). We say that
\mathcal{A}
is
t-Hermitian positive definite
if

\langle\mathcal{X},\mathcal{A}*_{t}\mathcal{X}\rangle_{F}>0

for every nonzero
\mathcal{X}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times 1\times p}
, where
\langle\text{ , }\rangle_{F}
denotes the Frobenius inner product.

Remark 3.8
.

Note that in general for any two hypermatrices
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times...\times n_{N}}
, one may define the Frobenius inner product of
\mathcal{A}
with
\mathcal{B}
to be:

\langle\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\rangle_{F}:=\sum\limits_{i_{1},...,i_{N}=1}^{n_{1},...,n_{N}}\overline{\mathcal{B}[i_{1},...,i_{N}]}\mathcal{A}[i_{1},...,i_{N}]\in\mathbb{C},

which is in fact an inner product and reduces to the classical Frobenius inner product when
N=2
. Note also that
\mathcal{X}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times 1\times p}
can be considered to be a matrix since only its first and third modes have non-trivial dimension, and likewise
\mathcal{A}*_{t}\mathcal{X}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times 1\times p}
so it can also be considered to be a matrix, hence
\langle\text{ , }\rangle_{F}
in the above definition is literally the Frobenius inner product in the classical sense.

Note also that the notion of
t
-Hermitian positive semi-definite
is identically defined except with
">"
being replaced with
"\geq"
and the nonzero assumption of
\mathcal{X}^{n\times 1\times p}
relaxed. In
[
19
, Theorem 4.4]
, Zheng et al. then prove a real-version of the following result, characterizing t-Hermitian positive definite hypermatrices:

Proposition 3.9

(Spectral Characterization of
t
-Positive Definiteness (
k=1
))
.

Let
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{H}_{1}^{(n,p)}
(i.e.
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n\times p}
and
\mathcal{A}
is
t
-Hermitian). Then the following are equivalent:

(i)

\mathcal{A}
is t-Hermitian positive definite.

(ii)

Each frontal slice of
\mathcal{A}
in the Fourier domain:
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{(l)}
, is Hermitian positive definite for each
l=1,...,p
.

(iii)

Every eigenvalue of
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{(l)}
is positive, for each
l=1,...,p
(such eigenvalues are referred to as
t-eigenvalues
of
\mathcal{A}
).

Using our carefully constructed definition of degree
1

t
-Hermitian forms and
t
-multilinear hypermatrix multiplication, we may reframe
t
-Hermitian positive definiteness in terms of degree
1

t
-Hermitian forms, in which case we obtain the following result (which yields a natural generalization to higher order hypermatrices, as we will show in the next subsection). Before giving this stating this proposition, however, we note that since
\mathcal{X}\in R^{n\times 1\times p}\cong R^{n\times p}
, we may view each tubal vector
\mathcal{X}
as an
n\times p
matrix of indeterminates in
x_{1},...,x_{n}
, and in particular each frontal slice of
\mathcal{X}
,
\mathcal{X}_{(l)}\in R^{n\times 1\times 1}\cong R^{n}
, as a vector of the indeterminates in
x_{1},...,x_{n}
. Now, without further ado:

Proposition 3.10

(t-Hermitian Positive Definiteness in Terms of Degree 1 Hermitian Forms)
.

Let
R
be the commutative unital
∗
-algebra as given in Definition
2.9
and consider the extension of the evaluation map
\mathrm{Ev}_{Z}:R^{n\times p}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{n\times p}
defined as in Remark
2.10
. Then
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{H}_{1}^{(n,p)}
is
t
-Hermitian positive definite if and only if for every
Z\in(\mathbb{C}^{n})^{p}
such that
w^{(l)}:=\mathrm{Ev}_{z^{(l)}}(\widehat{\mathcal{X}}_{(l)})\neq 0
for each
l\in[p]
, we have that

h_{\mathcal{A}}\left(\mathrm{Ev}_{Z}(\mathcal{X})\right)\succ 0,

where
"\bullet\succ 0"
means that every entry in vector
\mathrm{fft}(\bullet)
is positive. At the slice level, this is the same as saying that
\mathcal{A}
is
t
-Hermitian positive definite if and only if for each
l\in[p]
, we have that

h_{\mathcal{A}}^{(l)}(w^{(l)})>0,

or equivalently,

\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{(l)}*(\overline{w},w)>0

for every nonzero
w\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
.

Proof.

By Fact
3.4
, a degree
1

t
-Hermitian form decomposes into a collection of degree 1 Hermitian forms in the frequency domain:

\left\{\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{(l)}*\left(\overline{\widehat{\mathcal{X}}_{(l)}},\widehat{\mathcal{X}}_{(l)}\right):l\in[p]\right\}.

Since
\mathcal{A}
is assumed to be
t
-Hermitian, each slice in the frequency domain (i.e.
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{(l)}
) is an
n\times n
Hermitian matrix; hence, under the extended evaluation map, each degree
1
Hermitian form reduces to a vector-matrix-vector product of the form:

\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{(l)}*(\overline{w},w)=w^{\dagger}\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{(l)}w,

for some
w\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
. By the classical spectral theorem for
n\times n
Hermitian matrices, such vector-matrix-vector products are positive for every nonzero
w\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
if and only if
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{(l)}
is Hermitian positive definite, from which it follows by Proposition
3.9
that
\mathcal{A}
is
t
-Hermitian positive definite.
∎

\displaystyle h

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{F}_{k}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{l}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\exists!f}

4.1

Definition 4.1
.

For hypermatrices
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{m_{1}\times...\times m_{M}\times k_{1}\times...\times k_{K}}

of

\mathcal{A}\boxtimes\mathcal{B}\in\mathbb{C}^{m_{1}\times...\times m_{M}\times n_{1}\times...\times n_{N}}

(\mathcal{A}\boxtimes\mathcal{B})[i_{1},...,i_{M},j_{1},...,j_{N}]:=\sum\limits_{l_{1},...,l_{K}=1}^{k_{1},...,k_{K}}\mathcal{A}[i_{1},...,i_{M},l_{1},...,l_{k}]\mathcal{B}[l_{1},...,l_{k},j_{1},...,j_{N}].

Note that when

From here on out, we will denote the set of order

(t-Einstein Product)

For hypermatrices
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\in\mathcal{T}_{k}^{(n,p)}

-Einstein product

Note that when

reduces to the normal

-product

(see equation

). Moreover, note that similarly as before, applying the FFT tubewise in the last mode, it turns out that the

\mathcal{A}

in the frequency domain; that is,

\widehat{\mathcal{A}\boxtimes_{t}\mathcal{B}}_{(l)}=\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{(l)}\boxtimes\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{(l)}\qquad\forall l\in[p].

For clarity and completeness, we write the following algorithm for obtaining the

Definition 4.3

For hypermatrices
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{T}_{k}^{(n,p)}

Definition 4.5
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an order

\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{\overbrace{n\times...\times n}^{k\text{ times}}\times\overbrace{n\times...\times n}^{k\text{ times}}}

Theorem 4.6

(Cubically Balanced Matrices Lift to Hypermatrix Algebra via t-Einstein Product)
.

Let
k
be any positive integer,
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{T}_{k}^{(n,p)}
, and define
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}:=\mathrm{fft}_{2k+1}(\mathcal{A})
. Then the map

\displaystyle\Phi:\mathcal{T}_{k}^{(n,p)}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\bigoplus\limits_{k=1}^{p}\mathcal{M}_{n^{k}}(\mathbb{C})

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle\mapsto(M_{1},...,M_{p})\quad\text{with}\quad M_{l}:=M_{\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{(l)}}^{cb}\quad\forall l\in[p]

is an involutive algebra isomorphism with respect to the generalized

-conjugate transpose. Moreover,
\Phi\big|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}^{(n,p)}}
defines an algebra isomorphism (over
\mathbb{R}
) between
\mathcal{H}_{k}^{(n,p)}
and
\bigoplus\limits_{l=1}^{p}\mathrm{Herm}(n^{k})
.

Proof.

First, note that since addition and scalar multiplication is defined componentwise in
\mathcal{T}_{k}^{(n,p)}
and since
M^{cb}
is a linear isomorphism, for any
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{C}
and
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\in\mathcal{T}_{k}^{(n,p)}
we immediately have

, proving that
\Phi
is linear; moreover, since the FFT is merely a change of basis transformation, the map
\Phi
is injective, hence a linear isomorphism. Next, since
\mathcal{A}\boxtimes_{t}\mathcal{B}
slice-wise reduces to the Einstein product in the frequency domain (applying FFT along tubal modes), it immediately follows that
\Phi(\mathcal{A}\boxtimes_{t}\mathcal{B})=\Phi(\mathcal{A})\Phi(\mathcal{B})
, proving that
\Phi
is an algebra isomorphism. Lastly, note that by definition of cubically balanced matricization, the generalized conjugate transpose of an even order cubical hypermatrix corresponds to the conjugate transpose of its corresponding cubically balanced matricization, hence it follows that
\Phi(\mathcal{A}^{H})=\Phi(\mathcal{A})^{H}
, making
\Phi
an involutive algebra isomorphism with respect to the generalized t-conjugate transpose; furthermore, from this fact about cubically balanced matricization, it also follows that
\Phi\big|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}^{(n,p)}}
is an isomorphism between
\mathcal{H}_{k}^{(n,p)}
and
\bigoplus\limits_{l=1}^{p}\mathrm{Herm}(n^{k})
, which recall is a real-vector space.
∎

Corollary 4.7

\mathcal{I}_{n,k}[i_{1},...,i_{k},j_{1},...,j_{k}]=\prod\limits_{l=1}^{k}\delta_{i_{l}j_{l}}.

Proof.

First, note that the identity in
\mathcal{M}_{n^{k}}(\mathbb{C})
is

, and also note that for any

. Furthermore, suppose

is a commutant degree
k

t
-Hermitian form, and assume in particular that the joint MTU diagonalization of

has the following form:

\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{(l)}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n^{k}}\lambda_{i}^{(l)}\overline{\mathcal{U}_{i}}\circ\mathcal{U}_{i}

for each

. Then in the frequency domain,

\widehat{h_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathrm{Ev}_{Z}(\mathcal{X}))}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n^{k}}\bm{\lambda_{i}}\odot\mathbf{u}_{i},

with
\odot
denoting the Hadamard product,

\bm{\lambda_{i}}[l]=\lambda_{i}^{(l)}\qquad\forall l\in[p],

and

\mathbf{u}_{i}(l):=\left|\mathcal{U}_{i}*\left(\mathrm{ev}_{z^{(l)}}(\widehat{\mathcal{X}}_{(l)}),...,\mathrm{ev}_{z^{(l)}}(\widehat{\mathcal{X}}_{(l)})\right)\right|^{2}\qquad\forall l\in[p].

Proof.

Observe that for each
l\in[p]
, we have that

\displaystyle\widehat{h_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathrm{Ev}_{Z}(\mathcal{X}))}[l]=\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{(l)}*\left(\overline{\mathrm{ev}_{z^{(l)}}(\widehat{\mathcal{X}}_{(l)})},...,\overline{\mathrm{ev}_{z^{(l)}}(\widehat{\mathcal{X}}_{(l)})},\mathrm{ev}_{z^{(l)}}(\widehat{\mathcal{X}}_{(l)}),...,\mathrm{ev}_{z^{(l)}}(\widehat{\mathcal{X}}_{(l)})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n^{k}}\lambda_{i}^{(l)}\left[(\overline{\mathcal{U}_{i}}\circ\mathcal{U}_{i})*\left(\overline{\mathrm{ev}_{z^{(l)}}(\widehat{\mathcal{X}}_{(l)})},...,\overline{\mathrm{ev}_{z^{(l)}}(\widehat{\mathcal{X}}_{(l)})},\mathrm{ev}_{z^{(l)}}(\widehat{\mathcal{X}}_{(l)}),...,\mathrm{ev}_{z^{(l)}}(\widehat{\mathcal{X}}_{(l)})\right)\right],

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\text{by assumption and multilinearity of }*

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n^{k}}\lambda_{i}^{(l)}\bigg[\underbrace{\bigg(\overline{\mathcal{U}_{i}}*\left(\overline{\mathrm{ev}_{z^{(l)}}(\widehat{\mathcal{X}}_{(l)})},...,\overline{\mathrm{ev}_{z^{(l)}}(\widehat{\mathcal{X}}_{(l)})}\right)\bigg)}_{\in\mathbb{C}}\circ\underbrace{\bigg(\mathcal{U}_{i}*\left(\mathrm{ev}_{z^{(l)}}(\widehat{\mathcal{X}}_{(l)}),...,\mathrm{ev}_{z^{(l)}}(\widehat{\mathcal{X}}_{(l)}\right)\bigg)}_{\in\mathbb{C}}\bigg],\quad\text{by Lemma }\ref{Outer Product - Multilinmult Relation}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n^{k}}\lambda_{i}^{(l)}\bigg(\overline{\mathcal{U}_{i}*\left(\mathrm{ev}_{z^{(l)}}(\widehat{\mathcal{X}}_{(l)}),...,\mathrm{ev}_{z^{(l)}}(\widehat{\mathcal{X}}_{(l)})\right)}\bigg)\bigg(\mathcal{U}_{i}*\left(\mathrm{ev}_{z^{(l)}}(\widehat{\mathcal{X}}_{(l)}),...,\mathrm{ev}_{z^{(l)}}(\widehat{\mathcal{X}}_{(l)})\right)\bigg)

Corollary 4.23

(Sufficient Condition for
t
-Hermitian Positivity)
.

Suppose
\mathcal{A}\in\mathrm{HSP}_{k}^{(n,p)}
is joint MTU diagonalizable. If each of
\mathcal{A}
’s
t
-matrix-tensor eigenvalues are positive, then
\mathcal{A}
is
t
-Hermitian positive definite.

Proof.

Suppose that each of the
t
-matrix-tensor eigenvalues of
\mathcal{A}
are positive; i.e.,
\lambda_{i}^{(l)}>0
for each
i\in[n^{k}]
and
l\in[p]
. In order to show that
\mathcal{A}
is
t
-Hermitian positive definite, we need to prove that for every
Z\in(\mathbb{C}^{n})^{p}
such that
w^{(l)}:=\mathrm{Ev}_{z^{(l)}}(\widehat{\mathcal{X}}_{(l)})\neq 0
for each
l\in[p]
, we have that

h_{\mathcal{A}}^{(l)}(w^{(l)})>0,

or equivalently that

\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{(l)}*\left(\overline{\mathrm{ev}_{z^{(l)}}(\widehat{\mathcal{X}}_{(l)})},...,\overline{\mathrm{ev}_{z^{(l)}}(\widehat{\mathcal{X}}_{(l)})},\mathrm{ev}_{z^{(l)}}(\widehat{\mathcal{X}}_{(l)}),...,\mathrm{ev}_{z^{(l)}}(\widehat{\mathcal{X}}_{(l)})\right)>0

for each
l\in[p]
. From the proof of the theorem above, this is equivalent to proving that

\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n^{k}}\lambda_{i}^{(l)}\mathbf{u}_{i}(l)>0

for each
l\in[p]
, where recall
\mathbf{u}_{i}(l):=\left|\mathcal{U}_{i}*\left(\mathrm{ev}_{z^{(l)}}(\widehat{\mathcal{X}}_{(l)}),...,\mathrm{ev}_{z^{(l)}}(\widehat{\mathcal{X}}_{(l)})\right)\right|^{2}
.

First, note that by definition
\mathbf{u}_{i}(l)\geq 0
for each
i\in[n^{k}]
and each
l\in[p]
; moreover, we claim that for each
l\in[p]
, atleast one of the
\mathbf{u}_{i}(l)
must be strictly positive. To see this, let
z\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
be nonzero and consider the linear functional on arbitrary order
k
hypermatrices

\displaystyle\Xi_{z}:\mathbb{C}^{n\times...\times n}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}

\displaystyle\mapsto\mathcal{U}*(\underbrace{z,...,z}_{k\text{ times}})

In general
\Xi
is nonzero because for instance on the rank-1 tensor
\mathcal{Z}:=z\circ...\circ z\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times...\times n}
, we have that

\Xi_{z}(\mathcal{Z})=(z\circ...\circ z)*(z,...,z)=(z*z)\circ...\circ(z*z)=\langle z,z\rangle^{k}>0

(with the second equality due to Lemma
4.21
and the third equality due to the definition of multilinear matrix multiplication). Now, since
\{\mathrm{hvec}(\mathcal{U}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n^{k}}
forms an orthonormal basis for
\mathbb{C}^{n^{k}}
, it follows from the inverse matricization map that
\{\mathcal{U}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n^{k}}
forms an orthonormal (with respect to the Frobenius norm) basis for
\mathbb{C}^{n\times...\times n}
.

If there exists
l\in[p]
such that
\mathbf{u}_{i}(l)
(which are just instances of
|\Xi_{z}(\mathcal{U}_{i})|^{2}
for some nonzero
z\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
) is
0
for each
i\in[n^{k}]
, then
\Xi_{z}
would be identically
0
on
\mathbb{C}^{n\times...\times n}
, which we know cannot be. Therefore, for each
l\in[p]
, there must exist atleast one such
\mathbf{u}_{i}(l)
which is strictly positive, from which it follows that

\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n^{k}}\lambda_{i}^{(l)}\mathbf{u}_{i}(l)>0,

completing the proof.
∎

Remark 4.24
.

Note that this is an interesting and non-trivial result because in general the tensor eigenvalues of an arbitrary even order hypermatrix do
not
coincide with its matrix-tensor eigenvalues. Indeed, perhaps surprisingly, there exists
t
-Hermitian positive definite hypermatrices which have negative
t
-matrix-tensor eigenvalues (as we demonstrate in the example below). That is, assuming positivity of the
t
-matrix-tensor eigenvalues is a
stronger
assumption than assuming positivity of
t
-
\widehat{H}
tensor eigenvalues. Thus, in this setting, the notion of positivity admits a spectral hierarchy!

Example 4.25

(Converse Fails)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{2\times 2\times 2\times 2\times 2}
be such that

\mathcal{A}[:,:,:,:,1]=\mathcal{A}[:,:,:,:,2].

That is, the two frontal slices of
\mathcal{A}
are identical; denote each of them as
\mathcal{S}
. Now, suppose in the frequency domain each frontal slice of
\mathcal{A}
is of the form:

\widehat{\mathcal{S}}[1,1,1,1]=\widehat{\mathcal{S}}[2,2,2,2]=1,

\widehat{\mathcal{S}}[1,2,1,2]=\widehat{\mathcal{S}}[1,2,2,1]=\widehat{\mathcal{S}}[2,1,1,2]=\widehat{\mathcal{S}}[2,1,2,1]=c

for some
c\in\mathbb{R}
, and all other entries are
0
. Since the FFT implements a linear isomorphism, it follows that
\mathcal{A}
is
t
-Hermitian (se Remark
4.4
), and furthermore, it follows that
\mathcal{S}
is partially symmetric, hence
\mathcal{A}
is
t
-Hermitian partially symmetric. Moreover, by Proposition
4.15
it follows that
\mathcal{A}
is joint MTU diagonalizable since
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{H}_{2}^{(n,p)}
and its frontal slices are identically (hence they trivially commute).

Let us now consider the unique degree
2
Hermitian form associated with
\widehat{\mathcal{S}}
; it is of the form:

h_{\widehat{\mathcal{S}}}(x_{1},x_{2})=\sum\limits_{i_{1},i_{2},j_{1},j_{2}=1}^{2}\mathcal{S}[i_{1},i_{2},j_{1},j_{2}]\overline{x_{i_{1}}}\overline{x_{i_{2}}}x_{j_{1}}x_{j_{2}}.

Let
z:=x_{1}
and
w:=x_{2}
. Then we have that

h_{\widehat{\mathcal{S}}}(z,w)=|z|^{4}+|w|^{4}+4c|z||w|.

Setting
c:=-\frac{1}{4}
, this reduces to

h_{\widehat{\mathcal{S}}}(z,w)=|z|^{4}+|w|^{4}-|z||w|.

From the (complex) AM-GM inequality, it follows that
|z|^{4}+|w|^{4}\geq 2|z||w|^{2}
, hence

h_{\widehat{\mathcal{S}}}(z,w)=|z|^{4}+|w|^{4}-|z||w|\geq 2|z||w|-|z||w|=|z||w|.

For any nonzero complex vector
\left[\begin{array}[]{c}z\\
w\end{array}\right]\in\mathbb{C}^{2}

z
and
w
are nonzero, then
|z||w|>0

; on the other hand, if one of
z
or
w
is
0

, then
|z|^{4}>0
and
|z||w|=0
, also implying that
h_{\widehat{\mathcal{S}}}(z,w)>0
. Thus, in either case, it follows that at
c=-\frac{1}{4}
,
\mathcal{A}
is
t
-Hermitian positive definite.

However, now considering the cubically balanced matricization
M_{\widehat{\mathcal{S}}}^{cb}=:M
, it follows from the definition of cubically balanced matricization that

M=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}1&0&0&0\\
0&c&c&0\\
0&c&c&0\\
0&0&0&1\end{array}\right].

It’s eigenvalues are
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}=1
,
\lambda_{3}=2c
, and
\lambda_{4}=0
. Therefore, setting
c:=-\frac{1}{4}
,

has a negative eigenvalue of
-\frac{1}{2}
. Thus, despite being
t
-Hermitian positive definite,
\mathcal{A}
has a negative
t
-matrix-tensor eigenvalue.

5
Conclusion

In this work, we introduced arbitrary degree
t
-Hermitian forms via a synthesis of the
t
-product and the multilinear matrix product. We established their canonical correspondence with higher order Hermitian partially symmetric hypermatrices and demonstrated that, under the FFT, they decompose into a collection of Hermitian forms. For commutant
t
-Hermitian forms, we derived a spectral decomposition of these forms, arising from a generalized tensor-eigendecomposition of Hermitian tensors. Our analysis of the consequences of this result revealed a spectral hierarchy: tensor eigenvalues characterize Hermitian positivity of the corresponding hypermatrix, while positivity of the associated matrix-tensor eigenvalues provides a strictly stronger sufficient condition for Hermitian positivity. This suggests several directions for further research, including identifying the structural conditions under which the two notions of positivity coincide and exploring the implications of this spectral hierarchy.

Lastly, the isomorphism
\mathcal{T}_{k}^{(n,p)}\cong\bigoplus\limits_{l=1}^{p}\mathcal{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, together with the
t
-determinant mentioned in Remark
2.33
, allows one to define the ”
t
-general linear group”

\mathrm{GL}_{t}(n,p):=\{\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n\times p}:\det_{t}(\mathcal{A})\neq 0\},

which by
\Phi
is canonically isomorphic to
\mathrm{GL}(n)^{\oplus p}
. One may then readily define other
t
-analogues of subgroups of the general linear group. Such groups naturally act on
\mathcal{T}_{k}^{(n,p)}
and
\mathcal{F}_{k,t}
at the slice-level, while groups such as
\mathrm{GL}(p)
act tubewise. Investigating the interaction of such slice-wise and tubal actions may provide a useful framework for developing invariants on
\mathcal{T}_{k}^{(n,p)}
, or possibly shed light on the intricate spectral properties emergent in
\mathcal{F}_{k,t}
.
