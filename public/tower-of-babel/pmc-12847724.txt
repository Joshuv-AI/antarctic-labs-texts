Exploring the aluminium paradox in calcium-aluminosilicate-hydrate decalcification
Authors: Yong Tao, Yining Gao, Mohammad Javad Abdolhosseini Qomi, Roland J.-M. Pellenq, Chi Sun Poon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Decalcification, a prevalent form of mineral dissolution, drives the long-term degradation of cementitious materials in civil and energy infrastructures and controls the weathering of silicates in Earth’s crust. The impact of aluminium (Al) incorporation on the decalcification of complex minerals like calcium-silicate-hydrates, the predominant binding phase in concrete, remains debated. This study combines metadynamics simulations, density functional theory calculations, and experimental characterizations to unravel the atomistic mechanisms of Al governing calcium-aluminosilicate-hydrate (C-A-S-H) decalcification. We reveal a two-fold effect of Al incorporation stemming from aluminosilicate chain variations. Increasing Al at a constant Ca/(Si+Al) ratio distorts aluminosilicate chains, weakening Ca-O bonds and accelerating decalcification. Conversely, increasing Al at a constant Ca/Si ratio leads to longer aluminosilicate chains, enhancing Ca surface restraints and hindering decalcification. Additionally, the preferential dissolution of Na ions effectively suppresses the dissolution of Ca ions. Our calculated activation free energies quantitatively predict experimental C-(A)-S-H decalcification kinetics across various temperatures. This work provides a framework for tailoring the composition of complex minerals for controllable degradation and offers fundamental knowledge relevant to broader geological and environmental processes.

Introduction

Mineral dissolution is a fundamental process shaping Earth’s surface and influencing a wide range of natural and engineered systems. From rock weathering of the global carbon cycle1 and the formation of soils2 to the degradation of modern infrastructures3 and the release of contaminants into the environment4, the rate at which minerals dissolve dictates the long-term evolution of these systems. Understanding the factors controlling dissolution kinetics is therefore crucial for predicting the fate of minerals in various contexts and developing strategies to enhance or mitigate this process depending on the application. Most minerals, however, exhibit significant variations in bonding environments and accessibility of ions at different crystallographic orientations. This results in anisotropic dissolution5, preferential etching along specific planes, and complex interactions with interlayer species, making it difficult to apply simplified models that assume uniform dissolution rates.

Calcium-silicate-hydrate (C-S-H), the predominant binding phase of ubiquitous concrete jungles, is an excellent model for studying the dissolution of complex minerals. Its disordered structure, variable composition, and sensitivity to environmental conditions make it a particularly challenging yet relevant material to investigate. C-S-H exhibits a layered nanostructure composed of intralayer calcium ions, defective silicate chains, and interlayer water and ions6–8. The diverse packing arrangements and densities of C-S-H nanoparticles create a multiscale porous network that binds other crystalline phases to form a high-strength composite9,10 (Fig. 1a, b). From a chemistry perspective, the bonding strength of the calcium-oxygen (Ca-O) bond is significantly weaker than that of the silicon-oxygen (Si-O) bond11, making Ca ions the Achilles heel in the C-S-H structure. Decalcification, i.e., the leaching of Ca ions from the C-S-H structure, has been recognized as a major degradation mechanism in concrete infrastructures, leading to reduced strength, increased permeability, and ultimately, compromised structural integrity12–15. Aluminium (Al), vastly present in Earth’s crust and engineered materials, is one of the few elements that alter the layered molecular structure of C-S-H and influence its decalcification behavior in a fundamental manner19. Due to similarities in coordination structures, it is widely accepted that Al can partially substitute Si in C-S-H without disrupting its layered structure, forming calcium-aluminosilicate-hydrate (C-A-S-H) gel20 as shown in Fig. 1c. Recent studies show that the Al content in C-A-S-H increases as the cement paste ages21. However, unlike silicon’s consistent four-coordinated structure, experimental and theoretical studies reveal that Al in C-A-S-H exhibits multiple coordination numbers from 4 to 622,23. The inherent differences in the electronic structures of Al and Si lead to discrepancies in their atomic charges and bonding characteristics within C-A-S-H24,25. Although the structure of C-A-S-H has been deciphered from the molecular scale24, the understanding of the impact of Al incorporation on C-A-S-H decalcification remains limited to macroscopic observations26. More importantly, some studies suggest that Al enhances ion adsorption and improves durability27,28, while others indicate that Al promotes decalcification26. Such paradoxical reports on Al’s role in C-A-S-H decalcification hinder our understanding and ability to mitigate the degradation of C-A-S-H, thereby compromising the long-term safety of concrete infrastructures.

Employing nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy combined with first-principles calculations and atomistic simulations, previous studies have elucidated the diverse coordination environments of Al in C-A-S-H, demonstrating that Al primarily substitutes bridging Si atoms within the silicate chains19,24,29,30. These understandings provide a crucial foundation for investigating C-A-S-H decalcification at the molecular level. The critical link between molecular-scale Ca ion dissolution and macroscopic decalcification kinetics lies in the thermodynamic free energy landscape. However, obtaining such information through conventional molecular dynamics (MD) simulations is challenging due to the limited timescale (typically within tens of nanoseconds) that restricts the sampling of the entire configuration space31. Recently, enhanced sampling methods like metadynamics32 have emerged as powerful tools for obtaining thermodynamic and kinetic information about mineral dissolution33–37. Unlike other methods reliant on predefined reaction pathways, metadynamics does not require such predefinitions, making it particularly advantageous for simulating complex processes like mineral dissolution.

In this study, we utilize well-tempered metadynamics38 techniques to unravel the underlying mechanisms and free energy landscape of C-A-S-H decalcification at the atomistic level. To comprehend the impact of Al incorporation, we compare two methods of introducing Al into the C-S-H molecular model with a Ca/Si ratio of 1.25 (denoted as C1.25SH). The resultant C-A-S-H models possess compositions of C1.25A0.2S0.8H (Al substitution scenario) and C1.25A0.2SH (Al addition scenario), which respectively feature identical Ca/(Si+Al) and Ca/Si ratios with the original C1.25SH (Fig. 1d). By revealing the relationship between decalcification free energy and surface characteristics under different Al incorporation scenarios, we will establish a unified fundamental understanding of how Al influences C-A-S-H degradation under complex compositions. Additionally, we attempt to bridge the gap between molecular simulations and experimental observations by considering the complexity of surface configurations to realistically represent C-A-S-H decalcification thermodynamics, overcoming the inherent limitations of MD simulations in quantitatively predicting dissolution kinetics. The insights and methods will provide a framework for tailoring the composition of layered materials for enhanced durability.

Results

Experimental characteristics of C-(A)-S-H decalcification

The decalcification process of synthesized C-(A)-S-H samples was investigated by dissolving them in pure water, followed by careful characterization of the crystal structures and atomic coordination structures (Fig. 2). By comparing the decalcification rates and equilibrium decalcification degrees of C1.25SH, C1.25A0.2SH, and C1.25A0.2S0.8H, we observe that the C-A-S-H samples with two different modes of Al incorporation decalcify differently (Fig. 2a). The Al substitution scenario (C1.25A0.2S0.8H) exhibits a significantly enhanced decalcification rate and degree compared to the control group (C1.25SH), whereas the Al addition scenario (C1.25A0.2SH) demonstrates the opposite effect. We performed X-ray diffraction (XRD) and NMR analyses to understand the evolution of the crystal structures and coordination structures of C-(A)-S-H during decalcification (Fig. 2b–e). The molecular configurations of Si and Al ions in different environments in C-(A)-S-H are illustrated in Fig. S1. As shown in Fig. 2b, the C1.25SH sample exhibits partially crystalline XRD patterns, a typical characteristic of C-S-H at low Ca/Si ratios. The measured interlayer spacing of the silicate sheet is 14.41 Å, closely resembling the 14 Å spacing observed in tobermorite crystals. Al incorporation distorts the original silicate chains in C-S-H, resulting in poorer crystallinity of C-A-S-H compared to C-S-H, agreeing with previous studies39. The absence of Q3 peaks in the NMR spectrum of C1.25SH (Fig. 2d) indicates a lack of cross-linking between silicate chains, a typical feature of lab-synthesized C-S-H40. Al incorporation promotes silicate chain lengthening and cross-linking, leading to the emergence of Q3-type silicate tetrahedra, which is in line with previous studies22,26.

Following decalcification, the interlayer spacing of C1.25SH decreases to 12.03 Å (Fig. 2c), suggesting that dissolved Ca ions primarily originate from the interlayer. The XRD patterns of C1.25A0.2SH and C1.25A0.2S0.8H remain largely unchanged except for slight smoothing, indicating that the structural framework of decalcified C-A-S-H remains largely intact with slightly improved crystallinity. The decrease in Q1 content, accompanied by an increase in Q2, Q3, and Q4 suggests further polymerization of aluminosilicate chains into a three-dimensional network structure within the decalcified C-(A)-S-H (Fig. 2d). Furthermore, the hexacoordinated Al content in C-A-S-H decreases after decalcification (Fig. 2e), implying that this configuration is relatively unstable.

The simulation snapshots show that the dissolution of Ca ions is accompanied by free hydroxyl groups from the surface (Fig. 3d), which agrees with the common experimental phenomenon that C-(A)-S-H dissolution leads to an alkalinity increase of the solution. The specific free energies of all intermediate and transition states (TS) extracted from Fig. 3a–c are summarized in Fig. 3e. The inner-sphere adsorbed state (S2) is found to be the most stable state during the Ca dissolution process, which is also true for barite dissolution as reported by previous studies42. For C1.25SH, the global free energy barrier for Ca dissolution (ΔAmax, defined as the energy difference between the lowest intermediate state and the highest transition state) is governed by TS1, i.e., the transition from surface-adsorbed state to inner-sphere adsorbed state, while TS2 defines the global energy barrier for C1.25A0.2S0.8H and C1.25A0.2SH systems. The calculated total dissolution free energy barriers are 58.2 kJ/mol, 40.1 kJ/mol, and 37.9 kJ/mol for C1.25A0.2SH, C1.25SH, and C1.25A0.2S0.8H, respectively. This energetic ordering suggests that the decalcification rate follows: C1.25A0.2SH < C1.25SH < C1.25A0.2S0.8H, aligning perfectly with the experimental observations from the C-(A)-S-H decalcification experiments (Fig. 2a). The incorporation of Al into the C-S-H structure clearly exerts a two-fold effect on the free energy barriers governing decalcification. To elucidate the fundamental mechanisms by which Al modifies the decalcification process at the atomic level, a detailed analysis of the factors contributing to these energetic differences is essential, which is the focus of the following section.

Two-fold effect of Al incorporation

To understand how Al incorporation impacts C-A-S-H decalcification energetics, we performed a detailed analysis of the chemical and geometrical characteristics of the C-(A)-S-H surfaces. Excluding water molecules, we categorized oxygen atoms on the C-(A)-S-H surface into two groups: oxygen in isolated hydroxyl groups (Oh) and oxygen within the aluminosilicate chains. The latter category was further subdivided into bridging (Ob), non-bridging (Onb), and silanol/aluminol group (Ohs) sites (Fig. 4a). We collectively refer to Ob, Onb, and Ohs as silicate oxygen (Os). The dissolution rate of minerals is determined by the net rate of particle detachment and attachment at the surface, which is governed by the chemical and geometric restraints of surface atoms43. Our simulations and previous studies on belite dissolution44,45 reveal that during the dissolution process, Ca ions gradually detach from coordinated Os ions, while coordinated Oh ions depart the surface along with the Ca ions. This behavior implies that Ca-Os bonds act as primary restraints that hinder the dissolution of surface Ca ions. In this regard, the free energy barrier of Ca dissolution is governed by both the number and strength of these Ca-Os bonds. Compared to C1.25SH, Al incorporation in C1.25A0.2SH leads to an increased Ob content (Fig. 4b), which is expected as the “Al addition” scenario elongates the aluminosilicate chains. Simultaneously, the Oh content decreases, which aligns with previous studies reporting reduced hydroxide content upon Al incorporation into C-S-H20. In the case of C1.25A0.2S0.8H, the Onb content decreases, coupled with an increase in Ohs content. This is because the introduced sodium (Na) content is not sufficient to compensate for the charge imbalance introduced by Al substituting Si (see the elemental composition in Table S1). Additional protons are required for charge compensation, which convert Onb into Ohs sites. The pronounced increase in Ob content in C1.25A0.2SH results in an increase in the number of Ca-Os bonds for surface Ca ions (Fig. 4c). In contrast, the number of Ca-Os bonds for surface Ca ions in C1.25A0.2S0.8H is similar to that in C1.25SH. These Ca-Os bonds serve as surface restraints for Ca dissolution, consequently contributing to the free energy barrier for Ca dissolution. The dependence of Ca dissolution free energy barriers on surface restraints has been confirmed in previous studies3,45.

To further examine the structural variations in calcium coordination caused by Al incorporation, we analyzed Ca-Os pair distribution functions (Fig. 4d–f). The main peaks within a pair distance of 3 Å demonstrate the nearest oxygen neighbors (i.e., coordinating oxygen) of Ca, which directly reflects the Ca-Os bond length. Generally, the Ca-Onb bond length (Fig. 4e) is smaller than the Ca-Ob and Ca-Ohs bond lengths, suggesting the Ca-Onb interactions are stronger. This is because the Onb ions are not charge-balanced compared to Ob and Ohs, which form stronger electrostatic attractions with Ca ions. In C1.25SH, there are fewer Ca-Ob bonds within a coordination distance of 3 Å, while C1.25A0.2SH and C1.25A0.2S0.8H feature distinct Ca-Ob bond distributions at ~ 2.65 Å. This is due to the structural distortion of aluminosilicate chains caused by Al incorporation, which agrees with the decreased crystallinity in the XRD patterns (Fig. 2b). In addition, the broader Ca-Ohs bond distributions in C1.25A0.2SH and C1.25A0.2S0.8H also confirm the structural distortion of aluminosilicate chains.

Since Onb is the predominant type of Os (Fig. 4b), Ca-Onb bonds contribute most significantly to the surface restraints for Ca ions. The slightly longer Ca-Onb bonds in C1.25A0.2SH and C1.25A0.2S0.8H (Fig. 4e) suggest weaker Ca-Onb interactions in these systems. The negative relationship between bond length and bond strength is evidenced by the bond length–bond order distributions in Fig. 2g. Bond order, defined as the difference between the numbers of electron pairs in bonding and antibonding molecular orbitals, was calculated with density functional theory (DFT) using the Mulliken method46, which is positively related to the strength of chemical bonds47. The statistical analysis of Ca-Os bond lengths and bond orders (Table 1) outlines broader bond length distributions and smaller bond orders in C1.25A0.2SH and C1.25A0.2S0.8H compared to C1.25SH. Therefore, a comprehensive understanding of C-(A)-S-H decalcification kinetics and thermodynamics necessitates considering the two-fold effect of Al incorporation, i.e., the number and strength of Ca-Os bonds. In the case of the “Al substitution” scenario (C1.25A0.2S0.8H), the decrease in Ca-Os bond strength is significant, while the number of Ca-Os bonds remains relatively unchanged compared to C1.25SH. Therefore, C1.25A0.2S0.8H exhibits enhanced susceptibility to decalcification. In contrast, although the “Al addition” scenario (C1.25A0.2SH) also exhibits a decrease in Ca-Os bond strength, this effect is counterbalanced by a considerable increase in Ca-Os bonds, which dominantly renders C1.25A0.2SH more resistant to decalcification compared to C1.25SH.

Quantitative prediction of C-(A)-S-H decalcification kinetics

To validate the impact of these Al-induced surface property changes on C-A-S-H decalcification thermodynamics, we calculated the dissolution free energy profiles for all surface Ca ions in each C-(A)-S-H system. As expected, the activation free energies (ΔA) of Ca dissolution exhibit a strong correlation with the initial number of Ca-Os bonds (Fig. 5a). The overall higher free energy observed for C1.25A0.2SH arises from the presence of numerous Ca sites on the surface with a higher number of Ca-Os bonds. The influence of Ca-Os bond order on ΔA is less pronounced than the effect of the number of Ca-Os bonds (Fig. 5b). This observation supports the notion that C1.25A0.2SH decalcification is primarily governed by the increase in Ca-Os bonds. In contrast, the Ca-Os bond order in C1.25A0.2S0.8H is generally smaller compared to C1.25SH, resulting in overall lower ΔA. Role of Na leaching in C-(A)-S-H decalcification

In C-(A)-S-H systems, the incorporation of Al is frequently associated with the presence of Na ions as charge-balancing species23,25,49, which are susceptible to leaching as widely recognized in alkali-activated materials50,51. Therefore, we also characterized Na leaching behaviors experimentally and calculated their influence on C-(A)-S-H decalcification thermodynamics. The comparison of Ca and Na leaching kinetics across C1.25SH, C1.25A0.2SH, and C1.25A0.2S0.8H (Fig. 2a and Fig. 6a) reveals that both the leaching rates and equilibrium leaching degrees of Na ions are substantially greater than those of Ca ions. As demonstrated by 23Na NMR spectroscopy (Fig. 6b), the almost complete disappearance of Na signals in the leached C1.25SH system indicates a near-total removal of Na ions, consistent with its high leaching degree (>80%) observed in Fig. 6a. Conversely, in the leached C1.25A0.2SH and C1.25A0.2S0.8H systems, the persistence of Na signals suggests that a significant fraction of Na ions remains in the solid phase, corresponding to their lower Na leaching degrees (~50%) in Fig. 6a. To gain further insight into the thermodynamics underlying Na dissolution and its impact on C-(A)-S-H decalcification, we calculated ΔA and rate constants for three representative scenarios (Fig. 6c and Fig. 6d): 1) single Ca ion leaching; 2) single Na ion leaching; and 3) sequential Ca ion leaching after Na ion removal. The calculated free energy barrier for Na dissolution (20.4 kJ/mol) is markedly lower than that for Ca dissolution (43.4 kJ/mol), which agrees with their experimentally observed leaching kinetics. Importantly, when Na ion is preferentially leached, the subsequent energy barrier for Ca ion dissolution increases to 48.0 kJ/mol, which is due to the enhanced electrostatic attraction between Ca ion and the negatively charged aluminosilicate chain generated upon Na ion removal. The calculated dissolution rate constants further corroborate these findings: the intrinsic rate constant for Na dissolution (2.07 × 107 s-1) significantly exceeds that for Ca ion (3.49 × 104 s-1), while the rate constant for Ca ion dissolution following Na ion removal decreases further to 1.05 × 104 s-1. These results indicate that the preferential dissolution of Na ions in C-(A)-S-H phases effectively suppresses the dissolution of Ca ions. It may, to some extent, explain the better durability of alkali-activated materials with C-N, K-A-S-H (N for sodium and K for potassium) as the main hydration products compared to Portland cement systems52.

Discussion

Our simulations reveal that the thermodynamic barriers governing C-(A)-S-H decalcification are intricately linked to the coordination environments of surface Ca ions. These environments, in turn, are sculpted by the presence and nature of structural defects within the C-(A)-S-H matrix, such as the length and distortion of the aluminosilicate chains. These structural features are to a significant extent dictated by the Ca/Si and Al/Si ratios within the C-(A)-S-H structure. Therefore, previous investigations have extensively explored the relationship between Al/Si and Ca/Si ratios and the properties of C-(A)-S-H21,53,54. Building upon the fundamental two-fold effects of Al ions elucidated in this work, we can establish a more comprehensive understanding of how chemical composition dictates C-(A)-S-H decalcification behavior. The chemical composition of C-(A)-S-H can be generally represented as CxAySzHn, where C, A, S, and H denote Ca, Al, Si, and aqueous species (H2O, OH-), respectively. The nuanced role of composition can be demonstrated in two scenarios: First, for a constant Ca/(Al+Si) ratio, increasing the Al content at the expense of Si primarily results in increased distortion of the aluminosilicate chains, which weakens the Ca-Os bond. It further contributes to lowering the activation free energy of surface Ca dissolution, thereby promoting C-(A)-S-H decalcification. Second, for a constant Ca/Si ratio, increasing the Al content leads to a decrease in the Ca/(Al+Si) ratio. This compositional shift significantly results in the formation of longer aluminosilicate chains, which increases the number of Ca-Os bonds per surface Ca ion. This structural modification increases the activation free energy of Ca dissolution, consequently inhibiting C-(A)-S-H decalcification. Beyond solid-phase composition, pore solution chemistry—including pH, ionic strength, and cation content—significantly affects the decalcification kinetics of C-(A)-S-H. Incorporation of Al is typically accompanied by charge-balancing alkali ions such as Na and K ions23,25, whose dissolution elevates the pore solution pH and stabilizes the aluminosilicate chains, thereby inhibiting decalcification. This effect becomes more pronounced in alkali-rich C-(A)-S-H such as alkali-activated material systems51, where extensive leaching of alkali ions increases the negative surface charge, enhancing electrostatic interactions that further suppress Ca ions dissolution.

Therefore, the impact of Al incorporation on C-(A)-S-H decalcification is not inherently straightforward but rather depends on the specific composition of the reference C-S-H system. This understanding has significant implications for predicting the long-term performance of cement-based infrastructure, especially in aggressive environments. For instance, the high concentration of sulfates in marine environments can lead to sulfate attack55,56, a process that involves the ingress of sulfate ions and their reaction with C-(A)-S-H, leading to the formation of expansive products. This process can be exacerbated at high Al/Si ratios, as it facilitates the breakdown of the C-(A)-S-H structure and accelerates the ingress of sulfates. On the other hand, by carefully controlling the Al content and ensuring a relatively low Ca/(Al+Si) ratio, it is possible to enhance the durability of Al-rich infrastructures. For instance, the large-scale geological trapping of CO2, where CO2 is injected into the subsurface to react with sedimentary rocks to form stable carbonate minerals, requires the long-term stability of the caprock57,58, which often comprises clay-rich formations containing significant amounts of Al. Furthermore, the insights from this study can be extrapolated to understand the broader implications of mineral weathering in natural systems. The weathering of aluminosilicate minerals, a major component of the Earth’s crust, plays a crucial role in the global carbon cycle. During weathering, the dissolved atmospheric CO2 in rainwater reacts with aluminosilicate minerals, releasing Ca and other ions into solution, which are ultimately transported to the oceans, where they can be utilized by marine organisms to form their shells and skeletons. This silicate weathering process acts as a natural sink for atmospheric CO2 to regulate Earth’s climate over geological timescales59. By understanding the factors controlling the dissolution of aluminosilicate minerals, such as the role of Al substitution and its impact on surface reactivity, we can better predict the rates of weathering and their influence on the global carbon cycle.

The findings of this study have far-reaching implications, extending beyond the realm of cement chemistry and sustainable engineering. By elucidating the fundamental mechanisms governing C-(A)-S-H decalcification, we gain valuable insights into the broader processes of mineral dissolution, weathering, and their impact on the environment. These insights are crucial for addressing critical challenges such as climate change mitigation, sustainable infrastructure development, and the long-term stability of engineered systems in diverse environments.

Methods

Models and force field

The Ca/Si ratio of mature Portland cement paste typically ranges from 1.2 to 2.3, with a mean of ~1.7560. However, when clinker in cementitious formulations is partially replaced by supplementary cementitious materials, the hydration products often exhibit lower Ca/Si ratios, accompanied by the incorporation of Al into the structure54. This scenario forms the context of our study. Additionally, C-S-H with a Ca/Si ratio of 1.0 - 1.5 is commonly employed as a reference system in studies investigating C-(A)-S-H22,26,61. Therefore, we chose a Ca/Si ratio of 1.25 as representative of such systems. Since the structure of C-(A)-S-H gel is close to the crystalline layered mineral tobermorite except that C-(A)-S-H has a higher Ca/Si ratio and shorter aluminosilicate chains, the most widely accepted method for constructing amorphous C-S-H molecular models is to remove the bridge [SiO2] groups of the silicate chains from crystalline tobermorite3,6,8. Our XRD testing shows that the C1.25SH sample has an interlayer spacing of 14.4 Å. Accordingly, we adopted crystalline 14 Å tobermorite62 with a Ca/Si ratio of 0.83 as a prototype to construct amorphous models of C-S-H and C-A-S-H. The triclinic primitive cell of 14 Å tobermorite was first converted into an orthorhombic unit cell and then extended to a supercell with dimensions of ~ 25 Å × 30 Å × 35 Å, which represents the nanoscale C-(A)-S-H particles observed in real systems10. These dimensions ensure that the models capture the structural and chemical heterogeneity of C-(A)-S-H surfaces while remaining computationally feasible. Bridge site [SiO2] groups were partially removed from the infinite silicate chains to match the realistic Ca/Si ratio and silicate chain length of C1.25SH. For the C-A-S-H models, Al ions were introduced into the C1.25SH structure using two different approaches (Fig. 1d): The first method involved partially replacing the bridging Si with Al to achieve a target Al/(Al+Si) ratio of 0.2. The second method was introducing an amount of [AlO2] to the bridge sites in C1.25SH to obtain an Al/Si ratio of 0.2. The former and latter models were labeled as C1.25A0.2S0.8H and C1.25A0.2SH, respectively. It is worth noting that for any given bulk composition, multiple atomic configurations of the amorphous C-(A)-S-H could exist. However, the macroscopic properties of C-(A)-S-H are predominantly determined by its chemical composition (e.g., Ca/Si ratio), rather than by the subtle variations between different random configurations of the same composition. For example, a landmark study by Pellenq et al.8 used grand canonical Monte Carlo simulations to generate numerous C-S-H structures. Their results clearly showed that the formation free energies of different cluster configurations are very tightly clustered for a given Ca/Si ratio, while there are significant energy differences between structures with different Ca/Si ratios. This demonstrates that structures with the same composition are, for practical purposes, thermodynamically equivalent. Therefore, our three models are not merely three random samples; they are chosen to be representative of three distinct thermodynamic and chemical families of C-(A)-S-H. This approach allows us to effectively and efficiently investigate how compositional differences—the most critical variable—impact the structure and dissolution properties.

To construct a more accurate C-(A)-S-H model, we conducted X-ray fluorescence (XRF) spectrometry to determine the element composition in our experimental C-(A)-S-H samples. It is observed that the samples contain a measurable amount of Na ions (Table S1), which agrees with previous studies that Al incorporation usually involves alkali metal ions for charge balance23,25,63,64. However, the measured Na/Si and Al/Si ratios are not equal, which means the Na ion is not the only charge compensation species for the Al incorporation. Therefore, we introduced the specific Na contents into the C-(A)-S-H models based on the XRF results and used additional H+ or OH- ions to balance the net charges. Specifically, for C1.25SH and C1.25A0.2SH, where the Na/Si ratio was found to be higher than Al/Si, OH- ions were introduced to maintain charge neutrality. For C1.25A0.2S0.8H, where Na/Si was found lower than Al/Si, the Na ions are insufficient to fully compensate for the charge defects. In this case, we introduced H+ by protonating the oxygen atoms of the aluminosilicate chains. The developed C-S-H and C-A-S-H models were fully annealed with MD using a reactive force field (ReaxFF)65,66 to release residual stress and obtain thermodynamically stable structures. For the annealing process, the system was heated from 300 K to 500 K at a rate of 0.4 K/ps and thermostated for 1000 ps. Then, it was cooled back to 300 K following the same procedure. After three annealing cycles, the system was equilibrated at 300 K for 1000 ps. The annealing process was conducted using LAMMPS67 software under the isothermal-isobaric ensemble with a time step of 0.2 fs.

It should be noted that the effective charge of the Ca ion in solution is not the formal +2 charge commonly assumed in classical force fields. Instead, ReaxFF calculates atomic charges dynamically using a charge equilibration scheme, providing a more realistic representation of the fractional charge of ions in solution. To validate the accuracy of the ReaxFF charge equilibration scheme, we compared the charges calculated by ReaxFF to those obtained from DFT calculations for a C1.25SH model (see Fig. S3a). The results show excellent agreement between ReaxFF and DFT charges. Also, previous ab initio calculations on tobermorite (C-S-H-like crystals) also showed that the Ca ions feature partial charges of 1.66 ~ 1.7 rather than the formal +2 charge68. For the same reason, classical force fields like CSH-FF and INTERFACE FF also use point charges (+1.7e) to describe the aqueous Ca ions in the C-S-H like minierals68,69. These confirm that the dynamic partial charges in ReaxFF are not an artifact, but rather a more physically realistic representation than the fixed integer charges used in non-polarizable force fields, which cannot capture these crucial polarization and charge transfer effects. In addition, we monitored the charge of the Ca ion during the dissolution process (see Figs. S3b,3c). When the Ca ion is fully dissolved, its charge slightly increases, consistent with the expected behavior of ions in solution. The charge changes reversibly as the Ca ion moves back and forth between the surface and solution, reflecting the dynamic nature of charge equilibration.

Metadynamics simulations

To simulate the decalcification process, slab models of C-(A)-S-H were constructed from the fully annealed bulk models by introducing a water-saturated slit pore of ~ 40 Å (Figs. S4–6). Normally, we need to calculate the surface energies of low-index facets to determine the most stable surface when constructing surface models of crystalline materials. However, tobermorite (prototype for C-(A)-S-H gel model), as a layered mineral, presents a unique case. It is formed by the stacking of calcium silicate layers in one direction, with interlayer interactions significantly weaker than those in other directions. Consequently, cleavage along the interlayer space is the dominant pathway. The constructed slab models of C1.25SH, C1.25A0.2SH, and C1.25A0.2S0.8H have surfaces (including both the top and bottom) containing 32 Ca ions each. We calculated the dissolution free energy profiles for all 32 Ca ions on each slab model, ensuring comprehensive coverage of the surface chemical variability. The one-dimensional free energy projections onto the Z-direction displacement of Ca ions are shown in Figs. S7–9. Free energy calculations were performed using well-tempered metadynamics38, an enhanced sampling method implemented through the Plumed software70 interfaced with LAMMPS. The well-tempered metadynamics simulations were conducted under a canonical ensemble using a ReaxFF66 with a time step of 0.2 fs.

Following previously established protocols for simulating Ca dissolution3,44, the three displacement components of surface Ca ions were employed as collective variables (CVs) to capture the Ca dissolution process. The selection has two main reasons. First, the three displacement components allow us to more accurately distinguish between the various states of the Ca ion during the dissolution process. In our previous studies on Ca dissolution in C-S-H and clinker, we observed that the dissolution pathway of the Ca ion is not a simple linear trajectory perpendicular to the surface. Instead, it follows a more complex, spiral-like path due to the asymmetry in the atomic arrangement of the mineral surface, a characteristic common to most cement minerals. Using only the water coordination number as a CV would fail to adequately differentiate certain intermediate states along this pathway. Second, the water exchange rate constant of Ca ions in water is significantly higher (>108 s-1) compared to that of magnesium ions (<106 s-1)71. This rapid exchange ensures that the water coordination number of the Ca ion adjusts automatically as its dissolution state changes. We analyzed the evolution of the coordination numbers of the Ca ion during dissolution when using the three displacement components as CVs. By projecting the three-dimensional free energy landscape onto the Ca coordination numbers with aqueous oxygen and surface silicate oxygen (Fig. S10), we observed a clear and physically sensible evolution. This demonstrates that while the coordination number is not biased, it is correctly and automatically captured as the Ca ion dissolves. This finding serves as a validation of both our CV selection and the underlying ReaxFF force field’s ability to describe these complex chemical changes. Additionally, we performed metadynamics simulation tests using a two-dimensional CV set composed of: (CV1) the z-displacement and (CV2) the Ca-H2O/OH⁻ coordination number (Fig. S11). The free energy barrier calculated using this {CN_Ow/Oh, dist.z} CV set is lower than the barrier calculated using the {dist.x, dist.y, dist.z} CV set. Moreover, we projected the original free energy landscape on the unbiased {CN_Ow/Oh, dist.z} CV set via reweighting analysis, the resulting activation barrier is also lower than that of our original {dist.x, dist.y, dist.z} CV set (Fig. S11). This indicates the {CN_Ow/Oh, dist.z} CV set is insufficient to resolve the true transition state from nearby intermediate states along the dissolution path. The overlap between the transition state and certain meta-stable intermediates leads to an averaging effect that artificially underestimates the true activation barrier. In contrast, the three-dimensional spatial CVs {dist.x, dist.y, dist.z} provide the necessary resolution to distinguish these states, allowing the system to map out a path that identifies the more accurate, higher-energy transition state.

The total simulation time depends on the metadynamics parameters and the sampling space. While we acknowledge that using highly precise parameters and long simulation times usually would yield more accurate results, computational resources often impose practical limitations. For instance, classical MD simulations can typically achieve hundreds of nanoseconds of sampling42,72,73, while ab initio MD (AIMD) simulations are often limited to <1 ns due to their computational cost33,36. In this study, we used reactive force filed simulations, which fall between AIMD and classical MD in terms of timescale. Therefore, the precision of our metadynamics parameters is also intermediate between those used in AIMD and classical MD. More importantly, this study primarily investigates the impact of surface structures on C-(A)-S-H decalcification thermodynamics. To achieve this, we aimed to sample a large number of surface sites to better represent the dissolution characteristics of amorphous C-(A)-S-H. While higher precision for a single Ca ion site could be achieved by focusing computational resources on one site, the decalcification characteristics of amorphous C-(A)-S-H are better captured by averaging data across a large number of Ca sites. As shown in Fig. 5, the dissolution activation free energies for different Ca sites vary significantly, and the differences due to sampling precision for a single site are relatively negligible by comparison. Given that our three C-(A)-S-H models include over 90 surface Ca sites to consider, we conducted extensive tests (Figs. S12–14) on metadynamics parameters to seek the most feasible balance between accuracy and computational cost. Based on the tests, Gaussian bias potentials with a width of 0.1 Å and an initial height of 1.5 kJ/mol were deposited every 600 MD steps, coupling with a bias factor of 15 to dynamically adjust the height of the Gaussian potentials. For multiple surface Ca sites, the simulation times were determined as 15–30 ns because Ca sites with higher dissolution activation free energies require longer metadynamics simulations to dissolve.

Since we are sampling a three-dimensional phase space, the dissolved Ca ion may explore multiple surface states when returning to the surface, potentially complicating the post-processing and reducing the efficiency of the metadynamics simulations. To minimize interference from multiple surface states while ensuring that the natural dissolution pathway of the Ca ion is preserved, we confined the sampling space to a cylindrical region with a radius of 2.8 Å in x-y plane and a height of 7 Å in z direction using quadratic walls with a strength of 150 kJ/mol. The radius of the sampling space was determined based on the structural features of the C-(A)-S-H surface (see Fig. S15a). Our simulation tests (Figs. S15b–d) showed that this radius is small enough to limit the Ca ion’s interaction with other surface states, yet large enough to allow the Ca ion to explore its natural dissolution pathway without artificial constraints.

To evaluate the convergence of metadynamics calculations, we conducted tests starting from different initial states along the Ca dissolution pathway including 1) the surface-adsorbed state (initial position); 2) the inner-sphere adsorbed state; and 3) the fully dissolved state (Fig. S16). In all three cases, the Ca ion successfully sampled all three states, and the overall dissolution activation free energy barriers were found to be similar across the different starting configurations.

Minimum free energy pathway

The minimum free energy pathway for ion dissolution was explored from the three-dimensional free energy landscape obtained from metadynamics simulations using the NEB method41,74. Based on the three-dimensional free energy landscape, we identified important intermediate states, which correspond to local energy minima. Between each pair of adjacent minima, several intermediate images were inserted through linear interpolation with a step size of 0.15 Å to construct an initial Ca dissolution pathway. All the images, including the local minima, were then connected with elastic bands using a defined spring constant. The total force acting on each image, which combines contributions from the elastic band and the underlying free energy field, was calculated. This force serves as the driving force for the optimization process. The images were adjusted iteratively to minimize the total energy of the system using the conjugate gradient algorithm. The optimization was considered complete when the energy difference between consecutive iterations fell below 10-4 kJ/mol and the displacement difference between images was less than 10-4 Å. For further details on the theoretical framework of the NEB method, we refer readers to Henkelman’s papers41,74.

Density functional theory calculations

To enhance the efficiency of DFT calculations, the bulk and surface models (without slit pore water) of C-(A)-S-H went through MD equilibration and energy minimization with ReaxFF before being used as initial models for geometry optimization within the DFT framework. For the surface models, atoms located more than 10 Å away from the surface were fixed to simulate the bulk environment and reduce computational cost. Employing the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) method under the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) scheme77, the on-the-fly generation (OTFG) ultrasoft pseudopotentials were used with a kinetic energy cutoff of 571 eV. The first Brillouin zone was sampled using a gamma-centered k-point grid of 1 × 1 × 1. The convergence criteria for energy and force were 10-6 eV/atom and 0.01 eV/Å respectively. After geometry optimization with DFT, the Ca-Os bond orders in C-(A)-S-H were calculated via the Mulliken method46. Bond order is defined as the difference between the numbers of electron pairs in bonding and antibonding molecular orbitals78. These DFT calculations were performed using the Cambridge Sequential Total Energy Package (CASTEP) software79. Following the same procedure and parameters, we also calculated the Bader charges to compare with the Mulliken charges using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP)80,81. The main difference was that the projector augmented plane wave (PAW)82 method was implemented within VASP to deal with pseudopotentials.

C-(A)-S-H synthesis

C-(A)-S-H was synthesized via a standard double precipitation method39. 150 mL of 0.1 M Na2SiO3 solution was adjusted to a pH of approximately 13.5 by carefully adding 10 M NaOH solution. Then, a 150 mL mixed solution of Ca(NO3)2·4H2O and Al(NO3)3·9H2O was added into the Na2SiO3 solution at a consistent rate of 2.2 mL/min while maintaining a stirring speed of 250 rpm. This reaction mixture was stirred continuously for 10 days at ambient temperature. The solid product was separated by centrifugation at 14,981 × g for 15 min, washed three times with an aqueous ethanol solution, and freeze-dried for 7 days. As the final composition of the C-(A)-S-H product may not precisely reflect the stoichiometric ratio of the starting reactants, multiple trial experiments were conducted to achieve the desired C-(A)-S-H compositions. Table S1 lists the selected samples with compositions closest to the target values.

Dissolution experiments and characterization

Decalcification experiments were conducted at temperatures of 10, 20, 30, and 40 °C. For each experiment, 2 g of C-(A)-S-H was dissolved in 300 g of deionized water within a 500 mL beaker. At various time intervals, aliquots were extracted from the solution and filtered through a 0.22 μm microporous membrane filter. The concentration of calcium ions in the filtered solutions was then quantified using an Agilent 5110 Inductively Coupled Plasma Optical Emission Spectrometer (ICP-OES). Decalcification degrees were calculated based on the measured calcium concentrations (Table S2), and decalcification rates were determined accordingly (Table S3). XRD patterns of the C-(A)-S-H samples before and after decalcification were collected using a Rigaku SmartLab 9 kW diffractometer equipped with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.54 Å) to determine phase composition. Scans were performed over a 2θ range of 5 ~ 60 ° with a step size of 0.02 ° and a scanning rate of 2 °/min. Solid-state 29Si and 27Al NMR spectroscopy was employed to assess the degree of polymerization of the silicate chains in the C-(A)-S-H samples before and after decalcification. NMR spectra were recorded on a Jeol ECZ500R 500 MHz NMR spectrometer operating at rotor spinning rates of 4000 Hz and 12000 Hz for 29Si and 27Al, respectively. Tables S4 and S5 present the fractions of different silicon and aluminium environments and the calculated mean chain lengths of the aluminosilicates. 23Na NMR spectra were acquired using a Bruker Avance NEO 400 MHz spectrometer operating at rotor spinning rates of 10 kHz.
