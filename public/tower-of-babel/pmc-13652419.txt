Cholesterolosis mimicking gallbladder adenomyomatosis on imaging in an asymptomatic patient with concurrent cholelithiasis: a case report
Authors: Hayfa A. Al-Olayan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Gallbladder cholesterolosis and adenomyomatosis (GA) share overlapping cross-sectional imaging features on CT enterography and MRCP, creating a significant diagnostic pitfall. Histopathology remains the only definitive means of distinction.Case presentation:A 44-year-old asymptomatic female was incidentally found to have gallbladder wall thickening and cholelithiasis on CT enterography. MRI/MRCP demonstrated focal fundal wall thickening with intramural high-signal foci, which was interpreted as fundal-type GA with a coexisting gallstone. Elective laparoscopic cholecystectomy was performed. Histopathology revealed chronic cholecystitis with focal cholesterolosis – no adenomyomatosis or Rokitansky-Aschoff sinuses were identified.Conclusion:Cholesterolosis can produce imaging features indistinguishable from fundal-type GA. Elective laparoscopic cholecystectomy provides definitive treatment of concurrent cholelithiasis and the histopathological confirmation needed to resolve diagnostic uncertainty.

Introduction and importance

Gallbladder cholesterolosis is a benign hyperplastic cholecystosis characterized by excessive accumulation of cholesterol esters within mucosal macrophages, occurring in approximately 9–26% of cholecystectomy specimens with a higher prevalence in women[1,2]. It may present as a diffuse form – the classic “strawberry gallbladder” – or as polypoid cholesterol deposits[2]. Unlike GA, cholesterolosis does not involve muscularis hyperplasia or RAS formation; however, intramural cholesterol crystal deposits generate T2 high-signal foci on MRCP and subtle wall thickening on CT enterography that closely mimic GA[3,4]. This overlap has been increasingly recognized in recent imaging reviews of hyperplastic cholecystosis and its mimics[5,6]. GA itself is characterized by mucosal epithelial and smooth muscle hyperplasia with intramural diverticula [Rokitansky-Aschoff sinuses (RAS)], classified into fundal, segmental, and diffuse subtypes; the segmental type carries an elevated malignancy risk[4,7]. Cholelithiasis coexists with cholesterolosis in 30–70% of cases[3,8].

The imaging overlap between cholesterolosis and GA has direct clinical consequences: GA – particularly the segmental subtype – carries different malignancy risk and long-term surveillance implications than cholesterolosis[4,7]. Since imaging cannot reliably distinguish the two, histopathological examination remains the definitive diagnostic standard[9,10]. We report a case of preoperative imaging-diagnosed GA that was revised to cholesterolosis on final histopathology, illustrating this diagnostic pitfall. Reports specifically detailing an asymptomatic patient in whom a confident preoperative diagnosis of fundal GA – based on the classic pearl-necklace sign – was overturned by histopathology remain limited[5,6,11], underscoring the educational value of this case for surgeons and radiologists managing incidental gallbladder wall thickening. This case has been reported in line with the SCARE 2025 checklist[12].

Case presentation

Patient information

A 44-year-old woman (BMI 28 kg/m2), a nonsmoker with no alcohol use, no prior biliary disease or abdominal surgery, and no family history of gallbladder malignancy, presented to the outpatient clinic for a constipation workup without biliary symptoms.

Clinical findings

The physical examination was unremarkable. Vital signs are BP, 128/78 mmHg; HR, 74 bpm; temperature, 36.7°C; and RR, 16/min. The abdomen was soft and non-tender; Murphy’s sign was negative, with no hepatosplenomegaly.

Diagnostic assessment

Laboratory investigations were within normal limits: WBC 8.3 × 109/L, Hb 12.4 g/dL, platelet count 258 × 109/L; total bilirubin 4.8 µmol/L, ALT 17.8 U/L, AST 16.5 U/L, and ALP 59.8 U/L. CT enterography, performed for constipation, incidentally revealed focal fundal gallbladder wall thickening, interpreted as most likely representing adenomyomatosis, with probable minimal central intrahepatic biliary dilatation. MRI/MRCP confirmed focal fundal wall thickening with intramural T2 hyperintense foci producing the characteristic pearl-necklace sign, interpreted as fundal-type adenomyomatosis; no biliary dilatation; no acute cholecystitis. All other abdominal organs were unremarkable (Fig. 1). Treatment and management

Following a multidisciplinary discussion (surgery, radiology, gastroenterology), elective laparoscopic cholecystectomy was recommended on the basis of the following: (1) confirmed cholelithiasis with a lifetime risk of biliary complications; (2) the need for histopathological confirmation to exclude occult malignancy; (3) patient age and good functional status making elective surgery low-risk; and (4) patient preference after thorough counseling. Written informed consent was obtained. Elective surgery was performed approximately 8 weeks after the initial CT enterography finding, an interval used for multidisciplinary discussion, radiological correlation with MRI/MRCP, and completion of outpatient surgical work-up; no symptom development or clinical deterioration occurred during this period.

Follow-up and outcomes

Standard four-port laparoscopic cholecystectomy was performed under general anesthesia by a consultant general surgeon. Intraoperative findings included a normal fundal wall, with no pericholecystic inflammation, adhesions, or local invasion. Operative time was 45 minutes; there was minimal blood loss; and there was no conversion to open surgery. The resected gallbladder specimen is shown in Figure 2. The patient was discharged the same-day, tolerating oral intake. No intraoperative or postoperative complications occurred. Histopathology revealed chronic cholecystitis with focal areas of cholesterolosis and a 15-mm mixed-composition gallstone. Microscopy demonstrated gallbladder mucosa lined by simple columnar epithelium, with the lamina propria diffusely infiltrated by foamy macrophages, forming a polypoid structure (Fig. 3; H&E, 40×). No RAS or muscularis hyperplasia were identified, definitively excluding GA. The patient was reviewed at 2 weeks, 6 weeks, and 3 months postoperatively, with full recovery, no recurrence of symptoms, and a return to normal activity at each assessment. Clinical discussion

This case illustrates that cholesterolosis can closely mimic the cross-sectional imaging appearance of fundal-type GA, though the two are not indistinguishable in all instances [5,9,10]. The preoperative MRCP diagnosis was established on the basis of focal fundal wall thickening and intramural T2 high-signal foci – the “pearl-necklace sign” classically attributed to fluid-filled RAS in GA[10,13] (Fig. 1). In its original validation series, the pearl-necklace sign carried a specificity of 92% for adenomyomatosis but a sensitivity of only 62%, and was absent in 28% of histologically confirmed GA cases; that cohort, however, was validated against gallbladder carcinoma rather than cholesterolosis, and, to our knowledge, no large-scale series has specifically quantified the false-positive rate of the sign when cholesterolosis is the true underlying pathology[10]. This gap in the literature is a limitation of the current evidence base rather than of the present report (Table 2). However, lipid-laden macrophages in cholesterolosis generate similar T2 high-signal foci due to the relaxation properties of intracellular lipid, reproducing this pattern without true RAS[4,9]. The key histological distinction – RAS and muscularis hyperplasia in GA versus superficial mucosal foam cell infiltration in cholesterolosis – is frequently not resolvable by MRCP or CT enterography alone[5,9,10], as confirmed histopathologically in the present case. A discrepancy was noted between the initial CT enterography, which raised the possibility of “probable minimal central intrahepatic biliary dilatation,” and the subsequent MRI/MRCP and biochemical profile, both of which excluded ductal dilatation. Given the entirely normal liver function tests and the absence of dilatation on the more biliary-specific MRCP sequence, this transient CT appearance most likely reflected a positional or partial-volume artifact rather than a true intermittent obstructive process, and it did not alter management.

Comparable diagnostic discordance between preoperative imaging and final histopathology has been described elsewhere in the hyperplastic cholecystosis literature. Mejri et al reported two cases labeled preoperatively as GA, including one asymptomatic patient in whom inconclusive cross-sectional imaging prompted cholecystectomy for histological clarification[11], while broader imaging reviews have identified cholesterolosis as one of the principal mimics that can be mistaken for GA on CT, ultrasound, and MRCP[5,6]. Our case adds to this limited body of literature by documenting the specific scenario of an asymptomatic patient with a confident preoperative pearl-necklace-sign diagnosis of fundal GA that was subsequently revised to cholesterolosis, reinforcing that this particular imaging pattern warrants cautious interpretation even when classically described features are present.

The management implications are significant. Isolated cholesterolosis without symptoms or gallstones does not routinely warrant cholecystectomy[3,8]. In contrast, GA management depends on the subtype (Table 3): segmental and diffuse forms warrant resection due to elevated malignancy risk, while fundal GA may be managed conservatively[4,7]. In our patient, coexisting cholelithiasis represented an independent surgical indication regardless of wall diagnosis, justifying elective cholecystectomy. The final histopathological diagnosis carries meaningful prognostic weight: cholesterolosis requires no long-term surveillance, unlike segmental GA[4,7]. Cholecystectomy in this setting, therefore, served a dual purpose – definitive treatment of cholelithiasis and histopathological clarification – allowing the patient to be counseled accurately once the preoperative GA label was revised[10,14].

Limitations include the inherent constraints of single-case generalizability; postoperative follow-up in this report extended to three months, and while the patient remained asymptomatic throughout, longer-term surveillance data are not yet available. Prospective studies systematically correlating preoperative GA diagnoses with postoperative histopathology, and dedicated series quantifying the false-positive rate of the pearl-necklace sign specifically against cholesterolosis, would better define the true prevalence of this diagnostic discordance.

Conclusion

Gallbladder cholesterolosis can produce CT and MRCP features that closely resemble fundal-type adenomyomatosis – as demonstrated in this case by the pearl-necklace sign[1] – resulting in a false-positive preoperative imaging diagnosis. Elective laparoscopic cholecystectomy provided both definitive treatment of concurrent cholelithiasis and the histopathological clarification (Fig. 3) necessary to establish the correct diagnosis. Surgeons and radiologists should recognize this imaging pitfall, supported by comparable observations in the wider literature[5,6,11], and counsel patients that a preoperative imaging diagnosis of GA requires histopathological verification, with final pathology potentially revealing alternative benign diagnoses with distinctly different prognostic implications.
