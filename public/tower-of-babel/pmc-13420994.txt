Temporal Association Between COVID‐19 Vaccination and HBsAg Loss in Chronic Hepatitis B Infection: An Interrupted Time Series Analysis
Authors: Jonggi Choi, Allison Carroll, Vy H. Nguyen, Megan Michta, Xuan Q. Zhang, Young‐Suk Lim, Raymond T. Chung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsHepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) loss, a functional cure in chronic hepatitis (CHB), is rare. Temporal changes in HBsAg loss rates were evaluated in relation to COVID‐19 vaccination rollout in South Korea.MethodsAn interrupted time series (ITS) analysis was performed using electronic health records of 59,946 adults with CHB (2002–2025). Monthly HBsAg loss incidence rates were estimated using a competing risk framework. National COVID‐19 vaccination coverage was used as a surrogate for population‐level exposure. A three‐phase segmental regression model assessed changes before vaccination, during a predefined vaccination effect period (18 months following ≥ 80% of two‐dose coverage), and after this period. Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) incidence was analysed as a negative control.ResultsOver 382,509 person‐years, 2483 HBsAg loss events occurred. Pre‐vaccination rates were stable (0.11–0.80 per 100 person‐years). Following the COVID‐19 vaccination rollout, the incidence increased to 0.96 in 2021, peaking at 1.27 in 2022, before declining to 0.93 in 2024. In the ITS model, no baseline trend was observed (p = 0.606). At the intervention point (November 2021), HBsAg loss rates increased significantly (incidence rate ratio; 1.56; 95% CI 1.26–1.93; p < 0.001). No significant slope change was observed during the vaccination period, while a negative slope change was observed post‐vaccination (coefficient −0.039 per month; p = 0.003). Findings were consistent across sensitivity and subgroup analyses. HCC incidence showed no significant changes.ConclusionCOVID‐19 vaccination was temporally associated with transiently higher HBsAg loss rates in CHB, followed by attenuation, suggesting a potential population‐level immunomodulatory effect on HBV dynamics.

Introduction

Chronic hepatitis B (CHB) affects over 296 million people worldwide [1], with hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) loss representing the ultimate treatment goal, which is often termed ‘functional cure’ [2]. Achievement of HBsAg loss confers substantial clinical benefits, including improved long‐term prognosis with reduced liver‐related mortality and morbidity [3, 4]. Patients who achieve HBsAg loss demonstrate a significantly lower risk of HCC development, and these beneficial effects are comparable, whether HBsAg loss occurs naturally or is treatment‐induced [5]. Importantly, even among patients who have already developed HCC, HBsAg loss is associated with reduced late recurrence following liver resection [6]. Additionally, once HBsAg loss is achieved, it demonstrates remarkable durability, with 90%–95% of patients maintaining seronegativity during long‐term follow‐up [7]. However, the incidence of HBsAg loss remains extremely low at < 1% per year whether spontaneous or treatment‐induced [2].

The COVID‐19 pandemic led to unprecedented global vaccination efforts, with different vaccine platforms demonstrating substantial efficacy in preventing severe disease and mortality [8, 9]. Beyond their intended effects, COVID‐19 vaccines have been associated with diverse immunological responses and unexpected clinical events due to their potent immune activation [10, 11]. Of particular interest to the patient population with CHB, a case series study reported reductions in HBsAg titres following COVID‐19 vaccination [12]. A Korean study demonstrated rapid but transient HBsAg titre reduction post‐vaccination and identified enhanced natural killer (NK) cell activity as a potential mechanism, suggesting that vaccine‐induced immune activation might influence chronic HBV infection dynamics [13]. These observations raise the intriguing possibility that COVID‐19 vaccination, through its immunomodulatory effects, might adventitiously promote HBsAg clearance.

Despite these suggestive findings, the association between COVID‐19 vaccination and actual loss of HBsAg, rather than merely reduction in titre, has remained largely unexplored. This represents a knowledge gap, as transient titre changes may not translate to permanent functional cure. Therefore, we conducted an interrupted time series (ITS) analysis to investigate whether the nationwide implementation of COVID‐19 vaccination was associated with changes in the HBsAg loss rate among patients with CHB. This natural experiment approach took advantage of the well‐defined temporal implementation of mass vaccination in Korea to assess its potential impact on this rare but crucial clinical outcome.

Patients and Methods

Study Design and Population

We conducted an ITS analysis using individual‐level electronic health records (EHRs) from the Asan Medical Center, Seoul, South Korea, combined with national‐level data on COVID‐19 vaccination coverage. The source population comprised all adult patients (age ≥ 18 years) with CHB who tested positive for HBsAg between 1 January 2001 and 31 December 2025. CHB was defined as positivity for HBsAg for at least 6 months documented in the EHR system.

To ensure incident HBsAg loss determination, we implemented exclusion criteria. First, we excluded 9009 patients whose first HBsAg‐positive test occurred before 1 January 2001. We then excluded eight patients who underwent liver transplantation before 1 January 2001. An additional 446 patients were excluded due to zero follow‐up. Finally, to implement a 1‐year washout period, we excluded 39 patients who experienced loss of HBsAg during 2001, ensuring that only incident cases occurring from 1 January 2002, were included in the analysis. This exclusion resulted in a final analytic cohort of 59,946 patients.

Patients were followed from their index date, the first date of confirmation of HBsAg positivity, or 1 January 2002, whichever was later, until HBsAg loss, death, liver transplantation, or end of study period (31 December 2025), whichever occurred first. This study design leverages individual‐level outcome data while using national vaccination coverage as an ecological exposure measure, recognizing that individual vaccination records were unavailable, but that vaccination probability increased proportionally with national coverage rates over time.

The baseline characteristics were determined at the index date. Laboratory values (platelet count, albumin, alanine aminotransferase [ALT], total bilirubin, cholesterol, creatinine, prothrombin time, hepatitis B e antigen [HBeAg] status and HBV DNA) were defined as the measurement closest to the index date. Baseline cirrhosis was defined as a cirrhosis diagnosis occurring at least 1 year before the index date, allowing sufficient time for diagnostic certainty. Nucleos(t)ide analogue (NUC) treatment was defined as any overlap between the observation period and the prescription period of NUC. NUC included entecavir, tenofovir disoproxil fumarate, tenofovir alafenamide, lamivudine, adefovir, telbivudine and clevudine.

COVID‐19 Vaccination Coverage (Exposure)

We obtained monthly COVID‐19 vaccination coverage data from the Korean Disease Control and Prevention Agency (KDCA), encompassing the period from February 2021 to October 2023. Vaccination coverage rates were calculated as the cumulative proportion of the Korean population who received each vaccination dose (first, second and third doses).

Individual baseline vaccination records were not available; therefore, we used national vaccination coverage as an ecological proxy for population‐level vaccine exposure, consistent with previous ecological and ITS studies evaluating the population health impact of vaccination campaigns [14, 15, 16]. We defined the primary intervention point as November 2021, when both the first‐dose and the second‐dose coverage reached more than 80% of the Korean population (Table S1). This threshold was selected based on several considerations: (i) 80% coverage represents a commonly cited target for achieving substantial population‐level immunity effects [17]; (ii) completion of the two‐dose primary series is associated with peak immune response for most COVID‐19 vaccine platforms [18, 19]; and (iii) this threshold balances adequate exposure saturation while maintaining sufficient pre‐intervention observation time for trend estimation.

We defined a ‘maintenance period’ that lasted from November 2021 to April 2023, during which vaccine‐induced immunomodulation was hypothesized to persist. This timeframe was determined based on the dynamics of vaccination coverage observed in Korea: first‐dose coverage peaked and plateaued in January 2022, second‐dose coverage peaked in March 2022, and third‐dose coverage reached its peak in April 2022, remaining stable thereafter. We defined the endpoint of the maintenance period as approximately 1 year after the final dose peak (third dose), recognizing that although vaccine effectiveness against infection wanes within 6 months [20], protection against severe outcomes and systemic immune activation can persist up to 12 months after vaccination [21]. This approach accounts for both the staggered completion of the vaccination series across the population and the duration of clinically relevant immunologic effects. The post‐vaccination period (Phase 3) was defined as beginning May 2023, during which vaccine effects were hypothesized to wane as vaccination rates plateaued and the time since vaccination increased across the population. This three‐phase framework (pre‐vaccination, vaccination effect period and post‐vaccination period) allowed us to capture both the immediate impact and the temporal dynamics of vaccination on HBsAg loss rates. Alternative intervention definitions and maintenance durations were explored in the sensitivity analyses.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was HBsAg loss, defined as at least two consecutive negative HBsAg tests separated by a minimum interval of ≥ 6 months, regardless of subsequent seroreversion in the EHRs. The date of the first negative HBsAg test was designated as the HBsAg loss date. To address a sustained response, subsequent HBsAg reversion (reappearance of HBsAg positivity after confirmed loss) was additionally evaluated. Hepatitis B surface antibodies (anti‐HBs) seroconversion after HBsAg loss was also assessed when follow‐up anti‐HBs data were available. We used a competing risk methodology to account for death and liver transplantation as competing events that prevent the observation of HBsAg loss. To validate the specificity of any observed association between vaccination rollout and HBsAg loss, we analysed two negative control outcomes: (1) HCC incidence based on histologic examination or imaging tests according to previously established definitions [3, 4, 5, 6], and (2) NUC treatment initiation rate, as treatment decisions are governed by fixed reimbursement criteria independent of vaccination. We hypothesized that if the observed changes in HBsAg loss rates were spurious or due to temporal confounding, similar changes should be observed in these negative control outcomes. Conversely, the absence of such changes would support the specificity of the vaccination effect to HBsAg loss.

Statistical Analysis

We calculated monthly HBsAg loss incidence rates using a competing risk framework. For each calendar month, the at‐risk population consisted of patients under observation during that month (i.e., those whose observational period overlapped with the calendar month). Person‐years (PYs) of follow‐up were calculated for each patient's contribution to each month, accounting for late entries and competing events. Monthly incidence rates were expressed per 100 PYs.

We fitted three‐phase segmental negative binomial regression models to assess changes in HBsAg loss rates associated with the COVID‐19 vaccination rollout. The model included: (i) a baseline time period (pre‐vaccination slope); (ii) an immediate level change at the intervention point (Phase 2 start); (iii) a slope change during the maintenance period (Phase 2); (iv) an immediate level change at the end of the maintenance period (Phase 3); and (v) a slope change during the post‐vaccination period (FPhase 3).

The model equation was [22]: logλₜ=logPYₜ+β0+β1time+β2phase2+β3time_since_phase2+β4phase3+β5time_since_phase3 where λ t is the expected event count at time t, PY t is PYs at risk (offset term), β 1 represents the pre‐vaccination trend, β 2 the immediate effect at the introduction of vaccination, β 3 the trend during the maintenance period, β 4 the immediate change when entering the post‐vaccination period, and β 5 the post‐vaccination trend. Negative binomial regression was used to account for overdispersion; when convergence failed, Poisson regression was used as the alternative. Autocorrelation was assessed using the Durbin–Watson test.

We conducted sensitivity analyses using different combinations of parameters, varying: (1) the definition of intervention (four thresholds), (2) maintenance duration (12 vs. 18 months), and (3) time aggregation for calculating the incidence of HBsAg loss (monthly, bi‐monthly, quarterly). Model performance was evaluated using Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), pseudo‐R2 (McFadden's) and root mean square error (RMSE). Models were ranked by combined performance across these metrics, and the top‐performing model was selected as primary. We performed an additional sensitivity analysis adjusting for HBsAg testing volume during each time interval by including testing frequency within the at‐risk cohort as a time‐varying covariate in the ITS model to assess potential ascertainment bias related to catch‐up testing during the COVID‐19 period.

Subgroup analyses were conducted according to: (1) age (< 60 vs. ≥ 60 years), (2) sex (male vs. female), (3) baseline cirrhosis status (present vs. absent), and (4) NUC treatment anytime during the follow‐up period (yes vs. no). Each subgroup analysis used the primary parameter set (first and second dose ≥ 80%, 18‐month maintenance, monthly aggregation). Separate three‐phase ITS models were fitted for each subgroup, with heterogeneity of effects assessed by comparing coefficient magnitudes and statistical significance across strata.

For the negative control analysis, we fitted a simple linear regression model (time trend only, without phase terms) to test whether the incidence of HCC and the NUC initiation rate showed secular trends unrelated to vaccination rollout. The absence of significant phase‐related changes in the incidence of HCC and the NUC initiation rate would support the specificity of any vaccination‐associated changes in HBsAg loss rates.

All analyses were performed using R version 4.3.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Statistical significance was set at a two‐sided α = 0.05. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Asan Medical Center with a waiver of informed consent (IRB number: 2023‐1481).

Results

Of 69,448 patients with documented CHB and HBsAg positivity between 2001 and 2025, we excluded 9502 patients, resulting in a final cohort of 59,946 patients included in the analysis (Figure 1). The median follow‐up duration was 4.1 years (interquartile range [IQR]: 0.8–10.4 years), contributing a total of 382,509 PYs of observation.

Incidence of HBsAg Loss Over Time

A total of 2483 HBsAg loss events occurred during the study period. Annual HBsAg loss incidence rates demonstrated temporal variation, ranging from 0.11 per 100 PYs in 2002 to 1.27 per 100 PYs in 2022 (Table S2). During the pre‐vaccination period (2002–2020), the annual rates remained relatively stable, fluctuating between 0.11 and 0.80 per 100 PYs. Following the nationwide rollout of COVID‐19 vaccination beginning in February 2021, incidence rates increased to 0.96 per 100 PYs in 2021 and peaked at 1.27 per 100 PYs in 2022. Subsequently, the rates dropped to 1.06 per 100 PYs in 2023 and to 0.93 per 100 PYs in 2024.

Among the 2483 patients with confirmed HBsAg loss, the median interval between the two consecutive negative HBsAg tests was 244 days (8.0 months). After confirmation of HBsAg loss, 2052 patients underwent additional follow‐up HBsAg testing: 94 (3.8%) patients showed reversion to HBsAg positivity, whereas 2389 (96.2%) maintained sustained HBsAg negativity. Anti‐HBs data were available for 2406 (96.9%) patients, with 1619 (65.2%) developed anti‐HBs seroconversion during follow‐up period. Among those achieving seroconversion, the median time to anti‐HBs positivity was 314 days (10.3 months).

The three‐phase ITS model did not show a significant baseline trend in HBsAg loss rates during the pre‐vaccination period (Phase 1; January 2002 to October 2021) (coefficient 0.00019 per month, 95% CI −0.00061 to 0.00099; p = 0.606) (Figure 2).

Sensitivity Analyses

We evaluated 23 alternative parameter specifications that varied intervention definitions, maintenance durations, and time aggregation units (Table S3). The top‐performing models included: (1) first dose ≥ 80% with 12‐month maintenance and quarterly aggregation; (2) first and second dose ≥ 70% with 12‐month maintenance and quarterly aggregation; and (3) first dose ≥ 80% with 18‐month maintenance and quarterly aggregation.

For all models, the Phase 2 level change ranged from 0.24 to 0.47 (median = 0.39), with 19 of 24 models (79%) statistically significant. The Phase 3 slope change ranged from −0.073 to −0.037 (median = −0.047), with 15 of 24 models (63%) statistically significant.

Adjustment for HBsAg testing volume showed that testing frequency was significantly associated with detection of HBsAg loss (coefficient 0.008, p = 0.0002). However, the Phase 2 level increase remained statistically significant following adjustment, although attenuated in magnitude (unadjusted IRR 1.57, p = 0.008; adjusted IRR 1.38, p = 0.040).

Subgroup Analyses

Subgroup analyses using the primary parameter specification revealed differential effects between the phases. Using the primary parameter specification, the Phase 2 level change was significant in patients aged < 60 years (coefficient = 0.467, p = 0.0003) but not in those aged ≥ 60 years (coefficient = 0.348, p = 0.097). Significant level increases were observed in both males (coefficient = 0.434, p = 0.002) and females (coefficient = 0.448, p = 0.011). Patients lacking baseline cirrhosis (coefficient = 0.443, p = 0.0002) showed significant effects, whereas those with cirrhosis did not (coefficient = 0.379, p = 0.486). Both NUC‐treated (coefficient = 0.482, p = 0.002) and untreated patients (coefficient = 0.425, p = 0.013) demonstrated significant level changes.

Phase 3 slope decreases were significant in patients aged < 60 years (coefficient = −0.038, p = 0.008), males (coefficient = −0.036, p = 0.019), females (coefficient = −0.043, p = 0.025), patients without cirrhosis (coefficient = −0.038, p = 0.003), and both NUC‐treated and untreated groups.

Negative Control Analysis

HCC incidence showed no significant changes in association with the national vaccination rollout (Table S4). At the intervention point, no level change was observed (coefficient = −0.024, 95% CI: −0.203 to 0.156; p = 0.799) (Figure 3), and no slope change was detected in the post‐vaccination period (coefficient = 0.007, 95% CI: −0.024 to 0.038; p = 0.467). Similarly, initiation of NUC treatment showed no significant Phase 2 level changes in the three‐phase ITS model (coefficient = −0.125; p = 0.337) (Table 3). Although the rates gradually increased over time (linear trend coefficient = 0.0022; p < 0.001), no vaccination‐specific signal was observed (Figure S1).

Discussion

In this large longitudinal cohort study using ITS analysis, we observed a significant temporal association between the COVID‐19 vaccination rollout and an increase in HBsAg loss rates in patients with CHB. This increase occurred immediately after vaccination implementation, was maintained for a defined period, and was followed by a gradual attenuation over time. These findings suggest a transient population‐level effect of COVID‐19 vaccination on the dynamics of HBsAg loss.

Our findings are consistent with emerging evidence suggesting that COVID‐19 vaccination may influence HBV‐related immune responses. A recent study from Korea demonstrated that COVID‐19 vaccination was associated with a transient decline in HBsAg titres among patients with CHB, with the most pronounced reduction occurring within 30 days after vaccination and a subsequent return towards baseline levels [13]. Similarly, a case‐based study from Japan reported that rapid reductions in HBsAg levels were more frequently observed after the initiation of COVID‐19 vaccination programmes compared with the pre‐vaccination period, although statistical significance was limited by the small sample size [12]. Although these prior studies were limited to the analysis of short‐term biochemical changes or small case series, our study extends these findings by demonstrating a temporal association with actual HBsAg loss at the population level using a structured ITS framework. However, because the nationwide COVID‐19 vaccination rollout substantially overlapped with the major SARS‐CoV‐2 infection waves, the observed association may reflect the combined immunologic effects of vaccination and infection rather than that of vaccination alone, which could not be evaluated separately in this ecological analysis.

Beyond HBV‐specific observations, COVID‐19 vaccination has been associated with various immune‐mediated phenomena, which suggest broad immunomodulatory effects. Several reports have described autoimmune hepatitis following COVID‐19 vaccination, raising the possibility of vaccine‐induced immune activation or dysregulation [23, 24]. In addition, cases of immune‐mediated flares of chronic viral hepatitis, including HBV and HCV, have been reported following vaccination, supporting the concept that vaccination may enhance antiviral immune responses. Furthermore, studies have demonstrated robust activation of innate and adaptive immunity following mRNA vaccination, including induction of type I interferons, activation of dendritic cells, and expansion of effector T‐cell responses [25]. These findings collectively support the hypothesis that COVID‐19 vaccination may exert important off‐target immunological effects beyond SARS‐CoV‐2‐specific immunity.

Several biological mechanisms may explain the observed association between COVID‐19 vaccination and increased HBsAg loss. mRNA vaccines are known to induce strong innate immune activation through pattern recognition receptors such as Toll‐like receptors (TLR7/8), leading to the production of type I interferons and pro‐inflammatory cytokines. Interferon signalling has long been recognized as a key mechanism for functional cure of HBV infection. Furthermore, a Korean study demonstrated that COVID‐19 vaccination was associated with a transient decrease in NKG2A‐expressing NK cells, suggesting reduced inhibitory signalling and enhanced NK cell activity [13]. Given that NK cells play an important role in HBV immune control, this shift towards a more activated NK cell phenotype may contribute to an accelerated decline in HBsAg. The transient nature of these immunological changes is also consistent with our observation of a waning effect over time.

An unexpected finding was the decline in HBsAg loss rates in the 2024–2025 period to levels below the pre‐vaccination baseline. This may reflect continued waning of the Phase 3 slope, depletion of patients more susceptible to immune‐mediated clearance during the Phase 2 peak, or random temporal variation. Testing frequency remained stable during this period, making ascertainment bias less likely. A longer follow‐up is needed to determine whether this represents a new steady state or a transient fluctuation.

This study has several important strengths. Although previous reports have described individual cases or short‐term changes in HBsAg titres, our study is, to our knowledge, the first to systematically evaluate the temporal relationship between COVID‐19 vaccination and HBsAg loss at the population level. We applied a rigorous ITS design that incorporates long‐term pre‐intervention trends, multiple sensitivity analyses, and consistent findings in subgroup analyses. Furthermore, we included the incidence of HCC as a negative control outcome within the same study population, which did not show any association with vaccination, supporting the specificity of our findings. Given that HBsAg loss is a rare event, our analysis was supported by a large, well‐characterized cohort featuring long‐term follow‐up, derived from a previously validated dataset used in multiple large‐scale studies [26, 27].

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, individual‐level vaccination data were not available. However, in South Korea, COVID‐19 vaccination was implemented through a government‐led programme with high uptake, with more than 90% of adults receiving at least two doses. Therefore, national vaccination coverage can reasonably serve as a proxy for population‐level exposure. Second, although we observed an attenuation of the association over time, detailed vaccination data during the later period of the analysis were limited. After the initial nationwide rollout, vaccination became optional rather than mandatory, and coverage rates were estimated to decline to approximately 30%–50% among adults. This limitation may have affected the precision of our estimates regarding waning effects. Third, we were unable to evaluate temporal changes according to vaccine type or to perform vaccine‐specific subgroup analyses. However, the Korean vaccination programme was predominantly based on mRNA vaccines (i.e., BNT162b2 and mRNA‐1273), which accounted for the majority of administered doses. Fourth, because HBsAg loss can only be detected when HBsAg levels are measured, changes in the surveillance intensity during the COVID‐19 period may have introduced ascertainment bias. Although testing frequency was significantly associated with event detection, the Phase 2 increase remained significant following the adjustment for testing volume, suggesting that increased surveillance alone was unlikely to fully explain the observed association. Finally, as an ecological analysis, causal inference at the individual level cannot be established.

In conclusion, the COVID‐19 vaccination rollout was temporally associated with a transient increase in HBsAg loss rates in patients with CHB, followed by gradual attenuation over time. These findings suggest a potential immunomodulatory effect of vaccination on the dynamics of HBV infection. Further studies incorporating individual‐level vaccination data and mechanistic analyses are warranted to better understand this association and its clinical implications.
