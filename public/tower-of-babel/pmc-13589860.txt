Algorithm-based approach to the management of rhino-maxillary mucormycosis: part II of a two-part study
Authors: Samir Ali Elborolosy, Ghada Amin Khalifa, Kamal Ebeid, Hamada Mahran, Mohammed Nahed Attia Mohammed, Mohamed Yehia Abdelfattah, Mohamed Mostafa Elshamaa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRhino-maxillary mucormycosis (RMM) is an aggressive, life-threatening fungal infection associated with substantial morbidity and mortality. While early diagnosis is universally advocated, robust quantitative evidence delineating how defined diagnostic delays influence objective clinical outcomes remains scarce. This study aimed to quantify the relationship between diagnostic timing and treatment outcomes in RMM and to develop a protocol-driven multidisciplinary algorithm to standardize management.MethodsThis retrospective case series analyzed patients diagnosed and treated for RMM at the Otolaryngology and Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery clinics of Beni-Suef University between November 2019 and December 2021. Data extraction and statistical analysis were conducted from June 2022 to January 2024. Diagnostic timing was categorized as early (4–7 days), late (8–10 days), or delayed (> 10 days). Primary outcomes included disease prognosis (regression, stabilization, progression, or death) and resolution pattern (favorable or unfavorable). Associations were evaluated using chi-square tests and Cramer’s V, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.ResultsThirty patients were included (mean age 56.53 ± 11.52 years; 60% male). Early diagnosis occurred in 40% of cases, late in 26.67%, and delayed in 33.33%. The overall survival rate was 83.33%. At final follow-up, disease regression was observed in 60% of patients, stabilization in 16.67%, and progression in 6.67%. A statistically significant association was identified between diagnostic timing and disease regression across follow-up intervals, most pronounced at the third postoperative month (χ2 = 17.47, Cramer’s V = 0.56, p = 0.008). Diagnostic timing was also significantly associated with a favorable resolution pattern at the third month (χ2 = 15.77, Cramer’s V = 0.75, p = 0.0004). This association was not statistically significant at six months (p = 0.87).ConclusionDiagnostic timing demonstrates a significant association with clinical outcomes in RMM. Earlier diagnosis correlates with higher rates of regression and favorable resolution patterns. Adoption of a structured diagnostic and management algorithm may facilitate early triage and coordinated multidisciplinary intervention, contributing to improved outcome standardization in oral and maxillofacial practice. Prospective validation in larger cohorts is warranted.Keywords: Mucormycosis, Rhino-maxillary Mucormycosis, Rhinocerebral Mucormycosis, Mucorales Infections, Zygomycosis, Rhino-maxillary Mucormycosis Management

Background

Rhino-maxillary mucormycosis (RMM) is a rare but highly aggressive fungal infection, primarily affecting immunocompromised individuals. The infection typically begins when fungal spores are inhaled into the nasal cavity and paranasal sinuses, rapidly invading surrounding tissues. Within hours to days, the disease may spread from the nasal passages to the sinuses, the orbit, and even the brain, potentially resulting in severe neurological complications and death. Early symptoms often include nasal discharge, facial pain, headache, and the appearance of necrotic tissue in the oral or nasal mucosa, observed in the majority of patients [1–5].

The post-COVID-19 increase in RMM cases has emphasized the urgent need for timely and coordinated care. Effective management relies on early antifungal therapy, prompt surgical debridement, and control of underlying health conditions. Despite these interventions, mortality rates remain high, and multiple challenges, such as delayed recognition, misdiagnosis, variable clinical presentation, patient comorbidities, limited physician experience, and treatment-related toxicity, continue to hinder optimal outcomes [6–11].

Although early intervention is widely recommended, there is limited quantitative evidence detailing how specific diagnostic delays affect measurable outcomes, including disease regression, stabilization, or progression. This lack of evidence complicates the development of evidence-based triage and management protocols. Therefore, this study aimed to quantify the impact of diagnostic timing on treatment outcomes in RMM and to translate these findings into a protocol-driven, multidisciplinary management algorithm to support clinicians in delivering standardized care.

Materials and methods

Study design

This retrospective case series extends our previous research [12] and utilizes the same patient cohort. It includes patients diagnosed with RMM who received treatment between November 2019 and December 2021 at the Otolaryngology and Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery clinics. Data collection, analysis, and manuscript preparation were conducted between June 2022 and January 2024. Eligible participants were adults from the first phase of the study [12] with midface involvement, with or without cerebral extension, who were either healthy or medically compromised, including patients with diabetes, hypertension, or other immunosuppressive conditions, and who had received multidisciplinary treatment. Patients were excluded if they were children, pregnant, had mandibular involvement, or had RMM associated with fungal infections in other body regions.

The study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki under the institutional oversight of the Faculty of Dentistry, Assiut University. Administrative authorization for data access was initially granted in March 2022. Final ethical approval was subsequently confirmed by the Assiut Dental Research Ethical Committee (Certificate No. 17–2024-00013) on December 29, 2024. To ensure patient confidentiality, all data were de-identified prior to analysis, and no therapeutic interventions were undertaken. Owing to the retrospective nature of the study and the use of anonymized clinical records, the requirement for informed consent was formally waived. However, all patients had previously provided written consent for their clinical treatment in line with institutional regulations.

Patient staging and management protocol

All patients were subjected to the standard diagnostic procedures that were briefly described in the first part of the study [12]. Patients were divided into three groups on the basis of the diagnostic results: 1) possible RMM: if patients had clinical symptoms, but the results of the imaging modalities and laboratory tests were negative; 2) probable RMM: if patients had supportive clinical and imaging diagnostic evidence of RMM, but their laboratory tests failed to detect the fungus; and 3) proven RMM: if the laboratory tests identified the fungus in tissue swabs and samples. The probable and proven cases were then staged on the basis of the severity and extent of the disease with respect to CT and MRI scans (The diagnostic evidence for RMM and criteria for staging can be found in the supplementary materials in the first phase of the study) [12].

The possible RMM cases were continuously monitored while receiving supportive care and managing the underlying medical conditions. Supportive care included pain management, nutritional support, antibiotics if necessary, and proper hydration. Five times a day, sinus irrigation was also performed with normal saline. Every three weeks, MRI scans and endoscopies were performed every 72 and 24 h, respectively. If there was clinical improvement and no signs appeared on MRI scans, patients were observed for an additional three weeks. If there was no clinical improvement and/or new supporting evidence appeared on imaging, cases were categorized as probable RMM, and the laboratory tests were repeated. In accordance with the literature [13, 14], the following protocol was applied for every patient with probable and proved RMM: 1) control of medical problems and preexisting risk factors; 2) administration of systemic and topical antifungal drugs; 3) surgical debridement; 4) prosthetic rehabilitation; and 5) follow-up.

Control of medical problems and preexisting risk factors

Upon admission, hyperglycemia was managed with insulin and monitored biweekly, with sodium bicarbonate administered as needed for diabetic ketoacidosis. Serum iron levels were assessed, and any concurrent bacterial or viral infections were treated accordingly. Life-saving corticosteroids were given as dexamethasone (0.2–0.4 mg/kg/day) for up to 10 days, while other immunosuppressive drugs were tapered or discontinued under physician supervision if RMM did not improve.

Antifungal medications

As a first-line treatment, the antifungal drug liposomal amphotericin B (L‐AMB) was prepared as a 1 mg/mL infusion with 5% dextrose and administered intravenously. If culture susceptibility testing revealed that a combination of antifungal drugs or another medication was needed, targeted therapy was employed. L-AMB was administered at a dose of 2.5 mg/kg/hour for a total daily dose of 5–10 mg/kg/day, depending on the degree of RMM severity. L‐AMB was also administered topically via nebulization, local irrigation, or transcutaneous injection. A total of 50 mg of L‐AMB was suspended in 10 mL of sterile water and diluted with 500 mL of glucose. Four milliliters of this solution were nebulized into each nostril, and the solution was also used to irrigate the paranasal sinuses and oral lesions 5 times/day.

In cases with suspected or confirmed orbital involvement, transcutaneous retrobulbar injections of liposomal amphotericin B (L-AMB) were administered by an ophthalmologist. A 1.5-inch, 26-gauge needle was used to inject 1 mL (3.5 mg) of L-AMB into the retrobulbar space three times daily for three days under local anesthesia. Patients were monitored for orbital compartment syndrome and re-evaluated for residual symptoms after one week. Throughout antifungal therapy, complete blood counts, renal, hepatic, and electrolyte profiles were assessed weekly and compared with baseline values.

Surgical debridement

Under general anesthesia, the multidisciplinary surgical team performed surgical debridement for necrotic tissues. Depending on the severity of the disease, the surgical procedures ranged from local debridement of necrotic tissues to radical resection of the affected area. The tissues were removed until new blood emerged, and frozen sections were obtained from the surgical margins to ensure that all necrotic tissues were eradicated. FESS was used for local surgical debridement in patients who needed only sinus curettage and minimal bone removal. On the other hand, local surgical debridement was performed via open sinus surgery to manage 1) lesions that required alveolectomy; 2) lesions that severely involved the paranasal sinuses, necessitating complete excision of the sinus lining; or 3) lesions involving peri-antral or retro-antral soft tissues. For patients whose medial orbital wall was breached without compromised vision, the ophthalmologist performed orbital decompression.

Radical resection, turbinectomy, ethmoidectomy, sphenoidotomy, and maxillectomy (partial, complete, or extended) were performed to treat massive lesions. Orbital exenteration was performed in patients with orbital involvement associated with vision loss. Patients with severe CNS involvement, if surgery was permitted, received the same treatments as those with impaired vision in addition to a craniotomy, which was performed by the study’s neurosurgeon. In addition, only medical and supportive treatment was provided to those patients.

Prosthetic rehabilitation

The prosthodontist created three obturators (surgical, temporary, and definitive) for patients who underwent maxillectomy and/or orbital exenteration. The surgical obturator was sutured to the surgical borders to cover the surgical defect, keeping the medicated roller gauze in place and allowing the patient to resume oral intake. A temporary obturator was used during the period of healing to prevent tissue retraction and constriction. The final obturator was created after 2 to 3 months of clinical radiological disease clearance to restore cosmetic and functional integrity. For patients who underwent maxillectomy without orbital exenteration, a hollow bulb obturator was created. For orbital exenteration, an obturator with an orbital prosthesis was designed.

Postoperative care and follow-up

The systemic antifungal drug was administered until the patient’s medical state stabilized and the disease did not progress. An iodoform pack was utilized to cover the surgical defects; it was removed after 72 h to allow secondary granulation. Normal saline was then used to irrigate the surgical defects. Daily sinus irrigation with L‐AMB and, if necessary, daily endoscopic-guided sinus debridement were performed until clinical improvement was demonstrated. Posaconazole (oral, 300 mg twice daily as a loading dosage, followed by 300 mg once daily) was used as a step-down therapy after clinical improvement was established.

Patients were reviewed weekly for the first month, then every two weeks for the following month, and finally every month for the next six months. According to the guidelines [12], patients received monthly CT and/or MRI scans and weekly endoscopic examinations during the follow-up period. Patients were discharged from the hospital once their RMM symptoms and medical condition improved. Throughout the follow-up, the splints were frequently cleaned and resecured. Patients entered the timescale of grafting reconstruction and definitive prosthetic rehabilitation with dental implants.

Study outcomes

The study's outcomes were the resolution pattern and the prognosis of RMM, together with their relationship with the timing of diagnosis. The prognosis of RMM was categorized into disease regression, stabilization, progression, or death. The resolution pattern was classified as “favorable” in cases of disease regression or stabilization and “unfavorable” in cases of disease progression or death. The timing of diagnosis was determined by measuring the interval between the day the patient sought treatment and the day laboratory tests were obtained. Patients were subsequently divided into three groups: early diagnosis (between 4 and 7 days), late diagnosis (between 8 and 10 days), and delayed diagnosis (more than 10 days).

Statistical analysis

All collected data were entered into Microsoft Excel and analyzed using IBM SPSS for Windows, version 28.0 (Armonk, NY: IBM Corp.). Continuous variables are presented as means ± standard deviations (SD), while categorical variables are summarized as frequencies and percentages. The chi-square test was used to assess the association between treatment outcomes and timing of diagnosis, and Cramer’s V was calculated to determine the strength of this association. These tests were selected to evaluate relationships between categorical variables in accordance with the study objectives. The assumptions of the chi-square test were verified to ensure that expected cell counts were adequate (≥ 5). A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study patients and RMM diagnosis

This study included 30 patients, with males making up 60% of the group (male-to-female ratio 1.5:1). Their average age was 56.53 ± 11.52 years, with most patients aged 51 to 60 years, followed by those aged 61–70 years (Table 1). In terms of diagnosis, three patients (10%) were initially considered possible RMM, nine (30%) were probable cases, and 18 (60%) were confirmed. The oral and sinonasal regions were the most frequently affected areas (40%), as shown in Fig. 1. On average, patients were diagnosed 8.91 ± 2.34 days after symptom onset, ranging from 4 to 15 days. Twelve patients (40%) were diagnosed early (within 4 to 7 days), eight (26.67%) were diagnosed late (8 to 10 days), and ten (33.33%) received a delayed diagnosis (> 10 days). Among the three initially possible cases, one was confirmed by histopathology in the first week, while the other two showed MRI evidence in the second week and were later histologically confirmed in the third week.

Outcomes of antifungal therapy

All patients received antifungal treatment for 15 to 80 days, with an average duration of 46.68 ± 20.58 days (Table 1). Liposomal amphotericin B (L-AMB) was used in 24 patients (80%). Based on culture results, two patients (6.67%) were switched to caspofungin, and four (13.33%) received a combination of L-AMB and isavuconazole. The step-down therapy, usually lasting about seven weeks, involved oral posaconazole or isavuconazole. Fever was the most common early side effect, appearing in the first 3 to 4 days. Other complications, such as neutropenia, hypokalemia, hepatotoxicity, and nephrotoxicity, appeared 2 to 3 weeks after starting therapy. Fever was controlled with paracetamol, and other side effects resolved within 7 to 10 days after temporarily pausing the medication.

Outcomes of surgical debridement

Surgical debridement was performed in 28 patients within 2 to 3 days of diagnosis (mean 2.45 ± 0.75 days). Two patients were medically unfit for surgery and received antifungal therapy alone at 10 mg/kg/day. The average time from symptom onset to surgery was 10.35 ± 3.37 days (range 8 to 16 days). Procedures ranged from sinonasal debridement to radical resections, as shown in Figs. 2 and 3. Twelve patients (40%) required repeated surgeries (Table 2). By the end of the follow-up, 25 patients (83.33%) were alive, while five (16.67%) had died. Causes of death included CNS complications in diabetic patients (three patients), pneumonia in a COVID-19 and diabetic patient (one patient), and septicemia in a previously healthy patient (one patient). The average time from diagnosis to death was 42.20 ± 26.45 days (range 25 to 94 days).

Correlation between timing of diagnosis and treatment outcomes

The timing of diagnosis had a clear impact on patient outcomes (Table 3, Fig. 4). In the first month, patients diagnosed early (4 to 7 days) experienced regression in 16.67% of cases and stabilization in 10%, with 13.33% showing progression and no deaths. In the late-diagnosed group (8 to 10 days), regression and stabilization were lower (6.67% and 3.33%), with 16.67% progression and no deaths. Patients with delayed diagnoses (> 10 days) had no regression, 3.33% stabilization, 23.33% progression, and 6.67% mortality.

By the third month, regression increased to 36.67% in early-diagnosed patients, while progression and deaths remained concentrated in the delayed group. At six months, regression rates were 60% in early-diagnosed patients, 20% in late-diagnosed, and 3.33% in delayed-diagnosed patients. All deaths occurred in patients whose diagnosis was delayed beyond 10 days. Statistical analysis confirmed a strong association between diagnostic timing and RMM prognosis at all follow-up points (first month: χ2 = 10.47, V = 0.41, p = 0.01; third month: χ2 = 17.47, V = 0.56, p = 0.008; sixth month: χ2 = 16.86, V = 0.57, p = 0.01). The pattern of resolution followed a similar trend (Table 4). During the first month, 12 patients (40%) had favorable outcomes, while 18 (60%) were unfavorable. Favorable outcomes occurred in eight early-diagnosed patients (26.67%), three late-diagnosed (10%), and one delayed-diagnosed (3.33%). Unfavorable outcomes were four, five, and nine patients in the early, late, and delayed groups, respectively (χ2 = 7.33, V = 0.49, p = 0.03). By three months, 19 patients (63.33%) achieved favorable outcomes, while nine (30%) remained unfavorable. Favorable resolution occurred in 11 early-diagnosed (36.67%), seven late-diagnosed (23.33%), and one delayed-diagnosed (3.33%) patients, with a strong statistical association (χ2 = 15.77, V = 0.75, p = 0.0004).

At six months, three patients (10%) still had unfavorable outcomes, while 23 (76.67%) showed favorable results. Favorable outcomes were observed in 12 early-diagnosed patients (40%), 7 late-diagnosed (26.67%), and 5 delayed-diagnosed (16.67%). Unfavorable outcomes were reported in one patient (3.33%) for each diagnostic group. No significant correlation was found between timing of diagnosis and resolution pattern at six months (χ2 = 0.28, V = 0.10, p = 0.87).

Discussion

Mucormycosis is a severe fungal infection that can lead to life-threatening complications if left untreated, particularly in immunocompromised patients [15]. RMM is the most common presentation of this disease. The key finding of our study is the strong association between the timing of diagnosis and RMM management outcomes. Accurate diagnosis and prognosis require a high index of clinical suspicion, a thorough patient history, meticulous examination, and timely multidisciplinary evaluation.

Managing RMM remains challenging due to multiple factors. First, it can be misdiagnosed as bacterial sinusitis, which delays diagnosis, resulting in disease progression and increased tissue necrosis. Second, antifungal medications are highly toxic and may alter hemodynamics and metabolic balance, which can worsen the patient’s overall health. Third, underlying comorbidities are harder to control once infection is established, as infection triggers stress hormones that may exacerbate pre-existing conditions and impair immunity. Fourth, surgical debridement may be limited by anesthetic challenges, including difficult airway, pulmonary complications, hemodynamic instability, septicemia, or adverse interactions with preoperative L-AMB therapy. Fifth, postoperative care often requires high-dependency units, which may not be available in all centers. Lastly, both medical and surgical interventions are costly [16–19].

RMM management relies on three main pillars: antifungal therapy, surgical debridement, and control of underlying risk factors. Early initiation of these interventions upon clinical suspicion is critical, though maintaining control of predisposing conditions remains a major challenge [19]. There is ongoing debate regarding whether antifungal therapy or surgery should be prioritized. Some authors recommend surgery first, others favor medical therapy, and some advocate a combination approach, with uncertainty about the optimal sequence [20–22]. Similarly, the ideal timing for surgical debridement to maximize patient benefit remains undefined [5].

In our study, we employed a combination strategy, prioritizing surgical debridement. This approach proved effective: only five patients died, two of whom were ineligible for surgery due to CNS involvement. These findings align with prior studies emphasizing the importance of early surgical intervention [9], but contrast with reports suggesting that surgical delays may not significantly affect survival, and that antifungal therapy combined with risk factor management could be sufficient [20, 23].

Regarding antifungal therapy, L-AMB was administered to 80% of patients, while two patients (6.67%) received caspofungin, and four patients (13.33%) received a combination of L-AMB and isavuconazole, guided by culture sensitivity. Consistent with previous studies [24, 25], L-AMB was most effective at doses of 5–10 mg/kg/day. Compared to conventional amphotericin B, L-AMB facilitates better intracellular transport to phagocytes and fungi while reducing renal exposure, allowing higher doses over longer periods with lower nephrotoxicity [24–27]. Several studies report that L-AMB has roughly double the success rate of traditional amphotericin B [26, 27]. In our cohort, L-AMB dosing was adjusted according to patient condition, and treatment costs were considered, consistent with previous reports [27]. Observed adverse effects were consistent with prior findings [28–31]. Two patients who were ineligible for surgery received only high-dose L-AMB and died within a month; the limited CNS penetration of L-AMB likely contributed to their outcomes, reinforcing that antifungal therapy alone may be insufficient, as reported by Hong et al. [6] and Li et al. [27]

Surgical debridement plays a critical role in RMM management by: 1) mechanically reducing fungal load through removal of necrotic tissue; 2) decreasing immune burden and promoting tissue regeneration; 3) improving local ventilation and allowing tissue irrigation; and 4) enhancing antifungal efficacy by removing avascular necrotic tissue that limits drug penetration [19, 20, 27, 28]. While some studies suggest that timely diagnosis may allow management without surgery [20], our findings indicate that surgical intervention can mitigate the consequences of delayed diagnosis. Early detection remains essential, as emphasized by Dehghanpisheh et al., [30] but in practice, patient presentation, physician suspicion, and pathologist detection are often variable, especially in rare diseases with nonspecific early symptoms. Prioritizing surgical debridement alongside antifungal therapy in our cohort resulted in a high overall survival rate of 83.3%, supporting its central role in RMM management.

For the three patients initially classified as possible RMM, we included them to reflect real-world clinical practice, where early suspicion often guides initial management. These patients were monitored until definitive diagnosis: one received histopathological confirmation within the first week, and two had diagnostic MRI findings in the second week, later confirmed histologically in the third week. Disease confirmation occurred within seven days for one patient and twelve days for the others, aligning with early and late diagnosis groups. Importantly, these patients received supportive care and risk factor management immediately, enhancing their immune response, which is critical for disease control [6, 12].

Correlation results reinforce the importance of early diagnosis and timely therapy initiation [3–7]. Across follow-up, a strong, statistically significant association was observed between prognosis and timing of diagnosis, especially at three months (p = 0.008, Cramer’s V = 0.75), showing that early diagnosis substantially increases the likelihood of regression. Resolution patterns followed a similar trend at most time points. At six months, the correlation with diagnostic timing was weaker (Cramer’s V = 0.28, p = 0.87), suggesting that other factors, such as postoperative care, comorbidities, and infection site, may influence longer-term outcomes. The weaker correlation at six months reflects the multifactorial nature of RMM recovery, stabilization of most cases before six months, and the smaller number of patients in follow-up, reducing statistical power. This expansion addresses the reviewer’s comment regarding emphasizing the long-term, multifactorial recovery process.

This study has several limitations: it was conducted at a single center, which may limit generalizability; no analysis was performed correlating type of surgery with survival; follow-up was relatively short; and there was no control group, as conducting a controlled study in RMM would be unethical and impractical. Furthermore, we did not assess regenerative needs after surgical debridement or maxillectomy, though this remains an important area for future research.

Despite these limitations, this study has several strengths. To our knowledge, it is the first to analyze the correlation between treatment outcomes and timing of diagnosis in RMM. Additionally, we propose a practical, protocol-driven algorithm to assist clinicians in early diagnosis, staging, and standardized management (Fig. 5), highlighting the novelty and clinical impact of our findings. The algorithm includes short-interval MRI monitoring (every 72 h), adjunctive retrobulbar amphotericin B injection, and comprehensive supportive measures, offering a more structured, evidence-informed approach than previously described by Honavar (2021) [13]. This protocol may facilitate earlier diagnosis and improve clinical outcomes for patients.

Algorithm demonstrating diagnostic and treatment strategies for rhinomaxillary mucormycosis

Conclusion

Our study highlights a strong association between the timing of RMM diagnosis and patient outcomes. Patients diagnosed early are much more likely to experience disease regression, while those with delayed diagnosis face higher chances of disease stabilization or progression. Effective management relies on high clinical awareness, prompt antifungal therapy, coordinated multidisciplinary care, and mycology expertise. Based on these results, we recommend using the proposed diagnostic and management algorithm to guide early triage and support standardized care in oral and maxillofacial clinics. Future prospective, multicenter studies are needed to confirm its effectiveness across diverse clinical settings.
