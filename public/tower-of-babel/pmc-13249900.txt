Exercise and smoking: health rivals revealing shared protective mechanisms in Parkinson’s?
Authors: Jules M Janssen Daalen, Sabine Schootemeijer, Thomas Oosterhof, Jilke M Ripping, Bastiaan R Bloem, Stephen N Gomperts, Michael A Schwarzschild
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCompelling epidemiological evidence suggests that exercise and smoking are modifiable risk factors that are linked to a reduced risk of Parkinson’s disease. These two risk factors represent opposite ends of a spectrum: exercise is universally embraced, while smoking is rightly eschewed for its established adverse health effects. Yet, intriguingly, preclinical evidence suggests that at their biological cores, exercise and some of the many components of tobacco may share strikingly similar working mechanisms that may favorably modify PD risk or disease course, for which definitive evidence is still lacking. Here, we deconstruct these overlapping and putative neuroprotective mechanisms. Our aim is to transform this unexpected overlap into an actionable perspective toward identifying novel targets for disease-modifying therapies that can slow the progression of Parkinson’s disease, and to inspire novel translational efforts in disease modification in PD. We stress that while both factors may theoretically inform disease-modifying strategies for PD, in practice, only exercise should be promoted for its health benefits, whereas smoking remains firmly contraindicated due to its known detrimental health effects.Subject terms: Diseases, Neurology, Neuroscience

Introduction

For decades, studies have fairly consistently reported negative associations between both smoking and physical activity and the risk of developing Parkinson’s disease (PD)1,2. Ever since, smoking has been a hot, intensely debated topic in PD research3–7. The inverse association of exercise with PD seems to square with its widespread beneficial effects8. By contrast, the inverse association between smoking and PD strikes many as paradoxical, as smoking is known to cause or hasten a number of human diseases, including lung cancer and cardiovascular disease9. It is therefore critical to underscore that, despite these epidemiological observations, smoking is unequivocally harmful and is not advocated as a preventive or therapeutic strategy for any condition. At its biological core, certain molecular mechanisms engaged by smoking may exert disease-modifying effects that could help explain the reduced risk of PD among smokers10–12. This notion has held up in several supportive Mendelian randomization studies that allow for causal inferences of a true disease-modifying effect, even if tempered by their inherent limitations13–15. Interestingly, various molecular mechanisms of action underlying the cellular effects of smoking overlap with those of exercise, including stabilization of hypoxia inducible factor 1α (HIF-1α) and upregulation of anti-oxidative mechanisms and PGC-1α, an important mitochondrial regulator16–25. As such, we find exercise and smoking to be biological brethren through their putative neuroprotective mechanisms, yet paradoxically situated at opposite extremes of the overall health risk-to-benefit spectrum among potential interventions for PD. Unraveling the shared mechanisms underlying these two putative disease-modifying behaviors for PD might inform further therapeutic pursuits. Notably, several interventions linked to one or more of these mechanisms have been or are now being explored in PD, including several small-molecule compounds, and even controlled exposure to nicotine, low-dose carbon monoxide, and moderate hypoxia26–29.

Here, we discuss the presumed neuroprotective mechanisms of smoking and exercise and then review their overlap. Finally, we highlight recent research investigating these pathways in the context of PD, and describe several trials of interventions tailored to these insights that may have potential to delay the onset or slow the course of PD, with the aim of catalyzing new translational efforts into disease modification in PD. We emphasize that the discussion on putative disease-modifying mechanisms behind smoking is intended solely to inform mechanistic understanding and guide the search for safe and targeted disease-modifying therapies, not to promote a smoking habit in any condition.

Epidemiology of potentially protective effects of smoking and exercise

Evidence for an association

The inverse association between smoking and the subsequent diagnosis of PD is among the most consistent lifestyle links across all of neuroepidemiology, and certainly among risk factors for PD. The first reports about smoking in PD were published over 50 years ago, and a 20-year-old landmark twin study showed a negative correlation between smoking dose and PD within twin pairs, even with a 10-year lag time before PD diagnosis10. Several studies report a dose-dependent association between smoking and risk of PD, with heavy smokers having the lowest risk of developing PD11,30,31 and former and current smokers having a lower risk of PD compared to participants who never, or occasionally, smoked2,11,13,32–34. Whether second-hand smoking is also inversely associated with risk of PD remains a topic of debate35–37. Some observational evidence suggests smoking behavior is associated with PD phenotype and progression. For example, some studies suggest that smokers have a later age of PD onset (suggesting disease modification)38,39, although other studies describe an earlier onset40. Moreover, some studies found that smokers have a lower (better) MDS-UPDRS-III41 and a different non-motor symptom spectrum42,43, but other studies report no associations with PD phenotype44. Lastly, although tobacco use is one of the most important risk factors for dementia and death in the general population, population studies report lower neurology-related mortality in current smokers compared to never-smokers in PD. Notably, the incidence of cancer-related mortality was significantly higher in smokers45.

On the other hand, exercise is a widely advised non-pharmacological intervention in PD management. Exercise offers symptomatic benefits, and may also exert potential disease-modifying effects, as suggested by observational evidence and supported by promising, though not definitive, findings from clinical trials. One synthesis of meta-analyses demonstrated that greater premorbid amounts of physical activity were associated with a dose-dependent reduced risk of developing PD1. In one study stratifying for task-related physical activity, only household and commuting-related physical activity were associated with a reduced PD risk, and leisure or occupation-related physical activity were not46. Exercise has dose-dependent positive symptomatic effects on gait and postural stability, independence in activities of daily living, and several cognitive symptoms47,48. Finally, two randomized controlled trials showed that aerobic exercise resulted in a stabilization of motor progression in PD49,50. In one of these studies, this was associated with adaptive plasticity of the brain, which coincided with enhanced cognitive performance51. This adaptive and compensatory neuroplasticity may reflect disease-modifying effects of exercise, although such claims cannot be made from these study designs. Intervention studies addressing the hypothesized preventive effects of exercise in prodromal cohorts are just beginning [NCT06193252].

Limitations

While the possibility of a protective effect of smoking in PD is supported by observational studies demonstrating a robust inverse association in combination with Mendelian randomization and preclinical studies, several alternative explanations remain plausible. First, the association could also be attributed to an intact and higher-than-average dopaminergic state or other molecular mechanisms that make one more prone to addictive behaviors like smoking and caffeine consumption. which is also consistently linked to a reduced risk of PD52,53. This is in keeping with a proposed addictive substance-averse premorbid personality54. This enhanced dopaminergic state might directly protect against PD onset. Alternatively, individuals with elevated risk-seeking behavior, which would lead them to pick up smoking, could also be genetically linked in other ways to factors associated with a lower PD risk. Notably, caloric restriction induced by smoking may be one confounding factor in this association. Some evidence suggests caloric restriction may have preventive effects for PD, but evidence regarding the threshold required for benefit and the effects on survival in non-human primates is conflicting55,56. Importantly, while caloric restriction has been hypothesized to hold preventive effects, malnutrition—reported in up to 60% of patients with PD—may complicate this relationship, given its association with adverse health outcomes and poorer quality of life57.

On the other hand, the reduced risk of PD among smokers may result from reverse causation, such that in the early, prodromal phase of PD, addictive behaviors such as smoking are less likely to develop or more likely to halt. Indeed, PD patients quit smoking with less difficulty than controls, possibly due to attenuated addictive effects of nicotine due to dopaminergic loss58. As noted above, Mendelian randomization studies report comparable risk reductions to observational studies13–15. Both Mendelian randomization studies and case-control studies remain susceptible to survival bias across groups. For example, putative anti-apoptotic smoking-induced effects on Bcl-2 or p53 lead to a higher risk for malignancies and might lead to unbalanced drop-out in the smoking group. However, such problems are not as prominent in prospective cohort studies, where smoking’s association with PD risk reduction remains strong2. One example is the link with diabetes, which has been associated with an increased risk of Parkinson’s disease. Exercise helps to reduce the risk of diabetes, and in doing so, it might indirectly help to reduce the risk of Parkinson’s disease, even without a direct neuroprotective effect on the nigrostriatal circuitry59. An argument against this interpretation is the notion that studies that have investigated diabetes as a risk factor for PD usually adjust for exercise patterns, yet these demonstrate a persistently increased PD risk in diabetics60,61. Another hypothetical factor is the link with motivational behaviors, which might well be reduced in persons in a prodromal phase of Parkinsonism who already have deficits in dopaminergic signaling, so that those individuals would be less keen to engage in exercise. This concept has never been studied formally, but is worth exploring further. In any case, the lack of mechanistic clarity underlying the association of smoking with reduced risk of PD hinders the identification of putative therapeutic targets, hampering the development of experimental and translational studies of candidate compounds free from the associated harm of tobacco use.

Protective mechanisms in smoking and exercise?

There are several thousand molecules in tobacco smoke, and the search to date for putative molecular mediator(s) of neuroprotection has been far from exhaustive. Although many molecules in tobacco and tobacco smoke are definitively harmful, several dozen individual constituents of tobacco or its smoke with potentially neuroprotective properties for PD have been identified and cataloged62–64.

First, as non-smokers who used snus (smokeless tobacco) have a substantially lower risk of PD, nicotine or other components of tobacco leaves have been suggested to be the mediating factor65,66. Experimental evidence supporting a role for nicotine includes its potential capacity to inhibit the action of striatal DAT or to increase action potentials along nigrostriatal axons, increasing dopamine levels in the synaptic gap67,68. One recent trial did not show disease-modifying effects of 1-year transdermal nicotine administration in manifest PD27. Second, preclinical studies report neuroprotective effects of carbon monoxide at the low doses seen in smokers, including reduction of α-synuclein pathology in association with activation of protective signaling cascades, including HIF-1α and heme oxygenase 1 (HO-1)26. Third, another mediating mechanism may be (intermittent) hypoxia, perhaps as a result of carbon monoxide exposure. Both cigarette smoke and carbon monoxide may activate HIF-1α17,69,70. Preclinical evidence supports neuroprotective effects of moderate intermittent hypoxia through activation of the hypoxia response pathway29. It should be noted that direct application of cigarette smoke extract on retinal epithelium actually worsens mitochondrial dysfunction and glycolysis by HIF-1α downregulation. This worsening was restored by incubating the cells in hypoxic conditions, as well as by administering the HIF prolyl hydroxylase inhibitor roxadustat71. Fourth, several compounds in tobacco smoke reduce MAO-B activity, thereby increasing dopamine levels72,73. Through this mechanism, compounds in tobacco smoke might impact striatal dopaminergic signaling similarly to caffeine, a different molecule linked with reduced PD incidence in epidemiological studies74. Lastly, smoking induces cytochrome P450 1A2 (CYP1A2), which plays important roles in detoxification of toxins, which is reviewed elsewhere75.

Of note, there is overwhelming evidence that smoking increases the risk of dementia76,77, especially vascular dementia, but reduces the risk of Lewy body dementia78. While this finding might cast doubt about the established relation between smoking and PD as both are usually slowly progressive neurodegenerative diseases occurring in later life, it should be noted that (vascular) dementia and PD have widely differing risk factors, most notably in divergent genetic, lifestyle, environmental, and vascular risk factors79,80.

Several preclinical and clinical studies have aimed to elucidate the neuroprotective mechanisms of exercise in the light of PD. These include increasing neuronal resilience through activating pro-survival pathways and improving adaptive response to energetic stress, such as activating anti-inflammatory control mechanisms and improving mitochondrial dysfunction, especially by enhancing mitochondrial turnover81–88. These mechanisms have been reviewed in detail recently89. Furthermore, preclinical evidence substantiates the induction of various growth factor-related mechanisms, including IGF-1, clusterin, irisin, Nrf2, BDNF, VEGF, and the hypoxia response pathway (through both HIF-1α and HIF-1α independent mechanisms)29,90. Theoretically, such mechanisms may be able to strengthen functional connectivity or alternatively induce compensatory mechanisms, as is seen in neuro-imaging studies51, although there is no available evidence to support this direct relation.

Hypoxia response pathway (HIF-1)

A compelling link between smoking and exercise lies in the innate response to hypoxia, which is mainly mediated by hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF) 1α29,91. Exercise leads to increased HIF-1 levels due to increased muscle oxygen consumption, and preclinical studies suggest HIF-1 is essential for the exercise-induced anti-apoptotic effects in neurons, but this is not investigated in experimental PD models specifically92–94.

Evidence supports HIF-1 activation by carbon monoxide through hemoglobin binding and secondary hypoxia, and by nicotine through the activation of a nicotinic acetylcholine receptor (nAChR)-mediated signaling pathway16,69,70. There is also evidence for HIF-1α stabilization by cigarette smoke in pulmonary tissue, but not in endothelial tissue, where smoke seems to decrease HIF-1 expression17,95. Preclinical studies suggest that activation of this hypoxia-response pathway through stabilizing HIF-1α has potential beneficial effects on pathophysiological mechanisms in PD, most importantly mitochondrial function, oxidative stress, and potentially neuro-inflammation, reviewed elsewhere29.

Nrf2/HO-1

The second mechanistic connection between exercise and smoking involves the activation of Nrf2 (nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2), which mediates the oxidative stress response and is neuroprotective in models of toxin-induced parkinsonism96,97. Exercise induces a systemic Nrf2-mediated redox stress response in an age- and dose-dependent manner, with limited studies demonstrating such effects in experimental PD models98–103. However, evidence for the effects of tobacco smoke exposure on Nrf2 activation is conflicting. Some studies demonstrate activation and translocation of Nrf2, potentially as a compensatory protective response to tobacco-induced oxidative stress18, whereas other studies indicate impaired Nrf2 signaling and the promotion of inflammation in response to tobacco smoke exposure104. In addition, both exercise and smoking induce HO-1, a stress protein and enzyme that plays a major role in the body’s antioxidative and anti-inflammatory defense systems62,105–108. It serves as a primary protective mechanism against oxidative stress and cellular injury caused by various noxious stimuli, including reactive oxygen species (ROS), inflammation, and hypoxia.

PGC-1α

Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator 1-alpha (PGC-1α) is an important regulator of mitochondrial turnover, including biogenesis, and is induced by exercise19–21. PGC-1α polymorphisms increase PD risk, and preclinical evidence demonstrates a protective role against toxin-induced parkinsonism109–111. Furthermore, use of glitazones (indirect) activators of PGC-1α used to improve insulin sensitivity in diabetics, is associated with reduced PD risk—although this effect could be mediated by diabetes-related effects112.-Regarding smoking, preclinical evidence suggests that PGC-1α is downregulated in response to tobacco-induced TNF-α activation113. However, active smokers have upregulated PGC-1α expression, possibly as a conditioning response of smoking behavior114. Nicotine might be a mediating factor in this longer-term response115. Contrarily, asthma-related research suggests adverse effects of nicotine on mitochondrial structural integrity in airway smooth muscle116. A phase 2 study did not demonstrate disease-modifying effects of pioglitazone, an activator of PPAR-γ (peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma) upstream of PGC-1α117.

Anti-inflammatory effects

The immunomodulatory effects of exercise are complex and comprise different effects in the short and long-term. Over intervention periods of weeks to months, exercise suppresses pro-inflammatory markers such as TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β in experimental PD models, while activating anti-inflammatory markers, such as IL-10 and TGF-β, also in the elderly118–122. Evidence for smoking is more conflicting. Whereas carbon monoxide may have a similar anti-inflammatory profile to exercise123 and nicotine induces inhibition of microglia and reduces TNF-α activation through the activation of the α7 nicotinic acetylcholine receptor (nAChR)22,124–128, tobacco exposure exerts acute pro-inflammatory effects by inducing the production of IL-1 and TNF-α129,130.

Other mechanisms

Some mechanisms that are considered important for the neuroprotective effects of exercise are minimally explored in studies of smoking. For example, the anti-inflammatory protein clusterin is of recent interest in the neuroinflammatory pathophysiology of PD90 and increases with exercise131. Evidence on its relation with smoking is sparse, although some studies suggest that cigarette smoke induces clusterin expression132. One study suggests a relation between smoking intensity and salivary clusterin levels96. Similarly, brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) is potently activated by aerobic exercise in a dose-dependent fashion. The acute effects of smoking on BDNF levels are unclear, but smokers have higher BDNF than non-smokers, and some studies suggest a dose-dependent increase in BDNF with duration of smoking behavior133,134. Irisin, a myokine, is one of the candidate mediators for increased BDNF expression with exercise135. Irisin already showed preventative potential in a mouse model of PD136. Whether it is affected by smoking is unclear.

Translation of evidence into therapeutic opportunities

First, we stress our unequivocal discouragement of smoking and our wholehearted support for exercise, especially aerobic exercise, for all. However, the evidence above warrants further consideration of the potential beneficial effects of smoking that might inform translational or early-phase trials. Although several experimental phase 1 and 2 trials are currently underway or have recently been published that target one or more of smoking’s components and have overlapping pathways with exercise, a more systematic consideration of multiple candidate convergent mediators of potential protective effects of exercise and smoking could hasten clinical development of novel targets. We note that regardless of the specific shared molecular mechanisms implicated by the inverse associations of exercise and smoking with PD, their therapeutic translation is particularly relevant to the prevention of PD (more than to its treatment after diagnosis), given that their strongest epidemiological links are to PD diagnosis.

Nicotine

Several studies have investigated whether nicotine is the prime mediating mechanism responsible for the neuroprotective effects of smoking. Evidence from cellular models suggests nicotine may be primarily neuroprotective through nicotinic cholinergic activation, which is a proposed mediating pathway for neuroprotective (and neuroplasticity-related) effects of exercise137,138. However, as recent studies of nicotine in manifest PD did not show clinical improvement, further initiatives are currently on hold27,139.

Low-dose carbon monoxide

With nicotine trials in PD demonstrating no clear benefit on PD symptoms or progression, carbon monoxide has emerged as a potential novel target. Low-dose carbon monoxide exposure in smokers activates the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway and induces functional hypoxia, which stabilizes HIF-1α140,141. These two mechanisms are also engaged through exercise, as discussed above. In this context, a recent preclinical study reported neuroprotective effects of low-dose carbon monoxide in animal models of PD. They showed protection of dopamine cells and reduced alpha-synuclein pathology in association with engagement of HIF-1α, HO-1 signaling, the lysosomal enzyme cathepsin D, and Polo-like kinase 2, the latter two proteins that contribute to α-synuclein degradation26. The potential of controlled carbon monoxide exposure as an interesting therapeutic strategy that links to exercise is underlined by recent advances in elite sports that combine carbon monoxide with altitude exposure (see hypoxic conditioning below)142. An oral liquid formulation of carbon monoxide with a completed phase 1 safety study [NCT03926819] and demonstrated pharmacokinetic and safety markers is slated for evaluation in a pending phase 2a clinical trial in PD.

Hypoxic conditioning

Evolutionarily preserved adaptive neuronal responses to hypoxia, including the hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF) cascade, may have a beneficial impact on pathophysiological mechanisms in PD143. Exercise increases HIF-1 because of increased metabolic demand88, and HIF-1 is necessary for exercise-induced nigral neuroprotection92. This provides a compelling argument for hypoxic conditioning, i.e., repeated exposure to moderate levels of hypoxia, as a non-pharmacological intervention in PD. Although visits to high altitude and chronic hypoxia have been proposed as PD interventions, these should not be equated with natural models of hypoxic conditioning. Notably, there is no evidence for a lower incidence of PD at high altitude. Furthermore, populations that permanently live at high altitude have genetic adaptations that cause a blunted HIF-1 response144.

Exposure to carbon monoxide-induced hypoxia or to hypoxia directly reduces oxidative stress and rescues nigrostriatal degeneration in a preclinical model of complex I deficiency145,146. Notably, obstructive sleep apnea, characterized by chronic intermittent hypoxia in association with hypercarbia, is associated with a greater risk of cognitive decline and greater risk of PD147–149.

Small-molecule approaches

Several small-molecule compounds that target mechanisms were discussed in the previous section. For the hypoxia response pathway, these mechanisms have been summarized elsewhere29. Briefly, stabilization of HIF-1α as the prime mediator of the hypoxia response pathway is effectively exerted by prolyl hydroxylase domain (PHD) inhibitors (e.g., daprodustat, dimethyloxalylglycine or DMOG, roxadustat), HIF-1α inducers (e.g., albendazole, agmatine), modifiers of hemoglobin-oxygen affinity (e.g., HypoxyStat150), and, less specific in its target, iron chelators (deferoxamine or DFO, deferiprone). Out of these, only deferiprone has been investigated in PD. Deferiprone decreased substantia nigra iron levels151, but in a phase 2 trial, the participants treated with deferiprone showed worse scores on a gold-standard motor scale for PD152. The latter was perhaps caused by a reduction in dopamine synthesis in the drug-naïve participants, so possible beneficial effects in levodopa-treated patients cannot be excluded.

Nrf2 stabilizers are small molecules that disrupt Keap1-mediated Nrf2 ubiquitination, decreasing its breakdown and increasing Nrf2 translocation into the nucleus153. Several such compounds have been tested in preclinical PD, most prominently sulforaphane154–156 (an anti-oxidant available as a supplement) and dimethylfumarate (commercially available as Tecfidera® in multiple sclerosis)157–160. Omaveloxolone is a KEAP1 inhibitor, currently under investigation in mitochondrial myopathy and recently FDA approved for Friedreich’s ataxia161–163. Comparisons between the potency of inducing downstream effects have yet to be conducted, and safety studies in PD are needed.

Some PGC-1α small molecule stabilizers have been proposed164,165, primarily for use in diabetes mellitus as a mediator of glucose metabolism. One of these small molecules (ZLN005) has been explored in preclinical parkinson models and demonstrates increased expression of mitochondrial respiratory chain-associated genes, improved mitochondrial viability, and reduced dopaminergic neuron loss166,167. Notably, diabetes, as opposed to smoking and exercise, is a medical condition associated with increased PD incidence of 25–40%168.

Lastly, given the anti-inflammatory properties of exercise and some components of tobacco smoke described above, a mixture of anti-inflammatory agents could be explored, targeting multiple pathways all at once. Such a pathophysiology-agnostic regimen avoids the single pathway paradigm that might underlie the failure of many disease-modifying efforts in unselected populations169. However, what such a cocktail should include, and whether it should be limited to anti-inflammatory agents, is currently unknown. Similarly, a better understanding of the nature of the association between smoking and Parkinson’s disease could inform future prevention studies in non-pharmacological interventions such as lifestyle interventions.

Conclusion

Smoking and exercise are two of the most disparate, yet compelling human behaviors associated with reduced incidence of PD. While on the surface these have striking overall opposite health effects, at their biological cores, these exposures share some remarkable similarities in their putative neuroprotective mechanisms. Understanding these overlapping mechanisms may provide insight into potential therapeutic strategies for PD using both non-pharmacological and molecular interventions, and the inception of novel translational studies focused on disease modification in PD. Ultimately, clinical trials may target these shared mechanisms in order to prevent PD as well as slow its progression.
