Effects of dietary protein level on growth performance, intestinal morphology, and vaccine-induced antibody responses in Sakini chickens during the starter phase
Authors: Suman Karki, Shanker Raj Barsila, Naba Raj Devkota, Masaaki Toyomizu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Indigenous chickens are widely presumed to perform adequately on low-protein diets; however, their protein requirements under semi-intensive production conditions remain poorly defined. The present study evaluated the effects of graded dietary crude protein (CP) levels on growth performance, intestinal morphology, physiological stress, and Newcastle disease (ND) vaccine-induced antibody responses in Sakini chickens. A total of 300 one-day-old chicks were fed a commercial pre-starter diet for 7 days and then allotted to isocaloric diets (2800 kcal ME/kg) containing 18%, 19%, 20%, 21%, or 22% crude protein (CP) from days 8—63 in a completely randomized design with three replicates per treatment. Feed intake was recorded daily, body weight was measured weekly, and ND antibody titers were determined on days 7, 14, and 21 post-vaccination. At the end of the experiment, intestinal histomorphometry and the heterophil-to-lymphocyte (H/L) ratio were analyzed. Birds fed ≥20% CP showed significantly greater body weight and improved FCR compared with those fed 18–19% CP (p < 0.05). Duodenal villus height and the villus height-to-crypt depth ratio were increased in the higher CP groups, whereas the jejunal morphology remained similar. The lowest H/L ratio was observed in birds fed 22% CP (p < 0.05), indicating reduced physiological stress. Protective ND hemagglutination-inhibition titers (≥ log₂4) were observed only in birds fed 21–22% CP, whereas birds fed ≤20% CP did not consistently reach this level. In contrast, carcass characteristics and muscle pH remained unaffected by dietary CP. These findings indicate that dietary protein level influences growth performance, selected intestinal traits, physiological stress, and vaccine-induced antibody responses in Sakini chickens during the starter phase. Diets containing ≥20% CP support improved growth performance, while higher levels (21–22% CP) are associated with more favorable physiological status and vaccine-induced antibody responses under the conditions of this study.

Introduction

In South Asia, indigenous chickens play a significant role in rural livelihoods due to their long-established ability to adapt to harsh environments, low-input feeding, and endemic disease pressures (Rachman et al., 2024; Loengbudnark et al., 2024). Among these genetic resources, the Nepalese Sakini chicken is particularly important because of its cultural significance, resilience, and suitability for smallholder production systems (Desta, 2021). Because indigenous chickens have traditionally been raised under scavenging or semi-scavenging conditions characterized by limited protein availability (Ginindza, 2023), the long-standing assumption has been that they can maintain acceptable productivity even when offered low-protein diets (Kpomasse et al., 2023; Singh et al., 2023). However, as production systems for indigenous chickens are increasingly shifting toward semi-intensive management, a clearer understanding of their responses to dietary protein is becoming increasingly important (Desta, 2021).

Dietary crude protein (CP) is a key determinant of growth performance in poultry as it directly supports muscle accretion, organ development, and metabolic activity, particularly during the early growth phase, when nutrient requirements increase sharply (Kidd et al., 2004). Broiler starter diets typically require 21–23% CP to meet these demands (NRC, 1994; Leeson and Summers, 2005), but applying these recommendations directly to indigenous chickens may be inappropriate because of differences in growth rate, metabolic efficiency, and feed intake patterns. Indigenous chickens are generally regarded as able to sustain adequate productivity on low-protein diets within scavenging systems (Kpomasse et al., 2023; Singh et al., 2023). However, multiple controlled studies have shown enhanced growth performance, nutrient utilization, and physiological responses when elevated dietary protein levels are administered (Mbajiorgu et al., 2011; Dana et al., 2011; Maliwan et al., 2019). These differing results indicate that the protein requirements in indigenous chickens are not static but may fluctuate based on production conditions and nutritional management. There aren't many studies that look at growth performance, intestinal development, physiological stress, and immune response all at once for many South Asian indigenous breeds, like Sakini chickens. This gap makes it harder to figure out the exact protein needs for better production systems.

In addition to growth, dietary protein influences intestinal development, which plays a pivotal role in shaping nutrient utilization efficiency. High-quality protein diets can improve absorptive capacity and lower mucosal turnover costs by increasing the VH:CD ratio, promoting villus elongation, and decreasing crypt depth (Yamauchi and Isshiki, 1991; Uni et al., 1998). On the other hand, insufficient protein consumption has been linked to poor gut development and decreased nutrient assimilation in broilers and other poultry species (Laudadio et al., 2012; Buwjoom et al., 2010). Determining the nutritional requirements of native chickens requires assessing histomorphological responses to dietary protein because gut maturation during early life significantly influences future growth potential.

In addition to gut development, immune competence is also closely influenced by protein nutrition. Protein deficiency can reduce vaccine responsiveness and increase physiological stress markers such as the heterophil-to-lymphocyte (H/L) ratio, because amino acids serve as essential substrates for lymphocyte proliferation, antibody synthesis, and cytokine production (Maxwell, 1993; Klasing, 2007; Yegani and Korver, 2008 In South Asia, smallholder poultry production is seriously threatened by Newcastle disease (ND), and the development of protective antibody titers after vaccination may depend on adequate protein supply during early growth. However, research on protein–immune interactions in indigenous chickens remains limited, and Sakini chickens have been scarcely studied in this context. Moreover, no study has comprehensively evaluated the effects of dietary crude protein (CP) levels on growth performance, intestinal morphology, physiological stress, and vaccine-induced immunity in Sakini chickens.

Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate the effects of graded dietary protein levels on growth performance, intestinal morphology, physiological stress, and vaccine-induced antibody responses in Sakini chickens. It is hypothesized that increasing dietary protein levels would improve growth performance and be associated with coordinated changes in the physiological parameters.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval

The research protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Agriculture and Forestry University Institutional Review Board, Nepal (Approval number AFU-FWA00031653).

Experimental design, diet and management

Three hundred Nepalese indigenous day-old chicks of the Sakini breed, purchased from Mahalaxmi Poultry Farm, Chitwan, were brooded and given prestarter commercial feed for seven days, with a composition of 22% CP and 3087 ME (kcal/kg) diet. After the 7-day prestarter period, birds were randomly distributed into fifteen cages with twenty chicks each, and treatments were randomly assigned to pens in a completely randomized design and fed the experimental diets from day 8 to 63 as a single feeding phase.

The five different diets were formulated by maintaining iso-levels of energy, i.e., 2800 kcal of metabolizable energy (ME/kg), combined with each of the five crude protein (CP) levels, i.e., 18%, 19%, 20%, 21%, and 22%, respectively. The feed samples were analyzed at the Pancharatna Lab in Chitwan, Nepal. The ingredients and nutrient compositions used in the experiment are presented in Table 1. The chicks were reared on deep litter, and feed and water were provided in the morning and evening with ad libitum access. Experimental procedure and sample collection

All the chicks were housed in a deep litter system. During sampling, every step was taken to ensure welfare and to handle chicks with minimal stress. Following the completion of histomorphometry, serology, and blood smear examination, and the collection of laboratory data, the samples were decoded and examined. Body weight was recorded weekly, and feed intake was measured daily to calculate body weight gain and feed conversion ratio (FCR), following standard poultry performance evaluation procedures (Leeson and Summers, 2005). Mortality was recorded daily and used to adjust performance calculations where necessary.

Birds were vaccinated with Newcastle disease (ND) vaccine (F1 strain; Indovax Pvt. Ltd., Batch No: F1423, India) at the 5th week of age via the ocular route (eye drop), following the manufacturer’s recommended dosage. Blood samples were collected prior to vaccination to assess baseline maternal antibody levels and subsequently at 7, 14, and 21 days post-vaccination, to evaluate the humoral immune response. Antibody titers were determined using the hemagglutination inhibition (HI) test according to standard procedures (NRC, 1994; WOAH, 2021). Antibody titers obtained from the hemagglutination inhibition (HI) test were expressed as log₂ values (i.e., reciprocal of the highest serum dilution showing complete inhibition), which is standard practice to normalize data and facilitate parametric statistical analysis. At the end of the experimental period, three birds were randomly selected from each replicate (pen) for histomorphometric, organ and blood measurements. Birds were selected within each pen while excluding individuals that were visibly diseased, injured, or exhibiting extreme body weights, to represent the average population within each . In the 8th week, three birds were randomly selected from each treatment group, and blood samples (2 mL from each bird) were collected and stored in EDTA vials before being sent to the laboratory for determination of the heterophil to lymphocyte ratio (H/L ratio).

Histomorphometric examination

From each bird, tissue samples of the duodenum, jejunum, and ileum were collected and processed for histological slide preparation at Seed Medical Laboratories Pvt. Ltd., Chitwan, Nepal. One slide was prepared per intestinal segment per bird, resulting in nine slides per replicate (three birds × three intestinal segments). Chicks were decapitated. One-centimetre-long intestinal samples from the duodenum (from the pancreatic loop), jejunum (posterior to the pancreatic loop and anterior to Meckel’s diverticulum) and ileum (posterior to Meckel’s diverticulum and anterior to the ileocecal junction) were collected, cleaned, fixed in 10% buffer formalin solution, and kept at the laboratory for 48 h. The samples were dehydrated via successively increasing concentrations of alcohol (70%, 80%, 90%, 95%, and 100%) at 1 h intervals and then cleared in xylene for 2 h each. The tissues were then processed by infiltration with molten wax, embedded in molten paraffin, and labelled. Five-micron-thick sections were cut via a microtome knife.

Later, the tissue sections were mounted on clean glass slides, air-dried, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin. The slides were observed under an I0S N-Plan at 4x/0.10 magnification with a light microscope (Trinocular Microscope (with Camera), Optika Microscope Italy, Model-C-B3). Microscopy images were captured via OPTIKA PROVIEW software (using Lenovo laptop proview with a C-B3 camera from Optika Proview, Italy). Photographs were taken at various tissue sample sites with fine adjustments. A slide from Erma Inc. (code: SM-001, Tokyo, Japan), featuring a 0.01 mm linear scale with 100 divisions, and each division was 100 μm, was employed as a reference for measurement. An image of the micrometre scale on the stage of the microscope used for capturing tissue samples was employed to calculate the VD and CD by setting it as the reference scale in ImageJ software (https://imagej.en.softonic.com/). For histomorphometric analysis, ten well-oriented villi per intestinal segment (duodenum, jejunum, and ileum) were measured for each sampled bird and averaged, which is consistent with standard histomorphological procedures reported in previous poultry studies (Awad et al., 2009; Xu et al., 2003; Iji et al., 2001).

Heterophil/lymphocyte (H/L) ratio determination

The H/L ratio was evaluated at the 8th week of the experimental period. Accordingly, 1 mL of fresh blood was collected from the wing vein and stored in EDTA tubes. A drop of blood was smeared onto a glass slide for each bird. Once air-dried, smears were stained with Wright’s Giemsa solution (G1020, Solarbio, Beijing) according to the manufacturer's instructions for microscopic observation (Houwen, 2002). One hundred leucocytes, including lymphocytes, heterophils and monocytes, were counted in different fields on each slide via a Leica DM500 microscope (Leica Microsystems GmbH, Wetzlar, Germany) at a magnification of 100× (oil), and the H/L ratio was calculated by dividing the number of heterophils by the number of lymphocytes (Campbell, 2015). The image was captured by an Infinity2 1.4 Megapixel CCD USB 2.0 camera and processed with Infinity Analyze software (Release 5.0.3, Lumenera Inc., Ottawa, Ontario, Canada). The magnification was 100× (oil).

Visceral organ examination

Three birds were randomly selected from each replication on the 8th week of the experimental period, birds were properly rested, then weighed and slaughtered by cutting the cervical blood vessels and five minutes after the bleeding. After scalding in water at a temperature of 56°C for 1 min, the birds were manually plucked and eviscerated, and the carcasses were stored at 4°C for 24 h, and then dissected. Weights of various cut-up parts, along with live weight, dressed weight, and breast and leg piece weights, were recorded separately for each bird. Then, dressing percentage was calculated as the ratio between the carcass weight and live body weight after fasting. The weight percentages of breast muscle, leg piece muscle and other parts were given as the percentages of cold carcass weight.

The physical analysis of the meat was performed by measuring the degree of cooking loss and the water-holding capacity (by the drip loss method). Later, the meat pH was measured 15 min post-mortem (pH 15 m), 45 min post-mortem (pH 45 m) and 48 h post-slaughter (pH 48 h) via a digital pH meter (Hanna Instruments HI981036 Food Care Meat pH Tester-Woonsocket, Rhode Island, ) equipped with a specialized electrode. The degree of cooking loss was determined by placing the meat inside polyethylene bags in a water bath. The samples were heated to an internal temperature of 75 °C and cooled for 15 min under running tap water. They were removed from the bags, dried with filter paper, and weighed. The cooking loss was expressed as the percentage of loss relative to the initial weight (Honikel, 1998). Drip loss was measured according to the modified procedure of Honikel (1998). To measure the WHC, 40 grams of meat were weighed, placed on a wire, hung on a plastic bottle, and stored at 4°C for 2 days. The sample was then reweighed on day 2, and drip loss (%) was calculated as [100 × (weight of drip/initial sample weight)].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using R Statistics (version 4.3.2) synchronized with R Studio (https://rstudio.com/products/rstudio/download/#download) via one-way analysis of variance. The mean difference was set by Duncan's multiple range test (DMRT) at p = 0.05. Before subjecting the parameters to ANOVA, the data were checked for normal distribution, and unless they met the criteria of normality, that parameter was subjected to Bartlett's test. The non-parametric data was log-transferred where necessary before being subjected to further analysis. R package agricolae was used for data analysis. During the first four weeks, the progression of cumulative feed intake was significantly influenced (p < 0.05) by the dietary protein levels: birds fed lower CP diets consumed more feed at each time point (Fig. 1). However, this difference diminished after the fifth week, and feed intake patterns became similar (p > 0.05) across all groups.

Despite compensatory feed intake, body weight was already reduced in the low-CP groups after the first week. Throughout the experiment, this difference persisted. Body weight was significantly higher in the 20, 21 and 22% CP group (545.20 ± 27.25 g; 519.62 ± 8.53 g; 509.36 ± 17.57 g) compared with the 18% or 19% CP group (404.18 ± 17.83 g; 427.83 ± 33.38 g) (p < 0.05).

As shown in Fig. 2, body weight was significantly affected by dietary CP level over time (p < 0.05), although the effect was not consistent across all time points, with no significant differences observed at days 35 and 56.

As a result, these patterns were reflected in the final feed conversion ratio (FCR). Compared to those in the 18% CP group, birds receiving ≥20% CP exhibited considerably lower FCRs, indicating more effective feed use (Fig. 3). Interestingly, the 22% CP group showed the lowest FCR, whereas the 18% CP group showed the highest FCR.

In the duodenum, birds fed the 22% CP diet exhibited significantly greater villus height (1659.6 ± 26.11 µm) than those in the 18% CP group (1173.3 ± 4.11 µm; p < 0.001). Birds in the 18% CP group showed significantly deeper crypts (522.6 ± 3.87 µm) compared with the higher CP groups (p = 0.003). Consequently, the VH-to-crypt depth ratio differed significantly among groups (p < 0.001), with the 21% CP group showing the highest ratio (10.0 ± 0.46). In the ileum, the VH-to-crypt depth ratio was significantly affected by dietary CP level (p = 0.005) and tended to increase with increasing CP. In contrast, neither villus height nor crypt depth alone differed significantly among treatments (p > 0.05). However, relatively large SEM values indicate considerable variability among replicates. Therefore, these results should be interpreted with caution, given the variability observed and the limited number of replicates. The observed effects were not consistently evident across all measured parameters under the conditions of this study.

Discussion

Growth performance

The present study suggests that Sakini chickens may require higher dietary protein levels during the starter phase than traditionally assumed. Indigenous chickens are frequently described as resilient birds that can maintain acceptable performance in low-input, scavenging-based production systems; however, these presumptions are primarily based on extensive management contexts and empirical feeding practices rather than controlled nutritional evaluations. In this experiment, birds fed diets with 18-19% CP exhibited increased feed consumption but consistently decreased body weight gain, suggesting that increased feed intake did not fully compensate for lower dietary protein levels. Similar limitations on compensatory feeding behaviors have been documented in other native breeds, such as Venda and Horro hens, where growth was limited by early-life protein deficiency despite sufficient feed availability (Dana et al., 2010; Mbajiorgu et al., 2011). These findings indicate that early growth may be sensitive to protein supply. The concept that the protein needs of native chickens differ depending on their genetic background and growth stage is further supported by data from controlled feeding experiments. Previous studies in indigenous chickens have reported variable responses to dietary protein levels, reflecting differences among genotypes and production conditions. For example, Haunshi et al. (2012) reported that diets containing 16% crude protein supported optimal growth performance in Aseel chickens, and increasing dietary protein above this level did not significantly improve body weight gain, suggesting relatively low protein requirements in this slow-growing genotype. In addition to protein level itself, dietary energy–protein balance has also been shown to influence growth performance. Mbajiorgu et al. (2011) demonstrated that growth and feed efficiency in indigenous Venda chickens were affected by the ratio of dietary energy to protein, indicating that nutrient balance, rather than protein concentration alone, plays a critical role. Such variation in nutritional responses among indigenous chickens may be partly explained by their considerable genetic and phenotypic diversity (Dana et al., 2010). The present study demonstrated that increasing dietary crude protein levels improved growth performance in Sakini chickens during the starter phase, with birds receiving ≥20% CP showing higher body weight and improved feed conversion compared with those fed lower protein diets. Taken together, these findings indicate that the adequacy of dietary protein in indigenous chickens may depend on both genotype and production conditions, rather than representing a universal low-protein adaptation.

Sakini chickens appeared to respond to higher dietary protein levels than those generally reported for some indigenous chicken populations, as indicated by improved performance and feed efficiency in birds fed diets containing ≥20% CP. Previous studies have reported variation in gastrointestinal development and nutrient utilization among different chicken genotypes (Wattanachant et al., 2004; Aviagen, 2014). While such factors may be relevant to differences in nutritional responses, they were not directly evaluated in the present study. Therefore, the interpretation of the observed responses is limited to the data obtained under the conditions of this study.

The dietary protein level had a significant effect on the feed conversion ratio (p < 0.05), with the 22% CP group showing the lowest feed conversion ratio compared to the low protein treatments. This supports the idea that Sakini chickens might perform better with a slightly higher amino acid density than that which is usually recommended for native breeds. This pattern may reflect similarities in early nutrient requirements between Sakini chicks and commercial broilers, whose starter diets typically contain 21–22% CP (Aviagen, 2014), although their growth potential remains lower than that of broilers. These similarities may arise because early growth is driven not only by growth rate but also by fundamental developmental processes, including intestinal maturation and immune development, which require sufficient amino acid supply across different chicken genotypes, although the magnitude of these responses may differ among breeds.

Overall, the present findings suggest that dietary CP levels of ≥20% may help prevent early growth restriction in Sakini chickens, and that further increases above this level may confer slight improvements in feed efficiency under the conditions of this study. The data suggest that early protein insufficiency may not be fully compensated by increased feed intake, highlighting the importance of developing diets for native chickens based on breed-specific nutritional requirements rather than general assumptions drawn from scavenging systems. These results provide evidence for reassessing protein recommendations for indigenous breeds, especially those raised under intensive production systems.

Intestinal morphology and nutrient absorptive capacity

Dietary crude protein level influenced intestinal morphology in Sakini chickens, with higher CP levels associated with increased villus height (VH) and VH-to-crypt depth ratio, particularly in the 21–22% CP groups. Similar morphological responses to dietary protein have been reported in other chicken genotypes, although the magnitude and direction of these changes may vary depending on genetic background and experimental conditions. Increased VH and VH-to-crypt depth ratio are generally associated with enhanced absorptive surface area; however, functional outcomes such as nutrient absorption efficiency were not directly measured in the present study. Therefore, the observed morphological changes may reflect improved intestinal structural development under higher dietary protein levels, but their direct physiological implications should be interpreted with caution. Such responses may also differ among indigenous chicken genotypes due to their considerable genetic and phenotypic diversity.

Physiological stress responses to dietary proteins

The heterophil-to-lymphocyte (H/L) ratio is a common indicator of physiological stress in chickens, including indigenous breeds (Maxwell, 1993). In the present experiment, Sakini chickens fed a diet containing 22% CP exhibited the lowest H/L ratio, whereas those fed a diet containing 18% CP showed the highest values. This pattern parallels the growth and intestinal responses observed in the same birds, suggesting a coordinated physiological adaptation to dietary protein supply. Similar stress-related responses to amino acid supply have been documented in commercial broilers, where leukocyte balance is maintained and stress-induced corticosterone secretion is reduced with increasing dietary amino acid density (Gross and Siegel, 1983). Although the H/L ratio is not a direct measure of immune competence, lower values are generally associated with reduced physiological stress. Therefore, the lower H/L ratios observed in the high-CP groups may indicate reduced physiological stress under adequate protein supply. However, overall immune competence was not directly assessed in the present study, and thus the broader implications of these changes should be interpreted with caution.

Dietary protein and vaccine-induced immunity against Newcastle disease

An apparent effect of dietary protein level on humoral immunity against Newcastle disease (ND) was observed in the present study. Birds fed 21–22% CP showed higher hemagglutination inhibition (HI) titers, reaching or exceeding the commonly accepted protective level (≥ log₂4), whereas those fed ≤20% CP generally showed lower titers. These findings indicate that dietary protein level influences the antibody response to ND vaccination, possibly reflecting differences in amino acid availability required for lymphocyte proliferation and antibody synthesis. Similar effects of dietary protein or amino acid supply on antibody production have been reported in commercial broilers (Klasing, 2007; Yegani and Korver, 2008), suggesting that protein availability plays an important role in shaping vaccine-induced humoral responses. Although HI titers quantify only the antibody response to the hemagglutinin-neuraminidase (HN) antigen (Alexander and Senne, 2008), titers ≥ log₂4 are widely accepted as practical indicators of protective immunity in poultry (Aldous and Alexander, 2001; Rahman et al., 2017). Therefore, the higher HI titers observed in the 21–22% CP groups suggest that these dietary protein levels support a more effective antibody response to ND vaccination in Sakini chickens.These results, together with the observed effects on growth performance and intestinal morphology, indicate that responses to dietary protein in Sakini chickens may depend on both genotype and production conditions, rather than reflecting a uniform nutritional requirement across indigenous breeds.

Carcass traits and meat quality stability across dietary protein levels

In contrast to the CP-dependent responses observed in growth performance, intestinal morphology, and vaccine-induced antibody response, the carcass characteristics of Sakini chickens were largely unaffected by the dietary protein levels tested in this experiment. The relative weights of the breast, thigh, and abdominal fat, as well as their dressing percentage, did not significantly differ among the treatments, indicating that the carcass yield of this native genotype is relatively insensitive to mild variations in dietary protein.

Similar patterns have been reported in other indigenous breeds, where carcass traits tend to show limited responsiveness to nutritional variation, possibly reflecting relatively slower muscle accretion associated with genetic background (Mbajiorgu et al., 2011; Dana et al., 2010; Mengesha, 2012).

The breast muscle pH showed a slight decrease with increasing dietary CP level; however, all values remained within the normal physiological range for poultry (≈5.7–6.1), and were higher than values typically associated with PSE-like defects. Previous studies have shown that meat quality deterioration is mainly associated with lower ultimate pH values (Qiao et al., 2002), suggesting that the minor differences observed here are unlikely to affect functional meat quality. Consistent with this, no significant differences were observed among treatments in drip loss or cooking loss. These minor shifts in ultimate pH in the present study may have reflected subtle changes in postmortem metabolic processes; however, they did not result in differences in functional meat quality. Similarly, there was no difference between the treatments in terms of drip loss or cooking loss. Meat quality characteristics are largely influenced by postmortem pH decline and muscle structural properties (Offer and Cousins, 1992; Huff-Lonergan and Lonergan, 2005), and the present results indicate that moderate variation in dietary protein did not markedly affect these attributes. Overall, carcass and meat quality characteristics appear to be relatively stable across dietary treatments, despite the clear effects of dietary protein on growth, intestinal morphology, and vaccine-induced responses.

Integrated effects of dietary protein on stress physiology and vaccine-induced responses

In this study, the heterophil-to-lymphocyte (H/L) ratio and Newcastle disease (ND) antibody titer both exhibited distinct, protein-dependent responses. The birds fed 22% CP showed the lowest H/L ratio, whereas those fed 18–19% CP had higher values, suggesting that lower dietary protein levels were associated with increased physiological stress. The ND vaccine response was likewise influenced by dietary protein level. Birds fed 21–22% CP showed higher HI titers, generally reaching or exceeding the commonly accepted protective level (≥ log₂4), whereas those fed ≤20% CP tended to show lower titers.

Importantly, the results suggest that dietary protein supply during early life may contribute to variation in vaccine responsiveness in this indigenous genotype. The lower antibody titers observed in the low-protein groups may reflect limited availability of amino acids required for antibody production. Together with the H/L ratio, these findings suggest that dietary protein level influences both physiological stress and vaccine-induced antibody responses in Sakini chickens. These results are consistent with previous findings in commercial broilers, where dietary protein and amino acid supply influence both stress indicators and antibody responses. The present findings also suggest that dietary protein supply during early growth may play an important role in shaping vaccine responsiveness in this indigenous genotype. Although compensatory increases in feed intake may partially support growth and maintain intestinal morphology under lower protein conditions, the present results indicate that physiological stress and antibody responses may be more sensitive to protein supply. Overall, dietary protein levels of 21–22% appear to support a more stable physiological status and a more effective antibody response to ND vaccination under the conditions of this study. A limitation of the present study is the relatively small number of replicates per treatment, which may have reduced statistical power and contributed to variability in some measured parameters. Although multiple birds were sampled within each pen, the pen was considered the experimental unit for all statistical analyses. Therefore, the findings, particularly those related to histomorphological traits, should be interpreted with caution and are limited to the conditions of this study.

Conclusion

The present study demonstrated that dietary crude protein levels during early growth influenced growth performance, selected intestinal morphological traits, physiological stress indicators, and vaccine-induced antibody responses in Sakini chickens. Improvements in body weight, feed efficiency, duodenal morphology, and Newcastle disease antibody titers were observed in birds fed higher protein diets, whereas carcass traits and most meat quality parameters were not significantly affected. These findings indicate that Sakini chickens respond to dietary protein levels during early growth, with diets containing ≥20% CP being associated with improved growth performance, and higher levels (21–22% CP) being associated with more favorable physiological status and antibody responses under the conditions of this study. However, these observations do not establish an optimal protein requirement, as formal optimization analyses were not conducted. Importantly, these results are specific to Sakini chickens and should not be generalized to all indigenous chicken populations. The responses observed in this study may reflect genotype-specific characteristics and the experimental conditions applied.

Future research should focus on defining protein and amino acid requirements more precisely for Sakini chickens under different production systems, as well as exploring nutritional strategies that maintain performance while reducing dietary crude protein levels to improve economic and environmental sustainability.
