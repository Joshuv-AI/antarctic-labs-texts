Under the bidirectional screening policy, impact of diabetes mellitus on patient and diagnosis delays of pulmonary tuberculosis patients in Ningxia, China: a propensity score analysis
Authors: Ying Zhang, Juan Lei, Wenxuan Li, Ye Li, Libo Liang, Xiaomei Tian, Wei Liu, Zehui Li, Siqi Wu, Longjia Wu, Xiaolin Wei, Hui Yin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough the policy of screening tuberculosis (TB) in individuals with diabetes mellitus (DM) was suggested, the coexistence of TB and DM is rapidly increasing in China, hindering the country’s TB elimination efforts. The delay in TB consultation and diagnosis poses a challenge for DM-TB patients. However, the impact of DM on TB is not fully understood. Our study aimed to evaluate patient and diagnosis delays in pulmonary tuberculosis (PTB) patients in Ningxia, China, investigate the relationship between DM comorbidity and delays, and provide recommendations for improving the implementation of TB prevention and control strategies.MethodsWe examined all PTB patients with DM registered in the Chinese TB Information Management System (TBIMS) in the Ningxia region from 2016 to 2019. Patient delay was defined as > 20 days from symptom onset to first medical consultation, and diagnosis delay as > 1 day from first consultation to diagnosis, both measured in days. In our study, possible covariates were screened based on relevant literatures, then mediating variables were excluded, and finally covariates were screened by univariate analysis. Propensity score matching (PSM) was conducted (1:1 nearest matching with a caliper of 0.25 standard deviation (SD) of the propensity score (PS), without replacement) using covariates including sex, age, ethnicity, household registration, occupation, type of hospital for initial PTB diagnosis, level of hospital for initial PTB diagnosis, X-ray, the presence of other types of TB, and patient source. Logistic regression was then employed to assess the impact of DM on patient and diagnosis delays. Additionally, we conducted sensitivity analyses, exploring different ratios and caliper sizes, and mahalanobis distance matching. To address the potential for residual confounding by age, we conducted a further sensitivity analysis using age as a continuous variable in the PS model.ResultsOut of 9,625 PTB patients registered in TBIMS, 260 (2.7%) were also diagnosed with DM. In the unadjusted analysis, there was no difference in the median duration of patient delay between NDM-PTB and DM-PTB patients (20 days vs. 22 days, P > 0.05), while the median diagnosis delay was significantly longer in DM-PTB patients (1 day vs. 3 days, P < 0.05). However, after PSM to control for confounders, regression analysis indicated that DM was associated with a higher likelihood of experiencing patient delay [odds ratio (OR) = 1.85; 95% confidence interval (CI) = 1.30–2.64], but not with diagnosis delay (OR = 1.29; 95% CI = 0.89–1.87). In conclusion, after balancing baseline characteristics, DM was a risk factor for patient delay in PTB patients, but not for diagnosis delay.ConclusionsOur study revealed that DM only exacerbated patient delay and ultimately had no significant effect on diagnosis delay. This suggests that screening for TB in DM patients can reduce diagnosis delay. Therefore, emphasizing community TB health education in DM patients is crucial. Additionally, there is a need to promote the integrated model of TB management and enhance the awareness of primary medical institutions’ doctors regarding the comorbidity of DM-PTB, differential diagnosis, and diagnostic capabilities to effectively prevent and control TB.

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) continues to pose a substantial global public health challenge, carrying a significant disease burden. Key measures for TB prevention and control include early detection, timely diagnosis, and prompt treatment. Delays in the diagnosis and treatment of TB patients not only contribute to broader complications and heightened disease severity but also escalate the risk of transmission to close contacts [1, 2]. Concurrent conditions can impact disease progression, treatment, and inevitably influence TB patients’ behaviors related to the disease [3, 4]. Diabetes mellitus (DM) stands out as one of the most prevalent comorbidities in TB patients [5, 6]. The International Diabetes Federation (IDF) forecasts a global DM patient count of 700 million by 2045 [7]. Pan et al. identified that in countries with a high TB burden, the prevalence of DM may exacerbate TB prevalence [8]. Various studies have explored the relationship between DM and TB (DM-TB), covering aspects such as the physiopathology of DM-TB, where innate and adaptive immunity in DM patients with TB infection may be affected [9]; risk factors of DM-TB, with age, sex, education, and smoking significantly associated with the comorbidity of DM-TB [5]; and characteristics of DM-TB, revealing that DM-TB patients are more likely to exhibit pulmonary cavities [10]. However, limited research has investigated the association between DM comorbidity and delays in TB patients, and the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. Previous studies by Chen [11], Htun YM [12], and Williams E [13] et al. suggested that DM was linked to prolonged patient and diagnosis delays in TB, largely attributed to poor awareness about TB and a higher proportion of older TB cases. Xiao et al. found that DM contributed to shorter health system delays for TB patients, attributed to improved knowledge and awareness of DM-TB and enhanced referrals for patients at high risk of TB among healthcare providers [14]. Moreover, the aforementioned research predominantly relies on regression analysis, which may not address data imbalance issues, and the relationship between DM and delays in TB patients appears inconsistent. Given that delays are detrimental to TB prevention and control and the strong link between DM and TB, it is imperative to clearly understand the impact of DM on delays in TB patients, and additional evidence is required to elucidate the relationship between DM and delays in TB patients. Matching methods can be employed to simulate data akin to a randomized trial to balance data bias and confounding factors. Our study, grounded in the Chinese region, provides supplementary support for the role of DM in delays among TB patients.

To address the dual challenge of TB and DM comorbidity, the World Health Organization (WHO) introduced the collaborative framework for TB-DM in 2011, recommending bidirectional screening for all DM and TB patients [15]. However, globally, an estimated 10 million people suffered from TB, with 1.4 million deaths attributed to TB in 2019 [16]. The current predicted global prevalence of DM among pulmonary tuberculosis (PTB) patients stands at 13.73%, still emphasizing the substantial and persistent double burden [6]. Similarly, China adopted the strategy of screening TB in DM in 2016. The overall prevalence of DM among TB patients in China is 7.8% [17], underscoring the double burden of DM and TB. This underscores the critical need to understand the reasons why the situation of TB and DM-TB remains critical despite the implementation of the bidirectional screening strategy. Therefore, it is necessary to study the influence of DM on the behavior of TB patients, so as for a more detailed and effective screening strategy to enhance the efficiency of TB prevention and control.

The current scenario of DM-TB comorbidity demands heightened attention. Ningxia Hui Autonomous Region (NHAR), situated in northwest China, has experienced hyperendemic TB for several decades according to national surveys [18]. TB remains a significant public health concern in Ningxia, with PTB being the predominant form, accounting for over 80% of all TB cases [19, 20].

To better understand the relationship between DM and TB and to achieve more reliable validation, we employ the propensity score matching (PSM) method to mitigate bias and confounding factors. Our analysis focuses on assessing the impact of DM on delay behavior in PTB patients in Ningxia, aiming to provide more reasoned recommendations for enhancing TB prevention and control strategies.

Method

Data collection

We utilized the Chinese TB Information Management System (TBIMS) to compile information on all PTB patients diagnosed and treated in the Ningxia region from January 1, 2016, to December 31, 2019. TBIMS, serving as an internet-based TB case reporting and management system, holds a crucial role in national TB control planning. It facilitates electronic case management for TB patients in China, storing medical records electronically. TBIMS encompasses comprehensive data, including basic information (e.g., age, sex, ethnicity, etc.), clinical characteristics (e.g., treatment history, sputum smear results, X-ray inspection, etc.), follow-up examinations, medical history (e.g., date of symptom onset, date of first seeking medical care, and date of diagnosis, etc.), and treatment information. The system also records patients with prior medical diagnoses of chronic diseases (e.g., DM, hypertension, etc.). By sharing data among different medical institutions, TBIMS enhances patient referrals and the management of TB cases.

Participants

The diagnosis of PTB adhered to the operating procedures outlined in the Guidelines for Implementing the National Tuberculosis Control Program in China [21]. During the research process, diagnosis is mainly based on sputum smear examination, supplemented by sputum culture and chest X-ray. The diagnostic process for patients with confirmed TB, presumptive TB, or related symptoms involves a three-step sputum examination. This includes providing an “instant sputum” sample during the outpatient visit on the first day, followed by “night sputum” and “morning sputum” samples for examination the next day [14]. A clinical diagnosis of PTB in smear-negative cases can be made by a qualified physician when persistent symptoms are present alongside suggestive evidence, such as chest X-ray abnormalities. This clinical diagnosis would then be officially recorded in the TBIMS.

The study population consisted of all 9,935 patients from TBIMS in Ningxia Province. After excluding patients with incomplete or incorrect information (sputum smear and X-ray results were missing; delay days were incorrect with logic errors) and those classified occupationally as children, a total of 9,625 patients were included (Fig. 1).

Measures

Exposure variable

In this study, the exposure variable was PTB patients with DM(DM-PTB) or without DM(NDM-PTB). The diagnostic criteria for DM are from the WHO [22].

Covariates

In our study, factors that influenced delays alone or both delays and DM were first selected from previous studies [11, 14, 23]according to the principle of covariate screening [24]. Next, we excluded mediating variables that may exist between DM and delays. Then identified patient, clinical, and health system possible covariates in the TBIMS, most of which were variables that affected delays. Finally, covariates were screened by univariate analysis between delay and non-delay groups. For the patient delay analysis, we considered sex, age, ethnicity, household registration, occupation, patient source, and the presence of other types of TB, as well as the type and level of the hospital for the initial PTB diagnosis. Since PTB patients did not undergo an examination before seeking healthcare, the patient delay analysis excluded clinical variables such as sputum smear results and X-ray. For the diagnosis delay analysis, we considered sex, age, ethnicity, household registration, occupation, patient source, sputum smear results, X-ray, the presence of other types of TB, and the type and level of the hospital for the initial PTB diagnosis.

Outcome variables

The outcome variables were patient and diagnosis delays. The determination criteria for these delays were not universally standardized. While most studies treated delay as a dichotomous variable, the specific time intervals defining delay varied among scholars and were seldom precisely and clearly defined. The definitions of delay used in the studies can be categorized as follows.

In our study, we adopted the second definition and set the cut-off points at 20 days for patient delay and 1 day for diagnosis delay. Patient and diagnosis delays were defined as the number of days from symptom onset to first consultation, and from first consultation to official diagnosis, respectively. In both calculations, the initial event date (symptom onset for patient delay, first consultation for diagnosis delay) was designated as day 0. Delays were defined as > 20 days for patient delay and > 1 day (i.e., diagnosis confirmed on day 2 or later) for diagnosis delay.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as the median and interquartile range (IQR), while categorical variables were expressed as numbers and percentages. Mann-Whitney U tests were employed to compare continuous variables, and Chi-square tests were utilized for categorical variables as unadjusted analyses.

Propensity score analysis (PSA) is a statistical method used to minimize biases in observational studies. The propensity score (PS) represents the likelihood of an individual undergoing treatment based on observed baseline characteristics, determined through Logit or Probit regression. Utilizing the PS, we can generate new datasets to achieve balance in covariates between treatment and control groups. Various approaches, including stratification, matching, regression adjustment, and inverse probability weighting, can be employed to estimate causal effects using the PS. To assess the impact of DM on patient and diagnosis delays in PTB patients, we applied Propensity Score Matching (PSM). Initially, Chi-square tests compared patient characteristic variables between delay and non-delay groups before matching. Covariates with a p-value ≤ 0.05 [30] were incorporated into the final matching model.

Separate PSM models were constructed for patient delay and diagnosis delay because the confounding structures differ for each outcome. Specifically, clinical variables (e.g., X-ray, sputum smear) were excluded from the patient delay covariates selection as they are not available at the stage of care-seeking and would lie on the causal pathway between DM and diagnosis. After covariates screening, covariates in the analysis of patient delay included sex, age, occupation, type of hospital for initial PTB diagnosis, level of hospital for initial PTB diagnosis, and patient source. For the analysis of diagnosis delay, additional covariates included ethnicity, household registration, X-ray, and the presence of other types of TB, excluding sex and age. The PS, calculated for each patient as the predicted probability of DM from multivariate logistic regression, facilitated the matching of DM-PTB and NDM-PTB patients using a (1:1) nearest neighbor approach with a caliper of 0.25 standard deviations (SD) of the PS, and without replacement. Standardized mean differences (SMD) assessed PSM performance, with SMD < 0.1 indicating covariate balance. In matched datasets, the outcomes were analyzed using logistic regression, which provided adjusted estimates in the form of odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI); these ORs measured the average treatment effect on the treated (ATT), reflecting the impact of DM on delay after controlling for the specified covariates. Sensitivity analysis, crucial in PSA, examined potential unmeasured confounding effects for result robustness. Sensitivity analyses involved 1:2 and 1:3 matchings to assess consistency across sample sizes. Additionally, logit of PS matching (1:1) with a caliper of 0.2 SD and mahalanobis distance matching were conducted. To address the potential for residual confounding by age, we conducted a further sensitivity analysis using age as a continuous variable in the PS model.

All statistical analyses were executed using R statistical software version 4.3.1 (R Project for Statistical Computing, Beijing, China), maintaining a two-sided significance level of 0.05.

Results

Characteristics of PTB patients

Out of 9,625 PTB patients, 260 (2.7%) had comorbid DM. The baseline characteristics of the entire cohort are summarized in Table 1. For all PTB patients, the median (interquartile range, IQR) patient delay was 20 (6–48) days, while the median (IQR) diagnosis delay was 1 (0–7) day.

When stratified by DM status, NDM-PTB patients had a median (IQR) patient delay of 20 (6.5–48) days, and a median (IQR) diagnosis delay of 1 (0–7) days. In contrast, DM-PTB patients had a median (IQR) patient delay of 22 (4.25–59) days, and a median (IQR) diagnosis delay of 3 (0–7.75) days. Table 1 presents the unadjusted comparisons of characteristics and delay durations between DM-PTB and NDM-PTB patients. In these unadjusted analyses, no significant difference in patient delay was observed between these two patient categories (P = 0.786). However, DM-PTB patients experienced a significantly longer diagnosis delay (P = 0.009). Significant differences were found between the two groups across all other patient characteristics listed in Table 1 (P < 0.05).

Univariate analysis between delay and non-delay groups

Patient, clinical, and health system possible covariates in the TBIMS were identified. Following the Chi-square test for covariate selection, Table 2 provides a summary of the covariates used in PSM. Among all PTB patients, the Chi-square test revealed significant differences (P < 0.05) in the variables of sex, age, occupation, type of hospital for initial PTB diagnosis, level of hospital for initial PTB diagnosis, and patient source between PTB patients with and without patient delay. Conversely, no significant differences were observed in ethnicity, household registration, and the presence of other types of TB between PTB patients with and without patient delay (P > 0.05, Table 2).

Similarly, significant differences (P < 0.05) were noted between PTB patients with and without diagnosis delay in the variables of ethnicity, occupation, household registration, type of hospital for initial PTB diagnosis, level of hospital for initial PTB diagnosis, X-ray, the presence of other types of TB, and patient source. However, no significant differences were identified in the comparison group of sex, age, and sputum smear results between PTB patients with and without diagnosis delay (P > 0.05, Table 2).

Balanced diagnostics after PSM

In the unadjusted data (Table 1), noteworthy differences were observed in baseline characteristics between DM-PTB and NDM-PTB patients.

Covariates were integrated into a logistic regression model, and propensity scores (PSs) were computed for each PTB patient. Out of the 9,625 participants, 520 were successfully matched. The covariates in the patient delay analysis and the diagnosis delay analysis were not exactly the same. For clear and intuitive display, we presented their balanced diagnostics results in the same table (Table 3). The matching technique achieved equilibrium in measured covariates between the DM-PTB and NDM-PTB groups based on the same PS. Following the matching process, the SMD for all covariates were below 0.1, indicating balance. Chi-square tests conducted on the matched dataset revealed no significant differences between the two groups across all covariates (P < 0.05), indicating good matching quality.

To isolate the independent effect of DM from other confounding factors, we employed PSM followed by logistic regression. Notably, DM emerged as a risk factor for patient delay in PTB patients (P < 0.05).

Sensitivity analysis results

We conducted sensitivity analysis to evaluate the robustness of our main findings, The results are summarized in the Table 5. These analyses consistently show that no matter what PSM algorithm is used, there is a positive correlation between DM and patient delay. In a key sensitivity analysis aimed at addressing the potential for residual confounding by age, we treated age as a continuous variable rather than a categorical variable for covariate screening. Univariate analysis showed that there was a difference in age between patients with and without patient delay (P = 0.011), but no such difference was observed in terms of diagnostic delay ((P = 0.420). Therefore, age was included in our patient delayed propensity score model as a continuous covariate. A matched queue was constructed using the 1:1 nearest neighbor method, and the caliper was 0.25 SD of PS. In this queue, the association between DM and patient delay was still statistically significant (OR = 1.46,95 CI:1.02–2.09; P < 0.001), which is consistent with our main results (OR = 1.85, 95 CI: 1.30–2.64).

Discussion

This study delved into the intricate relationship between DM and delays among PTB patients. Through the PSM method, we sought to uncover the impact of DM on these delays experienced by PTB patients in Ningxia, China. The results indicated that the experience of DM was positively associated with patient delay, but not to diagnosis delay.

The median days of patient delay in our study were 20 days, slightly lower than the 28 days reported in Sub-Saharan African countries [31] but longer than findings from other Chinese studies, such as Shenzhen [32]. Additionally, the median diagnosis delay in our study was only 1 day, markedly shorter than the 12–34 days reported in low-and middle-income countries [31]. Notably, areas such as Wuzhong City, Guyuan City, and Zhongwei City of Ningxia, situated in underdeveloped regions in western China, demonstrated prolonged patient delays, attributable to economic underdevelopment, limited health resources, and challenging geographical accessibility [33]. The predominantly farming population in these areas, characterized by relatively low income, may delay seeking medical services due to economic constraints. Furthermore, insufficient awareness of TB emerged as a significant risk factor [25], with our study highlighting poor TB knowledge in Ningxia [34].

The short diagnosis delay observed in our study is noteworthy and may be attributed to the TB management model adopted in Ningxia. In China at that time, three prevalent TB management models existed: the TB dispensary model, the specialist model, and the integrated model [35]. Ningxia employed the integrated model, where in designated hospitals offered outpatient TB services at the county and prefecture levels. Previous studies in China have demonstrated the superior performance of the integrated model compared to the other two models. Specifically, it has been shown to enhance case detection rates, increase case referrals, and significantly reduce diagnosis delay days [36]. Furthermore, from 2016 to 2019, the implementation of the China-Gates TB project Phase III in Ningxia, supported by the China National Health Commission and the Bill & Melinda Gates Foundation, played a crucial role. During this period, Ningxia upgraded laboratory facilities and introduced molecular biology diagnostic equipment across all designated hospitals. This initiative not only improved the accuracy of TB diagnosis but also contributed to a substantial reduction in diagnosis delay days. From the standpoint of PTB prevention and control, the integrated model that proved effective in Ningxia should be replicated and improved nationwide.

Interestingly, our study revealed that DM-PTB patients were inclined to experience patient delay, consistent with findings by Williams E [13]. This potentially suggest that although the policy of screening TB among DM patients in Ningxia has been proposed and gradually implemented, it might be insufficient to reduce the patient delay rate. On the one hand, DM patients may have poor self-management and treatment adherence [37], leading to a lack of attention and cooperation with PTB screening. On the other hand, DM patients may lack awareness of the comorbidity of DM-TB, believing they were not at risk of PTB and thus unwilling to undergo TB symptom screening or imaging examinations without compulsory screening requirements [38]. Moreover, even subjected to such PTB screening, DM patients may undergo psychological changes upon being labeled as TB suspects, with TB stigma driving individuals to conceal their condition [25], thereby hindering access to available services and resulting in patient delay. Additionally, DM patients exhibited an increased susceptibility to TB infection, as DM can heighten the risk of developing TB through various mechanisms. Prior studies suggest that certain DM symptoms, such as fatigue or weight loss, may mask initial and general TB symptoms during the phase when respiratory symptoms are still absent, resulting in atypical clinical presentations [39]. With a compromised immune function, DM patients become susceptible to lower respiratory tract infections, and symptoms like cough, fever, and chills may overlap with typical TB manifestations. This overlap may lead patients to mistakenly perceive their condition as a common illness, such as a cold or fever, prompting many DM-PTB patients to be unaware of their TB status. Consequently, they may choose self-treatment or respiratory care instead of seeking specialized PTB healthcare. At present, PTB screening for DM patients in conjunction with the National Basic Public Health Service Program is still in the initial stage, and the related process and screening methods are yet to be perfected. Our current focus remains on the proactive consultation of PTB patients, and we should work together to promote the active screening of DM patients, in order to provide a basis for optimizing the strategy of active discovery of PTB in DM patients in China.

However, DM did not lead to a significant diagnosis delay, consistent with Xiao [14], but inconsistent with findings from Chen [11] and Htun YM [12]. Perhaps this was less likely the case in DM-PTB patients due to several practical possibilities. Firstly, this may be attributed to the implementation of the strategy to screen TB patients among DM patients, which has enhanced the knowledge and awareness of DM among primary medical institution doctors, facilitating the referral of PTB suspects among DM patients. Secondly, DM patients often seek medical services in the respiratory departments of general hospitals when they present with nonspecific symptoms like cough, fever, and chills [40]. These hospitals, often TB designated, are more likely to provide internal referrals, allowing for prompt diagnosis and treatment without undue delay. General hospitals have outstanding advantages such as the ability to diagnose and treat basic diseases, comorbidities and critical illnesses, and the possibility of multidisciplinary collaboration. Therefore, tertiary hospitals should be encouraged to declare their qualifications as designated hospitals for PTB in accordance with the law and to undertake PTB treatment and care. Additionally, Ningxia in China has developed an artificial intelligence image screening system for TB based on over 300,000 chest X-ray radiographs of TB patients marked by top experts in the field. This system can efficiently screen and refer suspected individuals by providing imaging results, indicating the location and suspected degree of the lesion, ultimately improving the efficiency of screening and referral processes.

Therefore, further strategies should be developed to not only optimize proactive screening work, but also mitigate patient and diagnosis delays. It is imperative to conduct community health education leveraging the National Basic Public Health Service Project. This effort aims to enhance DM patients’ awareness of the comorbidity of DM and TB, reduce TB stigma, and foster a greater willingness to undergo screening. Concurrently, the promotion of the integrated model, leveraging the expertise of general hospitals in the diagnosis and treatment of endocrine diseases, will expedite the screening, diagnosis, and treatment of DM-PTB patients. Additionally, under the integrated model, sustained investment in human resources and training for primary medical institutions is crucial. This approach will motivate healthcare practitioners in primary medical institutions to address the issue of DM-PTB, deepen their understanding of its pathogenesis and clinical characteristics, and enhance their ability to identify, diagnose, and conduct effective screening.

Conclusions

Our study suggests that in Ningxia, the integrated model may play a pivotal role in reducing patient delay and diagnosis delay. Furthermore, while DM is associated with significant patient delay in PTB patients, it does not exert a substantial impact on diagnosis delay, indicating the potential efficacy of the strategy to screen TB patients among DM patients. These findings underscore the importance of raising DM patients’ awareness of the comorbidity, mitigating TB stigma, and promoting screening acceptance, alongside the nationwide implementation of the integrated model. Particularly, ongoing investment in human resources and training is essential to enhance primary medical institutions’ doctors’ awareness, differential diagnostic abilities, and overall screening efficacy.

Limitations

Our study has limitations. Firstly, being a cross-sectional survey, it is susceptible to systematic bias from confounding factors and lacks the ability to establish exact causality. The use of PSM in this paper addressed this concern by correcting confounding factors between groups and mitigating the inability to establish causal relationships. Secondly, due to TBIMS limitations, certain socioeconomic status (SES) indicators, such as income and education, were unavailable. However, occupation, reflecting income and education to some extent, served as a proxy indicator for SES. Several previous studies have utilized occupation as a representative SES indicator [41, 42]. Therefore, in this study, occupation was used to explore the impact of SES on the health and healthcare-seeking behavior of PTB patients. Thirdly, as our study was restricted to diagnosed pulmonary TB patients, we lacked data on diabetic individuals screened negative for TB. This design prevents assessment of the bidirectional screening’s overall sensitivity and might have led to an overestimation of delays if presymptomatic cases were missed. However, we believe this bias was minimal during the early implementation phase, when detection was primarily passive. Fourthly, while our propensity score matching included the type and level of the diagnosing hospital, it did not account for the specific medical care site or the year of diagnosis. Temporal changes in diagnostic technology (e.g., the introduction of rapid molecular tests) and unmeasured differences in practice patterns between individual hospitals could potentially influence delay times and represent sources of residual confounding. Future studies using individual hospital identifiers and adjusting for time trends as fixed effects would provide a more robust assessment. Moreover, the absence of data on DM management and follow-up schedules constitutes another limitation. Without information on the frequency of healthcare contacts or glycemic control, we could not explore the potential “double-edged sword” effect of regular diabetic care, which may simultaneously facilitate and hinder timely TB diagnosis through different mechanisms.
