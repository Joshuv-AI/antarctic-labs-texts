Discrete-time, discrete-state multistate Markov models from the perspective of algebraic statistics
Dario Gasbarra, Kaie Kubjas, Sangita Kulathinal

Abstract
We study discrete-time, discrete-state multistate Markov models from the perspective of algebraic statistics. These models are commonly studied in event history analysis, and are characterized by the state space, the initial distribution and the transition probabilities. The event histories provide information on the states occupied at instances over a time period and the transitions between states are governed by the multistate model. A finite path under the multistate Markov model is a particular set of states occupied at finite time instances { 1 , … , n } \displaystyle\{1,\dots,n\} . The main goal of this paper is to establish a bridge between event history analysis and algebraic statistics. The joint probabilities of finite paths in these models has a natural monomial parametrization in terms of initial distribution and transition probabilities. We study the polynomial relations among joint path probabilities. We show that nonhomogeneous multistate Markov models of arbitrary order when the statistical constraints on the parameters are disregarded, are slices of decomposable hierarchical models. This yields a complete description of their vanishing ideals as toric ideals generated by explicit families of binomials. Moreover, the variety of this vanishing ideal equals the nonhomogeneous multistate Markov model on the probability simplex. In contrast, homogeneous multistate Markov models exhibit different algebraic behavior, as time homogeneity imposes additional polynomial relations, leading to vanishing ideals that are strictly larger than in the nonhomogeneous case. We derive families of binomial relations that vanish on homogeneous multistate Markov models and show with examples that these relations form only a partial generating set of the vanishing ideal in general. Finally, we analyze maximum likelihood estimation from both statistical and algebraic perspectives. For nonhomogeneous multistate Markov models, the classical formula in statistics for computing maximum likelihood estimates agrees with the formula for nondecomposable hierarchical models. In the homogeneous setting, examples show that solving the MLE problem algebraically can be substantially more involved than using classical statistical methods in this setting. Finally, we provide real data applications where we apply the statistical theory to obtain the maximum likelihood estimates of the parameters under specific multistate Markov models.

Introduction

Event history analysis provides a statistical framework for modeling processes that evolve over time
through sequences of events. A central class of models in this area is given by multistate Markov
models, which describe such processes via transitions between a finite set of states. In discrete
time, the probability of an observed path or a trajectory factors multiplicatively in terms of transition
probabilities, giving rise to polynomial relations among joint path probabilities. In this paper, we
study the algebraic structure underlying these models.

1.1
Event history and multistate Markov models

We begin by recalling the statistical formulation of multistate Markov models and by presenting several canonical examples drawn from event history analysis. These examples serve two purposes: they illustrate the modeling assumptions underlying multistate Markov models, and they highlight the common structural feature that will be exploited throughout the paper, namely that observations correspond to finite paths whose probabilities factor as products of the initial distribution and transition probabilities.

In this context, the term
event history
refers to information and data about events occurring during a unit’s lifetime.
Units and events usually carry context-dependent definitions. For example, in life history studies, units are people (or subjects, individuals), and events are outcomes related to health, such as the diagnosis of a specific disease, birth, death, fertility, or sociodemographic status (education, employment, marriage). In engineering applications, units are systems, and events are related to the functioning of the system, such as transitions between normal, defective, and failed states.

Event history analysis is a multidisciplinary area of statistics concerned with the timing and occurrence of one or multiple events during a unit’s lifetime. Such analyses treat event times as random variables and account for both their occurrence and temporal ordering, with modeling choices depending on the study design and data-generating mechanism. A common feature of event history data is that events are recorded over time for each unit, leading to longitudinal data structures. Multistate models provide a flexible framework for analyzing such processes and are widely used across the life sciences, engineering, insurance, economics, and social sciences.

A multistate model characterizes an event history process by specifying a discrete state space
\displaystyle S
together with the allowed transitions between states. Event history data are used to estimate the transition rates
under the assumed multistate model. When an event history process is modeled using a multistate framework, each event corresponds to a specific type of transition between two states. Both continuous- and discrete-time multistate models are studied in the literature. We will consider discrete-time multistate models under Markov assumption (Section
2
) in this paper and refer to them as multistate Markov models or multistate Markov chains. In this paper, we use the term Markov chains to indicate Markov chains with all possible transitions. With this convention, it is clear that a multistate Markov chain will typically have forbidden transitions according to the structure of the multistate model. Multistate models are fully characterized by the state space, the initial distribution and the transition probabilities. In this paper, we focus on discrete-time, discrete-state multistate Markov chains and describe the dynamics of the chains via transition probabilities. Under these assumptions, the probability of an observed path admits a multiplicative factorization determined by the initial distribution and successive state transition probabilities (see Section
2
).
This factorization induces a natural monomial parametrization of joint path probabilities and thereby leads to natural questions from the perspective of algebraic statistics. While the first-order Markov chains have been studied in algebraic statistics (see, e.g.,
[

), the
\displaystyle k

\displaystyle k

1.2
An algebraic perspective on multistate Markov models

In all these examples, an observation corresponds to a finite path through the state space, and its
probability admits a multiplicative factorization determined by successive state transitions. This
factorization depends only on the Markov structure, the order of dependence, and possible structural
constraints such as forbidden transitions or absorbing states. From an algebraic perspective, this multiplicative structure induces a monomial parametrization.
The Zariski closure of the image of this monomial parametrization is an algebraic variety that contains the multistate Markov model as a subset.

When imposing the statistical constraints on the parameters of the monomial parametrization, the vanishing ideal of the above variety captures all polynomial constraints among joint path probabilities implied by the model assumptions. As a result, determining generators of this ideal provides an algebraic characterization of the model and has direct consequences for identifiability, model equivalence, and likelihood-based inference.
This algebraic formulation provides a unified framework for comparing different classes of multistate Markov models and for understanding how modeling assumptions translate into algebraic constraints. One caveat is that this algebraic characterization does not account for inequality constraints which would lead to a study in real algebraic geometry.

Algebraic methods play a
central role in discrete statistical modeling

[

,
10

.
Several algebraic approaches to statistical models have been developed in related contexts,
particularly for log-linear, graphical, and conditional independence models.
Many classical models, including log-linear
and graphical models, are equal to a probability simplex intersected with an algebraic variety defined by binomial ideals. For decomposable graphical
models, the vanishing ideals are toric when statistical constraints on parameters are disregarded and yield closed-form maximum likelihood estimators with
maximum likelihood degree one

Algebraic encoding of conditional independence beyond classical graphical models has also been

However, multistate Markov models do not fit directly into these frameworks: their structural assumptions such as time-homogeneity or forbidden transitions induce algebraic constraints that go beyond standard conditional independence models. Despite their central role in event history analysis
[

, these algebraic features have not been systematically explored.
Within this algebraic context, the present work provides a unified algebraic-statistical study of discrete-time multistate Markov models.

Main results and contributions

We summarize below the main results and contributions of this paper.
We formulate discrete-time multistate Markov models as algebraic statistical models via monomial
parametrizations of joint path probabilities
(Section

are decomposable hierarchical models (Lemma

allows us to use existing results on decomposable models to obtain an explicit description
of the defining equations in terms of binomial generators
(Proposition
4.5
), building on

[
9
,

. We further show that models
with forbidden transitions or absorbing states arise as coordinate slices of the general
nonhomogeneous model, and we describe how their vanishing ideals are obtained by adding linear
relations (Proposition
4.4
and
Corollary
4.7
).

In contrast, we show that homogeneous multistate Markov models satisfy additional algebraic
relations.
We derive a general family of binomial

ideal even when we disregard the statistical constraints on the parameters. We also analyze maximum likelihood estimation from both statistical and algebraic
perspectives. In the nonhomogeneous case, we discuss that the formula for maximum likelihood estimates for decomposable hierarchical models agrees with the classical formulas from statistics, while in the homogeneous case we see that the algebraic formulation leads to more challenges than the
classical statistical approach (Section
6
).
Finally, we illustrate the theory on classical multistate Markov models from event history analysis,
including illness-death type models and models with structural constraints, highlighting the
practical implications of the algebraic structure for inference and model interpretation

We emphasize that the algebraic viewpoint is used here to understand structure and constraints;
in some cases, classical statistical methods remain simpler for inference.

2
Discrete-time, discrete-state multistate Markov models

We consider discrete-time, discrete-state Markov chains and briefly review the basic definitions and the factorization of path probabilities. Throughout the paper, motivated by applications in survival and event history analysis, we use the term
Markov chain model
for an unrestricted discrete-time, discrete-state Markov model, and
multistate Markov model
for a model with forbidden transitions.

First-order Markov chains.

A discrete-time stochastic process
\displaystyle\{X_{\ell}:\ell\geq 1\}
with a finite state space
\displaystyle S
is a first-order Markov chain if, for each time
\displaystyle\ell\geq 2
, the conditional distribution of
\displaystyle X_{\ell}
given the past depends only on the most recent state
\displaystyle X_{\ell-1}
. That is, for all
\displaystyle\ell\geq 2
and all
\displaystyle i,j\in S
,

\displaystyle\displaystyle P(X_{\ell}=j\mid X_{1}=i_{1},\dots,X_{\ell-2}=i_{\ell-2},X_{\ell-1}=i)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=P(X_{\ell}=j\mid X_{\ell-1}=i):=a_{ij}^{(\ell)}.

(2.1)

The condition (
2.1
) is called the Markov property. If the transition probabilities do not depend on
\displaystyle\ell
, the chain is
time-homogeneous
, i.e.
\displaystyle a_{ij}^{(\ell)}=a_{ij}
for all
\displaystyle\ell
and all
\displaystyle i,j\in S
.

The joint probability of a length-
\displaystyle n
path
\displaystyle(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n})
can be written in terms of transition probabilities as

\displaystyle\displaystyle p_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=P(X_{1}=i_{1},\ldots,X_{n}=i_{n})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=P(X_{1}=i_{1})\prod_{\ell=2}^{n}P(X_{\ell}=i_{\ell}\mid X_{1}=i_{1},\ldots,X_{\ell-1}=i_{\ell-1})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\pi_{i_{1}}\prod_{\ell=2}^{n}P(X_{\ell}=i_{\ell}\mid X_{\ell-1}=i_{\ell-1})=\pi_{i_{1}}\prod_{\ell=2}^{n}a_{i_{\ell-1}\,i_{\ell}}^{(\ell)},

(2.2)

where
\displaystyle\pi_{i_{1}}=P(X_{1}=i_{1})
denotes the initial distribution. In particular, if the initial state is fixed (e.g.
\displaystyle\pi_{0}=1
), then the factor
\displaystyle\pi_{i_{1}}
can be omitted in (
2.2
).

Since
\displaystyle\pi_{i_{1}}
and
\displaystyle a_{i_{\ell-1}\,i_{\ell}}^{(\ell)}
represent (conditional) probabilities, they satisfy

\displaystyle\displaystyle\biggl\{\pi_{i_{1}}\geq 0\;\text{for all}\;i_{1}\in S,\;\text{and}\sum_{i_{1}\in S}\pi_{i_{1}}=1,\;\text{and}

\displaystyle\displaystyle a_{i_{\ell-1}\,i_{\ell}}^{(\ell)}\geq 0,\;i_{\ell-1},i_{\ell}\in S,\text{and}\;\sum_{i_{\ell}\in S}a_{i_{\ell-1}\,i_{\ell}}^{(\ell)}=1,\text{for all}\;\ell\in\{2,\ldots,n\}\;\text{and}\;i_{\ell-1}\in S\biggr\}.

The first-order Markov chains are well-studied in algebraic statistics, see
[
19
, Section 1.1]
.

Higher-order Markov chains.

More generally, a discrete-time stochastic process
\displaystyle\{X_{\ell}:\ell\geq 1\}
with state space
\displaystyle S
is a
\displaystyle k
-th order Markov chain if, for each
\displaystyle\ell>k
, the conditional distribution of
\displaystyle X_{\ell}
given the past depends only on the most recent
\displaystyle k

\displaystyle\ell>k
and all states in
\displaystyle S

\displaystyle\displaystyle P(X_{\ell}=i_{\ell}\mid X_{1}=i_{1},\dots,X_{\ell-1}=i_{\ell-1})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=P(X_{\ell}=i_{\ell}\mid X_{\ell-k}=i_{\ell-k},\dots,X_{\ell-1}=i_{\ell-1})

\displaystyle\displaystyle:=a^{(\ell)}_{i_{\ell-k}\cdots i_{\ell-1}i_{\ell}}.

(2.3)

The condition (
2.3
) is the Markov property for the
\displaystyle k-
th order Markov chain. In the time-homogeneous case,
\displaystyle a^{(\ell)}_{i_{\ell-k}\cdots i_{\ell-1}i_{\ell}}=a_{i_{\ell-k}\cdots i_{\ell-1}i_{\ell}}
for all
\displaystyle i_{\ell-k},\ldots,i_{\ell}\in S
and
\displaystyle\ell>k
.
The joint probability of a length-
\displaystyle n
path
\displaystyle(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n})
,
\displaystyle n>k
, can be written in terms of transition probabilities as

\displaystyle\displaystyle p_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=P(X_{1}=i_{1},\ldots,X_{n}=i_{n})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=P(X_{1}=i_{1},\ldots,X_{k}=i_{k})\prod_{\ell=k+1}^{n}P(X_{\ell}=i_{\ell}\mid X_{1}=i_{1},\ldots,X_{\ell-1}=i_{\ell-1})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\pi_{i_{1}\ldots i_{k}}\prod_{\ell=k+1}^{n}P(X_{\ell}=i_{\ell}\mid X_{\ell-k}=i_{\ell-k}\ldots X_{\ell-1}=i_{\ell-1})=\pi_{i_{1}\ldots i_{k}}\prod_{\ell=k+1}^{n}a^{(\ell)}_{i_{\ell-k}\cdots i_{\ell-1}i_{\ell}},

(2.4)

where
\displaystyle\pi_{i_{1}\ldots i_{k}}=P(X_{1}=i_{1},\ldots,X_{k}=i_{k})
denotes the initial distribution.

Since
\displaystyle\pi_{i_{1}\ldots i_{k}}
and
\displaystyle a^{(\ell)}_{i_{\ell-k}\cdots i_{\ell-1}i_{\ell}}
represent (conditional) probabilities, they satisfy

\displaystyle\displaystyle\biggl\{\pi_{i_{1}\ldots i_{k}}\geq 0,\;\text{for all}\;i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\in S,\;\;\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\in S}\pi_{i_{1}\ldots i_{k}}=1,\;\text{and}

\displaystyle\displaystyle a^{(\ell)}_{i_{\ell-k}\cdots i_{\ell-1}i_{\ell}}\geq 0,\;\;\sum_{i_{\ell}\in S}a^{(\ell)}_{i_{\ell-k}\cdots i_{\ell-1}i_{\ell}}=1,\;\text{for all}\;\ell\in\{k+1,\ldots,n\},i_{\ell-k},\cdots,i_{\ell-1}\in S\biggr\}.

(2.5)

Models with forbidden transitions.

In practice, the nature of the underlying process may impose additional constraints on the admissible
transitions between states in the process
\displaystyle\{X_{\ell},\ell\geq 1\}
. In such cases, the resulting model
can be viewed as a slice of the general model. The assumption that certain transitions are forbidden
is typically expressed by setting the corresponding transition probabilities to zero, which in turn
implies that all paths containing forbidden transitions have probability zero.

For example, under the illness-death model (Figure
3

\displaystyle 1\rightarrow 0
are forbidden, so
\displaystyle P(X_{\ell}=0\mid X_{\ell-1}=1)=a_{10}=0
for all

\displaystyle\ell>1

Figure
3

\displaystyle P(X_{\ell}=j\mid X_{\ell-1}=2)=a_{2j}=0
for

\displaystyle j=0,1
, and
\displaystyle P(X_{\ell}=2\mid X_{\ell-1}=2)=a_{22}=1
. By the same token, the initial
state may also be restricted, depending on the research question and study setting.
Under a Markov chain model with three states, the number of possible length-
\displaystyle 4
paths

\displaystyle(X_{1},\dots,X_{4})
is
\displaystyle 3^{4}
, whereas under the illness-death model (a three-state model with forbidden
transitions), with no restriction on the initial state, there are only
\displaystyle 14
possible paths
(Table

For another illustrative example of forbidden transitions, we consider a Markov chain on the alphabet

3
Algebraic statistical framework

In this section we translate the probabilistic factorization of path probabilities from
Section
2
into an algebraic framework. The key point is that the
Markov property yields a monomial parametrization of the joint path probabilities in terms of an
initial distribution and transition parameters. We formalize this parametrization, and explain how time-homogeneity and forbidden transitions fit into this
setup. Finally, we recall basic facts on toric ideals, since the models considered here are defined
by monomial parametrizations and hence have toric vanishing ideals.

3.1
Parametrization and implicitization

The goal of this section is to describe the algebraic setup corresponding to the statistical identity (
2.4
). The factorization of joint path probabilities in (
2.4
) defines a monomial parametrization of the joint distribution on paths.
We define the parametrization map

\phi:\;\mathbb{R}^{S^{k}}\times\prod_{\ell=k+1}^{n}\mathbb{R}^{S^{k+1}}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{S^{n}},\qquad\phi_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n}}\!\left(\pi_{i_{1}\ldots i_{k}},\bigl(a^{(\ell)}_{i_{\ell-k}\cdots i_{\ell}}\bigr)_{\ell=k+1}^{n}\right)=\pi_{i_{1}\ldots i_{k}}\prod_{\ell=k+1}^{n}a^{(\ell)}_{i_{\ell-k}\cdots i_{\ell-1}i_{\ell}}.

The pullback of
\displaystyle\phi
is the ring homomorphism

\phi^{*}:\mathbb{R}[p_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n}}]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\bigl[\,\pi_{i_{1}\ldots i_{k}},\;a^{(\ell)}_{i_{\ell}\,i_{\ell+1}\cdots i_{\ell+k}}\;\big|\;\ell=k+1,\ldots,n\bigr],\quad\phi^{*}(p_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n}})=\pi_{i_{1}\ldots i_{k}}\prod_{\ell=k+1}^{n}a^{(\ell)}_{i_{\ell-k}\cdots i_{\ell-1}i_{\ell}}.

In the homogeneous setting, the transition parameters

\displaystyle a^{(\ell)}_{i_{\ell-k}i_{\ell-k+1}\cdots i_{\ell}}

coincide for all
\displaystyle\ell
. We therefore drop the upper index and write

\displaystyle a_{i_{\ell-k}i_{\ell-k+1}\cdots i_{\ell}}
in the above formulas. We use the same notation for both parametrizations as it is usually clear from the context whether we are in nonhomogeneous or homogeneous setting.

Forbidden transitions are incorporated by setting the corresponding transition parameters to zero, yielding coordinate slices of the general parametrization.

We study the kernel of the pullback
\displaystyle\phi^{*}
, denoted
\displaystyle I:=\ker(\phi^{*})
, which is an ideal in

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}[p_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n}}]
. The elements of the kernel are polynomials in the joint probabilities
\displaystyle p_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n}}
. These polynomials vanish on all the joint probabilities in the corresponding models. Determining generators of this ideal is known as the implicitization
problem and provides a complete algebraic description of the model. In the next two sections, we
investigate this problem in the nonhomogeneous and homogeneous settings. For more information on the implicitization problem, we refer the reader to
[
6
, Chapter 3
§3]
.

3.2
Toric ideals

Toric ideals are classical objects in algebraic geometry, arising as defining ideals of affine toric
varieties, and have been extensively studied from geometric, algebraic, and combinatorial
perspectives. In algebraic statistics, toric ideals arise naturally as vanishing ideals of statistical
models defined by monomial parametrizations, known as log-linear models or discrete exponential
families

[
7
,
9
,
19
,
15
]
.

We briefly recall the relevant definitions; see
[
19
]
for further details.

Definition 3.1
.

Let

\displaystyle\phi^{*}:\mathbb{R}[p_{1},\ldots,p_{m}]\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}[\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{r}]

be a ring homomorphism such that each
\displaystyle\phi^{*}(p_{i})
is a monomial in the variables

\displaystyle\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{r}
. The
toric ideal
associated to
\displaystyle\phi^{*}
is the kernel

I_{\phi^{*}}:=\ker(\phi^{*})\subseteq\mathbb{R}[p_{1},\ldots,p_{m}].

The ideal
\displaystyle I_{\phi^{*}}
is prime and defines the Zariski closure of the image of the corresponding
monomial parametrization. Moreover,
\displaystyle I_{\phi^{*}}
encodes all polynomial relations among the
variables
\displaystyle p_{1},\ldots,p_{m}
implied by the parametrization. For a vector

\displaystyle u=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{m})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{m}
, we write
\displaystyle p^{u}:=p_{1}^{u_{1}}\cdots p_{m}^{u_{m}}
.
A fundamental structural result shows that
\displaystyle I_{\phi^{*}}
is generated by binomials: for any

\displaystyle u,v\in\mathbb{N}^{m}
satisfying
\displaystyle\phi^{*}(p^{u})=\phi^{*}(p^{v})
, the binomial
\displaystyle p^{u}-p^{v}
lies in

\displaystyle I_{\phi^{*}}
, and such binomials generate the ideal; see, for example,

[
19
, Proposition 6.2.4]
.

Remark 3.2
.

An equivalent description of toric ideals can be given in terms of integer matrices.
Let
\displaystyle A\in\mathbb{Z}^{r\times m}
be the matrix whose
\displaystyle i
-th column records the exponent vector of
the monomial
\displaystyle\phi^{*}(p_{i})
in the variables
\displaystyle\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{r}
. Then the toric ideal

\displaystyle I_{\phi^{*}}
is generated by all binomials
\displaystyle p^{u}-p^{v}
such that
\displaystyle Au=Av
, or equivalently,

\displaystyle u-v\in\ker_{\mathbb{Z}}(A)
. In this formulation, the algebraic relations among the joint
probabilities correspond to integer dependencies among the columns of
\displaystyle A
. This matrix representation is particularly useful for maximum likelihood estimation on log-linear models.

When the statistical constraints on the parameters are disregarded, both the homogeneous and nonhomogeneous multistate Markov models considered in this paper are defined by monomial parametrizations of joint path probabilities in terms of transition parameters.
Consequently, the corresponding vanishing ideals are toric ideals in the sense of
Definition
3.1
.

4
Nonhomogeneous multistate Markov models

In this section, we study the implicitization of nonhomogeneous
\displaystyle k
-th order multistate Markov models.
Our main goal is to describe the generators of the vanishing ideal of the model and to understand how
this description changes in the presence of forbidden transitions.
The key observation is that nonhomogeneous multistate Markov models when the statistical constraints on the parameters are disregarded can be identified with slices of
decomposable hierarchical models.
This identification allows us to apply existing results on the structure of vanishing ideals
of hierarchical models.
Our contribution is twofold: first, we make this connection explicit for higher-order Markov chains,
and second, we show how forbidden transitions are reflected algebraically by passing to coordinate
slices of the corresponding toric variety.
In Section
4.1
, we establish the connection to hierarchical models.
In Section
4.2
, we describe the generators of the
vanishing ideal for the general nonhomogeneous model and its restricted variants when the statistical constraints on the parameters are disregarded.
Finally, in Section
4.3
, we show that imposing the summing-to-one
constraint on the toric variety recovers the statistical model inside the probability simplex.

4.1
Connection to hierarchical models

In the nonhomogeneous setting, Markov chains can be viewed as decomposable hierarchical models in the sense of algebraic statistics. This observation allows one to use established results on the structure and generating sets of the associated vanishing ideals for decomposable models
[
9
,
7
,
19
]
.

Definition 4.1
.

A
simplicial complex
on a finite ground set
\displaystyle[n]
is a collection

\displaystyle\Gamma\subseteq 2^{[n]}
such that for all
\displaystyle F\in\Gamma
, every subset

\displaystyle F^{\prime}\subseteq F
also belongs to
\displaystyle\Gamma
.
A simplicial complex
\displaystyle\Gamma
is
reducible
with decomposition

\displaystyle(\Gamma_{1},B,\Gamma_{2})
if
\displaystyle\Gamma_{1}\cup\Gamma_{2}=\Gamma
,

\displaystyle\Gamma_{1}\cap\Gamma_{2}=2^{B}
, and
\displaystyle\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2}\neq 2^{B}
.
Finally,
\displaystyle\Gamma
is
decomposable
if it is reducible and both components

\displaystyle\Gamma_{1}
and
\displaystyle\Gamma_{2}
are either decomposable or simplices (of the form

\displaystyle 2^{F}
for some
\displaystyle F
).

We briefly recall the notion of hierarchical models.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
\displaystyle X=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{m})
be an
\displaystyle m
-dimensional discrete random vector, where each
variable
\displaystyle X_{i}
has finite state space
\displaystyle[r_{i}]
.
Let
\displaystyle\textstyle{R=\prod_{i=1}^{m}[r_{i}]}
denote the joint state space, and fix a simplicial
complex
\displaystyle\Gamma
on the ground set
\displaystyle[m]
.
The
hierarchical model
associated with
\displaystyle\Gamma
consists of all probability
distributions
\displaystyle(p_{i})_{i\in R}
on
\displaystyle R
that admit a parametrization of the form

p_{i}=\frac{1}{Z(\theta)}\prod_{F\text{ facet of }\Gamma}\theta^{(F)}_{i_{F}},\qquad i\in R,

(4.1)

where
\displaystyle\theta^{(F)}_{i_{F}}
are nonnegative parameters associated with the facet
\displaystyle F
,

\displaystyle i_{F}
denotes the restriction of
\displaystyle i
to the coordinates indexed by
\displaystyle F
,
and
\displaystyle Z(\theta)
is a normalizing constant ensuring that
\displaystyle\textstyle{\sum_{i\in R}p_{i}=1}
.
The hierarchical model is said to be
decomposable
if the simplicial complex

\displaystyle\Gamma
is decomposable.

The following lemma shows that the general nonhomogeneous higher-order multistate
Markov model fits naturally into this framework.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\displaystyle\phi
be the parametrization map of a nonhomogeneous
\displaystyle k
-th order multistate Markov model. The set
\displaystyle\phi\left(\textstyle{\mathbb{R}^{S^{k}}\times\prod_{\ell=k+1}^{n}\mathbb{R}^{S^{k+1}}}\right)\cap\Delta_{|S|^{n}-1}
is a decomposable
hierarchical model as defined in Definition
4.2
.

Proof.

Consider the parametrization map
\displaystyle\textstyle{p_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n}}=\pi_{i_{1}\ldots i_{k}}\prod_{j=k+1}^{n}a^{(\ell)}_{i_{\ell-k}\ldots i_{\ell}}}
for a nonhomogeneous
\displaystyle k
-th order Markov multistate model on the state space
\displaystyle S
.
Define a simplicial complex
\displaystyle\Gamma
on
\displaystyle[n]
whose facets are
the
\displaystyle(k+1)
-subsets

\displaystyle\{1,\ldots,k+1\}
,
\displaystyle\{2,\ldots,k+2\},\ldots
,
\displaystyle\{n-k,\ldots,n\}
.
This simplicial complex is decomposable since it admits the recursive decomposition obtained by
splitting off the last facet
\displaystyle\{n-k,\ldots,n\}
along the separator
\displaystyle\{n-k,\ldots,n-1\}
,
and iterating.

We relabel the parameters in (
4.1
) by setting
\displaystyle\theta^{(\{1,\ldots,k+1\})}_{i_{\ell-k}\ldots i_{\ell}}:=\pi_{i_{1}\ldots i_{k}}a^{(k+1)}_{i_{1}\ldots i_{k+1}}
and

\displaystyle\theta^{(\{\ell-k,\ldots,\ell\})}_{i_{\ell-k}\ldots i_{\ell}}:=a^{(\ell)}_{i_{\ell-k}\ldots i_{\ell}}
for

\displaystyle\ell=k+2,\ldots,n
.
With these identifications, the product over facets in (
4.1
)
equals
\displaystyle\textstyle{\prod_{\ell=k+1}^{n}\theta^{(\{\ell-k,\ldots,\ell\})}_{i_{\ell-k}\ldots i_{\ell}}}=\pi_{i_{1}\ldots i_{k}}\textstyle{\prod_{\ell=k+1}^{n}a^{(\ell)}_{i_{\ell-k}\ldots i_{\ell}}}=p_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n}}
(up to the normalizing constant).
Hence the set
\displaystyle\phi\left(\textstyle{\mathbb{R}^{S^{k}}\times\prod_{\ell=k+1}^{n}\mathbb{R}^{S^{k+1}}}\right)\cap\Delta_{|S|^{n}-1}
is a hierarchical model for
\displaystyle\Gamma
, as desired.
∎

From an algebraic perspective, forbidding transitions corresponds to setting certain joint path
probabilities identically equal to zero.
The following proposition makes this precise.

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
\displaystyle P_{F}
denote the set of joint probability variables

\displaystyle p_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n}}
that involve at least one forbidden transition.
Then
\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{F}
can be identified with the coordinate slice

\mathcal{V}_{F}=\mathcal{V}\cap\{\,p\mid p_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n}}=0\text{ for all }p_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n}}\in P_{F}\,\}.

Proof.

For the inclusion “
\displaystyle\subseteq
”, let
\displaystyle p\in\mathcal{V}_{F}
.
By construction,
\displaystyle p
arises from the parametrization of
\displaystyle\mathcal{V}
with all
forbidden transition parameters set to zero.
Hence
\displaystyle p
lies in
\displaystyle\mathcal{V}
and satisfies
\displaystyle p_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n}}=0
for all

\displaystyle p_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n}}\in P_{F}
, proving the left-hand inclusion.

For the inclusion “
\displaystyle\supseteq
”, let
\displaystyle p
lie in the right-hand side.
Then
\displaystyle p
admits a parametrization in
\displaystyle\mathcal{V}
, possibly with nonzero values
assigned to parameters corresponding to forbidden transitions.
Since all joint probabilities involving forbidden transitions vanish, these
parameters may be set to zero without changing
\displaystyle p
.
The resulting parameter choice lies in the parameter space of
\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{F}
,
so
\displaystyle p\in\mathcal{V}_{F}
.
∎

4.2
Implicit description

We now describe the vanishing ideal of the general nonhomogeneous
\displaystyle k
-th order multistate Markov model when the statistical constraints on the parameters are disregarded.
Since this model is hierarchical as shown in Section
4.1
, its vanishing ideal is generated by binomials corresponding to
conditional independence statements between appropriate blocks of variables.

Proposition 4.5
.

The vanishing ideal
\displaystyle\ker(\phi^{*})
of the general
\displaystyle k
-th order multistate Markov model is generated by polynomials of the form

\displaystyle\displaystyle p_{i_{1}\ldots i_{r},\,j_{r+1}\ldots j_{r+k},\,s_{r+k+1}\ldots s_{n}}\,p_{i^{\prime}_{1}\ldots i^{\prime}_{r},\,j_{r+1}\ldots j_{r+k},\,s^{\prime}_{r+k+1}\ldots s^{\prime}_{n}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\qquad-p_{i_{1}\ldots i_{r},\,j_{r+1}\ldots j_{r+k},\,s^{\prime}_{r+k+1}\ldots s^{\prime}_{n}}\,p_{i^{\prime}_{1}\ldots i^{\prime}_{r},\,j_{r+1}\ldots j_{r+k},\,s_{r+k+1}\ldots s_{n}},

or, equivalently, in block notation,

p_{IJS}p_{I^{\prime}JS^{\prime}}-p_{IJS^{\prime}}p_{I^{\prime}JS},

where
\displaystyle|I|=|I^{\prime}|=r
,
\displaystyle|J|=k
, and
\displaystyle|S|=|S^{\prime}|=n-k-r
.
These binomials correspond to exchanging the values of the blocks
\displaystyle I
and
\displaystyle I^{\prime}
while keeping the
conditioning block
\displaystyle J
fixed.

The result is a special case of a general result about vanishing ideals for decomposable hierarchical models
[
9
, Theorem 4.5]
.

Remark 4.6
.

From the statistical viewpoint, the claim in Proposition
4.5
means that these relations vanish on the model and can be written equivalently as the equality of the ratios, provided the path probabilities are nonzero:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\qquad\frac{P\!\left(X_{1}=i_{1},\ldots,X_{r}=i_{r},\,X_{r+1}=j_{r+1},\ldots,X_{r+k}=j_{r+k},\,X_{r+k+1}=s_{r+k+1},\ldots,X_{n}=s_{n}\right)}{P\!\left(X_{1}=i_{1},\ldots,X_{r}=i_{r},\,X_{r+1}=j_{r+1},\ldots,X_{r+k}=j_{r+k},\,X_{r+k+1}=s^{\prime}_{r+k+1},\ldots,X_{n}=s^{\prime}_{n}\right)}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{P\!\left(X_{1}=i^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,X_{r}=i^{\prime}_{r},\,X_{r+1}=j_{r+1},\ldots,X_{r+k}=j_{r+k},\,X_{r+k+1}=s_{r+k+1},\ldots,X_{n}=s_{n}\right)}{P\!\left(X_{1}=i^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,X_{r}=i^{\prime}_{r},\,X_{r+1}=j_{r+1},\ldots,X_{r+k}=j_{r+k},\,X_{r+k+1}=s^{\prime}_{r+k+1},\ldots,X_{n}=s^{\prime}_{n}\right)}.

By Bayes’ formula, we get for the left-hand side of the above ratios:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{P\!\left(X_{1}=i_{1},\ldots,X_{r}=i_{r},\,X_{r+1}=j_{r+1},\ldots,X_{r+k}=j_{r+k},\,X_{r+k+1}=s_{r+k+1},\ldots,X_{n}=s_{n}\right)}{P\!\left(X_{1}=i_{1},\ldots,X_{r}=i_{r},\,X_{r+1}=j_{r+1},\ldots,X_{r+k}=j_{r+k},\,X_{r+k+1}=s^{\prime}_{r+k+1},\ldots,X_{n}=s^{\prime}_{n}\right)}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{P\!\left(X_{r+k+1}=s_{r+k+1},\ldots,X_{n}=s_{n}\ \middle|\ X_{1}=i_{1},\ldots X_{r}=i_{r},X_{r+1}=j_{r+1},\ldots,X_{r+k}=j_{r+k}\right)}{P\!\left(X_{r+k+1}=s^{\prime}_{r+k+1},\ldots,X_{n}=s^{\prime}_{n}\ \middle|\ X_{1}=i_{1}.\ldots,X_{r}=i_{r},X_{r+1}=j_{r+1},\ldots,X_{r+k}=j_{r+k}\right)}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{P\!\left(X_{r+k+1}=s_{r+k+1},\ldots,X_{n}=s_{n}\ \middle|\ X_{r+1}=j_{r+1},\ldots,X_{r+k}=j_{r+k}\right)}{P\!\left(X_{r+k+1}=s^{\prime}_{r+k+1},\ldots,X_{n}=s^{\prime}_{n}\ \middle|\ X_{r+1}=j_{r+1},\ldots,X_{r+k}=j_{r+k}\right)},

Similarly, applying the Bayes’ formula for the right-hand side, we get the same expression, which shows that the two ratios are equal.

The description in Proposition
4.5
easily extends to models in which some transitions are forbidden.

Corollary 4.7
.

With the notation of Proposition
4.4
, the vanishing
ideal of
\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{F}
is obtained from that of
\displaystyle\mathcal{V}
by adding the polynomials

\displaystyle p_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n}}\in P_{F}
.

In particular, Corollary
4.7
implies that no new higher-degree generators arise from forbidden transitions beyond those
already present in the unrestricted model.

Proof.

As discussed in the previous subsection,
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{F}
is a slice of the general model
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}
.
The vanishing ideal of the intersection of two algebraic sets is the sum of their vanishing ideals.
In the present setting, this implies that the generators of the restricted model are obtained by
adding the polynomials
\displaystyle p_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n}}\in P_{F}
to the generating set of the general model.
∎

Remark 4.8
.

Note that Proposition
4.4
and Corollary
4.7
,
apply equally to homogeneous multistate Markov models, since their proofs
do not use the nonhomogeneous assumption.

4.3
Inside probability simplex

In this section we show that
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{V}\cap\Delta_{|S|^{n}-1}
in the nonhomogeneous setting.

Proposition 4.9
.

We have
\displaystyle\phi(\Theta)=\phi\left(\mathbb{R}^{S^{k}}\times\prod_{\ell=k+1}^{n}\mathbb{R}^{S^{k+1}}\right)\cap\Delta_{|S|^{n}-1}
.

Proof.

The left-hand side is contained in the right-hand side because it is clearly contained in
\displaystyle\phi(\mathbb{R}^{S^{k}}\times\prod_{\ell=k+1}^{n}\mathbb{R}^{S^{k+1}})
and the statistical constraints on the parameters guarantee that it is contained in
\displaystyle\Delta_{|S|^{n}-1}
. Now, let
\displaystyle(p_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n}})\in\phi(\mathbb{R}^{S^{k}}\times\prod_{\ell=k+1}^{n}\mathbb{R}^{S^{k+1}})\cap\Delta_{|S|^{n}-1}
. It means that the coordinates of this point sum to 1, and it satisfies

p_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n}}=\pi_{i_{1}\ldots i_{k}}\prod_{\ell=k+1}^{n}a^{(\ell)}_{i_{\ell-k}\cdots i_{\ell-1}i_{\ell}},

where all the parameters are real numbers.

Note that we can write

p_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n}}=\prod_{\ell=k+1}^{n}b^{(\ell)}_{i_{\ell-k}\cdots i_{\ell-1}i_{\ell}},

where
\displaystyle b^{(k+1)}_{i_{1}\ldots i_{k+1}}=\pi_{i_{1}\ldots i_{k}}a^{(k+1)}_{i_{1}\ldots i_{k+1}}
, and
\displaystyle b^{(\ell)}_{i_{\ell-k}\ldots i_{\ell}}=a^{(\ell)}_{i_{\ell-k}\ldots i_{\ell}}
for
\displaystyle\ell\geq k+2
. This way we find different values for parameters that are mapped to the same point by
\displaystyle\phi
. Moreover here the value of the first parameter
\displaystyle\pi_{i_{1}\ldots i_{k}}
is 1.

We will introduce new values

a^{\prime(\ell)}_{i_{\ell-k}\ldots i_{\ell}}=\frac{b^{(\ell)}_{i_{\ell-k}\ldots i_{\ell}}\sum_{j_{\ell+1},\ldots j_{n}}\prod_{t=\ell+1}^{n}b^{(t)}_{j_{t-k}\ldots j_{t}}}{\sum_{j_{\ell},\ldots j_{n}}\prod_{t=\ell}^{n}a^{(t)}_{j_{t-k}\ldots j_{t}}},

where
\displaystyle j_{m}=i_{m},
if
\displaystyle m
is outside sum limits. Also,

\pi^{\prime}_{i_{1}\ldots i_{k}}=\frac{\sum_{j_{k+1}\ldots j_{n}}\prod_{\ell=k+1}^{n}b^{(\ell)}_{j_{\ell-k}\ldots j_{\ell}}}{\sum_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}\prod_{\ell=k+1}^{n}b^{(\ell)}_{j_{\ell-k}\ldots j_{\ell}}},

where, again,
\displaystyle j_{m}=i_{m},
if
\displaystyle m
is outside the sum limits.
The new parameters correspond to conditional probabilities (they sum to 1 when varying over the last index).

The denominator of
\displaystyle\pi^{\prime}_{i_{1}\ldots i_{k}}
is 1, as it is the sum of all coordinates
\displaystyle(p_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n}})
. Also, when writing the monomial parametrization with new parameters, denominator of each parameter is a multiplier in the numerator of the previous parameter. Thus, it is easy to see that

p_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n}}=\prod_{\ell=k+1}^{n}a^{(\ell)}_{i_{\ell-k}\cdots i_{\ell-1}i_{\ell}}=\pi^{\prime}_{i_{1}\ldots i_{k}}\prod_{\ell=k+1}^{n}a^{\prime(\ell)}_{i_{\ell-k}\cdots i_{\ell-1}i_{\ell}}.\qed

Remark 4.10
.

The
\displaystyle k
-th order nonhomogeneous Markov chain can also be seen as a discrete directed graphical model on
\displaystyle n
vertices with an edge
\displaystyle i\rightarrow\ell
for every
\displaystyle\ell\in\{k+1,\ldots,n\}
and
\displaystyle i\in\{\ell-k,\ldots,\ell-1\}
. Proposition
4.9
follows also from
[
17
, Theorem 3.27]
.

The statement of Proposition
4.9
extends verbatim to models with
forbidden transitions by intersecting both sides with the corresponding coordinate hyperplanes. On the other hand, although we have equality
\displaystyle I(\mathcal{M})=\textstyle{\sqrt{I(\mathcal{V})+\left\langle 1-\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}}p_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n}}\right\rangle}}
over the complex numbers
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}
, we do not know whether this equality also holds
over the real numbers
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}
.

5
Homogeneous multistate Markov models

In the nonhomogeneous setting, multistate Markov models when the statistical constraints on the parameters are disregarded admit a clean algebraic description:
they are decomposable hierarchical models, and their vanishing ideals are generated by explicit
families of binomials arising from local Markov moves
(Section
4
).
Imposing time homogeneity fundamentally changes this picture.
Although homogeneous multistate Markov models are still defined by monomial parametrizations,
the identification of transition parameters across time induces additional polynomial relations
among joint path probabilities.
As a consequence, homogeneous models generally fall outside the class of decomposable hierarchical
models, and their vanishing ideals are strictly larger than those of their nonhomogeneous counterparts.

This difference already appears in simple examples.
For instance, in the homogeneous illness-death model
(see column Prob (homogeneous), Table
1
),
the elimination ideal contains binomial relations that are not implied
by the Markov property alone.
For example, while the binomial

p_{0012}p_{0111}-p_{0011}p_{0112}

appears in the nonhomogeneous case, the additional
relations

p_{0011}p_{0012}-p_{0001}p_{0112}\qquad\text{and}\qquad p_{0011}^{2}-p_{0001}p_{0111}

reflect the imposed equality of transition probabilities across time points.

From an algebraic perspective, these additional relations arise from identifying transition
parameters associated with different time indices.
Several families of binomial relations for homogeneous models can be derived directly from the
parametrization by exploiting shift invariance of the transition probabilities.
In particular, odds-ratio–type relations analogous to those in the nonhomogeneous case
vanish on all homogeneous Markov chains.
However, explicit computations show that these relations do not, in general, generate the full
vanishing ideal
\displaystyle\ker(\phi^{*})
.

As a first step toward understanding the algebraic structure of homogeneous models,
we identify a general family of binomial relations that necessarily belong to the vanishing
ideal.
The following proposition provides a homogeneous analogue of
Proposition
4.5
.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}
be the
\displaystyle k
-th order homogeneous multistate Markov chain.
Fix two states
\displaystyle\psi,\eta
and two blocks

\Gamma=(\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{k}),\qquad\Delta=(\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{k}).

Then the vanishing ideal
\displaystyle I(\mathcal{M})
contains all binomials of the form

p_{I,\,\Gamma,\,\psi,\,\Delta,\,J}\,p_{I^{\prime},\,\Gamma,\,\eta,\,\Delta,\,J^{\prime}}-p_{I,\,\Gamma,\,\eta,\,\Delta,\,J}\,p_{I^{\prime},\,\Gamma,\,\psi,\,\Delta,\,J^{\prime}},

where
\displaystyle I,I^{\prime},J,J^{\prime}
are index blocks such that the concatenations

I\,\Gamma\,\psi\,\Delta\,J,\quad I^{\prime}\,\Gamma\,\psi\,\Delta\,J^{\prime},\quad I\,\Gamma\,\eta\,\Delta\,J,\quad\text{and}\quad I^{\prime}\,\Gamma\,\eta\,\Delta\,J^{\prime}

define paths of length
\displaystyle n
.
If one of the blocks
\displaystyle I
or
\displaystyle I^{\prime}
is empty (respectively,
\displaystyle J
or
\displaystyle J^{\prime}
is empty),
then the corresponding block
\displaystyle\Gamma
(respectively,
\displaystyle\Delta
)
may have length strictly smaller than
\displaystyle k
.

Proof.

Assume
\displaystyle I,J,I^{\prime},J^{\prime}
are nonempty (the empty-block case is identical). Fix states

\displaystyle\psi,\eta
and fixed states
\displaystyle\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{k}
and
\displaystyle\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{k}
.
We first treat the interior case, i.e. when the symbol
\displaystyle\psi
(or
\displaystyle\eta
) occurs at a position

\displaystyle r
with
\displaystyle k+1\leq r,\ell\leq n-k
, so that the
corresponding joint-probability entries are

p_{i_{1},\dots,i_{r-k-1},\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{k},\psi,\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{k},i_{r+k+1},\dots,i_{n}}\quad\text{and}\quad p_{j_{1},\dots,j_{\ell-k-1},\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{k},\psi,\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{k},j_{\ell+k+1},\dots,j_{n}},

and likewise with
\displaystyle\psi
replaced by
\displaystyle\eta
.

Since we are in the
homogeneous

\displaystyle k
-th order Markov model, the joint probability of a path
factors as

p_{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}}=\pi_{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}}\,\prod_{t=k+1}^{n}a_{x_{t-k}\cdots x_{t-1}x_{t}},

where the transition parameters
\displaystyle a_{x_{t-k}\cdots x_{t-1}x_{t}}
do
not
depend on
\displaystyle t
.

Consider the ratio of the two path probabilities that differ only by
\displaystyle\psi
versus
\displaystyle\eta
at the
position
\displaystyle r
. By cancellation of all common factors in the above product, we get

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{p_{i_{1},\dots,i_{r-k-1},\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{k},\psi,\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{k},i_{r+k+1},\dots,i_{n}}}{p_{i_{1},\dots,i_{r-k-1},\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{k},\eta,\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{k},i_{r+k+1},\dots,i_{n}}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{a_{\gamma_{1}\cdots\gamma_{k}\psi}\,a_{\gamma_{2}\cdots\gamma_{k}\psi\delta_{1}}\cdots a_{\psi\delta_{1}\cdots\delta_{k}}}{a_{\gamma_{1}\cdots\gamma_{k}\eta}\,a_{\gamma_{2}\cdots\gamma_{k}\eta\delta_{1}}\cdots a_{\eta\delta_{1}\cdots\delta_{k}}}.

Exactly the same cancellation argument for the ratio at the position
\displaystyle\ell
yields

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{p_{j_{1},\dots,j_{\ell-k-1},\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{k},\psi,\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{k},j_{\ell+k+1},\dots,j_{n}}}{p_{j_{1},\dots,j_{\ell-k-1},\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{k},\eta,\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{k},j_{\ell+k+1},\dots,j_{n}}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{a_{\gamma_{1}\cdots\gamma_{k}\psi}\,a_{\gamma_{2}\cdots\gamma_{k}\psi\delta_{1}}\cdots a_{\psi\delta_{1}\cdots\delta_{k}}}{a_{\gamma_{1}\cdots\gamma_{k}\eta}\,a_{\gamma_{2}\cdots\gamma_{k}\eta\delta_{1}}\cdots a_{\eta\delta_{1}\cdots\delta_{k}}}.

Hence, the two ratios are equal.
Equivalently, by Bayes’ formula, the equality holds
if and only if

\displaystyle\displaystyle P\!\left(X_{r}=\psi\mid X_{1}=i_{1},\ldots,X_{r-k-1}=i_{r-k-1},\,X_{r-k}=\gamma_{1},\ldots,X_{r-1}=\gamma_{k},\right.

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left.X_{r+1}=\delta_{1},\ldots,X_{r+k}=\delta_{k},\,X_{r+k+1}=i_{r+k+1},\ldots,X_{n}=i_{n}\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=P\!\left(X_{\ell}=\psi\mid X_{1}=j_{1},\ldots,X_{\ell-k-1}=j_{\ell-k-1},\,X_{\ell-k}=\gamma_{1},\ldots,X_{\ell-1}=\gamma_{k},\right.

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left.X_{\ell+1}=\delta_{1},\ldots,X_{\ell+k}=\delta_{k},\,X_{\ell+k+1}=j_{\ell+k+1},\ldots,X_{n}=j_{n}\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=P\!\left(X_{r}=\psi\mid X_{r-k}=\gamma_{1},\ldots,X_{r-1}=\gamma_{k},\,X_{r+1}=\delta_{1},\ldots,X_{r+k}=\delta_{k}\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=P\!\left(X_{\ell}=\psi\mid X_{\ell-k}=\gamma_{1},\ldots,X_{\ell-1}=\gamma_{k},\,X_{\ell+1}=\delta_{1},\ldots,X_{\ell+k}=\delta_{k}\right).

By the
\displaystyle k
-th order Markov property, conditioning on the full configuration may be
reduced to conditioning on the
\displaystyle k
nearest neighbors,

P(X_{r}=\psi\mid X_{1},\ldots,X_{r-1},X_{r+1},\ldots,X_{n})=P(X_{r}=\psi\mid X_{r-k},\ldots,X_{r-1},X_{r+1},\ldots,X_{r+k}),

for
\displaystyle k+1\leq r\leq n-k
. Moreover, by homogeneity, this conditional distribution depends only on the local block

\displaystyle(\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{k},\,\psi,\,\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{k})
and is therefore independent
of the absolute position
\displaystyle r
.
This is precisely the defining property of a homogeneous
\displaystyle k
-th order Markov chain.

Finally, clearing denominators yields the binomial

p_{I,\,\gamma_{1}\ldots\gamma_{k},\,\psi,\,\delta_{1}\ldots\delta_{k},\,J}\,p_{I^{\prime},\,\gamma_{1}\ldots\gamma_{k},\,\eta,\,\delta_{1}\ldots\delta_{k},\,J^{\prime}}-p_{I,\,\gamma_{1}\ldots\gamma_{k},\,\eta,\,\delta_{1}\ldots\delta_{k},\,J}\,p_{I^{\prime},\,\gamma_{1}\ldots\gamma_{k},\,\psi,\,\delta_{1}\ldots\delta_{k},\,J^{\prime}},

which therefore belongs to
\displaystyle I(\mathcal{M})
.

For boundary cases (when
\displaystyle r\leq k
or
\displaystyle r>n-k
), the same cancellation argument applies:
the ratio is a product of the transition factors whose windows intersect
\displaystyle r
, and the set of
affected windows is again determined by the available neighbors.
Thus the ratio still depends only on the local configuration and not on the global position,
and the same cross-multiplication yields the stated binomial.∎

Remark 5.2
.

For a homogeneous
\displaystyle k
-th order multistate Markov model, the path probability

\displaystyle p_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n}}
depends only on the sufficient statistics, namely the counts
of length-
\displaystyle(k+1)
contiguous blocks.
Consequently, if a permutation
\displaystyle\sigma
of the time indices preserves these
block counts and the initial
\displaystyle k
-block, then

\displaystyle p_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n}}=p_{\sigma(i_{1})\ldots\sigma(i_{n})}

for all parameter values, and the linear binomial

\displaystyle p_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n}}-p_{\sigma(i_{1})\ldots\sigma(i_{n})}
lies in the vanishing ideal

\displaystyle I(\mathcal{M})
.

Computations indicate that the binomials in
Proposition
5.1
and Remark
5.2

do not generate the vanishing ideal
\displaystyle I(\mathcal{V})=\ker(\phi^{*})
in the homogeneous case. This is illustrated in Example
3
, where the Gröbner basis of
\displaystyle\ker(\phi^{*})
contains binomials of degree three that cannot be obtained from Proposition
5.1
and Remark
5.2
.
Moreover, the following example shows that the
inclusion of ideals in (
3.1
) can be strict.

Example 3
.

In this example we show that the inclusion of ideals (
3.1
)

\sqrt{\ker(\phi^{*})+\langle 1-\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}}p_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n}}}\rangle\subseteq I(\phi(\Theta)).

can be strict in the homogeneous setting.
Consider the case
\displaystyle n=3
,
\displaystyle k=1
, with binary state space
\displaystyle S=\{0,1\}
.
Let

be the ideal defining the homogeneous multistate Markov parametrization together with the normalization
constraints. Then

\displaystyle I(\phi(\Theta))=I\cap\mathbb{R}[p_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n}}]
. Using
Macaulay2
, we find that this elimination ideal is generated by

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\ker(\phi^{*})=\langle\,

\displaystyle\displaystyle p_{011}p_{110}-p_{010}p_{111},\quad p_{011}p_{100}-p_{001}p_{110},\quad p_{001}p_{100}-p_{000}p_{101},

\displaystyle\displaystyle p_{001}p_{110}^{2}-p_{010}p_{100}p_{111},\quad p_{000}p_{011}p_{101}-p_{001}^{2}p_{110},\quad p_{000}p_{101}p_{110}^{2}-p_{010}p_{100}^{2}p_{111}\,\rangle.

We must also impose

Therefore

Since both of these ideals are radical, by the ideal-variety correspondence, they define different sets. However, at this point we do not know if these sets are different also inside the probability simplex. We will show that this is the case in Section

by showing that maximum likelihood estimates over both sets are different.

6
Maximum likelihood estimation and likelihood geometry

In this section, we compare classical statistical maximum likelihood estimation with algebraic approaches. We show
that for homogeneous multistate Markov models the algebraic formulation leads to more challenging questions than the
classical statistical approach.

6.1
Nonhomogeneous multistate Markov model

Let
\displaystyle\mathbf{X}^{(m)}=(X^{(m)}_{1},\dots,X^{(m)}_{L+1})
, with
\displaystyle L>k
, for
\displaystyle m=1,\dots,M
, be
\displaystyle M
independent realizations of a nonhomogeneous
\displaystyle k
-th order Markov chain with initial probabilities
\displaystyle\pi_{i_{1}\dots i_{k}}>0
and transition probabilities
\displaystyle a^{(\ell)}_{i_{\ell-k}\dots i_{\ell-1}i_{\ell}}>0
. Note that the forbidden transitions are accounted for through the conditions on the transition probabilities.
For the
\displaystyle k
-th order nonhomogeneous model, the likelihood function of the parameters
\displaystyle(\pi,a)
given the observed data
\displaystyle(\mathbf{X}^{(1)},\dots,\mathbf{X}^{(M)})
is

\displaystyle\displaystyle P(\mathbf{X}^{(1)},\dots,\mathbf{X}^{(M)})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\prod_{m=1}^{M}\left(\pi_{X^{(m)}_{1}\dots X^{(m)}_{k}}\prod_{\ell=k+1}^{L+1}a^{(\ell)}_{X^{(m)}_{\ell-k}\dots X^{(m)}_{\ell-1}X^{(m)}_{\ell}}\right).

(6.1)

The maximum likelihood estimators of the initial distribution and the transition probabilities are then the empirical proportions as given below.

\displaystyle\displaystyle\hat{\pi}_{i_{1}\dots i_{k}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\sum\limits_{m=1}^{M}{\bf 1}(X_{1}^{(m)}=i_{1},\dots,X_{k}^{(m)}=i_{k})

\displaystyle\displaystyle\hat{a}_{i_{\ell-k}\dots i_{\ell-1}i_{\ell}}^{(\ell)}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{\sum\limits_{m=1}^{M}{\bf 1}(X_{\ell-k}^{(m)}=i_{\ell-k},\dots,X_{\ell-1}^{(m)}=i_{\ell-1},X_{\ell}^{(m)}=i_{\ell})}{\sum\limits_{m=1}^{M}{\bf 1}(X_{\ell-k}^{(m)}=i_{\ell-k},\dots,X_{\ell-1}^{(m)}=i_{\ell-1})}.

(6.2)

It is worth noting that, for a given history
\displaystyle(i_{\ell-k},\dots,i_{\ell-1})
, the above estimators determine
the transition probabilities to all but one state
\displaystyle i_{\ell}
. The remaining transition probability is
obtained by enforcing the normalization constraints in (
2.5
).

For a first-order nonhomogeneous Markov chain, these expressions simplify to

\displaystyle\displaystyle\hat{a}^{(\ell)}_{i_{\ell-1}i_{\ell}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathbf{1}\!\left(X^{(m)}_{\ell-1}=i_{\ell-1},\;X^{(m)}_{\ell}=i_{\ell}\right)}{\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathbf{1}\!\left(X^{(m)}_{\ell-1}=i_{\ell-1}\right)}=\frac{\#\{\text{transitions }i_{\ell-1}\to i_{\ell}\}}{\#\{\text{visits to state }i_{\ell-1}\}},

(6.3)

that is, the empirical conditional frequency of transitions from
\displaystyle i_{\ell-1}
to
\displaystyle i_{\ell}
at time
\displaystyle\ell
.

In this case, the maximum likelihood estimator of the initial distribution is

\displaystyle\displaystyle\hat{\pi}_{i_{1}}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathbf{1}\!\left(X^{(m)}_{1}=i_{1}\right),\qquad i_{1}\in S.

(6.4)

The MLE of the joint probability of a length-
\displaystyle n
path
\displaystyle p_{i_{1}\dots i_{n}}
is obtained by substituting

\displaystyle\hat{\pi}_{i_{1}}
and
\displaystyle\hat{a}^{(\ell)}_{i_{\ell-1}i_{\ell}}
into the factorization in (
2.2
).
This estimator coincides with a special case of the MLE formula for decomposable hierarchical models
in algebraic statistics, expressed here in the language of Markov chains.

Let
\displaystyle\mathbf{u}=(u_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n}})
denote the vector of observed counts for joint state sequences,
where

u_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n}}=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathbf{1}\!\left(X_{1}^{(m)}=i_{1},\ldots,X_{n}^{(m)}=i_{n}\right)\in\mathbb{N}

is the number of times the sequence
\displaystyle(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n})
appears in the data.
The total number of observations is

\displaystyle\textstyle{M=\sum_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n}}u_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n}}.}

For a fixed
\displaystyle k<n\leq L+1
, the likelihood function of the length-
\displaystyle n
path probabilities
based on the data
\displaystyle(X_{1}^{(m)},\ldots,X_{n}^{(m)})
,
\displaystyle m=1,\ldots,M
, is

L(p;\mathbf{u})=\prod_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}}p_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n}}^{\,u_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n}}}.

Unlike the likelihood in (
6.1
), which uses the full
trajectory data, this likelihood depends only on the empirical counts of length-
\displaystyle n
paths.
The goal of maximum likelihood estimation is to determine the probabilities

\displaystyle p=(p_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n}})
within the model that maximize
\displaystyle L(p;\mathbf{u})
.
This is a constrained optimization problem, where the constraints
are given by the polynomial equations defining the model, as described in
Section
4
. In general, the likelihood equations may admit
multiple complex critical points.
For generic data vectors
\displaystyle\mathbf{u}
, the number of nonsingular complex critical points is constant and is
called the
maximum likelihood degree
(ML degree) of the model
(see, e.g.,
[
10
, Chapter 6]
).
The ML degree serves as a measure of the algebraic complexity of the maximum likelihood estimation problem, although one has to keep in mind that it does not account for critical points that are singular or on the boundary of the model.

It is a classical result that the ML degree of any decomposable hierarchical model is equal to one.
In particular, the maximum likelihood estimator exists uniquely and can be expressed as a rational
function of the data vector
\displaystyle\mathbf{u}

(see, e.g.,
[
10
, Section 2.1.7]
).
Since the general
\displaystyle k
-th order multistate Markov model is decomposable,
it follows that its maximum likelihood estimator admits an explicit closed-form expression.

To state this formula, we introduce notation for marginal counts of the data vector.
For a subset
\displaystyle R\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}
and fixed states
\displaystyle(i_{r})_{r\in R}
, define the marginal count

u\big|_{\,i_{r}:\,r\in R}\;=\;\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}\\
j_{r}=i_{r}\ \text{for all }r\in R\end{subarray}}u_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}}.

Proposition 6.1

(
[
10
]
, Proposition 2.1.7)
.

Consider the general
\displaystyle k
-order multistate Markov model and let
\displaystyle u
be a vector of observed counts, and
\displaystyle M
denote the total number of counts.
If all denominators below are nonzero, then the MLE is given by

\hat{p}_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n}}=\frac{1}{M}\frac{\prod_{j=k+1}^{n}u|_{i_{j-k}\ldots i_{j}}}{\prod_{j=k+1}^{n-1}u|_{i_{j-k+1}\ldots i_{j}}},

if the vector
\displaystyle u
satisfies that none of the denominators vanish.

This expression coincides with the classical empirical transition–probability estimator,
written in joint-path coordinates.
Indeed, once the ML-estimators of the parameters are computed,
their product under the model parametrization yields exactly the normalized expressions
in Proposition
6.1
.

As an illustration, consider the case
\displaystyle k=1
.
The relevant marginal counts are

u\big|_{i_{\ell}}=\#\{\text{visits to state }i_{\ell}\},\qquad u\big|_{i_{\ell-1}i_{\ell}}=\#\{i_{\ell-1}\to i_{\ell}\text{ direct transitions}\}.

It follows that

\displaystyle\displaystyle\hat{p}_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\,\frac{\prod_{\ell=2}^{n}\#\{i_{\ell-1}\to i_{\ell}\text{ direct transitions}\}}{\prod_{\ell=2}^{n-1}\#\{\text{visits to state }i_{\ell}\}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{\#\{i_{1}\to i_{2}\text{ direct transitions}\}}{M}\prod_{\ell=3}^{n}\frac{\#\{i_{\ell-1}\to i_{\ell}\text{ direct transitions}\}}{\#\{\text{visits to state }i_{\ell-1}\}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{\#\{\text{visits to state }i_{1}\}}{M}\,\frac{\#\{i_{1}\to i_{2}\text{ direct transitions}\}}{\#\{\text{visits to state }i_{1}\}}\prod_{\ell=3}^{n}\frac{\#\{i_{\ell-1}\to i_{\ell}\text{ direct transitions}\}}{\#\{\text{visits to state }i_{\ell-1}\}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\hat{\pi}_{i_{1}}\prod_{\ell=2}^{n}\hat{a}^{(\ell)}_{i_{\ell-1}i_{\ell}}.

Forbidden transitions.

We now turn to models in which certain transitions are forbidden.
For such models, the conclusion of Proposition
6.1
continues to hold
for all nonvanishing path probabilities.
Indeed, if a transition is forbidden, then any path containing that transition has probability
zero under the model, and the corresponding empirical counts

\displaystyle u_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n}}
must also be zero.
Consequently, these paths do not contribute to the likelihood function.
From an algebraic perspective, the vanishing ideal of a model with forbidden transitions
is obtained from that of the general model by adding the linear equations

\displaystyle p_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n}}=0
for all forbidden paths.

The maximum likelihood estimation problem in this setting can therefore be formulated as
the maximization of the likelihood function over the general model, subject to additional
polynomial constraints.
A direct computation using Lagrange multipliers shows that the estimator given in
Proposition
6.1
satisfies the likelihood equations for all
coordinates corresponding to nonvanishing probabilities.
Thus, the same closed-form expression yields the maximum likelihood estimator on the
restricted model, after removing the zero-probability paths.

6.2
Homogeneous multistate Markov model

In the
\displaystyle k
-th order homogeneous Markov model, we assume that the same transition
probabilities apply at every
\displaystyle k
-step transition, independently of the time index.
As a consequence, the likelihood function has the same form as in
(
6.1
), except that the transition
probabilities no longer depend on
\displaystyle\ell
:

\displaystyle\displaystyle P(\mathbf{X}^{(1)},\dots,\mathbf{X}^{(M)})=\prod_{m=1}^{M}\biggl(\pi_{X_{1}^{(m)}\dots X_{k}^{(m)}}\prod_{\ell=k+1}^{L+1}a_{X_{\ell-k}^{(m)}\dots X_{\ell-1}^{(m)}X_{\ell}^{(m)}}\biggr).

(6.5)

The maximum likelihood estimator of the initial distribution remains the same as
in (
6.2
). In contrast, the estimator of the transition probabilities
in this case are obtained by summing over all time points
\displaystyle\ell
:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\hat{a}_{i_{\ell-k}\dots i_{\ell-1}i_{\ell}}=\frac{\sum\limits_{m=1}^{M}\sum\limits_{\ell=k+1}^{L+1}{\bf 1}\!\left(X_{\ell-k}^{(m)}=i_{\ell-k},\dots,X_{\ell-1}^{(m)}=i_{\ell-1},X_{\ell}^{(m)}=i_{\ell}\right)}{\sum\limits_{m=1}^{M}\sum\limits_{\ell=k+1}^{L+1}{\bf 1}\!\left(X_{\ell-k}^{(m)}=i_{\ell-k},\dots,X_{\ell-1}^{(m)}=i_{\ell-1}\right)}.

(6.6)

The variances of the maximum likelihood estimators, under both nonhomogeneous and homogeneous multistate Markov models, can be obtained from the Fisher information matrix in the usual way; see, for example,
[
21
, Chapter 7]
.

From the algebraic perspective, a natural first idea is to apply existing approaches for computing the MLE in log-linear models since
\displaystyle I(\mathcal{V})
is a toric ideal. However, in Example
3
we showed that
\displaystyle I(\mathcal{V})+\left\langle 1-\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}}p_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n}}\right\rangle
and
\displaystyle I(\mathcal{M})
can be different ideals. In Example
4
we demonstrate on an example that one indeed gets different answers using formulas (
6.2
) and (
6.6
) and an approach for computing MLE for log-linear models.

For general statistical models defined by polynomial equations and inequalities, one can find the MLE by constructing the
likelihood ideal
(
[
19
, Algorithm 7.2.4]
). The likelihood ideal is generated by polynomials that are obtained by the method of Lagrange multipliers for the constrained optimization problem of maximizing the likelihood function subject to the polynomial equations defining the model. In certain cases, MLE is the nonnegative real solution of the likelihood ideal that maximizes the likelihood function. However, there are also some caveats such as that the MLE can be a singular or boundary point of the model in which case it is usually not the solution of the likelihood ideal. Moreover, as we will discuss in Example
4
, there can be practical challenges working with the likelihood ideal. Although algebraic techniques have frequently played an important role in developing deeper insight into maximum likelihood estimation
[
16
]
, for homogeneous multistate Markov models a direct statistical approach using standard formulas provides a simpler solution, whereas the algebraic approach is unnecessarily complicated.

Example 4
.

We continue Example

for the illness-death

Let

By

(6.7)

whenever such a solution exists.

In our example, the ideal corresponding to equations (

complex solutions, counted with multiplicity. Five of the

Note that
\displaystyle\hat{p}\neq\hat{p}^{\prime}
.
This is explained by
\displaystyle\textstyle{I(\mathcal{V})+\left\langle 1-\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}}p_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n}}\right\rangle}
and
\displaystyle I(\mathcal{M})
being different ideals. The ideal
\displaystyle I(\mathcal{V})
is a toric ideal of dimension seven and degree
\displaystyle 31
generated by
\displaystyle 18

\displaystyle\textstyle{I(\mathcal{V})+\left\langle 1-\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}}p_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n}}\right\rangle}
is an ideal of dimension six and degree
\displaystyle 31
that is generated by
\displaystyle 19
polynomials. On the other hand, the vanishing ideal of the model
\displaystyle I(\mathcal{M})
is an ideal of dimension four and degree
\displaystyle 25
generated by
\displaystyle 31
polynomials.

The second algebraic approach is constructing the likelihood ideal for
\displaystyle I(\mathcal{M})
. A step towards this is constructing the augmented Jacobian of
\displaystyle I(\mathcal{M})
, a modification of the Jacobian matrix. In this particular example, the augmented Jacobian is a
\displaystyle 31\times 14
matrix and at a critical point
\displaystyle p

\displaystyle\hat{p}
,

\displaystyle\hat{p}
is a regular point of the interior of the model.

This example shows that
classical statistical methods provide a simpler and more direct route to the MLE in this case although the MLE is a regular point of the interior of the model.

the observed probability distribution; see, for example,

[

.

Let
\displaystyle\phi\colon\Theta\rightarrow\mathcal{M}_{\phi}

to a

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\phi}
, with
\displaystyle\mathrm{im}(\phi)=\mathcal{M}_{\phi}
.

\phi^{-1}(f(\theta))=\theta\quad\text{for all }\theta\in\Theta.

7
Examples and applications

For the purposes of a computational illustration, we use data which includes all of the English words that have ever been used in William Shakespeare’s works. We use this dataset to construct a general discrete-time multistate Markov model. The state space
\displaystyle S
includes 27 states: 26 states representing the 26 letters in the English alphabet, and one absorbing state that represents a space (hereafter denoted by
\displaystyle\mathcal{\Box}
).
Ultimately, our construction entails that each word becomes a specific path and gets absorbed into
\displaystyle\mathcal{\Box}
.

Words in the corpus evidently have varying lengths. In order to ensure that each trajectory is of the same length, and to thus use a common time horizon across all trajectories, we consider a maximum length
\displaystyle L
corresponding to the (second) longest word in the state space (note that the longest word
honorificabilitudinitatibus
from medieval Latin, with 27 characters, was disregarded for the sake of convenience). All of the words shorter than this were then padded with a space character (absorbing state) up until the end of the time horizon. Therefore, we may write the time horizon of the process as
\displaystyle\ell=1,2,\dots,L,L+1
. Including the
\displaystyle L+1
time point ensures that we can explicitly capture the final letter-to-space transition. To summarize, we represent words as Markovian paths, and a word of length
\displaystyle k<L
is modeled as a trajectory
\displaystyle X_{1},\dots,X_{L+1}
, whereby
\displaystyle X_{\ell}
is the letter at the
\displaystyle\ell
-th time point and
\displaystyle X_{t}=\mathcal{\Box}
for times
\displaystyle\ell\geq k+1
. We are also then interested in the number of times that each Markov trajectory, or word, appears in the dataset, and we therefore explicitly keep a count of the number of occurrences of each word since this is essential for the construction of a likelihood function. As way of illustration, in our dataset, we have
\displaystyle L=17
, so if we consider the word “and”, then
\displaystyle X_{1}=\text{a}
,
\displaystyle X_{2}=\text{n}
, and
\displaystyle X_{3}=\text{d}
;
\displaystyle X_{4}=X_{5}=\dots=X_{17}=X_{18}=\mathcal{\Box}
. We finally also remark that words consisting of just one letter, such as “s” (resulting from the use of an apostrophe to indicate possession or prefixes) have been discarded from the dataset.

Discussion and future directions

In this paper, we studied discrete-time multistate Markov models from the perspective of
algebraic statistics and made explicit the connection between the multistate modelling
framework and algebraic–geometric descriptions of statistical models.
To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work that systematically bridges these two
areas and establishes a precise correspondence between them.
While algebraic statistics is a comparatively recent field, multistate and event-history
models have a long and well-developed history in applied statistics.
Making this connection explicit enables a bidirectional transfer of ideas: algebraic methods
provide new structural insights into multistate models, while classical modelling questions
give rise to new algebraic problems.

One fundamental aspect of multistate and event-history models that we did not address in
detail is the treatment of incomplete data, in particular right censoring.
In many applications, subjects may be lost to follow-up or leave the study before its end, so
that their true state is unknown beyond the censoring time.
One possible approach is to enlarge the state space by introducing censoring as an additional
absorbing state.
This can be natural when censoring is informative, but in the case of independent censoring,
paths involving early censoring may not be of primary interest and may obscure the dynamics
on the original state space.
Understanding how censoring mechanisms can be incorporated into the algebraic framework, and
how they affect the resulting varieties and vanishing ideals, remains an open problem.

Question 1
.

How can right censoring and other forms of incomplete observation be incorporated into the
algebraic-statistical description of multistate Markov models, and how do they affect the
associated algebraic varieties and vanishing ideals?

Our results also clarify the relationship between algebraic and classical likelihood-based
approaches.
Proposition
6.1
, together with the computational
example in
Section
7
, shows that for nonhomogeneous first-order multistate Markov
models, algebraic and statistical approaches lead to identical maximum likelihood estimators.
An important direction for future work is to investigate whether this correspondence extends
beyond point estimation.

Question 2
.

How can uncertainty quantification for multistate Markov models, such as Fisher information
or asymptotic variance, be expressed and interpreted within the algebraic framework?

From an algebraic perspective, the most substantial open problems concern homogeneous
multistate Markov models.
While Proposition
5.1
and Remark
5.2
identify
families of binomial relations that necessarily vanish on homogeneous models, explicit
computations indicate that these relations do not generate the full vanishing ideal in
general.

Question 3
.

What is the vanishing ideal of a
\displaystyle k
-th order homogeneous multistate Markov model?

Another natural direction concerns the interaction between homogeneity and structural
constraints such as forbidden transitions or absorbing states.
While in the nonhomogeneous setting such restrictions correspond to coordinate slices of the
general model, their algebraic effect in the homogeneous case is less well understood.

Question 4
.

How do forbidden transitions interact with homogeneity at the level of vanishing ideals?

Finally, several connections to related areas merit further exploration.
Nonhomogeneous discrete-time multistate Markov models are discrete directed graphical models. We think that homogeneous discrete-time multistate Markov models might have an interpretation as colored discrete graphical models.
Mixtures of multistate Markov models are standard tools for modeling population heterogeneity. It would be interesting to study them from an algebraic perspective.
Connections to reversible Markov chains, models for higher order multistate Markov models, phylogenetic models, and matroid-theoretic structures
suggested by certain binomial relations point to further opportunities for interaction
between algebraic statistics and applied stochastic modelling.
