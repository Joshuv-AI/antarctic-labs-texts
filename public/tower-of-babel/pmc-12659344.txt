Clinical characteristics and healthcare burden of neurofibromatosis type 1 in Saudi Arabia: a single centre experience
Authors: Mohammed A. Almuqbil, Maha Al Ammari, Nada S. Al Dhayyan, Seena Thomas Kaithathara, Mohamed Al Balwi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNeurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1; 613113) is a hereditary neurocutaneous disorder that causes tumors in the nervous system, significantly impacting the quality of life (QoL). It is characterized by diverse clinical manifestations, including café-au-lait macules (CALMs), axillary or inguinal freckling, Lisch nodules, skeletal abnormalities, and various types of neurofibromas. Plexiform neurofibromas (PN), a common complication of NF1, are often inoperable and prone to recurrence. The study aimed to describe the clinical characteristics and healthcare burden of NF1, including those with PN and those receiving Selumetinib therapy, in Saudi Arabia.MethodsThis retrospective observational study was conducted at the National Guard Health Affairs King Abdulaziz Medical City in Saudi Arabia. Patient medical records were retrospectively reviewed from January 2016 to January 2024. We included all patients diagnosed with NF1 who fulfilled the National Institutes of Health (NIH) diagnostic criteria in 2021 or had a confirmed pathogenic NF1 variant on genetic testing.ResultsA total of 60 patients with NF1 were included; 55.2% of them were females. CALMs were the most common cardinal criteria, affecting 80% of the patients. Among NF1 patients, 12 had PN (20%). Only four patients received Selumetinib therapy. Genetic testing was performed in 39 patients, revealing pathogenic NF1 variants in 29 (74.4%). Pain medications were used by eight patients (13.3%). NF-1-related pain negatively impacted patients’ attention (24%), outdoor activities (24%), and social interactions with friends (20%). Among NF1 patients, 28 (46.7%) required hospitalization, twelve ER visits were conducted by seven (11.7%) NF1 patients, and outpatient services were utilized by nearly all NF1 patients (96.7%), with 1076 outpatient visits. The overall financial burden was high, with NF1 patients incurring $64.5 million, PN patients $13.5 million, and PN patients treated with Selumetinib $3.7 million.ConclusionThis study highlights the clinical and healthcare challenges of NF1 and PN in Saudi Arabia, emphasizing the need for a multidisciplinary approach that combines medical, psychological, and financial support. The limited access to Selumetinib represents a gap. Increasing treatment accessibility and financial support are key to improving the outcomes and QoL.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13053-025-00325-8.

Introduction

Neurofibromatosis (NF) is a group of genetic neurocutaneous disorders involving the development of multiple tumors that primarily affect the nervous system and the skin. These tumors are mostly benign but sometimes become malignant. NF comprises three main subtypes: neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1; 613113), neurofibromatosis type 2 (NF2; 101000), and schwannomatosis, with NF1 being the most prevalent subtype worldwide [1].

NF1, known as von Recklinghausen’s disease, is an autosomal dominant genetic disorder that occurs in approximately 1 in 2,500 to 1 in 3,000 individuals [2, 3]. It is caused by a loss-of-function mutation in the NF1 gene, located on chromosome 17q11.2. This leads to reduced production of neurofibromin, a tumor suppressor protein that normally inhibits RAS signaling to regulate cell growth [4, 5]. Without neurofibromin, the RAS/MAPK pathway becomes overactive, particularly MEK, resulting in uncontrolled cell growth and proliferation [6, 7].

The characteristic clinical signs of NF1 typically emerge in early childhood, with 50% of cases exhibiting these signs during their first year of life, and 97% showing them by the age of 8 years [8]. Moreover, the manifestations of NF1 are multisystemic. Common features include café-au-lait macules (CALMs), which are the most common sign of NF1, and axillary or inguinal freckling. Ocular findings include Lisch nodules and optic gliomas, while skeletal abnormalities include scoliosis and tibial dysplasia. Additionally, neurofibromas, the hallmark of NF1, may appear in cutaneous, subcutaneous, or plexiform forms [9, 10].

Plexiform neurofibromas (PN) are a serious type of sizable, mostly benign tumor that are frequent in NF1 patients. They can develop deep along nerves, spreading in any part of the body, making them hard to remove by usual procedures like surgery [11, 12]. Benign PN may lead to pain, visual impairment, disfigurement, neurological deficits, and impaired quality of life (QoL) [13]. Furthermore, PN can transform into malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumors (MPNSTs) in 8–13% of cases [14].

Recent treatment advances, including the introduction of MEK inhibitors like Selumetinib, have provided new treatment options for inoperable symptomatic PN. However, real-world data, especially in Saudi Arabia, on the outcomes of such medications remain underexplored.

Research on NF1 in Saudi Arabia is limited, with most available literature focusing on pediatrics. Therefore, this study aims to understand NF1 within the Saudi Arabian context by comprehensively describing the clinical characteristics and healthcare burden. It focuses on patients with NF1, including those with PN and those receiving Selumetinib therapy.

Methods

Study design and settings

This is a single-center, observational, retrospective, non-comparative study conducted at the National Guard Health Affairs King Abdulaziz Medical City in Saudi Arabia. Patient medical records were retrospectively reviewed from January 2016 to January 2024. The study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [15].

Participants

The inclusion criteria comprised Saudi children or adults aged three months or older, diagnosed with NF1 either by meeting the National Institutes of Health (NIH) diagnostic criteria in 2021 [16], or by carrying a confirmed pathogenic NF1 variant on genetic testing. The study covered the period from January 2016 to January 2024. Patients who did not meet these inclusion criteria were excluded. All eligible individuals were included due to the limited number of patients, utilizing a non-probability consecutive sampling technique.

For descriptive purposes, patients were classified into three subgroups: [1] NF1 patients overall [2], NF1 patients with plexiform neurofibromas (PN), and [3] PN patients treated with Selumetinib.

Variables

The primary outcome measures included age-specific demographic, clinical, medical, and surgical characteristics, as well as the frequency of NF1-associated tumors such as PN, MPNST, and optical pathway glioma (OPG). Additional outcomes included patient-related health outcomes, treatment patterns, and outcomes of Selumetinib use among Saudi NF1 patients.

Secondary outcome measures focused on healthcare resource utilization, NF1-related costs, and the financial burden on patients and their caregivers.

Data sources/ measurement

The data of all included patients were extracted from the MNGHA medical record system (BestCare 2.0 A). The collected data included demographics and patient characteristics, such as the length of hospital stay, the number of emergency room (ER) or clinic visits per year, and the age at which NF1 was diagnosed. We also collected the dates of the first PN diagnosis and the first symptomatic, inoperable PN diagnosis. Additionally, the total number of PNs per patient, the number of diagnostic criteria met, and genetic analysis findings were recorded.

Clinical manifestations of NF1, including CALMs, ocular manifestations, OPG, PNs, and MPNST, were also documented. Furthermore, information on treatment patterns and outcomes, such as surgeries, adjunct treatments, and Selumetinib therapy, was collected.

Moreover, face-to-face interviews were conducted with the included patients to assess patient-reported outcomes. These included pain intensity and QoL, measured using validated questionnaires such as the self-report Numeric Rating Scale-11 (NRS-11) for target tumor pain intensity (for children aged ≥ 8 years) [17], and Pediatric Quality of Life Inventory (PedsQL) [18].

Indirect costs were estimated from productivity loss due to ER visits, hospitalizations, and outpatient care. The value of a lost working day was calculated by dividing the national GDP per capita by the average number of working days per year, resulting in $49,567 per day, which was multiplied by the number of days lost to derive indirect costs.

Ethical considerations

The research was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at King Abdullah International Medical Research Center (KAIMRC), with the identifying number IRB/0092/24. Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to the face-to-face interviews.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using both descriptive and inferential statistics. All categorical variables were presented as frequencies (percentages), while continuous, normally distributed variables were presented by (mean ± SD). The analyses were conducted using R software version 4.3.1.

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics of the study population

Demographics and clinical profile

A total of 60 patients with NF1 were included in the study, 55.2% of them were females. The majority of patients (60%) were aged less than 18 years, while 41.4% were over 18 years old. Half of the participants were classified as underweight (Body mass index (BMI) < 18.5 kg/m²), and 33.9% had a normal weight (BMI between 18.5 and 24.9 kg/m²) (Table 1).

Plexiform neurofibromas (PN) and tumor burden

Among NF1 patients, 12 had PN (20%), with six having a single PN, and the disease was operable among 71.7% of patients. Regarding the diagnosis of PN, the highest proportion of PN diagnoses occurred before the age of 18 years (Table 1).

Clinical manifestations and diagnostic criteria

CALMs are the most common cardinal criteria, affecting 80% of the study population. Iris Lisch nodules follow at 33.3% and OPG at 11.7%. In contrast, MPNSTs were rare and were identified in only two patients (Table 1). Group-level differences in clinical manifestations are summarized visually in Fig. 1.

We found that 21 patients (35%) met 2 of the NIH criteria. Additionally, 14 patients (23.3%) met 1 criterion, 11 patients (18.3%) met three criteria, and 10 patients (17%) met four criteria. Only three patients (5%) of the study population met all five criteria (Table 1).

Genetic testing

Genetic testing was also performed on 39 patients (65%), revealing that 29 patients had a pathogenic mutation, while three displayed variants of uncertain significance (VUS) (Table 1).

Selumetinib and adjunct medications treatment pattern

Selumetinib characteristics

Selumetinib was prescribed for four patients (6.7%) with NF1 who were diagnosed with PN. The prescribed dose was 30 mg twice daily for 50% of patients, 25 mg twice daily for 25% of patients, or an initial 40 mg once daily that was increased to 40 mg twice daily after a month. Selumetinib was almost safe, with no adverse events among 75% of NF patients receiving Selumetinib, and only one patient experienced skin manifestations such as acneiform eruptions with itching and erythematous papules on the face, chest, and back (Table 2).

Adjunct treatments in NF1 patients

Among the overall NF1 patients, pain medications were utilized among 8 (13.3%) patients, 4 (6.7%) used chemotherapy, and 22 (37%) received other adjunct medications (Table 2).

Patient-reported health-related outcomes

The mean total NRS pain score was (5.8 ± 7.8) among all NF1 populations. In the subgroup of patients with PN, the mean total NRS pain score was (7.8 ± 7.2). Additionally, Selumetinib improved the total NRS pain score in PN patients who received it, showing a mean score of (6.7 ± 3).

NF-1-related pain negatively impacted patients’ attention (24%), outdoor activities (24%), social interactions with friends (20%), physical activities (20%), sleep quality (24%), and mood (40%). Among PN patients who received Selumetinib, pain negatively affected the mood and sleep quality in two patients (66.7% for each domain), and social interaction with friends in one patient (33.3%).

The mean total PedsQL among the overall NF1 patients was (13.9 ± 15). Additionally, it was (15.6 ± 10.6) among those with PN and (14 ± 5.5) among PN patients who received Selumetinib (Table 3). These findings are further illustrated in Fig. 2, which shows PedsQL domain scores across groups.

Financial burden of NF1

Between 2016 and 2024, a total of nine NF1 patients had surgeries, accounting for 25 procedures. The total cost of these surgeries was $130,013.82. Among patients who developed PN, a total of 15 surgeries costing $69,502.07 were done between 2016 and 2024 in six patients. The highest individual cost recorded was $10,373.44 for a benign soft tissue tumor excisional biopsy in the hip and pelvis (Table 5). Discussion

Our retrospective study aimed to describe real-world demographic and clinical characteristics among children and adults with NF1 and PN in Saudi Arabia. Our findings emphasize that NF1 is associated with a considerable tumor burden, especially PN. It is also associated with diverse clinical manifestations, including café-au-lait macules, Lisch nodules, and optic pathway gliomas. While genetic testing showed pathogenic variants in most tested individuals, only a minority of patients received Selumetinib, a MEK inhibitor approved for inoperable symptomatic PN. Additionally, our results highlight the difficulties experienced by NF1 patients, including elevated pain levels, diminished QoL, and considerable socioeconomic consequences originating from direct frequent healthcare utilization and increased expenses.

Previous studies have demonstrated an increased risk of cancer among NF1 patients, up to five-fold compared to the general population [19–21]. Uusitalo et al. observed that the incidence of brain tumors was higher among pediatric patients, while MPNST was significantly more prevalent among adolescents and young adults [21]. Among our cohort, 20% developed PN, 11.7% developed OPG, and 3.3% developed MPNST, all lower than those reported in the literature. In a larger retrospective study in Saudi Arabia, OPG was detected in 36.4% of patients with NF1, while PN was observed in 23.4% [22]. Bashiri and his colleagues have observed a higher incidence of OPG (56.6%) and PN (24%) among Saudi children [23]. Surprisingly, MPNST was not observed in the patients included in these two studies. This discrepancy may be attributed to the small sample size and the heterogeneity of the study populations.

Aligned with the literature, we found that CALMs are the most common cardinal criteria, affecting 80% of the study population, followed by Iris Lisch nodules at 33.3%. Both are considered significant indicators of the disease [22–24].

In our cohort, 25 surgical excisions were performed for NF1-associated PNs and tumors. Until now, treatment options for NF1-associated PNs and tumors have primarily been limited to surgical removal, as traditional chemotherapy and radiotherapy have shown limited effectiveness [25–27]. Nevertheless, in numerous instances, surgical removal is not an option due to the tumor’s size or location, and it poses a significant risk of postoperative complications [28, 29]. The limited treatment options for NF1-associated PNs have led to substantial unmet medical needs. Recent advances in targeted therapies, such as the MEK inhibitor Selumetinib, have begun to address these gaps. Selumetinib gained FDA approval in 2020 for treating pediatric NF1 patients aged ≥ 2 years with symptomatic, inoperable PN [30]. Previous clinical trials on Selumetinib have indicated that it is associated with a significant reduction in tumor volume and pain, as well as improvements in function and QoL [31, 32].

In our study, Selumetinib was prescribed to four patients with PN. We observed low pain scores among those patients, with almost no adverse events. This suggests that Selumetinib could be an effective option for managing symptoms. However, further research is required to properly understand its long-term advantages, effects on the overall QoL, and cost-effectiveness in larger patient populations.

A recent study with 583 NF1 patients supports the importance of early management, and reducing the number of cutaneous neurofibromas, particularly on the face, may lead to enhanced QoL [33]. Furthermore, neuropsychological assessment plays a crucial role in caring for NF1 patients, particularly children. Regular evaluations, especially concerning visual-spatial skills and attention deficits, are essential for tailoring appropriate support services and promoting cognitive development [34].

Pain medications were utilized by 13.3% of the included NF1 patients. For instance, in a cross-sectional study of pediatric and adolescent populations with NF1, pain interference was closely associated with anxiety, depressive symptoms, and poor QoL. Interestingly, even though 33% of children receive pain medication, both caregiver (73%) and self-reported (59%) assessments reveal that pain substantially disrupts daily activities [35]. These findings highlight the complex relationship between physical and psychological health in NF1 patients. They emphasize the importance of integrated psychosocial interventions in conjunction with medical treatment.

The diagnosis of NF1 is usually clinical, though genetic testing may be useful in confirming the diagnosis in those not meeting the clinical criteria. Also, genetic testing is invaluable in differentiating NF1 from Legius syndrome and spotting atypical cases in children [36]. Genetic analysis of our population revealed mutations in 32 NF1 patients; of these, 29 were pathogenic, and 3 were VUS.

A previous study conducted by Alfurayh et al. found that 82% of pediatric patients with NF1 in Saudi Arabia had mutations in the NF1 gene. Among these mutations, 36.6% were classified as nonsense mutations, 24.4% as missense mutations, 14.6% as splicing site mutations, 12.2% as frameshift mutations, 8.5% as microdeletions, and 3.75% as whole gene deletions. Interestingly, the authors observed no clear correlation between the genotype and phenotype of NF1 [24]. Nevertheless, despite offering significant diagnostic information, genetic testing cannot foresee disease symptoms or future complications.

The financial challenges associated with NF1 present a pressing concern that demands an urgent focus. Patients and their caregivers often face considerable financial difficulties stemming from elevated medical expenses and work disruptions, amplifying the overall impact of the condition. Aligned with previous studies, our findings confirm that NF1-related care, especially for patients undergoing surgery, entails substantial expenses due to extensive healthcare utilization through frequent ER visits, hospitalization, and outpatient care.

Previous studies have shown that NF1 patients frequently require hospitalization, prolonged inpatient stays, and specialized medical services, all driving up healthcare expenditures [37]. Yang et al. (2021) estimated that lifetime medical costs for NF1 patients are substantially higher than those for the general population, with surgical interventions and tumor management accounting for a significant share of these expenses [38].

This research has several limitations. Its retrospective, descriptive design hinders the ability to establish causal relationships and may lead to biases stemming from incomplete or missing data. Furthermore, because the study was conducted in a single center in Saudi Arabia, the findings may not be entirely applicable to different healthcare environments or populations. Unmeasured confounding variables, such as disease severity and variations in treatment adherence, may also have affected the results. Another limitation of this study is the heterogeneity of the study population, which included both children and adults, patients with and without PN, and a very small subgroup treated with Selumetinib. Although we attempted to classify patients into relevant subgroups, this heterogeneity may have introduced variability and limits the generalizability of our findings.

Future research should focus on key areas and insights gained from this study. Longitudinal studies with larger cohorts are essential to better understand the long-term clinical progression of NF1 and PN in the Saudi population, as well as the sustained efficacy and safety of Selumetinib. Further investigation into barriers to Selumetinib access, such as cost and limited awareness among healthcare providers, is crucial to improve treatment access. Moreover, evaluating the cost-effectiveness of different treatment options and developing culturally tailored psychosocial and financial support programs for patients and caregivers would be beneficial. Finally, qualitative research can offer valuable perspectives on the experiences, daily challenges, and unmet needs of individuals affected by NF1 in this region.

Conclusions

This retrospective study provides valuable real-world insights into the clinical and demographic characteristics of NF1 and PN from a single tertiary center in Saudi Arabia. While our findings highlight important challenges, larger multicenter studies are needed to better represent the national disease landscape. Our findings underscore the significance of a patient-centered, multidisciplinary approach to managing NF1. Despite advances such as the MEK inhibitor Selumetinib, treatment uptake remains limited, highlighting a critical gap in therapeutic access. Addressing the clinical, financial, and psychosocial challenges associated with this condition can result in improved outcomes and a better QoL for NF1 patients. Strengthening financial aid policies and expanding access to advanced treatments will be crucial for reducing the overall burden of this complex disorder. Our findings regarding Selumetinib are preliminary and should be considered exploratory. Further multicenter and longitudinal studies are needed to improve the understanding of the clinical spectrum and long-term economic impact of NF1 and PN in Saudi Arabia.
