Dual versus Single Kidney Transplantation in Contemporary U.S. Practice: A National Matched Cohort Study
Authors: Abdulghani Abou Koura, Alex Judd, Stephanie Silpe, Juan F. Guerra, Danielle Ladie, Kenneth Brayman, Shawn J. Pelletier, Yazan Al‐Adwan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundHard‐to‐place kidneys remain underutilized despite ongoing organ shortage. Dual kidney transplantation (DKT) may improve use of these grafts, but contemporary national data are limited.MethodsWe performed a retrospective cohort study using national databases from March 2015 to December 2024. Outcomes after DKT and single kidney transplantation (SKT) were compared in the overall cohort and after 2:1 propensity score matching (PSM) using Kaplan‐Meier, Cox, and logistic regression.ResultsA total of 140,707 deceased donor kidney transplants were analyzed, including 1,236 (0.9%) DKT. In the overall cohort, DKT was associated with lower graft and patient survival. After 2:1 PSM, 1,105 DKT recipients were matched to 2,210 SKT recipients with balanced recipient age (63.7 vs 63.9 years) and KDPI (75.5 vs 75.8; SMD <0.03). After PSM, DKT was associated with higher 1‐, 3‐, and 5‐year graft survival (92.2%, 81.8%, and 69.8% vs 87.7%, 75.5%, and 59.9%; all P < 0.001), while DGF rates were similar.ConclusionDKT remains an uncommon but important strategy for utilization of hard‐to‐place donor kidneys. After matching recipients with comparable age and KDPI, DKT was associated with favorable graft and patient survival and similar DGF rates, supporting selective use in carefully chosen recipients.

Introduction

For patients with end‐stage renal disease (ESRD), kidney transplantation (KT) is associated with longer survival, improved quality of life, and greater long‐term cost‐effectiveness than dialysis [1, 2]. However, the demand for KT continues to exceed organ availability. As of October 2025, the Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network (OPTN) waiting list included 93,297 candidates for KT, while only 27,760 transplants were performed in 2024 in the United States [3]. In parallel, underutilization of hard‐to‐place deceased donor kidneys remain a major challenge. Although 14% of recovered kidneys have a Kidney Donor Profile Index (KDPI) of ≥85%, nearly half of these kidneys are ultimately not transplanted [4]. Concerns regarding inferior graft outcomes, delayed graft function (DGF), and longer hospital length of stay contribute to this low utilization [5, 6].

Single kidney transplantation (SKT) remains the standard approach for most deceased donor kidneys. It allows one donor to benefit two recipients and is generally associated with shorter operative times and less procedural complexity [4, 7, 8, 9]. However, hard‐to‐place kidneys from older donors, with high KDPI, or with adverse biopsy findings may be less suitable for SKT because of concerns about inadequate nephron mass and poorer function. Dual kidney transplantation (DKT) is one strategy to improve utilization of such kidneys by implanting both kidneys into a single recipient to augment nephron mass [10, 11]. This approach may reduce non‐utilization of hard‐to‐place kidneys, but its application remains inconsistent. Determining whether kidneys should be used as SKT or DKT is challenging, and there are no universally accepted clinical or histologic criteria to guide this decision [12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19].

Prior studies comparing DKT and SKT have reported mixed findings. Some suggest that DKT may be associated with acceptable graft function and favorable outcomes when hard‐to‐place kidneys are used, whereas others have reported greater perioperative burden and inconsistent long‐term survival [4, 7, 20, 21, 22]. However, interpretation of these studies has been limited by relatively small sample sizes [20]. Given the continued underutilization of hard‐to‐place kidneys and the absence of clear national contemporary data, further evaluation of current outcomes is warranted. Therefore, we performed a national registry‐based study comparing outcomes after DKT and SKT in the overall cohort and in a propensity score‐matched cohort. Our aim was to describe contemporary graft and patient survival associated with DKT relative to SKT among selected deceased donor KT recipients.

Methods

Study Design

We conducted a retrospective cohort study using data from the OPTN and Scientific Registry of Transplant Recipients (SRTR) database. All adult KT performed in the United States between March 2015 to December 2024, were screened for inclusion. We excluded pediatric recipients, previous KT recipients, living donor recipients, and donors weighing <20 kg. Due to the retrospective nature of the study and abstraction of de‐identified data from the national registry, the present study was deemed exempt by the Institutional Review Board. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Data Collection and Variable Definition

Recipient variables included age at transplantation, sex, body mass index (BMI), height, weight, ethnicity, waitlist time, distance from donor hospital to transplant center, dialysis status at transplantation, and serum creatinine at transplantation. Primary diagnosis was grouped as diabetes mellitus type II, hypertensive nephrosclerosis, polycystic kidney disease, or other. Transplant era was categorized as 2015‐2017, 2018‐2020, and 2021‐2024. Donor variables included age, sex, height, weight, BMI, ethnicity, Kidney Donor Profile Index (KDPI), cold ischemia time (CIT), cause of death, history of hypertension or diabetes, donation after circulatory death (DCD) status, terminal serum creatinine, and ex vivo machine perfusion use.

Postoperative outcomes included delayed graft function (DGF), hospital length of stay, resumption of maintenance dialysis after transplantation, serum creatinine at discharge, 6 months, and 1 year, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) at 6 months and 1 year, retransplantation, and graft and patient survival. DGF was defined as the need for dialysis within the first week after transplantation. eGFR was calculated using the 2021 CKD‐EPI creatinine equation incorporating serum creatinine, recipient sex, and age at the corresponding follow‐up timepoint. Graft and patient survival were evaluated at 1, 3, and 5 years after transplantation.

Propensity Score Matching (PSM)

Because allocation to DKT versus SKT is inherently nonrandom, PSM was performed to reduce measured baseline differences between groups. Propensity scores were estimated using logistic regression with recipient age, KDPI, primary diagnosis, and donor interstitial fibrosis severity category covariates. These variables were selected based on clinical relevance and their potential influence on allocation of hard‐to‐place kidneys to dual versus single implantation. A 2:1 nearest‐neighbor matching algorithm without replacement was applied, matching two SKT recipients to each DKT recipient. Covariate balance before and after matching was assessed using absolute standardized mean differences (SMD), with values <0.10 considered acceptable.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes were graft and patient survival. Secondary outcome was DGF. Outcomes were assessed in both the unmatched and propensity score‐matched cohorts.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range (IQR), as appropriate, and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Continuous variables were compared using t‐test or Mann‐Whitney U test, and categorical variables using the chi‐square test or Fisher exact test, as appropriate. Graft and patient survival were estimated using the Kaplan‐Meier method and compared with the log‐rank test. To evaluate the association between transplant type and graft failure, Cox proportional hazards regression models were performed in both the unmatched and matched cohorts and reported as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals test. For DGF, logistic regression models were similarly performed in the unmatched and matched cohorts and reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs. A two‐sided p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using SPSS version 31.0.0.0 and RStudio version 2025.05.1+513.

Results

Study Cohort

From March 2015 to December 2024, a total of 140,707 kidney transplants were analyzed, including 1,236 (0.9%) DKT. Following 2:1 PSM, 1,105 DKT recipients were compared with 2,210 matched SKT recipients. The median follow‐up was 2.2 years for DKT and 2.1 years for SKT.

Recipient and Donor Baseline Characteristics Before Matching

Baseline characteristics of recipients and donors are summarized in Tables 1 and 2. Compared with SKT, DKT recipients were older (62.9 ± 9.6 vs 54.5 ± 13.1 years) and more often diagnosed with diabetes (41.7% vs 30.6%), both p < 0.001. They also had shorter wait‐list times (262 vs 447 days; p < 0.001) and greater transplant distances (142 vs 90 nautical miles; p < 0.001). DKT donors were older (54.8 ± 15.6 vs 40.5 ± 14.2 years) and more frequently had hypertension (59.8% vs 30.0%), diabetes (22.7% vs 8.6%), and high KDPI ≥ 85 (37.5% vs 5.6%) (all p < 0.001). CIT was longer for DKT than SKT (23.2 ± 9.0 vs 18.8 ± 8.2 h; p < 0.001). Cerebrovascular death was more common among DKT donors (40.5% vs 22.5%; p < 0.001), and machine perfusion was used more (40.4% vs 33.0%; p < 0.001).

Post‐Matching Cohort

After PSM, baseline characteristics were well balanced across all variables (Table 4). The mean recipient age was 63.7 ± 8.6 years in DKT and 63.9 ± 8.3 years in SKT, while the mean KDPI was 75.5 ± 19.1 vs 75.8 ± 18.6, respectively (SMD < 0.03 for both). Distributions of primary ESRD‐etiology (SMD < 0.07) and allograft biopsy interstitial fibrosis (SMD = 0.04) were also well balanced. CIT remained longer in DKT recipients than SKT recipients (23.45 ± 8.81 vs 20.78 ± 7.87 h; SMD = 0.319).

Postoperative results for the matched cohort are presented in Table 5. Rates of DGF were similar between groups. Sensitivity Analysis Supports Favorable Graft Outcomes After DKT

Sensitivity analyses are shown in Table 6. In the unmatched cohort, DKT was associated with a higher hazard of graft failure on unadjusted analysis, but this association was attenuated after adjustment for age and reversed after adjustment for KDPI, with DKT associated with a lower hazard of graft failure in the fully adjusted model (HR 0.808, 95% CI 0.715–0.912; p < 0.001). Adjustment for CIT alone did not eliminate the crude disadvantage (HR 1.186, 95% CI 1.052–1.338; p = 0.005). In the fully adjusted model including age, KDPI, and CIT, DKT was associated with a lower hazard of graft failure (HR 0.798, 95% CI 0.706–0.900; p < 0.001). In the matched cohort, DKT remained associated with a lower hazard of graft failure across all models, including the fully adjusted model (HR 0.731, 95% CI 0.630–0.849; p < 0.001). For DGF, DKT was associated with higher odds in the unmatched cohort on unadjusted analysis and after adjustment for age or CIT alone; however, the association was no longer significant after adjustment for KDPI, with or without age. After adjustment for age, KDPI, and CIT, DKT was associated with lower odds of DGF (OR 0.843, 95% CI 0.748–0.948; p = 0.005). In the matched cohort, DKT was not associated with DGF in any model, including adjustment for age, KDPI, and CIT (OR 0.929, 95% CI 0.799–1.080; p = 0.341).

Discussion

In this national registry analysis of adult deceased donor KT, DKT accounted for less than 1% of all KTs performed in the United States. In crude analysis, DKT recipients had lower graft and patient survival and a higher rate of DGF, but these findings must be interpreted in the context of different donor and recipient profiles between groups. After PSM, DKT was associated with better graft and patient survival, while DGF rates were comparable between groups. Sensitivity analysis further showed that the apparent crude disadvantage of DKT was attenuated after adjustment, supporting the consistency of the matched outcomes. Taken together, these findings suggest that, among selected recipients of higher‐risk kidneys, DKT can provide acceptable and potentially favorable post‐transplant outcomes, while also highlighting the importance of careful recipient and graft selection.

An important message from this study is that statistical significance should be interpreted alongside the magnitude and clinical context of observed differences. In the unmatched cohort, DGF was more common in DKT recipients than SKT recipients, but this difference occurred in the setting of older donors, and higher KDPI in the DKT group. Likewise, graft survival differences in the unmatched cohort were statistically significant, yet the absolute separation in survival was modest, suggesting limited clinical meaning when considered apart from the marked baseline imbalance between groups. By contrast, in the matched cohort, DGF rates were comparable and the separation in graft survival was more pronounced over time, supporting the possibility that among recipients of kidneys with more comparable baseline risk, DKT may confer a more meaningful medium‐term advantage. This distinction between statistical and clinical interpretation is especially relevant in observational comparisons such as this one.

The clinical role of DKT remains nuanced. Implanting 2 kidneys into a single recipient may improve nephron mass when donor kidneys are considered hard‐to‐place, but it also reduces the number of recipients who may benefit from transplantation. Our findings suggest that DKT may be most relevant when graft quality is reduced that single implantation raises concern for poorer long‐term performance, yet the kidneys remain potentially transplantable if used together. In such cases, decision‐making should integrate donor characteristics, including KDPI and biopsy findings, with recipient age, comorbidity burden, and the consequences of remaining on dialysis. For selected recipients with significant comorbidity, accepting kidneys with high KDPI as a dual graft may represent a reasonable strategy to achieve transplantation and avoid the morbidity associated with prolonged dialysis, while still maintaining acceptable medium‐term outcomes. At the same time, this strategy must be balanced against the competing priority of single implantation when that approach is likely to provide adequate graft function and allow transplantation of an additional candidate. Although several clinical algorithms have been proposed to guide the allocation of kidneys to single or dual implantation based on donor renal function, histologic findings, and comorbidities, there remains no uniform consensus [20].

The feasibility of DKT is also shaped by allocation logistics. Contemporary policy prioritizes sequential, single‐kidney placement down the match run, whereas DKT requires early identification of a suitable recipient and coordinated placement of both kidneys from one donor [23, 24]. Following the 2014 Kidney Allocation System (KAS), DKT activity fell as broader sharing prolonged CIT and hard‐to‐place kidneys ‘timed out’ before reaching dual allocation, contributing to rising non‐utilization; the 2019 Dual Kidney Allocation policy sought to counter this by prioritizing local dual allocation for high‐KDPI offers, though donor‐age and KDPI thresholds for primary dual allocation were not adopted [24]. The subsequent shift to 250‐nautical‐mile allocation further increased CIT and nonuse [25]. Therefore, allocation timing and center‐level logistics should also be considered when evaluating the role of DKT in improving utilization.

This study has several limitations inherent to its observational, retrospective design using a national registry. Although the OPTN/SRTR database provides comprehensive multicenter coverage of KT in the United States, it is subject to data entry errors, incomplete reporting, and residual confounding. Importantly, DKT is selected at both the donor and transplant‐center level; decisions regarding dual versus single implantation may reflect center‐specific practices, surgeon judgement, organ acceptance thresholds, and recipient selection factors that are not fully recorded in the registry. Interpretation of procurement biopsies is also limited by center‐level variability, lack of standardized reporting, and reliance on frozen‐section analysis, which may introduce sampling error and artifact. Additionally, detailed biologic and perfusion‐related parameters, such as glomerular filtration rate, inflammatory markers, metabolic recovery metrics, or real‐time functional assessments, are not captured in the registry and could not be incorporated into our models. Despite these limitations, the large sample size, national scope, and use of both matched and adjusted sensitivity analyses provide a robust contemporary assessment of DKT outcomes across U.S. transplant practice.

In conclusion, DKT remained uncommon but was associated with favorable outcomes among matched recipients of hard‐to‐place deceased donor kidneys. While crude analyses reflected the higher‐risk donor and recipient characteristics of the DKT group, matched analyses demonstrated improved graft and patient survival with similar DGF rates. Together, these findings support DKT as an important option in carefully selected clinical settings and highlight the need to integrate graft quality and recipient factors when considering dual versus single implantation.
