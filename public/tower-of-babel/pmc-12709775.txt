Parameter efficient fine-tunning of foundation model to facilitate tumor response prediction for ovarian cancer patients
Authors: Ke Zhang, Patrik Gilley, Neman Abdoli, Theresa C. Thai, Yong Chen, Lauren Dockery, Kathleen Moore, Robert S. Mannel, Qinggong Tang, Yuchen Qiu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn clinical practice, it is critically important to predict the tumor response of chemotherapy at an early stage. However, the performance of existing clinical markers cannot achieve satisfactory accuracy and reliability. To overcome this limitation, in this study we proposed a foundation model based approach to improve prediction performance.MethodsWe adopted a CLIP (Contrastive language-image pretraining) based foundation model, which contains a total of 12 transformer layers for both image and textual encoders. This model has been extensively pretrained on a non-medical image-text dataset. Meanwhile, a parameter efficient low rank adaptor (LoRA) was incorporated into the transformer layers for fine-tuning purpose. The adaptors were inserted at various layers of both encoders, and their corresponding performances were evaluated and compared. The experiments were conducted on a retrospective dataset containing a total of 182 advanced stage ovarian cancer cases, among which 124 were responders and 58 were non-responders.ResultsThe best performance was achieved by adding LoRA adaptors on the lowest 5 transformer layers for both image and textual encoders, which yields an AUC (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve) of 0.785 ± 0.039 and an ACC (accuracy) of 0.780 ± 0.032. As a comparison, the conventional transfer learning strategy fine-tuned ResNet and ViT models achieved AUCs of 0.754 ± 0.089 and 0.707 ± 0.079, respectively.ConclusionThis study initially demonstrates the feasibility of parameter efficient training with a foundation model for chemotherapy response prediction, highlighting its potential to support clinical decision making.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12880-025-02033-0.

Introduction

Ovarian carcinoma is a highly aggressive malignancy in gynecologic oncology, with a five-year relative survival rate of 51% [1]. Due to the disease complexity, in current clinical practice it is difficult to identify ovarian carcinoma at an early stage, and most patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage with metastatic tumors in various organs [2, 3]. To control tumor metastasis, chemotherapy is necessary after primary debulking surgery (PDS) which surgically removes all visible lesions [4–6]. Given the tumor heterogeneity, patients’ responses to the chemotherapy vary significantly across subgroups [4]. To improve outcomes and minimize the side effects of ineffective treatments, it is crucial to accurately predict the response of residual cancer to chemotherapy following PDS, thereby enabling personalized treatment for each individual.

Currently, three types of clinical information are being investigated for response prediction: antigen biomarkers, histopathology images, and radiographic images. For antigen biomarkers in particular, CA125 is the most popular marker for tumor response prediction; nevertheless, its measured concentration levels are significantly affected by various epidemic and clinical factors [7], which limits its prediction accuracy and clinical applications [8]. Tumor histopathology evaluation, although routinely used in clinical practice, has notable limitations. While recent studies suggested that tumor histology type and grade are valuable prognostic markers [9, 10], the association between tumor grades and therapy responses remain inconsistent, with both positive and negative results were reported [11]. In addition, currently used grading systems produce low inter-pathologist agreement [12], further reducing reproducibility and reliability of these assessments. Next, radiographic imaging examination is another important approach for treatment assessment of ovarian cancer patients [13, 14], which can be achieved by a variety of modalities including: computed tomography (CT), ultrasound (US), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and positron emission tomography / CT (PET/CT). Among these modalities, CT has several advantages including wide availability, high diagnostic performance, quick image acquisition, and low operating cost [15]. However, current assessments are majorly based on changes in tumor size before and after chemotherapy, which cannot predict tumor response prior to treatment. The existing diagnostic guideline (Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors [16]) is heavily dependent on radiologists’ visual assessment of tumor diameter [16, 17], which may lead to high inter-radiologist variability and low correlation with clinical outcomes [18, 19].

Meanwhile, deep learning (DL) technology [20–22] has emerged as a powerful tool for extracting comprehensive information from complex data and identifying the most discriminative features for a specific task. Despite its effectiveness and wide application in medical imaging field [23–33], very few deep learning related studies have been focused on tumor response prediction, likely due to the limited size of available clinical datasets. Foundation models such as CLIP (Contrastive language-image pretraining) [34], BERT (Bidirectional encoder representations from Transformers) [35], and Llama (Large language model Meta AI) [36] are pretrained on massive datasets and can be adapted to diverse downstream tasks, offering an opportunity to mitigate this limitation. However, directly applying conventional full parameter fine-tunning to these large models on small clinical datasets is infeasible. Recently proposed parameter efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) methods [37–40] address this technical challenge by adding a number of parameter efficient adaptors on the pretrained foundation model, and only these lightweight adaptors will be optimized during downstream training. Building on those advances, a growing number of studies have explored the use of foundation models in various oncology prediction tasks. For example, pretrained foundation models have been used as feature extractors to analyze CT scans for survival prediction in lung cancer [41] and outcome prediction in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma [42]. Additionally, large language models (LLMs) have been fine-tuned for radiation oncology tasks to assess their potential clinical applicability [43]. While these studies underscore the potential of foundation models in oncology, their application to chemotherapy response prediction with CT imaging remains largely unexplored.

To address this gap, this study explored the use of LoRA (Low-rank adaptation) [39], a widely recognized and versatile PEFT strategy, to adapt pretrained models for integrating pre-chemotherapy CT imaging data with tumor response prediction. By leveraging LoRA, we aim to reduce the impact of limited clinical data while taking advantage of pretrained large-scale foundation models. To our knowledge, this is the first study to fine-tune CLIP with LoRA for chemotherapy response prediction using CT images. The methodology, experimental findings, and corresponding implications are outlined in the following sections.

Materials and methods

Database

This retrospective study has been approved by the University of Oklahoma Health Sciences Center Institutional Review Board (IRB13649). The dataset used in this study includes a total of 182 cases diagnosed with advanced stage ovarian cancer. All patients underwent systematic chemotherapy following PDS to manage recurrent high-grade ovarian, peritoneal, or tubal carcinoma. Among the 182 cases, 124 were classified as responders, achieving a 6-month progression-free survival (PFS) [44] of “Yes”, while 58 patients were identified as non-responders showing minimal response to treatment. Demographic and clinical characteristics, including age distribution, number of tumors, and slice counts of the largest tumor, are provided in Supplementary Fig. 1. The CT images were collected under standardized clinical protocols after PDS, using either a GE Light Speed VCT 64 or a GE Discovery 600 16-row detector scanner, with a tube voltage of 120 kVp. The current was set according to the patient’s body size, ranging from 100 to 600 mA.

Given that some cases may present multiple tumors, for each case we selected the single CT image showing the largest cross-sectional area of the largest tumor. As most tumors are located in the abdominal region, the abdominal soft tissue window was applied to map pixel intensity values to the range 0-255. The tumors were initially segmented from CT slices by a hybrid algorithm [45], followed by a visual review and manual correction. Further details about the segmentation are provided in our previously published paper [46]. The segmented tumor was then shifted to the center of the image and cropped using a 200 × 200 square window, ensuring that the entire tumor fits within the window for all cases. The process is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Model architecture of CLIP

There are two main separate building blocks within the CLIP model [34] to conduct contrastive learning: a vision encoder and a text encoder (Fig. 2). Those two encoders were pretrained on 400 million mutually matched image-text pairs [47]. The embeddings extracted from the input image and text are subsequently projected into a shared embedding space of the same dimensionality. Within this space, the model learns to align matched image-text pairs to maximize similarity, while simultaneously minimizing similarity for unmatched pairs to enhance the model’s discriminative power.

Fine-tuning CLIP with LoRA

With the emergence of large pre-trained models, such as BERT [50] and GPT-3 [51], which continuously grow in size, fine-tuning these models for downstream tasks becomes increasingly challenging. To mitigate overfitting and fully exploit the embedded knowledge in the large pretrained models when training on limited downstream datasets, a series of PEFT methods [52] have been proposed. These methods are characterized by fine-tuning only a small portion of existing parameters or integrating a minimal number of additional trainable parameters, aiming to achieve optimal adaptation to a specific downstream task without overfitting. Low-rank adaptation (LoRA) [39] is a representative PEFT method that introduces a trainable weight matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\varDelta\:\varvec{W}$$\end{document}, derived from the product of two low-rank matrices \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\varvec{W}}_{A}\in\:{\mathbb{R}}^{r\times\:n}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\varvec{W}}_{B}\in\:{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times\:r}$$\end{document}, into a frozen pretrained weight matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\varvec{W}}_{\varvec{T}}$$\end{document}:3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\varvec{h}=\varvec{x}({\varvec{W}}_{\varvec{T}}+{\varvec{W}}_{\varvec{B}}\varvec{W}}_{A})=\varvec{x}({\varvec{W}}_{\varvec{T}}+\varDelta\:\varvec{W})$$\end{document}.

During fine-tuning, only the added matrices \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\varvec{W}}_{A}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\varvec{W}}_{B}$$\end{document} are updated, while all original parameters remain frozen, as depicted in Fig. 3b. Although LoRA can be implemented to any portion of the network, in this study it is specifically applied on the attention mechanisms: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\varvec{W}}_{q}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\varvec{W}}_{k}$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\varvec{W}}_{v}$$\end{document} within selected Transformer blocks, for reduced complexity while preserving model performance. Different small rank values (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:r=1,\:2,\:3$$\end{document}) were tested to evaluate their impact on performance.

Given that there are 12 Transformer blocks for both image and text encoders in the ViT-B/16 and ViT-B/32 based CLIP variants, we trained multiple models in this study, each with a distinct subset of Transformer blocks fine-tuned using LoRA. Specifically, we started applying LoRA on the 1st lowest or highest block, producing a model labeled as “Low” or “High” (Fig. 3c). Next, the lowest (or highest) two blocks were fine-tuned with LoRA from the original pretrained model to obtain a new model. Different models were progressively created until all 12 Transformer blocks were fine-tuned. The resulting CLIP models are designated as ViT-B/16, 32-Low or ViT-B/16, 32-High, respectively, with each category containing models fine-tuned on a different number of Transformer blocks.

Training implementations and evaluation metrics

During the training process, we fixed all pretrained weights and only fine-tuned the low-rank matrices in LoRA. The training utilized the Adam optimizer [53] with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\beta\:}_{1}=0.9$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\beta\:}_{2}=0.99$$\end{document}, and a learning rate of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{7\times\:10}^{-4}$$\end{document}. The models were trained for a total of 1000 epochs, with the first 70 steps allocated to warm up followed by a cosine decay schedule for the rest. We also applied weighted sampling [54] in training by assigning probabilities inversely proportional to class counts. In this way, instances from the underrepresented non-responsive class have an improved likelihood of being selected for training in each iteration, thereby mitigating the impact of the substantial imbalance in the dataset. Data augmentation involved random 90-degree rotations as well as horizontal and vertical flips.

As a comparison, we fine-tuned all original parameters of the CLIP model without utilizing LoRA. Additionally, we also employed a conventional head-only fine-tuning strategy without the text encoder involved: the projection layer of the CLIP image encoder was substituted with a trainable, randomly initialized one-layer classification head with two outputs. Only this added classification head was updated during training, while keeping all earlier layers frozen. Similarly, three classic deep learning models pretrained on ImageNet 1 K [55], ResNet 50 [56], ViT B/16, and ViT B/32 [48], were also retrained in this conventional manner for comparison. For these baseline models, three learning rates (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{7\times\:10}^{-5}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{7\times\:10}^{-4}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{7\times\:10}^{-3}$$\end{document}) were tested within each cross-validation fold, and the model achieving the best validation performance in that fold was reported. All other training procedures, including the optimizer, warmup and cosine decay schedule, weighted sampling, and data augmentation, were identical to those used in LoRA based fine-tuning process. The experiments were implemented with PyTorch [57] on the NVIDIA RTX 6000Ada GPU, providing 48GB of VRAM (video random access memory).

We conducted stratified 5-fold cross-validation to assess the models’ performance [58], maintaining a consistent proportion of the two classes in each fold. This stratification helps reduce evaluation bias by training and testing on datasets that are close to true class distributions. The average classification Accuracy (ACC) and AUC (area under the receiver operating characteristic [ROC] curve) values are reported, providing a comprehensive examination of models’ capabilities [59].

Discussion and conclusion

In this study, we adopted a parameter efficient fine-tuning method (i.e. LoRA) to optimize a CLIP based foundation model for predicting ovarian cancer chemotherapy response following PDS treatment. The results indicate that LoRA method enables the CLIP model to capture domain-specific knowledge of chemotherapy response effectively, achieving remarkable prediction performance. This study highlights a promising strategy for chemotherapy response prediction employing one of the most current deep learning methods, with several noteworthy patterns that need further discussion, as outlined below.

First, almost all LoRA fine-tuned CLIP models achieved higher performance on the chemotherapy response task than conventional ImageNet pretrained models, except for the ViT-B/32 variant with LoRA applied to its higher Transformer layers (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:N=8,\:r=3$$\end{document}), which showed a slightly lower AUC (0.741) than ResNet-50 (0.754). LoRA based adaptation also outperformed full fine-tuning of the CLIP models. This suggests that CLIP models pretrained on vast image-text data can better capture tumor response related patterns in CT images when adapted with LoRA, which works by introducing lightweight, trainable matrices into selected existing weight matrices (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\varvec{W}}_{q}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\varvec{W}}_{k}$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\varvec{W}}_{v}$$\end{document} in this study), enabling the model to refine representations at multiple levels. Meanwhile, LoRA allows flexible selection of a low rank r that approximates the intrinsic rank [61] and is just sufficient to capture the essential updates for the downstream task. Compared with full fine-tuning, which updates all parameters of the CLIP model, LoRA achieves superior adaptability with substantially fewer trainable parameters and a lower risk of overfitting, effectively leveraging CLIP’s strong generalization abilities. Meanwhile, conventional head-only fine-tuning is applied to models pretrained on the relatively smaller ImageNet dataset, which provides less diverse semantic representations than CLIP models. Moreover, this approach only updates the final classification layers while keeping most pretrained weights fixed, further restricting the model’s ability to adapt to new tasks.

Second, ViT-B/16 consistently outperformed ViT-B/32 in distinguishing responders from non-responders, both in CLIP models and in ImageNet pretrained ViTs, regardless of the adopted fine-tuning method. This superiority is likely attributed to the smaller patch size in ViT-B/16, which facilitates it to extract finer spatial details and perform more intricate attention modeling. In comparison, ViT-B/32 may fail to capture these subtle textures with its larger patch size, leading to a weaker performance in the study.

Third, we found that LoRA is more effective than the head-only approach for CLIP fine-tuning, in which the text encoder was removed and only the image encoder was updated. Interestingly, the head-only approach also performs better on conventional ImageNet pretrained models than on CLIP’s image encoder. This difference may be attributed to the fact that CLIP was pretrained with a joint contrastive objective, forcing the two encoders to learn semantically aligned representation. As a result, the interactions between the text and image encoders are crucial for transferring CLIP’s knowledge to new domains, and a fine-tuned classification head on the image encoder alone cannot effectively interpret the image features extracted by the pretrained CLIP. In our setup, the text input is limited to just two static prompts, whereas the image input includes the full set of CT scans from all cases. Given this imbalance, the results emphasize that updating both the image and text encoders is essential to fully leverage joint vision-language representations for optimal classification with CLIP.

Fourth, fine-tuning the lower-level Transformer blocks of CLIP models with LoRA appears to yield better outcomes compared to targeting the higher-level blocks. Additionally, lower-level blocks required a smaller rank r during fine-tuning, indicating that fewer trainable parameters are sufficient to adapt these blocks to the new domain while minimizing overall computational cost. In deep learning models, the low-level layers are responsible for learning general features, such as edges and textures, while the high-level layers encode more abstract and complex representations [62]. This suggests that the higher-level Transformer blocks become more specialized and function as a robust semantic backbone, making them less flexible for adapting to the CT image based tumor response prediction task. In contrast, the lower-level blocks remain more general and transferable, requiring only modest updates for fine-tuning.

Though this study provides valuable insights, there are several limitations that should be addressed in future work. First, CLIP was fine-tuned on a single 2D slice corresponding to the largest tumor area for each patient, rather than on 3D volumetric data, potentially missing important spatial features critical for tumor characterization [33, 63, 64]. Additionally, for patients with multiple tumors, other lesions containing potentially informative regions were not used, representing a promising source of additional information for future model improvement [65, 66]. Second, while we utilized the segmented tumor regions, the impact of segmentation accuracy on model performance was not evaluated. Third, the study provided two fixed textual prompts for the text encoder, leaving opportunities for performance improvement using more diverse prompts [67, 68]. Incorporating richer and clinically relevant prompt designs could enable the CLIP model to establish more robust associations between image and text features. Fourth, this study focused on the LoRA as a representative fine-tuning strategy, while other PEFT methods, such as Adapters [37], and BitFit [69], were not explored. In future work, evaluating and comparing alternative approaches would offer a more comprehensive perspective on efficient adaptation strategies for CLIP in chemotherapy response prediction. In addition, as CLIP was pretrained on non-medical dataset, a certain degree of domain shift may exist when transferring it to medical imaging. Future work could also explore fine-tuning foundation models that were pretrained on medical datasets [70] and compare their adaptation performance with general purpose foundation models. Fifth, although cross-validation was employed to assess model performance, further evaluation and validation on more diverse external datasets are still needed to confirm the generalizability of this approach. In spite of these limitations, this study serves as an initial performance assessment of CLIP models fine-tuned with LoRA for predicting chemotherapy response. The findings offer useful insights into how the large scale vision-language foundation model can be adapted to downstream clinical imaging tasks and highlight the potential of PEFT methods to adapt effectively to tasks with limited imaging data.
