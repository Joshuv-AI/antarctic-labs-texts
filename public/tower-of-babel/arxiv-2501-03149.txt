Velocity Addition/Subtraction in Special Relativity
Domenico Giulini

Abstract
We reconsider velocity addition/subtraction in Special Relativity (SR) and re-derive its well-known non-commutative and non-associative algebraic properties in a self contained way, including various explicit expressions for the Thomas angle, the derivation of which will be seen to be not as challenging as often suggested. All this is based on the polar-decomposition theorem in the traditional component language, in which Lorentz transformations are ordinary matrices. In the second part of this paper we offer a less familiar alternative geometric view, that leads to an invariant definition of the concept of relative velocity between two states of motion, which is based on the boost-link-theorem, of which we also offer an elementary proof that does not seem to be widely known in the relativity literature. Finally we compare this to the corresponding geometric definitions in Galilei-Newton spacetime, emphasising similarities and differences. Regarding the presentation of the material we will pursue an uncompromising pedagogical strategy, willingly accepting repetitions and occasional redundancies if deemed beneficial for clarity and the avoidance of anticipated misunderstandings. An appendix with four sections includes some mathematical details on results needed in the main text, as well some recollections on notions like semi-direct products of groups and affine spaces.

In classical mechanics we are used to the fact
that the relative velocity between two
states of motion is independent of the
third state of motion (of the “observer”)
from which it is reckoned. In Special Relativity
this ceases to be true in a twofold way:
regarding its measure and regarding the space
of which it is an element of. Nevertheless,
all assignments are naturally fully covariant and
hence consistent with the principle of relativity.

All of these aspects will be clarified in
this paper, whose main result is stated and
fully proved in
Section
3
. It may be
summarised as follows: Let
\mathcal{S}
denote the
set of
states of motion
, which is a
maximally symmetric 3-dimensional Riemannian
manifold of constant negative curvature
(given by the set of unit timelike future
pointing vectors). For any ordered pair

(s_{1},s_{2})
of elements in
\mathcal{S}
the

relative velocity
between them is
a section

s\mapsto\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}(s,s_{1},s_{2})\in T\mathcal{S}

in the tangent bundle over
\mathcal{S}
.
The expression
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}(s,s_{1},s_{2})
is
a rational function of its arguments (formula
(
130a
)) which is
equivariant under the action of the
Lorentz group, in the sense that for any
Lorentz transformation
L
it obeys

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}(Ls,Ls_{1},Ls_{2})=L\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}(s,s_{1},s_{2})
.
Its geometric interpretation is that of
the velocity
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}
(in units of
c
) of
the unique boost
B(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}})
relative to
s

that links
s_{1}
with
s_{2}
. The uniqueness
statement here is known as
boost-link-theorem
,
of which we give an elementary and constructive
proof. The analytic expression of the corresponding
boost map (formula (
134
))
is likewise a rational function of

(s,s_{1},s_{2})
.

The organisation of the paper is as follows.
Section
2
presents in a
self-contained
fashion the known story on Einstein’s law of
velocity addition/subtraction in component
form. Polar decomposition is used to decompose
elements in the Lorentz group into a boost
and a rotation. It is emphasised that this
decomposition is not natural but rather
dependent of a distinguished state of motion.
Explicit formulae for the polar decomposition
of the composition of two boosts are given to
derive once more Einstein addition and also
Thomas Rotation, the properties of which are
derived. In particular, uniqueness of polar
decomposition is used to prove that Einstein
addition endows the open unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{3}

with the algebraic structure of a non-associative
quasigroup with identity, called a
loop
.

Section
3

replaces the common matrix notation by a
proper geometric setting in which the
analytic expressions merely contain states
of motion and their scalar products.
The boost-link-theorem is proved and taken
as basis for the definition of “relative
velocity”, namely as the velocity of the
boost that links the two given states
(s_{1},s_{2})
.
We will call it the “link velocity”. Next to
the two states it also depends on the state
s

relative to which we identify the linking
Lorentz transformation as a pure boost.
Since the group generated by pure boosts is
the entire Lorentz group, that reference to

s
is necessary. We emphasise that this
latter reference renders the notion of relative
velocity a ternary (rather than binary) relation,
and that this fact is
not
in conflict with
the relativity principle as sometimes claimed.

Section
4
compares the
findings of Section 3 to the case of Galilei-Newton
spacetime, which we again characterise in proper
geometric terms. Here boosts form indeed a
3-dimensional subgroup, which is even abelian
and normal, such that the relative velocity
between two states
(s_{1},s_{2})
of
motion—now defined in terms of the geometric
structures given here—does
not
require the
specification of a third reference state relative
to which the linking Galilei transformation is
identified as pure boost.

Finally, an Appendix of four parts contains
the statement and proof of the polar-decomposition
theorem, a proof of the statement (used in the
main text) that parallel-transport along geodesics
on the space
\mathcal{S}
of states is equivalent to
boost transformations, and two summaries of
elementary mathematical concepts used in the
text, namely semi-direct products (for groups)
and affine structures (on sets).

Notation and conventions:

Throughout spacetime is a 4-dimensional real

V

and dual space
V^{*}
. By
the Minkowski metric
,
denoted by
\eta
, we understand a non-degenerate
symmetric bilinear form on
V
of signature

(-,+,+,+)
.

Velocity addition/subtraction: the traditional story

In this section we recall the standard,
matrix-representation-based derivation of
velocity addition/subtraction, including
an analysis of the less widely known
algebraic structure it defines. We shall
give complete proofs of all relevant
statements. Some of these proofs appear
shorter and yet easier to follow than
those in the published literature.

2.1
A reminder on the elementary text-book
derivation

A frequently-seen derivation of the velocity
addition in SR ist this: A particle
P

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}(t^{\prime})

relative to an inertial system
I^{\prime}
with whose
coordinates are labelled by
(t^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime})

Relative to another inertial system,
I

the boost transformation between

I^{\prime}

is

\displaystyle t

\displaystyle\,=\,\gamma(t^{\prime}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}/c^{2})\,,

(1a)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}

\displaystyle\,=\,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}+(\gamma-1)\boldsymbol{\mathbf{n}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{n}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime})+\gamma\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}t^{\prime}\,,

(1b)

where

\gamma:=(1-\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\|^{2}/c^{2})^{-1/2}\,,\quad\boldsymbol{\mathbf{n}}:=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}/\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\|\,.

(2)

The particle’s
P

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}(t^{\prime})

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}

then become a functions of

\displaystyle dt

\displaystyle\,=\,\gamma(1+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}^{\prime})\,dt^{\prime}\,,

(3a)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{d}}x

\displaystyle\,=\,\bigl[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}^{\prime}+(\gamma-1)\boldsymbol{\mathbf{n}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{n}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}^{\prime})+\gamma\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\bigr]\,dt^{\prime}\,.

(3b)

Hence, the particle’s velocity relative to
I

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}=\frac{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}^{\prime}_{\|}+\gamma^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}^{\prime}_{\perp}}{1+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}^{\prime}/c^{2}}\,,

(4)

{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}^{\prime}_{\|}:=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{n}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{n}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}^{\prime})\quad\text{and}\quad\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}^{\prime}_{\perp}:=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}^{\prime}_{\|}

are the orthogonal projections of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}^{\prime}

Alternative ways to write (
4

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}

\displaystyle\,=\ \frac{1}{1+\frac{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}^{\prime}}{c^{2}}}\left\{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}^{\prime}+\frac{\gamma}{c^{2}(\gamma+1)}\,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\times(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\times\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}^{\prime})\right\}

(6a)

\displaystyle\,=\ \frac{1}{1+\frac{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}^{\prime}}{c^{2}}}\left\{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}^{-1}u^{\prime}+\frac{\gamma}{c^{2}(\gamma+1)}\,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\,(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}^{\prime})\right\}\,.

is parallel to

(in which case

\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\|=c

This immediately follows from the equation

2.5
Polar decomposition of boost products

As in section (

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}:\mathring{B}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\times\mathring{B}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})

) for

)
with

\displaystyle c

\displaystyle\,=\,\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}(1+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{1}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{2})\,,

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{n}}_{1}\otimes\boldsymbol{\mathbf{n}}_{1}
,

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{n}}_{2}\otimes\boldsymbol{\mathbf{n}}_{2}

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{a}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}

7
7

7

Note that

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{a}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}

is the

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{a}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}

counted positively with respect to the

(compare

31a
) and (

On the other hand, according to the general formula for the rotation angle, we have

(48)

using (

Replacing

according to
(

that we can solve:

Now, (

where, as in (

We have deliberately written the denominator in

\gamma_{1}

\gamma_{2}

\varphi

An alternative way to write it is in terms

\rho_{i}
(

8
8

8

(

2.6
On the magnitude of Thomas rotation

In Fig.
1
we
show the Thomas rotation for the special
case of mutually perpendicular velocities of
equal magnitude; i.e.
\cos(\varphi)=0

and
\gamma_{1}=\gamma_{2}=\gamma_{*}
. In this
case (
52
) reduces to

\cos(\theta)=\frac{2\gamma_{*}}{1+\gamma^{2}_{*}}\,,

(56)

which is a monotonically decreasing function
of
\gamma_{*}\in(1,\infty)
ranging from
1

to zero and corresponding to a monotonically
increasing angle from
0
to
\pi/2
.

More generally, we may ask for the angle

\varphi
at which
\theta
is largest for
given
\gamma_{1{,}2}
. This can be answered
using either expression
(
52
) or
(
54
), if in the latter
case we regard
\gamma
again as function of

\gamma_{1{,}2}
and
\varphi
according to
(
46c
). This latter
possibility turns out to be slightly more
convenient, so let’s follow this strategy and
consider (
54
). Since

\cos
is monotonically decreasing in

[0,\pi]
, a maximum of
\theta
corresponds
to a minimum of
\cos(\theta)
, which we now
seek. The only dependence of
\cos(\theta)

on
\varphi
is through
\gamma
. The

\varphi
derivative of the latter is

\gamma^{\prime}:=d\gamma/d\varphi=-\beta_{1}\gamma_{1}\beta_{2}\gamma_{2}\sin(\varphi)
,
and
d\cos(\theta)/d\varphi=\bigl(d\cos(\theta)/d\gamma\bigr)\gamma^{\prime}
.
Hence stationary points exist either for

\gamma^{\prime}=0
, which is for the boundary values

\varphi=0
or
\varphi=\pi
that correspond
to aligned and anti-alingned velocities and
which correspond to
\theta=0
(hence a minimum),
or for
d\cos(\varphi)/d\gamma=0
, containing
the maxima. In view of (
54
),
the latter equation is equivalent to

\frac{d}{d\gamma}\,\frac{(1+\gamma+\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2})^{2}}{(1+\gamma)}=\frac{1+\gamma+\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}}{(1+\gamma)^{2}}\,(1+\gamma-\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2})=0\,.

(57)

Since all
\gamma
’s are positive, this is
in turn equivalent to

\gamma=\gamma_{\rm max}:=\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}-1

(58)

which, according to (
53
),
corresponds to an angle
\varphi_{\rm max}
between
the velocities that satisfies

\cos(\varphi_{\rm max})=\frac{\gamma_{\rm max}-\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}}{\sqrt{(\gamma^{2}_{1}-1)(\gamma^{2}_{2}-1)}}=-\sqrt{\frac{(\gamma_{1}-1)(\gamma_{2}-2)}{(\gamma_{1}+1)(\gamma_{2}+2)}}\,.

(59)

The fact that this is negative means that

\varphi_{\rm max}>\pi/2
. This means that the maximal
Thomas angle is only obtained for obtuse angles
between the velocities. The value of the maximal
Thomas angle is given by inserting
(
58
) into (
54
):

\begin{split}\cos(\theta_{\max})=&\ \frac{(1+\gamma_{\rm max}+\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2})^{2}}{(1+\gamma_{\rm max})(1+\gamma_{1})(1+\gamma_{2})}-1\\
=&\ \frac{3(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2})-\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}-1}{(1+\gamma_{1})(1+\gamma_{2})}\\
=&\ 1-2\,\frac{(\gamma_{1}-1)(\gamma_{2}-1)}{(\gamma_{1}+1)(\gamma_{2}+1)}\\
&=\ 1-2\cos^{2}(\varphi_{\rm max})\\
&=\ -\ \cos(2\varphi_{\rm max})\,.\end{split}

(60)

From this we infer

\cos(\theta_{\rm max})+\cos(2\varphi_{\rm max})=2\,\cos\left(\frac{\theta_{\rm max}+2\varphi_{\rm max}}{2}\right)\cos\left(\frac{\theta_{\rm max}-2\varphi_{\rm max}}{2}\right)=0\,,

2
:

•

Let
M
be a set and
\phi:M\times M
a map,
denoted by
(a,b)\mapsto\phi(a,b)=:a\cdot b
.
Then the pair
(M,\phi)
is called a

magma
(sometimes also

groupoid
). Hence

\bigl(\mathring{B}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3}),\oplus\bigr)

is a magma.

•

A magma
(M,\phi)
is called a

semigroup
if
\phi
is associative,
i.e.
a\cdot(b\cdot c)=(a\cdot b)\cdot c

for all
a,b,c\in M
. Hence

\bigl(\mathring{B}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3}),\oplus\bigr)

is
not
a semigroup.

•

A magma
(M,\phi)
is called a

quasigroup
if for each pair

(a,b)\in M\times M
there is a unique pair

(x,y)\in M\times M
such that
a\cdot x=b

and
y\cdot a=b
. This property is also
called
divisibility
.
Theorem
6

then shows that
\bigl(\mathring{B}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3}),\oplus\bigr)
is a quasigroup.

•

A magma
(M,\phi)
is said to have an
identity
if there exists an
e\in M

such that
e\cdot a=a\cdot e=a
for all

a\in M
. Such an

is necessarily
unique (even if
(M,\phi)
is not a
quasigroup). As for Einstein addition

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{0}}
is an identity (compare (

)), we infer that

\bigl(\mathring{B}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3}),\oplus\bigr)

is a
quasigroup with identity
.

•

In a quasigroup with identity each element

has a unique left and a unique right
inverse, namely that
x
and that
y
satsifying

and
a\cdot y=e
.

and

. From (

\bigl(\mathring{B}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3}),\oplus\bigr)

•

A quasigroup with identity is called a

Hence

is a loop that is not a group, but is special insofar as left-and right-inverse elements
coincide.

3.1
States of motion and the non-naturalness of
polar decoposition

Above we used the polar decomposition to
decompose a Lorentz transformation
into a spatial rotation and a boost. It is
important to realise that this operation is

not
natural. It depends on a preferred
state of motion which in the matrix formulation
above was given by the choice of the
timelike vector
e_{0}
of the chosen basis. Any

R(\underline{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{D}}})
takes place in the corresponding “rest space” that is the
orthogonal complement of
e_{0}

of our rotations

spacelike 2-plane within that “space” and
pointwise fixes the timleike 2-plane orthogonal

e_{0}

Similarly, any Boosts

take place
in a timelike 2-plane containing
e_{0}
and
pointwise fixes the spacelike orthogonal
complement. Decompositions with respect
to different choices of states of motion
are
a priori
incomparable, as they
refer to different “spaces” between which
no natural identification (isomorphism) exists.

Defining link velocity

Given three states of motion,

Let

Proof.

Remark 16

The condition of equivariance

Equation (

We have just seen that the boost relative

(s_{2}-s_{1})

depending on

.
This suggests to compute the deviation
of

from

in

“sticks out” of

\mathrm{Span}\{s_{1},s_{2}\}

the norm of the

-orthogonal

In order to determine the latter, we note

.

Let

; the
\eta
-orthogonal projector into

\mathrm{Span}\{s_{1},s_{2}\}

\mathrm{P}_{(s_{1},s_{2})}^{\|}=\frac{s_{1}\otimes s_{1}+s_{2}\otimes s_{2}-\gamma_{12}(s_{1}\otimes s_{2}+s_{2}\otimes s_{1})}{\gamma^{2}_{12}-1}\,.

We note that
\mathrm{P}_{(s_{1},s_{2})}^{\|}

s_{1}

s_{2}

a
\eta
-symmetric linear map whose kernel

\eta
-orthogonal complement
of
\mathrm{Span}\{s_{1},s_{2}\}

, hence

\mathrm{P}_{(s_{1},s_{2})}^{\|}s_{2}=s_{2}
(symmetry) and therefore

\mathrm{P}_{(s_{1},s_{2})}^{\|}\circ\mathrm{P}_{(s_{1},s_{2})}^{\|}=\mathrm{P}_{(s_{1},s_{2})}^{\|}
.

In passing we note that expression

-orthogonal projector into the

-orthogonal complement

so that the squared norm of the

&=\frac{1-\gamma_{1}^{2}-\gamma_{2}^{2}-\gamma_{12}^{2}+2\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}\gamma_{12}}{\gamma_{12}^{2}-1}\,,\end{split}

where we used
(

)

Appendix A

Let

, or

(A^{\dagger})^{\dagger}=A

.
Also,

19
19

19

In this subsection we abbreviate the

under

the reader in one form or another,
though perhaps it is useful to recall the
essential structural properties in a way
adapted to the language used in this paper.

In the main text we characterised Minkowski
spacetime by the following 6-tuple

(M,V,+,\eta,o_{V},o_{T})
, where
(M,V,+)
is a
4-dimensional real affine space,
\eta\in V^{*}\otimes V^{*}

is a symmetric, non-degenerate, bilinear
form on
V
of signature
(-,+,+,+)
,
o_{V}

denotes an overall orientation of
V
, and,
finally,
o_{T}
denotes a time-orientation of

(V,\eta)
. Similarly we characterised
Galilei-Newton spacetime as a 5-tuple

(M,V,+,\tau,h,o_{V})
, where
\tau\in V^{*}
is
an oriented time-distance function and

h\in[\mathrm{Ker}(\tau)]^{*}\otimes[\mathrm{Ker}(\tau)]^{*}

is a Euclidean metric (symmetric positive definite
bilinear form) on
\mathrm{Ker}(\tau)
. Note that

\mathrm{Ker}(\tau)
receives an orientation from

o_{V}
and
\tau
(the latter defines a
time-orientation). In this appendix we recall
the meaning of
(M,V,+)
and also explain the
notion of affine automorphisms and affine bases.
There will be no need to restrict the dimension
n

which we keep general. Also, we could have
easily generalised the underlying number field
to
\mathbb{C}
, but for definiteness we stick with

\mathbb{R}
. Hence we start with a general definition of
the notion of a real affine space.

D.1
Affine spaces

Definition 38
.

A real affine space of dimension
n

is a triple
(M,V,\phi)
, where
M
is a
set,
V
is an
n
-dimensional real
vector space, and
\phi
is a simply transitive
action of
V
– considered as abelian group –
on
M
. Here “action” means that there is a
group-homomorphism from
V
into the group

\mathrm{Bij}(M)
of bijections of
M
(with
group multiplication being given by composition
of maps, denoted by
\circ
, and the neutral
element being the identity map). Hence there is a map

\phi:V\rightarrow\mathrm{Bij}(M)\,,\quad v\mapsto\phi_{v}\,,

(210)

which for all
v^{\prime},v\in V
obeys,

\displaystyle\phi_{v=0}

\displaystyle\,=\,\mathrm{id}_{V}\,,

(211a)

\displaystyle\phi_{v^{\prime}+v}

\displaystyle\,=\,\phi_{v^{\prime}}\circ\phi_{v}\,.

(211b)

That this action is “simply transitive”
means that for any ordered pair
(m,m^{\prime})

of points in
M
there exists a unique

v\in V
such that
\phi_{v}(m)=m^{\prime}
.
Note that ordinary transitivity would be that
without the uniqueness statement.

In passing we remark that for any group action
on a set we generally have that simple transitivity
implies ordinary transitivity, as well as that the
action is free. The latter means that the
homeomorphism (
210
)
is injective, i.e.

\phi_{v}=\mathrm{id}_{V}\Rightarrow v=0
.
Now, for abelian groups the converse
holds: A free and transitive action
is necessarily simply transitive.
Hence we could have required
\phi

to be free and transitive.
We leave the simple proof of the
equivalence to the reader.

Recall that group multiplication in
V

is vector addition “
+
”. Now, it
is general practice, although this
may be confusing at first, to denote
the action of
V
on
M
by the very
same symbol, i.e. to write
m+v
instead
of
\phi_{v}(m)
, and hence to
eliminate all explicit reference to

\phi
. For a given group action
\phi
,
this does not lead to ambiguities since
whether a “
+
” means group action
on
M
or vector addition in
V
is
uniquely determined by whether the
“
+
” stands between an element of

M
and an element of
V
or between two
elements of
V
, respectively.
Equations (
211
) then
assumes the simple form

\displaystyle m+0=m\,,

(212a)

\displaystyle(m+v)+v^{\prime}=m+(v+v^{\prime})\,.

(212b)

Note that in (
212b
) both “
+
” on the left-hand side are
group actions whereas the first “
+
”
on the right-hand side is a group acton
and the second is vector addition.

Since for any given two
m,m^{\prime}\in M
there
exists a unique
v\in V
so that

m^{\prime}=m+v
, we may write
m^{\prime}-m=v
.
Then, trivially,
m^{\prime}=m+(m^{\prime}-m)
. There are other “obvious” relations, like

(m^{\prime}-p)+(p-m)=m^{\prime}-m
or
m+(m^{\prime}-p)=m^{\prime}+(m-p)
,
valid for all
m^{\prime},m,p
in
M
. Likewise,
one has
(m^{\prime}-m)=-(m-m^{\prime})
, where here
the two “
-
” on the right-hand side
have two different meanings: In the
bracket it denotes the difference
operation in
M
, in front of the bracket
scalar multiplication with

(-1)\in\mathbb{R}
in
V
. Other obvious
notational simplifications apply, like

m-v:=m+(-v)
.

As an alternative to Definition
38

above, affine spaces can be defined via the difference
map just introduced:

\Delta:M\times M\rightarrow V\,,\quad(m^{\prime},m)\mapsto\Delta(m^{\prime},m)=m^{\prime}-m\,.

(213)

Definition 39

(alternative to
Definition
38
)
.

A real affine space of dimension
n
is a triple

(M,V,\Delta)
, where
M
is a set,
V
is an

n
-dimensional real vector space, and

\Delta:M\times M\rightarrow V
is a map that
satisfies the following two conditions
for any
o,m,m^{\prime},m^{\prime\prime}
in
V
:

\displaystyle\Delta_{o}:M\rightarrow V\,,\quad m\mapsto\Delta_{o}(m):=\Delta(m,o)\quad\text{is a bijection}\,,

(214a)

\displaystyle\Delta(m^{\prime\prime},m^{\prime})+\Delta(m^{\prime},m)=\Delta(m^{\prime\prime},m)\,.

(214b)

That the existence of
(M,V,\phi)
implies

(M,V,\Delta)
has been shown above. Conversely,
given
(M,V,\Delta)
with
\Delta
satisfying the
axioms above, we deduce a simply-transitive action

\phi
of
V
on
M
by setting
\phi_{v}(m):=\Delta_{m}^{-1}(v)
.
Indeed, from (
214b
) we get
for
m^{\prime\prime}=m^{\prime}=m
that
\Delta(m,m)=0
,
which is equivalent to
\Delta^{-1}_{m}(0)=m

for all
m
, which in turn implies (
211a
).
The second condition (
211b
) can be deduced as follows: (
214b
)
is equivalent to

\Delta_{m^{\prime}}(m^{\prime\prime})+\Delta_{m}(m^{\prime})=\Delta_{m}(m^{\prime\prime})
, which in turn is equivalent to

\Delta_{m}^{-1}\bigl(\Delta_{m^{\prime}}(m^{\prime\prime})+\Delta_{m}(m^{\prime})\bigr)=m^{\prime\prime}
for all

m^{\prime\prime},m^{\prime},m
. Setting
\Delta_{m^{\prime}}(m^{\prime\prime})=:v^{\prime}

and
\Delta_{m}(m^{\prime})=:v
in order to replace
in that equation
m^{\prime\prime}
and
m^{\prime}
by

v^{\prime}
and
v
, this is equivalent to

\Delta_{m}^{-1}(v^{\prime}+v)=\Delta^{-1}_{m^{\prime}}(v^{\prime})
. Finally,
setting
m^{\prime}=\Delta_{m}^{-1}(v)
on the right-hand side,
this is seen to be equivalent to

\phi_{v^{\prime}+v}(m)=\phi_{v^{\prime}}\bigl(\phi_{v}(m)\bigr)
for
all
m,v,v^{\prime}
, and hence to (
211b
).

Summing up we can say that in an affine space we can add vectors
to points and take differenced of points according to the
rules given above. However, points cannot be added. To be
sure, any point
o\in M
defines a bijection

\phi_{o}:M\rightarrow V
via
m\mapsto\phi_{o}(m):=(m-o)
.
But the linear structure thereby pulled back to
M
, which
is given by
m+m^{\prime}:=\phi^{-1}_{o}\bigl(\phi_{o}(m)+\phi_{o}(m^{\prime})\bigr)=o+(m-o)+(m^{\prime}-o)
, depends on the choice of
o
. In fact,
through an appropriate choice of
o

any
point

p
of
M
can be obtained as result of such an
“addition” of
m
and
m^{\prime}
: just choose

o=m+(m^{\prime}-p)=m^{\prime}+(m-p)
.

D.2
Affine maps and groups

Definition 40
.

Let
A=(M,V,\phi)
and
A^{\prime}=(M^{\prime},V^{\prime},\phi^{\prime})
be two
affine spaces. An
affine map
from
A
to

A^{\prime}
consists of a pair
(F,f)
of maps,

F:M\rightarrow M^{\prime}\,,\qquad f:V\rightarrow V^{\prime}\quad\text{linear}\,,

(215a)

such that

F\circ\phi=\phi^{\prime}\circ(f\times F)\,.

(215b)

Here we explicitly displayed the action
\phi
of

V
on
M
and likewise
\phi^{\prime}
of
V^{\prime}
on
M^{\prime}
,
which we consider as maps

\phi:V\times M\rightarrow M
,
(v,m)\mapsto\phi_{v}(m)
,
and

\phi^{\prime}:V\times M^{\prime}\rightarrow M^{\prime}
,
(v,m^{\prime})\mapsto\phi^{\prime}_{v}(m^{\prime})
,
respectively.
In our simplified notation, in which both actions
are written by a common
(+)
-sign, this reads

F(p+v)=F(p)+f(v)\,,

(215c)

for all
p\in M
and all
v\in V
.

Before we proceed we explicitly check that
(
215c
) makes sense, i.e.
leads to the same result independent of how
we represent a point
p\in M
as “sum” of
a point with a vector. So let

p=p_{1}+v_{1}=p_{2}+v_{2}
;
then
F(p_{1}+v_{1})=F(p_{1})+f(v_{1})
and
F(p_{2}+v_{2})=F(p_{2})+f(v_{2})
. But
F(p_{2})=F(p_{1}+(p_{2}-p_{1}))=F(p_{1})+f(p_{2}-p_{1})=F(p_{1})+f(v_{1}-v_{2})

and linearity of
f
shows that indeed both sides
are equal.
Note that condition (
215c
)
says that once we know the map
f
and the
value
F(q)
of the map
F
for
a single point
q
, we know the map
F
, i.e.

F(p)
for any
p
, namely
F\bigl(p=q+(p-q)\bigr)=F(q)+f(p-q)
.

In view of the alternative definition of affine spaces
in terms of the difference map
\Delta
, we could also
have given a corresponding alternative definition of
an affine map:

Definition 41

(alternative to
Definition
40
)
.

Let
A=(M,V,\Delta)
and
A^{\prime}=(M^{\prime},V^{\prime},\Delta^{\prime})

be two affine spaces. An
affine map
from

A
to
A^{\prime}
consists of a pair
(F,f)
of maps,

F:M\rightarrow M^{\prime}\,,\qquad f:V\rightarrow V^{\prime}\quad\text{linear}\,,

(216a)

such that

\Delta^{\prime}\circ(F\times F)=f\circ\Delta\,.

(216b)

In our simplified notation, in which the
difference-map is written by a
(-)
-sign,
this reads

\Delta^{\prime}\bigl(F(p)-F(q)\bigr)=f(p-q)\,,

(216c)

for all
q,p\in M
.

Definition 42
.

An affine map between affine spaces is called
an
affine isomorphism
iff the map
F:M\rightarrow M^{\prime}
is a bijection.
This is equivalent so the requirement for

f:V\rightarrow V^{\prime}
to be a bijection (and hence
a vector-space isomorphism).
An affine isomorhism of an affine space to itself
is called an
affine automorphism
.
The set of affine automorphisms of an affine
space
A
forms a group under composition which
is called the
general affine group
,
denoted by
\mathrm{GA}(A)
.

In order to understand the group-theoretic
structure of
\mathrm{GA}(A)
we first need a
label-set that faithfully labels each of
its elements. This can be obtained in the
following way: Choose a reference point

o\in M
and use it to label
F
by the pair

(v,f)
, where
v\in V
is defined by
F(o)=o+v
,
or
o:=F(o)-o
. The action of
F
on a general
point
p
is then

F(p)=F\bigl(o+(p-o)\bigr)=o+v+f(p-o)\,.

(217)

Note that the argument of
f
is always
the difference between the argument
p
and
the chosen base-point
o
, so that no
f

moves
o
. In this way we identify
\mathrm{GL}(V)

with that subgroup of
\mathrm{GA}(A)
which
stabilises
o
. We will see below how this
identification behaves under changes of
o
.

Suppose now that we have two affine automorphisms

F_{1}
and
F_{2}
, which we label by

(v_{1},f_{1})
and
(v_{2},f_{2})
as just
explained, with reference to the same point

o
. The action of the composition

F_{1}\circ F_{2}
on a general point can then
be calculated:

F_{1}\circ F_{2}(p)=F_{1}\bigl(o+v_{2}+f_{2}(p-o)\bigr)=o+v_{1}+f_{1}(v_{2})+f_{1}\circ f_{2}(p-o)\,.

(218)

Form that we infer the multiplication law
for
\mathrm{GA}(A)
in the chosen
parametrisation

(v_{1},f_{1})(v_{2},f_{2})=\bigl(v_{1}+f_{1}(v_{2})\,,\,f_{1}f_{2}\bigr)

(219)

where composition of maps are now written by
simple juxtaposition in order to stress that
it is group multiplication (in
\mathrm{GA}(A)

and
\mathrm{GL}(V)
). We infer that

\mathrm{GA}(A)
is isomorphic to the semi-direct
product of the abelian group
V
with

\mathrm{GL}(V)
:

GA(A)\simeq V\rtimes\mathrm{GL}(V)\,.

(220)

The homomorphism

\alpha:\mathrm{GL}(V)\rightarrow\mathrm{Aut}(V)
,
whic we need in order to define a semi-direct
product, is just the identity if we use the
isomorphism
\mathrm{Aut}(V)\simeq\mathrm{GL}(V)
.

However, it is important to keep in mind that
this isomorphism depends on the chosen basepoint

o
. Had we chosen another one, say
o^{\prime}=o+w
,
then

\begin{split}F(p)&=o+v+f(p-o)\\
&=o^{\prime}+v+(o-o^{\prime})+f\bigl(p-o^{\prime}+(o^{\prime}-o)\bigr)\\
&=o^{\prime}+v+\Bigl[-w+f\bigl(p-o^{\prime}+w\bigr)\Bigr]\\
&=o^{\prime}+v+\bigl[T_{-w}\circ f\circ T_{w}\bigr](p-o^{\prime})\,,\end{split}

(221)

where
T_{w}:V\rightarrow V
,
v\mapsto v+w

denotes the translation-action of
V
onto
itself. This means that the same affine map

F
that with respect to the basepoint
o

is represented by the pair
(v,f)
will be
represented with respect to
o^{\prime}=o+w
by
the pair
(v^{\prime},f^{\prime})
where
v^{\prime}=v
and

f^{\prime}=T_{-w}\circ f\circ T_{w}
. This is
intuitively obvious, since the representation
(
220
) of

\mathrm{GA}(A)
by the semi-direct product
selects amongst all subgroups
\mathrm{GL}(V)

that one which fixes the selected base-point
o
.
In changing
o
to
o^{\prime}
we also change the subgroup
in
\mathrm{GA}(A)
from the stabiliser subgroup
of
o
to that of
o^{\prime}=o+w
. These two stabiliser
subgroups are clearly related by conjugation with
a translation.

An invariant characterisation of of the affine group

\mathrm{GA}(A)
in terms of
V
and
\mathrm{GL}(V)

would be so say that
\mathrm{GA}(A)
is a
“splitting downward extension of
\mathrm{GL}(V)

by
V
” or, equivalently, a “splitting upward
extension of
V
by
\mathrm{GL}(V)
)” (compare
Appendix
C
), which in any
case means that we have a splitting short exact
sequence with normal subgroup
V
(translations) and
quotient group
Q=\mathrm{GL}(V)
:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\{1\}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces V}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{GA}(A)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{GL}(V)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\{1\}\,.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces i}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\sigma_{o}}

(222)

The splitting homomorphism (embedding)

\sigma_{o}:\mathrm{GL}(V)\rightarrow\mathrm{GA}(A)

depends on the chosen base-point
o\in M
and is
not natural (i.e. no one is distinguished). And two
different ones, say
\sigma_{o}
and
\sigma_{o^{\prime}}

with
o^{\prime}-o=w
, result in different image-subgroups
in
\mathrm{GA}(A)
which are conjugate by an

This means that whereas it makes sense to
speak of
the
translations, since
they form a normal subgroup, it does

not
make sense to speak of
the

subgroup of homogeneous transformations:

\mathrm{GL}(V)
is a
quotient
-
not a sub-group. It may be considered as
a subgroup, though not uniquely. There are
as many
different
subgroups isomorphic
to
\mathrm{GL}(V)
in
\mathrm{GA}(A)

as there are points in
V
. None of them
is intrinsically distinguished.

Remark 48

Finally we point out that the existence of preferred
charts endows affine spaces with a differentiable
and even analytic structure. A function

f:M\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is called differentiable/analytic, if

f_{B}:=f\circ\phi_{B}^{-1}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

is. This is independent of what chart
B
we use,
since obviously
f_{B^{\prime}}=f_{B}\circ\phi_{BB^{\prime}}

As
\phi_{BB^{\prime}}
is, according to (
226b
), an
affine-linear map, hence in particular analytic,
we infer that
f_{B^{\prime}}
is smooth/analytic iff

f_{B}
is.
