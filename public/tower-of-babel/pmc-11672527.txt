Precision in Sensing
Authors: J. Justin Gooding
Published: 2024

Precision and accuracy form the very foundation of analytical chemistry including, of course, sensors. Thus, the launch of a new ACS journal for Precision Chemistry in 2023 certainly raised our interest here at ACS Sensors. We were curious how the scope of the new journal would fit with the scope of ACS Sensors. We were delighted to see that it is perfectly complementary. The scope of Precision Chemistry has, at its very heart, an emphasis on precise measurement, which clearly aligns with the central tenet of analytical chemistry. But it also has an incredibly strong emphasis, first, on the formation and assembly of precise materials and, second, on the development of precise biochemical tools for biomolecular engineering and manipulation. These two foci are vitally important to the way that sensor science is developing, with the rise of both materials chemistry and molecular biology.

The need for precision in materials and processes in sensors will not be a surprise to any scientist. What is interesting here is the interdependence between the precision required and the advancement of sensors toward asking more sophisticated questions on progressively smaller sample sizes. For example, we now have sensors that measure individual molecules. Single-molecule sensors require exquisite precision in their fabrication, but the precision required can depend on the use case of the single-molecule sensor. Take nanopore sensors, for example: the quality of information they provide is entirely dictated by how precisely the nanopore is prepared and by the information we seek from the sensors. If we are seeking molecular identification using a nanopore, most commonly a biological nanopore is used to exploit the incredible precision by which biological molecules are produced. Similarly, modification of that single nanopore, to give it greater capabilities to recognize the molecule, needs molecular-level control. As such, the modification needs molecular precision, hence our need for molecular biology tools. If that nanopore, however, was needed for molecular counting, the precision requirements might be less severe because “how many?” is a somewhat less demanding scientific question than “what is it?”. In this case, maybe one is more inclined to use a solid-state nanopore, which can be made with extraordinary nanoscale precision, but without the molecular-scale precision of biological nanopores.

The interdependence between precision, sample size and scientific question is not limited to single-molecule sensors. Take classical modified electrodes for catalytic sensing of a species such as a drug molecule. Modifying an electrode with catalytic nanomaterials might not require high precision in the size and shape of nanomaterials because the sensor is an ensemble sensor that detects perhaps micromoles of molecules. Hence variation in the nanomaterials often averages out and become insignificant once the system is calibrated. However, if two types of materials are required to work synergistically to detect the species, the precision of how these types of nanomaterials are arranged on an electrode may become crucial to the final sensor performance. The ultimate case of this is probably a single-atom catalyst, where the substrate upon which it operates also facilitates the reaction. The single-atom catalyst could be regarded as having atomic-level precision in the assembly of two materials—a world away from nanoparticles of various sizes randomly being attached to an electrode. Even then, however, the use case of the electrode still determines the precision of where these single atoms are placed. If, for the sake of argument, the modified electrode sensor is for a pharmaceutical or small bioactive molecule that is being oxidized or reduced in a buffer solution, the precision of the environment that the catalytic centers are placed in is significantly more relaxed than if the analyte is to be detected in a biological fluid where many species can foul the electrode.

The underlying message here is that precision chemistry is absolutely crucial in the modern world of sensing. How materials are synthesized, assembled, and arranged will play a vital role in the performance of the sensor. We just always need to keep in mind that the precision required for a given sensor is highly dependent on what the sensor is used for. In other words, like so many other aspects of analytical chemistry, the precision needs to be fit-for-purpose. There is no point in developing highly precise methods that complicate the fabrication of a sensor if the precision that the method affords is not needed. We are excited to see the developments in precision chemistry that allow us to make better sensors that are fit-for-purpose.
