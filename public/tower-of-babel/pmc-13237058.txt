Enhanced water desalination via PVDF-chabazite mixed matrix membranes in vacuum membrane distillation
Authors: Maksymilian Plata-Gryl, Francesco Galiano, Francesca Russo, Rokaya Ben Abed, Alberto Figoli, Roger Gläser, Roberto Castro-Muñoz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Vacuum membrane distillation is foreseen as a membrane unit operation capable of providing access to drinking water in the future, through the saline water desalination. To increase the permeation of standard hydrophobic membranes, such as PVDF, mixed matrix membranes are defined as the next generation of membranes and prepared by adding a filler into the polymer phase. Therefore, the aim of this work was to evaluate the effect of a chabazite zeolite filler (from 0.5 to 4 wt%) into PVDF membranes, which were prepared by a non-solvent induced phase separation, and later tested, for the first time, for water desalination application at different operating temperatures. Chabazite was selected due to its high-water affinity (comparable to zeolite 4 A or 13X) and exceptional hydrothermal stability. To explain the impact of the chabazite, resulting membranes were thoroughly characterized with different analytical techniques, such as scanning electron microscopy with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM-EDX), nitrogen sorption, gas liquid porometry, water contact angle, and mechanical testing. As part of the outcomes, significant enhancement of the desalination performance was observed upon the addition of the chabazite; with the 4 wt% formulation being the best-performing. The permeate flux at 40 °C increased 2.7 times compared to the pristine PVDF membrane, e.g., 6.133 ± 0.540 and 2.256 ± 0.203 kg m− 2 h− 1, respectively. Despite different nature of the chabazite (hydrophilic) and PVDF (hydrophobic), the compatibility was sufficient to avoid formation of non-selective gaps and completely reject salt (99.99%). The membrane performed stable, with high salt rejection, for over 16 h before pore wetting was observed. Although, some issues with chabazite aggregation and pore wetting were observed, the chabazite demonstrated promising potential as a filler that enhances water transport.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-48961-x.

Introduction

The global problem of limited access to clean and safe drinking water is becoming increasingly important. Today, over 2 billion people are facing this problem, and approximately 10% of the global population reside in areas with high water stress. While Earth is predominantly covered by water, only 3% is freshwater, and a mere 0.5% of it is readily accessible. This scarcity affects human health both directly and indirectly. For instance, agriculture accounts for more than 70% of all freshwater withdrawals, meaning water shortages can quickly threaten food security1,2.

Desalination of saline waters is viewed as a key approach, next to wastewater treatment, to mitigate water scarcity, especially in coastal and arid areas. So far, thermal desalination technologies (multi-stage flash distillation and multi-effect distillation) and membrane-based reverse osmosis (RO) are commercially viable3. The final choice of the desalination process depends on the salt concentration in the saline water and the availability of energy sources. The thermal desalination process suffers from high energy consumption, while RO efficiency strongly depends on the salt concentration in the feed. As it increases the osmotic pressure, which needs to be overcome with hydraulic pressure, it also makes it challenging to produce water.

Potential implications of potable water scarcity are pushing the research efforts toward the development of new processes and strategies for desalination. Especially, membrane-based separations (such as reverse osmosis, nanofiltration, forward osmosis, pervaporation and membrane distillation) are envisioned as energy-efficient alternatives. Among them, membrane distillation is a promising process that bridges phase change thermal and membrane-based processes, offering a salt rejection rate of almost 100%.

Membrane distillation (MD) is a process driven by a vapor pressure gradient across a semi-permeable membrane, created by a temperature difference between a hot feed and a cold permeate. The separation relies on hydrophobic and microporous membranes, which allow the water to pass through only as a vapor phase, rejecting all non-volatile solutes, e.g., salts. Moreover, MD can process highly saline water because the driving force is the vapor pressure difference, contrary to RO where high concentration of salt in the feed requires high hydraulic pressure for desalination4. The drawback is that MD is not selective toward other volatile contaminants. The MD has four main configurations: direct contact (DCMD), air gap (AGMD), sweeping gas (SGMD) and vacuum (VMD). The comparison of different configurations suggests that VMD can produce higher flux, by creating a strong pressure gradient. Also, VMD membranes are less prone to concentration polarization5.

The MD process effectiveness is entirely dependent on keeping the liquid out of membrane pores. Therefore, both membrane fouling and wetting are critical aspects in MD operation. To avoid the issue of pore wetting, MD membranes are typically made from hydrophobic, thermally stable polymers, e.g., polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) or polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF). The latter is the most common in research due to the good processability (soluble in common organic solvents), although it is not as hydrophobic as PTFE. PVDF membranes can be easily produced by a simple phase separation in a non-solvent (e.g. water); so-called non-solvent phase separation (NIPS) process. Typically, additional component, like pore forming agents, are added to the dope solution to control the porosity of membranes6,7.

To overcome the limitations and increase productivity of PVDF membranes, mixed matrix membranes (MMMs) have been introduced. MMMs are composites that combine polymeric matrix with inorganic filler to increase the hydrophobicity, thermal stability, fouling resistance, or to enhance the water vapor transport. Despite the progress, challenges exist in their fabrication, namely filler agglomeration, poor interfacial adhesion, scalability4,8,9. To date, a wide range of fillers have been explored in PVDF MMMs, including graphene10, graphene oxide11, carbon nanotubes12, silica13, titanium dioxide14, metal-organic frameworks15, or zeolites16. Especially, the last group, microporous aluminosilicates, is drawing attention in the membrane technology due to the high surface area, and excellent chemical and thermal stability, that potentially provide increased permeability with enhanced physicochemical properties17,18. Zeolites proved to be effective filler in MMMs used for water purification in RO19–21. Chabazite is a type of zeolite constructed by stacked sequences of six-membered rings of tetrahedra (of SiO4 and AlO4) that are arranged into a framework with large, interconnected cavities. The framework can be accessed through eight-membered ring apertures of molecular dimensions which endow chabazite with molecular sieving properties. Plenty of different materials with the chabazite framework topology can be synthesized (SSZ-13 and SAPO-34 being prime examples) and some of them have been incorporated into polymeric membranes, typically for gas separations22,23, but also for water purification24. Chabazite affinity to water is very similar to that of zeolite 4 A and 13X, yet it exhibit exceptional hydrothermal stability25,26. This makes chabazite an interesting candidate for a filler in MMMs, which can increase the water flux while maintaining selectivity and integrity of a membrane. The open question is, to what extent will the hydrophilic nature of chabazite intensify the membrane wetting problem observed in the VMD processes. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report of using chabazite, obtained by facile hydrothermal conversion of zeolite Y, in water desalination. Therefore, in this work, MMMs loaded with chabazite zeolite are prepared and tested in the water desalination using VMD process. The effect of filler loading on membrane properties was analyzed and later investigated on desalination performance as a function of operating temperature.

Experimental

Synthesis of chabazite

Chabazite was synthesized via hydrothermal conversion of a commercial zeolite Y (Süd Chemie). The synthesis procedure was based on27. Briefly, in a 1 L polypropylene bottle, 25 g of zeolite Y was mixed with 198.2 mL of deionized water and 26.8 mL of 45 wt% KOH (reagent grade, 90%, Fluka). The bottle was placed in a preheated oven (95 °C) for 96 h. After that, the bottle was taken out from the oven and cooled down to the room temperature. The white solid product (chabazite) was separated from the KOH solution using centrifugation (5 min at 4500 RPM). To remove all residual KOH, the solid was washed multiple times with deionized water (until neutral pH of the supernatant). After each washing step, the solid was separated by centrifugation for 15 min. Finally, chabazite was dried in the oven at 95 °C and labeled as CHA-K.

Chabazite with sodium cation (CHA-Na) was prepared by ion-exchange of as-synthesized CHA-K (with potassium cation) as follows: 1 M NaCl was mixed with CHA-K in a round bottom flask in 1 g : 40 mL ratio. Mixture was refluxed at 70 °C under stirring for 24 h. The supernatant was separated by centrifugation (5 min at 4500 RPM). The solid was washed with deionized water and separated by centrifugation (15 min at 4500 RPM) 4 times. The ion exchange procedure was repeated 5 times.

Characterization of chabazite

Nitrogen sorption isotherms were recorded at -196 °C using a BELSORP-miniX (Microtrac Retsch GmbH, Germany). Prior to measurements, samples were degassed in vacuum at 250 °C for 6 h. The total specific surface area was determined from the adsorption branch of the isotherm, using the Brunauer-Emmet-Teller (BET) model. For the chabazite sample with potassium counter ion, the relative pressure range for calculations was p/p0 = 0.05–0.30, while for sample with sodium as a counter ion it was reduced to p/p0 = 0.01–0.10.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images were taken with a Leo 1530 instrument (Zeiss, Germany). Chabazite samples were immobilized on carbon tabs with the use of carbon paste and sputtered with gold or carbon for conductivity. Samples were investigated at an acceleration voltage of 5 kV.

The powder XRD patterns were measured at a room temperature using a G670 diffractometer (Huber, Germany), operated with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.154 nm). The diffraction patterns were recorded in the range of 2θ between 5 and 60 ° with a step size of 0.005°.

Preparation of chabazite/PVDF membranes

Membranes were prepared by phase inversion, namely non-solvent induced phase separation (NIPS) process. Dope solutions with 15 wt% of PVDF were prepared in DMF solvent (ACS, 99.8+, Alfa Aesar) at 70 °C, with mechanical stirring. MMMs were obtained by doubling the chabazite loading (0.5, 1, 2 or 4 wt%) to the dope solution. Before adding to PVDF solution, chabazite was dispersed in small quantity of DMF using a magnetic stirrer and followed by a sonication. Each dope solution contained 4 wt% PEG 400 (AmBeed) as a pore former. Full list of prepared dope solutions is presented in Table S1. Prior to casting, the dope solution was mixed overnight and degassed on a rotary evaporator (Rotavapor R-300, Buchi, 25 °C, 500 mbar) for 30 min. Casting was performed on a glass plate with micrometric film applicator (1806 F, BEVS) with the gap size of 150 μm, at ambient conditions. The applied polymeric film was coagulated in deionized water (ultrapure Type I, Direct-Q® 3UV-R system, Merck Millipore). After detaching from the glass support, the membrane was removed from water and dried in air overnight. Before characterization and VMD tests, membranes were washed at least 3 times with warm deionized water to remove residual pore former. The resulting membranes were designated as PVDF-CHA-X, where X stand for the filler loading in wt%.

Desalination by vacuum membrane distillation (VMD)

The experimental setup used for VMD is presented in Fig. 1. Model seawater (3.5 wt%) was prepared by dissolving NaCl (p.a., Chempur) in ultrapure Type I water (provided by Direct-Q® 3UV-R, Merck Millipore). Feed was placed in a 1-liter glass tank in a thermostat bath (LTD 6, Grant) for temperature control. The feed stream was recirculated in continuous mode. Then, membrane cell was made from borosilicate glass and had membrane active area of 12.56 cm2. The vacuum pump (CRVpro 5, Welch Vacuum Products) was connected to the permeate side of the cell. The vacuum level was set to 0.5 mbar and was controlled by the pressure transducer (Baratron 728A2, MKS) and the electrovalve (1,6NMB MS, Brukert) connected to a microcontroller. Between the membrane cell and the vacuum generation unit, the cold trap (KF 29-GL-Z, KGW-Isotherm) was placed to condense the permeate using liquid nitrogen. After test run, the permeate was melted, weighted and transferred to a gas-tight vial. Desalination performance was analyzed at 20, 40, 60 and 80 °C. Results and discussion

The incorporation of any filler, in this case chabazite, aims to increase the PVDF membrane permeability while avoiding the formation of non-selective transport pathways and loss of the selectivity. Figure 2 presents the desalination performance data as a function of operating temperature and chabazite loading. The water flux for all membranes increased significantly with temperature (Fig. 2a), which is expected as the driving force in the VMD process is the vapor pressure difference across the membrane. Also, the flux was increased by the addition of chabazite to the PVDF matrix (Fig. 2c). The flux, in fact, gradually increased with higher chabazite loadings, and no adverse effect on salt rejection was observed (Fig. 2b). This is interesting because addition of a hydrophilic filler to a hydrophobic polymer matrix should result in an increase leakage through non-selective voids at the filler-polymer interface. We hypothesize that apart from pore forming role, PEG can increase the polymer-filler compatibility by interacting with zeolite through hydrogen bonding, bridging both components and minimizing the formation of non-selective interfacial voids, thus ensuring that the flux enhancement is due to preferential transport pathways in chabazite particles.

All membranes almost exclusively transported water while rejecting salt, achieving the rejection levels of 99.8%, regardless of the temperature and chabazite loading. The highest and the most stable rejection was obtained for membranes with highest chabazite loading (with 2 and 4 wt% loading). Interestingly, the PVDF-CHA-4 exhibited the highest permeation, which was over 6 and almost 3 times higher than the permeation of pristine PVDF at 20 °C and 80 °C, respectively. It is worth mentioning that higher chabazite loading than 4 wt% during membrane preparation resulted in a lack of stability of casted membranes (cracks, holes, and big aggregates of chabazite particles), due to increased viscosity of the dope solution and high concentration of the filler.

The significant enhancement of the water flux can be a result of new and faster transport pathways created by the microporous zeolite. The specific surface area of chabazite is 470 m2 g− 1 (Figure S1). Chabazite was synthesized with potassium cations in the framework. As a result, a so-called ‘trapdoor zeolite’ with potassium ions occupying the entrances to the internal voids of the chabazite zeolite is obtained. At the low temperatures of nitrogen sorption (-196 °C), the thermal motion of the potassium ions is reduced by yielding very small specific surface area of chabazite (< 20 m2 g− 1), Figure S1). At higher temperatures, the potassium ion will relocate within the chabazite framework as not block the cage windows29, make the internal pore volume accessible for water molecules (e.g. in VMD experiments). The ‘true’ specific surface area of chabazite was assessed with sodium exchanged chabazite. The smaller cations of sodium cannot efficiently block entrances to zeolite cages even at low temperature. Thus, the internal surface area was probed and the nitrogen sorption provides the above mentioned value of 470 m2 g− 1. The isotherm (type I) for the sodium exchanged chabazite reveals the microporous character of the chabazite framework. Both values, for chabazite with potassium and sodium counter ions, are in good agreement with data reported in the literature29,30. The SEM images and XRD patterns confirm the identity of chabazite (Figure S2 and Figure S3, respectively). From SEM images, an average crystal size of 100–300 nm was determined.

The increase in the water flux was observed despite increasing the thickness as more chabazite was loaded in the membrane. The thickness of PVDF-CHA membranes is reported in Table 1. In fact, both values correlated almost linearly (Figure S4). Therefore, the increase in the permeability must have been more significant than the increase of the resistance caused by the increased membrane thickness. It emphasizes the role of chabazite in enhancing the PVDF membrane performance. Especially that, neither the porosity nor the mean flow pore diameter (given in Table 1) changes can explain such increase in the flux. The chabazite filler role is mainly in creation of preferential intra-crystalline water transport pathways. Because the chabazite pore size is larger than the water molecules, they are transported through chabazite channels with much lower resistance compared to diffusing through dense polymer matrix. Additionally, hydrophilic nature of chabazite may favor the solubility coefficient of water and consequently its flux.

To gain more insight into the structure of PVDF-CHA membranes, SEM was used to image the top layer and cross-sections of membranes, which are presented in Fig. 4. For instance, characteristic feature of membranes prepared by the NIPS process is the presence of finger-like macrovoids, right beneath the dense top layer, which are followed by bi-continuous phase. Interestingly, the best-performing PVDF-CHA-4 membrane was almost completely free of this macrovoids. The formation of the sublayer morphology is dictated by the solvent-nonsolvent diffusion rate. If it is high, the instantaneous demixing occurs in a coagulation bath and macrovoids are formed, but if it is low enough, the demixing is delayed and the formation of macrovoids is suppressed7. Therefore, the morphology of the PVDF-CHA-4 membrane can be explained by increased viscosity of the dope solution with 4 wt% of chabazite. It slowed down the mass exchange rate in the coagulation bath, delaying the demixing, and prevented the nonsolvent from penetrating to polymer rich phase, thus resulting in a denser structure. Additionally, the top surface of the PVDF-CHA-4 was the least rough with some pores visible (black spots), that could facilitate the water transport and further explain the superior performance of the membrane with 4 wt% chabazite loading. Results suggest an optimal balance between enhanced transport and filler aggregation for a PVDF-CHA-4 sample.

Since the membrane structure determines the mechanical properties, it should be expected that the limited presence of macrovoids will enhance the mechanical properties of the membrane. However, as presented in Table 1, the Young’s modulus did not change significantly, while the tensile strength was reduced. The elongation at break for PVDF-CHA-4 was 137 ± 12% compared to 167 ± 35 for pristine PVDF. This can be a result of limited compatibility between polymer and chabazite. In SEM images, the aggregation of the chabazite particles can be observed, especially at the vicinity of the macrovoids. Figure S5 presents the SEM images with magnification on macrovoids. The accumulation of filler particles in that particular region can be associated with the rate of solvent diffusion to the coagulation bath and limited polymer-filler interaction. Fast solvent exchange possibly induced the transport of filler particles toward the top surface of the membrane and their accumulation near macrovoids. This would be supported by the fact that in the PVDF-CHA-4 membrane (without macrovoids) cross-section images do not show visible aggregates. They are probably evenly distributed within the bi-continuous (porous) phase. Although not visible in the image, their presence is confirmed by the SEM-EDX analysis that detected Si, Al and K elements in all of membranes. The elemental composition is provided in Table S2. It suggests that chabazite incorporated in membranes is in unaltered form, as the Si/Al ratio is very similar to the value measured for pure as-synthesized chabazite. As a perspective of this membrane formulation, particular attention should be paid to uniform distribution of chabazite particles within the polymer matrix, e.g., this could be achieved by a priming procedure32. Another option is to use silane coupling agents or grafting polymers to alter the chabazite surface chemistry to make it more hydrophobic and compatible with polymer matrix33–35. The lack of macrovoids in the PVDF-CHA-4 membrane can also explain its lowest porosity. However, considering the standard deviations of porosity results, it can be concluded that in terms of the membrane porosity there is no statistically significant difference between the samples investigated. Nevertheless, the compatibility of membrane components was sufficient to avoid the formation of non-selective gaps at the boundary between them and thus retain selectivity.

Additionally, intrinsic properties of the chabazite, like surface hydrophilicity, can help improve the performance of the membrane, by lowering the resistance for the water molecules permeation. The WCA values measured on the top (active) surface of membranes are presented in Table 1. The initially hydrophobic character of the PVDF membrane became more hydrophilic as the chabazite was introduced into the polymer matrix. However, increasing the loading above 1 wt% did not change the WCA value significantly, which was around 85° for membranes with filler loadings between 1 and 4 wt%. This value is close to what is considered a threshold value between hydrophobic and hydrophilic character of the surface, i.e., 90°. This enabled PVDF-CHA membranes to be used in VMD applications but problems with wetting of the membrane pores were observed.

To assess the long-term stability of PVDF-CHA membranes, the best-performing formulation, i.e., PVDF-CHA-4 was used for a long run test at 80 °C. The results are presented in Fig. 5. The membrane exhibited a steady flux of ca. 6 kg m− 2 h− 1, with a salt rejection rate of almost 100% for almost 16 h. After this stage, a rapid increase in flux was observed while experimenting with a salt rejection drop. Relatively fast membrane performance deterioration was due to a pore wetting during operation. It is a central challenger in MMMs engineering with hydrophilic fillers. In the water treatment by the vacuum membrane distillation the hydrophilic zeolite, having a high affinity for water, increase the flux through intra-crystalline preferential transport pathways. However, it may lead to accumulation of water molecules around the hydrophilic filler that displace the weak van der Waals forces connecting filler and polymer particles. As a result, non-selective gaps at the boundary between them are created and a pore wetting is observed. The trade-off between flux and wetting could be managed by, e.g., treating chabazite with silane coupling agents. Alternatively, the compatibility between the chabazite and PVDF can be increased by altering the Si/Al ratio. By increasing the Si content more hydrophobic, and more PVDF compatible chabazite can be obtained, potentially enhancing the stability of the membrane.

As the best performing membrane formulation, PVDF-CHA-4 membrane performance was compared with other similar membranes used in VMD for desalination, as presented in Table 2. It should be stressed out that different conditions (feed concentration, temperature, pressure, and system configuration) render a direct numerical comparison of flux values difficult. However, it indicates that the PVDF-CHA-4 membrane is competitive, both in terms of salt rejection and flux. Generally, most membranes presented in Table 2 exhibit a complete rejection of salt ions. When comparing permeate flux, the PVDF-CHA-4 membrane shows similar values in relation to literature data, except for a few membranes that display better productivity. For example, PVDF filled with GO (4 wt%), that similarly to chabazite has hydrophilic nature, yielded a remarkable flux of 36 kg m− 2 h− 1 with almost complete salt rejection but, speaking from experience, such values should be considered cautiously. Nevertheless, chabazite demonstrated promising potential as a functional filler that can endow the MMMs with improved performance compared with pristine PVDF. To some extent, addressing the issues, such as particle aggregation and implementing a new fabrication strategy for preventing pore wetting, can further enhance the scalability of MMM formulations and their durability for long-term desalination applications.

Conclusions

Vacuum membrane distillation typically requires porous membranes with sufficiently high hydrophobicity to efficiently desalinate water. However, this work demonstrated that the introduction of a hydrophilic chabazite zeolite (obtained by transformation of zeolite Y) as filler in PVDF membranes, prepared by phase inversion, significantly improved the performance of the membrane in terms of water permeation, without the loss of selectivity. The salt rejection of all membranes, containing different amounts of chabazite (from 0.5 to 4 wt%), was almost complete (above 99.98%). The sample with the highest chabazite loading (PVDF-CHA-4) exhibited the best performance, with permeate flux of 6.133 ± 0.540 and 1.500 ± 0.134 kg m− 2 h− 1 at 80 and 40 °C, respectively, which compared to pristine PVDF membrane, it was 2.7 and 4.1 times higher, respectively. This increase was attributed to new water transport pathways created by the porous zeolite filler particles. Additionally, the hydrophilic nature of chabazite reduced the resistance to water molecules mass transfer across the membranes. The long-term test demonstrated that the PVDF-CHA-4 membrane performance was stable for over 16 h. Analysis of membranes structure by the SEM revealed that incorporation of chabazite changed the morphology and porous structure of membranes. Increased amount of chabazite increased the dope solution viscosity, delaying the demixing in coagulation bath, and resulted in suppressed macrovoids formation in case of PVDF-CHA-4, for which, also pores in the top layer were detected. In this work, we also point out that one of the drawbacks of PVDF-CHA membranes deal with chabazite aggregation. Despite this, the compatibility of membrane components was sufficient to avoid formation of non-selective gaps. In the future, this problem could be addressed by the priming procedure at the dope solution preparation stage, or filler’s surface chemistry modification. The comparison of the PVDF-CHA-4 membrane productivity to the literature data also indicates that chabazite is a promising filler for enhancement of the MMMs performance, with potential for further development.
