Surgical care in Canada and the cost of inaction
Authors: Edward J Harvey, Chad G Ball
Published: 2026

Something has shifted. The conversations happening in our hospital corridors, in department meetings, and even in the pages of this journal have taken on a different tone. The tone is marked less by the familiar frustrations of underfunding and more by something harder to quantify, maybe more like a creeping sense of futility. Surgeons in Canada are not simply overworked — they are operating within a system that is structurally misaligned with the demands placed upon it. The consequences for patients and for the profession are becoming increasingly difficult to ignore. The data are abundant, published, and even damning. The wait time crisis is not a queue management problem, despite the claims of provincial governments and hospital management, who are only too happy to blame physicians. People in Canada wait an average of 57.5 weeks for orthopedic surgery from the time of referral — the longest wait of any surgical specialty.1 This figure has increased since the pandemic.1 For hip and knee arthroplasty, the proportion of patients receiving their procedure within the recommended 6-month window remains below pre-2019 levels.2 These statistics don’t show simple inconvenience; they are associated with functional decline, opioid dependence, lost employment, and preventable deterioration in an aging population. The reflex response to these numbers is to invoke operating room (OR) efficiency, centralized booking, and single-entry models. Certainly, our institutes have triggered these methods — with little success and a lot of anger among the physicians. These tools have some value, but none of them address the more fundamental issue: ORs that are not adequately funded, staffed, or scheduled to meet existing demand. We might be able to pseudo-optimize our way through the existing systems and still fall short. Efficiency is a multiplier; it is not a substitute for capacity.

We are living a workforce paradox that unfortunately was manufactured and perpetuated. Perhaps most troubling is that we simultaneously face a shortage of surgical access and an excess of trained surgical graduates who cannot find employment. The Canadian Occupational Projection System forecasts a national shortfall of 5400 surgical specialists by 2033.3 Yet across the country, newly credentialed orthopedic surgeons, general surgeons, and subspecialists are unable to secure hospital privileges and operating time.4 This is not a supply problem — it is a funding and governance problem. We train surgeons and then create conditions that make it impossible to employ them where they are needed. The downstream consequences include credential creep, where graduates pursue subspecialty fellowships to improve their employability, which in turn adds to the acceleration of the disappearance of the generalist surgeon, particularly in rural Canada.5 We are, in effect, systematically dismantling one of the foundations of accessible surgical care in this country.

Burnout is a problem. It is seen as a personal failing, but it should be seen as a system signal. A recent qualitative investigation of surgeon burnout in Canada found that surgeons are not burning out because surgery is hard; they are burning out because the administrative and systemic machinery surrounding surgical care has become an obstacle course that must be navigated before a single incision is made.6 Limited OR availability, nonspecific referrals, inadequate support infrastructure, and funding models that penalize rather than reward complexity are organizational failures, not individual ones. When experienced surgeons exit the workforce early, we lose accumulated technical skill, mentorship capacity, and institutional memory that cannot be rapidly replaced. The cost of that attrition, measured in patient outcomes, is real but largely invisible in the way we account for health system performance.

Provincial governments must be held accountable to address these problems. The resources expended on producing a fully trained surgeon in Canada are extraordinary. Allowing that investment to sit idle or to be exported is not a policy choice that can be rationalized on the basis of budget constraints alone. Our professional organizations, including the Canadian Association of General Surgeons, the Canadian Orthopaedic Association, and the Royal College of Physicians and Surgeons of Canada, must move beyond advocacy documents and toward coordinated, public-facing pressure campaigns with measurable targets. Finally, technology must be a genuine part of the solution, not a rhetorical one. Artificial intelligence–driven triaging, digital perioperative pathways, remote monitoring, and telesurgical consultation are not future concepts; they are active pilot initiatives in parts of Canada that could be scaled with appropriate investment and policy alignment.7 We return to this theme repeatedly in CJS because we believe it represents a real inflection point, provided the will to act exists. Finally, those of us who lead surgical departments, train residents, and serve on hospital committees must be willing to name these problems plainly, to the right people, at the right tables. The culture of quiet endurance has long characterized surgery in Canada and is a cultural asset in the OR. It is a liability in the boardroom. Surgery in Canada has been, and remains, world-class in its science, its training, and its technical execution. The question before us is not whether we have the talent. It is whether we have the institutional architecture and political resolve to deploy that talent in service of the patients who are waiting. In far too many cases, they have been waiting far too long.
