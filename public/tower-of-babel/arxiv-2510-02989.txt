Transport of Event Equation: Phase Retrieval from Defocus Events
Kaito Hori, Chihiro Tsutake, Keita Takahashi, Toshiaki Fujii

Abstract
To time-efficiently and stably acquire the intensity information for phase retrieval under a coherent illumination, we leverage an event-based vision sensor (EVS) that can detect changes in logarithmic intensity at the pixel level with a wide dynamic range. In our optical system, we translate the EVS along the optical axis, where the EVS records the intensity changes induced by defocus as events. To recover phase distributions, we formulate a partial differential equation, referred to as the transport of event equation , which presents a linear relationship between the defocus events and the phase distribution. We demonstrate through experiments that the EVS is more advantageous than the conventional image sensor for rapidly and stably detecting the intensity information, defocus events, which enables accurate phase retrieval, particularly under low-lighting conditions.



Keywords.

adaptive optics, phase retrieval, transport of intensity equation, event-based vision sensor.

3
Transport of Intensity Equation

In this section, we aim to recover the phase distribution
\phi_{x,\tilde{z}}
on the focus plane from the defocus-image intensities since image sensors can measure the intensity only.
Throughout this paper, we assume that the intensity distribution at the focus plane is constant
4
4

4

Typical examples of such objects/images include the lens (refractive index distribution) of the human eye
[
21
]
.
, i.e.,
I_{x,\tilde{z}}=I
.
Under this assumption, the TIE can be formulated as the following elliptic partial differential equation (a.k.a. Poisson equation)
[
5
,
6
]
:

\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}\phi_{x,\tilde{z}}=-\frac{k}{I}\frac{\partial}{\partial z}I_{x,\tilde{z}},

(1)

where
k
is the wavenumber and
\partial I_{x,\tilde{z}}/\partial z
is referred to as the axial linear-intensity derivative.
The closed-form solution of the TIE is as follows
[
11
]
:

\phi_{x,\tilde{z}}=\mathscr{F}^{-1}\bigg[\frac{1}{\omega^{2}+C}\mathscr{F}\bigg[-\frac{k}{I}\frac{\partial}{\partial z}I_{x,\tilde{z}}\bigg]\bigg],

(2)

where
\mathscr{F}
is the Fourier transform operator along the spatial axis
x
,
\omega
is the spatial frequency, and
C
is a constant for suppressing noise amplification
[
22
]
.
Note that
\omega^{2}
and
\omega^{-2}
are the forward and inverse transfer functions of
\partial^{2}/\partial x^{2}
, respectively.

The axial linear-intensity derivative can be computed using the defocus images
I_{x,\tilde{z}\pm\Delta}
as follows:

\frac{\partial}{\partial z}I_{x,\tilde{z}}\simeq\frac{I_{x,\tilde{z}+\Delta}-I_{x,\tilde{z}-\Delta}}{2\Delta}.

(3)

However, capturing
I_{x,\tilde{z}\pm\Delta}
is time-consuming due to exposure times and unstable under low-lighting conditions.
The instability primarily arises from the following four processes, which are often assumed in the image restoration literature
[
23
,
24
]
.

1.

Poisson process
reproduces shot noise due to the stochastic nature of photon arrivals.

2.

Gaussian process
models electronic readout noise. We assume a zero-mean Gaussian process with the standard deviation
\sigma_{\mathrm{TIE}}
.

3.

Clipping
limits intensity values that exceed the sensor’s dynamic range prior to analog-to-digital conversion. It is particularly problematic under low-lighting conditions, leading to a loss of detail in dark regions.

4.

Quantization
reproduces rounding errors due to analog-to-digital conversion. Defocus changes smaller than the quantization step size are eliminated by this process.

Proposed Method

Unlike conventional image sensors, the EVS records the time
t
, spatial coordinate
x
, and polarity of intensity changes (the red and blue points in Fig.
1

We translate the EVS over a distance of
2\Delta

2T

The position of the EVS, denoted by
z_{t}\in[\tilde{z}-\Delta,\tilde{z}+\Delta]
, can be written as a function of
t

z_{t}=\frac{\Delta}{T}t+\tilde{z}-\Delta,\quad(0\leq t\leq 2T).

(4)

An event at
(x,z_{t})

(x,t)

(5)

where

We integrate events along

6(a)
and

Suppose that there is no noise in the recorded events.
Under this assumption,
E_{x,\tilde{z}-\Delta\rightarrow\tilde{z}+\Delta}
and (
5
) have the following relationship.

E_{x,\tilde{z}-\Delta\rightarrow\tilde{z}+\Delta}=\frac{\log I_{x,\tilde{z}+\Delta}-\log I_{x,\tilde{z}-\Delta}}{\mu}

(6)

Equation (
6

3

As a result, the sensitivity of the EVS remains consistent regardless of the intensity scale.
This characteristic enables the EVS to stably detect fine defocus changes even under low-lighting as well as high lighting conditions
6
6

6

In other words, the EVS operates over a wide dynamic range.
.
Moreover, event acquisition is more time-efficient than capturing defocus images with exposure times.

We aim to reconstruct
\phi_{x,\tilde{z}}
from
E_{x,\tilde{z}-\Delta\rightarrow\tilde{z}+\Delta}
.
We use the following elementary relationship:

\frac{1}{I}\frac{\partial}{\partial z}I_{x,\tilde{z}}=\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\log I_{x,\tilde{z}}\simeq\mu\frac{E_{x,\tilde{z}-\Delta\rightarrow\tilde{z}+\Delta}}{2\Delta}.

(7)

Substituting (
7

1
), we obtain the following Poisson equation, referred to as the TEE:

\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}\phi_{x,\tilde{z}}=-k\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\log I_{x,\tilde{z}}.

(8)

The solution can be obtained by replacing
I^{-1}\partial I_{x,\tilde{z}}/\partial z
in (
2
) with
\partial\log I_{x,\tilde{z}}/\partial z
.

Remark 6:

We finally mention the readout delays of events.
In the current experimental setup,
we focused on simple and practical phase objects represented by low-order Zernike polynomials.
For these objects, the RMSE values in optical experiments (Fig.
8
) were comparable to those in computer simulations without the delay (Fig.
4
).
Furthermore, events were accumulated over a sufficiently long time (1 second).
Based on these results,
we believe that the effect of delay is nearly negligible for practical phase objects.
However, when the object becomes complex, e.g., a superposition of high-order Zernike polynomials,
the delay probably becomes problematic and affects the quality of phase retrieval.
We would like to address this issue in future work.

6
Conclusion

We proposed a phase retrieval method using an event-based vision sensor (EVS)
[
20
]
.
We showed that a wide dynamic range characteristic of the EVS allows us to rapidly and stably measure the axial intensity derivative.
We also demonstrated, both theoretically and numerically, that the phase distribution can be recovered using the
transport of event equation (TEE)
and the axial intensity derivative (defocus events).
We believe that our study will contribute to advancing adaptive optics.
