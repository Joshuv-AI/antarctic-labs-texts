Neutrophil extracellular traps in the tumor microenvironment, metastasis, therapy, and beyond: advances, challenges, and perspectives
Authors: Jingwen He, Zhaokai Zhou, Guangyang Cheng, Huabing Li, Jiaqi Tu, Zixuan Fan, Xiuting Qiu, Wenjie Chen, Yajun Chen, Ling Li, Chen Li, Zhengrui Li, Lina Chen, Qiong Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), the DNA-protein structures released by neutrophils within the tumor microenvironment (TME), play a role in cancer that extends far beyond their traditional antimicrobial function. Specifically, NETs exert multifaceted and context-dependent effects on tumor progression, metastasis, immune regulation, and therapeutic response, showing their robust activity in shaping the complex landscape of tumor biology. This review systematically elucidates how NETs promote tumorigenesis by inducing DNA damage and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), and drive metastatic spread through mechanisms such as trapping circulating tumor cells, remodeling the pre-metastatic microenvironment, and reactivating dormant cells. Concurrently, NETs shape an immunosuppressive TME by modulating T cells, NK cells, and macrophages, while mediating resistance to chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and immunotherapy. Based on these mechanisms, targeting NET formation (such as inhibiting PAD4 or NE) or degrading their structures (such as using DNase I) has emerged as a potential strategy to enhance the efficacy of existing therapies. Therefore, deepening the understanding of the multifunctional regulatory networks underlying NETs within the TME holds significant implications for developing novel precision cancer therapies.Keywords: Neutrophils, Neutrophil extracellular traps, Cancer, Tumor microenvironment, Metastasis, Treatment resistance

Introduction

Neutrophils, a crucial element within the immune system, function as regulators and effectors in immune-mediated reactions [1]. Neutrophils in humans and mice exhibit a short half-life of 6–12 hours [2]. Neutrophils mainly function by being drawn to the location of infection or inflammation, recognizing and phagocytosing microorganisms, and then killing pathogens through a variety of cytotoxic mechanisms, for instance, creating neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) [3]. In 1996, research revealed that phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) stimulated a specific self-destructive mode of neutrophil death [4]. Neutrophils dying in this pattern show chromatin structure disintegration, nuclear enlargement, cytoplasmic penetration by nuclear components, and eventual rupture of the cell membrane [4]. Once activated, neutrophils discharge granule proteins and chromatin, which jointly create an extracellular filamentous fibrous structure [5].

Neutrophils have long been simply positioned low on the immune tree, and this oversimplified view has prevented deeper exploration of their role in supporting homeostasis in the body and in complex disease states such as cancer [6]. Undoubtedly, neutrophils constitute a vital and fundamental component within the intricate tumor microenvironment (TME), a critical relationship substantiated by research [7]. NETs capture and kill pathogens and were originally described as a mechanism for neutrophil neutralization of pathogens, but NETs are also released in aseptic inflammation, promoting thrombosis and possibly mediating tissue damage [8]. The biological roles of NETs are complex and diverse, including killing tumor cells, promoting tumor development, enhancing tumor invasion and metastasis, promoting angiogenesis, and constructing an immunosuppressive microenvironment [9]. In return, tumor-produced chemokines such as CXCL [10–15] and IL-8 [16, 17] can trigger the creation of NETs.

NETs interact closely with the TME. Herein, this review focuses on exploring the impact of NETs on tumors, discussing the mechanisms of NETosis and the role of NETs in promoting tumor initiation, progression, and metastasis from a basic research perspective. Moreover, we elucidate the potential link between NETs and drug resistance, proposing development strategies aimed at targeting NETs or disrupting NET-cancer cell interactions from a clinical practice standpoint.

NET formation

NETs are complex, filamentous fibrous structures composed primarily of highly decondensed chromatin fibers, histones, and granule proteins [18]. These granule proteins comprise matrix metalloproteinase 9 (MMP-9), neutrophil elastase (NE), myeloperoxidase (MPO), cathepsin G (CG), and other antimicrobial proteins [19]. Histones (H2A, H2B, H3, and H4), granule granzymes (NE, MPO, and PR3, among others), and cytoplasmic proteins (e.g., calreticulin and lactoferrin) in NETs all contribute to their bactericidal activity [20]. NETs are primarily released through a unique form of cell death known as NETosis [21]. NETosis can be primarily categorized into suicidal NETosis, vital NETosis, and mitochondrial NETosis[20] (Table 1). Suicidal NETosis comprises the release of granule contents into the cytoplasm, alterations in histone proteins causing chromatin decondensation, breakdown of the nuclear envelope, and plasma membrane permeabilization[20] (Fig. 1). Yipp et al. first described vital NETosis as a rapid process in which neutrophils maintain viability and functional activity while releasing NETs [22, 23]. Mitochondrial NETosis was discovered by Yousefi et al., wherein surviving neutrophils form NETs containing DNA derived from mitochondria rather than nuclear DNA, with NET formation dependent on ROS [24]. NETosis typically activates in response to stimuli such as PMA [4], lipopolysaccharides (LPSs) [25], antibodies [26], immune complexes [27], and various bacteria [28].

Upstream signaling pathways of suicidal NETosis

NE, MPO [29], and the nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) oxidase complex [30] are all implicated in the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and/or DNA decondensation. Of these, NADPH oxidase (NOX) and mitochondria are the major sources of ROS in neutrophils [31–33]. The upstream signaling pathways that activate NOX in response to pathogens, leading to ROS production and subsequent NETosis, remain incompletely understood. Structurally, NOX is a multiprotein complex assembled from a membrane-integral flavocytochrome b, composed of a 91 kDa glycoprotein and a 22 kDa subunit, and a set of four cytosolic factors: p47phox, p67phox, p40phox, and the G-protein Rac234. Early research indicated that receptor-mediated oxidase activation was initially thought to involve calcium and/or PKC [35, 36]. PKC-mediated phosphorylation of the subunits of NOX initiates a structural realignment, thereby uncovering the functional domain of NOX and facilitating its membrane assembly [35, 36]. Furthermore, various other kinases, including the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK), phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K), and protein kinase B (PKB, also known as Akt) [37–42], have been implicated in this process. Hakkim et al. demonstrated that the Raf-MEK-ERK pathway proves essential for PMA-induced NETosis, as it activates NOX and upregulates expression of anti-apoptotic proteins [43].

Receptors that trigger the release of NETs

Different stimuli trigger NETosis through various receptors. The toll-like receptors (TLRs) respond to diverse pathogens and damage-associated signals [44]. For instance, TLR4 recognizes lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and activates NOX-dependent NETosis [44]. In a ventilator-induced lung injury mouse model, HMGB1 drives NETosis in the liver via TLR4/MyD88/TRAF6 signaling; pharmacological inhibition of this pathway attenuates both NET formation and hepatic damage [45]. Similarly, using the imiquimod-induced mouse model of psoriasis, NETs contribute to skin inflammation through epidermal TLR4/IL-36 receptor crosstalk and downstream MyD88/NF-κB signaling; both TLR4 inhibition and NETs disruption alleviate disease [46]. A study by Stephen et al. identified platelet TLR4 functions as a threshold switch for the formation of NETs in severe sepsis [47]. Earlier work by Andonegui et al. established that platelets express functional TLR4: in wild-type mice, LPS induced platelet aggregation, whereas this response was absent in TLR4-deficient platelets [48]. TLR4 promotes platelet-neutrophil adhesion and propels NETosis [47].

Ligation of P-selectin glycoprotein ligand 1 (PSGL-1) on neutrophils by P-selectin induces Syk phosphorylation and intracellular calcium flux, resulting in PAD4 activation, while concurrently enhancing platelet-neutrophil adhesion to regulate NET formation [49]. Using intravital microscopy in models of acute lung injury and endotoxemia, Sreeramkumar et al. demonstrated that PSGL-1 on neutrophils forms uropod-protruding clusters that scan for activated platelets via P-selectin; this interaction is required for NET release and subsequent vascular injury, as blockade of PSGL-1 completely abrogated NETosis and resulted in tissue preservation [50]. In parallel, platelets present high-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) to neutrophils, where it binds to the receptor for advanced glycation end products (RAGE) and thereby stimulates NETosis [51, 52]. Beyond platelets, damaged hepatocytes also release HMGB1 and histones, which stimulate NETosis via TLR4- and TLR9-MyD88 signaling pathways [53].

In fungal cell walls, β-glucans trigger NETosis via interaction with Dectin-1 and the CD11b/CD18 receptor, whereas mannan is recognized by TLR2, TLR4, CD14, and Dectin-1, primarily triggering NET release through a ROS-dependent pathway [54]. Beyond structural components, secreted fungal polysaccharides, particularly the hyphal proteins Sap4 and Sap6 also induce NETosis, with Sap4 and Sap6 showing the highest potency [54]. Notably, plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs) interact with Aspergillus fumigatus to release pDC extracellular traps (pETs) composed of DNA and citrullinated histone H3, structures strikingly similar to NETs [55]. NETosis induced by fungal mannans involves Dectin-2 and complement receptor 3 (CR3)-mediated recognition of β-glucan [55, 56]; however, the full repertoire of receptors and signaling molecules governing this process remains to be identified. Trypanosoma cruzi and its soluble antigens can induce NETosis through TLR2 and TLR4 stimulation [57], while HIV triggers this process via TLR7 and TLR8 [58]. For immune complex (IC)-induced NETosis, human neutrophils utilize the IgG receptors FcγRIIa and FcγRIIIb [59]. These receptors initiate distinct signaling cascades by engaging unique PKC isoform combinations, thereby selectively regulating neutrophil effector functions [59]. Notably, FcγRIIIb surpasses FcγRIIa in its capacity to induce actin polymerization, β1 integrin activation, and NET formation [60]. Mechanistically, this enhanced efficacy may stem from FcγRIIIb activation of the transient receptor potential magnesium channel 2 (TRPM2)—a calcium channel that facilitates calcium influx from the extracellular environment into the cell [60]. Notably, this pathway differs from the FcγRIIa-mediated mechanism of intracellular calcium store release [60].

Tumor-derived factors can also bind to certain receptors on neutrophils, thereby triggering NETosis [61] (Table 2). For instance, cathepsin C (CTSC) secreted from breast cancer cells drives the enzymatic activation of membrane-bound proteinase 3 (PR3) on neutrophils. This activation facilitates interleukin-1β (IL-1β) processing and nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) activation, leading to the upregulation of IL-6 and CCL3 that further recruit neutrophils [61]. In addition, the CTSC-PR3-IL-1β axis stimulates ROS generation in neutrophils and facilitates NETosis; NETs in turn, degrade thrombospondin-1, thus fostering the metastatic colonization of cancer cells in the lung [61]. Beyond these tumor-derived mediators, the TME contains various chemokines and cytokines that act on CXCR1 and CXCR2 to modulate neutrophil function [62]. Using orthotopic Lewis lung carcinoma (LLC) models (cells from ATCC) and autochthonous Ptenfl/flLkb1fl/fl (PL) genetically engineered mouse models, Kwak et al. demonstrated that CXCR1/2 antagonism slows tumor growth but does not prevent neutrophil recruitment into tumors [62]. Instead, it impairs NETosis, an effect associated with reduced ROS release [62].

ROS-related chromatin decondensation

Under appropriate stimulation, NOX gets activated, transferring electrons from cytoplasmic NADPH to molecular oxygen within the phagosome lumen, generating superoxide anion [63]. Superoxide is subsequently converted to hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂), a substrate for MPO [63]. The H₂O₂/MPO system promotes nucleotide release and activates nucleotide-hydrolyzing enzymes [63]. MPO facilitates the transport of nucleotides released from azurophilic granules into the nucleus [63]. NE plays a critical role in NET formation, as NE⁻/⁻ mice fail to generate NETs in a Klebsiella pneumoniae lung infection model [29]. NE partially degrades specific histone fractions and acts synergistically with MPO to drive chromatin decondensation [63]. In addition, NE has been shown to degrade F-actin, a process contributing to neutrophil immobilization [63]. Concurrently, ROS enhances calcium influx and activates peptidylarginine deiminase 4 (PAD4) in conjunction with protein kinase Cζ (PKCζ) [20, 40]. In a calcium-dependent manner, PAD4 mediates the conversion of histone H3 arginine residues to citrulline, resulting in chromatin decondensation [18]. ROS-dependent activation of gasdermin D (GSDMD) leads to pore formation in the nuclear envelope and plasma membrane [64]. Consequently, decondensed chromatin is released into the cytoplasm, where it mixes with granule proteins to form NETs [64]. This process ultimately results in neutrophil lysis and death [64]. In summary, ROS serves a pivotal role in chromatin decondensation triggered by stimuli including PMA.

Effects of NETs on Tumors

Accumulating evidence has demonstrated that NETs exhibit high expression in multiple tumor types [65]. Extensive follow-up studies have confirmed an association between overloaded NETs and carcinoma development [9, 66, 67]. Neutrophils form a key component within the TME, participating in tumor initiation, metastasis, immune regulation, and anti-tumor responses [9, 65,69] (Fig. 2). Conversely, cancer cells may trigger the development of NETs [70].

NETs in cancer initiation

Chronic inflammation stands as a well-recognized catalyst underlying tumor initiation [71]. Neutrophils serve a critical role in the TME, generating a destructive inflammatory environment that damages tissues and significantly promotes tumor formation [9]. Using a zebrafish melanoma model, Wculek et al. found that oncogenically transformed cells are promoted by neutrophils recruited at the wound site and provide them with paracrine proliferative support, which they call the neutrophils the cancer-burning flame [72]. More over, Windt et al. demonstrated in STAM mouse models, induced by neonatal streptozotocin injection and long-term high-fat diet, that NETs facilitate tumor initiation by recruiting monocyte-derived macrophages, upregulating pro-inflammatory cytokines including IL-6 and TNF-α, and sustaining chronic hepatic inflammation to drive malignant transformation [73].

Numerous findings have demonstrated a significant role for NETs in tumor development. Using a murine orthotopic model of pancreatic cancer with Panc02 cells, Miller-Ocuin et al. demonstrated that genetic ablation of Padi4 in Padi4−/− mice or pharmacological disruption of NETs with daily intraperitoneal DNase I treatment notably reduced primary tumor growth and improved overall survival [74]. Mechanistically, pancreatic stellate cells lacking the receptor for RAGE failed to respond to NETs, resulting in diminished tumor cell proliferation [74]. Although the full spectrum of NET-driven pro-tumorigenic mechanisms remains to be elucidated, several NET-associated components have been implicated in tumor promotion, including NE, MPO, MMP-9, ROS, and immunosuppressive factors [75]. For instance, ROS generated during NETosis induces DNA damage and driver mutations in lung epithelial cells in vitro, providing a direct link between NETs and oncogenic transformation [75]. In the intestinal mucosa, Butin-Israeli et al. demonstrated that the release of neutrophil-derived microparticles enriched with pro-inflammatory miR-23a and miR-155 [76]. Through a ROS-independent mechanism, these miRNAs promote double-strand break (DSBs) accumulation in epithelial cells, as shown in both HCT116 and CaCo-2 cell lines and validated in patient-derived colonoids [76]. Using HCT116 human colorectal cancer (CRC) cells and Huh7 hepatocellular cancer cells, Yazdani et al. demonstrated that NET-derived NE activates TLR4, leading to PGC1α upregulation and enhanced ATP production [77]. This effect was confirmed in MC38 cells and in subcutaneous and hepatic metastasis models, where genetic PAD4 deletion or pharmacological NE inhibition suppressed mitochondrial biogenesis and tumor growth [77]. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that NETs contribute to tumor initiation through multiple parallel mechanisms, including genotoxic damage, stromal remodeling, metabolic reprogramming, and immunosuppression.

NETs promote tumor metastasis

Metastasis stands as the principal driver of cancer-related deaths [78]. Accumulating evidence from animal models and clinical studies has implicated NETs in multiple steps of the metastatic cascade [69, 79–83]. In triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) specimens, Park et al. detected abundant NET deposition [83]. Using murine 4T1 and human BT-549 TNBC cells, they demonstrated that cancer cells induce NET formation in a G-CSF-dependent manner, requiring NOX, PAD4, and neutrophil proteases [83]. These NETs promote cancer cell invasion and migration in vitro—effects abrogated by DNase I. In vivo, DNase I-coated nanoparticles significantly reduced lung metastatic burden in both experimental and spontaneous metastasis models, without affecting primary tumor growth [83]. Clinically, patients with liver metastases from breast or colon cancer exhibit higher circulating NET biomarkers than metastasis-free patients or those with metastases to other organs, correlating with abundant NET infiltration in hepatic lesions [84]. In a murine model of breast cancer liver metastasis established by intrasplenic injection of E0771 cells, PAD4 deficiency markedly reduced hepatic metastatic burden, underscoring a functional role for NETs in liver colonization [84]. NETs contribute to multiple steps of the metastatic cascade, including promoting angiogenesis, forming the pre-metastatic niche, driving epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), facilitating circulating tumor cell (CTC) extravasation, and reactivating dormant tumor cells [31, 85–89] (Fig. 3).

NETs promote tumor metastasis. Composed of core functional components including proteases (e.g., NE, MMP-9, CG) and DNA scaffolds, NETs drive metastasis through multiple pathways: (1) Acting on vascular endothelial cells, NETs up-regulate VEGF-A, release pro-angiogenic factors, and up-regulate ICAM-1 and ET-1 to promote angiogenesis; they also disrupt the endothelial barrier and cell junctions, thereby increasing vascular permeability. (2) Acting on CTCs, NETs capture them, prolong their retention, recruit them via the ILK-β-parvin pathway, activate EMT and the TLR4-COX2 pathway, and construct a favorable microenvironment, thus enhancing CTC invasion. (3) Acting on immune cells, NETs secrete immunosuppressive factors, induce PD-L1 expression, and promote Treg expansion; these effects lead to T cell exhaustion, directly inhibit anti-tumor immunity, and foster the expansion of immunosuppressive cells. (4) Degrading the ECM by cleaving laminin (which awakens dormant cancer cells), hydrolyzing VE-cadherin, and activating Notch1 signaling (which induces EMT)

Angiogenesis

Tumor cells need an adequate blood supply to grow [90]. NETs have been shown to influence the vascular system through multiple mechanisms [18, 91–93]. NETs are abundant in cholangiocarcinoma (CCA) tissues and correlate with poor patient prognosis [85]. Using RBE and KMBC human CCA cell lines, Zhang et al. manifested that NET DNA, the DNA scaffold of NETs, promotes cancer cell proliferation, migration, and invasion in vitro, effects abrogated by DNase I treatment [85]. Mechanistically, NET DNA binds to αV integrin (ITGAV) on CCA cells, activating the NF‑κB pathway to drive proliferation, epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), and metastasis [85]. In a transposon‑based CCA mouse model, PAD4 deficiency markedly reduced tumor burden and suppressed NF‑κB activation in vivo [85]. Furthermore, NET DNA directly upregulates vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGF‑A) expression in cancer cells via ITGAV/NF‑κB signaling, promoting angiogenesis as evidenced by tube formation assays and CD34 staining in murine tumors [85].

Aldabbous et al. first linked NETs to angiogenesis in pulmonary hypertension patients, who showed elevated plasma NET markers and extensive NETosis within vascular lesions [94]. In human pulmonary endothelial cells, NETs induced NF‑κB‑dependent angiogenesis in Matrigel assays via MPO/H₂O₂/TLR4 signaling, an effect confirmed in vivo [94]. NETs also triggered the release of proangiogenic factors, including MMP-9, heparin-binding epidermal growth factor-like growth factor (HB-EGF), and urokinase-type plasminogen activator (uPA), as well as endothelin-1 (ET-1), promoting endothelial permeability, cell motility, and pulmonary artery smooth muscle cell proliferation [94]. Christoffersson et al. demonstrated that VEGF-A recruits a proangiogenic neutrophil subset to hypoxic tissue [95]. In a syngeneic pancreatic islet transplantation model, islet-derived VEGF-A attracted CD11b⁺/Gr-1⁺/CXCR4ʰⁱ neutrophils that delivered high amounts of MMP-9, an enzyme critical for revascularization [95]. Neutrophils recruited by VEGF-A contained 10-fold more MMP-9 than those recruited to inflammatory stimuli, and MMP-9 deficiency impaired vessel penetration into grafts and caused persistent vascular abnormalities [95]. These findings identify a VEGF-A–MMP-9 axis in neutrophil-driven angiogenesis [95]. Besides, NETs also enhance cytokine/growth factor discharge from macrophages[96]. HPAECs co‑cultured with NETs upregulate intercellular adhesion molecule‑1 (ICAM‑1), a key angiogenic modulator, while promoting VEGF‑induced endothelial migration and accelerating endothelial progenitor cell recruitment [96, 97]. Additionally, MPO/H₂O₂ signaling is a key determinant of the pro-inflammatory and pro-angiogenic effects exerted by NETs [98]. MPO catalyzes lipid peroxidation and activates TLR4 to trigger NF-κB activity, ICAM-1 expression, and ET-1 release [99, 100].

Epithelial-mesenchymal transition

EMT serves as a crucial driver of tumor advancement, boosting cancer cell motility, invasiveness, and resistance to apoptosis [101–104]. Accumulating evidence has linked NETs to EMT induction in various cancer types [105–107]. Using A549 and PC9 human lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) cell lines, Han et al. demonstrated that knockdown of Phospholipid Scramblase 1 (PLSCR1) impaired NET formation in coculture with neutrophils, suppressed tumor growth and lung metastasis in a subcutaneous xenograft mouse model, and reversed EMT markers including upregulation of E-cadherin and downregulation of N-cadherin and vimentin, identifying PLSCR1 as a dual regulator of NETs and EMT in LUAD progression [106]. Martins-Cardoso et al. showed that in MCF7 breast cancer cells, NETs downregulate E‑cadherin, upregulate N‑cadherin and fibronectin, and increase ZEB1 and Snail expression, accompanied by enhanced migration and upregulation of IL‑1β, IL‑6, IL‑8, and MMPs [108]. Similarly, Ma et al. showed that treating HGC-27 and MKN-45 gastric cancer (GC) cells with NETs upregulates vimentin and N-cadherin while repressing E-cadherin, indicative of EMT induction [109]. Mechanistically, this effect depended on SERPINE-1 upregulation and subsequent activation of the TGF-β/Smad pathway, as reflected by increased expression of TGF-β1, TGF-βR1, TGF-βR2, phosphorylated Smad2/3, and Smad4. In vivo, employing a splenic injection liver metastasis model inoculated with HGC-27 cells, they confirmed that NETs promote liver metastasis [109]. Kajioka et al. revealed that NETs stimulate EMT in pancreatic cancer cells, with upregulation of vimentin and α-SMA and downregulation of E-cadherin, an effect mediated by NET-associated HMGB1. In vivo, thrombomodulin-mediated degradation of HMGB1 not only reversed these EMT changes but also suppressed liver metastasis in an ischemia-reperfusion mouse model [110]. A similar pro-EMT effect has been observed in colon cancer, where Stehr et al. found NETs in 44% of 85 tumor specimens examined, with their presence correlating with high histopathological grade and lymph node involvement [111]. In vitro, NETs induced filopodia formation, enhanced motility, and upregulated mesenchymal markers such as vimentin and fibronectin, and EMT-promoting transcription factors, including ZEB1 and Slug, in DLD1 and SW480 cells [111]. Moreover, Dimitrov et al. uncovered a distinct mechanism in EGFR‑driven lung cancer, showing that HCC827 and H1975 cells adhere to NETs via α5β1 integrin and CCDC25, leading to Notch 1 activation, upregulation of Zeb1, Slug, and Twist, and enhanced migration, accompanied by downregulation of EGFR signaling [89]. Interestingly, the DNA scaffold of NETs may be dispensable for EMT induction [108]. Cardoso et al. treated breast cancer cells with DNase, which disrupted the structure of NETs but did not significantly affect their invasive and migratory abilities [108]. Moreover, the breakdown of NET-DNA does not significantly impact EMT, yet it diminishes the spread of tumors [110]. In contrast, pharmacologic inhibition of NE or NET-derived HMGB1 eliminated EMT-induced metastasis [110]. The researchers venture to hypothesize that EMT induction is primarily influenced by NET proteases. Together, these studies establish NETs as direct inducers of EMT across multiple cancer types, with the primary drivers being protein components rather than the DNA backbone.

Promoting pre-metastatic niche

Once cancer cells acquire invasive properties, their successful colonization at distant sites depends critically on the pre-metastatic niche (PMN), a supportive microenvironment sculpted by the primary tumor prior to CTC arrival [112, 113]. Newly disseminated tumor cells that endure in the circulation and infiltrate distant organs are influenced by the microenvironment of distant organs [114, 115]. The seed and soil hypothesis, proposed in 2017, offers a vivid visualization of the role played by pre-transfer ecological niches: disseminated tumor cells (“seeds”) selectively and actively adapt the “soil” of distant metastatic sites, paving the way for metastatic spread [115]. An increasing amount of evidence has indicated NETs as key architects of the PMN across multiple metastatic organs, including the liver, lung, and omentum [86, 116, 117]. Rather than serving merely as passive traps for CTCs, NETs actively remodel the PMN through at least two distinct yet interconnected mechanisms: immune suppression and direct physical capture.

The immunosuppressive functions of NETs in the PMN have been elucidated in several contexts [79, 86, 118]. Lee et al. established an orthotopic mouse model of ovarian cancer (OC) using ID8 cells and found that NETs induce CXCL13 expression in mesothelial cells [86]. CXCL13 recruits peritoneal innate‑like B cells, which respond to NET stimulation by producing IL‑10 through SHP‑1 inactivation and ROS generation [86]. This cascade promotes regulatory T cell expansion, establishing an immune‑deficient niche that permits metastatic colonization [86] (Fig. 4). Zheng et al. identified a complementary mechanism in the lung, demonstrating that Th2 cytokines IL-4 and IL-13 reprogram lung mesenchymal stromal cells isolated from pre-metastatic stage MMTV-PyMT mice to produce complement C3 [118]. The cleavage product C3a then recruits neutrophils and promotes NETosis via the C3a-C3aR axis, thereby amplifying local immunosuppression in the pre-metastatic niche [118]. Moreover, Zhou et al. demonstrated that tumor cell-released autophagosomes (TRAPs) induce the formation of PD-L1-decorated NETs via the HMGB1-TLR4-Myd88-ERK/p38 signaling pathway [79]. These NETs directly suppress CD8 + T cell function, an effect that could be reversed by combined treatment with DNase I and an anti-PD-L1 antibody, revealing a mechanism by which NETs contribute to an immunosuppressive premetastatic niche in the lung, underscoring the multifaceted ways in which NETs sculpt an immunosuppressive milieu [79].

NETs induce omental mesothelial cells to produce the chemokine CXCL13, which mediates the recruitment of peritoneal B cells to the newly formed microenvironment. NETs stimulate B cells to produce IL-10 through a mechanism involving ROS production and SHP-1 inactivation. B-cell-derived IL-10 promotes the proliferation and aggregation of immunosuppressive Tregs, thereby establishing a tolerant microenvironment that facilitates subsequent tumor cell metastasis to the omentum

Beyond immune modulation, NETs also contribute to PMN formation by directly engaging CTCs [61]. Xiao et al. demonstrated that CTSC, which activates PR3 on neutrophils [61]. Using human MDA-MB-231 and mouse 4T1 breast cancer cells, they showed that CTSC-driven PR3 activation triggers IL-1β-p38-ROS signaling and subsequent NETosis [61]. These NETs degrade thrombospondin-1, an extracellular matrix protein that inhibits metastasis, thereby promoting tumor cell colonization in the lung [61]. In vivo, using mouse models of breast cancer lung metastasis, CTSC overexpression enhanced metastatic burden, while CTSC knockdown or pharmacological inhibition with AZD7986 suppressed metastasis [61]. Additionally, Xie et al. showed that in hepatocellular carcinoma, tumor-derived SPP1 binds to CD44 on alveolar epithelial cells, stimulating CXCL1 production and subsequent neutrophil recruitment and NET formation [117]. In orthotopic and experimental metastasis models with Hepa1-6 and H22 cells, these NETs trapped disseminated tumor cells and promoted metastatic outgrowth, effects that could be prevented by CXCR2 inhibition or DNase I [117]. Notably, this process can be amplified by systemic factors. According to Pan et al., chronic stress induces pulmonary epithelial cells to secrete acetylcholine, which potentiates NETosis, while concurrently upregulating CXCL2 to recruit additional neutrophils, a dual mechanism that links neuroendocrine signaling to PMN remodeling [119]. Together, these studies reveal that NETs function not as passive scaffolds but as active organizers of the pre‑metastatic niche, orchestrating immunosuppression and tumor cell entrapment through tissue‑specific molecular programs.

NETs promote CTC extravasation

Cancer cell reimplantation, whether originating from an in situ tumor or an early metastatic site, necessitates that circulating tumor cells (CTCs) navigate the bloodstream and successfully breach the endothelial barrier to form new colonies in distant organs [120]. The survival, growth, circulation, extravasation, and colonization capabilities of CTCs are significantly impacted by NETs [88]. NETs create a defensive shield, emitting immunosuppressive cytokines to safeguard CTCs against the cytotoxic effects of NK cells [9]. Furthermore, NETs serve as a physical scaffold that captures CTCs and facilitates their migration to distant sites, thereby promoting metastatic dissemination [9]. Not only do NETs capture CTCs to extend their retention time, but they also augment the migratory and invasive abilities of CTCs, thus expediting the metastatic progression of tumors [92, 121]. In contrast to trapping and killing pathogens, in the case of severe infections, NETs, while capable of trapping CTCs, facilitate early attachment of cancer cells to remote organ locations [122]. Using a murine model of hepatic ischemia-reperfusion injury, Ren et al. demonstrated that surgical stress promotes distant metastasis by activating platelets via the TLR4-ERK5-GPIIb/IIIa axis [123]. Following tail vein injection of MC38 or CT26 colon cancer cells, the resulting platelet-CTC aggregates were preferentially captured by NETs deposited in the pulmonary microvasculature, with platelets serving as a physical bridge tethering CTCs to NETs [123]. In addition, Yang et al. showed that NETs internalized by HepG2 and MHCC97H cells activate TLR4 and TLR9 signaling, leading to COX2 upregulation and enhanced metastatic potential [82]. RNA-seq analysis identified COX2 as a key mediator of this NET-induced inflammatory response, along with other pro-inflammatory genes, including IL-1α/β and CSF-1 [82]. Furthermore, NETs can actively recruit CTCs [84]. Yang et al. proved that NET-DNA serves as a chemotactic factor, directly attracting MDA-MB-231 and HCT116 cells via the transmembrane receptor CCDC25 [84]. Upon sensing extracellular NET-DNA, CCDC25 activates the ILK-β-parvin pathway to improve cell motility and directional migration, thereby promoting metastatic dissemination [84].

NET-associated proteases and ROS generated during NETosis collectively enhance the migratory capacity of CTCs [31, 122, 124, 125]. NET protease acts to degrade the extracellular matrix, subsequently promoting cancer cell extravasation and metastasis[126]. For example, NE cleaves diverse ECM components, including collagen, elastin, and fibronectin, and induces EMT to promote CTC invasion [127]. NE also promotes CTC extravasation by disrupting endothelial junctions and increasing vascular permeability [128]. Moreover, NE activates MMPs, which in turn degrade ECM components and release pro-angiogenic factors [127, 129]. Additionally, MMP-8 and MMP-9 cleave a variety of collagen proteins [130, 131]. Guan et al. demonstrated that NET-associated CG decreases E-cadherin expression in HuH7 cells, thereby promoting hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) cell invasion in vitro [132]. This pro-invasive effect of CG may be further amplified in the NET-rich TME, as biochemical studies have shown that DNA binds CG with high affinity and renders it resistant to inhibition by its endogenous serpin inhibitors, such as α1-antichymotrypsin and α1-proteinase inhibitor [133]. ROS generated during NETosis disrupt endothelial junctions and promote tumor extravasation [31]. In a mouse model of obesity-associated breast cancer metastasis, McDowell et al. exhibited that neutrophil-derived MPO-generated HOCl downregulates the tight junction protein JAM-1 on lung endothelial cells, increasing vascular permeability and facilitating the influx of CTCs into the lung parenchyma [134]. Collectively, these interconnected mechanisms converge to enhance CTC invasiveness and extravasation.

NETs awaken dormant tumor cells

DTCs can remain dormant for years or decades without causing symptoms, even after radical treatment; they can survive by growth arrest, evasion of immunosurveillance, or development of drug resistance, but may eventually reactivate proliferation, leading to metastatic progression or recurrence [135]. NETs have been proven to play a crucial role in reactivating dormant cancer cells [136, 137]. Sustained lung inflammation induced by LPS instillation or tobacco smoke exposure promotes NET formation, as demonstrated by Albrengues et al. using mouse models of breast cancer dormancy established via intravenous injection of D2.0R or MCF-7 cells [138]. These NETs, through the sequential action of NE and MMP-9, cleave laminin and remodel the extracellular matrix. The proteolytically remodeled laminin activates integrin α3β1 signaling in dormant cancer cells, driving their re-entry into the cell cycle and subsequent proliferation [138]. Beyond inflammatory stimuli, cancer therapy itself has recently been identified as another trigger of NET-mediated dormancy reactivation [139]. He et al. showed that chemotherapeutic agents such as doxorubicin or cisplatin induce fibroblast senescence in the lung microenvironment, leading to the secretion of factors, including complement C3, that promote NETosis [139]. The resulting NETs similarly cleave laminin and activate integrin signaling, awakening dormant 4TO7 and 4T1 breast cancer cells and promoting lung metastasis in orthotopic mouse models [139]. Thus, NETs represent a key mediator in the reactivation of DTCs, with direct implications for cancer treatment and patient outcomes.

NETs sculpt an immunosuppressive tumor immune environment

Tumor cells, immune cells, cytokines, and other related components collectively constitute the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) [140]. TIME components collectively control tumorigenesis and progression. In addition to driving tumor metastasis and progression, NETs are now recognized as crucial constituents of the TIME [70] (Fig. 5).

NETs on innate immune cells

Dendritic cells and Macrophages Macrophages and dendritic cells (DCs), the key innate immune cells regulating the anti-tumor immune response, primarily function as antigen presenters [141, 142]. Donis-Maturano et al. demonstrated that NETs exert time-dependent dual effects on human monocyte-derived macrophages and DCs [143]. Short-term exposure (30 min) upregulated the costimulatory molecules CD80 and CD86, indicating early activation. However, prolonged exposure (6 to 24 h) induced cell death accompanied by mitochondrial membrane depolarization, activation of caspase-3 and AIF, and ultrastructural mitochondrial damage [143]. NETs also modulate DC activation through multiple mechanisms. For instance, free fatty acid-induced NETs activate human monocyte-derived DCs, leading to upregulated expression of CD40, CD86, and HLA-DR, as well as increased production of IL-1β, IL-12, and TNF-α [144]. Conversely, NETs suppress LPS-induced maturation of human monocyte-derived DCs (moDCs), as shown by reduced expression of HLA-DR, CD80, CD83, and CD86 and decreased production of TNF-α, IL-6, IL-12, and IL-23 [145]. Furthermore, Guimarães-Costa et al. discovered that NETs added to IL-4/GM-CSF-treated human monocytes closely interact with these cells, downregulating IL-4 receptor expression. This shifts monocyte differentiation away from DCs and toward an M2 macrophage phenotype, an effect reversed by NE inhibition [146].

Moreover, Nakazawa et al. have shown that both macrophage M1 and M2 phenotypes can digest NETs [147]. Interestingly, M2 induced pro-inflammatory responses and M1 enhanced extracellular DNA in early interactions with NETs, together promoting the antipathogenic effects of NETs, and activation of CAD in M1 macrophages in late interactions with NETosis may be involved, at least in part, in the clearance of extracellular DNA [147]. Additionally, M2 macrophages facilitate the proliferation, migration, invasion, and drug resistance of tumor cells [148–150]. Macrophages orchestrate NET removal through DNase-mediated cleavage of the DNA scaffold and endocytosis of the remaining DNA fragments [151]. Proinflammatory stimulation of macrophages augments macrophage megacytosis to enhance the degradation of NETs, thereby accelerating their uptake and catabolism [151]. Moreover, the cytoplasmic nucleic acid exonuclease TREX 1, also known as DNase III, has a critical function in macrophages for the breakdown of NETs following their phagocytosis [152, 153]. Upon stimulation with IFN-γ and LPS, inflammatory M1 macrophages release NET-like structures composed of DNA and extracellular proteins, a phenomenon termed MET (macrophage extracellular traps) [154]. Ma et al. discovered that NE augmented the release of the protease calcium-dependent papain-like cysteine protease-2 (Calpain-2) from macrophages, which in turn led to macrophage phagocytosis failure [155]. Together, these studies reveal a complex interplay between NETs and macrophages, affecting not only NET clearance but also macrophage phenotype and function.

Natural killer cells Natural killer (NK) cells, a subset of innate cytotoxic lymphocytes, mediate tumor cell killing through perforin and granzyme release, a function critical to cancer immunosurveillance [156, 157]. NET fulfills a regulatory role for NK cells. By forming a physical barrier around tumor cells, NETs block the access of CD8+ T cells and NK cells, preventing their cytotoxic effects [12]. Zhang et al. revealed that NETs accumulate in human CRC tissues, where their abundance correlates inversely with the number and cytotoxic function of infiltrating NK cells [158]. When incubated with NETs isolated from CD1+ neutrophils, NK cells underwent apoptosis in a dose-dependent manner, an effect abrogated by pharmacological inhibition of NET release with Cl-amidine [158]. Furthermore, Gong et al. demonstrated that NETs induced by radiotherapy (RT) impair NK cell anti-tumor immunity in HCC [159]. In an orthotopic Hepa1-6 mouse model, RT increased intratumoral NET deposition and serum NET levels, which correlated with reduced NK cell function [159]. Notably, NK cell-mediated tumor cell elimination requires direct cell-to-cell contact, a process that can be hindered by NETs and associated thrombotic structures, thereby promoting metastatic potential [160–162].

In addition to their physical barrier function, NETs may inhibit NK cells through other mechanisms. Transcriptomic analysis of lung tissue from COVID-19 patients suggested that NET-associated genes, including LGALS9 and CEACAM1, could negatively regulate NK cell function, although this remains to be functionally validated [163]. Proteases in the NET can also have effects on NK cells [164, 165]. Using human peripheral blood NK cells, Valayer et al. demonstrated that CG cleaves the activating receptor NKp46 in a time- and concentration-dependent manner [164]. Mass spectrometry identified cleavage sites within the extracellular Ig-like domains of NKp46 [164]. Cleavage of NKp46 by CG severely affects NK cell-mediated responses to NKp46, including IFN-γ production and cell degranulation [164]. Besides, Peng et al. revealed that co-culture of NK cells with human pancreatic cancer cell lines SW1990 and BxPc-3 upregulated MMP-9 expression, and inhibition of MMP-9 with TIMP-1 partially restored NK cell function, including receptor expression and cytotoxicity [165].

NETs on adaptive immune cells

T cell T cells have long been regarded as the major subset of immune cells involved in tumor immunity [166]. Apart from forming a physical barrier to prevent T cell infiltration, NETs also inhibit T cells in the TIME through a variety of mechanisms. T cell exhaustion represents a double-edged sword: while it protects against autoimmunity by restraining aberrant T cell activity, it simultaneously compromises host defense against persistent infections and cancer [167]. Song et al. showed that NET-DNA inhibits CD8+ T cell function and impairs anti-tumor immune responses by binding to transmembrane and coiled-coil domains 6 (TMCO6) [168]. TGFβ1 triggers the generation of NETs, which subsequently attract CD8+ T cells [168]. Engagement of TMCO6 by NET-DNA on CD8+ T cells leads to TCR signaling inhibition and subsequent blockade of NF-κB p65 nuclear translocation [168]. As a result, T cell function is compromised, contributing to HCC progression [168]. This procedure simultaneously boosts apoptosis and secretion of TGFβ1, culminating in a positive feedback loop that constantly triggers NETs production and an immunosuppressive state [168]. Clinical samples confirm that CD8+ T cells expressing TMCO6 exhibit depletion phenotypic features [168]. Moreover, Kaltenmeier et al. revealed that a large proportion of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, which are tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, express multiple inhibitory receptors and exhibit functional and metabolic depletion characteristics in TME enriched with NETs [169]. In mice with CRC liver metastases established by portal vein injection of MC38 cells, a NET-rich microenvironment led to tumor-infiltrating CD8+ and CD4+ T cells upregulating multiple inhibitory receptors, including PD-1, Tim3, and Lag3, and exhibiting functional and metabolic exhaustion characterized by reduced cytokine production and impaired mitochondrial function [169]. DNase I treatment decreased NETosis, suppressed tumor growth, and restored T cell function in vivo [169]. Mechanistically, NETs harbor the immunosuppressive ligand PD-L1, which induces T cell depletion and dysfunction; employing PD-L1-knockout NETs or co-culturing with PD-1-knockout T cells nullified this impact [169]. In addition, the serum levels of soluble PD-L1 and MPO-DNA, recognized as a NET marker, were found to be increased in patients undergoing surgical resection for colorectal liver metastases [169]. In another similar study, Zhou et al. found that PD-L1 carried by NETs suppressed T cell function in a murine model of breast cancer [79]. TRAPs induced the formation of NETs decorated with PD-L1, which inhibited IFN-γ secretion and proliferation of CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells in vitro and in vivo [79]. Employing an anti-PD-L1 antibody to block NETs resulted in the recovery of T cell function, and the combination with DNase I further improved T cell activity [79]. These studies suggest that PD-L1 is present within NETs and exerts an inhibitory effect on T cell function [79, 169]. The immunosuppressive capacity of NETs derives not only from their physical properties but also encompasses their molecular composition, such as PD-L1 [79, 169].

Upon activation, primary CD4 + T cells differentiate into diverse functional subsets, such as Th1, Th2, and Tr1 cells [170]. CD4+ helper T cell (CD4+ Th) subsets contribute to the orchestration of anti-tumor immune responses through diverse mechanisms [171–173]. During the maturation phase of moDC, NETs impair their capacity to provoke T cell proliferation in both autologous and allogeneic environments and modulate the polarization process of CD4+ T lymphocytes by increasing the discharge of Th2 cytokines (IL-5 and IL-13) in addition to inhibiting the release of Th1 and Th17 cytokines (IFN-γ and IL-17) [145]. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) stimulated with NETs exhibited elevated expression of CD4+ T cells and Th1 cytokines [174]. NETs can modulate the vascular endothelium, thereby indirectly influencing T cell recruitment and function [175]. Li et al. found that NETs-stimulated retinal microvascular endothelial cells (RMECs) not only boost CD4+ T cell adhesion and activation but also drive their differentiation into effector T-cells (Teffs), including Th1 and Th17 subtypes [175]. Jia et al. further demonstrated that NETs contribute to CD8+ T cell recruitment and subsequent tissue remodeling [176]. In a murine model of renal fibrosis, NETs promoted macrophage production of CXCL9, CXCL10, and CXCL11, resulting in the infiltration of CD8+ T cells into the injured kidneys [176]. CD8+ T cells produced granzyme B, which induced EMT in tubular epithelial cells and activated fibroblasts in vitro [176]. Consistently, granzyme B-expressing CD8+ T cells were detected in human kidney samples from patients with obstructive nephropathy [176]. In conclusion, multiple studies demonstrate that NETs perform immunomodulatory roles on CD8+ T cells and CD4+ T cells, thus advancing tumor progression.

Anti-tumor Effects of NETs

Conversely, neutrophils and NETs can also exert defensive functions within the tumor microenvironment under certain conditions [9, 177]. Yee et al. demonstrated that neutrophils isolated from brain tumors in a PDZ-binding motif-driven glioblastoma (GBM) mouse model can kill co-cultured tumor cells in vivo by inducing ferroptosis, and that myeloperoxidase (MPO) plays a crucial role in this mechanism [178]. Cui et al. demonstrated that NE proteolytically cleaves CD95 to liberate its death domain, thereby selectively killing multiple cancer cell types while sparing non-cancer cells. NE treatment also elicited a CD8+ T cell-mediated abscopal effect that suppressed distant metastasis [179]. Schedel et al. detected NETs in human primary melanoma tissues, specifically in ulcerated regions where they were induced by necrotic or ulcerative tumor cells [180]. In vitro, NETs adhered to A375 and WM35 cells via integrin-mediated interactions, inhibiting cell migration through physical entrapment [180]. Moreover, co-incubation with NETs induced necrosis in both melanoma cell lines, as shown by increased 7-AAD positivity, an effect reversed by DNase I treatment [180]. Additionally, Muqaku et al. reported that in high-grade serous ovarian cancer (HGSOC), NETosis correlates with non-miliary peritoneal metastases, a pattern associated with a more favorable prognosis compared to miliary spread [181]. Integrated multi-omics and FACS analyses of ascites samples revealed that non-miliary samples exhibited increased levels of NET-associated proteins and eicosanoids indicative of NOX-independent NETosis, along with higher CD8+ T cell infiltration and reduced regulatory T cells [181]. The authors proposed that NETs may contribute to improved survival by modulating adaptive immunity, as reflected by the S100A8/CRP ratio, which correlated with better outcomes across multiple independent patient cohorts [181]. Separately, Li et al. demonstrated that the combination of the glutaminase inhibitor CB-839 and 5-FU induces NET formation in PIK3CA-mutant CRC models [182]. In xenograft models established with HCT116, DLD1, and RKO human CRC cells, the drug combination increased intratumoral NETs, and disruption of NETs by DNase I or neutrophil depletion attenuated its antitumor efficacy [182]. Similar results were obtained in syngeneic models using CMT93 and MC38 cells with Pik3ca E545K knock-in mutations, as well as in a genetically engineered mouse model with colon-specific Pik3ca H1047R mutation (CDX2P-CreERT2 Apcfl/+ KrasLSL–G12D/+ Pik3caLSL–H1047R/+)182. Mechanistically, the drug combination upregulated IL-8 in human CRC cells to recruit neutrophils, while increasing ROS in neutrophils to trigger NETosis. Cathepsin G (CTSG) released from NETs entered CRC cells via RAGE, cleaved 14-3-3ε, induced BAX mitochondrial translocation, and initiated apoptosis [182]. In a phase II clinical trial, increased NET levels in post-treatment tumor biopsies were associated with longer progression-free survival in patients with PIK3CA-mutant CRC [182]. The studies demonstrate that NETs can exert direct antitumor effects. Separately, a recent paradigm shift in the understanding of type 2 immunity has revealed its tumoricidal potential, mediated by eosinophils, ILC2s, and Th2-associated cytokines [183]. Given that NETs have been shown to promote Th2 polarization [145], they may indirectly influence the type 2 immune landscape, with potential implications for antitumor immunity that warrant further investigation.

NETs influence anti-tumor therapies

TME exerts a crucial influence on multiple aspects of cancer progression, with a particular emphasis on treatment resistance [184–187]. As understanding of NETs deepens, mounting evidence underscores their crucial role in conferring resistance to diverse cancer treatments, particularly regarding tumor responses to chemotherapy, RT, and immunotherapy [188, 189].

NET promotes chemotherapy resistance

TME can confer resistance to chemotherapy, the ability for cancer cells to develop resistance to a drug, which in turn affects therapeutic outcomes, most notably in advanced metastatic disease [190]. When chemotherapy takes effect, it triggers cell injury and death, which significantly affects TME, inflammatory cell recruitment, and activity [191, 192]. In this context, neutrophilic infiltration frequently corresponds to a weak reaction to chemotherapy across various types of human cancers [191, 192].

NET strongly correlates with chemotherapy resistance [190]. Employing a mouse model of breast cancer lung metastasis established by 4T1 cells, Mousset et al. demonstrated that chemotherapeutic agents elicit neutrophil infiltration and NET production, events that impair therapeutic efficacy [190] (Fig. 6). Chemotherapy-exposed cancer cells emit IL-1β, a factor that activates the creation of NETs [190]. Inducing chemotherapy resistance requires two NET-linked proteins: integrin αvβ1 and MMP-9, wherein integrin αvβ1 captures latent TGF-β, while MMP-9 cleaves and activates the captured latent TGF-β [190]. Activation of TGF-β fosters EMT of the cancer cells, a process that is associated with chemoresistance [190]. Thus, focusing on the IL-1β-NET-TGF-β signaling pathway could diminish or avoid chemoresistance in the metastatic scenario [190]. At the inflammatory location, the CXCR2 pathway serves as a pivotal element for neutrophil recruitment [193]. Xie et al. demonstrated that loss of EHF, mediated by TP53 mutation, enhanced regulation of CXCR2 neutrophil recruitment and their spatial arrangement, ultimately resulting in resistance to both chemotherapy and immunotherapy in the KPC genetically engineered pancreatic cancer mouse model (LSL-KrasG12D/+, LSL-Trp53R172H/+, Pdx1-Cre) and Ehf-knockout mice [193]. Mechanistically, EHF deficiency induces CXCL1 transcription and enhances CXCR2 neutrophil migration in vitro and in vivo [193]. Blocking CXCL1 or CXCR2 abolished this effect, and depletion of CXCR2 + neutrophils reversed EHF deficiency-induced drug resistance in vivo [193]. In addition, the study by Tamura et al. demonstrated that in the KOC-2S and SKOV3 cell lines cultured in three-dimensional collagen gels, NETs reduced the diffusion of doxorubicin and attenuated drug-induced apoptosis, which was restored by degrading NETs with DNase I [194]. Lin et al. found that DNA liberated from NETs is internalized into cancer cells through endocytosis and resides in the cytoplasm, where it binds anthracycline-based chemotherapeutic drugs and confers resistance to these agents in multiple myeloma and solid tumor models [195]. Various experiments have confirmed that neutrophils and NETs facilitate the induction of chemoresistance. However, the role of NETs in chemotherapy is highly context-dependent and may even exert opposing effects under specific genetic or microenvironmental conditions. A specific phenomenon was observed in CRC, gastric cancer (GC), and high-grade OC: a positive correlation between the increase in neutrophil counts and the improvement in the efficacy of chemotherapy [196–200]. Interestingly, the beneficial effects of chemotherapy-induced NETs against tumors were demonstrated in a study by Li et al. [182]. Joint treatment using the glutaminase inhibitor CB‑839 alongside 5‑FU exerted antitumor effects in part through NETs in mouse models of PIK3CA-mutant CRC, facilitating the attraction of neutrophils to the tumor [182]. The drug combination upregulated IL-8 in cancer cells, recruiting neutrophils into tumors, and increased ROS in neutrophils to induce NET formation [182]. CG from NETs entered CRC cells via RAGE, cleaved 14-3-3 proteins, released BAX, and triggered apoptosis [182]. Disruption of NETs by DNase I or neutrophil depletion attenuated the therapeutic efficacy, indicating that NETs contributed to the antitumor effects of the drug combination [182].

Chemotherapy-induced NETs promote drug resistance in breast cancer lung metastasis. Following chemotherapy, tumor cells killed by the treatment release ATP. This ATP activates the NLRP3 inflammasome via the P2RX7 receptor. NLRP3 then activates Caspase-1, which cleaves the IL-1β precursor into mature IL-1β. IL-1β subsequently promotes NETosis. Concurrently, chemotherapy induces tumor cells to produce CXCL1 and CXCL5, recruiting neutrophils to the lungs. The NET component integrin-αvβ1 captures latent TGF-β and activates it with MMP-9 involvement. TGF-β binds to corresponding receptors on tumor cells, driving EMT via the SMAD signaling pathway. Key molecular hallmarks of this transformation include suppression of E-cadherin expression and upregulation of EMT transcription factors such as Snail, Slug, ZEB1, and ZEB2. This phenotypic shift towards a mesenchymal-like state confers enhanced chemotherapy resistance upon tumor cells

NETs compromise the efficacy of radiotherapy

RT, an established therapeutic modality, employs ionizing radiation to eradicate or suppress malignant cell proliferation [201]. Substantial evidence indicates that targeting NETs modulates RT efficacy, underscoring their critical involvement in the RT response [177, 202–204]. Specifically, in a syngeneic mouse model of bladder cancer established by subcutaneous injection of MB49 cells, Shinde-Jadhav et al. demonstrated that reducing NETs through DNase I treatment, NE inhibition, or genetic ablation of PAD4 enhanced the efficacy of RT [202]. Furthermore, a systematic review by Raymakers et al. reported that in multiple murine models of lung, pancreatic, and soft tissue sarcoma, neutrophil depletion prior to RT improved therapeutic efficacy, suggesting a protumor role of neutrophils in the post-RT TME [205]. Using human neutrophils and HT29 colon cancer spheroids, Teijeira et al. demonstrated that low-dose gamma radiation (0.5–1 Gy) directly induces NETosis, and these NETs impair NK cell and T cell cytotoxicity, thereby compromising antitumor immunity [206]. Importantly, NETs also detrimentally impact post-RT tissue recovery [207]. Park et al. showed that oral administration of the NE inhibitor AZD9668 attenuated NET-induced endothelial and cutaneous damage [207]. This intervention improved endothelial tight junction expression, reduced vascular leakage and inflammation, and consequently accelerated dermal healing following RT [207]. Regarding therapeutic strategies, Sun et al. engineered hybrid nanoparticles comprising DNase I conjugated to gold (DNase I@Au). Aerosol inhalation of these DNase I@Au nanoparticles facilitated efficient delivery in a 4T1-induced lung metastasis mouse model and an A549 human lung cancer xenograft mouse model, enhancing radiosensitization and reducing tumor burden [204]. In CRC, Wang et al. found that PAD4 expression was upregulated in tumor tissues and correlated with poor prognosis [203]. Pharmacological inhibition of PAD4 with GSK484 enhanced radiosensitivity in SW480 and HCT116 cells in vitro and in a murine xenograft model in vivo, while also suppressing NET formation [203]. Conversely, RT-induced IL-1β augments neutrophil recruitment to the TME and NETosis via CXCL chemokine upregulation, fostering a more immunosuppressive TME [205]. Collectively, these findings indicate that targeted therapeutic strategies against NETs, combined with RT, may significantly enhance therapeutic efficacy.

NETs drive immunotherapy resistance

Cancer immunotherapy aims to amplify the key capability of the immune system to identify and eliminate cancer cells [208, 209]. NETs are deemed to have a significant impact on the regulation of tumor and immune cell behavior. Importantly, the immunosuppressive TME formed by NETs hinders the anticancer efficacy of immunotherapy, consequently reducing effectiveness across various therapeutic approaches [67]. Building on this, Zhou et al. discovered that tumor-associated macrophage-derived extracellular vesicles (TAM-EVs) formed an immunosuppressive microenvironment in a mouse model of colorectal liver metastases (CRLM), consequently inhibiting anti-PD-1 immunotherapy [210]. This environment is characterized by increased tumor stem cell potential alongside abundant neutrophil NET formation [210]. Mechanistically, TAM-EVs carry fibrinogen-like 2 (FGL2), which binds to FCGR2B on tumor cells and activates a p-STAT3/IL-1β positive feedback loop that promotes cancer cell stemness [210]. IL-1β, in turn, mediates crosstalk between cancer cells and neutrophils, driving NET formation [210]. Notably, IL-1β blockade reduced NETs and synergized with anti-PD-1 therapy in this model, suggesting a potential translational strategy for CRLM [210]. Similarly, Ye et al. demonstrated better therapeutic efficacy by synergistically promoting anti-PD-L1 therapy using transformable iron nanochelators in combination with PAD4 inhibitors in a murine model of breast cancer [211]. Meanwhile, Feng et al. showed that MPO was significantly upregulated in colorectal adenocarcinoma (COAD) patient samples, with its expression levels showing remarkable correlations with tumor malignancy and clinical outcomes [212]. Spatial analysis using multiplex immunohistochemistry further revealed a close association between MPO and both Tregs and PD-1 + Tregs, implicating MPO in shaping the TIME [212]. Complementing these findings, in an orthotopic pancreatic cancer mouse model established by implantation of KPC-derived tumor cells, Zhang et al. found that IL-17 blockade inhibited neutrophil recruitment and NETosis, thereby enhancing the efficacy of IL-17/PD-1 combination therapy [213]. Yang et al. subsequently illustrated a NET-mediated immune escape pathway promoting HCC progression: NETs upregulate CD73 expression through NF-κB pathway activation and promote Tregs infiltration to mediate immune escape in mouse models established by subcutaneous implantation of Hepa1-6 cells and hydrodynamic plasmid transfection, as well as in HCC cell lines Hepa1-6, HCCLM3, HepG2, and human HCC organoids [214]. Beyond molecular mechanisms, NETs form a physical barrier that fosters immunotherapeutic insensitivity and resistance by blocking contact between immunocytotoxic cells and tumor cells [215, 216]. Additionally, NETs inhibit anti-tumor immunotherapy by disrupting metabolic pathways [217]. Specifically, in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) patients, NETs form a microdomain where histone protease S (CTSS) cleaves human arginase (hARG1) into a distinct molecular form that possesses enhanced enzymatic activity at physiological pH, which potently regulates L-arginine degradation and inhibits T lymphocytes [217]. Within NET-enriched TMEs, CD4+ and CD8+ T cells express inhibitory receptors (PD-1, T-cell immunoglobulin mucin molecule 3 (Tim-3), and lymphocyte activation gene 3 (LAG-3)) while exhibiting functional and metabolic depletion phenotypes [218]. Moreover, NETs regulate the gene expression profile of initial CD4+ T cells through TLR4, promoting their differentiation into Tregs [218]. Collectively, NETs effectively attenuate the cytotoxicity of NK cells and CD8+ T cells against tumor cells, significantly undermining immunotherapy efficacy [218]. Summarily, previous findings have disclosed that NETs not only provide novel potential biomarkers for immunotherapy prognosis but also emerge as promising targets to assist both treatment and prognosis prediction [219–222], as exemplified by creating NET-related stratification systems.

How to target NET

NETs intricately participate in diverse aspects of tumor progression and significantly influence antitumor therapy and mortality in cancer patients [223]. Using specific inhibitors to target NETs could enhance tumor control and yield synergistic benefits when combined with existing therapeutic strategies [224]. Therapeutic Agents Targeting NETs in Clinical Development

Preventing NETosis

NET Components

PAD4 inhibitor PAD4 is an essential enzyme in ROS-dependent NETosis, and its inhibition reduces circulating NET levels, with beneficial effects on tumor progression, metastasis, and anticancer therapy [203, 211]. researchers applied the PAD4 inhibitor YW3-56 to counteract the generation of NETs triggered by sonodynamic therapy (SDT) to inhibit cancer cell proliferation and enhance metastatic tumor efficacy [225]. By functionalizing YW3-56 onto amorphous CoW-layered double hydroxide (a-CoW-LDH) nanosheets, the multifunctional nanoformulation a-LDH@356 was designed to synergize SDT with immunotherapy [225]. After loading YW3-56, a-LDH@356 reduced the up-regulation of citrullinated histone H3 and NET release under ultrasound irradiation, which synergistically enhanced the anti-tumor metastasis effect [225]. In the 4T1-established breast cancer model, the combined application of a-CoW-LDH and YW3-56 elevated the proportion of immune-activated cells, induced M1 macrophage polarization, and stimulated the anti-tumor response; and under ultrasound, it reduced the expression of PD-1 in immune cells to inhibit immune escape, which not only prevented the progression of primary tumors with a tumor suppression rate of 69.5%, but also effectively inhibited tumor metastasis by minimizing the number of lung metastatic nodules.

NE inhibitor NE is a key enzymatic component of NETs that not only promotes inflammation but also drives tumor progression by remodeling the TME and facilitating metastasis [127]. The specific NE inhibitor sivelestat has been shown to effectively block this process [226]. In an in vitro study using primary human neutrophils, Quan et al. demonstrated that treatment with sivelestat-containing nanoparticles (pH/ROS@(PTX/SIV)) markedly inhibited PMA-induced NET formation [226]. This inhibitory effect was further validated in vivo in an orthotopic 4T1 mouse model of triple-negative breast cancer, where the same formulation significantly reduced the expression of NET markers (MPO and NE) within tumor tissues, indicating effective suppression of tumor-associated NETosis [226]. Mechanistic insights into NE inhibition are also provided by Liu et al. in a murine model of renal ischemia-reperfusion injury [227]. Although studied in a non-oncological context, their work confirms that sivelestat treatment leads to a significant decrease in the expression of canonical NET markers, including CitH3 and MPO, directly linking NE inhibition to the suppression of NET formation [227]. To overcome the pharmacological limitations of sivelestat, such as its short half-life, Tang et al. engineered a biomimetic nanoplatform (HM@ST/TeTeLipos) for targeted NE inhibition in a murine model of ischemic stroke (tMCAO) [228]. Treatment with HM@ST/TeTeLipos effectively suppressed NETosis in the ischemic brain, as reflected by reduced levels of the NET marker H3Cit and diminished extracellular DNA fiber deposition [228].

MMP-9 inhibitor MMP-9 has a critical effect on tumor cell invasion and metastasis; thus, the design and synthesis of MMP-9 inhibitors represent a highly promising area of research [229–232]. Rotenberg et al. designed highly specific MMP-9 inhibitors modelled on TIMP2s [233]. Earlier work enhanced the specificity but reduced the affinity of N-TIMP2 for MMP-9 [233]. To address this, researchers screened a library of N-TIMP2 mutants through yeast surface display technology, ultimately selected two mutants for expression, purification, and inhibitory activity testing [233]. This strategy generated two variants with superior affinity and selectivity for MMP-9 [233]. The most effective mutant, REY (carrying N14R, S69E, and A70Y substitutions), demonstrated a 2-fold greater affinity for MMP-9 than wild-type N-TIMP2 and up to 1.1 × 10⁴-fold enhanced selectivity relative to other MMP isoforms [233]. In MDA-MB-231 triple-negative breast cancer cells, REY markedly reduced invasion and proliferation at nanomolar concentrations without cytotoxicity, establishing it as a viable candidate for MMP-9-targeted cancer therapy [233]. Separately, Li et al. demonstrated that MG-3C works by directly suppressing MMP-9, thereby influencing the EGFR-STAT3 signaling pathway in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), which induces G2/M phase cell cycle arrest, inhibits proliferation, and triggers cell death [232]. Notably, when administered subcutaneously or orally, MG-3C substantially reduced tumor growth in NSCLC xenograft models with the T790M mutation and showed excellent in vivo biosafety [232]. These outcomes imply that MG-3C may serve as a promising therapeutic option for NSCLC cases involving the T790M mutation [232].

MPO inhibitor MPO not only plays a crucial role in NETosis, but excessive production of MPO-derived oxidants is harmful, especially in inflammatory diseases, where they promote inflammation and oxidative stress by oxidising biomolecules [234]. MPO deficiency or the use of inhibitors can alleviate inflammation and tissue damage [234]. Employing a murine model of atherosclerosis featuring a C-terminal mutation in scavenger receptor class B type 1 (SR-BI) combined with Ldlr knockout, Shamsuzzaman et al. administered the MPO inhibitor AZM198 to these mice [235]. The treatment yielded significant benefits: survival rates increased by over 90%, while the occurrence of plaque rupture, myocardial infarction, and stroke decreased by more than 60% [235]. Additionally, circulating high-sensitivity cardiac troponin I levels were reduced, and the formation of NETs within lesions was diminished [235]. MPO inhibitors not only reduce NET formation but also reduce the risk of tumor-related cardiovascular events [235–238].

PR3 inhibitor Kreideweiss et al. demonstrated that BI 1,291,583 covalently and reversibly binds to human cathepsin C (CatC), resulting in selective and complete suppression of its enzymatic activity [239]. Specifically, in a mouse model exposed to LPS challenge, this inhibition manifested as concentration-dependent reduction in NE-activated U937 cells and dose-dependent significant decrease in NE and PR3 activity within neutrophils from bronchoalveolar lavage fluid [239]. Beyond pharmacological inhibition, PR3 activity is also subject to endogenous regulation [240]. Antitrypsin (AAT) functions as the primary physiological inhibitor of PR3, and mechanistic studies using human neutrophils have shown that AAT blocks PR3 binding to its cell surface receptor CD177 [240]. This interaction reduces the availability of neutrophil surface antigens targeted by PR3-ANCA autoantibodies, thereby attenuating a key step in autoimmune neutrophil activation.

Gasdermin D inhibitor

GSDMD, a pore-forming protein, has emerged as a critical executor of NET release [241–243]. Silva et al. demonstrated that GSDMD is functionally active in neutrophils from septic patients and in mice subjected to cecal ligation and puncture (CLP) [244]. Genetic deletion of GSDMD or its pharmacological inhibition abrogated NET formation, reduced multiple organ dysfunction, and improved survival [244].

Several inhibitors of GSDMD have been identified, acting through either direct targeting of GSDMD or modulation of its upstream activators [245]. Hu et al. showed that disulfiram, a drug used for alcohol dependence, covalently modifies the Cys191/Cys192 residues of human and mouse GSDMD, thus blocking pore formation without affecting upstream inflammasome activation or cytokine processing [245]. In mice challenged with LPS, disulfiram treatment significantly reduced mortality, cytokine release, and organ damage [245]. Similarly, Zhang et al. reported that ivermectin, an antiparasitic drug, directly binds GSDMD and suppresses its oligomerization, thereby inhibiting cathepsin B-induced NET formation [246]. In mice bearing orthotopic B16F10 tumors, ivermectin administration suppressed lung metastasis, accompanied by reduced serum NET markers and increased tumor-infiltrating CD8 + T cells [246]. Other strategies target upstream pathways that drive GSDMD activation [247, 248]. In an orthotopic model of 4T1 tumors in mice, Miao et al. demonstrated that mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mROS) promote GSDMD-dependent NETosis [247]. Treatment with the GSDMD inhibitor LDC7559, which blocks GSDMD downstream of mROS, suppressed lung metastasis, reduced myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), and increased CD8 + T cell infiltration [247]. Using a murine model of HCC associated with a high-fat diet, Pan et al. showed that the PPARα agonist bezafibrate activates PPARα, which in turn inhibits cGAS-STING and NF-κB signaling, leading to reduced GSDMD expression and subsequent suppression of NET release, ultimately resulting in enhanced infiltration of cytotoxic lymphocytes into tumor tissues [248].

NET-related receptors

CXCR1/2 inhibitor CXCR1/2 antagonists prevent neutrophil chemotaxis and lower the neutrophil levels within tumors [62, 216, 249]. In human pancreatic cancer cell lines, including MIAPaCa-2, KP4, and BxPC3, Nogi et al. demonstrated that gemcitabine treatment induces IL-8 secretion, which in turn activates neutrophils via CXCR1/2, leading to the formation of chemotherapy-induced NETs [249]. Pharmacological inhibition of CXCR1/2 with navarixin suppressed this process in vitro [249]. In a subcutaneous tumor model established with MIAPaCa-2 cells in mice, combining navarixin with gemcitabine reduced intratumoral NET accumulation and enhanced treatment efficacy [249]. Using an IL-8-humanized mouse strain bearing intracranial GL261 tumors, Liu et al. showed that reparixin, another CXCR1/2 inhibitor, slowed tumor growth and improved survival in both IL-8-humanized and wild-type mice [250]. Reparixin treatment reduced MDSCs in tumors, including those induced by anti-PD-1 therapy, and combination with PD-1 blockade further decreased Tim-3 and PD-1 expression on T cells [250]. In particular, IL-8 antibodies neutralise IL-8 in a manner equivalent to blocking its receptors CXCR1 and CXCR2, thereby enhancing T cell-mediated antitumor immunity via eliminating inhibitory MDSCs [250].

Disrupting the NET structure

DNase DNase can cleave NETs, which are then degraded intracellularly by macrophages [251]. The aggregation of NETs hinges on the hydrolytic activity of DNase I and DNase II towards DNA, and the proactive phagocytosis of NETs by macrophages mandates a preliminary treatment of NETs with DNase I [251]. To achieve precise delivery of DNase I in clinical applications, Yang et al. developed a novel biomimetic DNase I delivery system (DCNV) that utilizes CXCR2-overexpressing cell nanovesicles to mimic the inflammatory chemotaxis of neutrophils, thereby enhancing drug accumulation [252]. Additionally, they employed bioorthogonal chemistry to immobilise DNase I, enabling it to exhibit significant enzymatic activity during NET degradation [252]. The unstable nature and rapid clearance of DNase-I represent significant limitations to its clinical application [253]. Wang et al. developed polymer nanocatalysts by immobilising DNase I on the surface of polymer nanoparticles, thereby enhancing the stability and prolonging the activity of DNase I while minimising toxicity, ultimately improving delivery and therapeutic efficacy [253].

Concluding remarks

NETs, as key regulators of the TME, drive tumor initiation and progression by means of multiple mechanisms. From DNA damage induction at the tumor initiation stage, to angiogenesis promotion and EMT activation during metastasis, to establishing pre-metastatic niches and facilitating CTC engraftment, NETs form a complex pro-tumor network. NETs also deeply engage in immune regulation, influencing innate and adaptive immunity to create an immunosuppressive microenvironment. Additionally, NETs significantly impact the efficacy of various anti-tumor therapies. Therapeutic strategies targeting NETs are showing multi-dimensional breakthroughs: basic research has revealed that PAD4 inhibitors can block the core pathways of NETosis, while DNase I delivery systems enhance the therapeutic window by precisely degrading NETs.

Despite these advances, critical questions remain regarding the contextual duality of NETs. While pro-tumor NETs are typically associated with chronic inflammation and immunosuppression, anti-tumor NETs have been documented in specific tumor contexts where they contribute to tumor cell clearance. The determinants governing this duality, including tumor genetics, NET composition, and microenvironmental factors, remain poorly defined. Furthermore, limitations in delivery dosage, precision, and safety of NET-targeting drugs hinder their clinical translation. In order to achieve clinical translation of neutrophil and NET-related research, integrating multi-omics analysis, organoid models, and artificial intelligence prediction is expected to facilitate breakthroughs across the entire chain of NET regulation[254, 255]. Ultimately, resolving the contextual duality of NETs will be essential to decipher when these structures become friend versus foe, and whether we can therapeutically tip the balance toward tumor suppression.
