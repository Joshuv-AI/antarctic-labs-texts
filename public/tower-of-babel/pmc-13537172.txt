Introduction

The quality and circulation efficiency of surgical consumables are directly tied to patient safety, infection control, and healthcare costs. Between the Central Sterile Supply Department (CSSD) and the Operating Room (OR), consumables move through a closed-loop process—sterilization packaging, distribution, intraoperative use, postoperative recovery, and reprocessing—that involves multiple steps and frequent cross-departmental information exchange. Traditional management models, however, are often characterized by a “heavy use, light traceability” orientation. CSSD staff find it difficult to obtain real-time OR consumption data, while OR nurses must devote considerable time to manual counting, requisitioning, and billing, leading to item loss, expiration waste, and billing discrepancies.1 This coordination gap is particularly pronounced in China’s tertiary hospitals, where surgical volumes have grown at 8–10% annually, with pre-digitalization studies reporting expiration waste rates of 3–5% and billing error rates exceeding 2% for high-value implants.1,2 These figures underscore the urgency of reform.

Digital technologies have recently offered new pathways for addressing these challenges. Unique Device Identification (UDI) enables full lifecycle traceability through a “one item, one code” system.3 Radio Frequency Identification (RFID)-enabled smart cabinets support automated batch sensing and real-time inventory tracking.4 The Supply-Processing-Distribution (SPD) model integrates intra-hospital logistics with clinical workflows.5 Despite initial successes in pilot implementations, current practices remain largely characterized by technology stacking: CSSD traceability systems, OR billing modules, and SPD platforms often operate independently, with inconsistent data standards and incompatible interfaces. Consequently, information chains frequently break at critical nodes—distribution, intraoperative consumption, and recovery confirmation—undermining the value of closed-loop digital collaboration and perpetuating manual workarounds.2

Several theoretical frameworks could potentially explain the CSSD-OR coordination problem. Actor-Network Theory would emphasize the heterogeneous network of human and non-human actors, while Diffusion of Innovation theory would focus on technology adoption dynamics. However, these frameworks tend to privilege either technological or social factors. Information Ecology Theory was selected for this study because it offers a more comprehensive lens that simultaneously accounts for information flows, technological tools, human actors, and institutional environments as interdependent and co-evolving elements—a requirement essential for understanding the multifaceted nature of cross-departmental coordination in healthcare settings.6,7 It posits that any information system is a dynamic ecosystem comprising four interdependent elements—information, technology, human actors, and environment—whose synergy is essential for effective functioning.6–8 Applying this lens to CSSD-OR consumable management requires re-examining cross-departmental information flow, role responsibilities, and policy instruments such as UDI mandates and DRG/DIP reforms—the latter being specific to the Chinese healthcare context. This enables systematic identification of “information chain ruptures” and repair through unified coding, data middleware, and responsibility chain design. Furthermore, to capture the organizational and inter-organizational dynamics involved, this paper later integrates Supply Chain Integration (SCI) theory and Socio-Technical Systems (STS) theory, which respectively address cross-departmental process coordination and the mutual optimization of technical and social subsystems.9,10

Against this backdrop, the present article adopts Information Ecology Theory as its analytical framework, focusing on the CSSD-OR consumable collaboration interface. It proposes a conceptual framework for CSSD-OR consumable collaborative management, drawing on the literature to support the framework’s theoretical foundations and empirical grounding. The framework centers on an information platform with RFID/UDI dual-code fusion as the sensing layer and SPD as the business backbone, and examines implementation challenges and strategies. The aim is to provide a theoretical reference and practical roadmap for hospitals to move beyond technology stacking toward ecological collaboration.

Methods

Our article presents a conceptual review and proposes a theoretical framework for CSSD-OR consumable collaborative management based on information ecology theory. Given the conceptual nature of this work, we adopted a narrative review approach to synthesize the literature and develop an integrated analytical framework.

Literature Search Strategy

We conducted a literature search in PubMed, Web of Science, CNKI (China National Knowledge Infrastructure), and Wanfang databases for publications up to March 2026. The search strategy combined keywords related to three domains: (1) surgical consumables/medical consumables/sterile supply; (2) information management/digital management/traceability; and (3) operating room/CSSD/central sterile supply. Boolean operators (AND/OR) were used to combine terms within and across domains. Additional references were identified by hand-searching the reference lists of included articles and reviewing citations in relevant systematic reviews.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

We included peer-reviewed journal articles, conference proceedings, and official policy documents published in English or Chinese that addressed digital technologies (UDI, RFID, SPD, blockchain, machine learning, cloud computing) in the context of hospital consumables management, with particular attention to studies involving CSSD-OR coordination. We excluded studies that focused solely on clinical outcomes without addressing management processes, as well as non-peer-reviewed opinion pieces and commercial white papers.

Synthesis Approach

Given the conceptual and interdisciplinary nature of this review—spanning information science, supply chain management, and healthcare operations—we did not apply formal quality assessment or meta-analytic synthesis. Instead, we extracted and synthesized findings relevant to four thematic areas: (1) the application of information ecology theory to healthcare settings; (2) the current state of digital technologies in CSSD-OR consumable management; (3) the identification of information chain ruptures and their root causes; and (4) proposed solutions and implementation challenges. The synthesis was interpretive and theory-driven, aiming to integrate disparate evidence into a coherent analytical framework.

Mapping of the Four Elements of Information Ecology in CSSD-OR Consumables Collaborative Management

Information Elements

In an ideal state, CSSD-generated data (sterilization batch numbers, UDI codes, sterilization parameters, biological monitoring results, distribution records) and OR-generated data (consumables requisitions, intraoperative usage records, patient IDs, billing status, recycling lists) would flow bidirectionally in real time. In practice, however, over 60% of CSSDs cannot automatically receive OR consumption data, and ORs cannot query CSSD sterilization progress in real time, resulting in “segmented recording and independent management”.2

Technical Elements

Key CSSD technologies include UDI labels, RFID tags, channel doors, and traceability terminals; OR technologies include RFID smart cabinets, handheld scanners, HIS billing interfaces, and surgical scheduling systems. Information ecology demands these tools be interconnected through data middleware—for example, RFID cabinet retrieval should simultaneously trigger CSSD replenishment signals, HIS billing requests, and inventory deductions. Yet current deployments remain fragmented, with RFID cabinets often relying on offline or semi-automatic synchronization, causing delays and errors.3

Human Elements

CSSD personnel (disinfectors, packaging nurses, distribution nurses) and OR personnel (scrub nurses, circulating nurses, surgeons) occupy distinct information niches. The primary barrier is unclear responsibility and non-standard operations—for instance, when intraoperative consumables are used without timely scanning, CSSD records remain inaccurate, and blockchain-based traceability fails without digital evidence at each node.11

Environmental Factors

Policy drivers such as the 2026 UDI mandate2 and DRG/DIP payment reforms12 create external pressure for integration, alongside hospital infection control standards. However, many hospitals still respond with compliance reporting rather than embedding these requirements into daily workflows. CSSD-OR systems often provide only month-end summaries, failing to deliver the case-level real-time cost data that DRG payment requires.

Analysis of Information Flow Obstacles and Chain Break Nodes in Two Departments

The theory of information ecology holds that the smooth flow of information is the “energy flow” of system health. In the collaborative management of consumables between the disinfection supply center and the operating room, the ideal information flow should have three characteristics: real-time (the sterilization status of CSSD and the consumption data of the operating room can be immediately and bidirectionally visible), accuracy (UDI/batch number, patient ID, billing item matching), and closed-loop (every link from “sterilization packaging” to “recycling and reprocessing” is recorded and traceable). However, a large number of literature and current situation reports indicate that there are systematic barriers and broken links in the information flow between the two departments at multiple key nodes. This section summarizes these obstacles into three typical types of broken chain nodes based on existing research.

“Issuing Receiving” Node: Status Unknown and Information Lag

This node covers CSSD sterilization package distribution, OR receipt, and storage in smart cabinets. Ideally, CSSD should track each package’s location and sterilization status, while OR should query batch and expiration data in real-time. However, literature reports that over 60% of ORs cannot query CSSD sterilization status in real-time, often requiring phone confirmation—a direct consequence of isolated CSSD and OR systems lacking automatic data synchronization.5 In addition, Zhang Peng et al1 pointed out that the traditional management model “emphasizes clinical practice and neglects management”. CSSD is only responsible for shipping according to application forms and does not track whether consumables have actually entered the operating room or been returned, resulting in long-term discrepancies between distribution records and actual inventory. This information lag not only leads to blindness in CSSD production planning (such as unnecessary packaging for repeated sterilization), but also increases the workload of operating room nurses for verification.

“Usage Billing” Nodes: Operational Gaps and Ambiguous Responsibilities

This is the most reported problem in the literature and the most direct disruption node for clinical impact. This node involves operating room nurses retrieving consumables from smart cabinets/shelves, unpacking and using them during surgery, associating patient information, and completing billing. In an ideal state, the retrieval action should automatically trigger inventory deduction, billing generation, and CSSD consumption feedback. However, multiple studies have pointed out that although many hospitals have deployed UDI scanning or RFID smart cabinets, the phenomenon of “systematic but still manual” is widespread.

The specific manifestations of chain breakage include: firstly, missed or incorrect scanning. In emergency situations during surgery, hand washing or itinerant nurses often use consumables first and make up for them after surgery, resulting in omissions and errors. The information technology process has increased the additional operational burden on nurses, making it difficult to strictly implement during peak surgical periods. Secondly, information cannot be automatically associated with patients. Existing systems are often able to record “a certain consumable being taken away”, but cannot automatically bind it with the patient ID of the surgery, and still require manual input or secondary confirmation, which can easily lead to billing errors or traceability disruptions. Thirdly, billing and consumption are not synchronized. The original reference points out that due to incompatible interfaces between HIS, SPD, and intelligent cabinet systems, consumable usage records cannot automatically generate billing items, resulting in financial discrepancies of “used but not billed” or “billed but not used”.

While many studies report positive outcomes, several implementation failures have also been documented. For instance, a tertiary hospital in eastern China deployed RFID smart cabinets in 2023, but after six months, the discrepancy rate between system records and actual inventory remained at 15%, and over 40% of nurses reported that the system increased rather than reduced their workload.2 The root cause was identified as the absence of HIS interface integration—the smart cabinet could record retrievals but could not automatically synchronize with billing or inventory systems, requiring dual manual entry. This case demonstrates that technology deployment without simultaneous system integration and workflow redesign is unlikely to achieve the anticipated benefits. Similarly, another study reported that a UDI scanning system implemented in a surgical center achieved only a 60% compliance rate six months post-launch, primarily due to perceived time pressure during surgery and lack of performance incentives.1 These negative cases, though less frequently highlighted in the literature, provide critical lessons that complement the positive findings and underscore the importance of socio-technical alignment.

“Recycling Reprocessing” Node: Feedback Deficiency and Quality Blind Spot

This node refers to the process in which the operating room returns used (or unused but unpacked) instrument packages and consumables residues to CSSD for cleaning, disinfection, sterilization, and repackaging. The ideal information flow should include: the type and quantity of returned packages, whether there is any special contamination (such as blood, sharp instrument damage), abnormal feedback during use (such as packaging damage, indicator cards not changing color), etc. CSSD adjusts the cleaning and sterilization plan based on this information and provides real-time feedback on the reprocessing progress (such as completed sterilization and pending release) to the operating room.

However, literature shows that this link is often the most overlooked “blind spot” in the information flow. The instrument bags returned from the operating room often have no abnormal usage records, and CSSD can only be processed according to the usual procedures. When sterilization fails or biological monitoring is positive, it is difficult to determine whether it is due to incomplete cleaning, improper loading, or contamination introduced during use due to the lack of information on the usage process. Bao Guochun et al5 also mentioned in their discussion of the SPD model that the recycling and traceability of high-value consumables currently rely heavily on paper documents and have not achieved a digital closed-loop. Xie Yanping and Wang Yonggang further pointed out that even with UDI codes, if the recycling process does not scan the code or record status information, the “last mile” of blockchain traceability will be broken.

The disconnection of this node not only increases the risk of infection control, but also causes resource waste: for example, some uncontaminated but opened consumables are directly discarded due to the inability to confirm their status; CSSD cannot predict the demand rhythm of the operating room and can only sterilize at a fixed frequency, resulting in some packages being idle and expired.

Ecological Explanation of Chain Break: Imbalance Between Information, Technology, Humans, and Environment

From the perspective of information ecology, the three chain breaks above are not isolated technical failures but manifestations of imbalance among the four elements. Information elements lack unified coding between departments (sterilization batch numbers in CSSD vs patient IDs in the OR). Technical systems remain heterogeneous and interface-closed, preventing automatic data flow. Human actors lack rigid operational standards and performance incentives that encourage precise recording. Environmental pressures—UDI mandates and DRG/DIP reforms—have not yet been translated into binding hospital requirements. The combined imbalance of these elements has created a widespread dilemma characterized by fragmented information chains, blurred responsibility chains, and uncontrolled risk chains.2

Integrated Analysis Framework from the Perspective of Information Ecology

Although element mapping has been carried out for SCCD-OR collaborative management based on the four elements of information ecology, and three typical types of disconnected nodes have been summarized and analyzed, these analyses are still limited to the level of problem diagnosis. To further explain the underlying mechanisms of chain breakage and guide future improvement directions, this section integrates supply chain integration theory and social technology system theory on the basis of information ecology theory, and constructs a multi-level integrated analysis framework.

Theoretical Basis of the Framework

The theory of information ecology emphasizes that the healthy operation of information systems relies on the dynamic collaboration of four elements: information, technology, humans, and the environment. This theory provides a fundamental analytical unit for CSSD-OR collaborative management: any chain break can be attributed to the absence of a certain element or an imbalance between elements. However, the explanatory power of this theory for cross departmental resource dependency relationships and organizational behavior in the process of technology implementation is limited, and supplementary theories need to be introduced.

The Supply Chain Integration (SCI) theory holds that information sharing, process coordination, and performance linkage between supply chain nodes are key to improving overall efficiency.9 Introducing this theory into the CSSD-OR scenario means that the flow of consumables between two departments should be viewed as an internal supply chain, achieving information integration (real-time data exchange), process integration (packaging distribution use recycling closed-loop design), and performance integration (linking indicators between the two departments). The Socio Technical Systems (STS) theory advocates that the effective operation of any work system must simultaneously optimize both the technical subsystem and the social subsystem (personnel, processes, culture).13 This theory explains why simply deploying RFID or UDI technology often fails - technological change must be accompanied by adjustments to workflow and performance incentives.

Structure and Elements of the Framework

Based on the above theories, we propose a three-tier integrated analysis framework:

Basic layer (information chain connectivity): Ensures real-time bidirectional flow of sterilization status, inventory, consumption, and recovery data between CSSD and OR via UDI/RFID sensing and unified data standards. This is the prerequisite for the entire framework.

Middle layer (technology-process adaptation): Reconstructs business processes through SPD to align technical tools (smart cabinets, scanning terminals) with frontline workflows, driven by performance incentives (eg, scanning integrity rate). This layer addresses the technical-social subsystem synergy required by STS theory.13

Guarantee layer (institutional-performance synergy): Leverages UDI mandates and DRG/DIP reforms to align cross-departmental performance goals (eg, inventory turnover, billing accuracy) and reduce resource dependence asymmetry through joint management committees.9,14

The Application Value of the Framework

The theoretical contribution of this framework is that it goes beyond the optimization perspective of a single technology or process, systematizing the CSSD-OR collaborative problem into three levels of “information process system”, and clearly indicating that the improvement of each level depends on its underlying foundation. On a practical level, this framework provides hospital managers with a layered diagnostic tool: if a high loss rate of consumables is found, the basic layer (whether the information chain is connected) can be checked first, then the intermediate layer (whether the technology is compatible and performance is linked) can be examined, and finally the guarantee layer (whether institutional pressure is effectively transmitted) can be examined. The framework also provides a testable hypothesis path for subsequent research (for example, “When the information chain is interconnected and performance is linked, the efficiency of RFID smart cabinets is significantly better than isolated deployment”).

Recent extensions of information ecology theory have moved beyond descriptive frameworks toward normative ones. Introne et al proposed a framework for conceptualizing “healthier information ecosystems” by drawing on theories from complex systems and ecological research, arguing that information ecosystems must be evaluated according to human values rather than purely technical metrics.15 This normative turn reinforces the need to assess CSSD-OR information ecosystems not only by technical connectivity but also by their alignment with clinical values such as patient safety, staff workflow efficiency, and institutional accountability.

Beyond theoretical extensions, recent work has begun to translate these concepts into actionable architectural frameworks. Nguyen et al proposed a conceptual integration framework for embedding the sterile supply cycle within broader smart hospital architectures, representing ORs, OR management, and the CSSD as interoperable but autonomous subsystems with an event-driven integration logic for cross-domain data exchange.16 Their five-layer architecture—comprising perception, network, gateway, knowledge, and application layers—offers a complementary lens to the three-tier framework proposed in this paper, reinforcing the importance of layered system design in achieving end-to-end visibility and data-driven coordination.

Key Technical Support and Integration Path

From the perspective of information ecology, the collaborative management of consumables between CSSD and OR requires the construction of a complete technical chain from data collection, platform integration to intelligent decision-making. This section summarizes the technologies of decentralized applications into four key supporting areas based on existing literature, and elucidates their integration logic in the connection between the two departments.

UDI/RFID Dual Code Fusion: CSSD-OR Unique Identification Basis

Unique Device Identification (UDI) is the cornerstone for achieving full lifecycle traceability of consumables. According to the 2026 announcement by the National Medical Products Administration and three other departments, starting from June 1, 2027, all Class II medical devices must implement UDI. UDI adopts international standards such as GS1, which enables full traceability of “one item, one code” from the factory to the patient. In the CSSD-OR scenario, the core value of UDI lies in the fact that CSSD assigns UDI codes (which can be printed as one-dimensional/two-dimensional codes or combined with RFID) to each sterilization package during the packaging process. The operating room can automatically associate patient information, billing items, and sterilization batch numbers by scanning the codes during use.1

However, UDI scanning requires individual operations, which is less efficient in emergency situations in the operating room. Radio frequency identification (RFID) technology provides a supplementary solution. RFID supports batch non-contact reading, and smart cabinets or channel doors can recognize dozens of tags within seconds.3 Integrating RFID tags with UDI codes (ie “dual code fusion”) enables each consumable to have both visual scanning and wireless sensing capabilities: when CSSD is packaged, UDI information is written to the RFID tag, and the operating room intelligent cabinet automatically senses the retrieval action, without the need for manual scanning of each code item.13 The literature shows that the RFID/UDI fusion scheme based on GS1 standard can reduce the error rate of inbound and outbound to below one in 100000.14

In CSSD-OR collaboration, the application nodes of dual code fusion include: batch recording of outbound through RFID channel doors during CSSD issuance; The intelligent cabinet automatically senses the incoming goods when receiving them in the operating room; The cabinet door automatically records the information of the user, time, and consumables when the nurse retrieves them; During postoperative retrieval, RFID handheld terminals are used to count and automatically compare with usage records.5 At present, some tertiary hospitals have deployed such fusion systems between disinfection supply centers and operating rooms, initially achieving a closed-loop system of “scanning and billing, and retrieving and tracing”.

RFID Smart Cabinet and SPD Mode: Intelligent Perception and Automatic Replenishment in Operating Room

RFID intelligent consumables cabinet is a key physical carrier for CSSD-OR connection. The cabinet is integrated with a dense antenna array, which can sense the real-time access actions of consumables inside the cabinet, automatically update inventory, and link access control permission management. Deploying RFID smart cabinets in the operating room can achieve the following functions: designated personnel, designated permission access; Retrieve automatic records and associate patient IDs; generate replenishment requests automatically when inventory falls below the safety threshold; Automatic warning for consumables approaching expiration date. These functions directly solve the “usage billing” disconnection problem.

The SPD (Supply Processing Distribution) model integrates CSSD and operating room logistics from the supply chain level. SPD coordinates internal hospital resources with third-party supplier resources to achieve intensive management of “use first, settle later”. In the CSSD-OR scenario, the specific path of SPD mode is: CSSD dynamically arranges sterilization production based on real-time consumption data in the operating room; Suppliers will automatically replenish according to the SPD platform; Consumables are directly delivered to the intelligent cabinet in the operating room, reducing intermediate inventory links. According to literature reports, after adopting the SPD model, the inventory turnover rate of operating room consumables increased by an average of over 30%, and the expiration loss rate decreased by 50%.4 In addition, the linkage between SPD and RFID smart cabinets can achieve an automatic closed-loop system of “consumption upon retrieval, billing upon consumption, and replenishment triggered upon billing”, greatly reducing manual intervention.

Data Center and Integration Platform: Breaking Down the Barriers Between CSSD and OR Systems

CSSD traceability system, operating room HIS billing system, SPD logistics platform, and HRP financial system are often provided by different vendors, with different data standards and incompatible interfaces, forming a “system island”. The theory of information ecology emphasizes that technological elements must achieve synergy through the platform layer. Therefore, building a unified data center or integrated platform is the key to breaking down barriers.

The core functions of the data center include: unified coding conversion (mapping the sterilization batch number and UDI code of CSSD to the patient ID and billing item code of HIS); Real time data synchronization (using ETL tools or message queues to automatically trigger HIS billing interfaces for intelligent cabinet access records); Data cleaning and quality management (automatic verification of UDI code integrity, RFID reading success rate, interception of abnormal data). Cloud computing technology provides elastic computing and storage capabilities for the data center, supporting data sharing in multi hospital or medical consortium scenarios.17 The literature points out that after adopting cloud platforms, the response speed of hospital asset management can be improved by more than 95%.18

In CSSD-OR collaboration, the integrated platform should be connected to at least three systems: CSSD traceability system (providing sterilization parameters and batch information), operating room material system (providing intelligent cabinet inventory and retrieval records), and HIS (providing patient information and billing confirmation). The platform also needs to provide standardized API interfaces for future access to medical insurance settlement platforms or regional UDI traceability platforms.

Blockchain and Machine Learning: Evidence Traceability and Demand Forecasting

Blockchain technology ensures tamper-proof traceability through distributed ledgers and smart contracts.19 For high-value implants, storing UDI codes, patient IDs, surgeons, and usage times on-chain creates a judicial-level evidence chain, while smart contracts can automate billing reconciliation.11 Machine learning (ML) optimizes demand forecasting and inventory: deep learning methods (LSTM, MTN) reduce prediction errors to 6.3%, and clustering algorithms (K-means) identify usage patterns to guide scientific inventory quotas.2,20

Beyond individual applications, recent systematic reviews have consolidated the evidence base. Zaib et al identified traceability and counterfeit prevention as primary blockchain use cases in pharmaceutical and medical device supply chains.21 Mallick et al found that blockchain-IoT convergence addresses interoperability and data fragmentation challenges in healthcare.22 Tebrizcik et al reported an 85% reduction in documentation errors and audit preparation time shortened from weeks to hours through blockchain-IoT integration.23 Empirically, a Chinese pilot study of an IoT-aided warehouse management system reduced physical inventory time from (840 ± 120) minutes to (12.6 ± 1.3) minutes (p < 0.0001).24

Technology integration ecosystem: UDI/RFID (perception) → data center (platform) → ML/blockchain (intelligence). Only through their collaboration can CSSD-OR achieve full closed-loop management.

Operating Mechanism: CSSD-OR Collaborative Closed-Loop Driven by Information Chain

The theory of information ecology emphasizes that the smoothness of the information chain is the core of achieving system collaboration. The information chain between CSSD and the operating room consists of four links: collection, transmission, processing, and feedback, which in turn connect the “industrial chain” of material circulation, the “risk chain” of risk warning, and the “responsibility chain” of responsibility tracing. This section summarizes the closed-loop operation mechanism and its role in improving the collaborative efficiency between the two departments based on practical cases and technical applications reported in the literature. The flowchart is shown in Figure 2.

The information chain comprises four sequential links—① collection (RFID/UDI automatic sensing), ② transmission (real-time upload via 5G/IoT), ③ processing (ML-based demand forecasting and automatic reconciliation), and ④ feedback (visual dashboard and auto-replenishment). These links drive three collaborative chains: the industrial chain (material flow from CSSD to OR to reprocessing) is driven by collection and transmission; the risk chain (early warnings for stock-out, expiration, and billing discrepancies) is driven by processing; and the responsibility chain (personnel traceability from packaging to usage to recovery) is driven by feedback. Dashed lines indicate specific driving relationships between information chain links and their corresponding outcome chains; the thick solid arrow indicates the overall driving direction from the information chain to the three collaborative chains. The looped arrow indicates the self-reinforcing, closed-loop nature of the information chain.

The Four Links of the Information Chain: From Collection to Feedback

Collection: Two primary methods exist: UDI-based scanning (manual, item-by-item) and RFID-based automatic sensing (batch, non-contact). RFID reduces single-operation time from 15 seconds to under 2 seconds, with a miss rate below 0.1%.4 Collected data include sterilization batch numbers, UDI codes, user identities, retrieval times, and associated patient IDs.

Transmission: Data are transmitted in real time via 5G/IoT to the data center, triggering downstream actions: RFID cabinet retrieval → platform → HIS billing requests, CSSD consumption confirmations, and SPD inventory deductions.6 Standardized interfaces (eg, HL7/FHIR) are essential; some hospitals achieve <5-second latency from retrieval to billing.18

Processing: The data center performs three functions: (1) ML-based demand forecasting using historical consumption and surgical schedules to predict 3–7 day demand and auto-generate replenishment orders; (2) automatic reconciliation comparing smart cabinet records with HIS billing to flag discrepancies; and (3) expiration alerts for consumables within 30 days, with access frozen upon expiry.

Feedback: Information is returned in actionable forms: visual dashboards for real-time inventory and consumption monitoring; auto-replenishment orders when inventory falls below thresholds; and audit trails recording operator, time, and item details for each transaction, enabling full accountability.

Linkage of Three Links: Industrial Chain, Risk Chain, and Responsibility Chain

The connectivity of the information chain directly drives the collaborative operation of the other three links.

Industrial chain (material flow): from CSSD sterilization and packaging → distribution to intelligent cabinets in the operating room → intraoperative retrieval → postoperative recycling → CSSD reprocessing. When the information chain reflects the inventory and consumption status of each node in real time, CSSD can dynamically adjust the sterilization production rhythm based on the actual consumption of the operating room, avoiding “excessive sterilization” or “supply shortage”. According to literature reports, after adopting the information chain driven SPD mode, the shortage rate of consumables in the operating room decreased by about 70%, and the turnover rate of CSSD sterilization kits increased by more than 40%.5

Risk Chain (Security Warning): The risk identification rules embedded in the information chain can provide early warning at multiple nodes. For example, triggering a “out of stock risk” warning when inventory falls below the safety threshold; Trigger the “expiration risk” warning when the expiration date is ≤ 30 days; The inconsistency between the access records of the smart cabinet and HIS billing triggers a “billing risk” warning; The mismatch between the number of recycled packages and the number of distributed packages triggers a “loss risk” warning. These warnings have shifted from passive post verification to active pre interception, significantly reducing the incidence of adverse events.

Chain of Responsibility (Personnel Traceability): Record the responsible person for the entire process of packaging, distribution, retrieval, and recycling of each consumable through UDI/RFID. The literature points out that this digital responsibility chain solves the dilemma of “not being able to find people when problems arise” in traditional management. For example, if the postoperative inventory of a high-value consumable is missing, the system can trace back to the last user, retrieval time, and operating room, and automatically notify the manager.

Typical Application Scenarios: Taking High-Value Implantable Consumables as an Example

Taking a surgery using high-value implants (such as artificial joints) as an example, the closed-loop operation driven by the information chain is as follows:

Collection: When packaging with CSSD, the UDI tag and RFID tag are combined and attached to the outer packaging of the implant, and the batch number and sterilization parameters are entered.

Transmission: Implants are distributed to the RFID smart cabinet in the operating room, and the system automatically records them for storage; During the operation, when the chief surgeon retrieves the equipment, the cabinet door senses and associates the patient ID and operating room number.

Processing: The data center automatically triggers: HIS generates billing items, CSSD system deducts inventory, SPD system generates replenishment requests. At the same time, the blockchain node records the usage information of the implant (patient, doctor, time, UDI code).

Feedback: After surgery, the system automatically compares the retrieval records with the inventory of recycling, and completes the closed-loop after confirming that there are no errors; If the implant is found to be unused but the packaging is damaged during recycling, the system will mark it as abnormal and notify CSSD to re sterilize.

According to literature reports, hospitals that adopt the above-mentioned closed-loop mechanism have reduced the billing error rate of implants from 2.5% to below 0.1%, and the postoperative traceability time has been shortened from several hours to seconds.11

Implementation Challenges and Response Strategies

Literature reports and practice have shown that the digital construction of CSSD and surgical consumables collaborative management faces four core obstacles. This section is based on the theoretical framework of information ecology and briefly summarizes its performance and countermeasures.

System Heterogeneity and Data Silos

The CSSD traceability system, operating room HIS, SPD platform, and financial system are often built by different suppliers at different times, with inconsistent data standards and incompatible interfaces, resulting in frequent breakage of the information chain at the three nodes of distribution reception, usage billing, and recycling reprocessing. Even if RFID smart cabinets or UDI scanning are deployed, if data needs to be manually exported and imported, the closed-loop efficiency will still be greatly reduced.

Response strategy: Establish a hospital level data center and use ETL tools to extract, clean, and synchronize data between heterogeneous systems without the need to replace existing systems; In the procurement contract, it is explicitly required that suppliers provide standardized API interfaces and incorporate interface openness into the evaluation criteria.

Clinical Operational Burden and Cultural Differences

Under the high-pressure environment during surgery, operating room nurses are considered to have additional burdens such as scanning codes item by item and checking expiration dates, resulting in frequent missed or incorrect scans; CSSD nurses focus on sterilization qualification rate as their core performance, but lack proactive attention to the accuracy of operating room consumption information. The difference in professional norms between the two departments creates an endogenous resistance to information sharing.

Response strategy: Adopt RFID batch insensitive reading technology to reduce active scanning operations; Include indicators such as “QR code completion rate” and “billing timeliness rate” into the performance evaluation of both departments, and establish cross departmental collaborative incentives; Carry out hierarchical training to persuade frontline personnel with practical benefits such as “reducing out of stock waiting”.

“Institutional Lag” in the Environment: Poor Policy Transmission and Compliance Costs

The mandatory implementation of UDI (fully implemented in 2027) and DRG/DIP payment reform have put forward clear requirements for the full traceability and real-time cost accounting of consumables,3 but most hospitals still cope with compliance checks in traditional reporting methods and fail to embed policy requirements into daily business processes.12 In addition, there is a lack of normalized joint decision-making mechanism between CSSD and operating rooms, and institutional pressure is difficult to transform into management improvement motivation.

Response strategy: Incorporate UDI data reporting format and DRG cost accounting logic into the system design phase to automatically generate compliance data in daily operations; Establish a CSSD-OR joint management committee to review key indicators such as traceability completeness rate and cost deviation rate on a monthly basis.19

Shortage of Composite Talents and Path Dependence

There is a serious shortage of composite talents who are familiar with clinical consumables management and have knowledge of information technology and supply chain. Hospitals overly rely on suppliers for “turnkey” construction, and after the system goes online, they are unable to operate and iterate internally, gradually falling into a vicious cycle of “system rigidity → decreased experience → decreased willingness to use → reduced investment → talent loss”.

Response strategy: Establish a dedicated position for “consumables data administrator”, select and train data analysis and system operation skills from nursing or medical staff; Adopting an iterative strategy of “small steps, fast running”, quickly achieving results in 1–2 pain point scenarios, striving for sustained investment with actual returns, and breaking path dependence.25

Ecological Logic Summary of Challenge Response

The above four types of challenges correspond to the imbalance of the four elements of information ecology: the mismatch between information and technology, conflicts in human roles, institutional lag in the environment, and the vicious cycle of the system caused by the joint action of the four elements. The core of coping strategies is not a single technological upgrade or process adjustment, but rather “system repair” - breaking through information silos through data platforms, reducing human burden through technological adaptation, reconstructing human behavior through performance collaboration, transforming environmental pressure into endogenous motivation through institutional embedding, and breaking path dependence through talent cultivation. Only through the collaborative evolution of the four elements can the information ecology of CSSD-OR consumables collaborative management truly move towards health and sustainability.

Summary and Prospect

This article proposes a conceptual framework for CSSD-OR consumable collaborative management based on information ecology theory and a synthesis of the literature. The analysis identifies systematic information chain ruptures at three nodes—distribution-receiving, usage-billing, and recycling-reprocessing—attributed to imbalances among the four information ecology elements: information (inconsistent coding), technology (system heterogeneity), human (role conflict), and environment (policy lag). The proposed three-tier framework (foundation: information chain connectivity; middle: technology-process adaptation; guarantee: institutional-performance synergy) integrates UDI/RFID as the perception layer, a data center as the platform layer, and ML/blockchain as the intelligence layer. This architecture can potentially achieve closed-loop integration of material flows, risk warning, and personnel traceability, with the potential of reducing shortage rates, expiration losses, and billing errors.

Implementation of this framework faces four challenges: system heterogeneity and data silos; clinical burden and cultural differences between departments; institutional lag with poor policy transmission; and shortages of interdisciplinary talent with path dependence on suppliers. These challenges confirm that technological deployment alone is insufficient without organizational and institutional adaptation.

The theoretical contribution is the first application of information ecology theory to CSSD-OR consumable management, integrated with Supply Chain Integration (SCI) and Socio-Technical Systems (STS) theories to form a testable three-layer framework. Practically, the effectiveness of digital consumables management depends not only on technology but also on systematic repair of weak links in the information ecology—connecting core information nodes between departments, aligning technology with frontline workflows through performance incentives, and leveraging external policy pressures (UDI mandates, DRG payment reform) to drive institutional change.

As a conceptual review, the framework represents a theoretical proposition requiring empirical validation. Given significant resource variation across hospitals, its generalizability needs testing. Future research should use case studies or quasi-experimental designs to quantify the relationship between information chain connectivity and management performance; explore large language models for intelligent consumables management; extend the scope to regional CSSD-multiple OR collaborations; and link consumables metrics to patient safety outcomes such as surgical site infection rates.
