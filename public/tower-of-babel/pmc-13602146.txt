A multimodal AI biomarker PATH-ORACLE improves prediction of recurrence in stage I lung adenocarcinoma
Authors: O Kilim, O Pipek, Z Sztupinszki, M Diossy, A Prosz, C Naceur-Lombardelli, S Veeriah, D Moore, M Jamal-Hanjani, A Hackshaw, J Fillinger, J Moldvay, I Csabai, C Swanton, Z Szallasi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSurgical resection is the standard treatment for stage I lung adenocarcinoma, in most cases without additional systemic adjuvant treatment. A substantial proportion of stage I cases recur, with a 5-year survival rate <50%. Clinical data suggest that adjuvant treatment, including immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy, may improve survival in such recurrent cases. Previously evaluated predictors, including the International Association for the Study of Lung Cancer (IASLC) grading system applied to histological sections and transcriptomic profiles, have not been sufficiently accurate or consistent for risk stratification or to guide therapeutic intervention. We hypothesized that these diverse diagnostic measurements carry complementary information that may provide higher prognostic power when combined.Materials and methodsWe developed PATH-ORACLE, a multimodal deep learning biomarker built on top of the prospectively validated transcriptomic-based Outcome Risk Associated Clonal Lung Expression (ORACLE) score, with the addition of routine histological sections processed by pretrained foundation models. Predictive performance was assessed in two independent cohorts.ResultsThe histology-only predictor outperformed automated IASLC grading and remained prognostic after adjustment for tumor size, grade and T-stage. PATH-ORACLE exceeded both constituent modalities, predicting 1-year recurrence with an area under the curve of 0.86 and 0.84 in the two independent validation cohorts, and separated high- and low-risk groups using a single prespecified cut-off.ConclusionsExtensive validation will be needed to convert PATH-ORACLE from a prognostic biomarker to a predictive biomarker that could be used to prioritize stage IB patients for adjuvant targeted therapy, chemotherapy, or immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy with or without liquid biopsy-based monitoring.Key words: early-stage lung adenocarcinoma, multimodal deep learning, recurrence prediction

Introduction

Surgical resection with mediastinal lymphadenectomy is the standard treatment for patients with stage I lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD). For stage IA patients, despite the 10%-20% 5-year recurrence rate, no further systemic treatment is currently recommended.1, 2, 3The 5-year recurrence rate for stage IB patients is even higher, up to 35%, but adjuvant chemotherapy is still optional and recommended only for patients that are considered to be high risk based on histological risk factors such as tumor size, tumor grade, lymphovascular invasion (LVI), and visceral pleural invasion (VPI).4 For patients with resected stage IB adenocarcinoma and epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) exon 19 deletion or exon 21 L858R point mutations, adjuvant targeted therapy with osimertinib is recommended.5 Such patients may also benefit from adjuvant atezolizumab after adjuvant chemotherapy.6 Lung cancer relapse is associated with significantly worse clinical outcome and reduced overall survival (OS). An accurate predictor of relapse will allow the close monitoring of high-risk patients by e.g. the genomic analysis of liquid biopsies, and systemic intervention could be initiated with likely long-term clinical benefit.7

Several types of diagnostic data modalities have been evaluated for predicting recurrence in early-stage LUAD. Pathological classification, such as the three-tiered International Association for the Study of Lung Cancer (IASLC) grading system,8 is informative of clinical outcome in early stage I cases.9 The interobserver variability of estimates of the proportion of the various histological subtypes within a given tumor was recently circumvented by an automated image analysis algorithm,10 further confirming the utility of histological classification for predicting recurrence in early-stage LUAD.

Gene expression-based predictors, such as Outcome Risk Associated Clonal Lung Expression (ORACLE)11,12 and RiskReveal (AIM-HIGH),13 have a clinically validated performance of predicting outcome when several stages of LUAD are evaluated together but can be improved for stage I cases. Mutational profiling based on next generation sequencing was also evaluated, with limited utility to predict relapse in stage I LUAD.14 Importantly, although the power of these methods to predict recurrence of stage I LUAD is significant, their accuracy [usually in the range of area under the curve (AUC) 0.6-0.65] does not reach the level that would determine changes in therapeutic interventions.

If the diagnostic information of these different diagnostic methods is complementary, then it is reasonable to assume that their combination may yield a more accurate predictor of clinical relapse. Previously, we successfully tested this assumption in high-grade serous ovarian carcinoma, where the combination of histological images with proteomics yielded an independently validated predictor of response to first-line platinum-based therapy.15

Traditionally, combining histological images with gene expression profiles or genomics is not a trivial task, but the emergence of multimodal deep learning has provided a computationally feasible strategy for combining such disparate diagnostic features.

In this article, we report a multimodal deep learning training of a predictor of recurrence of early-stage LUAD on four independent cohorts covering 710 patients from three geographic regions, from which matched hematoxylin–eosin (H&E) histological images and gene expression measurements were both available. We found that the multimodal deep learning combination we term ‘PATH-ORACLE’ provides a more accurate predictor of relapse in stage I LUAD compared with transcriptomics or histology-based prediction alone.

Methods

Patients and cohorts

This retrospective observational study analyzed 710 patients across four independent cohorts [Clinical Proteomic Tumor Analysis Consortium (CPTAC), KORANYI, The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA)-LUAD, and TRACERx]. We selected only pathological stage IA or IB cases [American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC), Ninth Edition]. Cohorts collected under earlier AJCC editions were reclassified and regraded by senior pathologists according to the ninth edition to ensure consistency. All patients underwent lobectomy with no neoadjuvant therapy.

The primary endpoint was relapse-free interval, defined as the interval from lobectomy to the first documented local, regional, or distant relapse confirmed by clinical, radiologic, or pathologic assessment; patients without recurrence were censored at last disease-free follow-up. The secondary endpoint was OS. Direct comparisons between modalities and models use the same patient subset. Because not all cases carry all modalities, each analysis uses a slightly different subset: the IASLC-versus-whole-slide image (WSI) comparison required grading and imaging, whereas multimodal PATH-ORACLE required matched imaging and transcriptomic data.

Derived histopathological features

PyrAmid pooliNg crOss stReam Attention networK (ANORAK) scores were directly calculated from WSIs using the official openly released pretrained model10 (https://github.com/xi11/AIgrading). The implementation was run on the TRACERx H&E WSIs and compared with published values to ensure consistency. This pipeline was then applied to the remaining slides across the three other cohorts. IASLC grades were provided for a subset of cases by the pathologists who reported on each cohort at the time of diagnosis. The IASLC was defined according to the updated definition.8

Machine learning-based modeling

Each WSI was embedded with the ConchV1.5 patch-level foundation model and pooled into a slide-level representation with the pretrained TITAN transformer. Transcriptomic risk was derived from published prognostic signatures; the three signatures generalizing best on held-out cohorts were retained, alongside a de novo relapse-specific discovery analysis. Binary somatic mutation status [EGFR, KRAS/NRAS, anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK)] was harmonized across cohorts. All predictors used elastic-net-regularized Cox proportional hazards models trained exclusively on TCGA-LUAD and evaluated on the independent TRACERx, CPTAC, and KORANYI cohorts, with every data-dependent transform fit on training folds only and frozen for external validation to prevent leakage. PATH-ORACLE integrates TITAN histopathology embeddings, gene-level transcriptomic features from the ORACLE signature, and clinical covariates by early (feature-level) fusion: the features are concatenated into a single input vector for one elastic-net-regularized Cox proportional hazards model. Model discrimination was quantified by time-dependent area under the receiver operating characteristic curve and Harrell’s concordance index (C-index) with bootstrap 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Full preprocessing, model specification, hyperparameters, gene lists, and validation procedures are provided in Supplementary Methods, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.iotech.2026.101614. Computation was performed on the Francis Crick Institute high-performance computing platform using A100 graphics processing units.

Results

Improving prognostic performance for stage IA/IB LUAD in unimodal settings

Deep learning-based classification of H&E whole-slide images is a more accurate predictor of recurrence than the IASLC grading system

Histological features of LUAD are associated with patient prognosis.9,19 Currently, the updated IASLC grading system can be used for risk stratification from H&E WSIs8 (Figure 1A). This grading system is based on the fifth edition of the World Health Organization classification of five main architectural patterns (Figure 1B) in invasive nonmucinous pulmonary adenocarcinoma: lepidic, acinar, papillary, micropapillary, and solid.20 This system is implemented by pathologists manually by visually estimating the percentage of each pattern in 5%-10% increments.

To establish a baseline, we evaluated the prognostic performance of pathologist-assigned IASLC grades across our validation cohorts. Single pathologist assessments showed consistently weak associations with relapse-free survival across two validation cohorts for which pathologist assessments were available (Figure 1C): TRACERx [n = 95; C-index 0.61, 95% CI 0.49-0.72, log-rank P = 0.067, hazard ratio (HR) 2.40, 95% CI 0.91-6.32] and KORANYI (n = 193; C-index 0.50, 95% CI 0.45-0.56, log-rank P = 0.881, HR 1.04, 95% CI 0.60-1.81). The CPTAC (n = 68) cohort was not analyzed as it was underpowered for this analysis because only 30% of the histopathology slides were graded by a pathologist. These results highlight the prognostic yet still limited predictive power of IASLC grading based on single pathologists’ evaluation in stage IA/IB disease. These findings are consistent with large scale (n = 1443) evaluations of IASLC grading.9

ANORAK addresses key limitations in manual IASLC grading, particularly interobserver variability and inconsistent quantification of growth patterns.10 ANORAK uses pixel-wise segmentation for automated, objective IASLC grading, reducing subjectivity in differentiating lepidic, papillary, and acinar patterns (Figure 1D).10

We applied ANORAK to generate automated IASLC grades across the three validation cohorts CPTAC, TRACERx, and KORANYI. ANORAK-derived grades showed modestly improved prognostic stratification in stage IA/IB LUAD compared with the single pathologist assessment. TRACERx (C-index 0.61, 95% CI 0.50-0.70, log-rank P = 0.102, HR 2.20, 95% CI 0.84-5.78), CPTAC (C-index 0.53, 95% CI 0.50-0.56, log-rank P = 0.490), and KORANYI (C-index 0.53, 95% CI 0.48-0.58, log-rank P = 0.132, HR 1.75, 95% CI 0.84-3.67; Figure 1C). By pooling all validation cases, ANORAK significantly stratified high- and low-risk group cases (log-rank P < 0.05; Figure 1E). Although ANORAK automates the pathologist-defined workflow, its reliance on previously defined histopathological features limits its ability to leverage any other complex morphological patterns that might exist with potential prognostic relevance beyond the IASLC framework.

To check if such patterns exist, we trained a WSI foundation model-based relapse predictor on the stage IA/IB subcohort of TCGA (n = 272). This data-driven approach enables automatic discovery of relapse-related features. WSIs were first patched and embedded at 0.5 μm2 resolution using ConchV1.521 (Figure 1F). These patch-level embeddings were then aggregated into slide-level representations using TITAN22 (Figure 1G; see ‘Materials and Methods’ section), yielding a fixed 768-dimensional vector for each slide (Figure 1H). We applied regularized Cox regression to learn relapse risk directly from censored and uncensored follow-up data in the TCGA-LUAD cohort. We validated the model across the same three independent cohorts (TRACERx, CPTAC, and KORANYI) using a fixed predefined risk cut-off discovered by the model outputs on the training set only.

The WSI foundation model-based predictor significantly outperformed both pathologist-assigned and ANORAK-derived IASLC grades across three independent validation cohorts (Figure 1C). External validation demonstrated robust generalization. For a 1-year cut-off AUC, the validation cohorts reached AUC of CPTAC = 0.659 (n = 48), TRACERx = 0.756 (n = 92), and KORANYI = 0.634 (n = 195). For 2-year cut-off AUC scores, results were 0.75 in CPTAC (n = 30), 0.75 in TRACERx (n = 88), and 0.66 in the KORANYI blind test cohort (n = 193) (Figure 1C). The C-index values were CPTAC = 0.63 (n = 68), TRACERx = 0.67 (n = 95), and KORANYI = 0.58 (n = 193) (95% CIs 0.37-0.76, 0.53-0.79, and 0.50-0.64, respectively). These results demonstrate that the WSI foundation model captures relapse-specific morphological signatures that generalize across institutions, scanners, and patient populations. When pooling all validation cases, this deep learning-based model significantly stratified high- and low-risk group cases (log-rank P < 0.001; Figure 1E and I).

In direct comparisons, the WSI foundation model-based predictor achieved superior prognostic stratification compared with ANORAK IASLC grading (C-index Δ = +0.10; 95% CI 0.03-0.18, bootstrap P = 0.011).

To test whether the WSI model adds prognostic information beyond IASLC grading, we stratified the pooled validation cohort (n = 358) by ANORAK grade then by WSI-predicted risk. Within ANORAK low-risk tumors (grade 1+2), the WSI predictor flagged a high-risk subset with a relapse rate roughly threefold higher than the concordant low-risk subset (19.7% compared with 5.9%), and it further separated risk within high-grade tumors. The model therefore restratifies relapse risk inside histological grade, most strikingly by surfacing a high-risk subgroup among low-grade tumors, capturing fine-grained morphology not encoded in the IASLC framework.

In multivariate Cox regression adjusting for tumor size, grade, and T-stage [pooled across all three validation cohorts TRACERx, CPTAC, and KORANYI with cohort as a stratifying covariate; LVI and VPI were recorded only in TRACERx], the WSI predictor remained an independent predictor of relapse (HR 1.83, 95% CI 1.14-2.92, P = 0.012). The association was essentially unchanged by grade adjustment (grade-free HR 1.93, 95% CI 1.22-3.05, P = 0.005), indicating that the WSI predictor carries prognostic information beyond histological grade.

To interpret what the WSI predictor learned from the WSIs, we took the highest-attention patches across all slides (Figure 2A) from all validation cohorts and clustered their embeddings to find common morphologies23 (Figure 2B). We sent samples of patches from each cluster for pathologist labeling (see Figure 2C and D for examples). Patches were presented to pathologists blinded to their cluster of origin and the model’s risk assignment.

We found that the learned clusters segregate primarily according to microenvironmental and stromal features rather than classical IASLC histological subtypes (Figure 2A-D). The high-risk clusters capture the biological and architectural features of the tumor and its surrounding microenvironment, while the low-risk clusters predominantly reflect the stromal response and the dynamic interaction between tumor and host tissue.

RNA-sequencing analysis—a LUAD stage I relapse-specific ORACLE signature

Transcriptomic markers could improve diagnostic precision in LUAD by identifying molecular subtypes associated with relapse risk and mortality.12,24 These profiles capture gene-expression programs and intracellular signaling states that may not be fully reflected in histopathological images and may contain complementary prognostic information.

The most promising approach is the ORACLE, which additionally takes into account spatial gene expression variability within a tumor.11 This is a prospectively validated 23-gene transcriptomic biomarker trained on TCGA stage I-III cases for OS.12 It stratifies patients with non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC) into high- or low-risk survival categories.

We ran a multicohort validation on the ORACLE signature and seven other transcriptomic signatures published in the last 5 years11,25, 26, 27, 28, 29, 30, 31 to understand how robust these are for stage I cases relapse prediction specifically (Figure 2E). Training on the combined gene set from the three best performing gene signatures from independent studies (ORACLE,12 Zhao,26 and Carr25) improved prognostic performance. Sparse Cox regression identified eight genes (LRP12, ANLN, PNP, PSG1, UPK1B, XBP1, PEBP4, and EDN3) that provided some signal for stage IA/IB relapse risk (Figure 2F).

No gene was significantly prognostic independently. Several of these genes have reported roles in LUAD progression relevant to recurrence: ANLN promotes proliferation and predicts shorter survival,26,32 LRP12 promoter hypermethylation and resulting silencing is associated with poor outcome and platinum resistance in NSCLC,33 and XBP134,35 and PEBP4 support invasion, epithelial-mesenchymal transition, and dormancy programs plausibly linked to residual disease. Conversely, EDN3 behaves as a tumor suppressor, consistent with its protective direction here.36

We found we could enhance robustness for stage IA/IB prediction, by retraining gene signatures using RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq) profiles for stage IA/IB cases explicitly for relapse instead of OS. ORACLE performance improved when retrained specifically with stage IA/IB cohorts for relapse risk cases [C-index TRACERx = 0.66, CPTAC = 0.81, TCGA fivefold cross-validation (CV) = 0.66] compared with the OS stage I-III trained ORACLE (C-index TRACERx = 0.64, CPTAC = 0.70, TCGA fivefold CV = 0.62) (Figure 2G). This suggests that retraining on the target IA/IB subset of patient data allows the model to capture stage-specific biology obscured when grouping stages I-III. The models were most predictive of relapse within 6 months to 2 years. We observed improved C-index scores of 5%-10% across all time-until-relapse cut-offs over the original ORACLE.

Interestingly, the learned coefficients on retraining showed weak to no correlation (Pearson r = 0.1143, P = 6.04 × 10−1, Spearman ρ = 0.0227, P = 9.18 × 10−1) with those from the original stages I-III OS training (Figure 2H).

Whole-transcriptome de novo discovery did not surpass curated published signatures. The best de novo model reached an internal cross-validated C-index of 0.56 (compared with 0.64 for refit ORACLE) and failed to surpass published signatures externally (CPTAC 0.75, TRACERx 0.67). The only model to modestly improved held-out performance (CPTAC 0.82, TRACERx 0.69) was an elastic-net reoptimization restricted to genes already in published signatures. Within these cohorts, we found no transcriptomic features adding prognostic value beyond published signatures.

To extend these findings, we investigated whether this newly identified stage I relapse-specific transcriptomic signature could be integrated with WSI-based data to capture orthogonal signals and improve risk prediction.

PATH-ORACLE: multimodal deep learning combining histological images and gene expression profiles outperforms previous predictors of relapse in multiple independent cohorts

We constructed a novel multimodal deep learning model, PATH-ORACLE, by concatenating embeddings, then combining WSI features discovered in the WSI-based analysis and the RNA-Seq signatures discovered in the RNA-Seq only analysis section. We found that histopathological and transcriptomic features are additive, i.e. the prediction accuracy of PATH-ORACLE is larger than its constituent modalities (Figure 3A). For the cases with both WSIs and RNA-Seq data in the validation sets, model performance was as follows. For CPTAC, AUCs were 0.86, 95% CI 0.69-0.98, P = 0.002 (n = 43) at 1 year; 0.86, 95% CI 0.67-0.99, P = 0.0021 (n = 24) at 3 years; and 0.97, 95% CI 0.86-1.00, P = 0.005 (n = 15; 12 events, 3 nonevents) at 5 years. For TRACERx, AUCs were 0.84, 95% CI 0.64–0.99, P = 0.0028 (n = 65) at 1 year; 0.71, 95% CI 0.54-0.86, P = 0.0065 (n = 66) at 3 years; and 0.78, 95% CI 0.61-0.91, P = 0.0014 (n = 39) at 5 years (Figure 3B). Together, both cohorts show strong early discrimination, with sustained long-term performance in CPTAC and moderate but significant long-term discrimination in the TRACERx cohort.

The multimodal model was consistently better than WSI alone. Pooled across TRACERx + CPTAC, the C-index was 0.70 for PATH-ORACLE compared with 0.63 for the WSI model. On CPTAC only, the gain was statistically significant (C-index = 0.72 compared with 0.58; bootstrap P = 0.03), and on TRACERx, the model showed improvement as well, although to a lesser extent (C-index = 0.72 compared with 0.64; P = 0.26). Importantly, using a single cut-off defined only with the TCGA discovery cohort for the risk scores predicted by PATH-ORACLE, patients can be efficiently stratified into high- and low-risk groups for relapse across multiple validation cohorts (TRACERx P = 0.003, HR 4.99, 95% CI 1.87-13.28; CPTAC P = 0.0003, HR 6.90, 95% CI 2.02-23.63). PATH-ORACLE was further compared against established risk factors of relapse and significantly outperformed them (Figure 3C), highlighting potential clinical utility in these findings.

We compared risk stratification between ORACLE and PATH-ORACLE to assess the impact of the multimodal approach. In the TRACERx cohort (n = 79), PATH-ORACLE corrected 19 ORACLE misclassifications (24.1%), reclassifying five of 17 relapse cases (29.4%) to high risk and 14 of 62 nonrelapse cases (22.6%) to low risk. Accounting for the remaining discordant classifications, this resulted in a net gain of eight correctly stratified patients (+10.1%). In the CPTAC cohort (n = 68), PATH-ORACLE correctly reclassified five patients previously misclassified by ORACLE (7.4% of the cohort), including one of 13 relapse cases (7.7%) reassigned to high risk and four of 55 nonrelapse cases (7.3%) reassigned to low risk. After accounting for cases in which PATH-ORACLE remained discordant with clinical outcome, this corresponded to a net improvement of two correctly stratified patients (+2.9%).

In multivariate Cox regression adjusting for tumor size, grade, and T-stage (pooled TRACERx + CPTAC; LVI and VPI were available only in TRACERx), PATH-ORACLE remained an independent predictor of relapse (HR 4.04, 95% CI 1.35-12.10, P = 0.013).

Motivated by prior studies reporting prognostic associations for molecular mutation markers,14,37 we assessed whether these variables were prognostic in our studied cohorts. In a mutation–survival analysis of 492 patients across four cohorts (TCGA-LUAD, TRACERx, CPTAC, and KORANYI), including 145 progression events, we found no statistically significant associations between EGFR, RAS, or ALK mutations and relapse (all P > 0.05). Although EGFR mutations exhibited a borderline protective trend (HR 0.66, 95% CI 0.41-1.05, P = 0.081), this effect was not reproducible across cohorts. These findings require validation in larger cohorts with more detailed molecular characterization, including variant allele frequency, specific mutation subtypes (for example, EGFR exon 19 deletions), and comutation patterns.

We next evaluated whether these additional features provided a complementary prognostic signal in a multimodal setting. Mutation status, together with LVI, VPI, and clinical variables, were added to PATH-ORACLE; however, these additions did not consistently improve performance across validation cohorts. This indicates that the evaluated molecular and pathological variables do not add meaningful prognostic information beyond PATH-ORACLE.

We evaluated PATH-ORACLE’s discriminative ability (C-index with 95% CI via 1000 bootstrap iterations) across clinical and molecular subgroups (age, sex, EGFR/RAS/ALK mutation status, T-stage, and smoking status) in TRACERx (n = 79), CPTAC (n = 68), and the combined cohort (n = 147) (Figure 3D). In the combined cohort, PATH-ORACLE achieved an overall C-index of 0.747 (95% CI 0.644-0.834) with consistent performance across most subgroups, but notably showed superior discrimination in EGFR-mutated patients (C-index 0.811, 95% CI 0.628-0.972, n = 27, five events) compared with EGFR-wt (C-index 0.687, 95% CI 0.539-0.829, n = 85, 18 events); RAS-wt patients (C-index 0.878, 95% CI 0.787-0.951, n = 58, 10 events) compared with RAS-mutated (C-index 0.559, 95% CI 0.368-0.750, n = 54, 13 events); and never smokers (C-index 0.915, 95% CI 0.823-1.000, n = 38, three events) compared with ever smokers (C-index 0.792, 95% CI 0.681-0.884, n = 78, 20 events). We treat these findings as exploratory only due to the small numbers of events in these subgroups.

Preoperative circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA)7,38,39 and PATH-ORACLE scores were not correlated (R2=0.01) (Figure 3E) in the TRACERx cohort, suggesting that they may contain complementary information. Although there were only 12 patients with simultaneously available ctDNA, WSI, and RNA-Seq data, the analysis suggests that ctDNA and the PATH-ORACLE biomarker may capture distinct biological dimensions of risk.

Machine learning models often benefit from a larger amount of training data. To evaluate whether additional training data could improve PATH-ORACLE performance, we performed a data scaling ablation study on the TCGA-LUAD training cohort (n = 272, 100%) (Figure 3F). We systematically reduced the training set size by 10% of the original data. Random subsampling was repeated five times for each data scale to reduce variance in results. Model performance degraded smoothly as training data decreased. PATH-ORACLE improved by +0.014 C-index per 10% increase in training data, suggesting that scaling to larger datasets could further improve performance. This highlights the need for larger scale data collection for model training and external validation.

Discussion

The evaluation of H&E-stained WSIs by pathologists has been an indispensable tool in the decision-making process for cancer therapy in patients. However, observer variability, especially when making quantitative estimates, introduces a significant uncertainty.40 Artificial intelligence/deep neural networks have provided a systematic, automated approach to extract robust features from pathological images allowing for more consistent evaluation.40, 41, 42 Recently published methods, such as ANORAK10 and PATQUANT,42 used deep learning-based segmentation for automated quantification of the previously identified morphological patterns commonly seen in LUAD. Similarly to previous studies, we found this approach performed more consistently in predicting relapse of stage I LUAD than manual grading. These methods, however, were principally designed with the aim of performing a more robust reproduction of the morphological evaluation already established by human pathologists rather than discovering novel, so far hidden prognostic histological features.

Recent advances in self-supervised learning43 have enabled WSI foundation models to encode histological slides as high dimensional feature vectors. The existence of ‘hidden’ features (spectrum of morphological signals, including subtle texture variations, spatial arrangements of cellular structures, and complex multiscale patterns) discovered by these foundation models is strongly suggested by several publications.44 For example, certain foundation model-extracted features were associated with EGFR and other gene mutations in LUAD,44 suggesting that the cancer driver mutation-associated biology may be reflected by thus far unidentified morphological features. Similar findings have been reported in prostate cancer, where deep learning models, using WSIs as input and trained directly on outcomes for biochemical recurrence, have outperformed automated Gleason grading systems.45,46

Based on this, we hypothesized that a similar strategy could identify fine-grained, relapse-specific morphological patterns in early-stage LUAD that may not have been previously described or codified in the IASLC grading system. We found that our deep learning-based predictor provided more accurate estimates of recurrence risk in stage I LUAD than artificial intelligence-assisted IASLC grading. This improved performance may reflect its ability to integrate complex biological features. These features may include known associations between IASLC morphological subtypes and immune cell infiltration patterns. Prior studies have shown that low-risk tumors are characterized by low-grade lepidic growth and dense lymphocytic infiltration. In contrast, high-risk tumors exhibit solid or cribriform growth patterns, necrosis, and tissue discohesion with sparse lymphocytes.41 It may be clinically informative to reevaluate the large number of existing cohorts by these methods. These improved predictions may further aid therapeutic decision making in stage I cases.

Integrating diverse diagnostic modalities such as histology, molecular markers, and gene mutations has long posed a significant analytical challenge in cancer care. Clinicians must navigate fragmented data systems where information resides in separate databases with inconsistent formats, while manually extracting clinically relevant findings from lengthy technical reports under substantial time pressure. Modern machine learning allows for the integration of these complementary data modalities, enabling quantitative multimodal models that capture synergistic features associated with LUAD relapse.

There is increasing evidence that this approach, based on the combined evaluation of histology, gene expression, gene mutations, etc., produces independently validated predictions that are more accurate than the predictions based on the various diagnostic modalities alone.47, 48, 49, 50 WSI data captures tissue structure and cell morphology, while RNA-Seq captures changes in gene activity. Using both together helps explain ambiguous findings in either modality, improving prediction accuracy. To this end, PATH-ORACLE improves risk stratification by building on a prospectively validated biomarker (ORACLE). These results raise the possibility that other diagnostic features, such as radiology, time series of routine laboratory measurements etc., may further increase the accuracy of predicting recurrence in stage I LUAD.

The increased accuracy of predicting recurrence of stage I LUAD, if further prospective validation confirms these preliminary results, raises the question of how to incorporate this information into clinical therapeutic decision making. For stage IB tumors, due to the lack of consistent OS benefit in randomized trials,51 routine adjuvant platinum-based chemotherapy is not consistently recommended.3 On the contrary, the CALGB 9633 study reported survival benefit for stage IB tumors with a size ≥4 cm that were treated with adjuvant carboplatin–paclitaxel relative to non–adjuvant-treated cases.52 Because worse survival in stage IB patients is mainly associated with relapse, it is likely that the observed survival benefit was at least partly due to delaying or preventing recurrence of disease. Therefore, prioritizing stage IB patients for adjuvant chemotherapy using an accurate method predicting relapse will likely improve OS sparing those patients of unnecessary chemotherapy side-effects that have low probability of recurrence. In fact, stage IB patients are offered adjuvant chemotherapies that are considered to be high risk based on histological risk factors such as tumor size, tumor grade, LVI, and VPI.4 However, assessing recurrence risk based on these factors, which could be readily employed in the clinic, have not been unified in a consistent, quantitative fashion, partly due to inconsistencies of detecting factors such as LVI.53 We were exploring here whether a deep learning method based on WSI and transcriptomics of surgical biopsies, that in principle could be processed in a centralized laboratory setting, could provide a more accurate and consistent alternative to histological risk factors. The potential survival benefit of biomarker-directed adjuvant chemotherapy in stage IB cases was recently highlighted by the AIM-HIGH trial.13 An accurate predictor of recurrence in stage IB cases could also be used to evaluate the potential benefit of adjuvant immune checkpoint inhibitor treatments, such as atezolizumab.6 Both published13 and unpublished stage IB cohorts exist that could be analyzed to determine whether high-risk recurrence cases benefit more from adjuvant therapy.

In stage IA disease, all major guidelines (European Society for Medical Oncology, American Society of Clinical Oncology, National Comprehensive Cancer Network) recommend observation alone, as neither adjuvant chemotherapy, immunotherapy, nor targeted therapy has demonstrated a survival benefit in this population.51,54 However, high-risk stage IA recurrence cases could be evaluated if close monitoring by liquid biopsy-based genetic analysis provides a robust early warning system. In case of a strong correlation, high relapse risk IA cases could be monitored in this manner, and an earlier, more active therapeutic intervention could be evaluated for improving long-term clinical outcome. This is especially relevant for EGFR-mutated LUAD where PATH-ORACLE is especially accurate. For stage IB cases, adjuvant osimertinib is recommended based on the ADAURA trial,55 but the benefit of this treatment in stage IA is not known. Considering the relatively low recurrence rate in stage IA cases, establishing the survival benefit of osimertinib treatment in a nonprioritized cohort may be more difficult and would require larger patient numbers than in a biomarker-directed trial. Therefore, a reliable predictor of relapse may significantly improve treatment strategies in stage IA LUAD as well.

There are several limitations in this study to acknowledge. Firstly, our model is built only from untreated patients, which avoids confounding by indication and provides a clean estimate of baseline relapse risk. However, it cannot identify who would benefit from adjuvant therapy, since high risk of recurrence in untreated patients does not necessarily imply high treatment responsiveness. Interpretability of WSI-level foundation models remains limited: although prognostic signals for relapse can be captured, distilling these into human-interpretable, novel morphological insights beyond current knowledge is challenging; emerging vision–language models may partially mitigate this. Data availability is incomplete across cohorts (e.g. absence of RNA-Seq data for KORANYI), restricting further validation of PATH-ORACLE. Furthermore, the numbers of cases that are recurrences are low across validation cohorts. Variation in surveillance schedules and follow-up intensity between the cohorts are acknowledged as potential sources of bias (see methods). Foundation models do not fully correct for domain shift or scanner-related artifacts. The shorter median follow-up across our cohorts (∼ 1, 2, and 4 years) is mitigated by the natural history of resected stage I LUAD, in which the recurrence hazard is strongly front-loaded, surging within 9-12 months and peaking again near year 2, a distant-metastasis-dominated pattern reproduced across large cohorts.56 The majority of stage I recurrences occur within 2 years, accounting for roughly 60% of the 5-year burden, and these early events are disproportionately distant with the worst postrecurrence survival.16,57 Our model is thus evaluated against the dominant and most consequential pattern of failure. We acknowledge that the minority of late recurrences, which arise beyond 2 to 5 years and are enriched for driver-mutated tumors, are undercaptured and will require longer follow-up to assess.
