Phytochemical Content across Different Harvest Weeks and Breeds of Juvenile Ginger (Zingiber officinale)
Authors: Bokary Sylla, Sara Lahoff, Kanaya Streeter, Andrew G. Ristvey, Victoria V. Volkis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Zingiber officinale, commonly known as ginger, has a long-standing history as a natural remedy for various ailments and diseases. Adult rhizomes are utilized in various applications including medicine, food, and beverages. However, previous studies have found that juvenile ginger harvested at 16 weeks has strong antiobesity properties, possibly due to antioxidant levels higher than those in mature ginger. This project aimed to explore the phytochemical properties of young ginger rhizomes at various stages of ripening to further support and explain the phytochemicals underlying these findings. Furthermore, this project also aimed to examine the influence of ginger cultivars and harvest times on the phytochemical properties, specifically antioxidant concentrations. This study has found that phenolics, flavonoids, and tannins have significantly higher concentrations in young ginger compared with mature rhizomes. At the same time, other properties, such as the composition and yield of essential oils, conductivity, and pH of the extracts, were not significantly affected by maturity. For example, Chinese ginger was observed to peak in antioxidant concentrations between weeks five and nine of maturity but declined in phenolic concentration by 35.92%, flavonoid concentration by 38.42%, and tannin concentration by 60.60% between weeks seven and 15 of maturity. This observed peak in antioxidants correlates with the antiobesity activity observed for samples of the same ripening studies. As such, this study will serve as preliminary exploratory research for further clinical and animal investigations into the antiobesity potential of juvenile ginger and how growing conditions affect its phytochemical properties.

Introduction

Zingiber officinale, more commonly known as ginger, is a member of the Zingiberaceae family and is commonly consumed as a spice. The part of the plant that is generally consumed is the rhizome, commonly mistaken as “ginger root”, a horizontal extension of the plant stem (Figure ). Ginger is typically grown in tropical regions and is usually propagated through small pieces of preexisting rhizomes. In particular, ginger takes about 6–7 weeks to sprout, 21–25 weeks in a vegetative stage, 4–6 weeks in a reproductive stage, and is mature for 8–12 weeks. This means that ginger can take from 35 to 43 weeks to mature.

Structure of ginger rhizome: (1) Aerial Shoot (2) Internode (3) Adventitious Root (4) Node (5) Bud (6) Scale Leaf.

Phytochemicals, biologically active compounds found in plants, in Z. officinale include polyphenolic compounds, saponins, oxalate, terpenes, and tannins, among various other essential oils and amino acids. ,− The primary bioactive compounds are 6-gingerol, 6-shogaol, zingerone, phenolics, and flavonoids. As a result of the presence of these phytochemicals, juvenile ginger has a variety of beneficial health benefits, including anti-inflammatory, antifungal, and anticancer properties, among other health benefits. ,

The key phytochemicals measured in this study were polyphenols, flavonoids, tannins, essential oils, and terpenes. Polyphenols are antioxidants and are studied extensively for their potential to reduce coronary heart disease, diabetes, hypertension, and some types of cancer. Flavonoids are a subclass of polyphenols and can protect against free radicals, inflammation, allergies, and viruses. Flavonoids have also been shown to block angiotensin-converting enzymes that raise blood pressure and the enzyme that produces estrogen. In plants, they aid in UV and light protection, metal chelation, pollinator and bird attraction, herbivore deterrence, and pathogen defense. , The general structure of flavonoids consists of three rings, often labeled as A, B, and C (Figure ) and may be further categorized by substitutions on those rings.

General structure of a flavonoid with the three rings, with positions labeled.

Tannins are water-soluble, polyphenolic compounds that can precipitate alkaloids, gelatin, and various other proteins. As their nature varies from plant to plant, the use of a tannin can vary depending on the source. This extends to medicinal applications, such as tonics and remedies for open wounds.

Essential oils are a class of hydrophobic, volatile plant compounds that have been used for bactericidal, virucidal, fungicidal, antiparasitic, insecticidal, medicinal, and cosmetic applications. Derived from the distillation of aromatic plants, they are composed of various volatile molecules, including terpenes and terpenoids. Closer looks at the antimicrobial properties of essential oils have backed their ability to defend against various bacteria and fungi. Terpenes, more specifically diterpenes, are a class of compounds that contribute to various biological activities, including anti-inflammatory, anticancer, and antifungal properties.

Where juvenile ginger separates itself from mature species lies in its phytochemical properties. It can reportedly yield twice the amount of phenolics and up to three times the amount of antioxidant activity. Characteristically, premature rhizomes are much smaller in size and have much softer outer skin, allowing for undemanding usage in culinary applications (Figure ). Juvenile ginger rhizomes also have more flexible growing conditions. They require fewer months to grow and can be grown in regions with shorter growing seasons. For instance, mature ginger can only be grown in USDA zones 8–12. In contrast, the juvenile ginger grown in this study was grown in USDA zone 7, meaning it was grown in an area with decreased sunlight and temperatures not suitable for mature ginger.

Size comparison of a mature ginger rhizome (left) to a rhizome harvested 5 weeks after sprouting.

Ginger has a long-standing history as a natural treatment for various ailments and diseases. , Ginger rhizomes have an extensive history of use as medicine, in addition to their uses in the food and beverage industries. Historically used as a natural remedy for headaches, nausea, colds, muscular pain, high cholesterol, and ulcers, the ginger rhizome is a household name in alternative traditional medicine. In modern medicine, ginger extracts and decoctions have also been used in antibacterial, diaphoretic, analgesic, antiemetic, and anti-inflammatory drugs. , For instance, recent studies have shown that ginger extracts are proficient at inhibiting harmful bacteria such as Salmonella, Mycobacterium, and Enterobacterium. Recent synthesis of ginger-derived silver nanoparticles has also been shown to inhibit a number of drug-resistant bacteria, such as E. coli.

Possible causes for ginger’s antiobesity properties come from a variety of sources. The compound 6-gingerol has been studied as a major contributor to preventing obesity. More specifically, it has been shown to inhibit lipid accumulation and adipogenesis, the process by which adipocytes accumulate in adipose tissuetissue used to store fatthroughout the body. A study conducted by the National University of Seoul proposed that another bioactive component of ginger, gingerenone A (Figure ), had a greater inhibitory effect on adipogenesis and was more effective at reducing diet-induced obesity with minimal downsides. The in vitro study of mice fed high-fat diets concluded that groups of mice receiving gingerenone A supplements of 50 mg/kg had overall lower body weights compared to the control group (30.82 g compared to 33.87 g). It was further found that groups treated with gingerenone A had significantly lower levels of free fatty acids and were unlikely to trigger lipodystrophy, an abnormal settling of lipids and free fatty acids.

Chemical structure of gingernone A.

Juvenile ginger, however, has been found to possess antiobesity properties directly. Studies conducted by Dr. Rafat Siddiqui of Virginia State University examined extracts made from juvenile ginger harvested prior to 16 weeks. These extracts inhibited lipid accumulation, triglyceride content, and α-amylase activity while improving glucose uptake when compared to mature ginger extracts. This demonstrated that juvenile ginger possesses antiobesity and antidiabetes potential not seen in mature ginger. Additionally, juvenile ginger extracts have been shown to inhibit the expression of adipogenesis genes CEBPβ and PPARγ and lipogenesis genes ACC and PEPCK, which have been linked to obesity and obesity-related complications, when studied in 3T3L1 adipocytes.

The goal of this study is to build upon this research by further examining phytochemical concentrations harvested prior to 16 weeks to identify chemicals that may be responsible for these properties. This will be done by examining phytochemical concentration variation resulting from different levels of maturity, as shown in Figure .

Conceptual framework used in this study.

Furthermore, this study will serve as a preliminary step for further animal and cellular examination of the mechanistic link between antioxidants found in ginger and obesity in mice and rats. Additional studies will examine the effects of cultural management, such as germination time, soil type, and irrigation amounts, on the ginger antioxidant concentrations during the ripening process.

Materials and Methods

Materials and Instrumentation

Chemical reagents included: Thermo Fisher Scientific AlCl3 (99% extra pure, anhydrous, granules), C2H6O (99% ACS spectroscopic grade), and C15H10O7 (95%). C2H3KO2 (certified ACS crystalline), Na2CO3 anhydrous (HPLC grade powder), FeCl3 (anhydrous), 37% HCl (certified ACS), CHCl3 (reagent ACS), H2SO4 (certified ACS), C76H52O46, tetrazolium blue chloride, and glacial C2H4O2 were all purchased from Fisher Scientific. Folin and Ciocalteu’s phenol reagent and C7H8O6 (ACS reagent grade) were purchased from MP Biomedicals (Santa Ana, CA). CH3COONa (Sigma Ultra minimum 99.0%) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Double-distilled water was used in all experiments. A Cary 60 spectrophotometer (Agilent, Santa Clara, U.S.) was used for all UV/vis analysis. Conductivity was measured on an Orion Star A212 benchtop conductometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, U.S.). pH was measured with a FiveEasy benchtop pH meter (Mettler Toledo, Greifensee, Switzerland).

Plant Materials

Five different cultivars of various juvenile ginger rhizomes (Blue Ring, Chinese, Indian, Yellow, and Red) were supplied by Dr. Rafat Siddiqui at Virginia State University (VSU). The samples were grown in 20-gallon containers using Miracle-Gro Moisture Control potting mix in high tunnels located at VSU Randolph Farm (345 samples). Maturity began to be measured the week after the samples sprouted, and samples were harvested weekly for weeks 1–3, and biweekly after week 3. Additionally, ginger was harvested up until 15 weeks in order to match a previous cellular study, rather than to full maturity. After harvesting, samples were kept in vacuum-sealed containers at −20 °C until they were processed and analyzed. Fresh (FW) and prelyophilized (DW) samples provided by VSU were then extracted and interpreted accordingly.

The growing period differed between 2021 (Year 1) and 2022 (Year 2), with Year 1 and Year 2 ginger being planted on October 17, 2021, and September 20, 2022, respectively, and harvested for different periods of time due to weather and temperature conditions (Figure ). In addition to the start date, the experimental design remained identical between the two years. The only other exception is that a red variety of ginger was added for Year 2 only.

Harvest delay between Year 1 and Year 2 of ginger.

Aside from the initial harvest date, differences in cultural management between years may have impacted the phytochemical production in ginger plants. This includes variations in applied nutrient fertility, as well as soil and climate conditions. For instance, differences in rainfall, temperature, and starting dates have been observed to impact ginger maturity and yield. ,

Another possibility is that the ginger samples from year two, week one (09/20/2022), are actually representative of the phytochemicals present in year one, week seven (09/28/2021), meaning that the data collected for year two samples would be shifted forward to accommodate the difference. The specific harvest dates for each sample are listed in the Supporting Information.

GC-MS Analysis of Terpenes and Essential Oils

In preparation for GC-MS analysis, 0.5 g of ground, freeze-dried sample was refluxed in 25 mL of methanol at 60 °C for 2 h. The resulting extract was used for GC-MS analysis.

A gas chromatography–mass spectrometer (GC/MS) (Shimadzu GCMS-QP2010, Kyoto, Japan) machine with helium as a carrier gas, equipped with a standard VF-5 fused silica capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm in. d., film thickness 0.25 μm), configured with a flow rate of 1 mL/min at 8.2317 psi and a temperature ramp of 3 °C/min until reaching a final temperature of 240 °C, was utilized to generate chromatographs for the determination of present terpenes and essential oil compositions.

GC/MS chromatograms were taken for lyophilized samples throughout the ripening process. Chromatograms were integrated such that identified compounds had a similarity match of more than 90% based on the m/z ratio, and the base area was no less than 400,000.

Phytochemical Screening

The phytochemical screening of ginger samples was carried out according to the methods described by Woisky and Salatino

Alkaloids

Wagner’s reagent was added to 3 mL of ginger extract. A color change to a reddish-brown color indicated the presence of alkaloids.

Flavonoids

0.5 g of ginger powder was mixed with 20 mL of water and shaken. A few drops of NaOH were then added to the mixture. The suspension turned yellow but became colorless with the addition of diluted hydrochloric acid. The combination of these color changes indicated the presence of flavonoids.

Tannins

0.5 g of ginger powder was suspended in water, heated, filtered, and combined with 0.1% ferric chloride. A color change to a brownish-green color indicated the presence of tannins.

Saponins

0.5 g of ginger powder was suspended in water, heated, and combined with a small amount of olive oil. The formation of a soluble emulsion indicated the presence of saponins.

Glycosides

Ginger extract was combined with an acetic acid–ferric chloride solution. The formation of a brown ring on the surface of the solution, after the addition of sulfuric acid, indicated that deoxysugars characteristic of cardenolides were present.

Steroids

0.5 g of ginger powder was combined with chloroform and sulfuric acid. The formation of a reddish-brown color indicated the presence of steroids.

Anthraquinones

0.5 g of ginger powder was combined with 5 mL of chloroform and subsequently shaken for 5 min. Following filtration, the solution was combined with an ammonia solution, and the mixture was shaken. A color change in the ammonia layer to a pink–violet or red color indicated the presence of anthraquinones.

Phenols

50 mg of ginger powder was combined with water, a solution of gelatin, and 10% NaCl. The formation of a white precipitate indicated the presence of the phenol compounds.

Oxalates

A total of 5 mL of ginger extract was combined with sulfuric acid and potassium permanganate. A change to a red color indicated the presence of oxalate.

Extraction in Ethanol/Water from Freeze-Dried and Fresh Ginger

Extraction of phenolic compounds is carried out according to the methods described by Zhang et al. and Kela et al. In order to perform extractions, 0.625 g of ground freeze-dried ginger or 1.25 g of finely chopped fresh ginger is combined with 25 mL of 80% ethanol in water and refluxed for 48 h. The resulting extract is vacuum-filtered and used for flavonoid, polyphenol, and tannin procedures.

Total Flavonoid Content

Total flavonoid content was measured through the use of procedures by Chang et al. and Jakobek et al. Calibration was performed using different quercetin concentrations (20–400 mg/L, R2 = 0.9988). The resulting calibration curve is presented in the supplemental data (Figure S1). For sample analysis, a solution was prepared consisting of 200 μL of extract, 600 μL of 95% ethanol, 40 μL of 1 M potassium acetate, and 40 μL of 10% w/v aluminum chloride. 1120 μL of water was added, and the solution was incubated for 30 min. An absorbance of 415 nm was used for analysis, with the total flavonoid concentration (TFC) being measured in mg of quercetin equivalent (QE)/g of dry weight (DW) or fresh weight (FW). The TFC was then measured using the following equation: TFC=A×V4.9×1000×m×DF

where A = average absorbance, V = collected volume of extract, m = mass of sample used for extract, and DF = dilution factor.

Total Polyphenol Content

Total polyphenol content was measured through the use of standard procedures for plant extracts. − Calibration was performed through the use of different gallic acid solutions (50–500 mg/L, R2 = 0.9994). The resulting calibration curve is presented in the supplemental data (Figure S2). A solution of 20 μL of extract, 1580 μL of water, and 100 μL of Folin–Ciocalteu’s reagent was created, combined with 300 μL of 20% w/v sodium carbonate solution, and incubated at 40 °C. The absorbance of the solution was measured at 765 nm, with the total polyphenol concentration (TPC) expressed in mg of gallic acid equivalent (GAE)/g of dry weight (DW) or fresh weight (FW). The TPC was then analyzed using this equation: TPC=A×V1.1×1000×m×DF

Where A = average absorbance, V = collected volume of extract, m = mass of sample used for extract, and DF = dilution factor

Analysis of Tannins

Total tannin content was determined by following standard procedures for plant extracts. − Calibration was performed using many tannic acid solutions (50–500 mg/L, R2 = 0.9987). The resulting calibration curve is presented in the supplemental data (Figure S3). A solution of 300 μL of extract, 100 μL of 0.01 M tetrazolium blue chloride, and 100 μL of 0.2 M sodium hydroxide was created. 1500 μL of 80% ethanol was then added, and the solution was measured at 527 nm. The Total tannin concentration (TTC) was measured in mg of tannic acid equivalent (TAE)/g of dry weight (DW) or fresh weight (FW). The TTC was then analyzed using the following equation: TTC=A×V1.3×1000×m×DF

Where A = average absorbance, V = collected volume of extract, m = mass of sample used for extract, and DF = dilution factor

Determination of Cations by ICP/MS

Prior to ICP/MS analysis, the conductivity of samples was analyzed using an Orion Star A212 (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, U.S.) benchtop conductometer, and pH was measured using a FiveEasy benchtop pH meter (Mettler Toledo, Greifensee, Switzerland), calibrated using pH 7, 10, and 4.01 standards. This was done to predict variations in the ICP/MS cation results.

For ICP/MS analysis, 2.5 mg of the desired samples was placed into Mars-6 microwave digestion tubes along with 10 mL of nitric acid. The Mars-6 digester (CEM, Matthews, U.S.) was configured for plant material samples and set to run for 1 h. Samples were then removed from the holster and transferred to 50 mL vials, which were then diluted to a 50 mL total volume using DI water. Samples were finally transferred to 15 mL vials before the analysis of calcium, potassium, iron, selenium, copper, sodium, cobalt, and manganese via ICP/MS (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan). Values were reported in % daily values per 100 g (% DV per 100 g).

Statistical Analysis and Limits of Detection

All experiments were performed in triplicate, and standard deviations were calculated where possible. A one-way ANOVA was also used to verify the statistical significance of the results within a 95% confidence interval, in combination with a Tukey post hoc test when necessary.

The limit of detection for flavonoids, polyphenols, and tannins was analyzed using procedures outlined by the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute and examples provided by Ervin and Kariuki. The stock solutions used were quercetin stock solutions diluted in 95% ethanol for flavonoids, and gallic acid and tannic acid solutions diluted in 20% ethanol for polyphenols and tannins, respectively. Solutions were measured against blanks of either 95% ethanol for flavonoids or water for polyphenols and tannins, and diluted by a factor of 2 until the absorbance remained constant. The final concentrations were defined as the limits of detection for each analyte. Absorbances were measured at 510, 765, and 527 nm for flavonoids, polyphenols, and tannins, respectively.

The highest concentration for Y1 was 38.64 ± 4.85 mg GAE/g, which was observed by the seventh week for yellow ginger. Average concentrations for yellow and Chinese ginger were also observed to be significantly higher than those of blue-ring, Indian, and yellow ginger (p < 0.001). Meanwhile, the highest concentration for Y2 was 28.91 ± 0.23 mg GAE/g, observed during the first week for Indian ginger, with averages for all breeds being significantly similar, with the exception of blue-ring ginger, which had a significantly lower average compared to other breeds (p < 0.05). While average concentrations in Y1 were significantly higher than in Y2 (p < 0.01), the lowest concentrations were observed for blue-ring ginger in both cases, being 10.40 ± 0.48 mg GAE/g during week five for Y1 and 8.02 ± 0.04 mg GAE/g during week nine for Y2. Additionally, blue-ring ginger was observed to have a slight rise in total phenolics after week nine. It is unknown why this occurred. As such, the total phenolics in blue-ring ginger with later harvest times may need further study.

When compared to literature values, polyphenol concentrations were lower than the highest literature value but were still as much as roughly 700% higher than the lowest literature value (Table ). − This indicates that juvenile ginger still possesses good phytochemical potential despite not matching the concentrations of mature ginger grown under more favorable conditions.

The effect of the delayed planting date used for Y2 can be directly observed in the different trends between Y1 and Y2. For instance, Y1 ginger was planted in October, while Y2 ginger was planted in September. This month-long delay extended to the timing of peak concentrations, as Y1 concentrations peaked during weeks five to nine, while Y2 concentrations peaked during weeks one to three. After these peak times, concentrations were observed to decrease steadily for both Y1 and Y2. Effect of Maturity on Mineral Content

Besides phenolic analysis, mineral content was also selected for study because of the role of cations in various metabolic pathways.

Prior to cation analysis, pH and conductivity were measured as a means of predicting possible variations in the mineral content. Between both years and all varieties of ginger, pH was observed to remain relatively constant, with the exception of blue-ring and yellow ginger having higher pH in Y2 compared to Y1 (Figures S12–13). The conductivity also did not vary due to maturity, with the only variance observed being between Y1 and Y2. For instance, Indian ginger dropped in conductivity between Y1 and Y2, while conductivity in Chinese and yellow ginger increased (Figures S14–15). As such, the lack of variation in both pH and conductivity indicated that mineral content may not be affected by maturity but may be affected by cultivar and cultural management.

This was further supported by ICP/MS analysis, which showed that the effect of maturity on cation content varied heavily between individual cations and cultivars (Table S34, Figures and ). For instance, while potassium and zinc had higher concentrations in all young ginger samples compared to old ginger samples, this increase ranged from 133% to 7.13% for potassium and 100% to 11.1% for zinc. Additionally, potassium and zinc were the only two minerals to increase for all five cultivars, with all other minerals not increasing for at least one cultivar. This shows that cultivar plays a large role in the effect of maturity on cation content. Growing conditions have also been shown to heavily influence cation content, as both young and old ginger samples contained more calcium, iron, potassium, magnesium, and sodium, as well as less copper when compared to literature values (Table ). ,,

This variance in the effectiveness of maturity between cultivars indicates that the difference in cations due to maturity is unlikely to be the cause of juvenile ginger’s antiobesity properties. Nevertheless, the presence of these cations still indicates another facet of ginger’s many health benefits due to their role in key metabolic pathways, such as the role of potassium and sodium in osmoregulation and metabolic balance.

Conclusions

Young ginger has proven to be more potent in phenolic antioxidant content than adult ginger, peaking exactly during the periods where the empirical antiobesity effect was reported. For instance, concentrations of phenolics, flavonoids, and tannins were observed to peak during weeks five and seven of maturity in the first year of observation and during weeks one to two of maturity in the second year of observation, but then steadily declined after both periods. As such, optimal harvest times for maximum antioxidant content should target ginger grown for five to 7 weeks after sprouting for earlier planting dates and one to three weeks for later planting dates. Additionally, the first year observed had higher concentrations on average. These high concentrations may have been caused by differences in cultural management conditions, such as variations in soil quality, decreasing sunlight, and lower temperatures.

However, based on the data available, the most optimal conditions for maximizing phytochemical yield would be through an earlier planting date and harvesting five to 7 weeks after sprouting. This means that farmers and ginger growers seeking to optimize antioxidant density would want to plant ginger early and harvest it during this time. This means that the ginger they harvest will have increased antioxidants with smaller ginger rhizomes, allowing for the creation of health or food products that are more antioxidant-rich while using the same or lesser amounts of ginger than previous products. Additionally, the decreased amount of growing time will also lead to a decrease in the need for resources, such as water or fertilizer.

While concentrations of antioxidants were observed to be influenced by maturity across all different varieties of ginger observed, essential oils, pH, conductivity, and cation content remained unaffected or were inconsistently affected by maturity. As such, this indicates that increased antioxidant concentrations are responsible for the antiobesity properties observed in juvenile ginger harvested prior to 16 weeks, as reported by previous studies such as those performed by Siddiqui et al. and Li et al.

As the overall lower antioxidant concentrations observed for Y2 ginger may have been a result of differences in cultural management conditions, a more horticulturally based study is currently being conducted on the basis of this research to more thoroughly examine the effects of these varying conditions. This research will examine the effects of cultural management through controlled soil quality and irrigation, allowing for a deeper understanding of the effects of cultural management on the ginger. This is especially important as soil quality, irrigation, and starting date, among other environmental conditions, have already been observed to impact ginger development in previous studies. ,

The lack of thorough examination of cultural management was not the only limitation of this study. Another key limitation was the effect of growing location and overall climate on phytochemical content. This is especially apparent, as geographical location has been shown to heavily influence phytochemical and mineral content, as seen in Table . Other key limitations can be found in the ginger rhizomes themselves, such as small rhizome size and quantity in earlier harvest weeks, the influence of the nutrient content of the ginger “seed”, and variations between individual ginger rhizomes of the same cultivar, have the potential to influence the accuracy of phytochemical results.

Lastly, another limitation of this study was the lack of utilization of direct biological assays. While this study has shown the antioxidant content of juvenile ginger and the possible cause of the antiobesity properties of juvenile ginger, further studies are needed to prove this link. For instance, this study was limited by a lack of in vitro and gene-level analysis. As such, this study will serve as a preliminary examination of the link between antiobesity and antioxidants found in juvenile ginger, supported by an in-depth animal study. Additionally, this study was limited in its control over factors such as temperature and irrigation. As such, this study will also serve to provide preliminary data for studies examining the effects of cultural management, such as the start date and irrigation practices, on the antioxidant concentrations found in juvenile ginger.

Due to its antiobesity potential, juvenile ginger harvested during weeks with peak antioxidant concentrationsnamely, between weeks five and ninehas the potential to be used in pharmaceutical and consumable products designed to prevent or treat obesity.
