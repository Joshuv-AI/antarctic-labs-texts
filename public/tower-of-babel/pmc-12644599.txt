The TRPV4-YAP axis mediates cytoskeletal and extracellular matrix remodeling in trabecular meshwork cells as a novel glaucoma mechanism
Authors: Jing Xu, Kexin Liu, Feng Wang, Ying Su
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Transient Receptor Potential Vanilloid 4 (TRPV4), a non-selective cation channel in trabecular meshwork (TM) tissue, is activated by forces like deformation and shear stress, playing a critical role in intraocular pressure (IOP) regulation. However, the mechanisms by which TRPV4 mediates TM cells responses to elevated intraocular pressure (IOP), along with the resulting regulatory outcomes, remain incompletely understood. In vitro, TM cells were mechanically stretched, and the effects on TRPV4 activation and calcium influx were evaluated via confocal microscopy, Western blotting, and immunofluorescence. Pharmacological agonists and inhibitors were used to investigate signaling mechanisms. In vivo, ocular hypertension (OHT) was induced in mice, and IOP was measured following drug treatments. Subsequently, IOP was measured after drug administration. Transcriptome sequencing was performed to detect TRPV4 activation-induced alterations in RNA expression and to explore associated regulatory pathways. Mechanical stretch significantly reduced YAP1 mRNA expression in TM cells. TRPV4 activation induced YAP nuclear translocation, which was inhibited by HC067047, confirming TRPV4-YAP signaling. Transcriptome sequencing confirmed that the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway is crucial in mediating YAP activation. In vivo, HC067047 and VP treatment significantly lowered IOP and reduced the accumulation of ECM proteins in TM tissues. Our study demonstrates the critical role of TRPV4-YAP signaling in TM cell function and IOP regulation. These findings indicate that targeting the TRPV4-PI3K/AKT-YAP axis may offer novel therapeutic strategies for glaucoma.​Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-25618-9.

Introduction

Glaucoma represents a heterogeneous group of disorders that lead to irreversible optic nerve damage. As the second most common cause of blindness worldwide1, it poses a significant public health challenge. Primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG), one of the most prevalent forms of glaucoma, involves optic nerve damage that can occur at any intraocular pressure (IOP) level. Long-term and effective reduction of IOP has a significantly positive impact on patient prognosis2–4.

When exploring the pathogenesis of POAG and the regulation of IOP, the trabecular meshwork (TM) is a vital component of the aqueous humor (AH) outflow pathway and plays a crucial role in regulating IOP homeostasis. TRPV4 is a non-selective cation channel that acts as a principal pathway for Ca2⁺ entry across various cell types5–7. TRPV4 channels are activated by factors such as stretch, cell deformation, and increased extracellular matrix (ECM) stiffness, thereby permitting substantial localized Ca2⁺ influx, which subsequently mediates downstream biochemical activities regulating cellular function8,9. The elevation of IOP results in the continuous mechanical stretching of TM cells, which activates various mechanosensitive channels and signaling pathways. Persistent TRPV4 signaling is a crucial aspect of TM biology and has been shown to significantly contribute to increased outflow resistance, both in vitro and in vivo10. Certain related pathways simultaneously modulate cellular and ECM metabolism in a steroid-mediated manner10,11, thereby influencing the resistance within the AH outflow pathway. Additionally, the phosphoinositide metabolism12, nitric oxide release13,14, PUFA release14, lipid remodeling15 and Piezo1 activation16 in the primary cilium are also signaling pathways through which TRPV4 regulates TM function. However, some studies have found that the removal of the primary cilium in TM does not affect TRPV4 signaling17. The expression of endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) in mammalian TM cells has been reported to be nearly negligible or entirely undetectable13,18,19. Additionally, metabolites of phospholipase A2, such as arachidonic acid, have been found to stimulate rather than suppress TRPV4 activity17; and TRPV4 activation appears unaffected by Piezo1 inhibition20. Although TRPV4 clearly contributes to pressure transduction in the TM and to maintaining Ca2+ homeostasis during stretch, its functional role remains controversial, with some studies linking its activation with both IOP reduction12–14 and elevation17.

In recent years, extensive research has focused on Yes-associated protein (YAP), a central element of the Hippo signaling pathway, due to its significant roles in the regulation of cell size, proliferation, apoptosis, and mechanosensing21,22. Multiple signaling pathways have been shown to intricately regulate the activity of YAP and its paralog, the transcriptional co-activator with PDZ-binding motif (TAZ), including the canonical Hippo kinase cascade, G protein-coupled receptors, Wnt signaling, and metabolic networks which involve Rho GTPases, energy stress, autophagy, and glycolysis22–27. Additionally, biomechanical stimuli such as cell morphology, cell–cell junction integrity, actin cytoskeleton dynamics, ECM rigidity, contractile behavior, focal adhesion forces, and cell–matrix signaling interactions have been reported to influence the subcellular localization of YAP28,29, thus modulating cell fate and function.

In summary, when YAP is prevented from translocating into the nucleus, it accumulates in the cytoplasm, and the phosphorylated form of YAP (p-YAP), which cannot bind to TEAD transcription factors, lacks any biological activity. Thus, the primary biological role of YAP is to mediate the nuclear regulation of gene expression. Based on this mechanism, the role of YAP can be understood from two key perspectives: first, it precisely regulates cellular and tissue functions through transcriptional control in response to physiological and pathological stimuli; second, abnormalities in this regulation are closely associated with the development of various diseases30. Recent whole-genome sequencing studies have further confirmed the presence of risk loci for YAP in open-angle glaucoma31, suggesting a potential causal relationship with dysfunction in AH outflow. These observations underscore the critical role of YAP in maintaining the functional integrity of the AH outflow pathway and its fundamental contribution to the pathophysiology of glaucoma.

Given that both TRPV4 and YAP play significant roles in the response of TM cells to mechanical stimuli, our research will explore the mechanisms of YAP signaling in TM cells and how modulating YAP activity influences TRPV4-induced cytoskeletal changes. The present study hypothesizes that targeting the YAP mechanotransduction pathway may improve TRPV4-induced cytoskeletal remodeling in TM cells, thereby offering new strategies for the treatment of POAG, this approach may provide novel insights and strategies for the prevention and therapeutic management of glaucoma.

Methods

Reagents

GSK1016790A (HY-19608), HC067047 (HY-100208), and Verteporfin (HY-B0146) were purchased from MCE (USA). The primary antibody for TRPV4 (DF8624) and MYOC (DF6483) were obtained from Affinity Biosciences (USA). Antibodies against α-SMA (14,395–1-AP), FN (66,042–1-Ig), COL1A1 (67,288–1-Ig), YAP1 (13,584–1-AP), AKT (10,176–2-AP), P-AKT (66,444–1-Ig) were sourced from ProteinTech (USA). Antibodies against PI3K (ab191606), P-PI3K (ab182651) were obtained from Abcam (USA). Antibodies against β-actin (#4970), GAPDH (#2118) and p-YAP (Ser-127, #13,008) were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology (USA). Alexa Fluor 488/594-conjugated IgG and DAPI were acquired from Invitrogen (CA, USA). The Fluo-4 calcium ion probe (F026) was obtained from DOJINDO (Japan). Immunoblotting reagents were supplied by SEVEN (China). Unless otherwise specified, all remaining chemicals were obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific or MCE.

Cell culture and characterization

HTMCs were sourced from Sciencell (USA) and cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM)/F-12 Medium (F12, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc., USA), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 μg/mL streptomycin. Cells were incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ environment. Experiments were performed with cells from passages 5 to 8 to prevent cellular senescence. Cells were exposed to different concentrations of pharmacological agents according to the experimental design, ensuring that the final concentration of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) remained consistent and below 0.1% across all groups. Cell identification was performed according to published standards32, wherein 90% confluence of HTMCs was achieved by culturing with 100 nM dexamethasone (DEX) in freshly replaced medium twice daily. After one week of treatment, Western blot analysis was performed to evaluate the expression levels of myocilin (MYOC).

Cell stretching

HTMCs were cultured in SC4Ha stretch chambers (2 cm * 2 cm; Menicon Co., Ltd., Nagoya, Japan), which are known for their strong cell adhesion properties, and maintained in F12 DMEM. Once the cells adhered and reached 50% confluence, a ShellPa Pro cell stretching system (Menicon Life Science, Nagoya, Japan) was used to apply fixed-frequency stretching to the chambers and attached HTMCs. The stretching protocol was configured to 1 s per cycle (1 Hz) with an amplitude of 15% of the original length. Control cells were maintained under the same conditions without the application of mechanical stretching. Thirty minutes prior to stretching, HC067047 (1 μM) or Verteporfin (1 μM) was added to the culture medium.

Calcium ion imaging

Intracellular calcium levels were assessed using Fluo-4 AM (S1060, Beyotime, China). The dye was diluted to a final concentration of 5 μM in Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS). Cells were incubated 30 min with the dye at 37 °C followed by a thorough washing step. Subsequently, cells were treated with the respective drugs. Fluorescence microscopy facilitated the observation of calcium influx. Real-time calcium influx was monitored using a confocal microscope.

Immunofluorescence

HTMCs were treated with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 10 min, and then blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h at room temperature. The cells were incubated with primary antibodies for 2 h, followed by three washes with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Subsequently, they were exposed to Alexa Fluor 488/594-conjugated secondary antibodies (Invitrogen, USA) for 1 h. Afterward, the cells were stained with phalloidin (abs47048273, Absin) for 1 h and counterstained with DAPI (4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole). Fluorescence was detected using a fluorescence microscope (× 60, EVOS M5000, USA).

Cell proliferation assay

Cell proliferation was assessed using the Click-iT EdU Cell Proliferation Kit, following the product instructions. Briefly, 1 × 105 HTMCs were seeded in culture dishes and incubated with 10 μM EdU solution at 37 °C for 12 h. Following incubation, cells were treated with 0.5 mL of Click-iT reaction mixture at room temperature for 30 min after being fixed with 4% buffered paraformaldehyde. Nuclei were incubated with DAPI (blue) for 5 min for staining at room temperature. Results were then captured using a Nikon Eclipse 80i microscope system (Japan). Fifteen to twenty random fields were selected, and EdU-positive cells (green) were manually counted to quantify the proportion of proliferating cells.

Reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was isolated from HTMCs using the TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, CA, USA) and treated with DNase I (Takara, Kusatsu, Japan) to eliminate genomic DNA. RNA integrity and quality were evaluated using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, DE, USA). Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using M-MLV reverse transcriptase (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and RT-qPCR was performed on a CFX96 system (Bio-Rad Laboratories, CA, USA) utilizing FastStart™ Universal SYBR® Green Master Mix (Rox, Roche, USA). The RT-qPCR protocol first undergoes an initial denaturation for 10 min at 95 °C, followed by 45 cycles, each consisting of 15 s at 95 °C and 30 s at 55 °C. The specific primer sequences are provided in Supplementary Table S1. All samples were analyzed in triplicate, and this procedure was repeated three times for technical replication. The gene expression levels of mechanically stretched and drug-treated groups were normalized against the control group (set to 1.0), using GAPDH as the reference gene. Quantification was performed using the 2^−ΔΔCT method.

Western blot

Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer containing 1% protease inhibitor cocktail (cOmplete, Roche, USA) and 2% phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (PhosSTOP, Roche, USA), followed by centrifugation at 13,400 × g for 15 min. The supernatant was first collected, then its protein content was subsequently determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, China). Proteins (20 μg per lane) were heated at 100 °C for 10 min to denature, and then subjected to electrophoresis on Easy PAGE® 10% (SW143, SEVEN, China) and 12.5% (SW145, SEVEN, China) Bis–Tris protein gels. Proteins were transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes using NuPAGE transfer buffer. Following a 1-h blocking step with 5% BSA, the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies prepared according to the manufacturer’s recommended dilution conditions at 4 °C overnight. After washing, membranes were incubated for 1 h with IRDye® 680RD Goat anti-Mouse IgG or IRDye® 800CW Goat anti-Rabbit IgG secondary antibodies (each diluted 1:10,000) at room temperature. After three washes with TBST, the membranes were visualized and assessed with an Odyssey® Infrared Imaging System.

Animal experiments

Male C57BL/6 J mice (8 weeks old, weight 20-25 g) were purchased from Liaoning Changsheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Mice were housed in a pathogen-free environment under a 12-h light/dark cycle, with ad libitum access to food and water. The room temperature was kept constant at approximately 22–23 °C. Anesthesia was induced via intraperitoneal injection of 150–200 μL of 1.25% Avertin, administered at a defined dosage of 20 μL/g body weight. Five mice were housed per cage. Mice were randomly assigned to two separate groups, each comprising 8 individuals: (1) Vehicle group received a 2μL injection of saline solution in the anterior chamber; (2) OHT group was administered a 2μL injection of HA (10 mg/mL in saline solution; catalog no.H1751; Sigma Chemical Co.) in the anterior chamber. The periocular region was disinfected using a 1% povidone-iodine solution, and the conjunctival sac was rinsed. Using a sterile microinjection syringe (30G 1/2-inch sterile needle, Hamilton, 10μL), a 2μL injection was administered at the edge of the clear cornea, avoiding contact with the lens and iris. Post-surgery, antibiotic eye ointment was applied. IOP and the general health of the animals were monitored on the day of surgery and daily for 14 days after surgery. Euthanasia was performed immediately in cases of severe distress by administering an overdose of anesthetic. Sample sizes were determined based on data from preliminary experiments. An independent co-author performed randomization and conducted blinded assessments of animal conditions.

Protein extraction from TM tissue of C57BL/6 J mice

On day 14 following intraocular drug injection into the mouse anterior chamber, IOP was measured. Subsequently, cardiac perfusion was performed, and eyes were collected.TM tissue was dissected from the eye ring for Western blot analysis (3–5 eyes per group). Under sterile conditions, the eyes were removed and washed with 500 mL of pre-chilled sterile saline containing 160,000 units of tobramycin. The eyes were then carefully dissected, making a circumferential incision 1 mm posterior to the limbus, and the posterior segment tissue, lens, iris, and part of the ciliary body were removed. The anterior segment was inverted, and the corneal endothelium was placed facing up on a sterile dissection dish. Using a dissecting microscope, the tissue adhering to the angle of the anterior chamber and any pigment was removed, distinguishing the Schwalbe’s line and the white scleral spur. The area between these two landmarks defines the TM. Under maximum magnification, the TM was carefully excised with fine scissors, avoiding the inclusion of pigment epithelium or corneal endothelial tissue. The tissue was immediately transferred to an ice bath for preservation to ensure protein integrity. The TM tissue was weighed, and ice-cold RIPA lysis buffer (containing PMSF and phosphatase inhibitors) was added at a 1:10 ratio (1 mL of lysis buffer per 100 mg of tissue). The tissue was homogenized using a mechanical homogenizer (IKA, T 10 basic ULTRA-TURRAX®) at a slow and even speed to ensure complete tissue lysis. The homogenized samples were then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant (protein extract) was collected, discarding the pellet. Protein concentration was measured using the BCA assay, and the samples were adjusted to the required concentration for subsequent experiments. The protein extract was aliquoted into small volume tubes (e.g., 20μL) and quickly stored at -80 °C to avoid repeated freeze–thaw cycles, ensuring protein activity.

RNA extraction and RNA-sequencing

Total RNA was extracted using the TRIzol method (Invitrogen, CA, USA) and treated with RNase-free DNase I (Takara, Kusatsu, Japan). Technicians from Hangzhou Kaitai Bio Laboratory performed the RNA sequencing analysis. Sequencing was conducted on an Illumina platform using the MiSeq instrument (NovaSeq 6000 System, Illumina Inc.). Quality control was executed with the Agilent Bioanalyzer (Qseq100 DNA Analyzer, Bioptic Inc.) and Real-Time PCR (Light Cycler 96, Roche Inc.). Genes with an FDR < 0.05 and an absolute log2 fold change (|log2FC|) ≥ 1.2 were defined as differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Moreover, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was carried out using R to identify pathways associated with the DEGs33–35.

IOP measurement

A Tonolab rebound tonometer was utilized to measure IOP in anesthetized mice. Six IOP measurements were taken for each eye (left and right) within one minute of anesthesia, and the average was calculated to determine a single IOP value for each measurement. Mice body temperature was kept between 38–40 °C during anesthesia with the aid of a Deltaphase isothermal pad (Braintee Scientific, Inc.). IOP and animal well-being were monitored at 9am on days 0–14 post-surgery. Each mice’s mean IOP was calculated, and these values were averaged to determine the group mean IOP ± SE.

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), derived from a minimum of three independent experiments. For comparisons between three or more groups, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test to assess the differences. Student’s t-test was used to compare data between two groups, with a p-value of < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

The dataset includes gene expression data and mechanical parameters (15% stretching, 1 cycle per second for 6 h) for primary HTMCs derived from normal human eyes. Transcriptomic profiles were provided for three stretched HTMC samples and three corresponding controls. Differential expression analysis revealed a significant downregulation of YAP1 mRNA (log2 fold change = -1.5, p = 0.0067) (Fig. 1A). To validate these findings, we performed DEX-induced cell identification experiments in HTMCs from Sciencell (Supplementary Figure S1A, B). Further, we examined in vivo alterations in YAP protein expression under elevated IOP by establishing an OHT model in C57BL/6 J mice. Upon successful model establishment, proteins were extracted from TM tissues for analysis. Western blot analysis revealed that after one week of OHT induction, the overall YAP protein levels in TM tissues were not significantly altered (Fig. 1B, C). However, immunofluorescence staining revealed a marked shift in YAP subcellular localization (Fig. 1D). In the TM tissues of OHT mice, nuclear localization of YAP increased compared to the control group, as evidenced by increased co-localization of red fluorescence (YAP) with DAPI in the TM region. This redistribution of YAP within TM in response to OHT suggests a potential impact on its regulatory functions.

Utilizing the ShellPa Pro cell stretching system (Menicon Co., Ltd., Japan), HTMCs were exposed to cyclic mechanical stretching (15% elongation, frequency 1 Hz), and TRPV4 expression was assessed. Representative immunofluorescence images of TRPV4 (red) confirmed that mechanical stretching did not induce significant changes in TRPV4 expression compared to static controls (Fig. 2A). Cellular responses were monitored at 3-h intervals over 24 h (Supplementary Figure S2). After 12 h of stretching, cells exhibited increased volume, sharper edges, and elongated, fibrous morphologies, with intercellular fiber bundles forming a more pronounced network structure. Calcium flux was assessed using the calcium-sensitive dye Fluo-4 AM. Continuous mechanical stretching resulted in a marked increase in intracellular calcium levels, which was markedly inhibited by the TRPV4-specific inhibitor HC067047 (Supplementary Figure S3).

Given the known role of YAP in mechanotransduction and cellular responses to mechanical signals, the subcellular localization of YAP was examined following mechanical stretching. Based on the observed timing of morphological changes under optical microscopy, 12 h were chosen as the observation time point. Immunofluorescence microscopy revealed that under mechanical stretching, YAP was primarily localized in the cytoplasm during the initial 12 h, subsequently translocating to the nucleus after 12 h. Treatment with the TRPV4 inhibitor HC067047 significantly reduced this nuclear translocation, indicating that TRPV4 activation is essential for YAP nuclear localization in response to mechanical stimuli (Fig. 2C, D).

The redistribution of YAP in response to mechanical stretching was further validated through Western blot analysis of nuclear and cytoplasmic fractions (Fig. 2E, F). Under stretching conditions, total YAP protein levels remained stable, while p-YAP levels in the cytoplasm significantly decreased, while nuclear YAP protein levels markedly increased. This suggests that mechanical stretching promotes YAP dephosphorylation, facilitates the translocation of YAP from the cytoplasm to the nucleus, thereby potentially influencing the expression of downstream genes. Treatment with the TRPV4 inhibitor restored nuclear YAP levels to baseline, underscoring the dependency of YAP translocation on TRPV4 activity.

YAP mediates TRPV4 activation in regulating TM cells viability and proliferation

Building on our previous findings, TRPV4 was further pharmacologically activated using the specific agonist GSK101. We assessed cell proliferation through EdU incorporation analysis and quantified cell viability with the CCK-8 assay. The EdU assay revealed that GSK101 treatment significantly increased HTMC proliferation. This effect was significantly inhibited by pretreatment with VP, a specific inhibitor of YAP transcriptional activity. In contrast, HC067047 treatment alone did not significantly affect HTMC proliferation. Moreover, co-treatment with HC067047 and VP did not replicate the suppression observed with VP alone. These results indicate that the GSK101-induced enhancement of cell proliferation is strongly dependent on YAP activity (Fig. 3A). TRPV4 activation induces ECM deposition and cytoskeletal remodeling in TM cells, which can be alleviated by VP

Generally, tissue stiffness is governed by the cytoskeleton and the ECM, two components that interact extensively within tissues. The TM is crucial for regulating the outflow of AH, accounting for 75% of the outflow pathway, and its stiffness plays a critical role in IOP regulation36. Previous studies have established a correlation between YAP and TM tissue stiffness37. In order to gain a more comprehensive insight into the role of the TRPV4-YAP signaling axis in modulating HTMCs functions, we performed an in-depth evaluation of the effects of TRPV4 activation on YAP activation, nuclear translocation, HTMC proliferation, ECM deposition, and cytoskeletal remodeling.

Initially, HTMCs were treated with GSK101 at concentrations established based on preliminary experiments for 24 h. Immunofluorescence staining and nuclear-cytoplasmic fractionation followed by Western blot analysis were performed to assess YAP activation and subcellular localization. These results demonstrated that GSK101 treatment significantly increased nuclear accumulation of YAP, with Western blot confirming a substantial rise in nuclear YAP protein levels (Fig. 4A-C). To assess ECM deposition, Western blot analysis was performed, and significant increases in FN, COL1A1, and α-SMA expression levels were observed following GSK101 treatment. suggesting that TRPV4 activation promotes the synthesis of ECM components (Fig. 4F, G). Subsequent treatment with the YAP-specific inhibitor VP significantly reduced GSK101-induced ECM protein expression, suggesting that the TRPV4-YAP axis is crucial in regulating ECM deposition.

In terms of cytoskeletal remodeling, immunofluorescence staining demonstrated that GSK101-treated HTM cells displayed significantly increased F-actin fibers (phalloidin-stained), forming more pronounced stress fibers. elevated expression of FN, COL1A1 and α‐SMA. VP treatment markedly attenuated GSK101-induced F-actin reorganization (Fig. 4H-J). These findings strongly support the involvement of the TRPV4-YAP signaling axis in cytoskeletal remodeling.

In summary, the YAP-specific inhibitor VP effectively mitigated TRPV4 activation-induced ECM deposition and cytoskeletal remodeling in HTMCs, highlighting the crucial role of YAP in TRPV4-dependent mechanotransduction processes.

Discussion

This study investigates the mechanistic role of the TRPV4-YAP signaling axis in mechanosensation in TM cells and IOP regulation. By pharmacologically activating the mechanosensitive ion channel TRPV4 through mechanical stretching and pharmacological stimuli, we verified the response mechanisms of TRPV4 and YAP in TM cells under mechanical stress. Transcriptomic sequencing revealed the presence of a "TRPV4-PI3K/AKT-YAP" signaling axis in TM cells that responds to mechanical force stimulation. In vivo validation using the C57BL/6 J OHT mouse model confirmed the regulatory role of the TRPV4-YAP axis in TM tissue stiffening and ECM remodeling induced by elevated IOP, illustrating its synergistic effect in IOP regulation.

A multi-ethnic genome-wide meta-analysis identified 127 risk loci for POAG, with YAP1 being one of the newly discovered loci that has garnered significant attention39. YAP, a transcriptional co-activator, plays a crucial role in mechanotransduction and is implicated in glaucoma. Our analysis of the GEO dataset GSE14768 showed that mechanical stretch altered YAP1 gene expression in TM cells. This change may be attributed to two main factors: first, YAP is a key effector of cellular mechanosensation, and its downregulation suggests that HTMCs in a mechanical stretch environment may weaken their response to external mechanical loads, leading to changes in cytoskeletal and matrix remodeling. Second, YAP activation often stimulates the expression of growth-related genes and cellular responses, but when YAP activity is upregulated, negative feedback mechanisms may suppress YAP1 gene transcription to prevent excessive accumulation, which could lead to abnormal proliferation or metabolic imbalance. Importantly, YAP’s function under mechanical stress is mainly reflected in its nuclear translocation and activation of specific transcriptional programs. Therefore, the dynamic distribution of YAP, rather than mRNA levels or total protein amounts, is crucial for its functionality.

In the in vivo OHT mice model, although total YAP protein expression in TM tissues remained unchanged, YAP underwent nuclear translocation. This suggests that YAP’s activity depends more on its cellular localization than its total protein level. The mechanical stress from elevated IOP may drive YAP from the cytoplasm to the nucleus, activating gene expression related to mechanical load response. YAP nuclear translocation could be the key step for rapid cellular response to mechanical stress, allowing cells to adapt to pressure, despite unchanged total YAP expression.

This difference in response mechanisms between in vitro and in vivo models highlights the dynamic regulation of YAP as a mechanosensitive molecule in different physiological environments. YAP’s translocation and functional activation may play a more decisive role than its protein expression in the process of cellular response to mechanical stress.

Understanding how mechanical strain experienced by tissues is translated into the cellular fate of TM cells is a critical aspect of glaucoma research. Previous studies have shown that stable stretching and compressive strain ranges of approximately 10% are ideal for in vitro studies of TM cells subjected to oscillatory amplitudes corresponding to physiological IOP levels40. These studies also quantified the relationship between IOP and the intensity of cellular stretching in the AH outflow pathway, revealing that pressure variations between 8 and 30 mmHg can induce up to 50% stretching of the cells in the outflow pathway41–43. Consequently, varying degrees of cellular stretching are frequently employed to replicate the mechanical stimuli associated with elevated IOP. Research employing various stretching levels (6%-20%) has led to different conclusions regarding the role of autophagy in the outflow pathway44, eNOS metabolism13, and cytoskeletal regulation11,17. The mechanical stimuli applied in these studies varied in both intensity and duration, which could account for the lack of a unified understanding regarding how mechanical stimulation regulates cellular outcomes. Alternatively, this variability could offer a new perspective on the role of mechanical stimuli in cellular regulation—suggesting that the effects of mechanical stimulation on cells are not uniform, but rather dynamic, depending on both the intensity and the duration of the stimulus. The significance of the biomechanical environment underscores the robust relationship linking the magnitude and time span of elevated IOP, the pace of glaucoma progression, and its severity3,4.

In our study, we utilized a 15% stretching strain (reflecting the in situ biomechanical stress of the AH outflow pathway) and a 1 Hz oscillatory cycle (reflecting ocular pulse dynamics)40,42,45,46. This approach aimed to unravel the molecular regulatory mechanisms governing the phenotypic transition of TM cells from an early “compensatory” phase to an irreversible “decompensatory” phase following prolonged mechanical stretching (12 h), focusing on the factors driving this transition. Although calcium influx is typically rapid, YAP translocation requires a certain amount of time to occur, possibly due to the accumulation of calcium signals or subsequent cellular responses.

Mechanosensitive cation channels are membrane proteins capable of sensing mechanical stimuli and regulating ion flow. These channels are activated by mechanical forces such as stretching, pressure, or shear stress, allowing cations (e.g., Ca2⁺, Na⁺) to enter the cell and initiate a cascade of intracellular signaling and physiological responses47. Current research provides conflicting evidence on the function of TRPV4 channels in biomechanical sensing, while their role in modulating AH outflow and maintaining IOP equilibrium is increasingly acknowledged. The pathways through which TRPV4 activation regulates AH outflow include F-actin polymerization, ROCK activation, increased α-SMA expression, the tyrosine phosphorylation of RhoA and key adhesion proteins (e.g., focal adhesion kinase, paxillin, and vinculin), as well as the translocation of phosphorylated enzymes to stress fibers. Furthermore, localized interactions between cells and the ECM are enhanced, and ECM protein secretion is increased11,17. Variations in pathological model construction and the use of different TRPV4 activators or inhibitors contribute to inconsistent regulatory outcomes48,49.

As a multi-modal cation channel, TRPV4 can swiftly open in response to mechanical stress, leading to elevated intracellular calcium ion concentration16 and mediating downstream physiological and pathological processes. This process not only facilitates cellular signal transduction but also regulates cell proliferation, apoptosis, and matrix remodeling50. In our study, the TRPV4-specific agonist GSK101 (25 nM) was used to activate TRPV4, thereby mimicking the YAP activation and nuclear translocation induced by mechanical stretching. Concurrently, an enhancement in HTMC proliferation was observed enhanced proliferation of HTMCs, an effect that was effectively reversed by VP, which confirmed TRPV4’s critical role in YAP-mediated responses to mechanical stress in TM cells.

Previous research has confirmed that, independent of the Hippo pathway’s kinase cascade, YAP may directly respond through alternative mechanisms to mechanical stimuli51. The dynamic shuttling of YAP between the cytoplasm and nucleus is a hallmark of its mechanosensitive regulation. When YAP is inactive, it remains phosphorylated in the cytoplasm, where it is eventually targeted for degradation. Upon dephosphorylation, YAP translocates into the nucleus and associates with TEAD family transcription factors, which induces of genes that promote cell growth and resistance to apoptosis30.

In this study, it was observed that after applying 15% (1 Hz) stretching stress for 12 h, YAP was activated and translocated to the nucleus in TM cells, accompanied by increased cytoplasmic p-YAP levels in the cytoplasm. This nuclear translocation was significantly reversed upon treatment with the TRPV4-specific inhibitor HC067047 (Fig. 2). Based on YAP’s known regulatory functions, it is speculated that the time-dependent remodeling of ECM in TM cells, particularly focusing on their phenotypic transitions from an early compensatory phase to an irreversible decompensatory phase under prolonged mechanical stress is closely associated to YAP activity.

Mechanical stimulation-induced TRPV4 activation in HTMCs resulted in ECM deposition and cytoskeletal remodeling, as demonstrated by elevated protein expression of FN, α-SMA, and COL1A1, along with enhanced F-actin stress fiber formation, ultimately contributing to elevated IOP17. The regulatory effects of TRPV4 on TM cells and its subsequent impact on IOP remain subjects of ongoing investigation. This study demonstrated that specific pharmacological activation of TRPV4 using GSK101 for 12 h led to upregulation of FN, COL1A1, and α-SMA, as confirmed by Western blot analysis. Immunofluorescence assays revealed that F-actin stress fibers became thicker and more bundled, indicative of cytoskeletal reorganization, a change that was reversed by VP treatment. These findings suggest that YAP plays a pivotal role in mediating TRPV4-induced changes in cellular stiffness. In line with this hypothesis, stimulation with GSK101 enhances stress fiber formation through the TRPV4-RhoA-ROCK axis11,17 and promotes the release of fibronectin, which directly inhibits trabecular outflow17. Steroid treatment with DEX has been shown to induce the expression of YAP and transcriptional coactivator with TAZ in a dose-dependent manner in human TM cells52. This upregulation was associated with cytoskeletal rearrangements, altered ECM protein expression, reduced cellular permeability, and increased cell proliferation, suggesting that YAP and TAZ are key mediators in DEX-induced glaucomatous changes.

Excessive ECM deposition and cytoskeletal remodeling lead to increased the stiffness of TM tissues, thereby impeding normal AH outflow which results in elevated IOP17,36. In the OHT model using C57 mice, specific inhibition of TRPV4 and YAP mitigated ECM accumulation in TM tissues and significantly reduced IOP in OHT mice. The combined inhibition group exhibited the most pronounced effect, indicating a synergistic role of TRPV4 and YAP in IOP regulation.

In a recent study, Redmon et al.10 found that TRPV4 channels are essential for OHT induction during circadian cycles and in pathological conditions that mimic primary and secondary glaucoma. Inhibition of TRPV4 has been shown to reduce OHT, consistent with the findings of Ryskamp et al.17. These findings help elucidate the range of potential downstream signaling pathways resulting from TRPV4-mediated Ca2+ influx, suggesting that actomyosin contraction regulated by Ca2+ is the most likely shared underlying mechanism of TRPV4 activation.

Our findings further highlight that TRPV4 activation is closely associated to the PI3K/AKT pathway, which in turn modulates YAP’s nuclear translocation and fibrotic gene expression. Specifically, pharmacological activation of TRPV4 results in increased upregulated phosphorylation of PI3K and AKT, promoting the nuclear accumulation of YAP. Conversely, blockade of PI3K or AKT significantly reduces YAP nuclear translocation, reducing ECM deposition and mitigating cytoskeletal reorganization. These observations position TRPV4-PI3K/AKT-YAP as a critical signaling axis that governs TM cell stiffness and IOP homeostasis, and suggest that targeting this pathway could provide effective strategies to reduce IOP and improve TM function. Our findings reveal that the transcriptional regulator YAP functions as a fundamental downstream element of TRPV4 channels in TM cells, responding to changes in the physical microenvironment or pharmacological activation. From a functional perspective, it was observed that after undergoing a certain level of mechanical stimulation, cells respond to TRPV4 activation through YAP nuclear translocation, thereby regulating cellular behavior rather than merely sensing biomechanical stress. This establishes a new and extensive transcriptional mechanism wherein the state of mechanosensitive channels and alterations in cellular stiffness guide cell behavior. These mechanisms may provide insights into how physical forces influence tissue morphogenesis and maintain in vivo homeostasis, particularly as tissues undergo continuous remodeling in response to mechanical environmental changes.

When YAP activity is inhibited, even under TRPV4 activation, cytoskeletal remodeling in TM cells is significantly reduced. This suggests that YAP plays a critical mediating role in the cytoskeletal changes induced by TRPV4 activation. Inhibiting YAP may represent a potential approach as a strategy to mitigate TRPV4 activation-induced cytoskeletal remodeling, potentially offering therapeutic benefits for reducing IOP increases or other pathological conditions associated with TRPV4 activation.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that TRPV4-mediated activation of YAP, promoting cytoskeletal remodeling and ECM deposition in TM cells, thereby enhancing the expression of TM stiffness-related proteins and contributing to IOP elevation. Pharmacological targeting of the TRPV4-PI3K/AKT-YAP signaling axis effectively reduces IOP and reverses pathological remodeling in TM tissues. These findings offer new insights into the pathogenesis of glaucoma and suggest promising therapeutic strategies for its clinical management.
