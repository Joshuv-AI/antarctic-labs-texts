Prevalence and Associated Factors in Nausea and Vomiting Among Pregnant Women in Eastern Ethiopia: A Community-Based Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Megertu Obsa Gelmesa, Fitsum Weldegebreal, Mangistu Abera, Aberash Beyene Derribow, Seboka Abebe Sori, Meron Degefa, Abera Kenay Tura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNausea and vomiting in pregnancy (NVP) is a condition characterized by nausea with or without vomiting that occurs during pregnancy. It ranges from mild morning sickness to the most severe form and affects women with significant morbidity and socioeconomic impacts. Despite this, NVP is less frequently studied in community-based populations with low-income settings than expected. Thus, this study aimed to assess the burden of NVP and its associated factors among pregnant women in Eastern Ethiopia.MethodsA community-based cross-sectional study design was conducted from July 1 to 15, 2021, among randomly selected pregnant women from the ongoing Kersa Health and Demographic Surveillance System. A structured, validated, interviewer-administered questionnaire containing the PUQE score was used. Data were entered into Epi-Data 4.6 and exported to SPSS 25 for analysis. Descriptive statistics and bivariable and multivariable logistic regression analysis were performed. Finally, a p-value < 0.05 in the multivariable regression was considered as a cut-off for statistically significant association.ResultsOut of 502 recruited women, 495 (98.6%) were included in the study. The prevalence of NVP in pregnancy was 70.3% (95% CI 66.5, 74.3). NVP was more likely among primigravida (AOR: 5.57; 95% CI: 2.57, 12.05), multigravida (AOR: 3.58; 95% CI: 2.08, 6.14), women with a family history of NVP (AOR: 3.23; 95% CI: 1.96, 5.43), and women with no exercise before pregnancy (AOR: 6.20; 95% CI: 3.53, 10.87), whereas women with planned pregnancies were less likely to experience NVP (AOR: 0.50; 95% CI: 0.30, 0.84).ConclusionsThe study indicated that 70.3% of pregnant women in eastern Ethiopia experienced NVP. Gravidity, family history of NVP, exercise before pregnancy, and unplanned pregnancy were significantly associated with NVP. Further longitudinal studies are recommended to establish temporal and causal relationships.Keywords: community-based, nausea, pregnancy, vomiting, Ethiopia

Introduction

Nausea and vomiting in pregnancy (NVP), commonly known as morning sickness, is one of the most common minor disorders of pregnancy, experienced by most pregnant women, and usually resolves during the first trimester, with occasional persistence until delivery.1,2 The severity of symptoms, timing of symptom onset, and duration vary both among women and across multiple pregnancies of the same woman. 3 According to the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG), nausea and vomiting in pregnancy are common conditions affecting most pregnant women. 2 Nausea affects approximately 70–80% of the pregnant population, and vomiting is experienced by about 50%.3,4 Nausea and vomiting range from mild to severe, with Hyperemesis gravidarum (HG) being the most severe end of the spectrum. 2 Hyperemesis gravidarum (HG) is defined as severe and persistent nausea and vomiting during pregnancy, resulting in dehydration, weight loss, and electrolyte imbalance.2,5 In Ethiopia, 74.5–74.9% of pregnant women are affected by NVP.6,7

Although various metabolic and neuromuscular factors have been implicated in the pathogenesis of NVP and HG, the exact cause of NVP is unknown. 6 Both mild and severe symptoms can have significant morbidities and socio-economic impacts 6 with profound effects on women’s health and quality of life during pregnancy. 8 Nausea and vomiting in pregnancy not only cause physical discomfort but also have a psychosocial effect on role adaptation and quality of life, resulting in lower acceptance of pregnancy.9,10 In the community, NVP is often perceived as a common symptom during pregnancy that does not require treatment. But in some cases, the severity of NVP can reach critical levels, leading to a range of complications from associated malnutrition, dehydration, and excessive weight loss, thus requiring hospital admission and continuous monitoring.11,12 Understanding the burden of NVP is essential for designing appropriate interventions to decrease the severity or duration of the condition. 6

The majority of existing studies on NVP are facility-based and, therefore, may not capture the full burden of the condition at the population level.6,13-15 In a country with low antenatal booking (39.6%) and institutional delivery (27%), facility-based studies are often subject to bias, as many women are not captured and only those with complications tend to attend health facilities.16-18 Furthermore, important behavioral and lifestyle-related determinants, such as physical exercise and cigarette smoking, have not been adequately addressed in previous studies in the Ethiopian context. Even with the high burden of NVP in most developing settings, existing literature suggests that the prevalence and associated factors of NVP in Ethiopia remain underexplored, with limited empirical evidence documenting their nature and influence on maternal and child health outcomes. Thus, this study aimed to address these gaps by assessing the prevalence of NVP and its associated factors among pregnant women in a population-based open cohort of the Kersa Health and Demographic Surveillance System in Eastern Ethiopia.

Methods

Study Settings and Period

The study was conducted among pregnant women participating in the ongoing Hararghe Health and Demographic Surveillance System (HDSS) from July 1 to 15, 2021. Hararghe HDSS is a population-based open cohort of demographic and health surveillance in three districts in eastern Ethiopia: Harar, Kersa, and Haramaya. The center was established to collect longitudinal population-based data on demographic, health, and environmental issues for planning and evaluating health intervention programs, enhancing research culture in the teaching-learning process, serving as a center for research, disseminating research findings, and promoting the utilization of research evidence to improve health services.19,20 The Harar HDSS covers 12 urban kebeles. Similarly, Kersa HDSS, established in September 2007, covers 24 kebeles, while the predominantly rural Haramaya HDSS, established in 2018, covers 12 rural kebeles. Overall, according to the 2020 Hararghe HDSS update, the surveillance system included 20,560 households with a total population of 115,025 individuals.19,20

Study Design and Population

A community-based cross-sectional study design was utilized. All pregnant women who were enrolled in the Hararghe HDSS constituted the source population, while those living in the randomly selected kebeles during the study period were the study population. Women who were critically ill, had severe mental illness, or had serious pregnancy-related complications (such as severe obstetric emergencies) were excluded due to their potential to impair the ability to provide informed consent and to confound the assessment of nausea and vomiting.

Sample Size Determination and Sampling Procedures

The sample size required for the study was determined by using a single population proportion formula with a prevalence of NVP (74.5%) 6 from a study in Addis Ababa, with a 95% confidence interval (CI), a margin of error of 4%, and a 10% allowance for non-response, yielding a final sample size of 502.

Regarding the sampling procedure, a two-stage sampling design was employed. In the first stage, 15 kebeles were selected by simple random sampling from the 48 kebeles included in the Hararghe Health and Demographic Surveillance System (HDSS). Of the selected kebeles, four were from Harar, seven from Kersa, and four from Haramaya HDSS. In the second stage, the total sample size was allocated proportionally to the number of eligible pregnant women registered in each selected kebele using the HDSS database as the sampling frame. Subsequently, eligible pregnant women were selected from each selected kebele using a computer-generated simple random sampling technique until the required sample size was achieved. Therefore, the primary sampling units were kebeles, and the final sampling units were individual pregnant women (Figure 1).

Study Variable and Operational Definitions

The dependent variable was nausea and vomiting in pregnancy (NVP). The independent variables included socio-demographic factors (age, marital status, religion, residence, educational level, occupation and family size), obstetric characteristics (gravidity, parity, pregnancy interval, gestational age, type of pregnancy, ANC visit, history of stillbirth, history of abortion and number of living children), behavioral and dietary related factors (excessive salivation, pica/food craving, food aversion, cigarette smoking, khat chewing, shisha smoking, social/family support and exercise before pregnancy), and Symptoms, effects and relieving factors of nausea and/or vomiting in pregnancy (gestational age at onset of symptoms (in months), pattern of symptoms, length of nausea per day, number of vomiting episodes per day, number of retching episodes per day, triggers of NVP, effect of NVP, relieving factors and health institution visit due to NVP).

Nausea and Vomiting in Pregnancy (NVP)

The primary outcome was NVP measured as a self-reported experience of nausea and/or vomiting during the current pregnancy. Participants who answered “Yes” were classified as having NVP (coded as 1), whereas those who answered “No” were classified as not having NVP (coded as 0). This binary classification was used to estimate the prevalence of NVP and as the dependent variable in the logistic regression analyses. Among participants classified as having NVP, symptom severity was further assessed using the Pregnancy-Unique Quantification of Emesis (PUQE) questionnaire, which evaluates the duration of nausea, the number of vomiting episodes, and the number of retching episodes during the previous 24 hours. 21 Accordingly, PUQE scores were categorized as;

Mild NVP

PUQE score ≤6. 21

Moderate NVP

PUQE score 7–12. 21

Severe NVP

PUQE score 13–15. 21

Exercise Before Pregnancy

Having a physical activity that normally leaves the woman exhausted, but not breathless (for example, brisk walking, gardening). 22

Hyperemesis Gravidarum

A severe form of nausea and repeated vomiting during pregnancy that leads to dehydration and admission. 23

Social/Family Support

Experience in obtaining support from husband, family, friends, or neighbors, if required. 24

Data Collection Tool and Measurements

Data were collected through face-to-face interviews using the Pregnancy Unique Quantification of Emesis Scoring System questionnaire, adapted from previous similar studies.6,25-28 The questionnaire was prepared in English, translated into the local languages (Afan Oromo and Amharic), and back to English to maintain its consistency.

The questionnaire was evaluated for reliability and validity. Internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach’s α, which yielded a value of 0.891, while content validity was ensured through the content validity index and content validity ratio as evaluated by a panel of experts to confirm the relevance and essentiality of the items. The data collection process was conducted at respondents’ homes to ensure a comfortable and familiar environment, allowing women to speak freely and set up a convenient time for a follow-up when respondents were unavailable at home. Respondents who were not available at their homes after three repeated visits were considered non-respondents. Women were first asked whether they had experienced nausea and/or vomiting during the current pregnancy. Participants who reported symptoms were subsequently assessed using the Pregnancy-Unique Quantification of Emesis (PUQE) questionnaire. The PUQE score was calculated from three items assessing the duration of nausea, the number of vomiting episodes, and the number of retching episodes during the previous 24 hours. Each item was scored from 1 to 5, resulting in a total score ranging from 3 to 15, which was used to classify symptom severity as mild (≤6), moderate (7–12), or severe (13–15). All data collectors and supervisors were trained for two days and performed practical exercises to familiarize themselves with the questionnaires. Eight data collectors who have prior experience in data collection collected the data. Four MSc midwives were used as supervisors.

Data Quality Control

Questionnaires were translated into the local language and back-translated into English to check their consistency. A two-day training was given to data collectors and supervisors on the overall objective of the study, the questionnaire, and the data collection process. A validated data collection tool was utilized. The translated questionnaire was pilot tested on 5% of the total sample size in two kebeles in Harar and Haramaya to ensure semantic equivalence between the original and translated versions before the actual data collection. In addition, the culturally adapted Pregnancy-Unique Quantification of Emesis (PUQE) items were used to ensure clarity and relevance in the local context. The principal investigator and supervisors closely supervised, checked, and reviewed all collected questionnaires daily to ensure completeness and allow for immediate corrective measures. Finally, after checking for data completeness and clarity, each questionnaire was coded with a unique identification number. Simple frequencies and cross-tabulation were done to look for missing values and outliers.

Data Processing and Analysis

The collected data were checked for completeness, coded, entered, and cleaned using EpiData version 4.6, and then exported to SPSS version 25 for analysis. Data completeness was assessed during data entry and cleaning. There were no significant item-level missing data; therefore, all available cases were included in the analysis. Both descriptive statistics and regression analyses were computed. Descriptive statistical analysis was performed to summarize and describe the characteristics of study participants, and the information was presented using frequencies, summary measures, figures, and tables. For the prevalence estimate and logistic regression analyses, NVP was analyzed as a binary outcome (Yes = 1, No = 0) based on participants’ self-reported experience of nausea and/or vomiting during the current pregnancy. PUQE scores were used solely to assess symptom severity among participants with NVP and were not used in defining the outcome variable. Factors associated with NVP were assessed using binary and multivariable logistic regression. All variables with p < 0.25 in the bivariable analysis were selected as candidates for multivariable analysis after checking for multicollinearity.

Multicollinearity was checked using the variance inflation factor (VIF) and standard error. Variance inflation factor (VIF) of >10 or standard error of >2 was considered as expressive of the existence of multicollinearity. No multicollinearity was detected during the analysis. The goodness-of-fit model fitness was checked by Hosmer-Lemeshow statistics (0.234), and the Omnibus test was significant (p-value < 0.0001), indicating good model fit. Adjusted Odds Ratio (AOR) with 95% confidence interval (CI) was estimated to show the association between variables and the NVP. Finally, variables with p <0.05 in the multivariable regression were declared as having a statistically significant association.

Results

Socio-Demographic Characteristics of Study Participants

A total of 502 pregnant women were planned to be included in the study. Of these, 495 were successfully interviewed, resulting in a response rate of 98.6%. Seven participants were not included due to unavailability during the data collection period despite repeated visits and/or refusal to participate. The mean age of study participants was 26.45 (±5.42) years, ranging from 16 to 40 years. The majority of the study participants were married (95.6%), rural residents (89.5%), and housewives (92.12%). Prevalence of Nausea and Vomiting in Pregnancy

One hundred eighty-nine (38.2%) of the respondents reported a family history of NVP. Overall, 348 (70.3%; 95% CI: 66.5, 74.3) women had nausea and vomiting in pregnancy, of whom 49.9% had moderate severity. Among women with severe NVP (PUQE ≥13), 16 (3.2%) reported hospitalization due to their symptoms during the current pregnancy (Figure 2).

In the present study, among participants with NVP, symptoms began in the first trimester in the majority of cases (97.13%). 157 (45.12%) experienced a morning (early) pattern of symptoms, and 38.50% reported that NVP was triggered by taste and/or smell. Additionally, 22.70% of the women reported that the condition affected their work or daily functioning. About 17.53% reported using pharmacological methods, while 43.40% avoided certain foods to relieve symptoms. A quarter (25.90%) of the women sought care from hospitals or clinics for their symptoms (Table 4).

Factors Associated With NVP

In the bivariable analysis, gravida, type of pregnancy, antenatal care, history of abortion, family history of NVP, social or family support, khat chewing, and exercise before pregnancy were associated with NVP at p < 0.25. However, in the multivariable logistic regression analysis, only gravida, type of pregnancy, family history of NVP, and exercise before pregnancy remained significantly associated with NVP. Accordingly, compared to grand multigravida women, primigravida and multigravida women were approximately five times (AOR= 5.57; 95% CI: 2.57, 12.05) and three times (AOR: 3.58; 95% CI: 2.08–6.14) more likely to develop NVP, respectively. Women with planned pregnancies were less likely to experience NVP (AOR= 0.50; 95% CI: 0.30, 0.84). Women with a family history of NVP were three times more likely to develop the condition (AOR= 3.26; 95% CI: 1.96, 5.43) compared to their counterparts. Moreover, women who did not exercise before pregnancy were six times more likely to develop NVP (AOR= 6.20; 95% CI: 3.53, 10.87) than those who exercised. (Table 5).

Discussion

Nausea and vomiting in pregnancy (NVP) are common conditions that may affect pregnancy outcomes and are associated with maternal morbidity. This study was conducted to assess NVP and its associated factors among pregnant women participating in the ongoing Hararghe Health and Demographic Surveillance System in Eastern Ethiopia. This community-based cross-sectional study identified factors associated with NVP in the study settings.

The prevalence of nausea and vomiting in pregnancy was 70.3% (95% CI: 66.5, 74.3). Gravidity, planned pregnancy, family history of NVP, and exercise before pregnancy were significant factors associated with NVP in this study. Our finding was comparable with findings from Ethiopia (74.5%), 6 Turkey(74.0%), 29 Poland (72.0%), 30 Australia (72.0%), 31 Iran (71.5%), 13 and Italy (70.0%). 32 However, it is higher than studies conducted in Canada (63.3%), 33 Spain (63.5%), 34 and Italy (59%). 35 The higher prevalence of NVP in our study compared with Canada, Spain, and Italy may be explained by several factors. Our study assessed symptoms throughout pregnancy, capturing cases that point-in-time studies may have missed. Differences in sample size and sampling methods may also contribute to the observed variations. Moreover, population characteristics, such as socio-demographics, dietary patterns, and cultural factors, may further account for these differences. In addition, the community-based design and inclusive gestational criteria likely allowed detection of cases that might be missed in other settings.

Conversely, our finding was lower than reports from Teaching hospitals of Ethiopia (74.9%), 7 Korea (80.7%), 25 Japan (82.7%), 36 Finland (88%), 37 and China (90.9%). 14 This variation may be related to differences in the study population, the time interval between studies, sample size, gestational restrictions, and study setting (institutional vs. community-based). Compared to the facility-based setting, where the community-based study was conducted, women without NVP may not have visited facilities, whereas women with NVP sought care during that time. This could be explained by the fact that most women visit health institutions only when they face complications or difficulties.16-18

The present study indicated that gravidities were significantly associated with nausea and vomiting during pregnancy. Consistent with previous studies, NVP was more likely among primigravida and multigravidas compared to grand multigravidas.6,38,39 This consistency may be explained by the elevated circulating levels of human chorionic gonadotropin (HCG) and other pregnancy-related hormones, which are particularly pronounced in primigravidae experiencing pregnancy for the first time and in multigravidas with higher cumulative exposure compared to grand multigravidas. Evidence indicates that NVP is more common in primigravidae and multigravidas due to heightened hormonal responses, immune system adaptations, psychosocial factors, and physiological adjustments to pregnancy. These effects tend to diminish with successive pregnancies, rendering grand multigravidas less affected. 40

In this study, planned pregnancy was found to be protective for NVP compared with unplanned pregnancies. This finding is in line with a study from Britain. 41 This consistency may be due to the psychological view of the women towards the unplanned.42,43 However, this finding is inconsistent with the studies conducted in Addis Ababa 6 and Iran. 13 This difference may be due to sociodemographic differences like lifestyle differences, the definition of the plan of pregnancy, methodology that these studies found were focused on severe nausea and vomiting, and vomiting alone. Furthermore, this study also showed that women who had a family history of NVP were more likely to have NVP than their counterparts. This finding is in line with the study conducted in Finland 37 and Italy. 35 A similar association was also reported in a meta-analysis by Gadsby et al 44 This is the fact that the genetic predisposition to the condition, evidence supports that daughters of a woman who experienced NVP are at increased risk of NVP.32,45,46

The current study identified a significant association between exercise before pregnancy and nausea and vomiting during pregnancy. Women with a sedentary lifestyle before pregnancy were more likely to experience NVP than their counterparts. This result is consistent with a study conducted in China. 28 Given that exercise is associated with decreased depression and control of excessive weight gain, 47 which are predictors of this association is expected.43,48 A previous study also demonstrated that lack of leisure-time physical activity before pregnancy was associated with an increased odds of hyperemesis gravidarum. 49 The study showed that both taste and smell are triggers of nausea and vomiting during pregnancy. This finding is supported by various studies.50,51 The findings indicated that due to NVP, women experienced loss of work time, impacts on marital relationships, confinement to staying at home, and avoidance of certain foods, with 22.7% reporting loss of work time. The evidence showed that nausea and vomiting affected work productivity, marital relationships, home confinement, and dietary habits. 52 Furthermore, the results showed that women relieved their symptoms by avoiding certain foods, going to the kitchen, avoiding some drinks, and using traditional medicine or medications. This is supported by a study conducted in Ethiopia on self-management of minor pregnancy disorders during pregnancy. 53 Additionally, the findings showed that 25% of women sought care due to nausea and vomiting during their current pregnancy.

Strengths and Limitations of the Study

The study employed a community-based cross-sectional design with an ideal sample size, enhancing the reliability and representativeness of the findings. Potential biases were minimized through clear objectives, validated and pretested questionnaires, training for data collectors and supervisors, random sampling, and statistical adjustments for confounding variables. Ethical guidelines were followed to ensure unbiased participation. However, even though efforts were made to minimize limitations, the study may have been influenced by recall bias and social desirability bias, as women may provide more socially acceptable responses. Additionally, since a cross-sectional design was used, cause-and-effect relationships could not be established.

Conclusions

The study found that 70.3% of pregnant women in eastern Ethiopia experienced nausea and vomiting in pregnancy (NVP). Gravidity, family history of NVP, exercise before pregnancy, and unplanned pregnancy were significantly associated with NVP. A considerable proportion of respondents did not seek care from health institutions, which may negatively affect maternal and fetal health and reduce women’s quality of life. Therefore, the findings suggest that potentially modifiable factors are associated with NVP among pregnant women in the study area. Future research is recommended to further investigate the causal relationships and broader maternal and birth outcomes of NVP, while also evaluating the effectiveness of lifestyle modification, early treatment, and family planning interventions for counseling and management to improve maternal and birth outcomes.

Recommendations

The following recommendations were given based on the findings to the concerned bodies:

The Hararghe Zone Health Office may consider strengthening counseling on nausea and vomiting in pregnancy during routine antenatal care and encouraging timely healthcare-seeking behavior among pregnant women in the study settings.

Health care providers could strengthen community-based maternal health education by promoting awareness of NVP symptoms and providing advice on lifestyle modifications through health extension workers and existing maternal health programs.

Health extension workers and community health workers may also play an important role in raising awareness about nausea and vomiting in pregnancy and its potential complications during antenatal care, while promoting healthy pregnancy practices, including appropriate physical activity before pregnancy.

Furthermore, future longitudinal studies are recommended to examine the causal relationship between NVP and pregnancy complications as well as maternal and birth outcomes across different settings in Ethiopia.

The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. A formal permission letter was obtained from Haramaya University and submitted to the Hararghe Health and Demographic Surveillance System office and the heads of the selected kebeles to facilitate data collection.

Consent to Participate

Written informed consent was obtained from all participants aged 18 years and above before participation. Furthermore, for participants who are illiterate or aged less than 18 years, written informed consent was obtained from their parents or legal guardians, and assent was obtained from the participants themselves. All consent forms were documented and securely stored by the research team. Confidentiality was ensured by excluding names and other personal identifiers from the questionnaires.
