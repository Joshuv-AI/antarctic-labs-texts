On the Probability of First Success in Differential Evolution: Hazard Identities and Tail Bounds
Dimitar Nedanovski, Svetoslav Nenov, Dimitar Pilev

Abstract
We study first-hitting times in Differential Evolution (DE) through a conditional hazard framework. Instead of analyzing convergence via Markov-chain transition kernels or drift arguments, we express the survival probability of a measurable target set A A as a product of conditional first-hit probabilities (hazards) p t = ℙ ⁡ ( E t ∣ ℱ t − 1 ) p_{t}=\mathbb{P}(E_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}) . This yields distribution-free identities for survival and explicit tail bounds whenever deterministic lower bounds on the hazard hold on the survival event. For the L-SHADE algorithm with current-to- p p best/1 mutation, we construct a checkable algorithmic witness event ℒ t \mathcal{L}_{t} under which the conditional hazard admits an explicit lower bound depending only on sampling rules, population size, and crossover statistics. This separates theoretical constants from empirical event frequencies and explains why worst-case constant-hazard bounds are typically conservative. We complement the theory with a Kaplan–Meier survival analysis on the CEC2017 benchmark suite. Across functions and budgets, we identify three distinct empirical regimes: (i) strongly clustered success, where hitting times concentrate in short bursts; (ii) approximately geometric tails, where a constant-hazard model is accurate; and (iii) intractable cases with no observed hits within the evaluation horizon. The results show that while constant-hazard bounds provide valid tail envelopes, the practical behavior of L-SHADE is governed by burst-like transitions rather than homogeneous per-generation success probabilities.



Keywords
Differential Evolution, First-Hitting Time, Conditional Hazard, Success Probability, Tail Bounds, Plateau Functions, Stochastic Optimization

Introduction

Differential Evolution (DE) is a widely used population-based algorithm for continuous optimization. Adaptive variants such as L-SHADE have demonstrated strong empirical performance across diverse problem classes, yet theoretical understanding of their convergence behavior remains limited. A key difficulty is that these algorithms are inherently non-stationary: the population size, parameter distributions, and archive contents all evolve dynamically during optimization, making standard analytical tools difficult to apply.

In this paper we study L-SHADE in terms of
first-hitting times
. Let

A_{\varepsilon}=\{x\in[l,u]^{d}:f(x)\leq f^{\star}+\varepsilon\},\qquad l<u,\ \varepsilon>0

denote
\varepsilon
-sublevel set of the global minimum
f^{\star}
.

Let
\tau_{A}
denote the first generation at which the population contains a point in
A
.
When runs are terminated by a budget of
B
function evaluations (NFEVs), we also consider the evaluation-indexed
hitting time
\tau_{A}^{\mathrm{eval}}
and the induced maximal generation
n(B)
determined by the (possibly
time-varying) population size.

Our starting point is an exact identity for the survival probability

\mathbb{P}(\tau_{A}>n)
in terms of
conditional first-hit hazards
.
Let
E_{t}
denote the event that the first hit of
A
occurs at generation
t
, and let

\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
be the post-generation filtration encoding the full algorithmic history up to time
t-1
.
Define the conditional first-hit probability (hazard)

p_{t}=\mathbb{P}(E_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}),\qquad t\geq 1,

and the survival event
H_{t-1}=\{\tau_{A}>t-1\}
.
Without any independence or stationarity assumptions, the survival function admits a product representation
in terms of the survival-only hazards
h_{t}=\mathbb{P}(E_{t}\mid H_{t-1})
.
This yields distribution-free tail bounds whenever one can lower bound
h_{t}
on survival.

To turn this viewpoint into a usable bound for L-SHADE, we construct a measurable
witness event

L_{t}

(depending only on the state at time
t-1
) that certifies a favorable configuration for
current-to-
p
best/1 with binomial crossover. On
H_{t-1}\cap L_{t}
we show that the conditional hazard admits a
deterministic lower bound of the form

p_{t}\geq a_{t}>0,

where
a_{t}
is explicit and depends only on (i) discrete sampling rules (index choices and memory slot selection),
(ii) population/archive sizes, and (iii) crossover tail probabilities.
Crucially, this separates the one-step success probability into:

1.

an
explicit theoretical factor

a_{t}>0
(typically small and conservative), and

2.

an
empirical frequency factor
describing how often the witness configuration occurs along the run.

Writing

\gamma_{t}=\mathbb{P}(L_{t}\mid H_{t-1}),

we obtain a survival-only hazard lower bound of the form

h_{t}\;=\;\mathbb{P}(E_{t}\mid H_{t-1})\;\geq\;a_{t}\,\gamma_{t},

and consequently an explicit survival envelope

\mathbb{P}(\tau_{A}>n)\;\leq\;\mathbb{P}(E_{0}^{c})\prod_{t=1}^{n}(1-a_{t}\gamma_{t})\;\leq\;\mathbb{P}(E_{0}^{c})\exp\!\Bigl(-\sum_{t=1}^{n}a_{t}\gamma_{t}\Bigr).

This “constant
\times
frequency” decomposition makes precise why worst-case constant-hazard bounds can be
valid yet pessimistic: even if
a_{t}
is tiny, the cumulative intensity
\sum_{t\leq n}a_{t}\gamma_{t}
can become large
when
L_{t}
occurs frequently (often in short clusters).

A three-step scheme for understanding success within a finite budget.

The paper is organized around the following scheme, which directly targets success within a finite evaluation budget:

1.

Empirical success-by-budget (good functions).

For a fixed budget
B
NFEVs we study the success probability

\mathbb{P}(\tau_{A_{\varepsilon}}^{\mathrm{eval}}\leq B)
.
Functions for which this probability is close to
1
are “good” at budget
B
in the practical sense that
a finite NFEV budget suffices with high probability.

2.

Witness frequency on survival.

For each generation
t
we quantify (and, in principle, estimate from repeated runs) the survival-conditional
frequency
\gamma_{t}=\mathbb{P}(L_{t}\mid H_{t-1})
, i.e., how often L-SHADE enters a configuration where the
one-step hazard admits an explicit certified lower bound.

3.

Certification via the hazard product.

Combining
h_{t}\geq a_{t}\gamma_{t}
with the exact survival product yields a certified tail bound for

\mathbb{P}(\tau_{A_{\varepsilon}}>n)
and, via
n=n(B)
, for
\mathbb{P}(\tau_{A_{\varepsilon}}^{\mathrm{eval}}>B)
.
Thus, for “good” functions, high success probability within a finite budget is explained by the build-up of
the cumulative witness intensity
\sum_{t\leq n(B)}a_{t}\gamma_{t}
.

Empirical survival regimes.

To connect the theory with practice we perform a Kaplan–Meier survival analysis on the CEC2017 benchmark suite
in dimension
d=10
under standard L-SHADE parameters and two evaluation budgets.
The survival curves reveal three distinct empirical regimes: (i) strongly clustered success, where the survival
function collapses in short bursts; (ii) approximately geometric tails, where a constant-hazard model is accurate;
and (iii) intractable cases with no observed hits within the evaluation horizon.
These results support the central message: mathematically valid constant-hazard envelopes are often conservative
because L-SHADE’s practical success is governed by configuration-dependent transitions rather than homogeneous
per-generation progress.

Contributions.

Our contributions are: (i) a hazard-based formulation of first-hitting times for DE yielding exact survival identities
and distribution-free tail bounds; (ii) a concrete witness event
L_{t}
for L-SHADE/current-to-
p
best/1 that yields an
explicit hazard floor on survival; and (iii) an empirical survival study (Kaplan–Meier and geometric envelopes) that
characterizes clustered versus near-geometric success regimes and explains the observed conservatism of worst-case
constant-hazard bounds.

Supplementary Material.

Supplementary Material is publically available at

https://github.com/snenovgmailcom/lshade_hazard_project

online and contains additional empirical analyses, including cluster geometry at hitting times and Kaplan–Meier validation of hazard bounds.

Let

\mathcal{F}_{t}=\sigma\left(S_{0},\dots,S_{t}\right),\qquad t\geq 0.

A\subset[l,u]^{d}
with positive Lebesgue measure (usually
\varepsilon

Define the disjoint family of
first-hit events

\displaystyle E_{0}

\displaystyle=\left\{(\exists i)\,x^{(0)}_{i}\in A\right\}\in\mathcal{F}_{0},

\displaystyle E_{t}

\displaystyle=\left\{(\forall s\leq t-1)(\forall i)\,x^{(s)}_{i}\notin A\ \text{and}\ (\exists i)\,x^{(t)}_{i}\in A\right\}\in\mathcal{F}_{t},\qquad t\geq 1,

and the

\tau_{A}=\inf\{t\geq 0:\ E_{t}\ \text{occurs}\}=\inf\{t\geq 0:\ (\exists i)\,x^{(t)}_{i}\in A\}.

Let the survival events be

H_{t}=\{\tau_{A}>t\}=\bigcap_{s=0}^{t}E_{s}^{c}\in\mathcal{F}_{t},\qquad t\geq 0.

We define the
conditional first-hit probabilities (hazards)
by

p_{0}=\mathbb{P}(E_{0}),\qquad p_{t}=\mathbb{P}(E_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}),\quad t\geq 1

and
survival-only hazard

h_{t}=\mathbb{P}(E_{t}\mid H_{t-1})
,
t\geq 1
. Obviously
H_{t-1}\in\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
, and

h_{t}=\mathbb{E}[p_{t}\mid H_{t-1}]=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{P}(E_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1})\mid H_{t-1}]=\mathbb{E}[p_{t}\mid H_{t-1}].

Also since
E_{t}=H_{t-1}\cap\left\{(\exists i)\,x^{(t)}_{i}\in A\right\}
, then

p_{t}=\mathbf{1}_{H_{t-1}}\,\mathbb{P}\left((\exists i)\,x^{(t)}_{i}\in A\,\big|\,\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right),\qquad t\geq 1,

(1)

so
p_{t}=0
automatically off the survival event.

Conditional Success

All identities and bounds in current section hold for any arbitrary measurable target set
A
.

Lemma 1
.

(see
[
6
]
)
For every
n\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{A}>n\}}=\prod_{t=0}^{n}\mathbf{1}_{E_{t}^{c}},\quad\mathbb{P}(\tau_{A}>n)=\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{t=0}^{n}\mathbf{1}_{E_{t}^{c}}\right].

(2)

Moreover, for every
n\geq 1

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\tau_{A}>n\mid\mathcal{F}_{n-1})=

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}_{H_{n-1}}(1-p_{n}),

(3)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\tau_{A}>n)=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}_{H_{n-1}}(1-p_{n})\right],\quad n\geq 1.

(4)

Lemma 2
.

(see
[
6
]
)
For every
n\geq 0
,

\mathbb{P}(\tau_{A}>n)\leq\mathbb{E}\Big[\prod_{t=0}^{n}(1-p_{t})\Big].

The exponential envelope in Theorem
1
is a classical
discrete-time survival bound: if conditional hazards
\gamma_{t}
are given,
then
\mathbb{P}(\tau>n)\leq\prod_{t=0}^{n-1}(1-\gamma_{t})\leq\exp(-\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}\gamma_{t})
follows from standard survival-analysis
arguments.

Theorem 1
.

(i) Assume there exists a sequence of real numbers
\{a_{t}\}_{t\geq 1}\subset[0,1]
such that for all
t\geq 1
,

p_{t}\geq a_{t}\quad\text{on }H_{t-1}\ \text{almost surely}.

Then for every
n\geq 1
,

\mathbb{P}(\tau_{A}>n)\leq\mathbb{P}(E_{0}^{c})\prod_{t=1}^{n}(1-a_{t}).

In particular, if
\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}a_{t}=\infty
, then
\mathbb{P}(\tau_{A}<\infty)=1
.

(ii) Assume there exists a sequence of real numbers
\{b_{t}\}_{t\geq 1}\subset[0,1]
such that for all
t\geq 1
,

p_{t}\leq b_{t}\quad\text{on }H_{t-1}\ \text{almost surely}.

Then for every
n\geq 1
,

\mathbb{P}(\tau_{A}>n)\geq\mathbb{P}(E_{0}^{c})\prod_{t=1}^{n}(1-b_{t}).

Moreover, if
b_{t}\in[0,1)
for all
t
and
\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}b_{t}<\infty
, then

\mathbb{P}(\tau_{A}=\infty)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(\tau_{A}>n)\;\geq\;\mathbb{P}(E_{0}^{c})\prod_{t=1}^{\infty}(1-b_{t})\;>\;0.

Corollary 1
.

If
a_{t}\equiv a\in(0,1]
, then

\mathbb{P}(\tau_{A}>n)\leq\mathbb{P}(E_{0}^{c})(1-a)^{n},\qquad\mathbb{E}[\tau_{A}]\leq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(\tau_{A}>n)\leq\frac{\mathbb{P}(E_{0}^{c})}{a}.

In particular,
\mathbb{E}[\tau_{A}]\leq 1/a
since
\mathbb{P}(E_{0}^{c})\leq 1
.

If
a_{t}=Ct^{-\alpha}
with
C
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
, then for all
n\geq 1
,

\mathbb{P}(\tau_{A}>n)\leq\mathbb{P}(E_{0}^{c})\exp\!\left(-\frac{C}{1-\alpha}\bigl((n+1)^{1-\alpha}-1\bigr)\right)=\mathbb{P}(E_{0}^{c})\exp\!\left(\frac{C}{1-\alpha}\right)\exp\!\left(-\frac{C}{1-\alpha}(n+1)^{1-\alpha}\right).

If
a_{t}=Ct^{-1}
with
C>0
, then for all
n\geq 1
,

\mathbb{P}(\tau_{A}>n)\leq\mathbb{P}(E_{0}^{c})(n+1)^{-C}.

Lemma 3
.

Let
\{L_{t}\}_{t\geq 1}
be events with
L_{t}\in\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
.
Assume that for all
t\geq 1
,

p_{t}\geq a_{t}\quad\text{on }H_{t-1}\cap L_{t},

where
\{a_{t}\}
is deterministic sequence of numbers.
Then

h_{t}=\mathbb{P}(E_{t}\mid H_{t-1})\geq a_{t}\mathbb{P}(L_{t}\mid H_{t-1}).

Proof.

Since
H_{t-1}\in\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
, we have

h_{t}=\mathbb{P}(E_{t}\mid H_{t-1})=\mathbb{E}\!\left[p_{t}\mid H_{t-1}\right].

On the event
H_{t-1}\cap L_{t}
, the assumption gives
p_{t}\geq a_{t}
, while on

H_{t-1}\cap L_{t}^{c}
we only use the trivial bound
p_{t}\geq 0

Therefore,

\mathbb{E}\!\left[p_{t}\mid H_{t-1}\right]\geq\mathbb{E}\!\left[a_{t}\,\mathbf{1}_{L_{t}}\mid H_{t-1}\right]=a_{t}\,\mathbb{P}(L_{t}\mid H_{t-1}),

which proves the claim.
∎

This decomposition separates algorithmic constants

from empirical frequencies

\mathbb{P}(L_{t}|H_{t-1})
. The remainder of the paper constructs concrete

for L-SHADE.

Population and archive.

Let

The population is

\mathcal{P}=\{1,\ldots,N\}

Let

denote the

,
with corresponding vectors

The archive stores parent vectors that were replaced by better offspring

\lfloor\mathrm{arc\_rate}\times N_{\mathrm{init}}\rfloor

(default

).
See

for the full L-SHADE specification.

The

Let
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{P}
denote the indices of the

m=\max\{1,\lceil pN\rceil\}

where

is a fixed parameter (default

).

Donor pools.

For a target index
i\in\mathcal{P}
and a chosen
p
-best index

,
the admissible donor sets are:

\displaystyle S_{i}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}\setminus\{i,b\},

\displaystyle s_{i}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=|S_{i}^{(1)}|=N-2,

\displaystyle S_{i}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=(\mathcal{P}\cup\mathcal{A})\setminus\{i,b\},

\displaystyle s_{i}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=|S_{i}^{(2)}|=N+|\mathcal{A}|-2.

The first donor

is drawn from

(population only),
while the second donor

is drawn from
S_{i}^{(2)}
(population
\cup

Historical memory.

L-SHADE maintains
H

), storing

(M_{F}[k],M_{CR}[k])
for
k\in\{1,\ldots,H\}
,

.
When generating parameters for individual

, a memory slot

is

, and then:

•

F_{i}\sim\mathrm{Cauchy}(M_{F}[K_{i}],0.1)

, truncated to

•

CR_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(M_{CR}[K_{i}],0.1)

.

Mutation (current-to-
p

Given target index
i
,
p
-best index
b
, donor indices
(r_{1},r_{2})
,
and scaling factor
F\in(0,1]
, the mutant vector before boundary handling is

\tilde{v}_{i}(F;b,r_{1},r_{2})=x_{i}+F\Delta_{i}(b,r_{1},r_{2}),

(5)

where the

is

\Delta_{i}(b,r_{1},r_{2})=(x_{b}-x_{i})+(x_{r_{1}}-x_{r_{2}}).

Thus, for fixed indices, the mutant traces an affine ray
F\mapsto x_{i}+F\Delta_{i}

emanating from the parent
x_{i}
.

Boundary handling (midpoint repair).

Let
\mathsf{BH}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[l,u]^{d}\to[l,u]^{d}
denote the midpoint repair operator.
For a candidate
\tilde{v}
and parent
x
, each coordinate is repaired as:

\displaystyle\mathsf{BH}(\tilde{v},x)_{j}=\begin{cases}(l_{j}+x_{j})/2,&\text{if }\tilde{v}_{j}<l_{j},\\
(u_{j}+x_{j})/2,&\text{if }\tilde{v}_{j}>u_{j},\\
\tilde{v}_{j},&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(6)

The
repaired mutant
is

v_{i}(F;b,r_{1},r_{2})=\mathsf{BH}\bigl(\tilde{v}_{i}(F;b,r_{1},r_{2}),\,x_{i}\bigr)\in[l,u]^{d}.

(7)

The operator
\mathsf{BH}
is Borel measurable (it is piecewise affine in each coordinate).

Binomial crossover.

Given the repaired mutant
v_{i}
, parent
x_{i}
, crossover rate
CR_{i}\in[0,1]
,
and a forced index
J_{i}\sim\mathrm{Unif}\{1,\ldots,d\}

is defined coordinatewise by:

(u_{i})_{j}=\begin{cases}(v_{i})_{j},&\text{if }U_{j}\leq CR_{i}\text{ or }j=J_{i},\\
(x_{i})_{j},&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

where
U_{1},\ldots,U_{d}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{iid}}}{{\sim}}\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)
.
The forced index

ensures that at least one coordinate comes from the mutant.

Selection.

If
f(u_{i})\leq f(x_{i})
, the trial replaces the parent in the next generation;
otherwise the parent survives.
When a trial strictly improves (
f(u_{i})<f(x_{i})
), the replaced parent is
added to the archive

.

Population size reduction (LPSR).

After selection, the population size is reduced according to

N_{\mathrm{new}}=\mathrm{round}\!\left(N_{\mathrm{init}}+(N_{\min}-N_{\mathrm{init}})\frac{\mathrm{NFE}}{\mathrm{MaxNFE}}\right),

. The worst individuals are removed if

.

Lemma 4
.

On the survival event
H_{t-1}
, we have

E_{t}=H_{t-1}\cap\left\{\exists i\in\{1,\dots,N_{t-1}\}:\ u^{(t)}_{i}\in A_{\varepsilon}\right\}.

(8)

Proof.

By definition of survival, for every parent in the current population
we have
x_{i}^{(t-1)}\notin A_{\varepsilon}
, hence
f(x_{i}^{(t-1)})>f^{\star}+\varepsilon
for all
i
.
Now fix any index
i
and suppose the trial lands in the success set:

.
Then
f(u_{t,i})\leq f^{\star}+\varepsilon<f(x_{i}^{(t-1)})
, so selection
must
accept the trial,
i.e.
x_{i}^{(t)}=u_{t,i}\in A_{\varepsilon}
.

Consequently, on

the event that some trial enters

is equivalent to the event that the next population contains an individual in
A_{\varepsilon}
,
which is exactly the first-hit event

(since

implies there were no earlier hits).
This proves (
8
).
∎

Remark 1
.

If the implementation applies a

step after selection (as in L-SHADE’s LPSR),
then (
8
) still holds provided the reduction removes only the

has

, hence is among the best and cannot be removed by remove worst.

Assumption 1

(Index sampling)

Fix a target index

In Section

4.2
An L-SHADE event and hazard lower bound

We now define a measurable event (depending only on the state at time
t-1
) under which
we will find a deterministic lower bound on the conditional first-hit probability

p_{t}=\mathbb{P}(E_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1})

Fix once and for all

0<F^{-}<F^{+}\leq 1,\qquad\Delta_{F}>0,\qquad c_{\mathrm{cr}}\in(0,1),\qquad g^{-}>0,\qquad q^{-}\in(0,1].

Here
[F^{-},F^{+}]\subset(0,1]
is the working range for
F
on which we
require a density lower bound;

\Delta_{F}>0
is the minimal Lebesgue measure of the success-
F

c_{\mathrm{cr}}\in(0,1)

CR_{i}\in[c_{\mathrm{cr}},1]
);

g^{-}>0
is a lower bound on the
F
-density; and

q^{-}\in(0,1]
is a uniform lower bound on the probability
\mathbb{P}(CR_{i}\geq c_{\mathrm{cr}})
.

For each
(i,b,r_{1},r_{2})
define the set of
successful
scaling factors

\mathcal{I}_{t}(i,b,r_{1},r_{2})\;=\;\Big\{F\in[F^{-},F^{+}]:\ v^{(t)}_{i}(F;b,r_{1},r_{2})\in A_{\varepsilon/2}\Big\}.

(9)

We emphasize that
\mathcal{I}_{t}(i,b,r_{1},r_{2})
is a subset of
[F^{-},F^{+}]
and can be a union of
intervals;
\lambda(\mathcal{I}_{t})
denotes its 1D Lebesgue measure (total length).

We encode this in the following assumptions.

Assumption 2

(Parameter sampling)
.

Fix a target index
i\in\mathcal{P}
. Conditioned on
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
:

1.

The memory slot
K_{i}
is sampled uniformly from
\{1,\ldots,H\}
, so

\mathbb{P}(K_{i}=k\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1})=1/H
for each
k
.

2.

Conditional on
(\mathcal{F}_{t-1},K_{i}=k)
, the random variables
F_{i}
and
CR_{i}

are independent, with
F_{i}
admitting a density
g^{F}_{k}()
on
(0,1]
.

Remark 2
.

Assumption

matches the L-SHADE implementation:
pick memory index
k
, then draw
F
from a truncated Cauchy centered at
M_{F}[k]

and
CR
from a clipped normal centered at
M_{CR}[k]
. The numbers
F
and
CR
are conditionally independent given the index
k
.

Assumption 3

(Binomial crossover)
.

Conditioned on
(\mathcal{F}_{t-1},CR_{i})
, the trial
u_{i}
is produced by:

1.

sample
J_{i}
uniformly from
\{1,\ldots,d\}
;

2.

for each

, inherit coordinate
j
from the mutant
v_{i}
with probability
CR_{i}

(independently across coordinates);

3.

inherit coordinate
J_{i}
from the mutant deterministically.

Remark 3
.

In L-SHADE with binomial crossover, it may occur that the trial vector

u_{i}
coincides with the mutant vector
v_{i}
except for the
forced coordinate
j_{\mathrm{rand}}
, so that the sampled crossover
rate
CR_{i}
does not effectively influence the offspring.
In such cases, the original L-SHADE algorithm records

CR_{i}=\perp
and excludes this value from the update of the
crossover-rate memory.
Throughout this paper, whenever we condition on
CR_{i}
or on events
involving crossover, this conditioning is understood to be restricted
to the event of non-degenerate crossover, on which
CR_{i}
is
well-defined.

We can now define the event that there exists a
witness configuration
in the current state.

Definition 1

(L-SHADE witness event
\mathcal{L}_{t}
)
.

Let
\mathcal{L}_{t}\in\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
be the event that there exist:
a target index
i\in\mathcal{P}
,
a
p
-best index

,

and

and a memory slot

such that:

\displaystyle\lambda\!\bigl(\mathcal{I}(i,b,r_{1},r_{2})\bigr)

\displaystyle\geq\Delta_{F},

(10)

\displaystyle\inf_{F\in[F^{-},F^{+}]}g^{F}_{k}(F)

\displaystyle\geq g^{-},

(11)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(CR_{i}\geq c_{\mathrm{cr}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1},K_{i}=k)

\displaystyle\geq q^{-}.

(12)

Remark 4
.

By construction,
\mathcal{L}_{t}
depends only on the state at time
t-1
:
the population and archive determine the index sets and the difference vector
\Delta_{i}(b,r_{1},r_{2})
,
while the L-SHADE memories determine the density

and the distribution of

.
Thus
\mathcal{L}_{t}\in\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
.

However, condition (

) involves the set of

, which depends on the objective geometry.
In general black-box settings this is
not
directly observable without function knowledge;
one may (i) verify it analytically for structured

(ii) approximate it numerically by evaluating

on a grid in

.

4.3
A lower bound on
p_{t}
on
H_{t-1}\cap\mathcal{L}_{t}

For an integer
r\in\{0,\ldots,d-1\}
, define the event

C_{r}(i)=\{\text{the trial }u_{i}\text{ inherits at least }d-r\text{ coordinates from }v_{i}\}.

(13)

Equivalently,
C_{r}(i)
means “at most
r
coordinates come from the parent
x_{i}
”.

Lemma 5
.

Under Assumption
3
, conditional on
CR_{i}=c
, the number of mutant
coordinates inherited into
u_{i}
is

1+\mathrm{Bin}(d-1,c),

so for any
r\in\{0,\ldots,d-1\}
,

\mathbb{P}\bigl(C_{r}(i)\mid CR_{i}=c\bigr)=\mathbb{P}\!\left(\mathrm{Bin}(d-1,c)\geq d-r-1\right).

In particular, define the deterministic constant

\eta_{r}(d,c_{\mathrm{cr}})=\mathbb{P}\!\left(\mathrm{Bin}(d-1,c_{\mathrm{cr}})\geq d-r-1\right).

(14)

Then on the event
\{CR_{i}\geq c_{\mathrm{cr}}\}
,

\mathbb{P}\bigl(C_{r}(i)\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1},CR_{i}\bigr)\geq\eta_{r}(d,c_{\mathrm{cr}}).

(15)

Proof.

Assumption
3
says: one coordinate is forced from the mutant, and each of the
remaining
d-1
coordinates is taken from the mutant independently with probability
c
.
Thus the number of mutant coordinates is
1+\mathrm{Bin}(d-1,c)
.
The event
C_{r}(i)
is exactly the event that this number is at least
d-r
,
i.e.,
\mathrm{Bin}(d-1,c)\geq d-r-1
. This gives the first identity.

For the monotonicity bound (
15
), note that the map

c\mapsto\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{Bin}(d-1,c)\geq d-r-1)
is nondecreasing in
c\in[0,1]
,
so on
\{CR_{i}\geq c_{\mathrm{cr}}\}
, the conditional probability of
C_{r}(i)
is at least

\eta_{r}(d,c_{\mathrm{cr}})
.
∎

Remark 5
.

For any
c_{\mathrm{cr}}\in(0,1)
, choosing
r=d-1-\lfloor(d-1)c_{\mathrm{cr}}\rfloor
yields

\eta_{r}(d,c_{\mathrm{cr}})\geq\frac{1}{2}\quad\text{uniformly in }d,

since the median of
\mathrm{Bin}(n,p)
lies in
\{\lfloor np\rfloor,\lceil np\rceil\}
.
For the L-SHADE initialization

, this gives
r=\lfloor(d-1)/2\rfloor
.

To use
C_{r}(i)
, we need an implication of the form (see Lemma
5
and (
10
)):

v_{i}\in A_{\varepsilon/2}\ \text{and}\ \|x_{i}-v_{i}\|_{\infty}\leq\delta\ \text{and}\ C_{r}(i)\ \Longrightarrow\ u_{i}\in A_{\varepsilon}.

Definition 2
.

Let us fix
\varepsilon>0
and an integer
r\in\{0,\dots,d-1\}
.
We say that
f
is
(\varepsilon,r,\delta)
-crossover-stable
on

if the following holds: for every
v\in A_{\varepsilon/2}
, every
x\in[l,u]^{d}
with
\|x-v\|_{\infty}\leq\delta
, and every index set
J\subseteq\{1,\dots,d\}
with
|J|\geq d-r
, the vector

defined by

u_{j}=\begin{cases}v_{j}&\text{if }j\in J,\\
x_{j}&\text{if }j\notin J,\end{cases}

satisfies

.

Proposition
2
establishes that every Morse function is
(\varepsilon,r,\delta)
-crossover-stable with appropriate sufficiently small

and
\delta\leq\delta_{\max}(\varepsilon,r)
.

The decomposition of
\gamma_{t,i}
into conditional sub-events follows standard probabilistic arguments for adapted processes; see, e.g.,
[
24
]
.

Lemma 6

(Hazard bound on
H_{t-1}\cap\mathcal{L}_{t}
)
.

Assume:

1.

Assumptions
1
–
3
hold.

2.

There exists
r\in\{0,\ldots,d-1\}
such that on the event
\mathcal{L}_{t}
,
for the witness target index
i
and witness indices
(b,r_{1},r_{2})
in
Definition
1
,

v_{i}(F;b,r_{1},r_{2})\in A_{\varepsilon/2}\ \text{and}\ \|x_{i}-v_{i}\|_{\infty}\leq\delta\text{ and }C_{r}(i)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad u_{i}\in A_{\varepsilon}.

(16)

Then on
H_{t-1}\cap\mathcal{L}_{t}
,

p_{t}=\mathbb{P}(E_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1})\geq a_{t},

(17)

where

a_{t}=\frac{1}{H}\frac{1}{m}\frac{1}{s_{i}^{(1)}(s_{i}^{(2)}-1)}(g^{-}\Delta_{F})(q^{-}\eta_{r}(d,c_{\mathrm{cr}})).

(18)

Here
i
is the witness target index from
\mathcal{L}_{t}
.

Proof.

Work on the event
H_{t-1}\cap\mathcal{L}_{t}
.
Since
\mathcal{L}_{t}\in\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
, conditioning on
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
treats the witness objects
(
i
, and the sets
\mathcal{B}
,
S_{i}^{(1)}
,
S_{i}^{(2)}
, the window
\mathcal{I}()
,
and the slot-dependent distributions) as fixed.

Step 1: Reduce

to a single individual’s trial.

By Lemma
4
, on
H_{t-1}
we have

E_{t}=H_{t-1}\cap\{\exists j:u_{j}\in A_{\varepsilon}\}.

In particular,

\{u_{i}\in A_{\varepsilon}\}\subseteq\{\exists j:u_{j}\in A_{\varepsilon}\},

so on
H_{t-1}
we have
\{u_{i}\in A_{\varepsilon}\}\subseteq E_{t}
.
Taking conditional probabilities given
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
yields

p_{t}=\mathbb{P}(E_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1})\geq\mathbb{P}(u_{i}\in A_{\varepsilon}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}).

(19)

Step 2: Define a concrete success event for target
i
.

Let
(b^{\star},r_{1}^{\star},r_{2}^{\star},k^{\star})
be a witness quadruple guaranteed by
\mathcal{L}_{t}
,
so that (
10
)–(
12
) hold.
Consider the event
\mathcal{A}
that, in the construction of the trial for target
i
,
the algorithm selects exactly these discrete choices:

\mathcal{A}=\{b=b^{\star}\}\cap\{(r_{1},r_{2})=(r_{1}^{\star},r_{2}^{\star})\}\cap\{K_{i}=k^{\star}\}.

By Assumptions
1
and
2
,

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{A}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1})=\frac{1}{m}\frac{1}{s_{i}^{(1)}(s_{i}^{(2)}-1)}\frac{1}{H}.

(20)

Step 3: Bound the probability of hitting the success-
F

On
\mathcal{A}
, the mutant has the fixed affine form
F\mapsto v_{i}(F;b^{\star},r_{1}^{\star},r_{2}^{\star})
.
By the witness condition (
10
), the set
\mathcal{I}(i,b^{\star},r_{1}^{\star},r_{2}^{\star})
has

\Delta_{F}
.
By (

), the

satisfies

g^{F}_{k^{\star}}(F)\geq g^{-}
for all

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigl(F_{i}\in\mathcal{I}(i,b^{\star},r_{1}^{\star},r_{2}^{\star})\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1},\mathcal{A}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{I}(i,b^{\star},r_{1}^{\star},r_{2}^{\star})}g^{F}_{k^{\star}}(F)\,dF

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\mathcal{I}(i,b^{\star},r_{1}^{\star},r_{2}^{\star})}g^{-}\,dF=g^{-}\lambda\bigl(\mathcal{I}(i,b^{\star},r_{1}^{\star},r_{2}^{\star})\bigr)

\displaystyle\geq g^{-}\Delta_{F}.

(21)

Step 4: Bound the crossover probability.

By (
12
),

\mathbb{P}(CR_{i}\geq c_{\mathrm{cr}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1},\mathcal{A})\geq q^{-}.

Condition on
(\mathcal{F}_{t-1},\mathcal{A},CR_{i})
and apply Lemma
5
:
on
\{CR_{i}\geq c_{\mathrm{cr}}\}
, the conditional probability of

is at least

.
Thus,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigl(C_{r}(i)\cap\{CR_{i}\geq c_{\mathrm{cr}}\}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1},\mathcal{A}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathbf{1}_{\{CR_{i}\geq c_{\mathrm{cr}}\}}\mathbb{P}(C_{r}(i)\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1},\mathcal{A},CR_{i})\;\middle|\;\mathcal{F}_{t-1},\mathcal{A}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathbf{1}_{\{CR_{i}\geq c_{\mathrm{cr}}\}}\eta_{r}(d,c_{\mathrm{cr}})\;\middle|\;\mathcal{F}_{t-1},\mathcal{A}\right]

\displaystyle=\eta_{r}(d,c_{\mathrm{cr}})\mathbb{P}(CR_{i}\geq c_{\mathrm{cr}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1},\mathcal{A})

\displaystyle\geq q^{-}\eta_{r}(d,c_{\mathrm{cr}}).

(22)

Step 5: Combine the bounds.

Consider the event

\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{A}\cap\{F_{i}\in\mathcal{I}(i,b^{\star},r_{1}^{\star},r_{2}^{\star})\}\cap\{CR_{i}\geq c_{\mathrm{cr}}\}\cap C_{r}(i).

On
\mathcal{S}
, by the definition of

we have

v_{i}(F_{i};b^{\star},r_{1}^{\star},r_{2}^{\star})\in A_{\varepsilon/2}
.
Together with

, the stability implication (

) gives

.
Hence
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\{u_{i}\in A_{\varepsilon}\}
and therefore

\mathbb{P}(u_{i}\in A_{\varepsilon}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1})\geq\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{S}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}).

Using the chain rule (conditioning on
\mathcal{A}
) and Assumption
2

(conditional independence of
F_{i}
and
CR_{i}
given the slot),
we multiply the bounds (

), (

), and (

):

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{S}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{A}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1})\mathbb{P}\bigl(F_{i}\in\mathcal{I}()\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1},\mathcal{A}\bigr)\mathbb{P}\bigl(C_{r}(i)\cap\{CR_{i}\geq c_{\mathrm{cr}}\}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1},\mathcal{A}\bigr)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{H}\frac{1}{m}\frac{1}{s_{i}^{(1)}(s_{i}^{(2)}-1)}(g^{-}\Delta_{F})(q^{-}\eta_{r}(d,c_{\mathrm{cr}})).

Combining with (
19
) yields (
17
) with

as in (

).
∎

Remark 6

(Structure of the bound (
18
))
.

Fix the thresholds
(F^{-},F^{+},\Delta_{F},c_{\mathrm{cr}},g^{-},q^{-})
and the integer
r
.
The lower bound (
18
) decomposes into three types of factors.

1.

Algorithmic/combinatorial factors (computable).

From the discrete sampling rules:

\frac{1}{H},\qquad\frac{1}{m},\qquad\frac{1}{s_{i}^{(1)}(s_{i}^{(2)}-1)}.

For L-SHADE defaults (
H=6
,
p=0.11
) at the initial population size
N=N_{\mathrm{init}}=18d
,
we have
m=\max\{1,\lceil pN\rceil\}
(e.g., for
d=10
,
m=\lceil 0.11180\rceil=20
,
so
1/m=0.05
).

For the two-pool sampling (Assumption
1
),

s_{i}^{(1)}=N-2,\qquad s_{i}^{(2)}=N+|\mathcal{A}|-2.

At archive capacity with
\mathrm{arc\_rate}=2.6
and
d=10

(so
N=180
and
|\mathcal{A}|\approx 2.6N_{\mathrm{init}}=468
),
we have
s_{i}^{(1)}=178
and
s_{i}^{(2)}=646
, hence

\frac{1}{s_{i}^{(1)}(s_{i}^{(2)}-1)}=\frac{1}{178645}\approx 8.7\times 10^{-6}.

Consequently,

\frac{1}{H}\frac{1}{m}\frac{1}{s_{i}^{(1)}(s_{i}^{(2)}-1)}\approx\frac{1}{6}\frac{1}{20}8.7\times 10^{-6}\approx 7.3\times 10^{-8}.

Note that due to population-size reduction (LPSR),
m
,
s_{i}^{(1)}
, and
s_{i}^{(2)}
typically decrease over time.

The crossover factor
\eta_{r}(d,c_{\mathrm{cr}})=\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{Bin}(d-1,c_{\mathrm{cr}})\geq d-r-1)

is a binomial tail probability that can be evaluated numerically.

2.

State-checkable factors.

The witness conditions (
11
)–(
12
) depend only on the L-SHADE memory state, so

\inf_{F\in[F^{-},F^{+}]}g^{F}_{k}(F),\qquad\mathbb{P}(CR_{i}\geq c_{\mathrm{cr}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1},K_{i}=k)

are checkable from the algorithm’s internal state.

3.

Problem-dependent factors.

The quantity
\lambda(\mathcal{I}(i,b,r_{1},r_{2}))
depends on the objective geometry
and cannot be computed without function knowledge
(though it may be bounded analytically for structured
f
).

4.

Empirical frequency.

The survival-conditional frequency
\gamma_{t}=\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{L}_{t}\mid H_{t-1})

reflects the algorithm–problem interaction and must be estimated empirically.

4.4
Tail bounds via event frequency

Define the deterministic survival-only hazard

h_{t}=\mathbb{P}(E_{t}\mid H_{t-1}),\qquad t\geq 1.

Then the survival events satisfy the exact recursion

\mathbb{P}(H_{t})=\mathbb{P}(H_{t-1})\mathbb{P}(H_{t}\mid H_{t-1})=\mathbb{P}(H_{t-1})\bigl(1-h_{t}\bigr),

so by iterating,

\mathbb{P}(\tau_{A_{\varepsilon}}>n)=\mathbb{P}(E_{0}^{c})\prod_{t=1}^{n}(1-h_{t}).

(23)

Now define the
L-SHADE event frequency on survival
by

\gamma_{t}=\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{L}_{t}\mid H_{t-1})\in[0,1].

Proposition 1
.

Assume the hypotheses of Lemma
6
, and suppose
a_{t}
in (
18
) is deterministic.
Then for every
t\geq 1
,

h_{t}=\mathbb{P}(E_{t}\mid H_{t-1})=\mathbb{E}[p_{t}\mid H_{t-1}]\ \geq\ a_{t}\,\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{L}_{t}\mid H_{t-1})=a_{t}\,\gamma_{t},

and consequently, for all
n\geq 1
,

\mathbb{P}(\tau_{A_{\varepsilon}}>n)\leq\mathbb{P}(E_{0}^{c})\prod_{t=1}^{n}(1-a_{t}\gamma_{t})\leq\mathbb{P}(E_{0}^{c})\exp\!\left(-\sum_{t=1}^{n}a_{t}\gamma_{t}\right).

(24)

In particular, if
\sum_{t\geq 1}a_{t}\gamma_{t}=\infty
, then
\mathbb{P}(\tau_{A_{\varepsilon}}<\infty)=1
.

Proof.

By definition,
p_{t}=\mathbb{P}(E_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1})
and
H_{t-1}\in\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
, so

h_{t}=\mathbb{P}(E_{t}\mid H_{t-1})=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_{E_{t}}\mid H_{t-1}]=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_{E_{t}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\ \middle|\ H_{t-1}\right]=\mathbb{E}[p_{t}\mid H_{t-1}],

which is just the tower property.

On the event
H_{t-1}\cap\mathcal{L}_{t}
, Lemma
6
gives
p_{t}\geq a_{t}
.
Therefore,

\mathbb{E}[p_{t}\mid H_{t-1}]\geq\mathbb{E}[a_{t}\,\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{L}_{t}}\mid H_{t-1}]=a_{t}\,\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{L}_{t}\mid H_{t-1})=a_{t}\gamma_{t},

where we used that
a_{t}
is deterministic.
Insert
h_{t}\geq a_{t}\gamma_{t}
into the exact product identity (
23
)
to get the product bound, and use
1-x\leq e^{-x}
to obtain the exponential bound.
The final claim follows because if
\sum_{t}a_{t}\gamma_{t}=\infty
then

\exp(-\sum_{t=1}^{n}a_{t}\gamma_{t})\to 0
, so
\mathbb{P}(\tau_{A_{\varepsilon}}=\infty)=0
.
∎

Remark 7
.

The bound (
24
) decomposes the survival probability into:

•

a
theoretical factor

a_{t}
(explicit but typically small), and

•

an
empirical frequency

\gamma_{t}=\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{L}_{t}\mid H_{t-1})
measuring how often the witness configuration occurs.

Even if
a_{t}
is tiny, the cumulative sum
\sum_{t\leq n}a_{t}\gamma_{t}
can grow large
when
\mathcal{L}_{t}
occurs frequently, explaining why worst-case bounds are conservative
yet almost-sure convergence still holds.

5
Morse functions

Let
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded domain such that
[l,u]^{d}\subset D
, and let
f\in C^{2}(D,\mathbb{R})
. Recall that
f
is a Morse function on
D
if all its critical points are nondegenerate, i.e.,
\nabla f(x)=0
yields
\det\left(\nabla^{2}f(x)\right)\neq 0
,
x\in\operatorname{int}(D)
.

It is well known that a Morse function defined on
[l,u]^{d}
has finitely many critical points, and hence finitely many local minima.

Let
x^{\star}\in\text{\rm Int}\,([l,u]^{d})
be a local minimum of
f
.
By Taylor’s theorem, there exists a neighborhood of
x^{\star}
such that

f(x^{\star}+y)=f(x^{\star})+\frac{1}{2}\,y^{\top}H(x^{\star})\,y+o(\|y\|^{2}),

(25)

where
H(x^{\star})=\nabla^{2}f(x^{\star})
is the Hessian at
x^{\star}
.
Throughout, for symmetric matrices
A,B
, we write
A\preceq B
to denote that
B-A
is positive semidefinite.

Since
x^{\star}
is a local minimum and
f
is Morse, the Hessian
H(x^{\star})
is positive definite.
Let

\mu_{0}=\lambda_{\min}\!\bigl(H(x^{\star})\bigr)>0,\qquad L_{0}=\lambda_{\max}\!\bigl(H(x^{\star})\bigr)<\infty.

Remark 8
.

There exist constants
0<\mu\leq L<\infty
and a radius
r_{0}>0
such that

\mu I\preceq\nabla^{2}f(x)\preceq LI,\qquad\forall x\in B(x^{\star},r_{0}).

This follows from continuity of
\nabla^{2}f
and
H(x^{\star})\succ 0
; for example one may take
\mu=\mu_{0}/2
and
L=2L_{0}
for
r_{0}
small enough.

The ratio
\kappa=\frac{L}{\mu}\geq 1
is the condition number of the Hessian in the neighborhood of the minimum
x^{\star}
.

The function
f
has an
L
-Lipschitz continuous gradient and is
\mu
-strongly convex on
B(x^{\star},r_{0})
.
For
\varepsilon>0
define the local sublevel set

A_{\varepsilon}=\{x\in B(x^{\star},r_{0}):f(x)\leq f(x^{\star})+\varepsilon\}.

If
2\varepsilon\leq\mu r_{0}^{2}
, then
A_{\varepsilon}
is convex and satisfies

B\!\left(x^{\star},\sqrt{\tfrac{2\varepsilon}{L}}\right)\ \subseteq\ A_{\varepsilon}\ \subseteq\ B\!\left(x^{\star},\sqrt{\tfrac{2\varepsilon}{\mu}}\right).

(26)

Throughout this section, we assume that
2\varepsilon\leq\mu r^{2}_{0}
.

Remark 9

(Replacing the Morse assumption by quadratic growth)
.

The arguments in Section
5
do not rely on global Morse theory
(nondegeneracy of saddles/maxima). It suffices that, for the relevant minimizer set
X^{\star}
,
there exist constants
\mu,L>0
and a neighborhood
U
of
X^{\star}
such that for all
x\in U
,

f^{\star}+\frac{\mu}{2}\,\mathrm{dist}(x,X^{\star})^{2}\ \leq\ f(x)\ \leq\ f^{\star}+\frac{L}{2}\,\mathrm{dist}(x,X^{\star})^{2}.

The lower bound is a uniform quadratic growth condition; for nonsmooth objectives it can be
formulated via tilt stability/metric regularity of the limiting subdifferential

[
12

Some preliminary results

Lemma 7
.

Let
f
be a Morse function, and let the number
\varepsilon
be chosen such that
A_{\varepsilon/2}\subset B(x^{\star},r_{0})
.
If
x\in A_{\varepsilon/4}
, then

B\left(x,\,r_{\mathrm{safe}}\right)\subseteq A_{\varepsilon/2},\qquad r_{\mathrm{safe}}=\left(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon}{L}}.

(27)

Proof.

Let
y\in B(x,r_{\mathrm{safe}})
and set
t=\|y-x\|
.
Since
x\in A_{\varepsilon/4}\subset B(x^{\star},r_{0})
, the Hessian bound

\nabla^{2}f\preceq LI
applies, and Taylor’s theorem yields

f(y)\leq f(x)+\langle\nabla f(x),y-x\rangle+\frac{L}{2}t^{2}.

Moreover, since
f
is
L
-smooth on
B(x^{\star},r_{0})
with minimizer
x^{\star}
,
we have
\|\nabla f(x)\|^{2}\leq 2L\,(f(x)-f^{\star})
for all
x\in B(x^{\star},r_{0})
.
Thus, for
x\in A_{\varepsilon/4}
,

\|\nabla f(x)\|\leq\sqrt{2L\frac{\varepsilon}{4}}=\sqrt{\frac{L\varepsilon}{2}}.

Thus,

f(y)-f(x)\leq t\sqrt{\frac{L\varepsilon}{2}}+\frac{L}{2}t^{2}.

For
t\leq r_{\mathrm{safe}}
, the right-hand side is at most
\varepsilon/4
,
and therefore

f(y)\leq f(x)+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\leq f^{\star}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.\qed

Proposition 2
.

Let
f
be a Morse function, and let the number
\varepsilon
be chosen such that
A_{\varepsilon/2}\subset B(x^{\star},r_{0})
.
Define
\delta_{\max}(\varepsilon,r)=(\sqrt{2}-1)\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon}{Lr}}
.

Then
f
is
(\varepsilon,r,\delta)
-crossover-stable (see Definition
2
) on
[l,u]^{d}
for every
0\leq\delta\leq\delta_{\max}(\varepsilon,r)
.

Proof.

Since
u
differs from

in at most
r

,

\|u-v\|\leq\sqrt{r}\,\delta
.

For

\|v-x^{\star}\|\leq\sqrt{\varepsilon/\mu}
.
For
\delta\leq\delta_{\max}(\varepsilon,r)
,

\|u-x^{\star}\|\leq\|u-v\|+\|v-x^{\star}\|\leq(\sqrt{2}-1)\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon}{L}}+\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon}{\mu}}\leq\sqrt{\frac{2\varepsilon}{\mu}}\leq r_{0},

Since

is convex and

holds, giving
\|\nabla f(v)\|\leq\sqrt{L\varepsilon}
.
Taylor expansion yields

Since

Substituting

Lemma 8
.

Assume that
f
is
\mu
–strongly convex on the line segment

\{x_{b}+\tau(v_{b}(F_{0})-x_{b}):\tau\in[0,1]\}
.
Suppose that

x_{b}\in A_{\varepsilon}\qquad\text{and}\qquad v_{b}(F_{0})\in A_{\varepsilon/4}

for some
F_{0}\in(0,1]
.
Define

v_{b}(F)=x_{b}+\frac{F}{F_{0}}\bigl(v_{b}(F_{0})-x_{b}\bigr),\qquad c=\frac{\mu\|v_{b}(F_{0})-x_{b}\|^{2}}{8\varepsilon}.

Then the success–
F
interval
\mathcal{I}_{t}(b,b,r_{1},r_{2})
satisfies

\lambda(\mathcal{I}_{t})\ \geq\ \frac{F_{0}}{3}.

Moreover, if
\theta_{-}(c)\in(0,1)
is the smaller root of
16c\theta^{2}-(3+16c)\theta+2=0
, then

\lambda(\mathcal{I}_{t})\ \geq\ F_{0}\,(1-\theta_{-}(c)),\qquad\lim_{c\downarrow 0}\theta_{-}(c)=2/3.

Proof.

Let
z=v_{b}(F_{0})-x_{b}
and write

v_{b}(\theta F_{0})=(1-\theta)x_{b}+\theta v_{b}(F_{0}),\qquad\theta\in[0,1].

By strong convexity of
f
on the segment
[x_{b},v_{b}(F_{0})]
,

f(v_{b}(\theta F_{0}))\leq(1-\theta)f(x_{b})+\theta f(v_{b}(F_{0}))-\frac{\mu}{2}\theta(1-\theta)\|z\|^{2}.

Using
f(x_{b})\leq f^{\star}+\varepsilon
and

f(v_{b}(F_{0}))\leq f^{\star}+\varepsilon/4
, we obtain

f(v_{b}(\theta F_{0}))-f^{\star}\leq\varepsilon\Bigl(1-\frac{3}{4}\theta\Bigr)-4\varepsilon c\,\theta(1-\theta).

A sufficient condition for
v_{b}(\theta F_{0})\in A_{\varepsilon/2}
is therefore

\frac{3}{4}\theta+4c\,\theta(1-\theta)\ \geq\ \frac{1}{2}.

(29)

Since
\theta(1-\theta)\geq 0
for all
\theta\in[0,1]
, the left-hand side of
(
29
) is bounded below by
\frac{3}{4}\theta
.
Hence (
29
) holds whenever

\frac{3}{4}\theta\geq\frac{1}{2}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\theta\geq\frac{2}{3}.

Thus all
\theta\in[2/3,1]
yield
v_{b}(\theta F_{0})\in A_{\varepsilon/2}
, and

\lambda(\mathcal{I}_{t})\ \geq\ (1-2/3)F_{0}=\frac{F_{0}}{3}.

Rewriting (
29
) gives the quadratic inequality

16c\,\theta^{2}-(3+16c)\theta+2\ \leq\ 0.

For
c>0
this holds for
\theta\in[\theta_{-}(c),\theta_{+}(c)]
, where

\theta_{-}(c)<\theta_{+}(c)
are the two roots.
Since
\theta\in[0,1]
, it suffices to take
\theta\geq\theta_{-}(c)
, which yields

\lambda(\mathcal{I}_{t})\ \geq\ F_{0}\,(1-\theta_{-}(c)).\qed

Remark 12
.

For Morse functions, once the population concentrates near a local minimizer,
local strong convexity implies that a sufficiently large interval of scaling factors
F

produces mutants in
A_{\varepsilon/2}
(Propositions
3
,
Lemmas
8
).
Moreover, donor concentration implies that a positive fraction

of donor pairs are geometrically “good”

As a result, the per-individual hazard bound no longer contains the catastrophic factor

6
Witness regime

The results of the previous sections sugest a qualitative change in the behavior of L-SHADE once the population becomes sufficiently concentrated near a local minimizer. Empirically, and as formalized by Remark
12
, the evolution of the algorithm can be naturally separated into two phases.

In the initial phase, the population may be widely dispersed. Donor differences are uncontrolled, the success –
F
values may be negligible, and the individual success hazard can be suppressed by combinatorial factors. In this regime, no uniform tail bound on the hitting time can be expected.

The second phase begins once the algorithm enters what we call the
witness regime
. In this regime, a non-negligible fraction of individuals is located within a neighborhood of a representative point
x_{b}
in a favorable basin. These individuals act as
witnesses
to the local geometry of the basin. In fact the strong convexity ensures the existence of a success–
F
interval. In the same time the donor concentration implies that a positive fraction
c_{\mathrm{pair}}(t)
of donor pairs are geometrically admissible.

Throughout this subsection, fix tolerances
0<\varepsilon_{\mathrm{in}}<\varepsilon_{\mathrm{out}}
: the target tolerance and the
witness (second) regime
tolerance. For each local minimizer
x_{j}
,
j=1,\dots,K
, define the local sublevel sets

A_{\varepsilon}^{(j)}=\left\{x\in[l,u]^{d}:\ f(x)\leq f(x_{j}^{\star})+\varepsilon\right\}\cap B(x_{j}^{\star},r_{0}),\qquad A_{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{loc}}=\bigcup_{j=1}^{K}A_{\varepsilon}^{(j)}.

The local hitting time and survival event are

\tau_{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{in}}}^{\mathrm{loc}}=\inf\left\{t\geq 0:\ (\exists i)\ x_{i}^{(t)}\in A_{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{in}}}^{\mathrm{loc}}\right\},\qquad H_{t}=\left\{\tau_{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{in}}}^{\mathrm{loc}}>t\right\}.

1/\lceil pN_{t-1}\rceil
.

Since
|\mathcal{C}_{t-1}|\geq m
and
i\neq b
, the set
\mathcal{C}_{t-1}\setminus\{b,i\}

has size at least
m-2
. Therefore, under uniform sampling of
r_{1}
from
P^{(t-1)}\setminus\{i,b\}

(which has size
N_{t-1}-2
), we have

\mathbb{P}(r_{1}\in\mathcal{C}_{t-1}\setminus\{b,i\}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1})=\frac{m-2}{N_{t-1}-2}.

Conditioning on such a choice of
r_{1}
, the set
\mathcal{C}_{t-1}\setminus\{b,i,r_{1}\}
has size at least
m-3
,
and
r_{2}
is sampled uniformly from a set of size
N_{t-1}+A_{t-1}-3
, hence

\mathbb{P}(r_{2}\in\mathcal{C}_{t-1}\setminus\{b,i,r_{1}\}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1},r_{1}\in\mathcal{C}_{t-1}\setminus\{b,i\})\geq\frac{m-3}{N_{t-1}+A_{t-1}-3}.

Finally, on
G_{t-1}
there exists a memory slot satisfying (
32
).
Choosing slots uniformly gives probability at least
1/H
to pick such a slot, and then

\mathbb{P}(F\in[F^{-},F^{+}]\mid\text{good slot})\geq\int_{F^{-}}^{F^{+}}g^{-}\,dF=g^{-}(F^{+}-F^{-}),\qquad\mathbb{P}(CR\geq c_{\mathrm{cr}}\mid\text{good slot})\geq q^{-}.

Multiplying the independent conditional lower bounds yields (
34
).
∎

6.3
A local improvement lemma

The next lemma shows that the descent obtained by moving toward a better reference point is preserved under sufficiently small perturbations.

Lemma 9
.

Fix a local minimum
x^{\star}
and assume
f
is
\mu
-strongly convex and
L
-smooth on
B(x^{\star},r_{0})
.

Let
x,z,x_{r_{1}},x_{r_{2}}\in B(x^{\star},r_{0})
satisfy
f(z)\leq f(x)
.
Fix
F\in(0,1)
and define the perturbed mutant by

y=(1-F)x+Fz,\qquad v=y+e,\qquad e=F(x_{r_{1}}-x_{r_{2}}).

Assume additionally that
v\in B(x^{\star},r_{0})
(e.g., enforced by a “safe radius” choice).
Then

f(v)\ \leq\ f(x)-\frac{\mu}{2}F(1-F)\|x-z\|^{2}\;+\;Lr_{0}\|e\|+\frac{L}{2}\|e\|^{2}.

(35)

In particular, if
\|x_{r_{1}}-x_{r_{2}}\|\leq r_{\mathrm{conc}}
and

Lr_{0}(F^{+}r_{\mathrm{conc}})+\frac{L}{2}(F^{+}r_{\mathrm{conc}})^{2}\ \leq\ \frac{\mu}{4}F^{-}(1-F^{+})\|x-z\|^{2},

(36)

then
f(v)\leq f(x)-c\|x-z\|^{2}
with
c=\frac{\mu}{4}F^{-}(1-F^{+})>0
; hence, under the selection,
the mutant (or any trial whose objective value is no larger than
f(v)
) is accepted over
x
.

Proof.

By
\mu
-strong convexity applied to the convex combination
y=(1-F)x+Fz
,

f(y)\leq(1-F)f(x)+Ff(z)-\frac{\mu}{2}F(1-F)\|x-z\|^{2}\leq f(x)-\frac{\mu}{2}F(1-F)\|x-z\|^{2},

since
f(z)\leq f(x)
.
By
L
-smoothness,

f(v)=f(y+e)\leq f(y)+\langle\nabla f(y),e\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\|e\|^{2}\leq f(y)+\|\nabla f(y)\|\|e\|+\frac{L}{2}\|e\|^{2}.

Finally, because
y\in B(x^{\star},r_{0})
and
\nabla f(x^{\star})=0
,
L
-smoothness implies

\|\nabla f(y)\|\leq L\|y-x^{\star}\|\leq Lr_{0}
.
Combining the last three displays yields (
35
).
If
\|x_{r_{1}}-x_{r_{2}}\|\leq r_{\mathrm{conc}}
, then
\|e\|\leq F^{+}r_{\mathrm{conc}}
,
and (
36
) guarantees that the noise terms are dominated by half of the descent term,
yielding
f(v)\leq f(x)-c\|x-z\|^{2}
with
c=\frac{\mu}{4}F^{-}(1-F^{+})
.
∎

6.4
Two-phase theorem with gap-free post-
T_{\mathrm{wit}}
tails

We now combine (i) a hazard lower bound conditional on the witness event
\mathcal{L}_{t}

(from the earlier hazard analysis), with (ii) the uniform witness probability on
G_{t-1}
,
to obtain a clean two-phase result.

Assumption 5

(One-step success hazard on
\mathcal{L}_{t}
)
.

Let
E_{t}
denote the event “success occurs at generation
t
”,
i.e., the newly generated trials at generation
t
contain at least one point in

A_{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{in}}}^{\mathrm{loc}}
.
Assume there exists a constant
a_{\min}>0
such that for all
t\geq 1
,

\mathbb{P}(E_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1},\mathcal{L}_{t})\ \geq\ a_{\min}\quad\text{on }H_{t-1}.

(This is exactly the type of statement established by the hazard theorem of the previous sections.)

Theorem 3

(Two-phase convergence from the stabilized witness regime)
.

Assume Assumption
5
and let
\gamma_{0}>0
be the uniform constant from
Proposition
5
.
Let
T_{\mathrm{wit}}
be the stabilization time from Definition
5
, and set

\tau=\tau_{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{in}}}^{\mathrm{loc}}
.
Then:

(i)

(
Conditional almost-sure success
)

\mathbb{P}(\tau<\infty\mid T_{\mathrm{wit}}<\infty)=1.

(ii)

(
Post-
T_{\mathrm{wit}}
geometric tail
)
Conditionally on
T_{\mathrm{wit}}=t_{0}<\infty
, for all
n>t_{0}
,

\mathbb{P}(\tau>n\mid T_{\mathrm{wit}}=t_{0})\ \leq\ \mathbb{P}(E_{0}^{c})\exp\!\big(-a_{\min}\gamma_{0}\,(n-t_{0})\big),

where
E_{0}=\{\exists i:\ x_{i}^{(0)}\in A_{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{in}}}^{\mathrm{loc}}\}
.

(iii)

(
Unconditional two-phase bound
)
For all
n\geq 0
,

\mathbb{P}(\tau>n)\ \leq\ \mathbb{P}(T_{\mathrm{wit}}>n)\;+\;\mathbb{P}(E_{0}^{c})\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\exp\!\big(-a_{\min}\gamma_{0}\,(n-T_{\mathrm{wit}})^{+}\big)\right].

Proof.

Fix
t\geq 1
. By Assumption
5
,

\mathbb{P}(E_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1})\geq\mathbb{P}(E_{t}\cap\mathcal{L}_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1})=\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{L}_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1})\mathbb{P}(E_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1},\mathcal{L}_{t})\geq a_{\min}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{L}_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}),

on
H_{t-1}
.
On the event
\{T_{\mathrm{wit}}\leq t-1\}
we have
G_{t-1}
by definition of stabilization,
and Proposition
5
yields

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{L}_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1})\geq\gamma_{0}
.
Hence, on
H_{t-1}\cap\{T_{\mathrm{wit}}\leq t-1\}
,

\mathbb{P}(E_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1})\ \geq\ a_{\min}\gamma_{0}.

Iterating the standard survival recursion

\mathbb{P}(\tau>n)=\mathbb{P}(E_{0}^{c})\prod_{t=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}(E_{t}^{c}\mid H_{t-1})

from time
t_{0}+1
onward on
\{T_{\mathrm{wit}}=t_{0}\}
gives

\mathbb{P}(\tau>n\mid T_{\mathrm{wit}}=t_{0})\leq\mathbb{P}(E_{0}^{c})\prod_{t=t_{0}+1}^{n}(1-a_{\min}\gamma_{0})\leq\mathbb{P}(E_{0}^{c})\exp\!\big(-a_{\min}\gamma_{0}\,(n-t_{0})\big),

proving (ii). Letting

yields (i).
For (iii), decompose

\mathbb{P}(\tau>n)\leq\mathbb{P}(T_{\mathrm{wit}}>n)+\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathbb{P}(\tau>n\mid T_{\mathrm{wit}})\mathbf{1}_{\{T_{\mathrm{wit}}\leq n\}}\right],

and apply (ii) inside the expectation.
∎

8
Conclusion

We presented a hazard-based framework for analyzing first-hitting times in Differential Evolution,
with a particular focus on adaptive population-based algorithms such as L-SHADE.
By expressing survival probabilities as products of conditional first-hit hazards,
we obtained distribution-free identities and tail bounds that hold without independence
or stationarity assumptions.

For L-SHADE with current-to-
p
best/1 mutation, we introduced an explicit algorithmic
witness event under which the conditional hazard admits a deterministic lower bound
depending only on sampling rules and population structure.
This yields a natural decomposition of the survival hazard into a theoretical constant
and an empirical event frequency, clarifying why constant-hazard bounds are necessarily
conservative when success occurs in short clusters rather than at a homogeneous rate.

The Kaplan–Meier analysis on the CEC2017 benchmark suite confirmed this picture empirically.
Across functions and evaluation budgets, we observed three distinct regimes:
(i) strongly clustered success, where hitting times concentrate in narrow bursts;
(ii) approximately geometric tails, where a constant-hazard model is accurate; and
(iii) cases with no observed hits within the evaluation horizon.
In most successful cases, the tightest valid geometric envelope rate was one to two
orders of magnitude smaller than the empirical post-hit hazard, demonstrating that
worst-case tail bounds substantially underestimate typical performance.

The results suggest that the practical behavior of L-SHADE is governed by rare
configuration-dependent transitions rather than by steady per-generation progress.
From a theoretical standpoint, the hazard viewpoint provides a unifying language
that bridges rigorous tail bounds and empirical survival analysis.
From a practical standpoint, it offers diagnostic tools for distinguishing between
clustered, geometric, and heavy-tailed regimes.

Several directions for future work remain open.
First, sharper bounds could be obtained by characterizing the frequency of witness
events along typical trajectories.
Second, extending the analysis to other mutation strategies and adaptive DE variants
may reveal which design choices promote clustered versus geometric behavior.
Finally, combining the hazard framework with explicit global exploration mechanisms
may yield provable almost-sure success guarantees while preserving practical efficiency.
