Hepatic Sarcoidosis Unmasked by Chronic Pruritus and Esophageal Varices: A Case Report
Authors: Ming Zheng, Keroles Saadalla, Sandipa Sharma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSarcoidosis is a granulomatous disease that affects multiple organs, commonly the liver, with manifestations from asymptomatic disease to cirrhosis and liver failure. A 43‐year‐old male presented with generalized pruritus. Liver biopsy demonstrated findings consistent with sarcoidosis, and treatment was initiated. We emphasize timely follow‐up in hepatic sarcoidosis, including esophagogastroduodenoscopy.

Introduction

Sarcoidosis is a systemic, non‐caseating granulomatous disease that affects multiple organs, with the liver being one of the most commonly affected organs after the lungs. Hepatic involvement ranges from 50% to 70% of the cases [1]. Most liver‐related cases are asymptomatic and discovered incidentally with abnormal liver biomarkers. However, sarcoidosis can progress to liver cirrhosis and liver failure in less than 1% of all cases of sarcoidosis [2]. Here, we present a unique case where hepatic sarcoidosis advanced to cirrhosis and the development of esophageal varices.

Case History

A 43‐year‐old Dominican male presented with three weeks of progressive generalized pruritus and dark urine. History included asthma, vitiligo, and long‐term pest control exposure without any prior history of similar symptoms. He endorses social alcohol use and rare cigarette use.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations and Treatment

The liver panel showed a cholestatic pattern and hyperbilirubinemia of 2 mg/dl, and bilirubinuria (Table 1). CT chest, abdomen and pelvis demonstrated right middle lobe infiltrate with nodularity, hepatosplenomegaly, with macronodular/lobular appearance of the liver along with nodular hypodensities in the spleen and scattered lymphadenopathy in the portal cava system, peripancreatic, peri‐splenic, and periaortic, with the largest measuring up to 2.8 cm × 2.5 cm (Figure 1). Additionally, MRI confirmed the findings and MRCP demonstrated nodular hepatic contour without focal liver mass, nor intrahepatic or extrahepatic bile duct dilation (Figure 2). Given the acute elevation of liver enzymes along with imaging showing scattered lymphadenopathy, lung infiltrate with nodularity, and nodularity of the liver and spleen, differentials of autoimmune hepatitis, tuberculosis, malignancy, and sarcoidosis were considered. Laboratory results were benign except for significantly elevated IgG and angiotensin‐converting enzymes (Table 1). This investigation for hepatic sarcoidosis prompted a liver biopsy, which showed numerous non‐necrotic granulomas in the portal tract, supporting the diagnosis. The patient was discharged on prednisone, with plans of outpatient follow‐up; however, the patient returned shortly afterwards due to an episode of upper GI bleed. He received octreotide and ceftriaxone, along with an upper endoscopy performed, where he was found to have esophageal varices requiring multiple banding (Figures 3 and 4). Carvedilol was initiated for secondary prevention of variceal hemorrhage.

Outcome and Follow‐Up

On rheumatological follow‐up, he was transitioned to infliximab, which improved the disease significantly, and we were able to taper down the prednisone. Patient has been doing well on infliximab.

Discussion

Sarcoidosis is a multisystemic disorder primarily affecting the lungs or intrathoracic lymph nodes [2]. Although the liver is the third most commonly affected organ, only around 20% present with clinically significant hepatic sarcoidosis [2, 3, 4]. Furthermore, less than 1% present with portal hypertension and cirrhosis, making our case peculiar [2]. The majority of patients with hepatic sarcoidosis are asymptomatic. Commonly encountered symptoms include fever, abdominal pain, pruritus, or fatigue, which are similar to the initial presentation in our patient [5]. Our patient had elevated ALP and Gamma GT, a pattern commonly observed in patients with hepatic sarcoidosis [6].

Portal hypertension (PH) in hepatic sarcoidosis can occur with or without liver cirrhosis. Various mechanisms have been postulated in the literature, the most common being the blockage of venous outflow by the granulomas or fibrosis. Other mechanisms are secondary to increased portal flow associated with massive splenomegaly, presence of arteriovenous shunts within the granulomas potentially contributing to the rise of portal pressure and the development of Budd–Chiari syndrome or portal vein thrombosis [2, 7, 8]. In our patient, PH followed cirrhosis, so the mechanism is likely similar to that in non‐sarcoid liver disease. Regardless of the mechanism, the result is an increase in portal venous pressure, subsequently leading to enlarged esophageal veins and thus the increased risk of bleeding.

Evidence of nodules, especially hypodense nodules, can usually be seen with imaging, with MRI being the most sensitive modality; however, the definite diagnosis of hepatic sarcoidosis requires surgical biopsy showing non‐caseating granulomas in the liver, along with exclusion of other diseases that can give similar clinical, laboratory, radiological, and pathological pictures [5, 10]. In this case, the most common differential diagnoses, primary biliary cirrhosis and primary sclerosing cholangitis, were ruled out with negative AMA and ANCA, respectively. Other possible etiologies for granulomatous diseases, including tuberculosis, leprosy, brucellosis, francisellosis, listeriosis, schistosomiasis, leishmaniasis, Hepatitis C, drugs, chemicals, and malignancy, were excluded using autoantibody testing, serological testing, and microbiological studies. Moreover, an elevated ACE level and the histopathological finding were consistent with sarcoidosis.

Asymptomatic hepatic cirrhosis doesn't usually warrant treatment; however, in those with symptoms or with laboratory evidence of cholestasis, corticosteroids and/or Ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA) should be considered [1]. As our patient was symptomatic, a tapering dose of prednisone and UDCA was started. Prednisone (low dose 10–20 mg/day or high dose 20–40 mg/day) is recommended for mild and severe symptoms, respectively [1]. It suppresses the inflammation, eventually decreasing the number of granulomas and providing relief of symptoms in mild cases with abdominal pain [11]. However, it sometimes remains ineffective, often requiring the addition of second or third‐line agents like azathioprine, methotrexate, or infliximab. Unfortunately, given the rarity of both disease and related clinical trials, studies demonstrating efficacy and long‐term benefits of these drugs are not well established [12]. Our patient was eventually transitioned to infliximab, given a poor response to steroids, which significantly improved the disease.

As hepatic sarcoidosis leading to PH is rare, it can often go unrecognized. Hence, this case underscores the importance of rheumatologic vigilance for non‐pulmonary manifestations, especially hepatic involvement in sarcoidosis, as it can be potentially progressive and fatal if not intervened early.
