Harnessing hydrogel technology for advanced periodontitis therapy: A multifunctional approach to oral health restoration
Authors: Xiaoqing Zhuo, Shaoqiang Wang, Li-Yuan Liu, Minmin Zhu, Zhenjiang Zhang, Yuqi Zhou, Maoshan Wang, Xudong Deng, Yanxia Miao, Niannian Li, Xue-Ting Wang, Hongguang Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryPeriodontitis is a bacterially driven inflammatory disease that can lead to tooth mobility and tooth loss. Conventional non-surgical therapy, antibiotics, and surgery may be limited by residual subgingival biofilms, antimicrobial resistance, and variable clinical outcomes. This review synthesizes recent studies of hydrogel-based systems for local periodontal therapy, focusing on stimuli-responsive release mechanisms, cargo-loading approaches, and regenerative outcomes in relevant animal models. Across these studies, hydrogels function as localized delivery matrices that can prolong retention at periodontal defects, regulate therapeutic exposure, and support tissue repair. We further evaluate translational considerations, including degradation behavior, long-term in vivo stability, and immunocompatibility. These findings position hydrogels as adaptable platforms for more precise periodontitis management and identify priorities for preclinical optimization before clinical translation.Keywords: periodontitis, hydrogel, drug delivery, antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, tissue regeneration, stimuli-responsive hydrogel, bioactive materials, minimally invasive therapy, clinical translation

Introduction

Epidemiological and pathological characteristics of periodontitis

As a common oral disease, periodontal disease has a high incidence and a wide range of prevalence. According to the 2021 Global Burden of Disease (GBD), severe periodontitis affects about 1.067 billion people worldwide. By 2050, its prevalence is projected to exceed 1.5 billion, affecting more than 44% of the global population.1 Periodontal disease is often present in adults, but some periodontal diseases occur earlier.2 Periodontal disease has many established risk factors, including smoking, diabetes, obesity, psychological stress, and older age.3 In addition, local contributing factors such as malocclusion and unfavorable periodontal anatomy (e.g., root furcation defects, enamel pearls) may promote plaque retention and disease progression.4

Periodontitis is primarily a chronic inflammatory disease of the periodontal supporting tissues mediated by local bacteria.5 Dental plaque biofilms attached to tooth surfaces and periodontal tissues consist of diverse bacteria and serve as the major pathogenic factor of periodontal diseases.6 As plaque accumulates, metabolic activities of periodontal pathogens alter the local oral microenvironment. For instance, Fusobacterium nucleatum reduces the local redox potential to create a favorable environment for anaerobes, thereby facilitating the colonization and proliferation of other anaerobic pathogens.7 Meanwhile, microbial metabolism changes the availability of local nutrients, including oxygen consumption and organic acid production, which further modulates the growth conditions for microorganisms.8 Eventually, the plaque microbiota shifts from a homeostatic community dominated by commensal bacteria to a dysbiotic community enriched with Gram-negative anaerobic pathogens such as Porphyromonas gingivalis and Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans.6,9

At this stage, adherent and co-aggregated pathogens, especially subgingival microorganisms, together with their metabolites, not only directly impair the integrity of epithelial cells but also interact with the host. This process triggers host immune and inflammatory responses, which further lead to gingival inflammation and disruption of periodontal homeostasis.10,11,12 The imbalance between pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages (M1) and pro-resolving M2 macrophages (M2), characterized by M1 predominance and inadequate synthesis of pro-resolving lipid mediators (PLMs) including lipoxins and resolvins, hinders the clearance of apoptotic neutrophils and the recovery of tissue homeostasis.9

Massive infiltration of neutrophils occurs in tissues, accompanied by excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and proteases, which induces oxidative stress and direct tissue damage.13 Subsequently, pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) are released, which not only exacerbate local inflammation but also stimulate stromal cells and osteoblasts to express receptor activator of nuclear factor-κB ligand (RANKL) while downregulating its decoy receptor osteoprotegerin (OPG), thereby promoting the differentiation and activation of osteoclasts and mediating alveolar bone resorption.14 A vicious cycle is thus formed within the tissues: persistent inflammation aggravates microbial dysbiosis, and the dysbiotic microbiota in turn sustains immune activation. This cascade results in progressive tissue destruction, periodontal pocket formation and attachment loss, and ultimately leads to tooth mobility and tooth loss15 (Figure 1).

Beyond compromising oral function and aesthetics, these effects elevate the risk of systemic diseases, significantly affecting patients’ physical and psychological health. Therefore, prioritizing the prevention and treatment of periodontal disease is essential for preserving oral function and promoting overall well-being.

Current situation and challenges of traditional treatment methods

As a chronic inflammatory oral disease affecting more than 50% of the world’s population, the treatment of periodontal disease has always been the focus of clinical research.16 At present, the clinical treatment of periodontitis mainly includes basic non-surgical treatment and later surgical treatment.

The non-surgical periodontal treatment aims to sterilize with mechanical debridement combined with antibiotics, which is the first step in treating periodontal disease in all patients.17 In clinical practice, professional ultrasound or manual instruments are used for gingival debridement and root surface planing to remove local pathogenic factors, such as supragingival and subgingival calculus and plaque.18 However, these mechanical methods, while effective, are time-consuming and prone to tooth site omissions. Moreover, due to anatomical limitations, they often fail to fully remove plaque biofilms from deep periodontal pockets, leading to low bacterial clearance and high recurrence rates.19 Antibacterial therapy includes the use of mouthwash and the use of antibiotics. Although it can reduce the accumulation of plaque, long-term use of Chlorhexidine mouthwash (CHX) will cause side effects such as staining.18 Antibiotics cannot eliminate all pathogenic bacteria, and their local concentrations are low and their action time is short. Overuse can easily cause bacterial dysfunction and microbial resistance, leaving hidden dangers for subsequent disease treatment.20 The overall goal of periodontal disease treatment not only includes controlling plaque to eliminate inflammation, but also restoring the morphology and function of periodontal tissue.21

Surgical interventions for periodontitis include flap surgery for thorough plaque and calculus removal, and procedures such as soft tissue grafts, bone grafts, and guided tissue regeneration (GTR) for restoring periodontal morphology and function.22,23 Flap surgery, a common option when non-surgical therapy falls short, is also the basis for GTR. Yet, these surgeries have strict indications and uncertain outcomes.24,25 Moreover, traditional treatment methods require patients to attend regular follow-up visits, cooperate with clinicians, and maintain long-term, proper periodontal care, which has high requirements for patient compliance and self-discipline.26 However, some patients may not be able to attend and maintain appointments on time due to various reasons, such as lack of oral hygiene knowledge and treatment cost issues, which will affect the treatment effect. In view of the above situation, it is necessary to overcome the limitations of traditional treatment methods and find new treatment methods for periodontal disease. Among diverse biomaterial platforms explored for periodontal therapy, hydrogels have emerged as particularly promising candidates. This narrative review synthesizes recent advances in hydrogel-based periodontal therapies from a structured literature search of PubMed and Web of Science databases (keywords: “hydrogel”, “periodontitis”, “periodontal”, “drug delivery”, “regeneration”; publications primarily from 2017 to 2025, with additional foundational studies included), adopting a narrative approach to enable mechanistic insights and critical cross-comparisons across diverse systems.

Hydrogel-based emerging strategies for the treatment of periodontitis

In recent years, emerging strategies for periodontal disease treatment have evolved from single anti-inflammatory or antimicrobial approaches to multifunctional synergistic therapies.27 Additionally, treatment has shifted from systemic application to local targeting, providing a more precise and effective solution for this chronic inflammatory disease.28 As three-dimensional hydrophilic polymer networks with high water content, tunable mechanical properties, and the ability to mimic the native extracellular matrix, hydrogels can be engineered to provide wet-tissue adhesion in the dynamic oral cavity and enable localized, sustained, or stimuli-responsive drug release.29,30,31,32

Despite these characteristics, recent reviews have primarily focused on hydrogels as antibacterial drug delivery systems, surveying applications in periodontal or broader biomedical contexts from isolated perspectives.33,34

In contrast, a growing body of literature has demonstrated the potential of hydrogels to simultaneously address the multifaceted pathogenesis of periodontitis: combining antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, and osteo-regenerative functions within a single injectable or implantable system.27,35,36 For instance, temperature-responsive and pH-responsive hydrogels have been developed to achieve spatiotemporally controlled release of antibiotics or bioactive ions in the acidic, inflammatory microenvironment of periodontal pockets.37,38 Similarly, hydrogels loaded with metal nanoparticles, growth factors, or stem-cell-derived extracellular vesicles have shown preclinical efficacy in promoting alveolar bone regeneration and modulating macrophage polarization in rodent periodontitis models.39,40,41 This review follows the pathological cascade of periodontitis across its antibacterial, regenerative, and anti-inflammatory phases. Within each phase, we examine hydrogel design strategies including stimuli-responsive delivery, biomimetic extracellular matrix architectures, and immunomodulatory payloads that address the specific constraints of the periodontal pocket. We also critically assess the key translational barriers—particularly degradation kinetics and immunocompatibility—that currently hinder clinical adoption.

Adaptability of hydrogels to the periodontal microenvironment

Hydrogels are hydrophilic materials with a three-dimensional network structure. They can be categorized into natural polymers that mimic the extracellular matrix (e.g., collagen, hyaluronic acid, chitosan, alginate) and synthetic polymers that offer superior mechanical tunability (e.g., polyethylene glycol derivatives, polyacrylic acid).42,43 Hydrogels typically possess tunable physicochemical properties, including mechanical strength, porosity, swelling behavior, and degradation kinetics. Their network stability and reversibility can be modulated through physical crosslinking (hydrogen bonds, electrostatic interactions) or chemical crosslinking (covalent bonds). These attributes have positioned hydrogels as versatile biomedical platforms.29,44,45 However, the periodontal environment imposes distinct challenges—constant moisture, cyclic masticatory forces, irregular subgingival anatomy, dysbiosis, and a chronically inflamed milieu.30 The following sections will examine how hydrogel engineering addresses these demands, with explicit attention to unresolved design trade-offs and translational barriers (Figure 2).

Wet-tissue adhesion and mechanical resilience under masticatory load

The periodontal pocket and gingival margin are continuously bathed in saliva and gingival crevicular fluid, creating a surface hydration layer that compromises the adhesion of conventional biomaterials and rapidly washes away therapeutic agents.30 Simultaneously, periodontal tissues are subjected to complex cyclic mechanical forces during mastication, including compression, shear, and tension; biomaterials with insufficient mechanical strength or fatigue resistance may fracture or be displaced under repetitive loading.30 The combined challenges of a sustained wet environment and dynamic mechanical stress demand that periodontal biomaterials possess essential adhesion and elasticity.

To counter salivary flushing, hydrogels can be surface-modified using agents such as polyethylene glycol (PEG), which acts as a surfactant to enhance intermolecular bonding in chitosan/alginate systems, improving swelling ratios and strengthening wet adhesion.46 Multilayered mucoadhesive films based on thiolated hyaluronic acid and polyvinyl alcohol further extend wet adhesion and residence time on oral mucosa.47 More recent advances in bioadhesive chemistry—such as catechol-functionalized polymers that form covalent and hydrogen bonds with tissue surfaces, physical interlocking and interpenetration strategies that create interfacial mechanical anchoring, and self-healing hydrogel networks—have yielded significantly enhanced adhesion under dynamic wet conditions.48

Specifically, mussel-inspired polydopamine incorporated into a polyacrylamide hydrogel matrix achieves high self-healing efficiency (94.11%) and repeatable adhesion (87% retention after 200 adhering/detaching cycles) through dynamic non-covalent interactions including hydrogen bonding, electrostatic interactions, and π-π stacking.49 Another class of hydrogels utilizes the hydrophobicity of phenyl groups synergistically with zwitterion-mediated electrostatic interactions, achieving superior swelling resistance (swelling ratio <4%) and robust tissue adhesion (adhesion strength of 85 kPa, interfacial toughness of 450 J/m2) under challenging wet conditions.50 Furthermore, by compositing PEDOT:PSS with adhesive ionic macromolecular dopants, a 3D-printable precursor ink has been developed, which, after freeze-thawing, yields hydrogels with high conductivity (1.2 S/m) and satisfactory adhesion, offering a new route for customizable bioelectronic interfaces.51 Concurrently, the mechanical properties of hydrogels can be tailored by crosslinking density and polymer chain composition.52 Chemically crosslinked hydrogels form stable covalent networks with superior mechanical strength and chemical stability53; for instance, alginate hydrogels exhibit tensile properties dependent on crosslinking density, illustrating polymer-composition-dependent mechanical output.54 Hydrogels possessing shape-memory and self-healing capabilities can recover their original form after mechanical damage, which is critical for maintaining therapeutic efficacy under cyclic masticatory loading and minimizing postoperative complications.55

Nevertheless, material design for the oral cavity involves inherent physical trade-offs. Strong mucoadhesion may impede epithelial migration and wound closure within the periodontal pocket. Moreover, increasing crosslinking density to enhance mechanical resilience typically reduces injectability, network porosity, and drug diffusion coefficients.56 The fatigue threshold of currently reported synthetic hydrogels is generally below 100 J/m2, which is far lower than that of biological soft tissues capable of withstanding millions of cyclic loading cycles; for example, the fatigue threshold of cartilage and tendon exceeds 1000 J/m2. Although recent studies have elevated the fatigue threshold of hydrogels to levels approaching those of native biological tissues by tuning crystallinity or viscoelasticity, the long-term performance of these hydrogels under dynamic mechanical microenvironments mimicking the periodontal ligament, such as stress relaxation and cyclic compression, remains to be systematically evaluated.

Injectability and in situ gelation for irregular periodontal anatomy

Periodontal pockets are irregularly shaped, narrow, and often extend deep into the subgingival space and furcation areas, making direct access, uniform filling, and stable retention of pre-formed scaffolds technically challenging.30

Certain hydrogels can maintain a liquid state at ambient temperature, enabling minimally invasive injection into target sites while preserving structural and functional integrity.57 In situ gelation refers to the process where liquid precursors transform into stable gels upon exposure to physiological triggers—such as body temperature, ionic strength, or pH changes—at the target location.58 Temperature-, pH-, and enzyme-responsive behaviors further facilitate intelligent gelation and subsequent controlled drug release within the periodontal pocket.59 The anatomical complexity of periodontal tissues, including the narrow, tortuous, and often partially enclosed morphology of deep periodontal pockets and furcation defect areas, requires that local drug delivery systems achieve adequate filling of the periodontal pocket without the need for surgical exposure.

Biological interface: Intrinsic bioactivity and biocompatibility

The periodontitis microenvironment is characterized by a dysbiotic polymicrobial biofilm containing periodontal pathogens alongside a dysregulated host immune-inflammatory response.30 An ideal biomaterial must not merely be biocompatible but actively interface with this complex biological setting without exacerbating inflammation or introducing foreign-body reactions. Beyond serving as passive carriers, certain hydrogel polymers exhibit intrinsic biological activity. Natural hydrogels such as chitosan and polyvinylamine inherently carry cationic charges that disrupt bacterial membranes and inhibit the colonization of specific oral pathogens.60,61 These base materials can suppress pathogenic bacterial growth and reduce post-surgical infection risks, even before the addition of exogenous antimicrobial payloads.62 Concurrently, hydrogels demonstrate excellent biocompatibility and biodegradability; hyaluronic acid- and chitosan-based hydrogels can be enzymatically degraded in vivo without triggering severe immune responses or causing long-term tissue damage.63,64,65 Research on immunomodulatory biomaterials further indicates that hydrogels can be designed to induce macrophage polarization into the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype and eliminate reactive oxygen species, thus realizing the modulation of the tissue immune microenvironment.66

Network-mediated drug delivery and controlled release

Effective management of periodontitis requires sustained local drug concentrations at the disease site; however, conventional delivery vehicles suffer from rapid clearance, poor retention in the periodontal pocket, and systemic side effects when administered orally. Hydrogels possess strong drug-loading capacity and can encapsulate diverse therapeutics, including small-molecule drugs, proteins, and peptides, with minimal impact on drug activity.63,67 By modulating crosslinking density and network composition, sustained drug release is achieved, extending therapeutic duration and enhancing efficacy.57 For example, methacrylated gelatin (GelMA) hydrogels can simultaneously load tissue-repair-promoting and antibacterial agents.68,69 The three-dimensional network structure facilitates localized, sustained, or stimuli-responsive release within the oral cavity, prolonging drug efficacy and improving therapeutic outcomes.70 The rational design of hydrogels for controlled drug delivery has been extensively reviewed.59

The clinical reality of first-generation local delivery systems underscores these limitations. For instance, a gelatin-based chlorhexidine system exhibits biphasic release with pronounced initial burst followed by rapid attenuation, inconsistent with the therapeutic requirement for long-term maintenance in periodontitis.71,72 Hydrogel engineering can bridge this gap by tuning the properties of the hydrogel matrix to control the release of encapsulated drugs. Higher crosslinking densities retard diffusion to achieve sustained release, while stimuli-cleavable bonds enable on-demand payload liberation in response to pathological triggers.31 This tunability makes hydrogels a pharmacologically versatile platform.

Integrative design trade-offs and translational boundaries

The design of hydrogels for periodontitis treatment entails unavoidable trade-offs among the physical parameters discussed above. Strong wet adhesion may adversely affect both the biodegradability of the hydrogel and its safe clearance from the gingival sulcus, whereas high mechanical strength and high crosslinking density are often achieved at the expense of injectability and drug diffusivity. Critically, the degradation rate of the hydrogel scaffold must be synchronized with the timeline of tissue regeneration.

In the landscape of local drug delivery for periodontitis, several systems have received regulatory approval and entered clinical practice, including non-degradable tetracycline fibers (Actisite®), degradable chlorhexidine gelatin chips (PerioChip®), minocycline PLGA microspheres (Arestin®), doxycycline in situ-forming gels (Atridox®), minocycline ointments (Periocline®), and metronidazole gels (Elyzol®).73,74 These systems achieve localized antimicrobial delivery via physical placement or injection into the periodontal pocket, significantly elevating local drug concentrations and improving clinical indices such as probing depth, clinical attachment level, and bleeding index. However, each carries inherent limitations: fibers require secondary removal and technically demanding placement; chips exhibit pronounced initial burst release; microspheres are prone to leakage from the pocket orifice; ointments offer short duration of action; and in situ gels, despite injectability, still require additional adhesive fixation for retention.73,74,75 Functionally, the majority of these products are restricted to antimicrobial action, with minimal capacity to promote periodontal tissue regeneration.74,75

In contrast, the multifunctional hydrogels reviewed herein integrate the four design dimensions examined in Sections wet-tissue adhesion and mechanical resilience under masticatory load, injectability and in situ gelation for irregular periodontal anatomy, biological interface: intrinsic bioactivity and biocompatibility, and network-mediated drug delivery and controlled release into a coherent therapeutic platform that addresses the limitations of established systems. Their three-dimensional crosslinked networks mimic the native extracellular matrix, enabling not only controlled release of antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory agents but also loading of growth factors, stem cells, or osteogenic factors to serve as tissue engineering scaffolds for alveolar bone and periodontal ligament regeneration—functions that conventional fibers, chips, microspheres, and ointments cannot readily achieve.31 The injectability and in situ gelation capability (Section injectability and in situ gelation for irregular periodontal anatomy) permit adaptive filling of irregularly shaped periodontal pockets or bone defects via minimally invasive injection, overcoming the placement technicality and anatomical incompatibility of pre-formed fibers and chips. Intrinsic bioactivity (Section biological interface: intrinsic bioactivity and biocompatibility)—such as chitosan’s cationic membrane disruption and M2 macrophage polarization—transforms the biomaterial from a passive drug container to an active therapeutic participant, unlike the pharmacologically inert matrices of conventional LDDS. Network-mediated controlled release (Section network-mediated drug delivery and controlled release) enables precise modulation of drug kinetics and stimuli-responsive on-demand liberation (pH, enzymatic, or oxidative triggers), achieving temporal delivery patterns tailored to disease dynamics rather than fixed degradation schedules.

However, this multifunctionality introduces exponentially greater engineering complexity. The mechanical resilience required to withstand masticatory forces and gingival crevicular fluid flushing often conflicts with the injectability and porosity needed for minimally invasive placement and cell infiltration.56 Similarly, the pursuit of strong wet adhesion must be balanced against the necessity for timely scaffold clearance and unimpeded epithelial migration. The degradation rate must be precisely tuned to match the tempo of periodontal tissue regeneration—a synchronization that remains challenging to achieve in vivo.57 Furthermore, many hydrogel formulations exhibit insufficient mechanical strength to resist oral masticatory forces and crevicular fluid flushing, risking premature fracture; some suffer from drug burst release, complicating long-term steady-state maintenance71,72; and the matching of degradation kinetics to tissue regeneration speed remains imprecisely controlled.59 These intrinsic design tensions define the current engineering frontier of periodontal hydrogels.31

Application strategies of hydrogel in the treatment of periodontitis

The pathogenesis of periodontal disease is intricate and can be roughly divided into three stages: bacterial infection, inflammatory response, and the destruction of periodontal tissues.76 In the treatment of periodontal disease, hydrogels can exert their effects by carrying targeted drugs or through their inherent therapeutic properties. In terms of antibacterial efficacy, hydrogels can effectively suppress the growth and proliferation of periodontal pathogens, reduce plaque accumulation, and lower the risk of inflammation by releasing loaded antibacterial agents or through their own antibacterial components.36 Hydrogels enable the sustained release of anti-inflammatory drugs to mitigate inflammation and alleviate pain/swelling, as well as the delivery of bioactive substances to promote the repair and regeneration of periodontal soft and hard tissues, ultimately restoring normal structure and function.77,78,79 Moreover, the emergence of smart-responsive hydrogels has brought new breakthroughs to periodontal therapy. These hydrogels can adapt intelligently to changes in the microenvironment of periodontal tissues under inflammatory conditions (e.g., pH, temperature, or electrical potential gradients), further enabling precise drug release in periodontal lesion areas and dynamic modulation of therapeutic effects, thereby enhancing the specificity and efficacy of treatment.37

Antibacterial hydrogel

Antibiotic-loaded hydrogel

Delivering antibiotics to the periodontal pocket via hydrogels enables high local drug concentrations, effectively eliminating pathogenic microorganisms while avoiding the systemic adverse effects associated with oral administration.80 The stable retention properties of hydrogels within the oral environment facilitate sustained antibiotic release, ensuring prolonged antimicrobial efficacy.81 Current research has integrated clinically common periodontal antibiotics (e.g., minocycline, doxycycline, metronidazole, ornidazole, levofloxacin) with hydrogel carriers, optimizing delivery efficiency through differentiated administration strategies (e.g., local injection, in situ gelation) and smart responsive designs (e.g., temperature/pH-triggered release).82

Ranch et al. developed a doxycycline-releasing in situ hydrogel based on a Poloxamer 407-chitosan-PEG 600 composite system. Doxycycline binds to the 30S ribosomal subunit to block bacterial protein synthesis.83 Pichayakorn and Boonme constructed chitosan microparticle/hydrogel/film systems loaded with nitroimidazoles. These antibiotics are activated by bacterial nitroreductases under anaerobic conditions, generating cytotoxic radicals that damage DNA; concurrently, protonated chitosan (–NH3+) further disrupts bacterial membranes via electrostatic interaction.84 Zang et al. engineered a thermosensitive chitosan/β-glycerophosphate hydrogel co-loaded with ornidazole and BMP-7: Ornidazole follows the same nitroreductase-dependent DNA damage pathway against anaerobes, whereas BMP-7 activates Smad1/5/8 signaling to upregulate Runx2 and OCN, promoting osteoblast differentiation and periodontal regeneration.85 In pursuit of broad-spectrum eradication of periodontal pathogens, Khan et al. developed a degradable chitosan film co-loaded with metronidazole and levofloxacin: Metronidazole targets anaerobic bacteria via the nitroreductase pathway, while levofloxacin inhibits DNA gyrase and topoisomerase IV, blocking DNA replication in facultative anaerobes and aerobes.86 Clinical trials demonstrated significant improvement in periodontal clinical indices, including gingival index, plaque index, and periodontal pocket depth, in patients receiving this film.86

Nevertheless, none of the aforementioned systems effectively cover facultative anaerobes or aerobic periodontal pathogens, leaving a gap in achieving true broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity. Khan et al. attempted to address this gap by combining metronidazole with levofloxacin; however, this dual-drug strategy inevitably increases formulation complexity and regulatory approval challenges.86 Furthermore, none of the cited studies systematically evaluated antimicrobial efficacy using clinically relevant multi-species biofilm models, representing a critical evidence gap in translating in vitro antimicrobial activity to in vivo therapeutic efficacy. Another notable shortcoming, particularly in chronic periodontitis requiring long-term treatment courses, is the burst release of drugs. In the study by Zang et al. , approximately 60% of ornidazole was released within 30 min.85 While such an initial burst release may facilitate rapid infection control, the subsequent insufficient maintenance of therapeutic concentrations could potentially lead to infection recurrence.

Metal ions-loaded hydrogel

Metal ions have emerged as a promising alternative to conventional antibiotics for periodontitis therapy due to their broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity, multi-target mechanisms of action, and low risk of inducing drug resistance.87,88,89 Metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) have attracted particular interest as versatile platforms for periodontal antimicrobial therapy, as they enable sustained release of bactericidal metal ions, controlled degradation, and multi-modal antibacterial effects—including metal ion release, photocatalytic reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, and chelation-enhanced membrane disruption.88,90,91

Among the various metal ions incorporated into MOF-based hydrogels, Ag+, Cu2+, and Zn2+ are the most extensively investigated, yet their antimicrobial efficacy and biosafety profiles differ substantially, necessitating a rational selection based on the specific demands of the periodontal microenvironment. Ag+ exhibits the highest potency, with minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) values against both Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria reaching sub-microgram per milliliter levels. Its mechanism involves electrostatic membrane adsorption, thiol-containing enzyme inactivation, and DNA binding.87,88 Cu2+ displays moderate antimicrobial activity (MIC 5–20 μg/mL), yet offers the unique advantage of concurrent pro-angiogenic and pro-osteogenic effects, promoting vascular endothelial growth factor expression and osteogenic differentiation through activation of the PI3K/Akt and MAPK signaling pathways.92 Zn2+ exhibits the weakest antimicrobial activity among the three (MIC 20–100 μg/mL), but is distinguished by excellent biocompatibility and the ability to inhibit RANKL-induced osteoclast differentiation while promoting osteoblast mineralization.93

Zn2+-based MOFs, particularly ZIF-8, remain structurally stable under neutral pH conditions but can be responsively degraded and released under acidic inflammatory microenvironments, enabling on-demand antibacterial activity.88

Beyond metal ion release, MOFs can function as photocatalytic antimicrobial agents by generating ROS upon light irradiation. For example, porphyrin-based MOFs such as PCN-224 have been shown to produce singlet oxygen (1O2) under visible light, achieving over 99% bacterial killing within minutes.89 The incorporation of metal nodes (e.g., Ti4+ doping) further enhances electron-hole separation and ROS generation efficiency.89 Enzyme-MOF composites represent another advanced strategy: Ce-NTA@Au@NH2-MIL-88B(Fe) exhibits dual enzyme-like activities, hydrolyzing extracellular DNA to disrupt biofilms while generating ·OH to kill exposed bacteria, offering a potent approach against persistent biofilm-associated infections.90

The hydrogel composite systems examined in the above-mentioned studies exhibit multifunctional synergistic effects in vitro and in small-animal models, yet several limitations warrant attention. First, antibacterial evaluations rely on in vitro diffusion assays (e.g., plate counting, live/dead staining), which cannot recapitulate the complex biofilm microenvironment of the periodontal pocket. Second, the pro-osteogenic and cytotoxic windows of copper ions are extremely narrow (5–10 μg/mL vs. >15 μg/mL), but no dynamic monitoring has been performed to determine whether local concentrations remain within this therapeutic window during hydrogel degradation.88,89 Furthermore, the long-term biosafety of MOF carriers remains unresolved: tissue accumulation of Zn2+ after ZIF-8 degradation and the potential genotoxicity of zirconium-containing MOFs (e.g., UiO-66 analogs) have not been evaluated in animal models.92,93

Nanoparticle-modified hydrogel

While metal-ion-based antibacterial approaches can avoid issues such as drug resistance caused by traditional antibiotics, they have drawbacks such as metal toxicity and poor stability.94 In contrast, metal nanoparticles offer better biocompatibility. The nano-size ensures high reactivity and flexible regulation, and they are more stable in carriers. Moreover, metal nanoparticles can eliminate bacteria through multiple mechanisms, including physically damaging bacterial cell membranes, generating ROS to disrupt bacterial metabolism, and inhibiting DNA replication.95 These properties enable environment-responsive antibacterial actions and multifunctional applications, giving metal nanoparticles a broader application scope and better overall performance. Owing to the aforementioned advantages, metal nanoparticles have garnered extensive attention in the field of periodontal disease treatment.96

Silver nanoparticles (AgNPs), gold nanoparticles (AuNPs), copper/copper oxide nanoparticles (CuNPs/CuONPs), and zinc/zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnNPs/ZnONPs) have been extensively studied in the field of periodontal therapy.94,97 Among these, AgNPs represent the most thoroughly investigated antimicrobial nanomaterial, exhibiting nanomolar minimum inhibitory concentrations against both Gram-negative bacteria (e.g., E. coli, P. gingivalis) and Gram-positive bacteria (e.g., S. aureus). Their antibacterial activity is closely related to particle size; AgNPs with a diameter of <10 nm possess a higher surface-to-volume ratio and highly reactive crystal facets, resulting in significantly greater antibacterial efficacy compared to larger particles.94,98 The antibacterial mechanisms of AgNPs primarily involve: (1) electrostatic adsorption to bacterial membranes leading to structural disruption; (2) release of Ag+ ions that bind to sulfur-containing proteins, causing enzyme inactivation; and (3) induction of reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, leading to oxidative stress.98 Notably, the antimicrobial action of AgNPs does not rely on specific molecular targets, making it difficult for bacteria to develop resistance through single gene mutations.95 However, the clinical application of AgNPs is limited by their dose-dependent cytotoxicity and the risk of argyria upon long-term exposure.98

The advantage of AuNPs lies in their excellent biocompatibility and potential for surface functionalization. Unlike AgNPs, the antibacterial activity of AuNPs does not depend on ROS generation but rather acts primarily by disrupting bacterial membrane potential, inhibiting ATPase activity, and blocking ribosome binding to tRNA.99 Zhang et al. coated 4,6-diamino-2-pyrimidinethiol-modified AuNPs (AuDAPT) onto the surface of clear aligners. This coating, stably attached via electrostatic self-assembly, demonstrated significant inhibitory effects against both planktonic P. gingivalis and its biofilm, without causing oral mucosal irritation.99 The multi-target mechanism of action of AuNPs makes them less likely to induce bacterial resistance.

CuNPs/CuONPs possess both antibacterial activity and pro-osteogenic/angiogenic functions, yet their action exhibits a significant concentration-dependent double-edged sword effect.97 Yang et al. integrated CuS nanoparticles into a gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) hydrogel. Upon irradiation with 808 nm near-infrared (NIR) light, the CuS nanoparticles generated photothermal heat, raising the temperature to 50 °C within 2 min and achieving a >97% killing rate against S. aureus, E. coli, and methicillin-resistant S. aureus.97 The antimicrobial effect of this system relies primarily on protein denaturation and irreversible bacterial membrane damage caused by the photothermal effect, rather than on Cu2+ release. Concurrently, this hydrogel promoted alkaline phosphatase activity and calcium nodule formation in bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells in vitro, and in a rat model of critical-sized mandibular bone defects, the bone volume fraction increased approximately 1.5-fold compared to the blank group.97 However, the concentration-effect relationship of Cu2+ is non-monotonic: appropriate concentrations (5–10 μg/mL) activate the PI3K/Akt and MAPK signaling pathways to promote vascular endothelial growth factor expression and osteogenic differentiation, whereas excessive Cu2+ (≥15 μg/mL) can generate excessive ROS via the Fenton reaction, inducing oxidative stress and apoptosis.100

The antibacterial activity of ZnNPs/ZnONPs is relatively weak, but their advantage lies in excellent biocompatibility and bone metabolism-regulating functions.100 Zn2+ acts by inhibiting RANKL-induced osteoclast differentiation and promoting osteoblast mineralization. Green-synthesized ZnONPs exhibit concentration-dependent antibacterial activity against S. mutans and Actinomyces viscosus, and show synergistic effects when combined with chlorhexidine.101

Beyond the limitations common to all antimicrobial hydrogels discussed previously, such as insufficient validation of efficacy against mature subgingival biofilms and a lack of long-term biosafety data—metal nanoparticle hydrogels present the following specific challenges that require consideration: (1) In terms of antibacterial efficacy, AgNPs > CuNPs > ZnNPs; in terms of biocompatibility, AuNPs > ZnNPs > AgNPs > CuNPs. This inverse relationship between efficacy and safety means that a single nanoparticle system can hardly achieve both broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity and long-term biosafety.101 (2) AgNPs with a diameter of <10 nm exhibit the strongest antibacterial activity but also the highest cytotoxicity; therefore, determining the optimal size range for metal nanoparticles is critical for maximizing the therapeutic window, and surface coatings can significantly influence nanoparticle biocompatibility by modulating ion release kinetics.94,95,100 (3) Cu2+ promotes osteogenic differentiation at 5–10 μg/mL but induces oxidative stress and apoptosis at ≥15 μg/mL via Fenton reaction-generated ROS, highlighting the importance of precisely controlling release kinetics.97,100 (4) Batch-to-batch variations in the chemical composition of plant extracts lead to fluctuations in the size, morphology, and surface properties of the resulting nanoparticles, severely hindering product reproducibility and standardization.101 Future research must intensify efforts to address these issues.

Intrinsically antibacterial hydrogels

Unlike the aforementioned hydrogels that rely on exogenous loading of antimicrobial agents, metal ions, or nanoparticles, intrinsically antibacterial hydrogels exert their bactericidal activity through the physicochemical properties of the polymer backbone itself, thereby sustaining antimicrobial efficacy even after the release of loaded drugs has been completed. This provides a non-negligible independent antibacterial function, thus reducing dependence on exogenous antibiotics and mitigating the risk of drug resistance.

Chitosan, a partially deacetylated derivative of chitin, is the archetypal intrinsically antimicrobial polysaccharide. Its mechanism is rooted in the protonation of primary amino groups (pKa ≈6.5), which generates polycationic domains that electrostatically bind anionic bacterial surface constituents—lipopolysaccharides in Gram-negative species and teichoic acids in Gram-positive organisms.58,60 This interaction disrupts membrane integrity, elevates permeability, and induces cytoplasmic leakage.60 Critically, the periodontal pocket under inflammatory conditions often exhibits localized acidification (pH 5.0–6.5) due to bacterial glycolysis and host inflammatory exudate, which may potentiate chitosan’s native antimicrobial activity without requiring exogenous acidification. However, the pH-dependent charge density of native chitosan limits its efficacy near neutral pH. Quaternization—introducing permanent cationic charges such as trimethylammonium groups—decouples antimicrobial activity from environmental proton concentration.102

Krishnankutty et al. developed quaternized chitosan (QC) porous membranes composited with strontium-containing nanoapatite, demonstrating that the introduction of quaternary ammonium groups endowed the material with permanent positive charges, thereby significantly enhancing its intrinsic antibacterial activity against periodontal pathogens compared to unmodified chitosan.103

Similarly, Yu et al. fabricated a phenylboronic acid-modified quaternary ammonium chitosan hydrogel (QPT@ZIF90-W), in which the quaternized chitosan backbone served as the primary bactericidal component, achieving over 90% antibacterial efficacy against Porphyromonas gingivalis and Fusobacterium nucleatum through electrostatic disruption of bacterial cell membranes.103,104 Notably, in both systems, the quaternized chitosan matrix provided intrinsic antimicrobial functionality that operated independently of any loaded therapeutic agents, ensuring sustained bactericidal activity throughout the scaffold’s residence in the periodontal pocket. These studies illustrate a critical design principle: the hydrogel backbone is not pharmacologically inert but actively participates in pathogen suppression, reducing the antibiotic burden required for therapeutic efficacy.

Nevertheless, several limitations persist. First, the cationic nature of quaternized chitosan, while essential for antibacterial activity, may compromise host cell compatibility at elevated concentrations or substitution degrees. Second, antibacterial evaluations in both studies were limited to planktonic bacterial models, leaving the performance against mature subgingival biofilms, where the dense extracellular matrix may impede cationic polymer penetration—largely unexplored. Third, translational evidence remains immature; neither study provided long-term in vivo biosafety data in large-animal models or addressed regulatory pathways for clinical approval.

Hydrogels for tissue regeneration

The complexity of periodontal tissue regeneration lies in the need for coordinated repair of alveolar bone, cementum, periodontal ligament, and gingival soft tissues simultaneously, a demand that traditional materials struggle to meet.105 Hydrogels, with their three-dimensional microenvironment mimicking the extracellular matrix (ECM), tunable physicochemical properties, and precise drug delivery capabilities, not only serve as structural scaffolds to guide directed cell migration and differentiation, but also regulate periodontal tissue regeneration by carrying bioactive components, making them the ideal vehicle to overcome this limitation.106,107 Additionally, hydrogels encapsulating functional payloads such as exosomes, growth factors, and herbal extracts optimize the periodontal microenvironment to directly or indirectly promote periodontal tissue repair and regeneration (Figure 3).

Hydrogels for delivery of growth factors and bioactive molecules

Hydrogel-based delivery systems for growth factors and bioactive molecules aim to actively regulate the recruitment, proliferation, and differentiation of host cells by mimicking the temporal signaling cascade of natural tissue repair, thereby achieving functional periodontal tissue regeneration while reducing drug dosage and minimizing potential side effects.108 The core advantage of this strategy lies in the ability of hydrogels to protect labile bioactive molecules through their three-dimensional network structure and to maintain spatiotemporal therapeutic concentrations at periodontal defect sites via sustained release mechanisms.78,109

Tan et al. utilized NapFFY supramolecular hydrogels to co-deliver stromal cell-derived factor-1 (SDF-1) and bone morphogenetic protein-2 (BMP-2), achieving synchronized sustained release of both factors for up to 35 days and synergistic periodontal bone regeneration.39 As illustrated in Figure 4, SDF-1 binds to the transmembrane G protein-coupled receptor CXCR4, activating downstream PI3K/Akt and MAPK/ERK signaling pathways to recruit endogenous bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells and periodontal ligament stem cells to the defect site, thereby ensuring directed cell homing and providing a cellular source for subsequent differentiation. Meanwhile, BMP-2 forms heterotetrameric complexes with type I and type II serine/threonine kinase receptors on the cell membrane, activating downstream Smad1/5/8 signaling, which subsequently forms complexes with Smad4 for nuclear translocation to regulate the expression of key osteogenic transcription factors such as Runx2 and Osterix, thereby exerting pro-osteogenic and cementogenic effects following cell homing.39 Notably, Tan et al. observed that the initial release rate of BMP-2 exceeded that of SDF-1, which correlates with their molecular weight difference (BMP-2: 15–16 kDa; SDF-1: 7 kDa). However, ideal bone regeneration may require SDF-1 release prior to BMP-2 to achieve sequential cell recruitment and differentiation induction, representing a challenge in temporal regulation that current systems have yet to address.

Zang et al. employed another pro-osteogenic factor, BMP-7 (also known as osteogenic protein-1). Although BMP-7 and BMP-2 belong to the same BMP family, they differ in receptor binding preference and downstream signaling networks: BMP-7 exhibits higher affinity for type I receptors ALK2 and ALK6 and possesses stronger anti-inflammatory and pro-chondrogenic activities alongside its osteogenic effects.85 Like BMP-2, BMP-7 signals through the Smad1/5/8 pathway depicted in Figure 4, ultimately upregulating Runx2 to drive osteogenesis. Ornidazole, activated by nitroreductase, induces DNA damage in anaerobic bacteria, effectively controlling postoperative infection and providing a sterile microenvironment for BMP-7-mediated osteogenesis. However, potential pharmacokinetic interplay exists between the two agents: ornidazole exhibits approximately 60% release within 30 min, whereas BMP-7 is slowly released over 14 days (approximately 80% cumulative release), suggesting temporal separation of anti-infection and pro-osteogenic functions.85

Building upon cell homing and osteogenic/cementogenic differentiation, Sowmya et al. further constructed a tri-layered nanocomposite hydrogel scaffold mimicking the native hierarchical architecture of the periodontium, with each layer loaded with tissue-specific signaling molecules to achieve synchronized and directed regeneration of cementum, periodontal ligament, and alveolar bone.110 This multi-factor approach coordinates the signaling networks summarized in Figure 4, wherein distinct pathways converge on periodontal tissue repair. Specifically: (1) recombinant human cementum protein 1 (rhCEMP1) binds to integrin receptors on periodontal ligament stem cells, activating the FAK/Src signaling pathway to upregulate cementogenic genes (e.g., CAP, BSP, OPN) and promote ordered deposition of hydroxyapatite crystals; (2) fibroblast growth factor 2 (FGF2) binds to FGFR1 receptors on fibroblast surfaces, activating the Ras/MAPK signaling pathway to promote the proliferation, migration, and type I collagen secretion of periodontal ligament fibroblasts, as well as inducing orderly arrangement of newly formed collagen fibers and their functional attachment to cementum and alveolar bone surfaces—namely, the formation of Sharpey’s fibers, a critical indicator of functional periodontal regeneration; and (3) platelet-rich plasma (PRP) contains high concentrations of multiple growth factors, including platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), transforming growth factor β (TGF-β), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), and insulin-like growth factor (IGF). PDGF recruits osteoprogenitors and promotes their proliferation, TGF-β regulates extracellular matrix synthesis, VEGF induces neovascularization to provide nutritional support, and IGF promotes terminal differentiation of osteoblasts.110 This composite system achieves true simultaneous multi-tissue regeneration.

The three studies described above represent three representative strategies for growth factor-delivering hydrogels in periodontal regeneration: “cell recruitment + differentiation” dual-factor synergy, “anti-infection + pro-osteogenesis” functional integration, and “tissue-specific” layered delivery. Nevertheless, several limitations persist. First, the dose-effect relationship remains poorly defined. Although BMP-2 and BMP-7 potently induce osteogenesis, high doses may cause adverse effects such as heterotopic ossification, local inflammation, and bone resorption; current dosing regimens are primarily based on empirical cell culture studies, lacking systematic in vivo optimization.39,110,111 Second, temporal regulation of multi-factor synergistic release is challenging. Third, although the tri-layered structure of Sowmya et al. restricts growth factor diffusion through spatial compartmentalization, the risk of signal molecule cross-diffusion at layer interfaces remains, which may lead to ectopic ossification in the cementum region or aberrant mineralization in the periodontal ligament region. Concurrently, mechanical weak points between layers may compromise functional integration of newly formed tissues under masticatory loads. Finally, the regenerative efficacy of these materials is closely linked to their system complexity, and all studies were conducted in healthy animal models without validation in chronic inflammatory microenvironments, where inflammatory cytokines may inhibit BMP-induced osteogenic differentiation via NF-κB pathway activation, and elevated protease activity may accelerate growth factor degradation.112

Hydrogels loaded with stem cells and their extracellular vesicles

In periodontal tissues, mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) can differentiate into cementoblasts, osteoblasts, and fibroblasts.113 However, studies reveal that their core repair mechanisms primarily rely on paracrine effects, regulating the local microenvironment through secretion of cytokines, growth factors, and extracellular vesicles (e.g., exosomes).114 Experimental evidence demonstrates that MSC exosomes promote the migration and proliferation of periodontal ligament cells via CD73-mediated adenosine receptor activation, subsequently triggering the AKT/ERK signaling pathways to regenerate alveolar bone and periodontal ligament.115 MSC derivatives are rapidly cleared when used alone in vivo, leading to diminished therapeutic efficacy.40 To address this limitation, researchers have employed hydrogels as carrier matrices to load exosomes into periodontal tissues. This strategy ensures sustained retention and targeted delivery of exosomes, thereby maintaining therapeutic effectiveness.

The combination strategies of stem cells/EVs from different sources with hydrogels exhibit significant differences in their targets, mechanisms, and limitations. Liu et al. incorporated BMSC-derived small extracellular vesicles (BMSC-sEVs) into a gelatin/laponite nanocomposite hydrogel, which inhibited osteoclast activity via the OPG-RANKL-RANK axis and promoted M2 macrophage polarization, thereby ameliorating the immune microenvironment and enhancing bone regeneration.116 This strategy combines immunomodulatory and osteogenic effects; however, the mechanical properties and degradation rate of the hydrogel require further optimization, and BMSC-sEVs show inferior periodontal tissue targeting compared to odontogenic-derived EVs. In contrast, He et al. demonstrated that dental pulp stem cell-derived EVs (DPSC-EVs) induce epithelial-mesenchymal transition in Hertwig’s epithelial root sheath cells via the TGF-β1/ERK signaling pathway, promoting cementogenic differentiation—a strategy specific to cementum regeneration, albeit with limited efficacy in deep bone defect repair and a risk of ectopic tissue formation.98 Studies by Zhao and Lei et al. showed that periodontal ligament stem cell-derived exosomes (PDLSC-Exos) restore the osteogenic capacity of inflamed PDLSCs in the inflammatory microenvironment by inhibiting the canonical Wnt signaling pathway (downregulating Wnt1, Wnt3a, β-catenin), while also promoting the proliferation and osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs.117,118 This strategy provides targeted regulation of the inflammatory microenvironment, but exosomes alone are insufficient for critical-sized bone defect repair.

All the above strategies share a core dilemma: how to balance “structural stability” with “bioactivity preservation”. Highly crosslinked hydrogels can prolong EV release, but excessive crosslinking may restrict EV diffusion and compromise membrane integrity due to a crowded microenvironment, leading to reduced bioactivity. Conversely, physically crosslinked hydrogels with low crosslinking density facilitate rapid EV release but fail to achieve long-term sustained release. To address this dilemma, various interfacial anchoring strategies have been developed. Deng et al. utilized the biotin-avidin system to anchor exosomes within a GelMA hydrogel, achieving up to 28 days of in vivo retention and over 7 days of sustained release via strong non-covalent interactions, thereby avoiding the bioactivity loss associated with chemical modification.119 However, this system introduces a non-native interaction (avidin), which may pose immunogenicity risks, and its preparation is relatively complex. In another approach, Ji et al. combined a thermosensitive hydroxypropyl chitin hydrogel loaded with MSCs with a 3D-printed PCL/nano-hydroxyapatite scaffold, achieving synergistic mechano-support and osteogenic/angiogenic effects by regulating macrophage M1-to-M2 polarization and secretion of VEGF, BMP-2, and other growth factors.120 Although this strategy provides favorable mechanical properties (compressive modulus >15 kPa), the hydrophobic surface of PCL lacks cell recognition sites, potentially limiting cell adhesion and long-term survival; further surface functionalization (e.g., with RGD peptides) is required to optimize interfacial interactions.

In summary, current hydrogel-based strategies for delivering stem cells or EVs have achieved significant progress in periodontal regeneration, yet several key trade-offs remain. First, source selection versus functional specificity: BMSC-sEVs possess broad-spectrum immunomodulatory and osteogenic activities but lack periodontal tissue targeting; odontogenic EVs (DPSC-EVs, PDLSC-Exos) exhibit better tissue specificity (e.g., cementum or periodontal ligament targeting) but are less effective in repairing critical-sized bone defects and often require combination with other osteogenic factors. Second, release kinetics versus bioactivity: high crosslinking density or strong anchoring (e.g., biotin-avidin) enables long-term sustained release but may impair EV membrane integrity or increase immunogenicity, whereas mild physical encapsulation preserves bioactivity but fails to meet the long therapeutic window required for chronic periodontitis. Third, mechanical support versus cytocompatibility: 3D-printed synthetic polymer scaffolds (e.g., PCL) provide excellent mechanical properties, but their hydrophobic surface and lack of cell recognition sites limit cell adhesion and tissue integration; natural hydrogels, despite superior biocompatibility, lack sufficient mechanical strength to maintain the spatial structure of complex periodontal defects.

Anti-inflammatory hydrogels

Anti-inflammatory drug-releasing hydrogels

The persistent progression of periodontitis is closely associated with dysregulation of the local inflammatory microenvironment. Conventional anti-inflammatory therapies (e.g., systemic administration or topical mouthwashes) are often limited by insufficient drug concentration, short duration of action, and non-specific immunosuppression.18 As an injectable and biodegradable local drug delivery platform, hydrogels can directly concentrate anti-inflammatory agents within the periodontal pocket, achieving spatiotemporally controlled release of anti-inflammatory drugs.80,121 However, significant differences exist among different anti-inflammatory strategies in terms of their targets, release mechanisms, cell-targeting capabilities, and scaffold-immune cell interfacial interactions. These differences directly determine their anti-inflammatory efficiency, safety, and clinical translational potential.

Based on their distinct anti-inflammatory mechanisms, current hydrogel strategies can be classified into three categories: broad-spectrum pharmacological inhibition, active immune reprogramming, and passive cytokine delivery.

The broad-spectrum inhibition strategy is exemplified by DZIF@PGel.122 Dexamethasone binds to the cytoplasmic glucocorticoid receptor, translocates into the nucleus, directly suppresses the transcriptional activity of NF-κB and AP-1 (downregulating TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and COX-2), and simultaneously induces IκBα expression to “lock” NF-κB in the cytoplasm, as mechanistically depicted in Figure 4 (NF-κB pathway), thereby achieving potent and broad-spectrum anti-inflammatory effects. In a rat periodontitis model, this hydrogel reduced the CEJ-ABC distance from approximately 1200 μm to about 770 μm and significantly decreased the number of TRAP-positive osteoclasts. This broad-spectrum strategy acts rapidly and potently, making it suitable for acute inflammatory storms, but its indiscriminate suppression comes at a cost: long-term use may inhibit osteoblast differentiation, delay bone healing, and increase the risk of secondary infection.123 Moreover, dexamethasone does not directly induce M2 polarization; instead, it creates a “low-stress” environment by lowering the overall inflammatory level, representing an indirect immunomodulation. Regarding release kinetics, DZIF@PGel exploits the acid responsiveness of ZIF-8 (80% release at pH 5.8 vs. 60% at pH 7.4) to achieve on-demand delivery of dexamethasone, while the GelMA/PPEMA double network provides a compressive modulus of approximately 40 kPa, allowing better maintenance of the defect space. However, a stiffer matrix may tend to preserve the M1 phenotype—a potential reason why macrophage polarization was not examined in that study.

Unlike the broad-spectrum inhibition of dexamethasone, active immune reprogramming strategies employ biologically derived factors to precisely modulate immune cell phenotypes. M2 macrophage-derived exosome hydrogels (Agevgel and Mel@M2-exos) naturally carry anti-inflammatory factors such as IL-10 and TGF-β, and are enriched in M2-associated proteins including Arg1 and CD163.124,125 After internalization by macrophages at the inflammatory site, these exosomes directly activate STAT6 and other signaling pathways, as shown in Figure 4, driving the M1-to-M2 conversion. In a diabetic mouse model, Agevgel significantly reduced M1 cells, increased M2 cells, and decreased the defect area by approximately 91%.124 Melatonin in Mel@M2-exos further targets the endoplasmic reticulum stress pathway (inhibiting PERK, ATF6, and IRE1, and downregulating GRP78, CHOP, and Caspase-3), thereby alleviating LPS-induced apoptosis of periodontal ligament cells and restoring their osteogenic/cementogenic differentiation capacity.125 The precision and durability of this strategy stem from the specific binding of exosomal surface adhesion molecules (LFA-1/VLA-4) to ICAM-1/VCAM-1 on inflammatory macrophages, achieving active targeted delivery, avoiding off-target effects of broad-spectrum immunosuppression, and markedly improving local bioavailability. However, this precision comes with high production costs, low yield, and batch-to-batch heterogeneity.

If active reprogramming can be viewed as “precision guidance,” then the SAP/IL-10 hydrogel represents the simplest passive release strategy.41 Its self-assembling peptide KLD1R forms nanofibers (diameter 10–20 nm) that provide a soft matrix (≈1 kPa), releasing approximately 70% of IL-10 within 5 days via simple diffusion. This system favors macrophage polarization toward M2 through the IL-10-mediated anti-inflammatory axis (Figure 4) and exhibits high biosafety, but its mechanical strength is extremely low, making it unable to withstand oral masticatory forces, and it lacks responsiveness to the inflammatory microenvironment.

A comprehensive comparison of the above three strategies reveals that the broad-spectrum inhibition strategy acts rapidly but is associated with considerable adverse effects; the active immune reprogramming strategy offers precision and long-lasting efficacy at a high cost; and the passive release strategy is safe and simple but lacks adequate mechanical strength and responsiveness. A future direction could be a “phased combination” approach: in the acute phase, an acid-responsive dexamethasone hydrogel is used to rapidly control inflammation, followed by switching to an M2-exo hydrogel to promote tissue regeneration after remission. Alternatively, exosomes can be engineered via genetic modification to lower production costs and enhance targeting. Only through systematic comparisons of different strategies in large-animal, long-term, and multi-center studies can an anti-inflammatory hydrogel platform truly suitable for clinical translation be identified.

Naturally derived compound-modified hydrogels

Different from the synthetic systems discussed in Section anti-inflammatory drug-releasing hydrogels, natural compounds do not merely inhibit inflammatory signaling or supplement anti-inflammatory mediators. Instead, they directly scavenge reactive oxygen species and activate endogenous antioxidant systems such as the Nrf2 pathway, while exerting multi-target synergistic effects including anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, and osteogenic activities. They place greater emphasis on restoring the intrinsic redox balance of tissues and exerting cytoprotective functions.126,127,128 Although natural compounds also suffer from drawbacks such as poor water solubility, low stability, and short half-life, they exhibit lower production costs and more controllable batch-to-batch consistency compared with exosomes.126 With the assistance of hydrogel carriers, these bioactive ingredients can be protected from rapid degradation in the oral environment and achieve sustained release.

By contrast, more in-depth research has been conducted on curcumin. Curcumin exerts anti-inflammatory effects by inhibiting nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) and cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2), converging on the NF-κB inflammatory axis depicted in Figure 4, reduces matrix degradation by suppressing matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9) and directly inhibits the growth of periodontal pathogens such as Porphyromonas gingivalis.127 On this basis, Liu et al. co-loaded curcumin and zinc oxide nanoparticles into a methylcellulose-based thermosensitive hydrogel (referred to as Cur/ZNP hydrogel), leveraging the antibacterial activity of Zn2+ and the anti-inflammatory/antioxidant properties of curcumin to achieve a synergistic effect.129 This hydrogel released approximately 70% of its curcumin content within 7 days and, in a rat model of ligature-induced periodontitis, significantly reduced the cementoenamel junction-alveolar bone crest (CEJ-ABC) distance from about 1.2 mm to 0.8 mm, showing superior efficacy over the single-agent formulations. However, the photostability of curcumin and the potential cytotoxicity of Zn2+ remain concerns, and the Cur/ZNP hydrogel lacks active targeting, with insufficient evidence for direct regulation of macrophage polarization.

Quercetin is another widely studied flavonoid. Wei et al. demonstrated that quercetin activates the nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (NRF2)/antioxidant response element (ARE) pathway, enhances the activity of antioxidant enzymes such as superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT), alleviates H2O2-induced senescence and apoptosis in periodontal ligament cells, and preserves their osteogenic differentiation capacity.128 Based on these findings, researchers have developed two quercetin delivery systems that exhibit distinct targeting strategies.

Zhu et al. loaded quercetin into mesoporous bioactive glass microspheres and then embedded them into a photo-crosslinkable composite hydrogel composed of gelatin methacryloyl, hyaluronic acid methacryloyl, and carboxymethyl chitosan methacryloyl (i-BH-Q). The mesoporous structure of the bioglass (pore diameter ≈50 nm) enabled sustained release of quercetin (≈42% release within 18 h).130 This system was the first to reveal that quercetin upregulates m6A methyltransferases such as methyltransferase-like 3 (METTL3), enhances the m6A modification of the Per1 gene, thereby alleviating oxidative stress-induced damage to orofacial mesenchymal stem cells (OMSCs) and promoting their osteogenic differentiation. Mechanistically, quercetin activates the Keap1-Nrf2-ARE antioxidant cascade (Figure 4), upregulating redox-protective enzymes to restore cellular homeostasis. In a rat model of periodontal bone defect, i-BH-Q significantly repaired the bone defect and restored the expression of redox-related proteins such as glucose transporter 1 (Glut1) and catalase (CAT). Although the bioglass microspheres themselves possess osteoinductive activity, their non-degradable nature may lead to long-term retention.

In contrast, Li et al. designed folic acid-modified quercetin microemulsions (FA-Qu-MEs) and dispersed them in a thermosensitive P407/PNIPAM hydrogel (designated FA-Qu-MEs@Gel) to achieve active targeting of macrophages.131 Quercetin scavenges intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS), modulating the ROS-macrophage polarization cascade depicted in Figure 4 (Nrf2 pathway; M1-to-M2 conversion), drives macrophage polarization from the pro-inflammatory M1 to the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, downregulates interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and interleukin-17 (IL-17), and upregulates interleukin-10 (IL-10), thereby restoring Th17/Treg immune homeostasis. Micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) showed that FA-Qu-MEs@Gel reduced the CEJ-ABC distance from approximately 5 mm–3.2 mm, and the bone volume fraction (BV/TV) reached 66.2%. While the active targeting strategy offers significant advantages, the long-term irritancy of the surfactant (HS15) used in the microemulsion and the relatively fast degradation rate of the hydrogel are limitations.

Among the natural compound-loaded hydrogels discussed above, the polyphenol mixture retains the synergistic effects of natural products but suffers from complex composition and unclear mechanisms132; the Cur/ZNP hydrogel exhibits clear antibacterial-anti-inflammatory synergy, yet its photostability and metal-related toxicity need to be addressed129; the i-BH-Q system reveals for the first time an m6A epigenetic regulatory mechanism and possesses strong osteoinductivity, but the carrier is non-degradable130; and the FA-Qu-MEs@Gel achieves active immunomodulation at the expense of high formulation complexity.131 Natural compound-based hydrogels offer the advantages of multi-target synergy and high biosafety; however, they still face major challenges regarding batch-to-batch consistency of therapeutic efficacy, long-term stability, and the path to clinical translation. Future directions may include the co-delivery of different natural compounds or the use of prodrug strategies to improve the stability and targeting of natural compounds.

Multilayer or topological hydrogels for anti-inflammation and tissue repair in periodontitis

In the treatment of periodontitis, although hydrogels loaded with antibacterial drugs or natural compounds can suppress inflammation, the persistent biofilm in the periodontal pocket, the dynamic mechanical stress from mastication, and the irregular morphology of the defect often render single-function drug delivery strategies insufficient to achieve ideal anti-inflammatory effects and tissue regeneration. Drawing inspiration from multilayer or topological hydrogels developed for burn and diabetic wounds, researchers have recently integrated physical barriers, temperature regulation, antibacterial activity, anti-inflammatory effects, and pro-repair functions into one system, creating multifunctional hydrogels that actively adapt to complex microenvironments, thereby offering new strategies for periodontitis treatment.

Taking burn wound first-aid as an example, Wan et al. developed a multilayer topological hydrogel patch (FBP).133 This patch consists of a soft antibacterial polyacrylamide-chitosan (PAAm-CS) layer and a stretchable cooling poly(vinyl alcohol)-chitosan (PVA-CS) layer with a mesh topological structure, encapsulated with polyethylene films to prevent dehydration. This multilayer design simultaneously endows the FBP with softness (Young’s modulus 83 kPa), high stretchability (fracture strain >106%), high thermal conductivity (0.54 W m−1 K−1), instant cooling, effective antibacterial activity (96.2% against S. aureus, 92.2% against E. coli), and excellent biocompatibility (94.04% cell viability at 20 mg mL−1 extract for 24 h). In a second-degree scald rat model, the FBP reduced tissue damage and edema formation while increasing the collagen/blood vessel ratio, significantly lowering inflammation levels. Although rapid cooling is not required for periodontitis, the topological structure that provides a physical antibacterial barrier and the anti-inflammatory mechanism of the FBP offer valuable insights. For example, a hydrogel with a micron-scale topological surface could be designed within the periodontal pocket to physically interfere with biofilm attachment, while the natural antibacterial activity of chitosan synergistically controls local inflammation.

For diabetic chronic wounds, which share pathological features with chronic periodontitis such as excessive inflammation, oxidative stress, and impaired healing, Zheng et al. developed multilayer multifunctional hydrogel microspheres (COQTCu).134 These microspheres are composed of methacrylated carboxymethyl chitosan (CSMA), oxidized carboxymethyl starch (OCMS), quaternized chitin (QC), tannic acid (TA), and copper ions (Cu2+), assembled layer-by-layer using multiple crosslinking strategies including photopolymerization, Schiff base reaction, electrostatic interactions, hydrogen bonding, and chelation. As the number of layers increases, the diameter and porosity decrease sequentially, achieving antibacterial, antioxidant, and anti-inflammatory functions dominated by the outer QC-TA-Cu layers, while the inner CSMA-OCMS matrix provides a scaffold for cell support. In a diabetic mouse model, COQTCu accelerated wound healing, promoted angiogenesis, reduced inflammatory infiltration, and improved collagen accumulation and maturation. The relevance of this layer-by-layer design to periodontitis lies in the fact that the periodontal pocket similarly requires an outer layer to rapidly scavenge reactive oxygen species and neutralize pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-1β, TNF-α), while the inner layer persistently supports the migration and differentiation of periodontal ligament cells and osteoblasts. Therefore, hydrogel microspheres or injectable hydrogels with a gradient “outer anti-inflammatory, inner pro-regenerative” function could be designed, achieving the release of drugs or active factors in a sequence that matches the timeline of periodontal tissue regeneration.

Extending the design strategy of multilayer or topological hydrogels to periodontitis holds promise for developing anti-inflammatory hydrogels that can actively adapt to the complex microenvironment of the periodontal pocket, including moisture, dynamic mechanical loading, and irregular anatomical morphology. By establishing differentiated crosslinking densities or functional layers in different regions of the hydrogel in contact with periodontal tissues, the upper layer can rapidly release antioxidants and antimicrobial peptides to control plaque and inflammation, while the lower layer slowly releases growth factors and maintains mechanical support to promote periodontal attachment regeneration. This structure-function integration strategy, which goes beyond simple drug release, provides a new and feasible approach for local anti-inflammation and tissue regeneration in periodontitis.

Spatiotemporally controlled drug release via stimuli-responsive hydrogels

Previous sections have classified hydrogel-based periodontal therapies according to biological effects: antibacterial (Section antibacterial hydrogel), regenerative (Section hydrogels for tissue regeneration), and anti-inflammatory (Section anti-inflammatory hydrogels). However, these functions are not mutually exclusive; instead, the pathological microenvironment of periodontitis demands spatiotemporally coordinated intervention. As typical responsive materials, intelligent stimuli-responsive hydrogels undergo remarkable changes in swelling behavior and mechanical properties, and undergo sol-gel transitions upon external stimulation, enabling excellent adaptation to complex external environments.80 Such hydrogels do not represent an independent therapeutic category but act as intelligent precision delivery platforms for the on-demand, site-specific, and controlled release of antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, and regenerative cargos described in Sections antibacterial hydrogel, hydrogels for tissue regeneration, and anti-inflammatory hydrogels. By sensing pathological signals in the periodontal pocket (e.g., decreased pH, oxidative stress, elevated enzyme activity, temperature changes, or external energy input), these systems trigger payload release at the right time and location to maximize therapeutic efficacy (Figure 5).

Triple-responsive therapeutic mechanism of the smart hydrogel for periodontitis

The hydrogel system integrates three distinct microenvironmental sensing modalities: (i) pH-responsive Schiff base bonds (C=N) that undergo hydrolytic cleavage under the acidic conditions of gingival crevicular fluid (pH ≈ 5.0–6.5), destabilizing the network to trigger drug release; (ii) temperature-responsive sol-gel transitions at 37 °C that enable injectable liquid-to-gel conversion upon placement within the periodontal pocket; and (iii) piezoelectric BTO nanoparticles that generate surface polarization potentials (∼52 mV) under cyclic masticatory forces, augmenting antibacterial activity via bacterial membrane rupture and ROS generation. Collectively, these stimuli orchestrate the spatiotemporally controlled liberation of antibacterial agents, anti-inflammatory mediators, and repair-related bioactive factors. pH-responsive hydrogels

The intelligent drug release behavior of pH-responsive hydrogels originates from the protonation-deprotonation equilibrium of acid-labile chemical bonds within their networks. Schiff base bonds (C=N, pKa ≈6.0–7.0) undergo hydrolytic cleavage in the acidic periodontal microenvironment, accelerating drug release via first-order kinetics; the hydrolysis rate constant can increase by 10- to 100-fold when pH drops from 7.4 to 5.0.38 Meanwhile, the polymer charge density (ζ-potential) of hydrogels changes significantly: chitosan-based hydrogels exhibit a ζ-potential of approximately +5 mV (near neutral) at pH 7.4, whereas amine protonation shifts the ζ-potential sharply to about +25 mV at pH 5.0. This not only enhances electrostatic repulsion-driven drug expulsion but also strengthens mucoadhesion to negatively charged gingival epithelia via cation-anion interactions.135

These mechanisms have been validated in diverse therapeutic scenarios. For antibacterial applications, the oxidized succinoglycan/chitosan (OSG/CS) hydrogel developed by Kim et al. employs Schiff base-crosslinked networks to achieve accelerated release of 5-fluorouracil (90% within 72 h) at pH 2.0, compared with only 60% at pH 7.4, thereby concentrating cytotoxicity precisely in the pathological microenvironment while protecting adjacent healthy mucosa.135 The carboxymethyl chitosan/oxidized dextran (CMCS/ODex) hydrogel constructed by Cai et al., also crosslinked via Schiff bases, maintains structural integrity in acidic inflammatory environments while enabling sustained release of embelin, effectively inhibiting Porphyromonas gingivalis infection and regulating macrophage M1/M2 polarization.38

For anti-inflammation and bone regeneration, the DZIF@PGel system developed by Li et al. integrates zeolitic imidazolate framework-8 (ZIF-8) into a photocrosslinked hydrogel. Using the acid-responsive property of ZIF-8 (80% dexamethasone release at pH 5.8 vs. 60% at pH 7.4), this platform achieves pH-triggered release of dexamethasone and Zn2+, exerting synergistic antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, and osteogenic functions within a single responsive matrix.122 Similarly, Yang et al. fabricated a metal-organic framework (MOF)-modified thermosensitive adhesive hydrogel (SFD/CS/ZIF-8@QCT), in which ZIF-8@QCT releases Zn2+ and quercetin under acidic conditions to simultaneously achieve antibacterial effects, immune reprogramming, and promotion of osteogenesis and angiogenesis.136

Although pH-triggered delivery is simple and efficient, several limitations restrict clinical translation. First, the actual dynamic pH range in the periodontal pocket (5.0–7.0) is much narrower than the extreme conditions (e.g., pH 2.0 vs. 7.4) used in most in vitro studies, which may compromise the release difference in vivo.135 Second, the strong buffering capacity of gingival crevicular fluid and saliva can rapidly neutralize local acidification, reducing the fidelity of pH triggering. Third, the hydrolysis rate of acid-labile bonds such as Schiff bases in the physiological environment is affected by both pH and temperature, which further influences the precision of controlled drug release.137

Future designs should incorporate dual or multi-stimuli responsiveness (e.g., pH + ROS, pH + enzyme) to enhance specificity and explore combinations of dynamic covalent bonds and noncovalent interactions to overcome the constraints of single-signal triggering.

Temperature-responsive hydrogels

Temperature-responsive hydrogels exploit reversible phase transitions near body temperature to form in situ drug depots within periodontal pockets, enabling sustained therapeutic release. Based on their response mechanisms, representative systems can be categorized into several types.

Sheshala et al. employed a poloxamer 407/188 system, which remains a sol at room temperature and undergoes a sol-gel transition upon entering the oral cavity as the temperature rises to 37°C due to micellar dehydration and stacking, releasing the encapsulated moxifloxacin for only 8 h—suitable for acute infections but lacking anti-inflammatory and regenerative functions.138 Xu et al. utilized a chitosan/β-glycerophosphate system: at low temperature, chitosan chains remain extended due to electrostatic repulsion; upon warming to 37°C, electrostatic interactions weaken while hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions strengthen, leading to gel formation.139 This system co-delivers aspirin (86.6% release in 3 days) and erythropoietin (83.4% over 21 days) in a staged manner, achieving anti-inflammation followed by pro-regeneration, yet it lacks intrinsic antibacterial activity.139 Ma et al. also used a chitosan/β-glycerophosphate thermosensitive matrix to co-deliver the antimicrobial peptide Nal-P-113 and polydopamine nanoparticles, achieving simultaneous release over 13 days with dual antibacterial and osteogenic effects, but its mechanical strength is insufficient, and it is prone to fracture under masticatory loads.140 Wang et al. combined a chitosan/β-glycerophosphate/gelatin system with the NIR-II photothermal agent T8IC: Under 808 nm irradiation, the local temperature rises to 45°C, accelerating sol-gel transition (within 2 min) while generating reactive oxygen species to enhance antibacterial activity, and sustaining BMP-2 release to promote osteogenesis.141 This system integrates antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, and osteogenic functions, but depends on an external light source with limited penetration depth.141

The common shortcomings of the above systems are passive release or dependence on external triggers, lack of active mechanical intervention, and insufficient validation in the complex periodontal pocket environment. To address these deficiencies, future research may introduce two new strategies with distinct smart response mechanisms, providing direct inspiration for improving temperature-responsive hydrogels for periodontitis.

The first is a biomimetic self-contracting hydrogel (PNS-HB).142 Its inner thermoresponsive PNIPAAm-alginate layer contracts by 56% at body temperature, generating active contractile force to pull wound edges together; the outer polydopamine-poly(acrylic acid) layer provides strong adhesion (interfacial toughness 162 J/m2). Extending this concept to periodontitis, one could design a hydrogel that undergoes centripetal contraction upon body temperature exposure within the periodontal pocket, actively approximating the pocket wall to the root surface and promoting epithelial attachment. More importantly, the contraction kinetics can be coupled with drug release: rapid release of antibacterial agents during early contraction, sustained release of anti-inflammatory factors during the maintenance phase, and slow release of growth factors at the late stage, thereby overcoming the functional fragmentation of the previous systems. Of course, the adaptability of such a hydrogel to the narrow, dynamic environment of the periodontal pocket requires validation, and its non-degradability needs improvement.

The second is a smart protease-responsive protein hydrogel (CD/BSA/GEL) constructed by thermal crosslinking of bovine serum albumin and gelatin with encapsulated ClO2.143 In the inflamed microenvironment, proteases degrade the hydrogel, releasing the encapsulated ClO2 for ∼4 days to eradicate intracellular P. gingivalis and disrupt biofilms, while released proteins promote osteogenesis. In a rat periodontitis model, this hydrogel downregulated IL-10 and TNF-α and alleviated alveolar bone loss, with its protein matrix neutralizing ClO2 toxicity. This protease-triggered paradigm enables on-demand antimicrobial and regenerative therapy for periodontitis, providing a new strategy for smart responsive hydrogel-based periodontitis therapy.

Piezoelectric hydrogel hydrogels

Piezoelectric hydrogels convert mechanical stress (e.g., mastication, ultrasound) into local electrical signals without external power or electrodes, offering a wireless, non-invasive strategy for periodontitis. Typically, tetragonal BaTiO3 nanoparticles embedded in a gelatin methacryloyl matrix generate a surface piezopotential of 30–52 mV under cyclic compression (0.9 MPa, 2 Hz) or ultrasound (1 MHz).144,145

This weak electrical cue triggers coordinated biological responses across cell types and pathogens. In inflamed periodontal ligament stem cells, it elevates mitochondrial membrane potential and ATP synthesis, restoring oxidative phosphorylation and upregulating osteogenic genes (Runx2, ALP, OCN) via Ca2+-dependent MLC phosphorylation. In macrophages, it shifts the M1/M2 balance toward the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, reducing IL-1β and TNF-α while increasing IL-10 and Arg-1. Against P. gingivalis, it induces oxidative stress (upregulation of oxyR) and downregulates adhesion (fimA) and biofilm-associated (porP) genes, reducing biofilm biomass by 41% and viable bacteria by 2–3 log.146,147

Despite these promising findings, critical limitations temper clinical translation. The generated voltage (<100 mV) is orders of magnitude lower than that used in conventional electrical stimulation (>1 V), and evidence that such low output consistently activates deep periodontal cells under real masticatory forces remains lacking. Long-term safety of piezoelectric fillers (e.g., BaTiO3) has not been systematically assessed, and no head-to-head comparison with standard nonsurgical therapy (scaling/root planing plus antibiotics) has been performed. Thus, while mechano-responsive piezoelectric hydrogels represent an innovative drug-free concept, their therapeutic superiority and feasibility require more rigorous validation under clinically relevant conditions.

Current clinical status and challenges

Despite remarkable progress in developing multifunctional hydrogels for periodontitis therapy in preclinical settings, the clinical translation of these formulations remains highly limited. Most hydrogel-based strategies are still in the proof-of-concept stage, with scarce clinical data, incomplete safety validation, and ambiguous regulatory pathways. This section critically evaluates preclinical evidence, compares hydrogels with clinically established local delivery systems, addresses biosafety and degradability concerns, discusses PK/PD gaps, and clarifies regulatory hurdles that hinder clinical implementation.

Preclinical in vivo evidence in animal models

In recent years, multifunctional hydrogel systems have demonstrated promising potential in the treatment of periodontitis in animal models, with efficacy validated in antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, and tissue repair aspects. For instance, Ruan et al. developed a minocycline hydrochloride-loaded hydrogel that significantly reduced periodontal pocket depth (PD) in rats after 14 days of administration, with the gingival index (GI) dropping to 0 (normal) by days 28 and 56, demonstrating high antibacterial efficacy.148 Lengert et al. designed a tannic acid-loaded silver alginate microcapsule hydrogel, which effectively improved microcirculatory disorders, reduced inflammatory responses in experimental periodontitis, and minimized adverse effects on healthy tissue.149 Yu et al. developed a self-healing hydrogel (DFH) incorporating TGF-β3 microspheres and basic Fibroblast Growth Factor (bFGF), which formed a porous structure in vivo and enabled sequential release of bFGF, significantly enhancing bone volume fraction, trabecular number, and trabecular thickness in rat alveolar bone.146

While these preclinical studies establish proof-of-concept, several limitations constrain translational validity. First, rodent periodontitis models—whether ligature-induced or bacterial inoculation—exhibit fundamentally different oral microbiomes, immune responses, and bone remodeling kinetics compared to human disease. The rat maxilla lacks the complex root anatomy and occlusal loading patterns of human dentition, potentially overestimating regenerative outcomes. Second, study durations rarely exceed 8 weeks, failing to capture long-term complications such as scaffold fragmentation, chronic inflammation, or aberrant mineralization. Third, outcome measures (e.g., bone volume fraction, histological scores) lack standardization across laboratories, precluding meta-analytic synthesis. Large-animal models (e.g., canine, porcine, or non-human primate) with periodontal architectures more closely approximating human anatomy are urgently needed to bridge this translational gap.6,147

Clinical evidence, regulatory barriers, and the path to approval

A fundamental question for clinical translation is whether periodontal hydrogels should be regulated as medical devices (structural/barrier function), drugs (pharmacological action), or drug-device combination products. The FDA classifies PerioChip® and Atridox® as drug-device combinations due to their primary drug-releasing function. Hydrogels with intrinsic antibacterial activity (Section intrinsically antibacterial hydrogels) may qualify as devices if the polymer itself is the therapeutic agent, whereas payload-dependent systems (Sections antibiotic-loaded hydrogel, metal ions-loaded hydrogel, and nanoparticle-modified hydrogel) will likely follow combination product pathways. This classification determines the required evidence package: device-focused submissions emphasize mechanical testing and biocompatibility (ISO 10993), while drug-focused submissions require comprehensive toxicology, PK/PD, and clinical efficacy trials. Multifunctional hydrogels integrate structural support, drug delivery, and bioactive signaling, leading to unclear regulatory criteria. This situation may require new evaluation systems or original classification standards, and their time to market could be extended by 2–5 years relative to single-function devices.

In response to the issues raised in Section preclinical in vivo evidence in animal models, researchers have conducted clinical trials on several hydrogel composite systems, the results of which have shown promise while also revealing the formidable limitations of hydrogel clinical translation. The chitosan LGX trial observed no adverse effects over 6 months in residual periodontal pocket treatment.150 The rhFGF-2/HA trial’s split-mouth design precluded systemic PK evaluation, and neither study reported whether therapeutic concentrations were maintained beyond the initial release phase.151 The ABM/P-15 hydrogel exhibited excellent soft tissue responses.152 However, these trials enrolled only 19–38 patients with 6–12 months of follow-up, insufficient for establishing long-term safety or efficacy profiles. More critically, none demonstrated superiority over existing therapies.

The reasons why hydrogels remain confined to laboratory science become evident upon examining the multifaceted constraints of clinical, commercial, and regulatory barriers. Clinically, the multifunctional design that distinguishes hydrogels from single-function delivery vehicles (Section integrative design trade-offs and translational boundaries) introduces exponentially greater validation complexity: each therapeutic modality requires independent characterization, thereby expanding trial scope and cost. Commercially, the periodontal local drug delivery system market is dominated by established products with clearly defined efficacy endpoints; the development cost for novel combination hydrogel products far exceeds their revenue potential, particularly when reimbursement pathways remain undefined. Regulatory ambiguity further amplifies these challenges.

Biosafety, degradability, and PK/PD considerations

Based on the experimental data of various hydrogel composite systems developed by researchers reviewed in Section application strategies of hydrogel in the treatment of periodontitis, the drug release profiles of these studies can be classified into the following categories: burst-dominant systems (e.g., ornidazole releasing 60% within 30 min85; 5-FU releasing ∼90% within 72 h135) prioritize rapid pathogen suppression, yet carry the risk of sub-MIC tails and resistance selection85,135; sustained-release platforms (e.g., BMP-2/SDF-1 sustained for 35 days39; Nal-P-113/PDNPs sustained for 13 days140) support prolonged pharmacodynamic exposure to promote regeneration and immunomodulation, but often lack empirical confirmation that local concentrations remain above therapeutic thresholds throughout the entire release period.39,140 Stimuli-responsive systems (e.g., pH-triggered ZIF-8122; NIR-II photothermal T8IC141) introduce conditional pharmacokinetic modulation. However, their triggering fidelity is constrained by the narrow dynamic range of periodontal pH (5.0–7.0) and the limited penetration depth of external energy sources.141 Despite the diversity of the aforementioned release profiles, it must be emphasized that these formulation kinetic data do not constitute pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic evidence. In other words, whether these in vitro release curves can be translated into local concentration-time relationships within the in vivo therapeutic window is entirely dependent on the profound constraints imposed by the periodontal microenvironment on drug disposition. Local drug delivery to the periodontal pocket presents unique PK/PD challenges distinct from systemic administration. The gingival crevicular fluid (GCF) volume is minute (∼0.5–2.0 μL per healthy site, increasing to 5–50 μL in inflammation), creating a high surface-area-to-volume ratio that accelerates drug clearance.82 Hydrogel depots must achieve Cmax (maximum concentration) exceeding the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of target pathogens (e.g., 0.06–0.5 μg/mL for P. gingivalis with doxycycline) while maintaining AUC0–t (area under the curve) sufficient for sustained efficacy. Critically, current hydrogel studies rarely report PK parameters; they rely on in vitro release profiles that poorly predict in vivo performance due to GCF flow, proteolytic degradation, and biofilm-mediated drug sequestration. Standardized PK/PD studies in large-animal models—measuring GCF drug concentrations, systemic absorption rates, and microbiological endpoints—are essential for regulatory submissions and clinical protocol optimization.

Regarding biosafety, as noted above, existing animal studies are typically limited to ≤8 weeks, and clinical trials span only 6–12 months, providing only short-term biosafety evidence. Whether residual cross-linking agents in synthetic hydrogels (e.g., acrylamide derivatives, photoinitiators) induce chronic inflammation over the long term remains uncertain. The accumulated doses of metal ions and nanoparticles in periodontal soft and hard tissues are likewise undetermined. Batch-to-batch variability in green-synthesized nanoparticles complicates safety standardization for clinical-grade manufacturing. The absence of standardized biocompatibility protocols specific to intra-pocket biomaterials further impedes regulatory clearance.

In summary, the absence of human trials for periodontal hydrogels arises from a convergence of regulatory ambiguity as drug-device combinations, lack of large-animal model standardization, insufficient long-term biosafety data, and an unvalidated link between rodent bone volume fraction and human clinical attachment gain. These barriers, rather than intrinsic technological limitations, explain the translational stagnation.

Conclusion and outlook

Conclusions

Hydrogels represent a versatile therapeutic platform for periodontitis management, integrating antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, and regenerative functions within a single injectable or implantable system. This review has critically examined how material design strategies address the specific physiological constraints of the periodontal pocket, while highlighting persistent translational barriers that have prevented clinical realization despite decades of preclinical research. This multifunctionality enables coordinated inflammation modulation, pathogen eradication, and periodontal tissue regeneration. Furthermore, stimuli-responsive hydrogels enhance the precision of targeted therapy. Animal models confirmed the efficacy of functional hydrogels in periodontal applications, and clinically approved hydrogel materials have demonstrated favorable therapeutic outcomes. Despite existing limitations, multifunctional hydrogels offer promise for the management of periodontal diseases (Table 1).

Future directions

To address current barriers to clinical translation, researchers must overcome existing limitations, including long-term stability, controllable biodegradation, and adaptability to the complex oral environment, and integrate interdisciplinary innovations to advance periodontal therapy from basic research to clinical practice. Future efforts should focus on the development of novel biomaterials, optimization of existing products, and exploration of more effective combination therapies to improve clinical outcomes and expand the scope of clinical applications.

Development of novel materials

Leveraging the robust drug-loading capacity of hydrogels, future research should aim to develop systems capable of precise, controlled drug release with enhanced antibacterial and anti-inflammatory effects. By optimizing cross-linking methods and surface modifications, hydrogels can be engineered with excellent biocompatibility and tailored mechanical strength to meet diverse clinical needs. For instance, incorporating nanomaterials (e.g., nanocellulose, nanoclusters) could significantly improve their antibacterial efficacy while mitigating the risk of drug-resistant bacterial strains. Additionally, co-loading natural extracts with intrinsic antibacterial and anti-inflammatory properties alongside other functional agents offers a biosafe, multi-mechanistic approach to addressing the complex pathophysiology of periodontal disease. Furthermore, integration with common clinical dental tools (e.g., light-curing lamps) should be explored to create hydrogels that are both user-friendly and compatible with standard dental procedures, thereby improving their clinical applicability in periodontal therapy.

Interdisciplinary research and applications

Interdisciplinary collaboration is critical to advancing hydrogel technology in periodontal therapy. By integrating nanotechnology, novel nanocomposite hydrogels with unique properties, including enhanced drug-loading capacity and targeted delivery, can be developed. For instance, applying principles from biomedical engineering to design hydrogels that mimic the properties of the ECM could promote cell adhesion, proliferation, and differentiation, further promoting periodontal tissue regeneration. Additionally, integrating hydrogels with gene therapy enables the localized delivery of genes that modulate immune responses or enhance tissue repair. This multidisciplinary approach not only broadens the functional scope of hydrogels but also addresses the multifactorial nature of periodontal disease through innovative, synergistic strategies.

Exploring pathways from laboratory to clinical translation

Translating hydrogel research from the laboratory to clinical practice remains a critical challenge, as detailed in Section current clinical status and challenges. Addressing the identified barriers, including evidentiary gaps in large-animal models, limited clinical trial data, regulatory classification ambiguity, and unresolved long-term biosafety questions, will require concerted efforts across preclinical, clinical, and regulatory sciences. For large-scale production, optimizing the cost-effectiveness of hydrogel manufacturing and application will significantly impact widespread clinical adoption. Realizing these advances will enable hydrogels to evolve into clinically viable solutions, providing integrated “anti-inflammatory–regeneration–functional restoration” therapies for periodontitis and ultimately contributing to the reestablishment of periodontal tissue homeostasis.
