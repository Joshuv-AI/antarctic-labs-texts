WeakSupCon: Weakly Supervised Contrastive Learning for Encoder Pre-training
Bodong Zhang, Hamid Manoochehri, Beatrice S. Knudsen, Tolga Tasdizen, Department of Electrical, Computer Engineering, University of Utah, Salt Lake City, UT, USA

Abstract
Weakly supervised multiple instance learning (MIL) is a challenging task given that only bag-level labels are provided, while each bag typically contains multiple instances. This topic has been extensively studied in histopathological image analysis, where labels are usually available only at the whole slide image (WSI) level, while each whole slide image can be divided into thousands of small image patches for training. The dominant MIL approaches take fixed patch features as inputs to address computational constraints and ensure model stability. These features are commonly generated by encoders pre-trained on ImageNet, foundation encoders pre-trained on large datasets, or through self-supervised learning on local datasets. While the self-supervised encoder pre-training on the same dataset as downstream MIL tasks helps mitigate domain shift and generate better features, the bag-level labels are not utilized during the process, and the features of patches from different categories may cluster together, reducing classification performance on MIL tasks. Recently, pre-training with supervised contrastive learning (SupCon) has demonstrated superior performance compared to self-supervised contrastive learning and even end-to-end training on traditional image classification tasks. In this paper, we propose a novel encoder pre-training method for downstream MIL tasks called Weakly Supervised Contrastive Learning (WeakSupCon) that utilizes bag-level labels. In our method, we employ multi-task learning and define distinct contrastive learning losses for samples with different bag labels. Our experiments demonstrate that the features generated using WeakSupCon significantly enhance MIL classification performance compared to self-supervised approaches across three datasets. Key words: Contrastive Learning, Multiple Instance Learning, Weakly Supervised Learning, Histopathological Image.

Introduction

Deep learning has been widely utilized in medical image analysis, particularly for histopathological image classification tasks. Traditional deep learning-based image classification requires training on a large dataset in a fully supervised manner, where each image is assigned a label during training. However, this requirement poses significant challenges in the medical domain, as labeling a vast number of images demands substantial effort from experts and is often impractical. To address this issue, multiple instance learning (MIL)

,
11
,
18
]
methods have been proposed to enable weakly supervised training, thereby reducing the burden of image labeling.

In multiple instance learning (MIL), instead of assigning labels at the instance level, labels are provided at the bag level. Each bag contains multiple instances. A bag is labeled as positive if it contains at least one positive instance and is labeled as negative if all instances within it are negative. The MIL framework is particularly useful in histopathological image classification, where labels are assigned only at the whole slide image (WSI) level. Histopathological slides are gigapixel-sized high-resolution scans of tissue samples, which are crucial for diagnosing diseases such as cancer. Typically, each slide can be divided into thousands of patches, which serve as inputs for deep learning models. A slide is considered a positive case as long as a portion of its patches are positive.

Due to computational constraints and the need to ensure the stability of MIL models under weak supervision, image patches are typically encoded into fixed embedded features before applying MIL methods. In attention-based MIL (AB-MIL)
[
8
]
, an attention-based operator is introduced to assign different weights to patches within a bag. The final representation of a bag (slide) is obtained as a weighted sum of the feature embeddings from its instances (patches). The attention mechanism enables the model to identify key instances that contribute to the slide-level labels, thereby making a greater impact on the embedded features of slides during MIL training. Transformer-based MIL (TransMIL)
[
14
]
leverages the mechanism to model relationships between instances, allowing the model to capture both global and local context. Double-Tier Feature Distillation Multiple Instance Learning (DTFD-MIL)
[
18
]
further enhances AB-MIL by introducing the concept of pseudo-bags to artificially increase the number of training bags. Each bag is split into a certain number of pseudo-bags, and instances are randomly assigned to these pseudo-bags. It is assumed that most pseudo-bags from an originally positive bag still contain positive patches, so the pseudo-bags retain the original bag’s label. In the first tier (tier-1), all pseudo-bags act as independent bags for AB-MIL training. In the second tier (tier-2), pseudo-bag feature distillation is applied to generate distilled features for pseudo-bags. These distilled features are then treated as embedded instance features within the original bag, enabling tier-2 AB-MIL training. This hierarchical approach improves the model’s ability to learn discriminative features under weak supervision.

While the MIL structure directly influences MIL classification results, the quality of patch feature embeddings is also crucial for the success of MIL classification. These features encapsulate the characteristics of instances in a way that facilitates learning the relationships between instances and the overall bag label. High-quality embedded features ensure that the aggregation process captures relevant patterns, thereby contributing effectively to bag-level classification. Although ImageNet pre-trained encoders are commonly used to generate patch features, the fact that ImageNet consists of natural images introduces a domain shift when these encoders are applied to histopathological images. An alternative way is to generate features by foundation models pre-trained on large histopathological images.

2
Method

Inspired by Supervised Contrastive Learning (SupCon), rather than pursuing a complex end-to-end multiple instance learning (MIL) solution, we propose weakly supervised contrastive learning (WeakSupCon) to enhance encoder pre-training by transitioning from a self-supervised to a weakly supervised approach.

In most common MIL classification tasks, where the goal is binary classification between negative and positive bags, we can treat all image patches within negative bags as negative samples. However, determining the true labels of patches within positive bags in pre-training is challenging, as they contain both positive and negative samples. To address this, we propose a multi-task learning approach that assigns different tasks to patch instances based on their bag labels. Fig.
2
illustrates the main concept of WeakSupCon. For each training batch during pre-training, we split samples into separate groups based on their bag labels. In the negative group, we apply a supervised version of the contrastive learning task by minimizing the distances among all features from samples with negative bag labels. We define a SimilarityLoss by taking the positive pair component from the SupCon Loss (
2
), as described below:

\mathcal{L}^{Similarity}=\sum_{i\in Neg}\frac{-1}{|Neg|}\sum_{j\in Neg,j\neq i}{z_{i}\cdot z_{j}/\tau}

(3)

Where
Neg
represents the negative group. In the positive group, we apply self-supervised contrastive learning (SimCLR) by defining the loss as follows:

\mathcal{L}^{SimCLR}=\sum_{i\in Pos}\ell_{i,p(i)}

(4)

where
Pos
represents the positive group,
p(i)
denotes another feature generated from same original sample as
i
but with a different augmentation,
\ell_{i,p(i)}
is defined as in equation (
1
). In total, our WeakSupCon loss will be:

\mathcal{L}^{WeakSupCon}=\mathcal{L}^{Similarity}+\mathcal{L}^{SimCLR}

(5)

The two groups are trained separately but simultaneously. Fig.
2
further illustrates the goal of WeakSupCon. When illustrating the features from both groups in the same feature space, the negative samples within positive bags tend to move closer to the negative samples in negative bags, as SimilarityLoss encourages the clustering of negative samples. In contrast, the positive samples in positive bags tend to move further away from negative samples due to the effect of the SimCLR loss. This separation makes MIL models more likely to assign higher attention weights to positive samples.

Experiments

3.1
Datasets

We conducted experiments on three datasets, including two publicly available datasets that are widely used in multiple instance learning settings like DTFD-MIL and TransMIL: the Camelyon16 dataset
[
5
,
6
]
and The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) lung cancer dataset, as well as single-institution kidney cancer dataset. Fig.
3
presents example patches from those datasets.

In Camelyon16, the training set consists of 159 Whole Slide Images (WSIs) labeled as normal and 111 WSIs labeled as tumor. We randomly selected 10% of the slides for validation in multiple instance learning (MIL) tasks. The test set includes 80 normal slides and 50 tumor slides. To extract patches, we followed the patch cropping approach from code in
[
18
]
, generating 1,262,245 patches from normal slides and 1,030,464 patches from tumor slides in the training set, excluding those reserved for validation in MIL.

We also provide a new single-institution kidney cancer dataset. Case-level (patient-level) labels are provided instead of slide-level labels, with each case potentially containing multiple slides. The task is to predict the presence of Renal Vein Thrombus (RVT) based solely on kidney tumor slides. The training set consists of 74 negative cases and 31 positive cases, while the validation set includes 12 negative cases and 8 positive cases. The test set comprises 18 negative cases and 11 positive cases. In total, the training set contains 862 slides. We cropped 400×400 patches with a stride of 400, generating 1,518,872 tiles with negative bag labels and 695,439 tiles with positive bag labels for the training set. Our single-institution kidney cancer dataset is publicly available with a data-sharing agreement by contacting authors.

Unlike the Camelyon16 and kidney cancer dataset, which distinguish between negative and positive labels, the TCGA lung cancer dataset consists of two lung cancer subtypes: Lung Adenocarcinoma (LUAD) and Lung Squamous Cell Carcinoma (LUSC). We followed the dataset split used in
[
11
]
. The training set contains 425 LUAD slides and 402 LUSC slides, while the test set consists of 106 LUAD slides and 109 LUSC slides. Similar to Camelyon16, we randomly selected 10% of the training slides for validation in MIL tasks and excluded them from pre-training. We used our own code for patch extraction pipeline, cropping 512×512 patches at 20× magnification with a stride of 512. In total, we generated 1,053,524 patches from LUAD slides and 1,030,464 patches from LUSC slides for WeakSupCon pre-training in the training set.

Among the two publicly available datasets, Camelyon16 and TCGA lung, the proportion of cancerous regions in positive slides varies significantly. In Camelyon16, it is estimated that less than 10% of the foreground area in a positive slide contains cancerous regions, whereas in the TCGA lung cancer dataset, cancerous regions typically occupy more than 80% of each positive slide.

3.2
Experiment settings

During encoder pre-training on the training sets, we used ResNet18 trained with various strategies, including SimCLR
[
2
,
3
]
, MoCo v3, SupCon, and our WeakSupCon. To pre-train encoders using SupCon loss, all patch samples in positive bags are treated as positive label. A batch size of 512 and a decaying learning rate were consistently used across all experiments, with ResNet18
[
7
]
set as the backbone for all models due to the limit of our computing resources. It is worth noting that the SupCon and our WeakSupCon are based on SimCLR, with modifications on contrastive loss functions. We pre-trained 100 epochs for SimCLR, MoCo v3, SupCon, and WeakSupCon. All encoders’ input image size after augmentation is fixed to 224 by 224.

After encoder pre-training, we extracted feature representations for all patches in the training, validation, and test sets and evaluated different encoder models using DTFD-MIL classification. For Camelyon16, we set the number of pseudo-bags to 5. While for the kidney cancer dataset and TCGA lung cancer dataset, we set the number of pseudo-bags to 30, considering the high proportion of positive patches in each bag. Given that the TCGA lung dataset contains two positive labels, we treated LUAD as the negative label to be able to apply our WeakSupCon. For comparison, we also generated features output by foundation models pre-trained on extremely large datasets, including Barlow Twins
[
15
]
and PathDino
[
1
]
.

Our experiments were conducted using Python 3.11.4, PyTorch 2.0.1, torchvision 0.15.2, and CUDA 11.8 on NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPUs. Each epoch required approximately 35 GB of GPU memory and around 2 hours to complete. We repeated each MIL experiment three times to get mean of accuracy, F1 score and AUC. Our code will be made publicly available soon.

3.3
Experiment results

Table
1
and Table
2
present the results across the three datasets. Overall, our WeakSupCon method achieves the best performance across all three datasets. The ResNet18 based encoders (MoCo v3, SimCLR, SupCon, and WeakSupCon) pre-trained on local datasets consistently outperform ResNet18 pre-trained on ImageNet, highlighting the significance of feature encoders in downstream MIL tasks. Additionally, two foundation models were included in the experiments. Across the three datasets, these foundation models demonstrate performance comparable to locally pre-trained self-supervised contrastive learning methods, including MoCo v3 and SimCLR. While domain shifts may still exist in foundation models, their pre-training on large histopathological datasets helps mitigate this effect. Interestingly, SupCon performs worse than SimCLR on the Camelyon16 dataset but outperforms SimCLR on the TCGA lung dataset. This discrepancy can be attributed to the proportion of positive patches within positive slides. In Camelyon16, fewer than 10% of patches in tumor slides contain actual tumor samples, leading to poor pseudo-labeling in SupCon. In contrast, WeakSupCon does not assign pseudo-labels to patches in tumor slides; instead, it allows the loss functions to automatically cluster negative samples while enabling positive samples to generate more distinct features. In the TCGA lung dataset, where more than 80% of patches contain cancerous regions, the pseudo-labeling in SupCon is more accurate. The superior performance of SupCon in the TCGA lung dataset also underscores the potential of contrastive learning in a supervised setting compared to its self-supervised counterpart. However, the TCGA lung dataset poses a significant challenge for WeakSupCon. First, real LUAD patches and non-cancerous patches within LUAD slides exhibit morphological differences, making it difficult to cluster them using SimilarityLoss. Additionally, the relatively small proportion of non-cancerous patches in LUAD slides complicates the task of pulling non-cancerous patches from LUSC slides toward the LUAD cluster. Despite these challenges, WeakSupCon still outperforms SimCLR, MoCo v3, and SupCon in the TCGA lung dataset across all metrics, though the performance gap is narrower compared to Camelyon16 and the kidney cancer dataset. In the kidney cancer dataset, accuracies are generally lower across all models compared to the two publicly available datasets, primarily due to the limited number of positive labels in the training set. Additionally, we observe significant variations in accuracy, F1 score, and AUC for MoCo v3, SimCLR, and SupCon. However, WeakSupCon demonstrates stable performance and achieves significantly higher results compared to other models.

4
Conclusion

In this paper, we propose Weakly Supervised Contrastive Learning (WeakSupCon) for encoder pre-training prior to Multiple Instance Learning (MIL) by leveraging bag labels. Despite the lack of patch-level labels, the features learned through WeakSupCon further enhance MIL performance compared to the self-supervised and supervised contrastive learning.
