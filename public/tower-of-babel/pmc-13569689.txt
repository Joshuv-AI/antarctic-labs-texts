Impact of Co‐Morbid Immunocompromise in HPV‐Associated Oropharyngeal Squamous Cell Carcinoma
Authors: Maxwell L. Weng, Tereza Vitkovska, Song Hon Hwang, Lauran K. Evans, Hong‐Ho Yang, Christopher Dann, Dipti Sajed, Maie A. St. John
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesTo characterize the survival and morbidity associated with immunocompromised status in patients with surgically resected Human Papillomavirus positive oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma (HPV + OPSCC).MethodsWe reviewed patients with surgically resected HPV + OPSCC at a tertiary institution between 2000 and 2023. The survival and morbidity associated with an immunocompromised status were assessed with multivariable Cox proportional hazards models controlling for patient, tumor, and treatment characteristics.ResultsAmong 278 patients with HPV+ OPSCC that met inclusion criteria, 14 patients were immunocompromised. Causes of immunocompromise: 4, leukemia or lymphoma; 3, organ transplantation; 3, medically immunosuppressed; 2, HIV; 2, myelodysplastic syndrome and pancytopenia. Adjusting for covariates, the immunocompromised patient group had significantly worse overall survival (64.3% vs. 91.7%; HR 4.12, 95% CI: 1.14–14.90, p < 0.005) compared to the non‐compromised group. The immunocompromised patient group did not have a significantly different postoperative length of stay (3.96 vs. 3.5 days; aβ 0.66, 95% CI: −1.30 to 2.64).ConclusionsIn this small, heterogeneous cohort of surgically resected HPV + OPSCC, immunocompromised status was associated with significantly worse overall survival; however, these exploratory findings should be interpreted cautiously.Level of Evidence3.

Background

Oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma (OPSCC) is a significant cause of mortality and morbidity, with its incidence rising globally [1]. While historically associated with tobacco and alcohol consumption, the epidemiology of OPSCC has shifted, with human papillomavirus (HPV) now recognized as the leading etiology, particularly in high‐income countries [2, 3]. Specifically, HPV now causes up to 70% of OPSCC [3]. In comparison to HPV− OPSCC, HPV+ OPSCC is associated with improved morbidity and mortality [4].

Among immunocompromised patients, HIV and the use of immunosuppressive therapies are known to have an increased risk of cancer, including up to a 6‐fold increased risk of OPSCC [2, 4, 5]. However, the impact of immunocompromised status on survival and morbidity in patients with surgically resected HPV‐OPSCC remains unclear.

Alsidawi et al. demonstrated that solid organ transplant recipients face a markedly increased risk of developing cancer compared to the general population, with head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) occurring at disproportionately higher rates [6]. This elevated risk is driven by long‐term immunosuppressive therapy as well as confounding exposures such as tobacco and alcohol, particularly in liver and heart transplant recipients. Immunocompromised patients are also more susceptible to oncogenic HPV infection. Notably, in this cohort, HPV‐positive oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma (OPSCC) was associated with improved survival relative to other post‐transplant head and neck cancers, suggesting HPV positivity remains a favorable prognostic marker even in immunosuppressed patients. However, outcomes were still inferior to those reported in randomized trials of HPV+ OPSCC among immunocompetent populations [6]. Consistent with this, Hamilton et al. characterized that patients with immunocompromised condition undergoing primary radiation for OPSCC had significantly poorer overall outcomes compared to their immunocompetent counterparts [2]. More recently, Salahuddin et al. found that HIV+ status independently predicts worse survival in patients with HPV+ OPSCC [4].

To our knowledge, data on outcomes of HPV+ head and neck cancer in immunocompromised patients undergoing surgical treatment remain limited and warrant further investigation. With advances in medical therapies allowing patients to live longer, the prevalence of immunocompromised individuals diagnosed with HPV+ OPSCC is expected to rise [5].

The objective of this study is to evaluate morbidity and survival in immunocompromised patients primarily treated with surgical resection for HPV+ OPSCC. We hypothesize that these patients will experience worse survival and higher morbidity compared to immunocompetent counterparts. By addressing this gap, our study aims to expand the current understanding of surgical outcomes in immunocompromised HPV+ OPSCC populations.

Methods

This study is a single institution, retrospective cohort study approved by the UCLA Institutional Review Board. We identified and included in this study all patients with primary OPSCC diagnosed between January 1, 2000 and December 31, 2023 at a single tertiary care academic hospital. Only patients with a confirmed p16‐positive testing at the time of initial diagnosis and treated with primary surgical resection with or without adjuvant therapies were included. Patients with an unknown primary who were found to have primary HPV+ OPSCC during workup were also included.

Data collected from electronic medical records included age at diagnosis, gender, race (White, African American, Asian, Latino, or other), smoking and alcohol use history (current or past use), surgical history (surgical approach), site of primary tumor (tonsil, base of tongue, and posterior pharyngeal wall), adjuvant treatment history, postop length of stay, postop diet (regular, soft, pureed, clear, NPO), date of recurrence, date of last follow up, and date and cause of death (per death certificates). Pathologic T, N, M classification and the presence of extracapsular spread, extranodal extension, lymphovascular invasion, and perineural invasion were extracted from pathology report of surgical samples. HPV status was assessed by p16 immunohistochemistry (> 70% staining).

Patients were classified as immunocompromised if, at the time of HPV+ OPSCC diagnosis, they had a diagnosis of a primary or secondary immunodeficiency (e.g., hematologic malignancy or HIV/AIDS) or were receiving systemic immunomodulating therapy for a solid organ transplant or autoimmune/connective tissue disease. While clinically diverse, these conditions were pooled for analysis based on the shared hallmark of impaired T‐cell mediated immunity, whether through disease‐related dysfunction or chronic iatrogenic immunosuppression, which is hypothesized to influence the immune‐mediated prognosis of HPV+ OPSCC [7]. Additional variables were extracted for patients classified as immunocompromised: immunocompromise etiology, total delivered adjuvant radiation dose (if applicable), adjuvant treatment modifications, and any related toxicity from adjuvant treatment (interruptions, emergency department visits, readmissions).

Patient characteristics were expressed as average and standard deviation for continuous variables and count (percentage) for categorical variables. Categorical variables were summarized using descriptive statistics and compared between groups by Fisher's exact test with a significance level of p < 0.05. Kruskal–Wallis test was used for continuous variables. A univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards test was performed for associations between patient characteristics and overall survival (OS), disease‐free survival (DFS), and cancer‐free survival (CSS). Covariates included in multivariate analysis were age at diagnosis, gender, race, smoking and alcohol use history (current or past use), site of primary tumor (tonsil, base of tongue, and posterior pharyngeal wall), tumor pathologic TNM classification, and treatment‐related covariate variables including the administration of adjuvant therapy (radiation or chemotherapy), with a specific covariate for contralateral adjuvant treatment (defined as the delivery of radiation or chemotherapy to the contralateral tonsil or neck). Kaplan–Meier methods were used to generate the graphs to visualize the survival differences between the immunocompromised and immunocompetent groups.

Anova test was used to assess for difference in length of stay. Log regression test was carried out to determine difference in discharge diet. Alpha of 0.05 was set for this study. R Statistical Software (v4.4.2; R Core Team 2024) was used to perform statistical analysis.

Results

Patient Characteristics

We identified 278 patients with a history of surgically treated primary HPV+ OPSCC that met the inclusion criteria. The patient characteristics and clinical features are summarized in Table 1. The average age of patients at the time of diagnosis was 61.5 years (range 35–93 years). There were 236 male (85%) and 42 female patients (15%). The majority of patients were white (n = 230, 83%), followed by Asian (n = 9, 3%), African American (n = 8, 3%), and Latino ethnicities (n = 8, 3%). More than half of patients were never smokers (n = 154, 56%), followed by former smokers (n = 112, 40%), and smokers at the time of diagnosis (n = 11, 4%). Half of patients reported alcohol use (n = 139, 50%). Fourteen patients (5%) were classified as immunocompromised, and immunocompromise etiologies were heterogeneous (Table 2), and the majority of these immunocompromised patients were diagnosed between 2016 and 2023. Age, gender, race, smoking history, and alcohol use history were not significantly different between the immunocompromised and immunocompetent groups.

OPSCC Characteristics

In the immunocompetent group, the primary tumor site was the tonsil in 46% of patients (n = 122), the base of tongue in 35% (n = 93), and unclassified in 19% (n = 49). Patients in the unclassified category either had multiple concurrent primary sites or incomplete data with missing information on the primary tumor location. In the immunocompromised group, 50% of patients (n = 7) had a base of tongue primary, 21% (n = 3) had tonsil primaries, and 29% (n = 4) were unclassified. Posterior pharyngeal wall cancers, though present in 18 cases reviewed, were never identified as the primary site in this dataset. No significant differences were observed between groups with respect to pathological T, N, or M staging (Table 1).

Immunocompromised patients demonstrated a significantly lower incidence of extracapsular spread in our analysis. However, no statistically significant differences were observed between groups with respect to extranodal extension, lymphovascular invasion, or perineural invasion.

Treatment Characteristics

In the immunocompetent group, 80% of patients had TORS (n = 210) compared to 20% who had open surgery (n = 54), and radiation therapy was administered to 77% (n = 203), of which a total of 36% received combined chemoradiation (n = 95). Overall, 50% of immunocompetent patients (n = 131) had contralateral tonsil treatment with radiotherapy to the contralateral side or chemoradiation. In the immunocompromised group, 71% of patients had TORS (n = 10) compared to 29% who had open surgery (n = 4), and radiation therapy was administered to 13 of 14 patients (93%), of which 5 received combined chemoradiation (36%). Two immunocompromised patients had treatment modifications: one could not undergo chemoradiation; one patient did not undergo chemoradiation due to frailty, and one patient did not undergo any adjuvant treatment due to their CREST syndrome (Table 2). Among the 11 immunocompromised patients with available data, radiation dose distribution was: 70 Gy (n = 3), 66 Gy (n = 3), 60 Gy (n = 4), 50 Gy (n = 1), and only one patient's treatment was interrupted (Table 2). Overall, 71% of immunocompromised patients had contralateral tonsil treatment. Adjuvant treatment‐related toxicity was common in immunocompromised patients, particularly dysgeusia, dysphagia, mucositis, xerostomia, weight loss, anorexia, radiation dermatitis, and pancytopenia (Table 2). Four immunocompromised patients were hospitalized for treatment‐related complications, and three patients needed G‐tube placement for severe dysphagia (Table 2). Overall, there were no significant differences in surgical approach, adjuvant treatment, or contralateral tonsil treatment (Table 1).

Survival Analysis

Among the immunocompetent patient group, 19% (n = 49) had documented recurrence and 9% (n = 24) expired, with a total 8% (n = 22) of those due to cancer‐associated death. Among the immunocompromised group, 21% (n = 3) of the patients had documented recurrence and 36% (n = 5) expired, with a total 21% (n = 3) of those due to cancer‐associated death (Table 2). There was also no significant difference in median follow‐up time between groups (Table 3).

Overall Survival (OS)

In multivariable Cox proportional hazards analysis that included age, sex, stage, primary site, smoking and alcohol use, adjuvant radiation, adjuvant contralateral treatment, several factors were significantly associated with OS. Immunocompromised status was associated with a significantly increased hazard of death (HR 4.12, 95% CI 1.14–14.90, p < 0.005). Patients with a history of smoking had a higher mortality risk (HR 2.68, 95% CI 1.09–6.59, p < 0.005), as did those with advanced pathological T classification (HR 2.13, 95% CI 1.33–3.42, p < 0.005). Additionally, adjuvant contralateral treatment (chemotherapy or bilateral tonsil radiation) was associated with a markedly elevated hazard (HR 12.17, 95% CI 2.20–67.38, p < 0.005) (Table 4).

Cancer‐Specific Survival (CSS)

Regarding CSS, base of tongue as the primary tumor was associated with significantly worse outcomes compared to tonsillar tumors (HR = 5.22, 95% CI: 1.44–18.88, p < 0.005). As with OS, smoking history remained a significant predictor (HR = 2.19, 95% CI: 1.06–7.52, p < 0.005), along with advanced T classification (HR = 2.38, 95% CI: 1.45–3.92, p < 0.005). Additionally, adjuvant contralateral treatment predicted worse CSS (HR = 10.58, 95% CI: 1.80–62.25, p < 0.005). Immunocompromised status and other factors did not reach statistical significance in this model (Table 4). Post hoc power analysis for CSS yielded a power of 24.7%, suggesting that the study was underpowered to achieve statistical significance due to the low number of cancer‐specific deaths.

Graph in Figure 2 demonstrates worse CSS in the immunocompromised patients. However, after performing multivariate analysis, there was no significant difference (Table 3).

Disease‐Free Survival (DFS)

In the DFS model, advanced pathological T stage was significantly associated with increased risk of recurrence (HR = 1.52, 95% CI: 1.05–2.22, p < 0.05). Similarly, patients receiving adjuvant contralateral treatment had poorer DFS outcomes (HR = 6.27, 95% CI: 1.83–21.51, p < 0.05). Additionally, adjuvant radiation therapy appeared protective, with a significantly lower hazard of recurrence (HR = 0.16, 95% CI: 0.04–0.62, p < 0.05). Immunocompromised status, smoking, and primary tumor site were not significant predictors of DFS (Table 4). Post hoc power analysis for DFS yielded a power of 7.5%. This low power, likely due to the modest HR of 1.41 and limited recurrences, suggests our study was not equipped to evaluate differences in disease‐free survival.

A Kaplan–Meier curve illustrating DFS in the immunocompromised and immunocompetent groups is shown in Figure 3. In both unadjusted and covariate‐adjusted analyses, no statistically significant differences in DFS were observed (Table 3).

Functional Outcomes

Regarding postoperative functional outcomes, we evaluated both length of stay and discharge diet. As shown in Table 5, no significant differences were observed between immunocompromised and immunocompetent patient groups in either metric. Pain was not assessed as a functional outcome due to variability in management regimens. Postoperative pain was typically treated with oxycodone in combination with acetaminophen or ibuprofen, or with stellate ganglion blockade, depending on surgeon preference.

Discussion

The impact of immunocompromised status on survival and morbidity in HPV+ OPSCC patients primarily treated with surgery is not well characterized in the literature. In our single‐institution study of patients with surgically resected HPV+ OPSCC, we evaluated clinical and pathological predictors of overall survival (OS), cancer‐specific survival (CSS), and disease‐free survival (DFS). Several important findings emerged that offer insights into prognostic factors and treatment outcomes in this patient population.

Our analysis observed an association between immunocompromised status and worse OS after controlling for factors such as age, gender, race, smoking and alcohol use, primary site tumor location, stage, and primary and adjuvant treatment modality.

Notably, no significant differences were found for immunocompromised patients in our CSS or DFS analysis. Combined with our finding of worse OS among immunocompromised patients, our analysis may suggest that the observed survival difference may be attributable to non‐cancer‐related factors rather than differences in cancer biology and progression. For primary radiotherapy treatment of HPV+ OPSCC, Hamilton et al. found that immunocompromise was an independent predictor for high recurrent and worse DFS, with only a noted trend toward worse OS [2]. However, our study was not sufficiently powered to detect potentially meaningful differences in CSS or DFS with post hoc power analysis at 24.7% and 7.5%, respectively. Thus, while our findings can suggest that non‐cancer‐related mortality disproportionately affects immunocompromised patients with HPV+ OPSCC who undergo primary surgical treatment, further investigation with larger cohorts is needed.

When categorized by broad etiology, our exploratory findings suggest that the nature of immunosuppression may influence oncologic outcomes. The four patients with hematologic malignancies (29% of immunocompromised) seemed to have worse outcomes with two recurrences, one cancer‐associated death, and one non‐cancer‐associated death. This is consistent with the hypothesis that patients with hematologic malignancies may possess dysfunctional adaptive immune cells, like tumor‐infiltrating HPV‐specific T cells, that Welters et al. have hypothesized as to how HPV+ OPSCC generally has favorable prognosis [7]. In contrast, the three patients who had solid organ transplants had no recurrences nor any deaths. Solid organ transplant recipients may have greater incidence of HPV‐related head and neck cancers, and Alsidawi et al. have suggested that patients with solid organ transplants have poor survival and higher risk of recurrence [6]. Likewise for patients who have autoimmune or connective tissue disease and patients who have HIV, studies have shown an increased risk of HPV‐associated cancer and even an association with worse overall survival for HIV, but our three patients with autoimmune or connective disease and our two patients with HIV had no recurrence and just one non‐cancer‐associated death [2, 4, 8, 9]. Finally, there is limited data on patients with myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS) and HPV+ OPSS, but there was one recurrence, one cancer‐associated death, and one non‐cancer associated death among our two patients with MDS. Due to our small sample size of immunocompromised patients, subgroup analysis by immunocompromise etiology was not feasible; however, our observed heterogeneity highlights a need for more investigation into the effects of specific immunocompromise etiologies on oncologic outcomes.

Adjuvant radiation dosing was not systematically de‐intensified among immunocompromised patients, but our data showed a substantial burden of acute toxicity among immunocompromised patients. Further study is required to determine if this burden significantly exceeds that of immunocompetent patients as four (29%) of our immunocompromised patients were hospitalized for emergent complications and three (21%) needed G‐tubes. These align with a previous study in patients with HIV and HPV+ OPSCC that showed how immunocompromise correlates with reduced treatment tolerance, and adjuvant treatment interruptions and complications are known to compromise locoregional control and overall survival in head and neck cancers [10, 11]. Future studies with the systematic extraction of treatment interruption and complication rates are needed to better define the safety profile for immunocompromised patients with HPV+ OPSCC.

The primary tumor site emerged as a significant prognostic factor in cancer‐specific outcomes: patients with base of tongue tumors had five times higher risk of cancer‐specific death compared to those with tonsillar tumors. This aligns with previous literature suggesting that base of tongue tumors may exhibit more aggressive behavior or may be less accessible to surgical resection and radiation dosing. Multiple studies have demonstrated that base of tongue SCC present at higher T stages, are more likely to have incomplete resection, and are associated with worse CSS and OS compared to other oropharyngeal locations such as tonsil. This is largely attributed to anatomical location, propensity for submucosal spread, and frequent presentation at advanced stages, which complicates surgical and radiation treatment [12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17]. Radiation doses for base of tongue tumors due to the need for comprehensive coverage of bilateral neck and adjacent mucosal sites, often results in higher dosing to surrounding normal tissues and increased risk of late toxicity [18].

While we did not specifically track the surgical approach variable in our analysis, most of our patients received the TORS approach with either a radical neck dissection, modified radical neck dissection, or selective neck dissection. None of our patients underwent a surgical approach with a known higher baseline morbidity such as a split mandible or mandibular resection. One other surgical variable that we did not track was ligation of the facial or external carotid artery during a corresponding neck dissection which would reduce the chance of a life‐threatening post‐tonsillectomy bleed. Regarding radiotherapy, we did not track the specific radiation dosages delivered to tumor subsites in our study.

Advanced pathological T classification demonstrated a strong association with outcomes across all three models, reflecting the increased tumor burden and local aggressiveness associated with higher T stages. The absence of significant associations with pN classification (high vs. low) in survival models may reflect limited sample size, misclassification, or effective treatment mitigating the impact of nodal burden.

The association of adjuvant contralateral treatment with poor outcomes across all three models (including an HR > 10 for CSS) likely serves as a marker of higher‐risk disease features rather than an independent causal factor. The clinical requirement for contralateral chemotherapy or bilateral radiation was often prompted by clinical or pathologic indicators of aggressive disease, such as advanced nodal stage or concerns regarding occult contralateral spread. While we included this variable in our multivariable modeling to adjust for these high‐risk profiles, the magnitude of the Hazard Ratio likely reflects residual confounding despite adjustment, as this variable serves as a surrogate for positive‐margin status or other aggressive features known to predict poor survival [19, 20, 21]. Conversely, adjuvant radiation was associated with a significantly lower risk of recurrence (HR 0.16), supporting its therapeutic role in improving local‐regional control.

Smoking history was a consistent negative prognostic factor for both OS and CSS, reinforcing its established role as a driver of adverse outcomes in overall OPSCC [22, 23, 24, 25, 26]. Interestingly, alcohol use history was not significantly associated with any survival outcome, suggesting its impact may be less pronounced when smoking is accounted for. However, given our study is a retrospective review, our analysis of smoking and alcohol use was limited to never, past, or current use and did not account for the quantity in pack years or number of drinks per week.

Regarding postoperative functional recovery, our analysis focused on hospital length of stay and discharge diet as standardized markers of immediate surgical convalescence and stability. While metrics such as long‐term gastrostomy tube (G‐tube) dependence, trismus, and formal speech or swallow scores provide deeper insight into quality of life, these data were not uniformly available across our retrospective cohort and are a limitation. Given the complex medical backgrounds and potentially heightened frailty of immunocompromised patients, the finding that these patients achieve similar early functional milestones as their immunocompetent counterparts is clinically significant and suggests that primary surgical intervention remains a viable and safe pathway for this population.

Our study is a retrospective review constrained by the available data, potentially leading to misclassification. First, the limited number of deaths and recurrences in our immunocompromised patients (n = 14) limited subgroup analysis by immunocompromise etiologies. The Cox proportional hazards models may also be subject to instability due to our limited sample size. Second, the lack of uniformly adjudicated cause‐of‐death data and reliance on potentially incomplete death certificates limits the precision of our cancer‐specific mortality conclusions. Additionally, toxicity grading was inconsistently documented in the medical record, precluding formal toxicity analysis and limiting our ability to quantify the full burden of adverse events. Finally, the extended study period (2000–2023) encompasses significant shifts in clinical practice, including standardization of p16 immunohistochemistry, multiple AJCC staging iterations, the adoption of TORS, and the transition to electronic health records. Additionally, the immunocompromised group was diagnosed concentrated in the latter half of the study period. We maintained diagnostic consistency by only including patients with confirmed p16‐positivity at the time of diagnosis. While patients were not retrospectively re‐staged to the 8th Edition, we utilized individual pathologic T and N classifications in our multivariate models rather than composite stage groupings. This approach ensured that the fundamental tumor and nodal burden was accounted for consistently across the longitudinal cohort, regardless of the contemporary staging edition used. We acknowledge that granular radiotherapy data for earlier cases were not consistently available, precluding a detailed analysis of dose distribution, field design, and treatment completion rates. Furthermore, many of our patients had complex medical backgrounds that were uncontrolled for and could have led to apparent discrepancies in analysis.

Conclusion

Our analysis demonstrated that immunocompromised patients with surgically resected HPV+ OPSCC had significantly worse overall survival compared to immunocompetent patients. However, given the small size and clinical heterogeneity of our immunocompromised cohort, these findings are exploratory and should be interpreted cautiously. Thus, immunocompromise should be considered during treatment planning and surveillance. Larger prospective studies are needed to better define the incidence, disease progression, treatment toxicity, and outcomes of HPV+ OPSCC in immunocompromised patients.
