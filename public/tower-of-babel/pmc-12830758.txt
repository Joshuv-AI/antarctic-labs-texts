SynergyGraph: predicting cell line specific drug combination synergy scores using knowledge graph representation and hypergraph modeling
Authors: Maryam Mehrabani, Amir Lakizadeh, Alireza Fotuhi Siahpirani, Mahdieh Salimi, Fatemeh Zare-Mirakabad, Ali Masoudi-Nejad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Identifying synergistic drug combinations is crucial for enhancing the effectiveness and safety of cancer treatments. However, many existing computational methods mainly depend on pairwise drug modeling or limited molecular descriptors, which overlook the complex biological relationships among drugs, cell lines, and target proteins. To address these challenges, we introduce SynergyGraph, a comprehensive framework that integrates various biomedical data through knowledge graph (KG) construction, embedding learning, and hypergraph-based modeling to predict drug synergy in a cell line-specific way. In the initial phase, a detailed KG is built by linking drugs, proteins, and cell lines through multiple biological associations. Entities and relations, along with their types, are embedded into a shared latent space using Word2Vec, while drug representations are further enriched with structural and physicochemical features. To capture higher-order biological interactions, we create a hypergraph where hyperedges connect known synergistic drug-drug-cell line triplets within a specified threshold, effectively reducing redundancy and improving safety in new combinations. We then employ UniGAT from the UniGNN framework to learn expressive node embeddings. A multi-module deep learning model comprising a BioEncoder, a UniGAT-based Graph Encoder, and a regression-based Decoder is trained to predict synergy scores for drug combinations. The SynergyGraph model demonstrates superior performance on a small subset of the DrugComb dataset. Our results show that SynergyGraph effectively captures complex biological interactions, overcomes pairwise limitations, and reduces redundant effects of dual-drug combinations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-33581-8.

Introduction

Over the past years, improved comprehension of the complex biology of cancer has led to the rapid development of specific anticancer medications. However, a significant barrier in the quest for effective cancer remedies is the development of drug resistance1. Resistance to monotherapy is frequently observed due to the heterogeneous nature of tumor responses to drugs2. This challenge underscores the need for innovative treatment strategies to improve therapeutic outcomes. Combination therapies have emerged as a promising approach to overcome the limitations of monotherapy by targeting multiple pathways and reducing tumor resistance and drug toxicity3. However, finding new synergistic drug combinations is difficult because there are so many possible pairs, and testing them all experimentally isn’t practical. Therefore, computational methods, intense learning models that combine chemical and omics data, are essential for discovering effective drug combinations efficiently4. To improve clarity and organization, this study categorizes computational drug synergy prediction methods into four main classes: deep learning-based models, natural language processing (NLP)-based models, convolutional neural network (CNN)-based models, and feature similarity-based models. In the category of deep learning-based models, we can mention the neural network-based DeepSynergy5, Hypergraph Synergy6, and DeepTraSynergy7 models, along with EC-DFR8, which employs a deep forest ensemble architecture. One of the pioneer computational models in this field was DeepSynergy. This model uses a simple feed-forward neural network with two hidden layers, taking drug chemical features and cell line genomic data as inputs to predict drug synergy. In continuation, the Hypergraph Synergy approach captures multi-drug and multi-gene relationships through hypergraph structures, effectively representing high-order interactions, but it is computationally intensive and less interpretable. DeepTraSynergy addresses these issues by employing transformer-based architectures that efficiently capture long-range dependencies and hierarchical relationships, offering improved scalability and interpretability through attention mechanisms, making it a strong alternative for drug synergy prediction. In another study, the EC-DFR method enhances interpretability by utilizing cascaded ensemble decision trees to quantify feature importance, thereby supporting biological insights and biomarker identification. However, it may struggle to model the complex hierarchical and nonlinear interactions inherent in drug synergy data. In the NLP concept, Yan Lin and colleagues introduced an innovative method called GTextSyn9, which adopts a sentence classification paradigm. It represents drugs and cell lines as biochemical entities and models their combinations as sentences with relational meaning. GTextSyn combines gene expression and chemical structure data through this approach to predict drug synergy, effectively integrating multimodal data. In the domain of CNN-based approaches for drug synergy prediction, PermuteDDS10 has been proposed as a robust end-to-end framework. It uses parallel subnetworks to process different drug fingerprints and cell line omics data, like gene expression and mutations. CNNs extract meaningful patterns from these inputs, which are then merged through a feature fusion layer to capture complex drug-cell interactions. The final synergy score is produced by combining outputs from multiple modules. Within the realm of similarity-based approaches, CFSSynergy11 emerges as a novel computational framework for predicting drug synergy. It uses a transformer-based architecture to extract rich, discriminative representations of drugs and employs Node2Vec12 to capture protein interaction features. This model builds a drug-cell line similarity matrix, integrates it with other similarity features, and feeds the combined representation into an XGBoost model to predict drug synergy. In a refined approach to utilizing multi-omics data within a similarity-based framework, the XDDC model13 employs XGBoost to predict synergistic drug combinations. It integrates chemical structures, side effects, and molecular targets of drugs with cell line data like gene expression, DNA methylation, mutations, CNVs, and RNA interference. By linking these features through biological pathways, the model improves both interpretability and prediction accuracy. A common limitation of many existing methods is that they rely on one or more types of drug and cell line features or similarity measures between them without fully capturing the diverse and complex relationships among these entities. Moreover, they often overlook the critical importance of selecting appropriate features. However, the choice of feature data for representing drugs and cell lines significantly impacts the accuracy of synergy prediction14. One promising approach to modeling complex relationships among drugs, diseases, and biological features is the use of knowledge graphs (KGs), as recently demonstrated in drug repurposing research15–17. This inspired us to introduce a new computational framework based on KG for predicting drug synergy scores. To improve the prediction of drug synergy in a cell line-specific context, we propose SynergyGraph, a multi-step deep learning framework that integrates biological knowledge with graph-based learning. First, a comprehensive KG is constructed by incorporating relationships among drugs, cell lines, and proteins using gene expression similarity, essential gene dependencies, protein-protein interactions, and drug response data. This KG is then transformed into a corpus of entity-relation triples and embedded using Word2Vec18, enabling the capture of functional associations in a shared latent space. To evaluate drug similarity, drug embeddings are further enhanced with molecular features and bioactivity signatures obtained from the Chemical Checker database19. Then, a Hypergraph is constructed to capture synergistic drug combinations across cancer cell lines. Specifically, each hyperedge connects drug pairs whose cell line-specific, drug-induced gene expression profiles show low similarity (Pearson correlation < 0.5 or Euclidean distance > 1). This criterion helps minimize cases where both drugs act on the same genes, allowing the model to focus on combinations with more distinct and potentially complementary effects. By focusing on combinations that interact synergistically rather than just additively, this approach aims to identify treatments with stronger therapeutic effects and fewer side effects. In this Hypergraph, drugs and cell lines are represented as nodes, with hyperedges connecting the corresponding combinations. We apply UniGNN20, a unified message-passing framework for hypergraphs, by combining knowledge graph (KG) embeddings for cell lines with enhanced drug embeddings. The novelty of our framework lies in its integration of rich biological knowledge and advanced graph modeling. We utilize a comprehensive knowledge graph to represent complex relationships between drugs and cell lines, and learn informative relational embeddings. These are combined with detailed chemical and structural drug features to create a stronger, more meaningful drug representation. By modeling synergy with hypergraphs, the framework captures interactions that go far beyond simple drug pairs. It also identifies combinations with complementary mechanisms by incorporating gene-expression dissimilarity. Finally, the UniGNN (UniGAT) architecture, designed specifically for hypergraph data, boosts both predictive performance and interpretability. In contrast to most existing synergy prediction models, which often rely on flat feature vectors or simpler graph architectures, our framework uniquely combines multi-modal embedding fusion, hypergraph modeling, and graph attention mechanisms, resulting in a more powerful and biologically informed prediction model.

We evaluate SynergyGraph against five state-of-the-art models using multiple validation strategies. Across all metrics and settings, including regression, classification, and generalization, SynergyGraph consistently outperformed baselines. It achieves the best results in synergy score prediction, synergy classification, and leave-one-out tests on unseen cell lines. Ablation studies confirm the importance of each model component, and case studies demonstrate its ability to uncover known and novel drug interactions.

Word2Vec is a natural language processing method that learns vector representations of words based on their context, capturing semantic relationships between them. This approach enables the integration of heterogeneous data types (e.g., drugs, proteins, and cell lines) into a common feature space while preserving contextual and functional associations. Drug embedding enrichment

In the third step of the framework, the goal is to enhance the representation of drugs by incorporating molecular similarity and biological features. This is achieved by combining data from the Chemical Checker (CC) database and the previously learned KG embeddings (Fig. 3). The CC is a database containing bioactivity data for nearly 800,000 small molecules. It provides 25 types of drug signatures, known as CC signatures, each capturing different biological or chemical characteristics of molecules. For this work, we use Signature Type 2, which includes network-based embeddings (length = 128) derived from Node2Vec. This embedding captures both explicit and implicit relationships among drugs by modeling their similarity within a network structure.

Synergy score prediction using UniGAT

In this section, we utilize one of the approaches from UniGNN (a Unified framework for Graph and Hypergraph Neural Networks) on our hypergraph to learn the nodes’ latent embeddings. The UniGNN architecture operates by generalizing the message-passing mechanism from GNNs (Graph Neural Networks) to hypergraphs, enabling the learning of expressive representations specific to hypergraph structures (See Supplementary Figure S2 and its explanation). Building on this approach, we design a deep learning framework to predict the synergy of drug combinations in a cell line-specific context. This framework integrates both feature-based and hypergraph-based representations and comprises three main modules: a BioEncoder, a Graph Encoder based on UniGATConv, and a Decoder (See Fig. 5). Results

In this section, we present a comprehensive evaluation of the SynergyGraph framework across both regression and classification tasks relevant to drug synergy prediction. We begin by outlining the standard evaluation metrics used in this domain, including those for continuous and binary prediction. We first assess the model’s performance in predicting synergy scores by treating the task as a regression problem. Predicted values are compared with ground-truth experimental measurements, and SynergyGraph is benchmarked against existing state-of-the-art methods. Next, we evaluate the model in a classification setting, where drug combinations are categorized as synergistic or non-synergistic based on predefined thresholds. The model’s ability to distinguish between these classes is thoroughly analyzed. To enhance interpretability, we investigate the embeddings learned for cell lines through the knowledge graph-based encoding. These embeddings are examined for their capacity to capture biologically meaningful patterns. We further explore SynergyGraph’s ability to uncover novel synergistic drug combinations. By applying the trained model to previously unseen drug pairs across various cell lines, we identify high-confidence candidates that may represent new therapeutic opportunities. Representative examples are provided to demonstrate their biological relevance. Finally, an ablation study is conducted to quantify the contribution of each major component of the SynergyGraph framework. This includes evaluating the impact of knowledge graph embeddings, drug feature enrichment, and hypergraph-based modeling on overall predictive performance. A comprehensive overview of all evaluation metrics is presented in Supplementary Sect. 5: Evaluation Metrics.

To evaluate the generalization capability of the SynergyGraph model, we perform leave-one-out cross-validation on 13 individual cell lines, as illustrated in Fig. 6. For each round, one cell line is excluded from training and used solely for testing, and Pearson’s r between predicted and actual synergy scores is calculated. The results (See Fig. 6) reveal that colon-derived cell lines, such as RKO, SW-620, and HCT116, achieve relatively high Pearson correlation coefficients, despite some of them having limited training samples (e.g., RKO with only 21 combinations and 7 unique drugs in the Supplementary Table S1). This suggests that these cell lines share drug response patterns, allowing the model to generalize well across them. HT29, although having the highest number of drug combinations (725) and unique drugs (46), showed only moderate performance, which may indicate tissue-specific variation. In contrast, prostate cancer cell lines show more varied results. While VCaP (75 combinations) performed reasonably well, LNCaP exhibited a negative Pearson’s r, indicating poor generalization. This is likely due to its extremely limited data (6 combinations with 4 unique drugs) and unique biological characteristics, such as androgen sensitivity and lymph node origin, which differ substantially from the more aggressive and androgen-independent prostate lines like PC-3 and VCaP. Breast, lung, and skin-derived cell lines show generally weaker correlations. For instance, MCF7 and HS578T (breast), A549 (lung), and A375 and SK-MEL-28 (skin) exhibited lower Pearson coefficients despite some having large sample sizes (e.g., MCF7 with 665 combinations). This suggests that these tissues may have more diverse or less predictable drug response patterns, or that the model has limited ability to transfer knowledge across these tissue types. Overall, these results demonstrate that both data quantity and biological similarity significantly impact model generalization in leave-one-out settings. The SynergyGraph model performs best on cell lines that either (i) share response characteristics with others in the training set or (ii) are supported by sufficient data. Conversely, cell lines with limited representation or unique biology (e.g., LNCaP) present a greater challenge and may benefit from tissue-specific modeling or transfer learning approaches.

Explanation of cell line embeddings

To better understand the structure of cell line embeddings, we project into a two-dimensional space using t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE)31. The t-SNE plot (Fig. 7) illustrates each cell line as a colored point and reveals two distinct groups:

At the same time, SW-620 and LOVO are often employed to study colorectal carcinoma with chromosomal instability (CIN). On the other hand, SK-MEL-28 cells exhibit an alteration in the gene encoding NRAS, specifically the Q61R mutation, which is observed in 15–20% of melanomas. Removing structural and physicochemical features from the model (V2) led to a slight increase in RMSE (from 7.536 to 7.63) and a minor decrease in Pearson’s r (from 0.741 to 0.728). This suggests that such features provide valuable molecular-level information such as size, polarity, and hydrophobicity that complement the relational knowledge captured by the knowledge graph. Without these chemically grounded signals, the model loses important discriminatory power, especially when differentiating between drugs that may appear similar in the network but have distinct physicochemical behaviors. Excluding knowledge graph-based drug embeddings (V3) resulted in a more pronounced increase in RMSE (to 7.824) while Pearson’s r remained similar (0.728), though with a noticeable increase in standard deviation. This indicates that knowledge graph embeddings contribute significantly to the model’s robustness by capturing complex biological context and relational dependencies such as PPIs and gene expression similarities that are not present in static features. Removing these embeddings limits the model’s ability to exploit structured domain knowledge, leading to reduced stability and less accurate synergy predictions. Replacing the UniGAT layer with a standard HGNN convolution layer (V4) led to the most significant performance drop, with the highest RMSE (8.01) and the lowest Pearson’s r (0.703) among all ablation variants. This highlights the critical role of the UniGAT layer in enabling attention-based, context-aware message passing within the hypergraph. UniGAT dynamically learns to weigh the importance of each node in a hyperedge, allowing the model to focus on the most relevant components of each drug-drug-cell line triplet. In contrast, HGNN applies uniform aggregation, treating all nodes equally, which limits the model’s ability to capture nuanced relationships and ultimately reduces both prediction accuracy and interpretability. Removing the hypergraph structure from the model (V5) resulted in a performance decline, with the RMSE increasing to 7.99 and Pearson’s r dropping to 0.713. This demonstrates the importance of the hypergraph in capturing higher-order interactions among drugs and cell lines. Unlike simple pairwise graphs, hypergraphs naturally represent the triplet-based synergy relationships central to this task. Eliminating this structure reduces the model’s ability to learn from joint effects across drug-drug-cell line combinations, thereby weakening its capacity to accurately predict synergistic interactions. Based on these findings, we can conclude that all components of the SynergyGraph model contribute to its overall performance in predicting drug synergy.

Discussion and conclusion

SynergyGraph offers a novel approach to predicting the synergy scores of drug combinations in a cell line-specific context. By integrating a KG and a hypergraph, the framework captures complex biological relationships between drugs and cancer cell lines, intending to improve therapeutic outcomes while reducing adverse effects. Unlike conventional approaches that treat relationships as simple binary connections, SynergyGraph embeds both entities and relation types into a shared latent space. Drug representations are further enriched with structural fingerprints and physicochemical properties, capturing nuanced similarities. To overcome limitations of pairwise modeling, SynergyGraph constructs a hypergraph where each hyperedge encodes a known synergistic drug-drug-cell line triplet. This structure incorporates correlations in drug-induced gene expression profiles, helping avoid redundant targeting of the same genes, a key factor in enhancing synergy and reducing adverse effects.

Moreover, the SynergyGraph model employs a more compact feature representation compared to the EC-DFR model, which uses a significantly larger feature vector. This streamlined approach may help reduce overfitting and lower computational demands. Despite SynergyGraph’s strong predictive performance, the study is subject to certain limitations. First, the KG is built specifically for our dataset. As new cell lines or drug pairs appear, it must be continually updated with relevant information and their relationships to existing entities. Furthermore, this study is constrained by the number of drug combinations available when using GP as a feature of drugs. However, as new drugs and cell lines emerge, obtaining GP data can be readily accomplished at a low cost through the L1000 experiment method or predictive models. For example, Zhu et al.42 introduced a deep learning-based model named DLEPS, which leverages the SMILES representations of molecules to predict the 978-dimensional gene expression profiles (GPs) from the LINCS dataset, achieving a Pearson correlation coefficient of 0.74. For future work, we plan to expand the knowledge graph by incorporating additional biological data such as drug fingerprints, signaling pathways, single-cell transcriptomics, and multi-omics data to enrich entity relationships and embeddings. Moreover, inspired by methods like SpaTalk43, which merges KGs with spatial transcriptomics, incorporating spatially resolved single-cell data and ligand-receptor interactions, could allow the model to capture how drug responses propagate within the tumor microenvironment, revealing context-dependent synergy patterns. We are also inspired by recent advances in graph-based machine learning. For instance, LCAAG44 excels at uncovering overlapping communities in attributed graphs, while FMvPCI45 effectively integrates multiple biological views to identify protein complexes. Leveraging these approaches, we plan to enhance our hypergraph modeling, allowing for more effective fusion of heterogeneous features and improved representation of overlapping functional modules. We also aim to improve the hypergraph model by introducing weighted hyperedges based on Pearson correlation and drug synergy scores, thereby capturing interaction strengths more effectively. Additionally, to address the challenge of cell lines with limited data, we propose leveraging transfer learning and multi-task learning approaches that enable knowledge sharing from well-characterized cell lines. Data augmentation and few-shot learning techniques will also be explored to enhance model robustness and predictive performance in low-data scenarios. These advancements are expected to further improve the accuracy, interpretability, and generalizability of synergy predictions.
