ERAS perioperative management measures in total hip replacement in HIV-positive patients with osteonecrosis of the femoral head
Authors: Shengtao Li, Bo Liu, Rui Ma, Kangpeng Li, Qiang Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aims to investigate the feasibility and effectiveness of enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) in HIV-positive patients diagnosed with osteonecrosis of the femoral head (ONFH) undergoing total hip replacement (THR).MethodsWe retrospectively included 80 HIV-positive patients diagnosed with ONFH who underwent THR between 2011 and 2022. Forty patients treated before August 2019 constituted the control group, receiving standard antiviral regimens and traditional perioperative management pathways. The remaining 40 patients, treated after August 2019, formed the study group, which followed the ERAS protocol. This protocol emphasized the use of more effective antiviral medications, rapid viral load reduction, immune enhancement, improved nutritional status, control of co-infections, prophylactic antibiotics, and anti-osteoporosis measures. We recorded patients' general status and imaging examinations before surgery, as well as detailed perioperative management strategies, antiviral regimens, durations, and immunological indicators for both groups. Targeted and standardized treatment measures were applied to the ERAS group, allowing for a comparison of the efficacy of perioperative management between the two patient groups.ResultsPreoperative nutritional and immune indicators were lower in the control group than in the study group, while inflammatory markers were higher. Postoperatively, immune, nutritional, and inflammatory indicators were significantly better in the ERAS group compared to the control group. Following antiviral treatment, the viral load was predominantly undetectable in the ERAS group (target not detected, TND). Comprehensive measures minimized complications in the ERAS group (P = 0.028, P < 0.05). The hospitalization duration for the ERAS group was significantly shorter than that of the control group, with both groups showing marked improvement compared to preoperative conditions and no incidents of loosening or dislocation.ConclusionStrengthening antiviral treatment, anti-infective strategies, incision care, and nutritional support effectively prevents and reduces complications such as delayed wound healing in HIV-positive patients. The implementation of ERAS measures requires careful attention to the patient’s immune status, close monitoring of clinical changes, and timely adjustments to treatment and care plans.Level of evidenceTreatment studies.Keywords: HIV-positive, Osteonecrosis of the femoral head, Total hip replacement, Enhanced recovery after surgery, Perioperative measures, Complications

Introduction

According to the United Nations Joint Programme on HIV/AIDS (UNAIDS), approximately 39 million people globally are living with HIV infection (PLWH), with about 29.8 million receiving life-saving treatment [1]. In 2020, 2021, and 2022, an additional 1.6 million individuals accessed HIV treatment each year. If this annual growth continues, the global goal of 35 million individuals on HIV treatment by 2025 will be attainable [1]. The introduction of highly active antiretroviral therapy (HAART) has significantly prolonged the survival of HIV-infected patients, leading to a gradual increase in both new cases and associated orthopedic complications. The incidence of osteonecrosis of the femoral head (ONFH) in PLWH is substantially higher—10 to 100 times—than in the general population [2]. Males aged 30–70 are most frequently affected, with 50% of cases being bilateral. In adults, the primary cause of ONFH is traumatic, often resulting from proximal hip fractures or dislocations. Non-traumatic risk factors for ONFH include prolonged corticosteroid use and alcohol abuse, which account for approximately 80% of cases. Other contributing factors include Caisson disease (dysbaric osteonecrosis), thromboembolic events (either acquired or congenital), Legg–Calvé–Perthes disease, radiation exposure, cytotoxic agents, Gaucher disease, hyperlipidemia, and pancreatitis. Some literature also reports cases of genetically determined ONFH; however, the etiology often remains unknown, leading to classification as idiopathic. Total hip replacement (THR) is typically performed in advanced ONFH stages or when rescue procedures fail.In ONFH, apoptosis of osteoblasts and suppression of bone marrow activity within the femoral head impairs the ability of bone to remodel, leading to femoral head collapse and premature osteoarthritis (OA). The natural history of femoral head necrosis shows a poor prognosis, with up to 80% of conservatively treated patients requiring THR [3, 4]. In the United States, over 300,000 THRs are conducted annually, with projections indicating an increase of 173% to 572,000 by 2030. This rise in procedures parallels the growing need for THR revisions and associated complications, with revision surgery rates expected to double by 2026 [5].In many Asian countries, the incidence of ONFH is even higher; in Japan, 2500–3300 new cases of ONFH are diagnosed each year, while in South Korea, the number of new cases per year is estimated to be 14,103, and the incidence rate has increased from 20.53/100,000 in 2002 to 37.96/100,000 in 2006 [6].

ONFH predominantly affects males in their fifth decade of life, leading to osteochondral collapse and premature hip osteoarthritis (HOA). Timely diagnosis is crucial for implementing joint-preserving treatments (JPT) aimed at delaying THR. Both conservative approaches (e.g., biophysical stimulation, such as pulsed electromagnetic fields and extracorporeal shock wave therapy) and surgical techniques (e.g., core decompression, osteotomies, non-vascularized, and vascularized bone grafting) are well-documented [7]. The use of precision surgery can reduce postoperative complications in both fractures and planned surgeries. For instance, computer-assisted navigation systems have been successfully employed in various orthopedic surgeries, including spinal and total hip/knee replacements [8–11]. Despite numerous surgical innovations and improvements in techniques and patient education, the incidence of prosthetic joint infections (PJI) and the necessity for THR revisions have increased, with recent literature reporting an annual PJI incidence ranging from 1.17 to 3.3% [12]. Concerns regarding PJI have led to increased focus on optimizing perioperative interventions for modifiable patient risk factors, such as body mass index (BMI), management of comorbidities (e.g., diabetes mellitus), and prevention of Staphylococcus aureus colonization. HIV status is a significant modifiable risk factor for PJI [13]. Early studies of PLWH reported joint infection rates in arthroplasty ranging from 14 to 25% [14, 15]. Research prior to HAART indicated that low CD4+ T-lymphocyte counts increased the likelihood of poor surgical outcomes and higher mortality rates in PLWH [16]. During the early stages of HAART, CD4+ T lymphocyte counts below 200 × 106/L were associated with increased rates of postoperative infections and PJI in HIV-infected hemophiliacs [17]. Even in HIV patients with generally normal organ function, immune function may be impaired, leading to increased surgical risks and complications. Therefore, special attention and therapeutic measures are necessary during the perioperative period compared to the general population.

However, many current studies lack an examination of the effectiveness and feasibility of applying Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) concepts or protocols in THR for HIV-positive patients with ONFH. In response, we explored and initiated perioperative rapid recovery (ERAS) management measures, which primarily include rapid and efficient preoperative antiviral therapy (HAART), enhancement of preoperative immune function, improvement of nutritional status, detection and management of comorbidities, rational use of antibiotics, and control of opportunistic infections. Patients treated prior to this initiative received traditional perioperative management measures (the control group), while those undergoing rapid recovery protocols comprised the ERAS group (the study group). This study aims to evaluate the effectiveness and safety of the ERAS protocol in HIV-positive ONFH patients undergoing THR and to explore the feasibility and impact of ERAS management measures in this population.

Information and methods

General information

This study included cases from HIV-positive patients diagnosed with osteonecrosis of the femoral head who presented to our orthopedic department between 2011 and 2022.

Inclusion criteria for the study group

Confirmed diagnosis of HIV infection, according to the Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of AIDS [18].

Based on these criteria, a total of 80 patients who underwent THR for ONFH between 2011 and 2022 were included in this study: 40 patients treated before August 2019 formed the control group, while 40 patients comprised the study group. Preoperative data collected included gender, age, vital signs, blood tests, liver and kidney function, BMI, and comorbidities (e.g., tuberculosis, syphilis, hepatitis B, hepatitis C, cardiopulmonary diseases). Imaging assessments (X-ray, CT, MRI) were performed, and ARCO staging was documented.

Perioperative treatment measures were meticulously recorded for both groups, including antiviral regimen and duration, as well as CD4+ and CD8+ T cell counts, CD4+/CD8+ ratios, and viral load. Nutritional status and immunization data were also collected. The ERAS group received standardized treatment measures preoperatively, during the procedure, immediately postoperatively, and one week after surgery. Key metrics such as operation time, blood loss, transfusion volume, postoperative changes in relevant indices, activities of daily living (ADL), and Harris hip scores were documented. Hospitalization duration, follow-up imaging changes, and early postoperative complications were recorded for both groups, along with the occupational exposure of medical personnel during the perioperative period (Table 1).

Surgical approach and perioperative management

Preoperative treatment and selection of the HAART program

HIV infection can lead to the reactivation of dormant pathogenic microorganisms in the body, impairing immune function and increasing susceptibility to opportunistic infections. To address these common complications, it is recommended that patients with CD4+ T lymphocyte counts below 200 cells/μL or a history of oral candidiasis receive prophylaxis against Pneumocystis carinii pneumonia (PCP) using cotrimoxazole. For patients not receiving prophylaxis, heightened vigilance for PCP is necessary, particularly in cases of postoperative pulmonary complications. For those with CD4+ T lymphocyte counts below 50 cells/μL and a history of Mycobacterium avium complex (MAC) infection, prophylactic treatment with clarithromycin or azithromycin may be considered [18, 23, 24]. Opportunistic infections remain a significant cause of mortality among HIV-positive individuals, and the World Health Organization recommends the early initiation of highly active antiretroviral therapy (HAART) within seven days of diagnosis.

We advocate for the early administration of a highly effective HAART regimen to achieve rapid viral load reduction prior to surgery, with long-term maintenance therapy afterward tailored to the patient's financial situation. The rapid and effective HAART regimen can reduce the patient's viral load to undetectable levels (< 20 copies/mL) within 2–4 weeks, thereby significantly lowering the risk of surgical site infections and other complications, as well as reducing the risk of healthcare workers contracting HIV from post-surgical occupational exposure [25–29]. A comprehensive set of perioperative preventive measures against opportunistic infections can further mitigate infection risk [30].

For patients in the Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) group, the HAART regimen was based on a newly recommended single-tablet regimen: BIC/FTC/TAF (Bitovir, Bic Enbrel), along with ABT (Abbavitide for injection), which was initiated immediately following HIV diagnosis without significant adverse effects. In contrast, the Traditional Protocol (TP) group received the classical regimen of TDF + 3TC + EFV, also initiated immediately post-diagnosis, without significant adverse effects. Notably, preoperative immunologic indices in the ERAS group, including CD4+ T lymphocyte and CD8+ T lymphocyte counts, were significantly higher than those in the TP group, while the viral load was lower in the TP group, with statistically significant differences (P < 0.05). This suggests that the HAART regimen in the ERAS group is superior in terms of achieving faster viral reduction and enhancing immunity, thereby reducing the incidence of opportunistic infections and occupational exposure risk for healthcare workers (Table 3).

Surgical approach and intraoperative management

All patients underwent unilateral THR in separate sessions using the posterior-lateral modified Gibson approach. The femoral head prostheses were made of ceramic materials, with 68 cases sourced from the German LINK company and 12 cases from the domestic Beijing Chunli company. To minimize occupational exposure, the surgical team was equipped with protective clothing resembling space suits, complete with a ventilation device (Stryker Company, USA), as well as protective masks, hats, face shields, two pairs of sterilized gloves (or French anti-virus double-layer gloves), and protective boots. The surgical team is relatively stable and comprises an orthopedic surgeon, an anesthesiologist, a nurse, a psychologist, and a nutritionist. Psychologists and dietitians assist with mental health and nutritional assessments to optimize the patient's condition. A perioperative program was developed based on evidence-based practices and principles of the Rapid Recovery Program (Table 2). The control group followed a traditional perioperative care protocol administered by the same team, aiming to shorten surgery duration while ensuring safety.

Before the surgical procedure commenced, the affected limb was washed with soapy water, wiped with alcohol, and covered with iodophor gauze for 15 min, followed by routine sterilization and drying. After each surgical step, the limb was rinsed with 100 ml of iodophor and saline to ensure complete hemostasis. In cases of acetabular abrasion, the approach depended on the patient's specific circumstances, including extensive femoral head necrosis or significant bone defects. If necessary, cancellous bone from the neck of the femoral head was harvested for grafting, as recommended by the surgeon. Prior to the operation, the surgeon conducted a thorough evaluation and preoperative preparation to ensure the safety and feasibility of the procedure. Postoperative rehabilitation and follow-up are equally important for evaluating surgical efficacy; thus, a larger bone graft of 2–4% of the specimen size was selected.

The acetabular prosthesis was sized 2–4 sizes larger than the trial mold, with screws used for enhanced fixation. For patients with significant osteoporosis, a biomimetic prosthesis or an extended shank was utilized to disperse stress. The operation time, total blood loss, and hospitalization duration in the ERAS group were significantly lower than those in the TP group (Table 6).

Postoperative management

Routine postoperative monitoring included assessments of CD4+ T cell count, CD8+ T cell count, and viral load (VL). For HIV-positive patients undergoing THR, aseptic technique was crucial, and antibiotics were administered postoperatively to prevent infection, with the duration adjusted based on the surgical incision type and patient condition. Discharge criteria were identical for both groups, including stable vital signs, a satisfactory mental state, absence of fever, dry dressings without exudate, tolerable and reducing pain, a normal diet, no discomfort during ambulation, and proficiency in rehabilitation training. The perioperative period adhered to the ERAS principles, resulting in significantly shorter hospitalization compared to the TP group (Table 6).

Postoperative follow-up and functional evaluation

CD4+ T-cell counts, CD8+ T-cell counts, and VL tests were conducted regularly at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months postoperatively, followed by annual assessments. The improvement in patients’ ability to perform daily activities was evaluated using the Activity of Daily Living Scale (ADL) at admission and discharge, with all patients demonstrating significant improvements compared to their admission status (Table 7). The Harris Hip Score (HHS) was assessed during follow-up to evaluate hip function improvement (Fig. 1). Recorded complications included surgical site infection, pneumonia, sepsis, cardiovascular events, mortality, lower extremity venous thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, urinary tract infection, cerebrovascular accidents, pressure ulcers, bowel obstruction, and shock. Additionally, perioperative occupational exposure among medical personnel was monitored to compare surgical complication incidence rates between the two groups. Orthopantomograms and frog-leg position X-ray examinations were repeated at each follow-up visit for imaging evaluation.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 26.0 software. Qualitative data were expressed as case numbers, while continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD). Comparisons between groups were performed using independent samples t-tests. For the evaluation of the same indices across three or more time points during the follow-up period, repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) was utilized. Categorical data were expressed as frequencies, with group comparisons analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact probability method. Differences were deemed statistically significant at P < 0.05.

Results

Patients in the TP group had a postoperative follow-up ranging from 13 to 62 months, with a mean duration of 24 months. In contrast, patients in the ERAS group had follow-up periods from 12 to 38 months, also with a mean of 24 months. Compared to the TP group, the ERAS group exhibited significantly shorter operation times (95.20 ± 42.65 min vs. 160.00 ± 46.40 min), reduced hospital stays (13.95 ± 4.80 days vs. 20.49 ± 3.78 days), and less total blood loss (580 ± 350 ml vs. 800 ± 366 ml) (Table 6). The ERAS protocol emphasizes early postoperative functional recovery and rehabilitation training, promoting rapid patient recovery by optimizing surgical processes, pain management, and early mobilization.

In terms of immune, nutritional, and inflammatory indices, the ERAS group significantly outperformed the TP group (Table 8). After a period of antiviral medication, most patients in the ERAS group had an undetectable viral load (TND); however, long-term adherence to antiviral therapy remained essential post-surgery.

Discussion

In ONFH, blood supply is inadequate to meet the metabolic needs of the bone. Initial management is typically conservative; however, surgical intervention is indicated if conservative measures fail [32]. PLWH are often immunocompromised, presenting with multiple comorbidities, which increase their susceptibility to opportunistic infections, chronic inflammation, and malnutrition. Studies indicate that PLWH undergoing various surgical procedures experience a higher incidence of postoperative complications, with varying complication rates reported in those undergoing arthroplasty [33–35]. Naziri et al. [33]. reported a complication rate of 2.9% for major complications and 5.2% for minor complications after THR in 9,275 HIV-positive patients. Besides CD4+ T-cell counts and HAART regimens, comorbid medical conditions also significantly influence complication rates [36, 37]. Hepatitis is the most prevalent comorbidity among PLWH, and the risk of postoperative infections is significantly elevated, especially in patients with chronic hepatitis C co-infected with HIV [38]. Such patients require specialized perioperative management. Recent studies have shown that thorough assessment and optimization of HIV status before THR, along with standardized perioperative management and postoperative follow-up, can significantly decrease postoperative complications in HIV-positive ONFH patients and enhance clinical outcomes [39–45]. Furthermore, these measures may also reduce the risk of occupational exposure among healthcare personnel.

In this study, we retrospectively included 80 HIV-positive ONFH patients. Following the introduction of regimens such as Bitovir + Ebovirate after August 2019, we initiated an enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) protocol for these patients undergoing THR and compared it to the traditional program (TP) regimen. Our findings indicate that HIV infection is a manageable risk factor for complications post-THR [15], with a reported infection rate of 0.76%−3.3% in the recent literature [46]. By matching patients based on age, surgical procedures, prosthesis types, and postoperative rehabilitation protocols, while ensuring an adequate sample size, we confirmed that the ERAS protocol significantly reduces postoperative complication rates compared to the TP protocol. Additionally, it improves surgical outcomes and safety for these patients and significantly mitigates the risk of occupational exposure to healthcare workers.

Advantages of the ERAS program

Parvizi et al. [15] noted a high incidence of deep infections and complications in hemophiliac HIV-positive ONFH patients following THR, with Lehman et al. [14]reporting a similar increase—about a tenfold rise in infection-related complications in HIV-positive ONFH patients compared to other orthopedic procedures. We conclude that maximizing preoperative immune status (e.g., CD4+ T-cell levels), nutritional status, and the efficacy of antiretroviral treatment (very low viral load) is critical for successful surgical outcomes, effectively reducing the rate of postoperative complications in PLWH undergoing THR. The ERAS program has significant advantages in reducing pain, promoting recovery, and minimizing surgical complications. This approach not only conserves medical resources but also alleviates the financial burden on patients and their families. Our study results demonstrated that the ERAS protocol reduces postoperative complication rates, facilitates rapid recovery of hip function, shortens hospital stays, reduces hospitalization costs, satisfies patients' psychological needs for swift recovery, and improves doctor-patient relationships.

Postoperative complications and their risk factors

In the TP group, 13 cases of short-term postoperative complications were observed, compared to 4 cases in the ERAS group (P = 0.028). These complications included opportunistic infections, delayed wound healing, and venous thrombosis of the lower extremities, all of which were effectively managed conservatively and may be linked to a shorter follow-up period. No complications such as periprosthetic infections or dislocations were reported. Migliorini et al. [6] identified male gender, prolonged symptom duration before treatment, higher Visual Analog Scale (VAS) scores, and lower Harris Hip Scores (HHS) as negative prognostic factors following ONFH treatment. Age, BMI, etiology of osteonecrosis, time from surgery to full weight bearing, and drill size did not correlate with clinical outcomes after treatment. Lin et al. [47] indicated that HIV positivity is not an independent risk factor for adverse surgical outcomes; rather, the complication rates among HIV-positive individuals without other comorbidities or known risk factors are comparable to those of the general population, emphasizing the relevance of comorbidities such as malnutrition, liver disease, and chronic kidney disease.

Recent advancements in antiretroviral therapy, such as the single-tablet formulation Bitovir (BIC/FTC/TAF), have enabled faster reductions in viral load, which effectively inhibits viral replication and swiftly decreases viral load in seminal fluid [48]. Our study compared the effects of the BIC/FTC/TAF regimen with that of tenofovir, lamivudine, and efavirenz (TDF/3TC/EFV) on postoperative complications following THR in HIV-positive ONFH patients. We found that viral suppression was more rapid in the ERAS group, which corresponded with a swift increase in CD4+ T-cell counts. Preoperative use of a rapid and effective antiretroviral regimen that lowers the viral load of PLWH to low or undetectable levels, combined with improvements in patients' nutritional status and a targeted antibiotic regimen, can significantly reduce surgical complications. Additionally, these measures can decrease the potential for occupational exposure to HIV among healthcare workers, making them more willing to operate on PLWH under safer conditions, thereby alleviating the burden on healthcare systems and families while improving patients' quality of life.

Postoperative evaluation

The ERAS protocol facilitates early care, accelerates viral suppression, enhances patients' quality of life, significantly improves early Harris scores, and reduces the financial burden associated with managing HIV and AIDS-related sequelae. Meintjes et al. [49] recommended that all HIV-positive ONFH patients, regardless of CD4+ T-cell counts or viral load, should adhere to the principles of the ERAS approach.

Shortcomings and limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it is a retrospective study, which may introduce bias. Second, patients underwent surgery over a span of up to 10 years, during which surgical techniques and treatments may have evolved, potentially affecting sample homogeneity. Third, this study may not fully represent the outcomes of THR in HIV-positive ONFH patients, necessitating further research with larger sample sizes and long-term follow-up to assess the long-term effects and complications of the ERAS protocol in this population.

Conclusion

By enhancing anti-infective treatment, surgical incision care, and nutritional support, we can effectively prevent and mitigate complications such as delayed wound healing. For HIV-positive patients with unique medical needs, implementing ERAS measures requires careful attention to the patient's immune status and close monitoring of their condition. Timely adjustments to treatment and nursing care plans, alongside personalized rehabilitation and psychological support, can help patients better manage postoperative pain, anxiety, and related issues, ultimately improving their quality of life and satisfaction.
