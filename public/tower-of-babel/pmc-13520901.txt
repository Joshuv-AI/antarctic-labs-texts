Declining body weight of moose calves related to parasite and disease burden
Authors: Marja Isomursu, Leena Ruha, Antti Uotila, Jyrki Pusenius
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBody weights of hunted moose calves have markedly declined in southwestern Finland during this millennium, but the cause of this phenomenon is yet unknown. We studied whether the decline is associated with prevalence of parasites and diseases. For this purpose, we collected tissue samples (lung, heart, liver and faeces) from moose calves hunted in autumn in study area with declining calf body weights (N = 45) and in control area with no decline in body weight (N = 19). We compared the findings between regions and studied the relationship between carcass weight and parasitological and pathological parameters. The organ samples revealed infections by several parasites, including the lungworms Dictyocaulus capreolus and D. skrjabini, the cestode Taenia lynciscapreoli and the lancet liver fluke Dicrocoelium dendriticum. Parasitic infections of the digestive tract (strongylid nematodes, Moniezia sp., Eimeria sp.) and larvae of the meningeal worm Elaphostrongylus alces were observed by examining faecal samples. Inflammatory lesions in the membranes surrounding lung, heart and liver and in bile ducts occurred in all areas. We found that moose calves from the area with low and declining weight had a wider range of parasites and pathologies than calves from the control area. There was a negative correlation between total parasite and pathology burden and the carcass and heart weights. Parasite and disease burden was highest and weight parameters were lowest in the areas with the highest moose and total cervid densities.Keywords: Moose calf, Weight, Parasite, Disease, Dictyocaulus

Introduction

Wild animals are commonly burdened by parasites that can have significant negative effects on nutritional status, growth and other aspects of energetic condition (Shanebeck et al., 2022). An individual is often infected by multiple parasites, and these co-infections can have cumulative effects that enhance the harm experienced by the host (Bordes and Morand, 2011, Vaumourin et al., 2015). Negative effects of parasites are driven e.g. by reduced foraging, costs of immune response and environmental stress, but some mechanisms may lead to positive connection between parasite infection and condition (Sánchez et al., 2018) indicating a complex nature of relationships between the host and the parasite in the wild.

In wild cervids, there is evidence of negative connections between internal parasite infections and body condition (Stien et al., 2002; Irvine et al., 2006; Davidson et al., 2015) as well as blood-feeding ectoparasites and survival (Debow et al., 2021). Lactation period in female deer may lead to an increased gastrointestinal parasite burden which results in lower survival and lower birth weight of calves (Albery et al., 2021). Juveniles may be particularly susceptible to the effects of parasites, as has been observed with red deer (Acerini et al., 2022). Sympatrically occurring cervids often share parasite species that may vary in pathogenicity depending on the host species (Lankester, 2010; Malcicka, 2015; Świsłocka et al., 2021).

The moose Alces alces is the largest species in the family Cervidae. It is a valuable game animal as around 40,000 moose are hunted annually in Finland during the legal hunt in autumn. In the southern part of Finland, the moose range overlaps the ranges of three other cervids, the abundant roe deer Capreolus capreolus and white-tailed deer Odocoileus virginianus and the sporadic fallow deer Dama dama. The two latter species have been introduced to the country as captive animals and later released to nature.

According to hunting statistics, a declining trend in the weights of hunted moose calves has been observed in several moose management areas (MMAs) in Finland in recent years. This has been especially notable in the southwestern part of the country where average calf weights have decreased ca. 15% during the last 20 years and are up to 20 kg lower than in neighbouring areas with no decline in weights. A similar trend has been observed in neighbouring country Sweden where weather conditions were believed to influence the calf weights (Holmes et al., 2021). The causes behind the declining calf weights are most likely complex and include a mixture of abiotic environmental factors and biotic factors, including parasites.

European moose are hosts for a wide range of parasites, e.g. gastrointestinal nematodes (Filip-Hutsch et al., 2021; Grandi et al., 2018), nose bot flies (Rolandsen et al., 2021) and vector-borne protozoans (Malmsten et al., 2019). The Finnish moose are known to harbour various kinds of parasites although reports on gastrointestinal and pulmonary parasites are lacking. The deer ked (Lipoptena cervi) may cause intense infestations but, in general, seems to cause little physiological harm (Paakkonen et al., 2012), although massive infections have been linked to severe hair loss in Norway (Madslien et al., 2011). Surveys based on blood samples of moose have revealed antibodies for the protozoan parasite Toxoplasma gondii (Jokelainen et al., 2010), and microfilariae of the filarioid nematodes Setaria tundra (Laaksonen et al., 2008) and Rumenfilaria andersoni (Laaksonen et al., 2015). A survey of spinal cords of hunted moose calves revealed infections of the protostrongylid nematode Elaphostrongylus alces (Nygrén et al., 2013). The moose also serves as an intermediate host for cestode parasites (Taenia spp., Echinococcus canadensis) of carnivores (Haukisalmi et al., 2011, 2016; Oksanen and Lavikainen, 2015). The effects of parasites on moose health in Finland are still mostly unclear.

The aim of this study was to shed light on the role of parasites and diseases in the issue of declining calf weights. We collected organ samples from hunted moose calves (6-7 months old) in MMAs with low and declining body weights (study area) and in those with stable body weights (control area) and (1) compared the body weight and prevalence of parasites and diseases between the study and control areas and (2) studied the relationship between observed parasite infections and diseases and body condition using carcass weight and heart weight as proxies. Additionally, we discuss the possible connection of cervid density with moose calf weight and parasite prevalence.

Material and methods

Study region and sample collection

Three MMAs were chosen to represent the affected region where the moose calf weights were notably low in national comparison and have clearly decreased in this millennium (from hereafter the study area) (Fig. 1, Fig. 2). Two MMAs, where the moose calf weights were at the national average level and no trend-like changes have occurred in this millennium were chosen as control (control area). In the turn of the millennium, the calf weights in all these five MMAs were approximately at the same i.e. national average level (Fig. 2). The MMAs of the study area were situated in south and southwestern Finland (Varsinais-Suomi VS, Uusimaa UU and Etelä-Häme EH). The control MMAs were located east (Etelä-Savo ES) and north (Satakunta - Pohjois-Häme SP) of the study area (Fig. 1). The study area is situated in the southernmost Finland, in the southern boreal and hemiboreal zones, while the control area belongs to the southern and middle boreal zones. One area (VS) stands out as much of it is situated in the archipelago of southwestern Finland (Fig. 1). The environmental conditions likely to be relevant to the condition of moose are described below for the study and control areas. Weather data is aggregated from the Finnish meteorological institute database (https://en.ilmatieteenlaitos.fi/), and data concerning landscape structure and vegetation from the database of the Natural Resources Institute Finland Luke (https://opendata.luke.fi/). Winter temperatures from January to March are on the average somewhat lower in the control (−3.3°C) compared to the study area (−1.1°C). Snow cover in February – March, when it is as its thickest, is on the average thicker in the control (32 cm) compared to the study area (16 cm). The average temperatures in the early growing season May – June are almost similar between the compared areas (control 13.4°C; study 13.2°C), whereas precipitation during that period is slightly higher in the control (52.3 mm) compared to the study area (47.6 mm). The overall productivity of environment, indicated by the proportion of arable land in the landscape, is lower in the control (10%) compared to the study area (14%). The landscape level proportion of scots pine (Pinus sylvestris) seedling and sapling stands is slightly lower in the control (17%) compared to the study areas (19%). The cover of bilberry (Vaccinium myrtillus) in forest field layer is slightly lower in the control areas (10%) compared to the study areas (16%), The study and control areas also differ in terms of moose and combined cervid density (incl. moose, white-tailed deer, roe deer and fallow deer). In the study area, especially white-tailed deer and roe deer density, but also moose density, is higher than in the control area. The fallow deer only occurs in the study area. Feeding places serving typically apples, carrots, grain or hay are managed in all MMAs. These are used for hunting and are mainly directed to white-tailed deer but are often visited by other cervids as well.

Moose hunters collected samples from moose calves hunted during legal hunting season from October 14th to December 3rd 2023 in the study and control areas. The calves to be studied were taken in the order of capture during regular moose hunting. We received complete samples from 64 moose calves (32 females and 32 males): 45 from the study area and 19 from the control area. The number of samples from the different MMAs was 20 for VS, 7 for UU, 18 for EH, 10 for ES and 9 for SP. Hunters reported the weighed carcass mass of the calf. Carcass weight is the weight of a slaughtered moose after the head, feet, internal organs, and skin have been removed. Also, they reported the sex of the calf, location and date of hunting, and collected internal organs (heart, lung, trachea and liver) and faeces (piece of rectum with droppings).

As samples were collected over an approximately 8-week period, harvest date could potentially influence calf weights due to continued growth during the hunting season. Therefore, we evaluated the relationship between harvest date and carcass mass using all calf carcass measurements recorded in the national hunting database (Oma riista) across the five MMAs in that specific time window (all together 814 calf carcass weight measurements). Linear models including calf sex and MMA as explanatory variables were fitted, and both linear and non-linear effects of sampling date, with and without interactions with MMA, were evaluated. As sampling date was not associated with carcass mass during the sampling period in any model (for all models p-value > 0.2), it was not included in further analyses. No statistically significant differences in calf sex distribution were observed among MMAs.

In addition to the systematic sampling, we received samples from four calves that were exceptionally small and thin. All four originated from the study area, two of them from VS and two from UU. They were hunted between October 15th – 28th. As these samples did not come from the systematic sampling program, they are not included in the statistical analyses, but the findings are described. All samples were sent either fresh or frozen to the laboratory of the Finnish Food Authority for further investigation.

Pathological and parasitological procedures

The heart was weighed in order to get a measurement relating to body size from all sampled calves. The heart was inspected along with the heart sack visually for any pathological lesions or parasites. Heart muscle was sliced to detect intramuscular parasitic cysts. Samples of heart muscle (epicardial surface) were taken for histology. In short, tissues were fixed in 10% formalin solution, embedded in paraffin and sectioned in 4 μm slices which were stained with haematoxylin and eosin. When signs of pericardial or epicardial inflammation (pericarditis) were observed the grade of the inflammation was estimated using scale of 1-5 where grades 1-2 mean only microscopical inflammatory lesions and grades 3-5 more severe cases with visible, macroscopic lesions (fibrous scarring of increasing extent and severity), grade 5 meaning a heart completely surrounded by thick fibrous and fibrinous inflammatory tissue.

The lung was inspected visually for signs of inflammation in the pleura (pleuritis) or parasitic nodules or cysts. Bronchi were flushed with tap water to extract large lung worms (Dictyocaulus sp.). Main bronchi were opened with scissors and inspected for remaining lung worms. Lung worms were counted and stored in 70% ethanol. Lung lobes were dissected in 1-2 cm slices to see parasitic cysts (cysticerci of Taenia sp., hydatid cysts of Echinococcus canadensis) or nodules (Varestrongylus alces) inside the lung tissue.

The liver was inspected visually for signs of inflammation (peritonitis) on the surface. The liver was sliced in ca. 1 cm slices to see parasitic cysts and biliary parasites and inflammatory changes (cholangitis). Liver flukes obtained from bile ducts were identified by morphology. Liver flukes were not counted, but presence or absence was recorded. Cysticerci were identified by morphology. Typical cysticercus from the liver of one calf was collected and stored in 70% ethanol for species confirmation by molecular methods.

Faeces were examined by quantitative flotation method for parasite eggs. A 3-g sample of faeces was mixed with tap water and then sieved. The sample was centrifuged (300 G) for 3 min. Supernatant was removed and the sediment was mixed with saturated sugar solution (specific gravity 1.25). Eggs in the mixture were counted with McMaster counting chamber. Parasite eggs or oocysts were identified morphologically on genus or order level. Limit of detection of the egg flotation method is 20 eggs per gram (epg).

Faeces were further examined by Baermann method for larvae of meningeal worms (Elaphostrongylus alces). A sample of faeces (25 g) was mixed with water in a funnel lined with gauze and fitted with a closed tube in the bottom. The sample was left to incubate overnight, and then 1-2 ml of the liquid was examined for larvae. Samples were checked by light microscopy (2.5x or 10× objective lenses). Larvae were fixed in 70% ethanol for species identification.

Molecular identification of parasites

The species of Dictyocaulus sp. (1 worm/infected calf), cysticercus from one calf and the larvae from faecal samples were identified by PCR.

DNA was isolated from the parasites using Qiagen's QiaAmp mini kit according to the manufacturer's instructions. The primers used in the PCR method for Dictyocaulus sp. and meningeal worms amplify the ITS2 region (NC1F: 5′-ACGTCTGGTTCAGGGTTGTT-3′ and NC2R: 5′-TTAGTTTCTTTTCCTCCGCT-3′) (Gasser et al., 1993; Höglund et al., 1999). The primers used for the cysticerci amplify COI region (JB3: 5′-TTTTTTGGGCATCCTGAGGTTTAT-3′ and JB4.5: 5′-TAAAGAAAGAACATAATGAAAATG-3′) (Bowles et al., 1995).

The PCR conditions were as follows: a final volume of 25 μl contained 5 μl of sample DNA, 10 mM Tris–HCl, (pH 8.8), 50 mM KCl, 1.5 mM MgCl2, 0.1% Triton®X-100, 0 0.2 mM dNTP, 0.4 μM primers and 1 U DyNAzyme II DNA polymerase (Thermo scientific). The PCR program started with an initial denaturation of 3 min at 94°C, the cycle (94°C, 50s, 58°C, 1 min and 72°C, 90s) was repeated 39 times and the final extension was done at 72°C for 10 min. For JB primers, the annealing temperature was 50°C in COI amplification PCR. The finished PCR products, approximately 300-550 bases in length, were separated and visualized by 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis. PCR products were excised from the gel and purified by E.Z.N.A. With Gel Extraction kit. PCR products were sequenced at the Biocenter Oulu Sequencing Center (University of Oulu, Finland). The results were analyzed by MEGA11 software (https://megasoftware.net) and compared with GenBank sequencing database.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses contained four sections. First, we examined area-level differences by comparing the prevalences of the different parasites and pathologies, parasite and disease burden, carcass weight and heart weight among the MMAs, with particular focus on differences between the study and control areas (Section 2.4.1). Second, we investigated individual-level associations between parasite infections, pathologies and body condition by examining whether parasite and disease burden, and individual parasites and pathologies, were associated with carcass and heart weight (Section 2.4.2). Third, we investigated co-occurrence patterns among parasites and pathologies (Section 2.4.3). Finally, we explored whether variation in cervid densities among MMAs was associated with differences in parasite and disease burden, carcass weight and heart weight (Section 2.4.4).

All computations were performed with R-software version 4.5.1 (R Core Team, 2025) utilizing following packages: stats (R Core Team, 2025), glmmTMB (Brooks et al., 2017), logistf (Heinze et al., 2025), emmeans (Lenth, 2025) cooccur (Griffith et al., 2016), lme4 (Bates et al., 2015), lmerTest (Kuznetsova et al., 2017), ggplot2 (Wickham, 2016), gt and gtsummary (Sjoberg et al., 2021; Iannone et al., 2025).

Prior to statistical analyses, response variables were checked for extreme values and distributions. To limit the influence of a single extreme observation, the number of Moniezia eggs per gram was truncated at 1000 eggs g−1 prior to all analyses in which this variable was used.

Effect of area on the occurrence of parasites and pathologies and weight parameters

The primary objective was to evaluate whether calves from the study area differed from those from the control area with respect to weight measures and parasite and pathology prevalences. However, because a preliminary inspection of the data revealed considerable heterogeneity among the MMAs of the study area (Table 1), we decided to model the effect of area at the MMA level rather than as a simple study-versus-control factor. Accordingly, separate generalized linear models (GLMs) were fitted for each response variable with MMA included as a fixed explanatory variable. Then the primary study question was addressed through contrasts examining differences between the pooled study and control areas. To gain more thorough insight about area-wise variation, post hoc contrasts were conducted also to compare individual study-area MMAs with the control area and with one another.

For some dichotomous response variables there were MMAs where all calves either had the parasite or infection or not. Such a separation would lead to infinite or undefined maximum likelihood estimates in standard logistic regression. Thus, we fitted binary logistic regression models using Firth's penalized likelihood method (Firth, 1993; Heinze and Schemper, 2002) using the logistf package in R (Heinze et al., 2025), which applies a bias-reducing penalization to the likelihood function.

For each response variable, we first tested the overall effect of MMA. To account for multiple testing across response variables, p-values for the overall MMA effects were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure (Benjamini and Hochberg, 1995). Contrasts were conducted only for response variables that showed a statistically significant FDR-adjusted MMA effect (p-value < 0.05). The two control MMAs (ES and SP) were pooled as one control area since no statistically significant differences with the response variables were observed between them (results not shown).

Relationships between parasites and pathologies and weight parameters

To identify factors connected with carcass and heart weight on individual scale, we first evaluated the effect of total parasite and disease burden on the heart and carcass weights using a linear model. The models included group (study area vs. control area), sex, and the total number of parasites and diseases per calf as fixed explanatory variables. Because the objective of these models was to evaluate individual-level relationships between parasite burden, pathologies and weight parameters rather than differences among MMAs, MMA was not treated as a fixed explanatory factor. Instead, it was initially considered as a random effect to account for potential variation among MMAs within the study and control groups. The contribution of the random effect was evaluated using likelihood ratio tests, and it was retained only when its inclusion significantly improved model fit (α = 0.05).

Next, we explored the individual contributions of specific parasites and pathologies to both weight outcomes. Separate models were fitted for each parasite or pathology, with group and sex included as fixed effects. MMA was again considered as a random effect and retained only when supported by likelihood ratio tests (α = 0.05).

Predictor performance was compared based on AIC and coefficients of determination (R2). To enable AIC-based model comparison, all heart and carcass weight models were fitted using maximum likelihood estimation.

Co-occurrence of pathologies and parasites

We studied the associations between pathologies and parasites using co-occurrence analysis (Griffith et al., 2016) where the observed number of co-occurrences is compared to the expected number of co-occurrences under the null hypothesis of random co-occurrences.

Cervid density, parasite and disease burden and weights

We briefly explored the possible connection of wild cervid densities with the MMA level variation in carcass and heart weights and parasite and disease burden. We evaluated the density of moose, white-tailed deer, roe deer and fallow deer and their combined density in the five MMAs. For all species, we used the average density over the years 2019–2023. The densities of moose were obtained using the procedure of moose population assessment of Natural Resources Institute Finland (Luke) (Pusenius et al., 2024). The densities of the other cervids are estimated by moose hunters based on observations in their hunting area during the hunting season. Estimates are recorded annually in the national hunting database (Oma riista).

As the cervid density values were available only at the MMA level (five areas), the analysis should be considered as exploratory. To avoid pseudo-replication, calf-level observations were also aggregated to MMA-level means and we settled on fitting separately a linear model for each three response variables (average carcass and heart weights, and parasite and pathology burden) using the moose and combined cervid densities as predictors, and presenting the results graphically. The densities of each cervid species in each MMA are shown as individuals/10 km2.

Results

Parasite species and pathological findings

Three lungworm species were found: Dictyocaulus capreolus (6 cases), Dictyocaulus skrjabini (5 cases) and Varestrongylus alces (5 cases). In three cases of Dictyocaulus infection, the species was not identified. Mixed infections were not observed. The two Dictyocaulus species were identified after comparing our data to the sequences of ITS2 region in the GenBank. The samples showed high identity (99.79%) with D. capreolus sequences (e.g. KM374679) or 100% match with D. skrjabini sequences (e.g. MN472750). The number of worms was low in D. skrjabini infections (1 - 2 worms/individual), while D. capreolus could cause more intense infestations (1 - 32 worms/individual). The species distribution seemed separated: D. capreolus occurred in the coastal and archipelago area (VS), but D. skrjabini was only found in inland (UU and EH). Nodules of V. alces were only rarely found in both the study and control samples (proportion of positive samples in study area 7%, control area 5%) and they are not included in statistical analyses.

In the liver samples, the lancet liver fluke Dicrocoelium dendriticum was found in two MMAs of the study area, VS and UU (Table 1), with prevalences 40% and 43%, respectively. The infected calves also had mild biliary tract inflammation (cholangitis) typical of parasite infection. Some calves (20 individuals in the study area, five in the control area) had similar mild changes in bile ducts indicating cholangitis but without observation of mature liver flukes.

Partial sequencing of CO1 region of the cysticercus found in liver showed 100% similarity with Taenia lynciscapreoli samples in the GenBank. Cysticerci of T. lynciscapreoli were found mostly in liver samples but occasionally also in lungs. In the calves originating from the study area, a single cysticercus was sometimes found only in the lungs (7% of the study area lungs positive). Infection intensity of T. lynciscapreoli in liver was low (1-7 cysts per calf) in all cases.

In the faecal samples, moderate amounts (median 250 epg) of ruminant tapeworm (Moniezia sp.) eggs and nematode eggs (median 60 epg) were found. Among the nematode eggs, intestinal nematode parasites Nematodirus sp. and Trichuris sp. were identified, while other findings were identified at the level of order Strongylida which includes several nematode parasite species of the digestive tract. In addition, oocysts of the protozoan intestinal parasites of the genus Eimeria (median 100 oocysts per gram) and larvae of the meningeal worm Elaphostrongylus alces (no larval counts performed) were found.

No parasites were found in the hearts.

Inflammatory lesions were observed in the heart and heart sack (pericardium), lungs and liver in both study and control areas (Table 1). Pericarditis in the heart was seen in chronic form where mild pericardial proliferation and lymphoplasmacytic infiltration in the pericardium and occasionally the underlying muscle (myocardium) were typical microscopical findings. Grossly, focal thickenings (ca. 0.5-2 mm) of the epicardium or pericardial sack of varying breadth were observed. The more serious cases (grades 4-5), where both gross reaction and microscopic findings were marked, were only observed in samples of the study area. Findings suggesting active bacterial or parasitic infection were not observed. Cases of pleuritis on the lung and peritonitis on the liver were mild with focal pleural or peritoneal (diaphragmatic surface of the liver) scarring in the form of thin fibrous proliferation. Cholangitis in the liver presented as mild thickening of bile duct walls, sometimes with yellowish exudate.

Comparison of the study and the control area and the study MMAs

Calves from the study area had on average, lower carcass and heart weights and a greater total number of parasite taxa and total number of parasites and pathologies (Table 3 and Fig. 3, Fig. 4, Fig. 5). Out of the 10 parasite or pathology parameters that showed statistically significant differences among all MMAs (Table 2), six were more prevalent or severe in the study area compared to the control area, while none showed the opposite pattern (Table 3, Fig. 3, Fig. 4, Fig. 5). As for parasite diversity, large lungworms of the genus Dictyocaulus and liver flukes D. dendriticum were found only in the study area (Table 1).

There were also differences between the MMAs within the study area. The MMA situated in the archipelago, VS, differed most from the control, as eight out of 10 parameters differed statistically significantly from the control area, while in MMAs UU and EH the corresponding numbers were 6 and 2 (Table 3 and Fig. 3, Fig. 4, Fig. 5). Between VS and EH, there were eight differing parameters pointing to heavier parasite and disease burden in VS. In UU, there were three parameters that showed heavier parasite or disease burden than in EH. However, Taenia infection seemed to be more common in EH than in other study MMAs.

Parasite and disease burden and weights

After accounting for parasite and disease burden and sex, calves from the study area remained on average 11 kg lighter than calves from the control area. (Table 4, Fig. 6). In addition, after accounting for group and parasite and disease burden, females were on average 5.6 kg lighter than males (Table 4). The inclusion of MMA as a random effect did not significantly improve model fit and was therefore not retained in the final model (likelihood ratio test, p = 1).

When examining the effects of individual parasites and pathologies, the strongest associations with carcass weight, based on model AIC, were observed for the occurrence of D. dendriticum and Taenia cysticerci, number of pathologies, number of parasite taxa, and pericarditis (Supplement Table S2). The presence of Taenia cysticerci was associated with increased carcass weight, whereas the other variables were associated with decreased carcass weight (Supplement Table S2).

As for heart weight, D. dendriticum, Eimeria sp. and pericarditis grade emerged as the strongest predictors based on model AIC (Supplement Table S3). Additionally, the number of pathologies, presence of Taenia cysticerci, and occurrence of pericarditis were all statistically significant predictors at the 0.05 significance level. As with carcass weight, the presence of Taenia cysticerci was associated with increased heart weight, while the other variables were associated with decreased heart weight (Supplement Table S3).

Carcass and heart weights were positively correlated (Pearson's r = 0.65, 95% CI: 0.46–0.78), indicating a moderate association between the two outcome measures.

Co-occurrence of pathologies and parasites

The occurrence of many parasitic infections and pathologies seemed to be significantly connected at the level of individuals (Fig. 8). The inflammatory conditions were connected with each other in many ways. For example, the occurrence of pericarditis and pleuritis was connected (p = 0.002) as 73% of calves with pleuritis also had pericarditis. In fact, all observed pathologies were positively connected with two other pathologies. Pericarditis was positively connected with Dictyocaulus sp. infection (p = 0.004). Not surprisingly, the occurrence of the liver fluke D. dendriticum was connected with cholangitis (p < 0.001). However, the occurrence of Taenia cysts was negatively connected with both pleuritis (p = 0.016) and peritonitis (p = 0.037). The only significant connection between the occurrence of parasites was a positive one with Eimeria sp. and D. dendriticum (p = 0.025).

Cervid density, parasite and disease burden and weights

Additional samples – exceptionally small calves

The average carcass weight of the four small calves was 42 kg and heart weight 770 g. The additional samples did not reveal any new diseases or parasites, but their average values differed somewhat from those of the actual study area material. For example, the number of parasite taxa and pathologies per individual was higher (6.5; study area 5.09) as well as the prevalences of Dictyocaulus sp. (75%; study area 31%), pericarditis (100%; study area 51%), cholangitis (100%; study area 69%) and peritonitis (75%; study area 36%). One of the calves had very severe pericarditis, the only case receiving grade 5. In this case, the entire epicardium was severely affected, thickened (2-4 mm) with soft, jelly-like fibrotic mass. Microscopically, the epicardium was thick with loose, fibrous tissue heavily infiltrated by lympho- and plasmacytes. Lung worms were identified as D. capreolus and the intensities of infection in the three infected calves were 5, 31 and 100 worms. On the other hand, only one calf had Taenia cysticerci and Eimeria sp. was not found in the four calves.

Discussion

We found a variety of parasites and inflammatory lesions in the moose calf samples in both study and control areas, but on average, a calf from the study area, where weights were low, had a larger number of parasites and pathologies than a calf from the control area. Lung worms (Dictyocaulus sp.) and liver flukes (D. dendriticum) were only found in the study area, and T. lynciscapreoli cysticerci were more common there. Inflammatory lesions on the membranes of major organs may also have been the result of parasitic infection. The significance of the association between parasite and disease burden and calf weight is further emphasized by the findings from the separate set of especially small calves from the study area that had multiple infections.

Lungworms of the genus Dictyocaulus were only found in the study area. In fact, we report here two Dictyocaulus species previously found in European cervids but not reported in Finland until now, namely D. capreoli and D. skrjabini. The first species has been described from roe deer and moose (Gibbons and Höglund, 2002), but the latter has originally been described from fallow deer and red deer (Pyziel et al., 2023). The number of Dictyocaulus findings was relatively low to make reliable inferences about the distribution of these species, but our data suggests that the two species may occur in separate areas, D. capreoli in VS and D. skrjabini in UU. The study area has a strong roe deer population, but the red deer does not exist in the wild in Finland. The fallow deer now forms a sparse but growing population originating from released captive deer in the study area. The findings of D. skrjabini in moose originated from UU, the core area of the fallow deer population. The occurrence of the two lungworm species in moose calves highlights the importance of the host species community for the spread of parasites.

Parasitic bronchitis and pneumonia caused by Dictyocaulus spp. are well known diseases in cattle and sheep, especially in calves and lambs (Taylor et al., 2007). There is less information about the clinical impacts in wild ruminants, but pathological lesions caused by D. cervi have been observed in the red deer (Pyziel et al., 2018). Roe deer infected with D. capreoli showed lower body weight than uninfected, and infection was more common in juvenile roe deer (Wild et al., 2024). The national wildlife disease surveillance has identified one case of fatal D. capreolus infection in a moose calf in Finland in the 2020's (The Finnish Food Authority database, unpubl.). These lungworms may be the most pathogenic of the parasites found in our study.

The other two parasite species that were more common in the study area seem to be less pathogenic for moose. The pathology caused by the liver fluke D. dendriticum is limited in the bile ducts but in some cases severe chronic infections can lead to cirrhosis (Taylor et al., 2007). In our material, cirrhotic findings or severe cholangitis were not observed, presumably due to low intensity of infections. The prevalence of T. lynciscapreoli cysticerci was four times higher in the study area. This cestode was identified fairly recently in Finland, and named after its typical definitive host, the lynx Lynx lynx and intermediate host, the roe deer (Haukisalmi et al., 2016). Since then, the species has been reported in Poland in roe deer (Myczka et al., 2020) and in Sweden in reindeer (Kautto et al., 2022). The combination of strong lynx and roe deer populations most probably supports the high prevalence of T. lynciscapreoli in the study area, exposing moose as well.

Irrespective of the area of origin, the heart and carcass weights were negatively correlated with parasite and disease burden as was revealed by the regression analysis. Same phenomenon has been found in the fallow deer and the red deer, as instead of any single parasite taxon, the sum of endoparasite taxa and body condition score were strongly negatively related (van Beest et al., 2023). Multiple parasite infections may be driven by one infection facilitating other infections which potentially causes cumulative costs for the host (Vaumourin et al., 2015). In our study, particularly the occurrences of inflammatory conditions were related to each other suggesting common etiology or one condition leading to another.

However, a connection between only a certain taxon of parasites and body condition index has been observed in cervids in some studies. For example, in adult moose, the prevalence of protostrongylid parasites was higher in individuals of low carcass weight (Brealey et al., 2025), and in red deer, body condition was negatively associated with infection intensity of abomasal nematodes of Ostertagiinae subfamily (Irvine et al., 2006). We found a negative connection with heart weight and Eimeria sp. infection. Coccidians of the genus Eimeria infect intestinal cells and are ubiquitous in nature although they primarily cause disease at high population densities (Samuel et al., 2001). Infection by Eimeria sp. induces immunity but juvenile animals without acquired immunity are susceptible to infection and clinical disease. The amounts of Eimeria oocysts in faecal samples of this study were low, median being 100 oocysts per gram (opg) while in clinical cases of coccidiosis in ruminants, faecal oocyst counts typically exceed 10,000 or even 100,000 opg. From a single faecal sample it is not possible to assess the stage of the infections, which – along with clinical symptoms - could have been beginning or ending. Interestingly, Davidson et al. (2015) found that moose with low body weights and poor condition had higher Eimeria sp. oocyst counts. Further studies on the effects of Eimeria on moose health would be warranted.

Pericarditis seemed to be the most significant individual pathological condition negatively related to the body size indices of calves. The etiology of the moose calf pericarditis could not be confirmed. The inflammation was nonsuppurative, and no traces of parasites were found in gross or histological examination. Pericarditis was connected to the occurrence of pleuritis and Dictyocaulus infection suggesting the involvement of lungworms in the inflammations. Another possible cause may be an infection with nematode parasites of the genus Setaria which live in the abdominal cavity of various mammals and are transmitted by haematophagous insects (Anderson, 2000). The species infecting cervids, Setaria tundra, has been found in Finnish semi-domesticated reindeer Rangifer tarandus, moose and roe deer. A survey found high infection prevalence in roe deer (33-50% depending on location) compared to moose (1.4-1.8%) (Laaksonen et al., 2008). Generally, S. tundra worms cause only mild peritoneal reaction in moose, typically seen on the surface of the liver, but it has caused severe outbreaks of peritonitis in reindeer calves (Laaksonen and Oksanen, 2009). In reindeer, we occasionally see the inflammation spread from abdominal cavity to thoracic cavity leading to pericarditis and pleuritis (Finnish Food Authority database). Notably, pericarditis, pleuritis and peritonitis were most prevalent in one of the study MMAs, the VS, which has the strongest roe deer population of the five MMAs.

It is worth noting that the group (study vs. control) alone, when controlling the effect of parasites, had a significant connection with the calf carcass weight, suggesting additional causes behind the recent decline of average calf weight in the study area. These may include climatic conditions (Pérez-Barbería et al., 2020), food quality (Holmes et al., 2023), demography (Sæther et al., 2003) as well as diseases and parasites not covered in our study.

There were some differences in the natural conditions between the study and control areas that may have relevance to moose condition. For example, the occurrence of scots pine seedling and sapling stands, considered the most used winter foraging sites by moose (Edenius et al., 2015), and cover of bilberry, an important summer and autumn forage of moose (Hjeljord et al., 1990), were both slightly higher in the study area with low calf weights. In addition, the overall productivity of environment was higher in the study area. Lower winter temperatures in the control area suggest somewhat lower thermoregulatory costs for the cold adapted moose (Renecker and Schwartz,1998) but thicker snow cover may cause energetic costs for moving moose there. The temperature and precipitation in the early part of growing season are thought to be important for moose as the palatability and nutritional value of plant tissue might be retained longer in relatively cold and humid conditions (Holmes et al., 2023), such as those in the control area. All in all, environmental conditions do not seem to offer a comprehensive explanation for the regional differences in moose calf weights.

However, the study and control areas differed notably with respect to cervid density. This was true for moose density and total cervid density (Fig. 9). The cervid densities were especially high in UU and VS, whereas the densities in EH did not differ very much from those of the control areas. Fallow deer only occurred in the study area. Earlier study comparing moose calf weights in Finland and Norway in 1991-2006 also found that calves weighed less in Norway where moose density was considerably higher (Tiilikainen et al., 2012). High population density can mean increased exposure to parasites and thus higher prevalences of parasite infections, especially with directly transmitted parasites (Arneberg, 2001; Hasik et al., 2025). For the moose, the density of the whole cervid population is significant for the spread of parasites because of many shared parasite species (e.g. Świsłocka et al., 2021). In addition, feeding stations commonly available for deer especially in the study area help maintain the high population density and may facilitate the transmission of parasites (e.g. Milner et al., 2014). The high joint density of cervids may decrease the per capita availability of high quality natural food like bilberry, that is preferred by all cervid species. Scarcity of high quality food could have negative effects on body condition and disease resistance. When comparing the five MMAs of our study, we found a tendency towards positive connection with parasite and disease burden (the sum of parasite taxa and pathologies per individual) and both moose density and combined cervid density. A similar finding was made in female roe deer in which the prevalence of gastrointestinal parasites was positively related with roe deer density (Body et al., 2011). Additionally, the weight parameters were lowest in the two MMAs of high cervid density.

In contrast to the negative effects, there was a positive correlation between the occurrence of Taenia cysticerci and both the carcass and heart weight. Additionally, pleuritis and peritonitis were less common in calves infected by Taenia. These findings would suggest that Taenia infection is more typical of calves in good health. In our material, the infections were mild with only 1-7 cysts per individual. The cysticerci T. lynciscapreoli are small (diameter 8-10 mm) and cause only minor damage in target organs (lung or liver). Parasites are generally expected to have negative influence on their host but in reality, their effects on host body condition can vary and positive effects can be found due to e.g. higher tolerance in individuals in good condition (Sánchez et al., 2018). In our case, the mild Taenia infections did not seem to cause notable harm in the liver. The amount of cysticerci may have simply been an indication of the level of food intake and consequently exposure to parasite eggs (e.g. Sánchez et al., 2018).

The negative relationships we found between certain indices of disease and parasite and moose calf highlight the importance of parasites and diseases on moose health. The combined burden of parasites and inflammatory lesions seemed to be more important than any single pathogen or lesion. Still, the findings should be interpreted with some caution. An observational study cannot reveal actual causal relationships. Parasite infection may decrease body condition but weak condition due to e.g. poor food quality may also lead to more intense infections which further deteriorate the condition – a vicious circle where it is virtually impossible to discern the main cause for poor condition (Beldomenico and Begon, 2010). This phenomenon could also act on population level. Population density can also be an important factor affecting calf weights by limiting resources and favoring the spread of cervid parasites. Parasites and diseases do seem to play a role in the problem of declining calf weights but to get a more complete picture, more knowledge is needed on the effect of other factors such as the characteristics of moose and other cervid populations as well as climatic and nutritional factors.
