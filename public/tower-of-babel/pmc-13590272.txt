ROS-Scavenging Hyperbranched Polymer Dot Nanozymes Combined with Fractional CO2 Laser Microchannels for Hair Follicle Regeneration
Authors: Yi-Hsuan Su, Yen-Jen Wang, Wen-Ping Jiang, Jing-Chun Yang, Audry-Yun-Xuan Chan, Yu-Hsuan Lee, Ching-Chang Cheng, Chang-Cheng Chang, Tzong-Yuan Juang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTopical nanomaterials for hair regeneration are limited by poor follicular penetration, while the regenerative window opened by fractional laser injury is transient and accompanied by rep air-constraining oxidative stress. We investigated whether fractional CO2 laser–generated microchannels could unlock the nanozyme activity of hyperbranched polymer dots (HPD) and accelerate hair follicle regeneration.Patients and MethodsHPD (3–5 nm), synthesized by A2+B3 condensation polymerization and displaying concentration-dependent scavenging of hydroxyl radicals, hydrogen peroxide, and superoxide anions, were tested in telogen-synchronized female C57BL/6 mice. Laser energy and spot density were optimized for microchannel formation and tissue tolerability. Five groups (n = 6) received vehicle, 5% minoxidil, HPD, laser alone, or laser plus HPD; laser was applied once on Day 1 and topical treatment daily through Day 15. Outcomes were hair coverage, skin pigmentation, optical coherence tomography (OCT), hematoxylin–eosin histomorphometry, and Ki67 and β-catenin immunofluorescence at Days 8, 11, and 15.ResultsOptimized irradiation (30 mJ, 196 spots cm−2, 6.5% coverage) produced reproducible microchannels reaching ~191 μm into the perifollicular dermis without ulceration. Combined treatment outperformed all comparators, increasing hair coverage 16.58-fold versus control by Day 10 (p = 0.0313), follicle length 2.96-fold by Day 11 (p = 0.0001), Ki67⁺ cells 30.49-fold (p < 0.0001), and β-catenin 9.32-fold (p < 0.0001), both still elevated at Day 15. OCT showed more complete microchannel closure with HPD than laser alone. Follicle number per cross-sectional field was highest after combined treatment but did not differ significantly from control (1.91-fold, p = 0.1016).ConclusionLaser microchannels act as both delivery portals and regenerative triggers, while HPD scavenges post-injury reactive oxygen species and sustains Wnt/β-catenin activation, producing a greater-than-additive acceleration of hair follicle regeneration exceeding minoxidil. This reflects activation and accelerated growth of existing follicles rather than de novo neogenesis, and offers a drug-free platform for regenerative dermatology.Keywords: hyperbranched polymer dots, nanozyme, reactive oxygen species, fractional CO2 laser, hair follicle regeneration, Wnt/β-catenin signaling

Introduction

Hair loss represents one of the most prevalent dermatological concerns worldwide and exerts a substantial psychosocial burden that extends beyond its physical manifestations.1 Conditions such as androgenetic alopecia and telogen effluvium affect hundreds of millions of individuals across age groups and sexes, frequently impairing self-esteem and quality of life.1–3 Despite this prevalence, therapeutic options remain limited. The approved pharmacological mainstays, topical minoxidil and oral finasteride, provide only modest and variable efficacy, require long-term or lifelong administration, and are frequently undermined by poor adherence.4–6 Finasteride carries the additional concern of systemic adverse effects, whereas minoxidil is constrained by inconsistent follicular responsiveness and rapid reversal of benefit upon discontinuation.5–8 These limitations underscore a persistent unmet need for strategies that can more effectively and durably reactivate hair follicle regeneration without systemic risk or dependence on continuous drug exposure.1,4

At a biological level, hair follicles are not static appendages but dynamic regenerative units that cycle through phases of growth, regression, and rest—anagen, catagen, and telogen, respectively.2,9 Lifelong regenerative capacity is sustained by hair follicle stem cells residing in specialized niches, particularly the bulge region.10,11 Activation of these stem cells and their subsequent differentiation drive the telogen-to-anagen transition and reconstitution of the hair shaft.11,12 Among the pathways orchestrating this process, Wnt/β-catenin signaling functions as a master regulator: canonical Wnt activation stabilizes β-catenin, promotes its nuclear translocation, and initiates the transcriptional programs required for stem cell proliferation and lineage commitment, whereas pathway suppression is closely associated with follicular quiescence and telogen maintenance.13–15 Crucially, the follicular microenvironment—including immune signaling and redox balance—exerts profound influence over Wnt/β-catenin activity. Excessive oxidative stress arising from aging, inflammation, or environmental insult disrupts signaling fidelity, impairs stem cell function, and contributes to delayed or incomplete regeneration.16–18 From this perspective, telogen arrest is best understood not merely as hair loss but as a failure of microenvironmental reactivation that prevents stem cells from re-entering a regenerative program.12,19

Efforts to overcome these constraints have sought either to stimulate follicular activity pharmacologically or to modulate local signaling directly. Minoxidil promotes hair growth largely through vasodilation and indirect activation of growth-promoting pathways, yet it lacks spatial and temporal precision and does not address the oxidative and inflammatory dysregulation of the follicular niche.5,20,21 Energy-based modalities have therefore attracted growing interest. Fractional lasers, originally developed for dermatological resurfacing, generate ordered arrays of microscopic thermal zones that initiate wound-healing cascades while preserving the surrounding tissue.20,22,23 This principle underlies wound-induced hair regeneration, in which controlled cutaneous injury reactivates dormant follicles through coordinated immune responses, macrophage recruitment, and cytokine release that converge on Wnt/β-catenin and transforming growth factor signalling. De novo follicle formation has also been described in this setting, but it characteristically requires large full-thickness wounds that re-epithelialise across an open wound bed—a regenerative context distinct from the microscopic, fractionated injury used in the present study.19,22,23 Fractional CO2 lasers are especially well suited to harnessing these mechanisms: by generating precise microchannels that extend into the dermis, they simultaneously trigger a regenerative wound response and open transient conduits that bypass the stratum corneum, thereby enabling transdermal delivery of therapeutic agents.24 This dual capacity is nonetheless self-limiting. The regenerative window opened by laser injury is transient and narrow, and, absent sustained biochemical support, the oxidative and inflammatory milieu that accompanies tissue injury can itself constrain the eventual outcome, leaving laser monotherapy incomplete or short-lived.21,24

In parallel, nanomaterials have emerged as versatile tools for modulating biological processes at the cellular and molecular levels.25 Nanozymes—nanomaterials with intrinsic enzyme-like activity—are of particular interest because, unlike natural enzymes, they offer superior physicochemical stability, resistance to denaturation, and activity that can be tuned through rational design.25,26 Carbon-based nanomaterials such as carbon nanodots, graphene quantum dots, and polymer dots have been widely explored in biomedicine for their biocompatibility, optical properties, and redox activity, with demonstrated capacity to scavenge reactive oxygen species, temper inflammation, and influence signaling pathways central to tissue repair.27–29 The convergence of such nanomaterials with energy-based devices is already reshaping therapeutic and aesthetic dermatology, where laser- and microneedle-assisted platforms are increasingly used to drive nanoparticles and nanocarriers into the skin for rejuvenation, scar remodeling, and localized delivery.30–33 Yet effective interfacing of nanomaterials with skin and hair follicles remains difficult: limited penetration, uncontrolled distribution, and poor temporal coordination with regenerative signaling have all constrained their translational impact, particularly after topical application.34,35

Hyperbranched polymer dots constitute a distinctive subclass of polymer-based nanomaterials well matched to these challenges. Synthesized by an A2+B3 polymerization strategy, they possess a non-conjugated hyperbranched architecture enriched with terminal amino and hydroxyl groups, and their small hydrodynamic diameter (approximately 3–5 nm) confers excellent aqueous dispersibility and favorable interaction with biological tissue.36 High synthesis yield and reproducibility further support scalable biomedical use. Beyond these structural attributes, hyperbranched polymer dots display pronounced nanozyme-like behavior, robustly scavenging hydroxyl radicals, superoxide anions, and hydrogen peroxide—an antioxidant capacity directly relevant to hair follicle regeneration, in which oxidative stress inhibits stem cell activation and Wnt/β-catenin signaling.33,37,38

Building on these properties, we have previously shown that topically applied hyperbranched polymer dots actively promote hair follicle regeneration in vivo, accelerating anagen entry, enhancing follicular growth, and upregulating Wnt/β-catenin signaling in a concentration-dependent manner and without conventional pharmacological agents.33,36 Their high biocompatibility and absence of observable toxicity reinforce their suitability for cutaneous use.36 As with other nanomaterials, however, their full biological potential is limited by suboptimal penetration and insufficient coordination with endogenous regenerative cues.39,40 This exposes a central gap: the intrinsic bioactivity of hyperbranched polymer dots is ultimately governed by whether they can reach and modulate the follicular microenvironment at the appropriate time.35,39–41

Against this background, integrating fractional CO2 laser technology with hyperbranched polymer dots offers a compelling and previously unexplored strategy. Laser-generated microchannels can be viewed as transient, spatially defined regenerative interfaces that breach the skin barrier, deliver nanomaterial to the follicular niche, and initiate wound-healing cascades, while the polymer dots applied within this window mitigate laser-induced oxidative stress, stabilize the microenvironment, and sustain pro-regenerative signaling. Such coupling is intended to move beyond the additive effects of two independent modalities toward a genuinely synergistic interaction between a physical and a nanomaterial-based intervention. Realizing such a platform clinically will nonetheless require attention to the broader hurdles facing all nanomaterial-based therapeutics—consistent physicochemical characterization, reproducible and scalable manufacturing, well-defined biodistribution and clearance, and long-term safety—together with regulatory frameworks for combined nanomaterial–device products that remain incompletely harmonized.42 Situating the present work within these considerations is essential if a laboratory demonstration of efficacy is to inform a credible path toward therapeutic and cosmetic application.

On this basis, we hypothesized that fractional CO2 laser–generated microchannels can unlock and amplify the biological activity of hyperbranched polymer dots, producing a synergistic enhancement of hair follicle regeneration. We propose specifically that laser treatment facilitates the penetration and spatial localization of the polymer dots while triggering a controlled wound response, whereas the polymer dots act as nanozyme-like regulators that scavenge excess reactive oxygen species and reinforce Wnt/β-catenin–mediated stem cell activation. To test this hypothesis, we systematically optimised fractional CO2 laser parameters to achieve effective follicular stimulation without ulceration and evaluated the combined intervention against the corresponding monotherapies and topical minoxidil in a telogen-synchronised murine model. Treatment effects on the activation and structural maturation of pre-existing follicles were assessed using longitudinal follicular morphometry, transverse cross-sectional follicular profile counts, and Ki67 and β-catenin immunofluorescence. Collectively, this integrated approach seeks to establish a nano–bio–physical paradigm for accelerated hair follicle regeneration with direct relevance to nanomedicine and regenerative dermatology.

Material and Methods

Synthesis and Physicochemical Properties of Hyperbranched Polymer Dots

Hyperbranched polymer dots (HPD) were synthesised using the previously reported A2+B3 one-pot condensation polymerisation method.36 Ethylenediaminetetraacetic dianhydride (EDTAD; 0.5 g, 2 mmol) was dissolved in anhydrous N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF; 100 mL) under a nitrogen atmosphere. A separate solution containing Jeffamine T-403 (1.8 g, 4 mmol) in tetrahydrofuran (THF; 100 mL) was prepared and maintained at 0°C under nitrogen. The EDTAD solution was added dropwise to the Jeffamine T-403 solution, and the reaction mixture was stirred continuously for 20 h while gradually returning to room temperature. The resulting product was collected by filtration, washed to remove residual solvents and unreacted precursors, and vacuum-dried. Before topical administration, HPD was dispersed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), passed through a 0.2-μm hydrophilic polytetrafluoroethylene filter, and prepared at a concentration of 50 mg/mL.

The molecular architecture, particle size, morphology, aqueous dispersibility, fluorescence characteristics, biocompatibility, and radical-scavenging properties of this HPD formulation have been characterised in previous publications and were not re-measured as newly generated outcomes in the present study. The previously reported physicochemical and biological properties are summarised in eTable S1. Briefly, the particles exhibit a non-conjugated hyperbranched polyamide architecture, a hydrodynamic diameter of approximately 3–5 nm, abundant terminal amino and hydroxyl groups, and concentration-dependent scavenging of hydroxyl radicals, hydrogen peroxide, and superoxide anions.

Animals, Ethical Approval, and Telogen Synchronisation

Female C57BL/6 mice were obtained from the National Laboratory Animal Center and housed under controlled conditions at 20–24°C and 50–70% relative humidity, with 10–15 air changes per hour and a 12-h light–dark cycle. Food and water were provided ad libitum. At approximately seven weeks of age, dorsal hair follicles in C57BL/6 mice are predominantly in telogen, providing a reproducible model for assessing experimentally induced telogen-to-anagen transition.

All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines and the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. The protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of China Medical University, Taichung, Taiwan (approval number CMUIACUC-2024-274). Mice were anaesthetised with inhaled isoflurane during depilation, laser exposure, and other procedures requiring immobilisation. At the designated terminal time points, mice were euthanised by carbon dioxide inhalation followed by cervical dislocation in accordance with the American Veterinary Medical Association Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals.

One day before treatment, dorsal hair was shortened with electric clippers and removed using depilatory cream. The exposed skin was inspected to exclude visible irritation or injury before experimental treatment. Phase 1: Optimisation of Fractional CO2 Laser Parameters

A preliminary optimisation experiment was conducted to identify fractional CO2 laser settings that produced reproducible microchannels and promoted follicular activation without ulceration. Twelve six-week-old female C57BL/6 mice were assigned to four groups of three animals: an untreated depilated control group and three laser groups receiving pulse energies of 10, 20, or 30 mJ.

Within each laser-treated animal, the depilated dorsal treatment field was divided into three craniocaudally arranged subregions. These subregions were exposed to spot densities of 100, 196, or 324 spots/cm2, corresponding to fractional coverage levels of 3.3%, 6.5%, and 10.7%, respectively. This within-animal density design permitted comparison of energy–density combinations while limiting between-animal variability and unnecessary animal use. Laser irradiation was performed once on Day 1 using an ST-350 fractional CO2 laser system.

Microchannel formation was evaluated on Day 1 by non-invasive optical coherence tomography (OCT). For cross-group energy comparisons, the region treated at 196 spots/cm2 was used as the reference density. Standardised dorsal photographs were obtained from Day 1 through Day 15 to document skin pigmentation, visible tissue injury, and hair regrowth. The parameter combination of 30 mJ and 196 spots/cm2, corresponding to 6.5% fractional coverage, produced clearly detectable microchannels without ulceration and was therefore selected for the subsequent efficacy experiment. The optimisation design, representative OCT images, and longitudinal macroscopic observations are presented in Figure 2A and 2B.

The sham-control and laser-only groups received 150 μL PBS over a 3 × 5-cm dorsal treatment area. The minoxidil group received 150 μL of 50 mg/mL topical minoxidil, corresponding to 0.5 mg/cm2. The HPD and laser-plus-HPD groups received 150 μL of 50 mg/mL HPD in PBS, also corresponding to 0.5 mg/cm2. Fractional CO2 laser treatment was delivered once on Day 1 at 30 mJ and 196 spots/cm2. Topical treatment was initiated immediately after laser exposure in the designated laser-treated groups and was subsequently administered once daily through Day 15, as illustrated in Figure 1B.

Immediate post-laser application was intended to utilise the period of greatest microchannel patency. OCT demonstrated progressive rather than instantaneous microchannel closure during Days 1–5. Subsequent daily HPD administration was used to maintain local cutaneous exposure during tissue repair and could also permit interaction through follicular and other appendageal routes after restoration of the interfollicular barrier. Because HPD penetration and tissue distribution were not directly quantified, the study did not assume that laser-enhanced transport remained constant throughout the 15-day treatment period.

The study was designed to compare the efficacy of the combined intervention with that of each individual treatment and the control conditions. A formal pharmacological synergy index was not calculated. Accordingly, combined-treatment effects were interpreted as greater or more sustained responses than those observed with either monotherapy, rather than as formally demonstrated pharmacological synergy.

Macroscopic Imaging and Hair-Coverage Analysis

Standardized dorsal photographs were obtained throughout the study under consistent positioning, illumination, camera distance, and magnification. Hair coverage was quantified using ImageJ software. Skin pigmentation was evaluated separately using a CASIO DZ-D100 dermocamera at 5× magnification. In C57BL/6 mice, progressive darkening of telogen-phase pink skin reflects follicular melanogenesis associated with anagen entry. Pigmentation was graded using a standardised 0–6 scale ranging from pink skin to dark grey or black skin. The reference scale and longitudinal pigmentation assessment are presented in eFigure S1. The grading scale served only as a scoring reference; all photographs and quantitative pigmentation measurements in the present study were obtained from the current experimental animals.

Optical Coherence Tomography

Laser-induced microchannels and their subsequent repair were assessed non-invasively using a 3D SKIN VIEWER OCT system. Imaging was performed immediately after laser treatment and longitudinally from Day 1 through Day 5. Microchannel morphology and depth were evaluated from cross-sectional images acquired under standardised settings. The same dorsal treatment region was examined at each time point whenever technically feasible. OCT was used to characterise structural changes and temporal channel closure; it was not used as a direct measure of HPD penetration or tissue biodistribution.

Histology and Histomorphometric Analysis

Dorsal skin was collected at the designated Day 8, Day 11, and Day 15 terminal sampling points according to the schedule shown in Figure 1B. Tissue specimens were fixed in neutral-buffered formalin, processed for paraffin embedding, and sectioned at a thickness of 3 μm. Longitudinal sections were prepared at Days 8, 11, and 15 to evaluate follicular elongation and bulb development. Transverse sections were prepared on Days 8 and 11 to quantify cross-sectional hair follicle profiles within a standardised sampling area.

Sections were stained with haematoxylin and eosin and imaged using an EVOS M5000 microscope under consistent acquisition conditions. Hair-follicle length was measured from the base of the bulb to the epidermal opening, and bulb diameter was measured at the widest portion of the bulb. For longitudinal morphometry, three randomly selected, adequately oriented follicles were measured for each animal and averaged to obtain one animal-level value. For transverse analysis, three randomly selected, non-overlapping fields were evaluated from the designated transverse section for each animal on Days 8 and 11. The three fields together represented a total analysed area of 0.06 mm2. All follicular profiles intersecting the defined fields were counted, and the field-level counts were averaged to generate one animal-level value before statistical analysis. Measurements and counts were performed using ImageJ. Because the number of follicular profiles intersected by a transverse plane may vary with follicular elongation, depth, calibre, and synchronised anagen entry, this outcome was interpreted as a cross-sectional follicular profile count rather than direct evidence of de novo follicle formation.

Immunofluorescence Staining and Quantification

Immunofluorescence staining was performed to assess cellular proliferation and canonical Wnt/β-catenin pathway activity. Paraffin sections were deparaffinised, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval. For Ki67 staining, antigen retrieval was performed in Tris–EDTA buffer at pH 9.0 for 12 min. Sections were blocked for 30 min and incubated with rabbit anti-Ki67 primary antibody (ServiceBio, GB111499; 1:500) for 1 h at room temperature, followed by an Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-rabbit secondary antibody for 30 min. For β-catenin staining, antigen retrieval was performed in citrate buffer at pH 6.0 for 12 min. Sections were blocked for 30 min and incubated with mouse anti-β-catenin primary antibody (Invitrogen, 13–8400; 1:200) for 1 h at room temperature, followed by an Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-mouse secondary antibody for 30 min. Nuclei were counterstained with SYTOX Deep Red nucleic acid stain.

Images were acquired using an EVOS M5000 fluorescence microscope under identical exposure and acquisition settings within each staining experiment. Three randomly selected follicles or predefined follicular regions of interest were analysed from each animal, and their measurements were averaged to obtain one animal-level value. Integrated fluorescence density was quantified using ImageJ. Ki67 and β-catenin fluorescence values were normalised to the mean value of the corresponding Day 11 sham-control group and are reported in arbitrary units.

Sample-Size Rationale and Statistical Analysis

No formal a priori power calculation was performed before initiation of the experiments. The group size was selected on the basis of preceding studies using comparable telogen-synchronised C57BL/6 models, pilot observations indicating substantial between-group effects, and the reduction principle of the 3Rs. To avoid pseudoreplication, the individual mouse, rather than each follicle, microscopic field, or region of interest, was treated as the experimental unit. This applied to every outcome, including hair follicle counts in transverse sections, for which the three field-level counts obtained from each animal were averaged before analysis rather than treated as independent replicates. Quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9. At each prespecified time point, overall differences among the five treatment groups were evaluated using one-way analysis of variance. Comparisons of each intervention group with the sham-control group were performed using Dunnett’s multiple-comparison test. For the Day 11 cross-sectional follicular profile analysis, additional Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons were performed between the combined fractional CO2 laser plus HPD group and the minoxidil and HPD-alone groups. The individual mouse was treated as the biological experimental unit, and the three field-level counts obtained from each animal were averaged before statistical analysis. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P value <0.05. Exact fold changes, adjusted P values, and the statistical comparator used for each analysis are reported in eTable S2.

Results

Optimization of Fractional CO2 Laser Parameters

Fractional CO2 laser pulse energy and spot density were evaluated jointly by OCT imaging and macroscopic observation to characterize microchannel formation and hair-regrowth kinetics across all parameter combinations (Figure 2A and 2B). At the 196 spots cm−2 (6.5%) reference density, OCT imaging on Day 1 revealed an energy threshold for microchannel formation: 10 mJ produced no identifiable microchannels under any density condition, 20 mJ produced only partial microchannel formation, and 30 mJ produced reproducible, vertically oriented microchannels extending into the dermis with a mean depth of approximately 191.3 μm, placing the injury zone within the upper dermis and perifollicular region. Across the 15-day observation window, the 30 mJ/196 spots cm−2 (6.5%) combination produced the earliest shift in skin pigmentation, with dorsal skin transitioning from pink toward gray by Day 7 and near-complete hair coverage by Day 15 (Figure 2B); no ulceration, scabbing, or delayed epithelialization occurred at this setting. On the basis of these observations, 30 mJ at 196 spots cm−2 (6.5% coverage) was adopted for all subsequent Phase 2 efficacy studies.

Macroscopic Hair Coverage, Skin Pigmentation, and Microchannel Closure

Using the optimized laser parameters (Table 1), hair-regeneration outcomes were assessed across all five treatment groups. Representative dorsal images showed marked differences in the extent and timing of hair regrowth among groups (Figure 3A). Quantitative hair coverage (Table 2 and Figure 3B) rose earliest in the combined fractional CO2 laser plus HPD group, which reached approximately 10.9% coverage by Day 10—a 16.58-fold increase relative to control (p = 0.0313; eTable S2)—whereas HPD alone, laser alone, and minoxidil did not reach significance at this time point. Coverage in the combined group remained the highest at every subsequent time point, approaching 88% by Day 15, compared with approximately 78% for laser alone, 70% for HPD alone, and 65% for minoxidil (Table 2; complete fold-changes and exact p-values in eTable S2).

Discussion

The present study demonstrates that combining fractional CO2 laser microchannel engineering with the intrinsic nanozyme activity of hyperbranched polymer dots accelerates and amplifies hair follicle regeneration well beyond either intervention alone or FDA-approved topical minoxidil. The combined treatment advanced regeneration across every level of analysis examined—macroscopic hair coverage (16.58-fold over control by Day 10), follicular architecture (2.96-fold follicle elongation by Day 11), stem cell proliferation (30.49-fold Ki67 increase), and canonical Wnt pathway activation (9.32-fold β-catenin increase)—and did so earlier and more durably than the monotherapies. The consistency of this advantage across independent, mechanistically distinct endpoints indicates that the two modalities are not merely co-administered but functionally coupled, each supplying what the other lacks.

Mechanistically, the results support a coherent redox-to-phenotype cascade. Fractional CO2 laser irradiation initiates a controlled wound-healing response marked by transient inflammation and a localized burst of reactive oxygen species, signals that are themselves part of the regenerative program21,43 yet, when excessive or prolonged, impair hair follicle stem cell function and destabilize Wnt/β-catenin signaling.33,38,44,45 By acting as a ROS-scavenging nanozyme, HPD buffers this oxidative surge, preserving stem cell viability and extending the window during which Wnt/β-catenin signaling remains active.33,37 Three observations anchor this interpretation. First, longitudinal OCT showed more complete microchannel resolution in the combined group than with laser alone, indicating that HPD is not a passive cargo passing through laser-generated conduits but an active participant in post-injury tissue restitution. Second, the combined group sustained elevated Ki67 through Day 15, consistent with continued rather than transient proliferation. Third, β-catenin remained elevated at Day 15, reflecting a prolonged signaling window that neither monotherapy reproduced. The downstream histomorphometric gains in follicle length and bulb diameter follow naturally from this sustained proliferative and Wnt-active state.

The magnitude of the combined response also supports a greater-than-additive interaction. At multiple endpoints and time points, the combined effect exceeded the summed responses of laser alone and HPD alone, satisfying the operational definition of greater-than-additivity adopted here. This is mechanistically expected: laser irradiation supplies both the physical delivery portal and the regenerative trigger that topical HPD alone cannot access, whereas HPD supplies the sustained redox stabilization that laser alone cannot provide; only their combination furnishes both spatial access and microenvironmental control. We deliberately describe this as greater-than-additive rather than applying a fixed-dose combination index such as Chou–Talalay, which is designed for dose-titrated cytotoxicity data and is not appropriate to a single-dose, multi-arm in vivo design; a Bliss-independence comparison of the observed combined response against the expectation from the two monotherapies is consistent with the same conclusion.

A question raised by the treatment schedule is how daily HPD application continues to reach the follicle when laser irradiation is performed only on Day 1 and the microchannels close within a few days. The resolution is threefold and evidence-based. Immediately after irradiation the channels remain patent long enough for the ultrasmall HPD (approximately 3–5 nm) to diffuse into the perifollicular dermis, a kinetic window well documented for fractional laser-assisted delivery.30 As the channels close, continued daily application accesses the follicle through the transappendageal route: topically applied nanoparticles preferentially accumulate in the hair follicle orifice,46 which functions as a long-term reservoir that sustains delivery to the follicular niche over days.47 In addition, the still-remodeling wound bed remains more permeable than intact skin during the repair phase. The persistence of elevated Ki67 and β-catenin through Day 15 provides functional confirmation that follicular exposure to bioactive HPD is maintained across the regenerative window rather than confined to Day 1.

Interpretation of the transverse histological findings requires careful distinction between an increased number of follicular profiles within a section and de novo formation of anatomically new follicles. No significant between-group differences were detected on Day 8. By Day 11, however, the combined fractional CO2 laser plus HPD group exhibited significantly more cross-sectional follicular profiles than the minoxidil and HPD-alone groups. This time-dependent separation is consistent with more advanced and synchronised telogen-to-anagen progression in the combined-treatment group, particularly when considered together with the earlier pigmentation, greater follicular elongation, increased bulb diameter, and elevated Ki67 and β-catenin signals observed in the same regenerative interval. Nevertheless, the combined group did not differ significantly from the sham control in the primary control-referenced analysis, and the number of follicular profiles intersected by a transverse section may be influenced by follicular depth, calibre, orientation, elongation, and the proportion of follicles simultaneously entering anagen. The Day 11 pairwise differences therefore support enhanced activation and structural progression of pre-existing follicles relative to the two non-laser active treatments, but they do not independently establish de novo follicle neogenesis. Demonstration of genuine neogenesis would require spatial evidence that new follicles arose in previously non-follicular skin, together with longer follow-up, developmental or neogenesis-specific markers, and preferably lineage-tracing analysis.

These findings also clarify the relationship between the present study and our prior work. We have previously shown that topical HPD possesses intrinsic pro-regenerative, Wnt-activating bioactivity in the same murine model. The novelty of the current study lies not in re-demonstrating that bioactivity but in introducing the fractional CO2 laser microchannel platform as a distinct enabling element that overcomes the penetration limitation of topical HPD, adds a regenerative wound-priming trigger, and yields the greater-than-additive outcome that HPD monotherapy cannot achieve. All in vivo data reported here were generated in a new, independent animal cohort. Relative to conventional therapy, the combined strategy outperformed minoxidil across all evaluated endpoints while remaining entirely drug-free, harnessing endogenous repair rather than continuous pharmacological stimulation and its attendant systemic risks. Because fractional CO2 laser technology is already clinically mature and the coupling of nanomaterials with energy-based devices is expanding across therapeutic and aesthetic dermatology,48 the platform is modular and could, in principle, be adapted to other regenerative contexts such as wound healing and scar remodeling, where control of the oxidative microenvironment is likewise pivotal. Realizing this potential clinically will nonetheless require addressing the hurdles common to nanomaterial–device combination products, including reproducible and scalable manufacturing, thorough physicochemical characterization, defined biodistribution and clearance, long-term safety, and the incompletely harmonized regulatory frameworks that govern such products.42

Several limitations should be acknowledged, together with the steps taken to address them. First, the fifteen-day observation window is sufficient to capture the telogen-to-anagen transition in this model but not the durability of the response across subsequent, treatment-free hair cycles; to mitigate snapshot bias we pre-specified the assessment time points from pilot data and acquired a dense Day 8-to-Day 15 series rather than isolated endpoints, and future studies incorporating treatment withdrawal and extended monitoring will be needed to establish long-term durability. Second, the study cannot claim de novo neogenesis: the follicle-density difference did not reach significance, and transverse follicle counts are in any case confounded by the hair-cycle-dependent geometry of the follicle and by the depth of the section plane, so that they cannot separate new follicle formation from anagen-associated changes in follicular architecture. We have accordingly restricted all interpretive and concluding statements to accelerated regeneration, and lineage tracing or neogenesis-specific markers in wound-scale models are proposed for future work. Third, no formal a priori power analysis was performed; group size was set according to the 3Rs principle of Reduction and prior practice in this model, and the order-of-magnitude effect sizes observed indicate that the study was adequately powered for the primary endpoints, although smaller or more variable effects in future work would warrant formal sample-size estimation. Fourth, outcomes were compared at discrete time points by one-way ANOVA rather than by repeated-measures or mixed-effects modeling; this approach is appropriate and was endorsed in review, but longitudinal modeling of time-by-treatment interactions would add resolution in subsequent studies. Fifth, the immunofluorescence readouts for Ki67 and β-catenin are associative, and direct causal confirmation that HPD-mediated ROS scavenging sustains Wnt activation would require targeted pathway or redox perturbation experiments. Finally, the efficacy study used a single strain and sex (female C57BL/6) and a single HPD dose and laser setting; because murine follicular synchrony and follicle dimensions differ from those in humans—where the larger follicle diameter may in fact favor transappendageal delivery46—translation will require validation across additional models, doses, and ultimately human studies, alongside formal pharmacokinetic, biodistribution, and chronic-safety evaluation of HPD.

Conclusion

This study establishes a drug-free, nano–bio–physical strategy in which fractional CO2 laser microchannels serve simultaneously as spatially defined delivery portals and regenerative triggers, while hyperbranched polymer dot nanozymes scavenge the post-injury reactive oxygen species that would otherwise constrain repair. The result is a sustained window of Wnt/β-catenin activation and hair follicle stem cell proliferation that translates into a greater-than-additive, temporally advanced regenerative response—exceeding HPD alone, laser alone, and minoxidil across macroscopic, structural, cellular, and molecular endpoints. Consistent with the histomorphometric data, this response reflects the activation and accelerated growth of existing follicles rather than de novo neogenesis. By coupling a clinically established physical modality with a biocompatible, scalable, and intrinsically bioactive nanomaterial, the approach offers a modular platform with potential relevance beyond alopecia to wound healing and scar remodeling, pending the long-term efficacy, safety, and translational studies outlined above.
