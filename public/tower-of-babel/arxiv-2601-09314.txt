Tail behavior of Markov-modulated generalized Ornstein-Uhlenbeck processes
Gerold Alsmeyer ??, Anita Behme

Abstract
We study the tail behavior of Markov-modulated generalized Ornstein-Uhlenbeck processes – that is, solutions to Langevin-type stochastic differential equations driven by a background continuous-time Markov chain. To this end, we consider a sequence of Markov modulated random affine functions Ψ n : ℝ → ℝ \Psi_{n}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R} , n ∈ ℕ n\in\mathbb{N} , and the associated iterated function system defined recursively by X 0 x := x X_{0}^{x}:=x and X n x := Ψ n − 1 ​ ( X n − 1 x ) X_{n}^{x}:=\Psi_{n-1}(X_{n-1}^{x}) for x ∈ ℝ x\in\mathbb{R} , n ∈ ℕ n\in\mathbb{N} . We analyze the tail behavior of the stationary distribution of such a Markov chain using tools from Markov renewal theory. Our approach extends Goldie’s implicit renewal theory [ 23 ] and can be seen as an adaptation of Kesten’s work on products of random matrices [ 31 ] to the one-dimensional setting of random affine function systems. These results have applications in diverse areas of applied probability, including queueing theory, econometrics, mathematical finance, and population dynamics.

Introduction

Langevin-type stochastic differential equations (SDEs) of the form

\displaystyle\operatorname{d}\hskip-1.0ptV_{t}\ =\ V_{t-}\,\operatorname{d}\hskip-1.0ptU_{t}\,+\,\operatorname{d}\hskip-1.0ptL_{t},\qquad t\geqslant 0,

(1)

are widely studied in the applied stochastics literature because they cover a large range of common models in various areas. Assuming that
(U,L)
is a bivariate Lévy process on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that
\Delta U>-1
, i.e.
U
a.s. has no jumps of size less or equal to
-1
, the solution to (
1
) is known as a
generalized Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (
GOU
) process
. This type of process is a generalization of the classical Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process which, historically, has been introduced by the eponymous authors as a model in physics in the article
[
39
]
from 1930. In financial mathematics, special instances of
GOU
processes include the classical Vasicek interest rate model
[
49
]
and several prominent volatility models
[
9
,
32
,
37
]
. In the actuarial sciences,
GOU
processes are used as risk models with investment structure
[
40
,
41
]
. Recently, Langevin-type SDEs have also been proposed in machine learning as a continuous-time approximation of stochastic gradient descent (SGD) algorithms
[
38
,
47
]
.

In all these applications it is of paramount importance to be able to quantify the tail behavior of the resulting process and/or its stationary distribution. Therefore, this problem has been investigated in various special cases
[
33
,
41
]
as well as in the general Lévy-driven setting
[
10
,
5
]
, where stationary solutions to (
1
) are also known as
exponential functionals
.

A key tool to tackle this question used in these works is that solutions to (
1
) appear as so-called
perpetuities
after discretization, that is, solutions of random affine recursions of the form

R_{n+1}\,=\,A_{n}R_{n}\,+\,B_{n},\quad n\in\mathbb{N},

(2)

for an i.i.d. sequence
(A_{n},B_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. Tails of stationary solutions to (
2
) in the i.i.d. case have been considered in the seminal works by Kesten
[
31
]
and Goldie
[
23
]
, who proved that - under suitable conditions - perpetuities in the stationary regime exhibit Pareto-like tails, which is also referred to as power-law behavior. As shown in
[
35
]
and
[
10
]
, this behavior carries over to the continuous-time setting and - as stated above - it is of significant interest in applications. In the afore-mentioned actuarial context of a ruin model with investment, for example, the polynomial decay rates of certain exponential functionals imply slow decay of the ruin probability
[
41
, Prop. 3.1]
as function of the initial capital.

In the present work, the case when
(U_{t},L_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
is modulated by a continuous-time Markov process
(J_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
will be addressed regarding the question how the modulation affects the tail behavior of a stationary solution to (
1
).
This means that
(J_{t},(U_{t},L_{t}))_{t\geqslant 0}
forms a
Markov-additive process (
MAP
)
with additive component
(U_{t},L_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that again
\Delta U>-1
,
and driving process
(J_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
, which is here supposed to have finite state-space
\mathscr{S}
.

Generally speaking, Markov modulation is a standard tool in many application areas to achieve greater flexibility in modeling. In the financial context Markov switching models have been used for about 40 years
[
27
,
26
]
by now, with Markov switching interest rates as well as Markov switching volatility models still being active topics of research
[
16
,
22
,
11
]
. Likewise, Markov-modulated risk models first appeared in the 1980s
[
29
,
44
,
7
]
, and Markov-modulated risk models with investments that solve an SDE of type (
1
) have now been studied for about 15 years
[
34
,
15
,
43
,
14
]
. In particular, and in analogy to the non-modulated case
[
40
, Thm. 3.2]
, it is shown in
[
14
, Thm. 4.2]
that the ruin probability of a Markov-modulated risk model with investment solving (
1
) can be expressed in terms of a Markov-modulated exponential functional. Thus again the decay rates of exponential functionals determine the decay rates of ruin probabilities in the considered models.
In the rather young and extremely active area of machine learning, first studies using Markov switching versions of SGD have appeared in
[
48
,
21
]
, and (
1
) with
|\mathscr{S}|>1
may serve as an approximation for such SGD algorithms in the future.

In the Markov-modulated setting, diffusion-type solutions to (
1
) and those with jumps are called
Markov-modulated Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (
MMOU
) processes
and
Markov-modulated generalized Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (
MMGOU
) processes
, respectively. For
MMOU
processes,
U
is supposed to be a Markov-modulated drift process and
L
a Markov-modulated Brownian motion with drift. This class was studied e.g. in
[
8
,
36
,
28
]
, the first two references regarding stationarity, ergodicity and also tail behavior, the last one regarding moments. There is also recent work on
MMGOU
processes, see
[
14
,
12
]
, but none of these consider tail behavior. On the other hand, the occurrence of jumps is clearly a necessary feature in applications, and the present work therefore aims to fill this gap.

In principle, the results by Goldie and Kesten are still applicable in the Markov-modulated setting: Considering the return times
\tau_{n}(j),n\in\mathbb{N}
of the Markov chain
J
to an arbitrary state
j\in\mathscr{S}
, one can easily check that any solution to (
1
) fulfills the random recursive equation

V_{\tau_{n}(j)}\,=\,A_{n}V_{\tau_{n-1}(j)}\,+\,B_{n},

(3)

with i.i.d. coefficients
(A_{n},B_{n})
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
. This fact has been applied in
[
36
]
for the diffusion case. However, subsampling at return times does not provide a deeper insight into the contributions of different regimes to the tail behavior: The resulting conditions for Pareto-tails depend on integrals of the arising
MAP
s over a cycle marked by two consecutive return epochs, which typically can hardly be re-expressed in terms of the characteristics of the given process. Therefore, a better understanding of the tail behavior of a stationary solution to (
1
) calls for an extension of the Kesten-Goldie implicit renewal theorem
[
31
,
23
]
to random affine recursions in a Markovian environment. Such extensions have so far only been considered and applied in some special cases. We mention
[
45
,
19
,
8
]
and especially the recent article
[
3
]
, where implicit renewal theory for a two-state Markovian environment is developed and then used to determine the stationary tail behavior of so-called asymptotically linear iterated function systems (
ALIFS
) on the line. The latter are characterized by being asymptotically equivalent to possibly distinct random affine functions at the two ends of the real line, thus at
\pm\infty
. Motivated by the fact that an environment of only two states is too restrictive for our purposes, this work provides an implicit renewal theorem for random affine recursions in a general finite Markovian environment, stated as Theorem
3.1
. We apply Theorem
3.1
to a finer discretization of (
1
), as compared to the coarser scheme in (
3
), which yields precise conditions for the emergence of Pareto-like tails in Markov-modulated exponential functionals. These results will be presented in Theorem
2.1
. The proof of Theorem
3.1
, in turn, builds on and extends techniques developed in
[
3
]
.

2
Main results

In this section, we state our main result, Theorem
2.1
, on the tail behavior of stationary solutions to the SDE (
1
) for a
MAP

(J,(U,L))
. As indicated earlier, the proof is based on a discretization argument combined with Theorem
3.1
. The proof of Theorem
2.1
is therefore deferred to the final Section

Setup.
Let
\mathscr{S}
be a finite set and assume we are given a filtered probability space
(\Omega,\mathscr{F},\mathbb{F},\mathbb{P})
, where
\mathbb{F}=(\mathscr{F}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
is a filtration of sub-
\sigma
-fields of
\mathscr{F}
satisfying the usual conditions of completeness and right-continuity. Let
(J,X)=(J_{t},X_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
be an
\mathbb{F}
-adapted Markov process taking values in
\mathscr{S}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with
d\geqslant 1
. We define
\mathbb{P}_{j}(\cdot):=\mathbb{P}(\cdot\,|\,J_{0}=j)
, as usual, and let
\mathbb{E}_{j}[\cdot]
denote the corresponding expectation operator. Then
(J,X)
is called a
(
d
-dimensional) Markov-additive process (
MAP
) with respect to
\mathbb{F}
if

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[f(X_{s+t}-X_{s})g(J_{s+t})|\mathscr{F}_{s}\right]\ =\ \mathbb{E}_{J_{s}}\left[f(X_{t}-X_{0})g(J_{t})\right]

(4)

for all
s,t\geqslant 0
, and all bounded measurable functions
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
,
g:\mathscr{S}\to\mathbb{R}
. Since
\mathscr{S}
is finite, it follows in this case that a family of independent
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued Lévy processes
\{X^{(j)}:j\in\mathscr{S}\}
can be defined such that, whenever
J_{t}=j
on an arbitrary interval
(t_{1},t_{2})
, the increments
(X_{t}-X_{t_{1}})_{t_{1}<t<t_{2}}
of the additive component of
(J,X)
are distributed as
(X_{t}^{(j)}-X_{t_{1}}^{(j)})_{t_{1}<t<t_{2}}
; see
[
6
, Chapter XI.2]
.

Whenever the continuous-time background process
J
jumps at some time
T_{n}
from state
i
to state
j
, it induces an additional jump
\Delta X_{T_{n}}=Z_{X,n}^{ij}
in
X
, where the distribution
G_{\!X}^{ij}
depends only on the pair
(i,j)
– not on the jump time or index – and is independent of all other randomness.

Given any càdlàg process
Y
, we denote by
Y_{t-}
the left-hand limit of
Y
at time
t>0
(with the convention
Y_{0-}:=Y_{0}
), and define the jump size at time
t
by
\Delta Y_{t}:=Y_{t}-Y_{t-}
. We assume throughout that
X_{0}=0
almost surely for any
MAP
. For further background on
MAP
s, we refer to
[
6
]
,
[
20
, Appendix]
, or
[
12
]
.

Given a bivariate
MAP

(J,(\zeta,\eta))=(J_{t},(\zeta_{t},\eta_{t}))_{t\geqslant 0}
, the
Markov-modulated generalized Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (
MMGOU
) process driven by
(J,(\zeta,\eta))
has been introduced in
[
14
]
as the process

\displaystyle V_{t}\ =\ {\mathrm{e}}^{-\zeta_{t}}\left(V_{0}+\int_{(0,t]}{\mathrm{e}}^{\zeta_{s-}}\,\operatorname{d}\hskip-1.0pt\eta_{s}\right),\quad t\geqslant 0,

(5)

where the initial value
V_{0}
and the process
(J_{t},(\zeta_{t},\eta_{t}))_{t\geqslant 0}
are conditionally independent given
J_{0}
. It has further been shown there that
(V_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
forms the unique solution to the SDE (
1
) for a bivariate
MAP

(J,(U,L))=(J_{t},(U_{t},L_{t}))_{t\geqslant 0}
that is uniquely determined by
(J,(\zeta,\eta))
via

\begin{pmatrix}U_{t}\\
L_{t}\end{pmatrix}\ =\ \begin{pmatrix}-\zeta_{t}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{(0,t]}\sigma_{\zeta}^{2}(J_{s})\,\operatorname{d}\hskip-1.0pts+\sum_{0<s\leq t}\left(\Delta\zeta_{s}+{\mathrm{e}}^{-\Delta\zeta_{s}}-1\right)\\[3.00003pt]
\eta_{t}-\int_{(0,t]}\sigma_{\zeta,\eta}(J_{s})\,\operatorname{d}\hskip-1.0pts+\sum_{0<s\leq t}\left({\mathrm{e}}^{-\Delta\zeta_{s}}-1\right)\Delta\eta_{s}\end{pmatrix},\quad t\geqslant 0,

(6)

see
[
14
, Eq. (2.24)]
or
[
12
, Eq. (4.1)]
. In particular, the resulting process satisfies
\Delta U>-1
.

Under the assumption that the background driving Markov chain
J
is ergodic with stationary distribution
\pi
, necessary and sufficient conditions for the existence of a stationary distribution of the
MMGOU
process (
5
) are given in
[
14
, Thm. 3.3]
. To state these, note that we write
\mathbb{P}_{\pi}[\cdot]:=\sum_{i\in\mathscr{S}}\pi_{i}\mathbb{P}_{i}[\cdot]
, as is standard.
Further,
(\widehat{J},(\widehat{\zeta},\widehat{L}))
denotes the dual of the Markov-additive process
(J,(\zeta,L))
, and we define
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{\pi}[\cdot]:=\sum_{i\in\mathscr{S}}\pi_{i}\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{i}[\cdot]
via
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{i}[\cdot]:=\mathbb{P}[\cdot\,|\,\widehat{J}_{0}=i]
, such that in particular

\displaystyle\text{the law of }\big(J_{(t-s)-},\,\zeta_{(t-s)-}-\zeta_{t},\,\eta_{(t-s)-}-\eta_{t}\big)_{0\leq s\leq t}\text{ under }\mathbb{P}_{\pi}

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt\text{ is equal to the law of }\big(\widehat{J}_{s},\,\widehat{\zeta}_{s},\,\widehat{\eta}_{s}\big)_{0\leq s\leq t}\text{ under }\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{\pi},

see e.g.
[
20

. Then by
[
14
, Thm. 3.3]
, the
MMGOU
process admits a nontrivial stationary distribution if and only if the integral
\int_{(0,t]}{\mathrm{e}}^{\widehat{\zeta}_{s-}}\,\operatorname{d}\hskip-1.0pt\widehat{L}_{s}
converges in
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{\pi}
-probability as

to some finite-valued random variable
V_{\infty}
. Moreover, in this case the stationary distribution of the
MMGOU
process under
\mathbb{P}_{\pi}
is uniquely determined as the distribution of the

V_{\infty}\ =\ -\int_{(0,\infty)}{\mathrm{e}}^{\widehat{\zeta}_{s-}}\,\operatorname{d}\hskip-1.0pt\widehat{L}_{s}\ =\ \widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{\pi}\text{\,-}\lim_{t\to\infty}\left(-\int_{(0,t]}{\mathrm{e}}^{\widehat{\zeta}_{s-}}\,\operatorname{d}\hskip-1.0pt\widehat{L}_{s}\right).

(7)

Necessary and sufficient conditions for convergence of integrals of the form (
7
) in terms of the characteristics of the appearing processes have been derived in
[
13
]
.

In the case the convergence in (
7
) fails, either no stationary distribution of the
MMGOU
process exists, or it is degenerate in the sense that there exists a sequence of constants
\{c_{j},\,j\in\mathscr{S}\}
such that
V_{t}=c_{J_{t}}

\mathbb{P}_{\pi}
-a.s. for all
t\geq 0
, and
\pi_{j}=\mathbb{P}_{\pi}(V_{t}=c_{j})
. As outlined in the proof of
[
14
, Thm. 3.3]
, the latter behaviour appears if and only if
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{\pi}
-almost surely

\int_{(0,t]}{\mathrm{e}}^{\widehat{\zeta}_{s-}}\,\operatorname{d}\hskip-1.0pt\widehat{L}_{s}\ =\ c_{\widehat{J}_{0}}+c_{\widehat{J}_{t}}{\mathrm{e}}^{\widehat{\zeta}_{t}},

which in turn is equivalent to an explicit dependency of the driving processes of the form

L_{t}=-\int_{(0,t]}c_{J_{s}-}\operatorname{d}\hskip-1.0ptU_{s}-\int_{(0,t]}\operatorname{d}\hskip-1.0ptc_{J_{s}},\quad\mathbb{P}_{\pi}\text{-a.s.},

(8)

see
[
13
, Prop. 4.7]
. We will exclude this case from our studies.

In order to formulate our result on the tail behavior of the stationary distribution of the process
(V_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, we denote the intensity matrix of
J
by
Q=(q_{ij})_{i,j\in\mathscr{S}}
. Further, we define

\Psi_{\zeta}(w)\ =\ \textsl{diag}\left[\psi_{j}(w):j\in\mathscr{S}\right]+Q^{\top}\circ\left(\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{e}}^{wZ^{jk}_{\zeta,1}}\right]\right)_{j,k\in\mathscr{S}}^{\top}\ \in\ \mathbb{C}^{|\mathscr{S}|\times|\mathscr{S}|},

(9)

for all
w\in\mathbb{C}
such that the right-hand side exists. This quantity is known as the
matrix exponent
of the
MAP

(J,\zeta)
; see, e.g.,
[
6
, Prop. XI.2.2]
or
[
20
]
.
Here and in the following,
\textsl{diag}[a_{j}:j\in\mathscr{S}]
denotes the diagonal matrix with entries
a_{j}
for
j=1,\dots,|\mathscr{S}|
;
\top
denotes matrix transposition; and “
\circ
” denotes the elementwise (Hadamard) product of matrices. Moreover,
\psi_{j}(w):=\log\mathbb{E}[{\mathrm{e}}^{w\zeta^{(j)}_{1}}]
is the Laplace exponent of the Lévy process
\zeta^{(j)}
. As described in
[
20
, Sec. A.1]
, the matrix exponent determines the joint Laplace transform of the additive component
\zeta
and the modulating chain
J
. Specifically,

\mathbb{E}_{i}\Big[{\mathrm{e}}^{w\zeta_{t}}\mathds{1}_{\{J_{t}=j\}}\Big]\ =\ e_{j}^{\top}{\mathrm{e}}^{t\Psi_{\zeta}(w)}e_{i},\quad\text{for all }i,j\in\mathscr{S},

(10)

where
e_{j}
denotes the
j
-th unit vector.
A closely related quantity that will also be important is the matrix
\Upsilon_{\zeta}(\theta)
, defined for
\theta\geqslant 0
by

\Upsilon_{\zeta}^{ij}(\theta)\ :=\ \mathbb{E}_{i}\Big[{\mathrm{e}}^{-\theta\zeta_{T_{1}}}\mathds{1}_{\{J_{T_{1}}=j\}}\Big]\ =\ q_{ij}\int_{0}^{\infty}e_{j}^{\top}{\mathrm{e}}^{t\Psi_{\zeta}(-\theta)}e_{i}\,{\mathrm{e}}^{-q_{ij}t}\,\,\operatorname{d}\hskip-1.0ptt,\quad i,j\in\mathscr{S},

(11)

where
T_{1}
denotes the time of the first regime switch, i.e.,

T_{1}\,:=\,\inf\{t>0:J_{t}\neq J_{t-}\}.

Our main result on the tail behavior of the stationary distribution is the following. Its proof is provided in Section
9
, and it builds upon an extension of Goldie’s implicit renewal theorem to the Markov-modulated setting, which will be stated as Theorem
3.1
in the next section.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
(J,(\zeta,\eta))
be a bivariate
MAP
such that the modulating process
J
has finite state space
\mathscr{S}
and is ergodic with stationary distribution
\pi
. Assume that
\eta\not\equiv 0
and there exists no sequence
\{c_{j},\,j\in\mathscr{S}\}
such that (
8
) holds.
If all Levy processes
\zeta^{(j)}
are of finite variation with zero drift, additionally assume that either there exists
j\in\mathscr{S}
such that the jump distribution of
\zeta^{(j)}
is nonlattice or that
Z_{\zeta}^{ij}
is nonlattice for at least one pair
(i,j)\in\mathscr{S}^{2}
with
q_{ij}>0
. Suppose further that the following conditions hold:

(A1)

There exists
\kappa>0
such that
\Upsilon_{\zeta}(\kappa)
has dominant eigenvalue
1
and is irreducible;

(A2)

For all
i,j\in\mathscr{S}
, the derivative

\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\Upsilon_{\zeta}^{ij}(\theta)\Big|_{\theta=\kappa}\ <\ \infty;

(A3)

\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[|\eta_{1}|^{\kappa\vee 1}]\ <\ \infty
;

(A4)

There exists
\varepsilon>0
such that for all
i,j\in\mathscr{S}
with
q_{ij}>0
,

\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{e}}^{-((\kappa\vee 1)+\varepsilon)Z^{ij}_{\zeta,1}}\right]\ <\ \infty,\quad\text{and}\quad\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{s\leqslant t}{\mathrm{e}}^{((\kappa\vee 1)+\varepsilon)|\zeta^{(j)}_{s}|}\right]{\mathrm{e}}^{q_{jj}t}\,\operatorname{d}\hskip-1.0ptt\ <\ \infty.

Then there exists a nondegenerate random variable
V_{0}
, which is conditionally independent of
(J,(\zeta,\eta))
given
J_{0}
, such that the
MMGOU
process defined in (
5
), driven by
(J,(\zeta,\eta))
and initialized at
V_{0}
, is strictly stationary. Furthermore, there exist finite constants
C_{i}^{+},C_{i}^{-}
such that

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}x^{\kappa}\,\mathbb{P}_{i}[V_{0}>x]\,=\,C_{i}^{+},\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{x\to\infty}x^{\kappa}\,\mathbb{P}_{i}[V_{0}<-x]\,=\,C_{i}^{-},

with
C_{i}^{+}+C_{i}^{-}>0
for all
i\in\mathscr{S}
.

Remark 2.2
.

The limiting constants
C_{i}^{\pm}
in Theorem
2.1
can be expressed explicitly in terms of the derivative of the leading eigenvalue of
\Upsilon_{\zeta}(\kappa)
, its associated right eigenvectors, and certain moments of the stationary distribution. See (
55
) and (
53
) for the precise formulas.

Remark 2.3
.

As shown in
[
14
]
, the SDE (
1
) can also be solved in the more general case
\Delta U\neq-1
, with solution given by

V_{t}\ =\ \mathscr{E}(U)_{t}\left(V_{0}\,+\,\int_{(0,t]}\mathscr{E}(U)_{s-}^{-1}\,\operatorname{d}\hskip-1.0pt\eta_{s}\right),\quad t\geq 0,

(12)

where
\mathscr{E}(U)
denotes the stochastic exponential of
U
, defined as the solution to the SDE
\operatorname{d}\hskip-1.0pt\mathscr{E}(U)_{t}=\mathscr{E}(U)_{t-}\,\operatorname{d}\hskip-1.0ptU_{t}
with initial condition
\mathscr{E}(U)_{0}=1
. The stochastic exponential of a semimartingale – such as the additive component of a
MAP
– can be expressed explicitly; see, e.g.,
[
42
, Thm. II.37]
. From this representation, it is immediate that any jump of
U
of size less than
-1
causes a sign change in
\mathscr{E}(U)
. In terms of perpetuities (i.e., time discretizations of solutions to (
1
)), this corresponds to random affine recursions of the form
R_{n}=A_{n}R_{n-1}+B_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, with
\mathbb{P}(A_{n}<0)>0
. This setting is also covered by Theorem
3.1
in the discrete-time case. The application to the process given by (
12
) can be carried out in direct analogy with the proof of Theorem
2.1
; however, we omit the details here in order to keep the presentation accessible.

Remark 2.4
.

A related result on the tail behavior of the classical generalized Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process (corresponding to the special case
|\mathscr{S}|=1
) is given in
[
35
, Thm. 4.5]
; see also
[
10
, Thm. 4.1]
for a generalization in the spirit of Remark
2.3
and under refined assumptions. Applying Theorem
2.1
in this setting recovers the known results, but under a slightly weaker condition on the moments of
\eta
: we only require assumption
(A3)
, whereas
[
10
]
assumes
\mathbb{E}|\eta_{1}|^{(\kappa+\varepsilon)\vee 1}<\infty
. This relaxation is possible due to new moment bounds for stochastic integrals derived in
[
12
]
.

3
An implicit renewal theorem in finite Markovian environment

The Kesten-Goldie implicit renewal theorem (
[
31
, Thm. 5]
and
[
23
, Thm. 2.3]
) characterizes the stationary tail behavior of iterations of i.i.d. random functions
\Psi_{n}(x)
on the real line under suitable conditions, a key one being that
\Psi_{n}(x)
is approximately linear as
|x|\to\infty
, in an appropriate sense. The purpose of this section is to extend the Kesten-Goldie result to the setting of real-valued random affine linear functions
A_{n}x+B_{n}
evolving in a Markovian environment.

Setup
. Let
\Xi=(\xi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
be a doubly infinite stationary and irreducible Markov chain with finite state space
\mathscr{S}
, transition matrix
P=(p_{ij})_{i,j\in\mathscr{S}}
and stationary law
\pi=(\pi_{i})_{i\in\mathscr{S}}
. We consider a sequence of random affine linear maps

\Psi_{n}(x)\ =\ A_{n}x+B_{n},\quad n\in\mathbb{Z},\ x\in\mathbb{R},

such that
(A_{n},B_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
is taking values in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and modulated by
\Xi
. This means that, conditioned upon
\xi_{k}=i_{k}
for
k\in\mathbb{Z}
and arbitrary
i_{0},i_{\pm 1},i_{\pm 2}\ldots\in\mathscr{S}
,

•

the
(A_{n},B_{n})
are conditionally independent,

•

the conditional law of
(A_{n},B_{n})
is homogeneous in
n
and only dependent on
(i_{n-1},i_{n})
, i.e.

\mathbb{P}\big[(A_{n},B_{n})\in\cdot\,|\xi_{n-1}=i_{n-1},\,\xi_{n}=i_{n}\big]\ =\ F_{i_{n-1}i_{n}}

for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
and a stochastic kernel
F
from
\mathscr{S}^{2}
to
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, where
F_{ij}
is used as shorthand for
F((i,j),\cdot)
.

The
Markov-modulated linear iterated function system (
MMLIFS
)
associated with the sequence
(\Psi_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
of random affine linear functions, and with driving chain
(\xi_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
, is defined by
(\xi_{n},R_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
, where

\displaystyle R_{n}\ :=\ \Psi_{n}(R_{n-1})\ =\ \Psi_{n}\cdots\Psi_{1}(R_{0})\quad\text{for }n=1,2,\ldots

(13)

and
\Psi_{n}\cdots\Psi_{1}
is used as shorthand for the composition
\Psi_{n}\circ\ldots\circ\Psi_{1}
. It constitutes a temporally homogeneous Markov chain having transition kernel

\Gamma((i,r),\{j\}\times\cdot)\ :=\ p_{ij}\,\mathbb{P}\big[A_{1}r+B_{1}\in\cdot\,|\xi_{0}=i,\xi_{1}=j\big]\quad\text{a.s.}

for all
i,j\in\mathscr{S}
and
r\in\mathbb{R}
, provided that
R_{0}
is measurable with respect to
\mathscr{F}_{0}
, where

\mathscr{F}_{n}\,:=\,\sigma((\xi_{k},A_{k},B_{k});k\leqslant n)\quad\text{for }n\in\mathbb{Z}

is the natural filtration induced by
(\xi,A,B)
.
Notice that the conditional law of
(\xi_{n},A_{n},B_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
given
(\xi_{0},R_{0})
does only depend on
\xi_{0}
and not on
R_{0}
, thus

\mathbb{P}\big[(\xi_{n},A_{n},B_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}\in\cdot\,|\xi_{0}=i,R_{0}=r\big]\ =\ \mathbb{P}_{i}\big[(\xi_{n},A_{n},B_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}\in\cdot\big],

where
\mathbb{P}_{i}
is used as common for
\mathbb{P}[\,\cdot\,|\xi_{0}=i]
for
i\in\mathscr{S}
and
\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}
for
\sum_{i\in\mathscr{S}}\lambda_{i}\mathbb{P}_{i}
if
\lambda=(\lambda_{i})_{i\in\mathscr{S}}
is a probability distribution on
\mathscr{S}
.

The
R_{n}
are also called
forward iterations
when opposed to the corresponding sequence of
backward iterations

\displaystyle\Psi_{1}\cdots\Psi_{n}(R_{0})\ =\ \mathit{\Pi}_{n}R_{0}\ +\ \sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathit{\Pi}_{k-1}B_{k},\quad n=1,2,\ldots,

(14)

where

\displaystyle\mathit{\Pi}_{0}\ :=\ 1\quad\text{and}\quad\mathit{\Pi}_{n}\ :=\ \begin{cases}\hfill A_{1}A_{2}\cdot\ldots\cdot A_{n}&\text{if }n=1,2,\ldots\\
A_{0}A_{-1}\cdot\ldots\cdot A_{n+1}&\text{if }n=-1,-2,\ldots\end{cases}

(15)

If
R_{0}=0
, these iterations take the form

\Psi_{1}\cdots\Psi_{n}(0)\ =\ \sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathit{\Pi}_{k-1}B_{k},\quad n=1,2,\ldots

(16)

with obvious limiting random variable as
n\to\infty
(if it exists)

\widehat{R}_{0}\ :=\ \sum_{k\geqslant 1}\mathit{\Pi}_{k-1}B_{k}.

(17)

The latter is called perpetuity due to its interpretation in insurance and finance as a discounted sum of perpetual payments. In the case when the
(A_{n},B_{n})
are i.i.d., Goldie and Maller
[
24
, Thm. 2.1]
established necessary and sufficient conditions for
\widehat{R}_{0}
to be a.s. finite and its law to be the unique stationary law of the Markov chain
(R_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
. In the present situation of Markov modulated coefficients, corresponding results have been obtained in
[
4
]
, but are complicated by the fact that in general the stationary law of
(R_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
is no longer the law of
\widehat{R}_{0}
but of a dual counterpart. In fact, the following has been shown by Brandt
[
17
]
: The choice

\displaystyle R_{0}\ :=\ \sum_{k\geqslant 0}\mathit{\Pi}_{-k}B_{-k}\ =\ B_{0}+A_{0}B_{-1}+A_{0}A_{-1}B_{-2}+\ldots

(18)

renders stationarity of
(\xi_{n},R_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
if the right-hand sum in (
18
) is a.s. finite. In this case, a doubly infinite
(\mathscr{F}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
-adapted stationary extension of the latter sequence can be explicitly defined in terms of the given stationary sequence
(\xi_{n},A_{n},B_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
, namely

R_{n}\ :=\ \sum_{k\geqslant 0}A_{n}\cdots A_{n-k+1}B_{n-k}\quad\text{for }n\in\mathbb{Z}.

(19)

It is this stationary representation we will work with hereafter when studying the tail behavior of the stationary law of
(R_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
. Let us point out that
R_{0}
, when formally defined by (
18
), is also a backward iteration limit and thus a perpetuity, but for the time-reversal
(\xi_{-n},A_{-n},B_{-n})_{n\geqslant 0}
, namely

R_{0}\ =\ \Psi_{0}\cdots\Psi_{-n}(R_{-n})\quad\text{for all }n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

(20)

As a consequence, the results from
[
4
]
can be used to directly infer the a.s. finiteness of
R_{0}
under the conditions stated in our main results below.

Nondegeneracy
. In the case of i.i.d.
(A_{n},B_{n})
, it is well-known that the law of
R_{0}
is nondegenerate iff

\mathbb{P}[A_{1}c+B_{1}=c\,]\,<\,1\quad\text{for all }c\in\mathbb{R},

(21)

see
[
24
, Rem. 2.3]
. For the Markov-modulated case, the corresponding condition has been provided in
[
4
, Subsect. 2.4]
, namely

\mathbb{P}_{\pi}[A_{1}c_{\xi_{1}}+B_{1}=c_{\xi_{0}}\,]\,<\,1\quad\text{for any choice }c_{i}\in\mathbb{R},\,i\in\mathscr{S}.

(22)

In the following, the
MMLIFS

(\xi_{n},R_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
associated with
(A_{n},B_{n})
is called nondegenerate if this condition holds.

We note that the definitions of
\mathbb{P}_{i}:=\mathbb{P}[\cdot\,|\,\xi_{0}=i]
and

\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}:=\sum_{i\in\mathscr{S}}\lambda_{i}\mathbb{P}_{i}
for any probability law

\lambda=(\lambda_{i})_{i\in\mathscr{S}}
on
\mathscr{S}
remain in force for the doubly infinite sequence

(\xi_{n},A_{n},B_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
. Moreover, we emphasize that stationarity under
\mathbb{P}_{\pi}
holds
not only for
(\xi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
but also for the extended sequences

(\xi_{n},A_{n},B_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
and
(\xi_{n},R_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
.

The following result is the announced extension of the Kesten–Goldie implicit renewal theorem
to
MMLIFS
. In the form stated, the i.i.d. case for
(A_{n},B_{n})
appears as
Theorems 2.4.4 and 2.4.7 in the monograph by Buraczewski et al.
[
18
]
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
(\xi_{n},A_{n},B_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
be a doubly infinite stationary sequence as introduced above,
and
(\xi_{n},R_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
the
MMLIFS
associated with
\Psi_{n}(x)=A_{n}x+B_{n}
for

n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Further assume

(B1)

There exists
\kappa>0
such that

\mathsf{P}(\kappa):=\big(\mathbb{E}_{i}[\,|A_{1}|^{\kappa}\mathbf{1}_{\{\xi_{1}=j\}}]\big)_{i,j\in\mathscr{S}}

has dominant eigenvalue
\rho(\kappa)=1
.

(B2)

\mathbb{E}|A_{1}|^{\kappa}\log|A_{1}|<\infty
and
\mathbb{E}|B_{1}|^{\kappa}<\infty
.

(B3)

\mathbb{P}\big[\log|A_{1}|-a_{\xi_{1}}+a_{\xi_{0}}\in d\mathbb{Z}\,\big|\,A_{1}\neq 0\big]<1

for any
d>0
and any choice of real numbers
\{a_{i}:i\in\mathscr{S}\}
.

(B4)

\mathbb{P}[B_{1}=0]<1
, hence

\min_{i,j\in\mathscr{S}}F_{ij}(\mathbb{R}_{\scriptscriptstyle>}\times\{0\})<1
.

Then
-\infty\leqslant\mathbb{E}\log|A_{1}|<0
, and
(R_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
has a unique stationary law and a
doubly infinite extension as defined in (
19
). Moreover, there exist nonnegative
constants
C_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle-}
and
C_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle+}
(explicitly defined in Section
8
) such
that the following assertions hold:

(a) If
\mathbb{P}[A_{1}\geqslant 0]=1
, then

\lim_{t\to\infty}t^{\kappa}\,\mathbb{P}_{i}[R_{0}>t]\ =\ C_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle+},\qquad\lim_{t\to\infty}t^{\kappa}\,\mathbb{P}_{i}[R_{0}<-t]\ =\ C_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle-}\quad\text{for all }i\in\mathscr{S}.

(23)

(b) If
\mathbb{P}[A_{1}<0]>0
, then

\lim_{t\to\infty}t^{\kappa}\,\mathbb{P}_{i}[R_{0}>t]\ =\ \lim_{t\to\infty}t^{\kappa}\,\mathbb{P}_{i}[R_{0}<-t]\ =\ \frac{C_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle-}+C_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle+}}{2}\quad\text{for all }i\in\mathscr{S}.

(24)

In both cases,
C_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle+}+C_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle-}>0
if
(\xi_{n},R_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
is nondegenerate.

Remark 3.2
.

The matrix
\mathsf{P}(\kappa)
defined in
(B1)
is irreducible. This follows from the irreducibility of the Markov chain
\Xi
with transition matrix
P
, as assumed at the beginning of this section. Since the state space
\mathscr{S}
is finite and assumption
(B1)
ensures that
A_{1}
does not vanish almost surely, there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that
\mathsf{P}(\kappa)\geqslant\varepsilon P
. Consequently, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have
\mathsf{P}(\kappa)^{n}\geqslant\varepsilon^{n}P^{n}
, which in turn implies the irreducibility of
\mathsf{P}(\kappa)
.

Remark 3.3
.

For the stationary law of
(R_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
, thus

\mathbb{P}_{\pi}[R_{0}\in\cdot]\ =\ \sum_{i\in\mathscr{S}}\pi_{i}\,\mathbb{P}_{i}[R_{0}\in\cdot\hskip 1.0pt],

it follows directly from (
23
) that

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}t^{\kappa}\,\mathbb{P}_{\pi}[R_{0}>t]\,=\,C^{\scriptscriptstyle+}\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{t\to\infty}t^{\kappa}\,\mathbb{P}_{\pi}[R_{0}<-t]\,=\,C^{\scriptscriptstyle-},

(25)

where

\displaystyle C^{\pm}\ :=\ \sum_{i\in\mathscr{S}}\pi_{i}\,C^{\pm}.

Similarly, (
24
) implies

\lim_{t\to\infty}t^{\kappa}\,\mathbb{P}_{\pi}[R_{0}>t]\,=\,\lim_{t\to\infty}t^{\kappa}\,\mathbb{P}_{\pi}[R_{0}<-t]\,=\,\frac{C^{\scriptscriptstyle-}+C^{\scriptscriptstyle+}}{2}.

(26)

Remark 3.4
.

Let
\mathscr{C}^{*}(\mathbb{R})
denote the space of bounded Lipschitz functions
\phi
on
\mathbb{R}
that vanish in a neighborhood of the origin, and let
\mathscr{C}_{-}^{*}(\mathbb{R})
and
\mathscr{C}_{+}^{*}(\mathbb{R})
be the subspaces consisting of functions in
\mathscr{C}^{*}(\mathbb{R})
that additionally vanish on
\mathbb{R}_{\scriptscriptstyle\geqslant}
and
\mathbb{R}_{\scriptscriptstyle\leqslant}
, respectively.

Since every
\phi\in\mathscr{C}^{*}(\mathbb{R})
is almost everywhere differentiable with bounded derivative
\phi^{\prime}
that also vanishes on a neighborhood of the origin, say
[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]
, integration by parts yields:

\mathbb{E}_{i}\phi(t^{-1}R_{0})\ =\ \int_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}\left(\phi^{\prime}(-x)\,\mathbb{P}_{i}[R_{0}<-tx]+\phi^{\prime}(x)\,\mathbb{P}_{i}[R_{0}>tx]\right)\operatorname{d}\hskip-1.0ptx,

for any
i\in\mathscr{S}
. Again, as a direct consequence of (
23
), we obtain:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\lim_{t\to\infty}t^{\kappa}\,\mathbb{E}_{i}\phi(t^{-1}R_{0})\ &=\ \int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{C_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle-}\phi^{\prime}(-x)+C_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle+}\phi^{\prime}(x)}{x^{\kappa}}\,\operatorname{d}\hskip-1.0ptx\\
&=\ \kappa\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{C_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle-}\phi(-x)+C_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle+}\phi(x)}{x^{\kappa+1}}\,\operatorname{d}\hskip-1.0ptx,\end{split}

(27)

where integration by parts renders the second identity. In the stationary case, as in Remark
3.3
,

\lim_{t\to\infty}t^{\kappa}\,\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\phi(t^{-1}x)\ =\ \kappa\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{C^{\scriptscriptstyle-}\phi(-x)+C^{\scriptscriptstyle+}\phi(x)}{x^{\kappa+1}}\,\operatorname{d}\hskip-1.0ptx

(28)

follows as well. The left-hand integral may be viewed as the multiplicative convolution, say
\hat{\phi}\star\nu(t)
, of
\hat{\phi}(t):=\phi(t^{-1})
and
\nu=\mathbb{P}[R_{0}\in\cdot]
at
t\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}
.

4
Prerequisites

This section collects the main prerequisites required for the proof of Theorem
3.1
.

4.1
Time-reversal and duality

The time-reversal and dual of the trivariate sequence
(\xi_{n},A_{n},B_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
is defined by

(\widehat{\xi}_{n},\widehat{A}_{n},\widehat{B}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\,:=\,(\xi_{-n},A_{-n},B_{-n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}},

which again forms a stationary process, with
(\widehat{A}_{n},\widehat{B}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
modulated by the Markov chain
\widehat{\Xi}:=(\widehat{\xi}_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
. However, the transition mechanism generally differs. Specifically:

•

\widehat{\Xi}:=(\widehat{\xi}_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
is the time-reversal of
\Xi
, with transition probabilities
\widehat{p}_{ij}:=\pi_{j}p_{ji}/\pi_{i}
and transition matrix
\widehat{P}=(\widehat{p}_{ij})_{i,j\in\mathscr{S}}
.

•

The conditional law of
(\widehat{A}_{n},\widehat{B}_{n})
given
\widehat{\Xi}
depends on
(\widehat{\xi}_{n},\widehat{\xi}_{n+1})
rather than
(\widehat{\xi}_{n-1},\widehat{\xi}_{n})
, and satisfies

\widehat{F}_{ij}\,:=\,\mathbb{P}\big[(\widehat{A}_{n},\widehat{B}_{n})\in\cdot\,\big|\,\widehat{\xi}_{n}=i,\,\widehat{\xi}_{n+1}=j\big]\,=\,F_{ji},

for all
n\geqslant 0
and
i,j\in\mathscr{S}
, where
F
is the stochastic kernel introduced in the previous section.

This shows that, strictly speaking, duality holds between the sequences
(\xi_{n},A_{n+1},B_{n+1})_{n\geqslant 0}
and
(\widehat{\xi}_{n},\widehat{A}_{n},\widehat{B}_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
, and that they are equal in law if and only if the driving chain
\Xi
is time-reversible and the modulation is symmetric, in the sense that
F_{ij}=F_{ji}
for all
i,j\in\mathscr{S}
.

Define
\widehat{\Psi}_{n}(x):=\widehat{A}_{n-1}x+\widehat{B}_{n-1}
for
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then the dual of the
MMLIFS

(\xi_{n},R_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
is the sequence
(\widehat{\xi}_{n},\widehat{R}_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
with

\widehat{R}_{n}\ :=\ \widehat{\Psi}_{n}(\widehat{R}_{n-1})\ =\ \widehat{\Psi}_{n}\cdots\widehat{\Psi}_{1}(\widehat{R}_{0}),\quad n=1,2,\ldots

(29)

The corresponding backward iterations are given by

\widehat{\Psi}_{1}\cdots\widehat{\Psi}_{n}(0)\ =\ \sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\widehat{\mathit{\Pi}}_{k}\widehat{B}_{k},\quad n=1,2,\ldots,

where
\widehat{\mathit{\Pi}}_{0}:=1
and
\widehat{\mathit{\Pi}}_{n}:=\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}\widehat{A}_{k}=\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}A_{-k}
for
n\geqslant 1
. If these iterations converge, then their limit clearly equals
R_{0}
as defined in (
18
), and the law of
R_{0}
coincides with the stationary distribution of the process
(\widehat{R}_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
. Moreover, by an application of Brandt’s result, the doubly infinite sequence
(\widehat{\xi}_{n},\widehat{R}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
is stationary when

\widehat{R}_{n}\ :=\ \sum_{k\geqslant 0}\widehat{A}_{n}\cdots\widehat{A}_{n-k+1}\widehat{B}_{n-k}\ =\ \sum_{k\geqslant 0}A_{-n}\cdots A_{-n+k-1}B_{-n+k}

for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Note that the definition of
\widehat{R}_{0}
coincides with (
17
).

Summarizing, let
\upsilon
and
\widehat{\upsilon}
denote the stationary distributions of the forward and dual sequences
(\xi_{n},R_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
and
(\widehat{\xi}_{n},\widehat{R}_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
, respectively. Then,

\upsilon\,=\,\mathscr{L}\left(\xi_{0},\sum_{n\geqslant 1}\widehat{\mathit{\Pi}}_{n-1}\widehat{B}_{n}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\widehat{\upsilon}\,=\,\mathscr{L}\left(\xi_{0},\sum_{n\geqslant 1}\mathit{\Pi}_{n-1}B_{n}\right),

(30)

where
\mathscr{L}(X)
denotes the law of the random variable
X
.

4.2
Induced Markov random walk and its dual

Define
S_{0}:=0
and
S_{n}:=\log|\mathit{\Pi}_{n}|
for
n\geqslant 1
. Then the sequence
(\xi_{n},S_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
forms a Markov random walk (
MRW
), as the increments
\zeta_{n}:=S_{n}-S_{n-1}=\log|A_{n}|
for
n\geqslant 1
are modulated by
\Xi
: they are conditionally independent given
\Xi
and have temporally homogeneous conditional distributions,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\zeta_{n}\in D\,|\,\Xi)\ =\ F_{\xi_{n-1},\xi_{n}}(\pm{\mathrm{e}}^{D}\times\mathbb{R}),

for measurable
D\subset\mathbb{R}
and all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, where
\pm{\mathrm{e}}^{D}:=\{\pm{\mathrm{e}}^{x}:x\in D\}
.
Equivalently, the bivariate process
\Xi^{\star}:=(\xi_{n},\zeta_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
, with
\zeta_{0}:=0
(for definiteness), defines a Markov chain on
\mathscr{S}\times\mathbb{R}
with transition kernel

Q(i,\{j\}\times D)\,:=\,\mathbb{P}_{i}[\xi_{1}=j,\log|A_{1}|\in D]\,=\,p_{ij}F_{ij}(\pm{\mathrm{e}}^{D}\times\mathbb{R}).

Hence, the law of
(\xi_{n},\zeta_{n})
conditional on the past depends only on
\xi_{n-1}
.
Moreover,
\Xi^{\star}
has stationary distribution
\pi^{\star}
given by

\pi^{\star}(\{j\}\times D)\ =\ \mathbb{P}_{\pi}[\xi_{1}=j,\log|A_{1}|\in D]\ =\ \sum_{i\in\mathscr{S}}\pi_{i}p_{ij}F_{ij}(\pm{\mathrm{e}}^{D}\times\mathbb{R}).

The
MRW

(\xi_{n},S_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
plays a central role in our analysis. Its renewal-theoretic properties – after a suitable change of measure
\mathbb{P}\rightsquigarrow\widehat{\mathbb{P}}
– are closely linked to the tail behavior of the stationary distribution of
(R_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
, in line with Goldie’s implicit renewal approach. Our extension addresses the case where the random walk is modulated by a finite Markov chain; see also
[
3
]
. The measure change is detailed in Subsection
4.4
.

Using (
15
), we can extend
(\xi_{n},S_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
to a doubly infinite sequence by setting

S_{n}\,:=\,\log|\mathit{\Pi}_{n}|\quad\text{for }n=-1,-2,\ldots,

and then define its time-reversed (dual) process as

(\widehat{\xi}_{n},\widehat{S}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\,:=\,(\xi_{-n},S_{-n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}.

In particular,
\widehat{S}_{0}=0
and

\widehat{S}_{n}\,=\,\log|\mathit{\Pi}_{-n}|\,=\,\log|\widehat{\mathit{\Pi}}_{n}|\,=\,\sum_{k=1}^{n}\widehat{\zeta}_{k}\quad\text{for }n\in\mathbb{N},

where
\widehat{\zeta}_{n}:=\log|\widehat{A}_{n-1}|=\log|A_{-n+1}|
. The associated Markov chain
(\widehat{\xi}_{n},\widehat{\zeta}_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
, with
\widehat{\zeta}_{0}:=0
, has transition kernel

\widehat{Q}(i,\{j\}\times D)\,=\,\widehat{p}_{ij}\widehat{F}_{ij}(\pm{\mathrm{e}}^{D}\times\mathbb{R})\,=\,\frac{\pi_{j}p_{ji}}{\pi_{i}}F_{ji}(\pm{\mathrm{e}}^{D}\times\mathbb{R}),

for
i,j\in\mathscr{S}
and measurable
D\subset\mathbb{R}
.

4.3
The Cramér transforms of
P
and its dual
\widehat{P}

The Cramér transforms of the transition matrix
P
and its dual
\widehat{P}
, along with their stochastic normalizations, play a central role in our analysis. In this subsection, we collect the relevant properties, focusing primarily on
P
, with comments on the necessary (but minor) modifications for
\widehat{P}
provided at the end.

The
Cramér transform
of
P
is defined as the matrix
\mathsf{P}(\theta)=(\mathsf{p}_{ij}(\theta))_{i,j\in\mathscr{S}}
, where

\mathsf{p}_{ij}(\theta)\ :=\ \mathbb{E}_{i}\Big[|A_{1}|^{\theta}\mathbf{1}_{\{\xi_{1}=j\}}\Big]\ =\ \mathbb{E}_{i}\Big[{\mathrm{e}}^{\theta S_{1}}\mathbf{1}_{\{\xi_{1}=j\}}\Big]\ =\ p_{ij}\Phi_{ij}(\theta),

(31)

for all
\theta\geqslant 0
, and where
\Phi_{ij}(\theta):=\mathbb{E}_{i}[{\mathrm{e}}^{\theta S_{1}}\,|\,\xi_{1}=j]
.

The entries of
\mathsf{P}(\theta)
are finite if and only if
\mathbb{E}_{i}[{\mathrm{e}}^{\theta S_{1}}]<\infty
for all
i\in\mathscr{S}
, which (since
\mathscr{S}
is finite) is equivalent to
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[{\mathrm{e}}^{\theta S_{1}}]<\infty
. The set of such
\theta\geqslant 0
defines the canonical domain
\mathbb{D}
of
\mathsf{P}(\theta)
. In fact,
\mathsf{P}(\cdot)
admits a holomorphic extension to the complex strip

\mathbb{D}_{\mathbb{C}}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\Re(z)\in\mathbb{D}\},

where
\Re(z)
denotes the real part of
z
. If Assumption
(B1)
holds, then
[0,\kappa]\subset\mathbb{D}
.
Note that
\mathsf{P}(0)=P
, and each entry
\mathsf{p}_{ij}(\theta)
is convex on
\mathbb{D}
, with derivative

\mathsf{p}_{ij}^{\prime}(\theta)\ :=\ \mathbb{E}_{i}\left[{\mathrm{e}}^{\theta S_{1}}S_{1}\mathbf{1}_{\{\xi_{1}=j\}}\right]\ =\ p_{ij}\Phi_{ij}^{\prime}(\theta),\quad i,j\in\mathscr{S}.

(32)

Furthermore, for any
i,j\in\mathscr{S}
,

\mathsf{p}_{ij}(\theta)\,>\,0\text{ for some }\theta\in\mathbb{D}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\mathsf{p}_{ij}(\theta)\,>\,0\text{ for all }\theta\in\mathbb{D},

(33)

and the same holds true for the entries
\mathsf{p}_{ij}^{n}(\theta)
for any
n\geqslant 2
.

Let
\rho(\theta)
denote the dominant eigenvalue of
\mathsf{P}(\theta)
, and let
u(\theta)=(u_{i}(\theta))_{i\in\mathscr{S}}
and
v(\theta)=(v_{i}(\theta))_{i\in\mathscr{S}}
be corresponding positive left and right eigenvectors, respectively, normalized such that

\sum_{i\in\mathscr{S}}u_{i}(\theta)\ =\ \sum_{i\in\mathscr{S}}u_{i}(\theta)v_{i}(\theta)=1.

Under
(B1)
and by Remark
3.2
, the matrix
\mathsf{P}(\kappa)
is irreducible with
\rho(\kappa)=1
. Hence, by (
33
),
\mathsf{P}(\theta)
for
any

\theta\in\mathbb{D}
is irreducible as well, and
\rho(\theta)>0
. It follows that the matrix
\rho(\theta)^{-1}\mathsf{P}(\theta)
is irreducible and nonnegative, with maximal eigenvalue 1 and the same eigenvectors (up to scaling) as
\mathsf{P}(\theta)
. As shown in
[
2
, p. 360]
, such a matrix – termed
quasistochastic
there – can be transformed into a proper irreducible stochastic matrix – referred to as the
normalized Cramér transform
– via

P(\theta)\ :=\ (p_{ij}(\theta))_{i,j\in\mathscr{S}}\ :=\ \frac{1}{\rho(\theta)}D(\theta)^{-1}\mathsf{P}(\theta)D(\theta)\ =\ \left(\frac{v_{j}(\theta)\mathsf{p}_{ij}(\theta)}{\rho(\theta)v_{i}(\theta)}\right)_{i,j\in\mathscr{S}},

(34)

where
D(\theta):=\textsl{diag}[v_{i}(\theta):i\in\mathscr{S}]
. Note that
p_{ij}(\theta)>0
if and only if
\mathsf{p}_{ij}(\theta)>0
.

The unique stationary distribution of
P(\theta)
– which is also its unique normalized positive left eigenvector – is given by

\pi(\theta)\ =\ D(\theta)u(\theta)\ =\ \big(u_{i}(\theta)v_{i}(\theta)\big)_{i\in\mathscr{S}}.

(35)

Let
\mathsf{p}_{ij}^{n}(\theta)
and
p_{ij}^{n}(\theta)
denote the
(i,j)
-entries of
\mathsf{P}(\theta)^{n}
and
P(\theta)^{n}
, respectively. Then, using (
34
), we obtain the relation

p_{ij}^{n}(\theta)\ =\ \frac{v_{j}(\theta)\,\mathsf{p}_{ij}^{n}(\theta)}{\rho(\theta)^{n}\,v_{i}(\theta)},

(36)

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
i,j\in\mathscr{S}
.

The following lemma extends Lemmata 4.1 and 4.2 from
[
3
]
to the more general setting considered here, where the modulating chain is not restricted to have only two states.

Lemma 4.1
.

For any
\theta\in\mathbb{D}
and
i,j\in\mathscr{S}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathsf{p}_{ij}^{n}(\theta)\,=\,\mathbb{E}_{i}\big[\mathit{\Pi}_{n}^{\theta}\mathbf{1}_{\{\xi_{n}=j\}}\big]\,=\,\mathbb{E}_{i}\big[{\mathrm{e}}^{\theta S_{n}}\mathbf{1}_{\{\xi_{n}=j\}}\big],

(37)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\sum_{j\in\mathscr{S}}\mathsf{p}_{ij}^{n}(\theta)\ =\ \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbb{E}_{i}[{\mathrm{e}}^{\theta S_{n}}]\ =\ \log\rho(\theta).

(38)

Moreover, the function
\theta\mapsto\log\rho(\theta)
is continuous and convex on its canonical domain
\mathbb{D}
, and smooth (i.e., infinitely differentiable) on the interior of
\mathbb{D}
. As a direct consequence, if
\rho(\kappa)=1
for some
\kappa>0
, then
\rho(\theta)<1
for
0<\theta<\kappa
.

To prove Lemma
4.1
, we will use the following proposition concerning the analyticity of the dominant eigenvalue
\rho(\theta)
of an analytic family of nonnegative matrices
\mathsf{P}(\theta)
, extended holomorphically to a complex domain. The result follows from the implicit function theorem for analytic maps, see e.g.
[
30
, Thm. 8.6]
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
\mathbb{D}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:0<\Re(z)<\theta_{0}\}
for some
\theta_{0}>0
, and let
\{\mathsf{P}(z):z\in\mathbb{D}\}
be a holomorphic family of matrices such that
\mathsf{P}(\theta)
is nonnegative with a positive (and hence simple) maximal eigenvalue
\rho(\theta)
for each real
\theta\in\mathbb{D}
. Then the function
\theta\mapsto\rho(\theta)
is infinitely differentiable on
(0,\theta_{0})
.

The identity in (
37
) can be established by induction on
n
. For the base case
n=1
, it follows directly from the definition of
\mathsf{p}_{ij}(\theta)
in (
31
). For the inductive step, assume the identity holds for some
n\geqslant 1
. Then

\displaystyle\mathsf{p}_{ij}^{n+1}(\theta)\

\displaystyle=\ \sum_{k\in\mathscr{S}}\mathsf{p}_{ik}(\theta)\mathsf{p}_{kj}^{n}(\theta)\ =\ \sum_{k\in\mathscr{S}}\mathbb{E}_{i}\left[{\mathrm{e}}^{\theta S_{1}}\mathbf{1}_{\{\xi_{1}=k\}}\right]\mathbb{E}_{k}\left[{\mathrm{e}}^{\theta S_{n}}\mathbf{1}_{\{\xi_{n}=j\}}\right]

\displaystyle=\ \sum_{k\in\mathscr{S}}\mathbb{E}_{i}\left[{\mathrm{e}}^{\theta S_{1}}\mathbf{1}_{\{\xi_{1}=k\}}\cdot{\mathrm{e}}^{\theta(S_{n+1}-S_{1})}\mathbf{1}_{\{\xi_{n+1}=j\}}\right]

\displaystyle=\ \mathbb{E}_{i}\left[{\mathrm{e}}^{\theta S_{n+1}}\mathbf{1}_{\{\xi_{n+1}=j\}}\right],

which completes the induction.

To prove (
38
), we assume for simplicity that the modulating chain
(\xi_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
, and hence
P
, is aperiodic. The extension to the periodic case via decomposition into cyclic classes is standard and omitted.

Under aperiodicity, the normalized Cramér transforms
P(\theta)
are themselves aperiodic for all
\theta\in\mathbb{D}
. Thus, by the ergodic theorem for finite Markov chains, we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}p_{ij}^{n}(\theta)\ =\ \pi_{j}(\theta)\ =\ u_{j}(\theta)v_{j}(\theta)\ >\ 0

for all
i,j\in\mathscr{S}
. Applying this to the representation in (
36
) gives

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\log\sum_{j\in\mathscr{S}}\mathsf{p}_{ij}^{n}(\theta)\

\displaystyle=\ \log\rho(\theta)+\frac{1}{n}\log\sum_{j\in\mathscr{S}}\frac{p_{ij}^{n}(\theta)v_{i}(\theta)}{v_{j}(\theta)}

\displaystyle=\ \log\rho(\theta)+\frac{1}{n}\log\sum_{j\in\mathscr{S}}\left(u_{j}(\theta)v_{i}(\theta)+o(1)\right)

\displaystyle=\ \log\rho(\theta)+o(1),

as
n\to\infty
, which proves (
38
).

Convexity of the function
\theta\mapsto\log\rho(\theta)
follows directly from (
38
) and standard properties of logarithmic moment generating functions (see also
[
19
, Cor. 2]
). Smoothness of
\rho(\theta)
on the interior of
\mathbb{D}
is a classical result, and follows from Proposition
4.2
.
∎

Turning to the dual transition matrix
\widehat{P}
, it can easily be verified that the associated Cramér transform
\widehat{\mathsf{P}}(\theta)
is related to
\mathsf{P}(\theta)
by

\widehat{\mathsf{P}}(\theta)\ =\ \mathit{\Pi}(\theta)^{-1}\mathsf{P}(\theta)^{\top}\mathit{\Pi}(\theta)\ =\ \bigg(\frac{\pi_{j}(\theta)\mathsf{p}_{ji}(\theta)}{\pi_{i}(\theta)}\bigg)_{i,j\in\mathscr{S}},

where
\mathit{\Pi}(\theta):=\textsl{diag}[\pi_{i}(\theta):i\in\mathscr{S}]
. The matrix
\widehat{\mathsf{P}}(\theta)
has the same dominant eigenvalue
\rho(\theta)
as
\mathsf{P}(\theta)
, but its left and right eigenvectors are different. Specifically,

\displaystyle\widehat{u}(\theta)\ =\ \mathit{\Pi}(\theta)v(\theta)\ =\ (\pi_{i}(\theta)v_{i}(\theta))_{i\in\mathscr{S}}\ =\ (u_{i}(\theta)v_{i}(\theta)^{2})_{i\in\mathscr{S}},

(39)

and

\displaystyle\widehat{v}(\theta)\ =\ u(\theta)^{\top}\mathit{\Pi}(\theta)^{-1}\ =\ (v_{i}(\theta)^{-1})_{i\in\mathscr{S}},

(40)

under the normalization
\sum_{i\in\mathscr{S}}\widehat{u}_{i}(\theta)=\widehat{u}(\theta)^{\top}\widehat{v}(\theta)=1
.

Analogous to (
34
), the stochastic normalization of the quasistochastic matrix
\rho(\theta)^{-1}\widehat{\mathsf{P}}(\theta)
is given by

\widehat{P}(\theta)\ :=\ \frac{1}{\rho(\theta)}\widehat{D}(\theta)^{-1}\widehat{\mathsf{P}}(\theta)\widehat{D}(\theta)\ =\ \frac{1}{\rho(\theta)}\widehat{D}(\theta)^{-1}\,\mathit{\Pi}(\theta)^{-1}\mathsf{P}(\theta)^{\top}\mathit{\Pi}(\theta)\,\widehat{D}(\theta),

where

\widehat{D}(\theta)\ :=\ \textsl{diag}[\pi_{i}(\theta)^{-1}u_{i}(\theta):i\in\mathscr{S}],

so that

\mathit{\Pi}(\theta)\widehat{D}(\theta)\ =\ \textsl{diag}[u_{i}(\theta):i\in\mathscr{S}].

Finally, since
\widehat{u}_{i}(\theta)\widehat{v}_{i}(\theta)=v_{i}(\theta)u_{i}(\theta)=\pi_{i}(\theta)
for all
i\in\mathscr{S}
, it follows that
\widehat{P}(\theta)
and
P(\theta)
have the same stationary distribution
\pi(\theta)
.

4.4
Measure change

Let
\mathbb{F}=(\mathscr{F}_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
be the natural filtration of
(\xi_{n},A_{n},B_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
and put
\mathscr{F}_{\infty}:=\sigma\{\mathscr{F}_{n}:n\geqslant 0\}
.
Note that
(\xi_{n},\zeta_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
and the
MRW

(\xi_{n},S_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
are
\mathscr{F}
-adapted.

Assuming
(B1)
– and thus in particular
\rho(\kappa)=1
for some positive
\kappa\in\mathbb{D}
– the convexity of
\rho
implies that
\rho(\theta)<1
for all
\theta\in(0,\kappa)
,
\rho^{\prime}(0)<0
, and
\rho^{\prime}(\kappa)>0
. Recall from Remark
3.2
that
\mathsf{P}(\kappa)
is irreducible, and by (
33
) the same holds for each
\mathsf{P}(\theta)
,
\theta\in\mathbb{D}
. Hence, the stochastic normalization (see (
34
))

P(\theta)\ =\ D(\theta)^{-1}\mathsf{P}(\theta)D(\theta)\ =\ \Bigg(\frac{\mathsf{p}_{ij}(\theta)v_{j}(\theta)}{v_{i}(\theta)}\Bigg)_{i,j\in\mathscr{S}}

is an irreducible transition matrix with unique stationary law
\pi(\theta)=(u_{i}(\theta)v_{i}(\theta))_{i\in\mathscr{S}}
(see (
35
)) for all
positive

\theta\in\mathbb{D}
.

For any such
\theta
and
i\in\mathscr{S}
, we have that

\left({\mathrm{e}}^{\theta S_{n}}v_{\xi_{n}}(\theta)\rho(\theta)^{-n}\right)_{n\geqslant 0}

is a positive
\mathscr{F}
-martingale under any
\mathbb{P}_{s}
,
s\in\mathscr{S}
. Indeed, for any
i\in\mathscr{S}
, on the event
\{\xi_{n}=i\}\in\mathscr{F}_{n}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{e^{\theta S_{n+1}}v_{\xi_{n+1}}(\theta)}{\rho(\theta)^{n+1}}\bigg|\mathscr{F}_{n}\bigg]

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{\theta S_{n}}}{\rho(\theta)^{n+1}}\mathbb{E}_{i}\left[{\mathrm{e}}^{\theta S_{1}}v_{\xi_{1}}(\theta)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{\theta S_{n}}}{\rho(\theta)^{n+1}}\sum_{j\in\mathscr{S}}\mathsf{p}_{ij}(\theta)v_{j}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{\theta S_{n}}v_{i}(\theta)}{\rho(\theta)^{n}}\sum_{j\in\mathscr{S}}p_{ij}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{\theta S_{n}}v_{i}(\theta)}{\rho(\theta)^{n}},

where Lemma
4.1
has been used in the second step, and (
36
) in the third.

Using this, we now define a new probability measure
\mathbb{P}_{i}^{(\theta)}
on
(\Omega,\mathscr{F}_{\infty})
as follows: for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and bounded measurable function

f:\mathscr{S}\times(\mathscr{S}\times\mathbb{R}_{\scriptscriptstyle>}\times\mathbb{R})^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, set

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}_{i}^{(\theta)}&\big[f(\xi_{0},(\xi_{1},A_{1},B_{1}),\ldots,(\xi_{n},A_{n},B_{n}))\big]\\
&:=\ \frac{1}{v_{i}(\theta)\rho(\theta)^{n}}\,\mathbb{E}_{i}\big[e^{\theta S_{n}}v_{\xi_{n}}(\theta)f(\xi_{0},(\xi_{1},A_{1},B_{1}),\ldots,(\xi_{n},A_{n},B_{n}))\big].\end{split}

(41)

As stated in the next lemma, the process
(\xi_{n},\zeta_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
remains a Markov chain on
\mathscr{S}\times\mathbb{R}
under the changed measure
\mathbb{P}_{i}^{(\theta)}
, but now with a modified transition kernel
Q^{(\theta)}
given by

Q^{(\theta)}g(i,x)\,:=\,\frac{1}{v_{i}(\theta)\rho(\theta)}\,\mathbb{E}_{i}\Big[e^{\theta\zeta_{1}}v_{\xi_{1}}(\theta)g(\xi_{1},\zeta_{1})\Big]

(42)

for all bounded functions
g:\mathscr{S}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
. Hence, the conditional law of
(\xi_{1},\zeta_{1})
given the initial state
(i,x)
depends only on
i
and not on
x
.

Lemma 4.3
.

For any
\theta\in\mathbb{D}
, the following assertions hold under the new probability measures
\mathbb{P}_{i}^{(\theta)}
,
i\in\mathscr{S}
:

(a)

The sequence
(\xi_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
is an irreducible Markov chain on
\mathscr{S}
with transition matrix
P(\theta)
as defined in (
34
) and unique stationary distribution
\pi(\theta)
given by (
35
).

(b)

The sequence
(\xi_{n},\zeta_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
is a Markov chain with transition operator
Q^{(\theta)}
. Its first component has initial state
i
under
\mathbb{P}_{i}^{(\theta)}
and is stationary under
\mathbb{P}^{(\theta)}:=\sum_{i}\pi_{i}(\theta)\mathbb{P}_{i}^{(\theta)}
.

(c)

The sequence
(\xi_{n},A_{n},B_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
is stationary under
\mathbb{P}^{(\theta)}
.

(d)

The sequence
(\xi_{n},S_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
is a
MRW
with driving chain
(\xi_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
and
S_{0}=0
.

(e)

The stationary drift of
(\xi_{n},S_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
under
\mathbb{P}^{(\theta)}
equals

\mathbb{E}^{(\theta)}S_{1}\ =\ \frac{\rho^{\prime}(\theta)}{\rho(\theta)}\ =\ \sum_{i,j\in\mathscr{S}}\frac{u_{i}(\theta)v_{j}(\theta)}{\rho(\theta)}\,\mathbb{E}_{i}\Big[|A_{1}|^{\theta}\log|A_{1}|\mathbf{1}_{\{\xi_{1}=j\}}\Big]\ =\ u(\theta)^{\top}P^{\prime}(\theta)v(\theta).

(43)

This drift is finite for all
\theta
in the interior of
\mathbb{D}
, and negative at
\theta=0
, i.e., under
\mathbb{P}^{(0)}=\mathbb{P}_{\pi}
. In fact
\mathbb{E}S_{1}=\mathbb{E}\log|A_{1}|=\rho^{\prime}(0)
.

(f)

If
\kappa>0
exists such that
\rho(\kappa)=1
, then the stationary drift under
\mathbb{P}^{(\kappa)}
is positive, but it can be infinite if
\kappa
is a boundary point of
\mathbb{D}
. In the latter case, (
43
) still holds with
\rho^{\prime}(\kappa):=\lim_{\theta\uparrow\kappa}\rho^{\prime}(\theta)
.

Except for part (c), the proof of this lemma is omitted, as it follows with only minor modifications from the proofs of Lemmata 4.3 and 4.4 in
[
3
]
, which treat the case
|\mathscr{S}|=2
.
To derive the second equality in (
43
), one simply uses the definition of
\mathbb{P}^{(\theta)}
, namely,

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}^{(\theta)}&f(\xi_{0},(\xi_{1},A_{1},B_{1}),\ldots,(\xi_{n},A_{n},B_{n}))\\
&=\sum_{i\in\mathscr{S}}\frac{u_{i}(\theta)}{\rho(\theta)^{n}}\,\mathbb{E}_{i}{\mathrm{e}}^{\theta S_{n}}v_{\xi_{n}}(\theta)f(\xi_{0},(\xi_{1},A_{1},B_{1}),\ldots,(\xi_{n},A_{n},B_{n})),\end{split}

(44)

for any bounded measurable function
f
. Combined with the definition of
\pi(\theta)
, this yields the expression for
\mathbb{E}^{(\theta)}S_{1}
.

We now turn to part (c). Define, for
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
i_{0},\ldots,i_{n}\in\mathscr{S}
,
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
, and any bounded measurable function
f:\mathscr{S}\times(\mathscr{S}\times\mathbb{R})^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\Gamma f(i_{0},(i_{k},x_{k})_{k=1,\ldots,n})\,:=\,\mathbb{E}_{i_{0}}\big[f(\xi_{0},(\xi_{k},A_{k},B_{k})_{k=1,\ldots,n})\,\big|\,(\xi_{k},\zeta_{k})=(i_{k},x_{k}),\,k=1,\ldots,n\big].

Then, using the stationarity of
(\xi_{n},\zeta_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
under
\mathbb{P}^{(\theta)}
from part (b), and recalling that
\pi_{i}(\theta)=u_{i}(\theta)v_{i}(\theta)
for
i\in\mathscr{S}
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\pi}^{(\theta)}

\displaystyle f(\xi_{m},(\xi_{m+k},A_{m+k},B_{m+k})_{k=1,\ldots,n})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\mathscr{S}}\frac{u_{i}(\theta)}{\rho(\theta)^{m+n}}\mathbb{E}_{i}\Big[{\mathrm{e}}^{\theta S_{m+n}}v_{\xi_{m+n}}(\theta)\,\Gamma f(\xi_{m},(\xi_{m+k},\zeta_{m+k})_{k=1,\ldots,n})\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}^{(\theta)}\Gamma f(\xi_{m},(\xi_{m+k},\zeta_{m+k})_{k=1,\ldots,n})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}^{(\theta)}\Gamma f(\xi_{0},(\xi_{k},\zeta_{k})_{k=1,\ldots,n})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}^{(\theta)}f(\xi_{0},(\xi_{k},A_{k},B_{k})_{k=1,\ldots,n}),

where the third equality follows by stationarity of
(\xi_{n},\zeta_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
. This shows that the law of
(\xi_{n},A_{n},B_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
under
\mathbb{P}^{(\theta)}
is invariant under time shifts, i.e., the sequence is stationary.
∎

Choosing the dual filtration as
\widehat{\mathscr{F}}_{0}:=\mathscr{F}_{0}
,

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathscr{F}}_{n}\,:=\,\sigma\big\{\widehat{\xi}_{0},(\widehat{\xi}_{k},\widehat{A}_{k},\widehat{B}_{k}):1\leqslant k\leqslant n\big\}\,=\,\sigma\big\{\xi_{0},(\xi_{-k},A_{-k+1},B_{-k+1}):1\leqslant k\leqslant n\big\}

for
n\geqslant 1
, and
\widehat{\mathscr{F}}_{\infty}:=\sigma\{\widehat{\mathscr{F}}_{n},n\geqslant 0\}
, we define the
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{i}^{(\theta)}
on
(\Omega,\widehat{\mathscr{F}}_{\infty})
by

\begin{split}\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{i}^{(\theta)}&f\big(\widehat{\xi}_{0},(\widehat{\xi}_{1},\widehat{A}_{1},\widehat{B}_{1}),\ldots,(\widehat{\xi}_{n},\widehat{A}_{n},\widehat{B}_{n})\big)\\
&:=\ \frac{\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{i}e^{\theta\widehat{S}_{n}}\widehat{v}_{\widehat{\xi}_{n}}(\theta)f(\widehat{\xi}_{0},(\widehat{\xi}_{1},\widehat{A}_{1},\widehat{B}_{1}),\ldots,(\widehat{\xi}_{n},\widehat{A}_{n},\widehat{B}_{n}))}{\widehat{v}_{i}(\theta)\rho(\theta)^{n}}\\
&=\ \frac{\mathbb{E}_{i}{\mathrm{e}}^{\theta S_{-n}}\widehat{v}_{\xi_{-n}}(\theta)f(\xi_{0},(\xi_{-1},A_{0},B_{0}),\ldots,(\xi_{-n},A_{-n+1},B_{-n+1}))}{\widehat{v}_{i}(\theta)\rho(\theta)^{n}}\\
&=\ \frac{v_{i}(\theta)}{\rho(\theta)^{n}}\,\mathbb{E}_{i}\Bigg[\frac{e^{\theta S_{-n}}f(\xi_{0},(\xi_{-1},A_{0},B_{0}),\ldots,(\xi_{-n},A_{-n+1},B_{-n+1}))}{v_{\xi_{-n}}(\theta)}\Bigg].\end{split}

(45)

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and bounded
f
as in (
41
), and for
\theta\in\mathbb{D}
and
i\in\mathscr{S}

(note that (
40
) has been utilized to get the last line). These probability measures form the exact dual analogs of the
\mathbb{P}_{i}^{(\theta)}
, and a dual analog of the previous lemma for the
MRW

(\widehat{\xi}_{n},\widehat{S}_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
and the associated sequence
(\widehat{\xi}_{n},\widehat{\zeta}_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
under the
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{i}^{(\theta)}
can now be stated in a straightforward manner. We refrain from doing so, but point out that the stationary law of
(\widehat{\xi}_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
under the
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{i}^{(\theta)}
is also
\pi(\theta)
and that (as a consequence)
(\widehat{\xi}_{n},\widehat{\zeta}_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
is a stationary Markov chain under
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}^{(\theta)}:=\sum_{i\in\mathscr{S}}\pi_{i}(\theta)\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{i}^{(\theta)}
, where

\begin{split}&\widehat{\mathbb{E}}^{(\theta)}f\big(\widehat{\xi}_{0},(\widehat{\xi}_{1},\widehat{A}_{1},\widehat{B}_{1}),\ldots,(\widehat{\xi}_{n},\widehat{A}_{n},\widehat{B}_{n})\big)\\
&=\ \widehat{\mathbb{E}}^{(\theta)}f\big(\xi_{0},(\xi_{-1},A_{0},B_{0}),\ldots,(\xi_{-n},A_{-n+1},B_{-n+1})\big)\\
&=\ \sum_{i\in\mathscr{S}}\frac{\pi_{i}(\theta)}{\widehat{v}_{i}(\theta)\rho(\theta)^{n}}\,\mathbb{E}_{i}e^{\theta S_{-n}}\widehat{v}_{\xi_{-n}}(\theta)f(\xi_{0},(\xi_{-1},A_{0},B_{0}),\ldots,(\xi_{-n},A_{-n+1},B_{-n+1}))\\
&=\ \sum_{i\in\mathscr{S}}\frac{v_{i}(\theta)\pi_{i}(\theta)}{\rho(\theta)^{n}}\,\mathbb{E}_{i}\Bigg[\frac{e^{\theta S_{-n}}f(\xi_{0},(\xi_{-1},A_{0},B_{0}),\ldots,(\xi_{-n},A_{-n+1},B_{-n+1}))}{v_{\xi_{-n}}(\theta)}\Bigg].\end{split}

(46)

for bounded functions
f
. Moreover, the sequence
(\widehat{\xi}_{n},\widehat{A}_{n-1},\widehat{B}_{n-1})_{n\geqslant 1}
is stationary under
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}^{(\theta)}
and
\widehat{\mathbb{E}}^{(\theta)}\widehat{S}_{1}=\mathbb{E}^{(\theta)}S_{1}
for any
\theta\in\mathbb{D}
.

4.5
Lattice-type of
(\xi_{n},S_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
and its dual

Let
\kappa
be given by
(B1)
. By definition, the

is nonlattice under
\mathbb{P}^{(\kappa)}
if

\mathbb{P}^{(\kappa)}\big[\zeta_{1}-a_{\xi_{1}}+a_{\xi_{0}}\in\,d\mathbb{Z}\big]\,<\,1

for any
d>0
and any
\{a_{i}:i\in\mathscr{S}\}\subset[0,d)
, see e.g.

. As one can easily see, this is equivalent to

, and by the same reasoning,

.

5
Condition
(B1)
: Making life easier by geometric sampling

Given assumptions
(B1–4)
of Theorem
3.1
, we now show that in
(B1)
the irreducible transition matrix
P
of the driving chain, as well as its Cramér transform
\mathsf{P}(\kappa)
, may be assumed to be
positive
without loss of generality. That is, we may assume that

p_{ij}>0\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{P}_{i}[A_{1}\neq 0\mid\xi_{1}=j]=F_{ij}(\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\})>0\quad\text{for all }i,j\in\mathscr{S}.

Since
\mathsf{P}(\theta)
is positive if and only if its normalization
P(\theta)
is positive, we focus on the latter, as its entries are transition probabilities and thus admit a probabilistic interpretation.

Furthermore, we show that if

\mathbb{P}[A_{1}>0]\wedge\mathbb{P}[A_{1}<0]>0,

then the transition probabilities

p_{ij}^{-}(\kappa):=\mathbb{P}_{i}^{(\kappa)}[\xi_{1}=j,A_{1}<0]\quad\text{and}\quad p_{ij}^{+}(\kappa):=\mathbb{P}_{i}^{(\kappa)}[\xi_{1}=j,A_{1}>0]

(47)

can also be assumed to be positive. All these conclusions extend to the dual quantities
\widehat{P}
,
\widehat{P}(\kappa)
, and
\widehat{p}_{ij}^{\,\pm}(\kappa)
as well. The key tool is
geometric sampling
.

Let
(\vartheta_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
be a doubly infinite sequence of random times, independent of
(\xi_{n},A_{n},B_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
, with
\vartheta_{0}=0
and i.i.d. increments
\vartheta_{n}-\vartheta_{n-1}
having a geometric distribution:

\mathbb{P}[\vartheta_{n}-\vartheta_{n-1}=k]=\frac{1}{2^{k}}\quad\text{for all }k\in\mathbb{N},\;n\in\mathbb{Z}.

Given the stationary
MMLIFS

(\xi_{n},R_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
, we define the geometrically sampled process

(\xi_{n}^{*},R_{n}^{*}):=(\xi_{\vartheta_{n}},R_{\vartheta_{n}})\quad n\geqslant 0,

and note, without proof, that this sequence is again a stationary
MMLIFS
with the same marginal law and associated sequence
(A_{n}^{*},B_{n}^{*})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
defined by

(A_{n}^{*},B_{n}^{*}):=\left(A_{\vartheta_{n-1}+1}\cdots A_{\vartheta_{n}},\;\Psi_{\vartheta_{n}}\circ\cdots\circ\Psi_{\vartheta_{n-1}+1}(0)\right),

modulated by
(\xi_{n}^{*})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
P^{*}=(p_{ij}^{*})_{i,j\in\mathscr{S}}
be the transition matrix of
(\xi_{n}^{*})_{n\geqslant 0}
under
\mathbb{P}=\mathbb{P}^{(0)}
, and let
P^{*}(\theta)=(p_{ij}^{*}(\theta))_{i,j\in\mathscr{S}}
denote its normalized Cramér transform. Define also

p_{ij}^{*\pm}(\theta):=\mathbb{P}_{i}^{(\theta)}[\xi_{1}^{*}=j,\;A_{1}^{*}\gtrless 0]\quad\text{for }\theta\in[0,\kappa],

and let
\widehat{P}^{*}
,
\widehat{P}^{*}(\theta)
, and
\widehat{p}_{ij}^{*\pm}(\theta)
denote the corresponding quantities for the geometrically sampled dual chain

Keeping the starred notation from the previous section, suppose now that

7
Exponential smoothing

Given a measurable function
f:\mathscr{S}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
f(i,\cdot)
is Lebesgue integrable for each
i\in\mathscr{S}
, we define
\overline{f}:\mathscr{S}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, called
exponential smoothing of
f
, as in
[
23
]
(when fixing
i
) by

\overline{f}(i,t)\ :=\ \int_{(-\infty,t]}{\mathrm{e}}^{-(t-x)}f(i,x)\,\operatorname{d}\hskip-1.0ptx\ =\ \mathbb{E}\big[f(i,t-Z)\big],\quad i\in\mathscr{S},

where
Z
denotes a standard exponential random variable. The main properties of exponential smoothing are summarized in the following lemma.

Lemma 7.1
.

Given
f,\overline{f}
as above, the following assertions hold:

(ES-1)

For each
i\in\mathscr{S}
, the function
\overline{f}(i,\cdot)
is directly Riemann integrable, and

\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(i,x)\,\operatorname{d}\hskip-1.0ptx\ =\ \int_{\mathbb{R}}\overline{f}(i,x)\,\operatorname{d}\hskip-1.0ptx.

(ES-2)

If
f
satisfies the Markov renewal equation
f=g+f*Q
for a bounded function
g
and a finite kernel
Q
from
\mathscr{S}
to
\mathscr{S}\times\mathbb{R}
, that is,

f(i,t)\ =\ g(i,t)\ +\ \int_{\mathscr{S}\times\mathbb{R}}f(s,t-x)\,Q(i,\operatorname{d}\hskip-1.0pts\times\operatorname{d}\hskip-1.0ptx)\quad\text{for all }(i,t)\in\mathscr{S}\times\mathbb{R},

then so does
\overline{f}
, namely

\overline{f}\,=\,\overline{g}+\overline{f*Q}\,=\,\overline{g}+\overline{f}*Q.

(ES-1)
is proved in
[
23
, Lemma 9.2]
. To show
(ES-2)
, observe that

\displaystyle\overline{f}(i,t)

\displaystyle=\ \mathbb{E}f(i,t-Z)

\displaystyle=\ \mathbb{E}g(i,t-Z)\ +\ \mathbb{E}\int_{\mathscr{S}\times\mathbb{R}}f(s,t-x-Z)\ Q(i,\operatorname{d}\hskip-1.0pts\times\operatorname{d}\hskip-1.0ptx)

\displaystyle=\ \overline{g}(i,t)\ +\ \int_{\mathscr{S}\times\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{E}f(s,t-x-Z)\ Q(i,\operatorname{d}\hskip-1.0pts\times\operatorname{d}\hskip-1.0ptx)

\displaystyle=\ \overline{g}(i,t)\ +\ \int_{\mathscr{S}\times\mathbb{R}}\overline{f}(s,t-x)\ Q(i,\operatorname{d}\hskip-1.0pts\times\operatorname{d}\hskip-1.0ptx).

for all
(i,t)\in\mathscr{S}\times\mathbb{R}
.
∎

It has been shown in
[
23
, Lemma 9.3]
that, for any random variable
X
and
\kappa>0
, the implication

\lim_{s\to\infty}\frac{1}{s}\int_{0}^{s}u^{\kappa}\,\mathbb{P}[X>u]\,\operatorname{d}\hskip-1.0ptu\ =\ C\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\lim_{s\to\infty}s^{\kappa}\,\mathbb{P}[X>s]\ =\ C

holds true. The connection with exponential smoothing becomes apparent by observing that this implication is equivalent to

\lim_{t\to\infty}\overline{f}(t)\ =\ C\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\lim_{t\to\infty}f(t)\ =\ C

(48)

where
f(t):={\mathrm{e}}^{\kappa t}\,\mathbb{P}[X>{\mathrm{e}}^{t}]
. In the present context, we apply this equivalence to the family of functions
f(i,t):={\mathrm{e}}^{\kappa t}\,\mathbb{P}_{i}[X>{\mathrm{e}}^{t}]
,
i\in\mathscr{S}
, with
C_{i}
in place of
C
.

\kappa\in(2^{\,n},2^{\,n+1}]

-a.s. convergence by
[
13
, Rem. 4.2]
. To prove the latter, note first that, by a generalization of Wald’s equation (see, e.g.,
[
6
, Cor. XI.2.6]
), (
81
) implies

\mathbb{E}\zeta_{1}\,=\,\frac{\mathbb{E}\zeta_{T_{1}}}{\mathbb{E}T_{1}}\,>\,0,

and hence
\lim_{t\to\infty}\zeta_{t}=\infty

\mathbb{P}_{\pi}
-a.s. Together with
(A4)
we can therefore conclude that the long-term mean
\kappa_{\zeta}
of
\zeta
(see, e.g.,
[
13
, Eq. (3.6)]
for its explicit definition) is positive and finite. From this, we immediately obtain for the dual process that
\lim_{t\to\infty}-\widehat{\zeta}_{t}=\infty

\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{\pi}
-a.s. and
0<\kappa_{-\widehat{\zeta}}<\infty
. Thus, by
[
13
, Prop. 5.2 and 5.7.1]
, we obtain the desired convergence of
\int_{(0,t]}{\mathrm{e}}^{\widehat{\zeta}_{s-}}\,\operatorname{d}\hskip-1.0pt\widehat{L}_{s}
provided that

\int_{(1,\infty)}\log(q)\ \mathbb{P}_{j}\Big[\sup_{0<t\leq\tau(j)}{\mathrm{e}}^{\widehat{\zeta}_{t-}}\,|\Delta(-\widehat{L}^{\sharp}_{t})|\in\,\operatorname{d}\hskip-1.0ptq\Big]\ <\ \infty,

(82)

where
\tau(j):=\inf\{t>0:J_{t}=j\neq J_{t-}\}
is the first return time of
J_{t}
to
j
, and

\widehat{L}^{\sharp}_{t}\ =\ \sum_{0<s\leq t}\Delta\widehat{L}_{s}\mathds{1}_{|\Delta\widehat{L}_{s}|>1}+\sum_{i,j\in\mathscr{S}}\sum_{n:T_{n}\leq t}Z_{\widehat{L},n}^{ij}

(83)

is a finite-variation pure-jump process.
By duality, we may replace
(\widehat{\zeta},\widehat{L}^{\sharp})
in (
82
) with
(-\zeta,-L^{\sharp})
, where
L^{\sharp}
is defined analogously to (
83
).
Further, from (
6
),

{\mathrm{e}}^{-\zeta_{t-}}\Delta L_{t}\,=\,{\mathrm{e}}^{-\zeta_{t}}\Delta\eta_{t}.

Thus, (
82
) is equivalent to

\int_{(1,\infty)}\log(q)\,\mathbb{P}_{j}\bigg[\sup_{0<t\leq\tau(j)}{\mathrm{e}}^{-\zeta_{t}}\,|\Delta\eta^{\sharp}_{t}|\in\,\operatorname{d}\hskip-1.0ptq\bigg]\ <\ \infty,

where
\eta^{\sharp}
is a finite-variation pure-jump process defined analogously to (
83
). Now, by
[
12
, Cor. 3.9]
, Assumption
(A3)
implies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\log^{+}|\eta_{1}^{(j)}|\,<\,\infty\quad\text{for all }j\in\mathscr{S}

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\log^{+}|Z^{ij}_{\eta}|\,<\,\infty\quad\text{for all }i,j\in\mathscr{S}\text{ with }q_{ij}>0,

while
(A4)
ensures finite moments for
\zeta
. Hence
(V_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
admits a unique stationary law. As this law coincides with
\nu
, the proof is complete. ∎

Appendix. The occupation measure formula

In the following, we provide a version of the
occupation measure formula
that is not the most general one, but is tailored to our needs.

Consider a probability space
(\Omega,\mathscr{F},\mathbb{P})
together with a filtration
\mathscr{F}=(\mathscr{F}_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
and a Markov chain
(M_{n},\mathscr{F}_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
with general state space
(\mathscr{X},\mathfrak{X})
. As before, we write
\mathbb{P}_{x}
for
\mathbb{P}(\,\cdot\,|\,M_{0}=x)
and define
\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}=\int_{\mathscr{X}}\mathbb{P}_{x}\,\lambda(dx)
for any probability measure
\lambda
on
(\mathscr{X},\mathfrak{X})
.
Let
\sigma\geqslant 1
be a stopping time with respect to
\mathscr{F}
and
\lambda
a distribution on
\mathscr{X}
such that

\mathsf{m}\,:=\,\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}\sigma\,<\,\infty\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}(M_{\sigma}\in\cdot)\,=\,\lambda.

Then the normalized occupation measure

\pi(A)\ =\ \frac{1}{\mathsf{m}}\,\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}\Bigg[\sum_{n=1}^{\sigma}\mathbf{1}_{A}(M_{n})\Bigg],\quad A\in\mathfrak{X}

(84)

defines a stationary distribution of
(M_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
. Equivalently,

\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}\Bigg[\sum_{n=1}^{\sigma}h(M_{n})\Bigg]\ =\ \mathsf{m}\,\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}h(M_{0})\ =\ \mathsf{m}\int h(x)\,\pi(dx)

(85)

for any measurable
h:\mathscr{S}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
such that at least one of

\mathbb{E}^{(\theta)}h^{-}(M_{0})
or
\mathbb{E}^{(\theta)}h^{+}(M_{0})
is finite. The formulas remain valid when summing over
n=0,\dots,\sigma-1
.

For the
MMLIFS

(\xi_{n},R_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
, which is stationary Markov chain under
\mathbb{P}_{\pi}
, we can choose
\sigma=\tau(i):=\inf\{n\geqslant 1:\xi_{n}=i\}
for arbitrary
i\in\mathscr{S}
. Then
\mathsf{m}=\mathsf{m}_{i}:=\mathbb{E}_{i}\tau(i)<\infty
by the positive recurrence of
(\xi_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
. Hence, formula (
84
) provides

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\pi}[(\xi_{0},R_{0})\in\cdot]\ =\ \frac{1}{\mathsf{m}_{i}}\,\mathbb{E}_{i}\Bigg[\sum_{n=0}^{\tau(i)-1}\mathbf{1}_{\{(\xi_{n},R_{n})\in\cdot\}}\Bigg],

(86)

in particular

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\pi}[R_{0}\in\cdot]\ =\ \frac{1}{\mathsf{m}_{i}}\,\mathbb{E}_{i}\Bigg[\sum_{n=0}^{\tau(i)-1}\mathbf{1}_{\{R_{n}\in\cdot\}}\Bigg]

(87)

for any
i\in\mathscr{S}
.
