Adsorption based on excitation
Authors: Shi-Chao Qi, Yan-Yan Han, Yong-Lan Liu, Yun-Jie Zhao, Qi-Qi Huang, Xiao-Qin Liu, Lin-Bing Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTraditionally, adsorption is believed to take place only in the ground state, but here we report an excitation-driven adsorption mode. Composites of metallophthalocyanines and carbonaceous substrates are prepared, among which the potassium-phthalocyanine (KPc)/reduced graphene oxide (RG) composite shows the most typical behavior. Femtosecond transient absorption spectroscopy and first-principles simulations reveal that under ultraviolet (UV) irradiation, the excited electrons in the composite transfer efficiently from KPc to RG, leaving long-lived excited holes on KPc that enable selective adsorption of CO molecules. In the dark, the composite shows negligible CO adsorption (0.06 mmol g⁻1), whereas its CO uptake increases to 1.20 mmol g⁻1 upon UV light, with a UV-modulated adsorption capacity ratio of up to 95%. This adsorption mode is also applicable to other metallophthalocyanine/carbonaceous composites. This work presents a potential strategy for photoexcitation utilization and the development of energy-efficient chemical separation technologies.Subject terms: Chemical engineering, Optical properties and devices, Porous materials

Introduction

In 1878, J. W. Gibbs first proposed the adsorption equation1, stating that adsorption arises from changes in interfacial concentration to minimize surface tension. Since then, adsorption processes, known for their low energy consumption, have been widely used, especially in chemical separation dominated by distillation, which accounts for 10–15% of global energy consumption2–4. Accordingly, designing and preparing sorbents with high adsorption capacity and selectivity has become a research focus5–8. Various synthetic strategies and industrial applications of sorbents are fundamentally based on Gibbs adsorption theory. Traditionally, adsorption was believed to occur only in the ground state mode9. In the ground state, sorbents function through well-defined open frameworks, specific pore sizes, or tailored chemical sites. For example, 3 A zeolite with open 8-membered ring windows in α-cages is used for gas drying and the separation of n-paraffins from n-/iso-paraffin mixtures10,11. Zeolites and porous carbons with abundant micropores are suitable for air separation or CO₂ capture12,13. Introducing alkaline sites into sorbents promotes acidic gas adsorption, and zeolites containing Ag(I) or Cu(I) cations can separate olefins from olefin/paraffin mixtures14–17. All the above applications of Gibbs adsorption theory are limited to the ground state of sorbents.

In recent years, studies have attempted to regulate adsorption behavior through light stimulation, which is generally achieved by introducing photoresponsive deformable molecules into adsorbents18,19. Specifically, upon irradiation with light of an appropriate wavelength, photoresponsive molecules such as azobenzene and diarylethene exhibit reversible structural transformations. Azobenzene experiences cis–trans isomerization, whereas diarylethene undergoes ring-opening and ring-closing reactions20. Such configurational changes can alter the pore size of the adsorbent, mask or unmask adsorption sites, or modify their polarity, thereby tuning adsorption performance. Notably, even after such structural transitions, the adsorption mode remains the ground-state adsorption mode, and light serves only as a switch for molecular configuration. In addition, some studies have noted that adsorbate molecules with conjugated or hyperconjugated functional groups may be excited and affect their adsorption behavior21. However, such transient excitation of the adsorbate is unstable, and the adsorption mode remains dominated by the ground state. In this study, we discover a singularity of adsorption phenomena, referred to as the excitation mode. This mode occurs in composites of metallophthalocyanines (MPcs) and reduced graphene oxide (RG). Macrocyclic conjugated molecules such as MPcs are widely used as photosensitizers22. They can be readily excited by ultraviolet (UV) light, and their fluorescence properties indicate the potential to form long-lived excited states. RG contains abundant conjugated electrons and conjugated orbitals, and has been extensively investigated in photovoltaics and electrochemistry23. We expected that their integration could promote the separation and transfer efficiency of photogenerated electrons and holes, and potentially enhance the stability of excited states. We focus on the KPc/RG composite with an optimized mass ratio as the representative sample. Using femtosecond transient absorption spectroscopy (fs-TAS) and first-principles simulations, we demonstrate that under UV irradiation, valence band (VB) electrons in KPc are excited and transfer efficiently to the low-energy empty bands (EB) of RG, leaving stable excited holes on the KPc ring (Fig. 1A).

Carbon monoxide (CO) and mixtures of CO with nitrogen (N2) were used as probe adsorbates. CO is an important C1 building block but highly toxic to the environment and detrimental to many catalytic processes24,25. However, separating CO from N2 is challenging due to their similar physical properties. In addition, CO is a simple dipolar molecule that serves as a good probe to characterize adsorption performance resulting from the interaction between photogenerated holes and dipolar molecules. CO and N2 share the same 14 electrons, and comparing their adsorption behaviors allows us to better understand the mechanism by which polarizable electrons in molecules respond to photogenerated holes. The excited holes on the composite act as adsorption sites that selectively trap CO molecules. KPc/RG shows almost no CO adsorption (0.06 mmol g⁻1; 273 K, 1 bar) in the dark, but its CO uptake increases to 1.20 mmol g⁻1 (273 K, 1 bar) under UV light. In conventional temperature swing adsorption (TSA) and pressure swing adsorption (PSA) processes, adsorbent regeneration constitutes the primary energy-consuming step in adsorption separation26. For example, in the temperature vacuum swing adsorption (TVSA) process for air carbon capture, heating energy for adsorbent regeneration accounts for nearly 80% of the total energy consumption27. In this study, the UV-responsive change in gas adsorption capacity enables adsorption–desorption to be controlled by light on/off, which may complement conventional TSA and PSA strategies. We further extend this excitation mode to other MPc/RG composites (M = H, Na, Ca) and to KPc supported on carbon nanotubes (CNT) and activated porous carbon (AC). These materials are constructed from structurally photostable photosensitizer molecules (MPc) supported on a carbonaceous substrate. Their photoresponse originates from photoinduced charge transfer, during which the molecular skeleton of MPc remains structurally stable. Accordingly, the adsorbents exhibit photostability under repeated UV irradiation.

Results

Structural characteristics of materials

KPc crystallizes in the monoclinic space group P21/a28. Powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) and Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectra match well with literature results28, confirming the successful synthesis of KPc (Figs. S1 and S2). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images show that KPc has a stick-like morphology (Fig. S3). After combining with RG, KPc crystallites are well dispersed while retaining moderate crystallinity. Elemental mapping confirms uniform distribution of KPc on RG (Fig. S3). No long stick-like KPc is observed in KPc/RG; instead, fragmented KPc crystallites appear in the high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HREM) image (Fig. S4). PXRD patterns further verify the crystalline state of KPc loaded on RG, and the intensity of KPc diffraction peaks gradually weakens with decreasing KPc loading (Fig. S1). These results confirm the successful preparation of the KPc/RG composite.

The interaction between supported KPc and RG is revealed in the HREM image of KPc/RG (Fig. 1B and Fig. S4), in which the graphitized crystal lattice of RG and the KPc lamella are overlapped each other. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) indicates that this strong interaction allows conjugated electrons on RG to donate to the KPc ring in the ground state29,30. As shown in Fig. 1C, deconvoluted K-2p3/2 XPS peaks of KPc indicate multiple conjugated environments for K+ coordinated with phthalocyanine (Pc). The peak at 291.8 eV is attributed to K+ electrons donated by both the coordinated Pc ring and adjacent Pc rings28,31. For KPc/RG, this peak is significantly enhanced, indicating additional electron donation from RG. Correspondingly, N-1s XPS peaks at lower binding energies are also enhanced by the interaction with RG (Fig. S5)31,32. Thermogravimetry (TG) and differential thermogravimetry (DTG) curves show that this strong interaction improves the thermal stability of supported KPc (Fig. S6).

As shown in Fig. S7 and Table S1, RG exhibits a typical Type-IV N2 adsorption–desorption isotherm with a Type-H3 hysteresis loop, indicating mesopores formed by lamellar particle aggregation. Its Brunauer–Emmett–Teller specific surface area (SBET) is 578 m2 g–1. Since KPc is nearly non-porous, SBET and pore volume of the composites decrease with increasing KPc content. The pore size distributions calculated by non-local density functional theory (NLDFT) of the composites are similar to those of RG. This suggests that although the loading of KPc blocked partial pore channels in RG and decreased the specific surface area of the composite, KPc was uniformly dispersed on RG without excessive local agglomeration, and therefore did not significantly alter the overall pore size distribution of the composite.

Generation of the excitation mode

Ultraviolet–visible (UV–Vis) absorption spectra show that both dissolved KPc and solid KPc/RG have characteristic absorption in the UV region (Fig. S8), so UV light centered at 365 nm was chosen as the excitation source for spectroscopic and adsorption measurements. As shown in Fig. 1D and E, the strongest fluorescence band of KPc is centered at 856 nm with a lifetime of 3 ns, while that of KPc/RG is at 839 nm with a lifetime of 4 ns. The blue-shifted fluorescence of KPc/RG implies that more low-energy valence bands contribute to excited–hole formation than in pure KPc. Under 365 nm UV excitation, the TAS of KPc shows efficient electron–hole separation, with a strong excited-state absorption (ESA) band at 550 nm, ground-state bleaching (GSB) at 650 nm, and stimulated emission (SE) at 700 nm (Fig. 2A)33,34. GSB and SE correspond to the UV–Vis absorption and fluorescence properties of materials, respectively. Since KPc/RG exhibits both UV–Vis absorption and fluorescence characteristics, it is necessary to distinguish GSB and SE in the TAS spectra. SE originates from the stimulated emission of excited states and typically appears at longer wavelengths than GSB due to the Stokes shift35,36. As a photosensitizer, KPc responds rapidly to UV light. However, density functional theory (DFT) calculations show that electron–hole separation in KPc is localized within the KPc ring (Fig. 2C). Such locally excited holes are not suitable for adsorbing polarized molecules because of the compensating effect of nearby excited electrons.

The TAS of RG shows that both ESA and GSB signals are intrinsically very weak due to its low absorption cross-section and ultrafast nonradiative relaxation of photogenerated carriers (Fig. 2D). Although slight overlap may exist in theory, the weak positive ESA is largely offset by the faint GSB, and no obvious positive ESA signal is observed in the whole detection range. This confirms that pure RG is hardly excited by UV light and cannot generate stable electron–hole pairs. The weak GSB arises from instantaneous excitation and de-excitation of electrons in the graphitic layers of RG; symmetric transient electron–hole pairs do not produce an effective induced dipole moment (Fig. 2F). For excited-hole-driven adsorption, both stable electron–hole separation and a suitable induced dipole moment are required. In contrast to KPc and RG, photoexcitation in KPc/RG is classified as charge-transfer excitation with efficient electron–hole separation (Fig. 2I). With coordinated K+ as a mediator, excited electrons transfer from the KPc ring to the RG sheet, so RG acts as an electron reservoir, leaving only excited holes on the KPc ring. Thus, electrons and holes are fully separated in KPc/RG, and charge-transfer excitation generates a strong dipole moment to drive adsorption.

As shown in Fig. 2G, TAS of KPc/RG confirms efficient electron–hole separation under UV irradiation, with a strong ESA band at 500–570 nm and intense GSB/SE bands at 600–750 nm. Although KPc enables electron–hole separation in the composite, the behavior differs substantially from pure KPc. Transient absorption difference (ΔA) profiles at representative time delays (Fig. 2B, 2E, 2H) reveal an additional ESA band at 480 nm for KPc/RG compared with KPc. Moreover, the GSB band of KPc/RG appears at 0.5 ps, and the GSB–SE band is significantly enhanced and broadened. A weak negative signal near 550 nm appears after 100 ps, which should be attributed to the long-lived component of GSB. This signal arises from the slow recovery of ground-state absorption caused by the slow decay of a small fraction of defect-trapped carriers or metastable excited states. The strong interaction between KPc and RG promotes excited–electron transfer. Although pure RG barely forms excited electron–hole pairs, its abundant conjugated orbitals (empty bands) efficiently accept excited electrons from KPc upon photoexcitation, enabling efficient electron–hole separation in the composite. DFT-calculated density of states (DOS) and projected DOS (PDOS) (Fig. 2J) show that the low-energy empty bands of RG are more favorable for accepting excited electrons from KPc than those of KPc itself. Furthermore, in the composite, the lowest unoccupied band is dominated by RG and the highest valence band by KPc, ensuring efficient charge separation upon excitation.

Adsorption in the excitation mode

Light-controlled adsorption measurements were performed under UV irradiation and in the dark, respectively. A temperature of 273 K was adopted for the static adsorption tests in this work, which is one of the feasible temperatures for measuring static adsorption isotherms of light gases such as CO37,38. A relatively low temperature can improve the light energy conversion efficiency of photosensitive materials39, and effectively suppress the thermal motion of gas molecules, helping to reveal the intrinsic interaction between gas molecules and adsorption sites on the adsorbent. Moreover, 273 K has been proven in previous studies to be an optimal testing temperature for CO adsorption over metallophthalocyanine-based materials40. The x-axis of the static adsorption isotherm represents the partial pressure of the measured pure gas component. Figure 3A and Table S2 show that neither RG nor KPc shows obvious changes in CO adsorption under UV light; RG even shows slightly reduced CO uptake, because neither material can generate effective dipolar charge separation. In contrast, KPc/RG exhibits dramatic light-enhanced CO adsorption, with capacity increasing from 0.06 to 1.20 mmol g⁻1 under UV irradiation. Static adsorption experiments were performed on three independent samples, and the standard deviation of the CO adsorption capacity of KPc/RG at 1 bar under UV irradiation was approximately 0.06 mmol g⁻1 (Fig. S9). When the temperature rises to 298 K, the CO adsorption capacity of KPc/RG drops to 0.56 mmol g⁻1 at 1 bar, revealing the temperature dependence of its photo-responsive CO adsorption performance (Fig. S10). The isosteric heat of adsorption (Qst) for CO on UV-irradiated KPc/RG is approximately 20 kJ mol⁻1, close to the DFT value of 25 kJ mol⁻1, indicating that excitation-mode adsorption is characteristic of physisorption. A series of composites with different ratios were prepared to verify excitation-induced CO adsorption. All composites show light-enhanced CO uptake but to varying degrees, while N2 adsorption on all sorbents is not significantly affected by UV light (Fig. S11, Table S2). According to inductively coupled plasma (ICP) analysis, the K weight percentages in mKPc/nRG composites are 0.94%, 2.08%, 4.01%, 5.32%, and 7.22% for m/n = 1/4, 1/2, 1/1, 2/1, and 4/1, respectively. The increase in K content corresponds to a higher KPc loading, potentially promoting the formation of a composite structure between RG and KPc. The optimized KPc/RG mass ratio of 2:1 best manifests the function of photogenerated holes.

DFT calculations (Fig. 4A) confirm that the adsorption sites for CO and N2 correspond exactly to the excited holes on the KPc ring (Fig. 2I). PDOS of CO and N2 changes noticeably upon excitation. As shown in Fig. 4B, CO occupies discrete energy levels in the ground state; this feature weakens after excitation, indicating that CO participates in the co-excitation of KPc/RG. The binding energy of CO on KPc/RG increases by 10.1 kJ mol⁻1 upon excitation, while that of N2 increases by only 5.0 kJ mol−1 (Table S3), leading to higher CO/N₂ selectivity on excited KPc/RG. The nature of this excited-state effect can be further elucidated by differential charge density (Fig. S12). In the ground state, adsorption of CO on KPc is dominated by weak electrostatic induction from K+, which is concentrated between the K+ ion and the C atom of CO. In the excited state, however, electrons of CO can fully participate in the co-excitation of KPc/RG. In other words, the photogenerated holes distributed over the entire KPc ring strongly induce the electrons of CO to polarize toward KPc, thereby significantly enhancing CO adsorption. In contrast, N2 barely participates in the excitation of KPc/RG at all. Thus, the photogenerated holes in KPc lead to a clear adsorption selectivity toward CO over N2.

Static adsorption isotherms were fitted using the Dual-Langmuir model to calculate ideal adsorption solution theory (IAST) selectivity for CO/N2 (Fig. S13, Table S4). For a CO/N2 mixture (10/90, v/v), CO/N2 selectivity under UV irradiation increases with pressure, and the representative KPc/RG composite achieves the highest selectivity of 16 at 1 bar. Mixed CO/N2 gases dominated by N2 are often adopted to simulate waste gas purification in the iron and steel industry41,42. For some special CO purification cases, such as the tail gas from low-temperature electrochemical CO2 reduction, the CO content can reach as low as 10%, bringing big challenges to subsequent separation and purification43. Therefore, in addition to pure-gas static adsorption tests, dynamic breakthrough measurements of KPc/RG were performed using a CO/N2 mixture (10/90, v/v). As shown in Fig. 3B, UV irradiation significantly boosts the CO/N2 separation performance of KPc/RG: CO breakthrough occurs at 360 s, allowing high-purity N2 to be collected directly during this period. Ab-initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) simulations based on the ground state and first excited state of KPc/RG further support the feasibility of excited-hole-induced CO separation from CO/N2 mixtures. As shown in Fig. 4C, the KPc/RG site in the ground state cannot stably capture either CO or N2; molecular thermal motion is random, and CO tends to escape. In contrast, the first-excited KPc/RG site shows strong affinity for CO. Under thermal motion, CO gradually replaces pre-adsorbed N2 at the KPc/RG site, realizing selective adsorption of CO. Videos of the full AIMD trajectories are provided in Supplementary Videos S1–S4. Adsorption–desorption switching over KPc/RG is also achievable with reduced energy input (Fig. 3C). Since KPc/RG shows negligible adsorption in the dark, excited states decay once UV light is turned off, enabling CO desorption without heating for adsorbent regeneration. When compared with other representative light-switchable sorbents based on photodeformable units, KPc/RG exhibits a light-modulated adsorption capacity change of up to 95% (Fig. 3D)44–55. A properly designed adsorption bed is essential for the application of photo-responsive adsorbents, with tubular fixed-beds representing a feasible design direction56. It was reported that the energy consumption of CO adsorption separation with a vacuum pressure swing adsorption (VPSA) system was 0.295 MJ kg–1 57. In contrast to conventional adsorption processes, of which energy consumption was dominated by desorption, the energy cost here mainly arises from CO adsorption under UV irradiation. Based on CO breakthrough experiments, the energy consumption for adsorptive separation of CO is estimated at 0.137 MJ kg⁻1.

We also investigated the adsorption of ethylene (C2H4) and ethane (C2H6) to determine whether photogenerated holes can interact with non-dipolar molecules via induction effects and to assess the strength of these interactions. As shown in Fig. S14, light-enhanced adsorption is still obvious for both adsorbates on KPc/RG. At 273 K and 1 bar, adsorption capacity increases from 0.22 to 0.67 mmol g⁻1 for C₂H₄ and from 0.26 to 0.86 mmol g⁻1 for C2H6 upon UV irradiation. DFT calculations confirm that adsorption again occurs at excited-hole sites, and PDOS shows that C2H4 and C2H6 molecules participate in co-excitation to some extent (Fig. S15). Binding energies of C2H4 and C2H6 on KPc/RG increase by 9.7 and 9.5 kJ mol⁻1, respectively (Table S3).

Other metallophthalocyanines and carbonaceous substrates

The excitation mode was extended to other MPc/RG composites (M = H, Na, Ca). TAS spectra (Figs. S16–S18) show that all MPc samples, as photosensitizers, exhibit intrinsic electron–hole separation. After combining with RG, all GSB–SE bands ranging from 600 to 750 nm are enhanced or broadened to varying degrees, confirming interfacial charge transfer between MPc and RG. The presence and type of Pc-coordinated cations are critical for excited-state stability and charge-separation efficiency. Without a coordinating cation, HPc/RG shows the fastest de-excitation among all MPc/RG samples. CaPc/RG exhibits slower de-excitation due to high-valence Ca2+, but its SE band at 700 nm is relatively weak, implying localized electron–hole separation with less efficient inter-component charge transfer. DFT-calculated excited electron/hole distributions support the TAS results (Figs. S19 and S20). All MPcs show localized excitation, but only NaPc/RG and KPc/RG exhibit efficient charge-separated excitation after combining with RG, with excited holes concentrated on the Pc ring. PDOS analysis confirms that the lowest empty band is dominated by RG and the highest valence band by NaPc (Fig. S21), as in KPc/RG. However, the smaller ionic radius of Na+ leads to lower charge-separation efficiency in NaPc/RG than in KPc/RG. UV-induced CO adsorption tests verify this trend (Fig. S22, Table S5). Only NaPc/RG shows relatively distinct light-enhanced CO adsorption, but to a lesser extent than KPc/RG (Figs. S23–S25). The difference in photoresponsive adsorption is attributed to different charge-separation efficiencies. We conclude that monovalent cations with larger radii (such as K+) in the Pc ring are essential for efficient dipolar charge separation.

We further tested the generality of this mode using KPc supported on CNT (KPc/nCNT) and AC (KPc/nAC). KPc crystallites are mainly dispersed on the outer surfaces of CNT and AC particles (Figs. S26 and S27). Strong interactions between KPc and carbon substrates are also observed (Figs. S28 and S29), and SBET and pore volume decrease gradually with increasing KPc loading (Figs. S30 and S31; Tables S6 and S7). Among KPc/nCNT samples, KPc/2CNT shows the most pronounced UV-enhanced CO adsorption, increasing from 0.08 to 1.10 mmol g⁻1 (Figs. S32 and S33; Table S8), with an IAST selectivity of 16 (Fig. S34, Table S9). AC has abundant micropores and shows moderate CO adsorption (0.68 mmol g⁻1) in the dark; UV irradiation slightly reduces its adsorption. In contrast, KPc/nAC composites show clear UV-enhanced CO adsorption. Notably, KPc/4AC exhibits a CO capacity increase from 0.53 to 1.30 mmol g⁻1 (Figs. S35 and S36; Table S10), with an IAST selectivity of 12 (Fig. S37, Table S11). Light-controlled adsorption–desorption switching can also be achieved over KPc/2CNT and KPc/4AC (Figs. S38 and S39). These results confirm that more carbonaceous materials can be used to prepare excitation-mode sorbents.

Discussion

In contrast to conventional ground-state mode, we have found excitation-mode adsorption using metallophthalocyanine (MPc)/carbonaceous substrate (CS) composites. With suitable MPc, strong interactions between MPc and CS drive efficient transfer of excited electrons from MPc to CS, leaving excited holes on the MPc ring that generate an induced dipole moment and act as adsorption sites. Monovalent cations with larger radii (e.g., K⁺) coordinated in the phthalocyanine ring are crucial for efficient electron–hole separation. Polar molecules, such as CO, respond much more strongly to excited holes than nonpolar molecules, such as N2. Thus, under UV irradiation, carbonaceous materials with insufficient adsorption ability in the ground state can show a marked improvement in adsorption performance in the excited state. Compared with recent reports that regulate the intrinsic adsorption site activity of the host material by grafting photoresponsive deformable molecules, this study constructs new active sites based on the excitation mode of adsorbent materials, which provides a reference for the application of photoexcitation in materials and the development of energy-saving adsorption separation technologies.

Methods

Materials preparation

The phthalocyanine (purity > 93%) was purchased from Aladdin Scientific Co., Ltd, and the RG (purity > 99.9%) was provided by the Institute of Coal Chemistry, Chinese Academy of Sciences. The CNT (purity > 95%) and AC (purity: C.P.) were purchased from Aladdin Scientific Co., Ltd, and Shanghai Titan Scientific Co., Ltd, respectively.

The phthalocyanine (Pc; 100 mg) was dispersed in N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF; 40.0 mL) via ultrasonic treatment, followed by dropwise addition of the solution of KOH (50 mg) dissolved in deionized water (5.00 mL). The mixture was vigorously stirred at 80 °C for 10 h, then centrifuged and washed sequentially with a DMF/H2O (3:1, v/v) mixture and anhydrous ethanol. After vacuum drying at 85 oC for 24 h, a dull greenish-blue powder of KPc was obtained. More details for synthesizing other MPc samples are given in the supplemental information.

Under a N2 atmosphere, 100 mg of RG powder was dispersed in 40.0 mL of DMF via ultrasonic treatment. Upon complete dispersion of RG in the solvent, a predetermined amount of KPc was introduced into the resulting DMF suspension, followed by an additional 30 min of ultrasonication. The mixture was subsequently stirred vigorously at 80 oC for 24 h, then transferred and subjected to 24 h of freeze-drying to yield the mKPc/nRG composite powder, in which m/n (= 4/1, 2/1, 1/1, 1/2, and 1/4, respectively) means their feed mass ratio. 2KPc/1RG is the representative sample denoted as KPc/RG in this study. More details on the preparation of other composite materials are given in the Supplementary Information.

Characterizations

The SEM was scanned on a Hitachi S4800 instrument. The HREM images were acquired using a JEOL JEM-F200 equipment at an accelerating voltage of 200 kV. The PXRD patterns were measured on a Bruker D8 Advance diffractometer. The FTIR spectra were scanned on a Nicolet Nexus 470 spectrometer. A Thermo Scientific Escalab 250Xi was used to record the XPS profiles. TG analyses were conducted under N2 atmosphere on a TG209F1 apparatus. The ICP analysis was performed using a Thermo Fisher iCAP PRO instrument equipped with an optical emission spectroscopy (OES). The UV–Vis absorption spectra were determined on a PerkinElmer LAMBDA 850+ Spectrophotometer. The fluorescence radiation and decay profiles were measured at ambient temperature with an Edinburgh FLS1000 spectrometer. The fs-TAS was measured on a Helios spectrometer of Ultrafast System (time window: 8 ns; time delay resolution: <14 fs) with a laser of Coherent Astrella (pulsed energy: > 7 mJ; pulse width: <100 fs; spot energy density: 0.029 mJ cm–2), and the chirp-correction was performed using the wavelength-dependent time-zero correction function built into Surface Xplorer software. Following degassing of the materials at 120 oC for 12 h, the 77 K N2 adsorption-desorption isotherms were acquired on a Micromeritics ASAP 2020 analyzer.

Adsorption experiments and computational methods

Static adsorption tests were conducted using the Micromeritics ASAP 2020. Before conducting the target gas test, the free space was quantified via helium purge. In a darkroom, the dark-state adsorption results were acquired, and the quartz sample cell was immersed in either a 273 K ice-water bath contained in a quartz beaker or a 298 K water bath, and fully wrapped in aluminum foil. For UV-irradiated adsorption, a xenon lamp (CEL-HXUV300) with an optical filter was used to provide UV irradiation. For the ice-water bath used, ice cubes accumulate on the upper layer of water and at the back of the quartz sample cell, leaving the UV irradiation window unobstructed (Fig. S40), and an infrared thermal imager (HM-TPH21Pro-3AQF) was used to monitor the sample surface temperature to ensure accurate testing temperature58–61. Adsorption–desorption switching tests of the sample were performed on the Micromeritics ASAP 2020. After the adsorption capacity was measured under dark (or UV irradiation) conditions, the sample was switched to UV irradiation (or dark) for further measurement without heating treatment between cycles. Temperature and light control conditions were the same as above.

Dynamic breakthrough tests were carried out in a quartz U-tube with a 4 mm inner diameter and 150 mm length, which was packed with 100 mg of the sorbent sample. The packed sample was treated by helium purge at 110 °C for 3 h. After cooling the sample, the U-tube was submerged in a 273K ice-water bath in a quartz beaker. The gas mixture of CO/N2 (10/90, v/v) was introduced at a flow rate of 2 mL/min. Post-adsorption effluent gas was analyzed via an SP-6890 gas chromatograph. The temperature and light control conditions are the same as the static adsorption tests.

Geometry optimization, electronic structure calculations, and AIMD simulations based on DFT were performed using the Vienna Ab-initio Simulation Package (VASP, 5.4 release). Additional computational details are provided in the supplemental information.
