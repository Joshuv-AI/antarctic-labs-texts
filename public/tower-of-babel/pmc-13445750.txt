CSF stroke volume measured in pediatric Chiari type 1 Malformation subjects before and after posterior fossa decompression surgery compared with healthy subjects
Authors: Brice Williams, Bliss Uribe, Francis Loth, Samir Sarda, Nilesh Desai, Joshua Chern, John Oshinski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChiari Malformation Type 1 (CM-1) is characterized radiologically by > 5 mm herniation of the cerebellar tonsils below the foramen magnum (FM). Tonsillar descent below the FM does not correlate well with symptom severity nor does it predict outcomes following posterior-fossa-decompression surgery. Using 2D phase-contrast magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) it is possible to measure cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) hydrodynamics non-invasively in CM-1 patients. The volume of fluid moved each heartbeat, or stroke volume, has shown promise as a new metric to characterize CM-1 malformation in adults, but has been relatively underexplored in the pediatric population. We measured CSF stroke volume in a pediatric CM-1 cohort before and after posterior fossa decompression surgery and in age-matched controls.Materials and methods24 pediatric CM-1 patients (age 9.3 ± 4.5, 7 M, 17 F) underwent posterior fossa decompression surgery to treat CM-1 symptoms and received an MRI pre-operatively and 6 months post-operatively. 8 additional healthy volunteers (age 7.8 ± 5.7, 5 F) were used as control subjects. ECG-gated 2D phase-contrast magnetic resonance (PCMR) scans were acquired in the axial orientation at the foramen magnum (FM). CSF stroke volume was calculated from flow by separately integrating positive and negative flow, then averaging the absolute values.ResultsThe CSF stroke volumes in the pre-surgical CM-1 subjects (0.26 ml ± 0.21) were lower than control subjects (0.55 ± 0.2, p < 0.01). Post-surgical stroke volumes in CM-1 subjects (0.44 ± 0.3) were not different than controls (p = 0.18). Pre-surgical stroke volumes were lower than post-surgical stroke volumes across all patients (p = 0.03), and in paired patients who had both pre- and post-surgical scans (p = 0.03). There was no difference in peak velocity pre-surgical and control (p = 0.7) or pre- and post-surgical subjects (p = 0.2). Pre-surgical patients with syrinx (10/15) showed no difference in stroke volume to patients without syrinx (p = 0.6).ConclusionHealthy pediatric control subjects had higher CSF stroke flow volume than CM-1 patients. Surgery increased CSF stroke volume in CM-1 patients to values equal to healthy control subjects, and post-surgical stroke volumes were significantly greater than pre-surgical volumes. CSF stroke volume shows potential for use as a flow metric in assessing pediatric CM-1 patients.

Background

Chiari Malformation-I (CM-1) is characterized by abnormal herniation of the cerebellar tonsils below the foramen magnum (FM), with a 5 mm descent below FM often cited as the cutoff for a radiologic definition [1, 2]. The tonsillar descent causes crowding in the FM and the cisterna magna and often impedes normal CSF flow dynamics. CM-1 is seen in both pediatric and adult patients with a population prevalence of 0.08–0.9% [3, 4]. Patients with CM-1 often experience headaches, motor dysfunction, paresthesia, and other general neurological symptoms [5–7]. Younger children often have difficulty verbalizing these symptoms. Approximately 40% of children with CM-1 have a fluid-filled cavity, or syrinx, which forms in the spinal cord and can accelerate symptoms [8]. Symptomatic CM-1 patients may undergo posterior fossa decompression (PFD) surgery [9, 10]. The surgery involves removing a section of opisthion bone and C1 vertebra with or without a duraplasty, and increases space for the cerebellar tonsils and the surrounding CSF fluid. The goals of surgery are to reduce crowding of the cerebellar tonsils, create space for normal CSF flow, and reduce symptoms. Surgery resolves symptoms and reduces or eliminates the syrinx in about 70% of patients [9, 11]. However, it is difficult to predict which patients will experience benefit from surgery. Additionally, there is poor correlation between morphometric measurements, such as amount of tonsillar herniation, and clinical symptoms or surgical outcomes. Therefore, there is a scientific and clinical need to identify better markers of CM-1 that could be indicative of symptom severity or predict surgical outcomes [12–15].

The tonsillar descent and cerebellar crowding in CM-1 patients often impedes normal cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) flow through the foramen magnum (FM) [16]. Therefore measures of CSF dynamics may better reflect disease severity and potentially be a better predictor of surgical outcomes than tonsillar herniation (Fig. 1). CSF dynamics can be evaluated by examining flow, velocity, or stroke volume. PFD Surgery increases the area available for CSF flow (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{A}_{CSF}$$\end{document} is increased) which may decrease resistance to flow. If flow increases from pre-surgery to post-surgery, that implies that cross-sectional area (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{A}_{CSF}$$\end{document}) pre-surgery was small enough that flow was reduced due to high resistance, and that surgery removes the resistance to CSF flow. It is therefore necessary to use a metric of CSF hydrodynamics that captures the reduction in flow when resistance is high. Peak velocity, and measures of peak flow rate at a single time point during the cardiac cycle do not capture this change in total flow over the cardiac cycle. CSF stroke volume, or the amount of fluid that moves forward (or backward) per heartbeat, measures the effect of resistance to flow over the cardiac cycle. CSF stroke volume has previously been used to measure CSF dynamics in adult CM-1 patients [16, 23], and was found to be a more repeatable measurement of CSF movement than velocity [24, 25].

The purpose of this study was to compare CSF stroke volume at the foramen magnum in pediatric CM-1 patients before and after surgery, and compare CSF stroke volumes in CM-1 to age-matched controls. We hypothesized that CSF stroke volume would increase after surgery and post-surgery values would be comparable to values seen in healthy control subjects.

Methods

Study population

All subjects were scanned at Children’s Healthcare of Atlanta (CHOA) under an IRB-approved protocol. 24 CM-1 pediatric subjects (9.3 ± 4.5 years of age, 7 M, 17 F) from an outpatient pediatric neurosurgery clinic underwent MR flow imaging before and after posterior fossa decompression surgery (Fig. 2). Average time between the two scans was 243 ± 67 days. All CM-1 subjects had a previous CT or outside MRI that suggested CM-1, had signs and symptoms consistent with that diagnosis, and were evaluated by a single neurosurgeon (JC). An additional 8 control subjects (7.8 ± 5.7 years of age, 5 F) underwent MRIs for non-specific headache symptoms but were determined to be normal in structure and function by a neuro-radiologist (ND). There was no significant difference between the age range of CM-1 vs. control patients (p = 0.44). Participants were scanned at the foramen magnum (FM).

Posterior fossa decompression surgery

All patients underwent posterior fossa decompression surgery between scans. Duraplasty was performed on 20/24 patients; the 4 that did not receive duraplasty were determined to have robustly pulsating dura during intra-operative ultrasound evaluation prior to entering the dura. For patients without a syrinx (n = 5), the indication for surgery was severe posterior headache that was episodic and induced by coughing, sneezing or straining. Other related symptoms included neck and shoulder pain, swallowing difficulties, snoring and paresthesia. When indicated, additional testing, including swallow and/or sleep study, were obtained.

Magnetic resonance imaging

Both control and CM-1 patients underwent a clinical MRI scan on 3.0 Tesla scanner (Siemens TRIO) that included sagittal ECG-gated cine PCMR images, and sagittal T1, T2, and FLAIR images. Additionally, axial (transverse) 2D PCMR images were acquired perpendicular to the spinal cord at the FM. In the post-surgical scan, the FM position was estimated using the pre-surgical scans. 2D PCMR scans were retrospectively ECG-gated. Relevant parameters included: spatial resolution = 1.2 mm x 1.2 mm, slice thickness=5 mm, TE=11ms, VENC=15 cm/s. Two segments were acquired per cardiac cycle, two signal averages were acquired, and 25 images were reconstructed over the cardiac cycle [26].

Quantitative measurements

Tonsillar descent below the FM was measured at the time of clinical scan by a hospital radiologist. Segment software (Medviso) was used to draw ROIs and determine CSF flow rate (ml/sec) as a function of time over the cardiac cycle on the PCMR images. ROIs were manually delineated around the subarachnoid space (SAS) where CSF flow was seen on phase images. Care was taken to exclude vascular structures. Velocity values were integrated on a pixel-by-pixel basis in the ROI to output the flowrate at each time point over the cardiac cycle.

Pre-surgical anatomy at the FM in CM-1 patients is often complex due to tonsillar crowding; to decrease the possibility of missing sub-pixel areas of CSF flow, a second set of ROIs were drawn to include the brainstem and SAS as a comparison analysis (see Results). Care was taken to avoid the vertebral vessels, which often travel in-plane at the level of the FM and border the brainstem anteriorly in the SAS. Cerebellum was not included in this ROI.

Using the flowrate data at each time point, the net stoke volume (ml) over the cardiac cycle was calculated for each scan. Over the ~ 2.5 min scan duration, it was assumed there was no measurable production of CSF, so the net flow over the cardiac cycle would be zero. The small net flow observed was assumed to be due to an offset of signal created by background phase noise [27]. This was corrected by subtracting the flow at every time point from the net flow per time point, such that the total integral of flow over the cardiac cycle was zero.

The area under the corrected flow curve was calculated for positive and negative flow. These two stroke volumes represent roughly the systolic and diastolic stroke volumes and must be equal due to net zero flow. To account for small errors in measurement, the average of the absolute value of these two volumes was taken to be the stroke volume. For all measurements of stroke volume, this average stroke volume is the measurement being referenced (Fig. 2). MATLAB 2024a was used for all calculations and statistical analyses.

Chicago chiari outcome scale (CCOS) scores

We retrospectively reviewed patient charts to calculate Chicago Chiari Outcome Scale (CCOS) scores using the method described in Aliaga et al., 2012 [28].

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics are reported for each subject group as the mean and standard deviation. All study variables were analyzed by parametric methods, either by paired or unpaired t-test. Differences were considered significant at p-value < 0.05. All statistical comparison groups were assessed for normality with a Kolmogorov-Smirnov test.

Results

We measured the CSF flow over the cardiac cycle in 24 pediatric patients with CM-1 malformation who underwent posterior fossa decompression surgery (Fig. 3A and B) and 8 healthy controls. Some CM-1 patients did not receive both pre- and post-surgery scans due to issues with scan duration, patient discomfort, or enrollment in the study before surgery (see Fig. 4). In total, 15 patients had pre-operative scans, 20 patients had post-operative scans, and 11 patients had both. The average tonsillar descent in patients with CM-1 was 13.0 ± 5.8 mm. There was no correlation between tonsillar descent and pre-surgical stroke volume at FM (n = 15, r2 = 0.15, p = 0.16).

Stroke volume in CM-1 subjects compared to control subjects

We calculated the CSF stroke volume over one cardiac cycle (see Methods). We found that the stroke volumes before surgery at FM in CM-1 patients were significantly lower (53%, mean ± SD: 0.26 ± 0.2 vs. 0.55 ± 0.2 ml) than in control subjects (p = 0.006, Fig. 3C). Stroke volumes increased after surgery (70%, 0.44 ± 0.3 ml), and post-surgical stroke volumes were not significantly lower than controls (p = 0.18).

Stroke volumes in CM-1 pre & post-surgery

We compared the stroke volumes before and after surgery at the FM. In an unpaired comparison post-surgery stroke volumes were 70% greater than pre-surgery (p = 0.03, Fig. 3C). For patients with both pre- and post-operative scans, post-surgical stroke volumes were also significantly greater in paired analysis (n = 11, 0.19 ± 0.17 vs. 0.44 ± 0.28 ml, p = 0.03, Fig. 3D).

Stroke volume in pre-surgical CM-1 with and without a syrinx

We investigated whether the presence of a syrinx might influence CSF flow at the FM. 10/15 (67%) pre-surgical patients had a syrinx. We found no difference in pre-surgical stroke volume at FM between patients with and without syrinx (0.24 ± 0.2 vs. 0.31 ± 0.3 ml, p = 0.57).

Comparison of CSF space and full brainstem ROIs

CSF spaces in the foramen magnum of Chiari patients can be difficult to identify and are often smaller than in normal subjects. To ensure we did not miss small or low flow spaces, we drew new regions of interest that included the brainstem at the FM and calculated the stroke volume including any solid-tissue movement of the brainstem. We found that the results did not change from the CSF-only segmentations. Pre-surgical CM-1 patients had lower stroke volumes than normals (0.31 ± 0.1 vs. 0.57 ± 0.2 ml, p = 0.001) while post-surgical stroke volumes (0.47 ± 0.3 ml) were not different to normals (p = 0.18, Fig. 3E). Pre- and post-surgical stroke volumes were different in unpaired comparison (p = 0.031) and paired comparison (p = 0.043). Syrinx also had no effect on stroke volume (p = 0.49).

Peak velocity

To compare to previous studies that reported changes in peak velocity pre/post-surgery, we measured the maximum systolic velocity in CM-1 patients and healthy controls. We found no difference in peak systolic velocity between CM-1 patients and controls, either pre-surgery (p = 0.7, 8.1 ± 7.2 vs. 7.1 ± 2.9) or post-surgery (p = 0.3, 9.96 ± 6.9 vs. 7.1 ± 2.9). There was also no difference between paired pre- and post-surgery CM-1 patients (p = 0.2, 6.1 ± 5.4, 10.0 ± 5.8). For each peak velocity value we calculated the z-score relative to the other velocities at that time point. The average z-score for systolic peak velocity was 3.15 SDs in CM-1 pre surgery, 4.02 SDs in CM-1 post surgery, and 4.11 SDs in healthy controls.

Clinical outcomes

We used the Chicago Chiari Outcomes Scale (CCOS) to retrospectively generate outcome scores for 11 patients for which we had imaging before and after surgery. A higher score indicates a better outcome, with scores in the 14–16 range reflecting complete or near-complete resolution of symptoms. All scores were in the range of 13–16 except one score of 10 (mean = 14.09 ± 1.7). CCOS score did not correlate with change in stroke volume from pre- to post-surgery (r = 0.19, p = 0.58).

Discussion

Traditionally, the severity of CM-1 is characterized using tonsillar descent, which has repeatedly been found to correlate poorly with patient functional recovery, symptoms, or surgical outcomes [12, 13, 29]. Here we measured CSF stroke volume in pediatric CM-1 patients before and after surgery [16, 23]. We found lower stroke volumes at the foramen magnum (FM) in patients with CM-1 than in healthy controls. After surgery this deficit in stroke volume is reversed; stroke volumes post-surgery were not different than healthy controls, and stroke volumes were significantly increased in post-surgery vs. pre-surgery patients. Because stroke volume is related to flow resistance, our findings suggest resistance to flow is higher in pediatric CM-1 subjects than healthy control subjects and surgery reduces this resistance back to levels of healthy control subjects. The increased CSF area posterior to the cerebellum post-surgery provides a lower resistance pathway for CSF flow than prior to surgery. Our in-vivo results agree with previous research that conducted fluid dynamics simulations to compute flow impedance [30, 31]. Impedance is the equivalent of resistance in the setting of pulsatile or oscillating flow.

Computational modelling studies have shown that the calculated impedance to CSF flow in the upper cervical spinal canal is greater in adult CM-1 compared to controls and greater pre-surgery compared to post-surgery [30], and flow impedance has been shown to be predictive of cough-associated headaches in adult CM-1 patients [23, 32, 33]. Researchers have also theorized that CM-1 subjects also have reduced compliance at the cervical spinal canal [34]. This lower compliance, combined with flow impedance at the same level, leads to intracranial pressure peaks during the cardiac cycle that may be amplified during activities such as coughing, sneezing, and physical exertion – activities which commonly lead to headache and other symptoms in CM-1 patients [12].

CSF stroke volume has been investigated in CM-1 adults. McIlvain et al. found using 2D PCMR that stroke volume was 29% greater (n = 108) in post-surgical CM-1 patients than pre-surgical patients (including those who did not ultimately undergo surgery), and surgical patients saw a 23% increase (n = 16) in stroke volume [16]. In pre-operative patients, Carlier et al. observed a lower stroke volume at C2/C3 in those who ultimately had headache relief following surgery compared to those who did not [35]. Their average stroke volume measured at FM in this adult study was 0.436 ± 0.301–0.504 ± 0.225 ml, which is higher than our measured pre-surgical pediatric stroke volumes (0.26 ± 0.2 ml). A study by Bhadelia et al. found no difference in stroke volume between CM-1 adults and healthy volunteers, but this was analyzed using a real-time pencil beam technique in only 8 subjects, and the study was designed to investigate the effects of coughing on flow. One previous study found that stroke volume in CM-1 subjects was less than half of that of normal subjects [36]. Another study found a change in CSF stroke volume in Chiari patients pre-surgery to post-surgery but only a limited number of subjects (n = 3) were examined and the findings were not statistically significant [37]. Stroke volume has not been used to measure CM-1 flow changes in pediatric populations.

In our cohort, we found that there was no difference in peak systolic velocity between CM-1 and normal subjects either pre- or post-surgery, and no difference pre- vs. post-surgery. Peak velocity has been used before to describe flow changes in pediatric CM-1 [17, 20, 36, 38], but we believe that stroke volume as a metric is more stable against individual pixel behavior and adds a dimension of information about CSF flow that peak velocity cannot. In patients with severe obstruction of the FM, the impedance to flow may result in a lower volume of CSF moving each heartbeat. In this situation the peak velocity would be higher, but would not reflect the fact that overall, the volume of CSF flow has decreased, whereas stroke volume would capture exactly this. Finally, stroke volume measurement has been found to have higher intra and inter-operative repeatability than velocity [24, 25]. Peak velocity measurement is susceptible to randomly high pixel values due to noise, which may also be present in normal controls and might explain the lack of difference we found. We used a z-score to show that the average maximum velocity pixel was greater than 2.5 standard deviations above the mean in both CM-1 and normal subjects, suggesting that these pixels are more likely to be noisy values rather than velocities reflective of the physiology. Stroke volume is superior to peak velocity in this aspect as it is less susceptible to outlier values.

In pediatric subjects with CM-1, the FM anatomy is highly complex pre-surgery and specific CSF flow channels are difficult to identify. Anatomy also varies greatly after surgery, so we ran a parallel study using ROIs which were guaranteed to capture the majority of smaller CSF flow channels, but also solid brainstem tissue. The results were the same using these new ROIs, suggesting that most of the effect of Chiari on CSF stroke volume was captured in the original ROIs which segmented clearly visible subarachnoid spaces, and that not much CSF is flowing through sub-pixel sized channels.

We found that there was no difference in CSF stroke volume at the FM between CM-1 patients with a syrinx and those without a syrinx. However, the analysis was not stratified by size of syrinx. These findings are in line with Brugieres et al. who found no difference in CSF velocities between adult subjects with and without syrinx at distance far from the syrinx [39]. Stroke volume was not measured in that study. Using 4D flow, Bunck et al. found no difference at multiple locations between adults with syrinx and healthy controls [38].

We endeavored to show that stroke volume may be a useful clinical metric by deriving CCOS scores from patient outcomes and comparing these to the change in stroke volume pre-to-post surgery. Although we did not find a correlation between stroke volume change and CCOS score, this is likely due to only having a narrow range of outcomes. The majority of patients in our cohort had complete or near-complete resolution of symptoms, indicated by high CCOS scores. Both CCOS scores and stroke volume changes reflected positive surgical outcomes across the cohort. At this juncture we cannot recommend using stroke volume alone to predict surgical outcomes.

Our study is limited by a small sample size and the fact that the number of controls was not matched exactly to the number of CM-1 subjects. Also due to patient compliance and scan time issues, not all patients were scanned before and after surgery. The study was conducted at a single hospital, and patients were evaluated by a single neurosurgeon (JC), and a single evaluator traced the ROIs on images. Future work will focus on the utility of stroke volume as a metric for CM-1 disease severity by evaluating surgical outcomes or symptomatology in a larger group.

Conclusion

We measured the cardiac-induced CSF stroke volume in 24 pediatric patients with Chiari 1 malformation (CM-1) and 8 healthy pediatric controls. We found that CSF stroke volume at the foramen magnum (FM) was significantly lower in CM-1 patients than controls. Posterior fossa decompression surgery restored CSF stroke volume to values seen in control subjects. Our study affirms that CSF stroke volume is a metric which can identify reduced flow in pre-surgical pediatric CM-1 patients and measure post-surgical hydrodynamic changes. While stroke volume alone cannot currently be used as a predictor for surgical outcomes, it could potentially be part of a multi-metric approach to evaluation.
