Generalizing symplectic topology from 1 to 2 dimensions
Ronen Brilleslijper, Oliver Fabert

Abstract
In symplectic topology one uses elliptic methods to prove rigidity results about symplectic manifolds and solutions of Hamiltonian equations on them, where the most basic example is given by geodesics on Riemannian manifolds. Harmonic maps from surfaces are the natural 2-dimensional generalizations of geodesics. In this paper, we give the corresponding generalization of symplectic manifolds and Hamiltonian equations, leading to a class of partial differential equations that share properties similar to Hamiltonian (ordinary) differential equations. Two rigidity results are discussed: a non-squeezing theorem and a version of the cuplength result for quadratic Hamiltonians on cotangent bundles. The proof of the latter uses a generalization of Floer curves, for which the necessary Fredholm and compactness results will be proven.



Introduction

In topology, one uses Morse theory to prove lower bounds for the number of
0
-dimensional objects, namely critical points of smooth functions on smooth manifolds. In symplectic topology, one uses Floer theory, an infinite-dimensional generalization of Morse theory, to prove lower bounds for the number of
1
-dimensional objects, like time-periodic orbits of (Hamiltonian) functions on symplectic manifolds. Despite the infinite-dimensionality one finds that Floer theory for Hamiltonian orbits has properties very similar to Morse theory for critical points: in the case of closed symplectic manifolds the lower bound is given by the sum of the Betti numbers. This naturally leads to the question whether the results of (symplectic) topology can be generalized to prove topological bounds for the number of
2
-dimensional or even higher dimensional objects in a finite-dimensional manifold.

We will give an affirmative answer for the case of
2
dimensions. There are two natural candidates for the generalization of symplectic geometry from
1
to
2
dimensions: holomorphic symplectic geometry and polysymplectic (or multi-/
2
-symplectic) geometry.

Holomorphic symplectic geometry
is a complex version of (real) symplectic geometry in the sense that one considers a complex manifold with a holomorphic symplectic
2
-form; in the similar way one considers complex-valued Hamiltonian functions which are assumed to be holomorphic. It is already known, see e.g. the work of Doan-Rezchikov and Kontsevich-Soibelman on holomorphic Floer theory, that the elliptic methods for proving rigidity results in symplectic geometry have a natural analogue in holomorphic symplectic geometry: the role of
2
-dimensional holomorphic curves in symplectic geometry is taken over by
3
-dimensional Fueter maps in holomorphic symplectic geometry (see
[
DR22
,
KS24
]
). However, it is not possible to formulate a non-linear Laplace equation, the natural higher-dimensional generalization of Newton’s equation, as a holomorphic Hamiltonian system. In particular, while geodesics are the basic examples of solutions of Hamilton’s equations in the real case, there is no holomorphic Hamiltonian system whose solution leads to harmonic maps, the natural
2
-dimensional generalization of geodesics. Moreover, there is no canonical real-valued action functional defined, but rather choices have to be made in order to find an action functional whose gradient lines may be studied.

Polysymplectic geometry
(and its multisymplectic and
k
-symplectic variants) is a geometric framework that has been developed to generalize symplectic geometry from classical mechanics to classical field theory, that is, to the case where the time coordinate is replaced by multiple (space-)time coordinates. In contrast to holomorphic symplectic geometry, it provides a framework in which non-linear Laplace equations (in particular harmonic maps) can be studied as generalized Hamiltonian systems. Here we would like to stress that the underlying Hamiltonian function is always real-valued. The central geometric object is the polysymplectic form, which is an
\mathbb{R}^{2}
-valued real
2
-form. Surprising at first glance, there does not exist anything like polysymplectic topology; in particular, there are no analogues of Gromov’s non-squeezing theorem as well as of Floer’s proof of the Arnold conjecture. Indeed, as we have already discussed in our previous papers
[
BF23
,
BF24
]
, it is not possible to generalize the elliptic methods of symplectic geometry to the standard polysymplectic setting: The corresponding generalization of Hamilton’s equations, called De Donder-Weyl equations, are not elliptic due to the presence of an infinite-dimensional kernel.

In this paper we discuss a geometric framework, that we call
complex-regularized polysymplectic geometry
, which is designed in order to combine the benefits of both candidates. It is possible to formulate non-linear Laplace equations, in particular harmonic maps, as Hamiltonian systems in our framework just like in polysymplectic geometry. At the same time, the elliptic methods of symplectic geometry generalize to our framework along the lines of holomorphic symplectic geometry. While we give an intrinsic definition, we prove an analogue of the Darboux theorem stating that our complex-regularized polysymplectic manifolds are locally standard. Since the polysymplectic structure builds on the holomorphic symplectic one, we can show that the holomorphic Hamiltonian functions and holomorphic Lagrangians of holomorphic symplectic geometry become part of our polysymplectic framework alongside the real Hamiltonian functions used to describe non-linear Laplace equations and harmonic maps. However, it turns out that this framework still shares rigidity properties similar to symplectic geometry. We discuss a non-squeezing result as well as a version of the cuplength result for quadratic Hamiltonians, which in particular provides a lower bound to the number of harmonic maps. Since it is apparent that both the polysymplectic as well as the holomorphic symplectic picture simultaneously play a central role in the rigidity result, we hope that the reader is convinced that our new geometric framework is the natural candidate for the generalization of symplectic geometry from
1
to
2
dimensions. At the end of
part

I
of the paper, we give examples of problems from both holomorphic symplectic as well as polysymplectic geometry that naturally lead to questions in the other. Both problems can be studied within our complex-regularized polysymplectic framework.

Overview of the article

This articles contains both an expository part to introduce the framework in which we work, and a technical part providing proofs of the rigidity results. We start by introducing polysymplectic geometry, with the non-linear Laplace equation as the main example of a Hamiltonian system. In
section

2
we discuss a different Hamiltonian formulation of the Laplace equation using Dirac operators, leading to the definition of complex-regularized polysymplectic manifolds. Consequently, the correspondence with holomorphic symplectic geometry is explained in
section

3
and we find a Darboux theorem for complex-regularized polysymplectic manifolds.
Section

4
describes the Lagrange formalism corresponding to this new Hamiltonian framework. To finish the expository part of the paper,
section

5
describes two problems that highlight the interplay between holomorphic symplectic and polysymplectic geometry. The first problem is obstruction theory to holomorphic Lagrangian embeddings, a problem from holomorphic symplectic geometry, which may be answered using the study of harmonic maps. The second one is the existence of harmonic maps with specified boundary. This problem in polysymplectic geometry may be formulated as a Hamiltonian PDE, whose boundary condition is given by a complex Lagrangian in a holomorphic symplectic manifold. Both problems discussed can be studied within the complex-regularized polysymplectic framework.

Subsequently, we turn to the rigidity results. In
section

6
, a non-squeezing result is discussed which in particular implies that morphisms between complex-regularized polysymplectic manifolds are stable under taking
C^{0}
-limits.
Section

7
discusses the action functional and formulates a version of the cuplength result for quadratic Hamiltonians, which is proven in
sections

8
,
9
and
10
by analysing a moduli space of Floer maps. While
section

8
proves the necessary
C^{0}
-bounds and discusses the general proof strategy,
sections

9
and
10
cover the Fredholm and compactness theories respectively.

Part I
Introducing the formalism

1
Polysymplectic geometry

In its most basic form, symplectic geometry studies maps
q\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}M\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where
M
is either
\mathbb{R}
or
S^{1}
, satisfying Newton’s equation

\displaystyle-\partial_{t}^{2}q=\frac{dV}{dq},

(1)

for some potential
V\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
. The Hamiltonian form of this equation can be described using a non-degenerate 2-form, called the symplectic form, that maps Hamiltonians on phase space
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
to vector fields. The flow of these Hamiltonian vector fields then yield first-order ODEs that generalize
equation

1
. For example, when the Hamiltonian is chosen to be purely quadratic in the momentum variable, the flow of the Hamiltonian vector field describes the geodesic equation. Since all the geometric information is stored in the symplectic form, this Hamiltonian formalism can be extended to any manifold admitting a non-degenerate 2-form.

As mentioned in the introduction, the polysymplectic formalism, in its most basic form, studies the non-linear Laplace equation

\displaystyle-\Delta q\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=-\sum_{l=1}^{d}\partial_{t_{l}}^{2}q=\frac{dV}{dq},

(2)

where in this case
M=\mathbb{R}^{d}
. When
M
is a surface, one would hope that the corresponding Hamiltonian formalism would be capable of describing harmonic maps, just like for
d=1
it can describe the geodesic equation.

From here on out
d=2
. The standard way of transforming
equation

2
into a first-order system is by introducing momentum variables for the different ’time’-directions. This yields the so-called De Donder-Weyl equations

\displaystyle\begin{split}-\partial_{1}p_{1}-\partial_{2}p_{2}&=\partial_{q}H\\
\partial_{1}q&=\partial_{p_{1}}H\\
\partial_{2}q&=\partial_{p_{2}}H,\end{split}

(3)

where
\partial_{l}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\partial_{t_{l}}
. Note that for
H(q,p_{1},p_{2})=\frac{1}{2}p_{1}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}p_{2}^{2}+V(q)
, indeed
equation

3
reduces to the nonlinear Laplace
equation

2
. We will denote by
Z
the map
Z=(q,p_{1},p_{2})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

The De Donder-Weyl equations form the basis of the different extensions of symplectic geometry to formalisms that can deal with PDEs. However, for the purpose of extending elliptic methods, they are unsuitable. The most apparent problem is that even though the Laplace equation is elliptic,
equation

3
is not. As pointed out in previous articles (e.g.
[
Bri06
]
), the differential operator that defines
equation

3
has an infinite-dimensional kernel. This can be easily seen as follows. Take
q
to be a constant and
p_{1}=\partial_{2}\psi
,
p_{2}=-\partial_{1}\psi
for any function
\psi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
. Then
-\partial_{1}p_{1}-\partial_{2}p_{2}=0
and also
\partial_{1}q=\partial_{2}q=0
, so that indeed
(q,p_{1},p_{2})
satisfies
equation

3
for
H\equiv 0
. This fact makes the extension of elliptic methods to the polysymplectic setting impossible. We refer to our previous articles
[
BF23
,
BF24
]
for more explanation on why this forms a problem in the specific context of Floer theory. Fortunately, it is possible to formulate a different Hamiltonian version of the Laplace
equation

2
using Dirac operators. In order to do so, we need to have complex structures on the spaces we are working with. These equations will lead to the notion of complex-regularized polysymplectic geometry. Before diving into this formalism in
section

2
, we discuss the geometric picture behind
equation

3
.

Remark 1.1
.

In this article we will focus on the case where the domain

has a trivial tangent bundle, meaning
M=\mathbb{R}^{2}
or

Since the polysymplectic formalism already contains the essence of our work and it clearly shows the relation to holomorphic symplectic geometry, we have decided to stick with this formalism for this article. The multisymplectic generalization is ongoing work of the authors, see also

Definition 1.2

(
[
Gün87
]
)
.

An
\mathbb{R}^{2}
-valued 2-form
\Omega\in\bigwedge^{2}T^{*}W\otimes\mathbb{R}^{2}
on a manifold
W
is called
polysymplectic
if it is closed and non-degenerate in the following sense:

\Omega(X,\cdot)=0\Rightarrow X=0\text{ for all }X\in TW.

Note that
\Omega
is given by a pair of closed 2-forms
\eta_{1},\eta_{2}
on
W
. The non-degeneracy condition is equivalent to the requirement that
\ker\eta_{1}^{\flat}\cap\ker\eta_{2}^{\flat}=\{0\}
.
For example, on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with coordinates
(q,p_{1},p_{2})
we may define the
\mathbb{R}^{2}
-valued form

\Omega_{DW}=\eta_{1}\otimes\partial_{1}+\eta_{2}\otimes\partial_{2},

where
\mathbb{R}^{2}
is spanned by the basis vectors
\partial_{1}
and
\partial_{2}
and

\displaystyle\eta_{1}=dp_{1}\wedge dq

\displaystyle\eta_{2}=dp_{2}\wedge dq.

Note that neither
\eta_{1}
nor
\eta_{2}
is non-degenerate, but their kernels have trivial intersection.

Whereas on symplectic manifolds there is a unique notion of a Hamiltonian, on a polysymplectic manifold there are actually two types of Hamiltonians, both of whom serve a different purpose. On the one hand, in symplectic geometry, a Hamiltonian gives rise to a vector field whose flow describes a differential equation. Since a polysymplectic form is
\mathbb{R}^{2}
-valued, its pairing with a vector field gives an element of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Thus, only for functions
W\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
there is a chance of relating them to the flow of a vector field. In the polysymplectic world, we call these functions currents. On the other hand, the polysymplectic language is actually designed in order to deal with PDEs, whereas a flow of a vector field gives an ODE. In order to describe PDEs, we need to study Hamiltonians
W\to\mathbb{R}
. The following definition describes both of these types of functions.

Definition 1.3
.

Let
(W,\Omega)
be a polysymplectic manifold.

(i)

A function
F\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}W\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
is called a
current
, if there exists a vector field
X_{F}
on
W
such that
\Omega(X_{F},\cdot)=dF
.

(ii)

A function
H\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}W\to\mathbb{R}
is called a
Hamiltonian
.

As mentioned, any current
F
induces a flow along the trajectories of its associated vector field
X_{F}
. This flow preserves the polysymplectic form. In order to relate a Hamiltonian
H
to a PDE, note that for any vector
v\in TW
and any map
Z\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{2}\to W
, the map
\Omega(v,dZ\cdot)
is a linear map from
\mathbb{R}^{2}
to itself. Therefore, we can take its trace and denote it by
\Omega^{\#}(dZ)(v)
. Note that when
\Omega=\eta_{1}\otimes\partial_{1}+\eta_{2}\otimes\partial_{2}
it follows that
\Omega^{\#}(dZ)=\eta_{1}(\cdot,\partial_{1}Z)+\eta_{2}(\cdot,\partial_{2}Z)
. The map
Z\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{2}\to W
is called a solution of the polysymplectic Hamiltonian equation if

\displaystyle dH=\Omega^{\#}(dZ)=\eta_{1}(\cdot,\partial_{1}Z)+\eta_{2}(\cdot,\partial_{2}Z).

(4)

In the example of
W=\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
\Omega=\Omega_{DW}

equation

4
.

For Hamiltonians
H
and currents

, one can define their Poisson bracket as
\{H,F\}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=-dH(X_{F})
. The flow of

preserves

that in the complex-regularized polysymplectic framework there is a remarkable connection between currents and holomorphic Hamiltonian systems.

2
An alternative Hamiltonian formulation using Dirac operators

Assume
M
is either
\mathbb{R}^{2}
or
\mathbb{T}^{2}
, so that
\{\partial_{1},\partial_{2}\}
forms a global frame of
TM
. As pointed out in
section

1
, we are looking for a Hamiltonian formulation of the nonlinear Laplace
equation

2

To this extent, we now assume that
q\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}M\to\mathbb{R}^{2n}
maps to even-dimensional Euclidean space equipped with its standard complex structure

\displaystyle-\Delta q=-4\partial_{\bar{t}}\partial_{t}q=\nabla V(q),

\partial_{t}=\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{1}-i\partial_{2})

\partial_{\bar{t}}=\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{1}+i\partial_{2})

To get to the De Donder-Weyl equations, we would introduce all four of the momentum vectors

5

\displaystyle\begin{split}p_{1}&=\partial_{1}q_{1}+\partial_{2}q_{2}\\

(6)

and get

5

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{\partial V}{\partial q_{1}}&=-\partial_{1}p_{1}+\partial_{2}p_{2}\\
\frac{\partial V}{\partial q_{2}}&=-\partial_{1}p_{2}-\partial_{2}p_{1}.\end{split}

(7)

Combined, we see that
equation

5
is equivalent to

\displaystyle\cancel{\partial}Z\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-2\partial_{\bar{t}}\\
2\partial_{t}&0\end{pmatrix}Z=\nabla H(Z),

(8)

where

and
H\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{4n}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

In contrast to

3

is a Dirac operator (clearly
\cancel{\partial}^{2}=-\Delta

A

Example 2.4

Our main example of a complex-regularized polysymplectic manifold is the cotangent bundle of a complex manifold. Let

Proposition 2.5

Let

By definition

\displaystyle\omega_{2}(X,Y)=-\omega_{1}(X,IY)=0,

3
Holomorphic Hamiltonian systems and a Darboux theorem

Recall from
remark

2.2
, that the polysymplectic form on
\mathbb{R}^{4n}
defined in
section

2
can be written in terms of a holomorphic symplectic form. In this section we will elaborate more on the relation between complex-regularized polysymplectic and holomorphic symplectic geometry. For an introduction to holomorphic symplectic geometry, see
[
Wag23

Recall that a holomorphic symplectic form on a complex manifold
(W,I)
is a holomorphic 2-form on
W
that is closed and whose restriction to the holomorphic tangent bundle
T^{(1,0)}W
is non-degenerate. A holomorphic Hamiltonian system is a triple
(W,\omega^{\mathbb{C}},F)
where
F\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}W\to\mathbb{C}
is holomorphic. In this case
F
induces a holomorphic vector field
\mathcal{X}_{F}
by
\omega^{\mathbb{C}}(\mathcal{X}_{F},\cdot)=dF
. Our first result is that regularized polysymplectic structures are in one-to-one correspondence with holomorphic symplectic structures.

Proposition 3.1
.

For a complex manifold
(W,I)
define the following two sets

\displaystyle\text{RegPol}(W,I)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{\Omega\mid\Omega\text{ is a complex-regularized polysymplectic form}\}

\displaystyle\text{HolSymp}(W,I)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{\omega^{\mathbb{C}}\mid\omega^{\mathbb{C}}\text{ is a holomorphic symplectic form}\}.

There is a bijection
\alpha\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\text{RegPol}(W,I)\to\text{HolSymp}(W,I)
given by

\alpha(\omega_{1}\otimes\partial_{1}+\omega_{2}\otimes\partial_{2})=\omega_{1}+i\omega_{2}

with inverse given by

\omega^{\mathbb{C}}\mapsto\omega^{\mathbb{C}}\otimes\partial_{t}+\bar{\omega}^{\mathbb{C}}\otimes\partial_{\bar{t}},

where as always
\partial_{t}=\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{1}-i\partial_{2})
and
\partial_{\bar{t}}
is its conjugate.

Proof.

Let
\Omega
be a complex-regularized polysymplectic structure on
(W,I)
and define
\omega^{\mathbb{C}}=\alpha(\Omega)
. Let
X^{\pm}\in T_{\mathbb{C}}W
such that
IX^{\pm}=\pm iX^{\pm}
. Then

\displaystyle\omega^{\mathbb{C}}(\cdot,X^{\pm})

\displaystyle=\omega_{1}(\cdot,X^{\pm})+i\omega_{2}(\cdot,X^{\pm})

\displaystyle=\omega_{1}(\cdot,X^{\pm})-i\omega_{1}(\cdot,IX^{\pm})

\displaystyle=\omega_{1}(\cdot,X^{\pm})\pm\omega_{1}(\cdot,X^{\pm}).

Indeed we see that
\iota_{X}\omega^{\mathbb{C}}=0
for
X\in T^{(0,1)}W
and
\omega^{\mathbb{C}}
is non-degenerate on
T^{(1,0)}W
. At this point,
\omega^{\mathbb{C}}
could still be any smooth (not necessarily holomorphic) section of
\Lambda^{2,0}W
. However, note that since
\omega^{\mathbb{C}}
is closed it must be holomorphic for the following reason. The closedness means that
0=d\omega^{\mathbb{C}}=\partial\omega^{\mathbb{C}}+\bar{\partial}\omega^{\mathbb{C}}
. Since
\partial\omega^{\mathbb{C}}
is a section of
\Lambda^{3,0}W
, whereas
\bar{\partial}\omega^{\mathbb{C}}
is a section of
\Lambda^{2,1}W
they both must vanish. We conclude that
\omega^{\mathbb{C}}
is a holomorphic symplectic form, yielding that
\alpha
is well-defined.

Vice versa, if
\omega^{\mathbb{C}}
is a holomorphic symplectic form then its real and imaginary parts are real symplectic forms and satisfy
\text{Im}(\omega^{\mathbb{C}})=-\text{Re}(\omega^{\mathbb{C}})(\cdot,I\cdot)
, since
\omega^{\mathbb{C}}(\cdot,I\cdot)=i\omega^{\mathbb{C}}
(see
[
Wag23
]
). So the map
\omega^{\mathbb{C}}\mapsto\omega^{\mathbb{C}}\otimes\partial_{t}+\bar{\omega}^{\mathbb{C}}\otimes\partial_{\bar{t}}
is well-defined and is clearly the inverse of
\alpha
.
∎

From this proposition it might seem like complex-regularized polysymplectic geometry is equivalent to holomorphic symplectic geometry. However, even though the manifolds that both frameworks allow are the same, the equations that they study are different. While in polysymplectic geometry Hamiltonians are real-valued and yield PDEs, in holomorphic symplectic geometry one studies holomorphic Hamiltonians, giving rise to holomorphic vector fields. As it turns out, the holomorphic Hamiltonians from holomorphic symplectic geometry correspond precisely to the currents in complex-regularized polysymplectic geometry.

Recall from
definition

1.3
that a function
F\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}W\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
on a polysymplectic manifold
(W,\Omega)
is called a current if
dF_{w}
lies in the image
\Omega^{\flat}_{w}(T_{w}W)
for every
w\in W
. In this case we define
X_{F}
by
X_{F}(w)=(\Omega^{\flat}_{w})^{-1}(dF_{w})
. Heuristically, currents describe symmetries of polysymplectic systems. On the other hand, holomorphic Hamiltonians describe symmetries of holomorphic symplectic systems. Given the correspondence in
proposition

3.1
between complex-regularized polysymplectic and holomorphic symplectic forms, it should come as no surprise that currents in our polysymplectic setting correspond to holomorphic Hamiltonians. This is the content of the next lemma, which will be proven at the end of this section.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
(W,I,\Omega)
be a complex-regularized polysymplectic manifold and
\omega^{\mathbb{C}}=\alpha(\Omega)
. When we identify
\mathbb{R}^{2}\cong\mathbb{C}
and
TW\cong T^{(1,0)}W
, we get that
F\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}W\to\mathbb{R}^{2}\cong\mathbb{C}
is a current if and only if it is a holomorphic function and in that case
X_{F}=\mathcal{X}_{F}
.

As a nice corollary of
proposition

3.1
, we get a Darboux theorem for complex-regularized polysymplectic manifolds.

Corollary 3.3

(Darboux theorem)
.

Let
(W,I,\Omega)
be a complex-regularized polysymplectic manifold. Around every point
w\in W
there are coordinates
q^{i}_{1},q^{i}_{2},p^{i}_{1},p^{i}_{2}
such that

\displaystyle I\frac{\partial}{\partial q^{i}_{1}}=\frac{\partial}{\partial q^{i}_{2}}

\displaystyle I\frac{\partial}{\partial p^{i}_{1}}=-\frac{\partial}{\partial p^{i}_{2}}

and

\displaystyle\omega_{1}=\sum_{i}(dp^{i}_{1}\wedge dq^{i}_{1}+dp^{i}_{2}\wedge dq^{i}_{2})

\displaystyle\omega_{2}=\sum_{i}(dp^{i}_{1}\wedge dq^{i}_{2}-dp^{i}_{2}\wedge dq^{i}_{1}).

Proof.

This follows immediately from
proposition

3.1
and the Darboux theorem in
[
Wag23
]
.
∎

Proof of
lemma

3.2
.

As these statements are local in nature, we may use the Darboux theorem above to compute in local coordinates. Note the minus sign in the complex structure on the
p
-coordinates. An easy computation shows that the image of
\Omega^{\flat}_{w}
is spanned by

\{dq^{j}_{1}\otimes\partial_{1}+dq^{j}_{2}\otimes\partial_{2},dq^{j}_{2}\otimes\partial_{1}-dq^{j}_{1}\otimes\partial_{2},dp^{j}_{1}\otimes\partial_{1}-dp^{j}_{2}\otimes\partial_{2},dp^{j}_{2}\otimes\partial_{1}+dp^{j}_{1}\otimes\partial_{2}\}.

That means that
dF
lies in this image precisely when

\displaystyle\frac{\partial F_{1}}{\partial q^{j}_{1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial F_{2}}{\partial q^{j}_{2}}

\displaystyle-\frac{\partial F_{1}}{\partial q^{j}_{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial F_{2}}{\partial q^{j}_{1}}

\displaystyle-\frac{\partial F_{1}}{\partial p^{j}_{1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial F_{2}}{\partial p^{j}_{2}}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial F_{1}}{\partial p^{j}_{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial F_{2}}{\partial p^{j}_{1}},

which are precisely the Cauchy-Riemann equations.

We also see that for currents
F
the corresponding vector field
X_{F}
is given by

X_{F}=\begin{pmatrix}-\partial_{p^{j}_{1}}F_{1}\\
-\partial_{p^{j}_{2}}F_{1}\\
\partial_{q^{j}_{1}}F_{1}\\
\partial_{q^{j}_{2}}F_{1}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{p^{j}_{2}}F_{2}\\
-\partial_{p^{j}_{1}}F_{2}\\
\partial_{q^{j}_{2}}F_{2}\\
-\partial_{q^{j}_{1}}F_{2}\end{pmatrix}.

On the other hand, the holomorphic vector field
\mathcal{X}_{F}
is given by
(\mathcal{X}_{F})_{q^{j}}=-\partial_{p^{j}}F=-\partial_{p^{j}_{1}}F_{1}-i\partial_{p^{j}_{2}}F_{1}
and
(\mathcal{X}_{F})_{p^{j}}=\partial_{q^{j}}F=\partial_{q^{j}_{1}}F_{1}-i\partial_{q^{j}_{2}}F_{1}
. The identification between the real and holomorphic tangent bundle of
W
takes place by taking
\mathcal{X}_{q^{j}}=X_{q^{j}_{1}}+iX_{q^{j}_{2}}
and
\mathcal{X}_{p^{j}}=X_{p^{j}_{1}}-iX_{p^{j}_{2}}
. The formulas above easily show that indeed
X_{F}
and
\mathcal{X}_{F}
coincide.
∎

In local coordinates, the Euler-Lagrange equations for

Proof.

We may assume

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{\partial L}{\partial q_{1}}(t,q,2\partial_{t}q)&=\partial_{1}\frac{\partial L}{\partial v_{1}}(t,q,2\partial_{t}q)-\partial_{2}\frac{\partial L}{\partial v_{2}}(t,q,2\partial_{t}q)\\

Example 4.2
.

Our main example is a quadratic Lagrangian

Without referring to coordinates, the equivalence between the Hamilton and Lagrange formalisms can also be seen by a variational principle.

Proposition 4.3
.

Suppose
L\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{T}^{2}\times TQ\to\mathbb{R}
is fibrewise convex and

is its Legendre transform as above. Then a map

, given by

is an extremum of the action functional

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{H}(Z)=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\left(\langle p,2\partial_{t}q\rangle-H(t,q,p)\right)\,dt_{1}\wedge dt_{2}.

Proof.

The proof is analogous to the corresponding statement for
d=1
. The idea is that when
Z
is extremal for
\mathcal{A}_{H}
, fiberwise

must be an extremum of the function

Since the Legendre transform is a projection operator on the space of convex functions, this happens precisely when
p=\frac{\partial L}{\partial v}(t,q,2\partial_{t}q)
and
\langle p,2\partial_{t}q\rangle-H(t,q,p)=L(t,q,p)
.
∎

Note that the term

\displaystyle\langle p,2\partial_{t}q\rangle

5
The best of both worlds

The motivation given at the start of this article for introducing the complex-regularized polysymplectic language, was to take advantage of ”ellipticity” of holomorphic symplectic geometry, whilst at the same time keep the connection with PDEs and harmonic maps from polysymplectic geometry. In this section we want to give examples of two problems in which we can clearly see the use for connecting these two worlds. This section is not meant to give rigorous proofs, but rather aims to describe the ideas involved.

Obstructions to holomorphic Lagrangian embeddings

Given a holomorphic symplectic manifold
W
of dimension
4n
and a closed complex manifold

of dimension

Remark 5.1
.

Note that for this construction a theory of harmonic spheres is required, whereas so far we only dealt with harmonic tori. Harmonic maps with boundary

The second problem we want to highlight is the study of harmonic maps with prescribed boundary conditions. This is actually a problem that is natural to ask in the polysymplectic framework, but as we shall see holomorphic symplectic geometry will pop up naturally here.

First, let’s look again at the
d=1
case. For a Riemannian manifold
(\mathcal{Q},g)
one can study the existence of geodesics with prescribed boundary points
q_{0},q_{1}\in\mathcal{Q}
. Geodesics arise as solution to the Hamiltonian equations on
T^{*}\mathcal{Q}
for
H(q,p)=\frac{1}{2}|p|_{g}^{2}
. The boundary conditions amount to fixing the ends of the Hamiltonian path to the Lagrangian submanifolds
T^{*}_{q_{0}}\mathcal{Q}
and
T^{*}_{q_{1}}\mathcal{Q}
in
T^{*}\mathcal{Q}
. Existence results then follow from studying the Lagrangian Floer homology. More generally, one can look for geodesics whose boundary points are confined to some submanifolds
\mathcal{R}_{1},\mathcal{R}_{2}\subseteq\mathcal{Q}
. On the Hamiltonian side, this imposes the condition that the Hamiltonian paths should start and end on the conormal bundles
N^{*}\mathcal{R}_{1}
and
N^{*}\mathcal{R}_{2}
respectively (see
[
Ass16
]
), which are also Lagrangian submanifolds.

For
d=2
, we start with a Kähler manifold
(Q,i,g)
and ask for the existence of a harmonic map
\Sigma\to Q
with boundary
\partial\Sigma
on some submanifold
R\subseteq Q
. As seen before, the equation describing harmonic maps can be described by the complex-regularized polysymplectic Hamiltonian
H(q,p)=\frac{1}{2}|p|^{2}_{g}
on
T^{*}Q
with its standard complex structure
I
and symplectic forms
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}
. Now, the boundary conditions translate into fixing the boundary of
Z\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Sigma\to T^{*}Q
to the conormal bundle
N^{*}R
. Just as before, the conormal bundle is Lagrangian with respect to
\omega_{1}
, but depending on the choice of
R
, it may have more structure. For example, if
R\subseteq Q
is a complex submanifold, then
N^{*}R\subseteq T^{*}Q
is an
I
-complex submanifold that is Lagrangian with respect to both
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}
, or in other words, it is a holomorphic Lagrangian with respect to the holomorphic symplectic form. On the other hand, if
R\subseteq Q
is a maximal totally real submanifold, then there exists a metric
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
on
T^{*}Q
, such that
N^{*}R
is Lagrangian with respect to the symplectic forms
\langle\cdot,I\cdot\rangle
and
\omega_{1}

is

.

Polysymplectic non-squeezing

The first rigidity result we want to present in this context is Gromov’s non-squeezing. Note that a diffeomorphism
\psi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}W\to W
of a complex-regularized polysymplectic manifold preserves
\Omega
if and only if it preserves both of the symplectic forms
\omega_{i}
. This yields the following non-squeezing result.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
\psi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{4n}\to\mathbb{R}^{4n}
be a diffeomorphism preserving the standard complex-regularized polysymplectic form on
\mathbb{R}^{4n}
. Also, let
B^{4n}_{r}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{4n}
be the ball of radius
r
and
B^{2}_{R}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
the ball of radius
R
in the
(q^{j}_{\alpha},p^{j}_{\beta})

and

. If

\psi(B^{4n}_{r})\subseteq B^{2}_{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{4n-2},

then

.

Proof.

Note that the
(q^{j}_{1},p^{j}_{1})
and
(q^{j}_{2},p^{j}_{2})
-planes are symplectic with respect to
\omega_{1}
and the
(q^{j}_{1},p^{j}_{2})
and

. The result follows immediately from Gromov’s non-squeezing.
∎

Even though this non-squeezing result follows immediately from the known theory for symplectic forms, it is important to note that this result definitely does not hold for general polysymplectic forms. To the authors’ knowledge, this is the first version of non-squeezing that is formulated for a class of polysymplectic manifolds. It is also worth mentioning that for

, this result coincides with the lowest dimensional case of a non-squeezing conjecture by Oh for holomorphic volume preserving maps (

As a consequence, it is now possible to study

Corollary 6.2

Let

The action functional and a cuplength result

Given a complex-regularized polysymplectic form there is a natural action functional on maps
\mathbb{T}^{2}\to W
. Assume that
\Omega=d\Theta
is exact and let
d\mathcal{V}=dt_{1}\wedge dt_{2}=-\frac{1}{2i}dt\wedge d\bar{t}
be the volume form on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
. We may contract
\Theta=\theta_{1}\otimes\partial_{1}+\theta_{2}\otimes\partial_{2}
with

to give

\widetilde{\Theta}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\theta_{1}\wedge dt_{2}-\theta_{2}\wedge dt_{1}\in\Lambda^{2}(W\times\mathbb{T}^{2}).

For
Z\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{T}^{2}\to W
, define
\widetilde{Z}=Z\times\text{id}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{T}^{2}\to W\times\mathbb{T}^{2}
and

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(Z)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\widetilde{Z}^{*}\widetilde{\Theta}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}(\theta_{1}(\partial_{1}Z)+\theta_{2}(\partial_{2}Z))d\mathcal{V}.

Let
\mathcal{A}_{H}(Z)=\mathcal{A}(Z)-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}H(Z)d\mathcal{V}
. The critical points of
\mathcal{A}_{H}
are precisely the solutions to the polysymplectic equation
dH=\Omega^{\sharp}(dZ)
. Note that this action functional is real-valued, making it possible to study gradient lines, whereas in holomorphic symplectic geometry one needs to make choices in order to get a real-valued action. Also, when
W=T^{*}Q
and
\Omega
is the complex-regularized polysymplectic form defined in
example

2.4

proposition

4.3
.

To study gradient lines of the action functional, we need to put a metric on the space of maps from
\mathbb{T}^{2}
to
W

-metric on this space, coming from a metric on

Proposition 7.1
.

For every complex-regularized polysymplectic manifold
(W,I,\omega_{1}\otimes\partial_{1}+\omega_{2}\otimes\partial_{2})
there exists a Riemannian metric
g
and anti-commuting almost complex structures

and
K
on
W
, such that

\displaystyle\omega_{1}

\displaystyle=g(\cdot,J\cdot)

\displaystyle\omega_{2}

\displaystyle=g(\cdot,K\cdot)

\displaystyle K

\displaystyle=IJ.

Proof.

See
appendix

A
.
∎

Pick a metric
g
on
W
as in

. Then the

-gradient of

is given by

\textup{grad}\,\mathcal{A}(Z)=J\partial_{1}Z+K\partial_{2}Z=\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\cancel{\partial}Z,

meaning that its negative gradient lines are described by the Fueter equation

\displaystyle I\partial_{s}Z+K\partial_{1}Z-J\partial_{2}Z=0.

Thus we may expect the role of holomorphic curves in symplectic geometry to be replaced by Fueter maps in complex-regularized polysymplectic geometry. The Fueter equation is the quaternionic analogue of the Cauchy-Riemann equations. Just as for holomorphic curves, there are compactness and Fredholm theories developed for Fueter maps (see e.g.
[

]
).

Gradient lines of the action functional above may be used to prove a cuplength result for solutions of

Remark 7.3

Floer maps

Now that we have the necessary bounds on the orbits, we turn to Floer maps. A Floer map is defined as a negative

-gradient flow line of the action and is given by

\displaystyle\partial_{s}Z+J\partial_{1}Z+K\partial_{2}Z=\nabla\widetilde{H}^{\rho}(Z)

(11)

\displaystyle J=\begin{pmatrix}0&-\textup{Id}_{2n}\\
\textup{Id}_{2n}&0\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle K=\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\

\displaystyle\partial_{s}q-2\partial_{\bar{t}}p

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\widetilde{H}^{\rho}}{\partial q}

\displaystyle\partial_{s}p+2\partial_{t}q

Lemma 8.3
.

Let

Theorem 9.1

We let

\displaystyle(d\mathcal{F}^{r})_{Z}=D-\beta_{r}(s)S_{Z},
