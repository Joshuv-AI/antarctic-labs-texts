The prevalence and association between electronic cigarette use and dry eye symptom severity among Palestinian university students: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Zaid Sawaftah, Ahmad Sawafta, Ali Bani Odah, Marah Abu Zant, Hamza Abualhasan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDry eye symptoms are common and can negatively affect visual function and quality of life, particularly among young adults. Electronic cigarette use has increased markedly in this age group; however, evidence regarding its association with dry eye symptoms, especially in Middle Eastern populations, remains limited.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among undergraduate students enrolled at multiple Palestinian universities in the West Bank between January and May 2025. Data were collected using a structured self-administered questionnaire assessing sociodemographic characteristics, smoking status, and dry eye symptoms measured by the Ocular Surface Disease Index (OSDI). Smoking status was categorized as non-smoking, exclusive e-cigarette use, exclusive traditional cigarette smoking, or dual use of e-cigarettes and traditional cigarettes. Background

Dry eye symptoms, a common manifestation of ocular surface disease, are characterized by tear film instability and ocular surface inflammation, resulting in symptoms such as dryness, burning, irritation, photophobia, and visual discomfort. These symptoms can negatively affect visual function, academic performance, and overall quality of life, particularly among young adults [1]. Several validated instruments are available to assess dry eye symptoms and related ocular surface complaints in both clinical and epidemiological settings, including the Ocular Surface Disease Index [2], the Dry Eye Questionnaire [3], and the McMonnies Dry Eye Questionnaire [4]. Among these instruments, the Ocular Surface Disease Index (OSDI) was selected in this study due to its strong validation, wide use in epidemiological research, and high reliability in assessing symptom severity in large population-based studies.

Electronic cigarettes (e-cigarettes), also known as vapes or vapor pens, are battery-powered devices that heat a liquid typically containing humectants, nicotine, and flavoring agents into an aerosol that is inhaled by the user [5].

Despite the growing body of evidence indicating potential health risks associated with e-cigarette use, these products have gained substantial popularity among young adults worldwide. In Palestine, the prevalence of e-cigarette use among university students has been reported to be notably high, reflecting an emerging public health concern [6]. Furthermore, evidence suggests that e-cigarette users often demonstrate significant gaps in knowledge regarding the potential health risks of these devices [7].

Conventional tobacco smoking has been consistently associated with adverse ocular outcomes, including dry eye symptoms and other ocular surface abnormalities [6].

Several biological mechanisms may explain the association between cigarette and e-cigarette exposure and dry eye pathology [5]. Aerosolized particles and chemical constituents present in cigarette smoke and e-cigarette vapor may disrupt tear film stability by altering the lipid layer and increasing tear evaporation [8]. In addition, oxidative stress and inflammatory mediators generated by smoking exposure may induce ocular surface inflammation and epithelial damage. Experimental studies have also suggested that smoking-related exposure may contribute to meibomian gland dysfunction, further impairing tear film homeostasis and promoting evaporative dry eye symptoms [5–8].

However, evidence regarding the ocular health effects of e-cigarette use remains limited. The existing literature examining e-cigarettes and ocular health is sparse, and most available studies have been conducted in Western populations, particularly in the United States. Consequently, the generalizability of these findings to other regions, including Middle Eastern countries such as Palestine, remains uncertain [8–10].

Available evidence has largely originated from Western populations, and data from Middle Eastern university-based populations remain scarce. Therefore, the aim of the present study is to estimate the prevalence of e-cigarette use and dry eye symptoms among Palestinian undergraduate students and to examine the association between smoking status particularly e-cigarette use—and dry eye symptom severity. Understanding this relationship may have important implications for health promotion efforts, including increasing awareness of potential ocular risks and informing preventive strategies among young adult populations.

Methods

Study design

This was a cross-sectional study conducted to examine the association between smoking status, particularly e-cigarette use, and dry eye symptom severity among undergraduate students.

Setting

The study was conducted among undergraduate students recruited from six primary recruitment sites in the West Bank between January and May 2025: An-Najah National University, Arab American University–Palestine, Palestine Technical University–Kadoorie, Birzeit University, Hebron University, and Bethlehem University. Participants

Students were eligible for inclusion if they were aged 18 years or older, enrolled as undergraduate students at one of the participating Palestinian universities during the study period, and willing to provide implied informed consent by completing the questionnaire.

Participants were eligible regardless of smoking status, including non-smokers, exclusive e-cigarette users, exclusive traditional cigarette smokers, and dual users of e-cigarettes and traditional cigarettes, as defined by the operational exposure criteria below.

Participants were excluded if they reported a history of diagnosed ocular diseases, including glaucoma, cataracts, keratoconjunctivitis, or previously diagnosed dry eye disease by a clinician; prior ocular surgery or ocular trauma; or systemic conditions known to independently affect ocular health, such as diabetes mellitus, Sjögren syndrome, thyroid disease, or collagen vascular diseases [11–13]. Participants were also excluded if they reported current use of medications known to alter tear production or ocular surface integrity, including antihistamines, anticholinergics, antiglaucoma medications, proton pump inhibitors, or isotretinoin [14, 15]. Additional exclusion criteria included contact lens use within the preceding month or ocular surgery within the past six months.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of An-Najah National University (IRB protocol number: Med. Feb. 2025/17). Participation was voluntary, and informed consent was implied through completion of the questionnaire. All data were collected anonymously and handled in accordance with ethical research standards.

Study size

The required sample size was calculated using OpenEpi’s online sample size calculator, assuming a population size of 70,000 students, a 95% confidence level, a 5% margin of error, and a design effect (DEFF) of 1, using the standard formula [16]. The minimum required sample size was estimated at 383 participants. To account for potential nonresponse or incomplete questionnaires, the target sample size was increased by 25%, resulting in a final target of 468 participants.

Post hoc power analysis was conducted to evaluate whether the achieved sample size was sufficient to detect an association between e-cigarette use and dry eye symptoms. Prior evidence indicates that the prevalence of dry eye symptoms among e-cigarette users and non-users is approximately 0.33 and 0.06, respectively [17]. Based on these estimates, a sample consisting of at least 98 e-cigarette users and 366 non-users provides 100% statistical power to detect a difference in dry eye symptom prevalence between groups at a two-sided α level of 0.05. Power calculations were performed using the Vanderbilt University Clinical and Translational Science Institute online power and sample size calculator [18]. Therefore, the final study sample was sufficient to detect a meaningful association between e-cigarette use and dry eye symptoms, if such an association truly exists.

Sampling strategy

Participants were recruited using a convenience sampling approach due to logistical constraints related to access to university enrollment lists. Recruitment was conducted across multiple universities and faculties during different days and times to increase sample diversity. The use of convenience sampling may introduce selection bias. To mitigate potential selection bias, recruitment was conducted across multiple universities, faculties, and time periods.

Variables

Operational definitions

Operational definitions were used to classify the study outcome and exposure variables to ensure consistency and reproducibility across participants.

Outcome variable

The primary outcome was dry eye symptom severity, assessed using the Ocular Surface Disease Index (OSDI). The OSDI is a validated, self-administered questionnaire used to evaluate ocular surface–related symptoms (e.g., dryness, irritation, and visual disturbance). It consists of 12 items scored on a 5-point Likert scale, generating a total score from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating greater symptom burden. Because the OSDI is symptom-based, the outcome in this study reflects OSDI-defined dry eye symptoms rather than a clinical diagnosis of dry eye disease. Based on established cutoffs, OSDI scores were categorized as normal (0–12), mild (13–22), moderate (23–32), and severe (≥ 33) [2].

Exposure variable

The primary exposure variable was smoking status, categorized based on self-reported use into four groups: non-smokers, exclusive e-cigarette users, exclusive traditional cigarette smokers, and dual users of both e-cigarettes and traditional cigarettes. Exclusive e-cigarette use was defined as e-cigarette use at least once per week for a minimum of three months, with no use of conventional cigarettes or other tobacco products during the preceding 12 months [19]. Dual use was defined as concurrent use of both e-cigarettes and traditional cigarettes for at least three months. Non-smokers were defined as individuals who reported no use of e-cigarettes or conventional cigarettes within the preceding 12 months [20].

Covariates

Covariates included age (continuous), sex (male/female), and academic year. These variables were selected a priori based on their potential association with dry eye symptoms and smoking behaviors and were included in multivariable analyses as potential confounders.

Data sources and measurement

Data were collected using a structured questionnaire developed for the purpose of this study. The questionnaire was administered in Arabic, the native language of the participants, to ensure clarity and comprehension. An English version was subsequently prepared for reporting purposes and included as a supplementary file.

The questionnaire consisted of three sections: (1) sociodemographic characteristics and smoking behaviors (including electronic cigarettes, traditional cigarettes, and waterpipe use), (2) ocular and visual health-related factors including screen time, contact lens use, and self-reported history of dry eye diagnosis, and (3) the Ocular Surface Disease Index (OSDI), a validated instrument used to assess dry eye symptom severity.

The researcher-developed sections were based on relevant literature and expert review to ensure content validity. A pilot study involving 20 students was conducted to assess clarity and feasibility, and minor modifications were made accordingly.

Quantitative variables

Age was treated as a continuous variable in the analysis. Dry eye symptom severity was treated as an ordinal outcome based on established OSDI categories. Smoking status was analyzed as a categorical variable with four predefined groups.

Statistical methods

Univariate analyses were conducted to describe participant characteristics using means and standard deviations or medians and interquartile ranges for continuous variables, and frequencies and percentages for categorical variables.

Bivariate associations between smoking status and dry eye symptom severity were examined using ordinal logistic regression. Ordinal logistic regression was selected due to the ordered nature of the dry eye severity outcome.

Multivariable ordinal logistic regression (proportional odds model) was subsequently performed to estimate adjusted associations between smoking status and dry eye symptoms severity, adjusting for age (continuous), sex, and academic year. Variables with p-values < 0.25 in univariable analysis were included in the multivariable model to adjust for potential confounding factors, as previously recommended in epidemiological modeling approaches [21].

Odds ratios (ORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. The proportional odds assumption was assessed using the test of parallel lines. Missing data were minimal and handled using complete-case analysis. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, with a two-sided p-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 450 undergraduate students were included in the final analysis. The sample characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The mean age of the participants was 21.46 years. Females accounted for 45.3% of the sample, while males represented 54.7%. The majority of participants were non-smokers (70.9%), followed by exclusive e-cigarette users (10.4%), exclusive traditional cigarette smokers (7.3%), and dual users of e-cigarettes and traditional cigarettes (11.3%). Most participants resided in cities (57.8%), followed by villages (39.3%) and camps (2.9%). Regarding dry eye symptom severity, the highest proportion of participants had normal symptoms (41.8%), followed by severe (27.3%), mild (19.1%), and moderate (11.8%). There were no missing data in variables included in the regression model.

Association between smoking status and dry eye symptom severity

Crude and adjusted associations between smoking status and dry eye symptom severity are presented in Table 2. In the crude analysis, Traditional cigarette smoking demonstrated an inverse association with dry eye symptom severity compared with non-smoking; however, this association was no longer statistically significant after adjustment. In the adjusted model, dual use of e-cigarettes and traditional cigarettes was significantly associated with higher odds of more severe dry eye symptoms compared with non-smoking (OR = 2.35; 95% CI 1.23–4.49; p = 0.010). No statistically significant associations were observed for exclusive e-cigarette use in either crude or adjusted models (adjusted OR = 1.64; 95% CI 0.89–3.02; p = 0.113). The test of parallel lines was not statistically significant (p = 0.931), indicating that the proportional odds assumption was satisfied.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is one of the first studies to examine the association between dry eye symptoms and e-cigarette use among undergraduate students in Palestinian universities in the West Bank. The Middle East, including Palestine, remains an underrepresented region in ocular epidemiological research, particularly regarding the effects of emerging tobacco products such as electronic cigarettes. This study addresses a significant gap in the literature by providing one of the first population-based assessments of the association between e-cigarette use and dry eye symptoms in a Middle Eastern university setting, where evidence remains scarce.

The present study demonstrated that smoking behaviors, particularly dual use of e-cigarettes and conventional cigarettes, were associated with increased severity of dry eye symptoms compared with non-smoking. This finding suggests a potential additive or synergistic effect of combined exposures on ocular surface health. Importantly, these associations were independent of academic year and other sociodemographic factors that may influence dry eye symptom risk.

In our sample, 21.7% of students reported e-cigarette use (exclusive or dual). This rising trend underscores the urgency of investigating the potential ocular health consequences of e-cigarette exposure. While several studies have examined the prevalence of dry eye symptoms among university students [22–24], there remains a paucity of research specifically evaluating the relationship between dry eye symptoms and e-cigarette use, particularly in Middle Eastern populations.

In the present study, the prevalence of dry eye symptoms was 58.2%, which is broadly consistent with findings reported among university students in other regions, although variability across studies is evident [24–26]. Such variation is likely attributable to differences in environmental conditions, including humidity and climate, as well as behavioral factors such as screen exposure and lifestyle habits. Methodological differences, particularly in the assessment tools and diagnostic thresholds used, may also contribute to inconsistencies in reported prevalence. The relatively high prevalence observed in this study aligns with findings from regions with similar environmental characteristics, supporting the role of environmental and lifestyle-related contributors to dry eye symptoms. This relatively high prevalence may be attributed to prolonged digital device use, increased screen exposure among students, and environmental factors such as low humidity and dust exposure in the region, which are known to exacerbate tear film instability [10, 27–31].

Within this context, the observed association patterns should also be interpreted in light of regional and global differences in smoking behaviors and e-cigarette regulation. This similarity may reflect converging global patterns of e-cigarette use among young adults; however, contextual factors such as environmental exposure, cultural smoking behaviors, and differences in device regulation in Middle Eastern countries may modify the magnitude of ocular effects compared with Western populations.

Consistent with prior literature, female participants in the present study had significantly higher odds of developing dry eye symptoms compared with males (OR = 2.52; 95% CI, 1.70–3.75). This sex-based disparity may be attributed to hormonal influences involving the hypothalamic–pituitary axis, sex steroids, glucocorticoids, thyroid hormones, insulin, and insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1), as well as immune modulation and lacrimal functional unit regulation. These biological mechanisms have been widely implicated in dry eye symptoms pathophysiology and are supported by findings from multiple population-based and cross-sectional studies [32–35].

For instance, a study conducted among 769 Palestinian adults reported a 1.5-fold increased risk of dry eye symptoms among females compared with males, while a large cross-sectional study of 1,858 Saudi participants found that females had significantly higher odds of dry eye symptoms (OR = 2.1; 95% CI, 1.7–2.6) [32–35].

With respect to smoking behaviors, our study found that dual use of e-cigarettes and conventional cigarettes was significantly associated with increased odds of dry eye symptom severity (OR = 2.35; 95% CI, 1.23–4.49). Our finding that dual use is associated with more severe ocular symptoms aligns with evidence from young adults in the United States [19]. This consistency across different geographic regions suggests that the ocular toxicity of e-cigarettes may be independent of ethnic or environmental variations, potentially driven by the universal chemical constituents of vaping aerosols. Furthermore, the lack of statistical significance in exclusive e-cigarette users in our study contrasting with some Western findings might be due to differences in ‘vaping topography’ (e.g., puff duration and frequency) or the specific nicotine concentrations prevalent in the Palestinian market, which warrants further investigation. This pattern overall suggests a potential dose–response relationship, where combined exposure to both combustion products and aerosolized chemicals results in greater cumulative ocular toxicity compared with single-source exposure [19].

Although our findings align with evidence suggesting a relationship between electronic cigarette use and dry eye symptoms, the existing literature remains inconsistent. Several systematic reviews and cross-sectional studies have reported that e-cigarette users experience higher OSDI scores and more severe ocular surface symptoms compared with non-users. For example, Md Isa et al. demonstrated significantly elevated dry eye symptom scores among vapers, while Kalayci et al. also reported worse tear film parameters and reduced ocular surface stability in e-cigarette users. However, other studies have suggested that the association may vary depending on exposure intensity, duration of use, and device characteristics, with some reporting weaker or non-significant associations after adjustment for confounders. These discrepancies across studies likely reflect differences in exposure definitions, population characteristics, and outcome assessment methods, particularly between symptom-based questionnaires and objective clinical measures [36, 37].

Differences in statistical significance across studies may be attributable to variations in exposure duration, device characteristics, sample size, and population demographics.

These mechanisms provide biological plausibility for the associations observed in our study. The mechanisms underlying the association between e-cigarette use and dry eye symptoms are likely multifactorial and related to chronic disruption of tear film stability and meibomian gland function. Experimental and clinical studies have shown that e-cigarette exposure is associated with reduced noninvasive tear breakup time (NITBUT). For instance, adult male vapers exhibited significantly lower NITBUT values (3.31 ± 0.97; P < 0.0001), while another study among 25 male adults reported a significant reduction in tear breakup time (6.96 ± 2.31; P = 0.002). Tear production, assessed using the Schirmer test, has shown inconsistent findings. Kalayci et al. reported reduced tear production among e-cigarette users, potentially due to alterations in lipid layer metabolites affecting the ocular surface. In contrast, Md Isa et al. observed increased tear production, possibly reflecting a compensatory homeostatic response to tear film instability. Meibography, which provides a direct and objective assessment of meibomian gland morphology, has demonstrated significantly greater gland loss and irregular gland distribution among e-cigarette users compared with controls, further supporting the role of meibomian gland dysfunction in dry eye symptoms pathogenesis [26, 36–39].

The findings of the present study highlight the need for further longitudinal and qualitative research to elucidate the temporal and causal pathways linking e-cigarette use and dry eye symptoms. Future studies should explore specific exposure characteristics, including duration of use, device voltage, and e-liquid composition, to better identify factors most detrimental to ocular surface health. Such evidence has important clinical and public health implications, particularly for increasing awareness among clinicians and young adults regarding the ocular risks associated with dual use of e-cigarettes and conventional cigarettes. Moreover, these findings may inform the development of targeted interventions and preventive strategies aimed at reducing dry eye symptoms burden in this population.

The present study has several strengths. First, it provides data from a Middle Eastern population, contributing region-specific evidence to a literature largely dominated by Western studies, this is of particular importance as it builds upon previous work in the region (e.g., Jordan and Saudi Arabia) that focused primarily on traditional tobacco [32–35], by now addressing the emerging ocular risks of electronic cigarettes in the Palestinian context. Second, the observed association between dual smoking and dry eye symptoms was independent of multiple potential confounders, including academic year, age, and sociodemographic characteristics. Third, the use of a validated screening instrument, the Ocular Surface Disease Index, enhanced measurement reliability and facilitated comparison with existing studies. Fourth, the study addresses an important public health issue by highlighting potential ocular hazards associated with emerging smoking behaviors. Finally, the relatively large sample size enhances statistical power and improves the diversity of the sample of Palestinian university students.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, because exposure and outcome were measured at the same time, the study cannot confirm the timing of dry eye symptom onset relative to smoking initiation and therefore cannot establish causality. Second, dry eye was assessed using a symptom-based questionnaire (OSDI) without objective clinical tests (e.g., tear breakup time, Schirmer test, or ocular surface staining); therefore, the findings should be interpreted as reflecting dry eye symptoms rather than clinically diagnosed dry eye disease. Additionally, categorization of the OSDI score into ordinal groups may have led to loss of information and reduced statistical sensitivity, potentially attenuating dose–response relationships between smoking exposure and dry eye symptom severity. Third, reliance on self-administered questionnaires introduces the possibility of reporting and measurement bias. Fourth, information on e-cigarette device characteristics (e.g., voltage, nicotine concentration, and frequency of use) was not collected and may influence symptom severity.

Fifth, although key confounders were adjusted for, residual confounding from unmeasured factors, including environmental conditions such as humidity, dust exposure, and daily screen time duration, could not be entirely ruled out. Despite these limitations, our study provides a robust baseline for understanding the ocular health implications of emerging smoking trends in the Palestinian context. Future longitudinal studies incorporating objective clinical measures are warranted to establish causality and further elucidate the long-term ocular effects of e-cigarette use in this population [19].

Conclusion

In this cross-sectional study of undergraduate students from Palestinian universities, dual use of e-cigarettes and conventional cigarettes was independently associated with greater severity of dry eye symptoms. Although exclusive e-cigarette use was not significantly associated with dry eye symptom severity after adjustment, the observed trend toward higher symptom burden suggests a potential adverse effect that warrants further investigation. The high prevalence of dry eye symptoms observed in this young adult population underscores the need for targeted public health awareness campaigns and clinical screening. Future prospective studies are needed to determine the long-term ocular health implications of emerging nicotine delivery systems [36–39].
