Diagnostic Concordance Between Physiotherapist and Emergency Physicians for Patients With a Musculoskeletal Disorder in the Emergency Department
Authors: Rose Gagnon, Kadija Perreault, Eveline Matifat, François Desmeules, Simon Berthelot, Luc J. Hébert
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundMany patients present to the emergency department (ED) with a musculoskeletal disorder. In consequence, some hospitals have integrated autonomous management of musculoskeletal disorders by physiotherapists after triage. Although potential benefits were demonstrated, few studies have examined the agreement between physiotherapists' and emergency physicians' diagnosis. A better understanding of diagnostic concordance between physiotherapists and emergency physicians could inform the implementation of this care model.Methods and FindingsSecondary analysis of data obtained through a pilot pragmatic randomised clinical trial. Data from patients presenting a minor musculoskeletal disorder managed by a physiotherapist and an emergency physician were used. Diagnostic concordance was examined using raw agreement and Gwet's first‐order agreement coefficient. Thirty‐six participants were assessed by both professionals (36.8 ± 18.2 years; W: 55.6%). Overall raw agreement was 86.1% and the diagnostic concordance was almost perfect (Gwet's AC1: 0.84, 95% CI: 0.69–0.98). The most common disagreement was when physiotherapists suspected a bone fracture or contusion, whereas emergency physicians diagnosed a ligament or meniscus disorder.ConclusionsOur results show excellent diagnostic concordance between physiotherapists and emergency physicians, thus supporting the safety of physiotherapy care in the ED. More studies are needed to confirm these results with a larger variety of diagnoses and age strata.

Introduction

Overcrowding and long waits in the emergency department (ED) have been worrisome problems for several years in many hospitals around the world. Overcrowding in the ED is thought to lead to several adverse effects such as increased time to care, increased length of stay in the ED, and increased medical errors (Bernstein et al. 2009; Carter, Pouch, and Larson 2014; Epstein et al. 2012; Forero et al. 2019; Santos et al. 2016). One of the main causes of the increase in the number of ED visits is the unavailability of primary care services (Lebec and Jogodka 2009; Roberge et al. 2007). In such a context, many patients turn to the ED for the management of their condition (Idil et al. 2018), especially when presenting musculoskeletal disorders (MSKDs). MSKDs are among the most frequent complaints, representing between 18% and 25% of all visits in some EDs (Bird, Thompson, and Williams 2016; CHUQ‐UL 2015; Weinstein, Yelin, and Watkins‐Castillo 2014). Patients who consult for MSKDs are often assigned a low priority at ED triage due to the non‐urgent nature of their condition (Gill and Stella 2013). As a consequence, hospitals in several countries have integrated, after initial nurse triage, autonomous management by physiotherapists (PT) of MSKDS, often labelled as advanced practice PTs. Studies conducted in the United Kingdom and in Australia indicate that initial assessment by a PT in the ED rather than by an emergency physician (EP) for MSKDs could reduce pain and disability, the average time to care, the average length of stay in the ED, the number of redundant medical consultations, the number of unnecessary drug prescriptions and imaging tests, an thus optimise overall management (Bird, Thompson, and Williams 2016; de Gruchy, Granger, and Gorelik 2015; Matifat, Méquignon, et al. 2019; Sohil, Hao, and Mark 2017).

Although the current literature on the integration of a PT in different clinical contexts such as EDs or orthopaedic clinics demonstrates potential benefits, few studies have examined the diagnostic concordance between PTs and physicians (Desmeules et al. 2012). While ways of reporting diagnostic concordance vary between studies, some studies carried out in orthopaedic clinics have reported proportions of diagnostic agreement between PTs and orthopaedic surgeons ranging from 74.5% to 90% and Kappa coefficients ranging from 0.69 to 0.70 (Desmeules et al. 2012). No significant differences were observed in terms of diagnostic accuracy between the two professionals with regard to prescriptions of arthroscopy or MRI (Desmeules et al. 2012). Regarding the ED, to our knowledge, only one previous study specifically looked at the diagnostic concordance between a PT and an EP in the ED (Lafrance et al. 2023). In a study conducted in 2019 (n = 113), Matifat, Perreault, et al. (2019) reported a raw agreement between the two professionals of 87% and a Kappa coefficient of 0.81 (95% CI: 0.72–0.90) (Matifat, Perreault, et al. 2019). However, the agreement was lower with regard to treatment modalities to be used and recommended discharge plan. Moreover, in a study by Sutton et al. (2015), a PT in the ED managed more than 1300 patients without any misdiagnoses or adverse events (95% CI: 0.0%–0.3%). A better understanding of diagnostic concordance between the PT and the EP in the ED could support decisions regarding the implementation of this care model.

Therefore, the purpose of this study was to determine the level of diagnostic concordance between EP and PT for patients presenting to the ED with a MSKD.

Methods

Study Design

This study is a secondary analysis of data obtained through a pilot pragmatic randomised controlled clinical trial that was conducted in a major academic ED (Quebec, Canada) (Gagnon et al. 2021). Two groups were recruited over a 24‐week period: the intervention group was managed by a PT and an EP in the ED and the control group was managed in the usual way by an EP. Participants were recruited consecutively, and randomised using block randomisation with stratification by affected body region. This study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the CHU de Québec ‐ Université Laval and registered at the US National Institutes of Health #NCT04009369.

Study Population

To be considered eligible, patients presenting to the ED had to meet the following criteria: (1) being aged between 18 and 80 years; (2) present a minor peripheral or vertebral MSKD with or without trauma; (3) have a triage score of 3 (urgent), 4 (less urgent), or 5 (non‐urgent) according to the Canadian Emergency Department Triage and Acuity Scale classification (Beveridge et al. 1998); (4) have the ability to legally consent to participate; (5) understand French in order to complete the study questionnaires orally or in writing and (6) be a beneficiary of the provincial health insurance plan (Régie de l'assurance maladie du Québec). Patients were not considered eligible if they had one of the following criteria: (1) a MSKD requiring emergency care (e.g., open fracture, dislocation, open wound); (2) a red flag (e.g., progressive neurological disorder, infectious symptoms); (3) a concomitant unstable clinical condition (e.g., pulmonary, cardiac, digestive and/or psychiatric) or (4) if they were already hospitalised or living in a long‐term care facility.

Data Collection and Procedures

Once the eligible patients had been triaged and their information registered into the ED's electronic information system, the research coordinator (R.G.) met with them to explain the project, answer their questions, and obtain their informed consent. Each participant was then instructed to complete a questionnaire in which they were asked to provide socio‐demographic information such as age, biological sex, presence of self‐reported other health conditions and income level. Thereafter, they were assigned to one of the two groups according to the randomisation sequence previously stated. Each participant assigned to the intervention group was managed by a PT and an EP. Following his interview, the PT conducted a brief physical examination of each participant. The PT was then asked to complete a standardised form detailing the diagnosis and recommended interventions. He also completed his usual clinical note in the patient's medical record. The standardised form and a copy of his clinical note were then included in the consultation request submitted to the EP. The ongoing internal policy at the ED during our study was to have all participants in the intervention group seen by the EP following the PT's assessment, which may have prevented the two clinicians from always being perfectly independent. Given the pragmatic nature of this study and the current legislative context, the EP had access to the assessment and diagnosis of the PT, but it is impossible to know whether the EP ever actually consulted the PT's clinical note. In addition, the professionals were free to communicate with each other regarding the management of individual patients. The diagnosis made by the EP was extracted from each participant's electronic records after their ED stay.

Outcome Measures

Our primary outcome was the diagnosis made by the PT and the EP for all participants in the intervention group. Since the vocabulary used by each professional for the same condition can sometimes differ, we first used the International Classification of Diseases 11 (ICD‐11) as the basis for our analyses (ICD‐11 for Mortality and Morbidity Statistics 2022). Hence, the diagnoses made by each professional were associated with the corresponding ICD‐11 diagnostic code. We then identified the higher order diagnostic categories associated with the initial diagnostic codes, again based on the ICD‐11. The diagnostic categories were as follows: disorder of ligament or meniscus (e.g., sprain), soft tissue disorder (muscle, fascia, tendon, cartilage), fracture or contusion, dizziness and giddiness, conditions associated with the spine, and overuse or chronic pain (e.g., osteoarthritis, recurrent instability). The assignment of ICD‐11 diagnostic codes, grouping into higher order diagnostic categories, and comparison of diagnoses was performed by two independent assessors (R.G. and S.B.). A third evaluator was involved to resolve any disagreement (L.J.H.; this step was used for three cases).

Data Analysis

Participants' characteristics were analysed using descriptive statistics. For the concordance analyses, we considered the primary diagnosis made by both providers. We examined the overall diagnostic concordance using the raw agreement and Gwet's first‐order agreement coefficient (AC1) (R Software, 4.0.2; package irrCAC, 1.0, proc gwet.ac1.raw) and reported a corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI). In addition to being less affected by prevalence and marginal probability than Cohen's Kappa, Gwet's AC1 has been shown to have a more stable inter‐rater reliability coefficient than Cohen's kappa (Wongpakaran et al. 2013). We interpreted the strength of agreement between the two professionals as proposed by Landis et Koch and reported by Sim et Wright. According to the following scale: ≤ 0 = poor, 0.01–0.20 = slight, 0.21–0.40 = fair, 0.41–0.60 = moderate, 0.61–0.80 = substantial et 0.81–1 = almost perfect (Sim and Wright 2005).

Results

Participants

Five hundred eighty‐nine participants were assessed for eligibility between September 2018 and March 2019 and 78 of them were recruited. The reasons for exclusion and the demographic characteristics of all participants are presented in the randomized controlled trial article (Gagnon et al. 2021). Forty participants were assigned to the intervention group and 38 to the control group. Four participants were managed by the PT but left the ED before being managed by the EP. Therefore, only 36 participants were included in the concordance analyses. The demographic characteristics of the participants managed by the PT are presented in Table 1. Participants' mean age was 36.8 years (SD: 18.2), and 55.6% were female. The mean pain intensity was 6.9 on a 0–10 scale (SD: 2.2) and the mean pain interference on function was 4.2 out of 10 (SD: 2.2). Moreover, participants had a mean pain catastrophizing score of 18.5 (/100) (SD: 13.3).

Diagnostic Concordance Between PT and EP

Overall, raw agreement between providers was 86.1% and the Gwet's AC1 was 0.84 (95% CI: 0.69–0.98, p < 0.0001) (Table 2). The matrix of concordance between the PT and the EP is presented in Figure 1. The most common reason for disagreement was the PT suspecting a bone fracture or contusion, whereas the EP's diagnosis was a ligament or meniscus disorder. Two participants were diagnosed with a disorder of ligament or meniscus by the PT and a soft tissue disorder by the EP. No adverse events were reported during the study in either group.

Discussion

The aim of our study was to determine the level of diagnostic concordance between EP and PT for patients presenting to the ED with a MSKD. Overall, we observed an excellent diagnostic concordance between the PT and the EP.

While the level of diagnostic concordance obtained in our study was considered almost perfect, a few minor disagreements between the two professionals' diagnoses were observed. Indeed, on three occasions, the PT judged that the participant had a fracture or contusion, whereas the EP's diagnosis was a ligament or meniscus disorder. These could be largely explained by the fact that, at the time of our study, the PT was not legally allowed to independently prescribe imaging tests. In some contexts, PTs in an advanced role can autonomously prescribe and summarily interpret some imaging exams such as plain radiographs following intensive training (Morris, Vine, and Grimmer 2015). In our study, the PT had to make a diagnosis based only on clinical signs and symptoms that could sometimes suggest a fracture and not just a sprain. The EP had access to any imaging tests they deemed necessary, allowing them to quickly confirm or rule out the presence of a fracture on a plain radiograph. Thus, although there was some level of discordance between the two professionals, it is possible that the level of diagnostic agreement could have been even higher if the PT would have been allowed to independently prescribe and interpret a plain radiograph. However, the PT was still able to identify all the fractures in the intervention group without the use of imaging tests.

Our study is the second to address diagnostic concordance between a PT and EP in the ED. The study of diagnostic concordance is important to support the development of innovative care models. The results of this study highlight the potential role of various professionals such as the PT in developing new effective care trajectories in the ED for patients presenting with a MSKD (Decary et al. 2017). Excellent diagnostic concordance most likely indicates the safety of having a PT in the ED for patients with a variety of MSKDs. Nonetheless, it is important to bear in mind that the roles and responsibilities of PTs working in EDs vary from country to country (e.g., prescription of complex imaging, medication, samples, etc.) (Physiotherapy 2023). It would therefore be interesting in the future to assess the impact of these different professional roles on diagnostic concordance between PTs and EPs.

There is currently no consensus definition of diagnostic concordance in the literature. Studies that address this concept often take place in a variety of settings such as orthopaedics clinics or EDs and include various professionals with varying levels of expertise. Therefore, it can be arduous to compare results between them (Decary et al. 2017; Lowry et al. 2020; Matifat, Perreault, et al. 2019). Moreover, diagnostic concordance analyses often do not allow to consider the level of diagnostic precision provided by each professional. Results from this study must therefore be interpreted with caution.

Although our sample size was small, the participants in the study presented a variety of MSKDs as previously reported (Gagnon et al. 2021). A major limitation of our study is that the EP had the possibility of consulting the PT's clinical note before making his own assessment and was therefore not entirely blinded. However, our study used a pragmatic design and hence assessed real‐life practice where the physicians had access to the PT's files. Furthermore, the results presented in our study are very similar to those obtained by Matifat et al. in their study where the PT and the EP were independent (Matifat, Perreault, et al. 2019). It is thus possible that this limitation had a minimal impact on the diagnostic concordance level obtained. Finally, some diagnostic categories based on the ICD‐11 such as ‘Conditions associated with the spine’ were very imprecise, which may have had an upward impact on diagnostic concordance. However, the use of this approach remains essential as the International Classification of Diseases provides a common basis for classifying the various conditions that can be managed in the ED, thus ensuring uniformity and standardisation in their coding (Vandyk 2015).

Conclusion

The results of this study suggest that the overall diagnostic concordance between PT and EP in the ED is almost perfect. Additional multicenter studies with larger sample sizes, and a greater variety of diagnoses and age strata are needed to verify the external validity of these results. In addition, it would also be interesting to conduct further studies where the PT would be allowed to prescribe imaging tests such as plain radiographs to observe its impact on the level of agreement between physicians and PTs in the context of an ED.
