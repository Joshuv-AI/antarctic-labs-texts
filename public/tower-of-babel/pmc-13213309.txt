Protocol for total RNA sequencing analysis of extracellular RNA from biofluids
Authors: Anneleen Decock, Kimberly Verniers, Philippe Decruyenaere, Annelien Morlion, Jasper Verwilt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryExtracellular or cell-free RNAs (exRNAs) derived from biofluids are utilized in biomarker studies to study health and diseases. Here, we present a protocol for total RNA sequencing analysis of blood plasma exRNA using the Switching Mechanism At 5′ end of RNA Template (SMART) cDNA synthesis technology. We describe all steps, from blood plasma preparation to sequencing data analysis. In addition to blood plasma, this protocol can also be applied to exRNA purified from other human, murine, and rat biofluids.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Decruyenaere et al.1Subject areas: Sequence analysis, Sequencing, RNA-seq

Before you begin

The protocol below describes the wet and dry lab steps for total RNA sequencing analysis of cell-free RNA (extracellular RNA, exRNA) purified from human, murine, or rat blood plasma, using the Switching Mechanism At 5′ end of RNA Template (SMART) cDNA synthesis technology developed by Takara Bio.1,2,3,4,5 More specifically, we explain how to prepare and quantify strand-specific, ribosomal RNA (rRNA)-depleted sequencing libraries from DNase-treated exRNA, using the SMARTer Stranded Total RNA-Seq Kit v3 – Pico Input Mammalian, or a reconfigured version of this kit, i.e., the SMART-Seq Total RNA Pico Input with UMIs (ZapR Mammalian) kit. Figure 1 summarizes the underlying principles of the library preparation, as well as the corresponding wet lab steps described in this section. This technology has also been used to profile exRNA purified from human urine3 and seminal plasma,6 from conditioned medium from cancer cells,3 and from blood plasma-, urine- and conditioned medium-derived extracellular vesicles.3

Note: The SMARTer Stranded Total RNA-Seq Kit v3 – Pico Input Mammalian and the SMART-Seq Total RNA Pico Input with UMIs (ZapR Mammalian) kit are updated versions of the SMARTer Stranded Total RNA-Seq Kit v2 – Pico Input Mammalian (Cat#634411-634414 (with indexing primers) or Cat#634417-634419 (without indexing primers), Takara Bio). Also, this v2 version of the kit has been successfully applied to exRNA purified from liquid biopsies.3,4,5,6 Note that Takara Bio has communicated the phasing out of both the v2 and v3 versions of the kit. Therefore, we recommend to use the SMART-Seq Total RNA Pico Input with UMIs (ZapR Mammalian) kit for new studies. Furthermore, we recommend not to combine reagents from different kit versions, nor using different kit versions within a single experiment or study.

Schematic overview of the different steps in the total RNA sequencing library preparation workflow using the SMARTer Stranded Total RNA-Seq Kit v3 – Pico Input Mammalian or the SMART-Seq Total RNA Pico Input with UMIs (ZapR Mammalian) kit

(A) The 1st-strand cDNA synthesis step starts with a DNase-treated, spike-in-supplemented RNA sample and involves total RNA fragmentation and cDNA synthesis. Random priming allows the generation of cDNA from all RNA fragments in the sample, including rRNA. When the SMARTScribe II Reverse Transcriptase reaches the 5′ end of the RNA fragment, the enzyme’s terminal transferase activity tails a few non-templated nucleotides to the 3′ end of the cDNA (shown as XXXXX). The SMART UMI Adapter (included in the SMART UMI-TSO Mix v3) base-pairs with the non-templated nucleotide stretch, initiating second-strand synthesis upon template switching, and contains 8 random nucleotides, which function as unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) in downstream analyses.

(B) In the next step, adapters for Illumina sequencing, including P5/P7 and i5/i7 indexes for flow cell clustering as well as regions recognized by sequencing primers for Read 1 and 2, are added via a first round of PCR amplification. The 5′ PCR primer binds to the SMART UMI Adapter sequence. The 3′ PCR primer binds to the sequence associated with the random primer.

(C) Ribosomal cDNA is then cleaved by ZapR Enzyme in the presence of mammalian-specific R-Probes. Non-ribosomal molecules are left untouched, with priming sites available on both 5ʹ and 3ʹ ends for further PCR amplification.

(D) The intact molecules are enriched by a second PCR amplification using P5/P7 primers, which are universal across all Illumina libraries. Read 1 generates sequences antisense to the original RNA, while Read 2 yields sequences sense to the original RNA. The first 8 nt of the second sequencing read (Read 2) are UMIs followed by 3 nucleotides of UMI-linker and 3 nontemplated nucleotides derived from the SMART UMI Adapter. Lastly, the PCR products are purified once more to yield the final cDNA library. The figure is based on the manufacturer’s manuals and was partly created in BioRender (Mestdagh, P. (2026) https://BioRender.com/qk3ojod). FMM: Fragmentation Master Mix; FSMM: First-Strand Master Mix; P1MM: PCR1 Master Mix; ZMM: ZapR Master Mix; P2MM: PCR2 Master Mix.

Innovation

This protocol is based on Takara Bio total RNA sequencing library preparation manuals (SMARTer Stranded Total RNA-Seq Kit v3 – Pico Input Mammalian and SMART-Seq Total RNA Pico Input with UMIs (ZapR Mammalian)), with specific optimizations for exRNA isolated from liquid biopsies. Such RNA is typically present at very low concentrations and is highly fragmented, but profiling of these conditions is not fully addressed in existing protocols. We provide detailed guidance and quality control steps tailored to exRNA inputs to ensure robust and reproducible library preparation.

The protocol includes instructions for manual execution as proposed in the original manuals, as well as for semi-automated execution, enabling scalable, high-throughput processing while reducing operator-dependent variability. We also describe how the workflow can be further automated to support implementation in large studies.

To improve usability and reproducibility, we provide templates for experimental design and sequencing setup, as well as a user-friendly computational processing pipeline with test data. This end-to-end workflow enables users to run, evaluate, and adapt the protocol, guided by comprehensive quality control checks.

To collect human or animal biofluids, institutional regulations may legally require researchers to obtain ethical approval from the competent ethics committee of the host institution. Here, human blood plasma data of Decruyenaere et al.1 were used, a study approved by the ethics committee of Ghent University Hospital (Ghent, Belgium; approval number EC/2016/0307). Written informed consent was obtained from all study participants.1

Blood plasma preparation

Timing: ∼2 h

CRITICAL: Note that different blood plasma preparation protocols may be used. However, pre-analytical variables highly impact the quantification of blood plasma-derived exRNAs.7 Therefore, we recommend standardizing blood sample collection and processing within a single study. Combining data from different blood collection tube types and/or different blood processing time intervals will introduce unacceptable biases into the results. We advise using citrate blood collection tubes and completing blood plasma preparation within 4 hours after collection.7 The use of manufacturer-designated preservation tubes (such as the Cell-Free RNA BCT, Cell-Free DNA BCT, PAXgene Blood ccfDNA Tube, or Cell-Free DNA Collection Tube) should be avoided for exRNA profiling.7

Here, we describe the preparation of double-spun plasma from whole blood collected in Vacuette 9 mL, sodium citrate 3.2% blood collection tubes, following the International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis (ISTH) guidelines. The resulting plasma is directly suitable for exRNA profiling, but can also be used for extracellular vesicle purification and subsequent exRNA profiling.3,8

RNA purification and genomic DNA removal using spike-in RNA

Timing: ∼3 h

CRITICAL: Note that different RNA purification methods may be used to purify exRNA from blood plasma, considering compatibility with the selected downstream genomic DNA removal method.7 Keep in mind, however, that the choice of RNA purification method also impacts exRNA quantification.7 Therefore, we recommend standardizing processing methods within a single study. Combining data derived from different RNA purification methods will introduce unacceptable biases in the results. We advise to maximize the blood plasma input volume and recommend to use the miRNeasy Serum/Plasma Kit or miRNeasy Serum/Plasma Advanced Kit (for blood plasma input volumes of 0.2–0.6 mL), or the QIAamp ccfDNA/RNA Kit (for blood plasma input volumes up to 4 mL) for RNA purification.7

Experimental design of the library preparation

Timing: ∼15 min

The protocol described in the Section step-by-step method details is based on the manufacturer’s manuals (SMARTer Stranded Total RNA-Seq Kit v3 – Pico Input Mammalian and SMART-Seq Total RNA Pico Input with UMIs (ZapR Mammalian) manuals) for the library preparation protocol of a single sample using monochannel pipettes. For profiling of multiple samples, the experimental template in File S1 can be used to set up the library plate layout and to calculate the reagent volumes required to prepare the different master mixes throughout the protocol, as well as to determine how to aliquot these master mixes into the library plate, depending on the number of samples being profiled.

In addition, the Section step-by-step method details provides details for executing the protocol using (electronic) multichannel pipettes (see key resources table for complete list and matching pipetting tips) and/or semi-automated pipetting (using the VIAFLO 96 and Mantis Liquid Dispenser (with silicone high volume chips)) to accelerate profiling of large sample numbers. Although manual pipetting (using electronic multichannel pipettes) can be used to process up to 96 samples simultaneously, we recommend using semi-automated pipetting for sample numbers greater than 32. It should be noted that the semi-automated pipetting instructions require more reagents than manual pipetting does, due to allowances for dead volume. One must consider whether the benefits of faster library preparation outweigh the cost of increased reagent consumption. In the protocol steps, it is indicated which pipetting option(s) can be used: multichannel pipetting, Mantis, or VIAFLO 96. For the protocol steps that use the Mantis Liquid Dispenser, the time required to add the master mix to different sample numbers is summarized in Table 2. For protocol steps in which the VIAFLO 96 can be used, the appropriate electronic pipetting speed settings are specified in the protocol description.

Note: For all VIAFLO 96 steps, it is recommended to use the ‘tip touch’ technique: lightly touch the GripTips to the side of the well, or briefly dip them into the liquid after dispensing, to ensure complete liquid release and minimize carryover.

R-probes

Timing: ∼10 min

During ribosomal cDNA depletion (see Section depletion of ribosomal cDNA, step 44), library fragments originating from rRNA (18S and 28S) and mitochondrial rRNA (m12S and m16S) are cut by ZapR Enzyme in the presence of these R-Probes (mammalian-specific, included in both versions of the library preparation kit). These R-Probes target mammalian nuclear 28S, 18S, 5.8S, and 5S rRNA and human mitochondrial m16S and m12S rRNA sequences.

Step-by-step method details

Timing: The time required for each step in the protocol depends on the researcher’s experience, the number of samples included in the library preparation, and whether semi-automated pipetting is used. For up to 96 samples, a skilled lab technician can complete the entire library preparation protocol in 3–4 days, depending on the chosen pipetting method. An additional day should be scheduled if the optional library size selection step is performed (see sectionlibrary size selection). Note that the protocol includes defined safe stopping points; it cannot be paused at any other step. When performing the protocol for the first time, we recommend allowing sufficient time.

Data analysis

After data analysis, it is important to assess data quality, as outlined in the Section quantification and statistical analysis below. Four parameters are essential for this sequencing data: strandedness, splicing, spike-in fraction, and duplication rates (also see problem 5 and potential solution in Section troubleshooting). To provide realistic values for these parameters, we selected the highest-quality samples from Decruyenaere et al.1 (Figure 4). First, we expect stranded sequencing data from this library preparation. More specifically, we expect the vast majority of reads to map in the ‘1+-,1-+,2++,2--’ orientation (following the RSeQC infer_experiment notation, Figure 4A). Second, although not all reads should cover splice junctions (since we are dealing with short-read sequencing), we expect about 10% of reads to map to known splice junctions (Figure 4B). Deviating values in these first two parameters might indicate DNA contamination of the RNA sample.23 Third, the fraction of spike-in RNA in the final counts should be high enough to be useful for normalization without taking up too much of the data. Therefore, we recommend that the spike-in fraction (ERCC or Sequin) not exceed 5-10% (Figure 4C). Note that altered Sequin spike-in vs. ERCC spike-in ratios during data analysis, can indicate issues during RNA purification. Lastly, duplication rates indicate the degree of PCR duplicates and increase with a lower starting amount of RNA. Since the protocol works with low amounts of RNA, specifically, we expect a large proportion of PCR duplicates. Our high-quality samples show a mode of around 37% of duplicates. Extremely high duplication rates (>90%) indicate low RNA input (Figure 4D). Incorrect plasma preparation or poor blood tube choice may lead to an increased or decreased RNA concentration or integrity. This can also result in deviations in these last two parameters: unexpected spike-in RNA ratios and high duplication rates (Figures 4C and 4D).

Quantification and statistical analysis

When reviewing sequencing results, it is essential to carefully examine the quality control (QC) metrics before proceeding with downstream analyses. Illumina’s BaseSpace platform (basespace.illumina.com) provides valuable run metrics, including the percentage of clusters passing filter that are assigned to the index (%PF), the percentage of wells (for patterned flow cells) or nonduplicated spots (for nonpatterned flow cells) on the flow cell containing clusters (%Occupied), the number of clusters (in millions) passing filtering (Reads PF), the percentage of reads aligned to the PhiX genome (%PhiX) and the percentage of bases with a quality score of 30 or higher (%≥Q30). The %PF should be ideally above 75-80% and the %Occupied above 85% (see Table 12 in Section expected outcomes; see Section problem 4 and potential solution in troubleshooting). For total RNA sequencing, we target approximately 20 million reads per sample for gene-level quantification or gene set enrichment analysis, and around 40 million reads per sample for more detailed investigations of low-abundance genes. Evaluate whether the proportion of reads aligning to the PhiX control corresponds to the concentration loaded during sequencing. Ideally, the %≥Q30 should be around 90% or higher. For a more detailed evaluation of sequencing quality, additional QC analysis should be performed using FastQC (v0.12.1).

Raw sequencing data can be processed using various tools. We use a comprehensive Snakemake workflow, available at Zenodo (Zenodo: https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.19113165). The workflow leverages multiple bioinformatics tools, each executed within its own conda environment to ensure reproducibility and ease of dependency management. It is adaptable to both single-end (SE) and paired-end (PE) sequencing data, and it supports both versions of the total RNA library preparation protocol. The data folders and parameters can be adjusted in the configuration file (config/config.yml), where the sequencing parameter specifies the sequencing type (either ‘se’ for single-end or ‘pe’ for paired-end).

Limitations

High-throughput sequencing platforms, including the Illumina system used here, require PCR amplification during library preparation to generate sufficient cDNA molecules for sequencing. In workflows optimized for biofluids, which are considered low-input samples, the number of PCR cycles is typically increased. This amplification step inevitably produces PCR duplicates – reads derived from the same original cDNA molecule – which can bias downstream analyses if not adequately addressed. In the SMART-Seq Total RNA Pico Input with UMIs protocol, a Unique Molecular Identifier is incorporated during reverse transcription to mitigate PCR amplification bias. This UMI is positioned at the 5′ end of Read 2, facilitating the identification of PCR duplicates in paired-end sequencing data. However, for single-end sequencing, this UMI is absent, rendering UMI-based deduplication unfeasible. Consequently, when processing single-end RNA sequencing data from biofluid samples, it is crucial to employ alternative computational tools to address PCR duplicates. Tools such as Picard (http://broadinstitute.github.io/picard/) or BBMap’s Clumpify utility (https://sourceforge.net/projects/bbmap/) can be utilized during the pre-processing stage to remove duplicates effectively. The expected percentage of duplicated reads varies depending on the tool used and the type of biofluid.

While our proposed computational workflow focuses on linear RNAs, circular RNAs can also be detected using this protocol. Quantification of circular RNAs is typically based on their characteristic backsplice junctions, and several specialized bioinformatics tools are available for this purpose.26

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

During the purification steps, the wells of the library preparation plate contain AMPure XP beads, and the supernatant needs to be discarded (e.g., Section purification of the RNA sequencing library, step 29) or transferred to a fresh 96-well PCR plate (e.g., Section purification of the RNA sequencing library, step 39). Aspiration of the supernatant might result in the aspiration of (a small amount of) beads.

Potential solution

Two potential solutions are proposed, depending on whether the supernatant is discarded or transferred. (1) If beads are observed in the pipette tips during supernatant removal, the fluid should be pipetted back into the original 96-well PCR plate. Incubate the plate for 1 or 2 more min on the magnetic stand. Lower the volume to be removed by 3–5 μL, and try again. Repeat if needed. Make sure that no beads are pipetted out. (2) If beads are observed in the pipette tips during supernatant transfer, the fluid also needs to be pipetted back into the original 96-well PCR plate. Then, add an additional volume (3–5 μL) of PCR-grade water, ZMM or Tris Buffer of the library preparation kit to the wells, depending on the solution in which the beads were rehydrated. Incubate the plate for 1 or 2 more min on the magnetic stand. Try again to transfer the indicated volume and repeat if needed. Make sure that no beads are pipetted out. As such, transfer volumes and continuation of the protocol are secured.

Problem 2

During the ‘Library validation’ (Section library validation, step 93) a small peak might appear before the expected library size. These peaks in the 125–200 bp range pinpoint the presence of primer dimers.

Potential solution

In that case, after pooling the individual libraries (Section library quantification and pooling, step 109), the final library pool should be re-evaluated for primer dimers (example in Figure 3). To assess the amount of dimers present in the library pool, a smear analysis can be performed using the ProSize Fragment Analyzer data analysis software (Agilent) to determine the percentage of fragments in the 125–200 bp range (similarly as described in Section library validation, step 94). When this percentage exceeds 15%, it is recommended to perform library size selection using Pippin Prep (Section library quantification and pooling).

Problem 3

Some individual library concentrations may be too high to be used immediately for pooling without exceeding the maximum well volume of a 96-well PCR plate (i.e., 200 μL, Section library quantification and pooling, step 106).

Potential solution

For these libraries, make a subdilution series from 4 to 10 nM. Then, further dilute to the selected pool concentration.

Problem 4

The %Occupied and %PF metrics deviate from the ideally obtained values (Section quantification and statistical analysis). This might indicate suboptimal loading concentrations during the sequencing run.

Potential solution

To optimize loading concentrations on the NextSeq 2000 or NovaSeq 6000 sequencing instrument, the %Occupied and %PF metrics should be evaluated in a Sequencing Analysis View (SAV) plot to determine if a run was underloaded, optimally loaded, or overloaded. If the resulting plot indicates the run is underloaded or overloaded, increase or decrease the loading concentration, respectively, to optimally load the library pool.

Such deviations do not necessarily invalidate the experiment but may indicate technical issues that require careful interpretation, exclusion of specific samples, or protocol optimization before continuing with downstream analyses.

•Evidence of DNA contamination: Suggested by a reduced proportion of reads mapping to the expected stranded orientation (Figure 4A) and/or a substantially lower fraction of reads mapping to known splice junctions (typically <10%) (Figure 4B).
