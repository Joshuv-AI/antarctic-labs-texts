Introduction

The incidence of testicular cancer has been steadily rising over the past several decades, with approximately 10 000 cases diagnosed in the United States in 2024 [1]. Of these tumors, 95% will be classified as testicular germ cell tumors (GCTs), and the remaining 5% will be classified as non‐GCTs. Non‐GCTs include Leydig cell tumors (LCTs), Sertoli cell tumors, and granulosa cell tumors [2]. Leydig cell tumors account for 1%–2% of all testicular tumors [3, 4]. LCTs arise from the interstitial Leydig cells, which are responsible for testosterone production in the testes. Malignant transformation is uncommon, with an occurrence reported in less than 10% of cases, typically involving metastasis to distant organs, such as the liver or lungs [5].

Like all testicular neoplasms, LCTs often present as a painless testicular nodule or swelling and are typically diagnosed in the third to sixth decades of life. These tumors may be hormonally active, with testosterone overproduction leading to precocious puberty in prepubertal boys and, less commonly, estrogen excess manifesting as gynecomastia and other feminizing symptoms [3]. Despite their rarity, LCTs carry significant clinical implications due to their hormone production and, in rare malignant cases, their potential for aggressive behavior [4].

Surgical excision is the standard treatment for LCTs and is typically curative in benign cases. The prognosis for benign tumors is excellent, while malignant cases require a more aggressive approach, including potential chemotherapy or radiation [4]. Malignancy is rare in LCTs, with the most common sites of metastasis being the retroperitoneal lymph nodes, liver, lungs, and bone [6]. It is also possible for metastatic disease to spread to the mediastinum through the thoracic duct [7]. Other diseases of the anterior mediastinum include lymphomas, sarcomas, and thymomas. Thymomas are an uncommon malignancy; however, they are the most common anterior mediastinal mass in adults [8]. There are no documented case reports discussing synchronous LCTs and thymomas.

Case Presentation

The patient is a 51‐year‐old male who presented to the urology clinic with a 2‐week history of left testicular pain, swelling, and a small palpable nodule. The patient denied a history of scrotal trauma, fevers, chills, dysuria, or gross hematuria. Pertinent medical history included a previous diagnosis of male‐factor infertility. Physical examination demonstrated palpable induration along the lateral aspect of the left testicle. A scrotal ultrasonography study demonstrated a 1.0 cm solid, heterogeneous lesion within the left testicle associated with hypervascularity and concomitant microlithiasis (Figure 1A–C). The right testicle was homogeneous without significant abnormalities. Tumor markers were obtained, demonstrating normal values, including lactate dehydrogenase (150 U/L), α‐fetoprotein (3.5 ng/mL), and β‐human chorionic gonadotropin (< 0.6 units).

Lateral view of left testis with scrotal ultrasound demonstrating (A) heterogeneous testicular lesion (yellow arrow), (B) hypervascularity of the lesion, and (C) microlithiasis (yellow arrow). The scale bar is 1.0 cm.

A left radical orchiectomy was performed 1 week following the initial presentation. Pathologic analysis of the specimen demonstrated findings consistent with a pT1a LCT with negative margins. The LCT was a 1.5 cm nodule, with magnification demonstrating abundant eosinophilic granular cytoplasm and round nuclei with prominent nucleoli (Figure 2A,B). Histochemical analysis demonstrated the tumor stained positive for calretinin, α‐inhibin, MART‐1, and weakly for CD9 (Figure 2C,D). There were also several 0.2–0.5 cm areas of germ cell neoplasia in situ identified in the specimen (Figure 2E). Secondary analysis with increased magnification revealed evidence of Reinke crystals (Figure 2F).

Histopathological findings of a Leydig cell tumor (LCT) (A) 1× magnification of a 1.5 cm LCT confined to the testis. (B) 20× magnification of the LCT with abundant eosinophilic granular cytoplasm and round nuclei with prominent nucleoli. (C) 1× magnification of the LCT stained with Inhibin A revealing diffuse positive cytoplasmic staining throughout the tumor. (D) 1× magnification of the LCT stained with calretinin with diffuse positive nuclear and cytoplasmic staining throughout the tumor. (E) 20× magnification of GCNIS in seminiferous tubules, exhibiting cells with abundant clear cytoplasm, hyperchromatic nuclei with prominent nucleoli lining the tubule, and a lack of spermatogenic maturation. (F) 40× magnification of a Reinke's crystalloid (black arrow).

The patient was scheduled for staging imaging, with computed tomography of the abdomen and pelvis demonstrating a 5 mm left lateral lobe pulmonary nodule. This was followed by a CT of the chest with contrast, demonstrating a 5.9 × 9.7 × 10.4 cm heterogeneous right anterior mediastial soft tissue mass (Figure 3). The patient was referred to interventional radiology for a core needle biopsy of the lesion, with pathology demonstrating a type AB thymoma. The patient subsequently underwent a sternotomy with anterior mass resection, with final pathology demonstrating a pT1aN0 type AB thymoma. The patient is planned for surveillance imaging with a CT of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis.

Discussion

Testicular cancer is relatively uncommon, affecting nearly 1 in 270 males [9]. Testicular cancer is classified into GCTs and non‐GCTs. Non‐GCTs are sporadic and account for only 5% of all testicular cancers. LCTs, a type of non‐GCT, are a rare pathology accounting for approximately 1%–2% of all testicular cancers [10, 11]. These tumors can be hormonally active and may produce testosterone or estradiol. In adults, most patients are asymptomatic if there is an excess of androgens. However, they may develop feminization with estrogen‐secreting tumors, with symptoms including gynecomastia, loss of libido, erectile dysfunction, and infertility. The patient in this case was phenotypically normal with a history of infertility. Other signs of LCTs include a palpable intratesticular mass, which was a presenting symptom in this case [10, 12].

Investigation of an LCT typically involves ultrasonography, with findings demonstrating a hypoechoic lesion with hypervascularity. The lesion may be homogeneous, or in the setting of malignancy, heterogeneous [13]. However, ultrasound may be confounding, as there has been a report of a cystic testicular malignancy that presented as a complex hydrocele on ultrasound [14]. Therefore, nonspecific findings on ultrasound should not rule out malignancy. Serum tumor markers, including α‐fetoprotein, β‐human chorionic gonadotropin, and placental alkaline phosphatase, should be within normal ranges, as was demonstrated in this patient. Serum testosterone is typically elevated, and serum estradiol is elevated in the setting of feminization [10].

Management of LCTs includes inguinal orchiectomy or partial orchiectomy. Malignant LCTs are treated with orchiectomy and with retroperitoneal lymphadenectomy if metastases are present [10]. LCTs are diagnosed based on pathologic features. The most common microscopic features include tumor cell nests or sheets with fibrovascular septa. Cell types include large polygonal cells with abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm with or without distinct cell borders, small cells with dense eosinophilic cytoplasm and grooved nuclei, or spindle‐shaped cells. Reinke crystals, a pink‐staining, rectangular structure, can also be seen and are distinctive for LCTs. Immunohistochemical markers can further assist in diagnosis, with positive markers including calretinin, Melan‐A, and α‐inhibin [10, 15, 16].

Follow up is dependent on the staging of the disease. Localized disease categorized as Stage 1 and with low‐risk features may be followed with observation, whereas localized disease categorized by Stage IIA and/or high‐risk features may consider early retroperitoneal lymph node dissection [17]. Advanced disease should be confirmed with a tissue diagnosis to rule out a synchronous malignancy and is best managed with surgical resection, given that systemic chemotherapy and radiotherapy have minimal effectiveness [10]. This case demonstrated a unique presentation of localized disease with a synchronous malignancy.

Conclusion

This case demonstrates the diagnosis and management of an uncommon testicular cancer with a rare pathology. Testicular cancer should be included in the differential diagnosis for a patient presenting with new‐onset testicular pain, swelling, and/or mass. Timely orchiectomy is critical for disease control and a definitive pathologic diagnosis. Leydig cell tumors (LCTs) are rare and may have a unique presentation due to hormone secretion. LCTs usually carry a favorable prognosis, and low‐risk patients can be followed with observation. However, they have the potential for metastatic spread and incidentally found masses during workup should be evaluated with a tissue diagnosis to rule out metastatic disease.
