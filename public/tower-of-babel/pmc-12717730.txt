Dominant depressive temperament and psychological resilience in obsessive-compulsive disorder
Authors: Arda Kazim Demirkan, Esra Aydin Sunbul, Emine Cengiz Cavusoglu, Merve Metin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study examined the relationship between obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) symptom severity and psychological resilience within the framework of dominant depressive temperament (DDT).MethodsA total of 129 patients with OCD were assessed using the Yale-Brown Obsessive-Compulsive Scale (Y-BOCS), the Resilience Scale for Adults (RSA), and the TEMPS-A questionnaire for temperament evaluation. Participants were classified as having DDT based on both established cut-off scores and standardized z-scores for the depressive subscale. Depression and anxiety levels were measured with the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI) and Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI).ResultsPatients with DDT showed significantly higher depression, anxiety, and obsession scores than those without DDT. They also displayed significantly lower scores in most RSA subdimensions (perception of future, structured style, social competence, family cohesion, and social resources), while perception of self did not differ. Interestingly, some resilience subdimensions such as structured style were relatively higher in the DDT group despite greater symptom severity, indicating a paradoxical pattern. Partial correlation analyses controlling for depressive symptoms revealed that, in the DDT group, higher OCD symptom severity was inversely associated with several resilience resources—particularly family cohesion.ConclusionsThe presence of DDT is linked to greater emotional vulnerability and reduced resilience resources, which may exacerbate OCD severity. These findings indicate that the interplay between affective temperament and resilience in OCD remains significant even after accounting for depressive symptoms, underscoring the importance of incorporating temperament profiling and resilience assessment into clinical evaluation. Interventions that target resilience may be particularly beneficial for OCD patients with DDT.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Temperament, Depression, Obsessive-compulsive disorder, Resilience, Psychological

Introduction

Obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) is a pervasive, often chronic psychiatric condition characterized by recurrent intrusive thoughts and images that provoke anxiety and distress, which patients attempt to alleviate or control via repetitive behaviors or mental rituals [1]. Its lifetime prevalence is estimated at 1.9-3.0% [2], and the disorder imposes a substantial burden on functioning and productivity worldwide [3]. Despite treatment advances, resistance remains high, affecting 40–60% of patients, with frequent comorbidities such as depression (19–90%) and bipolar disorder (up to 21.5%) [4]. Recent reviews underscore the heavy personal and societal costs of treatment-resistant OCD [5, 6].

Psychological resilience—the ability to adapt to adversity and maintain psychological well-being—has emerged as a key determinant of psychopathology severity and prognosis [7, 8]. It is shaped by individual traits, social and familial support [9], and genetic predispositions [10, 11]. Transdiagnostic perspectives position resilience as a cross-cutting construct relevant to multiple psychiatric disorders, including OCD [12, 13]. Recent research has further elaborated resilience as a mediating factor between vulnerability and adaptive outcomes. For instance, resilience mediates the link between attachment and well-being [14] and between trauma and psychotic-like experiences [15], while reduced resilience has been associated with higher symptom burden in OCD [16, 17]. Moreover, environmental stressors such as the COVID-19 pandemic have been shown to exacerbate obsessive-compulsive symptoms in both clinical and general populations [18] and to interact with temperament and resilience profiles [19].

Affective temperament, meanwhile, refers to stable, biologically rooted predispositions associated with mood disorders [20]. These temperaments, often viewed as subclinical manifestations of mood traits, may increase vulnerability to psychopathology and provide early markers for prevention [21, 22]. Recent studies have extended the role of affective temperaments beyond mood disorders, suggesting they may also influence the course and treatment response in OCD [23]. In line with this, Favaretto et al. provided a comprehensive synthesis of the clinical implications of affective temperaments across psychiatric disorders, emphasizing their predictive value for symptom trajectories, treatment response, and resilience-related outcomes [24]. This perspective further supports the conceptualization of depressive temperament as a stable trait influencing psychopathological vulnerability.

However, despite growing interest in temperament and resilience, a specific focus on dominant depressive temperament (DDT) in OCD remains limited. Most prior research has examined affective temperaments in broad diagnostic groups, emphasizing mood or psychotic disorders, with little attention to how particular dominant temperaments shape resilience processes in OCD. Considering that comorbid mood disorders are known to worsen prognosis and complicate treatment [3, 4], clarifying the role of DDT within OCD may reveal an underrecognized pathway through which vulnerability translates into greater symptom severity and treatment resistance.

The present study therefore aims to investigate the relationship between OCD symptom severity and psychological resilience within the framework of DDT. By addressing this gap, our work seeks to provide novel insights into the temperamental underpinnings of OCD, highlighting mechanisms that may inform individualized treatment strategies and preventive interventions.

Materials and methods

Participants and procedure

Between January and June 2021, 199 adult patients with a primary diagnosis of OCD were recruited from the psychiatry outpatient clinic of a government-based research hospital specializing in psychiatric disorders. A non-probabilistic convenience sampling method was used: eligible patients presenting to various outpatient clinics were selected using a computer-based listing system and contacted regarding their willingness to participate.

Among them, 150 patients provided informed consent. Inclusion criteria were: (i) a confirmed OCD diagnosis within the past 1 to 5 years, (ii) at least one clinical visit to the treatment center within the past six months, and (iii) adherence to prescribed pharmacological treatment for a minimum of three months.

After comprehensive clinical evaluation, 21 patients were excluded due to: presence of psychotic symptoms, diagnosis of bipolar disorder, active substance abuse, or clinically significant cognitive impairment, as determined by the evaluating psychiatrist based on clinical interview and functional impairment noted in daily living activities (Fig. 1).

Cohort flow diagram

Common psychiatric comorbidities such as generalized anxiety disorder and major depressive disorder were not excluded, considering their high prevalence among OCD patients and the aim to maintain external validity. However, bipolar disorder was excluded due to its episodic nature and potential confounding effect on emotional and functional measures.

All OCD diagnoses were reconfirmed using DSM-5 criteria by an experienced psychiatrist with at least four years of clinical expertise. Sociodemographic data were recorded prior to scale administration.

The study was approved by the local ethical committee of Training and Research Hospital for Mental Health and Neurological Diseases (date/approval number: 15.06.2020/20200121), and written informed consent was obtained. The participants were informed of the voluntary nature of their involvement, and they were reminded that they would be allowed to terminate their participation at any point. Standardized assessment procedures were applied uniformly to all potential participants.

Materials

Sociodemographic data Form

This form was designed by researchers to record participants’ sociodemographic data.

Yale-brown obsessive-compulsive scale (Y-BOCS)

Developed by Goodman et al., this tool measures OCD severity. The scale comprises 10 items, each rated from 0 to 4, providing a total score range of 0–40, with 20 points each for obsessions and compulsions. The Turkish validity and reliability study was conducted by Karamustafaoğlu et al. [25].

Temperament evaluation of Memphis, Pisa, Paris, and San Diego – auto questionnaire (TEMPS-A)

Akiskal developed the scale for evaluating affective temperament. Individuals respond to the questions with a “yes” or “no” based on their lifetime experiences. The established cut-off points for ascertaining the predominance of depressive, cyclothymic, hyperthymic, irritable, and anxious temperamental traits are 13, 18, 20, 13, and 18, respectively. Predominant temperaments are discerned based on the z-scores of their respective affective temperament subtypes. The Turkish validity and reliability study of this 100-item scale, was conducted by Vahip et al. [26].

Operationalization of “dominant” depressive temperament (DDT)

For each participant, the five raw TEMPS-A subscale totals were first standardized to sample-based z-scores using the formula z = (X − µ) / σ, where µ and σ represent the sample mean and standard deviation for each subscale. A participant was classified as having DDT if the depressive subscale met the Turkish cut-off (≥ 13) and the depressive z-score was the highest among the five subscales. If two or more subscales met their respective cut-offs, the case was labeled as “mixed,” unless the depressive z-score exceeded the next-highest subscale by ≥ 0.5 SD. The NoDDT group comprised all remaining participants who did not meet the depressive cut-off or whose highest z-score corresponded to a non-depressive temperament, including those with no subscale above the cut-off.

This rule-based algorithm, which included explicit cut-offs, standardized z-score computation, and a pre-specified tie-breaking rule, was defined a priori to ensure transparency and reproducibility. Based on this algorithm, participants were dichotomized into DDT and NoDDT groups for primary analyses. This approach was chosen to minimize misclassification, although it may have excluded participants with subthreshold or mixed temperamental features.

Resilience scale for adults (RSA)

It was developed by Friborg et al. [27] to measure individuals psychological resilience level. The Turkish validity and reliability study was conducted by Basim and Cetin [28].

Beck anxiety inventory (BAI)

It is a 21-item self-report questionnaire in which each item is rated on a 4-point Likert scale. The total score range is from 0 to 63, with higher scores indicating more severe symptom severity. The Turkish validity and reliability study was conducted [29].

Beck Depression Inventory (BDI):

It is a 21-item self-report questionnaire that determines the severity of depression. Individuals rate themselves between 0 and 3 for each item (0: least, 3: most; score range: 0 to 63). The total score is the sum of all items. The tool is validated and reliable in Turkish [30].

Based on the TEMPS-A scale, dominant temperaments were ascertained based on the z-scores corresponding to the specific affective temperament subtype, and patients were divided into two groups and compared.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using the SPSS 25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A Kolmogorov-Smirnov or Shapiro-Wilk test was employed to determine the normal distribution of the variables. Results were presented as mean ± standard deviation or number (%). For normally distributed data, group variables were compared using the Student’s t-test for continuous variables, and the chi-square test for categorical data. The correlation coefficients and their significance were computed using Pearson’s correlation analysis.

For post-hoc analysis, a Bonferroni test for multiple comparisons was used to overcome the risk of type I error. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Missing data accounted for less than 3% across all variables. Cases with incomplete responses on major scales (Y-BOCS, TEMPS-A, or RSA) were excluded listwise from the corresponding analyses. The pattern of missingness was evaluated using Little’s MCAR test, which indicated that data were missing completely at random (p > 0.05).

Partial correlation analyses controlling for depression (BDI scores) were performed to examine associations between OCD symptom severity and resilience dimensions. Depression was controlled as a covariate to account for the influence of current depressive symptoms while maintaining conceptual independence from anxiety, which often covaries with depression but may not specifically mediate the relationship between affective temperament and resilience.

In the DDT group, significant inverse correlations were observed between Y-BOCS obsession scores and perception of the future (r = − 0.379, p = 0.032), social resources (r = − 0.372, p = 0.036), and family cohesion (r = − 0.501, p = 0.004), as well as between compulsion scores and family cohesion (r = − 0.649, p < 0.001). Moderate inverse associations were also detected between compulsion scores, social competence (r = − 0.383, p = 0.031), and social resources (r = − 0.397, p = 0.024). Effect sizes corresponding to these associations (Cohen’s r²) ranged from 0.14 to 0.42, indicating small-to-moderate clinical relevance.

In the NoDDT group, weaker inverse correlations were found between Y-BOCS obsession scores and perception of future (r = − 0.236, p = 0.021) and between compulsion scores and perception of self (r = − 0.280, p = 0.006) and social resources (r = − 0.275, p = 0.007) (Table 4).

Together, these results indicate that resilience exerts a robust protective effect against OCD symptoms, anxiety exacerbates severity, and the role of DDT is complex—linked to greater depression and anxiety yet paradoxically associated with lower overall OCD severity once covariates are accounted for.

Discussion

The primary objective of this study was to explore the relationship between OCD symptom severity and psychological resilience within the context of a dominant depressive temperament DDT. We found that in cases characterized by DDT, depression, anxiety, and obsession scores were higher, while resilience scores were lower in most sub-dimensions, with the exception of perception of self. Conversely, in OCD cases both with and those without a dominant depressive temperament, compulsion and perception of self scores were similar. Notably, the association between OCD symptom severity and resilience appeared to fluctuate in the presence of DDT. In OCD patients who also exhibited DDT, a significant inverse relationship was identified between both obsession and compulsion scores and the family cohesion factor. However, other intermediate or weak correlations that were apparent in this group were either less frequent or not present in the NoDDT group.

Affective temperament has emerged as a focal point in the exploration of personality traits related to OCD. Our study design, which excludes bipolar disorder, parallels that of D’ambrosio et al. [31], who discovered hyperthymic (13.8%), cyclothymic (19.2%), depressive (16.8%), and irritable (4.8%) temperaments among patients with OCD. Interestingly, while the cyclothymic affective temperament was dominant among the OCD patients in their study, our study presents a strikingly different picture, with the most common dominant temperament being depressive (24.4%) and no instances of hyperthymic temperament. The higher prevalence of cyclothymic temperament (9.1%) in the aforementioned study can possibly be explained by the high comorbidity of bipolar disorder. In line with our study, Fistikci et al. [32] also identified depressive temperament (34.1%) as the most common dominant temperament and reported no instances of hyperthymic temperament. The similarities between these studies could be attributed to cultural influences on affective temperament, given that both studies were conducted in centers with similar demographic characteristics and disease severity among patients. It is important to note that the aim of this study was not to identify the distribution of temperaments in OCD patients but to investigate specifically the impact of DDT on resilience. This focus may have introduced selection bias, and the generalizability of the findings should therefore be considered cautiously.

Research involving patients with OCD has proposed that specific personality traits may significantly impact on the severity and course of the disorder [33]. Several studies have explored the correlation between dominant cyclothymic temperament and OCD, along with its clinical implications, using an affective temperament approach [31, 34]. While depression is the most prevalent comorbid condition with OCD, the influence of a depressive temperament -viewed as a subthreshold or precursor to depression- on OCD remains understudied. Given the strong conceptual alignment between Cloninger’s harm avoidance model and Akiskal’s depressive temperament [35], it is pertinent to compare our findings with those of the extant literature. Many studies employing Cloninger’s model have posited that harm avoidance is more prevalent in OCD patients than in healthy individuals, thereby exacerbating the clinical complexities associated with OCD [33, 36]. Acknowledging the significance of temperament in OCD, Ettelt et al. [37] further proposed that harm avoidance may have a hereditary predisposition in relatives of OCD patients. From this perspective, it is anticipated and consistent with our findings that the DDT group would exhibit more severe clinical symptoms and lower resilience scores compared to the NoDDT group in our study.

Beyond its direct association with symptom severity, depressive temperament may also interact with other vulnerability factors that are well established in OCD, such as cognitive rigidity and insecure attachment patterns. Cognitive rigidity—the difficulty in shifting mental sets or adapting to new perspectives—has been consistently reported in OCD [38, 39]. Depressive temperament, characterized by pessimism and reduced flexibility, may exacerbate this rigidity by reinforcing maladaptive thought patterns and impairing problem-solving. Similarly, insecure attachment styles, which are common in OCD [40, 41], may be compounded by depressive temperament traits, leading to heightened dependence on others and reduced confidence in social bonds. These interactions could partly explain why patients with DDT experience both greater symptom burden and distinct patterns of resilience, as rigidity and insecure attachment may undermine adaptive coping despite the presence of certain resilience resources.

The connection between temperament and resilience in OCD patients has been the subject of only a limited number of studies. While some research involving OCD patients suggests a negative correlation between the severity of obsessive-compulsive symptoms and resilience [42], other studies have found no such relationship [43]. The use of the self-report format RSA scale in our study to measure resilience in our study may have led patients with depressive symptoms to evaluate themselves in a pessimistic and exaggerated manner. The same possibility exists for the TEMS-A scale; thus, the responses from clinically significant depressive patients may have influenced the assessment of depressive temperament in OCD. To mitigate this concern, we adjusted for depressive symptom scores when examining the relationship between OCD severity and resilience in the two groups. Interestingly, we found a negative correlation between OCD severity and resilience in the DDT group, but not in the NoDDT group. This discrepancy may help explain the inconsistent results in previous studies. If future work with larger samples corroborates the impact of depressive temperament seen in our study, temperament assessment may become an important component of OCD evaluation and guide decisions regarding non-pharmacological interventions.

An apparent paradox emerged in our findings: patients with DDT displayed greater OCD severity but also scored higher on certain resilience dimensions. Several possible explanations may account for this inconsistency. First, measurement biases inherent to self-report instruments may have contributed, as individuals with depressive temperament might over- or under-report specific resilience traits depending on their mood or cognitive style [44, 45]. Second, defensive reporting styles could play a role, whereby patients consciously or unconsciously portray themselves as more resilient to minimize perceived vulnerability [46, 47]. Third, it is also possible that certain resilience subdimensions, such as structured style or social resources, genuinely function as compensatory protective factors even in the presence of greater symptom severity, reflecting adaptive coping in specific domains rather than global resilience [27, 48]. This interpretation aligns with the notion of “illusory resilience,” where perceived strengths coexist with clinical impairment [12, 49].

Another possible mechanism underlying this paradox involves the cognitive–affective biases characteristic of depressive temperament. Individuals with a stable depressive disposition tend to engage in negative self-referential processing, which amplifies perceived inadequacy and reduces self-related resilience appraisals. At the same time, contextual or relational domains—such as family and social cohesion—may remain relatively intact or even become compensatory coping resources. This dissociation between internal and external resilience facets aligns with cognitive models of depression emphasizing self-referential negativity [50] and with recent frameworks describing resilience as a dynamic balance between self-focused and relational strengths [51].

A further explanation for the paradoxical pattern may be that certain resilience resources act as compensatory mechanisms in the context of elevated vulnerability. In particular, family cohesion and structured style may provide functional coping strategies even when depression and anxiety are more severe. This possibility may be especially relevant in cultural contexts such as Turkey, where family support plays a central role in daily functioning and may buffer against the adverse effects of psychopathology. Together, these factors suggest that resilience in OCD patients with DDT may be multifaceted and not uniformly protective, underscoring the need for multimethod and longitudinal designs to disentangle subjective perceptions from objective resilience processes.

Although we adjusted for current depressive symptoms using BDI scores, residual confounding may remain, as self-report instruments are particularly vulnerable to mood-congruent biases in patients with elevated depressive symptomatology. This limitation should be considered when interpreting the observed associations between resilience and OCD severity.

In addition to theoretical implications, our findings carry several practical clinical implications. Temperament profiling (e.g., TEMPS-A) could be integrated into the assessment of OCD patients as part of a comprehensive biopsychosocial evaluation. Identifying a dominant depressive temperament early may alert clinicians to patients at greater risk of higher symptom burden, lower resilience, and treatment resistance, thereby facilitating more individualized treatment planning. Our results also suggest that resilience enhancement should be prioritized in DDT patients. Interventions such as cognitive–behavioral therapy with exposure and response prevention (ERP) may be complemented by resilience-building modules, including cognitive restructuring, problem-solving training, and stress management techniques. Given that family cohesion emerged as a strong protective factor, involving families through psychoeducation or family therapy may be particularly beneficial for DDT patients. Finally, mindfulness-based and acceptance-oriented approaches, which foster flexibility and adaptive coping, may be especially suitable for this subgroup. By systematically considering temperament traits alongside resilience profiles, clinicians may better anticipate obstacles and design tailored interventions, thereby improving treatment outcomes for OCD patients with DDT.

Recent advances in resilience research support the view of resilience as a dynamic, multidimensional construct rather than a static trait [12]. This aligns with our finding that certain resilience dimensions, such as family cohesion, exert stronger protective effects than others, particularly in patients with DDT. Moreover, contemporary work emphasizes resilience as a transdiagnostic factor across psychiatric conditions, including OCD [8, 13]. These perspectives reinforce the clinical relevance of targeting resilience in treatment planning. In parallel, the emerging field of precision psychiatry highlights the importance of individual differences in temperament and resilience for tailoring interventions [52]. Our results, showing that depressive temperament interacts with resilience resources to shape OCD severity, are consistent with this approach and suggest that temperament profiling may help identify patient subgroups who could benefit from resilience-focused, personalized care. Recent temperament research further supports this interpretation, as affective temperaments are increasingly recognized as transdiagnostic markers influencing the course of multiple psychiatric disorders [22, 23]. By situating our findings within these contemporary frameworks, we not only replicate earlier work but also extend recent models that advocate for precision, resilience-oriented psychiatry.

Taken together, our findings can be conceptualized within a model where depressive temperament represents a stable vulnerability factor that increases emotional distress and symptom burden in OCD. This vulnerability is partly mediated by mechanisms such as cognitive rigidity and insecure attachment, which limit adaptive coping and exacerbate clinical severity. At the same time, resilience resources—particularly family cohesion and structured style—may buffer some of these effects but are not uniformly protective, resulting in the paradoxical patterns observed in our data. From a clinical perspective, this model suggests that temperament and resilience should be jointly assessed to identify patients at heightened risk, and that treatment strategies should aim both to reduce maladaptive cognitive-interpersonal processes and to enhance resilience resources in order to optimize outcomes.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, although we excluded patients with severe neurological or medical conditions, residual confounding from unmeasured somatic comorbidities cannot be ruled out. Second, while depressive symptoms were statistically adjusted for, conceptual and measurement overlap between dominant depressive temperament and current depressive symptomatology remains a possible source of bias. Third, recruitment was limited to a single specialized tertiary center, and a non-probabilistic convenience sampling approach was used, which may restrict external validity. Moreover, cultural factors, such as the central role of family cohesion and social support in Turkish society, may have amplified some of the observed associations between resilience and symptom severity. Therefore, replication across different cultural and multi-center contexts is warranted. Fourth, OCD diagnoses were established through detailed clinical evaluation in line with DSM-5 criteria, but not through structured diagnostic interviews (e.g., SCID), which may modestly limit diagnostic reliability. Fifth, the relatively small number of participants with non-DDT temperaments (e.g., anxious or irritable) prevented meaningful subgroup analyses and may have reduced the precision of between-group comparisons. Sixth, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference regarding the directionality of observed associations, and future longitudinal studies are needed to clarify temporal relationships between temperament, resilience, and OCD symptom severity. Finally, the reliance on self-report instruments introduces the possibility of depressive and perfectionistic response biases—particularly relevant in OCD populations characterized by introspective doubt—which could have influenced self-assessed resilience and temperament profiles.

Despite these limitations, this study provides valuable preliminary insights into the interaction between affective temperament, resilience, and OCD severity, and offers a basis for future longitudinal, multi-center, and multi-method research integrating structured diagnostic assessments and clinician-rated measures.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides novel evidence that dominant depressive temperament alters the interplay between OCD symptom severity and resilience. These findings underscore the importance of jointly assessing temperament profiles and resilience factors in clinical practice. Interventions that target maladaptive cognitive–interpersonal processes while simultaneously strengthening resilience resources—particularly family cohesion and structured coping—may be especially beneficial for OCD patients with DDT. Future longitudinal research is warranted to confirm these associations and inform more personalized treatment strategies.
