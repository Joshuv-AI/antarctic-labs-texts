Artificial intelligence and machine learning in global cardiac surgery: A scoping review
Authors: Shubh K Patel, Jiawen Deng, Adham Elsherbini, Nitish Bhatt, Amrit Grewal, Rashmi Nedadur, Michael WA Chu, Dominique Vervoort
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiovascular diseases remain the leading cause of death globally, with 80% of deaths occurring in low- and middle-income countries. Despite advancements in cardiac surgery, access to care remains limited in low- and middle-income countries and remote settings in high-income countries. Artificial intelligence and machine learning have the potential to democratize cardiac surgery access by reducing dependence on geography, while improving diagnostics, surgical planning, and peri- and postoperative care. This scoping review explores the application of artificial intelligence/machine learning in global cardiac surgery, focusing on low- and middle-income countries.MethodsA scoping review was conducted using Ovid MEDLINE, Embase, Web of Science, and Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature databases for studies on artificial intelligence/machine learning in cardiac surgery in low- and middle-income countries. Articles were summarized to assess trends, model use, and focus areas.ResultsA total of 83 studies were included. The median sample size was 1521 (interquartile range, 523-5477). Most studies focused on adult populations (84.3%). Valve surgery (19.3%) was the most common domain followed by coronary surgery (15.7%) and congenital surgery (13.3%). Postoperative outcomes were the most common focus (55.4%), followed by mortality prediction (25.3%). Geographically, 74.7% of studies were from China, with limited representation from individual other low- and middle-income countries. XGBoost (25.3%) and random forest (16.9%) were the most commonly used machine learning models. Research increased substantially after 2020, particularly in postoperative outcomes and mortality prediction.ConclusionsArtificial intelligence/machine learning applications in global cardiac surgery are expanding, particularly in postoperative outcomes and mortality prediction. However, significant geographical differences persist, with most studies originating from China. Future research should prioritize expanding artificial intelligence/machine learning applications in other low- and middle-income countries and underexplored areas.Key Words: artificial intelligence, global cardiac surgery, machine learning

Central Message

AI holds substantial promise in global cardiac surgery, but broader clinical impact will require rigorous validation, stronger infrastructure, and equitable research capacity.

Perspective

AI may enhance global cardiac surgery through improved risk prediction, decision support, and postoperative care. However, the current evidence base remains geographically concentrated, methodologically heterogeneous, and limited in scope. Future progress will depend on context-sensitive validation, ethical implementation, and sustained investment in local data ecosystems.

Cardiovascular diseases remain the leading cause of mortality globally, responsible for approximately 18 million deaths annually, with 80% occurring in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs).1 Despite significant advancements in medical care, a stark disparity exists in access to cardiac surgical services, particularly in LMICs. Nearly 6 billion people—approximately 93% of those living in LMICs—lack access to timely and necessary cardiac surgical care, a crisis exacerbated by socioeconomic and infrastructural barriers.2,3 This inequity in access to care contributes to the disproportionate burden of cardiac diseases in LMICs, where late-stage presentations and limited treatment options often result in poorer outcomes compared with high-income countries.4

Over the past few decades, cardiac surgery has undergone remarkable advancements, significantly improving survival and quality of life for patients with complex cardiac conditions. Innovations in surgical techniques, perioperative care, and postoperative management have collectively contributed to cardiac procedures' increased safety and efficacy.5, 6, 7 However, these advancements have predominantly benefited populations in high-income countries.8 In contrast, LMICs struggle to keep pace with these developments due to financial constraints, a shortage of skilled healthcare professionals, and budding health systems.8,9 Addressing these challenges requires not only expanding access to existing surgical interventions but also harnessing new technologies that can bridge the gap in global cardiac care in an accelerated manner compared with historical developments in high-income countries.

Artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) are driving a paradigm shift in healthcare, offering the potential to enhance diagnostic accuracy, optimize treatment strategies, and improve patient outcomes.10 These technologies, capable of analyzing vast datasets with speed and precision, are increasingly integrated into various aspects of healthcare, ranging from predictive analytics to personalized medicine.10,11 In cardiac surgery, AI and ML are beginning to play an important role in addressing pressing challenges, such as refining surgical planning, forecasting patient outcomes, and managing postoperative complications.10, 11, 12, 13 The impact of these technologies may be particularly promising in LMICs, where specialist shortages and infrastructure constraints limit access to care. By enabling more accurate diagnoses and optimizing surgical interventions, AI and ML hold the potential to democratize access to high-quality cardiac care on a global scale.14,15 Furthermore, the application of AI and ML can facilitate the development of context-specific treatment protocols and decision-making tools tailored to unique contextual challenges faced by different countries and regions.16,17

This scoping review aims to explore the application of AI and ML in global cardiac surgery. By examining the current literature, the review will assess how these technologies are being used to enhance the delivery of cardiac surgical care, particularly in LMICs, where access to such care is more limited. The review will also identify gaps in the existing research and propose areas for future investigation.

Materials and Methods

Search Strategy and Selection Criteria

A scoping review was performed using 4 major databases—Ovid MEDLINE, Ovid Embase, Web of Science, and the Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature—to identify relevant studies from the inception of each database up until April 23, 2024. Free-text search terms tailored to capture literature on AI, ML, cardiac surgery, and research conducted in LMICs were used. The complete search strategy, including specific terms and Boolean operators, is detailed in Online Data Supplement 1.

For inclusion, studies needed to meet the following criteria: They must involve a substantial AI component, specifically a nonlinear computational model such as ML techniques, be conducted in an LMIC as defined by the World Bank Country and Lending Groups Classification, and focus on any aspect of cardiac surgery. Studies were excluded if they did not involve cardiac surgery (eg, focused exclusively on transcatheter interventions), assessed linear risk models, or were secondary studies, abstracts, posters, review articles, letters, commentaries, or editorials. This review followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews guidelines.18 Robotic surgery was excluded because it constitutes predominantly hardware-based operative modality rather than AI/ML-based computational models.

Data Extraction

Covidence Review software was used for the initial screening of titles and abstracts, which was conducted independently by 2 reviewers (S.K.P. and J.D.). After this, a full-text review of the shortlisted articles was performed to assess their eligibility. Data extraction from the selected studies was conducted by 1 reviewer (S.K.P.) and subsequently cross-verified by another reviewer (J.D.) to ensure accuracy. Discrepancies were resolved through consultation with a third reviewer (D.V.). The extracted data included information on study location, characteristics of participants, primary end points, types and origins of AI used, AI performance outcomes, and the clinical subspecialties within cardiac surgery involved. Where available, we further extracted the best-performing model, the reported performance metric or metrics, and the performance value for the best-performing model, including measures of discrimination, calibration, classification performance, regression error, and clinical utility. Additionally, studies were categorized according to their primary end points, which included mortality prediction (death as the primary outcome), medication management, surgical outcomes (intraoperative), postoperative outcomes (nonfatal complications), and disease-specific prediction. Categorical data were summarized as counts with frequencies. Articles were further qualitatively summarized. Given the diverse ways that AI/ML techniques were applied and evaluated across the included studies—often with differing performance metrics, thresholds, and validation methods—we did not formally assess effectiveness or scalability. Moreover, as a scoping review, this article is hypothesis-generating rather than aimed at answering specific research questions.

Results

Our database search yielded a total of 3596 records after removing duplicates and retracted studies (Figure 1). Of these, 3070 records were screened based on titles and abstracts, resulting in 186 studies identified for full-text retrieval. One study was unavailable, leaving 185 full-text articles for review. After exclusions due to various reasons—such as robotic surgery (n = 46), non-AI/ML focus (n = 23), abstract-only publications (n = 12), non-LMIC focus (n = 12), noncardiac surgery (n = 7), letters without data (n = 1), and narrative reviews (n = 1)—a total of 83 studies were included. Detailed references, study characteristics, best-performing models, reported performance metrics, and performance values for the best-performing models are provided in Online Data Supplement 2.

Study Characteristics

Table 1 provides an overview of the subspecialties, population age groups, and study designs represented in the 83 included studies. The median patient sample size across the included studies was 1521 (interquartile range, 523-5477). Most studies (84.3%, n = 70) focused on adult populations, 10 studies (12.0%) focused on pediatric patients, and 2 studies (2.4%) included both adult and pediatric populations. The most common subspecialty was valve surgery (19.3%, n = 16), followed by coronary surgery (15.7%, n = 13) and congenital surgery (13.3%, n = 11). Aortic surgery was featured in 8 studies (9.6%), transplant surgery in 4 studies (4.8%), and 6 studies (7.2%) covered general cardiac surgery without specifying a subspeciality. Mixed-subspeciality series made up 23 studies (27.7%): The most common combinations were coronary + valve (6.0%, n = 5), aortic + coronary + valve (6.0%, n = 5), and aortic + coronary + congenital + valve (7.2%, n = 6); all other permutations—including aortic + coronary, electrophysiology-related mixes, heart failure surgery, and “other”—occurred in 2.4% or less of studies. Regarding study design, retrospective cohort studies predominated, representing 83.1% (n = 69) of the included studies, whereas prospective cohort studies accounted for 7.2% (n = 6). Other study designs included retrospective post hoc analyses (2.4%, n = 2), randomized controlled trials (2.4%, n = 2), numerical studies, cross-sectional analyses, and case-control studies, each contributing 1.2% (n = 1).

Thematic and Geographical Distribution

Figure 2 illustrates the thematic and geographical distribution of the studies included. A majority of studies focused on postoperative outcomes (55.4%, n = 46), followed by mortality prediction (25.3%, n = 21). Research on surgical outcomes (7.2%, n = 6), medication management (8.4%, n = 7), and disease-specific prediction (2.4%, n = 2) represented smaller portions. Geographically, 74.7% (n = 62) of studies originated from China, with further contributions from Brazil and Iran (6 each; 7.2%), India (3; 3.6%), and multinational collaborations (4; 4.8%). Single studies were identified from Turkey and Ethiopia.

Artificial Intelligence Model Use

Table 2 outlines the distribution of the best-performing AI learning models across different model purposes in the 83 included studies. The majority of models (83.1%, n = 69) were used for classification tasks, with regression (10.8%, n = 9), a combination of classification and regression (2.4%, n = 2), image segmentation (1.2%, n = 1), unsupervised learning for clustering analysis (1.2%, n = 1), and other purposes (1.2%, n = 1) being less common. When examining the specific types of models, tree-based ensembles dominated: XGBoost appeared most often (25.3%, n = 21), primarily for classification (29.0%, n = 20), followed by random forests (16.9%, n = 14). Classic logistic regression was third (10.8%, n = 9), with feedforward neural networks (6.0%, n = 5) and single decision trees (4.8%, n = 4) following behind. Gaussian Naïve Bayes was featured in 3 studies (3.6%), whereas 5 other supervised methods—adaptive neuro-fuzzy inference systems, CatBoostClassifier, gradient boosted machines, optimal classification trees, and support vector machines—were each reported twice (2.4% each). The remaining techniques were reported only once each (1.2%).

Model Performance

Reported model-performance measures were heterogeneous across the included studies, reflecting differences in clinical end points, model purposes, validation strategies, and reporting practices (Online Data Supplement 2). Performance metrics were not mutually exclusive, because many studies reported multiple measures. Discrimination metrics were most commonly reported, with area under the curve, area under the receiving operating characteristic curve, receiver operating characteristic–area under the curve, C-statistic, or C-index reported in 69 studies (83.1%). Accuracy or correct prediction measures were reported in 36 studies (43.4%), sensitivity or recall in 34 studies (41.0%), specificity in 26 studies (31.3%), precision or positive predictive value in 16 studies (19.3%), and F1-score or F-measure in 13 studies (15.7%). Calibration-related measures, including Brier score, calibration plots, Hosmer-Lemeshow testing, or expected calibration error, were reported in 16 studies (19.3%), whereas decision-curve analysis or related clinical-utility measures were reported in 7 studies (8.4%). Regression-oriented studies most often reported error-based or agreement measures, including mean absolute error, mean squared error, root mean squared error, R2, or correlation coefficients, which were reported in 10 studies (12.0%). Across classification studies, reported discrimination was frequently moderate to high; however, direct comparison across models was limited by heterogeneity in outcomes, populations, data sources, validation methods, and reporting thresholds.

Temporal Trends

The temporal distribution of publications included in this review shows an increasing research focus on AI/ML in cardiac surgery, particularly after 2020, with the number of studies peaking in 2022 (Figure 3). Figure 4 presents the temporal distribution of studies across different publication themes. Postoperative outcome studies grew markedly after 2021, rising to 14 studies in 2022 and 16 in 2023, with a further 8 appearing in 2024. Studies using AI/ML to predict mortality also increased, moving from 2 studies in 2020 to 4 in 2021 and peaking at 6 in 2022 before declining slightly to 4 in 2023 and 3 in 2024. Medication management studies first appeared in 2018 and reached 3 publications in 2022. Research on surgical outcomes was scattered, typically 1 study per year from 2017 to 2023, before doubling to 2 studies in 2024. Disease-specific prediction remained rare, with single publications in 2022 and 2023, and 1 “other” study was noted in 2022.

Discussion

Previous reviews have primarily concentrated on high-income settings or specific topics, such as postoperative outcomes,19,20 resulting in a fragmented understanding of AI/ML's broader impact on cardiac surgery in resource-constrained environments. This scoping review addresses this gap by identifying key trends in the application of AI and ML to global cardiac surgery, based on 83 studies that met the inclusion criteria. The majority of studies (74.7%, n = 62) originated from China, highlighting a geographical imbalance. Contributions from other LMICs, such as Brazil, Iran, and India, were limited, and many regions with significant cardiovascular disease burdens, such as sub-Saharan Africa, were almost absent from the research landscape. This concentration likely reflects disparities not only in research funding and infrastructure but also in access to high-quality interoperable datasets required for AI/ML technologies. Many LMIC health systems lack integrated databases capable of supporting longitudinal data capturing. Addressing this gap will require investment in scalable data infrastructures and international collaborative approaches, such as transfer learning frameworks that leverage large datasets from high-income countries while enabling local model adaption.

The thematic analysis revealed that the majority of studies (55.4%, n = 46) focused on postoperative outcomes, suggesting that AI/ML has been most widely applied to optimizing care after cardiac surgery. This is in line with the global trend of leveraging AI to predict and manage postoperative complications, which remain a key determinant of patient survival and quality of life. Mortality prediction was the second most common focus (25.3%, n = 21), reflecting AI's role in enhancing predictive accuracy and potentially reducing mortality through earlier interventions. In contrast, research on surgical outcomes and disease-specific prediction was relatively sparse. Given the critical importance of optimizing surgical procedures themselves, this underrepresentation is concerning. AI/ML technologies hold the potential to refine surgical planning, personalize interventions, and improve intraoperative decision-making, yet these applications remain underexplored in the current literature. Likewise, disease-specific prediction models, which could tailor interventions based on patient characteristics and disease progression, were also less frequently addressed. Expanding research in these areas could lead to significant improvements in surgical outcomes and personalized care.

The distribution of AI/ML models showed that classification tasks dominate the field, with XGBoost and random forest models being the most commonly used for classification and regression applications. These models are well suited for tasks such as classifying risk factors, predicting outcomes, and identifying complications, which may explain their widespread use. For instance, studies have applied gradient-boosted machine models and random forest techniques to predict mid- to long-term mortality in heart transplant recipients and among patients undergoing coronary artery bypass grafting.21,22 However, although classification models are critical, the relatively low use of regression models (n = 9) and unsupervised learning (n = 1) indicates an underuse of other powerful AI/ML approaches that could offer deeper insights into patient outcomes, treatment optimization, and data-driven decision-making. Moreover, it is important to acknowledge that AI/ML is not a universal remedy for risk prediction; standard regression models often perform equally well or even better in certain contexts.23 As such, careful consideration is needed to determine when and where AI/ML methods are most appropriate. Interestingly, more advanced models, such as convolutional neural networks and reinforcement learning, were only used in single studies. This suggests that although the field is embracing AI/ML, the adoption of cutting-edge techniques remains limited.

The performance data further demonstrate that most studies emphasized discrimination, particularly area under the curve–based metrics, whereas calibration, clinical utility, and external validation were less consistently reported. This distinction is clinically important: A model with acceptable discrimination may still be poorly calibrated, difficult to interpret, or inadequately transportable to different health systems, surgical populations, or resource settings. In the context of LMICs, where case mix, perioperative pathways, data completeness, and health-system capacity may differ substantially across countries, model performance in a single-center or single-country dataset cannot be assumed to translate into generalizable risk prediction. Future studies should report standardized discrimination and calibration measures, include decision-curve or net-benefit analyses where appropriate, and prioritize external, temporal, and geographic validation before clinical implementation. Increased integration of these reporting practices, alongside judicious use of advanced methods, could help address more complex clinical challenges while improving the reliability, interpretability, and scalability of AI/ML tools in resource-constrained settings.

The temporal trends highlight a surge in AI/ML research in cardiac surgery post-2020, particularly in the areas of postoperative outcomes and mortality prediction. This increase may be driven by the broader integration of AI into healthcare and a growing recognition of its potential to improve patient outcomes. However, the relatively few studies focused on medication management and surgical outcomes suggest important gaps in the literature. As AI/ML continues to evolve, future research should expand into these underexplored areas, particularly in LMICs where optimized medication protocols and surgical techniques could have the most significant impact on patient care. Notably, the advent of large language models like ChatGPT in 2022 could further accelerate AI-driven applications in global cardiac surgery, ranging from facilitating access to educational resources (especially in regions facing limited educational opportunities or international tuition barriers) to improving documentation and note-taking in under-resourced hospitals. Although our post–November 2022 search window was brief, and new publications often lag behind technological breakthroughs, upcoming studies will likely explore the role of LLMs in enhancing both clinical decision-making and operational efficiencies within cardiac surgery, thereby potentially transforming patient care across diverse healthcare settings.

Medication management was addressed in just 8.4% of studies. Amruthlal and colleagues24 developed a mobile application using support vector machines to optimize anticoagulation management in patients undergoing valve surgery in India. These findings underline the uneven distribution of AI applications, emphasizing the need to diversify research focus. Although advanced techniques, such as reinforcement learning and convolutional neural networks are emerging globally, their uptake in LMICs remains constrained by limited data infrastructure, high-quality labeled datasets, and cross-disciplinary collaboration.

The geographical and thematic differences identified in this review underscore the urgent need for collaborative international efforts to support AI/ML research in underrepresented regions. Future investigations should prioritize expanding geographical representation by supporting research initiatives in sub-Saharan Africa, Southeast Asia, and other underserved regions. Beyond local model development, collaboration can include structured data sharing, model sharing, and knowledge translation frameworks that enable algorithms developed in data-rich settings to be adapted to LMIC contexts. Approaches such as transfer learning and federated learning, whereby models are iteratively trained across sites without requiring raw data exchange, may facilitate equitable capacity building while mitigating data sovereignty concerns. Cloud-based platforms and web-enabled decision support tools also allow models housed in resource-rich environments to be securely accessed via mobile or browser-based interfaces, reducing infrastructure dependence. Increasing global internet connectivity, including fiberoptic and satellite-based networks, further enables real-time model updating, remote consultation, and cross-border sharing of imaging and clinical data, offering a scalable pathway to improve access to cardiac surgery in LMICs.

Moving forward, there is a critical need to channel the momentum of AI/ML research into practical, high-impact implementations in LMICs, where constrained resources amplify the value of cost-effective solutions. Readers who want to deepen their understanding of AI's foundations in cardiac care are encouraged to consult recent reviews that outline its emerging role in cardiac surgery and cardiovascular medicine, as well as the unique ethical considerations that arise.13,25,E1,E2 Beyond the immediate clinical context, several major global health agencies and philanthropic organizations, including the United States Agency for International Development, The Rockefeller Foundation, the Bill & Melinda Gates Foundation, and the World Health Organization, have highlighted AI/ML as an important tool to bolster health systems at scale.E3-E6 These initiatives highlight the importance of co-creating AI interventions with local communities, ensuring equitable access to advanced technologies, and building robust digital infrastructures. Recent systematic reviews in LMIC settings have similarly noted the need for robust data ecosystems, better evaluation of real-world performance, and a focus on trust-building to realize the full potential of AI in these regions.E7,E8 By drawing on these broader frameworks, future research and policy efforts should aim to (1) expand AI-driven educational resources for clinicians and trainees in under-resourced settings; (2) integrate and adapt AI solutions to local data ecosystems; (3) address ethical and governance challenges specific to LMICs; and (4) conduct rigorous impact evaluations, including cost-effectiveness analyses, to inform sustainable adoption. Such a multifaceted, context-driven approach will help realize the transformative potential of AI/ML to reduce cardiac care disparities worldwide.

Limitations

This scoping review has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the search strategy used was comprehensive but not exhaustive, and there is a possibility that some relevant studies were overlooked. The exclusion of studies published in languages other than English further limits the comprehensiveness and generalizability of the findings, potentially omitting valuable insights from non–English-speaking regions. Second, the heterogeneity of study designs, methodologies, and outcome measures among the included studies complicates the synthesis and generalization of results. Although Online Data Supplement 2 summarizes available model-performance metrics for the best-performing models, the variability in reported end points, thresholds, validation approaches, and performance measures precluded pooled comparison of model performance across studies. Furthermore, the review did not assess the quality of the included studies, which may have introduced biases or inconsistencies in the reported findings. Last, although this review identifies key gaps in knowledge and suggests directions for future research, it does not directly address the feasibility or cost-effectiveness of implementing AI/ML solutions in LMICs. Translating the identified implications into actionable strategies requires further investigation, including pilot programs and real-world implementation studies.

Conclusions

This scoping review demonstrates the growing role of AI and ML in global cardiac surgery, particularly in improving postoperative outcomes and mortality prediction. However, significant disparities in geographical representation remain, with underrepresentation of LMICs and underexplored areas such as surgical outcomes and medication management. Future research must prioritize diversity, collaboration, and innovation to maximize the potential of AI/ML in addressing the global burden of cardiovascular diseases, particularly in resource-constrained settings.
