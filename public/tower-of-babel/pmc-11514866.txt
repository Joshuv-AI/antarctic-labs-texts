Applications of blockchain technology in long-term care: use cases, potentials, and barriers
Authors: Beate Steurer, Birgit Trukeschitz, Ulrike Schneider
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDigitalization in long-term care (LTC) is being discussed as a means of easing pressures on care service delivery, optimizing care coordination and planning processes, and improving service quality. With its advanced data protection and process automation features, blockchain technology could help solve data security and privacy issues, especially for the implementation of assistive technologies. Blockchain (BC) is thus a new technology being tapped in LTC. However, little is known about the fields, scope, and experiences of BC applications in this sector.ObjectiveThis systematic review aims to provide an extensive overview of current discussions of BC technology in LTC settings. Particular attention was given to the use cases, the objectives behind applying the new technology, and barriers as well as facilitating factors for the implementation.MethodAccording to the PRISMA methodology, we conducted a systematic literature review of academic articles and conference papers published between 2016 and 2023. The search through the Scopus database yielded 194 results, 29 of which were selected according to our inclusion and exclusion criteria.ResultsResearch on BC technologies has taken off in LTC since 2016. Scientific interest centers around use cases for Ambient Assisted Living (AAL), care service management, and the improved integration of care ecosystems. However, most of the selected studies discuss prototypes that have neither yet been extensively evaluated nor been implemented and tested in real-world scenarios. Among the implementation barriers, technical immaturity, stakeholder cooperation, organizational change management, access to adequate IT infrastructure and end-user acceptance issues were most evident.ConclusionFuture research is needed to gain insights into context-specific implementation efforts, user experiences, benefits, and pitfalls of using BC technologies in LTC settings. While many expectations are tied to BC applications, potential negative effects have received little attention in the literature. Whether BC technology can serve as a mitigator between privacy and security preservation and improved information sharing in LTC settings is an important topic for further research.Keywords: Blockchain, Long-term care, Systematic review, AAL, Care service management, Care ecosystems, Privacy, Security, Health data

Background

Digitalization in long-term care (LTC) is being discussed as one important way to ease pressures on care workers, make care coordination and planning more efficient, and improve service quality [24]. At the same time, technology adoption faces challenges specific to the LTC context as this sector requires solid protection of sensitive data, specific attention to user acceptance and ethical questions, as well as complex change processes [19]. So far, most attention has been paid to information and communication technologies (ICTs), assistive technologies including digital devices that support mobility, sensor-based monitoring, Ambient Assisted Living (AAL) and telehealth systems; and robotic systems [24, 47, 52]. Recently, the benefits of blockchain technology have been discussed in the context of LTC to improve data access and security, optimize processes, and increase the efficiency of planning and scheduling services [20, 53].

A blockchain (BC) comprises a registry of data records which are secured by means of cryptography. The name derives from the fact, that data is organized in blocks which are connected or chained together via cryptographic functions. Thus, the registry can be extended by linking a new block to the chain of existing blocks. Furthermore, the removal of a single block is not possible without breaking the chain, rendering the record immutable. This in turn guarantees that the data cannot be manipulated or forged [45]. In a BC, data are stored in decentralized networks rather than on a single server [26]. Whereas BC technology is commonly known for cryptocurrency applications, the technology has been explored for additional purposes in a variety of fields, as it provides a new way to store and share information [26, 34]. In some BCs, it is possible to restrict data access and viewing as well as editing permissions. By means of smart contracts, which establish sequential rules, BC technology can automate process flows and enhance data security [34]. The technology has attracted heightened interest in the healthcare domain. Scientific interest has mainly centered around Electronic Health or Patient Records and Clinical trial support systems [22, 25]. Further fields of applications being tapped concern telemedicine, automated diagnostic services and pharma supply-chain management [13, 25, 26]. Even though research on BC technology in LTC is recent and emerging, LTC has received comparatively little attention in this context.

This paper aims to provide a systematic review of current discussions and use cases of BC technology in LTC settings. First, we map out and provide an overview of the research activity in LTC since the emergence of the BC. Second, through a qualitative content analysis, we report on the use cases, the objectives behind the new technology, and barriers as well as facilitating factors for the implementation. Overall, the review aims to provide a comprehensive report on the existing knowledge as a starting point for future research on BC applications in this area.

Methods

This paper applied the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA). This methodology lays out a step-for-step guide for planning, conducting and documenting the search procedure [36]. As follows, we outline the search strategy, information sources used, the rationale behind choosing and eliminating texts for the qualitative synthesis, the selection process, and methods applied for the qualitative analysis.

Search strategy

An initial exploration of the literature revealed that there were no publications on BC in LTC before 2016 and that most publications on BC technologies are in English. The systematic search was thus limited to sources in English within the time frame 2016 to 2023. Following the PRISMA requirements, we used a uniform set of keywords. As shown in Fig. 1, we combined subject terms for BC technology with terms for specific LTC settings, services and target groups to search for literature that focused on specific aspects of LTC.

Search strategy: keyword combination

Information sources

The main source for papers was the Scopus database because it encompasses large numbers of sources across disciplines. The EBSCO and PubMed databases were used to cross-check for additional articles in the field of medicine and related sciences. However, in terms of eligible articles, we only found duplicates. Therefore, all sources were obtained from the Scopus database.

Eligibility criteria

Articles had to address BC technology in relation to long-term care and/or have a narrow focus on the target groups of older adults or adults with instrumental activities in daily living (IADL) limitations. Papers addressing the health care context more broadly were considered only when they included use cases in the LTC sector. Only journal articles and conference papers were considered. Supplementary conference material were automatically removed. Studies were excluded if they focused on the health sector more generally, on specific medical issues (such as diabetes care) or if the main focus was on a different technology.

Selection process

We conducted multiple cycles of selection, review and revision. The primary selection was carried out by the first author. The second and third authors reviewed the selection, and together with the first author, adapted the search strategy and final selection of articles.

Synthesis of results

For the synthesis of results published in eligible papers, we applied the qualitative content analysis method of Mayring [33], which aims to identify key messages within large amounts of text. We generated categories deductively and used them to synthesize the content of publications included in the review. In a further step, this process was complemented by inductive theory building through open coding. The initial categories used for coding were closely oriented towards the research questions “use cases”, “objectives”, “effects”, and “implementation processes”. Additionally, open coding served to identify the most prevalent topics and arguments within these categories. Within “objectives”, this included “reorganizing care”, “trust and security” and “user empowerment”; within the category “implementation processes”, “user acceptance and usability”, “technical factors”, and “organizational factors” emerged as subcategories. All full texts were manually read and analysed.

Mitigation of potential bias

We aimed to minimize the risk of selection bias in our review by applying the PRISMA guidelines for systematic reviews. The systematic search strategy features clearly defined keywords as well as the inclusion and exclusion criteria to enhance reproducibility. The search strategy was subjected to cycles of feedback and adaptation among our team. Feedback cycles were also applied to the interpretation of the texts involving all authors in order to reduce a potential outcome bias. Grey literature or unpublished studies were not within the scope of this review.

Timespan

Looking at the initial 194 results within the targeted time span (2016–2023), the annual scientific production snowballed between 2019 and 2020 and reached a new temporary peak with at least 45 publications in 2023. Scientific activity in this field is therefore recent but emerging. The 29 selected studies were all published between 2019 and 2023.

Geographical distribution

BC in LTC has attracted research interest around the globe. However, most publications within all the retrieved results from the initial search are related to research institutions in China and India. Among our selected 29 studies, almost half (13) were published by authors affiliated with Asian research institutions, with the majority from China. Further studies were published by authors affiliated with research institutions in Anglo-Saxon countries (8), continental European research institutes (7) and institutions in South America (1) (Table 2).

Disciplines and methods

We grouped the selected studies according to their disciplinary anchoring (Table 2). Twenty of the 29 studies included in the synthesis are Computer Science or Engineering papers, 12 of which present proof-of-concept papers, i.e., conceptual and technological papers for a prototype without evaluation in a real-world scenario. Two systematic literature reviews [14, 20] have attempted to provide an overview of BC in specific LTC settings. The remaining six papers discuss prototypes, including evaluations through surveys [8, 10, 50], qualitative case studies [53, 58] and a usability test [9]. These evaluations were either used to inform the development of the concept or abstained from measuring usability or acceptance in depth. In only two cases [10, 50] were care clients consulted. In the other cases, the study participants were either care organizations’ managers or employees. The survey studies are based on small samples and short questionnaires with rather broad questions concerning attitudes or needs.

Apart from Computer Science and Engineering, two papers applied a Medicine/Public Health approach [32, 56], and seven studies were situated within the Social Sciences [2, 27, 28, 31, 51, 59, 60]. These nine papers strongly emphasize business, finance and public health aspects. The studies mostly addressed questions concerning business process innovations in care service management or the organization of health information management. Accordingly, six studies applied conceptual modelling methods based on process or stakeholder analyses, which aimed at developing innovative concepts for BC usage from a business process perspective [2, 27, 28, 51, 56, 60]. One further study [59] discussed the potential of BC applications through a strategic management analysis. Two studies within the social sciences and public health/medicine are concerned with broader questions of potentials and adoption challenges of BC in LTC [31, 32]. They applied a literature and policy analysis [32] and a survey study [31]. The latter is based on a sample of 181 managers of long-term care institutions. It analyses the drivers of BC adoption in LTC by means of a partial least squares structural equation model.

Theoretical underpinnings

The vast majority of analyzed texts takes a hands-on approach to developing new technology or creating process innovation. Accordingly, most papers did not base their studies on established theoretical frameworks. If so, they used application-oriented models or guidelines such as a mutual-support model [12, 59], service quality characteristics [10], quality of life criteria [50], staffing optimisation programming models [53], time-banking guidelines [8] or fall prediction models [41]. Only three studies referred to theoretical frameworks such as innovation theory and technology-organisation-environment framework models [31], social action theory [51], and theories from transaction cost economics [58].

Use cases and thematic focus

Figure 3 below provides an overview of the most dominant use cases and topics addressed in the selected literature. We find a strong thematic emphasis on AAL applications [1, 5, 18, 20, 29, 35, 37, 41, 42, 50, 55, 56] (see Sect. 3.2.1), suggesting that there is recent and increasing interest in the development of new BC-based smart devices for home and care monitoring. The second largest thematic cluster focuses on BC in home care related to managing the logistics and efficient planning of home care services [8, 10, 28, 38, 39, 60]. Another form of care service management is discussed in terms of volunteer service management (time banking), again emphasizing care at home [9, 12, 59]. The literature on care ecosystems aims at improved integration of health and care services at a higher administrative level, including public institutions, hospitals, insurance companies, etc. [2, 27, 51]. Singular studies have explored the potential application of BC for institutional care planning [53] or insurance management [58]. Three studies [14, 31, 32] aimed at a broader discussion of the role or potential of BC in healthcare [32] and LTC [14, 31].

Types of BC

In relation to data access regulation, BCs can be private (only selected and verified participants can access data information and perform transactions within a closed network, which is controlled by one authority) or public (transactions are transparently recorded and accessible to all participants of a decentralized network). Non-public BCs are termed “permissioned” meaning that specific roles or permissions are allocated to certain users [49]. Consortium BCs are similar to private BCs, in which multiple participating organizations authorize transactions [54]. Among the conceptual studies, 16 papers suggest private or consortium BCs [1, 2, 5, 9, 12, 18, 28, 38, 39, 41, 50, 51, 53, 55, 56, 58]. Only three papers explicitly put forward the use of a public BC [8, 10, 60]. Some papers did not specify which exact kind of BC is applied or envisioned [5, 29, 31, 35, 42, 59].

The collection of literature shows a variety of technological solutions with different combinations of technologies to be fit for purpose. BC is used for supporting data exchange in Internet-of-Things (IoT)-networks (mostly for AAL-settings) [1, 53, 55] and in combination with mobile health applications [5, 37]. For improved care service management in community care, one paper discussed BC with Artificial Intelligence (AI) [10]. Ethereum (a public BC network) was most cited as ecosystem used for the BC systems in the discussed contexts [8, 35, 37, 51, 55]. Bazel et al. [9] and Cui et al. [27] used tools for cross-chain collaboration. Some studies integrated BC with the Interplanetary File System (IPFS) [35, 37] or Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources (FHIR) [5, 38, 39].

Qualitative results

To provide an overview of current discussions on BC technology in LTC, we focused our qualitative synthesis on three thematic aspects: the most prominent use cases discussed in the literature, the objectives stated behind applying BC, and factors deemed important for BC implementation. We have therefore structured the following report according to these three thematic clusters (see Fig. 4).

Thematic clusters overview

Thematic cluster I: use cases for blockchain technology in LTC

Ambient assisted living (AAL) supported by blockchain

The main use cases of BC in the context of LTC are AAL technologies embedded in the home to monitor and document health status and care requirements. In this context, BC technology is framed as a solution to safely store, share, and control access to health-related data in IoT-supported environments [20]. Among the 12 papers on AAL applications, nine are proof-of-concept papers suggesting prototypes. These concepts and prototypes include systems featuring cameras, wearable devices, and sensors that collect biometric data (such as blood pressure, heart rate, and body temperature), track activities and locations or recognize motion. The objectives are to prevent accidents, improve emergency responses, support remote treatment of chronic diseases or render care schedules more individualized and targeted by improved mechanisms to predict behaviour [14, 18, 29, 32, 35, 37, 42, 50, 55]. A particular (and recently emphasized) concern is optimizing fall prediction. Three studies presented concepts for improved solutions supported by wearable technologies [1, 41, 42]. Further applications include consent management procedures in AAL environments [56] and smart applications to promote physical activity among older adults [5].

Decentralized blockchain-based system architectures for care service management

New applications for the management of home care aim to improve processes of care service management among clients, care organizations and care workers, relatives, and volunteers [8, 10, 28, 38, 39, 60]. Compared to extant centralized systems, new systems using BC technologies promise flexibility, customizability, and process automation. This means, for example, that tasks are assigned flexibly according to changing requirements or the availability of care workers [8, 10]. Such process optimization requires improved collection and circulation of information assisted by assessments of client requirements (e.g., via AAL devices) [28] or interoperability among different parties for more efficient information flows [38, 39, 60].

Coordination and management also matter for time-banking systems, in which care services are organized based on the exchange of time credits instead of financial remuneration. The studies [9, 12, 59] discuss the potential of BC technology to establish a decentralized and secure data exchange system for voluntary assistance or care in exchange for time tokens that are stored and can be used for requesting similar services in the future. One study [58] applied this approach to the procedures of private LTC insurance to reduce transaction costs among stakeholders (clients, medical facilities, insurance, nursing homes, etc.) while maintaining privacy and trust. Another study [53] proposed a process management application for nursing homes that helps monitor and rationalize care tasks in favour of improved service quality and more effective organization.

Integrating care ecosystems via blockchain technology

BC applications are often framed as important tools for facilitating more integrated care ecosystems with greater care continuity [2, 27, 51]. The primary objective is to improve communication flows and collaboration among a range of stakeholders (including hospitals, clients, health care providers, volunteers, technology providers, and pharmaceutical and device manufacturers etc.) to develop a more holistic and efficient approach to care service provision and information exchange [2, 27, 32, 51]. As such, the suggested conceptual models operate on a large scale aiming to improve communication and coordination across organizations, e.g. between hospitals, public authorities and LTC organizations. The studies within this cluster conducted process or stakeholder analyses in combination with conceptual modelling to develop concepts for optimized process and information flows [2, 27, 51].

Thematic cluster II: objectives behind the application of blockchain technology in LTC

In all the examined papers, the problem statements referred to the growing need for care services in light of demographic changes. The identified challenge is to increase the quantity of services while accounting for the individual needs of the vulnerable populations addressed [8, 10]. The key objectives of BC-based digital solutions are to enhance quality of life by improving quality of care [1, 14, 27–29, 32, 38, 39, 42, 50] and to ensure the affordability of care services [1, 35, 42, 55, 59, 60]. Within this wider scope, we identified three main objectives pursued with the application of BC in LTC.

Reorganizing care

To address increasing demand of care services, labour shortages in the sector, and rising costs for individuals and governments, BC-based solutions are part of two main strategies: expanding care at home and delivering care services more efficiently [32, 53, 59]. Expanding care at home, often referred to as “aging in place” or “active aging”, is expected to support independent living and decrease pressure on formal LTC provision. BC in AAL applications is supposed to contribute to this by improving home monitoring and (remote) care [1, 5, 14, 18, 20, 29, 35, 37, 41, 42, 50, 55, 56, 59]. Optimizing care planning and service delivery through more targeted and efficient care organization and process automation is expected to increase the productivity of care work, thus mitigating labour shortages [8–10, 14, 38, 39, 53]. Concerning BC innovation designed for care management in a nursing home, the authors of one study [53] argue that the resulting improvement of work organization can lead to reduced staffing costs within an institution. Furthermore, BC applications are expected to contribute to containing public spending on LTC as they support time banking or volunteering and help reduce the number of accidents such as falls in private households, which means lower public spending on medical treatments [1, 18, 29, 41]. An important objective in the context of making care ecosystems more efficient is enhancing the interoperability of different systems to connect fragmented client information and coordinate complementary services, thus closing communication and service gaps and increasing the efficiency of information exchanges [20, 38, 51, 58].

Data security and storage concerns – potentials for increased trust

Data security is particularly important in LTC where personal and health-related data are highly sensitive and require enhanced data protection standards [20]. The literature stipulates that BC features such as decentralization, immutability of records and encryption mechanisms are ways to account for heightened security provision [20, 31, 56]. BC-based applications are thus framed as solutions that improve confidentiality and security for the safe distribution of information and collaboration among different parties [14, 20].

Data immutability in the BC holds that records cannot be changed after they are generated [9, 32, 39, 58]. In LTC, this is important for generating trust for the collaboration among remote parties because it allows for improved transparency and traceability of processes and information. Two studies [8, 9], for example, confirm that tracking care service transactions and assignment status could be improved by using a BC-based tool. Providing an overview and enhanced control over delivery and performance, as well as increasing transparency of the burdens experienced by carers, are expected to result in an improvement of these services in different care environments [8–10, 56].

Smart contracts can be used to regulate procedures (such as authentication processes for accessing data) among a range of stakeholders, disclose the criteria for these processes and catalyze enhanced process automation [8]. This reduces manual operations for decision making, reviewing or auditing, thus eliminating the need for third-party intermediaries, such as trust keepers or reviewers, who could be biased [8, 35, 39, 53, 55]. BC technology can establish secure interoperability via health data transfer and accessibility for different users [20, 38, 39, 58]. It is therefore presented as a possible mitigator of trust issues in care networks, which is an important step towards increased integration of care ecosystems [20, 56, 58].

Digital inclusion and user empowerment by using blockchain technology

Enabling or empowering users is another objective dominantly put forward in the reviewed studies. Some BC-based platforms are supposed to increase real-time information about home care service provision [8] or improve monitoring of personal health status [50]. Further enabling functions are described by [2, 8, 56] as the suggested tools could support users by introducing simplified and more transparent procedures, particularly for vulnerable populations. The objective is to improve clients’ understanding of these procedures and increase their competences to complete and follow administrative procedures such as issuing insurance claims and giving consent in AAL environments [2, 56]; see also Royal et al. [40]. This applies not only to individual users or patients but also to care organizations or staff, who obtain heightened control over the care services performed [38, 53].

AAL applications are expected to increase independence and user control over service provision, and they could also improve safety, e.g., by monitoring persons living alone [35, 37, 50]. At the same time, however, AAL applications raise privacy issues. In this context, BC technology has been discussed as an instrument for improved privacy protection [5, 35, 37, 41, 50]. Others [56] envision a BC-based platform for consent management processes that allows for flexible, understandable, and transparent means of informed consent.

Thematic cluster III: barriers and facilitators of implementation and technology adoption

To date, research on the implementation processes and adoption issues of BC technologies in LTC is rare, as to the largest part the technologies are still in development [31]. As follows, we summarize the challenging domains that so far were identified or hinted at in the literature.

User acceptance and usability

BC is a rather novel technology and mature models for its application in LTC are still lacking. Therefore, the lack of experience with this type of technology leads to insecurity concerning the overall benefits of the implementation, in general, and system efficiency and security provisions, in particular [8, 14, 31, 55, 56]. Privacy and data security (see 3.3.2) are indeed among the unsolved issues discussed in the literature, which leads to rather low trust in the new technology [1, 8, 27, 35, 51, 53, 56]. For example, one study [5] found that there is not yet an adequate solution to protect privacy in AAL or smart city applications. In this sense, a survey of potential care clients [50] revealed a strong reluctance to share data related to health, movement and activity recognition, location and demographics. The implementation of BC technologies thus requires the development of security mechanisms (such as user authentication) and a common understanding and acceptance of privacy regulations among all stakeholders [20]. This can be especially challenging given that a heterogeneity of stakeholders brings about potentially diverging goals related to the resources and information shared [51].

New technologies in the context of LTC generally face usability barriers. What concerns digital design, care clients’ special requirements are often overlooked or subject to generalization [9, 50, 56]. In addition, the reluctance to change behaviour is always an impeding factor in technology adoption and could further intensify due to these usability barriers [50, 51]. This poses a further difficulty in terms of privacy regulations, as authentication procedures may be difficult for elderly or impaired users [5] (see also Elahi et al. [15]).

Technological factors

Limited data storage capacity of blockchain systems is often mentioned as an issue because it can cause low transaction speed, high resource consumption and high transaction costs [1, 8, 9, 14, 27, 29, 35]. In this sense, scalability is often named a challenge [14, 20, 37]. To mitigate the issue of storage capacity, [8] argue that gateway technologies might be needed “to bridge data access across on- and off-chain systems” ([8] p. 2); [18] suggest off-chain data storage with on-chain storage of hash values of the data, which would make it safe and mitigate problems with transaction speed, e.g. cloud solution for storage [53]; and [35, 37] propose the usage of the IPFS, a distributed storage platform. To address scalability, [51] refer to the possibility of segmenting target groups, e.g. separating home care recipients and nursing home residents in terms of different health data networks. However, this might substantially lower the original goal of improving care continuity.

The interoperability of different types of traditional data storage systems (databases, flat files, or cloud) with BC is needed to enable integrated care ecosystems and to ensure integration into legacy systems. This has been identified as another barrier to implementation and system efficiency and poses particular concerns for data confidentiality and privacy [8, 14, 20, 27].

Facilitating conditions in LTC

BC technology adoption is demanding because the new technology must be embedded in the existing organizational and technical system [9] noted that organizations (especially in the care sector) might lack the expertise or resources to integrate BC technology into their systems (see also [32, 39]). Interoperability and scalability are therefore not only technical issues but also organizational challenges, because they require cooperation between different companies and institutions as well as structural changes in (business) processes [2, 51, 56, 58]. Furthermore, different LTC settings and environments need tailored solutions, which increases complexity and resource intensity [32, 51].

Within organizations, top management support has been found to be highly relevant for the implementation of BC technology [31]. This entails active implementation efforts in terms of resource allocation, workshops and training for employees and presupposes that managers or decision-makers know about the options and advantages of these technologies [8, 9, 31, 53]. A related factor concerns the costs of technology implementation. The acquisition of new devices can be costly for LTC institutions [32] as well as for the home care sector [20, 42].

The implementation efforts in healthcare and LTC greatly rely on stakeholder involvement including government and industry partners [56]. In this sense, government policies in terms of incentivized pilot projects and regulatory support have been found to be highly beneficial for implementation efforts [8, 31, 51, 56]. However, thus far, BC adoption has not been a major concern in the care sector. Even in the context of China, where government support for technology is comparatively high, only a few policies aimed at promoting its adoption in the care sector have been developed [31].

Discussion

Research on BC-based technologies in an LTC context has taken off since 2016. Scientific interest centers around potential use cases for improved home monitoring systems through AAL applications, improved service management in mobile/remote care, nursing homes or insurance provision, and information management for more integrated care ecosystems. In these discourses, demographic change and growing care needs are framed as a problem to be tackled through business process optimization, improved information management and supporting independent living at home. BC technology is discussed as the potential key to improved data security and the creation of trusted environments for collaboration. In light of the sensitive nature of health data, data security constitutes an important challenge to meet. The significance of the technology lies in ensuring data security while allowing for increased information flows and data sharing.

Although progress is made, the current status of knowledge also has limitations. Most of the selected studies are either completely conceptual papers (proof of concept or conceptual models) or feature merely technical evaluations of prototypes. While the technical feasibility, system efficiency and improved performance in distributed environments could be proven in test settings (see for example [8, 10, 38, 39]), none of the technologies was implemented and extensively tested in real-world scenarios. This is highlighted by the self-declared limitations within the papers, which in some cases state that testing or implementation in real-world scenarios was outside the scope of the studies [1, 2, 5, 12, 51, 56, 58]. Further studies conceded that the testing sample for the evaluation of the technology was small [10] or not representative [31], or that the evaluation design was not longitudinal, which would have been important for assessing an innovation process [18, 31]. Therefore, insights into context-specific implementation efforts, user experiences, benefits and experienced barriers or negative outcomes are missing, as most references more prominently discuss potentials and ideas rather than measured effects [14].

The results of our review suggest that, to date, the real-world application of BC in LTC is small and that most applications are still in the testing phase. Therefore, gaining insights into implementation or dissemination attempts would have required additional empirical investigations, which were outside the scope of this study. However, we can corroborate some of our findings about the reasons for adoption barriers with literature from the wider healthcare context, in which case studies have mainly investigated BC use for Electronic Health Records (EHRs). A strand of research on BC technology has furthermore defined important adoption metrics (see for example [48, 49, 57]. While this was not reflected in our literature, assessment frameworks for organizational readiness provide insights for important prerequisites to implementation in the health sector.

Balasubramanian et al. [4] and Sanda et al. [44] suggest an assessment framework for organisational readiness in the healthcare sector. The proposed model includes several dimensions of organisational readiness including motivational readiness (need for change), engagement readiness (interest and knowledge in the technology), technology readiness and structural readiness (available resources and infrastructure). Additionally, these dimensions are related to the readiness of the respective stakeholders (government, business partners, solution providers, end-users). The key findings from the case studies suggest that in healthcare (although depending on the respective environment) stakeholder collaboration is one of the most important factors. This particularly relates to government initiatives to enable, adopt and regulate BC technology [4]. It also means that BC adoption requires a high degree of stakeholder cooperation with all stakeholders needing to obtain an understanding of the technology [6, 44, 57]. Stakeholders were found to be discouraged by a lack of regulations and laws pertaining to BC technology or a lack of knowledge thereof [4, 44]. Privacy, trust and data protection are the key concerns of industry partners as well as end users [4, 6, 44], which is in line with the findings from the analysed studies in this review.

In terms of technological readiness, Alzahrani et al. [3] find that BC is still a rather immature technology with only few realized projects. Our findings suggest technical immaturity in terms of scalability (storage limitations) and interoperability issues to be most present in the concepts for LTC [20, 31]. In the context of EHRs, performance and storage issues were found to be present in adopted BC technologies alongside cost trade-offs [11]. Costs are indeed repeatedly named as an important adoption metric, which is also reflected in our results [43].

Introducing BC means that organizations face transformation on multiple levels [32, 57]. Consistent with our findings, senior management support for implementing change was thus detected as an essential factor [6]. In LTC organizations, however, managers’ attitudes towards new technology were found to differ greatly, which might pose challenges for adoption [7, 46]. Another central factor for BC implementation is access to adequate IT infrastructure [6]. In this regard, LTC might be a particularly challenging environment as digitalization is lagging behind other health care sectors, the digital systems and technologies used are highly diverse and institutional change is difficult [7, 19, 21, 30, 47, 51]. Broader strategies for digitalization in LTC might thus bee needed in order to support the integration of BC and related technologies into existing LTC processes and support relevant innovation efforts in this sector in general [20].

Directions for future research

Firstly, implementation requirements for disruptive technologies such as BC are an especially important field for future examination as the LTC context poses difficulties in this regard. Further examinations of organizational readiness requirements in healthcare and LTC are needed as to date comprehensive assessment models for these sectors are missing [3].

Secondly, against the backdrop of rising interest and the development of new applications for BC technologies, especially the strong interest in AAL health and care monitoring systems found in this study, ethical aspects should not remain underexplored. Although privacy preservation and non-intrusiveness of assistive technologies are matters of concern expressed in the literature, these aspects have not been subjected to in-depth analysis or broader reflection [18, 29], and in some cases, data security has not been discussed at all [58]. Whether BC technology can serve as a mitigator between privacy preservation and improved information sharing, or whether it poses yet another challenge for privacy preservation due to enhanced transparency are important subjects for further research.

Finally, the selected studies within this review have only marginally included end-user perspectives and show overly simplified imaginations of the target users [28, 55]. To minimize negative implications and improve usability as well as trust and user acceptance, development processes should feature interdisciplinary, participatory approaches oriented towards user requirements [16, 17, 24].

Conclusion

This review took a systematic approach to the application, objectives, experiences and barriers of blockchain (BC) technology in the context of long-term care (LTC). The qualitative synthesis of the selected papers indicates that scientific interest in the technology has strongly accelerated since 2016. The topic has received global attention under the premise that BC adoption could be part of strategies aimed at addressing long-standing issues related to health data security. This could help achieving more efficient data and process management in healthcare and LTC contexts. The most prominent use cases in LTC relate to AAL applications for home and health monitoring, decentralized architectures for improved care management in different care settings, and concepts for a better integration of care ecosystems.

Evaluations of BC applications have so far mainly focused on technological aspects. Insights into implementation efforts, adoption drawbacks and general experiences from testing in real-world settings are still missing. However, the literature provides some clues as to the most prominent challenges specific to applications used in the LTC context. In this sense, stakeholder cooperation, government support, technical maturity with regards to scalability, storage capacity and interoperability, concerns over privacy and data protection as well as compatibility with existing IT infrastructure were named as important factors for BC adoption strategies.

To realise the potentials of BC technology for the LTC sector, future research should look into implementation requirements, the framework conditions for digital infrastructures in LTC and user acceptance issues. As the context of LTC poses many challenges for digital innovation, the implementation of disruptive technologies will require institutional or organisational solutions and broader political support strategies. With the rising interest especially in AAL and care management innovation, it will be important to consider the ethical aspects of potential side effects, which should go hand in hand with more interdisciplinary approaches for improved understanding of user requirements.
