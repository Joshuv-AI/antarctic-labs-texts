Bootstrap SGD: Algorithmic Stability and Robustness
Andreas Christmann, Yunwen Lei

Abstract




In this paper some methods to use the empirical bootstrap approach for stochastic gradient descent (SGD) to minimize the empirical risk over a separable Hilbert space are investigated from the view point of algorithmic stability and statistical robustness. The first two types of approaches are based on averages and are investigated from a theoretical point of view. A generalization analysis for bootstrap SGD of Type 1 and Type 2 based on algorithmic stability is done. Another type of bootstrap SGD is proposed to demonstrate that it is possible to construct purely distribution-free pointwise confidence intervals of the median curve using bootstrap SGD.

1
Introduction

Bootstrap is an effective statistical method to improve the generalization of machine learning models by resampling. The basic idea is to first use sampling with replacement to produce
B
bootstrap samples from the original training data set. Then, for the
b
-th bootstrap sample (
b=1,\ldots,B
), a learning algorithm is applied to produce a model
h^{(b)}
. Finally, an aggregation method is used to combine these local models for prediction.
There exists a huge literature on bootstrap, and we refer to
[
7
,
8
,
9
]
and the references therein.

Depending on the aggregation method, we have different types of bootstrap methods.
In this paper, we consider three types of bootstrap methods for stochastic gradient descent (SGD) methods, which become the workhorse behind the success of many machine learning applications and have received a lot of attention
[
22
,
24
,
14
]
.
For bootstrap of Type 1, we take an average of weight parameters and use the model associated to the averaged weight parameter for prediction. For bootstrap of Type 2, we first use each local model for prediction, and then take an average of these predicted outputs as the final prediction. For bootstrap of Type 3, we also first predict by each local model but then use the highly robust median of these predicted output values to obtain
pointwise confidence intervals for the median and pointwise tolerance intervals.

Due to the resampling and the aggregation scheme, bootstrap can be useful to improve the stability and robustness, which intuitively means the ability to withstand perturbations and outliers, which are both common in many applications
and in routine data. We say an algorithm is stable if the output of the algorithm is not sensitive to the perturbation of a training dataset
[
1
,
14
,
27
,
20
]
. Pioneering stability analysis rigorously shows the improvement of stability by bootstraping
[
10
]
. However, their analysis treated multiple copies of a training example as a single example, which is not the choice in practice.
Furthermore, bootstrap is also useful to do inference such as building confidence intervals for some parameters of interest
[
7
]
. This inference is useful to understand how reliable the prediction is
[
4
,
12
]
.

In this paper, we study the algorithmic stability and robustness of bootstrap SGD.
Our main contributions are summarized as follows.

•

We study the
\ell_{1}
- and
\ell_{2}
-argument stability of bootstrap SGD applied to convex, smooth and Lipschitz problems. Our
\ell_{2}
-argument stability analysis shows how the bootstrap samples improve the generalization behavior. We consider bootstrap SGD of Type 1 and Type 2. For bootstrap SGD of Type 1, our analysis is able to handle multiple copies of a training example in a bootstrap sample, which was treated as a single example in the existing analysis
[
10
]
. We give the first stability analysis for bootstrap SGD of Type 2.

•

Using a well-known result from order statistics, one can
easily construct pointwise confidence intervals for the median and
pointwise tolerance intervals. From our point of view,
this enlarges the options of bootstrap SGD. Although we do not investigate
these intervals from a theoretical point of view, we show their usefulness
in a numerical example.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. We introduce the background in Section
2
. We study the stability of bootstrap SGD in Section
3
, and the distribution-free confidence intervals in Section
4
. We present numerical analysis to illustrate the behavior of bootstrap SGD in Section
5
, and conclude with a short discussion in Section
6
. The proofs are given in Section
7
.

Background

2.1
Algorithmic Stability

Let
\mathbb{P}
be a probability measure defined over a sample space

\mathcal{Z}=\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
, where
\mathcal{X}
is an input space and
\mathcal{Y}
is an output space.
Let
S=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})\in\mathcal{Z}^{n}
be a training dataset of size
n
, based on which we aim to build a function

h:\mathcal{X}\mapsto\mathcal{Y}
for prediction.
We assume the prediction function
h
is indexed by a parameter
\mathbf{w}\in\mathcal{W}
, where
\mathcal{W}
is a normed space and has the role of a parameter
space.
Let
\ell:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\mapsto\mathbb{R}_{+}
be a loss function, and we denote
\ell(h_{\mathbf{w}}(x),y)
the loss suffered by using
h_{\mathbf{w}}
to do prediction on
z=(x,y)
.
For brevity, we denote
f(\mathbf{w};z):=\ell(h_{\mathbf{w}}(x),y)
. The behavior of a model on training and testing is then measured by the empirical and population risk as follows

F_{S}(\mathbf{w}):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(\mathbf{w};z_{i}),\hskip 10.00002ptF(\mathbf{w}):=\mathbb{E}_{z}[f(\mathbf{w};z)],

where
\mathbb{E}_{z}[\cdot]
denotes the expectation with respect to (w.r.t.) the distribution of
z
.
We often apply a (randomized) learning algorithm
A
to (approximately) minimize the empirical risk, and we denote by
A(S)
the model derived by applying
A
to the dataset
S
.
We will assume in this paper – if not otherwise mentioned – that
\mathcal{W}
is a separable Hilbert space and that the
(measurable)
1

1

We will always assume that the learning algorithm
A
is measurable, i.e., for all

n\geq 1
, the map
(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})^{n}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
,
(S,x)\mapsto A(S)(x)
is measurable with respect to the universal completion of the product
\sigma
-algebra on
(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})^{n}\times\mathcal{X}
,
where
A(S)(\cdot)
denotes the decision function of the learning algorithm
A
.
learning algorithm maps into a separable Hilbert space. Examples are
of course
\mathbb{R}^{d}
or separable reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces.
The relative behavior of
A(S)
as compared to the best model
\mathbf{w}^{*}=\arg\min_{\mathbf{w}\in\mathcal{W}}\;F(\mathbf{w})
is referred to as the excess population risk
F(A(S))-F(\mathbf{w}^{*})
, which can be decomposed as follows

F(A(S))-F(\mathbf{w}^{*})=F(A(S))-F_{S}(A(S))+F_{S}(A(S))-F_{S}(\mathbf{w}^{*})+F_{S}(\mathbf{w}^{*})-F(\mathbf{w}^{*}).

We refer to the first term
F(A(S))-F_{S}(A(S))
and the third term
F_{S}(\mathbf{w}^{*})-F(\mathbf{w}^{*})
as the generalization gap, as they measure the difference between training and testing. We refer to the second term
F_{S}(A(S))-F_{S}(\mathbf{w}^{*})
as the optimization error as it measures the suboptimality of
A(S)
as measured by the training error. The optimization error is a central concept in optimization theory and has been extensively studied in the literature
[
24
]
. The generalization gap is a central concept in statistical learning theory, which is closely related to the stability
[
16
,
23
,
26
,
32
]
and robustness
[
3
,
15
,
31
,
29
]
of the learning algorithm. In this paper, we will leverage the algorithmic stability to study the stability of boostrap algorithms. We first introduce several popular stability concepts.
We denote
S\sim\widetilde{S}
if they are neighboring datasets, i.e.,
S
and
\widetilde{S}
differ by a single example. Let
\epsilon>0
and
\|\cdot\|_{2}
denote the
\ell_{2}
norm.

Definition 1

(Uniform stability
[
1
]
)
.

Let
A
be a (randomized) learning algorithm. We say
A
is uniformly stable with parameter
\epsilon
if
\sup_{S\sim\widetilde{S}}\sup_{z}\mathbb{E}_{A}[f(A(S);z)-f(A(\widetilde{S});z)]\leq\epsilon
, where
\mathbb{E}_{A}[\cdot]
denotes the expectation w.r.t. the distribution of the algorithm
A
.

Definition 2

(Argument stability)
.

Let
A
be a (randomized) learning algorithm. We say
A
is
\ell_{1}
-argument stable with parameter
\epsilon
if
\sup_{S\sim\widetilde{S}}\mathbb{E}_{A}[\|A(S)-A(\widetilde{S})\|_{2}]\leq\epsilon
. We say
A
is
\ell_{2}
-argument stability with parameter
\epsilon
if
\sup_{S\sim\widetilde{S}}\mathbb{E}_{A}[\|A(S)-A(\widetilde{S})\|_{2}^{2}]\leq\epsilon^{2}
.

It is clear that
\ell_{2}
-argument stability is stronger than
\ell_{1}
-argument stability, which further implies uniform stability if
f
is Lipschitz continuous.

Definition 3
.

Let
g:\mathcal{W}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
, where
\mathcal{W}
is a normed space. Let
L,G\geq 0
.

•

We say
g
is
G
-Lipschitz continuous if for any
\mathbf{w},\mathbf{w}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{W}
we have
|g(\mathbf{w})-g(\mathbf{w}^{\prime})|\leq G\|\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}^{\prime}\|_{2}
.

•

We say
g
is
L
-smooth if for any
\mathbf{w},\mathbf{w}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{W}
we have
\|\nabla g(\mathbf{w})-\nabla g(\mathbf{w}^{\prime})\|_{2}\leq L\|\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}^{\prime}\|_{2}
, where
\nabla
denotes the gradient operator.

•

We say
g
is convex if for any
\mathbf{w},\mathbf{w}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{W}
we have
g(\mathbf{w})\geq g(\mathbf{w}^{\prime})+\langle\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}^{\prime},\nabla g(\mathbf{w}^{\prime})\rangle
, where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the dot product.

Example 1
.

Let
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\mathcal{Y}\subset\mathbb{R}
, and
z=(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
.

(i)

The least squares loss function
f=f_{\text{LS}}
is defined by
f(\mathbf{w};z)=\frac{1}{2}(y-h_{\mathbf{w}}(x))^{2}
.

(ii)

The logistic loss function
f=f_{\text{c-logis}}
for binary classification is defined by

f(\mathbf{w};z)=\ln\bigl(1+\exp(-yh_{\mathbf{w}}(x))\bigr)
, where
\mathcal{Y}=\{-1,+1\}
.

(iii)

The logistic loss function
f=f_{\text{r-logis}}
for regression is defined by

f(\mathbf{w};z)=-\ln\frac{4e^{y-h_{\mathbf{w}}(x)}}{(1+e^{y-h_{\mathbf{w}}(x)})^{2}}

[
28
]
.

Basic computations show that

\displaystyle\nabla f_{\text{LS}}(\mathbf{w};z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(h_{\mathbf{w}}(x)-y)\nabla h_{\mathbf{w}}(x),

\displaystyle\nabla f_{\text{c-logis}}(\mathbf{w};z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{y}{1+\exp(yh_{\mathbf{w}}(x))}\nabla h_{\mathbf{w}}(x),

\displaystyle\nabla f_{\text{r-logis}}(\mathbf{w};z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tanh\bigl((h_{\mathbf{w}}(x)-y)/2\bigr)\nabla h_{\mathbf{w}}(x).

Note, that these three loss functions are convex with respect to
h_{\mathbf{w}}(x)
.
To clearly see the property of these loss functions, we consider a linear model
h_{\mathbf{w}}(x)=\mathbf{w}^{\top}x
and assume
\|x\|_{2}\leq 1
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
\mathbf{w}^{\top}
means the transpose of
\mathbf{w}
. In this case, it can be directly checked that all these loss functions are smooth. Furthermore, the logistic loss for both regression and binary classification is Lipschitz continuous. For example, it is clear that

\big\|\nabla f_{\text{c-logis}}(\mathbf{w};z)\big\|_{2}=\frac{|y|}{1+\exp(yh_{\mathbf{w}}(x))}\cdot\big\|\nabla h_{\mathbf{w}}(x)\big\|_{2}\leq\|x\|_{2}\leq 1.

However, the least square loss is not Lipschitz continuous since

\|\nabla f_{\text{LS}}(\mathbf{w};z)\|_{2}=|h_{\mathbf{w}}(x)-y|\cdot\|\nabla h_{\mathbf{w}}(x)\|_{2}=|\mathbf{w}^{\top}x-y|\cdot\|x\|_{2},

which goes to infinity as the norm of
\mathbf{w}
goes to infinity.

The following lemma shows the quantitative connection between stability and generalization.
The first part shows the connection between generalization and
\ell_{1}
-argument stability, while the second part shows the connection between generalization and
\ell_{2}
-argument stability.

Lemma 1

(Stability and Generalization
[
17
]
)
.

Let
A
be an algorithm. Let
G,L,\epsilon>0
.

•

Suppose
A
is
\ell_{1}
-argument stable with parameter
\epsilon
. If for any
z
, the map
\mathbf{w}\mapsto f(\mathbf{w},z)
is
G
-Lipschitz continuous, then
\mathbb{E}[F(A(S))-F_{S}(A(S))]\leq G\epsilon
.

•

Suppose
A
is
\ell_{2}
-argument stable with parameter
\epsilon
. If for any
z
, the map
\mathbf{w}\mapsto f(\mathbf{w},z)
is
L
-smooth, then

\mathbb{E}[F(A(S))-F_{S}(A(S))]\leq\frac{L\epsilon^{2}}{2}+\epsilon\big(2L\mathbb{E}[F_{S}(A(S))]\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

2.2
Bootstrap SGD

Bootstrap methods were introduced by
[
7
,
8
]
and can be used to estimate
standard errors of estimators in a nonparametric manner.
There exists a huge literature on bootstrap methods, see e.g.

[
9
]
and the references therein, and for specific bootstrap results for machine learning methods based on kernels we refer to
[
2
]
and

[
4
]
.
Bootstrapping can in particular be used
to construct distribution-free confidence intervals and distribution-free tolerance intervals.

Let
S=(\mathbf{z}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{z}_{n})
. Bootstrap methods first build
B\in\mathbb{N}
bootstrap samples by repeatedly drawing samples from
S
, each of which is of size
m
.
Let

I_{b}=(i_{b,1},\ldots,i_{b,m}),\hskip 10.00002ptb=1,\ldots,B,

be bootstrap indices which are drawn independently from the uniform distribution over
[n]=\{1,\ldots,n\}

with replacement
. Then, the
b
-th bootstrap sample is

S^{(b)}=(\mathbf{z}_{i_{b,1}},\ldots,\mathbf{z}_{i_{b,m}}).

(2.1)

Definition 4

(Bootstrap method)
.

Let
S^{(b)}
be defined as Eq. (
2.1
). Then, we apply an algorithm to
S^{(b)}
and get a model
\mathbf{w}^{(b)}
. The bootstrap method outputs one of the following models for final prediction:

•

Type 1:
h_{S}^{(1)}=h_{\bar{\mathbf{w}}}
, where
\bar{\mathbf{w}}=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\mathbf{w}^{(b)}
.

•

Type 2:
h_{S}^{(2)}=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}h_{\mathbf{w}^{(b)}}
.

•

Type 3:
h_{S}^{(3)}(x)=\mathrm{median}_{1\leq b\leq B}\{h_{\mathbf{w}^{(b)}}(x)\}
,
x\in\mathcal{X}
.

Definition 5

(Bootstrap SGD)
.

For the
b
-th data set, at the
t
-th iteration, bootstrap SGD first selects
j_{b,t}
from the uniform distribution over
[m]
and then update
\mathbf{w}_{t}^{(b)}
as follows

\mathbf{w}_{t+1}^{(b)}=\mathbf{w}_{t}^{(b)}-\eta_{t}\nabla f(\mathbf{w}_{t}^{(b)};\mathbf{z}_{i_{b,j_{b,t}}}),

where
(\eta_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of positive step sizes.
After
T
iterations, bootstrap SGD outputs
A(S)=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\mathbf{w}_{T+1}^{(b)}
.
If not otherwised mentioned, we use
\mathbf{w}_{1}^{(b)}=0
as the starting value.

3
Algorithmic Stability of Bootstrap SGD

3.1
Stability Analysis for Bootstrap SGD of Type 1

Let
\widetilde{S}=(\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{n})
be a neighbouring dataset of
S
, i.e.,
S
and
\widetilde{S}
only differ by a single example.
We will assume in this paper, if not mentioned otherwise, that the learning algorithm is
permutation invariant. Hence without loss of generality, we can assume that
S
and
\widetilde{S}
differ by the last example, i.e.,

\mathbf{z}_{i}=\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{i},\hskip 10.00002pt\text{if }i<n.

We assume
\mathbf{z}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{z}_{n},\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{n}
are independently drawn from the same distribution. The proofs of results in this subsection are given in Section
7.1
.

Lemma 2
.

Let
(\mathbf{w}_{t}^{(b)})
and
(\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{t}^{(b)})
be produced by bootstrap SGD based on
S
and
\widetilde{S}
, respectively. Assume the map
\mathbf{w}\mapsto f(\mathbf{w};\mathbf{z})
is convex and
L
-smooth. If
\eta_{t}\leq 1/L
, then

\big\|\mathbf{w}_{T+1}^{(b)}-\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{T+1}^{(b)}\big\|_{2}\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\eta_{t}\delta_{t,b}\mathbb{I}[i_{b,j_{b,t}}=n],

where we introduce

\delta_{t,b}=\|\nabla f(\mathbf{w}_{t}^{(b)};\mathbf{z}_{n})-\nabla f(\mathbf{w}_{t}^{(b)};\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{n})\|_{2}

and
\mathbb{I}[i_{b,j_{b,t}}=n]
is the indicator function, taking value
1
if
i_{b,j_{b,t}}=n
, and
0
otherwise.

As we will show in Lemma
10
,
\mathbb{E}_{A}[\mathbb{I}[i_{b,j_{b,t}}=n]]=\frac{1}{n}
. Therefore, we can apply Lemma
2
to get the following theorem on the stability of bootstrap SGD.

Theorem 3

(Stability bounds)
.

Let assumptions in Lemma
2
hold. Then

\mathbb{E}_{A}\big[\|A(S)-A(\widetilde{S})\|_{2}\big]\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\eta_{t}\mathbb{E}_{A}[\delta_{t,1}\mathbb{I}[i_{1,j_{1,t}}=n]]

and

\mathbb{E}_{A}\big[\|A(S)-A(\widetilde{S})\|_{2}^{2}\big]\leq\frac{2}{mB}\sum_{r=1}^{m}\frac{rC_{m}^{r}(n-1)^{m-r}}{n^{m}}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{A}\Big[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\eta_{t}^{2}\delta_{t,1}^{2}\big|\sum_{k=1}^{m}\mathbb{I}[i_{1,k}=n]=r\Big]+\\
\frac{2}{m^{2}B}\sum_{r=0}^{m}\frac{r^{2}C_{m}^{r}(n-1)^{m-r}}{n^{m}}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{A}\Big[\Big(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\eta_{t}\delta_{t,1}\Big)^{2}\big|\sum_{k=1}^{m}\mathbb{I}[i_{1,k}=n]=r\Big]+(1-\frac{1}{B})\Big(\mathbb{E}_{A}\Big[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\eta_{t}\delta_{t,1}\mathbb{I}[i_{1,j_{1,t}}=n]\Big]\Big)^{2}.

Remark 1
.

The hypothesis stability of bootstrap methods has been studied in
[
10
]
. Specifically, assume the base machine has hypothesis stability
\gamma_{k}
for learning with a dataset of size
k
and
m=n
. Then, the hypothesis stability parameter
\beta
of the bootstrap method satisfies
[
10
]

\beta\leq G\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{k\gamma_{k}}{n}\mathrm{Pr}\big\{d(I)=k\big\},

where
I=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n})
and
d(I)
denotes the number of distinct sampled points in one bootstrap sample. Here
i_{j},j\in[n]
follows the uniform distribution over
[n]
. However, the analysis there treats multiple copies of a training point as one point, which is not the case in practice. It is also indicated there how to extend their results to the case where multiple copies of a point are treated is an open question
[
10
]
. As a comparison, our analysis does not require this simplifying assumption and therefore provides an affirmative solution to their open question. It should be mentioned that the analysis in
[
10
]
considers bootstrap with a general base machine. As a comparison, this paper considers bootstrap with the base algorithm being SGD.

Corollary 4
.

Let assumptions in Lemma
2
hold. Assume that the map
\mathbf{w}\mapsto f(\mathbf{w};\mathbf{z})
is
G
-Lipschitz continuous. Then

\mathbb{E}_{A}\big[\|A(S)-A(\widetilde{S})\|_{2}\big]\leq\frac{2G^{2}}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\eta_{t}

and

\mathbb{E}_{A}\big[\|A(S)-A(\widetilde{S})\|_{2}^{2}\big]\leq\frac{8G^{2}}{Bn}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\eta_{t}^{2}+\frac{8G^{2}}{Bn}\Big(\frac{2}{n}+\frac{1}{m}\Big)\Big(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\eta_{t}\Big)^{2}+\frac{8G^{2}(B-1)}{n^{2}B}\Big(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\eta_{t}\Big)^{2}.

Remark 2
.

Since
m\leq n
, we get

\mathbb{E}_{A}\big[\|A(S)-A(\widetilde{S})\|_{2}^{2}\big]\lesssim\frac{G^{2}}{Bn}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\eta_{t}^{2}+G^{2}\Big(\frac{1}{Bmn}+\frac{1}{n^{2}}\Big)\Big(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\eta_{t}\Big)^{2},

where we denote
A\lesssim B
if there exists a universal constant
C
such that
A\leq CB
. We also denote
A\gtrsim B
if there exists a universal constant
C
such that
A\geq CB
. We denote
A\asymp B
if
A\lesssim B
and
A\gtrsim B
.

We can combine the above stability bounds and Lemma
1
to derive generalization bounds in Theorem
5
. We omit the proof for brevity.

Theorem 5

(Generalization bounds)
.

Let
A
be the bootstrap SGD with
T
iterations. Suppose for any
z
, the map
\mathbf{w}\mapsto f(\mathbf{w};z)
is convex,
G
-Lipschtiz continuous and
L
-smooth. Then

\mathbb{E}[F(A(S))-F_{S}(A(S))]\leq\frac{2G^{2}}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\eta_{t}

and

\mathbb{E}[F(A(S))-F_{S}(A(S))]\lesssim\frac{LG^{2}}{Bn}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\eta_{t}^{2}+\Big(\frac{LG^{2}}{n^{2}}+\frac{LG^{2}}{Bmn}\Big)\Big(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\eta_{t}\Big)^{2}+\\
G\big(L\mathbb{E}[F_{S}(A(S))]\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\Big(\frac{1}{Bn}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\eta_{t}^{2}+\Big(\frac{1}{n^{2}}+\frac{1}{Bmn}\Big)\Big(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\eta_{t}\Big)^{2}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(3.1)

Remark 3
.

If we choose
\eta_{t}\asymp 1/\sqrt{T}
and
T\asymp n
, then Eq. (
3.1
) further implies

\mathbb{E}[F(A(S))-F_{S}(A(S))]\lesssim\frac{LG^{2}}{n}+\frac{LG^{2}}{Bm}+G\big(L\mathbb{E}[F_{S}(A(S))]\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\Big(\frac{1}{n}+\frac{1}{Bm}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}},

which is a decreasing function of
Bm
. This shows that the bootstrap helps improve the generalization behavior of SGD, which is reasonable since sampling and averaging intuitively improves the stability of a learning algorithm. The above generalization bound also involves the empirical risk and therefore can benefit from a small training error. That is, if
\mathbb{E}[F_{S}(A(S))]
is very small, then the generalization bound can improve to the order of
\frac{LG^{2}}{n}+\frac{LG^{2}}{Bm}
.

Example 2

(Linear models)
.

Consider a linear model
h_{\mathbf{w}}(x)=\mathbf{w}^{\top}x
. Then, it is clear that

|h_{\mathbf{w}}(x)-h_{\mathbf{w}^{\prime}}(x)|=\big|(\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}^{\prime})^{\top}x\big|\leq\|\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}^{\prime}\|_{2}\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\|x\|_{2}.

Therefore, Eq. (
3.2
) holds with
G_{h}=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\|x\|_{2}
. It is clear that Eq. (
3.2
) also holds for kernel models with bounded kernels.

Example 3

(Shallow neural networks)
.

Consider a shallow neural network (SNN) with
N
nodes in the hidden layer

h_{\mathbf{W}}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{j}\sigma(\mathbf{w}_{j}^{\top}x),

where
\alpha_{j}\in\{-1/\sqrt{N},1/\sqrt{N}\}
are fixed,
\sigma:\mathbb{R}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
is an activation function and
\mathbf{W}=(\mathbf{w}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{w}_{N})
are trainable weights with
\mathbf{w}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. If
\sigma
is
G_{\sigma}
-Lipschitz continuous, then, for any
\mathbf{W},\mathbf{W}^{\prime}
, we have

\displaystyle\big|h_{\mathbf{W}}(x)-h_{\mathbf{W}^{\prime}}(x)\big|

\displaystyle=\Big|\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{j}\big(\sigma(x^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{j})-\sigma(x^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{j}^{\prime})\big)\Big|\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\big|\sigma(x^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{j})-\sigma(x^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{j}^{\prime})\big|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{G_{\sigma}}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\big|x^{\top}\big(\mathbf{w}_{j}-\mathbf{w}_{j}^{\prime}\big)\big|\leq\frac{G_{\sigma}\sup_{x}\|x\|_{2}}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\big\|\mathbf{w}_{j}-\mathbf{w}_{j}^{\prime}\big\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{G_{\sigma}\sup_{x}\|x\|_{2}}{\sqrt{N}}\sqrt{N}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\big\|\mathbf{w}_{j}-\mathbf{w}_{j}^{\prime}\big\|_{2}^{2}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=G_{\sigma}\sup_{x}\|x\|_{2}\|\mathbf{W}-\mathbf{W}^{\prime}\|_{2},

where
\|\cdot\|_{2}
denotes the Frobenius norm of a matrix.
Therefore, Eq. (
3.2
) holds for SNNs with
G_{h}=G_{\sigma}\sup_{x}\|x\|_{2}
. Although a SNN is not convex, it was shown that it is
\mu
-weakly convex with the weak convexity parameter
\mu
(the smallest eigenvalue of a Hessian matrix) being of the order of
1/\sqrt{N}

[
25
,
30
]
. Then, our stability analysis still applies for sufficiently large
N

[
18
,
25
]
.

As a corollary, we present the following generalization bounds. It shows that bootstrap SGD of Type 2 enjoys similar stability and generalization bounds of bootstrap SGD of Type 1.

Corollary 7

(Generalization bound)
.

Let assumptions in Theorem
6
hold. Assume
a\mapsto\ell(a,y)
is
G_{\ell}
-Lipschitz continuous for any
y
and
\mathbf{w}\mapsto f(\mathbf{w};z)
is
G
-Lipschitz continuous for any
z
. Then

\mathbb{E}_{A}[\ell(h_{S}^{(2)}(x),y)]-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{A}[\ell(h_{S}^{(2)}(x_{i}),y_{i})]\leq\frac{2GG_{h}G_{\ell}}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\eta_{t}.

(3.4)

Furthermore, if for any
y
, the function
a\mapsto\ell(a,y)
is
L_{\ell}
-smooth, then

\mathbb{E}_{A}[\ell(h_{S}^{(2)}(x),y)]-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{A}[\ell(h_{S}^{(2)}(x_{i}),y_{i})]\lesssim G_{\ell}GG_{h}\bigg(\frac{1}{Bn}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\eta_{t}^{2}+\Big(\frac{1}{Bmn}+\frac{1}{n^{2}}\Big)\Big(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\eta_{t}\Big)^{2}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
+\frac{G^{2}G_{h}^{2}L_{\ell}}{Bn}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\eta_{t}^{2}+G^{2}G_{h}^{2}L_{\ell}\Big(\frac{1}{Bmn}+\frac{1}{n^{2}}\Big)\Big(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\eta_{t}\Big)^{2}.

(3.5)

Remark 4
.

If we choose
\eta_{t}\asymp 1/\sqrt{T}
and
T\asymp n
, then Eq. (
3.4
) implies that

\mathbb{E}_{A}[\ell(h_{S}^{(2)}(x),y)]-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{A}[\ell(h_{S}^{(2)}(x_{i}),y_{i})]\lesssim\frac{GG_{h}G_{\ell}}{\sqrt{n}},

which, however, does not show the effect of the bootstrap. For the same step size and iteration number, Eq. (
3.5
) shows that

\mathbb{E}_{A}[\ell(h_{S}^{(2)}(x),y)]-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{A}[\ell(h_{S}^{(2)}(x_{i}),y_{i})]\lesssim G_{\ell}GG_{h}\Big(\frac{1}{Bm}+\frac{1}{n}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}+G^{2}G_{h}^{2}L_{\ell}\Big(\frac{1}{Bm}+\frac{1}{n}\Big).

This shows that increasing
B
would improve the generalization behavior of the output model.

In this section, we present experimental results to show the behavior of bootstrap SGD with different types.

6
Discussion

In this paper three methods to use the empirical bootstrap approach for
SGD to minimize the empirical risk over a separable Hilbert space were investigated from the view point of
algorithmic stability and statistical robustness.
In Type 1 and Type 2 one simply computes the average from the bootstrap
approximations which yield

h_{S}^{(1)}=h_{\bar{\mathbf{w}}}
, where

\bar{\mathbf{w}}=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\mathbf{w}_{T+1}^{(b)}
, and

h_{S}^{(2)}=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}h_{\mathbf{w}_{T+1}^{(b)}}
, respectively.
These two types of bootstrap SGD have the property
that the estimated functions
h_{S}^{(1)}
and

h_{S}^{(2)}
are both elements of the Hilbert space.
In Type 3, one computes the pointwise median of the bootstrap approximations
which yields

h_{S}^{(3)}(x)=\mathrm{median}_{1\leq b\leq B}\{h_{\mathbf{w}_{T+1}^{(b)}}(x)\}
,
x\in\mathcal{X}
. Our results show that these bootstrap SGD methods
have some desirable algorithmic stability and robustness properties if
the loss function has some easy to check properties, e.g. convexity, smoothness and Lipschitz continuity. In our toy example we demonstrated that it can even handle Cauchy distributed errors.

We presented generalization analysis for bootstrap SGD of Type 1 and Type 2 based on algorithmic stability. We considered both
\ell_{1}
- and
\ell_{2}
-argument stability. For SGD of Type 1, the
\ell_{1}
-argument stability does not show the effect of bootstrap.
The underlying reason is that the
\ell_{1}
-argument stability fails to incorporate the second-order information such as the variance.
As a comparison, the
\ell_{2}
-argument stability analysis yields stability bounds of the order of

\Big(\frac{1}{Bn}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\eta_{t}^{2}\Big)^{1/2}+\Big(\frac{1}{Bmn}+\frac{1}{n^{2}}\Big)^{1/2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\eta_{t}~.

This shows that the increasing the number of bootstrap samples improves the stability and generalization behavior of the output models. Our
\ell_{2}
-argument stability analysis exploits the variance reduction property by bootstrapping. The pioneering work
[
10
]
studied the hypothesis stability of bootstrap methods, which, however treated multiple copies of a training point as one point. It was posed as an open question there on how to handle multiple copies as different training points. Our stability analysis provides an affirmative solution to this open question. For bootstrap SGD of Type 2, we impose an additional assumption on the Lipschitz continuity of the prediction function, which holds for many models such as linear models and shallow neural networks. To our knowledge, this is the first stability analysis for bootstrap SGD of Type 2.

Bootstrap SGD of Type 3 allows the construction of pointwise
distribution-free

confidence and
distribution-free
tolerance intervals using a well-known fact
from order statistics. This type of bootstrap SGD has the property that the estimated median regression curve is not necessarily an element of the Hilbert space of functions which is used as the hypothesis space. Whether this property
may be considered as an advantage or as a disadvantage probably depends on the
specific application. E.g., if the Hilbert space only contains very smooth functions,
which is true if a classical Gaussian RBF kernel is used, but the Bayes function
is non-continuous, this property may be considered as an advantage.

It seems possible to enhance the robustness and stability properties of
bootstrap SGD even more. Although the random variables in each bootstrap sample are drawn in an independent and identically distributed manner from the empirical distribution computed from the training data set, it can happen that a few bootstrap samples are “untypical” in the sense
that the empirical distribution of the bootstrap sample differs a lot from the
empirical distribution of the training data set.
This can happen e.g. if just by chance some data points do occur very often in a specific bootstrap sample with index
b^{\prime}
.
In this case, it can happen that for this specific bootstrap sample the quantities
\mathbf{w}_{T+1}^{(b^{\prime})}
(or
h_{\mathbf{w}_{T+1}^{(b^{\prime})}}
) differ a lot from the
majority
of the other values of
\mathbf{w}_{T+1}^{(b)}
(or
h_{\mathbf{w}_{T+1}^{(b)}}
),
b\neq b^{\prime}
.
This phenomenon can have some undesired consequences in particular to
data sets with a small to moderate sample size
n
.
Then an empirical bootstrap estimate can be strongly influenced by this outlying value, if one uses the classical average of the values
\mathbf{w}_{T+1}^{(b)}
in Type 1 or of the values
h_{\mathbf{w}_{T+1}^{(b)}}
in Type 2.
For example, imagine that in the toy example given in Section
5
, the extreme
y
-value
1726.99
occurs in a bootstrap sample several times.
One idea to robustify the empirical
bootstrap SGD in this case is to replace the average by a more robust estimator.
One example is to use a one-step W-estimator, see
Hampel et al. [13, p. 116]
.
Let us briefly sketch this approach although it is beyond the scope of this paper.
First, we use SGD to compute
\mathbf{w}_{T+1}^{(tr)}
and
h_{\mathbf{w}_{T+1}}^{(tr)}

for the
training
data set.
Then a one-step W-estimator for bootstrap SGD of
\mathbf{w}
based on the bootstrap samples can be defined by

\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{OS}=\frac{\sum_{b=1}^{B}\mathbf{w}_{T+1}^{(b)}\cdot u_{1}(\mathbf{w}_{T+1}^{(b)}-\mathbf{w}_{T+1}^{(tr)})}{\sum_{b=1}^{B}u_{1}(\mathbf{w}_{T+1}^{(b)}-\mathbf{w}_{T+1}^{(tr)})},

(6.1)

where
u_{1}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to(0,1]
is an appropriately chosen positive weight function which downweights outlying quantities.
In a similar manner one can define a one-step W-estimator for bootstrap SGD of
h
by

\widehat{h}_{OS}=\frac{\sum_{b=1}^{B}h_{\mathbf{w}_{T+1}^{(b)}}\cdot u_{2}(h_{\mathbf{w}_{T+1}^{(b)}}-h_{\mathbf{w}_{T+1}^{(tr)}})}{\sum_{b=1}^{B}u_{2}(h_{\mathbf{w}_{T+1}^{(b)}}-h_{\mathbf{w}_{T+1}^{(tr)}})}.

(6.2)

This generalizes the bootstrap SGD estimators considered in this paper,
as is obvious from the special case that the functions
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
are equal to the constant function
1
.
A statistical analysis of such one-step W-estimators will probably
require a modification of the first inequality in (
7.2
).
Please note that these one-step W-estimators in (
6.1
) are different, but have a similar structure than the multivariate M-estimators investigated in
Maronna et al. [21, p. 184, (6.16)]
.

7.1
Proofs for Bootstrap SGD of Type 1

The following lemma establishes the non-expansiveness of gradient operator
\mathbf{w}\mapsto\mathbf{w}-\eta\nabla\ell(\mathbf{w})
, which was established by
Hardt et al. [14]
.

Lemma 8

(
[
14
]
)
.

Suppose the function
\ell
is convex and
L
-smooth. Then for all
\mathbf{w}_{1}
,
\mathbf{w}_{2}

\in\mathcal{W}
and
\eta\leq 1/L
, we have

\|\mathbf{w}_{1}-\eta\nabla\ell(\mathbf{w}_{1})-\mathbf{w}_{2}+\eta\nabla\ell(\mathbf{w}_{2})\|_{2}\leq\|\mathbf{w}_{1}-\mathbf{w}_{2}\|_{2}.

Proof of Lemma
2
.

According to Definition
5
, we know that

\mathbf{w}_{t+1}^{(b)}-\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{t+1}^{(b)}=\mathbf{w}_{t}^{(b)}-\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{t}^{(b)}-\eta_{t}\nabla f(\mathbf{w}_{t}^{(b)};\mathbf{z}_{i_{b,j_{b,t}}})+\eta_{t}f(\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{t}^{(b)};\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{i_{b,j_{b,t}}}).

We consider two cases. If
i_{b,j_{b,t}}\neq n
, then
\mathbf{z}_{i_{b,j_{b,t}}}=\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{i_{b,j_{b,t}}}
and therefore we can apply Lemma
8
to show that

\displaystyle\big\|\mathbf{w}_{t+1}^{(b)}-\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{t+1}^{(b)}\big\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\big\|\mathbf{w}_{t}^{(b)}-\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{t}^{(b)}-\eta_{t}\nabla f(\mathbf{w}_{t}^{(b)};\mathbf{z}_{i_{b,j_{b,t}}})+\eta_{t}f(\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{t}^{(b)};{\mathbf{z}}_{i_{b,j_{b,t}}})\big\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\big\|\mathbf{w}_{t}^{(b)}-\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{t}^{(b)}\big\|_{2}.

Otherwise, we know

\displaystyle\big\|\mathbf{w}_{t+1}^{(b)}-\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{t+1}^{(b)}\big\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\big\|\mathbf{w}_{t}^{(b)}-\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{t}^{(b)}-\eta_{t}\nabla f(\mathbf{w}_{t}^{(b)};\mathbf{z}_{n})+\eta_{t}f(\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{t}^{(b)};\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{n})\big\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\big\|\mathbf{w}_{t}^{(b)}-\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{t}^{(b)}\big\|_{2}+\eta_{t}\|\nabla f(\mathbf{w}_{t}^{(b)};\mathbf{z}_{n})-\nabla f(\mathbf{w}_{t}^{(b)};\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{n})\|_{2}.

We apply the above inequality recursively and derive the stated bound (note
\mathbf{w}_{1}^{(b)}=\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{1}^{(b)}

Lemma 9
.

Let
i_{1},\ldots,i_{m}
follow from the uniform distribution over
[n]
. Then for any
r\in[m]
we know

\mathrm{Pr}\Big\{\sum_{t=1}^{m}\mathbb{I}[i_{t}=n]=r\Big\}=\frac{C_{m}^{r}(n-1)^{m-r}}{n^{m}}.

Proof.

Since
i_{t}\in[n]
, the vector
(i_{1},\ldots,i_{m})
can take
n^{m}
different possible values. Furthermore, suppose
\sum_{t=1}^{m}\mathbb{I}[i_{t}=n]=r
. We can first consider all possible
\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{r}\}
such that
i_{j_{k}}=n
for all
k\in[r]
, and there are
C_{m}^{r}
choices. For each
k\not\in\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{r}\}
,
i_{k}
takes
n-1
possible values. Therefore, the number of outcomes with
\sum_{t=1}^{m}\mathbb{I}[i_{t}=n]=r
is
C_{m}^{r}(n-1)^{m-r}
.
The stated inequality then follows directly. The proof is completed.

Lemma 10
.

Let
i_{b,j_{b,t}}
be defined in Definition
5
. Then

\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{I}[i_{b,j_{b,t}}=n]]=\frac{1}{n}.

Proof.

We know

\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{I}[i_{b,j_{b,t}}=n]]=\mathrm{Pr}\big\{i_{b,j_{b,t}}=n\big\}=\sum_{r=0}^{m}\mathrm{Pr}\Big\{\sum_{k=1}^{m}\mathbb{I}[i_{b,k}=n]=r\Big\}\cdot\mathrm{Pr}\Big\{i_{b,j_{b,t}}=n\big|\sum_{k=1}^{m}\mathbb{I}[i_{b,k}=n]=r\Big\}.

It is clear that
\mathrm{Pr}\Big\{i_{b,j_{b,t}}=n\big|\sum_{k=1}^{m}\mathbb{I}[i_{b,k}=n]=r\Big\}=r/m
. Therefore, we can apply Lemma
9
to derive that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{I}[i_{b,j_{b,t}}=n]]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{mn^{m}}\sum_{r=1}^{m}rC_{m}^{r}(n-1)^{m-r}=\frac{1}{n^{m}}\sum_{r=1}^{m}C_{m-1}^{r-1}(n-1)^{m-r}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{m}}\sum_{r=0}^{m-1}C_{m-1}^{r}(n-1)^{m-r-1}=\frac{(1+n-1)^{m-1}}{n^{m}}=\frac{1}{n},

where we have used the following identity

C_{m}^{r}\cdot r=\frac{m!r}{r!(m-r)!}=\frac{(m-1)!m}{(r-1)!(m-r)!}=mC_{m-1}^{r-1}.

(7.1)

The proof is completed.

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{A}\big[\ell^{\prime}(h_{S}(x_{i}),y_{i})\big(h_{S_{i}}(x_{i})-h_{S}(x_{i})\big)\big]+\frac{L_{\ell}}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{A}\big[\big(h_{S_{i}}(x_{i})-h_{S}(x_{i})\big)^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{A}\Big(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\ell^{\prime}(h_{S}(x_{i}),y_{i}))^{2}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\Big(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(h_{S_{i}}(x_{i})-h_{S}(x_{i})\big)^{2}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}+\frac{L_{\ell}}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{A}\big[\big(h_{S_{i}}(x_{i})-h_{S}(x_{i})\big)^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{A}[(\ell^{\prime}(h_{S}(x_{i}),y_{i}))^{2}]\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\Big(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{A}\big[\big(h_{S_{i}}(x_{i})-h_{S}(x_{i})\big)^{2}\big]\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}+\frac{L_{\ell}}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{A}\big[\big(h_{S_{i}}(x_{i})-h_{S}(x_{i})\big)^{2}\big],

where we have used the Cauchy’s inequality. The stated bound follows directly.
∎

Proof of Corollary
7

\mathbb{E}_{A}[\ell(h_{S}^{(2)}(x),y)]-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{A}[\ell(h_{S}^{(2)}(x_{i}),y_{i})]\leq\\
