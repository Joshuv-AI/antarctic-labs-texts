Substance P and somatostatin neurons limit facial itch by recruiting distinct nociceptive circuits in the brainstem
Authors: Joshua J Wheeler, Nidha Williams, Aditi Vigneshwar, B Duncan X Lascelles, Thierry Olivry, Santosh K Mishra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractItch is a complex noxious sensation associated with many skin and systemic conditions, which varies in intensity and quality across different body regions. Despite its prevalence, the molecular and cellular mechanisms underlying regional itch differences remain poorly understood. Investigating the neural basis of regional itch differences, we identified a functional divergence in neuropeptide signaling and central circuit engagement between the trigeminal and spinal systems, which was independent of peripheral innervation density. Utilizing a combination of behavioral, pharmacological, genetic, and molecular assays, we identified a unique population of trigeminal (TG) neurons that facilitate specialized itch-pain coding. Our results indicate that while histamine receptors HRH1 and HRH3 are both involved in mediating mixed itch-and-pain sensations, the specific activity of Substance P (SP)- and Somatostatin (SST)-expressing neurons orchestrates this transition in the cheek. This behavioral shift is mediated by a central mechanism wherein sensory neurons activation recruits distinct nociceptive circuits within the brainstem. In brief, these findings provide insights into the molecular and cellular mechanisms underlying regional itch differences, highlighting the importance of considering anatomical location when developing targeted treatments.Subject terms: Molecular neuroscience, Somatic system

Introduction

Somatosensory neurons in the dorsal root ganglia (DRG) and trigeminal ganglia (TG) detect various stimuli, generating distinct sensations and responses. DRG neurons innervate the skin of the limbs and torso, whereas TG neurons innervate unique sensory areas in the head and neck, including the eyes, nose, mouth, meninges, and vibrissae. Reports have identified genes linked to somatosensory neurons’ detection specificity in DRG and TG. DRG RNA-sequencing reveals distinct expression profiles for proprioceptive and itch-responsive neurons predominantly in mice1,2 and humans3–5. However, TG neuronal populations differ from DRG, as they lack proprioceptive neurons and innervate several unique sensory environments: the eyes, nasal passages and sinuses, meninges, and, in animals, vibrissae. Recent research has found TG-specific differences at the transcription level4,6,7, but whether that drives a differential behavioral response or differences in itch circuitry in mice is unknown.

In humans, the severity of itch is perceived differently across body regions, with the face and torso8–10 exhibiting distinct intensity thresholds. Animal models have recapitulated these phenomena, showing fewer scratching bouts in response to intradermal pruritogen injections in the cheek than dorsal nape11,12. This difference is consistent across common pruritogens11–19, including histamine11,12. Notably, facial injection of pruritogens and algogens activates a partially overlapping population of interneurons found in the spinal trigeminal nucleus17. In contrast, DRG itch and pain signals primarily activate separate populations of interneurons3,19,20. The distinct neural coding in the TG versus the DRG suggests the involvement of specific neuropeptides and signaling pathways involved in transmitting itch signals from the face versus the body. While itch sensations are critically propagated by brain natriuretic peptide (BNP)/somatostatin-expressing neurons (NP3), they are also transduced by distinct populations of MrgprD-expressing (NP1) and MrgprA3-expressing (NP2) pruriceptors, which respond to beta3-alanine and chloroquine, respectively20,21. Activation of these sensory neurons triggers the release of neuropeptides such as SP and BNP. In the spinal dorsal horn, these neuropeptides activate specific interneuron circuits to process these pain and itch sensations19–22. However, whether the trigeminal system utilizes these same labeled lines or employs a more integrated signaling strategy to process facial sensations remains an area of active investigation.

Here, we investigated the mechanisms underlying regional differences in pruriceptive processing between the face and the body using a multifaceted approach combining mouse genetics with behavioral, pharmacological, and molecular techniques. We first identify the behavioral divergence between the dorsal nape and cheek in response to histaminergic and non-histaminergic pruritogens. Next, we utilized local pharmacological antagonism and mast cell ablation to isolate the role of peripheral sensory neurons, alongside experiments in mice lacking B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) or Substance P (SP). These findings are corroborated by transcriptional activation data showing distinct recruitment in the TG compared to the DRG. Furthermore, we investigated the central circuits involved by mapping the activation of TacR1-expressing and NPR1-expressing interneurons within the trigeminal nucleus caudalis (TNC or Sp5C) via immediate early genes (Egr1 or cFos) expression. By comparing molecular profiles in human tissues, we validated the translational relevance and conservation of these mechanisms in the human sensory system. Collectively, these studies identify a mandatory synergy between histamine receptor subtypes and neuropeptidergic signaling that distinguishes facial itch from that of the body.

Results

Regional scratching responses differ between the face and neck

We initially performed a literature review (Supplemental Data) of papers published between January 2009 and June 2023 (Fig. 1B, C) which revealed significant differences in scratching bouts when normalized for time between these two regions in response to various pruritogens, including for histamine (*p < 0.0001), chloroquine (*p = 0.0008), and SLIGRL-NH2, a PAR-2 specific agonist (*p = 0.0006) (Fig. 1B). These data were further confirmed when we combined all scratching bouts/minute values for all pruritogens (*p = 0.0073) (Fig. 1C). Based on this systematic review, we sought to compare the behavior responses of these two regions to confirm these results using two common pruritogens: histamine11 and chloroquine14.

To investigate the regional scratching response between the face and neck, we comprehensively analyzed scratching behavior in C57Bl6/J mice following intradermal injections of pruritogens into the dorsal nape and cheek. Histamine elicited significantly fewer scratch bouts of ipsilateral cheek-directed scratching than dorsal nape-directed scratching bouts (*p = 0.0040) (Fig. 1D) in C57Bl6/J mice injected with histamine. In contrast, we did not find any significant differences when comparing the total amount of time spent scratching directed at these areas (*p = 0.2947) (Fig. 1E), and no significant differences were seen in the average bout duration (*p = 0.2540) (Fig. 1F). The time course of histamine-induced scratching bouts differed significantly between the dorsal nape and cheek (Fig. 1G). Ipsilateral cheek-directed scratching bouts peaked within the first 5 min (*p < 0.0001) (Fig. 1G) and the number of dorsal nape-directed scratching bouts peaked at 15 min (*p = 0.0001) (Fig. 1G). We found similar results using chloroquine (CQ) in C57Bl6/J mice. In C57Bl6/J mice, CQ elicited significantly fewer scratching bouts in the ipsilateral cheek as compared to the dorsal nape (*p = 0.0332) (Fig. 1L) and significantly reduced total scratching times (*p = 0.0164) (Fig. 1M). As with histamine, the average scratch bout duration did not change between the two injection regions following i.d. CQ (Fig. 1N). Unlike histamine, CQ only elicited significantly more scratching bouts in the dorsal nape 15 min after injection (*p = 0.0025) (Fig. 1O).

To confirm that our findings in C57Bl6/J mice were not strain-specific, we repeated the experiments using CD-1 mice, a commonly used itch research outbred mouse line (Fig. 1H–K)23. Our histamine (i.d) results showed a similar pattern: fewer ipsilateral cheek-directed scratching bouts (*p < 0.0001); however, we found significantly shorter scratching times than dorsal nape-directed scratching bouts (*p < 0.0001) (Fig. 1H, I). Unlike with C57BL/6J mice, bout duration significantly differed between regions in the CD-1 mice (*p = 0.0380) (Fig. 1J). The time course of histamine-induced scratching bouts differed between regions, with cheek-directed scratching peaking within 5 min, and significant differences were observed at 15 (*p = 0.0002), 20 (*p = 0.0477), and 30 min (*p = 0.0009) (Fig. 1K). We also injected CQ (i.d.) into the dorsal nape and cheek of CD-1 mice to again confirm that these results were not strain-specific. As expected, CD-1 mice performed significantly fewer scratching bouts directed at the ipsilateral cheek as compared to the dorsal nape following CQ injection (*p = 0.0013) (Fig. 1P), which then resulted in these mice spending significantly less time scratching the ipsilateral cheek overall (*p = 0.0007) (Fig. 1Q). Unlike histamine, CQ injection did not cause the average bout duration to change between the two injection regions in CD-1 mice (Fig. 1R). Finally, CQ injection into the nape caused significantly more bouts of scratching immediately following injection and continuing for 15 min (*p < 0.0001 [0–5 min], *p = 0.0043[5–10 min], and *p = 0.0014 [10–15 min]) (Fig. 1S). Overall, our data suggests that regional differences in itch are conserved across outbred and inbred mouse lines, with the cheek having a significantly reduced number of histamine- and chloroquine-induced scratching bouts compared to the dorsal nape, indicating that the face is less responsive to histamine- and CQ-induced itch.

Regional itch variations are not explained by peripheral innervation densities

Given the difference in histamine-induced itch between the dorsal nape and cheek, we hypothesized that there may be a difference in innervation density of itch-sensing neurons between the facial dermatomes innervated by the TG24 and the dorsal nape dermatomes innervated by the DRG25. To assess this, we generated SST-cre::Ai9 mice in which tdTomato was conditionally expressed in SST-expressing neurons. We chose to generate SST-cre::Ai9 mice since previous work has demonstrated that SST-expressing DRG sensory neurons are one sensory neuron population, the other being MrgprA318, responsible for mediating itch signals induced by histamine20,26. We found that the skin of the cheek has a significantly higher degree of innervation by SST-cre::Ai9 neurons as compared to the dorsal nape (*p = 0.0158) (Fig. 2A–C). As an additional control, we also stained these sections for PGP9.5, a pan-neuronal marker for cutaneous innervation27 and found a significant increase in overall innervation in the cheek as compared to the dorsal nape (*p = 0.0132) (Fig. 2A–D). Due to the increased innervation density of SST-cre::Ai9 afferents in the cheek, we hypothesized that there was an increase in the number of SST-expressing neurons in the TG. As expected, we found significantly more SST-expressing neurons in the TG as compared to the DRG (*p = 0.0029) (Fig. 2E–G). This increase is likely to be responsible for increased SST-positive afferent density between the dorsal nape and the cheek. Our findings demonstrate that the observed reduction in scratching in the cheek is not correlated with differences in overall innervation density or the density of itch-sensing neurons.

Distinct role of SP in modulating itch response in the cheek and dorsal nape

Since innervation density did not explain the regional differences in itch response, we hypothesized that pain-associated neuropeptides, specifically Substance P (SP)28, might modulate ipsilateral cheek-directed scratching behavior. Based on previous observations, we found that in the dorsal nape, all SST-expressing DRG sensory neurons are positive for BNP20. Further, these BNP-expressing DRG sensory neurons are not positive for Substance P (SP)21, a canonical pain-associated neuropeptide28. While the co-expression of Substance P (SP) and Brain Natriuretic Peptide (BNP) has been shown to be mutually exclusive in the DRG21, their co-expression status in the TG remains uncharacterized. To test if SP modulated itch responses in the cheek, we used knockout (KO) mice lacking SP or BNP and quantified scratching bouts after histamine injection into both the dorsal nape and the cheek (Fig. 3). We found that SP-KO mice exhibited significantly more ipsilateral cheek-directed scratching bouts than wild-type controls (*p = 0.0007) (Fig. 3A), with no differences in dorsal nape-directed scratching bouts (*p = 0.2390) (Fig. 3B). We used BNP-KO mice as a control, as these mice should lack a scratching behavior phenotype, and demonstrated that the loss of BNP resulted in a significant decrease in the total number of dorsal nape (*p = 0.0126) and ipsilateral cheek-directed (*p = 0.0338) scratching bouts (Fig. 3E, F). To confirm our histamine-induced scratching behavior results in SP-KO and BNP-KO mice, we pharmacologically inhibited the SP receptor (TacR1) with L-733,06029,30. We found that these mice exhibited significantly higher amounts of ipsilateral cheek-directed scratching bouts than vehicle-treated mice (*p = 0.0198) (Fig. 3C). Like the SP-KO mice, L-733,060 treated mice had no significant differences in dorsal nape-directed scratching bout compared to vehicle-treated mice (*p = 0.3568) (Fig. 3D). Mice treated with the NPR1 antagonist JS-1131 experienced significantly fewer scratching bouts in both the ipsilateral cheek (*p = 0.0338) and the dorsal nape (*p = 0.0396) (Fig. 3G, H). These findings suggest that SP plays distinct roles in modulating itch response in the cheek and dorsal nape, with SP inhibiting and BNP promoting itch in the cheek.

While our global knockout data suggested that SP is required for this itch-pain response, we sought to determine whether this effect was mediated by peripheral skin cells or central neural circuits. To rule out the role of the skin in transducing this itch, we performed two targeted experiments focusing on local receptors and mast cells. First, we locally co-inject L-733,060 with histamine into the two regions of interest (Fig. 3D, E). We did not find any significant changes in scratching behavior with local co-injection of L-733,060. In a second set of experiments, we generated MCPT5-cre::ROSADTA mice (Cre+/DTA+) to ablate cutaneous mast cells. Mice with ablated mast cells did not exhibit any significant differences in scratching behavior in the cheek or nape (Fig. 3J, K) when compared to littermate control mice (Cre-/DTA+). This provides strong evidence for cellular and spatial specificity, indicating that the requirement for SP in this model is not mediated by peripheral itch-sensitive cells but rather by sensory release of neuropeptide to the downstream in the CNS for generating sensation.

Histamine receptor-specific itch induction

To investigate which histamine receptors mediate histamine-induced itch in these regions, we built on our previous findings on regional differences in itch between the cheek and nape. We focused on the four histamine receptors, three of which are expressed in DRG sensory neurons. scRNA-sequencing data showed that HRH1 mRNA is expressed predominantly in SST- and MrgprA3-positive DRG sensory neurons involved in itch, while HRH3 is expressed in SP-positive DRG sensory neurons involved in pain2. To determine which histamine receptor(s) are responsible for transducing histamine-induced itch, we injected 2-pyridylethylamine (HRH1-specific agonist)32 and immethridine (HRH3-specific agonist)33 into the dorsal nape and cheek of mice. We found that 2-pyridylethylamine induced HRH1-mediated itch in both the dorsal nape and ipsilateral cheek, with no significant differences in scratching bouts compared to histamine-injected mice (*p = 0.5208, nape; *p = 0.7708, cheek) (Fig. 4B, C). We found that while both 2-Pyr and histamine induced cheek-directed scratching, 2-Pyr elicited significantly more pain-related cheek wipes than histamine (*p = 0.0230) (Fig. 4D). Immethridine also induced more ipsilateral cheek wipes (*p = 0.136) (Fig. 4D), but fewer scratching bouts than histamine (*p < 0.0001, nape; *p = 0.0264, cheek) in both regions. Immethridine induced fewer scratching bouts than 2-pyridylethylamine in the nape (*p = 0.0002, nape), but not the cheek (*p = 0.2373). Lastly, to determine which nocifensive response is mediated by which histamine receptor, we used the HRH1 antagonist, diphenhydramine, and the HRH3 antagonist, JNJ-5207852, to specifically block these receptors in mice that then had histamine injected into the cheek. We found that blocking either HRH1 or HRH3 abrogated both itch (scratching) (*p = 0.0031 [diphenhydramine], *p = 0.0003 [JNJ-5207852]) and pain (wiping) (*p = 0.0001 [diphenhydramine], *p = 0.0003 [JNJ-5207852]) responses (Fig. 4C, D). While H1R and H3R have been traditionally associated with itch and pain, respectively, our data suggests a mandatory synergistic role for both receptors in mediating these behaviors.

Regional variations in sensory neuron populations underlying itch and pain

Our previous findings on regional differences in itch and SST/SP innervation led us to investigate the populations of SST-, SP-, MrgprA3-, HRH1-, and HRH3-expressing sensory neurons in the dorsal root ganglion (DRG) and trigeminal ganglion (TG) of mice, which we also further validated in humans. Our SST-cre::Ai9 innervation results indicated that there should be a larger proportion of SST-positive TG sensory neurons. Additionally, our behavioral results indirectly indicate that SP-positive TG sensory neurons are important in modulating ipsilateral cheek itch. Since pain signaling is capable of modulating itch sensation and vice versa19,20, we hypothesized that there is a change in the number of TG sensory neurons that are positive for both SST and SP. We found that overall, there was a significant increase in the percentage of TG sensory neurons positive for both SST and SP as compared to the DRG (*p = 0.0012) (Fig. 5A–E). Since itch is predominantly perceived to occur on the skin, we used the neuronal tracer wheat germ agglutinin (WGA-350) to determine if this increase in TG neurons co-expressing SST and SP occurred in skin-innervating TG neurons. We found a larger population of cheek skin-innervating TG sensory neurons positive for both SST and SP compared to dorsal nape-innervating DRG sensory neurons (*p = 0.0051) (Fig. 5F–H), indicating that this population is important in modulating itch transmission in the TG.

Since we found an increase in skin-innervating, SST-positive neurons that express SP, we next investigated the expression of HRH1, HRH3, and MrgprA3 in skin-innervating sensory neurons in mice (Fig. 6). Of the skin-innervating DRG neurons, only 3% co-expressed HRH1 with SP (Fig. 6A, G). In the TG, the percentage of skin-innervating neurons co-expressing HRH1 and SP increased to 5.2% (Fig. 6B, G). We found a similar trend with skin-innervating neurons co-expressing HRH3 and SP: 8.5% of skin-innervating DRG neurons co-expressed HRH3 and SP, while in skin-innervating TG neurons, this percentage increased to 22.6% (Fig. 6C, D, H). Finally, a similar trend was found for MrgprA3: 2.3% of skin-innervating DRG neurons co-expressed MrgprA3 and SP, while this percentage increased to 16.9% of skin-innervating TG neurons (Fig. 6E, F, I). Also, we found that roughly half of skin-innervating DRG neurons expressing MrgprA3 also expressed SST, and that nearly all skin-innervating TG neurons expressing MrgprA3 also expressed SST (Fig. 6I). To assess the translational relevance of our findings, we performed ISH in human DRG and TG for HRH1, HRH3, SST, and SP. First, we determined that there is an increase in the percentage of TG neurons co-expressing SST and SP (Fig. 7A–E), like what is seen in mice. We also stained human DRG and TG for HRH1 and HRH3 in addition to SST and SP, and found that these populations also underwent similar changes in co-expression in the TG as compared to the DRG (Fig. 7F–M).

To examine why histamine-induced sensory activation results in less scratching behavior in the cheek compared to the nape of the neck, we integrated ISH with functional ELISA assays to evaluate differential neuropeptide release/distinct peripheral and central neuronal circuit engagement. At the peripheral level, we observed that histamine induced significantly higher expression of early growth response protein 1 (EGR1) with SP (Fig. 8A–C) and also EGR1+/SST+/SP+ (6–7-fold changes) in TG compared to DRG. Functionally, we found that primary cultured TG sensory neurons release significantly more SP than primary cultured DRG sensory neurons following incubation with histamine (*p = 0.0265) (Fig. 8G). At the central level, TNC has a significantly larger population of neurons expressing TacR1 (receptor for SP), Npr1 (receptor for BNP), and the immediate early gene cFos as compared to the spinal dorsal horn (Fig. 8D–F). Interestingly, we found the 50% reduction of Npr1+/cFos+ in the TNC compared to the spinal dorsal horn. These results suggest that the trigeminal system transforms an itch signal into a nociceptive signal through this Npr1/TacR1 convergence at the higher center.

Discussion

Our findings suggest a regional variation in itch response between the cheek and dorsal nape (Figs. 1 and 8G). These differences do not appear to be explained by variations in innervation density (Fig. 2) but may instead relate to distinct populations of sensory neurons expressing specific neuropeptides and receptors (Figs. 3 and 4). The divergence in facial histaminergic and chloroquine-dependent itch processing appears to be associated with the specific proportion of TG neurons co-expressing Substance P (SP) with Sst, MrgprA3, Hrh1, and Hrh3 (Figs. 5–7) and higher expression of Egr1/Sst/Sp as compared to the DRG. Furthermore, our pharmacological data (Fig. 4) point toward HRH3 as a potential transducer of histamine-induced pain in addition to itch. These histological patterns were also observed in human DRG and TG samples (Fig. 7), suggesting translational relevance. At the circuit level, histamine activation of receptors expressed within MrgprA3-positive neurons likely contributes to nocifensive wiping in the cheek, conclusions supported by pharmacological data (Fig. 4) and SP release in the TG compared to DRG (Fig. 8G). Additionally, a higher percentage of co-expression of cFos in Npr1+ and TacR1+ cells in TG compared to DRG provides evidence that intradermal histamine recruits SP-expressing TG sensory neurons, which may trigger central SP release and activate Npr1+/TacR1+ expressing TNC interneurons (Fig. 8D–F). Our study indicates that facial itch processing is molecularly and behaviorally distinct from the body; while body itch (DRG) has been modeled on a relatively segregated population, the TG may utilize a more integrated system where SP-mediated synergy plays a role in the itch-pain switch.

We found a distinct neuropeptide signature in the TG that supports our claim of regional specificity. Our data show that, unlike the DRG, a significantly higher percentage of itch-related neurons expressing HRH1 or MrgprA3 in the TG co-express both SST and Substance P (SP) (Figs. 5 and 6). This finding is crucial because it provides molecular evidence for a more complex peripheral coding system in the TG. While it’s well-established that glutamate is the major neurotransmitter for all primary sensory neurons34, it’s the co-expressed neuropeptides that provide a contextual code, or label, for the central nervous system to interpret the signal as itch or pain20,21,28. This dual neuropeptide expression in the TG provides a basis for the different behavioral outcomes we observed in the face, which is consistent with the report that low-dose capsaicin provokes scratching, whereas higher doses induce wiping16,19.

This unique molecular signature in the TG suggests a circuit that can process both itch and pain signals. First, we demonstrate significantly increased expression of SST+/SP+ neurons, which also express EGR1, a marker of DRG and TG neuronal activation (Fig. 8). Further, we demonstrate with ELISA that primary culture TG releases significantly more SP when exposed to histamine (Fig. 8G). We also demonstrate that intradermal injection of histamine triggers induction of cFos in Npr1+ and TacR1+ TNC interneurons, bolstering our hypothesis that our SST+/SP+TG neurons, upon reaching the TNC, engage with central circuits in a way that is distinct from the DRG to dorsal horn. We propose a circuit in which these TG neurons act as the initial signal, differentially engaging downstream interneurons to transmit either an itch or a pain signal. While the Nppb-GRP-GRPR signaling axis is well-characterized in the spinal cord, our data suggests a parallel mechanism within the trigeminal system for cheek-directed itch. The finding that GRPR-expressing neurons, the downstream targets of the NPR1-excitatory interneuron relay, can function as projection neurons35,36 is particularly relevant to the TNC, suggesting that the H1/H3-mediated synergistic signal originates in the TG and is processed through a TNC gate, where GRPR+ projection neurons may then relay the integrated itch-pain information directly to higher-order centers like the parabrachial nucleus.

An unexpected finding was the presence of a population of NPR1+/TacR1+ interneurons, which exists in the TNC but is not found in the spinal dorsal horn, and which are activated following intradermal histamine administration (Fig. 8D–F). How these interneurons might differentiate between itch versus pain sensations is an area that requires further investigation, especially given that there is evidence of TacR1+ neurons functioning as spinal projection neurons required for transmitting itch sensations from the spinal dorsal horn to the parabrachial nucleus35,37 and contain another subset that mediate pain, as is seen in the spinal dorsal horn38.

Our findings on the co-expression of Substance P with other itch-related markers warrant a more nuanced discussion of its role. While we initially focused on the association of SP with pain, it is now clear from a substantial body of literature, including the work by Azimi et al., that SP also plays a significant role in itch signaling, particularly through its actions on Mas-related G protein-coupled receptors on peripheral skin mast cells and sensory neurons39. Our finding that a high percentage of HRH1-positive and HRH3-positive neurons co-express SP suggests that SP may not only transmit pain signals but may also contribute to the switch from itch to pain, possibly by activating a separate pain circuit. The finding that the HRH3-specific agonist, immethridine, induced wiping rather than scratching further supports this idea. This histamine-triggered SP release (Fig. 8F) may activate a previously identified monosynaptic facial pain circuit40,41, accounting for the increase in pain following intradermal histamine injection.

A major limitation in this study is that we used global knockout mice and pharmacological inhibition. Since SP and SST are expressed in other neuronal tissues, removing these genes globally does mean that our results could be due, in part or entirely, to the loss of SP or SST in the spinal cord, spinal trigeminal nucleus, or brain. Similarly, our TacR1 antagonist, L-733,060, has been demonstrated to cross the blood–brain barrier29, meaning that our change in itch behavior could be due to antagonism of TacR1 in the spinal cord, spinal trigeminal nucleus, or brain. JS-11 also has limited ability to cross the blood–brain barrier31, meaning that our reductions in itch in JS-11-treated mice could be due to antagonism of NPR1 in the brain, as opposed to antagonism of NPR1 in the spinal cord and trigeminal nucleus.

We demonstrated a correlation between the unique neuropeptide signature and behavioral outcomes. Future work using cell-specific targeting and optogenetic techniques will be essential to establish causality and to fully elucidate the complete nociceptive pathway. This future research should also investigate the specific types of central neurons our identified TG neurons connect with, specifically addressing whether they synapse with labeled line or polymodal projection neurons, a key point highlighted by Moser and Giessler in their work on the nucleus caudalis42.

Next, our findings on the distinct expression of facial itch may have significant clinical implications for conditions that disproportionately affect the head and face. The unique neuropeptide signature we identified in the trigeminal ganglia (TG), particularly the co-expression of Substance P (SP) and Somatostatin (SST), suggests that the neural pathways for facial itch (Fig. 8H) are fundamentally different from those in the body and may explain conditions like trigeminal neuralgia, shingles, and facial atopic dermatitis are often resistant to standard treatments and are characterized by a mixture of both itch and pain.

In summary, our study highlights the importance of regional differences in sensory processing by identifying distinct neuron subpopulations in the TG (Fig. 8H). Our findings provide a critical foundation and a clear rationale for future studies that can directly investigate the central circuits and their synaptic plasticity in the TNC, with significant implications for understanding and treating itch disorders of the face.

Materials and methods

Systematic review

To perform our systematic review of itch literature to determine if other researchers had reported differences in the amount of scratching seen in the dorsal nape and cheek, PubMed was searched from 2007 through 2022 with the key words “ITCH” or “PRURITUS” and “NAPE”, “DORSAL NAPE”, or “CHEEK”. Also included were the terms “HISTAMINE”, “CHLOROQUINE”, “SLIGHRL-NH2” or “PAR-2”, “SEROTONIN”, “AYPGKF-NH2” or “PAR-1”, “IL-31” or “INTERLEUKIN 31”. Cathepsin E data is from an article that used another pruritogen as a control. Articles not perform research in mice were excluded from analysis. Articles were only included if they performed intradermal (i.d.) injections. In articles with multiple doses, the highest dose was used. Additionally, in articles using wild-type and knockout mice, data from only wild-type mice were included in our analysis. Dosing was generally consistent across articles: histamine, serotonin, SLIGRL-NH2, AYPGKF-NH2, and chloroquine used 90–120 μg per injection. IL-31 used 30 μg for dosing. Since there is no set duration for studying scratching behavior, papers found scored scratching behavior for any duration between 15 min up to 60 min; therefore, the number of scratching bouts was normalized to the “# scratching bouts/min” for comparison. This was achieved by determining the length of the y-axis in pixels and the length in pixels of the mean value of the bar or the distance between the x-axis and the centroid point in line graphs in these manuscripts. The y-axis in all articles found corresponds to the number of scratching bouts counted during those references’ recording period. To calculate the “# scratching bouts/min”, the pixel value height of the bar graph or distance from the x-axis was divided by the total length of the y-axis.

L-733,060 (Catalog # 1145), 2-Pyridylethylamine (Catalog # 2478), and immethridine (Catalog # 2315) were ordered from Tocris. Scratching behavior

Histamine injection: All mice received intradermal injections of 100 µg/20 µL histamine in sterile 1× phosphate-buffered saline (1× PBS). This concentration and volume were used for all intradermal injections. Immethridine and 2-Pyridylethylamine were intradermally injected with the same 100 µg/20 µL concentration. Mice were injected in either Dorsal Nape11 or Cheek13. L-733,060 was dissolved in sterile 1× PBS, and 50 µL of a 1 mg/kg solution was administered intravenously 30 min prior to histamine injection29,30. JS-11 was dissolved in DMSO, and the injection solution consisted of 10% Tween-80, 20% DMSO, and 70% Normal saline. A total of 16.3 µg/50 µL of JS-11 was administered intraperitoneally 30 min prior to histamine injection31. The same mice were used for both the Cheek and Nape tests. For drug administration injections, mice were randomly assigned to receive Vehicle, L-733-060, orJS-11. Mice were then randomized to the injection site. After a 10-day washout period, mice were treated with vehicles, then treated with JS-11 or L-733,060, and mice treated with JS-11 or L-733,060 were treated with vehicles. Diphenhydramine (10 mg/kg)46 and JNJ-5207852 (10 mg/kg)47 were administered intraperitoneally 30 min prior to the injection of histamine into the right cheek. For all experiments, mice were recorded for 30 min post injection. Chloroquine (CQ) was injected at a concentration of 100 µg/20 µL into the dorsal nape or the cheek of C57Bl6 or CD-1 mice and recorded for 30 min.

Immunohistochemistry

For Tuj1 antibody staining, DRG and TG were isolated from SST-cre::Ai9 mice, fixed in 4% PFA for 24 h at 4 °C, cryoprotected using a 30% sucrose solution incubation (~72 h at 4 °C), and then frozen in OTC media. Sections were cut at 14 µm and placed in 4% PFA for 15 min at room temperature. Slides were then blocked with a solution of 5% BSA with 0.1% Triton-X100. Rabbit Anti-mouse Tuj (Abcam, Catalog # ab18207) (1:500 in 5% blocking solution) was applied to sections, which were then incubated overnight at 4 °C. After 16–24 h, the slides were washed in ice-cold 1× PBS three times and then incubated for 45 min at room temperature with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (Thermo Fisher, catalog # A-11008). Samples were imaged on a Leica DM500B epifluorescent microscope. For PGP9.5 staining, skin from the cheek and dorsal nape was collected after depilation with Nair. The tissue was then fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 24 h at 4 °C, followed by cryoprotection in a 30% sucrose solution for approximately 72 h at 4 °C. The samples were then embedded and frozen in OCT (Optimal Cutting Temperature) compound. Immunohistochemistry was performed on the frozen sections. A primary antibody, rabbit antiPGP9.5 (Proteintech, Catalog #14730-1), was diluted to 1:250 in 5% blocking solution and applied to the sections. The sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C. Following incubation, slides were washed three times with ice-cold 1× PBS. A secondary antibody, Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (Thermo Fisher, Catalog #A-11008), was then applied and incubated for 2 h at room temperature. The samples were imaged using a Leica Stellaris confocal microscope with a white light laser source.

Skin innervation tracing

Mice were briefly anesthetized with isoflurane prior to injection of wheat germ agglutinin (WGA) that was conjugated to Alexa Fluor 350 (Invitrogen, Catalog # W11263) (WGA-350) to determine changes in the population sizes of skin-innervating DRG and TG neurons48. WGA-350 was injected intradermally into both cheeks and the dorsal nape. Both TG and C3-T2 DRG were isolated 24 h after injection48. TG and DRG were then placed in OCT media and quickly frozen on dry ice and stored at −80 °C for up to 3 months. Cheek injections were bilateral to obtain the greatest potential number of TG sensory neurons that innervate the skin.

Fluorescent in situ hybridization

In situ hybridization was carried out using the RNAscope Multiplex Fluorescent Reagent Kit v2 (ACD, Catalog # 323100). DRG and TG were isolated from 8-week-old mice 24 h after receiving 50 µL of a 1 mg/mL solution of Alexa Fluor-conjugated Wheat Germ Agglutinin (WGA-350) and frozen in OTC media at −80 °C within 30 min. These sections were cut at a thickness of 10 µm and placed onto Superfrost Gold Plus Slides (Fisher, cat. # 15-188-48). After sectioning, sections were air-dried at −20 °C for 2 h. After this, slides were placed in 4% paraformaldehyde (4% PFA) made with DEPC water (Thermo Fisher, Catalog # 750023) at 4 °C for 15 min. Slides were then washed twice in 1× PBS made with DEPC water. Following this wash step, slides were dehydrated at room temperature in 50% ethanol for 5 min, 70% ethanol for 5 min, and two washes in 100% ethanol for 5 min. The 50% ethanol and 70% ethanol solutions were made with DEPC water. The slides were then kept at −20 °C in 100% ethanol overnight. Ethanol was purchased from Millipore Sigma (Catalog # 459836-2L).

The following day, the slides were removed from 100% ethanol and allowed to air-dry at room temperature for 5 min. A hydrophobic barrier was drawn around the samples using a hydrophobic barrier pen (ACD, Catalog # 310018). The hydrophobic barrier was allowed to dry for 5 min. After drying, roughly 5 drops of RNAscope hydrogen peroxide were added to fill the area within the hydrophobic barrier on the slide and incubated for 10 min at room temperature. The slides were then washed in DEPC-treated water. After this wash, roughly 5 drops of RNAscope Protease III were added to fill the area within the hydrophobic barrier and incubated for 30 min at room temperature. The slides were then washed twice in 1× PBS made with DEPC water. Sections were hybridized on the same day; therefore, sodium citrate was not used.

For hybridization, the following probes were ordered from ACD: Human TAC1 (Substance P) (Catalog# 310711-C3), human SST (Catalog # 310591-C2), human HRH1 (Catalog # 416501), human HRH3 (Catalog # 402191), mouse Tac1 (Catalog # 410351- C2), mouse SST (Catalog # 404631-C3), mouse Hrh1 (Catalog #491141), mouse Hrh3 (Catalog # 428481), mouse Npr1 (Catalog # 484531-C3), mouse TacR1 (Catalog # 428781), mouse EGR1 (Catalog # 423371), and mouse Fos (Catalog # 316921-C2). All DRG and TG slides were stained with the corresponding species-specific probes (human or mouse). All slides were stained with SST and Tac1, and either Hrh1 or Hrh3. To hybridize the probes, the hydrophobic barrier area was filled with ~6 drops of the appropriate probe mixture and then incubated for 2 h at 40 °C in a HybEZ oven. After this incubation, slides were washed in 1× wash buffer twice for 2 min (each wash). For the first two amplification steps, ~6 drops of RNAscope Multiplex FL v2 Amp1 or Amp2 were added to the area within the hydrophobic barrier and incubated for 30 min at 40 °C. For the third hybridization step, RNAscope Multiplex v2 Amp3 was added to the area within the hydrophobic barrier and incubated for 15 min at 40 °C. After each hybridization step, the slides were washed twice in 1× wash buffer for 2 min at room temperature.

To develop the HRP signal, the following steps were performed: roughly 6 drops of RNAscope Multiplex FL v2 HRP-C1, HRP-C2, or HRP-C3 were added to the area within the hydrophobic barrier on the slide and incubated for 15 min at 40 °C. Slides were then washed twice for 2 min in 1× wash buffer at room temperature. To the area in the hydrophobic barrier 200 µL of Opal 520 (channel 1) (Akoya Biosciences, Catalog # FP1487001KT), Opal 570 (channel 2) (Akoya Biosciences, Catalog # FP1488001KT), or Opal 690 (channel 3) (Akoya Biosciences, Catalog # FP1497001KT) was added, and the slides were incubated at 40 °C for 30 min. All dyes were diluted 1:1000. After incubation, slides were washed twice in 1× wash buffer for 2 min at room temperature. Finally, roughly 6 drops of RNAscope Multiplex FL v2 HRP blocker were added to the area within the hydrophobic barrier and incubated at 15 min at 40 °C. After this incubation, the slides were washed twice in 1× wash buffer for 2 min at room temperature.

Cell counting and quantification

Images were collected on a Leica DMB500 epifluorescence microscope. Cells were counted based on appearance since DRG and TG neurons have distinctly round to ovoid shapes with a large, round central gap where the nucleus is located. Skin-innervating neurons were normalized to the total number of WGA-350 neurons. In Figs. 5 and 7, cell populations were normalized to the total number of SST+ cells counted. In Fig. 2, the percentage of SST-expressing neurons was normalized to the total number of TUJ-positive neurons counted. In Fig. 6, cell populations were normalized to the total number of WGA-350 neurons counted. In Fig. 8, cell populations were normalized to the total number of EGR1+ cells or cFos+ cells counted. Cells were defined as >10 puncta surrounding or occluding the DAPI signal. A minimum of 300 neurons was counted per mouse per ganglia or spinal region, and a minimum of 200 neurons was counted per human per ganglia. Intraepidermal nerve fiber images were acquired using a Leica DMB epifluorescent microscope at 40× magnification. For each mouse, 3 sections were analyzed, and 3 fields of view were captured from each section. Intraepidermal nerve fibers were counted manually using ImageJ software. Nerve fiber density was calculated as the number of fibers per millimeter of epidermal length.

Substance P (SP) ELISA

Primary sensory neurons were isolated from the dorsal root ganglia (DRG) and trigeminal ganglia (TG) and placed in ice-cold Neurobasal A media (Thermo Fisher, 10888022) supplemented with PenStrep (Thermo Fisher, 15140122), Glutamax (Thermo Fisher, 35050061), and B27 (Thermo Fisher, 17504044). Respective ganglia were then digested using a mixture of dispase-collagenase (5 mg collagenase (MPBio, 02150704-CF) in 500 µL HBSS (VWR, 02-0121-0500) and 500 µL of Dispase (Stemcell Technologies, 07913)) in the water bath set at 37 °C for 1 h, with mixing every 15–20 min by gently flicking the tube. The ganglia were further dissociated using a glass Pasteur pipette (20–30 times) to obtain a single-cell suspension. The cells were then washed in 10 mL of neurobasal media and centrifuged for 15 min at 1000 rpm. The resulting cell pellet was resuspended in 500 µL of neurobasal media, counted, and plated at a density of 12,000 cells per well of a 24-well plate coated with 15 µg/mL poly-L-ornithine (Sigma, P3655) and 100 µg/ml laminin (Sigma, L2020). Wells with and without cells were stimulated with either 50 μM histamine (Sigma, H7125) or vehicle (DMEM/PBS). Cells were incubated for 15 min at 37 °C with 5% CO2. After this incubation, the supernatant was collected and stored at –80 °C immediately. The amount of SP released from these cells was measured using the Substance P ELISA kit (Abcam, ab288318).

Statistical analysis

Data was analyzed in GraphPad Prism (version 10.0.0 or higher). Paired 2-tailed t-tests were performed to compare histamine-induced scratching in the cheek versus the dorsal nape. Region-based data was analyzed with a 2-way ANOVA with a post hoc Holm – Šídák test for multiple comparisons. Other data was compared with a 1-way ANOVA with a post hoc Holm – Šídák test for multiple comparisons. Literature data was compared using an unpaired 2-tailed Mann–Whitney U-test. For all tests, the initial significance value was set to α = 0.05.
