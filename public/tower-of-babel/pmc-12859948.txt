FOXK1 drives colorectal cancer progression by transcriptionally activating GRB2
Authors: Yu Wang, Jianan Wang, Dan Ye, Shaohui Yang, Wei Cui
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Colorectal cancer (CRC) represents a leading cause of global cancer-related mortality. Despite substantial therapeutic advancements, persistent challenges of local recurrence and distant metastasis continue to compromise patient survival outcomes. Tumorigenesis and progression are orchestrated through complex signal transduction networks. It is imperative to delineate critical signaling pathways in CRC for identifying novel therapeutic targets. Transcription factor forkhead box 1 (FOXK1) and adaptor protein growth factor receptor-bound protein 2 (GRB2) regulate tumor initiation and progression across diverse malignancies. However, their functional interplay and precise mechanistic contributions in CRC remain elusive. In this study, FOXK1 overexpression promoted cellular proliferation and metastatic dissemination in CRC models. And it is correlated significantly with adverse clinical prognosis. Mechanistically, FOXK1 transcriptionally activates GRB2 by directly binding to its promoter region, thereby driving the malignant phenotype of CRC. These findings elucidate the FOXK1/GRB2 signaling axis. And they provide unprecedented insights into transcriptional regulatory networks governing CRC pathogenesis. As multifunctional signaling hubs, both FOXK1 and GRB2 represent promising candidates for molecularly targeted CRC therapies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-03706-w.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most commonly diagnosed malignancy and represents the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, accounting for nearly 900,000 annual fatalities. CRC typically presents with no discernible clinical symptoms in its early stages and is frequently diagnosed only after progression to advanced disease [1–4]. Current treatment modalities for CRC include surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and targeted therapy. With advances in tumor molecular biology and precision medicine, molecularly targeted therapy has emerged as a cornerstone of personalized and comprehensive treatment strategies for CRC [5].

The Forkhead Box K (FOXK) proteins represent a subfamily within the evolutionarily conserved FOX transcription factor superfamily, characterized by a conserved DNA-binding domain. FOX family comprises 19 phylogenetically distinct subfamilies (designated FOXA through FOXS), with 50 human FOX transcription factors identified to date [6–10]. FOX proteins orchestrate critical cellular processes—including cell cycle progression, proliferation, apoptosis, autophagy, and carcinogenesis—through sequence-specific binding to the conserved cis-element (5′-TTGTTTAC-3′) via their DNA-binding domain [11–17]. In humans, the FOXK subfamily consists of two isoforms (FOXK1 and FOXK2) that modulate diverse regulatory pathways. Accumulating evidence demonstrates FOXK1 overexpression across 16 distinct human malignancies compared with normal tissues, implicating its functional significance in tumorigenesis and disease progression [18]. FOXK1 is overexpressed in CRC based on previous studies, and the malignant phenotype in the CRC cells could be inhibited with the knockdown of the protein [19, 20]. Further mechanistic investigations delineating FOXK1 interactions with downstream molecular targets during CRC pathogenesis remain imperative, as such insights hold substantial translational potential for developing novel targeted therapeutic strategies.

Growth factor receptor-binding protein 2 (GRB2), a ubiquitously expressed non-enzymatic adaptor protein, mediates transduction of extracellular signaling to intracellular cascades through dynamic interactions with diverse molecular partners [21]. Structurally, human GRB2 features a central SH2 domain that recognizes phosphorylated tyrosine residues (e.g., activated receptor tyrosine kinases), flanked by two Src homology 3 (SH3) domains conferring specificity for proline-rich sequence motifs. Beyond its fundamental roles in cellular physiology, GRB2 contributes substantively to oncogenic processes—including proliferation, invasion, metastasis, and angiogenesis—modulating pathogenesis, progression, and therapeutic outcomes across diverse malignancies such as chronic myeloid leukemia, breast cancer, bladder cancer, cervical cancer, gastric cancer, pancreatic cancer, hepatocellular carcinoma, and colorectal carcinoma [22–25].

GRB2 overexpression interacts with epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), triggering Ras activation that recruits downstream Raf proteins and subsequently activates the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway, thereby promoting proliferation and differentiation in bladder cancer cells [26, 27]. Similarly, the GRB2-EGFR interaction mediated MAPK activation pathway contributes to occurrence and progression of pancreatic cancer [28–30]. GRB2 was also found to be highly expressed in breast cancer, which promote the development of breast cancer by activating the Akt signaling pathway. Additionally, researchers have discovered that GRB2 interacts with the LMTK3 kinase to facilitate the proliferation and metastasis in breast cancer cells [31–33]. Meanwhile, Ding et al. have found that Pseudopodium enriched atypical kinase 1 (PEAK1) kinase was usually underexpressed in CRC. And overexpressed PEAK1 inhibits the growth, invasion and metastasis of CRC cells. PEAK1 has further found to inhibit the occurrence and metastasis of colorectal tumors by up-regulating PPP1R12B inhibiting the activation of the Grb2/PI3K/Akt signaling pathway [34].

Both FOXK1 and GRB2 are overexpressed in CRC, yet their potential interplay remains uncharacterized. This study demonstrates that FOXK1 drives to CRC progression and is associated with poor prognosis by transcriptionally activating GRB2. These findings extend the current understanding of FOXK1 and GRB2-mediated signaling cascades and offer novel insights into regulatory mechanisms underlying CRC pathogenesis.

Materials and methods

Tissue collection and cell culture

30 samples of CRC tissues and adjacent normal tissues were collected post-surgery from CRC patients in Lihuili Hospital Affiliated to Ningbo University, which are approved by the hospital’s ethics committee (Ethics Approval Number: KY2024SL398-01). Moreover, informed consent forms were obtained from all participating patients. Human colorectal carcinoma cell lines (SW1116, Lovo, SW620, SW480) acquired from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, USA) were maintained in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 100 μg/mL streptomycin and 100 U/mL penicillin. Cells were incubated at 37 °C under a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. The FHC cell line was cultured in DMEM medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum under identical incubation conditions.

Animal experiments

All animal experiments are conducted by Lihuili Hospital Affiliated to Ningbo University. BALB/c nude mice (4–6 weeks old; 20.0 ± 2.0 g; Male) bought from Charles River. Transfected with sh- FOXK1 or sh-NC cells (2 × 105 cells per mouse, 0.2mLPBS) were injected subcutaneously into the lower back of mice. Measuring the tumor size every 5 days and tumor mass after 25 days was required. Tumor volume size (mm3) was measured with a vernier caliper and calculated as: volume = length × width2 × 0.5. When the tumor diameter reached 5 mm, the mice were randomly divided into treatment group with 5 mice in each group. They were killed at 25 days, and the tumors were dissected.

RNA sequencing

The collected cell samples were extracted to total RNA using TRIzol reagent. The cDNA was be amplificated through PCR assay to increase the amount of cDNA to requirements of RNA sequencing. Sequence quality for all samples was assessed using FastQC (v0.11.7), and quality trimming was conducted using FASTX-Toolkit (v 0.0.14) to remove bases with a Phred33 score of less than 30 while retaining the resulting reads of at least 50 bases in length. Gene expression profiling was performed on the basis of the number of reads. Fragments per kilo base of exon model per million mapped reads (FPKM) values of each unigene were used to estimate the expressed values and transcript levels using SAMtools (v1.7) and HTSeq-count (v0.9.1). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were obtained by DESeq2 (v1.30.1) with a P-value cutoff ≦0.05 and an absolute fold-change of ≧1. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were screened out by comparing the high-throughput sequencing results of gene expression differences between CRC cells and normal cells. In addition, clustering analysis of cells based on gene expression data, gathering similar cells together helps to discover cell heterogeneity and different cell subpopulations.

RNA extraction and RT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from harvested cells using TRIzol reagent according to standard protocols. Reverse transcription was performed with Oligo(dT) primers using mRNA templates to synthesize complementary DNA (cDNA), following manufacturer-specified procedures. The relative mRNA expression levels of FOXK1 and GRB2 mRNA were detected by SYBR real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR based on the 2−ΔΔCt methods. Primer sequences for FOXK1 and GRB2 amplification are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Short hairpin RNA (shRNA) mediated gene knockdown technology

Specific short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) targeting FOXK1 and GRB2 were designed using the online shRNA design tool provided by Thermo Fisher Scientific. The corresponding oligonucleotide sequences were commercially synthesized and subsequently ligated into the pLKO.1 vector via homologous recombination. When the cells reached the logarithmic growth phase, the pLKO.1-shRNA plasmids/control plasmids and viral packaging plasmids were transfected into the cells. Drug selection was then applied to obtain shFOXK1 or shGRB2 in CRC cells (SW1116, Lovo). After screening for cells stably integrated with the shRNA vector, the selected cells were monoclonalized and cultured through limited dilution to obtain single-cell-derived cell lines. The primer designed for constructing the knockdown of FOXK1 and GRB2 cell lines in this study were listed in Table S2.

Construction of overexpressing FOXK1 cell line

The coding sequence (CDS) region of FOXK1 was amplified by PCR, ligated into the expression vector, and transformed into DH5α competent Escherichia coli cells. Successfully recombined plasmids were purified for subsequent experiments. After CRC cells reach full confluency observed under microscope, the cells were transfected recombinant plasmid/control plasmid containing FOXK1 and virus packaging plasmid for virus packaging with Opti-MEM transfection solution. And drugs were added to screen the CRC cells overexpressing FOXK1.

The collection of protein samples for Western blot assay

30 mg of tissue samples were cut from CRC tissues and adjacent normal tissues, or cell samples were collected into EP tubes. These samples were lysed with 300 μL strong RIPA lysate and 3 μL protease inhibitor on ice for 10–30 min through ultrasonication. And then were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm at 4 °C for 10 min, of which supernatant was transferred it to a new EP tube and was added 50 μL of SDS loading buffer to be boiled for 10 min. The prepared protein samples were separated via SDS-PAGE electrophoresis, which were performed Western blot analysis in the transferring buffer with PVDF membrane. The PVDF membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with specific primary antibodies after transferring, and then incubated with HRP-labeled secondary antibodies at room temperature for 2 h. Finally, the images were visualized and captured through chemiluminescence imaging system. The antibody information required for WB analysis in this study were listed in Table S3.

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK8) assay

Cell samples were resuspended in complete medium supplemented with serum and adjusted to a density of 5 × 103 to 5 × 104 cells/mL using a hemocytometer. The cell suspension was seeded into 96-well plates at a volume of 100 μL per well and pre-incubated for 24 h at 37 °C under 5% CO2. Thereafter, CCK-8 solution was added to each well and incubated for 1–4 h. The optical density at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader.

EdU assay

The collected cell samples were resuspended with serum-containing medium, and the hemocytometer count was simultaneously inoculated to 2 × 105 single-cell suspension, which were incubated overnight in a 37 °C 5%CO₂ incubator. The diluted EdU reagents were added into each well for incubation at 37 °C 5%CO₂ incubator for 2 h. Add 4% paraformaldehyde to each well to fix cells for 30 min and permeabilize cells with Triton X-100 for 10 min. The fluorescent dye and DAPI nuclei dye were added into per well, which incubated at room temperature protected from light for 30 min. Images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope and quantified for cell proliferation.

Transwell assay

Collected cell samples were resuspended in serum-containing medium with mitomycin-C (10 μg/mL), and 100 μL of the cell suspension was seeded into the upper chamber of a Transwell insert. The lower chamber was filled with 500 μL of RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum. The assembly was incubated at 37 °C under 5% CO₂ for 24 h. Following incubation, cells on the lower membrane surface were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and enumerated using light microscopy.

Scratch assay

Three parallel reference lines were inscribed on the external bottom surface of six-well cell culture plates using a sterile marker. Cells were seeded into each well according to experimental groupings at a density of 5 × 105 cells per well. Upon reaching 90–100% confluency, two linear scratches were generated perpendicular to the pre-marked lines using a 200 μL pipette tip, creating consistent intersections to serve as fixed observation points. Phase-contrast images were acquired at predetermined magnifications to document initial wound widths. Mitomycin-C (10 μg/mL) were added to the plates, and plates were subsequently incubated at 37 °C under 5% CO₂. Scratch closure was monitored at designated intervals by imaging the same reference locations. Cell migration distances were quantified using ImageJ software based on the recorded images.

Luciferase assay

A series of truncated fragments derived from the GRB2 promoter region were amplified by PCR and cloned into a luciferase reporter plasmid using homologous recombination. Following amplification and purification, the constructed plasmids were transfected into CRC cells stably overexpressing FOXK1 (OE-FOXK1). Subsequently, luciferin substrate was added and enzymatically cleaved by luciferase, resulting in light emission. Luminescence intensity was quantified using a luminometer.

ChIP-qPCR assay

After OE-FOXK1 CRC cells reach full confluency observed under microscope, the cells were incubated at 37 °C with a final concentration of 1% formaldehyde for 10 min for crosslinking. And then glycine was added to infiltrate the cells to terminate the cross-linking reaction, and the cells were collected by cell scraper. SDS Lysis Bufffer was added to the cells and disrupted by ultrasound on ice. These samples were centrifuged at 10,000×g at 4 °C for 10 min, and transferred the supernatant to a new EP tube, in which 100 μL supernatant was pipetted into the new EP tube as the experimental group (IP group), and 100 μL supernatant was piped into the new EP tube as the control group (IgG group). And ChIP Dilution Buffer was added into the IP group and IgG group samples at 4 °C for incubation 1 h. Each tube was added to Protein A Agrose and incubated at 4 °C for 2 h. Antibodies were added to the IP group and IgG antibodies were added to the Input group, and the instrument was incubated overnight at 4 °C inverted. Each set of EP tubes is placed on a magnetic stand, allowed to stand until magnetically separated, and the supernatant is discarded. After washing multiple times with IP Wash Buffer, the supernatant is discarded after magnetic separation. The eluate was then added and the supernatant was collected after centrifugation at 4 °C. Add NaCl to each tube to unravel the cross-linking. Purification of DNA from samples is performed using DNA purification spin columns for qPCR validation. The preparation of the qPCR reaction system was carried out based on the experimental instructions, and qPCR analysis was carried out.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 8 software was used for the statistical analysis of the experimental data during the study. Quantitative data was expressed as mean ± standard, as indicated by at least three experiments. Comparison between two groups was performed using Student’s t-test, while comparisons among multiple groups were analyzed with one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were assessed using the log-rank test. The experiments were statistically significant when p < 0.05. * indicates p < 0.05; ** indicates p < 0.01, *** denotes p < 0.001.

High expression of FOXK1 was found in multiple cancers through pan-cancer expression analysis of the TCGA database (Fig. 1A), such as CRC (Fig. 1B), suggesting that it functions as oncogenic properties in multiple tumors. Further analysis combined with GEO database confirmed that FOXK1 also showed significant high expression in CRC (Fig. 1C). In addition, overexpressed FOXK1 was closely associated with poor prognosis of patients based on survival curve analysis (Fig. 1D). Western blot analysis verified FOXK1 protein upregulation in six paired clinical CRC specimens compared to adjacent normal tissues (Fig. 1E). Meanwhile, FOXK1 is also overexpressed in CRC from mRNA expression level by RT-PCR (Fig. 1F). Compared with normal Colorectal cell (FHC), the FOXK1 was up-regulated in 4 cases of CRC cells through RT-PCR (Fig. 1G) and Western-blot (Fig. 1H). In addition, through univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses, we identified USP1 as an independent poor prognostic factor for CRC (Fig S1A). Successful generation of stable FOXK1-knockdown cell lines (sh-FOXK1#1 and sh-FOXK1#2) was confirmed by reduced mRNA and protein expression via RT-qPCR (Fig. 2A) and Western blot analysis (Fig. 2B). A series of phenotypic experiments were performed in which cell counting kit-8 (Fig. 2C) and Edu (Fig. 2D) were used to measure the proliferation of sh-FOXK1 cell, and the proliferation of sh-FOXK1 was down-regulated. Besides, the scratch assay (Fig. 2E) and Transwell assay (Fig. 2F) were carried out to study the migration ability of cells, indicating that the migration ability was suppressed in the sh-FOXK1 cells. Next, we established tumor models by subcutaneously injecting CRC cell-sh-NC and CRC cell-sh-FOXK1 into mice. Notably, we found that knocking down FOXK1 significantly inhibited tumor growth in vivo (Fig. 2G–I). In addition, IHC and HE staining assays showed that the expression of Ki-67 in CRC tissues was significantly decreased after knocking down FOXK1 (Fig. 2J). FOXK1 transcriptionally activates GRB2

FOXK1 binds specific DNA motifs within downstream target promoters via its conserved DNA-binding domain to modulate transcriptional activity. To functionally characterize this regulatory mechanism, the CDS of human FOXK1 was ectopically expressed in two colorectal carcinoma cell lines (SW1116 and Lovo), establishing stable FOXK1-overexpressing models (OE-FOXK1). qRT-PCR confirmed significantly elevated FOXK1 mRNA levels in OE-FOXK1 cells relative to vector controls (Fig. 4A). Two putative FOXK1-binding sites were searched through bioinformatic prediction tools within the GRB2 promoter region. In the study, several truncated GRB2 promoter fragments and two point-mutated GRB2 promoter fragments, as well as wild type GRB2 promoter, fused to a luciferase reporter were constructed and were transfected into the SW1116 and Lovo cells. Luciferase assays demonstrated specific binding of FOXK1 to binding site 1 (Fig. 4B). Chromatin immunoprecipitation quantitative PCR (ChIP-qPCR) further validated this physical interaction (Fig. 4C). GRB2 promotes the proliferation and migration of CRC cells in vitro

To investigate the functional role of GRB2 overexpression in CRC, stable GRB2-knockdown cell lines were established using shRNA technology. The mRNA expression of GRB2 was significantly downregulated in sh-GRB2 CRC cell lines (Fig. 5A), indicating that the sh-GRB2 cell lines were successfully constructed. The CCK8 and Edu phenotypic assays demonstrated that the proliferation of sh-GRB2 cells was inhibited (Fig. 5B, C), and their migration ability also were remarkedly downregulated through Transwell and scratch assay (Fig. 5D, E). In addition, we also attempted to identify the downstream signaling pathway of GRB2 to further elucidate its function. Interestingly, Western Blot assays revealed that the phosphorylation levels of EGFR, PI3K, and AKT were inhibited after GRB2 knockdown (Fig. 5F). 740Y-P is an activator of the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway. Subsequently, we treated sh-GRB2 CRC cells with 740Y-P and conducted functional assays. Interestingly, we found that the activator 740Y-P effectively reversed the inhibitory effects of sh-GRB2 on CRC cell proliferation and migration (Fig S3A–D). To further investigate the relationship between GRB2 and PI3K, we performed CO-IP experiments for validation. Importantly, we discovered that there was an interaction between GRB2 and the p85 subunit of PI3K (Fig. 5G). These results indicated that GRB2 knockdown suppressed the PI3K-PI3K/AKT pathway. FOXK1 drives CRC progression by transcriptionally activating GRB2

FOXK1 was demonstrated to directly bind the GRB2 promoter via its DNA-binding domain. Simultaneously, Both FOXK1 and GRB2 overexpression were demonstrated to facilitated the proliferation and migration in CRC cells. To identify whether overexpressed FOXK1 is involved in the proliferation and migration phenotype of CRC cells via binding to GRB2, the knockdown of GRB2 via shRNA in FOXK1-overexpressing cell lines (OE-FOXK1) was constructed. Compared with OE-FOXK1 CRC cell, the knockdown of GRB2 in the OE-FOXK1 CRC cell (OE-FOXK1-shGRB2) markedly inhibited the proliferation demonstrated by the CCK8 and Edu assays (Fig. 6A, B). Furthermore, Transwell migration and wound healing assays revealed markedly impaired migratory capacity in OE-FOXK1-shGRB2 cells (Fig. 6C, D). Next, we also found via Western Blot assays that the phosphorylation levels of EGFR, PI3K, and AKT were increased after FOXK1 overexpression (Fig. 6E). This indicated that the PI3K/AKT pathway in CRC cells was activated. Interestingly, we noted that GRB2 knockdown could reverse this activation effect (Fig. 6E). Discussion

CRC represents one of the most prevalent malignancies worldwide and remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality [35, 36]. Despite substantial progress in diagnostic and therapeutic modalities for CRC, persistent challenges such as local recurrence and distant metastasis contribute to unfavorable survival outcomes among affected patients [37–39]. Therefore, elucidating the signaling transduction mechanisms between colorectal tumor cells and the tumor microenvironment is imperative. In addition, these processes are critically involved in tumorigenesis and disease progression. And we may yield actionable therapeutic targets from them. In this study, FOXK1 overexpression was found to promote proliferation and metastasis in CRC cells and correlated with adverse clinical outcomes (Fig. 1E–H and Fig S1A). This was corroborated with earlier findings by Wu et al. regarding the functional interaction between FOXK1 and FHL2 in CRC [40]. Furthermore, our findings are also consistent with the previous study that lncRNA LINC01503 inhibits the expression of MiR-4492 to regulate the up-regulation of FOXK1 expression and enhances the proliferation and invasion in CRC cells [41].

GRB2, a ubiquitously expressed adaptor protein, mediates multiple signal transduction pathways. And it is intimately associated with tumorigenesis, progression, and clinical outcomes across various cancers [42]. Nevertheless, the potential interaction between FOXK1 and GRB2 in CRC remains unexplored. GRB2 was also found to be overexpressed in CRC in this study (Fig. 3F–H). And overexpressed GRB2 was involved in regulating the proliferation and migration phenotype in CRC cells through CCK8, Edu, Transwell and scratch assays (Fig. 5A–E). The role of GRB2 in enhancing cellular migration is further supported by its established function in pancreatic cancer. Similarly, elevated GRB2 expression has been documented in breast cancer. These findings align with previous reports by Ding et al. in CRC, which indicates that the GRB2/PI3K/AKT pathway contributes to tumor development and metastasis [32].

In this study, integrated analysis of transcriptome sequencing data, validated by RT-PCR and Western blot assays, demonstrated that FOXK1 knockdown resulted in the downregulation of GRB2 expression in CRC cells (Fig. 3B–D). Furthermore, we demonstrated that FOXK1 binds directly to the GRB2 promoter via its DNA-binding domain to activate transcriptional expression (Fig. 4A–E). This resulted in elevated GRB2 levels that promoted malignant phenotypes in CRC (Fig. 6A–D). However, this study has not yet elucidated the specific downstream effector proteins through which FOXK1 promotes CRC progression via the transcriptional activation of GRB2. GRB2 binds to phosphotyrosine and proline-rich motifs as key downstream signal transduction proteins in multiple oncogenic signaling pathways. Meanwhile, GRB2 is identified to participate in activating downstream effector proteins or signaling pathways, including RTK, EGFR, AKT, LMTK3 kinase, GAB1, and GAB2. It leads to the proliferation, differentiation and metastasis in tumor cells [43, 44]. For example, GRB2 regulates PI3K/AKT and ERK/MAPK signaling, suppressing the invasion and metastasis of LUAD (39216120). Furthermore, we confirmed via Western Blot that knockdown of GRB2 reduced the phosphorylation levels of EGFR, PI3K, and AKT, thereby inhibiting the PI3K-AKT pathway (Figs. 5F and 6E). In addition, the activator 740Y-P of PI3K effectively reversed the inhibitory effects of GRB2 knockdown on CRC cell proliferation and migration (Fig. S3A–D). And there was an interaction between GRB2 and the p85 subunit of PI3K (Fig. 5G). Comprehensive studies of signal transduction networks in CRC warrant urgent investigation to elucidate the regulatory mechanisms underlying disease progression.

In summary, FOXK1 enhances the proliferative and migratory capacities of CRC cells by transcriptionally activating GRB2, thereby promoting malignant progression and contributing to poor prognosis in CRC. Consequently, both FOXK1 and GRB2 represent promising multi-target therapeutic candidates for molecularly targeted interventions in colorectal carcinoma.
