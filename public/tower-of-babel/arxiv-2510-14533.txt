Transferable Equivariant Quantum Circuits for TSP: Generalization Bounds and Empirical Validation
Monit Sharma, Hoong Chuin Lau

Abstract
In this work, we address the challenge of generalization in quantum reinforcement learning (QRL) for combinatorial optimization, focusing on the Traveling Salesman Problem (TSP). Training quantum policies on large TSP instances is often infeasible, so existing QRL approaches are limited to small-scale problems. To mitigate this, we employed Equivariant Quantum Circuits (EQCs) that respect the permutation symmetry of the TSP graph. This symmetry-aware ansatz enabled zero-shot transfer of trained parameters from n − n- city training instances to larger m-city problems. Building on recent theory showing that equivariant architectures avoid barren plateaus and generalize well, we derived novel generalization bounds for the transfer setting. Our analysis introduces a term quantifying the structural dissimilarity between n − n- and m − m- node TSPs, yielding an upper bound on performance loss under transfer. Empirically, we trained EQC-based policies on small n − n- city TSPs and evaluated them on larger instances, finding that they retained strong performance zero-shot and further improved with fine-tuning, consistent with classical observations of positive transfer between scales. These results demonstrate that embedding permutation symmetry into quantum models yields scalable QRL solutions for combinatorial tasks, highlighting the crucial role of equivariance in transferable quantum learning.

2
Foundations and Related Work

This work is motivated by three principles central to combinatorial optimization and learning theory:
symmetry
,
locality
, and
generalization
. These principles guide our use of Equivariant Quantum Circuits (EQCs) in scalable quantum reinforcement learning (QRL). Below we review their role and the associated literature.

2.1
Symmetry

Permutation symmetry is inherent in the Traveling Salesman Problem (TSP): relabeling cities does not alter the tour length or solution structure. Equivariant Quantum Circuits (EQCs)
[
11
]
, central to our methodology, embed this property by design. EQCs enforce a structural bias that compels learned policies to depend only on relational features such as graph topology and inter-city distances, rather than absolute city indices. When TSP instances are drawn from the same distribution (e.g., cities sampled uniformly from the unit square), larger problems represent natural statistical extensions of smaller ones. Consequently, an EQC trained on smaller instances can internalize generic heuristics, such as prioritizing local proximity or avoiding long jumps early in a tour,that remain effective as instance size grows.

This role is analogous to classical graph neural networks (GNNs), which are well-established in their ability to generalize across graphs of varying sizes when tasks exhibit permutation symmetry
[
21
]
. Prior work by Skolik et al.
[
12
]
demonstrated that a shallow (depth-1) EQC trained on 20-city TSPs generalized effectively to unseen instances of the same size, outperforming QAOA and surpassing non-equivariant circuits that failed to scale. Our work builds directly on these findings by extending the analysis to
cross-size transfer
, where parameters trained on
n
-city instances are applied to larger
m
-city problems.

2.2
Locality

Locality provides a second crucial inductive bias for scalability. Classic heuristics such as the nearest-neighbor method
[
22
]
, Christofides’ algorithm
[
23
]
, and insertion-based approaches
[
24
,
25
]
all rely on locally greedy decisions, constructing tours incrementally based on immediate neighborhood information. This property underpins their ability to scale to larger instances without global recomputation.

In machine learning, several studies reinforce the centrality of locality. Ouyang et al.
[
21
]
showed that RL agents trained on 50-city TSPs generalized effectively to instances with up to 1000 cities when using permutation-equivariant architectures. Fu et al.
[
14
]
introduced a compositional framework where small pretrained GNNs are recursively applied to larger TSPs. In contrast, Joshi et al.
[
26
]
demonstrated that RL solvers trained only on trivially small problems fail completely on larger instances, highlighting the need for inductive biases such as permutation symmetry and locality. More recently, Zhang and Li
[
27
]
showed that RL agents relying only on local neighborhood information still produce near-optimal tours for large graphs. Together, these findings suggest that if EQCs can encode locality-aware heuristics, their learned strategies should naturally persist across instance sizes.

2.3
Generalization

Generalization links symmetry and locality into a coherent framework for scalable learning. Under standard assumptions, including independent uniform sampling of cities, adherence to the triangle inequality, and the dominance of short edges in optimal tours, policies trained on small TSPs should maintain their effectiveness when transferred to larger ones. Euclidean TSPs are known to exhibit scale-invariant local structure, and classical results such as the Beardwood–Halton–Hammersley theorem
[
28
,
29
]
imply that optimal tour length grows smoothly as
\Theta(\sqrt{n})
. This supports the idea of
graceful performance degradation
, where approximation error increases slowly with instance size and can be mitigated through limited fine-tuning rather than full retraining.

In quantum models, generalization is intertwined with trainability. Quantum neural networks often encounter barren plateaus, where gradients vanish exponentially with system size
[
30
,
31
]
. A trade-off also arises between expressivity and generalization: overly expressive circuits risk overfitting, while highly constrained circuits lack flexibility
[
32
,
33
]
. Symmetry-based designs offer a way forward. Schatzki et al.
[
18
]
proved that permutation-equivariant QNNs avoid barren plateaus by restricting optimization to symmetry-consistent subspaces, while Mernyei et al.
[
34
]
empirically confirmed stable scaling with depth and qubit count. From a quantum information perspective, adding qubits to a local EQC primarily affects redundant degrees of freedom, while global entangling gates such as

U_{G}(\mathcal{E},\gamma)=\exp\left(i\gamma\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}}Z_{i}Z_{j}\right)

distribute parameter influence uniformly across edges. This phenomenon echoes parameter concentration observed in QAOA
[
35
,
16
]
, further suggesting robustness to scale.

Collectively, these insights support our central hypothesis: EQCs trained on small TSP instances encode transferable, instance-independent structural information. Symmetry ensures consistency across permutations, locality provides scale-invariant heuristics, and the absence of barren plateaus ensures that fine-tuning is efficient. Together, these principles make scalable quantum optimization via cross-size transfer both theoretically plausible and empirically promising.

3
Theoretical Framework for Zero-Shot Transfer

3.1
Background and Motivation

A significant practical hurdle in applying QRL, much like its classical counterpart, is the often-substantial computational cost associated with training an agent from scratch for every new problem instance or scale. Transfer learning, particularly zero-shot transfer, offers a compelling strategy to mitigate this issue. In this paradigm, a model trained on a source task is applied directly to a new, related target task without any further training on the target task’s data
[
36
]
. For TSP, this could mean training a QRL agent on an n-city problem and then deploying it to solve an m-city problem, ideally with
m>n
. The ability to effectively transfer learned knowledge would drastically improve the scalability and utility of QRL approaches.

3.2
Problem Statement

This work addresses the specific scenario of zero-shot transfer learning in the context of QRL for the TSP. A QRL agent, whose policy or value function is parameterized by a Quantum Neural Network (QNN), is first trained to find optimal or near-optimal solutions for an
n
-city TSP. The parameters
\theta^{*}
obtained from this training phase are then directly applied to an
m
-city TSP, where
m>n
. The central objective of this work is to derive a theoretical bound on the expected performance of this transferred QRL agent when evaluated on the larger
m
-city problem instances.

3.3
Approach and Contribution

The theoretical derivation presented in this work will be grounded in the principles and mathematical framework of Theorem 4 from Schatzki et al. (2024),
[
18
]
. This theorem provides generalization guarantees for
S_{n}
-equivariant QNNs, which are particularly relevant for problems with inherent permutation symmetries, such as the TSP. More details about this theorem can be found in Appendix
A
.

The primary contribution of this paper is a novel theoretical performance bound for the described zero-shot transfer scenario. This bound aims to relate the expected performance on the
m
-city target task to:

•

performance achieved on the
n
-city source task

•

relevant parameters of the QRL model, particularly those related to the complexity of the equivariant QNN

•

"dissimilarity" or "gap" between the
n
-city and
m
-city TSP tasks, which arises from the change in problem scale and structure.

The derivation of such a bound is essential for understanding the fundamental limits and potential of transferring QRL policies across different problem sizes. It moves beyond empirical observations by providing a theoretical basis for how performance might degrade or be maintained during transfer. This involves a careful adaptation of a generalization bound, which concerns performance on unseen data from the same task distribution, to a transfer learning bound, which concerns performance on a different (though related) target task distribution
[
37
]
. A key challenge and focus will be the introduction and characterization of a task dissimilarity metric that bridges this conceptual gap.

3.4
Notation and Preliminaries

The following lists the key notation that will be used in this paper:

•

P_{k}(\theta)
: True performance of the QRL agent with parameters
\theta
on a
k
-city TSP. We assume higher
P_{k}(\theta)
is better (e.g., expected reward).

•

\theta_{n}^{*}
: Parameters obtained by training the QRL agent on the
n
-city TSP using
M_{n}
training episodes/samples.

•

P_{n}(\theta_{n}^{*})
: True performance of the agent with parameters
\theta_{n}^{*}
on the
n
-city TSP.

•

\mathcal{G}_{n}(\delta)
: Generalization error on the
n
-city source task,

\mathcal{G}_{n}(\delta)=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{T_{e_{n+1}}}{M_{n}}}+\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{M_{n}}}\right)

with probability at least
1-\delta

[
18
]
. Thus,

P_{n}(\theta_{n}^{*})\geq\widehat{P}_{n}(\theta_{n}^{*})-\mathcal{G}_{n}(\delta),

where
\widehat{P}_{n}(\theta_{n}^{*})
is the empirical performance on the training set.

•

P_{m}(\theta_{n}^{*})
: True performance of the agent on the
m
-city TSP when using the transferred parameters
\theta_{n}^{*}
and
S_{m}
-adapted generators. This is the quantity we wish to bound.

•

\mathcal{D}_{n\rightarrow m}
: An upper bound on performance drop due to zero-shot transfer, i.e.,

\mathcal{D}_{n\rightarrow m}\geq\left|P_{n}(\theta_{n}^{*})-P_{m}(\theta_{n}^{*})\right|

under certain conditions, or more specifically,

\mathcal{D}_{n\rightarrow m}\geq P_{n}(\theta_{n}^{*})-P_{m}(\theta_{n}^{*}).

Here,
P_{k}(\theta)
specifically denotes the performance of the
k
-city EQC evaluated with parameters
\theta
, i.e.,
P_{k}(\theta):=P_{k}(U_{k}(\theta))
, where
U_{k}(\theta)
is the equivariant quantum circuit architecture indexed by problem size
k
.

Assumption on Source-Task Training Quality.

We assume that the QRL policy trained on the
n
-city source task achieves performance close to the task optimum. Specifically, we allow for a bounded sub-optimality gap:

P_{n}(\theta_{n}^{*})=P_{n}^{\text{opt}}-\varepsilon_{n},

where
\varepsilon_{n}
denotes the residual optimality gap after training.

3.5
Problem Formulation: Zero-Shot Transfer for TSP with QRL

To develop a theoretical framework for analyzing zero-shot transfer in TSP Quantum Reinforcement Learning (QRL), we begin by precisely formulating the transfer setting and identifying a set of foundational assumptions. These assumptions serve to map the supervised learning framework underpinning
[
18
]
(see Appendix
A
for more details), which is centered on generalization guarantees for
S_{n}
-equivariant quantum neural networks, to the sequential decision-making dynamics characteristic of QRL.

•

Source Task (
T_{n}
):
The QRL agent is trained to solve an
n
-city TSP. The agent’s behavior is determined by a policy
\pi_{\theta}
(or a value function
V_{\theta}
), where
\theta
represents the trainable parameters of its internal QNN. Through interaction with
n
-city TSP environments (e.g., various graph instances or a fixed graph explored over many episodes), the agent learns optimal parameters
\theta_{n}^{*}
.

–

The state representation for an
n
-city TSP typically encodes information such as the current city, the set of visited cities, and potentially information about remaining cities or path costs. This state is encoded into
N_{q}(n)
qubits.

–

The action space involves selecting the next city to visit from the set of unvisited cities.

–

The reward function guides the agent, typically providing negative rewards proportional to edge distances and a large positive reward upon completing a valid tour, or simply the negative total tour length at the end of an episode.

•

Target Task (
T_{m}
):
The agent is then tasked with solving an
m
-city TSP, where
m>n
. Crucially, in the zero-shot transfer setting, the parameters
\theta_{n}^{*}
learned from
T_{n}
are used directly, without any further training or fine-tuning on
m
-city TSP instances. The state representation is now for
m
cities, encoded on
N_{q}(m)
qubits.

•

QRL Agent Architecture:
We assume the core of the QRL agent (e.g., the policy network or value function approximator) is a QNN,
U(\theta)
. The architecture of this QNN is initially defined for
N_{q}(n)
qubits. Its adaptation to operate on
N_{q}(m)
qubits while using the fixed parameters
\theta_{n}^{*}
is a critical point that will be detailed in the assumptions.

•

Performance metric (
P_{k}(\theta)
).

Throughout we adopt the
“higher-is-better”
convention: larger
P_{k}(\theta)
means a better tour.
To ensure that the Hoeffding-type generalisation terms in
[
18
]
apply, we
affinely rescale
every concrete metric so that

0\;\leq\;P_{k}(\theta)\;\leq\;1\quad\text{for all $k$ and all $\theta$}.

Typical instantiations are:

–

Expected tour-quality reward.

Let
L(\theta)
be the expected tour length produced by the agent and

L_{\max}>L_{\text{opt}}
a deterministic upper bound (e.g. tour length of the
nearest-neighbour heuristic).

P_{k}(\theta)\;=\;1-\frac{L(\theta)-L_{\text{opt}}}{L_{\max}-L_{\text{opt}}}\;\in\;[0,1].

(Equivalently, you may work directly with the reward

R=-L
and then normalise to
[0,1]
.)

–

Probability of optimality.

P_{k}(\theta)=\Pr[\text{agent outputs an optimal tour}]
—already in
[0,1]
.

–

Approximation ratio.

For ratio
r(\theta)=L(\theta)/L_{\text{opt}}\geq 1
fix a known worst-case
bound
\rho_{\max}
(e.g.
2
in the Euclidean metric); set

P_{k}(\theta)\;=\;1-\frac{r(\theta)-1}{\rho_{\max}-1}\;\in\;[0,1].

Henceforth every reference to “shorter route”, “negative tour length”, or
similar uses this rescaled, reward-style metric.

3.6
Formal Assumptions for the Transfer Bound

Our derivation relies on a set of assumptions ensuring symmetry preservation, smoothness of performance metrics, and Lipschitz continuity across parameters and problem sizes. For clarity, we summarize them here:

•

Equivariance:
The circuit and policy remain
S_{k}
-equivariant across problem sizes.

•

Generalization:
Source-task training satisfies standard generalization bounds under i.i.d. or replay settings.

•

Problem Encoding:
Each city is mapped to one qubit, ensuring task–architecture alignment.

•

Smoothness:
Optimal performance varies continuously with problem size (Euclidean TSP assumption).

•

Regularity:
The performance functional is Lipschitz-continuous in parameters, instances, and unitaries.

Full technical statements and proofs are provided in Appendix
C
.

4
A Theoretical Bound on Cross-Instance Transfer Performance

Building on the theoretical foundations established by
[
18
]
(Appendix
A
) and the assumptions
(A1–A7)
introduced earlier (see Appendix
C
), we now derive a formal upper bound on the performance
P_{m}(\theta_{n}^{*})
of the QRL agent when deployed on the
m
-city target task
T_{m}
using parameters
\theta_{n}^{*}
learned from the
n
-city source task
T_{n}
. The objective is to quantify the extent to which the agent’s performance on
T_{m}
can be predicted based on its training dynamics on
T_{n}
, the
S_{n}
-equivariant circuit structure, and the dissimilarity
\mathcal{D}_{n\rightarrow m}
(see Appendix
D
for more details) between the source and target tasks. This bound offers a theoretically grounded estimate of the potential performance degradation induced by zero-shot transfer across different problem sizes, while respecting the symmetry-aware architecture of the underlying quantum neural network.

4.1
Structure of the Bound

Our goal is to establish a lower bound for
P_{m}(\theta_{n}^{*})

in terms of
P_{n}(\theta_{n}^{*})
and the change in performance due to transfer.

Let

\Delta P_{n\rightarrow m}(\theta_{n}^{*})=P_{n}(\theta_{n}^{*})-P_{m}(\theta_{n}^{*})

represent the drop in performance when transferring from the
n
-city task to the
m
-city task using parameters
\theta_{n}^{*}
.

We know from the generalization bound on the source task that:

P_{n}(\theta_{n}^{*})\geq\widehat{P}_{n}(\theta_{n}^{*})-\mathcal{G}_{n}(\delta)

Substituting this into the expression for
P_{m}(\theta_{n}^{*})
:

P_{m}(\theta_{n}^{*})\geq\left(\widehat{P}_{n}(\theta_{n}^{*})-\mathcal{G}_{n}(\delta)\right)-\Delta P_{n\rightarrow m}(\theta_{n}^{*})

The core of the derivation is to find a suitable upper bound for
\Delta P_{n\rightarrow m}(\theta_{n}^{*})
, which we have denoted conceptually as
\mathcal{D}_{n\rightarrow m}
. We can establish that

\Delta P_{n\rightarrow m}(\theta_{n}^{*})\leq\mathcal{D}_{n\rightarrow m},

then:

P_{m}(\theta_{n}^{*})\geq\widehat{P}_{n}(\theta_{n}^{*})-\mathcal{G}_{n}(\delta)-\mathcal{D}_{n\rightarrow m}

With probability at least
1-\delta
:

P_{m}(\theta_{n}^{*})\geq\widehat{P}_{n}(\theta_{n}^{*})-\mathcal{G}_{n}(\delta)-\mathcal{D}_{n\rightarrow m}

This structure clearly separates the sources of sub-optimality on the target task:

•

\widehat{P}_{n}(\theta_{n}^{*})
: The empirical performance on the source task.

•

\mathcal{G}_{n}(\delta)
: The error due to imperfect learning (finite samples
M_{n}
) on the source task.

•

\mathcal{D}_{n\rightarrow m}
: The additional error or performance drop incurred due to the zero-shot transfer to a different-sized problem.

Characterizing
\mathcal{D}_{n\rightarrow m}
presents the principal analytical challenge, as it must capture the nuanced effects arising from scaling the problem size and deploying parameters optimized for the
n
-city setting within an
m
-city QNN architecture with structurally adapted equivariant generators.

4.2
Quantifying Task Dissimilarity
\mathcal{D}_{n\to m}

A central challenge in extending single-task generalization bounds to transfer is
to quantify the performance loss when reusing parameters
\theta^{*}_{n}
, optimized for
an
n
-city TSP, on a larger
m
-city instance. We denote this penalty by

\mathcal{D}_{n\to m}
.

Intuitively,
\mathcal{D}_{n\to m}
captures two key effects:

1.

Parametric mismatch:
the same parameters
\theta^{*}_{n}
act on scaled
circuit generators (
\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{j}O_{j}
vs.
\tfrac{1}{m}\sum_{j}O_{j}
), producing
systematic deviations in the induced unitaries.

2.

Structural shift:
even under equivariance, the statistical and geometric
properties of optimal TSP tours evolve with problem size, leading to smooth but
non-negligible performance differences.

Together, these contributions define the transfer penalty:

\Delta P_{n\to m}(\theta^{*}_{n})\;\leq\;\mathcal{D}_{n\to m}=D^{(\mathrm{param})}_{n\to m}+D^{(\mathrm{struct})}_{n\to m}.

The detailed derivation of this decomposition, including operator-norm
bounds for parametric mismatch and concentration-based bounds for structural
shift, is given in Appendix
E
.

4.3
Final Performance Bound

We now synthesize the results to obtain a rigorous lower bound on the target task performance
P_{m}(\theta_{n}^{*})
, achieved by deploying parameters
\theta_{n}^{*}
trained on the source task with
n
cities. This bound integrates both the generalization guarantee on the source distribution (Assumption
(A2) from Appendix
C
) and the non-heuristic transfer penalty
\mathcal{D}_{n\rightarrow m}
derived in Section
4.2
.

Theorem 1
(Zero-Shot Transfer Performance Bound)

Let
\theta_{n}^{*}
denote the parameters trained on
M_{n}
i.i.d. episodes from the
n
-city task. Under Assumptions
(A1)
–
(A7)
, the performance on the
m
-city target task satisfies, with probability at least
1-\delta
:

P_{m}(\theta_{n}^{*})\;\geq\;\widehat{P}_{n}(\theta_{n}^{*})-\underbrace{\mathcal{G}_{n}(\delta)}_{\text{generalization error}}-\underbrace{\mathcal{D}_{n\rightarrow m}}_{\text{transfer penalty}},

where:

\mathcal{G}_{n}(\delta)=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{T_{e_{n+1}}}{M_{n}}}+\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{M_{n}}}\right),

and:

\boxed{\mathcal{D}_{n\rightarrow m}:=L_{U}\cdot\|\theta_{n}^{*}\|_{1}\cdot e^{\theta_{\max}\|A\|_{\text{op}}}\cdot 2\cdot\|A\|_{\text{op}}\cdot\frac{m-n}{m}+C^{\prime}\cdot\frac{m-n}{\sqrt{n}}}.

As discussed in Section
3.4
, the empirical source performance can be written as

P_{n}(\theta_{n}^{*})=P_{n}^{\mathrm{opt}}-\varepsilon_{n},

where
\varepsilon_{n}
captures the residual sub-optimality of the trained policy.

By the source generalization guarantee (Assumption (A2)), with probability at least
1-\delta
,

P_{n}(\theta_{n}^{*})\;\geq\;\widehat{P}_{n}(\theta_{n}^{*})\;-\;\mathcal{G}_{n}(\delta).

Substituting
P_{n}(\theta_{n}^{*})=P_{n}^{\mathrm{opt}}-\varepsilon_{n}
into the transfer bound yields the equivalent form

\boxed{\,P_{m}(\theta_{n}^{*})\;\geq\;P_{n}^{\mathrm{opt}}\;-\;\varepsilon_{n}\;-\;\mathcal{G}_{n}(\delta)\;-\;\mathcal{D}_{n\to m}\,}\quad\text{(with probability }\geq 1-\delta\text{)}

4.4
Discussion and Implications

The derived performance bound

P_{m}(\theta^{*}_{n})\;\geq\;\widehat{P}_{n}(\theta^{*}_{n})-\mathcal{G}_{n}(\delta)-\mathcal{D}_{n\to m}

provides a principled characterization of zero-shot transfer in Equivariant Quantum Reinforcement Learning (QRL). It delineates two distinct mechanisms of performance degradation:

1.

Generalization Error.
The term
\mathcal{G}_{n}(\delta)
arises from finite training on the source task and depends on the effective hypothesis complexity
T_{e_{n+1}}\sim n^{3}
. It diminishes with increasing training episodes
M_{n}
, capturing classical sample-efficiency behavior.

2.

Transfer Penalty.
The term
\mathcal{D}_{n\rightarrow m}
quantifies the degradation incurred when reusing parameters across problem sizes, reflecting both architectural mismatch and structural differences between the
n
- and
m
-city TSP instances.

Both terms scale with the problem-size gap
m-n
but in distinct ways: the parametric component grows proportionally to
(m-n)/m
and remains uniformly bounded, while the structural component increases as
(m-n)/\sqrt{n}
, modulated by the parameter norm
\|\theta_{n}^{*}\|_{1}
. This ensures that performance degrades gracefully for moderate size shifts, provided the generator architecture evolves smoothly and the hypothesis class remains expressive.

Overall, the bound formalizes the intuition that larger source models, trained on instances with greater
n
, exhibit stronger generalization to larger tasks. It thereby motivates
size-aware training strategies
and supports the empirical observation that transfer performance improves monotonically with the source-task scale. Further analysis of scalability trends, design principles for transferable QRL, and the role of symmetry and data assumptions is provided in Appendix
F
.

6
Conclusion and Future Directions

This study demonstrates that embedding permutation symmetry into quantum reinforcement learning agents
enables knowledge learned on small TSP instances to transfer effectively to larger ones.
The theoretical bound we derived explains this transferability in terms of generalization and a transfer penalty,
while our empirical results confirm that such equivariant policies not only generalize zero-shot but also
serve as powerful initializations when fine-tuned.
We now conclude with some reflection on its significance and future works.

6.1
Significance and Limitations

The derived bound provides several significant theoretical insights:

•

It offers a formal framework for analyzing zero-shot transfer in the context of equivariant QML models, extending single-task generalization guarantees.

•

It explicitly identifies the key factors that influence transfer performance, particularly the interplay between source task learning quality and the inherent difficulty of transferring across different problem scales.

•

It underscores the importance of symmetry (
S_{k}
-equivariance) not only for efficient learning on a single task but also as a potentially beneficial structural prior for transfer learning, as it ensures the model respects fundamental problem invariances across different scales.

However, the analysis also has limitations:

•

The precise functional form and magnitude of the constants within the transfer penalty term
\mathcal{D}_{n\rightarrow m}
remain abstract. A more concrete, first-principle derivation of
\mathcal{D}_{n\rightarrow m}
would require a significantly deeper dive into the geometric properties of the QNN parameter landscapes and the specific dynamics of the QRL agent, which is beyond the scope of this work.

•

The bound relies on a specific set of assumptions regarding the QRL agent, TSP encoding, and the transfer mechanism. Deviations from these assumptions (e.g., different QNN adaptation strategies, or TSPs with fundamentally different structures at different scales) could lead to different performance characteristics.

•

The analysis is purely theoretical and does not account for practical hardware noise or imperfections in implementing the QNN, which also notes an area for future work for their single-task results.

6.2
Future Research Directions

This work opens several avenues for future research:

•

Refining
\mathcal{D}_{n\rightarrow m}
:
A primary goal would be to develop a more rigorous and detailed mathematical characterization of the transfer penalty
\mathcal{D}_{n\rightarrow m}
. This could involve tools from optimal transport, information theory, or by analyzing the sensitivity of the QNN’s output to changes in its underlying equivariant generators when parameters are fixed. Incorporating more specific structural properties of TSP instances (e.g., from random matrix theory or geometric graph theory) could also yield tighter bounds.

•

Investigating Alternative Transfer Mechanisms:
The current work assumes direct parameter transfer with adaptation of generator forms. Future research could explore:

–

Fine-tuning a subset of layers of the
S_{m}
-adapted QNN using a small amount of
m
-city data (few-shot transfer).

–

Layer-wise transfer strategies, where earlier layers (potentially capturing more general features) are transferred, and later layers are re-initialized or fine-tuned.

–

Methods for transforming or adapting the parameters
\theta_{n}^{*}
themselves to better suit the
m
-city problem, perhaps guided by the change in
T_{e_{n+1}}
to
T_{e_{m+1}}
.

•

Impact of Noise:
Extending the analysis to include the effects of quantum hardware noise on the generalization error in the source task and on the transfer penalty
\mathcal{D}_{n\rightarrow m}
is crucial for assessing practical applicability on near-term quantum devices.

•

Broader Applicability:
While we focused on TSP, the approach of adapting Theorem 1 and introducing a transfer penalty term could potentially be applied to other hard combinatorial optimization problems with underlying permutation symmetries (such as Quadratic Assignment Problem, Flowship Scheduling Problem), where equivariant QRL agents can be trained and generalization bounds applied similarly.

In conclusion,the theoretical bound derived in this paper provides a foundational step towards understanding the capabilities and limitations of zero-shot transfer for equivariant QRL agents. It emphasizes that while symmetry offers powerful advantages for learning, the challenges of transferring knowledge across different problem scales are non-trivial and warrant careful theoretical and empirical investigation.

Supplementary Information A
Context and Foundations

Theorem 4 in Schatzki et al. (2024)
[
18
]
provides a significant theoretical guarantee regarding the generalization capabilities of
S_{n}
-equivariant Quantum Neural Networks. Understanding this theorem is foundational to its adaptation for the zero-shot transfer learning scenario in QRL for TSP.

A.1
Statement of Theorem 4

The theorem is stated as follows:
Consider a Quantum Machine Learning (QML) problem with a loss function as described in Eq. (4) of
[
18
]
. Suppose that an
n
-qubit
S_{n}
-equivariant QNN
U(\theta)
is trained on
M
samples to obtain some trained parameters
\theta^{*}
. Then the following inequality holds with probability at least
1-\delta
:

\text{gen}(\theta^{*})\leq\mathcal{O}\Bigg(\sqrt{\frac{Te_{n+1}}{M}}+\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{M}}\Bigg)

Here,
\text{gen}(\theta^{*})
is the generalization error, defined as the absolute difference between the true loss
\mathcal{L}(\theta^{*})
(expected loss over the true data distribution) and the empirical loss
\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta^{*})
(average loss over the
M
training samples):
\text{gen}(\theta^{*})=|\mathcal{L}(\theta^{*})-\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta^{*})|
.

A.2
Key Definitions and Assumptions

The terms and assumptions underpinning Theorem 4 are crucial for its interpretation and subsequent adaptation:

•

S_{n}
–Equivariant QNN:
The QNN, denoted
U(\theta)
, is assumed to operate on
n
qubits and possess
S_{n}
-equivariance. This means that the QNN’s action commutes with the permutation of qubits. Specifically, each layer of the QNN is generated by
S_{n}
-equivariant Hermitian operators (e.g., from the set
\mathcal{G}=\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}X_{j},\;\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}Y_{j},\;\frac{2}{n(n-1)}\sum_{k<j}Z_{j}Z_{k}\right\}
. Furthermore, the measurement observable
O
used in the loss function is also
S_{n}
-equivariant (e.g., from the set

\mathcal{M}=\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j},\;\frac{2}{n(n-1)}\sum_{k<j,j=1}^{n}x_{j}x_{k},\;\prod_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}\right\}

Mathematically,
S_{n}
-equivariance implies that for any permutation
\pi\in S_{n}
and its corresponding unitary representation
R(\pi)
acting on the
n
qubits, the QNN satisfies

U(\theta)R(\pi)\rho R(\pi)^{\dagger}U(\theta)^{\dagger}=R(\pi)U(\theta)\rho U(\theta)^{\dagger}R(\pi)^{\dagger}

•

T_{e_{n+1}}
(Tetrahedral Numbers):
This term,
T_{e_{n+1}}=\binom{n+3}{3}
, represents the dimension of the submanifold of
S_{n}
-equivariant unitaries. It is a critical parameter as it quantifies the effective complexity or the number of independent degrees of freedom of the
S_{n}
-equivariant QNN. For large
n
,
T_{e_{n+1}}
scales as
\Theta(n^{3})
. This polynomial scaling, in contrast to the exponential dimension of the space of general
n
-qubit unitaries (
4^{n}-1
for
SU(2^{n})
), is a direct consequence of the symmetry constraint and is fundamental to the favorable generalization properties.

•

M
:
This denotes the number of training samples drawn from the source task’s data distribution, used to train the QNN and obtain the parameters
\theta^{*}
.

•

Loss Function:
The QML model employs a loss function of the form
l_{\theta}(\rho_{i})=\mathrm{Tr}[\mathcal{U}_{\theta}(\rho_{i})O]
, where
\mathcal{U}_{\theta}(\rho_{i})=U(\theta)\rho_{i}U(\theta)^{\dagger}
is the evolution of an input state
\rho_{i}
under the QNN. The observable
O
is assumed to have a bounded operator norm, and the coefficients
c_{i}
in the empirical loss
\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta)=\sum_{i=1}^{M}c_{i}l_{\theta}(\rho_{i})
are also bounded (e.g.,
|c_{i}|\leq 1/M
for classification tasks).

A.3
Prook Sketch and Underlying Mathematical Concepts

The proof of Theorem 4, as detailed in the methods section (specifically referencing their Theorem 5 for the covering number bound), relies on bounding the
\epsilon
-covering number of the set of
S_{n}
-equivariant QNNs. A covering number
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{V},d,\epsilon)
is the minimum number of balls of radius
\epsilon
(with respect to a metric
d
) needed to cover the set
\mathcal{V}

[
18
]
.

1.

Block-Diagonal Structure:
A cornerstone of the argument is the representation theory of
S_{n}
. An
S_{n}
-equivariant unitary
U(\theta)
can be decomposed into a block-diagonal form

U(\theta)\cong\bigoplus_{\lambda}I_{m_{\lambda}}\otimes U_{\lambda}(\theta).

Here,
\lambda
labels the irreducible representations (irreps) of
S_{n}
that appear in the decomposition of the
n
-qubit Hilbert space,
I_{m_{\lambda}}
is an identity matrix of dimension
m_{\lambda}
(the multiplicity of the irrep
r_{\lambda}
of
S_{n}
which corresponds to the dimension of an irrep of
U(2)
by Schur-Weyl duality), and
U_{\lambda}(\theta)
are
d_{\lambda}
-dimensional unitary matrices (
d_{\lambda}
being the dimension of the irrep
r_{\lambda}
of
S_{n}
, which corresponds to the multiplicity of an irrep of
U(2)
). The paper
[
18
]
uses
d_{\lambda}
for the dimension of
U_{\lambda}(\theta)
and
m_{\lambda}
for its repetition factor.

2.

Covering Number Bound:
The
\epsilon
-covering number of the set
\mathcal{V}_{n}
of
n
-qubit unitary
S_{n}
-equivariant QNNs with respect to the operator norm
\|\cdot\|
is bounded as:

\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{V}_{n},\|\cdot\|,\epsilon)\leq\left(\frac{6}{\epsilon}\right)^{2\sum_{\lambda}d_{\lambda}^{2}}.

The sum
\sum_{\lambda}d_{\lambda}^{2}
is precisely the Tetrahedral number
T_{e_{n+1}}
. This step is crucial: it shows that the “size” of the function class implementable by
S_{n}
-equivariant QNNs scales polynomially with
n
(via
T_{e_{n+1}}\sim n^{3}
).

3.

Generalization Bound from Covering Numbers:
This polynomial bound on the covering number is then leveraged within standard machine learning theory frameworks that relate covering numbers to generalization error (e.g., based on Rademacher complexity or VC dimension, though directly applied via a result from their reference which uses covering numbers). Smaller covering numbers imply that the function class is less complex, and thus fewer samples are needed to achieve good generalization.

The overall trainability and expressibility context of
S_{n}
-equivariant QNNs also relies on the assumption that the QNN ansatz can form approximate 2-designs on each isotypic block (i.e., for each
U_{\lambda}(\theta)
). This implies that the QNN is sufficiently expressive within the symmetry-constrained subspaces. While the proof of Theorem 4 itself focuses on the covering number of the unitary manifold, this underlying expressivity ensures that the QNN can effectively explore this manifold.

A.4
Significance for QML

Theorem 4 carries profound implications for quantum machine learning. It demonstrates that by strategically incorporating problem symmetries (specifically
S_{n}
-equivariance) into the QNN architecture, it is possible to achieve generalization from a number of training samples M that scales only polynomially with the problem size
n
(specifically,
M\in\mathcal{O}(n^{3})
for fixed error
\epsilon
and confidence
\delta
). This contrasts sharply with generic, problem-agnostic QNNs, which may suffer from barren plateaus and could require an exponential number of training samples to generalize effectively due to their vast expressivity exploring an exponentially large hypothesis space. The reduction in effective model complexity from exponential to polynomial, quantified by
Te_{n+1}
, is the key to this improved sample efficiency. This finding strongly advocates for the principles of Geometric Quantum Machine Learning (GQML), where symmetries are encoded as geometric priors into learning architectures

Supplementary Information B
TSP Encoding and Equivariance

For completeness, we describe the encoding of the Traveling Salesman Problem (TSP) used in our formulation and its connection to permutation symmetry.

•

Qubit Representation:
For problems exhibiting
S_{k}
symmetry (permutation symmetry over
k
items), a natural encoding involves associating one qubit with each of the
k
cities. Thus, an
n
-city TSP is encoded using
N_{q}(n)=n
qubits, and an
m
-city TSP uses
N_{q}(m)=m
qubits. Under this encoding, a permutation of city labels directly corresponds to a permutation of the qubits.

•

Symmetry of TSP:
The TSP itself is inherently
S_{k}
-symmetric: permuting the labels of the cities does not change the set of possible tours or the length of the optimal tour; it only relabels the nodes in that tour. The reward function for a
k
-city TSP must reflect this
S_{k}
invariance.

•

Agent’s Policy Symmetry:
The QRL agent’s policy
\pi_{\theta}
should ideally also be
S_{k}
-invariant. This means that if the cities are relabeled (qubits permuted), the optimal action (choice of next city) should transform consistently with the relabeling. An
S_{k}
-equivariant QNN is a means to construct such an
S_{k}
-invariant policy.

Supplementary Information C
Detailed Assumptions for the Transfer Bound

To establish a theoretical performance bound for zero-shot transfer in TSP Quantum Reinforcement Learning (QRL), we introduce the following assumptions. These formal conditions map the supervised generalization framework of
[
18
]
, derived for
S_{n}
-equivariant quantum neural networks (QNNs), to the reinforcement learning setting with sequential structure and policy execution.

(A1)

Equivariant Architecture.
The QNN component
U(\theta)
of the QRL agent trained on
n
qubits is
S_{n}
-equivariant. That is, each unitary layer is generated by operators invariant under all permutations
\pi\in S_{n}
. Typical examples include terms of the form
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}X_{j}
,
\frac{2}{n(n-1)}\sum_{j<k}Z_{j}Z_{k}
, and similarly for
Y
or other Hermitian generators. The measurement observable is also assumed to be
S_{n}
-equivariant.

(A2)

Generalisation on the
n
-city source task.

(A2-i) Fresh-instance regime (i.i.d. episodes).

The agent is trained for
M_{n}
episodes, each started on an independently
drawn instance
\mathcal{I}^{(e)}\sim\mathcal{D}_{n}
; the exploratory policy
is fixed
a priori
.
Episode returns
\{R^{(e)}\}_{e=1}^{M_{n}}
are therefore i.i.d. and
bounded in
[0,1]
. Hence Theorem 4 of

[
18
]
gives, with probability
\geq 1-\delta
,

\left|\widehat{P}_{n}(\theta_{n}^{*})-P_{n}(\theta_{n}^{*})\right|\leq\mathcal{G}_{n}^{\mathrm{(iid)}}(\delta),\quad\mathcal{G}_{n}^{\mathrm{(iid)}}(\delta)=\mathcal{O}\!\Bigl(\sqrt{\tfrac{T_{e_{n+1}}}{M_{n}}}+\sqrt{\tfrac{\log(1/\delta)}{M_{n}}}\Bigr).

(A2-ii) Finite-pool replay regime.

Instead, suppose only a pool

\mathcal{P}_{n}=\{\mathcal{I}^{(1)},\dots,\mathcal{I}^{(N_{\text{pool}})}\}

of
N_{\text{pool}}
distinct instances is available and each is replayed

K
times (
M_{n}=N_{\text{pool}}K
).
Define the
per-instance
empirical reward

\widehat{P}_{\mathcal{P}}(\theta):=\frac{1}{N_{\text{pool}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{pool}}}R(\theta,\mathcal{I}^{(i)}).

The replayed returns are correlated, but the
N_{\text{pool}}
instance
averages remain i.i.d.
Hence the same theorem yields

\left|\widehat{P}_{\mathcal{P}}(\theta_{n}^{*})-P_{n}(\theta_{n}^{*})\right|\leq\mathcal{G}_{n}^{\mathrm{(pool)}}(\delta),\quad\mathcal{G}_{n}^{\mathrm{(pool)}}(\delta)=\mathcal{O}\!\Bigl(\sqrt{\tfrac{T_{e_{n+1}}}{N_{\text{pool}}}}+\sqrt{\tfrac{\log(1/\delta)}{N_{\text{pool}}}}\Bigr).

(A2-iii) Online Q-learning with dependent returns

If episodes are generated on a single instance or the policy is
updated on-the-fly, the return sequence becomes
\beta
-mixing with
mixing time
\tau_{\beta}
.
Applying uniform-stability results for

\beta
-mixing processes

[
38
,
39
]
gives

\left|\widehat{P}_{n}(\theta_{n}^{*})-P_{n}(\theta_{n}^{*})\right|\leq\mathcal{G}_{n}^{\mathrm{(mix)}}(\delta),\quad\mathcal{G}_{n}^{\mathrm{(mix)}}(\delta)=\mathcal{O}\!\Bigl(\sqrt{\tfrac{T_{e_{n+1}}\;\tau_{\beta}}{M_{n}}}\Bigr).

In all cases, the right-hand side
\mathcal{G}_{n}(\delta)
replaces the
generalisation term in subsequent bounds.

(A3)

Equivariant Problem Encoding.
The
k
-city TSP is encoded using
k
qubits, with each city mapped to a distinct qubit. The reward function is invariant under permutations of city indices, implying
S_{k}
-invariance of the TSP instance. This ensures alignment between the task symmetry and QNN symmetry.

Remark 1 (Circuit expressibility).

Theorem 4 of
[
18
]
assumes that each intermediate QNN layer ensemble approximates a 2-design. This assumption may fail under excessive policy updates due to expressibility saturation. To preserve anticoncentration and avoid barren plateaus
[
40
,
41
]
, we restrict the EQC depth to
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
, ensuring the circuit remains expressible and gradients remain informative.

(A4)

Policy Invariance.
The policy
\pi_{\theta}
induced by the QNN and measurement process is
S_{k}
-invariant. This follows from the equivariant structure of the QNN and the symmetry of the reward function, as formalized in Lemma 1 of
[
18
]
.

(A5)

Equivariant Transfer to Target Task.
The
S_{n}
-equivariant QNN is extended to
m
qubits (
m>n
) by scaling the generator definitions. Specifically, each generator of the form
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}O_{j}
is replaced by
\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}O_{j}
, where
O_{j}
is a local Hermitian operator (e.g.,
X_{j}
,
Y_{j}
,
Z_{j}
). The learned parameters
\theta_{n}^{*}
are applied directly to the
m
-qubit architecture without modification. The resulting QNN remains
S_{m}
-equivariant by construction.

(A6)

Smoothness of Optimal Performance Across Problem Sizes.

We assume that the
n
-city and
m
-city TSP instances are drawn from the same distribution family, specifically, from the class of
Euclidean TSPs
, where cities are sampled i.i.d. from a compact domain (e.g., the unit square in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
), and edge weights correspond to Euclidean distances.

Under this setting, the classical
Beardwood–Halton–Hammersley (BHH)
theorem
[
28
]
guarantees that the expected length of the optimal tour through
k
such points satisfies:

L_{k}^{\text{opt}}\sim\beta_{2}\sqrt{k},\quad\text{as }k\to\infty,

where
\beta_{2}>0
is a universal constant (estimated to lie within the range
0.625<\beta_{2}<0.903

[
42
]
). This implies that the optimal tour length grows sublinearly and smoothly with problem size.

We define the normalized optimal performance metric as:

P_{k}^{\text{opt}}:=1-\frac{L_{k}^{\text{opt}}-L_{\text{opt}}}{L_{\max}-L_{\text{opt}}}\in[0,1],

where
L_{\text{opt}}
and
L_{\max}
are fixed deterministic lower and upper bounds on the tour length (e.g., based on MST and heuristic baselines).

This theorem makes no formal statement about the scaling for small, finite
k
, and rigorous non-asymptotic bounds are an area of active research. Based on a heuristic first-order approximation of the asymptotic form, we conjecture the following non-asymptotic high-probability bound for the normalized optimal performance metric.

Applying the Lipschitz continuity of the square-root function and concentration bounds for subadditive Euclidean functionals
[
43
,
44
]
, we obtain the following high-probability bound:

\big|P_{n}^{\text{opt}}-P_{m}^{\text{opt}}\big|\leq C^{\prime}\cdot\frac{m-n}{\sqrt{n}},

for some constant
C^{\prime}>0
depending on
\beta_{2}
,
L_{\max}-L_{\text{opt}}
, and the deviation margin from concentration.

This assumption ensures that the optimal policy landscape evolves continuously and smoothly with increasing problem size. It rules out abrupt phase transitions or sharp structural changes in optimal solutions as
n\to m
.

(A7)

Performance-metric Regularity.
For every city count
k
:

(a)

Parameter-space Lipschitzness.

There exists a constant
L_{\theta}>0
such that

\big|P_{k}(\theta)-P_{k}(\theta^{\prime})\big|\leq L_{\theta}\,\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|_{2}\quad\forall\,\theta,\theta^{\prime},

where
\|\cdot\|_{2}
denotes the Euclidean norm.
This holds because each parametrized layer in the EQC is generated by an operator of unit spectral norm and the reward has been rescaled to
[0,1]

[
45
,
46
]
.

(b)

Instance-space Lipschitzness.

Let a TSP instance be represented by its distance matrix
D\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}
.
For any two instances with the same
k
,

\big|P_{k}(\theta;D)-P_{k}(\theta;D^{\prime})\big|\leq L_{D}\,\|D-D^{\prime}\|_{F}\quad\forall\,\theta,

where
\|\cdot\|_{F}
is the Frobenius norm and
L_{D}=k/(L_{\max}-L_{\text{opt}})
. This follows from the fact that tour length is 1-Lipschitz in each edge weight, and
P_{k}
is an affine rescaling of tour length
[
47
]
.

(c)

Unitary-space Lipschitzness.

There exists a constant
L_{U}>0
such that for any two unitaries
U,U^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}(2^{k})
,

\big|P_{k}(U)-P_{k}(U^{\prime})\big|\leq L_{U}\,\|U-U^{\prime}\|,

where
\|\cdot\|
denotes the operator norm. This holds when
P_{k}(U)=\mathrm{Tr}(MU\rho U^{\dagger})
for bounded measurement observable
M
and density operator
\rho
, due to:

\big|\mathrm{Tr}(MU\rho U^{\dagger})-\mathrm{Tr}(MU^{\prime}\rho U^{\prime\dagger})\big|\leq\|M\|\cdot\|U\rho U^{\dagger}-U^{\prime}\rho U^{\prime\dagger}\|.

(d)

Size-smoothness of Optimal Performance.

Let
L_{k}^{\mathrm{opt}}
denote the optimal tour length on
k
-city Euclidean TSP instances.
By the Beardwood–Halton–Hammersley theorem
[
28
]
,
there exists a constant
c>0
such that

L_{k}^{\mathrm{opt}}=c\sqrt{k}(1+o(1))
almost surely.
Assuming the performance metric
P_{k}
is an affine rescaling of
L_{k}^{\mathrm{opt}}

(as in (A7-2)), there is a constant
C^{\prime}>0
such that for
m>n
,

\big|P_{n}(\theta_{n}^{\mathrm{opt}})-P_{m}(\theta_{m}^{\mathrm{opt}})\big|\;\leq\;C^{\prime}\,\frac{m-n}{\sqrt{n}}.

This captures the structural smoothness of the performance landscape across problem sizes.

Together, (A7-1)–(A7-4) ensure that the performance metric
P_{k}
varies smoothly under small changes in parameters, input instances, or unitary representations. These regularity properties are critical for applying generalization and transfer bounds.

Among these, assumption
(A5)
plays a particularly critical role in enabling zero-shot transfer. If instead one were to apply the
n
-qubit QNN directly to a padded or projected subspace of the
m
-qubit system, the
S_{m}
-symmetry would be violated, and the equivariant structure required to invoke Appendix
A
would no longer hold. By scaling generator definitions appropriately (e.g., using
\frac{1}{m}\sum X_{j}
in place of
\frac{1}{n}\sum X_{j}
), we preserve the
S_{m}
-equivariance of the circuit. The parameters
\theta_{n}^{*}
, although trained on a different operator basis, now act on a structurally aligned QNN.

This semantic mismatch, arising from reusing
\theta_{n}^{*}
on scaled generators, is a primary source of performance divergence. Since
\frac{1}{n}\sum X_{j}
and
\frac{1}{m}\sum X_{j}
are distinct operators for
m\neq n
, the layerwise transformations differ, even though the parameter values remain unchanged. This effect will be explicitly captured as a task dissimilarity term in the final generalization-transfer bound.

Supplementary Information D
Quantifying Task Dissimilarity
\mathcal{D}_{n\rightarrow m}

A critical component in bridging the gap between a single-task generalization bound
[
18
]
and a transfer learning bound is the introduction of a term that quantifies the “dissimilarity” or “transfer penalty” incurred when applying a model trained on a source task
T_{n}
to a target task
T_{m}
. Let this term be denoted
\mathcal{D}_{n\rightarrow m}(\theta_{n}^{*})
, representing an upper bound on the expected performance drop due to the transfer itself, even if
\theta_{n}^{*}
were perfectly optimal for
T_{n}
.

This dissimilarity arises from several interconnected factors:

1.

Change in Problem Scale and Structure:
The most obvious difference is the increase in the number of cities from
n
to
m
. This fundamentally alters the problem:

•

The search space of possible tours grows factorially (from
(n-1)!/2
to
(m-1)!/2
).

•

The geometric or combinatorial structure of optimal (or near-optimal) solutions can change. Features or patterns relevant for small
n
might be less relevant or need to be combined differently for larger
m
.

2.

Parameter Mismatch due to Generator Adaptation:
As per Assumption 5 (A5), the parameters
\theta_{n}^{*}
were optimized for QNN layers involving
n
-scaled generators (e.g.,
H_{l}^{(n)}
). When the same
\theta_{n}^{*}
are used with
m
-scaled generators (e.g.,
H_{l}^{(m)}
) for the
m
-city problem, the resulting unitary evolution

U_{m}(\theta_{n}^{*})=\prod_{l}e^{-i\theta_{n,l}H_{l}^{(m)}}

will generally be different from what would be optimal for the
m
-city problem. The parameters
\theta_{n}^{*}
are “tuned” to the specific dynamics and reward landscape of the
n
-city problem interacting with
H_{l}^{(n)}
; this tuning is now applied to
H_{l}^{(m)}
. This is a direct source of sub-optimality.

3.

Representational Capacity Mismatch:
The
S_{n}
-equivariant QNN used for the source task has an effective complexity related to
T_{e_{n+1}}\sim n^{3}
. An optimal QRL agent for the
m
-city TSP might ideally require an
S_{m}
-equivariant QNN with complexity related to
T_{e_{m+1}}\sim m^{3}
. Since
m>n
,
T_{e_{m+1}}>T_{e_{n+1}}
. The model trained on
n
cities, even if its parameters
\theta_{n}^{*}
are used within an
S_{m}
-form QNN, might inherently lack the representational capacity to express the optimal policy for the more complex
m
-city problem if the number of parameters in
\theta_{n}^{*}
is tied to the
n
-city complexity. However, if
\theta_{n}^{*}
has a fixed number of parameters, and these are applied to the adapted
m
-city generators, the issue is more about whether these specific parameter values are suitable for the
m
-city problem’s landscape, rather than the number of parameters itself being insufficient (as the number of parameters in
\theta_{n}^{*}
is fixed). The crucial aspect is that the
function realized
by
U_{m}(\theta_{n}^{*})
might be far from the optimal function
U_{m}(\theta_{m}^{\text{opt}})
.

The term
\mathcal{D}_{n\rightarrow m}
aims to capture the performance degradation due to these factors. Drawing parallels with classical transfer learning theory,
\mathcal{D}_{n\rightarrow m}
acts analogously to a domain discrepancy or a bias introduced by transferring a representation learned for one distribution (or task difficulty) to another
[
37
]
. For instance, one might compare this to the “Sim-to-Real Gap” where performance degradation occurs as source and target domains diverge significantly
[
36
]
. In our case, the “divergence” is primarily driven by the change in problem size (
n\rightarrow m
) and the consequent structural and parametric mismatches. The term
d_{\mathcal{F},\mathcal{F}_{0}}(h^{\prime};h)

[
37
]
, representing the worst-case representation difference, serves a similar conceptual purpose to our
\mathcal{D}_{n\rightarrow m}
.

The formulation of
\mathcal{D}_{n\rightarrow m}
should ideally depend on:

•

The sizes
n
and
m
.

•

The complexities of the QNNs involved, characterized by
T_{e_{n+1}}
and
T_{e_{m+1}}
.

•

Properties of the TSP instances (e.g., range of edge weights, geometric distribution of cities), which might be encapsulated in a constant or a slowly varying function.

•

The specific parameters
\theta_{n}^{*}
and the QNN architecture (implicitly, as these define the function being transferred).

The central challenge lies in formulating a mathematically rigorous and practically meaningful expression for the task dissimilarity term
\mathcal{D}_{n\rightarrow m}
. This quantity should capture the extent to which transferring parameters
\theta_{n}^{*}
, trained on the
n
-city task
T_{n}
, to an
m
-city QNN with appropriately scaled
S_{m}
-equivariant generators affects downstream performance. Crucially, the interaction between fixed parameters
\theta_{n}^{*}
and the modified generator definitions for
T_{m}
introduces architectural shifts that are not trivially reducible to source-task generalization behavior. In particular, a parameter set
\theta_{n}^{*}
with high generalization error on
T_{n}
may, under certain conditions, exhibit unexpectedly high or low performance on
T_{m}
, depending on the alignment between learned symmetries and the target task structure. A principled performance bound must therefore characterize the expected or worst-case degradation, under reasonable regularity assumptions such as task smoothness or structural continuity across problem sizes.

Supplementary Information E
Decomposition of Performance Degradation

We begin by analyzing the sources of performance degradation when transferring a trained QRL policy from an
n
-city problem to an
m
-city instance. Let
\theta_{n}^{*}
denote the parameters obtained after training on the source task. The total transfer gap is defined as the difference in performance across tasks under the same transferred policy:

\Delta P_{n\to m}(\theta_{n}^{*}):=P_{n}(U_{n}(\theta_{n}^{*}))-P_{m}(U_{m}(\theta_{n}^{*})).

To isolate the impact of imperfect training, we add and subtract the source optimal value
P_{n}^{\mathrm{opt}}
, yielding:

\Delta P_{n\to m}(\theta_{n}^{*})=\underbrace{P_{n}^{\mathrm{opt}}-P_{m}(U_{m}(\theta_{n}^{*}))}_{\text{Performance degradation from optimal source}}-\underbrace{\left(P_{n}^{\mathrm{opt}}-P_{n}(U_{n}(\theta_{n}^{*}))\right)}_{\text{Source sub-optimality}~(\varepsilon_{n})}.

The second term,
\varepsilon_{n}
, captures the residual optimality gap due to imperfect training on the source task. This decomposition separates the effects of sub-optimal training from the impact of architectural and problem-size mismatch. We now refine the remaining term to better understand transfer-induced degradation.

Transition to Policy-Level Decomposition.

To analyze how
\theta_{n}^{*}
performs when transferred to an
m
-city architecture, we first lift the source unitary into the target Hilbert space:

U_{n}^{\uparrow}(\theta_{n}^{*}):=U_{n}(\theta_{n}^{*})\otimes\mathbb{I}_{2^{m-n}},

which embeds the
n
-qubit policy into the
m
-qubit system. However, this lifted operator does not respect the
S_{m}
-symmetry that is intrinsic to the target task.

To conceptually align it with the target’s permutation symmetry, we define its group-twirled channel
[
48
]
:

\tilde{\mathcal{U}}_{n}^{\uparrow}(\rho):=\frac{1}{|S_{m}|}\sum_{\pi\in S_{m}}P_{\pi}U_{n}^{\uparrow}(\theta_{n}^{*})P_{\pi}^{\dagger}\,\rho\,P_{\pi}U_{n}^{\uparrow}(\theta_{n}^{*})^{\dagger}P_{\pi}^{\dagger},

where
P_{\pi}
denotes the permutation operator for
\pi\in S_{m}
.
Because both the environment dynamics and the performance functional
P_{m}(\cdot)
are assumed permutation-invariant (Assumption A1), the twirling does not change the achieved performance:

P_{m}(\tilde{\mathcal{U}}_{n}^{\uparrow})=\frac{1}{|S_{m}|}\sum_{\pi\in S_{m}}P_{m}(P_{\pi}U_{n}^{\uparrow}P_{\pi}^{\dagger})=P_{m}(U_{n}^{\uparrow}).

This lets us insert a symmetry-aligned intermediate policy between the source and target policies, and algebraically decompose the transfer gap as:

\displaystyle P_{n}(U_{n}(\theta_{n}^{*}))-P_{m}(U_{m}(\theta_{m}^{*}))

\displaystyle=\underbrace{P_{n}(U_{n}(\theta_{n}^{*}))-P_{m}(\tilde{\mathcal{U}}_{n}^{\uparrow})}_{\text{Structural task difference}}

\displaystyle\quad+\underbrace{P_{m}(\tilde{\mathcal{U}}_{n}^{\uparrow})-P_{m}(U_{m}(\theta_{m}^{*}))}_{\text{Parametric mismatch}}.

The first term captures the loss purely from distributional or structural shifts between the
n
- and
m
-city tasks, even after symmetry alignment. The second term isolates the additional loss from reusing the source-trained parameters
\theta_{n}^{*}
instead of the target-optimal
\theta_{m}^{*}
.

Together with the source sub-optimality term
\varepsilon_{n}
, these components form a complete decomposition of zero-shot transfer loss, guiding the analysis and empirical results in the following sections.

Remark 1
(On Notation Simplification)

Since the performance functional
P_{m}(\cdot)
is permutation-invariant (Assumption A1),
the group-twirled channel
\tilde{\mathcal{U}}_{n}^{\uparrow}
and the lifted unitary
U_{n}^{\uparrow}
achieve the same performance:

P_{m}(\tilde{\mathcal{U}}_{n}^{\uparrow})=P_{m}(U_{n}^{\uparrow})
.
Therefore, for the purpose of bounding the parametric mismatch,
we henceforth work directly with
U_{n}^{\uparrow}
to simplify notation.

Bounding the Parametric Mismatch.

Let the QNN unitaries for the
n
-city and
m
-city architectures be defined as:

U_{n}(\theta_{n}^{*})=\prod_{l=1}^{d}e^{-i\theta_{n,l}^{*}H_{l}^{(n)}},\quad U_{m}(\theta_{n}^{*})=\prod_{l=1}^{d}e^{-i\theta_{n,l}^{*}H_{l}^{(m)}},

where
H_{l}^{(k)}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}A_{j}
denotes the
l
-th generator on
k
qubits, and each local Hermitian term satisfies
\|A_{j}\|\leq\|A\|_{\text{op}}
.

Step 1: Generator Difference.

We begin by bounding the difference between the two generators:

\|H_{l}^{(m)}-H_{l}^{(n)}\|=\left\|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}A_{j}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}A_{j}\right\|\leq 2\cdot\frac{m-n}{m}\cdot\|A\|_{\text{op}}.

Step 2: Non-Commuting Unitaries via Telescoping Sum.

The unitaries
U_{n}^{\uparrow}
and
U_{m}
are products of exponentials of generally non-commuting operators. To control their difference, we apply a telescoping expansion:

U_{m}-U_{n}^{\uparrow}=\sum_{l=1}^{d}\left(\prod_{j=l+1}^{d}e^{-i\theta_{n,j}^{*}H_{j}^{(m)}}\right)\left(e^{-i\theta_{n,l}^{*}H_{l}^{(m)}}-e^{-i\theta_{n,l}^{*}H_{l}^{(n)}\otimes\mathbb{I}}\right)\left(\prod_{j=1}^{l-1}e^{-i\theta_{n,j}^{*}H_{j}^{(n)}\otimes\mathbb{I}}\right).

Using the fact that all unitary factors have norm 1, we obtain:

\|U_{m}-U_{n}^{\uparrow}\|\leq\sum_{l=1}^{d}\left\|e^{-i\theta_{n,l}^{*}H_{l}^{(m)}}-e^{-i\theta_{n,l}^{*}H_{l}^{(n)}\otimes\mathbb{I}}\right\|.

Step 3: Bounding Exponential Differences.

For Hermitian operators
X
and
Y
, a standard inequality gives:

\|e^{-i\theta X}-e^{-i\theta Y}\|\leq|\theta|\cdot\|X-Y\|\cdot e^{|\theta|\cdot\max(\|X\|,\|Y\|)}.

Applying this to each layer:

\left\|e^{-i\theta_{n,l}^{*}H_{l}^{(m)}}-e^{-i\theta_{n,l}^{*}H_{l}^{(n)}\otimes\mathbb{I}}\right\|\leq|\theta_{n,l}^{*}|\cdot\|H_{l}^{(m)}-H_{l}^{(n)}\|\cdot e^{|\theta_{n,l}^{*}|\cdot\|A\|_{\text{op}}}.

Letting
\theta_{\max}:=\max_{l}|\theta_{n,l}^{*}|
, and using the generator bound above, we get:

\|U_{n}^{\uparrow}(\theta_{n}^{*})-U_{m}(\theta_{n}^{*})\|\leq 2\cdot\|A\|_{\text{op}}\cdot e^{\theta_{\max}\|A\|_{\text{op}}}\cdot\|\theta_{n}^{*}\|_{1}\cdot\frac{m-n}{m}.

Step 4: Translating Unitary Deviation to Performance Degradation.

By Assumption (A7), the performance metric
P_{k}(U)
is Lipschitz-continuous in the unitary with constant
L_{U}
. Therefore:

|P_{m}(U_{n}^{\uparrow}(\theta_{n}^{*}))-P_{m}(U_{m}(\theta_{n}^{*}))|\leq L_{U}\cdot\|U_{n}^{\uparrow}(\theta_{n}^{*})-U_{m}(\theta_{n}^{*})\|.

Final Result:

\boxed{\mathcal{D}_{n\to m}^{(\text{param})}\leq L_{U}\cdot 2\cdot\|A\|_{\text{op}}\cdot e^{\theta_{\max}\|A\|_{\text{op}}}\cdot\|\theta_{n}^{*}\|_{1}\cdot\frac{m-n}{m}}

This bound quantifies the error incurred from reusing the same trained parameters
\theta_{n}^{*}
in a new circuit with scaled generators. The bound scales linearly with the relative problem-size gap
(m-n)/m
, and with the
\ell_{1}
norm of the parameters, which reflects the circuit depth and magnitude of control angles. The exponential factor
e^{\theta_{\max}\|A\|_{\text{op}}}
ensures rigor in the non-commutative setting but remains moderate in practice (e.g.,
\leq e^{\pi}
for
|\theta|\leq\pi
and
\|A\|_{\text{op}}=1
).

Bounding the Structural Task Difference.

Assumption (A6) asserts that the optimal performance metric varies smoothly across problem sizes in Euclidean TSP settings. Specifically, leveraging the Beardwood–Halton–Hammersley theorem
[
28
]
and associated concentration bounds, we obtain the following high-probability bound on the difference in optimal performances between the
n
-city and
m
-city tasks:

\boxed{\mathcal{D}_{n\to m}^{(\text{struct})}:=\left|P_{n}(\theta_{n}^{\mathrm{opt}})-P_{m}(\theta_{m}^{\mathrm{opt}})\right|\leq C^{\prime}\cdot\frac{m-n}{\sqrt{n}}}

for some constant
C^{\prime}>0
depending on geometric properties of the TSP instance distribution and the performance metric normalization. This term captures the structural dissimilarity induced by the change in problem size, and it reflects the fact that the optimal policy landscape evolves gradually in random Euclidean TSPs without abrupt phase transitions.

Combined Transfer Gap Bound.

Combining both contributions yields the following upper bound:

\Delta P_{n\to m}(\theta_{n}^{*})\leq\mathcal{D}_{n\to m}^{(\text{param})}+\mathcal{D}_{n\to m}^{(\text{struct})},

which results in:

\boxed{\Delta P_{n\to m}(\theta_{n}^{*})\leq L_{U}\cdot\|\theta_{n}^{*}\|_{1}\cdot e^{\theta_{\max}\|A\|_{\text{op}}}\cdot 2\cdot\|A\|_{\text{op}}\cdot\frac{m-n}{m}+C^{\prime}\cdot\frac{m-n}{\sqrt{n}}}

This defines
\mathcal{D}_{n\to m}
as:

\boxed{\mathcal{D}_{n\to m}:=L_{U}\cdot\|\theta_{n}^{*}\|_{1}\cdot e^{\theta_{\max}\|A\|_{\text{op}}}\cdot 2\cdot\|A\|_{\text{op}}\cdot\frac{m-n}{m}+C^{\prime}\cdot\frac{m-n}{\sqrt{n}}}

This expression captures both the architectural shift in generator definitions and the underlying variation in problem difficulty between source and target sizes. It provides a clear pathway for quantifying the risk of zero-shot transfer in QRL with size-adaptive equivariant circuits.

Supplementary Information F
Analysis and Implications of the Derived Bound

The derived bound provides a principled characterization of the performance achievable when transferring a policy trained on
n
-city TSP instances to larger
m
-city instances using
S_{k}
-equivariant QRL agents:

P_{m}(\theta_{n}^{*})\;\geq\;\widehat{P}_{n}(\theta_{n}^{*})-\mathcal{G}_{n}(\delta)-\mathcal{D}_{n\rightarrow m},

where
\mathcal{G}_{n}(\delta)
accounts for generalization error on the source task and
\mathcal{D}_{n\rightarrow m}
quantifies the penalty incurred by transfer. The explicit form of the penalty,

\mathcal{D}_{n\to m}=L_{U}\cdot\|\theta_{n}^{*}\|_{1}\,e^{\theta_{\max}\|A\|_{\text{op}}}\cdot 2\|A\|_{\text{op}}\cdot\frac{m-n}{m}+C^{\prime}\cdot\frac{m-n}{\sqrt{n}},

exposes two distinct sources of degradation: generator mismatch between
n
- and
m
-qubit circuits, and the inherent structural shift that arises as problem size increases.

F.1
Interpretation of Components

The first contribution to performance loss,
\mathcal{G}_{n}(\delta)
, captures the discrepancy between the empirical reward observed during training and the true expected reward on the
n
-city task. It reflects the classical generalization behavior of learning systems: the effective capacity of
S_{n}
-equivariant QNNs scales as
T_{e_{n+1}}\sim n^{3}
, implying that the sample complexity grows polynomially with the problem size. As the number of training episodes
M_{n}
increases,
\mathcal{G}_{n}(\delta)
decays as
\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{M_{n}})
, showing that sufficiently extensive training can make this term negligible. Importantly, this component establishes a floor on achievable transfer performance: weak learning on the source task inevitably propagates to the target.

The second contribution,
\mathcal{D}_{n\to m}
, is the novel element of our analysis and isolates the cost of transferring parameters across problem sizes. The first term in
\mathcal{D}_{n\to m}
quantifies the generator mismatch: because the equivariant circuit generators scale as
\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{j}A_{j}
for the source and
\tfrac{1}{m}\sum_{j}A_{j}
for the target, reusing
\theta_{n}^{*}
induces a systematic discrepancy. This penalty grows linearly with
(m-n)/m
and scales with both the
\ell_{1}
norm of the parameters and their maximal magnitude, reflecting the degree to which the learned circuit is tuned to the
n
-qubit dynamics. The second term captures the structural shift between the two tasks. By appealing to Euclidean TSP regularity (Assumption A6), we bound the difference in optimal performance as
C^{\prime}(m-n)/\sqrt{n}
, which grows smoothly with the problem size gap. Together, these terms guarantee that transfer degradation is gradual rather than catastrophic, in sharp contrast with non-equivariant circuits where symmetry breaking can cause performance collapse.

F.2
Scalability Implications

The explicit decomposition of the bound yields clear insights into scalability. On the source side, the
n^{3}
dependence of
T_{e_{n+1}}
indicates that learning with EQCs is polynomial in problem size—substantially more favorable than the exponential blow-up faced by generic QNNs. On the transfer side,
\mathcal{D}_{n\to m}
shows that moderate increases in problem size (
m
close to
n
) incur only mild penalties, whereas large jumps produce more pronounced degradation. This suggests that a curriculum in which models are trained incrementally on growing
n
is more effective than attempting a single leap from very small to very large instances.

F.3
Design Principles for Transferable QRL

Beyond asymptotic considerations, the bound highlights several principles for constructing transferable QRL agents. First, transfer is most reliable when source and target sizes are close, since both penalty terms vanish as
m\to n
. Second, enforcing equivariance is crucial: it ensures that the learned policy remains insensitive to permutations of city labels and that the architecture scales consistently with problem size. Third, regularization of parameter magnitudes during training can reduce generator mismatch, directly lowering the leading term of
\mathcal{D}_{n\to m}
. Finally, the assumption of smooth task families, such as uniformly random Euclidean TSPs, underpins the validity of the structural term. For distributions exhibiting abrupt combinatorial transitions, transfer penalties may be underestimated, underscoring the importance of choosing problem families with stable local statistics when seeking transferability.

F.4
Symmetry and Data Assumptions

The effectiveness of cross-size transfer ultimately hinges on symmetry preservation and data assumptions. The TSP is invariant under permutations of city labels, inducing
S_{k}
symmetry for any
k
-city instance. Our QNNs are designed to be
S_{n}
-equivariant at training time and, by scaling the generators appropriately,
S_{m}
-equivariant at deployment. This architectural consistency ensures that symmetry-preserving inductive biases are retained during transfer. If equivariance were broken—for example, by embedding an
n
-qubit circuit into a larger system without adapting generators—the penalty
\mathcal{D}_{n\to m}
could grow without bound.

Distributional assumptions also play a critical role. Whereas prior generalization results
[
18
]
are framed in supervised learning with i.i.d. samples, QRL operates in a sequential setting with non-stationary trajectories. We assume that training on
n
-city TSPs involves sufficient exploration and episodes to approximate the optimal value function well. The symmetry-preserving design of EQCs helps avoid barren plateaus and provides a rich hypothesis space, making this assumption reasonable. When deployed on larger
m
-city tasks, the shared symmetry ensures that learned strategies remain semantically relevant, with performance loss captured precisely by
\mathcal{D}_{n\to m}
.

F.5
Transfer Feasibility

The derived bound thus provides a quantitative roadmap for scalable quantum reinforcement learning. If
\mathcal{D}_{n\to m}
grows polynomially—as our analysis indicates—then zero-shot transfer is feasible across modest size differences, while fine-tuning can recover performance for larger gaps. In contrast, if transfer costs grow sharply with
m/n
, even equivariant circuits would be limited to near-sized transfers. Our results therefore motivate a practical strategy: train EQC-based policies on moderately large instances, and extend to larger ones incrementally within the range where
\mathcal{D}_{n\to m}
remains controlled. This balance between transfer cost and transfer feasibility underpins the scalability of QRL for combinatorial optimization.

Supplementary Information G
Incorporating the Theoretical Performance Degradation Bound

To strengthen the connection between the theoretical analysis and the empirical results, this section introduces a methodology for augmenting the performance plots with a theoretically derived degradation curve. We operationalize the abstract transfer penalty term
\mathcal{D}_{n\rightarrow m}
by providing principled estimates for its constituent constants. This enables a direct comparison between the predicted theoretical performance loss and the observed empirical results, offering a quantitative validation of the theoretical framework.

G.1
Operationalizing the Transfer Penalty Term
\mathcal{D}_{n\rightarrow m}

The central theoretical result is a lower bound on the zero-shot transfer performance, given by

P_{m}(\theta^{*}_{n})\geq\hat{P}_{n}(\theta_{n}^{*})-\mathcal{G}_{n}(\delta)-\mathcal{D}_{n\rightarrow m}

To visualize this bound, it is necessary to compute a numerical value for the transfer penalty,
\mathcal{D}_{n\rightarrow m}
.

As in Section
4.2
, this term is composed of two distinct contributions: a parametric mismatch penalty and a structural task difference penalty:

\mathcal{D}_{n\rightarrow m}=\mathcal{D}_{n\rightarrow m}^{\text{(param)}}+\mathcal{D}_{n\rightarrow m}^{\text{(struct)}}

where:

\mathcal{D}_{n\to m}^{(\text{param})}\leq L_{U}\cdot 2\cdot\|A\|_{\text{op}}\cdot e^{\theta_{\max}\|A\|_{\text{op}}}\cdot\|\theta_{n}^{*}\|_{1}\cdot\frac{m-n}{m}

and

\mathcal{D}_{n\to m}^{(\text{struct})}:=\left|P_{n}(\theta_{n}^{\mathrm{opt}})-P_{m}(\theta_{m}^{\mathrm{opt}})\right|\leq C^{\prime}\cdot\frac{m-n}{\sqrt{n}}

G.1.1
Estimating the Unitary Lipschitz Constant
L_{U}

The constant
L_{U}
quantifies the sensitivity of the performance metric to changes in the circuit unitary, as defined by the inequality
|P_{k}(U)-P_{k}(U^{\prime})\leq L_{U}||U-U^{\prime}||
. While a precise calculation of this constant for an arbitrary model is computationally hard, a reasonable and well-justified upper bound can be established from the definition of the performance metric itself. The performance is given by the expectation value
P_{k}(U)=\text{Tr}(MU\rho U^{\dagger})
, where
M
is the measurement observable and
\rho
is the initial state. The difference in performance for two unitaries
U
and
U^{\prime}
can be bounded as follows:

\displaystyle\left|P_{k}(U)-P_{k}(U^{\prime})\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\operatorname{Tr}\left(M(U\rho U^{\dagger}-U^{\prime}\rho U^{\prime\dagger})\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\|M\|\cdot\|U\rho U^{\dagger}-U^{\prime}\rho U^{\prime\dagger}\|_{tr}

\displaystyle=\|M\|\cdot\|U\rho U^{\dagger}-U\rho U^{\prime\dagger}+U\rho U^{\prime\dagger}-U^{\prime}\rho U^{\prime\dagger}\|_{tr}

\displaystyle\leq\|M\|\cdot\left(\|U\rho(U-U^{\prime})^{\dagger}\|_{tr}+\|(U-U^{\prime})\rho U^{\prime\dagger}\|_{tr}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\|M\|\cdot\left(\|U\|\cdot\|\rho\|_{tr}\cdot\|(U-U^{\prime})^{\dagger}\|+\|U-U^{\prime}\|\cdot\|\rho\|_{tr}\cdot\|U^{\prime\dagger}\|\right)

Since
U
and
U^{\prime}
are unitary (
\|U\|=\|U^{\prime}\|=1
) and
\rho
is a density matrix (
\|\rho\|_{tr}=1
), this simplifies to:

\left|P_{k}(U)-P_{k}(U^{\prime})\right|\leq\|M\|\cdot\left(\|U-U^{\prime}\|+\|U-U^{\prime}\|\right)=2\|M\|\cdot\|U-U^{\prime}\|

This establishes an upper bound for the Lipschitz constant as
L_{U}\leq 2||M||
. This approach is consistent with general treatments of Lipschitz continuity in quantum machine learning models
[
49
,
50
,
51
]
.

The preceding analysis establishes the theoretical foundation for the performance bound, demonstrating that the parametric degradation penalty is proportional to the term
(m-n)/m
. The full coefficient for this term is a complex quantity involving the unitary Lipschitz constant (
L_{U}\leq 2\|M\|_{op}
), the norm of the learned parameters (
\|\theta\|
), and other model-specific properties.

Rather than relying on potentially loose worst-case estimates for these individual constants, we consolidate them into a single, effective scaling factor,
\alpha_{n}
, for each source model size
n
. This allows us to construct a tight and illustrative bound that retains the theoretically-derived structure, while pragmatically accounting for the difficulty in calculating the precise value of the overall coefficient. This
\alpha_{n}
is therefore best understood as the empirically measured strength of the parametric penalty for a given source model.

G.1.2
Estimating the Structural Dissimilarity Constant
C^{\prime}

The constant
C^{\prime}
captures the intrinsic change in the optimal tour quality as the problem size scales from
n
to
m
. Its definition arises from Assumption (A6), which posits that
|P_{n}^{\text{opt}}-P_{m}^{\text{opt}}|\leq C^{\prime}\frac{m-n}{\sqrt{n}}
. This assumption is grounded in the Beardwood-Halton-Hammersley (BHH) theorem, which for the Euclidean TSPs with points sampled i.i.d. from the unit square, states that the optimal tour length
L_{k}^{opt}
scales asymptotically as
L^{opt}_{k}\approx\beta_{2}\sqrt{k}

[
52
,
28
]
.

We can use this relationship to derive an estimate for
C^{\prime}
. The performance metric is normalized as
P_{k}^{\text{opt}}:=1-\frac{L_{k}^{\text{opt}}-L_{\text{opt}}}{L_{\max}-L_{\text{opt}}}
, where
L_{opt}
and
L_{max}
are fixed lower and upper bounds on the tour length. The difference in optimal performance is then:

|P_{n}^{\text{opt}}-P_{m}^{\text{opt}}|=\left|\left(1-\frac{L_{n}^{\text{opt}}-L_{\text{opt}}}{L_{\text{max}}-L_{\text{opt}}}\right)-\left(1-\frac{L_{m}^{\text{opt}}-L_{\text{opt}}}{L_{\text{max}}-L_{\text{opt}}}\right)\right|=\frac{|L_{m}^{\text{opt}}-L_{n}^{\text{opt}}|}{L_{\text{max}}-L_{\text{opt}}}

Substituting the BHH approximation, we get:

|P_{n}^{\text{opt}}-P_{m}^{\text{opt}}|\approx\frac{|\beta_{2}\sqrt{m}-\beta_{2}\sqrt{n}|}{L_{\text{max}}-L_{\text{opt}}}=\frac{\beta_{2}(\sqrt{m}-\sqrt{n})}{L_{\text{max}}-L_{\text{opt}}}

Using the approximation
\sqrt{m}-\sqrt{n}=\frac{m-n}{\sqrt{m}+\sqrt{n}}\approx\frac{m-n}{2\sqrt{n}}
for
m\approx n
, we have:

|P_{n}^{\text{opt}}-P_{m}^{\text{opt}}|\approx\frac{\beta_{2}}{2(L_{\text{max}}-L_{\text{opt}})}\cdot\frac{m-n}{\sqrt{n}}

This gives an estimate for the constant:
C^{\prime}\approx\frac{\beta_{2}}{2(L_{\text{max}}-L_{\text{opt}})}
. Based on extensive numerical simulations in the literature
[
52
]
, a widely accepted estimate for the BHH constant is
\beta_{2}\approx 0.712
. For the normalization constants, we can use the empirical data from our experiments.

Supplementary Information H
The Scalability Challenge of Simulating Equivariant Quantum Circuits

H.1
Limitations of State-Vector Simulation

The empirical validation in this work was performed using state-vector simulation. In this framework, the full quantum state of an
m
-qubit system is represented explicitly as a vector of
2^{m}
complex amplitudes. The application of a quantum gate is modeled as the multiplication of this vector by a
2^{m}\times 2^{m}
unitary matrix.

The computational complexity of this approach scales exponentially with
m
:

•

Memory Complexity:

\mathcal{O}(2^{m})

•

Time Complexity (per gate):

\mathcal{O}(2^{m})
for local gates, and up to
\mathcal{O}(m2^{m})
for the global EQC gates

This exponential scaling introduces a hard ceiling on problem sizes that can be simulated in practice. Even with state-of-the-art supercomputers, state-vector simulation becomes infeasible for systems larger than roughly 40–50 qubits, a threshold often referred to as the classical supremacy barrier. As such, extending empirical analysis of Equivariant Quantum Circuits (EQCs) to problem instances of realistic size (e.g.,
m\geq 100
for TSP) is beyond reach using this methodology alone.

H.2
A More Scalable Alternative: Tensor Network Simulation

A natural direction for overcoming this limitation is the use of tensor network methods, specifically the
Matrix Product State (MPS)
representation, together with
Matrix Product Operators (MPOs)
for implementing gates. Unlike the state-vector approach, which stores the entire exponential-size wavefunction, an MPS represents an
m
-qubit state as a sequence of interconnected tensors of bounded rank.

The storage requirement of an MPS is

\mathcal{O}(m\cdot d\cdot\chi^{2}),

where
d=2
for qubits and
\chi
is the bond dimension, a hyperparameter that controls the entanglement captured across any bipartition of the qubit chain. For states with limited entanglement,
\chi
can remain small and constant, enabling simulation of systems with hundreds or even thousands of qubits with memory that scales only linearly in
m
. This approach is the cornerstone of modern computational physics for strongly correlated one-dimensional quantum systems.

H.3
Challenges with Global Gates and the Role of MPOs

A key challenge in applying MPS simulation to EQCs is handling the global entangling gates characteristic of the architecture. Naively, the application of a global unitary such as

U=e^{-i\theta H},\qquad H=\sum_{j=1}^{m}O_{j},

where each
O_{j}
is a local Pauli operator, would cause the bond dimension to grow exponentially, eliminating the efficiency advantage.

However, an important structural property of such generators makes them particularly suitable for MPO-based simulation. Operators expressed as sums of local terms admit efficient MPO representations. In fact, even for a global operator such as
H=\sum_{j=1}^{m}X_{j}
, the MPO bond dimension required is exactly
2
, independent of system size
m
. This observation provides a pathway to simulate EQCs efficiently using tensor networks, since the global gates central to the model can be encoded compactly as MPOs.

H.4
Workflow for Tensor-Network Simulation

The anticipated simulation strategy for EQCs on large-scale TSP instances would proceed as follows:

1.

State Initialization.
The initial product state (e.g.,
\ket{0}^{\otimes m}
) is prepared as an MPS with bond dimension
\chi=1
. A maximum allowable bond dimension
\chi_{\max}
is set to control the accuracy–efficiency trade-off.

2.

MPO Construction.
For each EQC layer with unitary
U_{l}=e^{-i\theta_{l}H_{l}}
, the generator
H_{l}
is represented as an MPO of constant bond dimension.

3.

Gate Application.
The unitary
U_{l}
is applied to the MPS using one of several standard approaches:

•

Trotter–Suzuki decomposition:
approximate
e^{-i\theta\sum_{j}O_{j}}\approx\prod_{j}e^{-i\theta O_{j}}
, applying local exponentials sequentially with temporary bond growth.

•

Time-Evolving Block Decimation (TEBD):
apply short-time evolutions using MPOs, followed by truncation.

•

Direct MPO Approximation:
approximate
U_{l}
itself as an MPO with small bond dimension and apply directly.

4.

Bond-Dimension Truncation.
After each gate, the MPS bond dimension increases. The state is truncated back to
\chi_{\max}
using Singular Value Decomposition (SVD), discarding small singular values and introducing a controlled approximation error.

5.

Iteration.
Repeat steps 2–4 for all circuit layers.

6.

Measurement.
Observables, also represented as MPOs, are computed from the final MPS efficiently.

H.5

The shift from state-vector simulation to MPS/MPO simulation changes the scaling behavior fundamentally. The table below summarizes the asymptotic resource requirements for simulating an
m
-qubit EQC of depth
d
.

Here,

This comparison shows that the exponential

If entanglement growth is sufficiently slow, a moderate bond dimension will allow simulations of TSP instances with hundreds of cities, well beyond the state-vector barrier. Future research should focus on empirically characterizing the entanglement profile of EQC-generated states to determine the feasibility of this approach.

Supplementary Information I
Beyond TSP: Equivariant Quantum Policies for Other Symmetric CO Tasks

While this work focuses on the Euclidean Traveling Salesperson Problem (TSP), the core principles of permutation-equivariant quantum circuit design naturally suggest a broader research agenda. We envision that the framework of Equivariant Quantum Circuits (EQCs) can be systematically extended to a wide range of label-symmetric combinatorial optimization (CO) tasks. Below, we outline several promising directions for future investigation.

Vertex-equivariant graph problems.

Problems such as
Max-Cut
,
Maximum Independent Set
(
MIS
), and
Vertex Cover
operate on graph vertices and exhibit full
S_{n}
symmetry under vertex relabeling. An EQC for these tasks could employ one qubit per vertex and use pooled 1-local and 2-local generators (e.g., global
X
-mixers and edge-weighted
ZZ
-entanglers), with pooling normalized by
n
or
|E|
. Such architectures would support zero-shot transfer across graph sizes by rescaling normalization constants.

Bipartite and assignment problems.

For
Linear Assignment
,
Matching
, and the
Quadratic Assignment Problem
(
QAP
), the natural symmetry group is
S_{n}\times S_{n}
(row and column permutations). A grid-structured qubit layout
(i,j)\in[n]\times[n]
combined with row- and column-wise pooled generators could enforce one-hot constraints equivariantly. Cost reuploading and higher-order interactions (e.g., quartic terms in
QAP
) may be approximated via low-rank decompositions while preserving symmetry.

Problems with product symmetries.

In
Graph Coloring
with
k
unlabeled colors, the joint symmetry
S_{n}\times S_{k}
(vertices and colors) suggests a tensor-product equivariant architecture. Similarly, multi-dimensional packing or covering problems (
MDKP
,
Set Packing
)—though partially symmetry-breaking due to heterogeneous weights—could still benefit from
S_{n}
-equivariant regularization, enabling improved generalization and cross-instance transfer.

Theoretical transfer guarantees.

Preliminary analysis (mirroring the TSP case) indicates that zero-shot transfer between problem sizes
n
and
m
should incur a bounded performance penalty of the form

D_{n\to m}\lesssim C_{1}\|\theta\|_{1}\frac{|m-n|}{\max(n,m)}+C_{2}\Delta_{\text{structure}}(n,m),

where
\Delta_{\text{structure}}
captures smooth changes in problem geometry (e.g., metric scaling in Euclidean instances or cost matrix perturbations). Formalizing these bounds across problem classes remains an open theoretical challenge.

In summary, the combination of symmetry-aware quantum circuit design, data reuploading, and pooled generator architectures offers a unifying pathway toward scalable, transferable quantum heuristics for symmetric CO problems. We hope this outlook inspires future work in both quantum algorithms and combinatorial optimization.
