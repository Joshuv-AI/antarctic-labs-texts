Introduction

Meiotic recombination is a fundamental process that generates genetic diversity, ensures proper chromosome segregation, and influences population dynamics. Although several genetic and evolutionary factors influencing recombination rate variation have been identified, the relative contribution of these factors in Iranian native chicken populations remains incompletely understood (Peñalba and Wolf, 2020; Payseur, 2025; Stapley et al., 2017). Recombination hotspots are specific genomic regions where recombination is concentrated, creating double-strand breaks (DSBs) in the DNA of meiotically dividing cells, which leads to the generation of new allelic combinations or haplotypes (Smeds et al., 2016; Singhal et al., 2015). These hotspots influence polymorphic patterns in the genome by generating nucleotide diversity. The recombination process enables adaptation to changing environments by separating benefits from deleterious allelic combinations (Singhal et al., 2015). Studies in birds have shown that recombination hotspots are usually enriched in promoters and CpG islands and overlap with functional elements (Singhal et al., 2015; Smeds et al., 2016; Kawakami et al., 2017). Research in Gallus gallus has demonstrated that recombination events are enriched near telomeres based on high-resolution linkage maps and genomic analyses of hotspots (Groenen et al., 2009; Elferink et al., 2010; Bursell et al., 2025). Understanding variation in recombination and the characteristics of hotspots is crucial for comprehending how evolutionary forces and selective pressures shape genome diversity.

Heat and drought have increasingly disrupted poultry production by reducing feed intake, impairing growth, exacerbating disease susceptibility, and increasing mortality (Asadollahpour Nanaei et al., 2022a; Pish Jang Aghajeri et al., 2023). Native chickens of Iran, due to their high genetic diversity and adaptability to harsh climatic conditions such as heat, drought, and diseases, are a valuable resource for breeding programs, sustainable production, and conservation of genetic resources in the era of climate change (Shahbazi et al., 2007; Asadollahpour Nanaei et al., 2022a). Investigating the recombination landscape in native ecotypes can provide new insights into the role of recombination in maintaining genetic diversity, adapting to the environment, and responding to natural selection pressures.

While the understanding of recombination patterns in domestic chickens has advanced significantly, almost all studies have focused on commercial populations subjected to intense artificial selection (Liu et al., 2024; Groenen et al., 2009; Elferink et al., 2010). In contrast, the recombination landscape of native ecotypes that have experienced natural selection and diverse environmental pressures remains largely unexplored. Although recombination hotspots have been mapped in commercial chicken populations (Groenen et al., 2009; Pengelly et al., 2016) and adaptive loci are increasingly studied in Iranian native ecotypes, no specific investigation of candidate genes located within recombination hotspots in Iranian native chickens has been conducted to date. Consequently, no functional annotation or comprehensive assessment of the potential adaptive roles of genes located at these hotspots has been reported for Iranian native chicken ecotypes. The identification of HSP90β (heat shock protein family) in native Iranian chickens, and the repeated confirmation of MAPK, p53, and PPAR (major molecular chaperones and stress-activated signaling cascades) in transcriptional responses of heat‑exposed birds, indicate conserved cellular defense modules. These findings support the prioritization of candidate adaptation genes in native chickens (Asadollahpour-Nanaei et al., 2022b; Bai et al., 2024; Lan et al., 2016).

The main objectives of this study were twofold: First, to map the high‑resolution recombination landscape in Iranian native chicken ecotypes (Khazak, Lari, Marandi, and Naked neck) in comparison with commercial breeds raised in Iran (Leghorn and Arian), and second, to identify candidate genes located within recombination hotspots of these populations. To achieve these objectives, we analyzed whole‑genome sequencing data from 77 chickens (see Materials and Methods for details). This study represents the first comprehensive report of the high‑resolution recombination landscape in Iranian native chicken populations. By comparing native ecotypes with commercial breeds, we investigated differences in overall recombination rates, hotspot density, and their spatial distribution. The findings are practically important for breeding programs aimed at developing heat‑ and drought‑resilient poultry, as they identify genomic regions and candidate genes that may contribute to heat tolerance and adaptation to harsh environments. Furthermore, this work provides a foundation for conservation genetics strategies to preserve site‑adapted genetic diversity and for marker‑assisted selection programs targeting stress resilience in poultry production systems facing increasing climatic challenges.

Materials and methods

Study population and sampling

Whole genomes of 77 chicken DNA samples were sequenced using the Illumina HiSeq 2000 platform in paired‑end mode with a mean coverage of 16.1× (range: 10.2–20.0×). This was conducted within the framework of the Global Chicken Genome Project (http://chicken.ynau.edu.cn/index/about/index.html) led by the Kunming Institute of Zoology, Chinese Academy of Sciences (KIZ-CAS) and described by Wang et al. (Wang et al., 2020, 2021). The samples included four native Iranian ecotypes (Khazak, n = 20; Lari, n = 15; Naked neck, n = 11; and Marandi, n = 6) and two commercial breeds reared in Iran (Leghorn, n = 11; and Arian, n = 14).

The Arian line is a commercial broiler breed developed in Iran. It was established in 1990 at the Line Chicken Complex in Babol Konar, Mazandaran, from Hybro-N (Netherlands) parent stock and has been subjected to intensive artificial selection for growth rate, feed conversion ratio, and meat quality. Recent genomic analyses have also indicated admixture between Arian and northern Iranian native ecotypes (Asadollahpour-Nanaei et al., 2022).

Initial quality control was performed using FastQC v0.11.9 (Andrews, 2010). Results indicated good base quality (Phred score ≥ 30 for >95% of bases), balanced nucleotide distribution, and no adapter contamination. The chicken reference genome (Gallus gallus GRCg7b, Ensembl release 113) was downloaded from the Ensembl database (Ensembl, 2024). Indexing of the reference genome was performed with Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (BWA) version 0.7.17 and Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK) v4.2.0.0 CreateSequenceDictionary.

Read alignment and variant calling

Short reads were aligned to the reference genome using the Burrows-Wheeler Aligner Maximal Exact Matches (BWA-MEM) algorithm version 0.7.17 (Li, 2013). SAM files were converted to BAM format and sorted. Duplicate reads from PCR were removed using the GATK (v4.2.0.0) MarkDuplicates tool (McKenna et al., 2010; Van der Auwera and O'Connor, 2020). Base quality score recalibration (BQSR) was then performed using BaseRecalibrator and ApplyBQSR. Variant calling was performed with the HaplotypeCaller tool in GVCF mode. The resulting GVCF files were merged using CombineGVCFs and jointly genotyped with GenotypeGVCFs. Finally, variants were filtered using VariantFiltration and SelectVariants based on the criteria QD < 2.0, FS > 60.0, and MQ < 40.0 to obtain a high‑quality set of Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) (DePristo et al., 2011) for subsequent recombination rate estimation from linked reads using neural networks (ReLERNN) (version 1.0.0).

Data preparation for ReLERNN analysis

Advanced filters were applied to prepare the Variant Call Format (VCF) file for analysis with ReLERNN version 1.0.0 (Adrion et al., 2020). Mean genome-wide sequencing depth after quality filtering was not uniform across all chromosomes; lower effective depth was observed on the highly repetitive W chromosome and on GC-rich micro-chromosomes. Consequently, sex chromosomes and micro-chromosomes were excluded from all recombination analyses, and macrochromosomal variants (chromosomes 1–28 and 32) were extracted using bcftools version 1.21 (Danecek et al., 2021) to focus on regions with high coverage and biological significance (approximately 95% of the genome). Biallelic variants were then selected and filtered using vcflib version 1.0.3 (Garrison et al., 2022) based on allele frequency (AF) ranging from 0.05 to 0.95. Finally, a custom BED file was created using fetchChromSizes, restricted to the target chromosomes, and used as standardized input for ReLERNN (version 1.0.0).

Recombination rate estimation with ReLERNN

Genome-scale recombination rates were estimated using ReLERNN (Adrion et al., 2020), a recurrent neural network method that infers the relative recombination rate ρ = 4Nₑr (where r is the recombination rate per generation and Nₑ is the effective population size) from genotype data. The method is suitable for non-model genomes such as chicken. The pipeline consisted of four main steps: data simulation (ReLERNN_SIMULATE), model training (ReLERNN_TRAIN), rate prediction (ReLERNN_PREDICT), and bootstrap bias correction (ReLERNN_BSCORRECT).

In the simulation step, training, validation and test sets were generated with the parameters –assumedMu 2 × 10⁻⁹ (Prentout et al., 2025), –assumedGenTime 1 (Wragg et al., 2012), –upperRhoThetaRatio 20, –nTrain 20000, –nVali 1000 and –nTest 1000. The model was trained with 100 training and 20 validation runs. Predictions were performed separately for the VCF file of each population, and bias correction was applied with 500 bootstrap replicates and 50 cutoffs.

ReLERNN predicts recombination rates in non-overlapping windows whose size is determined by local SNP density. These variable-sized windows were subsequently linearly interpolated to a uniform 1-kb resolution to enable consistent hotspot detection, visualisation and all downstream analyses. Although ReLERNN assumes demographic equilibrium, previous applications to both commercial and indigenous Iranian chicken populations have produced reliable relative recombination maps despite known bottlenecks (Liu et al., 2024).

The final outputs comprised predicted (PREDICT) and bootstrap-corrected (BSCORRECTED) recombination rates reported, for each chromosome, in units of c/bp together with a 95% confidence interval. These data were used to generate recombination maps and to perform subsequent statistical analyses. Recombination rates were estimated independently for each of the six populations. The internal bootstrap procedure implemented in ReLERNN_BSCORRECT (500 replicates) was applied uniformly to all groups, including the smallest sample (Marandi, n = 6).

Calibration and visualization of recombination landscapes

Because an empirically measured mutation rate (μ) specific to Iranian native chicken populations is not available, the relative recombination rate estimates (ρ = 4Nₑr) produced by ReLERNN (version 1.0.0) were converted to absolute rates (cM/Mb) by applying a global scaling factor of 100. This factor was selected so that the genome-wide average approximated the consensus value reported for domestic chicken linkage maps (approximately 3 cM/Mb; Groenen et al., 2009; Elferink et al., 2010). The same scaling approach has been used in previous ReLERNN-based studies of chickens and other non-model vertebrates when absolute rates are required (Adrion et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2024).

To assess the robustness of our conclusions to this assumption, we repeated the entire downstream analysis pipeline under three alternative scaling factors (50×, 100× and 200×). Genome-wide mean rates scaled linearly with the factor, as expected. In contrast, the number and genomic coordinates of recombination hotspots remained completely unchanged, because hotspots were defined by a relative threshold (≥ 2.5 × local background rate and lower 95% confidence interval > 0). Consequently, both the ranking of populations and the characteristic telomeric enrichment pattern were invariant to the choice of scaling factors (Supplementary Table S2). We therefore conclude that the principal comparative results of this study are robust to uncertainty in the absolute calibration.

The calibrated data were then processed and visualized using R software version 4.2.2 (R Core Team, 2022) and the ggplot2 (Wickham, 2016), dplyr (Wickham et al., 2023), viridis (Garnier et al., 2021), and data.table (Dowle and Srinivasan, 2023) packages. For each chromosome and population, four types of plots were generated: a full line plot with a LOESS‑smoothed trend (span = 0.05), a 95% confidence interval bar (for BSCORRECTED data), a zoomed view of the first 40 windows (≈4 Mb), and a box plot of the recombination rate distribution. Coloring was performed using the Viridis palette, and figures were saved in PNG format at 600 dpi resolution.

Calculation of genome-wide recombination rates and identification of hot spots

Genome‑wide recombination rates were calculated for macrochromosomes (1 to 28 and 32) and weighted by the length of each chromosome. For hotspot detection, a permutation-based false-discovery-rate procedure (10 000 permutations per chromosome, FDR < 0.05) was applied to control multiple testing across the tested 1-kb windows. The background rate was estimated using a moving average (k = 150), and hotspots were defined as regions with a recombination rate ≥2.5 times the background rate and a lower 95% confidence interval (CI95LO) > 0, following the threshold established in previous chicken studies (Groenen et, al., 2009; Elferink et al., 2010). Chromosomes 16 (containing the Major histocompatibility complex (MHC) complex) and 32 (the W chromosome) were excluded from the analysis due to their unique genomic structures and known suppression of recombination. Hotspots were calculated separately for each chromosome and then merged into a single dataset. Differences in genome-wide recombination rates among the six populations were tested with the Kruskal-Wallis rank-sum test. Effect size was quantified as ƞ2 = (H – k + 1)/(N – k) where H is the Kruskal-Wallis statistic, k is the number of groups and N is the total number of 1-kb windows. Pairwise post-hoc comparisons were performed with Dunn’s test and Bonferroni correction. Genome-wide means are reported with 95% confidence intervals.

Recombination hotspot annotation and candidate gene identification

Recombination hotspots (within ±500 bp of the peak of each 1‑kb window) were prepared in region format for the Variant Effect Predictor (VEP) online tool from Ensembl (Ensembl, 2024). The input file included coordinates (chrom, start, end) with alleles = "<DUP>", strand = "+", and a unique identifier. Annotation was performed using the Gallus gallus species settings (GRCg7b assembly), Region format, and Transcript annotation options (RefSeq + Ensembl). The VEP output included the following fields: consequence, symbol, gene, biotype, feature, and impact. Genes were then extracted and restricted to protein‑coding genes only; non‑coding biotypes (lncRNA, miRNA, snRNA, snoRNA, pseudogenes, and similar) and genes without a valid symbol were removed.

Results

Sequencing data characteristics and quality control

The average sequencing coverage across all samples was 16.1× ± 2.4× (range: 10.2–20.0×). The highest coverage was observed in the Khazak ecotype (mean 18.5×) and the lowest in the Leghorn breed (mean 12.4×) (Table 1). After reading alignment with BWA-MEM version 0.7.17, variant calling with GATK (v4.2.0.0) HaplotypeCaller, and application of quality filters (QD > 2.0, FS < 60.0, MQ > 40.0), a total of 62,749,827 biallelic SNPs were retained. The highest number of SNPs was recorded in the Lari ecotype (13,568,516) and the lowest in the Leghorn breed (6,372,485) (Table 1).

Whole-genome recombination rates in indigenous and industrial populations

After bootstrap correction and calibration, the average whole-genome recombination rate across macrochromosomes was 16.78 ± 12.43 cM/Mb. The highest mean rate was observed in the Naked neck ecotype and the lowest in the Leghorn breed (Table 2). These differences were highly significant (Kruskal-Wallis test: χ² = 1,117,525, df = 5, P < 2.2 × 10⁻¹⁶, effect size η² = 0.202). Dunn’s post-hoc tests with Bonferroni correction confirmed that the Leghorn breed differed significantly from all other populations (Padj < 0.001). Differences among the native ecotypes and the Arian breed were smaller in magnitude but remained statistically significant in most pairwise contrasts.

Spatial pattern of recombination rate in macrochromosomes

The spatial pattern of calibrated recombination rates was examined on chromosome 1 (the largest chicken chromosome, approximately 200 Mb) as a representative of the whole genome (Fig. 1). Recombination rates increased significantly in telomeric regions (0–20 Mb and 180–200 Mb), reaching >100 cM/Mb, while decreasing to <10 cM/Mb in centromeric and pericentromeric regions (Fig. 1B). This pattern was observed in all six populations; however, the intensity of telomeric peaks was considerably higher in Naked neck and Arian than in Leghorn.

On chromosome 16 (containing the major histocompatibility complex, MHC) and chromosome 32 (the W chromosome), recombination rates were close to zero in all populations, and no significant peaks were detected. This finding is consistent with previous reports of strong recombination suppression in the MHC and W chromosome regions in chickens (Kaufman et al., 1999; Bellott et al., 2017); therefore, these two chromosomes were excluded from the main analyses. Except for chromosomes 16 and 32, the recombination landscape of the other macrochromosomes resembled that of chromosomes 1 and 2 (Supplementary Figures S1–S27).

Identification and distribution of recombination hotspots

A total of 239,011 1‑kb windows were identified as recombination hotspots, covering 22.70% of the macrochromosomes (chromosomes 1–28, excluding chromosomes 16 and 32). The highest number and genomic coverage were observed in the Arian breed (75,839 regions, 7.22% of the genome) and Naked neck ecotype (64,244 regions, 6.12% of the genome), while the Leghorn breed had the lowest (11,563 regions, 1.10% of the genome) (Table 3). The average fold enrichment of hotspots across populations ranged from 2.89 to 3.38 (Table 3), which is consistent with values reported in previous studies based on genetic maps and next‑generation sequencing data in chickens (Groenen et al., 2009; Elferink et al., 2010).

Candidate genes located within recombination hotspots

Using Ensembl Variant Effect Predictor (VEP) (Ensembl, 2024), a total of 4,812 unique genes were identified within recombination hotspot boundaries (±500 bp from the peak of each 1‑kb window). After excluding non‑protein‑coding genes and genes without official symbols, 2,864 protein‑coding genes remained. From this set we performed targeted literature searches (PubMed, Google Scholar, NCBI Gene) using each gene symbol combined with the keywords “heat tolerance”, “heat stress”, “drought”, “osmotic stress”, “thermotolerance” and “environmental adaptation”. Only genes for which at least one peer-reviewed study reported a functional or associative link to one of these phenotypes were retained. The ten genes highlighted in the main text (TLR3, TLR2, SLC6A19, NAALADL2, HSPB7, ETNPPL, CHORDC1, ABCG2, TRPM8, and UNC13C) met this criterion. We emphasis that this is a literature-supported prioritization step only; no functional validation, RNA-seq, GWAS or selection analysis was performed.

A broader list of hotspot-overlapping genes with supporting references is provided in Supplementary Table S1. These genes – such as YAP1, ZBED1, WDR55, SIK2, SIRT1, NFX1, NFXL1, MC1R, MCU, KRT222, GRHL3, CDK5RAP3, CD109, HS3ST5, FZD5, FGF19, HSPD1, and CNTNAP2 – have known functions in environmental adaptation in other species (plants, mammals, or other birds) but are not discussed in detail in the main text; the interested reader is referred to Supplementary Table S1 for their chromosomal locations, functional annotations, population distributions, and supporting references.

Several genes identified in the current study have been previously reported in chickens (especially in native populations from arid and semi‑arid regions) as being associated with heat tolerance and environmental adaptation. These include HS3ST5, FZD5, FGF19, HSPD1, and CNTNAP2, all of which showed significant enrichment in the native Iranian chicken populations (see also Supplementary Table S1). This concordance supports the validity of the recombination hotspot‑based approach for identifying protein-coding genes in native chickens.

Discussion

Key findings and importance of recombination patterns in iranian native chicken populations

This study provides the first high‑resolution recombination landscape for Iranian native chicken ecotypes. Native populations (Khazak, Lari, Marandi, and Naked neck) together with the Arian breed exhibited significantly higher genome‑wide recombination rates and hotspot densities compared to the commercial Leghorn breed (Table 2, Table 3; Kruskal‑Wallis test, p < 0.001). The spatial distribution of recombination followed the classical avian pattern – strong telomeric peaks (>100 cM/Mb) and sharp decreases in central regions (<10 cM/Mb), consistent with previous reports in birds (Singhal et al., 2015; Smeds et al., 2016). The intensity and number of peaks were notably higher in native populations and Arian than in Leghorn, reflecting their higher overall recombination rates and greater genetic diversity. Complete suppression of recombination on chromosomes 16 (MHC) and 32 (W) also agreed with earlier findings (Groenen et al., 2009; Elferink et al., 2010; Fulton et al., 2016).

These findings demonstrate that Iranian local populations, despite traditional management, maintain a richer recombination landscape than industrial breeds. This difference has important implications for understanding the role of recombination in preserving genetic diversity and facilitating adaptation to harsh climatic conditions such as heat and drought (Hoffmann and Willi, 2008; Qanbari et al., 2014).

Biological interpretation of the observed differences in the recombination landscape

Quantitative differences and the effects of domestication

The substantially higher recombination rates and hotspot densities observed in native Iranian ecotypes and the Arian breed (compared to Leghorn) reflect differences in selection history and population demography. The reduced recombination in Leghorn is consistent with findings that recombination rates decrease during chicken domestication, likely due to strong artificial selection for economic traits (e.g., egg production) that favor larger linkage blocks (Liu et al., 2024; Weng et al., 2019; Hill and Robertson, 1966). In contrast, native ecotypes have experienced lower selection pressure and limited gene flow, which has preserved higher genetic diversity and recombination rates (Burt, 2006; Comeron et al., 2012).

Mechanistically, the strong telomeric concentration of recombination observed in all six populations aligns with the known avian pattern where, in the absence of PRDM9, hotspots are primarily controlled by open chromatin, promoters, and synaptonemal complex structure (Singhal et al., 2015; Kawakami et al., 2017). Recent cytological evidence indicates that a longer synaptonemal complex and smaller DNA loops are associated with higher crossover density in telomeric regions (Rossi and Pigozzi, 2025). The greater intensity of telomeric peaks in native ecotypes likely reflects the preservation of natural recombination control mechanisms that have been disrupted in commercial breeds through inbreeding and population bottlenecks (Nachman, 2001; Charlesworth and Charlesworth, 1985).

Spatial changes in hotspots and the role of recombination in adaptation and evolution

Beyond differences in overall recombination rates, domestication can also alter the spatial distribution of hotspots, a phenomenon known as hotspot turnover – some regions that are hotspots in native populations may lose this status in commercial breeds and vice versa (Liu et al., 2024). These spatial changes are likely driven by local selective sweeps and artificial selection for specific genes, which can reshape the recombination landscape (Liu et al., 2024). Consequently, the differences observed between Leghorn and native ecotypes are significant not only in global recombination rates but also in the genomic locations of hotspots. The greater enrichment of hotspots in heat‑ and drought‑tolerant ecotypes (e.g., Naked neck and Arian) suggests that higher recombination may facilitate the generation of genetic diversity necessary for environmental adaptation (Hoffmann and Willi, 2008). This pattern is consistent with reduced selective interference, lower genetic load, and increased efficiency of natural selection (Otto and Barton, 2001; Comeron et al., 2012). Overall, native Iranian ecotypes have maintained a recombination landscape closer to the natural state, which may provide an evolutionary advantage in the face of climate change (Otto and Barton, 2001).

Interpretation of the difference in recombination rates between leghorn and Arian breeds

Although both Arian and Leghorn are commercial breeds, their recombination rates differ substantially (higher in Arian). Leghorn has undergone over a century of intensive international selection, resulting in high inbreeding, population bottlenecks, and reduced recombination (Liu et al., 2024; Weng et al., 2019). In contrast, Arian has been raised under Iranian farm conditions with greater environmental stresses, which may have favored the maintenance of higher genetic diversity and recombination (Hoffmann and Willi, 2008). Thus, Arian represents a transitional state between indigenous (high recombination) and pure commercial (low recombination) patterns, highlighting the importance of breed history and selection intensity in shaping the recombination landscape (Comeron et al., 2012; Bursell et al., 2025).

Genes located within recombination hotspots of Iranian native chickens

In line with the second objective of this study, Among the 2,864 protein-coding genes that fall inside hotspot boundaries, we highlight ten loci that have prior literature support for involvement in cellular responses to heat or osmotic stress. We stress that the present study provides only spatial evidence of overlap. No expression data under heat stress, no selection-signature analyses and no phenotypic association data were generated. Therefore, any adaptive hypothesis remains speculative and is presented solely as a direction for future research.

Immune‑related genes: TLR3 and TLR2 (chr 4), enriched in Khazak and Lari populations, play crucial roles in pathogen recognition and innate immunity. Under heat stress, they contribute to inflammatory response and homeostasis regulation (Sejian et al., 2019). Polymorphisms in these genes are associated with heat tolerance and infection resistance in small ruminants (Basu et al., 2015; Vandana et al., 2019).

Thermoregulation and stress response: TRPM8 (chr 7) encodes a temperature‑sensitive ion channel involved in hypothalamic thermal homeostasis under water imbalance and heat stress (Kozyreva et al., 2024). HSPB7 (chr 21) is a small heat shock protein critical for cellular protection against heat stress; a missense mutation in this gene is associated with heat tolerance in Chinese indicine cattle (Zeng et al., 2019). CHORDC1 (chr 1) acts as a protein chaperone and has been identified as a potential genetic marker for environmental adaptation in livestock (Liu et al., 2025).

Metabolism and osmotic regulation: NAALADL2 (chr 9) regulate glutamate metabolism and oxidative stress response and has been associated with heat tolerance in Egyptian goats (Aboul-Naga et al., 2025). SLC6A19 (chr 2) is involved in neutral amino acid transport in kidney and intestine, playing a role in osmotic regulation and nitrogen balance; polymorphisms associated with dehydration tolerance have been reported in camels (Cao et al., 2019). ETNPPL (chr 4) participates in phosphatidylethanolamine metabolism and was identified as a heat tolerance candidate in Egyptian goats (Aboul-Naga et al., 2025). ABCG2 (chr 6) functions in multidrug transport and toxin resistance and has been identified as a selective sweep associated with heat tolerance in Du’an goats (Chen et al., 2020). UNC13C (chr 10) has been linked to heat tolerance in Egyptian sheep, possibly through kinase activity and cellular signalling regulation (Aboul-Naga et al., 2022).

Previously reported genes: Several genes identified in this study have been previously reported in chickens associated with heat tolerance and environmental adaptation, including HS3ST5, FZD5, FGF19, HSPD1, and CNTNAP2. Their enrichment in native Iranian populations supports the validity of our hotspot‑based approach (see Supplementary Table S1 for details and references).

The higher recombination rates and hotspot densities in native Iranian ecotypes (Table 3) reflect the maintenance of greater genetic diversity, making these populations valuable sources of beneficial alleles for industrial breeds (Qanbari et al., 2014; Muir et al., 2008). Ecotypes with high recombination (e.g., Naked neck) and the Arian breed hold promise for producing stress‑resilient hybrids, which is increasingly important under climate change (Hoffmann and Willi, 2008; Scherf and Pilling, 2015). These populations can be effectively utilized in marker‑assisted selection (MAS) programs to enhance genetic diversity and improve complex traits such as feed efficiency and disease resistance (Goddard and Hayes, 2009; Wolc et al., 2015; Dekkers and Hospital, 2002). Preserving indigenous populations as living gene banks is essential, as reduced diversity in commercial breeds increases vulnerability to emerging diseases and climate stresses (Notter, 1999; Oldenbroek and van der Waaij, 2015).

This study has several limitations. First, absolute recombination rates depend on an assumed mutation rate and a post-hoc scaling factor. Although sensitivity analyses demonstrated that relative rankings and hotspot locations are invariant to the precise factor chosen (Supplementary Table S2), absolute rate values should be interpreted with caution. Future work incorporating empirically measured mutation rates or multi-generational pedigree data would allow more precise absolute calibration. Second, sequencing depth was not uniform across all chromosomes. After quality filtering, the mean genome-wide coverage was 10–20×, with lower effective depth on the highly repetitive W chromosome and several GC-rich micro-chromosomes. Consequently, all recombination analyses were restricted exclusively to macro-chromosomes 1–28 and 32. Low to moderate coverage in the retained regions may still reduce accuracy in repetitive or centromeric areas (Adrion et al., 2020; Booker et al., 2020). Third, sample sizes were uneven across populations, with Marandi represented by only six individuals. Although recombination landscapes were inferred independently for each population and the internal bootstrap of ReLERNN was applied to all groups, the precision of the Marandi-specific estimates is reduced. Importantly, a sensitivity analysis in which Marandi was completely excluded left the ranking of the remaining populations and the elevated recombination rates of native ecotypes relative to Leghorn unchanged. Nevertheless, Marandi-specific point estimates should be interpreted with caution (Supplementary Table S3). Fourth, population structure and demographic history: ReLERNN assumes population equilibrium, which may not hold perfectly for all commercial breeds with known bottlenecks. However, sensitivity analyses and prior validation on similar chicken populations (Liu et al., 2024) confirm that relative rankings and hotspot locations are robust. No additional population-structure correction (e.g., PCA or ADMIXTURE on the SNP data) was applied because the method is designed to infer recombination directly from LD patterns.

Ultimately, the present analyses are purely computational in nature. Consequently, functional validation of the genes located within recombination hotspots — for instance, through assessing their expression under heat stress — constitutes an important avenue for future research.

Conclusions

This study provides the first high‑resolution recombination landscape for Iranian indigenous chicken ecotypes. Native populations exhibited significantly higher recombination rates and hotspot densities compared to the commercial Leghorn breed, with a spatial pattern strongly concentrated in telomeric regions and sharply decreased in central regions, consistent with known features of avian genomes. These differences likely reflect lower selective pressure in native populations, which have preserved greater genetic diversity. In addition, we identify a set of protein‑coding genes within these hotspots that may serve as a useful foundation for future functional and association studies. Together, these findings provide a foundation for the sustainable use of Iranian native genetic resources and underscore the importance of conserving local diversity against industrial domestication pressures.
