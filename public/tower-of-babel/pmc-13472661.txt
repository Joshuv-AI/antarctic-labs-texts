Refractory Esophageal Varices Requiring Transjugular Intrahepatic Portosystemic Shunt Placement in Oxaliplatin-Induced Portosinusoidal Vascular Disorder
Authors: James Vu, Ashna Aggarwal, Maud Morshedi, Valentina Medici
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Colorectal carcinoma is a leading cause of cancer-related mortality in the United States. Capecitabine and oxaliplatin, a standard first-line chemotherapy regimen contains oxaliplatin, which rarely causes sinusoidal injury leading to portosinusoidal vascular disorder (PSVD). We report a rare case of oxaliplatin-associated PSVD presenting with esophageal varices refractory to banding, ultimately requiring transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt placement with variceal embolization. With few similar cases reported in the literature and in the absence of guidelines on managing refractory esophageal varices in PSVD, this case demonstrates a successful outcome with transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt placement.

INTRODUCTION

Colorectal carcinoma is a leading cause of cancer-related mortality in the United States.1 A combination of 5-fluorouracil, leucovorin, and oxaliplatin or capecitabine and oxaliplatin remain common first-line chemotherapy regimens for CRC.2 A rare adverse effect of oxaliplatin is liver injury through sinusoidal damage, which can progress to sinusoidal obstruction, hepatic congestion, and ultimately a form of portosinusoidal vascular disorder (PSVD).3 While hepatic synthetic function is typically preserved in PSVD, sparing patients from the ascites and hepatic encephalopathy typical of cirrhotic portal hypertension — the most clinically significant complication — remains the development of esophageal varices.4,6We report a rare presentation of a patient with oxaliplatin-induced PSVD and esophageal varices who presented nonacutely and whose multiple sessions of endoscopic variceal band ligation failed before ultimately requiring transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt (TIPS) with variceal embolization.

CASE REPORT

A 61-year-old man with a history of high-grade rectal adenocarcinoma status post low anterior resection and both neoadjuvant and adjuvant capecitabine and oxaliplatin chemotherapy (10 total cycles), completed 2 years before and in surveillance, presented after routine laboratory testing revealed severe anemia (hemoglobin 4.6 g/dL). He endorsed fatigue, but otherwise denied hematochezia, melena, hematemesis, and jaundice, and digital rectal examination was unremarkable. Of note, he had persistent mild thrombocytopenia with platelet counts consistently 100–149 × 109/L 1 year following adjuvant chemotherapy. Laboratory studies showed severe microcytic, iron-deficiency anemia (mean corpuscular volume 76.8 fL, ferritin 4.8 ng/mL, iron <20 μg/dL, iron saturation 3%) with appropriate reticulocytosis; folate and vitamin B12 levels were normal. He received 3 units of packed red blood cells with appropriate hemoglobin improvement to 7.5 g/dL. Although inpatient endoscopic evaluation was recommended, given his clinical stability and symptomatic improvement, the patient preferred to complete further workup in the outpatient setting.

Two weeks later, esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) was performed, revealing grade III esophageal varices in the mid and lower esophagus and portal hypertensive gastropathy; colonoscopy was unremarkable. He underwent variceal band ligation successfully; however, over the following months, he underwent a total of 5 serial EGDs with repeated attempts at banding which did not resolve his varices. In the interim, imaging with doppler ultrasound and abdominal computed tomography confirmed patency of the portal and hepatic veins, but was notable for stigmata of portal hypertension including splenomegaly and esophageal varices. Of note, he had no history of significant alcohol use, hepatic steatosis, or infectious hepatitis. The patient was started on carvedilol for variceal prophylaxis and referred to the outpatient hepatology clinic for further management. Before his clinic visit, he required an additional hospitalization for recurrent severe anemia requiring transfusion, during which repeat endoscopy revealed no active bleeding from his esophageal varices persistent and portal hypertensive gastropathy.

Following a multidisciplinary discussion between hepatology and interventional radiology, TIPS placement with variceal embolization was ultimately recommended given his varices refractory to multiple sessions of variceal band ligation and his portal hypertensive gastropathy, which was felt to be contributing to his chronic anemia. A preprocedural echocardiogram showed no evidence of cardiac dysfunction or pulmonary hypertension. He tolerated the TIPS procedure well (Figure 1). Direct intraprocedural pressures showed a pre-TIPS mean portal pressure of 32 mm Hg and right atrial pressure of 11 mm Hg, yielding a portosystemic gradient of 21 mm Hg which confirmed the presence of clinically significant portal hypertension. Liver biopsy obtained during the procedure ultimately demonstrated hepatic congestion and perisinusoidal fibrosis without cirrhosis, confirming the diagnosis of PSVD (Figure 2). Given the absence of an alternative etiology on history, a broader workup for other causes of PSVD was deferred, as oxaliplatin associated liver injury due to previous exposure was considered the most likely culprit. Following TIPS, his anemia and thrombocytopenia improved (Table 1), and a follow-up esophagogastroduodenoscopy 4 months later showed interval improvement with only small grade I esophageal varices and complete resolution of portal hypertensive gastropathy (Figure 3).

DISCUSSION

PSVD encompasses a spectrum of hepatic vascular lesions that produce portal hypertension in the absence of cirrhosis. Recognized etiologies include drug and toxin exposure (e.g. oxaliplatin), immune-mediated, prothrombotic disorders, chronic infection, and genetic conditions—although a substantial proportion of cases remain idiopathic. The true prevalence of PSVD is unclear, as it is rare and likely underdiagnosed, as preserved synthetic function and its indolent presentation can delay recognition.5 Regarding this case, oxaliplatin is a rare and underrecognized cause of PSVD and can induce sinusoidal endothelial injury and perisinusoidal fibrosis, leading to the development of portal hypertension despite preserved hepatic architecture.3 PSVD typically develops months to years after completion of therapy, and the risk appears to be associated with cumulative oxaliplatin exposure. Persistent thrombocytopenia with platelet counts below 150 × 109/L occurring approximately 1 year after oxaliplatin therapy has been identified as a potential early marker of developing PSVD, as observed in this case.7

Esophageal varices from oxaliplatin-induced PSVD requiring TIPS placement is a rare phenomenon, with a comprehensive literature review identifying only 2 such cases—both of which presented acutely with upper gastrointestinal bleeding necessitating urgent inpatient management.6,8 This is consistent with the broader literature, in which a 2023 literature review of 36 cases of oxaliplatin-induced portal hypertension found that the majority were managed with endoscopic ligation alone.8 As such, our case represents a distinct and rare clinical course: a patient who developed esophageal varices secondary to oxaliplatin-induced PSVD with an indolent, nonhemorrhagic presentation and was managed longitudinally as an outpatient with multiple sessions of variceal band ligation and carvedilol. After proving refractory to these measures, the patient ultimately required TIPS placement with variceal embolization.

There are no formal guidelines specifically addressing the management of esophageal varices arising from oxaliplatin-induced PSVD. Therapeutic strategies have been largely extrapolated from the management of varices in cirrhotic portal hypertension with endoscopic variceal ligation and nonselective beta-blockers typically employed as first-line therapy.8

When endoscopic approaches are exhausted, TIPS represents a rational escalation of therapy, and may be better tolerated in the setting of PSVD than in decompensated cirrhosis, as demonstrated in a previous study where patients with PSVD had significantly lower rates of hepatic encephalopathy, hepatic impairment and long-term mortality following TIPS insertion for variceal bleeding.9

This case emphasizes several practical points for clinicians managing patients with a history of oxaliplatin-based chemotherapy. First, PSVD should be considered in any patient presenting with unexplained thrombocytopenia, splenomegaly, or iron-deficiency anemia in the years following treatment completion—even in the absence of cirrhosis risk factors or abnormal liver biochemistries. Second, endoscopic surveillance should be initiated, and varices refractory to band ligation may warrant individualized, multidisciplinary evaluation with consideration of TIPS. Finally, TIPS with or without variceal embolization represents an effective and well-tolerated definitive strategy in this population, particularly when endoscopic approaches have been exhausted.
