finite free probability and S S transforms of Jacobi processes
Nizar Demni ∗, Nicolas Gilliers ∗ , † {}^{*,\,\dagger}, Tarek Hamdi △

Abstract
. In this paper, we study the S S transforms of Jacobi processes in the frameworks of free and finite free probability theories. We begin by deriving a partial differential equation satisfied by the free S S transform of the free Jacobi process, and we provide a detailed analysis of its characteristic curves. We turn next our attention to the averaged characteristic polynomial of the Hermitian Jacobi process and to the dynamic of its roots, referred to as the frozen Jacobi process . In particular, we prove, for a specific set of parameters, that the former aligns up to a Szegö variable transformation with the Hermite unitary polynomial. We also provide an expansion of the averaged characteristic polynomial of the Hermitian process in the basis of Jacobi polynomials. Finally, we establish the convergence of the frozen Jacobi process to the free Jacobi process in high dimensions by using the finite free S transform. In doing so, we prove a general result, interesting in its own, on the convergence of the finite differences of the finite free S S transform, which paves the way to obtain asymptotics of differential-difference equations satisfied by time-dependent finite free S transforms of polynomial sequences with positive roots.

1.3.
(Finite) free S transforms

To clarify connections between finite free probability and Voiculescu’s theory, finite analogues of various transforms central to free probability have been defined, including the
S
-transform
[
6
]
. The first proposal for the finite free
S
- transform is due to Marcus
[
25
]
, followed a few years later by another proposal, motivated by the CLT for finite free multiplicative convolution
[
1
]
. Marcus’s definition is general enough to cover the finite free multiplicative convolutions of distributions supported on the positive half-line and on the circle, but is much less practical for computations. For this reason, we prefer to work with the definition proposed in
[
1
]
in the present work.

Recall that if
\mu
is a probability measure on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
different from the dirac measure at
0
and
\psi_{\mu}\colon\mathbb{C}_{+}\to\mathbb{C}
is the moment transform of
\mu
defined over the complex plane minus the positive half-line, then
\psi_{\mu}
is univalent on the left upper-half plane
i\mathbb{C}_{+}
. Besides
\psi_{\mu}(i\mathbb{C}_{+})
is a domain contained in the disc with diameter
(-1+\mu({0}),0)
and
(-1+\mu({0}),0)\subset\psi_{\mu}(i\mathbb{C}_{+})
. The free
S
-transform of
\mu
by, for
z
in the vicinity of
(-1+\mu({0}),0)
is then defined by

S_{\mu}(z)=\frac{1+z}{z}\psi_{\mu}^{(-1)}(z).

Moreover, it factorizes under the free multiplicative convolution of distributions on the positive half-line.

1.4.
Our contributions

In the present work, we prove out-of-equilibrium equivalents of the previously stated results.
More precisely, we investigate the
S
-transforms of Jacobi processes within the frameworks of both free and finite free probability theories.
We begin by deriving a partial differential equation (PDE) that the
S
-transform of the free Jacobi process satisfies, as stated in Theorem
3.1.1
. Once we do, we provide a detailed analysis of its stationary analytic solutions around the origin in relation to the angle operator between two free orthogonal projections, viewed as an operator in the compressed probability space. For the sake of simplicity and motivated by
[
14
]
, we determine the characteristic curves of this PDE for the special parameter values
\lambda=1
and
\theta=1/2
and obtain an explicit expression of the S transform locally around the origin.

Our focus is then turned to the averaged characteristic polynomial of the Hermitian Jacobi process and to the dynamics of its roots. In this respect, we write a direct proof of the fact that the former satisfies the inverse Jacobi-heat equation and prove further that the latter are contained in the interval
(0,1)
.
In particular, when
\lambda=1
and
\theta=1/2
, we show that the averaged characteristic polynomial at time
t
aligns, up to a Szegö type transformation, with the Hermite unitary polynomial at time
2t
. This result is indeed the finite free analogue of Corollary 3.3 from
[
12
]
. Additionally, we provide an expansion of the averaged characteristic polynomial of the Hermitian process in the basis of Jacobi polynomials, valid for any initial data. To this end, we appeal to the dual Cauchy identity for multivariate Jacobi polynomials and to the semi-group density of the Jacobi eigenvalue process. Doing so allows us to use the mutual orthogonality of these polynomials, leading to the sought expansion.

Finally, we establish that the crystallization dynamics of the Jacobi process tend, in the regime of high degree and high dimension, towards the free Jacobi process and write two proofs of this result.
The first one relies on the equi-continuity of the moment sequence of the empirical root distribution and on the fact that the sequence of the moments of the free Jacobi process is uniquely determined by the differential system it satisfies. While the second proof is based on the finite free equivalent of the
T
-transform in Voiculescu’s theory. This alternative approach is noteworthy because it requires a general technical result that we prove below regarding the convergence of the finite
T
-transform and of its discrete derivative. Once proved, we derive a differential-difference equation for the
T
-transform of the Hermitian Jacobi process and recover in the
large size regime, the previously derived PDE for the free S transform.

2.
Reminder: Jacobi Processes

Since our concern will be on the Hermitian Jacobi process and on its large-size limit, the free Jacobi process, we recall some facts needed in the sequel.

2.1.
The Hermitian Jacobi process

Let
(Y_{t}(d))_{t\geq 0}
be a unitary Brownian motion of size
d
and variance
t
. Let

P_{m}
and
Q_{p}
be two diagonal orthogonal projections of rank
m
and
p
respectively, with
m\leq p
. The Hermitian Jacobi process
(X_{t}(m))_{t\geq 0}
of parameters
(r,s,m)
is the
m\times m
matrix-valued stochastic process defined by:

X_{t}(m)\oplus 0_{d-m}=P_{m}Y_{t}(d)Q_{p}Y_{t}^{\star}(d)P_{m}

where

r:=p-m,\quad s=d-p-m:=q-m.

Its eigenvalues process
(\lambda_{t}^{i},t\geq 0)_{1\leq i\leq m}
of
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
satisfy the following stochastic differential system:

(1)

d\lambda_{t}^{i}=\sqrt{2\lambda_{t}^{i}(1-\lambda_{t}^{i})}dB_{t}^{i}+\left[p-(p+q)\lambda_{t}^{i}+\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{\lambda_{t}^{i}(1-\lambda_{t}^{j})+\lambda_{t}^{j}(1-\lambda_{t}^{i})}{\lambda_{t}^{i}-\lambda_{t}^{j}}\right]dt,

where
(B_{t}^{i},t\geq 0)_{1\leq i\leq m}
are independent Brownian motions
[
8
]
. Up to the time change
t\mapsto 2t
, this random particle system is an instance of the
\beta
-Jacobi process studied in
[
10
]
corresponding to the value
\beta=2
. In particular, Corollary 2.1 shows that the eigenvalues process is globally defined in time provided that
p\wedge q>m-1/2
. Corollary 7 in
[
18
]
shows that the eigenvalues of
X_{t}
are almost surely distinct and that global existence holds even under the weaker condition
p\wedge q>m-1
. Consequently, Theorem 4.1 in
[
10
]
extends to
p\wedge q>m-1
so that the probability distribution of the eigenvalues process
(\lambda_{t}^{i},t\geq 0)_{1\leq i\leq m}
has a density with respect to Lebesgue measure in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, for any
p\wedge q>m-1
.

2.2.
The free Jacobi process

The free Jacobi process was introduced in
[
10
]
as the large-size limit of the Hermitian Jacobi process
(X_{t/d(m)}(m))_{t\geq 0}
in the asymptotic regime:

(2)

\displaystyle\lambda\theta=\lim_{m\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{m}{d(m)}\in(0,1],\quad\lambda=\lim_{m\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{m}{p(m)}>0.

For any time
t\geq 0
, we can define it in an abstract way as

X_{t}:=PY_{t}QY_{t}^{\star}P

where
P,Q
are free (in Voiculescu’s sense) orthogonal projections in a non commutative probability space
(\mathcal{A},\tau)
with ranks
\tau(P)=\lambda\theta
and
\tau(Q)=\theta
, and
(Y_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is the so-called free unitary Brownian motion
[
7
]
.
Viewed as an operator in the compressed probability space
(P\mathcal{A}P,\tau/\tau(P))
, the Cauchy transform
G_{t}^{(\lambda,\theta)}
of its spectral distribution satisfies the following non-linear partial differential equation (PDE):

\partial_{t}G_{t}^{(\lambda,\theta)}=\partial_{z}\left\{[(1-2\lambda\theta)z-\theta(1-\lambda)]G_{t}^{(\lambda,\theta)}+\lambda\theta z(z-1)[G_{t}^{(\lambda,\theta)}]^{2}\right\}.

Consequently, the moment generating function

(3)

\mathcal{M}_{t}^{(\lambda,\theta)}(z)=\frac{1}{z}G_{t}^{(\lambda,\theta)}\left(\frac{1}{z}\right)

satisfies:

\partial_{t}\mathcal{M}_{t}^{(\lambda,\theta)}=-z\partial_{z}\left\{[(1-2\lambda\theta)-\theta(1-\lambda)z]\mathcal{M}_{t}^{(\lambda,\theta)}+\lambda\theta(1-z)[\mathcal{M}_{t}^{(\lambda,\theta)}]^{2}\right\},

which reduces when
\lambda=1,\theta=1/2,
to

\partial_{t}\mathcal{M}_{t}^{(1,1/2)}=-\frac{z}{2}\partial_{z}\left\{(1-z)[\mathcal{M}_{t}^{(1,1/2)}]^{2}\right\}.

The solution of this equation subject to the initial data

\mathcal{M}_{0}^{(1,1/2)}(z)=\frac{1}{1-z}

was determined in
[
12
]
and used in
[
14
]
to derive expansions of the corresponding
R
and
S
transforms, based on Lagrange inversion formula.

Other degenerate limiting regimes were investigated in
[
4
]
for the Jacobi eigenvalue process, and gave rise to Marchenko-Pastur and Wigner distributions.
At the root level, these regimes correspond to the ones studied in
[
15
]
.

3.
S
transform of the free Jacobi Process

This section is devoted to the study of the S transform of the free Jacobi process. More precisely, Theorem
3.1.1
below provides a PDE satisfied by this analytic (around the origin) map, whose stationary solutions are shown to coincide with the S transform of the angle operator
PQP
between two free orthogonal projections (viewed as an operator in the compressed probability space). Though this coincidence is expected because the free Jacobi process converges weakly to
PQP
, it is not straightforward from a partial differential equation perspective. For that reason, we find it better to check it using standard (by now) facts from free probability theory, such as the Nica-Speicher convolution semi-group and the relation between the S and the free cumulant generating functions.

3.1.
A PDE for the
S
transform

For any time
t\geq 0
, denote
\eta_{t}^{(\lambda,\theta)}
the local inverse around zero of

\psi_{t}^{(\lambda,\theta)}:=\mathcal{M}_{t}^{(\lambda,\theta)}-1,

where we recall that
\mathcal{M}_{t}^{(\lambda,\theta)}
is the moment generating function of the free Jacobi process at time
t
.
Since this nonnegative operator is non-degenerate (its first moment is non-zero), its
S
transform
\mathcal{S}_{t}^{(\lambda,\theta)}
is defined locally by

\mathcal{S}_{t}^{(\lambda,\theta)}(z):=\frac{1+z}{z}\eta_{t}^{(\lambda,\theta)}(z),

where the value at
z=0
is obtained after removing the singularity at
z=0
. Then the PDE (
3
) entails:

Theorem 3.1.1
.

The
S
transform of the free Jacobi process satisfies locally around the origin the PDE:

(4)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathcal{S}_{t}^{(\lambda,\theta)}(z)=(2\lambda\theta z+1)\mathcal{S}_{t}^{(\lambda,\theta)}-\theta(1+2\lambda z)[\mathcal{S}_{t}^{(\lambda,\theta)}(z)]^{2}-\frac{\theta z(1+\lambda z)}{2}\partial_{z}[\mathcal{S}_{t}^{(\lambda,\theta)}(z)]^{2}.

Proof.

Since
\psi_{t}^{(\lambda,\theta)}[\eta_{t}^{(\lambda,\theta)}(z)]=z
, then the chain rule entails:

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{z}\psi_{t}^{(\lambda,\theta)}[\eta_{t}^{(\lambda,\theta)}(z)]\partial_{z}\eta_{t}^{(\lambda,\theta)}(z)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\psi_{t}^{(\lambda,\theta)}[\eta_{t}^{(\lambda,\theta)}(z)]+\partial_{t}[\eta_{t}^{(\lambda,\theta)}](z)\partial_{z}\psi_{t}^{(\lambda,\theta)}[\eta_{t}^{(\lambda,\theta)}(z)].

It follows that

\displaystyle\partial_{t}[\eta_{t}^{(\lambda,\theta)}]=-\partial_{t}\psi_{t}^{(\lambda,\theta)}[\eta_{t}^{(\lambda,\theta)}]\partial_{z}[\eta_{t}^{(\lambda,\theta)}]

\displaystyle=[\eta_{t}^{(\lambda,\theta)}]\partial_{z}\left\{[(1-2\lambda\theta)-\theta(1-\lambda)z](\psi_{t}^{(\lambda,\theta)}+1)+\lambda\theta(1-z)[(\psi_{t}^{(\lambda,\theta)}+1)]^{2}\right\}[\eta_{t}^{(\lambda,\theta)}]

\displaystyle\times\partial_{z}[\eta_{t}^{(\lambda,\theta)}]

\displaystyle=[\eta_{t}^{(\lambda,\theta)}]\partial_{z}\left\{(1+z)\left[(1-2\lambda\theta)-\theta(1-\lambda)\eta_{t}^{(\lambda,\theta)}\right]+\lambda\theta(z+1)^{2}\left(1-\eta_{t}^{(\lambda,\theta)}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=[\eta_{t}^{(\lambda,\theta)}]\partial_{z}\left\{(1-\lambda\theta+z+\lambda\theta z^{2})-\theta(1+(1+\lambda)z+\lambda z^{2})\eta_{t}^{(\lambda,\theta)}\right\}

\displaystyle=(2\lambda\theta z+1)[\eta_{t}^{(\lambda,\theta)}]-\theta(1+\lambda+2\lambda z)\left[\eta_{t}^{(\lambda,\theta)}\right]^{2}-\frac{\theta}{2}(1+z)(1+\lambda z)\partial_{z}\left[\eta_{t}^{(\lambda,\theta)}\right]^{2}.

Multiplying both sides by
(1+z)/z
and using the fact that

\displaystyle\partial_{z}\left[\mathcal{S}_{t}^{(\lambda,\theta)}\right]^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{(1+z)^{2}}{z^{2}}\partial_{z}\left(\eta_{t}^{(\lambda,\theta)}\right)^{2}-2\frac{1+z}{z^{3}}\left(\eta_{t}^{(\lambda,\theta)}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{(1+z)^{2}}{z^{2}}\partial_{z}\left(\eta_{t}^{(\lambda,\theta)}\right)^{2}-\frac{2}{z(1+z)}\left(\mathcal{S}_{t}^{(\lambda,\theta)}\right)^{2},

we get the desired result.
∎

3.2.
Stationary analytic (around the origin) solutions of (
4
)

These are the solutions of (
4
) which do not depend on time. As such, they satisfy the ordinary differential equation:

(2\lambda\theta z+1)\mathcal{S}^{(\lambda,\theta)}-\theta(1+2\lambda z)[\mathcal{S}^{(\lambda,\theta)}(z)]^{2}-\frac{\theta z(1+\lambda z)}{2}\partial_{z}[\mathcal{S}^{(\lambda,\theta)}]^{2}(z)=0.

The zero function is obviously the trivial solution. Otherwise, any non-zero stationary analytic solution satisfies

\theta z(1+\lambda z)\partial_{z}\mathcal{S}^{(\lambda,\theta)}(z)+\theta(1+2\lambda z)\mathcal{S}^{(\lambda,\theta)}(z)=1+2\lambda\theta z.

Noting that the LHS of this ODE may be written as
\partial_{z}[\theta z(1+\lambda z)\mathcal{S}^{(\lambda,\theta)}(z)]
, we readily see that

\mathcal{S}^{(\lambda,\theta)}(z)=\frac{\lambda\theta z^{2}+z+\theta c}{\theta z(\lambda z+1)},\quad z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0,-\frac{1}{\lambda}\}\ {\rm and}\ c\in\mathbb{C}.

But analyticity around the origin forces
c=0
and we end up (after removing the singularity at
z=0
) with

\mathcal{S}^{(\lambda,\theta)}(z)=\frac{\lambda\theta z^{2}+z}{\theta z(\lambda z+1)}=\frac{\lambda\theta z+1}{\lambda\theta z+\theta}.

This is the
S
transform
S_{Q}
of an orthogonal projection
Q
of rank
\theta
taken at
\lambda\theta z
. Equivalently, it is the
S
transform of the weak limit as
t\rightarrow+\infty
of the spectral
distribution of the free Jacobi process.
The latter may be defined as the spectral distribution of the angle operator
PQP
between two free orthogonal projections
P
and
Q
in a non commutative probability space
(\mathscr{A},\tau)
with ranks
\tau(P)=\lambda\theta
and
\tau(Q)=\theta
, viewed as an operator in the compressed probability space
(P\mathscr{A}P,\tau/\tau(P))
. Using the Nica-Speicher convolution semi-group, see
[
28
]
, Lecture 14,
the Voiculescu’s
R
transform
\mathcal{R}_{PQP}
of
PQP
in the compressed space is given by

R_{Q}(\lambda\theta z)

where
R_{Q}
is the Voiculescu’s
R
transform of
Q
in
(\mathscr{A},\tau)
, (see
[
28
]
, page 211). Let

\mathcal{C}_{PQP}(z):=z\mathcal{R}_{PQP}(z),\quad C_{Q}(z):=zR_{Q}(z),

be the shifted Voiculescu R transforms (also known as free cumulant generating functions) of
PQP
and of
Q
, respectively. Then (
[
28
]
, Lecture 16)

\mathcal{C}_{PQP}(z)=zR_{Q}(\lambda\theta z)=\frac{1}{\lambda\theta}C_{Q}(\lambda\theta z).

Consequently, the local inverse around
z=0
of
\mathcal{C}_{PQP}
is given by

\mathcal{C}_{PQP}^{-1}(z)=\frac{1}{\lambda\theta}C_{Q}^{-1}(\lambda\theta z).

Finally, the functional relations

\mathcal{C}_{PQP}^{-1}(z)=z\mathcal{S}_{PQP}(z),\quad C_{Q}^{-1}(z)=zS_{Q}(z),

yield

z\mathcal{S}_{PQP}(z)=\frac{\lambda\theta z}{\lambda\theta}S_{Q}(\lambda\theta z)\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\mathcal{S}_{PQP}(z)=S_{Q}(\lambda\theta z)=\mathcal{S}^{(\lambda,\theta)}(z).

In a nutshell,

\mathcal{S}^{(\lambda,\theta)}(z)=\lim_{t\rightarrow+\infty}\mathcal{S}_{t}^{(\lambda,\theta)}(z):=\mathcal{S}_{\infty}^{(\lambda,\theta)}(z)

is the non-trivial analytic stationary solution of the PDE (
4
).

3.3.
Analysis of the Characteristic curves for
\lambda=1,\theta=1/2

In this paragraph, we restrict our attention to the special parameter set
\theta=1/2
,
\lambda=1
, and assume for sake of simplicity that
\mathcal{S}^{(1,1/2)}_{0}(z)=1
(in which case the initial spectral distribution of the free Jacobi process is
\delta_{1}
). This restriction is mainly motivated by the fact that the moment-generating function
\mathcal{M}_{t}^{(1,1/2)}

admits an explicit expression, as proved in
[
12
]
, from which a series expansion of
\mathcal{S}_{t}^{(1,1/2)}
was derived in
[
14
]
making use of Lagrange inversion formula.
As we shall see below, the analysis of the Characteristic curves of the pde (
4
) in the special case corresponding to
\lambda=1,\theta=1/2
leads to a considerably more tractable expression of

\mathcal{S}^{(1,1/2)}_{t}
than the one displayed in Proposition 5.2. from
[
14
]
.

Proposition 3.3.1
.

Let
t\geq 0
. The S transform
\mathcal{S}_{t}^{(1,1/2)}
is given locally around
z=0
by

(5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{t}^{(1,1/2)}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{z^{2}+2z-\kappa_{t}^{-1}(z)}{z(1+z)}=\mathcal{S}_{\infty}^{(1,1/2)}(z)-\frac{\kappa_{t}^{-1}(z)}{z(1+z)},

where
\kappa_{t}
is defined by

\kappa_{t}(z)=\frac{(1+\sqrt{1+z})\xi_{2t}(\sqrt{1+z})+(\sqrt{1+z}-1)}{1-\xi_{2t}(\sqrt{1+z})}.

For any time
t>0
,
\xi_{2t}
is the inverse, in a vicinity of
u=1
, of the Herglotz transform of the spectral distribution of the free unitary Brownian motion at time
2t

[
7
]
:

\xi_{2t}(u)=\frac{u-1}{u+1}e^{ut}.

Proof.

If
\theta=1/2,\lambda=1,
then the PDE (
4
) reduces to:

(6)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathcal{S}_{t}^{(1,1/2)}(z)

\displaystyle=(1+z)\mathcal{S}_{t}^{(1,1/2)}(z)-\frac{1+2z}{2}\left[\mathcal{S}_{t}^{(1,1/2)}(z)\right]^{2}-\frac{z(1+z)}{4}\partial_{z}\left[\mathcal{S}_{t}^{(1,1/2)}\right]^{2}(z).

For a fixed
z
near the origin, the characteristic curve
s\mapsto z(s)
of the PDE (
6
) starting at
z
satisfies locally (in time) the ODE

(7)

z^{\prime}(s)=\frac{1}{2}z(s)(1+z(s))f(s),\quad z(0)=z.

where we set:

f(s):=\mathcal{S}_{t}^{(1,1/2)}(z(s)).

It follows that

f^{\prime}(s)=(1+z(s))f(t)-\frac{1+2z(s)}{2}f(s)^{2},\quad f(0)=\mathcal{S}_{0}^{(1,1/2)}(z).

Substituting the ODE (
7
) into the second yields

2\frac{z^{\prime\prime}(s)z(s)(1+z(s))-z^{\prime}(s)^{2}(1+2z(s))}{z(s)^{2}(1+z(s))^{2}}=\frac{2z^{\prime}(s)}{z(s)}-2\frac{z^{\prime}(s)^{2}(1+2z(s))}{z(s)^{2}(1+z(s))^{2}},

which reduces to

z^{\prime\prime}(s)=z^{\prime}(s)(1+z(s)).

A first integration gives

z^{\prime}(s)-z^{\prime}(0)=z^{\prime}(s)-\frac{z(1+z)}{2}f(0)=z(s)+\frac{z^{2}(s)}{2}-z-\frac{z^{2}}{2},

or equivalently

z^{\prime}(s)=z(s)+\frac{z^{2}(s)}{2}-z\left(1-\frac{S_{0}^{(1,1/2)}(z)}{2}\right)-\frac{z^{2}}{2}(1-S_{0}^{(1,1/2)}(z)).

Specializing the last ODE to
\mathcal{S}_{0}^{(1,1/2)}(z)=1
, a second integration then gives (locally in time)

\frac{z(s)+1-\sqrt{1+z}}{z(s)+1+\sqrt{1+z}}=\frac{\sqrt{z+1}-1}{\sqrt{z+1}+1}e^{\sqrt{1+z}s}=\xi_{2s}(\sqrt{1+z}),

while equation (
7
) yields again:

\mathcal{S}_{t}^{(1,1/2)}(z(s))=f(s)=\frac{z^{2}(s)+2z(s)-z}{z(s)(1+z(s))}.

Consequently, it holds locally in time that:

(8)

z(s)=\frac{(1+\sqrt{1+z})\xi_{2s}(\sqrt{1+z})+(\sqrt{1+z}-1)}{1-\xi_{2s}(\sqrt{1+z})}=\kappa_{s}(z).

Now, recall from
[
7
]
, Lemma 12, that for any fixed time
t
, there exists a Jordan
\Gamma_{t}
lying in the right half-plane and containing
w=1
where
\xi_{t}
is a one-to-one map onto the open unit disc. As a matter of fact, there exists a neighbourhood of the origin such that for any
z
there, the associated characteristic curve
s\mapsto z(s)
is globally defined in time.

Finally, fix
t>0
and assume for a while that
z\mapsto\kappa_{t}(z)
is locally invertible around the origin. Then
z=\kappa_{t}^{-1}(z(t))
and we finally get:

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{t}^{(1,1/2)}(z(t))

\displaystyle=\frac{z^{2}(t)+2z(t)-\kappa_{t}^{-1}(z(t))}{z(t)(1+z(t))},

whence the expression of
\mathcal{S}_{t}^{(1,1/2)}(z)
follows.

Coming back to the local invertibility of
\kappa_{t}
around the origin, observe that

\kappa_{t}(z)=\sqrt{1+z}\frac{1+\xi_{2t}(\sqrt{1+z})}{1-\xi_{2t}(\sqrt{1+z})}-1,

so that one only needs to check the same property for the map

v\mapsto v\frac{1+\xi_{2t}(v)}{1-\xi_{2t}(v)},

around
v=1
. Since the derivative of this map at
v=1
is given by
1+2\xi_{2t}^{\prime}(1)=1+e^{t}\neq 0
, the proposition is then proved.
∎

Remark 3.3.2
.

For any time
t\geq 0
, let
V_{t}
be defined by:

V_{t}(z)=z(1+z)\mathcal{S}_{t}^{(1,1/2)}(z),

From the PDE (
6
), we readily infer

\partial_{t}V_{t}=(1+z)V_{t}-\frac{1}{4}\partial_{z}V_{t}^{2},

and in turn
z\mapsto\kappa_{t}^{-1}(z)=z^{2}+2z-V_{t}(z)
satisfies

\partial_{t}\kappa_{t}^{-1}=\frac{1}{4}\partial_{z}[\kappa_{t}^{-1}]^{2}-\frac{z^{2}+2z}{2}\partial_{z}\kappa_{t}^{-1}.

On the other hand, the proposition yields the limit

\lim_{t\rightarrow+\infty}\kappa_{t}^{-1}=0,

This aligns with the fact that the Jordan domain
\Gamma_{t}
shrinks to
w=1
in the limit as
t\rightarrow+\infty
and the fact that
=\xi_{2t}(1)=0=\kappa_{t}(0)
for any time
t\geq 0
.

4.
Averaged characteristic polynomial of the Hermitian Jacobi Process

In this section, we proceed to the study of the averaged characteristic polynomial of the Hermitian Jacobi process. The main results are described as follows:

•

In Proposition (
4.1.1
), we revisit Voit’s result showing that the averaged characteristic polynomial satisfies the inverse Jacobi-heat equation for
p\wedge q>m-(1/2)
.
In doing so, we prove it directly using stochastic calculus, without relying on elementary symmetric polynomials. We also emphasise that this result holds for the larger set
p\wedge q>m-1
by virtue of Corollary 7 in
[
18
]
.

•

Corollary
4.1.2
gives then an explicit expression of this polynomial in the Jacobi polynomial basis when starting at
(x-1)^{m}
, while Proposition
4.1.4

shows that all its roots remain in the interval
(0,1)
. In this respect, we would like to stress that this last result is far from being obvious from a differential equation perspective since the dynamics of the roots are highly nonlinear. Actually, it holds true in our framework since this deterministic particle system results from ’freezing’ the eigenvalues process (
1
).

•

Proposition
4.2.1
is the finite free analogue of Corollary 3.3. proved in
[
12
]
: when
\lambda=1,\theta=1/2
, the image of the roots of
the averaged characteristic polynomial of the Hermitian Jacobi process by the transformation
x\mapsto 2\arccos(\sqrt{x})
coincides with the roots of the Hermite unitary polynomial.

•

Proposition
4.3.1
provides an expansion of
\chi_{t}^{(r,s,m)}
which is valid for any initial value of the eigenvalues process (
1
). While Proposition (
4.1.1
) also
shows that such expansion follows from the inverse Jacobi-heat equation, the one provided by Proposition
4.3.1
relies on the so-called dual Cauchy identity satisfied by multivariate Jacobi polynomials.
This identity originates in algebraic combinatorics (see
[
29
]
and references therein) and opens the way to use the mutual orthogonality of the multivariate Jacobi polynomials.

4.1.
Averaged Characteristic Polynomial

Let

F(x;u_{1},\dots,u_{m})=\prod_{i=1}^{m}(x-u_{i}),\quad x\in[0,1],

then the averaged characteristic polynomial of
X_{t}
is:

\chi_{t}^{(r,s,m)}(x):=\mathbb{E}F(x;\lambda_{t}^{1},\dots,\lambda_{t}^{m}).

In the series of papers
[
33
]
,
[
34
]
,
[
35
]
, the roots of the characteristic polynomial of the (rescaled)
\beta
-Jacobi particle system valued in
[-1,1]
are studied, among others.
In particular, Corollary 3.4 from
[
33
]
shows that the averaged elementary symmetric polynomials in this random system do not depend on
\beta
. Besides, Theorem 4.4 from
[
35
]
describes the dynamics of the roots of the averaged characteristic polynomial through an inverse Jacobi-heat equation.
Though the
\beta
-Jacobi particle systems in
[0,1]
and in
[-1,1]
are related by an affine transformation, the relation between elementary symmetric polynomials in both corresponding roots is far from being trivial. Nonetheless, one expects that the dynamics of the roots associated with the Jacobi particle system in
[0,1]
are still governed by an inverse Jacobi-heat equation. In this regard, a direct application of Itô’s formula together with appropriate algebraic transformations shows that this is indeed true.

Proposition 4.1.1
.

Let
m\geq 1
. Assume
p\wedge q>m-1
, then
(t,x)\mapsto\chi_{t}^{(r,s,m)}(x)
solves the inverse heat equation:

\partial_{t}\chi_{t}^{(r,s,m)}(x)=-\left\{\mathcal{L}_{x}^{(r,s)}+m(r+s+m+1)\right\}\chi_{t}^{(r,s,m)}(x),

where

\mathcal{L}_{x}^{(r,s)}:=x(1-x)\partial_{xx}^{2}+[(r+1)-(r+s+2)x]\partial_{x}

is the one-dimensional Jacobi operator.

Proof.

Let

F(x,\lambda_{t}^{1},\dots,\lambda_{t}^{m})=\prod_{i=1}^{m}(x-\lambda_{t}^{i}),\quad x\in\mathbb{R},

be the characteristic polynomial of the Hermitian Jacobi process
X_{t}
and recall that the eigenvalues are almost surely simple. Then, Itô’s formula entails:

dF(x,\lambda_{t}^{1},\dots,\lambda_{t}^{m})=\textrm{Local Martingale}-F(x,\lambda_{t}^{1},\dots,\lambda_{t}^{m})\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{p-(p+q)\lambda_{t}^{i}}{x-\lambda_{t}^{i}}\\
-F(x,\lambda_{t}^{1},\dots,\lambda_{t}^{m})\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{1}{x-\lambda_{t}^{i}}\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{\lambda_{t}^{i}(1-\lambda_{t}^{j})+\lambda_{t}^{j}(1-\lambda_{t}^{i})}{\lambda_{t}^{i}-\lambda_{t}^{j}}dt.

Note in passing that the quadratic variation terms have zero contribution since

\partial_{u_{i}u_{i}}F(x,u_{1},\dots,u_{m})=0,

and since the Brownian motions
(B_{t}^{i},t\geq 0)_{1\leq i\leq m}
are independent.
Using

\partial_{x}F(x,u_{1},\dots,u_{m})=F(x,u_{1},\dots,u_{m})\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{1}{x-u_{i}},

it follows that

\displaystyle F(x,\lambda_{t}^{1},\dots,\lambda_{t}^{m})\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{p-(p+q)\lambda_{t}^{i}}{x-\lambda_{t}^{i}}

\displaystyle=p\partial_{x}F(x,\lambda_{t})-(p+q)F(x,\lambda_{t})\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{\lambda^{i}_{t}}{x-\lambda_{t}^{i}}

\displaystyle=p\partial_{x}F(x,\lambda_{t})-(p+q)F(x,\lambda_{t})\sum_{i=1}^{m}(\frac{x}{x-\lambda^{i}_{t}}-1)

\displaystyle=p\partial_{x}F(x,\lambda_{t})-(p+q)(x\partial_{x}F(x,\lambda_{t})-mF(x,\lambda))

\displaystyle=\partial_{x}F(x,\lambda_{t})(p-(p+q)x)+m(p+q)F(x,\lambda_{t})

where we used the shorthand notation
(x,\lambda_{t})
to denote
(x,\lambda_{t}^{1},\dots,\lambda_{t}^{m})
.
Furthermore, the term

2\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{1}{x-\lambda_{t}^{i}}\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{\lambda_{t}^{i}(1-\lambda_{t}^{j})+\lambda_{t}^{j}(1-\lambda_{t}^{i})}{\lambda_{t}^{i}-\lambda_{t}^{j}}

may be symmetrised as

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{\lambda_{t}^{i}(1-\lambda_{t}^{j})+\lambda_{t}^{j}(1-\lambda_{t}^{i})}{\lambda_{t}^{i}-\lambda_{t}^{j}}\left[\frac{1}{x-\lambda_{t}^{i}}-\frac{1}{x-\lambda_{t}^{j}}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{\lambda_{t}^{i}(1-\lambda_{t}^{j})+\lambda_{t}^{j}(1-\lambda_{t}^{i})}{(x-\lambda_{t}^{i})(x-\lambda_{t}^{j})}

\displaystyle=2\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{\lambda_{t}^{i}(1-\lambda_{t}^{j})}{(x-\lambda_{t}^{i})(x-\lambda_{t}^{j})}.

On the other hand, one readily computes:

\partial_{xx}^{2}F(x,u_{1},\dots,u_{m})=F(x,u_{1},\dots,u_{m})\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{1}{(x-u_{i})(x-u_{j})}

and split, for any
1\leq i\neq j\leq m
,

\displaystyle\frac{x(1-x)}{(x-u_{i})(x-u_{j})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-x}{x-u_{j}}+\frac{u_{i}(1-x)}{(x-u_{i})(x-u_{j})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-x}{x-u_{j}}+\frac{u_{i}(1-u_{j})}{(x-u_{i})(x-u_{j})}-\frac{u_{i}}{x-u_{i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-x}{x-u_{j}}+\frac{u_{i}(1-u_{j})}{(x-u_{i})(x-u_{j})}-\frac{x}{x-u_{i}}+1.

Consequently, again with the shorthand notation
(x,u)
for
(x,u_{1},\dots,u_{m})
, one gets:

\displaystyle x(1-x)\partial_{xx}^{2}F(x,u)

\displaystyle=[m(m-1)+(m-1)(1-x)\partial_{x}-(m-1)x\partial_{x}]F(x,u)

\displaystyle+F(x,u)\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{u_{i}(1-u_{j})}{(x-u_{i})(x-u_{j})},

whence we deduce that

\displaystyle F(x,\lambda)\sum_{i=1}^{m}

\displaystyle\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{\lambda_{t}^{i}(1-\lambda_{t}^{j})}{(x-\lambda_{t}^{i})(x-\lambda_{t}^{j})}=x(1-x)\partial_{xx}^{2}F(x,\lambda)-(m-1)[m+(1-2x)\partial_{x}]F(x,\lambda).

Gathering all the terms, we get the following SDE for the characteristic polynomial of
X_{t}
:

(9)

d[F(x,\lambda_{t})]=\textrm{Local Martingale}+\\
-x(1-x)\partial_{xx}^{2}F(x,\lambda_{t})-(r+1-(s+r+2)x)\partial_{x}F(x,\lambda_{t})-m(d-m+1)F(x,\lambda_{t})

Finally, the bracket of the local martingale part is given by

2\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{t}^{i}(1-\lambda_{t}^{i})\prod_{j\neq i}(x-\lambda_{j}),

and is obviously bounded for any fixed
x
in a bounded interval. Consequently, the local martingale part is a true martingale, and the inverse Jacobi-heat equation follows after taking the expectation of both sides of (
9
).
∎

It is known that the Jacobi operator
\mathcal{L}_{x}^{(r,s)}
admits a complete set of eigenpolynomials given by the orthogonal Jacobi polynomials
Q_{j}^{(r,s)}(x),j\geq 0
:

Q_{j}^{(r,s)}(x):=\frac{(r+1)_{j}}{j!}{}_{2}F_{1}(-j,r+s+j+1,r+1;x),

where
{}_{2}F_{1}
stands for the Gauss hypergeometric function. More precisely, one has:

\mathcal{L}_{x}^{(r,s)}[Q_{j}^{(r,s)}(\cdot)](x)=-j(j+r+s+1)Q_{j}^{(r,s)}(x).

Consequently,

\displaystyle\left\{\mathcal{L}_{x}^{(r,s)}+m(d-m+1)\right\}Q_{j}^{(r,s)}(\cdot)(x)

\displaystyle=\left\{-j(j+r+s+1)+m(r+s+m+1)\right\}Q_{j}^{(r,s)}(x)

\displaystyle=(m-j)(r+s+1+m+j)Q_{j}^{(r,s)}(x).

Proposition
4.1.1
then yields the first part of the following corollary.

Corollary 4.1.2
.

With
p,q,m
as in Proposition
4.1.1
, let

\chi_{0}^{(r,s,m)}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{m}c_{j}^{(r,s,m)}Q_{j}^{(r,s)}(x)

be the expansion of the averaged characteristic polynomial at
t=0
in the Jacobi polynomial basis. Then

\chi_{t}^{(r,s,m)}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{m}c_{j}^{(r,s,m)}e^{-(m-j)(r+s+1+m+j)t}Q_{j}^{(r,s)}(x).

In particular, if
\chi_{0}^{(r,s,m)}(x)=(x-1)^{m}
, then

\chi_{t}^{(r,s,m)}(x)=(-1)^{m}m!\sum_{j=0}^{m}e^{-(m-j)(r+s+1+m+j)t}Q_{j}^{(r,s)}(x)\frac{(r+s+1+2j)\Gamma(r+s+1+j)}{\Gamma(r+s+2+m+j)}\binom{m+s}{m-j}.

Proof.

It suffices to find the expansion of
(x-1)^{m}
in the Jacobi polynomial basis. But this is afforded by the following formula
[
23
]
:

\left(\frac{1-u}{2}\right)^{m}=\sum_{j=0}^{m}\frac{(-m)_{j}(\alpha+j+1)_{m-j}(\alpha+\beta+2j+1)}{(\alpha+\beta+j+1)_{m+1}}P_{j}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(u),\quad u\in[-1,1],

where
P_{j}^{(\alpha,\beta)}
is the
j
-th Jacobi polynomial in
[-1,1]
:

P_{j}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(1-2x)=Q_{j}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(x),\quad\alpha,\beta>-1.

Indeed, the symmetry property
P_{j}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(u)=P_{j}^{(\beta,\alpha})(-u)
transforms the last formula into

\left(\frac{1+u}{2}\right)^{m}=\sum_{j=0}^{m}(-1)^{j}\frac{(-m)_{j}(\alpha+j+1)_{m-j}(\alpha+\beta+2j+1)}{(\alpha+\beta+j+1)_{m+1}}P_{j}^{(\beta,\alpha)}(u).

Substituting
u=1-2x,\beta=r,\alpha=s
there, we obtain the expansion:

(10)

\displaystyle(1-x)^{m}=m!\sum_{j=0}^{m}\frac{\Gamma(s+m+1)\Gamma(r+s+j+1)(r+s+2j+1)}{(m-j)!\Gamma(j+s+1)\Gamma(r+s+m+j+2)}Q_{j}^{(r,s)}(x)

where we used the representation of the Pochhammer symbol valid for positive real numbers:

(y)_{j}=\frac{\Gamma(y+j)}{\Gamma(y)},\quad y>0.

Applying the shifted Jacobi-heat semi-group

e^{-t(\mathcal{L}_{x}^{(r,s)}+m(d-m+1))}

to (
10
), we are done.
∎

Remark 4.1.3
.

As
t\rightarrow+\infty
, only the term
j=m
in the above expansion of

\chi_{t}^{(r,s,m)}(x)
gives a non zero contribution.
Accordingly, the limiting averaged characteristic polynomial

\chi_{\infty}^{(r,s,m)}(x):=\lim_{t\rightarrow+\infty}\chi_{t}^{(r,s,m)}(x)

is proportional to the monic Jacobi polynomial
Q_{m}^{(r,s)}(x)/k_{m}^{(r,s)}
, where
k_{m}^{(r,s)}
is the leading term of
Q_{m}^{(r,s)}(x)
. This is in agreement with the fact that the averaged characteristic polynomial of the Jacobi unitary ensemble, the weak limit of the Hermitian Jacobi process as
t\rightarrow+\infty
, is given by a Jacobi polynomial. Indeed, the eigenvalues of the JUE (Jacobi Unitary Ensemble) are given by a Selberg weight, as such its averaged characteristic polynomial is an instance of the celebrated Aomoto integral (see e.g.
[
24
]
).

For any fixed time
t\geq 0
, let
(x_{k}^{(r,s,m)}(t))_{1\leq k\leq m}
be the root sequence of the polynomials
\chi^{(r,s,m)}_{t}
:

(11)

\chi_{t}^{(r,s,m)}(x)=\prod_{m\geq k\geq 1}(x-x_{k}^{(r,s,m)}(t))=\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{m\geq i\geq 1}(x-\lambda_{i}(t))\right].

Then this sequence admits the following properties:

Proposition 4.1.4
.

The roots
(x_{j}^{(r,s,m)}(t))_{1\leq j\leq m}
of
\chi_{t}^{(r,s,m)}
are all real. In addition, up to re-indexing, they satisfy the following ODE:

(12)

\displaystyle\frac{dx_{j}^{(r,s,m)}}{dt}(t)

\displaystyle=(p-(p+q)x_{j}^{(r,s,m)}(t))

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt+\sum_{k\neq j}\frac{x_{j}^{(r,s,m)}(t)(1-x_{k}^{(r,s,m)}(t))+x_{k}^{(r,s,m)}(t)(1-x_{j}^{(r,s,m)}(t))}{x_{j}^{(r,s,m)}(t)-x_{k}^{(r,s,m)}(t)},

\displaystyle=(r+1)-(r+s+2)x_{j}^{(r,s,m)}(t)

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt+2x_{j}^{(r,s,m)}(t)(1-x_{j}^{(r,s,m)}(t))\sum_{k\neq j}\frac{1}{x_{j}^{(r,s,m)}(t)-x_{k}^{(r,s,m)}(t)}.

Besides, for any time
t>0
and any
1\leq j\leq m
,
x_{j}^{(r,s,m)}(t)\in(0,1)
.

Proof.

The first part of this proposition is due to Voit, as stated in Theorem 4.4 of
[
35
]
. Now, let’s turn to the second statement. We have

\prod_{k=1}^{m}x_{k}^{(r,s,m)}(t)=\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{k=1}^{m}\lambda_{k}(t)\right]>0

since
\lambda_{k}(t)>0
almost surely for any
1\leq k\leq m

[
11
,
18
]
. It follows that
x_{k}^{(r,s,m)}(t)\neq 0
for any
1\leq k\leq m
. Similarly,

e_{k}^{(r,s,m)}(x_{1}^{(r,s,m)}(t),\dots,x_{m}^{(r,s,m)}(t))=\mathbb{E}[e_{k}^{(r,s,m)}(\lambda_{1}(t),\dots,\lambda_{m}(t))]>0

which implies that
x_{k}^{(r,s,m)}(t)>0
for any
1\leq k\leq m
. In fact, if all the elementary symmetric polynomials on real numbers
x_{1},\dots,x_{m}
are positive,
then so do
(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})
. This is proved by setting

J(x):=\prod_{k=1}^{m}(x+x_{k})

and argue that if
x_{i}<0
for some

, this yields the following contradiction:

0=J(-x_{i})=\sum_{k=1}^{m}(-x_{i})^{m-k}e_{k}(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})>0.

To prove now that

for any
1\leq k\leq m
, observe that

\prod_{k=1}^{m}(x-(1-x_{k}^{(r,s,m)}(t))=\prod_{k=1}^{m}(x_{k}^{(r,s,m)}(t)-(1-x))=\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{k=1}^{m}(x-(1-\lambda_{k}(t))\right].

But

is a Jacobi particle system with parameters

. As a matter of fact,

.
∎

1\leq k\leq 2m\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{\tan(\theta_{j}(t)/2)}{\tan(\theta_{k}(t)/2)}-1\right)^{-1}.

Taking out the summand corresponding to
k=2m-j+1
and remembering that the angles come into opposite pairs, the last sum splits into:

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\neq j\\
1\leq k\leq 2m\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{\tan(\theta_{j}(t)/2)}{\tan(\theta_{k}(t)/2)}-1\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\neq j\\
1\leq k\leq m\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{\tan(\theta_{j}(t)/2)}{\tan(\theta_{k}(t)/2)}-1\right)^{-1}-\left(\frac{\tan(\theta_{j}(t)/2)}{\tan(\theta_{k}(t)/2)}+1\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}+2\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\neq j\\
1\leq k\leq m\end{subarray}}\frac{\tan^{2}(\theta_{k}(t)/2)}{{\tan^{2}(\theta_{j}(t)/2)}-{\tan^{2}(\theta_{k}(t)/2)}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}+2\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\neq j\\
1\leq k\leq m\end{subarray}}\frac{\cos^{2}(\theta_{j}(t)/2)-\cos^{2}(\theta_{j}(t)/2)\cos^{2}(\theta_{k}(t)/2)}{{\cos^{2}(\theta_{k}(t)/2)}-{\cos^{2}(\theta_{j}(t)/2)}}.

As result, for any
1\leq j\leq m
, the map
\eta_{j}
satisfies the ODE:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\eta_{j}(t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{2m-1}{2}\eta_{j}(t)+\frac{1}{4}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\neq j\\
1\leq k\leq m\end{subarray}}\frac{\cos^{2}(\theta_{j}(t)/2)-\cos^{2}(\theta_{j}(t)/2)\cos^{2}(\theta_{k}(t)/2)}{\cos^{2}(\theta_{k}(t)/2)-\cos^{2}(\theta_{j}(t)/2)}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2m-1}{2}\eta_{j}(t)+\frac{1}{4}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\neq j\\
1\leq k\leq m\end{subarray}}\frac{\eta_{j}(t)(1-\eta_{k}(t))}{\eta_{j}(t)-\eta_{k}(t)}

Equivalently,
\tilde{\eta}_{j}(t):=\eta_{j}(2t)
satisfies:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\tilde{\eta_{j}}(t)

\displaystyle=-(2m-1)\tilde{\eta}_{j}(t)+\frac{1}{2}+2\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\neq j\\
1\leq k\leq m\end{subarray}}\frac{\tilde{\eta_{j}}(t)(1-\tilde{\eta}_{k}(t))}{\tilde{\eta}_{j}(t)-\tilde{\eta}_{k}(t)}

Finally, notice that

\displaystyle 2\frac{\tilde{\eta}_{j}(t)(1-\tilde{\eta}_{k}(t))}{\tilde{\eta}_{j}(t)-\tilde{\eta}_{k}(t)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\tilde{\eta}_{j}(t)(1-\tilde{\eta}_{k}(t))+\tilde{\eta}_{k}(t)(1-\tilde{\eta}_{j}(t))}{\tilde{\eta}_{j}(t)-\tilde{\eta}_{k}(t)}+\frac{\tilde{\eta}_{j}(t)(1-\tilde{\eta}_{k}(t))-\tilde{\eta}_{k}(t)(1-\tilde{\eta}_{j}(t))}{\tilde{\eta}_{j}(t)-\tilde{\eta}_{k}(t)}

\displaystyle=1+\frac{\tilde{\eta}_{j}(t)(1-\tilde{\eta}_{k}(t))+\tilde{\eta}_{k}(t)(1-\tilde{\eta}_{j}(t))}{\tilde{\eta}_{j}(t)-\tilde{\eta}_{k}(t)},

we end up with:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\tilde{\eta_{j}}(t)

\displaystyle=\left(m-\frac{1}{2}\right)-(2m-1)\tilde{\eta}_{j}(t)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\neq j\\
1\leq k\leq m\end{subarray}}\frac{\tilde{\eta}_{j}(t)(1-\tilde{\eta}_{k}(t))+\tilde{\eta}_{k}(t)(1-\tilde{\eta}_{j}(t))}{\tilde{\eta}_{j}(t)-\tilde{\eta}_{k}(t)}.

This is the ODE (
12
) with
p=q=m-(1/2)
. But Theorem 1.1. in
[
4
]
shows (after performing an affine variable change)
that (
12
) admits a unique solution for any
p,q>m-1
which remains in the open domain
\{0<x_{m}<\cdots<x_{1}<1\}
at any time
t>0
. It follows that

\cos^{2}\left(\frac{\theta_{j}(2t)}{2}\right)=\tilde{\eta_{j}}(t)=x_{j}(t)

for any
1\leq j\leq m
and any
t\geq 0
, therefore

\displaystyle H_{2m}(z,2t)

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{m}|z-e^{i\theta_{j}(2t)}|^{2}

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{m}(z^{2}-2z\cos(\theta_{j}(2t))+1)

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{m}(z^{2}-2z(2x_{j}(t)-1)+1)=(4z)^{m}\chi^{(-1/2,-1/2,m)}_{t}\left(\frac{z+z^{-1}+2}{4}\right).

∎

z_{0}

4.3.
A more general expansion

We derive a more general expansion of the averaged characteristic polynomial
\chi_{t}^{(r,s,m)}
which is valid for any initial value
w=\lambda_{0}
of the eigenvalues process. Our main ingredients are the heat kernel of the latter and the dual Cauchy identity
satisfied by the (symmetric) multivariate Jacobi polynomials recalled below. These polynomials are mutually orthogonal with respect to the unitary Selberg weight. This property
is not satisfied by any orthogonal set of multivariable polynomials since it requires the orthogonality of any two polynomials
corresponding to different partitions. For the sake of completeness, we provide a brief reminder of the key facts and results necessary to prove the expansion below. We refer the reader to the paper
[
11
]
for more details.

Let

\tau=(\tau_{1}\geq\tau_{2}\geq...\geq\tau_{m}\geq 0)

be a partition of length at most
m
and let
(\tilde{Q}_{j}^{(r,s)})_{j\geq 0}
be the sequence of orthonormal Jacobi polynomials with respect to the beta weight:

u^{r}(1-u)^{s}{\bf 1}_{[0,1]}(u).

These are given by

\tilde{Q}_{j}^{(r,s)}(x):=\frac{Q_{j}^{(r,s)}}{||Q_{j}^{(r,s)}||_{2}}=\left[\frac{(2k+r+s+1)\Gamma(k+r+s+1)k!}{\Gamma(r+k+1)\Gamma(s+k+1)}\right]^{1/2}Q_{j}^{(r,s)}(x).

Then the orthonormal multivariate Jacobi polynomial corresponding to
\tau
is defined by:

\displaystyle\tilde{Q}_{\tau}^{(r,s,m)}(y_{1},...,y_{m}):=\frac{\det(\tilde{Q}_{\tau_{i}-i+m}^{(r,s)}(y_{j}))_{1\leq i,j\leq m}}{V(y_{1},...,y_{m})},

where

V(y_{1},...,y_{m}):=\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq m}(y_{i}-y_{j}),

is the Vandermonde determinant. If the coordinates
(w_{1},...,w_{m})
overlap, this definition still makes sense by either applying L’Hôpital’s rule or equivalently by using the expansion of
\tilde{Q}_{\tau}^{(r,s,m)}
in the Schur polynomial basis.

The multivariable Jacobi polynomials are symmetric (invariant under permutations) and satisfy the remarkable property of being mutually orthonormal with respect to the unitary Selberg weight:

W^{(r,s,m)}(y_{1},\dots,y_{m}):=[V(y_{1},\dots,y_{m})]^{2}\prod_{i=1}^{m}y_{i}^{r}(1-y_{i})^{s}{\bf 1}_{0<y_{m}<...<y_{1}<1},\quad r,s>-1.

Actually, for any two different partitions
\tau
and
\kappa
, one has:

\int Q_{\tau}^{(r,s,m)}(y_{1},...,y_{m})Q_{\kappa}^{(r,s,m)}(y_{1},...,y_{m})W^{(r,s,m)}(y_{1},\dots,y_{m})dy_{1}\cdots dy_{m}=0,

as one readily checks using Andreief’s identity.

Replacing
\tilde{Q}_{j}^{(r,s)}
with
Q_{j}^{(r,s)}
, we get the orthogonal multivariate Jacobi polynomials
(Q_{\tau}^{(r,s,m)})_{\tau}
in
[0,1]^{m}
, and performing further the variable change
y\mapsto 1-2y
, one gets the orthogonal multivariate Jacobi polynomials
(P_{\tau}^{(r,s,m)})_{\tau}
in
[-1,1]^{m}
.

Now, recall that the semi-group density of the eigenvalues process of
X_{t}
starting at
w
admits the following absolutely-convergent expansion:

\displaystyle G_{t}^{r,s,m}(w,y):=\sum_{\tau}e^{-\nu_{\tau}t}\tilde{Q}_{\tau}^{(r,s,m)}(w)\tilde{Q}_{\tau}^{r,s,m}(y)W^{r,s,m}(y_{1},\dots,y_{m}),\quad r,s>-1,

where

\displaystyle\nu_{\tau}:=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\tau_{i}(\tau_{i}+r+s+1+2(m-i)).

Now, we are ready to prove the following proposition:

Proposition 4.3.1
.

For any
w\in[0,1]^{m}
,

\chi_{t}^{(r,s,m)}(x)=\frac{1}{(-2)^{m(m+1)/2}}\sum_{j=0}^{m}(-1)^{m-j}e^{-\nu_{(1^{m-j})}t}\frac{Q_{(1^{m-j})}^{(r,s,m)}(w)}{k_{1^{m-j}}^{(r,s,m)}}\frac{Q_{j}^{(r,s)}(x)}{k_{j}^{(r,s)}},

where
1^{m-j}
is the partition with only
(m-j)
ones,
k_{1^{m-j}}^{(r,s,m)}

is the leading coefficient of
P_{(1^{m-j})}^{(r,s,m)}
and
k_{j}^{(r,s)}=k_{j}^{(r,s,1)}
is the leading coefficient of
P_{j}^{(r,s)}:=P_{j}^{(r,s,1)}
.

Proof.

Recall from
[
29
]
the dual Cauchy-identity:

\prod_{i=1}^{N}\prod_{j=1}^{K}(u_{i}+v_{j})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda=(\lambda_{1}\geq\dots\lambda_{N}\geq 0)\\
\lambda_{1}\leq K\end{subarray}}\frac{P_{\mu}^{(s,r,m)}(v_{1},\dots,v_{K})P_{\lambda}^{(r,s,m)}(u_{1},\dots,u_{N})}{k_{\mu}^{(s,r,m)}k_{\lambda}^{(r,s,m)}},\quad u_{i},v_{j}\in[-1,1],

where

\mu=(N-\lambda^{\prime}_{K},\dots,N-\lambda^{\prime}_{1}),

\lambda^{\prime}=(\lambda^{\prime}_{1}\geq\dots\geq\lambda^{\prime}_{K})
is the conjugate partition of
\lambda
and
k_{\lambda}^{(r,s,m)}
is the leading coefficients
P_{\lambda}^{(r,s,m)}
. Note in passing that the representation

P_{j}^{(r,s)}(u)=\frac{(r+1)_{j}}{j!}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(-j,r+s+j+1,r+1;\frac{1-u}{2}\right)

shows that

k_{j}^{(r,s,1)}=\frac{(r+s+1+j)_{j}}{j!2^{j}},

and in turn, the determinantal form of
P_{j}^{(r,s,m)}
entails

k_{\lambda}^{(r,s,m)}=\prod_{j=1}^{m}k_{\lambda_{j}-j+m}^{(r,s,1)},

for any partition
\lambda
.

We specialise this formula by putting

N=1,\quad K=m,\quad u_{1}=1-2x,\quad v_{j}=2y_{j}-1.

Then
\lambda=\lambda_{1}\in\{0,\dots,m\}
is a row so that
\lambda^{\prime}=1^{\lambda_{1}}
is a column and in turn
\mu=1^{m-\lambda_{1}}
is a column as well.
Using the mirror property satisfied by the Jacobi polynomials in
[-1,1]
, we get

\displaystyle(-2)^{m}F(x,y_{1},\dots,y_{m})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{m}\frac{P_{1^{m-j}}^{(s,r,m)}(2y_{1}-1,\dots,2y_{m}-1)P_{j}^{(r,s,1)}(1-2x)}{k_{1^{m-j}}^{(r,s,m)}k_{j}^{(r,s,1)}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{m}(-1)^{m-j}\frac{P_{1^{m-j}}^{(r,s,m)}(1-2y_{1},\dots,1-2y_{m})P_{j}^{(r,s)}(1-2x)}{k_{1^{m-j}}^{(r,s,m)}k_{j}^{(r,s,1)}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{m}(-1)^{m-j}\frac{Q_{1^{m-j}}^{(r,s,m)}(y_{1},\dots,y_{m})Q_{j}^{(r,s)}(x)}{(-2)^{m(m-1)/2}k_{1^{m-j}}^{(r,s,m)}k_{j}^{(r,s,1)}}.

Now, we can write the heat kernel
G_{t}^{(r,s,m)}
as:

\displaystyle G_{t}^{(r,s,m)}(w,y):=\sum_{\tau}e^{-\nu_{\tau}t}\frac{Q_{\tau}^{(r,s,m)}(w)Q_{\tau}^{(r,s,m)}(y)}{\left(\prod_{j=1}^{m}||Q_{\tau_{j}+m-j}^{(r,s)}||_{2}\right)^{2}}W^{(r,s,m)}(y_{1},\dots,y_{m}),

then appeal to the mutual orthogonality of
(Q_{\tau}^{(r,s,m)})_{\tau}
to compute the integral:

\chi_{t}^{(r,s,m)}(x)=\int F(x,y_{1},\dots,y_{m})G_{t}^{(r,s,m)}(w,y)dy_{1}\cdots dy_{m}.

Doing so only leaves the partitions
\tau=1^{m-j},0\leq j\leq m
whence the sought expansion follows.
∎

5.1.
High-dimensional regime

We start by proving that in the high-dimensional regime afforded by (
2
), the counting measure

\mu_{t}^{(r,s,m)}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\delta_{x_{i}(t)}

of the roots of
\chi_{t}^{(r,s,m)}
converges to the spectral distribution of the free Jacobi process at time
t>0
, provided that the convergence holds at
t=0
.
For any time
t\geq 0
, let

m_{\ell}^{(r,s,m)}(t)(t)=\mu^{(r,s,m)}_{t}(x^{\ell}),\quad\ell\geq 0,

be the moment sequence of
\mu_{t}^{(r,s,m)}
.

Proposition 5.1.1
.

For any
r,s
and
m\geq 1
an integer, the trajectory of the infinite sequence of moments
(m_{\ell}^{(r,s,m)}(t)(t))_{\ell\geq 0}
is the solution of the following lower triangular differential system :

\frac{d}{dt}m_{1}^{(r,s,m)}(t)=p-(p+q)m_{1}^{(r,s,m)}(t),

and for any
\ell\geq 2
:

(16)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}m_{\ell}^{(r,s,m)}(t)(t)

\displaystyle=-\ell(p+q-\ell+1)m_{\ell}^{(r,s,m)}(t)(t)

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt+\ell(p-\ell+1)m_{\ell-1}^{(r,s,m)}(t)+m\ell\sum_{k=0}^{\ell-2}(m_{k}^{(r,s,m)}(t)(t)-m_{k+1}^{(r,s,m)}(t)(t))m_{\ell-1-k}^{(r,s,m)}(t)(t).

Proof.

Appealing to the ODE (
12
), we derive

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}m_{\ell}^{(r,s,m)}(t)(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\ell}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}(x^{i}(t))^{\ell-1}\frac{d}{dt}x^{i}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\ell}{m}\bigg(mpm_{\ell-1}^{(r,s,m)}(t)-m(p+q)m_{\ell}^{(r,s,m)}(t)(t)

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{(x^{i}(t))^{\ell}(1-x^{j}(t))+(x^{i}(t))^{\ell-1}x^{j}(t)(1-x^{i}(t))}{x^{i}(t)-x^{j}(t)}\bigg).

Now, we expand

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{(x^{i}(t))^{\ell}(1-x^{j}(t))+(x^{i}(t))^{\ell-1}x^{j}(t)(1-x^{i}(t))}{x^{i}(t)-x^{j}(t)}

\displaystyle\hskip 142.26378pt=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{-2(x^{i}(t))^{\ell}x^{j}(t)}{x^{i}(t)-x^{j}(t)}+\frac{(x^{i}(t))^{\ell}+(x^{i}(t))^{\ell-1}x^{j}(t)}{x^{i}(t)-x^{j}(t)},

and make the first double sum for
l\geq 2
symmetric as:

\displaystyle-2\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{(x^{i}(t))^{\ell-1}(x^{i}(t)x^{j}(t))}{x^{i}_{t}-x^{j}(t)}

\displaystyle=-2\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{x^{i}(t)x^{j}(t)[(x^{i}(t))^{\ell-1}-(x^{j}(t))^{\ell-1}]}{x^{i}_{t}-x^{j}(t)}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{k=0}^{\ell-2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j\neq i}x^{i}(t)x^{j}(t)(x^{i}(t))^{k}(x^{j}(t))^{\ell-2-k}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{k=0}^{\ell-2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j\neq i}(x^{i}(t))^{k+1}(x^{j}(t))^{\ell-1-k}

\displaystyle=-m^{2}\sum_{k=0}^{\ell-2}m_{k+1}^{(r,s,m)}(t)m_{\ell-1-k}^{(r,s,m)}(t)+m(\ell-1)m_{\ell}^{(r,s,m)}(t).

Proceeding in the same way, the second term may be written as

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{(x^{i}(t))^{\ell}+(x^{i}(t))^{\ell-1}x^{j}(t)}{x^{i}_{t}-x^{j}(t)}

\displaystyle=m^{2}\sum_{k=0}^{\ell-2}m_{k}^{(r,s,m)}(t)m_{\ell-1-k}^{(r,s,m)}(t)-m(\ell-1)m_{\ell-1}^{(r,s,m)}.

Gathering all the terms, we get (
16
). The ODE satisfied by
m_{1}^{(r,s,m)}
is derived exactly along the same lines noticing that the double sum is empty.
∎

We now proceed to the aforementioned convergence result. To this end, recall from
[
10
]
that the moments

m_{\ell}(t):=\frac{\tau(X_{t}^{\ell})}{\tau(P)},\quad\ell\geq 1,\quad m_{0}(t)=1,

of the free Jacobi process

X_{t}=PU_{t}QU_{t}^{\star}P,

viewed as an operator in the compressed algebra
(P\mathscr{A}P,\tau/\tau(P))
, satisfy the differential system:

(17)

\frac{d}{dt}m_{\ell}(t)=-\ell m_{\ell}(t)+\ell\theta m_{\ell-1}(t)+\ell\lambda\theta\sum_{j=0}^{\ell-2}m_{\ell-j-1}(t)[m_{j}(t)-m_{j+1}(t)],

where
\tau(P)=\lambda\theta\in(0,1],\tau(Q)=\theta\in(0,1]
.

Corollary 5.1.2
.

Assume that for any
\ell\geq 1
,
m_{\ell}^{(r(m),s(m),m)}(0)
converges as
m
goes to infinity in the regime described in (
2
). Then, for any time
t>0
,

\tilde{m}_{\ell}^{(r(m),s(m),m)}(t):=m_{\ell}^{(r(m),s(m),m)}(t)\left(\frac{t}{d(m)}\right)

converges to
m_{\ell}(t)
as well.

Proof.

Given that for all
m
,
\mu^{(r,s,m)}_{t/d(m)}
has support in
(0,1)
, the family formed by these measures is tight. It is then sufficient to prove that there is a unique limit point with respect to the weak topology.
However, again by the compactness of the support, we find it better to prove the same statement for the moments.
Indeed, for any
\ell\geq 0
, the family
\{\tilde{m}_{\ell}^{(r(m),s(m),m)}(t),\,t\geq 0\}
is uniformly bounded in
m
.
Moreover, we readily infer from (
16
) that for any
\ell\geq 1
,

(18)

\frac{d}{dt}\tilde{m}_{\ell}^{(r,s,m)}(t)=\ell\frac{p-\ell+1}{d}\tilde{m}_{\ell-1}^{(r,s,m)}-\ell\frac{d-\ell+1}{d}\tilde{m}_{\ell}^{(r,s,m)}+\frac{m}{d}\ell\sum_{k=0}^{\ell-2}(\tilde{m}_{k}^{(r,s,m)}-\tilde{m}_{k+1}^{(r,s,m)})\tilde{m}_{\ell-1-k}^{(r,s,m)}.

Since
\ell
is fixed, then the right-hand side of (
18
) is uniformly bounded for sufficiently large
m,p(m),d(m)
. Consequently,
\|\partial_{t}\tilde{m}_{\ell}^{(r(m),s(m),m)}\|_{\infty}
is so whence equicontinuity follows.
By Arzelà-Ascoli Theorem, the moments sequence
\{\tilde{m}^{(r(m),s(m),m)}\}_{m}
converges locally uniformly in
t
along a subsequence and in turn so does
d\tilde{m}^{(r(m),s(m),m)}/dt
.
Passing to the limit in (
18
) and using induction on
\ell
,
we conclude that any limiting moment sequence satisfies (
17
) for any
\ell
. Since the latter admits a unique solution for any fixed intial data at
t=0
, there is one and only one accumulation point for
\tilde{m}_{\ell}^{(r(m),s(m),m)}(t)
with prescribed initial data. The proposition is proved.
∎

: it is the piecewise right-continuous function defined on the open interval

by

T_{p_{m}}^{(m)}(v)=\begin{cases}0,&v\in(0,\frac{r}{m})\\
\displaystyle\frac{m-k+1}{k}\frac{e^{(m)}_{m-k+1}(p)}{e^{(m)}_{m-k}(p)},&v\in[\frac{k-1}{m},\frac{k}{m}),\quad k=r+1,\ldots,m.\end{cases}

Note in passing that while the finite free S transform is defined on a finite set of real numbers, the finite free T transform is defined in the open interval

T_{p_{m}}^{(m)}\left(\frac{m-k}{m}\right)=\frac{1}{\displaystyle{S}_{p_{m}}^{(m)}\left(-\frac{k}{m}\right)}.

5.2.2.
Convergence of the finite differences

We let
\nabla^{(m)}
be the operator of finite right-differentiation with step
1/m
acting on functions of
\mathbb{R}

\nabla^{(m)}\colon\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{R}}\to\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{R}},\quad\nabla^{(m)}g(v):=m\left(g\left(v+\frac{1}{m}\right)-g(v)\right),\quad v\in\mathbb{R}.

Likewise, the discrete left-derivative of

\tilde{\nabla}^{(m)}\colon\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{R}}\to\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{R}},\quad\tilde{\nabla}^{(m)}g(v)=m\left(g(v)-g\left(v-\frac{1}{m}\right)\right),\quad v\in\mathbb{R}

Most of the following statements will be formulated by using the right-derivative operator
\nabla^{(m)}
, although all have their counterparts valid for the left derivative operator
\tilde{\nabla}
.

Before stating the next Theorem, recall that if
(p_{m})_{m\geq 1}
is a sequence of polynomials with increasing degree
m

the counting measure of its roots :

\mu\llbracket p_{m}\rrbracket:=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{z\,:\,P(z)=0}\delta_{z}.

If
\mu\llbracket p_{m}\rrbracket
converges weakly to some probability measure
\mu
, we say that
(p_{m})_{m\geq 1}
is a
converging sequence
. From
[
1
]

(see Theorem 1.1. there), if for any

,

has only positive roots and if
\mu\neq\delta_{0}
, then
\mu\llbracket p_{m}\rrbracket
converges to
\mu
if and only if

(S^{(m)}_{p_{m}})_{m}
converges to the free
S
-transform
S_{\mu}
of
\mu
in the following sense: for any
v\in(0,1-\mu\{0\})

and any sequence
(k_{m})_{m\geq 1}
such that

1\leq k_{m}\leq m,\quad\lim_{m\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{k_{m}}{m}=t,

one has:

\lim_{m\rightarrow+\infty}{S}_{p_{m}}^{(m)}\left(-\frac{k_{m}}{m}\right)=S_{\mu}(-t).

We will also make use of the following interpolation over the interval
(0,1)
of the finite free
S
-transform of the polynomials

\mathcal{S}^{(m)}_{p_{m}}\colon(0,1)\to\mathbb{R},\quad\mathcal{S}^{(m)}_{p_{m}}(v)=S^{(m)}_{p_{m}}(-\frac{\lceil{mv}\rceil}{m}),\quad v\in\left(0,1-\frac{r_{m}}{m}\right),

where we recall that

is the ceiling function and

in

(not to be confused with

. In this regard, we again
emphasize that this interpolated S transform

is only defined on the interval

is defined on the whole interval

The relation (

T^{(m)}_{p_{m}}(1-v)=\frac{1}{\mathcal{S}^{(m)}_{p_{m}}(v)},\quad v\in\left(0,1-\frac{r_{m}}{m}\right).

Theorem 5.2.1
.

Let
(p_{m})_{m\geq 1}
be a converging sequence of monic polynomials with positive roots and increasing degrees
m
. Let
\mu
be the limiting measure :

\mu\llbracket p_{m}\rrbracket\underset{m,+\infty}{\longrightarrow}\mu,

and assume that
\mu\neq\delta_{0}
. Then,

\displaystyle[\nabla^{(m)}](\mathcal{S}^{(m)}_{p_{m}})(v)=-\partial_{v}{S}_{\mu}(-v)+o(1),\quad m\rightarrow+\infty,

locally uniformly on

.

Remark 5.2.2

From the statement of Theorem

T

Corollary 5.2.3
.

Continuing the setting of Theorem
5.2.1
, if

p_{m}

Proof.

Given that for any

T_{p_{m}}^{(m)}(v)=\frac{1}{\mathcal{S}^{(m)}_{p_{m}}(1-v)},

we infer

\displaystyle\nabla^{(m)}T^{(m)}_{p_{m}}(v)

\displaystyle=-m\frac{\mathcal{S}^{(m)}_{p_{m}}(1-v-\frac{1}{m})-\mathcal{S}^{(m)}_{p_{m}}(1-v)}{\mathcal{S}^{(m)}_{p_{m}}(1-v-\frac{1}{m})\mathcal{S}^{(m)}_{p_{m}}(1-v)}

5.3.
Finite free
T
-transform of the Frozen Jacobi process

In this paragraph, we put together the results proved in the last two sections to obtain a differential-difference equation satisfied by the finite free

T

\chi^{(r,s,m)}_{t}
of the Hermitian Jacobi process. Once obtained, we pass to the limit therein and derive a PDE satisfied by the
T

Let
e^{(r,s,m)}_{n}(t),0\leq n\leq m,
be the elementary symmetric functions in the roots of
\chi^{(r,s,m)}(t)
:

\chi^{(r,s,m)}(t)=\sum_{n=0}^{m}(-1)^{n}e_{n}^{(r,s,m)}(t)x^{m-n},\quad e_{0}^{(r,s,m)}(t)=1.

The following proposition provides a differential system satisfied by
e^{(r,s,m)}_{n}(t),1\leq n\leq m
. In this respect, the reader readily notices that it is simpler than the one
derived in
[
33
]
, Lemma 3.1., and corresponding to the Jacobi particle system in
[-1,1]
.

Proposition 5.3.1
.

For any
1\leq n\leq m
and any time
t>0
:

\frac{d}{dt}e^{(r,s,m)}_{n}(t)=-n(p+q-(n-1))e_{n}^{(r,s,m)}(t)+(m-(n-1))(p-(n-1))e^{(r,s,m)}_{n-1}(t).

Proof.

According to Proposition
4.1.1
, for any time
t\geq 0
:

(26)

\partial_{t}\chi_{t}^{(r,s,m)}(x)=-m(d-m+1)\chi_{t}^{(r,s,m)}(x)-\mathcal{L}_{x}^{(r,s)}[\chi_{t}^{(r,s,m)}](x).

One readily infers from (
26
):

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{x}^{(r,s)}\chi_{t}^{(r,s,m)}(x)=

\displaystyle x(1-x)\partial_{xx}^{2}+[(r+1)-(r+s+2)x]\partial_{x}\chi_{t}^{(r,s,m)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{m}(-1)^{k}e^{(r,s,m)}_{k}(t)\left(x(1-x)\partial_{xx}^{2}x^{m-k}+[(r+1)-(r+s+2)x]\partial_{x}x^{m-k}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{m}(-1)^{k}e^{(r,s,m)}_{k}(t)(m-k)(m-k-1)(x^{m-k-1}-x^{m-k})

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=0}^{m}(-1)^{k}e^{(r,s,m)}_{k}(t)(m-k)[(r+1)x^{m-k-1}-(r+s+2)x^{m-k}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{k=0}^{m}(-1)^{k}(m-k)\{(m-k-1)+(r+s+2)\}e^{(r,s,m)}_{k}(t)x^{m-k}

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}(-1)^{k}(m-k)[r+1+m-k-1]e^{(r,s,m)}_{k}(t)x^{m-k-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{k=0}^{m}(-1)^{k}(m-k)(m-k+r+s+1)e^{(r,s,m)}_{k}(t)x^{m-k}

\displaystyle-\sum_{k=1}^{m}(-1)^{k}(m-k+1)[r+m-k+1]e^{(r,s,m)}_{k-1}(t)x^{m-k}.

By equating coefficients on both sides of (
26
), we obtain for any
k\geq 1
:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}e^{(r,s,m)}_{k}(t)=

\displaystyle\left[-m(d-m+1)+(m-k)(m-k+r+s+1)\right]e^{(r,s,m)}_{k}(t)

\displaystyle\hskip 142.26378pt+(m-k+1)(r+m-k+1)e^{(r,s,m)}_{k-1}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[-m(d-m+1)+(m-k)(p+q-m-k+1)\right]e^{(r,s,m)}_{k}(t)

\displaystyle\hskip 142.26378pt+(m-k+1)(p-k+1)e^{(r,s,m)}_{k-1}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-k(p+q-k+1)e^{(r,s,m)}_{k}(t)+(m-k+1)(p-k+1)e^{(r,s,m)}_{k-1}(t).

∎

Using this proposition, we obtain the following corollary (we recall the notation
\lceil{\cdot}\rceil
for the ceiling function):

Corollary 5.3.2
.

Let
z\in(0,1)
and
t\geq 0
. Then

\partial_{t}T_{t}^{(r,s,m)}(z)=[2(m-\lceil{mz}\rceil)-(p+q)]T_{t}^{(r,s,m)}(z)+p-2(m-\lceil{mz}\rceil)\\
+(\frac{\nabla^{(m)}T_{t}^{(r,s,m)}(z)}{T_{t}^{(r,s,m)}(z+\frac{1}{m})})(p-m+\lceil{mz}\rceil+1))({m-\lceil{mz}\rceil}).

Proof.

Let
z\in(0,1)
. We put
n=m-[mz]
hereafter.
We start by differentiating
T^{(r,s,m)}(t)
in the time variable
t
. Recall:

\displaystyle T_{t}^{(r,s,m)}(z)=\frac{n}{m-n+1}\frac{e^{(r,s,m)}_{n}(t)}{e^{(r,s,m)}_{n-1}(t)},

Hence,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}T^{(r,s,m)}(z)=\frac{n}{(m-n+1)e^{(r,s,m)}_{n-1}(t)}\frac{de^{(r,s,m)}_{n}}{dt}(t)-\frac{T_{t}^{(r,s,m)}(z)}{e^{(r,s,m)}_{n-1}(t)}\frac{de^{(r,s,m)}_{n-1}}{dt}(t).

Appealing to Proposition
5.3.1
, we infer the following equation for the derivative of
T_{t}^{(r,s,m)}(z)
:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}T_{t}^{(r,s,m)}(z)

\displaystyle=(n(n-1)-n(p+q))T_{t}^{(r,s,m)}(z)+{n}(p-(n-1))

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt-T_{t}^{(r,s,m)}(z)(n-1)((n-2)-(p+q))-\frac{T_{t}^{(r,s,m)}(z)}{T_{t}^{(r,s,m)}(z+\frac{1}{m})}(p-(n-2))({n-1}).

Writing the quotient:

\frac{T_{t}^{(r,s,m)}(z)}{T_{t}^{(r,s,m)}(z+\frac{1}{m})}=-\frac{\nabla^{(m)}T_{t}^{(r,s,m)}(z)}{T_{t}^{(r,s,m)}(z+\frac{1}{m})}+1

and keeping in mind the relation

, we get the desired result after straightforward computations.
∎

•
