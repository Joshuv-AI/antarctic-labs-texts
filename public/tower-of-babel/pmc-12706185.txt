Intravenous fluid mismanagement: time for national stewardship and quality improvement
Authors: Andrew Breen, Ashley Miller, Alan Timmins, Greg Barton, Justin Kirk-Bayley, Marcus John Edwards Peck, Huw John Davis, Jonathan Wilkinson
Published: 2025

Introduction

In 2013, National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) published Clinical Guideline 174 (CG174),1 guiding healthcare providers in UK hospitals on the safe administration of intravenous fluid therapy. This guidance was subsequently updated in 2017 and reviewed in 2021 without being substantially altered. The guidance provides educational insights into patients’ physiological requirements for water and electrolytes before categorising the indications for intravenous fluid prescription as routine maintenance, resuscitation, replacement and redistribution requirements. The guidance is supplemented by numerous posters, graphics and other aide-memoires to facilitate and promote good practice. Widespread implementation of this guidance will individualise and rationalise intravenous fluid prescribing practice and minimise the potential harms associated with incorrect or injudicious use of intravenous fluids. Given recent global shortages in access to intravenous fluids, adoption of best-practice, as well as new initiatives such as ‘Sip til Send’,2 ensures optimal and sustainable use of intravenous fluids across healthcare.

Intravenous fluid mismanagement causes patient harm and increases costs

The potential harms associated with intravenous fluid mismanagement arise from the effects of fluid overload, dehydration and electrolyte abnormalities. There are also harms associated with inappropriate prescription of colloid preparations. Intravenous fluids also need to be considered in the wider context of other routes of fluid and electrolyte administration, for example, enteral/parenteral nutrition, intravenous and effervescent medications. Commonly occurring harms associated with intravenous fluid prescription

Fluid overload, dehydration and electrolyte imbalance each can lead to increased duration of hospital stay, incurring additional costs and reduced bed availability. Each of these can arise through the prescription of the incorrect fluid formulation or the incorrect volume of fluid. When associated with intravenous fluid prescribing errors, the consequent harms and additional costs are avoidable and unnecessary.

Understanding the scale of intravenous fluid exposure and patient harm

Intravenous fluid prescription is extremely commonplace across all inpatient medical specialties. It is a necessary component of the treatment of all patients with suspected sepsis, those undergoing major surgery and any patient unable to meet their daily requirements of fluid and electrolytes. Analysis of global intravenous fluid production and intravenous fluid sales reveals that billions of litres are produced each year. Further, data suggest that, across the UK, Australia and the USA alone, millions of exposures in millions of patients occur each year.3 These figures indicate that the overall prevalence of intravenous fluid use must be high, and the scale of intravenous fluid use suggests that improvements to prescribing practice have the potential to reduce harm across the entire inpatient population.

Quality measurement and management in intravenous fluid prescription

When CG174 was introduced, the authors suggested that one in five patients receiving intravenous fluids suffered harm.4 While such a figure is eye-catching, there is little data to describe the true extent of the harm associated with intravenous fluid prescription. This is unsurprising, given the difficulty of attributing harm to intravenous fluids in complex, heterogeneous patient populations. Outcome measures describing complications from intravenous fluid prescription are challenging to isolate: such complications are non-specific and occur in many disease processes, so establishing a cause and effect for intravenous fluid-related harm is difficult. For example, while adverse consequences of fluid overload include pulmonary oedema, delayed mobilisation after surgery or chest infection, it is complex to attribute episodes of any of these directly to the intravenous fluid management of a patient. Viewed from the opposite perspective, for a patient who develops a hospital-acquired pneumonia as a consequence of iatrogenic fluid overload, it is unlikely that the link between the two will be established and recorded.

In the absence of reliable outcome measures, we must look towards structure and process measures to ensure that the correct actions are taken to minimise the potential harm of intravenous fluids. NICE Quality Standard 66 (QS66)5 presents four quality statements as follows:

Hospitals must have an intravenous fluids lead who has overall responsibility for training, clinical governance, audit and review of intravenous fluid prescribing and patient outcomes.

The requirement to ensure adequate training and to produce 24-hour management plans has been achieved on small-scale improvement projects6 and represents realistic standards.

Regarding incident reporting, NICE suggested a number of surrogate outcomes that can be used when assessing the quality of intravenous fluid prescription. The occurrence of hyponatraemia and hypernatraemia during intravenous fluid infusion, as well as the occurrence of pulmonary oedema and peripheral oedema during intravenous fluid infusion, is proposed as reportable outcome measures to be investigated by an embedded governance process.5

The quality standards therefore highlight the need for leadership, education, patient management plans and incident reporting.

A survey: current levels of engagement with NICE CG 174 in England

We report the results of a survey of National Health Service (NHS) trusts in England investigating compliance with the recommendations in CG174 and QS66.

Less than half of acute NHS trusts in England who responded have produced an intravenous fluid therapy policy that is aligned to CG174, and only 33% audited compliance against this policy. Organisational leadership of intravenous fluid therapy, either from a lead consultant (20% of respondents) or lead nurse (15% of respondents), is lacking. This indicates that intravenous fluid therapy is not seen as a priority for quality improvement within the NHS in England and highlights the need for a nationwide improvement project to raise the standards of intravenous fluid prescription.

Why is there poor engagement with an established NICE Guideline?

Some possible explanations for poor engagement with NICE recommendations are as follows.

Since the introduction of CG174, there has been very little national emphasis on compliance or quality improvement regarding the published guidance. In contrast to other national initiatives (such as venous thromboembolism prophylaxis or prevention of bloodstream infections), providers are not held to account by NHS England regarding intravenous fluid prescription.

System-wide quality improvement projects require significant resource and must be able to yield measurable patient benefit to justify resource allocation.7 8 The absence of outcome measures specific to intravenous fluid management makes it challenging to link improvement activity to patient outcomes. It is possible that the absence of suitable accountability measures has been a deterrent to the implementation of a national programme to improve compliance with CG174.

Education in good prescribing practices

All acute clinicians have a role in intravenous fluid prescription, and it is considered core knowledge for prescribers. Despite this, education regarding intravenous fluid prescription is not consistently delivered to prescribers. Although a number of resources exist to guide the education of prescribers, there is currently no national education programme aligned to NICE CG174 for either students or professionals.

Medical graduates are expected to be able to prescribe medicines safely as soon as they commence employment,9 but consistently report feeling unprepared to prescribe intravenous fluids.10 Education resources targeting intravenous fluid prescribers improve understanding of the principles of intravenous fluid requirements and clinical application, as well as confidence of prescribers.11

Among specialists in intensive care medicine and anaesthesia, understanding of intravenous fluid prescription requirements is lacking. A survey of 1045 healthcare professionals working predominantly in these specialties across 95 countries demonstrated poor knowledge of intravenous fluid physiology and wide variation in prescribing patterns.12

Brief education interventions have been shown to be effective at aligning intravenous fluid prescribing with current guidelines13 14 and could be employed at a national level to improve practice and minimise harm.

Clinical decision support tools

Clinical decision support (CDS) tools, especially those integrated with prescribing systems, aim to improve outcomes by promoting adherence to guidelines, using patient-specific information to individualise prescriptions (eg, patient weight, electrolyte measurements) and minimising avoidable harm through prescribing errors.1517 CDS tools are therefore well-suited to optimising intravenous fluid prescribing, ensuring adherence to clinical guidelines based on current evidence. The implementation of CDS-based initiatives has been shown to enhance prescribing safety, minimising risks associated with fluid overload, electrolyte imbalances and inappropriate fluid selection.18

Overall, the integration of CDS tools into intravenous fluid prescribing workflows represents a significant opportunity to promote patient safety and clinical efficiency. By leveraging evidence-based decision support, healthcare providers can improve the precision of intravenous fluid prescription, enhance adherence to guidelines and reduce the harms associated with intravenous fluid mismanagement.

Experience of a national intravenous fluid improvement initiative

The potential for harm reduction through a large-scale intervention across entire acute hospital system has been recognised previously. The National IV Fluid Improvement Programme in Scotland19 was launched in February 2018. It was a Scottish Government initiative, run through the Modernising Patient Pathways Programme, aiming to support implementation of standard guidance on fluid therapy and support this via education, training and data collection to reduce variation.20 The programme provided support and resources for Boards to change practice to reflect NICE CG174. By the end of 2019, informal data collection indicated an improvement in following recommendations, but before formal evaluation could start in 2020, the pandemic started. Subsequently many of the staff involved were redeployed and priorities changed. After the pandemic, NHS recovery planning resulted in changed priorities; intravenous fluids were no longer seen as a priority and funding was not renewed.

Still, the Scottish programme met with considerable success in improving practice across a large geographical area and population. In the absence of an official account regarding why the programme was deprioritised, it is not which clear barriers and enablers which could be applied to a larger UK project. Nonetheless, the experience gained in Scotland could provide valuable lessons for a larger project to be attempted across the UK.

Intravenous fluid stewardship

Intravenous fluid stewardship refers to the systematic approach to optimising the administration of intravenous fluids to ensure effective, safe and patient-specific care while minimising potential harms. This concept focuses on individualised prescribing that aligns with the physiological needs of patients and the specific clinical context, avoiding common harms such as fluid overload or electrolyte disturbances. Stewardship programmes emphasise adherence to evidence-based guidelines, weight-based prescriptions and precise fluid balance monitoring, using multidisciplinary collaboration to improve clinical outcomes. Fluid balance monitoring and associated improvement interventions, in particular, present challenges. Nonetheless, improvements are achievable and can make an important difference to the quality of fluid management.21 Doctors, pharmacists and nurses all have key roles in any fluid stewardship programme.

Fluid stewardship frameworks can mitigate risks of over-resuscitation in critical care settings with tailored intervention.22 Volume overload by maintenance intravenous fluid mismanagement is commonplace throughout acute care settings and can be minimised by a comprehensive fluid stewardship approach which takes account of all sources of fluid and electrolyte administration (eg, intravenous medications).23

To strengthen the conceptual basis of fluid stewardship, its structure can be aligned with the well-established framework used across healthcare quality improvement: structure, process and outcome. The structural elements encompass organisational policy, leadership, education resources and prescribing infrastructure aligned with NICE CG174 and QS66. Process elements include competency assessment, audit and bedside review of intravenous fluid prescriptions. Outcome measures relate to reductions in iatrogenic harm, such as fluid overload, electrolyte disturbance and avoidable acute kidney injury.

A call to action for a national intravenous fluid stewardship programme

NICE CG174 provides clear guidance on intravenous fluid prescribing. This guidance was produced, and is regularly reviewed, by an expert panel in accordance with NICE procedures. The authors of this article believe that CG174 contains no controversial or incorrect recommendations and that implementing this guidance in every hospital in the UK would reduce the patient harm caused by intravenous fluid mismanagement. The scale of patient exposure to intravenous fluids strongly suggests that the potential for harm reduction is very high.

Conclusion

The management of intravenous fluid therapy in hospitals is critical to ensuring patient safety and improving clinical outcomes. While NICE CG174 provides comprehensive recommendations for the safe and effective use of intravenous fluids, the limited engagement with these guidelines across NHS trusts, as shown by our survey, highlights a significant gap in practice. Addressing this gap requires a coordinated effort to prioritise intravenous fluid stewardship, underpinned by robust leadership, consistent education and system-wide quality improvement initiatives. A national framework for intravenous fluid management—incorporating mandatory compliance policies, enhanced training programmes and effective prescribing tools—can significantly reduce patient harm and healthcare costs. By fostering accountability and embedding these principles within clinical practice, the NHS can set a benchmark for excellence in intravenous fluid therapy. Implementing these changes is not just an opportunity for improvement but an imperative to safeguard hospitalised patients from avoidable harm.
