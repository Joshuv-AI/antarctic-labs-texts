Decoupling topological and molecular features for interpretable biomolecular interaction prediction
Authors: Qi Wu, Yinbo Liu, Feng Yang, Weihong Huang, Xiaolei Zhu, Juan Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPredicting biomolecular interactions is fundamental to understanding cellular mechanisms and advancing drug discovery. However, biomolecular interactions exhibit immense diversity across multiple dimensions. Most existing computational methods are designed to handle one specific task or data modality, which limits their applicability and generalization capability in broader scenarios. To address this methodological rigidity, we propose a flexible framework for multi-modal feature fusion in biomolecular interaction prediction (FlexBIP). The core of FlexBIP lies in its modular architecture, which decouples intrinsic molecular features from complex graph topologies, enabling the adaptive integration of node attributes, edge properties, and auxiliary graph information. The flexible fusion methodology breaks through the limitations of task-specific models. This design enables FlexBIP to adaptively process and integrate biological data of different types and from various sources, including homogeneous interactions between molecules of the same type, heterogeneous interactions between different molecular classes, as well as qualitative binary, multi-class, and quantitative regression prediction tasks. Our research has yielded exciting results. In extensive testing across 15 benchmark datasets, covering 8 major categories of biomolecular associations, FlexBIP’s performance comprehensively surpasses that of 25 state-of-the-art specialized models. Crucially, in data-scarce “cold-start” scenarios that simulate the discovery of new molecules, FlexBIP continues to demonstrate remarkable robustness and predictive accuracy. Furthermore, FlexBIP provides robust and reliable interpretability for various downstream analysis tasks.

Introduction

Biological systems are governed by intricate molecular interaction networks spanning multiple organizational levels, where interactions between different biomolecules collectively form the fundamental regulatory framework underlying life processes. The systematic prediction of these interactions is crucial for elucidating disease mechanisms, discovering therapeutic targets, and ensuring clinical safety [1–3]. However, the predictive modeling of these networks faces a fundamental challenge rooted in their inherent diversity. Biomolecular interactions can be systematically categorized along two key dimensions. The first is Entity Type, where interactions can occur in homogeneous scenarios between molecules of the same type, or in heterogeneous scenarios between different molecular classes. The second is Prediction Goal, which, based on its complexity, can be divided into three task types: binary qualitative prediction, multi-class qualitative prediction, and quantitative prediction.

This diversity has spurred the development of numerous highly specialized computational methods, which, despite their success, are often limited in their design to a single prediction task. For instance, in the field of protein–protein interactions (PPIs) [4], researchers have developed specialized models such as HIGH-PPI [5], which focuses on capturing the hierarchical structure of protein complexes; the DNE [6] framework, for learning network embeddings to identify functional modules; and the MCGLPPI [7] model, which balances predictive accuracy with computational efficiency through coarse-grained molecular representations. Similarly, in the domain of drug–drug interactions (DDIs), models also focus on specific sub-problems. For example, DGAT-DDI [8] is specifically designed to capture the prevalent asymmetry in these interactions, while EmerGNN [9] is dedicated to solving the cold-start challenge for emerging drugs. Furthermore, this trend of specialization extends to models for predicting a wide range of other associations, including drug–target interactions (DTIs) [10–13], drug–target affinities (DTAs) [14–16], circular RNA (circRNA)–drug interactions [17], drug–gene interactions [18], and miRNA–disease interactions [19]. Even though some methods can handle multiple subtasks, they are often limited to specific domains. For example, the DTIAM [16] model is capable of handling multiple subtasks within the drug and target, but it cannot be applied to other interaction classes like PPIs. While these specialized models demonstrate strong performance, critical gaps remain unaddressed. This “one model for one task” paradigm creates computational silos, limits the potential for cross-domain knowledge transfer, leads to redundancy in model development, and possesses insufficient capabilities for downstream biomolecular analysis.

Flexible tools have become standard in many other areas of biological analysis. Fields like single-cell sequencing, spatial transcriptomics, and metabolomics analysis now benefit from simple, general, and flexible model frameworks with high generalization capabilities. In contrast, the field of molecular interaction prediction urgently needs a similar end-to-end tool that spans from data preprocessing and predictive modeling to downstream analysis. Furthermore, different molecular entities possess unique features, making it difficult for previous methods to establish a flexible modeling paradigm. In contrast, feature-aware Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) can directly adapt to graph structures with diverse features for end-to-end learning. Their inherent versatility serves as an ideal technological foundation for achieving flexible cross-network prediction. Inspired by this, we propose Flexible Biomolecular Interaction Prediction (FlexBIP), a framework designed to address this challenge through a flexible, multi-modal fusion architecture (Fig. 1). The core of FlexBIP’s generalizability lies in its modular design; the framework utilizes a multi-feature-aware GNN architecture and an adaptive graph structure processing mechanism, making it highly adaptable to diverse biomolecular interaction prediction tasks.

Overview of FlexBIP.

Built upon conventional GNN models, FlexBIP is designed for methodological flexibility, supporting multiple types of feature fusion, including node, edge, and auxiliary data features. By utilizing edge-feature-aware and node-feature-aware layers to process edge and node features from different perspectives, it obtains more comprehensive node representations. This enhances the model’s ability to learn topological information between nodes and strengthens its perception of edge information by incorporating additional node features. A dynamic multi-class contrastive loss function is employed to balance learning across different classes. Furthermore, for multi-hop association prediction, FlexBIP can also learn features from an auxiliary graph to enhance learning on the primary graph.

To validate the generalizability of this fusion architecture, we extensively evaluated FlexBIP by comparing it with 25 state-of-the-art methods across 15 datasets spanning 8 distinct types of biological associations, conducting 10 repeated experiments. Our work demonstrates that FlexBIP consistently outperforms various specialized models in both classic prediction tasks and challenging cold-start scenarios. In addition, we also conducted a variety of downstream analysis tasks, including principal component analysis (PCA) and hierarchical clustering analysis, multi-class comparisons based on PCA, visualization of biomolecular 3D structures and their interaction relationships, interaction network visualization, interaction association prediction result visualization, and performance evaluation of DTA prediction models. Furthermore, our model can perform interaction attribution analysis, quantifying the contribution strength of specific sites (such as drug–target pairs) in biomolecular interactions and outputting an interaction score to characterize binding affinity or functional association.

Materials and methods

Datasets and preprocessing

To comprehensively validate the versatility and robustness of a flexible prediction framework, our benchmark must represent the inherent diversity of biomolecular interactions. Inspired by this, we first systematically categorize interaction prediction tasks along two key dimensions based on their core biological and computational properties. The first dimension is entity type, which distinguishes between homogeneous interactions that occur between molecules of the same type, and heterogeneous interactions that occur between different molecular classes. The second dimension is prediction goal, which differentiates tasks based on the nature and complexity of their output. This includes binary qualitative prediction, which aims to predict the existence of a single type of interaction and is computationally a binary classification task; multi-class qualitative prediction, which aims to predict which specific type of interaction manifests between two entities from multiple possibilities and is a multi-class classification task; and quantitative prediction, which aims to predict the strength or degree of an interaction and is a regression task.

Based on the classification framework above, for homogeneous interactions, we addressed both multi-class qualitative prediction, for which we selected the PPI01 and DDI02 datasets. The PPI01 dataset, derived from the STRING) [20] database’s SHS27k [21] subset, focuses on Homo sapiens protein interactions and is a multi-classification dataset encompassing various PPI types, while the DDI02 dataset leverages the TWOSIDES [22] public dataset, known for its multiple interaction types between drug pairs, making it suitable for multi-type DDI prediction. The binary qualitative prediction, for which we used the PPI02 and DDI01 datasets, the PPI02 dataset contains Saccharomyces cerevisiae [23] protein interactions from its interactome, while the DDI01 dataset uses asymmetric DDI data from DrugBank [24]. For heterogeneous interactions, we selected the DTI01 and DTI02 datasets for binary qualitative prediction in the domain of DTIs. The DTI01 dataset is the large-scale, heterogeneous Hetionet dataset, compiled by Himmelstein et al. [25] from 29 public biomedical resources. The DTI02 dataset is composed of four subsets, sourced from the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) BRITE [26], BRENDA [27], SuperTarget [28], and DrugBank databases [29]. Finally, for quantitative prediction, we used the DTA01 dataset. The DTA01 dataset employs the KIBA dataset constructed by Tang et al. [30], which is a large-scale kinase inhibitor bioactivity dataset integrating various measurement types like Ki, Kd, and IC50. To ensure result stability, all datasets underwent 10 repeated experiments. In each experiment, 20% of the dataset was randomly sampled as an independent test set for evaluating the model’s generalization, with the remainder used as the training set for parameter learning and optimization. Modular and interpretable architecture

First, the FlexBIP model can flexibly handle various types of biomolecular inputs; whether it is a protein, drug, or target, it can be accepted as the initial input. The key to this design is its universality, which lays the foundation for subsequent flexible processing. The core design philosophy of the framework is to decouple the intrinsic features of a node from its graph structure information within the network. Rather than a purely architectural separation followed by naive concatenation, our decoupling is mechanistically enforced to prevent the semantic dilution of molecular features. FlexBIP strictly isolates the sequence-pair attention encoding from the graph convolutions. Furthermore, the integration of these decoupled streams is executed through a Conditional Fusion module. Instead of standard linear concatenation, the decoupled topological embeddings serve as contextual conditions that dynamically modulate the molecular interaction representations. In the processing workflow, the input node features are first encoded into information-dense node embedding vectors by a Feedforward Neural Network. Concurrently, edge information describing the connections between nodes is also extracted. Subsequently, the node feature matrix and edge information are jointly fed into a Graph Convolutional Network (GCN) [31] core. Within this module, the model integrates the graph’s topological structure into each node’s representation through an iterative aggregation of neighborhood information, thereby generating context-rich graph embeddings. To further refine key information, the FlexBIP model also utilizes a graph attention aggregation network to process node and edge features. This allows the model to dynamically assign different importance weights to different neighboring nodes, thereby extracting the most relevant edge information embeddings for a specific node. Furthermore, the framework supports the fusion of an Auxiliary Graph. When an external knowledge base or auxiliary dataset is available, its graph information can similarly be processed by an independent GCN to generate auxiliary graph embeddings, providing additional informational dimensions to the main model.

As shown in Fig. 1, after the node, graph, edge, and potential auxiliary graph embeddings are obtained, these feature representations from different dimensions are concatenated to form a comprehensive and rich combined feature vector. This vector is then input into a multi-class similarity decoder, which ultimately predicts whether an interaction exists between a specific pair of molecules and outputs a prediction score. Furthermore, when the input data includes sequence text information, such as the amino acid sequences of proteins and the Simplified Molecular-Input Line-Entry System (SMILES) strings of drugs, FlexBIP activates a powerful Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers (BERT)-like [32] sequence-pair encoder. This encoder can process biological sequence pairs as if they were natural language, deeply capturing complex dependencies within and between sequences through a multi-head attention mechanism. This process not only generates highly informative sequence-pair embeddings but also calculates the attention weights for various sites (e.g. amino acid residues or chemical groups) between the molecule pair. These weights intuitively reflect which key regions the model focused on when making a prediction (such as the highlighted ASP (aspartic acid)-231 residue in the figure), thereby greatly enhancing the interpretability of the model’s prediction results and helping researchers understand the underlying mechanisms of intermolecular interactions.

Performance on multiple biomolecular network prediction tasks

For a comprehensive validation of a flexible prediction framework’s versatility and robustness, we carefully selected seven representative benchmark datasets. These datasets cover the main combinations within our framework, allowing for a rigorous test of the model’s generalization capabilities. We compared FlexBIP against various baseline methods (K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) [33], Random Forest (RF) [34], Logistic Regression (LR) [35], GCN [31], MCGLPPI [7], DeepNano [36], HIGH-PPI [5], GRACE [37], VGNAE [38], DNE [6], DeepDDI [39], MeTDDI [40], DGAT-DDI [8], CompGCN [41], SkipGNN [42], EmerGNN [9], KGE_NFM [10], MPNN_CNN [11], DTIAM [16], IHDFN-DTI [12], TransformerCPI [13], DeepDTA [14], and GraphDTA [15]) on the PPI01, PPI02, DDI01, DDI02, DTI01, DTI02, and DTA01 datasets. The results presented in Fig. 2 demonstrate that FlexBIP possesses significant advantages across multiple datasets and tasks, validating its generalization capability across diverse scenarios. Furthermore, FlexBIP not only achieves highly statistically significant performance improvements over the top-performing baselines (Wilcoxon signed-rank test, P < .01, as annotated with ** in Fig. 2), but is also less susceptible to random fluctuations, thereby exhibiting stronger robustness. Ablation and parameter sensitivity study

To explore the role of the components of the FlexBIP model in different graph structures and task types, we designed ablation experiments for homogeneous graphs and heterogeneous graphs, and compared the performance changes of the model in multi-classification, single-classification, and affinity prediction tasks. We constructed four FlexBIP model variants: one (“w/ BCELoss”, i.e., with Binary Cross-Entropy Loss) refers to the modification where the multi-class contrastive loss function of the FlexBIP model is removed, and the BCE loss function is adopted instead; another did not use node features (“w/o NodeFeat”) to observe the impact of missing node-related information on the model; the third omitted edge feature processing (“w/o EdgeFeat”) to evaluate the importance of edge features; and the fourth (“w Dot Decoder”) indicates the removal of the similarity linear decoder from the FlexBIP model, with the dot product decoder being used in its place.

The superior performance of FlexBIP stems from three core mechanisms: (i) structural feature decoupling: FlexBIP isolates intrinsic molecular features from complex graph topologies, integrating node, edge, and auxiliary features adaptively. Ablation studies (e.g. “w/o NodeFeat,” “w/o EdgeFeat”) confirm their necessity for capturing context-rich embeddings. (ii) Dynamic multi-class contrastive loss: to combat severe class imbalance in biomolecular networks, we utilize a margin-based contrastive loss with dynamic negative sampling to balance training objectives. (iii) Sequence-pair attention encoding: a BERT-like encoder captures complex intra- and inter-sequence dependencies via multi-head attention, generating informative embeddings and pinpointing interaction sites for enhanced accuracy and interpretability.

To investigate how the model responds to diverse parameters and shed light on how different hyperparameters influence its performance, we carried out a comprehensive sensitivity analysis of the model’s parameters. As shown in Fig. 3b, by systematically adjusting parameters such as the number of GCN network layers, the number of hidden features, the dropout rate, the learning rate, and the weight decay coefficient, we deeply explored the relationship between different hyperparameters and the model performance. The parameter sensitivity analysis for other datasets can be found in Supplementary Fig. S1.

For practical deployment, we recommend the following parameter strategies: (a) architecture: use 4–8 GCN layers and 128–256 hidden features for standard datasets but switch to shallower layers for massive graphs to avoid over-smoothing and avoid dimensions over 500 to prevent overfitting. (b) Regularization: apply moderate dropout (0.1–0.2) and low weight decay (1 × 10−5) for standard tasks but enforce stricter constraints for cold-start scenarios. Conclusion

Predicting diverse biomolecular interactions is central to advancing systems biology and medicine, as these interactions collectively regulate cellular functions, disease mechanisms, and therapeutic outcomes. Despite recent advancements that have improved prediction accuracy, most existing tools are still limited to single interaction types [5, 9, 10]. They lack the ability to unify modeling across multiple biological networks, offer poor interpretability, and often struggle to integrate multimodal features or support comprehensive downstream analysis.

FlexBIP addresses these limitations by providing a flexible framework for multi-type biomolecular interaction prediction. It performs effectively across various association prediction tasks, including DDIs, PPIs, DTIs, DTAs, circRNA–drug interactions, drug–gene interactions, miRNA–disease interactions, and gene–disease interactions. Its feature-aware design provides broad applicability, enabling the model to handle diverse biomolecular interaction and affinity prediction problems, demonstrating strong generalization capabilities to adapt to different data features and structural challenges. FlexBIP also supports a variety of downstream analyses and performs well even in cold-start scenarios involving new molecules.

Like other graph-based methods, FlexBIP has a potential limitation: it relies on the availability of underlying network structures. Nevertheless, FlexBIP’s versatility allows it to extend beyond the tested interaction types, with promising applications for modeling other crucial interactions, such as microRNA–target or circRNA–protein interactions. Its interpretable downstream analyses, including interaction attribution scores, further enhance its utility in mechanistic exploration. To biologically contextualize these outcomes, FlexBIP provides a robust mechanistic basis with significant clinical implications for developing improved treatment protocols. Specifically, pinpointing exact binding affinities guides the optimization of lead compounds to reduce off-target toxicity. Furthermore, our network-level predictions provide actionable hypotheses for drug repurposing and preemptive risk assessment of adverse effects, ultimately enhancing patient safety prior to clinical trials.

We anticipate that FlexBIP, as an open-source tool, will become a powerful framework for advancing biomolecular interaction research, empowering researchers to leverage multimodal network data for prediction and functional interpretation in low-data and cross-task scenarios.
