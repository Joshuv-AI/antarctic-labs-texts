Skin-mimicking biogel-based iontronic sensor with hierarchical bionic coupling for dexterous tactile e-skin
Authors: Yidan Chen, Shu Wang, Xilu Ye, Chenghui Lv, Jialin Wei, Yingxin Zhang, Jianfeng Ping, Yibin Ying, Lingyi Lan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe human fingertip skin, with its interlocked epidermal-dermal architecture and dense tactile receptors, enables unique perception and inspires the design of innovative electronic skin (e-skin). However, most e-skin materials fail to replicate the hierarchical integration of composition, structure, and function found in natural skin. Drawing direct inspiration from the dermal extracellular matrix–a composite hydrogel reinforced by a collagen-hyaluronic acid, here we report an e-skin that mimics key composition and architectural features of human skin. By incorporating sodium lactate and montmorillonite nanosheets into a gelatin matrix, we engineer a biomimic gel with mechanical and hydration properties similar to those of natural skin through dynamic ionic crosslinking and hydrogen bond networks. The microstructure resembling the dermal papillae is designed using a sandpaper-templating strategy. The resulting iontronic sensor achieves high sensitivity (466.3 kPa−1), rapid response (47 ms), and a wide pressure detection range (20 Pa–2000 kPa). We further develop a stretchable, ultrathin, hand-shaped iontronic sensor array that integrates seamlessly with a dexterous robotic hand, achieving precise, nondestructive grasping and high-fidelity, multichannel pressure mapping.Subject terms: Polymers, Mechanical properties, Sensors and biosensors

Introduction

Human fingertip skin, a multilayered structure of interlocked epidermis and dermis, is densely innervated with tactile receptors that enable exquisite mechanosensation1,2. This sophisticated architecture has inspired the development of electronic skins (e-skins) capable of dual-adapting sensing (both fast-adapting and slow-adapting)3–6 and high-resolution tactile perception7–9 using capacitive pressure sensors based on electrical double-layer (EDL) effect10,11. However, current implementations primarily rely on polymer films or ionic elastomers12–15, materials that are fundamentally dissimilar to natural skin or represent simplistic composites, thus failing to fully replicate the native skin environment. Moreover, most existing studies mainly simulate dermal papillae structure through abrasive surface patterning or spacer-assisted contact modulation without clarifying the intrinsic coupling between material properties and structural functions16–18. This gap limits the integrated emulation of skin components, structural design, and advanced sensory behaviors.

The dermis, which constitutes the bulk of natural skin, can be viewed as a composite hydrogel of collagen, elastin, and hyaluronic acid19,20. The dense three-dimensional network of collagen and elastin in the dermis imparts strength and elasticity to the structure, while the evenly distributed hyaluronic acid provides hydration. Continuous interlocking “epidermal spine-dermal papillae” structures connect the dermis to the epidermis, enabling rapid stress dissipation21. Merkel disk receptors embedded in these structures sense light touches, whereas deeper Ruffini endings detect large deformations, together ensuring high sensitivity and a broad perceptual range22. Replicating such structural-functional synergy with hydrogel-based materials offers a compelling strategy for tactile sensor design23.

Here, we report an e-skin that progressively mimics the composition, structure, and function of the dermis, achieving skin-like mechanical properties with enhanced tactile performance. We engineered a biomimic gel (biogel) by incorporating sodium lactate (SL, a natural moisturizing factor) and montmorillonite (MMT) nanosheets into a gelatin network, hereafter referred to as GSM biogel. Ionic crosslinking between SL and gelatin enhanced mechanical strength and hydration, while electrostatic interactions with MMT provided additional non-covalent crosslinking, further improving toughness (Fig. 1a). Free ions from MMT, together with engineered microstructures, produced a tunable EDL effect that enabled nanoscale charge separation, yielding the pressure sensor with a high sensitivity (466.3 kPa−1), rapid response (47 ms), and a wide pressure detection range (20 Pa–2000 kPa). Leveraging the high-performance of the sensor, we developed a stretchable, ultra-thin, hand-shaped iontronic sensor array that can conform seamlessly to a dexterous robotic hand (Fig. 1b). We further integrated this sensor array with signal acquisition system to construct a tactile system. This system provides real-time, multi-channel pressure feedback, enabling the dexterous robotic hand to perform precise and non-destructive object manipulation (Fig. 1c). Our approach outlines a versatile strategy for creating next-generation e-skins with tailored properties for advanced haptic applications.

Results

Skin-mimicking design of GSM biogel

Inspired by the structural composition of the dermis, gelatin, SL, and MMT were selected to prepare GSM biogel. Gelatin exhibits reversible temperature-dependent phase transition, characterized by the self-assembly of its disordered polypeptide chains into short local triple helices. This structural similarity to the secondary structure of natural collagen makes gelatin an ideal material for mimicking the properties of collagen fibers24. Pure gelatin hydrogels (15 wt%) exhibited weak mechanical strength (elastic modulus: 11.41 kPa; tensile stress: 46.95 kPa), far below that of natural skin (0.1–2 MPa) (Fig. 2a, Supplementary Fig. 1, and Supplementary Table 1)25,26. Incorporation of SL (20 wt%) produced a gelatin-SL (GS) biogel with enhanced tensile stress (0.73 MPa) and elastic modulus (0.20 MPa), attributed to ionic interactions between SL carboxyl groups and gelatin amino groups (Fig. 2a, Supplementary Fig. 1, and Supplementary Table 1)27. Further addition of ultrasound-exfoliated MMT yielded a GSM biogel with enhanced properties (tensile stress: 1.88 MPa; elastic modulus: 1.40 MPa), attributed to secondary crosslinking induced by the negatively charged MMT nanosheets (Fig. 2a, Supplementary Fig. 1, and Supplementary Table 1)28. The uniform dispersion of these nanosheets promoted the minimization of the length distribution of polymer chains between the nanosheets. Consequently, a densely entangled polymer structure was formed, improving both the elastic modulus and toughness of the biogel.

To elucidate the interactions between SL, MMT, and the gelatin matrix in GSM biogel, we performed attenuated total reflectance-Fourier transform infrared (ATR-FTIR) spectroscopy and Raman spectroscopy. The incorporation of SL and MMT induced blue shifts in the amide III (C-N), amide II (N-H), and amide I (C=O) bonds of gelatin from 1232 cm−1, 1531 cm−1, and 1628 cm−1 to 1247 cm−1, 1575 cm−1, and 1637 cm−1, respectively, indicating strengthened coordination among gelatin amino groups (Fig. 2b)29. These shifts suggested ionic crosslinking between positively charged ammonium ions in gelatin and the negatively charged carboxylates in SL and surface charges of MMT nanosheets27. The N-H stretching band near 3313 cm−1 shifted to lower wavenumbers upon SL and MMT addition, suggesting enhanced hydrogen bonding (Fig. 2b)30. Raman spectra further showed intensified C-O stretching in both GS and GSM biogels, reflecting increased carboxyl content from SL, while the amide I and amide A bands shifted to higher and lower wavenumbers, respectively, confirming strengthened intermolecular and hydrogen-bonding interactions (Fig. 2c)30. These results indicated that SL and MMT synergistically strengthen the gelatin network through the formation of enhanced ionic crosslinking and hydrogen bonding, thereby modulating the intermolecular interactions within the biogel matrix (Fig. 2d).

To assess the impact of MMT weight ratio on biogel performance, a series of biogels with varying formulations was prepared (Supplementary Table 2). Increasing the content of MMT led to a decrease in the light transmittance across the visible spectrum (Supplementary Fig. 2a). At an MMT content of 1 wt%, insoluble MMT began to settle at the bottom of the solution, with their size and number increasing at higher concentrations (Supplementary Fig. 2b). The incorporation of MMT at concentrations below 0.5 wt% resulted in a biogel with enhanced mechanical properties, as tensile stress increased from 0.73 MPa to 1.88 MPa and the elastic modulus rose from 0.20 MPa to 1.40 MPa. This enhancement is attributed to the role of MMT as a physical crosslinker, where hydroxyl groups on its layered surfaces form hydrogen bonds with gelatin and SL. When the MMT ratio exceeded 0.5 wt%, tensile tests showed decreased elastic modulus and tensile strength (Fig. 2e and Supplementary Fig. 3). Excessive MMT reduces the spacing between charged layers and promotes aggregation into larger particles, which act as stress concentrators and weaken the mechanical properties of the biogel. Besides, MMT nanosheets contain abundant dissociable Na+ ions in their interlayer spaces that act as charge carriers. The ionic conductivity increased with MMT content up to 0.5 wt% but decreased at higher loadings (Supplementary Fig. 4), attributed to the fact that well-dispersed MMT nanosheets at low concentrations facilitate ion transport by providing negatively charged surfaces and generating local electric fields, while at excessive concentrations, densely packed MMT nanosheets physically obstruct continuous ionic pathways, hindering ion mobility31. Together, these results identified 0.5 wt% MMT as the optimal concentration for balancing mechanical and electrical performance.

Enhancing and stabilizing hydration is of paramount importance for biogel to achieve skin-like mechanical compliance for long-term reliability and maintain stable ionic conductivity for high-performance sensing. To assess the influence of SL and MMT on the hydration of the biogel, we prepared biogels with varying formulations (Supplementary Tables 2, 3). The biogels stabilized at 45–50 wt% of their initial weight after 12 h, with negligible dependence on MMT content, indicating a minimal role of MMT in water retention (Supplementary Fig. 5). In contrast, SL substantially enhanced both softness and water retention (Supplementary Figs. 6, 7). However, when the SL concentration exceeded 20 wt%, surface droplet aggregation occurred, identifying 20 wt% as the optimal concentration that maximizes hydration benefits while preserving structural homogeneity.

The hydration behavior indicated that the biogel underwent substantial water loss immediately after gelation and reached an equilibrium state after 12 h. Tensile tests were therefore performed to investigate the evolution of mechanical properties during this equilibration process. The results revealed that the elastic modulus increased significantly after 12 h at 22 °C and 60% relative humidity (RH) (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Fig. 8), which was attributed to the formation of triple helix structures in the gelatin chains and the density of their entanglement. The crystallinity of the gelatin was further assessed using differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) and X-ray diffraction (XRD). The DSC tests revealed that the glass transition temperature (Tg) of the GSM biogel rose with prolonged storage time, increasing from 34.7 °C to 46.7 °C, which suggested the formation of additional triple helical structures supported by amorphous gelatin chains (Fig. 2g). XRD further showed distinct diffraction peaks at approximately 6.2° and 7.5° (the diameters of the triple helix perpendicular to the chain direction) and a peak at around 31.6° (relating to single helix structures) after 6 h of storage. Additionally, characteristic amorphous protein peaks appeared near 20° after 12 h, suggesting reorganization from disordered chains to a stable three-dimensional protein network (Fig. 2h). These structural evolutions paralleled gradual moisture loss, implicating water content as a key factor governing molecular interactions and mechanical stability (Supplementary Fig. 9). The GSM biogel after 12 h of equilibration demonstrated slight weight variation under different humidity conditions (Supplementary Fig. 10), reflecting the intrinsic hygroscopic nature of SL within the network. Accordingly, all biogels used in subsequent mechanical tests and sensor fabrication were equilibrated before use to ensure consistent performance.

The resulting GSM biogel exhibited uniform stretchability (Supplementary Fig. 11) and high compressive strength, with a compressive modulus of 0.31 MPa (0–30% strain), higher than that of pure gelatin hydrogel (0.02 MPa) (Fig. 2i and Supplementary Fig. 12a). Additionally, the GSM biogel featured stable compressive resistance, as confirmed by maintaining 92.1% of its original height after being subjected to an external force of 100 N (Supplementary Fig. 12b). Cyclic compression tests revealed a hysteresis loop during the first compression cycle exhibits, attributed to non-covalent interactions within the viscoelastic hydrogel network temporarily break and reform during the initial loading. In subsequent cycles, the progressive reduction in hysteresis indicated that the internal network gradually reached a dynamic equilibrium, leading to stabilized energy dissipation (Fig. 2j)24. The combination of skin-like composition, enhanced hydration retention, mechanical resilience and ionic conductivity of the GSM biogel makes it a compelling material for high-performance tactile sensors.

Skin-mimicking design of the iontronic sensor

In human fingertip skin, dermal papillae enhance pressure sensitivity by increasing contact area. Mimicking this structure, a graded intrafillable structure fabricated by sandpapers was patterned on the biogel surface to enhance sensitivity and expand working range (Supplementary Fig. 13)32. Additionally, inspired by Merkel disk receptors and Ruffini endings, which enable light-touch and deep-pressure perception, respectively5, we designed an iontronic sensor by sandwiching biogel between Cu/polyethylene terephthalate (PET) electrodes (Fig. 3a). A 500-μm-thick polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) ring served as a spacer to physically separate the biogel from the upper electrode, thereby ensuring a low initial capacitance in the absence of external pressure. A 100-μm-thick PDMS film was further employed as an encapsulation layer to enhance the hydration stability of the biogel (Supplementary Fig. 14a), which helps maintain consistent performance under different humidity conditions (Supplementary Fig. 14b). Benefiting from the bionic design, the sensitivity of the iontronic sensor with microstructure was measured at 213.7 kPa−1 within 39.3 kPa, 39.4 kPa−1 over the range of 39.3–937.4 kPa, and 12.3 kPa−1 over the range of 937.4–2000 kPa (Supplementary Fig. 15a), which was higher than that without microstructure (13.5 kPa−1 within 372.9 kPa, 66.4 kPa−1 over the range of 372.9–703.7 kPa, 18.6 kPa−1 over the range of 703.7–2000 kPa) (Supplementary Fig. 15b). Notably, the introduction of the microstructure effectively increases the effective contact area between the biogel and electrode, optimizes stress distribution under external stimuli, and facilitates ion transport at the interface. These effects amplify the iontronic response to subtle pressure variations, thus enhancing the sensor’s sensitivity compared to its planar counterpart32,33. The standalone incorporation of 0.5 wt% MMT enhanced the sensitivity achieving values of 17.2 kPa−1 within 557.3 kPa, 60.4 kPa−1 over the range of 557.3–1083.8 kPa, and 36.0 kPa−1 over the range of 1083.8–2000 kPa (Supplementary Fig. 15c), which was due to the increase in the dielectric constant of the biogel, resulting in an increase in the capacitance value of the parallel plates. Under the dual effects of 0.5 wt% MMT and microstructure, the sensitivity of the iontronic sensor was ultimately enhanced to 466.3 kPa−1 within 18.6 kPa, 86.0 kPa−1 over the range of 18.6–683.7 kPa, and 27.2 kPa−1 over the range of 683.7–2000 kPa (Fig. 3b). The dissociable Na⁺ ions released from the interlayer of MMT effectively boost the carrier concentration, which in turn accelerates interfacial ion migration under pressure stimulation and amplifies the signal response, ultimately driving a substantial enhancement in the sensor’s sensitivity. The iontronic sensor was not only able to detect subtle pressure changes with a limit of detection (LOD) of 20 Pa (Fig. 3c), but also able to operate reliably under high-pressure ranges like weight-bearing activities (Supplementary Fig. 16). The iontronic sensor demonstrated response/recovery time of 48/57 ms under 5 kPa (Fig. 3d) with a pressure-relevant dynamic response (Supplementary Fig. 17), and maintained stable, low-creep performance over thousands of compression cycles (Supplementary Fig. 18). The iontronic sensor was also able to respond to the continuous pressure with different loading speeds, with its response rate scaling accordingly (Supplementary Fig. 19), highlighting its dynamic adaptability. Owing to the optimized biogel material and tailored device design, the overall sensitivity and working range of the iontronic sensor surpassing most reported iontronic sensors with microstructures (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Table 5).

Finite element simulations combined with equivalent circuit analysis elucidate the pressure-dependent sensing mechanism of the iontronic sensor and explain the three sensitivity regions observed experimentally. Initially, the sandpaper-like top microstructure was separated from the electrode by an air gap, resulting in a total capacitance dominated by the air capacitance (Cair), as EDL capacitance between the GSM biogel and the lower electrode (CEDL0) was usually several orders of magnitude larger than Cair34 (Fig. 3f). Under low pressure (<20 kPa), the simulations showed strain concentrated at the tips of the microstructures, where initial contact with the electrode occurred (Fig. 3g and Supplementary Table 4). This contact induced the accumulation of Na⁺ ions at the interface, forming distributed EDLs whose capacitance scaled with both contact area and ion concentration (Fig. 3f). The resulting rapid increase in contact area accounts for the high initial sensitivity of 466.3 kPa−1 observed experimentally (Fig. 3b). As pressure increased to an intermediate range (20–700 kPa), the finite element simulation revealed that deformation propagates from the tips to the sidewalls of the microstructures with a progressive contact area growth (Fig. 3g), leading to a capacitance approximated by the EDL capacitance at the contacting interface (CEDL) (Fig. 3f). In the high-pressure region (>700 kPa), the simulations indicated that the microstructures become gradually compressed, with further deformation governed by bulk compression of the biogel. This transition in deformation mechanism corresponded to a change in the equivalent circuit, where the total capacitance approaches the series combination of the upper and lower interface EDL capacitances ((CEDL−1 + CEDL0−1)−1)35. The GSM biogel, characterized by its mechanical properties and biomimetic microstructure design, contributes to the iontronic sensor’s broad operating range and high sensitivity. This combination lays a solid foundation for the application of integrated e-skin in bionic tactile sensing.

Hand-shaped iontronic sensor array

A hand-shaped iontronic sensor array that conforms seamlessly to a dexterous robotic hand was further developed to emulate the tactile sensing capability of the human hand (Fig. 4a). The sensor array was designed according to the primary contact regions during grasping, consisting of a 1 × 2 sensing unit on each fingertip and a 3 × 3 unit on the palm (Supplementary Fig. 20). The electrodes were fabricated by screen-printing stretchable silver paste (Supplementary Fig. 21) onto a thermoplastic polyurethane (TPU) substrate, forming circular, serpentine, and square patterns (30 μm thick) with teardrop-shaped connections (Supplementary Fig. 22). The circular patterns (5 mm diameter) served as the sensing electrodes in direct contact with the biogel, while the serpentine interconnects (0.4 mm width) were designed to accommodate tensile strain. The square patterns were aligned with the finger joints of dexterous robotic hand to enhance wear resistance during repeated bending. To further enhance stretchability and fracture resistance, PET fixing discs (200 μm thick) were attached to the outer side of the TPU substrate beneath the circular electrodes (Supplementary Fig. 23), and PDMS spacers (500 μm thick) were laminated onto the inner side of the TPU above the square electrodes. These components confine deformation primarily to the serpentine electrodes, forming an “island-bridge” structure in which the circular and square electrodes act as strain-isolated islands and the serpentine electrodes function as stretchable bridges32,33. This design effectively decouples mechanical strain from the sensing regions, minimizing stress concentration on the biogel and ensuring stable sensing performance under dynamic deformation. The electromechanical stability of this design was demonstrated by maximum tensile tests36, which showed that the Ag/TPU electrode sustained strains up to 60% without failure (Supplementary Fig. 24a), consistent with finite-element simulations (Fig. 4b). The cyclic tensile tests further indicated its durability under repeated deformation, with the electrode surviving 50 stretch-release cycles at 10% strain (Supplementary Fig. 24b). This design also effectively suppressed signal crosstalk, as demonstrated by the isolated biogel with PDMS spacer achieving a crosstalk rejection ratio (CRR) of 66.02 dB (Supplementary Fig. 25a), while due to ion transfer (Supplementary Fig. 26) the shared biogel exhibited a pronounced crosstalk phenomenon with a CRR of only 10.75 dB (Supplementary Fig. 25b).

Through the assembly of sandwiching GSM biogels and PDMS spacers between two Ag/TPU electrodes with PET fixing discs using the cross-marking and geometric feature alignment strategy (Supplementary Fig. 27), the hand-shaped iontronic sensor array was successfully fabricated (Supplementary Fig. 28). The resulting hand-shaped iontronic sensor array was relatively thin, with a thickness ranging from 200 μm to 1.2 mm (Supplementary Fig. 29), while maintaining stretchability and could withstand deformations such as twisting and rolling (Fig. 4b). This inherently stretchable electronic device can maintain close contact with interfaces even during movement and variations in size, thereby fulfill the complex and long-term operational demands of dexterous hands37. Beyond electromechanical stability under stretching, the uniformity of sensing performance across the array is equally critical for reliable tactile mapping. All sensing units of the hand-shaped iontronic sensor array exhibited highly repeatable sensing performance across different sensing units, demonstrating fabrication reproducibility (Supplementary Fig. 30). For integration, excess TPU substrate was trimmed, and the array was mounted on the dexterous robotic hand with double-sided tapes, followed by a 100-μm-thick PDMS encapsulation. This assembly strategy is broadly adaptable, as circuit size can be adjusted to fit different prosthetic models.

To assess the effectiveness of the hand-shaped iontronic sensor array for tactile perception, we initially tested one of the sensing units. Image recognition and other machine-assisted methods often struggle to accurately determine the ripeness of certain climacteric fruits, while tactile sensors can offer force signals related to fruit deformation, enhancing recognition accuracy22. Building on this principle, we conducted single-finger pressing tests using the robotic system consisted of a dexterous robotic hand and an industrial robotic arm to evaluate the ripeness of fruits (Supplementary Fig. 31). The finger pad or tip pressed against fruits from a defined initial position, ensuring full contact and a baseline with initial capacitance between 1 and 50 pF (Supplementary Fig. 32). During pressing, the reactive force from the fruit compressed the electrode toward the microstructured biogel, generating a capacitance change proportional to the applied force (Fig. 4d). We tested four representative climacteric fruits at two ripeness stages—kiwi, mango, plum, and peach. Each fruit was pressed three times, producing capacitance traces with consistent pre- and post-peak changes (Fig. 4e). Peak capacitance values varied among fruits, with unripe kiwi showing the strongest signal and ripe plum the weakest (Fig. 4f). The measurement mechanism of the elastic modulus of fruits was based on the Boussinesq model, which accounts for elastic deformation and stress transmission resulting from the contact force between the sensor and the fruit (Supplementary Notes 1, 2). Comparison with texture analyzer measurements revealed a strong correlation between sensor-derived and instrument-measured force, indicating that the iontronic sensor array can provide an indirect, non-destructive measure of fruit elastic modulus (Fig. 4g).

The dexterous hand integrated with the sensor array for tactile perception

Human grasping relies on the coordination of dexterous hands, sensory nerve fibers, and the central nervous system38. To emulate this behavior, industrial dexterous hand employ rod-driven finger bones for flexible grasping of objects of varying sizes and shapes. The hand-shaped iontronic sensor array was designed with shared rows and columns, reducing connection lines and improving system integration. To mimic the high-fidelity signal acquisition of biological sensory nerves, a crosstalk suppression circuit incorporating reed relays and shift registers was implemented (Supplementary Figs. 33, 34). The reed relays feature low on-resistance and an off-state insulation of up to 1011 Ω, effectively suppressing interference from leakage currents and parasitic capacitances. A sequential scanning strategy was implemented for precise, point-by-point signal acquisition across the matrix (Supplementary Fig. 35). Together with hardware, signal acquisition software, robot motion controller, and real-time visualization software on the host computer, these components formed a fully integrated tactile recognition system for adaptive grasping (Fig. 5a). Based on the design of the spacer-assisted isolated biogel and galvanically isolated circuitry, the tactile recognition system demonstrated a CRR of 56.48–62.40 dB comparable to that of the structurally isolated benchmark, confirming the combined effectiveness of the structural and circuit-level strategies (Supplementary Fig. 36).

Based on the integrated tactile recognition system capable of rapid and multi-channel signal acquisition, we tested fruits of varying sizes and shapes, which required the dexterous hand adapt its grasp while avoiding damage. The entire experimental procedure consisted of three main steps: getting close, gripping and transferring, and releasing. During each step, the hand-shaped iontronic sensor array collected electrical signal values from the corresponding areas of the dexterous hand in real time (Supplementary Fig. 37). To assess the reliability of signal acquisition under dynamic conditions, we first performed repeated grasping tests on a single fruit across multiple cycles. The hand-shaped iontronic sensor array produced reproducible signal patterns corresponding to the pressure distribution during each grasp, demonstrating its sensing stability and repeatability (Supplementary Fig. 38). We next examined the sensor array’s ability to distinguish between different fruits based on their size, shape, and required grasping gestures. Three representative examples were selected including a pear (full-hand grasp), a cherry (three-finger pinch), and a mango (secure five-finger hold). The resulting capacitive heatmaps showed distinct spatial patterns, with activated channels corresponding to the contacting regions. Signal changes were most pronounced during the gripping and transferring stages, reflecting actual grasping dynamics (Supplementary Fig. 39a–d). Additionally, we conducted grasping experiments on other fruits, including apple, tomato, carambola, cucumber, and kiwi, and obtained similar results (Supplementary Fig. 39e–i). These results confirmed that the integrated hand-shaped iontronic sensor array enabled effective pressure perception in dexterous grasping.

Based on the applications described above, we demonstrated that the dexterous hand integrated with the hand-shaped iontronic sensor array could detect fruit ripeness via single-channel signals and distinguish fruit types via multi-channel signals. Building on this, we investigated whether multi-channel signals could further differentiate ripeness levels within the same fruit during grasping, a scenario more representative of agricultural harvesting. Peaches at controlled ripening stages were grasped using a consistent gesture. As observed previously, capacitance changes were most pronounced during gripping and transfer. Peak capacitance decreased systematically with increasing ripeness from hard to soft peaches, consistent with single-finger pressing results (Fig. 5b–d). To enhance classification accuracy, we employed machine learning (ML) to extract discriminative features and construct multidimensional information models, and we further developed the Tactile Classification-Fruit Ripeness Model (TC-FRM). The dataset comprised 244 samples categorized into three ripeness levels (soft, middle, and hard) (Fig. 5e). Among these, 189 samples served as the development cohort (split 7:3 for training and internal testing), while 55 independent samples were reserved for external validation. Dimensionality reduction with t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) revealed well-separated clusters, confirming that tactile features extracted from raw signals were highly discriminative (Fig. 5f). Following feature extraction and normalization, we systematically evaluated several supervised learning algorithms (see Methods for details). Among the tested models, including support vector machine (SVM), gradient boosting (GB), extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), logistic regression (LR), k-nearest neighbors (kNN), and naive bayes (NB), the random forest (RF) model demonstrated the best performance, achieving a cross-validation (CV) score of 0.9692 and 100% accuracy on the internal test set (Fig. 5g–h and Supplementary Fig. 40). Optimal hyperparameters and CV results were summarized in Supplementary Tables 6, 7. These results established RF as the core model for subsequent analyses. External validation on the 55 independent samples yielded 100% prediction accuracy, demonstrating the model’s ability to capture intrinsic biological features rather than noise (Supplementary Fig. 41). Finally, we embedded the TC-FRM into a robotic manipulator system, enabling real-time and accurate classification of fruit ripeness (Supplementary Fig. 42 and Supplementary Movie 1). This integration highlighted the potential of the TC-FRM to endow robotic systems with tactile intelligence, enabling broader applications in the human-machine interaction.

Discussion

In conclusion, we developed a bioinspired e-skin that unites hierarchical material design, microstructure engineering, and heterogeneous encapsulation to replicate both the multilayered architecture of human skin and the spatial distribution of Merkel disk receptors and Ruffini endings. This design enhances ionic charge carrier density and optimizes contact mechanics, enabling high sensitivity to gentle pressure and a broad detection range under large deformations. We further assembled the iontronic sensor into a hand-shaped iontronic sensor array and integrated them into a dexterous robotic hand with ML-enabled signal processing, achieving precise spatial tactile perception in real time. Demonstrations of grasping tests on fruits of varying shapes and ripeness levels validate the system’s capability for real-time object assessment. These results establish a robust platform for advanced tactile sensing, human-machine interaction, and next-generation humanoid robotics, while the scalable fabrication approach provides a practical pathway toward broader prosthetic and robotic applications.

Methods

Materials

Gelatin (G108394) was sourced from Aladdin. SL (60% aqueous solution, S817835) and MMT (sodium-based, with a specific surface area of 240 m2 g−1, M916670) were purchased from Macklin. The silicone elastomer kit (Sylgard 184) was acquired from Dow Corning. LY-508 stretchable silver paste was obtained from Shenzhen Yilai. All chemicals were used as received, without further processing.

Preparation of gelatin hydrogel

1.5 g of gelatin powder was added to 8.5 g of deionized water, and heated at 50 °C for 1 h to obtain a clear and viscous gelatin solution (Supplementary Table 1). After being stirred firmly, the gelatin solution was allowed to stand for 30 min to remove bubbles under the heating conditions. Finally, the gelatin solution was poured into the polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) mold and cooled for a specified period to obtain a gelatin hydrogel.

Preparation of GS biogel

Using the gelatin solution described above, a specific amount of SL was gradually added to the solution while stirring, ensuring that the SL content in the biogel precursors was 0 wt%, 10 wt%, 15 wt%, 20 wt%, 25 wt%, and 30 wt%, respectively (Supplementary Table 3). Finally, the biogel precursor was poured into the PTFE mold and cooled for a specified period to obtain a GS biogel.

Preparation of GSM biogel

Starting with the gelatin solution above, SL (20 wt%) was slowly incorporated into the solution under stirring. Additionally, MMT was treated with ultrasonication at 20 kHz in deionized water for 2 h, followed by continuous stirring for 20 h to prepare mother solutions at concentrations of 0.1 wt%, 0.5 wt%, 1 wt%, 5 wt%, 10 wt%, and 20 wt%. These MMT solutions were thoroughly mixed with the reaction mixture. Finally, the biogel precursor was poured into a PTFE mold and cooled to produce GSM biogel with MMT contents of 0 wt%, 0.01 wt%, 0.05 wt%, 0.1 wt%, 0.5 wt%, 1 wt%, and 2 wt%, respectively (Supplementary Table 2).

Preparation of microstructured biogel

After preheating the glass plate to 55 °C, the precursor was poured into a reaction cell made of sandpaper (5000 #) and two glass plates, sealed with silicone rubber. The entire setup was then cooled and cured at room temperature to create a microstructured biogel.

Fabrication of microstructured biogel-based iontronic sensor

The microstructured biogel, after being equilibrated at 22 °C and 60% RH for 12 h (approximately 400 μm in height), was cut into a circle with a 5 mm diameter and then sandwiched between 100-μm-thick Cu/PET electrodes, separated by a 500-μm-thick PDMS spacer. Finally, the sensor was packaged with two layers of 100-μm-thick PDMS. The initial capacitance of the sensor was approximately 1 pF.

Fabrication of hand-shaped iontronic sensor array

Electrodes and spacer patterns were designed using AutoCAD software. The electrodes were fabricated by screen printing silver paste onto a polyester fabric screen coated with a photosensitive adhesive (PLUS8000), utilizing a 200-mesh screen. This screen fabric was attached to a flexible TPU substrate that measured 100 μm in thickness. During screen printing, integrated cross-shaped alignment markers were printed at key positions on both electrodes to serve as universal reference points throughout the assembly process. The silver paste was applied to the screen and quickly printed onto the substrate under approximately 0.4 MPa of pressure. Following a drying process at 90 °C for 2 h, the Ag/TPU electrodes were successfully produced. Subsequently, a laser etching system (PLS6MW, SYS, Temotech Technology) was used to pattern the electrodes. A PDMS membrane with a thickness of 500 µm was treated using air plasma (PDC-002, Harrick Plasma) at 30 W for 40 s. The treated PDMS was then immediately bonded to one of the Ag/TPU electrodes under approximately 40 kPa of pressure. Alignment was achieved by fitting the circular openings of the treated PDMS spacers directly onto the underlying circular electrodes using the cross-shaped markers as guides, while ensuring the spacer simultaneously covered the surrounding square electrode traces to prevent unintended electrical exposure. Under optical guidance using the cross-shaped alignment markers, the microstructured biogels sensing units were then placed into the same circular openings of the PDMS spacer. Another Ag/TPU electrode, were gently assembled onto the PDMS spacer. The entire assembly was clamped between patterned PET fixing discs, each with a thickness of 200 µm, and finally encapsulated in a 100-μm-thick PDMS encapsulation.

Mechanical tests

Tensile and compression tests on the biogels were performed using an electronic universal testing machine equipped with a 100 N load cell (UTM2102, Shenzhen Suns Technology Co., Ltd). For the tensile test, samples were cut into dumbbell shapes measuring 4 mm in width and 1 mm in thickness using a mold knife. These samples were then elongated at a rate of 30 mm min−1 until failure. The elastic modulus was calculated from the slope of the linear portion of the stress-strain curve within the strain range of 0 to 10%. Unless otherwise noted, all biogel samples used for tensile test were stabilized in moisture under controlled conditions (22 °C, 60% RH, 12 h). Additionally, for the stretching of samples with different storage times, 0.5, 6 and 12 h of storage were selected, respectively. For the compression test, samples after being moisture-stabilized were cut into circular shapes with a height of 7 mm and a diameter of 5 mm and were compressed at a speed of 2 mm min−1. For the cyclic compression test, samples after equilibration were cut into circular discs (2 mm in height and 5 mm in diameter) and compressed to ~80% strain at a compression rate of 2 mm min −1.

Water retention tests

The biogels were cured and then cut into uniform blocks. The initial mass of each biogel block was recorded at this stage. The blocks were subsequently placed in a temperature and humidity-controlled chamber (22 °C, 60% RH) and weighed at various time intervals to assess water retention.

Characterizations of biogels

ATR-FTIR spectroscopy was conducted using a Nicolet iS50 (Thermo Fisher) spectrometer equipped with an attenuated total reflectance accessory to investigate the intermolecular interaction forces of gelatin hydrogel, GS biogel, and GSM biogel. Raman spectra were collected using a LabRam HR Evolution Raman system (Jobin Yvon Horiba) with a 785-nm argon laser as the excitation source. DSC was performed using a TGA2 (Mettler-Toledo) instrument, heating the sample from 20 °C to 100 °C at a rate of 10 °C min−1 under a nitrogen atmosphere with a flow rate of 50 mL min−1. XRD tests were carried out with a Bruker D8 Advance diffractometer at a scanning speed of 2° min−1 over a range of 5° to 60°.

Ionic conductivity tests

The ionic conductivity of GSM biogels with varying MMT contents was measured using the AC impedance technique on an electrochemical workstation (CHI760E, Chenhua). Samples were assembled in a symmetric cell configuration consisting of two stainless steel electrodes. Measurements were conducted at an initial voltage of 0 V, with an amplitude of 5 mV, over a frequency range of 0.1 Hz to 1000 kHz. The ionic conductivity (σc, S cm−1) of the biogels was defined as σc = (1000×L)/(Rb×S), where L (cm) represents the thickness of the biogel, Rb (Ω) is the bulk resistance determined by the intersection between the extrapolated linear portion of the impedance spectrum and the real axis, and S (cm2) denotes the effective contact area between the biogel and the stainless steel electrodes.

Characterizations of microstructured biogel-based pressure sensor

The microstructure of both the GSM biogel and the GSM biogel-based pressure sensor was examined using scanning electron microscopy (SEM, GeminiSEM560, Zeiss). External pressure was applied to the sensor using a computer-controlled electronic universal testing machine (UTM2102, Shenzhen Suns Technology). Capacitance measurements were obtained from each sensor using an LCR meter (E4980AL, Keysight). Sensor sensitivity (S) was defined as S = δ(ΔC/C0)/δP, where P denotes the applied pressure, ΔC is the change in capacitance (C-C0), and C0 ( ~ 1 pF) is the initial capacitance. Dynamic pressures up to 2000 kPa were sequentially applied, and the capacitance at each pressure was recorded at a frequency of 1000 Hz. The sensor LOD refers to the minimum pressure that the sensor can detect, measured by an electronic tensile testing machine equipped with compression fixtures (XLD-20E, Jingkong Mechanical Testing). Response and recovery times, referring respectively to the durations required for the sensor output to reach a stable value under applied pressure and to return to its baseline after pressure withdrawal, were assessed by instantly placing and removing a weight of a certain mass directly onto the sensor and recording the capacitance signal using an LCR meter with a higher sampling rate (TH2840B, Tonghui). For the fatigue test, the testing machine was Shenzhen Suns UTM2102 with a 100 N load cell with a force control mode applying triangular waves ranging from 0 to 1000 kPa at a frequency of 1 Hz.

Finite element analysis

Finite element analysis was conducted using the commercial software Abaqus 2022. A two-dimensional model was constructed to simplify the geometry based on Broussonetia papyrifera. In modeling the microstructured GSM biogel-based pressure sensor, the GSM biogel was represented as an incompressible Neo-Hookean material with a Young’s modulus of 1.33 MPa (Supplementary Table 4). The Cu/PET electrode was modeled as a rigid plate and compressed downward. Frictionless, non-penetrable contact was assumed. The total contact area was monitored as the applied pressure was increased up to 2 MPa. In modeling the hand-shaped iontronic sensor array, the Young’s modulus and yield strength of TPU substrate, PET fixing discs, and Ag circuits were presented in Supplementary Table 4. The total contact area was observed as the biaxial stretch increased up to 60%.

Signal acquisition system

The signal acquisition system consisted of several functional modules. The microcontroller unit (MCU) was built around the STM32F103RCT6 as the central processing unit. The multiplexing module comprised a switch array constructed from reed relays and controlled by shift registers, enabling multi-channel array signal routing. The capacitive-to-digital converter (CDC) module employed two four-channel FDC2214 devices for capacitive signal acquisition. During operation, the acquired signals were first read and preprocessed by the MCU and subsequently transmitted to a personal computer via a CH340C-based USB-UART bridge, enabling real-time visualization and further data analysis.

Experimental operation of intelligent fruit recognition

The fruit experiment testing platform consisted of a CR7 six‑axis robotic arm (Dobot CR7, Shenzhen Dobot Corp) and a six-degree-of-freedom linkage-driven five-fingered dexterous hand (BrainCo Revo 1, Zhejiang Qiangnao Technology). The dexterous hand assembled with hand-shaped iontronic sensor array was connected to a signal acquisition system for sensing pressure information during grasping. Intelligent fruit recognition consisted of three main components: assessing the ripeness of fruits using a single-finger press, identifying the type of fruit through a full-hand grasp, and determining the ripeness of peaches with a full-hand grasp. The evaluation of fruit ripeness using a single-finger press was conducted on both unripe and ripe fruits, including kiwi, mango, plum, and peach. Except for peaches, all other fruits were purchased on the same day from a wholesale fruit market in Hangzhou, Zhejiang Province, ensuring freshness and the absence of surface spots or damage. Peaches were obtained from a peach orchard in Wuxi, Jiangsu Province, harvested on the same day, and transported to the laboratory via cold chain transportation for 24 h. All fruits were then placed in a constant environment (25 °C) and allowed to ripen for 10 to 16 days, depending on the variety (e.g., kiwi for 16 days, mango for 10 days, plum for 12 days, and peach for 12 days). A robotic arm controlled sensor displacement to ensure a consistent indentation depth of 3 mm. Given the varied shapes and sizes of different fruits, a full-hand grasp was employed for apples, pears, and tomatoes; a three-finger grasp for cherries, carambolas, cucumbers, and kiwis; and a secure five-finger grasp for mangoes.

Data processing and feature extraction

The raw datasets were categorized into three ripeness levels (soft, middle, and hard), and 19-channel signals from each sample were extracted for analysis. For each fruit dataset, baseline removal was first applied to confine the initial capacitance values within the range of 1–50 pF. Subsequently, a band-pass fast Fourier transform filter with cutoff frequencies of 0.125–0.375 Hz was employed to suppress high-frequency noise and reduce undesired signal interference, followed by a sliding-window smoothing algorithm with a window size of 10 points to enhance signal stability. Based on this preprocessed data, feature engineering was performed to extract both statistical and temporal descriptors from the multichannel time-series signals. Extracted features included basic statistical measures such as mean, standard deviation, minimum, maximum, median, range, skewness, and kurtosis, quantile features at 25%, 75%, and 90%, differential features including the mean, standard deviation, and maximum absolute value of the first-order differences, and peak-related features derived from the number of detected peaks. To minimize the effects of dimensional inconsistency and outliers, all features were standardized using the RobustScaler method, resulting in a multidimensional feature matrix that served as the input for subsequent model training and analysis.

Supervised learning modeling and parameter optimization

To systematically compare the performance of different classification methods, we selected a diverse set of commonly used supervised learning algorithms and optimized their hyperparameters using grid search with five-fold CV. All models were evaluated in parallel to ensure a fair comparison. RF is an ensemble method that aggregates multiple decision trees to improve robustness and reduce overfitting. GB sequentially fits weak learners to minimize residual errors, and XGBoost extends this strategy with enhanced regularization and computational efficiency. SVM is a margin-based classifier that identifies the optimal separating hyperplane and leverages kernel functions to handle nonlinear decision boundaries. LR is a linear model that estimates class probabilities via the logistic function, with L1 regularization applied for feature selection. kNN is a distance-based classifier that assigns labels according to the majority vote among the nearest neighbors. NB is a probabilistic classifier based on Bayes’ theorem under the assumption of conditional independence among features, and it generally performs well with default parameter settings, requiring little to no additional tuning. For all models except NB, the parameter search ranges are summarized in Supplementary Table 8.

Model evaluation

The dataset was divided into training and test sets at a 7:3 ratio using a stratified split to preserve class distribution. Five-fold CV was performed on the training set to evaluate different hyperparameter combinations. In each fold, four subsets were used for training and one for validation, and the average validation accuracy was calculated. The optimal hyperparameters were selected based on the highest mean validation accuracy. The final model was retrained on the full training set and evaluated on the independent test set. Performance was assessed using overall accuracy and confusion matrices. Both raw and normalized confusion matrices were reported to provide a comprehensive evaluation of class-wise prediction performance.
