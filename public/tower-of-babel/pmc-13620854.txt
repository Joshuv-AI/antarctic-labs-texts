A One Health Molecular Investigation and Exploratory Epidemiological Analysis of Cryptosporidium spp. in A Shared Human‐Animal Ecosystem
Authors: Majed H Wakid, Dipesh Aryal, Sharifuzzaman, Saifa Saba Hossain, Abu Rakib M Beni Amin, Md Shafiqul Islam, Md Shahiduzzaman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction Cryptosporidium spp. are enteric protozoan parasites of public health importance, particularly in low‐resource settings where humans, animals and environmental water sources frequently interact. This study investigated the prevalence, molecular diversity and environmental exposure‐related variables in relation to Cryptosporidium infection in a rural community.MethodsA cross‐sectional study was conducted using 180 faecal samples collected from humans, cattle, chickens, fish, cats, dogs, and ducks. Speciation and subtyping were determined through PCR amplification and sequencing of the 18S rRNA and gp60 genes. Epidemiological and environmental data were collected, and univariable analyses were conducted. An exploratory multivariable logistic regression model was subsequently performed for the human subset.ResultsThe overall prevalence of Cryptosporidium infection was 9.40%, with the highest rates observed in humans (14.8%) and cattle (13.5%). Molecular characterisation identified C. hominis, C. parvum, C. bovis and C. felis. Among humans, C. hominis was the predominant, whereas C. parvum was also detected, including mixed infections. gp60 analysis identified multiple subtype families, including Ia, Ib, IIa, IIc and IId. Phylogenetic analysis demonstrated close genetic relationships between Bangladeshi isolates and previously reported reference strains from South Asia and the Middle East. Higher odds of Cryptosporidium infection were observed among individuals reporting river bathing and consumption of untreated water, whereas lower odds were observed among those reporting regular handwashing. No statistically significant associations were identified between evaluated environmental variables and infection among non‐human hosts.ConclusionThe study demonstrated the circulation of multiple Cryptosporidium species and subtype families within a shared human‐animal ecosystem. The contaminated surface water and inadequate hygiene practices may represent important environmental exposure interfaces for Cryptosporidium infection in the study area. However, given the cross‐sectional design and limited number of positive cases, the epidemiological findings should be interpreted as exploratory associations rather than definitive transmission pathways. Further longitudinal One Health investigations are needed.Keywords: Cryptosporidium, environmental exposure, gp60 subtyping, molecular epidemiology, One Health, rural Bangladesh, water‐associated transmission, zoonotic parasites

Introduction

Species of the genus Cryptosporidium are protozoan parasites of significant public health and veterinary concern, causing cryptosporidiosis in humans and animals across diverse ecological settings (Helmy and Hafez 2022; Gattan et al. 2024). The disease manifests as a spectrum ranging from asymptomatic carriage to severe diarrhoea and vomiting, and can be fatal, particularly in young children and immunocompromised individuals (Wakid et al. 2026; El‐Wakil et al. 2023). In low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs), cryptosporidiosis is a leading cause of diarrhoeal disease in children under five years of age and contributes substantially to childhood morbidity, growth faltering and mortality (Snelling et al. 2007; Steiner et al. 2018). The burden is particularly high in South Asian countries, including Bangladesh, India and Nepal, where inadequate water, sanitation and hygiene (WASH) infrastructure, close human–animal interactions and environmental contamination facilitate sustained transmission (Karim et al. 2024; Dhakal et al. 2023; Murugesan et al. 2017). Within the Hindu Kush Himalayan (HKH) region, mixed livestock production and shared water resources further increase opportunities for zoonotic transmission (Ghimire et al. 2020). Cryptosporidium has been reported from numerous hosts, including humans, cattle, buffaloes, sheep, goats, pigs, horses, yaks, companion animals, wildlife, birds and fish, underscoring its broad host range and the importance of a One Health approach to understanding its epidemiology (Zahedi and Ryan 2020; Hatam‐Nahavandi et al. 2019).

Transmission of Cryptosporidium occurs through multiple pathways, including direct person‐to‐person contact, zoonotic spillovers from animals and ingestion of contaminated food or water. C. hominis and C. parvum are the species most frequently associated with human disease; however, other species such as C. meleagridis, C. andersoni, C. canis and C. felis have also been reported (Xiao et al. 2002; Stensvold et al. 2014), highlighting the parasite's broad host range and zoonotic potential. In Bangladesh, the close proximity between humans and domestic animals, coupled with shared water sources and open defecation practices, creates an environment highly conducive to cross‐species transmission within a One Health framework.

Livestock, especially cattle, are significant reservoirs for zoonotic transmission. Infected calves often experience reduced productivity and increased mortality, leading to substantial economic losses (Roblin et al. 2023). Species such as C. parvum, C. bovis, C. andersoni and C. ryanae are commonly detected in cattle, with C. parvum posing the greatest zoonotic risk to humans (Bulumulla et al. 2025; Yan et al. 2025). Poultry, including chickens and ducks, also contribute to environmental contamination. Zoonotic genotypes such as C. meleagridis, C. parvum and C. baileyi have been identified in birds (Zhou et al. 2004; Gomes et al. 2012; Wu et al. 2021). While C. hominis has occasionally been reported in birds, its presence is generally considered rare and may represent mechanical passage rather than true infection (Wu et al. 2021). Other domestic animals such as goats, dogs and cats are increasingly recognised as potential reservoirs, harbouring species including C. xiaoi, C. ubiquitum, C. canis, and C. felis, respectively, all of which possess zoonotic potential (Ryan et al. 2014). These animals shed environmentally resistant oocysts into surrounding environments, contaminating soil and, critically, water sources used by humans and other animals.

Environmental contamination, particularly of surface water, plays a pivotal role in Cryptosporidium transmission in rural Bangladesh. The Brahmaputra River, which flows through many communities, is frequently used for bathing, washing and as a direct recipient of domestic and livestock waste. The widespread use of contaminated surface water for daily activities, including recreational exposure, represents a major pathway for human infection (Xiao and Feng 2017; Kotloff et al. 2013). Previous studies in Bangladesh have reported Cryptosporidium prevalence in children (2.3%) and calves (15.7%) (Karim et al. 2024), underscoring the ongoing public health challenge. However, these studies have primarily focused on either human or animal populations independently, thereby neglecting the complex interactions occurring at the human–animal–environment interface.

Fish, both wild and farmed, have recently emerged as hosts of epidemiological interest. Although piscine‐specific species have been described, zoonotic species such as C. parvum and C. hominis have also been detected in fish worldwide (Couso‐Pérez et al. 2022; Golomazou and Karanis 2020; Koinari et al. 2013). In Bangladesh, aquaculture is widespread and is often conducted in natural water bodies vulnerable to faecal contamination. Despite previous investigations of helminth parasites in fish (Chandra 2006; Aunamika et al. 2022), Cryptosporidium remains largely uninvestigated in this context.

Despite increasing knowledge regarding cryptosporidiosis, a comprehensive understanding of the molecular epidemiology and interconnected transmission dynamics of Cryptosporidium across multiple host species, including humans, cattle, poultry, fish and domestic cats, within a shared high‐risk environment in Bangladesh remains limited. This study addresses this critical knowledge gap by investigating the prevalence, molecular diversity and transmission pathways of Cryptosporidium in a rural Bangladeshi community. By employing molecular tools targeting the 18S rRNA (Xiao et al. 1999; Xiao and Feng 2017) and gp60 genes (Oladele et al. 2025), this study aims to identify the circulating species and subtypes among diverse hosts and to elucidate potential zoonotic and anthroponotic transmission routes. The findings will provide crucial insights to support integrated One Health interventions focusing on water safety, sanitation infrastructure, livestock management and hygiene education, ultimately contributing to the reduction of cryptosporidiosis in Bangladesh and other LMIC settings.

Materials and Methods

Study Area and Environmental Context

This cross‐sectional study was conducted in Seshmore village, Boyra, in Mymensingh, Bangladesh (24°43′6.31″N, 90°26′38.04″E) (Figure 1). The village, with a population of approximately 7000 and a land area of 3.357 km2, lies along the Brahmaputra River and is characterised by dense human settlement, poor sanitation and frequent human‐animal interactions.

Map of Seshmore village, Boyra, Mymensingh, Bangladesh, showing the study area along the Brahmaputra River.

The community includes small‐scale hybrid cattle farms, free‐ranging livestock (cattle, sheep and goats), stray dogs and cats and free‐roaming poultry and ducks that access the river.

Field observations were conducted to characterise environmental conditions and human‐animal‐environment interactions within the study area, including water sources, sanitation, livestock management and other behavioural factors relevant to potential Cryptosporidium transmission. Sanitary infrastructure was found to be consistently inadequate, with frequent leakage of human faecal matter from poorly maintained latrines and open drainage systems into nearby water bodies. Household wastewater from kitchens and bathrooms was observed being discharged directly into ponds and rivers. River water was frequently used by residents for bathing and washing utensils. Livestock, including cattle and goats, had unrestricted access to riverbanks for grazing, defecation and bathing. Additionally, free‐ranging poultry were observed scavenging near waste piles and water sources, and untreated livestock manure was commonly dumped near water bodies (Figure 2). These environmental and behavioural observations indicate conditions that may facilitate environmental contamination and exposure to Cryptosporidium at the human‐animal‐environment interface. Therefore, the area provided an appropriate setting for investigating the prevalence and molecular diversity of Cryptosporidium within a One Health framework.

Potential environmental exposure interfaces for Cryptosporidium within a shared human‐animal ecosystem. (1) Leakage of human faecal matter from poorly maintained latrines and open drainage systems; (2) Disposal of faecal‐contaminated waste from free‐ranging poultry near water bodies; (3) Recreational bathing by children and adults in river water potentially contaminated by human or animal waste; (4) Grazing and defecation of cattle along riverbanks;

(5) Unrestricted access of goats and other livestock to surface water sources; (6) Direct discharge of untreated household wastewater into ponds and rivers; (7) Use of river water for washing utensils and domestic purposes; (8) Bathing of livestock in rivers, potentially contributing to environmental contamination through faecal shedding; (9) Fishing and other water‐contact activities occurring within shared water sources; (10) Disposal of untreated livestock manure near water bodies.

Sample Collection

A total of 180 samples were collected from humans and animals in the study area. Human stool samples (n = 54) were obtained voluntarily from community residents following informed consent. The study included 15 symptomatic participants and 39 asymptomatic participants. Recruitment was based on voluntary participation and was not stratified by age or clinical status. Symptomatic participants were identified based on a history of diarrhoea.

Animal samples included faeces from cattle (n = 37), chickens (n = 19), cats (n = 17), dogs (n = 13) and ducks (n = 15). Animal faecal samples were obtained either directly from the rectum or from fresh deposits to minimise environmental contamination.

Fish were collected from local markets present in the study area that were caught from the nearby river (Brahmaputra). For gut content sampling, fish were surface‐sterilised with 70% ethanol before dissection to minimise external contamination. The entire gastrointestinal tract, from the oesophagus to anus, was aseptically removed, and its contents were collected.

All samples were collected with informed consent and transported to the laboratory in sterile containers on ice. Specimens were preserved in 2.5% potassium dichromate at 4°C until processing. Prior to DNA extraction, samples were thoroughly washed by six rounds of centrifugation and rinsed with cold distilled water to remove the preservative. Information on diarrhoeal history was obtained from human participants using a structured questionnaire. The history of diarrhoea was defined as the occurrence of at least three loose or watery stools within a 24 h period at any time during the two weeks preceding sample collection. Additional information on livestock management such as housing, feeding and health status was recorded from household observations and interviews.

DNA Extraction and Genotyping

DNA was extracted from all collected samples using QIAamp Fast DNA Stool Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Germany) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The extracted DNA was evaluated for both quality and quantity prior to downstream analyses. DNA concentration and purity were assessed by the Multiskan SkyHigh Microplate Spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific) based on the A260/A280 ratio. DNA samples with an A260/A280 ratio of 1.8‐2.0 were considered to be of acceptable purity for PCR analysis. DNA integrity was verified by agarose gel electrophoresis. To further validate the quality and PCR amplifiability of the extracted DNA, amplification of a host constitutive housekeeping gene (β‐actin) was performed as an internal control. Eight samples initially failed one or more DNA quality criteria and were re‐extracted. Following re‐extraction, all samples met the predefined quality criteria and yielded successful β‐actin amplification. Therefore, all 180 samples were included in the subsequent pathogen‐specific PCR analysis.

Genotyping was performed using a two‐step nested PCR protocol to amplify the 18S rDNA gene (Ryan et al. 2003). For the primary PCR, a PCR product of 763 bp was amplified using the forward primer 18SiCF2 (5‐GACATA TCA TTC AAG TTT CTG ACC‐3) (base pair position 292) and the reverse primer 18SiCR2 (5‐CTG AAG GAGTAA GGA ACA ACC‐3) (base pair position 1007). The primers used in this study were synthesised by Macrogen Inc., Korea, and were purified using the multiple oligonucleotide purification cartridge method before PCR amplification.

The PCR reaction was performed in 25 µl volume containing 12.5 µL of 2 × PCR Master Mix (Takara, Japan), 0.5 µM of each forward and reverse primer, 2 µL of template DNA and nuclease‐free water to adjust the final volume. Positive controls included DNA extracted from field isolated Cryptosporidium species confirmed by sequence analysis. Negative controls consisted of PCR reactions with nuclease‐free water in place of template DNA.

Amplification was performed in a miniPCR (Oxford) with the following cyclic conditions: The reaction consisted of 40 cycles of denaturation at 94°C for 30 s, annealing at 58°C for 30 s and extension at 72°C for 30 s, preceded by an initial denaturation at 94°C for 5 min and followed by a final extension at 72°C for 10 min. For the nested PCR, a 587 bp fragment was amplified using 1µl of the primary PCR product and the primers 18SiCF1 (5′‐CCT ATC AGC TTT AGA CGG TAG G‐3′; position 289) and 18SiCR1 (5′‐TCT AAG AAT TTC ACC TCT GAC TG‐3′; position 851), as described by Ryan et al. (2003). The conditions for the secondary PCR were identical to those for the primary PCR.

GP 60 Subtyping

Gp60 subtyping was performed only on samples that tested positive for Cryptosporidium by nested PCR targeting the 18S rRNA gene. Samples yielding successful 18S rRNA amplification were subsequently subjected to nested PCR targeting the 60 kDa glycoprotein (gp60) gene for subtype identification of Cryptosporidium species, including C. hominis and C. parvum, following the protocols of Alves et al. (2003) and Pinto et al. (2021). The primary PCR was performed using primers AL3531 (5′‐ATA GTC TCC GCT GTA TTC‐3′) and AL3535 (5′‐GGA AGG AAC GAT GTA TCT‐3′), yielding a product of approximately 1000 bp. The secondary (nested) PCR employed primers AL3532 (5′‐TCC GCT GTA TTC TCA GCC‐3′) and AL3534 (5′‐GCA GAG GAA CCA GCA TC‐3′), producing an amplicon of about 850 bp (Alves et al. 2003). Both primary and secondary reactions were carried out under the same thermal cycling conditions: an initial denaturation at 94°C for 3 min, followed by 35 cycles of 94°C for 45 s, 52°C for 45 s and 72°C for 60 s, with a final extension at 72°C for 5 min. The PCR mixture composition was identical to that used for 18S rRNA gene amplification. DNA from previously confirmed Cryptosporidium isolates of known species and subtypes were used as positive controls and nuclease‐free water for negative controls.

Sequencing

The 18S rRNA and gp60 gene fragments amplified by nested PCR were analysed by electrophoresis on a 1% agarose gel. DNA bands of the expected sizes were excised and purified using the Universal DNA Purification Kit (Tiangen, China). The purified products were sequenced using the Sanger dideoxy chain‐termination method (Sanger et al. 1977) on an ABI 3730xl DNA Analyser at GeneCreate Biotech, China, for species confirmation and gp60‐based subtyping.

All obtained sequences were carefully edited and curated prior to analysis using BioEdit v7.2.5. Raw chromatograms were visually inspected using sequencing analysis software, low‐quality bases at the ends were trimmed and ambiguous nucleotide calls were resolved where possible. Only high‐quality sequences were used for BLASTn searches against the GenBank nucleotide database.

C. parvum subtypes were designated based on (i) the major subtype family determined by BLAST similarity, (ii) the numbers of TCA and TCG repeats in the trinucleotide repeat region and (iii) the number of ACATCA repeats immediately following the trinucleotide repeat region (Sulaiman et al. 2005). The 18S rRNA gene sequences were deposited in GenBank under accession numbers LC879018‐LC879026, LC886093‐LC889096, and PV123885. The gp60 gene sequences were deposited under accession numbers LC887699‐LC887706 and LC887708‐LC887712.

Phylogenetic Analysis

Sequences obtained from genotyping were compared with reference sequences retrieved from the GenBank nucleotide database of the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). Representative sequences from humans and animals were selected based on previous publications and sequence availability in GenBank. These sequences were used to construct phylogenetic trees illustrating the genetic relationships between human and animal Cryptosporidium isolates.

The evolutionary history was inferred using the neighbour joining method (Saitou and Nei 1987), chosen for its practical advantages in relation to our study objectives, particularly for preliminary screening and visualisation of genetic diversity. The optimal tree is presented, with the percentage of replicate trees in which the associated taxa clustered together in the bootstrap test (1,000 replicates) shown next to the branches (Felsenstein 1985). Evolutionary distances were calculated using the Maximum Composite Likelihood method (Tamura et al. 2004) and are expressed as the number of base substitutions per site. This analysis involved 21 nucleotide sequences, with all ambiguous positions removed for each sequence pair (pairwise deletion). The final dataset comprised 836 positions. Evolutionary analyses were performed using MEGA11 (Tamura et al. 2021).

Statistical Analysis

The data were analysed to assess the prevalence and distribution of Cryptosporidium spp. infection across multiple host species and to explore behavioural, environmental and management‐related variables associated with infection. The primary outcome variable was infection status (positive or negative), determined by amplification of the 18S rRNA gene.

Descriptive statistics were computed to summarise infection frequencies and prevalence rates across the seven host groups included in the study: humans, cattle, chickens, fish, cats, dogs and ducks. Host‐level differences in prevalence among species were evaluated using Pearson's chi‐square (χ2) test.

For the human subset (n = 54), univariable analyses were conducted to explore associations between infection status and selected exposure‐related variables, including river bathing, washing utensils in the river, presence of manure near water sources, drinking water treatment practices, handwashing practices, drinking water source and recent history of diarrhoea. All variables were categorised as binary or categorical variables based on questionnaire responses. Variables related to hygiene and water treatment practices were classified according to the consistency of reported behaviours. Associations between exposure variables and infection status were evaluated using Pearson's chi‐square test, and odds ratios (ORs) were calculated to describe the direction and magnitude of associations.

For non‐human hosts (n = 126), environmental and husbandry‐related variables were similarly evaluated using univariable analyses. These variables included grazing near riverbanks, livestock access to river water, presence of manure near water sources and discharge of wastewater into rivers. Associations between these variables and infection status were assessed descriptively using Pearson's chi‐square test.

Due to the relatively small number of positive human cases identified in the study, the exploratory multivariable logistic regression analysis was intentionally restricted to a limited number of biologically relevant and non‐overlapping exposure variables to minimise model overfitting. Variables selected for inclusion in the final exploratory model included river bathing, untreated drinking water consumption and handwashing practice. Adjusted odds ratios (AORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to estimate associations between selected exposure variables and human Cryptosporidium infection.

Given the cross‐sectional design and limited number of positive cases, the multivariable findings were interpreted as exploratory epidemiological associations intended to support hypothesis generation rather than definitive causal inference. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and statistical significance was considered at p < 0.05.

Results

Prevalence of Cryptosporidium Infection Across Host Species

A total of 180 faecal samples were collected from various host groups, including humans (n = 54), cattle (n = 37), chickens (n = 19), fish (n = 25), cats (n = 17), dogs (n = 13) and ducks (n = 15). Among the 54 human participants, 15 (27.8%) were symptomatic and 39 (72.2%) were asymptomatic at the time of enrolment. The overall prevalence of Cryptosporidium infection across all samples was 9.40% (17/180; 95% CI: 5.6%–14.7%). The highest infection rate was observed in humans (14.8%, 8/54; 95% CI: 6.6%–27.1%), followed by cattle (13.5%, 5/37; 95% CI: 4.5%–28.8%). Lower prevalence rates were found in fish (8.0%, 2/25; 95% CI: 1.0%–26.0%), chickens (5.3%, 1/19; 95% CI: 0.1%–26.0%) and cats (5.9%, 1/17; 95% CI: 0.1%–28.7%). No Cryptosporidium infection was detected in samples from dogs or ducks. The distribution of Cryptosporidium infection among the different host species was statistically significant (Pearson's χ2 = 11.32, p = 0.045) (Table 1).

Phylogenetic Analysis of 18s rRNA and GP60

Phylogenetic analysis based on partial gene sequences of 18s rRNA revealed clear clustering of Cryptosporidium isolates according to species identity (Figure 3A). The neighbour‐joining tree resolved four well‐supported clades corresponding to C. hominis, C. parvum, C. felis and C. bovis, with Bangladeshi isolates clustering within their respective species clades alongside global reference sequences, indicating high genetic similarity and limited geographic structuring. The Bangladeshi C. bovis isolates clustered tightly with global counterparts, supported by strong bootstrap values (≥96%). Notably, Bangladeshi C. hominis isolates formed a tightly supported subcluster (bootstrap ≥ 99%).

Consistent results were obtained from gp60‐based phylogenetic analysis, which resolved two major clades corresponding to C. parvum and C. hominis (Figure 3B). Isolates generated in this study (blue dots) clustered with reference sequences from Asia, Africa, Europe and Australia, with several Bangladeshi isolates forming well‐supported subclusters, suggesting local genetic relatedness. Toxoplasma gondii was used as an outgroup to root both trees.

Human‐Associated Exposure Variables

Univariable analysis of human‐associated exposure variables (Table 3) identified several variables that showed statistically significant differences in relation to Cryptosporidium infection. Individuals who reported river bathing showed a higher prevalence of infection (28.6%) compared to those who did not (9.1%) (p = 0.030). Similarly, washing utensils in the river and the presence of livestock manure near water sources showed a higher prevalence of infection. Conversely, regular handwashing and treatment of drinking water before consumption showed a lower prevalence of infection. Individuals with a recent history of diarrhoea also showed a higher prevalence of infection.

Animal‐Associated Environmental Variables

No statistically significant associations were identified between evaluated environmental variables and Cryptosporidium infection among animal hosts (Table 5). Infection prevalence was numerically higher among animals exposed to river‐associated environmental conditions, including grazing near riverbanks and the presence of manure near water sources; however, these differences were not statistically significant.

Discussion

This study investigated the prevalence, molecular diversity and exploratory epidemiological associations of Cryptosporidium spp. within a shared human‐animal ecosystem in rural Bangladesh using a One Health framework. The findings demonstrated the circulation of multiple Cryptosporidium species and subtype families across humans, cattle, poultry, fish and domestic cats, highlighting the ecological complexity of cryptosporidiosis in resource‐limited rural settings. Overall prevalence was 9.4%, with the highest infection rates observed in humans (14.8%) and cattle (13.5%). Molecular analyses identified C. hominis, C. parvum, C. bovis and C. felis, while gp60 subtyping revealed multiple subtype families, including Ia, Ib, IIa, IIc and IId. Exploratory multivariable analysis further suggested that river bathing and consumption of untreated water may represent important environmental exposure interfaces, whereas lower odds of Cryptosporidium infection were observed among individuals reporting regular handwashing.

The prevalence of Cryptosporidium infection identified among humans in this study was higher than previously reported estimates from broader community‐based studies in Bangladesh (Karim et al. 2024). This difference may reflect the localised environmental conditions of the study area, which was characterised by dense human settlement, close human–animal interactions, inadequate sanitation and extensive reliance on shared surface water sources. Similar environmental conditions have previously been associated with increased transmission of enteric protozoan parasites in low‐resource settings (Kotloff et al. 2013; Steiner et al. 2018). The prevalence observed in cattle was comparable to previous reports from Bangladesh and other South Asian settings, supporting the recognised role of bovines as important reservoirs of zoonotic Cryptosporidium species (Karim et al. 2024; Robertson et al. 2020).

The predominance of C. hominis among human isolates suggests that anthroponotic transmission may play an important role within the study population. This observation is consistent with findings from many LMICs where person‐to‐person and waterborne transmission of C. hominis are common, particularly in densely populated communities with inadequate sanitation infrastructure (Xiao and Ryan 2004; Feng et al. 2018). At the same time, the detection of C. parvum in humans, cattle, poultry and fish indicates the presence of zoonotic species within the shared ecosystem. Although identical gp60 subtypes were not identified across all host species, the coexistence of zoonotic Cryptosporidium species in multiple hosts suggests overlapping environmental contamination pathways and potential opportunities for cross‐species transmission. However, because identical subtype matches were not demonstrated among host species, direct interspecies transmission cannot be inferred from the present data.

The gp60 subtype diversity identified in this study further supports the complex epidemiology of cryptosporidiosis in the region. Human‐associated C. hominis subtypes belonging to subtype families Ia and Ib have previously been associated with human infections globally, particularly in developing‐country settings characterised by waterborne and household transmission (Oladele et al. 2025; Bujila et al. 2024). Similarly, the detection of C. parvum subtype families IIa, IIc and IId is epidemiologically important because these subtype families have been linked to zoonotic and environmental transmission in both humans and livestock (Ryan et al. 2014). The detection of subtype IIaA11G2R1 in poultry and IId subtype families in cattle may indicate circulation of livestock‐associated strains within the study ecosystem, although the limited number of positive samples precludes definitive conclusions regarding host adaptation or transmission directionality.

An important finding of this study was the molecular detection of Cryptosporidium in fish collected from the Brahmaputra River system. Although gp60 amplification was unsuccessful for these isolates, 18S rRNA sequencing confirmed the presence of C. parvum. Previous studies have reported the detection of zoonotic Cryptosporidium species in fish, although the epidemiological significance of fish‐associated infections remains incompletely understood (Couso‐Pérez et al. 2022; Koinari et al. 2013). However, detection of Cryptosporidium DNA alone does not confirm active biological infection, host competence, or reservoir status. In the absence of successful gp60 subtyping, developmental‐stage characterisation, histopathological evidence, or infectivity studies, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and may reflect passive carriage of oocysts or environmental contamination rather than true infection. Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted as evidence of environmental occurrence rather than confirmation that fish act as competent reservoirs of Cryptosporidium.

Phylogenetic analyses based on 18S rRNA and gp60 sequences demonstrated close clustering between Bangladeshi isolates and previously reported global reference strains, particularly from South Asia and the Middle East. The relatively limited geographic structuring observed in the phylogenetic trees is consistent with previous studies showing substantial genetic conservation among common Cryptosporidium species and subtype families across endemic regions (Feng et al. 2018). The clustering of study isolates within well‐supported species‐specific clades further validated the molecular identification methods used in this investigation.

The epidemiological analyses identified several environmental and behavioural variables linked to human infection. Individuals reporting river bathing had significantly higher odds of infection, while consumption of untreated drinking water was also positively linked to Cryptosporidium infection. These findings are biologically plausible given the extensive use of the Brahmaputra River for bathing, washing utensils, livestock access and disposal of household wastewater observed during field investigations. Cryptosporidium oocysts are highly resistant to environmental degradation and can persist in aquatic environments for prolonged periods, facilitating waterborne transmission (Efstratiou et al. 2017; Zahedi and Ryan 2020). Similar associations between untreated water exposure and cryptosporidiosis have been documented in other endemic settings (Shoultz et al. 2016).

Lower odds of Cryptosporidium infection were observed among individuals reporting regular handwashing. This finding is consistent with previous studies highlighting the importance of hand hygiene in reducing exposure to enteric pathogens (Steiner et al. 2018). Together, these findings reinforce the importance of integrated environmental sanitation and hygiene interventions for reducing Cryptosporidium exposure in rural settings where human and animal activities frequently overlap.

No statistically significant associations were identified between evaluated environmental variables and infection among non‐human hosts. Although infection prevalence was numerically higher among animals exposed to river‐associated environments, the relatively small number of positive cases within several host groups likely limited statistical power. In addition, environmental exposure among animals within the study area may have been relatively homogeneous due to shared grazing and water‐access practices, thereby reducing the ability to detect measurable differences between exposure categories.

Overall, the epidemiological and molecular findings suggest that contaminated surface water and inadequate hygiene practices may contribute to Cryptosporidium exposure within the study setting, highlighting the importance of integrated One Health and WASH interventions to reduce environmental transmission in rural communities.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this study. First, the cross‐sectional design limits the ability to infer temporal relationships or establish causal transmission pathways between humans, animals, and environmental sources. Accordingly, although environmental and behavioural observations identified multiple opportunities for human‐animal‐environment interactions, the direction and frequency of transmission events cannot be determined. Second, although the study incorporated multiple host species within a shared ecological setting, the number of positive samples in several groups remained relatively small, which may have reduced statistical precision and limited broader generalisability. Third, the multivariable logistic regression model was intentionally restricted to a limited number of biologically relevant variables because of the small number of positive human cases. Therefore, the observed associations between river bathing, untreated drinking water, handwashing and infection should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis‐generating rather than confirmatory.

Finally, environmental water samples were not directly tested for Cryptosporidium contamination, which limited the ability to microbiologically confirm waterborne transmission pathways suggested by epidemiological observations.

Despite these limitations, this study provides important baseline data regarding the molecular epidemiology of Cryptosporidium within a high‐risk rural ecosystem in Bangladesh. The integration of molecular characterisation across multiple host species with environmental and behavioural exposure assessment represents an important contribution to understanding the ecology of cryptosporidiosis in resource‐limited settings. The findings support the need for future longitudinal One Health investigations incorporating environmental sampling, larger population sizes and genomic approaches to better characterise transmission dynamics and environmental contamination sources.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated the circulation of multiple Cryptosporidium species and subtype families within a shared human–animal ecosystem in rural Bangladesh, highlighting the epidemiological complexity of cryptosporidiosis in resource‐limited settings. C. hominis and C. parvum were the predominant species detected, with zoonotic species identified across humans, cattle, poultry, fish and domestic cats. The detection of diverse gp60 subtype families further suggests substantial genetic diversity within the study area.

Exploratory epidemiological analyses indicated that river bathing and consumption of untreated water may represent important environmental exposure interfaces for human infection, whereas lower odds of infection were observed among individuals reporting regular handwashing. These findings, together with field observations of poor sanitation and extensive human‐animal interaction, support the potential role of contaminated surface water in maintaining environmental transmission cycles.

Importantly, this study provides one of the few integrated molecular investigations of Cryptosporidium across multiple host species and environmental exposure settings in Bangladesh using a One Health framework. Although the cross‐sectional design and relatively small number of positive cases limit causal inference, the findings provide valuable baseline evidence regarding parasite circulation at the human‐animal‐environment interface.

Overall, the study underscores the need for integrated One Health interventions focusing on water safety, sanitation improvement, livestock waste management and hygiene promotion to reduce exposure risks in vulnerable rural communities. Future longitudinal studies incorporating environmental sampling and larger multi‐host datasets are needed to better characterise transmission dynamics and clarify the relative contributions of anthroponotic, zoonotic and environmental pathways in cryptosporidiosis transmission.
