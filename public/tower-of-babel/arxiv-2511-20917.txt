From closed to open strings: the tensionless route in Kalb–Ramond background and noncommutativity
and Sourav Maji

Abstract
We study tensionless bosonic strings propagating in the presence of a constant Kalb–Ramond background and show how closed strings undergo a transition into open strings in this regime. The tensionless (Carrollian) limit induces a universal gluing between the worldsheet oscillators, which we generalize to include the effects of a constant B B -field. We derive the modified mixed boundary conditions, construct the corresponding gluing matrix and obtain the generalized induced vacuum as a squeezed boundary state. This vacuum continuously emerges from the closed string vacuum through a Bogoliubov transformation, providing a precise realization of the closed-to-open string transition. We further extend the analysis to toroidal compactifications, and show that the mechanism of worldsheet Bose–Einstein condensation persists unaltered. In the second part of the paper, we give a unified symplectic derivation of the open string noncommutative parameter. We show that the boundary symplectic form alone determines the noncommutative geometry, and that in the tensionless limit the B B -field term becomes the unique surviving contribution. Its inverse produces a noncommutative parameter similar to the Seiberg–Witten result, but arising intrinsically from the Carrollian worldsheet dynamics. Our results establish a coherent picture in which the emergence of open strings, boundary noncommutativity, and generalized vacuum structure all arise naturally from the worldsheet geometry of null strings in a B B -field background.

Introduction

The behavior of string theory at extremely high energies has long been a source of deep curiosity and theoretical challenge. One striking feature of this regime is the divergence of the string partition function once the temperature exceeds a critical value-the so-called Hagedorn temperature

Remarkably, in this ultra-high-energy limit, scattering amplitudes among strings become different

This high-energy regime stands in stark contrast to the familiar low-energy limit of string theory, where strings effectively shrink to point-like objects and the theory reduces to supergravity. The transition between these two extremes is governed by a single intrinsic scale: the string length
\alpha^{\prime}

T\sim 1/\alpha^{\prime}

\alpha^{\prime}\to 0

\alpha^{\prime}\to\infty

T\to 0

For a relativistic point particle, taking the mass to zero forces it to move at the speed of light along null geodesics; its worldline becomes null (lightlike). Similarly, a string’s tension plays the role of “energy per unit length.” In the tensionless limit, the string’s worldsheet-its two-dimensional spacetime trajectory-becomes a null surface in spacetime.
The study of such null strings dates back to Schild
[
6

[
7

\begin{split}S_{\text{ILST}}=\int_{\text{WS}}d^{2}\xi\,V^{a}V^{b}\,\partial_{a}X^{\mu}\partial_{b}X^{\nu}\,\eta_{\mu\nu}.\end{split}

(1.1)

Here,
V^{a}
are vector densities on the worldsheet. They replace the combination
T\sqrt{-\gamma}\,\gamma^{ab}
that appears in the Polyakov action for tensile strings. In the tensionless limit, this replacement takes the precise form

\begin{split}T\sqrt{-\gamma}\,\gamma^{ab}\quad\longrightarrow\quad V^{a}V^{b}.\end{split}

(1.2)

The right-hand side is inherently degenerate: its determinant vanishes identically, reflecting the fact that the worldsheet being a null surface.

The action
S_{\text{ILST}}

V^{a}=(1,\,0)~,

(1.3)

corresponds to fixing the worldsheet coordinates such that time evolution is generated solely along the
\tau

\begin{split}S_{\text{ILST}}^{\text{gauge-fixed}}&=\int_{\text{WS}}d^{2}\xi\,\partial_{\tau}X^{\mu}\partial_{\tau}X_{\mu}.\end{split}

(1.4)

This is precisely the action of a free massless scalar field in a Carrollian setting-often referred to as a Carroll scalar.

Tensionless limit as a worldsheet Carroll limit.

In the tensionless limit, the string becomes long and floppy. This behavior can be realized by applying a singular scaling transformation to the worldsheet coordinates
(\sigma,\tau)

\tau\to\epsilon\tau,\quad\sigma\to\sigma,\quad\alpha^{\prime}\to c^{\prime}/\epsilon,\quad\epsilon\to 0.

(1.5)

Intuitively, this rescaling causes the spatial direction
\sigma

1.5

[
20

In terms of worldsheet velocities

\frac{v}{c}=\frac{\sigma}{\tau}\to\infty,

(1.6)

which describes the regime where the speed of light on the worldsheet vanishes. This justifies interpreting the tensionless limit as an UR limit on the worldsheet. The appearance of such a Carrollian structure signals that the tensionless worldsheet geometry becomes null.

Specifically, it suggests a profound duality: Carrollian symmetries on the tensionless string worldsheet could be holographically dual to Carrollian field theories residing on the null boundary of AFS. This perspective offers a promising pathway toward a first-principles, string-theoretic realization of flat-space holography-transcending bottom-up constructions.

In this paper, our goal is to explore the detailed physics of tensionless string theory in the presence of a constant background
B

Recent work
[
14
]
studied the remarkable phenomenon where open strings emerge from closed strings in the tensionless limit of bosonic string theory.
In this limit, the worldsheet dynamics undergo an ultra-relativistic contraction, and the residual symmetry algebra transitions from two copies of the Virasoro algebra to the three-dimensional BMS
3
algebra.
The corresponding vacuum of the tensile closed string theory continuously evolves into an induced vacuum that satisfies Neumann boundary conditions,
which can be identified with the boundary state of an open string.
Physically, this process can be interpreted as a phase transition on the worldsheet, in which the perturbative closed string excitations undergo a Bose–Einstein-like condensation,
leading to the emergence of a new open string phase.
This condensation was further connected to the Hagedorn transition in string thermodynamics, where the effective string tension vanishes and a long-string configuration dominates.

In the present work, our goal is to explore the detailed physics of this tensionless phase transition in the presence of a constant background
B

The inclusion of the
B

and may modify the structure of the induced vacuum and the emergent open string boundary conditions.
We aim to investigate how the interplay between the
B
-field background and the tensionless limit tells the nature of the closed-to-open string transition.

While most discussions focus on the tensile open string, there has been growing interest in understanding what happens in the tensionless or null limit. This regime has drawn attention in recent years for its potential relevance to high-energy limits of string theory and to Carrollian or ultra-relativistic dynamics.
It is natural to ask whether the noncommutative structure that appears for tensile strings survives in this extreme regime, and if so, how it manifests when the usual canonical terms vanish.

One of the aims of this paper is to answer this question in a unified way.
We show that both the tensile and the tensionless strings can be described using the same symplectic language, and that in both cases the boundary two-form plays the central role.
In the tensile case, the metric and the
B
-field combine to produce a deformed symplectic structure whose inverse gives the familiar noncommutative parameter of Seiberg and Witten.
In the tensionless limit, the canonical momentum vanishes on the boundary, and the only surviving contribution comes from the
B
-term. However, the resulting structure still defines a consistent noncommutative phase space.
In other words, the tensionless string realizes the same type of noncommutativity as an intrinsic feature rather than as a limit of the tensile theory.

The comparison between these two regimes also highlights a more profound geometric unity.
The open-string noncommutativity that Seiberg–Witten described through operator algebras can be viewed here as a direct consequence of the boundary symplectic geometry, while the tensionless string provides a degenerate but an extension of the same structure.
This common viewpoint allows us to see the tensionless string not as a separate system, but as the natural endpoint of the same geometric mechanism that governs the tensile theory.

Main results: part I.

In this paper, we derive the boundary conditions of a constant Kalb–Ramond background field that arise in this background and show that they take a mixed form, interpolating between purely Neumann and oblique conditions depending on the strength of the Kalb–Ramond field.

Then, we construct the induced vacuum state of the theory, which we call the generalized induced vacuum. This state is characterized by a constant matrix that identifies left- and right-moving oscillators in a background-dependent way. When the Kalb–Ramond field is switched off, this matrix reduces to the identity, and the induced vacuum becomes the familiar Neumann boundary state of an open string. In this sense, the presence of the antisymmetric background deforms the gluing between the two sectors while preserving its overall structure.

We then show that the induced vacuum can be written explicitly as a squeezed state built from pairs of left- and right-moving oscillators. This state automatically satisfies all the required gluing conditions and represents the physical ground state of the tensionless string in the background field. The construction reveals that, in the Carrollian limit, a closed string effectively behaves as an open string attached to a space-filling brane whose worldvolume geometry is modified by the background field.

Furthermore, we demonstrate that the transformation from the usual closed string vacuum to the induced vacuum can be described by a Bogoliubov transformation acting on the oscillator modes. This transformation mixes oscillators and encodes the emergence of open-string-like degrees of freedom in the tensionless limit. As the tension parameter approaches zero, the closed string vacuum continuously evolves into the induced vacuum, showing explicitly how open string behavior arises from a closed string theory in this regime.

We also extend the analysis to the case where the tensionless string is compactified on a torus with a constant Kalb–Ramond background.

Taken together, these results provide a coherent picture of how tensionless strings interact with constant antisymmetric backgrounds. The tensionless limit corresponds to a Carrollian contraction of the worldsheet geometry. The analysis clarifies the emergence of open string from closed string and establishes that the key features of the induced vacuum and worldsheet condensation persist even in the presence of Kalb–Ramond field.

Main results: part II.

In the second part of this work, we discuss the symplectic and geometric structure of the theory, showing that the tensionless worldsheet in a constant Kalb–Ramond background naturally acquires a noncommutative phase-space geometry. Our goal here is to understand, from first principles, how the intrinsic Carrollian dynamics of the null worldsheet leads to a boundary symplectic form and how this gives rise to noncommutative coordinates for the effective open-string endpoints.

Starting from the gauge-invariant action obtained in the first part, we analyze its canonical structure and identify the relevant symplectic potential associated with variations of the worldsheet embedding coordinates. When we calculate the boundary contribution to the symplectic potential, we find that the only surviving term originates from the background field and it defines a first-order symplectic form along the boundary of the worldsheet.
The inverse of this symplectic form defines the Poisson bracket on the boundary coordinates, and it has been observed that the endpoints of the tensionless string do not commute. In other words, the background field induces a natural noncommutative geometry on the space probed by the string endpoints. The strength of the noncommutativity depends on the antisymmetric 2-form
B
-field. This construction provides a precise geometric realization of how open-string-like noncommutativity arises directly from the intrinsic symplectic structure of a null worldsheet.

We then compare this result with the well-known Seiberg–Witten analysis of noncommutative geometry in the tensile regime. In the large
B
-limit, the noncommutative parameter becomes
\Theta^{ij}\sim B^{-1}
, which is the same as the noncommutative parameter computed for the tensionless string. But the origins are different: in the tensile case, it emerges as a limiting deformation of the full two-dimensional conformal field theory, whereas in the tensionless case, it is inherently encoded in the geometry of the null (Carrollian) worldsheet.

2
Tensionless strings in the presence of a uniform Kalb–Ramond field

We review the analysis of tensionless strings in the presence of a uniform Kalb–Ramond field from
[
21
]
.
We consider a relativistic string moving in a
D
-dimensional flat spacetime with Lorentzian signature, in the presence of a constant antisymmetric Kalb–Ramond field
B_{\mu\nu}
. The Polyakov-like version can be obtained via an auxiliary Lagrangian approach. We begin with the following action

S=T\int_{\text{WS}}d^{2}\xi\left[\sqrt{-\det\gamma_{ab}}+B_{\mu\nu}\dot{X}^{\mu}X^{\prime\nu}\right],

(2.1)

where
T
is the string tension,
\xi^{a}=(\tau,\sigma)
are the worldsheet coordinates, and
\gamma_{ab}
is the induced metric on the two-dimensional worldsheet, defined by

\gamma_{ab}=\partial_{a}X^{\mu}\partial_{b}X^{\nu}\eta_{\mu\nu}.

(2.2)

Here,
X^{\mu}(\tau,\sigma)
(
\mu=0,1,\dots,D-1
) represent the embedding functions of the string in spacetime, and
\eta_{\mu\nu}
is the flat Minkowski metric. We use the convention

\displaystyle\dot{}\equiv\frac{\partial}{\partial\tau},\quad^{\prime}\equiv\frac{\partial}{\partial\sigma}.

Varying the action with respect to
X^{\mu}
yields the equation of motion

\dot{\Pi}^{\mu}+K^{\prime\mu}=0,

(2.3)

where
\Pi_{\mu}
and
K_{\mu}
are the canonical momentum and spatial conjugate current, respectively, derived from the Lagrangian density. They are given explicitly by

\displaystyle\Pi_{\mu}

\displaystyle=T\left(\frac{(\dot{X}\cdot X^{\prime})X^{\prime}_{\mu}-X^{\prime 2}\dot{X}_{\mu}}{\sqrt{(\dot{X}\cdot X^{\prime})^{2}-\dot{X}^{2}X^{\prime 2}}}+B_{\mu\nu}X^{\prime\nu}\right),

(2.4)

\displaystyle K_{\mu}

\displaystyle=T\left(\frac{(\dot{X}\cdot X^{\prime})\dot{X}_{\mu}-\dot{X}^{2}X^{\prime}_{\mu}}{\sqrt{(\dot{X}\cdot X^{\prime})^{2}-\dot{X}^{2}X^{\prime 2}}}-B_{\mu\nu}\dot{X}^{\nu}\right).

The canonical momentum satisfies two primary constraints

\Pi_{\mu}X^{\prime\mu}=0,\qquad(\Pi_{\mu}-TB_{\mu\nu}X^{\prime\nu})^{2}+T^{2}X^{\prime 2}=0.

(2.5)

These constraints reflect the diffeomorphism invariance and gauge structure of the theory on the worldsheet. Before taking the tensionless limit of the action, it is instructive to analyze the structure of the action given in (
2.1
). At first glance, in the tensionless limit
T\to 0
, the action appears to vanish due to the presence of the square root term. However, one can transition to the Hamiltonian formalism to obtain a finite form of the Polyakov action even as
T\to 0
. In what follows, we demonstrate how this method applies to the tensionless case.
The canonical Hamiltonian associated with the action (
2.1
) can be derived using the expressions for
\Pi_{\mu}
from (
2.4
), leading to

\Pi_{\mu}\dot{X}^{\mu}-\mathcal{L}=0.

(2.6)

This result can also be understood from the reparameterization invariance of the original action (
2.1
). Consequently, the total Hamiltonian is constructed as a linear combination of the constraints given in (
2.5
)

\mathcal{H}_{T}=\rho\,\Pi_{\mu}X^{\prime\mu}+\frac{\lambda}{2}\left\{(\Pi_{\mu}-TB_{\mu\nu}X^{\prime\nu})^{2}+T^{2}X^{\prime 2}\right\}.

(2.7)

Here,
\lambda
and
\rho
are Lagrange multipliers introduced to enforce the constraints. The interpolating Lagrangian is then defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{I}

\displaystyle=\Pi_{\mu}\dot{X}^{\mu}-\mathcal{H}_{T}

(2.8)

\displaystyle=\Pi_{\mu}\dot{X}^{\mu}-\rho\Pi_{\mu}X^{\prime\mu}-\frac{\lambda}{2}\left[\Pi^{2}+T^{2}X^{\prime 2}-2TB_{\mu\nu}\Pi^{\mu}X^{\prime\nu}+T^{2}B_{\mu\nu}B^{\mu}_{\rho}X^{\prime\nu}X^{\prime\rho}\right].

In this formulation,
\lambda
and
\rho
are treated as auxiliary scalar fields on the worldsheet. Since
\Pi_{\mu}
is an auxiliary field, it can be integrated out from the interpolating Lagrangian. After performing this integration, we arrive at the following effective Lagrangian

\mathcal{L}_{I}=\frac{1}{2\lambda}\left[\dot{X}^{2}-2\rho(\dot{X}\cdot X^{\prime})+(\rho^{2}-\lambda^{2}T^{2})X^{\prime 2}+2\lambda TB_{\mu\nu}\dot{X}^{\mu}X^{\prime\nu}\right].

(2.9)

We now interpret the coefficients of
\dot{X}^{2}
,
\dot{X}\cdot X^{\prime}
, and
X^{\prime 2}
as components of a two-dimensional worldsheet metric
g^{\alpha\beta}
, such that

g^{\alpha\beta}=\begin{bmatrix}1&-\rho\\
-\rho&\rho^{2}-\lambda^{2}T^{2}\end{bmatrix},\quad\sqrt{-g}=\sqrt{-\det g_{\alpha\beta}}=\frac{1}{\lambda T}.

(2.10)

This leads to the Polyakov-form action

S_{I}=\frac{T}{2}\int_{\text{WS}}d^{2}\xi\left(\sqrt{-g}g^{ab}\partial_{a}X^{\mu}\partial_{b}X^{\nu}\eta_{\mu\nu}+\epsilon^{ab}B_{\mu\nu}\partial_{a}X^{\mu}\partial_{b}X^{\nu}\right).

(2.11)

Now, taking the tensionless limit that is, letting
T\to\epsilon T
with
\epsilon\to 0
, we must rescale the worldsheet variables to maintain a non-degenerate structure. Specifically, we redefine the metric and
B
-field as

T\sqrt{-g}g^{ab}\to V^{a}V^{b}\quad\text{and}\quad B_{\mu\nu}\to\frac{B_{\mu\nu}}{\epsilon},\quad\text{where}\quad V^{a}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}}(1,-\rho).

(2.12)

This rescaling allows us to extract a well-defined limit of the action despite the vanishing string tension.

Here,
V^{a}
represents a timelike vielbein density (vector density) on the worldsheet, which encodes the local geometry of the two-dimensional surface. We now introduce a rescaled antisymmetric field
\mathfrak{B}_{\mu\nu}
defined by

\mathfrak{B}_{\mu\nu}=\frac{1}{\alpha^{\prime}}B_{\mu\nu},\quad T=\frac{1}{2\pi\alpha^{\prime}},

(2.13)

where
\alpha^{\prime}
is the string length parameter. The scaling of the
B
-field ensures that
\mathfrak{B}_{\mu\nu}
remains finite in the tensionless limit. With this redefinition, the action for a tensionless string in the presence of a
B
-field becomes

S=\int_{\text{WS}}d^{2}\xi\left(V^{a}V^{b}\partial_{a}X^{\mu}\partial_{b}X^{\nu}\eta_{\mu\nu}+\frac{1}{2\pi}\epsilon^{ab}\mathfrak{B}_{\mu\nu}\partial_{a}X^{\mu}\partial_{b}X^{\nu}\right).

(2.14)

This expression will serve as the primary object of study in the remainder of this work. It is important to note that this action generalizes the so-called ILST action
[
7
]
, originally proposed for tensionless strings in flat spacetime, by incorporating a consistent coupling to a constant antisymmetric tensor field.

Generalized induced vacuum condition in the presence of a uniform Kalb–Ramond field

We consider the tensionless string action in a constant antisymmetric background

S\;=\;\int_{\text{WS}}d^{2}\xi\;\Big(V^{a}V^{b}\,\partial_{a}X^{\mu}\partial_{b}X^{\nu}\,\eta_{\mu\nu}\;+\;\frac{1}{2\pi}\,\epsilon^{ab}\,\mathfrak{B}_{\mu\nu}\,\partial_{a}X^{\mu}\partial_{b}X^{\nu}\Big),

(3.1)

where
\xi^{a}=(\tau,\sigma)
are worldsheet coordinates,
V^{a}
is the vector density of the tensionless formalism,
\eta_{\mu\nu}
is a flat target-space metric, and
\mathfrak{B}_{\mu\nu}=-\mathfrak{B}_{\nu\mu}
is constant.
We split the Lagrangian density into

\mathcal{L}_{1}\equiv V^{a}V^{b}\partial_{a}X^{\mu}\partial_{b}X_{\mu},\qquad\mathcal{L}_{B}\equiv\frac{1}{2\pi}\epsilon^{ab}\mathfrak{B}_{\mu\nu}\partial_{a}X^{\mu}\partial_{b}X^{\nu}.

(3.2)

Kinetic term.

Varying
\mathcal{L}_{1}
gives

\delta\mathcal{L}_{1}=2V^{a}V^{b}\partial_{a}X_{\mu}\,\partial_{b}(\delta X^{\mu}).

(3.3)

Integrating by parts,

\delta S_{1}=-\int_{\text{WS}}d^{2}\xi\,2\partial_{b}\!\big(V^{a}V^{b}\partial_{a}X_{\mu}\big)\,\delta X^{\mu}+2\!\int_{\partial\Sigma}d\tau\,n_{b}V^{a}V^{b}\partial_{a}X_{\mu}\,\delta X^{\mu},

(3.4)

where
n_{b}
is the outward-pointing normal one-form along
\partial\Sigma
.

\mathfrak{B}
-term.

Varying
\mathcal{L}_{B}
and using antisymmetry of
\mathfrak{B}
,

\delta\mathcal{L}_{B}=\frac{1}{\pi}\,\epsilon^{ab}\mathfrak{B}_{\mu\nu}\,\partial_{a}\delta X^{\mu}\,\partial_{b}X^{\nu}.

(3.5)

Integrating by parts,

\delta S_{B}=-\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\text{WS}}d^{2}\xi\,\epsilon^{ab}\mathfrak{B}_{\mu\nu}\partial_{a}\partial_{b}X^{\nu}\,\delta X^{\mu}+\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\partial\Sigma}d\tau\,n_{a}\epsilon^{ab}\mathfrak{B}_{\mu\nu}\partial_{b}X^{\nu}\,\delta X^{\mu}.

(3.6)

The bulk term vanishes since
\epsilon^{ab}\partial_{a}\partial_{b}X^{\nu}=0
.

Total variation.

The total boundary contribution is

\delta S\big|_{\partial\Sigma}=\int_{\partial\Sigma}d\tau\,\Big[\,2n_{b}V^{a}V^{b}\partial_{a}X_{\mu}+\tfrac{1}{\pi}n_{a}\epsilon^{ab}\mathfrak{B}_{\mu\nu}\partial_{b}X^{\nu}\Big]\delta X^{\mu}.

(3.7)

1.
Kinetic term.
With
V^{a}=(1,0)
and
n_{a}=(1,0)
(fixed-
\tau
boundary), we compute

n_{b}V^{b}=1.

(3.8)

Thus,

2n_{b}V^{a}V^{b}\partial_{a}X_{\mu}=2\,\partial_{\tau}X_{\mu}.

(3.9)

2.
\mathfrak{B}
-term.
Using coordinates
(\tau,\sigma)
with
\epsilon^{\tau\sigma}=+1
, we have

n_{a}\epsilon^{ab}\partial_{b}=\partial_{\sigma}.

(3.10)

Hence, the second term becomes

\tfrac{1}{\pi}\,\mathfrak{B}_{\mu\nu}\,\partial_{\sigma}X^{\nu}.

(3.11)

Putting both contributions together, the boundary condition arising from
\delta S\big|_{\partial\Sigma}=0
is

\partial_{\tau}X_{\mu}+\tfrac{1}{2\pi}\,\mathfrak{B}_{\mu\nu}\,\partial_{\sigma}X^{\nu}\bigg|_{\partial\Sigma}=0\,.

(3.12)

In the gauge choice
V^{a}=(1,0)
, the equation of motion (e.o.m.) for the scalar fields, along with the constraints associated with this gauge, yield the following conditions

\partial_{\tau}^{2}X^{\mu}=0;\quad\partial_{\tau}X\cdot\partial_{\sigma}X=0;\quad\partial_{\tau}X\cdot\partial_{\tau}X=0.

(3.13)

In shorthand notation, we have

\ddot{X}^{\mu}=0,\quad\dot{X}\cdot X^{\prime}=0,\quad\dot{X}^{2}=0.

(3.14)

where we used the convention

\displaystyle\dot{}\equiv\frac{\partial}{\partial\tau},\quad^{\prime}\equiv\frac{\partial}{\partial\sigma}.

These equations describe the dynamics of the string in the tensionless limit.
We now impose closed string boundary conditions on the worldsheet, requiring that the spacetime coordinates satisfy
X^{\mu}(\tau,\sigma+2\pi)=X^{\mu}(\tau,\sigma)
, analogous to the case of tensile strings. Under these conditions, the e.o.m. can be solved using a mode expansion of the form

X^{\mu}(\sigma,\tau)=x^{\mu}+\sqrt{2c^{\prime}}B_{0}^{\mu}\tau+\sqrt{2c^{\prime}}\sum_{n\neq 0}\frac{i}{n}\left(A_{n}^{\mu}-in\tau B_{n}^{\mu}\right)e^{-in\sigma}.

(3.15)

Here,
c^{\prime}
is a constant with dimensions of
[L]^{2}
, serving as the tensionless analog of the string coupling constant
\alpha^{\prime}
that appears in the standard tensile string mode expansion.
We define the harmonic oscillators

C_{n}^{\mu}=\frac{1}{2}(A_{n}^{\mu}+B_{n}^{\mu}),\quad\tilde{C}_{n}^{\mu}=\frac{1}{2}(-A_{-n}^{\mu}+B_{-n}^{\mu}).

(3.16)

These satisfy

[C_{m}^{\mu},C_{n}^{\nu}]=m\delta_{m+n,0}\eta^{\mu\nu},\quad[\tilde{C}_{m}^{\mu},\tilde{C}_{n}^{\nu}]=m\delta_{m+n,0}\eta^{\mu\nu}.

(3.17)

We lower indices with the spacetime metric
\eta_{\mu\nu}
(so
\partial_{\tau}X_{\mu}=\eta_{\mu\nu}\partial_{\tau}X^{\nu}
).
We first compute the derivatives of the mode expansion.
The
\tau
-derivative is

\partial_{\tau}X^{\mu}=\sqrt{2c^{\prime}}\,B_{0}^{\mu}+\sqrt{2c^{\prime}}\sum_{n\neq 0}B_{n}^{\mu}e^{-in\sigma},

(3.18)

and the
\sigma
-derivative is

\partial_{\sigma}X^{\mu}=\sqrt{2c^{\prime}}\sum_{n\neq 0}\left(A_{n}^{\mu}-in\tau B_{n}^{\mu}\right)e^{-in\sigma}.

(3.19)

Now, inserting these into the boundary condition

\partial_{\tau}X_{\mu}+\frac{1}{2\pi}\mathfrak{B}_{\mu\nu}\partial_{\sigma}X^{\nu}\bigg|_{\partial\Sigma}=0,

(3.20)

and dividing by the common factor
\sqrt{2c^{\prime}}
yields

\eta_{\mu\nu}B_{0}^{\nu}+\sum_{n\neq 0}\eta_{\mu\nu}B_{n}^{\nu}e^{-in\sigma}+\frac{1}{2\pi}\mathfrak{B}_{\mu\nu}\sum_{n\neq 0}\left(A_{n}^{\nu}-in\tau B_{n}^{\nu}\right)e^{-in\sigma}=0.

(3.21)

Now, we equate the constant (zero-frequency) piece and each Fourier coefficient of
e^{-in\sigma}
.
For the zero mode (
n=0
term)

\eta_{\mu\nu}B_{0}^{\nu}=0\,.

(3.22)

For each
n\neq 0
, the coefficient of
e^{-in\sigma}
must vanish

\frac{1}{2\pi}\mathfrak{B}_{\mu\nu}A_{n}^{\nu}=-\left(\eta_{\mu\nu}-\frac{in\tau}{2\pi}\mathfrak{B}_{\mu\nu}\right)B_{n}^{\nu}.

(3.23)

Now, we derive the generalized induced vacuum condition using the boundary condition

\frac{1}{2\pi}\mathfrak{B}_{\mu\nu}A_{n}^{\nu}=-\left(\eta_{\mu\nu}-\frac{in\tau}{2\pi}\mathfrak{B}_{\mu\nu}\right)B_{n}^{\nu},

(3.24)

and the definitions of the new fields

C_{n}^{\mu}=\frac{1}{2}(A_{n}^{\mu}+B_{n}^{\mu}),\quad\tilde{C}_{-n}^{\mu}=\frac{1}{2}(-A_{n}^{\mu}+B_{n}^{\mu}).

(3.25)

Our goal is to rewrite the boundary condition (
3.24
) entirely in terms of
C_{n}^{\mu}
and
\tilde{C}_{-n}^{\mu}
.
From the definitions (
3.25
), we solve for
A_{n}^{\mu}
and
B_{n}^{\mu}
. Adding the two equations

B_{n}^{\mu}=C_{n}^{\mu}+\tilde{C}_{-n}^{\mu}.

(3.26)

Subtracting the two equations

A_{n}^{\mu}=C_{n}^{\mu}-\tilde{C}_{-n}^{\mu}.

(3.27)

Now, we substitute equations (
3.27
) and (
3.26
) into the boundary condition (
3.24
) and simplifying, the equation becomes

\left(\eta_{\mu\nu}+\frac{1-in\tau}{2\pi}\mathfrak{B}_{\mu\nu}\right)C_{n}^{\nu}+\left(\eta_{\mu\nu}-\frac{1+in\tau}{2\pi}\mathfrak{B}_{\mu\nu}\right)\tilde{C}_{-n}^{\nu}=0.

(3.28)

We have rewritten the boundary condition entirely in terms of the variables
C_{n}^{\mu}
and
\tilde{C}_{-n}^{\mu}
. In the quantum theory, the induced vacuum
\ket{I_{\mathfrak{B}}}
must satisfy this operator equation as a constraint. We have

\Bigg[\left(\eta_{\mu\nu}+\frac{1-in\tau}{2\pi}\mathfrak{B}_{\mu\nu}\right)C_{n}^{\nu}+\left(\eta_{\mu\nu}-\frac{1+in\tau}{2\pi}\mathfrak{B}_{\mu\nu}\right)\tilde{C}_{-n}^{\nu}\Bigg]\ket{I_{\mathfrak{B}}}=0.

(3.29)

Remarks.

Setting
\mathfrak{B}_{\mu\nu}=0
, the equation simplifies to

\eta_{\mu\nu}(C_{n}^{\nu}+\tilde{C}_{-n}^{\nu})=0.

(3.30)

This implies (for each
\mu
)

C_{n}^{\mu}+\tilde{C}_{-n}^{\mu}=0.

(3.31)

This is the simplified boundary condition when the
\mathfrak{B}
-field is absent.

The induced vacuum conditions are

(C_{n}^{\mu}+\tilde{C}_{-n}^{\mu})|I_{0}\rangle=0,\quad\forall n.

(3.32)

This matches with the result in
[
14
]
.

We take the tensionless limit (worldsheet Carroll limit) by scaling
\tau\to\epsilon\tau
and sending
\epsilon\to 0
. We get,

\frac{1\mp in\tau}{2\pi}\longrightarrow\frac{1}{2\pi}=a\quad(\text{say}).

(3.33)

Thus, the coefficients of
C_{n}^{\nu}
and
\tilde{C}_{-n}^{\nu}
become identical and mode-independent. The boundary condition simplifies to,

\left[(\eta_{\mu\nu}+a\mathfrak{B}_{\mu\nu})C_{n}^{\nu}+(\eta_{\mu\nu}-a\mathfrak{B}_{\mu\nu})\tilde{C}_{-n}^{\nu}\right]\ket{I_{\mathfrak{B}}}=0.

(3.34)

3.1
Gluing matrix in the presence of a uniform Kalb–Ramond field

Our goal is to rewrite the equation

\left[(\eta_{\mu\nu}+a\mathfrak{B}_{\mu\nu})C_{n}^{\nu}+(\eta_{\mu\nu}-a\mathfrak{B}_{\mu\nu})\tilde{C}_{-n}^{\nu}\right]\ket{I_{\mathfrak{B}}}=0.

(3.35)

in the form

\big(C_{n}+R\,\tilde{C}_{-n}\big)\ket{I_{\mathfrak{B}}}=0\qquad(n>0),

(3.36)

and determine the gluing matrix
R
.
We define the matrices

\displaystyle M_{+}

\displaystyle=\eta+a\mathfrak{B},\quad\text{with components }(M_{+})_{\mu\nu}=\eta_{\mu\nu}+a\mathfrak{B}_{\mu\nu},

(3.37)

\displaystyle M_{-}

\displaystyle=\eta-a\mathfrak{B},\quad\text{with components }(M_{-})_{\mu\nu}=\eta_{\mu\nu}-a\mathfrak{B}_{\mu\nu}.

(3.38)

Then, Eq. (
3.35
) becomes

(M_{+})_{\mu\nu}C_{n}^{\nu}\ket{I_{\mathfrak{B}}}+(M_{-})_{\mu\nu}\tilde{C}_{-n}^{\nu}\ket{I_{\mathfrak{B}}}=0.

(3.39)

This is a vector equation in Lorentz index space (one equation for each
\mu
). We can write

(M_{+})_{\mu\nu}C_{n}^{\nu}\ket{I_{\mathfrak{B}}}=-(M_{-})_{\mu\nu}\tilde{C}_{-n}^{\nu}\ket{I_{\mathfrak{B}}}.

(3.40)

Assuming
M_{+}=\eta+a\mathfrak{B}
is invertible, we multiply both sides by the inverse matrix
(M_{+}^{-1})^{\mu\rho}

\delta^{\rho}_{\nu}C_{n}^{\nu}\ket{I_{\mathfrak{B}}}=-(M_{+}^{-1})^{\rho\mu}(M_{-})_{\mu\nu}\tilde{C}_{-n}^{\nu}\ket{I_{\mathfrak{B}}}.

(3.41)

Thus,

C_{n}^{\rho}\ket{I_{\mathfrak{B}}}=-\left[M_{+}^{-1}M_{-}\right]^{\rho}{}_{\nu}\tilde{C}_{-n}^{\nu}\ket{I_{\mathfrak{B}}}.

(3.42)

In vector form (suppressing Lorentz indices), this reads

C_{n}\ket{I_{\mathfrak{B}}}=-R\,\tilde{C}_{-n}\ket{I_{\mathfrak{B}}},

(3.43)

where we define

R=M_{+}^{-1}M_{-}=(\eta+a\mathfrak{B})^{-1}(\eta-a\mathfrak{B}).

(3.44)

We therefore obtain the desired form

\big(C_{n}+R\,\tilde{C}_{-n}\big)\ket{I_{\mathfrak{B}}}=0\qquad(n>0),

(3.45)

with the gluing matrix
R
given by

R=(\eta+a\mathfrak{B})^{-1}(\eta-a\mathfrak{B}).

(3.46)

The matrix
R
acts on the Lorentz indices of the oscillator modes. Its components are

R^{\mu}{}_{\nu}=\left[(\eta+a\mathfrak{B})^{-1}(\eta-a\mathfrak{B})\right]^{\mu}{}_{\nu}.

(3.47)

So, we see that in the tensionless limit, the gluing matrix
R
becomes independent of the oscillator level
n
and the worldsheet time
\tau
. This is a crucial simplification: the identification between
C_{n}
and
\tilde{C}_{-n}
is now universal across all modes.
It implies that the emergent open string sees a constant, mode-independent background geometry.

3.2
Squeezed-state boundary state and gluing condition

We derive the oscillator part of the boundary state
\ket{I_{\mathfrak{B}}}
and show that it satisfies the gluing condition

\big(C_{n}+R\,\tilde{C}_{-n}\big)\ket{I_{\mathfrak{B}}}=0\qquad(n>0),

(3.48)

using only the oscillator algebra and standard vacuum conventions.

Set up and conventions.

The oscillators satisfy the commutation relations

\displaystyle[C_{m}^{\mu},\,C_{n}^{\nu}]

\displaystyle=m\,\delta_{m+n,0}\,\eta^{\mu\nu},

(3.49)

\displaystyle[\tilde{C}_{m}^{\mu},\,\tilde{C}_{n}^{\nu}]

\displaystyle=m\,\delta_{m+n,0}\,\eta^{\mu\nu},

(3.50)

with all the cross-commutators vanishing

[C_{m}^{\mu},\,\tilde{C}_{n}^{\nu}]=0\quad\forall\,m,n.

(3.51)

The squeezed-state boundary ket (ignoring normalization and zero modes) is

\ket{I_{\mathfrak{B}}}=\mathcal{N}\exp\!\Big(-\sum_{k>0}\frac{1}{k}C_{-k}^{\mu}R_{\mu}{}^{\nu}\tilde{C}_{-k\,\nu}\Big)\ket{0}_{c}.

(3.52)

We define the exponent operator

X\equiv-\sum_{k>0}\frac{1}{k}C_{-k}^{\mu}R_{\mu}{}^{\nu}\tilde{C}_{-k\,\nu},

(3.53)

so that
\ket{I_{\mathfrak{B}}}=\mathcal{N}e^{X}\ket{0}_{c}
.
Our goal is to prove

(C_{n}^{\mu}+R_{\mu}{}^{\nu}\tilde{C}_{-n\,\nu})\ket{I_{\mathfrak{B}}}=0\quad\text{for all }n>0.

(3.54)

Key commutator computation.

We compute
[C_{n}^{\alpha},X]
. Only the term with
k=n
contributes, since
[C_{n}^{\alpha},C_{-k}^{\mu}]=n\delta_{k,n}\eta^{\alpha\mu}

\begin{split}[C_{n}^{\alpha},X]&=-\sum_{k>0}\frac{1}{k}[C_{n}^{\alpha},\,C_{-k}^{\mu}]R_{\mu}{}^{\nu}\tilde{C}_{-k\,\nu}\\
&=-\frac{1}{n}[C_{n}^{\alpha},\,C_{-n}^{\mu}]R_{\mu}{}^{\nu}\tilde{C}_{-n\,\nu}\\
&=-\frac{1}{n}(n\eta^{\alpha\mu})R_{\mu}{}^{\nu}\tilde{C}_{-n\,\nu}\\
&=-\eta^{\alpha\mu}R_{\mu}{}^{\nu}\tilde{C}_{-n\,\nu}.\end{split}

(3.55)

In vector notation, this becomes

[C_{n},\,X]=-R\,\tilde{C}_{-n},

(3.56)

where the left-hand side denotes the vector of component commutators, and the right-hand side is the vector with components
-\eta^{\alpha\mu}R_{\mu}{}^{\nu}\tilde{C}_{-n\,\nu}
.

Moving
C_{n}
through the exponential.

We use the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff (BCH) identity

C_{n}e^{X}=e^{X}\left(C_{n}+[C_{n},X]+\frac{1}{2!}[[C_{n},X],X]+\cdots\right).

(3.57)

Now, we observe

•

[C_{n},X]\propto\tilde{C}_{-n}
, a creation operator (since
n>0
),

•

X
is a sum of bilinears in creation operators
C_{-k},\tilde{C}_{-k}
for
k>0
,

•

all such creation operators commute among themselves at different levels:
[\tilde{C}_{-n},\tilde{C}_{-k}]=0
for
n\neq k
, and even for
n=k
,
[\tilde{C}_{-n},\tilde{C}_{-n}]=0
(no self-commutator),

•

thus
[[C_{n},X],X]=0
, and all higher nested commutators vanish.

Therefore, the BCH series terminates after the first term:

C_{n}e^{X}=e^{X}\left(C_{n}+[C_{n},X]\right).

(3.58)

Substituting
[C_{n},X]=-R\tilde{C}_{-n}
, we get

C_{n}e^{X}=e^{X}\left(C_{n}-R\,\tilde{C}_{-n}\right).

(3.59)

Action on the vacuum.

Acting on
\ket{0}_{c}
, and using
C_{n}\ket{0}_{c}=0
for
n>0

\begin{split}C_{n}e^{X}\ket{0}_{c}&=e^{X}\left(C_{n}-R\,\tilde{C}_{-n}\right)\ket{0}_{c}\\
&=e^{X}\left(0-R\,\tilde{C}_{-n}\ket{0}_{c}\right)\\
&=-e^{X}R\,\tilde{C}_{-n}\ket{0}_{c}.\end{split}

(3.60)

Therefore,

C_{n}\ket{I_{\mathfrak{B}}}=\mathcal{N}C_{n}e^{X}\ket{0}_{c}=-\mathcal{N}e^{X}R\,\tilde{C}_{-n}\ket{0}_{c}.

(3.61)

Commutation of
R\,\tilde{C}_{-n}
with
e^{X}
.

Now, we consider
R\,\tilde{C}_{-n}\ket{I_{\mathfrak{B}}}=\mathcal{N}R\,\tilde{C}_{-n}e^{X}\ket{0}_{c}
.
We claim that
R\,\tilde{C}_{-n}
commutes with
X
, because

•

\tilde{C}_{-n}
is a creation operator (negative mode),

•

X
is a sum of terms
C_{-k}^{\mu}\tilde{C}_{-k\,\nu}
,

•

[\tilde{C}_{-n},C_{-k}]=0
since left- and right-movers commute,

•

[\tilde{C}_{-n},\tilde{C}_{-k}]=0
for all
k>0
, because
-n-k<0\neq 0
, so no commutator survives,

•

hence
[\tilde{C}_{-n},X]=0
, and so
[R\,\tilde{C}_{-n},X]=0
.

Thus,
R\,\tilde{C}_{-n}
commutes with
e^{X}

R\,\tilde{C}_{-n}e^{X}=e^{X}R\,\tilde{C}_{-n}.

(3.62)

Therefore

R\,\tilde{C}_{-n}\ket{I_{\mathfrak{B}}}=\mathcal{N}R\,\tilde{C}_{-n}e^{X}\ket{0}_{c}=\mathcal{N}e^{X}R\,\tilde{C}_{-n}\ket{0}_{c}.

(3.63)

Final result: gluing condition.

Now, we compute the full gluing condition

\begin{split}(C_{n}+R\,\tilde{C}_{-n})\ket{I_{\mathfrak{B}}}&=C_{n}\ket{I_{\mathfrak{B}}}+R\,\tilde{C}_{-n}\ket{I_{\mathfrak{B}}}\\
&=-\mathcal{N}e^{X}R\,\tilde{C}_{-n}\ket{0}_{c}+\mathcal{N}e^{X}R\,\tilde{C}_{-n}\ket{0}_{c}\\
&=0.\end{split}

(3.64)

Hence, we conclude

\big(C_{n}+R\,\tilde{C}_{-n}\big)\ket{I_{\mathfrak{B}}}=0\quad\text{for all }n>0.

(3.65)

as desired.

Consistency checks.

When the
\mathfrak{B}
-field is absent, the conditions for the induced vacuum are expressed as

(C_{n}^{\mu}+\tilde{C}_{-n}^{\mu})|I_{0}\rangle=0,\quad\forall n.

(3.66)

This corresponds to the condition of a Neumann boundary state, and its solution is given by

|I_{0}\rangle=\mathcal{N}\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{n}C_{-n}\cdot\tilde{C}_{-n}\right)|0\rangle_{c},

(3.67)

where
\mathcal{N}
is a normalization constant.

By definition,

R^{\mu}{}_{\nu}=\big[(\eta+a\mathfrak{B})^{-1}(\eta-a\mathfrak{B})\big]^{\mu}{}_{\nu}.

(3.68)

Setting
\mathfrak{B}=0
, we obtain

R^{\mu}{}_{\nu}\Big|_{\mathfrak{B}=0}=(\eta^{-1}\eta)^{\mu}{}_{\nu}=\delta^{\mu}{}_{\nu},

(3.69)

so
R\to 1
. Therefore, the
\mathfrak{B}
-field boundary condition

(C_{n}^{\mu}+R^{\mu}{}_{\nu}\tilde{C}_{-n}^{\nu})\ket{I_{\mathfrak{B}}}=0~~,

(3.70)

reduces to

(C_{n}^{\mu}+\tilde{C}_{-n}^{\mu})\ket{I_0}=0,

(3.71)

which is the Neumann boundary condition.
We define the squeezed operator

A=-\sum_{k>0}\frac{1}{k}C_{-k}^{\rho}R_{\rho}{}^{\sigma}\tilde{C}_{-k\,\sigma},\qquad\ket{I_{\mathfrak{B}}}=\mathcal{N}e^{A}\ket{0}_{c}.

(3.72)

We use the BCH identity

e^{A}C_{n}^{\mu}e^{-A}=C_{n}^{\mu}+[A,C_{n}^{\mu}]+\frac{1}{2}[A,[A,C_{n}^{\mu}]]+\cdots~~.

(3.73)

Since
A
is bilinear in negative-frequency oscillators (
-k<0
) and
C_{n}^{\mu}
has mode
n>0
, only the first commutator survives (higher-order commutators vanish due to mode mismatch).
Using the oscillator algebra

[C_{m}^{\alpha},C_{p}^{\beta}]=m\,\eta^{\alpha\beta}\delta_{m,-p},

(3.74)

we find for
k>0

[C_{-k}^{\rho},C_{n}^{\mu}]=-k\,\eta^{\rho\mu}\delta_{k,n}.

(3.75)

Now, we compute the commutator

\begin{split}[A,C_{n}^{\mu}]&=-\sum_{k>0}\frac{1}{k}\left[C_{-k}^{\rho}R_{\rho}{}^{\sigma}\tilde{C}_{-k\,\sigma},\;C_{n}^{\mu}\right]\\
&=-\sum_{k>0}\frac{1}{k}R_{\rho}{}^{\sigma}\tilde{C}_{-k\,\sigma}\left[C_{-k}^{\rho},C_{n}^{\mu}\right]\\
&=-\sum_{k>0}\frac{1}{k}R_{\rho}{}^{\sigma}\tilde{C}_{-k\,\sigma}(-k\,\eta^{\rho\mu}\delta_{k,n})\\
&=R_{\rho}{}^{\sigma}\eta^{\rho\mu}\tilde{C}_{-n\,\sigma}.\end{split}

(3.76)

Raising the index using
\eta^{\rho\mu}
, this becomes

[A,C_{n}^{\mu}]=R^{\mu}{}_{\sigma}\tilde{C}_{-n}^{\sigma}.

(3.77)

Thus,

e^{A}C_{n}^{\mu}e^{-A}=C_{n}^{\mu}+R^{\mu}{}_{\sigma}\tilde{C}_{-n}^{\sigma}.

(3.78)

Acting on the squeezed state

\begin{split}\big(C_{n}^{\mu}+R^{\mu}{}_{\sigma}\tilde{C}_{-n}^{\sigma}\big)\ket{I_{\mathfrak{B}}}&=e^{A}C_{n}^{\mu}e^{-A}\cdot e^{A}\ket{0}_{c}\\
&=e^{A}C_{n}^{\mu}\ket{0}_{c}.\end{split}

(3.79)

Therefore,

\big(C_{n}^{\mu}+R^{\mu}{}_{\sigma}\tilde{C}_{-n}^{\sigma}\big)\ket{I_{\mathfrak{B}}}=0,\qquad(n>0),

(3.80)

as required.
As
\mathfrak{B}\to 0
,
R\to 1
, so the squeezed state becomes

\ket{I_{\mathfrak{B}}}\xrightarrow{\mathfrak{B}\to 0}\mathcal{N}\exp\!\left(-\sum_{k>0}\frac{1}{k}C_{-k}\cdot\tilde{C}_{-k}\right)\ket{0}_{c},

(3.81)

which is precisely the Neumann boundary state. The annihilation proof reduces accordingly: with
R=1
, we have
[A,C_{n}^{\mu}]=\tilde{C}_{-n}^{\mu}
, and hence

(C_{n}^{\mu}+\tilde{C}_{-n}^{\mu})|I_{0}\rangle=0.

(3.82)

We have shown that

•

R\to 1
as
\mathfrak{B}\to 0
,

•

the squeezed state
\ket{I_{\mathfrak{B}}}
reduces to the Neumann boundary state.

Hence, the Neumann boundary states are fully consistent in the
\mathfrak{B}\to 0
limit.

3.3
Bogoliubov generator

Now, let us review the analysis of the transition from closed to open string in
[
14
]
. The transition from closed to open string can be understood through a Bogoliubov transformation on the worldsheet. Specifically, the relation between the original closed string oscillators
C_{n}^{\mu}
and the new open string-like modes
\alpha_{n}^{\mu}
is given by

\alpha_{n}^{\mu}=e^{iG}C_{n}e^{-iG}=\cosh\theta\,C_{n}^{\mu}-\sinh\theta\,\tilde{C}_{-n}^{\mu},

(3.83)

\tilde{\alpha}_{n}^{\mu}=e^{iG}\tilde{C}_{n}e^{-iG}=-\sinh\theta\,C_{-n}^{\mu}+\cosh\theta\,\tilde{C}_{n}^{\mu},

(3.84)

where the generator
G
is defined as

G=i\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\theta\left[C_{-n}\cdot\tilde{C}_{-n}-C_{n}\cdot\tilde{C}_{n}\right],\quad\tanh\theta=\frac{\epsilon-1}{\epsilon+1}.

(3.85)

This transformation effectively “mixes” modes of the closed string, producing a new vacuum state that encodes boundary conditions.

The resulting vacuum state
|0\rangle_{\alpha}
, expressed in terms of the original closed string oscillators, takes the form of a squeezed state

|0\rangle_{\alpha}=\sqrt{\cosh\theta}\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\exp\left[\frac{\tanh\theta}{n}\,C_{-n}\cdot\tilde{C}_{-n}\right]|0\rangle_{c}.

(3.86)

This state represents the ground state of the transformed oscillator algebra - the open string vacuum - constructed entirely within the Hilbert space of the closed string theory.

To understand how an open string emerges from a closed one, consider the parameter
\epsilon
, which governs the tension scale. When
\epsilon=1
, we have
\tanh\theta=0
, and the vacuum reduces to the original closed string vacuum:
|0\rangle_{\alpha}=|0\rangle_{c}
. As
\epsilon
deviates from unity, the vacuum evolves under the action of the Bogoliubov transformation - it becomes increasingly “squeezed,” as shown in Eq. (
3.86
). In the limit
\epsilon\to 0
, corresponding to the tensionless limit, we find
\tanh\theta\to-1
. The vacuum then becomes,

|0\rangle_{\alpha}=\mathcal{N}\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\exp\left[-\frac{1}{n}C_{-n}\cdot\tilde{C}_{-n}\right]|0\rangle_{c},

(3.87)

which is precisely the
induced vacuum

|I_{0}\rangle
introduced. This state satisfies Neumann boundary conditions in all spatial directions - the defining property of a space-filling D25-brane in bosonic string theory. Thus, an
open string
, free to move in all dimensions, arises naturally from a
closed string
by taking the
tensionless limit
.

Following
[
14
]
, Bogoliubov generator is expressed as

G=i\sum_{n>0}\theta\left(C^{\mu}_{-n}R_{\mu}{}^{\nu}\tilde{C}_{-n\,\nu}-\text{h.c.}\right).

(3.88)

We have the transformed oscillators

e^{iG}C_{n}^{\mu}e^{-iG}=\cosh\theta\,C_{n}^{\mu}-\sinh\theta\,R^{\mu}{}_{\nu}\tilde{C}_{-n}^{\nu}.

(3.89)

Induced vacuum is

\ket{0}_{\alpha}=e^{iG}\ket{0}_{c}=\mathcal{N}\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\exp\left(\frac{\tanh\theta}{n}C^{\mu}_{-n}R_{\mu}{}^{\nu}\tilde{C}_{-n\,\nu}\right)\ket{0}_{c}.

(3.90)

We can recover the squeezed boundary state in the limit
\tanh\theta\to-1

\ket{I_{\mathfrak{B}}}=\mathcal{N}\exp\left(-\sum_{n>0}\frac{1}{n}C^{\mu}_{-n}R_{\mu}{}^{\nu}\tilde{C}_{-n\,\nu}\right)\ket{0}_{c}.

(3.91)

3.4
Physical interpretation of the gluing condition

\mathfrak{B}=0
case: Neumann gluing.

When the background Kalb–Ramond field is absent, the oscillator gluing condition simplifies to

\left(C_{n}^{\mu}+\tilde{C}_{-n}^{\mu}\right)\ket{I_0}=0.

(3.92)

This corresponds to a Neumann boundary state, which describes an open string whose endpoints are free to propagate in all spacetime dimensions. For a detailed exposition of boundary states see
[
23
, cf. section
6.2.1 Boundary Conditions
]
. Thus an open string is obtained by taking a tensionless limit of a closed string. An open string with Neumann boundary conditions along all spacetime directions is equivalent to a space-filling D25-brane in bosonic string theory. In this picture, what begins as a closed string theory can effectively give rise to-or be reinterpreted as-such a space-filling brane.

Turning on a constant
\mathfrak{B}
.

When a constant background
\mathfrak{B}
-field is switched on, the gluing condition no longer equates
C_{n}
and
\tilde{C}_{-n}
directly. Instead, it acquires the form

\left(C_{n}+R\tilde{C}_{-n}\right)\ket{I_{\mathfrak{B}}}=0,

(3.93)

where
R
is a constant matrix determined by the background. This eliminates one set of degrees of freedom, effectively inducing a boundary on the worldsheet and reorganizing the closed-string vacuum into the vacuum of an open string in the presence of the background
B
-field. This provides a physical mechanism by which a closed string dynamically transitions into an open string in the tensionless regime.

4
Tensionless strings with a background Kalb–Ramond field compactified on a torus
T^{d}

We now consider a closed tensionless string compactified on
T^{d}
, propagating in background constant
B
-field
[
21
]
. The anti-symmetric
B
-field has non-vanishing entries only along those compact directions.

Suppose the compactification radius of the
I
-th dimension is
R_{I}
.
Compactifying
d
directions means we identify the coordinates as

X^{I}\;\sim\;X^{I}+2\pi\omega^{I}R_{I},\qquad I=1,\dots,d,

(4.1)

so that the closed string coordinates satisfy

X^{I}(\sigma+2\pi,\tau)\;=\;X^{I}(\sigma,\tau)+2\pi\omega^{I}R_{I},

(4.2)

with
\omega^{I}
denoting integer winding numbers.

It is convenient to introduce dimensionless variables by rescaling

\tilde{X}^{I}\equiv\frac{X^{I}}{R_{I}}.

(4.3)

Under this redefinition the metric and
B
-field components in the compact directions transform as

\displaystyle G_{IJ}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial X^{K}}{\partial\tilde{X}^{I}}\,\frac{\partial X^{L}}{\partial\tilde{X}^{J}}\,\eta_{KL}=R_{I}R_{J}\eta_{IJ},

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}_{IJ}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial X^{K}}{\partial\tilde{X}^{I}}\,\frac{\partial X^{L}}{\partial\tilde{X}^{J}}\,\mathfrak{B}_{KL}=R_{I}R_{J}\mathfrak{B}_{IJ},

(4.4)

and the inverse metric reads

G^{IJ}=\frac{1}{R_{I}R_{J}}\,\eta^{IJ}.

(4.5)

In the scaled coordinates the identification rule (
4.1
) simplifies to

\tilde{X}^{I}\;\sim\;\tilde{X}^{I}+2\pi\omega^{I},

(4.6)

which implies the periodicity condition

\tilde{X}^{I}(\sigma+2\pi,\tau)-\tilde{X}^{I}(\sigma,\tau)=2\pi\omega^{I}.

(4.7)

Recall, our gauge choice is,
V^{\alpha}=(v,0)
. Expressed in terms of
\tilde{X}^{I}
, the gauge-fixed action becomes

S=\int d^{2}\xi\Big(v^{2}\,\dot{\tilde{X}}^{I}\dot{\tilde{X}}^{J}G_{IJ}+\frac{1}{2\pi}\epsilon^{ab}\mathbb{B}_{IJ}\,\partial_{a}\tilde{X}^{I}\partial_{b}\tilde{X}^{J}\Big).

(4.8)

Note that this action looks similar to the action (
2.14
), but now with the rescaled
\eta
,
\mathfrak{B}
and
X^{I}
s. The equation of motion for
\tilde{X}^{I}
reads,

\ddot{\tilde{X}}^{I}=0.

(4.9)

This equation can be solved with the modified periodic boundary condition using the following mode expansion,

\tilde{X}^{I}(\tau,\sigma)=\tilde{x}^{I}+\sqrt{\frac{c^{\prime}}{2}}A_{0}^{I}\sigma+\sqrt{\frac{c^{\prime}}{2}}B_{0}^{I}\tau+i\sqrt{\frac{c^{\prime}}{2}}\sum_{n\neq 0}\frac{1}{n}\left(A_{n}^{I}-in\tau B_{n}^{I}\right)e^{-in\sigma}.

(4.10)

The momentum conjugate to
\tilde{X}^{I}
is

\tilde{\Pi}^{I}=\frac{1}{c^{\prime}}\sum_{J=1}^{d}G_{IJ}\,\dot{\tilde{X}}^{J}+\sum_{J=1}^{d}\mathbb{B}_{IJ}\,\tilde{X}^{\prime J}.

(4.11)

Averaging over
\sigma
then gives the centre-of-mass momentum, which combines both Kaluza-Klein and winding contributions:

\tilde{\pi}^{I}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\sigma\,\tilde{\Pi}^{I}=\sum_{J=1}^{d}K^{J}G_{IJ}+\sum_{J=1}^{d}\mathbb{B}_{IJ}\,\omega^{J}.

(4.12)

Because the canonical momentum generates translations, the factor

\exp\!\left(i\sum_{I}\tilde{\pi}^{I}\tilde{X}^{I}\right)
in the quantum mechanical wavefunction must also respect the periodicity (
4.6
).
This requires

\omega^{I}\tilde{\pi}^{I}\in\mathbb{Z},

(4.13)

so that
\tilde{\pi}^{I}
is quantized

\tilde{\pi}^{I}=k^{I},\qquad k^{I}\in\mathbb{Z}.

(4.14)

We again rewrite this expression in more convenient way
[
21
,
22
]
, by defining

\tilde{X}^{I}=\sqrt{\tfrac{c^{\prime}}{2}}\,Y^{I}.

(4.15)

The mode expansion of
Y^{I}
is

Y^{I}=y^{I}+A^{I}_{0}\sigma+B^{I}_{0}\tau+i\sum_{n\neq 0}\frac{1}{n}\,(A^{I}_{n}-in\tau B^{I}_{n})\,e^{-in\sigma}.

(4.16)

Here,
\tilde{x}^{I}=\sqrt{\frac{c^{\prime}}{2}}y^{I}
. Compactification ensures that both zero modes are present. The
A^{I}_{0}
and
B^{I}_{0}
can be calculated as,

(B_{0})^{I}=\sqrt{2c^{\prime}}\Big(k^{I}-\sum_{J=1}^{d}B_{IJ}\,\omega^{J}\Big),\qquad A^{I}_{0}=\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{c^{\prime}}}\,\omega^{I}.

(4.17)

Alternatively, one may decompose
Y^{I}
into left- and right-movers in terms of the oscillators
C_{n}
and
{\tilde{C}_{n}}
as

\displaystyle Y^{I}_{L}

\displaystyle=y^{I}_{L}+k^{I}_{L}(\tau+\sigma)+i\sum_{n\neq 0}\frac{1}{n}\,(C^{I}_{n}-in\tau C^{I}_{n})\,e^{-in\sigma},

\displaystyle Y^{I}_{R}

\displaystyle=y^{I}_{R}+k^{I}_{R}(\tau-\sigma)+i\sum_{n\neq 0}\frac{1}{n}\,(\tilde{C}^{I}_{n}-in\tau\tilde{C}^{I}_{n})\,e^{in\sigma}.

(4.18)

The coefficients
k^{I}_{L,R}
represent the left- and right-moving momenta of the string in compact directions.
Imposing the identifications (
4.1
) and (
4.14
) yields

(k^{I})_{L,R}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left[\sqrt{c^{\prime}}\Big(k^{I}-\sum_{J=1}^{d}\mathbb{B}_{IJ}\,\omega^{J}\Big)\;\pm\;\frac{1}{\sqrt{c^{\prime}}}\sum_{J=1}^{d}G_{IJ}\,\omega^{J}\right].

(4.19)

4.1
Boundary conditions

As discussed in section
3
, the boundary conditions now reads as,

\partial_{\tau}\tilde{X}_{I}+\tfrac{1}{2\pi}\,\mathbb{B}_{IJ}\,\partial_{\sigma}\tilde{X}^{J}\bigg|_{\partial\Sigma}=0.

(4.20)

We choose
v=1
.

We now use the mode expansion
Y^{I}=Y_{L}^{I}+Y_{R}^{I}
which are related to
\tilde{X}^{I}
via equation (
4.15
). In terms of
Y^{I}
the above equation can be rewritten as,

\partial_{\tau}Y_{I}+\tfrac{1}{2\pi}\,\mathbb{B}_{IJ}\,\partial_{\sigma}Y^{J}\bigg|_{\partial\Sigma}=0.

(4.21)

The
\tau
-derivative is

\partial_{\tau}Y^{I}=k_{L}^{I}+k_{R}^{I}+\sum_{n\neq 0}(C_{n}^{I}-in\tau C_{n}^{I})e^{-in\sigma}+\sum_{n\neq 0}(\tilde{C}_{n}^{I}-in\tau\tilde{C}_{n}^{I})e^{in\sigma},

(4.22)

and the
\sigma
-derivative is

\partial_{\sigma}Y^{I}=k_{L}^{I}-k_{R}^{I}+\sum_{n\neq 0}(C_{n}^{I}-in\tau C_{n}^{I})e^{-in\sigma}+\sum_{n\neq 0}(\tilde{C}_{n}^{I}-in\tau\tilde{C}_{n}^{I})e^{in\sigma},

(4.23)

Now, inserting these into the boundary condition, we get the following

(i) for the zero mode:

k_{I}+\tfrac{1}{2\pi}\mathbb{B}_{IJ}\kappa^{J}=0,

(4.24)

where
k^{I}=(k_{L}^{I}+k_{R}^{I})
and
\kappa^{I}=(k_{L}^{I}-k_{R}^{I})
.

(ii) for oscillator/non-zero (
n\neq 0
) modes: we extract the coefficient of
e^{-in\sigma}
and we get

\Bigg[\left(G_{IJ}+\frac{1-in\tau}{2\pi}\mathbb{B}_{IJ}\right)C_{n}^{J}+\left(G_{IJ}-\frac{1+in\tau}{2\pi}\mathbb{B}_{IJ}\right)\tilde{C}_{-n}^{J}\Bigg]=0,

(4.25)

G_{IJ}
and
\mathbb{B}_{IJ}
are defined in equation (
4.4
). Again, the above equation can be written in terms of
\eta_{IJ}
and
\mathfrak{B}_{IJ}
and this gives the same form as in (
3.28
) with
\mu=I
, i.e.,

\Bigg[\left(\eta_{IJ}+\frac{1-in\tau}{2\pi}\mathfrak{B}_{IJ}\right)C_{n}^{J}+\left(\eta_{IJ}-\frac{1+in\tau}{2\pi}\mathfrak{B}_{IJ}\right)\tilde{C}_{-n}^{J}\Bigg]=0.

(4.26)

The induced vacuum
|I_{\mathfrak{B}}\rangle
must satisfy this operator equation as a constraint. We have

\Bigg[\left(\eta_{IJ}+\frac{1-in\tau}{2\pi}\mathfrak{B}_{IJ}\right)C_{n}^{J}+\left(\eta_{IJ}-\frac{1+in\tau}{2\pi}\mathfrak{B}_{IJ}\right)\tilde{C}_{-n}^{J}\Bigg]|I_{\mathfrak{B}}\rangle=0.

(4.27)

4.2
Gluing matrix

We take the tensionless limit (worldsheet Carroll limit) by scaling
\tau\to\epsilon\tau
and sending
\epsilon\to 0
. We get,

\frac{1\mp in\tau}{2\pi}\longrightarrow\frac{1}{2\pi}=a\quad(\text{say}).

(4.28)

We finally have

\big(C_{n}+R\,\tilde{C}_{-n}\big)\ket{I_{\mathfrak{B}}}=0\qquad(n>0),

(4.29)

with the gluing matrix
R
given by

R=(\eta+a\mathfrak{B})^{-1}(\eta-a\mathfrak{B}).

(4.30)

The matrix
R
acts on the indices of the oscillator modes. Its components are

R^{I}{}_{J}=\left[(\eta+a\mathfrak{B})^{-1}(\eta-a\mathfrak{B})\right]^{I}{}_{J}.

(4.31)

The squeezed operator is given by

\begin{split}\ket{I_{\mathfrak{B}}}&=\mathcal{N}\text{exp}\Bigg[{-\sum_{n>0}\frac{1}{n}\;C_{-n}^{I}\,R_{I}{}^{J}\,\tilde{C}_{-n\,J}}\Bigg]\ket{0}_{c}.\end{split}

(4.32)

As
\mathfrak{B}\to 0
,
R\to 1
, so the squeezed state becomes

\ket{I_{\mathfrak{B}}}\xrightarrow{\mathfrak{B}\to 0}\mathcal{N}\exp\!\left(-\sum_{n>0}\frac{1}{n}C_{-n}\cdot\tilde{C}_{-n}\right)\ket{0}_{c},

(4.33)

which is precisely the Neumann boundary state.

4.3
Bogoliubov generator

Bogoliubov generator is expressed as

G=i\sum_{n>0}\theta\left(C^{I}_{-n}R_{I}{}^{J}\tilde{C}_{-n\,J}-\text{h.c.}\right).

(4.34)

We have the transformed oscillators

e^{iG}C_{n}^{I}e^{-iG}=\cosh\theta\,C_{n}^{I}-\sinh\theta\,R^{I}{}_{J}\tilde{C}_{-n}^{J}.

(4.35)

Induced vacuum is

\ket{0}_{\alpha}=e^{iG}\ket{0}_{c}=\mathcal{N}\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\exp\left(\frac{\tanh\theta}{n}C^{I}_{-n}R_{I}{}^{J}\tilde{C}_{-n\,J}\right)\ket{0}_{c}.

(4.36)

We can recover squeezed boundary state in the limit
\tanh\theta\to-1

\ket{I_\mathfrak{B}}=\mathcal{N}\exp\left(-\sum_{n>0}\frac{1}{n}C^{I}_{-n}R_{I}{}^{J}\tilde{C}_{-n\,J}\right)\ket{0}_{c}.

(4.37)

4.4
Bose–Einstein condensation on the worldsheet

One of the most striking features of the tensionless strings is the collapse of excited tensile perturbative states onto the induced vacuum, a process that has been interpreted as a form of Bose–Einstein condensation
[
14
]
. In the absence of compactification or with trivial winding, oscillator excitations lose their individuality and collectively condense into the degenerate ground state. We now revisit this phenomenon in the presence of a constant Kalb–Ramond background.

Consider a physical tensile perturbative state,

|\zeta_{n}\rangle=\rho_{\mu\nu}\,\alpha_{-n}^{\mu}\,\tilde{\alpha}_{-n-\ell}^{\nu}\,|0,0,k^{\mu},k_{I},\omega^{I}\rangle_{\alpha}\,.

(4.38)

where
\rho_{\mu\nu}
is polarization tensor,
k_{I}

are quantized momenta and
\omega^{I}
are winding numbers.
The level matching condition for the tensile string with
d
compactified directions is,

\tilde{N}-N=\sum_{I}k_{I}\omega^{I}\equiv\ell

(4.39)

Now, we want to understand the evolution of the state
|0,0,k^{\mu},k_{I},\omega^{I}\rangle_{\alpha}
as we take tensionless limit (
\epsilon\to 0
). Around tensionless limit the tensile string vacuum can be perturbatively expanded in terms of
\epsilon
in following way,

|0,0,k^{\mu},k_{I},\omega^{I}\rangle_{\alpha}=|0\rangle_{I}+\epsilon\,|I_{1}\rangle+\epsilon^{2}\,|I_{2}\rangle+\cdots.

(4.40)

Recalling the definition of tensile vacua

\alpha_{n}|0\rangle_{\alpha}=\tilde{\alpha}_{n}|0\rangle_{\alpha}=0\quad\forall\,n>0.

(4.41)

If one writes down the tensile oscillators
\alpha_{n}
,
\tilde{\alpha}_{n}
in terms of tensionless oscillators
A_{n}
and
B_{n}
, one can finally arrive at the following conditions (depends crucially on the value of
\ell
) in the
\epsilon\to 0
limit:

Case
\ell=0
: condensation.

The background
B_{IJ}
only enters the level-matching condition through the combination

(k_{I}-B_{IJ}\,\omega^{J})
. However, the antisymmetry of
B_{IJ}
ensures that the bilinear
form
B_{IJ}\,\omega^{I}\omega^{J}
vanishes. Consequently, the condition
\ell=0
remains unaltered
by the
B
-field. In this case the perturbative state collapses onto the induced vacuum,

|\zeta_{n}\rangle\;\longrightarrow\;2n\eta^{\mu\nu}\rho_{\mu\nu}\,|0,0,k^{\mu},k_{I},\omega^{I}\rangle_{I}.

(4.42)

This represents Bose–Einstein condensation
of excitations into the degenerate ground state, exactly as in the
B=0
theory.

Case
\ell>0
: unphysical.

As before, the states with positive mismatch between left and right movers are projected out
by the residual BMS constraints. The Kalb–Ramond background does not rescue these states,
so they remain unphysical.

Case
\ell<0
: partial survival.

If
n\geq|\ell|
, the state is again unphysical. If
n<|\ell|
, the state vanishes in the limit.
Thus the condensation channel is completely obstructed once
\ell\neq 0
.

In summary, the presence of a constant Kalb–Ramond field does not modify the essential
mechanism of Bose–Einstein condensation in the induced vacuum sector. The antisymmetry of

B_{IJ}
ensures that the condensation criterion remains governed by the simple momentum-winding
pairing

\ell=\sum_{I}k_{I}\,\omega^{I}.

(4.43)

The
B
-field only reshuffles the definitions of the effective lattice vectors
(k_{I},\omega^{I})
,
but once the level-matching condition is satisfied, the condensation into the induced vacuum
proceeds identically.

5
Symplectic derivation of noncommutative parameter: tensile case

We now come to the other goal of this paper: to understand noncommutative picture for tensionless strings. In the tensionless regime, the worldsheet metric becomes degenerate, and the usual notions of left- and right-moving oscillators no longer exist. The worldsheet dynamics reduces to a first-order, ultralocal system rather than a two-dimensional conformal field theory. Because of this degeneration, the standard operator or path-integral approach
to compute the open-string two-point function cannot be applied; there is no nontrivial propagator or oscillator algebra from which one could extract a commutator. The standard operator approach
[
25
]
for computing noncommutativity parameter using 2-pt function in tensile theory is discussed in appendix
B
and comptation of 2-pt function for tensioless strings is discussed in appendix
C
. The symplectic formalism does not rely on mode expansions or worldsheet conformal structure, it
operates at the classical level of action and identifies the boundary phase-space structure through the symplectic two-form. Because of this, we use the symplectic approach for the tensionless case, where the canonical kinetic term collapses and only the boundary
B
-term survives. In this limit, the noncommutative structure can still be derived consistently by inverting the boundary symplectic form, even though the usual 2d CFT machinery is no longer available.

Before directly going into the tensionless case, in this section we plan to discuss the computation of the noncommutative parameter
\Theta^{ij}
for tensile open strings using the symplectic 2-form of the theory. Consider an open tensile bosonic string propagating in a flat
(g,B)
background, the Polyakov action in conformal gauge is

S=\frac{1}{4\pi\alpha^{\prime}}\int d\tau d\sigma\left[g_{ij}(\dot{X}^{i}\dot{X}^{j}-X^{\prime i}X^{\prime j})+2(2\pi\alpha^{\prime})B_{ij}\dot{X}^{i}X^{\prime j}\right],

(5.1)

where dots and primes denote
\partial_{\tau}
and
\partial_{\sigma}
respectively.

Variation of the action yields the equation of motion for the free fields and the boundary condition at the boundary
\sigma=0,\pi
is,

\displaystyle g_{ij}X^{\prime j}+2\pi\alpha^{\prime}B_{ij}\dot{X}^{j}

\displaystyle=0.

(5.2)

The canonical momentum can be calculated as,

\displaystyle\Pi_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi\alpha^{\prime}}g_{ij}\dot{X}^{j}+B_{ij}X^{\prime j}.

(5.3)

5.1
The symplectic form

In the canonical formulation, the coefficient of the time derivative in the
Lagrangian density defines the presymplectic one-form on the space of
fields
[
33
]
. The analogue of the mechanical relation
\theta=p_{i}\,\delta q^{i}

is therefore

\theta_{\Sigma}=\int_{\Sigma}d\sigma\,\Pi_{i}\,\delta X^{i},

(5.4)

which we refer to as the
presymplectic potential
on an equal-time
slice
\Sigma
. Its exterior derivative in field space,

\Omega_{\Sigma}=\delta\theta_{\Sigma}=\int d\sigma\,\delta\Pi_{i}\wedge\delta X^{i}
,
defines the bulk symplectic form
[
26
]
.

For an open string, however, the boundary condition
(
5.2
) constrains the variations of
\dot{X}
and

X^{\prime}
at the endpoints, so the independent physical degrees of freedom live
on the boundary phase space.
The relevant symplectic structure is obtained by restricting
(
5.4
) to the boundary constraint surface,
where
\dot{X}
is eliminated in favour of
X^{\prime}
(or vice versa) using
(
5.2
).
This restriction defines the
boundary symplectic potential

\theta_{\partial\Sigma}=\int_{\partial\Sigma}d\sigma\,\Pi_{i}\,\delta X^{i},

(5.5)

evaluated on the boundary condition

g_{ij}X^{\prime j}+2\pi\alpha^{\prime}B_{ij}\dot{X}^{j}=0
.
Taking its exterior derivative gives the corresponding boundary
symplectic form,

\Omega_{\partial\Sigma}=\delta\theta_{\partial\Sigma}
,

\Omega_{\partial\Sigma}=\int d\sigma\,\delta\Pi_{i}\wedge\delta X^{i}.

(5.6)

When
B=0
, this defines the standard Poisson bracket for the fields
X^{\mu}
and
\Pi^{\mu}
,

\displaystyle\big[\,X^{\mu}(\tau,\sigma),\,\Pi_{\nu}(\tau,\sigma^{\prime})\,\big]_{P.B.}

\displaystyle=\delta^{\mu}_{\nu}\,\delta(\sigma-\sigma^{\prime}),

(5.7)

\displaystyle\big[\,\Pi_{\mu}(\tau,\sigma),\,\Pi_{\nu}(\tau,\sigma^{\prime})\,\big]_{P.B.}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\big[\,X^{\mu}(\tau,\sigma),\,X^{\nu}(\tau,\sigma^{\prime})\,\big]_{P.B.}

\displaystyle=0.

These are the standard equal-time commutation relations for free fields. However, in the presence of
B
-fields, these are no longer valid; noncommutativity comes into play.

Equation (
5.2
) can be written in matrix form as,

\displaystyle\mathbf{g}X^{\prime}+2\pi\alpha^{\prime}\mathbf{B}\dot{X}

\displaystyle=0.

(5.8)

We now write
X^{\prime}
in terms of
\dot{X}
and substitute it to the canonical momenta. This gives the following,

\displaystyle\Pi=\frac{1}{2\pi\alpha^{\prime}}\,\mathbf{g}\dot{X}+\mathbf{B}X^{\prime}=\left[\frac{1}{2\pi\alpha^{\prime}}\,\mathbf{g}-2\pi\alpha^{\prime}\,\mathbf{B}\mathbf{g}^{-1}\mathbf{B}\right]\dot{X}=\mathbf{A}\dot{X},

(5.9)

where
\mathbf{A}:=\frac{1}{2\pi\alpha^{\prime}}\,\mathbf{g}-2\pi\alpha^{\prime}\,\mathbf{B}\mathbf{g}^{-1}\mathbf{B}
.

With this notation, the equal-time symplectic potential on the boundary can be written as,

\theta_{\partial\Sigma}=\int d\sigma\;\Pi_{i}\,\delta X^{i}=\int d\sigma\;\dot{X}^{T}\mathbf{A}\,\delta X,

(5.10)

matrix multiplication is implied:

\dot{X}^{T}\mathbf{A}\,\delta X\equiv\dot{X}^{i}A_{ij}\delta X^{j}
.

Varying once more gives the symplectic form,

\Omega_{\partial\Sigma}=\delta\theta_{\partial\Sigma}=\int d\sigma\;\bigl(\delta\dot{X}^{T}\mathbf{A}\,\delta X\bigr).

(5.11)

Integrating by parts in
\tau
(on the equal-time slice; boundary terms vanish) and antisymmetrizing (since
\delta X\wedge\delta X
is antisymmetric), we obtain

\begin{split}\Omega_{\partial\Sigma}&=\frac{1}{2}\int d\sigma\left(\delta\dot{X}^{T}\mathbf{A}\,\delta X-\delta X^{T}\mathbf{A}\,\delta\dot{X}\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\int d\sigma\;\delta X^{T}\bigl(\overleftarrow{\partial_{\tau}}\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{A}\overrightarrow{\partial_{\tau}}\bigr)\delta X.\end{split}

(5.12)

Since
\mathbf{A}
is constant (i.e. the background fields
g_{ij}
and
B_{ij}
are constant),
\partial_{\tau}\mathbf{A}=0
, and this reduces to

\Omega_{\partial\Sigma}=\int d\sigma\;\delta X^{T}\mathbf{A}\,\delta\dot{X}=\frac{1}{2}\int d\sigma\;\delta X^{T}\mathbf{A}\,\overleftrightarrow{\partial_{\tau}}\delta X,

(5.13)

where the bidirectional derivative is defined by

\delta X^{T}\mathbf{A}\,\overleftrightarrow{\partial_{\tau}}\delta X\;\equiv\;\delta X^{T}\mathbf{A}\,\partial_{\tau}\delta X-(\partial_{\tau}\delta X)^{T}\mathbf{A}\,\delta X.

(5.14)

A more convenient way is to rewrite
\mathbf{A}
in terms of the standard
\mathbf{E}
and
\mathbf{E}^{T}
matrices

\mathbf{E}:=\mathbf{g}+2\pi\alpha^{\prime}\,\mathbf{B},\qquad\mathbf{E}^{T}:=\mathbf{g}-2\pi\alpha^{\prime}\,\mathbf{B},

(5.15)

where
\mathbf{g}
is a symmetric metric (
\mathbf{g}^{T}=\mathbf{g}
) and
\mathbf{B}
is antisymmetric (
\mathbf{B}^{T}=-\mathbf{B}
), so
\mathbf{E}^{T}
is indeed the transpose of
\mathbf{E}
.

Then one has the algebraic identity

\mathbf{A}=\frac{1}{2\pi\alpha^{\prime}}\,\mathbf{E}\,\mathbf{g}^{-1}\,\mathbf{E}^{T}.

(5.16)

The symplectic 2-form can now be written as,

\Omega_{\partial\Sigma}=\frac{1}{4\pi\alpha^{\prime}}\int d\sigma\;\delta{X}^{T}\,(\mathbf{E}\,\mathbf{g}^{-1}\,\mathbf{E}^{T})\,\overleftrightarrow{\partial_{\tau}}\delta X=\frac{1}{4\pi\alpha^{\prime}}\int d\sigma\;\delta{X}^{T}\,\mathbf{M}\,\overleftrightarrow{\partial_{\tau}}\delta X,

(5.17)

where
\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{E}\,\mathbf{g}^{-1}\,\mathbf{E}^{T}
. We now use the boundary condition once to write
\dot{X}
in terms of
X^{\prime}
,

\delta X^{\prime}=-2\pi\alpha^{\prime}\,\mathbf{g}^{-1}\mathbf{B}\,\delta\dot{X}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\delta\dot{X}=-\frac{1}{2\pi\alpha^{\prime}}\,\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{g}\,\delta X^{\prime}.

(5.18)

Substituting this in equation (
5.17
) gives the following,

\Omega_{\partial\Sigma}=\frac{1}{2\,(2\pi\alpha^{\prime})^{2}}\int d\sigma\left[\delta X^{\prime T}\,\mathbf{g}\,\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{M}\delta X\;+\;\delta X^{T}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{g}\,\delta X^{\prime}\right].

(5.19)

Since all background fields are constant, and the boundary variations vanish, we may use

\int d\sigma\,\delta X^{\prime T}\mathbf{Q}\,\delta X=-\int d\sigma\,\delta X^{T}\mathbf{Q}^{T}\,\delta X^{\prime},

(5.20)

for any constant matrix
\mathbf{Q}
.
Take
\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{g}\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{M}
, then

\mathbf{Q}^{T}=\mathbf{M}^{T}(\mathbf{B}^{-1})^{T}\mathbf{g}^{T}=\mathbf{M}(-\mathbf{B}^{-1})\mathbf{g}=-\mathbf{M}\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{g}.

(5.21)

Hence,

\int\delta X^{\prime T}\mathbf{g}\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{M}\,\delta X=\int\delta X^{T}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{g}\,\delta X^{\prime}.

(5.22)

Substituting back into (
5.20
), both terms in the integrand are identical, and the prefactor
1/2
cancels. We thus obtain

\Omega_{\partial\Sigma}=\frac{1}{(2\pi\alpha^{\prime})^{2}}\int d\sigma\,\delta X^{T}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{g}\,\delta X^{\prime}.

(5.23)

We antisymmetrize the expression in
\sigma
by noting that only the antisymmetric part of
\mathbf{Q}
contributes:

\displaystyle\int d\sigma\,\delta X^{T}\mathbf{Q}\,\delta X^{\prime}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\int d\sigma\,\delta X^{T}\bigl(\mathbf{Q}-\mathbf{Q}^{T}\bigr)\overleftrightarrow{\partial_{\sigma}}\delta X,

(5.24)

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}^{T}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{g}^{T}(\mathbf{B}^{-1})^{T}\mathbf{M}^{T}=\mathbf{g}\,(-\mathbf{B}^{-1})\,\mathbf{M}=-\mathbf{g}\,\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{M},

(5.25)

where we used
\mathbf{g}^{T}=\mathbf{g}
,
\mathbf{B}^{T}=-\mathbf{B}
, and
\mathbf{M}^{T}=\mathbf{M}
(since
\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{E}\,\mathbf{g}^{-1}\mathbf{E}^{T}
is symmetric).

Therefore,

\mathbf{Q}-\mathbf{Q}^{T}=\mathbf{M}\,\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{g}+\mathbf{g}\,\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{M}.

(5.26)

Inserting this into the previous line gives

\Omega_{\partial\Sigma}=-\frac{1}{2\,(2\pi\alpha^{\prime})^{2}}\int d\sigma\,\delta X^{T}\,\bigl(\mathbf{M}\,\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{g}+\mathbf{g}\,\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{M}\bigr)\overleftrightarrow{\partial_{\sigma}}\delta X.

(5.27)

The linearized open-string boundary condition is

\mathbf{g}\,\delta X^{\prime}+2\pi\alpha^{\prime}\,\mathbf{B}\,\delta\dot{X}=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\overleftrightarrow{\partial_{\sigma}}=-\,2\pi\alpha^{\prime}\,\mathbf{g}^{-1}\mathbf{B}\,\overleftrightarrow{\partial_{\tau}}.

(5.28)

Substitute this into (
5.27
). The two minus signs cancel, and one factor of
2\pi\alpha^{\prime}
cancels from the denominator, yielding

\displaystyle\Omega_{\partial\Sigma}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi\alpha^{\prime}}\int d\sigma\,\delta X^{T}\bigl(\mathbf{M}\,\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{g}+\mathbf{g}\,\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{M}\bigr)\mathbf{g}^{-1}\mathbf{B}\,\overleftrightarrow{\partial_{\tau}}\delta X

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi\alpha^{\prime}}\int d\sigma\,\delta X^{T}\underbrace{\bigl(\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{g}\,\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{M}\,\mathbf{g}^{-1}\mathbf{B}\bigr)}_{\mathbf{S}}\overleftrightarrow{\partial_{\tau}}\delta X.

(5.29)

Hence

\Omega_{\partial\Sigma}=\frac{1}{4\pi\alpha^{\prime}}\int d\sigma\,\delta X^{T}\mathbf{S}\,\overleftrightarrow{\partial_{\tau}}\,\delta X,\qquad\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{g}\,\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{M}\,\mathbf{g}^{-1}\mathbf{B}.

(5.30)

By definition, the equal-time symplectic form can be written as

\Omega_{\partial\Sigma}[\delta_{1},\delta_{2}]=\frac{1}{2}\int d\sigma\Big(\delta_{1}X^{T}\mathbf{K}_{A}\,\delta_{2}X-\delta_{2}X^{T}\mathbf{K}_{A}\,\delta_{1}X\Big)=\int d\sigma\,\frac{1}{2}\,\delta X^{i}\wedge\delta X^{j}\,\mathbf{K}_{A,ij}.

(5.31)

Equal-time interpretation.

In constructing the boundary symplectic form we always work on a fixed time slice

\Sigma=\Sigma_{\tau}
of the worldsheet.
This “equal-time” restriction is essential: the symplectic structure is a geometric
two-form on phase space and must therefore be defined using the values of the fields
at a single time.

The presymplectic potential and its exterior derivative are evaluated at fixed
\tau
,

\theta_{\Sigma_{\tau}}=\int d\sigma\,\Pi_{i}(\tau,\sigma)\,\delta X^{i}(\tau,\sigma),\qquad\Omega_{\Sigma_{\tau}}=\delta\theta_{\Sigma_{\tau}}.

(5.32)

Any expression involving the bidirectional time derivative,

\delta X^{T}S\,\overleftrightarrow{\partial_{\tau}}\,\delta X=\delta X^{T}S\,\partial_{\tau}\delta X-(\partial_{\tau}\delta X)^{T}S\,\delta X,

(5.33)

is therefore interpreted purely as an antisymmetrization in field space.
Because all variations are evaluated at the same value of
\tau
, the actual time derivative

\partial_{\tau}
never acts on physical evolution; it only serves as a bookkeeping device
to extract the antisymmetric part of the kernel
S
.
Thus we use the standard identity

\frac{1}{2}\int d\sigma\,\delta X^{T}S\,\overleftrightarrow{\partial_{\tau}}\,\delta X\;=\;\int d\sigma\,\delta X^{T}S\,\delta X,

(5.34)

which converts the “equal-time” antisymmetrized derivative into the wedge product

\delta X\wedge\delta X
defining the symplectic two-form.
In this way the final symplectic structure is entirely algebraic, as required for a
well-defined Poisson bracket on the equal-time phase space.

For constant backgrounds, the kernel
\mathbf{K}_{A}
obtained in this way contains no
worldsheet derivatives and is entirely determined by the matrices
\mathbf{g}
,
\mathbf{B}
, and

\mathbf{E}=\mathbf{g}+2\pi\alpha^{\prime}\mathbf{B}
.
Using matrix manipulations with
\mathbf{S}
from (
5.30
) and the definition
\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{E}\,\mathbf{g}^{-1}\mathbf{E}^{T}
, one finds

\mathbf{K}_{A}=\frac{1}{4\pi\alpha^{\prime}}\,(\mathbf{E}\,\mathbf{g}^{-1}\mathbf{E}^{T})^{-1}\,(2\pi\alpha^{\prime}\,\mathbf{B})=\frac{1}{4\pi\alpha^{\prime}}\,\mathbf{M}^{-1}\,(2\pi\alpha^{\prime}\,\mathbf{B}),

(5.35)

which is antisymmetric because
\mathbf{M}^{-1}
is symmetric and
\mathbf{B}
is antisymmetric.

The matrices
\mathbf{g}
,
\mathbf{B}
,
\mathbf{M}
and
\mathbf{S}
are related by the following equation,

-\bigl(\mathbf{S}\,\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{g}-\mathbf{g}\,\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{S}\bigr)=\mathbf{M}^{-1}\bigl(2\pi\alpha^{\prime}\,\mathbf{B}\bigr).

(5.36)

The symplectic 2-form takes the compact, algebraic form

\Omega_{\partial\Sigma}=\frac{1}{4\pi\alpha^{\prime}}\int d\sigma\,\delta X^{T}\Big[(\mathbf{E}\,\mathbf{g}^{-1}\mathbf{E}^{T})^{-1}(2\pi\alpha^{\prime}\,\mathbf{B})\Big]\delta X=\int d\sigma\,\frac{1}{2}\,\delta X^{i}\wedge\delta X^{j}\,\mathbf{K}_{A,ij}.

(5.37)

5.2
The noncommutative parameter

From (
5.35
), we have

\mathbf{K}=\frac{1}{4\pi\alpha^{\prime}}\bigl(\mathbf{E}\,\mathbf{g}^{-1}\mathbf{E}^{T}\bigr)^{-1}\bigl(2\pi\alpha^{\prime}\,\mathbf{B}\bigr).

(5.38)

The Poisson tensor is the inverse of
\mathbf{K}

[
32
,
54
]
,

\boldsymbol{\Theta}=\mathbf{K}^{-1}.

(5.39)

Using the identity

\bigl(\mathbf{E}\,\mathbf{g}^{-1}\mathbf{E}^{T}\bigr)^{-1}(2\pi\alpha^{\prime}\,\mathbf{B})=-\,2\pi\alpha^{\prime}\,\mathbf{E}^{-1}\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{E}^{T})^{-1},

(5.40)

we find

\mathbf{K}=-\frac{1}{2}\,\mathbf{E}^{-1}\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{E}^{T})^{-1}.

(5.41)

This implies that the noncommutative parameter
\mathbf{\Theta}
is,

\mathbf{\Theta}=-\,(2\pi\alpha^{\prime})^{2}\,\Bigl[\bigl(\mathbf{g}+2\pi\alpha^{\prime}\,\mathbf{B}\bigr)^{-1}\,\mathbf{B}\,\bigl(\mathbf{g}-2\pi\alpha^{\prime}\,\mathbf{B}\bigr)^{-1}\Bigr].

(5.42)

This is the standard Seiberg–Witten noncommutativity parameter
[
25
]
. Decomposing
(\mathbf{g}+2\pi\alpha^{\prime}\mathbf{B})^{-1}
into its symmetric and antisymmetric
parts reproduces the standard open-string metric

\mathbf{G}=\mathbf{g}-(2\pi\alpha^{\prime})^{2}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{g}^{-1}\mathbf{B}
and the familiar boundary correlator structure.

In the large-
\mathbf{B}
limit
[
25
]
, it reduces to

\Theta^{ij}\;\longrightarrow\;(\mathbf{B}^{-1})^{ij}.

(5.43)

6
Noncommutativity in tensionless strings

We now derive the noncommutative parameter
\Theta_{ij}
for the
tensionless
(null) string
in the presence of a constant background
B
-field.
The analysis parallels the symplectic derivation for the tensile open string,
However, in the present case, the canonical kinetic term degenerates, and only the
B
-coupling survives.

6.1
Action and the boundary condition

We start from the ILST (null string) action in the gauge
V^{\alpha}=(v,0)
with

v^{2}=\frac{1}{2\pi c^{\prime}}
:

S=\int d\tau\,d\sigma\,\Big[v^{2}\,g_{ij}\,\dot{X}^{i}\dot{X}^{j}+\frac{1}{\pi}\,B_{ij}\,\dot{X}^{i}X^{\prime j}\Big],

(6.1)

where dots and primes denote
\partial_{\tau}
and
\partial_{\sigma}
.
Both
g_{ij}
and
B_{ij}
are taken to be constant, with
B_{ij}=-B_{ji}
.

Varying
X^{i}\to X^{i}+\delta X^{i}
gives

\displaystyle\delta S

\displaystyle=\int d\tau\,d\sigma\,\Big[2v^{2}g_{ij}\,\dot{X}^{j}\,\delta\dot{X}^{i}+\frac{1}{\pi}B_{ij}\,\delta\dot{X}^{i}X^{\prime j}+\frac{1}{\pi}B_{ij}\,\dot{X}^{i}\delta X^{\prime j}\Big].

(6.2)

Integrating by parts in
\tau
and
\sigma
in the bulk (and neglecting total derivatives)
leads to the ultralocal equation of motion

\ddot{X}^{i}=0,

(6.3)

which simply states that each point on the string evolves freely in
\tau
.

The nontrivial information comes from the
time-boundary term
, which is

\delta S\big|_{\tau-\text{bdy}}=\int d\sigma\,\Big[2v^{2}g_{ij}\,\dot{X}^{j}+\frac{1}{\pi}B_{ij}\,X^{\prime j}\Big]\delta X^{i}.

(6.4)

Requiring
\delta S=0
for arbitrary
\delta X
at the time boundaries yields

2v^{2}\,g_{ij}\,\dot{X}^{j}+\frac{1}{\pi}B_{ij}\,X^{\prime j}=0,

(6.5)

or equivalently in compact matrix form,

\frac{1}{c^{\prime}}\,\mathbf{g}\,\dot{X}+\mathbf{B}\,X^{\prime}=0.

(6.6)

This is the tensionless analogue of the mixed Neumann boundary condition
[
25
]
,

g_{ij}\partial_{n}X^{j}+2\pi\alpha^{\prime}B_{ij}\,\partial_{t}X^{j}=0,

(6.7)

in the tensile theory.
From (
6.1
), the canonical momentum density is

\Pi_{i}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\dot{X}^{i}}=2v^{2}g_{ij}\dot{X}^{j}+\frac{1}{\pi}B_{ij}X^{\prime j}=\frac{1}{\pi}\Big(\frac{1}{c^{\prime}}\mathbf{g}\,\dot{X}+\mathbf{B}\,X^{\prime}\Big)_{i}.

(6.8)

Substituting the boundary condition (
6.5
) immediately gives

\Pi_{i}\big|_{\tau-\text{bdy}}=0.

(6.9)

In the tensionless regime (
\alpha^{\prime}\to\infty
,
c^{\prime}\to\infty
), the canonical (metric) term does not contribute to the boundary dynamics,
and only the
B
-term will control the boundary symplectic structure.

6.2
The symplectic 2-form

The first-order structure in the Lagrangian,

\mathcal{L}_{\text{first-order}}=\frac{1}{\pi}B_{ij}\,\dot{X}^{i}X^{\prime j},

(6.10)

identifies the canonical 1-form (symplectic potential)
[
31
]

\theta_{\partial\Sigma}=\int d\sigma\;a_{i}\,\delta X^{i},\qquad a_{i}=\frac{1}{\pi}B_{ij}\,X^{\prime j}.

(6.11)

Integrating by parts in
\sigma
(assuming variations vanish at spatial ends) and
using antisymmetry of
B
, one finds

\displaystyle\int d\sigma\,B_{ij}\,X^{\prime j}\,\delta X^{i}

\displaystyle=-\int d\sigma\,B_{ij}\,X^{j}\,\partial_{\sigma}\delta X^{i}=\frac{1}{2}\int d\sigma\,B_{ij}\,X^{i}\,\delta X^{j}.

(6.12)

Thus, we can write the boundary symplectic potential as,

\theta_{\partial\Sigma}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int d\sigma\,B_{ij}\,X^{i}\,\delta X^{j}.

(6.13)

The factor of
\tfrac{1}{2}
arises from antisymmetrization
between
(i,j)
indices when eliminating
\partial_{\sigma}
.

Taking the exterior derivative in field space gives the boundary symplectic form

\Omega_{\partial\Sigma}=\delta\theta_{\partial\Sigma}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int d\sigma\;B_{ij}\,\delta X^{i}\wedge\delta X^{j}.

(6.14)

This expression is purely algebraic (no worldsheet derivatives) and
independent of
c^{\prime}
and
g
.
It is non-degenerate on the subspace where
B_{ij}
has rank.

The kernel of (
6.14
) is
\frac{1}{2\pi}B_{ij}
, whose inverse is
2\pi\mathbf{B}^{-1}
.
Thus the equal-time Poisson brackets read

\{X^{i}(\sigma),X^{j}(\sigma^{\prime})\}_{\partial\Sigma}=2\pi\,(B^{-1})^{ij}\,\delta(\sigma-\sigma^{\prime}).

(6.15)

Upon quantization,

[X^{i}(\sigma),X^{j}(\sigma^{\prime})]=i\,2\pi\,(B^{-1})^{ij}\,\delta(\sigma-\sigma^{\prime})

(6.16)

6.3
\Theta_{\text{tensile}}\to\Theta_{\text{tensionless}}

The two noncommutative parameters for tensile and tensionless theory presented above are structurally similar, yet they describe
two distinct physical regimes of the string worldsheet.

In the
tensile
case, the worldsheet theory is a genuine two-dimensional
conformal field theory with a non-degenerate induced metric.
Both the metric term and the
B
-term contribute to the canonical structure.
The full boundary symplectic kernel in that case is

\mathbf{K}_{A}^{(\text{tensile})}=\frac{1}{4\pi\alpha^{\prime}}\,\mathbf{M}^{-1}\,(2\pi\alpha^{\prime}\,\mathbf{B}),\qquad\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{E}\,\mathbf{g}^{-1}\,\mathbf{E}^{T},\quad\mathbf{E}=\mathbf{g}+2\pi\alpha^{\prime}\,\mathbf{B}.

(6.17)

Inverting the symplectic kernel gives the Poisson tensor

\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{\text{tensile}}=-\,(2\pi\alpha^{\prime})^{2}\Big[(\mathbf{g}+2\pi\alpha^{\prime}\mathbf{B})^{-1}\,\mathbf{B}\,(\mathbf{g}-2\pi\alpha^{\prime}\mathbf{B})^{-1}\Big].

(6.18)

This expression is the standard Seiberg–Witten formula for the
noncommutative parameter
[
25
]
.
It contains both symmetric and antisymmetric contributions, the former giving
the open-string metric

\mathbf{G}=\mathbf{g}-(2\pi\alpha^{\prime})^{2}\,\mathbf{B}\mathbf{g}^{-1}\mathbf{B}.

Tensionless reduction.

The
tensionless
(or Carrollian) string corresponds to the regime

T\sim\frac{1}{2\pi\alpha^{\prime}}\longrightarrow 0,\qquad\text{or equivalently }\alpha^{\prime}\longrightarrow\infty.

(6.19)

In this limit, the coefficient of the metric term in the action vanishes,
and the dynamics becomes first order in time derivatives.

To extract the corresponding limit of (
6.18
), note that
the combination
2\pi\alpha^{\prime}\mathbf{B}
appears ubiquitously.
Define

\mathbf{F}:=2\pi\alpha^{\prime}\mathbf{B},

(6.20)

and take
\alpha^{\prime}\to\infty
keeping
\mathbf{F}
finite and large.
Then

\mathbf{E}=\mathbf{g}+\mathbf{F}\simeq\mathbf{F},\qquad\mathbf{E}^{T}=\mathbf{g}-\mathbf{F}\simeq-\,\mathbf{F}.

Substituting into (
6.18
),

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{\text{tensile}}

\displaystyle\simeq-\,(2\pi\alpha^{\prime})^{2}\,\Big[\mathbf{F}^{-1}\,\mathbf{B}\,(-\mathbf{F}^{-1})\Big]=(2\pi\alpha^{\prime})^{2}\,\mathbf{F}^{-1}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{F}^{-1}.

(6.21)

Using
\mathbf{F}=2\pi\alpha^{\prime}\mathbf{B}
, we find

\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{\text{tensile}}\;\xrightarrow[\alpha^{\prime}\to\infty]{}\;\mathbf{B}^{-1}.

(6.22)

This is precisely the noncommutativity tensor
derived directly from the tensionless analysis,

\Theta^{ij}_{\text{tensionless}}=(B^{-1})^{ij}.

Physical interpretation.

The equivalence (
6.22
) can be interpreted in two different ways:

1.

In the tensile theory
, the limit
\alpha^{\prime}\to\infty

(or equivalently vanishing tension
T\to 0
)
suppresses the second-order metric term in the Polyakov action.
The worldsheet metric becomes degenerate, and the first-order

B
-term dominates, turning the theory effectively into a
topological boundary system.

2.

In the tensionless formulation
, one starts from the
degenerate worldsheet from the outset. The phase-space structure
is governed entirely by the
B
-term, and its inverse defines
the Poisson brackets directly. No limiting procedure is required:
noncommutativity is an intrinsic property of the null worldsheet.

Therefore, the tensionless symplectic structure

\Omega_{\partial\Sigma}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int d\sigma\,B_{ij}\,\delta X^{i}\wedge\delta X^{j},\qquad\Theta^{ij}_{\text{tensionless}}=(B^{-1})^{ij},

(6.23)

is the natural Carrollian analogue of the tensile symplectic form

\Omega_{\partial\Sigma}=\frac{1}{4\pi\alpha^{\prime}}\int d\sigma\,\delta X^{T}(\mathbf{E}\mathbf{g}^{-1}\mathbf{E}^{T})^{-1}(2\pi\alpha^{\prime}\mathbf{B})\,\delta X,

(6.24)

\Theta^{ij}_{\text{tensile}}=-(2\pi\alpha^{\prime})^{2}\bigl[(\mathbf{g}+2\pi\alpha^{\prime}\mathbf{B})^{-1}\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{g}-2\pi\alpha^{\prime}\mathbf{B})^{-1}\bigr]^{ij}.

(6.25)

The two forms match exactly in the large-
B
, zero-tension limit of tensile theory.
In other words,

\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha^{\prime}\to\infty\\[2.0pt]
2\pi\alpha^{\prime}\mathbf{B}\ \text{fixed}\end{subarray}}\Theta^{ij}_{\text{tensile}}=\Theta^{ij}_{\text{tensionless}}=(B^{-1})^{ij}.

(6.26)

The tensionless string thus provides an
intrinsic geometric realization

of noncommutativity that in the tensile case emerges only after taking
the
\alpha^{\prime}\to\infty
limit.
Both descriptions yield the same antisymmetric Poisson structure on the boundary phase space, but their origin differs:
in the tensile case, it is a limiting deformation of the full 2D CFT;
in the tensionless case, it is a built-in feature of the null (Carrollian)
worldsheet geometry.

7
Conclusions and future directions

In this work, we have presented a comprehensive analysis of the emergence of open strings from closed tensionless strings in the presence of a uniform Kalb–Ramond background and investigated how noncommutativity arises in this setting through a purely symplectic approach.

In the first part, we established that the inclusion of a constant antisymmetric Kalb–Ramond background modifies the induced vacuum of the tensionless string, leading to a generalized gluing condition between modes. This deformation is encoded in a constant gluing matrix which smoothly interpolates between the standard Neumann condition and its background-deformed version. The resulting induced vacuum takes the form of a squeezed boundary state, manifesting the continuous evolution from the closed-string vacuum to an open-string-like configuration as the tension goes to zero. Furthermore, we extended this construction to the case of compactification on
T^{d}
, where the interplay between winding and momentum modes leads to a Kalb–Ramond background-field-dependent deformation of the induced vacuum, while the mechanism of Bose–Einstein condensation on the worldsheet remains robust and unaltered by the antisymmetric background.

In the second part, we investigated the symplectic and geometric origin of the noncommutative structure associated with strings in constant
(g,B)
backgrounds. Starting from first principles, we showed that both the tensile and the tensionless strings can be described within a unified symplectic framework. The boundary contribution to the symplectic potential encodes all the information about the noncommutative phase-space geometry. In the tensile regime, this reproduces the well-known Seiberg–Witten relation between the open-string metric
G_{ij}
, the antisymmetric parameter
\Theta^{ij}
, and the background
B_{ij}
. In the tensionless regime, the symplectic structure degenerates in the bulk but survives on the boundary, where it defines an intrinsic noncommutative geometry governed solely by the background
B
-field. Remarkably, the noncommutativity of the tensionless string arises not as a limit of the tensile theory, but as an intrinsic feature of the null (Carrollian) worldsheet.

Future directions

Our analysis opens several avenues for further research.

Flux backgrounds and the role of
\mathcal{F}=B+F
in the tensionless phase.

A natural extension of our analysis is to incorporate a worldvolume gauge field
A_{\mu}
, which in the tensile string enters only through the gauge-invariant combination

\mathcal{F}=B+F
. Our results indicate that the emergent open string phase and its boundary symplectic structure behave as a Carrollian analogue of a brane. In this framework, turning on

F_{\mu\nu}
amounts to the replacement
B\to\mathcal{F}
in the boundary conditions, gluing matrix, and symplectic two-form. This would lead to a generalized noncommutativity parameter

\Theta\sim\mathcal{F}^{-1}
, providing a tensionless counterpart of the familiar
B+F
deformation in the Seiberg–Witten analysis. Understanding how gauge flux and bound-state
structures arise directly from the null worldsheet, without relying on a pre-existing D-brane, would offer a novel perspective on flux backgrounds in the Carrollian regime and may reveal tensionless analogues of dielectric or Myers-type effects.

Supersymmetric generalization.

Incorporating worldsheet fermions
\psi^{i}
and their conjugate momenta extends the symplectic form to a super-symplectic structure. Such an extension would allow one to study the deformation of the boundary superalgebra induced by the background field and to determine how the supersymmetry transformations act on the noncommutative phase space of the endpoints.

Slowly varying backgrounds.

The analysis in this paper assumed constant
(g,B)
fields, for which the boundary
symplectic kernel becomes purely algebraic.
A natural extension is to treat slowly varying backgrounds with

\partial_{k}B_{ij}\neq 0
.
In
[
25
]
, Seiberg and Witten showed that such variations
produce derivative corrections to the open-string metric, the noncommutativity
parameter.
From the symplectic point of view, these corrections would manifest as new

\partial B
contributions to the boundary two-form, making the kernel no longer
purely algebraic.
Understanding these corrections directly at the level of the boundary symplectic
structure could give a geometric derivation of the derivative-corrected
open-string data and may shed light on how noncommutativity behaves for
tensionless strings in inhomogeneous backgrounds.

Quantization and Moyal deformation.

Our symplectic derivation naturally suggests a quantization scheme for the boundary phase space of tensionless strings. A detailed study of the associated Moyal
\star
-algebra
[
58
]
and its physical interpretation could yield new insights into the quantum geometry of null strings.

Covariant phase-space formulation.

It would be interesting to revisit our analysis using the covariant phase-space formalism of Crnković, Witten, and Zuckerman
[
34
,
35
]
(see also
[
36
,
37
,
38
,
39
,
40
]
). This framework constructs the symplectic form directly from the action without choosing an equal-time slicing, and naturally incorporates boundary contributions in the presence of a
B
-field.
Applying it to the tensionless worldsheet could provide a compact, covariant interpretation of the boundary symplectic structure obtained here.

World-volume actions for D-branes in the tensionless regime.

Given that the induced vacuum behaves as an open string attached to a space-filling brane, it would be valuable to derive the world-volume actions for D-branes. A Carrollian analogue of the Born–Infeld action, particularly in the presence of a constant
B
-field, may clarify the emergence of gauge dynamics in the tensionless phase.

Higher-form backgrounds and generalized charges.

Including RR fields or higher
p
-form backgrounds can test the robustness of the closed-to-open transition. Such setups may lead to tensionless analogues of dielectric branes or Myers-like effects
[
41
]
driven by worldsheet constraints.

Appendix A
Symplectic form, Poisson brackets and the Moyal
\star
-Product

In this appendix, we review some basic ideas about symplectic form and Poission brackets
[
53
,
54
]
.
The symplectic formulation of classical mechanics provides a coordinate-independent
and geometrically natural language for describing dynamical systems.
Rather than treating coordinates and momenta separately, one regards the

phase space
as a smooth, even-dimensional manifold
M
,
equipped with a closed and non-degenerate two-form
\Omega
,
called the
symplectic form
.
This two-form is the central object of Hamiltonian dynamics, from which
both the equations of motion and the structure of Poisson brackets emerge.

A.1
Symplectic form and its properties

A
symplectic manifold

(M,\Omega)
is a differentiable manifold
M

equipped with a differential two-form

\Omega=\frac{1}{2}\,\Omega_{\mu\nu}(q)\,dq^{\mu}\wedge dq^{\nu},

(A.1)

satisfying

d\Omega=0,\qquad\det(\Omega_{\mu\nu})\neq 0.

(A.2)

The first condition expresses the closure of the form under exterior differentiation,
while the second ensures its non-degeneracy.
The non-degeneracy implies the existence of an inverse matrix
\Omega^{\mu\nu}
,
defined by

\Omega^{\mu\alpha}\,\Omega_{\alpha\nu}=\delta^{\mu}_{\;\nu}.

(A.3)

The closure condition
d\Omega=0
means that, at least locally,
there exists a one-form
A
such that

\Omega=dA,\qquad A=A_{\mu}(q)\,dq^{\mu},

(A.4)

called the
symplectic potential
or
canonical one-form
.
There is a gauge-like freedom

A\;\longrightarrow\;A+d\Lambda,

(A.5)

for any smooth function
\Lambda(q)
, which leaves
\Omega
invariant.

Canonical transformations and Hamiltonian vector fields

Let,
\xi=\xi^{\mu}(q)\,\partial_{\mu}
be a vector field on
M
.
The Lie derivative gives its action on the symplectic form,

\mathcal{L}_{\xi}\Omega=i_{\xi}d\Omega+d(i_{\xi}\Omega).

(A.6)

A transformation generated by
\xi
is
canonical

if it preserves
\Omega
, that is,
\mathcal{L}_{\xi}\Omega=0
.
Since
d\Omega=0
, this reduces to

d(i_{\xi}\Omega)=0.

(A.7)

Therefore,
i_{\xi}\Omega
is a closed one-form.
If the first cohomology group,
H^{1}(M)
is trivial, one can write

i_{\xi}\Omega=-df,

(A.8)

for some function
f(q)
on
M
.
This function
f
is called the
generator

of the canonical transformation, and
\xi
is the corresponding

Hamiltonian vector field
.
In local coordinates, the components of
\xi
can be written as

\xi^{\mu}=\Omega^{\mu\nu}\,\partial_{\nu}f.

(A.9)

Hence, the flow generated by
f
acts on any other function
g

through

\xi(g)=\Omega^{\mu\nu}\,\partial_{\mu}f\,\partial_{\nu}g.

(A.10)

This operation will define the Poisson bracket.

Every smooth function on phase space generates a Hamiltonian flow
and thus corresponds to an infinitesimal canonical transformation. Observables are no longer passive quantities;
each defines a transformation preserving the symplectic structure.
In particular, the Hamiltonian function governing the energy
of the system generates the time evolution.

A.2
Poisson brackets

Given two smooth functions
f(q)
and
g(q)
on
M
,
their
Poisson bracket
is defined by

\{f,g\}=\Omega^{\mu\nu}\,\partial_{\mu}f\,\partial_{\nu}g.

(A.11)

This operation measures the infinitesimal change of one observable under the
canonical flow generated by the other.
For any function
F(q)
, its variation under the flow of
f
is

\delta F=\{F,f\}.

(A.12)

From the antisymmetry of
\Omega^{\mu\nu}
, one has

\{f,g\}=-\{g,f\},

(A.13)

and from the closure of
\Omega
,
the Poisson bracket satisfies the
Jacobi identity
,

\{f,\{g,h\}\}+\{g,\{h,f\}\}+\{h,\{f,g\}\}=0.

(A.14)

The set of smooth functions on
M
thus forms a Lie algebra
under the Poisson bracket.

In local canonical coordinates
(x^{i},p_{i})
,
where

\Omega=dp_{i}\wedge dx^{i},

(A.15)

one finds the familiar relations

\{x^{i},x^{j}\}=0,\qquad\{x^{i},p_{j}\}=\delta^{i}_{j},\qquad\{p_{i},p_{j}\}=0.

(A.16)

Remarks.

It is often instructive to compare the symplectic formulation with other approaches.
If we apply the definition of the Poisson bracket directly to the phase-space
coordinates themselves, we find

\{q^{\mu},q^{\nu}\}=\Omega^{\mu\nu}.

(A.17)

This simple relation expresses that the “basic Poisson brackets” of the coordinates
are nothing but the inverse of the symplectic structure.
In other words,
\Omega^{\mu\nu}
plays the same role in the algebra of functions
that
\Omega_{\mu\nu}
plays in the geometry of the manifold.
This identification provides a clear link between the geometric and algebraic
descriptions of classical mechanics.

Symplectic potential and gauge analogy

The canonical one-form
A=p_{i}\,dx^{i}
satisfies
\Omega=dA
.
Under an infinitesimal canonical transformation generated by
f
,
one finds

\delta_{\xi}A=\mathcal{L}_{\xi}A=i_{\xi}dA+d(i_{\xi}A)=-df+d(i_{\xi}A),

(A.18)

so that, up to an exact differential,

A\rightarrow A+d\Lambda,\qquad\Lambda=i_{\xi}A-f.

(A.19)

This transformation law is identical to that of a
U(1)
gauge potential,
with
A
playing the role of the connection and
\Omega=dA

its curvature.
This analogy underlies the geometric interpretation of quantization,
where the symplectic form is identified with the curvature of a line bundle.

Darboux’s theorem and canonical coordinates

A fundamental result of symplectic geometry is
Darboux’s theorem
,
which states that in the neighbourhood of any point on
M
,
one can always find local coordinates
(x^{i},p_{i})

such that

\Omega=dp_{i}\wedge dx^{i}.

(A.20)

This means that all symplectic manifolds are locally equivalent;
they possess no local invariants.
The physical content of a Hamiltonian system is therefore encoded
not in the local form of
\Omega
,
but in its global and topological properties.

The proof proceeds by induction.
Starting from a nonconstant function
p_{1}(q)
,
one defines its conjugate
x^{1}
via the flow generated by
p_{1}
,
so that
\{x^{1},p_{1}\}=1
.
Restricting
\Omega
to the submanifold
p_{1}=x^{1}=0

yields a reduced two-form
\Omega^{*}

on a
(2n-2)
-dimensional subspace.
Repeating this process iteratively, one obtains all canonical pairs,
thereby reducing
\Omega
to the canonical Darboux form.

Poisson geometry and quantization

The symplectic and Poisson structures together define the geometric
framework of classical mechanics.
A Poisson manifold generalizes this setting by allowing for degenerate brackets,
which naturally appear in systems with constraints or gauge symmetries.
The physical phase space is then obtained by quotienting out the degenerate directions,
leading to the Dirac bracket formalism.

At the quantum level, the Poisson algebra becomes the algebra of commutators:

\{f,g\}\;\longrightarrow\;\frac{1}{i\hbar}\,[\hat{f},\hat{g}].

(A.21)

This correspondence, the cornerstone of canonical quantization,
shows how the symplectic and Poisson structures provide the classical seeds of quantum mechanics.

A.3
The Moyal
\star
-Product

We closely follow
[
56
,
57
,
58
]
for the Moyal
\star
-Product.

Definition

On
\mathbb{R}^{n}
equipped with a constant antisymmetric matrix
\Theta^{ij}
, the Moyal (or Groenewold–Moyal)
\star
-product between two smooth functions
f,g
is defined by

(f\star g)(x)=f(x)\exp\!\left(\frac{i}{2}\overleftarrow{\partial_{i}}\Theta^{ij}\overrightarrow{\partial_{j}}\right)g(x).

(A.22)

Expanding the exponential gives

(f\star g)(x)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{m!}\left(\frac{i}{2}\right)^{m}\Theta^{i_{1}j_{1}}\cdots\Theta^{i_{m}j_{m}}(\partial_{i_{1}}\cdots\partial_{i_{m}}f)(x)(\partial_{j_{1}}\cdots\partial_{j_{m}}g)(x).

(A.23)

Basic properties

(1) Associativity.

For constant
\Theta^{ij}
, the bidifferential operator

\exp\big(\tfrac{i}{2}\overleftarrow{\partial_i}\Theta^{ij}\overrightarrow{\partial_j}\big)

acts associatively, hence

(f\star g)\star h=f\star(g\star h).

This follows directly from the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formula for constant Poisson structures.

(2) Deformation of the commutative product.

Expanding to second order in
\Theta^{ij}
:

f\star g=fg+\frac{i}{2}\Theta^{ij}\partial_{i}f\,\partial_{j}g-\frac{1}{8}\Theta^{i_{1}j_{1}}\Theta^{i_{2}j_{2}}\partial_{i_{1}}\partial_{i_{2}}f\,\partial_{j_{1}}\partial_{j_{2}}g+\mathcal{O}(\Theta^{3}).

(A.24)

Hence the
\star
-commutator reproduces the Poisson bracket at leading order,

[f,g]_{\star}\equiv f\star g-g\star f=i\,\Theta^{ij}\partial_{i}f\,\partial_{j}g+\mathcal{O}(\Theta^{3}).

(A.25)

(3) Trace property.

For sufficiently decaying functions or periodic boundary conditions,

\int d^{n}x\,(f\star g)=\int d^{n}x\,fg=\int d^{n}x\,(g\star f).

(A.26)

The equality holds because the extra terms in the expansion are total derivatives.

(a) Coordinate products.

For coordinate functions
x^{i}
one finds

x^{i}\star x^{j}=x^{i}x^{j}+\frac{i}{2}\Theta^{ij},\qquad[x^{i},x^{j}]_{\star}=i\Theta^{ij}.

(A.27)

Thus, the
\star
-product realizes the noncommutative coordinate algebra

[x^{i},x^{j}]=i\Theta^{ij}
.

(b) Plane waves.

For
f(x)=e^{ik\cdot x}
and
g(x)=e^{ip\cdot x}
,

e^{ik\cdot x}\star e^{ip\cdot x}=e^{i(k+p)\cdot x}\,e^{-\frac{i}{2}k_{i}\Theta^{ij}p_{j}}.

(A.28)

Hence the
\star
-commutator of plane waves is

[e^{ik\cdot x},e^{ip\cdot x}]_{\star}=2i\,e^{i(k+p)\cdot x}\,\sin\!\Big(\frac{1}{2}k_{i}\Theta^{ij}p_{j}\Big).

(A.29)

(c) Gaussian example.

Let
f(x)=e^{-\frac{1}{2}x^{T}Ax}
and

g(x)=e^{-\frac{1}{2}x^{T}Bx}
, where
A,B
are symmetric.
Using the Fourier representation and the plane-wave composition rule,
one obtains another Gaussian

f\star g(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det\!\left(1+\frac{1}{4}\Theta A\Theta B\right)}}\exp\!\left[-\tfrac{1}{2}x^{T}Cx\right],

(A.30)

where
C
is an explicit symmetric matrix depending on
A,B,\Theta
.
This identity is useful in heat-kernel and propagator computations in noncommutative field theory.

Relation to Weyl quantization

The Moyal
\star
-product is the image of operator multiplication under the Weyl map
W

W(f)\,W(g)=W(f\star g).

(A.31)

In this sense,
\star
-multiplication is the pullback of operator composition to the space of functions, and quantizes the classical Poisson algebra determined by
\Theta^{ij}
.

Connection to the
B
-Field and noncommutative geometry

In open string theory, a constant background
B
-field induces a noncommutative structure on the D-brane worldvolume with

\Theta^{ij}=-(2\pi\alpha^{\prime})^{2}\Big[(g+2\pi\alpha^{\prime}B)^{-1}B(g-2\pi\alpha^{\prime}B)^{-1}\Big]^{ij}.

(A.32)

In the large-
B
limit, this reduces to

\Theta^{ij}\simeq(B^{-1})^{ij}
.
Therefore, the Moyal
\star
-product with parameter
\Theta^{ij}
describes the effective noncommutative geometry of open string endpoints in the presence of a constant
B
-field.

Endpoint commutator

For boundary coordinates
X^{i}(\tau)
, regarded as functions on the D-brane worldvolume,

[X^{i}(\tau),X^{j}(\tau)]_{\star}=X^{i}\star X^{j}-X^{j}\star X^{i}=i\,\Theta^{ij}.

(A.33)

Substituting
\Theta^{ij}
from the Seiberg–Witten relation gives the full dependence on
(g,B,\alpha^{\prime})
.
In the tensionless limit, one recovers
[X^{i},X^{j}]_{\star}=i(B^{-1})^{ij}
,
as discussed in the main text.

Remarks

•

The
\star
-product provides an explicit deformation quantization of the Poisson manifold with structure tensor
\Theta^{ij}
.

•

It preserves associativity and reduces to the usual product in the commutative limit
\Theta^{ij}\to 0
.

•

In string theory, the noncommutative phase factors
e^{-\tfrac{i}{2}k_{i}\Theta^{ij}p_{j}}
appear in open-string amplitudes and account for the mixing between the endpoints induced by the
B
-field background.

Appendix B
Seiberg–Witten derivation of the noncommutative parameter

For completeness, let us discuss the original derivation of the noncommutative parameter

\Theta^{ij}
as given by Seiberg and Witten
[
25
]
.
Their argument is based on the worldsheet operator formulation of the open string
propagating on a D-brane in the presence of a constant metric
g_{ij}

and an antisymmetric
B
-field.
The mixed boundary condition induced by
B_{ij}

leads to a deformation of the equal-time commutator of the boundary coordinates,
which is identified with the spacetime noncommutativity parameter.

B.1
Boundary condition and reflection relation

Consider an open tensile string on the upper half plane (UHP), with the boundary at
\text{Im}(z)=0
, where
z=\tau+i\sigma
, and
\bar{z}=\tau-i\sigma
.
We decompose the embedding coordinates
(x^{i})
into holomorphic and anti-holomorphic parts as following,

x^{i}(z,\bar{z})=X^{i}(z)+\widetilde{X}^{i}(\bar{z}).

(B.1)

The open string boundary condition at
\text{Im{(z)}}=0
reads
[
25
]
,

g_{ij}(\partial-\bar{\partial})x^{j}+2\pi\alpha^{\prime}B_{ij}(\partial+\bar{\partial})x^{j}\Big|_{z=\bar{z}}=0.

(B.2)

This couples the tangential and normal derivatives of
x^{i}
, and hence the holomorphic
and antiholomorphic components of the field.

Evaluated on the boundary
z=\bar{z}=\tau
, the condition becomes

(g+2\pi\alpha^{\prime}B)_{ij}\,\partial X^{j}(\tau)=(g-2\pi\alpha^{\prime}B)_{ij}\,\bar{\partial}\widetilde{X}^{j}(\tau).

(B.3)

Introducing the matrix

M=(g+2\pi\alpha^{\prime}B)^{-1}(g-2\pi\alpha^{\prime}B),

(B.4)

This relation implies that the antiholomorphic coordinate can be written as a reflected image
of the holomorphic one:

\widetilde{X}^{i}(\bar{z})=(M^{-1})^{i}{}_{\,j}\,X^{j}(z^{*}),\qquad z^{*}=\overline{z}.

(B.5)

This ‘reflection rule’ will be used to express the full propagator on the UHP
in terms of holomorphic correlators only.

B.2
Bulk correlators

In the bulk of the UHP, the free correlators are standard:

\displaystyle\langle X^{i}(z)X^{j}(w)\rangle

\displaystyle=-\alpha^{\prime}g^{ij}\ln(z-w),

(B.6)

\displaystyle\langle\widetilde{X}^{i}(\bar{z})\widetilde{X}^{j}(\bar{w})\rangle

\displaystyle=-\alpha^{\prime}g^{ij}\ln(\bar z-\bar w).

(B.7)

Using equation (
B.5
), the full open-string propagator can be decomposed into four terms:

\displaystyle\langle x^{i}(z,\bar{z})\,x^{j}(z^{\prime},\bar{z}^{\prime})\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle X^{i}(z)X^{j}(z^{\prime})\rangle+\langle X^{i}(z)\widetilde{X}^{j}(\bar{z}^{\prime})\rangle

\displaystyle\quad+\langle\widetilde{X}^{i}(\bar{z})X^{j}(z^{\prime})\rangle+\langle\widetilde{X}^{i}(\bar{z})\widetilde{X}^{j}(\bar{z}^{\prime})\rangle.

(B.8)

Each term is evaluated as follows.
The first is the ordinary holomorphic correlator:

\langle X^{i}(z)X^{j}(z^{\prime})\rangle=-\alpha^{\prime}g^{ij}\ln(z-z').

(B.9)

The mixed term
\langle X^{i}(z)\widetilde{X}^{j}(\bar{z}^{\prime})\rangle
gives

\displaystyle\langle X^{i}(z)\widetilde{X}^{j}(\bar{z}^{\prime})\rangle

\displaystyle=(M^{-1})^{j}{}_{\,l}\,\langle X^{i}(z)X^{l}(z^{\prime*})\rangle

\displaystyle=-\alpha^{\prime}\,g^{il}(M^{-1})^{j}{}_{\,l}\,\ln(z-\bar z').

(B.10)

Similarly,

\displaystyle\langle\widetilde{X}^{i}(\bar{z})X^{j}(z^{\prime})\rangle

\displaystyle=(M^{-1})^{i}{}_{\,k}\,\langle X^{k}(z^{*})X^{j}(z^{\prime})\rangle

\displaystyle=-\alpha^{\prime}\,(M^{-1})^{i}{}_{\,k}\,g^{kj}\,\ln(\bar z-z'),

(B.11)

and

\displaystyle\langle\widetilde{X}^{i}(\bar{z})\widetilde{X}^{j}(\bar{z}^{\prime})\rangle

\displaystyle=(M^{-1})^{i}{}_{\,k}(M^{-1})^{j}{}_{\,l}\,\langle X^{k}(z^{*})X^{l}(z^{\prime*})\rangle

\displaystyle=-\alpha^{\prime}\,(M^{-1})^{i}{}_{\,k}\,g^{kl}\,(M^{-1})^{j}{}_{\,l}\,\ln(\bar z-\bar z').

(B.12)

Combining all four contributions, one obtains

\displaystyle\langle x^{i}(z,\bar{z})\,x^{j}(z^{\prime},\bar{z}^{\prime})\rangle

\displaystyle=-\alpha^{\prime}\Big[\,g^{ij}\ln(z-z')+g^{il}(M^{-1})^{j}{}_{\,l}\ln(z-\bar z')

(B.13)

\displaystyle+(M^{-1})^{i}{}_{\,k}\,g^{kj}\,\ln(\bar z-z')+(M^{-1})^{i}{}_{\,k}\,g^{kl}\,(M^{-1})^{j}{}_{\,l}\,\ln(\bar z-\bar z')\Big].

We now define
R=M^{-1}=(g-2\pi\alpha^{\prime}B)^{-1}(g+2\pi\alpha^{\prime}B)
.
Equation (
B.13
) then takes the compact form

\displaystyle\langle x^{i}x^{j}\rangle=-\alpha^{\prime}\Big[g^{ij}\ln(z-z')+g^{ik}R^{j}{}_{\,k}\ln(z-\bar z')+R^{i}{}_{\,k}g^{kj}\ln(\bar z-z')+R^{i}{}_{\,k}g^{kl}R^{j}{}_{\,l}\ln(\bar z-\bar z')\Big].

(B.14)

B.3
Symmetric and antisymmetric parts

Introduce the symmetric and antisymmetric combinations

\displaystyle A^{ij}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\big(g^{ik}R^{j}{}_{\,k}+R^{i}{}_{\,k}g^{kj}\big),

(B.15)

\displaystyle B^{ij}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\big(g^{ik}R^{j}{}_{\,k}-R^{i}{}_{\,k}g^{kj}\big).

(B.16)

The propagator can then be rewritten in a more transparent form:

\displaystyle\langle x^{i}(z)x^{j}(z^{\prime})\rangle

\displaystyle=-\alpha^{\prime}\Big[g^{ij}\ln|z-z^{\prime}|-g^{ij}\ln|z-\bar{z}^{\prime}|

\displaystyle\quad+G^{ij}\ln|z-\bar{z}^{\prime}|^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2\pi\alpha^{\prime}}\,\Theta^{ij}\ln\!\frac{z-\bar{z}^{\prime}}{\bar{z}-z^{\prime}}\Big]+D^{ij},

(B.17)

where

\displaystyle G^{ij}

\displaystyle=A^{ij}=\tfrac{1}{2}\big(g^{ik}R^{j}{}_{\,k}+R^{i}{}_{\,k}g^{kj}\big),

(B.18)

\displaystyle\theta^{ij}

\displaystyle=2\pi\alpha^{\prime}\cdot 2B^{ij}=2\pi\alpha^{\prime}\big(g^{ik}R^{j}{}_{\,k}-R^{i}{}_{\,k}g^{kj}\big).

(B.19)

Inverting the above expressions using

R=(g-2\pi\alpha^{\prime}B)^{-1}(g+2\pi\alpha^{\prime}B)
, one finds

\displaystyle G_{ij}

\displaystyle=g_{ij}-(2\pi\alpha^{\prime})^{2}(Bg^{-1}B)_{ij},

(B.20)

\displaystyle\Theta^{ij}

\displaystyle=2\pi\alpha^{\prime}\Big(\frac{1}{g+2\pi\alpha^{\prime}B}\Big)^{ij}_{\!A},

(B.21)

where the subscript
A
denotes the antisymmetric part.
Here
G_{ij}
is the open-string metric, and
\Theta^{ij}
the antisymmetric
noncommutativity parameter.

B.4
Boundary correlator and equal-time commutator

On the boundary (
z=\tau
and
z^{\prime}=\tau^{\prime}
are real),

\ln\frac{z-\bar{z}^{\prime}}{\bar{z}-z^{\prime}}=i\pi\,\epsilon(\tau-\tau^{\prime}).

(B.22)

The boundary two-point function becomes

\langle x^{i}(\tau)x^{j}(\tau^{\prime})\rangle=-\alpha^{\prime}G^{ij}\ln(\tau-\tau')^{2}+\tfrac{i}{2}\Theta^{ij}\,\epsilon(\tau-\tau^{\prime}).

(B.23)

The antisymmetric piece proportional to
\epsilon(\tau-\tau^{\prime})

defines the equal-time commutator

[x^{i}(\tau),x^{j}(\tau)]=i\,\Theta^{ij},

(B.24)

which expresses the noncommutativity of the open-string end points.

Appendix C
Two-point function in tensionless strings

In the standard tensile string theory, the Polyakov action with a non-degenerate worldsheet metric leads to the familiar logarithmic propagator on the worldsheet
[
42
,
43
,
44
,
45
]
,

\langle X^{i}(z,\bar{z})\,X^{j}(w,\bar{w})\rangle=-\,\frac{\alpha^{\prime}}{2}\,g^{ij}\,\ln|z-w|^{2},

(C.1)

reflecting the two-dimensional nature of the conformal worldsheet with independent left- and right-moving sectors.
In contrast, in the
tensionless limit
(
T\!\to\!0
or
\alpha^{\prime}\!\to\!\infty
),
the worldsheet metric becomes degenerate (Carrollian), and the dynamics reduce effectively to a one-dimensional theory along the worldsheet ‘time’ direction.
As a result, the two-point function becomes linear in the worldsheet time and ultralocal in the spatial coordinate.

C.1
Action and equation for the Green’s function

The free tensionless string action in the gauge
v^{\alpha}=(1,0)
takes the ultralocal form,

S=\frac{1}{2}\int d\tau\,d\sigma\,g_{ij}\,\partial_{\tau}x^{i}\,\partial_{\tau}x^{j},

(C.2)

where
g_{ij}
is the target-space metric.
As the worldsheet metric is degenerate, the action contains only
\tau
-derivatives;
the coordinate
\sigma
acts merely as a label along the string, with no coupling between different
\sigma
-slices.

The quadratic operator associated with (
C.2
) is

\mathcal{D}_{ij}(\tau,\sigma;\tau^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime})=-\,g_{ij}\,\partial_{\tau}^{2}\,\delta(\tau-\tau^{\prime})\,\delta(\sigma-\sigma^{\prime}).

(C.3)

The corresponding Green’s function
G^{ij}(\tau,\sigma;\tau^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime})
satisfies,

\mathcal{D}_{ik}(\tau,\sigma;\tau^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime})\,G^{kj}(\tau^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime};\tau^{\prime\prime},\sigma^{\prime\prime})=\delta_{i}^{\;j}\,\delta(\tau-\tau^{\prime\prime})\,\delta(\sigma-\sigma^{\prime\prime}).

(C.4)

Because
\mathcal{D}
acts only on
\tau
, the solution factorizes as

G^{ij}(\tau,\sigma;\tau^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime})=g^{ij}\,G_{\tau}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})\,\delta(\sigma-\sigma^{\prime}),

(C.5)

where
G_{\tau}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})
satisfies,

-\,\partial_{\tau}^{2}G_{\tau}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})=\delta(\tau-\tau^{\prime}).

(C.6)

C.2
Solution and normalization

Equation (
C.6
) admits the symmetric solution on
\mathbb{R}
,

G_{\tau}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})=-\,\frac{1}{2}\,|\tau-\tau^{\prime}|,

(C.7)

since
\partial_{\tau}^{2}\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}|\tau-\tau^{\prime}|\right)=\delta(\tau-\tau^{\prime})
.
This corresponds to the unique Green’s function that is symmetric under
\tau\leftrightarrow\tau^{\prime}
and vanishes at infinity.
Homogeneous linear terms in
\tau
or
\tau^{\prime}
correspond to zero modes and are fixed by the chosen boundary conditions.

If
\sigma
is periodic,
\sigma\in[0,2\pi]
,
the delta function should be replaced by the periodic distribution

\delta_{2\pi}(\sigma-\sigma^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{in(\sigma-\sigma^{\prime})}.

(C.8)

C.3
The two-point function

Combining the above results, the free two-point function of the embedding coordinates in the tensionless theory is

\langle x^{i}(\tau,\sigma)\,x^{j}(\tau^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime})\rangle=-\,\frac{1}{2}\,g^{ij}\,|\tau-\tau^{\prime}|\,\delta(\sigma-\sigma^{\prime}).

(C.9)

This propagator exhibits the expected ultralocality of the degenerate worldsheet theory:
different
\sigma
-points do not interact, and the correlation is linear (rather than logarithmic) in the worldsheet time separation.

Hence, while the tensile string propagator is logarithmic due to its two-dimensional conformal structure,
the tensionless (Carrollian) limit yields the linear and ultralocal propagator (
C.9
),
signalling the collapse of left- and right-moving modes into a single degenerate worldsheet dynamics.
