Mycobacterium tuberculosis transmission from tuberculosis patients with and without recognized symptoms: a case-contact study in eastern China
Authors: Cheng Chen, Xinsong Hu, C. Robert Horsburgh, Wenjuan Wu, Chi Chen, Yan Shao, Hui Jiang, Zhuping Xu, Jun Jiang, Qiao Liu, Wei Lu, Limei Zhu, Leonardo Martinez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A large number of tuberculosis patients manifest with minimal or no symptoms, and the infectiousness of these individuals is not well characterized. Index cases were consecutively enrolled from the four health centers in Jiangsu Province. Symptoms among index cases were assessed covering the 3 months prior to diagnostic evaluation, in addition to those present at the time of diagnosis. Concurrently, one control community was selected for QuantiFERON screening. Mycobacterium tuberculosis infection was measured through QuantiFERON for all groups. 1,050 contacts of 473 tuberculosis patients, and 560 unexposed controls were included. Asymptomatic tuberculosis among index cases varied from 15% (no symptoms), to 24% (no cough), and 44% (no prolonged cough). The relative risk of QuantiFERON positivity was higher than controls compared to contacts of both asymptomatic (aRR; 2.06; 1.28–3.33) and symptomatic tuberculosis patients (aRR; 2.23; 1.66–3.01). When using alternative QuantiFERON cutoffs, the relative risk was consistently higher among both contact groups compared to controls. When comparing contacts of symptomatic and asymptomatic tuberculosis patients, similar rates of QuantiFERON and ESAT6-CFP10 skin test levels were seen, regardless of the cutoff. This study suggests that tuberculosis patients that are minimally symptomatic or without recognized symptoms may be infectious.

Introduction

Tuberculosis is among the deadliest infectious diseases globally with over 10 million estimated patients occurring globally per year and over 1 million deaths1. The classic historical paradigm for tuberculosis control programs has been to diagnose tuberculosis through clinical presentation to healthcare services or active case finding using symptom-based algorithms2–4. Individuals with symptoms then undergo bacteriological testing and, if positive, are diagnosed and notified. Individuals with asymptomatic tuberculosis are often not diagnosed during routine community screenings using symptom-based approaches5. When patients seek clinical care and undergo tuberculosis diagnostic evaluations based solely on symptom screening, instances of asymptomatic tuberculosis are likely to be overlooked6–8. However, people with asymptomatic tuberculosis contribute a large proportion of tuberculosis with some estimates at around 7 million people5. Despite this, the infectiousness of these individuals to their close social network is not well elucidated.

Few studies have compared Mycobacterium tuberculosis exposure in contacts of individuals with tuberculosis with asymptomatic and symptomatic tuberculosis. An important retrospective study of a mass screening in Vietnam found that household child contacts of asymptomatic, smear positive index cases had greater tuberculin positivity compared to background controls9. A pooled analysis of mass screening studies (including the Vietnam study) merged contact studies from four countries finding statistically similar rates of tuberculin positivity in contacts of asymptomatic versus symptomatic patients10. These studies retrospectively investigated data previously collected and may have missed important opportunities to adjust for potential confounding. Large, observational, case-contact studies with Mycobacterium tuberculosis testing of persons exposed to asymptomatic and symptomatic tuberculosis are not available, but they are needed to further understand infectiousness potential and transmission dynamics.

Through a large household contact study in eastern China, we evaluated the immunoactivity magnitude to Mycobacterium tuberculosis infection (measured through both the QuantiFERON-TB Gold In-Tube [QuantiFERON] and ESAT6-CFP10 tests) in close household contacts of asymptomatic tuberculosis patients defined through different symptom thresholds. Additionally, we compared rates to contacts of tuberculosis patients and matched community controls. We assessed whether participant, index, or household factors modified or impacted differences in Mycobacterium tuberculosis infection status among contacts.

Results

Index tuberculosis patients were identified through both community screening, including individuals with or without tuberculosis-related symptoms, and clinical detection, including self-presentation for care or referral to health centers because of symptoms, abnormal imaging, or other clinical concern. The household contacts of included index cases were enrolled for Mycobacterium tuberculosis infection screening. Meanwhile, one community from one of the four sites was selected for Mycobacterium tuberculosis exposure screening.

The study prospectively enrolled 483 tuberculosis index cases, including 14% from community screening, and 473 patients with validated information of tuberculosis symptoms were included for the final analysis (Fig. 1). Index cases were mostly male (N = 338; 71%), were bacteriologically positive (N = 401; 85%) on Xpert, culture, or smear, and were detected through health-facility diagnoses (85%). All had radiographic X-rays suggestive of tuberculosis, regardless of symptoms. 210 persons with tuberculosis were farmers (44%) while the remaining had a variety of professions (Appendix Table 4). The median age was 57 (interquartile range, 42–71). Approximately 85% of tuberculosis patients had at least one tuberculosis-related symptom. The most common symptom was cough (N = 353; 74%) and most of these had prolonged cough (75% of those with cough). Other symptoms were less prevalent (all <20%) with the most common being weight loss (N = 69; 15%). The number of asymptomatic tuberculosis patients depended sharply on the definition used. When using a ‘no symptoms’ definition, 15% (N = 72) were classified as asymptomatic tuberculosis. Characteristics of index cases were similar. Concurrently, we enrolled 560 randomly selected community controls that were unexposed to tuberculosis. In total, eight household contacts were diagnosed with coprevalent tuberculosis (8/1,071; 0.7%). All contacts with coprevalent tuberculosis were exposed to index cases with symptoms (8/905 [0.9%] versus 0/145 [0%] among contacts exposed to index cases without symptoms) and no patients occurred among controls.

QuantiFERON and ESAT6-CFP10 results comparing community controls and contacts exposed to symptomatic and asymptomatic tuberculosis

Among all groups, 298 (19.9%) participants were QuantiFERON positive using traditional diagnostic cutoff values (i.e., >0.35 IU/mL) at the time of household evaluation. At this cutoff, the proportion testing QuantiFERON positive was lower among unexposed community controls (14%) compared to both contacts of symptomatic tuberculosis patients (23%) and contacts of asymptomatic tuberculosis patients (24%) (Table 3). Using alternative QuantiFERON cutoffs, similar results were seen. For example, at a QuantiFERON cutoff of >4.0 IU/mL, the prevalence of QuantiFERON positivity was 2%, 7%, and 7% among unexposed controls, contacts exposed to asymptomatic tuberculosis, and contacts exposed to symptomatic tuberculosis, respectively. The ESAT6-CFP10 skin test was used only in household contacts and not community controls. Broadly, prevalence was lower for the ESAT6-CFP10 skin test at all cutoffs compared to the QuantiFERON. Despite this, results were largely similar among contacts exposed to symptomatic and asymptomatic tuberculosis. For example, at a ESAT6-CFP10 skin test cutoff of >10 mm, the prevalence of positivity was 14% and 11% among contacts exposed to symptomatic and asymptomatic tuberculosis. Similar differences were seen at other millimeter cutoffs.

In hierarchical regression models with a random effect accounting for household clustering, we first compared QuantiFERON positivity (at >0.35 IU/mL cutoff) among contacts exposed to symptomatic and asymptomatic tuberculosis (using our primary definition of asymptomatic tuberculosis; i.e., no symptoms). The relative risk of QuantiFERON positivity among contacts exposed to symptomatic tuberculosis compared contacts exposed to asymptomatic was null (crude relative risk, 1.00; 95% CI, 0.65–1.54). Additional adjustment for contact, index case, and household characteristics had minimal overall change to the model output (range of adjusted relative risk of 1.07–1.13) (Table 4). When we used the ESAT6-CFP10 skin test (at >10 mm cutoff), the relative risk was 1.36 (95% CI, 0.74–2.50). The relative risk elevated further when adjusting for further variables but remained nonsignificant in all models. For example, when adjusting for contact age, sex, prior tuberculosis and household annual income, the adjusted relative risk reached 1.75 (95% CI, 0.89–3.67). When using alternative definitions of asymptomatic tuberculosis, QuantiFERON positivity remained similar between these two sets of contacts but was statistically elevated when using the ESAT6/CFP10 Discussion

In this prospectively enrolled household contact study of 1,031 individuals with close household exposure to tuberculosis from Eastern China, 24% of close contacts were QuantiFERON positive with no differing rates based on whether the index case was symptomatic or asymptomatic. We found similar rates of QuantiFERON positivity among contacts exposed to symptomatic and asymptomatic tuberculosis regardless of adjustment and restriction of our study sample and when we altered the definition of asymptomatic tuberculosis. These results largely remained when using an alternative diagnostic—the ESAT6/CFP10 skin test. Contacts of both asymptomatic and symptomatic tuberculosis had higher rates of QuantiFERON positivity compared to unexposed controls from the same community. This study suggests that people with tuberculosis that are minimally symptomatic or without symptoms are likely to be infectious and may have similar transmission potential compared to symptomatic tuberculosis patients.

When using either the QuantiFERON or ESAT6-CFP10 skin test, we found statistically similar rates of positivity among contacts exposed to asymptomatic and symptomatic tuberculosis. Our study results could be due to several reasons. First, asymptomatic tuberculosis patients may be similarly infectious compared to those with symptoms. There is empirical evidence suggesting that characteristics of patients, other than typical tuberculosis-related symptoms, are likely to influence infectiousness of tuberculosis patients, including tidal breathing11,12. Second, contact saturation may occur in exposed households. Increased Mycobacterium tuberculosis exposure may not be relevant in a household exposure context13,14. Although asymptomatic tuberculosis patients are likely to be earlier in their disease process compared to symptomatic patients, these persons may still have had disease for a long period of time15. Third, symptoms may be transient and fluctuate over time16. Approximately 19% of index cases without any recognizable symptoms at the household evaluation self-reported a respiratory symptom at some time in the past 3 months; this suggests a lack of symptoms at any one timepoint may have poor specificity to symptom status throughout the disease course and Mycobacterium tuberculosis transmission could have occurred earlier than the baseline visit.

Importantly, we found that contacts of people with both asymptomatic and symptomatic tuberculosis had higher QuantiFERON positivity compared to matched unexposed community controls. This was consistent regardless of the definition of asymptomatic tuberculosis that we used including no tuberculosis-related symptoms at all, chronic cough, or any cough. The common consensus has long been that cough is necessary for Mycobacterium tuberculosis transmission; however, this has been largely doubted in recent years5,17–20. Recent face-mask sampling studies found that exhaled Mycobacterium tuberculosis output was not associated with cough frequency12. The use of a Respiratory Aerosol Sampling Chamber to detect bioaerosols found that tidal breathing may be a significant contributor to transmission among people with tuberculosis11. These results suggest mechanisms other than cough—such as tidal breathing—may also be relevant and/or important components to ongoing Mycobacterium tuberculosis transmission.

There are limitations to this study. First, because we did not base our analysis on QuantiFERON and/or ESAT6/CFP10 conversions, nondifferential misclassification is possible. Second, although some tuberculosis index patients were found through mass screening and routine physical examinations, symptomatic patients were more likely to be found through symptom referral to the health care system. If people with symptomatic tuberculosis found through mass screening are distinct from those that present to healthcare services—for example, those diagnosed through mass screening may be diagnosed earlier in their disease spectrum—this may lead to a differential infectiousness time period to their contacts. This bias, if present, would lead to bias towards the null. Adjusting for method of patient detection (i.e., screening through physical examinations, symptom referral, etc.), we found no changes in our model estimates suggesting likely minimal impact. Third, we did not perform formal chest radiograph scoring to quantify radiographic burden of disease among index cases. Although cavitation was recorded, a more detailed radiographic severity measure may have provided additional information on disease burden and potential infectiousness. Fourth, reliance on symptom reporting is a limitation of any study that investigates asymptomatic tuberculosis5. Misclassification of symptoms may occur for several reasons; for example, stigma or lack of acknowledgement of seriousness of symptoms among people that smoke, and self-reported symptoms might not be adequate for disease state classification21. Furthermore, the prevalence of asymptomatic tuberculosis might be varied in different burden settings22. Fifth, our results may not be applicable to places with a high HIV burden where a large proportion of people with tuberculosis are also living with HIV. Extending this investigation to a higher HIV burden area would be useful to assess the relationship between asymptomatic tuberculosis and infectiousness among people with HIV23. People with HIV and tuberculosis may have differential infectiousness compared with those with tuberculosis but without HIV24–26.

In light of the above, from this large contact study with a corresponding community control, we consistently found that rates of QuantiFERON and/or ESAT6/CFP10 positivity among contacts of asymptomatic and symptomatic tuberculosis were similar amongst each other but considerably higher than community controls. Our analysis was consistent regardless of how we defined asymptomatic tuberculosis, the diagnostic used, and adjustment of potential confounders. These findings add to the growing empirical evidence that persons diagnosed with tuberculosis with minimal or no symptoms may contribute meaningfully to Mycobacterium tuberculosis transmission.

Methods

In this study, we consecutively included tuberculosis patients from four health centers in Jiangsu province, including Kunshan, Liangxi, Danyang, and Zhenjiang. The four study sites had similar socioeconomic status. Index tuberculosis patients, irrespective of tuberculosis symptoms, were included. Index tuberculosis patients were identified through two broad pathways: (1) routine physical examinations and screening, including individuals with or without tuberculosis-related symptoms; and (2) clinical detection, including self-presentation for care or referral to health centers because of symptoms, abnormal imaging, or other clinical concern. The household contacts of included index cases were enrolled for Mycobacterium tuberculosis screening. One community from one of the four sites was selected for Mycobacterium tuberculosis infection screening, and enrolled participants without tuberculosis exposure and had no tuberculosis disease history. All participants in this study (contacts, community controls, and index cases) were enrolled between June 2022 and December 2023 (Fig. 1). Sex was considered in the study design and sex of participants was determined based on the medical record. Our research complies with all relevant ethical regulations; This study was reviewed and approved by the ethics committee of Center for Disease Control and Prevention of Jiangsu Province. All eligible participants signed written informed consent. No compensation was provided for the enrolled participants. Contacts with positive test results from either of the two tests (QuantiFERON or ESAT6-CFP10 skin test) were referred to receive preventive treatment if tuberculosis was excluded after screening.

We evaluated the association between QuantiFERON positivity among three groups: (i) close household contacts of symptomatic tuberculosis index cases; (ii) close household contacts of asymptomatic tuberculosis index cases; and (iii) randomly selected controls from the general population with no household tuberculosis exposure and tuberculosis diagnosis history.

Symptomatic and asymptomatic index tuberculosis definition

Index tuberculosis patients were consecutively enrolled from the four participating health centers. Symptoms among index cases were assessed using a standardized questionnaire covering the 3 months prior to diagnostic evaluation, in addition to symptoms present at the time of diagnosis. Recorded symptoms included any cough, weight loss, night sweats, fever, chest pain, and loss of appetite. For each symptom, we recorded presence, duration, and approximate onset date. We also recorded the reason for evaluation/presentation, including routine physical examination or screening, symptoms, abnormal imaging, clinician referral, or other clinical concern. Sputum smear microscopy, GeneXpert MTB/RIF, and mycobacterial culture were performed for each patient. The questionnaire is provided in the Appendix.

In this study, the definition of asymptomatic tuberculosis was used based on the symptoms of index tuberculosis. We also included two definitions of asymptomatic tuberculosis. These included: (i) no prolonged cough (ie, duration ≥2 weeks), (ii) no cough at all (of any duration). Meanwhile, asymptomatic tuberculosis was defined according to the current WHO recommendation with no symptoms (ie, absence of cough, fever, chest pain, night sweats, and weight loss). We defined asymptomatic tuberculosis as one of these asymptomatic stages for analysis6,8. All index patients, either bacteriological positive or negative, were included to define symptomatic and asymptomatic tuberculosis (appendix Table 1).

Household contacts

For this study, close household contacts were defined as persons residing in the same household as the index who had ≥8 h of exposure per week with the tuberculosis index at any time in the 3 months prior to diagnosis. Exclusion criteria included: (i) suffering from other severe infectious diseases; (ii) pregnancy or breastfeeding; (iii) persons severely immunocompromised (e.g., receiving anti-TNF-α); and (iv) unable to participate because of other health problems (e.g., tumors).

For eligible participants, sociodemographic information was collected through structured sociodemographic and clinical questionnaires by trained interviewers. We collected information on age, sex, height, weight, ethnicity, prior exposure history, bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG) vaccination, sleep in the same bedroom with index tuberculosis (as a measure of Mycobacterium tuberculosis exposure intensity) household annual income, amongst others (the full survey is provided in the Appendix). Chest X-ray films were obtained for all individuals prior to administration of the ESAT6-CFP10 skin test and the QuantiFERON to exclude tuberculosis disease. If chest X-ray results were abnormal, a GeneXpert MTB/RIF test was used for tuberculosis diagnosis according to World Health Organization guidelines. After tuberculosis was excluded, all participants were given each diagnostic test. Blood samples were first collected for the QuantiFERON before administering the ESAT6-CFP10 skin test on the right forearm. The ESAT6-CFP10 skin test was performed using the Mantoux method27,28 and was read 48–72 h after administration in accordance with guidelines. The readers of skin indurations were blinded to the results of the QuantiFERON.

Recruitment and testing of community control participants

To include a control group, we concurrently recruited community control households from one of the four sites in which persons with tuberculosis were recruited. We selected one community from the four health center catchment areas and collected information congruent to that collected from household contacts. Because the number of household contacts substantially exceeded the number of community participants available for matching, we randomly selected 60% of household contacts for the matching step only to define the reference age and sex distribution for the community comparison group. Community participants were then matched to household contacts of index cases by age (±10 years) and sex. This random selection was not used for model fitting or inference; regression analyses were subsequently conducted in the matched analytic dataset.

Participants with current tuberculosis and those with current household tuberculosis exposure were excluded. Sociodemographic information was collected through structured sociodemographic and clinical questionnaires, which were the same as those used for household contacts. After the survey, chest X-rays were obtained for each participant, and QuantiFERON testing was performed for all enrollees.

Diagnostic testing and procedures

Collection, processing, and interpretation of the QuantiFERON-TB Gold In-Tube test (QuantiFERON) test were performed according to the manufacturer’s (QIAGEN) recommendations. Briefly, venous blood was collected in 3 tubes (NIL, Antigen and Mitogen) and incubated at 37 °C for 20 h. After incubation, samples were stored at −20 °C until the enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay was performed, which was within 2 weeks. Interferon-γ levels (international units [IU] per milliliter) were quantified with a 4-point standard curve. QuantiFERON analysis software was used to generate the results. The software performed a quality control assessment of the assay, generated a standard curve, and provided both quantitative (IU per milliliter) and qualitative (positive, negative, or indeterminate) results. The value of 0.35 IU/mL was adopted as the positivity cut-off according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Meanwhile, the lower and upper bounds of a reported uncertainty range—between 0.20 IU/mL and 0.70 IU/mL—were employed29. Other cutoffs included higher thresholds, such as 4.00 IU/mL, as further sensitivity analyses. Higher thresholds were used to evaluate robustness to threshold choice. In addition, higher quantitative IGRA values may represent ‘stronger’ immunoreactivity/higher mycobacterial antigen burden potentially indicating more recent exposure.

The ESAT6-CFP10 skin antigen test, a recombinant reagent mainly including ESAT-6 and CFP-10, was developed by the Zhifei Longcom Biologic Pharmacy Company, China. The ESAT6-CFP10 skin test was approved by the National Medical Products Administration as a standard single test and is widely used in China.

Statistical analysis plan

The prevalence of QuantiFERON positivity among both contacts and controls and ESAT6-CFP10 skin test positivity among household contacts were estimated using standard contingency tables and stratified by potential index case, contact, and household risk factors. We evaluated correlations among variables using polychoric correlation coefficients30, which measure correlation between ordered levels where the latent trait can be considered continuous and normally distributed. The balance of baseline characteristics between contacts and controls was assessed using standardized mean differences. Similarly, balance was measured between contacts of symptomatic and asymptomatic tuberculosis patients was also assessed. Standardized mean differences represent the difference in means or proportions divided by standard error; imbalance was defined as an absolute value greater than 0.20 (small effect size).

We conducted two sets of comparison groupings: (i) three groups including unexposed controls (considered the reference category in these models), contacts of symptomatic tuberculosis, and contacts of asymptomatic tuberculosis patients; (ii) two groups including contacts of asymptomatic tuberculosis (considered the reference category in these models) and contacts of symptomatic tuberculosis patients. For comparison (i) we included only QuantiFERON testing because unexposed controls were only tested with this diagnostic. For comparison (ii) we conducted separate models using either the QuantiFERON or the ESAT6-CFP10 skin test as contacts were administered both tests.

To reduce the pool of candidate risk factors, we performed univariate item analysis on contacts with complete data on all variables. We then built a multivariable regression model that included main effects and interaction terms. We added variables one at a time that were related to QuantiFERON or ESAT6-CFP10 skin test positivity. We used modified Poisson regression with robust standard error variance to conduct all model building. This regression model considers the clustering of household contacts31,32 and allows for direct estimation of relative risks in observational studies33,34. Confidence intervals were considered for statistical significance in all models. Standardized mean differences were used to assess imbalance between secondary characteristics. All the statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.3.1.
