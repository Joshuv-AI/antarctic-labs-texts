Genomic and Untargeted Metabolomic Analysis of Secondary Metabolites in the Streptomyces griseoaurantiacus Strain MH191 Shows Media-Based Dependency for the Production of Bioactive Compounds with Potential Antifungal Activity
Authors: Margaret Ramarajan, Rosangela Devilla, Lachlan Dow, Ned Walsh, Oliver Mead, Mohamed Cassim
Mohamed Zakeel, Marta Gallart, Alan E Richardson, Louise F Thatcher
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractStreptomyces species can form beneficial relationships with hosts as endophytes, including the phytopathogen-inhibiting strain, Streptomyces griseoaurantiacusMH191, isolated from wheat plants. Using genomic characterization and untargeted metabolomics, we explored the capacity of strain MH191 to inhibit a range of fungal phytopathogens through the production of secondary metabolites. Complete genome assembly of strain MH191 predicted 24 biosynthetic gene clusters. Secondary metabolite production was assessed following culture on six different media, with the detection of 205 putative compounds. Members of the manumycin family, undecylprodigiosin, and desferrioxamine were identified as the predominant metabolites. Antifungal activity was validated for undecylprodigiosin and manumycin. These compounds were produced from different BGCs, which showed similarity to asukamycin, undecylprodigiosin, and FW0622 gene clusters, respectively. The growth of strain MH191 on different media illustrated the metabolic regulation of these gene clusters and the strain’s extended chemical potential, with the asukamycin gene cluster alone, producing a variety of antifungal metabolites. The study highlights the extended chemical capability of strain MH191, which could be exploited as a biological control agent for designing future crop protection solutions.Keywords: biocontrol, antifungal, crop protection, genomics, metabolomics

Introduction

Streptomyces belongs to one of the largest bacterial phyla, Actinobacteria, which are Gram-positive, sporulating, filamentous bacteria found most commonly in aquatic and terrestrial environments.1−3 The Streptomyces genus is a source of a wide variety of structurally distinct secondary metabolites with agricultural and pharmaceutical benefits, and more than 7000 bacterially derived bioactive compounds are from this genus.4−6 Subsequently, Streptomyces spp. have received attention for potential use as microbiological control agents (MBCAs) in crop protection, particularly due to their ability to produce a variety of antifungal metabolites that are effective toward a range of plant pathogens.7−9 For example, several species including S. violaceusniger, S. griseus, S. rochei, S. roseoflavus, and S. sampsonii have been tested as MBCAs against a range of fungal phytopathogens including Sclerotinia sclerotiorum, which causes bean white mold and stem rot diseases,10,11Fusarium oxysporum, the causal agent of Fusarium wilt in banana,12 and Botrytis cinerea, the causal agent of tomato gray mold.13 MBCAs could play a crucial role in maintaining and improving crop production as awareness among farmers about the advantages of beneficial microorganisms increases and steps are put into place to ensure the reduction of synthetic pesticides by supplementing or substituting their use with biopesticides.14 Signs of such a change are evident as more MBCA-based biopesticides are being introduced in the market.9,15 Indeed, Streptomyces strains have been developed as commercial products. These include Actinovate AG (Novozymes BioAg Inc.) derived from the Streptomyces lydicus strain WYEC 108, which is effective against pathogens such as powdery mildew, Phytophthora, Fusarium, Rhizoctonia, and Pythium,16 and Rhizovit derived from S. rimosus, which is effective against Pythium, Phytophthora, Botrytis, and Rhizoctonia solani.17

MBCAs have antagonistic activity, which can manifest as the result of one or more modes of action, such as antibiosis, competition, or parasitism.18 Most often, the mode of action of MBCAs is poorly understood. Insight can be garnered from the secondary metabolite potential of these microbes, their biological activity and expression patterns, and can aid in developing application strategies for effective crop management.19 However, not all secondary metabolites are active in all conditions, and their underlying biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) may require specific conditions, stimuli, or heterologous expression to produce active products.9,20

One of the simplest yet effective pleiotropic-based approaches for the expression of BGCs is the alteration of culture conditions. This includes factors such as pH, temperature, media composition, aeration, fermentation method, introduction of enzyme inhibitors, and addition of chemical elicitors, additives, or through coculturing.20,21 For example, Lim et al. investigated the prodigiosin metabolic pathway in S. coelicolor A3(2) by culturing in two different media that were either nutrient-rich or nutrient-restrictive.22 Another study highlighted the significance of carbon and nitrogen sources, pH, and temperature on Streptomyces secondary metabolite production.23 In a One Strain Many Compounds (OSMAC) approach, the metabolome of a marine Streptomyces sp. was investigated when grown on six different media over the course of seven days, enabling the selection of conditions that were most optimal for metabolite production.24

In previous studies, we identified a candidate Streptomyces MCBA strain, MH191, that showed antifungal activity in vitro against a range of wheat and canola fungal pathogens and enhanced defense gene expression in Arabidopsis plants when it was supplied as a pretreatment and the plants were subsequently challenged with the necrotrophic fungal pathogen Sclerotinia sclerotiorum.25−27 To elucidate the strain’s mechanism of action, in this study, we utilized an OSMAC approach coupled with biochemoinformatic analysis to capture its biosynthetic potential and link metabolome components to fungal control.

First, we generated a complete genome assembly to confirm species identity and then predicted biosynthetic gene clusters. Second, when grown on media varying in carbon and nitrogen sources, the strain was subjected to dual-plate competition assays against a variety of fungal phytopathogens to elicit its bioactive potential. Lastly, we collected LC-HRMS data under different media conditions, and the predicted BGCs and their secondary metabolites were used to develop a list of expected compounds for untargeted metabolomics. The most abundant compounds detected in the metabolome of strain MH191 were confirmed using analytical standards, and their antifungal properties were substantiated. This biochemoinformatic investigation lays the foundation to build MH191 into an effective MBCA for crop health management.

Materials and Methods

Microbial Strains and Culture Conditions

The bacterial isolate used in this study was Streptomyces griseoaurantiacus strain MH191, isolated from the roots of field-grown wheat plants from Western Australia, as outlined by O’Sullivan et al.27 The strain was routinely maintained on half-strength potato dextrose agar (1/2 PDA) plates at 28 °C and stored as spore-stocks at −80 °C. For in vitro fungal suppression assays and metabolite extractions, 20 μL of the MH191 spore culture (∼108 spores/mL) were plated onto different media that included: (i) mannitol soya flour (SFM) medium (20 g/L mannitol and 20 g/L soya flour),28 (ii) International Streptomyces Project-2 (ISP2) medium (4 g/L yeast extract, 10 g/L malt extract, and 4 g/L dextrose) (BD, USA), (iii) International Streptomyces Project-3 (ISP3) medium (20 g/L white oats and 1 mL/L of trace salt solution), (iv) 1/2 PDA (2 g/L potato extract and 10 g/L dextrose) (BD, USA), (v) yeast extract-starch (YES) medium (2 g/L yeast extract and 10 g/L starch),29 and vi) yeast-malt extract (YME) medium (4 g/L yeast extract, 10 g/L malt extract, and 4 g/L dextrose).30 In all cases, cultures were incubated at 28 °C for up to 10 days.

Plant pathogenic fungal strains used in this study were Verticillium dahliae isolate Mirrabooka PP342 (from Gossypium sp.), Ascochyta rabiei isolate 17CUR007 (from Cicer arietinum L.), Leptosphaeria maculans isolate D20 (from Brassica napus), Sclerotinia sclerotiorum (from Brassica napus), Rhizoctonia solani AG8 (from Triticum aestivum),31Fusarium oxysporum Fo5176 (from Brassica oleracea),32 and a plant beneficial fungus Trichoderma harzianum Tr906 (Triticum sp.).33 An enhanced green-fluorescent protein (eGFP)-tagged strain of Verticillium dahliae (isolate BRIP 71171) was also used.34Verticillium, Rhizoctonia, Fusarium, and Sclerotinia were maintained in 1/2PDA plates at room temperature in darkness, whereas Ascochyta, Leptosphaeria, and Trichoderma were maintained in 1/2 PDA plates at room temperature in ambient light.

In Vitro Fungal Suppression Assay

Solid media plates: strain MH191 was grown on 1/2 PDA medium and incubated at 28 °C for 2 weeks as described above and challenged with fungal phytopathogens as described in detail by O’Sullivan et al. (2021). In brief, a single agar plug from six different plant pathogenic fungi and one plant beneficial fungus were placed on the opposing side of the Streptomyces strain. Each assay was performed in quadruplicate, and plates containing agar plugs without the Streptomyces strain were used as controls. The plates were incubated for 7–60 days depending on the growth of the phytopathogen, at room temperature under ambient light or dark depending on the phytopathogen. The plates were scored by measuring the distance of the fungal growth from the plug in both control (n = 2) and test plates (n = 4) using ImageJ.35 Percentage inhibition of each fungal species was calculated using the following equation, where Dc and Dt stand for the distances of fungal growth in the control and test (with MH191) plates.

Microtiter plate liquid bioactivity assays: Plates were set up in 96-well flat bottom plates using a 2-fold serial dilution of metabolites or standard compounds, where 50 μL of sample in each well was air-dried and then resuspended in 100 μL of ultrapure water containing 5% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). Commercial standards of undecylprodigiosin and manumycin were dissolved in DMSO for testing. Fifty microliters of a Verticillium dahliae spore suspension (∼105 spores/mL) constitutively expressing eGFP were added to each well.34 The growth of the fungal spores was subsequently observed by brightfield and eGFP fluorescence over a period of 24 to 48 h using a BioTek Cytation 1 cell imaging multimode reader (Agilent, USA) as described by Sabburg et al.34

Genome Sequencing and Genome Mining for Secondary Metabolism

Genomic DNA was isolated from a liquid culture of strain MH191 grown in 1/2 PDB. Biomass was harvested via centrifugation during the logarithmic growth phase. DNA was extracted using the Wizard Genomic DNA purification kit (Promega, USA) and sequenced using PacBio HiFi Long-read technology (AGRF, Australia). Reads were assembled using CANU (2.2.0) with PacBio HiFi settings and an estimated genome size of 8 Mb.36 Contamination was assessed via basic local alignment tool (BLAST) comparisons between contigs and the NCBI database with respective e-value and identity thresholds of 1 × 10–5 and 75%.37 The completeness of the genome assembly was estimated by analyzing the Streptomycetales data set (odb10) of Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Orthologs (BUSCO v5.2.2).38 Biosynthetic gene clusters of strain MH191 were detected and analyzed using antiSMASH version 7.1.0:39,40https://antismash.secondarymetabolites.org/#!/start.

Phylogenetic Analysis of Strain MH191

20 Streptomyces reference genomes were selected and retrieved from the NCBI database, guided by a genus-wide phylogenetic study done by Labeda et al.41 The pairwise average nucleotide identity (ANI) was calculated between these genomes and the MH191 PacBio genome sequence using the FastANI tool.42 A distance matrix was constructed using the inverse of the ANI values (100 – ANI%) within the RStudio package.43 To elucidate the evolutionary relationships among these genomes, a bootstrapped hierarchical analysis comprising 1000 bootstrap replicates was conducted using pvclust.44 The resulting phylogenetic tree was visualized using ggtree,45 with approximately unbiased (AU) values reported for each branch. The scripts used for executing this analytical pipeline are publicly accessible via the GitHub repository: https://github.com/nedwalsh/fastANI-phylogenetic_pipeline.

Chemicals and Materials

All solvents used for metabolite extraction were Optima or Pierce LC-MS grade (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Analytical standards – undecylprodigiosin hydrochloride from Streptomyces parvulus (Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany) and Manumycin A from Streptomyces sp. (Bioaustralis Fine Chemicals, Australia) – were of high purity (>95%) and used for identity confirmation. Ampicillin sodium salt (Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany) was used as an internal standard.

MH191 Metabolome Analysis

Metabolite Extraction from Agar Plates and Liquid Culture

A modified microscale extraction procedure was used to extract the metabolites from agar plates.46 Solid media experiments were performed with six replicates per medium (six plates: uninoculated medium control; six plates: medium inoculated with MH191). Extracts were prepared by cutting three plugs of 6 mm diameter from the culture plates using a cork drill, along with uncultured plates as a control. The plugs were transferred to a 2 mL microcentrifuge tube and 1 mL of a solvent mixture of methanol–ethyl acetate (1:3) containing 1% (v/v) formic acid was added. Ampicillin was added at 0.2 mg/mL of solvent mixture as the internal standard. The plugs were extracted ultrasonically for 60 min, at 80 kHz and 330 W in an Elmasonic P 120H sonicator (Elma Schmidbauer GmbH, Germany). The extract was transferred to a clean microcentrifuge tube, and the organic phase was evaporated to dryness under a gentle stream of nitrogen in a TurboVap Classic LV automated evaporation system (Biotage, Sweden). Samples were resuspended in 400 μL of methanol containing 0.6% (v/v) formic acid and 2.5% (v/v) ultrapure water. All samples were filtered through a 0.2-μm centrifuge filter PTFE (Micro Science, Australia) and, after dilution (1:10) with methanol, they were transferred into clean 2 mL amber vials with an insert (Agilent Technologies, USA) for liquid chromatography high-resolution mass-spectrometry (LC-HRMS) analysis.

Solid-phase extraction (SPE) from strain MH191 was conducted using 20 mL of liquid culture (ISP2 and YES) in triplicate by centrifugation at 5000 g for 15 min and the supernatant was filtered through a 33 mm poly(ether sulfone) Millex-GP syringe filter unit (0.22 μm) (Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany). An Agilent Bond Elut PLEXA (200 mg, 3 mL) column was used to concentrate the metabolites using the Vac Elut-20 system (Agilent Technologies, USA). Samples were eluted using 2 mL of methanol (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and the eluate was transferred to a clean microfuge tube, evaporated to dryness, resuspended, and filtered as outlined above. Diluted samples (1:20) were similarly prepared for LC-HRMS analysis or transferred (100 μL) to Corning Costar 96-well, cell culture-treated flat-bottom microplates for the bioactivity assay.

Untargeted Metabolite Analysis

The extracted metabolites were analyzed using an LC-HRMS untargeted approach with data-dependent acquisition (DDA) mode. A Q-Exactive (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with a heated electrospray ionization (HESI) source coupled with a Vanquish ultrahigh performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) system was used to analyze the samples. The extracted metabolite (2 μL) was injected on a ZORBAX Eclipse Plus C18 Rapid Resolution HD (1.8 μm particle size, 2.1 × 150 mm Agilent Technologies, USA) column coupled to a ZORBAX Eclipse Plus C18 guard (1.8 μm particle size, 2.1 × 5 mm Agilent Technologies, USA) column for chromatographic separation. The column was kept at 30 °C with a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min. The elution gradient was performed using mobile phase A containing 0.1% formic acid in water and mobile phase B containing 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile. The initial conditions were set at 5% B for 1 min, increasing to 80% over 16 min and then to 100% B over a further 3 min and holding for 4 min. The gradient was returned to the initial conditions in 0.5 min and remained for 4.5 min at 5% B to allow column equilibration before the next injection. Samples were analyzed in positive ionization mode with HESI source parameters set to 3.5 kV spray voltage, S-lens RF of 50, inlet capillary temperature of 256 °C, sheath gas flow rate of 48 arbitrary units (AU), auxiliary gas flow rate of 11 AU, sweep gas flow rate of 1 AU, and auxiliary gas temperature of 413 °C. The MS1 scan was acquired at a m/z range of 80–1200 at a resolution of 35 000, the automatic gain control (AGC) target was set at 3 × 106, and maximum injection time at 200 ms. The DDA method included 6 MS/MS spectra (Top N) per MS scan for the most abundant ions, AGC target of 1 × 106, IT of 100 ms, and dynamic exclusion of 4 s. A normalized collision energy of 30 eV was used for high-energy collision-induced dissociation and a default charge state of 1.

Metabolomic Data Processing

Mass spectrometry data acquired (.raw files) were input into Compound Discoverer 3.3. software (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) for processing and visualization. For the experimental design, the study factors were defined as the sample type, and their values were assigned to all input files in the batch. Blank and quality control values were not assigned and described as n/a. A modified metabolomics data processing workflow included steps such as selecting spectra, aligning retention times (to align chromatographic peaks in all input files to a single pooled QC file), detecting compounds (with a precursor mass tolerance of ≤5 ppm, minimum peak intensity of 500 000, at a peak detection of 1.5 S/N signal-to-noise threshold), grouping compounds (with a mass tolerance of 5 ppm, and retention time tolerance of <0.1 min), filling gaps (with a mass tolerance of 5 ppm and an S/N threshold of 1.5) followed by SERRF QC correction,47 and marking background compounds. Identification of peaks was conducted using mzCloud (compound classes: endogenous metabolites, natural products, and small molecules), mzVault (endogenous metabolites spectral library – autoprocessed and reference), MassList, (Mass Bank48 and NPAtlas 2.049), ChemSpider as selected database searches at 5 ppm search. The molecular network node was added to the workflow to group the related compounds into molecular families based on their MS2 spectra and molecular formula. The workflow also included an expected compounds list (with the list of compounds generated using antiSMASH39 predicted BGC products). The peak visualization result included peak RT, compound’s calculated molecular weight, mass-to-charge ratio (m/z), peak area, and normalized peak area.

Statistical Analysis

The relative abundance (peak area) of secondary metabolites, represented by annotated features, across all six media types was assessed using a combination of statistical techniques. First, one way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) classified the significant metabolites using Tukey’s honestly significant difference (Tukey’s HSD) posthoc test with a minimum threshold p-value ≤ 0.05. To explore the overall patterns and inherent structure within the data set, a principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted using an unsupervised method. Additionally, to discriminate the influence of media dependency on the relative levels of metabolite production, a partial least squares-discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) was performed. All the above analyses were done using MetaboAnalyst 6.0 software:50https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/faces/ModuleView.xhtml.

In dual-competition bioassays, the inhibition percentage of each fungal pathogen against MH191, measured across three distinct media types (1/2 PDA, ISP3, and SFM), was analyzed using a two-way ANOVA. Mean separation was conducted using the Tukey’s HSD test. All analyses, unless mentioned otherwise, were performed using the RStudio statistical software platform.43

Results

Genome Sequence of Streptomyces Strain MH191 Indicates Broad and Diverse Metabolite Potential

To initiate a biochemoinformatic study of Streptomyces strain MH191, we first generated a complete genome assembly, resulting in one chromosome of 7.1 Mb and a single plasmid of length 99.1 kb, with an average GC content of 73.6% (deposited in the GenBank accession number: PRJNA369718). Genome benchmarking using BUSCO returned a completeness score of 99.1% (comprising 1564 complete orthologs, 2 fragmented orthologs, and 13 missing). This assembly was based on ∼73× coverage and is consistent with that previously reported by Belt et al. (2024) using short-read Illumina sequencing across 296 scaffolds. The whole genome-based taxonomic characterization of strain MH191 using pairwise ANI calculations with NCBI reference genomes confirmed it as Streptomyces griseoaurantiacus (>99% similarity) (Figure S1), corroborating earlier 16S rDNA-based molecular phylogenetic analysis.27

The potential secondary metabolism of strain MH191 was assessed using genome mining software antiSMASH, from which 24 putative secondary metabolite (biosynthetic) gene clusters (BGCs) were detected (Table 1). Of these, eight were identified as polyketide gene clusters (Types I, II, and III), five as nonribosomal peptide synthetase (NRPS) gene clusters or NRPS-like gene clusters, RiPP gene clusters, six as terpene synthase gene clusters, and three as siderophore gene clusters, with the remainder being associated with ectoine or melanin synthesis pathways. Nine of the predicted BGCs showed higher than 80% similarity to clusters that are listed in the MIBiG database, which provides standards for annotation on BGCs and their metabolites,51 five showed moderate similarity of greater than 25%, and the rest of the BGCs (ten) were found to have low similarity of less than 25% (Table 1). Combined, these predicted biosynthetic gene clusters accounted for approximately 816 kbp, which is approximately 11% of the total bacterial genome.

Biosynthetic gene cluster number 2 was of particular interest due to its unusual assignment as furan. The core biosynthetic gene identified by antiSMASH was annotated to belong to a protein family of oxidoreductases, suggested that the resulting metabolite was responsible for the formation of an epoxide group rather than furan. This assignment was supported by the gene (and the BGC in general) bearing a similarity to the known asukamycin cluster (BGC0000187, MIBiG repository). Further evidence of this putative assignment was supported by the identification of a three-gene subcluster predicted to synthesize 2-amino-3-hydroxycyclopent-2-enone, which is a characteristic moiety of both asukamycin and manumycin. Isolates of S. griseoaurantiacus are known to produce manumycin,52−54 leading us to hypothesize that this BGC is associated with the production of an epoxyquinone polyene manumycin such as manumycin A. Antifungal and insecticidal properties of manumycin A have previously been well established.55−57 This BGC may also produce other manumycin-type antibiotics, such as SW-B, that have antifungal activity against a range of fungal phytopathogens.58

Biosynthetic gene cluster number 11 showed 95% similarity to an undecylprodigiosin gene cluster of Streptomyces coelicolor strain A3(2) that is capable of producing prodigiosins such as undecylprodigiosin and metacycloprodigiosin. The 47 kb BGC comprising 36 genes is suspected to produce the red-pigmented pyrrole antibiotic, undecylprodigiosin, with known antifungal activity.59,60 This correlates with our observation that strain MH191 shows distinct red pigmentation on media such as ISP2 and 1/2 PDA (Figure 2).

Other notable BGCs with high similarity to known clusters included gene cluster number 15, which showed similarity with the desferrioxamine E BGC (BGC0001478, MIBiG repository) and codes for a suite of siderophores.61 Gene clusters (numbers 5, 7, 16, and 18) coding for the terpenes hopene and geosmin, and for melanin and ectoine, respectively, (Table 1) have been shown to be common traits shared by a wide range of Streptomyces species,62 while the remaining predicted gene clusters represent hypotheticals or unknowns, sharing less than 50% similarity to known clusters.

Streptomyces Strain MH191 Exhibits Suppressive Activity Against a Broad Range of Fungal Pathogens In Vitro, Which is Influenced by Media Composition

In vitro dual-competition results revealed a significant interaction (p < 0.001) between the media type and the fungal species being suppressed by the strain MH191 (Figure 1 and Table S1). Compared to the control, the MH191 strain exhibited moderate to strong antifungal activity (30–65% growth inhibition) against all fungal species tested in this study, except Trichoderma (<20% growth inhibition). Varying extent of growth inhibition (antifungal activity) was also evident across different media, especially for Sclerotinia where there was significantly greater inhibition when grown on 1/2 PDA and ISP3 media (Figure 1a). The significant reduction in antifungal activity observed against Sclerotinia on SFM media plates might be because SFM encouraged sporulation, as has been shown for other strains of Streptomyces.63,64 Interestingly, when tested on ISP3 media, strain MH191 did not produce any characteristic red pigmentation that has previously been associated with the presence of undecylprodigiosin65 and as compared to growth on ISP2 media (Figure S2). This suggests that undecylprodigiosin is likely not the only metabolite responsible for the antifungal activity in strain MH191.

Targeted Evaluation of Undecylprodigiosin and Manumycin for Phytopathogen Antifungal Activity

Undecylprodigiosin and manumycin were selected as two candidate antifungal secondary metabolites potentially encoded in the genome of strain MH191. To evaluate and confirm their antifungal activity, they were assessed in a sensitive image-based bioassay incorporating a GFP-tagged Verticillium fungal strain that enables inhibition against both spore germination and mycelial growth and development to be monitored.34 After 24 h and up to 68 h of coincubation, undecylprodigiosin and manumycin A (analytical standards) impaired the germination of Verticillium spores at concentrations down to 51.25 μg/mL and 50 μg/mL of diluted samples, respectively (Figure 2). The IC50 of undecylprodigiosin and manumycin A was found to be 58.98 μg/mL (using a log absolute IC50 range of 1.730 to 1.813) and 19.49 μg/mL (using a log absolute IC50 range of 1.263 to 1.315), respectively, based on the relative growth of the Verticillium when plotted against the logarithmic concentration of the analytical standards (Table S2 andFigures S3 and S4). Meschke et al. found that the application of purified undecylprodigiosin (50 μg) reduces the microsclerotia formation of V. dahliae.59

Variable Levels of Secondary Metabolites via an OSMAC Approach

To ascertain if S. griseoaurantiacus strain MH191 produced the antifungals undecylprodigiosin or manumycin, or other compounds contributing to the strain’s antifungal activity (Figure 1), an OSMAC approach was undertaken using solid nutrient media that varied in carbon and nitrogen sources (ISP2, ISP3, 1/2 PDB, YES, SFM, and YME) along with two examples of liquid (ISP2 and YES) media (Table S3). Other media, such as supplemented minimal medium,66 that yielded slow or insufficient growth were excluded from further analyses. A total of 3305 and 1669 features (putative compounds) were detected from solid and liquid media, respectively. The features without annotation were filtered out, and the features present in media controls were removed. This resulted in 205 and 252 features that were predicted to be unique putative secondary metabolites from strain MH191 extracted from solid and liquid media, respectively. The peak areas of the background-subtracted and filtered features are represented in a heatmap (Figures 5 and S5), which shows the relative abundance of these annotated putative secondary metabolites present in different solid media. The most abundant metabolites of interest were undecylprodigiosin and SW-B, both of which have previously been reported to have antimicrobial activity (Table 2). Undecylprodigiosin production was observed in all media, except ISP3. Even though the production of this compound was not observed in SFM under the tested laboratory conditions, its production in this medium had previously been evident in pilot experiments (data not shown). Likewise, SW-B production was evident in all media that were tested. The diverse types of manumycins were produced in all media, with the lowest abundance observed in SFM. On the other hand, compounds produced by strain MH191 that were unique to SFM and not found in other media were observed, such as putative sarmentine (Figures 5 and S22), which has been previously reported to have herbicidal properties, and bipolamide B (Figures 5 and S23), which has antifungal activity (Table 2).

These putative metabolite profiles of strain MH191 were used to build a molecular network based on compound similarity using the Compound Discoverer molecular network node. The putative compounds from solid media grouped into two distinct molecular families: one based on manumycin-type derivatives with 34 putative compounds, and the other, the SW-B molecular family, with 46 putative compounds (Figure 6). The integrity of the manumycin molecular family was verified with an analytical standard of manumycin A (Figure S8). Interestingly, there was no evidence of any grouping around undecylprodigiosin, which was confirmed using an analytical standard (Figure S6). Molecular network analysis was also conducted with the liquid spent media (ISP2 and YES). This analysis generated two large and three small molecular families (Figure S24). The two largest molecular families contained SW-B and manumycin, along with 34 and 14 other compounds, respectively. Additional molecular families were identified as pseudonocardian A (with seven compounds) and desferrioxamine-like compounds (with six compounds), which presumably resulted from the NRPS-independent siderophore BGC (number 15) that closely matched the desferrioxamine gene cluster (BGC0001478) (Table 1).

Discussion

Microbiological control agents (MBCAs) as a tool for the control of crop diseases are an avenue of research with continued interest. Research in this field is especially pertinent when considering the growing need for novel fungicide treatments, as fungal pathogens worldwide are becoming increasingly resistant to current treatments.129 However, current research into MBCAs often lacks physiological, molecular, and metabolic information regarding their bioactivity. Here, we set out to identify the key genetic elements underlying antifungal activity from a candidate Streptomyces MBCA, combining genome sequencing and genome mining with metabolomic workflows to elucidate the scope of antifungal metabolites produced. The results of this research showed that Streptomyces strain MH191 is a candidate producer of antimicrobial compounds, which could be linked to several key biosynthetic gene clusters.

The complete genome sequence of strain MH191 was assembled from long-read sequencing and subsequently identified as a member of the Streptomyces griseoaurantiacus species clade. This species is of particular interest regarding a wide range of research directions, owing to its secondary metabolism. For example, manumycin and the related asukamycin, both of which were produced by Streptomyces strain MH191, are the subject of ongoing anticancer studies.130 Therefore, along with the focus of this research on controlling crop disease, it is expected that these genomic and metabolomic data will guide future studies on the bioactivities or bioproduction of individual manumycin congeners. Genome mining revealed a diverse array of BGCs concomitant with the phylogeny of this organism. A total of 24 putative secondary metabolite clusters were predicted. Two clusters of particular interest were the predicted furan cluster and prodigiosin cluster, which were putatively assigned as producing a 2-amino-3-hydroxycyclopent-2-enone containing secondary metabolites such as asukamycin and a tripyrrole red-pigmented antibiotic (((5′Z)-4′-methoxy-5′-[(5-undecyl-1H-pyrrol-2-yl)methylene]-1H,5′H-2,2′-bipyrrole) (Table 1). The asukamycin BGC appears to belong to a gene cluster family, producing diverse epoxyquinone-containing secondary metabolites, as shown by Castro-Falcón et al.131 and the 47 kb prodigiosin BGC appears to produce a tripyrrole metabolite undecylprodigiosin.54,60,132

To analyze the biosynthetic products of these clusters, the metabolome of MH191 was analyzed using an untargeted LC-HRMS approach. This methodology successfully identified an array of secondary metabolites, including manumycin and asukamycin, along with several other compounds that derive from the same gene cluster. The relative concentrations of secondary metabolites produced under different conditions were also acquired, which indicated that the metabolic product of this BGC can be modified with changes to culture conditions. This work highlights the potential of a single gene cluster to produce a family of compounds.

Six nutritionally rich media were tested for enhancing the metabolite production of strain MH191, as media components such as carbon and nitrogen play critical roles in the regulation of secondary metabolite production.133,134 For example, diverse types of manumycins were produced in all media, but the lowest abundance was observed in SFM and YES media that lack glucose. In media rich in glucose, such as ISP2 and YME, the cultures of MH191 turned red (an indication of the production of undecylprodigiosin) and were media eliciting the highest undecylprodigiosin production. On the other hand, no undecylprodigiosin was evident in ISP3 media, comprised of oatmeal extract and trace salts (Figures 5 and S2). We speculate that the presence of some media component or lack thereof led to the inhibition of undecylprodigiosin in this medium. As inferred from the dual-competition assay, the absence of undecylprodigiosin in the ISP3 plates did not significantly influence the antifungal potential of strain MH191, indicating the presence of other antifungal compounds in the metabolite cocktail that can effectively suppress the phytopathogens tested. Manipulation of media state was also very informative, where liquid media shed light on the production of siderophores135,136 (desferrioxamine B, D2, and X2) (Figure S5). In particular, desferrioxamine B was not detectable in the various solid media tested but was the most abundant secondary metabolite detected in liquid media, with relative abundance higher than undecylprodigiosin and members of the manumycin family.

While the antifungal and cytotoxic properties of the tripyrrole compound undecylprodigiosin have been reported,59,75 where the three-pyrrole ring could be part of the pharmacophore, and the side chain could influence cytotoxicity,74 we found manumycin to have fungicidal and fungistatic properties (identified in one replicate experiment), which to our knowledge is the first time this has been reported. Previous studies into manumycin and the related compounds asukamycin and alisamycin identified only weak fungicidal or bactericidal properties55,137−139 and cytotoxic effects by inhibiting farnesyl transferase.140,141 This study showed that manumycin inhibits spore germination, acting as a fungicide, which was demonstrated by the cell-imaging bioassay that we utilized. It is also possible that the fungicidal/fungistatic properties of manumycin are synergistic with other antifungal compounds produced by S. griseoaurantiacus strain MH191 or other microbes in the environment.

Untargeted metabolomics identified that a range of manumycin congeners were produced simultaneously. This suggests that S. griseoaurantiacus is a promising biocontrol agent, which can inhibit fungal growth using a suite of antimicrobials, including volatile compounds described in other research with strain MH19125. This multipronged mode of action is hypothesized to improve the durability of MBCAs, prolonging their usefulness in the field.15 Furthermore, of the features that were identified from MH191 extracts after noise filtering, only 10% could be assigned annotations with a high level of confidence. This highlights the potential to discover further bioactive compounds produced by strain MH191, but also the limitations of current chemical libraries to provide annotations.

Epoxyquinoid compounds like manumycin regularly possess tantalizing antimicrobial properties142,143 and therefore stand as promising metabolites, mediating biological control. On the other hand, prodigiosins are photosensitive and degrade rapidly when liberated from cells (such as during excretion or extraction), and thus, it is hard to propose a role for this molecule when excreted, rather than as an endometabolite, in protecting strain MH191 from predation.

Testing against different fungi showed that the antifungal activity of MH191 was far more pronounced against plant pathogens, even though they belong to different phyla, in contrast to the mutualist species Trichoderma. It is unclear why Trichoderma is not affected. While one could speculate that, from an ecological perspective, strain MH191 has evolved to inhibit plant pathogens while promoting other plant-beneficial organisms, further studies on how MH191 impacts beneficial organisms and indigenous microbial communities are required. It should be acknowledged that for MH191 to be developed as a biocontrol strain for commercial use, there will need to be more pathogenicity testing against a wider selection of known beneficial fungi and bacterial strains, for example, other Trichoderma, Mycorrhizae, and inoculant bacteria such as Rhizobia. Presently, we tested strain MH191 against one beneficial fungus only, and it showed no significant effect on its growth. However, further work with broader screening is needed.

This study utilized a range of targeted bioassays, genome mining, and untargeted metabolomics to elucidate the biochemistry of a potential microbiological control agent. In doing so, we illustrate that the antifungal properties of biocontrol agents are tailored to certain pathogens, suggesting that their use in the field may leave native beneficial microbiota more intact compared to synthetic antifungal compounds. The secondary metabolite manumycin was shown to possess antifungal activity, potentially ascribing an ecological function to this metabolite. Finally, we illustrate how these antifungal properties are mediated by a suite of compounds, which may be a more durable alternative to the use of single broad-range fungicides.
