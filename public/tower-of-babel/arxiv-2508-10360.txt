A dataset and model for recognition of audiologically relevant environments for hearing aids: AHEAD-DS and YAMNet+
Henry Zhong, Jörg M. Buchholz, Julian Maclaren, Simon Carlile, Richard Lyon

Abstract
Scene recognition of audiologically relevant environments is important for hearing aids; however, it is challenging, in part because of the limitations of existing datasets. Datasets often lack public accessibility, completeness, or audiologically relevant labels, hindering systematic comparison of machine learning models. Deploying these models on resource-constrained edge devices presents another challenge. Our solution is two-fold: we leverage several open source datasets to create AHEAD-DS, a dataset designed for scene recognition of audiologically relevant environments, and introduce YAMNet+, a sound recognition model. AHEAD-DS aims to provide a standardised, publicly available dataset with consistent labels relevant to hearing aids, facilitating model comparison. YAMNet+ is designed for deployment on edge devices like smartphones connected to hearing devices, such as hearing aids and wireless earphones with hearing aid functionality; serving as a baseline model for sound-based scene recognition. YAMNet+ achieved a mean average precision of 0.83 and accuracy of 0.93 on the testing set of AHEAD-DS across fourteen categories of audiologically relevant environments. We found that applying transfer learning from the pretrained YAMNet model was essential. We demonstrated real-time sound-based scene recognition capabilities on edge devices by deploying YAMNet+ to an Android smartphone. Even with a Google Pixel 3 (a phone with modest specifications, released in 2018), the model processes audio with approximately 50ms of latency to load the model, and an approximate linear increase of 30ms per 1 second of audio. Our website and code https://github.com/Australian-Future-Hearing-Initiative .

Introduction

State of the art hearing devices incorporate scene recognition in order to identify audiological environments and apply appropriate processing (filters, amplification, beam forming, etc) to improve a user’s hearing experience
[
3
]
.

Several machine learning (ML) models have been proposed for scene recognition
[
24
]
. However, there are several limitations: ML models are challenging to port to edge devices
[
4
]
and datasets have inconsistent labels (As outlined in Table
1
).

It is important that models can run on edge devices to preserve privacy by avoiding the transmission of sensitive data and to minimise the cost of data transmission. However, not all models are immediately deployable to devices, such as hearing aids and wireless earphones with hearing aid functionality (henceforth will be collectively referred to as hearing devices), or to portable devices such as smartphones that operate in tandem with hearing devices. Resource constraints and data format compatibility must be overcome for model deployment on the edge
[
23
]
.

Models are trained and tested using datasets with different scene labels and are thus not directly comparable. These datasets are either not public or incomplete, and general-purpose sound datasets do not have labels that are necessarily relevant to hearing devices
[
15
]
.

We were motivated to address these problems for several reasons: There is a gap in publicly available standardised datasets for scene recognition of audiologically relevant environments for hearing devices, and an open-source baseline ML model designed for edge devices is currently unavailable. Our ultimate goal is to improve the hearing experience for users of hearing devices. This paper makes the following contributions:

•

A description of a sound dataset named
Another HEaring AiD scenes Data Set
(AHEAD-DS). This dataset was created for the purpose of scene recognition of audiologically relevant environments for hearing devices, which allows comparison of models using a standardised dataset with consistent labels. The dataset is released under a permissive licence, in a ready-to-use state, divided into training/validation/testing sets and annotated with the ground truth.

•

A description of a ML model called YAMNet+ for sound based scene recognition. The model can act as a baseline model for further development of sound based scene recognition models. The model is released under a permissive licence, including model definitions, training, testing code, and code to convert it into a format deployable to edge devices.

•

We implemented YAMNet+, trained it using AHEAD-DS, and presented benchmark results including precision, recall and inference speed. We have deployed the model to a smartphone to demonstrate it works on an edge device.

This paper is organised as follows. The second section presents an overview of existing datasets and audiologically relevant environment labels. The third section presents an overview of several sound recognition models. The fourth section provides a description of AHEAD-DS and how the dataset was created. The fifth section describes the YAMNet+ sound recognition model. Section six presents the results of training and testing YAMNet+ using AHEAD-DS. Section seven discusses the overall findings of this paper and describes the possible future direction of the research. Section eight draws some conclusions.

This level of granular sound recognition is unnecessary: it is only necessary to recognise and respond to motor vehicle noises to improve the hearing experience. However, data can still be used through transfer learning or labels can be remapped to more broad labels.

Conversely, TAU scenes provide location-based recordings which are too general, such as at airports and metro stations. The sound of a motor vehicle will be similarly disruptive irrespective of whether they occur at either location. However, labels could be modified and made more specific, but this would likely require listening to and re-annotating the recordings.

UrbanSound8K and ESC50 provide extensive data. Both datasets contain very specific labels, such as the sounds from specific animals. Like AudioSet, this level of granularity is unnecessary for the purposes here. However, the data is still useful for transfer learning.

Given these issues, we created our own data set using HEAR-DS as a starting point.

Sound Recognition Machine Learning Models

Three machine learning models relevant for sound recognition are presented in this section, PANN
[
17
]
, AST
[
10
]
, and YAMNet
[
38
]
. The models in all of these studies were trained, validated, and tested using the AudioSet dataset. The findings are summarised in Table
2
. The extensive training dataset makes any of the models suitable for tuning via transfer learning.

While more sound recognition models exist, they are minor variations on the three aforementioned models
[
40
]
. Most models make use of frequency domain based features, such as log mel spectrograms. They use either convolution or attention neural network blocks with dense layers. The spectrograms are treated as images and processed using an image processing neural network. Minor variations involve switching spectrograms with log mel spectrograms, or a different configuration of neural network blocks. A minority of models such as PANN use the raw waveform as a feature.

The three models PANN, AST and YAMNet have the following tradeoffs.

•

PANN and AST achieve better mean average precision (mAP) results compared to YAMNet, 0.44 and 0.46 vs 0.31 respectively, using the testing set of AudioSet. However, PANN and AST require over 20 times more parameters compared to YAMNet, approximately 81 and 86 million compared to 3.7 million respectively. This poses a challenge for deploying PANN and AST to resource-constrained edge devices.

•

YAMNet is implemented using the TensorFlow ML framework, while PANN and AST are implemented using the Pytorch ML framework. A robust software pipeline exists to port any TensorFlow model to an edge device, such as an Android phone, using TensorFlow Lite. However, YAMNet’s implementation is not fully open source, while model definitions and weights have been released, training code is missing from the code repositories.

To address these issues, we developed our own model by creating a variation of YAMNet.

4
Another HEaring AiD scenes Data Set (AHEAD-DS)

We leverage several open source datasets to create a dataset which we called AHEAD-DS. A summary of the dataset is shown in Table
3
. A summary of the sources of the dataset is shown in Table
4
. There are a total of 9968 recordings, 10s per recording, across 14 different labels. Each instance is single-channel, 16-bit integers and sampled at 16 kHz.

Despite the limitations outlined earlier, AHEAD-DS is based on HEAR-DS and CHiME 6 Dev
[
36
]
. HEAR-DS provides a solid foundation for the following reasons:

•

The recordings of environment sounds have been open sourced with a permissive licence.

•

According to the authors of HEAR-DS, the 14 labels have been chosen from audiologically relevant environments for hearing devices by audiologists and Audifon, a manufacturer of hearing aids.

AHEAD-DS follows the design of HEAR-DS, it employs an identical set of 14 labels. This set comprises six environmental sound categories and an additional six categories representing speech instances within these respective environments. Hearing devices likely require different signal processing strategies to optimise speech intelligibility in each acoustic setting
[
3
]
. Therefore, the detection of speech embedded within specific environments represents a step towards creating an improved hearing experience for a hearing devices user.

The main issue with HEAR-DS is that the dataset is not in a fixed, ready-to-use state. This is because the published data is unmixed, and some pseudo-random procedures were used during the production of the HEAR-DS dataset. Since the code has not been published, these procedures cannot be fully reproduced. This causes the following problems.

•

It is not possible to determine the split for training and testing.

•

It is not possible to produce results which can be compared directly against the ones in the HEAR-DS paper.

The advantage of AHEAD-DS is that the dataset is in a ready-to-use state. The code for the mixing procedure is published and all mixing parameters are documented and reproducible. This is important because it removes a variable when comparing ML models. The speech and environment sounds have been released in both mixed and unmixed forms, eliminating any ambiguity regarding the subsets used to derive our new dataset.

The sounds from each environment for AHEAD-DS were taken from HEAR-DS. The authors of HEAR-DS recorded each scene using a dummy head. The head was equipped with a synthetic ear, the GRAS KB1065/1066 Pinnae. An Audiofon microphone was placed in each ear of the dummy head in an
in-the-canal
position. A Focusrite Scarlett 18i6 sound interface was used for analogue to digital conversion. All recordings were initially captured as 32-bit floating-point values sampled at 48 kHz, and later downsampled to a 16 kHz sampling rate with 16-bit integer quantisation. There were a total of 5929 clips of 10s, from 6 different environments.

Music samples from 10 genres were taken from HEAR-DS. The authors of HEAR-DS obtained the music from GTZAN
[
32
]
. All music samples were 10s long, 16-bit integers and sampled at 16 kHz. There were a total of 2992 clips of 10s of music.

One issue raised about AHEAD-DS and HEAR-DS was the constant acoustic signature of the
reverberant environment
might interfere with the classification of the acoustic signature of the other environments. For sound recognition, if a human can identify the presence of a reverberant environment, an acoustic signature exists for reverberant environments. Deep learning will automatically generate compensating features; these features are issues of concern for manual feature engineering; but not for deep learning
[
19
]
.

CHiME 6 Dev was used for speech samples in AHEAD-DS instead of CHiME 5, which was used in HEAR-DS. Both contain the same speech recordings, but the start and end times of all recordings have been corrected in CHiME 6 Dev to ensure all recordings are synchronised. CHiME 6 dev is a subset of CHiME 6; it is smaller than the whole dataset but sufficiently long for mixing with environment sounds to provide all the
speech in
\langle environment\rangle
clips and to produce
interfering speakers
. The
interfering speakers
clips were derived entirely from CHiME 6 dev.

The authors of CHiME 6 dev recorded the speech in two sessions. Each session in two different home environments consisting of an open space with dining, kitchen, and lounge areas. A total 44 sound files from CHiME 6 Dev were used in AHEAD-DS, 24 recordings from the first environment and 20 in the second. Each session consisted of four people and would begin by having participants take turns to introduce themselves via speaking and then read out a short passage. Once this is complete, participants engage in casual conversation as they prepare and share a meal together.

The authors of CHiME 6 dev made the recordings by placing six Microsoft Kinects throughout the home in the first session and five in the second. However, the Kinect version is unspecified. Each Kinect recorded four sound streams simultaneously, using 16-bit integers, sampled at 16 kHz.

In CHiME 6 additional speech was recorded using body-worn microphones, but only the recordings from the Kinect were used. The recordings from body-worn microphones lacked acoustic realism as they sounded like clean studio recordings.

The label
interfering speakers
contains only speech taken from CHiME 6 and is not mixed with environment sounds. It represents a scenario with multiple speakers where the speech is intelligible. The label
cocktail party
contains multiple speakers but the speech is unintelligible.

Compared to datasets like AudioSet, AHEAD-DS is still relatively small, which is a disadvantage. In later sections, we analyse the implications of having a small dataset (See
Label Smoothing, Augmentation, Unfreezing Only the Last Layer, and Random Weight Initialisation
section).

4.1
Data Processing Workflow

The data processing workflow is shown in Figure
1
.

The sounds sourced from HEAR-DS and CHiME 6 Dev had been encoded in Waveform Audio File Format (WAV) as single-channel, 16-bit integers, and sampled at 16 kHz using several different equipment configurations as described above. AHEAD-DS retained this format.

The levels of CHiME 6 dev and
music
were high as they were optimised for playback. The signal levels of the other recordings were much lower, and not optimised for playback. The levels were standardised before mixing the clips. The audio files were converted into 32-bit floating-point format during standardisation and mixing, then converted back to 16-bit integers, and saved to disk. For everything except for CHiME 6 Dev and
music
, the audio was standardised by dividing by the RMS of
cocktail party
and multiplying by the RMS of CHiME 6 Dev. For
music
the audio was standardised by dividing by the RMS of
music
and multiplying by the RMS of CHiME 6 Dev.

These adjustments ensure that the levels of
cocktail party
and
music
were approximately similar to conversations in CHiME 6 Dev. Recordings using new hardware configurations must be standardised, this only needs to be done once per device, to ensure recordings of conversations using the new device have similar levels to CHiME 6 Dev. Test results in a later section indicate that this procedure is critical to achieving good performance (See
Gain Sensitivity Analysis
section).

The first second from each recording in CHiME 6 dev is discarded, as it contains a loud beep to signal the start of the recording. The next 1100s were extracted from each file in 10s intervals. This generated 110 clips per file for a total of 4840 clips of speech.

For every environment from HEAR-DS, half the clips were mixed with speech, the other half are kept unmixed to represent the environment label. There are six environment labels which need to be mixed with speech. Of the 4840 speech clips taken from CHiME 6 Dev, 3793 clips were mixed with environmental sounds. 1047 clips were assigned to the label
interfering speakers
without further modifications.

For every five speech and environment sound file pairs, we mix them at
-10,-5,0,5,10
dB signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) respectively. When speech and environment sounds were mixed, we boosted the levels of the quieter sound until it had equal RMS to the louder sound (this procedure occurs on top of standardisation). During mixing, speech was used as the signal and the environment sounds were used as noise. The speech levels were adjusted to the required SNR. The sounds of
cocktail party
and
interfering speakers
were used without mixing. No speech clip was used more than once.

The levels on the quieter sounds were boosted, as several sound clips for
quiet indoors
had very low RMS. When speech was mixed at
-10
dB SNR, only near zero values were added. There were no or minimal signal changes after quantisation to 16-bit integers, when the mixed sound clips were saved back to disk. This causes sound clips with imperceptible speech being labelled with
speech in quiet indoors
, which is detrimental to model performance. The level boost ensures sound clips have acoustic realism and avoid labelling issues. Without the level boost, a human listener would not be able to determine there was speech in many
speech in quiet indoors
. With the level boost, background sounds are still nearly inaudible on manually listening, so the background acoustic signature is preserved.

After standardisation and mixing, we divide sounds for each label into training, validation, and testing sets using a percentage split of 70, 10, and 20 respectively.

4.2
Data Augmentation

Data augmentation was applied as a regularisation technique
[
29
]
. It was applied on-the-fly only during training. Three augmentation procedures were applied, each independently with a probability of fifty percent: a gain of between -6 to 6 dB; noise to perturb the data, from a uniform distribution within range -0.003 to 0.003; and the length of the clip is shrunk or stretched between 0.9 and 1.1, using the Fourier resampler
[
25
]
. These augmentation procedures were chosen because they were fast to implement, and parameter ranges were small to perturb the data, without changing it so much that on manual listening it is unrecognisable from the original. In a later section we find augmentation improves performance (See
Label Smoothing, Augmentation, Unfreezing Only the Last Layer, and Random Weight Initialisation
section).

5
YAMNet+ Sound Recognition Model

The architecture diagram of YAMNet+ is shown in Figure
2
. YAMNet+ is based on published YAMNet models
[
38
]
with the same internal architecture. We created a complete workflow for YAMNet+, as YAMNet was released with model definitions and weights but missing other components such as training code. We have released all code, making the implementation fully open source. For YAMNet+ we created training code, code to load weights, code for training via transfer learning, testing code, and conversion code to downsampling the model for deployment to edge computing platforms (We measured model speed using our test platform, a Google Pixel 3). YAMNet+ takes the waveform as input. A log mel spectrogram is computed from the waveform. The spectrogram is treated as a bitmap and processed using MobileNet
[
13
]
. The MobileNet starts with a convolutional block and a series of 13 blocks containing a depthwise separable convolution, batch normalisation, ReLU activation, convolution, batch normalisation and ReLU activation. There is a penultimate global average pooling layer. The final layer is a fully connected layer containing sigmoid activation of length equal to the number of class labels.

YAMNet+ slices the waveform into windows of length 960ms with a 480 millisecond overlap, therefore, a 10 second clip will be split into 20 windows. Zero padding is added to ensure the input waveform is of sufficient length. Sound recognition is performed on every 960 millisecond window; scores were not combined for a waveform divided across multiple windows; each window is treated separately. We use 960 milliseconds as it is a hyperparameter which is fixed by the use of transfer learning; this is essential to get good performance (See
Label Smoothing, Augmentation, Unfreezing Only the Last Layer, and Random Weight Initialisation
section). If a longer window is required, it is possible to work around the fixed length by adding the results from multiple windows and applying a softmax mathematical operation.

YAMNet was originally trained using AudioSet containing 521 class labels. Both YAMNet and YAMNet+ contain 3,751,369 parameters when the model is initialised using 521 class labels. There are 3,743,831 parameters when the model is initialised using 14 class labels, as used in HEAR-DS and AHEAD-DS.

Training occurred for a maximum of 100 epochs. An Adam optimiser
[
16
]
was used, with a learning rate of 0.00001. A decay-on-plateau learning rate scheduler was used. After 3 epochs, if performance on the validation set did not improve, the learning rate decays by multiplying by 0.5 after 6 epochs, if performance on the validation set did not improve, the training cycle was terminated early. The order of the training data was shuffled after every epoch.

Focal loss
[
20
]
with
\alpha=0.25
and
\gamma=2
, was used. Focal loss down-weights easy and common classes, to better handle unbalanced datasets (AHEAD-DS is unbalanced).

A data loader was implemented to cycle through the data during training. It converts sound clips and labels into the format used during training. The sound clips are in WAV format and the labels are stored in a spreadsheet. Testing code was implemented to compute the confusion matrix, precision, recall, and mean average precision.

In order to optimise YAMNet+ for deployment on edge devices, the full-precision model was converted to a half-precision 16-bit floating-point model. This reduces the model size to 6.19 MiB. All subsequent testing was done using the 16-bit floating-point model.

:
The architecture of YAMNet+. The model computes the log mel spectrogram from the waveform. This is passed to a MobileNet which assigns confidence values to each label.

5.1
Label Smoothing

Label smoothing
[
22
]
with a smoothing factor of 0.1, was applied to the loss function. Label smoothing is a regularisation technique, which works by subtracting a small value from 1 for true classes, and adding a small value to 0 for incorrect classes. In a later section we show that label smoothing improves performance (See
Label Smoothing, Augmentation, Unfreezing Only the Last Layer, and Random Weight Initialisation
section).

5.2
Transfer Learning, Weight Initialisation, and Frozen Layers

YAMNet+ was initialised using transfer learning
[
41
]
, where the weights were initially trained using AudioSet. Two ways of applying transfer learning were tested with YAMNet+. One method is to freeze all except the last layer, alternatively parameters in all layers are trainable. In a later section we will see having all layers trainable results in better performance (See
Label Smoothing, Augmentation, Unfreezing Only the Last Layer, and Random Weight Initialisation
section).

5.3
System Setup

YAMNet+ was developed using Python and TensorFlow. The development system was running Debian 12, FFmpeg 5.1.6, Python 3.11.2, TensorFlow 2.15.1 and 2.17.0, TensorFlow IO 0.37.1, and Keras 3. We make use of existing model definition code from YAMNet; this code is not fully compatible with newer versions of TensorFlow. We use the older TensorFlow to load and save a YAMNet model into a format which is compatible with newer TensorFlow. Then we use the newer TensorFlow to further tune this model.

TensorFlow Lite was used to convert the model to 16 bits to reduce the memory and computation requirements. The TensorFlow Lite model was deployed through an Android phone application
[
21
]
. The Android application was written using Flutter 3.24.5 and Dart 3.5.4.

The edge computing test system was a Google Pixel 3 phone running Android 13. The development system used a i7-1195G7 processor, 32 GiB of memory and a solid-state drive.

The models were trained using only the central processing unit (CPU). It was not possible to get graphics processing unit (GPU) acceleration due to compatibility issues with TensorFlow; however, GPU use would reduce training time significantly.

6
Test Results

Precision and recall curves are shown in Figure
3
. The confusion matrix is shown in Figure
4
. The metrics were produced using the test set of AHEAD-DS. Unless otherwise noted, tests were conducted using parameters outlined in the previous sections. Results shown in this section have been rounded to 2 decimal places.

A mean Average Precision (mAP) of 0.83 was achieved. This metric was calculated by averaging precision values across thresholds from 0.0 to 1.0 (in 0.1 increments) for each label, and then averaging those results. A threshold was applied to determine the label for each 960 millisecond window and compared against the ground truth. This gives the true positive, false positive, true negative, and false negative values. A higher mAP indicates better performance.

Label Smoothing, Augmentation, Unfreezing Only the Last Layer, and Random Weight Initialisation

The results of validating several configurations, by adjusting hyperparameters are shown in Figure

. The best overall result occurs under the default configuration with validation loss, mAP, and accuracy of 0.11, 0.85, and 0.95 respectively. The default configuration had label smoothing enabled, augmentation enabled, all layers were trainable, and weight initialisation used weights trained on AudioSet instead of random initialisation. The learning rate was set to 0.00001 for all experiments in this section.

When label smoothing was not applied the ML model produced a validation loss, mAP, and accuracy of 0.02, 0.82, and 0.95 respectively (compared to 0.11, 0.85, and 0.95 when label smoothing was enabled). The configuration with no label smoothing produced a low validation loss of 0.02. However, this result did not lead to better mAP or accuracy; this occurs when predictions are wrong but still relatively close to the correct results. The application of label smoothing yielded improved overall results.

When augmentation was not applied the ML model produced validation loss, mAP, and accuracy of 0.12, 0.84, and 0.94 respectively (compared to 0.11, 0.85, and 0.95 when augmentation was enabled). Augmentation yielded a very small impact on performance.

When only the last layer remained unfrozen the ML model produced a validation loss, mAP, and accuracy of 0.18, 0.87, and 0.80 respectively (compared to 0.11, 0.85, and 0.95 when all layers were trainable). This configuration produced the highest mAP of 0.87. However, a high mAP can still be achieved even when there are many false negatives in the presence of few false positives. The default configuration had better validation loss and accuracy results. Therefore, having all layers trainable yielded improved overall results.

When using random weight initialisation, the ML model produced a validation loss, mAP, and accuracy of 0.53, 0.61, and 0.15 respectively (compared to 0.11, 0.85, and 0.95 when using weights trained on AudioSet). The metrics indicate the ML model produced with random weight initialisation was likely not practically useful. Initialising the weights, trained on AudioSet, through transfer learning was essential to achieving a performant ML model.

The implications of the small size of AHEAD-DS can be observed in the analysis of results using random weight initialisation. Random weight initialisation produced a high loss for both training and validation, 0.53 and 0.53 respectively. This indicates underfitting. The learning curves show that fine tuning weights trained on AudioSet is necessary, when faced with the limited training data in AHEAD-DS.

The tradeoff of using transfer learning is that several hyperparameters become fixed. Changing a hyperparameter impacts all parameters dependent on the said hyperparameter. A new model will need to be initialised and re-trained from scratch using AudioSet, before transfer learning can be applied. This makes it resource intensive to change hyperparameters such as extending the window length beyond 960ms.

7
Discussion

7.1
Summary of Findings

The experiments demonstrate that YAMNet+ achieves promising performance, with a mAP of 0.83 and an accuracy of 0.93. The study highlights two critical findings that are essential for YAMNet+ to perform well. One is the necessity of transfer learning from the pre-trained YAMNet model on AudioSet to achieve satisfactory performance. The other is the necessity of proper adjustment of sound levels to ensure optimal performance.

The real-time sound-based scene recognition capabilities of YAMNet+ on edge devices were successfully demonstrated. On a Google Pixel 3 smartphone (a phone with modest specifications, released in 2018), the model processes audio with approximately 50ms of latency to load the model, and an approximate linear increase of 30ms per 1 second of audio. This indicates the feasibility of deploying such models for real-time applications on resource constrained devices.

The workflow and toolchain created for training and deploying a scene recognition model to edge devices is useful for future research in this area.

7.2
Comparison with Previous Work

Compared to the original YAMNet public release, YAMNet+ is an improvement because we implemented a complete workflow, so it can be tuned with transfer learning. YAMNet published without training code can only be used as a black box. YAMNet+ is also lightweight with 3.7 million parameters compared to the
>80
million parameters of other sound recognition models
[
17
]

[

]
.

AHEAD-DS allows comparison of models using a standardised dataset with consistent labels, divided into training/validation/testing sets, and annotated with the ground truth. AHEAD-DS is particularly valuable given the difficulty of directly comparing existing studies, where datasets may be incomplete, not in a ready-to-use state, labels differ between datasets, and the code and datasets may not be publicly available.

7.3
Limitations

The testing performed in previous sections reveal several limitations of the YAMNet+ and AHEAD-DS. Both training and validation loss of 0.53 show the AHEAD-DS dataset is small in size, and leads to underfitting when training YAMNet+ from scratch using random weight initialisation. We worked around this problem by using transfer learning, where the model is initialised using weights trained on AudioSet.

Another implication of the small dataset is the observed small improvement after applying regularisation techniques such as label smoothing and augmentation. A model must generalise better to perform well on a larger dataset, therefore regularisation techniques should be more impactful if the AHEAD-DS is expanded.

The use of transfer learning comes with its own tradeoffs. We are locked to classifying 960 millisecond windows, as this is a hyperparameter which is fixed by the use of weights from an existing model. It is possible to work around the fixed length by adding the results from multiple windows and applying a softmax mathematical operation.

The tests of gain sensitivity analysis indicate adjustment of the levels is necessary to achieve optimal performance. Recordings using new hardware configurations must be standardised, this only needs to be done once per device, to ensure recordings of conversations using the new device have similar levels to CHiME 6 Dev.

The final notable limitation is the dependency on TensorFlow. YAMNet+ is built using the TensorFlow ML library, as it is necessary to maintain compatibility with YAMNet weights, required for transfer learning. TensorFlow has GPU compatibility problems, disabling the use of a GPU for quick model training and complicating the codebase.

7.4
Next Steps

Future work can target three areas: the dataset, the model, and implementation details. The dataset is still relatively small compared to the likes of AudioSet and one finding is that transfer learning is essential to achieve reasonable performance. Therefore, the next step is to collect more data for a new version of AHEAD-DS. More sound clips, more varied sound environments, and more varied recording equipment configurations ensures models trained using a future version of AHEAD-DS are more generalisable.

We can improve the dataset by choosing scene labels in a systematic way. In this paper we only adopted labels from an existing study and looked at several studies which apply labels to sound clips. We found a lack of rigorous studies to explain why these labels are good choices. Examples such as
speech in noise
seem very generic and
one talker on subway platform
seem oddly specific. We have yet to determine the balance between labels being too specific or too generic, concerning the acoustic environments and listening contexts critical from an audiological perspective. This is important as good scene labels are crucial in hearing research, especially when conducting Ecological Momentary Assessment (EMA)
[
12
]
. EMAs are common in hearing research and involve surveying participants in real-time, and scene recognition models can automate this process, saving time and reducing the participants’ cognitive load. However, if these labels are ambiguous, inconsistent, or too broad/narrow, model accuracy will suffer, negating the benefits of automation and potentially increasing the burden on participants.

We may be able to improve the results by making better use of the limited data in AHEAD-DS. Speech and environment sounds can be left unmixed but standardised in the training set. More varied training data can be generated if they are mixed on-the-fly. So long as the test data is not mixed on-the-fly; results still remain comparable between test runs.

Things that would improve the model implementation would include rebuilding the model by first cleanly separating feature computation and model code. This will enable further experimentation with other features such as time domain based features. This may enable more effective detection of acoustic properties such as reverberations, which are more salient in the time domain. Rebuilding the model enables the ability to test different architectures such as EfficientNet or ResNet. Newer architectures such as EfficientNet might boost precision and recall.

We can improve the model implementation by using a modern ML library such as JAX or Pytorch. This allows us to avoid the complex legacy code and backward compatibility problems of TensorFlow. It would also significantly speed up training as more modern ML libraries have better compatibility with GPU acceleration.

We want to test the implementation on more types of edge hardware. This will allow us to determine the minimum resources necessary to enable real time sound recognition. If possible we want to deploy ML models to very power and resource constrained devices such as hearing devices and still maintain the ability to react to sounds in real time.

8
Conclusion

This study addresses key challenges in scene recognition for hearing devices by introducing a refined dataset and machine learning model. AHEAD-DS is a publicly available dataset specifically designed for audiologically relevant environments, offering a standardised dataset for comparing models with consistent labels and in a ready-to-use state. Complementing this, YAMNet+ is presented as a robust sound recognition model, optimised for deployment on resource-constrained edge devices like smartphones. YAMNet+ only requires 3.7 million parameters compared to
>80
million parameters of other sound recognition models. It serves as an open-source baseline, including model definitions, training, and testing code. Benchmarking YAMNet+ on AHEAD-DS yielded a mAP of 0.83 and an accuracy of 0.93. The model’s efficiency was demonstrated on a Google Pixel 3 smartphone, processing each second of audio in approximately 30ms with 50ms latency. The established workflow and toolchain for training and deploying such models on edge devices offer a valuable resource for future advancements in this field.
