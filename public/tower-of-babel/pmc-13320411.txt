Anatomical Variability of the Proximal Femur: A Morphometric Study Relevant to Orthopedic Implant Design
Authors: Bahattin PASLI, Mehmet ULKIR, Barış KAFA
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveProximal femur morphometry plays a crucial role in fracture management and hip arthroplasty by affecting implant fit, biomechanics, and surgical outcomes. Therefore, the utilization of anatomical data reflecting inter-individual variability is essential for optimizing implant design and minimizing implant-bone mismatch. This study aimed to evaluate proximal femoral morphometry and provide data relevant to implant design.MethodsA total of 73 unpaired dry femurs of unknown age and sex (43 right, 30 left) were analyzed. Measurements included femoral length, neck-body angle, and femoral head and neck dimensions. All measurements were independently performed by two anatomists and repeated using a tape measure, a digital caliper, and a protractor. Interobserver and intra observer reliabilities were assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC). Statistical analyses were performed, including descriptive statistics, pairwise comparisons, and correlation analyses.ResultsMeasurement reliability ranged from good to excellent (ICC: 0.749-0.999). Mean femoral length was 43.7±3.0 cm, and mean neck-body angle was 126.0°±4.6°. No significant side differences were observed for femoral length, femoral head, or femoral neck dimensions; however, the neck-body angle differed significantly (p<0.001). The correlation analysis revealed positive associations among parameters of the femoral head and neck. Femoral length showed strong correlations with head dimensions, whereas the neck-body angle demonstrated no significant correlations with other parameters. The lack of age and sex data should be considered when interpreting these findings.ConclusionsThe proximal femur exhibits substantial morphometric variability. These findings provide preliminary data that may contribute to the existing literature and help inform the design of future implants and prostheses.

INTRODUCTION

The increase in life expectancy is expected to lead to a rise in the number of trauma patients, similar to trends observed in many chronic diseases1. Projections suggest that the annual global incidence of hip fractures will increase sharply, from 1.66 million cases in 1990 to an estimated 6.26 million by 20502. A similar upward trend is expected in hip osteoarthritis3. These trends highlight the increasing clinical and public health burden associated with an aging population and emphasize the growing importance of an accurate anatomical understanding of the proximal femur for fracture fixation and hip arthroplasty.

Proximal femur fractures are a prevalent type of traumatic injury that can occur even with relatively low energy trauma in elderly individuals and often lead to significant morbidity4. In addition to their clinical impact, fractures impose a substantial economic burden due to prolonged hospital stays and the need for extensive rehabilitation services5. While these injuries are often observed in younger individuals following high-energy trauma, appropriate management is particularly important, as they are associated with substantial loss of workforce6.

Therefore, understanding the morphometric characteristics of the proximal femur is increasingly important for optimizing surgical management, achieving proper prosthetic alignment, and guiding hip implant design7, 8. Crucial morphometric parameters, including femoral head and neck dimensions and neck-body angle, directly inform prosthesis selection, screw positioning, and implant fit. Accurate matching of these dimensions to an individual’s anatomy is critical for preventing complications such as osteolysis, aseptic loosening, improper load distribution, micromotion of the implanted stem, stress shielding, and patient discomfort9, 10. Furthermore, discrepancies between implant geometry and bone morphology can hinder implantation, promote abnormal load transfer and wear, and thereby adversely affect surgical outcomes11.

The efficacy of treatment is dependent not only on the successful implementation of surgical methodologies, but also on the utilization of implants and prostheses that are meticulously adapted to the patient’s unique anatomical characteristics7. However, previous reports have indicated that implants may not always conform to femoral morphometry, potentially resulting in implant-bone mismatch12, 13, 14. This implant-bone mismatch may lead to unnecessary bone removal during the implantation process15. This issue can be attributed to a mismatch between the characteristics of the population on which the implant was designed and the characteristics of the population to which the implant is marketed7, 15.

In this context, this study aimed to evaluate the morphometric characteristics of the proximal femur and provide data that may contribute to optimizing implant and prosthesis design for the management of fractures and for hip arthroplasty.

MATERIALS and METHODS

The study protocol was approved by the Hacettepe University Health Sciences Research Ethics Committee (approval no: SBA 26/034, date: 06.01.2026). A total of 84 unpaired dry femurs of unknown age and sex, constituting a sample from Türkiye, were examined in the Macroscopic Anatomy Laboratory of the Department of Anatomy. No demographic information was available to confirm that these specimens fully represent the population of Türkiye. The sample size was determined based on the availability of intact, measurable femora that met the inclusion criteria within the institutional osteological collection. The lack of data on age and sex represents an inherent limitation of the osteological collection; no predictive or advanced analytical methods were applied to estimate these variables, which may consequently limit interpretation of the findings. After a thorough examination, 11 femurs that were incomplete, exhibited defects affecting the measurement parameters, or had disrupted cortical integrity were excluded from the study. The remaining specimens were numbered and their sides were recorded. The following parameters were measured on the femur and are shown in Figure 1.

Length of the Femur (LF); defined as the distance from the tip of femoral head and inferior border of the medial condyle.

Neck-body Angle (NBA); is defined as the angle between the axis of the femoral neck and the axis of the body of the femur. The NBA plays a crucial role in restoring hip biomechanics, ensuring proper load distribution16.

Parameters of Head of Femur

Femoral head dimensions are fundamental in prosthetic design, as directly influence joint stability, range of motion, contact mechanics, therefore accurate sizing is essential to minimize dislocation risk, reduce implant-bone mismatch, and prevent complication such as increased wear and groin pain17, 18.

Transverse Diameter of Femoral Head (TDH); defined as the maximum linear distance from the medial to the lateral end of the femoral head.

Vertical Diameter of Femoral Head (VDH); defined as the maximum linear distance from the cranial to the caudal end of the femoral head.

Transverse Diameter of Base of Femoral Head (TDBH); defined as the linear distance from the medial to the lateral end of the base of the femoral head.

Vertical Diameter of Base of Femoral Head (VDBH); defined as the linear distance from the cranial to the caudal end of the base of the femoral head.

Circumference of the femoral head (CH); defined as the circumference of the femoral head.

Parameters of Neck of Femur

Femoral neck dimensions is crucial for hip biomechanics and prosthetics stability; parameters such as neck width and length influence screw selection, positioning, and fixation stability in fracture management, and femoral stem sizing, prosthetic offset, and alignment in hip arthroplasty, thereby contributing to implant stability, optimal load distribution, and reduced risk of dislocation or aseptic loosening17, 19, 20.

Length of the Femoral Neck (LN); defined as distance from the base of the femoral head to distal end of intertrochanteric line.

Diameter of the Femoral Neck [superior-inferior] (DNSI); defined as the linear distance from superior to the inferior midpoint of neck of femur.

Diameter of the Femoral Neck [anterior-posterior] (DNAP); defined as the linear distance from anterior to the posterior midpoint of neck of femur.

Length of the axis of the Femoral Neck (LAFN); is defined as the linear distance along the femoral neck axis from the center of the femoral head to the lateral cortex of the femur.

Circumference of the Femoral Neck (CN); defined as the circumference of the femoral neck.

Measurement Methods

Femoral length and head and neck circumferences were measured using a tape measure (1-mm precision); the neck-body angle was measured using a digital protractor (0.01° precision); and the remaining parameters were measured using a digital caliper (0.01-mm precision). All digital measurement devices were calibrated by setting them to zero before each measurement. Parameters were independently measured by two anatomists with 6 years (BP) and 9 (MÜ) years of experience, respectively. Each observer repeated all measurements twice, with a 1-week interval between repetitions, yielding four measurements per parameter. Intra-observer reliability was assessed using repeated measurements obtained from each observer, while inter-observer reliability was evaluated based on the first set of measurements independently performed by the observers.

Statistical Analysis

Interobserver and intraobserver reliability were assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). ICC values below 0.50 were considered to indicate poor reliability, values between 0.50 and 0.75 indicated moderate reliability, values from 0.76 to 0.90 indicated good reliability, and values greater than 0.90 represented excellent reliability21. Descriptive statistics, including median, mean, standard deviation, and minimum and maximum values, were calculated. Normality was evaluated using histograms and the Shapiro-Wilk tests. Pairwise comparisons were performed using independent-samples t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests, depending on whether the data were normally distributed. The relationships among the parameters were evaluated using Pearson’s and Spearman’s correlation tests. Correlation coefficients were interpreted as strong (0.80-1.00), moderate (0.60-0.80), fair (0.30-0.60), and poor (below 0.30). A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 23 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

RESULTS

A total of 73 femurs were analyzed, including 43 right-sided and 30 left-sided specimens.

Measurement Reliability

Interobserver reliability analysis of the femoral neck parameters showed moderate-to-good reliability for DNSI and DNAP (ICC: 0.749 and 0.779, respectively), whereas other parameters demonstrated excellent reliability. Intraobserver repeatability demonstrated excellent ICC values across all parameters, except for DNSI and DNAP, which showed good reliability (ICC range: 0.772-0.841) (Table 1).

Femoral Length and Neck-body Angle

The mean LF and NBA were 43.7±3.0 cm (range, 37.0-51.4 cm) and 126.0°±4.6° (range, 116.8°-137.1°), respectively. No statistically significant side differences were found in femoral length (p=0.974); however, the NBA showed significant side differences (p=0.001) (Table 2).

Femoral Head Parameters

The mean TDH, VDH, TDBH, and VDBH were 44.9±3.8 mm, 43.4±3.7 mm, 41.6±3.4 mm, and 44.0±3.6 mm, respectively. The mean CH was 14.1±1.1 cm. No statistically significant differences were observed between sides for femoral head parameters, including transverse and vertical head diameters and head circumference (p>0.05). Detailed measurements of femoral head dimensions are presented in Table 2.

Femoral Neck Parameters

The mean LN, DNSI, DNAP and LAFN were 40.8±8.6 mm, 31.9±3.3 mm, 25.8±3.4 mm, and 94.7±8.4 mm, respectively. The mean CN was 10.7±0.9 cm. No statistically significant side-to-side differences were observed for LN, DNSI, DNAP, LAFN, and CN (p>0.05). Femoral neck measurement values are summarized in Table 2.

Correlation Analysis

Correlation analysis revealed significant positive associations among most femoral head and neck morphometric parameters. LF showed strong positive correlations with femoral head parameters, except for TDBH, with which a moderate positive correlation was observed. In addition, LN, DNSI, and DNAP exhibited fair to moderate positive correlations.

No significant correlations were found between NBA and the other parameters. Moderate positive correlations were observed between femoral head and neck diameters, whereas neck length showed fair positive correlations with these diameters. While CH showed moderate positive correlations with LN, DNSI, and DNAP, CN exhibited moderate positive correlations with all femoral head diameters (Table 3).

DISCUSSION

In the present study, the morphometric characteristics of the proximal femur were evaluated, revealing considerable variability that underscores the importance of accounting for anatomical differences in fracture management, implant design, and total hip arthroplasty. As implant geometry and sizing are largely derived from morphometric data, such variability may directly influence implant fit, primary stability, load transmission and long-term biomechanical performance9, 10. Population differences have been widely reported7, 15, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28, 29, 30 (Tables 4, 5), indicating that implants designed using data from one population may not be fully compatible with others. For example, implants in Japan are developed based on population-specific data, highlighting the clinical importance of such approaches7.

Consistent with previous reports, significant positive correlations were observed between femoral length and proximal femur parameters22, 27, 28. In the present study, strong to moderate correlations were observed among head parameters, whereas fair to moderate correlations were observed among neck parameters. In contrast, no significant correlation was observed for the NBA (Table 3). These relationships may have implications for prosthesis sizing, and in anthropological contexts to the indirect estimation of human stature22, 27, 28.

The NBA, which facilitates alignment between the acetabulum and the femoral head, is of substantial importance in hip joint biomechanics. This angle represents an anatomical adaptation that enhances hip rotation and increases freedom of movement by positioning the lower limb farther from the pelvis16. The mean NBA observed in the present study (126°) falls within the range reported in the literature (109°-152°)7, 15, 23, 24, 25, 26, 28, 29, 30, although considerable variability has been documented (Table 4). In the present study, no significant correlation was identified between the NBA and other femoral head and neck parameters. Previous studies have reported inconsistent findings regarding the correlation between the NBA and VDH, DNSI, DNAP, LN, and LAFN7, 16, 24, 30; this variability may complicate prosthesis design.

In total hip arthroplasty, the standard femoral prosthesis is typically designed with a NBA of 131°31, which exceeds the mean values reported in previous studies24, 26, 28, 30 (Table 4). This discrepancy may affect implant positioning and postoperative biomechanics; however, this relationship was not directly evaluated in the present study. Moreover, NBA is a critical factor in fracture fixation with dynamic hip screws. While two-part intertrochanteric fractures typically require a 135° screw, lower mean NBA values may limit the suitability in certain populations, warranting further clinical investigation15.

Femoral head prosthesis design is another key determinant of clinical outcomes in total hip arthroplasty15, 29. Although larger femoral heads are often preferred to reduce the risk of dislocation, they may be associated with increased wear, biomechanical disturbances, and groin pain17. In the present study, transverse and vertical head diameters were comparable to those reported in previous dry-bone and imaging-based studies (33.0-54.1 mm and 31-60.0 mm, respectively). Among these studies, Umer et al.29 reported the highest mean transverse femoral head diameter (50.1 mm), while İyem et al.7 reported the highest mean vertical diameter (48.1 mm) (Table 4). In the present study, fair-to-moderate positive correlations were identified between femoral head and neck parameters (Table 3). However, these correlations appear to be inconsistent with those previously reported (Table 6).

Femoral neck morphology plays a crucial role in maintaining native hip biomechanics and ensuring prosthetic stability19. Increased femoral neck width has been associated with a higher fracture risk in older individuals20, and screw selection is particularly important in femoral neck fracture fixation. A 20-mm screw typically requires a minimum safety margin of approximately 2.5 mm during placement32. In the present study, the mean DNSI value of 31.9±3.3 mm suggest a potentially sufficient safety margin for 20 mm screws10, 33, consistent with previous reports; however, this interpretation should be approached with caution, as no direct surgical or clinical outcome data were assessed7,15,23-27,30. Additionally, accurate restoration of femoral neck length is essential for maintaining limb length symmetry, optimizing gait mechanics, and preventing postoperative walking disturbances7.

Comparative studies evaluating radiographic measurements against dry bone specimens have reported significant discrepancies for parameters such as VDH, DNSI, LFN, and LAFN, whereas NBA measurements appear to be less affected7, 34. These findings suggest that radiographic techniques may not fully replicate absolute morphometric values obtained from direct bone measurements, emphasizing the continued relevance of dry bone studies for establishing accurate anatomical reference standards.

The influence of sex and age could not be assessed in the present study due to the lack of demographic information. Previous studies have reported no significant side differences for parameters such as LF, TDH, VDH, DNSI, LN and LAFN, while demonstrating significant sex differences in these variables7, 15, 25, 28. İyem et al.7 reported a poor correlation between age and proximal femoral parameters. There is inconsistency in the literature regarding the relationship between the NBA and sex7, 15, 25, 23.

The observed correlations among femoral head, neck, FL, and NBA parameters suggest that implants based on non-local morphometric data may not adequately represent all populations. Such mismatches may increase the risk of suboptimal implant fit and biomechanical imbalance. With the expected exponential increase in hip surgeries over the coming decades, proximal femoral morphometry is likely to play an increasingly critical role in both implant design and treatment planning. In this context, patient-specific or modular implant designs may offer a more appropriate approach by accommodating interindividual and population differences. Importantly, the clinical interpretations presented in the present study should be considered preliminary, as no direct clinical outcome data were evaluated. Therefore, while the findings provide useful morphometric data, caution is warranted when extrapolating these results to clinical practice, particularly given the lack of age and sex information, which limits interpretation of subgroups and may contribute to the observed variability. Future studies integrating morphometric findings with surgical and functional outcomes are needed to validate these assumptions. In addition, the incorporation of computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) into subsequent research could provide complementary morphometric information, enhance the accuracy of acetabular and femoral evaluations, and allow direct correlation with clinical and in vivo parameters.

Study Limitations

The main limitations of this study include the relatively small sample size for femur specimens and the lack of information on age and sex. The sample size was determined by the inclusion of all intact and measurable femora that were available in the institutional osteological collection. Accordingly, no a priori sample size calculation was applied. Furthermore, measurements were performed on dry bones, which may not fully reflect in vivo biomechanics, and bone quality was not assessed, potentially limiting the direct clinical applicability of the findings. Moreover, femoral morphometric characteristics can be affected by ethnic origin and individual lifestyle factors, including habitual and repetitive movements, thereby limiting the generalizability of the results. Our measurements were limited to the bony femoral head, whereas including cartilage thickness in the measurements would provide more accurate values for clinical correlation. Additionally, important parameters for prosthesis design, such as the medullary canal diameter, were not evaluated. Finally, the lack of corresponding CT or MRI imaging data restricted the morphometric analysis to osteological measurements.

CONCLUSION

The findings of this study demonstrate substantial morphometric variability of the proximal femur, which may be relevant for implant design and surgical planning. Incorporation of patient-specific and modular design principles, together with anatomical considerations specific to each patient, can improve prosthesis compatibility, facilitate individualized surgical planning, and potentially enhance long-term clinical outcomes in proximal femoral fracture management and hip arthroplasty.
