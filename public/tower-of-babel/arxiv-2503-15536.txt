Current and quantum transport factor of fermionic system in fermionic bath
Jayarshi Bhattacharya, Gautam Gangopadhyay, Sunandan Gangopadhyay

Abstract
This paper explores the dynamics of current and the quantum transport factor in a fermionic system with a central oscillator interacting with two fermionic reservoirs at different temperatures. We derive the master equation for the system’s density matrix, accounting for energy exchange between the system and the reservoirs. The current is analyzed in relation to system parameters and reservoir temperatures, revealing that the quantum transport factor differs from classical systems, approaching Carnot efficiency at high temperatures but being different at low temperatures. We also examine the power spectrum of the current, providing insights into current fluctuations and their temperature dependence. Furthermore, we derive the Fokker-Planck equation and the Glauber-Sudarshan P-representation, where the steady-state probability distribution takes a Gaussian form, confirming the system behaves like a harmonic oscillator. This work advances the understanding of quantum transport in fermionic systems and provides a foundation for future research in quantum thermodynamics.

II
Current for Fermions

In this article, we analyze a fermionic oscillator that interacts with two separate reservoirs, an emitter and a collector. Each of these reservoirs is maintained at a different temperature, denoted by
T_{e}
for the emitter and
T_{c}
for the collector. What makes this setup particularly interesting is that both reservoirs are fermionic in nature, meaning they follow Fermi-Dirac statistics rather than classical or bosonic distributions.

To describe the dynamics of this system, we construct the Hamiltonian, which captures the interactions between the oscillator and the reservoirs. This Hamiltonian accounts for the internal energy of the fermionic oscillator, the energy of the emitter and collector, and the coupling between the oscillator and each reservoir. By carefully analyzing this Hamiltonian, we can study key properties such as energy transport, dissipation, and quantum correlations within the system. The Hamiltonian we start with reads

\displaystyle\hat{H_{s}}

\displaystyle=\hbar\omega_{s}\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}

(1a)

\displaystyle\hat{H_{e}}

\displaystyle=\hbar\sum\limits_{j}\omega^{e}_{j}\hat{b}_{j}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{j}

(1b)

\displaystyle\hat{H_{c}}

\displaystyle=\hbar\sum\limits_{j}\omega^{c}_{j}\hat{d_{j}}^{\dagger}\hat{d_{j}}

(1c)

\displaystyle\hat{H_{i}}

\displaystyle=\hbar\sum\limits_{j}\left(\Gamma_{ej}\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{j}+\Gamma_{ej}^{*}\hat{a}\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{j}+\Gamma_{cj}\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{d_{j}}+\Gamma_{cj}^{*}\hat{a}\hat{d_{j}}^{\dagger}\right)

(1d)

In this context,
\hat{a}
,
\hat{b}_{j}
, and
\hat{d_{j}}
represent operators satisfying the fermionic anti-commutation relations
\left\{\hat{b}_{j},\hat{b_{k}}^{\dagger}\right\}=\delta_{jk}=\left\{\hat{d_{j}},\hat{d_{k}}^{\dagger}\right\}
and
\left\{\hat{a},\hat{a}^{\dagger}\right\}=1
. The Hamiltonian described above, which models a single fermionic oscillator interacting with two reservoirs at distinct temperatures, holds significance in the study of quantum particle transport. Specifically, investigating transport behavior in systems with discrete energy levels has been a topic of considerable interest
[
37
,
38
,
39
]
, making the model in this work particularly relevant. Earlier in
[
15
]
, similar model was considered, but with Grassmannian coupling. In real world, the couplings are usually meant to be found as ordinary numbers. So in this work we kept the coupling constants as ordinary numbers and found useful results.

The quantum current to be discussed later can be realized by configuring a source-system-sink setup, where the source and sink are bosonic reservoirs characterized by the Hamiltonians provided in eqs. (
1b
) and (
1c
). Additionally, it is worth noting that the problem of a single Fermionic oscillator coupled to a reservoir also has significance in quantum optics. Here, the reservoir oscillators can correspond to the Fourier modes of the radiation field into which an excited atom undergoes spontaneous emission
[
8
]
.

We employ the density matrix approach in the interaction picture to further our analysis. The differential equation
[
8
,
9
,
10
]
governs the time development of the density matrix
\hat{\rho_{s}}
in the above picture.

\displaystyle\frac{d\hat{\rho_{s}}}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}~~\text{Tr}_{e,c}\left[\hat{H_{i}}(t),\left[\hat{H_{i}}(t^{\prime}),\hat{\rho}(t^{\prime})\right]\right]~.

(2)

where,
\hat{\rho}(t^{\prime})=\hat{\rho_{s}}(t^{\prime})\otimes\hat{\rho_{e}}(0)\otimes\hat{\rho_{c}}(0)
.

With
\hat{H_{i}}(t)
standing for the interaction Hamiltonian, this equation depicts the intricate relationship amongst the system and its surroundings. It is essential to understanding our fermionic system’s dynamics.

It is then possible to calculate the temporal evolution of the density matrix
\hat{\rho}
in the Schröedinger picture by applying the methods previously employed in
[
8
,
9
,
15
]
.

\displaystyle\frac{d\hat{\rho}}{dt}

\displaystyle=-i\omega\left[\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a},\hat{\rho}\right]-\frac{\gamma_{e}+\gamma_{c}}{2}\left(\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}\hat{\rho}+\hat{\rho}\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}-2\hat{a}\hat{\rho}\hat{a}^{\dagger}\right)-\left(\gamma_{e}\bar{n}_{e}+\gamma_{c}\bar{n}_{c}\right)\left(\hat{\rho}-\hat{a}\hat{\rho}\hat{a}^{\dagger}-\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{\rho}\hat{a}\right)

(3)

where
\omega=\omega_{s}+\Delta+\Delta^{\prime}
is the shifted frequency of the system.

The coefficients in question are essential for measuring the system’s dissipative processes. In addition,
\bar{n}_{e}
and
\bar{n}_{c}
represent the number densities, which are specified as

\displaystyle\bar{n}_{e}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{e^{\frac{\hbar\omega_{s}}{k_{B}T_{e}}}+1}

(4a)

\displaystyle\bar{n}_{c}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{e^{\frac{\hbar\omega_{s}}{k_{B}T_{c}}}+1}~.

(4b)

These number densities reflect the equilibrium distributions of fermions within the emitter and collector reservoirs.

It is important to note that the master equation found in eq. (
3
) looks quite different to what found in
[
15
]
due to the coupling coefficient being ordinary numbers and not the Grassmanian ones. Nevertheless, we shall see that the current can be found in similar form as found in

]

To gain insight into the dynamics of this fermionic system, it is essential to analyse the average number of fermions within the system. Represented as
\left<\hat{n}(t)\right>
, this quantity can be obtained by studying the time evolution of the system’s density matrix, yielding

\displaystyle\left<\dot{\hat{n}}\right>(t)

\displaystyle=\left<\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}\dot{\hat{\rho}}(t)\right>

\displaystyle=-\left(\gamma_{e}+\gamma_{c}\right)\left(\left<\hat{n}\right>(t)-\frac{\gamma_{e}\bar{n}_{e}+\gamma_{c}\bar{n}_{c}}{\gamma_{e}+\gamma_{c}}\right)~.

(5)

Solving this differential equation gives

\displaystyle\left<\hat{n}\right>(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma_{e}\bar{n}_{e}+\gamma_{c}\bar{n}_{c}}{\gamma_{e}+\gamma_{c}}+\left(\left<\hat{n}(0)\right>-\frac{\gamma_{e}\bar{n}_{e}+\gamma_{c}\bar{n}_{c}}{\gamma_{e}+\gamma_{c}}\right)e^{-(\gamma_{e}+\gamma_{c})t}~.

(6)

This statement concisely illustrates the evolution of the average number of fermions over time, taking into consideration the damping effects controlled by
\gamma_{e}
and
\gamma_{c}
as well as the initial state of the system, represented as
\left<\hat{n}(0)\right>
.

To proceed, we analyse the steady-state number density, symbolised by
\bar{n}_{s}
, which sheds light on the system’s long-term behavior. By setting
\left<\dot{\hat{n}}\right>(t)=0
in eq. (
5
), the steady-state value is obtained as

\displaystyle\bar{n}_{s}=\frac{\gamma_{e}\bar{n}_{e}+\gamma_{c}\bar{n}_{c}}{\gamma_{e}+\gamma_{c}}~.

(7)

Interestingly, this result can also be reformulated as

\displaystyle\gamma_{e}(\bar{n}_{e}-\bar{n}_{s})=\gamma_{c}(\bar{n}_{s}-\bar{n}_{c})~.

(8)

Equation (
7
) expresses the steady-state number density, illustrating how the system stabilizes over time. Meanwhile, eq. (
8
) highlights the equilibrium established between the emitter and collector reservoirs, with
\gamma_{e}
and
\gamma_{c}
playing critical roles. This equation reflects the flux balance achieved in steady-state conditions.

In this framework, we then explore the idea of current.
\frac{\gamma_{e}\gamma_{c}}{\gamma_{e}+\gamma_{c}}(\bar{n}_{e}-\bar{n}_{c})
is a rewrite of the left-hand side of eq. (
8

\gamma_{e}(\bar{n}_{e}-\bar{n}_{s})
. This form demonstrates the inherent connection between the resulting quantum current and the imbalance in the emitter and collector reservoirs. Interestingly, a well-known result in the literature is the factor
\frac{\gamma_{e}\gamma_{c}}{\gamma_{e}+\gamma_{c}}

[
15
,
40

\displaystyle I(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left[\gamma_{e}\left({\bar{n}_{e}}-\left<\hat{n}\right>(t)\right)+\gamma_{c}\left(\left<\hat{n}\right>(t)-{\bar{n}_{c}}\right)\right]~.

(9)

Substituting
\left<\hat{n}\right>(t)
from eq. (
6

\displaystyle I(t)

\displaystyle=I_{s}+\left(I_{0}-I_{s}\right)e^{-\left(\gamma_{e}+\gamma_{c}\right)t}

(10)

where
I_{0}
and
I_{s}
are given by

\displaystyle I_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left[\gamma_{e}\left({\bar{n}_{e}}-\left<\hat{n}\right>(0)\right)+\gamma_{c}\left(\left<\hat{n}\right>(0)-{\bar{n}_{c}}\right)\right]~;

(11)

\displaystyle I_{s}

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma_{e}\gamma_{c}}{\gamma_{e}+\gamma_{c}}(\bar{n}_{e}-\bar{n}_{c})~.

(12)

When
\bar{n}_{e}=1
and
\bar{n}_{c}=0
, this is perfectly in line with the relation found in
[
15
]
, which is
I_{s}=\frac{\gamma_{e}\gamma_{c}}{\gamma_{e}+\gamma_{c}}
. This demonstrates that the current has a comparable nature even when the coupling constants between the fermionic system and the fermionic bath are not Grassmanian as shown in
[
15

III
quantum transport factor

We now follow the technique used in
[
22
]
to define quantum transport factor. We introduce the concept of steady-state energy loss per unit time, denoted as
E_{s}
, which is a key parameter characterizing the system’s behavior. This energy loss is defined as

\displaystyle E_{s}

\displaystyle=\hbar\omega_{s}I_{s}

\displaystyle=\hbar\omega_{s}\frac{\gamma_{e}\gamma_{c}}{\gamma_{e}+\gamma_{c}}(\bar{n}_{e}-\bar{n}_{c})

(13)

The energy released from the system to the environment at equilibrium is denoted by
E_{s}
.

The energy the system gets from the emitter per unit of time, represented by
Q
, is then taken into account. This amount can be expressed as

Q=f(\gamma_{e},\gamma_{c},T_{e},T_{c})\hbar\omega_{s}\bar{n}_{e}

(14)

Here,
f(\gamma_{e},\gamma_{c},T_{e},T_{c})
has dimensions of
time^{-1}
. The quantity
Q
indicates the energy loss per unit time from the emitter due to the physical processes occurring within the system, and it is proportional to the energy of the fermions in the emitter reservoir.

The quantum transport factor
\eta_{s}
offers important insights into the system’s performance. It is defined as

\displaystyle\eta_{s}

\displaystyle=\frac{E_{s}}{Q}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{f}\frac{\gamma_{e}\gamma_{c}}{\gamma_{e}+\gamma_{c}}\left(1-\frac{\bar{n}_{c}}{\bar{n}_{e}}\right)~.

(15)

The above expression’s high-temperature limit will now be investigated. From eq.(s) ((
20
), (
15
)), we may formulate the high-temperature quantum transport factor as

\displaystyle\eta_{s}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{f}\frac{\gamma_{e}\gamma_{c}}{\gamma_{e}+\gamma_{c}}\left(1-\frac{e^{\frac{\hbar\omega_{s}}{k_{B}T_{e}}}+1}{e^{\frac{\hbar\omega_{s}}{k_{B}T_{c}}}+1}\right)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{f}\frac{\gamma_{e}\gamma_{c}}{\gamma_{e}+\gamma_{c}}\left(1-\frac{2+\frac{\hbar\omega_{s}}{k_{B}T_{e}}}{2+\frac{\hbar\omega_{s}}{k_{B}T_{c}}}\right)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{f}\frac{\gamma_{e}\gamma_{c}}{\gamma_{e}+\gamma_{c}}\left[1-\left(1+\frac{\hbar\omega_{s}}{2k_{B}T_{e}}\right)\left(1-\frac{\hbar\omega_{s}}{2k_{B}T_{c}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{f}\frac{\gamma_{e}\gamma_{c}}{\gamma_{e}+\gamma_{c}}\frac{\hbar\omega_{s}}{2k_{B}T_{c}}\eta_{c}

(16)

where
\eta_{c}
represents the well-known Carnot engine efficiency given by

\displaystyle\eta_{c}=1-\frac{T_{c}}{T_{e}}~.

(17)

Our next step is to determine the form of
f
. This can be accomplished by observing that the expression for
\eta_{s}
must correspond to the well-known Carnot efficiency in the high-temperature limit. This condition directly leads to the conclusion that
f
must equal
\frac{\gamma_{e}\gamma_{c}}{\gamma_{e}+\gamma_{c}}\frac{2k_{B}T_{c}}{\hbar\omega}
. As a result, the steady-state quantum transport factor can be expressed as

\displaystyle\eta_{s}

\displaystyle=\frac{2k_{B}T_{c}}{\hbar\omega}\left(1-\frac{\bar{n}_{c}}{\bar{n}_{e}}\right)~.

(18)

This demonstrates unequivocally that the quantum transport factor has an upper constraint of unity.

Earlier in
[
22
]
, it has been shown that the quantum transport factor for a bosonic system connected with two bosonic thermal reservoir becomes

\displaystyle\eta_{b}

\displaystyle=1-\frac{\bar{n}_{c}^{b}}{\bar{n}_{e}^{b}}

(19)

where
\bar{n}_{c}^{b}
and
\bar{n}_{e}^{b}
denotes the bosonic thermal reservoir known as collector and emitter number density respectively. The expressions read

\displaystyle\bar{n}_{e}^{b}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{e^{\frac{\hbar\omega_{s}}{k_{B}T_{e}}}-1}

(20a)

\displaystyle\bar{n}_{c}^{b}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{e^{\frac{\hbar\omega_{s}}{k_{B}T_{c}}}-1}~.

(20b)

Interestingly the bosonic quantum transport factor also matches Carnot efficiency given in eq. (
17
) while temperature goes to infinity. But at low temperature the bosonic transport factor and fermionic transport factors are quite different in nature. The results are plotted in Fig.
1
.

Power spectrum of current

From eq. (
9
), we found the current to be

\displaystyle I(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left[\gamma_{e}\left({\bar{n}_{e}}-\left<\hat{n}\right>(t)\right)+\gamma_{c}\left(\left<\hat{n}\right>(t)-{\bar{n}_{c}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left<\left(\gamma_{e}\bar{n}_{e}-\gamma_{c}\bar{n}_{c}\right)\hat{\mathbb{I}}-\left(\gamma_{e}-\gamma_{c}\right)\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}\right>

\displaystyle\hat{\iota}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\left[\left(\gamma_{e}\bar{n}_{e}-\gamma_{c}\bar{n}_{c}\right)\hat{\mathbb{I}}-\left(\gamma_{e}-\gamma_{c}\right)\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}\right]~.

\displaystyle=-\left(\gamma_{e}+\gamma_{c}\right)\left(\hat{\iota}-I_{s}\hat{\mathbb{I}}\right)

Following the prescription presented in

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\begin{pmatrix}\hat{\iota}\\
I_{s}\hat{\mathbb{I}}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}-\gamma_{e}-\gamma_{c}&\gamma_{e}+\gamma_{c}\\

Comparing the standard formulation we can identify

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{A}}(t)=\begin{pmatrix}\hat{\iota}(t)\\

to write

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\hat{\mathcal{A}}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{M}\hat{\mathcal{A}}(t)~.

(26)

For
\tau>0
, one gets from eq. (

) as

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\tau}\left<\hat{\iota}(t)\hat{\mathcal{A}}(t+\tau)\right>

\displaystyle=\mathcal{M}\left<\hat{\iota}(t)\hat{\mathcal{A}}(t+\tau)\right>~.

25

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\tau}\left<\hat{\iota}(t)\hat{\iota}(t+\tau)\right>

\displaystyle=I_{s}^{2}+\left(\left<\hat{\iota}^{2}(t)\right>-I_{s}^{2}\right)e^{-\left(\gamma_{e}+\gamma_{c}\right)\tau}+\left(\gamma_{e}+\gamma_{c}\right)\left(I_{s}I_{0}-I_{s}^{2}\right)\tau e^{-\left(\gamma_{e}+\gamma_{c}\right)\tau}~.

\displaystyle=\mathcal{M}\left<\hat{\mathcal{A}}(t+\tau)\hat{\iota}(t)\right>~.

\displaystyle\left<\hat{\iota}(t+\tau)\hat{\iota}(t)\right>

\displaystyle=I_{s}^{2}+\left(\left<\hat{\iota}^{2}(t)\right>-I_{s}^{2}\right)e^{-\left(\gamma_{e}+\gamma_{c}\right)\tau}+\left(\gamma_{e}+\gamma_{c}\right)\left(I_{s}I_{0}-I_{s}^{2}\right)\tau e^{-\left(\gamma_{e}+\gamma_{c}\right)\tau}~.

\displaystyle\left<\hat{\iota}(0)\hat{\iota}(\tau)\right>

]

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial}{\partial\gamma}\left[\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-\gamma|\tau|}e^{i\omega\tau}d\tau\right]

\displaystyle=2\frac{\gamma^{2}-\omega^{2}}{\left(\gamma^{2}+\omega^{2}\right)^{2}}

and

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{i\omega\tau}d\tau

\displaystyle=2\pi\delta(\omega)

P-REPRESENTATION AND FOKKER-PLANCK EQUATION FOR Fermionic SYSTEM

In this section we aim to calculate the coherent state representation of the density matrix, known as Glauber-Sudarshan P-representation
[
11
]
. Fermionic creation and annihilation operators, unlike their bosonic counterparts, adhere to the following anticommutation relations, ensuring that the particles conform to Fermi-Dirac statistics

\displaystyle\left\{{a}_{i},{a}^{\dagger}_{j}\right\}=\delta_{ij}

(37a)

\displaystyle\left\{{a}_{i},{a}_{j}\right\}=\left\{{a}^{\dagger}_{i},{a}^{\dagger}_{j}\right\}=0

(37b)

Fermions have only two possible energy eigenstates. Designating these as number states, the fermionic step-down and step-up operators act on them as follows

\displaystyle{a}\left|n\right>

\displaystyle=\sqrt{n}\left|1-n\right>

(38a)

\displaystyle{a}^{\dagger}\left|n\right>

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1-n}\left|1-n\right>

(38b)

Given the unique nature of fermions, it is not possible to define a fermionic coherent state using ordinary numbers. Therefore, we adopt the prescription of
[
41
,
11
,
16
]
in this work. The normalized fermionic coherent state is thus defined as

\displaystyle|\xi\rangle

\displaystyle=D(\xi)|0\rangle

(39)

Here,
D(\xi)
represents the spin displacement operators, defined as

\displaystyle D(\xi)

\displaystyle=e^{{a}^{\dagger}\xi-\xi^{*}{a}}

\displaystyle=1+\left({a}^{\dagger}\xi-\xi^{*}{a}\right)+\left({a}^{\dagger}{a}-\frac{1}{2}\right)\xi^{*}\xi

(40)

Clearly,
\xi
-s are not ordinary numbers. They are said to be the Grassmann numbers. For any mode
i
,
\xi_{i}
and
\xi_{i}^{*}
are Grassmann numbers for the corresponding mode, obeying the following anticommutation relations

\displaystyle\left\{\xi_{i},\xi_{j}\right\}

\displaystyle=0

(41a)

\displaystyle\left\{\xi_{i}^{*},\xi_{j}\right\}

\displaystyle=0

(41b)

\displaystyle\left\{\xi_{i}^{*},\xi_{j}^{*}\right\}

\displaystyle=0~.

(41c)

We also assume the anticommutation of Grassmann numbers
\xi_{i}
and
\xi_{i}^{*}
with the operators
{a}
and
{a}^{\dagger}
as

\displaystyle\left\{\xi_{i},a_{j}\right\}

\displaystyle=0

(42a)

\displaystyle\left\{\xi_{i}^{*},a_{j}\right\}

\displaystyle=0

(42b)

\displaystyle\left\{\xi_{i},a_{j}^{\dagger}\right\}

\displaystyle=0

(42c)

\displaystyle\left\{\xi_{i}^{*},a_{j}^{\dagger}\right\}

\displaystyle=0~.

(42d)

Then the properties of displacement operator becomes evident. We can write

\displaystyle{D}^{\dagger}(\xi){D}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{I}

(43a)

\displaystyle{D}^{\dagger}(\xi){a}{D}(\xi)

\displaystyle=({a}+\xi)

(43b)

\displaystyle{D}^{\dagger}(\xi){a}^{\dagger}{D}(\xi)

\displaystyle=({a}^{\dagger}+\xi^{*})

(43c)

Now from eq. (
39
), we can write the coherent state as

\displaystyle\left|\xi\right>

\displaystyle=D(\xi)\left|0\right>

\displaystyle=\left[1+\left({a}^{\dagger}\xi-\xi^{*}{a}\right)+\left({a}^{\dagger}{a}-\frac{1}{2}\right)\xi^{*}\xi\right]\left|0\right>

\displaystyle=\left[1+{a}^{\dagger}\xi-\frac{1}{2}\xi^{*}\xi\right]\left|0\right>~.

(44)

In second equation we used the identity
a\left|0\right>=0
. Now we can write for coherent states

\displaystyle a\left|\xi\right>

\displaystyle=aD(\xi)\left|0\right>

\displaystyle=D(\xi)D^{\dagger}(\xi)aD(\xi)\left|0\right>

\displaystyle=D(\xi)({a}+\xi)\left|0\right>

\displaystyle=\xi D(\xi)\left|0\right>

\displaystyle=\xi\left|\xi\right>

(45)

and

\displaystyle a^{\dagger}\left|\xi\right>

\displaystyle=a^{\dagger}\left[1+{a}^{\dagger}\xi-\frac{1}{2}\xi^{*}\xi\right]\left|0\right>

\displaystyle=\left[1-\frac{1}{2}\xi^{*}\xi\right]a^{\dagger}\left|0\right>

\displaystyle=\left[1-\frac{1}{2}\xi^{*}\xi\right]\left\{-\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}\left(1+a^{\dagger}\xi\right)\right\}\left|0\right>

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}\left(1+{a}^{\dagger}\xi-\frac{1}{2}\xi^{*}\xi\right)\left|0\right>

\displaystyle\hskip 22.76219pt+\frac{\xi^{*}}{2}\left(1+{a}^{\dagger}\xi-\frac{1}{2}\xi^{*}\xi\right)\left|0\right>

\displaystyle=\left(-\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}+\frac{\xi^{*}}{2}\right)\left|\xi\right>

(46)

where we used the identities like
(a^{\dagger})^{2}=\xi^{2}=(\xi^{*})^{2}=0
in the above steps. Similarly we can write for the conjugate cases as

\displaystyle\left<\xi\right|a^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\xi^{*}\left<\xi\right|

(47)

\displaystyle\left<\xi\right|a

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi^{*}}+\frac{\xi}{2}\right)\left<\xi\right|~.

(48)

Now like above expressions, we can rederive the results found in
[
16
]
as follows

\displaystyle a\left|\xi\right>\left<\xi\right|

\displaystyle=\xi\left|\xi\right>\left<\xi\right|

(49a)

\displaystyle a^{\dagger}\left|\xi\right>\left<\xi\right|

\displaystyle=\left(-\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}+\xi^{*}\right)\left|\xi\right>\left<\xi\right|

(49b)

\displaystyle\left|\xi\right>\left<\xi\right|a^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\xi^{*}\left|\xi\right>\left<\xi\right|

(49c)

\displaystyle\left|\xi\right>\left<\xi\right|a

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi^{*}}+\xi\right)\left|\xi\right>\left<\xi\right|~.

(49d)

These results will be necessary to calculate the Fokker-Planck equation from eq. (
3
). From Glauber and Cahil’s prescription, we adopt the
P
-representation to express the density matrix as

\displaystyle\rho

\displaystyle=-\int d\xi^{*}d\xi P(\xi^{*},\xi,t)\left|\xi\right>\left<\xi\right|~.

(50)

Now we need to incorporate two rules for Grassmann variables before computing the Fokker-Planck equation.
\theta
being a Grassmann variable, we can define the integration as

\displaystyle\int d\theta

\displaystyle=0

(51a)

\displaystyle\int\theta d\theta

\displaystyle=1

(51b)

and the Taylor series expansion of any function containing Grassman variable can be written as

\displaystyle f(\theta)

\displaystyle=f_{0}+f_{1}\theta

(52)

where
f_{0}
and
f_{1}
are the ordinary constants.

We can also define the Dirac delta function with Grassmanian variables. We know that the delta function has following property
[
42
]

\displaystyle\int d^{2}\xi\delta^{(2)}(\xi,\xi^{*})=1~.

(53)

Then it is possible to define the delta function if and only if

\displaystyle\delta^{(2)}~(\xi,\xi^{*})

\displaystyle=\xi\xi^{*}~.

(54)

Since the probability distribution
P(\xi^{*},\xi,t)
must be an even function on both
\xi
and
\xi^{*}
, it must be of the form

\displaystyle P(\xi^{*},\xi,t)

\displaystyle=P_{0}(t)+P_{1}(t)\xi^{*}\xi~.

(55)

Now using the above expressions, we can write eq. (
3
) in the following form

\displaystyle\int d^{2}\xi\frac{\partial P}{\partial t}\left|\xi\right>\left<\xi\right|

\displaystyle=\int d^{2}\xi P[\mathrm{I}+\mathrm{II}]\left|\xi\right>\left<\xi\right|

(56)

where

\displaystyle\mathrm{I}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\gamma_{e}+\gamma_{c}}{2}\left(\xi\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}+\xi^{*}\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi^{*}}+4\xi^{*}\xi\right)

(57)

and

\displaystyle\mathrm{II}

\displaystyle=\left(\gamma_{e}\bar{n}_{e}+\gamma_{c}\bar{n}_{c}\right)\left(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\xi^{*}\partial\xi}-\xi\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}-\xi^{*}\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi^{*}}-2\xi^{*}\xi\right)~.

(58)

Putting the results of eq.(s) ((
57
),(
58
)) in eq.(
56
), we get by comparing both sides

\displaystyle\int d^{2}\xi\frac{\partial P}{\partial t}\left|\xi\right>\left<\xi\right|=-\frac{\gamma_{e}+\gamma_{c}}{2}

\displaystyle\int d^{2}\xi\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}(\xi P)+\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi^{*}}(\xi^{*}P)+4\xi^{*}\xi P\right)\left|\xi\right>\left<\xi\right|

\displaystyle+\left(\gamma_{e}\bar{n}_{e}+\gamma_{c}\bar{n}_{c}\right)\int d^{2}\xi\left(\frac{\partial^{2}P}{\partial\xi^{*}\partial\xi}-\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}(\xi P)-\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi^{*}}(\xi^{*}P)-2\xi^{*}\xi P\right)\left|\xi\right>\left<\xi\right|~.

(59)

Now we can look upon each term in eq. (
59
). For that we remember eq. (
55
), and then we get for the first term

\displaystyle\int d^{2}\xi\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}(\xi P)\left|\xi\right>\left<\xi\right|

\displaystyle=\int d^{2}\xi\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}(\xi P_{0}(t))\left|\xi\right>\left<\xi\right|

\displaystyle=P_{0}(t)\int d^{2}\xi\left|\xi\right>\left<\xi\right|

\displaystyle=P_{0}(t)~.

(60)

The second term of the above equation reads

\displaystyle\int d^{2}\xi\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi^{*}}(\xi^{*}P)\left|\xi\right>\left<\xi\right|

\displaystyle=\int d^{2}\xi\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi^{*}}(\xi^{*}P_{0}(t))\left|\xi\right>\left<\xi\right|

\displaystyle=P_{0}(t)\int d^{2}\xi\left|\xi\right>\left<\xi\right|

\displaystyle=P_{0}(t)~,

(61)

and the final term

\displaystyle\int d^{2}\xi\frac{\partial^{2}P}{\partial\xi^{*}\partial\xi}\left|\xi\right>\left<\xi\right|

\displaystyle=\int d^{2}\xi\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\xi^{*}\partial\xi}\left(P_{0}(t)+P_{1}(t)\xi^{*}\xi\right)\left|\xi\right>\left<\xi\right|

\displaystyle=-P_{1}(t)~.

(62)

where we used the completeness relation

\displaystyle\int d^{2}\xi\left|\xi\right>\left<\xi\right|

\displaystyle=1~.

(63)

We use this completeness relation again to solve the last term in eq. (
59
). We write

\displaystyle\int d^{2}\xi~\xi^{*}\xi P\left|\xi\right>\left<\xi\right|

\displaystyle=\int d^{2}\xi~\xi^{*}\xi P\int d^{2}\chi\left|\chi\right>\left<\chi\mid\xi\right>\left<\xi\right|~.

(64)

The last integration over Grassmanian variable
\chi
only survives if
\xi=\chi
. Then from eq. (
64
), we get

\displaystyle\int d^{2}\xi~\xi^{*}\xi P\left|\xi\right>\left<\xi\right|

\displaystyle=\int d^{2}\xi~\xi^{*}\xi P

\displaystyle=\int d^{2}\xi~\xi^{*}\xi\left(P_{0}(t)+P_{1}(t)\xi^{*}\xi\right)

\displaystyle=-P_{0}~.

(65)

The differential equation then comes down from eq. (
55
) to the form

\displaystyle\int d^{2}\xi\frac{\partial P}{\partial t}\left|\xi\right>\left<\xi\right|=\alpha P_{1}(t)+\beta P_{0}(t)~.

(66)

where
\alpha=\left(\gamma_{e}\bar{n}_{e}+\gamma_{c}\bar{n}_{c}\right)
and
\beta=\gamma_{e}+\gamma_{c}
are constants.

Now let us look upon the initial conditions and steady state conditions. In the steady state we know

\displaystyle\frac{\partial P}{\partial t}=0~.

(67)

Hence we get from eq. (
66
)

\displaystyle\alpha P_{1}^{eq}+\beta P_{0}^{eq}=0~.

(68)

Again we can use the normalisation condition for the equilibrium as

\displaystyle\int d^{2}

\displaystyle\xi P(\xi,\xi^{*},t)=1

\displaystyle\int d^{2}

\displaystyle\xi P_{1}(t)\xi^{*}\xi=1

\displaystyle P_{1}(t)=-1~.

(69)

Then at the steady state, comparing eq.(s) (
68
),(
69
), we get

\displaystyle P_{0}^{eq}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\alpha}{\beta}=\frac{\gamma_{e}\bar{n}_{e}+\gamma_{c}\bar{n}_{c}}{\gamma_{e}+\gamma_{c}}~.

(70)

Again, we can impose the initial condition as we did for the bosonic case. Initially, the distribution is considered located at a particular point, thus yielding a delta function. So the initial probability distribution will be

\displaystyle P^{in}(\xi,\xi^{*},t=0)=\delta^{(2)}~(\xi,\xi^{*})=\xi\xi^{*}.

(71)

Thus the initial value for
P_{0}
and
P_{1}
are

\displaystyle P_{0}(0)

\displaystyle=0~;

(72)

\displaystyle P_{1}(0)

\displaystyle=-1~.

(73)

Now let us chose a smooth differentiable function
f(t)
such that,

\displaystyle P_{0}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{\beta}f(t)

(74)

\displaystyle P_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=-1

(75)

From the boundary condition we can ensure that
f(0)=0
and
f(\infty)=1
.

Also we can write the term

\displaystyle\int d^{2}\xi\frac{\partial P}{\partial t}\left|\xi\right>\left<\xi\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int d^{2}\xi\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(P_{0}(t)-\xi^{*}\xi\right)\left|\xi\right>\left<\xi\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dot{P}_{0}(t)

(76)

where we used the facts that
\int d^{2}\xi\left|\xi\right>\left<\xi\right|=1
and
P_{1}(t)=-1
. Hence the differential equation (
66
) becomes

\displaystyle\dot{P}_{0}(t)

\displaystyle=-\alpha+\beta P_{0}(t)

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\frac{df}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\alpha+\alpha f(t)

\displaystyle f(t)

\displaystyle=1-e^{-\beta t}~.

(77)

Hence we have

\displaystyle P_{0}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\left(1-e^{-\beta t}\right)

(78)

\displaystyle P_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=-1~.

(79)

Thus, the
P
representation becomes

\displaystyle P(\xi,\xi^{*},t)=\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\left(1-e^{-\beta t}\right)-\xi^{*}\xi~.

(80)

For a consistency check, we can find the steady state condition, that is
t\to\infty
, as

\displaystyle P(\xi,\xi^{*},t\to\infty)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{\beta}-\xi^{*}\xi

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{\beta}e^{-\beta\xi^{*}\xi/\alpha}~.

(81)

Clearly, the steady state distribution is a Gaussian distribution, as one finds for the bosonic case.

VI
Conclusion

The dynamics of a fermionic oscillator connected to two fermionic reservoirs,
T_{e}
and
T_{c}
, each with a different temperature, are examined in this article. With an emphasis on current flow, the quantum transport factor, and the power spectrum, we investigated a number of crucial facets of the system’s behavior. We were able to characterize the development of the system and identify important relationships, including the current flow from the emitter to the collector, utilizing the flux balance condition by obtaining the master equation for the density operator under the Markovian approximation.

In order to evaluate the effectiveness of energy transmission within the system, we created the quantum transport factor. Our findings demonstrated that, despite certain parallels with classical efficiency measures, the quantum transport factor operates differently in fermionic systems. In particular, we found that, in contrast to the behavior seen in bosonic systems, the quantum transport factor can be less than the classical Carnot efficiency, especially at low temperatures. Our knowledge of quantum transport in fermionic systems is significantly expanded by this discovery.

Additionally, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of the power spectrum of the current by employing well-established techniques to compute the current-current correlation function. This approach enabled us to gain deeper insights into the frequency-dependent behavior of the current fluctuations and how they evolve with temperature. By systematically varying the temperature and examining the resulting changes in the power spectrum, we were able to identify key trends and underlying physical mechanisms governing the system’s response. Our findings exhibited a strong qualitative agreement with previous studies, further reinforcing the validity of our methodology and confirming the robustness of our results. This consistency with prior work highlights the reliability of our approach and suggests that the techniques we employed can be effectively used to explore similar systems in future investigations.

Finally, in order to better comprehend the probability distribution of particles in the system, we extended our analysis by obtaining the Fokker-Planck equation. We were able to better manage the intricacy of fermionic states by using Grassmann algebra for fermionic operators, particularly when working with non-steady-state situations. Additionally, our work showed that steady-state results from previous research may be recovered by the time-dependent solution of the Fokker-Planck equation, bridging the gap between steady-state and non-steady-state analysis.

In conclusion, this work provides a thorough examination of fermionic transport dynamics, illuminating the complex behavior of quantum systems connected to fermionic reservoirs. Our results pave the way for further research in open quantum systems and their useful applications in quantum technologies by providing important new information on temperature, transport parameters, and quantum efficiency in fermionic systems.
