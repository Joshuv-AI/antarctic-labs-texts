Abstract
. We provide the first known upper bounds for the packing dimension of weighted singular and weighted ω \omega -singular matrices. We also prove upper bounds for these sets when intersected with fractal subsets. The latter results, even in the unweighted setting, are already new for matrices. Further, even for row vectors, our results enlarge the class of fractals for which bounds are currently known. We use methods from homogeneous dynamics, in particular we provide upper bounds for the packing dimension of points on the space of unimodular lattices, whose orbits under diagonal flows p p -escape on average.

1.
Introduction

Singular matrices are a class of vectors for which Dirichlet’s theorem in Diophantine approximation can be infinitely improved. In this paper, we will be concerned with a more general setting, that of weighted singular matrices, to study which we need to introduce quasi-norms. Fix
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
,
a=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
b=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})
such that

(1)

\displaystyle a_{1}\geq a_{2}\geq\cdots\geq a_{m}>0,

\displaystyle\quad b_{1}\geq b_{2}\geq\cdots\geq b_{n}>0,

(2)

\displaystyle a_{1}+\cdots+a_{m}=1,

\displaystyle\quad b_{1}+\cdots+b_{n}=1.

We define a
quasi-norm

\|.\|_{a}
on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
as
\|x\|_{a}=\max_{i}|x_{i}|^{1/a_{i}}
for all
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Similarly, define a quasi-norm
\|.\|_{b}
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
as
\|y\|_{b}=\max_{j}|y_{j}|^{1/b_{j}}
for all
y=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Definition 1.1
.

A matrix
\theta\in\mathrm{M}_{m\times n}(\mathbb{R})
is called
(a,b)
-singular
if for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
T_{\varepsilon}>0
such that for all
T>T_{\varepsilon}
, there exists
(p,q)\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}\times(\mathbb{Z}^{n}\setminus\{0\})
such that the following holds

\displaystyle\|p+\theta q\|_{a}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{T},

\displaystyle\|q\|_{b}

\displaystyle\leq T.

We denote by
\mathrm{Sing}(a,b)\subset\mathrm{M}_{m\times n}(\mathbb{R})
, the set of all
(a,b)
-singular vectors in
\mathrm{M}_{m\times n}(\mathbb{R})
.

Definition 1.2
.

We define the
(a,b)
-uniform exponent of irrationality of a matrix
\theta\in\mathrm{M}_{m\times n}(\mathbb{R})
, denoted
\omega(\theta,a,b)
, as the supremum of
\omega>0
such that for all large
T
, there exists
(p,q)\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}\times(\mathbb{Z}^{n}\setminus\{0\})
such that the following holds

\displaystyle\|p+\theta q\|_{a}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{T^{1+\omega}}

\displaystyle\|q\|_{b}

\displaystyle\leq T.

We call a matrix
\theta\in\mathrm{M}_{m\times n}(\mathbb{R})
, an
(a,b,\omega)
-singular
matrix (we say
\theta\in\mathrm{Sing}(a,b,\omega)
) if
\omega(\theta,a,b)\geq\omega
.

We refer the reader to
[
2
]
for a comprehensive study of uniform Diophantine exponents. In the unweighted setting,
\omega
-singular matrices have been considered previously, cf.
[
1
,
7
,
15
]
. From Minkowski’s convex body Theorem, it is easy to see that
\mathrm{Sing}(a,b,0)=\mathrm{M}_{m\times n}(\mathbb{R})
. Moreover, if
{\omega}>0
, then
\mathrm{Sing}(a,b,\omega)\subset\mathrm{Sing}(a,b)
.

Although the study of the classical ‘non-weighted’ situation is more ubiquitous, Diophantine approximation with weights has been extensively studied in recent times. We refer the reader to
[
6
]
for an introduction. The set of singular vectors is clearly nonempty, and due to a classical result of Khintchine, it contains uncountably many vectors when the dimension is greater than
1
. Moreover, a modification of a classical argument in
[
3
]
shows that the set of singular vectors has zero Lebesgue measure.

The question of estimating the Hausdorff dimension of singular vectors turns out to be a difficult problem and has received much attention in the last two decades. In a landmark work
[
5
]
, Y. Cheung showed that the dimension of
\mathrm{Sing}((1/2,1/2),1)\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is
4/3
. This was subsequently generalized to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
in an important work of Cheung and Chevallier
[
4
]
. Another important result was obtained by Kadyrov, Kleinbock, Lindenstrauss and Margulis in
[
11
]
using methods from homogeneous dynamics. Namely, a sharp upper bound on the more general set of
singular on average

m\times n
matrices was obtained using integral inequalities as introduced in the famous work
[
8
]
on the Oppenheim conjecture. The complementary lower bound was obtained by Das, Fishman, Simmons and Urbański in
[
7
]
using methods from the parametric geometry of numbers, see also the recent paper
[
17
]
for more results in this direction. In
[
16
]
, Shah and Yang obtained bounds for the dimension of certain singular vectors lying on affine subspaces.

Following a research direction suggested by Mahler, the topic of metric Diophantine approximation on fractals has recently witnessed a surge in popularity. In
[
1
]
, the authors (Problem 6) pose the following question: “What is the Hausdorff dimension of the set of singular pairs whose entries belong to the middle third Cantor set?”
Clearly, this question can be framed in much wider generality. In
[
12
]
, Khalil upgraded the integral inequality approach of
[
11
]
using a beautiful argument to deal with singular vectors on fractals. That is, an upper bound on the Hausdorff dimension of singular vectors lying on self-similar fractals in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
that satisfied the open set condition was obtained. The only other progress that we are aware of is the work of Schleischitz
[
15
]
where
lower bounds
on the packing dimension of singular vectors lying on a certain specific class of fractals were obtained.

As for weighted singular vectors, in
[
14
]
, the Hausdorff dimension of weighted singular vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
was calculated. In higher dimensions, a lower bound was recently obtained by Kim and Park in
[
13
]
using an appropriate generalization of the technique of
[
14
]
that involves the construction of a fractal set contained in
\mathrm{Sing}(a,1)
. In general, this question is wide open and of considerable interest as pointed out, for instance in Section 5.7 of
[
7
]
.

In this paper, we provide a general method for obtaining upper bounds on the packing dimension of singular and, more generally,
(a,b,\omega)
singular vectors in a wide variety of contexts. We consider matrices and also permit intersections with fractals. Even in the unweighted setting, we expand the current known scope of Problem 6 of Bugeaud, Cheung and Chevalier mentioned above, by including more fractals. We can now state our main theorem.

Theorem 1.3
.

Fix
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
a=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
b=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfy conditions (
1
) and (
2
). For
1\leq l\leq d-1
, define
w_{l}
as follows:

w_{l}=\begin{cases}a_{m}+\cdots+a_{m+1-l},&\text{if }l\leq m,\\
1-(b_{1}+\cdots+b_{l-m}),&\text{if }l>m.\end{cases}

For each
1\leq i\leq m
and
1\leq j\leq n
, let
\Phi_{ij}
be an iterated function system (IFS) consisting of contracting similarities on
\mathbb{R}
with equal contraction ratios, satisfying the open set condition. Let
\mathcal{K}_{ij}
be the limit set of
\Phi_{ij}
, and define

\mathcal{K}=\{\theta\in\mathrm{M}_{m\times n}(\mathbb{R}):\theta_{ij}\in\mathcal{K}_{ij}\text{ for all }i,j\}.

Assume that
\dim_{H}(\mathcal{K}_{ij})>0
for all
i,j
. Then there exist constants
\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{m+n-1}>0
(depending only on
\mathcal{K}
) such that the following results hold:

•

The packing dimension of
\mathrm{Sing}(a,b)\cap\mathcal{K}
satisfies:

(3)

\displaystyle\dim_{P}(\mathrm{Sing}(a,b)\cap\mathcal{K})\leq\dim_{P}(\mathcal{K})-\frac{1}{a_{1}+b_{1}}\left(\min_{1\leq l\leq d-1}\eta_{l}w_{l}\right).

•

For any
\omega>0
, the packing dimension of
\mathrm{Sing}(a,b,\omega)\cap\mathcal{K}
satisfies:

(4)

\displaystyle\dim_{P}(\mathrm{Sing}(a,b,\omega)\cap\mathcal{K})\leq\dim_{P}(\mathcal{K})-\frac{1}{a_{1}+b_{1}}\left(\min_{1\leq l\leq d-1}\eta_{l}w_{l}+\frac{\eta_{1}a_{m}b_{n}\omega}{a_{m}+b_{n}+a_{m}\omega}\right).

Moreover, the constants
\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{m+n-1}
can be explicitly chosen in the following cases:

(1)

If
\mathcal{K}=\mathrm{M}_{m\times n}([0,1])
, we can take:

(5)

\displaystyle\eta_{l}=\begin{cases}\frac{m}{l},&\text{if }l\leq m,\\
\frac{n}{m+n-l},&\text{if }l>m.\end{cases}

(2)

If
n=1
, we can take:

(6)

\displaystyle\eta_{l}=\frac{m}{l}\min_{1\leq i\leq m}\dim_{H}(\mathcal{K}_{i1}).

(3)

If
m=1
, we can take:

(7)

\displaystyle\eta_{l}=\frac{n}{n+1-l}\min_{1\leq j\leq n}\dim_{H}(\mathcal{K}_{1j}).

Remark 1.4
.

Note that the packing dimension of any set is larger than the Hausdorff dimension of the set. Thus (
3
) and (
4
) also give an upper bound for the Hausdorff dimension of the respective sets.

Remark 1.5
.

As will be shown in the proof, the constants
\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{m+n-1}\in\mathbb{R}
in Theorem
1.3
, can be chosen as any point in the following closed region:

\displaystyle 0<\eta_{i}

\displaystyle\leq\zeta_{i}(\mu)\quad\text{for all }1\leq i\leq d-1,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\eta_{i-j}}+\frac{1}{\eta_{i+j}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\eta_{i}}\text{ for all }1\leq i\leq m+n-1,j\leq\min\{i,m+n-i\},

where
1/\eta_{0}=1/\eta_{m+n}:=0
and
\mu
equals product of normalised
\dim(\mathcal{K}_{ij})
-dimensional Hausdorff measure on
\mathcal{K}_{ij}
. The definitions of
\zeta_{1}(\mu),\ldots,\zeta_{m+n-1}(\mu)
are given in Section
3
. These constants encode information about whether the
\mathcal{K}
is close to certain hypersurfaces. For
m=1
or
n=1
, these hypersurfaces degenerate to coordinate planes, enabling non-trivial lower bounds for
\zeta_{1}(\mu),\ldots,\zeta_{d-1}(\mu)
, see Section
4
. Similarly, explicit computations are possible when
\mathcal{K}=\mathrm{M}_{m\times n}([0,1])
.

One of the main ingredients of the proof of Theorem
1.3
is Dani’s correspondence, which gives that
(a,b)
-singular matrices corresponds to certain divergent trajectories in the space
\mathcal{X}_{m+n}
of unimodular lattices in
\mathbb{R}^{m+n}
. More precisely, let

(8)

\displaystyle g_{t}=\begin{pmatrix}t^{a_{1}}\\
&\ddots\\
&&t^{a_{m}}\\
&&&t^{-b_{1}}\\
&&&&\ddots\\
&&&&&t^{-b_{n}}\\
\end{pmatrix},\quad u(\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}I_{m}&\theta\\
&I_{n}\end{pmatrix}.

for
t>0
and
\theta\in\mathrm{M}_{m\times n}(\mathbb{R})
. Then
\theta
is
(a,b)
-singular if and only if the diagonal orbit
(g_{t}u(\theta)\mathbb{Z}^{d})_{t\geq 1}
is divergent. A different way of quantifying the notion of singularity is the notion of singularity on average introduced in
[
11
]
. Given
x\in\mathcal{X}_{m+n}
and
0<p\leq 1
, we define
\mathrm{Divergent}_{a,b}(x,p)
as the set of all
\theta\in\mathrm{M}_{m\times n}(\mathbb{R})
such that

\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\liminf_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\delta_{g_{e^{t}}u(\theta)x}\{y\in\mathcal{X}:\lambda_{1}(y)\leq\varepsilon\}\,dt\geq p,

where
\lambda_{1}(y)
denotes the length of the shortest non-zero vector in
y
. It is then clear and will also be shown in Lemma
7.1
that
\mathrm{Sing}(a,b)\subset\mathrm{Divergent}_{a,b}(\mathbb{Z}^{m+n},1)
. Similarly, as will be shown in Lemma
7.2
, if
\theta
is
(a,b,\omega)
-singular then for all
\omega^{\prime}<\omega
, there exists
T(\omega^{\prime})
such that for all
t>T(\omega^{\prime})
, we have

\lambda_{1}(g_{t}u(\theta)\mathbb{Z}^{d})<t^{\frac{-a_{m}b_{n}\omega^{\prime}}{a_{m}+b_{n}+a_{m}\omega^{\prime}}}

for all large
t
. Thus, Theorem
1.3
follows from the following more general theorem.

Remark 1.7
.

The proof of Theorem
1.6
closely follows the framework developed in
[
11
]
. However, the setting of fractal measures and weighted dynamics introduces several additional challenges. To explain these, we divide the proof into two main parts.

The first part involves constructing a family of height functions on the space of unimodular lattices, tailored to satisfy the contraction hypothesis for fractal measures. This construction relies on establishing an expansion property for representations and using the integral inequalities introduced in
[
8
]
. Proving the expansion property for fractal measures constitutes the first key contribution of the paper.

The second part applies the contraction hypothesis for the height function iteratively to obtain an upper bound on exceptional trajectories. Although this approach is inspired by
[
11
]
, the setting with unequal weights presents significant technical difficulties. The unequal weights result in uneven expansion on
\mathrm{M}_{m\times n}(\mathbb{R})
under conjugation, making the iterative process particularly delicate, especially when dealing with fractal measures. To address these difficulties, we prove the contraction hypothesis not only for the original fractal measure but also for measures arising from small perturbations. Furthermore, the arguments require careful handling of heavy notation and intricate calculations to ensure the validity of the iterative process. These iterative arguments represent the second key contribution of this paper.

2.1.
Packing Dimension

The
i
-dimensional packing measure of a set
F\subset\mathbb{R}^{l}
is defined as

(14)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{i}(F):=\inf\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\tilde{\mathcal{P}}^{i}(F_{i}):F\subset\bigcup_{j=1}^{\infty}F_{j}\right\},

where

(15)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{P}}^{i}(F)=\inf_{\varepsilon>0}\sup\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}|B_{j}|^{i}:\begin{subarray}{c}(B_{j})_{1}^{\infty}\text{ is a countable disjoint collection of balls }\\
\text{ with centers in $F$ and with diameter $|B_{j}|<\varepsilon$ for all $j$.}\end{subarray}\right\}

The packing dimension of a set
F\subset\mathbb{R}^{l}
is defined as

(16)

\displaystyle\dim_{P}(F)=\inf\{i:\mathcal{P}^{i}(F)=0\}=\sup\{i:\mathcal{P}^{i}(F)=\infty\}.

We will need the following important lemma.

Lemma 2.1

(
[
9
, Lem. 3.8]
)
.

Let
F
be a non-empty bounded subset of
\mathbb{R}^{l}
. Then

\dim_{P}(F)\leq\limsup_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\frac{\log L_{\delta}(F)}{-\log\delta},

where
L_{\delta}(F)
denotes the smallest number of sets of diameter at most
\delta
that cover
F
.

2.3.
Iterated Function Systems

A contracting similarity is a map
\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
of the form
x\mapsto cx+y
where
c\in(0,1)
, and
y\in\mathbb{R}
. A
finite similarity Iterated Function System
with constant ratio (
IFS
) on
\mathbb{R}
is a collection of contracting similarities
\Phi=(\phi_{e}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R})_{e\in E}
indexed by a finite set
E
, called the alphabet, such that there exists a constant
c\in(0,1)
independent of
e
so that

\phi_{e}(x)=cx+w_{e},

for all
e\in E
.

Let
B=E^{\mathbb{N}}
. The coding map of an IFS
\Phi
is the map
\sigma:B\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined by the formula

(18)

\displaystyle\sigma(b)=\lim_{l\rightarrow\infty}\phi_{b_{1}}\circ\cdots\circ\phi_{b_{l}}(0).

It is well known that the limit in
(
18
)
exists and that the coding map is continuous. The image of
B
under the coding map, called the limit set of
\Phi
, is a compact subset of
\mathbb{R}
, which we denote by
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{K}(\Phi)
. We define for
{\tilde{e}}=(e_{1},\ldots,e_{l})\in E^{l}
,

(19)

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\tilde{e}}=\phi_{e_{1}}\circ\cdots\circ\phi_{e_{l}}(\mathcal{K}),\quad\text{ and set }\mathcal{F}(l)=\{\mathcal{K}_{{\tilde{e}}}:{\tilde{e}}\in E^{l}\}.

We will say that
\Phi
satisfies the
open set condition
(OSC for short) if there exists a non-empty open subset
U\subset\mathbb{R}
such that the following holds

\displaystyle\phi_{e}(U)\subset U

\displaystyle\text{ for every }e\in E

\displaystyle\phi_{e}(U)\cap\phi_{e^{\prime}}(U)=\emptyset,

\displaystyle\text{ for every }e\neq e^{\prime}\in E.

Let
\mathrm{Prob}(E)
denote the space of probability measures on
E
. For each
\nu\in\mathrm{Prob}(E)
we can consider the measure
\sigma_{*}\nu^{\otimes\mathbb{N}}
under the coding map. A measure of the form
\sigma_{*}\nu^{\otimes\mathbb{N}}
is called a
Bernoulli measure
.

The following proposition is well known (see for e.g.
[
10
, Prop. 5.1(4), Thm. 5.3(1)]
for a proof).

Proposition 2.2
.

Suppose
\Phi=\{\phi_{e}:e\in E\}
is an IFS satisfying the open set condition with the limit set
\mathcal{K}
. Let
c
denote the common contraction ratio of
(\phi_{e})_{e\in E}
and
p=\#E
. Then the Hausdorff dimension
s
of
\mathcal{K}
is
-\log p/\log c
. Also, the
s
-dimensional Hausdorff measure
H^{s}
satisfies
0<H^{s}(\mathcal{K})<\infty
. Moreover if
\mu
denotes the normalised restriction of
H^{s}
to
\mathcal{K}
, then
\mu
is a Bernoulli measure and equals
\sigma_{*}\nu^{\otimes\mathbb{N}}
, where
\nu
is the uniform measure on
E
, i.e,
\nu(F):=\#F/\#E
for all
F\subset E
. Additionally, for every
l\in\mathbb{N}
and distinct sequences
{\tilde{e}}_{1}\neq{\tilde{e}}_{2}\in E^{l}
, we have
\mu(\mathcal{K}_{{\tilde{e}}_{1}}\cap\mathcal{K}_{{\tilde{e}}_{2}})=0
. Furthermore, there exists a constant

\mu
in the following cases.

4.1.
The Matrix Case

In this case, we assume that
\mathcal{K}=\mathrm{M}_{m\times n}([0,1])
and work with the dyadic model.
That is, for all
1\leq i\leq m
and
1\leq j\leq n
, we assume that
\Phi_{ij}
contains the following two contracting similarities:
x\mapsto x/2
and
x\mapsto(x+1)/2
. The main goal of this subsection is to prove the following lemma.

Lemma 4.1
.

If
\mathcal{K}=\mathrm{M}_{m\times n}([0,1])
, then

\zeta_{l}(\mu)\geq\begin{cases}\frac{m}{l}\quad\text{if }l\leq m,\\
\frac{n}{m+n-l}\quad\text{if }m<l\leq d-1.\end{cases}

To prove this lemma, we will use some results from
[
11
]
. First, we define the following for
t\in\mathbb{R}
:

(43)

\displaystyle h_{t}=\begin{pmatrix}e^{nt}I_{m}&0\\
0&e^{-mt}I_{n}\end{pmatrix}.

Now, we state the required results from
[
11
]
:

Proposition 4.2

(
[
11
, Prop. 3.1]
)
.

For any
l\in\{1,\ldots,d-1\}
, define
\beta_{l}
as follows:

\beta_{l}=\begin{cases}\frac{m}{l}&\text{if }l\leq m,\\
\frac{n}{m+n-l}&\text{if }l>m.\end{cases}

Then, there exists a constant
c>0
(depending only on
m
and
n
) such that for any
t\geq 1
and any
v=v_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge v_{l}\in\wedge^{l}\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

\int_{{\mathrm{SO}}(d)}\|h_{t}kv\|^{-\beta_{l}}\,dk\leq cte^{-mnt}\|v\|^{-\beta_{l}}.

Lemma 4.3

(
[
11
, Lem. 3.5]
)
.

There exists a neighborhood
V
of the identity in
\mathrm{M}_{m\times n}(\mathbb{R})
such that for any
s_{0}\in\mathrm{M}_{m\times n}(\mathbb{R})
,
t,\beta>0
,
l\in\{1,\ldots,d-1\}
, and a vector
v=v_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge v_{l}\in\wedge^{l}\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

\int_{V+s_{0}}\|h_{t}u(\theta)v\|^{-\beta}\,d\theta\ll(1+\|s_{0}\|)^{\beta}\int_{K}\|h_{t}kv\|^{-\beta},

where the implied constant depends only on
m
,
n
, and
\beta
.

Using the above results, we can now prove the lemma
4.1
.

Proof of Lemma
4.1
.

For
1\leq l\leq d-1
, define

\beta_{l}=\begin{cases}\frac{m}{l}&\text{if }l\leq m,\\
\frac{n}{m+n-l}&\text{if }l>m.\end{cases}

Now fix
1\leq l\leq d-1
and
v=v_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge v_{l}\in\wedge^{l}\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\|v\|=1
. Also, fix
0<\varepsilon<1
and let
t>1
be such that
e^{t(m+n)}\varepsilon=K
, where
K=\|\{u(\theta):\theta\in\mathrm{M}_{m\times n}([0,2])\}\|
. Let us define the set
E(v,\varepsilon)=\{\theta\in\mathrm{M}_{m\times n}(\mathbb{R}):\|\pi_{l+}(u(\theta)v)\|<\varepsilon\}
.

Now, let
V
be a neighbourhood of the identity in
\mathrm{M}_{m\times n}(\mathbb{R})
such that Lemma
4.3
holds. Let
s_{0},\ldots,s_{p}\in\mathrm{M}_{m\times n}(\mathbb{R})([0,2])
be a finite set such that
\mathrm{M}_{m\times n}(\mathbb{R})([0,2])\subset\cup_{i=1}^{p}(V+s_{i})
. From the definition of
\mu^{(r)}
, it is easy to see that
\operatorname{supp}(\mu^{(r)})\subset\mathrm{M}_{m\times n}([0,2])
. Thus, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathrm{M}_{m\times n}(\mathbb{R})}\|h_{t}u(\theta)v\|^{-\beta_{l}}\,d\mu^{(r)}(\theta)

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{i=0}^{p}\int_{V+s_{i}}\|h_{t}u(\theta)v\|^{-\beta_{l}}\,d\theta

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{i=0}^{p}(1+\|s_{i}\|)^{\beta_{l}}\int_{K}\|h_{t}kv\|^{-\beta_{l}}\,dk\quad\text{(using Lemma \ref{KKLM Lem 3.5})}

\displaystyle\ll\int_{K}\|h_{t}kv\|^{-\beta_{l}}\,dk

(44)

\displaystyle\ll te^{-mnt}\|v\|^{-\beta_{l}}=te^{-mnt},

where the implied constant depends only on
m,n
.

Next, for all
\theta\in E(v,\varepsilon)\cap\mathrm{M}_{m\times n}([0,2])
, we have the following estimates:

\displaystyle\|h_{t}u(\theta)v\|

\displaystyle=\max\left\{|(h_{t}u(\theta)v)_{I}|:\#I=l\right\}

\displaystyle=\max\left\{e^{(\#(I\cap\{1,\ldots,m\})nt)-(\#(I\cap\{m+1,\ldots,d\})mt)}|(u(\theta)v)_{I}|:\#I=l\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\max\Bigg\{e^{\frac{mnt}{\beta_{l}}}\|\pi_{l+}(u(\theta)v)\|,\;e^{\frac{mnt}{\beta_{l}}-(m+n)t}|(u(\theta)v)_{I}|:\#(I\cap\{1,\ldots,m\})<\min\{l,m\}\Bigg\}

\displaystyle\leq\max\Bigg\{e^{\frac{mnt}{\beta_{l}}}\varepsilon,\;e^{\frac{mnt}{\beta_{l}}}\frac{\varepsilon}{K}\|u(\theta)\|\|v\|\Bigg\}

\displaystyle\leq e^{\frac{mnt}{\beta_{l}}}\varepsilon.

Thus, we conclude that

E(v,\varepsilon)\cap\mathrm{M}_{m\times n}([0,2])\subset\{\theta\in\mathrm{M}_{m\times n}(\mathbb{R}):\|h_{t}u(\theta)v\|<e^{\frac{mnt}{\beta_{l}}}\varepsilon\}\quad\text{for all }r\in\Xi.

Using (
44
) and applying Markov’s inequality, we obtain the following bound for all
\rho<\beta_{l}
:

\displaystyle\mu^{(r)}(E(v,\varepsilon))

\displaystyle\leq e^{mnt}\varepsilon^{\beta_{l}}\int_{\mathrm{M}_{m\times n}(\mathbb{R})}\|h_{t}u(\theta)v\|^{-\beta_{l}}\,d\mu^{(r)}(\theta)

\displaystyle\ll e^{mnt}\varepsilon^{\beta_{l}}te^{-mnt}=t\varepsilon^{\beta_{l}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(\log K-\log\varepsilon)}{m+n}\varepsilon^{\beta_{l}}

\displaystyle\ll\varepsilon^{\rho},

where the implied constant depends only on
m,n,\rho
. Thus, by Lemma
3.5
, we conclude that
\zeta_{l}(\mu)\geq\rho
. Since this holds for all
\rho<\beta_{l}
, the lemma follows.
∎

4.2.
Case
n=1

Lemma 4.4
.

For
n=1
, we have
\zeta_{l}(\mu)\geq\min\{\sum_{i\in I}s_{i1}:\#I=d-l\}
for all
1\leq l\leq d-1
.

Proof.

Fix
1\leq l\leq d-1
and define
\gamma=\min\left\{\sum_{i\in I}s_{i1}:\#I=d-l\right\}
. Fix
v=v_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge v_{l}\in\wedge^{l}\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\|v\|=1
. For all
0<\epsilon<1
, define the set

E(v,\epsilon)=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}:\|\pi_{+}(u(x)v)\|<\epsilon\right\}.

Let

K=\left\|\left\{u(x):x\in\bigcup_{r\in\Xi}\operatorname{supp}(\mu^{(r)})\right\}\right\|.

Since
\mu
has compact support, it follows that
K<\infty
. Additionally, let
\lambda>0
be sufficiently large so that for all
r\in\Xi
and
1\leq i\leq m
, the following holds for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
:

(45)

\displaystyle\mu^{(r)}_{i1}([x-y,x+y])\leq\lambda y^{s_{i1}},

where the existence of such a
\lambda
follows from Proposition
2.2
and the definition of
\mu^{(r)}_{ij}
.

To estimate
\mu^{(r)}(E(v,\epsilon))
, we consider two cases:

Case 1:

\|\pi_{l-}(v)\|\leq\frac{1}{2K}
. In this case, we have
\|\pi_{l+}(v)\|=\|v\|=1
. Thus, for
x\in\bigcup_{r\in\Xi}\operatorname{supp}(\mu^{(r)})
, we get

\|\pi_{l+}(u(x)v)\|\geq\|\pi_{l+}(u(x)\pi_{l+}(v))\|-\|\pi_{l+}(u(x)\pi_{l-}(v))\|\geq\|\pi_{l+}(v)\|-\|u(x)\pi_{l-}(v)\|\geq 1-K\cdot\frac{1}{2K}=\frac{1}{2}.

Therefore, in this case, we have

\mu^{(r)}(E(v,\epsilon))\leq 2^{\gamma}\epsilon^{\gamma}.

Case 2:

\|\pi_{l-}(v)\|>\frac{1}{2K}
. In this case, there exists a subset
\{m+1\}\subset I\subset\{1,\ldots,m+1\}
of cardinality
l
such that
|v_{I}|\geq\frac{1}{2K}
. Let us define, for each
i\notin I
, the set

I_{i}=I\cup\{i\}\setminus\{m+1\}.

Define the set

E^{\prime}(v,\epsilon)=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}:\text{for all }i\notin I,\ |(\pi_{+}(u(x)v))_{I_{i}}|<\epsilon\right\}.

It is clear that
E(v,\epsilon)\subset E^{\prime}(v,\epsilon)
.

Now, to estimate
\mu^{(r)}(E^{\prime}(v,\epsilon))
, we note that by explicit computation, for every
J\subset\{1,\ldots,m\}
, the following holds:

(\pi_{+}(u(x)v))_{J}=v_{J}+\sum_{j\in J}\mathbf{e}_{j,J}v_{J\cup\{m+1\}\setminus\{j\}}x_{j1},

for some
\mathbf{e}_{j,J}\in\{\pm 1\}
. Thus, for all
i\notin I
, we have

(\pi_{+}(u(x)v))_{I_{i}}=\mathbf{e}_{i,I_{i}}v_{I}x_{i1}+\left(v_{I_{i}}+\sum_{j\in I\setminus\{m+1\}}\mathbf{e}_{j,I_{i}}v_{I_{i}\cup\{m+1\}\setminus\{j\}}x_{j1}\right).

If we fix
x_{j1}
for
j\in I\setminus\{m+1\}
, then for all
i\notin I
and
\epsilon>0
, the condition
|(\pi_{+}(u(x)v))_{I_{i}}|<\epsilon
is equivalent to the condition that
x_{i1}
belongs to an interval of size at most
\frac{\varepsilon}{|v_{I}|}<2K\epsilon
, which has
\mu^{(r)}_{i1}
-measure less than
\lambda(2K\epsilon)^{s_{i1}}
(using equation (
45
)). By Fubini’s theorem, we then obtain

\mu^{(r)}(E(v,\epsilon))\leq\mu^{(r)}(E^{\prime}(v,\varepsilon))\leq\lambda^{m+1-l}(2K)^{\sum_{i\notin I}s_{i1}}\varepsilon^{\sum_{i\notin I}s_{i1}}\leq\lambda^{m+1-l}(2K)^{\sum_{i\notin I}s_{i1}}\varepsilon^{\gamma}.

Thus, by Lemma
3.2
, we conclude that
\zeta_{l}(\mu)\geq\gamma
. This completes the proof of the lemma.

∎

6.
The Contraction Hypothesis

Definition 6.1

(The Contraction Hypothesis)
.

Suppose
Y
is a metric space equipped with a continuous action of
G
. Given a collection of functions
\{f_{\tau}:Y\rightarrow[0,\infty]:\tau\in S\}
for some unbounded set
S\subset(0,\infty)
and
\beta>0
, we say that
\mu
satisfies the
((f_{\tau})_{\tau},\beta)
-
contraction hypothesis
on
Y
if the following properties hold:

(1)

The set
Y_{f}=\{y\in Y:f_{\tau}(y)=\infty\}
is independent of
\tau

Lemma 6.4

Suppose
Y
is a locally compact second countable metric space equipped with a continuous
G
action. Given a closed
G
-invariant subset
Y^{\prime}\subset Y
,
0<q<p\leq 1
and

, then for all compact subsets

and

, there exists

such that for every

\frac{1}{M}\#\{l\in[1,M]\cap\mathbb{N}:g_{t}^{l}u(x)y\notin K\}\geq q.

Proof.

Fix
x\in\mathrm{Div}(y,Y^{\prime},p)
and a compact set
K\subset Y\setminus Y^{\prime}
. Note that it is enough to show that

(58)

\displaystyle\limsup_{M\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{M}\#\{l\in[1,M]\cap\mathbb{N}:g_{t}^{l}u(x)y\in K\}\leq 1-p.

To prove (
58
), define
K^{t}=\cup_{\tau=t^{-1}}^{1}g_{\tau}K
. Clearly if
g_{t}^{l}u(x)y\in K
, then
g_{\tau}u(x)y\in K^{t}
for all
\tau\in[t^{l-1},t^{l}]

\displaystyle\limsup_{M\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{M}\#\{l\in[1,M]\cap\mathbb{N}:g_{t}^{l}u(x)y\in K\}

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{M\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{M}\sum_{l=1}^{M}\frac{1}{\log t}\int_{(l-1)\log t}^{l\log t}\delta_{g_{e^{\tau}}u(x)y}(K^{t})\,d\tau

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\delta_{g_{e^{\tau}}u(x)y}(K^{t})\,d\tau

\displaystyle\leq 1-p,

where last inequality follows from the definition of
\mathrm{Div}(y,Y^{\prime},p)
. Hence (
58
) holds. This proves the lemma.

Lemma 7.1
.

Proof.

By definition,
