Socioeconomic factors, error positivity, and performance monitoring in children
Authors: Jordan Strack, William J Gavin, Angélica C Heredia-Flores, Cristina Nava Rios, Patricia L Davies, Emily C Merz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSocioeconomic disadvantage has been robustly linked with lower academic achievement in children. These associations may be partially attributable to the effects of socioeconomic disadvantage on error monitoring neural activity in children. The error positivity (Pe) is an event-related potential component that reflects higher-order neural processing involved in recognizing and evaluating errors. In this study, we examined the associations among socioeconomic factors (family income-to-needs ratio, parental educational attainment), Pe amplitude, and parent-reported performance monitoring in typically developing children. Participants were 5- to 13-year-olds (N = 108; 57% male; 13% Hispanic/Latine; 75% White, non-Hispanic/Latine) from a range of socioeconomic backgrounds. Electroencephalography (EEG) data were collected while children completed a speeded arrow flanker task. Parents completed the Task-Monitor scale of the Behavior Rating Inventory of Executive Function, Second Edition. Results indicated that lower parental education was significantly associated with reduced Pe amplitudes in children, independent of age and flanker task error rate. Reduced Pe amplitudes were significantly associated with higher levels of performance monitoring difficulties in children. Family income-to-needs ratio was not associated with Pe amplitude in children. These findings suggest that lower socioeconomic resources are associated with altered neural processing following errors in children. These differences at the neural level could underlie behavioral differences children exhibit in everyday settings, such as home and school.Keywords: Family income, Parental education, Neural function, Cognitive control, Error processing, Flanker task

Introduction

Socioeconomic disadvantage consistently predicts reduced academic achievement in children and adolescents (Bradley and Corwyn, 2002, Duncan et al., 2017, Liu et al., 2022). These associations are partially attributable to socioeconomic differences in neural function in children and adolescents (Farah, 2017, Merz et al., 2019, Rosen et al., 2018). Performance monitoring is crucial to academic success but has received less attention in this literature. Performance monitoring includes noticing mistakes and adjusting attention and behavior following mistakes in order to reach goals (error monitoring) (Coles et al., 2001, Gehring et al., 1993). Error monitoring is believed to support self-regulation by signaling the need for greater cognitive control (executive function) to meet task demands. Performance monitoring skills emerge and start to develop during early childhood, undergoing a protracted developmental course. Developmental advances in performance monitoring skills have been found to support learning processes in academic settings (Hirsh and Inzlicht, 2010, Kim et al., 2016, Roebers, 2017). Through effects on children’s proximal environments, socioeconomic disadvantage (e.g., lower family income, parental educational attainment) may influence the neural development of error monitoring. Yet, these associations are not well understood. Thus, one main goal of this study was to examine the associations between socioeconomic factors and neural processing following errors in children.

Error monitoring event-related potential components

Event-related potential (ERP) methods, with their high temporal resolution, are well suited to studying neural function during cognitive task performance. Response-locked ERPs reflect the summed activity of multiple neural processes associated with response preparation, execution, and evaluation, and therefore contain several components. Among these, the main error monitoring ERP components are the error-related negativity (ERN) and error positivity (Pe) (Falkenstein et al., 1991, Gehring et al., 1993). The response-locked ERP produces the ERN around 50–100 ms after an error followed by the Pe, which is a slow positive deflection peaking around 200–500 ms after an error (Ullsperger et al., 2014). These ERP components reflect independent or dissociable systems of earlier vs. later error monitoring, respectively (Boen et al., 2022, Di Gregorio et al., 2018). The Pe is centro-parietally distributed on the scalp and has also been examined at frontal-central sites (Boen et al., 2022, Laurens et al., 2010, Lin et al., 2020, Lin et al., 2022).

In this study, we focused on the Pe, which as a later (as opposed to earlier) ERP component after the commission of an error, may be more closely related to higher-order cognitive processing stages (Marsh et al., 2023). The Pe is thought to reflect conscious awareness of errors, attention allocation to errors, and post-error behavioral adjustments (Endrass et al., 2007, Ficarella et al., 2019, Murphy et al., 2012, Nieuwenhuis et al., 2001, Overbeek et al., 2005, Wessel et al., 2011). In studies of error awareness, Pe amplitudes have been consistently found to be larger for aware errors compared to unaware errors (Endrass et al., 2012, Hughes and Yeung, 2011, Logan et al., 2015, Murphy et al., 2012, Nieuwenhuis et al., 2001, O’Connell et al., 2007, Shalgi et al., 2009, Steinhauser and Yeung, 2010). Despite less conclusive evidence on the neural source of the Pe, findings suggest that it may originate from the anterior cingulate (Herrmann et al., 2004, O’Connell et al., 2007, van Boxtel et al., 2005, van Veen and Carter, 2002), prefrontal, and parietal cortices (Masina et al., 2019, van Veen and Carter, 2002). Greater Pe amplitudes have been linked with heightened inhibitory control (Marsh et al., 2023) and academic achievement in children (Kim et al., 2016).

In some studies, the Pe as described above has been referred to as the late Pe and distinguished from an earlier Pe component (Arbel and Donchin, 2009). The early Pe is thought to overlap with processes reflected in the ERN, whereas the late Pe has been found to be more dissociable from the ERN (O’Connell et al., 2007, Thurm et al., 2020, Ullsperger et al., 2014, van Veen and Carter, 2002). Thus, we focused on the late Pe (referring to it as the Pe) in our study given our focus on higher-order cognitive processing.

Socioeconomic factors and error positivity amplitudes in children

Socioeconomic disadvantage is expected to influence the development of error awareness and attention allocation to errors in children through multiple proximal environmental factors, such as reduced cognitive stimulation (e.g., environmental enrichment) and increased exposure to stressors (Farah, 2017). Socioeconomic disadvantage reflects decreased access to economic, human, and social resources (Bradley and Corwyn, 2002, Duncan et al., 2017, McLoyd, 1998). Socioeconomically disadvantaged families often have less time and fewer resources to use toward cognitive and language stimulation for children (e.g., quality of language input, trips to libraries and museums, early childhood education and care, learning opportunities in the home). As a result, children’s experiences are likely to be different in terms of how often errors are highlighted, how feedback is delivered, and how responding to mistakes is scaffolded during everyday interactions. In addition, parents with reduced economic resources often experience greater stress due in part to economic pressure. Stress levels can impact how parents respond in problem-solving situations with children, such as when children make mistakes while trying to complete an activity. Reduced cognitive stimulation and increased family stress have been associated with altered development of the cognitive skills needed for goal-directed behavior (e.g., executive function) (Hackman et al., 2015, Rosen et al., 2020, Suor et al., 2017, Vrantsidis et al., 2020). Thus, evidence suggests that socioeconomic conditions may shape children’s experiences in ways that influence their neural processing of errors during cognitive tasks.

The few studies on socioeconomic factors and error monitoring ERP components in children and adolescents have only focused on the ERN, and not the Pe. Of note, these studies of the ERN have yielded mixed results, with some finding significant associations (Brooker, 2018, Chong et al., 2020, Conejero et al., 2018, Raturi et al., 2025) and others not finding them (Meyer and Klein, 2018, Pirzada and Valadez, 2025). Yet, to our knowledge, there are no studies of socioeconomic differences in Pe amplitude in children or adolescents.

Commonly used socioeconomic measures, such as family income and parental educational attainment, are significantly correlated but also distinct from one another (Farah, 2017). These socioeconomic factors are thought to influence children’s cognitive development through partially overlapping but also different proximal mechanisms (Braveman et al., 2005, Duncan and Magnuson, 2012). For example, compared to low parental education, low family income has been more strongly associated with material hardship and increased parental stress. Compared to family income, parental education may be more strongly linked to the quality of language input in the home environment (Merz et al., 2020). Thus, it is recommended that socioeconomic factors be analyzed separately rather than combined into a socioeconomic status (SES) composite (Pollak and Wolfe, 2020). This approach allows for greater insight into potential proximal mechanisms and can yield results that are informative for policy and practice.

Associations between error positivity amplitudes and error monitoring

Associations between Pe amplitudes and behavioral measures of performance monitoring in children have been understudied. Although performance monitoring skills are often measured based on task performance (e.g., post-error slowing), they can also be reliably measured using rating scales, such as parent report questionnaires (Isquith et al., 2013, Jacobson et al., 2020, Toplak et al., 2013). Parent report measures offer information on children’s performance monitoring in their everyday lives, capturing real-world behavior in various activities outside of the lab. Yet, no studies have focused on associations between Pe amplitude and parent-reported performance monitoring in children.

Current study

The goal of this study was to examine the associations among socioeconomic factors, Pe amplitudes, and parent-reported performance monitoring in typically developing 5- to 13-year-olds from a range of socioeconomic backgrounds. We focused on this age range because it corresponds to a developmental window during which performance monitoring systems show maturation and are malleable to environmental influences (Boen et al., 2022, Roebers, 2017). This age range also corresponds to a time when interventions may be especially beneficial, positioning results to be applicable to a developmental period when interventions may be timely. Electroencephalography (EEG) data collected while children performed a speeded flanker task were used to measure Pe amplitude. Family income-to-needs ratio and parental education were examined separately.

We hypothesized that lower socioeconomic resources would be associated with error monitoring at the neural (lower Pe amplitude) and behavior levels (greater parent-reported task monitoring difficulties). Furthermore, we expected that associations at the neural level would be stronger than those at the behavioral level given the enhanced sensitivity of neural measures. We also expected that smaller Pe amplitudes would be associated with greater parent-reported performance monitoring difficulties.

Methods

Participants

In a medium-sized city in the western U.S., participants were recruited by posting flyers in the local community; conducting outreach with schools and childcare centers; visiting and connecting with local organizations; and posting on community-centered social media sites. Interested parents completed an eligibility phone call. Children were eligible if they were 5 – 13 years of age and fluent in English. Children were not eligible if they had been diagnosed with a neurological disorder, neurodevelopmental disorder (e.g., autism spectrum disorder), bipolar disorder, or schizophrenia; were the product of a multiple birth (e.g., twin, triplet); or were born preterm (< 37 gestational weeks at birth). Parents were eligible if they were fluent in either English or Spanish. During the eligibility phone call, to attain a socioeconomically diverse sample, parents were asked about their highest level of educational attainment (high school diploma or fewer years of education; some college; four years of college; or more than four years of college). Families were enrolled if the parent’s educational attainment fell into a bin for which the participation quota had not been reached (Merz et al., 2020).

Participants were 108 5- to 13-year-olds (57% male). Most children (75%) were reported to be White, non-Hispanic/Latine; 13% were Hispanic/Latine and 9% were Asian, non-Hispanic/Latine. Family income ranged from ≤ $999 to ≥ $300,000 annually, and parental educational attainment ranged from 11 to 22 years (see Table 1 for descriptive statistics on sample characteristics). Only one child per family was permitted to participate in this study.

Procedure

Parents and children completed one in-person 3-hour lab visit between 2021 and 2025. During the lab visit, parents first provided written informed consent, and children provided assent. Then, two trained research assistants took the child into an electrically shielded, sound-attenuating booth and fitted them with an EEG cap. After a 3-min artifact training period, children completed a speeded flanker task while EEG data were collected. During the lab visit, the parent completed a set of questionnaires. All study procedures were approved by the Colorado State University Institutional Review Board.

Socioeconomic measures

Family income-to-needs ratio

Parents reported on annual household income and the number of adults and children in the household. Parents reported income in bins which were recoded into the mean of each bin for analyses (Strack et al., 2026). Family income-to-needs ratio was computed by dividing total household income by the U.S. federal poverty threshold for the family size for the year they participated in the study. A higher income-to-needs ratio indicates greater economic resources.

Parental education

Parents reported years of educational attainment for themselves and the child’s second parental figure. The highest parental education in the household was used in analyses. As expected, family income-to-needs ratio was significantly and positively correlated with parental education (see Table 2).

Electrophysiological methods

EEG data were recorded with 64 electrodes from the Active Two BioSemi system (BioSemi Inc., Amsterdam, The Netherlands) according to the 10–20 electrode placement system. Two electrodes, the common mode sense (CMS) and the driven right leg (DRL), generated the reference voltage for the online EEG data collection. Signals collected from the left and right earlobes were collected to be averaged and used for the offline reference. Two other electrodes were placed above and below the participant’s left eye to track vertical eye movements. An additional two electrodes were placed on the left and right temple to track horizontal eye movements. The data was sampled at 1024 Hz.

Speeded flanker task

Children completed a speeded version of the Eriksen flanker task (Eriksen and Eriksen, 1974), which is the most widely used task for reliably eliciting the Pe (Imburgio et al., 2020). On each trial, five horizontally aligned arrows were presented, and children were asked to respond by pressing one of two buttons indicating the direction of the middle arrow, while ignoring the adjacent arrows. They pressed a button with their right index finger if the middle arrow pointed right or with their left index finger if the middle arrow pointed left. On congruent trials, all arrows point in the same direction (160 trials), while on incongruent trials, the middle arrow points in the opposite direction of the other arrows (320 trials). E-prime software (version 3) (Psychology Software Tools, Pittsburgh, PA) was used for task presentation. Participants completed a brief practice block of 12 trials before data collection began. There were 240 trials presented in two blocks, for a total of 480 trials. On each trial, the stimulus was presented rapidly, with a duration of 250 ms and an initial interstimulus interval (ISI) of 1400 ms.

The E-prime program reevaluated the error rate every 30 trials, allowing for the adjustment of the ISI by ±100 ms if the error rate was either greater than 30% or fewer than 10%, such that if the participant made too many errors, the presentation of stimuli slowed down with a longer ISI. And if the participant made too few errors, the presentation of stimuli sped up with a shorter ISI (Lin et al., 2020). The ISI parameters were set to not decrease below 800 ms, which allows sufficient time for the brain to process the stimulus before the next stimulus is presented, or increase above 2100 ms. Behavioral task performance measures included response time (RT) calculated for correct and incorrect trials, error count, and error rate. All children completed all 480 trials of the flanker task, except one child who completed 240 trials.

Pe waveform and component analysis

The EEG data were preprocessed using Brain Vision Analyzer (version 2.3) software (Brain Products GmbH, Gilching, Germany). Data were re-referenced to the averaged bilateral earlobe sites and filtered with a 0.1–30 Hz bandpass filter. Data were then segmented into response-locked segments. The duration of segments was 600 ms before an incorrect response to 800 ms following the incorrect response. These segments were then baseline corrected with the period of −600–400 ms prior to the incorrect response (Davies et al., 2004). Segments (i.e., trials) with RTs faster than 100 ms were excluded from ERP data analyses. Eye movement artifacts were removed using the vertical electrooculogram (VEOG) channel using customized MATLAB code, followed by a second baseline correction. Segments containing voltages greater than ±150 μV at the midline sites (Fz, FCz, Cz, Pz) were rejected. Pe amplitudes were derived from incorrect (error) trials only, collapsing across congruent/incongruent trial types.

Adaptive Woody filter

The Adaptive Woody filter (Harris and Woody, 1969, Woody, 1967) was used to adjust for trial-to-trial latency variability (i.e., latency jitter) in the ERPs on incorrect trials (Gavin et al., 2019, Lin et al., 2020). In the processing stages described above, the segments were saved and then called into the MATLAB Adaptive Woody filter program. Using a template from the individual’s averaged ERP, the Adaptive Woody filter program shifts each segment forward and backwards in time until each segment is maximally aligned to the template which includes the Pe component. Once this alignment has been found, the shifted segments are averaged to obtain a “latency-adjusted” averaged Pe. The Adaptive Woody filter procedure also provides summary measures of trial-to-trial variability of the Pe latency (Gavin et al., 2019). The Adaptive Woody filter removes the confound of trial-to-trial latency variability in an individual’s ERP, reducing measurement error in ERP analyses (Gavin et al., 2019).

Finally, the Pe amplitudes were measured on the Adaptive Woody filtered averaged ERP of incorrect trials for each child. The Pe component had an early and late positive wave, consistent with previous research (O’Connell et al., 2007, Thurm et al., 2020, Ullsperger et al., 2014, van Veen and Carter, 2002). Late Pe amplitude was used in analyses (see Figure S1). Scoring was completed in MATLAB utilizing a custom-built program that automatically selects the peak and latency of the peaks in a specified time window (Gavin, 2013). This program also allows visual inspection so that if the peak is on a slope at either end of a window, the peak measure can be moved manually to the proper location. The window for the late Pe peak was 220–600 ms. The window for the negative peak prior to the late Pe was 160–400 ms.

Computation of Pe amplitude

Peak-to-peak late Pe amplitudes were used in this study. The peak-to-peak amplitude was computed by subtracting the previous negative peak from the late Pe amplitude. The grand averages for response-locked ERP amplitudes are provided in Figure S1. Late Pe amplitude data scored from the FCz electrode site were used in analyses, consistent with previous work (Boen et al., 2022, Ding et al., 2023, Laurens et al., 2010, Lin et al., 2020, Lin et al., 2022). Children with a flanker task error rate higher than 40% (n = 7) were excluded from analyses to exclude those who may have been performing at random, consistent with previous studies (Compton et al., 2013, DuPuis et al., 2015, Mehra et al., 2022). Figure S2 is a diagram showing the inclusion and exclusion of participants illustrating the number of participants with Pe data included in analyses. There were no significant differences in family income-to-needs ratio (p = .07), parental education (p = .28), age (p = .26), sex (p = .86), or race/ethnicity (p = .27) between those with and without Pe amplitude data used in analyses.

Performance (task) monitoring

Parents reported on children’s task monitoring using the Behavior Rating Inventory of Executive Function, Second Edition (BRIEF2) (Gioia et al., 2015). The BRIEF2 is a 63-item questionnaire used to assess everyday behaviors associated with executive function. Parents respond to items asking about children’s behavior in the past 6 months. Items are rated on a 3-point scale, with options of 1 (never), 2 (sometimes), and 3 (often). The Task-Monitor scale (5 items), which measures difficulty noticing minor errors in work output (e.g., “Does not check work for mistakes”), was used for this study given its strong conceptual overlap with what the Pe measures at the neural level. Item scores were summed to calculate the raw score for the Task-Monitor scale. Higher scores indicate greater difficulty with task monitoring. The BRIEF2 has high reliability and validity, with strong internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha =.86) of the Task-Monitor scale in the standardization sample (Gioia et al., 2015, Jacobson et al., 2020). Internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha) was.87 for the Task-Monitor scale for our sample. One parent did not complete the BRIEF2.

Statistical analyses

Analyses were conducted using SAS (version 9.4). Paired sample t tests were used to compare average RTs during error and correct trials. Pearson correlation analyses were used to examine associations between flanker task performance and main study variables. Then, multiple linear regression analyses were conducted with Pe amplitude as the dependent variable and one socioeconomic factor (either family income-to-needs ratio or parental education) as the independent variable. Family income-to-needs ratio and parental education were analyzed as predictors in separate regression models. Similar regression analyses were conducted with parent-reported task monitoring as the dependent variable and one socioeconomic factor as the independent variable. Also, multiple linear regression analyses were conducted with parent-rated task monitoring as the dependent variable and Pe amplitude as the independent variable.

Child age was included as a covariate in all regression analyses. Child sex was not associated with Pe amplitude (p = .80) but was associated with parent-rated task monitoring (p = .01), so it was included in analyses of task monitoring but not Pe amplitude. Child race/ethnicity was examined as a categorical predictor of Pe amplitude and parent-reported task monitoring in separate linear regression models. Two categories (Black or African American, American Indian or Alaska Native) were not included in these analyses due to small cell size (< 3 participants, see Table 1). Race/ethnicity was not significantly associated with Pe amplitude, either in an unadjusted model, F(2, 90) = 1.05, p = .35, or when controlling for age, F(2, 89) = .72, p = .49. Similarly, race/ethnicity was not significantly associated with parent-reported task monitoring in an unadjusted model, F(2,99) = .11, p = .89, or when controlling for age, F(2,98) = .57, p = .56. Therefore, child race/ethnicity was not included as a covariate in analyses. Effect sizes (ηp2) are presented, with values of .01, .06, and .14 indicating small, medium, and large effects, respectively (Richardson, 2011). We also tested whether the correlation between socioeconomic resources and Pe amplitude was statistically different than the correlation between socioeconomic resources and parent-reported task monitoring using Meng, Rosenthal, and Rubin’s z test (Meng et al., 1992) via the cocor package in R (Diedenhofen and Musch, 2015).

Results

Descriptive statistics for socioeconomic factors, Pe amplitude, task monitoring, and flanker task performance are provided in Table 1. Zero-order correlations for these variables are provided in Table 2.

Flanker task performance

Paired sample t tests indicated that children exhibited longer RTs on correct trials compared to error trials, t(98) = 18.50, p < .001. After controlling for age and sex, lower parental education was significantly associated with a higher error rate on the flanker task (β = −.30, p = .002), but family income-to-needs ratio was not significantly associated with flanker task error rate (β = −.12, p = .20). After controlling for age and sex, parental education (β =.01, p = .83) and family income-to-needs ratio (β =.08, p = .19) were not associated with error trial RT. Also, after controlling for age and sex, parental education was significantly associated with correct trial RT (β =.13, p = .04), but family income-to-needs ratio was not significantly associated with correct trial RT (β =.09, p = .13). After controlling for age and sex, parental education (β =.07, p = .23) and family income-to-needs ratio (β =.09, p = .14) were not significantly associated with average (all trial) RT.

Socioeconomic factors and Pe amplitudes

Lower parental education was significantly associated with reduced Pe amplitudes in children while controlling for age, β = .31, p = .001, ηp2 = .10 (see Fig. 1), and this association remained significant after additionally controlling for flanker task error rate, β = .22, p = .02, ηp2 = .05. Family income-to-needs ratio was not significantly associated with Pe amplitudes in children (p = .61). The interaction between parental education and age was not significant (p = .66).

Socioeconomic factors and task monitoring

Neither parental education, β = -.12, p = .14, nor family income-to-needs ratio, β = -.09, p = .29, was significantly associated with parent-reported task monitoring while controlling for age and sex. The correlation between parental education and Pe amplitude was significantly greater than that between parental education and parent-rated task monitoring (z = 2.82, p = .002).

Sensitivity analyses

Parental education (r = -.14, p = .18) and family income-to-needs ratio (r = -.13, p = .22) were not significantly associated with mean ISI. In addition, parental education remained significantly associated with Pe amplitude after additionally controlling for mean ISI, β = .23, p = .02, ηp2 = .06. Also, regression analyses were conducted controlling for flanker task error count rather than flanker task error rate, and the results were the same. Specifically, lower parental education was significantly associated with reduced Pe amplitude in children while controlling for age and flanker task error count, β = .22, p = .02, ηp2 = .06.

Discussion

The goal of this study was to examine the associations among socioeconomic factors, error-related neural activity, and performance monitoring during middle childhood. Error monitoring supports self-regulation and learning from mistakes, which are crucial to academic success (Dubinsky and Hamid, 2024, Roebers, 2017, Zhang and Fiorella, 2023). Specifically, in this EEG study, we focused on the Pe component, which is later occurring after an error and reflects higher-order cognitive processing of errors. Examining both Pe amplitude and parent-reported performance monitoring allowed us to capture independent measures of performance monitoring at the neural and behavioral levels, respectively.

Socioeconomic factors and Pe amplitude

Lower parental education was significantly associated with reduced Pe amplitudes in children, with a medium effect size. This association remained significant after accounting for the number of error trials on the flanker task. These results are consistent with the few previous studies showing socioeconomic differences in ERN amplitude in toddlers (Conejero et al., 2018) and adolescents (Chong et al., 2020, Raturi et al., 2025), and in the early development of the ERN in preschoolers (Brooker, 2018). In line with this previous research, our findings point to a link between lower socioeconomic resources and blunted neural responses to errors in children. Furthermore, our results suggest that this pattern may be observable for a separate and later-occurring error monitoring ERP component, the Pe, in children. The Pe has a more consistent association with conscious error recognition compared to the ERN (Boen et al., 2022, Di Gregorio et al., 2018, Ficarella et al., 2019, Murphy et al., 2012, Nieuwenhuis et al., 2001, Overbeek et al., 2005). The Pe has also been associated with attention allocation to errors and the accumulation of evidence indicating error commission (Schroder et al., 2017). Several authors have suggested that the Pe is similar to the stimulus-locked P300 or P3b component (Davies et al., 2001, Endrass et al., 2012, Ridderinkhof et al., 2009). Thus, lower Pe amplitudes may reflect lower error awareness and attention allocation to errors, potentially making it more difficult to engage self-regulation to learn from errors and adjust behavior following errors.

Although parental education was significantly associated with Pe amplitude, family income-to-needs ratio was not. And as expected, parental education and family income-to-needs ratio were significantly and positively correlated with each other. These findings are consistent with previous work suggesting that different socioeconomic factors (components of SES) may have distinct patterns of associations with children’s cognitive and health outcomes (Braveman et al., 2005, Duncan and Magnuson, 2012, Farah, 2017, Pollak and Wolfe, 2020).

Lower parental education may be associated with reduced Pe amplitudes in children through multiple proximal factors. Educational attainment is linked with access to various forms of human and social capital that can affect parenting practices and the home environment, influencing children’s neural and cognitive development (Davis-Kean et al., 2021, Harding et al., 2015). Lower parental education has been associated with reduced cognitive and language stimulation and opportunities for enrichment (e.g., book reading, trips to museums and libraries, language input quality, conversational exchanges), which in turn are associated with lower scores on executive function tasks in children (Hackman et al., 2015, Merz et al., 2019, Merz et al., 2020, Rosen et al., 2020). These environmental factors may shape children’s neural responses to mistakes. Parents’ educational attainment may impact how they provide feedback and scaffold children’s error monitoring during tasks, potentially influencing Pe amplitude in children.

Performance monitoring begins to develop during early childhood when the home environment is central to children (Roebers, 2017). As children get older, in addition to the home environment, the school environment also plays a role in shaping children’s cognitive development. Children from under-resourced backgrounds often have reduced access to high-quality schools (Liu et al., 2022). Aspects of school and classroom quality, such as how teachers provide feedback and guidance in responding to mistakes, have been associated with the development of error monitoring in children (Steuer et al., 2013).

Pe amplitude and parent-reported performance monitoring

Reduced Pe amplitude was associated with higher levels of parent-reported performance monitoring difficulties in children, with a medium effect size. Research on performance monitoring and related cognitive skills often relies on measures based on task performance. Rating scale measures complement performance-based measures by capturing behavior in everyday settings outside of the lab (Isquith et al., 2013, Jacobson et al., 2020, Toplak et al., 2013). In the few previous studies of associations between Pe amplitude and executive function skills in children, results have varied (Brooker et al., 2011, Marsh et al., 2023). Results from the current study suggest that children with blunted Pe amplitudes may exhibit error monitoring difficulties in their everyday lives. Altered neural processing of errors may be associated with difficulties with error monitoring in real-world settings, including school.

The association between parental education and Pe amplitude was significantly greater than that between parental education and parent-reported performance monitoring in children. This pattern of results suggests that compared to performance monitoring at the behavioral level, Pe amplitude was more sensitive to variability in parental education. This result is consistent with previous research indicating that neural measures can reveal associations even when associations at the behavioral level are not found (Raizada and Kishiyama, 2010).

There was variability in children’s Pe amplitude at every level of parental education (see Fig. 1), consistent with findings from previous studies on socioeconomic differences in children’s neural development (Brooker, 2018). For example, some children from families with lower socioeconomic resources exhibited higher Pe amplitude, which could indicate resilience. Multiple genetic and environmental factors may moderate the associations between socioeconomic factors and neural processing of errors in children. Positive and protective factors such as parental warmth and high-quality schools may counteract the effects of socioeconomic disadvantage, supporting children’s performance monitoring skills. In addition, changes over time in socioeconomic resources may moderate these associations. Longitudinal designs are needed to examine these possibilities and investigate how socioeconomic factors may influence the development of error-related neural activity in children. Given that the children in our sample ranged from 5 to 13 years of age, we examined age as a moderator but did not find a significant interaction.

Strengths and limitations

This study has many strengths, including a sample recruited with socioeconomic diversity in mind. In addition, we employed well-established ERP methods involving a carefully designed, developmentally sensitive speeded flanker task and the computation and analysis of late Pe amplitude data that adjusted for latency jitter (Davies et al., 2004, Gavin et al., 2019, Lin et al., 2020, Lin et al., 2022). Participants were typically developing, which reduces the possibility of confounding due to diagnosis of serious neurological or psychiatric disorders. This study also has limitations to take into account when interpreting the results. The cross-sectional, correlational design does not allow for inferences about causality. In addition, the sample was primarily White, non-Hispanic/Latine. Therefore, results may not be generalizable to samples with different racial/ethnic compositions.

Conclusion

Results from this study are the first to show socioeconomic differences in Pe amplitude, a neural measure related to error awareness, in children. Lower parental education was associated with reduced Pe amplitude in children. And reduced Pe amplitudes were separately related to increased task monitoring difficulties in everyday settings, as reported by parents. Together, these results indicate that lower socioeconomic resources are associated with blunted neural processing of errors in children, possibly interfering with self-regulation and learning from errors. These findings align with integrating support for children’s performance monitoring skills into intervention strategies aimed at reducing socioeconomic disparities in academic achievement.
