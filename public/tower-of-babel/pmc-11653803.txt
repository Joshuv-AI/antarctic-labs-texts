Agent based modelling of blood borne viruses: a scoping review
Authors: Seun Ale, Elizabeth Hunter, John D Kelleher
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe models that historically have been used to model infectious disease outbreaks are equation-based and statistical models. However, these models do not capture the impact of individual and social factors that affect the spread of common blood-borne viruses (BBVs) such as human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), hepatitis C virus (HCV), and hepatitis B virus (HBV). Agent-based modelling (ABM) is an alternative modelling approach that is gaining popularity in public health and epidemiology. As the field expands, it is important to understand how ABMs have been applied. In this context, we completed a scoping review of research that has been done on the ABM of BBVs.MethodThe inclusion/exclusion criteria were drafted using the idea of Population, Concept, and Context (PCC). The Preferred Reporting Item for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis, an extension to scoping review (PRISMA-ScR), was employed in retrieving ABM literature that studied BBVs. Three databases (Scopus, Pubmed, and Embase) were systematically searched for article retrieval. 200 articles were retrieved from all the databases, with 10 duplicates. After removing the duplicates, 190 papers were screened for inclusion. After analysing the remaining articles, 70 were excluded during the abstract screening phase, and 32 were excluded during the full-text decision. Eighty-eight were retained for the scoping review analysis. To analyse this corpus of 88 papers, we developed a five-level taxonomy that categorised each paper based first on disease type, then transmission mechanism, then modelled population, then geographic location and finally, model outcome.ResultsThe result of this analysis show significant gaps in the ABM of BBV literature, particularly in the modeling of social and individual factors influencing BBV transmission.ConclusionThere is a need for more comprehensive models that address various outcomes across different populations, transmission and intervention mechanisms. Although ABMs are a valuable tool for studying BBVs, further research is needed to address existing gaps and improve our understanding of individual and social factors that influence the spread and control of BBVs. This research can inform researchers, modellers, epidemiologists, and public health practitioners of the ABM research areas that need to be explored to reduce the burden of BBVs globally.Keywords: Agent-based model, Blood-borne diseases, Scoping review, Individual factors, Social factors, Epidemiology

Introduction

Blood-borne viruses (BBVs) are transmitted through contact with infected blood or body fluid [1]. This infectious transmission can take various means, including unprotected sexual intercourse with an infected individual, reuse of unsterilised medical equipment like needles and syringes, vertical transmission (transmission from infected mother to child during child delivery or breastfeeding), unscreened blood transfusion and sharing of injections by injection drug users (IDUs) [2, 3]. Failure to treat BBVs may result in health complications, severe cases, and death [4].

BBVs continue to pose serious hazards to global public health despite various research breakthroughs in epidemiology. The Center for Disease Control and Prevention National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (CDC-NIOSH) listed Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), Hepatitis B virus (HBV), and Hepatitis C virus (HCV) as three of the main BBVs [5]. The summary of global HIV epidemics in 2022 published by World Health Organization (WHO), showed that 39 million people were HIV positive, with an annual incidence of 1.3 million, and 630 thousand people died of HIV globally [6–8]. According to the Centers for Disease Control in 2022, 296 million people are affected by HBV, with an annual incidence of 1.5 million, and 820 thousand deaths are attributable to HBV every year [9]. According to the WHO, 58 million people are living with HCV globally, with an annual incidence of 1.8 million. In 2019, there were 290 thousand deaths globally from HCV [10].

Some programs and research have been done to mitigate the spread of BBVs, for example needle exchange programs (NEPs) were introduced to lessen the transmission of BBVs among intravenous drug users [11]. The use of pre-exposure prophylaxis has been a good control measure for reducing BBV incidence rate among men who have sex with men (MSM) [12]. Opioid substitution therapy (OST) has been an effective BBV control strategy among injection drug users (IDU) [13]. Harm reduction programs (HR) are effective in reducing the HIV and HCV incidence among drug users (DUs) [14].

The design and implementation of public health policies in response to BBVs can be improved with the use of effective disease modelling techniques which are crucial for preventing and managing disease outbreaks [15]. Disease modelling can assist in assessing the likely effectiveness of an intervention for a given community. This was made clear during the COVID-19 pandemic, when models of disease transmission were important in guiding public health initiatives [16]. However, research on BBVs has not received enough modelling attention [17].

Agent-based modelling (ABM) is a computational approach that can be used to simulate a system. It is capable of simulating the actions of agents which can represent people, animals, populations, and organisations in a well-defined world or environment [18]. The agents have attributes and are capable of making decisions based on predefined rules that impact how they interact with other agents and move through the environment [19]. ABM can combine diverse agent types and can accommodate complex rules defining agents’ actions which allows for the application of ABMs for various fields [20]. The models are built from the individual level up, allowing a close examination of how micro-level behaviours lead to macro-level patterns [21]. The interaction of the agents within the population can reveal emergent patterns occurring at the population level. For instance, if agents develop immunity over time in a population, it can lead to collective resistance to infection spread [22]. ABM is useful for studying systems where traditional compartmental and statistical models may be unable to explain the emergent complexity associated with the system [23].

ABM is particularly suitable for epidemiological modelling because it can capture individual interactions of agents such as behavioural changes. For instance, agents may avoid crowded areas, use protections, or increase risky behaviours depending on seeming risk levels or the attitude of the agent toward the infection outbreak [24]. Also, ABM is able to capture overall population dynamics [25]. For instance, when agents avoid crowded areas or start taking preventive measures in response to an outbreak, the overall transmission rate might be reduced. ABMs are successful in simulating several complex factors which are important in understanding BBV dynamics, such as unstable housing, and the impact of needle exchange programs on the control of BBVs which compartmental and statistical models cannot capture [26].

The focus of this scoping review is to identify and map how ABMs have been used to capture the impact of individual and social factors such as geography, populations, risks, transmission modes, etc. that influence the spread of three major BBVs (HIV, HCV and HBV) and their co-infections, and to determine the gaps that exist in the body of literature on modelling BBVs using ABMs.

Methods

The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analyses extension for scoping reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines and checklist were followed for this scoping review [27, 28]. All of the elements on the PRISMA-ScR checklist were used in this study, except for the critical appraisal of individual sources because the primary objective of our scoping review is to map existing literature on ABM of BBVs and to identify research gaps to guide future research directions rather than evaluating the quality of individual studies.

Eligibility criteria

A general overview of research that has been carried out on BBVs dynamics using ABM is the subject of this research. The retrieval of articles was done based on this research focus. The following inclusion standards were established:

any ABM research on the following BBVs: HIV, HCV, and HBV;

any ABM research on the following BBVs: HIV, HCV, and HBV;

any ABM research on the spread of BBVs through a human population;

any ABM research on the spread of BBVs through a human population;

any between host ABM research on the spread of BBVs;

any between host ABM research on the spread of BBVs;

any ABM research on human-to-human transmission;

any ABM research on human-to-human transmission;

any peer-reviewed ABM studies indexed in the Scopus, Medline PubMed, or Embase databases with a publication date before July 31st, 2023.

any peer-reviewed ABM studies indexed in the Scopus, Medline PubMed, or Embase databases with a publication date before July 31st, 2023.

Any articles not meeting all the inclusion criteria were excluded. The following document types were also excluded: editorials, whole books, book reviews, survey papers, corrections, grey literature (unpublished articles), and individual perspective pieces.

Article retrieval and screening

The following sections explain how we retrieved articles from the chosen databases and screened them for inclusion in our analysis.

Article retrieval

The databases searched for article retrieval were Pubmed, Scopus, and Embase. These databases were chosen due to their high-quality indexing standards and international reputations. They are also known to have articles on public health-related research, including ABM or individual-based modelling (IBM). IBM is a term that is often used interchangeably in the literature with ABM [29]. Other research in the databases are infectious diseases, epidemiology, and mathematical modelling of the dynamics of disease transmission.

The searches took place between the 15th and the 31st of July 2023. Using the relevant search terms, boolean expressions were created to sort through the titles and abstracts of the papers. Two filters were used (1) Publications in peer-reviewed journals, and (2) written in English. In order to ensure that the design of the review includes the relevant concepts needed to understand how ABMs have been used to model BBVs, we used the Population, Concept, and Context (PCC) to define our search keywords, as shown in Table 1.

In scoping reviews, keywords play an important role in helping researchers find and gather required articles from the database. Keywords are special words that represent the main ideas or topics of interest being sought in the database. A set of keywords was used to build the search queries in this scoping review. The keywords were classified into two categories: keyword1 and keyword2.

Keyword1 was used for the set of terms referencing the BBVs we are considering in this research, which includes; Hepatitis B, Hepatitis C, Human immunodeficiency virus, HBV, HCV, HIV, hep c, hep b, liver disease, liver infection, AIDS, acquired immune deficiency syndrome, Serum Hepatitis, blood-borne virus and blood-borne disease.

Keyword 2 is a set of terms often used for referencing ABMs. These include agent-based modelling, ABM, individual-based modelling and IBM. Three reviewers worked together to decide on the eligible articles.

Screening

Our screening process and results of the screening process are summarised in Fig. 1. We initially retrieved 200 articles and 10 were discarded on the grounds of duplication. Two authors independently read the title and abstract of the remaining 190 articles, and, based on the research objectives, screened the articles using the pre-established eligibility criteria in “Eligibility criteria” section. Any articles that the two authors disagreed on inclusion or exclusion were sent to the third author who reviewed the articles and then scheduled a meeting where all three authors analysed and unanimously made a final decision on the exclusion or inclusion. After completing the title and abstract screening phase, the full text of the articles was also screened using the same approach. Seventy articles were excluded during the abstract screening, and 32 were excluded during the full-text screening.

In order to trace the usage of ABMs to model BBVs, we analysed the retrieved articles by year of publication. This analysis of articles by year of publication is shown in Fig. 2, the first ABM of BBV transmission identified in our review was published in 2008. The data shows that from 2008 to 2011, ABM was not a popular tool for simulating the transmission of BBVs. From 2021, it appears that research on ABMs for BBVs has returned to pre-pandemic level.

Qualitative analysis

In order to understand specific features in the models and determine important factors to consider in our analysis, we performed a word cloud analysis presented in Fig. 3 using Nvivo 14. Words such as HIV, prep (pre-exposure prophylaxis), msm (men who have sex with men), incidence, and years have the highest occurrences. This result shows that more ABM of BBVs has been carried out on the msm populations than any other population. This makes sense as msm have the highest risk of sexually transmitted diseases globally and one mode of transmission for BBVs is sexual transmission [117]. Prep has also been known as one of the most cost-effective [118] and most used preventive measures among msm. Other crucial words like pwid (people who inject drugs), infections, transmission, treatment, risk, model, prevalence, population, and data were in the next categories of high occurrence but not as frequent as the first category. The impact of drug injection in the dynamics of BBV is important [119] and many ABMs have been calibrated within the pwid populations. Words, such as epidemics, women, impact, and people, also occur relatively frequently. Lastly, light grey words like behaviour, partnership, strategy, etc., aren’t as common but do occur to some extent. This word cloud overviews the topics in the articles and makes it easier to find the important ideas, features, and connections for further analysis.

Word cloud analysis

HIV and HCV are the only diseases in the word cloud. HIV is among the words in bold letters while HCV is among the words in light grey. This suggests that the majority of papers in our review simulates the spread of HIV. Thus, we categorized the retained articles based on the disease they modelled, and this analysis of ABM usage in modelling each of the BBVs considered in this scoping review is presented in “Disease” section. Transmission is also present in the word cloud and is an important topic in epidemiology. Because we see other words in the word cloud that might refer to transmission, such as pwid and sexual, we decided it would be important to analyse the retained articles based on the transmission mode as presented in “Transmission” section. Other high frequency words in the word cloud, such as pwid and msm, refer to high risk populations for BBVs [119, 120]. This suggests that the population being modelled is a key feature of ABMs targetting BBVs. Consequently, we analysed the retained articles with respect to the populations being modelled, and this analysis is presented in “Population” section. We also noticed words such as Africa and South in the word cloud and this motivated us to consider geography and location as part of our analysis, this is presented in “Geography” section. Words like intervention, data, transmission, and cost, are also seen in the word cloud and can be connected along the theme of model outcomes. For example, a paper could be analyzing the impacts of different interventions or transmission types or determining the costs of different interventions, or validating a model with real-life data. This led to the analysis of the model outcomes of each of the retained articles that is presented in “Model outcomes” section.

Mapping of current literatures using tree diagrams

The analysis of the word cloud identified the main factors of analysis for the literature, starting with the disease, followed by transmission mechanism, then population, then geography, and finally model outcome. Using this sequence of categories to iteratively decompose the literatures, we create a tree-based map of the current literature that provides an overview of ABM research for BBVs. Figures 4, 5, and 6 show the connections between all the components, revealing paths that have been widely explored in order to understand gaps in the ABM of BBVs. The number in each node records the number of papers in the corpus that reach that node in the tree as they are filtered down from the top of the tree to the leaf nodes. “Disease” to “Model outcomes” sections shows how different categories of the tree diagrams are interconnected. We have ordered our analysis of the tree starting from the type of disease modelled followed by transmission type, then followed by the modelled population, then followed by geography and finally the model outcome. For presentation, we split the tree into three trees based on the disease type. Figure 4 presents the tree diagram for HIV, Figs. 5 and 6 presents tree diagrams for HBV and HCV respectively. To classify the articles into the different trees we assessed which BBV was the focus of the article and assigned it to the corresponding tree. If an article included an analysis of more than one disease we give it a separate subgroup and it is not included in the tree diagram. The detailed explanation of the disease taxonomic group and its analysis is in “Disease” section. The taxonomic group that follows from the word cloud analysis is transmission. From our word cloud analysis, this category is based on words such as transmission, sexual and pwid. When categorising the retained articles, we searched for concepts that related to transmission in each article. We found that papers tended to vary based on the mode of transmission that was being modeled, and four distinct transmission mechanisms were found across papers: antenatal, injection, sexual and sexual & injection. These became our classes within the taxonomic group transmission. Additionally, we included a generic transmission group for those articles that did not specify a transmission mode. The identified transmission modes can be seen in Fig. 4. More details about transmission taxonomic group and its analysis is in “Transmission” section. The next taxonomic group in our tree is population. This group was created based on the words population, msm and pwid in the word cloud. We analysed each of our retained articles to determine if the described models focused on a specific population and identified two levels that correspond well with what was identifid in the word cloud: msm and IDU where IDU corresponds to the pwid population. In addition to these two groups we found that some models were not built on a specific population and we categorized these articles into a general population group. More details about population taxonomic group and its analysis is in “Disease” section. The following level of the taxonomy is geography. Geography was included as a level of the taxonomy upon identification of words in the word cloud such as Africa and South. To determine what types of geography should be included in the tree we did an analysis of the retained literature and looked at the geographies that were represented in the models. We found that there was a wide range of countries that were represented and our analysis highlighted a dichotomy in retained articles: those modelling the Global South and those modelling the Global North. The detail explanation of Geography taxonomic group and its analysis is in “Geography” section. Finally, the last level in the taxonomy is model outcomes. This group was created based on the words treatment, prevention, data, cost and intervention in the word cloud. We evaluated the retained articles to determine the aim of the analysis of each paper and found that the articles could be classified into four main outcomes: validation, intervention, treatment and cost. Further details about the model outcome and its analysis is in “Model outcomes” section.

Disease

The first layer of the tree diagram in Figs. 4, 5, and 6 is the disease component. We categorised the retrieved articles based on the specific types of diseases they addressed, as shown in Fig. 7. For easier understanding of the tree diagrams, we did three different tree diagrams, each representing a disease type we considered in this review. The detailed explanation of the diagram with respect to the various diseases is given in “Contextualising research findings and identifying research gaps” section. Our analysis by disease type resulted in seven distinct subgroups: HBV, HCV, HIV, HIV & STI, HIV & Human papillomavirus (HPV), HIV & HCV, and HIV & herpes simplex virus type 2 (HSV-2). We used STI (sexually transmitted infection) if the paper only referred to generalised STI, but if there is a co-infection mentioning a specific STI, then we give it a separate subgroup. The majority of the retained articles found in the review were on HIV. Interest in researching HIV has grown because of the stigma attached to it. Awareness creation and advocacy efforts against HIV stigmatisation have also helped in creating a strong research environment [121]. HIV also receives more research funding and grants globally than other BBVs from international donors [122], this greater funding and grants may be responsible for more models of HIV than the other BBVs studied in this review.

Articles by diseases

In order to determine if the trend of more focus on HIV research exists in other research approaches beyond ABMs, we did a general search for all articles of any type in the past two years on HIV, HCV, and HBV in our chosen databases Pubmed, Scopus and Embase, using the keywords for each type of the disease as in “Article retrieval” section. The results are shown in Fig. 8. Results from two databases, Pubmed and Scopus, supported our finding that there is more research on HIV than HBV and HCV. However, Embase contains more research work on HCV than HIV. We believe that results from PubMed and Scopus databases are more reliable for epidemiological research as Pubmed focuses on clinical medicine and public health, and Scopus is suitable for epidemiological research involving interdisciplinary studies and tracks citations across various fields. Embase focuses more on drug related studies [123–125]. Consequently, we believe that the trend we observe in our corpus of ABM of BBV research articles of the majority of the research focusing on HIV does generalise across other approaches to modelling BBV spread.

Articles by databases

Transmission

A major part of understanding the spread of a virus is comprehending the methods of transmission. Given that transmission was highlighted as a key topic in our word cloud analysis, as shown in Fig. 2, we recognized that methods of BBV transmission are critical components in ABMs for BBVs. Accordingly, the second layer of the tree diagram in Figs. 4, 5, and 6 detailed the transmission component. As depicted in the tree diagrams, the identified methods of BBV transmission include sexual, drug injection, antenatal, sexual & injection and generic, which cover articles where the method of BBV transmission was not mentioned. The distribution of articles based on the type of transmission they address is illustrated in Fig. 9. From the tree diagram in Figs. 4, 5, and 6, all the transmission methods identified in the word cloud analysis in Fig. 2 including antenatal, sexual, injection, combination of sexual & injection and generic transmission are modelled by ABM articles of HIV. However, HCV and HBV articles have only modelled injection and sexual transmission respectively. This might be because there are more ABM articles on HIV than HBV and HCV as mentioned earlier, see in Fig. 7. As can be seen in Fig. 9, most ABM research on BBVs considered a sexual mode of transmission. This may be because BBVs are mostly transmitted sexually since most newly diagnosed HIV patients are in a sexual relationship with HIV-positive partners [126], and most of our retained studies focus on HIV. The next most popular transmission mode was drug injection. There is a high prevalence of BBVs among drug injection users [127]. Hence, that may account for why many models are developed to measure the impacts of drug injection on BBV spread. The next transmission mode considered was both sexual & drug injection. This makes sence because the reduction of sexually transmitted diseases such as HIV among drug-using populations is a major concern to public health [128].

Articles by transmission modes

Population

Population emerged as a significant theme in our word cloud analysis, as illustrated in Fig. 2. This topic is further elaborated upon through the third layer of the tree diagrams in Figs. 4, 5, and 6, where various aspects of the population component are detailed. The analysis of the retained articles, as depicted in Fig. 10, focused on the specific populations modeled. According to the insights derived from the tree diagrams in Figs. 4, 5, and 6, three key populations were identified in our study of the articles: IDU, MSM, and the general population. The general population was used for a model that did not focus on any specific group of people. Our analysis shows that more articles were considering a general population, while MSM and IDU populations have almost equal numbers of papers. Most new HIV cases in developed countries are attributable to MSM and IDUs [129]. In Canada, about 67.3% of new HIV infections in 2014 were linked to a combination of MSM, IDU, and both MSM & IDU exposures [120]. In United States, MSM accounted for approximately 69% of new HIV diagnoses, while IDU contributed about 6%, and MSM-IDU exposure around 2% in 2019 [130]. In the United Kingdom, MSM represented about 48% of new HIV diagnoses, IDU about 2%, and MSM-IDU exposure about 1% [131].

Articles by modelled population

Geography

Africa and South are in the word cloud in Fig. 2 and the fourth layer of the tree diagram in Figs. 4, 5, and 6 is the geographic component that classifies a model’s geographic location as whether a country is part of the Global South or Global North. The terms Global South and Global North are classifications based on the economies of countries rather than their geographical locations. Global North refers to the advanced and economically stable countries, while Global South is the developing countries. Global South versus Global North distinction is employed to explore regional representation in ABM studies of BBVs, which may reveal disparities in research focus and funding sources. This analysis is intended to reveal any gaps in geographic representation within the literature, rather than suggest specific funding origins or investment levels by developed versus developing nations. This section can provide a contextual overview of how ABM research on BBVs is distributed at global level. Our analysis as shown in Fig. 11 reveals that more research on ABMs of BBVs has been done by the Global North. This might be because we only retrieved articles that were written in English. On the other hand, it suggests the claim that public health and epidemiological research of the Global South needs further support [132]. The tree diagram in Figs. 4, 5, and 6 shows that articles from both the Global North and Global South address all types of transmission, with two exceptions: antenatal transmission is discussed exclusively in articles from the Global South, and HBV sexual transmission is only modelled in articles from the Global North.

Articles by Global North VS Global South

In order to do a deeper investigation into the usage of ABM to model BBV by different geographical locations, we analysed the retrieved articles based on the geographical continents as shown in Fig. 12. The result shows that Africa and North America have a higher number of articles on ABMs of BBVs than other continents. Asia and South America have the smallest number of research outputs. The large number of articles from Africa makes sense as Sub-saharan African countries have the highest HIV prevalence rates according to a report from Global HIV & AIDS Statistics 2022 [133]. Research by [134] shows that despite the fact that many countries are experiencing a decline in HIV incidence, America and some African countries are still experiencing growth in HIV incidence and prevalence.

Model outcomes

Words like transmission, cost, and data in the word cloud in Fig. 2 led to the analysis of the retained articles with respect to the model outcomes. Consequently, the final layer of the tree diagram in Figs. 4, 5, and 6 is the model outcome component. We explored the retained articles based on key factors that were modelled, as seen in Fig. 13. These factors include cost, intervention, transmission, and validation. The cost refers to when the model focuses on accounting for the expenses of implementing an intervention, such as preexposure prophylaxis among men who have sex with men (MSM), harm reduction among IDUs, and sharing of free condoms among the sexually active population. The intervention refers to when the model examines the effect of specific control measures on the incidence and prevalence of the disease. Transmission was used when the research investigated how different modes of disease spread influenced the incidence and prevalence of disease outbreaks. Finally, validation refers to when the model was focused on comparing the model’s output with real-life data. The model outcomes depicted in the tree diagrams in Figs. 4, 5, and 6 provide insights into the research focus across different geographical locations and identify which modeling outcomes have been underexplored by researchers. Our analysis reveals that most articles concentrated on intervention strategies, followed by discussions on transmission dynamics. However, fewer articles were dedicated to validation and cost-related considerations.

Contextualising research findings and identifying research gaps

In this section, we contextualise our findings in this review with other epidemiological studies and also present the research gaps that exist in modelling BBVs using ABMs.

HIV

As can be seen in Fig. 4, the only HIV article on antenatal transmission was found in the Global South. This agrees with the report of [135], which indicated a high rate of vertical HIV transmission from mother to child during pregnancy, childbirth, or breastfeeding in the Global South compared to the Global North. Developed countries like the United Kingdom, United States, and France have achieved vertical transmission rates as low as 0.2% to 0.3% with effective HIV treatment and managed delivery. However, in Sub-Saharan Africa and other parts of the global south, transmission rates range from 2% to 25% [135].

However this article on HIV antenatal transmission only focused on validating the model with real-life data. A gap exists in using the models on antenatal transmission to understand the effects of transmission on incidence and prevalence. Also, antenatal intervention effectiveness, and cost as model outcomes have not been considered.

HIV models on injections as the transmission method considered two populations (the general population referring to those models not exclusively built on IDU, and the second model built on IDU population only). Models built on general population data were done in the Global South, and the research outcome focused on intervention. The effects of transmission and the cost of interventions have not been considered. Models built on IDU population data were done in the Global North. Our findings on model location agree with the report of [136] that IDU plays a significant role in HIV transmission in the global north, while the major HIV transmission in the Global South is among heterosexuals. The research outcomes of the HIV models on injection transmission among the IDUs were designed to focus on transmission and intervention. Howerver, gaps still exist in developing HIV models for IDU that are validated using real-life data. Also there is need to build HIV model that can be used to estimate the cost implications of intervention strategies.

It is noteworthy that the sexual mode of transmission of HIV has received more research than other modes of transmission, and all the research outcomes, cost, validation, transmission effect, and validation have been considered. The HIV plus sexual transmission articles were categorised into two groups based on the modelled populations (MSM and general population). The general population refers to all other sexual populations different from MSM. The articles on MSM population from the Global North have modelled all the research outcome categories transmission, intervention, cost and validation. In contrast, the articles from the Global South have only modelled intervention. Articles on the general population from the Global South have modelled all the research outcomes categories, while articles from the Global North have only modelled transmission and intervention. Our findings align with the report of [136] that MSM contributes more to the HIV prevalence in the Global North. At the same time, HIV primarily spreads through sexual behaviour among heterosexuals in the Global South.

HBV

As seen in Fig. 5, the only retained article on HBV was used to model a general population, and it focused on the sexual mode of transmission. The model outcome was the intervention strategy and the geographical location is Global North. This make sense as most HBV transmission in the Global South occurs through vertical transmission at childbirth [137], while HBV transmission mostly occur among adolescents and adults in the Global North through sexual contact and injection drug use [138, 139]. There is gap in developing ABMs that can simulate other HBV transmission modes other than sexual transmission. There is also a gap in developing ABMs for HBV among specific population such as children under age 5 who have the highest risk of HBV [140]. Also, different control strategies such as vaccination have not been considered. Overall, more work needs to be done using ABMs to model HBV as there is still a large gap in this research area.

HCV

As seen in Fig. 6 the retained articles on HCV have only considered injections as the major transmission mode, focusing on the IDU population only. The research outcomes focused on intervention and cost. More models are found in the Global North than the Global South. The mapping on the tree diagram agrees with [141] that the use of injection drugs is the single most important risk factor for acquiring HCV infection in the Global North. Gaps exist in using ABMs to understand the effect of transmission on incidence and prevalence. Also, there is a need to work on a model focusing on data validation.

HCV is primarily spread through three transmission modes, which include reuse or inadequate sterilisation of medical equipment in healthcare, blood transfusion and injection sharing by injection drug users [142]. As seen in our tree diagram in Fig. 6, only injection sharing has been considered using ABM. Transmission through sexual practices (like MSM or having multiple sexual partners) and antenatal are less common. There is a need to develop ABM that will capture other HCV transmission modes.

Types of ABMs used in modelling the retained corpus

In this section, we analysed the retained articles by types of ABM used as shown in Fig. 14 in order to understand the frequency of usage of different ABMs and also determine whether some of the ABM types suit BBVs better than others. We categorise ABM into six groups: agent-based modelling or individual-based modelling ABM/IBM. These models use the general ABM or IBM without any special or exclusive definition of networks, links, geography, and other ABM features. The agents have unrestricted movement and interaction within the modelled environment, influencing disease transmission [143]. Other categories of ABMs are hybrid ABMs which combines elements from various modelling approaches, including compartmental models (e.g., SIR or SEIR models). Hybrid models capture both macroscopic and microscopic aspects of disease spread [144]. Network-based ABMs incorporate complex network structures to represent connections and interactions between individuals [145]. Spatially explicit ABMs represent geographic layouts and spatial relationships [146]. In spatially explicit ABMs, agents move within a defined environment, providing insights into spatial patterns of disease transmission [147]. Some articles combined two types of model categories in our groupings; these are hybrid & network-based, spatially-explicit & hybrid models.

Articles by types of ABM

Our analysis of the papers shows that spatially explicit models have not been widely used to model BBVs, as limited models with spatial data were seen in our retained articles. The spatially explicit models require detailed geographical and epidemiological data, which may be difficult to obtain for BBVs. Accurate data on the locations of infected individuals, as well as patterns of movement and interaction, are essential for these models [148]. Another reason for having limited spatially explicit ABMs of BBVs might be because BBV transmission requires effective contact among the agents for infection to occur, and it’s not airborne. Thus, location might not really be a major factor in the spread of BBVs.

As seen in Fig. 14 many of the articles implemented network-based ABMs. This might be because contacts among agents play a crucial role in BBV transmission, and incorporating agent networks into ABMs allows researchers to capture these interactions more realistically [149].

Conclusion

Through an analysis of the components of ABMs of BBVs such as modelled disease, transmission mode, population, geography, model outcome and type of ABM focusing on HIV, HBV, and HCV, we made key findings and identified research gaps.

Key findings

We found that the trend in using ABM for modelling BBVs has notably increased since 2012.

Additionally, HIV received the most attention from ABM of BBV modellers, likely due to the global awareness of the disease and the associated stigma of HIV infection. The analysis showed a difference between the global south and global north regarding vertical transmission rates from mother to child.

The only retained ABM of HBV focused on sexual transmission and a general population was modelled. The model outcome focused on intervention. Further study reveals that vertical transmission is a significant mode of HBV transmission, particularly among children under five years old.

For HCV, the reviewed literature centred on injection sharing among drug users, neglecting other modes of transmission such as inadequate sterilisation of medical equipment and blood transfusion.

Limitations and future work

It is important to note that a limitation of our study is that we only considered articles written in English, potentially excluding valuable insights from non-English publications. Future work should consider broader article retrieval to include non-English publications in order to capture some information that we might not have captured.

Also we did not conduct a critical appraisal of individual sources of articles. While our review provides an overview of the existing literature on ABMs of BBVs, a critical appraisal of individual sources could offer valuable insights into the quality and reliability of the included studies. In future work, incorporating such a critical appraisal could improve the quality and reliability of the research findings.

Another limitation of this scoping review is that we did not compare the usage of ABM in modelling BBVs with other traditional modelling approaches, such as compartmental or statistical models. Although this study did not provide a comparative analysis of ABM usage with other modelling methods, it compared the retrieved studies based on the type of diseases as seen in “Disease” section. Our review contributes to the broader understanding of ABM usage in disease modelling by presenting what has been covered in the usage of ABM to model BBV dynamics and outline the research gaps. The authors recommend that future research should consider comparing the usage of traditional epidemiological modelling with the ABM approach.

Summary

While ABMs have been a tool for studying the HIV pandemic, research on HBV and HCV remains limited. There is a need for further investigation into transmission dynamics and intervention strategies, particularly among specific populations. Additionally, gaps still exist in data validation and cost-related model outcomes. Addressing these gaps will contribute to a better understanding of BBV transmission and aid in developing effective public health interventions to reduce the global burden of BBVs.
