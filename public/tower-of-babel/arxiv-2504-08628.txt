Gradient Descent Robustly Learns the Intrinsic Dimension of Data in Training Convolutional Neural Networks
Chenyang Zhang, Peifeng Gao, Difan Zou, Yuan Cao

Abstract
Modern neural networks are usually highly over-parameterized. Behind the wide usage of over-parameterized networks is the belief that, if the data are simple, then the trained network will be automatically equivalent to a simple predictor. Following this intuition, many existing works have studied different notions of “ranks” of neural networks and their relation to the rank of data. In this work, we study the rank of convolutional neural networks (CNNs) trained by gradient descent, with a specific focus on the robustness of the rank to image background noises. Specifically, we point out that, when adding background noises to images, the rank of the CNN trained with gradient descent is affected far less compared with the rank of the data. We support our claim with a theoretical case study, where we consider a particular data model to characterize low-rank clean images with added background noises. We prove that CNNs trained by gradient descent can learn the intrinsic dimension of clean images, despite the presence of relatively large background noises. We also conduct experiments on synthetic and real datasets to further validate our claim.

Introduction

Neural networks have become a cornerstone in modern machine learning, demonstrating remarkable performance across various domains. A common characteristic of modern networks is their tendency to be highly over-parameterized. Interestingly, it has been demonstrated that over-parameterized models trained by standard optimization algorithms exhibit a preference for simplicity
(
Soudry et al., 2018
;
Ji and Telgarsky, 2019b
;
Nacson et al., 2019
;
Gunasekar et al., 2018
;
Gunasekar et al., 2017
;
Li et al., 2018
;
Arora et al., 2019
;
Razin and Cohen, 2020
;
Chizat and Bach, 2020
;
Phuong and Lampert, 2020
)
: if the training data can be fitted well by a simple predictor, then after training, an over-parameterized model may effectively reduce to this simple predictor.

A notable line of recent works has considered notions of “ranks” to characterize how simple the over-parameterized neural network after training is
Gunasekar et al. (2017)
;
Li et al. (2018)
;
Arora et al. (2019)
;
Chizat and Bach (2020)
;
Zhou et al. (2022)
;
Jacot (2022)
. Specifically,
Gunasekar et al. (2017)
;
Li et al. (2018)
;
Arora et al. (2019)
showed that when training over-parameterized matrix factorization models and linear neural networks, gradient descent has an implicit bias towards a low-rank solution. Similar conclusions have also been demonstrated for more nonlinear networks. Specifically,
Chizat and Bach (2020)
showed that the effective hidden layer neurons in a two-layer neural network is sparse.
Zhou et al. (2022)
empirically demonstrated that the hidden neural weight vectors condense on isolated orientations when learning easy tasks, and provided explanations of this phenomenon with theoretical case studies.
Jacot (2022)
further formulated two notions of ranks, namely the Jacobian rank and the Bottleneck rank, for vector-valued neural networks, and demonstrated that over-parameterized networks tend to achieve small ranks.

In this work, we aim to study the “ranks” of two-layer convolutional neural networks (CNN) when learning from data sets with a clean low-rank structure from a new perspective: we examine the robustness of the neural network rank when background noises of increasing levels are added to the original low-rank data. Interestingly, we can draw the following conclusion:

The rank of CNN is more robust to background noises, compared with the rank of data.

An illustration of this claim is given in Figure
1
. From Figure
1
, it is evident that for both MNIST and CIFAR-10 images, as the level of background noise increases, the rank of the matrix composed of the data inputs rises sharply, eventually leading to an explosion. In comparison, the rank of the matrix representing CNN filters consistently remains close to the PCA rank (i.e. rank of clean data), demonstrating much less fluctuation. Therefore, these empirical observations match the claim that the rank of the CNN filters is more robust to background noise than the rank of data.

The major contributions of this paper are as follows:

•

We reveal the “rank robustness” phenomenon in training convolutional neural networks. In particular, we add different levels of background noises to clean low-rank data and then use CNNs to fit these noisy data. We observe that, even if a significant amount of noise has been added which causes the stable rank of the data to explode, the stable rank of the CNN filters can still remain around the rank of the clean data. This suggests that the rank of CNN is more robust to the noise compared to the rank of data.

•

We theoretically prove that the observed phenomenon happens when training a two-layer CNN on a data model with multiple patches, where some of the patches contain the object content for classification while others are filled with background noise. More specifically, we show that under a wide range of noise levels, the CNN model will learn the rank of the clean data.
In comparison, we also show that under the same noise levels, the data rank can provably explode.

•

Our theoretical analysis is based on a careful examination of the CNN training dynamics. Specifically, we develop theoretical tools to demonstrate that different convolutional filters of the CNN are updated by gradient descent in distinct directions, with each filter’s direction determined by its random initialization. We believe that these novel analysis tools can be applied to the study of CNNs from other perspectives as well and are of independent interest.

Problem setup

As outlined in the introduction, this paper aims to provide a theoretical explanation for an intriguing empirical phenomenon concerning the “rank” of the filters in a convolutional neural network (CNN) when applied to noisy data. However, the classic definition of rank is not suitable for our study, as it is highly susceptible to minor perturbations: even the slightest noise can cause the data to achieve full rank. While in experiments, the “approximate rank” of a matrix is usually clear by looking at the distribution of singular values, a theoretical characterization of this phenomenon requires a mathematically more rigorous definition. Fortunately, the definition of “stable rank” meets this requirement, which could be regarded as a “continuous counterpart” of the classic rank
(
Georgiev et al., 2021
)
. Stable rank was first introduced in
Rudelson and Vershynin (2007)
for studying the low-rank approximation of large matrices, and we provide a formal definition in the following.

Definition 2.1
.

For a matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times d_{2}}
, the stable rank of
\mathbf{A}
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathrm{StableRank}(\mathbf{A}):=\frac{\|\mathbf{A}\|_{F}^{2}}{\|\mathbf{A}\|_{2}^{2}}

Compared to the classic definition of rank, stable rank is analogous in many aspects but considerably more well-behaved: it is more stable to small perturbations and it is differentiable.
To illustrate its robustness to small perturbations, consider a diagonal matrix

\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
with its first diagonal with its first diagonal entry set to
1
and all other entries equal to
\epsilon
. As
\epsilon\to 0
, the classic rank of
\mathbf{A}
remains
d
until
\epsilon
exactly equals to 0. In contrast, the stable rank of
\mathbf{A}
equals
1+(d-1)\epsilon
, approaching
1
smoothly as
\epsilon
decreases
(
Frei et al., 2022
)
. Therefore, stable rank is a suitable quantity to characterize the impact of noise at different levels.

Besides the definition of the stable rank, we also need to give a concrete mathematical description of the image data with noisy backgrounds. In order to enable the analysis of CNNs and demonstrate out empirical observations in Figure
1
, we particularly hope to mathematically capture the following nature of the data:

(i)

The data consists of multiple “patches” (blocks of “pixels” with localized features).

(ii)

Some patches are “clean object patches”, the others are “noisy background patches”.

(iii)

The “clean object patches” share a low-rank structure.

Motivated by this, we consider performing a case study based on a particular data model to theoretically demonstrate the robustness of the stable rank of the CNNs. The definition of the data model
\mathcal{D}
is given as follows:

Definition 2.2

(Low-rank clean images with noisy backgrounds)
.

Let
\ \mathcal{U}_{+1},\mathcal{U}_{-1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be two disjoint sets of orthonormal vectors.
A data pair
(\mathbf{X},y)
is generated as follows. The label
y
is generated as a Rademacher random variable. The input
\mathbf{X}
is of the form
[\mathbf{x}^{(1)},\mathbf{x}^{(2)},\dots,\mathbf{x}^{(P)}]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times P}
with each column denoting a patch. Given
y
, a non-empty index set
{\mathcal{S}}\subsetneqq[P]
is randomly generated. Then for all
p\in[P]
, if
p\in{\mathcal{S}}
, the patch
\mathbf{x}^{(p)}
is assigned as a vector uniformly chosen from
\mathcal{U}_{y}
. Otherwise,
\mathbf{x}^{(p)}
is assigned as a noise vector generated from
N(\mathbf{0},\sigma_{\mathrm{noise}}^{2}\cdot(\mathbf{I}-\sum_{\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{U}_{+1}\cup\ \mathcal{U}_{-1}}\bm{\mu}\bm{\mu}^{\top}))
.

In the data model
\mathcal{D}
defined above, we consider the sets
\mathcal{U}_{+1}
and
\mathcal{U}_{-1}
following the intuition that they should consist of “basis vectors” that can form objects belonging to classes
+1
and
-1
respectively. We assume that
|\mathcal{U}_{+1}|=|\mathcal{U}_{-1}|=K
for the ease of the study of “ranks”, as it is evident that the rank of the clean data should be
2K
. Importantly, the data model is flexible regarding the distribution of the index set
{\mathcal{S}}
, and all our results hold as long as
{\mathcal{S}}
is nonempty and
{\mathcal{S}}\subsetneqq[P]
. This allows the model to better match the nature of image data, where patches with particular spatial relationships can together form an object.
Besides, our definition of the noise vector distribution
N(\mathbf{0},\sigma_{\mathrm{noise}}^{2}\cdot(\mathbf{I}-\sum_{\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{U}_{+1}\cup\ \mathcal{U}_{-1}}\bm{\mu}\bm{\mu}^{\top}))
ensures that the Gaussian noises are generated from the orthogonal complement of
\mathcal{U}_{+1}\cup\ \mathcal{U}_{-1}
. By separating the “object patches” with
p\in{\mathcal{S}}
and the “background patches” with
p\in{\mathcal{S}}^{c}
, the data model only allows the noises to be added to the backgrounds of the images, which aligns with our experiment setup that produces Figure
1
.
Moreover, as a data model for a supervised training task, Definition
2.2
ensures that object patches are drawn from
\mathcal{U}_{y}
and are therefore related to the label
y
, while background patches are independent of
y
. Consequently, this data model accurately captures the nature of image classification tasks, where only the actual object in the image is directly related to the class of the image.

Note that similar data models have been considered for the studies of a variety of different topics, including knowledge distillation
(
Allen-Zhu and Li, 2022
)
, algorithmic biases
(
Zou et al., 2023
;
Lu et al., 2023
)
, and the “benign-overfitting” phenomenon
(
Cao et al., 2022
;
Kou et al., 2023
)
. Our data model in Definition
2.2
is more general and covers most of the models studied in these existing works.

We consider training CNNs using a dataset
\{(\mathbf{X}_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
for binary classification, where each training data pair
(\mathbf{X}_{i},y_{i})
is generated independently from the distribution given in Definition
2.2
. To calculate the data rank, we consolidate all training inputs into a single matrix, as
\widehat{\mathbf{X}}=[\mathbf{x}_{1}^{(1)},\cdots,\mathbf{x}_{1}^{(P)},\mathbf{x}_{2}^{(1)},\cdots,\mathbf{x}_{2}^{(P)},\cdots,\mathbf{x}_{n}^{(1)},\cdots,\mathbf{x}_{n}^{(P)}]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times nP}
. Then the following proposition theoretically demonstrates that the stable rank of the data matrix
\widehat{\mathbf{X}}
will explode at the initial stages of noise level
\sigma_{\mathrm{noise}}
growth.

Proposition 2.3
.

Suppose that
d\geq\widetilde{\Omega}(n^{4})
, and
K,P\leq O(1)
. For any positive
\delta
satisfying
\log(1/\delta)\leq O(d)
, the following inequalities concerning the stable rank of
\widehat{\mathbf{X}}
hold with probability at least
1-\delta
.

•

If
\sigma_{\mathrm{noise}}\sqrt{d}\leq O(1)
, then

\displaystyle 2K-O\bigg(\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{n}}\bigg)\leq\mathrm{StableRank}(\widehat{\mathbf{X}})\leq 2K+O\big(\sigma_{\mathrm{noise}}^{2}d\big).

•

If
\sigma_{\mathrm{noise}}\sqrt{d}\geq\Omega(1)
, then

\displaystyle\mathrm{StableRank}(\widehat{\mathbf{X}})\geq\Big(2K+\Omega\big(\sigma_{\mathrm{noise}}^{2}d\big)\Big)\wedge n.

Proposition
2.3
characterizes the stable rank of the data matrix
\widehat{\mathbf{X}}
across different noise levels
\sigma_{\mathrm{noise}}
. Specifically, it identifies a boundary for the noise level
\sigma_{\mathrm{noise}}\sqrt{d}
, where the stable rank of the data matrix exhibits significantly different patterns on either side of this boundary. Here, we consider the value of
\sigma_{\mathrm{noise}}\sqrt{d}
rather than
\sigma_{\mathrm{noise}}
, as
\sigma_{\mathrm{noise}}
merely represents the scale at each pixel of background patches.
By multiplying the factor
\sqrt{d}
, it correctly reflects the overall noise level of each background patch. When
\sigma_{\mathrm{noise}}\sqrt{d}\leq O(1)
, the stable rank of
\widehat{\mathbf{X}}
is close to the clean data rank
2K
, with a difference at most
O(1)
. While once the noise level expresses the boundary such that
\sigma_{\mathrm{noise}}\sqrt{d}\geq\Omega(1)
, the stable rank of
\widehat{\mathbf{X}}
will be greater than
2K
, exceeding it by an amount at least
\Omega(1)
.

Two-layer CNNs.
We consider a two-layer convolutional neural network whose filters are applied to the
P
patches
\mathbf{x}^{(1)},\mathbf{x}^{(2)},\ldots,\mathbf{x}^{(P)}
,
and the second layer parameters of the network are fixed as
+1/m
’s and
-1/m
’s. The network can be written as

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{W},\mathbf{X})=F_{+1}(\mathbf{W}_{+1},\mathbf{X})-F_{-1}(\mathbf{W}_{-1},\mathbf{X}).

(2.1)

Here,
F_{+1}(\mathbf{W}_{+1},\mathbf{X})
,
F_{-1}(\mathbf{W}_{-1},\mathbf{X})
are defined as

\displaystyle F_{j}(\mathbf{W}_{j},\mathbf{X})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}{\sum_{r=1}^{m}}\sum_{p=1}^{P}\sigma(\langle\mathbf{w}_{j,r},\mathbf{x}^{(p)}\rangle),

where
j\in\{+1,-1\}
, and

m
is the number of convolutional filters in
F_{+1}
and
F_{-1}
. We consider Huberized ReLU activation function
\sigma(\cdot)
defined as follows:

\displaystyle\sigma(z)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &0,&&\text{if}\ z\leq 0;\\
&\frac{z^{q}}{q\kappa^{q-1}},&&\text{if}\ z\in[0,\kappa];\\
&z-\bigg(1-\frac{1}{q}\bigg)\kappa,&&\text{if}\ z\geq\kappa,\end{aligned}\right.

(2.2)

where
\kappa,q
are both absolute constants that do not rely on the training size
n
, dimension
d
, and the number of filters
m
, with
q\geq 3
.
We use
\mathbf{w}_{j,r}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
to denote the weight for the
r
-th filter (i.e., neuron), and
\mathbf{W}_{j}
is the collection of model weights associated with
F_{j}
. We also use
\mathbf{W}
to denote the collection of all model weights. We note that our CNN model can also be viewed as a CNN with average global pooling.

Training algorithm.

We train the above CNN model by minimizing the empirical loss on training data set
\{(\mathbf{X}_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
with cross-entropy loss function. Specifically, we define the training loss function as follows:

\displaystyle L_{S}(\mathbf{W})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}}\ell[y_{i}\cdot f(\mathbf{W},\mathbf{X}_{i})],

where
\ell(z)=\log(1+\exp(-z))
is the cross-entropy loss function for binary classification.

We consider gradient descent starting from Gaussian initialization, where each entry of
\mathbf{W}_{+1}
and
\mathbf{W}_{-1}
is sampled from a Gaussian distribution
N(0,\sigma_{0}^{2})
, where
\sigma_{0}^{2}
is the variance. The gradient descent update of the filters in the CNN can be written as

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}_{j,r}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{w}_{j,r}^{(t)}-\eta\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{w}_{j,r}}L_{S}(\mathbf{W}^{(t)})

(2.3)

for
j\in\{\pm 1\}
and
r\in[m]
.

3
Stable rank of CNNs

In the previous section, we introduce some preliminaries of this paper: we define “stable rank,” the main focus of our study, and demonstrate in Proposition
2.3
that the stable rank of the data significantly increases during the initial stages of noise level growth. In this section, we present our main results regarding the stable rank of the CNN filters: compared to that of the data, the stable rank of the CNN filters is more robust to noise—it remains close to the rank of clean data,
2K
, for a wide range of varying noise level.

Before we demonstrate our results, we first present some necessary conditions regarding the parameters of our training, including the dimension
d
, the training sample size
n
, neural network width
m
(number of filters), learning rate
\eta
, and initialization scale
\sigma_{0}
.

Condition 3.1
.

Suppose that

1.

Dimension
d
is sufficiently large:
d=\widetilde{\Omega}(m^{4}\vee n^{4})

2.

Training sample size
n
and neural network width
m
satisfy
n,m=\Omega(\mathrm{polylog}(d))
.

3.

The learning rate
\eta
satisfies
\eta\leq\widetilde{O}(1\wedge\sigma_{\mathrm{noise}}^{-2}d^{-1})
.

4.

The standard deviation of Gaussian initialization
\sigma_{0}
is small:

\sigma_{0}\leq\widetilde{O}\big(d^{-1/2}\wedge\sigma_{\mathrm{noise}}^{-1}d^{-1}\big)
.

These assumptions are widely made in a series of recent works on the benign overfitting phenomena of gradient descent in learning over-parameterized CNN models
(
Chatterji and Long, 2021
;
Kou et al., 2023
;
Cao et al., 2022
)
.
In what follows, we briefly explain the reasons and intuition behind these assumptions.
Specifically, the neural network width satisfies
m=\Omega(\mathrm{polylog}(d))
to ensure that, with a sufficiently high probability, the model training will neither collapse nor vanish along any basis vector during initialization (i.e., at least one filter is activated for each
\bm{\mu}
in
\mathcal{U}_{+1}\cup\ \mathcal{U}_{-1}
). Additionally, the learning rate
\eta
is required to be small enough to guarantee the convergence of the gradient descent iterations of the training loss. And the condition on the initialization scaling
\sigma_{0}
ensures that gradient descent performs feature learning rather than learning random kernels. Finally, we further assume that the number of basis vectors in both
\mathcal{U}_{+1}
and
\mathcal{U}_{-1}
, and the number of patches in each input is at a constant order, i.e.
K,P\leq O(1)
. These conditions remain consistent with the Proposition
2.3
.

Now we are ready to deliver our main theorem, which characterizes the stable rank of the CNN filters
\mathbf{W}^{(T)}
after a certain number of iterations,
T
. Besides, we also present convergence results for both training loss
L_{S}(\mathbf{W}^{(T)})
and test loss
L_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbf{W}^{(T)})
, where the test loss
L_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbf{W})
is defined as
L_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbf{W})=\mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{X},y)\sim\mathcal{D}}[\ell(y\cdot f(\mathbf{W},\mathbf{X}))]
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Suppose that Condition
3.1
holds, and further assume that
\sigma_{\mathrm{noise}}\sqrt{d}\leq\widetilde{O}(n^{1/q})
. Then at any iteration
T=\eta^{-1}\mathrm{poly}(\sigma_{\mathrm{noise}}^{-2}d^{-1},\sigma_{0}^{-1},n,m,d)\geq\widetilde{\Omega}\big(\frac{m}{\eta\sigma_{0}^{q-2}}\big)
, with probability at least
1-O(m^{-1})
, the following conclusions hold.

1.

The stable rank of CNN filters is close to the rank of clean data,
2K
:

\displaystyle\Big|\mathrm{StableRank}(\mathbf{W}^{(T)})-2K\Big|\leq O\bigg(\frac{1}{\log T}\bigg).

(3.1)

2.

The training loss converges at a rate proportional to the reciprocal of the iterations:

\displaystyle L_{S}(\mathbf{W}^{(T)})\leq O\bigg(\frac{m^{2}}{\eta T}\bigg).

(3.2)

Additionally, this convergence rate is tight: if the number of object patches of each input
\mathbf{X}_{i}
, i.e.
|{\mathcal{S}}_{i}|
, is
1
, then it holds that
L_{S}(\mathbf{W}^{(T)})=\Theta\big(\frac{m^{2}}{\eta T}\big)
.

3.

The CNN trained by gradient descent achieves small test loss:

\displaystyle L_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbf{W}^{(T)})\leq O\bigg(\frac{m^{2}}{\eta T}\bigg)+\exp\big(-\widetilde{\Omega}(d)\big).

(3.3)

The proof of Theorem
3.2
is given in Section
6
. The first conclusion in
Theorem
3.2
demonstrates that, within a polynomial number of iterations, the CNN filters will reach a stable rank that is close to
2K
, which is the rank of the clean data.
Notably, this result is established under the condition that
\sigma_{\mathrm{noise}}\sqrt{d}\leq\widetilde{O}(n^{1/q})
. In comparison, by Proposition
2.3
, the stable rank of the data matrix is only close to
2K
under the condition
\sigma_{\mathrm{noise}}\sqrt{d}\leq O(1)
. A more illustrative comparison between the stable ranks of the CNN filters and the data matrix is given in Figure
2
. These comparisons clearly demonstrate the capability of CNNs to robustly capture the rank of clean data in the presence of relatively large background noises.

The second and third conclusions in Theorem
3.2
show that gradient descent can successfully train the CNN model to achieve small training and test loss. These two conclusions back up our analysis on the stable rank by demonstrating that our analysis is built upon a proper setup where CNN training is successful. We would like to remark that establishing these theoretical guarantees on the training and test losses is particularly challenging. First of all, due to the complicated nature of the CNN model, the training objective function (
2.1
) is highly non-convex, and therefore it is challenging to show that gradient descent can minimize the training loss arbitrarily close to zero, which implies the convergence to a
global minimum
. In addition, Theorem
3.2
is established in a high-dimensional setting where the number of parameters (
2md
) is significantly larger than the sample size
n
. In this context, the CNN will eventually
overfit
the training data (achieving a training loss arbitrarily close to zero, as shown in the second conclusion), making it challenging to establish strong guarantees on the test loss.

4
CNNs learn the clean-data subspace

In the previous section, we present our main results regarding the stable rank of CNN filters: the stable rank of CNN filters approaches the rank of the clean data. A natural conjecture to explain this phenomenon is that the filters successfully learn the structure of the clean data, namely the basis vectors associated with object patches. This also accounts for why two-layer CNNs can achieve small training and test losses simultaneously. To rigorously verify this conjecture, we carefully examine the components of each filter throughout the training process. Our findings reveal there exist two distinct patterns among the filters: some significantly learn a particular basis vector, while others remain close to their initial state. To better present our theoretical findings, we denote
\mathcal{N}
as the orthogonal complement to the subspace spanned by the basis vectors in
\mathcal{U}_{+1}\cup\ \mathcal{U}_{-1}
, i.e.
\mathcal{N}=\mathrm{span}(\mathcal{U}_{+1}\cup\ \mathcal{U}_{-1})^{\perp}
. We also denote
\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{N}}
as the projection matrix of the subspace
\mathcal{N}
, i.e.
\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{N}}=\mathbf{I}_{d}-\sum_{y\in\{\pm 1\}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\bm{\mu}_{y,k}\bm{\mu}_{y,k}^{\top}
. Then based on these notations, we provide a formal illustration of our theoretical findings in the following theorem.

Theorem 4.1
.

Denote
\mathcal{U}_{+1}=\{\bm{\mu}_{1,1},\bm{\mu}_{1,2},\ldots,\bm{\mu}_{1,K}\}
and
\mathcal{U}_{-1}=\{\bm{\mu}_{-1,1},\bm{\mu}_{-1,2},\ldots,\bm{\mu}_{-1,K}\}
.
Under the same conditions as Theorem
3.2
, there exist
K
distinct filters
\mathbf{w}_{1,r_{1,1}},\cdots,\mathbf{w}_{1,r_{1,K}}
in
\mathbf{W}_{+1}
corresponding to
\bm{\mu}_{1,1},\cdots,\bm{\mu}_{1,K}
, and
K
distinct filters
\mathbf{w}_{-1,r_{-1,k}},\cdots,\mathbf{w}_{-1,r_{-1,K}}
in
\mathbf{W}_{-1}
, corresponding to
\bm{\mu}_{-1,1},\cdots,\bm{\mu}_{-1,K}
, respectively. At any large iteration
T=\eta^{-1}\mathrm{poly}(d^{-1}\sigma_{\mathrm{noise}}^{-2},\sigma_{0}^{-1},n,m,d)\geq\widetilde{\Omega}\big(\frac{m}{\eta\sigma_{0}^{q-2}}\big)
,
the following results hold for all
j\in\{\pm 1\}
and
k\in[K]
, with probability at least
1-O(m^{-1})
:

\displaystyle\Big\|\mathbf{w}_{j,r_{j,k}}^{(T)}-\big(m\log(T)\big)\bm{\mu}_{j,k}\Big\|_{2}\leq O\big(m\log(m\vee\eta^{-1})\big);

\displaystyle\big\|\mathbf{w}_{j,r}^{(T)}\big\|_{2}\leq O(m^{-1})+\widetilde{O}(\sigma_{0}d^{1/2}),\kern 5.0pt\text{if}\kern 5.0ptr\neq r_{j,k}.

Moreover,
\big\|\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{N}}\cdot(\mathbf{w}_{j,r}^{(T)}-\mathbf{w}_{j,r}^{(0)})\big\|_{2}\leq O(\sigma_{0}n^{1/2})
for all
j\in\{-1,+1\}
and
r\in[m]
.

Theorem
4.1
demonstrate that for each basis vector
\bm{\mu}_{j,k}
, there exists exactly one distinct filter
\mathbf{w}_{j,r_{j,k}}
can significantly learn this basis vector. Specifically, Theorem
4.1
straightforwardly implies that when
T
is large, the length of the projection of
\mathbf{w}_{j,r_{j,r}}^{(t)}
onto
\bm{\mu}_{j,k}
, i.e.,
\langle\mathbf{w}_{j,r_{j,r}}^{(t)},\bm{\mu}_{j,k}\rangle
, is bounded from both above and below at the same order of
m\log(T)
. This indicates that we can accurately characterize the growth of
\mathbf{w}_{j,r_{j,r}}^{(t)}
along the direction of
\bm{\mu}_{j,k}
. For those other filters not corresponding to any basis vector, their norm is upper bounded by a small value, suggesting that they remain close to their initialization throughout the training process. Additionally, for all filters, their projection onto the subspace
\mathcal{N}
remains at an exceptionally low level. This implies that all filters, including those that can increase significantly, learn very little from the background noises. Therefore, Theorem
4.1
elaborates on the properties of CNNs trained by gradient descent in more details, and provides supplementary results that further explain why the stable rank results of the CNN holds as stated in Theorem
3.2
.

According to the conclusions of Theorem
4.1
, we can intuitively treat
\mathbf{w}_{j,r_{j,k}}^{(T)}\approx m\log(T)\bm{\mu}_{j,k}
and
\mathbf{w}_{j,r}^{(T)}\approx\mathbf{0}
if
r\neq r_{j,k}
. One interesting finding is that the coefficient
m\log(T)
remains consistent across different basis vectors. Based on this observation, if we rearrange the rows in
\mathbf{W}_{j}
such that the first
K
rows exactly represent those
K
filters corresponding to different basis vectors from
\mathcal{U}_{j}
in order, the permutated filter matrix will align directionally with the matrix
\mathbf{W}_{j}^{*}=[\bm{\mu}_{j,1},\cdots,\bm{\mu}_{j,K},\mathbf{0}_{d},\cdots,\mathbf{0}_{d}]^{\top}
. The following corollary provides a formal illustration of this conclusion.

Corollary 4.2
.

Let
\mathbf{W}_{j}^{*}=[\bm{\mu}_{j,1},\cdots,\bm{\mu}_{j,K},\mathbf{0}_{d},\cdots,\mathbf{0}_{d}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
with
j\in\{\pm 1\}
. Under the same conditions as Theorem
3.2
, there exist permutation matrices
\mathbf{P}_{+},\mathbf{P}_{-}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
. At any large iteration

T=\eta^{-1}\mathrm{poly}(\sigma_{\mathrm{noise}}^{-2}d^{-1},\sigma_{0}^{-1},n,m,d)\geq\widetilde{\Omega}\big(\frac{m}{\eta\sigma_{0}^{q-2}}\big)
, with probability at least
1-O(m^{-1})
, it holds that

\displaystyle\Bigg\|\frac{\mathbf{W}_{+1}^{*}}{\|\mathbf{W}_{+1}^{*}\|_{F}}-\mathbf{P}_{+}\frac{\mathbf{W}_{+1}^{(T)}}{\|\mathbf{W}_{+1}^{(T)}\|_{F}}\Bigg\|_{F},\Bigg\|\frac{\mathbf{W}_{-1}^{*}}{\|\mathbf{W}_{-1}^{*}\|_{F}}-\mathbf{P}_{-}\frac{\mathbf{W}_{-1}^{(T)}}{\|\mathbf{W}_{-1}^{(T)}\|_{F}}\Bigg\|_{F}\leq O\bigg(\frac{1}{\log(T)}\bigg).

Corollary
4.2
demonstrates that as the training proceeds, the normalized filter matrix will approach the normalized clean data matrix at a rate of
O\big(\frac{1}{\log(T)}\big)
. This corollary intuitively explains why the stable rank of CNN filters
\mathbf{W}^{(T)}
approaches
2K
. Based on the directional convergence results, we can approximately take
\mathbf{W}_{+1}^{(T)}\approx\frac{\|\mathbf{W}_{+1}^{(T)}\|_{F}}{\|\mathbf{W}_{+1}^{*}\|_{F}}\mathbf{P}_{+}^{-1}\mathbf{W}_{+1}^{*}
. Multiplication by an orthogonal matrix or a constant does not affect the stable rank, as the stable rank is determined by the relative scales of different singular values. Since the stable rank of
\mathbf{W}_{+1}^{*}
is
K
, we can conclude the stable rank of
\mathbf{W}_{+1}^{(T)}
will approach
K
, at the same rate as their directional convergence. Similar conclusion also holds for
\mathbf{W}_{-1}^{(T)}
. Additionally, as the components of
\mathbf{W}_{+1}^{(T)}
and
\mathbf{W}_{-1}^{(T)}
are nearly orthogonal based on our assumption that
\mathrm{span}(\mathcal{U}_{+1})
and
\mathrm{span}(\mathcal{U}_{-1})
are orthogonal, it is evident that the stable rank of
\mathbf{W}^{(T)}
is approximately
2K
.

6
Overview of proof technique

In this section, we explain how we establish our main theoretical results. We begin by introducing several key lemmas that play a crucial role in proving Theorem
4.1
. We then present a proof for Theorem
4.1
based on these lemmas. Finally, we demonstrate the proof for Theorem
3.2
, which can be easily derived from the results of Theorem
4.1
.

6.1
Proof of Theorem
4.1

We first introduce some further notations regarding the data model
\mathcal{D}
defined in Definition
2.2
. For each input
\mathbf{X}_{i}
with
i\in[n]
,
{\mathcal{S}}_{i}
determines the indices of the object patches among this input, with
s_{i}=|{\mathcal{S}}_{i}|
. We denote
\bm{\nu}_{i,1},\cdots,\bm{\nu}_{i,s_{i}}\in\mathcal{U}_{y_{i}}
as the basis vectors in the
i
-th training data point.
Additionally, we denote
\bm{\xi}_{i,1},\bm{\xi}_{i,2},\cdots,\bm{\xi}_{i,P-s_{i}}
as the Gaussian noise vectors assigned to the background patches, i.e.,
\bm{\xi}_{i,k^{\prime}}\sim N\big(\mathbf{0},\sigma_{\mathrm{noise}}^{2}\cdot(\mathbf{I}_{d}-\sum_{y\in\{\pm 1\}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\bm{\mu}_{y,k}\bm{\mu}_{y,k}^{\top})\big)
. Based on these notations,
we can rewrite our gradient descent iterative formula (
2.3
) as

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}_{j,r}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{w}_{j,r}^{(t)}-\eta\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{w}_{j,r}}L_{S}(\mathbf{W}^{(t)})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{w}_{j,r}^{(t)}-\frac{\eta}{nm}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{p=1}^{P}\ell_{i}^{\prime(t)}\cdot\sigma^{\prime}(\langle\mathbf{w}_{j,r}^{(t)},\mathbf{x}_{i}^{(p)}\rangle)\cdot jy_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{(p)}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{w}_{j,r}^{(t)}-\frac{\eta}{nm}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{P-s_{i}}\ell_{i}^{\prime(t)}\cdot\sigma^{\prime}(\langle\mathbf{w}_{j,r}^{(t)},\bm{\xi}_{i,k^{\prime}}\rangle)\cdot jy_{i}\bm{\xi}_{i,k^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{\eta}{nm}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{s_{i}}\ell_{i}^{\prime(t)}\cdot\sigma^{\prime}(\langle\mathbf{w}_{j,r}^{(t)},\bm{\nu}_{i,k}\rangle)\cdot jy_{i}\bm{\nu}_{i,k},

(6.1)

where
\ell_{i}^{\prime(t)}=\ell^{\prime}[y_{i}\cdot f(\mathbf{W}^{(t)},\mathbf{X}_{i})]
.
This iterative rule reveals that the update of all filters
\mathbf{w}_{j,r}
’s are always a linear combination of the basis vectors from
\mathcal{U}_{+1}
,
\mathcal{U}_{-1}
, and Gaussian noise vectors
\bm{\xi}_{i,k^{\prime}}
’s generated in the subspace
\mathcal{N}
, which is the complement subspace of
\mathcal{U}_{+1}\cup\ \mathcal{U}_{-1}
. Based on these observations, to characterize the content learned by each filter, it is sufficient to study the inner products between this filter and the basis vectors and noise vectors. For each basis vector
\bm{\mu}_{y,k}
from
\mathcal{U}_{y}
, we provide a careful analysis of the dynamic process of its inner product with each filter
\mathbf{w}_{j,r}
. As to demonstrate our desired conclusions regarding stable rank, we need a refined quantification of the projection of each filter onto each basis vector. While for noise vectors, we do not consider the inner product with each filter. Instead, we focus on the projection of each filter
\mathbf{w}_{j,r}
into the subspace
\mathcal{N}
, i.e.
\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{N}}\cdot\mathbf{w}_{j,r}^{(t)}
. We propose this design as a small upper-bound for
\big\|\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{N}}\cdot\mathbf{w}_{j,r}^{(t)}\big\|_{2}
is sufficient to illustrate that the filter
\mathbf{w}_{j,r}
learns little from any noise vectors.

When training starts, filters exhibit distinct patterns according to initialization.

The working mechanism of CNNs, in which each filter interacts with all filters, implies that these filters exhibit symmetry and translation invariance. These properties also hold when the filters are updated using gradient descent. Specifically, we note that the iterative rule (
6.1
) is nearly the same for different filters, differing only in their initialization along various directions, i.e.
\langle\mathbf{w}_{j,r}^{(0)},\bm{\mu}_{y,k}\rangle
’s and
\langle\mathbf{w}_{j,r}^{(0)},\bm{\xi}_{i,k^{\prime}}\rangle
’s. Based on this observation, we intuitively conjecture that the initialization of the inner products
\langle\mathbf{w}_{j,r}^{(0)},\bm{\mu}_{y,k}\rangle
might play a key role in analyzing the increasing trajectory of
\langle\mathbf{w}_{j,r}^{(t)},\bm{\mu}_{y,k}\rangle
. Therefore, we focus on the filters with the specific initialization, and successfully demonstrate that the filters with the maximum inner product with a basis vector at initialization will exhibit significantly distinct patterns during the early training phase. We provide a formal illustration of this phenomenon in the following lemma.

Lemma 6.1
.

Under the same conditions as Theorem
3.2
,
for each basis vector
\bm{\mu}_{j,k}
from
\mathcal{U}_{j}
, let
\mathbf{w}_{j,r_{j,k}}
denote the filter in
\mathbf{W}_{j}
with largest inner product with
\bm{\mu}_{j,k}
, i.e
\mathbf{w}_{j,r_{j,k}}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}_{\mathbf{w}_{j,r}\in\mathbf{W}_{j}}\langle\mathbf{w}_{j,r}^{(0)},\bm{\mu}_{j,k}\rangle
. Then with probability at least
1-O(m^{-1})
, the filters
\mathbf{w}_{j,r_{j,k}}
’s are distinct for different basis vector
\bm{\mu}_{j,k}
, and there exist an iteration number
T_{1}=\widetilde{\Theta}\big(\frac{m}{\eta\sigma_{0}^{q-2}}\big)
such that the following results hold for all
j\in\{\pm 1\}
and
k\in[K]
:

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{w}_{j,r_{j,k}}^{(T_{1})}\bm{\mu}_{j,k}\rangle\geq\kappa,\kern 5.0pt\text{and}\kern 5.0pt\big|\langle\mathbf{w}_{j,r}^{(T_{1})},\bm{\mu}_{j,k}\rangle\big|\leq\frac{1}{4Km},\kern 5.0pt\text{if}\kern 5.0ptr\neq r_{j,k}.

Moreover,
\big\|\bm{\Xi}_{j,r}^{(t)}\big\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{\sigma_{0}^{2}nP}{2}
hold for all
j\in\{\pm 1\}
,
r\in[m]
and
0\leq t\leq T_{1}
, where
\bm{\Xi}_{j,r}^{(t)}=\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{N}}\cdot(\mathbf{w}_{j,r}^{(t)}-\mathbf{w}_{j,r}^{(0)})
.

Theorem
3.2
asserts that for each
\bm{\mu}_{j,k}\in\mathcal{U}_{j}
, there exists a specific filter in
\mathbf{W}_{j}
can significantly learn this basis vector. Lemma
6.1
identifies this specific filter as the one with the largest inner product at initialization. Furthermore, Lemma
6.1
demonstrates there exist an iteration
T_{1}=\widetilde{\Theta}\big(\frac{m}{\eta\sigma_{0}^{q-2}}\big)
. At this iteration, those filters having the largest inner product with certain basis vectors, i.e.
\mathbf{w}_{j,r_{j,k}}
’s, will exhibit significantly different patterns compared to other filters: the projection of
\mathbf{w}_{j,r_{j,k}}^{(T)}
onto
\bm{\mu}_{j,k}
attains
\kappa
, while those of other filters stay at a small value. Moreover, the projection of all filters into the subspace
\mathcal{N}
is extremely small. Since each entry of filters is initialized from standard Gaussian distribution, by the symmetry of Gaussian distribution, the same filter having the largest inner product for multiple basis vectors simultaneously can only occur with extremely small probability. Therefore, we can intuitively get the idea that
\mathbf{W}_{j}^{(T_{1})}
already exhibits a low-rank structure with rank
K
: it contains
K
row, each approximately aligned with a distinct basis vector in
\mathcal{U}_{j}
, while other rows remain close to
\mathbf{0}
.

Matching lower and upper bounds for projections when loss converges.

As we discussed above, the conclusions of Lemma
6.1
can already intuitively imply that
\mathbf{W}_{j}^{(T_{1})}
approximately exhibits a low-rank structure with rank
K
. However, to obtain an effective estimation of the stable rank, it is essential to accurately characterize all singular values, providing both lower and upper bounds. The conclusion of Lemma
6.1
presenting merely a lower bound
\kappa
is not sufficient to derive a satisfactory conclusion regarding stable rank. Additionally, it is clear that a constant-order lower bound for the inner products
\langle\mathbf{w}_{j,r_{j,k}}^{(t)},\bm{\mu}_{j,k}\rangle
’s is insufficient to guarantee the convergence of the loss, given our two-layer CNNs model (
2.1
). Therefore, we consider the subsequent training stage of two-layer CNNs, and demonstrate a matching upper and lower bound for the increase rate of
\langle\mathbf{w}_{j,r_{j,k}}^{(t)},\bm{\mu}_{j,k}\rangle
’s in the following lemma.

Lemma 6.2
.

Under the same conditions as Theorem
3.2
, and with
\mathbf{w}_{j,r_{j,k}},\bm{\Xi}_{j,r}
defined as in Lemma
6.1
, at any large iteration
T^{*}=T_{1}+\eta^{-1}\mathrm{poly}(\sigma_{\mathrm{noise}}^{-2}d^{-1},\sigma_{0}^{-1},n,m,d)
, the following results hold for all
j\in\{\pm 1\}
and
k\in[K]
, with probability at least
1-O(m^{-1})
:

\displaystyle\Big|\langle\mathbf{w}_{j,r_{j,k}}^{(T^{*})},\bm{\mu}_{j,k}\rangle-m\log(T^{*}-T_{1})-2m\log m+m\log\delta\Big|\leq O(m);

\displaystyle\big|\langle\mathbf{w}_{j,r}^{(T^{*})},\bm{\mu}_{j,k}\rangle\big|\leq\frac{1}{2Km},\kern 5.0pt\text{if}\kern 5.0ptr\neq r_{j,k}.

Moreover,
\big\|\bm{\Xi}_{j,r}^{(t)}\big\|_{2}^{2}\leq 2\sigma_{0}^{2}nP
for all
j\in\{\pm 1\}
,
r\in[m]
and
0\leq t\leq T^{*}
.

Lemma
6.2
illustrates that during any polynomial number of iterations exceeding
T_{1}
defined in Lemma
6.1
, the increase of the inner product
\langle\mathbf{w}_{j,r_{j,k}}^{(t)},\bm{\mu}_{j,k}\rangle
is simultaneously bounded from above and below. Moreover, both the upper and lower bounds have matching rates, increasing logarithmically with respect to
t
. Besides, the projections of other filters onto each basis vector or the subspace
\mathcal{N}
, always remain bounded by some small value. As we have discussed previously, the filters
\mathbf{w}_{j,r_{j,k}}
are distinct for different basis vectors with high probability. These observations intuitively explain the conclusions that
\mathbf{w}_{j,r_{j,k}}^{(T^{*})}\approx m\log(T^{*}-T_{1})\bm{\mu}_{j,k}
, and
\mathbf{w}_{j,r}^{(T^{*})}\approx\mathbf{w}_{j,r}^{(0)}
if
r\neq r_{j,k}
in Theorem
4.1
.

The filters in
\mathbf{W}_{+1}
barely learn basis in
\mathcal{U}_{-1}
and vice versa.
In Lemmas
6.1
and
6.2
, we clearly quantify the increase of
\langle\mathbf{w}_{1,r}^{(t)},\bm{\mu}_{1,k}\rangle
and
\langle\mathbf{w}_{-1,r}^{(t)},\bm{\mu}_{-1,k}\rangle
through the entire training process. However, by the gradient descent iterative rule (
6.1
), we still need to calculate
\langle\mathbf{w}_{1,r},\bm{\mu}_{-1,k}\rangle
and
\langle\mathbf{w}_{-1,r},\bm{\mu}_{1,k}\rangle
. The following lemma indicates that these terms are bounded by a small amount throughout the training.

Lemma 6.3
.

Under the same conditions as Theorem
3.2
, for all
t>0
,
r\in[m]
, and
k\in[K]
, with probability at least
1-O(m^{-1})
, it holds that

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{w}_{1,r}^{(t)},\bm{\mu}_{-1,k}\rangle,\langle\mathbf{w}_{-1,r}^{(t)},\bm{\mu}_{1,k}\rangle\leq\widetilde{O}(\sigma_{0}).

Now, we are ready to prove our main Theorem
4.1
.

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

We establish the proof for
j=1
, while the proof for
j=-1
is totally identical. Let
T=\eta^{-1}\mathrm{poly}(\sigma_{\mathrm{noise}}^{-2}d^{-1},\sigma_{0}^{-1},n,m,d)\geq\widetilde{\Omega}\big(\frac{m}{\eta\sigma_{0}^{q-2}}\big)
. For any
\bm{\mu}_{1,k}\in\mathcal{U}_{+1}
and its corresponding filter
\mathbf{w}_{1,r_{1,k}}
, we can calculate the norm of
\mathbf{w}_{1,r_{1,k}}
as

\displaystyle\big\|\mathbf{w}_{1,r_{1,k}}^{(T)}-\big(m\log T\big)\cdot\bm{\mu}_{1,k}\big\|_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big\|\mathbf{w}_{1,r_{1,k}}^{(0)}+\sum_{y\in{\pm 1}}\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{K}\langle\mathbf{w}_{1,r_{1,k}}^{(T)}-\mathbf{w}_{1,r_{1,k}}^{(0)},\bm{\mu}_{y,k^{\prime}}\rangle\bm{\mu}_{y,k^{\prime}}+\bm{\Xi}_{1,r_{1,k}}^{(T)}-\big(m\log T\big)\cdot\bm{\mu}_{1,k}\big\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\big\|\mathbf{w}_{1,r_{1,k}}^{(0)}\big\|_{2}+\sum_{y\in{\pm 1}}\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{K}\big|\langle\mathbf{w}_{1,r_{1,k}}^{(0)},\bm{\mu}_{y,k^{\prime}}\rangle\big|+\sum_{k^{\prime}\neq k}\big|\langle\mathbf{w}_{1,r_{1,k}}^{(T)},\bm{\mu}_{1,k^{\prime}}\rangle\big|+\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{K}\big|\langle\mathbf{w}_{1,r_{1,k}}^{(T)},\bm{\mu}_{-1,k^{\prime}}\rangle\big|

\displaystyle+\big|\langle\mathbf{w}_{1,r_{1,k}}^{(T)},\bm{\mu}_{1,k}\rangle-m\log T\big|+\big\|\bm{\Xi}_{1,r_{1,k}}^{(T)}\big\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\widetilde{O}(\sigma_{0}d^{1/2})+\widetilde{O}(2K\sigma_{0})+O\Big(\frac{K}{m}\Big)+\widetilde{O}(K\sigma_{0})+O\big(m\log(m\vee\eta^{-1})\big)+O(\sigma_{0}n^{1/2})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle O\big(m\log(m\vee\eta^{-1})\big).

The first inequality holds by triangle inequality. The second inequality is derived from Lemma
6.2
, Lemma
6.3
, and concentration results in Appendix
E.1
, which guarantee that
\big\|\mathbf{w}_{1,r}^{(0)}\big\|_{2}\leq\widetilde{O}(\sigma_{0}d^{1/2})
and
\big|\langle\mathbf{w}_{1,r}^{(0)},\bm{\mu}_{y,k}\rangle\big|\leq\widetilde{O}(\sigma_{0})
for all
r\in[m]
,
y\in\{\pm 1\}
and
k\in[K]
. The last inequality follows from the scale relationships among these parameters as assumed in Condition
3.1
.
For other filters
\mathbf{w}_{1,r}
with
r\neq r_{1,k}
for all
k\in[K]
, we have

\displaystyle\big\|\mathbf{w}_{1,r}^{(T)}\big\|_{2}\leq

\displaystyle\big\|\mathbf{w}_{1,r}^{(0)}\big\|_{2}+\sum_{y\in\{\pm 1\}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\big|\langle\mathbf{w}_{1,r}^{(T)}-\mathbf{w}_{1,r}^{(0)},\bm{\mu}_{y,k}\rangle\big|+\|\bm{\Xi}_{1,r}^{(T)}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq

7
Conclusions and future work

In this paper, we study the rank of convolutional neural networks (CNNs) trained by gradient descent. We theoretically show that the two-layer CNN will converge to a low-rank structure which aligns with the ’clean’ complexity of the data sample which further implies that the neural network exhibits robustness of the rank to noises in data. We make numerical experiments both on real data sets MNIST, CIFAR10 and our synthetic data sets. The experiment results support our theoretical findings. We predict this result can also be extended to deeper and more general neural networks, which is a feasible future research direction.

Our paper studies the “rank robustness” phenomenon for two-layer CNN models, while it will be more important and interesting to explore deeper models. Then, we will need to explore a preciser definition of the rank of the model in the deeper setting as it will also be related to the intermediate embedding. Exploring this direction will be one of our future studies. Besides, it is also interesting to investigate whether a similar phenomenon can be also observed for other architectures, such as vision transformer, and other tasks, such as generative model or self-supervise learning. This can better help us understand the distinct training patterns across different tasks with different architectures.

C.3

Before we prove the Lemma

Lemma C.2

Under Condition

for all

Proof of Lemma
C.2
.

We first prove that for all

, if
\langle\mathbf{w}_{j,r}^{(0)},\bm{\mu}_{j,k}\rangle\geq\langle\mathbf{w}_{j,r^{\prime}}^{(0)},\bm{\mu}_{j,k}\rangle

\langle\mathbf{w}_{j,r}^{(t)},\bm{\mu}_{j,k}\rangle\geq\langle\mathbf{w}_{j,r^{\prime}}^{(t)},\bm{\mu}_{j,k}\rangle

. By multiplying
\bm{\mu}_{j,k}
on both sides of (
6.1
), and the orthogonality between basis vectors and noises, we obtain that

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{w}_{j,r}^{(t+1)},\bm{\mu}_{j,k}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\mathbf{w}_{j,r}^{(t)},\bm{\mu}_{j,k}\rangle-\frac{\eta}{nm}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{j,k}}\ell^{\prime}[y_{i}\cdot f(\mathbf{W}^{(t)},\mathbf{X}_{i})]\sigma^{\prime}\big(\langle\mathbf{w}_{j,r}^{(t)},\bm{\mu}_{j,k}\rangle\big)jy_{i}

\displaystyle=\langle\mathbf{w}_{j,r}^{(t)},\bm{\mu}_{j,k}\rangle-\frac{\eta}{nm}\sigma^{\prime}\big(\langle\mathbf{w}_{j,r}^{(t)},\bm{\mu}_{j,k}\rangle\big)\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{j,k}}\ell_{i}^{\prime(t)}jy_{i}.

By definition of

. Therefore

\langle\mathbf{w}_{j,r}^{(0)},\bm{\mu}_{j,k}\rangle<0

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{w}_{j,r}^{(t+1)},\bm{\mu}_{j,k}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\mathbf{w}_{j,r}^{(t)},\bm{\mu}_{j,k}\rangle-\frac{\eta}{nm}\sigma^{\prime}\big(\langle\mathbf{w}_{j,r}^{(t)},\bm{\mu}_{j,k}\rangle\big)\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{j,k}}\ell_{i}^{\prime(t)}.

(C.3)

And we could notice that the only item specific to filter

\displaystyle x^{(t+1)}_{r}=x^{(t)}_{r}+\eta C_{t}\sigma^{\prime}\big(x^{(t)}_{r}\big)

is a non-decreasing function. Therefore we conclude that a filter with a larger initialization will always have a larger increment in each iteration, which completes the proof for the case that

Appendix E

E.1
Concentration results

Lemma E.1
.

Suppose that
\delta>0
, then for any
\mathcal{I}\subseteq[n]
, with probability at least
1-\delta

\displaystyle|\{i\in\mathcal{I}:y_{i}=1\}|,~|\{i\in\mathcal{I}:y_{i}=-1\}|=\frac{|\mathcal{I}|}{2}+O\Big(\sqrt{|\mathcal{I}|\log(1/\delta)}\Big).

E.1
.

By Hoeffding’s inequality, with probability at least
1-O(\delta)
, we have

\displaystyle\Bigg|\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\ind\{y_{i}=1\}-\frac{1}{2}\Bigg|\leq O\Bigg(\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{|\mathcal{I}|}}\Bigg).

Therefore,

\displaystyle|\{i\in\mathcal{I}:y_{i}=1\}|=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\ind\{y_{i}=1\}=\frac{|\mathcal{I}|}{2}+O\Big(\sqrt{|\mathcal{I}|\log(1/\delta)}\Big).

This proves the result for
|\{i\in\mathcal{I}:y_{i}=1\}|
. The proof for
|\{i\in\mathcal{I}:y_{i}=-1\}|
is exactly the same, and we can conclude the proof by applying a union bound.
∎

Lemma E.2

\delta>0
, then for

1-\delta/2
, we have

\displaystyle\Bigg|K_{1,k}-\frac{\sum_{y_{i}=1}s_{i}}{K}\Bigg|,\Bigg|K_{-1,k}-\frac{\sum_{y_{i}=-1}s_{i}}{K}\Bigg|\leq O\Big(\sqrt{n\log(1/\delta)}\Big).

Applying a union bound over the results from two inequalities above completes the proof.
∎

Lemma E.4
.

Suppose that
z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, then
\mathbb{P}(|z|\leq t)=O\left(t\right)
.

Proof of Lemma
E.4
.

We use
\phi(x)
to denote the density function of the standard Gaussian random variable, and then we know that
\max\phi(x)=\phi(0)
. By this fact,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|z|\leq t)=2\int_{0}^{t}\phi(x)dx\leq 2\phi(0)t=O\left(t\right),

which completes the proof.
∎

Lemma E.5
.

Suppose that
\delta>0
and
d=\Omega\big(\log(nm/\delta)\big)
. Then with probability at least
1-O(\delta)
, it holds that

\displaystyle\|\bm{\xi}_{i,k}\|_{2}^{2}=\Theta(\sigma_{\mathrm{noise}}^{2}d);

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{w}_{j,r}^{(0)}\|_{2}^{2}=\Theta(\sigma_{0}^{2}d);

\displaystyle\big|\langle\bm{\xi}_{i,k},\bm{\xi}_{i^{\prime},k^{\prime}}\rangle\big|\leq O\big(\sigma_{\mathrm{noise}}^{2}\cdot\sqrt{d\log(n^{2}/\delta)}\big)

for all
j\in\{+1,-1\}
,
r\in[m]
, and all
i,i^{\prime}\in[n]
,
k\in[P-s_{i}],k^{\prime}\in[P-s_{i^{\prime}}]
such that
\{i,k\}\neq\{i^{\prime},k^{\prime}\}
.

Proof of Lemma
E.5
.

By Bernstein’s inequality, with probability at least
1-O(\delta/n)
we have

\displaystyle\big|\|\bm{\xi}_{i,k}\|_{2}^{2}-\sigma_{\mathrm{noise}}^{2}d\big|=O\big(\sigma_{\mathrm{noise}}^{2}\cdot\sqrt{d\log(n/\delta)}\big).

Therefore, as long as
d=\Omega\big(\log(n/\delta)\big)
, we have

\displaystyle\|\bm{\xi}_{i,k}\|_{2}^{2}=\Theta(\sigma_{\mathrm{noise}}^{2}d).

Similarly, by Bernstein’s inequality, with probability at least
1-O(\delta/m)
we have

\displaystyle\big|\|\mathbf{w}_{j,r}^{(0)}\|_{2}^{2}-\sigma_{0}^{2}d\big|=O\big(\sigma_{\mathrm{noise}}^{2}\cdot\sqrt{d\log(m/\delta)}\big).

Therefore, as long as
d=\Omega\big(\log(m/\delta)\big)
, we have

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{w}_{j,r}^{(0)}\|_{2}^{2}=\Theta(\sigma_{0}^{2}d).

Moreover, for any
i,i^{\prime},k,k^{\prime}
with
\{i,k\}\neq\{i^{\prime},k^{\prime}\}
, clearly
\langle\bm{\xi}_{i,k},\bm{\xi}_{i^{\prime},k^{\prime}}\rangle
has mean zero and by Bernstein’s inequality, with probability at least
1-O(\delta/n^{2})
we have

\displaystyle|\langle\bm{\xi}_{i,k},\bm{\xi}_{i^{\prime},k^{\prime}}\rangle|\leq O\big(\sigma_{\mathrm{noise}}^{2}\cdot\sqrt{d\log(n^{2}/\delta)}\big).

Applying a union bound completes the proof.
∎

Lemma E.6
.

Suppose that
d\geq\Omega\big(\log(mn/\delta)\big)
,
m=\Omega\big(\log(1/\delta)\big)
. Then with probability at least
1-O(\delta)
, it holds that

\displaystyle\big|\langle\mathbf{w}_{j,r}^{(0)},\bm{\mu}_{j,k}\rangle\big|=O\left(\sqrt{\log(m/\delta)}\cdot\sigma_{0}\right),

\displaystyle\big|\langle\mathbf{w}_{j,r}^{(0)},\bm{\xi}_{i,k^{\prime}}\rangle\big|=O\left(\sqrt{\log(mn/\delta)}\cdot\sigma_{0}\sigma_{\mathrm{noise}}\sqrt{d}\right)

for all
r\in[m]
,
j\in\{\pm 1\}
,
i\in[n]
,
k\in[K]
and
k^{\prime}\in[P-s_{i}]
. Besides,

\displaystyle\max_{r\in[m]}\langle\mathbf{w}_{j,r}^{(0)},\bm{\mu}_{j,k}\rangle=\Omega(\sigma_{0}),

\displaystyle\max_{r\in[m]}\langle\mathbf{w}_{j,r}^{(0)},\bm{\xi}_{i,k^{\prime}}\rangle=\Omega\left(\sigma_{0}\sigma_{\mathrm{noise}}\sqrt{d}\right)

for all
j\in\{\pm 1\}
,
i\in[n]
,
k\in[K]
and
k^{\prime}\in[P-s_{i}]
. Moreover,

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{w}_{j,r}^{(0)},\bm{\mu}_{j,k}\rangle\left(1+\Theta\left(\frac{\delta}{m^{2}}\right)\right)\leq\max_{r\in[m]}\langle\mathbf{w}_{j,r}^{(0)},\bm{\mu}_{j,k}\rangle

for all
r\neq\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}_{r\in[m]}\langle\mathbf{w}_{j,r}^{(0)},\bm{\mu}_{j,k}\rangle
,
j\in\{\pm 1\}
and
k\in[K]
.

Proof of Lemma
E.6
.

It is clear that for each
r\in[m]
,
\langle\mathbf{w}_{j,r}^{(0)},\bm{\mu}_{j,k}\rangle
is a Gaussian random variable with mean zero and variance
\sigma_{0}^{2}
. Therefore, by Gaussian tail bound and union bound, with probability at least
1-O(\delta)
,

\displaystyle\big|\langle\mathbf{w}_{j,r}^{(0)},\bm{\mu}_{j,k}\rangle\big|\leq O\big(\sqrt{\log(m/\delta)}\cdot\sigma_{0}\big).

Moreover,
\mathbb{P}\big(\sigma_{0}\|\bm{\mu}\|_{2}/2>\langle\mathbf{w}_{j,r}^{(0)},\bm{\mu}_{j,k}\rangle\big)
is an absolute constant, and therefore by the condition on
m
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\sigma_{0}/2\leq\max_{r\in[m]}\langle\mathbf{w}_{j,r}^{(0)},\bm{\mu}_{j,k}\rangle\big)

\displaystyle=1-\mathbb{P}(\sigma_{0}/2>\max_{r\in[m]}\langle\mathbf{w}_{j,r}^{(0)},\bm{\mu}_{j,k}\rangle\big)

\displaystyle=1-\mathbb{P}\big(\sigma_{0}/2>\langle\mathbf{w}_{j,r}^{(0)},\bm{\mu}_{j,k}\rangle\big)^{2m}

\displaystyle\geq 1-O(\delta).

By Lemma
E.5
, with probability at least
1-O(\delta)
,
\|\bm{\xi}_{i,k^{\prime}}\|_{2}=\Theta\big(\sigma_{\mathrm{noise}}\sqrt{d}\big)
for all
i\in[n]
and
k^{\prime}\in[P-s_{i}]
. Therefore, the result for
\langle\mathbf{w}_{j,r}^{(0)},\bm{\xi}_{i,k^{\prime}}\rangle
follows the same proof as
\langle\mathbf{w}_{j,r}^{(0)},\bm{\mu}_{j,k}\rangle
.

Lastly, for different
r,r^{\prime}
and
\forall t>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{\big|\langle\mathbf{w}_{j,r}^{(0)},\bm{\mu}_{j,k}\rangle\big|\vee\big|\langle\mathbf{w}_{j,r^{\prime}}^{(0)},\bm{\mu}_{j,k}\rangle\big|}{\big|\langle\mathbf{w}_{j,r}^{(0)},\bm{\mu}_{j,k}\rangle\big|\wedge\big|\langle\mathbf{w}_{j,r^{\prime}}^{(0)},\bm{\mu}_{j,k}\rangle\big|}\leq 1+t\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(1-t\leq\frac{\big|\langle\mathbf{w}_{j,r}^{(0)},\bm{\mu}_{j,k}\rangle\big|}{\big|\langle\mathbf{w}_{j,r^{\prime}}^{(0)},\bm{\mu}_{j,k}\rangle\big|}\leq 1+t\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\big|\langle\mathbf{w}_{j,r}^{(0)},\bm{\mu}_{j,k}\rangle\big|\leq 2t\big|\langle\mathbf{w}_{j,r^{\prime}}^{(0)},\bm{\mu}_{j,k}\rangle\big|\right)=O(t),

where the last equality holds from Lemma
E.4
and the fact that
\langle\mathbf{w}_{j,r}^{(0)},\bm{\mu}_{j,k}\rangle
and
\langle\mathbf{w}_{j,r^{\prime}}^{(0)},\bm{\mu}_{j,k}\rangle
are independent Gaussian random variables with mean
0
and same variance. By this result, let
t=\Theta(\frac{\delta}{m^{2}})
and use union bound, we could deduce that with probability at least
1-O(\delta)
,

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{w}_{j,r}^{(0)},\bm{\mu}_{j,k}\rangle\left(1+\Theta\left(\frac{\delta}{m^{2}}\right)\right)\leq\max_{r\in[m]}\langle\mathbf{w}_{j,r}^{(0)},\bm{\mu}_{j,k}\rangle

for all
r\neq\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}_{r\in[m]}\langle\mathbf{w}_{j,r}^{(0)},\bm{\mu}_{j,k}\rangle
.
∎

E.2
Tensor power methods

The following lemmas are inspired by
Allen-Zhu and Li (2022)
;
Jelassi et al. (2022)

Lemma E.7
.

If a positive sequence
\{x_{t}\}_{t=0}^{\infty}
satisfies the updating rules
x_{t+1}=x_{t}+\eta\cdot C_{t}\cdot x_{t}^{q-1}
, then
\forall k\in\mathbb{N},\zeta\in(0,1)
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{t>0,x_{t}\leq(1+\zeta)^{k}x_{0}}\eta C_{t}\leq\frac{\zeta}{x_{0}^{q-2}}\frac{1-\frac{1}{(1+\zeta)^{(q-2)k}}}{1-\frac{1}{(1+\zeta)^{(q-2)}}}+\eta\cdot\left[(1+\zeta)^{q-1}\sum_{g=0}^{k-1}C_{{\mathcal{T}}_{g+1}-1}+C_{{\mathcal{T}}_{k}}\right],

(E.1)

and

\displaystyle\sum_{t>0,x_{t}\leq(1+\zeta)^{k}x_{0}}\eta C_{t}\geq\frac{\zeta}{x_{0}^{q-2}(1+\zeta)^{q-1}}\frac{1-\frac{1}{(1+\zeta)^{(q-2)k}}}{1-\frac{1}{(1+\zeta)^{(q-2)}}}-\frac{\eta}{(1+\zeta)^{q-1}}\sum_{g=1}^{k-1}C_{{\mathcal{T}}_{g}-1},

(E.2)

where
{\mathcal{T}}_{g}
be the first iteration such that
x_{t}\geq(1+\zeta)^{g}x_{0}

Proof of Lemma
E.7
.

By the definition of
{\mathcal{T}}_{g}
, we have

\displaystyle x_{{\mathcal{T}}_{g+1}}-x_{{\mathcal{T}}_{g}}=\sum_{t\in[{\mathcal{T}}_{g},{\mathcal{T}}_{g+1})}\eta C_{t}x_{t}^{q-1}\geq\sum_{t\in[{\mathcal{T}}_{g},{\mathcal{T}}_{g+1})}\eta\cdot C_{t}\cdot\big[x_{0}(1+\zeta)^{g}\big]^{q-1},

(E.3)

and

\displaystyle x_{{\mathcal{T}}_{g+1}}-x_{{\mathcal{T}}_{g}}

\displaystyle=x_{{\mathcal{T}}_{g+1}-1}-x_{{\mathcal{T}}_{g}}+\eta\cdot C_{{\mathcal{T}}_{g+1}-1}\cdot x_{{\mathcal{T}}_{g+1}-1}^{q-1}

\displaystyle\leq\zeta(1+\zeta)^{g}x_{0}+\eta\cdot C_{{\mathcal{T}}_{g+1}-1}\cdot\big[x_{0}(1+\zeta)^{g+1}\big]^{q-1}.

(E.4)

By combining (
E.3
) and (
E.4
) in order and rearranging some items, we could deduce,

\displaystyle\sum_{t\in[{\mathcal{T}}_{g},{\mathcal{T}}_{g+1})}\eta C_{t}\leq\frac{\zeta}{x_{0}^{q-2}[(1+\zeta)^{q-2}]^{g}}+\eta(1+\zeta)^{q-1}C_{{\mathcal{T}}_{g+1}-1}.

Take a telescoping sum of this result, and then we finish the proof of (
E.1
). For (
E.2
), considering the opposite direction of the inequalities (
E.3
) and (
E.4
), repeating the previous process will get the result.
∎

Lemma E.8
.

Suppose there are two positive sequence
\{x_{t}\}_{t=0}^{\infty}
and
\{y_{t}\}_{t=0}^{\infty}
satisfying the following updating rules:

\displaystyle x_{t+1}=x_{t}+\eta\cdot C_{t}\cdot{x_{t}}^{q-1};

\displaystyle y_{t+1}=y_{t}+\eta\cdot C_{t}\cdot{y_{t}}^{q-1},

with
q\geq 3
and
\frac{x_{0}}{y_{0}}\geq 1+c
, where
c
is a small positive number. For any two positive number
A_{x}
and
A_{y}
, let
T_{x}
,
T_{y}
are the first time s.t.
x_{T_{x}}\geq A_{x}
and
y_{T_{y}}\geq A_{y}
respectively. If we have
0<C_{t}<\bar{C}
and
\eta
and
y_{0}
are both sufficiently small such that
\eta=\widetilde{O}\left(\frac{c}{\bar{C}y_{0}^{q-3}A_{y}}\right)
and
\frac{y_{0}}{A_{y}}\leq O(c)
, then it holds that
T_{x}\leq T_{y}
.

Proof of Lemma
E.8
.

For a positive
\zeta>0
, let
k_{1},k_{2}
be the smallest integer s.t.
x_{0}(1+\zeta)^{k_{1}}\geq A_{x}
and
y_{0}(1+\zeta)^{k_{2}}\geq A_{y}
. From these definitions, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\log(\frac{A_{x}}{x_{0}})}{\log(1+\zeta)}\leq k_{1}<\frac{\log(\frac{A_{x}}{x_{0}})}{\log(1+\zeta)}+1,

and

\displaystyle\frac{\log(\frac{A_{y}}{y_{0}})}{\log(1+\zeta)}\leq k_{2}<\frac{\log(\frac{A_{y}}{y_{0}})}{\log(1+\zeta)}+1.

By Lemma
E.7
, we further derive that

\displaystyle\sum_{t=0}^{T_{x}}\eta C_{t}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\zeta}{x_{0}^{q-2}}\frac{1-\frac{1}{(1+\zeta)^{(q-2)k_{1}}}}{1-\frac{1}{(1+\zeta)^{(q-2)}}}+\eta\cdot\left[(1+\zeta)^{q-1}\sum_{g=0}^{k_{1}-1}C_{{\mathcal{T}}_{g+1}-1}+C_{{\mathcal{T}}_{k_{1}}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\zeta}{x_{0}^{q-2}}\frac{1}{1-\frac{1}{(1+\zeta)^{(q-2)}}}+\eta\cdot(1+\zeta)^{q-1}(k_{1}+1)\bar{C},

(E.5)

and

\displaystyle\sum_{t=0}^{T_{y}}\eta C_{t}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\zeta}{y_{0}^{q-2}(1+\zeta)^{q-1}}\frac{1-\frac{1}{(1+\zeta)^{(q-2)k_{2}}}}{1-\frac{1}{(1+\zeta)^{(q-2)}}}-\frac{\eta}{(1+\zeta)^{q-1}}\sum_{g=1}^{k_{2}-1}C_{{\mathcal{T}}_{g}-1}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\zeta}{y_{0}^{q-2}(1+\zeta)^{q-1}}\frac{1-\left(\frac{y_{0}}{A_{y}}\right)^{q-2}}{1-\frac{1}{(1+\zeta)^{(q-2)}}}-\frac{\eta}{(1+\zeta)^{q-1}}(k_{2}-1)\bar{C}.

(E.6)

We use (
E.6
) minus (
E.5
) and get

\displaystyle\sum_{t=0}^{T_{y}}\eta C_{t}-\sum_{t=0}^{T_{x}}\eta C_{t}

\displaystyle\geq\underbrace{\frac{\zeta}{1-\frac{1}{(1+\zeta)^{(q-2)}}}\left\{\frac{1-\left(\frac{y_{0}}{A_{y}}\right)^{q-2}}{y_{0}^{q-2}(1+\zeta)^{q-1}}-\frac{1}{x_{0}^{q-2}}\right\}}_{I_{1}}

\displaystyle\qquad-\underbrace{\eta\bar{C}\left\{\frac{k_{2}-1}{(1+\zeta)^{q-1}}+(1+\zeta)^{q-1}(k_{1}+1)\right\}}_{I_{2}}.

We consider the value of
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
separately and carefully choose
\zeta
such that

\displaystyle(1+\zeta)^{q-1}=\left(1-\frac{y_{0}}{A_{y}}\right)^{2}\left(1+c\right)^{q-2}=1+\Theta\left(c\right).

The last equality is from our assumption
\frac{y_{0}}{A_{y}}=O\left(c\right)
, and we could also conclude
\zeta=\Theta\left(c\right)
. Then for
I_{1}
, we have,

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\zeta}{y_{0}^{q-2}(1-\frac{1}{(1+\zeta)^{(q-2)}})\left(1+c\right)^{q-2}}\left\{\frac{1-\frac{y_{0}}{A_{y}}}{\left(1-\frac{y_{0}}{A_{y}}\right)^{2}}-1\right\}=\Omega\left(\frac{1}{y_{0}^{q-3}A_{y}}\right).

(E.7)

Because
\frac{1}{1-\frac{1}{(1+\zeta)^{(q-2)}}}=\Theta\left(\zeta\right)
. For
I_{2}
, we have,

\displaystyle I_{2}\leq\eta\bar{C}\Theta\left(k_{1}\vee k_{2}\right)=\eta\bar{C}\widetilde{\Theta}\left(\frac{1}{c}\right).

(E.8)

Now by combining (
E.7
) and (
E.8
), we could conclude that
\sum_{t=0}^{T_{y}}\eta C_{t}-\sum_{t=0}^{T_{x}}\eta C_{t}\geq 0
, which completes the proof.
∎

Lemma E.9
.

Suppose a positive sequence
\{x_{t}\}_{t=0}^{\infty}
satisfies the following iterative rules:

\displaystyle x_{t+1}\geq x_{t}+\eta\cdot C_{1}\cdot{x_{t}}^{q-1};

\displaystyle x_{t+1}\leq x_{t}+\eta\cdot C_{2}\cdot{x_{t}}^{q-1},

with
C_{2}\geq C_{1}>0
. For any
v>x_{0}
, let
T_{v}
be the first time such that
x_{t}\geq v
, then for any constant
\zeta>0
, we have

\displaystyle T_{v}\leq\frac{1+\zeta}{\eta C_{1}x_{0}^{q-2}}+\frac{(1+\zeta)^{q-1}C_{2}\log(\frac{v}{x_{0}})}{C_{1}},

(E.9)

and

\displaystyle T_{v}\geq\frac{1}{(1+\zeta)^{q-1}\eta C_{2}x_{0}^{q-2}}-\frac{\log(\frac{v}{x_{0}})}{(1+\zeta)^{q-2}}.

(E.10)

Proof of Lemma
E.9
.

Similar to the definition in Lemma
E.7
, let
{\mathcal{T}}_{g}
be the first iteration such that
x_{t}\geq(1+\zeta)^{g}x_{0}
. Moreover, let
g^{*}
be the smallest integer such that
(1+\zeta)^{g^{*}}x_{0}\geq v
, resulting

\displaystyle\frac{\log(\frac{v}{x_{0}})}{\log(1+\zeta)}\leq g^{*}<\frac{\log(\frac{v}{x_{0}})}{\log(1+\zeta)}+1.

For
t={\mathcal{T}}_{1}
,

\displaystyle x_{{\mathcal{T}}_{1}}\geq x_{0}+\sum_{t=0}^{{\mathcal{T}}_{1}-1}\eta C_{1}x_{t}^{q-1}\geq x_{0}+{\mathcal{T}}_{1}\eta C_{1}x_{0}^{q-1},

and we could obtain that

\displaystyle{\mathcal{T}}_{1}\leq\frac{x_{{\mathcal{T}}_{1}}-x_{0}}{\eta C_{1}x_{0}^{q-1}}.

(E.11)

Consider the upper-bound iteration of
x_{{\mathcal{T}}_{1}}
and the fact that
x_{{\mathcal{T}}_{1}-1}\leq x_{0}(1+\zeta)
, we could get

\displaystyle x_{{\mathcal{T}}_{1}}\leq x_{{\mathcal{T}}_{1}-1}+\eta C_{2}x_{{\mathcal{T}}_{1}-1}^{q-1}\leq x_{0}(1+\zeta)+\eta C_{2}x_{0}^{q-1}(1+\zeta)^{q-1}.

(E.12)

Combining the results from (
E.11
) and (
E.12
), we obtain that,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{T}}_{1}\leq\frac{\zeta}{\eta C_{1}x_{0}^{q-2}}+\frac{(1+\zeta)^{q-1}C_{2}}{C_{1}}.

Similarly for
g>1
,

\displaystyle x_{{\mathcal{T}}_{g}}\geq x_{{\mathcal{T}}_{g-1}}+\sum_{t={\mathcal{T}}_{g-1}}^{{\mathcal{T}}_{g}-1}\eta C_{1}x_{t}^{q-1}\geq x_{{\mathcal{T}}_{g-1}}+\eta C_{1}({\mathcal{T}}_{g}-{\mathcal{T}}_{g-1})x_{0}^{q-1}(1+\zeta)^{(g-1)(q-1)},

(E.13)

and we could bound the difference
x_{{\mathcal{T}}_{g}}-x_{{\mathcal{T}}_{g-1}}
by the following formula,

\displaystyle x_{{\mathcal{T}}_{g}}-x_{{\mathcal{T}}_{g-1}}\leq x_{{\mathcal{T}}_{g}-1}+\eta C_{2}x_{{\mathcal{T}}_{g}}^{q-1}-x_{{\mathcal{T}}_{g-1}}\leq\zeta(1+\zeta)^{g-1}x_{0}+\eta C_{2}x_{0}^{q-1}(1+\zeta)^{g(q-1)}.

(E.14)

Combining the results from (
E.13
) and (
E.14
), we obtain that,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{T}}_{g}\leq{\mathcal{T}}_{g-1}+\frac{\zeta}{\eta C_{1}x_{0}^{q-2}(1+\zeta)^{(g-1)(q-2)}}+\frac{(1+\zeta)^{q-1}C_{2}}{C_{1}}.

(E.15)

Taking a telescoping sum of the results of (
E.15
) from
g=1
to
g=g^{*}
and by the fact that
T_{v}\leq{\mathcal{T}}_{g^{*}}
, we finally get (
E.9
). Now consider another side, similarly for
t={\mathcal{T}}_{1}
, we have

\displaystyle x_{{\mathcal{T}}_{1}}\leq x_{0}+\sum_{t=0}^{{\mathcal{T}}_{1}-1}\eta C_{2}x_{t}^{q-1}\leq x_{0}+{\mathcal{T}}_{1}\eta C_{2}x_{0}^{q-1}(1+\zeta)^{q-1}.

Substitute that
x_{{\mathcal{T}}_{1}}-x_{0}\geq\zeta x_{0}
, we get

\displaystyle{\mathcal{T}}_{1}\geq\frac{\zeta}{\eta C_{2}x_{0}^{q-2}(1+\zeta)^{q-1}}.

(E.16)

For
g>1
, similarly we could derive,

\displaystyle x_{{\mathcal{T}}_{g}}\leq x_{{\mathcal{T}}_{g-1}}+\sum_{t={\mathcal{T}}_{g-1}}^{{\mathcal{T}}_{g}-1}\eta C_{2}x_{t}^{q-1}\leq x_{{\mathcal{T}}_{g-1}}+\eta C_{2}({\mathcal{T}}_{g}-{\mathcal{T}}_{g-1})x_{0}^{q-1}(1+\zeta)^{g(q-1)}

(E.17)

and we could also lower bound the difference
x_{{\mathcal{T}}_{g}}-x_{{\mathcal{T}}_{g-1}}
by

\displaystyle x_{{\mathcal{T}}_{g}}-x_{{\mathcal{T}}_{g-1}}\geq x_{{\mathcal{T}}_{g}}-x_{{\mathcal{T}}_{g-1}-1}-\eta C_{2}x_{{\mathcal{T}}_{g-1}-1}^{q-1}\geq\zeta(1+\zeta)^{g-1}x_{0}-\eta C_{2}x_{0}^{q-1}(1+\zeta)^{(g-1)(q-1)}.

(E.18)

Combining the results from (
E.17
) and (
E.18
), we obtain that,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{T}}_{g}\geq{\mathcal{T}}_{g-1}+\frac{\zeta}{\eta C_{2}x_{0}^{q-2}(1+\zeta)^{g(q-2)+1}}-\frac{1}{(1+\zeta)^{q-1}}.

(E.19)

Taking a telescoping sum of the results of (
E.19
) from
g=1
to
g=g^{*}-1
and by the fact that
T_{v}\geq{\mathcal{T}}_{g^{*}-1}

E.10

∎

E.3
Sequence increase bounds

The following lemma characterizes the increase of positive sequence with exponential factor. Similar results are also applied in
Cao et al. (2023)
;
Meng et al. (2024b)
.

Lemma E.10
.

Suppose that a positive sequence
x_{t}
,
t\geq 0
follows the iterative formula

\displaystyle x_{t+1}=x_{t}+c_{1}e^{-c_{2}x_{t}}

for some
c_{1},c_{2}>0
.
Then it holds that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{c_{2}}\log(c_{1}c_{2}t+e^{c_{2}x_{0}})\leq x_{t}\leq c_{1}e^{-c_{2}x_{0}}+\frac{1}{c_{2}}\log(c_{1}c_{2}t+e^{c_{2}x_{0}})

for all
t\geq 0
.

Proof of Lemma
E.10
.

We first show the lower bound of
x_{t}
. Consider a continuous-time sequence
\underline{x}_{t}
,
t\geq 0
defined by the integral equation with the same initialization.

\displaystyle\underline{x}_{t}=\underline{x}_{0}+c_{1}\cdot\int_{0}^{t}e^{-c_{2}\underline{x}_{\tau}}\mathrm{d}\tau,\quad\underline{x}_{0}=x_{0}.

(E.20)

Note that
\underline{x}_{t}
is obviously an increasing function of
t
. Therefore we have

\displaystyle\underline{x}_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\underline{x}_{t}+c_{1}\cdot\int_{t}^{t+1}e^{-c_{2}\underline{x}_{\tau}}\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle\leq\underline{x}_{t}+c_{1}\cdot\int_{t}^{t+1}e^{-c_{2}\underline{x}_{t}}\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle=\underline{x}_{t}+c_{1}\exp(-c_{2}\underline{x}_{t})

for all
t\in\mathbb{N}
.
Comparing the above inequality with the iterative formula of
\{x_{t}\}
, we conclude by the comparison theorem that
x_{t}\geq\underline{x}_{t}
for all
t\in\mathbb{N}
. Note that (
E.20
) has an exact solution

\displaystyle\underline{x}_{t}=\frac{1}{c_{2}}\log(c_{1}c_{2}t+e^{c_{2}x_{0}})

Therefore we have

\displaystyle x_{t}\geq\frac{1}{c_{2}}\log(c_{1}c_{2}t+e^{c_{2}x_{0}})

for all
t\in\mathbb{N}
, which completes the first part of the proof. Now for the upper bound of
x_{t}
, we have

\displaystyle x_{t}

\displaystyle=x_{0}+c_{1}\cdot\sum_{\tau=0}^{t-1}e^{-c_{2}x_{\tau}}

\displaystyle\leq x_{0}+c_{1}\cdot\sum_{\tau=0}^{t}e^{-\log(c_{1}c_{2}\tau+e^{c_{2}x_{0}})}

\displaystyle=x_{0}+c_{1}\cdot\sum_{\tau=0}^{t}\frac{1}{c_{1}c_{2}\tau+e^{c_{2}x_{0}}}

\displaystyle=x_{0}+\frac{c_{1}}{e^{c_{2}x_{0}}}+c_{1}\cdot\sum_{\tau=1}^{t}\frac{1}{c_{1}c_{2}\tau+e^{c_{2}x_{0}}}

\displaystyle\leq x_{0}+\frac{c_{1}}{e^{c_{2}x_{0}}}+c_{1}\cdot\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{c_{1}c_{2}\tau+e^{c_{2}x_{0}}}\mathrm{d}\tau,

where the second inequality follows by the lower bound of
x_{t}
as the first part of the result of this lemma. Therefore we have

\displaystyle x_{t}

\displaystyle\leq x_{0}+\frac{c_{1}}{e^{c_{2}x_{0}}}+\frac{1}{c_{2}}\log(c_{1}c_{2}t+e^{c_{2}x_{0}})-\frac{1}{c_{2}}\log(e^{c_{2}x_{0}})

\displaystyle=c_{1}e^{-c_{2}x_{0}}+\frac{1}{c_{2}}\log(c_{1}c_{2}t+e^{c_{2}x_{0}})

This finishes the proof.
∎

E.4
Singular value lemmas

Lemma E.11

(Corollary 5.35 in
Vershynin (2010)
)
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be an
d_{1}\times d_{2}
matrix
whose entries are independent standard normal random variables. Then for every
\delta\geq 0
,
with probability at least
1-2\exp(-\delta^{2}/2)
, one has

\displaystyle\sqrt{d_{1}}-\sqrt{d_{2}}-\delta\leq\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{A})\leq\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{A})\leq\sqrt{d_{1}}+\sqrt{d_{2}}+\delta,

where
\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{A})
indicates the smallest singular value of
\mathbf{A}
and
\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{A})
indicates the largest singular value of
\mathbf{A}
.

Lemma E.12
.

Let
\mathbf{A}=[\mathbf{a}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{a}_{P_{1}}]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times P_{1}}
and
\mathbf{B}=[\bm{b}_{1},\cdots,\bm{b}_{P_{2}}]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times P_{2}}
with
d\gg P_{1},P_{2}
. Additionally, the columns in
\mathbf{A}
are perpendicular to columns in
\mathbf{B}
, i.e.
\langle\mathbf{a}_{p_{1}},\bm{b}_{p_{2}}\rangle=0
holds for all
p_{1}\in[P_{1}]
and
p_{2}\in[P_{2}]
. Consider concatenating
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}
into one matrix
\mathbf{C}
, i.e.
\mathbf{C}=[\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}]
, then it holds that

\displaystyle\{\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{C}),\lambda_{2}(\mathbf{C}),\cdots,\lambda_{P_{1}+P_{2}}(\mathbf{C})\}=\{\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A}),\lambda_{2}(\mathbf{A}),\cdots,\lambda_{P_{1}}(\mathbf{A})\}\cup\{\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{B}),\lambda_{2}(\mathbf{B}),\cdots,\lambda_{P_{2}}(\mathbf{B})\},

where
\lambda_{i}(\cdot)
denote the
i
-th singular value of a matrix in descending order.

Proof of Lemma
E.12
.

By the connection between the definition of singular value and eigenvalue, we can make spectral decomposition for both
\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}^{\top}\mathbf{B}
as,

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}=\sum_{i=1}^{P_{1}}\lambda_{i}^{2}(\mathbf{A})\mathbf{v}_{A,i}\mathbf{v}_{A,i}^{\top};\quad\mathbf{B}^{\top}\mathbf{B}=\sum_{i=1}^{P_{2}}\lambda_{i}^{2}(\mathbf{B})\mathbf{v}_{B,i}\mathbf{v}_{B,i}^{\top},

where
\mathbf{v}_{A,i}
is the
i
-th eigenvector corresponding
\lambda_{i}^{2}(\mathbf{A})
, and
\mathbf{v}_{B,i}
is the
i
-th eigenvector corresponding
\lambda_{i}^{2}(\mathbf{B})
. By the orthogonality between
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
, we can derive that

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}^{\top}\mathbf{C}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}&\mathbf{0}_{P_{1}\times P_{2}}\\
\mathbf{0}_{P_{2}\times P_{1}}&\mathbf{B}^{\top}\mathbf{B}\end{bmatrix}.

We further generate
\widetilde{\mathbf{v}_{A,i}}\in\mathbb{R}^{P_{1}+P_{2}}
for all
i\in[P_{1}]
by concatenating zero vector to the end of
\mathbf{v}_{A,i}
, i.e.

\widetilde{\mathbf{v}_{A,i}}=[\mathbf{v}_{A,i}^{\top},\mathbf{0}_{P_{2}}^{\top}]^{\top}
. Similarly, we also generate
\widetilde{\mathbf{v}_{B,i}}\in\mathbb{R}^{P_{1}+P_{2}}
for all
i\in[P_{2}]
by concatenating zero vector at the start of
\mathbf{v}_{B,i}
, i.e.

\widetilde{\mathbf{v}_{B,i}}=[\mathbf{0}_{P_{1}}^{\top},\mathbf{v}_{B,i}^{\top}]^{\top}
. Then we can rewrite
\mathbf{C}^{\top}\mathbf{C}
as

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}^{\top}\mathbf{C}=\sum_{i=1}^{P_{1}}\lambda_{i}^{2}(\mathbf{A})\widetilde{\mathbf{v}_{A,i}}\widetilde{\mathbf{v}_{A,i}}^{\top}+\sum_{i=1}^{P_{2}}\lambda_{i}^{2}(\mathbf{B})\widetilde{\mathbf{v}_{B,i}}\widetilde{\mathbf{v}_{B,i}}^{\top},

which is the spectral decomposition of
\mathbf{C}^{\top}\mathbf{C}
by the fact that all
\widetilde{\mathbf{v}_{A,i}}
’s and
\widetilde{\mathbf{v}_{B,i}}
’s are normalized and orthogonal to each other. Since the singular value of
\mathbf{C}
is the square root of the eigenvalue of
\mathbf{C}^{\top}\mathbf{C}
, we complete the proof.
∎

Lemma E.13

(Min-max principle of singular value)
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a matrix and
\lambda_{k}(\mathbf{A})
be its
k
-th singular values in descending order. Then it holds that

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}(\mathbf{A})=\max_{S:\mathrm{dim}(S)=k}\min_{\mathbf{x}\in S,\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}=1}\big\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\big\|_{2};

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}(\mathbf{A})=\min_{S:\mathrm{dim}(S)=d-k+1}\max_{\mathbf{x}\in S,\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}=1}\big\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\big\|_{2}.
