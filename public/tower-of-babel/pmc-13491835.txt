Implementing a primary-tertiary shared care model for enhancing cascade testing for familial hypercholesterolaemia: an effectiveness–implementation hybrid type III pre–post study
Authors: Mitchell Sarkies, Madeline Calder, David Sullivan, Shubha Srinivasan, Catherine Spinks, Emile Belramoul, Ronald J. Trent, Claire Trumble, Charlotte Hespe, Stephen C. H. Li, Luke Elias, Brendan Goodger, Karen Birkenhead
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFamilial hypercholesterolaemia (FH) is a common but underdiagnosed genetic condition that leads to high low-density lipoprotein cholesterol and premature cardiovascular disease. Cascade genetic testing remains underutilised as a screening approach. Implementation strategies can increase the diffusion of innovations such as genetic medicine into non-genetic specialty settings more rapidly through dissemination networks. This study aimed to evaluate the success of a multifaceted implementation strategy to increase cascade testing for relatives of a person diagnosed with FH using a primary-tertiary shared care model.MethodsA multisite effectiveness–implementation hybrid type III pre–post study was conducted between 2022 and 2024 to compare the implementation of a new shared care model for FH cascade testing in NSW, Australia. During the control period, cascade testing of relatives was offered to FH index cases by a genetic counsellor at the lipid clinic. In the implementation strategy period, FH index cases were offered a cascade testing expression of interest form to provide to their relatives. Relatives were contacted by a genetic counsellor and provided with a pre- and post-cascade testing package, which was also provided to their general practitioner.ResultsA total of n = 25 index and n = 6 cascade cases were included in the control period and n = 81 index and n = 56 cascade cases in the implementation period. The number of cascade genetic tests per index case increased significantly from 0.24 in the control period to 0.52 in the implementation strategy period (incident rate ratio = 4.62; 95% CI: 0.39, 8.84; p = 0.032). Among relatives tested, there was no difference in the proportion with a confirmed FH gene change per index case (IRR = 2.15; 95% CI: 0.65, 7.01; p = 0.207). There was no difference in the proportion of index cases with at least one cascade test (OR = 1.49; 95% CI: 0.53, 4.19; p = 0.442).ConclusionsImplementation of our model increased FH cascade testing compared to previous standard care. These findings suggest a greater role for cascade testing in primary care settings. This opens new opportunities for integrating genetic screening into routine general practice, particularly for autosomal dominant conditions such as FH.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s43058-026-00988-x.

•This study demonstrates how a primary–tertiary shared care model can increase uptake of cascade genetic testing for familial hypercholesterolaemia in real-world settings.

•It shows that general practitioners can successfully deliver genetic testing when supported by central coordination and tailored implementation strategies.

•The implementation strategy was informed by the Diffusion of Innovations theory and targeted the following mechanisms: information transmission, social learning, and externality effects.

•Findings contribute to the literature on mainstreaming genetic medicine by illustrating how non-genetics specialists can be engaged in care pathways for autosomal dominant conditions.

Background

Rapid advances in genetic medicine have expanded opportunities for precision healthcare, therapies and diagnostics. However, health systems are struggling to adopt new genetic innovations, even where there is proven benefit [1–5]. In high-income countries, demand for genetics expertise far exceeds available supply, prompting calls to upskill non-genetics health professionals and integrate genetic medicine into routine care across all specialties [6]. This challenge will only intensify with the prospect of population-scale genetic screening. Genetic conditions with autosomal dominant inheritance are at the forefront of the need to mainstream genetic medicine. The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention has identified that conditions, such as familial hypercholesterolaemia (FH), Lynch Syndrome and hereditary breast and ovarian cancer (HBOC), have significant potential to impact public health as they are highly prevalent and carry a 50% likelihood of being passed on to children [7]. Implementing improved screening and management for these conditions can serve as a model that could be extrapolated to other similar genetic conditions more broadly.

FH is an exemplar genetic condition for testing novel implementation strategies to enhance the mainstreaming of clinical genetics. FH is one of the most common inherited conditions and leads to extremely high concentrations of low-density lipoprotein cholesterol from birth [8]. It substantially increases the risk of cardiovascular disease, often leading to premature myocardial infarction before age 50 [9, 10]. Early identification, genetic testing, and cholesterol-lowering therapy is safe from childhood, effective and cost-effective, reducing the risk of cardiovascular disease and death [9]. Despite its public health impact, fewer than 10% of people with FH globally have been identified and treated [11, 12]. Family cascade genetic testing is the most cost-effective approach to detect new FH cases [13]. This systematic process of genetic testing family members of a person diagnosed with FH leads to very high-test yields (one in two tests positive for first-degree blood relatives) but remains substantially underutilised [14].

Diffusion of innovations theory asserts that the adoption of genetic medicine advances will follow a predictable S-shaped curve pattern, with innovators and early adopters eventually giving way to more widespread use as the majority follow [15]. Targeted implementation strategies can encourage health professionals or organisations to adopt new innovations earlier [16]. For example, strengthening links with innovators or early adopters within a diffusion network is thought to increase the likelihood others will adopt the new idea sooner [17]. These network effects are driven by underlying psychological mechanisms, such as through information transmission, social learning, and externality effects [18]. Information transmission can be augmented by opinion leaders who act as agents for change through their social influence and technical competence [19]. Social learning from others’ experience also allows individuals to make better-informed adoption decisions by observing the outcomes for those that uptake the innovation earlier [20]. The externality effect of one person’s decision to undertake FH genetic testing creates potential benefits for others, beyond the individual diagnosed [18, 20]. For example, when the first person in a family is diagnosed with FH (known as the index case) this enables cascade testing of blood relatives to determine whether they also carry the genetic condition, allowing for early identification and treatment. Packaging implementation strategies to target the behavioural factors theorised to influence the integration of genetic medicine for conditions such as FH may support efforts to increase uptake. By framing genetic testing as a routine component of conventional care, these strategies could help normalise its use and facilitate more widespread adoption.

The aim of this study was to evaluate the success of a multifaceted implementation strategy to increase cascade genetic testing for blood relatives of a person diagnosed with FH using a primary-tertiary shared care model.

Methods

The Sydney Local Health District Ethics Review Committee (RPAH Zone) approved the conduct of this research (Protocol ID X22-0333), and our study protocol has been published [21]. The manuscript follows the Standards for Reporting Implementation Studies (StaRI) guidelines [22].

Implementation context

In 2020, Australia introduced funding reimbursement for FH genetic testing through the Medicare Benefits Schedule (MBS). One MBS Item supports diagnostic testing to identify germline variants responsible for FH in the first affected family member (index case), which can only be ordered by a “specialist or consultant physician”. The other is for predictive testing of a patient who has a first- or second-degree relative with a documented pathogenic germline gene variant for FH (cascade testing), which can be ordered by GPs in primary care settings. The release of these MBS Items created an opportunity to coordinate cascade testing by non-genetics specialists across primary and tertiary care settings. Using FH index cases diagnosed within a tertiary hospital lipid clinic as a starting point, the coordination of cascade genetic testing for relatives represents a potentially feasible approach to increase the uptake of this technology, enabling non-genetics specialists to identify and manage patients with FH in primary care.

Study design

An effectiveness–implementation hybrid type III pre–post study was conducted between 28 August 2022 and 27 August 2024. We compared the success of a new primary-tertiary shared care model for FH cascade genetic testing over a 12-month period (2023–24) against a historical usual care control condition (2022–23). A quasi-experimental design was selected due to three key considerations: i) the absence of comparable tertiary hospital lipid clinics undertaking genetic testing for FH in NSW Health to serve as control sites; ii) the consistently low baseline usage of FH cascade genetic testing MBS Items across 2020–2022 in New South Wales (NSW) (mean = 12 per year), comparable to other Australian jurisdictions; and iii) concerns that seeking individual consent for a parallel randomised controlled trial would introduce further barriers to cascade testing and compromise both internal and external validity of the study by introducing selection bias, limiting the generalisability of our sample and findings to real-world healthcare environments. A Hybrid Type III approach was used because the effectiveness and cost-effectives of FH cascade genetic testing is well established [9, 12]. Therefore, we do not seek to replicate evidence in support of cascade testing; instead, the primary focus of this study is to evaluate the implementation of a shared care model to facilitated increased cascade testing in different healthcare settings under real-world conditions.

This study reports on the Implementation Phase of a broader research program to develop, implement and evaluate a strategy to improve the detection of FH (Fig. 1). The research program was structured using the Exploration, Preparation, Implementation, Sustainment (EPIS) Framework, which is a four-phase implementation process model that considers factors that determine implementation success across different ecological levels of the system (e.g. organisational and individual) [23]. In Phase 1 (Exploration), a national implementation strategy for enhancing the detection of FH was developed in consultation with over 100 key interest-holders across all Australian states and territories [8, 24]. Phase 2 (Preparation) focussed on local tailoring of a primary-tertiary shared care model for facilitating FH cascade testing to the NSW setting [25]. Phase 3 (Implementation) is described in this paper which reports on the implementation and evaluation of the shared care model. Phase 4 (Sustainment) will be the focus of future research to maintain and scale-up the shared care model.Fig. 1Four phase research program according to the EPIS framework

During the conduct of this study, there were two deviations from the methods described in our study protocol. The following implementation process outcomes were not reported as sufficient data was unable to be collected: i) proportion of index cases that provide the family letter to their relatives; and ii) proportion of relatives who followed up with their GP to receive test results.

Participants and setting

This multisite study was based in the Vascular Health Clinic at Royal Prince Alfred Hospital (RPAH), NSW, Australia. The clinic receives around 120 referrals for suspected FH cases annually and is based in metropolitan Sydney. The local health district employs around 16,000 staff and provides health services for over 700,000 people. There are 577 general practices within the Central and Eastern Sydney region providing primary care services to 1.5 million individuals. The RPAH Vascular Health Clinic collaborated with a paediatric endocrinology clinic at the Sydney Children’s Hospital Network – Westmead to facilitate cascade testing for children and adolescent FH probands. In the primary care setting, a total of n = 23 general practices participated in the model of care.

The study involved a collaboration between the included healthcare professionals affiliated with the RPAH Vascular Health Clinic and the paediatric endocrinology clinic at the Sydney Children’s Hospital Network – Westmead, encompassing both internal and external providers engaged in the delivery of care. Internal participants included specialist physicians such as cardiologists, endocrinologist, and pathologists, as well as clinic nurses and genetic counsellors directly involved in patient assessment, diagnosis, and management. External participants comprised referring general practitioners, private cardiologists and paediatricians who facilitated patient entry into the clinic and contributed to ongoing community-based care.

A patient was defined as an FH index case if they were either i) a proband newly diagnosed with FH during the relevant period, or ii) a cascade case who tested positive and subsequently generated a new relative who underwent cascade testing in that period. Index cases who had originally been diagnosed outside of the study periods were assigned to the period in which their relative was cascade tested. Patients who had initially been classified in the 2022–23 period were reassigned to the 2023–24 period if they re-attended the clinic during the 2023–24 period, as they were then managed under the new model of care.

Procedures

Genetic testing was performed at RPAH. All exons of LDLR and PCSK9 were sequenced. For APOB, testing primarily focused on exon 26. Furthermore, interpretation of variants outside exon 26 is challenging, particularly in determining gain-of-function or loss-of-function effects. Copy number variants in LDLR were detected using next-generation sequencing with the Twist platform. Multiplex Ligation-dependent Probe Amplification (MLPA) was not used for LDLR CNV detection, and no MLPA assays were available for APOB or PCSK9. Lipoprotein(a) blood tests were not included as they were not covered by Medicare in Australia at the time of the study.

Control period: family cascade genetic screening processes and procedures

FH index cases were reviewed at the RPAH Vascular Health Clinic between 27 August 2007 and 30 July 2024. The FH index cases were phenotypically diagnosed using the Dutch Lipid Clinic Network Criteria and confirmation of diagnosis using genetic testing was undertaken for those providing written informed consent. Cascade genetic testing, education and written information was provided to FH index cases by a genetic counsellor to share with relatives. Relatives were then required to obtain their own referral to the lipid clinic and placed on an average eight month wait list for an appointment, where cascade testing was undertaken at the clinic where written informed consent was provided. Education also included the option for relatives who are not located locally to seek cascade testing at a general genetics clinic in their local health district.

Implementation strategy period: family cascade genetic screening processes and procedures

Index cases with a genetically confirmed diagnosis of FH who visited the RPAH Vascular Health Clinic between 28th August 2023 and 27th August 2024 were offered an expression of interest (EOI) form either electronically or hard copy to distribute to their relatives to learn more about how they could undergo cascade testing via their GP. Relatives were also given the option to attend the lipid clinic if this was their preference. FH index cases received a text message reminder two weeks after initial distribution if no EOI had been submitted by a relative. Relatives who completed the EOI were contacted within an average of one day from expressing interest. Clinic staff contacted relatives who had completed the EOI form to discuss cascade genetic testing with their GP and were provided (electronically) a cascade testing package to take to their GP appointment (Appendix 1). The package was also sent (email or post) to their nominated GP. A post-test results package was sent via email to the relative’s GP the same or next day after receiving the genetic test results to support GPs in returning the genetic test result. A detailed record was maintained to track implementation metrics, including the number of index cases provided with the EOI, number of EOIs accepted by the index cases, number of relatives who completed the EOI, follow up text messages sent, and cascade testing packages sent. Implementation strategy described according to the template for intervention Description and replication (TIDieR) guidelines

The shared care model involves the tertiary public hospital lipid clinic centrally coordinating FH cascade genetic testing by relatives’ GPs. The model and supporting implementation strategies were co-designed with health professionals, health consumers, policy makers and other interest-holders. The hospital lipid clinic will act as an “opinion leader” within a network of FH index cases, relatives and their GPs to support the adoption of genetic testing by non-genetics specialists.

Opinion leaders have the potential to enhance information transmission within a diffusion network because of their informal influence on the attitudes or behaviours of others based on their credibility, technical competence, social accessibility, and alignment with group norms [26–28]. Peer leaders with professional status could play a role in establishing new norms for FH genetic testing, but the best way to optimise their effectiveness remains unclear [28, 29]. Another source of informal influence on clinical practice is patient expectations and demands for services. Previous studies have reported that GPs consider patient-related factors as a primary driver of care decisions, such as the desire to maintain a good relationship with their patients [25, 30]. This demand-side driver of care provision could be leveraged to increase the uptake of new innovations like genetic testing for FH.

Genetically confirmed FH index cases were offered a co-designed cascade genetic testing EOI letter to give to their relatives. The EOI included contact information for the lipid clinic and a link to an online form for further information about cascade testing. Relatives were also given the option to attend the lipid clinic if this was their preference.

In addition, a cascade testing package was provided to relatives that completed the EOI form and their nominated GP. The package included i) a one-page cascade testing guide for the GP, ii) a prefilled pathology request form, iii) a genetic testing consent form, iv) a patient FH factsheet, v) links to GP resources and lipid clinic contact details for additional support.

The model also includes a post genetic test results package, which is sent to the relative’s GP after results are returned. For a positive result, the package includes i) results letter for the GP, ii) results letter for the patient (relative), iii) cascade testing EOI family letter, and iv) National FH Registry consent information.

The EOI, cascade testing package, and positive post genetic test results package can be found in the online Supplemental Material for our published protocol [21].

FH index cases with suspected or phenotypically diagnosed FH who underwent diagnostic genetic testing were offered the co-designed cascade genetic testing EOI letter to give to their relatives during the pre-test counselling appointment on an individual patient basis. Genetically confirmed FH index cases were offered a clinic appointment with a genetic counsellor in person or via telephone for post-test genetic counselling, discussion of risk notification for family members, cascade testing, and reminded to notify their at-risk relative(s) or offered the cascade genetic testing EOI letter, if it was not provided at the pre-test counselling appointment. Reminder SMS text messages were sent to the index cases when an EOI was not received from one of their relatives.

Relatives that completed the EOI form were contacted by the lipid clinic to discuss cascade genetic testing. They were emailed, or mailed, the cascade testing package, which was also sent to the relatives nominated GP. The package included all information needed by the GP to conduct cascade testing of first- and second-degree relatives. Relatives underwent cascade genetic testing either through their GP or via the lipid clinic, based on their preference and convenience. Samples were provided either via saliva or blood sample. Samples were returned either to the hospital pathology department or private pathology providers.

Results from the test were returned from NSW Health Pathology, or other private testing laboratories, to the ordering GP (with the lipid clinic carbon-copied) or directly to the lipid clinic, depending on where testing took place. Relatives were sent an SMS text message reminder if no test results were received by the lipid clinic.

The relative’s GP was then sent the post genetic test results package. GPs were encouraged to contact the lipid clinic throughout the process for assistance via telephone or email, as needed.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was undertaken using Stata 19 software [34] and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Descriptive statistics were used to summarise patient characteristics and implementation outcomes using mean and standard deviation (SD) for continuous, and frequencies and proportions for categorical data. Patient characteristics data were compared between study period using Chi-squared test of independence or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical and t-tests or Wilcoxon rank-sum (Mann–Whitney) tests for continuous data.

Evidence of intervention delivery count data was analysed using a Zero-Inflated Poisson regression to determine the incidence rate ratio for the number of cascade tests per index case and relatives with a confirmed FH gene change. This model was selected after considering that the cascade genetic testing data included an excess of zeros and examining a Poisson model which indicated overdispersion. We then compared a Zero-Inflated Poisson and Negative Binomial regression. The dispersion parameter (alpha) in the Negative Binomial model was not statistically significant, suggesting that the degree of overdispersion did not warrant the added complexity of this model. Logistic regression was used to compare the evidence of intervention delivery binary data for index cases with at least one cascade genetic test. Univariate analyses of index case age and sex were explored as potential confounders which were non-significant, and therefore not added as covariates in the models. Other covariates were not explored due to high levels of missing data.

Discussion

Our multifaceted strategy to implement a primary-tertiary shared care model for FH cascade genetic testing increased the number of relatives tested and diagnosed per index case. The majority of FH cascade testing was driven by a minority of index cases, but once a relative completed the EOI most proceeded to undergo genetic testing. Around half of the relatives completed cascade genetic testing with their GP, suggesting a preference among some patients to undertake testing in primary care. This also demonstrates the capacity and willingness of non-genetics specialists to deliver genetic testing when supported by a coordinated model of care.

There were relatively high levels of implementation fidelity with most FH index cases provided EOIs, text message reminders when EOIs weren’t received from relatives, and cascade testing packages sent to both the relatives and their GPs. On average it took less than a week for relatives to be contacted after completing the EOI or for the cascade testing package to be sent to the relative and their GP. However, it took 2–6 months (over 100 days) for pathology results to be reported and a post-test results package to be sent to the GP, which represent a potential negative factor for implementation success. Despite resource allocation challenges within the health system that likely impacted implementation fidelity, our focus on co-design and end user engagement meant regular communication was undertaken, whereby the lipid clinic genetic counsellor followed up with the pathology lab and patients throughout the study period.

Our implementation strategies targeted three mechanisms for the Diffusion of Innovations: i) information transmission, ii) social learning, and iii) externality effects. Opinion leadership was used to facilitate information transmission through a network of non-genetics specialists by centralising the coordination of cascade testing, providing technical expertise and regular communication from the RPAH Vascular Health Clinic. It has been hypothesised that opinion leaders must be uniquely positioned within an interpersonal communication network, creating a link for patterned flows of information [35]. It is also important that opinion leaders occupy a “bridging” position within a network [36]. Centralising the coordination of cascade testing from the RPAH Vascular Health Clinic meant that the clinic was centrally located within a network and could connect information flows from the FH index case to their relatives and the relatives GPs. Providing technical expertise and regular communication ensured that the clinic served as a “bridging” node within the network by creating a continuous flow of information to the relatives and their GPs at point of care when it was needed. This type of informal information exchange is regarded as a key ingredient in innovation diffusion and may be more effective than formal approaches such as didactic lectures [37].

Social learning was promoted by providing a localised pre- and post-test package to the relatives and their GPs. These packages were co-designed with end-users to better integrate generic knowledge about FH and genetic testing into more localised and tacit knowledge about how to best undertake the process within a primary care setting [38]. Experiential knowledge about how to undertake FH cascade genetic testing remains distributed across a relatively small number of lipid clinics in Australia [14], which can present a barrier to more widespread adoption [39]. Healthcare professionals can play a key role in sharing knowledge through social networks [40]. However, the siloed nature of the Australian healthcare system, whereby the federal government oversees primary care services and state/territory governments manage public hospitals and public health programs, limits the reach of these social networks across primary and tertiary care settings. By positioning relatives of the index case as connecting nodes, our pre- and post-test package was able to be distributed through a new knowledge-sharing network that linked FH specialists with non-FH specialists and facilitated the transfer of information across professional settings.

The externality effect of our strategy was potentially a powerful mechanism driving the adoption of genetic testing by non-genetic specialists. The decision of one individual (FH index case) to pursue genetic testing generated potential benefits for their relatives who were not necessarily active patients of the RPAH Vascular Health Clinic, reinforcing the rationale for the involvement of those relative’s GPs. Patients often have long-term, trusting and deep relationships with their GP [41]. Many of the relatives signalled a clear preference to undergo cascade genetic testing with their GP, creating a patient-demand driver for the adoption of this model of care as part of routine practice. Previous studies have suggested that patients play an active role in general practice consultations [42]. By positioning the patient (relative) as the initiator of the testing process with their GP, our strategy harnessed this demand-side driver of care, which may have encouraged uptake of cascade genetic testing among these non-genetics’ specialists.

Our shared-care model generated 0.52 new FH diagnoses per index case, which is comparable with previous research on FH cascade screening programs. Lee et al. conducted a systematic review of 10 studies and reported a median of 0.92 new FH diagnoses per index case (range: 0.22 to 8.00), using various strategies such as direct and indirect contact with relatives [43]. While our findings are within the lower range of previous studies, we are one of the first to demonstrate that cascade genetic testing can be successfully conducted by GPs in primary care settings with centralised coordination and support. IMPACT-FH adopted a pragmatic approach that allows FH index cases to choose how they communicate their diagnosis to relatives within a population genomic testing program in the US [44]. This program achieved a comparatively higher proportion of index cases initiating either cholesterol or genetic cascade testing compared with our strategy; however, this translated to a comparatively lower number of 0.19 new genetically confirmed FH cases per index case [44]. Providing FH index cases with a family letter remains a common strategy in routine practice to facilitate cascade testing. However, previous research has shown that passive approaches consistently result in lower uptake of testing among relatives [45]. In contrast, our findings and those from IMPACT-FH suggest that more active, theoretically informed and co-designed strategies can improve cascade genetic testing uptake. These approaches may enhance equity of access to life-saving treatment for those who remain unaware that they have FH.

The application of an implementation science process model (EPIS Framework) enabled the identification of contextual factors and tailoring of an implementation strategy package across the different phases of implementation, which aligned with both interest-holder needs and system-level constraints [23]. Furthermore, by grounding our strategy in the Diffusion of Innovations theory, our study not only demonstrated the potential benefit of a primary-tertiary shared care model for cascade genetic testing but also enabled the examination of underlying mechanisms of practice change. While this pragmatic implementation study is highly generalisable to real-world healthcare environments, there are some limitations to using a pre-post design particularly to its internal validity. There was a much smaller number of FH index cases in 2022–23 compared with the 2023–24 period. The 2022–23 period was characterised by turnover of genetic counselling staff and periods of temporary and part-time staffing appointments, potentially limiting the number of follow up clinic appointments during this period. Lower rates of positive index case diagnostics during this period may reflect less rigorous assessment practices, which relied primarily on referral information due to ongoing clinical restrictions following the peak of the COVID-19 pandemic that limited in-person evaluations. Furthermore, there is uncertainty whether any FH cascade testing was being conducted in general practice prior and unrelated to the study. Outside of the 6 FH cascade tests facilitated by the clinic during the control period, there were another 29 FH cascade tests reimbursed using the MBS Item number 73353 in NSW during the control period (according to Medicare Item Reports). The length of time from genetic testing to return of results was impacted by lab staffing constraints. Our relatively small overall sample size limited our ability to consider multiple potential confounding variables in our statistical model, which is important when conducting non-randomised studies where imbalances in patient characteristics between the groups could influence findings. Another limitation is a lack of follow up data on care after diagnoses such as whether patients were initiated on lipid lowering medications and if they achieved LDL-cholesterol reduction targets.

Conclusions

The primary-tertiary shared care model significantly increased the uptake of FH cascade genetic testing compared to previous lipid clinic-based standard care. However, the proportion of FH index cases generating cascade genetic tests did not differ between the models. Cascade genetic testing was successfully undertaken in primary care settings with the relatives’ GP, supported by central coordination from the public hospital lipid clinic. These findings suggest a greater role for cascade genetic testing in primary care settings and new opportunities for integrating genetic screening into routine general practice. Future research should examine strategies to increase cascade genetic testing amongst a larger proportion of index cases within scaled up coordinating centres for cascade testing of autosomal dominant genetic conditions.
