Qualitative EEG morphology predicts early Antiseizure medication response in newly diagnosed epilepsy: A prospective cohort study
Authors: Segvan M.S. Kucher, Mohammed Aziz Sulaiman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEpilepsy is a complex neurological disorder with diverse electroclinical characteristics. While diagnosis is guided by the ILAE 2025 classification, the prognostic value of specific interictal epileptiform discharge (IED) morphologies for initial antiseizure medication (ASM) selection remains incompletely characterized.ObjectiveTo determine whether qualitative EEG characteristics at diagnosis predict early ASM responsiveness across six major epilepsy syndromes.MethodsIn this prospective cohort study, 150 newly diagnosed, drug-naïve patients (25 per syndrome) including Focal Epilepsy, FBTCS, BECTS, CAE, GGE, and JME underwent standardized baseline EEG with activation procedures. ASMs were prescribed according to routine clinical practice. Follow-up clinical evaluation and EEG were performed at 3 months. The primary outcome was EEG normalization; secondary outcomes included ≥50% seizure reduction and seizure freedom.ResultsEEG normalization rates varied significantly across syndromes, ranging from 76% in CAE to 20% in FBTCS. Qualitative IED morphology was associated with normalization (p < 0.01). Patients achieving EEG normalization at 3 months were significantly more likely to be seizure free (p < 0.001).ConclusionIn well-defined electroclinical syndromes, qualitative IED morphology appears to provide additional prognostic information regarding early ASM response. These findings suggest that EEG morphology may complement syndrome-based assessment and that variability in spike and sharp-wave morphology may reflect underlying electrophysiological heterogeneity extending beyond fixed syndrome labels, supporting further investigation using standardized morphology analysis in larger, controlled studies.

Introduction

Epilepsy is a prevalent chronic neurological disorder affecting over 50 million people worldwide, with significant impacts on quality of life, healthcare systems, and society [1]. Modern understanding frames epilepsy not as a single disease, but as a spectrum of disorders arising from diverse etiologies, including structural, genetic, metabolic, and immune-mediated causes [2]. The electroencephalogram (EEG) remains a cornerstone of diagnosis, essential for classifying seizure types, detecting interictal epileptiform discharges (IEDs), and identifying specific electroclinical syndromes [3], [4].

The recently updated International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE) classification 2025 emphasizes an integrated, multi-level diagnostic approach, combining seizure type, epilepsy type, syndrome, and etiology [5]. A key component of this framework is the recognition of characteristic EEG signatures associated with specific syndromes, such as 3-Hz generalized spike-wave in childhood absence epilepsy (CAE) [6], [7] or centrotemporal spikes in benign epilepsy with centrotemporal spikes (BECTS). These signatures reflect the underlying network pathophysiology of each condition [8].

In clinical practice, the initial choice of antiseizure medication (ASM) is guided by seizure and syndrome classification, patient comorbidities, and physician experience [9]. While pragmatic, this approach often does not incorporate finer details of the EEG, such as the qualitative morphology of IEDs including their shape, stability, and spatial distribution [10]. Consequently, many patients undergo sequential trials of ASMs before achieving seizure freedom, a process that prolongs the risk of seizures and associated morbidity [11], [12].

The physiological basis of IEDs suggests that their morphology may carry prognostic information. Distinct waveforms such as spikes, polyspikes, or spike-wave complexes arise from different imbalances in neuronal excitability and synchronization, involving mechanisms like T-type calcium channel bursts or impaired GABAergic inhibition [13]. These mechanisms align differentially with the modes of action of various ASMs [14], [15]. Furthermore, the persistence of IEDs after treatment initiation has been inconsistently linked to worse clinical outcomes [16], though high quality prospective data are limited [17], [18].

A critical, understudied question is whether the qualitative morphology of IEDs at diagnosis can predict early ASM response, particularly in drug-naïve patients with well-defined syndromes [19], [20]. This prospective cohort study was designed to address this gap. We aimed to evaluate the association between qualitative EEG morphology and early treatment outcomes across six common epilepsy syndromes, with the goal of generating hypotheses for future, more definitive studies on EEG-based prognosis.

Methods

Study design and setting

This was a prospective, observational cohort study conducted at the Epilepsy and Neurophysiology Unit of a tertiary referral center. A prospective design was chosen to ensure standardized EEG acquisition, uniform baseline assessment, and reliable temporal sequencing between ASM initiation and outcomes [21]. The study followed STROBE guidelines and was approved by the Duhok Directorate of Health's Research Ethics Committee (Approval Number: 29052024–4-14). All participants or their guardians provided written informed consent.

Participants

Consecutive, newly diagnosed, drug-naïve patients aged 6–60 years were screened for enrollment.

Inclusion Criteria:1.Diagnosis of epilepsy per ILAE 2025 classification [5].2.Classification into one of six predefined electroclinical syndromes: Focal Epilepsy (FE), Focal to Bilateral Tonic Clonic Seizures (FBTCS), Benign Epilepsy with Centrotemporal Spikes (BECTS), Childhood Absence Epilepsy (CAE), Genetic Generalized Epilepsy (GGE), and Juvenile Myoclonic Epilepsy (JME).3.Baseline EEG showing interictal epileptiform discharges (IEDs) consistent with the clinical syndrome.4.Ability to complete follow-up.

A target sample of 150 patients (25 per syndrome) was enrolled to allow for balanced group comparisons.

EEG acquisition and analysis

Acquisition: All EEGs were performed using a 21-channel digital system with the 10–20 international placement. A standardized protocol included resting wakefulness (eyes open/closed), hyperventilation (3 min), photic stimulation (1–30 Hz), and sleep recording when feasible, in accordance with published recommendation [22].

Qualitative Analysis: Two board-certified neurophysiologists, blinded to clinical outcomes, independently reviewed all EEGs. IEDs were classified by morphology (spike, sharp wave, spike-wave, polyspike, polyspike-wave), distribution, field, and reactivity to activation. For subjects with multiple IED morphologies, the most prevalent pattern (constituting >50% of discharges) was designated as the primary morphology. EEGs without a single predominant pattern were classified as “mixed.” Inter-rater reliability was excellent (Cohen's κ > 0.80).

Treatment and follow-up

ASMs were prescribed according to routine clinical practice and international guidelines [11], [13], [23]to preserve real world applicability. Treatment adherence was monitored via patient interview and the MMAS-8 scale.

All participants underwent a standardized clinical evaluation and repeat EEG at 3 months (± 1week). This interval was selected as a pragmatic early time point to assess initial electrographic response, rather than a surrogate of long-term remission in prior studies [14].

Outcomes

Primary Outcome: Complete EEG normalization at 3 months, defined as the absence of unequivocal IEDs during the standardized recording.

Secondary Outcomes:1.≥50% reduction in seizure frequency from baseline.2.Seizure freedom (no seizures) since the last follow-up.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS v. 30. Descriptive statistics are presented as mean ± SD or n (%). Group comparisons used chi-square tests (categorical) and ANOVA (continuous). Logistic regression was used to assess univariate associations between IED morphology and EEG normalization. Given the inherent clinical coupling between syndrome, EEG morphology, and ASM choice, the primary analysis was syndrome descriptive. A secondary, exploratory logistic regression was performed on the pooled cohort using a simplified morphology classification (e.g., ‘fragmented’ vs. ‘well-formed’) as the sole predictor. A p-value <0.05 was considered significant.

Concordance between EEG and clinical evaluation

Across the cohort, varying degrees of concordance were observed between baseline clinical suspicion and EEG findings. In several cases, EEG findings supported a specific epilepsy syndrome despite low initial clinical suspicion, while in others, high clinical suspicion was corroborated by corresponding EEG features. Cases in which both clinical suspicion and EEG findings were negative were not classified into a predefined syndrome. A smaller subgroup demonstrated high clinical suspicion with non-supportive EEG patterns; these cases were excluded from the study in accordance with the predefined exclusion criteria. Primary outcome: EEG normalization at 3 months

Overall, 78 of 150 patients (52%) achieved complete EEG normalization at the 3 month follow up. EEG normalization rates differed significantly among epilepsy syndromes (p < 0.001). The highest normalization rates were observed in CAE (76%) and BECTS (68%), followed by JME (60%) and GGE (56%), whereas lower rates were observed in Focal Epilepsy (32%) and FBTCS (20%).

Association between EEG normalization and seizure outcomes

At the 3-month follow-up, 112 patients (74.7%) achieved a ≥ 50% reduction in seizure frequency, and 85 patients (56.7%) were seizure-free. EEG normalization was strongly associated with seizure freedom (p < 0.001). This association was observed across all epilepsy syndromes, with consistently higher rates of seizure freedom among patients who demonstrated EEG normalization compared with those with persistent interictal epileptiform discharges.

Exploratory analysis of predictors

In an exploratory logistic regression analysis of the cohort using a simplified IED morphology classification (‘fragmented’ vs. ‘well-formed’), morphology was significantly associated with seizure-free outcome at 3 months (OR: 3.1, 95% CI: 1.4–6.9, p = 0.006). A separate multivariate analysis including morphology, ASM type, and adherence also identified several factors associated with EEG normalization, including generalized polyspike wave morphology (OR 3.2, p = 0.008) and high treatment adherence (OR 2.2, p = 0.04). Age of onset, sex, and family history were not significant predictors.

Discussion

This prospective cohort study explored the association between qualitative interictal EEG morphology and early antiseizure medication response in patients with newly diagnosed, well-defined epilepsy syndromes. The principal findings were: [1] a strong, syndrome-specific association between IED morphology and early EEG normalization; [3] a significant correlation between EEG normalization at 3 months and clinical seizure freedom; and [4] exploratory evidence that a simplified morphological classification may hold prognostic value across syndromes. These findings generate the hypothesis that detailed EEG analysis could supplement syndromic diagnosis in predicting early treatment outcomes.

Complementary roles of EEG and clinical evaluation

Electroclinical epilepsy syndromes are defined by the integration of both clinical features and EEG characteristics. However, EEG and clinical evaluation function as complementary diagnostic pillars, and the role of EEG is not inferior to clinical assessment; in selected circumstances it may even be superior. EEG may confirm a diagnosis when clinical suspicion is low, reinforce the diagnosis when both are concordant, or help exclude a syndrome when clinical suspicion and EEG findings are both negative. Conversely, when clinical suspicion remains high despite a non-supportive EEG, treatment decisions may still rely on clinical judgment. Importantly, the diagnostic role of EEG should not be constrained by static syndrome labels. There are different types of spikes and different types of sharp waves, each exhibiting distinct morphology, duration, amplitude, slope, and field distribution. These variations suggest that IEDs are not uniform electrophysiological events but may reflect diverse underlying mechanisms, such as sodium channel hyperexcitability, calcium channel dysfunction, or impaired GABAergic inhibition. This conceptual framework supports the rationale for investigating qualitative EEG morphology as a dynamic physiological marker that may provide mechanistic insights beyond traditional syndrome-based categorization. Furtheremore, a finer qualitative analysis of residual epileptiform dischsrges may provide additional insights into the mechanism underlying refractory epilepsy.

EEG normalization as an early biomarker

The strong association between EEG normalization at 3 months and seizure freedom aligns with a body of literature suggesting persistent IEDs may signify an unstable neuronal network with a higher risk of seizure recurrence [1], [3]. Our data support the view that early electrographic improvement is a favorable prognostic sign. However, we emphasize that normalization on a single routine EEG is an imperfect surrogate for durable seizure control. Its predictive value over longer follow-up and its relationship to cognitive outcomes, particularly in pediatric syndromes like CAE and BECTS where this link has been suggested [4], [5], [24], [25], require further study.

Syndrome-specific patterns and mechanistic insights

The high prevalence of classic EEG “fingerprints” (e.g., centrotemporal spikes in BECTS, 3-Hz spike-wave in CAE) reinforces the neurobiological validity of these electroclinical syndromes within the ILAE 2025 framework [8]. The observed treatment response patterns are consistent with known drug mechanisms. For instance, the high rate of EEG normalization with valproate in syndromes featuring polyspike-wave discharges (JME, GGE) aligns with its broad modulation of thalamocortical oscillations [11]. Similarly, the response of focal epilepsies to levetiracetam may relate to its SV2A-mediated action on synaptic vesicle release in localized cortical networks [13]. These observations support a pathophysiological rationale for the associations found but do not establish causality.

The interplay of syndrome, morphology, and treatment

A central consideration in interpreting this study is the intrinsic coupling between syndromic diagnosis, qualitative EEG features, and ASM selection in clinical practice. Our design reflects real world decision making, where these variables are confounded. Therefore, while we observed clear morphology response associations (e.g., focal sharp waves with levetiracetam response), we cannot attribute predictive value solely to IED morphology independent of syndrome. This is a key limitation. The exploratory analysis suggesting a cross-syndromic association using a simplified morphology classification is hypothesis-generating and must be validated in studies where treatment allocation is blinded to specific morphological features.

Clinical implications and future directions

The primary clinical implication of this work is to highlight qualitative EEG morphology as a candidate biomarker worthy of systematic, prospective evaluation. It should not yet alter standard, syndrome based prescribing. Future research should focus on:1.Multicenter studies with longer follow-up to assess the durability of early EEG normalization.2.Investigating the prognostic role of EEG morphology in patients with non-diagnostic or atypical EEGs, a common clinical challenge not addressed here.3.Integrating quantitative EEG measures and cognitive outcomes to build a more comprehensive predictive model.

Limitations

Our findings must be interpreted within the study's constraints: [1] the single-center, observational design; [3] the short-term (3-month) follow-up; [4] the focus on patients with clear syndromes and definitive IEDs, excluding those with ambiguous features; and [5] the aforementioned fundamental confounding between diagnosis, EEG interpretation, and treatment choice. While these factors limit generalizability, they do not invalidate the observed syndromic associations, which provide a foundation for more definitive research.

Conclusion

This prospective study suggests that within well-defined electroclinical syndromes, the qualitative morphology of interictal epileptiform discharges is associated with early antiseizure medication response. The correlation between EEG normalization at three months and seizure freedom further supports the potential prognostic value of early electrographic assessment. These findings highlight the rich physiological information embedded in the routine EEG and reinforce that EEG and clinical evaluation function as complementary diagnostic tools. In certain situations, EEG may provide diagnostic clarity even when clinical suspicion is low. The observed variability in spike and sharp-wave morphology suggests that EEG carries dynamic, mechanistic information extending beyond fixed syndrome labels. While not yet sufficient to alter standard clinical practice, these findings provide a strong rationale for larger, controlled studies to determine whether standardized EEG morphology analysis can improve early treatment selection and long-term outcomes in epilepsy.
