Predictive value of blood composite ratios for Kawasaki disease and coronary artery lesions in febrile children
Authors: Shuhuan Li, Senhong Guan, Lanyuan Liu, Sheng Chen, Zhaohan Yan, Yunying Zeng, Fengshan Ouyang, Jiankai Zhong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundKawasaki disease (KD) is a systemic vasculitis in children, with coronary artery lesions (CALs) presenting as severe complications. Early diagnosis is of paramount importance; however, current biomarkers lack both simplicity and reliability. This study assessed the predictive value of blood composite ratios (BCRs) for KD and CAL.MethodsIn this retrospective study, we analyzed data from 153 pediatric patients with febrile illness from 2016 to 2023. BCRs, including the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), the eosinophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (ELR), the eosinophil-to-monocyte ratio (EMR), and the eosinophil-to-basophil ratio (EBR), were calculated from admission laboratory data. The relationships between BCRs and the incidence of KD and CAL were evaluated using statistical and regression analyses.ResultsPatients with KD presented markedly elevated NLRs, ELRs, EMRs, and EBRs in comparison with those in the febrile control group. The NLR and ELR were found to be significantly associated with the incidence of KD and CAL, with optimal cutoff values of 0.632 and 0.01, respectively. The combined use of BCR indices resulted in increased predictive efficacy for the incidence of KD.ConclusionsBCRs, particularly the NLR and ELR, are valuable for the early identification of high-risk KD patients and CAL damage. These ratios have the potential to enhance clinical management and optimize patient care, although validation in prospective studies is needed.Keywords: Kawasaki disease, Coronary artery lesions, Blood composite ratios, Predictive value, Pediatric cardiology

Introduction

Kawasaki disease (KD), also referred to as mucocutaneous lymph node syndrome, is an acute febrile illness that is characterized by self-limited systemic vasculitis, with its core pathological feature being systemic medium and small vessel vasculitis [1]– [2]. Predominantly affecting children younger than five, KD has emerged as the leading cause of acquired heart disease in pediatric populations in China. The most severe complications of KD are coronary artery lesions (CALs) and coronary artery aneurysm (CAA), with an estimated 15–25% of affected children developing these conditions, which can progress to myocardial infarction and ischemic heart disease [3]. Without timely intervention, the mortality rate can reach 1% within six weeks of disease onset [4]. Early diagnosis and intensive treatment are crucial for reducing the incidence of CALs. The diagnosis of KD relies on the identification of specific clinical phenotypes; however, the variability in these phenotypes poses a challenge in diagnosing incomplete or suspected cases, potentially leading to misdiagnosis and delayed optimal treatment, with complications such as CALs [5]. Therefore, there is a significant need to identify predictive and prognostic markers for KD in children presenting with fever.

The pathogenesis of KD is closely linked to genetic predispositions, inflammatory responses, and the excessive activation of immune cells [6]. The current literature suggests that uncontrolled, persistent inflammation may contribute to the formation of CALs and the subsequent development of CAAs [7]. A substantial body of evidence from numerous studies has demonstrated that augmenting initial anti-inflammatory combination therapy, which includes ulinastatin, steroids, infliximab, and interleukin-1 antagonists, can reduce the incidence of clinically advanced lesions [8–11]. Furthermore, there are similarities in the pathogenesis and clinical characteristics of multisystem inflammatory syndrome in children and KD patients, which can also result in CAA [12]. These findings have led us to hypothesize that stress-related inflammatory markers associated with exogenous infections serve as potential predictors of KD.

Research indicates that several inflammatory cytokines and T cell surface markers can be used as predictors for the occurrence and progression of KD [13]– [14]. However, these biomarkers currently lack cost-effectiveness and reliability, limiting their widespread clinical application. Although several risk scoring systems have been developed in Japan to predict intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) resistance, these systems have not been widely adopted because of their relatively low sensitivity in other countries [15]– [16]. Identifying novel predictive and prognostic markers for KD that do not increase the burden of routine testing and associated medical costs is imperative. These markers could assist clinicians in the early identification of KD and potential CAL risks, enabling the implementation of prompt intensified intervention and reducing the incidence of severe complications.

Recent research has identified blood composite ratios (BCRs) of inflammatory cells as novel and stable biomarkers with broad clinical relevance in the context of inflammatory diseases, mortality, and prognosis [17]– [18]. An abundance of evidence suggests that BCRs are commonly employed in clinical settings as an indicator for monitoring inflammation and predicting patient prognosis [19]– [20]. Thus, BCRs, such as the eosinophil-to-neutrophil ratio (ENR), the eosinophil-to-basophil ratio (EBR), the eosinophil-to-monocyte ratio (EMR), and the eosinophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (ELR), may serve as stable biomarkers for children with KD. However, the variability and predictive value of these ratios in children with KD have yet to be fully elucidated. Consequently, the objective of this study was to investigate the diagnostic efficacy of BCRs for KD and their ability to predict CALs, thereby providing scientific evidence for the implementation of early intervention and prevention strategies aimed at reducing the incidence of severe complications associated with KD.

Methods

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles set forth in the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Institutional Review Board of Shunde Hospital of Southern Medical University (Approval Number: KYLS20240201). As this was a retrospective study, the requirement for written informed consent was waived in accordance with the ethical standards set forth by the Institutional Review Board of Shunde Hospital of Southern Medical University. The dataset consisted of information on pediatric patients treated for fever at Shunde Hospital of Southern Medical University and was collected from January 2016 to December 2023. The patients were divided into two distinct groups: a KD group and a febrile control group. The KD group consisted of patients who had been diagnosed with the condition.

The diagnostic parameters for KD were derived from the 2017 American Heart Association (AHA) guidelines [21]. All patients received the standard treatment regimen, which included intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) and aspirin, in accordance with the established guidelines. The exclusion criteria included patients who were discharged against medical advice; individuals with congenital cardiovascular diseases, tumors, or hematological diseases; patients with recurrent KD; and patients with other severe complications, such as macrophage activation syndrome, shock, severe infection, or sepsis.

A comprehensive dataset encompassing demographic information (e.g., sex and age in months), laboratory parameters, and CAL indicators at the time of admission was assembled for pediatric patients diagnosed with KD. The laboratory data were derived from initial blood tests conducted at the time of admission for patients diagnosed with KD. The following parameters were specifically analyzed: white blood cell (WBC) count, neutrophil (NEU) count, platelet (PLT) count, lymphocyte (LYM) count, red cell distribution width (RDW), hemoglobin (Hb) concentration, and mean platelet volume (MPV). The following laboratory parameters were also analyzed: C-reactive protein (CRP), serum albumin (ALB), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), the erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), and serum sodium (Na). The data were used to calculate the following ratios: the eosinophil-to-neutrophil ratio (ENR), the eosinophil-to-basophil ratio (EBR), the eosinophil-to-monocyte ratio (EMR), the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), and the eosinophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (ELR).

CALs were defined on the basis of body surface area-standardized dimensions, specifically, a Z score of the coronary artery ≥ 2.0. The Z score was based on the maximum internal diameter of the coronary artery (right coronary artery, left anterior descending artery, or left circumflex artery) or a maximum absolute internal diameter of 3 mm (for children aged 5 years and younger), 4 mm (for children aged 5 years and older), or 1.5 times the diameter of an adjacent segment. The Kobayashi formula was employed for the calculation of body surface area and Z scores [22].

A total of 196 pediatric patients with febrile illness were managed by our institution during the study period. Fourteen patients were excluded because of the absence of essential laboratory data; 6 patients were excluded because they did not undergo echocardiography at the time of admission; and 8 patients were excluded because of recurrent KD episodes. Among the remaining 168 patients, 15 were excluded for various reasons: 9 patients received initial intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) or corticosteroid treatment at different medical institutions; 4 patients were excluded because of self-discharge; and 2 patients were excluded because they were hospitalized for less than 24 h (Fig. 1).

Statistical analysis

The statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS 26.0 software (IBM SPSS Statistics, Armonk, NY, USA), R software version 4.4.0 (Institute for Statistics and Mathematics, Vienna, Austria) and GraphPad Prism version 9 (GraphPad software, San Diego, California, USA). The normality of the data distribution was verified using the Shapiro‒Wilk test and a homogeneity test. Data that were normally distributed are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation. Comparisons between groups were conducted using either the independent samples t test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). If the data did not conform to a normal distribution, a nonparametric analysis was conducted using the Mann‒Whitney U test. Comparisons between groups were conducted using Fisher’s exact test, the chi-square test, or Pearson’s chi-square test, with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. The variance inflation factor (VIF) was employed to identify instances of collinearity. Significant indicators and risk factors were identified through the application of univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses. The performance of the model was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and the area under the curve (AUC). The optimal cutoff point was identified using a ROC curve, with the objective of maximizing sensitivity and specificity. To optimize BCRs in clinical practice, the threshold value and specificity, sensitivity, negative predictive value (NPV), and positive predictive value (PPV) were assessed. The level of statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Clinical characteristics of the study participants

A total of 153 patients were enrolled in this study, consisting of 98 males (64.1%) and 55 females (35.9%). Among them, 80 patients (52.3%) were assigned to the febrile control group and 73 (47.7%) to the Kawasaki disease (KD) group(Table 1). No significant differences were observed between the two groups with respect to a history of prematurity or the presence of pericardial effusion. Compared with those in the control group, patients in the KD group were, on average, younger, had lower body weights, and experienced prolonged febrile episodes upon admission. Additionally, the KD group exhibited greater proclivity for conjunctival injection, rash/redness of the lips, swelling/desquamation of the fingertips/toes, nonsuppurative cervical lymphadenitis, and coronary artery lesions; moreover, the Harada and Kobayashi scores were greater in the KD group.

Elevated BCRs in KD

Compared with the febrile control group, patients diagnosed with KD presented markedly elevated NLRs (3.1703 [1.9493–6.1703] vs. 1.7840 [0.7836–2.9458], p = 0.0016), ELRs (0.0658 [0.0210–0.1602] vs. 0.0215 [0.0081–0.0714], p = 0.0454), EMRs (0.2368 [0.0543–0.5086] vs. 0.0812 [0.0282–0.2522], p = 0.0364), and EBRs (5.0000 [1.1008–11.5972] vs. 1.6190 [1.0000–5.3750.0000.3750], p = 0.0134). Although the ENR x10 (0.1795 [0.0480–0.5590] vs. 0.1324 [0.0390–0.4102]) in patients with KD was greater than that in the febrile control group, no statistically significant difference was detected (p = 0.7831) (Fig. 2).

Relationship between BCRs and KD at admission

The RCS test method was employed to assess BCRs and KD occurrence, with adjustments made for potential confounding variables (Fig. 3). The results revealed a statistically significant association between the initial NLR and KD incidence, both in the overall model (P = 0.001) and in the nonlinear model (P = 0.009). Conversely, EBR was significantly overall correlated with KD incidence (P = 0.021); however, the nonlinear relationship was not statistically significant (P = 0.234).

Association between BCRs and cals at admission

To evaluate the association between BCRs and the incidence of CALs, the RCS test method was used to adjust for potential confounding effects (Fig. 4). After adjusting for confounding factors, a significant association was noted between the initial NLR and CAL incidence, as demonstrated in both the overall (P = 0.013) and nonlinear (P = 0.008) models. Conversely, the ELR demonstrated a significant overall correlation with CAL incidence (P = 0.003); however, the nonlinear relationship was not statistically significant (P = 0.120).

ROC curve analysis and prediction of KD

We conducted a ROC curve analysis to determine the diagnostic efficacy of BCRs in patients with KD (Fig. 5). The analysis demonstrated that the NLR (AUC = 70.75%; p < 0.001), the ELR ×10 (AUC = 70.06%; p < 0.001), and the EMR (AUC = 63.36%; p = 0.0044) had substantial predictive value for the diagnosis of KD. The predictive value of EBR was lower at 60.74% (p = 0.0219). In contrast, the diagnostic value of ENR ×10 was insufficient (AUC = 54.32%; p = 0.3573).

Binary logistic regression modeling analysis for KD prediction

The risk factors for KD were identified through binary logistic regression analysis. Individual risk factors at the time of admission included younger age in months; shorter height; lower weight; a longer duration of fever prior to admission; elevated levels of CRP, PCT, CK, NLR, and EBR; and lower albumin levels. The associations between the NLR, EBR, and incidence of KD at admission, both adjusted and unadjusted for various factors, are presented in Table 3. In Model 1, a significant positive correlation was observed between the NLR (OR = 1.165, 95% CI 1.049–1.294, P = 0.004) and EBR (OR = 1.050, 95% CI 1.007–1.094, P = 0.021) and KD incidence, without any adjustments (Table 4). Subsequently, partial and full adjustments for potential confounding variables consistently demonstrated that elevated NLRs and EBRs were significant risk factors for the occurrence of KD at admission in Model 2 (NLR: OR = 1.002, 95% CI 1.002–1.005, P = 0.021; EBR = 1.002, 95% CI 1.002–1.005, P = 0.021) and Model 3 (NLR: OR = 1.002, 95% CI 1.002–1.005, P = 0.021; EBR: OR = 1.002, 95% CI 1.002–1.005, P = 0.021) (Table 4).

Enhancing the predictive capability for KD through integrated BCR indices

A multivariate ROC curve analysis for binary outcomes revealed that the combined application of NLR + EBR (AUC = 76.34%, 95% CI 0.6893–0.8376, P < 0.001), NLR + EMR (AUC = 75.09%, 95% CI 0.6750–0.8267, P < 0.001), NLR + ELR (AUC = 75.12%, 95% CI 0.6749–0.8275, P < 0.001) and all BCR indices (AUC = 75.89%, 95% CI 0.6844–0.8334, P < 0.001) demonstrated a marked enhancement in predictive efficacy for KD incidence compared with the use of a single BCR index (Fig. 6).

Discussion

KD is an acute, self-limiting, systemic vasculitis strongly correlated with the immune response and inflammatory processes [23]. The disease affects a range of small- to medium-sized arteries, involving multiple organs, including the kidneys, lungs, and coronary arteries [1–3]. Despite the development of several scoring models by researchers to predict the risk of KD in febrile patients on the basis of the immunopathological basis of vascular inflammation in KD patients, there remains an urgent need for more specific and reliable biomarkers to predict the risk of KD and CALs [15]– [16, 24]. Accordingly, the present study was undertaken to ascertain the potential utility of BCRs in predicting the occurrence of KD and CALs in febrile pediatric patients, thus assisting pediatricians in the selection of more efficacious and tailored therapeutic regimens.

Our study builds upon the existing body of research by examining the role of BCRs in the prediction of KD and CALs in febrile children. Our findings indicate a notable elevation in standard inflammatory markers, such as white blood cell count and C-reactive protein, during the acute phase of KD. This finding is consistent with the established understanding of KD pathogenesis, which involves a robust inflammatory response [25]. Notably, we observed a significant increase in neutrophil counts in KD patients, which is in line with previous studies highlighting the role of neutrophils in vasculitis development [26]. However, the diagnostic specificity of neutrophils is limited, constraining their utility as standalone diagnostic markers.

Furthermore, this study revealed an anomalous elevation in eosinophil levels, which was observed in 90.34% of children with KD. Eosinophils are critical immune cells in the blood that play pivotal roles in regulating immune system hyperactivation and promoting inflammatory responses [27]. Chemokines and cytokines have been demonstrated to stimulate the accumulation of eosinophils, while activated eosinophils have the capacity to secrete cytokines, thereby further enhancing the inflammatory cascade [28]. The available evidence suggests that eosinophils may be useful in distinguishing KD from other febrile illnesses and are associated with IVIG resistance in KD patients [29]. Kuo et al. reported a negative correlation between increased eosinophil counts post-IVIG treatment and the incidence of IVIG resistance in KD patients [30]. Consequently, alterations in the eosinophil count prior to and following IVIG administration may represent a promising indicator for predicting and assessing IVIG resistance in KD patients. Although eosinophils demonstrate limited diagnostic efficacy in distinguishing KD from other febrile illnesses, our study provides support for these hypotheses. While eosinophils show potential as diagnostic indicators, their low specificity limits their broader application in clinical settings.

In recent years, BCRs have attracted increasing interest for their extensive clinical relevance in predicting mortality and prognosis in a range of inflammatory diseases, allergies, and cancers [17]– [18, 31]. He identified the NLR among BCRs as a predictor of cardiac event risk and mortality in patients undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention, as well as adverse outcomes in patients with malignancy [32]. A study conducted by Wen demonstrated a significant correlation between the incidence of heart failure in patients with acute coronary syndrome who had undergone percutaneous coronary intervention and the levels of lymphocytes and eosinophilic granulocytes [33]. Furthermore, it demonstrated superior diagnostic accuracy in predicting coronary slow flow compared with eosinophils or lymphocytes alone. In a related study, Holub and Biete reported that higher ELR values were associated with poorer survival outcomes, with an AUC of 0.61 for ELR in predicting survival outcomes [31]. The aforementioned studies indicate that BCRs may be more effective predictive tools than established biomarkers. To our knowledge, no research has explored the correlation between BCRs and the incidence of KD. In light of the aforementioned evidence, our study is the first to emphasize the significance of BCRs in predicting the occurrence of KD in febrile children.

The findings of our study indicate a positive correlation between elevated NLR, ELR, EMR, and EBR in febrile children and the incidence of KD. In contrast to previous studies, our investigation focused on BCRs in children with KD, examining the interaction between eosinophils and other inflammatory cells [27, 34]– [35]. The NLR, ELR, EMR, and EBR were significantly greater in patients with KD than in the controls. According to the RCS model, there was a significant overall and nonlinear association between the NLR and the incidence of KD and CALs. In contrast, the EBR demonstrated a significant linear relationship with KD incidence, whereas the ELR showed a significant linear relationship with CAL incidence. Furthermore, the analysis revealed that an NLR cutoff value of 0.632 is predictive of both KD and CAL incidence, with a significant increase in incidence observed when the NLR exceeds this value. The cutoff value for the EBR in predicting the incidence of KD is 10.2, with a marked increase in incidence when EBR levels exceed this value. With respect to the prediction of CAL incidence by the ELR, the optimal cutoff value was identified as 0.01, with a significant increase in incidence observed when the ELR exceeded this value. In light of the aforementioned evidence, the findings of the present study align with those of previous studies in this field. BCRs are effective means of reflecting the risk of cardiovascular events caused by various diseases [31, 33]. Consequently, our findings provide valuable metrics for the early identification of high-risk KD patients and high-risk CALs in children. The monitoring of the NLR, EBR, and ELR can facilitate the enhancement of patient management, which is of paramount importance for the optimization of clinical care and the reduction in future adverse events.

The preliminary results of the ROC analysis for the NLR, ELR, EMR and EBR individually demonstrated that the NLR is a reliable biomarker for distinguishing Kawasaki disease, with a high level of sensitivity and specificity. The predictive value of the NLR remained robust even after multivariate adjustment. The ELR demonstrated the second-best diagnostic performance, whereas the EMR and EBR exhibited moderate diagnostic efficacy. A composite ROC analysis was subsequently conducted, combining the indicators with significant predictive value. Notably, the combination of the NLR and EBR had the highest predictive value for KD incidence, with an AUC of 76.34%, which was significantly greater than that of all the other BCRs. Consequently, these biomarkers should be regarded as adjunctive diagnostic tools rather than standalone indicators for KD. The development of simple and reliable diagnostic ratios based on these markers holds promise for providing frontline clinicians with practical tools to identify high-risk patients. In regions with limited access to echocardiographic evaluations, employing a high-risk threshold for these ratios could play a pivotal role in prioritizing patients who may require urgent cardiac assessment for suspected KD.

The diagnosis of KD continues to pose a significant clinical challenge, even in regions with advanced healthcare infrastructure. This difficulty arises from the disease’s variable and often subtle clinical presentations, which frequently lead to delays in diagnosis. Such challenges are further amplified in resource-limited settings where access to echocardiography and specialized laboratory tests is restricted. These diagnostic delays not only increase the likelihood of missed or late diagnoses but also substantially increase the risk of coronary artery complications, a primary concern in the management of KD. The principal objective of our study was to address these challenges by identifying accessible and cost-effective biomarkers derived from routine complete blood count parameters. These biomarkers are envisioned as early “alert systems,” enabling more vigilant clinical monitoring and timely therapeutic interventions. By facilitating earlier recognition and management, the proposed approach aims to reduce the risk of irreversible coronary artery sequelae and improve clinical outcomes in both resource-rich and resource-limited settings.

It is important to acknowledge the limitations of this retrospective study, which include constraints in controlling for potential confounding factors due to the data sampling method. While the observed alterations in blood ratios provide valuable insights into the complex interplay between BCRs and the immune response, they do not establish a direct causal link between KD and CALs. Nevertheless, our results demonstrated significant associations between BCRs and KD incidence, as well as CALs, highlighting the need for validation in prospective studies.

Conclusions

Our study provides compelling evidence for the use of BCRs, particularly the NLR and ELR, as valuable metrics for the early identification of high-risk KD patients and CAL damage. The incorporation of BCR indicators significantly enhances the predictive capacity of conventional models for KD and its associated CALs. These findings have important implications for clinical practice, potentially for optimizing patient management and reducing the incidence of adverse outcomes.
