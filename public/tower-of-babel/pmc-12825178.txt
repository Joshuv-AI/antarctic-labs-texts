Cultural clashes in maternity care: midwifery students’ struggles with communication and cultural differences — a qualitative study from Turkey
Authors: Esra ÖZER, Şerife İrem DÖNER
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTranscultural care has gained importance with the global increase in migration. This study aimed to explore and describe midwifery students’ feelings, thoughts, and experiences regarding transcultural midwifery approaches in healthcare delivery in Turkey.MethodsThis qualitative descriptive study was conducted between January and June 2024 with 42 female midwifery students studying at the Faculty of Health Sciences, Department of Midwifery, Ankara Medipol University. Data were collected through semi-structured, face-to-face interviews and analyzed using inductive qualitative content analysis. Codes were developed directly from the data, grouped into sub-categories, and then abstracted into categories.ResultsThree main categories and ten sub-categories were identified: [1] Implementation Challenges (communication difficulties, cultural differences, lack of knowledge); [2] Emotional Reactions (empathy, stress, prejudice, curiosity, uncertainty); and [3] Student Perceptions (awareness of cultural diversity, gender-related experiences). Students expressed both enthusiasm and anxiety about caring for culturally diverse patients. Language barriers, lack of cultural knowledge, and uncertainty about appropriate care practices were major challenges. While participants shared similar experiences, variations were observed in emotional responses and coping strategies depending on clinical experience.ConclusionMidwifery students’ experiences revealed a balance between curiosity and uncertainty when providing care to patients from different cultures. Integrating transcultural care content and simulation-based learning into midwifery curricula could enhance students’ competence and confidence in providing culturally sensitive maternity care. Background

Globalization and migration have led to increased cultural diversity in different societies around the world [1]. Turkey is the country with the largest refugee population in the world, hosting more than 3 million people from Syria and 300.000 people of different ethnicities since 2016 [2]. Most of the refugee population consists of women of reproductive age [3]. This population has many health problems related to migration [4]. In Turkey, midwives play a key role in protecting and improving the health of migrant women during antenatal, birth, and postnatal care. They primarily work in hospital-based maternity units and provide community-based maternal health services in primary care centers. Although home births are uncommon, midwives contribute to ensuring safe and culturally sensitive maternity care. However, there is limited research on whether women in Turkey perceive their maternity care as culturally respectful, which highlights the need for studies exploring midwifery students’ perspectives. In addition, transcultural education is not comprehensively included in the national midwifery curriculum, which may limit students’ preparedness to provide culturally competent care. These factors together justify the relevance and necessity of this study.

However, when midwives and nurses lack the necessary cultural competence, their prejudiced or negative approaches toward individuals, families, or communities from different cultural backgrounds may negatively affect the quality of health care [5]. Turkey suggests that such negative attitudes make it difficult to comply with treatment, reduce the rate of utilization of health services, and negatively affect the physical and psychiatric health of refugees [6].

Leininger’s transcultural care theory is often tailor-made to fit the cultural values, beliefs and lifestyles of the individual, group or organization. Transcultural care theory endeavors to provide cognitively based, helpful, supportive and facilitative culturally compatible nursing care [7]. The aim of care is to comply with or have meaning and health outcomes that are beneficial for people with different or similar cultural backgrounds [8]. Transcultural care refers to holistic care to protect the health and well-being of individuals and to help them cope with barriers in the treatment process. A health professional who does not recognize the value and importance of culturally appropriate care cannot provide effective care [9]. The cultural competence of midwives is an important factor in improving and promoting maternal and child health [10].

Midwives need to have sufficient knowledge of different cultural backgrounds and traditions to be able to provide culturally respectful care [11]. Therefore, midwifery students need to develop cultural awareness, knowledge and skills through an intercultural midwifery curriculum before graduation [12]. After graduation, midwives are expected to be culturally competent global citizens, ready to provide health care services to people from diverse cultural backgrounds and ethnicities [13]. The International Confederation of Midwives (ICM) has emphasized the need to take women’s cultural beliefs and norms into account in midwifery care in antenatal, birth and postnatal care [14].

In the literature, although there are studies on transcultural care on nursing students, studies with midwifery students are limited [13, 15, 16]. Women and children constitute a large part of the migration that Turkey has received in recent years. Considering this situation, transcultural care is important in the field of midwifery. The aim of this study was to explore the challenges midwifery students face in applying transcultural midwifery approaches in relation to communication, cultural differences, and gender roles.

Methods

Research design

A qualitative descriptive research design grounded in a constructivist paradigm was used in study. This design was chosen to gain an in-depth understanding of midwifery students’ experiences and the meanings they attribute to caring for patients from different cultural backgrounds. The constructivist paradigm assumes that reality is co-constructed between the researcher and participants. Therefore, this approach allowed the researchers to describe participants’ experiences as they were lived, without imposing preconceived theoretical frameworks. The study followed the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) to ensure methodological transparency and rigor [17]. In addition to adhering to SRQR guidelines, validity and reliability were ensured through data triangulation (using both written notes and audio recordings), researcher triangulation (involving multiple researchers in the data analysis process), expert validation of coded transcripts, and maintaining an audit trail to document analytical decisions throughout the study. Constructivist qualitative research assumes that knowledge and meaning are co-constructed through the interaction between the researcher and participants. This paradigm was suitable for this study because it aimed to explore how midwifery students construct their understanding of transcultural care through their own lived experiences.

Sample and participants

This study was conducted between January and June 2024 at Ankara Medipol University, Faculty of Health Sciences, Department of Midwifery. Semi-structured, face-to-face interviews were carried out with midwifery students from different academic years. In total, 42 students aged 19–34 years participated in the study (Table 1). In Turkey, midwifery education is a four-year undergraduate program. Students begin their first clinical practice in the spring semester of the first academic year and continue their placements throughout the program under the supervision of academic as well as clinical instructors. The sample size was determined based on the principle of data saturation, which is widely accepted in qualitative research [18].

Recruitment process

Eligible students were identified through a preliminary interview. They were invited via e-mail, which included detailed information about the purpose and procedures of the study. Students who agreed to participate provided written informed consent, after which individual interview dates and times were arranged. Interviews were conducted individually in quiet, private rooms within the educational institution to ensure confidentiality and comfort.

Data collection

Method

Data were collected through semi-structured, face-to-face individual interviews conducted at the university. All participants were midwifery students in the Faculty of Health Sciences where the research team works, and the first author teaches and supervises clinical placements in this program. Thus, the students had already interacted with the research team for several months before data collection, which helped them feel comfortable discussing their experiences. In total, 42 interviews were carried out, yielding 942 min of audio-recorded material (mean 22 min per interview). Semi-structured interviews were chosen to enable participants to describe concrete clinical encounters and the emotions associated with transcultural care in their own words, while allowing the interviewer (the second author, a research assistant trained in qualitative interviewing techniques) to use probing questions (e.g. “Can you tell me more about that?”, “What happened next?”, “How did you feel at that moment?”) to obtain rich, experience-near accounts.

Data collection tool

Two data collection instruments were used:

A descriptive information form (5 questions) including socio-demographic characteristics such as age and socio-economic status.

A semi-structured interview form (11 open-ended questions) specifically developed for this study to explore midwifery students’ perceptions, feelings, and experiences regarding transcultural midwifery practices in health service delivery.

The interview guide was created based on a literature review and expert opinions to ensure content validity. The general topics covered in the interview guide are presented in Table 3.

Data collection process

During data collection, the semi-structured interview technique was applied consistently for all participants. Each participant’s statements were recorded both in writing on the interview form and digitally with a voice recorder to ensure accuracy and completeness. Interviews were held in a quiet environment that allowed participants to speak comfortably.

Rigor

Trustworthiness was addressed using the criteria of credibility, dependability, confirmability and transferability. The research team included midwives and nurses with expertise in qualitative research and transcultural care, which enriched the interpretation of the findings. All participants were midwifery students in the faculty where the research team works, and the first author teaches and supervises clinical placements in this program. Thus, a pre-existing educational relationship and familiarity had developed over previous semesters. We approached this relationship reflexively as a potential source of both trust and power asymmetry. Participation was strictly voluntary, non-participation or withdrawal had no consequences for course grades or clinical assessments, and confidentiality was emphasized. The first author did not participate in the interviews and instead engaged with the data from a more external, reflective position during analysis, which helped to minimize the risk that students would feel pressured to provide socially desirable responses.

All interviews were transcribed verbatim and analyzed using the qualitative content analysis approach described by Graneheim and Lundman (2004). The analytic process involved repeated reading of the transcripts, identification and condensation of meaning units, coding, grouping similar codes into sub-categories and abstraction into broader categories [21]. Analysis was supported by ATLAS.ti 9 software (Scientific Software Development GmbH, Berlin, Germany). A total of 649 codes were generated and grouped into 10 sub-categories under 3 main categories. In line with Braun and Clarke’s (2006) six phases of thematic analysis, we systematically documented familiarization with the data, generation of initial codes, development and review of categories, and the production of the final report to enhance methodological transparency. Illustrative quotations are presented in the Results section to demonstrate the link between the raw data and the interpretations.

Researcher triangulation was ensured by involving all authors in the review and discussion of codes and categories until consensus was reached, thereby providing an audit trail of analytic decisions. To further enhance confirmability, selected transcript excerpts together with the corresponding codes and categories were reviewed by two public health nursing experts and a social worker, who confirmed that the interpretations were credible. Transferability was supported through purposive sampling from different academic years, maximum variation in participants’ socio-demographic characteristics and clinical experiences, and the provision of thick description and direct quotations. Because some members of the research team work in the institution where the students study, they were able to observe the clinical context in which transcultural encounters occurred. While this closeness required careful reflexivity due to potential power imbalances, it also facilitated a nuanced, context-sensitive interpretation of the students’ accounts.

Categories 1: implementation challenges

Midwifery students faced various difficulties in implementing transcultural midwifery approaches during clinical practice. These challenges negatively affected their ability to provide adequate care and their job satisfaction. The implementation challenges were categorized under three sub-categories: Communication Challenges, Cultural Differences, and Cultural Awareness.

Communication challenges included anxieties and difficulties arising from language differences, inadequacies in expression, and misunderstandings caused by cultural or educational disparities. Students emphasized that not sharing a common language and lack of interpreters created serious barriers in providing care.

One student emphasized the helplessness of not sharing a common language, explaining:

“Even though I don’t have many opportunities to meet individuals from different cultures, Syria, which is the culture and actually the country, is the one I encounter most frequently. A woman speaking Arabic was admitted with mastitis when I was working in the oncology department and she was 23 weeks pregnant. The first thing we guessed from mastitis was that she had a baby at home who was at the age of breastfeeding, but she had stopped breastfeeding because she was pregnant and her milk ducts were blocked. Despite being 23 weeks pregnant, we were facing a mother who hadn’t had any tests done. We couldn’t understand what we were trying to explain, but we couldn’t understand what she was saying either. In fact, we had no choice but to wait with our hands tied until the interpreter arrived. After that day, I realized more and more that a midwife and a health professional should improve themselves in different languages. And sign language came to my mind. Therefore, I thought it would be right for all health personnel, including me, to learn sign language while they were still in school”. (Interviewee 18)

Another participant described how miscommunication caused emotional distress and patient neglect:

“Since she did not speak our language, there was a communication problem. They could not take care of her properly because there were few staff and a lot of work in the hospital. Once, there was a Syrian woman in the pediatric emergency unit. Her baby was tachycardic, and she was making gestures that we interpreted as asking for food for the baby. We said no, thinking she wanted to feed the baby. Then she tried to say something else, but I didn’t understand, so I used Google Translate. It turned out that the woman herself had been there since the previous night, unable to reach anyone and unable to communicate — she had been waiting hungry all that time. I was really upset; I immediately informed the midwives and nurses, and we gave the mother something to eat.” (Interviewee 10).

Similarly, a few students noted that patients sometimes refused care due to misunderstanding medical instructions:

“First of all, I had difficulty in communicating. They did not want to receive care because we could not communicate. Because they could not understand how to give care or they misunderstood and made wrong applications”. (Interviewee 41)

Overall, communication difficulties created stress and frustration among students and limited their ability to provide culturally sensitive care.

Cultural Differences; Having insufficient knowledge about the beliefs, values and health perceptions of different cultures, etc.

One student described how cultural norms regarding fertility prevented women from accepting medical advice:

“We say that the pregnant woman has given birth too many times and that the body is now very tired and we want her tubes to be tied, especially most of these pregnant women are Syrian, but pregnant women say that they do not want this situation, the reason is that their husbands marry someone else, that is, they tell us unhappily that there will be consequences such as the case of kuma or divorce. We tell them that they should take some time off so that the body can recover. However, they do not accept it due to cultural differences”. (Interviewee 30)

Another student highlighted how traditional newborn care practices conflicted with medical guidelines, such as applying kohl to a baby’s eyes despite infection risks:

“Once I went to the baby and mother in the postnatal ward to provide breastfeeding counselling and active sucking. But the state of the baby surprised me very much, they had drawn kohl on both eyes of the baby, in fact, doing this to the newborn causes eye infection. Although I explained this many times, they were doing it because they wanted the baby to have black eyebrows and eyes according to their culture. Eventually we deleted it, of course. It is really difficult to communicate with Syrian patients and explain the procedure to them. I also experienced many issues related to birth companions. While the woman wanted her husband to be with her, her mother-in-law argued that it was shameful. According to their culture, it was considered inappropriate for the woman to interact with me while her husband was present, so she hesitated even during anamnesis and care.” (Interviewee 42).

These examples illustrate that cultural differences can challenge evidence-based practice and require midwives to balance respect for cultural beliefs with patient safety.

Cultural Awareness: Being unaware of one’s own cultural prejudices, perspectives, not having enough knowledge about the beliefs, values and traditions of different cultures, etc.

One student shared how encountering a circumcised woman during birth made her reflect on her own limited knowledge about different cultural traditions:

“…The birth was completed. Some time later, the woman wanted to wrap her baby tightly in a cloth, and I asked her why. She said, ‘The baby will get cold.’ I told her that it would be enough to cover the baby so that it would not get cold, and that wrapping too tightly was wrong and harmful. According to their culture, the baby is wrapped so tightly that it cannot move. I was astonished when I heard that.” (Interviewee 28).

Another student emphasized the importance of empathy and mutual understanding when cultural or religious values conflicted with routine care:

“In fact, we try to provide the same care to individuals from different cultures. However, it can sometimes disturb the patient in terms of religion or there are many times when we cannot get along in terms of language. In this case, we try to meet the patient at a common point”. (Interviewee 39)

These reflections indicate that cross-cultural encounters increased students’ self-awareness and highlighted the need for education on cultural humility and sensitivity in midwifery training.

Categories 2: emotional reactions

Midwifery students’ various emotional reactions were identified when addressing their experiences of transcultural midwifery approaches in health service delivery. These responses can be influenced by various factors such as the student’s individual experiences, cultural background, educational level and clinical environment. They may be excited by the opportunity to work with patients from different cultures and learn about their perspectives, or they may be concerned that they do not have enough knowledge and skills or do not know how best to care for a patient from a different culture. This category includes six sub-categories: empathy, stress, prejudice, curiosity, uncertainty, and communication difficulties, which reflect the emotional and cognitive experiences of midwifery students towards transcultural midwifery approaches in healthcare delivery.

Empathy; efforts to understand the perspectives of patients from different cultures and to empathies with them.

One student explained how she tried to connect emotionally despite communication barriers:

“Firstly, I was very anxious, I was very afraid of giving wrong information or offending and upsetting them. Then I adapted myself to those individuals, but I did it without giving up my own essence. I tried to learn their language, but I failed. I tried to understand them and their feelings and put myself in their shoes. It was a nice experience for me to see different lives, different cultures, to perceive them and to try to learn”. (Interviewee 5)

Another student highlighted how empathy helped her respond to patients’ fear and protect their privacy:

“The other person felt helpless because he was looking at me with eyes full of fear and I felt very lonely because he couldn’t understand what to do, putting myself in his place. Due to language and cultural differences, they had difficulty in understanding what we wanted to explain and what they should do. Syrian and Afghan individuals were very careful about privacy. When we realised this, we paid as much attention as possible”. (Interviewee 38)

Stress; Observation of emotional reactions such as restlessness, tension, contraction, sweating and difficulty in focusing.

One student described how a patient’s reaction during a vaginal examination caused intense anxiety:

“I have given antenatal, labour and postnatal care to women from different communities in Turkey or from different parts of the world. I have understood this from their posture, the way they dress, the way they speak, their language or their cultural approach to babies. I had a lot of difficulty in communicating. Before I was going to perform vaginal palpation on a foreign patient, of course, I tried to explain the procedure as much as I could. I tried to explain that this was important for the baby, but she never allowed the touch and started shouting by closing her legs. As a student, I was in a difficult situation there, I was stressed, stressed, I thought I made a wrong application. I felt the need to express myself and explain”. (Interviewee 3)

Another student explained that observing patients persist in traditional practices despite medical advice increased her sense of helplessness:

“When I encounter individuals with whom we have cultural differences as a health professional, I have seen that they continue to do what is in their own culture, even if I explain what is medically beneficial for the mother and baby or for a woman who is about to enter menopause to protect, maintain and improve her health. For example, a woman going through menopause drinking litres of boiled onion juice, giving the baby fennel to drink to prevent gas, brewing cumin and giving it to drink. The cultural differences and traditional practices of the patient profile that I am in charge of caring for and the decline and deterioration in their health have increased my fear and stress”. (Interviewee 37)

These experiences show that cultural misunderstandings can lead to emotional strain for midwifery students, underscoring the importance of emotional preparedness and supervision during cross-cultural clinical encounters.

Prejudice; Conscious or unconscious reluctance to provide care for individuals from different cultures, often due to fear of inadequate communication or support.

One student stated:

“I learn cultural information by asking questions and chatting with the pregnant woman when I enter her room to take care of her. I try to understand what their culture is like, what they care about and what is right for them. I do not want to prejudge the pregnant woman even if our cultures clash at some points. I do not speak in a judgemental way and I listen to what they tell me. I try to convey something from my own culture on some of the issues they ask me to help them with. I want to explain the truths they know wrong in a way that they will not try to be prejudiced. Sometimes I research their culture on the internet”. (Interviewee 8)

Another noted the challenges of working with less educated patients, which sometimes reinforced stereotypes:

“We have problems especially in terms of communication and speech. They oppose the maintenance and applications that need to be done. Their lack of knowledge was high and their education level was low”. (Interviewee 24)

These accounts reveal that while prejudices may arise, reflective communication can help reduce bias and improve intercultural understanding.

Curious; The desire to get to know culturally different people and their cultures, the desire to acquire new knowledge, the desire to have a different experience, etc.

As one participant explained:

“It is very exciting to get to know a new culture while providing service to our patients. Wondering about the behaviours we see from different cultures, researching on the internet, asking patients increases our knowledge”. (Interviewee 32)

Similarly, another student highlighted that interacting with culturally diverse patients expanded her perspective:

“I understand the pregnant women who come to the hospital by chatting with them and then I get information by asking or researching the things I am curious about. I learn new cultures through the communication I establish with the patients in the department where I am in practice”. (Interviewee 31)

This curiosity fostered openness and helped transform anxiety into motivation for cultural learning.

Uncertainty: Caring for a patient with an unknown culture, emotional reactions caused by uncertainty, etc.

One student reflected:

“There was a particularly urgent case. The woman’s water broke, her patency was almost complete and everything progressed very quickly. But the problem with me was that we could not communicate with the woman at all, we could not express ourselves, we could not manage that moment. At that moment, only uncertainty prevailed. The whole team was so tense until the baby was born”. (Interviewee 6)

Another described frustration when interpreter support was delayed during emergencies:

“Certainly interpreters are requested, but sometimes they do not arrive on time. In situations where rapid intervention is required, uncertainty and waiting is too long. It can be painful and exhausting for the patient”. (Interviewee 14)

Such moments of uncertainty underscored the need for structured communication support and cross-cultural training in clinical environments.

Categories 3: student perceptions

Midwifery students reported that they lacked theoretical knowledge and practical experience related to transcultural midwifery approaches in health service delivery, rather than deficiencies in general clinical skills, and that they had limited opportunities to provide care for patients from different cultural backgrounds. This category includes two sub-categories: lack of knowledge and gender, which represent the experiences of midwifery students regarding transcultural midwifery approaches in health service provision.

Lack of knowledge; Insufficient knowledge content for transcultural midwifery approaches, not having enough clinical experience with patients from different cultures, etc.

One student explained:

“Although we have clinical knowledge and experience, we cannot reflect this to patient groups from different cultures. We do not know what to do and how to behave in cross-cultural service provision. We learn by experimenting”. (Interviewee 37)

Another participant emphasized that this gap was common even among practicing midwives:

“Especially during my summer internship, I learnt that caring for patients from different cultures requires a completely different competence. We were not trained for these, I felt my deficiencies so much. This was not only in us, but also in the midwives and nurses of the graduated years. This deficiency should be eliminated in our culturally rich country”. (Interviewee 11)

Gender; Gender perceptions in different cultures, etc.

One participant explained that traditional norms limited women’s autonomy in seeking care:

“Since they are not fully adapted to where they live, they do not know where the health centres or hospitals are located. Women’s health, unfortunately, women are a little bit withdrawn and hesitant when it comes to health. Due to their culture, they do not apply to health centres or hospital-style health institutions unless necessary, or they go with their mothers-in-law”. (Interviewee 36)

Another student added that reproductive decisions were predominantly male-dominated:

“The majority of women do not know the concept of family planning. They do not know how to protect themselves. They were always saying that my husband wants to have children. People of different cultures especially do not know these issues. Their only duty is to give birth. There is also the fear that if she does not want to give birth, she will be imposed on. Even if we try to explain, it seems impossible to change the norms that exist in their culture. (Interviewee 20)

These statements highlight the importance of addressing gender-sensitive approaches within transcultural midwifery education to promote equitable healthcare for women from diverse backgrounds.

Discussion

Transcultural care awareness should be laid at the undergraduate education level of the midwifery profession. As a result of the study, midwifery students stated that they had difficulty in communicating while providing care. Midwifery students were found to experience emotions such as empathy, stress, prejudice, curiosity and uncertainty in health service provision. In addition, it was determined that students lacked theoretical/clinical knowledge of transcultural midwifery approaches and did not have a good command of gender perceptions in different cultures.

Migrants are at higher risk of maternal and neonatal morbidity and mortality than local women in the region of origin [22]. Midwives are unable to provide effective care to migrant women due to the language barrier, limited patient-carer trust, misunderstandings and cultural differences [23]. This situation constitutes an obstacle in the provision of health care services, even with sufficient knowledge and experience.

The most important and primary barrier in transcultural health services is language. Due to the language barrier, it is seen that the needs cannot be met because of communication breakdowns and lack of knowledge of cultural expectations [24]. In this study, midwifery students expressed that they had difficulty in expressing themselves and understanding women while providing transcultural care. As a result, it was also determined that they experienced misunderstandings, could not understand cultural beliefs and values, and had insufficient knowledge about the health perceptions of different cultures.

Research from Sweden, the United Kingdom, and Turkey has consistently demonstrated that midwives experience significant communication challenges when caring for migrant or ethnically diverse women [23, 25, 26]. Across these contexts, language barriers, differing cultural expectations of care, and the lack of institutional interpreter support were identified as major obstacles to providing safe and effective maternity care. The findings of the present study align with this international evidence, confirming that language barriers remain one of the most common and critical challenges in transcultural midwifery practice. Students emphasized that not sharing a common language often led to misunderstandings, limited trust, and compromised quality of care. Strengthening institutional support mechanisms—such as providing on-site interpreters, developing multilingual patient education materials, and offering language-training opportunities for healthcare staff—may help to overcome these barriers and enhance equitable health service delivery.

Health professionals have difficulties in coping with the traumas experienced by women as well as cultural and language barriers while providing care to women from different cultures [27]. For example, a significant number of migrant/refugee women experience anxiety, depression and trauma-related symptoms due to the difficulties they have experienced [28]. No study has been found to examine the emotions felt by midwifery students caring for traumatized women. In this study, it was determined that midwifery students experienced various emotional reactions such as empathy, stress, prejudice, curiosity and uncertainty because of caring for women from different cultures. In a study conducted in Switzerland to examine the difficulties encountered by professionals providing reproductive health services to women seeking asylum, it was reported that health professionals felt the same emotions as women experiencing trauma [29]. A qualitative study of Irish midwives found that working with traumatized women elicited feelings of empathy, compassion and powerlessness. It was also reported that midwives experienced feelings of sadness, worry and anxiety for the women they cared for [30]. In a study conducted on nursing students in Turkey, it was reported that students felt hopelessness, sadness and anger [31]. Health professionals’ experiences of working with migrants can shape their perceptions and attitudes towards transcultural care [32]. Emotions experienced by health professionals while providing care affect the professional-patient relationship and cause clinical outcomes to be affected [33]. Therefore, in addition to the need for clinical support for midwives caring for traumatized women, it is important to include trauma-related educational content for midwifery students at the undergraduate level.

Transcultural care provision is complex for health professionals and students due to different interpretations of personal and organizational factors [34]. The main aim of transcultural care is to individualize care by addressing the physical, psychological, emotional, spiritual and social needs of patients as well as developing the knowledge and skills required to provide culturally diverse care [35].Students need to have cultural competence to develop their transcultural professional practice. Training on cultural competence should be provided at the undergraduate education level. In this study, it was found that midwifery students lacked knowledge about how to provide transcultural midwifery care. In the literature, no study measuring students’ knowledge about transcultural care was found. Considering that the foundations of transcultural care should be laid at the undergraduate education level, measuring the knowledge levels of students and developing educational interventions for this will shed light on future studies, and future research should also include the perspectives and emotional experiences of women from different cultures to provide a more comprehensive understanding of transcultural maternity care.

Transcultural care should be understood and skillful by all members of the healthcare team. The training and foundation of transcultural care is laid at the undergraduate level. For this reason, issues related to transcultural care skills should be addressed in a broad framework in education programmed. Transcultural service provision contents for practice in clinical trainings will also be initiatives to improve equality in health service provision.

Conclusion

This study revealed that midwifery students experience both enthusiasm and uncertainty when providing care to patients from different cultural backgrounds. They are motivated by the opportunity to learn from diverse cultural perspectives; however, they also express concerns about their limited knowledge, communication skills, and uncertainty in providing culturally appropriate care. Language barriers and lack of knowledge were identified as the main challenges. These findings emphasize the importance of integrating theoretical and clinical components of transcultural midwifery into undergraduate curricula. Including pre-hospital simulations and clinical placements that allow students to interact with patients from different cultures may enhance their competence and confidence, ultimately improving the quality and equity of maternity care services. Future studies should also include the perspectives and emotional experiences of women from different cultures to provide a more comprehensive understanding of transcultural maternity care.

Strengths and limitations

This study provides an in-depth and context-specific understanding of midwifery students’ experiences in providing transcultural care. It is one of the few qualitative studies conducted in Turkey that explores the challenges and emotions midwifery students face while caring for women from diverse cultural backgrounds. The inclusion of direct quotations from participants enhances the credibility and richness of the data. Moreover, the use of rigorous qualitative methods and adherence to the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) strengthened methodological reliability.

However the study has certain limitations. The findings are based on participants from a single institution, which may limit the generalizability of the results to other educational settings or cultural contexts. Additionally, self-reported experiences may reflect personal biases and recall limitations. Future studies using multi-center samples and mixed-method designs could provide more comprehensive insights into transcultural midwifery education and practice.
