Reflective writing in general practice residency: perceived usefulness of complex and authentic situation reports in France
Authors: Kève-Yann Lara, Rémi Simonet, Aude Allemang-Trivalle
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundReflective writing is widely promoted in health professions education worldwide. In France, Complex and Authentic Situation Report (CASR) is the most widely implemented reflective format during general practice residency and is almost systematically required for diploma validation. However, its perceived usefulness has rarely been investigated, despite its potential role in sustaining student motivation and engagement. This study explored trainees’ perceptions and associated factors.MethodsA cross-sectional descriptive survey was conducted online between September and November 2024 among French general practice trainees. The anonymous survey link was disseminated through French Departments of General Practice, residents’ unions, and Facebook groups of trainees. Statistical analyses combined bivariate tests with false discovery rate correction and a multivariable ordinal logistic regression model.ResultsAmong the 608 respondents, only 10.2% considered CASRs “quite” or “very useful” and 39.1% reported having invented or copied a clinical situation when completing a CASR. A smaller number of required assignments (1 to 6) was associated with higher perceived usefulness (p = 0.007). Perceived usefulness was significantly associated with competency development (p < 0.001), considering CASRs a good tool for practice exchange with peers (p = 0.008) and satisfaction from positive evaluation (p < 0.001).ConclusionsIn this study, only a minority of trainees perceived CASRs as useful. Low levels of trainee motivation and reports of invented or copied clinical situations suggest limited adherence to the exercise. Perceived usefulness was associated with contextual factors that may be modifiable within training programs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09131-9.

Introduction

Reflective writing assignments have progressively become established in both undergraduate and postgraduate training of healthcare professionals across disciplines [1]. Serving as a bridge between theory and practice, they encourage reflective processes by prompting learners to question their professional practices and challenge existing certainties. They provide opportunities to identify gaps in knowledge and competencies after clinical encounters and to seek strategies to address them. Through this process, both trainees and experienced professionals “write their own story” and develop through it. Reflective writing has therefore been demonstrated to contribute to professional identity formation, competency acquisition, and the development of empathy [2–6].

Nevertheless, adherence to reflective writing varies considerably depending on the type of support provided, the intended use, and the context in which these assignments are undertaken. In the United Kingdom, for example, reflective writing linked to high-stakes assessment or certification has, in some cases, been reported to discourage rather than engage trainees [7, 8].

The literature describes multiple forms of reflective writing in medical education, including narrative essays, reflective journals, internship logs, short clinical vignettes, peer-group case narratives, and longitudinal portfolio documentation, among others [1–6, 9].

In France, reflective writing was formally integrated into residency training (third-cycle medical education) as part of the 2017 national reform, which leads to the national diploma of Doctor of Medicine. In general practice residency, several forms of written academic work are required to populate general practice trainees (GPTs) portfolios and contribute to validation of the national Diploma of Specialized Studies (DES, from the French “Diplôme d’Études Spécialisées”) in General Practice [10, 11].

Among these formats, the Complex and Authentic Situation Report (CASR, known in French as Récit de Situation Complexe Authentique, RSCA) has become the most widely implemented format within French University Departments of General Practice (Départements de Médecine Générale, DMGs), with its production being almost systematically required as part of residency training. CASRs contribute to students’ portfolios during their four-year residency in general practice and are used for both formative purposes, through pedagogical feedback, and summative purposes, through their contribution to the final validation of the DES [10, 11].

They are intended to promote reflective practice by encouraging residents to analyze a lived clinical situation perceived as complex or challenging, identify competencies mobilized or lacking, and formulate clinical questions that are subsequently addressed through an evidence-based approach.

The CASR framework, detailed in guidance coedited by the National College of General Practice Teachers (Collège National des Généralistes Enseignants, CNGE) [12], follows a sequence conceptually aligned with experiential learning models: description of the experience, reflective analysis, integration of theoretical resources, and reapplication of learning to future comparable situations. CASRs are predominantly evaluated by designated tutors, although university-affiliated supervisors or faculty committees may also be involved. In most DMGs, this assessment is systematic and predominantly qualitative, though quantitative elements may sometimes be incorporated [11]. It most frequently relies on the CNGE CASR assessment grid or closely related local adaptations [9, 11]. The CNGE assessment grid focuses on narrative coherence, depth of reflection, appropriate mobilization of theoretical resources, and the transferability of learning to future clinical situations.

Previous literature has consistently demonstrated that perceived usefulness plays a key role in student engagement, enhancing persistence, performance, and investment in mandatory assignments [13–17]. However, the perceived usefulness of CASRs among GPTs in France remains underexplored. In this study, perceived usefulness was defined as the degree to which an academic task is considered important by students for achieving their goals, such as academic success or the acquisition of professional competencies in general practice.

A deeper understanding of how trainees perceive the usefulness of CASRs, and of the factors likely to influence these perceptions, appears essential to inform pedagogical adaptations and optimize educational support in postgraduate medical training.

The following research question was therefore formulated: What is the perceived usefulness of CASRs among French general practice trainees, and which factors are associated with this perception?

Methods

Study design

A cross-sectional descriptive study was conducted using a self-administered online questionnaire specifically developed for this research (Appendix 1). The survey was hosted on the Eval&Go platform (Eval&Go, Montpellier, France), an online survey solution compliant with the General Data Protection Regulation. The platform was used for questionnaire dissemination and response collection.

No directly identifiable data were requested, and respondents’ IP addresses were neither collected nor accessible to the investigator. The anonymized dataset was accessible only to the research team and was shared via a password-protected file for statistical analysis.

Questionnaire development

In the absence of a previously validated instrument specifically designed to assess the perceived usefulness of CASRs, an original questionnaire was developed by the principal investigator, a general practice teacher.

The questionnaire was not intended to develop or validate a psychometric scale but to explore contextual dimensions of perceived usefulness.

The instrument was informed by motivational theories of perceived task value (expectancy-value theory and self-determination theory) as well as by literature on reflective writing and portfolio use in medical education [17–19]. Items exploring perceived improvement in professional competencies were developed in reference to Compagnon’s competency “marguerite” [20] and the European definition of general practice from the WONCA [21]. One item addressed the biopsychosocial approach described by Engel [22], a key theoretical foundation of general practice.

The questionnaire explored four dimensions: overall perceived usefulness (primary outcome), perceived impact on clinical and professional competencies, the institutional role of CASRs in DES validation, and behavioral indicators of engagement. Perceived usefulness of CASRs was assessed globally, without distinguishing between formative and summative functions.

The questionnaire was reviewed by the second author, a general practitioner and medical educator, and by a biostatistician, who suggested minor syntactic revisions and clarifications regarding the scaling and measurement levels of the response options. It was subsequently pre-tested with three individuals outside the medical curriculum to assess clarity and comprehensibility of the items, and then with three GPTs enrolled in the General Practice residency program to evaluate the average completion time and feasibility in real training conditions.

Participants

Eligible participants were GPTs enrolled in French medical schools since 2020 at the latest. GPTs from first to sixth validated semesters were eligible. Inclusion criteria required participants to still be registered in a general practice residency in France and to have completed at least one CASR at the time of the survey. The only exclusion criterion was incomplete questionnaires.

Sample size

According to data published by the CNGE [23], 13,817 trainees were enrolled in the DES in General Practice in France. To account for annual fluctuations in enrollment, a rounded target population of 15,000 trainees was used for sample size calculation. With a 95% confidence level and a 5% margin of error, the minimum required sample size was estimated at 375 respondents.

Recruitment and data collection

An anonymous survey link was distributed between September and November 2024 through two distinct channels.

First, DMGs were identified through publicly available institutional contact information and contacted directly by the principal investigator. Twenty-three DMGs were contacted by email; seven agreed to disseminate the survey to their residents via their internal mailing lists, four declined, and the remaining departments did not respond.

Second, the survey link was posted on Facebook groups of residents’ unions and on regional Facebook groups of general practice residents.

Participation in survey dissemination was voluntary. The study relied on convenience sampling.

Outcome measure

The primary outcome was the response to the question: “Overall, how useful do you find CASRs for your general practice training?” with four possible answers: “Not useful at all,” “Not very useful,” “Quite useful,” or “Very useful.”

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using JAMOVI and R software. Qualitative variables were described as counts and percentages, and quantitative variables as means, medians, interquartile ranges, and ranges. To increase statistical power, the categories “Quite useful” and “Very useful” were combined.

Bivariate analyses were performed using the Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for qualitative variables and the Kruskal–Wallis test for quantitative variables. When necessary, Fisher’s exact test with simulated p-values (2000 replicates) was used. Variables that remained statistically significant after Benjamini–Hochberg correction and that met validity assumptions were subsequently entered into a multivariable ordinal logistic regression model.

Bivariate analyses were conducted by the principal investigator and independently checked by the biostatistician. The correction and multivariable ordinal regression model was specified and performed by the biostatistician.

Use of artificial intelligence

Artificial intelligence (ChatGPT) was used to assist with literature searches, English translation, and language editing. All statistical analyses and interpretations were performed by the authors.

Results

Sample characteristics

The questionnaire was distributed between September and November 2024. Among the 735 students who accessed the consent and eligibility page, 690 met the inclusion criteria, but only 612 continued with the survey. Four were excluded due to incompleteness, resulting in a final sample of 608 respondents.

Participants came from nearly all medical schools in France (Fig. 1).

Women represented the majority (70.6%, n = 429), followed by men (29.1%, n = 177) and other genders (0.3%, n = 2). The predominant age group was 25–29 years (87.7%, n = 533). Respondents were distributed across residency stages according to the number of completed semesters, with relatively similar proportions from two to five semesters (15–18.9%, n = 91–115), fewer having completed only one semester (7.1%, n = 43), and slightly more having completed six semesters (22.0%, n = 134).

Perceived usefulness of CASRs

Only 10.2% (n = 62) of respondents considered CASRs to be “Quite useful” or “Very useful.”

Gender was significantly associated with perceived usefulness (Fisher, p < 0.001), with men more likely to rate CASRs as “Not useful at all” or “Not very useful.” Age group was not associated (Fisher, p = 0.569).

Perceived usefulness was associated with training seniority (Fisher sim, p = 0.016). Descriptively, positive perceptions tended to be more frequent among residents in earlier semesters.

Reported use of CASRs across respondents’ faculties

Among respondents, 60.7% (n = 369) reported having received training on CASRs during residency, mostly in the first semester (94%, n = 347) and always before the fourth. Among those who had received training, 86.7% (n = 320) reported that the usefulness of CASRs had been explained. However, neither having received training (Χ², p = 0.234), nor the timing of the training (Fisher, p = 0.861), nor being explicitly told about CASRs usefulness (Χ², p = 0.164) was significantly associated with higher perceived usefulness.

Concerning the number of CASRs required by universities during residency, irrespective of whether they were used for formative purposes or for diploma validation, 65.1% of respondents (n = 396) reported being required to produce between 1 and 6 CASRs, 31.7% (n = 193) between 7 and 12, and 3.1% (n = 19) more than 12. On average, respondents had written 4.34 CASRs (median: 4; min–max: 1–12) at the time of the survey. The number already written was not significantly associated with perceived usefulness (Fisher sim, p = 0.148), unlike the number required (Fisher, p = 0.007): CASRs were perceived as more useful when the required number was at the lower end (1–6).

In addition, 39.1% (n = 238) of respondents admitted to having invented or copied a clinical situation to complete a CASR. Among trainees rating CASRs as “Not useful at all” (n = 249), 58.6% reported having invented or copied a clinical situation (n = 146), compared with lower proportions in other usefulness categories (χ², p < 0.001). Self-reported overall motivation to write CASRs was low on a 0–10 scale (mean: 2.04; median: 2; interquartile range: 3; range: 0–9), with 34.9% (n = 212) scoring 0 and only 0.2% (n = 1) scoring 9.

Usefulness of CASRs for general practice

Most respondents felt that CASRs did not improve their clinical practice. The most frequently cited improvements were in pharmacological and non-pharmacological management (43.6%, n = 265), patient follow-up (27%, n = 164), and diagnostic hypotheses (20.6%, n = 125) (Fig. 2).

Improvements consistent with a holistic approach to care were reported in social (44.7%, n = 272), psychological (38.3%, n = 233), and biological dimensions (22.2%, n = 135) (Fig. 3).

A significant association was found between CASRs being considered useful for competencies development and higher perceived usefulness (Kruskal–Wallis, p < 0.001). In multivariable ordinal regression, perceiving CASRs as contributing positively to professional competencies (OR = 1.92; p < 0.001) significantly increased the likelihood of finding them useful.

Additional uses of CASRs

Respondents generally felt that CASRs did not help them identify gaps in their practice (74.7%, n = 454), exchange practices (72.5%, n = 441), or improve the quality of their other academic writings (68.4%, n = 416).

However, all three were significantly associated with perceived usefulness (Χ², p < 0.001). In multivariable analysis, considering CASRs a good tool for practice exchange with peers doubled the probability of perceiving them as useful (OR = 2.51; p = 0.008).

Evaluation and certification

For 89.6% (n = 545), CASRs were not a reliable indicator of competency improvement. Evaluation by tutors was judged “Not useful at all” (56.9%, n = 346) or “Not very useful” (37%, n = 225) for improving general practice competencies.

A positive evaluation provided satisfaction for 52.1% of respondents (n = 317). Both satisfaction and the perception that tutors’ comments improved competencies were significantly associated with higher perceived usefulness (Χ², p < 0.001).

A large majority (88.8%, n = 540) believed CASRs should not be mandatory for diploma validation, although 83.9% (n = 510) acknowledged that they would not have completed them as seriously without this requirement. On average, respondents would have preferred to write 2.68 CASRs during their residency (median: 3; interquartile range: 4; range: 0–12).

CASRs and career plans

The vast majority of respondents (91.1%, n = 554) intended to practice as general practitioners after residency, and most considered themselves likely to be “fairly competent” at that time (75.6%, n = 419). No significant association was found between expected competency level and perceived usefulness of CASRs (Fisher, p = 0.586).

The belief that CASRs contributed to becoming better physicians was significantly associated with perceived usefulness (Χ², p < 0.001). However, for most respondents, CASRs contributed little or not at all (91.6%, n = 557).

Overall, GPTs judged CASRs as “Not very useful” (48.8%, n = 297) or “Not useful at all” (41%, n = 249) for their training, with only a minority rating them as “Quite useful” (9.4%, n = 57) or “Very useful” (0.8%, n = 5).

Discussion

Main findings

This study highlights a discrepancy between the central place that CASRs appear to occupy in many general practice training pathways and the relatively low level of usefulness perceived by GPTs.

Several factors appear to influence perceptions of CASRs usefulness. Some are non-modifiable, such as gender or stage of residency. Others, however, may be influenced by educational stakeholders (teachers, tutors, supervisors), including the quality and encouraging nature of feedback, the number of CASRs required, and their potential adaptation as better tools for practice exchange.

In addition, several potential benefits of CASRs, statistically associated with higher perceived usefulness, could be emphasized during feedback sessions. These include improvements in medical approach, competency development in general practice, identification of professional gaps, enhancement of academic writing.

In contrast, some factors appeared unrelated to perceived usefulness, including age, completion of dedicated CASR training, and the intention to practice general medicine after residency.

Comparison with the literature

CASRs are generally included within a broader portfolio of academic assignments contributing to DES validation. The multiplicity of tasks allows for a more comprehensive assessment of general practice competencies [24]. However, this may dilute the specific value of CASRs in the certification process. Since portfolios are judged as a whole, a poorly written CASR can be offset by stronger submissions in other formats, leading some trainees to prioritize other tasks to ensure diploma validation.

Low motivation to complete CASRs can be interpreted through the lens of Deci and Ryan’s self-determination theory [25, 26]. These authors demonstrated that tangible, controlling rewards (e.g., diploma validation) may paradoxically decrease intrinsic motivation. When CASRs are perceived as an imposed institutional requirement subject to external judgment, they may reduce autonomy and self-determination, thereby diminishing engagement despite their relevance for DES validation.

Conversely, positive verbal reinforcement, such as encouragement and praise, can enhance the perception of competence, a basic psychological need supporting intrinsic motivation. The quality and tone of feedback provided by evaluators thus appear crucial: rather than focusing solely on normative assessment, feedback should encourage progress, particularly in reflective competencies. The central role of educational quality and teacher–learner interactions in fostering motivation has already been documented [27].

Furthermore, the multiplicity of academic tasks, combined with demanding clinical duties, may contribute to overload—a recognized factor in burnout and disengagement [28]. Writing CASRs, which is time-consuming, competes with other priorities, particularly when mandatory study time is not respected in some clinical settings. Even when time is available, trainees may not dedicate it to CASR writing [29]. As noted in Buckley’s BEME review, “the time commitment required for portfolio development may compromise other learning or deter students from engaging, unless compelled by assessment requirements” [30]. Two temporalities therefore coexist: the institutional one prescribed by faculties, and the personal one dictated by trainees, who ultimately set the pace of portfolio production.

Another challenge relates to the conflation of formative and summative purposes. In most faculties, CASRs are intended to foster reflexivity while also contributing to evaluation and certification. This dual function, already noted as a source of tension for educators [31], may confuse students. By blurring objectives, trainees may fail to take ownership of CASRs as genuine learning tools, as has been reported elsewhere [32, 33]. Without strong engagement in reflective development, perceived usefulness remains limited. Clarifying the pedagogical objectives of CASRs and identifying factors that promote positive uptake therefore appear essential.

Interestingly, the lack of association between dedicated CASR training and perceived usefulness contrasts with findings from other educational settings. Suarez et al. argued that “when teachers explicitly present the meaning and usefulness of assignments, they may influence students’ behavioral engagement and time management” [13]. It is possible that the elements most likely to enhance the perceived usefulness of CASRs are not sufficiently emphasized or made explicit during training.

Study limitations

Limited participation from certain subdivisions may restrict the generalizability of the findings across France. In addition, the sampling approach and a modest response proportion may have introduced selection biases, further limiting representativeness.

The use of an anonymous online link may have introduced a self-selection bias, possibly overrepresenting GPTs dissatisfied with CASRs, which may have influenced the overall perception of usefulness.

Variations in the implementation, requirements, and pedagogical use of CASRs across universities may limit the comparability of responses and restrict the transferability of the findings across universities.

In most DMGs, CASRs are integrated into GPTs’ portfolios, which typically serve both formative and summative functions. For this reason, the distinction between CASRs used exclusively for formative purposes and those used exclusively for summative purposes was not explicitly examined. Exploring potential differences in perceived usefulness according to their assessment function might have provided additional insight.

Finally, because the survey was conducted during the second semester of the 2024 academic year, students who had failed to validate their first semester could not select “zero semesters completed,” and may thus have been inadvertently excluded.

Implications and perspectives

Strengthening students’ intrinsic motivation may require dedicating time to clarifying the benefits of academic assignments [34]. Allowing GPTs to define personal objectives at the outset may help them better integrate CASRs into their professional development and career trajectory.

Aligning CASRs more closely with the competencies they are most likely to improve (pharmacological and non-pharmacological management, psychosocial care, holistic approach, complexity, and patient-centered communication) could also increase their relevance. Other competencies might be better developed through alternative pedagogical methods. Offering students some choice in reflective modalities, such as simulations, peer groups, or role play, may further enhance engagement.

Furthermore, the level of difficulty of CASRs should be carefully balanced to prevent boredom from overly simple tasks or discouragement from excessively difficult ones [1, 35].

Beyond writing itself, feedback plays an important role [36–38]. As in general practice clerkships, where supervision evolves from close observation to progressive autonomy, CASRs might benefit from similarly scaffolded support. An initial phase of individualized formative feedback, focused on progress and encouragement, could increase both effectiveness and perceived usefulness. Teacher involvement is therefore essential to shaping student perceptions [34].

Conclusion

CASRs are widely implemented within French general practice residency programs, yet in this study only a minority of trainees perceived them as useful for their training. Low levels of motivation and the proportion of trainees reporting that they had invented or copied clinical situations suggest that engagement with the exercise may sometimes be limited.

Although this study does not assess the objective educational effectiveness of CASRs, reflective writing relies on active and authentic participation. When engagement is low, the educational benefits expected from such assignments may be less likely to be achieved.

The associations identified in this study indicate that perceived usefulness is associated with contextual factors, some of which may be modifiable, such as workload, feedback, and the place of CASRs within training programs.

Future studies combining quantitative and qualitative approaches could help better understand how reflective assignments are experienced by trainees and how perceived usefulness influences competency development.
