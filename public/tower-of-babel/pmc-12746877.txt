Reading the hCG tea leaves
Authors: Alexander M Quaas
Published: 2025

Informing patients of their quantitative serum human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) levels after insemination or embryo transfer treatments represents an integral part of the day-to-day contemporary assisted reproductive technology (ART) practice all over the world. Given the cruel reality that patients must face a binary outcome of “pregnant” vs. “not pregnant,” strategies to survive the dreaded “two-week wait” have been discussed and developed by patient support groups and clinical psychologists (1). But could this “rollercoaster of hope and fear” be reduced to a “one-week wait?”

The hCG is arguably the “MVP” (most valuable protein) in the field of reproductive medicine, given its vital importance from both a diagnostic and therapeutic standpoint. First discovered by Ascheim and Zondek in the first half of the 20th century as a gonad-stimulating substance present in the blood and urine of pregnant women, it was initially thought to be produced by the pituitary gland (2). Further investigation revealed that this was the case for its glycoprotein “siblings” follicle-stimulating hormone, luteinizing hormone, and thyroid-stimulating hormone, but that hCG is mainly produced in the placenta. All four siblings share the same 92 amino acid α-subunit but differ in the amino acid length of the β-subunit and the degree of glycosylation. Any detection of hCG in the urine or serum enables a qualitative dichotomous determination of pregnancy status, whereas serial quantification of hCG in the serum can assist with the diagnosis and monitoring of biochemical pregnancies, miscarriages, ectopic pregnancies, molar pregnancies, and intrauterine pregnancies destined to result in a live birth. When the outcome and the location of an early pregnancy are uncertain, serial hCG measurements are used to follow “pregnancies of unknown location.” Providers and patients have sought to interpret the magnitude of the hCG level at defined timepoints after embryo transfers or inseminations to predict pregnancy viability and the likelihood of multiple pregnancy, akin to “reading hCG tea leaves.” Given the precise knowledge of embryo transfer timing (and therefore implantation) in ART compared with spontaneous pregnancies, the diagnostic and predictive potential of singular hCG measurements is higher compared with spontaneous conceptions, in analogy to early ultrasound monitoring in spontaneous vs. ART-conceived pregnancies (3).

From a therapeutic standpoint, the likeness of hCG to its closest sibling luteinizing hormone is used to trigger the process of ovulation and final oocyte maturation in ART treatments. Different forms of hCG are produced by trophectoderm cells to “rescue” and maintain corpus luteum function and promote implantation and placentation. After implantation, the trophectoderm-derived syncytiotrophoblast is responsible for a sharp increase in hCG secretion.

In this month’s issue of F&S Reports, Kidera et al. (4) report the results of their cohort study seeking to “study the earliest timepoint at which serum hCG can predict live birth after blastocyst frozen embryo transfer (FET), and to determine the cutoff values for hCG levels on days 7–11 after frozen-thawed embryo transfer with blastocysts”. Using data from 18,491 FET cycles, the investigators found similarly high area under the curve values (>0.954) for the receiver operating characteristic analysis between serum hCG levels and the diagnosis of live birth with serum hCG determination 7, 8, 9, 10, and 11 days after blastocyst FET. Using their receiver operating characteristic curves, they established serum hCG cutoff values for days 7–11 after transfer. The identified cutoffs to predict live birth for days 7 and 8 after transfer were 31.05 and 46.6 mIU/mL, respectively.

The investigators should be commended for the comprehensive analysis of clinically relevant data using a large dataset from a 6-year period. Another strength of the study is the innovative concept of serum hCG testing at a much earlier timepoint than in standard ART practice and in prior studies. The relatively small sample size (n = 194) of patients undergoing day 7 testing, representing only 1% of the total study population, is a limitation of the study. It is unclear which patients opted to undergo earlier testing and why, subjecting the study findings to potential selection bias. The single-site nature of the study, interpreted by the investigators as a strength because of “little variation in the data due to procedures and culture methods,” limits generalizability. In addition, the study population only consisted of hormone replacement cycle FETs and “true” natural cycle FETs (NC-FET). It appears that no patients undergoing modified NC-FETs using an hCG trigger were included in the study, and therefore unclear whether the same hCG cutoffs apply to predict live birth.

How can the findings by Kidera et al. (4) change our daily clinical practice? With prediction studies, the clinical utility of predicting an outcome is a matter of debate. From a patient’s perspective, it may be reassuring to know that the chance of a live birth is high, and it may be easier to prepare for a potential negative outcome if it is communicated early that the chance of live birth is low. However, most predictions, including those made on the basis of the findings of this study, are not 100% accurate: a negative outcome could still happen after an optimistic prediction, and vice versa. No therapeutic intervention exists to alter the outcome on the basis of the prediction made. It is questionable whether early testing makes the wait after embryo transfer easier or harder: in some patients earlier and more information decreases anxiety levels; in others, it may increase it. Similarly, in the context of laboratory updates after an oocyte retrieval, some patients prefer frequent updates at every intermediate step, whereas others prefer to be left alone until definitive (euploid) blastocyst information is available to decrease the “rollercoaster” nature of the process.

To potentially implement the findings of this study into clinical practice, it would be important to ascertain that the negative predictive value of an undetectable day 7 serum hCG is high, which was not done in this study. If patients had to continue FET medications and routinely return to the clinic a second time after a negative day 7 hCG test, it would be impractical and add insult to injury for patients with unsuccessful treatment attempts.

Furthermore, given the increased use of modified NC-FETs in contemporary clinical practice, it would be vital to know if the study findings are valid when this treatment modality, including a 10,000-unit hCG trigger 7 days before transfer, is used. On the basis of the elimination half-life of hCG, most patients should have cleared all detectable trigger-attributable hCG 14 days after its use. However, it would be important to have definitive data to support this before changes in practice protocols.

In a potential prospective follow-up study to the investigation by Kidera et al. (4), patients could be randomized on the day of hCG testing to confirm the retrospective results. Additionally, the patient experience and preference of testing at different timepoints could be assessed. Interventions to ease patient anxiety and distress between embryo transfer and the first hCG test, such as empathic physician contact via phone call, have been studied, with promising results (5). Future studies could evaluate how such interventions compare with earlier testing, and whether the two approaches could improve the patient experience synergistically during the waiting period.

There is no doubt that the “hCG tea leave reading” will continue for the foreseeable future of ART practice; however, thanks to studies like the one by Kidera et al. (4), perhaps with increased sophistication.
