Cutting balloon for the treatment of type 2 spontaneous coronary artery dissection
Authors: Andreas S. Triantafyllis, Konstantinos Antoniades, Sotirios Kotoulas, Ibraheem Aqeel, Georgios Sotiris, Leonidas E. Poulimenos
Published: 2026

Spontaneous coronary artery dissection (SCAD) is an underrecognized cause of acute coronary syndrome, particularly in middle-aged women without conventional cardiovascular risk factors. Even though conservative treatment is generally advocated, revascularization during invasive coronary angiography (ICA) is reserved for cases of ongoing ischemia, hemodynamic instability, or large myocardial territory at risk [1].

We report the case of a 53-year-old woman with no prior medical history who presented to the emergency department with acute retrosternal chest pain. The electrocardiogram recorded De Winter-like ischemic changes, an anterior ST-elevation myocardial infarction equivalent, while echocardiography revealed severe anterior hypokinesis.

Urgent ICA demonstrated an abrupt, smooth reduction of the luminal diameter in the mid segment of the left anterior descending artery (LAD), with TIMI 0 flow distally (Figure 1 A, white and yellow arrows, respectively). This angiographic image corresponded to SCAD type 2B of the mid LAD, due to an intramural hematoma causing compression of the true lumen resulting in complete distal vessel occlusion.

ICA showing multiple radiolucent lines (white arrows) with full restoration of coronary flow (yellow arrows), after cutting balloon inflation, corresponding to SCAD type 1 (B). Follow-up ICA demonstrating recovery of the vessel architecture with TIMI 3 flow (white arrows) (C). Optical coherence tomography (OCT) depicting nearly complete vessel healing with the presence of just trivial residual dissection (asterisk) (D)

Taking into consideration the persistent chest pain and high-risk anatomy, we proceeded with percutaneous coronary intervention. An Asahi Sion Blue coronary guidewire was navigated safely within the true lumen up to the distal end of the vessel. A small semi-compliant balloon (0.85 × 10 mm) was advanced, to confirm correct positioning of the wire within the true lumen.

Subsequently, we used a cutting balloon (2.5 × 15 mm) to intentionally transform SCAD type 2B to type 1, in order to depressurize the intramural hematoma and restore flow while letting the newly developed SCAD type 1 heal gradually to normal vessel architecture. We inflated twice in nominal pressures (6 atm) for 10 s, with minimum contrast injections thereafter. Inflation of the cutting balloon induced controlled intimal disruption, promoting fenestration and subsequent decompression of the intramural hematoma into the true lumen. The application of a smaller cutting balloon than the reference vessel diameter minimized the risk of vessel injury while optimizing procedural efficacy [2]. Drainage of the intramural hematoma to the true lumen converted SCAD type 2B to type 1, with full restoration of coronary flow (TIMI 3) (Figure 1 B, white and yellow arrows, respectively), along with complete resolution of symptoms. Suboptimal patient cooperation precluded intracoronary imaging.

The patient remained asymptomatic and hemodynamically stable during hospitalization. Left ventricular systolic function showed partial recuperation, and she was discharged on aspirin and clopidogrel for 1 year.

At 3-month follow-up, ICA showed recovery of vessel anatomy with preserved TIMI 3 flow (Figure 1 C, white arrows). Optical coherence tomography illustrated nearly complete vessel healing with the presence of just trivial residual dissection distally, in less than one quadrant extending to 0.8 mm (Figure 1 D, asterisk). The patient remained asymptomatic, with recovery of left ventricular ejection fraction.

Acute management of SCAD should prioritize in TIMI 3 flow rather than restoring normal coronary architecture. In SCAD type 2, the use of cutting balloons may represent a useful bailout strategy in carefully selected patients with ongoing ischemia and flow-limiting intramural hematoma when conservative management is not appropriate.
