Europe's failure to deliver on drug-resistant tuberculosis
Authors: The Lancet Regional Health – Europe
Published: 2026

Europe accounts for a comparatively low share of the global tuberculosis burden, yet the proportion of newly diagnosed cases with multidrug-resistant (MDR) or rifampicin-resistant (RR; a surrogate for MDR) tuberculosis in the WHO European Region is seven times the global average. This is Europe's tuberculosis paradox: some of the world's wealthiest and most advanced health systems are failing to implement known tools to tackle drug-resistant tuberculosis.

Drug-resistant tuberculosis is more difficult to diagnose and treat, with a treatment success rate of 77·0% for people with MDR/RR-tuberculosis and only 40·2% for those with extensively drug-resistant tuberculosis in the WHO European Region. Every failure along the care pathway, from delayed diagnosis and incomplete susceptibility testing to interrupted treatment and inadequate follow-up, creates an opportunity for resistant strains to persist and spread.

One in five tuberculosis cases in the WHO European Region are not diagnosed or not reported. Although the burden of known cases is concentrated in Eastern Europe and Central Asia, limited clinical familiarity can delay recognition in low-incidence settings, which increases the risk of transmission (including resistant strains). Closing the diagnostic gap and tackling drug resistance are inseparable: once tuberculosis is detected, rapid drug-susceptibility testing is essential to ensure an effective regimen and prevent selection for resistance.

Yet, 2 years after WHO recommended a new, all oral 6-month regimen of bedaquiline, pretomanid, linezolid, and moxifloxacin (BPaLM) for people with MDR/RR-tuberculosis, only half of countries surveyed in the region had access to all drugs in the regimen and the relevant drug-susceptibility testing. BPaLM rollout should therefore be accompanied by universal access to the relevant drug-susceptibility testing.

Europe's low tuberculosis incidence has created a small and fragmented market for essential medicines, making some of the most effective treatments difficult to procure. In parts of western and northern Europe, supplying these drugs is commercially unattractive. Purchasing medications in smaller quantities reduces countries' negotiating power and makes procurement costly and complex. In many countries in the EU/European Economic Area (EEA), the BPaLM regimen can cost over €40,000, whereas the same regimen can be obtained for less than $300 in low-income and middle-income countries through the Global Drug Facility (ie, an international procurement mechanism for affordable tuberculosis treatments in low-income and high-burden settings), including within the region.

High costs and supply interruptions can delay treatment or force clinicians to use longer, more toxic, and less effective regimens, increasing the risk of discontinuation and loss to follow-up. WHO calls to scale-up the BPaLM regimen should therefore be accompanied by mechanisms for coordinated procurement and strengthened regional supply chains. Access to effective treatment should not depend on the commercial attractiveness of a national market.

These failures of access become particularly stark during humanitarian crises and in the context of migration. Overcrowded living conditions along the migration pathway, interrupted access to diagnosis and treatment, and loss of medical records can all facilitate tuberculosis transmission. The war in Ukraine exposed how unprepared many European health systems are for dealing with drug-resistant tuberculosis. Within 2 years of the invasion in Ukraine, the proportion of foreign-born individuals with MDR/RR-tuberculosis in the EU/EEA originating from Ukraine increased from 16% to 50%. Displaced patients were not always able to continue the same treatment, and fear of persecution and stigma can prevent people from seeking care.

European health systems need to adapt to the reality that patients move across borders, guaranteeing care regardless of migration status, creating transferrable clinical records, and maintaining access to the most appropriate medicines. Continuity of care is an ethical obligation and a prerequisite for controlling resistance. In tuberculosis care, a border must never become a break in treatment, where patients are failed and the effectiveness of vital medicines is undermined.

Drug-resistant tuberculosis must be central to Europe's antimicrobial resistance (AMR) response, yet national tuberculosis and AMR plans often remain disconnected. As Thomas Theo Brehm and colleagues argue in this issue, new treatments will succeed only if matched by effective antimicrobial stewardship. Europe has the tools and resources to tackle tuberculosis but does not possess the political will and systems to deliver them consistently. National AMR plans should include explicit and adequately funded commitments to drug-resistant tuberculosis, ensuring that effective treatments reach every patient, across every border. Ending tuberculosis depends on implementing existing tools rapidly, consistently, and at scale, while protecting the medicines on which the world depends.
