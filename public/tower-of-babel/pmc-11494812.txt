Measurement properties of the Brazilian-Portuguese version of the PROMIS-29 questionnaire and the Impact Score in patients with chronic low back pain
Authors: Claudia Regina Moya, Tatiane da Silva, Leonardo Oliveira Pena Costa
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundA task force from the US National Institute of Health aimed to establish parameters for surveys with patients with chronic low back pain (LBP) by recommending the use of the Patient-Reported Outcome Measurement Information System 29-item (PROMIS-29) questionnaire and the stratification according to the Impact Score. We aimed to assess the internal consistency, reliability, measurement error, construct validity and responsiveness of the Brazilian-Portuguese version of the PROMIS-29 questionnaire (version 2.1) and the Impact Score in patients with chronic LBP.MethodsA prospective cohort study was carried out with patients with chronic LBP who were receiving physiotherapy treatment in clinics in São Paulo and Vitória cities, Brazil. The sample included 102 patients with chronic LBP, aged between 18 and 80 years, and with the ability to read and write in the Brazilian-Portuguese language. Exclusion criteria were patients with serious spinal conditions, cognitive impairment, pregnant women, and patients who had undergone lumbar surgery within the past six months. The PROMIS-29 questionnaire, the 12-item Short-Form Health Survey and the Roland-Morris Disability Questionnaire were applied. The PROMIS-29 questionnaire was reapplied after 24 hours and 4 weeks.ResultsThe PROMIS-29 questionnaire showed Cronbach’s Alpha values between 0.81 (anxiety) and 0.91 (depression). The ICC2.1 ranged from 0.85 (95% CI: 0.77 to 0.89) to 0,97 (CI 95%: 0.95 to 0.98). The SEM values ranged between 0.85 (physical function) and 2.62 (ability to participate in social roles and activities). The Impact Score showed Cronbach’s Alpha, ICC2.1 and SEM values of 0.91, 0.97 (95% CI: 0.95 to 0.98) and 0.56, respectively. The PROMIS-29 questionnaire and the Impact Score showed sufficient construct validity. The PROMIS-29 questionnaire showed moderate responsiveness, while the Impact Score showed sufficient responsiveness.ConclusionsBoth the Brazilian-Portuguese version of the PROMIS-29 questionnaire and the Impact Score showed sufficient internal consistency, reliability, construct validity and responsiveness in Brazilian patients with chronic LBP. The results support the US National Institutes of Health’s recommendation to use the PROMIS-29 questionnaire and the Impact Score in patients with chronic LBP. We suggest that future studies evaluate the structural validity of the Brazilian-Portuguese version of the PROMIS-29 questionnaire.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Measurement properties, PROMIS-29, Impact score, Chronic low back pain

Background

Patient-reported outcome measures are widely used in low back pain (LBP) clinical trials [1]. The most recent core outcome set for clinical trials in non-specific LBP concluded that the constructs of physical function, pain intensity and health-related quality of life should be measured [2]. However, a study by the Initiative on Methods, Measurement, and Pain Assessment in Clinical Trials (IMMPACT) identified that the main clinical outcomes from the perspective of people who experience chronic pain are the enjoyment of life, emotional well-being, fatigue, weakness, sleep-related problems, besides pain reduction [3]. Therefore, it is recommended to measure constructs related to psychological and social factors, in addition to pain intensity and disability in patients with chronic LBP [4].

The US National Institute of Health (NIH) established the NIH Task Force on Research Standards for Low Back Pain (NIH Task Force), which is an initiative aiming to standardize studies on patients with chronic LBP [5]. The NIH Task Force recommended the Patient-Reported Outcome Measurement Information System 29-item (PROMIS-29) [6] questionnaire when researchers want to measure multiple constructs in clinical trials with patients with chronic LBP [5]. The PROMIS-29 questionnaire is a short, fixed-item questionnaire that assesses the main constructs in chronic health conditions: physical function, depression, anxiety, fatigue, sleep disturbance, ability to participate in social roles and activities, pain interference and pain intensity [6].

The NIH Task Force also recommended stratifying patients with chronic LBP according to the Impact Score due to the difficulty in classifying these patients according to the pathoanatomic and pathophysiology causes of LBP [5]. The Impact Score is composed of the constructs of physical function, pain interference and pain intensity and is calculated through nine items from the PROMIS-29 questionnaire [5].

The measurement properties of the PROMIS-29 questionnaire were assessed in different health conditions and populations [7–9]. The item banks of the constructs of physical function, depression, anxiety, fatigue, sleep disturbance, ability to participate in social roles and activities, pain interference and pain intensity from PROMIS initiative have already been translated, cross-culturally adapted to the Brazilian-Portuguese language and had their measurement properties tested in general Brazilian population [10–20]. However, the measurement properties of the short versions of the constructs of the PROMIS-29 questionnaire have not yet been assessed in Brazilian patients. In addition, the measurement properties of the Impact Score have not been widely tested in patients with chronic LBP [7].

We aimed to evaluate the measurement properties of the Brazilian-Portuguese version of the PROMIS-29 questionnaire (version 2.1) and the Impact Score in Brazilian patients with chronic LBP. Therefore, reliability (reliability, internal consistency and measurement error), construct validity and responsiveness of the PROMIS-29 questionnaire and the Impact Score were assessed.

Methods

Study design

This is a prospective cohort study with patients with chronic LBP. We defined LBP as pain between the lower margin of the twelfth rib and the lower gluteal folds (with or without pain radiating to one or both legs) and which limits usual activities or changes the daily routine for more than one day [21].

The study was approved by the research ethics committee of the Universidade Cidade de São Paulo (CAAE: 25194119.2.0000.0064).

We used the Consensus-based Standards for the Selection of Health Measurement Instruments (COSMIN) Study Design checklist for Patient-reported outcome measurement instruments [22] and the COSMIN reporting guideline for studies on measurement properties of patient-reported outcome measures [23] to ensure that the measurement properties of the PROMIS-29 questionnaire and Impact Score were assessed and reported appropriately.

Participants

Between December 2019 and July 2021, patients with chronic LBP undergoing physiotherapy treatment at five clinics in the cities of São Paulo and Vitória were invited to participate in the study. Each patient was receiving individualized/pragmatic treatment according to their specific needs.

The convenience sample was composed of patients with chronic LBP (i.e., duration of pain of three months or more), aged between 18 and 80 years, with or without referred leg pain and with the ability to read and write in the Brazilian-Portuguese language. We excluded patients with serious spinal pathologies (e.g., cauda equina syndrome, lumbar spine fracture, malignancy, infectious process), cognitive impairment, pregnant women and patients who had undergone lumbar surgery within the past six months.

The sample size was determined according to the recommendations of COSMIN [24], which considers it ideal to test the measurement proprieties of a patient-reported outcome measure in at least 100 patients.

Procedures

Patients who agreed to participate were notified about the study procedures and the Informed Consent form. Next, they were invited to respond to a demographic and anthropometric questionnaire, the Brazilian-Portuguese versions of the 12-item Short-Form Health Survey (SF-12 – version 2), the Roland-Morris Disability Questionnaire (RMDQ) and the PROMIS-29 questionnaire (version 2.1), through an in-person interview or telephone call. Patients also responded to the PROMIS-29 questionnaire by telephone after 24 hours and four weeks. The 24-hour interval was chosen to assess reliability to ensure that patients would remain clinically stable. Previous research on the clinical course of chronic low back pain indicates that patients generally remain stable within this timeframe, independent of treatment interventions [25]. Furthermore, the 24-hour interval has been used in several studies evaluating the reliability of Patient-Reported Outcomes Measures in patients with LBP [26–28]. The four-week interval was chosen to assess responsiveness for two reasons: first, there is evidence from an inception cohort study showing that patients with chronic LBP improve over time [29]. This was confirmed by a high-quality systematic review with meta-analysis of prospective studies which showed an initially favorable clinical course of chronic LBP, with patients substantially improving within six weeks [25]. Second, patients in our study were receiving physiotherapy treatment and were likely to improve not only due to intervention effects, but also due to placebo, natural history, regression to the mean and Hawthorne effects [30].

Measures of interest

The PROMIS-29 questionnaire

The PROMIS-29 questionnaire assesses eight relevant constructs in chronic health conditions: physical function, depression, anxiety, fatigue, sleep disturbance, ability to participate in social roles and activities, pain interference (i.e. the extent to which pain disrupts or limits an individual’s physical, mental, and social functioning [31]) and pain intensity [6]. The constructs of the PROMIS-29 questionnaire are measured by four items, each with five response options ranging from 1 (less of the construct) to 5 (more of the construct). The exception is the construct of pain intensity, which is measured by a single item with eleven response options, ranging from 0 (no pain) to 10 (worst pain imaginable) [32]. Each construct of the PROMIS-29 questionnaire is scored using the T-score, in which a score of 50 represents the US general population mean with a standard deviation of 10. High T-score values represent more of the construct being measured. Therefore, for the constructs with a negative meaning (depression, anxiety, fatigue, sleep disturbance and pain interference), T-score values above 50 indicate worse than the population mean, while T-score values below 50 indicate better than the population mean. In the other hand, for the constructs with a positive meaning (physical function and ability to participate in social roles and activities), T-score values above 50 indicate better than the population mean, and T-score values below 50 indicate worse than the population mean. Only the construct of pain intensity does not use the T-score and is measured on a 0–10 scale [33].

The impact score

The Impact Score is calculated by the sum of the nine items of the constructs of physical function, pain interference and pain intensity from the PROMIS-29 questionnaire [5]. In the Impact Score, the score of the construct of physical function is inverted: in the PROMIS-29 questionnaire, 1 indicates greater severity and 5 indicates lower severity, while in the Impact Score, 1 means “least severe” and 5 means “most severe” [5]. The single item of the construct of pain intensity is measured on a 0–10 scale, where 0 means no pain and 10 means the worst imaginable pain [5]. Therefore, the Impact Score ranges from 8 (least impact) to 50 (greatest impact) [5]. Although the NIH Task Force stratified the Impact Score into mild, moderate, and severe, they recognized the cutoff points as arbitrary. Consequently, they recommended reporting the actual scores directly, allowing researchers the flexibility to apply additional categorizations [5].

Reference measures

The Roland-Morris Disability Questionnaire (RMDQ)

The RMDQ assesses disability associated with low back pain through 24 items, which include: 15 items on physical activity; 3 about sleep; 2 on psychosocial characteristics; 2 on household chores, 1 on food and 1 on pain frequency [34]. The RMDQ items correspond to phrases that patients report when they experience low back pain. Patients are instructed to answer “yes” if the sentence describes how they are feeling in the day or “no” if the sentence does not describe how they feel [35]. The score is obtained by adding up the items answered with “yes” and, therefore, varies between 0 (no disability) and 24 (maximum disability) [34].

The measurement properties of the Brazilian-Portuguese version of the RMDQ have been tested in patients with LBP (Cronbach’s Alpha of 0.92 and ICC2.1 of 0.95 [95% CI: 0.93 to 0.97]) [26].

The 12-item short-form health survey (SF-12)

The SF-12 questionnaire assesses health-related quality of life and was generated from the SF-36 questionnaire [36]. The SF-12 questionnaire has 12 items that assess eight constructs: physical functioning, role physical, bodily pain, general health, vitality, social functioning, role emotional and mental health [37]. The constructs of physical functioning, role physical, role emotional and mental health are scored using 2 items. In the other hand, the score of the constructs of bodily pain, general health, vitality and social functioning is obtained through 1 item [38]. The SF-12 questionnaire provides one score for physical health (Physical Component Summary) and one for mental health (Mental Component Summary). The score for each construct of the SF-12 questionnaire, for the Physical Component Summary and for the Mental Component Summary is obtained through an algorithm and varies between 0 (lowest quality of life) and 100 (highest quality of life) [38].

The measurement properties of the SF-12 questionnaire have been assessed in patients with LBP (Cronbach’s Alpha Coefficient for Physical Component Summary of 0.77 and for Mental Component Summary of 0.80) [39].

Data analysis

Statistical analyzes were performed using the IBM Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS), version 23. Descriptive statistics were used for the demographic and anthropometric data. When patients failed to respond to the 24-hour or the four-week follow-up, their data were excluded from the reliability and responsiveness analysis, respectively (complete case analysis) [40].

Reliability

The internal consistency of the PROMIS-29 questionnaire and the Impact Score was assessed through Cronbach’s alpha, in which values between 0.70 and 0.95 were considered sufficient [41]. The reliability of the PROMIS-29 questionnaire and the Impact Score was assessed through the type 2.1 Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC2.1), in which values above 0.70 were considered sufficient [41]. The ICC2.1 was calculated through the formula: (MSB- MSE)/(MSB + (k-1)*MSE + k*(MSR-MSE)/n), where MSB is the mean square between subjects, MSE is the mean square error, MSR is the mean square between raters, k is the number of rates and n is the number of subjects [42]. The measurement error was evaluated using the Standard Error of the Measurement agreement (SEM) and was calculated by multiplying the standard deviation of the mean difference and the square root of 1 minus the ICC2.1 (SD √1-ICC2.1) [43]. The SEM values were converted into the Smallest Detectable Change at 95% confidence (SDC), using the formula SDC = 1.96 x √2 x SEM [43].

Construct validity

The construct validity of the PROMIS-29 questionnaire was assessed through hypothesis testing. It was hypothesized that the most similar constructs between the PROMIS-29 questionnaire and the SF-12 would show a high correlation (|r| ≥ 0.7), and that less similar constructs would show a moderate correlation (0.5 ≤ |r| < 0.7) [44]. These hypotheses were based on a previous study that assessed the measurement proprieties of the PROMIS-29 questionnaire in patient with rheumatological conditions and correlated the constructs of the PROMIS-29 questionnaire and the constructs of the SF-36 questionnaire – the long version of the SF-12 [8]. The construct validity of the Impact Score was assessed through the correlation with the RMDQ score. It was hypothesized that the Impact Score would show a high correlation with the RMDQ. When 75% of the hypotheses were confirmed, the construct validity was considered sufficient; when 75 to 50% of the hypotheses were confirmed, the construct validity was considered moderate and, when less than 50% of the hypotheses were confirmed, the construct validity was considered poor [45].

Responsiveness

The responsiveness of the PROMIS-29 questionnaire was also assessed through hypothesis testing. It was hypothesized that the PROMIS-29 questionnaire and the Impact Score would show better scores after four weeks [41]. The comparison between the mean scores of the constructs of the PROMIS-29 questionnaire and the Impact Score at baseline and at four-week follow-up was performed using the Paired Sample t-Test, with a level of significance of α = 0.05 [46]. When 75% of the hypotheses were confirmed, responsiveness was considered sufficient; when 75 to 50% of the hypotheses were confirmed, responsiveness was considered moderate, and when less than 50% of the hypotheses were confirmed, responsiveness was considered poor [45].

Results

Deviations from the original research protocol

Between December 2019 and July 2021, 106 patients with chronic LBP were invited to participate in the study. Due to the outbreak of the coronavirus pandemics, data collection was conducted only through telephone calls to maintain social distance.

Participants

Of the 106 patients with LBP invited to participate in the study, one refused to participate due to lack of time (0.9%), two were excluded because they had lumbar fracture (2%) and one because of having cognitive impairment due to schizophrenia according to family members (0.9%). Therefore, the sample was composed of 102 patients, of which 101 responded to the 24-hour follow-up (99%) and 100 responded to the four-week follow-up (98%). The patient who did not respond to the 4-week follow-up was hospitalized, while the patient who did not respond to the 24-hour and 4-week follow-up lost interest in the study. To assess internal consistency and construct validity, a sample of 102 patients was used; for reliability and measurement error, 101 patients were included; and to assess responsiveness, the sample consisted of 100 patients.

Of the 102 patients in the study, 68 (66.7%) were women, with a mean age of 52.54 (SD 14.4) years old, a mean weight of 74.97 (SD 14.3) kg and a mean height of 1.66 (SD 0.9) m. Most of the patients had LBP for more than 5 years (43.1%), had leg pain due to LBP (79.4%), were not taking medication due to LBP (59.8%) and did not undergo lumbar surgery (90.2%).

The construct of anxiety of the PROMIS-29 questionnaire showed the worst score at baseline (62.7 [SD 7.9], while the construct of fatigue showed the best score (52.4 [SD 8.5]), as shown in Table 1.

Discussion

Main findings

The Brazilian-Portuguese version of the PROMIS-29 questionnaire and the Impact Score showed sufficient values of internal consistency, reliability and construct validity. The Brazilian-Portuguese version of the PROMIS-29 questionnaire showed moderate responsiveness, and the Impact Score showed sufficient responsiveness.

Comparison with previous studies

A previous study that assessed the measurement proprieties of the Thai version of the PROMIS-29 questionnaire in patients with chronic LBP found Cronbach’s Alpha values between 0.84 (sleep disturbance) and 0.94 (pain interference), ICC2.1 values between 0.57 (CI: 0.46 to 0.67) and 0.76 (CI: 0.66 to 0.83), and SEM values between 1.05 (pain intensity) and 5.38 (anxiety) [9]. The current study showed higher ICC2.1 values for all constructs of the PROMIS-29 questionnaire, that may be explained by the higher between-subjects T-scores differences, as per the magnitude of the ICC2.1 is influenced by the sample variability [43]. The study with Thai patients with chronic LBP found smaller between-subjects T-scores differences for almost all constructs of the PROMIS-29 questionnaire: physical function [43.6 (7.2)], depression [48.9 (8.7)], fatigue [51.2 (7.8)], sleep disturbance [48.8 (7.7)], ability to participate in social roles and activities [51.9 (7.7)] and pain interference [57.3 (6.2)]. The only construct that showed a higher between-subjects T-score difference was anxiety [57.0 (9.3)] [9]. Therefore, in the current study, the standard deviation (SD) of the T-scores values of the constructs of the PROMIS-29 questionnaire ranged from 7.2 (pain interference) to 9,9 (depression), whereas the study with the Thai sample showed SD values between 6.2 (pain interference) and 9.3 (anxiety) [9].

The hypotheses for the construct validity analysis were based on a study that evaluated the measurement properties of the PROMIS-29 questionnaire in patients with rheumatological conditions [8] and correlated the constructs of the PROMIS-29 questionnaire with the constructs of the SF-36 questionnaire – the long version of the SF-12. We hypothesized that more similar constructs would show a high linear correlation and that less similar constructs would show a moderate linear correlation. We correlated the construct of depression of the PROMIS-29 questionnaire with the construct of role emotional of the SF-12 questionnaire, which are not very similar. However, we found a moderate negative linear correlation (r = -0.55), similar to what was found in the study with patients with rheumatoid arthritis, osteoarthritis and systemic lupus erythematosus (-0.57, -0.52 and − 0.62, respectively) [8]. We correlated the construct of sleep disturbance of the PROMIS-29 questionnaire with the construct of general health of the SF-12 questionnaire, while the study with patients with rheumatological conditions correlated the construct of sleep disturbance with a similar measurement instrument. However, we chose not to add another measure and extend the assessment time.

The Brazilian-Portuguese version of the PROMIS-29 questionnaire showed moderate responsiveness in Brazilian patients with chronic LBP at the four-week follow-up. Similarly, a study with American older adults with chronic musculoskeletal pain showed sufficient responsiveness for most of the constructs of the PROMIS-29 questionnaire at the three-week follow-up [7]. Both studies showed sufficient responsiveness for the Impact Score [7].

Meaning of the study and future research

The Brazilian-Portuguese version of the PROMIS-29 questionnaire may be an advantageous patient-reported outcome measure when clinicians and researchers wish to measure multiple constructs in patients with chronic LBP. The PROMIS-29 questionnaire is a short-form that assesses the main constructs in chronic health conditions. In addition, it is possible to classify patients according to the Impact Score using the PROMIS-29 questionnaire, as recommended by the NIH Task Force. We suggest that future studies evaluate the measurement properties of the PROMIS-29 questionnaire and Impact Score in patients with acute LBP. Although the NIH Task Force has recommended it for patients with chronic LBP, the PROMIS-29 questionnaire and the Impact Score may also be useful for patients with acute LBP. Furthermore, we suggest that future studies evaluate the structural validity of the Brazilian-Portuguese version of the PROMIS-29 questionnaire. Although this study did not address this measurement property, structural validity is essential for ensuring the questionnaire’s internal consistency. Additionally, we suggest that future studies assess the measurement properties of the Brazilian-Portuguese version of the PROMIS-29 questionnaire using a more diverse sample from different regions of Brazil.

Strengths and weaknesses

This was the first study that assessed the measurement properties of the Brazilian-Portuguese version of the PROMIS-29 questionnaire and the Impact Score. Although the item bank of the constructs of physical function, depression, anxiety, fatigue, sleep disturbance, ability to participate in social roles and activities, pain interference and pain intensity have already been translated and cross-culturally adapted to Brazilian-Portuguese [10–20], the measurement properties of the short versions of the PROMIS-29 constructs had not yet been tested. In addition, there was limited evidence on the measurement properties of the PROMIS-29 questionnaire and Impact Score in patients with chronic LBP. Our study found that the Brazilian-Portuguese version of the PROMIS-29 questionnaire and the Impact score have sufficient measurement proprieties and may be useful for clinicians and researchers who work with patients with chronic LBP.

The study has some limitations. First, the sample was composed of patients from only two Brazilian cities (São Paulo and Vitoria), restricting the generalization of the results to the entire Brazilian population. Second, the T-scores of the PROMIS-29 questionnaire were calculated based on the values of the general US population and, therefore, there may be some differences for the Brazilian population. Third, the patients’ cognitive function was evaluated only subjectively, according to the examiner’s judgment, since it was decided not to add another measurement instrument (for example, the Mini-Mental State Examination [47]). Fourth, we used a short interval to assess the reliability of the PROMIS-29 questionnaire and the Impact Score (24-hour interval), which may have inflated the results. However, if we had gone beyond 24 hours, we would have taken the risk of patients changing their clinical status, since they were receiving physiotherapy treatment. Moreover, the 24-hour interval has been used in several studies [26–28] evaluating the reliability of patient-reported outcomes measures in patients with LBP. Finally, we did not assess the structural validity of the constructs of the Brazilian-Portuguese version of the PROMIS-29 questionnaire. Nevertheless, the unidimensionality of the constructs of the PROMIS-29 questionnaire has been demonstrated in previous studies. For instance, one study comparing the performance of the PROMIS-29 questionnaire across general population samples from the United Kingdom, France, and Germany found that the invariance was maintained across different languages using multi-group confirmatory factor analysis [48].

Conclusion

The Brazilian-Portuguese version of the PROMIS-29 questionnaire (version 2.1) and the Impact Score showed sufficient internal consistency, reliability, construct validity and responsiveness in Brazilian patients with chronic LBP. The results support the NIH Task Force recommendation to use the PROMIS 29 questionnaire and the Impact Score in patients with chronic LBP.
