The Complexity of Thermalization in Finite Quantum Systems
Dhruv Devulapalli, T. C. Mooney, James D. Watson

Abstract
Thermalization is the process through which a physical system evolves toward a state of thermal equilibrium. Determining whether or not a physical system will thermalize from an initial state has been a key question in condensed matter physics. Closely related questions are determining whether observables in these systems relax to stationary values, and what those values are. Using tools from computational complexity theory, we demonstrate that given a Hamiltonian on a finite-sized system, determining whether or not it thermalizes or relaxes to a given stationary value is computationally intractable, even for a quantum computer. In particular, we show that the problem of determining whether an observable of a finite-sized quantum system relaxes to a given value is 𝖯𝖲𝖯𝖠𝖢𝖤 \mathsf{PSPACE} -complete, and so no efficient algorithm for determining the value is expected to exist. Further, we show the existence of Hamiltonians for which the problem of determining whether the system thermalizes is contained in 𝖯𝖲𝖯𝖠𝖢𝖤 \mathsf{PSPACE} and is 𝖯𝖲𝖯𝖠𝖢𝖤 \mathsf{PSPACE} -hard under quantum polynomial time reductions. We also present evidence that determining thermalization is 𝖯𝖲𝖯𝖠𝖢𝖤 \mathsf{PSPACE} -hard under classical polynomial time reductions and state a physically reasonable conjecture under which it is 𝖯𝖲𝖯𝖠𝖢𝖤 \mathsf{PSPACE} -complete. In light of recent results demonstrating undecidability of thermalization in the thermodynamic limit, our work shows that the intractability of the problem is due to inherent difficulties in many-body physics rather than particularities of infinite systems.

Introduction

Statistical mechanics has been remarkably successful at describing the physics of large numbers of particles in thermal equilibrium. Experimental outcomes of systems in thermal equilibrium can be predicted using statistical ensembles based on macroscopic observables, independently of the system’s microstate. Therefore, the process through which an isolated system evolves toward thermal equilibrium, known as thermalization, has been a central topic of investigation in condensed matter physics.
In classical systems, the emergence of thermalization has been attributed to ergodicity and chaotic dynamics
[
1
]
. Ergodic systems evolve such that they eventually come arbitrarily close to every point in phase space on a constant energy surface.
As classical measurements can be modeled as long-time averages, the long-time behavior of ergodic systems is hence similar to that of a uniform distribution over the constant-energy submanifold of phase space, also known as the classical microcanonical ensemble
[
2
,
3
]
. The true relationship between thermalization and ergodicity in classical systems is subtle and nuanced. Ergodicity is difficult to prove and has only been rigorously shown for some systems
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
]
, notably for systems with suitably strong chaotic behavior
[
8
,
9
]
. Indeed, it has been argued that ergodicity is neither necessary nor sufficient for thermalization
[
10
]
. Nevertheless, ergodicity has remained a useful conceptual tool to understand thermalization in classical systems.

However, the ergodic explanation of thermalization can’t be naturally extended to isolated quantum systems. In particular, unitary time evolution does not sample all wavefunctions within a given energy range of the starting state, and hence cannot be ergodic. As an alternative, the Eigenstate Thermalization Hypothesis (ETH)
[
11
,
12
,
1
]
has been proposed to explain the exhibition of thermalization in isolated quantum systems. ETH proposes that observables with slowly varying diagonal elements (in the energy eigenbasis) and small off-diagonal elements approach their microcanonical thermal values. However, the ETH has not been proven to be generally applicable. Indeed, systems exist that do not thermalize, due to phenomena such as many-body localization
[
13
]
, quantum many-body scars
[
14
]
, and integrability
[
1
,
15
]
(although one can consider integrable systems as equilibrating to generalized Gibbs ensembles that take their additional conservation laws into account
[
16
,
17
]
).

A large volume of theoretical and experimental work has gone into determining which properties and initial states ensure thermalization
[
18
,
19
,
20
,
21
,
22
]
.
A natural question thus arises: How can we determine whether a given quantum system thermalizes?
While this question has been studied by physicists, it was first considered from the viewpoint of computational complexity theory by
[
23
]
, who proved that even for product initial states with translationally invariant Hamiltonians, the problem of determining whether a system thermalizes or not is undecidable in the limit of an infinitely large system.
Their results utilize techniques inspired by previous work in the field of Hamiltonian complexity proving complexity and computability results in physics
[
24
,
25
]
.

However, in the lab — and nature more generally — we only have access to physical systems of finite size. This raises the question of how difficult it is to predict thermalization of these finite-sized systems.
While the result of
[
23
]
proving undecidability in the thermodynamic limit, along with the more general hardness of simulating quantum systems, suggests this may be a difficult task, it is entirely possible that there exists an efficient algorithm for it. The result of
[
23
]
makes use of a reduction from an undecidable problem (the Halting problem) to the problem of determining whether a system thermalizes, but undecidable instances may require a large (unbounded) size and therefore may not be seen in systems of finite size such as those encountered in nature and the lab.

In this work we consider the problem of determining whether a
finite-sized

One relevant state of interest that describes the thermal behavior of an isolated system is the microcanonical ensemble, which represents the set of possible states within a fixed range of energy of our initial state.

Definition 1.2

(Microcanonical Ensemble)
.

For a Hamiltonian

((Informal) Finite-Size Thermalization to the microcanonical state,

\mathsf{PSPACE}

.

\mathsf{FSRelax}

, with a 1-local translationally invariant observable

.

We then show a

\mathsf{FSRelax}
to decide
\mathsf{FSTherm(MC)}
in
\mathsf{PSPACE}

\mathsf{PSPACE}

such that

is the microcanonical ensemble of

. Therefore, we have:

Theorem 1.6
.

There exists a translationally invariant, nearest neighbour Hamiltonian and 1-local, translationally invariant observable such for which

\mathsf{FSTherm(MC)}
is
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-hard under quantum polynomial time reductions. In addition, for any such Hamiltonian and observable,

\mathsf{PSPACE}

. Further details on this restriction and the possibility of removing it in future work are discussed in
Section

6.2

Section

In the case of thermalization to the Gibbs state, we use similar techniques to show that
\mathsf{FSTherm(Gibbs)}
is contained in
\mathsf{PSPACE}

Theorem 1.7
.

\mathsf{FSTherm(Gibbs)}\in\mathsf{PSPACE}
.

We further present evidence and a physically justified conjecture that
\mathsf{FSTherm(Gibbs)}
is
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-hard. Assuming our conjecture, we may obtain

-hardness for

Finally, we show that the experimentally relevant problem of determining whether finite-sized systems of qubits thermalize in polynomial time is
\mathsf{BQP}
-complete.

Our results show evidence for the difficulty of understanding the mechanisms of relaxation and thermalization, and it’s intractability from a complexity theoretic perspective even for systems in nature or the lab.

Theorem

1.5
demonstrates that we should not expect there to be an efficient way of finding (or even approximating) the time-averaged expectation value of local observables.
Furthermore,
Theorem

1.6
shows that in general any “easy-to-check” condition which ensures thermalization will not apply to all Hamiltonians.
The systems we design may be of further physical interest, since our results show that even simple systems (translationally invariant, 1D nearest-neighbour Hamiltonians and initial product states) can exhibit highly complex relaxation and thermalization dynamics.

Preliminaries

We briefly introduce classical and quantum Turing machines. A more in-depth treatment can be found in
[
26
]
and
[
27
]
. Turing machines consist of a tape with cells onto which symbols can be written, as well as a tape head, which both stores a state and points to a specific tape cell. The tape head is able to read the symbol on the cell, and based on the read symbol and the head’s state, (potentially) both write to the cell and move the head to an adjacent cell, according to a transition function. A quantum Turing machine can be defined similarly, but with a quantum tape and the ability to perform quantum operations (Toffoli gates, Hadamard gates, phase-shift gates, or single-qubit measurements in the computational basis) on the quantum tape
[
28
]
.
A promise problem is a pair
(A_{yes},A_{no})
where
A_{yes},A_{no}\subseteq\{0,1\}*
are sets of strings satisfying
A_{yes}\cap A_{no}=\varnothing

[
28
]
.
We are primarily interested in the following classes of promise problems.
The first class
\mathsf{PSPACE}
, is the class of decision problems solvable by a deterministic Turing Machine in polynomial space.
More formally:

Definition 2.1

(
\mathsf{PSPACE}
)
.

A promise problem
A=(A_{yes},A_{no})
is in
\mathsf{PSPACE}
(polynomial space) if and only if there exists a deterministic Turing machine running in polynomial space that accepts every string
x\in A_{yes}
and rejects every string
x\in A_{no}
.

\mathsf{PSPACE}
contains the commonly known classes
\mathsf{P},\mathsf{BQP},\mathsf{NP}
, and it is believed that
\mathsf{PSPACE}
is not contained in them (and thus
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-complete problems are believed to be harder to solve).
There is a corresponding class for quantum computers,
\mathsf{BQPSPACE}
, which is roughly the class of decision problems solvable by a quantum Turing Machine in polynomial space.

Definition 2.2

(
\mathsf{BQPSPACE}
,
[
28
]
)
.

A promise problem

is in

(bounded-error quantum polynomial space) if and only if there exists a quantum Turing machine running in polynomial space that accepts every string
x\in A_{yes}
with probability at least 2/3 and accepts every string
x\in A_{no}
with probability at most 1/3.

It is known that
\mathsf{BQPSPACE}=\mathsf{PSPACE}

]
.

(
\mathsf{PrQSPACE}

A promise problem

\mathsf{PrQSPACE}

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{N}\coloneqq\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}dt\bra{\psi_0}e^{iHt}\mathcal{A}_{N}e^{-iHt}\ket{\psi_0}

(11)

The problem we will be interested in is, roughly, given an initial state
\ket{\psi_0}

\mathcal{A}_{N}
does this state relax to after an infinite amount of time?
In order to study this from a complexity theoretic viewpoint, we define this as a decision problem.

Definition 2.11

((Formal) Finite Size Relaxation
\mathsf{FSRelax}
)
.

Consider a
k
-local Hamiltonian
H
acting on
N
qudits with constant local Hilbert space dimension

.
Let

Let the long time average of

be as given in

Either

. Let
\{\ket{\lambda_i}\}
be the eigenstates of

decides whether the observable
\mathcal{A}_{N}

The problem of determining whether the system thermalizes is closely related to determining relaxation, where the observable relaxes specifically to the expected value for a thermodynamic ensemble. We consider two relevant ensembles: the microcanonical ensemble (
Definition

1.2
) and the Gibbs ensemble.
The expectation value of a local observable
O
with respect to the microcanonical ensemble is:

\displaystyle\langle O\rangle_{\text{MC}}\coloneqq\traceOp[O\rho_{\textsf{MC}}(H,E)]=\frac{1}{W}\sum_{i}f\left(\frac{\lambda_{i}-E}{w}\right)\bra{\lambda_i}O\ket{\lambda_i}

(12)

where
W=\sum_{i}f\left(\frac{\lambda_{i}-E}{w}\right)
is a normalizing factor and
\{\ket{\lambda_i}\}
are the eigenstates of the relevant Hamiltonian.

In the rest of the paper, we select
f(x)=e^{-\pi x^{2}}
, such that

\rho_{\textsf{MC}}(H,E)=\frac{1}{\sum_{i}e^{-\pi\left(\frac{\lambda_{i}-E}{w}\right)^{2}}}\sum_{i}e^{-\pi\left(\frac{\lambda_{i}-E}{w}\right)^{2}}\outerproduct{\lambda_i}{\lambda_i}

(13)

By choosing a Gaussian window function, we ensure that the distribution of energies in the microcanonical ensemble is peaked at
E
and decays exponentially with distance. While in principle, the microcanonical state could be defined with a rectangular window function, this has the disadvantage of making the state sensitive to the precision of
w
in the case where there are eigenenergies close to the boundaries
\lvert E\pm w\rvert
. Previous work
[
37
]
has used projectors onto the eigenspace above or below a certain value to prepare the Gibbs states, but with the requirement that the value is sufficiently far from the spectrum of
H
. In order to avoid the requirement of a promise that the boundaries
\lvert E\pm w\rvert
are away from the spectrum of
H
and to avoid non-analyticities, we make use of a smooth window function.
For ease of notation, and since we are usually only concerned with the microcanonical ensemble at an energy corresponding to that of a single initial state, in the rest of the paper we denote
\rho_{\textsf{MC}}(E)
as
\rho_{\textsf{MC}}
.

Another state which can represent a thermalized system is the canonical ensemble, or Gibbs state, which represents the state at a fixed temperature
(1/\beta)

Definition 2.12

(Canonical Ensemble)
.

\rho_{\textsf{G}}=\frac{e^{-\beta H}}{\traceOp[e^{-\beta H}]},

When we consider thermalization to the Gibbs state,

\ket{\psi_0}

\frac{\traceOp[He^{-\beta H}]}{\traceOp[e^{-\beta H}]}=E

3
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-hardness of
\mathsf{FSRelax}

We show the
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-hardness of
\mathsf{FSRelax}
by reducing a computationally hard problem, the finite-size halting problem
\mathsf{FSHalt}
, to
\mathsf{FSRelax}
.

Definition 3.1

(Finite Size Halting (
\mathsf{FSHalt}
))
.

The problem
\mathsf{FSHalt}
takes as input a Turing machine
M,
an input
x
and an integer
n
in unary.
It outputs
YES
if
M
halts on
x
within
n
tape space, and
NO
if it does not.

By definition,
\mathsf{PSPACE}
consists of all languages for which membership can be decided by a deterministic Turing machine in polynomial space. For any language
L\in\mathsf{PSPACE}
decided by a Turing machine
M
we can define a TM
M^{\prime}
that runs
M
, and halts on a Yes instance, or runs infinitely on a No instance. The language
L
can thus be decided by
\mathsf{FSHalt}
with
M^{\prime}
, the instance
x
, and
n=\polyOp(|x|)
as input.
Further,
\mathsf{FSHalt}\in\mathsf{PSPACE}
since we can simply simulate
M
with a universal Turing machine restricted to polynomial space.
Therefore,
\mathsf{FSHalt}
is
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-complete.

We present a brief overview of our proof strategy to show that
\mathsf{FSRelax}
is
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-hard, which is closely related to the construction used by
[
23
]
. Our reduction relies on constructing a Hamiltonian that encodes the action of a Universal Reversible Turing Machine (URTM), and the input state to
\mathsf{FSRelax}
encodes the input to the URTM. We design our Hamiltonian and observable such that the long-time average of the observable depends on whether the URTM halts or not. Therefore, the relaxation value of the observable allows us to determine the solution to instances of
\mathsf{FSHalt}
, and thus
\mathsf{FSRelax}
must be as hard as
\mathsf{FSHalt}
.
Later, in
Section

6
, we show how to modify this construction to apply to
\mathsf{FSTherm(MC)}
.
We discuss the relationship between our hardness construction and that of
[
23
]
in
Section

8
.

3.1
Turing Machines and Setup

We construct a Turing machine
\mathcal{M}
composed of three TMs:

•

TM1, a 10-state, 8-symbol URTM
[
38
]
. The states of the finite control are
Q_{u}
and the symbols for the tape are
\Gamma_{u}

•

TM2, which has 1 state
r
and 2 symbols
(a_{1},a_{2})

•

TM3, which has 10-states
Q_{rev}
, and uses the same 8-symbols
\Gamma_{u}
to run TM1 in reverse.

Each site of our physical system represents one cell or the finite control of the TM tape. Our system is chosen to have periodic boundary conditions, so our tape is also periodic. Similar to
[
23
]
, we denote the cells with symbols from TM1 and TM3 as
M
-cells, and the cells with symbols of TM2 as
A
-cells.

Our TM
\mathcal{M}
functions as follows.
Assume in the initial state we fix a constant
\alpha
as the fraction of cells which are to be
M
-cells, and we evenly distribute
M
and
A
cells.
The initial state has the input to TM1 directly on the
M
cells. Every
A
cell starts with the symbol
a_{1}
. TM1 commences on the M cells, and when it encounters an
A
cell it simply skips over in the same direction. TM1 functions as a URTM on the M cells.
If TM1 halts, the finite control state changes to the state
r
and TM2 commences, flipping every A cell from
a_{1}
to
a_{2}
. Eventually, TM2 wraps around the tape and encounters an
A
cell with the symbol
a_{2}
– this is the first cell encountered by TM2, adjacent to the halting cell of TM1. Then, the finite control state is changed to a state of TM3, which starts on the halting cell of TM1 and runs TM1 in reverse, before finally halting. By running TM1 in reverse, but with the A cells flipped, we ensure that in the case of halting, the A cells remain flipped for a sufficiently long duration.
TM3 has a special halting state which it enters at the point when it would transition to the initial state of TM1.

In order to ensure that our Hamiltonian is 2-local, we split each step of TM1 and TM3 into 2 - first to change the control state, and then to move the control either left or right.
Thus the set of symbols and states required is:

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle=\{W,M\}\times(Q_{u}\cup Q_{rev})\cup\{r\},

\displaystyle\Gamma

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{u}\cup\{a_{1},a_{2}\},

(15)

the “
W
" corresponding to the write substep and the “
M
” corresponding to the move substep.

We note
|Q_{u}|=10,|\Gamma_{u}|=8
, and
|Q_{rev}|=10
.
To ensure the combined TM functions as intended, we furnish the combined TM with an additional transition rule which takes it out of the halting state of TM1, and moves its control to the state
r
, thereby initiating TM2, as well as a transition rule for when TM2 encounters
a_{2}
, which then halts TM2 and initiates TM3.
Thus, if TM1 does not halt on its input,
\mathcal{M}
has all A-cells filled with
a_{1}
, and if TM1 halts on its input,
\mathcal{M}
has all A-cells filled with
a_{2}
.

3.2
TM-to-Hamiltonian Mapping

We now need find a way of mapping our Turing Machine
\mathcal{M}
to a Hamiltonian.
Fortunately, this is a well studied problem, originally in Ref.
[
39
]
, and a variety of TM-to-Hamiltonian mappings have been developed for proving hardness of properties of Hamiltonians
[
40
,
41
,
42
]
.

Let
U
be the unitary which implements the transition rule of a given TM.
In particular, if there is a transition rule
\ket{ab}\rightarrow\ket{cd}
, then we define the term
U_{abcd}=\ket{cd}\bra{ab}
, applied to all relevant qudits.
We then define the set of transition rules to be
\mathcal{T}
and the corresponding Hamiltonian to be:

\displaystyle H=\sum_{abcd\in\mathcal{T}}(U^{\dagger}_{abcd}+U_{abcd}).

(16)

Now let
\ket{w_0}
be an initial state of some computation in the computational basis.
We can then define the state after the
k^{th}
application of the TM transition rule as
\ket{w_k}=V^{k}\ket{w_0},
where
V:=\sum_{abcd\in\mathcal{T}}U_{abcd}
maps product states representing some Turing machine configuration to those of the Turing machine’s incrementation by the reversibility of the transition relations described by
\mathcal{T}
.

Given a particular input state
\ket{w_0}
, the encoded computation either (a) halts, (b) loops forever.
We can apply a unitary transformation to this get the Hamiltonian in a block-diagonal form, where each of the blocks corresponds to a different input state (see
[
35
]
or
[
42
, Section 5]
).
Within each block, the Hamiltonian has the structure of a graph Laplacian of either a line graph (halting case) or loop (non-halting case).
Thus for a given input state, we can define an effective Hamiltonian
H_{\text{eff}}
corresponding to the particular input state:

\displaystyle H_{\text{eff}}=\sum_{k=0}^{T-2}\ket{w_k+1}\bra{w_k}+h.c.,

(17)

where
T
is either the halting time, or the time until the TM begins to loop.
This effective Hamiltonian has eigenstates of
the adjacency matrix of a line graph or loop which are
[
43
,
44
]
:

\displaystyle\lambda_{j}(H_{\text{eff}})=2\cos\left(\frac{(j+1)\pi}{T+1}\right)

(18)

for for halting and

\displaystyle\lambda_{j}(H_{\text{eff}})

\displaystyle=2\cos\left(\frac{2\pi j}{T}\right)

(19)

for non-halting,
j\in\{0,1,\dots,T-1\}.

Due to the determinism of Turing Machine evolution and the finite number of configurations of the machine, we see that
T=\mathcal{O}(d^{N}).

3.3
Long-Time Expectation Values

Given the TM-to-Hamiltonian mapping presented in
Section

3.2
, we can encode the TMs given in
Section

3.1
.
We now consider an initial state:

\displaystyle\ket{w_0}=\ket{q_0}\otimes\ket{y},

(20)

where
q_{0}
corresponds to the the TM head in its initial state and
y\in\Gamma^{N},
with a proportion
(1-\alpha)
evenly spaced out A-cells in the state

for into the URTM’s tape symbols. We then choose the TM1 to run the problem

Lemma 3.2
.

If

or never halts, we have that

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{N}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{1-\alpha}{2}\left[1-\frac{2}{2T_{h}+N+1}\right]&\text{halting}\\
0&\text{non-halting}.\end{cases}

(21)

Proof.

We begin by considering the limit definition for
\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{N}:

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{N}

\displaystyle=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}dt\bra{w_0}e^{iHt}\mathcal{A}_{N}e^{-iHt}\ket{w_0}

\displaystyle=\lim_{T\to\infty}\sum_{ij}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}dte^{i(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j})t}c_{i}^{*}c_{j}\bra{\lambda_i}\mathcal{A}_{N}\ket{\lambda_j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{ij}\delta_{\lambda_{i},\lambda_{j}}c_{i}^{*}c_{j}\bra{\lambda_i}\mathcal{A}_{N}\ket{\lambda_j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{ij}\delta_{i,j}c_{i}^{*}c_{j}\bra{\lambda_i}\mathcal{A}_{N}\ket{\lambda_j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}|c_{j}|^{2}\bra{\lambda_j}\mathcal{A}_{N}\ket{\lambda_j},

(22)

where for the penultimate equality we have used that none of the eigenvalues of the effective Hamiltonian have the same energy (as per
Eq.

18
).

If TM1 never halts, then TM2 never starts and none of then
a_{1}
cells are ever flipped to
a_{2}
and we see that

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{N}\ket{w_j}=0,\quad\forall j.

(23)

This in turn means
\mathcal{A}_{N}\ket{\lambda_j}=0
for all

as all
\ket{\lambda_j}
are superpositions of the
\ket{w_k}.
Thus, for non-halting,
\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{N}=0.

[
23
, Eq. S.46]
show that for halting,
\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{N}
becomes:

\displaystyle\sum_{j}|c_{j}|^{2}\bra{\lambda_j}\mathcal{A}_{N}\ket{\lambda_j}

\displaystyle=\frac{3}{2(T+1)}\left(\bra{w_0}\mathcal{A}_{N}\ket{w_0}+\bra{w_{T-1}}\mathcal{A}_{N}\ket{w_{T-1}}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{T+1}\sum_{j=1}^{T-2}\bra{w_j}\mathcal{A}_{N}\ket{w_j}-\frac{1}{2(T+1)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq j,j^{\prime}\leq T-1\\
j^{\prime}=j\pm 2\end{subarray}}\bra{w_j}\mathcal{A}_{N}\ket{w_{j'}}.

(24)

We now analyze the terms of this equation.
We have that the
M
cells are evenly spaced. We assume that every
M
cell is followed by
\alpha^{-1}-1

A
-cells, and that
\alpha N
of these

blocks constitute the whole Turing Machine. Thus, noting that
\bra{w_0}\mathcal{A}_{N}\ket{w_0}=0,

\bra{w_{T-1}}\mathcal{A}_{N}\ket{w_{T-1}}=1-\alpha,
and the final term in
Eq.

24
is
0,
it remains to calculate

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{T-2}\bra{w_j}\mathcal{A}_{N}\ket{w_j}

\displaystyle=(1-\alpha)\left[T-2-(T_{h}+N+1)+1\right]+\sum_{j=T_{h}+1}^{T_{h}+N}\bra{w_j}\mathcal{A}_{N}\ket{w_j}

\displaystyle=(1-\alpha)\left[T-2-(T_{h}+N)\right]+\sum_{j=T_{h}+1}^{T_{h}+N}\bra{w_j}\mathcal{A}_{N}\ket{w_j}.

(25)

If
\ket{w_j}
has
l

A
-cells with the symbol
a_{2}
, then

\bra{w_j}\mathcal{A}_{N}\ket{w_j}=l\bra{a_2}A\ket{a_2}.

(26)

Due to the uniformity of the placement of the
M
-cells (and recalling our assumption that
\alpha N\in\mathbb{Z}
), for
j\in[T_{h}+1,T_{h}+N]
,

\displaystyle\sum_{j=T_{h}+1}^{T_{h}+N}\bra{w_j}\mathcal{A}_{N}\ket{w_j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{\left[j-\lfloor\alpha j\rfloor\right]}{N}\bra{a_2}A\ket{a_2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{N}j-\sum_{j=1}^{N}\lfloor\alpha j\rfloor\right]\bra{a_2}A\ket{a_2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\left[\frac{N(N+1)}{2}-\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\lfloor\alpha j\rfloor-N\right]\bra{a_2}A\ket{a_2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\left[\frac{N(N+1)}{2}-\alpha^{-1}\sum_{k=0}^{\alpha N-1}k-N\right]\bra{a_2}A\ket{a_2}

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{N+1}{2}-\frac{1}{\alpha N}\frac{\alpha N(\alpha N-1)}{2}-1\bra{a_2}\right]A\ket{a_2}

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-\alpha)N}{2}\bra{a_2}A\ket{a_2}.

(27)

Thus,

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{T-2}\bra{w_j}\mathcal{A}_{N}\ket{w_j}

\displaystyle=(1-\alpha)[T-(T_{h}+N/2+2)]\bra{a_2}A\ket{a_2}.

(28)

Combining this with

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{N}

\displaystyle=(1-\alpha)\frac{T-(T_{h}+N/2+1/2)}{T+1}\bra{a_2}A\ket{a_2}.

Finally, noting that

and

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{N}

\displaystyle=(1-\alpha)\frac{T_{h}+N/2-1/2}{2T_{h}+N+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\alpha}{2}\left[1-\frac{2}{2T_{h}+N+1}\right].

(30)

∎

Theorem 3.3

Proof.

problem.
The action of the final TM only occur if the initial TM halts.
We see from
Lemma

3.2
that the time-averaged expectation value is:

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{N}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{1-\alpha}{2}\left[1-\frac{2}{2T_{h}+N+1}\right]&\text{halting}\\
0&\text{non-halting}.\end{cases}

(31)

By choosing
\alpha=\Theta(1)
to be a sufficiently small constant we get, for sufficiently large
N
, separated values of
\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{N}
in the halting and non-halting cases respectively.
Since
\mathsf{FSHalt}
is
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-hard, then determining which case will occur for
\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{N}
is also
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-hard.
We show later in
Lemma

3.4
that the promise that
|\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}|>d^{-\polyOp(m)}
is satisfied by this Hamiltonian and input state.
∎

To find the local Hilbert space dimension of the Hamiltonian, we simply need to add up the necessary Hilbert space size for the TMs to function.
We realise that the total Hilbert space size will be the size of all the alphabet symbols plus the size of all the TM head state symbols.
Thus we expect the local Hilbert space dimension to be:

\displaystyle d

\displaystyle=2\times(|Q_{u}|+|Q_{rev}|)+|\{r\}|+|\Gamma_{u}|+|\{a_{1},a_{2}\}|

\displaystyle=51.

(32)

Finally we verify that the Hamiltonian and initial state we have used for our hardness construction satisfies the promise of
\mathsf{FSRelax}
.

Lemma 3.4

(Promise is Satisfied)
.

Let
\ket{w_0}
be an initial basis state, and let
H
be a Hamiltonian from a TM-to-Hamiltonian mapping on
N
qudits.
Then:

\displaystyle\min_{ij}|\lambda_{i}(H_{\text{eff}})-\lambda_{j}(H_{\text{eff}})|=\Omega\left(d^{-2N}\right).

(33)

Proof.

From
Eq.

18
, we see that the minimum difference in energies occurs when for
j=i+1
, giving:

\displaystyle|\lambda_{j+1}(H_{\text{eff}})-\lambda_{j}(H_{\text{eff}})|

\displaystyle=2\left(\cos\left(\frac{(j+1)\pi}{T+1}\right)-\cos\left(\frac{j\pi}{T+1}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=4\left|\sin\frac{\pi}{2(T+1)}\sin\frac{(j+1)\pi}{2(T+1)}\right|

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\pi^{2}}{4(T+1)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\Omega(T^{-2})=\Omega(d^{-2N}).

(34)

∎

4
Containment of
\mathsf{FSRelax}
in
\mathsf{PSPACE}

We show that
\mathsf{FSRelax}\in\mathsf{PSPACE}
by demonstrating a
\polyOp(N)
-space quantum algorithm to estimate
\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{N}
. Combined with the result that
\mathsf{BQPSPACE}\subseteq\mathsf{PSPACE}

[
27
]
, and using the fact that
\mathsf{FSTherm(MC)}
is an instance of
\mathsf{FSRelax}
, this shows that
\mathsf{FSTherm(MC)}\in\mathsf{PSPACE}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{A}_{N}
be defined as in
Eq.

10
.
For any
\epsilon\in[0,1/4],
there exists a quantum circuit to prepare a
(4,N\lceil\mathrm{log}_{2}d\rceil+5,\epsilon)
-block encoding of
\sqrt{1-\mathcal{A}_{N}/2dN}

using
\mathcal{O}\left(\polyOp(\logOp(N))\polyOp(\logOp(\epsilon^{-1}))\right)

ancillas, with a classical description that can be computed in time
\mathcal{O}(\polyOp(N)\polyOp(\logOp(\epsilon^{-1}))).

Proof.

We begin by noting that
\mathcal{A}_{N}
is a
dN
-sparse matrix, and that all its entries are less than or equal to
1
in magnitude. Thus, by
Lemma

2.5
, we have that we can produce a
(dN,\lceil\mathrm{log}_{2}(d)\rceil N+3,\epsilon_{2})
-block encoding of
\mathcal{A}_{N}
with
\mathcal{O}\left(\polyOp\left(\mathrm{log}\left(\frac{d^{2}N^{2}}{\epsilon_{2}}\right)\right)\right)
ancillas.

By
Lemma

2.7
, for all
\epsilon_{1}\leq 1/4,
there exists a polynomial
p(x)
of degree
\mathcal{O}(\logOp(\epsilon_{1}^{-1}))
such that

\sup_{x\in[-1,1]}\left|\frac{1}{4}\sqrt{1-x/2}-p(x)\right|\leq\epsilon_{1},

(35)

and
\sup_{x\in[-1,1]}|p(x)|\leq 1/2.
Thus, by
Lemma

5.3
, we can prepare a
\left(1,\lceil\mathrm{log}_{2}(d)\rceil N\!+\!5,\mathcal{O}\left(\logOp(\epsilon_{1}^{-1})\sqrt{\epsilon_{2}/dN}\!+\!\delta\right)\right)
-block encoding of
p\left(\frac{\mathcal{A}_{N}}{dN}\right)
with
\mathcal{O}\left(\logOp(\epsilon_{1}^{-1})\polyOp\left(\mathrm{log}\left(\frac{d^{2}N^{2}}{\epsilon_{2}}\right)\right)\right)
ancillas,
\mathcal{O}(\logOp(\epsilon_{1}^{-1})(\polyOp(N)+\polyOp(\logOp(\epsilon_{2}^{-1}))))
fundamental gates, and a classical description that can be computed in time

\mathcal{O}\left(\logOp(\epsilon_{1}^{-1})\left(\polyOp(N)+\polyOp\left(\logOp(\epsilon_{2}^{-1})\right)\right)+\polyOp\left(\logOp(\epsilon_{1}^{-1})\right)+\polyOp(\logOp(\delta^{-1}))\right).

(36)

Thus, to obtain the desired result, it suffices to take
\delta,\epsilon_{1},\logOp(\epsilon_{1}^{-1})\sqrt{\epsilon_{2}/dN}=\Theta(\epsilon).
To do this, we see that we can take
\delta,\epsilon_{1}=\Theta(\epsilon),
and
\epsilon_{2}=\Theta\left(\left(\frac{\sqrt{N}\epsilon}{\logOp(\epsilon^{-1})}\right)^{2}\right).

∎

We use this block encoding circuit to estimate
\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{N}
.

Lemma 4.2
.

For any
0<\epsilon\leq 1/4,
there is a quantum algorithm that takes space
\mathcal{O}\left(\epsilon^{-2}\polyOp(\logOp(\epsilon^{-1}))\polyOp(N)\right)

to return
\Gamma
such that

|\Gamma-\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{N}|\leq\epsilon

(37)

with probability of at least
2/3.

Proof.

Note that we can uniquely write our initial state as
\ket{\psi_0}=\sum_{i}c_{i}\ket{\lambda_i}
with
\ket{\lambda_i}
a
\lambda_{i}
-eigenvector of
H
such that
\lambda_{i}\neq\lambda_{j}
for all
i\neq j
. Further, by the promise condition, we have that there exists

m=\polyOp(N)
that satisfies
\min\left\{d^{-N},\inf_{i\neq j}|\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}|\right\}>2^{-m}
for all
i
for which
c_{i}\neq 0.

We begin by noting that we can implement a unitary
\tilde{U}_{\epsilon_{1}}
such that
\|\tilde{U}_{\epsilon_{1}}-e^{iH}\|\leq\epsilon_{1}2^{-5(m+1)}
in polynomial space with an efficient classical description via standard Trotterization techniques
[
45
]
.
If we perform phase estimation on
\tilde{U}_{\epsilon_{1}}
with
\mathcal{O}(m)
bits of phase precision, we would then get an approximation to the phase estimation circuit on
e^{iH}
with (operator norm) error at most
\epsilon_{1}.

Let
\ket{\tilde{\lambda}_i^{(m,\epsilon_22^{-(1+m)})}}
be the output in the phase register of running quantum phase estimation of
e^{iH}
on
\ket{\lambda_i},
desired to be accurate to
m
-bits of precision with error parameter
\epsilon_{2}2^{-(m+1)}.
By
Lemma

2.9
, we have that this can be achieved by a circuit with
3(m+1)+\lceil 2\mathrm{log}_{2}\epsilon_{2}^{-1}\rceil
total qubits. Despite the required exponential depth of the phase estimation circuit, it has an efficient classical description due to its repeated structure - so a description of each layer of gates can be computed in polynomial time classically.
Then, by
Corollary

2.10
we have:

\left|\innerproduct{\tilde{\lambda}_i^{(m,\epsilon_2 2^{-(m+1)})}}{\tilde{\lambda}_j^{(m,\epsilon_2 2^{-(m+1)})}}-\delta_{i,j}\right|\leq\epsilon_{2}2^{-m},

(38)

and hence,

\left|\sum_{i,j=0}^{|\sigma(H)|-1}c_{i}c_{j}^{*}\bra{\lambda_j}\mathcal{A}_{N}\ket{\lambda_i}\innerproduct{\tilde{\lambda}_i^{(m,\epsilon_2 2^{-(m+1)})}}{\tilde{\lambda}_j^{(m,\epsilon_2 2^{-(m+1)})}}-\sum_{i=0}^{|\sigma(H)|-1}|c_{i}|^{2}\bra{\lambda_i}\mathcal{A}_{N}\ket{\lambda_i}\right|\leq\epsilon_{2}.

(39)

The long time average of
\mathcal{A}_{N}
for our initial state
\ket{\psi_0}

can be expressed as:

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{N}

\displaystyle=\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}dt\sum_{ij}e^{-i(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j})t}c_{i}^{*}c_{j}\bra{\lambda_i}\mathcal{A}_{N}\ket{\lambda_j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{|\sigma(H)|-1}|c_{i}|^{2}\bra{\lambda_i}\mathcal{A}_{N}\ket{\lambda_i}.

(40)

We now describe a quantum
\mathcal{O}(\polyOp(N))
space protocol to estimate
\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{N}
. An outline of our algorithm is shown below:

\displaystyle\ket{0^a}\left(\ket{\psi_0}\ket{0^{3(m+1)+\lceil 2\mathrm{log}_2\epsilon_2^{-1}\rceil}}\right)=

\displaystyle\ket{0^a}\left(\sum c_{i}\ket{\lambda_i}\ket{0^{3(m+1)+\lceil 2\mathrm{log}_2\epsilon_2^{-1}\rceil}}\right)

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\text{Phase estimation of }\tilde{U}_{\epsilon_{2}}}

\displaystyle\ket{0^a}\left(\sum c_{i}\ket{\lambda_i}\ket{\tilde{\lambda}_{i}^{(m,\epsilon_22^{-(m+1)})}}+\ket{\zeta}\right)

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\text{Block encoding $\frac{1}{4}\sqrt{1-\mathcal{A}_{N}/2dN}$}}

\displaystyle\ket{0^a}\left(\sum\frac{c_{i}}{4}\sqrt{1-\mathcal{A}_{N}/2dN}\ket{\lambda_i}\ket{\tilde{\lambda}_{i}^{(m,\epsilon_22^{-(m+1)})}}+\ket{\zeta'}\right)+\ket{\xi}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\text{Measure first register}}

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}[0^{a}]\approx\frac{1}{16}\sum_{i}|c_{i}|^{2}\bra{\lambda_i}(1-\mathcal{A}_{N}/2dN)\ket{\lambda_i}=\frac{1-\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{N}/2dN}{16}.

(41)

First, we prepare the state

\ket{0^a}\otimes\left(\ket{\psi_0}\ket{0^{3(m+1)+\lceil 2\mathrm{log}_2\epsilon_2^{-1}\rceil}}\right)
.
The first register has
a=\lceil N\mathrm{log}_{2}d\rceil+5=\mathcal{O}(N)
qubits.
Since
m=\mathcal{O}(\polyOp(N))
, this state can be prepared in
\mathcal{O}(\polyOp(N))
space. For convenience, we use a qubit-based algorithm rather than a qudit based algorithm, where each qudit of the original state is replaced with
\lceil{\logOp(d)}\rceil
qubits. We designate the first
a
qubits, which will act as a control, as register 1, and the remaining register as register
2.

Next, we perform phase estimation on register 2
for the operator
\tilde{U}_{\epsilon_{2}}

, taking it to
m
bits of precision and with
2
-norm error in the approximation register of
\epsilon_{2}2^{-(m+1)}
.
The resulting state at the end of this step is:

\ket{0^a}\left(\sum c_{i}\ket{\lambda_i}\ket{\tilde{\lambda}_i^{(m,\epsilon_22^{-(m+1)})}}+\ket{\zeta}\right),

(42)

where
\ket{\tilde{\lambda}_i^{(m,\epsilon_22^{-(m+1)})}}
is as above, and
\ket{\zeta},
the error from performing the QPE on
\tilde{U}_{\epsilon_{1}}
instead of

satisfies
\|\ket{\zeta}\|\leq\epsilon_{1}.
We are once again able to give an efficient classical description of this phase estimation.

We now apply a block encoding of
\sqrt{1-\mathcal{A}_{N}/2dN}/4
to our state. By
Lemma

4.1
, we can do so to
\epsilon_{3}
error using
\mathcal{O}(\polyOp(\logOp(N))\polyOp(\logOp(\epsilon_{3}^{-1})))

The resulting state is:

\ket{0^a}\left(\sum\frac{c_{i}}{4}\sqrt{1-\mathcal{A}_{N}/2dN}\ket{\lambda_i}\ket{\tilde{\lambda}_{i}^{(m,\epsilon_22^{-(m+1)})}}+\ket{\zeta'}\right)+\ket{\xi}

(43)

Where
(\bra{0^a}\otimes I)\ket{\xi}=0,
and
\|\ket{\zeta'}\|\leq\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{3}.

Thus, we see that if we measure the first register, we will obtain

with probability

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}[0^{a}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\frac{c_{i}^{*}c_{j}}{16}\bra{\lambda_i}\left(1-\mathcal{A}_{N}/2dN\right)\ket{\lambda_j}\innerproduct{\tilde{\lambda}_i^{(m,\epsilon_22^{-(m+1)})}}{\tilde{\lambda}_j^{(m,\epsilon_22^{-(m+1)})}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{i}\left[\frac{c_{i}}{4}\left(\bra{\zeta'}\sqrt{1-\mathcal{A}_{N}/2dN}\otimes I\right)\ket{\lambda_i}\ket{\tilde{\lambda}_i^{(m,\epsilon_22^{-(m+1)}}}\!+\!\frac{c_{i}^{*}}{4}\bra{\lambda_i}\bra{\tilde{\lambda}_i^{(m,\epsilon_22^{-(m+1)}}}\left(\sqrt{\mathcal{A}_{N}}\!\otimes\!I\ket{\zeta'}\right)\!\right]\!,

(44)

and hence,

\left|\mathrm{Pr}[0^{a}]-\frac{1-\frac{\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{N}}{2dN}}{16}\right|\leq\frac{\epsilon_{2}}{16}+\frac{1}{2}(\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{3}).

(45)

Thus, by Hoeffding’s inequality, for any
\epsilon_{4}>0,
it suffices to repeat this measurement
\lceil\epsilon_{4}^{-2}\rceil
times and take the sample average to get an
(\epsilon_{2}/16+(\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{3})/2+\epsilon_{4})
-estimate of
\frac{1-\frac{\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{N}}{2dN}}{16}
with probability at least

To get overall error of
\epsilon
in the estimate of
|\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{N}|
with that probability, it suffices to take
\epsilon_{2}=\epsilon/8dN
,
\epsilon_{4}=\epsilon/128dN,
and
\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{3}=\epsilon/64dN.
Thus, we see that to repeat this measurement, we need in total

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\left(\epsilon^{-2}\left(\polyOp(N)+\polyOp(\logOp(N\epsilon^{-1}))+\polyOp(\logOp(N))\polyOp(\logOp(N\epsilon^{-1}))\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-2}\polyOp(\logOp(\epsilon^{-1}))\polyOp(N))

(46)

space.
∎

Finally, this algorithm allows us to decide
\mathsf{FSRelax}
. Hence, we have

Lemma 4.3
.

\mathsf{FSRelax}\in\mathsf{PSPACE}
.

Proof.

Lemma

4.2
allows us to construct a probabilistic quantum polynomial space algorithm to decide
\mathsf{FSRelax}
. The algorithm in
Lemma

4.2
relies on postselection.
We can use
Lemma

4.2
to probabilistically compute
\Gamma
such that
|\Gamma-\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{N}|\leq\epsilon^{\prime}
for some
\epsilon^{\prime}<\epsilon
. In the event of the correct postselection outcomes occurring, since we have the promise that either
|\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{N}-A^{*}|\leq\epsilon
or
|\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{N}-A^{*}|\geq c\epsilon
, one of the two following cases holds.

1.

If
|\Gamma-A^{*}|\leq\epsilon+\epsilon^{\prime}
then
|\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{N}-A^{*}|\leq\epsilon.

2.

Otherwise,if
|\Gamma-A^{*}|\geq c\epsilon-\epsilon^{\prime}
, then
|\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{N}-A^{*}|\geq c\epsilon.

Hence if our algorithm postselects correctly,
\mathsf{FSRelax}
can be decided by a polynomial space quantum algorithm that computes
|\Gamma-A^{*}|
.
On the other hand, if our algorithm does not postselect correctly, we can simply return YES or NO at random. For YES instances of
\mathsf{FSRelax}
, we accept with probability strictly greater than
1/2
, while for NO instances, we accept with probability strictly less than
1/2
. Hence,
\mathsf{FSRelax}\in\mathsf{PrQSPACE}
.
By
[
27
]
,
\mathsf{PrQSPACE}\subseteq\mathsf{PSPACE}
. Therefore,
\mathsf{FSRelax}\in PSPACE
.
∎

follows immediately from

4.3
and

3.3
.

\mathsf{FSTherm(Gibbs)}

In this section we’ll show that
\mathsf{FSTherm(MC)}
and
\mathsf{FSTherm(Gibbs)}
are contained in
\mathsf{PSPACE}
. We will first show a polynomial quantum space algorithm to compute

\mathsf{FSTherm(MC)}
by reducing any instance of
\mathsf{FSTherm(MC)}
to an instance of
\mathsf{FSRelax}
. Similarly, using a polynomial space quantum algorithm to compute
\traceOp[\mathcal{A}_{N}\rho_{\textsf{MC}}]
, we show that
\mathsf{FSTherm(Gibbs)}\in\mathsf{PSPACE}
. Throughout this section, we restrict to systems of qubits rather than qudits, in order to make use of qubit block-encoding results. However, this containment result is general, since a system of a
k
-local Hamiltonian on
N

-local Hamiltonian on

qubits.

First, we prove some preliminary lemmas about block-encodings, which we will use to prepare an approximation of the microcanonical state.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
H
be a Hermitian matrix on
N
qubits, and
\alpha\geq\left\lVert H\right\rVert
. Let the eigenvectors and eigenvalues of
H
be
\{\ket{\lambda_i},\lambda_{i}\}
. For a polynomial
p
, let
U
be an
(N+m)
-qubit,
(\alpha,m,0)
-block encoding of
p(H/\alpha)
.
Let
\ket{\psi}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{N}}}\sum_{i}\ket{\lambda_i}_{a}\ket{\lambda_i}_{b}
, where
N
qubits form the main computational register
a
, and
N
qubits form an auxiliary register
b
.
Then,
U
can be used to prepare the state

\ket{\psip(p)}\coloneqq\frac{p(H/\alpha)_{a}\ket{\psi}}{\left\lVert p(H/\alpha)_{a}\ket{\psi}\right\rVert}.

(47)

such that

\rho_{\mathrm{poly}}(p)\coloneqq\traceOp_{#1}[\outerproduct{\psip(p)}{\psip(p)}]=\sum_{i}\frac{(p(\lambda_{i}/\alpha))^{2}}{\sum_{j}(p(\lambda_{i}/\alpha))^{2}}\outerproduct{\lambda_i}{\lambda_i}

(48)

Proof.

Let
c
be the register of the
m
block encoding ancilla qubits.
U
acts non-trivially on registers
a
and
c
.
By
Definition

2.4
,
U
prepares the state

(49)

where
\bra{0^m}_{c}\ket{\phi^{\perp}}=0
, and

\displaystyle p(H/\alpha)_{a}\ket{\psi}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{n}}}\sum_{i}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{N}}}p(H/\alpha)_{a}\ket{\lambda_i}_{a}\ket{\lambda_i}_{b}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{N}}}\sum_{i}p(\lambda_{i}/\alpha)\ket{\lambda_i}_{a}\ket{\lambda_i}_{b}.

(50)

The state obtained after postselecting on the correct block-encoding being performed is

\displaystyle\ket{\psip(p)}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{p(H^{\prime}/\alpha)_{a}\ket{\psi}}{\left\lVert p(H^{\prime}/\alpha)_{a}\ket{\psi}\right\rVert}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sum_{j}(p(\lambda_{i}/\alpha))^{2}}}\sum_{i}p(\lambda_{i}/\alpha)\ket{\lambda_i}_{a}\ket{\lambda_i}_{b}.

(51)

It follows that

\rho_{p}\coloneqq\traceOp_{#1}[\outerproduct{\psip(p)}{\psip(p)}]=\sum_{i}\frac{(p(\lambda_{i}/\alpha))^{2}}{\sum_{j}(p(\lambda_{i}/\alpha))^{2}}\outerproduct{\lambda_i}{\lambda_i}.

(52)

∎

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
H
be a Hermitian matrix on
N
qubits, and

2.8

(54)

where

\displaystyle\lvert(p(\lambda_{i}/\alpha))^{2}-e^{-\pi\lambda_{i}^{2}/w^{2}}\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\eta\lvert p(\lambda_{i}/\alpha)+e^{-\pi\lambda_{i}^{2}/(2w^{2})}\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\eta(2+\eta)

\displaystyle\implies|\Delta|

\displaystyle\left\lVert\rho_{\mathrm{poly}}(P_{\mathrm{Gauss}(w/\alpha)}^{(\eta)})-\rho_{\textsf{MC}}(H,0)\right\rVert_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\left|\frac{S(p(\lambda_{i}/\alpha))^{2}}{S(S+\Delta)}-\frac{(S+\Delta)e^{-\pi\lambda_{i}^{2}/w^{2}}}{S(S+\Delta)}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i}\left(\frac{\epsilon_{\eta}}{|S+\Delta|}+\left|\frac{\Delta e^{-\pi\lambda_{i}^{2}/w^{2}}}{S(S+\Delta)}\right|\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\epsilon_{\eta}2^{N}}{|S+\Delta|}.

(57)

\left\lVert\rho_{\mathrm{poly}}(P_{\mathrm{Gauss}(w/\alpha)}^{(\eta)})-\rho_{\textsf{MC}}(H,0)\right\rVert_{1}\leq\frac{2\epsilon_{\eta}}{e^{-\pi\alpha^{2}/w^{2}}-\epsilon_{\eta}},\\

(58)

Selecting
\eta\leq\frac{\epsilon_{p}e^{-\pi\alpha^{2}/w^{2}}}{9}<1

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\eta}\leq\frac{\epsilon e^{-\pi\alpha^{2}/w^{2}}}{3}

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\left\lVert\rho_{\mathrm{poly}}(P_{\mathrm{Gauss}(w/\alpha)}^{(\eta)})-\rho_{\textsf{MC}}(H,0)\right\rVert_{1}\leq\epsilon_{p}.

(59)

The degree of
p
is
\mathcal{O}(\frac{\alpha}{w}\logOp(1/\eta))=\mathcal{O}(\frac{\alpha}{w}(\logOp(1/\epsilon)+\pi\alpha^{2}/w^{2}))

∎

Lemma 5.3
.

Then, a single application of
U
(postselecting on the correct block-encoding outcome) can be used to prepare a state
\rho^{\prime}
such that

\left\lVert\rho^{\prime}-\rho_{\textsf{MC}}(H,0)\right\rVert_{1}\leq\sqrt{8\epsilon^{\prime}}+\epsilon_{p}.

(60)

Proof.

As in
Lemma

5.2
, let
\ket{\psi}\coloneqq\sum_{i}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{N}}}\ket{\lambda_i}_{a}\ket{\lambda_i}_{b}
, where
N
qudits form the main computational register
a
,
N
qudits form an auxiliary register
b
, and
\ket{\lambda_i}
are the eigenstates of
H/\alpha
.
\ket{\psi}
is the maximally entangled state represented in the basis of eigenstates of
H/\alpha
. We also define a register
c
for the
n_{c}
block encoding ancilla qudits.
U
acts on all three of these registers and by definition of the block-encoding,

\left\lVert\bra{0^{n_c}}_{c}U\ket{0^{n_c}}_{c}-p(H/\alpha)\right\rVert\leq\epsilon^{\prime}.

(61)

Let

\ket{\psi_p}\coloneqq\frac{p(H/\alpha)_{a}\ket{\psi}}{\left\lVert p(H/\alpha)_{a}\ket{\psi}\right\rVert}.

(62)

Then, as in
Proposition

5.1

\rho_{\mathrm{poly}}=\traceOp_{#1}[\outerproduct{\psi_p}{\psi_p}].

(63)

Let
M\coloneqq\bra{0^{n_c}}_{c}U\ket{0^{n_c}}_{c}
. Then, let the state obtained after postselecting on the correct block-encoding being performed be

\ket{\phi}=\frac{M\ket{\psi}}{\left\lVert M\ket{\psi}\right\rVert}.

(64)

Further, let

\rho^{\prime}\coloneqq\traceOp_{#1}[\outerproduct{\phi}{\phi}].

(65)

Then, by Proposition 9 of
[
30
]
,

\left\lVert\ket{\psi_p}-\ket{\phi}\right\rVert\leq\epsilon^{\prime},

(66)

By monotonicity of the trace distance under the partial trace,

\left\lVert\traceOp_{#1}[\outerproduct{\phi}{\phi}]-\traceOp_{#1}[\outerproduct{\psi_p}{\psi_p}]\right\rVert_{1}\leq\left\lVert\outerproduct{\phi}{\phi}-\outerproduct{\psi_p}{\psi_p}\right\rVert_{1}.

(67)

Using this, and the relation between the fidelity and the trace distance, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\lVert\rho^{\prime}-\rho_{\mathrm{poly}}\right\rVert_{1}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{2\epsilon^{\prime}-\epsilon^{\prime 2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{8\epsilon^{\prime}}.

(68)

Combining this with
Lemma

5.2
using the triangle inequality, we obtain

\left\lVert\rho^{\prime}-\rho_{\textsf{MC}}(H,0)\right\rVert_{1}\leq\sqrt{8\epsilon^{\prime}}+\epsilon_{p}.

(69)

∎

Finally, we have the required ingredients to prepare the microcanonical state.

Lemma 5.4
.

For any
k
-local Hamiltonian
H
on
N
qubits, and energy
E
, there is a quantum algorithm using
\mathcal{O}(\polyOp(N,\logOp(1/\epsilon)))
space and
\mathcal{O}(\polyOp(N,\logOp(1/\epsilon))\exp(((\norm{H} + |E|)/w)^2))
expected time that prepares a state
\rho
such that

\left\lVert\rho-\rho_{\textsf{MC}}(H,E)\right\rVert_{1}\leq\epsilon.

(70)

Proof.

Our algorithm is simple. Starting in the initial state
\ket{\psi}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{s}}}\sum_{i}\ket{\lambda_i}_{a}\ket{\lambda_i}_{b}
, we apply a block encoding such that the resultant state, after postselecting on the correct block encoding being performed and tracing out the subsystem
b
, is a good approximation of the microcanonical state. It remains to be shown that such a block encoding can be constructed, and to analyze the approximation error as well as the time and space requirements.

First, we show how the block encoding is constructed. Since each term of
H
is
k
-local and there are
\mathcal{O}(\polyOp(n))
terms in
H
,
H^{\prime}\coloneqq H-EI
is
\mathcal{O}(2^{k}\polyOp(n))
-sparse. We can select
\alpha\geq\left\lVert H\right\rVert+|E|
so that the eigenvalues of
H^{\prime}/\alpha
lie in
[-1,1]
.
\alpha=\polyOp(n)
, so by
Lemma

2.5
,
H^{\prime}
has an
(\polyOp(n),\polyOp(n),\epsilon_{H})
-block-encoding that can be implemented by a circuit with
\mathcal{O}(\polyOp(n),\polyOp(\logOp(\frac{1}{\epsilon_{H}})))
gates and
\mathcal{O}(\polyOp(n),\polyOp(\logOp(\frac{1}{\epsilon_{H}})))
space.

We make use of the polynomial approximation for a Gaussian function
P_{\mathrm{Gauss}(w/\alpha)}^{(\eta)}
(
Lemma

2.8
) with degree
\deg(P_{\mathrm{Gauss}(w/\alpha)}^{(\eta)})
which we will determine later. Along with
Lemma

2.6
, this implies there is a circuit
\tilde{U}_{\delta}
with
\mathcal{O}(\polyOp(n),\polyOp(\logOp(\frac{1}{\epsilon_{H}})))
gates that implements a
{(1,\polyOp(n),4\deg(P_{\mathrm{Gauss}(w/\alpha)}^{(\eta)})\sqrt{\epsilon_{H}/\alpha}+\delta)}
block-encoding of
P_{\mathrm{Gauss}(w/\alpha)}^{(\eta)}(H^{\prime}/\alpha)
, and has a classical description computable in time
\mathcal{O}(\polyOp(n,\deg(P_{\mathrm{Gauss}(w/\alpha)}^{(\eta)}),\logOp(1/\delta)))
.

The above construction makes use of parameters
\eta,\epsilon_{H},\delta
. We would like to choose these parameters such that we approximate the target state well, and the postselection probability is not too small.
For the first condition,
we apply
Lemma

5.3
with
\epsilon^{\prime}=4\deg(P_{\mathrm{Gauss}(w/\alpha)}^{(\eta)})\sqrt{\epsilon_{H}/\alpha}+\delta
, we have

\left\lVert\rho^{\prime}-\rho_{\textsf{MC}}(H,0)\right\rVert_{1}\leq\left(\sqrt{32\deg(P_{\mathrm{Gauss}(w/\alpha)}^{(\eta)})\sqrt{\epsilon_{H}/\alpha}+8\delta}\right)+\epsilon_{p}.

(71)

We will solve for parameters such that the following condition is fulfilled:

\left\lVert\rho^{\prime}-\rho_{\textsf{MC}}\right\rVert_{1}\leq\epsilon.

(72)

For our second condition, we require that our algorithm doesn’t take too long. The time taken is determined by the probability of correctly postselecting, which we now estimate. Let
M\coloneqq\bra{0^{n_c}}_{c}\tilde{U}_{\delta}\ket{0^{n_c}}_{c}
. The probability of postselecting to the correct state is
\left\lVert M\ket{\psi}\right\rVert^{2}
.
Observe that

\displaystyle\left\lVert M\ket{\psi}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\geq\left\lVert P_{\mathrm{Gauss}(w/\alpha)}^{(\eta)}(H^{\prime}/\alpha)\ket{\psi}\right\rVert-\left\lVert(M-P_{\mathrm{Gauss}(w/\alpha)}^{(\eta)}(H^{\prime}/\alpha))\ket{\psi}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\geq\left\lVert P_{\mathrm{Gauss}(w/\alpha)}^{(\eta)}(H^{\prime}/\alpha)\ket{\psi}\right\rVert-4\deg(P_{\mathrm{Gauss}(w/\alpha)}^{(\eta)})\sqrt{\epsilon_{H}/\alpha}-\delta

\displaystyle\geq e^{-\pi\alpha^{2}/(2w^{2})}-\eta-4\deg(P_{\mathrm{Gauss}(w/\alpha)}^{(\eta)})\sqrt{\epsilon_{H}/\alpha}-\delta.

(73)

In order for the probability of correct postselection to be lower bounded, require that the probability of correct postselection remains of the order of its leading term, i.e.,

\left\lVert M\ket{\psi}\right\rVert=\Omega(\exp(-\pi\alpha^2/(2w^2))).

(74)

We now solve for
\eta,\epsilon_{H},\delta
such that
Eq.

72
and
Eq.

74
are fulfilled.

First, to partially satisfy
Eq.

72
, we select
\epsilon_{p}=\epsilon/2
. This implies
\eta\leq\frac{\epsilon e^{-\pi\alpha^{2}/w^{2}}}{18}
.

Equation

72
is then satisfied if
\sqrt{8\delta}<\epsilon/4
and
\sqrt{32\deg(P_{\mathrm{Gauss}(w/\alpha)}^{(\eta)})\sqrt{\epsilon_{H}/\alpha}}<\epsilon/4
.

Now, in order to satisfy
Eq.

74
, we impose the constraint that
\eta,4\deg(P_{\mathrm{Gauss}(w/\alpha)}^{(\eta)})\sqrt{\epsilon_{H}/\alpha},\delta
are each less than
\frac{1}{4}e^{-\pi\alpha^{2}/(2w^{2})}
. By our choice of
\epsilon_{p}
, we have already obtained a sufficient condition for
\eta
.

To simultaneously fulfill
\sqrt{8\delta}<\epsilon/4
and
\delta<\frac{1}{4}e^{-\pi\alpha^{2}/(2w^{2})}
, we select
\delta<\epsilon^{2}e^{-\pi\alpha^{2}/(2w^{2})}/32
.

We make use of the fact that
\deg(P_{\mathrm{Gauss}(w/\alpha)}^{(\eta)})=\gamma\left(\frac{\alpha}{w}(\logOp(2/\epsilon)+\pi\alpha^{2}/w^{2})\right)
for some constant
\gamma
.
To simultaneously fulfill
\sqrt{32\deg(P_{\mathrm{Gauss}(w/\alpha)}^{(\eta)})\sqrt{\epsilon_{H}/\alpha}}<\epsilon/4
and
4\deg(P_{\mathrm{Gauss}(w/\alpha)}^{(\eta)})\sqrt{\epsilon_{H}/\alpha}<\frac{1}{4}e^{-\pi\alpha^{2}/(2w^{2})}
, we select

\epsilon_{H}\leq\alpha\left(\frac{\epsilon e^{-\pi\alpha^{2}/(2w^{2})}}{128\gamma\left((\alpha/w)(\logOp(2/\epsilon)+\pi\alpha^{2}/w^{2})\right)}\right)^{4}.

(75)

With the chosen values of
\eta,\epsilon_{H},\delta
,
Eq.

72
and
Eq.

74
are satisfied. We now analyze the time and space requirements of the algorithm. The probability of correct postselection is
\left\lVert M\ket{\psi}\right\rVert^{2}
. Hence, in order to postselect onto the target state
\rho^{\prime}
, we require an expected
\mathcal{O}(e^{\pi\alpha^{2}/w^{2}})
repetitions of the block encoding circuit. We do not need any additional space despite an expected total exponential depth since we are repeating the same circuit until correct postselection.
Further, the depth of the block-encoding circuit as well as the classical computation time to obtain its description remain polynomial since
\logOp(\frac{1}{\epsilon_{H}}),\logOp(\frac{1}{\delta})=\mathcal{O}(\logOp(1/\epsilon)(\alpha/w)^{2})
.

The depth of the block-encoding circuit scales as

\mathcal{O}(\left(\frac{\alpha}{w}\right)\logOp(1/\eta)\times\polyOp(\logOp(1/\epsilon_{H})))=\mathcal{O}(\polyOp(N,\logOp(1/\epsilon),1/w)).

(76)

Therefore, to prepare
\rho
we require
\mathcal{O}(e^{\pi\alpha^{2}/w^{2}})
expected executions of a
\mathcal{O}(\polyOp(N,\logOp(1/\epsilon),1/w))
depth circuit, which can be performed in
\mathcal{O}(\polyOp(N,\logOp(1/\epsilon),1/w))
space.
∎

This algorithm allows us to estimate observables of the microcanonical state.

Corollary 5.5
.

The algorithm in
Lemma

5.4
can be used to estimate
\widehat{\mathcal{A}_{N}}
such that
|\widehat{\mathcal{A}_{N}}-\traceOp[\mathcal{A}_{N}\rho_{\textsf{MC}}]|\leq\epsilon
using
\mathcal{O}(\polyOp(N,\logOp(1/\epsilon)))
space and
\mathcal{O}(\polyOp(N,\logOp(1/\epsilon))\exp(((\norm{H} + |E|)/w)^2))
expected time.

Proof.

Note that by the triangle inequality,
\left\lVert A_{N}\right\rVert\leq\max_{i}\left\lVert A_{i}\right\rVert=c
for some constant
c
. Let
\rho
be the state as prepared in
Lemma

5.4
, to precision
\epsilon/c
.
Applying Holder’s inequality,

\displaystyle|\traceOp[\mathcal{A}_{N}\rho-\mathcal{A}_{N}\rho_{\textsf{MC}}(H,E)]|

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathcal{A}_{N}(\rho-\rho_{\textsf{MC}}(H,E))\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\|\rho-\rho_{\textsf{MC}}(H,E)\|_{1}\|\mathcal{A}_{N}\|

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon.

(77)

Hence by preparing
\rho
and measuring
\mathcal{A}_{N}
, we can estimate
\traceOp[\mathcal{A}_{N}\rho_{\textsf{MC}}]
to arbitrary constant precision.
\mathcal{A}_{N}
is a sum of
N
single site (and hence commuting) observables. A single measurement of
\mathcal{A}_{N}
can be performed by preparing a sample of
\rho
and measuring each of the
A_{i}
observables sequentially, adding them, and dividing by

. By Hoeffding’s inequality, to estimate
\traceOp[\mathcal{A}_{N}\rho_{\textsf{MC}}]
to precision
\epsilon
with probability at least

requires
\mathcal{O}(1/\epsilon^{2})
samples. Hence there is a a probabilistic polynomial space quantum algorithm to estimate
\traceOp[\mathcal{A}_{N}\rho_{\textsf{MC}}]
to constant precision. We note however, that this algorithm requires postselection, which may only succeed with inverse-exponentially small probability when

.
∎

Finally, we can use this algorithm to prove one of our main results.

Theorem 5.6
.

\mathsf{FSTherm(MC)}\in\mathsf{PSPACE}
.

Proof.

Using the algorithm from
Corollary

5.5
along with the probabilistic polynomial space quantum algorithm to estimate

to constant precision
Lemma

4.2
, when

,
\mathsf{FSTherm(MC)}
can be decided by a polynomial-space quantum algorithm that simply compares the two values, if our algorithm postselects correctly, which occurs with non-zero probability. On the other hand, in the event our algorithm does not postselect correctly, we can Accept or Reject with uniform probability. Hence, there is a quantum algorithm that such that for YES instances of
\mathsf{FSTherm(MC)}
, we accept with probability strictly greater than

, while for NO instances, we accept with probability strictly less than
1/2
. Hence,
\mathsf{FSTherm(MC)}\in\mathsf{PrQSPACE}
.
By
[

]
,
\mathsf{PrQSPACE}\subseteq\mathsf{PSPACE}
. Therefore,
\mathsf{FSTherm(MC)}\in\mathsf{PSPACE}
.
∎

Another statistical ensemble of interest is the Gibbs ensemble

. An alternative to

is the problem of deciding whether a system thermalizes to the

Lemma 5.7
.

There is a quantum algorithm using
\mathcal{O}(\polyOp(N,\logOp(1/\epsilon)))

The Gibbs state

can be
\epsilon
-approximated with a block-encoding of circuit depth
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\beta}\logOp(1/\epsilon))
and
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{d^{N}/Z})

[

,

,
47
]
.
Note that

, so estimating
\traceOp[\rho_{\textsf{G}}\mathcal{A}_{N}]
to constant precision only requires
\mathcal{O}(\exp(N))

-depth circuit, which can be done in polynomial quantum space.
∎

Since
\mathsf{PrQSPACE}\subseteq\mathsf{PSPACE}
,

follows immediately from

.

6
Hardness of
\mathsf{FSTherm(MC)}
and
\mathsf{FSTherm(Gibbs)}

In this subsection we will show that
\mathsf{FSTherm(MC)}
is as hard as
\mathsf{FSHalt}
under
quantum
polynomial time reductions. We prove this by showing that given an instance of
\mathsf{FSHalt}
, we may use a poynomial time quantum algorithm to construct an instance of
\mathsf{FSTherm(MC)}
. We note that since we make use of a quantum polynomial time reduction rather than a classical one, our reduction differs from the standard formal complexity theoretic reduction.
We modify the
\mathsf{FSRelax}
hardness construction such that we have
A^{*}=\traceOp[\rho_{\textsf{MC}}\mathcal{A}_{N}],
thus showing that there exist observables such that determining whether the system thermalizes is as hard as a
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-complete problem. We describe a high-level overview of our approach:

1.

We pad the end of the Turing machine even more in the case of halting, so that when it halts,
\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{N}=(1+\mathcal{O}(1/N))(1-\alpha)\bra{a_2}A\ket{a_2}.
This is covered in
Section

6.1
.

2.

We double the size of the local Hilbert space, and modify
A_{i}
by introducing parameters such that the two halves of the Hilbert space have different, tunable values of
\langle A_{i}\rangle
. We then modify the Hamiltonian so that, in the microcanonical state, both halves of the Hilbert space are represented equally. This construction allows us to tune the microcanonical expectation of
\mathcal{A}_{N}
. This is covered in
Section

6.2
.

3.

Finally, we show that a polynomial time quantum algorithm can be used to tune these parameters such that in the case of halting,
\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{N}
is equal to the microcanonical average of
\mathcal{A}_{N},
and both are separated from
0,
the non-halting value of
\mathcal{A}_{N}.
This is covered in
Section

6.3
.

Finally, in
Section

6.4
, we discuss some evidence and suggest a conjecture under which
\mathsf{FSTherm(Gibbs)}
is
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-hard (and hence
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-complete due to
Lemma

5.7
).

6.1
Modifying the TM

We modify the Turing Machine construction such that the values of
\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{N}
on halting inputs and on non-halting inputs are sufficiently separated, so that we may perform our tuning procedure in the following sections to reduce
\mathsf{FSRelax}
to
\mathsf{FSTherm(MC)}
.
We first show how to asymptotically increase
\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{N}
in the case of halting to
(1-\alpha)\bra{a_2}A\ket{a_2}
, which we require for technical reasons in our tuning construction in
Section

6.2
.
As discussed in
Section

3.1
, TM1 performs the actual computation of a URTM, TM2 flips A cells if TM1 halts, and TM3 runs TM1 in reverse. Since in the case of halting, the A cells are flipped by TM2 and remain flipped for the duration of TM3, the long-time average of
\mathcal{A}_{N}
is determined by the duration of TM2 and TM3. We can thus modify this average by changing the time spent in TM3.
We achieve this by modifying TM3 to first run a buffer which performs a full loop around the tape for each element of the tape (i.e., taking
N(N+1)
steps). Then, we continue as before with TM3 running TM1 in reverse, but with an additional buffer that iterates the finite control around the entire loop until it returns to its initial location in between each (computational) step of TM3.
Since a full loop around the tape takes time
N
, we thus increase the time spent in the computational part of TM3 by a factor of
N+1
.

To ensure the correct TM action, we add to the tape alphabet of TM3 such that every symbol in the tape alphabet has a “place-marker” copy which will be what the TM initially writes to the tape.
This copy lets the finite-control know when it has returned to its most recent write cell.
To facilitate the traversal, we will also add a copy of every finite-control state, which the update rules will just shift through the ring until it hits the “place-marker” tape-symbols.
For the first buffer, we need to add another state of the head corresponding to the specific buffer, and two symbols to the tape alphabet to help keep track of the progress of the
N
loops around the tape. Specifically we will initialize the two symbols, pushing one of the symbols in one direction after every loop, and only finishing when the symbols come in contact again.
Thus, in the case of halting, this additional looping increases the total run-time to
(N+2)T_{h}+(N+1)N
. The first buffer ensures that this run-time is large even when
T_{h}<N
.

Lemma 6.1
.

With the above modified Turing Machine construction, If
y
encodes a Turing Machine that either halts in time
T_{h}
or never halts, then there exists
\delta\in(0,N^{-1}]
such that

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{N}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}(1-\alpha)\left[1-\delta\right]\bra{a_2}A\ket{a_2}&\text{halting}\\
0&\text{non-halting}.\end{cases}

(79)

Proof.

The proof proceeds along exactly the same lines as the proof of
Lemma

3.2
.
Revisiting
Eq.

29
, with the modification that
T=(N+2)T_{h}+(N+1)N,
we have that

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{N}

\displaystyle=(1-\alpha)\frac{T-(T_{h}+N/2+1/2)}{T+1}

\displaystyle=(1-\alpha)\frac{(N+2)T_{h}+(N+1)N-(T_{h}+N/2+1/2)}{(N+2)T_{h}+(N+1)N+1}\bra{a_2}A\ket{a_2}

\displaystyle=(1-\alpha)\left[1-\frac{T_{h}+N/2+3/2}{(N+2)T_{h}+(N+1)N+1}\right]\bra{a_2}A\ket{a_2}

\displaystyle=(1-\alpha)\left[1-\delta\right]\bra{a_2}A\ket{a_2},

(80)

where
\delta\coloneqq\frac{T_{h}+N/2+3/2}{(N+2)T_{h}+(N+1)N+1},
and
0<\delta\leq 2N^{-1}.

The non-halting case will still have
\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{N}=0.

∎

6.2
Modifying
H,

\mathcal{H},
and
A

In addition to this modification to the Turing Machine, we will also modify the Hilbert space and Hamiltonian. The goal of this modification is to:

•

Push the microcanonical expectation value of
\mathcal{A}_{N}
to the middle of
A
’s spectrum.

•

Give us control of
\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{N}
in the halting case in such a way that we are guaranteed that it crosses the microcanonical expectation away from the non-halting case’s value (zero).

To do these, we will replace our local Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
with a new Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{tune}:=\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathcal{H}
of doubled dimension.
We will denote the projection onto
\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathbf{0}
by
\Pi
and the projection onto
\mathbf{0}\oplus\mathcal{H}
by
\Pi^{\prime}.
Denote the basis of
\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathbf{0}
by
\{\ket{a}\ :\ a\in Q\cup\Gamma\}
and that of
\mathbf{0}\oplus\mathcal{H}
by
\{\ket{a'}\ :\ a\in Q\cup\Gamma\}.
Let

H_{TM}\coloneqq\sum_{abcd\in\mathcal{T}}\outerproduct{cd}{ab}+h.c,

(81)

represent Turing-machine evolution on
\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathbf{0},
and

H_{TM}^{\prime}\coloneqq\sum_{abcd\in\mathcal{T}}\outerproduct{c'd'}{a'b'}+h.c,

(82)

represent Turing machine evolution on
\mathbf{0}\oplus\mathcal{H}.
We then let
H\coloneqq H_{TM}+H^{\prime}_{TM}.
Note that
[H_{TM},H^{\prime}_{TM}]=0.
We also define

F\coloneqq\sum_{a\in Q\cup\Gamma}\outerproduct{a'}{a}+h.c.

(83)

Next, we slightly modify
A_{i}.

Let
A_{i}\coloneqq p\outerproduct{a_2}{a_2}_{i}+q\Pi^{\prime}_{i}
, where
p,q
are parameters we will choose.
With these modifications, we compute the expectation of
\mathcal{A}_{N}
for the microcanonical ensemble and Gibbs ensembles as special cases of any ensemble which is analytic in
H
.

Lemma 6.2
.

Consider any
\rho\propto f(H)
where
f
is analytic and
\rho
is unit trace. Then,

\traceOp[\mathcal{A}_{N}\rho]=\frac{q}{2}+p\traceOp[\outerproduct{a_2}{a_2}_{1}\rho].

(84)

Proof.

First, note that
[F,H]=0.
As
\rho\propto f(H)
for some analytic function
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R},
it follows that
[\rho_{\textsf{MC}},F]=0
by the existence of a power series expansion of
f;
we note that this holds for arbitrary functions
f
whose domain contains the spectrum of
H,
but this is a bit more involved to prove.
Therefore, as
F^{2}=I,

\displaystyle\traceOp[\Pi^{\prime}_{i}\rho]

\displaystyle=\traceOp[\Pi^{\prime}_{i}F\rho F]

\displaystyle=\traceOp[F\Pi^{\prime}_{i}F\rho]

\displaystyle=\traceOp[\Pi_{i}\rho]

\displaystyle=\traceOp[(I-\Pi^{\prime}_{i})\rho].

(85)

Thus, as
\rho
is unit-trace,
\traceOp[\Pi^{\prime}_{i}\rho]=1/2.

Hence, we have

\displaystyle\traceOp[\mathcal{A}_{N}\rho]

\displaystyle=\frac{p}{N}\sum_{i}\left(\traceOp[\outerproduct{a_2}{a_2}_{i}\rho]+q\traceOp[\Pi^{\prime}_{i}\rho]\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{q}{2}+p\traceOp[\outerproduct{a_2}{a_2}_{1}\rho].

(86)

where we have used the fact that
\rho
is translationally invariant since
H
is translationally invariant, which follows from the same logic as showing that
[\rho,F]=0
above.
∎

Applying
Lemma

6.2
to the microcanonical state we have

Corollary 6.3
.

\traceOp[\mathcal{A}_{N}\rho_{\textsf{MC}}]=\frac{q}{2}+p\traceOp[\outerproduct{a_2}{a_2}_{1}\rho_{\textsf{MC}}].

(87)

Likewise, applying
Lemma

6.2
to the Gibbs state we have

Corollary 6.4
.

\traceOp[\mathcal{A}_{N}\rho_{\textsf{G}}]=\frac{q}{2}+p\traceOp[\outerproduct{a_2}{a_2}_{1}\rho_{\textsf{G}}].

(88)

6.3
A quantum polynomial time reduction from
\mathsf{FSHalt}
to

8
Discussion

We have shown there exist Hamiltonians for which, given an initial state and an observable to measure, predicting the long-time average of the observable is computationally intractable even for a quantum computer.
An immediate consequence of this is that, assuming
\mathsf{PSPACE}
does not collapse to a lower (easy) complexity class, there is no “easy” way of generically determining whether a given Hamiltonian and initial state thermalizes.
This should be compared to the eigenstate thermalization hypothesis, which states that Hamiltonians satisfying certain conditions on their matrix elements should thermalize.
Hence, either checking whether a Hamiltonian satisfies the conditions of the ETH on its matrix elements must not be computationally easy, or the ETH must not be a full characterization of all Hamiltonians which thermalize.

We briefly discuss here the similarities and differences between our construction and that of
[
23
]
. Primarily, their work focuses on systems in the thermodynamic limit (i.e.
N\rightarrow\infty)
, while we focus on finite sized systems. While their result applies to almost-uniform product state inputs, our hardness construction is for non-uniform product state inputs in order to satisfy the promise of inverse-exponentially-separated eigenvalues for the eigenvectors with support on the input state. We take advantage of this relaxation to change the Turing machines used, as we do not need to decode the input to the URTM. We also make use of a more concise local Hilbert space for our hardness proof of
\mathsf{FSRelax}
, although we currently require greater local dimension to prove the hardness of
\mathsf{FSTherm(MC)}
.
Finally, as their result shows undecidability, they do not need to prove containment for their decision problem.
Our contribution in this work also includes
\mathsf{PSPACE}
algorithms to compute the long time observable average, as well as the microcanonical expectation values, and thereby decide
\mathsf{FSTherm(MC)}
.

There are several limitations to our work.
In our tuning construction, we showed hardness for a restricted family of microcanonical states, with energy windows having inverse-logarithmic width ratio with the Hamiltonian norm. This is a technical restriction which arises due to the complexity of preparing the microcanonical state. In addition, in our proof of
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-containment of
\mathsf{FSTherm(MC)}
, we assumed that
w=\Omega(\frac{1}{\polyOp(N)})
. In future work, we hope to show
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-completeness for the more general microcanonical state, with narrower energy windows.
Our tuning construction also requires large local Hilbert space dimension. We hope to refine this result in future versions of this work. A hardness result with low local dimension would make our results much more applicable to ‘natural’ Hamiltonians.

Further Work

While in
Section

4
we showed that
\mathsf{FSTherm(MC)}
is contained in
\mathsf{PSPACE}
for both the Gibbs ensemble and the microcanonical ensemble with greater than inverse-polynomial width of the energy window, our hardness proof in
Section

6
only applies to the microcanonical ensemble with an energy window
w=\Omega(\left\lVert H\right\rVert/\sqrt{\logOp(N)})
. Further, the hardness proof relies on a quantum polynomial time reduction as opposed to a more standard classical polynomial time reduction. In future work, we hope to show hardness under the more standard reduction, as well as for most general ensembles.
In
Section

6.4
, we presented a conjecture under which
\mathsf{FSTherm(Gibbs)}
is
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-hard. If this conjecture is true, then combined with
Lemma

5.7
,
\mathsf{FSTherm(Gibbs)}
is
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-complete (and using only a classical polynomial time reduction). In future work, we hope to verify this conjecture.
Our argument in
Section

6.4
that there is a classical polynomial time reduction from
\mathsf{FSHalt}
to
\mathsf{FSTherm(Gibbs)}
(if our conjecture is true) makes use of the fact that in 1D for constant
\beta
, there exists a polynomial time classical algorithm to compute Gibbs expectation values
[
48
]
. This algorithms make use of the locality of
H
and exponential decay of correlations to construct matrix product operators for Gibbs states. In our tuning construction in
Section

6.2
to prove hardness of
\mathsf{FSTherm(MC)}
, which relies on computing local observables, we do not make use of the additional structure of
H
when estimating local observables of
\rho_{\textsf{MC}}
. One possible strategy to make the reduction from
\mathsf{FSHalt}
to
\mathsf{FSTherm(MC)}
classical rather than quantum could be to make use of this structure to show that local observables of
\rho_{\textsf{MC}}
can be estimated efficiently classically. Such an approach may show that
\mathsf{FSTherm(MC)}
is indeed
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-complete.

One direction for future work is investigating complexity of different formulations of thermalization and equilibration. An example of a notion of thermalization and ensembles that is not captured by our work is equilibration of integrable systems to generalized Gibbs ensembles
[
51
,
52
]
. An additional open question is related to thermalization of subsystems. For example, in a system with two parts
A,B
, with dynamics generated by a hamiltonian
H=H_{A}+H_{B}+H_{AB}
, we may like to know under which conditions and inverse temperatures
\beta
the behavior of subsystem
A
is captured by the Gibbs ensemble
\propto e^{-\beta H_{A}}
.

An alternative route to further research is to consider the reasons why Hamiltonians do not thermalize from a complexity-theoretic perspective.
Some relevant thermalization-inhibiting phenomena include many-body localization (MBL) and quantum scars.
While the complexity of simulating systems with MBL been studied previously
[
53
,
54
]
, the authors of this work do not know of any results concerning the complexity of determining MBL from a description of the Hamiltonian.

Two additional obvious and interesting paths are as follows: finding constructions with smaller local Hilbert space dimensions, and proving a complexity result without the assumption on the gap between eigenvalues. Recent work has shown the presence of ultraslow relaxation in systems based on the complexity of word problems, with small local dimension
[
55
]
. In future work we hope to explore whether similar techniques can be used to yield results on the hardness of thermalization but with smaller local dimension.
