Clustering in Varying Metrics
Deeparnab Chakrabarty, Jonathan Conroy, Ankita Sarkar

Abstract
We introduce the aggregated clustering problem, where one is given T T instances of a center-based clustering task over the same n n points, but under different metrics. The goal is to open k k centers to minimize an aggregate of the clustering costs—e.g., the average or maximum—where the cost is measured via k k -center/median/means objectives. More generally, we minimize a norm Ψ \Psi over the T T cost values. We show that for T ≥ 3 T\geq 3 , the problem is inapproximable to any finite factor in polynomial time. For T = 2 T=2 , we give constant-factor approximations. We also show W[2]-hardness when parameterized by k k , but obtain f ⁡ ( k , T ) ​ poly ​ ( n ) f(k,T)\mathrm{poly}(n) -time 3-approximations when parameterized by both k k and T T . When the metrics have structure, we obtain efficient parameterized approximation schemes (EPAS). If all T T metrics have bounded ε \varepsilon -scatter dimension, we achieve a ( 1 + ε ) (1+\varepsilon) -approximation in f ⁡ ( k , T , ε ) ​ poly ​ ( n ) f(k,T,\varepsilon)\mathrm{poly}(n) time. If the metrics are induced by edge weights on a common graph G G of bounded treewidth 𝗍𝗐 \mathsf{tw} , and Ψ \Psi is the sum function, we get an EPAS in f ⁡ ( T , ε , 𝗍𝗐 ) ​ poly ​ ( n , k ) f(T,\varepsilon,\mathsf{tw})\mathrm{poly}(n,k) time. Conversely, unless (randomized) ETH is false, any finite factor approximation is impossible if parametrized by only T T , even when the treewidth is 𝗍𝗐 = Ω ⁡ ( poly ​ log ⁡ n ) \mathsf{tw}=\Omega(\mathrm{poly}\log n) .

Clustering problems such as the
k

In its simplest form, the objective is to “open”

Such problems have been extensively studied over the past 50 years

In this paper, we consider clustering problems
when the distance function underlying the metric space can change over time

New England town, and the distances are travel times over the road network in the town. On one hand, travel times may be quite different in January and June given the weather differences over the months.
On the other hand, one can’t hope to open different locations in different seasons. Our decision ought to take into account the different costs over time, for example by selecting a set of facilities that minimizes the average cost over the year. Although this sounds a natural question,
to our knowledge what happens to the complexity of clustering with changing metrics hasn’t been studied so far.

One may be tempted to simply perform clustering on a single metric space defined by average distances over all months. However, this does not work: the cost of a client at each timestep is computed by take a minimum over a set of facilities, and this minimum is not preserved when we take an average.

General Metrics.

Open Directions

We end this section mentioning some open directions for future study. If one observes the metrics we use to prove our hardness results, we notice that
they “change a lot” as one moves from
d_{t}
to
d_{t+1}
. Can one obtain positive algorithmic results if this change is bounded? Concretely, assume

d_{t}
is the shortest path metric on
G_{t}=(V,E_{t})
and
d_{t+1}
is the same on
G_{t+1}=(V,E_{t+1})
. What if we forced
|E_{t}\Delta E_{t+1}|\leq O(1)
?
Again taking the road-network example, these could lead to certain road-closures or road-building, but in a single period perhaps not too many edges are added or deleted.
We do not know any lower bound for this version, nor do we know how to design algorithms. Structurally, this seems to require an understanding of how shortest paths change with insertion/deletion of edges
which may be of independent interest.

Another interesting, but slightly less concrete, direction is to explore
online
algorithms where an algorithm is allowed to change the centers but the distance metric is revealed only after the algorithms decision is made. Can one design “low-regret” algorithms as in
[
Zin03
]
?
The sum-aggregate objective is in fact the benchmark with which regret is measured, and our results show that approximating this benchmark for general metrics is hard. With this in view, what would be a good definition of regret?

Coming back to aggregate clustering, a concrete open problem is the following. The aforementioned hardness when
G
is a star actually has
F\neq C
, but the case of
V=C=F
is also interesting.
Are there
f(T)\cdot\mathrm{poly}(n,k)
approximation or even exact algorithms for
\Psi
-aggregated
k
-supplier/median problem when
G
is a tree? We believe this is an interesting generalization of
k
-median problem on trees and worthy of exploration.

Theorem 3

(Hardness of approximation when
T\geq 3
)
.

For any homogeneous aggregator
\Psi
and any
z\in\mathbb{N}\cup\left\{\infty\right\}

\Psi

Proof.

We show that it is NP-hard to decide whether the optimum value is finite or infinite for any
\Psi

(k,z)
-clustering problem.
We reduce from the (perfect) 3D Matching problem where, recall, we are given a

where
|V_{1}|=|V_{2}|=|V_{3}|
any every hyperedge

intersects every
V_{t}
exactly once. The goal is to decide whether or not there exists a

(pairwise disjoint subset)
M\subseteq E

of hyperedges which spans all vertices. Equivalently,
|M\cap e|=1
for all
e\in E
.

Given such a 3D Matching instance
(V_{1}\uplus V_{2}\uplus V_{3},E)

T=3

G
with vertex set

•

Suppose this clustering instance admits a

•

Suppose

. We know that

This reweighting scheme essentially follows from a much more general result by Schaefer

We adapt this lemma to our setting.

3.1
3
-approximation for Aggregate

Let

\mathrm{cost}_{\infty}(d_{t};\mathtt{OPT})=\max_{j\in C}d_{t}(j,\mathtt{OPT})

Let us denote

\mathrm{poly}(n)

and
\mathtt{opt}_{2}
. We now describe an algorithm, very similar to the one in
[
HS86
]
, which returns a subset
\mathtt{ALG}
of facilities
with
\mathrm{cost}_{\infty}(d_{t};\mathtt{ALG})\leq 3\mathtt{opt}_{t}
for

-approximation for the aggregate
k
-supplier problem for the

\Psi

For
t\in\{1,2\}
, define
B_{t}(a,r)=\left\{b\in F\cup C:d_{t}(a,b)\leq r\right\}
. We run the Hochbaum-Shmoys
[

with parameter
\mathtt{opt}_{t}
.
To briefly describe this: (i) initially all clients are “uncovered”, (ii) pick an arbitrary
uncovered client

j\in C
and add it to a set
R_{t}
of representatives, define

B_{t}(j,2\mathtt{opt}_{t})\cap C
to be
j
’s “children”, and mark
j
and all its children as “covered”, and (iii) continue the above till all clients covered. The main observations, which are easy to check using triangle inequality and the fact that
\mathtt{opt}_{t}
’s were correct guesses, are the following for
t\in\{1,2\}

•

The collection of balls
\left\{B_{t}(j,\mathtt{opt}_{t})\cap F\right\}_{j\in R_{t}}
are pairwise disjoint.

•

For any
j\in R_{t}
,
B_{t}(j,\mathtt{opt}_{t})\cap\mathtt{OPT}\neq\emptyset
. The above two imply
|R_{t}|\leq k
.

•

For every
v\in C
,
d_{t}(v,R_{t})\leq 2\mathtt{opt}_{t}
.

We now construct a solution
\mathtt{ALG}
of size
\leq k
such that for any
t\in\{1,2\}
and any
j\in R_{t}
, we have
\mathtt{ALG}\cap B_{t}(j,\mathtt{opt}_{t})\neq\emptyset
;
that is,
\mathtt{ALG}
hits every ball in the above two collections. This can be solved using
matroid intersection
since there are only two collections of disjoint balls.
Once we have such an
\mathtt{ALG}
, it is easy to see using triangle inequality that, for any
t\in\{1,2\}
,
d_{t}(v,\mathtt{ALG})\leq d_{t}(v,j)+d_{t}(j,\mathtt{ALG})\leq 3\mathtt{opt}_{t}
where
j
was

R_{t}
.

To give more details on how to find
\mathtt{ALG}
, for
t\in\{1,2\}
define the partition
\mathcal{P}_{t}
of
F
formed by
\left\{B_{t}(j,\mathtt{opt}_{t})\cap F\right\}_{j\in R_{t}}\cup Z_{t}
, where
Z_{t}
are all the facilities of
F
not in any ball.
We define the “budget” of each part to be

— this is the number of facilities
\mathtt{ALG}
can “leave out” — and set the budget of

to be

.
We say
S\subseteq F
is independent in partition matroid

if it picks at most budget from each part; we seek the set
S\subseteq F
of the largest cardinality which is independent in both partition matroids.
We return
\mathtt{ALG}:=F\setminus S
.
By design,
\mathtt{ALG}
hits every
B_{t}(j,\mathtt{opt}_{t})
for
t\in\{1,2\}
and
j\in R_{t}
. Since

is a candidate

which is in the intersection of the two partition matroids,

|\mathtt{ALG}|=|F|-|S|\leq|F|-|F\setminus\mathtt{OPT}|=|\mathtt{OPT}|\leq k
.

In sum, we can find a solution
\mathtt{ALG}
such that
\mathrm{cost}_{\infty}(d_{t};\mathtt{ALG})\leq 3\mathtt{opt}_{t}
for
t\in\{1,2\}
, and thus
\Psi(\mathrm{cost}_{\infty}(d_{1};\mathtt{ALG}),\mathrm{cost}_{\infty}(d_{2};\mathtt{ALG}))\leq 3\Psi(\mathtt{opt}_{1},\mathtt{opt}_{2})

This completes the proof of the following theorem.

Theorem 12

\mathrm{poly}(n)

\Psi
-aggregate
k

To obtain an constant approximation for the

At a high level, such an issue arises when one studies the

\gamma_{j}^{\left(t\right)}:=\min_{i\notin F_{j}^{t}}d_{t}(i,j)

Fact 15

For every

•

Suppose

Lemma 16

Rounding to Half-integral Solution.
We are now ready to describe the algorithm which is encapsulated in the following lemma.

Lemma 18
.

There is a polynomial time algorithm that yields a half-integral solution
(\hat{x}_{1},\hat{x}_{2},\hat{y})
of
LP
s.t.
\forall t\in\left\{1,2\right\}
,
\sum_{j\in\mathsf{Reps}_{t}}\left\lvert\mathsf{child}_{t}(j)\right\rvert\sum_{i\in F}d_{t}(i,j)\hat{x}_{t}(i,j)\leq 6\cdot\sum_{v\in C}C_{t}(v)

Our goal now becomes to find a half-integral
\hat{y}\in\left\{0,\frac{1}{2},1\right\}^{F}
so that, for each
t\in\left\{1,2\right\}
,
T_{t}(\hat{y})\leq 6\cdot\sum_{v\in C}C_{t}(v)
.
If we are able to do so, then by
Lemma

we would be done.
To find such a half-intergral solution, we define the following polytope and linearization of
T_{t}
’s.
The polytope
\mathcal{P}
has variables
(z,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{F\cup\mathsf{Reps}_{1}\cup\mathsf{Reps}_{2}}

\max

\mathcal{P}
ensure that

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}:=\Bigg\{(z,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{F\cup\mathsf{Reps}_{1}\cup\mathsf{Reps}_{2}}:

\displaystyle z(F)\leq k;\,\forall t\in\left\{1,2\right\},\forall j\in\mathsf{Reps}_{t},

\displaystyle z(B_{j}^{t})\geq\frac{1}{2},\,z(G_{j}^{t})+\lambda_{t}(j)\geq 1\Bigg\}

The following follows from the presence of only “two partitions” plus noting the

Claim 19
.

\mathcal{P}
has half-integral extreme points.

Proof.

Consider the constraint matrix
M
of
\mathcal{P}
. Since the constant terms in the constraints are half-integral, it suffices
[
HK56
]
to show
that

is totally unimodular (TU). Indeed,

looks

\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c}\hbox{\multirowsetup$A$}&I\\
&0\end{array}\right]

where we have the

z

corresponding to the
\lambda_{t}
-variables.

The dimension of

is
|\mathsf{Reps}_{1}|+|\mathsf{Reps}_{2}|
. The matrix

is the incidence matrix of two laminar systems — in fact, it is two partitions
coarsened by the universe. Such a system is TU
[
Swa16
]
. A TU matrix padded with columns with at most one

in them remains TU.
∎

Over
\mathcal{P}
, we can then consider the

of the following linearization of the

\forall t\in\left\{1,2\right\},\forall j\in\mathsf{Reps}_{t}

\displaystyle W_{t}(z,\lambda_{t}):=\sum_{j\in\mathsf{Reps}_{t}}\left\lvert\mathsf{child}_{t}(j)\right\rvert W_{t}(z,\lambda_{t},j)\,.

The following claim encapsulates that we can find an extreme point of (any polytope)

which is a
2

simultaneously
in a sense made clear below.
This allows us to get the desired

Claim 20
.

Consider a polytope
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{m}
, linear functions
W_{1},W_{2}:\mathbb{R}^{m}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, and a point
p\in\mathcal{P}
.
There is a polynomial time algorithm which returns an extreme point
\hat{p}
of
\mathcal{P}
such that

W_{t}(\hat{p})\leq 2W_{t}(p)
for
t\in\{1,2\}
.

Proof.

By Carathéodory’s theorem
[
Car11
,
Ste13
]
, we can write
p
as a linear combination of at most
(m+1)
extreme points of
\mathcal{P}
.
More precisely, we can find, in polynomial time, a subset
\mathcal{E}
of extreme points such that
|\mathcal{E}|\leq m+1
and
p=\sum_{q\in\mathcal{E}}\mu_{q}\cdot q

for some
\mu_{q}
’s which form a probability distribution

. We claim that one of the
q\in\mathcal{E}
is the desired point, and we can find which one by enumeration.
To see why, observe that for
t\in\{1,2\}
,
\EXP_{\hat{p}\sim D_{p}}[W_{t}(\hat{p})]=W_{t}(p)
, and so by Markov,

\Pr_{\hat{p}\sim D_{p}}[W_{t}(\hat{p})>2W_{t}(p)]<\frac{1}{2}
. So, by union bound
\Pr_{\hat{p}\sim D_{p}}[\exists t\in\{1,2\}:~W_{t}(\hat{p})>2W_{t}(p)]<1
implying

\Pr_{\hat{p}\sim D_{p}}[W_{t}(\hat{p})\leq 2W_{t}(p),~~t\in\{1,2\}]>0
which proves the above claim.
∎

Rounding to Integral solution.
We round
(\hat{x}_{1},\hat{x}_{2},\hat{y})
to an integral solution, losing a constant factor, and this is very similar to the ideas in
[
CGTS02
,
Swa16
]
; here also we follow the latter’s framework.

Below are the main ideas, and the details are in
Section

3.4
.

The first step is another
filtering
step. For
t\in\{1,2\}
and
j\in\mathsf{Reps}_{t}
, define
S^{t}_{j}:=\{i\in F~:~\hat{x}_{t}(i,j)>0\}
and let
\hat{C}_{t}(j):=\sum_{i\in F}\hat{x}_{t}(i,j)d_{t}(i,j)
;
note that
\sum_{j\in\mathsf{Reps}_{t}}|\mathsf{child}_{t}(j)|\hat{C}_{t}(j)\leq 6\sum_{v\in C}C_{t}(v)
. Now we define a subset of “super-representatives”: we pick
\ell\in\mathsf{Reps}_{t}
with smallest
\hat{C}_{t}(\ell)

and add it to
\mathsf{Super}_{t}
removing every
j
with
S^{t}_{j}\cap S^{t}_{\ell}\neq\emptyset
from consideration. In the end, we have
S^{t}_{j}
’s pairwise disjoint for
\mathsf{Super}_{t}
’s and each
j\in\mathsf{Reps}_{t}
shares
a facility with a super-representative in
\mathsf{Super}_{t}
.

Next, as in the rounding to the half-integral case, we define a particular linear function which works as a proxy for
\hat{C}_{t}(j)
. To define this proxy, for every
j\in\mathsf{Reps}_{t}
call the two facilities in
S^{t}_{j}

primary and secondary in ascending order of distance (duplicating facilities if needed). For
t\in\{1,2\}
and
j\in\mathsf{Reps}_{t}
, let
\ell\in\mathsf{Super}_{t}
be the super-representative which
j
shares a facility with, and define

A_{t}(z,j):=\begin{cases}\sum_{i\in S_{\ell}^{t}}d_{t}(i,j)z(i)\hfill\text{if }\mathsf{prim}_{t}(j)\in S_{\ell}^{t}\\
\sum_{i\in S_{\ell}^{t}}d_{t}(i,j)z(i)+\left(d_{t}(\mathsf{prim}_{t}(j),j)-d_{t}(\mathsf{sec}_{t}(j),j)\right)\cdot z(\mathsf{prim}_{t}(j))&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

It’s instructive to think of all
z(i)
’s as
1/2
; in that case
A_{t}(z,j)
is either the average of
j
’s distance to
\ell
’s facilities when
j
’s closest facility is shared with
\ell
,
or it is the average of
j
’s distance to it closest facility and distance to the other facility that
\ell
goes to whom
j
doesn’t share. The importance of the above function is captured in a couple of observations: (A) for any
t
and any
j\in\mathsf{Reps}_{t}
, we can upper bound
A_{t}(\hat{y},j)\leq 2\hat{C}_{t}(j)
, that is,
A_{t}(\hat{y},j)
isn’t too bad a proxy, and perhaps more usefully (B) given any
z
such that
z(S^{t}_{\ell})=1
for all
super representatives
, we can find an assignment of any
j\in\mathsf{Reps}_{t}
to facilities with connection cost
\leq A_{t}(z,j)
. These two facts, and the fact that we can minimize linear functions over the intersection of two partition matroids (defined by the
S^{t}_{\ell}
’s for
t\in\{1,2\}
and
\ell\in\mathsf{Super}_{t}
), gives us an
O(1)
-approximation. This is because the relevant polytope has integral extreme points (see
28
).
Observation (A) holds because we can charge
j
’s journey to
\ell
’s “other facility” to
\ell
’s connection cost which is smaller than
j
’s cost by design; this uses half-intergrality. The details are in
Lemma

27
. Observation (B) is immediate if
\mathsf{prim}_{t}(j)\in S_{\ell}^{t}
or if
z(\mathsf{prim}_{t}(j))=0
; otherwise, if
z(\mathsf{prim}_{t}(j))=1
then
j
will travel to its closest facility while
A_{t}(z,j)

will be average of two number which are larger than the minimum. The statement is in fact true for fractional
z
’s as well and the proof is in
Lemma

26
.

All in all, we get the following theorem; the factor
28
could
definitely be improved, but perhaps no better than
8
using these methods.

Theorem 21
.

When
T=2
, there is a polynomial time
28
-approximation algorithm for sum-aggregate
k
-median.

Remark 22

(Generalizing to arbitrary norm aggregators)
.

In the above proof, we note that the solution
S
we return has the property that
\mathrm{cost}(d_{t};S)\leq 28\sum_{j\in C}C_{t}(j)
, for
t\in\{1,2\}
.
This allows us to generalize the above theorem for any aggregator
\Psi
which is a norm. Instead of a linear program, we would have a convex program.
More precisely, we have variables
C_{1},C_{2}
where
C_{t}:=\sum_{j\in C}C_{t}(j)
and the objective would minimize
\Psi(C_{1},C_{2})
.
By the property of our rounding and the homogeneity of
\Psi
, we would get that
\Psi(\mathrm{cost}(d_{1};S),\mathrm{cost}(d_{2};S))\leq 28\Psi(C_{1},C_{2})\leq 28\mathtt{opt}
.

Remark 23

(Generalizing
(k,z)
-clustering)
.

The above theorem focused on the
k
-median problem. However, the same methodology also gives an
O(1)
-approximation for the
\Psi
-aggregated
(k,z)
-clustering problem
due to the fact that we only use triangle inequalities over “bounded number of hops”. This is a folklore observation (see, for instance, Footnote 1 in
[
CS19
]
).
To obtain this, first we replace
d_{t}(i,j)
with
d_{t}(i,j)^{z}
in the linear/convex program.
Next, we use the “relaxed triangle inequality”, which follows from Lemma A.1 in
[
MMR19
]
, that says if we have
r+1
points
a_{1},\ldots,a_{r+1}
, then
d(a_{1},a_{r+1})^{z}\leq r^{z-1}\sum_{i=1}^{r}d(a_{i},a_{i+1})^{z}
.
In our proof above, we never invoke the triangle inequality on more than
4
points, and thus, everything goes through with a “hit” of
3^{z-1}
.
Note, though, that the definition of the proxy function

would have
3
replaced by
3^{z}
.
In the end, we would get a
O(1)^{z}
approximation to the sum of the
z
th powers, and since we take the
z
th root, this gives a
O(1)
-approximation.

Lemma 26

FPT(

Theorem 30
.

For any homogenous aggregator
\Psi
, any
z\in\mathbb{N}\cup\left\{\infty\right\}
, and any constant error parameter
\varepsilon>0
,
there is an algorithm that computes a
(3+\varepsilon)
-approximate solution to

\Psi
-aggregate
(k,z)
-clustering
in time
O(\varepsilon^{-1}k)^{O(kT)}\cdot 2^{O(k^{2}T^{2})}\cdot\mathrm{poly}(n)

Proof.

We adapt an existing FPT
3
-approximation
[
CAGK+19
, Section 2.1]
for standard
(k,z)
-clustering (i.e.
T=1
).
Fix an error parameter
\varepsilon>0
.

In our aggregate cluster instance, let
C
denote the client set and let
F
denote the facilities. For each scenario
t\in[T]
(defined by a metric
d_{t}
and a client weights
w_{t}
), we first compute a
coreset

C_{t}\subseteq C

of size
O(\varepsilon^{-2}k\log n)

[
FL11
]
; that is, obtain a new client set
C_{t}
and new weights
w^{\prime}_{t}
on
C_{t}
with the property that for any subset of facilities
S\subseteq F
,

\sum_{u\in C_{t}}w^{\prime}_{t}(u)\cdot d_{t}(u,S)\in(1\pm\varepsilon)\cdot\sum_{v\in C}w_{t}(v)\cdot d_{t}(v,S)\,.

Henceforth we work with the coresets
C_{t}
rather than the entire client set
C
in each metric.

Let
\mathtt{OPT}\subseteq F
be the optimal solution to the aggregate clustering instance, with (unknown) centers
o_{1},\dots,o_{k}
. For an
i\in[k]
and
t\in[T]
, let
C_{i,t}
be the cluster served by
o_{i}
under

. Define the client
\ell_{i,t}
to be the closest client (in the coreset

) to
o_{i}
, that is
d_{t}(o_{i},C_{i,t})=d_{t}(o_{i},\ell_{i,t})
, and define
r_{i,t}\coloneqq d_{t}(o_{i},\ell_{i,t})
.

For each scenario
t\in[T]
and index
i\in[k]
, we can guess (by enumeration) the client
\ell_{i,t}
in time
\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{1}{O(\eps^{-2}k \log n)}{k}
.
We can’t guess
r_{i,t}
exactly, but we can guess it up to the closest power of
(1+\varepsilon)
: we assume WLOG (up to a
1+\varepsilon
loss in the approximation factor) that the aspect ratio of
d_{t}
is
\mathrm{poly}(n)
, and so bucketing pairwise distances of
d_{t}
by powers of
(1+\varepsilon)
reduces the number of possible distances to
O(\log_{1+\varepsilon}n)=O(\varepsilon^{-1}\log n)
. Overall, in time
(\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{1}{O(\eps^{-2}k \log n)}{k}\cdot O(\varepsilon^{-1}\log n)^{k})^{T}
, we can guess each
\ell_{i,t}
and
r_{i,t}
. By
39
, this overhead is
O(\varepsilon^{-1}k)^{O(kT)}\cdot 2^{O(k^{2}T^{2})}\cdot\mathrm{poly}(n)

=O_{\varepsilon,k,T}(1)\cdot\mathrm{poly}(n)
.

Suppose our guesses are correct. For each
i\in[k]
, consider the set
F_{i}
containing the facilities that are within distance

of
\ell_{i,t}
in every metric
d_{t}
, i.e.
F_{i}:=\cap_{t=1}^{T}B_{t}(\ell_{i,t},r_{i,t})\cap F
. If our guesses are correct, then
F_{i}
is non-empty since
o_{i}
lies in this intersection. Let our solution
\mathtt{ALG}
consist of an arbitrary
f_{i}\in F_{i}
for each
i\in[k]
. For a client

by definition of

; and also
d_{t}(\ell_{i,t},f_{i})\leq(1+O(\varepsilon))\cdot r_{i,t}=(1+O(\varepsilon))\cdot d_{t}(\ell_{i,t},o_{i})
. Using these, we get

\displaystyle d_{t}(v,\mathtt{ALG})

\displaystyle\leq d_{t}(v,f_{i})\leq d_{t}(v,o_{i})+d_{t}(o_{i},\ell_{i,t})+d_{t}(\ell_{i,t},f_{i})

\displaystyle\leq d_{t}(v,o_{i})+(2+O(\varepsilon))\cdot d_{t}(o_{i},\ell_{i,t})\leq(3+O(\varepsilon))\cdot d_{t}(v,o_{i})

for every

and
t\in[T]
. Since the
(k,z)
-clustering cost and the aggregator

are both homogeneous functions, this yields a
(3+O(\varepsilon))
-approximation.

Rescaling
\varepsilon
by a constant yields the desired result.
∎

Simpler algorithm in a special case

In the special case of
k
-supplier, we have a simpler
(3+\varepsilon)
-algorithm that doesn’t utilize coresets, and runs in
O(\varepsilon^{-O(T^{2})})\cdot O(k)^{O(kT)}\cdot\mathrm{poly}(n)

Guessing radii.
We use the same bucketing as above, which contributes a
(1+\varepsilon)
factor and allows us to assume that there are

Now let
\mathtt{opt}_{t}:=\mathrm{cost}(d_{t};S)
for each
t\in[T]
, and note that each

is some

\mathtt{opt}_{t}
in each metric. Then for each
v\in V
and

Guessing clusters.
For each

, we run Plesník’s
[

]

. These Plesník subroutines yield, for each

•

Clusters

•

A center

•

For any two distinct clusters

Thereafter, for each

Suppose the (unknown) centers in
\mathtt{OPT}
are

. With

For each

3

Definition 31

[covering]

[

A key component of the (

respectively. We assume we know the cost
\mathtt{opt}
of the optimal solution
6
6

6

•

An “inner distance” vector

.
(This distance represents a requirement that we open some center in

The DP entry

represents the minimum cost paid by clients in

\mathcal{T}_{b}

\boldsymbol{o}
and while obeying the restriction that we must open centers in

to satisfy
\boldsymbol{i}
. Formally,

\mathrm{DP}[b,k^{\prime},\boldsymbol{o},\boldsymbol{i}]\coloneqq\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq F\cap V_{b}:|S|=k^{\prime}\\
\forall t\in[T],\forall x\in X,d_{t}(x,S)=i_{t,x}\end{subarray}}\mathrm{cost}(V_{b},S,\boldsymbol{o})

(1)

where

\mathrm{cost}(b,S,\boldsymbol{o})\coloneqq\sum_{t\in[T]}\sum_{c\in V_{b}}w_{t}(c)\cdot\min\left(d_{t}(c,S),\min_{x\in X_{b}}({d_{t}(c,x)+o_{t,x}})\right).

(2)

If no such

exists then we set

Objective.

Observe that if
b
is the root bag of the tree decomposition, then the cost of the optimal aggregate solution

is precisely

Base Cases.
Our base case is when

Correctness of recurrence.

We show that recurrence (
3
) correctly outputs the value of the DP cell according to the definition (
1
).

First we show that
\eqref{eq:dp-rec}\leq\eqref{eq:dp-def}
, by observing that
for any
\mathrm{DP}[b,k^{\prime},\boldsymbol{o},\boldsymbol{i}]
, there is a choice of consistent

Indeed, let

be the set of centers minimizing (
1
), such that
\mathrm{cost}(b,S,\boldsymbol{o})=\mathrm{DP}[b,k^{\prime},\boldsymbol{o},\boldsymbol{i}]
. Let
\ell
and

be the children of bag

, and let

and

.
We define variables as follows:

•

Set

.

•

and

•

1+\varepsilon
approximation in FPT time.
We have described an exact algorithm that runs in time
n^{O(T\cdot\mathsf{tw})}
. We now sketch how to improve the running time, if one allows
1+\varepsilon
approximation.
By a standard reduction, we can assume that that aspect ratio (ie, the ratio of the largest distance to smallest distance) in each metric is
\mathrm{poly}(n,\varepsilon^{-1})
at the cost of
1+\varepsilon
approximation. Note that in the exact algorithm, we duplicated some vertices and connected them by 0-weight edges, to enforce the assumption that the facilities and clients appear only in leaf bags — now that we want bounded aspect ratio, we instead add
\frac{\varepsilon}{\mathrm{poly}(n)}
-weight edges, again losing only a
1+\varepsilon
factor in the approximation. We then round every distance in each metric to the closest power
(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{\log n})
; now there are
O(\log_{1+\frac{\varepsilon}{\log n}}n)=O(\varepsilon^{-1}\log^{2}n)
distinct distances rather than
O(n^{2})
. In our DP, rather than guessing
o_{t,x}
and
i_{t,x}
from the set of
O(n^{2})
distances, we guess the distances only to the closest power of
(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{\log n})
. When checking consistency in
Definition

38
, we modify the conditions [Constraint
\boldsymbol{i}
is satisfied] and [Assumptions
\boldsymbol{o}^{\ell},\boldsymbol{o}^{r}
are justified] to only require that the equalities hold up to a
1+\frac{\varepsilon}{\log n}
approximation factor.
The overall running time is
(\frac{\log n}{\varepsilon})^{O(T\cdot\mathsf{tw})}\mathrm{poly}(n)
, which is bounded above by
\varepsilon^{-O(T^{2}\cdot\mathsf{tw}^{2})}\mathrm{poly}(n)
by
39
.

Each recursive call of the DP accumulates
1+\frac{\varepsilon}{\log n}
error due to the rounding. To be more precise, if the recursive evaluation of the DP cell at bag
b
has value
\kappa
, and the bag
b
has height
h
in the tree
\mathcal{T}
, then one can show (by an inductive argument) that there is a valid set of centers
S\subseteq V_{b}
with cost at most
(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{\log n})^{h}\cdot\kappa
according to the definition (
1
). As the height of the tree decomposition
\mathcal{T}
is
O(\log n)
, overall we get a solution with approximation ratio
1+O(\varepsilon)
. Rescaling
\varepsilon
by a constant factor yields the claim.

Generalizing to
(k,z)
-clustering and
k
-supplier.
We can generalize the DP to work with stochastic aggregate
(k,z)
-clustering for any
z
. In the definition of the DP, we now use

\mathrm{cost}(b,S,\boldsymbol{o})\coloneqq\sum_{t\in[T]}\left(\sum_{c\in V_{b}}w_{t}(c)\cdot\min(d_{t}(c,S),\min_{x\in X_{b}}(d_{t}(c,x)+o_{t,x}))^{z}\right)^{1/z}

so that the DP entry at a bag
b
gives us the
(k,z)
-clustering cost of the subtree rather than
k
-median cost. We modify the recurrence: rather than just taking a sum of the two children DP entries, we set

\mathrm{DP}[b,k^{\prime}\boldsymbol{o},\boldsymbol{i}]\leftarrow\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}({k^{\prime}}^{\ell},\boldsymbol{o}^{\ell},\boldsymbol{i}^{\ell},{k^{\prime}}^{r},\boldsymbol{o}^{r},\boldsymbol{i}^{r})\\
\text{consistent with }\\
(k^{\prime},\boldsymbol{o},\boldsymbol{i})\end{subarray}}\left(\left(\mathrm{DP}[\ell,{k^{\prime}}^{\ell},\boldsymbol{o}^{\ell},\boldsymbol{i}^{\ell}]\right)^{z}+\left(\mathrm{DP}[r,{k^{\prime}}^{r},\boldsymbol{o}^{r},\boldsymbol{i}^{r}]\right)^{z}\right)^{1/z}.

We use this recurrence because in
(k,z)
-clustering we have the property that
\mathrm{cost}(b,S,\boldsymbol{o})=\left(\mathrm{cost}(\ell,S_{\ell},\boldsymbol{o}^{\ell})^{z}+\mathrm{cost}(r,S_{r},\boldsymbol{o}^{r})^{z}\right)^{1/z}
, rather than the property that
\mathrm{cost}(b,S,\boldsymbol{o})=\mathrm{cost}(\ell,S_{\ell},\boldsymbol{o}^{\ell})+\mathrm{cost}(r,S_{r},\boldsymbol{o}^{r})
that we used for
k
-median. The proof of correctness of the
(k,z)
-clustering recurrence is similar to that of the
k
-median recurrence, once we replace the linearity of
k
-median cost with the new property just described. Our
1+\varepsilon
approximate DP also works for
(k,z)
-clustering, because distorting distances by a factor
1+\varepsilon
only changes
\mathrm{cost}(b,S,\boldsymbol{o})
by at most a factor
1+\varepsilon
.

By setting
z=\infty
, we recover the aggregate
k
-supplier problem. To be precise, our definition of the DP becomes

\mathrm{cost}(b,S,\boldsymbol{o})\coloneqq\sum_{t\in[T]}\max_{c\in V_{b}}\left(w_{t}(c)\cdot\min(d_{t}(c,S),\min_{x\in X_{b}}(d_{t}(c,x)+o_{t,x}))\right)

and

\mathrm{DP}[b,k^{\prime}\boldsymbol{o},\boldsymbol{i}]\leftarrow\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}({k^{\prime}}^{\ell},\boldsymbol{o}^{\ell},\boldsymbol{i}^{\ell},{k^{\prime}}^{r},\boldsymbol{o}^{r},\boldsymbol{i}^{r})\text{ consistent}\\
\text{with }(k^{\prime},\boldsymbol{o},\boldsymbol{i})\end{subarray}}\max\left(\mathrm{DP}[\ell,{k^{\prime}}^{\ell},\boldsymbol{o}^{\ell},\boldsymbol{i}^{\ell}],\mathrm{DP}[r,{k^{\prime}}^{r},\boldsymbol{o}^{r},\boldsymbol{i}^{r}]\right).

Correctness follows from the fact that
\mathrm{cost}(b,S,\boldsymbol{o})=\max(\mathrm{cost}(\ell,S_{\ell},\boldsymbol{o}^{\ell}),\mathrm{cost}(r,S_{r},\boldsymbol{o}^{r}))
.

Fact 39
.

For any
f(n)=n^{o(1)}
and any variable
\tau\geq 1
, the function
(f(n))^{\tau}
is bounded above by
O_{\tau}(1)+n
.
For example, when
f(n)=\log n
, we have
(\log n)^{\tau}\leq 2^{\tau^{2}}+n
.

Proof.

Let
g(n)=\log f(n)
, and note
g(n)=o(\log n)
.
If
\tau
satisfies
k\tau\leq\log n
, then clearly
f(n)^{\tau}\leq n
. On the other hand, if
\tau>\frac{\log n}{k}
, then the fact that
g(n)=o(\log n)
implies that
\tau
grows as a function of
n
, and so
f(n)^{\tau}=O_{\tau}(1)
.

We now give an example that provides explicit bounds for the case
f(n)=\log n
.
If
\tau\leq\log\log n
, then
(\log n)^{\tau}\leq 2^{(\log\log n)^{2}}\leq n
. Otherwise, if
\tau>\log\log n
, then
(\log n)^{\tau}=2^{\tau\log\log n}\leq 2^{\tau^{2}}
.
∎

Observation 40
.

Suppose we are given an instance

-clustering, defined by metrics

, client set
C
, and facility set

. We construct an instance
