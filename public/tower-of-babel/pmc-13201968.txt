Developing a de novo vertebral artery (VA) dissecting aneurysm (VADA), defined by serial imaging demonstrating a previously normal VA, is rare. Its development after occlusion of the contralateral VA due to VADA is even more uncommon, with few cases reported in the literature.1–9 The formation of a contralateral VADA has been attributed to hemodynamic alterations following occlusion of the affected VA, likely occurring in the setting of an underlying vulnerability of the arterial wall. However, the precise pathophysiological mechanisms remain unclear.

Management of a VADA after contralateral VA occlusion is particularly challenging because parent artery occlusion is often not feasible. In such situations, reconstructive endovascular strategies may be required to preserve critical branches while securing the lesion.

Herein, we describe a radiographically confirmed case of a patient who developed de novo VADA shortly after spontaneous occlusion of the contralateral VA due to VADA and was successfully treated with stent-assisted coiling (SAC) while preserving the posterior inferior cerebellar artery (PICA). We also review previously reported cases, mostly from East Asia, and discuss their clinical course and treatment strategies. This report provides insight into the possible hemodynamic mechanisms underlying the development of de novo VADA.

Illustrative Case

A 52-year-old male with mild hypertension and a history of smoking presented with a 2-week history of persistent occipital headache and posterior neck pain. The patient had no history suggestive of connective tissue disorders, trauma, or chiropractic manipulation. No clinical features indicative of an underlying arteriopathy were identified. Genetic evaluation was not performed. Neurological examination revealed no focal deficits. MRI (Fig. 1A and B) and digital subtraction angiography (DSA) (Fig. 1C and D) demonstrated a right VADA that had already progressed to complete occlusion of the affected arterial segment. At that time, the contralateral left VA appeared normal on both MRI and DSA without evidence of dissection or aneurysmal change. Because the patient remained neurologically stable and the affected VA was already occluded, conservative management was selected. The patient’s symptoms gradually improved, and the patient was discharged without neurological deficit.

Six weeks after onset, the patient developed recurrent occipital pain similar to the initial symptoms. Follow-up MRI revealed a newly formed dissecting aneurysm involving the left VA (Fig. 1E and F). Subsequent MRI and DSA performed 2 weeks later demonstrated further enlargement of the lesion, measuring approximately 10 mm in maximum diameter (Fig. 1G−I). Detailed angiographic evaluation revealed that the origin of the PICA was involved in the lesion but was located on the opposite side of the dissecting cavity (Fig. 1J).

Because parent artery trapping would have compromised PICA flow and the contralateral VA had already been occluded, a reconstructive endovascular strategy was selected to preserve the parent artery and maintain adequate posterior circulation. Ten weeks after the initial onset, SAC was performed using the jailing technique. Dual antiplatelet therapy (aspirin and clopidogrel) was administered perioperatively. Systemic heparinization was performed during the procedure to maintain an activated clotting time of approximately 250–300 seconds. Five detachable coils were deployed within the dissecting aneurysm, achieving a volume embolization ratio of 18.4% (Fig. 1K and L). The PICA was successfully preserved with satisfactory flow through the stented segment (Fig. 1M and N).

Postoperatively, the patient developed transient diplopia and mild sensory disturbance, which resolved spontaneously within several days. MRI demonstrated several small punctate infarctions in the left pons and cerebellum; however, these lesions were clinically silent and did not result in permanent neurological deficits. The patient recovered well and was discharged without neurological deficits. Follow-up DSA at 1 year demonstrated complete obliteration of the aneurysm with preservation of the parent artery and PICA, and no evidence of recurrence.

Discussion

De novo VADA developing after contralateral VA occlusion is rare. Although new dissecting lesions after therapeutic or spontaneous occlusion of a major artery have been described, radiographically confirmed de novo formation with clear documentation of a previously normal artery remains uncommon. In the present case, serial imaging demonstrated a normal contralateral VA at the initial presentation, followed by rapid development of a new dissecting aneurysm within several weeks.

A review of previously reported cases (Table 1) showed a mean patient age of approximately 47 years,1–9 consistent with the typical age distribution of cervical artery dissection.10 The relatively young age and paucity of conventional vascular risk factors suggest that intrinsic factors, including hemodynamic stress and arterial wall vulnerability, may contribute to pathogenesis. Most cases demonstrated a left-to-right lesion sequence, with a slight tendency for occlusion of the dominant VA followed by VADA of the nondominant VA. Although the significance of this observation remains unclear, this pattern may partly reflect the higher prevalence of left VA in the general population,11 including East Asian populations.12 Moreover, occlusion of the dominant VA may lead to greater hemodynamic alterations compared with occlusion of the nondominant VA. While the initial VADA most often presented with subarachnoid hemorrhage, the second VADA was often asymptomatic, possibly because the second lesions were incidentally detected during routine radiological follow-up after treatment or occlusion of the initial VADA. The interval between the initial and contralateral VADAs varied widely, ranging from several hours to several years. These findings suggest that not only acute hemodynamic stress but also long-term vascular remodeling may contribute to the development of contralateral VADA.

Regarding treatment, in most cases, the initial VADA was treated with parent artery occlusion, including surgical or endovascular trapping. This treatment strategy is a commonly accepted approach for VADA, since the development of a contralateral VADA is generally not anticipated at the time of the initial treatment. Earlier reports often selected conservative management of the second VADA, with sometimes unfavorable outcomes,3–5 likely because treatment options were limited when the contralateral VA had already been occluded. In recent years, however, the introduction of reconstructive endovascular techniques such as SAC has allowed lesion treatment while preserving the parent artery. As shown in Table 1, the reported cases demonstrate the evolution of treatment strategies over time and the corresponding improvement in clinical outcomes.6–9 Although flow diverter devices were not commercially available in Japan at the time of treatment in this case, they have since emerged as a reconstructive option for VADAs.13,14 As summarized in Table 2, both SAC and flow diversion represent reconstructive strategies with overlapping indications, and their selection depends on anatomical considerations and clinical context.

Our search of the English-language literature showed that all reported cases originated from East Asia. In addition to the cases included in Table 1, de novo contralateral VADA without occlusion of the initial VADA,15 simultaneous bilateral VADAs and contralateral VADA growth after endovascular trapping of one side,16 and transient caliber enlargement of the contralateral VA after endovascular trapping of the affected side VADA have also been reported.17 These cases also originated in Japan and Korea. This geographic clustering may reflect the higher prevalence of intracranial artery dissection itself in East Asian populations.10

Mechanistically, occlusion of one VA may lead to compensatory redistribution of blood flow, resulting in increased wall shear stress in the contralateral VA. Such abrupt changes in cerebral blood flow can induce vascular remodeling and aneurysm formation.18–20 This hemodynamic mechanism has also been suggested by a previous report of transient contralateral VA enlargement after VA occlusion.17 Although quantitative hemodynamic analyses were not performed in the reported cases, the development of contralateral VADA shortly after VA occlusion in some cases supports a flow-related mechanism. However, the possibility of an inherent vulnerability of the arterial wall cannot be excluded; such intrinsic fragility, together with potential mechanical triggers such as cervical manipulation, may also contribute to the development of VA dissection.21 In the present case, however, no history of trauma or chiropractic manipulation was identified.

Observations

The present report describes a radiographically confirmed case of de novo VADA developing shortly after spontaneous occlusion of the contralateral VA due to VADA. Serial imaging demonstrated a previously normal artery that subsequently developed a dissecting aneurysm within several weeks. Because the PICA originated adjacent to the lesion and the contralateral VA had already been occluded, SAC was selected as a reconstructive endovascular strategy to preserve the parent artery and maintain posterior circulation flow. Review of previously reported cases suggests that earlier cases were frequently managed conservatively and were associated with unfavorable outcomes, whereas more recent cases treated with reconstructive endovascular techniques have demonstrated improved clinical results.

Lessons

This report is limited by its single-case nature and the absence of quantitative hemodynamic analysis. Nevertheless, the clear radiographic chronology provides insight into this rare condition. Our findings demonstrated that de novo VADA may develop rapidly after contralateral VA occlusion, likely due to altered hemodynamic stress. Careful imaging surveillance is therefore essential, particularly in East Asian populations. When parent artery occlusion is not feasible, reconstructive endovascular strategies such as SAC may represent an effective flow-preserving treatment option.
