A Pile of Shifts I: Crossed Products
Shelley Hebert, Slawomir Klimek, Matt McBride

Abstract
. We discuss C ∗ -algebras associated with several different natural shifts on the Hilbert space of the s s -adic tree, that is, the tree of balls in the space of s s -adic integers.



1.
Introduction

It is well known that Cantor sets could be treated as the boundary of a directed rooted tree
[
16
]
via Michon correspondence.
Consider a
s
-regular infinite directed rooted tree called the
s
-adic tree. We adopt here the following definition of such a tree: all vertices have the out-degree equal to
s
and all vertices except the root have the in-degree equal to one (the in-degree of the root is equal to zero).
For such a tree, the boundary is the set of infinite out-paths starting at the root. Those can be naturally identified with the ring of
s
-adic integers
{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}
, see equation (
2.1
).

The vertices
\mathcal{V}
of the
s
-adic tree can be thought of as corresponding to balls in
{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}
, with ball inclusion defining edges of the
s
-adic tree, which can be considered as a coarse grained approximation of the space of
s
-adic integers. The corresponding Hilbert space
\ell^{2}(\mathcal{V})
carries a straightforward, diagonal representation of the algebra of continuous functions on
{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}
.
In previous papers
[
9
]
,
[
11
]
,
[
12
]
of some of the authors, a version of a unilateral shift on
\ell^{2}(\mathcal{V})
was used to construct a “forward” derivative leading to a spectral triple over
C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
.
However, there are other interesting shifts on that Hilbert space corresponding to different arithmetic and geometric aspects of the space of
s
-adic integers.

The purpose of this paper and the companion paper
[
8
]
is to study natural C
∗
-algebras in
\ell^{2}(\mathcal{V})
generated by
C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
along with specific shifts. Those algebras are interesting examples of C
∗
-algebras with connections to number theory and combinatorics, a subject of much interest
[
4
]
,
[
18
]
.

Given a C
∗
-algebra
A
and an endomorphism there is a number of ways to construct a new C
∗
-algebra, desirably an extension of
A
, called the crossed product. Often an intermediate extension procedure is needed to enlarge the algebra
A
up to a better behaved “coefficient” C
∗
-algebra. This paper serves as a source of interesting examples of such constructions and we determine how each of these shift algebras can be identified with a crossed product by an endomorphism, in fact a monomorphism with a hereditary range. In the companion paper we delve deeper into the structure of the shift algebras and compute their K-Theory.

This paper is organized as follows. In the next section we introduce four shifts on the
s
-adic tree and the corresponding C
∗
-algebras.
Then we discuss coefficient algebras and the related crossed products. Particularly useful here is the reference
[
13
]
which considers, in general, C
∗
-algebras generated by a given concrete C
∗
-algebra and a partial isometry. What makes our considerations special is the existence of what we call a “gauge action”, a symmetry that leaves
C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
invariant.

After the preliminaries, each algebra is studied in turn in its own dedicated section. While the algebras are quite different, there are number of similarities.
Propositions
4.2
,
5.2
,
6.5
and
7.8
describe, in each case, generators of the minimal coefficient algebra extension of
C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
.
Also, analogous Propositions
4.3
,
5.3
,
6.6
and
7.7
, each describes the corresponding gauge invariant subalgebra through a “Toeplitz” map.
The main results contained in Theorems
4.4
,
5.4
,
6.7
and
7.9
describe each algebra as a crossed product by a monomorphism with hereditary range. They are a straightforward consequence of the results directly preceding the theorems and general considerations in Section 3.

2.
Trees and Shifts

2.1.
s
-adic Integers

For
s\in{\mathbb{N}}
with
s\geq 2
, the space of
s
-adic integers
{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}
is defined as a set consisting of infinite sums:

{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}=\left\{x=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}x_{j}s^{j}:0\leq x_{j}\leq s-1,j=0,1,2,\ldots\right\}\,.

(2.1)

We call the above expansion of
x
its
s
-adic expansion. Note that
{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}
is isomorphic with the countable product
\prod{\mathbb{Z}}/s{\mathbb{Z}}
and so, when the product is equipped with the Tychonoff topology, it is a Cantor set. It is also a metric space with the usual norm
|\cdot|_{s}
which is defined as follows:

|0|_{s}=0\textrm{ and, if }x\neq 0,|x|_{s}=s^{-n},

where
x_{n}
is the first nonzero term in the above
s
-adic expansion. The only possible distances are powers of
s
and
0
giving
{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}
unusual properties specific to non-Archimedean metrics, see
[
17
]
. In particular, every point in a ball is its center, two balls are either disjoint or one is contained in the other. Additionally, any ball of radius
s^{-n}
is a union of precisely
s
disjoint balls of radius
s^{-n-1}
. We also have that locally constant functions are dense in
C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
.

Moreover,
{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}
is an Abelian ring with unity with respect to addition and multiplication with a carry and we can identify
{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}
with the projective limit:

{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}\cong\lim_{\longleftarrow}{\mathbb{Z}}/s^{n}{\mathbb{Z}}\,,

where the projections in the above system are given by reductions modulo powers of
s
.
Using the division algorithm modulo
s
repeatedly, we can associate an
s
-adic expansion to any integer and so
{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}
naturally contains a copy of
{\mathbb{Z}}
. For more details, see
[
10
]
.

2.2.
Hilbert Spaces and Shifts

We can naturally associate an infinite directed rooted tree to the ring
{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}
called the
s
-adic tree. The set of vertices
\mathcal{V}
of this tree is defined to be the set of all balls in
{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}
. The set of edges of the
s
-adic tree is defined as follows: the edges are all ordered pairs of balls
(v,v^{\prime})
such that
v^{\prime}\subsetneqq v
, and there is no nontrivial ball between
v
and
v^{\prime}
. The root is the ball of radius one, which is
{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}
, and the structure of the balls implies that all vertices have out-degree equal to
s
and all vertices except the root have in-degree equal to one, while the in-degree of the root is equal to zero.

In fact, by choosing a particular integer center of a ball, we see that any ball is of the form:

\left\{y\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}:|y-x|_{s}\leq\frac{1}{s^{n}}\right\}

where
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
and
0\leq x<s^{n}
. Consequently we can write
\mathcal{V}
in the following way:

\mathcal{V}=\{(n,x):n=0,1,2,\ldots,0\leq x<s^{n}\}\,.

Throughout this paper we use the Hilbert space
H=\ell^{2}(\mathcal{V})
with canonical basis
\{E_{(n,x)}\}
. Let
f\in C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
and define a bounded operator
M_{f}:H\to H
by

M_{f}E_{(n,x)}=f(x)E_{(n,x)}\,.

Notice, due to the density of
{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
in
{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}
, that we have that

\|M_{f}\|=\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}}{|f(x)|}=\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}}{|f(x)|}=||f||_{\infty},

and so
f\mapsto M_{f}
is a faithful representation of the C
∗
-algebra
C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
.

There are four main shifts acting on
H
that are the focus of this paper and which we call the Bunce-Deddens shift, the Hensel shift, the Bernoulli shift, and the Serre shift. We use the word “shift” as those operators resemble the usual unilateral shift. All four shifts are defined on
H
with formulas given on the basis elements as follows:

(1)

The Bunce-Deddens Shift:

U:H\to H
via

UE_{(n,x)}=E_{(n+1,\,x+1)}\,.

(2)

The Hensel Shift:

V:H\to H
via

VE_{(n,x)}=E_{(n+1,\,sx)}\,.

(3)

The Bernoulli Shift:

S:H\to H
via

SE_{(n,x)}=\frac{1}{s}\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}E_{(n+1,\,sx+j)}\,.

(4)

The Serre Shift:

W:H\to H
via

WE_{(n,x)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{s}}\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}E_{(n+1,\,x+js^{n})}\,.

Let us comment on those definitions. The Bunce-Deddens shift is well-defined, since if
x<s^{n}
then
x+1<s^{n+1}
. This particular shift uses only the additive structure of
{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}
. For the Hensel shift, if
x<s^{n}
, then
sx<s^{n+1}
and this shift uses only the multiplicative structure of
{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}
. The Bernoulli shift is another arithmetic shift, using both the additive and multiplicative structures of
{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}
: if
x\leq s^{n}-1
and
j\leq s-1
then
sx+j\leq s^{n+1}-1
.
The Serre shift, used in
[
9
]
,
[
11
]
,
[
12
]
, is essentially a shift along the edges of the
s
-adic tree. It is well defined since if
x\leq s^{n}-1
and
j\leq s-1
then
x+js^{n}\leq s^{n+1}-1
.

A computation yields their respective adjoints, where care has to be taken to find the kernels of those adjoints:

(1)

U^{*}E_{(n,x)}=\left\{\begin{aligned} &E_{(n-1,\,x-1)}&&\textrm{ if }n\geq 1,0<x\leq s^{n-1}\\
&0&&\textrm{ if }n=0\textrm{ or }x=0\textrm{ or }s^{n-1}<x<s^{n}\end{aligned}\right.

(2)

V^{*}E_{(n,x)}=\left\{\begin{aligned} &E_{(n-1,\,x/s)}&&\textrm{ if }n\geq 1,\,s\mid x\\
&0&&\textrm{ if }n=0\textrm{ or }s\nmid x\end{aligned}\right.

(3)

S^{*}E_{(n,x)}=\left\{\begin{aligned} &E_{(n-1,\,(x-x\textrm{ mod }s)/s)}&&\textrm{ if }n\geq 1\\
&0&&\textrm{ if }n=0\end{aligned}\right.

(4)

W^{*}E_{(n,x)}=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{1}{\sqrt{s}}E_{(n-1,\,x\textrm{ mod }s^{n-1})}&&\textrm{ if }n\geq 1\\
&0&&\textrm{ if }n=0.\end{aligned}\right.

It is easy to verify that

U^{*}U=V^{*}V=S^{*}S=W^{*}W=I,

hence each one is an isometry. For each of these four shifts
\mathcal{J}\in\{U,V,S,W\}
we have the corresponding C
∗
-algebras, which are the main object of study of this paper:

A_{\mathcal{J}}:=C^{*}(\mathcal{J},M_{f}:f\in C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s}))\,.

There is an important structure shared by all these algebras which, by analogy with graph algebras
[
18
]
, we call a gauge action. For
\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}}
, consider the following strongly continuous one-parameter family
\{\mathcal{U}_{\theta}\}
of unitary operators on
H
defined by

\mathcal{U}_{\theta}E_{(n,x)}=e^{2\pi in\theta}E_{(n,x)}.

(2.2)

We define a one-parameter family
\{\rho_{\theta}\}
of automorphisms, the
gauge action

\rho_{\theta}:A_{\mathcal{J}}\to A_{\mathcal{J}}
by the formula

\rho_{\theta}(a)=\mathcal{U}_{\theta}a\mathcal{U}_{\theta}^{-1}.

Then on generators of
A_{\mathcal{J}}
we have

\rho_{\theta}(\mathcal{J})=e^{2\pi i\theta}\mathcal{J}\,,\quad\rho_{\theta}(\mathcal{J}^{*})=e^{-2\pi i\theta}\mathcal{J}^{*}\,,\quad\textrm{and}\quad\rho_{\theta}(M_{f})=M_{f}\,.

It easily follows by an approximation argument that for every
a\in A_{\mathcal{J}}
the map
\theta\mapsto\rho_{\theta}(a)
is continuous.

3.
Coefficient algebras with a gauge action

3.1.
Coefficient algebras

The notion of a coefficient algebra was introduced in
[
13
]
. A review of this concept, with additional material suitable for our purposes, is the main theme of this section.

Given a Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}
, let
A\subseteq B({\mathcal{H}})
be a C
∗
-subalgebra of
B({\mathcal{H}})
containing the identity
I
and let
{\mathcal{J}}\in B({\mathcal{H}})
be an isometry,
{\mathcal{J}}^{*}{\mathcal{J}}=I
. We use the notation
A_{\mathcal{J}}
for the C
∗
-algebra generated by
A
and
{\mathcal{J}}
. We call
A
a
coefficient algebra
of the C
∗
-algebra
A_{\mathcal{J}}
if
A
and
{\mathcal{J}}
satisfy the following conditions:

\alpha(a):={\mathcal{J}}a{\mathcal{J}}^{*}\in A,

and

\beta(a):={\mathcal{J}}^{*}a{\mathcal{J}}\in A,

for all
a\in A
.

It is easy to see that
\alpha
is an endomorphism of
A
and we have the following properties:

\beta\alpha(a)=a,\,\alpha\beta(a)=\alpha(I)a\alpha(I),\textrm{ and }\beta(I)=I.

Moreover, the map
\beta:A\to A
is linear, continuous, positive and has the “transfer” property:

\beta(\alpha(a)b)=a\beta(b),

and thus is an example of Exel’s transfer operator
[
6
]
. Because we have
\alpha\beta(a)=\alpha(I)a\alpha(I)
it is a complete transfer operator of
[
1
]
and in particular a non-degenerate transfer operator of
[
6
]
, i.e. it satisfies
\alpha(\beta(I))=\alpha(I)
. Also notice that if the algebra
A
is commutative, or more generally if
\alpha(I)
is in the center of
A
, then
\beta
is also an endomorphism:

\beta(a)\beta(b)=\mathcal{J}^{*}a\mathcal{J}\mathcal{J}^{*}b\mathcal{J}=\mathcal{J}^{*}a\alpha(I)b\mathcal{J}=\mathcal{J}^{*}ab\alpha(I)\mathcal{J}=\mathcal{J}^{*}ab\mathcal{J}\mathcal{J}^{*}\mathcal{J}=\mathcal{J}^{*}ab\mathcal{J}=\beta(ab).

Clearly
\alpha(I)
is a projection and notice that
\textrm{Ran}(\alpha)
, the range of
\alpha
, is a hereditary subalgebra of
A
and we have

\textrm{Ran}(\alpha)=\alpha(I)A\alpha(I).

3.2.
Coefficient algebras and crossed products

Suppose that
A
is a unital C
∗
-algebra and
\alpha
is an endomorphism of
A
. Stacey in
[
19
]
(and Murphy in
[
14
]
) defines the crossed product
A\rtimes_{\alpha}{\mathbb{N}}
as the universal unital C
∗
-algebra generated by
a
’s in
A
, and an isometry
{\mathcal{J}}
, subject to the relation:

\alpha(a)={\mathcal{J}}a{\mathcal{J}}^{*},\ a\in A.

Given a unital C
∗
-algebra
A
, an endomorphism
\alpha:A\to A
, and a transfer operator
\beta
, the Exel’s crossed product is the universal C
∗
-algebra generated by a copy of
A
and element
{\mathcal{J}}
subject to the relations:

(1)

{\mathcal{J}}a=\alpha(a){\mathcal{J}}

(2)

{\mathcal{J}}^{*}a{\mathcal{J}}=\beta(a)

(3)

(Redundancy Condition) If for
a\in\overline{A\alpha(A)A}
and
k\in\overline{A\mathcal{J}\mathcal{J}^{*}A}
we have
ab{\mathcal{J}}=kb{\mathcal{J}}
for all
b\in A
then
a=k
.

Interestingly, see for example the appendix of
[
7
]
, if
\alpha
is a monomorphism with hereditary range then both seemingly quite different concepts of crossed products by endomorphisms coincide if we take
\beta
to be the transfer operator given by the equation:

\beta(a):=\alpha^{-1}(\alpha(I)a\alpha(I)).

Suppose again that
A
a coefficient algebra of the C
∗
-algebra
A_{\mathcal{J}}
. Using the above commutation relations one can arrange the polynomials in
{\mathcal{J}}
,
{\mathcal{J}}^{*}
and
a
so that the
a
’s are in front of the powers of
{\mathcal{J}}
and the powers of
{\mathcal{J}}^{*}
are in front of the
a
’s. This leads to the following observation (
[
13
]
, Proposition 2.3):
the vector space
\mathcal{F}(A,{\mathcal{J}})
consisting of finite sums

x=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}{\mathcal{J}}^{n}+\sum_{n<0}({\mathcal{J}}^{*})^{-n}a_{n}\,,

(3.1)

where
a_{n}\in A
, is a dense
*
-subalgebra of
A_{\mathcal{J}}
.
Additionally, using
{\mathcal{J}}^{n}={\mathcal{J}}^{n}({\mathcal{J}}^{*})^{n}{\mathcal{J}}^{n}
and its adjoint
one can always choose coefficients
a_{n}
such that

a_{n}=a_{n}{\mathcal{J}}^{n}({\mathcal{J}}^{*})^{n},\ n\geq 0\textrm{ and }a_{n}={\mathcal{J}}^{-n}({\mathcal{J}}^{*})^{-n}a_{n},\ n<0.

We say that
A_{\mathcal{J}}
satisfies the O’Donovan condition if for every
x\in\mathcal{F}(A,{\mathcal{J}})
we have:

||a_{0}||\leq||x||.

A key result (
[
13
]
Theorem 2.7) says that if
A_{\mathcal{J}}
satisfies the O’Donovan condition then the coefficients
a_{n}
above are unique and, more importantly,
A_{\mathcal{J}}
is isomorphic to the crossed product C
∗
-algebra
A\rtimes_{\alpha}{\mathbb{N}}
, see
[
2
]
and the appendix of
[
7
]
.

If the algebra
A
is commutative we get a stronger relation:

a{\mathcal{J}}={\mathcal{J}}\beta(a).

Also, the
*
-subalgebra
\mathcal{F}(A,{\mathcal{J}})
can be described as consisting of finite sums

x=\sum_{n\geq 0}{\mathcal{J}}^{n}b_{n}+\sum_{n<0}b_{n}({\mathcal{J}}^{*})^{-n}\,

with
b_{n}\in A
. Under the O’Donovan condition, the coefficients
b_{n}
above are again unique.

3.3.
Gauge action

In this and the following subsection we consider additional structure, not systematically explored in the existing literature, which we call a gauge action. For
\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}}
, assume that there is a one-parameter family
\{\mathcal{U}_{\theta}\}
of unitary operators on
{\mathcal{H}}
satisfying the following commutation properties:

\mathcal{U}_{\theta}\,a=a\,\mathcal{U}_{\theta}\,,\quad\mathcal{U}_{\theta}\mathcal{J}=e^{2\pi i\theta}\mathcal{J}\mathcal{U}_{\theta}\,

for all
a\in A
.
We define a one-parameter family
\{\rho_{\theta}\}
of automorphisms
\rho_{\theta}:B({\mathcal{H}})\to B({\mathcal{H}})
by the formula

\rho_{\theta}(a)=\mathcal{U}_{\theta}a\mathcal{U}_{\theta}^{-1}.

It follows that on generators of
A_{\mathcal{J}}
we have

\rho_{\theta}(\mathcal{J})=e^{2\pi i\theta}\mathcal{J}\,,\quad\rho_{\theta}(\mathcal{J}^{*})=e^{-2\pi i\theta}\mathcal{J}^{*}\,,\quad\textrm{and}\quad\rho_{\theta}(a)=a,\ \ a\in A.

(3.2)

Clearly the map
\theta\mapsto\rho_{\theta}(x)
is continuous for
x\in\mathcal{F}(A,{\mathcal{J}})
. It follows by an approximation argument that it is continuous for every
x\in A_{\mathcal{J}}

As a consequence of the existence of a gauge action we have an expectation

E:A_{\mathcal{J}}\to A_{\mathcal{J}}

given by the formula:

E(x):=\int_{0}^{1}\rho_{\theta}(x)\,d\theta.

(3.3)

Then, for a finite sum given by equation (
3.1
) we obtain

a_{n}=E(x({\mathcal{J}}^{*})^{n}),\ n\geq 0\textrm{ and }a_{n}=E({\mathcal{J}}^{-n}x),\ n<0.

In particular, we have:

||a_{0}||=||E(x)||\leq||x||,

so the existence of a gauge action implies the O’Donovan condition and so
A_{\mathcal{J}}\cong A\rtimes_{\alpha}{\mathbb{N}}
.

Consider now the range
\textrm{Ran}(E)
of
E
. On one hand, for
x\in\mathcal{F}(A,{\mathcal{J}})
we have
E(x)\in A
. It follows from the density of
\mathcal{F}(A,{\mathcal{J}})
and from the continuity of
E
that
\textrm{Ran}(E)=A
.
On the other hand, let
A_{\mathcal{J},\textrm{inv}}
be the invariant subalgebra of
A_{\mathcal{J}}
with respect to the automorphism
\rho_{\theta}
, that is

A_{\mathcal{J},\textrm{inv}}=\{a\in A_{\mathcal{J}}:\rho_{\theta}(a)=a\textrm{ for all }\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}}\}\,.

By the assumptions, we have
A\subseteq A_{\mathcal{J},\textrm{inv}}
. But for any
x\in A_{\mathcal{J}}
we have from the definition that
E(x)\in A_{\mathcal{J},\textrm{inv}}
so

A=\textrm{Ran}(E)=A_{\mathcal{J},\textrm{inv}}.

This observation is important for the topic we discuss next.

3.4.
Coefficient algebra extensions

As before, let
A\subseteq B({\mathcal{H}})
be a
*
-subalgebra containing the identity
I
of
B({\mathcal{H}})
and let
{\mathcal{J}}\in B({\mathcal{H}})
be an isometry,
{\mathcal{J}}^{*}{\mathcal{J}}=I
. Assume that there exists a gauge action satisfying equation (
3.2
), but we do not assume that
A
is a coefficient algebra of
A_{\mathcal{J}}
. We want to consider enlargements of
A
that form coefficients for
A_{\mathcal{J}}
.

We call a
*
-subalgebra
B\subseteq B({\mathcal{H}})
a coefficient algebra extension of
A
if
A\subseteq B
,
A_{\mathcal{J}}=B_{\mathcal{J}}
and
B
is a coefficient algebra of
B_{\mathcal{J}}
. The collection of coefficient algebra extensions of
A
is non-empty, since
A_{\mathcal{J}}
itself is such an extension and it is closed under arbitrary intersections. It follows that there exists the
minimal coefficient algebra extension
of
A
.

Notice that, from the properties of
\rho_{\theta}
, for every
a\in A_{\mathcal{J},\textrm{inv}}
we have:

\mathcal{J}^{*}a\mathcal{J}\in A_{\mathcal{J},\textrm{inv}}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\mathcal{J}a\mathcal{J}^{*}\in A_{\mathcal{J},\textrm{inv}}\,.

This means that
A_{\mathcal{J},\textrm{inv}}
is a coefficient algebra extension of
A
. In particular, we have the following general result, which allows us to realize each of the shift algebras of the previous section as a crossed product by a monomorphism with a hereditary range.

Proposition 3.1
.

There is an isomorphism of C
∗
-algebras:

A_{\mathcal{J}}\cong A_{\mathcal{J},\textrm{inv}}\rtimes_{{\alpha}_{\mathcal{J}}}{\mathbb{N}}\,,

where
{{\alpha}_{\mathcal{J}}}:A_{\mathcal{J},\textrm{inv}}\to A_{\mathcal{J},\textrm{inv}}
is an endomorphism, in fact a monomorphism with hereditary range, given by

{{\alpha}_{\mathcal{J}}}(a):=\mathcal{J}a\mathcal{J}^{*}.

Moreover,
A_{\mathcal{J},\textrm{inv}}
is the minimal coefficient algebra extension of
A
.

Proof.

We already mentioned that the existence of the gauge action implies the O’Donovan condition and thus
A_{\mathcal{J}}\cong A_{\mathcal{J},\textrm{inv}}\rtimes_{{\alpha}_{\mathcal{J}}}{\mathbb{N}}
. (Because
{{\alpha}_{\mathcal{J}}}
is a monomorphism with hereditary range, all major definitions of the crossed product by endomorphism coincide in this case.)

Let
B
be the minimal coefficient algebra extension of
A
. We observed above that the coefficient algebras coincides with the invariant subalgebras:

B=\textrm{Ran}(E)=B_{\mathcal{J},\textrm{inv}}=A_{\mathcal{J},\textrm{inv}}

as claimed.
∎

4.
The Bunce-Deddens Shift

4.1.
The Algebra
A_{U}

In this section we consider the first shift algebra

A_{U}=C^{*}(U,M_{f}:f\in C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})),

where the shift
U
is given on the basis elements of
H=\ell^{2}(\mathcal{V})
by
UE_{(n,x)}=E_{(n+1,\,x+1)}
. The corresponding maps on
A_{U}
are:

\alpha_{U}(x):=UxU^{*}\in A_{U},\textrm{ and }\beta_{U}(x):=U^{*}xU\in A_{U},

for
x\in A_{U}
.

We define the maps
\mathfrak{a}_{U}
and
\mathfrak{b}_{U}
on
C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
as follows:

\mathfrak{a}_{U}f(x)=f(x-1)\quad\textrm{and}\quad\mathfrak{b}_{U}f(x)=f(x+1)\,.

Both are automorphisms on
C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
and we have:

\mathfrak{a}_{U}(I)=\mathfrak{b}_{U}(I)=1\textrm{\ and\ }\mathfrak{a}_{U}\circ\mathfrak{b}_{U}=\mathfrak{b}_{U}\circ\mathfrak{a}_{U}=1\,.

The following observation relates the maps
\mathfrak{a}_{U}
and
\mathfrak{b}_{U}
with
U
and
M_{f}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

For any
f\in C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
, the operators
U
and
M_{f}
satisfy the following relations:

M_{f}U=UM_{\mathfrak{b}_{U}f}\quad\textrm{and}\quad UM_{f}U^{*}=M_{\mathfrak{a}_{U}f}UU^{*}\,.

Proof.

On the basis elements of
H
we have

M_{f}UE_{(n,x)}=M_{f}E_{(n+1,\,x+1)}=f(x+1)E_{(n+1,\,x+1)}

and

UM_{\mathfrak{b}_{U}f}E_{(n,x)}=U\mathfrak{b}_{U}f(x)E_{(n,x)}=f(x+1)UE_{(n,x)}=f(x+1)E_{(n+1,\,x+1)}.

This shows the first relation.
We also have that

\displaystyle UM_{f}U^{*}E_{(n,x)}

\displaystyle=UM_{f}\left\{\begin{aligned} &E_{(n-1,\,x-1)}&&\textrm{ if }n\geq 1,0<x\leq s^{n-1}\\
&0&&\textrm{ if }n=0\textrm{ or }x=0\textrm{ or }s^{n-1}<x<s^{n}\end{aligned}\right.

\displaystyle=f(x-1)\,U\left\{\begin{aligned} &E_{(n-1,\,x-1)}&&\textrm{ if }n\geq 1,0<x\leq s^{n-1}\\
&0&&\textrm{ if }n=0\textrm{ or }x=0\textrm{ or }s^{n-1}<x<s^{n}\end{aligned}\right.

\displaystyle=M_{\mathfrak{a}_{U}f}\,UU^{*}E_{(n,x)}\,.

∎

From Lemma
4.1
, and the fact that
U
is an isometry, it follows additionally that

UM_{f}=M_{\mathfrak{a}_{U}f}U\quad\textrm{and}\quad U^{*}M_{f}U=M_{\mathfrak{b}_{U}f}.

(4.1)

4.2.
The Coefficient Algebra of
A_{U}

The main goal is to identify
A_{U}
as a crossed product by an endomorphism. Notice however that
UC({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})U^{*}
is not generally contained in
C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
by Lemma
4.1
and so
C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
is not a coefficient algebra of
A_{U}
. Crossed products by endomorphisms work best for coefficient algebras and we show below how to enlarge
C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
to obtain such a coefficient algebra.

Define the mutually orthogonal projections
\{P_{n}\}
by

P_{0}=I-UU^{*}\textrm{ and }P_{n}=U^{n}P_{0}(U^{*})^{n}.

Below we show that the C
∗
-algebra

B_{U}:=C^{*}(M_{f},P_{n}:f\in C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s}),n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0})

is the smallest coefficient algebra of
A_{U}
containing
C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
.

Proposition 4.2
.

The algebra
B_{U}
is commutative and it is the minimal coefficient algebra extension of
C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
. Thus, we can identify
B_{U}
with the invariant algebra

A_{U,\textrm{inv}}=\{a\in A_{U}:\rho_{\theta}(a)=a\textrm{ for all }\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}}\}.

Proof.

From the relations of Lemma
4.1
, equations (
4.1
) and the fact that
\mathfrak{a}_{U}
and
\mathfrak{b}_{U}
are inverses of each other, it follows that
P_{n}
commutes with
M_{f}
for every
n
and
f
. This demonstrates that
B_{U}
is commutative.

Using formulas

\beta_{U}(P_{n})=U^{*}P_{n}U=P_{n-1}\textrm{ for }n\geq 1,\ \beta_{U}(P_{0})=0,\ \alpha_{U}(P_{n})=UP_{n}U^{*}=P_{n+1}\textrm{ for }n\geq 0

as well as

U^{*}M_{f}U=M_{\mathfrak{b}_{U}f},\ UM_{f}U^{*}=M_{\mathfrak{a}_{U}f}(I-P_{0}),

and commutativity of
B_{U}
, we see that
\beta_{U}
is a homomorphism and thus,
B_{U}
is a coefficient algebra extension of
C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
. Notice that, by definition,
P_{n}=\beta_{U}^{n}(I)-\beta_{U}^{n+1}(I)
must be in any coefficient algebra extension of
C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
so
B_{U}
must be the smallest such extension. Finally, we noticed in the previous section that in general the gauge invariant subalgebra is the smallest coefficient algebra extension.
∎

4.3.
The Structure of the Coefficient Algebra of
A_{U}

We can also describe the smallest coefficient algebra extension of
C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
more explicitly. In fact, we demonstrate below that the algebra
A_{U,\textrm{inv}}
is isomorphic to the tensor product
c({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0})\otimes C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
, where
c({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0})
is the space of convergent sequences.

We start with a quick summary of relevant information about tensor products of C
∗
-algebras, see for example
[
15
]
.

•

All Abelian C
∗
-algebras are nuclear.

•

If
A
is nuclear then for any C
∗
-algebra
B
there is only one C
∗
-norm on the algebraic tensor product of
A
and
B
. The corresponding completion is simply denoted by
A\otimes B
below.

•

If
X
and
Y
are compact Hausdorff spaces and
B
is any C
∗
-algebra then we have
C(X)\otimes C(Y)\cong C(X\times Y)
and
C(X)\otimes B\cong C(X,B)
.

Thus, we can view
c({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0})\otimes C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
as the space of uniformly convergent sequences of continuous functions on
{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}
:

c({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0})\otimes C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})\cong c({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0},C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s}))\cong\{F=(f_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}}:f_{n}\in C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s}),\ \lim_{n\to\infty}f_{n}\textrm{ exists}\}\,.

We use the following notation for the uniform limit:

C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})\ni f_{\infty}:=\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{n}.

Proposition 4.3
.

The map

c({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0},C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s}))\ni F\mapsto T_{U}(F):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(M_{f_{n}}-M_{f_{\infty}})P_{n}+M_{f_{\infty}}\in B_{U}=A_{U,\textrm{inv}}

(4.2)

is an isomorphism of C
∗
-algebras.

Proof.

Notice that for block diagonal operators the norm is equal to the supremum of the norms of the blocks. It follows that

\left\|\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(M_{f_{n}}-M_{f_{\infty}})P_{n}\right\|\leq\sup_{n}||M_{f_{n}}-M_{f_{\infty}}||=||f_{n}-f_{\infty}||_{\infty}<\infty,

and so the map
F\mapsto T_{U}(F)
is well defined.

Let
P^{\perp}
be the projection onto the subspace of
H
orthogonal to the ranges of all the
P_{n}
’s. Then we can write an alternative formula for
T_{U}(F)
as follows:

T_{U}(F):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}M_{f_{n}}P_{n}+M_{f_{\infty}}P^{\perp}.

Then, because all terms in the above formula commute and the projections are mutually orthogonal, we see that the map
F\mapsto T_{U}(F)
is a C
∗
-homomorphism. Additionally, the formula in equation (
4.2
) implies that the generators of
B_{U}
are in the range of
T_{U}
. Consequently, the range of
T_{U}
must be equal to
B_{U}
.

To show that the map
F\mapsto T_{U}(F)
is an isomorphism we compute the norm
||T_{U}(F)||
using the observation above about block diagonal operators. This requires to compute the norm of
M_{f_{n}}
restricted to the range of
P_{n}
.

A simple calculation using the formulas for
U
and
U^{*}
yields

\textrm{ Ran }P_{n}=\textrm{span}\,\{E_{(m+n,x+n)}:m=0,\,x=0\textrm{ or }m\geq 1,\,s^{m-1}<x<s^{m}\}.

As a consequence, we have the following observation regarding the range of
P_{n}
:

\displaystyle\{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}:\text{there exists }m\text{ so that }E_{(m,x)}\in\text{Ran\ }P_{n}\}

\displaystyle=\{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}:x\geq n,\,x\neq n+s^{m},\,m\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}\}.

Using this observation we have that

\displaystyle\|M_{f_{n}}|_{\textrm{Ran }P_{n}}\|

\displaystyle=\sup\left\{|f_{n}(x)|:x\text{\ such that there exists\ }m\text{\ so that\ }E_{(m,x)}\in\text{Ran\ }P_{n}\right\}

\displaystyle=\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}}|f_{n}(x)|=||f_{n}||_{\infty},

since the set
\{x:\text{there exists\ }m\text{\ so that\ }E_{(m,x)}\in\text{Ran\ }P_{n}\}
, described explicitly above, is dense
{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}
. Thus, we have

||T_{U}(F)||=\sup_{n}||f_{n}||_{\infty}

since the norm of the last term,
M_{f_{\infty}}P^{\perp}
, is no more than the above supremum.
It follows that
B_{U}
is isomorphic to
c({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0})\otimes C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
.
∎

4.4.
A_{U}
as a Crossed Product

Now that we have identified a coefficient algebra of
A_{U}
, the invariant subalgebra
B_{U}
isomorphic to
c({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0})\otimes C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
, we can use the general results from Section 3 to describe
A_{U}
as a crossed product by an endomorphism.

Consider the morphisms
\tilde{\alpha}_{U},\tilde{\beta}_{U}
on
c({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0})\otimes C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
given by

\tilde{\alpha}_{U}F(n,x)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &F(n-1,\,x-1)&&\textrm{if }n\geq 0\\
&0&&\textrm{if }n=0\end{aligned}\right.\quad\textrm{and}\quad\tilde{\beta}_{U}F(n,x)=F(n+1,\,x+1)\,.

A simple calculation shows that we have the following relations:

\alpha_{U}(T_{U}(F))=U^{*}T_{U}(F)U=T_{U}(\tilde{\beta}_{U}F)\quad\textrm{and}\quad\beta_{U}(T_{U}(F))=UT_{U}(F)U^{*}=T_{U}(\tilde{\alpha}_{U}F)\,.

The following main result of this section is now a direct consequence of Proposition
3.1
, Proposition
4.2
, Proposition
4.3
and the above formula. Clearly,
\tilde{\alpha}_{U}
is a monomorphism of
c({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0})\otimes C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
with hereditary range.

Theorem 4.4
.

We have the following isomorphism of C
∗
-algebras:

A_{U}\cong(c({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0})\otimes C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s}))\rtimes_{\tilde{\alpha}_{U}}{\mathbb{N}}\,.

The significance of the above result is that the crossed product on the right-hand side is a universal object, defined in terms of generators and relations, and thus can be studied by considering its convenient, concrete representations.

5.
The Hensel Shift

5.1.
The Algebra
A_{V}

In this section, we consider the algebra

A_{V}=C^{*}(V,M_{f}:f\in C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})),

where the Hensel shift
V
is given on the basis elements of
H
by
VE_{(n,x)}=E_{(n+1,\,sx)}
.
The corresponding maps on
A_{V}
are:

\alpha_{V}(x):=VxV^{*}\in A_{V},\textrm{ and }\beta_{V}(x):=V^{*}xV\in A_{V},

for
x\in A_{V}
.
The analysis turns out to be quite similar the Bunce-Deddens shift and in particular the gauge invariant coefficient algebra is also Abelian.

Consider also the following maps:
\mathfrak{a}_{V},\mathfrak{b}_{V}:C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})\to C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
given by

\mathfrak{a}_{V}f(x)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &f\left(\frac{x}{s}\right)&&\textrm{if }s|x\\
&0&&\textrm{else}\end{aligned}\right.\quad\textrm{and}\quad\mathfrak{b}_{V}f(x)=f(sx)\,.

It is routine to check that both
\mathfrak{a}_{V}
and
\mathfrak{b}_{V}
are endomorphisms of
C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
. We have that

(\mathfrak{b}_{V}\circ\mathfrak{a}_{V})f(x)=\mathfrak{b}_{V}(\mathfrak{a}_{V}f(x))=\mathfrak{a}_{V}f(sx)=f(x)\,,

and thus
\mathfrak{b}_{V}\circ\mathfrak{a}_{V}=I
. Additionally, we have

\mathfrak{b}_{V}(I)=I\textrm{ and }(\mathfrak{a}_{V}\circ\mathfrak{b}_{V})f=\mathfrak{a}_{V}(I)f.

Here

\mathfrak{a}_{V}(I)(x)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &1&&\textrm{if }s|x\\
&0&&\textrm{else}\end{aligned}\right.

is the characteristic function of the subset of
{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}
of elements divisible by
s
. It follows from that formula that the range of
\mathfrak{a}_{V}
is
\mathfrak{a}_{V}(I)C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
. Thus
\mathfrak{a}_{V}
is a monomorphism with hereditary range.

Lemma 5.1
.

Given
f\in C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
, then
V
and
M_{f}
satisfy the following relations:

M_{f}V=VM_{\mathfrak{b}_{V}f}\quad\textrm{and}\quad VM_{f}V^{*}=M_{\mathfrak{a}_{V}f}(I-P_{(0,0)})\,,

where
P_{(0,0)}
is the projection onto (the subspace generated by) the basis element
E_{(0,0)}
.

Proof.

We first apply
M_{f}V
to the basis elements of
H
:

M_{f}VE_{(n,x)}=M_{f}E_{(n+1,\,sx)}=f(sx)E_{(n+1,\,sx)}=VM_{\mathfrak{b}_{V}f}E_{(n,x)}\,.

Similarly we have

\displaystyle VM_{f}V^{*}E_{(n,x)}

\displaystyle=VM_{f}\left\{\begin{aligned} &E_{(n-1,\,x/s)}&&\textrm{if }n\geq 1,s|x\\
&0&&\textrm{else}\end{aligned}\right.=f\left(\frac{x}{s}\right)V\left\{\begin{aligned} &E_{(n-1,\,x/s)}&&\textrm{if }n\geq 1,s|x\\
&0&&\textrm{else}\end{aligned}\right.

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{aligned} &f\left(\frac{x}{s}\right)E_{(n,x)}&&\textrm{if }n\geq 1,s|x\\
&0&&\textrm{else}\end{aligned}\right.=M_{\mathfrak{a}_{V}f}(I-P_{(0,0)})E_{(n,x)}\,.

∎

From Lemma
5.1
, and the fact that
V
is an isometry, it follows, similarly to the Bunce-Deddens shift, that we have:

VM_{f}=M_{\mathfrak{a}_{V}f}V\quad\textrm{and}\quad V^{*}M_{f}V=M_{\mathfrak{b}_{V}f}.

5.2.
The Coefficient Algebra of
A_{V}

We have the following observation:

M_{f}P_{(0,0)}=f(0)P_{(0,0)},

and consequently Lemma
5.1
implies that

P_{(0,0)}=M_{\mathfrak{a}_{V}(I)}-VV^{*}\,.

This shows that
P_{(0,0)}
is an element of
A_{V}
.
If we define

P_{(n,0)}=V^{n}P_{(0,0)}(V^{*})^{n}\textrm{ for }n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}\,,

we get
P_{(n,0)}\in A_{V}
for all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
. Additionally,
P_{(n,0)}
is the one-dimensional projection onto (the subspace generated by) the basis element
E_{(n,0)}
.

Below we show that the C
∗
-algebra
B_{V}
generated by
M_{f}
’s and
P_{(n,0)}
:

B_{V}:=C^{*}(M_{f},P_{(n,0)}:f\in C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s}),n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0})

is the smallest coefficient algebra of
A_{V}
containing
C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
.

Proposition 5.2
.

The algebra
B_{V}
is commutative, it is the minimal coefficient algebra extension of
C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
, and the algebra
A_{V,\textrm{inv}}
is equal to
B_{V}
.

Proof.

We already established in Proposition
3.1
that the gauge invariant subalgebra
A_{V,\textrm{inv}}
is the minimal coefficient algebra extension of
C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
.

It easily follows from the above definitions that we have the following relation:

M_{f}P_{(n,0)}=f(0)P_{(n,0)}\,.

Consequently,
B_{V}
is commutative.

Next, we want to show that
B_{V}
is a coefficient algebra for
A_{V}
, i.e. it is closed under the actions of
\alpha_{V}
and
\beta_{V}
. Since
B_{V}
is commutative,
\beta_{V}
restricted to
B_{V}
is a homomorphism. Thus, it is enough to inspect the actions of
\alpha_{V}
and
\beta_{V}
on generators of
B_{V}
.
The formulas

\alpha_{V}(P_{(n,0)})=VP_{(n,0)}V^{*}=P_{(n+1,0)}\,,\quad\beta_{V}(P_{(n,0)})=V^{*}P_{(n,0)}V=P_{(n-1,0)}

(taking
P_{(-1,0)}=0
) and

V^{*}M_{f}V=M_{\mathfrak{b}_{V}f}\textrm{ and }VM_{f}V^{*}=M_{\mathfrak{a}_{V}f}(I-P_{(0,0)}),

clearly establish that
B_{V}
is a coefficient algebra extension of
C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
.

Notice that from the definitions, the generators of
B_{V}
are gauge invariant and so we have the inclusion
B_{V}\subseteq A_{V,\textrm{inv}}
. But since
A_{V,\textrm{inv}}
is the minimal coefficient algebra,
B_{V}
must be equal to
A_{V,\textrm{inv}}
.
∎

5.3.
The Structure of the Coefficient Algebra of
A_{V}

Since
B_{V}
is a unital, commutative C
∗
-algebra it must be of the form
B_{V}\cong C(X_{V})
for some compact Hausdorff space
X_{V}
. The purpose here is to describe such a space.

Let
X_{V}
be the following closed subspace of the compact topological space
{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}\times[0,1]
:

X_{V}=\{(x,0):x\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}\}\cup\left\{\left(0,\frac{1}{n+1}\right):n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}\right\}.

Since
X_{V}
is a union of two sets, any continuous function
F
on
X_{V}
consists of two functions: a continuous function
f
on
{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}\cong\{(x,0):x\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}\}
and a sequence
(x_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
of values of
F
on the discrete set
\{\left(0,\frac{1}{n+1}\right):n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}\}
, while the continuity of
F
at
(0,0)
requires
\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}x_{n}=f(0)
. Consequently, we have:

C(X_{V})\cong\{F=(f,(x_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}):f\in C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s}),\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}x_{n}=f(0)\}

Proposition 5.3
.

The map

C(X_{V})\ni F\mapsto T_{V}(F):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(x_{n}-f(0))P_{(n,0)}+M_{f}\in B_{V}=A_{V,\textrm{inv}}

(5.1)

is an isomorphism of C
∗
-algebras.

Proof.

Let
P_{*}^{\perp}
be the projection onto the subspace of
H
orthogonal to the ranges of all the
P_{(n,0)}
’s. Then we can write an alternative formula for
T_{V}(F)
as follows:

T_{V}(F):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}x_{n}P_{(n,0)}+M_{f}P_{*}^{\perp}.

It is easily seen from those formulas that the map
F\mapsto T_{V}(F)
is a C
∗
-homomorphism and that the generators of
B_{V}
are in the range of
T_{V}
, implying that the range of
T_{V}
must be equal to
B_{V}
.

As in the proof of Proposition
4.3
we compute the norm
||T_{V}(F)||
using the fact about the norms of block diagonal operators. This requires to compute the norm of
M_{f}
restricted to the range of
P_{*}^{\perp}
, that range is simply spanned by those
E_{(m,x)}
with nonzero
x
.
Consequently, we have that

\displaystyle\|M_{f}|_{\textrm{Ran }P_{*}^{\perp}}\|

\displaystyle=\sup\left\{|f(x)|:x\text{\ such that there exists\ }m\text{\ so that\ }E_{(m,x)}\in\text{Ran\ }P_{*}^{\perp}\right\}

\displaystyle=\sup_{0<x\in{\mathbb{Z}}}|f(x)|=||f||_{\infty},

since the set
\{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}:x>0\}
is dense
{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}
. Hence,

||T_{V}(F)||=||F||_{\infty},

establishing the desired isomorphism.
∎

5.4.
A_{V}
as a Crossed Product

Consider the morphisms
\tilde{\alpha}_{V},\tilde{\beta}_{V}
on
C(X_{V})
given by

\tilde{\alpha}_{V}(f,(x_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty})=(\mathfrak{a}_{V}f,(x_{n-1})_{n=0}^{\infty})\quad\textrm{and}\quad\tilde{\beta}_{V}(f,(x_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty})=(\mathfrak{b}_{V}f,(x_{n+1})_{n=0}^{\infty}),

taking
x_{-1}=0
.
A simple calculation shows that we have the following relations:

V^{*}T_{V}(F)V=T_{V}(\tilde{\beta}_{V}F)\quad\textrm{and}\quad VT_{V}(F)V^{*}=T_{V}(\tilde{\alpha}_{V}F)\,.

(5.2)

Similarly to the previous section we obtain the following main result of this section as a direct consequence of Proposition
3.1
, Proposition
5.3
, and equations (
5.2
).

Theorem 5.4
.

We have the following isomorphism of C
∗
-algebras:

A_{V}\cong C(X_{V})\rtimes_{\tilde{\alpha}_{V}}{\mathbb{N}}\,.

6.
The Bernoulli Shift

6.1.
The Algebra
A_{S}

Recall that the Bernoulli Shift
S:H\to H
is defined as:

SE_{(n,x)}=\frac{1}{s}\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}E_{(n+1,\,sx+j)}\,.

In this section we study the algebra

A_{S}=C^{*}(S,M_{f}:f\in C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})).

The goal, as before, is to identify the gauge-invariant subalgebra and write the algebra
A_{S}
as a crossed product of a coefficient algebra by an endomorphism. It turns out that the gauge-invariant subalgebra is non-commutative and the analysis of the Bernoulli shift has a slightly different flavor than the previous two shifts.

Like in the other cases we have maps
\mathfrak{a}_{S},\mathfrak{b}_{S}:C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})\to C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
defined by

\mathfrak{a}_{S}f(x)=f\left(\frac{x-x\textrm{ mod }s}{s}\right)\quad\textrm{and}\quad\mathfrak{b}_{S}f(x)=\frac{1}{s}\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}f(sx+j)\,.

It is clear that
\mathfrak{a}_{S}
is an endomorphism but
\mathfrak{b}_{S}
is not a homomorphism as it was before, however
\mathfrak{b}_{S}
is positive and linear. We also have that
\mathfrak{b}_{S}(I)=I=\mathfrak{a}_{S}(I)
. It is also straightforward to see that
\mathfrak{a}_{S}
has trivial kernel.

Consider the following computation:

(\mathfrak{b}_{S}\circ\mathfrak{a}_{S})f(x)=\mathfrak{b}_{S}(\mathfrak{a}_{S}f(x))=\frac{1}{s}\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}\mathfrak{a}_{S}f(sx+j)=\frac{1}{s}\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}f(x)=f(x)\,.

Consequently this implies that
\mathfrak{b}_{S}\circ\mathfrak{a}_{S}=1
and that
\textrm{Ran }\mathfrak{b}_{S}=C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
.

Lemma 6.1
.

Given
f\in C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
,
S
and
M_{f}
satisfy the following relations:

S^{*}M_{f}S=M_{\mathfrak{b}_{S}f}\quad\textrm{and}\quad SM_{f}=M_{\mathfrak{a}_{S}f}S\,.

Proof.

We check the two relations on the basis elements
\{E_{(n,x)}\}
. Consider the following:

S^{*}M_{f}SE_{(n,x)}=\frac{1}{s}S^{*}M_{f}\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}E_{(n+1,\,sx+j)}=\frac{1}{s}S^{*}\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}f(sx+j)E_{(n+1,\,sx+j)}=M_{\mathfrak{b}_{S}f}E_{(n,x)}

proving the first relation. For second relation we compare the following two calculations:

SM_{f}E_{(n,x)}=f(x)\frac{1}{s}\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}E_{(n+1,\,sx+j)}

while

M_{\mathfrak{a}_{S}f}SE_{(n,x)}=M_{\mathfrak{a}_{S}f}E_{(n+1,\,sx+j)}=\frac{1}{s}\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}\mathfrak{a}_{S}f(sx+j)E_{(n+1,\,sx+j)}=f(x)\frac{1}{s}\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}E_{(n+1,\,sx+j)}\,.

These two show the second relation completing the proof.
∎

6.2.
Relations with Cuntz-Toeplitz Algebras

The goal of this subsection is to identify
A_{S}
with a well-understood C
∗
-algebra that is easier to describe. For
j=0,1,\ldots,s-1
, consider the characteristic functions
\chi_{j}
given by the following formula for any
x\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}
:

\chi_{j}(x)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &1&&\textrm{ if }x\textrm{ mod }s=j\\
&0&&\textrm{else.}\end{aligned}\right.

This is a characteristic function for the ball with center
j
and radius
s^{-1}
.
Notice that for each
j
,
\chi_{j}
is locally constant and hence is a continuous function on
{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}
. Thus for each
j
,
\chi_{j}
defines a map
M_{\chi_{j}}:H\to H
and we can define operators
S_{j}:H\to H
for each
j=0,\ldots s-1
by

S_{j}=sM_{\chi_{j}}S.

Let
C_{S}
be the C
∗
-algebra generated by the
S_{j}
’s, that is,

C_{S}:=C^{*}(S_{j}:j=0,\ldots,s-1).

We have the following key observation.

Proposition 6.2
.

We have the following equality of C
∗
-algebras:

A_{S}=C_{S}.

Proof.

For each
j=0,\ldots,s-1
, we have by definition that
S_{j}\in A_{S}
, and thus we have
C_{S}\subseteq A_{S}
. To show the reverse containment, we show that the generators of
A_{S}
are in
C_{S}
.

Note that

S_{j}E_{(n,x)}=M_{\chi_{j}}\sum_{l=0}^{s-1}E_{(n+1,\,sx+l)}=E_{(n+1,\,sx+j)}\,,

(6.1)

and so it follows that

S=\frac{1}{s}\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}S_{j}\,.

Consequently we have that
S\in C_{S}
. Direct calculations show that
S_{j}^{*}S_{k}=\delta_{jk}I
and that

\displaystyle S_{j}S_{j}^{*}E_{(n,x)}

\displaystyle=S_{j}\left\{\begin{aligned} &E_{(n-1,\,(x-x\textrm{ mod }s)/s)}&&\textrm{ if }n\geq 1,x\textrm{ mod }s=j\\
&0&&\textrm{else}\end{aligned}\right.

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{aligned} &E_{(n,x)}&&\textrm{ if }n\geq 1,x\textrm{ mod }s=j\\
&0&&\textrm{else}\end{aligned}\right.

\displaystyle=(I-P_{(0,0)})M_{\chi_{j}}E_{(n,x)}\,

where, as before,
P_{(0,0)}
is the projection onto (the subspace generated by) the basis element
E_{(0,0)}
. It follows that we have:

\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}S_{j}S_{j}^{*}=(I-P_{(0,0)})\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}M_{\chi_{j}}=I-P_{(0,0)}

and hence
P_{(0,0)}\in C_{S}
. Notice that for
j=0,\ldots,s-1
we have

M_{\chi_{j}}=(I-P_{(0,0)})M_{\chi_{j}}+P_{(0,0)}M_{\chi_{j}}=S_{j}S_{j}^{*}+\delta_{j0}P_{(0,0)}

and so
M_{\chi_{j}}\in C_{S}
. Also notice that for each
k=0,\ldots,s-1
,

\displaystyle S_{k}M_{\chi_{j}}S_{k}^{*}E_{(n,x)}

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\chi_{j}\left(\frac{x-k}{s}\right)E_{(n,x)}&&\textrm{ if }n\geq 1,x\textrm{ mod }s=k\\
&0&&\textrm{else}\end{aligned}\right.

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{aligned} &E_{(n,x)}&&\textrm{ if }n\geq 1,x\textrm{ mod }s=k,\frac{x-k}{s}\textrm{ mod }s=j\\
&0&&\textrm{else}\end{aligned}\right.

which is just a multiplication operator by the characteristic function for the ball centered at
k+sj
with radius
s^{-2}
. Consequently that multiplication operator is in
C_{S}
. Repeating this argument yields that for any ball, multiplication by the corresponding characteristic function is in
C_{S}
.

Since any locally constant function is a finite linear combination of characteristic functions we have that multiplication by locally constant functions also belong to
C_{S}
. Since the locally constant functions on
{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}
are dense in
C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
, for each
f\in C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
we get
M_{f}\in C_{S}
. This completes the proof.
∎

While the above proposition shows that the algebras are the same, it also yields two key relations between the
S_{j}
’s, namely

S_{j}^{*}S_{k}=\delta_{jk}I\text{ for all }j,k=0,\ldots,s-1\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}S_{j}S_{j}^{*}=I-P_{(0,0)}\,.

(6.2)

This identifies
A_{S}
with the Cuntz-Toeplitz algebra, see below.

It is clear that
P_{(0,0)}
is a diagonal compact operator, and in fact, the following proposition shows that all compact operators on
H
are in
C_{S}
.

Proposition 6.3
.

The algebra
\mathcal{K}(H)
of compact operators on
H
is an ideal of
A_{S}
.

Proof.

For
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
,
0\leq x<s^{n}
, expand
x
as

x=x_{0}+x_{1}s+x_{2}s^{2}+\cdots+x_{n-1}s^{n-1},

with the coefficients satisfying
0\leq x_{k}<s
, for
k=0,\ldots,n-1
.
Define the following operator on
H
by

S_{(n,x)}=S_{x_{0}}S_{x_{1}}\cdots S_{x_{n-1}}

where
S_{x_{k}}
is given by equation (
6.1
) for
k=0,\ldots,n-1
. Clearly
S_{(n,x)},S_{(n,x)}^{*}
are in
A_{S}
. Define the following elements of
A_{S}
for
0\leq x<s^{n},0\leq y<s^{m}
:

P_{(n,x),(m,y)}=S_{(n,x)}P_{(0,0)}S_{(m,y)}^{*}.

By direct calculation we have
that for
m,n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
and
0\leq x<s^{n}
, and
0\leq x<s^{m}

\{P_{(n,x),(m,y)}\}_{n,m,x,y}

form a system of matrix units for the standard basis
\{E_{(n,x)}\}
of
H
. These matrix units generate
{\mathcal{K}}(H)
.
∎

6.3.
Cuntz and Cuntz-Toeplitz Algebras

We recall here basic definitions and properties of Cuntz and Cuntz-Toeplitz algebras from
[
3
]
, following the detailed treatment in
[
5
]
.

Let
\mathcal{O}_{s}
be the Cuntz algebra indexed by
s\geq 2
. This algebra is the universal C
∗
-algebra with generators
\{u_{j}\}_{j=0}^{s-1}
and relations

u_{j}^{*}u_{k}=\delta_{jk}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}u_{j}u_{j}^{*}=1\,.

(6.3)

The algebra
\mathcal{O}_{s}
is simple which follows from the following strong property: for every non-zero
a\in\mathcal{O}_{s}
there are
a_{1},a_{2}\in\mathcal{O}_{s}
such that

a_{1}aa_{2}=I,

see Theorem V.5.6 of
[
5
]
.

As before, for
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
,
0\leq x<s^{n}
, expand
x
as

x=x_{0}+x_{1}s+x_{2}s^{2}+\cdots+x_{n-1}s^{n-1}.

(6.4)

Define the following elements of
\mathcal{O}_{s}
:

u_{(n,x)}=u_{x_{0}}u_{x_{1}}\cdots u_{x_{n-1}}.

By Lemma V.4.1 of
[
5
]
any product of
u_{(n,x)}
’s and
u_{(m,y)}^{*}
’s has a reduced expression with no
u_{(n,x)}
’s to the right of any
u_{(m,y)}^{*}
’s.
Consequently, the *-subalgebra Pol
(\mathcal{O}_{s})
of finite linear combinations of products
u_{(n,x)}u_{(m,y)}^{*}
is dense in
\mathcal{O}_{s}
.

A Cuntz-Toeplitz algebra
\mathcal{TO}_{s}
indexed by
s\geq 2
is the universal C
∗
-algebra generated by
s
isometries
s_{i}
,
i=0,\ldots,s-1
such that

s_{j}^{*}s_{k}=\delta_{jk}I\text{ for all }j,k=0,\ldots,s-1\,.

For
(n,x)
as in equation (
6.4
) we define the following elements of
\mathcal{TO}_{s}
:

s_{(n,x)}=s_{x_{0}}s_{x_{1}}\cdots s_{x_{n-1}}.

Similarly, the *-subalgebra Pol
(\mathcal{TO}_{s})
of finite linear combinations of products
s_{(n,x)}s_{(m,y)}^{*}
is dense in
\mathcal{TO}_{s}
.

To understand the irreducible representations of a Cuntz-Toeplitz algebra, consider two cases of representations:
\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}s_{j}s_{j}^{*}=I
and
\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}s_{j}s_{j}^{*}<I
. In the first case, a representation of
\mathcal{TO}_{s}
is a representation of
\mathcal{O}_{s}
which, by simplicity of
\mathcal{O}_{s}
, is unique up to isomorphism. In the second case the projection

P:=I-\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}s_{j}s_{j}^{*}

is non-zero. If
e_{(0,0)}
is a unit vector in the range of
P
, then
Pe_{(0,0)}=e_{(0,0)}
and so we have
s_{j}^{*}e_{(0,0)}=0
for all
j
. It is easy to verify that the vectors
e_{(n,x)}:=s_{(n,x)}e_{(0,0)}
form an orthonormal set and their span is invariant for such a representation of
\mathcal{TO}_{s}
and thus they span the whole Hilbert space by irreducibility. Such a representation is isomorphic to the representation of
A_{S}
in
B(H)
by correspondence
e_{(n,x)}\mapsto E_{(n,x)}
which sends
s_{j}
to
S_{j}
for all
j
. Consequently, there are only two irreducible representations of
\mathcal{TO}_{s}
. Lemma V.5.2 of
[
5
]
says that the ideal
\langle P\rangle
generated by the projection
P
is isomorphic to the algebra of compact operators and the factor algebra
\mathcal{TO}_{s}/\langle P\rangle
is isomorphic to the Cuntz algebra
\mathcal{O}_{s}
. It follows that the representation of
A_{S}
in
B(H)
is a defining representation for the Cuntz-Toeplitz algebra.

To summarize, our key relations in equation (
6.2
) imply that
A_{S}
is the Cuntz-Toeplitz algebra. By the structure of Cuntz-Toeplitz algebra indicated above and
Proposition
6.3
we get the following result:

A_{S}/\mathcal{K}(H)\cong\mathcal{O}_{s}\,.

This implies that we have the following short exact sequence:

0\rightarrow\mathcal{K}(H)\rightarrow A_{S}\overset{q_{S}}{\rightarrow}\mathcal{O}_{s}\rightarrow 0

(6.5)

where
q_{S}:A_{S}\to\mathcal{O}_{s}
is the corresponding quotient map.

6.4.
The Toeplitz Map

We will define and study a very useful Toeplitz map

T_{S}:\mathcal{O}_{s}\to A_{S}.

To do that we need the following concrete representation of the Cuntz algebra in the Hilbert space
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
. It is defined by specifying
s
orthogonal isometries given on standard basis elements
\{e_{l}\}_{l\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
by the following formulas:

u_{j}e_{l}=e_{sl+j},\quad\text{for }j=0,\ldots,s-1.

Formulas for the adjoints are:

u_{j}^{*}e_{l}=\left\{\begin{aligned} &e_{(l-j)/s}&&\textrm{ if }l\equiv j\textrm{ mod }s\\
&0&&\textrm{ otherwise}.\end{aligned}\right.

It is easy to verify that the relations in equation (
6.3
) are satisfied.

Define the map
\phi:\mathcal{V}\to{\mathbb{Z}}
by

\phi(n,x):=s^{n}+x.

It is easily seen that
\phi
is one-to-one. This map is used to define an isometry

\iota:\ell^{2}(\mathcal{V})\to\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}})

on basis elements by

\iota(E_{(n,x)})=e_{\phi(n,x)}.

Its adjoint
\iota^{*}:\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}})\to\ell^{2}(\mathcal{V})
is given by

\iota^{*}e_{l}=\left\{\begin{aligned} &E_{\phi^{-1}(l)}&&\textrm{ if }l\in\textrm{ Ran }\phi\\
&0&&\textrm{ otherwise}.\end{aligned}\right.

Our Toeplitz map is initially defined from
B(\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}))
to
B(H)
by the formula

T_{S}(a):=\iota^{*}a\iota.

Clearly,
a\mapsto T_{S}(a)
is linear and continuous, and we have
T_{S}(I)=I
and
T_{S}(a)^{*}=T_{S}(a^{*})
. The key property relevant for our discussion of the Cuntz and Cuntz-Toeplitz algebras is the following observation.

Proposition 6.4
.

T_{S}(u_{(n,x)}u_{(m,y)}^{*})=S_{(n,x)}S_{(m,y)}^{*}+C_{(n,x),(m,y)},

where
C_{(n,x),(m,y)}
is non-zero only if both
x
and
y
are in the range of
\phi
. When
x
and
y
are in the range of
\phi
so that
x=s^{j}+x^{\prime}
,
y=s^{l}+y^{\prime}
where
j,l\geq 1,0\leq x^{\prime}<s^{j},0\leq y^{\prime}<s^{l}
, then
C_{(n,x),(m,y)}
is a one-dimensional, and hence compact, operator on
H
given by

C_{(n,x),(m,y)}E_{(k,z)}=\left\{\begin{aligned} &E_{(j,x^{\prime})}&&\textrm{ if }k=l\textrm{ and }z=y^{\prime}\\
&0&&\textrm{ otherwise}.\end{aligned}\right.

Proof.

We outline the key steps of this otherwise straightforward calculation.
We have the following easily established formulas:

S_{(n,x)}E_{(k,z)}=E_{(k+n,s^{n}z+x)}

and

S_{(n,x)}^{*}E_{(k,z)}=\left\{\begin{aligned} &E_{(k-n,(z-x)/s^{n})}&&\textrm{ if }k\geq n\textrm{ and }s^{n}|(z-x)\\
&0&&\textrm{ otherwise}.\end{aligned}\right.

Similarly, we have

u_{(n,x)}e_{l}=e_{s^{n}l+x}

and

u_{(n,x)}^{*}e_{l}=\left\{\begin{aligned} &e_{(l-x)/s^{n}}&&\textrm{ if }l\equiv x\textrm{ mod }s^{n}\\
&0&&\textrm{ otherwise}.\end{aligned}\right.

The last formula implies that in the expression

T_{S}(u_{(n,x)}u_{(m,y)}^{*})E_{(k,z)}=\iota^{*}(u_{(n,x)}u_{(m,y)}^{*})\iota E_{(k,z)}=\iota^{*}u_{(n,x)}u_{(m,y)}^{*}e_{\phi(k,z)},

the term
u_{(m,y)}^{*}e_{\phi(k,z)}
is non-zero if and only if

s^{k}+z\equiv y\textrm{ mod }s^{m}.

If
k<m
the last condition means equality
y=s^{k}+z
, since
z<s^{k}
. In particular, if
k<m
, for that term to be non-zero
y
must be in the range of
\phi
and then

\iota^{*}u_{(n,x)}u_{(m,y)}^{*}e_{\phi(k,z)}=\iota^{*}u_{(n,x)}e_{0}=\iota^{*}e_{x}.

From the formula for
\iota^{*}
the expression is non-zero if and only if
x
is in the range of
\phi
. Consequently, this case leads to the one-dimensional operator
C_{(n,x),(m,y)}
. In all other cases we have

T_{S}(u_{(n,x)}u_{(m,y)}^{*})=S_{(n,x)}S_{(m,y)}^{*}
.∎

This result has several important consequences. We already noticed that the *-subalgebra Pol
(\mathcal{O}_{s})
of finite linear combinations of products
u_{(n,x)}u_{(m,y)}^{*}
is dense in
\mathcal{O}_{s}
. Similarly, applying the calculations of Lemma V.4.1 of
[
5
]
to any product of
S_{(n,x)}
’s and
S_{(m,y)}^{*}
’s we see that it can be simplified so that no
S_{(n,x)}
’s are to the right of any
S_{(m,y)}^{*}
’s. This implies that the *-subalgebra Pol
(A_{S})
of finite linear combinations of products
S_{(n,x)}S_{(m,y)}^{*}
is dense in
A_{S}
. Additionally, since
\mathcal{K}(H)\subseteq A_{S}
, we have that indeed the Toeplitz map
T_{S}
maps
\mathcal{O}_{s}
to
A_{S}
by continuity.

If
q_{S}:A_{S}\to\mathcal{O}_{s}
is the quotient map then we have that
q_{S}
is zero on compact operators and

q_{S}(S_{(n,x)}S_{(m,y)}^{*})=u_{(n,x)}u_{(m,y)}^{*},

for all
n
,
m
,
x
,
y
. It follows by continuity again that

q_{S}(T_{S}(a))=a

for every
a\in\mathcal{O}_{s}
. Finally, the key importance of the Toeplitz map is in the following observation: for every
a,b\in\mathcal{O}_{s}
we have

T_{S}(ab)-T_{S}(a)T_{s}(b)\in\mathcal{K}(H).

Indeed, by continuity, it is enough to verify the above for
a,b
of the form
S_{(n,x)}S_{(m,y)}^{*}
.
Invoking Lemma V.4.1 of
[
5
]
again the statement follows from Proposition
6.4
.

6.5.
The Coefficient Algebra of
A_{S}

Recall that the *-subalgebra Pol
(A_{S})
of finite linear combinations of products
S_{(n,x)}S_{(m,y)}^{*}
is dense in
A_{S}
. This observation, and the gauge action on
S_{(n,x)}
’s and
S_{(m,y)}^{*}
’s suggest to consider the following subalgebra of
A_{S}
:

B_{S}:=C^{*}(S_{(n,x)}S_{(n,y)}^{*}:n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0},0\leq x,y<s^{n}).

Proposition 6.5
.

The subalgebra
B_{S}
is the minimal coefficient algebra extension of
C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
and the algebra
A_{S,\textrm{inv}}
is equal to
B_{S}
.

Proof.

Clearly
B_{S}\subseteq A_{S,\textrm{inv}}
. To prove the other inclusion we consider
a\in A_{S,\textrm{inv}}
, let
\epsilon>0
and choose
a_{\epsilon}\in\textrm{Pol}(A_{S})
such that:

||a-a_{\epsilon}||<\epsilon.

Applying the expectation from equation (
3.3
) we obtain:

||a-E(a_{\epsilon})||=||E(a-a_{\epsilon})||<||a-a_{\epsilon}||<\epsilon.

This completes the proof since
E(a_{\epsilon})\in B_{S}
.
∎

6.6.
Gauge Invariant Compact Operators

Next we briefly discuss the structure of the algebra
\mathcal{K}(H)_{\mathrm{inv}}
of gauge invariant compact operators, which is a subalgebra of
B_{S}
.

For
n=0,1,\ldots
, let
H_{n}
be the finite dimensional subspace of
H
spanned by basis vectors
E_{(n,x)}
with
0\leq x<s^{n}
, so that the dimension of
H_{n}
is
s^{n}
and

H=\bigoplus_{n=0}^{\infty}H_{n}.

Recall that from definition (
2.2
), the operator
\mathcal{U}_{\theta}
is a scalar multiplication by
e^{2\pi in\theta}
on each
H_{n}
. Consequently, if a bounded operator
a
on
H
is
\rho_{\theta}
invariant and
m\neq n
we have:

(E_{(n,x)},aE_{(m,x)})=(E_{(n,x)},\rho_{\theta}(a)E_{(m,x)})=(\mathcal{U}_{\theta}^{-1}E_{(n,x)},a\,\mathcal{U}_{\theta}^{-1}E_{(m,x)})=0.

It follows that gauge invariant operators are block diagonal:

a=\bigoplus_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n},

where
a_{n}:H_{n}\to H_{n}
are finite rank operators. Such an operator is compact if and only if
||a_{n}||\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. Consequently, the algebra

\mathcal{K}(H)_{\mathrm{inv}}
of invariant compact operators is an AF algebra that can be written as a direct limit:

\mathcal{K}(H)_{\mathrm{inv}}=\lim_{\underset{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}}{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{A}_{n}\,,

where

\mathcal{A}_{n}:=\bigoplus_{j=0}^{n}M_{s^{n}}({\mathbb{C}}),

and the connecting maps
\mathcal{A}_{n}\to\mathcal{A}_{n+1}
are given by:

(a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\mapsto(a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{n},0)

6.7.
A_{S}
as a Crossed Product

Consider the following subalgebra of
\mathcal{O}_{s}
:

\mathcal{O}_{s,\mathrm{inv}}:=C^{*}(u_{(n,x)}u_{(n,y)}^{*}:n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0},0\leq x,y<s^{n}).

It is known, see
[
3
]
, that
\mathcal{O}_{s,\mathrm{inv}}
is the invariant subalgebra of
\mathcal{O}_{s}
with respect to the gauge action on
\mathcal{O}_{s}
and that

\mathcal{O}_{s,\mathrm{inv}}\cong UHF(s^{\infty}).

Using the above Toeplitz map
T_{S}:\mathcal{O}_{s}\to A_{S}
we can give a more detailed description of the gauge invariant algebra
B_{S}
.

Proposition 6.6
.

Every
b\in B_{S}
can be uniquely written as

b=T_{S}(a)+c,

(6.6)

where
a\in\mathcal{O}_{s,\mathrm{inv}}
and
c\in\mathcal{K}(H)_{\mathrm{inv}}

Moreover, we have the following short exact sequence of C
∗
-algebras:

0\rightarrow\mathcal{K}(H)_{\mathrm{inv}}\rightarrow B_{S}\rightarrow\mathcal{O}_{s,\mathrm{inv}}\rightarrow 0.

Proof.

We begin by constructing the desired
a
. Put
a:=q_{S}(b)\in{\mathcal{O}}_{S}
. Then we have

q_{S}(b-T_{S}(a))=q_{S}(b)-q_{S}(T_{S}(a))=q_{S}(b)-a=q_{S}(b)-q_{S}(b)=0.

So we get
c:=b-T_{S}(a)\in{\mathcal{K}}(H)
.

To obtain uniqueness, we show that if
T_{S}(a)\in{\mathcal{K}}(H)
then
a=0
. Supposing that
a\neq 0
, there are
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
in
{\mathcal{O}}_{S}
so that
a_{1}aa_{2}=I
. Applying
T_{S}
to the left side we get

I=T_{S}(I)=T_{S}(a_{1}aa_{2})=T_{S}(a_{1})T_{S}(a)T_{S}(a_{2})+\tilde{c}

where
\tilde{c}
is compact. But
T_{S}(a)
is compact by assumption, thus the above expression is compact. This implies that
I
is compact, an obvious contradiction.

Finally, notice that the short exact sequence in equation (
6.5
) induces the short exact sequence of invariant algebras.
∎

Since
B_{S}
is equal to
A_{S,\textrm{inv}}
the map
\alpha_{S}
is a monomorphism of
B_{S}
with a hereditary range. Similarly to the previous sections we obtain the following main structural result as a direct consequence of Proposition
6.5
and Proposition
3.1
.

Theorem 6.7
.

We have the following isomorphisms of C
∗
-algebras:

A_{S}\cong B_{S}\rtimes_{\alpha_{S}}{\mathbb{N}}\,.

7.
The Serre Shift

7.1.
The Algebra
A_{W}

In this final section, the goal is to study the algebra

A_{W}=C^{*}(W,M_{f}:f\in C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})).

Recall that the Serre Shift is given by:

WE_{(n,x)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{s}}\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}E_{(n+1,\,x+js^{n})}\,.

As with the other algebras, we describe a relation between
W
,
W^{*}
and
M_{f}
. We have the following lemma:

Lemma 7.1
.

The operators
W
,
W^{*}
, and
M_{f}
satisfy the following relations on the basis elements of
H
:

\displaystyle W^{*}M_{f}WE_{(n,x)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{s}\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}f(x+js^{n})E_{(n,x)}

\displaystyle WM_{f}W^{*}E_{(n,x)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{s}f(x\textrm{ mod }s^{n-1})WW^{*}E_{(n,x)}

where

WW^{*}E_{(n,x)}=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{1}{s}\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}E_{(n,x\textrm{ mod }s^{n-1}+js^{n-1})}&&\textrm{ if }n\geq 1\\
&0&&\textrm{ else.}\end{aligned}\right.

Proof.

The formulas follow by direct calculations similar to those in Lemmas
4.1
,
5.1
, and
6.1
.
∎

In light of this lemma it does not appear, a priori, that there is an automorphism or even an endomorphism of
C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
associated to
A_{W}
to construct a crossed product. The right hand side of the above formulas suggest however the following: if
F:\mathcal{V}\to{\mathbb{C}}
is a bounded function, define

\displaystyle\mathfrak{a}_{W}F(n,x)

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{aligned} &F(n-1,\,x\textrm{ mod }s^{n-1})&&\textrm{ if }n\geq 1\\
&0&&\textrm{ else}\end{aligned}\right.

\displaystyle\mathfrak{b}_{W}F(n,x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{s}\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}F(n+1,\,x+js^{n})\,.

Moreover, for a bounded function
F
, define the operator
M_{F}
acting on
H
by

M_{F}E_{(n,x)}=F(n,x)E_{(n,x)}.

With those considerations, similarly to Lemma
7.1
we get

W^{*}M_{F}W=M_{\mathfrak{b}_{W}F}\quad\textrm{and}\quad WM_{F}=M_{\mathfrak{a}_{W}F}W\,.

(7.1)

The next goal is to identify for which
F
the corresponding diagonal operator
M_{F}
is in
A_{W}
.

7.2.
The Diagonal Subalgebra

Consider the space of complex-valued functions
\mathcal{F}
defined on
\mathcal{V}
as:

\{F:\mathcal{V}\to{\mathbb{C}}:\textrm{there is an }f_{F}\in C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s}),\textrm{ so that }\lim_{n}\underset{0\leq x<s^{n}}{\textrm{sup }}|F(n,x\textrm{ mod }s^{n})-f_{F}(x)|=0\}\,.

It is not difficult to see that
\mathcal{F}
is a commutative C
∗
-algebra with the usual supremum norm.

Define the following subset of
\mathcal{F}

\mathcal{F}_{\infty}=\{F\in\mathcal{F}:\lim_{n}\underset{0\leq x<s^{n}}{\textrm{sup }}|F(n,x)|=0\}\,.

Notice that
\mathcal{F}_{\infty}
is a
*
-subalgebra and an ideal of
\mathcal{F}
. Also, if the set

\mathcal{V}=\{(n,x):n=0,1,2,\ldots,0\leq x<s^{n}\}

is equipped with the discrete topology, then we notice that
\mathcal{F}_{\infty}
can be seen as the space of continuous functions vanishing at infinity on
\mathcal{V}
:

\mathcal{F}_{\infty}=C_{0}(\mathcal{V})\,.

Clearly, any
F\in\mathcal{F}
can be uniquely decomposed as follows:

F(n,x)=f_{F}(x)+\varphi(n,x)

where
f_{F}\in C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
and
\varphi\in\mathcal{F}_{\infty}
. Moreover we have the following isomorphism:

\mathcal{F}/\mathcal{F}_{\infty}\cong C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})\,.

The main reason we are interested in the algebra
\mathcal{F}
is explained in the following result.

Proposition 7.2
.

For every
F\in\mathcal{F}
we have
M_{F}\in A_{W}
.
Moreover,
\mathcal{F}
is the smallest commutative C
∗
-algebra of bounded functions on
\mathcal{V}
such that the following conditions are satisfied:

(1):

\mathcal{F}
contains all of the functions
(n,x)\mapsto f(x)
with
f\in C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})

(2):

\mathcal{F}
is closed under the maps
\mathfrak{a}_{W}
and
\mathfrak{b}_{W}
.

Proof.

Notice that by the definition of
\mathfrak{a}_{W}
for
F\in{\mathcal{F}}
we have

|\mathfrak{a}_{W}F(n,x)-f_{F}(x)|\leq|F(n-1,\,x\textrm{ mod }s^{n-1})-f_{F}(x\textrm{ mod }s^{n-1})|+|f_{F}(x)-f_{F}(x\textrm{ mod }s^{n-1})|.

From the definition of
f_{F}
, the first term above goes to zero as
n\to\infty
. By continuity of
f_{F}
, the second term also goes to zero as
n\to\infty
.
Similar estimates also work for
\mathfrak{b}_{W}F
. We obtain:

f_{\mathfrak{a}_{W}F}=f_{\mathfrak{b}_{W}F}=f_{F}.

(7.2)

Thus we have that
{\mathcal{F}}
is closed under
\mathfrak{a}_{W}
and
\mathfrak{b}_{W}
.

Let
(g_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
with
g_{n}\in\mathcal{F}_{\infty}
be defined by

g_{n}(m,x)=\begin{cases}1&n=m\\
0&\text{else}.\end{cases}

Since the set
\left\{g_{n}f:f\in C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s}),n=0,1,\ldots\right\}
separates points of
\mathcal{V}
and has the non-vanishing property, since
g_{n}(n,x)=1\neq 0
, the Stone-Weierstrass Theorem yields that

C^{*}(g_{n}f:f\in C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s}),n=0,1,\ldots)=\mathcal{F}_{\infty}.

Thus it suffices to show that
M_{g_{n}}\in A_{W}
for all
n\geq 0
. We proceed inductively.

If we put

\chi_{1}(x)=e^{2\pi ix/s}\in C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s}),

then we have

W^{*}M_{\chi_{1}}W=M_{\mathfrak{b}_{W}\chi_{1}}=M_{\chi_{1}}(1-M_{g_{0}}).

Thus
M_{g_{0}}\in A_{W}
since
M_{\chi_{1}}
is invertible.

Assume
M_{g_{0}},M_{g_{1}},\ldots,M_{g_{n-1}}\in A_{W}
. It follows that
M_{g}\in A_{W}
for all
g\in c_{0}({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0})
with
g(m)=0
for
m\geq n
. This is because such
g
’s form a
n
-dimensional space and functions
\{g_{0},\ldots,g_{n-1}\}
are a basis for this space.

For
n=0,1,\ldots
, define

\chi_{n}(x)=e^{2\pi ix/s^{n}}

and note that we have

W^{*}M_{\chi_{n}}W=M_{\mathfrak{b}_{W}\chi_{n}}.

Furthermore, we get

\mathfrak{b}_{W}\chi_{n+1}(m,x)=\dfrac{1}{s}\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}e^{2\pi i\left(\frac{x+js^{m}}{s^{n+1}}\right)}=\chi_{n+1}(x)\dfrac{1}{s}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}e^{2\pi ij\left(\frac{s^{m}}{s^{n+1}}\right)}.

Consider the function on the right-hand side of the above equation:

h_{n}(m):=\dfrac{1}{s}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}e^{2\pi ij\left(\frac{s^{m}}{s^{n+1}}\right)}.

It follows from the above calculation and invertibility of
M_{\chi_{n}}
that
M_{h_{n}}\in A_{W}
.
Notice that
h_{n}
has the following property:

h_{n}(m)=\begin{cases}1&m>n\\
0&m=n.\end{cases}

Then the function
(1-h_{n}-g_{n})(x)=0
for
m\geq n
, and thus by induction hypothesis
M_{1-h_{n}-g_{n}}\in A_{W}
. Hence we have
M_{g_{n}}\in A_{W}
, completing the induction.

To show that
\mathcal{F}
is the smallest C
∗
-algebra of bounded functions on
\mathcal{V}
satisfying the two conditions notice any such algebra would necessarily contain the functions
\mathfrak{b}_{W}(\chi_{n})
. But the above proof implies then that such an algebra must contain all
g_{n}f
with
f\in C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
for
n=0,1,\ldots
and consequently must contain
\mathcal{F}
.
∎

As a consequence, we get the following equality of C
∗
-algebras:

Proposition 7.3
.

The
C^{*}
-algebra
\textrm{C}^{*}(W,M_{F})
generated by
W
and
M_{F}
with
F\in\mathcal{F}
is equal to
A_{W}
.

Proof.

Since
C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})\subseteq\mathcal{F}
, it follows that
A_{W}\subseteq\textrm{C}^{*}(W,M_{F})
.
From the previous proposition, for every
F\in\mathcal{F}
we have
M_{F}\in A_{W}
, which implies that
\textrm{C}^{*}(W,M_{F})\subseteq A_{W}
.
∎

7.3.
The Coefficient Algebra of
A_{W}

Next consider the mutually orthogonal projections
\{\mathcal{P}_{n}\}
given by

\mathcal{P}_{0}=I-WW^{*}\textrm{ and }\mathcal{P}_{n}=W^{n}\mathcal{P}_{0}(W^{*})^{n}.

Below we show that the C
∗
-algebra
B_{W}
generated by
M_{F}
’s and
\{\mathcal{P}_{n}\}
:

B_{W}:=C^{*}(M_{F},\mathcal{P}_{n}:F\in\mathcal{F},n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0})

is the minimal coefficient algebra extension of
C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
and so the algebra
A_{W,\textrm{inv}}
is equal to
B_{W}
, see Proposition
7.8
below. While it is clear that
B_{W}\subseteq A_{W,\textrm{inv}}
, it is not evident that
B_{W}
is a coefficient algebra. This is because the map
\beta_{W}
is not a homomorphism and thus verifying that
B_{W}
is closed under
\beta_{W}
requires more structural work than just inspecting the generators of
B_{W}
.

The result that
A_{W}=C^{*}(W,M_{F}:F\in\mathcal{F})
allows for extra flexibility. We use it in the proof of the following observation.

Proposition 7.4
.

The set
\mathcal{K}(H)
of compact operators on
H
is an ideal of
A_{W}
.

Proof.

To show that
A_{W}
contains the ideal of compact operators
\mathcal{K}
we proceed to show the system of units that generate
\mathcal{K}(H)
are in
A_{W}
, similarly to an argument in the proof of Proposition
6.3
for the Bernoulli shift.

Define
\chi_{m,l}:\mathcal{V}\to{\mathbb{C}}
by

\chi_{m,l}(n,x)=\begin{cases}1&n=m\text{ and }|x-l|_{s}\leq s^{-m}\\
0&\text{else}\end{cases}.

Since
\chi_{m,l}(n,x)=0
for
n>m
, we get that

\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\sup\limits_{0\leq x<s^{n}}|\chi_{m,l}(n,x)-0|=0,

hence
\chi_{m,l}\in\mathcal{F}
.
The units
P_{(n,x),(m,y)}
that generate
\mathcal{K}(H)
are defined by

P_{(n,x),(m,y)}=s^{\frac{n+m}{2}}M_{\chi_{n,x}}W^{n}(W^{*})^{m}M_{\chi_{m,y}},

where
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
,
0\leq x<s^{n}
,
m\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
and
0\leq y<s^{m}
.
To verify this claim, we calculate
P_{(n,x),(m,y)}E_{(k,z)}
in steps:

\displaystyle s^{m/2}(W^{*})^{m}M_{\chi_{m,y}}E_{(k,z)}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}s^{m/2}(W^{*})^{m}E_{(m,z)}&k=m,z\equiv y\bmod s^{m}\\
0&\text{else}\end{cases}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}E_{(0,0)}&k=m,z\equiv y\bmod s^{m}\\
0&\text{else}.\end{cases}

But
0\leq z,y<s^{m}
, so the only way for these to be congruent is if they are equal. Thus

s^{m/2}(W^{*})^{m}M_{\chi_{m,y}}E_{(k,z)}=\begin{cases}E_{(0,0)}&k=m,y=z\\
0&\text{else}.\end{cases}

The remaining portion of
P_{(n,x),(m,y)}E_{(k,z)}
is:

\displaystyle s^{n/2}M_{\chi_{n,x}}W^{n}E_{(0,0)}

\displaystyle=s^{n/2}M_{\chi_{n,x}}\dfrac{1}{s^{n/2}}\sum_{j_{0}=0}^{s-1}\sum_{j_{1}=0}^{s-1}\cdots\sum_{j_{n-1}=0}^{s-1}E_{(n,j_{0}+j_{1}s+\cdots+j_{n-1}s^{n-1})}

\displaystyle=M_{\chi_{n,x}}\sum_{j=0}^{s^{n}-1}E_{(n,j)}=E_{(n,x)}.

Thus we obtain:

P_{(n,x),(m,y)}E_{(k,z)}=\begin{cases}E_{(n,x)}&(m,y)=(k,z)\\
0&\text{else}.\end{cases}

These are precisely the matrix units for the canonical base of
H
, and so
\mathcal{K}(H)\subseteq A_{W}
.
∎

Notice that the formula

P_{(n,x),(n,y)}=s^{n}M_{\chi_{n,x}}W^{n}(W^{*})^{n}M_{\chi_{n,y}}

from above implies that
P_{(n,x),(n,y)}
are in
B_{W}
for all
n=0,1,\ldots
and
0\leq x,y<s^{n}
. This is because
\chi_{n,x}\in\mathcal{F}
and

W^{n}(W^{*})^{n}=I-\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\mathcal{P}_{j}.

Consequently,
\mathcal{K}(H)_{\mathrm{inv}}
is a subalgebra, in fact an ideal in
B_{W}
.

Notice also that if
F\in\mathcal{F}_{\infty}=C_{0}(\mathcal{V})
then
M_{F}\in\mathcal{K}(H)_{\mathrm{inv}}
since
M_{F}
is a diagonal operator with coefficients vanishing at infinity.
In particular, for any
F\in\mathcal{F}_{\infty}
, we have that
M_{F-\mathfrak{a}_{W}(F)}
is compact, an observation used several times below.

Recall that we can view
c({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0})\otimes C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
as the space of uniformly convergent sequences of continuous functions on
{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}
:

c({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0})\otimes C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})\cong c({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0},C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s}))\cong\{G=(g_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}}:g_{n}\in C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s}),\ \lim_{n\to\infty}g_{n}\textrm{ exists}\}\,,

with the following notation for the uniform limit:

g_{\infty}:=\lim_{n\to\infty}g_{n}
.

Consider the following Toeplitz-like map

c({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0},C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s}))\ni G\mapsto T_{W}(G):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{P}_{n}(M_{g_{n}}-M_{g_{\infty}})\mathcal{P}_{n}+M_{g_{\infty}}\in B_{W}.

Mutual orthogonality of the
{\mathcal{P}}_{n}
’s ensures that the series is convergent because

\left\|\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{\mathcal{P}}_{n}(M_{g_{n}}-M_{g_{\infty}}){\mathcal{P}}_{n}\right\|=\sup_{n}\|M_{g_{n}}-M_{g_{\infty}}\|<\infty

since
g_{n}\to g_{\infty}
. This Toeplitz map is the key to describing the structure of
B_{W}
.

We need the following simple but essential information.

Lemma 7.5
.

For every
F\in\mathcal{F}
the commutator
[M_{F},W]
is compact. For every
F\in\mathcal{F}
and
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
the commutator
[M_{F},{\mathcal{P}}_{n}]
is compact.

Proof.

[M_{F},W]=M_{F}W-WM_{F}=M_{F}W-M_{\mathfrak{a}_{W}(F)}W=M_{F-\mathfrak{a}_{W}(F)}W,

and
M_{F-\mathfrak{a}_{W}(F)}
is a compact diagonal operator.

The second statement follows from the first since the map
a\mapsto[M_{F},a]
is a derivation and so it satisfies the Leibniz Rule.
∎

We use Lemma
7.5
to verify the following crucial property of the Toeplitz map
T_{W}
.

Proposition 7.6
.

For all
G,G^{\prime}\in c({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0},C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s}))
we have

T_{W}(G)T_{W}(G^{\prime})-T_{W}(GG^{\prime})\in\mathcal{K}(H)_{\mathrm{inv}}.

Proof.

By continuity, it is sufficient to check the case where
T_{W}(G)
is a finite sum, that is, where the sequence
\{g_{n}\}
is eventually constant. In this case, we have

T_{W}(G)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}{\mathcal{P}}_{n}(M_{g_{n}}-M_{g_{\infty}}){\mathcal{P}}_{n}+M_{g_{\infty}}\\

where
g_{n}=g_{\infty}
for
n>N
. Using Lemma
7.5
this expression can be rewritten as follows:

T_{W}(G)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}{\mathcal{P}}_{n}M_{g_{n}}{\mathcal{P}}_{n}+{\mathcal{P}}_{\leq N}^{\perp}M_{g_{\infty}}{\mathcal{P}}_{\leq N}^{\perp}+c,

(7.3)

where
c
is compact and

{\mathcal{P}}_{\leq N}^{\perp}=I-\sum_{n=0}^{N}{\mathcal{P}}_{n}.

If we consider two eventually constant sequences
G,G^{\prime}
, take
N
large enough so that both
g_{n}
and
g_{n}^{\prime}
are constant for
n>N
. Then it follows from formula (
7.3
) and Lemma
7.5
that we have
T_{W}(GG^{\prime})-T_{W}(G)T_{W}(G^{\prime})\in{\mathcal{K}}(H)_{\mathrm{inv}}
.
∎

The following result describes the structure of the algebra
B_{W}
.

Proposition 7.7
.

Every
a\in B_{W}
can be uniquely written as

a=T_{W}(G)+c,

(7.4)

for some
c\in\mathcal{K}(H)_{\mathrm{inv}}
and
G\in c({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0})\otimes C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
.

Proof.

Define

B_{W}^{\prime}:=\left\{T_{W}(G)+c:G\in c({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0},C(Z_{s})),\,c\in{\mathcal{K}}(H)_{\mathrm{inv}}\right\}\subseteq A_{W,\mathrm{inv}}.

First we notice that
B_{W}^{\prime}
is a
*
-subalgebra of
A_{W}
. It is clearly a subspace of
A_{W}
and is closed under adjoints, thus it is sufficient to show that
B_{W}^{\prime}
is closed under products, but this immediately follows from Proposition
7.6
.

Next we demonstrate that
B_{W}^{\prime}=B_{W}
. We already noticed that for all
G\in c({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0},C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s}))
we have
T_{W}(G)\in B_{W}
and also
{\mathcal{K}}(H)_{\mathrm{inv}}\subseteq B_{W}
. So
B_{W}^{\prime}\subseteq B_{W}
.

For the reverse inclusion we only need to check that the generators of
B_{W}
are in
B_{W}^{\prime}
. For any
F\in\mathcal{F}
we decompose

M_{F}=M_{f_{F}}+M_{F-f_{F}}

and notice that
F-f_{F}\in\mathcal{F}_{\infty}
and therefore
M_{F-f_{F}}\in{\mathcal{K}}(H)_{\mathrm{inv}}
.

Let
G_{F}\in c({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0},C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s}))
be a constant sequence where each term is equal to
f_{F}
.
Then we have

T_{W}(G_{F})=M_{f_{F}},

which implies that
M_{F}\in B_{W}^{\prime}
.

Now fix
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
and put
G_{n}=(0,\ldots,0,1,0,\ldots)
where the lone
1
is in the
n^{th}
place. Then

T_{W}(G_{n})={\mathcal{P}}_{n}(1-0){\mathcal{P}}_{n}={\mathcal{P}}_{n},

and so
{\mathcal{P}}_{n}\in B_{W}^{\prime}
, establishing the required inclusion. In particular, every
a\in B_{W}
can be written as a sum
a=T_{W}(G)+c
,
for some
c\in\mathcal{K}(H)_{\mathrm{inv}}
and
G\in c({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0},C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s}))
.

Uniqueness comes from the fact that
T_{W}(G)\in{\mathcal{K}}(H)
implies that
G=0
. Indeed, assume

a:=T_{W}(G)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{\mathcal{P}}_{n}(M_{g_{n}}-M_{g_{\infty}}){\mathcal{P}}_{n}+M_{g_{\infty}}\in{\mathcal{K}}(H).

Then

\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}_{m}a{\mathcal{P}}_{m}={\mathcal{P}}_{m}(M_{g_{m}}-M_{g_{\infty}}){\mathcal{P}}_{m}+{\mathcal{P}}_{m}M_{g_{\infty}}{\mathcal{P}}_{m}

is also compact, and so
{\mathcal{P}}_{m}M_{g_{m}}{\mathcal{P}}_{m}
is compact. By Lemma
7.5
this is equivalent to
{\mathcal{P}}_{m}M_{g_{m}}
being compact.

To proceed further we compute diagonal inner products:

{\mathcal{P}}_{0}E_{(n,x)}=\begin{cases}E_{(n,x)}-\dfrac{1}{s}\sum\limits_{j=0}^{s-1}E_{(n,x\bmod s^{n-1}+js^{n-1})}&n\geq 1\\
0&\text{else}\end{cases}

and so

\langle E_{(n,x)},{\mathcal{P}}_{0}E_{(n,x)}\rangle=1-\frac{1}{s}.

Consequently
{\mathcal{P}}_{0}
cannot be compact. Moreover, by the definition of
{\mathcal{P}}_{m}
, we have that
{\mathcal{P}}_{0}=(W^{*})^{m}{\mathcal{P}}_{m}W^{m}
, thus if
{\mathcal{P}}_{m}
were compact this would force
{\mathcal{P}}_{0}
to be compact. Hence
{\mathcal{P}}_{m}
is not compact.

If
f\in C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
then
M_{f}E_{(n,x)}=f(x)E_{(n,x)}
. This does not depend on
n
and we have:

{\mathcal{P}}_{0}M_{f}E_{(n,x)}=f(x){\mathcal{P}}_{0}E_{(n,x)}

As above, we have that

\langle E_{(n,x)},{\mathcal{P}}_{0}M_{f}E_{(n,x)}\rangle=f(x)\left(1-\frac{1}{s}\right).

So
{\mathcal{P}}_{0}M_{f}
not compact unless
f\equiv 0
.

Consider the following calculation:

(W^{*})^{m}{\mathcal{P}}_{m}M_{f}W^{m}={\mathcal{P}}_{0}(W^{*})^{m}M_{f}W^{m}={\mathcal{P}}_{0}M_{\mathfrak{b}_{W}^{m}(f)}={\mathcal{P}}_{0}M_{f}+{\mathcal{P}}_{0}M_{\mathfrak{b}_{W}^{m}(f)-f}.

Notice that by equation (
7.2
) the term
{\mathcal{P}}_{0}M_{\mathfrak{b}_{W}^{m}(f)-f}
is compact. If
{\mathcal{P}}_{m}M_{f}
were compact, the above equation would imply that
{\mathcal{P}}_{0}M_{f}
is compact. Hence again,
{\mathcal{P}}_{m}M_{f}
is not compact unless
f\equiv 0
.

To summarize, if
T_{W}(G)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{\mathcal{P}}_{n}(M_{g_{n}}-M_{g_{\infty}}){\mathcal{P}}_{n}+M_{g_{\infty}}
is in
{\mathcal{K}}(H)
then
{\mathcal{P}}_{m}M_{g_{m}}
must be as well. But we have from above that this is never compact unless
g_{m}=0
for all
m
. So
T_{W}(G)
is compact if and only if
G=0
. This implies the uniqueness in the decomposition of the elements of
B_{W}
.
∎

As a consequence, we obtain the following properties of the subalgebra
B_{W}
.

Proposition 7.8
.

The algebra
B_{W}
is the minimal coefficient algebra extension of
C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
and so the algebra
A_{W,\textrm{inv}}
is equal to
B_{W}
. Moreover, we have the following short exact sequence of C
∗
-algebras:

0\rightarrow\mathcal{K}(H)_{\mathrm{inv}}\rightarrow B_{W}\rightarrow c({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0})\otimes C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})\rightarrow 0.

(7.5)

Proof.

Clearly
B_{W}\subseteq A_{W,\textrm{inv}}
. To prove the other inclusion we need that
B_{W}
is a coefficient algebra. We use here the results from Proposition
7.7
. Thus, we need to show that for
T_{W}(G)+c\in B_{W}
, both
W(T_{W}(G)+c)W^{*}
and
W^{*}(T_{W}(G)+c)W
are in
B_{W}
. As
c
is compact and the compact operators form an ideal, we will drop the
c
from the calculations below.

We have the following helpful formulas:

\displaystyle W{\mathcal{P}}_{n}=WW^{n}{\mathcal{P}}_{0}(W^{*})^{n}(W^{*}W)=W^{n+1}{\mathcal{P}}_{0}(W^{*})^{n+1}W={\mathcal{P}}_{n+1}W

(7.6)

\displaystyle W^{*}{\mathcal{P}}_{n}=W^{*}W^{n}{\mathcal{P}}_{0}(W^{*})^{n}=W^{n-1}{\mathcal{P}}_{0}(W^{*})^{n-1}W^{*}={\mathcal{P}}_{n-1}W^{*},

where again we use
{\mathcal{P}}_{-1}=0
. Additionally, equation (
7.1
) implies that for
F\in\mathcal{F}
we have

\beta_{W}(M_{F})=M_{\mathfrak{b}_{W}F}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\alpha_{W}(M_{F})=M_{\mathfrak{a}_{W}F}(I-{\mathcal{P}}_{0})\,.

Since eventually constant sequences with values of
C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
are dense in
c({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0},C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s}))
it is enough to consider such sequences. Applying those formulas for an eventually constant sequence
G\in c({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0},C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s}))
we get:

\displaystyle\alpha_{W}(T_{W}(G))=W(T_{W}(G))W^{*}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{N}{\mathcal{P}}_{n+1}(M_{\mathfrak{a}_{W}(g_{n})}-M_{\mathfrak{a}_{W}(g_{\infty})})(I-{\mathcal{P}}_{0}){\mathcal{P}}_{n+1}+M_{\mathfrak{a}_{W}(g_{\infty})}(I-{\mathcal{P}}_{0})

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{N}{\mathcal{P}}_{n}(M_{\mathfrak{a}_{W}(g_{n-1})}-M_{\mathfrak{a}_{W}(g_{\infty})}){\mathcal{P}}_{n}+M_{\mathfrak{a}_{W}(g_{\infty})}

where
g_{-1}=0
. Since for
f\in C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
we have that
M_{f}-M_{\mathfrak{a}_{W}(f)}
is compact by equation (
7.2
), we obtain

\alpha_{W}(T_{W}(G))=T_{W}(G^{\prime})+c

where
c
is compact and
G^{\prime}=(g_{n}^{\prime})_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}}
with
g_{n}^{\prime}=g_{n-1}
.

A similar argument shows that
\beta_{W}(T_{W}(G))=W^{*}T_{W}(G)W\in B_{W}
.
Thus
B_{W}
is a coefficient algebra, establishing the final part of the equality.

Because
\mathcal{K}(H)_{\mathrm{inv}}
is an ideal in
B_{W}
we have the short exact sequence:

0\rightarrow\mathcal{K}(H)_{\mathrm{inv}}\rightarrow B_{W}\rightarrow B_{W}/\mathcal{K}(H)_{\mathrm{inv}}\rightarrow 0.

The short exact sequence in equation (
7.5
) follows now from Proposition
7.6
and Proposition
7.7
which establish that the map:

c({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0},C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s}))\ni G\mapsto T_{W}(G)+\mathcal{K}(H)_{\mathrm{inv}}\in B_{W}/\mathcal{K}(H)_{\mathrm{inv}}

is an isomorphism of C
∗
-algebras.
∎

Since
B_{W}
is equal to
A_{W,\textrm{inv}}
by Proposition
7.8
, we have that the map
\alpha_{W}
is a monomorphism of
B_{W}
with a hereditary range. Similarly to the previous sections we obtain the following main structural result about the algebra
A_{W}
as a direct consequence of Proposition
3.1
.

Theorem 7.9
.

We have the following isomorphisms of C
∗
-algebras:

A_{W}\cong B_{W}\rtimes_{{\alpha}_{W}}{\mathbb{N}}\,.
