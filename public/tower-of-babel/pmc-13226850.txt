Extraintestinal enterobiasis presenting as a cervical mass in an immunocompromised adult after colorectal cancer surgery: A case report
Authors: Jacek Sokołowski, Władysław Rozwadowski, Małgorzata Kołos, Eliza Brożek-Mądry
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Enterobius vermicularis is a common intestinal helminth, whereas extraintestinal disease is rare and may present as a tumor-like lesion. We report a 50-year-old man with common variable immunodeficiency (CVID) and prior right hemicolectomy with adjuvant chemotherapy for cecal adenocarcinoma (pT3N1bM0) who developed a slowly enlarging left-sided cervical mass during oncologic follow-up. The lesion was painless and showed no overlying skin changes. Neck ultrasonography demonstrated a hypoechoic, round mass over the sternothyroid muscle with an elongated hyperechoic compartment and no Doppler flow. Surgical excision was performed; macroscopy suggested a lymph node. Histopathology excluded malignancy and unexpectedly revealed adult Enterobius vermicularis containing eggs embedded within inflammatory stroma. C-reactive protein was mildly elevated without leukocytosis or eosinophilia. Stool examination was negative. Whole-body MRI revealed no other extraintestinal foci. Albendazole was administered in two doses separated by two weeks with clinical resolution; the patient remains under outpatient surveillance. This case highlights that ectopic enterobiasis can mimic cervical lymphadenopathy and metastatic disease, particularly in immunocompromised patients, and that histopathology is critical for diagnosis and management.

Introduction

Enterobiasis (pinworm infection) is among the most frequent human helminth infections worldwide [1]. The infection is typically confined to the intestinal lumen and perianal area; however, ectopic (extraintestinal) manifestations have been reported, often presenting as inflammatory masses that can mimic neoplasia [2]. We describe an exceptionally unusual presentation of Enterobius vermicularis identified within a cervical mass in an adult with CVID after colorectal cancer surgery, emphasizing diagnostic pitfalls in oncologic follow-up.

Case

A 50-year-old non-smoking man with CVID and a history of cecal adenocarcinoma presented with a gradually enlarging left-sided neck mass evolving over several months. CVID was diagnosed in May 2023, and regular IVIG replacement (Privigen® 0.4 g/kg every four weeks) was initiated. In July 2023, moderately differentiated cecal adenocarcinoma (pT3N1bM0) was treated with right hemicolectomy followed by six cycles of adjuvant XELOX (oxaliplatin/capecitabine) between August 2023 and January 2024 with good tolerance. Comorbidities included hypothyroidism, heart failure with reduced ejection fraction, prior pulmonary embolism, hypercholesterolemia, and hip osteoarthritis.

On examination, the cervical mass was palpable and painless, without warmth or erythema and without visible overlying skin changes. The patient denied gastrointestinal symptoms and reported no head-and-neck trauma.

Neck ultrasonography showed a hypoechoic, round mass over the sternothyroid muscle with an elongated hyperechoic compartment and no detectable blood flow on Doppler imaging (Fig. 1A–B). Histopathologic examination excluded nodal metastasis and instead demonstrated adult Enterobius vermicularis containing eggs embedded within inflammatory stroma (Fig. 1C–D). The presence of parasite structures within the lesion together with a tissue inflammatory reaction supported a true ectopic infection rather than specimen contamination.

Laboratory testing revealed mildly elevated CRP with no leukocytosis and no eosinophilia. Stool examination for Enterobius vermicularis was negative. Whole-body MRI did not identify other extraintestinal foci.

Discussion

This case demonstrates an exceptionally rare extraintestinal manifestation of Enterobius vermicularis presenting as a deep cervical mass in an immunocompromised adult. In oncologic follow-up, a new cervical lesion raises immediate concern for metastatic disease; yet, imaging findings are non-specific and histopathology can decisively change management.

Ectopic enterobiasis has been described in multiple anatomic sites and can produce granulomatous or necrotizing inflammatory lesions that mimic tumors [2]. Involvement in the head-and-neck region is exceptionally uncommon; to our knowledge, only one prior case of an Enterobius-related neck granuloma has been published [3]. Similar diagnostic confusion has been reported for extraintestinal lesions initially interpreted as metastatic disease in patients with malignancy [4], [5].

The mechanism by which E. vermicularis reaches deep cervical tissues remains uncertain. Plausible explanations include inadvertent transfer of eggs from contaminated hands to skin with microscopic abrasions, followed by a localized inflammatory response; alternatively, aberrant migration and penetration through disrupted mucosal barriers has been proposed for other extraintestinal sites [2]. In the present patient, a combination of immune dysfunction (CVID), prior intestinal surgery, and recent chemotherapy may have altered mucosal and systemic defenses, facilitating ectopic survival long enough to generate a mass-like lesion [6]. These hypotheses remain speculative and cannot be proven from this single case.

Stool examination may be negative in enterobiasis, and diagnosis of intestinal infection is classically based on detection of eggs from the perianal region (e.g., early-morning adhesive tape sampling) [1]. Therefore, a negative stool result does not exclude enterobiasis. In this case, perianal sampling was not available, which is an important limitation.

Management of ectopic enterobiasis is typically based on (1) local excision or drainage when required for diagnosis or symptom control, and (2) systemic anthelmintic therapy to eradicate residual intestinal infection and reduce reinfection risk [1]. Standard regimens use albendazole, mebendazole, or pyrantel pamoate, typically repeated after 2 weeks [1]. Household treatment and hygiene measures (handwashing, nail trimming, laundering of bedding/clothing) are commonly recommended to prevent reinfection [1].

Conclusion

Extraintestinal enterobiasis should be considered in the differential diagnosis of atypical cervical masses—particularly in immunocompromised patients and those with prior gastrointestinal surgery—because it can closely mimic malignant disease. Histopathologic assessment remains the cornerstone for diagnosis and appropriate management.
