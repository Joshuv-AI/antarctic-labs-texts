Further analysis of Peeling Sequences
Dániel Gábor Simon 1,2

Abstract
. Let P ⊂ ℝ 2 P\subset\mathbb{R}^{2} be a set of n n points in general position. A peeling sequence of P P is a list of its points, such that if we remove the points from P P in that order, we always remove the next point from the convex hull of the remainder of P P . Using the methodology of Dumitrescu and Tóth [ 11 ] , with a more careful analysis, we improve the upper bound on the minimum number of peeling sequences for an n n point set in the plane from 12.29 n / 100 12.29^{n}/100 to 9.78 n / 500 9.78^{n}/500 .

1.
Introduction

A set of points
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
lies in general position if no three points of
P
are collinear. Let
P
be such a set. We define a peeling algorithm in the following way: Take the convex hull of the current point set, remove one point from the hull, and write down its label. Repeat on the set without that point. Stop when there are no more points left in the set.

This way, we generated a sequence of the labels of the vertices. It is called a
peeling sequence
. The set of all peeling sequences consists of all orderings of the labels that can be achieved by the above algorithm.

Definition
.

Given a point set
P
in general position in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, let
g(P)
denote the number of peeling sequences of
P
.

Let

g(n)=\min_{|P|=n}g(P)
, where the minimum is taken over all
n
-element point sets
P
in general position in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Peeling sequences in this form were first introduced by Dumitrescu
[
8
]
. The currently known best bounds are
\Omega(3^{n})\leq g(n)\leq O(12.29^{n})
by Dumitrescu
[
8
]
, and Dumitrescu and Tóth
[
11
]
respectively. In this paper, we improve the upper bound to
g(n)\leq O(9.78^{n})
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
g(n)
be the minimum number of peeling sequences a set of
n
points can have. For any
n\geq 6
,

g(n)\leq\frac{9.78^{n}}{500}.

The question was also investigated in higher dimensions. The interested reader can find the current best upper bounds in the author’s other paper
[
20
]
.

Related work.
Many previously investigated properties of point sets are connected to the convexity of the set. In our example, the number of peeling sequences is maximal for a convex
n
-gon, and minimal for some highly nonconvex structure. Similar parameters connected to the convexity are the number of
triangulations
(see
[
1
,
10
,
18
]
for some bounds), the number of
polygonizations
(see
[
13
]
,
[
19
]
), the number of
non-crossing perfect matchings
(see
[
6
]
), etc.

Peelings of sets of points have also appeared in the literature before. Many results are about
convex-layer peeling

[
5
,
7
,
15
]
, where at each step all vertices of the current convex hull are being removed, and the total number of layers is of interest. Such methods have been used in the proof of the famous empty hexagon theorem
[
14
]
[
16
]
; see also
[
9
]
. Computer graphics also uses different peeling algorithms, the affine curve shortening
[
2
,
12
,
17
]
is an interesting example.

2.
Construction

The construction is identical to that defined by Dumitrescu and Tóth
[
11
]
. We describe it here for self-containment purposes and to facilitate understanding of the other types of sets considered in this paper.

We define the sets
S_{n}
recursively for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Each set
S_{n}
contains
n
points, and is "flat": all points are very close to a line compared to the minimum pairwise distance in
S_{n}
.

Base cases:

Let
S_{1}=\{\mathbf{o_{1}}\}
be a single point,
S_{2}=\{\mathbf{o_{2}},\mathbf{u}\}
be two distinct points on the
x

Recursive step:
For
n\geq 3
we construct
S_{n}
as follows. Let
n=n_{1}+n_{2}+n_{3}
where each
n_{i}
is either
\lfloor\frac{n}{3}\rfloor
or
\lceil\frac{n}{3}\rceil
, and
n_{1}\geq n_{2}\geq n_{3}
. Assume recursively that
S_{n_{1}},S_{n_{2}},S_{n_{3}}
have already been defined.

Placement of the sets:
Let
\mathbf{o_{n}}
be a point serving as the origin of
S_{n}
. Let
r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}
be three rays emanating from
\mathbf{o_{n}}
with angles of
120\degree
between consecutive rays in clockwise order. Take

to lie along the positive
x
-axis.
For each

, place a rotated and translated copy of

so that its first ray’s direction aligns with ray

, as illustrated in Figure

x

S_{n_{1}}
has strictly larger
x
-coordinate than any point of
S_{n_{2}}
, and any point of
S_{n_{3}}
has a strictly smaller

3.
Estimation Lemmas

The following statements are required for the proof of Theorem
1.1
. They were also used in the analysis of Dumitrescu and Tóth
[
11
]
.

Definition
.

For any
0\leq p\leq 1
, define

H(p)=-p\log_{2}p-(1-p)\log_{2}{(1-p)}

the
binary entropy function
.

With this function, we can use the following estimate from
[
4
]
, Corollary 10.3.

Lemma 3.1
.

For any integer
n\geq 1
, and
0\leq\alpha\leq\tfrac{1}{2}
,

\binom{n}{\alpha n}\leq 2^{nH(\alpha)}

The next lemma is a simple estimate.

Lemma 3.2
.

For any positive integers
n,k,l
satisfying
\frac{n}{l+1}\leq k\leq n
,

\binom{n}{\lceil k\rceil}\leq l\binom{n}{\lfloor k\rfloor}.

The proofs of the next two lemmata are also discussed in
[
11
]
Section 3.2. The first follows from a simple induction argument.

Lemma 3.3
.

For all integers
n\geq 36
and
0\leq k\leq n
,

\binom{n}{k}\leq\frac{2^{n}}{7}

Moreover,

\binom{n}{k}\leq\frac{2^{n}}{2}

for all positive integers
n
.

Proof 3.4
.

It is easy to verify the statement for
n=36
. For larger
n
, observe that

\binom{n}{k}=\binom{n-1}{k-1}+\binom{n-1}{k}\leq 2\times\frac{2^{n-1}}{7}=\frac{2^{n}}{7},

which completes the induction.
The moreover part holds for
n=1
, and the same inductive argument applies.

To prove the next lemma, we need to define
simplified peeling sequences
.

Definition
.

Let
Z=X_{1}\cup X_{2}\cup\dots\cup X_{k}
where the sets
X_{i}
are pairwise disjoint. Let
\pi
be a peeling sequence of
Z
. Replace each point in
\pi
with the symbol
i
, where
i
is the index of the set
X_{i}
containing the point. The resulting sequence
\pi^{*}
of indices is called a
simplified peeling sequence
of
Z
with respect to this partition.

Lemma 3.5
.

[Dumitrescu, Tóth]

Let
Z=X_{1}\cup X_{2}\cup\dots\cup X_{k}
be a set of points in general position, such that for all
i\neq j

X_{i}\cap X_{j}=\emptyset
,
|X_{i}|=

n_{i}
, and
|Z|=\sum_{i=1}^{k}n_{i}=n
. Then

g(Z)\leq\binom{n}{n_{1},n_{2},n_{3},\dots,n_{k}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}g(X_{i}).

Proof 3.6
.

We first estimate how many peeling sequences correspond to the same simplified peeling sequence
\pi^{*}
.

Fix an index
i
. Restricting
\pi
to the positions where
\pi^{*}
equals
i
yields a subsequence
\pi_{i}
. This subsequence must be a peeling sequence of
X_{i}
, as otherwise those points could not have been removed in this order even in the absence of other points. Thus, for each
i
, there are at most
g(X_{i})
possible choices for
\pi_{i}
. Therefore, the number of peeling sequences corresponding to a fixed
\pi^{*}
is at most

\prod_{i=1}^{k}g(X_{i}).

To conclude the proof, it is enough to count the number of distinct simplified peeling sequences.
Each such sequence consists of exactly
n_{i}
occurrences of the symbol
i
, for each
i=1,\dots,k
. Hence, the total number of such sequences is at most

\binom{n}{n_{1},n_{2},n_{3},\dots,n_{k}}=\frac{n!}{n_{1}!n_{2}!n_{3}!\dots n_{k}!}.

Multiplying the two upper bounds concludes the proof.

4.
Proof of the main Theorem

We prove the following theorem, from which Theorem
1.1
follows.

Theorem 4.1
.

For all
n\geq 6
,

g(S_{n})\leq 9.78^{n}/500\quad\text{and}\quad g(B_{n})\leq 8.67^{n}/500.

Proof 4.2
.

We proceed by induction on
n
.

For the base cases, we verify the statement for
6\leq n\leq 53
, since all larger instances are constructed from these.

If we construct
S_{n}
and
B_{n}
as discussed before, they satisfy the required bounds for these values of
n
. Note that in the first
\frac{n}{3}-1
steps of peeling
S_{n}
, the convex hull consists of
3
vertices. This yields the bound

g(S_{n})\leq 3^{\lfloor\frac{n}{3}\rfloor}\left\lceil\frac{2n}{3}\right\rceil!

which is already sufficient in the given range of
n
.

For
B_{n}
, only the first
\lfloor\frac{n}{9}\rfloor
steps guarantee exactly
3
vertices on the convex hull. This gives

g(B_{n})\leq 3^{\lfloor\frac{n}{9}\rfloor}\left\lceil\frac{2n}{3}\right\rceil!,

which is also sufficient in the required range of
n
.

For
n\geq 54
, we conclude by induction. Suppose the statement holds for all

\lfloor\frac{n}{9}\rfloor\leq k\leq n-1.

That is,
S_{k}
has at most
a^{k}/500
peeling sequences, and
B_{k}
has at most
b^{k}/500
, where
a=9.78
, and
b=8.67
.

We analyse the behaviour of each
B_{n}
and
S_{n}
, based on the first step at which an entire ray disappears. Depending on how many vertices have been removed by that point, we can deduce some information about the remaining two other rays by the pigeonhole principle. By induction, we can bound the number of peeling sequences on these rays, and combine them with Lemma
3.5
.

Using the sets
B_{n}
has the purpose of making these pigeonhole principle arguments more refined. In particular, it enables us to apply stronger bounds to any ray that has lost at least a
2/9
fraction of its vertices. Ultimately, this leads to a stronger bound than only analysing the
S_{n}
-s.

4.1.
B_{n}

We begin with the analysis of
B_{n}
. Throughout this subsection, we assume inductively that the theorem holds for all sets up to
S_{n-1}
and
B_{n-1}
. We split the peeling sequences into classes according to the index of the last element whose removal made one of the
3
main rays to disappear. We call this index
s
. We refer to Figure
4
for an example value of
s
.

In all the following cases, unless stated otherwise, we assume that the first ray to disappear during peeling is the one that initially contains only a single subray. Let
\sigma_{i}
denote the total number of peeling sequences in case
i
, for
1\leq i\leq 6
. We show that for each
i
,

\sigma_{i}\leq C_{i}b^{n}

for suitable constants
C_{i}
, where
\sum_{i=1}^{6}C_{i}<1/500
.
Then we can conclude that

\sigma=\sum_{i=1}^{6}\sigma_{i}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{6}C_{i}b^{n}\leq b^{n}/500,

proving the first half of the theorem.

Figure 4
.
An unflattened representation of
B_{18}
, with a labeling of the starting elements of a peeling sequence. For simplicity, we place vertices on the same ray as collinear, but they are just close to it. Here, the first ray disappears after the
7
th removed element, hence
s=7
would hold for this sequence.

Case 1

\lfloor n/9\rfloor\leq s\leq\lceil 7n/27\rceil.

Let
T_{1}
be the set of all simplified peeling sequences on the tripartition defined by the
3
rays, for which the smallest ray disappears at an index
s
in the above interval.

Now we estimate
|T_{1}|
. The small ray must disappear within the first at most
\lceil 7n/27\rceil
steps, so their indices can be in at most
\binom{\lceil 7n/27\rceil}{\lceil n/9\rceil}
allocations. For each such allocation, the remaining two rays can be placed in at most
\binom{\lceil 2n/3\rceil}{\lceil n/3\rceil}
ways.

Then we can bound the size of
T_{1}
by the use of our estimation lemmas in section
4
, and by using Lemma
3.2
with
l=2
.

\begin{split}|T_{1}|\leq\binom{\lceil 7n/27\rceil}{\lceil n/9\rceil}\cdot\binom{\lceil 2n/3\rceil}{\lfloor n/3\rfloor}&\leq 2\binom{\lceil 7n/27\rceil}{\lfloor\frac{3}{7}\lceil 7n/27\rceil\rfloor}\cdot\binom{\lceil 2n/3\rceil}{\lfloor n/3\rfloor}\\
&\leq 8\cdot 2^{7H(3/7)n/27+2n/3}/7\leq 2^{0.9221n}\cdot 8/7\end{split}

For each
\pi^{*}\in T_{1}
, the order of removals up to step
s
is fixed.
Hence, the smallest ray’s element order is determined, and the possible orderings of the other elements are bounded above by
g(S_{\lceil\frac{n}{3}\rceil})g(S_{\lceil\frac{n}{3}\rceil})
.
Hence, by using induction, the total number of such peeling sequences is at most:

\sigma_{1}\leq|T_{1}|\cdot g(S_{\lceil n/3\rceil})^{2}\leq(2^{0.9221n}\cdot 8/7)\cdot(a^{2n/3}/250000)\cdot a^{4/3}\leq C_{1}\cdot b^{n}

This inequality holds provided that

b^{n}\geq a^{2n/3}\cdot 2^{0.9221n}

8\cdot a^{2/3}/1750000=C_{1}.

Case 2

\lceil 7n/27\rceil\leq s\leq\lceil 8n/27\rceil

In this range, by the pigeonhole principle, one of the other two rays must have turned into a subset of
B_{\lceil n/3\rceil}
by step
s
.

We estimate
T_{2}
:

|T_{2}|\leq\binom{\lceil 8n/27\rceil}{\lceil n/9\rceil}\cdot\binom{\lceil 2n/3\rceil}{\lfloor n/3\rfloor}\leq 8\cdot 2^{8H(3/8)n/27+2n/3}/7\leq 2^{0.9495n}\cdot 8/7

By induction and a similar argument to the previous case, the total number of such peeling sequences is at most:

\begin{split}\sigma_{2}&\leq|T_{2}|\cdot g(S_{\lceil n/3\rceil})\cdot g(B_{\lceil n/3\rceil})\\
&\leq(2^{0.9495n}\cdot 8/7)\cdot(a^{n/3}/500)\cdot(b^{n/3}/500)\cdot a^{2/3}\cdot b^{2/3}\\
&\leq C_{2}\cdot b^{n}\end{split}

This inequality holds if

b^{n}\geq a^{n/2}\cdot 2^{1.4243n}

8\cdot a^{2/3}\cdot b^{2/3}/1750000=C_{2}.

Case 3

\lceil 8n/27\rceil\leq s\leq\lceil 12n/27\rceil

By the pigeonhole principle, now we know that in the first
s
steps, either both of the remaining rays have turned into a subset of a
B_{\lceil n/3\rceil}
, or one ray has lost an entire subray.

Using Lemma
3.2
with
l=3
, we obtain

\begin{split}|T_{3}|\leq\binom{\lceil 12n/27\rceil}{\lceil n/9\rceil}\cdot\binom{\lceil 2n/3\rceil}{\lfloor n/3\rfloor}&\leq 3\cdot\binom{\lceil 12n/27\rceil}{\lfloor\frac{3}{12}\lceil 12n/27\rceil\rfloor}\cdot\binom{\lceil 2n/3\rceil}{\lfloor n/3\rfloor}\\
&\leq 12\cdot 2^{12\cdot H(3/12)n/27+2n/3}/7\\
&\leq 2^{1.0273n}\cdot 12/7\end{split}

If a ray loses a subray, it is a subset of two copies of
S_{\lceil n/9\rceil}
. An upper bound on the number of ways the remainder of this ray can be peeled follows by Lemma
3.5
, showing it is at most
\binom{2\lceil n/9\rceil}{\lceil n/9\rceil}\cdot g(S_{\lceil n/9\rceil})^{2}\leq 2\cdot 2^{2n/9}\cdot g(S_{\lceil n/9\rceil})^{2}
. Hence,

\begin{split}\sigma_{3}&\leq|T_{3}|\cdot max[(g(B_{\lceil n/3\rceil})\cdot g(B_{\lceil n/3\rceil}),g(S_{\lceil n/3\rceil})\cdot 2\cdot 2^{2n/9}\cdot g(S_{\lceil n/9\rceil})^{2}]\\
&\leq(2^{1.0273n}\cdot 12/7)\cdot max[(b^{n/3}/500)^{2}\cdot b^{4/3},\\
&(a^{n/3}/500)\cdot a^{2/3}\cdot 2^{2n/9+1}\cdot(a^{n/9}/500)^{2}\cdot a^{16/9}]\\
&\leq C_{3}\cdot b^{n}\end{split}

This inequality holds if

b^{n}\geq max(2^{3.0819n},a^{5n/9}\cdot 2^{1.2496n})

max(12\cdot b^{4/3}/1750000,24\cdot a^{22/9}/875000000)=C_{3}.

Case 4

\lceil 12n/27\rceil\leq s\leq\lceil 15n/27\rceil

By the pigeonhole principle, at least two additional subrays have disappeared until step
s
. This can happen in two ways: Either one ray has lost
2
subrays so it became a subset of an
S_{\lceil n/9\rceil}
, or both rays lost
1
-
1
subrays, each becoming a subset of a pair of
S_{\lceil n/9\rceil}
’s.

Using
l=4
in the application of Lemma
3.2
we obtain

|T_{4}|\leq\binom{\lceil 15n/27\rceil}{\lceil n/9\rceil}\cdot\binom{\lceil 2n/3\rceil}{\lfloor n/3\rfloor}\leq 16\cdot 2^{15\cdot H(3/15)n/27+2n/3}/7\leq 2^{1.0678n}\cdot 16/7.

If a ray loses a subray, it is a subset of two copies of
S_{\lceil n/9\rceil}
. An upper bound on the number of ways the remainder of this ray can be peeled follows by Lemma
3.5
, showing it is at most
\binom{2\lceil n/9\rceil}{\lceil n/9\rceil}\cdot g(S_{\lceil n/9\rceil})^{2}\leq 2\cdot 2^{2n/9}\cdot g(S_{\lceil n/9\rceil})^{2}
. Hence,

\begin{split}\sigma_{4}&\leq|T_{4}|\cdot max[(g(S_{\lceil n/9\rceil})\cdot g(S_{\lceil n/3\rceil}),4\cdot 2^{4n/9}g(S_{\lceil n/9\rceil})^{4})]\\
&\leq(2^{1.0678n}\cdot 16/7)\cdot max[(a^{n/9}/500)\cdot(a^{n/3}/500)\cdot a^{14/9},\\
&2^{4n/9}\cdot(a^{n/9}/500)^{4}\cdot 4\cdot a^{32/9}]\\
&\leq C_{4}\cdot b^{n}\end{split}

Since the second part of the maximum is strictly larger for
n\geq 54
, this inequality holds when:

b^{n}\geq a^{4n/9}\cdot 2^{1.5123n}

64a^{32/9}/437500000000=C_{4}.

Case 5

\lceil 15n/27\rceil\leq s\leq\lceil 21n/27\rceil

By the pigeonhole principle, at least two extra subrays disappeared from the construction until step
s
. This can happen in only one way: one ray is a subset of
S_{\lceil n/9\rceil}
, and the other one is a subset of a pair of
S_{\lceil n/9\rceil}
.

We use
l=6
when applying Lemma

.

|T_{5}|\leq\binom{\lceil 21n/27\rceil}{\lceil n/9\rceil}\cdot\binom{\lceil 2n/3\rceil}{\lfloor n/3\rfloor}\leq 24\cdot 2^{21\cdot H(3/21)n/27+2n/3}/7\leq 2^{1.127n}\cdot 24/7

The total number of such sequences is at most:

\begin{split}\sigma_{5}&\leq|T_{5}|\cdot g(S_{\lceil n/9\rceil})\cdot 2\cdot 2^{2n/9}\cdot g(S_{\lceil n/9\rceil})^{2}\\
&\leq(2^{1.127n}\cdot 24/7)\cdot(a^{n/9}/500)\cdot 2^{2n/9}\cdot(a^{n/9}/500)^{2}\cdot a^{24/9}\cdot 2\leq C_{5}\cdot b^{n}\end{split}

This inequality holds if

b^{n}\geq a^{n/3}\cdot 2^{1.3493n}

48\cdot a^{24/9}/875000000=C_{5}.

Case 6

\lfloor 9n/27\rfloor\leq s\leq\lceil 21n/27\rceil

In this case, the first ray to disappear is a longer
S_{\lceil n/3\rceil}
ray, not the short ray.

We count with all the possible
s
values at once and give an upper bound on the number of simplified peeling sequences again, similar to the other cases. Here we can take

|T_{6}|\leq\binom{\lceil 21n/27\rceil}{\lceil n/3\rceil}\cdot\binom{\lceil 12n/27\rceil}{\lceil n/9\rceil}\leq 24\cdot 2^{21\cdot H(9/21)n/27+4n/9}/7\leq 2^{1.2108n}\cdot 24/7

The total number of such sequences is at most:

\sigma_{6}\leq|T_{6}|\cdot(g(S_{\lceil n/3\rceil})\cdot g(S_{\lceil n/9\rceil}))\leq(2^{1.2108n}\cdot 24/7)\cdot(a^{n/3}/500)\cdot(a^{n/9}/500)\cdot a^{14/9}\leq C_{6}\cdot b^{n}

This inequality holds if

b^{n}\geq a^{4n/9}\cdot 2^{1.2108n}

24\cdot a^{14/9}/1750000=C_{6}.

, and on the values of the coefficients, in order to make the induction work. If they are all fulfilled by

,

.

So

b^{n}\geq 8.47^{n}\geq max(2^{3.0819n},a^{5n/9}\cdot 2^{1.2496n})

b^{n}\geq 7.86^{n}\geq a^{4n/9}\cdot 2^{1.5123n}

b^{n}\geq 5.45^{n}\geq a^{n/3}\cdot 2^{1.3493n}

b^{n}\geq 6.38^{n}\geq a^{4n/9}\cdot 2^{1.2108n}

We can clearly see that the conditions hold for

.

We also calculate the coefficients:

C_{1}=8\cdot a^{2/3}/1750000\leq 0.00003

C_{2}=8\cdot a^{2/3}\cdot b^{2/3}/1750000\leq 0.00009

C_{4}=64\cdot a^{32/9}/437500000000\leq 0.00001

C_{6}=24\cdot a^{14/9}/1750000\leq 0.00048

Since

, the induction for

follows.

S_{n}
.

4.2.
{S_{n}}

Now we perform the same type of analysis for
a
and the sets
S_{n}
. As before, we always assume by induction that the theorem holds up until
B_{n-1}
and
S_{n-1}
.

As previously, let
s
denote the index of the last element of the first disappearing ray in the modified peeling sequence.

Case 1

\lfloor 9n/27\rfloor\leq s\leq\lceil 13n/27\rceil

Using
l=2
in Lemma
3.2
we obtain

|T_{1}|\leq 3\cdot\binom{\lceil 13n/27\rceil}{\lfloor n/3\rfloor}\cdot\binom{\lceil 2n/3\rceil}{\lfloor n/3\rfloor}\leq 24\cdot 2^{13\cdot H(4/13)n/27+2n/3}/7\leq 24\cdot 2^{1.0955n}/7

The number of peeling sequences on each remaining rays can be upper bounded by
g(S_{\lceil n/3\rceil})
. Thus, the total number of sequences is at most:

\sigma_{1}\leq|T_{1}|\cdot(g(S_{\lceil n/3\rceil})^{2}\leq(24\cdot 2^{1.0955n}/7)\cdot(a^{n/3}/500)^{2}\cdot a^{4/3}\leq C_{1}\cdot a^{n}

The last inequality holds if

a^{n}\geq 2^{3.2865n}

24\cdot a^{4/3}/1750000=C_{1}.

Case 2

\lceil 13n/27\rceil\leq s\leq\lceil 14n/27\rceil

By the pigeonhole principle, at least one of the remaining rays got reduced to a subset of a
B_{\lceil n/3\rceil}
.

|T_{2}|\leq 3\cdot\binom{\lceil 14n/27\rceil}{\lfloor n/3\rfloor}\cdot\binom{\lceil 2n/3\rceil}{\lfloor n/3\rfloor}\leq 24\cdot 2^{14\cdot H(5/14)n/27+2n/3}/7\leq 24\cdot 2^{1.1543n}/7

Thus,

\begin{split}\sigma_{2}&\leq|T_{2}|\cdot g(S_{\lceil n/3\rceil})\cdot g(B_{\lceil n/3\rceil})\\
&\leq(24\cdot 2^{1.1543n}/7)\cdot(a^{n/3}/500)\cdot(b^{n/3}/500)\cdot b^{2/3}\cdot a^{2/3}\\
&\leq C_{2}\cdot a^{n}\end{split}

The last inequality holds if

a^{n}\geq 2^{1.7315n}\cdot b^{n/2}

24\cdot a^{2/3}\cdot b^{2/3}/1750000=C_{2}.

Case 3

\lceil 14n/27\rceil\leq s\leq\lceil 15n/27\rceil

Either both remaining rays got reduced to a subset of
B_{\lceil n/3\rceil}
, or one of them lost an entire subray and became a subset of a union of two
S_{\lceil n/9\rceil}
’s.

|T_{3}|\leq 3\cdot\binom{\lceil 15n/27\rceil}{\lfloor n/3\rfloor}\cdot\binom{\lceil 2n/3\rceil}{\lfloor n/3\rfloor}\leq 24\cdot 2^{15\cdot H(6/15)n/27+2n/3}/7\leq 24\cdot 2^{1.2061n}/7

By Lemma
3.5
, the number of ways a pair of
S_{\lceil n/9\rceil}
can be peeled is at most
\binom{2\lceil n/9\rceil}{\lceil n/9\rceil}\cdot g(S_{\lceil n/9\rceil})^{2}\leq 2\cdot 2^{2n/9}\cdot g(S_{\lceil n/9\rceil})^{2}
.

Thus,

\begin{split}\sigma_{3}&\leq|T_{3}|\cdot max[g(S_{\lceil n/3\rceil})\cdot 2\cdot 2^{2n/9}\cdot g(S_{\lceil n/9\rceil})^{2},g(B_{\lceil n/3\rceil})^{2}]\\
&\leq(24\cdot 2^{1.2061n}/7)\cdot max[(a^{n/3}/500)\cdot 2^{2n/9}(a^{n/9}/500)^{2}\cdot 2\cdot a^{22/9},\\
&(b^{n/3}/500)^{2}\cdot b^{4/3}]\\
&\leq C_{3}\cdot a^{n}\end{split}

The last inequality holds if

a^{n}\geq max(2^{3.2138n},2^{1.2061n}\cdot b^{2n/3})

max(48\cdot a^{22/9}/875000000,24\cdot b^{4/3}/1750000)=C_{3}.

Case 4

\lceil 15n/27\rceil\leq s\leq\lceil 17n/27\rceil

At least one of the remaining rays lost a subray, say ray
A
. In addition to that, either ray
A
misses another sub-subray (a ray of a subray), or the other ray is a subset of
B_{\lceil n/3\rceil}
.

|T_{4}|\leq 3\cdot\binom{\lceil 17n/27\rceil}{\lfloor n/3\rfloor}\cdot\binom{\lceil 2n/3\rceil}{\lfloor n/3\rfloor}\leq 24\cdot 2^{17\cdot H(8/17)n/27+2n/3}/7\leq 24\cdot 2^{1.2948n}/7

When the two rays are a subset of a
B_{\lceil n/3\rceil}
and a subset of a pair of
S_{\lceil n/9\rceil}
respectively, we already know what estimates we can use.

When one ray is a subset of the union of a copy of
S_{\lceil n/9\rceil}
and two copies of
S_{\lceil n/27\rceil}
, by Lemma
3.5
and the estimation lemmas, we get an upper bound of
\binom{\lceil 3n/27\rceil+2\lceil n/27\rceil}{\lfloor{3n/27}\rfloor}\binom{2\lceil n/27\rceil}{\lceil n/27\rceil}\cdot g(S_{\lceil n/9\rceil})\cdot g(S_{\lceil n/27\rceil}^{2})\leq 8\cdot 2^{7n/27}\cdot g(S_{\lceil n/9\rceil})\cdot g(S_{\lceil n/27\rceil})^{2}
for the number of peeling sequences of the ray.

Thus,

\begin{split}\sigma_{4}&\leq|T_{4}|\cdot max[g(S_{\lceil n/3\rceil})\cdot 2^{7n/27+3}\cdot g(S_{\lceil n/9\rceil})\cdot g(S_{\lceil n/27\rceil})^{2},\\
&g(B_{\lceil n/3\rceil})\cdot 2\cdot 2^{2n/9}\cdot g(S_{\lceil n/9\rceil})^{2}]\\
&\leq max[(192\cdot 2^{1.2948n}/7)\cdot(a^{n/3}/500)\cdot 2^{7n/27}\cdot(a^{n/9}/500)\cdot(a^{n/27}/500)^{2}\cdot a^{98/27},\\
&(b^{n/3}/500)\cdot 2^{2n/9}\cdot(a^{n/9}/500)^{2}\cdot 2\cdot a^{16/9}\cdot b^{2/3}]\\
&\leq C_{4}\cdot a^{n}\end{split}

The last inequality holds if

a^{n}\geq max(2^{3.2277n},2^{1.9506n}\cdot b^{3n/7})

max(192\cdot a^{98/27}/437500000000,48\cdot a^{16/9}\cdot b^{2/3}/875000000)=C_{4}.

Case 5

\lceil 17n/27\rceil\leq s\leq\lceil 18n/27\rceil

Either two different subbrays have disappeared, or the two rays got reduced to a subset of
B_{\lceil n/3\rceil}
, and to a subset of
S_{\lceil n/9\rceil}\cup S_{\lceil n/27\rceil}
respectively.
The two entire subrays could miss from two separate rays or from the same ray. This gives
3
cases.

|T_{5}|\leq 3\binom{\lceil 18n/27\rceil}{\lfloor n/3\rfloor}\cdot\binom{\lceil 2n/3\rceil}{\lfloor n/3\rfloor}\leq 12\cdot 2^{2n/3+2n/3}/49\leq 12\cdot 2^{4n/3}/49

When one ray is a subset of a copy of
S_{\lceil n/9\rceil}
and of
S_{\lceil n/27\rceil}
, by Lemma
3.5
and the estimation lemmas, we get an upper bound of
\binom{\lceil 3n/27\rceil+\lceil n/27\rceil}{\lfloor{3n/27}\rfloor}\cdot g(S_{\lceil n/9\rceil})\cdot g(S_{\lceil n/27\rceil})\leq 2\cdot 2^{4n/27}\cdot g(S_{\lceil n/9\rceil})\cdot g(S_{\lceil n/27\rceil})
for the number of peeling sequences of the ray.

Thus,

\begin{split}\sigma_{5}&\leq|T_{5}|\cdot max[g(B_{\lceil n/3\rceil})\cdot 2\cdot 2^{4n/27}\cdot g(S_{\lceil n/9\rceil})\cdot g(S_{\lceil n/27\rceil}),\\
&g(S_{\lceil n/3\rceil})\cdot g(S_{\lceil n/9\rceil}),4\cdot 2^{4n/9}\cdot g(S_{\lceil n/9\rceil})^{4}]\\
&\leq(12\cdot 2^{4n/3}/49)\cdot max[(b^{n/3}/500)\cdot 2^{4n/27}(a^{n/9}/500)\cdot(a^{n/27}/500)\cdot 2\cdot a^{50/27}\cdot b^{2/3},\\
&(a^{n/3}/500)\cdot(a^{n/9}/500)\cdot a^{14/9},2^{4n/9}\cdot(a^{n/9}/500)^{4}\cdot 4a^{32/9}]\\
&\leq C_{5}\cdot a^{n}\end{split}

The last inequality holds if

a^{n}\geq max(2^{40n/23}\cdot b^{9n/23},2^{12n/5},2^{16n/5})

max[24\cdot a^{50/27}\cdot b^{2/3}/612500,12\cdot a^{14/9}/1225,48\cdot a^{32/9}/306250000]\cdot 10^{-4}=C_{5}.

Case 6

\lceil 18n/27\rceil\leq s\leq\lceil 22n/27\rceil

Two subrays have disappeared.
The two subrays could miss from two separate rays or from the same ray, giving two cases.
In the case when the subrays are missing from separate rays, we also know that one of the rays also misses another sub-subray (an
n/27
piece) by the pigeonhole principle. For all these kinds of ray structure, we have our estimates from previous cases.

|T_{6}|\leq 3\binom{\lceil 22n/27\rceil}{\lceil n/3\rceil}\cdot\binom{\lceil 2n/3\rceil}{\lfloor n/3\rfloor}\leq 24\cdot 2^{22H(9/22)n/27+2n/3}/7\leq 24\cdot 2^{1.4620n}/7

Thus,

\begin{split}\sigma_{6}&\leq|T_{6}|\cdot max[g(S_{\lceil n/3\rceil})\cdot g(S_{\lceil n/9\rceil}),\\
&2\cdot 2^{2n/9}\cdot g(S_{\lceil n/9\rceil})^{2}\cdot 8\cdot 2^{7n/27}\cdot g(S_{\lceil n/9\rceil})\cdot g(S_{\lceil n/27\rceil})^{2}]\\
&\leq(24\cdot 2^{1.4620n}/7)\cdot max[(a^{n/3}/500)\cdot(a^{n/9}/500)\cdot a^{14/9},\\
&2^{13n/27}\cdot(a^{n/9}/500)^{3}\cdot(a^{n/27}/500)^{2}\cdot 16\cdot a^{98/27}]\\
&\leq C_{6}\cdot a^{n}\end{split}

The last inequality holds if

a^{n}\geq max(2^{2.26317n},2^{3.28n})

max(24\cdot a^{14/9}/1750000,384\cdot a^{98/27}/437500000000)=C_{6}.

Case 7

\lceil 22n/27\rceil\leq s\leq\lceil 27n/27\rceil

At least three different subrays have disappeared.
Two subrays miss from one ray, and one subray misses from the other one.

|T_{7}|\leq 3\binom{\lceil 27n/27\rceil}{\lceil n/3\rceil}\cdot\binom{\lceil 2n/3\rceil}{\lfloor n/3\rfloor}\leq 24\cdot 2^{27H(9/27)n/3+2n/3}/7\leq 24\cdot 2^{1.5850n}/7

Thus,

\begin{split}\sigma_{7}&\leq|T_{7}|\cdot(g(S_{\lceil n/9\rceil})\cdot 2\cdot 2^{2n/9}\cdot g(S_{\lceil n/9\rceil})^{2})\\
&\leq(24\cdot 2^{1.5850n}/7)\cdot(a^{n/9}/500)^{3}\cdot 2^{2n/9}\cdot 2\cdot a^{24/9}\\
&\leq C_{7}\cdot a^{n}\end{split}

The last inequality holds if

a^{n}\geq 2^{2.7109n}

48\cdot a^{24/9}/875000000=C_{7}.

Collecting all constraints, we need to verify that
a=9.78
satisfies each inequality.

a^{n}\geq 9.76^{n}\geq 2^{3.2865n}

a^{n}\geq 9.78^{n}\geq 2^{1.7315n}\cdot b^{n/2}

a^{n}\geq 9.74^{n}\geq max(2^{3.2138n},2^{1.2061n}\cdot b^{2n/3})

a^{n}\geq 9.76^{n}\geq max(2^{3.2277n},2^{1.9506n}\cdot b^{3n/7})

a^{n}\geq 9.19^{n}\geq max(2^{40n/23}\cdot b^{9n/23},2^{12n/5},2^{16n/5})

a^{n}\geq 9.72^{n}\geq max(2^{2.26317n},2^{3.28n})

a^{n}\geq 6.55^{n}\geq 2^{2.7109n}

We also need to check the sum of the coefficients:

C_{1}=24\cdot a^{4/3}/1750000\leq 0.00029

C_{2}=24\cdot a^{2/3}\cdot b^{2/3}/1750000\leq 0.00027

C_{3}=max(48\cdot a^{22/9}/875000000,24\cdot b^{4/3}/1750000)\leq 0.00025

C_{4}=max(192\cdot a^{98/27}/437500000000,48\cdot a^{16/9}\cdot b^{2/3}/875000000)\leq 0.00002

C_{5}=max(24\cdot a^{\frac{50}{27}}\cdot b^{\frac{2}{3}}/6125,12\cdot a^{\frac{14}{9}}/12.25,48\cdot a^{\frac{32}{9}}/3062500)\cdot 10^{-6}\leq 4\cdot 10^{-5}

C_{6}=max(24\cdot a^{14/9}/1750000,384\cdot a^{98/27}/437500000000)\leq 0.00048

C_{7}=48\cdot a^{24/9}/875000000\leq 0.00003

\sum_{i=1}^{7}C_{i}\leq 0.00138<0.002=\frac{1}{500}
which is sufficient, and concludes the proof of the theorem.

5.
Concluding Remarks

Despite considerable effort, the author was unable to improve the trivial lower bound on the minimal number of peeling sequences, this therefore remains an open problem. The intuition behind the upcoming conjecture comes from the Fractional Erdős-Szekeres Theorem
[
3
]
, which implies that for any
n
there are
\alpha n
many points, such that their convex hull forms a
4
-gon for many steps.

Conjecture 5.1
.

The minimal number of peeling sequences of
n
points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is at least
c(d+1+\epsilon)^{n}
for some positive constants
c
and
\epsilon
. So
g_{d}(n)=\Omega((d+1+\epsilon)^{n})
.

The upper bound can be improved with a more careful case analysis, for example by considering subrays of greater depth. It would also be interesting to find a different way of deriving a strong upper bound on the number of peeling sequences of
S_{n}
. The construction appears optimal: If the next vertex is always chosen randomly from the convex hull, one would expect to only choose between
3
different vertices for most of the steps.
