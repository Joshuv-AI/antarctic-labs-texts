Prevalence of Schistosoma haematobium and Schistosoma mansoni co-infection in sub-saharan Africa: a systematic review and meta-analysis

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSchistosomiasis remains a significant public health challenge, especially in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA). While individual species prevalence is well documented, the global burden of concurrent co-infection remains poorly quantified, despite its significant clinical and therapeutic implications. This study determined the prevalence of Schistosoma haemtobium (Sh) and Schistosoma mansoni (Sm) co-infection in SSA and its variation with age and geographical location.MethodsFollowing PRISMA guidelines, a systematic search of PubMed, African Journal Online, EMBASE and Google Scholar for studies published between 1980 and 2025 reporting Sh and Sm co-infection was conducted. A random-effects model was used to estimate pooled prevalence, with subgroup analyses conducted by geographical region and age. The study protocol was registered with PROSPERO (CRD420251176915).ResultsTwenty-six studies involving 33,121 participants across 11 African countries were included. The overall pooled prevalence of Schistosoma co-infection was 19.1% [95% CI; 12.3%-27.0%, I2= 99.6%, n = 26 studies p < 0.001]. The Western Africa region had the highest pooled prevalence of 26.9% [CI; 14.6%-41.4%, I2 = 99.6%, n = 19 studies, p < 0.001] while the Eastern Africa had the lowest prevalence of 4.8% [CI; 1.7%-9.2%, I2 = 97.2%, n = 5 studies. p < 0.001]. There was a significant difference in the pooled prevalence of Schistosoma co-infection across the various African sub-regions (p < 0.001). There was no significant difference in the pooled prevalence of co-infection across the various age groups (p = 0.47).ConclusionThe prevalence of Schistosoma co-infection is substantial in SSA, particularly within the West African subregion. However, the high heterogeneity observed in this study necessitates a cautious interpretation of the data.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13726-4.

Introduction

Schistosomiasis, a water-borne neglected tropical disease (NTD) caused by blood flukes of the genus Schistosoma, remains a significant public health burden [1]. The disease affects over 250 million people globally, with Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) disproportionately impacted, accounting for an estimated 90% of the global case burden and related mortality [2, 3]. Clinically, acute infection, or Katayama syndrome, presents with systemic symptoms such as fever, malaise, and myalgia [4]. If left untreated, chronic infection can progress to severe secondary complications [5].

In SSA, the disease is primarily caused by two species: Schistosoma haematobium (Sh), the agent of urogenital schistosomiasis, and Schistosoma mansoni (Sm), which causes intestinal and hepato-splenic schistosomiasis [2]. Transmission is contingent upon specific freshwater snail intermediate hosts: Bulinus spp. for S. haematobium and Biomphalaria spp. for S. mansoni. The distinct, yet frequently overlapping, geographical ranges of these parasites are driven by the sympatric occurrence of their respective snail hosts [6, 7], particularly around large water bodies and irrigation schemes.

S. haematobium is largely confined to Africa and the Middle East, while S. mansoni is found in Africa, the Arabian Peninsula, South America, and the Caribbean, the most concentrated areas of co-endemicity occur in SSA [7]. The extensive distribution of both snail vectors across SSA has led to multiple transmission hotspots where co-endemicity is frequently observed [8].

The sympatric distribution of Bulinus and Biomphalaria hosts, often facilitated by ecological changes such as dam construction and irrigation expansion, places populations in SSA at high risk of co-infection with S. haematobium and S. mansoni [9]. Consequently, high rates of mixed infections are reported across the continent.

The dynamics of co-infection are shaped by a confluence of social, environmental, and biological factors. Pooled prevalence estimates across SSA are 25.6% for S. mansoni and 28.8% for S. haematobium, with human infection rates correlating positively with those in snail hosts [10]. Biologically, the physical co-existence of both species within the definitive human host facilitates significant interactions. A robust positive association has been observed, where individuals infected with one species often exhibit a higher intensity of the other [11]. This finding suggests a potential biological mechanism promoting co-susceptibility, beyond shared environmental exposure [11].

Co-infection with S. haematobium and S. mansoni complicates schistosomiasis presentation and prognosis, requiring a combined focus on both urogenital and intestinal morbidity [12]. This complexity demands a critical re-evaluation of control strategies reliant on mass drug administration (MDA). While the efficacy (Egg Reduction Rates) of praziquantel (PZQ) is not significantly altered by co-infection status, co-infected individuals often have higher worm burdens [11]. Consequently, they are less likely to achieve complete parasitological cure following standard treatment [11]. These mixed infections present unique challenges for diagnosis, treatment, and control programs, influencing pathology, treatment efficacy, and transmission dynamics. Despite this, the burden of Schistosoma co-infection in SSA is unknown, representing a critical gap in the literature. This study aims to fill this gap by conducting a systematic review and meta-analysis to determine the prevalence of S. haematobium and S. mansoni co-infection in the SSA and its variation with age and geographical location.

Methodology

Protocol and registration

This systematic review and meta-analysis was designed and executed in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement. The study protocol was registered with PROSPERO with a reference number (CRD420251176915).

Information sources and search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted across the PubMed, African Journals Online (AJOL) EMBASE, and Google Scholar electronic databases to identify relevant studies published between January 1980 and June 2025. The search strategy combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms with Boolean operators and was adapted for the specific syntax of each database. The details of the search items were presented in Supplementary Table 1. The reference lists of all included articles were manually scanned to identify additional eligible publications. There was no language restriction.

Eligibility criteria and study selection

Studies were selected based on the Condition, Context, and Population (CoCoPop) framework. Eligible studies included cross-sectional or cohort studies that reported the prevalence of Schistosoma haematobium and Schistosoma mansoni co-infection. We excluded studies that relied on self-reported diagnoses, reported prevalence of hybrid or ectopic schistosomiasis, reported co-infection in pregnant and immunodeficient populations, abstracts without an accompanying full-text publication and case reports.

The study selection followed a two-stage process involving two independent reviewers. Initially, titles and abstracts of all retrieved records were screened for relevance using Rayyan software [13]. Subsequently, the full texts of potentially relevant articles were retrieved and assessed against the inclusion criteria. Disagreements at either stage were resolved through discussion to reach a consensus or through arbitration by a third reviewer. The selection process is detailed in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1).

Data extraction process

A standardized data extraction form was developed with input from a panel of investigators, including parasitologists. Two reviewers independently extracted data from all included studies. Extracted information included the surname of the first author, publication year, study location (country/region), study design, study duration, sample size, age group of study population (children, adult or mixed population of children and adult), proportion of study participants that were female, mean age of study participants, and the number of individuals with co-infection. A third reviewer resolved any discrepancies.

Quality appraisal and risk of bias

The methodological quality and risk of bias of each included study were independently assessed by two reviewers using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Checklist for Prevalence Studies [14]. This nine-point tool scores one point for each of the criteria satisfied by the study assessed. Studies were categorized as having high (7–9), medium (4–6), or low (0–3) quality. Sensitivity analyses were performed to evaluate the influence of low-quality studies on the final pooled estimates.

Data synthesis and analysis

The study-specific estimates were derived from the DerSimonian-Laird random effects model [15] because of the expected substantial clinical, methodological, and geographical heterogeneity across the included studies spanning multiple African regions.Statistical heterogeneity was quantified using the I2 statistic, where values of < 25%, 25–50%, and > 50% were interpreted as low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively [16].

To investigate sources of heterogeneity, subgroup analyses were conducted based on geographical regions and study population age groups (children, adult or mixed population of children and adult). The Q-test based on ANOVA was used for subgroup comparisons. For studies that reported the prevalence of co-infection in multiple countries or different age groups, each subgroup was analyzed separately [11, 17]. Publication bias was assessed using Egger’s regression test and Begg’s rank correlation test [18]. A contour-enhanced funnel plot was generated to distinguish asymmetry due to publication bias from that due to heterogeneity by displaying regions of statistical significance. The trim-and-fill method was employed to estimate the number of potentially missing studies and compute an adjusted pooled estimate. For sensitivity analysis, we performed a leave-one-out meta-analysis. Each of the 29 included studies was sequentially omitted, and the meta-analysis re-run on the remaining 28 studies using a DerSimonian–Laird random-effects model with the Freeman–Tukey double arcsine transformation. For each iteration, we recorded the recalculated overall pooled prevalence and the between-region test of subgroup differences (Qₑ) Statistical significance for publication bias tests was set at p < 0.05. All statistical analyses were conducted using Stata version 19.0 (Stata Corp., 2025).

Ethical considerations

Institutional ethical approval was not required for this study.

Results

In this systematic literature search, 461 articles were identified, and after excluding duplicates, title, and abstract screening, 73 articles were selected for full-text review. The screening resulted in the inclusion of 26 eligible studies (Fig. 1 shows the process of selecting the eligible studies) [11, 12, 17, 19–41].

The studies were from 11 African countries with a total of 33,121 individuals tested for Schistosoma co-infection.

The publication years were from 1993 to 2024. The sample sizes of component studies ranged from 115 to 12,140 participants, with a mean female proportion of 50.2%. There were 18 studies with exclusively children population [11, 12, 17, 19, 21–26, 28, 30, 32, 34, 37–40]; 7 studies had a mixed population of children and adults, 7 [18, 20, 27, 29, 33, 35, 36], and 2 studies had adult population alone [31, 34]. Twenty-four (82.8%) of the studies had high methodological quality (82.8%, n = 24) [12, 17–21, 23–35, 37–40] (Table 1 shows data extracted from the selected studies).

The overall pooled prevalence of Schistosoma co-infection in SSA was 19.1% [95% CI; 12.3%-27.0%, I2= 99.6%, n = 26 studies p < 0.001]. The Western Africa region showed the highest pooled prevalence of 26.9% [CI: 14.6%-41.4%, I2 = 99.6%, n = 19 studies, p < 0.001]. This was followed by Central Africa, 14.6%. [CI; 0.0%-70.0%, I2 = 99.8%, n = 3 studies p < 0.001], Southern Africa 5.6% [CI; 0.0%-17.0%, I2 = 98.6%, n = 2 studies p < 0.001], and the lowest was in Eastern Africa 4.8% [CI; 1.7.0%-9.2%, I2 = 97.2%, n = 5 studies. p < 0.001]. There was a significant difference in the pooled prevalence of Schistosoma co-infection across the various African sub-regions (p < 0.001). The lowest and highest prevalence rates were seen in Senegal and Mali, respectively. Figure 3 shows the pooled prevalence of Schistosoma co-infection across the various regions of SSA.

The recalculated pooled prevalence ranged from 0.17 (95% CI: 0.11–0.25) to 0.20 (95% CI: 0.12–0.30) across all iterations, a narrow band around the primary estimate. The between-region Qₑ statistic remained statistically significant in all 29 iterations (p-values: <0.001 to 0.019). Although Egger’s (< 0.001) and Begg’s tests (p = 0.02) indicated funnel plot asymmetry, the contour-enhanced funnel plot (Supplementary Fig. 1) revealed that most of the studies had non-significant results (p > 0.10), with no clustering in regions of statistical significance. Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis determined the pooled prevalence of co-infection of Schistosoma haematobium and Schistosoma mansoni across endemic regions. The study showed an overall pooled prevalence of 19.1% among 33,121 participants across 26 studies that were included.

The pooled prevalence of co-infection in this study is lower than the pooled prevalence of 28.8% and 25.6% reported for S. haematobium and S. mansoni, respectively in a previous systematic review and meta-analysis [42]. While this study did not extract infection intensity across primary studies, existing literature suggests critical biological implications for co-infected individuals. The physical co-existence of two morphologically and pathologically distinct species within the human host may complicate the clinical presentation [11]. This dual burden requires a combined focus on both urogenital and intestinal morbidity, which is associated with increased severity of schistosomiasis in the affected population [43].

Furthermore, the clinical management of co-infection remains complicated by conflicting evidence regarding treatment efficacy [26]. Meurs et al. reported that this is largely driven by the heavily aggregated worm burdens and the synergistic pathological damage in the intestinal and urogenital tracts [29].

There was a significant difference in the pooled prevalence of co-infection across Africa. The highest and lowest figures were reported in Western Africa and East Africa with values of 26.9% and 4.8%, respectively. This aligns with the data from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 study, confirming that West Africa had the highest Age-Standardized Prevalence Rate (ASPR) on the continent [44]. This regional dominance is ecologically driven by the extensive sympatric distribution of Bulinus and Biomphalaria snails in the Senegal and Niger River basin [45, 46]. Specific high-prevalence foci in Senegal and Mali match previous reports associating large hydrological projects, like the Diama Dam, with the expansion of snail habitats. For example, Meurs et al. (2013) documented pronounced micro-geographical heterogeneity within Northern Senegal, where changes in ecology allowed for year-round transmission of both species. By contrast, lower rates of co-infection in East Africa might reflect more clearly defined ecological niches for the intermediate hosts; however, small-scale hotspots in Kenya, such as Lake Victoria, illustrate that co-endemicity remains a risk where water contact patterns overlap [19].

This study found no statistically significant difference in the pooled prevalence of Schistosoma co-infection between children and adults. While both groups have environmental vulnerabilities, driven by occupational exposure in adults and recreational water contact in children, this parity is likely influenced by underlying epidemiological factors. Specifically, the widespread implementation of school-based mass drug administration (MDA) has likely reduced the overall infection burden in children [47, 48]. Concurrently, older populations may benefit from partial, age-dependent acquired immunity resulting from years of cumulative exposure, which naturally mitigates their infection intensity [49, 50].

Therapeutically, praziquantel (PZQ) remains the primary intervention. However, current WHO guidelines for preventative chemotherapy now advocate for a more integrated approach based on focal endemicity. This shift is crucial for co-endemic regions, as the complex morbidity of harboring two distinct parasite species complicates treatment outcome [51]. While PZQ resistance remains an evolving area of research, optimizing treatment strategies to address the unique pathological burden of co-infection is necessary to effectively manage the parasite reservoir in these dual-endemic populations [51].

This study has some limitations. First, most of the studies included in the review used traditional microscopy, such as urine filtration and Kato-Katz, which have historically been shown to possess low sensitivity in light-intensity infections, though being the gold standard. Recent evidence suggests that molecular diagnostics, such as polymerase chain reaction, detect higher co-infection rates than the standard parasitological methods [52, 53].

Second, the heterogeneity of the included studies was high and may be partly explained by regional differences in ecological factors that affect the distribution of the intermediate host of this parasite. Therefore, the result of the study should be cautiously interpreted. It is also important to interpret these findings within the context of the broad 30-year timeframe (1993–2024) of the included studies. Over the past two decades, the massive scale-up of Mass Drug Administration (MDA) programs by the World Health Organization has significantly shifted the epidemiological landscape of schistosomiasis. Consequently, our pooled estimate represents an average historical burden across these three decades, rather than a precise snapshot of current point-prevalence. While mapping this temporal decline mathematically would be highly informative, the cross-sectional, fragmented nature of the available literature precludes robust spatio-temporal modeling. Despite these limitations, this is the first study that estimated the magnitude of Schistosoma co-infection in the SSA, to the best of our knowledge.

In conclusion, the magnitude of schistosome co-infection is high in the endemic region and often neglected. We recommend a shift from species-oriented vertical approaches to MDA that is effective against both species and worm load, as well as improvements in water quality, sanitation, and Hygiene (WASH). There should be upscaling of effort promoting the awareness on transmission, prevention, treatment of the diseases, and the vector control by managing the snail habitats to reduce transmission [54]. Potential research for the future must be dedicated to the implementation of sensitive molecular surveillance to identify co-endemic hotspots and assess treatment outcomes for PZQ in co-infected, heavily infected patients.
