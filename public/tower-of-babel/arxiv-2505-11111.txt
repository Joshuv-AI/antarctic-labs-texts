FairSHAP: Preprocessing for Fairness Through Attribution-Based Data Augmentation
Lin ZhuTechnical University of Denmark s232291@student.dtu.dk, Yijun BianUniversity of Copenhagen yibi@di.ku.dk, Lei YouTechnical University of Denmark leiyo@dtu.dk

Abstract
Ensuring fairness in machine learning models is critical, particularly in high-stakes domains where biased decisions can lead to serious societal consequences. Existing preprocessing approaches generally lack transparent mechanisms for identifying which features or instances are responsible for unfairness. This obscures the rationale behind data modifications. We introduce FairSHAP, a novel preprocessing framework that leverages Shapley value attribution to improve both individual and group fairness. FairSHAP identifies fairness-critical instances in the training data using an interpretable measure of feature importance, and systematically modifies them through instance-level matching across sensitive groups. This process reduces discriminative risk—an individual fairness metric—while preserving data integrity and model accuracy. We demonstrate that FairSHAP significantly improves demographic parity and equality of opportunity across diverse tabular datasets, achieving fairness gains with minimal data perturbation and, in some cases, improved predictive performance. As a model-agnostic and transparent method, FairSHAP integrates seamlessly into existing machine learning pipelines and provides actionable insights into the sources of bias. Our code is on https://github.com/youlei202/FairSHAP .



1
Introduction

Machine learning (ML) has become one of the most prominent and rapidly evolving fields in artificial intelligence (AI), with researchers continuously developing methods to enhance model accuracy and reliability. However, beyond improving predictive performance, it is equally important to consider the social and economic implications of ML applications, particularly in sensitive domains such as healthcare, finance, and law. In these real-world scenarios, prioritizing accuracy alone may not be the best for society; fairness and interpretability must also be taken into account. For instance, the COMPAS study highlighted racial bias in criminal risk assessment, where dark skin individuals were disproportionately classified as high-risk for recidivism compared to their white counterparts
(
ProPublica
;
Angwin et al. 2016
)
. Similarly, the Amazon hiring algorithm case, reported by Reuters in 2018, highlighted gender bias in automated recruitment systems, where resumes containing the word “women” or referencing women’s colleges were disproportionately downgraded compared to those of male applicants
(
Dastin 2018
)
. Such biases underscore the need for responsible AI models that balance accuracy performance with fairness.

Bias mitigation methods for tabular data have been widely explored at three primary stages: preprocessing, in-processing, and post-processing
(
Caton and Haas 2024
;
Mehrabi et al. 2021
)
.
Preprocessing
approaches modify data before training to enhance fairness and include
(1)
perturbation-based techniques
(
Weerts et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2019
;
Calmon et al. 2017
;
Feldman et al. 2015
)
,
(2) reweighting methods

(
Calders and Žliobaitė 2013
;
Kamiran and Calders 2012
)
,
(3) fair representation learning

(
Zemel et al. 2013
)
, and
(4) sampling

(
Shekhar et al. 2021
;
Ustun et al. 2019
;
Bastani et al. 2019
)
.
In-processing
methods directly alter the learning algorithm through adversarial learning, constrained optimization, regularization, or reweighting
(
Wan et al. 2023
;
Zafar et al. 2017
;
Kamishima et al. 2012
)
.
Post-processing
techniques adjust model outputs post-training through calibration and thresholding to satisfy fairness criteria
(
Petersen et al. 2021
;
Dwork et al. 2018
;
Hardt et al. 2016
)
.

Although preprocessing methods provide essential mechanisms for improving model fairness and performance, each approach exhibits inherent limitations. Reweighting techniques, although effective in addressing sample imbalance, may substantially reduce the effective sample size, thereby exacerbating data sparsity and impairing model stability and generalization capabilities. Fair representation learning typically requires the design of dataset-specific strategies, significantly increasing implementation complexity and hindering the achievement of consistent data preprocessing across diverse tasks. Sampling-based approaches also present challenges: upsampling via generative models introduces a large volume of synthetic data, raising concerns regarding data authenticity and potential distributional shifts, whereas direct downsampling substantially diminishes the volume of training data, adversely affecting the model’s generalization ability. In comparison, perturbation-based preprocessing methods are particularly attractive due to their model-agnostic nature and relatively minimal impact on predictive accuracy. Nonetheless, excessive perturbation can undermine data integrity, necessitating a careful balance between improving fairness and preserving the fidelity of the original data.

While most existing methods directly optimize for group fairness, this type of approaches may sacrifice the interests of many individuals. Recent work
(
Long et al. 2023
)
points out that optimizing solely for group fairness can lead to individual arbitrariness, prompting reconsideration of whether it is possible to improve group fairness by enhancing individual fairness. Prior research
(
Binns 2020
)
examines the apparent conflicts between individual and group fairness, concluding that these two notions are actually different reflections of the same underlying principles: consistency and egalitarianism. Under certain conditions, it is theoretically possible to satisfy both forms of fairness simultaneously. We argue that if a model treats every individual fairly, it will effectively ensure fairness across the entire group as well.
Our approach aims to improve both individual and group fairness
.

Motivated by this insight, we propose an innovative approach that explicitly emphasizes improving individual fairness, hypothesizing that addressing individual-level biases can concurrently enhance group-level fairness.
Our approach positions fairness enhancement as an interpretability-driven task
, where transparency in identifying bias at the individual level facilitates targeted and principled mitigation.
To operationalize this concept, we utilize Shapley values, a widely recognized interpretability metric that quantifies feature importance in individual predictions. Shapley values allow us to pinpoint precisely which features and data points contribute most significantly to fairness disparities. Through targeted adjustments informed by Shapley values, our method ensures data integrity and interpretability while effectively reducing bias.
Our main contributions are as follows:

•

We propose FairSHAP, a novel fairness enhancement method leveraging Shapley value analysis and instances matching. It systematically identifies fairness-sensitive data points using individual fairness measures and Shapley values, selectively modifying these points to enhance fairness.

•

FairSHAP consistently achieves superior fairness improvements with minimal data perturbation, surpassing existing benchmark methods.

•

FairSHAP effectively reduces disparities in
discriminative risk (DR)
,
demographic parity (DP)
, and
equality of opportunity (EO)
across diverse datasets, with negligible accuracy loss and, in some cases, improvements in predictive performance.

•

As a preprocessing technique, FairSHAP integrates seamlessly into existing ML workflows, providing fully reproducible and transparent implementations.

By aligning fairness enhancement closely with interpretability, our proposed approach paves the way for more accountable, transparent, and equitable ML systems.

Preliminaries

We summarize some necessarily preliminary knowledge here before elaborate on our methodology.

2.1
Shapley value and relevant methods

The Shapley value is a foundational concept in cooperative game theory, originally introduced to fairly distribute a collective payoff among players based on their individual contributions. Given its axiomatic fairness properties, the Shapley value has been widely adopted in various domains, including ML explainability, resource allocation, and feature attribution
(
Sundararajan and Najmi 2020
)
.

For a set of players (or elements)
\mathcal{N}=\{1,2,\dots,n\}
and a characteristic function
v:2^{\mathcal{N}}\to\mathbb{R}
that assigns a value to each coalition (or combination)
S\subseteq\mathcal{N}
, the Shapley value of player
k
is defined as:

\textstyle\phi_{k}(v)=\sum_{S\subseteq\mathcal{N}\setminus\{k\}}\tfrac{|S|!(n-|S|-1)!}{n!}\big(v(S\cup\{k\})-v(S)\big).

(1)

This formula ensures a fair allocation by averaging the marginal contribution of each player over all possible coalition formations. The key properties of the Shapley value include
efficiency
,
symmetry
,
dummy player
, and
additivity
, making it a unique solution satisfying these fairness criteria.

Reference data
In SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) library
(
Lundberg and Lee 2017
)
, reference data serves as a baseline distribution used to simulate the absence of features when computing Shapley values. By replacing features of the instance to be explained with values sampled from the reference data, SHAP estimates the marginal contribution of each feature. The selection of reference data significantly influences the interpretation results.

Baseline Shapley
This method is proposed to address the issue that feature values cannot be empty when computing Shapley values using ML
(
Lundberg and Lee 2017
;
Merrick and Taly 2020
;
You et al. 2024
)
. It relies on a specific reference data point
\mathbf{r}_{j}
as a reference for any
\mathbf{x}_{i}
, and the set function is defined accordingly:

v_{\text{B}}^{(i,j)}(S)=f(\mathbf{x}_{i,S};\mathbf{r}_{j,\mathcal{F}\setminus S})-f(\mathbf{r}_{j})\,,

(2)

where a feature’s absence is modeled using its value in the reference baseline data point
\mathbf{r}
, and
\mathcal{F}
represents the set of all features.

Random baseline Shapley
This method is proposed to address the issue of feature absence in Shapley value computation by using a random sampling approach from a reference distribution
\mathcal{D}

(
Lundberg and Lee 2017
)
, which is a variant of Baseline Shapley and is applied in the famous SHAP library.
Instead of relying on a single reference data point, it uses a reference distribution to model missing feature values. The set function is defined as follows:

v_{\text{RB}}^{(i)}(S)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{D}}\big[f(\mathbf{x}_{i,S};\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{F}\setminus S}^{\prime})\big]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[f(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\right],

(3)

2.2
Discrimination risk (
DR
), an individual fairness measure

Since Shapley value can only be applied to individual instances for feature attribution based on the output results, we consider combining it with individual fairness measures rather than group fairness measures. This approach is more suitable for analyzing single instances and enhances the focus on and evaluation of individual fairness, while also providing a means to further mitigate potential biases inherent in group fairness measures.

This approach allows us to leverage both the original data and background data to select feature values that can effectively reduce biases in the training set. In previous work,
Bian et al. 2023
proposed a critical individual fairness measure, and we show the
DR
(
L_{\text{DR}}
) as follows:

L_{\text{DR}}(f,\mathbf{x}_{i})=|f(\mathbf{x}_{i,\mathcal{F}\setminus\{A\}},A=a)-f(\mathbf{x}_{i,\mathcal{F}\setminus\{A\}},A=\tilde{a})|\leq\varepsilon\,,

(4)

where
\mathbf{x}_{i}
is an instance,
\mathcal{F}
represents all features,
a
is the original value of the sensitive attribute
A
, and
\tilde{a}
is its perturbed value.
A higher
L_{\text{DR}}(f,\mathbf{x})
indicates that the model’s predictions are overly sensitive to changes in the sensitive attribute, potentially reflecting bias.

For definitions of other group fairness measures, such as
DP
,
EO
and
predictive quality parity (PQP)
, please refer to Appendix
B
.

3
The proposed
FairSHAP
and its theoretical foundation

Data bias and algorithmic bias are the primary causes of biased models. Our method is model-agnostic and focused on mitigating the bias in training set.

We argue that sample imbalance in minority groups or certain variables may cause the model to learn shortcuts associated with a specific group, leading to unintended biases. One potential approach is to use generative models to upsample the minority group, although this may produce a large amount of data whose authenticity requires further validation. Alternatively, downsampling the majority group could be considered, but this may significantly reduce the overall data volume, potentially compromising the model’s generalization ability. We contemplate whether it might be possible to enable direct knowledge transfer and learning between different groups as a more effective solution.

3.1
Framework of FairSHAP

To address this problem, we propose FairSHAP, a pre-processing method that enhances individual fairness by identifying fairness-critical positions in the training set using Shapley values, followed by targeted data augmentation. The method aims to reduce individual fairness measure
DR
as a primary objective, while also leading to reduction in group fairness measures such as
DP
and
EO
.

FairSHAP consists of two interconnected components, which is represented in Figure
1
. The left-hand side of the illustration corresponds to the first component, while the right-hand side depicts the second component. These components work together to identify and mitigate bias in the training data. Below, we describe each component in detail:

(1)
On the left side of the figure, the process begins by dividing the training set into two groups based on the sensitive attribute (e.g., gender, race, etc.). To ensure a precise mapping relationship
between the two groups, a nearest neighbor matching procedure is applied. This step aligns instances from one group with similar instances in the other group, creating matched pairs that facilitate a more equitable analysis. This alignment is visually emphasized in the left portion of the figure, highlighting the pairing process.

(2)
On the right side of the figure, the focus shifts to leveraging these matched pairs for bias mitigation. Specifically, one group is selected as the target group, while the other group serves as the reference dataset for computing Shapley values for instances within the target group. These Shapley values are combined with
DR
to identify specific feature values that contribute to fairness disparities. Once identified, these feature values are modified to reduce
DR
. The process is repeated with the roles of the two groups reversed, ensuring that each group can effectively learn useful feature values from the other. This bidirectional learning approach helps to capture a more balanced and comprehensive representation of the data, ultimately improving fairness and reducing disparities. Finally, the modified instances from both groups are combined to form an augmented training set, which is designed to reduce biases and improve fairness.

Together, these two components—illustrated on the left and right sides of Figure
1
—form a cohesive framework for addressing fairness issues in ML datasets.

Overall framework of FairSHAP. (Left) Training data are first split by sensitive attribute and aligned via nearest-neighbor matching to produce paired instances. (Right) For each target group, feature values whose Shapley value exceeds a threshold are adjusted to reduce
DR

3.2
Theoretical foundation

We establish the theoretical foundation for the effectiveness of FairSHAP in reducing
DR
, an individual fairness measure. Our analysis begins with a lower bound on the improvement in fairness that can be achieved by modifying features with Shapley values exceeding a given threshold
T
. Specifically, we consider features that are deemed
fairness-critical
, i.e., those whose Shapley contributions
\phi_{k}(x)
are large in magnitude, as these are likely to have a significant impact on fairness.

By replacing such features in an input instance
\mathbf{x}
with their corresponding values from a matched reference instance
\mathbf{r}
, which is from reference data
\mathcal{B}
in Algorithm
2
, FairSHAP effectively neutralizes the influence of fairness-critical features. The following theorem quantifies the resulting reduction in DR.

Theorem 3.1

(Instance-wise DR reduction)
.

Let
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,1]
be a classifier, and let
\phi_{k}(\mathbf{x})
denote the Shapley contribution of feature
k
on instance
\mathbf{x}
. Fix a threshold
T>0
and define the set of
fairness-critical
features

\mathcal{S}_{T}(\mathbf{x})=\bigl\{\,k\in[d]\mid|\phi_{k}(\mathbf{x})|\geq T\bigr\}.

Given a matched reference instance
r
(from reference set
\mathcal{B}
), FairSHAP constructs

\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{after}}=\bigl(x_{1},\dots,x_{k-1},r_{k},x_{k+1},\dots,x_{d}\bigr)\quad\forall\,k\in\mathcal{S}_{T}(\mathbf{x}).

Then the
DR
satisfies

\textstyle L_{\mathrm{DR}}\bigl(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{after}}\bigr)=L_{\mathrm{DR}}\bigl(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{before}}\bigr)-\sum_{k\in\mathcal{S}_{T}(\mathbf{x})}\phi_{k}(\mathbf{x})\;\leq\;L_{\mathrm{DR}}\bigl(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{before}}\bigr)-T\,|\mathcal{S}_{T}(\mathbf{x})|\,.

This theorem guarantees that each replaced feature contributes at least
T
toward reducing the instance-wise
DR
. The detailed proof is provided in appendix
C
.

Let
D_{a}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{F}\setminus\{A\}})
denote the conditional distribution of non-sensitive features given
A=a\in\{0,1\}
.
We assume the conditional probability distribution of
\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{F}\setminus\{A\}}
under the condition
A=0
equals to that under the condition
A=1
, then
D_{0}(\mathbf{x})=D_{1}(\mathbf{x})=D(\mathbf{x})
.

Theorem 3.2

(Identical-distribution case)
.

If
D_{0}=D_{1}
, then for any classifier
f
,

L_{\mathrm{DP}}(f)\;\leq\;L_{\mathrm{DR}}(f).

Theorem 3.3

(General case)
.

For any classifier
f
, regardless of
D_{0}
and
D_{1}
,

L_{\mathrm{DP}}(f)\;\leq\;L_{\mathrm{DR}}(f)\;+\;\mathrm{TV}\bigl(D_{0},D_{1}\bigr),

where
\mathrm{TV}(D_{0},D_{1})
is the total variation distance.

Theorems
3.2
and
3.3
establish that any uniform reduction in individual fairness measure
L_{\mathrm{DR}}
induced by FairSHAP also yields a corresponding decrease in
DP
gap
L_{\mathrm{DP}}
. Detailed proofs are deferred to Appendix
D
.

Thus, our theoretical analysis not only justifies the design of FairSHAP but also highlights its ability to simultaneously improve both individual and group fairness guarantees in a principled and interpretable manner.

Experiments

We conducted a comprehensive experimental evaluation to assess the proposed method’s performance across five critical metrics: accuracy,

Our experiments incorporates 5 typical tabular datasets spanning criminal justice, finance, and social services domains: COMPAS

Overall setup

We used the aforementioned datasets, where the sensitive attribute in each dataset was binarized. Additionally, categorical features were one-hot encoded, and numerical features were standardized.
Besides, we used the XGBClassifier from the XGBoost library with its default settings in the whole project. All experiments were conducted on a server equipped with a 13th Gen Intel(R) Core(TM) i5-13500H CPU (12 cores, 16 threads, base frequency of 2.60 GHz). The average computation time varies depending on the dataset size and feature dimensionality. For the German Credit dataset, the average processing time is within 3 minutes . For COMPAS , it is around 5 minutes. On larger datasets such as Adult, the computation time increases significantly, taking approximately 1 hour.

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we introduced FairSHAP, an innovative preprocessing framework that leverages Shapley value attribution to enhance fairness in ML models. FairSHAP effectively identifies and systematically adjusts fairness-critical instances using interpretable feature importance measures and instance-level matching across sensitive groups. Our approach significantly improves fairness metrics such as
DR
,
DP
and
EO
across various datasets. Crucially, FairSHAP achieves these improvements while perturbing the data as minimally as possible, largely preserving data fidelity and, in certain cases, even enhancing predictive accuracy. As a transparent and model-agnostic method, FairSHAP integrates into existing machine learning pipelines, providing actionable insights into bias sources and fostering accountable and equitable AI systems.

Appendix B
Definition of other group fairness measures

Demographic parity (DP)

is a group fairness measure that ensures the outcomes of a predictive model are independent of a sensitive attribute, such as gender or race. A model satisfies demographic parity if the probability of assigning a positive outcome is equal across different demographic groups. This ensures that individuals from different groups have the same likelihood of receiving a favorable prediction, regardless of their sensitive attribute.

Mathematically, demographic parity is defined as:

\begin{split}L_{\text{DP}}(f)=\Bigl|\,&\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{D}}\bigl(f(\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{F}\setminus\{A\}},A=0)=1\mid A=0\bigr)\\
&-\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{D}}\bigl(f(\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{F}\setminus\{A\}},A=1)=1\mid A=1\bigr)\Bigr|\leq\varepsilon.\end{split}

(6)

where
f
represents the model’s decision function,

\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{F}\setminus\{A\}}
denotes the input features with the sensitive attribute
A
removed,
and
A\in\{0,1\}
is the sensitive attribute itself.
The probability
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{D}}
is taken over the data distribution
\mathcal{D}
.
The fairness constraint is satisfied if the absolute difference between the rates of positive predictions for group
A=0
and group
A=1
does not exceed the threshold
\varepsilon
. A model violating this condition exhibits
disparate impact
, which can lead to unfair treatment of underrepresented groups.

Equality of opportunity (EO)

is another group fairness criterion that ensures individuals who qualify for a positive outcome (e.g.,
y=1
) have an equal chance of receiving it, regardless of their sensitive attribute. This principle is particularly important in domains such as hiring, lending, and healthcare, where fairness in decision-making is crucial.

Mathematically, EO is defined as:

\begin{split}L_{\text{EO}}(f)=|&\hskip 10.00002pt\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{D}}(f(\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{F}\setminus\{A\}},A=0)=1\mid A=0,y=1)\\
&-\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{D}}(f(\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{F}\setminus\{A\}},A=1)=1\mid A=1,y=1)|\leq\varepsilon\,.\end{split}

(7)

This criterion ensures that among individuals who truly qualify for a positive outcome (
y=1
), the model’s predicted positive rate remains consistent across different demographic groups. A model failing to satisfy this condition exhibits
disparate treatment
, potentially disadvantaging certain groups in high-stakes decision-making scenarios.

Predictive quality parity (PQP)

ensures that the accuracy of positive predictions is similar across different demographic groups. Specifically, it requires that the probability of a true positive outcome, given a positive prediction, is consistent for all groups.

Mathematically, PQP is defined as:

\begin{split}L_{\text{PQP}}(f)=|&\hskip 10.00002pt\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{D}}(y=1\mid f(\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{F}\setminus\{A\}},A=0)=1,A=0)\\
&-\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{D}}(y=1\mid f(\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{F}\setminus\{A\}},A=1)=1,A=1)|\leq\varepsilon\,,\end{split}

(8)

where
f
is the model’s decision function,
\mathbf{x}
represents the input features,
A
is the sensitive attribute, and
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{D}}
is the probability over the data distribution
\mathcal{D}
. The fairness condition is met if the difference between these probabilities does not exceed a threshold
\varepsilon
.

A model satisfying PQP ensures that positive predictions are equally reliable across groups. This is crucial in applications like healthcare or finance, where incorrect predictions can have serious consequences. If PQP is violated, certain groups may receive unreliable predictions, leading to unfair outcomes.

Appendix C
DR Reduction Achieved by FairSHAP

Lemma C.1

(Shapley decomposition of individual DR)
.

Let
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a factual instance with sensitive attribute
a\in\{0,1\}
and flipped value
\tilde{a}
.
Define the individual discriminative risk

\ \mathrm{DR}(\mathbf{x})=L_{\text{DR}}(f,\mathbf{x})=\bigl|f(\mathbf{x},A=a)-f(\mathbf{x},A=\tilde{a})\bigr|.

Fix a reference instance
\mathbf{r}
obtained from reference data
\mathcal{B}
and consider the coalition game

v_{\text{DR}}^{(\mathbf{x})}(S)\;=\;\mathrm{DR}\bigl(\mathbf{x}_{S};\,\mathbf{r}_{\mathcal{F}\setminus S}\bigr),\qquad S\subseteq[d].

Let
\phi_{k}(\mathbf{x})
be the Shapley value of feature
k
in this game and
\phi_{0}(\mathbf{x})
the grand-baseline term.
Then

\textstyle\mathrm{DR}(\mathbf{x})=\phi_{0}(\mathbf{x})+\sum_{k=1}^{d}\phi_{k}(\mathbf{x}).

Proof.

The Shapley value satisfies the
efficiency
axiom, which states that the sum of all players’ (features’) Shapley values equals the value of the grand coalition.
Taking the grand coalition
S=[d]
in
v_{\text{DR}}^{(\mathbf{x})}
yields
v_{\text{DR}}^{(\mathbf{x})}([d])=\mathrm{DR}(\mathbf{x})
.
Hence the decomposition follows directly from efficiency.
∎

Theorem C.2

(Instance-wise DR reduction achieved by FairSHAP)
.

Fix a threshold
T>0
and define the set of fairness-critical features

\textstyle S_{T}(\mathbf{x})=\bigl\{k\in[d]\,\big|\,|\phi_{k}(\mathbf{x})|\geq T\bigr\}.

Let
\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{after}}
denote the instance obtained from
\mathbf{x}
after FairSHAP replaces every feature
k\in S_{T}(\mathbf{x})
by its value in the matched reference instance
\mathbf{r}
.
Then

\textstyle\mathrm{DR}\bigl(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{after}}\bigr)=\mathrm{DR}\bigl(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{before}}\bigr)-\sum_{k\in S_{T}(\mathbf{x})}\phi_{k}(\mathbf{x})\leq\mathrm{DR}\bigl(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{before}}\bigr)-T\,|S_{T}(\mathbf{x})|.

Proof.

By Lemma
C.1
,

\textstyle\mathrm{DR}\bigl(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{before}}\bigr)=\phi_{0}(\mathbf{x})+\sum_{k\in S_{T}(\mathbf{x})}\phi_{k}(\mathbf{x})+\sum_{k\notin S_{T}(\mathbf{x})}\phi_{k}(\mathbf{x}).

After FairSHAP editing, each feature
k\in S_{T}(\mathbf{x})
is copied from
\mathbf{r}
, causing its contribution to vanish; thus

\textstyle\mathrm{DR}\bigl(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{after}}\bigr)=\phi_{0}(\mathbf{x})+\sum_{k\notin S_{T}(\mathbf{x})}\phi_{k}(\mathbf{x}).

Subtracting these equalities gives

\textstyle\mathrm{DR}\bigl(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{after}}\bigr)=\mathrm{DR}\bigl(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{before}}\bigr)-\sum_{k\in S_{T}(\mathbf{x})}\phi_{k}(\mathbf{x}).

For every
k\in S_{T}(\mathbf{x})
we have
|\phi_{k}(\mathbf{x})|\geq T
.
Applying the triangle inequality yields

\textstyle-\sum_{k\in S_{T}(\mathbf{x})}\phi_{k}(\mathbf{x})\leq-T\,|S_{T}(\mathbf{x})|.

Substituting this bound into the previous equality completes the proof.
∎

Let
\mathcal{X}

\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{F}\setminus\{A\}}
be the space of non

A\in\{0,1\}
a binary sensitive attribute and a is the value of A.

\mathbf{x}

(9)

When considering multiple instances, the discriminative risk is computed as the expected value over the data distribution
\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{F}\setminus\{A=a\}}\sim D_{a}
, as follows:

L_{\text{DR}}(f)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_{i,\mathcal{F}\setminus\{A\}}\sim D}[L_{\text{DR}}(f,\mathbf{x}_{i})].

(10)

Demographic parity.

&-\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{F}\setminus\{A\}}\sim\mathcal{D}}\bigl(f(\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{F}\setminus\{A\}},A=1)=1\mid A=1\bigr)\Bigr|.\end{split}

(11)

where the probability is taken over the data distribution
\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{F}\setminus\{A=a\}}\sim D_{a}

Let
D_{0}(\mathbf{x})
and
D_{1}(\mathbf{x})
denote the conditional
feature distributions given
A=0
and
A=1
, respectively. Assume that after marginalising out the sensitive attribute,
both groups share the same feature distribution:

D_{0}(\mathbf{x})=D_{1}(\mathbf{x})=D(\mathbf{x}).

Theorem D.1
.

Under this assumption, for any classifier
f
,

L_{\text{DP}}(f)\leq L_{\text{DR}}(f)

Proof.

Define

p_{a}=\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{F}\setminus\{A\}}\sim\mathcal{D}}\bigl(f(\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{F}\setminus\{A\}},A)=1\mid A=a\bigr)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{F}\setminus\{A\}}\sim\mathcal{D}}\bigl[\mathbb{I}\{f(\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{F}\setminus\{A\}},a)=1\}\bigr],\quad a\in\{0,1\}.

By the definition of
L_{\text{DP}}
given in (
11
), we have

L_{\text{DP}}(f)=|p_{0}-p_{1}|=\biggl|\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{F}\setminus\{A\}}\sim D}\bigl[\mathbb{I}\{f(\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{F}\setminus\{A\}},0)=1\}-\mathbb{I}\{f(\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{F}\setminus\{A\}},1)=1\}\bigr]\biggr|.

Applying Jensen’s inequality (triangle inequality for expectations), we obtain:

L_{\text{DP}}(f)\leq\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{F}\setminus\{A\}}\sim\mathcal{D}}\bigl|\mathbb{I}\{f(\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{F}\setminus\{A\}},0)=1\}-\mathbb{I}\{f(\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{F}\setminus\{A\}},1)=1\}\bigr|.

Note that the difference inside the absolute value can only be non-zero if the classifier’s prediction changes when flipping the sensitive attribute
A
. Thus, by definition of
L_{\mathrm{DR}}
in (
10
), for any
\mathbf{x}
:

\bigl|\mathbb{I}\{f(\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{F}\setminus\{A\}},0)=1\}-\mathbb{I}\{f(\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{F}\setminus\{A\}},1)=1\}\bigr|\leq L_{\text{DR}}(f).

Taking expectation over
\mathbf{x}\sim D
, we have:

L_{\text{DP}}(f)\leq\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{F}\setminus\{A\}}\sim D}[L_{\text{DR}}(f,\mathbf{x}_{i})]=L_{\text{DR}}(f),

which completes the proof.
∎

D.2
Case 2: Non
-
identical conditional distributions

Let
D_{0}(\mathbf{x})
and
D_{1}(\mathbf{x})
denote the conditional feature distributions given
A=0
and
A=1
, respectively. Denote their total-variation distance by

\mathrm{TV}(D_{0},D_{1})=\sup_{B\subseteq\mathcal{X}}|\,D_{0}(B)-D_{1}(B)|.

Theorem D.2
.

Under this assumption, for every classifier
f
, we have:

L_{\text{DP}}(f)\leq L_{\text{DR}}(f)+\mathrm{TV}(D_{0},D_{1}).

Proof.

Choose a coupling
\pi(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{x}_{1})
whose marginals correspond to
D_{0}
and
D_{1}
, respectively. By definition,

p_{0}-p_{1}=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\bigl[f(\mathbf{x}_{0},0)-f(\mathbf{x}_{1},1)\bigr].

Decompose the difference as:

f(\mathbf{x}_{0},0)-f(\mathbf{x}_{1},1)=\underbrace{f(\mathbf{x}_{0},0)-f(\mathbf{x}_{0},1)}_{\leq L_{\text{DR}}(f)}+\bigl[f(\mathbf{x}_{0},1)-f(\mathbf{x}_{1},1)\bigr].

The first term is bounded above by
L_{\text{DR}}(f)
for every pair
(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{x}_{1})
. Integrating the second term over
\pi
, we obtain an upper bound given by the definition of total-variation distance:

\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\bigl[f(\mathbf{x}_{0},1)-f(\mathbf{x}_{1},1)\bigr]\leq\mathrm{TV}(D_{0},D_{1}).

Thus,

L_{\text{DP}}(f)\leq L_{\text{DR}}(f)+\mathrm{TV}(D_{0},D_{1}),

completing the proof.
∎

D.3
Implications

•

In identical Conditional Feature Distributions,
reducing individual sensitivity immediately
improves group fairness.

•

When conditional feature distributions diverge,
Theorem
D.2
shows that
lowering
L_{\text{DR}}
still tightens an
upper bound

on demographic
-
parity disparity.
The residual term
\mathrm{TV}(D_{0},D_{1})
is
an irreducible gap caused by population shift rather
than model bias.
