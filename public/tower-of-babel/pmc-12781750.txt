Determinants of quality antenatal care among adolescent girls and women in Sierra leone: insights from the 2019 demographic health survey
Authors: Ibrahim Franklyn Kamara, Sia Morenike Tengbe, Sattu Issa, Yatta Maltida Musa, Bobson Derrick Fofanah, Binyam Hailu, George Ameh, Augustus Osborne
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAntenatal care (ANC) is essential for improving maternal and child health outcomes, as it helps prevent pregnancy complications and reduces maternal and child mortality. Ensuring that all pregnant women receive comprehensive, high-quality ANC is critical for a positive pregnancy experience. This study aimed to identify the determinants of quality ANC visits among pregnant adolescent girls and women in Sierra Leone.MethodsWe analyzed data from the 2019 Sierra Leone Demographic and Health Survey, including 7,276 adolescent girls and women who had a live birth or stillbirth in the two years preceding the survey. Quality antenatal care was defined as receipt of all essential ANC components: at least four ANC visits, receipt of tetanus toxoid injection, blood pressure measurement, urine and blood sample collection, and counseling on pregnancy complications. Binary logistic regression was used to identify factors associated with quality ANC, adjusting for demographic and socioeconomic variables. Survey weights were applied to account for the sampling design.ResultsOverall, 79.7% of adolescent girls and women received quality antenatal care services. In the fully adjusted mixed effects model, attending four or more ANC visits (aOR: 1.92; 95% CI: 1.42–2.59) and receiving care from a skilled provider (aOR: 1.80; 95% CI: 1.40–2.31) were both strongly associated with increased odds of receiving quality ANC. Conversely, initiating ANC in the second trimester was linked to lower odds of receiving quality care (aOR: 0.61; 95% CI: 0.51–0.74) compared to those who began care in the first trimester. Socioeconomic factors also played an important role: adolescent girls and women in the richest wealth quintile (aOR: 1.89; 95% CI: 1.12–3.19) and those residing in the Western region (aOR: 3.78; 95% CI: 2.26–6.31) were significantly more likely to receive quality ANC visits. Furthermore, urban residence was associated with lower odds of receiving quality ANC visits (aOR: 0.68; 95% CI: 0.47–0.97) compared to rural areas. While higher education level, being married, and having media access were positively associated with quality ANC visits, these relationships did not reach statistical significance.ConclusionWhile most adolescent girls and women in Sierra Leone received quality antenatal care, significant disparities persist based on demographic, socioeconomic, and healthcare-related factors. These findings underscore the urgent need for targeted interventions by the national directorate of reproductive and child health, reproductive health and family planning, and school and adolescent health programmes. Strategies should prioritize improving early ANC initiation, expanding access to skilled providers, and addressing barriers faced by the poorest and urban populations. Tailored community outreach, education campaigns are essential to reduce inequities and ensure that all pregnant adolescent girls and women receive comprehensive, high-quality ANC services.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12978-025-02236-2.

Introduction

Despite notable progress in reducing the global burden of deaths related to pregnancy and childbirth, maternal mortality remains a global health priority, with about 260,000 women dying in 2023 alone [1]. More concerning is that 92% of these deaths occur in low- and middle-income countries (LMIC) [2], especially in sub-Saharan Africa, which accounts for 70% of maternal deaths [3, 4]. The highest rates are found in West African countries, with nations like Sierra Leone that recorded 920 maternal deaths in 2023 [1]. These figures underscore the need for effective public health interventions to accelerate reductions in maternal and neonatal mortality across the region.

Antenatal care (ANC) has been proven to significantly prevent and reduce maternal and newborn mortality by ensuring the early detection of high risks and pre-existing conditions, management of pregnancy-related and concurrent illnesses, and promoting nutritional interventions [5–7]. Furthermore, several studies have shown a positive correlation between quality ANC and better pregnancy outcomes [5, 8]. Additionally, evidence has shown that ANC services not only contribute to a 41% reduction in neonatal deaths but also provide a critical platform for maternal and neonatal healthcare providers to implement evidence-based interventions that safeguard maternal and child health [9].

In response to the persistent burden of maternal mortality, the World Health Organisation’s recommendations on ANC for a positive pregnancy experience provide an evidence-based framework for ANC services, aiming to deliver quality healthcare for the pregnant woman and fetus, thereby maintaining a healthy pregnancy that transitions into positive labour and delivery, which is patient-centred [10]. In this guideline, 8 ANC contacts with skilled health providers are recommended, and during these contacts, various context-specific interventions should be implemented as they ensure better outcomes by reducing perinatal mortality and improving women’s health as compared to the previous four visits [11]. In Sierra Leone, these interventions include blood pressure and weight measurements, malaria and anti-helminth prophylaxis, urine and blood sample investigations, tetanus toxoid administration, and the provision of information on potential pregnancy-related complications and breastfeeding as stated in the national emergency obstetric care guideline and the reproductive, maternal, newborn, child and adolescent health strategy [10, 12]. These recommendations are designed to optimize perinatal outcomes and promote women’s health beyond pregnancy.

However, ensuring access to quality ANC remains a challenge in many countries in Africa, including Sierra Leone [13, 14]. Although coverage of ANC has increased in LMIC, maternal and newborn deaths continue to remain high [6]. In Sierra Leone, since the introduction of the Free Healthcare Initiative in 2010, which grants free access to healthcare services for pregnant women, lactating mothers, and under-five children nationwide, ANC coverage has increased from 58.6% in 2008 to 78.2% in 2019 [15]. During the same period, the maternal mortality ratio (MMR) has dropped significantly from 1360 in 2010 and is currently at 354 [16]. However, despite these commendable efforts, the MMR in Sierra Leone is still high, making it challenging for the country to meet the sustainable development goal MMR target of less than 70 by 2030 [1]. This gap underscores the need to move beyond coverage and focus on the quality of ANC provision.

Multiple factors, including maternal education, socioeconomic status, access to skilled health providers, and geographical disparities, have been identified as critical determinants of ANC quality [6]. However, there is a paucity of evidence on how these determinants interact to influence the quality of ANC received by adolescent girls and women in Sierra Leone. This study aims to address this knowledge gap by comprehensively examining the determinants of quality ANC among adolescent girls and women using data from the 2019 Sierra Leone Demographic and Health Survey. The findings will provide actionable insights to inform policy, guide resource allocation, and enhance the effectiveness of maternal and child health interventions in Sierra Leone and similar settings.

Methods

Data source

This study utilized data from the 2019 Sierra Leone Demographic and Health Survey (SLDHS), a nationally representative cross-sectional survey designed to collect comprehensive information on health and demographic indicators among women of reproductive age. The survey employed a stratified two-stage cluster sampling design, ensuring adequate representation of various regions and demographic groups across the country. Data collection was conducted through face-to-face interviews using standardized questionnaires administered by trained enumerators. The SLDHS dataset is publicly available and can be accessed through the Demographic and Health Surveys Programme.

Outcome variable

The primary outcome variable for this analysis was the quality of ANC received by adolescent girls and women. Quality of ANC was defined as the receipt of a set of essential care components among those who experienced a live birth or stillbirth in the two years preceding the survey. Specifically, quality ANC was measured by whether adolescent girls and women received all of the following key components during their most recent pregnancy: blood pressure measurement, urine sample collection, blood sample collection, assessment of the baby’s heartbeat, counseling about maternal diet, counseling about breastfeeding, and discussion regarding vaginal bleeding. The study population included adolescent girls and women who had a live birth or stillbirth within the specified time frame and who received ANC for their most recent birth event. For each component, the numerator was the number of adolescent girls and women who reported receiving that specific care (e.g., m42c_1 = 1 for blood pressure measurement), and the denominator was the total number in the population base (e.g., m80 = 1 for live births). The proportion of adolescent girls and women receiving all recommended ANC components was calculated, and a binary outcome variable was created indicating whether an individual received quality ANC (“Yes”) or not (“No”).

Explanatory variables

The explanatory variables for this study were carefully selected based on existing literature and theoretical frameworks that highlight their potential impact on the quality of antenatal care [7]. These variables encompassed a range of demographic, socioeconomic, and healthcare-related factors. Demographic variables included age groups (15–19, 20–34, 35–49), marital status (never in union, married), and the number of children (zero, one-two, three-four, five or more). Socioeconomic factors comprised educational attainment (none/primary, secondary, higher), wealth index (poorest, poorer, middle, richer, richest), and place of residence (rural, urban). Additionally, healthcare access variables were included, such as media access (whether the individual has access to TV, radio, or newspapers: yes/no), the number of antenatal care visits (less than four, four or more), the timing of the first antenatal care visit (first trimester, second trimester, third trimester), and the presence of a skilled ANC provider (yes/no).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses for this study were performed in two distinct phases using Stata version 18.0. In the initial phase, we conducted a bivariate analysis to examine the distribution of adolescent girls and women receiving quality antenatal care across twelve individual and contextual variables. The results were presented as percentages with confidence intervals (CIs), and p-values were utilized to evaluate the significance of the associations between the variables. Only those variables with p-values less than or equal to 0.05 were included in the subsequent regression analysis. The use of a p-value threshold of < 0.05 for selecting variables in the multivariable analysis was chosen to ensure that only those variables with statistically significant associations were included in the final regression model. While more lenient cutoffs such as p < 0.25 are sometimes used in exploratory analyses to capture potential confounders or weaker associations, our approach prioritized robustness and interpretability by focusing on variables with stronger evidence of association in the bivariate analysis. This strict cutoff minimizes the risk of overfitting and reduces the inclusion of spurious or non-relevant variables, thereby enhancing the validity and reliability of our findings. By limiting the multivariable model to variables with p < 0.05, we aimed to provide clearer insights into the most influential factors associated with quality antenatal care, supporting more targeted policy recommendations.

In the final phase, we carried out a mixed-effects multilevel binary logistic regression analysis to assess the strength of associations between the individual and contextual variables and the likelihood of quality antenatal care, utilizing a four-model approach. Model I acted as a null model, demonstrating the variations in quality of antenatal care attributable to clustering at the primary sampling units (PSUs). Models II and III incorporated individual and contextual variables, respectively, while Model IV included all explanatory variables. During this analysis, we checked for multicollinearity using Variance Inflation Factors (VIFs) and found no issues, as all VIF values were below five. The regression analysis produced both fixed and random effects; the fixed effects revealed the relationships between the explanatory variables and the likelihood of quality antenatal care, presented as adjusted odds ratios (aOR) with 95% CIs. The random effects indicated the variability in ANC visits, represented by the intra-cluster correlation coefficient (ICC) values across the models. We also performed goodness-of-fit tests to ensure the robustness of our models. To identify the most suitable model for the analysis, we compared the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) values and log-likelihood estimates, concluding that Model IV was the optimal model for interpreting the fixed effect results. Prior to analysis, the dataset was weighted, and the surveyset command was consistently applied throughout the study.

Results

Prevalence of quality ANC among adolescent girls and women in Sierra Leone

The analysis revealed that 79.7% of adolescent girls and women in Sierra Leone received quality antenatal care. Quality ANC coverage was highest among women with higher educational attainment (90.6%), while the lowest was among women with no or only primary education (77.1%). Similarly, the highest prevalence of quality ANC was observed among those with media access (83.9%), those with no children (81.8%), and those residing in the Western region (92.6%), whereas the lowest prevalence was noted among those without media access (75.9%), those with five or more children (75.1%), and those in the Southern region (67.7%) (Table 1).

Factors associated with quality antenatal care visits among women and adolescent girls in Sierra Leone

Fixed effect results

In Model IV of the mixed effects analysis, several factors were associated with the likelihood of receiving quality antenatal care among adolescent girls and women in Sierra Leone (Table 3). Attending four or more ANC visits was strongly associated with higher odds of receiving quality care (aOR: 1.92; 95% CI: 1.42–2.59). Initiating ANC in the second trimester was associated with lower odds of quality ANC care compared to first trimester initiation (aOR: 0.61; 95% CI: 0.51–0.74). Receiving care from a skilled provider was also significantly associated with higher odds of quality ANC care (aOR: 1.80; 95% CI: 1.40–2.31). Adolescent girls and women in the richest wealth quintile had higher odds of receiving quality care (aOR: 1.89; 95% CI: 1.12–3.19), while those residing in urban areas had lower odds compared to rural residents (aOR: 0.68; 95% CI: 0.47–0.97). Regional variation was observed, with those in the Western region (aOR: 3.78; 95% CI: 2.26–6.31), Northern (aOR: 3.54; 95% CI: 2.45–5.12), and Northwestern region (aOR: 3.45; 95% CI: 2.38–5.00) having much higher odds of receiving quality care compared to the Eastern region. Education level, marital status, and media access showed positive associations with quality care, but these were not statistically significant in the fully adjusted model.

Random effect results

The random effects model further complements these findings by providing insights into the variance attributable to the PSU. The PSU variance remains consistent across models, with a value of 1.20 (95% CI: 0.95, 1.51) in Model IV, indicating that there is a notable amount of variability in quality care that can be attributed to the clustering of women within PSUs. The ICC in Model IV is 0.268, suggesting that approximately 26.8% of the total variance in quality antenatal care visits is due to differences between clusters, highlighting the importance of considering the hierarchical structure of the data. The Wald chi-square statistics show a significant improvement in model fit with each successive model, culminating in a value of 229.55 for Model IV, which indicates that the included variables explain a substantial amount of the variability in quality care. The log-likelihood improves from − 3247.1072 in the empty model to -3098.1244 in Model IV, while the AIC decreases from 6498.214 to 6240.249, suggesting that Model IV provides a better fit for the data.

Discussion

In Sierra Leone, only 79% of adolescent girls and women received quality ANC (all seven essential components), highlighting substantial gaps despite high ANC contact. This study is particularly significant given the critical role that quality ANC plays in improving maternal and child health outcomes. We noted disparities among demographics, socioeconomic factors, and health-related factors with respect to quality ANC care, which have important implications for public health policy and practice.

Our findings indicate that women with higher educational attainment received better quality ANC compared to those with no education or only primary education. This aligns with existing literature, such as a study conducted in Tanzania showed that women with higher education had adequate ANC services as compared to those with lower educational status [17]. The correlation between education and health-seeking behavior is well-documented, as higher educational levels empower women to advocate for their health needs and navigate healthcare systems more effectively [17]. For instance, women with secondary or higher education are more likely to complete the recommended number of ANC visits and to actively seek out necessary services during these visits [18, 19]. Therefore, investing in the education of adolescent girls and women is not just a matter of equity but a strategic public health intervention that could lead to improved quality of care, a more positive pregnancy experience, and ultimately, a reduction in maternal and child mortality rates in Sierra Leone.

Furthermore, our study highlights the importance of health education and media access in promoting positive pregnancy experiences. We observed that adolescent girls and women with access to media reported receiving higher quality ANC compared to those without such access. This finding resonates with a study conducted in Nepal, which also found that women of reproductive age who have access to media better utilize antenatal care services as compared to their counterparts [18]. Access to media, particularly radio and television, can enhance health literacy, enabling women to make informed decisions about their healthcare [19]. Additionally, media can serve as a platform for disseminating essential information regarding maternal health, including dietary recommendations and breastfeeding practices [20]. Therefore, stakeholders should prioritize health education initiatives that leverage media to reach and inform pregnant women and adolescents.

The World Health Organization recommended that adolescent girls and women should have eight ANC visits for a positive pregnancy experience [21]. Our study found that adolescent girls and women who attended four or more ANC visits had better care as compared to their counterparts who attended fewer than four visits. This finding is consistent with research conducted in two government general hospitals in Sidama National Regional State, Southern Ethiopia, which also documented that adolescent girls and women who attended four or more ANC visits received quality ANC care [22]. The frequency of healthcare interactions is critical, as it facilitates the delivery of various essential interventions and services to pregnant women [23]. Thus, it is imperative for the Directorate of Reproductive and Child Health, along with family planning and adolescent health programs, to implement strategies that encourage all pregnant women to complete the WHO-recommended eight ANC visits. This could involve community outreach programs, incentives for attendance, and educational campaigns that emphasize the importance of regular check-ups throughout pregnancy.

Our findings indicate that pregnant adolescent girls and women who received ANC services from skilled birth attendants had quality ANC as compared to non-skilled birth attendants [24]. This finding is corroborated by a study conducted in Ethiopia, which also noted that women who received services from skilled birth attendants received quality ANC as compared to their counterparts [25]. This is expected as a skilled birth attendant who has the required knowledge, skills, and competencies will deliver quality ANC as compared to a non-skilled birth attendant. The Ministry of Health and relevant stakeholders must prioritize the training and retention of skilled healthcare workers in all healthcare facilities. Our findings point to actionable, patient-centered strategies that directly reflect the determinants associated with quality ANC in Sierra Leone. Practical approaches include tailored counseling on danger signs and breastfeeding, appointment reminders, flexible clinic hours, community health worker follow-up for late starters, and referral pathways that prioritize skilled-provider contact. Grounding these steps in our empirical findings can help reduce inequities and improve the delivery of essential ANC components.

A clear disparity in ANC quality was observed across the wealth quintiles, with the poorest women receiving the lowest quality of care and the richest women receiving the highest quality. This disparity is not only seen in Sierra Leone, as a National survey from 63 low-and middle-income countries that utilized data from DHS and multiple indicator cluster surveys documented that adolescent girls and women in the richest quintiles received better quality ANC as compared to their counterparts [26]. Given the observed socioeconomic disparities in quality ANC, interventions should directly target the determinants identified in our analysis. Priority actions include: provider training on respectful, non-discriminatory, and equity-focused care to reduce bias; expanding financial risk protection (e.g., fee waivers, vouchers, transport subsidies) to mitigate cost barriers for lower-income women; and community- and facility-level accountability mechanisms (e.g., patient charters, grievance redress) to reinforce equitable care standards.

Our results reveal pronounced regional disparities in the quality of antenatal care, with women in the Western, Northern, and Northwestern regions having substantially higher odds of receiving all essential ANC components than those in the Eastern region, and descriptive analyses indicating the Southern region has the lowest prevalence of quality ANC. These subnational gaps likely reflect intersecting contextual factors service readiness constraints (e.g., stock-outs of diagnostics and supplies, fewer skilled providers, weaker supervision), geographic and transport barriers that reduce continuity and visit time, and socioeconomic disadvantage that our model shows is associated with lower-quality care alongside variability in adherence to ANC content checklists and mentorship. Taken together, these findings argue for targeted, region-sensitive interventions: in Southern (and Eastern) districts, strengthen supply chains and routine supervision, standardize delivery of ANC components through checklists and audit-and-feedback, expand outreach/mobile ANC for hard-to-reach communities, and implement demand-side supports (transport vouchers, fee waivers for essential tests) and equity-focused provider training. District-level scorecards and client feedback mechanisms should be used to monitor component-specific gaps and guide resource allocation, ensuring that improvements in contact translate into equitable gains in ANC content where deficits are greatest.

Strengths and limitations

Our study has several strengths. First, the study addresses a priority maternal health issue in Sierra Leone and contributes to SDG-related policy dialogue. Second, it uses nationally representative data to examine determinants of quality ANC among pregnant adolescent girls and women in Sierra Leone, providing context-specific evidence to inform programs and policy. Third, we used a large dataset, and the participants were from all 16 districts in the country, which supports the generalizability of our findings with precise estimates. Fourth, we followed the strengthening of the reporting of observational studies in the epidemiology guideline to report our research findings. Even though our study has several strengths, it also has some limitations. Our study is prone to recall bias. Furthermore, since DHS uses a self-reporting approach, there is a risk of inherent bias and misunderstanding of the questions, leading to inaccurate responses. We use the word quality not in the true meaning of clinical quality, but rather what percentage of services were offered to the pregnant adolescent girls and women of the total number of services that should have been received as per guidelines.

Conclusions

We observed that the proportion of pregnant adolescent girls and women who received quality ANC was h Pregnant adolescent girls and women with secondary and higher education, those with less number of children, those not in union, those who have access to media, first ANC visit in the first trimester, and presence of skilled provider.

To overcome these disparities, we recommend effective planning, implementation, and monitoring of the WHO 8 ANC visits recommendation for a positive pregnancy experience, as this will reduce maternal morbidity, mortality, stillbirths, and child mortality rates in the country.
