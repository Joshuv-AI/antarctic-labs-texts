Line-parallelisms of PG ​ ( n , 2 ) \text{PG}(n,2) from Preparata-like codes
Philipp Heering, Vladislav Taranchuk

Abstract




Abstract

Partitions of the binary linear Hamming code into Preparata-like codes are known to induce line-parallelisms of PG
(n,2)
. In this paper, we show that if
P
is any Preparata-like code contained in the binary linear Hamming code
H
of the same length, then
H
can be partitioned into additive translates of
P
. This generalizes a result of Baker, van Lint, and Wilson who prove this fact for the class of generalized Preparata codes. We give an explicit description for line-parallelisms obtained from such a partition via crooked Preparata-like codes and establish an equivalence criterion for such line-parallelisms.

Keywords:
Preparata-like codes, Crooked functions, Line-parallelisms, Equivalence

Introduction

Let
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n+1}
be the
(n+1)
-dimensional vector space over the finite field with
q
elements,
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. For
n\geq 3
we denote by PG
(n,q)
the
n
-dimensional projective space, i.e. an incidence geometry whose points are the 1-dimensional subspaces of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n+1}
and whose lines are the 2-dimensional subspaces of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n+1}
with incidence being defined via inclusion. A
line-spread
of PG
(n,q)
is a partitioning of the points of PG
(n,q)
into lines. A
line-parallelism
of PG
(n,q)
is a partitioning of the lines of PG
(n,q)
into line-spreads.
It is known that line-spreads exist in PG
(n,q)
if and only if
n
is odd
[
21
]
. On the other hand, it is a long standing open question to determine whether line-parallelisms exist in PG
(n,q)
for any prime power
q
and odd positive integer
n
. In this paper, we will only consider line-spreads and line-parallelisms, hence we will call them spreads and parallelisms.

Below, we give a brief survey of known parallelisms. We separate the cases
q\neq 2
and
q=2
. One reason for this distinction is that the results in this paper yield new constructions of parallelisms of
{\rm PG}(n,2)
. However, a more important reason is that historically, constructions of parallelisms of
{\rm PG}(n,q)
differ greatly between the cases
q=2
and
q\neq 2
. Almost all known parallelism of
{\rm PG}(n,2)
fall out as a corollary of the fact that the linear Hamming code ca be partitioned into translates of a Preparata-like code of the same length.
The existence of such a partition using the classical Preparata code was first demonstrated by Zaitsev, Zinoviev, and Semakov
[
45
]
in 1973, and independently by Baker
[
1
]
in 1976. This was further extended by Baker, van Lint, and Wilson
[
3
]
in 1983 who gave a construction of the generalized Preparata codes
1
1

1

In this paper, a
generalized Preparata code
always refers to the codes described in
[
3
]
.
and showed that these too can be used to partition the linear Hamming code. Our review of the literature on parallelisms of
{\rm PG}(n,2)
and on partitions of the linear Hamming code has indicated that recent results on partitions of the Hamming code have not been incorporated into the literature on parallelisms of
{\rm PG}(n,2)
.
The results of V. A. Zinoviev and D. V. Zinoviev
[
46
]
from 2016, for example, imply the existence of a new class of parallelisms that have not yet been considered.
We also came across some old results that seem to have been forgotten. Therefore, aside from highlighting our own contribution to the construction of new parallelisms of
{\rm PG}(n,2)
, we make it a priority to survey all relevant results and give the reader a complete overview of the current state of the relevant research.

1.1
Parallelisms of
{\rm PG}(n,q)
,
q\neq 2

Since the 1970’s, research on the existence of parallelisms of PG
(n,q)
has been an active area. In 1973, Denniston
[
14
]
constructed the first family of parallelisms for each
q>2
in PG
(3,q)
using the Klein quadric. Around the same time, Denniston
[
13
]
also gave an example of a cyclic regular parallelism of
{\rm PG}(3,8)
. In 1974, Beutelspacher
[
6
]
gave a construction of parallelisms of PG
(n,q)
when
q>2
and
n=2^{k}-1
for any
k>1
. The main idea in Beutelspacher’s construction was the embedding of PG
(n,q)
into PG
(n,q^{2})
and proceeding to construct parallelisms via induction.

In 1998, Prince
[
36
]
enumerated (up to equivalence) all cyclic parallelisms of
{\rm PG}(3,5)
, there are a total of 45 of them, 2 of which are also regular parallelisms. Later in the same year, Penttila and Williams
[
33
]
constructed cyclic regular parallelisms of
{\rm PG}(3,q)
for each
q\equiv 2\pmod{3}
. This was achieved by a special partitioning of the points of the Klein quadric and then using the Klein correspondence to obtain a parallelism. Several methods have been designed to obtain new parallelisms of
{\rm PG}(3,q)
from old ones, see
[
15
,
23
,
32
]
.

No new cases for the existence of parallelisms were resolved until 2023, when Feng and Xu
[
44
]
showed that parallelisms exist in PG
(n,q)
for
q=3,4,8,16
and for all odd
n
. Their approach depended on proving that the existence of a certain type of parallelism of
{\rm PG}(n,q)
can be reduced to finding a special set of spreads in
{\rm PG}(3,q)
. In
[
44
]
, such a set was found for each value of
q
listed above.

For a more detailed history of parallelisms of PG
(n,q)
, see the survey by Johnson
[
24
]
. It’s worth noting that since this survey was published in 2003, there has been a flurry of research on parallelisms of PG
(3,q)

[
32
,
40
,
41
]
. In particular, we point out that recently, Pavese and Santonastaso
[
32
]
proved for
q>2
that
{\rm PG}(3,q)
admits at least
\Theta(q^{q-1}q!)
inequivalent parallelisms when
q
is even and
\Theta(q^{2q-3})
inequivalent parallelisms of when
q
is odd.

1.2
On Preparata-like and Hamming-like codes

A binary linear code
C
with parameters
[m,k,d]
is a
k
-dimensional subspace of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}
such that the Hamming distance between any two codewords in
C
is at least
d
. The binary linear Hamming code
H_{n}
is a linear code with parameters
[2^{n}-1,2^{n}-n-1,3]
. Its parity-check matrix is the
n\times(2^{n}-1)
matrix whose columns are formed by the distinct non-zero vectors of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
. A (not necessarily linear) code
C
is called
Hamming-like
if it has the same parameters as
H_{n}
for some
n
. A
Preparata-like
code
P_{n}
is a binary code with length
2^{n}-1
, size
2^{2^{n}-2n}
and minimum distance 5. Preparata-like codes can only exist when
n
is even. The first infinite family of such codes was given by Preparata
[
35
]
in 1968. The description of these codes was subsequently simplified and generalized in 1983
[
3
]
. Today, there are many known examples of Preparata-like codes
[
26
,
42
]
.
An extended Preparata-like code
EP_{n}
can be obtained from a Preparata-like code
P_{n}
by adding a parity-check bit, thereby increasing the length and minimum distance of the code both by 1. In a similar manner, one can obtain an extended Hamming-like code
EH_{n}
from a corresponding Hamming-like code
H_{n}
. We remark that results in the literature are given sometimes for the extended codes and sometimes for non-extended versions. There is a natural relationship between the two, so unless stated otherwise, we give all results in terms of their implication for the non-extended version of Preparata-like and Hamming-like codes.

In
[
45
]
, it was shown that any Preparata-like code
P_{n}
is contained in a Hamming-like code
H_{n}
of the same length
m
. In particular, it was shown that
H_{n}=P_{n}\cup E
, where
E
is the set of all codewords in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}
whose distance to
P_{n}
is at least 3.

It is known that if an extended Preparata-like code is
\mathbb{Z}_{4}
-linear (via the gray map, see
[
19
]
), then the corresponding extended Hamming-like code containing it is also
\mathbb{Z}_{4}
-linear as was shown by Borges, Phelps, Rifá, and Zinoviev
[
8
]
. The linear extended Hamming code
EH_{n}
is not
\mathbb{Z}_{4}
-linear except when
n\leq 4

[
19
]
. In fact, for all known Preparata-like codes
P_{n}
, exactly one of the following holds:

1.

P_{n}
is contained in the linear Hamming code
[
3
,
42
]
, or

2.

EP_{n}
is
\mathbb{Z}_{4}
-linear and is contained in an extended Hamming-like code which is
\mathbb{Z}_{4}
-linear
[
8
,
19
,
26
]
.

Since the seminal results of
[
1
,
3
,
45
]
, Preparata-like codes and their relation to the Hamming-like codes containing them, have been studied extensively. As we noted, the class of generalized Preparata codes are known to give rise to a partition of the linear Hamming code
[
1
,
3
,
45
]
. It is also known that any extended
\mathbb{Z}_{4}
-linear Preparata-like code
EP_{n}
partitions the extended
\mathbb{Z}_{4}
-linear Hamming-like code containing it by additive translates of
EP_{n}
over
\mathbb{Z}_{4}

[
8
]
. Furthermore, it was recently shown that it is possible to introduce a group operation on the generalized Preparata codes and the linear Hamming code so that both would be interpreted as group codes under this operation
[
46
]
. Consequently, a new and inequivalent partition of the Hamming code was given by the cosets of the generalized Preparata code in the linear Hamming code via this group operation.

In 2009, Zinoviev asked if any Preparata-like code gives rise to a partition of the corresponding Hamming-like code containing it (see
[
20
]
). Our first result resolves this question in the affirmative in the case that the Hamming-like code is linear.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
P_{n}
be a Preparata-like code contained in the linear Hamming code
H_{n}
of the same length. Then
H_{n}
can be partitioned into additive translates of
P_{n}
.

In the next section, we describe how such partitions induce parallelisms of
{\rm PG}(n,2)
and give an accounting of all known parallelisms of
{\rm PG}(n,2)
.

1.3
Parallelisms of
{\rm PG}(n,2)

Let
H_{n}
and
EH_{n}
be the linear and extended linear Hamming codes respectively. Likewise, let
P_{n}
and
EP_{n}
be some Preparata-like and extended Preparata-like code. Recall that the parity-check matrix of
H_{n}
has as columns precisely the set of all non-zero vectors of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
. Thus, any codeword
c\in H_{n}
can be considered to be the characteristic vector of a subset
S_{c}\subset\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
satisfying
\sum_{x\in S_{c}}x=0
. Since the minimum weight of
H_{n}
is 3, we observe that this implies that the minimum-weight codewords of
H_{n}
are precisely all vectors which are characteristic vectors of the sets
\{a,b,a+b\}\subset\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
, where
a\neq b
. In other words, these are precisely the lines of
{\rm PG}(n-1,2)
. Observe that likewise, the minimum-codewords of
EH_{n}
can be identified as subsets
S\subset\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
of cardinality 4, satisfying
\sum_{x\in S}x=0
. Consequently, all such vectors are the characteristic vectors of some affine plane in AG
(n,2)
.

A partition of
H_{n}
into additive translates of
P_{n}
also partitions the minimum-weight codewords of
H_{n}
, and therefore the lines of
{\rm PG}(n-1,2)
. Note that since each translate is a Preparata-like code, it has minimum distance
5
. Thus, any two codewords of weight 3 in the same translate must have disjoint supports. A quick computation shows that
|H|=2^{n-1}|P|
, which implies that in each translate the minimum weight codewords correspond to spreads of
{\rm PG}(n-1,2)
, and therefore, all the translates together constitute a parallelism of
{\rm PG}(n-1,2)
. All of this extends naturally to
EH_{n}
and
EP_{n}
, where one obtains that the codewords of weight 4 in each translate of
EP_{n}
form a 2-design of affine planes in AG
(n,2)
. This implies that the Steiner quadruple system arising from the points and planes of AG
(n,2)
is resolvable.

The paper
[
46
]
, which gives a new partition of the extended linear Hamming code into cosets of the extended generalized Preparata code, also demonstrates that the corresponding partition of the affine planes is inequivalent to those obtained in
[
3
]
. Furthermore,
[
46
, Lemma 9]
implies that each part in the partition corresponds to a unique spread in
{\rm PG}(2n-1,2)
and so this construction actually yields a new class of inequivalent parallelisms of
{\rm PG}(2n-1,2)
, however, to our knowledge, this implication has not been noted anywhere.

1.4
On crooked functions

Crooked functions were introduced by Bending and Fon-der-Flaass
[
5
]
in order to generalize the family of distance-regular graphs constructed by De Caen, Mathon, and Moorhouse
[
11
]
.

Definition 1.2

(
[
5
]
)
.

Let
f
be a function on
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
. The function
f
is called crooked if it satisfies the following conditions:

1.

f(0)=0
.

2.

f(x)+f(y)+f(z)+f(x+y+z)\neq 0
for any three distinct
x,y,z\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
.

3.

f(x)+f(y)+f(z)+f(x+a)+f(y+a)+f(z+a)\neq 0
for any
a\neq 0
and any
x,y,z\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
.

Remark
: The definition we give above is the original definition of crooked functions. We note that in more recent literature, a broader class of functions is sometimes referred to as crooked
[
7
,
27
]
. In this paper, any function called crooked strictly obeys Definition
1.2
.

Definition 1.3

(
[
5
]
)
.

Let
f
and
f^{\prime}
be two crooked functions over
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
.

1.

We say that
f^{\prime}
is affine equivalent to
f
if there exists an
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
and linear permutations
L_{1},L_{2}
of
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
such that
f^{\prime}(x)=L_{1}(f(L_{2}(x)+\alpha))+L_{1}(f(\alpha))
.

2.

We say that
f^{\prime}
is linearly equivalent to
f
if
f^{\prime}
is affine equivalent to
f
with
\alpha=0
.

Since their introduction, crooked functions have found many applications, including the construction of new uniformly packed codes and Preparata-like codes
[
42
]
. Godsil and Roy
[
17
]
have shown that crooked functions are characterized by the corresponding construction of Preparata-like codes. We describe these codes below.

Definition 1.4
.

Let
f
be a function over
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
. Let
X\subset\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{*}
and let
Y\subset\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
. Denote by
\mathbbm{1}_{X}
the characteristic vector of length
2^{n}-1
of the set
X
and denote by
\mathbbm{1}_{Y}
the characteristic vector of length
2^{n}
of the set
Y
. Define the code
P_{f}
to be the set of all codewords of the form
(\mathbbm{1}_{X},\mathbbm{1}_{Y})
with
X\subset\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{*}
and
Y\subset\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
where
X
,
Y
satisfy:

1.

|Y|
is even

2.

It holds that

\sum_{x\in X}x=\sum_{y\in Y}y.

3.

It holds that

\sum_{x\in X}f(x)=\sum_{y\in Y}f(y)+f\left(\sum_{y\in Y}y\right).

Remark
: Observe that conditions (1) and (2) are precisely the conditions which imply that the codewords also belong to the linear Hamming code of length
2^{n+1}-1
.

Proposition 1.5

(
[
17
,
42
]
)
.

The code
P_{f}
is a Preparata-like code if and only if
f
is crooked.

Since crooked Preparata-like codes of length
2^{n+1}-1
are contained in the linear Hamming code of the same length, Theorem
1.1
implies that crooked functions also give rise to parallelisms of
{\rm PG}(n,2)
. Next, we explicitly describe how one can construct a parallelism from a crooked function. For this we identify PG
(n,2)
with
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n+1}
which we identify with
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}\times\mathbb{F}_{2}
.

Definition 1.6
.

Let
f
be a crooked function over
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
.
We define the coloring function
c_{f}:(\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}\times\mathbb{F}_{2})\times(\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}\times\mathbb{F}_{2})\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
via

c_{f}((x,x_{1}),(y,y_{1}))=f(x+y)+f(x)+f(y)+f(x_{1}y+y_{1}x).

In Section
4
, we show that
c_{f}
induces a coloring of the lines of PG
(n,2)
, where the colors are the elements of
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{*}
.

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
f
be a crooked function over
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
. The color classes of
c_{f}
constitute pairwise disjoint spreads and the induced parallelism is denoted by
\Pi_{f}
.

Two parallelisms
\Pi_{1}
and
\Pi_{2}
of PG
(n,2)
are said to be equivalent if there exists a collineation of PG
(n,2)
which maps the spreads of
\Pi_{1}
to the spreads of
\Pi_{2}
. Our third main result establishes an equivalence criterion for parallelisms coming from our construction.

Theorem 1.8
.

Let
f
and
f^{\prime}
be crooked functions over
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
with
n>1
odd and let
\Pi_{f}
and
\Pi_{f^{\prime}}
be the parallelisms induced by
c_{f}
and
c_{f^{\prime}}
.

1.

If
f
and
f^{\prime}
are linearly equivalent, then
\Pi_{f}
and
\Pi_{f^{\prime}}
are equivalent.

2.

Suppose further that
f
and
f^{\prime}
are quadratic and that
n>3
. It holds that
\Pi_{f}
and
\Pi_{f}^{\prime}
are equivalent if and only if

f
and
f^{\prime}
are affine equivalent.

Below, we give a brief survey of the known families of inequivalent crooked functions. This requires us to first take a detour through the world of almost perfect nonlinear (APN) functions which contain the class of crooked functions
[
5
]
.

A function
f
on
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
is said to be APN if the equation

f(x+a)+f(x)=b

has precisely 0 or 2 solutions in
x
for each
a,b\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
, and
a\neq 0
.
Observe that any crooked function is necessarily APN
[
34
]
.
APN functions were introduced by Nyberg
[
31
]
, who demonstrated their utility in cryptography. Several notions of equivalence, such as linear equivalence, affine equivalence, (extended affine) EA-equivalence and CCZ-equivalence have been introduced in the study of APN functions and much work has gone into finding new inequivalent APN functions
[
10
,
28
]
.
The definition of linear equivalence and affine equivalence for APN functions are the same as for crooked functions. EA-equivalence however is a more general notion. Two APN functions
f
and
f^{\prime}
are EA-equivalent if there exist affine permutations
A_{1},A_{2}
and an affine function
A_{3}
such that
f^{\prime}(x)=A_{1}(f(A_{2}(x)))+A_{3}(x)
. We remark that two crooked functions that are affine equivalent are necessarily also EA-equivalent as APN functions.

It is known that crooked functions are necessarily bijective
[
5
]
. It follows that any quadratic APN permutation is crooked. To date, the only known crooked functions are quadratic. It has been conjectured that any crooked function must be quadratic, and this conjecture has been confirmed in some special cases
[
7
,
27
]
. Until 2008, the only known crooked functions were precisely
f(x)=x^{2^{t}+1}
when
n
is odd and
\gcd(n,t)=1
. Since then, several new infinite families have been discovered
[
10
,
28
]
. Consequently, our construction implies the existence of inequivalent parallelisms of PG
(n,2)
to those from
[
1
,
3
,
25
,
45
]
.

The following list contains the only known examples of crooked functions, all of which are quadratic APN permutations. We remark that the families (1) and (2) below have been proven to be EA-inequivalent to each other
[
10
]
. Family (3) is conjectured to be inequivalent to families (1) and (2).

1.

Let
n
be odd and gcd
(t,n)=1
. Then

f(x)=x^{2^{t}+1}

is a quadratic APN permutation over
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
. These correspond to the generalized Preparata codes. The functions of the form above (without restrictions on
n
and
t
) are referred to as
Gold functions

[
18
]
. Their APN properties were observed by Nyberg
[
31
]
. It is known that
x^{2^{t}+1}
and
x^{2^{s}+1}
are EA-equivalent if and only if
s=t
or
s=n-t
.

2.

Let
s
and
k
be positive integers such that
\gcd(6,k)=\gcd(3k,s)=1
. Define
i=sk\pmod{3}
,
t=3-i
and
n=3k
. Let
w\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{*}
be an element of order
2^{2k}+2^{k}+1
. Then

f(x)=x^{2^{s}+1}+wx^{2^{ik}+2^{tk+s}}

is a quadratic APN permutation over
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
. This family was constructed by Budaghyan, Carlet and Leander
[
10
]
. To our knowledge no equivalence criterion has been given which specifies the parameter sets
(s,w)
that yield EA-equivalent functions.

3.

Let
n
be an odd positive integer not divisible by 7. Let
i
a positive integer satisfying
\gcd(i,n)=1
and set
q=2^{i}
. Then the function (in triviate form)

In this section we introduce crooked and APN functions as well as parallelisms as separate objects and collect some preliminary results.

2.1
APN and crooked functions

Let
f
be an APN function over
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
. A key tool which is often used in the study of these functions is the
derivative of
f
in the direction of
a\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{*}
, denoted

D_{a}(f(x))=f(x+a)+f(x).

It was shown in
[
5
]
that crooked functions can be characterized by the image sets of their derivatives in any fixed direction.

Proposition 2.1

(
[
5
]
)
.

Let
f
be a function on
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
satisfying
f(0)=0
and let
a\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{*}
. Then
f
is crooked if and only if the sets

H_{a}=\{D_{a}(f(x)):x\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}\}

are all distinct and each
H_{a}
is the complement of a hyperplane in
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
.

Remark
: Consider a crooked function
f
, let
a\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{*}
and let
H_{a}
be as in the proposition above. As
f(a)\in H_{a}
, we have that
H_{a}
is the complement of the hyperplane defined by
H_{a}+f(a)
.

An important property of crooked functions, which can be proven quickly from the definition, is that any crooked function must be bijective. Our construction of the parallelisms of
{\rm PG}(n,2)
using crooked functions will rely on this fact, among other things.

Lemma 2.2

(
[
5
]
)
.

Let
f
be a crooked function over
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
. Then
f
is a permutation.

For every APN function
f
, there is an associated symmetric bivariate function which will also play a key role in our construction.

Definition 2.3
.

For an APN function
f
we define the bivariate function
B_{f}:\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}\times\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
by

B_{f}(x,y)=f(x)+f(y)+f(x+y)
.

If
f
is quadratic, then it follows that
B_{f}(x,y)
is bilinear. It has in fact been shown that if
f
and
f^{\prime}
are two quadratic APN functions for which
B_{f}=B_{f^{\prime}}
, then
f
and
f^{\prime}
are EA-equivalent (see Lemma 5 in
[
16
]
).

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
f
be a function over
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
. Then
f
is APN if and only if
B_{f}(a,x)=b
has zero or two solutions in
x
for each
a\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{*}
and
b\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
.

Proof.

Recall that
f
is an APN function if and only if for each
a\neq 0
, we have that
f(x)+f(x+a)=b
has zero or two solutions for any
b\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
.
By defining
b^{\prime}:=f(a)+b
, we have that
f
is an APN function if and only if

f(x)+f(x+a)=b^{\prime}
has zero or two solutions.
Equivalently,
f
is an APN function if and only if for each
a\neq 0
we have that

B_{f}(a,x)=f(x)+f(x+a)+f(a)=b

has zero or two solutions.
∎

2.2
Spreads and Parallelisms

In this preliminary subsection, we collect results on parallelisms.
For integers
q,a,b\in\mathbb{Z}
with
q\geq 2
we define the Gaussian coefficient

\displaystyle{\begin{bmatrix}b\\
a\end{bmatrix}_{q}}:=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{a}\frac{q^{b-a+i}-1}{q^{i}-1}&\text{if $0\leq a\leq b$,}\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Unless specified otherwise we can always assume that
n
is an odd, positive integer.

Recall from the introduction that a spread of
{\rm PG}(n,q)
is a partition of the points into lines.
Furthermore, a parallelism
\Pi
of
{\rm PG}(n,q)
is a partition of the lines into spreads.

Definition 2.5
.

Two parallelisms
\Pi_{1}
and
\Pi_{2}
of
{\rm PG}(n,q)
are equivalent if there exists a collineation
\kappa
on
{\rm PG}(n,q)
that induces a one-to-one correspondence between the spreads of
\Pi_{1}
and the spreads of
\Pi_{2}
.

Let
P
be a fixed point of
{\rm PG}(n,q)
. No two distinct lines incident with
P
can be in one spread, but every spread has to contain a line that is incident with
P
. This implies the following: If
\Pi
is a parallelism of
{\rm PG}(n,q)
the number of spreads in
\Pi
is equal to the number of lines incident with
P
, which is
{\begin{bmatrix}n\\
1\end{bmatrix}_{q}}
.

The size of a spread can be calculated as the quotient of the number of points in
{\rm PG}(n,q)
and the number of points on each line. We conclude that a spread has size

\frac{{\begin{bmatrix}n+1\\
1\end{bmatrix}_{q}}}{q+1}=q^{n-1}+...+q^{4}+q^{2}+1.

Lemma 2.6
.

For any spread and any hyperplane of
{\rm PG}(n,q)
, the number of lines of the spread contained inside the hyperplane is
q^{n-3}+...+q^{4}+q^{2}+1
.

Proof.

Let
H
be a hyperplane and let
S
be a spread of
{\rm PG}(n,q)
.
All points of
H
are covered by lines of the spread.
Each line of
S
contained inside
H
covers
q+1
points of
H
and each line of
S
not contained inside
H
covers
1
point of
H
.
Let
s
be the number of lines of
S
that are contained in
H
.
Then
|H|=s(q+1)+|S|-s
and so

\displaystyle s

\displaystyle=\frac{|H|-|S|}{q}

\displaystyle=\frac{{\begin{bmatrix}n\\
1\end{bmatrix}_{q}}-{\begin{bmatrix}n+1\\
1\end{bmatrix}_{q}}/(q+1)}{q}

\displaystyle=\frac{(q^{n-1}+...+q^{2}+q+1)-(q^{n-3}+...+q^{4}+q^{2}+1)}{q}

\displaystyle=\frac{q^{n-1}+...+q^{5}+q^{3}+q}{q}

\displaystyle=|S|-q^{n-1}.\qed

3
Partitioning the Hamming code

In this section we prove Theorem
1.1
. The proof is fairly straightforward and follows the proof of this fact for generalized Preparata codes
[
3
]
quite closely. For convenience we restate our first main result.

Theorem
1.1
.

Let
P_{n}
be a Preparata-like code contained in the linear Hamming code
H_{n}
of the same length. Then
H_{n}
can be partitioned into additive translates of
P_{n}
.

Proof.

Suppose that
P_{n}
is contained in
H_{n}
.
First, assume that the zero codeword is in
P_{n}
.
Since
H_{n}
is a binary linear Hamming code, we can identify the coordinates of the codewords with the non-zero vectors of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
. Let
\{e_{1},e_{2},\dots,e_{n}\}
be the usual standard ordered basis for
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
and define
T:=\text{span}\{e_{2},\dots,e_{n}\}\setminus\{0\}
. Let
\alpha\in T
and define
t_{\alpha}
to be the characteristic vector (with coordinates ordered in the same way as the codewords in
H_{n}
) of the set
\{e_{1},\alpha,e_{1}+\alpha\}
.
As the sum of the elements in
\{e_{1},\alpha,e_{1}+\alpha\}
equals
0
, we have that
t_{\alpha}\in H_{n}
for any such
\alpha
. Furthermore, we observe that
t_{\alpha}
has weight 3, and
t_{\alpha}+t_{\beta}
has weight 4 if
\alpha\neq\beta
, as
t_{\alpha}
and
t_{\beta}
have a common element in their support.

Let
P_{n}
be the Preparata-like code contained in
H
and define the set
P_{n}+t_{\alpha}=\{p+t_{\alpha}:p\in P_{n}\}
. We claim that the sets

\displaystyle\{P_{n}\}\cup\{P_{n}+t_{\alpha}:\alpha\in T\}

(1)

form a partition of
H_{n}
. Observe that since
P_{n}\subset H_{n}
,
t_{\alpha}\in H_{n}
, and
H_{n}
is linear, we have
P_{n}+t_{\alpha}\subset H_{n}
. As
|P_{n}+t_{\alpha}|=|P_{n}|=2^{2^{n}-2n}
and
|H_{n}|=2^{2^{n}-n-1}=2^{n-1}|P_{n}|=(|T|+1)|P_{n}|
, it suffices to show that the sets (
1
) are disjoint.

Recall that
P_{n}
has minimum distance 5. Since
t_{\alpha}
is a vector of weight 3, then
P_{n}
and
P_{n}+t_{\alpha}
are necessarily disjoint. If
\alpha
and
\beta
are distinct elements in
T
with
(P_{n}+t_{\alpha})\cap(P_{n}+t_{\beta})\neq\emptyset
, then there exists codewords
c,d\in P_{n}
such that
c+t_{\alpha}=d+t_{\beta}
which implies
c+d=t_{\alpha}+t_{\beta}
. We already noted that
t_{\alpha}+t_{\beta}
has weight 4, which would imply that
c+d
has weight
4
. This yields that the distance between
c,d\in P_{n}
is 4, a contradiction. Thus, (
1
) is a partition of
H_{n}
into additive translates of
P_{n}
.

If the zero codeword is not in
P_{n}
, we may add any codeword
c\in P_{n}
to
P_{n}
to obtain the code
P_{n}^{\prime}=P_{n}+c
. Now
P_{n}^{\prime}
contains the zero codeword and is still contained in
H_{n}
, since
H_{n}
is linear. As
P_{n}^{\prime}
is an additive translate of
P_{n}
the arguments above all work the same way if one replaces
P_{n}
by
P_{n}^{\prime}
.
∎

Identify
{\rm PG}(n,2)

\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n+1}

Definition
1.6

Let

\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}

We define the coloring function

Clearly the function

is symmetric. Next, we study when the function yields

.

Lemma 4.1
.

For a crooked function
f
over
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
, we have
c_{f}((x,x_{1}),(y,y_{1}))=0
if and only if
(x,x_{1})=(0,0)

(x,x_{1})=(y,y_{1})

Proof.

If
(x,x_{1})=(0,0)

(x,x_{1})=(y,y_{1})

c_{f}((x,x_{1}),(y,y_{1}))=0
. So let us assume that
c_{f}((x,x_{1}),(y,y_{1}))=0

x_{1},y_{1}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}
, so in view of the symmetry there are three cases to consider.

Case 1:

x_{1}=y_{1}=0
.

In this case

is equivalent to
f(x+y)+f(x)=f(y)
. Since
f
is crooked, and therefore APN, it follows that for a fixed
y
there are precisely
0
or
2
solutions in
x
. The solutions are
x=y
and
x=0
.

Case 2:

x_{1}=1
,
y_{1}=0
.

In this case

). Therefore, we have

which yields

Case 3:

.

In this case

. If the elements
x
and
y
are distinct, then so are
f(x)
and
f(y)
, as
f
is a permutation. Therefore, we have
x=y
.
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

For a crooked function
f
over
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
the function

induces a coloring on the lines of
{\rm PG}(n,2)
, where the colors are the elements of
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{*}

Proof.

Let
(x,x_{1}),(y,y_{1})\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}\times\mathbb{F}_{2}
be distinct and nonzero. The line spanned by
(x,x_{1})
and
(y,y_{1})

\displaystyle c_{f}((x,x_{1}),(x+y,x_{1}+y_{1}))

\displaystyle=f(x)+f(x+y)+f(x+(x+y))+f(x_{1}(x+y)+(x_{1}+y_{1})x)

\displaystyle=f(x)+f(x+y)+f(y)+f(x_{1}x+x_{1}y+x_{1}x+y_{1}x)

\displaystyle=f(x)+f(x+y)+f(y)+f(x_{1}y+y_{1}x)

\displaystyle=c_{f}((x,x_{1}),(y,y_{1})).\qed

Notation 4.3
.

Let
\mathcal{H}\subset{\rm PG}(n,2)
be the hyperplane identified with
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}\times\{0\}
.

As can be observed in the definition of the coloring function

.

Let

The equivalence problem

Our goal in this section is to prove Theorem

.
First, we discuss some prerequisites regarding collineations of projective spaces.

A collineation of a projective space is an permutation of the points of said space that maps collinear points to collinear points. For a thorough discussion of collineations see
[
12
]
.
The fundamental theorem of projective geometry states that every collineation of
{\rm PG}(n,q)
can be identified with a bijective, semilinear map on
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n+1}

[
12
]
.
The only automorphism of
\mathbb{F}_{2}
is the identity, so all semilinear maps of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n+1}
are linear.
Finally, we remark that every linear map on
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n+1}
can be represented by an matrix in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{(n+1)\times(n+1)}
.

Let
\kappa
be a collineation of
{\rm PG}(n,2)
and let

\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, and

, and
\beta\in\mathbb{F}_{2}
, such that we can write
M

M=\begin{pmatrix}A&\alpha\\
B&\beta\\
\end{pmatrix}.

Let us consider a vector
\overline{x}
of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n+1}
. Again we use block notation to write
\overline{x}=(x,x_{1})^{\top}
, where
x_{1}
is the block that contains only the last coordinate and
x
is the block that contains the first
n
coordinates. Using this notation it may be clear that
M\overline{x}=(Ax+\alpha x_{1},Bx+\beta x_{1})
.

As we identify
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}

\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}

\kappa((x,x_{1}))=(Ax+\alpha x_{1},Bx+\beta x_{1})

For the remainder of this section we use
c_{f}\kappa
to denote the map

c_{f}\kappa:(\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}\times\mathbb{F}_{2})\times(\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}\times\mathbb{F}_{2})\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
,
((x,x_{1})),(y,y_{1}))\mapsto c_{f}(\kappa((x,x_{1})),\kappa((y,y_{1})))
.

Remark
:
In view of Definition
1.3
and Definition
2.5
, we see that two crooked functions
f
and
f^{\prime}
over
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
give rise to equivalent parallelisms via
c_{f}
and
c_{f^{\prime}}
if there exists a permutation
\sigma
of
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
with
\sigma(0)=0
and a collineation
\kappa
of
{\rm PG}(n,2)
such that
\sigma c_{f^{\prime}}=c_{f}\kappa
.

It is straight forward to prove part 1 of Theorem
1.8
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
f
and
f^{\prime}
be linearly equivalent. Then
\Pi_{f}
and
\Pi_{f^{\prime}}
are equivalent.

Proof.

If
f
and
f^{\prime}
are linearly equivalent, there exist linear permutations
L_{1},L_{2}
of
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
such that
f^{\prime}=L_{1}fL_{2}
.
For two points
(x,x_{1}),(y,y_{1})\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}\times\mathbb{F}_{2}
, this implies the following

\displaystyle c_{f^{\prime}}((x,x_{1}),(y,y_{1}))

\displaystyle=f^{\prime}(x)+f^{\prime}(y)+f^{\prime}(x+y)+f^{\prime}(x_{1}y+y_{1}x)

\displaystyle=L_{1}(f(L_{2}x))+L_{1}(f(L_{2}y))+L_{1}(f(L_{2}(x+y)))+L_{1}(f(L_{2}(x_{1}y+y_{1}x)))

\displaystyle=L_{1}(f(L_{2}x))+L_{1}(f(L_{2}y))+L_{1}(f(L_{2}x+L_{2}y))+L_{1}(f(x_{1}L_{2}y+y_{1}L_{2}x))

\displaystyle=L_{1}\bigl(f(L_{2}x)+f(L_{2}y)+f(L_{2}x+L_{2}y)+f(x_{1}L_{2}y+y_{1}L_{2}x)\bigr)

\displaystyle=L_{1}\bigl(c_{f}((L_{2}x,x_{1}),(L_{2}y,y_{1}))\bigr).

By defining
\sigma=L_{1}^{-1}
and
\kappa
via
\kappa((x,x_{1}))=(L_{2}x,x_{1})
, it follows that
\sigma c_{f^{\prime}}=c_{f}\kappa
.
∎

Our last goal is to show part 2 of Theorem
1.8
. This is more involved and the proof is split up into several lemmas. Recall that we denote by
\mathcal{H}
the hyperplane of
{\rm PG}(n,2)
that is identified with
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}\times\{0\}
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
n>1
be an odd integer.
For
\overline{x}\in\mathcal{H}
the image of
c_{f}(\overline{x},.):\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
is an
(n-1)
-dimensional subspace of
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
that is unique to
\overline{x}
.

Note that
\overline{x},\overline{y}\in\mathcal{H}
are identified with
(x,0),(y,0)
. Thus, we have that
c_{f}(\overline{x},\overline{y})=f(x+y)+f(x)+f(y)
and the lemma above is directly implied by Proposition
2.1
and the remark following it.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
f
be a quadratic crooked function. Let
\mathcal{C}
be a subspace of
{\rm PG}(n,q)
with codimension
2
, contained in
\mathcal{H}
. If
n>3
there is a spread
S
contained inside the the parallelism
\Pi_{f}
defined by
c_{f}
that satisfies the following property: Not all lines of
S
that are contained in
\mathcal{H}
are in
\mathcal{C}
, but at least one is.

Proof.

We assume that there is no spread inside the parallelism
\Pi_{f}
that has the desired property. This implies that if a spread
S
has a line contained inside
\mathcal{C}
, then all the lines of
S
contained inside
\mathcal{H}
are already contained inside
\mathcal{C}
.
By Lemma
2.6
there are
|S|-2^{n-1}=2^{n-3}+\ldots+2^{4}+2^{2}+1
lines of
S
in
\mathcal{H}
. This is precisely the size of a spread in PG
(n-2,2)
. So, in particular
\Pi_{f}
induces a parallelism
\Pi_{f}(\mathcal{C})
on
\mathcal{C}
.
This implies that for a fixed
\overline{x}\in\mathcal{C}
, the image set
\{c_{f}(\overline{x},\overline{y}):\overline{y}\in\mathcal{C}\}
is the same, call it
X
.
As
f
is quadratic,
c_{f}
is bilinear on
\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{H}
, hence the image
\{c_{f}(\overline{x},\overline{y}):\overline{y}\in\mathcal{C}\}
is a subspace of
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
. A point in
\mathcal{C}
is incident with
{\begin{bmatrix}(n-1)-1\\
2-1\end{bmatrix}_{2}}={\begin{bmatrix}n-2\\
1\end{bmatrix}_{2}}
lines in
\mathcal{C}
, which is the number of nonzero elements in an
(n-2)
-dimensional subspace in
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
. Hence
X
is an
(n-2)
-dimensional subspace of
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
.

On the other hand, let
\overline{x}\in\mathcal{C}
and define
\mathcal{H}_{\overline{x}}=\{c_{f}(\overline{x},\overline{y}):\overline{y}\in\mathcal{H}\}
. The discussion above implies that for any
a,b\in\mathcal{C}
we have
\mathcal{H}_{a}\cap\mathcal{H}_{b}=X
. But Lemma
5.2
states that the hyperplane
\mathcal{H}_{a}
is distinct for each
a
. Note that there are precisely
{\begin{bmatrix}n-(n-2)\\
(n-1)-(n-2)\end{bmatrix}_{2}}={\begin{bmatrix}2\\
1\end{bmatrix}_{2}}=3
hyperplanes of
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
which can pairwise intersect in the same codimension 2 subspace of
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
. This would imply that the number of points in
\mathcal{C}
is 3, which cannot be since
n>3
.
∎

For
n=3
, Lemma
2.6
implies that a spread has only one line in a fixed hyperplane.
Therefore, the condition
n>3
in Lemma
5.3
is necessary and this is the reason why we need
n>3
throughout this section and in Theorem
1.8
.
By Lemma
4.2
the map
c_{f}
assigns each line of
{\rm PG}(n,2)
a color. Therefore, we will write
c_{f}(\ell)
to denote the color assigned to the line
\ell
of
{\rm PG}(n,2)
.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
f
and
f^{\prime}
be quadratic crooked functions over
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
with
n>3
and let
\sigma
be a permutation of
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
with
\sigma(0)=0
. Furthermore, let
\kappa
be a collineation of
{\rm PG}(n,2)
such that

\sigma c_{f}=c_{f^{\prime}}\kappa.

Then
\kappa(\mathcal{H})=\mathcal{H}
.

Proof.

Let
\Pi_{f}
and
\Pi_{f^{\prime}}
be the parallelisms defined by
c_{f}
and
c_{f^{\prime}}
respectively.
Recall that given a quadratic function
f
over
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
, we have a corresponding symmetric bilinear function
B_{f}(x,y)=f(x+y)+f(x)+f(y)
. Observe that given a coloring function
c_{f}
, we obtain that
c_{f}((x,0),(y,0))=B_{f}(x,y)=f(x+y)+f(x)+f(y)
. Thus, restricting
c_{f}
to
\mathcal{H}
, we recover
B_{f}
.

Suppose for a contradiction that
f
and
f^{\prime}
are quadratic APN permutations for which there exists a permutation
\sigma
of
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
and collineation
\kappa
of
{\rm PG}(n,2)
such that
\sigma c_{f}=c_{f^{\prime}}\kappa
and
\kappa(\mathcal{H})\neq\mathcal{H}
. This yields
\kappa^{-1}(\mathcal{H})\neq\mathcal{H}
. Clearly
c_{f^{\prime}}=c_{f^{\prime}}\kappa\kappa^{-1}
and so as
c_{f^{\prime}}
is bilinear on
\mathcal{H}
, we have that
c_{f^{\prime}}\kappa
is bilinear on
\kappa^{-1}(\mathcal{H})
. Therefore,
\sigma c_{f}
is also bilinear on
\kappa^{-1}(\mathcal{H})
.

Set
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{H}\cap\kappa^{-1}(\mathcal{H})
and observe that
\mathcal{C}
has codimension
2
as
\mathcal{H}\neq\kappa^{-1}(\mathcal{H})
. By Lemma
5.3
, there is a spread
S
in
\Pi_{f}
that has a line
\ell_{1}
in
\mathcal{C}
but also a line
\ell_{2}
in
\kappa^{-1}(\mathcal{H})
that is not contained in
\mathcal{C}
. Now take any line
k
not contained in
\mathcal{H}
which intersects
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
. Denote
\ell_{1}\cap k=P_{1}
and
\ell_{2}\cap k=P_{2}
.
Clearly such a line
k
belongs to a spread distinct from
S
. Consider now the planes
\pi_{1}=\langle\ell_{1},k\rangle
and
\pi_{2}=\langle\ell_{2},k\rangle
. Let
m_{1}
be the line in
\pi_{1}
distinct from
\ell_{1}
and
k
, but which contains
P_{1}
. Likewise let
m_{2}
be the line in
\pi_{2}
which is distinct from
\ell_{2}
and
k
, but contains
P_{2}
. We note that all of these points, lines, and planes are contained in
\kappa^{-1}(\mathcal{H})
.

We claim that
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
belong to the same spread of the parallelism defined by
c_{f^{\prime}}\kappa=\sigma c_{f}
but to distinct spreads of
\Pi_{f}
, which is a contradiction. Let
x,y,z,w\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
, such that
\ell_{1}=\langle(x,0),(y,0)\rangle
,
\ell_{2}=\langle(z,0),(w,1)\rangle
and
k=\langle(w,1),(x,0)\rangle
. Thus,
m_{1}=\langle(x,0),(w+y,1)\rangle
and
m_{2}=\langle(w,1),(z+x,0)\rangle
. Since
\sigma c_{f}
is bilinear on
\kappa^{-1}(\mathcal{H})
, we have

\displaystyle\sigma c_{f}(m_{1})

\displaystyle=\sigma c_{f}((x,0),(w+y,1))

\displaystyle=\sigma c_{f}((x,0),(w,1))+\sigma c_{f}((x,0),(y,0))

\displaystyle=\sigma c_{f}(k)+\sigma c_{f}(\ell_{1}).

Likewise, we have

\displaystyle\sigma c_{f}(m_{2})

\displaystyle=\sigma c_{f}((w,1),(z+x,0))

\displaystyle=\sigma c_{f}((w,1),(x,0))+\sigma c_{f}((w,1),(z,0))

\displaystyle=\sigma c_{f}(k)+\sigma c_{f}(\ell_{2}).

We assumed that
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
come from the same spread of
\Pi_{f}
, this yields
\sigma c_{f}(\ell_{1})=\sigma c_{f}(\ell_{2})
which implies
\sigma c_{f}(m_{1})=\sigma c_{f}(m_{2})
. Hence
c_{f}(m_{1})=c_{f}(m_{2})
, which yields that
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
are in the same spread in the parallelism
\Pi_{f}
.

Now, let us run these computations again on
c_{f}
, demonstrating that
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
cannot belong to the same spread of
\Pi_{f}
:

\displaystyle c_{f}(m_{1})

\displaystyle=c_{f}((x,0),(w+y,1))

\displaystyle=B_{f}(x,w+y)+f(x)

\displaystyle=B_{f}(x,w)+f(x)+B_{f}(x,y)

\displaystyle=c_{f}((x,0),(y,0))+c_{f}((x,0),(w,1))

\displaystyle=c_{f}(k)+c_{f}(\ell_{1}).

On the other hand

\displaystyle c_{f}(m_{2})

\displaystyle=c_{f}((w,1),(z+x,0))

\displaystyle=B_{f}(w,x+z)+f(z+x)

\displaystyle=B_{f}(w,x)+B_{f}(w,z)+f(x+z)

\displaystyle=f(w)+f(x)+f(w+x)+f(w)+f(z)+f(w+z)+f(x+z)

\displaystyle=c_{f}((x,0),(w,1))+f(x)+c_{f}((z,0),(w,1))+f(z)+f(x+z)

\displaystyle=c_{f}(k)+c_{f}(\ell_{2})+f(x)+f(z)+f(x+z).

If
c_{f}(m_{1})=c_{f}(m_{2})
, then it must be that
f(x)+f(z)+f(x+z)=0
and so
x=0
,
z=0
or
x=z
. All of which are contradictions, since
x,z\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{*}
and
\ell_{2}\neq k
. Thus it must be that
\kappa(\mathcal{H})=\mathcal{H}
.
∎

At the beginning of this section we stated that we can understand a collineation
\kappa
via
\kappa((x,x_{1}))=(A(x)+\alpha x_{1},B(x)+\beta x_{1})
, where
A
and
B
are linear. Now let
f
,
f^{\prime}
,
\sigma
and
\kappa
be as in Lemma
5.4
.
As
\kappa(\mathcal{H})=\mathcal{H}
, there exists a linear function
A
on
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
such that
\kappa((x,0))=(Ax,0)
for all
x\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{*}
. We want to deduce from this that if
\sigma c_{f}=c_{f^{\prime}}\kappa
, then
\sigma
is linear. Before we manage to do so we need the following technical lemma.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
f
be a quadratic crooked function and let
\Pi_{f}
be the associated parallelism.
For any two spreads
S_{1},S_{2}\in\Pi_{f}
there are intersecting lines
\ell_{1}\in S_{1}
and
\ell_{2}\in S_{2}
with
\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\subset\mathcal{H}
.

Proof.

In Lemma
5.2
we have already shown that for
\overline{x}\in\mathcal{H}
the image
c_{f}(\overline{x},.)(\mathcal{H})
is an
(n-1)
-dimensional subspace
\mathcal{H}_{x}
of
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
that is unique to
x
.

There are precisely
{\begin{bmatrix}n\\
1\end{bmatrix}_{2}}
points in
\mathcal{H}
, and so there are
{\begin{bmatrix}n\\
1\end{bmatrix}_{2}}
many distinct
(n-1)
-dimensional subspaces
\mathcal{H}_{x}
of
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
to consider.
However, the number of
(n-1)
-dimensional subspaces of
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
is exactly
{\begin{bmatrix}n\\
1\end{bmatrix}_{2}}
. So, we can interpret every
(n-1)
-dimensional subspaces of
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
as the image of
c_{f}(\overline{x},.)
for some
\overline{x}\in\mathcal{H}
.
In particular if one picks two elements of
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{*}
, they are contained in some
(n-1)
-dimensional space
T
of
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
and we can find a point
\overline{x}\in\mathcal{H}
such that the image of
c_{f}(\overline{x},.)
is
T
.
∎

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
f
and
f^{\prime}
be quadratic crooked functions over
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
with
n>3

and let
\sigma
be a permutation of
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{*}
with
\sigma(0)=0
. Furthermore, let
\kappa
be a collineation of
{\rm PG}(n,2)
such that

\sigma c_{f}=c_{f^{\prime}}\kappa
. Then
\sigma
is linear.

Proof.

Let
u,v\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{*}
. In view of Lemma
5.5
there exist
x,y,z\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{*}
such that
B_{f}(x,y)=c_{f}((x,0),(y,0))=u
and
B_{f}(z,y)=c_{f}((z,0),(y,0))=v
.
Furthermore, we have seen that there is a linear operator
A
such that
\kappa((x,0))=(Ax,0)
.
We have

\displaystyle\sigma(u+v)

\displaystyle=\sigma(B_{f}(x,y)+B_{f}(z,y))

\displaystyle=\sigma(B_{f}(x+z,y))

\displaystyle=\sigma(c_{f}((x+z,0),(y,0)))

\displaystyle=c_{f^{\prime}}((A(x+z),0),(Ay,0))

\displaystyle=B_{f^{\prime}}(A(x+z),Ay)

\displaystyle=B_{f^{\prime}}(Ax,Ay)+B_{f^{\prime}}(Az,Ay)

\displaystyle=c_{f^{\prime}}((Ax,0),(Ay,0))+c_{f^{\prime}}((Az,0),(Ay,0))

\displaystyle=\sigma c_{f}((x,0),(y,0))+\sigma c_{f}((z,0),(y,0))

\displaystyle=\sigma(u)+\sigma(v).\qed

We are now in a position to complete the proof of part 2 of Theorem
1.8
.

Proposition 5.7
.

Let
f
and
f^{\prime}
be quadratic crooked functions over
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
with
n>3
. It holds that
\Pi_{f}
and
\Pi_{f}^{\prime}
are equivalent if and only if there exist linear permutations
\sigma
and
A
of
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
such that
f^{\prime}(x)=\sigma f(Ax+\alpha)+\sigma(f(\alpha))
.

Proof.

Suppose that
\Pi_{f}
and
\Pi_{f^{\prime}}
are equivalent. Lemmas
5.4
and
5.6
imply that there exist a linear permutation
\sigma
and a collineation
\kappa
of
{\rm PG}(n,2)
which fixes
\mathcal{H}
such that
\sigma c_{f^{\prime}}=c_{f}\kappa
.
Let
A\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n\times n}
, as well as
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, and
B\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{1\times n}
, and
\beta\in\mathbb{F}_{2}
, such that we can understand
\kappa
via the
(n+1)\times(n+1)
matrix

\begin{pmatrix}A&\alpha\\
B&\beta\\
\end{pmatrix}.

Recall also that
\kappa((x,x_{1}))
is understood via

\kappa((x,x_{1}))=(Ax+\alpha x_{1},Bx+\beta x_{1})
. As
\kappa(\mathcal{H})=\mathcal{H}
we have
\kappa((x,x_{1}))=(Ax+\alpha x_{1},x_{1})
.

Observe that
\sigma c_{f^{\prime}}((x,1),(0,1))=\sigma f^{\prime}(x)
and
c_{f}\kappa((x,1),(0,1))=f(Ax+\alpha)+f(\alpha)
. Thus,

f^{\prime}(x)=\sigma^{-1}f(Ax+\alpha)+\sigma^{-1}f(\alpha).

Now suppose there exist
\sigma,A
linear permutations of
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
and an element
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
such that
f^{\prime}(x)=\sigma(f(Ax+\alpha))+\sigma(f(\alpha))
. Let
\kappa(x,x_{1})=(Ax+\alpha x_{1},x_{1})
be understood as a collineation of
{\rm PG}(n,2)
. We will show that it follows that
\sigma^{-1}c_{f^{\prime}}((x,x_{1}),(y,y_{1}))=c_{f}\kappa((x,x_{1}),(y,y_{1}))
, thus implying that the parallelisms
\Pi_{f^{\prime}}
and
\Pi_{f}
are equivalent. To do this, we consider two cases, either
(x_{1},y_{1})\in\{(0,1),(1,0),(1,1)\}
or
(x_{1},y_{1})=(0,0)
.

Let
(x_{1},y_{1})\in\{(0,1),(1,0),(1,1)\}
. Since it must be the case that exactly two of the terms
x_{1},y_{1},x_{1}+y_{1}
are equal to 1, we demonstrate that for
(x_{1},y_{1})=(0,1)
, we have
\sigma c{f^{\prime}}((x,0),(y,1))=c_{f}(\kappa(x,0),\kappa(y,1))
. The computations for the other two cases are identical.

\displaystyle\sigma^{-1}c_{f^{\prime}}((x,0),(y,1))

\displaystyle=\sigma^{-1}f^{\prime}(y)+\sigma^{-1}f^{\prime}(x+y)

\displaystyle=f(Ay+\alpha)+f(\alpha)+f(A(x+y)+\alpha)+f(\alpha)

\displaystyle=f(Ay+\alpha)+f(Ax+Ay+\alpha)

\displaystyle=c_{f}((Ax,0),(Ay+\alpha,1))

\displaystyle=c_{f}(\kappa(x,0),\kappa(y,1)).

Now we consider the case
(x_{1},y_{1})=(0,0)
. First, we observe that

\displaystyle\sigma^{-1}f^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=f(Ax+\alpha)+f(\alpha)

\displaystyle=f(Ax+\alpha)+f(\alpha)+f(Ax)+f(Ax)

\displaystyle=f(Ax)+c_{f}((Ax,0),(\alpha,0)).

Furthermore recall that for a quadratic function
f
we have that
c_{f}((Ax,0),(\alpha,0))
is the bilinear function
B_{f}(Ax,\alpha)
. Thus

\displaystyle\sigma^{-1}c_{f^{\prime}}((x,0),(y,0))

\displaystyle=\sigma^{-1}f(x)+\sigma^{-1}f(y)+\sigma^{-1}f(x+y)

\displaystyle=f(Ax)+B_{f}(Ax,\alpha)+f(Ay)+B_{f}(Ay,\alpha)+f(A(x+y))+B_{f}(A(x+y),\alpha)

\displaystyle=c_{f}((Ax,0),(Ay,0))+B_{f}(Ax,\alpha)+B_{f}(Ay,\alpha)+B_{f}(Ax+Ay,\alpha).

\displaystyle=c_{f}((Ax,0),(Ay,0))+B_{f}(Ax+Ay,\alpha)+B_{f}(Ax+Ay,\alpha).

\displaystyle=c_{f}((Ax,0),(Ay,0))

\displaystyle=c_{f}(\kappa(x,0),\kappa(y,0)).\qed

6
Concluding Remarks

In
[
17
]
, it was shown that a crooked function can be characterized by the Preparata-like code it defines as well as by the distance-regular graph it produces
[
5
,
11
]
. We remark that this characterization does not seem to immediately extend to our definition of parallelisms via the coloring function
c_{f}
. In particular, going through the proof of Theorem
1.7
, we see that case 4 follows from a specialization of the definition of crookedness, and does not require the full strength of the definition of crookedness.

In particular, it follows from our arguments in Section
4
that
f
(over
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
) defines a parallelism of
{\rm PG}(n,2)
via the coloring function
c_{f}
if and only if
f
is an APN permutation which satisfies
f(x)\neq f(x+y)+f(y)+f(x+z)+f(z)
for all
x,y,z\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
, and
x\neq 0
.

Let
H_{x}=\{D_{x}(y):y\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}\}
be the set of all derivatives in the direction of
x
. The following statements can be shown to be equivalent:

1.

c_{f}((x,x_{1}),(y,y_{1}))=f(x+y)+f(x)+f(y)+f(x_{1}y+y_{1}x)
defines a parallelism.

2.

f
is an APN permutation which satisfies
f(x)\not\in H_{x}+H_{x}
for all
x\neq 0
.

3.

Let
F_{x,a}=\{a,f(x)+a\}
(a coset of
\{0,f(x)\}
of the additive group of
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
). Then
|F_{x,a}\cap H_{x}|=1
for all
x,a\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
.

Since any quadratic APN permutation is crooked, we performed a search over known non-quadratic APN permutations. We did not find any functions which satisfy these more relaxed conditions on
f
. It is possible that the relaxed conditions in fact imply crookedness, but this does not seem to follow immediately.

Finally, we note that that it may be possible to construct multiple inequivalent line-parallelisms starting with one quadratic function
f
.
If
f^{\prime}(x)=f(x)+L(x)
where
L
is a linear function such that
f^{\prime}
is EA-equivalent to
f
but not affine equivalent, then Theorem
1.8
implies that the corresponding parallelism
\Pi_{f^{\prime}}
is inequivalent to
\Pi_{f}
.
For crooked functions however, this seems to be a nontrivial matter. A more detailed discussion about this can be found in
[
42
, Section 3]
, where such a function
L
is called a
