Innovations and Challenges in Syphilis Screening and Prevention Among Men Who Have Sex With Men: A Scoping Review
Authors: Mariam Assi, Kimberly A. Workowski, Leandro A. Mena
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Syphilis rates among men who have sex with men have risen sharply worldwide, reflecting evolving sexual networks, persistent gaps in screening and care, and behavioral changes that have accompanied the broader uptake of HIV preexposure prophylaxis. At the same time, a new generation of biomedical tools, such as doxycycline postexposure prophylaxis and rapid point-of-care diagnostics, are reshaping prevention. However, implementation has been inconsistent across programs and jurisdictions, and important challenges remain in access, effectiveness, and sustainability. This narrative review synthesizes current evidence on innovative syphilis screening and prevention strategies among men who have sex with men, highlighting both promising interventions and critical limitations. We emphasize doxycycline postexposure prophylaxis, point-of-care testing, self-testing, and digital health innovations for their potential to extend reach to underserved populations and address structural barriers to care and argue that durable control of the epidemic will require equity-focused, multidisciplinary strategies integrating clinical, public health, and community-based efforts.

Syphilis, caused by the spirochete Treponema pallidum, remains a major global public health concern despite the availability of diagnostics and curative treatment. Following periods of decline in many regions, syphilis has reemerged over the past decade, with sustained transmission reported across diverse geographic settings.1 The World Health Organization has highlighted the disproportionate burden among key populations, particularly in urban contexts where sexual networks intersect with ongoing HIV epidemics.1

Men who have sex with men (MSM) account for a substantial proportion of new syphilis cases in many countries.2,3 This burden reflects a complex interplay of changes in sexual network connectivity, persistent structural inequities, and gaps in screening, linkage to care, and treatment completion. While important advances have been made in HIV prevention, particularly with the expansion of pre-exposure prophylaxis (HIV PrEP), expansion of digital health tools, and the emergence of biomedical Sexually Transmitted Infection (STI) prevention strategies such as doxycycline postexposure prophylaxis (doxyPEP), have reshaped both opportunities and challenges for syphilis control. The optimal use of these tools and their implications for syphilis prevention are poorly understood and, in some settings, have coincided with rising STI incidence.4

In this context, there is a critical need to reassess current approaches to syphilis prevention. This narrative review synthesized evidence presented at an international syphilis conference alongside recent literature to examine innovations in screening, testing, and prevention strategies among MSM. We focus on 3 intersecting domains: (1) evolving epidemiologic and network dynamics, (2) advances and limitations in diagnostic and screening approaches, and (3) effectiveness and implementation challenges of prevention strategies, including emerging biomedical tools. We aim to identify what is effective, where important gaps remain, and how prevention efforts can be strengthened to achieve more equitable and sustained reductions in syphilis incidence.

Importantly, we situate these developments within a broader equity and syndemic framework, recognizing that syphilis transmission is shaped not only by individual behaviors but also by structural determinants such as racism, stigma, healthcare access, and policy. Durable control of syphilis will require integrated strategies that align clinical innovation and public health infrastructure and community-based implementation, with particular attention to populations disproportionally affected by the current epidemic.

EPIDEMIOLOGY OF SYPHILIS IN MSM

The epidemiology of syphilis among MSM reflects a complex interplay of behavioral, structural, and biological factors. In the United States, more than 186,301 syphilis cases were reported in 2024, continuing a decade-long upward trend.2 MSM bear a disproportionate burden of disease, accounting for over half of primary and secondary syphilis diagnoses among men for whom the sex of their sex partners was reported.2 Similar patterns are observed internationally. European surveillance data report sustained increases in syphilis notifications across European Union/European Economic Area countries, with most cases occurring among MSM.3

This burden is particularly concentrated in urban centers, where dense sexual networks and overlapping partnerships facilitate rapid STI transmission.5 In cities such as San Francisco and Atlanta, high rates of HIV coinfection and concentrated sexual networks contribute to sustained transmission of syphilis and other bacterial STIs.6 The increasing use of geosocial networking applications (e.g., Grindr, Jack’d) has further reshaped these network dynamics by enabling rapid partner acquisition and increasing connectivity within sexual networks. A systematic review and meta-analysis found that MSM who use geosocial networking smartphone applications report higher numbers of sexual partners and greater odds of bacterial STI diagnoses compared with nonusers.7

The expansion of HIV PrEP has been associated with changing STI epidemiology among MSM, including rising syphilis rates in some settings. In an interrupted time-series analysis of US national data, HIV PrEP implementation has coincided with rising syphilis rates in some settings; however, these trends likely reflect a combination of increased screening, underlying network dynamics, and secular trends rather than a direct causal effect.4 In Australia, surveillance following nationwide HIV PrEP implementation showed that among 22,730 gay and bisexual men using HIV PrEP, syphilis incidence increased from 6.2 to 9.8 per 100 person-years between 2016 and 2019, whereas chlamydia and gonorrhea incidence declined.8 Behavioral changes after HIV PrEP initiation may contribute to these patterns in some cohorts. In a multicity US study, 37.9% of HIV PrEP users reported stopping or decreasing condom use, and 31.6% reported increased partner numbers.9 However, multivariable analyses suggest that condom use alone does not fully explain STI risk among HIV PrEP users. In a large prospective HIV PrEP cohort, condom use was not independently associated with STI acquisition, whereas younger age, higher partner numbers, and participation in group sex were significant predictors of infection.10 These findings suggest that broader sexual network dynamics, rather than condom use alone, may drive STI transmission in HIV PrEP-era MSM populations.

Several studies highlight the role of high-density sexual networks characterized by multiple partners, group sex, and recreational drug use (chemsex). A 2025 meta-analysis of 13 studies found that chemsex was associated with increased odds of syphilis infection (pooled odds ratio [OR], 1.74; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.34–2.27), largely mediated by practices such as condomless sex, multiple partners, and prolonged sexual encounters.11 Multipartner chemsex appears particularly important; UK data demonstrated that multipartner chemsex was associated with significantly higher odds of syphilis among MSM living with HIV [adjusted odds ratio (aOR) 2.6], HIV PrEP users (aOR 1.9), and other MSM (aOR 4.0).12 Consistent with these findings, repeat syphilis infections among MSM are commonly associated with HIV infection, substance use, particularly methamphetamine, and multiple sexual partners.13 Within HIV PrEP cohorts, STI diagnoses also tend to be highly concentrated, with 1 study finding that 25% of participants accounted for 76% of all STI diagnoses.10

Demographic inequities further shape these epidemiologic patterns. Black MSM experience disproportionately high rates of syphilis compared with White MSM.14 US estimates of primary and secondary syphilis among MSM in 2014 found rates of 583.9 per 100,000 among Black MSM compared with 168.4 per 100,000 among White MSM, a disparity observed across all US regions.14 Earlier surveillance data also showed that between 2005 and 2008, absolute increases in syphilis rates among Black MSM were 8.0 times greater than those among White MSM, while increases among Hispanic MSM were 2.4 times greater.15 These disparities are not explained by higher risk behavior, suggesting that structural factors, including racism, homophobia, economic marginalization, reduced access to preventive services, and assortative sexual mixing within higher-prevalence networks, play a central role in driving inequities.16,17 Hispanic MSM similarly face elevated risks shaped by barriers such as limited English proficiency, lack of health insurance, immigration-related concerns, and intersectional stigma related to being an immigrant, Latino, and a sexual minority, which may delay testing and treatment.18

Sexual diversity within MSM populations adds further complexity to these epidemiologic patterns. Men who have sex with men and women (MSMW) represent a substantial subset of the MSM population, comprising an estimated 35% of MSM in the United States and 32.8% of MSM in Asian samples, with bisexual behavior appearing particularly common among younger individuals; in 1 study of MSM aged 12–24 years, 53% were classified as MSMW.19–21 Network analyses support considerable connectivity between MSM and female sex partners that could facilitate syphilis transmission. A Baltimore network analysis found that 14.5% of MSM reported direct linkage to female partners (MSMW) and an additional 22.3% had indirect linkage through male partners who also had female partners, demonstrating substantial potential for network bridging between MSM and heterosexual populations and highlighting pathways through which syphilis transmission could extend beyond MSM networks.22 These overlapping sexual networks can facilitate transmission between MSM and heterosexual populations, highlighting the limitations of surveillance systems that categorize infections strictly by behavioral groups. Molecular epidemiologic studies support this interconnected view of transmission. Phylogenetic analyses of HIV molecular surveillance data have demonstrated that although most transmissions occur within MSM networks, a measurable proportion of transmission links connect MSM with heterosexual women, indicating potential bridging across sexual networks.23 Together, behavioral, network, and molecular evidence suggest that transmission dynamics cannot be fully understood within rigid risk-category boundaries, underscoring the need for prevention strategies that address overlapping sexual networks.

Together, these epidemiologic patterns highlight how syphilis transmission is shaped by sexual network dynamics, structural inequities, and healthcare access barriers, underscoring the need for prevention strategies that address both biological and social drivers of infection.

SYPHILIS SCREENING, TESTING GAPS, AND CARE CASCADE

Current guidelines recommend that MSM be screened for syphilis at least annually, with more frequent screening (every 3–6 months) for individuals at higher risk, including those with HIV infection, MSM using HIV PrEP or doxyPEP, multiple sexual partners, or a history of STIs.13 The CDC, the US Preventive Services Task Force, and the HIV Medicine Association all endorse this risk-stratified approach.13,24 For persons with HIV who are sexually active, guidelines recommend syphilis screening at least annually and every 3–6 months for those at ongoing risk.25 However, the US Preventive Services Task Force acknowledges that the optimal screening interval for individuals at increased risk has not been definitively established.24

Mathematical modeling studies suggest that more intensive screening could substantially reduce syphilis transmission in MSM populations. Increasing screening frequency from twice yearly to quarterly could reduce syphilis prevalence by up to 84% from peak levels, while the population screening coverage required for elimination decreases from approximately 62% with annual screening to 23% with quarterly screening.26,27 These findings highlight the potential population-level benefits of intensified screening strategies in high-incidence networks.

Despite these recommendations, STI screening among MSM remains suboptimal. In the 2017 American Men’s Internet Survey of over 10,000 US MSM, only 42% reported any STI test in the prior 12 months, and just 16% reported an extragenital test, with lower testing among men reporting fewer recent partners despite comparable or higher risk.28 Screening gaps also persist among MSM living with HIV and engaged in care: studies of HIV clinics have documented annual extragenital gonorrhea and chlamydia testing rates of roughly 2% to 32%, even as syphilis screening reaches 66% to 76%.29,30 Surveillance data reveal that among MSM with incident syphilis (primary or secondary), only about 35% are identified in the primary stage, indicating that most primary cases are missed and untreated, allowing continued transmission during the highly infectious early period.31 Multiple factors contribute to these gaps: the anticipation of experiencing homophobic discrimination in healthcare settings may lead many sexually active MSM to delay needed screening and treatment, thus remaining infectious to partners for longer periods than other populations.17 Qualitative studies reveal that confidentiality and privacy are the most important factors influencing MSM decisions about whether and where to get tested, with young men and Black and ethnic minority men particularly sensitive to concerns about exposure in their communities.32,33

Substantial attrition also occurs along the syphilis care cascade after screening. In a study of MSM and transgender women in Lima, Peru, 90.4% of individuals with positive syphilis screening tests saw a physician within 28 days, but among those asked to return for confirmatory results, 62.5% did not return within 28 days. Among individuals prescribed 3 weekly doses of benzathine penicillin for treatment, only 54% completed all doses on schedule.34 A systematic review of barriers to treatment completion identified healthcare system-related factors, including limited supply of and access to medication, inadequate follow-up systems, and social and economic factors such as transportation barriers.35 Supply-side constraints have added a further layer of structural risk. Benzathine penicillin G (BPG) is the only recommended treatment for syphilis in pregnancy and first-line for all stages, yet its global supply is fragile: at least 39 countries reported stock-outs between 2014 and 2016, and only 3 Chinese manufacturers of the active pharmaceutical ingredient remain in the global market.36,37 In the United States, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) declared a Bicillin L-A shortage in April 2023, driven by rising syphilis incidence and single-source manufacturing; the shortage has since been repeatedly extended, and in 2026, the FDA authorized temporary importation of Lentocilin while CDC urged jurisdictions to preserve BPG for pregnant patients and substitute doxycycline for nonpregnant adults with early or late latent disease.38,39 These constraints are particularly consequential for MSM, who bear most early syphilis cases and whose networks depend on timely single-dose treatment to interrupt transmission—a reminder that screening and prevention innovations rest on a treatment supply chain that itself remains vulnerable.

INNOVATIONS IN SYPHILIS SCREENING

Several innovations in syphilis screening have emerged to address the limitations of traditional laboratory-based testing and improve access among high-risk populations, including MSM.

POINT-OF-CARE TESTING

Rapid point-of-care tests (POCTs) represent a significant advance, requiring only a fingerstick blood sample and providing results in 15 to 30 minutes, enabling same-day treatment.40 Systematic reviews of commercially available rapid treponemal tests demonstrate sensitivities of 74% to 90% and specificities of 94% to 99% compared with laboratory reference assays.41 Dual HIV-syphilis POCTs have been evaluated extensively among MSM populations: a multicenter study across Italy, Malta, Peru, and the United Kingdom found pooled sensitivities of 73% to 79% and specificities >99% for syphilis, with both tests detecting >90% of probable active syphilis cases [defined by reactive rapid plasma reagin (RPR) and treponemal test results]42; 75% of participants preferred dual POCTs due to convenience.42 A Canadian study of 2 extremely rapid (<5 minutes) dual syphilis/HIV POCTs demonstrated excellent performance for active syphilis (RPR ≥1:8), with sensitivities of 97% to 98% and specificities >99%, and 85% of participants with infectious syphilis were treated on the same day as the positive POCT result.43 However, sensitivity is substantially lower for samples with non-reactive RPR (28%–54%), limiting utility for detecting previously treated or very early infection.43 A central limitation of treponemal-only POCTs is that they cannot distinguish active, prior, and treated infection, and it remains unclear how their results alone—particularly in individuals with previous syphilis—should guide treatment decisions without additional confirmatory testing.40 This issue is especially salient in MSM, among whom reactive treponemal tests are common due to a high burden of prior infection, with seropositivity generally highest in MSM living with HIV and MSM using PrEP44 Treponemal-only POCTs nonetheless retain important clinical utility. In settings where patients are unlikely to return for results, they enable same-day diagnosis and treatment within a test-and-treat model, reducing delays in care and shortening the infectious period45–48. A recent modeling study comparing rapid testing algorithms found that while treponemal-only rapid diagnostic testss remain suitable for antenatal screening, dual treponemal/nontreponemal algorithms may be preferable for MSM, who carry a higher burden of prior infection49.

Pairing POCT use with clinical assessment and, where feasible, confirmatory nontreponemal testing can help balance the benefits of same-day treatment against the risk of overtreatment in this population.

A rapid POCT that detects both treponemal and nontreponemal antibodies has been evaluated and found to be sensitive and specific, though the sensitivity of the nontreponemal component is lower in samples with low (<1:8) RPR titers.50 A recent study of a dual treponemal/nontreponemal rapid test in people living with HIV found that it did not meet WHO performance targets, with the nontreponemal component showing only 46.5% specificity—highlighting the need for improved diagnostic tools and rigorous validation.51

SYPHILIS SELF-TESTING

Self-testing has emerged as a promising strategy to expand access to screening among MSM by reducing stigma, increasing privacy, and overcoming barriers related to clinic-based care and provider access. A systematic review found that 88% of individuals who received self-test kits completed testing, with high user acceptability due to convenience, privacy, rapid results, and autonomy.52 A randomized controlled trial in China demonstrated that providing syphilis self-testing significantly increased testing uptake compared with standard of care (63%–66% vs 14.7%), with the majority (78.5%) of self-testers reporting never having tested for syphilis previously.53 The cost per person tested was substantially lower with self-testing (US$26.55) compared with standard of care (US$66.19).53 Peer distribution models have also shown promise: a multicity randomized clinical trial (RCT) found that peer distribution of self-tests increased syphilis testing uptake among MSM compared with facility-based testing, with costs per person tested reduced from $760.60 (standard of care) to $83.78 to $93.10 (self-testing arms).54 The recent approval of over-the-counter syphilis treponemal self-tests represents an important advance for expanding diagnostic access, though implementation challenges remain, including ensuring equitable access, supporting vulnerable groups, and securing linkage to care.55

NUCLEIC ACID AMPLIFICATION TESTS

Although there is no FDA-cleared nucleic acid amplification tests (NAATs) for syphilis, some reference and commercial laboratories offer laboratory-developed assays capable of detecting Treponema pallidum DNA. These assays perform best in primary syphilis, with reported sensitivities generally ranging from approximately 70% to 90%, and specificities typically exceeding 95%.56,57 NAATs are particularly valuable for diagnosing early syphilis when serologic tests may be falsely negative, as antibodies to T. pallidum are not detectable until 10 to 15 days after chancre appearance.40 A study combining antibody and NAAT testing in MSM demonstrated increased sensitivity of syphilis screening.58 T. pallidum DNA can be detected in oropharyngeal swabs (26.8%) and whole blood (26.7%) of untreated individuals with early syphilis, with combined testing identifying 40.8% of cases—suggesting NAATs may be particularly useful for diagnosis of very early or asymptomatic syphilis.59 However, NAATs require technical expertise and laboratory equipment, and have not been thoroughly clinically validated.40

DIGITAL HEALTH INNOVATIONS

Digital health interventions are being piloted to promote testing uptake among MSM. Social network strategies and mobile technologies have been used to recruit participants from MSM-oriented social apps for syphilis testing programs.53 Smartphone applications have been developed to facilitate self- and partner-testing: in 1 study, 77% of MSM participants self-tested using a dual HIV/syphilis kit (mean 3.7 times over 3 months), and 54% tested partners, with high acceptability for both the test and accompanying app.60 The COVID-19 pandemic accelerated interest in home testing paired with telemedicine or online ordering of test kits.61 However, current self-tests rely on treponemal antibody testing, which may be falsely negative in early infection or remain positive after treatment; the development of nontreponemal tests suitable for self-testing remains a research gap of high interest.41

Strategies with Evidence of Effectiveness

DoxyPEP is the most significant recent advance. Three RCTs—the French IPERGAY substudy, the US DoxyPEP trial, and the DOXYVAC trial—demonstrated 77% to 88% reductions in syphilis incidence among MSM taking 200 mg doxycycline within 72 hours of condomless sex.62–64 Final doxyPEP results confirmed sustained efficacy: quarterly STI incidence was 12% vs 30.5% with standard care [risk ratio (RR), 0.39; P < 0.0001].65 Real-world data from San Francisco showed a 51% population-level decrease in early syphilis among MSM following guideline release, and a Kaiser Permanente study of MSM on PrEP showed syphilis positivity dropped from ~2% to <0.5% after doxyPEP initiation.66,67 The CDC recommends offering doxyPEP to MSM with a bacterial STI in the past 12 months.68 These efficacy gains, however, come with legitimate concerns about selection for antimicrobial resistance, both in target STIs and in off-target pathobionts; an in silico analysis identified 19 pathobionts in which doxyPEP could theoretically select for resistance to tetracyclines and other antimicrobial classes,69 and a modeling study of MSM in the United States projected that widespread doxyPEP could meaningfully increase the prevalence of tetracycline-resistant Neisseria gonorrhoeae over the coming decade.70 These findings underscore the need for robust surveillance of antimicrobial resistance in gonorrhea, commensal Neisseria, and other exposed organisms, as doxyPEP uptake expands.

Beyond chemoprophylaxis, partner-based interventions remain central pillars of syphilis control. Presumptive treatment of contacts exposed within 90 days can treat approximately one-third who become secondarily infected, preventing disease progression and onward transmission.71 However, the effectiveness of partner notification is constrained in MSM sexual networks characterized by multiple and often anonymous partners, where contacts may be difficult to identify or reach and exposures frequently occur through digital platforms that do not readily support follow-up. These limitations argue for complementary strategies, including network-informed interventions, digital and app-based notification tools, and intensified screening in high-incidence populations, to extend the reach of case-finding beyond named partners alone.

Clinic-based interventions targeting MSM show moderate effectiveness. In an HIV-positive MSM cohort, EMR alerts doubled STI detection (syphilis incidence: 3.5 → 9.8/100 person-years).72 SMS reminders increased syphilis testing among MSM (67% vs 39%, P < 0.001) and early latent syphilis detection (1.7% vs 0.4%).73 A meta-analysis found text messaging increased STI/HIV testing (OR, 1.83).74 Among MSM, 90% preferred SMS as the reminder method, with neutrally framed or personalized messages preferred.75

Social marketing campaigns targeting MSM show short-term benefits. The Drama Downunder campaign among Australian gay men found awareness was associated with STI testing (prevalence ratio, 1.5), though long-term effects were modest—among HIV-positive MSM, syphilis testing actually declined postcampaign.76,77 The Syphistory campaign among Canadian gbMSM found campaign exposure was associated with recent syphilis testing (aOR, 2.73).78

Interventions with Evidence of Ineffectiveness

Mass antibiotic strategies for syphilis prevention have not been evaluated in MSM populations. Evidence from other populations suggests limited effectiveness; for example, a randomized trial of monthly azithromycin chemoprophylaxis among female sex workers found no reduction in syphilis incidence (RR, 1.02), despite decreases in gonorrhea and chlamydia.79 In addition, macrolide-resistant Treponema pallidum is now widespread globally, with A2058G and A2059G mutations in the 23S rRNA gene detected in 28% to 88% of samples across geographically diverse sites, limiting the feasibility of azithromycin-based approaches.80

There is no evidence supporting the use of expedited partner therapy for syphilis. Existing expedited partner therapy data are limited to chlamydia and gonorrhea in heterosexual populations and are not generalizable to syphilis or MSM.81–83 Evidence for partner notification strategies in syphilis is also limited, with few trials and no clear guidance on optimal approaches, highlighting a need for high-quality studies.84

Condom promotion campaigns have limited evidence for syphilis reduction among MSM specifically. A systematic review found all 12 epidemiologic studies had significant methodologic limitations.85 A Cochrane review found no clear effect on STI transmission (syphilis RR, 0.91) despite increased reported condom use.86

Outreach at sex-on-premises venues frequented by MSM achieves high yield (5% positivity, including 0.8% syphilis) but has limited reach; participation rates are only 10% to 24%.87,88 Bathhouse regulation has no evidence demonstrating a reduction in syphilis or other STI transmission in MSM.89

INTERVENTIONS WITH INSUFFICIENT EVIDENCE

Web-Based and Home Testing

Web-based STI testing shows promise among MSM. A UK RCT found e-STI testing nearly doubled uptake (50.0% vs 26.6%; RR, 1.87) and reduced time to test by 1 week.90 Implementation of online postal self-sampling in England was associated with 2.1- to 2.5-fold increases in testing activity, with high uptake among MSM (incidence rate ratio, 2.9–3.6).91 The Test Yourself Colorado program found 15.7% STI positivity among MSM (vs 6.9% overall), though Hispanic clients had lower test return rates (37.1% vs 45.9%), raising equity concerns.92 A UK study of 3-site online self-testing found 71.8% of chlamydia and 89.9% of gonorrhea infections were extragenital only, underscoring the importance of comprehensive testing in online programs.93

Financial Incentives

Financial incentives show context-dependent effectiveness. A Canadian outreach study found that incentivized visits ($10 gift cards) were associated with higher syphilis case-finding (3.8% vs 1.9%) and 100% treatment rates.94 In China, a “pay-it-forward” strategy increased gonorrhea/chlamydia test uptake to 56% vs 18% with standard care, with 95% of participants choosing to donate for future testing.95 Syphilis self-testing with lottery incentives achieved 65.7% uptake vs 14.7% with standard care among Chinese MSM.53

Vaccine Development

Syphilis vaccine development has advanced but remains preclinical. An mRNA-lipid nanoparticle vaccine encoding TP0954 was more effective than protein vaccines in attenuating lesion development in rabbit models.96 OMP-based vaccines, including a tri-antigen cocktail (TprC/TprK/Tp0751) and vaccines targeting TprC variants, Tp0136, and Tp0663, have shown partial protection in rabbits, attenuating chancres and inhibiting dissemination, but none have achieved complete protection.97,98 The optimal vaccine platform remains undefined, and immune correlates of protection are poorly understood.99

Conclusion

The ongoing syphilis epidemic among MSM reflects persistent gaps in prevention, diagnosis, and treatment in the context of highly connected sexual networks and structural barriers. Recent advances, including expanded screening strategies, digital health approaches, and doxyPEP, offer important opportunities to reduce transmission, but their impact will depend on how effectively they are implemented in real-world settings. DoxyPEP has demonstrated substantial efficacy in reducing syphilis among MSM in randomized trials, but questions remain regarding long-term use, antimicrobial resistance, and equitable access. As use expands, programs should incorporate monitoring for antimicrobial resistance and ensure that access is not limited to populations already engaged in care. At the same time, optimizing existing interventions remains essential. Increasing screening frequency to every 3 to 6 months for individuals at ongoing risk, implementing same-day testing and treatment where feasible, and strengthening linkage to care and treatment completion are immediate, actionable priorities. Digital and community-based strategies, including self-testing and peer distribution models, may further expand reach, particularly for individuals who face barriers to clinic-based care.

Finally, current trends highlight the need for stronger integration across STI and HIV prevention programs serving MSM. Aligning syphilis screening, treatment, and prevention strategies with existing HIV prevention platforms, including PrEP services, will be critical to improving early detection, reducing reinfection, and addressing transmission in interconnected sexual networks.

Sustained reductions in syphilis incidence among MSM will depend on scaling integrated HIV and STI prevention strategies that combine biomedical innovation, frequent screening, and network-informed approaches, with deliberate focus on equitable access for populations at highest risk.
