Complex and Recurrent Hiatal Hernia Repair Using a Pedicled Rectus Abdominis Flap: Surgical Technique and Case Series
Authors: Jenna Hannouille, Fadi Charara, Vincenzo Simonelli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Recurrent hiatal hernias represent a significant surgical challenge, with recurrence rates of 10.2%–25.5% after conventional repairs. Patients with complex recurrent hernias who have undergone multiple operations have limited therapeutic options due to technical complexity and altered anatomy. This study presents a novel autologous salvage technique using a superiorly pedicled rectus abdominis muscle (RAM) flap for complex recurrent hiatal hernia repair.Methods:Between August 2019 and May 2022, four patients with symptomatic recurrent hiatal hernias underwent RAM flap reconstruction after multiple failed conventional repairs. The superiorly pedicled RAM flap, based on the superior epigastric artery, was harvested laparoscopically or via laparotomy and inserted tension-free into the mediastinal cavity through the esophageal hiatus. Outcomes were assessed using clinical evaluation, radiological imaging (computed tomography scan and barium swallow), and Gastroesophageal Reflux Disease Health-Related Quality of Life questionnaires at medium- and long-term follow-up (>2 y).Results:All procedures were technically successful without major complications. The mean hospital stay was 6.5 days. At mean long-term follow-up of 46.5 months, no hiatal hernia recurrences were observed on imaging. All patients demonstrated significant symptom improvement with mean Gastroesophageal Reflux Disease Health-Related Quality of Life score improvement of −23 points at medium-term follow-up, with continued improvement long-term. Minor complications included abdominal bulging in two patients without functional impairment and one incisional hernia requiring revision.Conclusions:The superiorly pedicled RAM flap represents a promising autologous salvage technique for complex recurrent hiatal hernias in patients who have undergone multiple operations, offering durable reconstruction with excellent symptom relief and no recurrences at long-term follow-up. The technique can be performed laparoscopically in selected cases.

Takeaways

Question: How can multi-recurrent hiatial hernias be surgically managed when the conventional techniques cannot be applied anymore?

Findings: We describe a novel autologous surgical technique using a rectus abdominis muscular flap to cure hiatal hernia recurrence patients with multiple operations.

Meaning: The pedicled rectus abdominis muscular flap is a reliable surgical salvage procedure for hiatal recurrence in patients with multiple operations.

INTRODUCTION

When conventional surgical techniques fail repeatedly, what options remain for patients with recurrent hiatal hernias? Common procedures for hiatal hernia repair include hernia sac excision, cruroplasty with or without mesh reinforcement, fundoplication, and gastropexy. Despite surgery being considered the gold standard for treatment, recurrence rates after primary procedure remain high, ranging from 10.2% to 25.5%, depending on follow-up procedures and duration.1 The guidelines published in 2013 by the Society of American Gastrointestinal and Endoscopic Surgeons2 compiled available evidence and clarified clinical and surgical approaches to the treatment of hiatal hernias. However, approximately 3.6%–6% of patients require surgical reintervention after primary antireflux surgery,3,4 creating a substantial population of patients who have undergone multiple operations, with increasingly limited therapeutic options.

The fundamental challenge lies not merely in the high recurrence rates, but in the progressive technical difficulty and diminishing success of each subsequent operation. Reoperative procedures face multiple obstacles: altered anatomy from previous operations, scarring that precludes standard techniques, and surgical complexity progressively increasing with each surgery. For patients who have exhausted standard options, surgeons face a critical gap in available techniques, particularly when dealing with large hiatal defects where tensioned repairs and prosthetic materials have already failed.

Vertical rectus abdominis myocutaneous (VRAM) and transverse rectus abdominis myocutaneous flaps, well established in breast and pelvic reconstruction,5 offer unique advantages: abundant vascularized tissue, reliable blood supply, and sufficient bulk to fill dead space. In this context, the rectus abdominis muscle (RAM) flap is used to provide autologous, well-vascularized tissue to reinforce the hiatal repair, thereby avoiding the use of prosthetic mesh and tensioned closure of the hiatus. In 2020, the senior author published the use of a superiorly pedicled RAM flap for multi-recurrent hiatal hernia repair,6 but long-term outcomes and technical refinements, including the possibility of laparoscopic harvest, remained unexplored. This study presents a novel autologous salvage technique using the superiorly pedicled RAM flap for complex recurrent hiatal hernias, evaluating outcomes through retrospective analysis with long-term follow-up, and introduces the first description of totally laparoscopic RAM flap harvest for this indication, offering a minimally invasive option for selected patients.

PATIENTS AND METHODS

Study Design

This retrospective case series was conducted at a single hospital (CHU Tivoli, La Louvière, Belgium) between August 2019 and December 2024. The study was approved by the institutional ethics committee, and all patients provided written informed consent for the procedure and subsequent data analysis.

Study Cohort

Between August 2019 and May 2022, 5 consecutive patients underwent the RAM flap technique for treatment of multi-recurrent hiatal hernia; however, only 4 were included in the study, as 1 patient was unreachable. There were no exclusion criteria for this retrospective analysis—all patients who underwent the procedure were included after approval was obtained. Exclusion criteria for performing the procedure itself included general contraindications to surgery or general anesthesia, patient refusal, and unavailability of the donor site. The procedures were performed by two senior surgeons (V.S. and F.C.) at a single center (CHU Tivoli, La Louvière, Belgium). The procedure was offered only to patients who had undergone multiple operations with recurrent symptomatic hiatal hernias for whom conventional techniques had already been used and were unlikely to yield good outcomes if repeated or were technically unachievable. The surgical technique was thoroughly explained to each patient preoperatively.

Data Collection

Data were collected from four patients between August 2019 and December 2024 and recorded in an anonymized database. For this study, all patients were invited to complete the Gastroesophageal Reflux Disease Health-Related Quality of Life (GERD-HRQL) questionnaire,7 objectively assessing the impact of the procedure on their quality of life at medium- to long-term follow-up. (See figure, Supplemental Digital Content 1, which shows GERD-HRQL, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E951.) The questionnaire was completed at 3 time points: (1) preoperative assessment (retrospective response), (2) medium-term follow-up (12–24 mo), and (3) long-term follow-up (>24 mo).

GERD-HRQL Questionnaire

The GERD-HRQL questionnaire is a validated disease-specific instrument consisting of 15 questions assessing heartburn severity (6 items), dysphagia (2 items), regurgitation (6 item), and medication effect (1 item). Each item is scored from 0 (no symptoms) to 5 (incapacitating symptoms), with a maximum total score of 75 indicating worst possible symptoms and 0 indicating no symptoms. A change of 10 points or more is considered clinically significant. The questionnaire also includes a satisfaction scale ranging from “satisfied” to “dissatisfied” with current health condition.7

Preoperative Planning

All patients underwent comprehensive preoperative evaluation including upper gastrointestinal contrast study (barium swallow), computed tomography (CT) of the chest and abdomen, and upper endoscopy when clinically indicated.

Surgical Technique: Rectus Abdominis Harvest and Inset

The initial surgical approach varied based on individual surgical history. Common early steps included adhesiolysis, hernia reduction, and sac dissection. Depending on previous procedures, Nissen fundoplication takedown and gastrojejunal bypass recalibration were also performed.

The surgery was performed either via laparotomy or laparoscopy. For laparotomy, a xipho-pubic or xipho-umbilical midline incision was used. In laparoscopy, 3 ports were placed, as shown in Figure 1, and a 30-degree angle optic was used. Regardless of approach, the principles of left RAM flap harvest were the same.

Laparoscopic Harvest and Flap Inset

Laparoscopic harvest began with transverse opening of the peritoneum and posterior sheath approximately 2–3 cm above the pubis using an electrocoagulation hook to identify the left rectus. The inferior part of the left RAM was transected from the pubis using the LigaSure system (LigaSure 5-mm Blunt Tip laparoscopic sealer/divider with nanocoating; Covidien, Medtronic, Dublin, Ireland). The left epigastric artery was sealed distally during the procedure. The muscle was progressively released from the anterior sheath using a monopolar hook and LigaSure clamp. The posterior sheath was opened longitudinally as needed during harvest. The flap was dissected proximally to the costal edge, keeping the deep superior epigastric pedicle under direct vision and protected at all times. (See video [online], which shows the laparoscopic harvest of the superiorly pedicled left RAM flap.)

The left RAM flap, pedicled superiorly on the left superior epigastric artery, was then rotated posteriorly and inserted tension-free into the intrathoracic hernia cavity through the predissected esophageal hiatus (Fig. 2). Special care was taken to avoid pedicle torsion. The flap was fixed laterally to the diaphragmatic pillar using nonabsorbable running sutures (V-Loc 3-0, Medtronic, Dublin, Ireland). Suturing was performed cautiously to avoid injuring the pedicle and to ensure tension-free fixation, preserving flap vascularization. A Jackson-Pratt drain was placed in contact with the flap. The posterior sheath was left open as the anterior sheath remained intact, minimizing the risk of abdominal wall hernia.

All procedures were performed in collaboration between plastic and abdominal surgeons, with the laparoscopic case specifically requiring close coordination between both specialties for optimal port placement and flap harvest.

Postoperative Management

Postoperatively, oral intake was resumed with liquid food as early as tolerated, followed by a soft diet for 15 days. The drain was removed once output was less than 30 mL/d. Light mobilization began on the first postoperative day. The first clinical follow-up occurred 10–15 days postdischarge. Imaging follow-up, including CT scan and barium swallow, was performed approximately 6 months after surgery.

Surgery Characteristics

One procedure was performed electively for recurrent symptoms—namely, dysphagia, gastroesophageal reflux, and radiologically confirmed hiatal hernia recurrence on preoperative CT scan. The remaining three procedures were performed in emergency settings due to incarcerated hiatal hernias. The elective procedure was conducted laparoscopically, whereas the three emergency procedures were performed via laparotomy (2 were primary open laparotomies, and one was initially attempted laparoscopically but converted to open due to extensive adhesions and poor visualization).

Regarding the flap design, 3 cases involved a purely muscular flap (patients 1, 2, and 4), whereas patient 3 received a myocutaneous flap (including a deepithelialized skin paddle, as in the classical VRAM flap). In this latter case, the patient underwent emergency surgery for a large hiatal hernia involving the right colon, which resulted in a wide hiatal defect requiring a thicker flap for adequate filling. Initially, this patient underwent emergent hernia reduction and colectomy for an incarcerated colon without closure of the crura. A rapid recurrence occurred, and the RAM flap technique was performed 7 days later as a salvage procedure.

Follow-up Characteristics and Complications

The mean length of hospital stay was 6.5 days (SD: 1.9). Three patients resumed oral intake on postoperative day 1, whereas patient 3—who received the myocutaneous flap following colectomy—began refeeding on day 6. The mean duration of long-term follow-up was 46.5 months (SD: 14.38; range: 28.88–63.9 mo). Individual patient follow-up data are detailed in Tables 1 and 3.

In terms of quality of life (Table 3), all patients demonstrated improvement at mid-term follow-up, with a mean GERD-HRQL score improvement of −23 points (SD: 9.96; mean mid-term follow-up: 23.82 ± 8.93 mo). Even greater improvements in quality of life were noted at long-term follow-up (mean: 46.50 ± 14.38 mo), with patients expressing high levels of satisfaction. Patient 2 was lost to long-term follow-up due to death from complications of colonic cancer, unrelated to the hernia pathology or its treatment.

DISCUSSION

Various techniques using autologous tissue have been described for the management of recurrent diaphragmatic hernia, including the use of posterior rectus sheath flaps,8,9 round ligament (ligamentum teres) reinforcement of the hiatus,10,11 and falciform ligament flaps for hiatal closure,12,13 all with favorable short-term outcomes and without the risks associated with prosthetic mesh. However, in severe cases, the hiatal defect can be large, and these autologous approaches may lack sufficient bulk and robustness. Although mesh-based techniques have demonstrated significantly lower recurrence rates in primary surgery,14,15 their effectiveness in reoperative procedures remains controversial. Singhal et al, in one of the largest cohorts of patients undergoing primary and reoperative antireflux surgery, reported that complications and patient-reported outcomes tend to worsen with each successive reoperation.16 For reoperative procedures, their preferred approach is Roux-en-Y bypass reconstruction.16 However, this may not be feasible in more advanced cases, and some patients may remain symptomatic even after such intervention, as observed in our cohort of tertiary (and higher-order) cases. Furthermore, in one of the largest prospective cohort studies, Furné et al demonstrated that surgical reintervention after antireflux surgery using conventional techniques yielded good symptomatic and objective outcomes in approximately 70% of patients,3 underscoring the need for alternative approaches such as autologous tissue reconstruction.

In our series, no recurrences were observed after a mean follow-up of 46.5 months (range: 28.88–63.9 mo), demonstrating stable and durable results up to 5 years postoperatively—an important finding, as most recurrences with conventional techniques tend to occur within the first 2 years.14

Although the use of abdominal muscle flaps has been described for various diaphragmatic defects, including congenital and postneoplasia resection defects,17–19 to our knowledge, this specific application of the rectus abdominal flap for recurrent hiatal hernias represents an extension of the indication and a modification of the harvesting technique compared to these established diaphragmatic approaches. The RAM flap technique has shown promising mid- and long-term outcomes, with significant symptom relief and high patient satisfaction. It offers the advantages of autologous tissue reconstruction, avoiding prosthetic material and tensioned repair. The flexibility of the flap—laparoscopic or open harvest, muscle-only or myocutaneous—makes it a versatile and robust surgical salvage solution for complex recurrent hiatal hernias. It enables reconstruction of medium to large diaphragmatic defects in patients with multiple operations and may help prevent recurrence by filling the mediastinal cavity.

The left rectus muscle was specifically chosen due to its favorable anatomical position relative to the esophageal hiatus, allowing for direct posterosuperior rotation without tension on the vascular pedicle. Additionally, preserving the right rectus muscle maintains better abdominal wall integrity and function.

A limitation of the technique is donor site morbidity, including abdominal wall weakening and bulging, which we observed in 2 patients. However, these had minimal functional impact and were deemed acceptable by the patients given the relief from GERD symptoms. Our experience suggests that bulging may occur with both open and laparoscopic approaches, though the mechanism differs. In the laparoscopic approach, despite preserving the anterior sheath, the disruption of the posterior sheath and muscle harvest can still lead to functional weakness, especially in patients who have already undergone multiple operations. However, the laparoscopic technique may offer advantages in terms of reduced postoperative pain and faster recovery. Donor-site morbidity and closure management following VRAM flap harvest have been extensively studied, particularly in breast and perineal reconstruction. Reported complications after transverse rectus abdominis myocutaneous flap harvest include seroma, hematoma, wound dehiscence, infection, reduced abdominal wall strength, bulging, and hernias.20 Risk factors for poorer outcomes include smoking, obesity, and diabetes,21–23 particularly in patients with multiple operations with weakened abdominal walls. In our approach, the posterior sheath and peritoneum were left open when the anterior sheath was intact, reducing the risk of hernia. Anterior sheath damage or resection has been shown to increase the risk of postoperative bulging and hernia.24 Although we acknowledge a relatively high level of donor-site morbidity, its functional impact was minor and outweighed by the significant clinical improvement. In high-risk or open cases, the use of mesh for donor site closure may be considered but must be weighed against the potential for infection, particularly in emergency settings, as in our study.25

To date, literature on laparoscopic harvest of the RAM is scarce. In 2000, Greensmith et al reported successful totally laparoscopic RAM flap harvest for free tissue transfer in heel reconstruction using a 5-port approach, noting difficulties in harvesting the upper muscle segment.26 A limited number of reports have described laparoscopic,27,28 reduced-port (3-port)29 and robotic30–32 approaches for RAM flap harvest. All were performed for free tissue transfer26,31 or perineal reconstruction using an inferiorly pedicled flap.27–29 Our technique is the first to describe the feasibility and safety of a totally laparoscopic, superiorly pedicled RAM flap harvest for diaphragmatic hernia repair. Importantly, the technique can be performed without robotic assistance, underscoring its practicality and accessibility without the need for expensive robotic systems, as noted in other studies.30–32

An important theoretical concern is the long-term fate of the denervated RAM flap and its potential impact on hernia recurrence. Muscle denervation causes progressive atrophy, with maximal changes occurring within 6–12 months.33 If atrophy-related recurrence were to occur, we would expect it during this period. However, with a minimum follow-up of 28.88 months and a mean follow-up of 46.5 months, no recurrences were observed. We believe the flap functions as an autologous scaffold undergoing fibrotic transformation and integrating into surrounding structures, creating a durable barrier independent of muscle mass, confirmed on the postoperative CT scans (Fig. 5).

Further prospective multicenter studies with larger cohorts are needed to refine the technique—particularly regarding donor site morbidity—and to confirm the reproducibility and long-term efficacy of this autologous muscular approach. The small and heterogeneous nature of our current series reflects the rarity of this indication, as the procedure is reserved for salvage cases in patients with multiple operations, in whom conventional methods are no longer feasible.

CONCLUSIONS

The use of autologous tissue may play a pivotal role in reoperative antireflux surgery. The RAM flap appears to be a reliable salvage option for severe, recurrent hiatal hernias in patients who have undergone multiple operations, offering durable reconstruction of medium to large hiatal defects with effective symptom relief. The successful laparoscopic approach, in selected cases, positions this innovation at the forefront of minimally invasive reconstructive surgery.
