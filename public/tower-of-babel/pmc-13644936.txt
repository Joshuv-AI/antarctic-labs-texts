Quantifying the benefits of improved operations for polio outbreak response: A model-based analysis
Authors: Yuming Sun, Pinar Keskinocak, Stephanie D Kovacs, Lauren N Steimle
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Novel type 2-containing oral poliovirus vaccine (OPV2) was introduced in 2021, and over two billion doses have since been used globally for outbreak response. Despite its reduced risk of virus reversion, stopping circulating vaccine-derived poliovirus type 2 (cVDPV2) outbreaks remains challenging in some countries, highlighting the need to improve outbreak response operations. This study adapted and extended a poliovirus transmission model to evaluate two operational changes in outbreak response: prioritizing under-vaccinated individuals to receive novel OPV2 (nOPV2) and increasing the number of vaccination rounds.Methods:We simulated cVDPV2 transmission in Nigeria from 2024 to 2028 under multiple outbreak response scenarios. These scenarios varied by vaccine allocation strategy: (1) baseline allocation where all target individual had equal chance of receiving nOPV2; (2) priority allocation where under-vaccinated individuals were prioritized based on their vaccination histories of OPV2 doses received through outbreak response or of inactivated poliovirus vaccine (IPV) doses received through routine immunization; (3) idealized allocation where individuals with the lowest immunity were prioritized. Scenarios also varied in the number of vaccination rounds (3 to 5 rounds per outbreak response). Outcome measures included outbreak size (cumulative cVDPV2 paralytic case counts), time to transmission interruption, and total nOPV2 doses used.Results:Transmission interruption was only achieved under the idealized allocation with five vaccination rounds per response. In all other scenarios, cVDPV2 transmission continued through the end of 2028. Compared with baseline allocation, priority allocation based on OPV2 vaccination history resulted in smaller outbreak sizes with fewer nOPV2 doses for the same number of vaccination rounds, whereas priority allocation based on IPV vaccination history led to larger outbreak sizes and higher nOPV2 use.Conclusions:Both prioritizing under-vaccinated individuals based on OPV2 vaccination history and increasing the number of vaccination rounds reduce cVDPV2 transmission. However, reactive outbreak response alone may be insufficient to stop cVDPV2 outbreaks in high-risk settings such as Nigeria, as the required operational improvements may exceed programmatic capacity. Future work should evaluate the role of expanded nOPV2 use in other vaccination programs, such as routine immunization, to support cVDPV2 elimination.Keywords: Polio eradication, Outbreak response, Under-vaccinated, Vaccine allocation, Simulation, Nigeria

Introduction

The Global Polio Eradication Initiative (GPEI) launched in 1988 [1] has made remarkable progress in reducing poliovirus transmission through the use of oral poliovirus vaccine (OPV) in routine immunization and vaccination campaigns. Wild poliovirus (WPV) types 2 and 3 were certified as eradicated in 2015 [2] and 2019 [3], respectively. WPV type 1 remains endemic only in Pakistan and Afghanistan. Overall, GPEI efforts have reduced global polio cases by more than 99% and prevented millions of cases of paralytic polio.

Despite these achievements, circulating vaccine-derived poliovirus (cVDPV) has emerged as a significant challenge to eradication. cVDPV occurs when the live-attenuated virus contained in OPV genetically reverts and regains transmissibility and neurovirulence, particularly in populations with insufficient immunity to halt virus circulation. To mitigate this risk, GPEI adopted a phased cessation of OPV use by serotype, following the eradication of the homotypic WPV. The first step was the global cessation of type 2-containing OPV (OPV2) in 2016. It was implemented through a globally coordinated “switch” from trivalent OPV (tOPV; containing all three serotypes) to bivalent OPV (bOPV; containing serotypes 1 and 3) in routine immunization that aims to vaccinate newborns at specific ages (e.g., 6, 10, and 14 weeks old [4]). To maintain immunity against type 2 poliovirus after OPV2 cessation, in 2015 before the switch, the GPEI supported the introduction of one dose of inactivated poliovirus vaccine (IPV; containing all three serotypes) in all countries using OPV in routine immunization [5]. Since 2020, because of the increased emergence and transmission of cVDPV type 2 (cVDPV2), the GPEI recommended adding a second IPV dose [6]. Because IPV contains inactivated poliovirus, its use does not result in cVDPV emergence. IPV provides strong humoral immunity and therefore protection against paralysis once infected by the poliovirus. However, unlike OPV, IPV has limited impact on intestinal mucosal immunity and thus limited protection against infection and transmission through the fecal-oral route in individuals not previously exposed to live poliovirus (e.g., WPV, cVDPV, and the virus contained in OPV). This property of IPV makes it less effective to stop outbreaks in settings with suboptimal hygiene and sanitation and high risk of transmission.

Since the switch in 2016, cVDPV2 outbreaks have accounted for over 60% of global polio cases [7]. These outbreaks were driven by several factors, including the failure to eliminate existing cVDPV2 before the switch, insufficient coverage with IPV in routine immunization, and the limited ability of IPV to interrupt transmission [8,9]. In addition, vaccination campaigns using monovalent OPV2 (mOPV2) in response to cVDPV2 outbreaks were delayed, and they had insufficient scope and coverage to interrupt existing transmission and prevent seeding new cVDPV2 emergences [8,9]. Vaccination campaigns conducted during outbreak response usually target children of a broader age group (e.g., 0–4 years old) over a short period (e.g., 4 days). Each response involves multiple rounds of vaccination campaigns, also known as outbreak response supplementary immunization activities (oSIAs).

To mitigate the risk of seeding new cVDPV2 from using mOPV2 in post-switch outbreak response, a novel OPV2 (nOPV2) was developed with enhanced genetic stability and reduced risk of reversion [10]. However, despite the deployment of over two billion nOPV2 doses globally since 2021 [11,12], cVDPV2 was detected in over 30 countries in 2025 [13]. These persistent outbreaks suggest that the problem is not OPV itself but the operational performance of outbreak response. It remains an open question whether relying on nOPV2 outbreak response alone can successfully interrupt persistent cVDPV2 outbreaks, and if so, what operational improvements are needed to achieve this goal.

Previous modeling studies suggested stopping cVDPV2 outbreaks required simultaneous improvements in multiple aspects of field outbreak response performance, such as timeliness and coverage, particularly in under-vaccinated areas [14–19]. These studies emphasized the importance of adhering to GPEI standard operating procedures that define timeliness as initiating vaccination campaigns within 4 weeks after detection and recommend achieving high coverage of at least 90% of children less than five years of age [20]. Some studies suggested that, during outbreak response, stakeholders should prioritize vaccinating “low-immunity individuals” especially when vaccine supply is limited [19]. Based on true immunity level, low-immunity individuals were defined as those with limited intestinal mucosal immunity due to no or limited exposures to live poliovirus through infection or OPV vaccination, such as individuals who received only IPV [19].

However, implementing these improvements is challenging in most of the areas affected by cVDPV2. Improving timeliness of outbreak response (e.g., within 4 weeks after detection according to the standard operating procedures [20]) is often not feasible because of logistical constraints in mobilizing vaccinators and obtaining vaccines and other supplies. Achieving high coverage (e.g., ≥90% according to the standard operating procedures [20]) in areas isolated by conflict, remoteness, or social marginalization is resource-intensive and difficult to monitor. Identifying low-immunity individuals before a campaign is also impractical, as no rapid and accurate immunity tests are currently available.

Given these challenges, we considered two practical operational improvements to outbreak response: increasing the number of oSIA rounds in each response and prioritizing “under-vaccinated individuals” to receive nOPV2 in each campaign. Based on vaccination history alone, we defined under-vaccinated individuals as those who received no or an insufficient number of poliovirus vaccine doses to achieve high seroconversion (e.g., at least 2 doses, which are typically associated with >90% seroconversion for all serotypes [21–27]). The aim of this study was to evaluate whether the aforementioned operational improvements of outbreak response could reduce incident polio cases, improve the use of the limited nOPV2 stockpile by increasing the number of polio cases prevented per dose, and finally interrupt cVDPV2 transmission.

We evaluated numerous outbreak response scenarios using Nigeria's cVDPV2 transmission. These scenarios varied from 3 to 5 oSIA rounds in each outbreak response and varied how nOPV2 doses were allocated among target individuals in each round. Tested vaccine allocation strategies during an oSIA round included: (1) a baseline allocation in which no one was prioritized and thus all target individuals had an equal chance of receiving nOPV2; (2) a priority allocation that prioritized nOPV2 doses to under-vaccinated individuals based on the history of IPV doses received through routine immunization (2a) or OPV2 doses received through oSIAs (2b); and (3) an idealized allocation that prioritized nOPV2 doses to low-immunity individuals based on true immunity level. In all scenarios, we assumed that vaccination timeliness, coverage, target age groups, and interval between consecutive rounds adhered to the GPEI standard operating procedures [20]. Specifically, the standard operating procedures recommend vaccinating at least 90% of children aged 0–59 months within the target geographic area in each round, initiating the first round within 4 weeks of detecting transmission, and conducting subsequent rounds at 4-week intervals.

Methods

Live poliovirus transmission model

In this study, we adapted a published live poliovirus transmission model (see [18] and its online supplemental materials for full details). The differential-equation-based model follows an extended Susceptible (S) – Exposed (E) – Infectious (I) – Susceptible compartmental framework, with the addition of an IPV-reacted (H) compartment which represents those who recently received an “effective” IPV dose but has not acquired the corresponding immunity induced by this dose yet. An effective vaccine dose is defined as a dose that successfully elicits immune response in the recipient and subsequently induces seroconversion.

All compartments are further stratified by age group (a), subpopulation (s), immunity group (i), and vaccination group (k). Compartments E and I are also stratified by virus strain (j). The model assumes that individuals mix homogeneously within a subpopulation but heterogeneously across subpopulations, with some heterogeneity in mixing by age [18]. The model characterizes twenty hypothetical virus strains to abstractly represent the reversion progress as the live-attenuated virus in Sabin-strain or novel OPV lose its attenuating mutations and becomes cVDPV that behaves like homotypic WPV in terms of transmissibility and neurovirulence [18,28,29].

The model includes eight immunity groups to represent varying levels of immunity arising from live poliovirus exposure (i.e., infection or receipt of an effective OPV dose), receipt of an effective IPV dose, and waning of immunity over time (on average two years [18,28]). These groups include unimmunized individuals (immunity group 0), IPV-only-immunized individuals (immunity groups 1–4), and live-poliovirus-immunized individuals (immunity groups 5–7). Immunity groups are ordered from 0 to 7 by increasing immunity levels quantified by reductions in susceptibility, infectiousness, duration of being infectious, and paralysis-to-infection ratio relative to unimmunized individuals [18,28].

To identify under-vaccinated individuals, we defined vaccination groups that capture vaccination history rather than immunity. Vaccination history records the number and timing of vaccine doses received, regardless of whether these doses are effective or not, and therefore serves as an observable proxy for immunity. For example, an individual that has received two OPV doses is expected to have a higher immunity level compared to another individual that has received only one OPV dose, if all doses are effective. Corresponding to the eight immunity groups, we defined eight vaccination groups (see Fig. 1) ranging from 0 (least vaccinated) to 7 (most vaccinated). These include unvaccinated (vaccination group 0), IPV-only-vaccinated (vaccination groups 1–4), and OPV-vaccinated individuals (vaccination groups 5–7).

As illustrated in Fig. 1(a), vaccination group i is defined to mirror the immunity represented by immunity group i under the assumption that all received vaccine doses are effective, implying the maximum immunity level achievable from those doses. For example, an individual in vaccination group 3 would belong to immunity group 3 if both received IPV doses are effective.

However, because vaccine effectiveness is not 100%, administered doses may not be recorded, and infection also contributes to immunity, individuals may belong to immunity group i and vaccination group k where i ≠ k. Fig. 1(b) illustrates all possible combinations of immunity and vaccination groups for an individual with one IPV dose and one OPV dose within the past two years and no prior infections. Depending on whether these doses are effective and recorded, this individual may be assigned to different vaccination and immunity groups. Additional details are provided in Supplemental Materials S1.

Transitions of individuals between model compartments are caused by birth, death, aging, disease dynamics (e.g., recovery from infection), OPV reversion, waning immunity, infection, and vaccination [17,18]. In the model, vaccination changes an individual's immunity group if the vaccine dose received is effective. Whether a vaccine dose is effective is determined by the estimated per-dose vaccine effectiveness with values between 0 and 1. Vaccination also changes an individual's vaccination group if the vaccine dose is recorded in their vaccination history. Whether a vaccine dose is recorded is based on the pre-defined recording probability with values between 0 and 1. Infection changes an individual's immunity group but not vaccination group. More details on transitions between model compartments can be found in [17,18] and Supplemental Materials S1.

Outbreak detection, oSIA triggering, and virus die-out

The model represents outbreak detection through acute flaccid paralysis (AFP) surveillance using a threshold based on weekly paralytic (polio) case counts. Because the differential-equation-based model produces continuous, fractional case counts [18], detection in a subpopulation is defined as the first week in which the number of new cases reaches or exceeds the threshold. Detection triggers an outbreak response in that subpopulation, consisting of multiple oSIA rounds with a pre-defined delay (e.g., 4 weeks) from detection to the first round and fixed intervals (e.g., 4 weeks) between consecutive rounds.

The model also captures “breakthrough transmission” following an outbreak response. In practice, breakthrough transmission is defined as detection of WPV or cVDPV more than 21 days (or 14 days in high-risk settings) after the last oSIA round [20]. Consistent with this, the model includes a “no-SIA-triggering period” after the final round. If weekly cases in the first week after this period meet or exceed the detection threshold, a new outbreak response is triggered. Detection thresholds and no-SIA-triggering periods depend on the studied population (see Section 2.3.1). The model does not incorporate environmental surveillance.

Fig. 2 illustrates the oSIA triggering process under a detection threshold of 1 case per week, a 4-week delay, 4-week intervals between rounds, a 2-week no-SIA-triggering period, and 3 oSIA rounds per response.

Non-isolated subpopulations in Northwest and Northeast Nigeria modeled.

To force virus die-out in the differential-equation-based model when there is a very small fractional number of infectious individuals [28], we defined a die-out threshold based on the proportion of infectious individuals contributing to transmission [18]. When this threshold is reached, the model sets the force of infection to zero, preventing any further infections and effectively stopping transmission (see details in [18]).

Simulations of cVDPV2 outbreaks in Nigeria

We built on a previously developed model of cVDPV2 transmission in the Northwest and Northeast geopolitical zones of Nigeria (see full details in [17–19]). As described in prior works [17–19], the model was calibrated and validated to AFP surveillance data on confirmed cVDPV2 paralytic cases. Using this validated model, we projected cVDPV2 transmission in Northwest and Northeast Nigeria over the study period of 2024–2028 under multiple outbreak response scenarios (see Section 2.3.1). We considered eleven age groups (ages 0–2 and 3–11 months and ages 1, 2, 3, 4, 5–9, 10–14, 15–24, 25–39 and ≥ 40 years) [18]. We separated Northwest and Northeast Nigeria into seven subpopulations [18] (see Fig. 3). General subpopulations 1 and 3 have relatively high historic vaccination coverage and 100% accessibility (defined as the probability that poliovirus vaccines can be delivered to an area [18]). Under-vaccinated subpopulations 2, 4, and 5 have lower historic vaccination coverage compared to general subpopulations and 100%, 99.4%, and 64.7% accessibility, respectively [18]. Isolated subpopulations 6 and 7 have 0% accessibility and extremely limited mixing with other subpopulations (i.e., <1%) due to insurgency [18].

Simplified example on the differences between vaccine allocation strategies AP (proportional allocation), AV (priority allocation based on vaccination history), and AM (priority allocation based on true immunity level)

Given evidence of cVDPV2 emergence following nOPV2 use [30], we updated the model assumptions [19] to incorporate a non-zero reversion risk for nOPV2. Compared to mOPV2, we assumed a reduced reversion risk by introducing a substantially delayed reversion process from nOPV2 to cVDPV2 [14–16,31]. This delay abstractly represents the additional recombination events required for the genetically stabilized virus in nOPV2 (i.e., the nOPV2 virus) to regain transmissibility and neurovirulence (see full details in [14] and Supplemental Materials S1.3). We further assumed slightly lower effectiveness for nOPV2 relative to mOPV2 [15], set at 90% of per-dose mOPV2 effectiveness. Given per-dose mOPV2 effectiveness of 0.73 in the Nigeria study, this corresponds to 0.66 for nOPV2. We assumed the same transmissibility (R0) for the nOPV2 virus and the mOPV2 virus, namely 56% of cVDPV2 R0 (7.2) [14–19,31]. Taken together, these assumptions represent a conservative scenario for nOPV2 characteristics and provide a lower bound for nOPV2 outbreak response outcomes [14].

To initialize simulations at the start of 2024, we updated the “initial condition” in the previous Nigeria study [19] to estimate the distribution of individuals across vaccination group. Initial condition specifies how many individuals are in each model compartment at simulation start. For children aged less than 5 years, we estimated vaccination histories using routine immunization coverage data derived from aggregated individual-level vaccination records (e.g., vaccination cards and parental recall) of received routine immunization vaccine doses [32]. Specifically, we estimated coverage with one IPV dose (IPV1) and two IPV doses (IPV2) by combining data from Nigeria's Demographic and Health Survey 2023–2024 [33] and Diphtheria, Tetanus, and Pertussis vaccine (DTP) coverage estimates in 2019–2024 from the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation [34], reflecting the co-administration of IPV with DTP [4]. These estimates were then used to assign children under 5 years to vaccination group 0 (unvaccinated) and vaccination groups 1–4 (IPV-only-vaccinated). We did not assign individuals to OPV-vaccinated vaccination groups 5–7, as OPV2 is not used in routine immunization and individual-level records from OPV2 outbreak response are unavailable. Individuals aged ≥5 years were assumed to be in vaccination group 0, as vaccination group assignments for these age groups do not affect transmission dynamics when oSIAs target children under 5 years. Additional details are provided in Supplemental Materials S2.

Tested outbreak response scenarios in Nigeria

During the study period, we assumed detection threshold of 1 case per week in all subpopulations [19]. This threshold represents a theoretically perfect AFP surveillance that detects the first cVDPV2 case as soon as it emerges, confirms an outbreak, and then triggers an outbreak response consisting of multiple oSIA rounds in the differential-equation-based model [17,18,28]. Given the persistent cVDPV2 transmission in Nigeria (i.e., a high-risk scenario), we assumed no-SIA-triggering period of 2 weeks [20].

Following the standard operating procedures [20], we assumed that the first oSIA round was implemented four weeks after the detection of an outbreak or breakthrough transmission (4-week delay), followed by additional rounds at 4-week intervals. Each round targeted children aged 0–59 months with a single dose of nOPV2 and aimed to achieve 90% coverage among accessible children. Because accessibility varies across subpopulations, the finally achieved oSIA coverage was adjusted accordingly. Specifically, the achieved coverage in a subpopulation was calculated as 90% multiplied by that subpopulation's accessibility. Given accessibility of 100%, 100%, 100%, 99.4%, 64.7%, 0%, and 0% for subpopulations 1–7, respectively, the resulting achieved coverage is 90%, 90%, 90%, 89.5%, 58.2%, 0%, and 0%.

We systematically varied two factors in an outbreak response: the number of oSIA rounds and the vaccine allocation strategy. We tested the implementation of 3, 4, and 5 oSIA rounds in an outbreak response. We tested four vaccine allocation strategies (see Table 1). These vaccine allocation strategies differed in (i) how limited nOPV2 doses in each oSIA round were distributed across target individuals based on different information on vaccination need (e.g., vaccination history or true immunity) and (ii) what changes in vaccination groups were tracked.

Tested vaccine allocation strategies in outbreak response scenarios.

We considered a baseline allocation strategy, AB, in which each target individual had an equal chance of receiving an nOPV2 dose. Under AB, doses were allocated proportionally to the size of each model compartment, reflecting current practice where oSIAs do not account for vaccination history or immunity. Because AB did not depend on vaccination groups, the model did not track changes in vaccination groups under this strategy (i.e., recording probability was 0 for all doses).

We considered two priority allocation strategies that targeted under-vaccinated individuals based on vaccination history: AP-RI and AP-oSIA. Under AP-RI, individuals were prioritized based on recorded IPV doses received through routine immunization. Accordingly, the model tracked changes in vaccination groups only through IPV doses from routine immunization, assigning a recording probability of 1 to these doses and 0 to all others. Under AP-oSIA, individuals were prioritized based on recorded nOPV2 doses received through oSIAs. In this case, the model tracked changes in vaccination groups only through nOPV2 doses from oSIAs, with a recording probability of 1 for these doses and 0 for all others.

We also considered an idealized allocation, AI, in which low-immunity individuals were prioritized. Because true immunity is not directly observable in practice, AI represents an ideal upper bound on possible vaccine allocation performance. Compared to AI, AP-RI and AP-oSIA are more feasible to implement, as vaccination history is more likely to be available. Since allocation under AI did not rely on vaccination groups (but immunity groups), the model did not track changes in vaccination groups (i.e., recording probability was 0 for all doses).

Fig. 4 illustrates how AB, AP and AI allocate limited nOPV2 doses across individuals using a simplified example. The example assumes three immunity groups, three vaccination groups, one age group, one subpopulation, and 12 susceptible individuals targeted by an oSIA round with 50% coverage (i.e., 6 doses). Of note, vaccination group and immunity group may not align, as vaccination history is only a proxy to true immunity level. Individuals in high vaccination groups may still have low immunity if prior doses were ineffective, while individuals in low vaccination groups may have high immunity due to prior infections. Under AB, doses are distributed proportionally, with 50% of individuals in each compartment receiving nOPV2 (see Fig. 4(b)). Under AP, doses are prioritized by vaccination group (0, then 1, then 2), resulting in all 6 doses being allocated to individuals in vaccination group 0 (see Fig. 4 (c)). Under AI, doses are prioritized by immunity group (0, then 1, then 2), resulting in all 6 doses being allocated to individuals in immunity group 0 (see Fig. 4(d)). As shown in Fig. 4(c), priority allocation based on vaccination history (AP) could vaccinate high-immunity individuals marked as under-vaccinated (i.e., the two individuals in immunity group 2 and vaccination group 0) at the risk of not vaccinating low-immunity individuals marked as well-vaccinated (the two individuals in immunity group 0 and vaccination group 2). Simulations of both AP and AI allow us to quantify how the misalignment between vaccination history and true immunity level impacts the outbreak response outcomes (see Section 2.3.2).

Outcome measures

We compared the outcomes of all scenarios by three measures: “time until die-out,” “outbreak size,” and “total nOPV2 doses administered.” Time until die-out measures the time point at which transmission is fully interrupted in the modeled population. Specifically, it is defined as the first week (in the study period) during which all subpopulations simultaneously have zero new cVDPV2 paralytic cases. In the model, this occurs after the die-out threshold is triggered (see Section 2.2), at which point transmission has ceased in all subpopulations and no further infections are generated. This definition implicitly assumes a perfect surveillance system that immediately detects the absence of cases and confirms interruption of transmission without delay. Therefore, the modeled time until die-out should be interpreted as the earliest possible time of confirming transmission interruption under ideal surveillance conditions. Outbreak size evaluates the cumulative number of cVDPV2 paralytic cases over the study period. Total nOPV2 doses administered counts the cumulative number of nOPV2 doses administered through oSIAs over the study period. More details on how to calculate these measures can be found in [17–19].

Fig. 5 compares outbreak size and total nOPV2 doses administered. For any number of oSIA rounds per outbreak response, vaccine allocation strategy AI consistently resulted in the smallest outbreak size (240–300 case counts) and required the fewest doses (204.4 to 248.1 million doses).

Outbreak size and total nOPV2 doses administered in tested outbreak response scenarios; scenarios with transmission interrupted are marked by asterisks.

Compared with the baseline allocation (AB), under a fixed number of oSIA rounds, priority allocation based on the history of OPV2 received through oSIAs (AP-oSIA) resulted in smaller outbreak sizes with fewer nOPV2 doses administered. In contrast, priority allocation based on the history of IPV received through routine immunization (AP-RI) led to larger outbreak sizes and higher nOPV2 use. For example, with 4 rounds per outbreak response, AB resulted in 343 case counts using 270.1 million doses, whereas AP-oSIA resulted in 309 case counts (10.0% reduction) using 264.4 million doses (2.1% reduction), and AP-RI resulted in 387 case counts (13.0% increase) using 304.5 million doses (12.7% increase).

For each vaccine allocation strategy, increasing the number of rounds per outbreak response increased total nOPV2 doses administered, but reduced outbreak size. For example, under AP-RI, outbreak size decreased from 409 case counts with 3 rounds per outbreak response to 364 case counts with 5 rounds per outbreak response (11.0% reduction), while total nOPV2 doses administered increased by 54.7%, from 226.3 to 350.1 million.

Discussion

This study examined the extent to which improved operations of outbreak response could help completely interrupt cVDPV2 transmission. More specifically, we evaluated the impact of increasing the number of oSIA rounds implemented in each response and targeting under-vaccinated individuals during oSIAs based on individual's vaccination history. To the best of our knowledge, this study stands as the first in polio modeling that (i) incorporates and simulates individual's vaccination history, (ii) conceptualizes differences between low-immunity (based on true immunity achieved) and under-vaccinated (based on vaccine doses recorded) individuals, and (iii) offers unique insights into how the prioritization of under-vaccinated children influences the die-out dynamics of persistent poliovirus transmission.

Our simulations showed that cVDPV2 transmission was eliminated only under the most idealistic scenario, in which five oSIA rounds with ≥90% coverage were conducted in each outbreak response and individuals with the lowest immunity levels were accurately identified and prioritized for vaccination. These findings underscore our most important insight: eliminating cVDPV2 transmission using nOPV2 through outbreak response alone remains challenging, as it requires operational excellence that is far beyond both current guidance and historical achievement. The number of oSIA rounds suggested by our analysis is higher than current recommendations of two rounds [20]. The assumed timeliness (≤4 weeks) and coverage (≥90%) are more favorable than what has been achieved in past campaigns, particularly in Africa [8,9,35–37]. Implementing more targeted vaccination, such as prioritizing individuals with low immunity or limited vaccine doses, would be difficult in practice, especially in settings with persistent cVDPV2 outbreaks. Rapid assessment of immunity is not feasible, and vaccination records are often incomplete or unavailable, particularly for doses delivered through outbreak response campaigns. Taken together, these results suggest that if outbreak response alone struggles to eliminate cVDPV2 transmission even under these favorable assumptions, then additional operational constraints, such as longer delays or lower coverage, would likely require even more intensive vaccination efforts and further delay elimination.

Our finding that increasing the number of oSIA rounds from three to five in each response led to smaller outbreak sizes, regardless of the vaccine allocation strategy, is important. This aligns with previous modeling studies that emphasized the role of additional vaccination rounds in accelerating outbreak control and interrupting transmission [14,17–19,37–42]. Current GPEI guidelines recommend and fund two oSIA rounds in response to cVDPV2 outbreaks [20]. However, since 2016, only a limited number of countries have successfully stopped cVDPV2 outbreaks with two rounds alone [43]. This limited success is not surprising, as in many low-resource settings, delayed detection and constraints in outbreak response capacity make it difficult to achieve timely campaigns with high coverage and adequately reach under-vaccinated populations at highest risk of sustaining transmission. Consistent with both existing modeling evidence and field experience, our findings suggest that GPEI should consider updating standard operating procedures to include more vaccination rounds in each response.

We also demonstrated that using children's OPV2 vaccination history from oSIAs to guide the nOPV2 allocation during outbreak response led to smaller outbreaks using fewer nOPV2 doses, whereas using IPV history from routine immunization did not provide the same benefit, compared with current guidelines that vaccinate all children regardless of prior vaccinations [20]. Further investigation showed that this difference arose because the effectiveness of using vaccination history depended on two factors (i) whether the history provided good alignment with the immunity against both infection and paralysis and (ii) whether low-immunity individuals are being prioritized while prioritizing under-vaccinated individuals (see Supplemental Materials S3). OPV2 records from oSIAs more accurately identified high-immunity children who acquired intestinal mucosal immunity. They marked those high-immunity children by high vaccination groups, which then made children in low vaccination groups were those truly with low immunity and were prioritized in subsequent oSIAs. In contrast, IPV records from routine immunization mainly reflected potential humoral immunity which poorly aligned with the immunity against infection and transmission. They tended to classify younger children into high vaccination groups, because they recently received IPV with high coverage, while older children, whose IPV doses were given years earlier with lower coverage, were classified into low vaccination groups. However, although IPV-vaccinated younger children were shielded from paralysis, they could still transmit poliovirus due to limited intestinal mucosal immunity while not being prioritized in oSIAs. On the other hand, older children, having experienced repeated exposures to cVDPV2 and nOPV2 campaigns, often had stronger intestinal mucosal immunity despite being labelled as low vaccination groups.

Prioritization of receiving nOPV2 during oSIAs based on true immunity consistently outperformed vaccine allocation strategies based on vaccination history, which underlines the limitations of histories of vaccines received through routine immunization or outbreak response activities as proxies for immunity against both infection and paralysis. Vaccination history reflects only potential protection achieved by administered doses. The identification of low-immunity individuals for vaccine prioritization would require not only accurate information on vaccine type, number of doses, and time since vaccination, but also knowledge of historical transmission and vaccine effectiveness in the area, which remains difficult in real life.

Altogether, our modeling results indicate that in the absence of highly sophisticated and extremely robust outbreak response, eliminating cVDPV2 through outbreak response alone remains extremely challenging. Consistent with findings from previous studies [38,44–47], our results suggest that incorporating nOPV2 into preventive vaccination programs, such as routine immunization, preventive SIAs, and continuous vaccination, may enhance control of cVDPV2 transmission. However, the final decision on the expanded use of nOPV2 outside outbreak response would need additional evaluation. In routine immunization, nOPV2 could be integrated into existing schedules alongside bOPV and IPV, either through concomitant administration or separate delivery. Clinical trial data [48] support the safety of concomitant use of bOPV and nOPV2. However, the co-administration interferes with immunogenicity for type 2 but not for types 1 and 3. Therefore, the benefits of co-administration, such as reduced vaccine delivery complexity and costs, need to be weighed against the reduced type 2 immunological effectiveness. Preventive SIAs using nOPV2 may provide a more flexible approach to rapidly vaccinate children across a broad age range, thereby enhancing population immunity against type 2 poliovirus and preventing its transmission. Continuous vaccination strategies, such as administering nOPV2 during health facility visits or at border crossings in high-risk regions, can improve equity by effectively reaching under-vaccinated children in conflict-affected areas and highly mobile populations. This has been demonstrated by the “Permanent Transit Point for Polio” strategy at the Pakistan–Afghanistan border [49].

While our analysis is framed around outbreak response, the operational insights are applicable to broader campaign-based vaccination strategies, including pre-planned SIAs that are increasingly implemented in Nigeria. Both outbreak response and pre-planned SIAs involve large-scale vaccination campaigns targeting broad age groups over short time periods. They differ mainly in coverage, timing, and triggering mechanisms. oSIAs are initiated following transmission detection and typically focus on outbreak-affected areas. Pre-planned SIAs are scheduled in advance, often as national or subnational immunization days. We do not expect these differences to affect our findings on the relative benefits of increasing the number of vaccination rounds and prioritizing low-immunity or under-vaccinated individuals in vaccination campaigns. At the same time, given that many countries continue to rely primarily on outbreak response for cVDPV2 control, our results provide a generic insight that eliminating persistent cVDPV2 transmission through outbreak response alone remains challenging, as the required level of operational performance may exceed what is feasible in practice.

Our study has several limitations which motivates future work. First, we adapted a deterministic compartmental model of poliovirus transmission [17,18], which has inherent limitations. In particular, the model does not incorporate stochastic elements, such as random variation in infectious contacts and chance extinction events, that can influence transmission dynamics, especially when transmission is low and very close to achieving die-out. As a result, the model captures average transmission behavior rather than the full range of possible epidemic trajectories. In addition, given the large space of parameter values and the interdependence between parameters, the model calibration procedure aimed to find one set of parameter values that reproduced cVDPV2 transmission in Northwest and Northeast Nigeria, without exploring uncertainty across alternative parameter sets [17,18]. Future work could further explore stochasticity and alternative parameter sets that provide good model fit, although this would require more intensive computation and evaluation.

Second, this study assumed idealized AFP surveillance. Poliovirus transmission was detected as soon as the first cVDPV2 paralytic case emerged. Transmission interruption was confirmed immediately once no new cases were generated after virus die-out. Under these assumptions, outbreak response was triggered without reporting delay, and the modeled time until die-out represented the earliest possible confirmation of elimination based on AFP surveillance alone. In practice, AFP surveillance may be delayed by reporting and investigation processes and may fail to detect asymptomatic transmission. Future work could incorporate more realistic surveillance assumptions, including reporting delays and integration with environmental surveillance, to better quantify their effects on outbreak response triggering and timelines for confirming elimination. Stochastic modeling approaches could explicitly quantify uncertainty in virus die-out and estimate the minimum duration of zero detections required to achieve high confidence in the absence of silent circulation [50–52].

Third, our assumptions on nOPV2 effectiveness and reversion introduce uncertainty into model projections. While emerging clinical and field evidence suggest that nOPV2 effectiveness is comparable to mOPV2 [30,53–55], the long-term transmission and reversion dynamics of nOPV2 remain incompletely understood and vary across settings [30]. Future work can therefore evaluate model outcomes under a range of assumptions on nOPV2 effectiveness and reversion to better quantify uncertainty and inform policy decisions.

Fourth, the model does not incorporate logistical and operational constraints that may affect the feasibility of prioritizing under-vaccinated children based on OPV2 history. In high-risk settings such as Nigeria, vaccination records on OPV2 doses received through outbreak response are often incomplete or unavailable, limiting the practical use of OPV2 vaccination history to guide vaccine allocation. Under these conditions, alternative approaches can be explored to guide resource allocation toward under-vaccinated communities [56–62], such as those in remote or conflict areas, highly mobile groups, or socially marginalized populations. Tools such as geographic information system-based tracking of vaccination teams may help support these efforts [63]. Even in settings where individual-level OPV2 vaccination records are accessible and reliable, further studies are needed to assess the costs and cost-effectiveness of strategies that prioritize under-vaccinated children, considering limited financial resources [64]. Although such strategies may reduce nOPV2 use, they could introduce additional operational costs associated with identifying, locating, and reaching those under-vaccinated.

Fifth, our findings highlight the challenges of eliminating persistent cVDPV2 transmission through outbreak response alone and suggest that stakeholders evaluate expanded nOPV2 use in preventive vaccination. However, demonstrating the limitations of outbreak response does not imply that expanded nOPV2 use will achieve elimination. Therefore, our study motivates further evaluation of its feasibility, effectiveness, and cost-effectiveness. Key considerations include how to allocate a limited global nOPV2 stockpile across countries to balance equity and effectiveness, and within countries, how to allocate doses between preventive and outbreak response vaccinations to maximize transmission reduction. Routine immunization coverage remains low in some high-transmission areas, which may limit the immediate impact of nOPV2 integration. However, evaluating the potential benefits of such strategies remains important, as substantial gains could justify efforts to strengthen routine immunization delivery. In addition, it remains uncertain how nOPV2 should be incorporated into the existing routine immunization schedules of bOPV and IPV. Future work could evaluate the cost-effectiveness, epidemiological impact, and operational feasibility of different nOPV2 schedules in routine immunization.

Finally, the number of vaccine doses projected in our simulations is lower than observed in Nigeria [53]. This difference does not reflect lower vaccination intensity in our scenarios. In fact, the tested scenarios assume higher coverage, more vaccination rounds, and shorter implementation delays than typically observed in practice. The lower number of projected doses arises from two assumptions. First, vaccine wastage was not included. Second, for simplicity, we tracked only doses administered to susceptible individuals, since doses given to exposed, infectious, or IPV-reacted individuals do not affect transmission dynamics in the model. Our study aims to compare the relative performance of vaccine allocation strategies in terms of vaccine use and outbreak outcomes. While incorporating vaccine wastage and doses administered to non-susceptible individuals would increase total dose estimates and better align with observed data, we expect our findings to remain unchanged: prioritizing low-immunity or under-vaccinated individuals reduces outbreak size while using fewer nOPV2 doses than the status quo.

Conclusions

Our study shows that prioritizing under-vaccinated children based on the history of OPV2 doses received through vaccination campaigns can reduce cVDPV2 paralytic case counts while using fewer nOPV2 doses compared with the current strategy that vaccinates children regardless of their vaccination history [20]. Meanwhile, adding more oSIA rounds consistently reduces the case burden. Given that more than two vaccination rounds per outbreak response are needed to eliminate cVDPV transmission, GPEI should consider updating the standard operating procedures. However, improvements in vaccine allocation and the number of vaccination rounds alone are not sufficient for complete interruption of cVDPV2 transmission in high-risk settings such as Nigeria. Achieving interruption would require exceptionally high-level operational performance: at least five timely (within 4 weeks) and high coverage (90%) nOPV2 oSIA rounds for each outbreak response, all capable of accurately identifying and targeting truly low-immunity children. This level of performance remains far beyond what has historically been feasible and represents a highly sophisticated and idealized scenario.

These findings highlight an important consideration for stakeholders: strengthening outbreak response alone through an improvement in the number of rounds, vaccine allocation strategies, and timeliness and coverage of campaigns may not be sufficient or practical to completely interrupt persistent cVDPV2 outbreaks. A more practical and sustainable path forward may involve combining reactive outbreak response vaccinations with preventive vaccinations such as the inclusion of nOPV2 in routine immunization. Pairing the preventive and reactive uses of nOPV2 may provide a more promising approach leading to the elimination of cVDPV2 and the achievement of global eradication goals.
