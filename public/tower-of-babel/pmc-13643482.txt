Impact of Electrical Spinal Cord Stimulation on Lower Urinary Tract Dysfunction in Mice With Spinal Cord Injury
Authors: Kang Jun Cho, Sergei Karnup, Mamoru Hashimoto, Kanako Matsuoka, Stephanie L Daugherty, Tadanobu Kamijo, Joon Chul Kim, Naoki Yoshimura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeWe aimed to assess the effects of electrical stimulation of the L3–4 spinal cord containing the lumbar spinal coordinating center (LSCC) for neurogenic lower urinary tract dysfunction (NLUTD) in mice with spinal cord injury (SCI).MethodsMale mice were divided into spinal cord intact (SI) and SCI groups. Two electrodes were placed over the epidural surface of the L3–4 spinal cord for electrical spinal cord stimulation (SCS). SCS was initiated when the intravesical pressure of voiding bladder contractions began rising. Cystometrograms (CMG) and external urethral sphincter (EUS)-electromyography (EMG) were recorded before, during and after SCS to examine the effects of SCS on CMG and EUS-EMG parameters in each group.ResultsIn SI mice, significant differences in CMG parameters were not found after SCS compared to pre-SCS values. However, in SCI mice, the ratio of the voided volume to bladder filling volume was significantly higher after SCS compared to pre- SCS values (P<0.001). Additionally, SCI mice after SCS showed a significantly lower nonvoiding contraction number (P=0.017) and a shorter intercontraction interval (P=0.007) compared to those without SCS. EUS-EMG parameters in SI mice without and with SCS showed no significant differences in the ratio of active phases (AP) to silent phases (SP). In contrast, SCI mice with SCS had a significantly lower AP to SP ratio compared to those without SCS (P<0.001).ConclusionsSCS applied to the L3–4 spinal cord can improve detrusor sphincter dyssynergia by increasing EUS relaxation to mitigate inefficient voiding as well as detrusor overactivity in SCI. Restoration of the LSCC function by SCS could be a therapeutic modality to improve NLUTD after SCI.Keywords: Spinal cord injuries, Neuromodulation, Voiding dysfunction, Detrusor sphincter dyssynergia

• HIGHLIGHTS

- Spinal cord stimulation improved detrusor sphincter dyssynergia and voiding efficiency in mice with spinal cord injury.

- The improvement in detrusor sphincter dyssynergia was associated with increased external urethral sphincter relaxation during voiding.

- Spinal cord stimulation also reduced nonvoiding contractions, suggesting a potential effect on detrusor overactivity.

INTRODUCTION

Detrusor sphincter dyssynergia (DSD), a type of neurogenic lower urinary tract dysfunction (NLUTD) that occurs following spinal cord injury (SCI), results in high urethral closure pressure during detrusor contractions, leading to elevated bladder pressure, reduced bladder compliance and decreased voiding efficiency (VE). Control of DSD is important for the health of SCI patients because it can cause upper urinary tract deterioration and increase the risk of urinary tract infections. Although some pharmacological agents have been reported to reduce intravesical pressure in patients with DSD after SCI, their therapeutic effects are limited [1, 2]; therefore, current effective treatments still largely rely on bladder catheterization, underscoring the need for a new treatment strategy for DSD.

To develop new treatments for DSD, better understanding of its pathophysiology, including the physiological features and organization of the spinal neuronal network related to bladder– external urethral sphincter (EUS) interaction, is required. In this regard, the lumbar spinal coordinating center (LSCC), located in the L3–4 spinal segments, is shown to play an important role in bladder–EUS coordination during micturition [3]. The major components of the neuronal circuitry in the LSCC are segmental interneurons and propriospinal neurons projecting to the L6–S1 spinal cord in rodents [4]. In spinal cord intact (SI) rats and male mice, EUS relaxation is induced during the bursting activity of EUS muscles; that is, rhythmic, reciprocal contractions and relaxations during micturition, which are mediated by spinal interneuronal circuits and modulated by descending inputs from supraspinal centers, although the precise role of the pontine micturition center (PMC) and its relationship to the LSCC remains uncertain [5, 6]. Previous studies in rodents also have revealed that activation of the LSCC at the L3–4 spinal cord is a key mechanism triggering EUS bursting activity during normal voiding [3, 7]. In cases where the PMC input is lost due to SCI, EUS bursting is rarely elicited due to the loss of PMC-mediated LSCC activation, resulting in DSD. Thus, in the absence of PMC input signals following SCI, the reinforcement of LSCC neural circuit function may improve EUS bursting activity to ameliorate DSD and inefficient voiding induced by SCI.

Electrical spinal cord stimulation (SCS), which was first introduced more than 50 years ago as a treatment for chronic pain [8], could be one such approach because it has been demonstrated to improve somatic motor control and various autonomic functions, including bladder and bowel control, sexual function, and pulmonary function, in individuals with chronic SCI [9]. Some animal studies have suggested that SCS of the upper lumbar spinal cord reduced EUS resistance in neurogenic NLUTD associated with SCI or pelvic nerve crush injury [10, 11]. However, the functional role of LSCC in treating NLUTD in SCI remains unclear, and most previous studies employed female rodents, with limited data available from males. Also, NLUTD in SCI reportedly exhibits sex-specific differences in the control of EUS function during voiding [6, 12]. Thus, we aimed to assess the effects of electrical stimulation of the L3–4 spinal cord for NLUTD using male SCI mice to investigate the functional role of LSCC in the recovery of NLUTD after SCI.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals

We divided male C57BL/6 mice aged 8–9 weeks into 2 groups: SI and SCI groups. The SCI group underwent complete transection of the T8–9 spinal cord with microscissors under isoflurane anesthesia, as described previously [12, 13]. A sterile Gelfoam sponge (Pfizer, USA) was placed between the cut ends of the spinal cord to control bleeding and maintain the separation. The wound was then closed, and the mouse was assessed for loss of motor function in the lower extremities to confirm SCI. Following the surgical procedure, SCI mice received ampicillin (100 mg/kg, once daily for 7 days) and ketoprofen (5 mg/kg, twice daily for 3 days) for postoperative prophylaxis of infection and analgesia. SCI mice with high residual urine due to NLUTD had their bladders manually emptied by lower abdominal compression once daily for 4 weeks until the final procedures. Animals were housed in an air-conditioned room maintained at 22°C–24°C under a 12:12-hour light-dark cycle (lights on at 07:00 AM), with free access to food and water.

Cystometry and External Urethral Sphincter-Electromyography

Cystometrograms (CMG) and EUS-electromyography (EMG) were conducted in conscious mice. In the SCI group, these procedures were performed 4 weeks after SCI surgery, following a period of routine care in the animal facility.

The procedure began with exposure of the bladder and urethra through a lower midline incision under isoflurane anesthesia. For CMG, a PE-50 catheter (Clay-Adams, USA) was inserted into the bladder via the dome and secured with sutures.

For EUS-EMG recordings, 2 epoxy-insulated stainless-steel wires (50.8-μm diameter; A-M Systems, USA) were used as electrodes. These wires were inserted into each side of the EUS under direct vision using 30G needles, with a 2-mm uninsulated tip portion of the electrode hooked at the needle tip. The needles were then withdrawn after inserting the electrode into the EUS muscle, and the electrodes were secured to the perineal skin with sutures to prevent displacement. A ground electrode was attached to the tail to reduce electrical noise during recordings.

After closing the lower abdominal wound, 2 wires for SCS were placed over the epidural space of the L3–4 spinal cord as described in the section below and Fig. 1, and the mice were placed into restraining cages (Economy holder 15–30 g; Kent Scientific, USA) to allow them to recover from isoflurane anesthesia. To alleviate surgical pain, lidocaine hydrochloride was applied to the wound before starting CMG and EUS-EMG recordings. The PE-50 bladder catheter was connected via a threeway stopcock to an infusion pump and a pressure transducer, and the EUS-EMG electrodes were connected to an amplifier. Subsequently, the bladder was infused with saline through the PE-50 catheter at a rate of 0.01 mL/min until regular, repetitive bladder contractions were observed with acclimation in the restraining cage. CMG parameters measured during a single CMG, for which the bladder was emptied each time after voiding contraction, included intercontraction interval (ICI), maximal voiding pressure, postvoid residual (PVR), ratio of voided volume (VV) to bladder filling volume, and a number of nonvoiding contractions (NVCs). NVC was defined as a ≥8-cm H2O increase in intravesical pressure from baseline. PVR was measured by gravity-draining the residual intravesical fluid through the bladder catheter after voiding. VV was estimated by subtracting PVR from the total saline infusion volume during a single CMG. In addition, EUS-EMG signals were recorded at a sampling rate of 2,000 Hz. The primary EMG parameter measured was the ratio of active phases (AP) to silent phases (SP) during EUS bursting associated with voiding contractions. The AP was defined as rectified and smoothed EMG activity exceeding baseline by a threshold for ≥30 ms to exclude noise fluctuations. The SP was defined as the intervening periods below threshold. For each contraction, integrated EMG activity was measured separately for AP and SP, and the AP/SP ratio was calculated as (ΣAP/ΣSP). We selected the AP/SP ratio as an evaluating parameter because it reflects the relative balance of EUS contraction versus relaxation during each voiding bladder contraction, while minimizing cycle-to-cycle variability of voiding events. Physiological parameters were measured using the standard pressure transducer-amplifier setup provided by ADInstruments (Colorado Springs, USA) and recorded with LabChart software (ADInstruments).

CMG and EUS-EMG parameters obtained before, during and after SCS were compared in each group of SI or SCI mice. We excluded cases when voiding reflexes were not adequately induced in CMG or EUS-recordings during bladder filling and/ or when EUS-EMG electrode dislocation was found after completing CMG and EMG examination.

Epidural SCS

Following insertion of a bladder catheter for CMG and electrodes for EMG in the supine position, a laminectomy at the T12 or T13 vertebra was performed in the prone position to expose the L3–4 spinal cord, which is anatomically aligned with the T12–13 vertebrae under isoflurane anesthesia [14].

Two Teflon-coated stainless-steel wires (AS632; Cooner wire Co., USA) with a diameter of 0.1016 mm were deinsulated for about 2 mm at 2 cm from the end, and placed over each lateral side of the dorsal epidural surface of the L3–4 spinal cord. Subsequently, the proximal and distal areas from the deinsulated portions of wires were secured to the back muscles with sutures (Fig. 1). After transferring the mice to restraining cages, the proximal ends of the wires were connected to a stimulus isolation unit (Grass Instruments, A-M Systems) coupled with a stimulator (S48; Grass Instruments, A-M Systems) before beginning electrical stimulation.

After the recovery from anesthesia, epidural SCS was applied to the L3–4 spinal cord surface with a pulse duration of 0.1–0.2 ms at a frequency of 2–4 Hz for 8–10 seconds. Stimulation was initiated at the onset of, or immediately prior to, the rise in intravesical pressure to anticipate the initiation of the voiding contraction, as triggering stimulation precisely at the exact moment of pressure increase was technically challenging. The onset of pressure rise was defined as the initial sustained upward deflection from a stable intravesical pressure baseline observed on the CMG trace. Stimulation timing was determined by realtime visual inspection of CMG by the same trained experimenter using consistent criteria to minimize variability related to reaction time. The intensity of SCS at 3–4 V was set at 1- to 1.5-fold of the threshold intensity that changes EMG activity of the EUS. During the experiments, stimulation intensity was carefully adjusted to minimize unintended body movements, thereby reducing potential confounding effects on bladder emptying due to intraabdominal pressure increases. In each group of SI or SCI mice, changes in the EUS behavior during SCS were evaluated by comparing EUS-EMG activity before and during SCS to identify the effects of SCS on EUS bursting activity during the voiding reflex. Also, changes in lower urinary tract function were evaluated by measuring storage and voiding parameters in single CMGs before and after SCS in each group of animals.

Statistical Analysis

For continuous data that did not fit a Gaussian distribution, the Mann–Whitney U-test was used to compare SI and SCI mice before SCS, whereas the Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed-rank test was used for within-group comparisons before and after SCS. Primary outcome measures, including the ratio of VV to bladder filling volume as an index of VE and parameters related to bladder–sphincter coordination, were predefined, whereas other urodynamic parameters were considered exploratory. Accordingly, no formal correction for multiple comparisons was applied. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism version 9.5.0 for Windows (GraphPad Software, USA). P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

We performed CMG and EUS-EMG recordings in 10 SI mice and 40 SCI mice, and the final data were obtained from 7 SI and 27 SCI mice in which the effects of SCS on CMG and EUSEMG activity were continuously evaluated during the experiments. Any mice in which voiding reflexes disappeared or markedly diminished during recording periods, likely due to additional spinal cord compression damage induced by intrathecal catheter implantation, were excluded from data analyses.

First, in both SI and SCI groups, CMG and EUS-EMG parameters were obtained during 2–3 voiding cycles without SCS. Thereafter, epidural SCS was started at the initial rise of intravesical pressure during the voiding reflex and applied for 8–10 seconds. SCS-induced changes in EUS behavior during voiding bladder contractions were analyzed by EMG parameters of EUS bursting activity during the SCS period (Figs. 2, 3). Then, immediately after SCS, CMG parameters were evaluated during the following 2 voiding cycles to investigate the effect of SCS on storage and voiding functions in the SI or SCI group.

Representative CMG and EUS-EMG recordings in SI and SCI mice are shown in Figs. 2 and 3, respectively. Before SCS, SCI mice exhibited a reduction in the ratio of VV to bladder filling volume (P=0.002) and increased PVR (P<0.001) during the voiding phase and increases in NVCs (P<0.001) and ICI (P<0.001) during the storage phase, compared to SI mice. Also, EUS-EMG recordings revealed that SCI mice without SCS had an increased ratio of AP to SP during the voiding reflex compared to SI mice without SCS (P<0.001), indicating the enhanced tonic activity of the EUS after SCI. When comparing CMG parameters before and after SCS, SI mice did not show any significant differences. However, in SCI mice, the ratio of VV to bladder filling volume after SCS was significantly greater compared to those before SCS (P<0.001), suggesting the improvement of VE. In addition, in SCI mice, the number of NVCs per minute during the bladder filling phase (P=0.017) and ICI (P=0.007) in single CMGs were significantly lower after SCS compared to pre-SCS values (Fig. 4; Table 1).

In EUS-EMG parameters, SI mice showed no significant difference in the AP/SP ratio with versus without SCS (P=0.578) compared to those before SCS. DISCUSSION

The results of this study demonstrated that epidural electrical SCS targeting the LSCC in the L3–4 spinal cord positively influenced CMG and EUS-EMG parameters in male mice with SCIinduced NLUTD, but had no significant influence on those parameters in SI mice.

In male mice with SCI at T8–10 spinal cord transection, this study showed increased ICI, a decreased ratio of VV to bladder filling volume on CMG recordings, and an increased ratio of AP to SP on EUS-EMG recordings compared to SI male mice, consistent with previous urodynamic findings reported in SCI mice [12, 15]. In the current study, the ratio of VV to bladder filling volume was used as a surrogate marker of VE as bladder filling volume was used as a substitution for bladder capacity, which is often difficult to measure accurately during awake CMG in mice. Thus, decreased VE and prolonged ICI in our study indicate impaired voiding function, likely due to SCI-induced DSD. Furthermore, the results of EUS-EMG recordings, showing shortened contraction and extended relaxation periods of EUS bursting after SCS, evident as a decreased ratio of AP to SP during voiding, suggest that DSD with tonic EUS activity contributes to reduced VE in SCI mice. SCS did not induce a non-physiological sustained relaxation of the EUS; rather, it restored coordinated physiological EUS bursting patterns with increased EUS relaxation periods during detrusor contractions, reflecting improved bladder–EUS coordination. Therefore, the observed improvements in VE and ICI following electrical SCS at the L3–4 spinal cord suggest an amelioration of NLUTD in SCI through the improvement of DSD. Because of the different EUS behavior between rodents and humans, which exhibits EUS bursting and relaxation during voiding, respectively, the findings in this study using mice may not fully be applicable to the DSD condition in SCI humans. However, despite species-specific differences in sphincter activation patterns, effective voiding in both species requires adequate detrusor sphincter coordination. From this perspective, SCS may improve inefficient voiding in SCI patients by enhancing the functional relaxation phase of the EUS during bladder contractions, rather than by replicating human-like sphincter relaxation patterns. In addition, although changes in lower urinary tract function of SI animals following SCS appear to be depending on the method or intensity of electrical stimulation [16], our approach for electrical SCS achieved the improvement of NLUTD in the SCI condition while minimizing changes in lower urinary tract function in healthy conditions.

In a previous report, epidural electrical stimulation of the L3 spinal cord has been shown to relax the EUS during voiding by inhibiting tonic EUS activity using female rats with chronic T8 SCI [10]. Another study using female rats with T9-level transection SCI showed that L3 spinal cord epidural stimulation could reduce ICI and induce rhythmic EUS activation that resulted in improvement of voiding performance [17]. Our present study more precisely evaluated the EUS behavior during SCS by analyzing the AP/SP ratio of EUS-EMG activity, and further demonstrated that electrical stimulation at the L3–4 spinal cord ameliorated DSD by prolonging the duration of EUS relaxation between tonic contractions, thereby improving inefficient voiding in a male mouse model of SCI. Moreover, enhancing the coordination between detrusor contraction and EUS relaxation through SCS at the L3–4 level suggests that the LSCC plays a critical role in the recovery of NLUTD following SCI. The LSCC at the L3–4 spinal cord has been recognized as a fundamental spinal circuit for generating bursting activity of EUS during normal voiding, and may also contribute to compensatory mechanisms when supraspinal inputs are disrupted after SCI [18].

Although the present study did not directly record neural activity from lumbosacral (L6–S1) interneurons or motoneurons, the functional improvements observed at the level of bladder– EUS coordination could indirectly suggest that L3–4 stimulation engages downstream lumbosacral output pathways via intersegmental spinal networks. Neuroplasticity and reactivation of spinal neural networks may be key processes for restoring function of the lower urinary tract impaired after SCI. Electrical SCS could promote these processes in SCI through the mechanisms including modulation of spinal reflex excitability, afferent activation, resulting in increased caudal circuit excitability, and/or facilitating the excitability of spinal motor neurons [19]. In terms of molecular mechanisms of electrical SCS, several possibilities have been explored, such as neuroplastic spinal cord remodeling, upregulation of neurotrophic factors, and modulation of neuroinflammation to facilitate functional recovery after SCI [20]. Thus, improvement of SCI-induced NLUTD by electrical stimulation of the LSCC likely involves similar mechanisms although further studies are needed to explore the chronic effects of SCS on spinal functional changes. Previous studies also reported that electrical stimulation of the L6 spinal cord in SCI rats induces only EUS tonic contraction, in contrast with the EUS relaxation observed during L3 SCS, highlighting the critical role of LSCC [10]. However, other studies have shown that electrical stimulation of the pudendal nerve or lower lumbar and upper sacral spinal segments could promote EUS relaxation and improve VE [21, 22]. Taken together with our observation of partial improvement in VE following LSCC stimulation, these findings suggest that effective modulation of bladder–EUS coordination after SCI likely involves interactions between LSCC-driven coordination and downstream lumbosacral motor circuits. Direct electrophysiological interrogation of L6–S1 interneurons and motoneurons during L3–4 stimulation will be an important focus of future studies to further delineate these downstream circuit mechanisms.

An increased number of NVCs during the storage phase is a characteristic finding of detrusor overactivity (DO) in SCI [13, 15]. In this study, we observed that L3–4 SCS was associated with a reduction in NVCs in SCI mice, suggesting a potential modulatory effect of SCS on neurogenic DO although underlying mechanisms of DO improvement after SCS are not clear. In chronic SCI animals, DO is not necessarily linked to DSD, and substantial plasticity of bladder afferent pathways and spinal reflex circuits is already present by 4-week postinjury [15]. Nevertheless, it is possible that LSCC-targeted stimulation indirectly stabilizes storage function by rebalancing excitatory and inhibitory interactions between bladder and sphincter control circuits. Although DO and DSD are dissociable phenomena in chronic SCI, altered sphincter activity and abnormal sensory feedback may facilitate bladder overactivity. Acute modulation of spinal coordinating networks may therefore reduce inappropriate reflex coupling that promotes NVCs. Supporting this possibility, thoracolumbar epidural stimulation, including targeting the L3 region, has been reported to modulate storage function in female rats with mild SCI [23]. In addition, DO after SCI involves neuroplastic changes in afferent pathways and reorganization of spinal reflex circuits [24]. Thus, while our observations are preliminary, they raise the possibility that LSCCtargeted SCS can influence abnormal reflex activity in NLUTD. Further studies are needed to clarify the underlying mechanisms and to distinguish direct effects on bladder afferent excitability from indirect effects through sphincter coordination.

There are some limitations in this study. First, we performed only acute experiments to examine the effects of SCS, which was applied for a short period of time during voiding. Therefore, it is not known whether chronic or repetitive SCS applications would be effective for the treatment of SCI-induced NLUTD. Secondly, we only analyzed the effects of SCS, during or immediately after the electrical stimulation; therefore, it is not known how long the SCS effects last after the stimulation ends. Future studies need to be planned to examine the longterm effect of SCS and the duration of post-SCS effects on SCIinduced NLUTD to establish the foundation for the clinical translation of SCS-based therapies in SCI. Thirdly, this study employed a complete T8–9 spinal transection model to allow controlled assessment of SCS-induced modulation of DSD by eliminating supraspinal influences. However, this model does not fully reflect the heterogeneous and often incomplete nature of clinical SCI. As a result, direct extrapolation of the present findings to all SCI populations should be made with caution. Future studies comparing the effects of LSCC-targeted SCS across different injury severities and lesion levels will be important to further define its translational relevance. Finally, the sample size of SI mice was relatively small and thus may have limited the statistical power to detect significant differences. The number of SI cohorts, which served primarily as physiological controls, was reduced for ethical reasons because they did not show apparent changes in EUS activity during or after SCS in preliminary experiments. In addition, multiple urodynamic parameters were analyzed, and secondary outcomes were evaluated exploratorily without formal correction for multiple comparisons, which should be considered when interpreting these results.

SCS targeting the L3–4 spinal cord, where the LSCC is located, can improve inefficient voiding and DSD by promoting EUS relaxation during the voiding reflex and also mitigate DO in chronic SCI. These findings suggest that SCS could restore impaired signal transduction in the LSCC after SCI, thereby improving NLUTD. This study advances our understanding of the functional role of the LSCC in post-SCI NLUTD and supports SCS as a potential therapeutic modality to address both storage and voiding dysfunctions following SCI.
