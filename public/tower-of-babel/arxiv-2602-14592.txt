FO and MSO Model Checking on Temporal Graphs
Michelle Döring, Jessica Enright, Laura Larios-Jones, George Skretas

Abstract
Algorithmic meta-theorems provide an important tool for showing tractability of graph problems on graph classes defined by structural restrictions. While such results are well established for static graphs, corresponding frameworks for temporal graphs are comparatively limited. In this work, we revisit past applications of logical meta-theorems to temporal graphs and develop an extended unifying logical framework. Our first contribution is the introduction of logical encodings for the parameters vertex-interval-membership (VIM) width and tree-interval-membership (TIM) width, parameters which capture the signature of vertex and component activity over time. Building on this, we extend existing monadic second-order (MSO) meta-theorems for bounded lifetime and temporal degree to the parameters VIM and TIM width, and establish novel first-order (FO) meta-theorems for all four parameters. Finally, we signpost a modular lexicon of reusable FO and MSO formulas for a broad range of temporal graph problems, and give an example. 1
Introduction

Many networks of interest, such as
public-transport systems or communication and interaction networks, consist of time-sensitive connections.
A standard abstraction of such networks is a
temporal graph

\mathcal{G}=(V,E,\lambda)
, that is, a static graph
G=(V,E)
as
footprint
together with an edge labelling
\lambda
assigning to each edge the time steps at which it is available.
This model strictly generalises static graphs, which are recovered as the special case in which all edges are available at all times.
While temporal graphs are more expressive, this comes at a computational costs:
many problems that are polynomial-time solvable on static graphs are computationally hard on temporal graphs, motivating an extensive line of work in parameterised complexity.

The core question in parameterised complexity is whether a problem is fixed-parameter tractable (
\mathtt{FPT}
) when parameterised by some measure that captures relevant properties of the input. For static graphs, one of the most studied
parameters is
treewidth
(
\mathsf{tw}
).
Two standard approaches to obtain
\mathtt{FPT}
algorithms parameterised by treewidth are:
(i) developing a dynamic program (see
[
16
, Section 7.3]
), or (ii) expressing the problem in a suitable
logic
and applying a logical
meta-theorem
.

First-order (FO)
and
monadic-second order (MSO)
logic provide formal languages for expressing combinatorial problems on graphs.
Logical meta-theorems unify results by establishing tractability not only for individual problems, but for entire classes of problems on entire classes of graphs, often based on the fragment of logic required.
The most prominent example is Courcelle’s theorem
[
14
]
, which implies that every property definable in MSO logic can be decided in linear time on graphs of bounded treewidth. On sparse graphs, FO logic admits analogous guarantees: Frick and Grohe
[
36
]
established tractability of FO model checking on planar graphs, and Grohe, Kreutzer, and Siebertz
[
37
]
extended this to the much broader nowhere dense graph class which includes planar graphs and graphs of bounded treewidth, degree, or expansion.
For a detailed background on logic and algorithmic meta-theorems we refer to the surveys
[
38
,
43
]
.

For temporal graphs, applications of logical meta-theorems so far exclusively use MSO formulations to obtain
\mathtt{FPT}
results under bounded treewidth combined with lifetime (largest time label) or temporal degree (number of temporal edges incident to one vertex)
[
18
,
20
,
28
,
29
,
32
,
39
,
44
,
55
]
.
In contrast, the FO meta-theorem route on planar or nowhere dense graphs has not yet been considered for temporal graphs.
Moreover, it is still common to obtain
\mathtt{FPT}
results via problem-specific dynamic programs with long technical proofs (see, e.g.,
[
11
,
41
]
), even in settings where a logical approach could yield a simpler and more modular proof.
Here, we provide a unifying framework for encoding temporal graphs along with example associated formulas. We also extend existing applications of logical meta-theorems to temporal graphs beyond bounded treewidth by enabling applications of FO meta-theorems for nowhere-dense footprints.

Beyond proof techniques, a central question is which parameters meaningfully capture temporal and structural complexity.
Recently, two new parameters have been introduced that are sensitive to the
order
of snapshots and thus capture temporal structure more intrinsically: vertex-interval-membership width (
\mathsf{vim}
) and tree-interval-membership width (
\mathsf{tim}
).
Enright et al.
[
27
]
study algorithmic tractability under these parameters, and give a procedural meta-algorithm for generating dynamic programs on a restricted class of problems: this is not a meta-theorem in the logical sense.
However, since bounded
\mathsf{vim}
and
\mathsf{tim}
bound the treewidth of the footprint, question arises whether it may be possible to prove a logical meta-theorem for these
measures.
We answer this affirmatively: we show that
\mathsf{vim}
and
\mathsf{tim}
admit encodings within our framework that preserve the structure needed for FO/MSO meta-theorems.

1.1
Our contribution

We provide a unifying framework including the pre-existing encodings for lifetime (
\Lambda
) and temporal degree (
\Delta^{t}
), and introduce novel encodings for the parameters
\mathsf{vim}
and
\mathsf{tim}
. These encodings are given in
\Cref
sec:graph_encodings.

In
\Cref
sec:temporal-meta, we show that all four encodings admit the application of FO and MSO meta-theorems. Concretely, we analyse the
Gaifman graph
induced by each encoding and prove that its structure is bounded by the respective parameter.
This allows us to lift the static meta-theorems to temporal graphs, yielding the following temporal meta-theorems: any property expressible in FO/MSO logic over the corresponding encoding yields an
\mathtt{FPT}
algorithm parameterised by the respective parameter.

Theorem 1
.

MSO Model Checking
on a temporal graph
\mathcal{G}=(G,\lambda)
is
\mathtt{FPT}
when parameterised by (i)
\mathsf{tw}+\Lambda
, (ii)
\mathsf{tw}+\Delta^{t}
, (iii)
\mathsf{vim}
, or (iv)
\mathsf{tim}
, where
\mathsf{tw}
is the treewidth of
G
.

Theorem 2
.

FO Model Checking
on a temporal graph
\mathcal{G}=(G,\lambda)
is
\mathtt{FPT}
when parameterised by (i)
\Lambda
if the footprint
G
is nowhere dense, (ii)
\Delta^{t}
, (iii)
\mathsf{vim}
, or (iv)
\mathsf{tim}
.

Finally,
in
\Cref
sec:cookbook,
we provide the
Logic Cookbook
a collection of FO and MSO formulas for over a dozen temporal graph problems studied in the literature.
Besides serving as a reusable reference for future work, this section highlights the simplicity and modularity of this logical tool. For example, in each of
[
6
,
10
,
11
,
27
,
41
]
, a substantial contribution is an
\mathtt{FPT}
result proven via a specialised dynamic program and lengthy correctness arguments; in our cookbook, the same
\mathtt{FPT}
result follows from a compact formula combined with the meta-theorem.

1.2
Related Work

The first work in logic that bears resemblance to temporal graph encodings is by Arnborg and Lagergren
[
2
]
, who provided an alternative proof of Courcelle’s MSO meta-theorem and, in particular, considered edge-labelled static graphs.
This perspective was later adopted by Zschoche et al.
[
55
]
to obtain an
\mathtt{FPT}
algorithm for
Temporal
(s,z)
-Separator
parameterised by
\mathsf{tw}+\Lambda
, introducing what we call the lifetime encoding.

Independently, Mans and Mathieson
[
47
]

studied different models of dynamic graphs (including temporal graphs) of bounded lifetime and proposed an encoding that was claimed to extend the MSO and FO meta-theorems of Courcelle and Frick–Grohe to this setting. However, their encoding contains a fundamental flaw, which was later identified and shown to be irreparable in the survey by Fomin et al.
[
32
]
. That survey gives a comprehensive exploration of possible notions of treewidth for temporal graphs (since no singular temporal treewidth definition exists), discussing both the lifetime and degree encodings in detail.
The degree encoding was introduced by Enright et al.
[
30
]
; they used it to show that
Temporal Reachability Edge Deletion
is in
\mathtt{FPT}
with respect to
\mathsf{tw}+\Delta^{t}
and solution size combined.

Subsequent work also uses an MSO approach:
Haag et al.
[
39
]
prove
\mathtt{FPT}
by
\mathsf{tw}+\Lambda
for
Temporal Feedback Edge/Connection Set
;
Kutner and Larios-Jones
[
44
]
show
\mathtt{FPT}
by
\mathsf{tw}+\Lambda
for
Temporal Reachability Dominating Set
;
Enright et al.
[
29
]
show
\mathtt{FPT}
by
\mathsf{tw}+\Delta^{t}
for
Counting Temporal Paths
via a counting variant of MSO;
Deligkas et al.
[
20
]
show
\mathtt{FPT}
by
\mathsf{tw}+\Lambda
, and
\mathsf{tw}+\Delta^{t}
for
Open
and
Closed Temporal Connected Component
; and
Mertzios et al.
[
48
]
use Courcelle-type reasoning over a
static
MSO formulation on the footprint for
Temporal Graph Realization with Fixed Stretch
.

2
Preliminaries

A
static graph

G=(V,E)
is a pair composed of a vertex set
V
and an edge set
E
, where every element of the edge set is a pair of vertices, and two vertices in an edge are said to be
adjacent
. A
clique
on
r
vertices, denoted
K_{r}
is a graph in which every pair of vertices is adjacent.

2.1
Temporal Graphs

A
temporal graph

\mathcal{G}=(V,E,\lambda)
consists of a static graph
G=(V,E)
, known as the
footprint
, and a
labelling
function
\lambda
.
The largest value of
\lambda
is referred to as the
lifetime

\Lambda
.
Alternatively, a temporal graph can be defined as a sequence
\mathcal{G}=\big(G_{t}=(V,E_{t})\big)_{t\in\Lambda}
of static
snapshots

G_{t}
.
The two representations are equivalent via:
E_{t}:=\{e\in E:t\in\lambda(e)\}
and
\lambda(e):=\{t\in\Lambda:e\in E_{t}\}
. A temporal graph is
(un)directed
if the footprint is (un)directed.
A pair
(e,t)
, where
e\in E
and
t\in\lambda(e)
, is a
temporal edge
with
label

t
. We denote the set of all temporal edges of
\mathcal{G}
by
\mathcal{E}
.
A
temporal path
is a sequence of temporal edges
\langle{(e_{i},t_{i})}\rangle
where
\langle{e_{i}}\rangle
forms a path in the footprint and the time labels
\langle{t_{i}}\rangle
are non-decreasing. If the time labels are strictly increasing, the path is
strict
; otherwise, it is
non-strict
.
If there is a temporal path from
u
to
v
, we say
u
reaches
v
.
A temporal graph is
temporally connected
if for all
u,v\in V
,
u
reaches
v
and vice versa.
A temporal graph where reachability is considered exclusively using (non-)strict paths is called a
(non-)strict temporal graph
.

2.2
Parameterised Complexity

We refer to the standard books for a basic overview of parameterised complexity theory
[
16
,
23
,
35
]
. At a high level, parameterised complexity studies the complexity of a problem with respect to its input size
n
and the size of a parameter
k
.
A problem is
fixed-parameter tractable
(
\mathtt{FPT}
) by
k
if it can be solved in time
f(k)\cdot\mathtt{poly}(n)
, where
f
is a computable function.
Showing that a problem is
\mathtt{W}[1]
-hard parameterised by
k
rules out the existence of such an
\mathtt{FPT}
algorithm under the assumption
\mathtt{W}[1]\neq\mathtt{FPT}
.
A less favourable, but still positive, outcome is an algorithm with an exponential running time
\mathcal{O}(n^{f(k)})
for some computable function
f
; problems admitting such algorithms belong to the class
\mathtt{XP}
.
A problem is
\mathtt{paraNP}
-hard if it remains
\mathtt{NP}
-hard even when the parameter
k
is constant. Thus,
\mathtt{paraNP}
-hardness excludes both
\mathtt{FPT}
and
\mathtt{XP}
algorithms under standard complexity assumptions.

2.3
Parameters for Temporal Graphs

We distinguish two families of parameters for temporal graphs:
static
parameters which constrain the structure of the (undirected) footprint and
temporal
parameters which bound the temporal structure locally or the activity over time. See
\Cref
fig:parameter-table for an overview.

Static graph parameters

We consider the maximum static degree
\Delta^{s}
, the pathwidth
\mathsf{pw}
, the treewidth
\mathsf{tw}
, and (for graph classes) nowhere denseness.

The
static degree
of a vertex
v
is defined as
\delta^{s}(v)=\lvert\{e\in E\colon v\in e\}\rvert
and
\Delta^{s}=\max_{v\in V}\delta(v)

denotes the
maximum static degree
of
\mathcal{G}
.

The
treewidth
of a graph measures how structurally close it is to being a tree. Small treewidth means that the graph can be organised into small, overlapping pieces with a tree-like skeleton.
Trees have treewidth
1
, an
m\times n
grid has treewidth
\max(m,n)
, and a complete graph on
k
vertices has treewidth
k-1
. The treewidth of a temporal graph always refers to the
undirected footprint
; for directed temporal graphs we take the treewidth of the underlying undirected graph of the directed footprint.

Definition 3

(Tree Decomposition, Treewidth
[
16
]
)
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be an undirected static graph.
A
tree decomposition
of
G
is a pair

(T,\{B_{u}\colon u\in V(T)\})
consisting of a tree
T
and a family of bags
B_{u}\subseteq V
such that

(i)

\bigcup_{u\in V(T)}B_{u}=V
,

(ii)

for every
e\in E
there exists
u\in V(T)
with
e\subseteq B_{u}
, and

(iii)

for every
v\in V
, the set
\{u\in V(T)\colon v\in B_{u}\}
induces a connected subtree of
T
.

The
width
of
T
is defined as
width(T):=\max_{u\in V(T)}\lvert B_{u}\rvert-1
.
The
treewidth
of
G
is

\mathsf{tw}(G):=\min\{width(T)\colon T\text{ is a tree decomposition of }G\}.

The
pathwidth

\mathsf{pw}(G)
is defined analogously to treewidth, with the restriction that the
path decomposition
is required to be a path instead of a tree. Hence
\mathsf{pw}(G)\geq\mathsf{tw}(G)
for all graphs
G
.
A class of graphs has
bounded treewidth
(respectively
bounded pathwidth
) if there exists a constant
k
such that every graph in the class satisfies
\mathsf{tw}(G)<k
(resp.
\mathsf{pw}(G)<k
).

Temporal graph parameters

We consider the lifetime
\Lambda
, the maximum temporal degree
\Delta^{t}
, the vertex-interval-membership width
\mathsf{vim}
, and the tree-interval-membership width
\mathsf{tim}
.

The
lifetime

\Lambda
of a temporal graph is its largest time label.

The
temporal degree
of a vertex
v
at time
t
is defined as
\delta^{t}(v)=\lvert\{(e,t)\colon v\in e,\;t\in\lambda(e)\}\rvert
and
\Delta^{t}=\max_{v\in V}\delta^{t}(v)
denotes the
maximum temporal degree
of
\mathcal{G}
.

The
vertex-interval-membership (VIM) width
and
tree-interval-membership (TIM) width
capture the maximum number of vertices that are simultaneously ‘participating’ in the temporal graph.
Both measures use the
activity-interval
of a vertex
v
, which starts at the first time at which
v
has an incident edge and ends after the last time at which
v
has an incident edge. For every snapshot outside this interval,
v
does not interact with the rest of the graph.

Definition 5

(activity-interval)
.

For
v\in V
, let
t_{min}(v)=\min\{\lambda(e)\colon e\in E,v\in e\}
and
t_{max}(v)=\max\{\lambda(e)\colon e\in E,v\in e\}
.
The
activity-interval of vertex

v
is defined as
A(v)=\left[t_{min}(v),t_{max}(v)\right]
.
Similarly, we define the activity-interval of a static edge
e\in E
as
A(e)=\left[\min\lambda(e),\max\lambda(e)\right]
.
We say
v
or
e
is
alive
at time
t
if
t\in A(v)
or
t\in A(e)
, respectively.

The VIM decomposition consists of a path with one bag per time step containing exactly the vertices which are alive at that time.
Note that every temporal graph has a unique VIM decomposition and that a temporally connected graph has VIM width exactly
n
. See
\Cref
fig:def-vim-tim left for an illustration of a VIM decomposition.

Definition 6

(Vertex-Interval-Membership Width (Bumpus and Meeks
[
6
]
))
.

The
vertex-interval-membership (VIM) decomposition
of a temporal graph
\mathcal{G}
is a sequence
(\pi_{t})_{t\in[\Lambda]}
of
bags
with
\pi_{t}=\{v\in V:t\in A(v)\}
.
The
vertex-interval-membership width
of a temporal graph
\mathcal{G}
is
\mathsf{vim}(\mathcal{G})=\max_{t\in[\Lambda]}\,\lvert\pi_{t}\rvert
.

The TIM width parameter generalises VIM width from a decomposition over a path over time to a tree. This is motivated by the fact that for many temporal problems, vertices which are alive at the same time but far from one another in the graph can be considered independently. The bags of a TIM decomposition have the property that any connected component of a snapshot is entirely contained in a bag at that time. Each bag at time
t
is connected to the bags at time
t-1
and
t+1
with which it shares vertices. This forms a tree-shape (see
\Cref
fig:def-vim-tim right).

Example from
[
27
]
of a VIM decomposition (
A
) and a TIM decomposition (
B
) of a temporal graph
\mathcal{G}
.

Definition 7

(Tree-Interval-Membership Width
[
27
]
)
.

A
tree-interval-membership (TIM) decomposition
of a temporal graph
\mathcal{G}
is a triple
(T,{\Pi},\tau)
consisting of a labelled directed tree
T
, a family of bags
{\Pi}=\{\pi_{i}\colon i\in V(T)\}
, and a node labelling function
\tau\colon V(T)\to[\Lambda]
such that:

(i)

for every
v\in V(\mathcal{G})
and
t\in[\Lambda]
there exists a unique
i\in V(T)
with
\tau(i)=t
and
v\in\pi_{i}
,

(ii)

for every
(uv,t)\in\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{G})
there exists an
i\in V(T)
with
\tau(i)=t
and
\{u,v\}\subseteq\pi_{i}
, and

(iii)

the directed edges of
T
are
E(T)=\{(i,j):\pi_{i}\cap\pi_{j}\neq\emptyset\text{ and }\tau(i)=\tau(j)+1\}
.

The
width
of a TIM decomposition is defined as
width(T):=\max\{|\pi_{i}|\colon i\in V(T)\}
.
The
TIM width
of a temporal graph
\mathcal{G}
is
\mathsf{tim}(\mathcal{G}):=\min\{width(T)\colon T\text{ is a TIM decomposition of }\mathcal{G}\}
.

Since the VIM bags form a (not necessarily optimal) path decomposition of the undirected footprint, it follows that
\mathsf{pw}(\mathcal{G})\leq\mathsf{vim}(\mathcal{G})
(and hence
\mathsf{tw}(\mathcal{G})\leq\mathsf{pw}(\mathcal{G})\leq\mathsf{vim}(\mathcal{G})
). Analogously, the TIM bags form a tree decomposition of the undirected footprint, and thus
\mathsf{tw}(\mathcal{G})\leq\mathsf{tim}(\mathcal{G})
.

2.4
Logic in Static Graphs

First-order logic
and
monadic second-order logic
provide a uniform language to express graph properties and, in turn, define whole graph classes by single descriptive sentences.

These logics are interpreted over
relational structures
. A relational structure
\mathcal{S}
consists of a finite universe
U
together with a finite set of relations
\mathfrak{R}
on
U
. Before formally introducing FO and MSO, we specify how a static graph
G=(V,E)
is represented as such a structure.

Definition 8
.

Let
G=(V,E)
and
D=(V,A)
be an undirected and a directed static graph, respectively. The undirected relational structure
\lfloor G\rfloor
is defined as:

universe:

U=V\cup E

unary relations:

V\subseteq U
,
E\subseteq U

relations:

.

•

\mathsf{inc}\subseteq E\times V
, where
\mathsf{inc}(e,v)\Leftrightarrow v\in e
.

In the directed relational structure
\lfloor D\rfloor
,
E
is replaced by
A
in the universe and unary relations, and
\mathsf{inc}
is replaced by (i)
\mathsf{source}\subseteq A\times V
, where
\mathsf{source}((x,y),v)\Leftrightarrow v=x
,
and (ii)
\mathsf{target}\subseteq A\times V
, where
\mathsf{target}((x,y),v)\Leftrightarrow v=y
.

Definition 9
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a relational structure with universe
U
and relations
\mathfrak{R}
.

Formulas in
monadic second-order logic (MSO)
use two types of
variables
: first-order variables
x,y,z,\dots
ranging over elements of
U

3
Temporal Graph Encodings

To apply logical meta-theorems to temporal graphs, we encode them as relational structures resembling the static graph encoding with additional structure to represent time.
Different encodings make different aspects of the temporal structure explicit and, crucially, affect the structure of the resulting representation.

We present four relational encodings—two known and two novel—each tailored to a specific notion of static and temporal sparsity. These encodings serve as the foundation for the temporal meta-theorems in
\Cref
sec:temporal-meta, yielding tractability of FO/MSO model checking under bounded (i)
\Lambda
, (ii)
\Delta^{t}
, (iii)
\mathsf{vim}
, and (iv)
\mathsf{tim}
possibly combined with structural static parameters.

(Non)strict, (un)directed variants.
We present all encodings explicitly for
strict, undirected
temporal graphs.
Encodings for
directed
temporal graphs can be obtained by replacing the incidence relation with source and target relations

\mathsf{source},\mathsf{target}\subseteq E\times V
, where
\mathsf{source}(e,v)
(resp.,
\mathsf{target}(e,v)
) holds if and only if
v
is the tail (resp., head) of
e
.
The distinction between strict and non-strict temporal reachability is handled as follows. In the degree encoding (
\Cref
def:degree_encoding), strictness is enforced at the structural level via the possible-successor relation (using
t^{\prime}<t
for strict and
t^{\prime}\leq t
for non-strict reachability). In all other encodings, strictness is enforced at the formula level (e. g., when defining a logical formula for temporal paths).

Lifetime encoding

The lifetime encoding represents a temporal graph as a time-labelled static graph, mirroring the standard mathematical definition.
The universe contains the vertices, temporal edges, and time steps. An incidence relation identifies the endpoints of each temporal edge, while a presence relation records when a temporal edge is present. A total linear order on the time steps encodes the global timeline.

Definition 3.15

(lifetime encoding)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}=((V,E),\lambda)=(V,\mathcal{E})
be an undirected strict temporal graph. The
lifetime encoding

\lfloor\mathcal{G}\rfloor_{\Lambda}
is the relational structure defined as:

universe

U=V\cup\mathcal{E}\cup L

unary relations

V(\cdot),\mathcal{E}(\cdot),L(\cdot)

binary relations

•

\mathsf{inc}\subseteq\mathcal{E}\times V

where
\mathsf{inc}((uv,t),x)\Leftrightarrow x=u\text{ or }x=v
,

•

\mathsf{pres}\subseteq\mathcal{E}\times L

where
\mathsf{pres}((uv,t),t^{\prime})\Leftrightarrow t^{\prime}=t
,

•

<^{T}\subseteq L\times L

where
t_{1}<^{T}t_{2}\Leftrightarrow t_{1}<t_{2}
.

Degree encoding

The degree encoding abstracts away from the concrete time labels of edges and instead focuses on the
relative order
of neighbouring temporal edges.
Rather than representing when an edge appears, the encoding captures which temporal edges can be taken consecutively along a temporal path.
In contrast to static graphs – where succession is determined simply by shared endpoints – this relation additionally depends on the order at which the edges are present.
As a result, the encoding directly represents temporal reachability via a partial order on temporal edges.

Definition 3.16

(degree encoding)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}=(V,\mathcal{E})
be an undirected strict temporal graph. The
degree encoding

\lfloor\mathcal{G}\rfloor_{\Delta^{t}}
is the relational structure defined as:

universe

U=V\cup\mathcal{E}

unary relations

V(\cdot),\mathcal{E}(\cdot)

binary relations

•

\mathsf{inc}\subseteq\mathcal{E}\times V
, where

\mathsf{inc}((uv,t),x)\Leftrightarrow x=u\text{ or }x=v
,

•

\mathsf{psuc}\subseteq\mathcal{E}\times\mathcal{E}
, where

\mathsf{psuc}((e_{1},t_{1}),(e_{2},t_{2}))\Leftrightarrow t_{1}<t_{2}\text{ and there exists }v\in V\text{ such that }\mathsf{inc}(e_{1},v)\text{ and }\mathsf{inc}(e_{2},v)
.

\lfloor\mathcal{G}\rfloor_{\Delta^{t}}

Two such temporal-edge-nodes are related in the possible-successor relation whenever the corresponding temporal edges share an endpoint and satisfy the temporal order.
This may induce a clique among all temporal edges incident to the same vertex.

VIM encoding

The VIM encoding directly represents the VIM decomposition of the temporal graph at the level of the relational structure.
The encoding contains a sequence of
bag elements
, one for each bag of the VIM decomposition.
Vertices and temporal edges are related to the bag elements in which they are alive, and successive bags are connected by a linear successor relation.
In contrast to the lifetime encoding, the bags are not
pairwise
adjacent; this avoids introducing a clique over time and ensures that the density of the structure is bounded by the VIM width of the graph.

Definition 3.17

(VIM encoding)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}=(V,\mathcal{E})
be a directed strict temporal graph with VIM decomposition
(\pi_{t})_{t\in[\Lambda]}
. The
relational VIM structure

\lfloor\mathcal{G}\rfloor_{\mathsf{vim}}
is defined as:

universe

U=V\cup\mathcal{E}\cup{\Pi}

unary relations

V(\cdot),\mathcal{E}(\cdot),{\Pi}(\cdot)

binary relations

•

\mathsf{inc}\subseteq\mathcal{E}\times V

where
\mathsf{inc}((uv,t),x)\Leftrightarrow x=u\text{ or }x=v
,

•

\mathsf{bag}\subseteq V\times{\Pi}

where
\mathsf{bag}(v,\pi_{t})\Leftrightarrow v\in\pi_{t}
,

•

\mathsf{pres}\subseteq\mathcal{E}\times{\Pi}

where
\mathsf{pres}((uv,t),\pi_{i})\Leftrightarrow\tau(i)=t\text{ and }u,v\in\pi_{i}
,

•

\mathsf{next}\subseteq{\Pi}\times{\Pi}

where
\mathsf{next}(\pi_{i},\pi_{j})\Leftrightarrow\tau(i)+1=\tau(j)
.

TIM encoding

Just as a TIM decomposition generalises the VIM decomposition of a temporal graph by allowing multiple bags per time, the TIM encoding is a generalisation of the VIM encoding.
While a VIM decomposition induces a path over the bags, a TIM decomposition organizes the bags in a tree structure. In a TIM decomposition, vertices in the same connected component of a snapshot must be in the same bag at that time, while those in different connected components may be in different bags.
Note that, since times are not part of the universe of this encoding, we cannot determine the time associated to a given bag without a formula (of length possibly lifetime) which finds its distance from a leaf bag labelled with time 1. This forces us to focus on the
relative
times of pairs of bags, as we do in the degree encoding.

Definition 3.18

(TIM encoding)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}=(V,\mathcal{E})
be a directed strict temporal graph with TIM decomposition
(T,{\Pi},\tau)
. The
relational TIM structure

\lfloor\mathcal{G}\rfloor_{\mathsf{tim}}
is defined as:

universe

U=V\cup\mathcal{E}\cup{\Pi}

unary relations

V(\cdot),\mathcal{E}(\cdot),{\Pi}(\cdot)

binary relations

•

\mathsf{inc}\subseteq\mathcal{E}\times V

where
\mathsf{inc}((uv,t),x)\Leftrightarrow x=u\text{ or }x=v
,

•

\mathsf{bag}\subseteq V\times{\Pi}

where
\mathsf{bag}(v,\pi_{i})\Leftrightarrow v\in\pi_{i}
,

•

\mathsf{pres}\subseteq\mathcal{E}\times{\Pi}

where
\mathsf{pres}((uv,t),\pi_{i})\Leftrightarrow\tau(i)=t\text{ and }u,v\in\pi_{i}
,

•

\mathsf{next}\subseteq{\Pi}\times{\Pi}

where
\mathsf{next}(\pi_{i},\pi_{j})\Leftrightarrow\tau(i)+1=\tau(j)\text{ and }(\pi_{i},\pi_{j})\in E(T)
.

4
Temporal Meta–Theorems

The temporal relational encodings from
\Cref
sec:graph_encodings allow the application of the FO and MSO model checking meta-theorems.
Concretely, we analyse the structure of these encodings via the
Gaifman graph
of each relational structure.
This yields temporal meta-theorems linking logical expressibility to algorithmic tractability: once a temporal property is expressed in FO/MSO over a suitable encoding, fixed-parameter tractability follows from the sparsity of the corresponding Gaifman graphs.

Definition 4.19

(Gaifman graph)
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be any finite relational structure with universe
U
and some set of relations
\mathfrak{R}=\{R_{1},R_{2},\dots\}
.
The
Gaifman graph

\gaifman(\mathcal{S})
is a static, simple, undirected graph with vertex set
U
and an edge
\{a,b\}
if and only if (i)
a\neq b
and (ii) there exist a relation
R\in\mathfrak{R}
with
k\geq 2
arguments and a tuple
(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})\in R
such that
a,b\in\{a_{1},\dots,a_{k}\}
.

The illustrations in
\Cref
fig:gaifman-degree_encoding,fig:gaifman-lifetime_encoding,fig:gaifman-vim_encoding,fig:gaifman-tim_encoding depict the Gaifman graphs of our four encodings.

In the next two subsections, we prove that for each of the parameters
\Lambda
,
\Delta^{t}
,
\mathsf{vim}
, and
\mathsf{tim}
, the Gaifman graph of the corresponding encoding preserves bounded treewidth (for MSO) and nowhere denseness (for FO), up to a factor depending only on the parameter.

4.1
Temporal Encodings preserve Treewidth

MSO model checking is fixed-parameter tractable on graph classes of bounded treewidth (
\Cref
thm:MSO-MC).
To apply the MSO meta-theorem to temporal graphs, it suffices to show that the Gaifman graph of each encoding has bounded treewidth whenever the corresponding parameter
\Lambda
,
\Delta^{t}
,
\mathsf{vim}
or
\mathsf{tim}
is bounded.
This has previously been established for the lifetime encoding
[
39
]
and the degree encoding
[
28
]
; we provide proofs for completeness and unified notation. For the VIM encoding and TIM encoding introduced in this work, we give novel proofs.

All proofs in this section follow the same structural pattern. We start from a (tree, VIM, or TIM) decomposition and locally adjust its bags to obtain a tree decomposition of the Gaifman graph. The proofs differ only in the underlying decomposition and in the resulting blow-up of the bag sizes. Since the individual arguments are largely parallel, readers familiar with one proof may safely skim the others, focusing on the encoding-specific bag constructions and width bounds.

(i)

First, we show

•

with

•

•

and

.

(ii)

\mathsf{inc}\subseteq\mathcal{E}\times V
: if

is an endpoint of
\varepsilon=(e,t)

Second, we show the statement for the TIM encoding. Recall that

Lemma 4.26

Let

\mathsf{tw}\big(\gaifman(\lfloor\mathcal{G}\rfloor_{\mathsf{tim}})\big)\ \leq\ \mathsf{tim}(\mathcal{G})^{2}+3\mathsf{tim}(\mathcal{G})-1\quad\text{and, in particular,}\quad\mathsf{tw}\big(\gaifman(\lfloor\mathcal{G}\rfloor_{\mathsf{tim}})\big)\in\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{tim}(\mathcal{G})^{2}).

Let

4.2
Temporal Encodings preserve Nowhere Denseness

FO model checking is fixed-parameter tractable on nowhere dense graph classes (
\Cref
thm:FO-MC), a broad family that includes, for example, planar graphs, graphs of bounded expansion, and graphs of bounded treewidth.
To lift FO meta-theorems to temporal graphs, it therefore suffices to show that the Gaifman graphs arising from our relational encodings form nowhere dense classes.

To the best our knowledge, FO meta-theorems have previously been considered for temporal graphs only in the work by Mans and Mathieson
[
47
]
who studied a variation of the lifetime encoding.
At the time, tractability of FO model checking was known only for the more restricted class of bounded-expansion graphs, which forms a proper subclass of nowhere dense graph classes.
While their approach aimed at establishing preservation of sparsity, a flaw in the encoding and the associated argument was later identified by Fomin et al.
[
32
]
:
Mans and Mathieson consider a generalisation of temporal graphs where the vertex set may vary over time. In their encoding of these
dynamic
graphs, the universe contains an element for every vertex appearance (a temporal vertex), rather than one element per static vertex as in our encoding.
They claimed one could identify whether two temporal vertices belong to the same underlying vertex using a constant-length formula. Fomin et al.
[
32
]
later showed that this would imply the existence of a constant-length formula for 3-colourability on constant-treewidth graphs (NP-hard by Dailey
[
17
]
), which would contradict the assumption that
P\neq NP
.

In what follows, we show that all four encodings preserve nowhere denseness when their respective parameter is bounded.
Among the four encodings, the lifetime encoding requires the most care. For the proof we require additional definitions and notation for depth-
r
topological minors.

Depth-
r
topological minors via models.

Recall that for static graphs
G
and
H
, we write
H\preceq_{r}G
, if some
r
-subdivision of
H
is isomorphic to a subgraph of
G
. An
r
-subdivision is obtained by replacing a set of edges in
H
by paths of length at most
r+1
such that the
internal vertices
(all but the endpoints) of all paths are disjoint.
Equivalently,
H\preceq_{r}G
holds if
G
contains a
depth-
r
model
of
H
, which is a pair
\eta=(\eta_{V},\eta_{E})
where

1.

\eta_{V}\colon V(H)\to V(G)
is an injective mapping, and

2.

for every edge
ab\in E(H)
,
\eta_{E}(ab)
is a simple path in
G
, called
model-path
, of length at most
r+1
which connects
\eta_{V}(a)
and
\eta_{V}(b)
,

such that for all distinct edges
ab,cd\in E(H)
, the paths
\eta_{E}(ab)
and
\eta_{E}(cd)
are internally vertex-disjoint, and no internal vertex of any path
\eta_{E}(ab)
lies in
\eta_{V}(V(H))
.
The vertices in
\eta_{V}(V(H))
are called the
branch-vertices
of the model.
Intuitively, the model represents how the graph
H
can be “found” inside
G
after stretching each edge of
H
into a short path of length at most
r+1
.
The process of
contracting
an edge
uv
yields the graph
G/uv
obtained by identifying
u
and
v
into a new vertex that is adjacent to every vertex that was adjacent to
u
or
v
in
G
, and then deleting self-loops and parallel edges (so that the result remains simple).

Lemma 4.28
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a nowhere dense class of static graphs with denseness-function
\mathsf{d}
, and consider the class of all temporal graphs
\mathcal{C^{\prime}}
with footprint in
\mathcal{C}
and maximum lifetime
\Lambda
.
Then the class of Gaifman graphs
\gaifman(\lfloor\mathcal{G}\rfloor_{\Lambda})
for each
\mathcal{G}\in\mathcal{C^{\prime}}
is nowhere dense. In particular,
the function

g(r):=\mathsf{d}(\lceil r/2\rceil)+2\Lambda

is a denseness-function for this class.

Proof 4.29
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a nowhere dense static graph class with denseness-function
\mathsf{d}
, and let
\mathcal{C^{\prime}}
be the class of all temporal graphs
\mathcal{G}=(G,\lambda)
with lifetime at most
\Lambda
and whose footprint
G
lies in
\mathcal{C}
.
We analyze the class of Gaifman graphs
\{\gaifman(\lfloor\mathcal{G}\rfloor_{\Lambda})\colon\mathcal{G}\in\mathcal{C^{\prime}}\}
.

Fix
\mathcal{G}\in\mathcal{C^{\prime}}
and let
G_{\gaifman}:=\gaifman(\lfloor\mathcal{G}\rfloor_{\Lambda})

Recall that the vertices of
G_{\gaifman}
are
V\cup\mathcal{E}\cup L
and:

•

edges between
V
and
\mathcal{E}
encode the incidence relation via
\mathsf{inc}
,

•

edges inside
L
form a clique (from the order
<^{T}
), and

•

edges between
\mathcal{E}
and
L
encode the presence relation via
\mathsf{pres}
.

For an illustration of
G_{\gaifman}
refer back to
\Cref
fig:gaifman-lifetime_encoding. We will refer to a vertex
a\in V\cup\mathcal{E}\cup L
of
G_{\gaifman}
as vertex node, temporal-edge node, or time node, respectively.

Step 1:
G_{\gaifman}-L
is nowhere dense with
\mathsf{d}_{\gaifman-L}(r):=\mathsf{d}(\lceil r/2\rceil)
.
Consider
G_{\gaifman}-L
.
This graph can be obtained from the footprint
G
by subdividing every edge
uv\in E(G)
once (resulting in a temporal-edge node
\varepsilon
) and multiplying this
u-\varepsilon-v
path for every temporal edge
(uv,t)\in\mathcal{E}
. Conversely,
G
can be obtained from
G_{\gaifman}-L
by contracting every temporal-edge node with one of its neighbours.

We show that if we can find an
r
-subdivision of a clique
K_{q}
in
G_{\gaifman}-L
, then there is an
\lceil r/2\rceil
-subdivision of
K_{q}
in
G
.
From this we conclude that
\mathsf{d}_{\gaifman-L}(r):=\mathsf{d}(\lceil r/2\rceil)
is a denseness function of
G_{\gaifman}-L
.

Fix
r\in\mathbb{N}
and
q\geq 4
, and assume that
K_{q}\preceq_{r}(G_{\gaifman}-L)
. Let
\eta
be a depth-
r
model of
K_{q}
in
G_{\gaifman}-L
.
In
G_{\gaifman}-L
, every temporal-edge node
\varepsilon=(uv,t)\in\mathcal{E}
has degree 2 and is adjacent exactly to the nodes
u
and
v
. Consequently, no temporal-edge node in
\mathcal{E}
can be a branch-vertex of a depth-
r
clique model when
q\geq 4
, and hence all branch-vertices must lie in
V
.
Additionally, each model-path of length
\ell
in
G_{\gaifman}-L
must alternate between
V
and
\mathcal{E}
, and therefore becomes a path of length at most
\lceil\ell/2\rceil
on
V
in
G
(after contracting all temporal-edge nodes with one of its neighbours).
Internal vertex-disjointness of the model-paths is preserved under contraction.
Thus, we obtain a depth-
\lceil r/2\rceil
model of
K_{q}
in
G
, and therefore
K_{q}\preceq_{\lceil r/2\rceil}G
.

By definition of
\mathsf{d}
as a denseness-function for
\mathcal{C}
, we have
K_{\mathsf{d}(\lceil r/2\rceil)}\not\preceq_{\lceil r/2\rceil}G
for all
r
. By our arguments above, this implies
K_{\mathsf{d}_{\gaifman-L}(r)}=K_{\mathsf{d}(\lceil r/2\rceil)}\not\preceq_{r}(G_{\gaifman}-L)
for all
r\in\mathbb{N}
, and hence

\mathsf{d}_{\gaifman-L}(r)=\mathsf{d}(\lceil r/2\rceil)
is a denseness-function for the class of all such graphs
G_{\gaifman}-L
.

Step 2:
G_{\gaifman}
is nowhere dense with
\mathsf{d}_{\gaifman}(r):=\mathsf{d}_{\gaifman-L}(r)+2\Lambda
.
Towards contradiction assume
K_{\ell}\preceq_{r}G_{\gaifman}

for
\ell:=\mathsf{d}_{\gaifman-L}(r)+2\Lambda
, and let
\eta
be a depth-
r
model of
K_{\ell}
in
G_{\gaifman}
.

Step 1 shows that
G_{\gaifman}-L
cannot contain a depth-
r
topological minor isomorphic to
K_{\mathsf{d}_{\gaifman-L}(r)}
.
Thus, the only way the model
\eta
can “beat” this bound is by exploiting clique of time nodes
L
. Importantly,
L
can help in two ways:
(i) by hosting branch-vertices, and
(ii) by serving as internal vertices on model-paths for clique edges. We show that both effects are limited by
|L|=\Lambda
.

First, we consider the time nodes in
L
that are branch-vertices.
Let
B
be the set of branch-vertices of
\eta
and denote by
B_{L}:=B\cap L
the branch-vertices in
L
and by
B_{\neg L}:=B\setminus L
the branch-vertices in
G_{\gaifman}-L
.
Since
|L|=\Lambda
, we have
|B_{L}|\leq\Lambda
and therefore
\lvert B_{\neg L}\rvert\geq\ell-\Lambda=\mathsf{d}_{\gaifman-L}(r)+\Lambda
.

Next, we consider the time nodes in
L
that are used by model-paths.
Let
F
be the set of clique edges
xy
whose model path contains a time node of
L
as an internal vertex. Since model-paths have to be internally disjoint, each time node in
L
can be used by the model-path of at most one clique edge, and therefore
\lvert F\rvert\leq\lvert L\setminus B_{L}\rvert\leq\Lambda
.
Let
S
be the set of endpoints (in
B_{\neg L}
) of the edges in
F
. Then
\lvert S\rvert\leq 2\lvert F\rvert\leq 2\Lambda
.

Define
B^{\prime}:=B_{\neg L}\setminus S
as the set of branch vertices outside
L
whose model-paths do not use
L
.
It holds
\lvert B^{\prime}\rvert\geq\lvert B_{\neg L}\rvert-2\Lambda\geq\mathsf{d}_{\gaifman-L}(r)
.
We claim that restricting
\eta
to the branch-vertices
B^{\prime}
yields a depth-
r
model of
K_{\lvert B^{\prime}\rvert}
in
G_{\gaifman}-L
.
To that end, consider any two distinct
x,y\in B^{\prime}
.
Then
x,y\notin L
and
xy\notin F
(otherwise
x\in S
or
y\in S
). Hence the model-path for
xy
does not go through
L
and must lie entirely in
G_{\gaifman}-L
.
Thus,
G_{\gaifman}-L
contains
K_{|B^{\prime}|}
as a depth-
r
topological minor, and in particular contains
K_{\mathsf{d}_{\gaifman-L}(r)}
as a depth-
r
topological minor. This is a contradiction to
\mathsf{d}_{\gaifman-L}
being a denseness-function for
G_{\gaifman}-L
.

Consequently,
K_{\mathsf{d}_{\gaifman-L}(r)+2\Lambda}\not\preceq_{r}G_{\gaifman}
for all
r\in\mathbb{N}
, and hence
g(r)=\mathsf{d}_{\gaifman-L}(r)+2\Lambda
is a denseness-function for the class of all such graphs
G_{\gaifman}
.

Step 3: Conclusion.
In summary, for every
r\in\mathbb{N}
and every graph
G_{\gaifman}=\gaifman(\lfloor\mathcal{G}\rfloor_{\Lambda})
arising from a temporal graph
\mathcal{G}\in\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
, we have
K_{\mathsf{d}(\lceil r/2\rceil)+2\Lambda}\not\preceq_{r}G_{\gaifman}
.
Equivalently, the function
g(r):=\mathsf{d}(\lceil r/2\rceil)+2\Lambda
is a denseness-function for the class
\{\gaifman(\lfloor\mathcal{G}\rfloor_{\Lambda}):\mathcal{G}\in\mathcal{C^{\prime}}\}
.

For the degree encoding, we show a stronger preservation result, namely that the maximum static degree of the Gaifman graph is bounded by the temporal degree of
\mathcal{G}
. Since graph classes of bounded maximum degree are nowhere dense, this immediately implies the desired result.

Lemma 4.30
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a nowhere dense class of static graphs with denseness-function
\mathsf{d}
, and consider the class of all temporal graphs
\mathcal{C^{\prime}}
with footprint in
\mathcal{C}
and maximum temporal degree
\Delta^{t}
.
For
\mathcal{G}\in\mathcal{C^{\prime}}
, let
H:=\operatorname{Gf}(\lfloor\mathcal{G}\rfloor_{\Delta^{t}})
be the Gaifman graph of the degree encoding of
\mathcal{G}
. Then
\Delta(H)\leq 2\Delta^{t}
and, consequently, the class of all such graphs
H
is nowhere dense.

Proof 4.31
.

Let
G_{\gaifman}:=\gaifman(\lfloor\mathcal{G}\rfloor_{\Delta^{t}})
be the Gaifman graph of the degree encoding
\lfloor\mathcal{G}\rfloor_{\Delta^{t}}
.
Recall from the degree encoding that the vertices of
G_{\gaifman}
are
V\cup\mathcal{E}
, where

\mathcal{E}
is the set of temporal edges.
The binary relations are:

•

\mathsf{source}((e,t),v)
and
\mathsf{target}((e,t),v)
, connecting every temporal edge to its endpoints; and

•

(e_{1},t_{1})\mathsf{psuc}(e_{2},t_{2})
, which holds iff the two temporal edges share an endpoint and
t_{1}\prec t_{2}
.

Thus, in the Gaifman graph
G_{\gaifman}
:

•

each temporal edge
(e,t)
is adjacent to its two endpoints; and

•

two temporal edges
(e_{1},t_{1})
and
(e_{2},t_{2})
are adjacent if and only if they share a vertex in
V
and their times are comparable (which is always the case, since
\prec
is a total order).

We can now bound the maximum degree of
G_{\gaifman}
in terms of the maximum temporal degree
\Delta^{t}
.

Let
v\in V
. By definition of
\Delta^{t}
, there are at most
\Delta^{t}
temporal edges incident with
v
, so in
G_{\gaifman}
the vertex
v
has at most
\Delta^{t}
neighbours (all of them in
\mathcal{E}
). There are no edges in
G_{\gaifman}
between two vertices of
V
.
Hence
\deg_{H}(v)\leq\Delta^{t}
.

Let
\varepsilon=(e,t)\in\mathcal{E}
where
e
has endpoints
u,v\in V
(for directed
graphs,
u
is the source and
v
the target; the argument is identical).
Then the neighbours of
\varepsilon
in
G_{\gaifman}
are
(1) its endpoints
u
and
v
(two vertices in
V
); and
(2) all other temporal edges incident with
u
or with
v
(the
\mathsf{psuc}
-neighbours).
By the definition of
\Delta^{t}
, there are at most
\Delta^{t}-1
temporal edges at
u
different from
(e,t)
, and at most
\Delta^{t}-1
such edges at
v
.
Therefore
\varepsilon
has at most
(\Delta^{t}-1)+(\Delta^{t}-1)
neighbours in
\mathcal{E}
, plus its two endpoint vertices.
In particular,
\deg_{H}(x)\leq 2+2(\Delta^{t}-1)\leq 2\Delta^{t}
.

Combining both cases, the maximum degree of
G_{\gaifman}
is bounded by
\Delta(G_{\gaifman})\leq 2\Delta^{t}
.

For the VIM encoding and the TIM encoding the nowhere dense preservation again follows from a stronger result: the parameters
\mathsf{vim}
and
\mathsf{tim}
upper bound the treewidth of the footprint of a temporal graph. Since graph classes with bounded treewidth are nowhere dense, the desired result follows directly from the treewidth bounds established in
\Cref
lem:vim_enc-preserves-tw,lem:tim_enc-preserves-tw.

Corollary 4.32
.

Let
\mathbf{C}
be a class of temporal graph with VIM width at most
\mathsf{vim}
. Then the class of all Gaifman graphs
\{\gaifman(\lfloor\mathcal{G}\rfloor_{\mathsf{vim}})\,\colon\,\mathcal{G}\in\mathbf{C}\}
is nowhere dense.
Equivalently, let
\mathbf{C^{\prime}}
be a class of temporal graph with TIM width at most
\mathsf{tim}
. Then the class of all Gaifman graphs
\{\gaifman(\lfloor\mathcal{G}\rfloor_{\mathsf{tim}})\,\colon\,\mathcal{G}\in\mathbf{C^{\prime}}\}
is nowhere dense.

4.3
Meta-Theorems for FO and MSO

We can now state the main algorithmic consequences for temporal model checking.
They imply that when a temporal property (such as existence of a connected component, of a matching, or of vertex-disjoint paths) can be expressed in an FO, respectively MSO, formula using the
\mathsf{X}
-encoding, then the corresponding computational problem is
\mathtt{FPT}
on nowhere dense, respectively bounded treewidth, graphs when parameterised by
\mathsf{X}
.

Theorem 4.33
.

MSO MC
on a temporal graph
\mathcal{G}=(G,\lambda)
is
\mathtt{FPT}
when parameterised by
(i)
\mathsf{tw}+\Lambda
, (ii)
\mathsf{tw}+\Delta^{t}
, (iii)
\mathsf{vim}
, or (iv)
\mathsf{tim}
.

Proof 4.34
.

The statement follows by combining the MSO model-checking meta-theorem on bounded-treewidth Gaifman graphs (
\Cref
thm:MSO-MC)
with
\Cref
lem:lifetime_enc-preserves-tw,lem:tdegree_enc-preserves-tw,lem:vim_enc-preserves-tw,lem:tim_enc-preserves-tw.

Theorem 4.35
.

FO MC
on a temporal graph
\mathcal{G}=(G,\lambda)
is
\mathtt{FPT}
when parameterised by
(i)
\Lambda
if the footprint
G
is nowhere dense, (ii)
\Delta^{t}

5.2
Temporal Paths, Walks, and Reachability

Reachability is a fundamentally global graph property. While first-order logic (FO) can express the existence of a path of fixed length
k
between two vertices, the length of the corresponding FO formula grows with
k
. It is a classical result that FO is not able to express reachability, i. e., the existence of a path of arbitrary length between two vertices; see for example
[
45
, Chapter 3.6]
.

In contrast, monadic second-order logic (MSO) can express reachability by quantifying over vertex or edge sets and enforcing the connectivity properties of a path in a single formula of constant size. This strict separation between FO and MSO with respect to reachability underlies the use of MSO as a logical framework for path-based properties in graph algorithms and meta-theorems.

For the four temporal encodings the reachability can be expressed using MSO, shown in
\Cref
subsubsec:MSO-reachability.
For lifetime encoding and non-strict temporal reachability the FO impossibility directly translates since otherwise one could express static reachability of a static graph
G
via a temporal graph
(G,{e,1\colon e\in E})
and then using the temporal formula. For strict temporal reachability, this is not directly true and for lifetime encoding one can even express in FO.

5.2.1
MSO formulas for temporal reachability

Temporal reachability is expressed by quantifying a set
P
of temporal edges and enforcing local degree conditions: the designated endpoints
u
and
v
are required to have degree
1
in
P
, every other vertex incident at an edge in
P
must have degree
2
in
P
, and all remaining vertices have degree
0
. These conditions guarantee that
P
forms a simple
u
–
v
path in the static footprint of the temporal graph. This static path is lifted to a temporal one, by an additional condition that the temporal edges in

5.8.5
A note on realisation and modification problems

We refer to problems in which we are given a static graph to label with times as a
realisation
problem, and a temporal graph which we are required to add/remove (temporal) vertices or edges as a
modification
problem. Realisation and modification problems have been expressed in MSO by capturing a property of the underlying graph which forces/forbids a temporal graph to have some (un)desired property
[
21
,
28
,
44
,
48
]
.

Note that existence of a labelling function can be encoded using a formula of length lifetime where the edges in the footprint graph are the union of
\Lambda
many sets.

\exists E_{1},\ldots,E_{\Lambda}\subseteq E,\forall e\in E,\exists E_{i}:e\in E_{i}

We can also force these sets to form a partition if we require a simple temporal graph (a temporal graph such that each edge is active exactly once). This can be achieved by appending the following formula to the formula above.

\big(e\in E_{i}\mathrel{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{\scalebox{0.66667}{$\Longrightarrow$}}}\bigwedge_{j\in 1,\ldots,\Lambda}(E_{j}=E_{i}\vee e\notin E_{j})\big)

Since modification problems in the literature require formulas which describe very specific properties of the footprint graph, we have not included them here. However, the temporal properties they aim to modify for may be found earlier in this section.

6
Future Work

There are several directions for future research on the use of logic in the study of temporal graphs.

With respect to temporal encodings, a natural direction is to identify further temporal parameters for which similar preservation and meta-theorems can be obtained. Possible candidates include the temporal feedback vertex number
[
39
]
, the temporal cliquewidth
[
26
]
, or a combination of underlying cliquewidth with lifetime or temporal degree.

In the work introducing the TIM width (
\mathsf{tim}
), Enright et al.
[
27
]
present two meta-algorithms that characterise the classes of computational problems that are fixed-parameter tractable with respect to
\mathsf{vim}
and
\mathsf{tim}
. For
\mathsf{vim}
, tractability is tied to a notion of
temporal locality
, while for
\mathsf{tim}
this locality is further structurally restricted.
This perspective is reminiscent of first-order logic which is inherently limited to see only local properties of a graph. An interesting question is whether these classes of problems admit a precise logical characterisation, e. g., by a suitable fragment of FO or MSO, and how such a characterisation relates to the VIM and TIM encodings.

Finally, a broader direction is to investigate logical tools beyond FO and MSO meta-theorems, such as logical transductions, game-based techniques, and methods inspired by the Weisfeiler–Leman framework.
Such tools may provide alternative ways to compare temporal graph classes and to reason about their structural and algorithmic properties.
Recent work has begun to study isomorphisms for temporal graphs under reachability notions
[
7
,
22
]
; an intriguing next step is to explore whether transductions or related techniques can be
extended,
used to further compare temporal graph classes,
in particular with respect to structural parameters and the computational complexity of temporal graph problems.
