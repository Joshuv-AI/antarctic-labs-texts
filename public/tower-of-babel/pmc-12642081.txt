Mechanical behavior of esthetic CAD/CAM post–core restorations: a 3D finite element analysis
Authors: Leyla Tutus, Ozge Parlar Oz, Irem Karagozoglu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aims to investigate the fracture resistance of CAD/CAM post-core restorations manufactured from different materials and to evaluate stress distribution using finite element analysis (FEA).MethodsRoot canal treatment was performed on 48 single-rooted mandibular second premolars, which were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 12). The experimental groups were as follows: Group PEEK (polyether ether ketone), Group Nano-hb (nanohybrid composite), Group GFR-C (glass fiber-reinforced composite), and Group Zir (zirconia). Post–core restorations were fabricated using a CAD/CAM system. Following thermocycling, the specimens were subjected to static loading in a universal testing machine. For finite element analysis, a mandibular second premolar model was constructed, and a load of 100 N was applied at a 45° angle to the long axis. The maximum von Mises stresses were recorded. Fracture resistance values were statistically analyzed using one-way ANOVA, and post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed with the Dunnett T-test.ResultsFracture resistance was highest in Group Zir (1240.20 N), followed by GFR-C (993.53 N), with PEEK (674.07 N) and Nano hb (666.10 N) showing significantly lower values (p < 0.05). FEA showed the highest stress concentration in the zirconia post-core (69.230 MPa), while the lowest was in PEEK (22.968 MPa). However, root stress was highest in PEEK (40.390 MPa) and lowest in zirconia (17.773 MPa). Zirconia and GFR-C groups showed a higher incidence of catastrophic fractures, whereas PEEK and Nano hb exhibited mostly favorable fracture patterns.ConclusionsFinite element analysis and examination of fracture types have shown that rigid zirconia post-core restorations cause severe fractures due to their mechanical properties. In contrast, materials with an elasticity modulus similar to that of dentin provide a more physiological stress distribution and result in minor fractures.Keywords: Post-core, CAD/CAM, Fiber-reinforced composite, Nano-hybrid composite, Polyetheretherketone

Background

Advances in endodontic treatment methods have made it possible to successfully treat teeth with extensive material loss. Teeth with excessive crown decay can be restored using post-core restorations that are supported by root canals. However, the long-term success of these systems is still a matter of discussion [1]. Post-core restorations used for retention and support can weaken the tooth structure due to the preparation of the post socket in the root canal. Selecting the appropriate post-core material for each case is important for the successful outcome of post-core restorations [2, 3]. Cast metal post-core restorations have been used for a long time. However, it has been suggested that they increase the risk of root fracture due to their high elastic modulus [4]. They can also cause debonding problems and have a poor aesthetic appearance [5, 6]. Prefabricated posts are frequently preferred as they are easy to use and can be produced from various materials. However, since post diameters are generally produced in standard sizes, it may be necessary to widen the root canal or use smaller diameter posts. This can prevent the posts from fitting perfectly into the root canal cavity, causing gaps to form at the post-dentin interface [7, 8]. Such gaps can facilitate microbial penetration, creating risks that may lead to microleakage and development of caries [9]. Therefore, in recent years, CAD/CAM technology has emerged as the preferred method for fabricating custom-made post-core restorations.

CAD/CAM technologies provide faster, more precise, esthetic and biologically compatible results than traditional methods in restorative and prosthetic treatments. The materails used in CAD/CAM technology have homogeneous properties, and the use of post-cores produced by milling these homogeneous blocks reduces structural defects in the post-core system and improves mechanical performance. There is a wide variety of material options that can be used with this technology. A high-performance glass fiber-reinforced composite material is used in prosthetic restorations. The material consists of 55% glass fiber and 45% epoxy resin, does not require firing, has an elastic modulus value close to dentin, has high fracture resistance. It is also biocompatible and lightweight [10]. Another material used in prosthetic and post-core restorations is polyether ether ketone (PEEK). PEEK posts have a low elastic modulus value, which is similar to that of dentin. They also have high fracture resistance, low density, biocompatibility, corrosion resistance, and they do not cause any allergic reactions [11]. Posts produced from nano hybrid composites offer superior performance in terms of both mechanical strength and esthetics. Their structure contains nano and micro filler particles, giving them a smooth surface and making them resistant to wear. This contributes to the long-term stability of restorations [12]. Compared to ceramic-based posts, zirconia-based ceramic posts exhibit higher bending and fracture resistance, but their resistance to shear forces is insufficient [13, 14]. Zirconia is recommended for use in post-core restorations due to its good retention and aesthetic properties, as well as its radio-opacity and biocompatibility [15].

Although various materials with different properties are used in post-core restorations, there is still no gold-standard material. However, ceramic, composite and fiber-reinforced posts are more advantageous in cases where aesthetics are important. Posts with an elastic modulus similar to that of dentin are recommended as they cause less stress on the root and result in fewer vertical root fractures [16].

Three-dimensional finite element analysis (3D-FEA) has become an important tool in dentistry, as it enables a detailed evaluation of biomechanical behavior under functional loads that would be difficult to assess in vitro. In particular, when applied to post-core restorations, 3D-FEA provides valuable insights into stress concentration patterns, material performance, and the potential risk of root fracture, thereby enhancing the clinical relevance of experimental findings [17]. In the present study, the fracture resistance of CAD/CAM-fabricated post-core restorations made of zirconia, PEEK, glass fiber-reinforced composite, and nano-hybrid composite was evaluated, and the stress distribution within the post-core and tooth root was analyzed using finite element analysis. The null hypothesis of the study was defined as follows: There is no difference in fracture resistance between post-core restorations produced with different materials, and there is no difference in terms of stress values resulting from forces applied to various post materials.

Materials and methods

The study was conducted on extracted human teeth, and ethical approval was obtained from the Gaziantep University Clinical Research Ethics Committee (Ethics Committee Approval No: 2023/150). Power analysis was performed using the G Power 3.1 program. According to the power analysis, the power is 80%, the error level is 0.05 and the hypothesis is two-tailed. A preliminary power analysis was conducted for the parameters to be measured in four independent groups. The effect size was calculated as 0.55 based on previous studies [16, 18]. Based on these results, it was determined that the study should involve a total of 44 samples, with at least 11 in each group. 4 groups were established to produce CAD/CAM post-core restorations. Preparation of tooth samples

A total of 48 single-rooted, single-canal mandibular second premolar teeth, with a minimum root length of 14 mm, extracted from patients aged 18–65 for periodontal reasons, were used. The labio-lingual and mesio-distal dimensions of these teeth were very close to each other (± 0.3 mm). Teeth with crown damage due to any reason such as decay or trauma, teeth that have undergone endodontic treatment, and teeth with cracks or fractures were not included in the study. The teeth were disinfected by soaking them in a 0.5% sodium hypochlorite solution for five minutes. The selected teeth were then stored in glass jars with lids in a 10% formaldehyde solution for one week before use. After this, they were stored in distilled water at room temperature.

Root canal treatment and post preparation

The teeth were prepared horizontally, 2 mm above the enamel-cementum junction, using diamond fissure burs (Azdent, Shenzhen, China), with water cooling via an aerator (KaVo, Warthausen, Germany), to create a ferrule effect. After preparing the cavity with a round bur, the working length was determined using a size 10 K-type file (FKG Swiss Endo-Le Locle, Switzerland). All samples were prepared with an endodontic motor (FKG Swiss Endo Rooter X3000, Le Locle, Switzerland) using the crown-down technique. The canals were enlarged to a final size of 35/0.04 with the iRace (FKG Swiss Endo-Le Locle, Switzerland) nickel-titanium rotary instrument system. Following canal preparation, the canals were sequentially irrigated with 5 ml of 17% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) for one minute, followed by physiological saline and 5.25% sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl), concluding with a final rinse of 2 ml of physiological saline. The canals were then dried using paper points (FKG Swiss Endo, Le Locle, Switzerland). The prepared teeth were filled using a single-cone technique with AH Plus canal paste (AH PLUS, Dentsply Sirona, Dentsply DeTrey GmbH, Konstanz, Germany), and 35/0.04 gutta-percha cones (FKG Swiss Endo-Le Locle, Switzerland). The post cavity was prepared immediately after root canal obturation, using a 1.5 mm diameter Peeso reamer, while maintaining 4 mm of gutta-percha at the apex. Immediate preparation was preferred to minimize disruption of the apical seal and to reduce unnecessary dentin removal.

Digital post-core design and production

The tips of dental syringes were cut off, filled with a low-viscosity addition-cured silicone impression material (Elite HD+, Zhermack, Rovigo, Italy). Prefabricated glass fiber posts were then placed in the tip section. This system was used to obtain the negatives of the existing post cavities. All impressions were scanned using a computer-assisted extraoral scanner (3Shape Lab Scanner E3, 3Shape, Copenhagen, Denmark), and the data was transferred to a digital system (Fig. 1). The post-core models were designed using computer-aided design software (ExoCAD GmbH, Darmstadt, Germany) (Fig. 2). A core with an 80 μm cement margin and a 4 mm cervico-incisal length was designed for each of the post-core models. A total of 48 digital post-core models were obtained from four materials: PEEK (Lot: 552006), zirconia (Lot: 37517300), fiber-reinforced composite (Lot: 200370123) and nano-hybrid composite (Lot: 2349595) CAD/CAM blocks. This was achieved using computer-aided manufacturing (CAM) and a milling machine (Redon GTR; Redon Technology, Istanbul, Turkey). The milling range was set to 98–16 mm (height and diameter) for production.

Cementation of post-core restorations

The PEEK posts were sandblasted with 110 μm of Al₂O₃ at a pressure of 2 bar, the zirconia posts were sandblasted with 50 μm of Al₂O₃ (Metoxide, Germany) [19, 20]. The GFR-C and Nano HB posts were roughened using 9% hydrofluoric acid for 60 s. The root canals of the prepared teeth were roughened with 37% phosphoric acid for 30 s. The teeth were rinsed, dried using a paper point and compressed air. Cementation was performed using a dual-cure resin cement (Panavia V5, Kuraray, New York, USA). Resin cement was placed into the root canal with spiral files (Lentulo; Dentsply Maillefer), cemented under continuous finger pressure. The composite resin cement was cured using a light (Ultradent Valo Cordless LED Curing Light, Utah, USA), which was applied perpendicular to the distal, mesial, labial and palatal surfaces of each specimen for 20 s per surface. Excess resin cement was carefully removed from the cervical margin using a microbrush and an explorer before final light polymerization.

Preparing samples for fracture resistance test

The region around the tooth roots was covered with wax to create a biological space. The samples were dipped into a melted wax container and then removed to create a wax coating. To standardize the samples, the same person completed the wax coating of the roots after waiting 3 s. The average PDL thickness was set at 0.2 mm [21].

All specimens were embedded in self-curing acrylic resin (Meliodent, Heraeus Kulzer, Hanau, Germany; elastic modulus ≈ 3 GPa) to form acrylic blocks. The wax between them was melted away, and a light-body impression material was injected into the resulting cavity in an attempt to mimic the periodontal ligament. The specimens underwent 10.000 thermal cycles between 5 °C and 55 °C, with a dwell time of 10 s and a transfer time of 30 s, using a thermocycling machine. This protocol has been reported to simulate approximately 1 year of clinical function (SD Mechatronik Termocycler, Germany).

Fracture resistance test

The acrylic samples were placed in a universal testing machine (Shimadzu AG-X, Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan) at a 45° angle between the long axis and the applied force in order to simulate the lateral forces exerted on teeth in the oral environment (Fig. 3). During testing, the acrylic cubes were stabilized in a custom-designed metal holder fixed to the universal testing machine to avoid any potential sliding or displacement. The speed of the force to be applied was set at 1 mm/min, force was applied to the samples from the palatal region, 2 mm apical to the incisal margin. The maximum force observed at the moment of failure was recorded in newtons (N). A visual examination was carried out on the types of failure that were occurring in the post-cores.

Assessment of stress distribution

The arrangement of the three-dimensional mesh structure and its conversion into a mathematically appropriate solid mesh structure, the creation of three-dimensional finite element analysis models, and the finite element stress analysis process were performed on HP workstations equipped with INTEL Xeon E-2286 processors running at 2.40 GHz and 64 GB ECC memory.

A tomography scan was taken of an adult to create the mandibular bone model used in the study. The tomography data was reconstructed with a slice thickness of 0.1 mm. This data was then transferred to 3DSlicer software in DICOM format and converted into 3D models. Three-dimensional models were transferred to ANSYS Spaceclaim software and cortical bone with a thickness of 2 mm was created, and trabecular bone was obtained by referring to the inner surface of the cortical bone.

The second premolar tooth was scanned using the 3Shape device and exported in STL format. The total root length of the tooth was modelled as 14 mm. The periodontal ligament was modelled with a thickness of 0.25 mm. The core was modelled to be 4 mm in height and 5 mm in diameter. The post-core was created with 9 mm within the root and 2 mm within the coronal dentin. The resin cement was modelled as 80 μm thickness, starting 2 mm below the post (Fig. 4). Four analysis models were obtained using four different post-core materials in the working model. To perform analyses and obtain accurate results in the mathematical models created, the relationships between the model’s components defined in the analysis programme. The BONDED-type contact definition was implemented for all components in contact in all analyses. This approach is based on the assumption that the parts move in full correlation with each other during movement. The linear properties of materials with given elastic modulus and Poisson’s ratio were used in the analyses (Table 2) [22]. The material properties of the analysed model were defined numerically. In all models, a force of 100 N was applied at a 45-degree angle, 2 mm below the lingual occlusal edge [23].

Statistical analysis

The homogeneity of variance between the different materials was examined using the Levene test. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare material groups. Post hoc comparisons of significance between pairs of groups were then conducted using the Dunnett test. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was used to analyse the relationships between quantitative variables. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Windows version 28.0 (IBM SPSS, Chicago, IL). Hypotheses were evaluated bilaterally and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results of finite element analysis

The maximum Von Misses stress values occurring in post-core materials and dentin surfaces are shown in Table 5. The highest stress value was observed in zirconia post-core (69.230 MPa), while the lowest value was observed in PEEK post-core. The maximum Von Mises stress value observed in the nano-hybrid composite post-core was 28.997 MPa, while the maximum Von Mises stress value observed in the GFR-C post-core was 30.098 MPa. Examining the images obtained from the post-cores reveals that, when force is applied at a 45° angle from the lingual side, the stresses are concentrated in areas close to the outer surface of the core region (Fig. 5). When examining the maximum Von Misses stress levels in dentin models, it was found that when high stress values were maintained on the post-core side, the stresses occurring in the tooth were lower (as the elastic modulus increases, the stress occurring in the tooth decreases). It was determined that the highest stress was observed in the post-core using PEEK material (40.390 MPa), and the lowest stress was observed in the post-core using zirconia material (17.773 MPa). The root images that were analysed demonstrate that stress is more concentrated in the cervical region of the root (Fig. 6).

Discussion

The restoration of endodontically treated teeth with a post-core restoration is a common treatment option in clinical practice. The variety of materials used in these restorations has increased with the development of digital systems. In the present study, we evaluated the latest available materials. According to the results obtained from the study, our primary hypothesis, ‘There is no difference in fracture resistance between post-core restorations produced with different materials’ and our secondary hypothesis, ‘There is no difference in stress values resulting from different forces applied to various post materials,’ were rejected. The research findings revealed a statistically significant difference between the mean fracture resistance of the different post-core materials and the stress values.

The materials used for post-core restorations, which are performed to increase the fracture resistance of root canal-treated teeth, should have mechanical properties that are similar to those of dentin. These materials should also be able to effectively bond to tooth tissue and be biocompatible [25]. Fokkinga et al. suggested in their study that the metal posts cause irreparable root fractures due to wedging at the root. This is related to the difference in elasticity modulus between the metal and dentin [26]. Although the use of rigid post-core materials such as yttria-stabilised tetragonal zirconia polycrystal (Y-TZP) has always been a subject of debate, it is stated that these posts, which have a higher elastic modulus than titanium and fiber posts, are more resistant to lateral forces. In a study, it was observed that zirconia posts produced as s using CAD/CAM technology provided higher durability, maximum compatibility with the canal, and sufficient aesthetics [27].

High-performance composite materials reinforced with advanced fiber strands are used as post material. In a study comparing the fracture resistance of glass fiber-reinforced composites with traditional fiber posts, it was reported that the fracture resistance of glass fiber reinforced post-cores was higher (%43,8) than that of traditional fiber posts. They also reported that all fractures occurring in glass fiber-reinforced composite posts were repairable, while 50% of fractures occurring in prefabricated posts were irreparable [28].

In a study comparing cast metal (Ni-Cr) post-cores, prefabricated glass fiber posts with composite cores and CAD/CAM zirconia post-cores in mandibular premolar teeth, Habibzadeh et al. reported that zirconia posts had the lowest fracture resistance and that catastrophic fractures were most common in this group. These results contrast with those of our study, in which zirconia posts exhibited the greatest fracture resistance, though the rate of catastrophic fracture was similar to that reported by Habibzadeh et al. [29]. According to the results of our study, 41% of irreversible damage to zirconia posts and cores is thought to be related to the material’s high stiffness.

Özarslan et al. compared prefabricated glass fiber posts with composite cores, PEEK post-cores and zirconia post-cores in maxillary central teeth. It has been reported that prefabricated glass fiber posts exhibit higher fracture resistance than PEEK and zirconia posts, and that PEEK posts exhibit more restorable fractures than glass fiber and zirconia posts [30]. Saisho et al. compared post-core restorations made from nano-hybrid ceramic, PEEK, glass fiber CAD, and nano-hybrid composite CAD/CAM blocks in single-rooted premolar teeth. These four materials have similar fracture resistance values and no significant differences between them have been reported [31]. In another study, the fracture resistance of post-core restorations produced from cast metal, nano-hybrid ceramics, PEEK, and prefabricated fiber posts was examined. It has been reported that the mechanical properties of the three groups are comparable to each other but much lower than those of cast metal posts [32]. In our study, zirconia post-core restorations were found to have higher fracture resistance compared to other groups. This difference can be attributed to the structural properties of the material.

There are different hypotheses in the literature regarding the optimal elastic modulus value for posts. Asmussen et al. evaluated different materials using finite element analysis, it was demonstrated that posts with a high elastic modulus create less stress in the dentin [33]. In a finite element analysis conducted by Zarone et al., the biomechanical behaviour of teeth restored with endocrowns was evaluated with respect to the elastic modulus of the restorative materials. The study demonstrated that materials with a high elastic modulus significantly modified the natural biomechanical response of the teeth. The same study also reports that materials with a low elastic modulus, such as composite resin, allow for the natural bending of the tooth under force, thereby reducing stress at the tooth restoration interface [34]. Escribano et al. also recommended the use of posts with an elastic modulus close to that of dentin in order to create a homogeneous structure with dentin and improve biomechanical performance However, while prefabricated posts were employed in that study, custom-made posts were utilized in the present investigation [35]. In our study, PEEK, nano-hybrid composite, and glass fiber composite posts, which have an elastic modulus close to that of dentin, were found to be repairable, while zirconia posts, which have a high elastic modulus, caused irreparable fractures. In their study, Panna et al. investigated the fracture resistance of two post-core systems using finite element analysis. In their study, light-transmitting posts concentrate stress at the post–core junction in the cervical region, whereas metal posts concentrate it at the core–tooth junction [36]. In our study, where maximum Von Misses stresses were evaluated using finite element analysis, it was observed that stress was concentrated in the cervical region of nano-hybrid composite, zirconia, PEEK and glass fiber composite posts, similar to previous studies [37, 38].

Restorations produced using CAD/CAM systems have many advantages over traditional methods [39]. CAD/CAM systems are commonly used to rehabilitate veneer crowns, inlays, onlays and implant-supported restorations [40]. Post-core restorations can also be performed more efficiently and precisely by using the advantages offered by CAD/CAM systems. Selecting the right material is also crucial for producing mechanically durable restorations. This study provides a comparative evaluation of CAD/CAM-fabricated post-core systems made from conventional and rarely studied materials with porperties close to dentin (PEEK and nano-hybrid composite). It offers new insights into the systems’ biomechanical behaviour and clinical implications, based on fracture patterns and stress distribution.

Limitations of the study

It is quite difficult to simulate the intraoral environment in laboratory studies. Many factors affect the fracture resistance of extracted teeth, such as tooth selection, tooth size, degree of tooth calcification, crown height, the area and direction of force application. In the present study, all factors were standardised as much as possible.

Conclusions

Within the limitation of this study, it could be concluded that rigid zirconia material causes severe fractures, whereas materials with an elasticity modulus similar to that of dentin result in repairable fractures and provide a more physiological stress distribution. Finite element analysis revealed that the material with a high elastic modulus generated high stress in the post-core restorations while creating lower stress on the root surface. The zirconia group showed a notably high standard deviation in fracture resistance (199.94), indicating significant variation among the specimens tested under same conditions. Future studies are recommended to implement more standardized protocols and tighter quality control measures during specimen production and testing to reduce data variability and improve the reliability of results for zirconia-based materials. The combined use of in vitro testing and finite element analysis provides a strong methodological approach, and the consistency between the two techniques highlights the scientific value of the present study.
