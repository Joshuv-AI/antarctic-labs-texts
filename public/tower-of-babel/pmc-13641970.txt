Age estimation through mandibular radiomic attributes in panoramic radiographs using convolutional neural networks
Authors: Ygor Alexandre Beserra de Sousa, Diego Filipe Bezerra Silva, Natália Rogério Borella, Maria Luiza dos Anjos Pontual, José Alberto Souza Paulino, Patrícia Meira Bento, Daniela Pita de Melo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeArtificial intelligence (AI)-based approaches to mandibular age estimation are limited by reliance on manually selected anatomical features. Thus, this study aimed to develop and validate a convolutional neural network (CNN)–based model to estimate age from panoramic radiographs through the analysis of mandibular radiomic attributes.Materials and MethodsThis was a cross-sectional study based on digital panoramic radiographs of patients aged 2 to 97 years. To develop the segmentation model, 600 radiographic images were manually annotated and used only for training. A U-Net–based CNN was implemented for the semantic segmentation task, successfully processing 7,832 radiographic images. The proposed age estimation model was built on a CNN architecture comprising three convolutional blocks, each consisting of a Conv2D layer followed by a MaxPooling2D layer, enabling progressive, hierarchical extraction of visual features.ResultsThe model achieved a mean absolute error (MAE) of 6.91 years, and a root mean square error (RMSE) of 9.41 years. The mean coefficient of determination (R2) was 0.799. In the comparison between chronological and predicted age, most observations were distributed close to the identity line. However, a slight increase in dispersion was observed in the older age groups.ConclusionThe findings suggest that the proposed CNN-based model shows promising results for estimating age from panoramic radiographs. Although a modest reduction in predictive precision was noted among older individuals, overall performance remained acceptable. However, these results should be interpreted with caution, given the limited data sources and the absence of external validation.Keywords: Age Determination by Skeleton, Mandible, Forensic Anthropology, Convolutional Neural Networks, Deep Learning

Introduction

Age estimation is widely used in legal contexts where an individual’s date of birth cannot be reliably verified, either due to the absence of civil registration records1,2,3,4,5 or suspicions of fraudulent documentation.1,4 In such cases, age assessment helps clarify legal matters concerning the credibility of the declared age, particularly in applications for international protection and in proceedings related to criminal responsibility.1,2,3,4,6,7 It is also frequently employed in migration control contexts1,4,5,8 and plays a fundamental role in adoption proceedings involving children without formal registration.4,5 Moreover, it contributes to the identification of deceased individuals5,8 and supports the reconstruction of biological profiles in cases involving missing people.3 Beyond the legal and forensic domains, its relevance also extends to clinical practice, where it is essential for establishing appropriate management strategies and determining the optimal timing of therapeutic interventions, such as in orthodontic treatment planning.7

Traditionally, age estimation is based on different approaches, including morphological methods applied to hard tissues, such as bones and teeth; biochemical methods, with emphasis on forensic analysis of deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA); and radiographic methods.3,6 In the field of dentistry, the latter method, particularly represented by panoramic radiography, stands out for its non-invasive nature, simplicity, and cost-effectiveness,3 in addition to being routinely used in clinical practice.9 Age assessment based on dental imaging is typically performed through the extraction and analysis of specific features from dental radiographs.10

Teeth and jawbones analyses stand out as highly effective methods for age estimation, as they combine strong biological relevance with non-invasive assessment techniques and remarkable structural durability.1,5 Despite this, most conventional methods used for age estimation are complex and time-consuming, and they rely heavily on human visual assessment, which is inherently prone to error and strongly dependent on the observer’s level of expertise and experience. In this context, the incorporation of artificial intelligence (AI) represents a promising alternative for addressing and potentially overcoming the limitations associated with conventional age estimation approaches.3

Although AI-based approaches have been applied to mandibular analyses for age estimation, most existing studies primarily focus on dental age assessment methodologies.11,12,13 While AI-models based on dental development demonstrate high accuracy, certain forensic scenarios present significant limitations. In cases where the mandible is recovered without teeth, due to post-mortem tooth loss, intentional avulsion, or environmental degradation, reliance on dental parameters cannot be assessed, restricting the use of tooth-based methods.11 Thus, AI studies have used the mandible based on pre-selected anatomical features.14,15,16 However, these approaches have key limitations, including reliance on manual feature selection, high interobserver variability, and the risk of failing to capture the full structural and textural complexity of the images, thereby compromising model robustness and generalizability.

In this context, the use of radiomics in these analyses overcomes the limitations of traditional methods, even when teeth are present. Radiomics is a technique that extracts detailed quantitative information from medical images, using predictive methods, to support clinical decision-making. It uses mathematical formulas to characterize various image features, such as contrast, homogeneity, and texture, within defined regions of interest.17 Moreover, radiomics can process large volumes of data that often elude human perception and are inaccessible through conventional visual inspection of images.18 Therefore, the present study aimed to develop and validate an AI-model based on a convolutional neural network (CNN) architecture to estimate the age of children, adults, and older individuals using panoramic radiographs through the analysis of mandibular radiomic attributes.

Materials and Methods

This cross-sectional study was approved by the local Research Ethics Committee (approval number 7,842,593). Furthermore, this study is in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Due to its retrospective design and the use of fully anonymized data, the requirement for informed consent was waived. All patient data were anonymized and handled with strict confidentiality prior to being accessed by the authors, in full accordance with the approved ethical protocol.

Sample selection

The sample consisted of 8,432 digital panoramic radiographs of two datasets from two public Oral Radiology services, localized in the Northeast of Brazil- the Federal University of Pernambuco and the State University of Paraíba. All images were obtained at the request of dental surgeons for diagnostic purposes in patients aged between 2 and 97 years. The images were acquired using OP3D cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) scanners (Kavo Dental GmbH, Baden Württemberg, Germany) and Planmeca ProMax 2D digital panoramic radiography equipment (Planmeca Oy, Helsinki, Finland), with 75 kVp, 8 mA and a rotation time of 9 s. The acquired panoramic radiographs had 2776 x 1480 pixels with a resolution of 280 dpi and an intensity of 8 bits. The images were stored on an external hard drive (Toshiba 1TB USB 3.0, 5400 rpm, Tokyo, Japan) in an encoded format, containing only information on the participants’ sex and age, thereby ensuring their anonymity. From the full dataset of 8,432 images, 600 were selected and manually annotated exclusively for training the segmentation model (U-Net). The remaining 7,832 images were then processed by the trained segmentation model and used as input for the age estimation model.

Digital panoramic radiographs with adequate quality were included, showing complete visualization of the mandible without significant cropping or structural loss. Only images with proper positioning, sufficient contrast, and sharpness for reliable assessment of anatomical structures were considered, as well as radiographs from individuals with known and recorded age. Radiographs presenting artifacts that could compromise analysis were excluded, including motion blur, positioning errors such as head rotation or tilt, structural superimposition, and the presence of metallic objects or dental prostheses, implants, or orthodontic appliances. Images with anatomical distortions were also excluded, including alterations resulting from pathologies, trauma, prior surgeries, or craniofacial anomalies that could interfere with mandibular morphology.

Radiographic images analysis and segmentation process

Initially, 600 panoramic radiographic images were manually segmented by two researchers to ensure consensus on the annotated region, after undergoing a preliminary noise reduction process using GIMP software (ver. 2.10.32, GIMP Development Team, Berkeley, USA). Following the annotation, a specialist reviewed 20% of the images to audit the data previously annotated by the two researchers. During the region of interest (ROI) definition step, the mandibular bone structures were carefully segmented, preserving their original position in the radiographs to ensure standardization and consistency of the images used in the study (Fig. 1).

A U-Net CNN was implemented for the task of semantic segmentation. All input images were resized to a fixed resolution of 224 × 224 pixels, preserving three channels (224 × 224 × 3). Pixel intensity values were normalized to the [0, 1] range by dividing by 255. The model architecture, consisting of an encoder and a decoder with skip connections, was trained in a supervised manner using reference segmentation masks. This process enabled the network to learn how to map input image pixels to their respective classes, accurately identifying and delineating the bone structures of interest. After the training and validation phases, the model was used to perform automated segmentation on the remaining dataset, successfully processing all 7,832 radiographic images.

A visual quality control procedure was performed. All automatically segmented images were inspected by a specialist to verify the anatomical consistency of the ROIs. Cases with evident segmentation failures (e.g., missing anatomical structures or severe inclusion of artifacts) were identified and removed from the final dataset. The age estimation model was trained using segmented images that inherently contain minor imperfections. This contributes to improving the model’s robustness to boundary inaccuracies, as supported by the results obtained through cross-validation.

Development of the age estimation model

The proposed model for age estimation consisted of a CNN architecture. The network is composed of a sequence of three convolutional blocks, each containing a Conv2D layer, followed by a MaxPooling2D layer, for the progressive and hierarchical extraction of visual features. The first block uses 32 filters, the second 64, and the third 128, all with a 3x3 kernel and ReLU activation function. This set of convolutional blocks was defined based on empirical evaluation. In preliminary experiments, more complex architectures, including deeper networks and transfer learning approaches, showed a tendency toward early overfitting, without consistent improvement in validation metrics (MAE and RMSE). Therefore, an incremental strategy was adopted, in which variations in network depth and the number of convolutional filters were evaluated. The actual architecture demonstrated a better balance between bias and variance.

After feature extraction, the feature maps are flattened and fed into a fully connected (Dense) layer of 64 neurons. A 50% dropout is applied after this layer to reduce overfitting. Finally, the output is provided by a single linear unit (Dense(1, activation='linear')), suitable for an age regression problem.

The initial layers extracted low-level attributes, such as edges, contours, and local variations in intensity. In the intermediate layers, the network learned more complex representations, including textures and anatomical patterns specific to the mandible. Finally, the deep layers consolidated high-level attributes related to global morphology and structural variations relevant to the classification or prediction task.

The convolutional process was accompanied by pooling operations, which gradually reduced the spatial dimensionality of the feature maps, concentrating essential information and minimizing redundancies. At the end of the convolutional stage, the feature maps were converted into single vectors, which represent, in a condensed way, the features automatically extracted by the network. The vectors extracted in the previous stage were forwarded to the fully connected layers. This approach allowed CNN to act not only as a regression model, but also as a hierarchical feature extractor, eliminating the need for manual feature extraction techniques.

The model was trained with a batch size of 64 and a learning rate of 0.001, using the Adam (Adaptive Moment Estimation) optimizer and MAE as the loss function. Training was conducted for up to 150 epochs, with early stopping applied to prevent overfitting: training was halted if the validation MAE did not improve for 25 consecutive epochs, and the weights from the best-performing epoch were restored. Although data augmentation is commonly used to enhance generalization, it was not applied in this study due to the relatively large dataset and the intention to avoid introducing artificial variability that could distort anatomical patterns relevant to age estimation.

Model generalization strategies

To ensure that the data partitioning process preserved the representativeness of the original sample, a stratification strategy was adopted. Initially, the distribution was performed by age group to prevent certain partitions from containing only individuals of a specific age, which could compromise the robustness of the training. For this purpose, the sample was divided into five age groups according to the percentage distribution observed in the dataset: the first group comprised approximately 25% of the samples, ranging from 2.25 to 21.33 years; the second group, 30%, from 21.42 to 40.42 years; the third group, 25%, from 40.5 to 59.58 years; the fourth group, 17%, from 59.67 to 78.67 years; and the fifth group, about 3%, from 78.83 to 97.83 years.

In addition, the distribution by sex was considered, with the dataset comprising 41.99% male and 58.01% female individuals. This proportion was maintained across all partitions created during cross-validation, ensuring that each subset used for training and testing reproduced the same composition observed in the overall dataset. In this way, it was ensured that the sex variable was equally represented in each iteration of the cross-validation. This procedure reduced the risk of sampling biases and helped the model’s results more accurately reflect the diversity of the original dataset, thereby increasing the reliability of the analyses.

To ensure that the training and evaluation process was carried out in a representative manner, a data stratification strategy was adopted, as described previously, ensuring that the distribution of the variables of interest was preserved in all subsets used. Therefore, the k-fold cross-validation technique was employed, with k = 10. In this method, the dataset was divided into 10 subsets of approximately equal size. In each iteration, nine parts were used for model training, while the remaining part was reserved for testing. The process was repeated ten times, so that each subset was used exactly once for testing and nine times for training.19 Therefore, 10 independent training sessions were conducted, each evaluated on a distinct test set. The results were obtained by averaging the metrics calculated across all iterations, to ensure greater robustness and reduce the influence of occasional variations in data division (Fig. 2).

Analysis of the results

To evaluate the performance of the developed model, error and adjustment metrics were used. Among the selected metrics, the Mean Squared Error (MSE), the Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), the Mean Absolute Error (MAE), and the coefficient of determination (R2) stand out, each providing complementary information about the performance of the regression model.

Results

The training set consisted of 7,832 mandibular images, of which 3,288 belonged to male individuals (41.99%) and 4,544 to female individuals (58.01%). Performance was similar between sexes. Males (n = 515) showed MAE of 7.46, RMSE of 10.21, and R2 of 0.78, while females (n = 660) had slightly lower errors (MAE = 7.36; RMSE = 9.74) and a comparable R2 of 0.77. The sample showed a wide age range, varying from 2.3 to 97.9 years, with a median age of 37 years (Fig. 3). Model performance worsened progressively with increasing age. The 2–21 years group showed the lowest errors (MAE = 3.58; RMSE = 5.93; R2 = -0.68; n = 260), while error metrics increased in the 21–40 (MAE = 6.89; RMSE = 8.68; R2 = -1.60; n = 376) and 40–59 years groups (MAE = 7.55; RMSE = 9.42; R2 = -1.63; n = 284). Performance declined further in the 59–78 years group (MAE = 10.55; RMSE = 12.78; R2 = -5.68; n = 213), with the worst results observed in individuals over 78 years (MAE = 18.79; RMSE = 21.17; R2 = -24.93; n = 42) (Table 1).

Discussion

This study aimed to develop and validate a CNN architecture to estimate individuals’ age using panoramic radiographs based on mandibular segmentation, in children, young people and the elderly. Bone structure plays a fundamental role in individual identification, age estimation, and forensic investigations. In children and adolescents, the mandible is particularly relevant due to its predictable growth patterns and the sequential development of teeth.11 In this context, assessing mandibular dimensions represents a promising approach for estimating age in growing individuals, and elderly. By analyzing mandibular morphometric measurements and validating their accuracy across diverse populations, it is possible to enhance forensic identification methods and improve the precision of age estimation in both living individuals and human remains.11

Previous studies have been investigating specific anatomical structures of the mandible using both panoramic radiographs14,15,16 and CBCT scans.20,21 In the study conducted by Acharya,14 the gonial angle was measured on digital panoramic radiographs from 100 subjects aged 18–89 years. The authors concluded that the proposed digital method could represent one of the few viable options for use in elderly individuals with few or no remaining teeth. In the study conducted by Bhardwaj et al.,15 300 panoramic radiographs of individuals aged 25–54 years were selected. Age-related changes were evaluated collectively using five parameters: gonial angle, antegonial angle, mental foramen, mandibular canal, and mandibular foramen. Among these variables, changes in the mandibular canal and mandibular foramen were found to be highly significant (P< 0.05) with advancing age.

Despite this, traditional morphological measurement methods for age estimation rely heavily on manual procedures, making them subjective, poorly reproducible, and prone to measurement error. They also depend significantly on professional expertise and are generally time-consuming and labor-intensive. In this context, deep learning has gained increasing popularity in the medical field, particularly CNN,8 as a means of overcoming the limitations of traditional techniques. CNNs are inspired by the functioning of biological neurons. By stacking multiple layers, a complete CNN model can be constructed to automatically extract relevant features and generate predictive outputs from medical images.22,23 In the scoping review conducted by Chen et al.,24 which aimed to investigate the application of artificial intelligence in image analysis for decision-making in clinical dentistry and to identify trends and research gaps in the current literature, CNNs were the most frequently reported type of neural network (74%).

In the study by Koch et al.,25 the aim was to evaluate improvements in forensic age prediction for children, adolescents, and young adults by training a custom CNN. The model was developed using 21,814 panoramic radiographs from 13,766 individuals aged 1 to under 25 years, with a particular focus on dental growth features. In the test dataset, a MAE of 0.93 ± 0.81 years and a MSE of −0.06 ± 1.23 years were achieved. Overall, 63% of predictions were accurate within 1 year, and 95% within 2.5 years. The CNN’s performance was comparable to that of human experts and effectively identified discrepancies in the reported ages of individuals.

In the present study, we obtained an R2 of 0.799, MAE of 6.91 ± 0.36 years, and RMSE of 9.41 years. Comparable studies reported R2 values ranging from 0.5611 to 0.955.3 However, MAE values were lower in those studies, varying from 1.54 years (95% CI: 1.33–1.76)11 to 3.1 years.3 Vila-Blanco et al.26 proposed an objective, repeatable, and fully automated approach to quantitatively describe the mandible in 1,195 panoramic radiographs of patients with age ranging from five to 70 years. Their method included linear distances and angles derived from eight anatomical landmarks, centroid size, shape variations from the mean shape, and additional shape parameters extracted using a point distribution model. The combination of a convolutional neural network (CNN) and four shape descriptors was validated for forensic sex and age estimation, achieving 87.8% accuracy and a MAE of 1.57 years. In the study by Oliveira et al.,3 a dataset of 12,827 panoramic radiographs representing the Brazilian population was used, covering an age range from 2.25 to 96.50 years. The authors employed a model adapted from InceptionV4, enhanced with data augmentation techniques to improve performance.

In this study, a slight increase in dispersion between actual and predicted age was observed in older individuals, indicating greater variability and reduced accuracy in this subgroup. This decline may be explained by prolonged exposure of teeth to the oral environment after eruption, including cariogenic and calculus-forming bacteria, dietary habits, oral hygiene practices, parafunctional habits, and systemic conditions such as diabetes that may worsen periodontal disease. These cumulative factors affect dental structures over time and can compromise age estimation, particularly in cases involving early tooth loss, prosthetic rehabilitations (e.g., crowns and implants), advanced periodontal bone loss, and extensive restorative treatments.3

Despite the promising results, this study has some limitations. Several factors may influence the performance of age estimation models. Therefore, subgroup analyses examining additional characteristics, such as sex and ethnicity, could provide a deeper understanding of the model’s performance across different populations. Furthermore, reliance on datasets from only two centers may have limited the generalizability of the developed models. In addition, the model’s accuracy may vary considerably depending on factors such as image quality, specific clinical settings, and the characteristics of the evaluated population. The proposed approach relies exclusively on mandibular images, without incorporating dental features, which makes the prediction task inherently more challenging. In addition, a relatively simple CNN was used, which may limit the model’s ability to capture complex anatomical patterns. The inclusion of the full age spectrum further increases variability and prediction difficulty, meaning that direct comparisons of MAE with studies using narrower age ranges or additional anatomical information should be interpreted with caution.

Even with image standardization, these variations may hinder robust external validation across different clinical contexts, thereby challenging the model’s generalizability and clinical applicability. The “black box” nature of deep learning models continues to pose a challenge. Although the model achieved strong predictive performance, identifying which specific radiomic features drive its predictions remains difficult. This inherent lack of interpretability is a trade-off of AI-based approaches and should be carefully considered when applying the results in clinical or forensic settings.

In addition, privacy, data security, and the ethical sourcing of training data are critical concerns that demand careful attention. Therefore, AI systems should be implemented with appropriate oversight and continuous validation, serving as decision-support tools rather than replacements for forensic experts, whose clinical expertise and contextual judgment remain essential for the responsible and reliable application of these technologies.27

Future studies should focus on external validation using multicenter and international datasets to evaluate the model’s generalizability across different populations and imaging equipment. In addition, employing explainable AI (XAI) techniques, such as saliency maps, could help identify the specific mandibular regions and radiomic features that most influence the model’s predictions, thereby enhancing interpretability and increasing acceptance in forensic applications.

The findings suggest that the proposed CNN-based model shows promising results for estimating age from panoramic radiographs. The observed agreement between predicted and chronological ages across a broad age range indicates its ability to capture relevant age-related imaging features. Although a modest reduction in predictive precision was noted among older individuals, overall performance remained acceptable. However, these results should be interpreted with caution, given the limited data sources and the absence of external validation. Further studies are needed to confirm the model’s generalizability and to better establish its applicability in clinical and forensic contexts.
