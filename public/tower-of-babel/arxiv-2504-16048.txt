PRIME: Fast Primal-Dual Feedback Optimization for Markets with Application to Optimal Power Flow
Nicholas Julian Behr, Mattia Bianchi, Keith Moffat, Saverio Bolognani, Florian Dörfler

Abstract
Online Feedback Optimization (OFO) controllers iteratively drive a plant to an optimal operating point that satisfies input and output constraints, relying solely on the input-output sensitivity as model information. This paper introduces PRIME (PRoximal Iterative MarkEts), a novel OFO approach based on proximal-point iterations. Unlike existing OFO solutions, PRIME admits a market-based implementation, where self-interested actors are incentivized to make choices that result in safe and efficient operation, without communicating private costs or constraints. Furthermore, PRIME can handle non-smooth objective functions, achieve fast convergence rates and rapid constraint satisfaction, and effectively reject measurement noise. We demonstrate PRIME on an AC optimal power flow problem, obtaining an efficient real-time nonlinear local marginal pricing scheme.



I
Introduction

Regulating a physical plant to a cost-optimal operating point is a fundamental goal in control theory
[
1
,
2
]
.
The classic solution approach involves pre-computing a reference signal, which an online controller then tracks in real-time. Feedforward reference optimization is, however, not robust to uncertainty in the plant model and optimization cost.

Online Feedback Optimization (OFO) has recently garnered interest as a solution to this challenge.
By directly interconnecting the optimization process with the true plant, OFO enables continual adaptation and exploits the robustness of feedback control
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
]
.
For this reason, OFO has found application in several engineering domains, most prominently power systems (
[
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
]
), but also communication networks
[
11
]
, smart building automation
[
12
]
, traffic
control
[

]
and process control
[

]
.

In its most basic form, OFO aims at solving the following optimization problem online:

\displaystyle\min_{u\in\mathbb{R}^{m},y\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\;

\displaystyle\phi_{u}(u)+\phi_{y}(y)

(1a)

s.t.

\displaystyle C_{u}(u)\leq 0

(1b)

\displaystyle C_{y}(y)\leq 0

(1c)

\displaystyle y=h(u).

(1d)

Here,
u\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
y\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
are the input and output of a physical plant. We model the plant through its input-output map
h:\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{p}
. This map could approximate a stable dynamical system with fast-decaying dynamics, such that the system output quickly converges to
h(u)
when a constant input
u
is applied. The map
h
does not need to be known; instead, OFO relies on online measurements of the output
y
and on the knowledge (or online estimate
[
15
,
16
,
17
]
) of the input-output sensitivity
\nabla_{u}h
.

In this work, we investigate the use of OFO in a market framework in which control actions are taken by multiple autonomous and self-interested actors, rather than by a single centralized decision-maker.
In this setup, a market operator aims to determine incentives (rewards or payments to be charged) such that the self-interested actors collectively steer the plant toward a socially optimal outcome.

Implementing OFO in such an online market presents some challenges.
One major challenge is enforcing the output constraints (
1c
).
While methods based on Lagrangian duality allow decentralization across the actors
[
18
]
, they often suffer from slow convergence and significant constraint violations during transients
[
19
]
.
Another issue is that the market operator does not know the actors’ costs and constraints, nor can it directly control their actions—instead, actors greedily react to incentives to maximize their profit.
These challenges were considered in
[
20
]
for a prosumer incentivization problem.
However, the bilevel-optimization formulation in
[
20
]
differs from the social welfare optimization goal in this paper.

Contributions
:
We present PRIME (PRoximal Iterative MarkEts), a new OFO approach to coordinate self-interested market participants (“input actors” and “output actors”) attached to a physical plant’s inputs and outputs (see Figure
I
).
The method builds on a primal-dual Lagrangian formulation, enhanced with proximal-point iterations, to improve convergence speed and enable non-smooth objective functions.

We introduce two different variants of PRIME.
PRIME-Y constructs the Lagrangian by dualizing (
1c
), and allows for input actors only.
PRIME-H instead constructs the Lagrangian by dualizing the input-output constraints in (
1d
), enabling both input and output actors, and thus a higher degree of decentralization.

PRIME requires the market operator to know only the plant’s input-output sensitivity
\nabla_{u}h
, but not the actors’ private costs or constraints.
Notably, the strictly convex incentive functions constructed by the market operator naturally compel each actor to respond by optimizing their (private) cost. This process implicitly reveals actors’ preferences and constraints without requiring central modeling, which is prone to model mismatch.

Via simulations on an Optimal Power Flow (OPF) problem, we show that PRIME matches the performance of standard centralized methods in terms of constraint resolution and convergence speed, while benefiting from the distributability of a market-based implementation.

II
Market model

In this work, we focus on the following instance of (
1
):

\displaystyle\min_{u\in\mathbb{R}^{m},y\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\;

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n_{u}}\phi_{u}^{j}(u_{j})+\sum_{l=1}^{n_{y}}\phi_{y}^{l}(y_{l})

(2a)

s.t.

\displaystyle C_{u}^{j}(u_{j})\leq 0,\;j\in 1,\dots,n_{u}

(2b)

\displaystyle C_{y}^{l}(y_{l})\leq 0,\;l\in 1,\dots,n_{y}

(2c)

\displaystyle y=h(u).

(2d)

which represents a market involving
n_{u}
input actors and
n_{y}
output actors. Here,
\phi_{u}^{j}
and
C_{u}^{j}

are the privately known costs and constraints of input actor
j
.
Each input actor
j
wishes to minimize
\phi^{j}_{u}
while satisfying its constraints

, and we denote
u=\begin{bmatrix}u_{1}^{\top}&\dots&u_{n_{u}}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
.
Similarly,
\phi_{y}^{l}
and

represent the private objective and constraints of the output actor
l
, and
y=\begin{bmatrix}y_{1}^{\top}&\dots&y_{n_{y}}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}

Input and output actors are connected via the plant (

).
The goal of the market operator is to superpose incentives such that when actors minimize their local cost subject to their private constraints, the plant is steered to a cooperative optimum, i.e., a solution to (
2
). In particular, note that:

1.

incentives, distributed by the market operator, influence the decisions of the actors;

2.

the self-interested actors react to incentives by minimizing their overall cost, given by their private cost function plus the payment due to the market operator.

This mechanism is illustrated in Figure
2
.
The input actors directly affect the plant via their decision variables
u_{j}
.
The output actors, on the other hand, do not have direct control over
y
. However, an output actor
l
can affect the market dynamics (and indirectly the output
y
) by choosing its decision variable
z_{l}
, as explained in Section
IV
.

II-A

Power Systems Example

We present an OPF problem as an example for the market model in (
2
).
Consider a distribution grid with
B+1
buses, including
B

y=(v_{b},\theta_{b})_{b\in\{1,2,\dots,B\}}

v_{b}
is the voltage magnitude and

We consider

\phi_{y}^{1}(y_{1})=(v_{1}-1)^{2}+(v_{2}-1)^{2}
means that Output Actor 1 constraints the voltage magnitudes between 0.95 and 1.05

On the distribution grid, there are also

input actors/prosumers that adjust their power injections and pay/are paid by the market operator to do so. Each prosumer

has a power injection

at a certain bus, where
P_{j}
and

are the active and reactive power injection, respectively.
The input of the system is the vector of power injections,
u=(u_{j})_{j\in\{1,2,\dots,n_{u}\}}

The power injections determine the voltages through the power flow
y=h(u)
. The setup described thus far corresponds to the scheme in Figure
2
.

It is also possible that there is only one output actor responsible for the voltage magnitudes at all the buses. In this case, the output actor can coincide with the market operator and can be considered a Distribution System Operator (DSO). This setup corresponds to the scheme in Figure

Review of OFO approaches

\displaystyle\min_{u\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\;

\displaystyle C_{u}(u)\leq 0

\displaystyle C_{y}(h(u))\leq 0.

IV-A

Market Interpretation of PRIME-Y

As per Section

Specifically, we would like to choose a payment

from each input actor

\displaystyle u_{j}^{k+1}=\argmin_{u_{j}\in C_{u}^{j}}\ \hat{\phi}_{u}^{j,k}(u_{j}):=\phi_{u}^{j}(u_{j})+p_{u}^{j,k}(u_{j}),

(10)

If the payment is positive, the actor pays the market operator, and if the payment is negative, the market operator pays the actor.

Unfortunately, it is not always possible for PRIME-Y’s

A notable exception is the case in which the output cost and constraints are

\begin{split}p_{u}^{j,k}(u_{j})&=\frac{\gamma_{u}}{2}(u_{j}-u_{j}^{k})^{\top}(u_{j}-u_{j}^{k})\\
&\qquad+u_{j}^{\top}e_{j}^{\top}\nabla_{u}h(u^{k})^{\top}(A_{y}^{\top}\lambda_{y}^{k+1}+c_{y}),\end{split}

Since the market operator requires this information, PRIME-Y does not allow for autonomous output actors.

Another way of seeing this is that the market operator coincides with the only output actor.

IV-B

PRIME-H OFO: dualizing the input-output constraint

Algorithms
1
,
2
,
3
exploit the formulation (
3
), which is obtained by replacing

in (

While this substitution eliminates

Thus, exploiting the formulation in (

Rather than substituting

To do so, we introduce an auxiliary variable

\begin{split}\mathcal{H}(u,z,\nu_{h})&=\phi_{u}(u)+\phi_{y}(z)+(h(u)-z)^{\top}\nu_{h}\\

The Lagrangian has two primal variable vectors,

, that correspond to the decisions of the input and output actors, respectively; and a dual variable
\nu_{h}
, related to the constraint

. The variable

can be understood as a target for the physical output

To seek a saddle-point of the Lagrangian

]

Market Interpretation of PRIME-H

The market operator provides the following incentive to each input actor

\displaystyle\begin{split}p_{u}^{j,k}(u_{j})&=\frac{\gamma_{u}}{2}(u_{j}-u_{j}^{k})^{\top}(u_{j}-u_{j}^{k})\\

\displaystyle\begin{split}p_{z}^{l,k}(z_{l})&=\frac{\rho+\gamma_{z}}{2}(z_{l}-z_{l}^{k})^{\top}(z_{l}-z_{l}^{k})\\

(15b)

where
g_{l}
is the matrix such that
y_{l}=g_{l}^{\top}y
.

It is easy to check that the best response (as in (

)) of the input and output actors coincides with the update of

and

Role of the output actors
: the output actors determine

. While the output actors do not directly change the system inputs, their decisions for

and the input actor incentives (

\nu_{h}^{k+1}
and the incentives

and

. Therefore, PRIME-H admits a market implementation and can be distributed

This differs from PRIME-Y, which does not allow for independent output actors and can be distributed over input actors only in the case of linear output costs and constraints.

\mathcal{Y}=[0,1]

PRIME-Y and PRIME-H differ in the treatment of output costs

In particular, PRIME-Y heads for the unconstrained optimum along the composition
\nabla_{u}\phi_{y}(h(u))

Instead, PRIME-H targets “legal” improvements to
y

h

Performance

Figure

The input actors are divided between prosumers, with cost of production
\phi_{u}^{j}(u_{j})=0.1P_{j}+0.1P_{j}^{2}+0.1Q_{j}^{2}
and production limited to box constraints
P_{j}\in[0,12.5]
and
Q_{j}\in[-2,2]

P_{j}=[-0.4]

Q_{j}=[-0.2]

Voltage magnitudes are constrained to
[0.95,1.05]^{B}
per unit on all buses.

For all methods, the dual variables are initialized at 0.
This cold start is challenging, as the dual variables must be built up before constraint satisfaction.
For this reason, the primal-dual method in Algorithm
2
is slow in resolving constraint violations and is outperformed by the Projected Primal method.
Notably, the Projected Primal method requires a cost and constraint oracle (i.e., it is centralized).
Remarkably, PRIME-Y and PRIME-H operate in a market fashion without this oracle and still achieve comparable resolution speeds.

In Figure
4(b)
, we introduce noise into the voltage measurements. The Projected Primal algorithm is sensitive to noise, while the primal-dual methods are not.
The Projected Primal method performs particularly poorly when operating close to output constraints (which is common for optimal points), as measurement noise frequently causes constraints to appear violated, leading the following input to be projected away from the optimum. This aggressive reaction to the measurement noise leads to the Projected Primal method’s “jittery” behavior in Figure
4(b)
.
PRIME-Y and PRIME-H instead deliver fast constraint violation resolution while rejecting the effect of the measurement noise.

VII
Conclusion

This paper introduced PRIME, a new feedback optimization approach based on proximal iterations. PRIME achieves rapid constraint violation resolution and robustness to measurement noise.
Most importantly, PRIME enables the design of optimal incentives in markets with self-interested actors without requiring access to their private costs or constraints.

Future work should address the possible strategic behavior of market actors.
This includes examining the risk of market dynamics manipulation and devising mechanisms to make PRIME truth-revealing and collusion-proof.
