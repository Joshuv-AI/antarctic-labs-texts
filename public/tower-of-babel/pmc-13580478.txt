Comment on “soluble cytokine receptor levels in obstructive sleep apnea patients treated with continuous positive airway pressure therapy”
Authors: Duyu Nunya, Kanika Bhatia
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We read with great interest the recent article by Kiriluk et al. titled “soluble cytokine receptor levels in obstructive sleep apnea patients treated with continuous positive air pressure therapy” published in Future Science OA [1]. The authors should be commended for investigating soluble cytokine receptor dynamics in patients with Obstructive Sleep Apnea (OSA) undergoing Continuous Positive Airway Pressure (CPAP) therapy, an area with growing translational relevance. Their use of soluble Cytokine receptors profiling rather than conventional circulating cytokine assessment represents an innovative methodological approach that may provide greater biological stability and insight into inflammatory signaling in OSA.

While the study contributes meaningfully to biomarker-based characterization of OSA related information, several additional methodological considerations may further contextualize the interpretation of the findings.

First, although CPAP compliance was categorized according to Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services criteria, the analysis did not appear to account for variability in nightly CPAP exposure beyond the dichotomous compliant/non-compliant classification [1]. Kirilik et al. reported significant changes in several soluble cytokine receptors following CPAP therapy [1]. Although the relationship between CPAP adherence and soluble cytokine receptor responses remains incompletely understood, differences in treatment duration and consistency may influence inflammatory biomarker responses [2,3]. Therefore, incorporating measures such as nightly usage hours or residual apnea-hypopnea index could have clarified whether differences in treatment exposure contributed to the observed biomarker changes.

Second, obesity is a major determinant of systemic inflammatory and may independently influence soluble cytokine receptor expression. Although Kiriluk et al. reported comparable baseline body mass index values between compliant and non-compliant groups [1], BMI alone does not fully reflect visceral adiposity, insulin resistance or other metabolic factors that contribute to inflammatory activity. Adjustment for these variables would therefore strengthen the attribution of the observed soluble receptor changes to CPAP therapy [4,5].

Another notable consideration relates to the interpretation of the observed reduction in soluble receptor levels. Soluble cytokine receptors can exhibit dual biological roles, functioning not only as a marker of cytokine activation but also as endogenous modulators capable of buffering inflammatory signaling pathways [6,7]. For example, soluble TNF receptors can neutralize excess TNF-α and limit downstream inflammatory responses, whereas soluble IL-6 receptor and sGP130 participate in regulating IL-6 trans-signaling, thereby influencing both pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory pathways [6–8]. Consequently, the reductions in soluble receptor concentrations observed after CPAP therapy may not simply indicate suppression of systemic inflammation. Instead, they may reflect restoration of immune homeostasis with reduced compensatory receptor shedding following alleviation of intermittent hypoxia, or altered cytokine receptor turnover accompanying normalization of inflammatory signaling [8]. A more detailed discussion of these potential mechanisms would further strengthen the biological interpretation of the reported biomarker changes and enhance the translational relevance of the findings.

Furthermore, Kiriluk et al. reported considerable baseline variability in soluble cytokine receptor levels, particularly for markers such as sEGFR and sGP130, suggesting heterogeneity within the study population [1]. OSA is increasingly recognized as a biologically heterogeneous disorder, and factors such as hypoxic burden, cardiovascular comorbidity, metabolic dysfunction and residual respiratory events may influence inflammatory profiles beyond apnea-hypopnea index alone [9]. Consideration of these phenotypic dimensions may provide further insight into variability in soluble cytokine receptor responses to CPAP therapy.

Finally, the assessment of soluble cytokine receptors only at baseline and 90 days may have overlooked early or transient inflammatory responses to CPAP therapy. Previous studies suggest that inflammatory biomarkers may change within the first few weeks of CPAP initiation before stabilizing with continued treatment [2,4]. Additional longitudinal measurements at intermediate time points could provide a more comprehensive understanding of biomarker dynamics during treatment.

Despite these considerations, the study by Kiriluk et al. provides valuable evidence supporting the potential of soluble cytokine receptor profiling in OSA. Future studies incorporating detailed CPAP adherence measures, metabolic phenotyping and longitudinal biomarker assessments may further clarify the clinical utility of these biomarkers in monitoring treatment response and disease heterogeneity.
