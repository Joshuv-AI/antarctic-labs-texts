Impact of wind forcing on surface transport in the Gulf of Naples
Authors: Luigi Gifuni, Paola de Ruggiero, Daniela Cianelli, Oxana Drofa, Silvio Davolio, Stefano Pierini, Enrico Zambianchi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this work, we use the high-resolution Campania Regional Ocean Model (CROM) forced with four modelled wind fields provided by different weather prediction systems (ERA5, SKIRON, WRF, and MOLOCH), to assess their performance in driving simulations of the Lagrangian surface transport in the Gulf of Naples. The simulated wind fields are first compared with the locally observed dataset recorded by the ISPRA weather station. The observed paths of four CARTHE drifters, released in the Bay of Naples in July and September 2021, are then compared with the simulated trajectories of virtual particles. The latter are obtained exploiting the current velocity field provided by CROM, forced with the above-mentioned wind products, as input of a Lagrangian particle tracking tool (TRACE). The accuracy of the simulations is quantified by adopting the Normalized Cumulative Lagrangian Separation Distance metrics. Our findings indicate that SKIRON attains the best score in most of the analyzed scenarios, particularly under weak wind conditions. However, in operational scenarios where wind accuracy cannot be assessed in advance, the use of a multi-source trajectory - averaging simulated drifts from different model configurations - provides a more robust solution. This multi-model approach helps to minimize errors that could arise from relying on a single wind product. Finally, considering the different wind forcings, significant differences were found both in surface exchange between two subregions of the Gulf of Naples and in the rate of beached particles.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-16851-3.Keywords: Tyrrhenian sea, Gulf of Naples, Lagrangian transport simulation, Drifter trajectories, Wind forcing sensitivity, Coastal monitoringSubject terms: Physical oceanography, Climate sciences

Introduction

Coastal areas face significant vulnerability due to their exposure to both natural hazards and human activities, making environmental monitoring essential for mitigating the impacts of anthropogenic pressure and extreme natural events. Effective monitoring is best done with a combination of a large number of observations and accurate regional ocean models1. Indeed, whilst observations offer high accuracy, they often lack spatial continuity. On the other hand, ocean model simulations provide continuous coverage in both time and space, allowing - in synergy with experimental data - a comprehensive understanding of marine dynamics at broader scales. The exchange between models and observations is of paramount importance for initialization, forcing, as well as for validation purposes.

The Gulf of Naples (GN, hereafter) (Fig. 1) is a remarkable coastal area of the Tyrrhenian Sea. Its unique morphology and oceanographic features make it an ideal location for oceanographic studies2. The basin shares typical features of coastal areas in the Mediterranean Sea including (i) strong seasonal variability, (ii) stratification of water masses, and (iii) a coastal circulation driven by the interplay of large-scale flows, local topography and local wind forcing. A detailed description of the region’s dynamical features can be found in3.

In addition, the high population density and the strong socio-economic interest, characterised by intense industrial, port and touristic activities, significantly increase the basin’s vulnerability to marine pollution. This makes environmental monitoring crucial for the preservation of the marine ecosystem and the sustainability of local natural resources. Given the region’s significant ecological and economic value, coupled with the heightened risk of marine pollution, there is a natural call for advanced tools able to predict the transport and dispersion of pollutants, including waste, plastics, and oil. Obviously, the quality of Lagrangian simulations has a particularly high practical relevance in the context of emergency operations like search and rescue or organization of efficient strategies for mitigating oil spills4. Fast and accurate simulations of pollutant transport is especially crucial in the case of the GN, where coastal and marine protected areas could be impacted within hours to days due to the gulf’s size and circulation patterns. Thus, a rigorous evaluation of the capabilities of modelling systems in this context is essential.

This work aims to assess the performance of the Campania Regional Ocean Model (CROM, see2,5,6 coupled with a Lagrangian particle tracking algorithm (TRACE, see7 in simulating the surface transport in the GN, when forced by wind data from different sources. It is well known that the short-term variability of surface currents is predominantly affected by wind, which significantly contribute to the dynamics of coastal regions and, consequently, to the movement of materials8,9. Thus, atmospheric models that correctly describe the main meteorological features combined with hydrodynamic models that well reproduce the existing sea currents are required for attaining reliable simulation results. It is worth noting that the ocean model used in this study has already been validated in previous research, demonstrating its ability to accurately reproduce basin-scale physical processes. Furthermore, previous validation efforts have primarily focused on an Eulerian perspective, relying on comparisons with altimetric data, CODAR fields, and both satellite and in situ observations2,5,6,10. However, whilst there is a clear understanding of the main driving forces shaping the circulation in the region, and assessed that CROM is able to simulate the mean circulation patterns with sufficient degree of realism, its performance in capturing Lagrangian dynamics - such as the transport of pollutants, or other materials - has only been poorly investigated. This represents an important challenge, if we consider that, as highlighted by Vandenbulcke et al.11, local features of surface currents and Lagrangian transport are often not well resolved, even by state-of-the-art ocean models. Based on this premise, the scientific questions that this study aims at investigating are the following:

This work thus provides the first evaluation of the CROM performance from a Lagrangian perspective. The presented outcomes will shed light on the impact of atmospheric forcing on the model’s ability to reliably reproduce Lagrangian trajectories. Moreover, the study highlights the significant role of wind in shaping basin-scale transport processes. The obtained results provide recommendations to optimize future sampling strategies, to strengthen the observational network and to improve the model accuracy in operational frameworks.

To address the aforementioned questions, we compared the simulated and observed trajectories of four surface drifters released in July and September 202112 in the northeastern sector of the basin, called the Bay of Naples (BN hereafter), a restricted area of the GN washing the city of Naples (see Fig. 1b). Lagrangian data have been used for validation purposes vs. numerical model outputs (e.g.13,14) and vs. land-based remotely sensed observations (e.g15,16.

The choice of these months was driven by the enhanced risk of pollutant spills during the summer season, which experiences a considerable increase of maritime traffic and tourism-related marine activities. For these reasons, evaluating the performance of our modelling system during this period is crucial for gaining insights essential to planning effective responses to environmental emergencies.

A sensitivity study is carried out by forcing CROM with four wind datasets provided by different atmospheric modelling systems operating at various horizontal resolutions (description and references in Section Models and Methods): the ERA5 reanalysis produced by the European Centre for Medium Weather Forecast system (ECMWF), the operational forecasts of the SKIRON prediction system of the National and Kapodistrian University of Athens, the operational forecasts of the WRF model provided by the Center for Monitoring and Modelling Marine and Atmosphere applications of the Parthenope University of Naples (CCMMMA), and the operational forecasts of the MOLOCH model of the CNR-ISAC prediction system. Moreover, as reference wind dataset, we used the observed wind data provided by the ISPRA weather station located at Molo Carmine (purple star in Fig. 1a). The comparison allows us to assess the consistency of the different wind modelling products with the observed wind field. Furthermore, apart from the score evaluation based on the comparison with the observed trajectories, we consider the periodic release of passive particles in two different subdomains, one set in the central region of the GN and the other one in a coastal sector, by following a similar approach applied in previous studies17,18. This exercise aims to quantify how the wind shapes the transport processes, highlighting the potential difference in the surface exchange between the two subregions and in the particle beaching rates considering the different wind forcing.

Results

Simulated surface dynamics

Here we analyze the surface current fields produced by the four numerical setups. First, a description of the monthly average conditions is provided. Then a detailed analysis of the simulated dynamics within the BN throughout the whole simulation period and during the drifter tracking hours is presented. This overview provides preliminary insights into how different wind products yield different dynamical conditions in the study area.

Supplementary Figures S1a-S1d show the monthly mean of the CROM simulated surface current field in the GN for July 2021. The circulation patterns, emerged by forcing the model with the four wind products, are almost similar, despite the different resolutions of the wind fields. A strong southeastward Tyrrhenian current off the Bocca Grande opening allowed sea waters to enter the Ischia and Procida channels (see Fig. 1). Generally, a counterclockwise circulation pattern is observed within the gulf. The current field obtained by forcing CROM with MOLOCH wind stress (Fig. S1d) appears to be less intense compared to the other hindcasts, especially in those areas close to the shoreline.

Supplementary Figures S1e-S1h show the monthly mean of the surface current field for September 2021. Again, from the qualitative comparison, the patterns are similar: a cyclonic circulation was observed offshore (near the Bocca Grande opening). In the innermost sectors, however, some differences in both direction and intensity arose. For instance, using the ERA5 wind field, the coastal current field near the BN sector shows a strong westward orientation. In the same region, the other models show greater variability in direction and a weaker current intensity.

This initial analysis suggests that the surface circulation in the GN is governed by two main drivers: (i) the local wind and (ii) the underlying basin-scale synoptic structures as also emerged by previous studies [e.g.,19]. Therefore, while the large-scale remote circulation patterns remain stable and consistent regardless of the wind forcing, the wind field modulates the smaller-scale dynamical features. This modulation affects the spatial variability of the current fields, thus influencing the transport processes within the area.

We now focus the analysis on the BN, where Lagrangian experiments were conducted. Supplementary Table S1 shows the basic statistics (maxima, minima, monthly mean and standard deviation), of the spatially averaged surface current field among all the CROM grid points falling within the area of Fig. 1b.

For July and September, the surface current field obtained by using MOLOCH and WRF as forcing returns the largest intensity. The mean intensity for July ranges from 4.8 cm/s (obtained using SKIRON) to 7 cm/s (using WRF). For September, the average intensity ranges from 3.6 cm/s (considering SKIRON) to 6.6 cm/s (considering WRF). Overall, SKIRON produces the weakest wind field and therefore returns the weakest surface current field, as also observed by20.

Figure 2 shows the clockwise (red line, negative) and counterclockwise (blue line, positive) spectra of the current field spatially averaged between all the CROM grid points falling within the area shown in Fig. 1b for July (Fig. 2a-d) and September (Fig. 2e-h). Overall, the positive spectrum dominates over the negative one; therefore, a counterclockwise rotation tendency of the flow is observed. In July and September, both the 24-hour and 12-hour peaks are well marked in all the hindcasts. The first peak is associated with the periodicity of the breeze regime, which is stronger during the summer period. The second corresponds to the transition every 12 h from sea breeze to land breeze. When using wind forcing from SKIRON and MOLOCH, a distinct spectral signal appears at a period of approximately 8 h in July. During this month, specific contributions near the inertial frequency (17.3 h) are not evident except under WRF wind stress (Fig. 2c), although peaks still occur around this frequency (indicating quasi-inertial oscillations). In September, the inertial signal becomes distinct under ERA5 wind stress, and as in July, a clear contribution around 8 h is observed under MOLOCH wind forcing.

In July, wind intensity ranged from 0.3 m/s in the early morning to a peak of 9.4 m/s in the afternoon. In September, it varied between 0.2 m/s and 7 m/s.

On July 8, despite the presence of south-westerly winds, the four drifters went south-westward (Fig. 4c), showing no clear correlation with the wind orientation (Fig. 4a). For this day, the average trajectory length was around 776 m, with a mean drift speed amounting to roughly 2% of the observed wind speed.

On July 13, the drifter trajectories revealed an abrupt change in the current field (Fig. 4d), highlighting the complexity of sub-mesoscale coastal dynamics and the inherent difficulty in simulating such behavior accurately. The average drift speed on this day was about 1% of the observed wind speed, even lower than the previous deployment.

By September 14, the drifters again exhibited a southwestward drift despite a southeasterly wind (Fig. 4b). Higher drift speed relative to wind speed is observed, averaging around 5% of the wind speed.

On September 27 and 29, the drifter paths (Figs. 4f, g) were more aligned with the prevailing wind direction (Fig. 4b), with drift speeds averaging around 8% of the observed wind speed.

Model skill score assessment

In each of the five numerical experiments conducted in July and September 2021, 100 passive tracers were released every twenty minutes in a circular area centered around the observed drifter position. The score was then evaluated based on the separation distance between the observed and simulated final positions, averaged among the simulated trajectories and normalized for the distance covered by each drifter at each initialization (see Models and Methods section).

More in depth, in Supplementary Figure S4 the separation distance21 of the best-performing model configuration for the five events is presented. Here we compare the outputs obtained by neglecting/maintaining the windage effect (see Methods section) in the Lagrangian equation (compare Figs. S4a-S4e with Figs. S4f-S4l). Supplementary Table S5 presents the scores computed by using the mean trajectories derived from the four wind products for each event.

Results provided in Fig. S4 and Supplementary Table S4 suggest that SKIRON provides the best performance, particularly in three scenarios: July 8, July 13, and September 14. However, ERA5 and MOLOCH occasionally outperformed SKIRON, providing the highest accuracy on September 27 and 29, respectively. WRF showed the lowest overall accuracy. This analysis highlights the strength of SKIRON’s performance, but also reveals that higher model resolution does not always guarantee an improved accuracy. As it was not possible to identify the best model in terms of wind speed, a multi-model approach considering results under different forcings (see Table S5) was undertaken and it proved to be more reliable.

Regarding the windage contribution, we observe an overall decrease in terms of accuracy in most of the scenarios, but in some cases (such as that of September 27 and 29), its effect improved the score.

Discussion and concluding remarks

The outcomes of this work allow a better understanding of surface transport dynamics in the coastal region of the GN and an improvement of the robustness of monitoring network. Indeed, evaluating the model performance is a crucial step for enhancing the efficiency of the monitoring capabilities of the area. In this context, we used the high-resolution Campania Regional Ocean Model (CROM) forced with four wind fields provided by different atmospheric modelling systems to assess their accuracy in simulation of Lagrangian surface transport in the GN.

The overall aim of the work was to identify the optimal Lagrangian model configuration to be used in an operational context. However, according to the obtained results (e.g., score analysis, Taylor diagrams), since none of the modelling systems emerged as systematically better than the others, we suggest a multi-model approach (as described below) to mitigate the impact of wind forcing inaccuracies on transport simulations. This approach could be used in the context of emergency operations such as pollutant spill events that may occur in the GN and other similar coastal regions subject to anthropic pressure. Furthermore, basin-scale transport analyses, through the investigation of concentration curves and beaching rates, allowed us to quantify how the sea surface dynamics is sensitive to the forcing wind and the impact on surface transport processes.

The accuracy of the model wind products was first evaluated per se against the observations taken at the ISPRA weather station. On a monthly scale, Taylor diagrams revealed that the MOLOCH operational weather prediction system attained the best performance in terms of wind direction, whilst the SKIRON system outperformed in terms of wind intensity. Then, the accuracy of the wind products was assessed from a Lagrangian perspective by analyzing the model scores under the four different configurations vs. trajectories of in situ surface drifters.

The analysis of the interconnections between the observed wind and the surface drifter trajectories highlights the complexity of nearshore surface dynamics. During the drifters’ tracking period, prevailing winds were weak and variable in direction, influencing the surface current response and contributing to the complexity of drifter trajectories simulation. In some cases (such as on September 27 and 29), the drift closely aligned with the wind vector, suggesting an almost instantaneous response to wind forcing. In other cases, no direct correlation with the wind direction was observed.

Consistently with previous studies [e.g.,22], it is observed that, whilst moderate or strong winds typically align instantaneous drifter motion with wind direction, this is not the case in calm conditions. In these scenarios, drift direction often showed little or even no correlation with wind. Moreover, also in23 it was found that in nearshore regions and under weak wind, the drifters’ motion was systematically to the left of the wind direction, whilst the Ekman-like deflections to the right of the wind direction were only occasionally observed. This result suggests that the motion of the surface layer does not necessarily adhere to the classical Ekman drift theory but may instead be influenced by the underlying dynamics of water masses. Furthermore, the response of the surface current, especially close to shore, can be also anisotropic and asymmetric, as the local effects of topography and the frictional balance with wind are not equivalent in the zonal and meridional directions.

The skill score computation revealed low skill and no clear correlation between higher resolution of the forcing atmospheric model and improved performance. Further research, with a more controlled experimental setup, is needed to disentangle the impact of resolution from the intrinsic characteristics of each wind product.

It is worth noting again that the experiments were performed only in summer and in a limited subregion of the GN. Although it is of paramount importance to consider the summer season as a test period, due to the increased potential risk of pollutant spills, we are aware that the results can only partly reflect the variety of dynamic regimes in the gulf.

Additionally, as observed in8, the accuracy of the model solutions is strongly dependent on the wind speed. This implies that different wind speed classes result in different model performances, and this highlights the difficulty of defining the best wind dataset in absolute. In our case, we only verified the accuracy of the model solutions for very low wind regimes (below 4 m/s). Similar low skill results were also found in4 in which the main simulations’ deficiencies occurred under low or moderate winds.

A more comprehensive assessment of the score would require testing different dynamic conditions, such as stronger wind regimes where we expect a drift more aligned with wind dynamics. Thus, for the future, the plan is to perform new Lagrangian experiments, releasing more drifters and for longer periods in different regions of the GN, by adopting a similar design of previous experiments carried out in the region (see e.g15).

The effect of windage on model accuracy varies depending on the specific conditions of each event. On September 27 and 29, including windage improved model performance, as shown by higher scores. During these days, the drifter speeds reached about 8% of the observed wind speed, significantly higher than in other cases. This suggests a greater influence of wind forcing on drifter motion. In contrast, for events such as July 8 and 13, and September 14, drifter speeds were lower (1–5% of observed wind speed) and their trajectories showed weaker or no correlation with wind direction. This behavior indicates that other processes, such as sub-mesoscale dynamics or interactions with the complex coastal bathymetry, may have dominated the surface flow. In such scenarios, windage may introduce errors, as the wind contribution to drifter motion is relatively minor.

Another potential reason of the score worsening under windage in several cases, could be the accuracy of the wind data. If the atmospheric model produces inaccurate wind forecasts, the windage effect may introduce additional errors. In scenarios where the wind data are particularly unreliable or poorly represented, the inclusion of windage may even amplify the forecast error.

According to these findings, given the uncertainties between the different atmospheric models, and the lack of a clear relationship between the model resolution and its performance, we highlight the need for strategies to mitigate the impact of wind forcing inaccuracies on transport simulations. To this end, we propose an approach based on the results of multiple model runs. This multi-model approach is particularly justified in an operational context since it takes into account unavoidable model uncertainties. By averaging the results obtained considering the different configurations (Table S5), this methodology reduces the performance dependence on a single product and provides a more robust representation of the transport pathways. In such a way we can minimize the uncertainties associated with individual wind fields, thereby increasing the reliability of surface transport simulations, especially in complex and dynamic coastal systems such as the GN.

A future strategy could involve running a hydrodynamic model with multiple atmospheric forcings, generating a range of drifts instead of a single trajectory. This would allow users to assess uncertainties and evaluate simulation reliability. Outlier trajectories deviating significantly from the mean could then be identified and potentially excluded.

However, if a dense enough observational network is available, the most reliable approach is likely the one proposed by8, where an ad hoc interpolation procedure based on a two-dimensional method developed by 24, already tested for a similar application in the Venice Lagoon25, was used to re-grid the scattered wind data over a regular mesh. As demonstrated in that study, the scores obtained by using observed wind data as forcing are consistently higher than those obtained with modeled wind products.

In this context, it is essential to increase the density of the meteorological observational network in the GN area, with the deployment of more weather sensors that can assist the accurate reconstruction of basin-scale weather conditions. We are therefore actively working on the improvement of the GN monitoring network, through the procurement and deployment of a larger number of weather stations. These data will be utilized both to further validate the modelling products and to provide forcing for the model.

The work proceeds with the quantification, this time on a larger scale, of the wind’s impact on surface transport. Analysis of concentration curves and particle beaching rates reveals significant differences, underscoring the crucial role of wind in shaping these processes. Additionally, when comparing the beaching rate of the particles released in different areas of the gulf, those released in the coastal sector exhibit a greater tendency to beach than those released farther offshore, as expected due to the geometry of the gulf. The semi-enclosed basin naturally confines particles released closer to the shore, thereby increasing the likelihood of beaching. These findings can be generalized to assess risks associated with pollutant spills. In a similar context, pollutants released in the inner sectors of the gulf are more likely to beach than those released in offshore areas. This has implications for environmental management, indicating that inner sectors of the gulf are more vulnerable to contamination and should be prioritized in terms of monitoring.

Finally, the comparison between simulated probability density maps and satellite Kd490 distribution patterns for the period, confirm and extend the findings of previous work2, further demonstrating the ability of CROM to simulate basin scale surface dynamics. In particular, the present work evaluated the model performance by adopting a Lagrangian framework, thus filling a gap in previous validation and contributing to a more comprehensive evaluation of the model’s applicability in operational contexts.

Models and methods

The ocean model

The high-resolution coastal ocean model CROM2 was used to simulate sea dynamics in the GN during drifter deployments (July and September 2021).

CROM is based on the Princeton Ocean Model (POM26, and its domain (Fig. 1a) spans the region from 40°N to 41.43°N and from 13°E to 15.36°E (the Campania Coastal System, CCS), which includes the GN as well as the Gulfs of Gaeta (GG) and Salerno (GS). A horizontal grid of curvilinear orthogonal coordinates with a resolution of 1/144° (Δy:772 m, Δx:579–591 m) is adopted. The vertical discretization consists of 40 sigma levels, with appropriate refinement designed to better resolve the surface and intermediate layers.

We nested the CROM with the hydrodynamic dataset provided by the Nucleus for European Modelling of the Ocean (NEMO)27. The model has a horizontal grid resolution of 1/24˚ (ca. 4 km) and 141 unevenly spaced vertical levels. The model solutions are corrected by a variational data assimilation scheme (3DVAR) of temperature and salinity vertical profiles and along-track satellite sea level anomaly observations27. No heat, freshwater, or radiative fluxes are used.

Wind stress followed Smith et al. methodology28 already adopted in the works of de Ruggiero et al.2,5.

Two monthly simulations were conducted for each deployment period. Lagrangian particle tracking tool (TRACE7), provided numerical trajectories.

ERA5 data are available on regular latitude-longitude grids with a horizontal spatial resolution of 0.25 degrees and 37 pressure levels. ERA5 has been available since 1940 and continues to be extended forward in time, with daily updates being made available 5 days behind real-time32.

ISPRA weather station

Naples meteorological station data from the National Tide Gauge Network (36 stations across Italy) was used as the primary reference dataset for model comparisons. Located at Molo Carmine (14.27 °E; 40.84 °N), this station provided wind speed and direction data at 10-minute intervals. Due to the location of this station, easterly winds are affected by the shielding effect of the Vesuvius volcano17. Detailed technical information on the station and its sensors can be found at mareografico.it.

The accuracy of each wind model product was evaluated through statistical comparisons using both Taylor diagrams and wind rose diagrams. Taylor diagrams offer insights into the statistical performance of each model through three key metrics: (i) the Pearson correlation coefficient, shown as the azimuthal angle on the blue contours, (ii) the centered root mean square error (RMSE), proportional to the distance from the observed point on the x-axis (green contours), and (iii) the standard deviation of the modeled data, represented as the radial distance from the origin (black contours).

Wind rose diagrams complement this analysis by visualizing the directional distribution and intensity of winds. In these diagrams, the length of each bin indicates the percentage frequency of winds from each direction, while different colors represent the frequency of winds within specific intensity ranges. Together, these tools provide a comprehensive statistical assessment, highlighting the strengths and limitations of each modeled wind field in simulated the observed dynamics.

Satellite-tracked lagrangian drifters

The low-cost artificial CARTHE drifter tracks currents centered 60 cm below the surface and is designed to enable large-scale deployments. The patented design was developed by a team of physical oceanographers and engineers at the University of Miami (see Supplementary Table S5 for the technical parameters).

Released in the northeastern sector of the GN (BN) during July and September 2021 deployments, these drifters provided Lagrangian trajectories used in the analysis.

The chosen site for the deployment was the Long-Term Ecological Research Mare-Chiara (LTER-MC) sampling station located 2nm off Naples city (Lat 40°49’ N, Lon 14°15’ E, the red star in Fig. 1b) at the boundary between eutrophic coastal waters and oligotrophic Tyrrhenian waters41–43.

The BN was chosen as a test site because the environmental quality of the marine ecosystem in this area is directly influenced by human activities41,43 and constantly receives urban sewage and other inputs from the city of Naples and adjacent areas18.

The deployment, which remains unchanged for all five experiments, involves the simultaneous release of four surface drifters, as depicted in the subplot of Fig. 1b.

Skill score evaluation

Forty Lagrangian simulations were conducted to analyze the model’s Skill Score (SS) compared to drifter trajectories.

We evaluated the impact of windage on advection in the SS calculations, although the immersed body of the drifter partially constrains the contribution of the wind drag acting on its emerged part. We recall that the windage is the additional drag force on (and hence the motion of) floating material due to the direct exposure to wind forcing at the ocean surface44. In this case, an extra wind drift parameterized as 0.6% of 10 m wind velocity was used in combination with the CROM simulated surface currents45.

Furthermore, the Lagrangian integration time step for these simulations is set to 1’, which is the internal time step adopted by CROM. Although we have verified that using a time step of 10 min is a good choice for satisfying the stability condition (CFL condition46,, since the observed trajectories span a limited temporal range (2, 3 h at least), to build a significant comparison we need the highest temporal resolution of the simulated dataset.

The Normalized Cumulative Lagrangian Separation Distance (NCLS) served as the SS metric, following21 with the SS correction by47. The choice of this metric for the score estimation is justified by its widespread use, applicability in several dynamic regions, and suitability for sparse observations such as in our case. As pointed out in47, the SS applied by Liu and Weiseberg21 may lead to an overestimation of the model score since negative values are set to zero. For instance, negative values indicate how far the NCLS is from the tolerance threshold n and thus give information on how far the simulated trajectory is from the observation. For each satellite-tracked drifter released during the five campaigns, the trajectory model was initialized every 20 min with 100 particles randomly released in a 100-meter radius around the observed location. In this way, instead of asking for a single particle model trajectory to follow the observation, we consider a collection of trajectories to account for uncertainty in location and sub-mesoscale turbulent processes. Indeed, the ocean dynamic can be chaotic, so small differences in initial position or launch time may cause similar trajectories to diverge rapidly48. The release of 100 particles per initialization was chosen to balance the need for adequate representation of sub-mesoscale variability with computational feasibility. Preliminary sensitivity tests indicated that increasing the number of particles beyond this point did not significantly improve the robustness of the mean trajectory but rather increased significantly the computational load. Therefore, this approach ensured a manageable simulation time while capturing relevant turbulent processes and maintaining model efficiency.

The SS was thus evaluated by considering the averaged end positions of the simulated trajectories at each initialization.

In addition to the computation of the SS based on the simulated trajectory obtained by considering each individual wind product, we also evaluated the score using the average trajectories derived from all four model configurations. This additional analysis was motivated by the practical challenges faced in operational contexts where the optimal wind forcing for model initialization is often not known in advance. By averaging the simulated results obtained by using multiple wind products, we aim to provide a robust trajectory estimate that mitigates the influence of inherent biases or inaccuracies of any single model. This approach allows us to capture a more balanced representation of the wind-driven dynamics, potentially providing not only a more stable and reliable results, but also an indication of the uncertainty. In this way, the averaged trajectory balances the strengths and limitations of each individual product, making it particularly useful in situations where uncertain wind forcing conditions might otherwise make the trajectory simulation inaccurate.

Basin-scale transport analysis

Eight basin-scale transport simulations explored inshore-offshore exchanges and particle beaching rates under different wind forcings.

To this end, passive particles were released daily in two different subdomains (covering an area of approximately 17 × 6 km) located in a coastal and central region of GN as representative of a coastal area characterized by reduced flushing due to restricted circulation and by variations in the quantity and quality of land runoff (region A in Fig. 6a) and an offshore marine area (region B in Fig. 6a) as representative of the “open water” system which has oligotrophic characteristics and is essentially driven by the seasonal succession of different water masses of external origin49. The boundary between the inshore and offshore zone is defined following the work by Uttieri et al.18. This threshold was set to correspond to the fluid boundary between Nisida (in the north, between Pozzuoli and Naples) and Punta Campanella (in the south, tip of the Sorrento peninsula).

Each region contains 500 virtual particles randomly deployed and daily released. The flying time, i.e., the time in which the particle tracking is computed, is set to 168 h (one week) which is close to the average residence time evaluated in the GN under the summer breeze regime50. Thus, for each scenario, a total of 24,000 numerical trajectories were computed to obtain a reliable statistical description. Under the weak dynamic conditions of the analyzed period and considering the selected flying time, the virtual particles never reach the southern and western boundaries of the model.

Particle beaching was parameterized by considering particles within 1 km of the model land cell as “beached” for the remainder of the simulation. Satellite data

Probability Density Maps57 obtained by forcing CROM with the four modelled winds were compared with the satellite maps of Kd490 (Diffuse Attenuation Coefficient at 490 nm) which may provide useful information about the dynamics of surface water masses in the region and the dispersion and transport of turbid coastal water, to check if the distribution of passive tracers emerging from the Probability Density Maps is coherent with what is observed from the satellite data. The parameter is measured by OLCI (Ocean and Land Colour Instrument) sensor and has a horizontal spatial resolution of 300 m. Daily maps are provided by CMEMS (the Copernicus Marine Environment Monitoring Service) implemented and operated by Mercator Ocean, which provides oceanographic products and services for maritime safety, coastal and marine environment, climate and weather forecasting, and marine resources users.

The adopted product (level 3) is affected by several spatial gaps, since the sea surface observation in the optical range is not possible during cloudy conditions, which must necessarily be filled. Spatial interpolation was executed using the algorithm developed by58.

Comparisons were made with both monthly mean Kd490 and snapshots for days with maximum data coverage (July 6th and September 9th).
