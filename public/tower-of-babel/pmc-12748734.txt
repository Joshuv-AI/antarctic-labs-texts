A hybrid fuzzy and convolutional neural network framework for urban road traffic risk and sustainability assessment
Authors: Zhaodong Zhong, Ziyan Ren
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractUrban redevelopment is essential to improving sustainability and livability, but traffic congestion is still a problem, not only during rush hour but also during construction, accidents, and other interruptions. Existing research frequently concentrates only on optimizing traffic flow, with little consideration given to stakeholder-driven viewpoints or environmental and anthropogenic risk concerns. Although Geographic Information Systems (GIS) have great promise for gathering and evaluating vast amounts of spatial data, little is known about how they might be used in comprehensive frameworks for traffic risk assessment. To fill these shortcomings, this study suggests a brand-new hybrid framework for evaluating traffic sustainability that combines GIS with a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) model and a Multi-Criteria Decision-Making (MCDM) technique. By (i) using a fuzzy Delphi method to systematically weight various environmental and anthropogenic criteria, (ii) utilizing oversampling methods to address data imbalances in risk prediction, and (iii) embedding CNN-based modeling in an interactive GIS platform to produce fine-grained, stakeholder-relevant risk maps, the framework significantly improves on previous research. The usefulness of the framework is demonstrated by a case study conducted in Foshan City, Guangdong Province, where road collapse segments were divided into five risk levels. Of these, 7% were categorized as high risk and 4% as extremely high risk, with the majority of these segments being in the eastern and southeastern regions. The training and testing accuracies of the Fuzzy-CNN model were 0.989 and 0.982, respectively, indicating high predictive performance. For municipal governments and urban planners, the generated traffic risk map offers a scalable and data-driven decision-support tool. This research tackles major shortcomings of current models in balancing sustainability, risk management, and urban resilience and advances the state of the art in GIS-based traffic risk analysis by explicitly integrating biodiversity and environmental elements into traffic planning.Keywords: Urban development, Sustainability, Road traffic assessment, Geographic information systems, Multi-Criteria Decision-Making, Convolutional neural network, Risk assessment, Traffic congestionSubject terms: Environmental sciences, Environmental social sciences, Geography, Geography

Introduction

The road network in China is growing quickly. Urban road collapse has become more frequent as road networks have grown. Frequently occurring urban road collapse incidents have resulted in serious harm1.The key to assessing and rebuilding urban road networks is urban road features, which are the digital representation of the elements that make up urban road functions. The precise modeling of urban terrain2, the high-precision representation of urban maps3, automated driving4, and traffic safety can all be supported by the fine-grained and high-precision extraction of urban road features.

Urbanization and economic development have a big influence on traffic growth and associated issues like congestion, overcrowding, an increase in accidents, and pollution.For developing nations and municipalities, as well as for economic prosperity and raising living standards in urban, periurban, and rural areas, transportation of people and products is crucial. On the other hand, urbanization and economic growth have a big influence on traffic growth and associated issues including congestion, overcrowding, an increase in accidents, and pollution.Furthermore, urban traffic congestion is now a typical occurrence during rush hours as well as at other occasions like accidents and road construction, which causes traffic on certain road segments to slow down. These kinds of accidents raise stress levels among drivers, contribute to pollution, and may interfere with emergency response activities. Such traffic accidents also result in a decline in life quality and put more strain on the city’s infrastructure. As a result, a sustainable transportation system must prioritize traffic safety5. Affordable, effective, accessible, secure, and ecologically friendly are all requirements for a sustainable transportation system6.

Although supply and demand are the primary causes of traffic issues, they are not the only ones because, more often than not, traffic source or destination location or the varying socioeconomic development levels of the city can have a greater impact on traffic congestion than the streets’ capacity7. Monitoring traffic conditions, identifying the reasons behind accidents, and examining the factors contributing to traffic congestion are all essential to ensuring an ideal traffic system. Numerous research has examined the causes of traffic congestion as well as its extent, intensity, duration, speed, and spread processes8.Some researchers have demonstrated that a road traffic mapping technique that effectively assesses traffic accessibility or detects vulnerabilities combines GIS based statistical data analysis with spatial and network analysis9. GIS-based analysis has helped academics better comprehend city traffic patterns and locate blackspots10. Consequently, GIS has shown value in both route design and traffic evaluation10. Many nations have adopted the GIS approach because of its many benefits when it comes to traffic problems11.

The effects of traffic congestion on the environment and socioeconomic development were examined in another research. There are other financial losses brought on by traffic besides the time lost in traffic jams and the expense of traffic accidents12. Additionally, traffic can delay the arrival of people and goods, which lowers competitiveness and productivity13. People become exhausted from spending so much time in traffic, which impairs their work ability and lowers their productivity. Additionally, they experience stress and frustration, which has an indirect impact on their social skills and health14.One of the most important steps in implementing the urban road-collapse risk finding procedure is creating an assessment model. Traditional assessment models, such as the weighted arithmetic mean15, the fuzzy comprehension evaluation method16, and the analytic hierarchy process17are frequently employed in the field of assessing the danger of ground collapse in order to calculate the likelihood of collapse based on pipelines, lithology, and other contributing factors. Because traditional assessment methods construct separate weighing structures for each assessment unit and divide the assessment units in the area, it can be challenging to balance accuracy and scope when evaluating cities using these approaches18,19.

Moslem et al.20, identifies, evaluate, and quantify important aspects of drivers’ behavior related to road safety in Budapest, Hungary. We evaluate driver preferences using the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) in a spherical fuzzy framework based on Spherical Fuzzy Sets (SFS). The degree of agreement amongst hierarchical driver groups is measured by Kendall’s test. ‘Lapses’ and ‘Errors’ are the most important factors for both experienced and novice drivers, according to our Spherical Fuzzy AHP (SFAHP) at Level 1. However, “Errors” and “Violations” are given priority by international drivers. In contrast to “Ordinary violations,” “Aggressive violations” are more common across all groups at Level 2. “Driving with alcohol use” is the top priority at Level 3. Level 1 of Kendall’s concordance shows little resemblance, however Levels 2 and 3 show substantial agreement surfaces. Moslem et al.21, builds a hierarchical lane-change model with two tiers of the most problematic qualities, based on the idea of the analytical hierarchy process (AHP). Therefore, in order to reduce ambiguity in the evaluation process, the fuzzy analytic hierarchy process (FAHP) approach was employed using fuzzy scale to precisely analyze the most relevant aspects affecting lane change22,23. The FAHP model estimation findings showed that “traffic characteristics” is the most important variable influencing lane-changing, based on the final measured weights for level 1. In contrast, “light conditions” was determined to be the least important factor associated with driver lane-change maneuvers when compared to the other listed parameters24. According to the results of the FAHP model, “traffic volume” and “speed” were the two most important factors affecting the lane change operations at level 225. Sensitivity evaluations that looked at a range of weight values and those corresponding to alternative values supported the model’s impartiality. Stakeholders can decide on strategic policy by taking into account and giving greater weight to the risk variables linked to lane changing in order to increase road safety, based on the evaluated results26,27.

Farooq et al.28, uses an effective decision-making model to assess the influence of important elements on road risks in order to assist sustainable transportation policy. The ambiguity and uncertainty present in expert opinions were managed through the use of the Fuzzy Analytic Hierarchy Process (FAHP). The elements influencing road safety were the subject of an analysis of a real-world decision-making situation. According to the first level’s FAHP data, “Poor Road Surface Condition” had the least influence on road risks (weight: 0.1332), while “Adverse Geometric Condition” was the most important component (weight: 0.6811). “Improper Road Width” was the most important sub-factor at the second level (weight: 0.2557), while “Excessive Potholes” was the least important (weight: 0.0129). Policymakers can prioritize initiatives that support safer, more sustainable transportation networks with the help of these findings, which offer an organized and data-driven basis.

Research gaps

In order to evaluate road collapse risks and support sustainable transportation planning, recent research have effectively shown the possibility of combining geospatial technology (GIS) with machine-learning approaches (like CNN) and MCDM methodologies29,30. However, the relevance of ecological and biodiverse criteria in road risk evaluations is frequently overlooked by these methods, which primarily concentrate on physical and anthropogenic factors (such as soil conditions, pipeline depth, and adjacent buildings)31. Additionally, the majority of current models often overlook stakeholder perspectives and expert knowledge in criterion selection and weight determination, and they fail to sufficiently account for data imbalance issues, which might compromise the risk mapping’s robustness and fairness32. In their suggested (GIS)-based Multiple-criteria Decision-Making (MCDM) paradigm, Krsti et al.33, combined interactive GIS maps with stakeholder input in a novel hybrid way. The fuzzy Delphi & fuzzy Decision-Making Trial and Evaluation Laboratory (DEMATEL) approaches are used to generate the criteria weights in the MCDM section of the model, while the Axial Distance-based Aggregated Measurement (ADAM) methodology is used to calculate the final ranking of the routes. This methodology was applied to many Trans-European Transport Network (TEN-T) routes, and the route connecting Hamburg/Bremerhaven, Wurzburg, and Verona was shown to have the least environmental and biodiversity impact. The GIS-MCDM method works well, however how accurate it is relies on how relevant the chosen criteria are. While ecological and efficiency considerations are given priority in this study, social and economic effects are not fully covered. Static evaluation weights might not account for shifting stakeholder interests, and they oversimplify relationships by treating interdependencies across criterialike economic and environmental effectsin isolation. Combining stakeholder inputs presumes consistent viewpoints, which may cause conflicts to go unnoticed.

Contribution of this study

This study’s primary goal is to create a hybrid GIS and MCDM-CNN framework for evaluating the danger of road collapse in an urban location (Foshan City) under complex circumstances. In order to support sustainable transportation planning and policy decisions, the framework attempts to integrate anthropogenic and environmental indicators, manage data imbalance through oversampling approaches, integrate expert knowledge with machine-learning techniques, and generate a trustworthy risk map.

The following factors led us to choose the hybrid GIS–CNN–MCDM–Fuzzy Delphi framework, even though these models are good at managing uncertainty and cutting down on repetition in preference elicitation:

Combining Image and Spatial Data: The suggested hybrid architecture, in contrast to PBWM or PSFAHP, enables the smooth integration of CNN-driven feature extraction from road-related datasets with GIS-based spatial data. Because simply preference-based models are unable to directly account for both geographical and non-spatial aspects, this integration is essential.

Materials and proposed methodologies

Data samples

Foshan is a city in the coastal southeast Chinese province of Guangdong. According to the 2020 census, Foshan has a population of about 9.5 million people and a total area of 3,798 km2. As of 2020, there were 5462 km of roads (grade 4 and higher) in total34. The geological makeup of Foshan is typical of the Pearl River Delta, with a wide distribution of soluble materials that promote ground collapse35. There are 43 significant ground collapse incidents occurred between 2014 and 2018, with the bulk taking place in the southeast and east.

There are 19 collapse incidents that occurred on urban roads were selected as accident samples out of the 43 ground collapse incidentsby looking into accident reports and field surveys; Fig. 1 displays the distribution of these cases.A total of 1000 locations were chosen at random from Foshan’s whole road network to serve as negative samples. The original dataset positive sample (19) and negative sample (1000) is separated into training and test sets and consists of both positive sample as 16 and 3 respectively and negative samples as 800 and 200 for training and testing set.

The Foshan City- Guangdong Province, China- distribution of ground collapse accidents on urban roads35.

We analyzed published small-sample studies in related geo-hazard and urban-collapse domains to put our dataset in context. A few notable recent studies are Jiang et al., (2023)36, an urban road-collapse risk study for Foshan that used 1,000 randomly sampled non-collapse road points and 19 verified collapse cases (the latter is methodologically closest to our work); Parra et al., (2023)37, which employed 86 landslide events and 86 non-landslide sites for susceptibility modeling; and Rafique et al., (2022)38, which presented deep-learning experiments detecting 130 manually annotated sinkholes in a LiDAR/drone dataset.

In contrast, the dataset used in this study includes 1,000 negative samples and 19 positive (collapse) samples (16/800 for training and 3/200 for testing). This is within the exact same order of magnitude as other small-sample geo-hazard studies (tens to a few hundred positive instances) and matches the positive-event count used in Jiang et al., (2023)36. Although the low frequency of road-collapse accidents inevitably limits the absolute number of positive events, the use of augmentation/oversampling approaches and our negative-sample pool (1,000 locations) enhance training variability and facilitate robust model validation.

Therefore, we take typical mitigating measures (data augmentation, stratified train/test split, and performance reporting on held-out data) to assure model validity, and we believe our sample size to be equivalent to in some ways more conservative than a number of previous small-sample investigations.

The study has relatively small number of positive samples (19 recorded road collapse instances) in comparison to the huge number of negative samples, which led to a class imbalance problem, is one of its limitations. This restriction results from the rarity of road collapse accidents and the paucity of official records of them in Foshan City. Spatial distribution of risk factors

A mix of manmade and geological factors is responsible for the concentration of high-risk sites in Foshan’s eastern and southeast sections. Fracture zones and comparatively poorer lithology are characteristics of these zones that make them more vulnerable to ground collapse. Rapid development in these areas has also led to the building of massive subterranean pipelines, dense road networks, and metro systems, all of which increase the anthropogenic stress on already delicate geological conditions. Correlation research also shows that soil type and low-lying topography have a substantial interaction with rainfall intensity and flood-prone areas, increasing the probability of collapse during rainy seasons. In contrast, during dry periods, when natural influences are less noticeable, manmade causes like pipeline density and construction activity become the main triggers. Temporal fluctuations, especially seasonal rainfall, are a crucial modulator of risk intensity, underscoring the combined relevance of natural and human-induced factors. All things considered, the results show that the combined and seasonally changing effects of human and natural forces make the eastern and southeastern zones extremely vulnerable, making them priority locations for monitoring and preventive measures.

Methodologies

This study proposed the Fuzzy-MCDM-CNN based urban road traffic risk assessment system. The components of the proposed system are depicted in Fig. 2 which is comprised of data collection, Model training and Model evaluation phases. In data collection, GIS data is gathered and the maps are converted into data followed by the collection of indicators for the assessment based on the created dataset. Then the training data is augmented using SMOTE to generate the positive augmented sample data. The original and augmented data are pass on to model training using Fuzzy based MCDM to allocate the weight to criteria and CNN based risk assessment.

Overview of proposed Urban Road traffic sustainability and risk framework.

GIS urban road centerline extraction

It is essential to use GIS-based biodiversity-oriented criteria for creating sustainable multimodal transportation routes. These standards guarantee that ecological system preservation is given top priority in transportation development, in addition to efficiency and connectivity. Planners can preserve ecosystem connectivity, identify and safeguard sensitive regions, and lessen the effects on important habitats and biodiversity hotspots by utilizing sophisticated GIS data.

The study used an automatic road centerline extraction technique based on the gridding concept: (i) Screening. According to the explanation in Sect. 3.1, the high-level road network in the experimental area is chosen as the experimental road in this article. (ii) Rasterization: To turn the road network obtained in the previous step into grid data, set a specific grid resolution.(iii) Filling the cavity. A certain threshold must be defined in order to fill the gaps in the translated grid data that occur at road intersections and between the reverse of the same road due to the “one to many” phenomenon of the road network. (iv) Vectorization of data. The simplified road line, often known as the road centerline, is obtained by automatically locating and vectorizing the grid area’s midpoint location based on the filled grid data.

The Land Use and Land Cover (C1) criterion states that intended intermodal transport routes interact with various land types, such as woods, agricultural regions, and urban areas. Urban sprawl is managed, highly urbanized zones are identified and avoided, and conflicts with commercial and residential neighborhoods are avoided by using Weighted Urban Proliferation (WUP) measurements, which quantify the percentage of urban areas, their dispersion, and land uptake per person.

To avoid disturbing areas set aside for the preservation of biodiversity, intermodal transportation routes are assessed using the Protected Areas and Conservation Zones (C2) criterion. The Emerald Network encompasses sites of high conservation concern and offers protection to EU member states. Nationally designated areas, or CDDAs, offer a comprehensive list of protected places throughout Europe. Planners use spatial assessments and GIS layer overlays to identify and mitigate possible effects on these critical zones.

The Biodiversity Hotspots and Important Habitats (C3) criterion is used to assess intermodal transportation routes that provide habitats for species preservation and places of significant ecological importance. As hotspots for biodiversity, it looks for and protects regions that sustain high levels of variety of species and unique ecosystems. Additionally, it seeks to safeguard critical habitats that are essential to the survival of endangered species and the maintenance of ecosystem function.This criterion reduces the harm that infrastructure development causes to natural ecosystems by assessing landscape connectivity. It takes into account how route planning affects ecosystem resilience, wildlife populations, and the delivery of ecosystem services. Route planning that takes habitat connectivity into account promotes sustainable development strategies that strike a balance between the demands of transportation and the goals of ecological conservation.

In order to identify and steer clear of regions that are particularly vulnerable to environmental harm, the Environmental Sensitivity and Vulnerability (C5) criterion assesses multimodal transportation routes. It promotes the development of sustainable infrastructure and aids in the preservation of fragile habitats. The criterion makes use of data gathered by the European Nature Information System36, offering information on various terrestrial and marine ecosystems around Europe. These data enhance knowledge of ecosystem types and its locations by combining habitat characteristics with information on geographical features. In order to maximize route efficiency and minimize environmental impact, the Topography and Elevation (C6) criterion assesses intermodal transport routes according to topographical parameters, including height, slope, and proximity to the sea. Low beaches, high coasts, inland, uplands, and mountains are the five terrain typologies into which this criterion divides landscapes using GIS data. By allocating land cover changes in accordance with these typologies, this elevation breakdown provides a clear insight of the structure of the landscape.

Hybrid GIS data with Fuzzy-CNN MCDM model

The use of MCDM approaches is necessary due to the evaluating complexity multimodal transport routes assisted by environmental sustainability and biodiversity. A more comprehensive decision-making process is ensured by the inclusion of several factors. This work improves on conventional MCDM applications in transport sustainability assessment by integrating GIS with the MCDM-DL model. Stakeholder weight assignment, spatial data integration, and sophisticated ranking techniques guarantee that the choice of transportation routes is both ecologically conscious and methodologically sound.Using the CNN method, GIS data expressed as criteria are employed as values for assessing and prioritizing the options. Nonetheless, the established criteria are interrelated, and these relationships must be considered when calculating their weights, or relative importance. The fuzzy method was chosen to carry out this process in light of this and its benefits over certain alternative approaches. Since several stakeholder representatives do the evaluations and have varying preferences for criteria, the Delphi technique had to be used in order to unify the various evaluations.

Figure 3 illustrates the proposed Fuzzy MCDM- CNN model structure. In this framework, the evaluation criteria are determined, fuzzy relation matrices are created, normalized, and defuzzed to get the final criteria weights, and GIS data are first processed through a fuzzy-Delphi–based multi-criteria decision-making (MCDM) model. In order to estimate risk, these weighted characteristics are subsequently fed into a convolutional neural network (CNN). In order to capture fine-grained local patterns while reducing dimensionality, the first two layers of the CNN architecture use 3 × 3 kernels with ReLU activation and Max-Pooling. The third layer uses a 5 × 5 kernel with ReLU and dropout to extract broader contextual relationships while improving generalization. The fourth layer uses a 5 × 5 kernel followed by ReLU, flattening, and fully connected layers for classification. Robust learning from spatial and risk elements is made possible by this hierarchical design, which strikes a compromise between local sensitivity and global context extraction. The model consistently performs well across different data partitions and is not biased toward a particular subset, according to the results of leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) and 10-fold cross-validation, which were both used to ensure model stability and dependability.

The comparison research demonstrates that the suggested Fuzzy-CNN method provides a more thorough and data-rich strategy for utilizing aerial photography to assess danger in road infrastructure. In contrast to earlier techniques, Fuzzy-CNN combines GIS-based coordinate referencing with all essential features, including road one-way detection, centerline extraction, cycle lane identification, isolation path detection, and surface and edge features. A more accurate and trustworthy “Low” risk categorization result is produced by this comprehensive incorporation of multi-attribute geographical data, which improves contextual comprehension.

However, despite using high-resolution imagery or LiDAR data, earlier studies like those by Verma et al.49, Li et al.50, and Liu et al.54, lack important features like surface, edge, or cycle lane attributes, which leads to a “High” risk label because of inadequate spatial detail and contextualization. In a similar vein, techniques that only use OSM or VHR miss important characteristics and perform worse when it comes to identifying intricate road features. Interestingly, even research like Lu et al.51, and Xu et al.53, show inadequate feature integration and still come to high-risk conclusions while employing useful datasets like VHR or Space Net. Consequently, the Fuzzy-CNN’s enhanced risk categorization performance over current methods is explained by its greater feature integration, utilization of aerial imagery, and GIS-based precision, all of which together validate its efficacy.

Due to variations in data sources, feature extraction techniques, and research goals, the dataset sizes employed in previous studies vary widely. While Yang et al.52, examined 150 road segments from street view photos and OSM data, Li et al.50used 130 road segments produced from LiDAR points in their small-sample investigations. Xu et al.53, processed 120 road segments using OSM and Space Net data, while Liu et al.54, used roughly 200–250 road segments using aerial photography. Three hundred road image patches from very high-resolution photography were used by Lu et a51. By using 1,000 negative (non-collapse) and 19 positive (collapse) road segments, the suggested Fuzzy-CNN framework, in contrast, maintains a similar number of positive cases as previous small-sample research while offering a bigger pool of negative samples. Particularly for rare-event prediction in urban road risk assessment, this mix of a sizable negative-sample pool with a moderate number of positive occurrences improves model training diversity and promotes robust evaluation.

The Foshan City dataset (2014–2018) was selected because it covers a crucial time frame during which several notable road collapse incidents have place, making it an appropriate basis for creating and evaluating the suggested model. The underlying geotechnical and urban risk elements (such as soil composition, groundwater levels, land use changes, and construction density) are still substantially important for risk assessment, even if we recognize that geological conditions and urban development may have changed since then. The suggested model is made to incorporate current GIS and environmental data in order to ensure temporal validity. This flexibility guarantees that the system can be improved over time using fresher datasets, preserving its relevance in the present. The methodology, especially the combination of CNN and Fuzzy-Delphi-MCDM, was purposefully designed to be adaptable and transferable for generalizability to other cities. The model can be recalculated using city-specific data, guaranteeing robustness in a variety of urban situations, despite the possibility of local geological and infrastructure variations55,56.

The extremely small number of original positive samples (19 occurrences) in our study is another drawback. This limits model training and could result in overfitting to synthetic data. Rare-event modeling frequently faces similar difficulties. For instance, oversampling or resampling strategies have been employed to improve model performance in landslide susceptibility studies when the positive (landslide) occurrences are significantly fewer than the negative ones. Using oversampling, Song et al.,(2023)57found that models trained on balanced datasets performed better than those trained on the original imbalanced data, especially in terms of recall and AUC-type metrics.After comparing many artificial oversampling methods for landslide prediction on extremely unbalanced real-world data, Agrawal et al.58, came to the conclusion that oversampling increased model resilience and sensitivity. Liu et al.59,validated the method using MCMC-based sample augmentation in a landslide environment and discovered that enhanced data increased the area under the ROC curve and classifier accuracy.We acknowledge that synthetic augmentation cannot completely replace actual observed data, even in light of historical precedents. To further verify the generalizability of our findings, we will test the suggested framework on datasets with a greater quantity of positive events in subsequent research.

Limitations and implications

Despite the fact that the suggested Fuzzy-CNN framework performs better when evaluating the risks associated with road infrastructure, there are a few things should be noted. The model’s usefulness in data-scarce places may be limited because its efficacy depends on the quantity and quality of GIS and aerial imaging data, which may not be consistently available in all regions. Furthermore, real-time implementation may be difficult due to the computational difficulty of mixing deep learning and fuzzy logic, especially in settings with constrained resources. The framework’s applicability to other metropolitan regions with different road architectures and data standards requires more testing, as the evaluation carried out in this study is geographically limited. Additionally, there is a class imbalance in the dataset, with low-risk zones predominating, which could cause the model to predict risk conservatively. Fuzzy logic is useful for controlling uncertainty, but it can also make models more complex, which could make it harder for non-technical stakeholders like legislators and urban planners to understand the results. To enhance the practical applicability of the proposed framework and address implementation challenges, a number of improvements are suggested. From a cost-benefit standpoint, the framework’s economic viability for local governments is highlighted by the fact that, despite the initial setup requiring investment in training and computational infrastructure, the long-term benefits such as decreased road collapse frequency, lower maintenance expenditure, and improved public safety far compensate these upfront costs. By combining fuzzy logic with standardized open-source resources like OpenStreetMap, public LiDAR repositories, aerial imagery, and municipal archives, the inherent variability and uncertainty of spatial and infrastructure datasets can be reduced with regard to data availability and quality. This reduces reliance on proprietary databases while increasing reliability. Additionally, the suggested framework shows good compatibility with current smart city and urban management systems by connecting model outputs with decision-support modules for risk monitoring, maintenance scheduling, and emergency response, it can offer practical, data-driven insights that improve urban safety and resilience.

Despite these drawbacks, the study has important consequences. The Fuzzy-CNN framework offers a scalable and adaptable method for locating high-risk areas, facilitating well-informed choices regarding infrastructure development and safety. It has a great deal of promise for integration into smart city systems for proactive monitoring and risk mitigation due to its outstanding performance across important metrics and capacity to integrate diverse road elements. Additionally, by highlighting the necessity for standardized road infrastructure datasets, this study advances the expanding field of uncertainty-aware AI models and can enhance the comparability and consistency of urban risk assessments across various geographic contexts. There are only 19 positive samples in the original collection. Although smaller samples offer insightful information, the limited sample size may restrict the model’s generalizability and raise the possibility of overfitting. To increase robustness, future research could enlarge the dataset or employ sophisticated data augmentation and transfer learning strategies. All things considered, the suggested approach marks a significant breakthrough in geospatial intelligence and risk-informed urban design.

Conclusion

This study uses GIS-integrated spatial data and aerial imagery to show how well the suggested fuzzy-CNN-based structure for road infrastructure risk assessment works. When compared to other approaches, Fuzzy-CNN greatly improves classification and risk prediction accuracy by using a wide range of road variables, such as road directionality, center line, cycle lanes, surface including edge features, and isolation pathways. According to performance review, Fuzzy-CNN regularly performs better than traditional deep neural networks (DNN) on a variety of measures, including testing data with much higher accuracy (98.2%), recall (97.3%), & Dice coefficient (82.1%). These outcomes demonstrate the model’s strong generalization and efficient interpretation of spatial data.

The limits of conventional approaches that rely on sparse or limited feature sets are further highlighted by the comparative analysis of earlier techniques. Due to insufficient contextual understanding, the majority of current methods that use VHR, LiDAR, or OSM data frequently produce high-risk classifications. The Fuzzy-CNN, on the other hand, can handle the imprecision and uncertainty present in metropolitan spatial data thanks to its incorporation of fuzzy logic, which enables a more accurate and nuanced risk assessment, namely in the low-risk category.All things considered, the results confirm that the suggested Fuzzy-CNN model is a scalable, interpretable, and precise method for mapping and assessing urban road risk. It may also find wider use in smart city planning, transport safety, and resilience of infrastructure analysis.

Future research directions

Future research can benefit from the incorporation of more sophisticated machine learning (ML) techniques to address the complexity of urban traffic systems, even though the fuzzy-CNN framework exhibits encouraging results. The ability of machine learning to capture complicated structural behaviors is exemplified by predictive machine learning models used in different engineering contexts, such as the machine learning-based study of stress in carbon- fiber rotating cylinders (Kayıran., 202560. Urban traffic hazard identification could be extended from DL applications in safety-critical infrastructure, including as high-accuracy detection of foreign item debris in aircraft (Mushtaq et al., 202561. Furthermore, multi-agent systems powered by reinforcement learning provide a mechanism to model traffic interactions in real time. These techniques have been demonstrated to maximize adaptive decision-making in dynamic situations in cooperative heterogeneous multi-agent systems that use deep reinforcement learning (Ullah et al., 202562. Intelligent coordination between cars, infrastructure, and planning systems would be made possible by extending such ideas to traffic networks. Incorporating these cutting-edge machine learning techniques will improve real-time decision support, scalability, and adaptability for sustainable urban traffic risk management.The following will be added to our effort in the future to broaden the dataset and findings: Adding historical documents from neighboring provinces and cities to enhance the favorable class, identifying unreported collapse instances by using remote sensing, high-resolution satellite images, and crowdsourcing urban hazard reporting; working with local organizations to gain access to prohibited or unpublished datasets. To modify models trained on bigger datasets from comparable geospatial contexts, transfer learning techniques might also be investigated.
