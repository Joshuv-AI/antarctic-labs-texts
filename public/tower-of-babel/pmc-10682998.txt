Development of N-Terminally Modified Variants of the CXCR4-Antagonistic Peptide EPI-X4 for Enhanced Plasma Stability
Authors: Mirja Harms, Rikke Fabech Hansson, Andrea Gilg, Yasser Almeida-Hernández, Jessica Löffler, Armando Rodríguez-Alfonso, Monica M. W. Habib, Dan Albers, Nermin S. Ahmed, Ashraf H. Abadi, Gordon Winter, Volker Rasche, Ambros J. Beer, Gilbert Weidinger, Nico Preising, Ludger Ständker, Sebastian Wiese, Elsa Sanchez-Garcia, Alexander N. Zelikin, Jan Münch
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
EPI-X4, a natural peptide CXCR4 antagonist, shows potential for treating inflammation and cancer, but its short plasma stability limits its clinical application. We aimed to improve the plasma stability of EPI-X4 analogues without compromising CXCR4 antagonism. Our findings revealed that only the peptide N-terminus is prone to degradation. Consequently, incorporating d-amino acids or acetyl groups in this region enhanced peptide stability in plasma. Notably, EPI-X4 leads 5, 27, and 28 not only retained their CXCR4 binding and antagonism but also remained stable in plasma for over 8 h. Molecular dynamic simulations showed that these modified analogues bind similarly to CXCR4 as the original peptide. To further increase their systemic half-lives, we conjugated these stabilized analogues with large polymers and albumin binders. These advances highlight the potential of the optimized EPI-X4 analogues as promising CXCR4-targeted therapeutics and set the stage for more detailed preclinical assessments.

Introduction

The endogenous peptide inhibitor of CXCR4 (EPI-X4) is a 16-mer peptide that selectively binds the C-X-C chemokine receptor type 4 (CXCR4), a transmembrane protein expressed on various cells, including immune and cancer cells.1,2 EPI-X4 is generated under acidic conditions by proteolysis of human serum albumin (HSA), a highly abundant protein found in the circulation and extravascular space.3 The cleavage is performed by the activity of acidic aspartic proteases, such as cathepsin D and E, which are ubiquitous in blood and the lysosomes of virtually all cells.4 EPI-X4 is composed of HSA residues 408-423 and it binds specifically to CXCR4 via its first seven amino acid residues.5 These amino acids establish interactions with mostly the minor binding pocket of the receptor, which prevents the engagement of CXCL12, the canonical chemokine ligand of CXCR4.5 Consequently, EPI-X4 acts as an endogenous antagonist of CXCR4 and inhibits CXCL12-induced receptor signaling and downstream effects.1,6 EPI-X4 also reduces the basal signaling activity of CXCR4 in the absence of CXCL12, which makes EPI-X4 an inverse agonist of CXCR4.1 Dysregulation or overexpression of CXCL12 and/or CXCR4 is associated with a variety of autoimmune, inflammatory, and cancer diseases, including tumor progression and metastasis.7−9 Thus, CXCR4 represents a promising drug target for the therapy of chronic inflammatory diseases and many types of cancer.10

Currently, the only FDA-approved CXCR4 antagonist is the small molecule AMD3100, which is used for peripheral blood stem cell transplantation but is not suitable for the treatment of solid tumors due to poor pharmacokinetics and adverse effects after prolonged administration.11,12 Therefore, there is a critical need for novel agents targeting CXCR4-related diseases. EPI-X4, with its high receptor specificity, low mitochondrial cytotoxicity, combined antagonistic, and inverse agonist activity, represents an interesting candidate for clinical development.1,2 However, the CXCR4 inhibitory activity of EPI-X4 is insufficient for therapeutic applications, with an IC50 value in the two-digit micromolar range.1 Therefore, to improve the potency of EPI-X4, quantitative structure–activity relationship (QSAR) studies were performed, which identified two 12-mer EPI-X4 derivatives, WSC02 and JM#21, with increased antagonistic efficacy in the nanomolar range.1,6 Further studies on mouse models demonstrated that topical administration of EPI-X4 JM#21 into the lungs or onto the skin effectively prevented airway inflammation and atopic dermatitis, respectively.6 However, all optimized EPI-X4 derivatives suffer from low stability in human plasma, with half-lives of less than 10 min6,13 Thus, there is a need to improve the stability of EPI-X4 derivatives to prolong their bioavailability for future applications as systemically administered drugs.

To this end, we investigated the degradation pathways of optimized EPI-X4 derivatives in human plasma and identified modifications to the protease-sensitive N-terminus to create more stable second-generation variants. Additionally, we assessed their CXCR4 antagonistic activity and plasma stability. Using our recently established all-atomistic biomolecular model of the CXCR4/EPI-X4 complex,5 we identified new lead compounds that are only seven amino acids long and stable in blood. We further conjugated these new leads to large polymers and albumin binders to increase their circulation half-lives in vivo.

Results

Metabolites of EPI-X4 WSC02 and JM#21 in Human Plasma

To clarify how optimized EPI-X4 derivatives are degraded in blood, we analyzed degradation products of EPI-X4 WSC02 and JM#21 after incubation in human plasma using a LC–MS/MS system. EPI-X4 WSC02 and JM#21 were both rapidly degraded with half-lives of 5.2 and 4.0 min, respectively, (Figure 1), confirming previous results.6,13 Interestingly, both peptides were exclusively processed via the N-terminus, while no C-terminal degradation products were detected (Figures 1 and S1). Thus, both optimized EPI-X4 derivatives are sequentially degraded by exopeptidases from the N-terminus, most likely leucyl amino peptidases, as previously reported for the original EPI-X4,1 and targeted modifications of the N-terminal region that prevent proteolytic cleavage may increase peptide stability in the blood.

Design and CXCR4 Binding of N-Terminally Modified EPI-X4 Derivatives

The N-terminus of EPI-X4 and its derivatives are critical for interacting with the CXCR4 binding pocket.5 Variants lacking the N-terminal L (EPI-X4) or I (WSC02 and JM#21) do not bind the receptor and are consequently inactive, explaining the rapid inactivation of the peptides by leucyl aminopeptidases in plasma.5,6,13 To improve plasma half-life, we therefore introduced modifications at the N-terminus of the most advanced 12-mer peptide JM#21.5,6 In total, 26 new JM#21 derivatives with modified N-termini, including variants with substitutions by natural or unnatural amino acids, were designed (Table 1).

All peptides were produced by Fmoc solid-phase peptide synthesis and evaluated for CXCR4 affinities applying a previously established antibody competition assay (Table 1).13 As expected, EPI-X4 replaced the antibody with an IC50 value of ∼2,6 μM, while EPI-X4 WSC02 and JM#21 were more active with IC50 values of 345 and 298 nM, respectively (Figure 2a–d and Table 1), confirming previous results.6,13 Replacement of the I1 in the sequence of JM#21 by acetylated amino acids (1, 2, 8, 9, and 21–24) strongly decreased the binding affinity of the peptide (Figure 2a and Table 1). Also, the substitution of I1 with glycine (G), d-phenylalanine (F), or d-valine (d-V) (6, 7, 13, 14, and 26) or substitution of L2 by d-L (with or without I1 substituted by M) (17–20) led to a decreased activity in the antibody-competition assay (Figure 2b–d). In contrast, the replacement of I1 by d-methionine (d-M) (4 and 16), d-leucine (d-L) (5 and 12), or valine (25) had no effect on CXCR4 binding (Figure 2b,c), and substitution of I1 with methionine (M) (3, 10, and 15) led to a slight enhancement (IC50 values between 103 and 151 nM) of activity (Figure 2b, Table 1). The substitution of L8 to M has only minor effects on CXCR4 affinity (1–14, 18, and 20).

All derivatives presented above, including JM#21, have a length of 12 amino acids. In a recent study, shortened variants of JM#21 were designed and characterized. These variants feature truncations at the C-terminus in combination with C-terminal amidation.5 We here modified the N-terminus of those variants by N-terminal substitution with d-isoleucine (d-I) (27 and 28) to increase their stability in plasma. Both variants with lengths of only 7 (27) and 9 (28) amino acids competed with the CXCR4 antibody with IC50 values of 263 and 453 nM, respectively, and are as active as the 12-mer JM#21 (IC50 of 298 nM) or the clinically approved AMD3100 (IC50 of 402 nM), which was used as a control compound (Figure 2e and Table 1).

Inhibition of CXCR4-Tropic HIV-1

HIV-1 infects cells via interaction with the CD4 receptor and one of the two major coreceptors, either CCR5 or CXCR4.14 Viruses utilizing CD4 and CXCR4 are termed X4-tropic and viral entry can be inhibited by the ligands CXCL12,15 EPI-X4, or AMD3100.1,16 As we have previously shown that an increased CXCR4 antagonizing activity of EPI-X4 derivatives largely correlates with antiviral activity against X4-HIV-1,6,17 we here determined the antiviral activity of the N-terminally modified variants. For this, TZM-bl reporter cells expressing CD4 and CXCR4 were incubated with the respective compounds and infected with X4-HIV-1. Infection rates were determined 3 days later and showed a concentration-dependent inhibition of viral infection by most compounds (Figures 3a,b, and S2 and Table 1). The 16-mer original EPI-X4 suppressed infection with an IC50 value in the higher μM range, whereas the optimized peptides EPI-X4 WSC02 and JM#21 were active with IC50 values of 562 and 126 nM, respectively, in accordance with the previous data.1,6,13,17 Antiviral activities for the N-terminally modified JM#21 variants correlated with values determined for the competition of CXCR4-antibody binding (r = 0.858, p = < 0.005, Pearson correlation). The most active derivatives were those modified by substitution with M (3, 10, and 15), V (25), or d-L (5 and 12) with IC50 values between 115 and 309 nM. The C-terminally truncated 9-mer EPI-X4-28 inhibited HIV-1 with an IC50 value of 551 nM, whereas the smaller 7-mer EPI-X4-27 was significantly less active with an IC50 value of almost 2 μM (Figure 3b).

Inhibition of CXCL12-Mediated Erk Phosphorylation

Next, the potencies of all peptides were determined by measuring the inhibition of CXCL12-mediated activation of the extracellular signal-related kinases (Erks). Erks are members of mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPKs) and are often involved in cancer progression if mutated or dysregulated.18 To analyze the CXCL12-induced inhibition of Erk phosphorylation, SupT1 cells were preincubated with the peptides for 15 min before cells were stimulated with CXCL12. The reaction was stopped after 2 min, and phosphorylated Erk1/Erk2 was subsequently stained with antibodies, and cells were then analyzed by flow cytometry (Figures 3c,d, and S3 and Table 1). In agreement with previous results, the unmodified peptides EPI-X4 WSC02 and JM#21 inhibited Erk activation with IC50 values of 8.1 and 1.4 μM, respectively.5,6 N-terminal modifications of JM#21 resulted in decreased antagonistic activities in most cases, confirming the importance of the peptide N-terminus for receptor interaction and antagonism. All derivatives with reduced activity in the CXCR4-antibody competition assay or the HIV-1 inhibition assay also showed no or largely decreased antagonistic activity for CXCL12-mediated Erk phosphorylation (Table 1). In addition, variants in which I1 was substituted with d-M (4, 11, and 16) did not inhibit CXCL12-mediated Erk activation. Also, L2 seems to be required for inhibition since substitution of L2 by M (10–14) or d-L (16–20) largely prevents the activity of the peptides, although receptor interaction has been shown above, especially for EPI-X4-10 and 12. The substitution of I1 with M (3 and 15) or V (25), however, had no or only a minor impact on the inhibition of Erk activation, with IC50 values between 1.2 and 4.7 μM. The only active 12-mer variant in which I1 was replaced by an unnatural amino acid (d-l) was EPI-X4-5 with an IC50 value of 3.7 μM. The C-terminally truncated and amidated variants EPI-X4-27 and EPI-X4-28, in which I1 was replaced by d-I, both still inhibited CXCL12-mediated phosphorylation of Erk1 and Erk2 with IC50 values of 6.8 and 4.5 μM, respectively (Table 2 and Figure 3d).

Inhibition of CXCL12-Induced β-Arrestin Recruitment

A G-protein-independent downstream signaling pathway of CXCR4 induced by CXCL12 is the recruitment of β-arrestin, which leads to receptor internalization and termination of the signal.7 Therefore, we tested if the EPI-X4 derivatives 4, 5, 12, 19, 27, and 28, which were active in other assays, also interfered with this downstream pathway. As expected, neither AMD3100 nor JM#21 or the other tested derivatives led to β-arrestin recruitment to the receptor if applied in the absence of CXCL12 (Figure S4). However, all ligands led to a dose-dependent inhibition of CXCL12-induced β-arrestin recruitment (Figure 3e). Here, AMD3100 was most active with an IC50 value of about 49 nM, confirming published data.19 JM#21 inhibited signaling with an IC50 value of about 106 nM. N-terminal modifications led to an activity decrease, with EPI-X4-5 and 12 being the most active inhibitors with IC50 values of 257 and 382 nM, respectively. Similar to the data obtained in the Erk-inhibition assay, modifications in EPI-X4-19 led to decreased antagonistic activity (IC50 = 11 μM). The truncated peptides EPI-X4-27 and 28 were as active as the precursor JM#21, with IC50 values of 92 and 118 nM, respectively (Figure 3f).

Inhibition of CXCL12-Mediated Migration

Next, the active derivatives were analyzed for their inhibition of the chemotaxis of cancer cells along a CXCL12 gradient. To this end, migration toward CXCL12 was analyzed after cancer T lymphoblasts were incubated with different concentrations of the peptides and controls. In agreement with previous results, JM#21 inhibited cancer cell migration with an IC50 value of about 1.9 μM. Although the tested N-terminally modified derivatives were moderately less active than the parental peptide, they all dose-dependently inhibited cancer cell migration to a similar level (Figure 3g,h).

Characterization of Leads by Computational Modeling

To gain more insights into the binding of the N-terminally modified peptides in comparison to their precursor JM#21, we performed Gaussian accelerated molecular dynamics (GaMD) simulations20,21 of EPI-X4-5 as well as the truncated versions EPI-X4-27 and 28 in complex with CXCR4 embedded in a phosphatidylcholine (POPC) model membrane. The conformational clustering of the peptides indicates a binding similar to that previously reported for JM#21.5 We categorized the conformations of the complexes during the simulations into five conformational clusters. Figure 4a shows the most populated cluster for the three peptides. As expected, binding motifs were less populated, and nonclustered conformations were more abundant for the 12-mer (5) compared to the shorter variants (27 and 28).

Percentages are reported with respect to the concatenated trajectories. (b) Binding modes of JM#21 (blue), EPI-X4-5 (gray), EPI-X4-27 (orange), and EPI-X4-28 (green) (representative structures). (c) Hydrogen bonds (cyan) and salt bridges (yellow) between CXCR4 and the peptides.

We next computed the root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF) of the peptides’ backbones and the extracellular (EC) coil of CXCR4 (residues 1–30) (Figure S5a). The RMSF values increase toward the C-terminal end of the peptides, which is more exposed to the solvent, while the N-terminus is buried and anchored into the minor subpocket of CXCR4.

We note that the EC coil is very flexible and is involved in the binding and stabilization of CXCL12.22 This coil could interact with the C-terminal region of the peptides. However, the RMSF values of the EC region did not deliver conclusive evidence of conserved binding of the peptides via the coil (Figure S5a, left). To further investigate this, we calculated the distribution of the number of H-bonds between the peptides and CXCR4 as a whole and with the EC coil in particular (Figures 4b,c, and S5b). Figure 4c shows the binding modes of the peptides and the residues of CXCR4 forming H-bonds and salt bridges. The salt bridges established between the charged residues of the peptides and CXCR4 fulfill the function of anchoring the peptide into the binding site. These interactions involve negatively charged amino acids D97, D187, E288, D262, and E277 of CXCR4. Albeit transient, EPI-X4-5 and 28 establish populated H-bonds with C28 and R30 of CXCR4, which are located in the EC region.

Furthermore, all peptides established strong interactions with D97 and D187, which are located at EC loops 1 and 2 of CXCR4, respectively. The binding site of the antibody 12G5 used for the competition experiments is a conformational epitope located on ECL1 and ECL2.23,24 The interactions of the peptides’ residues (in particular, R6) with these amino acids of CXCR4 could affect the conformation of the epitope of 12G5, explaining the results of the competition experiments. Moreover, the flexibility of the C-terminal regions of the peptides, especially EPI-X4-5, could hinder the binding of the antibody. Taken together, the modified derivatives bind to the minor subpocket of CXCR4 in a similar way to JM#21, with a strong preference for acidic residues. Furthermore, the introduction of d-amino acids at position 1 does not negatively affect the binding since the minor subpocket has enough space to accommodate the D-residues.

Stability in Human Plasma

To determine the stability of all generated peptides in human plasma, loss of receptor binding activity over time was quantified using the CXCR4 antibody competition assay, as described previously.13 For this, peptides were spiked into fresh human plasma and incubated at 37 °C. Samples were taken either immediately (t = 0) or after 2 and 8 h. Afterward, samples were serially diluted in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and activities to compete with the CXCR4 antibody were analyzed for all samples in parallel using flow cytometry (Figure S6). The sample concentration, which inhibits 50% antibody binding (IC50) for each time point, was determined by nonlinear regression and normalized to the IC50 value of t = 0. Figure 5a shows the shift of the activity curves for JM#21 upon incubation in plasma. Plasma incubation leads to a 12-fold increase in the apparent IC50 value after 2 h and a 44-fold after 8 h. This corresponds to ∼9 and 3% of the remaining activity, respectively. In contrast, the stabilized EPI-X4-27 showed almost no change in IC50 values after incubation in plasma (Figure 5b), suggesting that this derivative is stable for several hours in human blood plasma.

With the exception of EPI-X4-3, 10, and 15, which all have substitutions of I1 to M, all N-terminally modified peptides were, in general, more stable than their precursor. Most stable peptides were those in which I1 or L2 was replaced by unnatural amino acids, i.e., acetylated amino acids (1, 2, 8, 9, and 21–24) and d-amino acids (4–6, 11–13, and 16–20) (Figures 5c and S6 and Table 1). Of note, the 9-mer EPI-X4-28 similar to the 7-mer EPI-X4-27—retained most, if not all, of its activity after 2 and even 8 h of incubation (Figure 5c and Table 1). Considering their high stability in human plasma and comparably high activity in the different in vitro assays, EPI-X4-4, 5, 12, 19, 27, and 28 were identified as lead compounds.

In Vivo Characterization

The possible toxic effects of lead compounds were evaluated using a widely used in vivo zebrafish embryo model.25 The assay is based on the exposure of zebrafish embryos at a stage of development at which they possess an almost fully developed and functional organ system.5,6 We evaluated the mortality of the embryos after 24 h of exposure, as well as the sublethal cytotoxicity (necrosis and lysis), developmental toxicity (developmental delay and malformations), and toxicity affecting specific organ systems, in particular, cardiotoxicity (heart edema and reduced blood circulation) and neurotoxicity (reduced touch escape response) using a standardized scoring system (Table S1). None of the tested peptides showed any signs of toxic effects and are thus safe for further in vivo investigation (Figure S7).

Three peptides were chosen for in vivo evaluation in C57BL/6 mice—the precursor peptide JM#21, and the two variants with the highest stability in plasma, EPI-X4-19 and 27. 70 μg of the respective peptides in 100 μL of saline were injected into the tail vein of the mice, and a blood sample was taken after 10 min to test for retained and active peptides (Figure 5d). Plasma was obtained by centrifugation, serially diluted in PBS, and tested for the apparent activity to dose-dependently compete with the CXCR4 antibody. The reduction of antibody binding caused by active peptides in the samples was recorded via flow cytometry. Neither for JM#21 nor for the d-amino acid version EPI-X4-19, remaining activity in the plasma samples was detected, most likely due to rapid renal elimination after injection (Figure 5e). For the 7-mer EPI-X4-27, the highest plasma concentration on cells partly inhibited antibody binding, indicating the presence of an active peptide fraction in the plasma samples (Figure 5e).

Synthesis and In Vitro Characterization of EPI-X4-5 and 27 Polymer Conjugates

Peptide optimization presented to this point relates to the affinity of binding at the active site and to the stability of the peptides to proteolytic degradation. Nevertheless, the optimized peptides are small molecules (molar mass below 1500 Da) and underwent fast elimination from the bloodstream, likely via renal secretion (Figure 5e). To counter this, we capitalize on our recent success in the design of macromolecular and supramolecular peptide conjugates.17 Conjugation to water-soluble, hydrophilic poly(ethylene glycol) (PEG) is a clinically validated approach to protraction of biological drugs. This technology is only rivaled by the association of peptides with serum albumin, an endogenous protein with a molar mass of 66.5 kDa and a blood half-life of up to 3 weeks (in humans). We have previously reported on peptide PEGylation and the design of albumin-affine conjugates using a DSPE lipid and cholesterol.17 The most successful conjugates exhibited higher potency of binding to CXCR4 (quantified through competitive binding assays analogous to those presented in Table 1) and, at the same time, higher stability in plasma. Herein, we applied these tools to develop conjugates of the optimized peptide sequences, EPI-X4-5 and 27—two of the most stable and active derivatives designed in the present study and made the first steps toward quantifying retention of the conjugates in blood. Sequences of the peptides used for conjugation, schematic illustrations of conjugates, and chemical formulas of albumin binders and PEG linkers are presented in Figure 6a–c. An overview of all conjugates designed in this study is presented in Table 2.

PEGylation was performed using commercially available polymer samples with molar masses of 5 or 20 kDa and equipped with the maleimide functionality via the peptide cysteine thiol (Figure 6a). A sample of PEG (20 kDa) was also available as a homobifunctional dimaleimide telechelic molecule and was used to prepare a conjugate with a peptide copy on each end of the synthetic polymer. Using the same conjugation chemistry, we have also prepared peptide conjugates with cholesterol-PEG and a new conjugate based on the trityl (Trt-) group, which we have previously shown to be an albumin binder.26 Trt-based prodrugs proved to afford the highest tumor accumulation within a panel of macromolecular and supramolecular glucuronides administered as cancer prodrug monotherapy.26 Finally, a sample of PEG (20 kDa) was used to generate a Y-shaped peptide dimer using a bis-sulfone reagent (Figure 6d). The latter has been originally developed for disulfide rebridging applications. In our hands, it has proven useful to create peptide conjugates with a divalent binding to the dimer form of CXCR4. Conjugate characterization was performed via MALDI and HPLC (representative data are shown in Figure 6e). In all cases, a shift in molecular weight consistent with the conjugation of one or two peptides to the polymer was confirmed by MALDI (Tables S2–S4). At the same time, HPLC validated that purification was efficient in removing the nonconjugated peptide from the final preparation of the conjugate (Figure 6).

The activity of the peptide conjugates was determined by antibody competition, as described above. Conjugation to PEG or TrtS-PEG decreased the potency of the binding of the peptide to CXCR4 (Table 2 and Figure S8). Only conjugation to chol-PEG had no impact on CXCR4 interaction. For EPI-X4-5, the Y-shaped PEG-conjugate was more active than the monovalent conjugate (8-fold and 2-fold for EPI-X4-5 and 27 conjugates, respectively), which might be attributed to interaction with CXCR4 in its dimeric form and confirms our previous results17 (Figure S8). We then tested the stability of all conjugates in human blood plasma using the antibody competition assay, as described above. Interestingly, while EPI-X4-5 conjugates did not lose activity during incubation time in plasma, EPI-X4-27 conjugates were less stable than the unconjugated peptide (Table 2 and Figure S9). Moreover, the activity of EPI-X4-5 conjugates to compete with the CXCR4 antibody seems to increase after incubation in plasma, making these candidates promising for further in vivo evaluation (Figures S9a and S10).

In Vivo Characterization of EPI-X4-5 and 27 Polymer Conjugates

Retention of peptide activity in blood circulation was quantified in mice using selected peptide conjugates: three EPI-X4-5 conjugates (30, 32, and 34) and four EPI-X4-27 conjugates (35, 36, 38, and 40). For comparison, we also included the telechelic dimeric version of JM#21 (JM#21 telech.), which was designed in our previous paper.17 Of note, cholesterol conjugates were excluded from in vivo evaluation due to minor toxic effects, observed in our previous study.17 Due to their large molecular weight compared to the unconjugated peptides, the conjugates were dosed at 35 mg/kg intravenously (700 μg/mouse in 100 μL saline). Apparent activity in blood plasma was determined after 4 h via the antibody displacement assay, as described above (Figure 7). For this, plasma samples were diluted 1:1 with the CXCR4 antibody and added to the CXCR4-expressing cells. The reduction of antibody binding caused by active peptides in the samples was recorded via flow cytometry.

The telechelic JM#21 conjugate, which has been designed in our previous study, exhibits a notably higher in vitro activity (IC50 = 0.8 μM17) compared to all other conjugates tested herein (IC50 = 1.2–13.2 μM). Despite that, plasma from mice injected with the JM#21 conjugate displaced only 12% of the antibody signal on cells, indicating the minimal presence of active peptide conjugate in the plasma. However, in contrast, several of the plasma samples from mice injected with the stabilized peptide variants retained their activity in inhibiting CXCR4 antibody binding. Particularly noteworthy are the plasma samples containing either the stabilized EPI-X4-5 telechelic version (34) or the EPI-X4-27 telechelic version (40), which retained more activity than the JM#21 version after 4 h of injection (Figure 7) (34% and 43%, respectively). Additionally, the PEG20-5 monomer (32) displayed some activity, suggesting a slower inactivation of the compound in vivo compared to that of the JM#21 variant. While samples containing the monovalent PEG20-27 conjugate (38) were only slightly more active than those containing the dimeric JM#21 conjugate, they were less active than the respective EPI-X4-5 conjugate (32). Hence, it is evident that the extent of antibody competition in the plasma samples relative to the activity observed in vitro strongly depends on both the PEG architecture (telechelic or monovalent) and the peptide sequence. Regarding the TrtS-conjugates (30 and 36), as well as the EPI-X4-27 coupled to a 5 kDa PEG (35), they displayed only partial activity in competing with the antibody (Figure 7). These findings emphasize that PEGylation using a 5 kDa polymer sample, whether with or without the presence of the albumin-binding Trt group, did not sufficiently prolong the blood residence time of the peptides. However, the use of a 20 kDa polymer sample for PEGylation led to a favorable improvement in the blood residence of the peptide.

Discussion and Conclusions

The optimized JM#21 derivative of the natural CXCR4 antagonist EPI-X4 showed promising therapeutic effects in different animal models of inflammatory diseases and cancer6,27 but suffered from poor stability in the blood.6 We here show that JM#21 is proteolytically processed by endopeptidases in plasma that exclusively degrade the peptide from the N-terminus while leaving the C-terminus intact. The peptide N-terminus (I1 in JM#21) is essential for receptor interaction and antagonistic activity, and a single truncation of the N-terminal L or I residue in EPI-X4 or JM#21 abrogates CXCR4 binding and antagonism.1 Thus, extensive changes at the N-terminal end of EPI-X4 and analogues are not tolerated.

To generate proteolytically resistant peptides, we introduced only subtle changes at the N-terminus, mainly by replacing I1 with non-natural D- and/or acetylated amino acids, which are largely resistant to proteolysis and are well-known modifications to prevent enzymatic degradation of peptides. Our analyses confirmed that most exchanges at the N-terminus of JM#21 result in more stable peptides, but at the cost of receptor binding and antagonizing activity. Replacement of I1 and L2 with homologue and nonhomologue natural l-amino acid residues, e.g., M (3), G (7), or V (25) only moderately affected receptor binding but did not increase stability. On the other hand, the introduction of an acetyl group at the peptide N-terminus always resulted in a marked loss of receptor binding and antagonizing activity, probably due to the hydrophobic nature of the bottom of the minor subpocket formed by Y45, F87, L91, W94, Y116, and F292, which might have unfavorable interactions with the acetyl group. However, some derivatives containing d-amino acids (e.g., 4, 5, 12, 27, and 28) showed similar CXCR4 inhibitory activity as JM#21 but were largely resistant to proteolytic degradation by plasma enzymes. Remarkably, none of these peptides showed any signs of lethal or sublethal cytotoxicity, nor developmental, cardio- or neurotoxicity in zebrafish embryos, even at very high doses, advocating for further studies to determine the pharmacokinetic properties in rodent models.

Small, linear peptides are typically subjected to renal filtration, reducing their circulation half-lives. To mitigate this, we conjugated stabilized EPI-X4 derivatives to large polymers and albumin binders, an approach we and others have previously demonstrated to be effective.17 Notably, all PEG-conjugated peptides from our study, particularly the EPI-X4-5 conjugates, exhibited extended plasma half-lives, likely due to the steric hindrance provided by the sizable PEG groups.28 It is essential to understand that in vivo clearance factors, such as cell-associated enzymes and receptor-mediated degradation, can differ. Thus, plasma stability might not directly correspond to in vivo half-lives postintravenous injection in mice. To further optimize systemic half-lives, we are considering the use of higher molecular weight PEGs, such as PEG 40 kDa, given their known impact on blood clearance and pharmacokinetics.29 Although PEGylation slightly reduced the anti-CXCR4 activity, it remains a promising strategy for enhancing the systemic longevity of these EPI-X4 derivatives, paving the way for the next-generation EPI-X4 analogue optimization.

While computational models suggest that d-amino acids are tolerated at the peptide’s N-terminal end, SAR studies highlight a preference for l-amino acids, like M or I, at this position in terms of affinity. Moving forward, our design endeavors aim to strike a balance between peptide affinity and in vivo plasma stability. Often, these two parameters exhibit inverse trends; however, by adjusting factors like formulation and conjugation, it is feasible to enhance peptide activity with only minor alterations to its inherent structure. By achieving both prolonged half-lives and robust receptor binding affinities, we anticipate an extended therapeutic window, potentially decreasing dosing frequency and thereby possibly boosting patient adherence in the long run. Such combined merits amplify the therapeutic promise of EPI-X4 derivatives for managing conditions influenced by aberrant CXCR4 signaling, including cancer and inflammatory diseases. Comprehensive studies are essential to fully decipher the translational capacity of EPI-X4 derivatives in both preclinical and clinical domains.

Methods

Peptide Synthesis

All chemicals were used as provided by the manufacturers. Amino acids were purchased from Novabiochem (Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany). N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF), 20% (v/v) piperidine in DMF, O-benzotriazole-N,N,N′,N′-tetramethyluronium-hexafluoro-phosphate (HBTU), and trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) were purchased from Merck Millipore (Merck KGaA). Triisopropylsilane (TIS) and diisopropylethylamine (DIEA) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Sigma-Aldrich Chemie GmbH Munich, Germany). Acetonitrile was purchased from J.T. Baker (Avantor Performance Materials B.V. 7418 AM in Deventer, The Netherlands). The peptides were synthesized automatically on a 0.10 mmol scale using standard Fmoc solid-phase peptide synthesis techniques with a microwave synthesizer (Liberty blue; CEM). A preloaded resin was used and provided in the reactor. The resin was washed with DMF. The Fmoc protecting group was removed with 3 mL of 20% (v/v) piperidine in DMF and initialized with a microwave, followed by washing with DMF. An amount of five equivalents of amino acids was added to the reactor, and then 5 equiv of HBTU was dosed into the amino acid solution. Subsequently, an amount of 10 equiv of DIEA was added to the resin. The coupling reaction proceeded with microwaves for a few minutes, and then the resin was washed in DMF. These steps were repeated for all amino acids in the sequence. The last amino acid was deprotected. Once the synthesis was completed, the peptide was cleaved in 95% (v/v) TFA, 2.5% (v/v) TIS, and 2.5% (v/v) H2O for 1 h. The peptide residue was precipitated and washed with cold diethyl ether (DEE) by centrifugation. The peptide precipitate was then allowed to dry under an air flow to remove residual ether. The peptide was purified using reversed-phase preparative high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC; Waters) in an acetonitrile/water gradient under acidic conditions on a Phenomenex C18 Luna column (5 mm pore size, 100 Å particle size, 250 × 21.2 mm). Following purification, the peptide was lyophilized on a freeze-dryer (Labconco) for storage prior to use. The molecular mass of the pure peptide was verified by liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LCMS; Waters). AMD3100 octahydrochloride hydrate was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (#A5602) and dissolved in H2O. CXCL12 was purchased from PeproTech (#300-28A) and dissolved at a concentration of 100 μg/mL in H2O.

All compounds are >95% pure by HPLC analysis.

Peptide Bioconjugation

General Procedure for Maleimide Monoconjugations

To an Eppendorf-tube containing the monovalent maleimide reagent (1 equiv, Table S2) was added the native peptide (3–6 equiv) in HEPES buffer (pH 6.5, 10 mM), resulting in a final polymer concentration of 5 mM (OBS: cholesterol-containing conjugations were conducted in 1:1 MeOH/buffer). The reaction mixture was incubated for 24–72 h at 25 °C before it was purified by gel filtration (stationary phase: Sephadex G-25, 45 cm, and mobile phase: Milli-Q water, 1 mL fractions). The collected fractions were analyzed with UV–vis spectroscopy to determine the presence of peptide, combined, and finally lyophilized (Figure S15).

General Procedure for Maleimide Diconjugation

To an Eppendorf-tube containing diMal-PEG20 kDa (1.0 equiv) was added the native peptide (6 equiv) in HEPES buffer (pH 6.5, 10 mM), resulting in a final polymer concentration of 5 mM. The reaction mixture was incubated for 24 h at 25 °C before it was purified by gel filtration (stationary phase: Sephadex G-25, 45 cm, and mobile phase: MiliQ water, 1 mL fractions). The collected fractions were analyzed with UV–vis spectroscopy at 280 nm to determine the presence of peptide and combined, respectively, before it was lyophilized, resulting in the peptide conjugate as a white solid (Figure S16 and Table S3).

General Procedure for Bis-sulfone Conjugations

To an Eppendorf-tube containing the bis-alkylating reagent (1 equiv) was added excess native peptide (10 equiv) dissolved in CHES buffer (50 mM with 10 mM EDTA, pH 9.6), resulting in a final polymer concentration of 5 mM. The reaction mixture was incubated at 50 °C for 48–72 h before it was purified by gel filtration (stationary phase: Sephadex G-25, 402010033-45 cm, and mobile phase: MiliQ water, 1 mL fractions). The collected fractions were analyzed with UV–vis spectroscopy at 280 nm to determine the presence of peptide and combined, respectively, before it was lyophilized, resulting in the peptide conjugate as a white solid (Figure S17 and Table S4).

Mass Spectrometry to Determine Peptide Stability in Plasma

A 50 μL-aliquot of human plasma was spiked with 7 μM JM#21 or WSC02. The mixture was stirred for a few seconds and incubated at 37 °C. Five microliter aliquots were separated after 0, 5, 10, 20, 30, 45, 60, 90, and 120 min, respectively. The aliquots were immediately diluted with 1.5 mL of 6 M GuHCl + 10 mM DTT + 50 mM NH4HCO3 and analyzed. The experiment was performed in triplicate. A 15 μL aliquot of every sample was analyzed by mass spectrometry on an Orbitrap Elite Hybrid mass spectrometry system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Bremen, Germany), as previously described.31

For visualization in XCalibur Qual Browser 2.0 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Bremen, Germany), extracted ion chromatograms (XICs) of the most intense isotope (±20 ppm), as theoretically predicted, at z = 3 were calculated. Using default parameters within the QualBrowser, peak areas were calculated and exported. Half-lives were determined by fitting the data (peak area vs time) to a one-phase exponential decay model using GraphPad Prism 8.4.3 (GraphPad Prism software LLC).

Computational Modeling

Peptides

The peptides were built manually based on the model of the binding mode of the CXCR4/EPI-X4 complex reported by Sokkar et al.5 The backbone of EPI-X4 was used as a template. The residues were mutated at the corresponding positions, and when necessary, residues of the C-terminal region were deleted using UCSF Chimera.32

Docking

The peptides were initially docked without the N-terminal D-residue using HADDOCK,33 with the Peptide protocol set on the default parameters, thus obtaining 200 solutions. The top 10 solutions were further refined with FlexPepDock,34 generating a total of 10,000 solutions (1000 per initial solution). The top 10% of the solutions were clustered with the VMD35 plug-in (https://github.com/luisico/clustering), with a RMSD = 2 Å for all atoms of the peptides. Three representative structures of the clusters, mostly bound to the minor subpocket of CXCR4, were selected for further MD simulations. For EPI-X4,27 a representative structure bound to the major pocket was also selected to extend the sampling.

MD Simulations

The D-residue was added to the N-terminal region of the peptides (d-Leu for EPI-X4-5 and d-Ile for EPI-X4-27 and 28) with the D_SwissSide chain plug-in36,37 of UCSF Chimera. All the systems were prepared using CHARMM-GUI Membrane Builder.38,39 CXCR4/peptide complexes were embedded in a lipid bilayer (257 POPC molecules) with 100 Å × 100 Å dimensions. The systems were solvated using the TIP3P water model,40 and 50 mM KCl was added. After minimization, equilibration runs were performed in 6 steps of 500 ps, applying harmonic position restraints with a starting force constant of 10 kcal/mol·Å2 on the atoms of the protein and the lipid heads, which was gradually reduced to zero. Long-range electrostatic interactions were calculated with the Particle Mesh Ewald method.41 Short-range Lennard-Jones and electrostatic interactions were calculated with a switching function between 10 Å and a cutoff value of 12 Å. The temperature was maintained at 300 K using the Langevin dynamics method,42 and the Nose–Hoover piston method was employed for keeping the pressure at 1 atm.43 Prior to the production runs, additional equilibration steps were carried out, which included a short classical MD simulation of 5 ns followed by 95 ns of GaMD where the force bias potential was calculated for its application in the production runs. For the biasing potential, the threshold value was set to the maximum potential energy sampled during the previous equilibration step. A maximum value of 10 kT was employed to control the standard deviation of the biasing potential. Three replicas of GaMD simulations21 were performed for each system (200 ns for each replica) with a time step of 2 fs. The simulations were carried out with the NAMD software (version 2.13)44 and the CHARMM36 force field.45

Analysis of the Trajectories

For the analysis, the last 200 ns of each replica was concatenated (for a total of 600 ns for each system). All of the frames were aligned onto the transmembrane domain (TMD, residues 30-303) of CXCR4. The clustering analysis of the backbone of the peptides was performed using the VMD plug-in with a cutoff RMSD = 2 Å. The RMSD was calculated with VMD. RMSF and H-bonds were calculated using the corresponding tools included in the GROMACS suite.46 The visualization and analysis of trajectories and structures were performed using VMD and UCSF Chimera.

Inhibition of β-Arrestin Recruitment to CXCR4

Inhibition of CXCL12-induced recruitment of β-arrestin-2 to CXCR4 was measured by the NanoBiT protein–protein-interaction assay (Promega Corporation) in a modified antagonistic protocol based on the system established by Hwang et al.47 In short, 2 × 104 HEK293T cells were seeded in clear 96-well flat well plates in 92 μL growth medium overnight at 37 °C. The next day, cells were transiently transfected with 50 ng SmBiT-β-arrestin-2 plasmid and 50 ng CXCR4-LgBiT plasmid (generously provided by Jong-Ik Hwang) using TransIT-LT1 transfection reagent (Mirus Bio LLC, Madison, WC, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After 16 h transfection, the medium was changed to 100 μL Opti-MEM (Gibco), and cells were placed back at 37 °C for 20 min. Subsequently, 25 μL of the Nano-Glo Live Cell Assay System prepared according to the manufacturer’s instructions was added to cells, and luminescence was measured with a Synergy H1 (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA) set to 37 °C for 10 min. Compounds were titrated in PBS and added for 10 min before cells were stimulated with 30 nM CXCL12. The luminescence was measured for 2 h. Kinetics were plotted in GraphPad Prism (version 9.4.0) from triplicate, and the area under the curve was calculated for each treatment. The signal in the buffer control without inhibitor was defined as the background and set to 0%. CXCL12-induced signal without an inhibitor was set at 100%.

Chemotaxis

Migration assays toward a 100 ng/mL CXCL12 gradient were performed with SupT1 cells using 96-well transwell assay plates (Corning Incorporated, Kennebunk, ME, USA) with 5 μm polycarbonate filters. First, 0.75 × 105 SupT1 cells resuspended in assay buffer (RPMI supplemented with 0.1% bovine serum albumin) were seeded into the upper chamber in the presence or absence of the compound, reaching a final volume of 50 μL, and allowed to settle down for 15 min. In the meantime, 200 μL of assay buffer supplemented with or without 100 ng/mL CXCL12 as well as compounds was filled into a 96-well-V plate. Cells were allowed to migrate toward CXCL12 by putting the upper chamber onto the 96-well-V plate. After a migration time of 4 h at 37 °C (5% CO2), lower compartments were analyzed for cell content by the CellTiter-Glo assay (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). Percentages of migrated cells were calculated as described before48 and normalized to the CXCL12-only control.

In Vivo Experiments

Toxicity in Zebrafish Embryos

For in vivo toxicity studies in zebrafish (Danio rerio), wild-type embryos were dechorionated at 24 h postfertilization (hpf) using digestion with 1 mg/mL Pronase (Sigma) in E3 medium (83 μM NaCl, 2.8 μM KCl, 5.5 μM 202 CaCl2, and 5.5 μM MgSO4). In a 96-well plate, three embryos per well were exposed for 24 h to 100 μL of E3-containing test peptides at the concentrations indicated in the figures. Two independent assays were performed, each with 10 × 3 embryos. The peptide solvent (PBS), diluted in E3, was used as a negative control at the same amount as introduced by the highest concentration of peptide stock. As a positive control for cytotoxicity, the pleurocidin antimicrobial peptide NRC-03 (GRRKRKWLRRIGKGVKIIGGAALDHL-NH2) was used at a concentration of 6 μM.49 Abamectin at a concentration of 3.125 μM was used as a positive control for neurotoxicity. At 48 hpf (after 24 h of incubation), embryos were scored in a stereomicroscope for signs of cytotoxicity (lysis and/or necrosis), developmental toxicity (delay and/or malformations), or cardiotoxicity (heart edema or reduced or absent circulation). Each embryo was also touched with a needle, and a reduced or absent touch response (escape movements) was evaluated as signs of neurotoxicity if and only if no signs of cytotoxicity were present in the same embryo. Embryos were categorized within each of these toxicity categories into several classes of severity, as detailed in Figure S7 and Table S1.

Mouse Experiments

Either 700 μg of EPI-X4-5 or 27 conjugates or 70 μg of unconjugated JM#21, EPI-X4-19, or 27 in 100 μL of 0.9% NaCl was intravenously injected in the tail vein of male or female C57BL/6NCrl (BL6) mice (unconjugated peptides) or SCID mice (peptide conjugates). After the indicated times, mice were killed by cervical dislocation, and the blood was sampled by immediate heart punctation. Blood was 19:1 diluted with 0.16 M NaEDTA and centrifuged at 2000g for 20 min at 4 °C to obtain plasma. Plasma was stored at −80 °C before being used for peptide detection. Plasma aliquots were stored at −80 °C until the remaining peptide activity in the plasma was determined by antibody-competition assay. For this, 100% of the plasma samples were diluted in PBS and added to CXCR4 expressing SupT1 cells together with a constant concentration of the fluorescently labeled CXCR4 antibody (clone 12G5). After 2 h, the unbound antibody was removed and cells were analyzed via flow cytometry. Mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) values were normalized to the PBS controls.

Statistics

IC50 values were determined by nonlinear regression curve fit using GraphPad Prism (version 9.3.1). For the toxicity assay in zebrafish embryos, the Chi-squared test was used to calculate whether the distribution of embryos into toxicity classes differed significantly between the PBS negative control and the test substances.
