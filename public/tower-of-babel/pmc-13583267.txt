Rapidly progressive calcinosis cutis in adult-onset dermatomyositis: A case report
Authors: Elona Bebla, Attiya Haroon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Calcinosis cutis is an uncommon but clinically significant complication of dermatomyositis, occurring in approximately 11%–20% of adult patients. We report a case of rapidly progressive, extensive calcinosis cutis in a 52-year-old woman with adult-onset dermatomyositis. Despite ongoing systemic immunosuppressive therapy, she developed biopsy-confirmed calcinosis within 3 months of initial presentation, with subsequent extension to the bilateral axillae, anterior chest wall, and proximal thighs causing functional impairment. Myositis-specific antibody panel results are pending. This case illustrates the aggressive potential of calcinosis in adult dermatomyositis, the limited therapeutic options once calcification is established, and the importance of early aggressive disease control.

Introduction

Dermatomyositis (DM) is an idiopathic inflammatory myopathy characterized by proximal muscle weakness and characteristic cutaneous findings including heliotrope erythema, Gottron’s papules, the shawl sign, and mechanic’s hands. Calcinosis cutis—the dystrophic deposition of insoluble calcium salts in the skin and subcutaneous tissues despite normal serum calcium and phosphate levels—is an uncommon but clinically significant complication of the disease.1,2

In adults with DM, calcinosis occurs in approximately 11%–20% of patients and is usually associated with long-standing or inadequately controlled disease. 3 In contrast, calcinosis occurs in up to 40% of patients with juvenile DM and is more closely linked to persistent inflammatory activity. 4 In adults, longer disease duration, fingertip ulcers, and anti-nuclear matrix protein-2 (anti-NXP2) antibodies have been identified as consistent risk factors.3,5

Calcific deposits may present as superficial nodules (calcinosis circumscripta), tumoral masses, or sheet-like calcification along myofascial planes (calcinosis universalis).1,2 These lesions may ulcerate, become infected, or restrict joint movement. Management remains challenging, as no randomized clinical trials have established an effective therapy specifically targeting calcinosis.1,2,6

We report a case of rapidly progressive, extensive calcinosis cutis in a 52-year-old woman with adult-onset DM.

Case presentation

A 52-year-old woman with a history of DM presented to Rao Dermatology on April 28, 2025, with two painful nodules on the medial thigh that had developed over several weeks. She was receiving systemic immunomodulatory therapy at the time of presentation.

Biopsy of one lesion demonstrated superficial and deep perivascular lymphocytic inflammation consistent with active cutaneous DM. Her treatment regimen was reviewed and continued.

Over subsequent visits, the patient developed progressive cutaneous deposits. A repeat biopsy on July 9, 2025, confirmed calcinosis cutis, demonstrating dystrophic calcium deposition within the dermis and subcutaneous tissue in the setting of normal serum calcium and phosphate levels.

Over the following months, calcification extended to the bilateral axillae, anterior chest wall, and proximal thighs. The lesions were firm and variably tender. At the time of this report, they limited shoulder abduction and hip flexion, causing pain that interfered with daily activities. Patient continues to endorse intermittent erythema and parenthesia to right medial thigh.

A myositis-specific antibody panel—including anti-NXP2, anti-MDA5, anti-TIF1-γ, anti-Mi-2, and anti-Jo-1—along with muscle enzymes (creatine kinase and aldolase) and cross-sectional imaging were ordered. Results show elevated aldolase 11.4, reference range <7.7 U/L. CK 1240 U/L, reference range 26–192 U/L in women, and positive anti-NXP2 autoantibodies.

The patient is currently being managed collaboratively between dermatology and rheumatology. Prior to the current regimen, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered without significant therapeutic benefit. Her current treatment regimen is monthly 1cc of s﻿teroid ILK 5 injections directly to calcinosis cutis deposits. Concurrently, rheumatology initiated tocilizumab (Acterma), an interleukin-6 receptor antagonist, to address underlying inflammatory process of DM.

Discussion

Calcinosis is an uncommon complication of adult DM and typically develops in the setting of prolonged disease activity. The rapid progression observed in this case—from biopsy-confirmed inflammation in April 2025 to confirmed calcinosis by July—represents an accelerated course, although rapid progression has been described in adult DM.5,7

In a cohort study of 126 adults with DM, Valenzuela et al. 3 reported calcinosis in 11.1% of patients. Affected individuals had longer disease duration, more frequent fingertip ulcers, and a strong association with anti-NXP2 antibodies. Disease duration and fingertip ulcers remained significant predictors across multivariate analyses. 3 Jiang et al. 5 reported similar findings and additionally identified dysphagia and younger age at diagnosis as independent risk factors. Autoantibody testing in our patient further clarifies her risk profile and accelerated progression.

The distribution of calcinosis in this case—axillary, chest wall, and proximal thigh involvement—is consistent with the truncal and proximal extremity pattern commonly observed in adult DM.3,8 Severe cases may progress to calcinosis universalis and may be complicated by infection, ulceration, or abscess formation. 7

The pathogenesis of calcinosis in DM is incompletely understood. The deposits are dystrophic and occur in damaged tissues despite normal systemic calcium and phosphate levels.1,4 Chronic microvascular injury and tissue ischemia likely contribute and may explain the association between calcinosis and digital ulceration. 3 Mitochondrial dysfunction and interferon-mediated oxidative stress have also been proposed as contributing mechanisms. Davuluri et al. 2 described a pathway in which mitochondrial damage leads to the release of calcified debris that promotes Interleukin-1beta (IL-1β), a pro inflammatory cytokine, and interferon signaling, potentially perpetuating inflammation, and calcification. 2 These findings have generated interest in Janus kinase inhibition as a potential therapeutic strategy.

Management of calcinosis remains challenging, as no therapy has demonstrated consistent efficacy. Evidence is limited to case reports, small series, and retrospective studies.1,2,6 IVIG has the strongest evidence for treatment of DM overall. The phase III landmark ProDERM trial demonstrated significant clinical improvement in adults with active DM receiving IVIG compared with placebo, leading to regulatory approval of IVIG for patients with calcinosis cutis. 9 In our patient, IVIG was administered without benefit, consistent with reports of variable response in calcinosis-specific outcomes. Although calcinosis was not specifically evaluated, disease control may indirectly reduce progression.

Sodium thiosulfate has been reported in case series to improve symptoms when administered intralesionally or systemically, although responses are variable and evidence remains limited.1,2 Bisphosphonates, diltiazem, minocycline, and colchicine have also been reported with inconsistent results.1,2,5,6 Surgical excision may be considered for isolated symptomatic lesions but carries risks including impaired wound healing, recurrence, and infection in immunosuppressed patients.1,7 In diffuse disease such as that observed in this case, surgical management is generally limited. In our patient, monthly ILK 5 injections have resulted in mild symptomatic improvement, and tocilizumab therapy, proceeding marginal benefit to date. The patient continues to present monthly for repeat injections and ongoing monitoring between dermatology and rheumatology. Early and effective control of inflammatory disease appears to be the most important modifiable factor in preventing calcinosis. Studies in juvenile DM have demonstrated that early aggressive immunosuppression reduces the risk of calcinosis, suggesting that cumulative tissue injury during uncontrolled disease contributes to its development (Figures 1 and 2).3,4

Conclusion

Calcinosis cutis is an uncommon but potentially disabling complication of adult DM. This case illustrates rapid progression of extensive calcinosis despite ongoing immunosuppressive therapy and highlights the limited evidence available to guide treatment once calcification develops. Positive anti-NXP2 autoantibodies, markedly elevated creatinine kinase (CK) enzyme and a lack of response to IVIG treatment were notable features of this case. Mild improvement with ILK 5 injections and tocilizumab therapy reflects the refractory nature of established calcinosis cutis. Management remains individualized and focuses on aggressive control of underlying disease activity, consideration of therapies such as IVIG or sodium thiosulfate, and multidisciplinary care. Further studies are needed to better define the mechanisms and optimal management strategies for calcinosis in DM.
