Dermoscopic Hallmarks of Lymphomatoid Papulosis: A Case Series of Five Histopathologically Proven Lesions
Authors: Caterina Mariarosaria Giorgio, Gabriella Brancaccio, Stefano Caccavale, Paola Vitiello, Giuseppe Argenziano, Gaetano Licata
Published: 2026

Introduction

Lymphomatoid papulosis (LyP) is a chronic, recurrent CD30+ cutaneous lymphoproliferative disorder that exhibits a paradoxical dual nature: histologically malignant yet clinically benign. The variable clinical presentation, often mimicking benign inflammatory, infectious or neoplastic dermatoses, makes early diagnosis challenging. Although dermoscopy is widely used for melanocytic and inflammatory diseases, its role in cutaneous lymphoproliferative disorders remains underexplored, particularly in LyP. Only a few case reports have provided limited dermoscopic data, and no consistent pattern has yet been validated [1–2].

Case Presentation

We report five histologically confirmed cases of LyP documented over the past decade at the Dermatology Unit of the University of Campania Luigi Vanvitelli, each supported by both clinical and dermoscopic imaging. In our series, the lesions were located on the lower limbs or trunk, and all cases were CD30-positive at immunohistochemistry. The clinical presentation ranged from papules and plaques to one nodular lesion. A recurring dermoscopic triad was observed across the non-nodular lesions: 1) a disrupted peripheral collarette of whitish scale, (2) a central milky-red to pinkish-white structureless area, and (3) asymmetrically distributed peripheral clusters of dotted and glomerular vessels. These features were variably accompanied by crusting, radial white streaks, or linear vascular structures (Figure 1A–B–C–D). One lesion, located on the abdominal flank and clinically nodular, differed in appearance, displaying a more compact, reddish-orange background devoid of central scale or vascular architecture. This lesion lacked a peripheral collarette and exhibited only peripheral isolated dotted vessels (Figure 2A–B). Histopathology confirmed it as LyP type C, whereas the others were classified as types A or B. This contrast reinforces the value of dermoscopy in raising suspicion and guiding biopsy toward histopathologically representative areas. Our findings resonate with previous isolated reports. Moura et al [3]. described dermoscopy of LyP as heterogeneous, occasionally showing central ulceration, glomerular vessels, or amorphous white areas, but without pattern reproducibility. Caccavale et al [4]. later identified different dermoscopic phases of LyP lesions, including milky-red structureless zones and white scales in early lesions, with increased vascular prominence and crusting in later stages. These observations, though valuable, stem from single-case experiences. Our present data support and expand upon these earlier findings, proposing a dermoscopic triad that may serve as a clinical clue in patients with recurrent or polymorphic papulonodular eruptions.

Conclusion

While dermoscopic features in LyP are not pathognomonic, their recognition may raise clinical suspicion and help distinguish LyP from its mimickers, such as arthropod bite reactions, pityriasis lichenoides, or early-stage mycosis fungoides. Moreover, identifying asymmetrically clustered vessels and a disrupted peripheral collarette may assist in selecting the most appropriate lesion for biopsy. Given the frequent self-resolution of LyP lesions, such directed sampling is critical to prevent underdiagnosis or misclassification. Future multicentric studies with larger cohorts are needed to confirm the reproducibility and specificity of this dermoscopic pattern, possibly paving the way for improved non-invasive diagnostic algorithms.
