Rapidly increasing kidney cancer burden among young adults: insights from the 1990–2021 Global Burden of Disease Study
Authors: Ming Hu, Zishuai Li, Zheyun Niu, Zhan Ding, Ping Li, Gaifen Liu, Xiong Zhou, Zheng Li, Yan Liu, Rui Pu, Xiaojie Tan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundKidney cancer (KC) is a major global health concern. Considering the significant impact of premature morbidity and mortality in young adults on the global disease burden, it is essential to conduct a longitudinal study on KC in young adults to develop cost-effective interventions.MethodsAge-standardized rates (ASRs) including incidence rate (ASIR), mortality rate (ASMR), and disability-adjusted life-years (DALYs) rate (ASDR), and ASDR attributable to risk factors (obesity, smoking, exposure to trichloroethylene, etc.) of KC were analyzed using Global Burden of Disease Study 2021 data (1990–2021), in the whole population and among young adults aged 20–39 years by sex, age, and socio-demographic index (SDI). Mortality to incidence ratio (MIR), percentage change, estimated annual percentage change (EAPC) and the correlation between ASR and SDI were evaluated. Future forecasts of KC burden until 2050 were performed.ResultsASIR among young adults aged 20–39 years increased (EAPC = 1.13%, P < 0.001), ranking second only to those over 80 years old. ASMR declined, with the slowest decrease observed among those aged 20–39 years (EAPC = -0.12%; P = 0.003), mainly due to the upward trend observed among males (EAPC = 0.11%, P = 0.022). No change of ASDR was detected among young adults despite of decreasing trend in the whole population. ASDR attributable to high BMI exhibited an overall increasing trend, particularly among young adults (EAPC = 1.28%, P < 0.001), whereas that attributable to smoking decreased most significantly (EAPC = -1.96%, P < 0.001). High SDI countries contributed the most to this declined trend, however they possess the highest baseline ASRs. ASMR and ASDR continued to rise in middle and low-middle SDI countries. By 2050 young male adults show a significant upward trajectory in KC burden, while the declining trends were observed in the whole population.ConclusionsThis study highlights the urgent need to address the increasing KC burden among young adults, particularly males, as well as in high, middle, and low-middle SDI countries.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-15160-2.

Introduction

Kidney cancer (KC) is a common tumor of the urinary system, accounting for approximately 2.2% of adult malignant tumors [1]. More than 50% of KC cases are accidentally discovered during imaging examinations. Approximately 20–30% of patients have metastases at early diagnosis, 30–50% progress to metastasis after local disease, and nearly 40% with localized renal cell carcinoma (RCC) have distant metastases even after surgery [2]. Metastatic KC has a high fatality rate, with a 5-year survival rate of only 12% [3]. According to the latest GLOBOCAN 2022 estimates from the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC), there were 435,000 new cases of KC globally and 156,000 deaths in 2022 [4]. The incidence and mortality rates are highest in transformed and developed countries, such as North America and Europe, and lowest in Asian and African countries [5].

To date, research on KC has been primarily focused on children under the age of 5 [6] and the elderly (the median age at diagnosis of KC is 75 years worldwide [7] and 64 years in the USA [8]). This traditional focus has resulted in relatively few studies on KC trends among young adults aged 20–39 years [9, 10].

Considering the substantial contributions of young adults to the economy and their families, premature morbidity and mortality in this population have a significant impact on the global disease burden [11]. Over the past three decades, economic development and industrialization have attracted a substantial influx of young people. This has led to improved health outcomes while also introducing a range of KC-related health issues in young adults, such as changes in lifestyle and diet and exposure to carcinogenic risk factors. Given that temporal and spatial variations in the global burden of KC among young adults over the past 30 years remain unclear, it is essential to conduct an epidemiological study to develop cost-effective interventions [12].

In contrast to GLOBOCAN, the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 provides longitudinal data on global cancer trends from 1990 to 2021.While previous analyses from GBD and GLOBOCAN have provided high-level overviews of the global KC burden, these reports generally concentrate on the entire population or employ broad age categorizations. This study specifically focuses on young adults, quantifying the standardized KC burden using GBD data with four age categories for each country and year, adjusted according to the GBD world standard population. While GLOBOCAN utilizes the Human Development Index (HDI) [13] as a composite measure of national development, one of its components—life expectancy at birth—is health-related. This introduces a potential source of bias. Comparison of health outcomes of countries with different HDI levels may overestimate associations due to the intrinsic health component. To address this limitation, the GBD developed the Socio-Demographic Index (SDI) [14] (Table S1), consisting of average national education level of individuals aged 15 years and older, total fertility rate among those under 25 years, and per capita lag-distributed income, none of which is directly health-related. Accordingly, this study employed SDI to investigate global epidemiological trends in KC. To our knowledge, this is the first comprehensive report on the global epidemiology of spatiotemporal patterns of KC and its modifiable risk factors among young individuals from 1990–2021 using most recent data.

Methods

Study design and data resources

This study is a longitudinal epidemiological study based on public data from the GBD 2021. Annual KC data by sex, age, and SDI were extracted from the GBD 2021 database (http://ghdx.healthdata.org) spanning 1990 to 2021 [15]. Our analysis included all 204 countries with detailed national-level data available in the GBD 2021 database, categorized by SDI quintiles. The present study complied with the Guidelines for Accurate and Transparent Health Estimates Reporting recommendations [16].

Definitions of disease categories

The International Classification of Diseases version 10 (ICD-10) codes (C64–C64.2, C64.9–C65.9, Z80.51, and Z85.52–Z85.54) and ICD-9 codes (189–189.1,189.5–189.6, and 209.24) were used to identify KC cases.

Age-, sex- and year-specific population data estimation

The population was grouped by age in increments of 5 years, resulting in 20 non-overlapping age groups: < 5 years, 5–9 years, 10–14 years, …, 85–89 years, 90–94 years, and ≥ 95 years. The standardized rates for individuals aged under 20 years and over 80 years were directly extracted from the GBD 2021 database. For the population aged 20–79 years, we consolidated age groups exhibiting similar disease burdens [17] and calculated the standardized rates of KC burden for four age categories (under 20 years, 20–39 years, 40–79 years, and 80 years and older) according to the GBD world standard population (Table S2).

The SDI was applied to evaluate the level of socioeconomic development of a country and has been proven to have a good correlation with KC outcomes [18]. SDI values range from 0 to 1, indicating the lowest to highest level of socioeconomic development. All countries were ranked as high SDI, high-middle SDI, middle SDI, low-middle SDI, and low SDI, according to SDI quintiles (Table S3).

Outcomes and risk factors

The primary outcomes were age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR), mortality rate (ASMR), and disability-adjusted life-years (DALYs) rate (ASDR) for KC. Secondary measures included mortality to incidence ratio (MIR), percentage change, estimated annual percentage change (EAPC), risk factor attributable to ASDR and future forecast until 2050.

Previous GBD rounds used systematic reviews to identify risk factors for KC, applying the World Cancer Research Fund's evidence standards ("probable" or "convincing"). These assessments identified smoking, obesity, and occupational trichloroethylene (TCE) exposure as the risk factors [19]. So smoking, obesity, and TCE exposure were involved in our study. The GBD estimates the attributable burden of risk factors using a comparative risk assessment framework [19]. Generally, the Population Attributable Fraction (PAF) is calculated for each risk factor. The PAF represents the proportion of KC cases that would be averted if exposure to a given risk factor were reduced to a theoretical minimum level. This calculation is based on the prevalence of exposure in the population and the relative risk (RR) of developing KC. The final attributable burden (e.g. attributable DALYs) is then determined by multiplying the total KC burden by the PAF.

The data on the prevalence of hypertension among males and females aged 20 years and older from 1995 to 2015 was extracted and analyzed from the 2016 Global Trends in Hypertension report published by the NCD Risk Factor Collaboration (NCD-RisC) [20].

Forecasts of global burden of disease of KC

Bayesian Quadratic Regression model was applied to project the global KC burden including ASIR, ASMR and ASDR to 2050 based on the GBD data from 1990–2021. The model operates within a Bayesian framework, building upon the standard linear model by incorporating squared terms of the predictors (X2), thereby enabling the capture of U-shaped or inverted U-shaped curvilinear trends.

Statistical analysis

All estimates are expressed as age-standardized rate (ASR) per 100,000 person, along with a 95% uncertainty interval (UI) according to the GBD algorithm. Age standardization is performed when comparing disease distribution among several populations with different age structures or within the same population over a period during which age structure changed. The formula for calculating ASR was shown in the Appendix.

Percentage change was used to represent the magnitude of variation in ASIR, ASMR, ASDR, and ASDR attributable risk factors for certain age groups between 1990 and 2021 [21]. The formula was shown in the Appendix.

EAPC was used to quantify temporal trends in ASRs for KC from 1990 to 2021.EAPC particularly served as a summary measure of long-term average change and does not assume strict linearity in year-to-year fluctuations [22]. The regression model was used to calculate EAPC and shown in the Appendix. An increasing trend was indicated when both the EAPC and lower limit of 95% CI were greater than 0, whereas a decreasing trend was indicated when both the EAPC and upper limit of 95% CI were less than 0. Otherwise, ASR was considered stable over time. MIR is obtained by dividing mortality rate by incidence rate.

For data-sparse regions, the GBD study employs a sophisticated modeling approach based on the principle of borrowing strength from existing data. This method integrates trends from adjacent years, neighboring countries and regions with similar socio-demographic characteristics to generate robust estimates. Where age-disaggregated data are unavailable, age-distribution patterns derived from high-quality sources are applied to allocate aggregated totals. All estimates are presented with 95% UI, which transparently reflect the degree of modelling uncertainty [15], thereby ensuring appropriate interpretation of the reliability of age-specific findings.

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 26, SPSS Inc.) and R programming language (version 4.1.0, https://www.R-project.org). Specifically, SPSS was used for initial data cleaning, management, and calculation of descriptive statistics, while R was used for all advanced modeling and visualization. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

ASMR of KC

The ASMR of KC worldwide increased from 1.99 in 1990, peaked in 1994 and 2005, then slowly declined to 1.91 in 2021 (EAPC = −0.14%; P < 0.001) (Fig. 1B, Table 1). When comparing mortality rates in 1990 and 2021, percentage change showed a significant increase only in those aged > 80 years, whereas the smallest decrease observed in those aged 20–39 years (Table 3). ASMR showed a unique increasing trend among those aged > 80 years (EAPC = 0.83%). The slowest decrease observed in those aged 20–39 years with EAPC of −0.12% (Table 3, Fig. 3), mainly due to the rising ASMR for males in this age group (EAPC = 0.11%). Among males, trends in ASMR changes varied greatly across age groups. The 20–39 years age group showed an increasing trend, trailed only by the > 80 years group, whereas declines were observed in the 40–79 and < 20 years groups (Table 3). ASDR of KC

The trends in ASDR were similar to those in ASMR. Global ASDR for KC increased from 53.0 in 1990, peaked in 1994 and 2005, and gradually declined to 47.3 in 2021 (EAPC = −0.37%; P < 0.001) (Fig. 1C, Table 1). ASDR showed a unique increase among the elderly aged > 80 years (EAPC = 0.70%), whereas it decreased in those aged < 20 yeas (EAPC = −1.04%) and 40–79 years (EAPC = −0.47%), with no change in those aged 20–39 years (EAPC = −0.06%) (Table 4, Fig. 3). MIR of KC

Global MIR for KC decreased continuously from 0.51 in 1990 to 0.42 in 2021 (EAPC = −0.69%; P < 0.001) (Fig. S3, Table 1). MIR was significantly negatively correlated with SDI (r = −0.89, 95% CI −0.90 to −0.87) (Fig. S4A), with a downward trend observed in all age groups, and was particularly significant in the 20–39 years age group (EAPC = −1.23%) (Table S7). The decline in MIR was observed mainly in countries with high, high-middle, and middle SDI (Table S8, Fig. S1D, Fig. S2D). The trend in MIR changes for both males and females was similar to that for the overall population (Fig. S3), with a declining trend across all age groups, most notably in the 20–39 years age group, with EAPCs of −1.16% and −1.42%, respectively.

ASDR attributable to the KC-related risk factors

The established risk factors of KC were shown in Table S9. Only certain risk factors were involved in the association study with ASDR including BMI, smoking, and occupational exposure to TCE, because those were the ones modelled in GBD for KC. Among these, high BMI and smoking were the main risk factors, with attributable percentages of 19.0% and 9.2%, respectively, in 2021. Exposure to TCE had a relatively weak contribution, with an attributable percentage of 0.06% in 2021 (Fig. S5A).

Globally, ASDR attributable to high BMI increased from 7.80 in 1990, peaked at 9.19 in 2010, and then continued to decline to 8.99 in 2021, showing an overall increasing trend (EAPC = 0.41%, P < 0.001) (Table 1, Fig. S6A). The growth rate of ASDR attributable to high BMI was the highest in the > 80 years age group (EAPC = 1.46%), followed by the 20–39 years (EAPC = 1.28%) age groups. A significant declining trend was detected among females aged 40–79 over the past three decades (EAPC = −0.28%), whereas the population of all other age groups of males and females showed growth trends (Table S10). ASDR attributable to high BMI was significantly positively correlated with SDI (r = 0.69, 95%CI 0.65 to 0.73) (Fig. S4B). ASDR attributable to smoking increased from 6.17 in 1990, peaked at 6.30 in 1994, and then continuously declined to 4.37 in 2021 (EAPC = −1.15%; P < 0.001) (Table 1, Fig. S6B). The most significant decrease in ASDR to smoking was observed in the 20–39 years age group (EAPC = −1.96%) (Table S12). ASDR attributable to smoking was significantly positively correlated with SDI (r = 0.73, 95%CI 0.71 to 0.76) (Fig. S4C). ASDR attributable to TCE exposure showed an upward trend over the past three decades (Fig. 5C), which should be further confirmed by longer observation periods and more data owing to the low baseline ASDR (Table 1). Although the correlation between TCE exposure-related ASDR and SDI was generally poor (r = 0.173, P < 0.001, Fig. S4D), increasing trend in ASDR attributable to TCE exposure was most evident in low-middle SDI countries (EAPC = 2.61%) (Fig. S6C). A similar trend was observed in the 20–39 years age group in these countries (EAPC = 1.61%).

In addition to the risk factors modeled in the GBD study, the established risk factor hypertension was also analyzed. The prevalence of hypertension has decreased to varying degrees across all age groups from 1995 to 2015. The smallest magnitude of decline was consistently observed in the 20–39 age group, with males exhibiting a notably more modest reduction than females (Fig. S5D).

Discussion

In this study we found that over the past 30 years, global ASIR of KC among young people aged 20–39 years has increased at a rate almost equivalent to that in the elderly aged > 80 years, and continue to keep a significant upward trend through 2050. Although global ASMR and ASDR of KC are both declining, the rate of decline is considerably slower among young people aged 20–39 than among their counterparts under 20 and aged 40–79. Moreover, young males in this age group exhibit a rising trend in both ASMR and ASDR. This evidence indicates that KC burden is quietly increasing among young people. General trend of KC burden of visualization graph is shown in Fig. S8.

Early life exposure and rising ASIR in young adults

Cancer trends in young adults, often under 50 years of age, reflect recent changes in carcinogenic exposure [23]. Approximately 40% of cancer cases and half of cancer deaths are attributed to modifiable risk factors, including unhealthy lifestyle habits such as smoking, among American adults aged 30 years and above [24]. Increased exposure to potentially modifiable behavioral risk factors, such as obesity [25], obesity-related hypertension [26], and early smoking [27], was detected in people born after the 1990s. This cohort effect partly explains the rapid increase in ASIR of KC and the slowest decline in ASDR among young people. A recent study indicated that, over the past 30 years, the prevalence of abdominal obesity among the global population aged 15–40 has doubled (from 16% to 34%), whereas among those over 40 years, it has increased from 44% to 58% [28]. Although hypertension has not yet been evaluated as a risk factor of KC in the GBD, it has been confirmed to be associated with increased risk of KC [5]. Regardless of sex, the smallest decline of prevalence of hypertension was observed among those aged 20–39 years from our analysis. Young people aged 20–39 years are the driving force behind sociocultural change, as they pursue new experiences and stimuli in various aspects of life, but this also signifies the age when many start smoking and risky behaviors peak. According to the GBD Tobacco Research Group report, between 1990 and 2015, global smoking prevalence typically peaked between ages 25and 35 for men, and around age 25 for women [29].

Metastatic KC and the slowed decline of ASMR and ASDR in young adults

Decline in ASMR for KC was slowest among young adults aged 20–39 years, which is somewhat related to the poor survival rates of metastatic KC in this younger population. Because young people have not yet reached an age of high tumor incidence, they rarely undergo regular physical examinations. By the time they seek medical attention for KC-related symptoms, the tumor may manifest as either an advanced-stage disease [30] or an earlier-stage tumor with more aggressive histological subtype. In a cohort study involving 445 patients with KC aged under 55 years, 16% of older patients (41–55 years) had metastasis at diagnosis, whereas the metastasis rate for younger patients aged under 40 years was 27%. The median overall survival time for young patients is 10.4 months, compared with 16.2 months for older patients [31]. Meanwhile, a study based on the surveillance, epidemiology, and end results (SEER)−18 database indicated that, apart from KC, young people (15–39 years) with metastatic cancer have a significantly higher survival rate than older people (40–79 years) [32].

SDI disparities in KC burden and the impact of KC prevention and control

ASIR, ASMR, and ASDR were positively correlated with SDI, whereas MIR was negatively correlated with SDI. Countries with higher SDI have higher socioeconomic levels, abundant medical resources, higher health literacy among residents, and stronger awareness of screening, leading to a higher detection rate of early-stage KC. Owing to the high life expectancy in high SDI countries, which largely covers the high-risk age group for KC, the probability of developing KC is increased. Additionally, the cancer registration systems in these countries are relatively well established. These factors are associated with higher ASIR in high SDI countries. Globally, ASMR and ASDR in the 20–39 years age group declined in high SDI countries, remained unchanged in high-middle SDI countries, and continued to rise in other countries. The declining trend in high SDI countries is mainly attributed to early diagnosis of KC, effective treatment, and primary prevention (smoking cessation and hypertension control). Early diagnosis in young adults substantially reduces Years of Potential Life Lost (YPLL) by preserving decades of economic productivity. Imaging is an important technology for early diagnosis of KC [33]. Abdominal ultrasonography has demonstrated excellent screening effectiveness over last century. A meta-analysis of 16 studies showed that 84.4% of KC detected by screening were at stage T1-T2N0, 13.7% at stage T3-T4N0, and only 2% were found to be lymph node-positive or metastatic at diagnosis [34], suggesting a potentially favorable stage shift in screening-detected disease, thereby improving the cure rate of RCC. In recent decades, treatment of KC has evolved from radical surgery combined with cytokine therapy, targeted therapies and immunotherapies. Although no cure currently exists for high-stage RCC, new therapies have undoubtedly extended the survival period of patients to varying degrees [35, 36]. However, these new treatments currently remain expensive and inaccessible for widespread use. Therefore, high SDI countries should optimize the treatment with cost-effective therapies and implement planned health screenings for young populations; whereas lower SDI countries, constrained by resources, should combine primary prevention (e.g. tobacco control) with targeted screening (e.g. abdominal ultrasound-based screening) for high-risk groups.

MIR is a significant public health indicator that primarily reflects the overall effectiveness of cancer prevention and treatment. All factors that influence cancer incidence or mortality rates, such as population aging, screening practices, diagnostic accuracy, and therapeutic effectiveness, could affect this index. In the present study, the most pronounced decline in MIR was observed in the 20–39 age group, driven primarily by substantial improvements in high, high-middle, and middle SDI countries. This trend can be attributed in part to reduced mortality resulting from earlier detection and treatment of KC, alongside the adoption of targeted therapies in recent decades. At the same time, the high baseline incidence of KC in these regions is also partially explained by evolving diagnostic practices—particularly increased incidental detection due to the growing use of medical imaging. Thus the increase is likely due to both a real rise in the disease occurrence (from risk factors like smoking and obesity) and better detection.

Sex-specific variations in KC burden and associated risk factors

Consistent with the overall population trend, the ASIR, ASMR, and ASDR of KC in young men aged 20–39 years were higher than those in women. A sex-specific genome-wide association study conducted among over 8,000 European patients with KC confirmed that 14q24.2 (DPF3) and 2p21 (EPAS1) are male-related KC risk genes [37]. The androgen receptor is expressed in renal tubular cells and kidney tumor. Lysine-specific histone demethylase 1 can regulate KC cell growth via epigenetic control of AR transcription factors [38, 39]. In addition, the difference in KC burden between men and women is related to accumulation of male-specific risk factors. According to the WHO Global Tobacco Use Trends Report 2000–2025 statistics, in 2020, there were 1.067 billion male tobacco users aged 15 years and older globally, nearly five times the number of female users [40]. A global meta-analysis involving 45.9 million individuals revealed that approximately one-fifth of children and adolescents had overweight or obese, affecting boys more significantly. This population is also more likely to develop hypertension [41]. In 2019, the hypertension control rate among adults aged 30–79 worldwide was lower in men than in women (18% vs. 23%). In our analysis, smoking, high BMI, and TCE exposure contributed more to ASDR in young men than in women, indicating that sex-specific exposure plays a significant role in the early KC burden.

Significant progress has been made in reducing the global prevalence of tobacco use; however, a large implementation gap remains for tobacco control [42]. Over the past 30 years, smoking-attributable ASDR has shown divergent trends: rapidly declining in high SDI countries, beginning to decline in high-middle SDI countries, but continuing to rise in middle and low-middle SDI countries. This indicates that tobacco control measures should be strengthened in middle and low-middle SDI countries.

Beyond the well-established risks factors, young adults now face increased exposure to westernized diets rich in processed foods and red meat, sedentary behaviors driven by office-based work and urban living, and greater occupational exposure to potential nephron-carcinogens like certain heavy metals in industrial settings [43]. These risk factors potentially contribute to the projected increase in KC burden and its sex-related disparities.

Strength and limitations

First, the comprehensive scope and systematic methodology of using the GBD dataset, provided a robust global, regional, and national perspective. Second, the novel, in-depth focus on the long-overlooked 20–39 age group addresses a significant gap in previous large-scale epidemiological studies. Third, the long-term trend analysis (since 1990) offered a unique longitudinal view that is often missing in cross-sectional studies.

Our study has several limitations. First, our conclusions rely on GBD 2021 data and inherit its methodological constraints [15, 19]. The accuracy of GBD estimates depends on primary data quality, which varies geographically. Second, many low and middle-low SDI countries lack comprehensive cancer registries, which likely leads to underreporting of KC burden. Anyway, the GBD study employed a core modelling strategy that borrows strength from existing data to generate robust estimates for regions with sparse data. Third, GBD aggregates KC histological subtypes with distinct risk factors and outcomes, limiting subtype-specific analysis. Finally, as an ecological study, it reveals population-level associations rather than establishing causality or explaining underlying mechanisms.

Conclusion

The global burden of KC has risen rapidly among young people aged 20–39 years since 1990 and is projected to continue increasing significantly through 2050.This highlights the need to prioritize this overlooked population. Although ASDR and ASMR have begun to decline in high-income countries, their high baseline KC burden means prevention and control in young populations will remain a key focus over the next decade. Greater attention should be given to young men in middle and low-middle SDI countries, where the disease burden is increasing. Implementing primary prevention strategies, including tobacco and hypertension control, occupational protections, and scheduled screening in high-risk population, will be highly cost-effective in these regions.
