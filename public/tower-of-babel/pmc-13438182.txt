Non‐Contiguous Double Thyroglossal Duct Cysts: A Case Report
Authors: Baogang Wang, Bo Sun, Shujun Li, Lurong Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThyroglossal duct cysts (TDCs) are conventionally considered solitary lesions connected to a persistent embryological tract. While rare double TDCs have been reported, they are almost invariably anatomically continuous. We report a paradigm‐shifting case of non‐contiguous double TDCs in a 50‐year‐old male, featuring two anatomically discrete cysts at the left vallecula and infrahyoid region with no interconnecting tract, to our knowledge, a finding not previously documented in this distinct anatomical configuration. Both lesions were completely excised and pathologically confirmed. This case challenges the conventional understanding of thyroglossal duct embryology and underscores the need for surgeons to anticipate and manage multiple independent remnants, which may require modifications to the standard Sistrunk procedure to ensure complete resection.Keywords: double cyst, embryological variation, non‐contiguous thyroglossal duct cyst, Sistrunk procedure

Introduction

Thyroglossal duct cysts (TDCs) represent the most prevalent congenital neck anomaly, arising from the failed involution of the thyroglossal duct following thyroid gland descent [1, 2]. The classic surgical teaching, enshrined in the Sistrunk procedure, is predicated on the existence of a single, continuous embryological remnant extending from the foramen cecum to the hyoid bone [3].

Although the occurrence of multiple TDCs is recognized as rare, a critical review of the literature reveals that these are predominantly “dumbbell‐shaped” cysts connected at the hyoid bone or linked by a thin, continuous tract [1, 2, 3]. These configurations still align with the model of a single, albeit bifid, persistent duct. The existence of truly discontinuous, non‐contiguous TDCs—suggesting multiple, independent foci of ductal persistence—remains a theoretical possibility with scant empirical evidence. To our knowledge, no prior case has definitively demonstrated two separate TDCs without any anatomical connection in this specific configuration.

Here, we present a case of non‐contiguous double TDCs. We believe this finding prompts a reconsideration of the embryogenesis of thyroglossal duct remnants and carries important implications for surgical management aimed at preventing recurrence.

Case History and Examination

A 50‐year‐old man presented March 5, 2024, with a painless, raised, oval neck mass (about 2 × 1.5 cm) in the anterior neck, noted for over 2 weeks. The mass was painless and mobile with swallowing. The patient denied redness, swelling, tenderness, foreign body sensation, dyspnea, or dysphagia. Medical history was unremarkable.

Exam revealed a bulge left of midline near the hyoid bone, without skin changes. Palpation showed a smooth, oval, non‐tender, mobile mass. Preoperative cervical CT examination demonstrated the thyroid gland situated in its normal pretracheal location, with no evidence of ectopic thyroid tissue. Differential Diagnosis, Investigations and Treatment

Flexible laryngoscopy showed a smooth, well‐defined, grayish‐white cystic lesion (1.3 × 1.5 cm) in the left vallecula (Figure 1), suggesting a tongue base origin and non‐contiguity. CT revealed an oval, slightly hypodense lesion anterior and below the hyoid (0.9 × 1.6 cm, 25 HU) with clear margins, supporting its separation from other cysts (Figure 2). The left epiglottic wall appeared thickened with mixed density. MRI showed a slightly long T1 and long T2 fat‐suppressed hyperintense lesion (1.4 × 1.2 cm) near the left thyroid cartilage with sharp borders. Another hyperintense lesion (1.2 × 1.6 cm) was in the mouth floor and left preepiglottic space (Figures 3 and 4), confirming distinct cysts. Preoperative diagnoses: thyroglossal duct cyst, epiglottic cyst. On March 7, 2024, the patient had plasma‐assisted endoscopic epiglottic cyst excision and a Sistrunk procedure for TDC removal. The Sistrunk procedure revealed a cyst beneath infrahyoid muscles, by the lateral thyroid cartilage, adherent to the hyoid's lower edge. The hyoid body was cut, dissection went to the foramen cecum; there was no connection to the epiglottic cyst. Next, laryngoscopy allowed aspiration of cyst fluid, with the cyst base near the tongue's foramen cecum. The cyst was fully removed with plasma dissection. Intraoperatively, the diagnosis was revised to lingual root cyst and thyroglossal duct cyst. Histopathology found cholesterol crystals, thyroid follicles, and inflammatory cells in both (Figure 5). Final diagnosis: double thyroglossal duct cysts.

Outcome and Follow‐Up

The patient recovered well with no postoperative complications. At one‐year follow‐up, there was no recurrence.

Discussion

This case report transcends the mere documentation of a rare entity; we believe it provides compelling evidence for a distinct non‐contiguous phenotype of thyroglossal duct anomaly. Our patient's presentation with two anatomically and surgically distinct cysts—one at the tongue base and another in the infrahyoid region, with no interposing tract—challenges the conventional understanding that TDCs originate from a single persistent duct.

Embryological Implications: Beyond a Single Persistent Duct

The prevailing embryological model, which posits the existence of a single, continuous persistent tract, provides a satisfactory explanation for the classic, solitary thyroglossal duct cyst [4]. This model is further supported by reported cases of double or multiple thyroglossal duct cysts, wherein the lesions are interconnected by a duct or linked via the hyoid bone [5, 6]. However, its explanatory power reaches its limit when confronted with our finding of anatomically non‐contiguous double cysts at separate sites. This discovery is fundamentally incompatible with the existence of a single, connecting conduit.

Conversely, the presence of non‐contiguous cysts suggests the possibility of multifocal, segmental failures of ductal obliteration during the embryonic involution of the thyroglossal duct. This would lead to the formation of multiple, independent epithelial rests along the original descent path, devoid of anatomical connection. This concept aligns with a recognized pathological process: when residual epithelial cell clusters are distributed across multiple regions along the migratory path due to incomplete ductal involution, they can manifest as multiple foci of heterotopic thyroid tissue [7]. This indirectly confirms that embryological epithelium with differentiation potential can indeed persist as discrete, independent “foci.” This provides a theoretical foundation for our hypothesis.

We propose that the pathogenesis of thyroglossal duct cysts may not be a simple binary phenomenon (complete involution versus a persistent duct), but rather may exist as a spectrum of focal ductal survival. At one end of this spectrum lies the classic presentation: a solitary cyst arising from a single, continuous epithelial remnant. At the opposite, more complex end lies the entity we describe: non‐contiguous, multifocal thyroglossal duct cysts, wherein discontinuous epithelial “islands” undergo independent cystic transformation. This framework reconciles established histological evidence of scattered remnants with this novel clinical phenotype, thereby prompting a shift in the diagnostic and surgical approach from pursuing a single “failed duct” to recognizing the potential for multiple independent points of embryological arrest.

This reconceptualization from a linear to a multifocal model carries important implications for surgical strategy. The classical Sistrunk procedure is, in essence, a targeted intervention designed for a single, continuous anatomical substrate. Its historical success is predicated on the en bloc removal of that central conduit [8]. Our case, however, demonstrates that the underlying pathology can be fundamentally different: discrete disease islands without a connecting bridge.

Diagnostic Re‐Evaluation: The Illusion of Continuity

Preoperative imaging (CT/MRI) and intraoperative dissection were pivotal in establishing the non‐contiguous nature of the cysts. This distinction is crucial [9]. Previous reports of anatomically continuous “double” thyroglossal duct cysts primarily involve two types: one presenting as dumbbell‐shaped cysts connected at the hyoid bone [10], and the other linked by a slender, fibrous tract [11]. In stark contrast, our case presents a truly anatomically discontinuous entity, with no anatomical connection between the two cysts, which are located at two distinct anatomical sites. This finding confirms that, without meticulous intraoperative dissection to definitively rule out an interconnecting tract, non‐contiguous cysts risk being misclassified as a continuous structure. Such misclassification leads to an incomplete understanding of their embryological origin and may consequently compromise the completeness of surgical resection.

Surgical Considerations: From Tract Chasing to Comprehensive Clearance

An important lesson from this case is that the operative strategy may need to be adapted. The Sistrunk procedure is predicated on the fundamental assumption of a central, continuous tract [12]. Our case suggests that this assumption may not hold for non‐contiguous variants. Therefore, the strategy should consider moving from excising a presumed conduit to ensuring the comprehensive clearance of all architecturally independent cystic structures.

In our patient, the surgical approach was tailored to the distinct anatomical locations of the two independent lesions. The infrahyoid cyst, which involved the hyoid bone and followed the typical thyroglossal tract pathway, was managed with the standard Sistrunk procedure. In contrast, the vallecular (lingual root) cyst was approached transorally via endoscopic plasma‐assisted excision, as this location is not accessible via the cervical Sistrunk approach without significant morbidity. This combined approach was selected to achieve complete excision of both lesions while minimizing surgical trauma and preserving normal anatomical structures. The absence of an interconnecting tract between the two cysts was confirmed intraoperatively, further justifying the use of two distinct surgical corridors.

Summary

We describe the first confirmed case of non‐contiguous double thyroglossal duct cysts. We believe this finding contributes a new conceptual category within thyroglossal duct pathology. It provides clinical validation for an embryological model of multifocal ductal persistence and suggests a refined surgical approach. For any suspected complex TDC, surgeons must actively seek to confirm or rule out non‐contiguity through advanced imaging and meticulous dissection, preparing for the possibility of managing multiple independent remnants to achieve a definitive cure.
