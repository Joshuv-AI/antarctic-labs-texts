Personalizing the Quadriceps Tendon Graft in Anterior Cruciate Ligament Reconstruction: A Novel, Continuous Method to Predict Graft Size Using Preoperative MRI
Authors: Zi Qiang Glen Liau, Bryan Khoo, Hubert Tuyishime, Matthew Song Peng Ng, Alexander Shao-Rong Pang, James Hoi Po Hui, Kevin Shea
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:The quadriceps tendon (QT) graft is increasingly popular in primary anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction (ACLR). However, postoperative quadriceps strength can be significantly weakened after use of a QT graft, compared to using hamstring tendon or bone–patellar tendon–bone grafts. There currently exists no continuous method to predict QT graft size based on preoperative magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) measurements; only dichotomous methods exist at limited cutoff values.Purpose:To (1) assess the accuracy of preoperative MRI QT thickness in predicting graft size dichotomously (<10 mm vs ≥10 mm), (2) assess the accuracy of the formula 2 ×√(WT/π) in predicting graft size continuously (within ±1.0 mm of the actual graft diameter), and (3) determine the interrater reliability of both methods; a minimum threshold of 10-mm QT graft diameter was selected given the young, high-demand athletic population in this cohort.Study Design:Cross-sectional study; Level of evidence, 3.Methods:This is a retrospective review of 130 patients who underwent primary ACLR with full-thickness QT autografts at a single tertiary institution from January 1, 2022, to July 17, 2025. A novel, reproducible method to measure QT thickness on the MRI sagittal view at the most proximal patellar attachment site was developed. Two blinded reviewers independently measured QT thickness. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed to determine the optimal threshold for predicting a final graft diameter <10 mm, with area under the curve (AUC) as the primary measure of discriminative ability. Sensitivity and specificity at the optimal threshold were additionally reported given their direct clinical relevance to intraoperative decision-making at a fixed cutoff.Results:The mean age was 16.4 years (95% CI, 15.9-16.9 years), with 59 females and 71 males. ROC analysis identified a preoperative QT thickness <8.3 mm as the optimal threshold for predicting a final graft diameter <10 mm, with an AUC of 0.818, a sensitivity of 98.2%, and a specificity of 39.1%. The authors applied the formula 2 ×√(WT/π), demonstrating how graft diameter can be predicted from preoperative MRI, where T is the measured preoperative MRI graft thickness and W is the harvested graft width. Using this formula, the authors found that 90.0% of grafts were within ±1.0 mm of the actual graft diameter.Conclusion:The authors present a novel, numerically continuous way of predicting harvested quadriceps graft size. A preoperative QT thickness <8.3 mm is 98.2% sensitive and 39.1% specific in predicting a final graft diameter <10 mm. Personalizing the width of the harvested QT according to patient's tendon thickness and surgeon's desired graft size may be considered to minimize unnecessary over- or underharvesting of QT in the future.Keywords: knee, anterior cruciate ligament, quadriceps tendon graft, MRI, graft personalization, prediction

Anterior cruciate ligament reconstructions (ACLRs) rank among the most frequently performed orthopaedic procedures worldwide. In the United States alone, an estimated 400,000 ACLRs were performed in 2017, representing a remarkable 461% increase from 1995 to 2017.2,3 In recent years, use of the quadriceps tendon (QT) graft has increased substantially, with excellent patient outcomes and low postoperative failure rates.15,21 The Swedish Knee Ligament Registry demonstrated that from 2005 to 2020, QT graft use in ACLR increased by 450%, from approximately 3% to 12%-13%, while hamstring tendon (HT) graft use declined from 90% to 81%. 25 This trend is similarly reflected in the United States, where QT autograft utilization increased steadily after 2020 with a concurrent decline in HT autograft use from 40.3% to 17.2% of all autograft ACLRs. 3

However, the use of the QT graft may be associated with significant postoperative quadriceps weakness. One study showed lower isokinetic quadriceps strength at 7 months postoperatively. 7 Other studies have demonstrated that quadriceps deficits can persist as far as 1 or 2 years postoperatively, although functional outcomes remain the same.5,9 While to our understanding there are no studies investigating if harvesting a dimensionally wider QT autograft can directly increase the risk of postoperative quadriceps weakness, it would be prudent to avoid harvesting an excessive amount of QT to reduce this complication. On the other hand, while there are also no studies informing us about the minimum QT graft size that should be harvested, graft diameter in general is a well-established predictor for graft failure requiring revision ACLR.6,8,22 Overharvesting too much QT might lead to quadriceps weakness, while underharvesting too little QT may result in early rerupture and graft failure, demonstrating there is a need to personalize the exact amount of QT graft harvested in each patient.

There already exists numerous practical methods to predict the size of the HT graft from straightforward preoperative magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) measurements, not only for quadrupled HT grafts with 92.6% sensitivity in identifying inadequately sized grafts, 12 but also, more recently, for HT grafts consisting of any number of folds with 95.8% sensitivity in identifying inadequately sized grafts. 13 However, based on our understanding, only 1 study in literature has thus far detailed the use of predicting QT graft size using preoperative MRI. 1 This 2021 study by Baghdadi et al 1 offers a limited dichotomous method to predict the adequacy of the eventual QT graft, defined as 8 mm. In other words, when given the preoperative QT graft dimensions, this study only provides the surgeon with a binary outcome of whether the QT graft is adequately sized or inadequately sized (hence the term “dichotomous method” is used to describe it). A thorough search of the literature revealed that there currently exists no continuous method to calculate the exact numeric dimensions of the QT graft based on preoperative MRI measurements, and there is no consistent, mathematically proven option in the literature to personalize the amount of QT graft harvested from each patient.

As such, the aims of this study were 3-fold: (1) Assess the accuracy of using QT thickness measured on preoperative MRI in predicting QT graft size in a dichotomous manner: <10 mm or ≥10 mm. (2) Assess the accuracy of a mathematical proof in predicting QT graft size in a continuous manner, within ±1.0 mm of the actual intraoperative QT graft size. (3) Determine the interrater reliability of the aforementioned methods.

Methods

This is a retrospective review of 153 patients who underwent primary ACLRs with full-thickness QT autografts at a single tertiary center by 1 board-certified orthopaedic surgeon between January 1, 2022, and July 17, 2025. Figure 1 shows the study flowchart with inclusion and exclusion criteria. A total of 130 patients were included in the final study.

Study flowchart with inclusion and exclusion criteria. MRI, magnetic resonance imaging; QT, quadriceps tendon.

Protocol for Measuring QT Thickness on MRI

Preoperative MRI fine-cut sequences performed on 1.5-T systems with a slice thickness of 0.6 mm were obtained for each patient. Two independent reviewers (B.K. and H.T.) underwent QT measurement training by the primary author (Z.Q.G.L.), an experienced orthopaedic sports surgeon. The reviewers measured a pilot set of 17 patient MRI scans and met with the primary author to review the measurements and ensure accuracy and precision.

The QT thickness was measured on MRI by first identifying precisely on the sagittal view the most proximal point of the QT that attaches onto the patella, referred to as the Liau attachment point. Importantly, this attachment is not at the proximal-most pole of the patella, which may frequently be mistaken for it, but instead lies some distance distal to the proximal-most pole, attaching over a proximal-distal distance of nearly 1 cm along the anterior aspect of the proximal patella (Figure 2, A and C). The QT thickness was then measured at this point, where the obliquity of its attachment allows the full thickness of the distal-most QT to be measured by drawing a line perpendicular to the QT axis. The MRI axial view was concurrently used to confirm that the selected sagittal cut represented the thickest portion of the QT (Figure 2B).

T2-weighted magnetic resonance imaging scans. (A) Sagittal view of the knee to locate the quadriceps tendon (QT) attachment at the superior-most portion of the superior pole of the patella. (B) Axial view of the knee to confirm the thickest portion of the QT. (C) Sagittal view of the knee showing the QT thickness at the attachment point and perpendicular to the tendon axis (in this case, 7.3 mm).

Mathematical Proof of Equation Used to Predict the QT Graft Diameter

The predicted QT graft diameter can be calculated in a continuous manner from the preoperatively measured QT thickness via the following mathematical equation: diameter of QT graft = 2 ×√(WT/π), where W is the harvested graft width and T is the preoperatively measured graft thickness (Figure 3). This applies a principle we previously established for hamstring grafts,12,13 which we term the “law of conservation of cross-sectional area of graft tissue”: the cross-sectional areas of the oval harvested and folded tendons are conserved and equal to the circular cross-sectional area of the final cylindrical graft. In the QT, the cross-sectional area of the rectangular tendon that is not folded is harvested, so the conserved area reduces to the rectangular product of width × thickness, giving the QT-specific form 2 ×√(WT/π). The present study derives this QT-specific form and validates the principle in the setting of the QT. While the geometric relationship between a rectangle and a circle of equal area is well established, the nontrivial contribution here is the underlying biomechanical assertion that graft tissue does not meaningfully compress, expand, or redistribute during the transition from a flat rectangular harvest to a cylindrical graft preparation. It is this physical conservation principle, applied to biological tissue, that we propose as a named law and that forms the foundational rationale for the predictive formula.

Data Collection Process

As this was a retrospective study, all MRI thickness measurements were performed after the index surgeries had been completed. First, 2 authors (B.K. and H.T.) performed measurements of QT thickness with strict adherence to the protocol described earlier. They made the measurements independently while blinded to each other's results and the intraoperative graft measurements. The mean of the values was used for analysis.

Intraoperatively, for each patient, full-thickness QT grafts were harvested manually using a scalpel and a ruler. During this harvesting process, a graft width of 9.5 mm to 10 mm was found to be consistently obtained. This width was verified using a ruler. The intraoperative diameter of each graft was then measured by an independent surgical assistant by passing the FiberLoop-whipstitched graft (Arthrex) through progressively larger sizing tubes in 0.5-mm increments. This diameter, which is also taken as the final QT graft diameter, was then collected for statistical analysis.

Finally, the mathematical equation 2 ×√(WT/π) was utilized to calculate the predicted QT graft diameter. For instance, if patient A had a preoperatively measured QT thickness of 7.3 mm and the graft width was 10 mm, the predicted QT graft diameter would be 2 ×√(10 × 7.3/π) = 9.64 mm.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using R Version 4.5.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing) by the third, blinded author (M.S.P.N.). Patient data were computed using means and 95% confidence intervals. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed to determine the optimal discriminant threshold for predicting a final graft diameter <10 mm, with area under the curve (AUC) as the primary measure of discriminative ability; sensitivity and specificity at the optimal threshold were additionally reported given their direct clinical relevance to intraoperative decision-making at a fixed cutoff. In addition, a logistic regression model was built to predict the probability of the final graft being adequately sized given a preoperatively measured QT thickness. Interrater reliability was calculated using percentage agreement and Cohen kappa. Pearson correlation and percentage agreement (within ±1.0 mm of the actual intraoperative QT graft size) were used to characterize our continuous prediction method. Interrater reliability was calculated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). P values <.05 were treated as statistically significant. Power analysis, performed using the previously mentioned pilot set of 17 patients, indicated that to achieve at least 90% power with an alpha of .05, a minimum of 96 patient cases were required.

This retrospective study was approved by the institutional review board (IRB) of the institution where the ACLRs were performed (IRB eProtocol No. 65352). Given the retrospective nature of the study and use of de-identified measurements obtained from imaging data, the requirement for informed consent was waived.

Results

Patient Data

Among the 130 patients included in the final study, the mean age was 16.4 years (95% CI, 15.9-16.9 years), with 59 females and 71 males. The mean QT thickness measured on preoperative MRI was 7.7 mm (95% CI, 7.53-7.91 mm). The mean intraoperative QT graft diameter was 9.8 mm (95% CI, 9.65-9.88 mm).

Dichotomous Method of Predicting QT Graft Size

Table 1 shows that a preoperatively measured QT thickness <8.3 mm predicts a final QT graft diameter <10 mm in 98.2% of patients. This takes into account the harvested width of the QT, which varies from patient to patient, but ranged from 9.5 mm to 10 mm for the patients in this study. Logistic regression is significant (P < .001), with the odds of the actual QT graft diameter being adequate increasing by 5.22 (95% CI, 2.89-10.63) for each additional 1 mm of the measured QT thickness. The AUC (Figure 4) was 0.818, which corresponded to an excellent 14 discriminatory ability. The cutoff value <8.3 mm was then determined using the ROC curve by maximizing sensitivity first, followed by specificity. Percentage agreement between the 2 authors performing the measurements was 89.0% with a Cohen kappa of 0.70, indicating substantial 11 interrater reliability.

Continuous Method of Predicting QT Graft Size

Pearson correlation (Figure 5) shows a significant linear relationship between the measured QT thickness on the MRI sagittal view and the actual graft diameter (r = 0.562; P < .001). Using the mathematical equation as described earlier, where predicted QT graft diameter = 2 ×√(WT/π), we found that 90.0% of the predicted graft diameters were within ±1.0 mm of the actual intraoperatively documented graft diameter. The ICC between the 2 authors was 0.922 (95% CI, 0.891-0.944), indicating excellent 10 interrater reliability.

Discussion

Our dichotomous prediction method demonstrated a sensitivity of 98.2% for obtaining a final QT graft that is <10 mm in diameter. As far as we know, this sensitivity is the highest reported in literature to date for dichotomous prediction of QT graft size, surpassing that of contemporaneous studies,1,23 most notably the study published by Baghdadi et al, 1 which reported that a measured QT thickness <6.7 mm is 97.4% sensitive for predicting a final graft diameter <8 mm. Substantial interrater agreement was observed, with a percentage agreement of 89.0% and a Cohen kappa of 0.70. The specificity of the dichotomous method was 39.1%, indicating that it may not reliably identify all cases of potential overharvesting when used alone. However, by supplementing the dichotomous threshold with the continuous prediction formula, surgeons can fine-tune the harvested graft width beyond a simple binary cutoff, minimizing unnecessary overharvesting while still achieving the desired graft diameter.

Studies on HT and bone–patellar tendon–bone (BPTB) grafts have suggested gold-standard graft sizes of at least 8 mm16,24 and 10 mm,4,19 respectively, below which there would be a high risk of postoperative graft rerupture and failure due to insufficient graft thickness. On the other hand, while there is currently no consensus in terms of ideal QT graft size in the literature, biomechanical and morphometric studies on 10-mm QT grafts demonstrate superior ultimate load to failure, greater cross-sectional area, and higher collagen content compared to HT or BPTB grafts.20,26,27 Based on these studies demonstrating superior biomechanical properties specifically at a 10-mm QT diameter, we selected 10 mm as the minimum acceptable graft diameter for this cohort of young, high-demand athletes, rather than adopting the 8-mm threshold established for HT grafts.

According to our understanding, this study also introduces the first continuous prediction method capable of calculating the exact QT graft diameter preoperatively, supported by an original mathematical proof where QT graft diameter is equal to 2 ×√(WT/π). The method demonstrated high accuracy, with 90.0% of predictions falling within ±1.0 mm of the actual graft diameter, and excellent reliability, with an ICC of 0.922. Furthermore, it remains highly practical, relying solely on simple preoperative MRI measurements obtainable from any standard MRI picture archiving and communication system; however, readers should note that all MRI scans in this study were acquired using fine-cut sequences with a 0.6-mm slice thickness, which is thinner than the 3.0-mm slice thickness typical of routine clinical MRI protocols.

We will now demonstrate how an orthopaedic surgeon might utilize the mathematical proof intraoperatively. For instance, Smith & Nephew offers QUADTRAC QT graft-cutting guides in 8-mm, 10-mm, and 12-mm widths, 18 while Arthrex provides QT stripper blades in 9-mm, 10-mm, and 11-mm widths. 17 Assume a QT that is measured to be 7.3 mm thick on the sagittal view of the preoperative MRI knee (Figure 2C). A surgeon who chooses to use an Arthrex QT stripper blade of 10-mm width will obtain a QT graft diameter equating to 2 ×√(10 × 7.3/π) = 9.64 mm. This would be insufficient if the surgeon is aiming for a QT graft that is at least 10 mm in diameter. Knowing this preoperatively, the surgeon can choose to instead utilize a QT stripper blade with a larger cutting width of 11 mm. This will result in a QT graft diameter of 2 ×√(11 × 7.3/π) = 10.11 mm, which would now be sufficient. This example demonstrates how small adjustments in cutting width can meaningfully alter the eventual QT graft diameter and highlights the value of continuous prediction over categorical estimates. Predicted QT graft diameters across a range of preoperative MRI thickness values and vendor-specific cutting widths are shown in Table 2. By integrating vendor-specific blade options with patient-specific MRI measurements, we hope to empower surgeons to rationally select the cutting width most likely to achieve the desired axial QT graft diameter, rather than relying on intraoperative trial and error.

In summary, our approach provides surgical teams with a more comprehensive understanding of the discrepancy between the actual and target QT graft sizes, thereby facilitating more informed intraoperative decision-making when selecting the appropriate graft technique. Additionally, preoperative knowledge of the expected graft diameter allows the surgeon to plan the appropriately sized tibial tunnel from the outset, avoiding intraoperative tunnel revision or expansion. We have developed an Excel-based calculator (Supplementary Data) that incorporates a predictive equation to automatically estimate the expected graft size based on 2 input parameters: QT width and QT thickness.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the degree of knee flexion during MRI acquisition was not considered in the analysis. While a knee MRI study is typically done in the supine position, depending on the acuity of the injury, patients’ knees may still be swollen and/or painful, leading to them being slightly flexed. A flexed knee, as opposed to a fully extended knee, may result in a QT that is under further tension, potentially leading to a thinner measurement. However, this limitation has not been reported in the literature and was similarly not addressed in prior studies on the subject. Despite this, our prediction method was still able to achieve high predictive accuracy. Second, all MRI scans in the study were performed using 1.5-T systems, although some centers now utilize 3-T MRI in clinical practice. We hypothesize that the higher spatial resolution afforded by 3-T imaging may, in fact, likely further enhance the accuracy of the predictions. Additionally, all MRI scans in this study were acquired using fine-cut sequences with a 0.6-mm slice thickness, which is thinner than the approximately 3.0-mm slice thickness used in routine clinical practice. It remains to be determined whether the measurement precision required for this method is achievable on standard resolution sequences. Furthermore, the data set predominantly consists of older adolescents and young adults, with few grafts <8 mm in diameter. Very young patients, in whom inadequate graft size is a more clinically relevant concern, are underrepresented in this cohort. Fourth, graft diameter was measured intraoperatively with minimal tension applied longitudinally along the graft, apart from the slight amount of tension generated by pulling the graft through increasingly larger sizing tubes in 0.5-mm increments. However, this limitation is unlikely to result in an overestimation of the actual graft diameter as the preoperative QT thickness is also measured with the QT itself being under minimal tension, since the knee MRI is done with the patient's knee in a fully or close to fully extended position. Lastly, the relationship between harvested QT width and postoperative quadriceps strength was not directly investigated in this study.

Future Directions

Future studies should validate this prediction method in younger pediatric patients, in whom inadequate graft size is a more clinically relevant concern, and assess performance across a range of MRI acquisition protocols, including standard 3.0-mm slice thickness sequences and higher-resolution 3-T imaging. Prospective studies are also needed to determine whether minimizing harvest width translates to improved postoperative quadriceps recovery. Real-time prospective validation during surgical planning will further establish the clinical applicability and robustness of this method.

Conclusion

An MRI-measured QT thickness <8.3 mm was 98.1% sensitive and 39.1% specific for identifying patients who ultimately obtained a QT graft diameter <10 mm, representing the highest reported sensitivity in the literature for dichotomous prediction of QT graft size. The study also presents the first continuous prediction method capable of estimating the exact QT graft diameter with 90.0% accuracy, where predicted values were within ±1.0 mm of the actual graft size. The methods demonstrated excellent reproducibility, with a percentage agreement of 89%, Cohen kappa of 0.70, and ICC of 0.922. By incorporating both the patient's native tendon thickness and the surgeon's desired graft diameter, this approach enables personalization of QT graft width, representing a key step toward minimizing unnecessary overharvesting and reducing its associated complications. Clinically, these findings are an important advancement toward the personalized planning of all ACLRs, setting the foundation for more precise, patient-specific surgical outcomes.
