Underground abnormal sensor condition detection based on gas monitoring data and deep learning image feature engineering
Authors: Guoquan Chang, Haoqian Chang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Underground gas sensors as the most intuitive tool for monitoring gas concentrations in underground mining, yet they are subject to frequent anomalies due to ground pressure, constructions, even malicious masking by workers. Due to the depth of underground mining and the complexity of the environment, it is almost impossible to manually monitor the status of the all the sensors. Thus, the ability to accurately identify the working status of gas sensors at the working face are critical importance to mining safety. In this paper, we propose a deep learning feature engineering based approach to coupling the relationship between underground sensors. Experiment results show that the relationship between gas sensors can be expressed by position and time, so that when a sensor such as upper corner T0 malfunctions, it can be detected by other sensors such as T1 and T2. By converting the gas concentration into the form of recurrence plots (RPs), we are able to transform time-series gas concentration data into images with more dimensions in time lag, and enabling the application of more efficient and accurate machine vision methods. Based on the location of sensors at the working face, we found that the sensors at positions T0, T1 and T2 are correlated as the wind flows through the tunnel and have a higher correlation in the subsections of the time series. And those correlation can directly use to check the operating status of the sensors. We also discuss whether the relationships between the data itself can be preserved at the feature level during the mapping of gas concentrations to features, since deep learning (DL) looks like the next promise future after digitization in the mining industrialization with more and more data analysis and placing the results under a larger decision. This feature-based approach for gas concentration analysis can also be used for prediction and early warning.

Introduction

Gas concentration play a crucial role in mining engineering [1]. As one of the most visually observable phenomena of underground working status, a sudden and dramatic change usually means an accident such as coal and gas outburst or gas explosion [2,3]. However, anomalies in underground gas sensors due to ground pressure, mining activities or even human intervention are very common. Such anomalies are not visually obvious, but rather lasts for quite some time, even if the sensors are artificially masked, the gas values are still present, only a deviation from the normal state exists [4]. Gas exists in the coal body or surrounding rock in the free or adsorbed state, will gradually release into the coal seam during the mining excavation [5,6], which lead the rise of the sensors value, then it will slowly decrease after the underground operation stops, and eventually forms a dynamic equilibrium with the mine ventilation system [7]. Such potential pattern changes are difficult to identify, thus, data-driven and feature extraction methods can be employed for gas sensors anomaly detection.

There are plenty of challenges for underground gas concentration data analysis, the data is collected by underground gas sensors and stored as a time series in the mine's centralized server room, so the form of the gas concentration can inversely reflect the working status of the sensor. The brunt of analyzing this data is that underground gas data is generally unlabeled [8], which leads to ambiguity of the outcomes when using unsupervised learning and making supervised learning nearly impossible. Another challenge is whether data-based models of complex industrial processes reflect nature or not, which is hard to identify. And the tough nut is that the underground gas data are multi-source, heterogeneous, massive and high-dimensional [10]. Also, the features of gas contain the variation of gas concentration values, which is important for underground hazard prediction, such as the main characteristics of gas in a coal and gas outburst accident [9] are abnormal gas flow, sensor values fluctuate (such as dramatic increase), etc. Usually such features are selected and analyzed by manual labor, but when the amount of data increases [11], this method becomes inefficient and cannot guarantee accuracy. Thus, it is important to study how to process the gas data and uncover the patterns behind it efficiently and accurately.

The digitization of industrial production is advancing at a rapid pace, with the vast majority of production processes being able to represented by some numerical value. There are plenty of sensors in the underground linked with machines, devices and miners. Underground gas sensors are becoming highly accurate (if no abnormalities) and those monitoring values will uploaded to the cloud server as time series data [12,13], which means these data are ordered correlated in time. But those data only provide observations, not analysis, so when a sensor fails, it is difficult to determine with human eye. Gas concentration data is usually high-dimensional, non-normally distributed and nonstationary, with nonlinearity and missing values, outliers and noise, correlation and autocorrelation, sampling data size and sampling lag, will both affect the analysis results. Meanwhile, allowing a model to capture those coupling relationships could also be a challenge, traditional statistical models, such as BP neural networks [14,15], the SVR method [16] for upper corner gas concentration prediction, LSTM neural networks [17], and ARIMA-LSTM model [18]. Despite the results achieved by these models, they also suffered an unacceptable time consumption and loss when dealing with large scale data, other models such as ELM [19], decision trees [20] also fail to fit the gas data appropriately and can introduce other problem such as missing labels in the dataset, which labelling gas data is often even more difficult.

Due to the 4th Industrial Revolution (or Industry 4.0) [11], data-driven machine learning (ML) method looks like the most promising future for the mining field. Deep learning (DL) [21], a sub-discipline of machine learning, has brought us numerous surprises in the last decade, with systems capable of matching and exceeding human-level performance on a range of image recognition [22], natural language processing [23], self-driving cars [24], medical applications [37] etc. Deep learning algorithms, such as CNNs [25], RNNs [26], etc., using computer algorithms to simulate the learning of the human brain and allowing the computer to recognize and acquire knowledge from the real world, thus updating the parameters of neural network for better performance based on the newly acquired knowledge. The biggest advantage of deep learning is that it can automatically learn and discover features and patterns in the data, making it particularly suitable for gas data pattern recognition tasks. Through such methods, it can provide technical support for the safety management of coal mining enterprises, effectively improve the level of coal mine safety production, reduce or make early warning before accidents occur, improve coal mine safety production conditions. In particular, Cai [27] proposed a machine learning based probability density machine method for effectively prevent gas explosion accidents. Song [6] used the Hurst indices of the time series of gas indicators related to coal spontaneous combustion at monitoring sites, the temporal propensity of coal spontaneous combustion can be determined. Wen [28] used seven state-of-the-art deep learning models for predicts the concentrate ash content of coal flotation with froth images, and other work like Liu [29], also shows that deep convolutional neural network are capable of handling interval transition in the ash content. The above literature is based on the fact that the sensors are working properly, which provides a good theoretical basis for detecting the sensor's operating condition.

In this paper, we discusses a deep learning based feature extraction clustering method by converting gas concentration data into the form of Recurrence plot (RP) [30] to detect the space-time relationship of the gas concentration sensor in the underground working face. There are two reasons to do this [31], firstly, the deep learning algorithms for image recognition have reached a very high level, and has a complete set of data processing and measurement methods [32], by training neural networks it is possible to effectively recognize features in images such as edges, corners and contours, parts of objects. And using GPUs acceleration the model can be trained and optimized quickly. Secondly, RP plots can preserve all the features of a time series as it is converted to an image [33,34], this implies more and more direct characterization relationships for time series.

We would like to address the questions about how to determine the status of gas sensors by gas data features: (1) The gas concentration values are a direct reflection of the working condition of the working faces and current sensor operating status. (2) Working face is a semi-closed environment and the flow of gas has a temporal and spatial pattern. (3) The correlations between the gas data are not only in the overall time, but are even stronger in the segmented data. (4) The features of the gas data actually show a high-dimensional relationship in lag time, but are difficult to find on a one-dimensional timeline, so the conversion to images not only preserves time, but also facilitates the extraction of data features. One of the main objectives of this paper is also to discuss how the clustering methods for gas data can be applied to a broader decision making framework [35], not only by discuss the feasibility of imaging gas data, but also demonstrate at the feature level that high-dimensional gas data retains the properties of the original data in the feature space.

The paper is organized as follows, in Section 2 we discuss the methodology used in this paper and background principles, Section 3 is the experiment, while Section 4 is a summary and discussion of the paper.

There were still a number of issues that needed to be addressed: 1) Gas data (such as methane, carbon monoxide, and airflow wrapped in exhaust gases.) is complex and subject to many conditions, for example, mining excavation will cause the gas concentration to rise and slowly fall when shutting down for safety checks, however, there are other conditions that can cause gas concentrations to rise such as loose coal bodies or even coal and gas outburst. 2) Another challenge is that with the development of hardware and cloud data, there has been an explosion in the amount of data available for observation and analysis. With dozens of sensors in a working face monitoring various indicators such as gas concentration, carbon monoxide concentration, wind speed, temperature, etc., these data are stored as time series and uploaded directly to the central server. Analyzing data of this magnitude is a heavy burden for traditional models, in our cases, the volume of data in a month is usually over a million.

As part of industrial data analysis, data pre-processing plays a major role [36] and in this way, the data processing directly determines the overall experimental results. Depending on the artificial setting, the geologic pressure environment, the gas content of the formation and the excavation method, each working face presents a different pattern of gas variations. For other work faces this trend may not be obvious, but wherever an environment meets this definition, there is a certain relationship between its sensors which can be detected.

Feature extraction with CNN

Convolutional neural networks, a type of artificial neural network, are designed to process multi-dimensional data such as image and time series data. Given its strong power in feature extraction. A CNN is a mathematical structure that typically contains three types of layers, a convolutional layer, a pooling layer and a fully-connected layer. The convolutional and pooling layers, perform feature extraction, while the third layer, the fully-connected layer, maps the extracted features to a final output, such as classification. The convolutional layer plays a key role in CNNs, which are composed of a stack of mathematical operations such as convolution, a specialized type of linear operation. Nonetheless, the performance of a neural network depends on the structure of the model, the parameters, the training method and, more importantly, data. However, to labeling gas data is a very important research direction, but currently it is almost impossible. As time series data, the value of gas concentration at a point tx must be the result of some factor at time tx−n, such as mining, support, coal seam shedding, human intervention, etc. At the same time, the source of the value is not just a single situation, but is the result of coupling multiple factors, generally affecting the gas concentration, including: the gas content of the coal seam, the depth, thickness, inclination, mining height, length of the working face and even the thickness of the adjacent seam.

For feature extraction, we used three pre-trained models from PyTorch, VGG16, VGG19 and ResNet18, as feature extractors, trained on standard computer vision benchmark datasets, such as ImageNet classification dataset that consists of 1000 classes with 1.28 million training images. The pre-trained models have a more sensitive sense of the features in the data, e.g., VGG16 trained on the ImageNet image recognition task took two weeks on four GPUs to reach its current results with 138 million parameters. Unlike doing the classification task, we do not need the last layer of softmax mapping, so we need to make structural modifications to the three CNNs, the shape changes of the input RP plots data and the modified model parameters are shown in Fig. 3.Where we only need the data input and feature extraction part, as a classical convolutional neural network, VGG makes extensive use of 3 × 3 convolutional kernels. By doing so, it increases the depth of the network while ensuring the same receptive field, which can improve the effectiveness of the neural network to a certain extent. VGG networks have the obvious advantage of being very simple and improving the performance of the model by continuously deepening the neural network, but at the cost of using more computational resources and requiring more parameters, the difference between VGG16 and VGG19 is that VGG16 contains 16 hidden layers (13 convolutional and 3 fully connected layers), while VGG19 contains 19 hidden layers (16 convolutional and 3 fully connected layers). Since we are not doing a classification task but to extract more useful features, VGG would be a very good feature extractor.Fig. 3Model architecture and the function of each part.Fig. 3

Model architecture and the function of each part.

By introducing the concept of residual learning, ResNet adds residual units to the neural network. It is possible to have more layers of neural networks and less time complexity, unlike VGG, for each layer of input x and the mapping H(x) learned by the network, ResNet adds a new layer after this one to learn F(x):=H(x)−x, which means features not learned at the previous layer, and the original mapping is recast into F(x)+x.

In summary, the advantages of using deep convolutional neural networks as feature extractors are: (1) reduced human interference in the gas feature selection process, (2) both global and local features are utilized, (3) it sheds the potential improvements of safety mining with the advances of neural networks for the computer vision tasks.

Clustering method and evaluation metrics

The gas features extracted by the CNN are obviously partly redundant, so it is necessary to reduce the dimensionality of the features. Unlike feature extraction, a dimensionality contraction approach is that reduction of all the features of all the data, i.e., the total number of data remains the same after dimensionality reduction, but the dimensionality of the data is replaced by the principal components. The process of obtaining the relevant attributes contained in the given input data and the size of the given data will be finally reduced, keeping only the main information.

In this paper we use UMAP (Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection) for dimensionality contraction. UMAP is a novel manifold learning technique that is constructed from a theoretical framework based in Riemannian geometry and algebraic topology. Most importantly, UMAP has no computational restrictions on embedding dimension, making it viable as a general purpose dimension reduction technique for machine learning and can furthermore improve the accuracy of the clustering algorithm and reduce the time consumption. Clustering analysis, as a statistical analysis method for studying sample categories, differs from classification tasks in that clustering focuses on unlabeled data whose categories are unknown. To evaluate the results of any clustering task there is a basic problem: the estimation of similarity, i.e., the criteria within the same cluster and between different clusters. Therefore, in this paper we have selected three post-cluster evaluation metrics to measure the clustering effect of gas features, namely: Davies-Bouldin indices, Silhouette Coefficiency and Calinski-Harabasz Indices.

The Davies-Bouldin Indices (DBI) is a metric for evaluating clustering algorithms, by measuring the mean of the maximum similarity of each cluster, the smaller the DBI, the better the clustering, as shown(5)DBI=1k∑i=1kmaxj≠i(avg(Ci)+avg(Cj)dcen(Ci,Cj))where Ci and Cj are different clusters.

The Silhouette Coefficiency refers to a method that reflects the consistency of the clustering of the data and can be used to assess the degree of dispersion between clusters after clustering. The Silhouette Coefficiency takes a value between [−1, 1], if it is close to 1 it means that the sample has a reasonable clustering result, if it is close to −1 it means that the sample is more deserving of being grouped into other clusters, if it is close to 0 it means that the sample is on the boundary of the cluster, in general, the Silhouette Coefficiency can be formulated as(6)S(i)=b(i)−a(i)max{a(i),b(i)}(7)a(i)=1|Ci|−1∑j∈Ci,i≠jd(i,j)(8)b(i)=mink≠i1|Ck|∑j∈Cid(i,j)where for an i in the sample, a(i) is the mean of the dissimilarity of this sample i to other points in its cluster, and b(i) is the minimum of the mean dissimilarity to all points that are note in this cluster.

The Calinski-Harabasz indices also known as Variance Ration Criterion, is the ratio of the sum of the between-clusters dispersion and within-clusters dispersion, with the smaller the covariance of the data within a category the better, and the larger the covariance between categories the better - in short, the higher the score the better the performance, and the CH indices can be formulated as(9)s=tr(Bk)tr(Wk)×nE−kk−1where for a set of data E of size nE, by clustering them into k clusters, the CH indices s is defined by the ration of Bk and Wk, namely between-clusters dispersion mean and within-cluster dispersion. Furthermore, Bk and Wk can be expressed as(10)Wk=∑q=1k∑x∈Cq(x−cq)(x−cq)TBk=∑q=1knq(cq−cE)(cq−cE)Twhere Cq are the set of points of cluster q, cq is the center of q, cE is the center of E, and nq is the number of points in cluster q.

Experiments

We used CNN to perform feature extraction and clustering computations on millions of data sets over close to three months, including VGG16/19, ResNet18. We measured the clustering results by the following performance: Davies-Bouldin Indices (DBI) and Silhouette Coefficiency and Calinski-Harabasz indices. Also, given the baseline, we manually process one of the three months, which means the data was fine-tuned (e.g. the values T0-T1-T2 correspond exactly in time, there are no misalignments etc.) by consulting with professional staff in the underground, and the results were then clustered for the full unadjusted data.

In our experiments, all gas data are independent but correlated, independent in the sense that the sensor at any one location can be taken out and analyzed independently, it is just that the pattern at that measurement point may not be applicable to the others, but the method still holds. Correlation means that the measurement points are aerodynamically compatible with each other, although this relationship varies from mine to mine and even from working face to working face, it is reasonable to use one measurement point to extrapolate to another, which provides the necessary support for multi-point coupling analysis. Furthermore, the selection of the gas data size was based on the size of common machine vision datasets such as MNIST, CIFAR, etc., and on the actual working schedule of the mine, which, as far as we know, varies from mine to mine, so the data selection should be combined with the baseline and the actual. Fault detection of underground gas sensors has always been a difficult problem, except for a very few obvious features (such as power failure, zero calibration, etc.) gas sensor failure is not easy to detect. Although the methods mentioned in this paper proved to be feasible in this selected mine working face, with various digging methods, working face environments, and even mining equipment can lead to different results. Therefore, it is necessary to study the method in the conditions of different mines, such as shift time, gas outflow from the working face and so on.

Conclusion

Underground working status are a complex coupled phenomenon but can be derived from the analysis of sensor monitoring data. However, underground sensors often malfunction because of the distance to the depth of the ground and the harsh coal mining environment. This paper analyses the time-space relationships of gas monitoring data by using deep learning image feature engineering, by converting the features of gas concentration in time series to image format and then performing feature extraction. We have found that the sensor monitoring data from different locations can be represented and measured in relation to each other, allowing us to determine working status and abnormalities. The analysis of gas concentrations can be further use to prevent accidents caused by gas such as gas explosions, gas leaks and even coal and gas outburst. Also, such analysis can help us understand the mechanics of underground gas such as the way they flow and sorption during the floating. To address the problem of unlabeled gas data, we provide a generic analysis method that allows the data to be sliced and classified, paving the way for subsequent large-scale data analysis. We believe this method can be used to further explore the complex mechanics of the underground mine, which can be used in the future as part of underground decision making and for things like early warning, staff allocation, resource mobilization etc.
