Orientable quadratic equations in wreath products
Alexander Ushakov, Chloe Weiers

Abstract
. In this paper we study the complexity of solving orientable quadratic equations in wreath products A ≀ B A\wr B of finitely generated abelian groups. We give a classification of cases (depending on genus and other characteristics of a given equation) when the problem is computationally hard or feasible. Keywords. Diophantine problem, quadratic equations, spherical equations, metabelian groups, wreath product, complexity, NP-completeness. 2010 Mathematics Subject Classification. 20F16, 20F10, 68W30.



1.
Introduction

Let
F=F(Z)
denote the free group on countably many generators
Z=\{z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
. For a group
G
, an
equation over
G
with variables in
Z
is an equation of the form
W=1
, where
W\in F*G
. If
W=z_{i_{1}}g_{1}\cdots z_{i_{k}}g_{k}
, with
z_{i_{j}}\in Z
and
g_{j}\in G
, then we refer to
\{z_{i_{1}},\ldots,z_{i_{k}}\}
as the set of
variables
and to
\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{k}\}
as the set of
constants
(or
coefficients
) of
W
. We occasionally write
W(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k})
or
W(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k};g_{1},\ldots,g_{k})
to indicate that the variables in
W
are precisely
z_{1},\ldots,z_{k}
and (in the latter case) the constants are precisely
g_{1},\ldots,g_{k}
.

A
solution
to an equation
W(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k})=1
over
G
is a homomorphism

\varphi\colon F*G\rightarrow G

such that
\varphi|_{G}=1_{G}
and
W\in\ker\varphi
. If
\varphi
is a solution of
W=1
and
g_{i}=\varphi(z_{i})
, then we often say that
g_{1},\ldots,g_{k}
is a solution of
W=1
. We also write
W(g_{1},\ldots,g_{k})
or
W(\bar{g})
to denote the image of
W(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k})
under the homomorphism
F*G\rightarrow G
which maps
z_{i}\mapsto g_{i}
(and restricts to the identity on
G
). Note that while some authors allow for solutions in some overgroup
H
(with an embedding
G\hookrightarrow H
), in this article we only consider solutions in
G
.

In this paper we assume that
G
comes equipped with a fixed generating set
X
and elements of
G
are given as products of elements of
X
and their inverses. This naturally defines the length (or size) of the equation
W=1
as the length of its left-hand side
W
.

Definition 1.1
.

An equation
W=1
is called
quadratic
if each variable appears exactly twice (as either
z_{i}
or
z_{i}^{-1}
).

The
Diophantine problem
(
{\mathbf{DP}}
) in a group
G
for a class of equations
C
is an algorithmic question to decide whether a given equation
W=1
in
C
has a solution. In this paper we study the class of quadratic equations over wreath products of finitely generated abelian groups.

1.1.
Classification of quadratic equations

We say that equations
W=1
and
V=1
are
equivalent

if there is an automorphism
\phi\in\Aut(F\ast G)
such that

\phi
is the identity on
G
and
\phi(W)=V
. It is a well
known consequence of the classification of compact surfaces
that any quadratic equation over
G
is equivalent, via an
automorphism
\phi
computable in time
O(|W|^{2})
, to an equation
in exactly one of the following three
standard forms
(see

[
2
,
6
]
):

(1)

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{m}z_{j}^{-1}c_{j}z_{j}

\displaystyle=1

\displaystyle m\geq 1,

(2)

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{g}[x_{i},y_{i}]\prod_{j=1}^{m}z_{j}^{-1}c_{j}z_{j}

\displaystyle=1

\displaystyle g\geq 1,m\geq 0,

(3)

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{g}x_{i}^{2}\prod_{j=1}^{m}z_{j}^{-1}c_{j}z_{j}

\displaystyle=1

\displaystyle g\geq 1,m\geq 0.

The number
g
is the
genus
of the equation, and both
g
and
m

(the number of constants) are invariants.
The standard forms are called, respectively,

spherical
,
orientable of genus
g
, and

non-orientable of genus
g
.

1.2.
Previous results

The Diophantine problem for
quadratic equations naturally generalizes fundamental (Dehn) problems of group theory such as the word and conjugacy problems. There is a profound relationship between quadratic equations and compact surfaces, which makes quadratic equations an interesting
object of study.

The study of quadratic equations originated with Malcev,
who in
[
14
]
considered some particular
quadratic equations over free groups. This line of research for free groups was continued with the study of solution sets in
[
6
]
. Furthermore,
{\mathbf{NP}}
-completeness was proved in
[
4
,
9
]
.
Various classes of infinite groups were analyzed:
hyperbolic groups were studied in

[
7
,
10
]
,
the first Grigorchuk group was studied in

[
11
]
and
[
1
]
,
free metabelian groups were studied in

[
12
,
13
]
,
and metabelian Baumslag–Solitar groups were studied in

[
15
]
.
The Diophantine problem for quadratic equations over the
lamplighter group was recently shown to be decidable by Kharlampovich, Lopez,
and Miasnikov
(see
[
8
]
). Additionally, Ushakov and Weiers proved that the Diophantine problem for orientable quadratic equations over the lamplighter group is decidable in polynomial time, and the problem for non-orientable equations of genus two is decidable in linear time and of genus one is
{\mathbf{NP}}
-complete (see
[
20
]
).

Natural applications of quadratic equations (particularly spherical equations)
to cryptography have also been explored. In particular,

[
19
]
establishes connections
between computational group theory and lattice-based cryptography
via spherical equations over a certain class of finite metabelian groups.

1.3.
Our results

This paper goes beyond the lamplighter group
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}\wr{\mathbb{Z}}
studied in

[
20
]
and provides a deep analysis
of the computational combinatorics for orientable quadratic equations
over wreath products
A\wr B
of finitely generated abelian groups.
In
[
20
]
, it was shown that
the number of conjugates
m
is the source of hardness for solving
equations (
1
) in the lamplighter group.
For a wreath product of general finitely generated abelian groups

A\wr B
the situation is more complicated.
Throughout the paper we assume that
A
is not trivial.

First, we show that for a general wreath product of finitely generated abelian groups
A
and
B
, the Diophantine problem for orientable equations remains in
{\mathbf{NP}}
, even when

A
and
B
are given as part of the input (the uniform problem).
However, in the general case, parameters such as the ranks of

A
and
B
and the genus of the equation impact the complexity
of solving these equations.

•

The complexity of the problem is directly influenced by the group
B
.
Specifically, for a fixed
A\wr B
,
the problem is
{\mathbf{NP}}
-hard if and only if

|B|=\infty
, see Corollary
5.2
.

•

The contribution of the group
A
to the complexity is limited in a sense
that the problem is
{\mathbf{NP}}
-hard for
A_{1}\wr B

if and only if it is
{\mathbf{NP}}
-hard for
A_{2}\wr B
,
see Corollary
5.7
.
However, if
A
is a part of the input, the problem becomes
{\mathbf{NP}}
-hard
even when
B={\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
is fixed, see Corollary
5.8
.

•

The genus
g
also plays a crucial role in the complexity of the equation.
If
g
is sufficiently large (when
g\geq\text{rank}(B)/2
), then
the equation can be solved in polynomial time, see Corollary
5.4
.
Otherwise, for intermediate values of
g
such as
g<\text{rank}(B)/2
,
the problem generally remains
{\mathbf{NP}}
-hard as shown in
Corollary
5.5
.

1.4.
Model of computation and representation of basic objects

We use a random-access machine as a model of computation.
Integers are given in unary form unless stated otherwise.
Elements of
A\wr B
are given as words, i.e., sequences of letters
of the group alphabet
\{a_{1}^{\pm 1},\ldots,a_{n}^{\pm 1},b_{1}^{\pm 1},\ldots,b_{m}^{\pm 1}\}
for
A\wr B
. Additional details for representation of certain objects are given as needed later in the paper.

1.5.
Outline

In Section
2
, we recall the definition of the wreath product

A\wr B
and review its basic properties. We also discuss the properties of finitely generated abelian groups and lattices,

In Section

QSP
), to which we later reduce the Diophantine problem for orientable quadratic equations. In Section
4
,
we examine the computational properties of several variations of

, which correspond to the Diophantine problem for certain natural classes of equations over wreath products
A\wr B
.
In Section
5
,
we use the computational properties of
QSP
established in Section
4

to draw conclusions about orientable quadratic equations.
The paper concludes with Section
6
.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Wreath products of abelian groups

We begin by reviewing basic facts about restricted wreath products.
Let
A
and
B
be groups. Define a set

A^{B}=\left\{\,f\colon B\to A\;\middle|\;|\supp(f)|<\infty\,\right\}

and a binary operation
+
on
A^{B}
which, for
f,g\in A^{B}
, produces
f+g\in A^{B}
defined by

(f+g)(x)=f(x)+g(x)\ \mbox{ for }\ x\in B.

For
f\in A^{B}
and
b\in B
, define
f^{b}\in A^{B}
by

f^{b}(x)=f(bx)\ \mbox{ for }\ x\in B,

which is a right
B
-action on
A^{B}
because
f^{e}=f
(where
e\in B
is the identity of
B
) and
f^{(b_{1}b_{2})}(x)=f(b_{1}b_{2}x)=(f^{b_{1}})^{b_{2}}(x)
.
Hence, we can consider a semidirect product
B\ltimes A^{B}

equipped with the operation

(\delta_{1},f_{1})(\delta_{2},f_{2})=(\delta_{1}+\delta_{2},f_{1}^{\delta_{2}}+f_{2}).

The group
B\ltimes A^{B}
is called the
restricted wreath product
of
A
and
B
and is denoted by
A\wr B
. Henceforth we assume that
A
and
B
are finitely generated abelian groups and use additive group notation.

It is convenient to treat
a\in A
as a function
f\in A^{B}
defined as

f(x)=\begin{cases}a&\mbox{ if }x=0,\\
0&\mbox{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

For
a\in A
and
b\in B
, that allows us to use the notation
a^{b}

for a function
f\in A^{B}
such that

f(x)=\begin{cases}a&\mbox{ if }x=-b,\\
0&\mbox{ otherwise,}\end{cases}

and, slightly abusing notation,
the corresponding element
(0,f)\in A\wr B
.
Then every
f\in A^{B}
can be expressed as

f=\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}^{-b_{i}},

if
\supp(f)=\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{k}\}
and
f(b_{i})=a_{i}
.
This notation is unique for
f
if the exponents
b_{1},\ldots,b_{k}
are distinct
and
a_{1},\dots,a_{k}
are nontrivial.

2.2.
A^{B}
as a group ring

For a commutative ring
R
with unity and a group

(G,\cdot)
we can define the
group ring

R[G]
of
G
with
scalar coefficients in
R
as the set of linear combinations
of elements of
G

R[G]=\left\{\,\sum_{g\in G}r_{g}\cdot g\;\middle|\;r_{g}\in R,\mbox{ where all but finitely many $r_{g}$ are $0$}\,\right\},

with addition defined by

\sum_{g\in G}r_{g}\cdot g+\sum_{g\in G}s_{g}\cdot g=\sum_{g\in G}(r_{g}+s_{g})\cdot g

and multiplication
\times
defined by

{\left(\sum_{g\in G}r_{g}\cdot g\right)}\times{\left(\sum_{g\in G}s_{g}\cdot g\right)}=\sum_{g\in G}t_{g}\cdot g,\mbox{ where }t_{g}=\sum_{g_{1}\cdot g_{2}=g}r_{g_{1}}s_{g_{2}}.

Now, by the fundamental theorem of finitely generated abelian groups,

A\ \simeq\ {\mathbb{Z}}^{s}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{\alpha_{1}}\times\dots\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{\alpha_{t}}.

This decomposition naturally defines
A
as a direct product of cyclic rings,
which makes
A
a commutative ring with unity and allows us to define
the group ring
A[B]
.
The mapping
\tau\colon(A^{B},+)\to(A[B],+)
defined by

f\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\tau}}{{\mapsto}}\ \sum_{b\in\supp(f)}f(b)\cdot b,

is an abelian group isomorphism.
Therefore, we can view
A^{B}
as a group ring with
the ring of scalars
A
and the basis
B
. Observe that, for a function
f\in A^{B}
, if
\supp(f)=\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{k}\}
and
f(b_{i})=a_{i}
, then
\tau(f)=a_{1}\cdot b_{1}+\dots+a_{k}\cdot b_{k}
.

Lemma 2.1
.

\tau(f)=a_{1}\cdot b_{1}+\dots+a_{k}\cdot b_{k}\ \ \ \Leftrightarrow\ \ \ \tau(f^{\delta})=a_{1}\cdot\delta^{-1}b_{1}+\dots+a_{k}\cdot\delta^{-1}b_{k}

for every
f\in A^{B}
and
\delta\in B
.

Proof.

For every
x\in B
and
a\in A
, we have

f(x)=a\ \ \Leftrightarrow\ \ f^{\delta}(\delta^{-1}x)=a.
Thus the statement holds.
∎

Therefore, multiplication on
A[B]
induces multiplication on
A^{B}

{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}^{b_{i}}\right)}\cdot{\left(\sum_{j=1}^{l}c_{j}^{d_{j}}\right)}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{l}a_{i}^{b_{i}}c_{j}^{d_{j}}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{l}(a_{i}c_{j})^{b_{i}+d_{j}}.

Denote by
1
the unity of
A
. Then
1^{0}
is the unity of
A^{B}
.
Observe that, for any
f\in A^{B}
and
b\in B
, the following identities hold:

1)

f^{b}=f\cdot 1^{b}
,

2)

f^{0}=f=f\cdot 1^{0}
,

3)

f-f^{b}=f(1^{0}-1^{b})
.

2.3.
Some properties of
A^{B}

Let us introduce some notation.
For a subgroup
N\unlhd B
, let
\varphi\colon B\to B/N
be the
canonical epimorphism defined by
b\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\varphi}}{{\mapsto}}b+N

and
\varphi^{\ast}\colon A^{B}\to A^{B/N}
the induced epimorphism
defined for
f\in A^{B}
and
x\in B/N
by

\varphi^{\ast}(f)(x)=\sum_{\varphi(y)=x}f(y).

For
f,g\in A^{B}
,
f=_{A^{B/N}}g
means that
\varphi^{\ast}(f)=\varphi^{\ast}(g)
.

For
{\overline{b}}=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{k})\in B^{k}
, define
a subgroup
N={\left\langle b_{1},\ldots,b_{k}\right\rangle}\leq B
,
a linear function
\lambda_{{\overline{b}}}\colon(A^{B})^{k}\to A^{B}
by

(f_{1},\ldots,f_{k})\ \ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\lambda_{{\overline{b}}}}}{{\mapsto}}\ \ f_{1}(1^{0}-1^{b_{1}})+\cdots+f_{k}(1^{0}-1^{b_{k}}),

and denote the epimorphism
\varphi^{\ast}\colon A^{B}\to A^{B/N}

defined above by
\pi_{\overline{b}}
.
It is easy to check that both functions are homomorphisms
and that
\im(\lambda_{{\overline{b}}})\subseteq\ker(\pi_{{\overline{b}}})

because

\displaystyle g\in\im(\lambda_{{\overline{b}}})

\displaystyle\ \ \Rightarrow\ \ g=f_{1}(1^{0}-1^{b_{1}})+\cdots+f_{k}(1^{0}-1^{b_{k}})\mbox{ for some }f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}\in A^{B}

\displaystyle\ \ \Rightarrow\ \ \pi_{{\overline{b}}}(g)=\pi_{{\overline{b}}}(f_{1})\pi_{{\overline{b}}}(1^{0}-1^{b_{1}})+\cdots+\pi_{{\overline{b}}}(f_{k})\pi_{{\overline{b}}}(1^{0}-1^{b_{k}})=0.

Lemma 2.2
.

a^{x}-a^{x+\delta}\in\im(\lambda_{\overline{b}})

for every
a\in A
,
x\in B
, and
\delta\in N
.

Proof.

Since
\delta\in N
, then
\delta=\alpha_{1}b_{1}+\dots+\alpha_{k}b_{k}

for some
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.
We proceed using induction on
\alpha=|\alpha_{1}|+\dots+|\alpha_{k}|
.
If
\alpha=0
, then
\delta=0
and there is nothing to prove.
Otherwise, without loss of generality, assume that
\alpha_{1}>0
. Let
\delta^{\prime}=(\alpha_{1}-1)b_{1}+\dots+\alpha_{k}b_{k}=\delta-b_{1}
. Let
\alpha^{\prime}
be the corresponding
\alpha
-value for
\delta^{\prime}
. Then we have

\displaystyle a^{x}-a^{x+\delta}

\displaystyle=(a^{x}-a^{x+\delta^{\prime}})+(a^{x+\delta^{\prime}}-a^{x+\delta})

\displaystyle=(a^{x}-a^{x+\delta^{\prime}})+a^{x+\delta^{\prime}}(1^{0}-1^{b_{1}}),

where
a^{x+\delta^{\prime}}(1^{0}-1^{b_{1}})\in\im(\lambda_{\overline{b}})

and
a^{x}-a^{x+\delta^{\prime}}
is an element of the same type with
\alpha^{\prime}<\alpha
.
Hence, by the induction assumption,
a^{x}-a^{x+\delta^{\prime}}\in\im(\lambda_{\overline{b}})

and
a^{x}-a^{x+\delta}\in\im(\lambda)
.
∎

Proposition 2.3
.

\im(\lambda_{{\overline{b}}})\ =\ \ker(\pi_{{\overline{b}}})
.

Proof.

We have seen that
\im(\lambda_{{\overline{b}}})\subseteq\ker(\pi_{{\overline{b}}})
. Let
g\in A^{B}
be a nontrivial function such that
g\in\ker(\pi_{\overline{b}})
. We now show that
\ker(\pi_{{\overline{b}}})\subseteq\im(\lambda_{{\overline{b}}})
using induction on
|\supp(g)|
. For the base case, we use
|\supp(g)|=2
; the statement in this case holds by Lemma
2.2
. Assume that the statement holds up to
|\supp(g)|=n
, and consider the case where
|\supp(g)|=n+1
. Since
g\in\ker(\pi_{\overline{b}})
, then at least two elements from
\supp(g)
collapse modulo
N
, i.e., for some
a,c\in A
,
x\in B
and
\delta\in N
we have

g=a^{x}+c^{x+\delta}+g^{\prime}=(a^{x}-a^{x+\delta})+(c+a)^{x+\delta}+g^{\prime}.

By Lemma
2.2
,

a^{x}-a^{x+\delta}\in\im(\lambda_{\overline{b}})\subseteq\ker(\pi_{{\overline{b}}})
. Hence,
(c+a)^{x+\delta}+g^{\prime}\in\ker(\pi_{{\overline{b}}})
. Since
|\supp((c+a)^{x+\delta}+g^{\prime})|<|\supp(g)|=n+1
, the statement holds by mathematical induction.
∎

Proposition 2.4
.

Consider
W=\sum_{i=1}^{k}f_{i}(1^{0}-1^{b_{i}})+W^{\prime}
,
where
b_{1},\dots,b_{k}\in B
are constants,

f_{1},\dots,f_{k},\ldots\in A^{B}
are unknowns, and
W^{\prime}
is independent of
f_{1},\dots,f_{k}
. Let
N={\left\langle b_{1},\dots,b_{k}\right\rangle}
. The following are equivalent:

(a)

W=0
has a solution in
A^{B}
,

(b)

W=0
has a solution in
A^{B/N}
,

(c)

W^{\prime}=0
has a solution in
A^{B/N}
.

Proof.

Equivalence of (b) and (c) is obvious. Below we prove
equivalence of (a) and (b).

“(a)
\Rightarrow
(b)”
Use homomorphism
\pi_{{\overline{b}}}\colon A^{B}\to A^{B/N}
.

“(a)
\Leftarrow

Suppose that

satisfy

in

.
Choose any

satisfying

.
Then
\pi_{\overline{b}}(W(f_{1}^{\ast},\ldots,f_{k}^{\ast},\ldots))=0
and we have

\underbrace{\sum f_{i}^{\ast}(1^{0}-1^{b_{i}})}_{\mbox{\scalebox{0.8}{belongs to}}\im(\lambda_{\overline{b}})}+W^{\prime}(\cdots)=W(f_{1}^{\ast},\ldots,f_{k}^{\ast},\ldots)\in\ker(\pi_{\overline{b}})\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle 2.3}}{{=}}\ \im(\lambda_{\overline{b}}).

Hence,
W^{\prime}(\cdots)\in\im(\lambda_{\overline{b}})

and, since

is independent of

,
there exist

satisfying

\sum_{i=1}^{k}f_{i}(1^{0}-1^{b_{i}})=-W^{\prime}(\cdots)
.
Thus,

has a solution in

.
∎

2.5.
Cayley graph

Let
G
be a group with a finite generating set
X
.
By
\Gamma_{G}
we denote the Cayley graph for
G
with respect to
X
.
Note that
G
is a discrete metric space in which, for any
g,h\in G
,
the
distance
from
g
to
h
,
d(g,h)
,
is the distance from
g
to
h
in
\Gamma_{G}
.
Let
S\subset G
. Then we define the
diameter
of
S
,
\diam(S)
, as

\diam(S)=\sup\left\{\,d{\left(g,h\right)}\;\middle|\;g,h\in S\,\right\}.

Similarly, we define the diameter of a function

f=a_{1}^{-g_{1}}+\cdots+a_{k}^{-g_{k}}\in A^{G}
as

\diam(f)=\diam{\left(\left\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{k}\right\}\right)}.

The
ball
of radius
r
in the Cayley graph of
G
is the set

\mathcal{B}_{r}=\left\{\,g\in G\;\middle|\;d(1,g)\leq r\,\right\}.

Define the
growth function

\gr_{X}(n)\colon{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}
of
G

as
\gr_{X}(n)=|\mathcal{B}_{n}|
.
The
geodesic length

|g|
of
g\in G
is the minimal
k
such that
g\in\mathcal{B}_{k}
.
It is a well-known fact that the growth function of an abelian group is bounded by a polynomial.

2.6.
The rank of an abelian group

Let
B
be a finitely generated abelian group.
In this paper by the
rank
of a finitely generated abelian group
B
we mean the minimum number of generators for
B
.
Suppose that
B
is given by the following data:

•

a set of generators
b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}
for
B
;

•

a set of relations
{\overline{r}}_{1},\ldots,{\overline{r}}_{m}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
for
B
.

The problem of finding
\text{rank}(B)
can be
solved in polynomial time by
constructing a matrix of the relations

{\overline{r}}_{1},\ldots,{\overline{r}}_{m}

and computing its Smith normal form
(see
[
18
]
), which gives a presentation for
B
as a direct product of cyclic groups

B\simeq{\mathbb{Z}}_{\alpha_{1}}\times\dots\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{\alpha_{y}}\times{\mathbb{Z}}^{k-y},

where
\alpha_{1}\mid\alpha_{2}\mid\dots\mid\alpha_{y}
.
The rank of
B
is the number of nontrivial factors in the product above, i.e.,

\text{rank}(B)=k-\max\{i\mid\alpha_{i}=1\}
.

Let
S\leq B
, where
B
is given as above and generators for

, viewed as the corresponding linear combinations of

, are given by

. We can then efficiently solve a similar problem of finding the rank of

. For that it is sufficient to find the space of relations for

. Consider a subgroup of

generated by concatenated vectors

where

is the standard basis for
{\mathbb{Z}}^{k}
.
Notice that

(t_{1},\ldots,t_{k})\mbox{ is a relation for }{\overline{s}}_{1},\ldots,{\overline{s}}_{k}\ \ \Leftrightarrow\ \ (\underbrace{0,\dots,0}_{n},t_{1},\ldots,t_{k})\in E.

The set of element
(0,\dots,0,t_{1},\ldots,t_{k})\in E

can be captured by solving the following linear vector-equation:

\alpha_{1}{\overline{r}}_{1}+\dots+\alpha_{m}{\overline{r}}_{m}+\beta_{1}{\overline{s}}_{1}+\dots+\beta_{k}{\overline{s}}_{k}={\overline{0}}

and then truncate the basis vectors by
deleting the first

The result generates the space of relations for

Once relations for
{\overline{s}}_{1},\ldots,{\overline{s}}_{k}
are found, we construct
a matrix of those relations and find

the same way as for
B
.
This can be done in polynomial time.

2.7.
Lattices

We now present several definitions and lemmas related to integer lattices.
Recall that an integer lattice

is a subgroup of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
.
Algebraically

, where

Associated with a given lattice

containing

The next lemma follows immediately from the definition of successive minima.

Lemma 2.5

Suppose that

for every

From the Gram–Schmidt orthogonalization process, define the orthogonalized vectors

and the

{\mathbf{v}}_{i}^{\ast}={\mathbf{v}}_{i}-\sum_{j<i}\frac{{\left\langle{\mathbf{v}}_{i},{\mathbf{v}}_{j}^{\ast}\right\rangle}}{\|{\mathbf{v}}_{j}^{\ast}\|^{2}}{\mathbf{v}}_{j}^{\ast},\ \ \ \ \pi_{i}({\mathbf{v}})=\sum_{j=i}^{n}\frac{{\left\langle{\mathbf{v}},{\mathbf{v}}_{j}^{\ast}\right\rangle}}{\|{\mathbf{v}}_{j}^{\ast}\|^{2}}{\mathbf{v}}_{j}^{\ast}.

Clearly, the Gram–Schmidt orthogonal vectors can be represented as

, where

.

Definition 2.6

3.1.
Notation and conventions

We first introduce some convenient notation and conventions for describing certain sequences of variables and constants.

•

•

When we say that a sequence of functions

For a sequence

.

3.2.
The quotient sum problem

Here we define a decision problem to which we subsequently
reduce the Diophantine problem for orientable quadratic equations.

Quotient sum problem

({\textsf{QSP}})

In:

Tuple
(A,B,{\overline{f}},h)
, where
A
and
B
are finitely generated abelian groups,
{\overline{f}}\in A^{B}
, and
h\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

Question:

Is there a sequence
{\overline{\delta}}\in B
and a subgroup
N\leq B
such that

(8)

\sum_{i=1}^{m}f_{i}^{\delta_{i}}=_{A^{B/N}}0\ \mbox{ and }\ \text{rank}(N)\leq h?

Some details of data representation are in order.
A finitely generated abelian group
A
is given by a set of generators
(symbols)
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
and a finite set of relations
R_{A}\subseteq{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
.
Elements of
A
are defined by vectors
(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}

using the formula

which is viewed as a composition
of generators (and their inverses),
i.e.
\alpha_{i}
’s are written in unary.
The size of a given representation
(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})
of
a
is

\size((\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}))=|\alpha_{1}|+\dots+|\alpha_{n}|.

Presentations of elements of
B
and their sizes are defined analogously.

A function

with
\supp(f)=\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{m}\}
and
f(b_{i})=a_{i}

is defined by a finite set of pairs
\{(a_{1},b_{1}),\ldots,(a_{m},b_{m})\}
.
The size of this representation of

is

\size(f)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}(\size(a_{i})+\size(b_{i})).

Given an instance
I=(A,B,{\overline{f}},h)
of
QSP
, we can define
\size(I)
as

\size(I)=\size(A)+\size(B)+\size({\overline{f}})+h,

where
\size(A)
(or
\size(B)
) is the size of the presentation of
A
(or
B
) and
\size({\overline{f}})=\sum\size(f_{i})
.

3.3.

as a function

By definition,

involves multiple parameters, namely

,
B
,
{\overline{f}}
, and

. To study the problem in more detail and explore the interactions

That allows us to use the following notation for versions of

•

.

and

.

-lamps each of length

Theorem 4.3
.

Let

and

be finitely generated abelian groups.
If

Fix any nontrivial

4.2.
Finite group

In this section we analyze

when the group

is finite.

Theorem 4.4
.

If
|B|<\infty
, then
{\textsf{QSP}}(A,B,\cdot,\cdot)
is in
{\mathbf{P}}

for any fixed finitely generated abelian group

.

Proof.

Fix an instance
I=(A,B,{\overline{f}},h)
of
QSP
.
Let
\{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\}
be the given generating set for
A
.
Then
X=\left\{\,a_{i}^{-b}\in A^{B}\;\middle|\;i=1,\dots,n,\ b\in B\,\right\}
, where

a_{i}^{-b}(x)=\begin{cases}a_{i}&x=b\\
0&x\neq b,\end{cases}

is a finite generating set for the abelian group
A^{B}
, i.e.
A^{B}
is an
n|B|
-generated abelian group. Now consider the following subsets of
A^{B}
:

\displaystyle V_{0}

\displaystyle=\{0\},

\displaystyle V_{i}

\displaystyle=V_{i-1}+\left\{\,f_{i}^{\delta}\;\middle|\;\delta\in B\,\right\},

\displaystyle(i

\displaystyle=1,\dots,m),

where

here is the Minkowski sum. It is straightforward to construct
V_{0},\dots,V_{m}
starting from
V_{0}
.
We claim that
V_{m}
can be constructed in polynomial time in terms
of
\size(I)
.

As usual, for
g\in A^{B}
, by
|g|_{X}
we denote the length of
g
in
generators
X
.
Also, notice that, by definition of
X
,
|g^{\delta_{i}}|_{X}=|g|_{X}

for every
\delta_{i}\in B
and

.
Then

\displaystyle f\in V_{j}

\displaystyle\ \Rightarrow\ f=\sum_{i=1}^{j}f_{i}^{\delta_{i}},\mbox{ for some }\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{j}\in B

\displaystyle\ \Rightarrow\ |f|_{X}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{j}|f_{i}^{\delta_{i}}|_{X}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{j}|f_{i}|_{X}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}|f_{i}|_{X}\leq\size(I),

and hence

.
Recall from Section
2.5
that
\gr_{X}(\size(I))\leq p(\size(I))

for some polynomial

. Therefore,
|V_{j}|\leq\gr_{X}(\size(I))\leq p(\size(I))

for every

.
It then follows that

can be constructed in polynomial time.

Finally, since
B
is fixed and finite,
it has finitely many subgroups

that can be precomputed
(together with their ranks).
Hence, we can test if condition (
8
)
holds for some
f\in V_{m}
and some

in polynomial time.
∎

In fact, a stronger statement holds.

Corollary 4.5
.

{\textsf{QSP}}(\cdot_{\text{rank}(A)\leq K},\cdot_{|B|\leq m},\cdot,\cdot)
is in
{\mathbf{P}}
.

Proof.

The proof of Theorem

works in this case because
the values of the growth function for

can be bounded by the same polynomial
for any groups

∎

At the same time, Theorem

does not generalize to the case when

-hard.
Ultimately, the source of hardness for the problem

4.3.
Clusters in

Let

Define an equivalence relation

The graph

for
{\overline{f}}

. For a sequence
{\overline{f}}
of functions
f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}
and

define the

of

\diam(C,{\overline{f}})=\diam{\left(\bigcup_{i\in C}\supp(f_{i})\right)}.

Let
\varphi\colon B\to B/N
be a canonical epimorphism and

.
We can define clusters modulo a subgroup
N
using a parallel construction. Define the equivalence relation
R_{\varphi}({\overline{f}})
as the transitive closure of

J_{\varphi}({\overline{f}})=\left\{\,(i,j)\in I\times I\;\middle|\;\varphi(\supp(f_{i}))\cap\varphi(\supp(f_{j}))\neq\varnothing\,\right\}.

Then a

for
{\overline{f}}
is an equivalence class for

for
{\overline{f}}

G_{\varphi}({\overline{f}})=(I,J_{\varphi}({\overline{f}}))
is called the

for
{\overline{f}}
.

Lemma 4.8
.

\diam(C,{\overline{f}})\leq\sum_{i\in C}\diam(f_{i})

for every
C\in{\mathcal{C}}({\overline{f}})
.

Proof.

We proceed with induction on
|C|
. If
|C|=1
, then

C=\{i\}
for some
i\in I
and

\diam(C,{\overline{f}})=\diam(f_{i})

and the statement holds.
Assume that the statement holds when
|C|=n
and consider a cluster
C

of
n+1
functions. Without loss of generality,
C=\{1,\ldots,n+1\}

and the cluster-graph
G({\overline{f}})

has a connected component on vertices
\{1,\ldots,n+1\}
.
That component must have at least one vertex that is not a cut-vertex.
Without loss of generality,
n+1
is not a cut-vertex.
Deleting
n+1
from
G({\overline{f}})
creates a connected component

and, by the induction assumption,

\diam(C^{\prime},{\overline{f}})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\diam(f_{i}).

Since

for some

and

contains some point

.
Hence, by the triangle inequality,

d(y,z)\leq d(y,x)+d(x,z)\leq\diam(C^{\prime},{\overline{f}})+\diam(f_{n+1})=\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\diam(f_{i})

for any

Therefore, the claimed inequality holds.
∎

{\mathcal{C}}({\overline{f}})

\varphi(\supp(f_{i}))\cap\varphi(\supp(f_{j}))=\varnothing

Recall that by

First, we show that the problem is efficiently decidable

Theorem 4.20

{\textsf{QSP}}(\cdot,B,\cdot,\cdot_{h\geq\text{rank}(B)})\in{\mathbf{P}}

“

Imposing a strict condition

on the number of functions in

allows to get a uniform result over the choice of

Theorem 4.23
.

Fix
h\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

{\textsf{QSP}}(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot_{|{\overline{f}}|=1},h)
is in
{\mathbf{P}}
.

Proof.

Suppose that a given instance
I=(A,B,{\overline{f}},h)
of
QSP
has a solution,
where
{\overline{f}}=(f_{1})
.
Then
f_{1}^{\delta_{1}}=_{A^{B/N}}0
for some
N\leq B
satisfying

\text{rank}(N)\leq h
.
For simplicity we assume that
B={\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
.
Clearly, we may assume that
\delta_{1}=0
.
Below we prove that the description of a suitable
subgroup
N
can be found in polynomial time.

As in the proof of Theorem
4.19
, we can define the set

S=\left\{\,p_{i}-p_{j}\;\middle|\;p_{i},p_{j}\in\supp(f_{1})\,\right\}\subseteq B,

and the subgroup
N^{\prime}={\left\langle S\cap N\right\rangle}\leq N
, which is generated by
some elements of
S

(in general, by more than
h
elements of
S
)
and that works as
N
, i.e.,

f_{1}=_{A^{B/{N^{\prime}}}}0\ \ \mbox{ and }\ \ \text{rank}(N^{\prime})\leq\text{rank}(N)\leq h.

Fix any basis for
N^{\prime}={\left\langle{\overline{v}}_{1},\dots,{\overline{v}}_{h}\right\rangle}

(notice that in general
{\overline{v}}_{i}\notin S
) and define a new subgroup

(12)

N^{\prime\prime}=N^{\prime\prime}({\overline{v}}_{1},\dots,{\overline{v}}_{h})=\left\{\,\alpha_{1}{\overline{v}}_{1}+\dots+\alpha_{h}{\overline{v}}_{h}\in B\;\middle|\;\alpha_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}\,\right\}.

Observe that
N^{\prime\prime}
is the maximum subgroup of
B

containing
N^{\prime}
and satisfying

\text{rank}(N^{\prime\prime})=\text{rank}(N^{\prime})
, i.e.,
N^{\prime\prime}
works as
N^{\prime}
.
Notice that (
12
) defines
N^{\prime\prime}

as an intersection of the vector space

\Span({\overline{v}}_{1},\dots,{\overline{v}}_{h})
and
B={\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
.
Furthermore,
{\overline{v}}_{1},\dots,{\overline{v}}_{h}\in N^{\prime}={\left\langle S\cap N\right\rangle}
.
Hence,
N^{\prime\prime}
can be captured by choosing up to
h

elements
{\overline{s}}_{1},{\overline{s}}_{2},\dots
from
S
as

(13)

N^{\prime\prime}=\left\{\,\alpha_{1}{\overline{s}}_{1}+\alpha_{2}{\overline{s}}_{2}+\dots\in B\;\middle|\;\alpha_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}\,\right\},

because for that purpose it is sufficient to capture the underlying vector space.
Therefore, to find
N^{\prime\prime}
one can enumerate all tuples of at most
h

elements
({\overline{s}}_{1},{\overline{s}}_{2},\dots)
from
S
,
and for each tuple check if
f_{1}=_{A^{B/{N^{\prime\prime}}}}0
.
To check the latter condition, it is not even necessary
to find a generating set for
N^{\prime\prime}
, one can work modulo

\Span({\overline{s}}_{1},{\overline{s}}_{2},\dots)
, which can be done in polynomial time.

Finally, the number of tuples of length
h
is
{|S|\choose h}=O(|S|^{h})

and
|S|=O(|\supp(f_{1})|^{2})
.
Hence, the number of all tuples
of length at most
h
is
O(|\supp(f_{1})|^{2h})
.
Thus, the described procedure can be performed in polynomial time.
∎

4.6.
Bounded number of functions

Here we consider a version of
QSP
in which
the number of given functions in
{\overline{f}}=(f_{1},\dots,f_{m})

is bounded by a fixed constant
M
.

Theorem 4.24
.

{\textsf{QSP}}(\cdot,B,\cdot_{|{\overline{f}}|<M},\cdot)
is in
{\mathbf{P}}

for any finitely generated abelian group
B
and
M\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

Proof.

By Theorem
4.20
,
{\textsf{QSP}}(\cdot,B,\cdot,\cdot_{h\geq\text{rank}(B)})\in{\mathbf{P}}
.
Hence, we may assume that

h<\text{rank}(B).

Consider an instance
I=(A,B,{\overline{f}},h)
of
QSP
, where

{\overline{f}}=(f_{1},\dots,f_{m})
satisfies
m\leq M
.
By definition,
I
is a positive instance if and only if there exists

({\overline{\delta}},N)
for
I
satisfying (
8
).
Below we describe an enumeration procedure
that finds a pairs
({\overline{\delta}},N)
satisfying (
8
)
for every positive instances
I

in time polynomial in terms of
\size(I)
.

By Lemma
4.17
, we may assume that

|\delta_{i}|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\size(f_{i})\leq\size(I)
.
Since
B
is an abelian group, its growth function is bounded by a
polynomial function
p(n)
.
Hence, the number of tuples
(\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{m})

satisfying
|\delta_{i}|\leq\size(I)

is bounded by
(p(\size(I)))^{M}
, which is a polynomial
in terms of
\size(I)
.
Therefore, we can enumerate all bounded tuples
{\overline{\delta}}=(\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{m})

one by one in polynomial time.

For a bounded sequence
{\overline{\delta}}
define

(14)

c=\sum_{i=1}^{m}f_{c_{i}}^{\delta_{i}}\in A^{B}.

Assume that for
{\overline{\delta}}
there exists a subgroup
N\leq B
such that

({\overline{\delta}},N)
satisfies (
8
), i.e.,

c=_{A^{B/N}}0\mbox{ and }\text{rank}(N)\leq h.

We claim that we can find such subgroup (perhaps not
N
itself)
as follows.
Define a set
S\subseteq B
as

S=S_{c}=\left\{\,p_{i}-p_{j}\;\middle|\;p_{i},p_{j}\in\supp(c)\,\right\}.

Let
S^{\prime}=N\cap S
and let
N^{\prime}={\left\langle S^{\prime}\right\rangle}\leq N
.
By Lemma
4.18
,
c=_{A^{B/N^{\prime}}}0
.
Since
N^{\prime}\leq N
, we have
\text{rank}(N^{\prime})\leq\text{rank}(N)\leq h
.
By construction, every
s\in S^{\prime}
satisfies
\|s\|\leq\size(I)
.
Then by Corollary
2.9
,

N^{\prime}
has a basis
\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{y}\}
satisfying
y\leq h<\text{rank}(B)
and

\|t_{i}\|\leq\sqrt{2^{y-1}}\size(I)\leq 2^{\text{rank}(B)/2}\size(I).

Here
2^{\text{rank}(B)/2}
is a constant – it is a characteristic of the fixed group
B
.
Therefore, the size of the set

T=T_{c}=\left\{\,(t_{1},\ldots,t_{y})\;\middle|\;\|t_{i}\|\leq 2^{\text{rank}(B)/2}\size(I)\,\right\}

is polynomial in terms of
\size(I)

and we can enumerate its elements one-by-one in polynomial time.
One tuple
(t_{1},\ldots,t_{y})\in T
is a generating set for
N^{\prime}
.
We can identify it by checking (
14
),
which can be done in polynomial time.
∎

5.
Orientable equations over
A\wr B

In this section we use the computational properties of

QSP
established in Section
4

to draw conclusions about the
Diophantine problem for orientable quadratic equations (
2
).
We consider different variations of the Diophantine problem:
the uniform problem (in which
A
and
B
are a part of the input)
and the non-uniform problem (in which
A
and
B
are fixed)
together with additional conditions that can be imposed
on a given quadratic equation.
We start with the global complexity upper bound
for the uniform Diophantine problem for orientable quadratic equations.

Corollary 5.1
.

The problem of deciding if a given orientable quadratic equation in a given wreath product
A\wr B
has a solution belongs to
{\mathbf{NP}}
.

Proof.

Proposition
3.2
translates a given equation into an instance of
{\textsf{QSP}}(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)
which, by Theorem
4.19
, is in
{\mathbf{NP}}
.
∎

5.1.
Non-uniform problem

Fix finitely generated abelian groups
A
and
B
. Here we discuss orientable equations over the fixed wreath product
A\wr B
.

Corollary 5.2
.

If
|B|=\infty
, then the Diophantine problem
for
spherical
equations in
A\wr B
is
{\mathbf{NP}}
-hard.

Proof.

Proposition
3.2
establishes a one-to-one correspondence
between spherical equations (
1
) in
A\wr B

and instances of
{\textsf{QSP}}(A,B,\cdot,0)
, which,
by Theorem
4.3
, is
{\mathbf{NP}}
-hard when
|B|=\infty
.
∎

Corollary 5.3
.

The Diophantine problem for orientable quadratic equations over
a wreath product of finitely generated abelian groups
A\wr B
is

•

polynomial-time decidable
\ \ \Leftrightarrow\ \

B
is finite;

•

{\mathbf{NP}}
-complete
\ \ \Leftrightarrow\ \

B
is infinite.

Proof.

By Corollary
5.1
, the problem is in
{\mathbf{NP}}
.
By Corollary
5.2
, the problem is
{\mathbf{NP}}
-hard when
|B|=\infty
.
Finally, Proposition
3.2
reduces
orientable quadratic equations (
2
) in
A\wr B

to instances of
{\textsf{QSP}}(A,B,\cdot,\cdot)
, which,
by Theorem
4.4
, are polynomial-time decidable if
|B|<\infty
.
∎

Corollary
5.3
gives a very limited picture
for orientable equations.
For
{\mathbf{NP}}
-hardness it employs spherical equations only
and neglects other types of orientable equations.
Below we analyze other cases with the goal
to identify the reasons for computational hardness.

5.1.1.
Non-uniform problem: genus
g

The value of the genus
g
of a given equation
{\mathcal{E}}

relative to the rank of the group
B
directly affects the hardness of solving
{\mathcal{E}}
.

Corollary 5.4
.

The Diophantine problem for the class of orientable quadratic
equations (
2
)
with genus satisfying
g\geq\text{rank}(B)/2

is in
{\mathbf{P}}
.

Proof.

Proposition
3.2
reduces
orientable quadratic equations (
2
) in
A\wr B

satisfying
g\geq\text{rank}(B)/2
to instances of
{\textsf{QSP}}(A,B,\cdot,2g)
, which, by
Theorem
4.20
, are decidable in polynomial time.
∎

The bound
g\geq\text{rank}(B)/2
cannot be improved,
i.e., there is a group
B
satisfying
2g<\text{rank}(B)
for which
the problem is
{\mathbf{NP}}
-hard.

Corollary 5.5
.

Fix
g,n\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
2g<n
.
The Diophantine problem for the class of orientable quadratic
equations (
2
)
of genus
g
over the group
{\mathbb{Z}}\wr{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}

is
{\mathbf{NP}}
-complete.

Proof.

Proposition
3.2
reduces
instances of
{\textsf{QSP}}({\mathbb{Z}},{\mathbb{Z}}^{n},\cdot,2g)
, satisfying the condition
2g<n
,
to orientable quadratic equations (
2
) in
{\mathbb{Z}}\wr{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}

of genus
g
. By Theorem
4.22
, the original problem

{\textsf{QSP}}({\mathbb{Z}},{\mathbb{Z}}^{n},\cdot,2g)
is
{\mathbf{NP}}
-complete.
∎

5.1.2.
Non-uniform problem: number of conjugates

The number of conjugates
m
in a given quadratic equation
{\mathcal{E}}
of type (
2
) is also responsible for the hardness of solving
{\mathcal{E}}
. Specifically, equations (
2
) with

Corollary 5.6
.

Fix
M\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
The Diophantine problem for the class of orientable quadratic
equations (
2
)
with the number of conjugates
m
bounded by
M

over
A\wr B
is in
{\mathbf{P}}
.

Proof.

Proposition
3.2
reduces
orientable quadratic equations (

) in

satisfying the condition

to instances of

{\textsf{QSP}}(\cdot,B,\cdot_{|{\overline{f}}|<M},\cdot)
, which, by
Theorem

, are decidable in polynomial time.
∎

5.1.3.
Non-uniform problem: group
A

The choice of the (nontrivial) group
A
is irrelevant
for
{\mathbf{NP}}
-hardness of the Diophantine problem.

Corollary 5.7
.

For any nontrivial finitely generated abelian groups
A_{1},A_{2}
, and
B

the Diophantine problem over
A_{1}\wr B
and
over
A_{2}\wr B
are either both
{\mathbf{NP}}
-hard or are both in

.

Proof.

By Proposition

, the Diophantine problems
over
A_{1}\wr B
and over

are polynomial-time
equivalent to
{\textsf{QSP}}(A_{1},B,\cdot,\cdot)
and
{\textsf{QSP}}(A_{2},B,\cdot,\cdot)

Hence, the statement follows from Corollary

.
∎

5.2.
Uniform problem

By Corollaries
5.1
and
5.2
, the uniform Diophantine problem for orientable equations is
{\mathbf{NP}}
-complete.

Here we make one observation about the contribution of the group
A
to the computational hardness of solving spherical equations.
Even though a particular choice of
A
does not affect the
{\mathbf{NP}}
-hardness of the problem as observed in Section
5.1.3
, we can argue that different choices of
A
make the problem easier or harder.

Corollary 5.8
.

The Diophantine problem for spherical equations
over the class of groups
\{{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}\wr{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}

is
{\mathbf{NP}}
-hard.

Proof.

Follows from Theorem
4.6
.
∎

Another observation is that
Malcev-type equations in wreath products of finitely generated abelian groups can be solved in polynomial time.

Corollary 5.9
.

Fix
g\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
The Diophantine problem for equations of genus
g

\prod_{i=1}^{g}[x_{i},y_{i}]=c

over a given wreath product
A\wr B
is in
{\mathbf{P}}
.

Proof.

Follows from Theorem
4.23
.
∎

6.
Conclusion

Ultimately, the main parameter responsible for the computational
hardness of the Diophantine problem for orientable equations (
2
)
over wreath products
A\wr B
is the number of conjugates
m
.
Imposing a bound on
m
makes the problem computationally
feasible (Corollary
5.6
).
Certain values of the genus
g
, the size of
B
, and
\text{rank}(B)

can make the problem feasible as well
(Corollaries
5.3
and
5.4
).
In the uniform case, when
A
and
B
are a part of the input,
unbounded rank of
A
makes the problem computationally hard
even for small finite
B
(Corollary
5.8
).
