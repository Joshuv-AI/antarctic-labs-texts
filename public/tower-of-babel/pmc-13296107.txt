Perioperative neurofilament light chain and glial fibrillary acidic protein as biomarkers of postoperative delirium: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Yan-Wu Yang, Yan Zhang, Tian-Yi Qu, Jing Yu, Zhi Wan, Mei-Ling Ge
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPostoperative delirium (POD) is a frequent and serious neurocognitive complication reflecting acute brain dysfunction and predicting long-term cognitive decline. Neurofilament light chain (NfL) and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) are biomarkers of neuronal and astroglial injury, but their relevance to POD remains uncertain.MethodsThis systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to determine whether there is an association between perioperative NfL or GFAP levels and POD. PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science were searched until January 2026. Odds ratios (ORs), standardized mean differences (SMDs), and diagnostic performance (AUC) were calculated using random-effects models.ResultsFourteen studies involving 2,604 participants were included, of whom approximately 30% developed POD. In categorical analyses, higher NfL levels were associated with increased POD risk across nine studies (pooled OR = 1.27, 95% CI 1.08–1.50), consistent across plasma (OR = 1.27) and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) (OR = 1.32) samples. Continuous analyses also showed higher NfL concentrations in delirious patients (Plasma SMD = 0.48; CSF SMD = 0.40). GFAP showed a significant but weaker and less consistent association (overall OR = 1.68, 95% CI 1.15–2.43), mainly driven by plasma data, while continuous analyses were nonsignificant. Diagnostic meta-analysis showed fair discriminative performance for NfL (AUC = 0.68) and moderate accuracy for GFAP (AUC = 0.63).ConclusionsThis meta-analysis demonstrates that perioperative elevations of NfL and GFAP are associated with POD. However, their diagnostic performance remains modest, and current evidence does not support their use as stand-alone diagnostic biomarkers. NfL showed a more stable and reproducible relationship, suggesting its potential role as a marker of perioperative neural stress and as a component of future multimodal delirium risk models.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07717-y.Keywords: Postoperative delirium, Neurofilament light chain (NfL), Glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), Biomarkers; Meta-analysis

Introduction

Postoperative delirium (POD), characterized by an acute and fluctuating disturbance of attention, awareness, and cognition, is among the most frequent complications in older surgical patients and typically arises within days after surgery. POD is associated with prolonged hospitalization, increased institutionalization, higher health-care costs and excess short- and long-term mortality [1–3]. Beyond these immediate consequences, converging longitudinal data indicate that POD is linked to accelerated cognitive decline and an increased risk of incident dementia, suggesting that POD may act as a clinical accelerator or unmasking event along the continuum of Alzheimer’s disease and related dementias (ADRD) [1, 4, 5]. Despite its prevalence and impact, the underlying mechanisms of POD and clinically useful tools for risk stratification and early detection remain major unmet needs [2, 6].

In recent years, there has been growing interest in biological markers that might capture the neurobiological processes underlying delirium. A series of observational studies, systematic reviews and meta-analyses have consistently implicated systemic and central nervous system inflammation in the pathogenesis of delirium, with elevated levels of C‑reactive protein, interleukin‑6 and other cytokines associated with higher POD risk [7–9]. Parallel work has highlighted markers of neuronal and glial injury, such as S100B, neuron-specific enolase and amyloid‑β–related measures, suggesting that delirium is accompanied by acute brain injury and may intersect with pathways involved in neurodegeneration [3, 10, 11]. Nevertheless, existing reviews emphasize that single inflammatory markers have limited predictive performance and that combining inflammatory readouts with direct indices of neuroaxonal and glial damage may offer greater clinical and mechanistic value.

Within this context, neurofilament light chain (NfL) and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) have emerged as particularly promising candidates. NfL is a structural protein of myelinated axons released into cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and blood following neuroaxonal injury. Elevated NfL concentrations have been documented across a broad spectrum of neurological conditions, including Alzheimer’s disease and other dementias, traumatic brain injury, stroke and hypoxic–ischemic encephalopathy, where they correlate with disease severity, neuroimaging markers of white matter damage and functional outcome [11–13]. GFAP, an intermediate filament protein predominantly expressed in astrocytes, reflects astroglial activation and injury. Increased GFAP levels have been observed in acute brain insults such as traumatic brain injury, intracerebral hemorrhage and cardiac arrest, as well as in chronic neurodegenerative and inflammatory disorders, and have shown prognostic utility in several of these settings [12–14]. These cross-disease data support NfL and GFAP as sensitive, blood-accessible indicators of neuroaxonal and astroglial pathology, although their value for subtler perioperative neurocognitive outcomes remains less certain.

Emerging evidence suggests that NfL and GFAP may also be informative in the perioperative setting, but their interpretation depends strongly on the timing of measurement. Preoperative elevations may reflect pre-existing neuroaxonal or astroglial pathology, limited neurocognitive reserve, cerebrovascular burden, or subclinical neurodegeneration, thereby identifying patients who are biologically vulnerable to POD. In contrast, postoperative elevations or increases from baseline may more directly reflect acute perioperative neural injury, inflammatory brain stress, or blood–brain barrier disruption. Therefore, distinguishing baseline biomarker status from dynamic postoperative changes is essential when interpreting the relationship between NfL, GFAP, and POD.

Proteomic and candidate-biomarker studies have identified both NfL and GFAP among a small set of repeatedly reported proteins associated with delirium, alongside inflammatory and coagulation-related markers [2, 4, 6]. However, findings across individual POD cohorts are heterogeneous, with variability in patient populations, baseline cognitive status, types of surgery, timing and matrix of sampling (plasma vs. CSF), analytical platforms and delirium assessment tools [2, 4, 7, 10]. As a result, it remains uncertain whether perioperative NfL and GFAP are consistently associated with POD occurrence and severity, whether their associations differ between blood and CSF, and how they compare in terms of potential predictive value.

These uncertainties define an important knowledge gap at the intersection of perioperative medicine, delirium research, and neurodegeneration. Clarifying the roles of NfL and GFAP in POD could not only enhance biomarker-based risk stratification but also elucidate the relative contributions of neuroaxonal and astroglial injury to acute postoperative brain dysfunction. Accordingly, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to evaluate perioperative NfL and GFAP levels in relation to POD, quantify their associations using both categorical and continuous effect measures, and compare findings across biological matrices (plasma versus cerebrospinal fluid). By synthesizing the available evidence, this study aimed to determine whether perioperative NfL and GFAP show reproducible associations with POD and whether they may provide complementary biological information for future biomarker-informed risk stratification, rather than to establish them as stand-alone diagnostic biomarkers.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted and reported in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 guidelines [15, 16]. This review was not prospectively registered. The eligibility criteria, search strategy, outcomes of interest, and statistical analysis plan were defined before full-text screening and data extraction. A completed PRISMA 2020 checklist was provided in Additional file 1: Table S1.

Literature search

A systematic search of published reports was performed in three databases: PubMed, Embase (OvidSP), and Web of Science. Search strategies were adapted to the syntax and indexing of each database and covered the period from database inception to January 2026. The search combined terms related to the exposure and outcomes of interest: (1) “neurofilament light” OR “neurofilament light chain” OR “NfL” OR “glial fibrillary acidic protein” OR “GFAP”; and (2) “delirium” OR “postoperative delirium” OR “perioperative delirium” OR “acute confusional state” OR “encephalopathy”. Search filters were not restricted by surgical type or clinical setting to maximize sensitivity. The full electronic search strategies for each database are provided in Additional file 1: Table S2. In addition, reference lists of all eligible articles and relevant narrative reviews on delirium biomarkers, NfL, and GFAP were hand-searched to identify further studies. Conference abstracts, case reports, editorials, opinion pieces, and reviews were excluded at the screening stage.

Study selection

Studies were eligible for inclusion in this systematic review and meta-analysis if they met all of the following criteria: (1) included adult participants undergoing any type of surgery or in a clearly defined perioperative setting; (2) measured NfL and/or GFAP in blood (plasma or serum) or CSF at a perioperative time point (preoperative baseline and/or postoperative within 7 days); (3) assessed delirium or POD using validated diagnostic or screening tools (e.g., Confusion Assessment Method, CAM-ICU, ICDSC, 3D-CAM, 4AT, or DSM criteria); (4) reported the association between NfL and/or GFAP levels and POD occurrence or severity; and (5) were original research articles published in peer-reviewed journals in English. Studies without a delirium outcome, without perioperative NfL/GFAP measurements, or lacking extractable effect estimates or group-wise biomarker data were excluded.

Data extraction

Study selection was performed by two independent reviewers (YWY and MLG). All titles, abstracts and full texts of potentially eligible articles were retrieved and screened. Discrepancies between the two reviewers were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached; if disagreement persisted, a third reviewer (ZW) was consulted. For each included study, data were extracted using a predesigned standardized form. The following information was recorded: first author’s name, year of publication, country or region, study design, clinical setting and type of surgery, sample size, patient population characteristics (including age and sex distribution, baseline cognitive status where available), timing and biological matrix of NfL and/or GFAP measurement (e.g. preoperative, immediate postoperative, postoperative day 1–3; plasma vs. cerebrospinal fluid), delirium assessment tool and diagnostic criteria, incidence and/or severity of postoperative delirium,, and adjusted effect estimates for the association between biomarker levels and POD with corresponding 95% confidence intervals, including the covariates used in multivariable models with corresponding 95% confidence intervals. For each eligible study, we extracted effect estimates according to biomarker type, biological matrix, sampling time, and analytical scale. For categorical analyses, we recorded whether the comparison was based on highest-versus-lowest categories, study-specific cutoffs, receiver operating characteristic curve-derived thresholds, or other categorical contrasts. For continuous analyses, we extracted group-wise biomarker values or continuous effect estimates and recorded whether biomarkers were analyzed in original units, log-transformed values, per-unit increments, or per-standard deviation increments. When studies reported multiple estimates, we recorded whether estimates were adjusted or unadjusted and extracted the covariates included in multivariable models when available. For studies reporting multiple biomarker time points, we classified assessments as preoperative baseline, postoperative, or serial perioperative measurements when available. This distinction was prespecified because preoperative biomarker levels were interpreted as potential indicators of baseline neurobiological vulnerability, whereas postoperative values or perioperative increases were considered more reflective of acute perioperative neural stress or injury.

Quality and publication bias assessment

Study quality evaluation for potential bias was independently performed by two reviewers (YWY and MLG) using the Quality in Prognostic Studies (QUIPS) [17]. The QUIPS framework comprises six key domains: study participation, study attrition, prognostic factor measurement, outcome measurement, study confounding, statistical analysis and reporting. Each domain was scored as low, moderate, or high risk of bias [18]. Domain-level judgments were retained as the primary risk-of-bias assessment, rather than relying on a numerical scoring rule. Overall judgments were used only as a descriptive summary and were interpreted in conjunction with the domain-level QUIPS results, with particular attention to confounding, biomarker measurement timing, outcome assessment, and statistical reporting.

Statistical analysis

All meta-analyses were performed using R software with the meta and meta for packages. Pooled estimates were derived separately for NfL and GFAP. Effect estimates were harmonized according to analytical scale before pooling. Categorical associations were pooled as odds ratios (ORs) using the contrast reported in the original study, most commonly higher versus lower biomarker levels defined by study-specific thresholds or distribution-based categories. When both adjusted and unadjusted categorical estimates were available, the most fully adjusted estimate was preferentially used; if no adjusted estimate was reported, the available unadjusted estimate was used and this limitation was considered in the interpretation. We did not impose a universal cutoff for NfL or GFAP because studies differed substantially in assay platform, biological matrix, sampling time, population characteristics, and analytical strategy. Continuous comparisons between patients with and without POD were pooled separately as standardized mean differences (SMDs; Hedges’ g), which allowed biomarker concentrations reported in different units or transformed scales to be placed on a common standardized scale. Categorical ORs and continuous SMDs were not combined in the same meta-analysis. For studies reporting multiple biomarker time points, we extracted the estimate corresponding to the analysis most relevant to the prespecified comparison and avoided including more than one effect estimate from the same cohort in a single pooled analysis. Preoperative baseline measurements were prioritized for analyses interpreted as prediction or risk stratification for subsequent POD. Postoperative or serial perioperative measurements were considered separately when reported, but the limited number of studies and heterogeneity in sampling schedules prevented a formal pooled analysis of within-patient biomarker trajectories. Pooled effect sizes were calculated using a random-effects model (restricted maximum likelihood, REML), and the Hartung–Knapp–Sidik–Jonkman adjustment was applied to account for small-sample uncertainty. Between-study heterogeneity was assessed using the Cochran Q test, I² statistic, and τ² variance, with I² values ≥ 50% or Q-test p ≤ 0.10 indicating substantial heterogeneity. Prediction intervals were additionally reported to indicate the expected range of true effects in future studies. Subgroup analyses were performed according to biological sample type (plasma vs. CSF), and between-subgroup heterogeneity was evaluated using the χ² test. A nonsignificant subgroup test was interpreted as an absence of statistical evidence for a subgroup effect rather than proof of equivalence between matrices. Where studies reported receiver operating characteristic curve data, we extracted study-level area-under-the-curve (AUC) estimates according to biomarker type, biological matrix, and timing of measurement. These AUCs were derived from ROC analyses reported in the original studies and were not consistently based on independent validation cohorts. Because thresholds, sampling time, assay platforms, and clinical populations varied across studies, AUC results were summarized as exploratory discriminative performance rather than definitive diagnostic accuracy. We did not perform a formal bivariate diagnostic meta-analysis because the number of contributing studies was small and paired sensitivity/specificity estimates at common thresholds were not consistently available. Publication bias was examined using Egger’s regression test, Begg’s rank correlation test, and visual inspection of funnel plot asymmetry. The trim-and-fill method was applied when asymmetry was detected to estimate adjusted pooled effects [19]. Sensitivity analyses were conducted using a leave-one-out approach to assess whether any single study disproportionately influenced the pooled estimates. All statistical tests were two-sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The literature search across PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science yielded a total of 191 records. After removing 152 duplicates, 39 unique articles were screened by title and abstract. Following preliminary screening, 27 studies were retrieved for full-text review. Of these, 13 were excluded because they involved non-surgical populations, lacked perioperative NfL or GFAP measurements, did not assess POD as an eligible outcome, reported incompatible outcomes, or did not provide extractable biomarker–POD association data. Detailed reasons for exclusion at the full-text stage are provided in Additional file 1: Table S4. Ultimately, 14 studies fulfilled all inclusion criteria and were incorporated into the final meta-analysis [19–32]. The detailed screening process is summarized in Fig. 1 following the PRISMA 2020 guidelines, with comprehensive search strategies provided in Additional File 1: Table S2.

PRISMA flow diagram of study selection

The included studies were published between 2018 and 2025, encompassing a total of 2,604 participants, of whom approximately 30% developed POD (Table 1). Most studies adopted prospective or retrospective cohort designs and were conducted across North America, Europe, and Asia. The surgical populations varied widely, including orthopedic, cardiac, gastrointestinal, and neurosurgical procedures. The mean age across cohorts ranged from 65 to 85 years, with males comprising 20%–90% of participants depending on the surgical population. Both NfL and GFAP were assessed across studies, with NfL reported in 13 cohorts and GFAP in 8. Most included studies measured NfL and/or GFAP at a preoperative baseline time point, whereas a smaller number also included early postoperative measurements, most commonly on postoperative day 1 or day 2. Accordingly, analyses based on preoperative values were interpreted primarily as prediction or risk stratification for subsequent POD, whereas postoperative or serial measurements were interpreted more cautiously as potential indicators of acute perioperative neural stress or concurrent discrimination of POD status. Delirium was diagnosed using validated assessment tools, most frequently the Confusion Assessment Method (CAM) or its variants (3D-CAM, CAM-ICU, CAM-S), while a few studies applied DSM-5 criteria or the Memorial Delirium Assessment Scale (MDAS). Methodological quality was assessed using the QUIPS tool. Among the included studies, seven were rated as having an overall low risk of bias, while one was judged to have a moderate risk of bias. Detailed domain-specific quality assessments were presented in Additional File 1: Table S3.

NfL and postoperative delirium

A total of nine studies were included to evaluate the association between NfL levels and POD. The relationship was examined using both categorical and continuous analyses and stratified by biological sample type (plasma vs. CSF). In the categorical analysis comparing higher versus lower NfL concentrations, pooled results showed that elevated NfL levels were significantly associated with an increased risk of POD (pooled OR = 1.27, 95% CI: 1.08–1.50; p < 0.001; Fig. 2), with substantial heterogeneity (I² = 77.9%). Subgroup analyses by sample type revealed positive associations in both matrices. For plasma NfL, the pooled OR from random-effects modelling was 1.27 (95% CI: 1.02–1.58), again with considerable heterogeneity (I² = 77.7%, τ² = 0.053; p < 0.0001). For CSF NfL, a comparable association was observed (pooled OR = 1.32, 95% CI: 1.02–1.72), but with markedly lower heterogeneity (I² = 18.4%, τ² = 0.017; p = 0.29), indicating more stable CSF-derived estimates. No significant subgroup differences were detected (χ² = 0.06, df = 1; p = 0.81), however, this finding should be interpreted as an absence of statistical evidence for a subgroup effect rather than proof of equivalence between plasma and CSF measurements.

Forest plot of the association between neurofilament light chain (NfL) levels and postoperative delirium risk: categorical analysis

When NfL was analysed as a continuous variable, pooled data confirmed higher concentrations in patients who developed POD. For plasma NfL, the random-effects (restricted maximum likelihood–Hartung–Knapp) meta-analysis yielded a pooled SMD of 0.48 (95% CI: 0.27–0.68; p < 0.01; I² = 0.4%, τ² = 0.0169; Fig. 3A), with a 95% prediction interval (PI) of 0.10–0.85, indicating that future studies are likely to report positive associations of similar magnitude. For CSF NfL, the pooled SMD was 0.40 (95% CI: 0.13–0.66; p < 0.05; I² = 0.4%, τ² = 0.0208; Fig. 3B), with a 95% PI of − 0.10 to 0.89, implying that although individual studies may occasionally yield null findings, the overall trend supports a positive association between higher NfL levels and POD risk. Collectively, these findings support a robust and biologically plausible association between perioperative NfL elevation and postoperative delirium.

GFAP and postoperative delirium

Six studies evaluated the association between GFAP levels and POD. In categorical analyses, higher GFAP concentrations were defined using the original study-specific thresholds or categorical comparisons. Owing to differences in assay platform, sample matrix, timing of measurement, and study population, these thresholds were not directly interchangeable across studies. Higher GFAP concentrations were associated with increased delirium risk under the random-effects model (pooled OR = 1.68, 95% CI: 1.15–2.43; Fig. 4). In subgroup analyses, the association remained significant for plasma GFAP (pooled OR = 1.70, 95% CI: 1.09–2.64), whereas the CSF subgroup demonstrated a similar but statistically nonsignificant trend (pooled OR = 1.72, 95% CI: 0.85–3.49). No significant subgroup difference was detected (χ²₁ = 0.00, p = 0.98), indicating consistent directionality across biological matrices. Considerable heterogeneity was observed across studies (I² > 80%).

Forest plot of the association between glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) levels and postoperative delirium risk: categorical analysis

In continuous-variable analyses, GFAP did not show a statistically significant association with delirium risk in either biological matrix. For plasma GFAP, the pooled SMD was 0.32 (95% CI: −0.11 to 0.74; 95% PI: −0.59 to 1.22; I² = 0.7%, τ² = 0.0821; Fig. 5A), and for CSF GFAP, the pooled SMD was 0.04 (95% CI: −0.70 to 0.78; 95% PI: −1.08 to 1.16; I² = 0.5%, τ² = 0.0396; Fig. 5B). The wide prediction intervals, spanning both negative and positive effects, suggest that future studies are unlikely to yield consistent effect estimates. Therefore, current data do not establish GFAP as a robust independent predictor of POD.

Exploratory discriminative performance of plasma NfL and GFAP for postoperative delirium

We summarized the reported discriminative performance of plasma NfL and GFAP for POD. Four studies comprising 757 participants contributed AUC data for NfL, whereas only two studies with 310 participants contributed AUC data for GFAP. These estimates were derived from study-level ROC analyses reported in the original studies and were not consistently based on independent validation cohorts. The weighted mean AUC, calculated using an inverse-variance model, was 0.683 (95% CI: 0.490–0.752) for NfL and 0.628 (95% CI: 0.490–0.730) for GFAP, indicating only modest discrimination. These AUC estimates were derived from studies that differed in biomarker timing, including preoperative baseline measurements and, in some cohorts, early postoperative or serial perioperative measurements. AUCs based on preoperative measurements should be interpreted as reflecting prediction or risk stratification for subsequent POD, whereas postoperative or serial measurements may be more relevant to concurrent discrimination of POD status or perioperative neural injury. Because cutoff values for ROC analyses were not consistently reported and were often derived within individual cohorts, the pooled AUC estimates should be interpreted as exploratory summaries rather than evidence supporting standardized diagnostic cutoffs or stand-alone clinical use. Although NfL showed a slightly higher discriminative ability (ΔAUC = 0.055), both biomarkers performed better than chance (AUC > 0.50). Forest plots demonstrated study-level estimates with moderate heterogeneity (Figure S1), and summary receiver operating characteristic (sROC) curves are presented in Fig. 6.

Publication bias and sensitivity analyses

Publication bias was assessed using multiple complementary approaches. Egger’s test suggested possible small-study effects (p = 0.0033), whereas Begg’s test (p = 0.1857) and visual funnel plot asymmetry analysis (p = 0.6757) showed no significant evidence. This discordance may reflect the limited number of studies, between-study heterogeneity, and differences in the statistical power and assumptions of each method. Trim-and-fill analysis estimated four potentially missing studies, but the adjusted pooled estimate remained statistically significant. after their imputation, the adjusted effect estimate remained statistically significant. Nevertheless, these findings should not be interpreted as excluding publication bias. Publication bias or small-study effects may still have influenced the magnitude of the pooled association (Figure S2). Leave-one-out sensitivity analyses did not identify a single study that disproportionately determined the overall direction of the pooled association. However, this analysis should be interpreted as evidence against dominance by one study rather than definitive proof of robustness, particularly given the small number of studies in several biomarker- and matrix-specific analyses (Figure S3).

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis synthesized evidence on perioperative neuronal and glial injury biomarkers in relation to POD. By quantitatively summarizing data from fourteen studies encompassing 2,604 participants, we found that higher NfL levels were associated with increased POD risk across plasma and CSF samples, with supportive findings in both categorical and continuous analyses. GFAP showed a weaker and less consistent association: categorical analyses suggested a positive association, but continuous analyses were nonsignificant and imprecise. Although NfL and GFAP showed modest discriminative performance, the available AUC estimates should be interpreted cautiously because they were based on a small number of heterogeneous study-level ROC analyses and were not consistently validated in independent cohorts. Therefore, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence that NfL or GFAP can be used as stand-alone clinical prediction or diagnostic tools. Rather, these biomarkers may provide complementary biological information regarding perioperative neural vulnerability when incorporated into multimodal models with clinical, cognitive, inflammatory, and perioperative risk factors.

The associations identified in this meta-analysis are biologically plausible and align with the view of POD as an acute manifestation of neuroaxonal injury and neuroinflammation. Within this framework, NfL and glial GFAP represent complementary biomarkers of distinct but interconnected processes—axonal damage and astroglial activation. Both proteins are released into CSF and blood in proportion to neuron and astrocyte injury across a wide range of neurological disorders [33–35]. NfL is a cytoskeletal protein highly enriched in large-caliber myelinated axons; disruption of axonal integrity leads to its diffusion into interstitial fluid, CSF, and subsequently blood [33, 35]. Surgical stress, systemic inflammation, and cerebral hypoperfusion can initiate neuroinflammatory cascades that damage axons and impair structural and functional connectivity—hallmarks of the transient cognitive dysfunction observed in delirium. Consistent with this biology, our meta-analysis demonstrated directionally uniform associations between elevated perioperative NfL concentrations and increased risk of POD. These findings are in line with prospective perioperative data: Fong et al. showed that higher preoperative and postoperative plasma NfL predicted incident delirium, greater delirium severity, and worse one-month cognitive outcomes, supporting NfL as a quantitative marker of perioperative neuroaxonal stress and vulnerability [21]. A broader meta-analysis of POD biomarkers also reported a positive association between blood-based NfL and delirium risk, showing a relatively large effect size despite including only two studies and exhibiting considerable heterogeneity [4]. Beyond the surgical setting, NfL elevations correlate with encephalopathy severity and outcomes in sepsis-associated encephalopathy [36], and with delirium/deep sedation days, mortality, and length of stay in critically ill patients [37]. In moderate–severe traumatic brain injury, plasma NfL strongly reflects the burden of diffuse axonal injury on MRI and predicts long-term neurodegeneration and functional outcome [38]. Longitudinal population data also show that higher peripheral NfL predicts future dementia and is associated with worse cognitive performance years before clinical diagnosis [34]. Collectively, this convergent evidence supports the interpretation that NfL indexes diffuse axonal injury and underlying “brain frailty,” providing a biologically grounded link between perioperative stress and delirium vulnerability.

GFAP is a type III intermediate filament protein specific to astrocytes and a canonical marker of astroglial activation. Astrocytes maintain synaptic metabolism, support blood–brain barrier (BBB) integrity, and orchestrate neuroinflammatory responses; upregulation and release of GFAP accompany astrogliosis and BBB disruption after CNS injury [35, 39]. During acute perioperative stress, systemic inflammation and neuronal injury can therefore trigger astrocytic activation and transient GFAP elevation. In our meta-analysis, higher GFAP levels were significantly associated with delirium in categorical comparisons, suggesting that astroglial activation accompanies clinically overt perioperative brain dysfunction. However, associations were less consistent in continuous analyses, which is compatible with prior work showing that GFAP’s diagnostic performance depends on sampling time, assay platform, and the integrity of the BBB [35]. In traumatic brain injury and stroke, GFAP rises rapidly after injury, correlates strongly with lesion volume and imaging abnormalities, and reaches high diagnostic accuracy for detecting intracranial lesions and guiding acute triage decisions [22, 38]. In contrast, perioperative delirium and sepsis-related encephalopathy often involve more diffuse, fluctuant insults, in which GFAP elevations may be more variable and tightly linked to specific temporal windows and BBB stress [4, 35, 36].

Previous systematic reviews and meta-analyses of POD and ICU delirium have primarily focused on inflammatory mediators. IL-6 and C-reactive protein (CRP) show robust and consistent associations with delirium occurrence across diverse surgical cohorts, underscoring a central role for systemic and neuroinflammation in delirium pathophysiology [4]. Structural neuronal biomarkers such as S100B and NfL have also been linked to POD, though they were examined in fewer and more heterogeneous studies [4, 11]. Similarly, a meta-analysis on postoperative cognitive dysfunction identified S100B, NSE, and Aβ as markers of postoperative neuronal injury and cognitive decline, with time-dependent effects [11]. Tau and amyloid-β (Aβ) have been more extensively explored in the contexts of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and postoperative cognitive dysfunction. Meta-analytic data from orthopedic surgery populations indicate that lower preoperative CSF Aβ₄₂ and higher tau species are risk factors for POD, suggesting that pre-existing AD-type pathology confers vulnerability to delirium rather than representing the acute delirium state itself [40]. Longitudinal studies further suggest that tau and Aβ primarily capture chronic neurodegenerative susceptibility, with relatively modest perioperative changes compared to NfL and GFAP [33–35]. Collectively, these findings highlight that tau and Aβ reflect long-term neuronal integrity, whereas NfL and GFAP act as more sensitive indicators of acute perioperative neural stress and injury.

Across traumatic brain injury, stroke, critical illness, and neurodegeneration, individual biomarkers such as S100B, GFAP, NSE, UCH-L1, NfL, tau, and Aβ have often been examined in isolation, limiting integrated understanding of their cellular origins and relevance to acute brain dysfunction [22, 33, 35]. By jointly synthesizing evidence on both neuronal (NfL) and glial (GFAP) markers in the perioperative context, the present meta-analysis helps address this gap and supports a dual-pathway model of POD pathophysiology in which axonal and glial processes act in concert. NfL likely reflects diffuse axonal injury and neuronal disconnection, whereas GFAP indexes secondary astroglial activation, BBB perturbation, and inflammatory reactivity following neuronal stress [33–36, 39]. This integrated perspective highlights the complementary contributions of neuronal and glial injury to the complex biology of postoperative delirium.

From a translational perspective, the current evidence supports only an adjunctive and hypothesis-generating role for NfL and GFAP. These biomarkers should not currently be used to guide anesthetic choice, surgical planning, or perioperative decision-making. Preoperative NfL or GFAP measurements may be most appropriately conceptualized as candidate risk-stratification markers that identify patients with reduced neurocognitive reserve or subclinical neurodegeneration before surgery. In contrast, postoperative or serial perioperative measurements may help capture acute neural stress or injury, but their value for diagnosing or monitoring POD remains uncertain and cannot replace standardized clinical delirium assessments. At present, no standardized clinical cutoff for perioperative NfL or GFAP can be recommended for POD prediction or diagnosis. Reported thresholds were study-specific and likely influenced by assay platform, biological matrix, sampling time, age, comorbidity burden, and surgical population. The potential value of these biomarkers may lie in complementing established clinical predictors, such as age, baseline cognition, frailty, comorbidities, surgery type, anesthesia exposure, inflammatory markers, and neuroimaging features, within future multimodal risk models. Future prospective studies should determine whether adding NfL or GFAP to validated clinical models improves discrimination, calibration, reclassification, and clinical decision-making. Only after such validation could biomarker-informed preventive strategies be considered. Although NfL has been associated with cognitive decline and neurodegenerative outcomes in other clinical contexts, the present meta-analysis did not evaluate long-term postoperative cognitive trajectories. Therefore, whether NfL links POD to subsequent cognitive decline remains a hypothesis that should be tested in longitudinal studies with standardized cognitive follow-up.

Strengths and limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged when interpreting this meta-analysis. First, the included studies were heterogeneous in terms of patient populations, surgical procedures, anesthetic regimens, delirium assessment tools, biomarker assay platforms, and sampling schedules. Although random-effects modeling and sensitivity analyses were applied, residual heterogeneity cannot be excluded, which may have influenced biomarker effect estimates and limits generalizability to specific surgical contexts. Second, the available studies varied substantially in the timing of biomarker assessment. Because many studies relied on preoperative baseline measurements, elevated NfL or GFAP may reflect pre-existing neural vulnerability rather than acute perioperative injury. Conversely, postoperative measurements or within-patient changes from baseline may be more suitable for detecting acute neuronal or astroglial injury. The limited number of studies with serial perioperative sampling prevented a formal analysis of within-patient biomarker trajectories; therefore, this meta-analysis cannot determine whether postoperative increases in NfL or GFAP improve early detection, monitoring, or diagnosis of POD beyond preoperative baseline measurements. Third, effect estimates were reported using heterogeneous analytical scales, including adjusted and unadjusted categorical contrasts, study-specific thresholds, continuous biomarker values, and standardized comparisons. Although we pooled categorical ORs and continuous SMDs separately and prioritized adjusted estimates when available, residual differences in assay platform, sampling time, cutoff definition, and covariate adjustment may have affected comparability across studies. Fourth, risk-of-bias concerns should be considered. Domain-level bias was present in some studies, particularly with respect to confounding, attrition, prognostic factor measurement, outcome assessment, and statistical reporting. Residual confounding is likely because baseline cognitive impairment, frailty, cerebrovascular disease, comorbidities, surgery type, anesthetic exposure, perioperative inflammation, and illness severity were not uniformly measured or adjusted for across studies. Fifth, the exploratory AUC analysis should be interpreted with caution. The number of contributing studies was small, particularly for GFAP, and the reported AUCs were generally study-level estimates rather than results from independent validation cohorts. Moreover, thresholds, sampling time, assay platforms, and patient populations differed substantially across studies. These factors limit the interpretability of pooled AUC estimates and preclude conclusions regarding stand-alone diagnostic or predictive use in clinical practice. Finally, potential publication bias and small-study effects cannot be excluded. Although trim-and-fill analysis did not change the overall direction of the findings, the significant Egger’s test suggests that asymmetry or small-study effects may have affected the magnitude of the pooled estimates. Because this work is based on observational evidence without mechanistic or neuropathological validation, conclusions should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

Conclusion

In summary, this meta-analysis provides integrated clinical evidence that higher NfL levels, and to a lesser and less consistent extent GFAP levels, are associated with POD. NfL demonstrated a more stable and reproducible relationship across analytical approaches, supporting its role as a candidate marker of perioperative neural vulnerability. GFAP showed a weaker yet directionally consistent association, suggesting that astroglial activation may accompany but not independently predict delirium risk. Together, these findings suggest that the NfL and GFAP are promising candidate biomarkers for further investigation, but current evidence does not support their use as stand-alone tools for POD prediction, diagnosis, or perioperative management. Large prospective studies with standardized sampling schedules, prespecified cutoffs, serial biomarker measurements, longitudinal cognitive follow-up, and independently validated multimodal prediction models are needed.
