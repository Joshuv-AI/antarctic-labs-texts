Associations of maternal uncertainty stress and social capital with adolescents’ smartphone use behavior: a cross-sectional study from rural China
Authors: Xinxin Ying, Yanqi Li, Ruyu Sun, Huan Zhou, Weifang Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSmartphone use behavior among adolescents is a growing public health concern. Maternal uncertainty stress, defined as psychological stress arising from perceived unpredictability and uncontrollability in daily life and future circumstances, may contribute to these behaviors but remains understudied. While social capital can protect against adolescent risks, its role in maternal stress effects, particularly in rural areas, is unclear. This study examines the associations between maternal uncertainty stress, three dimensions of social capital, and adolescents’ smartphone use behaviors in rural China.MethodsA total of 1,215 adolescents (Mage = 13.38 ± 0.53 years, 52.4% males) and their mothers (Mage = 40.47 ± 4.80 years) were enrolled from five rural middle schools 7th grade in Zhejiang Province, China (2023). Data were collected through validated self-reported questionnaires. Multiple logistic regression models and structural equation modeling were used to explore the relationships between maternal uncertainty stress, social capital dimensions (social network, social participation, and cognitive social capital), and two domains of adolescents’ smartphone use behavior: longer-duration use and non-study-related content engagement.ResultsMaternal uncertainty stress was significantly associated with adolescents’ smartphone use behaviors, including longer-duration use and non-study-relate content engagement. Social capital, particularly cognitive social capital, played a bidirectional role as both a correlate of adolescent smartphone behaviors and a protective factor to mitigate the impact of maternal uncertainty stress.ConclusionsMaternal uncertainty stress and social capital are important factors influencing adolescents’ smartphone use behaviors in rural China. Intervention programs aimed at reducing adolescent longer-duration and non-study use smartphone use should prioritize strengthening maternal social capital and providing effective coping strategies to manage uncertainty stress.Trial registrationNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27508-9.

Background

With smartphones playing a central role in young people’s daily lives [1], adolescent smartphone use has become an increasingly prominent issue worldwide, attracting growing attention from international policy and health organizations, including the World Health Organization (WHO) and Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD), because of its potential implications for child and adolescent well-being [2]. This issue is particularly salient in China, where recent data indicate that 62.5% of underage internet users engage in online gaming, and 56.4% of underage internet users specifically playing mobile games [3]. The issue is even more obvious in rural regions: according to the China Internet Usage Report on Minors (2022), 94.9% of rural minors accessed the internet via smartphones in 2021, compared with 89.1% in urban areas [4]. While smartphones offer numerous benefits, including facilitating communication, expanding access to information, and offering entertainment [2, 5], longer-duration or non-study use has been consistently associated with a range of adverse outcomes, including academic underachievement, sleep disturbances, mental health problems, and reduced physical activity [5, 6].

Research on factors influencing adolescents’ behaviors has gradually moved beyond individual-level determinants to consider the role of the family environment [7]. Drawing on intergenerational transmission theory, parental psychological states, behaviors, and social contexts are transmitted to adolescents through daily interactions, emotional climates, and parenting practices, thereby shaping adolescents’ behavioral patterns and health-related habits. Evidence from other national contexts had already highlighted the pivotal role of family environments in shaping adolescents’ digital behaviors. For example, a longitudinal study using the Growing Up in Ireland ’98 Cohort found that parental influences, particularly how parents respond to misbehaviour, significantly predicted adolescents’ risk of excessive Internet use in later adolescence, while shared offline activities such as playing games or sports with parents served as protective factors [8]. In addition, recent research on early ICT exposure suggests that home access and use of information and communication technologies were more strongly associated with later perceived digital competence than school-based exposure, underscoring the importance of the family context in adolescents’ digital skill development [9]. As primary caregivers in most families, mothers play a particularly critical role in this intergenerational process [10].

Uncertainty stress is defined as a form of psychological distress arising from an individual’s perceived inability to predict, control, or make sense of important aspects of life, especially regarding future circumstances [11]. Unlike general life stress, which is often associated with identifiable and time-limited stressors, uncertainty stress is characterized by chronic ambiguity and uncontrollability, and has been shown to exert unique effects on autonomic regulation and coping processes [12]. A growing body of evidence suggests that uncertainty stress is more strongly associated with maladaptive coping strategies than general stress. For example, studies among university students have shown that higher levels of uncertainty stress are associated with increased risks of Internet addiction [13], suicidal ideation [14], and deliberate self-harm [15], with some effects exceeding those of general life stress [14, 15]. Although existing empirical evidence on uncertainty stress has primarily focused on student populations, these findings highlight the behavioral relevance of uncertainty stress across the life course. Within the family context, maternal uncertainty stress may disrupt emotional regulation, parenting practices, and family interactions, thereby influencing adolescents’ daily behaviors, including smartphone use. This mechanism may be particularly salient in rural settings, where families are more frequently exposed to structural uncertainties, such as economic instability, limited access to health and educational resources, and weaker formal support systems. These contextual stressors may heighten mothers’ perceived uncertainty and amplify its potential spillover effects on adolescents’ daily behaviors.

In contrast, social capital is commonly defined as the supportive resources embedded within an individual’s social network and the capacity to access and mobilize these resources [16]. Conceptually, social capital is typically divided into two fundamental dimensions: cognitive social capital, which reflects perceived trust, reciprocity, and shared values, and structural social capital, which refers to the scope and intensity of social ties, including social participation and social networking [17]. Existing research has increasingly recognized social capital as a protective factor that mitigates psychological stress and promotes healthier behaviors [14, 15].

Social capital has been consistently associated with greater resilience and improved health outcomes across multiple levels. At the community level, it is widely recognized as a social determinant of obesity and mental disorders, as well as a protective factor for physical health [18–21], with these associations validated across diverse populations [22, 23]. For example, studies among Ukrainian refugees have shown that social capital alleviates psychological distress, underscoring its relevance for mental health interventions in vulnerable populations [24]. Importantly, these protective effects also extend to the family context. At the family level, maternal social capital has been shown not only to support maternal mental well-being across different settings [25], but also to shape adolescents’ developmental outcomes, including adolescents’ educational trajectories, later-life developmental outcomes [26] and overall health [27].

Beyond its direct associations with health outcomes, social capital may influence adolescent behaviors through its effects on psychological stress. Existing studies indicate that social capital promotes healthier dietary behaviors among young adults [18] and reduces self-harm and suicidal behaviors among university students [14, 15], primarily by alleviating psychological distress. By providing access to information, emotional support, and tangible assistance, social capital can buffer stress exposure, enhance coping capacities, and reduce engagement in maladaptive behaviors.

Despite these advances, key gaps remain regarding the intergenerational implications of uncertainty stress and the stress-buffering role of social capital in adolescents’ smartphone use, particularly in rural contexts. To address these gaps, this study integrates uncertainty stress and social capital within an intergenerational transmission framework to examine their joint associations with adolescents’ smartphone use in rural China. Guided by this theoretical perspective, maternal uncertainty stress is conceptualized as a potential psychological pathway linking maternal social capital to adolescents’ smartphone behaviors. We hypothesized that higher maternal uncertainty stress would be associated with poorer adolescent smartphone use behaviors, whereas higher levels of maternal social capital would be associated with healthier smartphone use behaviors, both directly and indirectly through reduced maternal uncertainty stress. By focusing on rural family contexts, this study aims to provide evidence relevant to public health authorities, child and adolescent affairs departments, and community-based family intervention initiatives, highlighting modifiable family-level targets for preventing longer-duration and non-study use smartphone use among adolescents. Methods

Participants and procedures

A cross-sectional, multi-stage stratified sampling design was employed in Zhejiang Province, China, from March to June 2023 to recruit junior high school students and their mothers. Two cities—Ningbo (coastal) and Jinhua (inland)—were purposively selected to capture geographic and socioeconomic diversity. One county-level administrative unit was then randomly selected from each city (Yuyao District in Ningbo and Wuyi County in Jinhua). Five rural junior high schools were recruited in cooperation with local education authorities, and all eighth-grade students in the selected schools were invited to participate.

The study was introduced to school administrators, teachers, students, and parents by trained research staff. Participation was voluntary, with no financial or material incentives provided. Written informed consent was obtained from mothers, and assent was obtained from adolescents prior to data collection.

Data were collected using structured, paper-based self-administered questionnaires. Adolescents completed the questionnaire at school under supervision, while mothers completed a separate questionnaire independently at home. Questionnaires were linked using unique identification codes to ensure accurate matching while preserving anonymity. After excluding incomplete, inconsistent, or unmatched questionnaires, 1,215 valid adolescent–mother pairs were retained from 1,321 invited pairs, yielding a response rate of 92.3%.

Measures

The participants were questioned about their smartphone use behaviors, demographic background, and for the mothers’ sample, their uncertainty stress and social capital were also assessed.

Dependent variable

Adolescent smartphone use behavior is measured using two variables: duration and content. To measure adolescent smartphone usage duration, this study employed a self-designed, single-item assessment question. Previous research has validated the reliability of using single-item measures for assessing phone usage duration [28]. Participants self-reported their total daily smartphone use by selecting from five options: less than 1 h, 1–2 h, 2–3 h, 3–4 h, or 4 h and above. In line with the recommendations from the “Healthy China Initiative (2019–2030)” [15], and existing evidence indicating a dose–response relationship between screen time and health outcomes [29, 30], we used a conservative 1-hour cutoff to distinguish lower from relatively longer daily smartphone use, rather than to define a clinical high-risk threshold. Adolescents were categorized into two groups based on their daily smartphone usage duration: “normal” (less than 1 h per day) and “longer-duration use” (1 h or more per day). Smartphone usage content was assessed by asking participants about their primary activities on their phones, which covered six categories: studying, gaming, watching live videos, watching movies or TV shows, social networking, and other activities [31]. Following classification methods from previous research [32], smartphone usage content was grouped into two categories: “study-related” and “non-study-related” use.

Independent variables

Maternal uncertainty stress

Uncertainty stress was measured using the Chinese version of the Uncertainty Stress Scale (USS), developed by Yang et al. [33]. This scale consists of 4 items, rated on a 5-point Likert scale. They respectively represent: “life and fate”, “values”, “changes”, and “means of achieving goals” [34]. The total score was used, with higher scores indicating higher levels of maternal uncertainty stress. Consistent with prior practice, severe stress was signified by a score above 12. The Cronbach’s alpha for this scale in this study was 0.92. The distribution of maternal uncertainty stress scores and the cutoff used to define high uncertainty stress are presented in Supplementary Figure S1(A).

Maternal social capital

Maternal social capital status was assessed at the individual level by the Social Capital Questionnaire, which has acceptable reliability and validity [14, 15]. There are three domains with a total of 12 items, rated on a 5-point Likert scale. The aspects pertained to cognitive social capital based on responses to four questions (e.g. “You believe that most people around you will help you when needed”), four questions on social participation (e.g. “How many times did you participate in leisure or entertainment activities organized by your unit/community/village committee during the past year?”), and four questions addressing social network (assessing number of good friends, trusted classmates/colleagues, helpful neighbors, close relatives, and cooperative partners). A total social capital score was derived by summing item scores. The higher the score, the greater the social capital. Scores above 12 indicated higher levels in each subdomain: social participation, social trust, and cognitive social capital [15]. The Cronbach’s alpha for this scale in this study was 0.78. The alpha coefficients for the three dimensions were 0.67 for cognitive social capital, 0.76 for social participation, and 0.82 for social network. The distribution of social capital scores and the cutoff used to define high social capital are presented in Supplementary Figure S1(B-D).

Covariates

Sociodemographic characteristics assessed in the survey included age, gender, race, only-child status, parental education, parental occupation, parental marital status, maternal phone usage duration, maternal smartphone usage content, and perceived family economic status. The measurement for maternal phone usage duration followed the same method as that used for the adolescents. Maternal Smartphone usage content was categorized into “non-work-related” and “work-related” activities. These factors have been considered in previous studies as important determinants of adolescent smartphone behavior [35]. Multicollinearity among covariates was assessed using variance inflation factors (all VIFs < 2.5), indicating that multicollinearity is not a concern.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analyses were employed to summarized participant characteristics. Chi-square tests and Fisher’s exact tests were conducted to examine associations between maternal uncertainty stress, maternal social capital dimensions and adolescent smartphone use behaviors. Multiple logistic regression models were used to identify the independent influencing factors of adolescent smartphone use behaviors. Structural equation models were employed to explore the mechanisms of how maternal social capital and maternal uncertainty stress impact adolescent smartphone use behaviors. Data were analyzed using SPSS 27 and Mplus 7.4, with significance set at p < 0.05. Structural equation models with binary outcomes were estimated in Mplus using the WLSMV estimator with a probit link, and model fit was assessed via χ²/df, RMSEA, GFI, NFI, and SRMR. ORs from logistic regression were interpreted as < 1.44 = very small, 1.44–2.47 = small, 2.48–4.26 = medium, ≥ 4.27 = large [36]. Standardized SEM effects were interpreted using Cohen’s criteria: 0.10–0.29 = weak, 0.30–0.49 = moderate, ≥ 0.50 = strong [37].

Mean scores for maternal measures were as follows: uncertainty stress (8.91 ± 3.86), cognitive social capital (13.21 ± 2.58), social participation (9.55 ± 3.52), and social network (12.64 ± 3.78). Regarding adolescent smartphone use, 348 participants (28.7%) reported longer-duration use, while 766 (63.0%) used their phones primarily for non-study purposes.

The majority of participants were Han ethnicity (96.5%) and non-only children (68.6%). Nearly all mothers were married (94.7%). Educational attainment varied, with 37.4% of fathers and 34.7% of mothers having completed junior high school as their highest level, while 13.8% of fathers and 16.1% of mothers held university degrees or higher. Most families (55.1%) reported middle-income status. Among mothers, 263 (21.6%) reported daily smartphone use of four hours or more, and 666 (54.8%) used their phones primarily for non-work-related activities.

Table 1 shows that only-child status (χ2 = 5.03, p < 0.05), maternal smartphone usage content (χ2 = 3.23, p < 0.05), paternal educational level (χ2 = 20.63, p < 0.001), paternal occupation(χ2 = 9.37, p < 0.01), maternal educational level (χ2 = 18.71, p < 0.001), maternal occupation (χ2 = 5.91, p < 0.01) were significantly associated with adolescent smartphone usage duration, while smartphone usage content was significantly associated with maternal smartphone usage duration (χ2 = 10.44, p < 0.05), maternal smartphone usage content (χ2 = 14.17, p < 0.001), paternal educational level (χ2 = 26.70, p < 0.001), paternal occupation(χ2 = 7.96, p < 0.01), maternal educational level (χ2 = 22.46, p < 0.001), maternal occupation (χ2 = 17.71, p < 0.01).

Both adolescent smartphone usage duration and content were positively correlated with maternal uncertainty stress (χ²=10.01, p < 0.01; χ²=18.20, p < 0.001, respectively), indicating that higher maternal stress levels were associated with longer-duration use and increased non-study-related content consumption. Conversely, adolescents whose mothers had higher cognitive social capital (χ²=59.35, p < 0.001; χ²=18.58, p < 0.001), greater social participation (χ²=12.03, p < 0.001; χ²=11.38, p < 0.001), and larger social networks (χ²=7.20, p < 0.01; χ²=10.58, p < 0.001) demonstrated shorter smartphone usage duration and lower engagement in non-study-related content.

Table 2 presents the analysis results concerning the relationships among maternal uncertainty stress, maternal social capital and adolescent smartphone use behaviors. After adjusting for covariates, adolescents with mothers experiencing high uncertainty stress were more likely to engage in longer-duration use (OR = 1.56, 95%CI: 1.09 to 2.24, p < 0.05, small effect) and more likely to use their phones for non-study-related content (OR = 1.94, 95%CI: 1.30 to 2.89, p < 0.0001, small effect) compared to those with mothers experiencing lower stress. Adolescents whose mothers had lower cognitive social capital (OR = 2.46, 95%CI: 1.87 to 3.24, p < 0.001,medium effect ), less social participation (OR = 1.66, 95%CI: 1.23 to 2.23, p < 0.01, small effect), and smaller social networks (OR = 1.92, 95%CI: 1.33 to 2.77, p < 0.01, small effect) were more likely to engage in longer-duration use. Additionally, adolescents whose mothers had lower maternal cognitive social capital (OR = 1.50, 95%CI: 1.14 to 1.98, p < 0.01, small effect), less social participation (OR = 1.21, 95%CI: 1.02 to 1.69, p < 0.05, small effect), and smaller networks (OR = 1.39, 95%CI: 1.08 to 1.80, p < 0.05, small effect) were more likely to use their phones for non-study-related content.

Discussion

This study expands the understanding of adolescent smartphone use and its influencing factors. Our cross-sectional survey found that 28% of adolescents engaged in prolonged smartphone use, surpassing the 22.3% reported in a large-scale study of Chinese adolescents [38]. Additionally, 63% used smartphones for non-study-related content. Our findings indicate that a higher proportion of rural adolescents engage in longer-duration smartphone use. This pattern may reflect limited access to alternative recreational activities, fewer structured after-school programs, and greater reliance on digital devices for social interaction and entertainment in rural contexts. These results highlight the importance of tailoring interventions and digital literacy programs to the rural environment.

Affirming our hypotheses, we found that higher uncertainty stress was a prominent risk factor for longer-duration and non-study use smartphone behaviors. Perceived uncertainty stress reflects a lack of control and predictability in life circumstances, which may affect parenting styles, emotional regulation, and family dynamics [39]. Parents experiencing high uncertainty stress may struggle with consistent parenting, leading to weaker behavioral regulation in adolescents. Previous studies have shown that uncontrollable life events are closely linked to maladaptive coping strategies, including poor mental health and maladaptive coping behaviors [40]. Maternal uncertainty stress is a significant psychological and social concern, adolescents in such environments may rely on smartphones to escape stress, seek social validation, or regulate emotions, further reinforcing longer-duration and non-study use usage patterns [41]. Our findings are consistent with previous evidence, showing that maternal uncertainty stress strongly predicts adolescent smartphone overuse, independent of family structure, parental smartphone habits, and socioeconomic background.

In affirming our original hypothesis, this study also found that maternal social capital plays a crucial role in both adolescent smartphone usage duration and content. Specifically, adolescents whose mothers exhibit higher levels of cognitive social capital tend to have more balanced smartphone usage patterns, engaging less in non-study-related content and spending less overall time on their devices. These findings align with prior research demonstrating that cognitive social capital serves as a protective factor in behavioral regulation, fostering greater psychological resilience and healthier lifestyle choices [17]. Maternal trust-based social capital may contribute to more effective parental guidance and digital supervision, thereby reducing longer-duration use and promoting more educational engagement. On the other hand, maternal social networks and social participation were positively associated with healthier patterns of smartphone use among adolescents. This suggests that mothers embedded in broader social circles may have access to more diverse parenting resources and strategies, indirectly shaping how adolescents engage with digital content.

We further found that social capital may influence adolescent smartphone behaviors through maternal uncertainty stress. Although the observed associations are modest, they are potentially meaningful, highlighting the role of maternal social resources in influencing adolescent digital behaviors in rural contexts. Specifically, mothers who frequently participate in social activities tend to have broader social networks and a stronger sense of trust, helpfulness, and organizational warmth, which reduce maternal uncertainty stress. In turn, this reduced uncertainty stress contributes to healthier patterns of adolescent smartphone use. Moreover, social participation and social networks primarily exert their effects by enhancing cognitive social capital, which further plays a crucial role in regulating adolescent smartphone use. In line with previous findings on social capital and mental health [18–21, 42], higher maternal cognitive social capital enables better stress management. Mothers with strong social networks can access informational and emotional support, helping them navigate uncertainty and maintain stable family environments. Moreover, we found key sociodemographic factors influencing adolescent smartphone behaviors included only-child status, maternal smartphone usage content, and family economic status. These disparities may affect parental guidance, digital literacy, and access to alternative activities, with adolescents from lower-income families engaging more in non-study smartphone use due to limited extracurricular opportunities and parental supervision [2, 43]. In addition to the role of family economic status, only-child status and maternal smartphone content were associated with smartphone usage duration, whereas maternal smartphone duration and content significantly influenced smartphone usage content. These findings align with those research indicating that family structure and parental digital habits shape adolescent screen time [8, 9, 43]. Intervention strategies should consider family structure, parental smartphone habits, and socioeconomic background to effectively promote healthier smartphone behaviors among adolescents, particularly in rural areas.

By highlighting the interconnected effects of maternal psychological stress and social resources, our findings extend existing literature and underscore the importance of family-centered intervention strategies aimed at reducing uncertainty stress and strengthening social capital.

From a theoretical perspective, these findings are consistent with intergenerational transmission theory, indicating that maternal uncertainty stress may represent a key pathway through which broader social and economic uncertainty is reflected in adolescents’ smartphone use behaviors. At the policy level, the results align with international public health frameworks advocated by WHO, UNICEF, and the OECD, which emphasize family-centered and preventive approaches to adolescent digital health. Rather than focusing exclusively on adolescents’ self-regulation, our findings suggest that reducing parental psychological stress and strengthening parental social capital may serve as important upstream targets for intervention, particularly in rural settings with limited family and community resources. Regarding generalizability, these associations may extend to other low- and middle-income contexts characterized by economic uncertainty and rapid digitalization. However, caution is warranted when extrapolating to urban or high-income settings, where parental digital literacy, community infrastructure, and institutional support differ substantially. The rural Chinese context may amplify the influence of maternal uncertainty stress and social capital on adolescent smartphone use.

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences, and the observed associations may reflect reverse or reciprocal pathways. For example, heavier adolescent smartphone use could contribute to higher maternal uncertainty stress. Future research should prioritize longitudinal approaches to clarify causal pathways and investigate potential mediators, such as social comparison tendencies or coping strategies. Second, all measures of adolescent smartphone use and maternal behaviors were self-reported, which may be subject to recall bias and social desirability, potentially influencing the observed associations. Third, although our analyses controlled for socioeconomic status, maternal smartphone usage, and only-child status, we did not include a comprehensive measure of adolescent psychological distress. Incorporating such variables could provide deeper insights into the indirect effects of maternal uncertainty stress on adolescent smartphone behaviors, particularly in relation to stress-coping mechanisms. Fourth, social capital is a multifaceted construct with influences at the individual, family, community, and societal levels. Our study focused on maternal cognitive social capital, social networks, and social participation, without considering broader community-level social capital that may also shape adolescent behaviors. Finally, data were clustered by school, but with only five schools, multilevel modeling would yield unstable estimates for school-level effects. Therefore, analyses were conducted at the individual level, and standard errors may be slightly underestimated. At the same time, We also acknowledge alternative explanations. Because adolescents self-reported both smartphone use duration and content, the observed associations may partly reflect shared method variance rather than true behavioral relationships. Other potential influences include social desirability in reporting study-related smartphone use and broader contextual factors such as community resources, school climate, migration status, left-behind children, and household adversity. These factors highlight the need for future studies to incorporate multi-informant and objective measures, as well as to consider broader social and environmental contexts when examining the links between maternal uncertainty stress, social capital, and adolescent smartphone behaviors.

Conclusions

Maternal uncertainty stress and social capital, especially the cognitive social capital, are determinants of adolescent smartphone behaviors. Adolescents whose mothers experience high uncertainty stress and have limited social capital face an increased risk of longer-duration use and engagement in non-study-related content. Intervention programs aimed at promoting healthier smartphone habits among adolescents should focus on reducing maternal uncertainty stress and strengthening maternal social capital.
