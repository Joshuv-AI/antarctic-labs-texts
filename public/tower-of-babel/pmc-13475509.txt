Methodological Caveats in Evaluating Perioperative Esketamine for Postoperative Pain Sensitivity: A Comment on the Study by Chen et al [Letter]
Authors: Shan Zhao, Jinglin Li
Published: 2026

Dear editor

I read with great interest the randomised clinical trial by Chen et al, entitled “Effect of Subanesthetic Doses of Esketamine on Early Postoperative Pain Sensitivity in Patients Undergoing Salpingectomy: A Randomized Clinical Trial”.1 The authors are commended for addressing an understudied population and for employing a rigorous double‑blind design. However, several methodological and interpretative issues warrant careful consideration, as they may affect the validity and generalisability of the conclusions.

Non‑equivalent Postoperative Opioid Regimens Confound the Causal Attribution of the Observed Effects

The study design incorporated two distinct postoperative patient‑controlled intravenous analgesia (PCIA) regimens: the esketamine group received a solution containing esketamine 1 mg/kg plus sufentanil 1 μg/kg, whereas the control group received sufentanil 2 μg/kg without esketamine. Thus, the two groups differed not only by the presence or absence of esketamine but also by a two‑fold difference in sufentanil dosage. While the authors justify this design by stating that the higher sufentanil dose in the control group was intended to “partially compensate for the anticipated analgesic contribution of esketamine”, this rationale introduces a fundamental logical circularity. The very magnitude of esketamine’s analgesic contribution is the effect being tested; to preemptively adjust the opioid dose in the comparator arm assumes the magnitude of the effect under investigation. Consequently, the study’s design can only support a conclusion regarding the superiority of a combination strategy (esketamine + lower‑dose sufentanil) over a high‑dose opioid strategy, but it cannot isolate the independent analgesic effect of esketamine itself, net of differential opioid exposure.2,3

This concern is particularly relevant given that opioid‑induced hyperalgesia (OIH) is a well‑established phenomenon whereby higher intraoperative or postoperative opioid doses can paradoxically increase postoperative pain sensitivity.4 The control group, receiving twice the sufentanil dose, may have experienced greater OIH, thereby inflating their PSQ scores and creating a spurious impression of esketamine efficacy. Conversely, the esketamine group’s lower sufentanil exposure may have reduced OIH‑related pain sensitisation, independent of any direct esketamine effect. Previous meta‑analyses have emphasised that the opioid‑sparing benefits of esketamine can only be properly evaluated when opioid regimens are standardised or when opioid consumption is explicitly treated as a co‑primary endpoint.5 In the present study, the absence of an equivalent opioid background precludes a clear causal interpretation.

The Use of the Pain Sensitivity Questionnaire as a Repeated‑Measure Outcome in the Acute Postoperative Period Raises Methodological Concerns

The Pain Sensitivity Questionnaire (PSQ) was originally developed and validated as a trait‑based measure of an individual’s inherent pain sensitivity, assessed by rating the expected pain intensity of hypothetical daily‑life scenarios.6 Its test‑retest reliability (ICC = 0.635) is only moderate, and the scale was not designed to capture state‑dependent fluctuations over short time intervals. Yet, in this study, the PSQ was administered repeatedly on postoperative days 1, 2, and 7—a period during which patients’ recent pain experiences, emotional states, and recovery trajectories are highly dynamic.

The authors themselves acknowledge that “postoperative PSQ scores may also be influenced by recent pain experience and emotional factors”. This is a critical concession, as it strikes at the very heart of the study’s primary endpoint. If the PSQ is indeed susceptible to such state‑dependent confounders, then its repeated administration in the dynamic postoperative period renders it challenging to ascertain whether the observed score changes reflect genuine alterations in pain sensitivity (the construct the study purports to measure) or merely transient fluctuations in pain intensity recall, mood, or situational anxiety.7 Without concurrent validation against objective psychophysical measures (eg, quantitative sensory testing or temporal summation), the primary outcome’s construct validity remains unsubstantiated.

Differential Attrition and the Discrepancy Between per‑Protocol and Intention‑to‑Treat Analyses Suggest Potential Bias

Of the 84 randomised patients, four were excluded from the final per‑protocol (PP) analysis—all from the control group (three lost to follow‑up, one transferred to the ICU due to postoperative moderate‑to‑severe anaemia). This unequal attrition raises concern, as the control group’s exclusion of a patient with a serious postoperative complication (anaemia requiring ICU admission) may have systematically removed a case with potentially higher pain sensitivity, thereby artificially lowering the control group’s mean PSQ scores and diminishing the observed treatment effect in the PP analysis.

The authors performed an intention‑to‑treat (ITT) sensitivity analysis using last observation carried forward (LOCF) imputation and a mixed‑effects model for repeated measures (MMRM). Encouragingly, the direction of effect was consistent. However, the magnitude of the between‑group difference was attenuated in the ITT analysis compared with the PP analysis: for PSQ‑total on postoperative day 1, the PP estimate was 0.79 (95% CI: 0.31–1.27), whereas the ITT estimate was 0.77 (95% CI: 0.29–1.25); more notably, the day 2 difference, which was significant in the PP analysis (P = 0.027), became marginal in the ITT analysis (adjusted P = 0.040). This attenuation suggests that the PP analysis may have overestimated the true treatment effect, and the robustness of the primary finding is less secure than claimed. Furthermore, the use of LOCF imputation—which assumes that missing values are missing completely at random (MCAR)—is questionable given that all dropouts occurred in the control group for reasons potentially related to the outcome.8 A more appropriate approach, such as multiple imputation or pattern‑mixture models, would have provided greater assurance regarding the handling of missing data.

The Mechanistic Inference Linking BDNF Elevation to the Analgesic Effect Lacks Formal Mediation Analysis

The authors report significantly higher serum BDNF levels in the esketamine group throughout the perioperative period and propose that BDNF modulation “may underlie the observed effects” on pain sensitivity. While this hypothesis is biologically plausible—given BDNF’s role in synaptic plasticity and nociceptive processing—the current data do not support a causal mechanistic interpretation.

First, the absence of a formal mediation analysis means that the relationship between BDNF elevation and PSQ reduction remains purely associational. BDNF elevation could equally be an independent epiphenomenon of esketamine administration, rather than a mechanistic link in the causal pathway between the intervention and the reduction in pain sensitivity. To establish BDNF as a true mediator, the data would need to demonstrate that: (a) the intervention predicts both BDNF and PSQ; (b) BDNF predicts PSQ (accounting for the intervention); and (c) the intervention’s effect on PSQ is significantly attenuated when BDNF is entered into the model.9 Second, the authors found no between‑group difference in serum PGE2 levels—a key pro‑inflammatory mediator of central sensitisation—which further complicates the mechanistic picture. Third, peripheral serum BDNF levels may not accurately reflect central nervous system BDNF dynamics, given the limited blood–brain barrier permeability and the complex regulation of BDNF in different compartments. Without a formal mediation model (eg, Baron–Kenny or structural equation modelling) or central biomarker assessment, the mechanistic claims remain speculative and should be tempered.

Conclusion

Chen et al have conducted a well‑designed trial that adds valuable preliminary evidence on the potential role of esketamine in emergency gynaecological surgery. However, the unequal opioid background between groups, the use of a trait‑based instrument to assess state‑dependent postoperative changes, the differential attrition affecting the control group, and the overinterpretation of BDNF as a mechanistic mediator collectively warrant caution in interpreting the findings. In essence, while the trial suggests a potentially useful clinical strategy, its design precludes a definitive conclusion about esketamine’s independent effect. For future research to definitively answer this question, we strongly advocate for protocols employing standardised opioid regimens—with opioid consumption clearly designated as a co‑primary endpoint—and the inclusion of validated, objective measures of pain sensitivity to disentangle drug‑specific from opioid‑sparing effects, alongside formal mediation analyses to clarify the underlying mechanisms. I hope these comments contribute to the ongoing refinement of perioperative esketamine research.
