Antinephrin autoantibodies: rethinking the pathogenesis of minimal change disease
Authors: Cheng Xue, Chenchen Zhou, Zhiguo Mao
Published: 2024

To the Editor:

Minimal change disease (MCD) remains a leading cause of nephrotic syndrome, particularly in children, and its pathogenesis has long been a subject of debate. The recent study by Hengel et al. [1] provides crucial insights into the role of antinephrin autoantibodies in podocytopathies, specifically MCD and idiopathic nephrotic syndrome (INS). This landmark study significantly reshapes our understanding of these conditions, suggesting that antigen-antibody complexes, rather than the traditionally hypothesized T-cell abnormalities, may drive MCD pathogenesis [2]. Furthermore, this discovery clarifies why B-cell depletion therapies are effective in treating these podocytopathies in our previous study [3].

One of the most debated aspects of MCD has been the absence of visible immune complexes in renal histology [4]. We propose that this could be due to the specific location and nature of podocyte damage caused by antinephrin antibodies [5]. This raises the question of whether MCD is truly a ‘minimal change disease’ or rather a ‘moderate change disease,’ where severe slit diaphragm damage prevents the retention of antigen-antibody complexes within the kidney tissue.

Additionally, the correlation between antinephrin autoantibody levels and disease activity, as demonstrated in the study, underscores their potential as biomarkers for disease monitoring and prognosis [1]. The experimental model further validates the pathological significance of these autoantibodies by replicating the clinical phenotype in mice, providing strong evidence of a direct pathogenic role [1]. The experimental replication of the disease phenotype in mice lends significant weight to the pathogenic role of these autoantibodies, further bridging the gap between clinical observation and laboratory findings.

In conclusion, this research not only deepens our understanding of the pathophysiology of MCD and INS but also paves the way for novel diagnostic and therapeutic strategies targeting antinephrin antibodies. These advances have the potential to markedly improve outcomes for patients suffering from these challenging conditions. The findings challenge existing paradigms and invite a reevaluation of current clinical approaches to these immune-mediated diseases. By identifying antinephrin autoantibodies as key drivers of MCD, clinicians can now explore targeted therapies that may offer better disease management and improved patient outcomes.
