Cutaneous Adverse Reactions to IL-17 and IL-23 Inhibitors: A Clinical Review of Reaction Patterns
Authors: Ingyun Park, Ali Bayrouti, Duncan MacIntyre, Vivian Le, Khoa B Nguyen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionIL-17 and IL-23 inhibitors have transformed the management of moderate-to-severe psoriasis and other inflammatory diseases through targeted immune modulation. Although generally well tolerated, an expanding spectrum of cutaneous adverse reactions has been reported, ranging from common paradoxical eruptions to rare immune-mediated dermatoses that may mimic primary inflammatory or autoimmune conditions.ObjectivesWe present a narrative review of cutaneous adverse reactions associated with IL-17 and IL-23 inhibitors. This review aims to summarize reported reaction patterns, proposed mechanisms, clinical and histopathologic features, and current diagnostic and therapeutic considerations to support clinical decision-making.MethodsWe performed a narrative review of English-language literature describing cutaneous adverse reactions associated with IL-17, IL-23, and IL-12/23 inhibitors indexed in PubMed, Google Scholar, and Web of Science from database inception through August 2025. Clinical trials, observational studies, case series, case reports, and reviews were evaluated, and findings were synthesized according to reaction phenotype.ResultsPsoriasiform and eczematous eruptions represent the most frequently reported cutaneous adverse reactions and may be clinically and histologically indistinguishable from idiopathic dermatoses. Less commonly reported reactions include lupus-like syndromes, bullous pemphigoid, lichenoid drug reactions, granulomatous eruptions, and vasculitic presentations, including IgA vasculitis. Proposed mechanisms involve paradoxical alterations in immune signaling following cytokine blockade rather than direct drug toxicity, although definitive pathways remain incompletely understood. Management strategies vary according to severity and phenotype, ranging from topical therapies and continued biologic use to discontinuation of the offending agent and systemic immunosuppression.ConclusionsIL-17 and IL-23 inhibitors are associated with a diverse spectrum of cutaneous adverse reactions that require recognition due to their potential overlap with primary dermatologic diseases. A structured approach to diagnosis and management may facilitate timely intervention. Further studies are needed to clarify incidence, risk factors, mechanisms, and evidence-based treatment strategies.

Introduction

The development of biological therapies has improved outcomes for patients with a range of immune-mediated inflammatory diseases [1,2]. IL-17 and IL-23 inhibitors are widely used in dermatology to treat moderate-to-severe plaque psoriasis and psoriatic arthritis [3]. These agents are effective, with favorable safety profiles, but cutaneous adverse effects are increasingly reported [4].

Although generally well tolerated, IL-17 and IL-23 inhibitors have been associated with diverse cutaneous adverse effects. These can range from mild injection site reactions to superficial infections and even paradoxical dermatoses such as atopic dermatitis (AD) or new-onset psoriasis [5–7]. These reactions may impact adherence or quality of life but are often underrecognized due to their variable presentation and clinical overlap with other skin diseases [7]. Despite being targeted therapies, both IL-17 and IL-23 inhibitors can paradoxically provoke inflammatory skin conditions, with IL-17 inhibitors more frequently implicated than IL-23 inhibitors, even in patients without prior dermatological disease [8]. This review provides a clinically focused overview of cutaneous adverse effects associated with IL-17 and IL-23 inhibitors, emphasizing reaction patterns, proposed mechanisms, and clinical presentation.

Consistent with the objectives of this review, we focus primarily on cutaneous adverse reactions associated with IL- 17 and IL-23 inhibitors. However, because paradoxical and off-target phenomena have also been described with IL-12/23 inhibitors such as ustekinumab, including granulomatous reactions and vasculitis syndromes, we briefly reference these reactions where relevant for contextual comparison [9]. The biologic agents included in this review are grouped by target class (see Table 1): IL-17A inhibitors (secukinumab, ixekizumab), the IL-17 receptor A inhibitor (brodalumab), IL-17A and IL-17F inhibitor (bimekizumab), IL-23(p19) inhibitors (guselkumab, risankizumab, tildrakizumab), and the IL-12/23p40 inhibitor (ustekinumab). All are approved for moderate-to-severe plaque psoriasis, and several are also approved for psoriatic arthritis.

Objectives

This manuscript aims to provide an updated overview of cutaneous adverse reactions associated with IL-17 and IL-23 inhibitors, focusing on their clinical presentation, proposed pathophysiologic mechanisms, and histopathologic findings. It also seeks to characterize the spectrum of reported reaction patterns, including psoriasiform eruptions, eczematous reactions, lupus-like syndromes, bullous pemphigoid, lichenoid reactions, granulomatous eruptions, and vasculitic presentations, while summarizing current diagnostic and management approaches to support clinical decision-making.

Methods

In this review, we conducted a narrative, non-systematic review of cutaneous adverse reactions associated with IL-17 and IL-23 inhibitors. A focused literature search from database inception to August 2025 was performed utilizing Google Scholar, PubMed, and Web of Science with the keywords “IL-17 inhibitors,” “IL-23 inhibitors,” “secukinumab,” “ixekizumab,” “brodalumab,” “bimekizumab,” “guselkumab,” “risankizumab,” “tildrakizumab,” “ustekinumab,” and “cutaneous adverse reactions,” “paradoxical reactions,” “psoriasiform,” “eczema,” “atopic dermatitis-like,” “lupus-like,” “bullous pemphigoid,” “lichenoid,” “granulomatous,” and “vasculitis.” Eligible studies include English-language, peer-reviewed human studies (trials, observational studies, case series/reports, and reviews) describing cutaneous adverse reactions associated with IL-17, IL-23, or IL-12/23 (ustekinumab) inhibition. We excluded non-cutaneous events and reports lacking clinical detail.

Due to the descriptive nature and heterogeneity of available data, no formal risk-of-bias assessment or quantitative meta-analysis was performed. Data were synthesized qualitatively and organized by reaction phenotype to emphasize clinical presentation, histopathology, proposed mechanisms, and management considerations.

Results

Mechanism of Action and Clinical Use of IL-17 and IL-23 Inhibitors

IL-17 and IL-23 inhibitors target key components of the inflammatory signaling cascade involved in cutaneous immunity [10]. These cytokines coordinate keratinocyte proliferation, leukocyte recruitment, and sustained immune activation in chronic inflammatory dermatoses [11].

IL-17, a downstream effector of the T helper 17 (Th17) signaling cascade, is primarily produced by Th17 cells and induces keratinocytes to release pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, promoting neutrophil recruitment and epidermal inflammation [12]. IL-23 acts upstream by maintaining Th17 cells that produce IL-17 [10]. Inhibiting either cytokine may alter cutaneous immune homeostasis, potentially contributing to unintended inflammatory responses.

IL-17 and IL-23 inhibitors were developed to suppress this inflammatory cascade in immune-mediated conditions such as psoriasis and psoriatic arthritis [3]. Research has demonstrated that IL-17 and IL-23 inhibitors have superior skin clearance compared to older biologics like tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) inhibitors and are more effective in challenging sites such as the scalp, nails, palms, soles, and genitals [10].

Although IL-17 and IL-23 inhibitors target similar therapeutic pathways, their safety profiles differ. IL-17 is critical for mucocutaneous defense, and its inhibition increases the risk of infections like oral candidiasis and nasopharyngitis [12,13]. Evidence regarding malignancy risk remains limited. A systematic review and meta-analysis [13] found low absolute short- and long-term rates of infection and malignancy within available follow-up periods with both drug classes, though findings were based on heterogeneous observational data with variable follow-up durations. Reported signals are nonconclusive, requiring cautious interpretation. Larger studies with longer follow-ups are required to better characterize potential long-term malignancy risk, as malignancies often develop over many years.

Cutaneous Adverse Reactions Psoriasiform Reactions

Psoriasiform reactions are a subset of paradoxical cutaneous adverse effects that may arise in patients treated with IL-17 and IL-23 inhibitors. Clinically, these reactions manifest as erythematous, scaly plaques with sharply demarcated borders, closely resembling plaque psoriasis. Lesions commonly appear on the scalp, extremities, and/or trunk and may be indistinguishable from idiopathic psoriasis in morphology and distribution [14].

The frequency of these reactions remains difficult to estimate because published evidence is heterogeneous and largely case-based. In a systematic review of 313 reports totaling 2049 paradoxical cutaneous reactions, 72 were attributed to IL-17/IL-17R inhibitors and 1–2 to IL-23(p19) inhibitors [14]. These figures represent report-level proportions within the systematic review and should not be interpreted as population incidence rates. Although the pathogenesis remains incompletely understood, these eruptions are thought to reflect immune dysregulation in which IL-17 or IL-23 blockade may paradoxically shift immune signaling toward Th1/Th17 pathways, increasing interferon-γ and IL-22 production and potentially unmasking latent psoriatic pathways, particularly in genetically predisposed individuals [14–16].

The onset of psoriasiform reactions usually ranges from several weeks to months after initiating the biologic, though delayed onset beyond one year has also been reported [14,17]. Diagnosis relies mainly on clinical evaluation, with histopathological confirmation when needed. Some patients may have a personal or family history of psoriasis or atopic conditions, which may predispose them to developing paradoxical skin reactions [18].

Histologically, psoriasiform reactions mirror classic psoriasis. Histology typically shows regular acanthosis, parakeratosis, a reduced granular layer, and a perivascular lymphocytic infiltrate [14]. Munro microabscesses, which are aggregates of neutrophils in the stratum corneum, are commonly observed [14]. In some cases, overlapping spongiotic changes may be present, particularly in lesions with overlapping eczematous features [14,17].

Eczematous Reactions

Eczematous eruptions are recognized as paradoxical cutaneous adverse events, occurring more frequently with IL-17 inhibitors than IL-23 inhibitors. Clinically, they present as pruritic, ill-defined erythematous patches or plaques with scale, often involving flexural areas and resemble acute eczema or AD in a nummular or generalized pattern. Cases have demonstrated both widespread and localized involvement, with some patients exhibiting a classic AD-like phenotype [15,17,23–25]. Reported frequencies with IL-17 inhibitors range from 2.2% to 12.1%, with higher rates reported primarily in small observational cohorts [15]. Most studies report 2.8% to 6.2% [24]. In contrast, eczematous reactions with IL-23 inhibitors are rare and primarily described in isolated case reports [14,18].

The pathogenesis of IL-17-associated eczematous reactions remains unclear but is thought to involve a paradoxical shift in the cytokine profile. Specifically, IL-17 inhibition may lead to a compensatory polarization of the immune response toward Th2 and Th22 immunity, with upregulated IL-4, IL-10, and IL-22 expression [15,24,25].

Onset ranges from days to months, with rare cases up to three years [17,23]. Laboratory findings are nonspecific, though elevated serum IgE may be seen, and a personal or family history of atopy may increase risk [18].

Histopathology reveals spongiotic dermatitis with marked spongiosis, minimal acanthosis, and a superficial lymphocytic infiltrate, sometimes with eosinophils. These features often resemble those of classic eczematous dermatitis or AD features [15,17]. Although paradoxical eczematous eruptions are more commonly associated with IL-17 inhibitors, similar findings have been rarely reported with IL-23 inhibitors.

The most common management of paradoxical eczematous eruptions induced by IL-17 or IL-23 inhibitors involves stopping the inhibitors, use of topical corticosteroids, topical calcineurin inhibitors, and, in severe cases, systemic immunosuppressants such as cyclosporine or oral corticosteroids [17,26].

Lupus-Like Reactions

IL-17 and IL-23 inhibitor-induced lupus-like syndromes are extremely rare among cutaneous adverse reactions. Clinically, they resemble drug-induced lupus (DIL) erythematosus, appearing as photosensitive, erythematous annular or discoid plaques, sometimes typically involving sun-exposed areas. In a few cases, in addition to cutaneous findings, systemic manifestations such as arthralgias or serositis may occur, but mucosal involvement is uncommon [27]. Reported cases are extremely rare and most reported cases are cutaneous lupus-like rather than true systemic DIL. Although lupus-like eruptions have been reported with several cytokine-targeted biologics, including TNF-α inhibitors, such reactions remain exceedingly rare with IL-17 and IL-23 inhibitors, with only isolated case reports to date [14].

Importantly, the relationship between IL-23 inhibition and lupus is not unidirectional. In addition to rare reports of paradoxical lupus-like reactions, there are instances of successful use of IL-23 inhibitors in patients with concomitant psoriasis and systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) without precipitating disease flares and, in some cases, with improvement in cutaneous or musculoskeletal symptoms [28]. These observations suggest that lupus-like reactions are not uniformly seen across IL-23 targeted therapies and may reflect individual susceptibility rather than a class-wide effect.

The proposed mechanism is thought to involve immune dysregulation with disturbed cytokine equilibrium and potential de novo triggering of latent autoimmunity in vulnerable patients. This may be enhanced by type I interferon (IFN-I) signaling or autoantibody production, primarily extrapolated from observations in TNF-α inhibitor–associated lupus [29]. However, no definitive mechanistic link between IL-17 and IL-23 inhibition and lupus-like manifestations has been established [14]. Onset typically occurs within a few weeks to months after drug initiation, consistent with the course of other paradoxical immune-mediated skin reactions [14].

Laboratory evaluation may reveal autoantibodies such as antinuclear antibodies (ANA), anti-double-stranded DNA (dsDNA), and markers for nonspecific systemic inflammation [30]. However, these markers lack specificity and may be positive in various conditions, limiting their diagnostic utility when interpreted in isolation. Histopathology typically shows interface dermatitis with basal vacuolar changes, apoptotic keratinocytes, and superficial and deep perivascular lymphocytic infiltrate, sometimes with dermal mucin deposition, which is consistent with cutaneous lupus erythematosus [31].

There is no specific management strategy for IL-17 or IL-23 inhibitor-induced lupus-like reactions beyond the discontinuation of the biologic and the use of standard lupus therapies. Management generally involves discontinuation of the suspected agent and standard lupus-directed therapy (e.g., hydroxychloroquine ± systemic corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants depending on severity) [32]. Given the rarity of reported cases and the presence of contrasting data showing safe use of IL-23 inhibitors in patients with established lupus, careful clinical judgment is warranted when attributing causality.

Bullous Pemphigoid

Bullous pemphigoid (BP) is a rare but recognized cutaneous adverse reaction to both IL-17 and IL-23 inhibitors [33–36]. Clinically, drug-induced BP is typically indistinguishable from idiopathic BP, presenting with tense bullae arising on pruritic urticarial plaques and often accompanied by erosions due to scratching [33–36]. Evidence is limited to case reports/series, and population-level frequency is unknown [33,35].

The pathogenesis is incompletely understood. IL-17 and IL-23 blockade may alter the cytokine milieu, potentially unmasking autoreactivity against BP180/BP230 antigens, through dysregulation of the Th17/Th23 axis. Autoantibody binding activates complement, resulting in dermoepidermal junction separation and blister formation [33,37–39]. Onset typically occurs within weeks to months of treatment initiation, commonly in patients treated for psoriasis [33–36].

No consistent clinical feature distinguishes drug-induced from idiopathic BP. Diagnosis relies on standard histopathology, direct immunofluorescence showing linear IgG and/or C3 deposition along the basement membrane zone. Serological testing for BP180 and BP230 autoantibodies is frequently positive, particularly for BP180, which often correlates with disease activity [33,35,40,41].

Management consists of discontinuation of the offending biologic and initiating standard BP therapy, usually systemic or high-potency topical corticosteroids, with rapid improvement in most cases [34–36]. Persistent or recurrent disease may develop, especially if the biologic is continued [33–36].

Other Rare or Emerging Cutaneous Reactions Lichenoid Drug Reactions

Lichen planus is a T cell-mediated autoimmune inflammatory condition affecting the skin, hair, nails, and mucous membranes. Although often idiopathic, lichenoid drug reactions have been reported with IL-17 and IL-23 inhibitors [42,43]. Clinically, these reactions present as violaceous, polygonal papules or plaques, with mucosal involvement appearing as reticular white plaques [42,44]. Onset following drug initiation is variable, ranging from one week to 21 months [44]. Histopathology shows a lichenoid/interface dermatitis with necrotic keratinocytes and a band-like lymphocytic infiltrate [42,44,45]. One case of IL-23 inhibitor therapy for presumed psoriasis reported lichen planus pemphigoides (LPP) with classic lichenoid features followed by subepidermal blistering, confirmed by histology and immunofluorescence [43].

The mechanism is currently unclear but may involve increased interferon-α signaling by plasmacytoid dendritic cells, similar to TNF-α inhibitors, though direct mechanistic evidence is lacking [46]. Discontinuation of the offending agent leads to resolution within several months [42]. Switching biological therapy (including anti-IL-17) has been reported without recurrence [44]. While rare, lichenoid reactions have been increasingly recognized with both IL-17 and IL-23 inhibitors and should be considered when new violaceous papules, mucosal involvement, or blistering lesions develop during treatment.

Granulomatous Eruptions

Granulomatous eruptions, including sarcoidosis-like presentations, are another rare reaction reported in patients receiving IL-17 and IL-23 inhibitors and are far more established with TNF-α inhibitors. The consideration of biologic agents in granulomatous disorders such as sarcoidosis has raised concerns about paradoxical reactions resembling these same conditions [9]. Multiple case reports have described granulomatous reactions arising in patients treated with IL-12/23 or IL-17 inhibitors, with symptom onset ranging from two months to three years after therapy initiation [9,47,48]. Histology shows non-caseating granulomas [9,48].

The pathogenesis remains poorly understood, but one hypothesis suggests IL-17 inhibition downregulates nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain-containing protein 2 (NOD2), which is involved in innate immune responses. This disruption may interfere with the stability and regulation of granulomatous structures, potentially contributing to their formation or reactivation [48]. This hypothesis is based on limited experimental data and remains unproven in humans. Discontinuation of the causative agent is the initial step in managing this adverse effect [9,47]. However, it is also important to address both the original condition for which the medication was prescribed as well as the adverse reaction itself. One case supports the use of adalimumab, given its efficacy in treating both psoriasis and sarcoidosis, with clinical improvement observed two months following therapy [9]. Although uncommon, granulomatous reactions should be considered in the differential diagnosis for patients presenting with new nodular lesions or sarcoid-like features while using IL-17 or IL-23 inhibitors.

Vasculitic Presentations

Leukocytoclastic vasculitis, including immunoglobulin A (IgA) vasculitis, has emerged as another rare adverse effect of both IL-17 and IL-23 inhibitors [49,50]. Clinically, patients may present with erythematous papules that can progress to purpuric lesions [49,51,52]. Histopathology typically reveals leukocytoclastic vasculitis with granular IgA deposition [49,52]. While drug-induced vasculitis often develops 1–3 weeks after exposure, biologic-associated reactions may follow a delayed or relapsing course [49,53]. In one case, a patient who had previously experienced IgA vasculitis on interferon-α therapy developed a recurrence within hours of the first dose of an IL-17 inhibitor. A separate case of central nervous system (CNS) vasculitis was reported in a patient treated with an IL-23 inhibitor who developed focal seizures and encephalopathy one month after initiation, where biopsy showed small-vessel vasculitis [50]. However, this evidence is limited to an isolated abstract, and causality is uncertain, with alternative etiologies requiring consideration.

Management of this adverse effect includes withdrawal of the offending medication alongside systemic corticosteroid therapy such as prednisone [51,52]. Though vasculitic reactions remain rare, IL-17 inhibitors have been associated with IgA-mediated leukocytoclastic vasculitis, while IL-23 inhibitors have been linked to cases of small-vessel vasculitis involving the CNS in isolated reports with uncertain causality. Clinicians should remain vigilant for vasculitic complications in patients receiving these agents who present with purpura, neurological symptoms, or other signs of vascular inflammation.

Pragmatic Clinical Management Algorithm for Cutaneous Reactions to IL-17 and IL-23 Inhibitors

No reaction-specific guideline exists for cutaneous adverse reactions to IL-17 or IL-23 inhibitors. Current guidance is largely extrapolated from the AAD–NPF psoriasis biologics guidelines, which address adverse effects broadly but do not provide a reaction-specific algorithm [54].

We propose a pragmatic, threshold-based approach to managing cutaneous reactions from IL-17 and IL-23 inhibitors (Figure 1) based on eruption severity, targeted evaluation, and stepwise management. Eruption severity may be classified as mild (localized eczematous or psoriasiform plaques), moderate (widespread or symptomatic), or severe (erythroderma, blistering disorders, vasculitis, or lupus-like eruptions) [14,15,17,18,26,55]. Initial evaluation should incorporate a focused history and medication timeline with skin biopsy (± direct immunofluorescence for blistering, vasculitis, or lichenoid presentations) and basic labs (CBC, CMP, ESR/CRP; autoantibodies if lupus-like disease is suspected) [15,17,26,55,56].

Management thresholds for treatment modification can be standardized. For mild reactions, continue biological therapy with topical corticosteroids or calcineurin inhibitors and close follow-up [15,17,26]. For moderate reactions, consider temporary interruption of the biologic if symptoms persist beyond 2–4 weeks of optimized topical therapy or if body surface area exceeds 5–10% (commonly used clinical thresholds); short courses of systemic corticosteroids or bridging DMARDs (e.g., cyclosporine, methotrexate) may be considered [15,18,26,56]. In severe reactions, discontinue the offending biologic for BP, confirmed vasculitis, lupus-like eruptions, or reactions requiring systemic steroids >4 weeks or involving >10% body surface area with functional impairment [14,15,17,18,26,55–57]. Rechallenge should generally be avoided unless the reaction was clearly attributable to another cause or resolved fully with minimal intervention [14,15,18,26]. Paradoxical eczematous eruptions associated with IL-17 inhibitors may improve after switching to an IL-23 inhibitor with resolution of cutaneous symptoms in many cases.

Conclusion

As IL-17 and IL-23 inhibitors become more widely used, a growing spectrum of cutaneous adverse reactions is being recognized. While most patients tolerate these therapies well, paradoxical eruptions are increasingly reported. Psoriasiform and eczematous reactions are the most frequently described. These adverse reactions are often clinically and histologically indistinguishable from the idiopathic disease. Less frequent reactions include lupus-like syndromes, BP, lichenoid eruptions, granulomatous reactions, and vasculitis, often documented through isolated case reports.

Although the exact mechanisms remain unclear, the proposed reactions in many of these cases appear to reflect shifts in immune signaling rather than direct drug toxicity. Most of the adverse reactions are manageable with drug cessation and supportive treatment, though delayed diagnosis can lead to increased morbidity. As evidence remains limited for rarer reaction types, future studies are needed to clarify incidence, risk factors, and optimal management strategies. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for new or worsening dermatoses in patients receiving these agents with or without a history of prior skin disease.
