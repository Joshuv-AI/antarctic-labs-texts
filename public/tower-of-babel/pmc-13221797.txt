False-Positive High-Sensitivity Troponin I in Clinical Practice
Authors: Achini Ishara Abeygunawardana Pihille Gedara, Angela Ratsch, Gabriela Strey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHigh-sensitivity cardiac troponin I (hs-cTnI) is essential for diagnosing myocardial infarction, however, false-positive results can significantly influence clinical management.Case Series SummaryOver 13 months, 11 patients at a regional Queensland hospital showed elevated hs-cTnI without supportive clinical or imaging evidence of myocardial infarction. Validation using i-STAT point-of-care testing and formal interference studies confirmed all results as false positives.DiscussionIn a setting without onsite cardiac services, analytical accuracy is critical. False-positive troponin results may trigger incorrect diagnoses, anticoagulation or antiplatelet therapy, interfacility transfers, invasive procedures, hospitalization, and heightened patient anxiety, all with significant implications for patient safety, resource use, and health care costs.Take-Home MessagesFalse-positive hs-cTnI results continue to represent a significant diagnostic challenge in the era of advanced analytical assays. Clinicians should remain vigilant when laboratory findings conflict with clinical presentations. Targeted interference investigations and confirmation through alternative assay platforms can mitigate organizational and patient risk.Key words: ECG, false-positive, troponin

Six patients presented with chest discomfort, 4 patients presented with dyspnea, and 1 patient with nausea. All patients had cardiovascular histories or risk factors, and all had hs-cTnI elevations detected on admission, with levels ranging from 58 to 667 ng/L (99th percentile: 45.2 ng/L, females: <34.1 ng/L, males: <53.5 ng/L),1 indicative of troponin leakage from cardiac muscle damage/ischemia.2 However, no patients had new electrocardiographic (ECG) changes or echocardiographic evidence of new cardiac wall motion abnormalities.

The perceived likelihood of acute coronary syndrome (ACS) shaped the early management approach. Five patients were treated as having non–ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction and were commenced on dual antiplatelet therapy and therapeutic enoxaparin. One of these patients left against medical advice after the initiation of cardiac-based treatment, and after cardiology review, the other 4 patients were treated for ACS-related conditions. Five patients were treated for non–ACS-related presentations, and 1 patient had a concurrent intracranial hemorrhage and was not commenced on antiplatelet or anticoagulation therapy.

Differential Diagnosis

An index of suspicion as to the validity of the hs-cTnI results was raised based on 1) no evidence of ECGs changes and 2) troponin z-scores within normal range. These findings triggered i-STAT troponin I testing (Abbott), which serves as an alternative platform for troponin analysis.3 The i-STAT troponin I test revealed normal values (<0.04 ng/mL) in 7 patients and marginal elevations in 3 patients, all inconsistent with their significant cardiac troponin I elevations; testing was not available for 1 patient. Interference studies confirmed analytical interference (ie, false-positive hs-cTnI results) in all 11 patients.

Discussion

The 11 patients exemplify the diagnostic challenges posed by hs-cTnI testing. All had persistent troponin elevations discordant with clinical findings. These patients underwent serial troponin I analyses over several days, despite a low clinical suspicion for myocardial infarction. Four patients were treated with dual antiplatelet therapy and therapeutic enoxaparin based on false-positive troponin results, leading to increased length of hospital stay and health care costs, with the potential for additional interventions including coronary computed tomography angiography or percutaneous coronary angiography. Interference study validation processes prevented unnecessary continuation of treatment or further interventions.

False-positive troponin elevations are relatively rare (0.5% to 3%), but they pose a significant risk to patient safety and care efficiency.4 When these spurious results are not identified, they can lead to inappropriate and potentially harmful treatment, prolonging hospital stay and increasing health care expenditure.

Troponin I

Cardiac troponin I is a regulatory protein specific to heart muscle that controls the calcium-mediated interaction of actin and myosin, essential for muscle contraction.2 In the event of myocardial injury, troponin I is released into the bloodstream, making it a sensitive and specific marker for myocardial infarction.5 High-sensitivity assays detect troponin earlier and in smaller quantities, facilitating rapid diagnosis. However, as highlighted by Chaulin,5 increasing sensitivity also raises the likelihood of detecting nonpathologic elevations and assay interference.

Troponin I measuring tests

Traditional troponin assays relied on enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays and chemiluminescent methods, typically reporting within 1 to 2 hours but with limited sensitivity for early infarcts.6 Modern hs-cTnI assays, such as Siemens Atellica, Abbott Architect, and Beckman Coulter Access, meet rigorous analytical standards of ≤10% coefficient of variation at the 99th percentile6 and detect very small amounts of troponin.

Point-of-care platforms such as i-STAT analyze troponin via bedside testing, with results available in just a few minutes instead of waiting for the central laboratory. This makes them especially valuable in emergencies, ambulances, and rural or remote clinics where laboratory access is limited. However, because these portable devices are less analytically advanced than full laboratory analyzers, their sensitivity is slightly lower, meaning they may not detect very small troponin elevations as reliably as high-sensitivity laboratory-based assays.7

According to our hospital's pathology department, the cost of an Atellica troponin I test (in 2026 USD) is $10.47, while the i-STAT point-of-care troponin I test costs $20.93.

Interference studies

Interference studies are critical for confirming false-positive results. False positives occur when biological factors or assay interference triggers an apparent elevation without true cardiomyocyte necrosis.5 Heterophile antibodies can interfere with a troponin test by binding onto the test's antibodies, creating a false signal that makes it appear that troponin is present.5 Heterophilic antibodies can be formed after exposure to animal-derived antigens, vaccines, viral infections, or dietary components, and they may persist for years.5 Their clinical significance lies in the potential to cross-link immunoassay antibodies nonspecifically, yielding false elevation in troponin results without underlying myocardial damage.8

In addition to heterophile antibodies, macrotroponin, an immune complex of troponin and immunoglobulin, can lead to persistently elevated results for weeks to months without evidence of cardiac injury, mainly owing to reduced renal clearance. Less common causes for troponin interference include the presence of rheumatoid factors, fibrin clots, and elevated alkaline phosphatases.8

Clinical significance of false-positive troponin

False-positive results can have wide ranging clinical consequences. Patients may undergo unnecessary admissions, telemetry monitoring, cardiac imaging, administration of medication, or even coronary angiography, all of which carry procedural risk and/or radiation exposure. Patients also experience psychological stress, fear of heart disease, and potential complications from unneeded anticoagulation, antiplatelet therapy, or contrast administration.

For health care systems, false-positive tests increase costs through repeated testing, increased resource use, prolonged hospital stays, and specialist consultations and treatment. In regional areas this may include transferring patients to tertiary care centers by air transport.

Recommendations

Based on our case series, we recommend that clinicians consider using the i-STAT troponin I assay early in presentations, where rapid turnaround is crucial. The i-STAT platform provides results in approximately 20 minutes and may help expedite clinical decision-making while reducing unnecessary serial testing and treatment.

It is also critical that clinicians engage with the laboratory early—preferably after the initial atypical result—if analytical interference is suspected, rather than waiting several days before requesting interference studies. Recognizing patterns in atypical troponin I levels, including nondynamic z-scores, can further assist in distinguishing myocardial injury from false-positive elevations.

Importantly, for long-term patient care, we recommend that health care providers clearly document the presence of falsely elevated troponin levels in the patient notes, on the discharge summary, and as an alert on any electronic patient information system. This information should also be communicated to the patient and the patient's general practitioner, ensuring they are aware of the false-positive findings. Accurate documentation can help prevent future unnecessary investigations, hospital admissions, and treatments based on misleading troponin elevations. However, caution needs to always be applied with notations of false-positive troponin findings to prevent inadvertently missing a true ACS in the future.

Conclusions

This series emphasizes the importance of close clinicopathological correlation, recognition of assay limitations, and early consultation with clinical biochemistry for interference studies when laboratory results are not consistent with the presenting scenario. False-positive troponin elevations can have wide-ranging consequences:

•Initiation of unnecessary antiplatelet and anticoagulant therapy, exposing patients to bleeding risk.

•Patient and family emotional distress based on an incorrect diagnosis of a myocardial infarction.

•Prolonged hospital admissions, additional imaging, increased resource use, and higher health care costs.

Our series reinforces the cautionary message from Millhouse et al8 and Lapić et al9 that clinicians must combine biochemical results with clinical context. Suspecting interference early, quickly repeating the test on different platforms, and working with the laboratory can help prevent harm. Understanding how false-positive troponins can materialize, such as from interfering antibodies, macrotroponin, or other substances, enables clinicians to recognize when results don't match the patient's condition and seek laboratory advice quickly.
