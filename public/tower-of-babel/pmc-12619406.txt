Socioeconomic gradients and mechanisms of chronic disease health literacy: the mediating role of preventive healthcare utilization in rural Shangdong, China
Authors: Liansen Wang, Xinyu Xu, Rui Li, Yuxuan Zhang, Yingjie Cai, Jing Tang, Xiaowei Yang, Jing Han, Fangyao Chen, Xiuli Qiao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo explore the social determinants and underlying mechanisms of health literacy in managing chronic diseases, and to analyze how socioeconomic status (SES) influences it.MethodThis study, based on Shandong Province’s 2022 Health Literacy Surveillance Database, selected 2,826 residents from some, counties in Shandong eligible areas using multistage stratified cluster random sampling. It explored the social determinants of chronic disease health literacy using multiple linear regression, multiple logistic regression, interactive analysis and mediation analysis.ResultsHigher SES (High vs. Low: OR = 2.21, 95%CI: 1.68–2.91, P < 0.001; Medium vs. Low: OR = 1.36, 95%CI: 1.08–1.71, P = 0.009), elevated household income (Highest vs. Lowest category: OR = 2.58, 95%CI: 1.58–4.22, P < 0.001), and higher educational attainment (Bachelor’s vs. Primary: OR = 3.46, 95%CI: 2.06–5.81, P < 0.001) were positively associated with higher chronic disease literacy. However, patients specifically diagnosed with hypertension (OR = 2.27, 95%CI: 1.35–3.79), coronary heart disease (OR = 2.57, 95%CI: 1.14–5.81), or diabetes (OR = 2.37, 95%CI: 1.21–4.63) demonstrated significantly higher literacy levels. The preventive healthcare services (indirect effect = 0.017, 95% CI: 0.016–0.041, P < 0.001), changes in health behavior (indirect effect = 0.007, 95% CI: 0.002–0.020, P < 0.001), and frequency of physical examinations (indirect effect = 0.006, 95% CI: 0.004–0.015, P < 0.001) significantly mediated the relationship between SES and chronic disease literacy. Subgroup analysis showed that in the low SES group, women (OR = 0.60, 95% CI: 0.50–0.75, P < 0.001) and elders (OR = 0.70, 95% CI: 0.42–0.91, P = 0.001) had significantly lower chronic disease literacy than men and young participants.ConclusionThis study elucidates the multidimensional mechanisms by which SES impacts chronic disease health literacy and provides a scientific basis for developing targeted interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12939-025-02677-y.Keywords: Socioeconomic status (SES), Chronic disease health literacy, Preventive healthcare utilization, Mediation analysis, Health disparities, Rural China, Health behavior change, Low-SES populations, Gender disparities, Aging population

Introduction

Chronic diseases are the leading cause of death worldwide, and their prevention and treatment are extremely important for most countries [1]. In recent years, China has faced great challenges in the field of chronic disease prevention and control. According to the National Health and Wellness Commission of China, there are more than 300 million patients with diagnosed chronic diseases in China, accounting for 86.6% of the total causes of death [2]. Due to systemic disparities in health, rural residents had a higher prevalence (82.6%) than urban residents (79.7%) [3]. Health literacy—the capacity to access, interpret, and apply health information—is critical for managing chronic conditions such as hypertension and diabetes. However, rural residents in China lag significantly behind urban counterparts: only 15.3% demonstrate adequate health literacy to engage in disease prevention and self-care practices [4]. Addressing these disparities is urgent, as improving rural health literacy could reduce disease incidence by 23% and healthcare costs by 18% according to modeling studies [5].

A robust body of evidence has established the socioeconomic gradient in chronic disease literacy, wherein individuals positioned higher on the socioeconomic spectrum systematically demonstrate superior health knowledge acquisition and self-management capacities compared to their socioeconomically disadvantaged counterparts [6]. This gradient manifests not merely through income stratification but through compounded advantages in educational accessibility and healthcare resource allocation [7]. In rural China, preventive healthcare utilization primarily encompasses routine physical examinations, which serve as a critical mechanism for health surveillance. Physical examinations, as a crucial component of preventive healthcare services, confer significant benefits in enhancing health literacy. The most reliable existing evidence indicates that patients benefit from regular check-ups because of the improvement in clinical preventive services and the fact that patients can obtain health information more promptly [8]. For example, Liu Yongbing et al. (2014) conducted a study on the elderly in nursing homes in Urumqi. The results showed that the health literacy scores of the elderly who could undergo regular physical examinations were significantly higher than those who did not (73 ± 31 points vs. 55 ± 28 points, P < 0.05). Moreover, the analysis confirmed the time since the last physical examination as an independent factor influencing health literacy (β=-7.261, P < 0.001), indicating a positive correlation between the frequency of physical examinations and the level of health literacy [9]. This result is consistent with the theoretical framework of health literacy, that is, by accessing health information and services through physical examinations, individuals can more effectively translate them into the ability to make self-health decisions [10]. In addition, Baker et al. (2004) pointed out that regular physical examinations can promote the internalization of health information, thereby improving health behaviors [11].

However, existing studies exhibit notable limitations. First, health literacy assessments predominantly tend to focus on functional dimensions or unidimensional socioeconomic status (SES) metrics, neglecting systematic analyses of multidimensional SES constructs [12]. Second, existing literature pays little attention to the mediating mechanisms of health behaviors and knowledge acquisition. For instance, the potential role of preventive healthcare service utilization (such as government-organized physical examinations) in mitigating the negative impact of socioeconomic disadvantages on health literacy has not been thoroughly explored [13]. In addition, existing studies pay insufficient attention to the socioeconomic gradients in health literacy within the context of some rural areas in China [14].

To address these gaps, this study integrates multidimensional SES assessment with an analysis of the mediating role of preventive healthcare utilization to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the determinants of chronic disease health literacy among rural Chinese residents.This study therefore aims to provide new empirical evidence on the relationship between socioeconomic factors, preventive healthcare utilization, and chronic disease literacy, thereby enriching the literature on public health and health economics.

Materials and methods

Sample and data

This study utilized data from the Shandong Provincial Health Literacy Survey. A multi-stage stratified cluster random sampling method was employed to select a representative sample of 2,826 residents. The sampling framework was based on the NCHS Urban-Rural Classification Scheme(RUCA) for Counties [15], which was adapted to reflect the developmental characteristics of Chinese counties. Accordingly, the following six types of counties were included: (1) Large central metro counties; (2) Large fringe metro counties; (3) Medium metro counties; (4) Small metro counties; (5) Micropolitan counties; (6) Noncore counties.

To specifically investigate health literacy in non-metropolitan areas, our sampling strategy focused on county types 4 (Small metro), 5 (Micropolitan), and 6 (Noncore), excluding the large central, fringe, and medium metro counties (types 1–3). Based on this focused framework, the study selected Yishui County (RUCA 6), Cao County (RUCA 5), Leling City (RUCA 4), Ju County (RUCA 6), Yuncheng County (RUCA 5), Shan County (RUCA 4), Linshu County (RUCA 5), Fei County (RUCA 6), Dongping County (RUCA 4), and Ningjing County (RUCA 5). From each selected county, three townships were randomly chosen; subsequently, two villages were selected from each township.

The inclusion criteria for participants were: (1) aged 15 years and above; (2) of Chinese nationality; (3) able to read and write. The exclusion criteria were: (1) individuals outside the specified age range; (2) those with mental disabilities that could impede their ability to complete the survey. No other restrictions were implemented. This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

To ensure a high response rate, trained survey personnel conducted face-to-face household interviews using a standardized questionnaire (see Supplementary File 2: Figure S1).

Socioeconomic status assessment

Following Zhang et al. [15], individual socioeconomic status (SES) was assessed using three variables collected at baseline: household income level, educational qualifications, and employment status. However, considering China’s extremely high medical insurance coverage, three variables - total household income level, educational qualifications, and employment status - were used instead of medical insurance coverage to evaluate individual level SES [16]. For pre-tax household gross income levels, participants selected from the following categories: (i) <¥ 5,000 (ii) ¥ 5000-¥ 10,000 (iii) ¥10,000-¥20,000 (iv) ¥20,000-¥30,000 (v) ¥30,000-¥50,000 (vi) ¥50,000-¥100,000 (vii) >¥100,000. Educational qualifications were recorded as: (i) Primary school or below (ii) Junior high school (iii) Senior high school/technical secondary (iv) Junior college (v) Bachelor’s degree or above. Employment status categories included: (i) Agricultural workers (ii) Factory/enterprise workers (iii) Commercial/service workers (iv) Government/public institution employees (v) Students (vi) Others (unemployed, caregivers, etc.). Variable definitions are detailed in Supplementary File 1: Table S1.

Subsequently, we applied Latent Class Analysis (LCA) to construct an unmeasured latent variable using multiple observed categorical variables, estimating socioeconomic status (SES) based on the aforementioned three variables in our dataset. The LCA procedure was implemented via the R packagepoLCA(v1.6.0), with a maximum iteration limit of 10,000 and a convergence tolerance threshold of1 × 10 − 6 [17]. To determine the optimal number of latent classes, we fitted LCA models ranging from 2 to 8 classes. Models failed to converge when the number of classes exceeded 5. Parameter selection was further guided by the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and likelihood ratio statistic (G2). Latent classes with an average posterior probability above 0.7 were considered classifications with acceptable uncertainty (Supplementary File 1: Table S2 and Supplementary File 2: Fig S2). All average posterior probabilities exceeded 0.75, confirming distinct socioeconomic strata. Ultimately, three latent classes were identified, corresponding to high, medium, and low SES based on item response probabilities (Supplementary File 1: Table S2).

Assessment of chronic diseases

This study assessed chronic disease health literacy, a competency relevant to the general population. Our analysis of chronic disease status focused on five predefined categories: (i) hypertension; (ii) heart disease; (iii) cerebrovascular diseases; (iv) diabetes; and (v) malignant tumors (see Supplementary File 1: Table S1). Participants reporting none of these were classified as “no chronic disease.” This selection was based on the high population burden of these conditions and their direct relevance to preventive healthcare services—a key mediator in our study. Following Zhu et al. and Said et al. [18, 19]. we defined participants with baseline cardiovascular disease (CVD), diabetes, and cancer using diagnosis records from the coded by the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision(ICD-10). Cerebrovascular disease (CeVD) is a group of neurological disorders caused by cerebral vascular lesions, mainly caused by factors such as arteriosclerosis, hypertension, thrombosis, and vascular rupture, including ischemic stroke (such as cerebral infarction), hemorrhagic stroke, transient ischemic attack (TIA), etc [20].Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is a group of disorders of the circulatory system caused by atherosclerosis, hypertension, metabolic abnormalities, etc., including coronary heart disease, stroke, heart failure, and others [21].

Preventive healthcare utilization (PHU)

Preventive healthcare utilization (PHU) was conceptualized as a multi-dimensional construct grounded in an integrated theoretical framework combining the Health Belief Model, Social Cognitive Theory, and the Transtheoretical Model. This framework posits that health behaviors are influenced by a continuum of factors, from the acquisition of knowledge and formation of intentions to the translation of those intentions into sustained action. Our PHU measure was designed to operationalize this continuum, capturing engagement that ranges from passive exposure to health information (e.g., video content), through the active use of enabling tools (e.g., digital apps), to the concrete utilization of clinical services (e.g., check-ups) and the ultimate adoption of improved health behaviors [22].

Health Check-ups and Screening encompasses routine physical examinations (such as blood pressure and blood glucose tests), cancer screenings (e.g., imaging and laboratory tests for breast cancer and colorectal cancer), and an early detection system for chronic diseases like hypertension and diabetes. The frequency of participation is quantified by the total number of health check-ups attended in the past 12 months (including free screening programs provided by government public health services and self-funded physical examination items). The 12-month recall period is a standard employed in major national health surveys [23, 24]to balance the minimization of recall bias with the capture of a representative cycle of preventive service utilization. This indicator is a variable used to reflect an individual’s compliance with health monitoring [25].

Digital Health Tool Utilization specifically refers to the monthly activity level of tracking health data, intervening in chronic diseases, or acquiring medical knowledge through smart terminals (such as health management mobile apps and wearable physiological parameter monitoring devices) or online platforms (such as internet medical consultation systems) [26].

Frequency of Health-Related Short Video Exposure is measured by the average weekly viewing frequency of users obtaining health-themed content on platforms like Douyin, Kuaishou, and Bilibili, either through active searches or passive pushes. The content includes chronic disease prevention and control strategies, scientific dietary nutrition guidance, and first aid skill demonstrations, aiming to evaluate the potential impact of new media on public health literacy [27].

Health Behavior Change was assessed through the item “Have you changed any health-related behaviors based on acquired health knowledge?“. This direct self-report measure captures the tangible translation of health knowledge into actionable behavior, reflecting the core concept of the action and maintenance stages in the Transtheoretical Model [28].

Definition of chronic disease literacy

Health literacy was assessed using the official Chinese Health Literacy Questionnaire developed by the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention. This study focused specifically on the chronic diseases (CD) literacy module. This module consisted of 9 questions with a total score of 12 points, derived from a combination of question types: 2 true/false questions (1 point each), 3 single-choice questions (1 point each), 2 multiple-choice questions (2 points for a fully correct answer), and 2 scenario-based questions (worth a total of 3 points). As the nationally mandated instrument, the questionnaire’s content validity and structural validity were rigorously established and affirmed by an expert panel from the National Health Commission during its development. In accordance with the scoring protocol of the national survey, respondents who scored ≥ 10 points (effectively meeting the 80% proficiency threshold) in this module were classified as having adequate chronic disease health literacy [29, 30].Our sample showed strong internal consistency with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.862.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics across the three socioeconomic status (SES) groups were compared using unpaired two-tailed t-tests or Mann-Whitney tests for continuous variables and χ² tests for categorical variables, depending on the data distribution. Continuous variables are presented as mean (standard deviation, SD) or median (interquartile range, IQR), and categorical variables are presented as numbers (percentages). Multivariable logistic regression was employed to examine the associations between SES, chronic comorbidities, preventive healthcare utilization (PHU), and chronic disease literacy. All regression models were adjusted for covariates, including age and sex. Adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) are reported. Multiplicative interaction and stratified analyses were conducted to assess the moderating effects of SES on the relationships involving PHU and chronic comorbidities. A two-sided P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Mediation analyses were conducted to evaluate the extent to which PHU and chronic comorbidities mediated the association between SES and chronic disease literacy. We performed causal mediation analysis using 5,000 bootstrap resamples to estimate the indirect effects. To account for the testing of multiple parallel mediation pathways, we applied the Bonferroni correction to control the family-wise error rate. All analyses were performed using R software, version 4.1.0.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of the participants. The study population had a mean age of 51.18 ± 14.131 years, and 54% (n = 1525) were women. Based on the composite socioeconomic status (SES) measure, 15.7% (n = 443) were classified as high SES, 56.6% (n = 1600) as medium SES, and 27.7% (n = 783) as low SES. Participants with low SES were predominantly agricultural workers. This group was also associated with fewer positive health-related practices, including lower frequencies of physical examinations, less exposure to health knowledge, less use of digital health tools, and fewer changes in health behaviors (all P < 0.001). Meantime, the low SES group had a significantly higher prevalence of chronic diseases and comorbid conditions, with a notably higher burden of hypertension (all P < 0.001). Furthermore, the heatmap of correlations (Fig. S3) revealed that chronic disease literacy and total household income were strongly correlated with other SES factors (P < 0.001), suggesting a close relationship between health literacy and socioeconomic status. A positive correlation was also observed between preventive healthcare utilization and health behavior change (P < 0.001).

The association between SES, preventive healthcare, chronic comorbidity factors, and chronic disease literacy

This study explored the influencing factors of chronic disease literacy through univariate logistic regression analysis. The results indicated that individuals with high socioeconomic status (OR = 2.21, 95% CI: 1.68–2.91, P < 0.001) and medium socioeconomic status (OR = 1.36, 95% CI: 1.08–1.71, P = 0.009) were significantly more likely to have chronic disease literacy compared to the low SES group. Similarly, each ascending category of total household income and educational attainment was positively associated with literacy (all P for trend < 0.001), with the highest income (OR = 2.58, 95% CI: 1.58–4.22) and education (OR = 3.46, 95% CI: 2.06–5.81) groups showing particularly strong effects. Positive health behaviors were strongly correlated with literacy: having made health behavior changes (OR = 2.56, 95% CI: 1.99–3.30, P < 0.001) and higher physical examination frequency were significant predictors (e.g., monthly examination OR = 8.67, 95% CI: 2.73–27.54). Notably, while the presence of any chronic disease was inversely associated with literacy in the unadjusted model (OR = 0.55, 95% CI: 0.34–0.87, P = 0.012), individuals with specific conditions such as hypertension (OR = 2.27, 95% CI: 1.35–3.79), coronary heart disease (OR = 2.57, 95% CI: 1.14–5.81), and diabetes (OR = 2.37, 95% CI: 1.21–4.63) demonstrated significantly higher literacy levels. In addition, higher preventive healthcare utilization (High vs. Low: OR = 2.71, 95% CI: 2.13–3.45) and more frequent health knowledge exposure (per time/week: OR = 1.09, 95% CI: 1.01–1.19) were also identified as protective factors. In contrast, age, gender, employment category, and digital health tool usage frequency did not show statistically significant associations with chronic disease literacy (Table 2; Fig. 1).

The mediating effect of preventive healthcare and chronic comorbidity factors on SES’s impact on chronic disease literacy

The mediation analysis revealed significant indirect effects of SES on chronic disease literacy through multiple pathways (Table 3; Fig. 2). Preventive healthcare utilization mediated 22.7% (95% CI: 16.38–31.24, P < 0.001) of the total effect, with an adjusted direct effect of 0.058 (95% CI: 0.034–0.082). Other significant mediators included physical examination frequency (mediation proportion: 8.0%, 95% CI: 5.12–12.05, P < 0.001), health behavior change (9.3%, 95% CI: 5.98–14.12, P < 0.001), digital health tool usage (4.0%, 95% CI: 2.03–6.58, P < 0.001), and frequency of health knowledge exposure (2.7%, 95% CI: 1.45–4.35, P < 0.001). In contrast, chronic disease situation, coronary artery disease (CAD), and cerebrovascular disease (CeVD) showed no significant mediation effects (all mediation proportions included zero in 95% CI except for a small proportion of hypertension, P > 0.05, Supplementary File 1: Table S3).

The interaction between preventive healthcare and chronic comorbidity factors on SES’s impact on chronic disease literacy

The interaction analysis (Table 4) revealed significant moderating effects of socioeconomic status (SES) on chronic disease literacy through specific behavioral and healthcare pathways. Preventive healthcare utilization exhibited a strong interaction with SES (F = 5.076, P = 0.006), indicating that higher SES amplified the benefits of preventive care on health literacy. Similarly, physical examination frequency (F = 2.66, P = 0.031) and frequency of health knowledge exposure (F = 2.627, P = 0.007) demonstrated significant SES-dependent effects, with high-SES individuals showing greater improvements in these domains. In contrast, health behavior change (F = 0.048, P = 0.953) and digital health tool usage (F = 1.849, P = 0.117) showed no significant interaction with SES.Joint analyses further illustrated these disparities (Fig. S4). High-SES groups displayed markedly enhanced outcomes in preventive health information utilization and physical examination adherence, whereas low-SES groups faced elevated risks for adverse health outcomes, including cardiovascular disease incidence and chronic disease progression.

The impact of socioeconomic inequality on chronic disease literacy among different gender and age subgroups

Stratified analyses by socioeconomic status (SES) revealed sex-specific disparities in chronic disease literacy (Supplementary File 1: Table S4). In the low-SES subgroup, females exhibited significantly lower odds of chronic disease literacy compared to males (OR = 0.60, 95% CI: 0.50–0.75, P < 0.001), while males in this group also showed reduced literacy (OR = 0.65, 95% CI: 0.55–0.78, P = 0.002). In contrast, no significant sex differences were observed in high-SES (female OR = 0.71, 95% CI: 0.37–1.36, P = 0.306; male reference) or medium-SES (female OR = 0.85, 95% CI: 0.70–1.05, P = 0.12) subgroups.Age-stratified analyses demonstrated SES-dependent variations in chronic disease literacy. In the low-SES subgroup, participants aged > 60 years had significantly lower literacy than those ≤ 60 years (OR = 0.70, 95% CI: 0.42–0.91, P = 0.001). Similarly, younger individuals (≤ 60 years) in low-SES groups also showed reduced literacy (OR = 0.62, 95% CI: 0.52–0.75, P = 0.016). No significant age-related disparities were observed in high-SES (OR = 0.95, 95% CI: 0.75–1.45, P = 0.327) or medium-SES (OR = 0.93, 95% CI: 0.72–1.39, P = 0.339) subgroups (Supplementary File 1: Table S5).

Discussion and conclusion

This study utilized a multidimensional socioeconomic status (SES) evaluation system. Comprehensive indicators of annual household income, education level, and occupational status were constructed, and a mediation analysis was employed to investigate the association between SES and chronic disease health literacy. The research results showed that SES was significantly positively correlated with health literacy level, with preventive medical utilization, physical examination frequency, and health behavior change as the main mediating pathways. Notably, significant interaction effects were observed between SES and the utilization of preventive medical care, physical examination frequency, and exposure to health knowledge.Although chronic diseases such as hypertension, coronary heart disease and diabetes are positively related to chronic disease literacy, their prevalence rate in the low SES population is significantly higher, suggesting that there is a two-way relationship between chronic disease burden and SES inequality.

Chronic disease health literacy, as a key competency for individuals to acquire, understand, and apply knowledge and skills for chronic disease prevention and treatment, is directly related to their health behavior choices, disease self-management effects, and overall health outcomes [28–30]. However, most studies have focused on the association between SES and health outcomes, but less on the mechanisms by which SES affects chronic disease health literacy; and most studies have defined SES only in terms of income, education, or occupation, failing to comprehensively reveal its multidimensional interactions.Kaleigh E Fidaleo’s research explored the impact of depression and socioeconomic status on the neurocognitive abilities of people living with HIV [31–34]. Similarly, a study by Younoh Kim explored that SES influences health gradient [35]. However, it is unclear whether SES has a sustained effect on chronic disease health literacy. In our study, we used household income level, educational qualification and occupational status to jointly define SES and assessed the association between composite SES and individual chronic disease literacy. We found that people with high SES had higher chronic disease health literacy, and this association appeared consistent across different sexes and age groups. The results of our analysis of each individual SES indicator were also consistent with previous studies [36–38], which lends support to the current findings.

Recent studies have shown that SES can change the incidence rate of cardiovascular diseases by influencing prevention and health care behavior [39]. However, there is currently no research evaluating the potential role of preventive healthcare behaviors in the association between SES and chronic disease health literacy. Therefore, in this study, mediation analysis was conducted, which indicated that individual preventive healthcare behaviors explained a substantial portion of the association between SES and chronic disease health literacy. The frequency of physical examinations has a particularly strong association with improving health literacy. The health literacy score of residents with quarterly physical examination frequency is higher than that of those without physical examination, which is consistent with the research conclusion of population health monitoring practice [40]. Additionally, health knowledge education and the use of electronic tools may represent a “cognitive” pathway facilitating health behavior change. However, in subgroup analysis by SES level and age, women in the low SES group and those over 60 years old had significantly lower chronic disease health literacy than other subgroups, while no similar differences were observed in the high/medium SES group. This result suggests that gender and age-related health inequalities in low SES populations may stem from differences in access to healthcare resources, such as less participation of women in health decision-making and barriers to digital tool use among older adults, while the socioeconomic advantages of high SES populations can mitigate such risks [41–43].

Previous studies have shown that preventive healthcare interventions can reduce various health risks, including cardiovascular disease incidence [15]. In our research, we consistently found that adhering solely to preventive healthcare behaviors such as regular check ups and health education was associated with a protective effect on overall chronic disease literacy. It is worth noting that this protective effect is more significant in low SES populations, indicating that preventive healthcare behaviors may alleviate the adverse effects of low SES. This finding highlights a potential need to strengthen targeted health education for these populations. Similar trends have also been reported in two other studies on diseases by the UK Biobank (UKB) [44, 45].

In addition, this study revealed a significant bidirectional contradiction in the effect of chronic disease factors on health literacy. Although patients with hypertension, CAD, and diabetes demonstrated significantly higher chronic disease health literacy than non-patients, the presence of any chronic condition, when analyzed collectively, was independently associated with lower health literacy. This contradiction suggests that specific chronic diseases (such as hypertension and diabetes) may drive patients to actively acquire health knowledge through disease management needs (positive individual effect), while generalized chronic disease burdens (such as coexistence of multiple diseases and undiagnosed diseases) may lead to literacy decline (negative group effect) due to resource crowding and health fatigue [46]. In the mediation analysis, only hypertension showed a weak indirect effect, while other chronic diseases (such as coronary heart disease, cerebrovascular disease) and chronic diseases as a whole did not pass the mediation test. These findings suggest that the impact of chronic diseases on health literacy is primarily direct rather than an indirect pathway through SES or behavioral factors. Furthermore, the prevalence of chronic diseases was significantly higher in the low-SES group, yet health literacy was lower than in the high-SES group. This pattern suggests that the interaction between chronic diseases and SES may exacerbate health inequalities. Specifically, low-SES patients may experience a potential cycle of “high disease burden and low health literacy,” whereas high-SES patients may benefit from a reinforcing cycle of “strong disease management capacity and high health literacy”.

We explored the potential mediating mechanism between SES and chronic disease health literacy from the perspectives of preventive healthcare and chronic comorbidities. This study has several strengths. First, standardized variable definitions and measurement methods were adopted to enhance consistency. Second, the use of multidimensional composite scores for SES and preventive healthcare provided a more comprehensive characterization of these constructs than reliance on single indicators. Third, the stratified analysis helped identify high-risk subgroups, which could inform the development of targeted prevention and control strategies.

There are several limitations to this study: firstly, chronic disease diagnosis is based on self-reported questionnaires, which may be influenced by reporting bias. Participants may not be able to accurately recall or report their disease status, leading to potential underreporting or misreporting. Secondly, the use of a 12-month recall period for preventive health check-ups, while a standard methodology to minimize recall bias, may not fully capture the health service utilization behaviors of all individuals. Furthermore, our assessment was confined to five specific chronic disease categories and did not encompass other major conditions, such as mental health disorders or chronic musculoskeletal diseases. Future research may consider using more objective chronic disease diagnostic methods, employing different recall timeframes to validate and compare findings, and expanding the scope of research factors to gain a more comprehensive understanding of the complex relationships involved. In addition, longitudinal studies will help track changes in chronic disease literacy over time and examine causal relationships between factors. Finally, the generalizability of our findings may be limited as our sample was drawn exclusively from rural areas of Shandong Province. Although Shandong is a major agricultural province, regional variations across China’s vast rural landscape mean that our results may not be generalizable to all rural Chinese populations.

Conclusion

In summary, this study contributes to a deeper understanding of the multidimensional mechanisms by which SES influences chronic disease health literacy, providing evidence to inform the development of targeted intervention strategies. Government departments should focus on enhancing the chronic disease prevention and control capabilities of vulnerable groups through measures such as conducting health education and providing training resources. Subsequent research should further deepen the exploration of mechanisms and develop effective strategies for improving health literacy to reduce the burden of chronic diseases.
