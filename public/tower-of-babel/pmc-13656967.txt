Right ventricular remodeling after veno-venous extracorporeal membrane oxygenation bridge to lung transplantation in adults with severe lung disease
Authors: Christian O’Donnell, Bettia Celestin, Jose Rios-Monterrosa, Giovanni Perottino, Hee Do Jeon, Katherine E Kent, Louise Y Sun, Kiran K Khush, Francois Haddad, Melissa A Vogelsong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRight ventricular (RV) dysfunction is a common complication of advanced lung disease, yet data on RV remodeling and long-term outcomes following veno-venous extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VV-ECMO) as a bridge to lung transplantation remain limited.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study of consecutive adult patients bridged to lung transplant on VV-ECMO from January 1, 2015, to December 31, 2022. Transthoracic echocardiograms were analyzed pre-transplant (on VV-ECMO) and post-transplant in the “early” (≤3 months) and “late” (3–12 months) phases. Within-patient changes in echocardiographic parameters were assessed using paired nonparametric tests. The association between pre-transplant RV systolic function, quantified by RV fractional area of change (RVFAC), and 3-year post-transplant survival was evaluated using Cox proportional hazards modeling.ResultsTwenty patients were included (65.0% male, median age 47.5 [IQR: 41.5–56.4]) with primary etiologies of interstitial lung disease (50.0%) and COVID-19 (30.0%). In the “early” post-transplant phase, median RV systolic pressure decreased from 70.0 to 31.2 mm Hg (p < 0.001) and RVFAC increased from 25.9% to 31.9% (p = 0.004), whereas tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion decreased from 15.0 to 10.3 mm (p < 0.001). RV and right atrial dilation improved, while RV free-wall thickness remained unchanged (5.4–5.8 mm, p = 0.601). Among patients with greater baseline RV dysfunction, substantial functional and structural recovery was observed at 1-year. Three-year survival was 85.0% and preoperative RV dysfunction was not associated with mortality (HR: 0.96 [95% CI: 0.14–6.87]).ConclusionsMeaningful RV recovery and favorable 3-year survival appear achievable after VV-ECMO bridge to transplantation, even among patients with moderate-to-severe pre-transplant RV dysfunction.Keywords: right ventricle, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO), lung transplantation, echocardiography, RV remodeling

Background

Right ventricular (RV) dysfunction is a common consequence of severe lung disease that portends a poor outcome.1, 2, 3, 4 The degree of RV systolic dysfunction resulting from pulmonary hypertension (PH) associated with lung disease—World Health Organization (WHO) Group 3 PH—is typically more severe than that noted in other forms of PH with similar pulmonary pressures.3, 4 This disproportionate RV dysfunction associated with Group 3 PH has been attributed to chronic myocardial hypoxia, hyperinflammatory endothelial damage, and older age with more cardiovascular comorbidities.4, 5 However, little is known about RV recovery from Group 3 PH, as treatment options are limited and few studies have examined reverse remodeling after lung transplantation.2

Meanwhile, use of veno-venous extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VV-ECMO) for refractory respiratory failure has expanded significantly.6, 7 The severity of lung disease and the often prolonged—sometimes weeks or months—duration of support place these patients at increased risk for RV dysfunction, which has been reported in up to 40% of patients on VV-ECMO and is independently associated with increased mortality.8, 9, 10 Characterizing the degree of RV dysfunction and its potential for recovery is therefore critical when weighing isolated lung versus combined heart-lung transplantation in this population.

Existing studies in non-ECMO cohorts are mixed with regards to the association between preoperative RV dysfunction and postoperative outcomes following lung transplantation.11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17 Current literature focuses primarily on pediatric patients and those with Group 1 PH, suggesting that decreases in pulmonary vascular resistance after lung transplant alone allow for reverse remodeling with improvement in RV chamber size and contractile function over time.18, 19, 20, 21 However, less is known about RV remodeling in adult patients post-transplant for severe lung disease, particularly among those bridged on VV-ECMO.

Therefore, the aim of this study is to quantify RV remodeling and functional recovery among critically ill patients bridged to bilateral lung transplant on VV-ECMO and to evaluate how preoperative RV function impacts post-transplant survival. We hypothesized that RV function would improve within 3-months post-transplant and that better preoperative RV function would be associated with favorable long-term outcomes.

Materials and methods

Study design

This retrospective cohort study investigated consecutive adult (≥18 years) patients with respiratory failure who were bridged on VV-ECMO to bilateral lung transplant from January 1, 2015 to December 31, 2022 at a single academic tertiary care hospital, followed for 3 years post-transplant. All adult patients who were successfully bridged to lung transplant and who had transthoracic echocardiographic (TTE) images available while on VV-ECMO prior to and within 12-months after transplant were included. Exclusion criteria included previous lung transplant, addition of an RV mechanical assist device, conversion to or addition of arterial ECMO support pre-transplant, or the absence of echocardiographic images of sufficient quality to obtain measurement of RV fractional area of change (RVFAC) determined by a board-certified cardiologist (B.E.C.). All transplants were performed on cardiopulmonary bypass following conversion from VV-ECMO. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB #68691).

Data collection

Demographic and clinical data were extracted from electronic medical records, including comorbidities, etiology of lung disease, most recent pulmonary function test results, and ventilatory and oxygenation parameters prior to ECMO cannulation. VV-ECMO configuration and duration of support prior to transplantation were recorded, as were post-transplant need for VV-ECMO, ICU and hospital length of stay, survival to hospital discharge, and 1- and 3-year survival.

Echocardiographic acquisition and evaluation

Pre-transplant echocardiographic measurements were obtained from the last TTE available while on VV-ECMO prior to lung transplant. An “early” post-transplant evaluation was performed using the last TTE available within the first 3-months (≤3 months) post-transplant and a “late” phase examination using the last TTE exam 3–12-months post-transplant (3–12 months), which enables consistent comparison with prior literature on RV reverse remodeling following lung transplantation that have utilized similar definitions.11, 18, 20, 21, 22 All echocardiographic images were acquired by registered sonographers on Philips EPIQ 7 or Philips IE33 systems as part of routine care. Structural and functional echocardiographic measurements were obtained using TomTec Software (ARENA TTA2, LOT 30.02), in accordance with American Society of Echocardiography guidelines, by a single board-certified cardiologist (B.E.C) who was blinded to the original reads and sequence of TTEs.23, 24 Measurements obtained included right atrial (RA) and left atrial (LA) volumes (cm3), RA and LA Lagrangian strain (%), RV systolic pressure (RVSP, mm Hg), RVFAC (%), basal and mid-cavity RV internal diameter in diastole (RVIDd, mm), RV free-wall thickness (mm), tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE, mm), tricuspid annular diameter (mm), tricuspid regurgitation (TR) grade, left ventricular (LV) ejection fraction (LVEF, %), LV eccentricity index (LVEi), and LV global longitudinal strain (LVGLS, %). RV free-wall strain (RVFWS, %) was calculated using a previously validated manual Lagrangian method,23, 25, 26, 27 derived from the fractional change in RV free-wall length between end-diastole and end-systole. This length-based geometric approach was initially adopted due to the unavailability of the automated speckle-tracking echocardiography (STE) module on our Tomtec workstation. Importantly, manual Lagrangian strain serves as a highly robust alternative to STE, particularly in acute respiratory failure or post-lung transplant where near-field artifacts and suboptimal echogenicity frequently cause automated RV tracking to fail. Upon subsequent integration of the STE module, an internal methodological validation was performed on a subset of 34 patients with measurable STE RV global longitudinal strain. The manual Lagrangian measurements demonstrated excellent correlation and absolute agreement with automated STE (Supplemental Figure 2), confirming its validity and interchangeability as a measure of longitudinal deformation in our cohort. Echocardiographic metrics of RV to pulmonary artery (RV-PA) coupling were assessed as RVFAC/RVSP, RVFWS/RVSP and TAPSE/RVSP.

Outcomes

Cardiopulmonary bypass has been associated with a documented shift in RV contractile pattern from longitudinal to transverse shortening, making longitudinal measures such as RVFWS and TAPSE potentially more susceptible to post-bypass changes, while RVFAC may better reflect global RV function during this period.28, 29, 30, 31, 32, 33, 34, 35 Thus, change from baseline to ‘early phase’ RVFAC was chosen a priori as the primary study endpoint. Secondary outcomes included 3-year post-transplant all-cause mortality and longitudinal change in other echocardiographic measures of right- and left-sided cardiac structure and function during the “early” and “late” post-transplant periods.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized using median (interquartile range [IQR]) and categorical variables as frequency (proportion). Pre- versus “early” and “late” post-transplant TTE measures were compared using paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test for continuous variables and paired McNemar-Bowker testing for categorical variables. Patients with TTE data in both the ‘early’ and ‘late’ phases were additionally analyzed using repeated measures ANOVA (continuous variables) and Stuart-Maxwell testing (categorical variables) to assess overall change over time. All patients had measurable RVFAC. Patients missing secondary endpoints were excluded from the corresponding secondary analyses.

Patients were followed for 3 years post-transplant and survival was modeled using Cox proportional hazards and described in Kaplan-Meier survival curves. A prespecified subgroup analysis compared pre- to “early” post-transplant RV function among patients with “better” and “worse” baseline RV function, defined as RVFAC above or below the cohort median (26%). The association between preoperative RV dysfunction (RVFAC ≥26% vs <26%) and 3-year post-transplant survival was modeled using Cox proportional hazards regression. Other postoperative outcomes such as hospital and intensive care unit length of stay post-transplant, and 1-year mortality were compared between patients with RVFAC ≥26% versus <26% using Fisher’s exact or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. All analyses were performed in R (version 4.1.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Patient characteristics

During the study period, 20 patients were successfully bridged to bilateral lung transplantation on VV-ECMO with adequate pre- and “early” post-transplant TTE images and 11 had sufficient “late” TTE images available for analysis (Supplemental Figure 1). Patients were predominantly male (65.0%), with a median age of 47.5 years (IQR: 41.5–56.4) with the leading diagnoses included interstitial lung disease (ILD, 50.0%) and COVID-19 (30.0%) (Table 1). The majority (55.0%) of patients were receiving invasive mechanical ventilation at the time of ECMO cannulation with moderate hypoxemia (PaO2/FiO2: 130.9 [IQR: 76.5–191.7]), hypercapnia (PaCO2: 66.2 mm Hg [IQR: 51.7–94.2]), and elevated plateau pressures (31.5 cmH2O [IQR: 29.3–38.0]). Patients were supported on VV-ECMO for a median of 19.5 days (IQR: 12.5–31.5) prior to transplantation, the longest run being 110 days, with mostly femoral to right internal jugular cannula configuration (65.0%).

Echocardiography

Pre-lung transplant baseline

Pre-transplant TTEs were performed a median of 8 (IQR: 3.0–12.3) days before transplantation. The median RVSP was 70 (IQR: 55.0–77.0) mm Hg and 75.0% of patients demonstrated moderate or severely impaired RV function by RVFAC (≤29%)24 at baseline (median RVFAC, 25.9% [IQR: 21.3–28.2]) (Supplemental Table 1). Most patients also had mild to severe RV dilation (median basal RVIDd: 44.0 [IQR: 40.5–50.0]), mild hypertrophy (median free-wall thickness: 5.4 mm [IQR: 5.0–6.0]), septal flattening (80.0%), and moderate tricuspid regurgitation (Supplemental Table 1). All patients had preserved LVEF and normal LVGLS prior to transplant (Supplemental Table 1).

“Early” post-transplant remodeling

‘Early’ phase echocardiograms were obtained a median of 28.0 (IQR: 16.8–37.0) days after lung transplant. In the “early” phase after transplantation, RVFAC increased from 25.9% (IQR: 21.3–28.2) to 31.9% (IQR: 27.2–37.3) (p = 0.004) while RVSP decreased by a median of 38 mm Hg (p < 0.001, Figure 1a–b). RVFWS was unchanged while there was a significant decrease in TAPSE (15.0 mm [IQR: 12.0–19.2] to 10.3 mm [IQR: 9.5–11.9], p < 0.001) (Figure 1c–d). Structurally, there was a median reduction in RV dilation of 8 mm (basal RVIDd, 44.0 mm [IQR: 40.5–50.0] to 36.0 mm [IQR: 34.5–38.5], p < 0.001) but no change in RV hypertrophy (Figure 1e–f). Additional significant post-transplant reductions in interventricular septal flattening and tricuspid regurgitation are detailed in Supplemental Table 1.

In subgroup analysis stratified by the cohort-derived median pre-transplant RVFAC, patients with lower pre-transplant RVFAC (<26.0%) demonstrated significant improvement in RVFAC, RVFWS, tricuspid annular diameter, RA volume, and LVEi in those with lower RVFAC (Table 2). “Late” post-transplant remodeling

Eleven patients had adequate follow-up TTEs in the “late” phase of recovery at a median of 304 days (IQR: 185.0–332.5) after lung transplant. RVFAC remained above baseline over the first year after lung transplant (pre: 23.7% [IQR 19.4–26.9], early: 28.8% [IQR: 26.9–35.6], late: 31.5% [IQR: 30.0–34.6], p = 0.023) and RVSP remained decreased from pre-transplant values (pre: 70 mm Hg [IQR: 56.0–77.0], early: 31.0 mm Hg [IQR: 28.7–34.5], late: 32.0 mm Hg [IQR: 29.7–37.5], p < 0.001, Figure 2a–b). RVFWS increased significantly over the first year after lung transplant (pre: −15.0% [IQR: −11.8 to 17.0], early: −18.8% [IQR: −15.6 to 22.6], late: −19.2% [IQR: −17.4 to 21.3], p = 0.005) while TAPSE remained persistently low at 12-months (13.2 mm, IQR: 10.8–14.4) (Figure 2c–d). Structurally, the RV cavity size and hypertrophy remained unchanged (Figure 2e–f). Additional significant reductions in mid-cavitary RVIDd, LVEi, tricuspid annular diameter, and tricuspid regurgitation over the first year are listed in Supplemental Table 2.

RV-PA coupling

All echocardiographic measures of RV-PA coupling significantly improved by 3-months and 1-year post-transplant (Figure 3). The relative change in RV-PA coupling from pre-transplant was greatest in RVFAC/RVSP, followed by RVFWS/RVSP, and then TAPSE/RVSP.

Post-transplant clinical outcomes

Vital status was available for all patients at 3 years post-transplant. All patients survived to 1 year and 17 (85%) survived to 3 years post-transplant, with 1 death among patients with lower baseline RVFAC (<26%) and 2 deaths in the higher baseline RVFAC group (Table 3). Lower preoperative RVFAC was not associated with 3-year survival (hazard ratio: 0.96 [95% CI: 0.14–6.87], p = 0.971, Figure 4). Additionally, preoperative RVFAC was not associated with ICU or hospital length of stay post-transplant (Table 3).

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study of 20 patients with severe respiratory failure bridged on VV-ECMO to bilateral lung transplantation, we found significant improvements in RV structure and function as early as 3-months post-transplant, which persisted over the first year postoperatively. Echocardiographically, we show that after transplantation: (1) RVFAC increased significantly as RVSP decreased, (2) longitudinal contraction as measured by TAPSE decreased, (3) RA and RV dilation, septal morphology, and tricuspid regurgitation all improved while RV hypertrophy persisted, and (4) the relative improvement in RV-PA coupling was greatest for measures that incorporated radial free-wall motion. These improvements were further affirmed in half of the cohort with lower-than-median RV function (RVFAC <26%) pre-transplant, suggesting that even patients with moderate-to-severe RV dysfunction bridged on VV-ECMO have the potential for RV recovery post-transplant and favorable long-term survival.

Extracorporeal support has been increasingly used as a bridge for older, sicker patients until suitable lungs are available for transplantation.6 Rando et al. report that while still rare, the percentage of lung transplant recipients bridged on ECMO support has steadily increased since 2005, with almost 5% of cases in the United Network of Organ Sharing (UNOS) database in 2019 receiving extracorporeal support preoperatively.6 At our institution, 11.8% (40 of 338, Supplemental Figure 1) of lung transplant patients were bridged to transplant on ECMO from 2015 to 2022. Our outcomes thus add to the growing body of evidence that critically ill patients with end-stage lung disease may be successfully bridged on VV-ECMO to lung transplantation.

Whether a patient with RV dysfunction secondary to severe lung disease requires a combined heart-lung transplant or an isolated lung transplant is a multifactorial clinical decision without standardized thresholds. A key variable in this complex decision is the presumed ability of the right heart to recover function after lung transplantation and wide institutional variation exists.36 Historically, pulmonary arterial hypertension with RV dysfunction was treated with combined heart-lung transplantation but treatment strategies shifted in the 1990s to early 2000s after multiple studies showed favorable recovery of RV function following lung transplant alone.18, 21, 36, 37, 38, 39 However, the majority of these studies were in pediatric patients or those with precapillary PH, while there is limited data on adults with RV dysfunction undergoing lung transplantation for parenchymal or airway disease. Here we show promising RV recovery following lung transplantation in a cohort of patients with severe pulmonary disease requiring VV-ECMO and frequent inotropic support with a preoperative median RVSP of 70 mm Hg and moderate to severe RV dysfunction and dilation. The decision regarding the best transplant approach is complex and considers many other patient-specific factors, but our hypothesis-generating results suggest that these patients may have the ability to recover after isolated lung transplant, although further prospective studies are needed.

The improved radial contraction (RVFAC) without improvement in longitudinal shortening as measured by TAPSE noted in our study after lung transplant is consistent with the findings of a prior study of RV function pre- and post-transplant in a non-ECMO cohort by Kusunose et al.11 These similar results were found despite the ECMO-bridged patients in our study having more severe RV dysfunction at baseline (median RVFAC of 26% vs 40% in Kusunose et al.).11 Meanwhile, the persistently low TAPSE noted in our cohort is consistent with prior studies suggesting that TAPSE has low sensitivity for global RV function post-pericardiotomy.11, 40, 41, 42, 43, 44 This is thought to be related to the loss of structural pericardial support post-pericardiotomy that contributes to longitudinal motion, which is then supplemented by increased free-wall radial and circumferential contraction—reflected by RVFAC—to maintain RV systolic function.40, 45

Mechanistically, Hansmann et al.,38 provide a deeper understanding of this post-transplant phenomenon using cardiac magnetic resonance strain imaging revealing significant improvement in RV radial and circumferential strain as early as 2-months after lung transplant alongside decreases in RV longitudinal strain. Several studies have demonstrated similar findings following cardiopulmonary bypass, noting decreases in markers of longitudinal contraction, such as TAPSE or RVFWS, with preserved markers of global RV function, such as 3-dimensional RVEF and RVFAC.28, 29, 30, 31, 32, 33, 34 Kuwajima et al. further showed in a small post-surgical cohort that RVFWS recovered in patients after 1 year, while TAPSE remained reduced in this group, suggesting that these measures may have different sensitivities for different aspects of RV function and recovery.33 Our study showed similar findings, with significant reduction in TAPSE and no change in RVFWS at 3 months despite an increase in RVFAC along with improvement in RVFWS but not TAPSE by 12 months. However, it should be noted that 3D RVEF was not measured nor was the study adequately powered to assess the relationship between individual echocardiographic measures and patient outcomes.

RV hypertrophy is a common compensatory mechanism in severe lung disease5 and the majority of our cohort had at least mild RVH pre-transplant. While most markers of RV function improved as early as 3 months post-transplant, mild RV hypertrophy persisted out to a year. The varying timescales of recovery likely relate to their mechanisms. The rapid reduction in RV afterload immediately following lung transplant may allow for restoration of normal ventriculoarterial coupling between the RV and pulmonary circulation and the early improvement in RV function observed, as suggested by the significant early improvements in measures of RV-PA coupling.46, 47 Meanwhile, the combination of relative myocardial ischemia resulting from compensatory hypertrophy and the chronic hypoxic and inflammatory state in the patient with respiratory failure and group 3 PH can lead to cellular and neurohormonal changes that in turn require slower cellular remodeling processes to resolve.46, 48, 49 However, case series by Gortner et al. 19 and Sarashina et al.21 suggests that RV hypertrophy does continue to recover over time post-transplant, while Hansmann et al.38 showed that RV free-wall thickness decreased as early as 2-months in a pediatric cohort, potentially suggesting a relationship between age or disease duration and reverse remodeling that this study was underpowered to investigate.

Limitations

This retrospective observational study is limited by a small sample size of a clinically heterogenous population at a single center, thus limiting generalizability. Due to the small study cohort, multiple comparisons were not adjusted for, increasing potential for a type I error. Excluding 5 patients that required the addition of an RV assist device or arterial ECMO support selected for patients with less hemodynamically significant preoperative cor pulmonale. Therefore, we cannot draw any conclusions regarding the potential for RV recovery in this more extreme subset of patients. Moreover, the study was not powered to detect relationships between echocardiographic findings and mortality, therefore these findings should be considered hypothesis-generating. The inclusion of patients with both subacute and chronic respiratory failure may have masked differences in post-transplant RV adaptation depending on the disease process and chronicity of pulmonary pathology, where acute inflammatory processes may differ. Additionally, 50% of patients prior to transplant and 20% of patients during the “early” post-transplant period echocardiogram were receiving positive pressure ventilation, which may have altered echocardiographic assessments of function compared to non-ventilated patients. Similarly, all transplants were performed on cardiopulmonary bypass, which is known to affect echocardiographic measures of RV function as described above. Thus, these findings may differ among patients who undergo transplant on ECMO or off-pump. Pre-ECMO right heart catheterization data was not available, therefore the etiology of the observed pulmonary hypertension cannot be definitively determined. Additionally, in the absence of 3D RVEF assessment, the increase in RVFAC despite decrease in TAPSE may reflect a redistribution of RV contraction rather than true improvement in global RV function. However, the development of PH and RV dysfunction is exceptionally common among patients supported on VV-ECMO and associated with worse outcomes, making our findings encouraging regardless of etiology. Lastly, we were not able to perform any risk adjustment due to the small cohort size and event rates, which reinforce the exploratory nature of our study.

Conclusion

This retrospective observational study of a small sample of critically ill patients bridged to lung transplantation on VV-ECMO revealed promising improvements in RV function and structure as early as 3-months post-transplant, which persisted up to 1-year post-transplant. Meaningful RV recovery appears possible even among patients with lower preoperative RV function and worse preoperative RV function was not associated with mortality. The overall 3-year survival of 85% provides exploratory evidence for the use of VV-ECMO for bridging patients with severe lung disease to transplantation, but further larger prospective trials are needed to validate these findings.
