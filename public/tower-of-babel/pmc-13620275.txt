“Accessible and Acceptable” Drop-In Mental Health Services for Children With Long-Term Conditions: A Mixed-Methods Exploratory Analysis of Integrated Services From a Health Equity Perspective
Authors: Émer Kenny, Anna Roach, Nyah Patel, Sophie Bennett, Isobel Heyman, Anna Coughtrey, Roz Shafran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionChildren and young people (CYP) with long-term physical health conditions (LTCs) are at increased risk of emotional and behavioural difficulties, yet barriers to psychological support remain, particularly for families from ethnic minority backgrounds. Mental health drop-in services were set up and evaluated at 6 paediatric healthcare settings, aiming to provide a more accessible route to care.MethodsThis study conducted a secondary analysis of quantitative and qualitative data collected as part of the multi-site roll-out of mental health drop-in services in paediatric healthcare settings to explore their accessibility and acceptability. The services provided short-term psychological interventions delivered by child wellbeing practitioners or equivalent, under supervision of a clinical psychologist 120 families provided quantitative data, and 104 families shared qualitative feedback. Differences in access routes and mental health symptoms were compared across White British families and those from different ethnicities. Inductive, latent content analysis of the open text feedback was conducted to understand families’ experiences of the drop-in services.ResultsCYP from ethnic minority backgrounds were over-represented in the present study (32%), when compared with nationwide CAMHs users that identify as non-white British (21%). Families from ethnic minority backgrounds reported comparable mental health outcomes and satisfaction levels to White British families. There was a significant difference in mental health change scores, with ethnic minority families showing greater improvements after accessing the service, t (46) = −3.05, p < .01, d = −1.08. Qualitative analysis found that the services were highly acceptable across ethnicities, highlighting themes of life-changing support, therapeutic relationships, and improved parent and family wellbeing.DiscussionDrop-in services embedded in paediatric healthcare settings are both accessible and acceptable, demonstrating positive clinical outcomes, and positive family feedback across different ethnic groups. This model may help reduce inequalities by improving access to psychological support for ethnic minority families with CYP living with LTCs.

Introduction

Approximately 1.7 million (23%) of children and young people (CYP) in England live with a long-term physical health condition (LTC) (Brooks et al., 2015; NICE, 2020). LTCs can be defined as physical health conditions for which a cure is unlikely, that require ongoing management and increased use of health services (Moore et al., 2019). CYP with LTCs are significantly more likely to develop mental health symptoms than their healthy peers, with research suggesting around 50% of CYP with LTCs meet diagnostic criteria for at least one mental health disorder, compared with around 16% of the general population (Butler et al., 2018). CYP with LTCs also report increased loneliness, decreased social functioning and reduced health-related quality of life (Bateman et al., 2023; Hall et al., 2019; McGlone and Long, 2020). These adverse outcomes are also reported by their wider families, with siblings and parents of children with LTCs facing increased stress alongside mental and physical health difficulties (Cohn et al., 2020; McKenzie Smith et al., 2018).

Previous research has indicated that individuals from ethnic minority backgrounds are at higher risk of developing multiple LTCs, that such conditions develop earlier when compared with the majority white population in the UK, and that these families report greater difficulties accessing support (Ekezie et al., 2023; Hayanga et al., 2024). In this paper, the term ethnic minority 1 refers to all ethnic groups except the white British group” (GOV.UK, 2021). Additional research suggests that ethnic minority families may be at an increased risk of developing mental health problems (Bamford et al., 2021) and show poorer treatment outcomes and reduced access to support (Cooper et al., 2013; Ruphrect-Smith et al., 2023).

Poor access to healthcare reported by families from ethnic minority backgrounds is consistent with the wider challenge of accessing evidence-based treatment for CYP with mental health disorders and LTCs despite evidence-based treatments showing effectiveness for this population (Catanzano et al., 2020). LTCs often exacerbate common barriers to accessing care, with families facing heightened healthcare costs and logistical issues due to the burden of navigating physical and mental healthcare systems simultaneously (Byrne et al., 2023; Macdonald et al., 2021; Naylor et al., 2012). Physical health and mental health services often operate separately, with different referral routes, staff and locations (King et al., 2024). These challenges are likely compounded for CYP from ethnic minority backgrounds, who experience additional, significant barriers accessing healthcare services – such as structural inequalities, cultural stigma and discrimination in healthcare (Danso & Danso, 2021; Garney et al., 2021; Nwokoroku et al., 2022). It is reported that CYP from ethnic minority backgrounds are less likely to contact a general practitioner regarding their mental health, or to receive primary mental healthcare support (Kapadia et al., 2022), with families reporting preferences of accessing support in places they regularly frequent, such as community centres or schools (Coelho et al., 2022).

Such barriers may be overcome by providing multiple entry points to support (i.e. clinician or self-referral), in a more-familiar environment.

One way of increasing access to psychological interventions for CYP with LTCs more widely is to integrate mental health support within physical health settings. A drop-in service, named “The Lucy Project”, was initially trialled at a specialist paediatric hospital which provided ‘low intensity’ psychological interventions such as guided self-help, signposting or referral to appropriate services (Catanzano et al., 2021; Shafran et al., 2021). Families could drop-in or self-refer, or could be referred to the services by their clinical team. This service was found to reduce emotional and behavioural symptoms and improve quality of life in young people with LTCs (Catanzano et al., 2021). It was also found to improve parental and sibling mental health (Bennett et al., 2021, 2023), was highly acceptable to families (Catanzano et al., 2023) and was cost effective (Clarke et al., 2022). There was initial evidence that the drop-in model improved accessibility to mental health support for families from ethnic minority backgrounds (Catanzano, Bennett, Kerry, et al., 2021) as families from Asian and Black backgrounds were over-represented in the study sample when compared to nationwide data from child and adolescent mental health services (CAMHS). Taken together, these findings suggest that integrated care via a drop-in service may be effective in increasing access to mental health support for ethnic minority families with LTCs.

This model of drop-in services, offering low intensity mental health support at point of need was subsequently adopted at six paediatric healthcare settings across England (Roach et al., 2024). An evaluation of 120 participants found significant reductions in emotional and behavioural symptoms (p < 0.01; Cohen’s d = 0.39) and improved quality of life (p < 0.01; Cohen’s d = 0.44) across CYP and their parents (Roach et al., 2025).

The aim of the current study was to use the data from the national roll-out study (Roach et al., 2025) to further understand both the accessibility and acceptability of the drop-in model, both of which are critical for implementing new services (Peterson et al., 2023). Accessibility, defined as the opportunity to reach and avail of services during situations of high need (Levesque et al., 2013), is particularly important to explore from the perspective of families from ethnic minority backgrounds, as adverse experiences with referral routes have been linked to poorer treatment outcome experiences and longer durations of untreated disorders (Clement et al., 2015; Hauenstein et al., 2019; Ruphrect-Smith et al., 2023). Therefore, we first sought to understand who accessed the service, specifically considering differences in access routes and mental health symptoms reported by families from different ethnic groups. Second, we explored how families perceived the service, investigating acceptability through understanding families’ qualitative experiences of the drop-in services.

Methods

Participants

The present research was part of a larger prospective single-arm, multi-site interventional trial (ISRCTN15063954) where families were recruited across six NHS sites in England: Cambridge and Peterborough Trust, University College London Hospital, Hinchingbrooke Hospital, Sheffield Children’s Hospital, Leeds Teaching Hospital and Peterborough City Hospital. The study protocol, and main evaluation paper provide further information on recruitment, methods and outcome measures (Roach et al., 2024, 2025).

Study participants had to be a patient, aged between 0-25 years, at a participating service for at least 6 months, or be a carer/family member of such patient. CYP and their families could self-refer to the service using the QR codes or email address located on posters at participating sites. Alternatively, CYP and families could also be referred by their clinician or direct care team, who, upon receiving their consent to contact, could share contact details with the study team.

Intervention

CYP and their families were offered an initial assessment within two weeks after consenting, to ensure “drop-in” support and timely access to treatment. Following the assessment and discussion with a supervisor, CYP with mild to moderate emotional and/or behavioural difficulties were offered low intensity CBT interventions (for anxiety, low mood or challenging behaviour). If these interventions were not clinically appropriate (e.g. if their concern surrounded adjustment to new condition or a bereavement) individuals were referred to other therapy modalities (such as narrative therapy, family therapy or counselling) or signposted towards other services (e.g. a local support group or charity). Parents accessing help for their own mental health were offered a one-off CBT session or referred or signposted for further support.

Measures

Demographic data were collected from 120 families (120 CYP and 94 parents) at baseline. Ethnicity, gender and first language spoken were self-reported for CYP aged 12 years and older. Parents reported ethnicity for CYP under 12 years old and self-reported their own ethnicity, gender, first language and number of CYP in their household. UK census categories from 2021 were used (Office for National Statistics, 2022) 2 .

Four mental health outcome measures were collected and are detailed below. Self-reported and parent-reported measures were used depending on age of the CYP.(1) The Strengths and Difficulties questionnaire (SDQ) is a 25-item survey used to measure common emotional and behavioural symptoms in young people (Goodman, 1997, 1999, Goodman and Goodman, 2011). It assesses five subscales: prosocial, hyperactivity, peer problems, emotional problems and conduct problems, and calculates a ‘total difficulties’ score. Each statement is rated on a 3-point Likert Scale of 0 (not true), 1 (somewhat true) and 2 (certainly true). The total difficulties score of the SDQ ranges from 0 to 40 and total scores of 17 and above are above clinical threshold on the parent-reported measures, and 20 and above for self-reported total scores. The SDQ has shown moderate test-retest reliability and good concurrent, convergent and discriminant validity (Goodman and Goodman, 2011). Each question asks how much a problem something is and is scored on a 5-point Likert scale from: 0 (never) to 4 (almost always). Scores are then transformed on a scale of 0-100 where higher scores indicate better quality of life. Total scores range from 0-21, and a higher score indicates greater symptoms of anxiety. Scores above 10 are considered to be in the clinical range. Mental health outcome measures were completed at baseline and 6 months follow-up. They were collected face to face, over the phone or online by a researcher not involved in the delivery of the intervention.

At follow up, the modified Client Satisfaction Questionnaire (CSQ-8) was also used. This is a widely used measure of service satisfaction in mental health services and was adapted for use in the previous drop-in centre study (Catanzano et al., 2021; Miglietta et al., 2018) 3 . There were 7 items in total and responses were scored on a five-point scale of 1 = ‘not at all’, 2 = ‘only a little’, 3 = ‘somewhat’, 4 = ‘quite a bit’ and 5 = ‘totally’. Higher scores indicate greater satisfaction. CYP aged 12 years and older, and parents of CYP of all ages were asked to complete the CSQ-8 at follow up.

Qualitative feedback was also collected at this time via open text feedback. CYP aged 12 years and older, and parents of all CYP were asked three optional open-ended questions at the end of the follow-up survey.(1) If the information and support you/your child received made a difference to you/your child’s mental or physical health, please give details here(2) What was most helpful about the information and support you received?(3) Please use the following space to share your experience of the Lucy Project and give any feedback.

Ethics

Written informed consent was obtained for all participants aged 16 and above (who had capacity to consent). In the case of children under the age of 16 years, assent was obtained alongside parental consent. To protect participant data, all survey answers were anonymous, and data stored on a password-protected computer.

Patient and Public Involvement

Throughout the study the Great Ormond Street Hospital Young People’s Advisory Group (YPAG) and Cambridge Parents’ Advisory Group were consulted and advised on the patient facing materials, online questionnaires and dissemination.

Data Analysis

Data were analysed using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences, SPSS version 27.0 (IBM Corp, 2020). Analyses for between-group differences for ethnic minority and white British families were conducted for all mental health outcomes at baseline, follow up and the difference in scores before and after accessing the integrated services. Service satisfaction scores obtained at follow up, and referral pathways were also compared. T-tests or Wilcoxon signed rank tests (for non-parametric data) were used to compare the independent groups and converted into standardised effect sizes (Cohen’s d). Chi-squared analysis were conducted when comparing categorical variables.

Qualitative data were analysed using traditional inductive latent content analysis methods (Graneheim et al., 2017). The qualitative analysis process was carried out using the NVIVO software system, version 14 (Allsop et al., 2022; Lumivero, 2023).

The primary researcher, EK, first familiarised herself with the data and decontextualised findings. Responses were then divided into ‘meaning units’, individual sections based on response’s underlying meaning, as defined by the researcher (Lindgren et al., 2020). Using open coding each meaning unit was assigned a code that captured their overall concept. A coding list with illustrative examples was developed and checked against the original data to ensure consistency in coding, available in Appendix A (Bengtsson, 2016). Codes were then merged into subthemes and, through comparison with the data and quantitative findings, synthesised into overarching themes.

Reflexivity

Reflexivity was emphasised throughout the analysis process. EK, a non-clinical MSc researcher, conducted a secondary analysis of the data, and was not involved in the initial data collection or intervention delivery process. The second senior researcher, AR, a doctoral student was directly involved in data collection and intervention delivery under the supervision of SB and RS, clinical psychologists. EK identifies as White Irish and AR as White British.

Regular meetings were held between EK and AR at the construction of subthemes and themes stage. EK’s analytic distance from the study supported her ability to focus on positive and critical feedback equally, while AR’s involvement in the study provided contextual insight and deeper knowledge of the topic. Reflexive discussions were regularly held between researchers, to critically consider how their perspectives, including their positionality as White researchers, might influence analysis. During meetings subthemes and themes were re-appraised to ensure they were reflective of the data, and researchers compared their interpretations of the data. Following agreement from both researchers, EK re-applied the final themes to the data to check fit.

Discussion

The main aim of this study was to understand whether drop-in services are an accessible and acceptable way of providing psychological support for CYP with LTCs and their families. To achieve this, the research examined ethnic differences in service usage and outcomes, alongside qualitative feedback on families’ experiences of the service. Results indicated that drop-in services at paediatric healthcare settings are both accessible and acceptable.

To explore accessibility, mental health symptoms, access routes and service satisfaction were compared across the different ethnic groups. Results indicated that families from ethnic minority backgrounds did not significantly differ from White British families in measures of emotional and behavioural symptoms, anxiety or depression at baseline, 6-month follow up, or when comparing differences before and after accessing the drop-in services. Additionally, there was no significant difference in satisfaction scores when comparing these groups. Overall, significant improvements in emotional and behavioural symptoms, reductions in anxiety and depression, alongside high degrees of satisfaction with the service, were observed across ethnic backgrounds.

The only significant mental health difference identified between the ethnic groups was the parent-reported quality of life at all time points. At baseline, parents from ethnic minority backgrounds reported significantly lower quality of life scores for their child when compared to White British parents. At follow up, this changed, and parents from ethnic minority families reported a significantly higher quality of life for their child. There may be some reasons for this, firstly the baseline measure may reflect that initially these CYP may have started with a higher burden of unmet needs, delayed diagnosis, or untreated symptoms. Regarding the positive improvements over 6 months, this could reflect the benefit of the intervention itself (even if starting from a lower baseline), and/or the benefit of being able to access any support, where previously this was difficult. Furthermore, as no differences were found when comparing self-reported quality of life, it is difficult to determine whether lower parent-reported PedsQL scores are a true reflection of CYP well-being. Research has previously shown that discrepancies between parent and CYP reported measures are pronounced in ethnic minority groups (Roberts et al., 2005).

When referral routes were compared between White British families and those from global majorities, there were significantly more White British families who came via self-referral, in line with previous IAPT research (Harwood et al., 2023). This may reflect differences in awareness of available services, with White British families perhaps more familiar with different options, or could be attributed to different cultural perceptions of help-seeking (Bhavsar et al., 2021; Memon et al., 2016). Asking for help may be perceived as a sign of failure or weakness, and thus directly self-referring for support may not feel comfortable (Jacobs & Pentaris, 2021).

When compared with nationwide CAMHS data, families from non- White British, ethnic minority backgrounds were over-represented in recruitment. It is reported elsewhere that 21% of CAMHS users identify as being from non White-British backgrounds (NIHR PenARC, 2022), whereas this study had a higher proportion of CYP from ethnic minority groups (32%). This is in line with findings from the original drop-in service study at one hospital, which included 39% of their sample from ethnic minority families (Catanzano et al., 2021), and may suggest that the drop-in model as an effective way to increase access to mental health support amongst ethnic minority populations. As explored above, this may not be explained solely via the self-referral option (as this study found significantly more White British families joining the study via this route). This could instead highlight other advantages of this integrated model such as awareness and support from physical health teams to share information and refer families directly and the flexibility around intervention modality to suit family’s needs (e.g. sessions could take place online to reduce in-person visits). The higher proportion of ethnic minority CYP using the service may also reflect the higher likelihood of CYP in this group to develop LTCs compared to those from White-British backgrounds (Ekezie et al., 2023; Hayanga et al., 2024).

Acceptability

To explore acceptability, qualitative feedback from open-text questions were analysed and satisfaction scores were compared. There were no differences between the ethnic minority and White British groups when comparing service satisfaction from both CYP and parent participants. This is particularly promising as previous research has associated ethnicity with lower levels of physical and mental healthcare satisfaction compared to their white counterparts (Cai & Robst, 2016; Pinder et al., 2016). When analysing the open-text feedback, families from all cultural backgrounds reported feeling grateful for the service, and described it as life-changing, helpful and supportive.

CYP from ethnic minority backgrounds highlighted the benefits of low intensity CBT related processes, such as breathing techniques and psychoeducation, which normalised experiences and promoted self-regulation. Such approaches may have helped ethnic minority CYP reduce self-stigma surrounding mental health. CYP across ethnicities also developed strong therapeutic relationships, and no references were made to cultural issues or lack of sensitivity from CBT practitioners, indicating a culturally competent service. Our findings demonstrate that therapeutic relationships are feasible to foster within a low intensity treatment model and aligns with previous findings that valued therapeutic relationships are important for positive mental health outcomes for CYP with LTCs (Ludlow et al., 2020; Moore et al., 2019; Shaw et al., 2019). Positive experiences with the low-intensity CBT process and improved mental health outcomes reported across ethnicities indicate low-intensity CBT is an acceptable and effective form of mental health care for CYP with LTCs, aligning with previous research (Catanzano et al., 2021a).

Findings revealed that drop-in services were perceived as an accessible form of mental health support for families, many of whom described having previously sought support elsewhere unsuccessfully. Timely access to care is crucial for CYP with LTCs, as long-wait times are associated with increased negative psychological outcomes such as self-harm, and risk of negative thought processes that one is not “deserving” of care (Jones et al., 2022; Punton et al., 2022). In this study CYP described mental health improvements primarily in terms of both their everyday functioning and general mindset.

Strengths and Limitations

This study has several strengths. It utilised a mixed-methods approach, capturing service outcomes and process factors, which are imperative to comprehensive service evaluation (Lindsay, 2013). The paper aimed to understand the accessibility and acceptability of these new services, both are key aspects of implementation (Peterson et al., 2023). Accessibility was best explored using the quantitative data (e.g. referral routes) and acceptability could be explored through analysing the open-text feedback. Mixed methods research offers a holistic insight into families’ experiences and can answer both “how” and “why” questions. This methodology was used to provide more depth and breadth, beyond what quantitative or qualitative data alone could provide, and is recommended for health research (Wasti et al., 2022).

The study included participants from different healthcare settings across England and had a high recruitment rate. Measures of mental health and satisfaction used standardised, validated questionnaires and where statistical differences were found between ethnic groups, the effect sizes were medium to large. This said, these findings should be interpreted with caution given the exploratory nature of the analysis and limited statistical power.

Further, all non-White British participants were grouped together for analysis, as the numbers in each ethnicity group was too small for meaningful comparisons. This may have concealed nuances in how different demographic groups both accessed psychological care, and the effect it had on mental health outcomes. In addition, the analysis did not correct for multiple comparisons and a formal power analysis was not completed as this was an exploratory study. It should also be acknowledged that participants may have been more motivated to complete follow up measures and provide feedback if they had positive experiences or received their favoured intervention through the project, creating potentially biased responses.

In addition, the majority of parental respondents were Mothers (92.6%), limiting the extent to which our findings can be applied to Fathers. While this is reflective of the wider research area, inclusion of Fathers’ experiences is important, due to the impact of having a child with a chronic condition on the entire family unit (Spurr et al., 2023).

Finally, as noted in the introduction, the term “global majority” is now greatly preferred as an inclusive, de-colonial alternative to “ethnic minority” (Lee et al., 2023). For this study, the way in which the comparisons were conducted meant that global majority was not an accurate or appropriate term to use, but future work should seek to use language that is more inclusive and preferred.

Conclusion

Overall, the study suggests that integrated mental health services delivering low intensity interventions are both accessible and acceptable to CYP with LTCs. Findings indicate that this model may be particularly effective in reaching families from ethnic minority groups within paediatric healthcare settings. There were similar mental health outcomes and high satisfaction among all demographic groups which supports the NHS strategy of integrating mental and physical healthcare. It is hoped that these findings can be shared widely so other settings may roll-out similar services to ensure CYP with LTCs receive the support they need regardless of ethnicity.
