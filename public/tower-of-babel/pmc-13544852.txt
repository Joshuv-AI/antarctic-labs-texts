Obstructing colonic involvement by gastric-origin adenocarcinoma during adjuvant chemotherapy after curative-intent gastrectomy: a case report with literature review
Authors: Mohammad B. Jahantab, Mahdokht Azizi, Mohammadamin Kashefi, Amirhossein Yousefian, Maral Mokhtari, Taha Negahdari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importance:Colonic involvement by gastric adenocarcinoma is rare and may mimic a new primary colorectal malignancy. Although clinicopathological assessment can support a gastric origin, it cannot reliably distinguish hematogenous or lymphatic dissemination and peritoneal implantation with secondary mural invasion.Presentation of case:A 52-year-old man underwent curative-intent total gastrectomy for distal gastric adenocarcinoma (pT4a pN2) in January 2024. Adjuvant folinic acid, fluorouracil, and oxaliplatin (FOLFOX) was initiated in February 2024. Approximately five months after the gastrectomy, while treatment was ongoing, he developed complete large-bowel obstruction due to a severe left-sided colonic stricture and underwent emergency left hemicolectomy. The resection contained two infiltrative lesions, including a dominant transmural lesion. Histology showed moderately differentiated adenocarcinoma. Immunohistochemistry showed CK7+, CK20+, CEA+, focal CDX2+, and SATB2−, favoring gastric-origin adenocarcinoma involving the colon over a conventional de novo colorectal primary. The route of colonic involvement remained indeterminate. The patient subsequently received palliative systemic treatment and died approximately 22 months after the initial diagnosis.Clinical discussion:This early, obstructing recurrence during active adjuvant therapy underscores the need to consider disease of gastric-origin in patients with a new colonic stricture after gastrectomy. Immunohistochemistry helps assign tumor origin, but it cannot establish the route of spread; microscopic peritoneal disease cannot be excluded despite the absence of macroscopic peritoneal disease at laparotomy.Conclusion:Gastric-origin adenocarcinoma should be considered in patients with previous gastric cancer who develop a colonic stricture. Accurate clinicopathological classification is essential for multidisciplinary treatment planning.

Introduction

Gastric cancer remains a major global health burden and an important cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide[1]. Despite curative-intent treatment, recurrence may occur through hematogenous, lymphatic, or peritoneal pathways, commonly involving the liver, peritoneum, lungs, and bones[2]. Colorectal involvement by gastric adenocarcinoma is uncommon but clinically important because it may mimic a conventional new primary colorectal malignancy[3–15].

Distinguishing a primary colorectal adenocarcinoma from a gastric-origin adenocarcinoma involving the colon can be challenging. Endoscopic diagnosis may be difficult when tumor predominantly involves deeper mural layers, while the mucosa is relatively preserved, resulting in limited, negative, or non-diagnostic superficial biopsy samples[3–5,9,15]. Immunohistochemistry can support the likely site of tumor origin when interpreted alongside morphology and clinical history; however, it cannot independently establish the route through which tumor reached the colon.

We report a patient who developed obstructive colonic involvement due to gastric-origin adenocarcinoma during adjuvant FOLFOX chemotherapy after curative-intent gastrectomy. This case report was prepared in accordance with the SCARE 2025 guideline[16].

Case presentation

A 52-year-old Iranian man with no significant past medical history or family history of malignancy presented in December 2023 with several months of progressive upper gastrointestinal symptoms, including epigastric discomfort, early satiety, intermittent nausea, fatigue, and unintentional weight loss.

Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy revealed an ulcero-infiltrative lesion in the distal stomach, and biopsy confirmed gastric adenocarcinoma. Contrast-enhanced staging computed tomography, performed in January 2024, showed focal distal gastric wall thickening with perigastric lymphadenopathy and no radiographic evidence of liver metastases, pulmonary metastases, ascites, or peritoneal dissemination.

Following multidisciplinary tumor-board discussion, the patient underwent open total gastrectomy with Roux-en-Y reconstruction in January 2024, with curative intent. No gross peritoneal seeding, ascites, or hepatic metastatic disease was identified intraoperatively.

Histopathological examination demonstrated well-differentiated adenocarcinoma (grade 1) with invasion through the gastric wall to the serosal surface, corresponding to pT4a disease (Fig. 1A). Adjuvant FOLFOX chemotherapy was initiated in February 2024.

In June 2024, approximately 5 months after gastrectomy and while adjuvant treatment was ongoing, the patient developed progressive constipation, increasing abdominal distension, intermittent colicky abdominal pain, nausea with occasional vomiting, inability to pass stool and flatus, generalized weakness, and poor oral intake.

Contrast-enhanced abdominal computed tomography demonstrated marked left-sided colonic wall thickening with severe luminal narrowing and substantial proximal colonic dilatation, including cecal dilatation. Colonoscopy showed a severe circumferential stricturing lesion involving the sigmoid and left colon, with broad, irregular, plaque-like ulcerated mucosa and marked distortion of the mucosal and vascular pattern. Given the limited superficial tissue sampling and the ulcerated inflammatory background, the findings were suspicious for adenocarcinoma but insufficient for definitive characterization; repeat or deeper tissue sampling was recommended.

Shortly afterward, the patient developed complete large-bowel obstruction requiring an urgent laparotomy. An emergency left hemicolectomy was performed.

The dominant lesion measured 3.5 × 2.8 × 1.8 cm and involved the full thickness of the colonic wall (Fig. 2A). Immunohistochemistry demonstrated positivity for CK7, CK20, and CEA, with focal positivity for CDX2 and negative staining for SATB2 (Fig. 3C–F). In the context of the previously resected gastric adenocarcinoma, this immunophenotype favored adenocarcinoma of gastric-origin involving the colon rather than a conventional de novo primary colorectal adenocarcinoma. Furthermore, the immunophenotype could not establish whether colonic involvement resulted from hematogenous spread, lymphatic spread, or peritoneal implantation with secondary serosal and mural invasion. Discussion

This case describes recurrent adenocarcinoma of gastric origin involving the colon during adjuvant FOLFOX chemotherapy after curative-intent gastrectomy. Its principal educational value is not that it demonstrates a specific route of spread, but rather that it highlights the need to consider recurrent gastric malignancy when a patient with a history of gastric cancer develops a new obstructing colorectal lesion.

Published evidence consists primarily of case reports, small series, and focused reviews[3–15]. A recent systematic review and a tertiary-center case series confirm that colorectal involvement of gastric cancer is rare, usually metachronous, clinically heterogeneous, and associated with unfavorable outcomes[3,4]. Metachronous colorectal involvement among the included cases occurred between approximately 7 months and 10 years after gastrectomy. In contrast, our patient developed symptomatic colonic involvement approximately 5 months after curative-intent gastrectomy, during ongoing adjuvant FOLFOX chemotherapy.

Reported pathological appearances ranged from polypoid or predominantly submucosal lesions to linitis-like full-thickness infiltration and serosal/subserosal outside-in involvement. Unlike the polypoid transverse-colon lesion reported by Shima et al[8], the dominant lesion in our case showed transmural involvement. This spectrum also explains why superficial endoscopic biopsy may underestimate or fail to establish the diagnosis of gastric-origin colorectal involvement when the mucosa is relatively preserved and the dominant tumor burden lies in the submucosa, muscularis propria, or serosa[5,9,15].

Similar diagnostic difficulty has been reported in published cases in which superficial colonoscopic biopsies were initially negative or nondiagnostic despite subsequent confirmation of adenocarcinoma of gastric-origin after surgical resection, particularly when the disease predominantly involved deeper mural or serosal layers[5,9,15]. When clinical and radiologic suspicion persists despite limited biopsy findings, repeat sampling, deeper tissue acquisition, where feasible, and multidisciplinary review should be considered.

Proposed mechanisms include hematogenous dissemination, lymphatic spread, and peritoneal implantation[2,4,12]. In this patient, the presence of a second, separate colonic lesion did not distinguish among hematogenous dissemination, lymphatic dissemination, and occult multifocal peritoneal implantation with secondary mural invasion. Nevertheless, negative cross-sectional imaging and the absence of macroscopic disease do not exclude small-volume or microscopic peritoneal dissemination, because computed tomography has limited sensitivity for peritoneal metastases in gastric cancer[17]. Accordingly, the most accurate description is colonic involvement by an adenocarcinoma of gastric-origin, with an indeterminate route of spread.

Conventional colorectal adenocarcinoma commonly shows CK20 and CDX2 expression and is frequently SATB2-positive, whereas gastric adenocarcinoma may show CK7 positivity with variable CK20 and CDX2 expression[18,19]. Conversely, CK20 positivity and focal CDX2 expression do not exclude gastric origin because intestinal differentiation and overlapping CK20/CDX2 expression may occur in upper gastrointestinal adenocarcinomas[18,19].

The occurrence of clinically significant recurrence during FOLFOX chemotherapy is another notable feature. Recent reviews describe multiple mechanisms of chemoresistance in gastric cancer, including altered cell-death pathways, drug transport and efflux, epithelial–mesenchymal transition, epigenetic regulation, cancer-stem-cell properties, and tumor-microenvironment effects[20,21]. However, this case cannot establish any specific mechanism because the exact treatment exposure and molecular tumor profiling were unavailable.

An emergency left hemicolectomy was performed for complete large-bowel obstruction and was not intended as a curative metastasectomy. In reported cases of gastric-origin colorectal involvement, surgery has generally been undertaken for obstruction relief, symptom control, or selected anatomically limited disease, while systemic treatment remains central to the management of recurrent gastric cancer[3,4,13].

Strengths and limitations

The main strength of this report is the detailed documentation of an uncommon and clinically consequential pattern of adenocarcinoma of gastric-origin involving the colon during adjuvant chemotherapy.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this is a single case report and cannot establish incidence, prognosis, causality, or an optimal management strategy. Second, the route of colonic involvement could not be determined. Third, paired immunohistochemical testing of the primary gastric and colonic lesions was unavailable. Fourth, molecular profiling data, including HER2 status, mismatch-repair or microsatellite-instability testing, PD-L1 expression, Epstein–Barr virus status, and next-generation sequencing, were unavailable in the accessible records. Finally, exact FOLFOX cycle dates, cumulative dose intensity, dose modifications, treatment interruptions, and details of subsequent systemic therapy could not be retrospectively reconstructed.

Conclusion

A new colorectal stricture in a patient with previous gastric cancer should prompt consideration of a recurrent adenocarcinoma of gastric origin involving the colon, even during adjuvant chemotherapy. Histopathology, immunohistochemistry, and clinical history can support the likely tumor origin and assist in distinguishing recurrent gastric disease from a conventional new primary colorectal adenocarcinoma. However, these methods cannot establish the route of colonic involvement. Accurate classification is important for multidisciplinary treatment planning, patient counseling, and selection of appropriate surgical and systemic management.
