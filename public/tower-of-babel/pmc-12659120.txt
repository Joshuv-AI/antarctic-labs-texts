Sleep quality and its relation to anthropometric and metabolic factors in overweight/obese patients with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease: a case-control study
Authors: Sara Arefhosseini, Shahrzad Tavakkoli, Helda Tutunchi, Majid Mobasseri, Mehrangiz Ebrahimi-Mameghani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo investigate whether poor sleep quality could predict non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD).ResultsIn this case-control study, sleep duration and quality were assessed in 72 overweight or obese patients with newly diagnosed NAFLD as “cases” compared with 72 age-and sex-matched adults without NAFLD as “controls”. Weight, height, and the circumferences of the waist, hip, and neck, as well as metabolic (glycemia, lipid profile, liver function biomarkers) and hematological factors (complete blood cell count), were assessed. Sleep quality was assessed using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), and the global PSQI score was classified into good sleep quality (< 6) and low sleep quality (≥ 6). Cases showed significantly greater body mass index (BMI), waist circumference, and waist-to-height ratio as well as serum levels of glucose, hemoglobin A1c, triglyceride, total cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, alanine and aspartate aminotransferases, and 25(OH)D than controls (p < 0.05). The PSQI score was significantly correlated with serum levels of high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, total iron-binding capacity, and 25(OD in patients with NAFLD. Although age and BMI were more likely to be associated with NAFLD (p = 0.042 and p < 0.001, respectively), no association was found for PSQI score as well as its components in NAFLD odds, after adjusting for the confounders.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13104-025-07573-w.Keywords: Sleep quality, Metabolic factors, Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease

Introduction

Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), strongly related to unhealthy lifestyle habits, is a rapidly growing health concern worldwide [1]. There is strong evidence indicating the association between NAFLD and metabolic disturbances such as obesity and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), or hyperlipidemia [1]. Besides the higher prevalence of obesity-related complications in developed countries, sleep disturbances are also prevalent and associated with the development of NAFLD in these societies [1]. Insufficient sleep influences not only mental health but also metabolic health, which contributes to the progression of NAFLD [2]. An extensively used questionnaire to assess sleep quality is the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), consisting of seven components including subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, habitual sleep efficiency, sleep disturbance, sleep medication use, and daytime dysfunction [3].

The associations between sleep duration (the length of time spent sleeping) and quality (how well and how long a person sleeps) with NAFLD have been addressed in several studies; however, the main relationship remains controversial [1, 2, 4–9]. The current evidence suggests a probable association of both long and short sleep time with NAFLD [10]. For example, Marin-Alejandre et al. [9] compared 94 overweight/obese subjects with NAFLD with 40 non-NAFLD controls and found marked differences in sleep efficiency, disturbance, and quality between the groups. Indeed, short sleep time and poor sleep quality were more prevalent in NAFLD. Results from a recent meta-analysis by Pirahesh et al. [10] failed to document any significant differences in sleep duration between patients with NAFLD and healthy controls [10].

Accumulating studies have linked inadequate sleep duration to obesity and multiple metabolic disorders which are closely associated with an increased risk of NAFLD [11, 12]. Lifestyle modifications aimed at achieving weight loss play a major role in the management of NAFLD. However, less attention has been paid to sleep disorders in such conditions. Moreover, there are limited studies investigating all components of the PSQI in assessing sleep quality in NAFLD, particularly adjusting for the confounding variables [9]. In fact, the associations of all components of the PSQI with NAFLD remain insufficiently understood. Beyond sleep duration, the effect of sleep quality on NAFLD differs by sex [2]. Hence, the current study sought to clarify the association between sleep quality and NAFLD by analyzing all elements of the PSQI separately by sex while controlling for confounding variables.

Materials and methods

Study procedures and population

In this case-control study, overweight and obese adults (25 kg/m² < body mass index (BMI) < 40 kg/m²) from both genders and aged 18 to 55 years were enrolled. An expert radiologist assessed liver echogenicity in a fasting state using ultrasonography. The severity of hepatic steatosis was categorized into four grades as follows: No steatosis (Healthy, Grade zero), mild steatosis (Grade I), moderate steatosis (Grade II), and severe steatosis (Grade III), based on the assessment of hepatorenal echo contrast, vessel blurring, and deep attenuation [13]. Seventy-four patients with early diagnosed NAFLD were classified as “cases” while healthy age and sex-matched subjects who were free from NAFLD (n = 74) were labeled as “controls”. Subjects who were alcohol drinkers or smokers, pregnant, lactating, menopause, or athletes, as well as individuals suffering from other chronic diseases and malignancies or receiving any type of medications, contraceptives, corticosteroids, and nutritional or herbal supplements within the three months preceding the study were excluded. The participants exhibiting any psychological disorders were not included in the study.

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tabriz University of Medical Science (IR.TBZMED.REC.1402.890). All eligible patients were fully informed about the study objectives and signed an informed consent form.

Sample size

The sample size was estimated based on the frequency of low sleep quality in patients with NAFLD (54%) compared with the frequency among age-sex matched controls (25%) reported by Marin-Alejandre et al. [9]. By considering a 95% confidence level and 90% power using G-power software, the sample size was calculated 74 subjects in each group.

Assessment of sleep quality index

To determine sleep quality status, the PSQI scale was applied, which included 19 self-administered questions in seven elements. The details of the scoring are presented in a supplementary file. The PSQI questionnaire used in the present study was validated by Mohammad Gholi Mezerji et al. [3, 14] in 2017, who assessed the reliability, validity, and factor structure of the Persian version of the PSQI questionnaire through a cross-sectional study on 1115 people aged 18–60 years selected by stratified random sampling method. Their results showed an excellent item content validity index (≥ 0.78), excellent scale content validity index (≥ 0.90), and the Cronbach alpha coefficient equal to 0.65, which all displayed satisfactory validity and reliability to measure the quality of sleep of Iranian people. Overall PSQI score was classified as: Good sleep quality (< 6) and Low sleep quality (≥ 6) [3, 14].

Assessment of anthropometric measures

Weight, height, waist circumference (WC), hip circumference (HC), and neck circumference (NC) of all patients were measured. BMI, WC to HC ratio (WHR), and WC to height ratio (WHtR) were also calculated according to the formula.

Biochemical assay

Levels of metabolic parameters including fasting blood sugar (FBS), hemoglobin A1C (HbA1c), TG, total cholesterol [15], and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-c) as well as serum iron, total iron binding capacity, creatinine and uric acid were assessed using enzymatic-colorimetric commercial kits (Pars-Azmoon Co., Tehran, Iran) and auto-analyzer (Hitachi 902, Tokyo, Japan). Then, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-c) was estimated using the Friedewald formula.

Furthermore, liver enzymes, including alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase, were assessed based on the International Federation of Clinical Chemistry. Electrochemiluminescence immunoassay was applied to determine serum ferritin levels. Indeed, serum levels of 25-hydroxyvitamin D were assessed using a high-sensitivity enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kit (Crystal Day, Shanghai), while chemiluminescence microparticle immunoassay (Abbott ARCHITECT i4000) was used to determine serum thyroid-stimulating hormone levels in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations of the laboratory. Moreover, complete blood count was assessed using an automated and calibrated Coulter CBCH1 counter.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using Statistical Package for Social Analysis (SPSS Inc., ver. 20, Chicago, IL, USA). To check the normal distribution of continuous variables, the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used. Associations between categorical variables were examined using the chi-square test, while differences in normally and non-normally distributed continuous variables were assessed using the independent samples t-test and Mann–Whitney U-test, respectively. The association between continuous variables was checked using Pearson correlation coefficient. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were applied to calculate the odds ratio (OR) and confidence interval (CI) 95% for NAFLD occurrence in relation to the sleep quality index score and its components by adjusting for the confounders. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To investigate the role of PSQI score in NAFLD odds, multivariate logistic regression was applied using two models (Table 5). In univariate logistic regression analysis, age, gender, occupation, BMI, and WC were found to be confounders for examining the association between sleep quality and NAFLD. After adjusting for age and gender, no association was found for PSQI score as well as its components in NAFLD odds. In the second model, by adding occupation, BMI, and WC to age and gender as potential confounders, high BMI was more likely to be associated with NAFLD (OR = 1.268, CI95%:1.151–1.397, p < 0.001) (Table 5).

4. Discussion

There is strong evidence indicating the circadian tendency between sleep and appetite as well as the reciprocal interaction between sleep and obesity. Sleep disturbances appear to be related to obesity and its related conditions, such as metabolic syndrome, T2DM, and possibly NAFLD [16]. In this case-control study, we observed that the PSQI score was significantly correlated with age, NC, WC, and iron-related hematological parameters only in patients with NAFLD. However, no association was found between global PSQI status or its components and NAFLD odds, after adjusting for the confounders.

In our study, poor sleep quality was found in 29.7% of the cases and 20.3% of the controls. No significant differences were found in PSQI components between the cases and controls. Our findings are in line with a case-control study assessing sleep habits on a population of 100 subjects with ultrasound-proven NAFLD vs. 55 healthy participants. The authors failed to illustrate any significant differences in terms of total sleep hours, short siesta, and optimal sleep time between the studied groups [7]. On the other hand, there are contraversies among our results and evidence from two other case-control studies [8, 9]. In this context, Ibinaiye et al. [8] in 2025 observed a significant difference in sleep duration after comparing 181 participants with cryptogenic steatotic liver disease with 181 healthy subjects and also documented the relation between reduced hours of sleep with the progression of liver disease among Nigerians. Marin-Alejandre et al. [9] also found marked differences in hepatic steatosis and liver stiffness related to deteriorated sleep characteristics namely by sleep quality score, disturbance score and sleep efficiency in cases compared with controls.

Additionally, our results on the increased frequency of poor sleep quality in patients with NAFLD are somewhat consistent with previous cross-sectional studies. Chou et al. [17] examined 6,663 Taiwanese adults using the PSQI and found that poor sleep quality was associated with a lower prevalence of mild and moderate-to-severe NAFLD in men. The results of the Shahrekord PERSIAN Cohort Study in Iran reported that NAFLD patients experienced shorter and less efficient sleep, later bed and wake times, and more frequent daytime napping and use of sleep medications, after adjusting for age and gender [18].

Possible factors that might be attributed to the observed difference among the findings are various study methodologies (cross-sectional vs. case-control study), assessing those who suffered from NAFLD with various disease severity vs. newly diagnosed patients, and large vs. relatively small sample size. Moreover, it should be noted that despite previous studies that mostly adjusted the results only for age and gender, we considered the other potential confounders in data analysis through various multivariate logistic regression analyses, not only for the global PSQI score but also for the components.

Results from the present study also showed that although age and BMI were independently and strongly associated with NAFLD, PSQI was not significantly linked to NAFLD odds even after adjusting for age, gender, BMI, FBS, TG, and TC which are consistent with previous findings. Um et al. [4] in a cohort study among 143,306 non-NAFLD Korean adults found that short sleep duration independently increased the risk of developing NAFLD, after adjusting for BMI or WC, suggesting the role of inadequate sleep quantity on NAFLD risk and severity. In another analysis of data from the same study on 86,530 patients with NAFLD, decreased sleep duration or persistently poor sleep quality over time was associated with an increased risk of NAFLD incident [19]. However, the relationship between sleep duration and NAFLD is controversial, as some observations reported a U-shaped association between sleep duration (both short and long) and the increased risk of hepatosteatosis. Meanwhile, most recent studies illuminated no significant (or dose-response) association [10, 20]. Results of cohort studies could show the impact of sleep duration on the development of the more severe form of NAFLD, incident NAFLD with liver fibrosis as the most important predictor of liver and non-liver mortality [10]. Meanwhile, these studies pointed out the use of a self-administered questionnaire as a subjective tool, as well as differences in confounding factors throughout the studies, as a source of heterogeneity in findings.

The underlying mechanisms of the association between sleep quality and obesity are unclear; however, insufficient sleep triggers complex biological mechanisms such as hormonal regulation, genetic susceptibility, gut microbiome, and inflammatory responses [21]. There is evidence suggesting that the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis is activated by sleep quality with increasing the secretion of stress hormones including cortisol, catecholamines, and hypothalamic orexin such as leptin and ghrelin which control appetite [22]. In addition, poor sleep quality and sleep fragmentation could also result in insulin resistance with no change in total sleep time [23]. There are limited cohort studies on the link between sleep quality components/changes and NAFLD risk. However, Ferrie et al. in a cohort study in the UK on 9781 participants reported a significant and positive association between sleep duration and the risk of T2DM, partly due to greater weight gain [24]. In addition, Song et al. [25] investigated 15,753 Chinese participants and showed a relation between short sleep duration and the risk of metabolic syndrome. There is evidence that late sleepers are at risk of metabolic disorders due to the imbalances between external and internal clocks which is not only resulted from postponed sleep but also from late meal eating at night and its relation with reduced melatonin secretion and disrupted circadian rhythm [26, 27]. Factors such as small sample size, methodology used, not including those with medication or nutritional supplements, excluding menopause women, not assessing biomarkers or tests objectively sleep problems as well as not considering sleeping-related factors such as workload, stress, and sleep apnea could be considered in results interpretation [28].

Our results also showed no significant between-group differences in taking sleeping medication. However, some studies have indicated associations between the history of medical conditions and the risk of NAFLD. Moreover, a recent observational meta-analysis on 15 studies reported that specially for those with a low rate of response to pharmacotherapy, obtaining an adequate amount of sleep may be useful for the prevention of NAFLD [11]. Theoretically, sleeping medications improve sleep quality, extend sleeping time, regulate the circadian rhythm, and improve insulin sensitivity. Melatonin suppression may also cause chronic inflammation and increase the risk of liver and other chronic diseases [29]. The underlying mechanisms of the relationship between sleep reduction and NAFLD could be explained by the role of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis and autonomic nervous system activities in regulating the immune system and cardiometabolic function [22]. Sleep deprivation not only decreases leptin and increases ghrelin concentrations and therefore triggers weight gain and NAFLD, but also results in insulin resistance and pro-inflammatory activity, which are the most important pathological hits in the progression of NAFLD [30].

The present study has some strengths, such as a relatively good research methodology compared with cross-sectional studies, using a validated questionnaire for assessing sleep quality and its components, applying ultrasonography for NAFLD diagnosis, and adjustment for possible sociodemographic and metabolic variables.

Limitations of the study.

The sample size of the study, estimated based on Marin-Alejandre et al. [9], appears to be insufficient for detecting small statistically significant differences in PSQI because of the study criteria, and further well-designed studies with larger sample sizes or population-based studies would be able to confirm the hypothesis properly. Other study limitations include:

Using a self-administered questionnaire for sleep quality and related errors.

Conclusion

Our results showed no relation between global PSQI and its components with NAFLD in Iranian adults, after adjusting for the confounders, although poor sleep quality was more common in patients with NAFLD than controls which may influence the quality of life of the patients or potentially worsen NAFLD progression. Further research is required to investigate the molecular mechanisms of chronic sleep deprivation and assess factors contributing in sleep quality by considering confounding variables such as gender, BMI status, dietary habit and physical activity.
