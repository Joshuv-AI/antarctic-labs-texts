Beyond Stoquasticity: Structural Steering and Interference in Quantum Optimization https://www.vc-gladius.com
Vicky Choi

Abstract
We present a theoretical analysis of the Dic-Dac-Doa algorithm, a non-stoquastic quantum algorithm for solving the Maximum Independent Set (MIS) problem. The algorithm runs in polynomial time and achieves exponential speedup over both transverse-field quantum annealing (TFQA) and classical algorithms on a structured family of NP-hard MIS instances, under assumptions supported by analytical and numerical evidence. The core of this speedup lies in the ability of the evolving ground state to develop both positive and negative amplitudes, enabled by the non-stoquastic 𝖷𝖷 \mathsf{XX} -driver. This sign structure permits quantum interference that produces negative amplitudes in the computational basis, allowing efficient evolution paths beyond the reach of stoquastic algorithms, whose ground states remain strictly non-negative. In our analysis, the efficiency of the algorithm is measured by the presence or absence of an anti-crossing, rather than by spectral gap estimation as in traditional approaches. The key idea is to infer it from the crossing behavior of bare energy levels of relevant subsystems associated with the degenerate local minima ( 𝙻𝙼 \mathtt{LM} ) and the global minimum ( 𝙶𝙼 \mathtt{GM} ). The cliques of the critical 𝙻𝙼 \mathtt{LM} , responsible for the anti-crossing in TFQA, can be efficiently identified to form the 𝖷𝖷 \mathsf{XX} -driver graph. Based on the clique structure of 𝙻𝙼 \mathtt{LM} , we construct a decomposition of the Hilbert space into same-sign and opposite-sign sectors, yielding a corresponding block-diagonal form of the Hamiltonian. We then show that the non-stoquastic 𝖷𝖷 \mathsf{XX} -driver induces a see-saw effect that shifts their bare energies, leading to analytically derived bounds on J 𝗑𝗑 J_{\mathsf{xx}} which support a two-stage annealing schedule that prevents anti-crossings throughout the evolution. The resulting speedup can be attributed to two mechanisms: in the first stage, energy-guided localization within the same-sign block steers the ground state smoothly into the 𝙶𝙼 \mathtt{GM} -supporting region, while in the second stage, the opposite-sign blocks are invoked and sign-generating quantum interference drives the evolution along an opposite-sign path. Finally, our analysis produces scalable small-scale models, derived from our structural reduction, that capture the essential dynamics of the algorithm. These models provide a concrete opportunity for verification of the quantum advantage mechanism on currently available universal quantum computers.

Introduction

A distinctive feature of quantum mechanics is that the wavefunction of a quantum state, expressed in a given basis, can have both positive and negative amplitudes—a characteristic with no classical counterpart, as classical probabilities must be non-negative.
Motivated by the goal of harnessing this intrinsically quantum property,
we proposed the
Dic-Dac-Doa
algorithm in
[
Choi2021
,
ChoiPatent
]
for the NP-hard Maximum Independent Set (MIS) problem.
The acronym originally stood for
Driver graph from Independent Cliques, Double Anti-Crossing, Diabatic quantum Optimization Annealing
.
The algorithm modifies standard transverse-field quantum annealing (TFQA)
[
Farhi2000
,
Farhi2001
,
AQC-eq
,
AQC-Review
]
by adding a specially designed
\mathsf{XX}
-driver term, aiming to overcome small-gap anti-crossings.
For completeness, we include the full algorithmic details and necessary refinements in this paper.

We now recall the system Hamiltonian for
Dic-Dac-Doa
:

\mathbbm{H}(t)=\mathtt{x}(t)\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{X}}+\mathtt{jxx}(t)\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{XX}}+\mathtt{p}(t)\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{problem}},

where

\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{X}}=-\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}^{x},\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{XX}}=\sum_{(i,j)\in{\mathsf{E}}(G_{\mathsf{driver}})}\sigma_{i}^{x}\sigma_{j}^{x},

and
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{problem}}
is the MIS-Ising Hamiltonian defined in Equation (
2.3
).
The time-dependent parameter schedule
\mathtt{jxx}(t)
depends on the
\mathsf{XX}
-coupling strength
J_{\mathsf{xx}}
.
In particular,
\mathtt{jxx}(t)\equiv 0
when
J_{\mathsf{xx}}=0
,
so the case
J_{\mathsf{xx}}=0
corresponds to TFQA, without the
\mathsf{XX}
-driver.
The system Hamiltonian is stoquastic (in the computational basis) if
J_{\mathsf{xx}}\leq 0
, and non-stoquastic if
J_{\mathsf{xx}}>0
.

The goal of our analysis is to demonstrate how and why an appropriately chosen
non-stoquastic
(i.e., positive) coupling strength
J_{\mathsf{xx}}
enables exponential speedup with
Dic-Dac-Doa
.
Specifically, we develop a theoretical framework for understanding the role of
J_{\mathsf{xx}}
in shaping the Hamiltonian structure and guiding ground state evolution.
We show that the algorithm succeeds in polynomial time by successively dissolving small-gap anti-crossings, one by one,
for a structured class of input instances, called
\mathtt{GIC}

Despite their structure, we argue in Section
3
that
\mathtt{GIC}

solving MIS on such instances would require exponential time unless
\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}
.
Our analysis methods combine analytical techniques and numerical verification, with perturbative and effective Hamiltonian arguements
that can in principle be made rigorous with further work.

First, we introduce some necessary terminology.
Throughout this paper, all Hamiltonians considered are real and Hermitian.
Accordingly, we restrict our attention to quantum states with real-valued amplitudes.
That is, the phase of each component is either
0
(corresponding to a positive sign) or
\pi
(corresponding to a negative sign).
We now formalize the sign structure of quantum states, which plays a central role in our analysis:

Definition 1.1
.

Let
|\psi\rangle=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{B}}\psi(x)|x\rangle
be a quantum state with real amplitudes in a basis
\mathcal{B}
.

•

|\psi\rangle
is called a
same-sign state
if
\psi(x)\geq 0
for all
x\in\mathcal{B}
.
That is, all components are in phase (with relative phase
0
).

•

|\psi\rangle
is called an
opposite-sign state
if there exist
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}
such that
\psi(x)>0
and
\psi(x^{\prime})<0
.
In this case, some components are out of phase, differing by a relative phase of
\pi
.

Unless stated otherwise, we take
\mathcal{B}
to be the computational basis when referring to same-sign or opposite-sign states.
More generally, the computational basis is assumed whenever the basis is unspecified.

Accordingly, we define the notions of same-sign and opposite-sign bases, sectors, and blocks:

Definition 1.2
.

An orthonormal basis consisting entirely of same-sign states is called a same-sign basis.
A basis that includes at least one opposite-sign state is called an opposite-sign basis.
A subspace spanned by a same-sign basis is called a same-sign sector; otherwise, it is called an opposite-sign sector.
A submatrix (or block) of a Hamiltonian is called a same-sign block if it is expressed in a same-sign basis.
Otherwise, it is referred to as an opposite-sign block.

Example.

The state
\ket{+}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{0}+\ket{1})
is a same-sign state,
while
\ket{-}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{0}-\ket{1})
is an opposite-sign state.
The computational basis
\{\ket{0},\ket{1}\}
is a same-sign basis of
\mathbbm{C}^{2}
,
and the Hadamard basis
\{\ket{+},\ket{-}\}
is an opposite-sign basis.

These definitions are closely related to the concept of stoquasticity, which constrains the sign structure of the ground state.
It is well-known that, by the Perron–Frobenius theorem, the ground state of a stoquastic Hamiltonian (i.e., one whose off-diagonal elements are non-positive in a given basis) is a same-sign state in that basis
[
AQC-eq
,
general-PF2
]
.
In particular, when expressed in the computational basis, the ground state of a stoquastic Hamiltonian is necessarily a same-sign state.
By contrast, the ground state of a non-stoquastic Hamiltonian may be either a same-sign or an opposite-sign state.
We refer to the former as
Eventually Stoquastic
and the latter as
Proper Non-stoquastic
, as introduced in
[
Choi2021
]
.

This distinction is crucial and can be understood in terms of the
admissible subspace
, defined as the subspace of Hilbert space that the instantaneous ground state of the system Hamiltonian can dynamically access.
The shape and sign structure of this subspace differ fundamentally depending on whether the Hamiltonian is stoquastic or properly non-stoquastic.
In the (eventually) stoquastic case, the admissible subspace remains confined to the same-sign sector of the computational basis, where all amplitudes are non-negative.
In the properly non-stoquastic case, this subspace expands to include the opposite-sign sector, allowing superpositions with both positive and negative amplitudes.

It is therefore clear that by “de-signing” the non-stoquastic Hamiltonian
[
de-sign
]
, one reduces the admissible subspace.
The larger the admissible subspace, the more possible evolution paths become accessible.
This raises the central question: can some of these new paths—particularly those involving smooth transitions rather than exponentially slow tunneling—be efficiently exploited?

We answer this question affirmatively in this work.
In particular, we show that

•

The
\mathsf{XX}
-driver opens access to opposite-sign sectors, enlarging the admissible subspace to include opposite-sign states;

•

In this expanded subspace,
sign-generating quantum interference
—interference that produces negative amplitudes in the computational basis—enables a
smooth evolution path
that bypasses tunneling.

This is the essence of going beyond stoquasticity: the ability to exploit a larger sign-structured subspace, which enables new evolution paths that are dynamically inaccessible in the stoquastic regime.

Informally, the
\mathsf{XX}
-driver graph, which consists of a set of independent cliques, induces a natural angular momentum structure in the Hilbert space. This structure allows for a block decomposition of the Hamiltonian into disjoint
same-sign
and
opposite-sign
blocks.
Importantly, in the signed basis induced by this decomposition, the off-diagonal terms involving non-stoquastic coupling strength
J_{\mathsf{xx}}
become diagonal.
Consequently,
J_{\mathsf{xx}}
induces a
see-saw

This effect introduces a fundamental trade-off: if
J_{\mathsf{xx}}
is too small,
the local minima in the same-sign block are not lifted high enough
(to remove the anti-crossing); if it is too large, the opposite-sign
blocks drop too low, inducing new anti-crossings.
As a result, the success of the algorithmic design depends critically on choosing appropriate
J_{\mathsf{xx}}
.
A central contribution of this work is the identification of four analytically derived bounds on
J_{\mathsf{xx}}
.
In addition, we identify two upper bounds on
J_{\mathsf{zz}}
that constrain the
problem Hamiltonian; these, together with the
J_{\mathsf{xx}}
bounds, define the
full feasibility window for successful evolution.
We then attribute the quantum speedup to two key mechanisms:
(1) steering the evolving ground state away from the local-minima-supporting region and directly into the global-minimum-supporting region (a mechanism we later formalize as
structural steering
);
and
(2) enabling sign-generating quantum interference between blocks of different signs, which produces negative amplitudes in the computational basis.
The detailed quantum interference mechanism will be illustrated in Section
9.4
.

By quantum interference, we mean the superposition of components in a quantum system that leads to amplitude cancellation or enhancement of the same basis state.
It is inherently quantum in nature, as only quantum systems admit decomposition into opposite-sign bases with complex or signed amplitudes. However, the mere presence of such interference does not, by itself, imply quantum advantage.
In our system, the observed speedup is attributed to a restricted form of quantum interference—namely, interference that produces negative amplitudes in the computational basis.
This form of sign-generating interference is not accessible to classical simulation or stoquastic dynamics, whose ground states remain confined to the non-negative cone.

To better understand the role of these mechanisms, we now return to the original motivation for introducing the
\mathsf{XX}
-driver—the formation of a double anti-crossing—and reinterpret its role in light of our current structural understanding.

Revisiting the Original Idea.

We begin by revisiting the original motivation behind the
Dic-Dac-Doa
algorithm, which was to convert the narrow anti-crossing in TFQA into a
double anti-crossing
(double-AC) by introducing the
\mathsf{XX}
-driver. The idea was that this transformation would enable exponential speedup through diabatic annealing, via a ground-to-excited-to-ground state transition. Numerical evidence supporting this
idea—including the splitting of degenerate local minima and the formation of a double-AC—was presented in
[
Choi2021
]
.
2

2

More concretely, we constructed an
\mathsf{XX}
-driver graph
G_{\mathsf{driver}}
from the set of independent cliques corresponding to the critical degenerate local minima
L
identified via polynomial-time TFQA. By applying a sufficiently large
\mathsf{XX}
-coupling strength
J_{\mathsf{xx}}
, we induced a splitting of the states in
L
into two subsets with opposite amplitudes, denoted
L^{+}
and
L^{-}
, ensuring that
L
remained in the instantaneous ground state when its energy was minimized. This resulted in two anti-crossings bridged by
(L^{+},L^{-})
, forming what we called a double-AC. This behavior was confirmed numerically through exact diagonalization.

While this observation guided the early design of the algorithm, our current structural understanding provides a more precise explanation.
The apparent double-AC actually arises from a pair of anti-crossings
between the ground-state energies of two blocks---one same-sign and one opposite-sign.

This was first

When these blocks are decoupled or only weakly coupled, the transition can be reinterpreted as effective adiabatic evolution confined to the same-sign block,
with the double-AC dynamically bypassed (see Section

In general, however, the coupling between the same-sign and opposite-sign blocks may not be weak,
and transitions between blocks become possible,
and the double-AC mechanism does not necessarily apply.

For example, the first anti-crossing may remain a block-level transition,
while the second—involving quantum interference—does not correspond to a small gap.

We conclude that the double-AC—though visually striking and initially suggestive of significance—is not essential for achieving quantum advantage.
What initially appeared as negative amplitude in the ground state can now be more precisely understood
as a manifestation of sign-generating quantum interference between same-sign and opposite-sign blocks.
Still, it was the numerical observation of this phenomenon in our original weighted MIS example
that led to the discovery of the Hamiltonian’s block structure
and the mechanisms governing the evolution of the quantum ground state.
Even if the double-AC ultimately serves only as a by-product of a deeper structural principle,
its appearance was a gateway to the framework we now develop.

In summary,
Dic-Dac-Doa
is motivated by turning around the obstacle of traditional stoquastic quantum annealing (TFQA)—specifically,
the presence of an anti-crossing caused by the competition between the
energies associated with
\mathtt{LM}{}
and
\mathtt{GM}

This anti-crossing obstacle has long been observed and explained using perturbative arguments in the small transverse-field regime (e.g.,
[

,

]
).
In our companion paper
[
Choi-Limitation
]
, we provide an explicit structural explanation and an analytical proof
for such an anti-crossing beyond the small transverse-field perturbative regime, for the class of structured input graphs.

In our analysis, the efficiency of the algorithm is measured by the
presence or absence of an anti-crossing, rather than by spectral gap
estimation as in traditional approaches (see e.g.
[
AQC-Review
]

and references therein). An anti-crossing can be rigorously defined
through reduction to a
2\times 2
effective Hamiltonian, with the
precise definition given in Section
5
.
The key idea is that the unperturbed energies (the diagonal entries of the
effective Hamiltonian) can be well-approximated by the bare energies of
relevant subsystems: the local minima (
\mathtt{LM}
), whose cliques are efficiently
identified in Phase I and used to construct the
\mathsf{XX}
-driver graph, and the
global minimum (
\mathtt{GM}
), which is assumed as a reference in the analysis.
This makes it possible to infer the
presence or absence of an anti-crossing directly from the crossing
behavior of bare energy levels, without explicitly constructing
the effective two-level Hamiltonian.
In particular, our structural analysis consists of three main
components, supported by both analytical and numerical evidence:

•

A structural decomposition of the Hilbert space induced by
the
\mathsf{XX}
-driver graph, separating same-sign and opposite-sign
blocks via angular momentum techniques.

•

Identification of a see-saw energy effect induced by the
non-stoquastic parameter
J_{\mathsf{xx}}
, which raises the energy associated with local
minima in the same-sign block while lowering that of opposite-sign
blocks.

•

Derivation of four analytical bounds on
J_{\mathsf{xx}}
and design of
a two-stage annealing schedule that guarantees correct ground state
evolution without encountering an anti-crossing, confirmed by
numerical evidence.

A by-product contribution of this work is the design of effective small-scale models, derived from our structural reduction, that capture the essential dynamics of the algorithm. These models provide a concrete opportunity for verification of the quantum advantage mechanism on currently available gate-model quantum devices through simulation.

Algorithm Overview.

The full algorithm consists of two phases:

•

Phase I:
Extract an independent-clique (IC) structure using a polynomial-time TFQA process:

1.

Use TFQA and polynomial annealing time to return the excited states involved in the anti-crossing.

2.

Extract a set of seeds (local minima states) from the resulting output.

3.

Apply a classical procedure to identify an independent-clique (IC) structure based on these seeds.

•

Phase II:
Define an
\mathsf{XX}
-driver graph using the IC structure and perform an annealing process with tunable
J_{\mathsf{xx}}
:

1.

Construct an
\mathsf{XX}
-driver graph
G_{\mathsf{driver}}
using the IC structure identified in Phase I.

2.

Define a new time-dependent Hamiltonian
\mathbbm{H}(t)=\mathtt{x}(t)\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{X}}+\mathtt{jxx}(t)\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{XX}}+\mathtt{p}(t)\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{problem}}
.

3.

For each feasible
J_{\mathsf{xx}}
, evolve adiabatically according to a three-stage schedule:

–

Stage 0
(optional initialization);

–

Stage 1
(energy-guided localization);

–

Stage 2
(interference-driven transition).

4.

Measure the final ground state to extract the optimal solution.

MIS Problem and
J_{\mathsf{zz}}
Coupling

The NP-hard weighted Maximum Independent Set (MIS) problem is formulated as follows:

Input:
An undirected graph
G=({\mathsf{V}}(G),{\mathsf{E}}(G))
with
N=|{\mathsf{V}}(G)|
, where each vertex
i\in{\mathsf{V}}(G)=\{1,\ldots,N\}
is assigned a positive rational weight
w_{i}
.

Output:
A subset
S\subseteq{\mathsf{V}}(G)
such that
S
is independent (i.e., for each
i,j\in S
,
(i,j)\not\in{\mathsf{E}}(G)
), and the total weight of
S
, given by

\mathtt{w}(S)=\sum_{i\in S}w_{i},

is maximized. We denote this optimal set as
\mathsf{mis}(G)
.

For simplicity, we focus on the unweighted MIS problem, where all
w_{i}=w=1
. However, we retain
w_{i}
in our formulation to allow for generalization to the weighted case and for later analysis purpose. In the unweighted setting,
\mathsf{mis}(G)
corresponds to the largest independent set in
G
.

2.1
The Role of
J_{\mathsf{zz}}
in the MIS-Ising Hamiltonian

As shown in
[
minor1
]
, the MIS problem can be encoded in the ground state of the MIS-Ising Hamiltonian:

\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{MIS\!-Ising}}(G)=\sum_{i\in{\mathsf{V}}(G)}(-w_{i})\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{i}+\sum_{(i,j)\in{\mathsf{E}}(G)}J_{ij}\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{i}\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{j},

(2.1)

where
J_{ij}>\max\{w_{i},w_{j}\}
for all
(i,j)\in{\mathsf{E}}(G)
. This formulation slightly modifies that of
[
minor1
,
Choi2021
]
, replacing
\sigma^{z}
with the shifted-
\sigma^{z}
operator
\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{i}:=\tfrac{I+\sigma^{z}_{i}}{2}
, whose eigenvalues are
\{0,1\}
, making the correspondence with the classical energy function direct:

\mathcal{E}(G)=\sum_{i\in{\mathsf{V}}(G)}(-w_{i})x_{i}+\sum_{(i,j)\in{\mathsf{E}}(G)}J_{ij}x_{i}x_{j},

(2.2)

where
x_{i}\in\{0,1\}
. The energy is minimized when no two adjacent vertices
i,j
satisfy
x_{i}=x_{j}=1
, ensuring that the ground state corresponds to
\mathsf{mis}(G)
.
For convenience, we refer to all
J_{ij}
as
J_{\mathsf{zz}}
, although these couplings need not be uniform, even in the unweighted case. The only requirement is that
J_{\mathsf{zz}}>\max\{w_{i},w_{j}\}
for each edge
(i,j)\in{\mathsf{E}}(G)
.

In the unweighted case (
w_{i}=1
), an independent set of size
m
has energy

E_{\mathsf{ind}}=-m,

while a dependent set has energy

E_{\mathsf{dep}}=J_{\mathsf{zz}}\cdot(\#\text{edges})-(\#\text{vertices}).

Thus, large
J_{\mathsf{zz}}
creates a large energy separation between independent-set states and dependent-set states.
In principle,
J_{\mathsf{zz}}
can be chosen arbitrarily large; however, excessively large values of
J_{\mathsf{zz}}
can be detrimental.
With the use of the
\mathsf{XX}
-driver in
Dic-Dac-Doa
, the dependent-set states can act as “bridges” between different groups of independent-set states to facilitate the smooth structural steering (to be elaborated in Section
9.3.2
).
The appropriate choice of
J_{\mathsf{zz}}
is crucial for the success of the algorithm.

For
Dic-Dac-Doa
, we use two different couplings:

•

J_{\mathsf{zz}}^{\mathsf{clique}}
: assigned to edges within cliques of the driver graph;

•

J_{\mathsf{zz}}
: assigned to all other edges.

That is, the MIS–Ising problem Hamiltonian takes the form

\displaystyle\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{problem}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in{\mathsf{V}}(G)}(-w_{i})\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{i}+J_{\mathsf{zz}}^{\mathsf{clique}}\sum_{(i,j)\in{\mathsf{E}}(G_{\mathsf{driver}})}\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{i}\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{j}+J_{\mathsf{zz}}\sum_{(i,j)\in{\mathsf{E}}(G)\setminus{\mathsf{E}}(G_{\mathsf{driver}})}\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{i}\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{j}.

(2.3)

The value of
J_{\mathsf{zz}}^{\mathsf{clique}}
is set sufficiently large to restrict the system to the clique low-energy subspace,
while
J_{\mathsf{zz}}
must satisfy two upper bounds,
J_{\mathsf{zz}}^{\text{\tiny inter}}
and
J_{\mathsf{zz}}^{\text{\tiny steer}}
,
to ensure the success of the algorithm.
The precise role of these bounds will be analyzed in Section
9.3
.

3
Classical Hard Instances of MIS and the
\mathtt{GIC}
Instances

Recall that an independent set is
maximal
if no larger independent set contains it.
Each maximal independent set corresponds to a local minimum of the energy function in Eq. (
2.2
).
A collection of maximal independent sets (
\mathtt{MLIS}
) all having the same size
m

corresponds to a set of
degenerate
local minima with equal energy
-m
.
When the meaning is clear (i.e., they all have the same size), we refer to such a collection simply as a
\mathtt{deg}
-
\mathtt{MLIS}
,
and its cardinality as the
degeneracy
.
In this work, we use the terms
degenerate local minima
and
\mathtt{deg}
-
\mathtt{MLIS}
interchangeably.

This section explores the relationship between classical hardness and the structure of
\mathtt{deg}
-
\mathtt{MLIS}
,
with a focus on how degeneracy influences computational complexity in both classical and quantum settings.
In Section
3.1
we show that classical hardness requires exponentially many
\mathtt{MLIS}
,
with at least one
\mathtt{deg}
-
\mathtt{MLIS}
exhibiting exponential degeneracy.
In Section
3.2
we introduce a structured form of degeneracy, denoted by
\mathtt{dMIC}
.
Finally, in Section
3.3
we define the class of
Graphs with Independent Cliques
(
\mathtt{GIC}
),
together with a reduction to fundamental bipartite structures—
G_{\mathsf{dis}}
and
G_{\mathsf{share}}
—that capture both classical hardness and
the structural features exploited by our algorithm.

3.1
Necessary Classical Hardness Conditions

The MIS problem was among the first shown NP-complete
[
GJ79
]
. A simple branching
algorithm
[
Tsukiyama1977
,
Johnson1988
]
without pruning (i.e., branch-without-bound)
enumerates all
\mathtt{MLIS}
in time
O(N\cdot\#\mathtt{MLIS})
, where
\#\mathtt{MLIS}
denotes the number of sets in
\mathtt{MLIS}
.
Thus, MIS can be solved by identifying the largest among them. In the worst case,

\#\mathtt{MLIS}=O(3^{N/3})
, though often significantly smaller—yet still exponential.

Observation 1.

A necessary condition for classical hardness is that the MIS instance contains exponentially many
\mathtt{MLIS}
.

Observation 2.

A necessary consequence is that at least one
\mathtt{deg}
-
\mathtt{MLIS}
must exhibit
exponential degeneracy
.

Observation 1 follows directly from the enumeration algorithm above.
Observation 2 follows from the pigeonhole principle:
since an independent set can have at most
N
distinct sizes,
at least one size class must contain exponentially many
\mathtt{MLIS}
.
Otherwise, if all
\mathtt{deg}
-
\mathtt{MLIS}
had at most polynomial degeneracy,
the total number of
\mathtt{MLIS}
would be polynomial—contradicting Observation 1.

As a side note, it is worth emphasizing that classical algorithms for
MIS

outperform Grover’s unstructured quantum search
[
Grover1996
,
Roland2002
]
, which requires
O(2^{N/2})
time.
As early as 1977, a simple and implementable algorithm achieved a runtime of
O(2^{N/3})
via careful branching analysis
[
Tarjan1977
]
, and this exponent has been further improved over time using more sophisticated branch-and-bound techniques, see
[
MWIS2019
]
for references.
Since other optimization problems such as
Exact-Cover
can be reduced to
MIS
with the same problem size
[
Choi-NP-reduction
]
, it follows that unstructured adiabatic quantum optimization
[
UnstructuredAQO2024
]
—while perhaps of theoretical interest—offers no practical algorithmic advantage.

3.2
\mathtt{dMIC}
: Structured Form of a
\mathtt{deg}
-
\mathtt{MLIS}

It is widely believed that a
\mathtt{deg}
-
\mathtt{MLIS}
with exponential degeneracy causes a tunneling-induced anti-crossing in TFQA.
We call such a
\mathtt{deg}
-
\mathtt{MLIS}

critical
.
The exponentially many
\mathtt{MLIS}
in a critical
\mathtt{deg}
-
\mathtt{MLIS}
must exhibit substantial vertex-sharing.
This suggests a natural partitioning of the involved vertices into
k
cliques,
where each maximal independent set includes exactly one vertex from each clique.
However, efficiently identifying such a partition may not always be feasible,
and multiple partitions may be needed to fully characterize a critical
\mathtt{deg}
-
\mathtt{MLIS}
.

In its simplest structured form, a critical
\mathtt{deg}
-
\mathtt{MLIS}
can be represented as a
\mathtt{dMIC}
,
namely a collection of mutually independent cliques whose vertices together generate exactly all the
\mathtt{MLIS}
in that
\mathtt{deg}
-
\mathtt{MLIS}
.

Definition 3.1
.

A
\mathtt{dMIC}
of size
k
consists of
k

independent cliques
(i.e., no edges exist between them),
denoted as
\text{Clique}(w_{i},n_{i})
,
where
w_{i}
is the vertex weight and
n_{i}
is the clique size, for
1\leq i\leq k
.
Each maximal independent set in the corresponding
\mathtt{deg}
-
\mathtt{MLIS}
is formed by selecting exactly one vertex from each clique.
In this case, the degeneracy of the
\mathtt{dMIC}
is given by

\prod_{i=1}^{k}n_{i}.

Moreover, given any single maximal independent set as a seed,
all
k
cliques in the
\mathtt{dMIC}
can be identified in linear time due to the independence condition.
For each vertex in the seed, the corresponding clique is obtained by collecting all vertices
that are mutually adjacent to it and simultaneously non-adjacent to all other seed vertices.
This procedure depends only on vertex adjacencies in the graph.

Under the assumption of the
\mathtt{dMIC}
structure,
we analytically establish in the companion paper
[
Choi-Limitation
]

that TFQA encounters a tunneling-induced anti-crossing with an exponentially small gap.
(This generalizes the so-called perturbative crossing,
which is defined only in the small transverse-field regime.)
While such an
(\mathtt{LM}{},\mathtt{GM}{})
-anti-crossing is typically a bottleneck for reaching the global minimum
\mathtt{GM}
,
here we turn it around and use it constructively to reach the local minima
\mathtt{LM}{}
(the obstacle) instead.
More specifically, a polynomial-time TFQA algorithm evolves adiabatically through this anti-crossing,
tracking the energy associated with
\mathtt{LM}{}
(blue) instead of transitioning to the global minimum
\mathtt{GM}{}
(red),
as illustrated in Figure
5
.
This outputs a maximal independent set configuration from
\mathtt{LM}
, which can be used to seed the IC-based driver construction in Phase I
of the
Dic-Dac-Doa
algorithm; see Table
1
.

Remark 3.2
.

When the cliques are
dependent
—that is, edges may exist between them—we refer to the structure as an
\mathtt{dMDC}
,
a
\mathtt{deg}
-
\mathtt{MLIS}
formed by a set of dependent cliques.
This represents a relaxed structure to which our analysis may be generalized.
The main difficulty in this relaxation is that the cliques can no longer be unambiguously identified,
unlike in the
\mathtt{dMIC}
case. Further discussion is deferred to future work.

Terminology and Abbreviations.

For convenience, we summarize below the key abbreviations and their meanings.
These notions are closely related: a
\mathtt{deg}
-
\mathtt{MLIS}
corresponds to degenerate local minima,
while
\mathtt{dMIC}
and
\mathtt{dMDC}
describe structured forms of such degeneracy.

3.3
\mathtt{GIC}
and Its Hardness

We now define the class of structured problem instances assumed in this paper.
A
Graph with Independent Cliques
(
\mathtt{GIC}
) is a graph whose structure satisfies the following properties:

•

Each critical
\mathtt{deg}
-
\mathtt{MLIS}
in the graph assumes the structure of a
\mathtt{dMIC}
;

•

It has a unique (global) maximum independent set, denoted as
\mathtt{GM}
.
which may be viewed as a
\mathtt{dMIC}
consisting entirely of singleton cliques;

•

The
\mathtt{GM}
shares at least one vertex with at least one clique in some critical
\mathtt{dMIC}
.

A critical
\mathtt{dMIC}
contains exponentially many
\mathtt{MLIS}
,
and its partial overlap with the
\mathtt{GM}
obscures the global structure,
making it difficult for classical algorithms to distinguish the true optimum from nearby local maxima.
The classical hardness of
\mathtt{GIC}
instances stems from these same two features:
(1) the exponential number of maximal independent sets in the
\mathtt{dMIC}
, and
(2) the partial vertex overlap between the global maximum and these local maxima,
which creates ambiguity and hinders global optimization.

While we do not prove the classical hardness of
\mathtt{GIC}
in the formal complexity-theoretic sense
(otherwise we would have proven
\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}
,
we note that the definition can be further strengthened if needed.
For example, one may require that the graph contains at least two critical
\mathtt{dMIC}
s,
and that most vertices of the
\mathtt{GM}
belong to cliques in these
\mathtt{dMIC}
s.
In other words,
\mathtt{GIC}
instances are designed to be structured enough for rigorous analysis,
yet rich enough to present classical difficulty.

Reduction to Fundamental Bipartite Structures.

Our key argument is that the analysis of any general
\mathtt{GIC}
instance
can be reduced to a sequence of simpler bipartite substructures,
each formed by a critical
\mathtt{dMIC}
and the global maximum (
\mathtt{GM}
).

Recall that each
\mathtt{dMIC}
corresponds to a set of degenerate local minima (
\mathtt{LM}
) in the MIS–Ising energy landscape.
The terms
\mathtt{LM}
and
\mathtt{dMIC}
are thus used interchangeably.
We use
\mathtt{GM}
to refer both to the (global) maximum independent set and to its corresponding global minimum in the energy landscape.
In what follows, we use
\mathtt{LM}
and
\mathtt{GM}
to denote the
\mathtt{dMIC}
and
\mathtt{GM}
in each such bipartite substructure, respectively.

3.3.1
Bipartite Substructures:
G_{\mathsf{dis}}
and
G_{\mathsf{share}}

We consider two bipartite substructures:

•

G_{\mathsf{dis}}
: The local minima (
\mathtt{LM}
) and the global minimum (
\mathtt{GM}
) are vertex-disjoint.

•

G_{\mathsf{share}}
: The
\mathtt{GM}
shares exactly one vertex with each clique in the
\mathtt{LM}
.

We begin with the
disjoint-structure graph

G_{\mathsf{dis}}=(V,E)
,
in which the vertex set
V
is partitioned into left and right (disjoint) vertex sets,
with the following structural properties:

•

The left component is defined by a set
L=\{C_{1},\dots,C_{m_{l}}\}
of
m_{l}
disjoint cliques,
each denoted
C_{i}=\mathsf{Clique}(w_{i},n_{i})
.
We let
V_{L}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{m_{l}}C_{i}
denote the full vertex set.

•

The right component
R
consists of
m_{r}
independent vertices, each with weight
w_{r}
.

•

Every vertex in
V_{L}
is adjacent to every vertex in
R
.

In this paper we mainly focus on the unweighted MIS case,
and assume uniform weights
w_{i}=w_{r}=w
.
Under the MIS–Ising mapping with these uniform weights,

V_{L}
corresponds to the degenerate local minima (
\mathtt{LM}
) with degeneracy
\prod_{i=1}^{m_{l}}n_{i}
,
while
R
defines the global minimum (
\mathtt{GM}
) with
m_{g}=m_{r}
.
Each local minimum (in
\mathtt{LM}
) corresponds to a maximal independent set of size
m_{l}
, and thus has energy
-m_{l}
.
The global minimum (
\mathtt{GM}
) has energy
-m_{g}
.

We now define the
shared-structure graph

G_{\mathsf{share}}
,
which differs from
G_{\mathsf{dis}}
in that each vertex in
R
is adjacent to all but one vertex in each clique of
L
.
This modification allows the
\mathtt{GM}
to include shared vertices from the cliques in
L
,
thereby introducing overlap with the
\mathtt{LM}
.
Structurally,
L
and
R
are defined exactly as in
G_{\mathsf{dis}}
,
but with the adjacency rule modified as above.
Specifically, the global maximum
\mathtt{GM}
consists of one shared vertex from each clique
C_{i}\in L
together with all
m_{r}
independent vertices in
R
, yielding a total size
m_{g}=m_{l}+m_{r}
.

(a)

The
\mathtt{LM}
and
\mathtt{GM}
are vertex-disjoint.

(b)

The
\mathtt{GM}
shares exactly one vertex with each clique in the
\mathtt{LM}
.

These bipartite substructures represent two extreme regimes of connectivity and

\mathtt{LM}
and the
\mathtt{GM}
.
One can view
G_{\mathsf{dis}}
as a special case of
G_{\mathsf{share}}
in which no vertices are shared between
\mathtt{LM}
and
\mathtt{GM}
.
Note that
G_{\mathsf{dis}}
is not classically hard on its own
(for example, one can remove all
\mathtt{LM}
vertices and directly identify the
\mathtt{GM}

However, it may still arise as a substructure during a single iteration of the full algorithm,
as discussed in Section
.

G_{\mathsf{dis}}
separately here for two reasons.
First, in the
G_{\mathsf{dis}}
case, the relevant blocks of the Hamiltonian are decoupled,
allowing the mechanisms of our algorithm to be simply illustrated.
Second, it highlights the core distinction introduced by shared vertices:
namely, that a single shared vertex can enable block coupling and necessitate
an upper bound on
J_{\mathsf{xx}}
to prevent an interference-involved block-level anti-crossing.
We note that for the TFQA case (
J_{\mathsf{xx}}=0
),
we argue in the companion paper
[

]
that it is sufficient
to analyze only the disjoint-structure case (for the presence of the anti-crossing and the gap size).

Remark 3.3
.

Our reduction to bipartite substructures is not presented as a formal derivation,
but rather as a structural perspective informed by effective Hamiltonian techniques.
Each individual anti-crossing is primarily governed by the interaction
between a set of critical local minima and the global minimum.
This motivates a stepwise reduction to low-energy effective Hamiltonians
that couple the
\mathtt{GM}
to one critical structure at a time—yielding a bipartite substructure,
with connectivity and shared vertices bounded by the
G_{\mathsf{share}}
worst case.
While a fully rigorous justification of this reduction is beyond the scope of this paper,
our detailed analysis of the
G_{\mathsf{share}}
instance,
together with numerical examples involving multiple local minima in Section
,
provides supporting evidence for the validity of this bipartite reduction perspective.

4
Revised
Dic-Dac-Doa
: System Hamiltonian and Annealing Schedule

In this section, we first recall the system Hamiltonian used in the original
Dic-Dac-Doa
algorithm,
then introduce the revised annealing schedule.
The full evolution consists of an optional initialization phase (Stage 0),
followed by the two-stage algorithmic core (Stage 1 and Stage 2).

4.1
Recall: System Hamiltonian of
Dic-Dac-Doa

The system acts on a Hilbert space of
N
spin-
\tfrac{1}{2}
particles,
one for each vertex of the problem graph
G
.
The Hamiltonian is expressed in terms of the spin operators

\mathrm{S}_{\mathsf{x}}=\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{x}
and
\mathrm{S}_{\mathsf{\tilde{z}}}=\tilde{\sigma}^{z}
,
where
\tilde{\sigma}^{z}
denotes the shifted
\sigma^{z}
operator.
The system Hamiltonian is defined in terms of a time-dependent annealing schedule:

\mathbbm{H}(t)=\mathtt{x}(t)\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{X}}+\mathtt{jxx}(t)\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{XX}}+\mathtt{p}(t)\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{problem}},

where

\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{X}}=-\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}^{x},\quad\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{XX}}=\sum_{(i,j)\in{\mathsf{E}}(G_{\mathsf{driver}})}\sigma_{i}^{x}\sigma_{j}^{x}.

The problem Hamiltonian
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{problem}}
, introduced in Section
2
, is recalled here for completeness:

\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{problem}}=\sum_{i\in{\mathsf{V}}(G)}(-w_{i})\,\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{i}+J_{\mathsf{zz}}^{\mathsf{clique}}\sum_{(i,j)\in{\mathsf{E}}(G_{\mathsf{driver}})}\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{i}\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{j}+J_{\mathsf{zz}}\sum_{(i,j)\in{\mathsf{E}}(G)\setminus{\mathsf{E}}(G_{\mathsf{driver}})}\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{i}\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{j}.

The original annealing parameters evolve as:

\mathtt{x}(t)=(1-t)\Gamma,\mathtt{jxx}(t)=t(1-t)J_{\mathsf{xx}},\mathtt{p}(t)=t.

We revise
Dic-Dac-Doa
by modifying its annealing schedule, specifically the functions
\mathtt{x}(t)
,
\mathtt{jxx}(t)
, and
\mathtt{p}(t)
.

4.2
Revised Annealing Schedule: Stages 0, 1 and 2

We describe the system using two Hamiltonians:
\mathbbm{H}_{0}(t)
, defined for
t\in[0,1]
, corresponding to the optional Stage 0; and
\mathbbm{H}_{1}(t)
, also defined over
t\in[0,1]
, governing the evolution during Stages 1 and 2.

There are three distinct values for the transverse field:
\Gamma_{0}>\Gamma_{1}>\Gamma_{2}
.
The three stages are distinguished by the values of these
\Gamma
’s: the transverse field is reduced from
\Gamma_{0}
to
\Gamma_{1}
during Stage 0, from
\Gamma_{1}
to
\Gamma_{2}
during Stage 1, and from
\Gamma_{2}
to
0
during Stage 2.

Stage 0 (optional).

This stage begins in the uniform superposition state
and evolves adiabatically toward the ground state of
\mathbbm{H}_{0}(1)=\mathbbm{H}_{1}(0)
.

The Hamiltonian for Stage 0 is

\mathbbm{H}_{0}(t)=\mathtt{x}(t)\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{X}}+\mathtt{jxx}(t)\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{XX}}+\mathtt{p}(t)\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{problem}},\quad t\in[0,1],

with

\mathtt{x}(t)=(1-t)(\Gamma_{0}-\Gamma_{1})+\Gamma_{1},\mathtt{jxx}(t)=tJ_{\mathsf{xx}},\mathtt{p}(t)=t.

During this stage, the problem parameters
w_{i}
,
J_{\mathsf{zz}}^{\mathsf{clique}}
, and
J_{\mathsf{zz}}

are gradually ramped to their final values as
\mathtt{p}(t)
increases from
0
to
1
,
while
\mathtt{jxx}(t)
increases linearly to its target value
J_{\mathsf{xx}}
.
If the ground state of
\mathbbm{H}_{0}(1)=\mathbbm{H}_{1}(0)
can be prepared directly—e.g., via quantum hardware initialization—
then Stage 0 may be omitted.

Main Stages 1 and 2 (algorithmic core).

The system Hamiltonian during the main two-stage evolution is

\mathbbm{H}_{1}(t)=\mathtt{x}(t)\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{X}}+\mathtt{jxx}(t)\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{XX}}+\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{problem}},\quad t\in[0,1].

The annealing schedule divides into two stages, determined by a structure-dependent parameter
\Gamma_{2}
.
We define the transition time
t_{\mathsf{sep}}:=1-\frac{\Gamma_{2}}{\Gamma_{1}}
,
so that Stage 1 corresponds to
t\in[0,t_{\mathsf{sep}}]
, and Stage 2 to
t\in[t_{\mathsf{sep}},1]
.
The transverse field is given by

\mathtt{x}(t)=(1-t)\Gamma_{1},

which decreases linearly from
\Gamma_{1}
at
t=0
to
\Gamma_{2}
at
t=t_{\mathsf{sep}}
, and then to
0
at
t=1
.
The XX-driver schedule is

\mathtt{jxx}(t)=\begin{cases}J_{\mathsf{xx}}&\text{for }t\in[0,t_{\mathsf{sep}}]\Leftrightarrow\mathtt{x}\in[\Gamma_{2},\Gamma_{1}],\\
\alpha\mathtt{x}(t)&\text{for }t\in[t_{\mathsf{sep}},1]\Leftrightarrow\mathtt{x}\in[0,\Gamma_{2}],\end{cases}

where
J_{\mathsf{xx}}=\alpha\Gamma_{2}
. That is,
\mathtt{jxx}(t)
remains constant during Stage 1,
and both
\mathtt{x}(t)
and
\mathtt{jxx}(t)
ramp linearly to zero during Stage 2.
The parameter
\alpha
controls the strength of the XX-coupling relative to the transverse field during Stage 2.
Its value is critical
and will be determined analytically in Section
9.3.1
.

Anti-Crossing and the Two-Level Hamiltonian
\mathbbm{B}(w,x)

The term
anti-crossing
is sometimes used loosely, so we begin with a precise notion in the two-level case, then extend it to multi-level systems, and finally classify different types of anti-crossing.
We also introduce a canonical two-level Hamiltonian whose eigensystem will be used throughout our analysis.

5.1
Level Repulsion vs. Anti-Crossing

We begin by distinguishing the concept of an
anti-crossing
(also called
avoided-crossing
) from level repulsion.

Consider a generic two-level Hamiltonian of the form

\mathbbm{H}(x):=\begin{bmatrix}e_{1}(x)&v(x)\\
v(x)&e_{2}(x)\end{bmatrix},

where
e_{1}(x)
,
e_{2}(x)
, and
v(x)
are real-valued functions of a parameter
x
.

The eigenvalues of this Hamiltonian are

\lambda_{\pm}(x)=\tfrac{e_{1}(x)+e_{2}(x)}{2}\pm\tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{(e_{1}(x)-e_{2}(x))^{2}+4v(x)^{2}},

and the energy gap between them is

\Delta(x):=\lambda_{+}(x)-\lambda_{-}(x)=\sqrt{(e_{1}(x)-e_{2}(x))^{2}+4v(x)^{2}}.

The off-diagonal term
v(x)
induces
level repulsion
: if
v(x)\neq 0
, then the eigenvalues never cross, and the gap
\Delta(x)
remains strictly positive.
Thus, assuming the off-diagonal coupling
v(x)
is nonzero, level repulsion is always present.

Definition 5.1
.

We say that an
anti-crossing
occurs when the two
unperturbed energy levels
e_{1}(x)
and
e_{2}(x)
cross, i.e.,

e_{1}(x_{*})=e_{2}(x_{*})
for some
x_{*}
, and the off-diagonal coupling

v(x_{*})\neq 0
.
In this case the eigenvalue curves form an anti-crossing with gap

\Delta_{\min}=2|v(x_{*})|.

The size of the anti-crossing gap depends on
|v(x_{*})|
: stronger coupling leads to a larger gap, while weaker coupling results in a narrower one.

By contrast, if the two diagonal entries
e_{1}(x)
and
e_{2}(x)
remain well separated for all
x
, then the system exhibits level repulsion but not an anti-crossing. Figure
6
illustrates an example of level repulsion without an anti-crossing.

The eigenvectors of the two-level Hamiltonian are given by

\ket{\lambda_{-}(x)}=\cos\theta(x)\ket{0}+\sin\theta(x)\ket{1},\quad\ket{\lambda_{+}(x)}=-\sin\theta(x)\ket{0}+\cos\theta(x)\ket{1},

where the mixing angle
\theta(x)
satisfies

\tan(2\theta(x))=\tfrac{2v(x)}{e_{1}(x)-e_{2}(x)}.

Thus, near the anti-crossing point
x=x_{*}
, the eigenstates interpolate between the unperturbed basis states.

Remark 5.2
.

The trigonometric expression for eigenvectors in terms of the mixing angle
\theta(x)
,
is equivalent to the rational-form representation

\ket{\lambda_{-}(x)}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{1+\gamma(x)^{2}}}\left(\gamma(x)\ket{0}+\ket{1}\right),\quad\ket{\lambda_{+}(x)}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{1+\gamma(x)^{2}}}\left(\ket{0}-\gamma(x)\ket{1}\right),

where the two parametrizations are related by

\gamma(x)=\tfrac{1}{\tan\theta(x)},\text{and}\quad\tan(2\theta(x))=\tfrac{2v(x)}{e_{1}(x)-e_{2}(x)}.

This rational-form expression is particularly useful for our analysis, as it aligns directly with the basic matrix form introduced below.

Remark 5.3
.

While the explicit forms of the eigenvectors are not directly used in this paper, they are included here for completion, and used in the companion paper
[
Choi-Limitation
]
for
bounding the anti-crossing gap.

Our earlier work
[
Choi2021
,
Choi2020
]
, including the development of the original
Dic-Dac-Doa
algorithm, was motivated by investigating the structural characteristics of eigenstates around the anti-crossing.

5.1.1
Anti-crossing in a Multi-level System

In a multi-level system, the notion of an anti-crossing extends naturally
by restricting the Hamiltonian to the two-dimensional subspace spanned by
the pair of eigenstates whose unperturbed energies intersect.
This reduction yields a
2\times 2
effective Hamiltonian that captures the
essential structure of the anti-crossing, including both the energy gap and
the interpolating behavior of the eigenstates.
Thus, the same framework as in the two-level case applies.

With this perspective, we refine the definition of an
(L,R)
-anti-crossing
given in
[
Choi2021
]
. Recall that
E_{0}^{A}(t)
denotes the ground state
energy of the Hamiltonian
\mathbbm{H}_{A}(t)
projected to the subspace spanned by
the subsets of
A
.
For simplicity, we will refer to this subpace as the subspace defined by
A
.

Definition 5.4
.

We say an anti-crossing is an
(L,R)
-anti-crossing at
t_{*}
if there exist
bare energies
E_{0}^{L}(t)
and
E_{0}^{R}(t)
such that:

1.

E_{0}^{L}(t)
and
E_{0}^{R}(t)
approximate the unperturbed energy levels
of the effective
2\times 2
Hamiltonian describing the anti-crossing
for
t\in[t_{*}-\delta,t_{*}+\delta]
for some small
\delta>0
; and

2.

E_{0}^{L}(t)
and
E_{0}^{R}(t)
cross at
t_{*}
, i.e.
E_{0}^{L}(t_{*})=E_{0}^{R}(t_{*})
.

See Figure
5
for an illustration.

Figure 5
:

Schematic of an
(L,R)
-anti-crossing.
Dashed lines: bare energies
E_{0}^{L}(t)
and
E_{0}^{R}(t)
crossing at
t_{*}
.
Solid gray curves: the two lowest eigenvalues of the Hamiltonian, showing
the avoided crossing that originates from this bare crossing.

In this sense, the anti-crossing is said to originate from the crossing of

E_{0}^{L}(t)
and
E_{0}^{R}(t)
. The unperturbed levels define the formal structure,
while the bare energies provide concrete analytic proxies that allow us to
identify and study the anti-crossing without explicitly constructing the
effective
2\times 2
Hamiltonian, which could be challenging.
In the companion paper
[
Choi-Limitation
]
, an effective
2\times 2
Hamiltonian
is explicitly constructed to derive a perturbative bound on the anti-crossing gap.

5.1.2
Types of Anti-Crossing

We classify anti-crossings according to whether the subspaces defined by
L

and
R
, when embedded in the full Hilbert space, overlap or are disjoint.

Tunneling-Induced Anti-Crossing.

If the embedded subspaces of
L
and
R
overlap, then
H_{L}
and
H_{R}

are submatrices of the same block in the Hilbert space decomposition.
In this case, off-diagonal terms within the block induce tunneling between
the two configurations. We refer to this as a

tunneling-induced anti-crossing
.

Block-Level Anti-Crossing.

If the embedded subspaces of
L
and
R
are disjoint, then
H_{L}
and
H_{R}

belong to two distinct blocks of the Hamiltonian. In this case, the ground
states of the two blocks may become nearly degenerate, leading to a true
crossing if the blocks are decoupled, or to an avoided crossing if
there is weak but nonzero inter-block coupling.
We refer to this as a
block-level anti-crossing
.

Figure
5
provides a generic schematic of an
(L,R)
-anti-crossing,
whether tunneling-induced or block-level. The interpretation depends on the
relation between the subspaces defined by
L
and
R
. In the block-level
case, for example, the two levels correspond to the ground states of two
distinct blocks—the same-sign block
H_{L}
(blue) and the opposite-sign
block
H_{R}
(red). A weak inter-block coupling lifts their degeneracy and
produces an avoided crossing. When the resulting gap is small, the system
evolves almost as if the blocks were decoupled, remaining adiabatically in
the same-sign block and following the blue path.

The original double anti-crossing (DAC) observed in
[
Choi2021
]
is, in
fact, a
double block-level anti-crossing
. Thus, instead of a diabatic transition—from ground to
excited to ground state—the evolution effectively remains confined to the
same-sign block, without any true inter-block transition. In this sense, the
block-level anti-crossing is dynamically bypassed.

There is also a more subtle variant, in which one of the two competing levels
is not an eigenstate of a single block, but a superposition of states from
different blocks. In this case, the coupling is not merely perturbative; the
anti-crossing reflects quantum interference between blocks. We refer to
this as an
interference-involved block-level anti-crossing
, an example is
shown in Figure
24
.

5.2
The Basic Matrix
\mathbbm{B}(w,x)
: Eigenvalues and Eigenstates

We define the following effective two-level Hamiltonian, which will serve as a basic building block for our analysis throughout the paper:

\mathbbm{B}(w,x):=\begin{bmatrix}-w&-\tfrac{1}{2}x\\
-\tfrac{1}{2}x&0\end{bmatrix},

(5.1)

where
w=w(t)
and
x=x(t)
are real-valued parameters, typically derived from problem Hamiltonians and driver strengths. This is a special case of a spin-
\tfrac{1}{2}
system, with analytic eigenstructure.
The eigenvalues of
\mathbbm{B}(w,x)

\beta_{k}=-\tfrac{1}{2}\!\left(w+(-1)^{k}\sqrt{w^{2}+x^{2}}\right),\quad k=0,1,

with normalized eigenvectors

\ket{\beta_{0}}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{1+\gamma^{2}}}\bigl(\gamma\ket{0}+\ket{1}\bigr),\quad\ket{\beta_{1}}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{1+\gamma^{2}}}\bigl(\ket{0}-\gamma\ket{1}\bigr),

(5.3)

where the mixing coefficient is

\gamma=\tfrac{x}{w+\sqrt{w^{2}+x^{2}}}.

Remark 5.5
.

For
w>0
, we use the standard basis

\ket{0}=\bigl[1,0\bigr]^{T}
,
\ket{1}=\bigl[0,1\bigr]^{T}
.
For
w<0
, the roles flip:

\ket{0}(w<0)=\ket{1}(w>0)
and

\ket{1}(w<0)=\ket{0}(w>0)
.

and

6
A Single Clique: Decomposition and Low-Energy Subspace Analysis

In this section, we focus on analyzing the Hilbert space associated with a single clique.
We begin, in Section
6.1
, by describing a natural decomposition of the Hilbert space into a low-energy subspace, consisting of independent-set states,
and a high-energy subspace, consisting of dependent-set states.
We describe the angular momentum structure and spectral properties of the low-energy subspace in Section
6.2
.

To fix notation, let
G_{c}=(V,E)
be a clique,
where
V=\{1,\dots,n_{c}\}
is the set of vertices, and

E=\{(i,j):i<j,\;i,j\in V\}
is the set of edges.
We assume all vertices in the clique have the same weight
w_{c}
,
i.e.,
w_{i}=w_{c}
for all
i\in V
.
For clarity, we will omit the graph argument
(G_{c})
in this section, with the understanding that all quantities refer to this single clique.

6.1
Decomposition of the Clique Space: Low and High Energy Subspaces

The Hilbert space
\mathcal{V}_{c}
for a clique of size
n_{c}
consists of
2^{n_{c}}
computational basis states, each corresponding to a binary string of length
n_{c}
. Among these, only
n_{c}+1
basis states correspond to independent sets:

\begin{array}[]{l}\mathbf{b}_{0}=\texttt{00\ldots 0}\\
\mathbf{b}_{1}=\texttt{10\ldots 0}\\
\mathbf{b}_{2}=\texttt{01\ldots 0}\\
\quad\vdots\\
\mathbf{b}_{n_{c}}=\texttt{00\ldots 1}\end{array}

with

\mathbb{N}_{\text{ind}}=\left\{\mathbf{b}_{0},\mathbf{b}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{b}_{n_{c}}\right\},

where each
\mathbf{b}_{i}
is a binary string of length
n_{c}
with a single 1 in position
i
(and 0s elsewhere), and
\mathbf{b}_{0}
is the all-zero string.

The energy associated with each singleton state is
-w_{c}
, while the empty set has energy 0.
In contrast, the energy of all other bit strings—which correspond to dependent sets—is at least
(J_{\mathsf{zz}}^{\text{clique}}-2w_{c})
.
By choosing
J_{\mathsf{zz}}^{\text{clique}}
sufficiently large, these dependent-set states become energetically inaccessible.
Hence, the Hilbert space admits a natural decomposition:

\mathcal{V}_{c}=\mathcal{L}^{\text{ind}}\oplus\mathcal{H}^{\text{dep}},

(6.1)

where
\mathcal{L}^{\text{ind}}
is the low-energy subspace spanned by
\mathbb{N}_{\text{ind}}
,
and
\mathcal{H}^{\text{dep}}
is the high-energy subspace spanned by the dependent-set states.

The remainder of this section focuses on the analysis of the low-energy subspace
\mathcal{L}^{\text{ind}}
.

6.2
Angular-Momentum Decomposition of the Low-Energy Subspace

The low-energy subspace
\mathcal{L}^{\text{ind}}
of a clique can be naturally expressed in the

total angular momentum basis
.
By changing to this basis,
\mathcal{L}^{\text{ind}}
decomposes into a single effective spin-
\tfrac{1}{2}

(two-dimensional same-sign sector) together with
(n_{c}-1)
spin-0
(one-dimensional opposite-sign) components.
In this representation, the relevant spin operators take a block-diagonal form,
which induces a block-diagonal structure in the restricted Hamiltonian.
This block-diagonal restricted Hamiltonian yields exact spectral formulas,
explains the
J_{\mathsf{xx}}
see-saw effect between the same-sign and opposite-sign blocks,
and extends naturally to the case of coupled subcliques, a key step toward the global
block decomposition of shared-structure graphs.

6.2.1
Low-Energy Subspace in Total Angular Momentum Basis
{\mathcal{B}}_{a}

Lemma 6.1
.

The total angular momentum basis for
\mathcal{L}^{\text{ind}}
consists of the states:

\displaystyle({\mathcal{B}}_{a})\left\{\begin{array}[]{llll}\ket{s,-(s-1)},&\ket{1,(s-1),-(s-1)},&\ldots&\ket{n_{c}-1,(s-1),-(s-1)}\\
\ket{s,-s}&&&\end{array}\right.

where
s=\tfrac{n_{c}}{2}
is the total spin.

Explicitly:

•

\ket{s,-s}=\ket{\mathbf{b}_{0}}
, representing the empty set.

•

\ket{s,-(s-1)}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{n_{c}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{c}}\ket{\mathbf{b}_{i}}
, a uniform superposition of all singletons with positive amplitudes.

•

\ket{k,s-1,-(s-1)}
, for
k=1,\ldots,n_{c}-1
, consists of a superposition of singleton states with both positive and negative amplitudes.

Thus,
\ket{s,-s}
and
\ket{s,-(s-1)}
are same-sign states, while
\ket{k,s-1,-(s-1)}
are opposite-sign states.

Proof.

The total Hilbert space of
n_{c}
spin-
\tfrac{1}{2}
particles decomposes into irreducible representations of total spin
s=\tfrac{n_{c}}{2}
,
followed by smaller spin sectors
(s-1,s-2,\ldots)
. According to the Clebsch–Gordan decomposition of
n_{c}
spin-
\tfrac{1}{2}
particles, we have:

\underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2}\otimes\tfrac{1}{2}\otimes\ldots\otimes\tfrac{1}{2}}_{n_{c}}=\tfrac{n_{c}}{2}\oplus\underbrace{\tfrac{n_{c}}{2}-1\oplus\ldots\oplus\tfrac{n_{c}}{2}-1}_{n_{c}-1}\oplus\ldots

This decomposition is a standard result in angular momentum theory and provides a natural organization of basis states according to their total spin quantum numbers.
The key observation is that the independent-set states reside in the lowest-weight subspace, corresponding to the smallest
m_{s}
values within these spin multiplets. Specifically, the independent-set states are spanned by the lowest-weight states
(m_{s}=-s,-(s-1))
in the spin-
s
multiplet and the last
(m_{s}=-(s-1))
states in spin-
(s-1)
multiplets. This yields the following basis:

({\mathcal{B}}_{a})\left\{\begin{array}[]{llll}\ket{s,-(s-1)},&\ket{1,(s-1),-(s-1)},&\ldots&\ket{n_{c}-1,(s-1),-(s-1)}\\
\ket{s,-s}&&&\end{array}\right.

One can check that:

\ket{s,-(s-1)}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{n_{c}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{c}}\ket{\mathbf{b}_{i}},\quad\ket{s,-s}=\ket{\mathbf{b}_{0}}.

For
k=1,\ldots,n_{c}-1
, let
\ket{k,s-1,-(s-1)}
denote the last (
m_{s}=-(s-1)
) state of spin-
(s-1)
. Since these states share the same
m_{s}
value, they must be orthogonal to
\ket{s,-(s-1)}
:

\ket{k,s-1,-(s-1)}=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{c}}a_{k_{i}}\ket{\mathbf{b}_{i}},

where
a_{k_{i}}
are Clebsch–Gordan coefficients.

For each
k\geq 1
, at least one coefficient
a_{k,j}<0
for some
j
, indicating that the superposition contains both positive and negative amplitudes. Hence, these states are opposite-sign.
∎

Remark (Basis Reordering).

For convenience, we reorder the basis states in
{\mathcal{B}}_{a}
as follows:

({\mathcal{B}}_{a}^{\prime})\quad\ket{s,-(s-1)},\,\ket{s,-s},\,\ket{1,s-1,-(s-1)},\,\dots,\,\ket{n_{c}-1,s-1,-(s-1)}.

That is, we swap the order of the two same-sign basis states.
This ordering simplifies the representation of operators in the next steps.

Basis Transformation.

The transformation between the computational basis
\{\ket{\mathbf{b}_{i}}\}
and the angular momentum basis
({\mathcal{B}}_{a}^{\prime})
can be derived either from the Clebsch–Gordan coefficients or directly from the relationships established in the proof. Specifically:

•

The state
\ket{s,-(s-1)}
is a uniform superposition over all singleton states
\{\ket{\mathbf{b}_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{n_{c}}
.

•

The remaining states
\ket{k,s-1,-(s-1)}
, for
k=1,\dots,n_{c}-1
, form an orthogonal complement to
\ket{s,-(s-1)}
within the subspace spanned by
\{\ket{\mathbf{b}_{1}},\dots,\ket{\mathbf{b}_{n_{c}}}\}
.

We denote the basis transformation matrix from the computational basis to the angular momentum basis by
{\mathbf{U}^{\mathsf{clique}}}
.

6.2.2
Spin Operators in the
{\mathcal{B}}_{a}^{\prime}
Basis

Consider the spin operators on
\mathcal{V}_{c}
:

\mathrm{S}_{\mathsf{Z}}=\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{c}}\sigma_{i}^{z},\quad\mathrm{S}_{\mathsf{\tilde{Z}}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{c}}\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{i},\quad\mathrm{S}_{\mathsf{X}}=\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{c}}\sigma_{i}^{x},\quad\mathrm{S}_{\mathsf{XX}}=\tfrac{1}{4}\sum_{ij\in{\mathsf{E}}(G_{\text{driver}})}\sigma_{i}^{x}\sigma_{j}^{x},

where
G_{\text{driver}}=G_{c}
.

We project these operators onto the low-energy subspace
\mathcal{L}^{\text{ind}}
using the projection operator
{\Pi^{\tiny{\mathsf{ind}}}}
, and then transform them into the
{\mathcal{B}}_{a}^{\prime}
basis via the basis transformation
{\mathbf{U}^{\mathsf{clique}}}
. For any operator
\mathrm{X}
, we use a bar to denote the operator:

\overline{\mathrm{X}}={\mathbf{U}^{\mathsf{clique}}}^{\dagger}{\Pi^{\tiny{\mathsf{ind}}}}\mathrm{X}{\Pi^{\tiny{\mathsf{ind}}}}{\mathbf{U}^{\mathsf{clique}}}.

Theorem 6.2
.

The restricted operators in the
{\mathcal{B}}_{a}^{\prime}
basis are block-diagonal and given explicitly by:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}&\overline{\mathrm{S}_{\mathsf{\tilde{Z}}}}=\tilde{\sigma}^{z}\oplus 1\oplus\cdots\oplus 1,\\[5.0pt]
&\overline{\mathrm{S}_{\mathsf{X}}}=\tfrac{\sqrt{n_{c}}}{2}\sigma^{x}\oplus 0\oplus\cdots\oplus 0,\\[5.0pt]
&\overline{\mathrm{S}_{\mathsf{XX}}}=\left(\tfrac{n_{c}-1}{4}\right)\tilde{\sigma}^{z}\oplus\left(-\tfrac{1}{4}\right)\oplus\cdots\oplus\left(-\tfrac{1}{4}\right).\end{array}\right.

where
\tilde{\sigma}^{z}
and
\sigma^{x}
act on the effective spin-
\tfrac{1}{2}
(two-dimensional same-sign) subspace, while the scalars act on spin-0 (one-dimensional opposite-sign) subspaces.

Proof.

Recall the reordered basis
{\mathcal{B}}_{a}^{\prime}
is:

\{|s,-(s-1)\rangle,\quad|s,-s\rangle,\quad|1,s-1,-(s-1)\rangle,\dots,\ket{n_{c}-1,s-1,-(s-1)}\}.

In standard angular momentum theory, the eigenvalues of
\mathrm{S}_{\mathsf{Z}}
for these states are:

•

\ket{s,-(s-1)}
: eigenvalue
m_{s}=-(s-1)

•

\ket{s,-s}
: eigenvalue
-s

•

\ket{k,s-1,-(s-1)}
: eigenvalue
-(s-1)
for all
k

Thus:

\overline{\mathrm{S}_{\mathsf{Z}}}=\begin{bmatrix}-(s-1)&0&0&\cdots&0\\
0&-s&0&\cdots&0\\
0&0&-(s-1)&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&0&\cdots&-(s-1)\end{bmatrix}

and the shifted operator
\overline{\mathrm{S}_{\mathsf{\tilde{Z}}}}
becomes:

\displaystyle\overline{\mathrm{S}_{\mathsf{\tilde{Z}}}}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0&\cdots&0\\
0&0&0&\cdots&0\\
0&0&1&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&0&\cdots&1\end{bmatrix}=\tilde{\sigma}^{z}\oplus 1\oplus\cdots\oplus 1.

To obtain
\overline{\mathrm{S}_{\mathsf{X}}}
, recall:

\mathrm{S}_{\mathsf{X}}=\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathrm{S}_{+}+\mathrm{S}_{-}),

where
\mathrm{S}_{+}
and
\mathrm{S}_{-}
are the (raising and lowering) ladder operators.
From angular momentum algebra:

\braket{s,-(s-1)|\mathrm{S}_{+}|s,-s}=\braket{s,-s|\mathrm{S}_{-}|s,-(s-1)}=\sqrt{n_{c}},

thus:

\overline{\mathrm{S}_{\mathsf{X}}}=\tfrac{\sqrt{n_{c}}}{2}\sigma^{x}\oplus 0\oplus\cdots\oplus 0.

Since
\mathrm{S}_{+}^{2}=\mathrm{S}_{-}^{2}=0
on
\mathcal{L}^{\text{ind}}
, it follows:

\mathrm{S}_{\mathsf{X}}^{2}=\tfrac{1}{4}(\mathrm{S}_{+}\mathrm{S}_{-}+\mathrm{S}_{-}\mathrm{S}_{+}),

and:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}&\mathrm{S}_{\mathsf{X}}^{2}\ket{s,-s}=\tfrac{n_{c}}{4}\ket{s,-s},\\
&\mathrm{S}_{\mathsf{X}}^{2}\ket{s,-(s-1)}=\tfrac{3n_{c}-2}{4}\ket{s,-(s-1)},\\
&\mathrm{S}_{\mathsf{X}}^{2}\ket{s-1,-(s-1)}=\tfrac{n_{c}-2}{4}\ket{s-1,-(s-1)}.\end{array}\right.

Using:

\mathrm{S}_{\mathsf{XX}}=\tfrac{1}{2}(4\mathrm{S}_{\mathsf{X}}^{2}-n_{c}),

we conclude:

\overline{\mathrm{S}_{\mathsf{XX}}}=\left(\tfrac{n_{c}-1}{4}\right)\tilde{\sigma}^{z}\oplus\left(-\tfrac{1}{4}\right)\oplus\cdots\oplus\left(-\tfrac{1}{4}\right).

∎

6.2.3
Decomposition into Spin-
\tfrac{1}{2}
and Spin-
0
Components

The transformed operators given by Theorem
6.2
offer a clear physical interpretation:
the low-energy subspace
\mathcal{L}^{\text{ind}}
decomposes into a direct sum consisting of a single effective spin-
\tfrac{1}{2}
subsystem,
together with
n_{c}-1
(opposite-sign) spin-
0
subsystems:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\text{ind}}=\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\right]_{n_{c}}\oplus\underbrace{0\oplus\cdots\oplus 0}_{n_{c}-1}.

(6.7)

The effective spin-
\tfrac{1}{2}
subsystem
\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\right]_{n_{c}}
is spanned by the two same-sign basis states:

\left|\tfrac{1}{2},-\tfrac{1}{2}\right\rangle=|s,-(s-1)\rangle,\quad\left|\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2}\right\rangle=|s,-s\rangle.

The spin-
0
components correspond to the opposite-sign basis vectors:

|k,s-1,-(s-1)\rangle,
for
k=1,\dots,n_{c}-1.

Correspondingly, the Hamiltonian decomposes into a same-sign two-dimensional effective spin-
\tfrac{1}{2}
block and
n_{c}-1
opposite-sign spin-0 blocks.
The system Hamiltonian
\mathbbm{H}_{1}
, which governs the evolution during Stages 1 and 2, when restricted to the low-energy subspace
\mathcal{L}^{\text{ind}}
of the clique
G_{c}
, takes the form

\overline{\mathbbm{H}_{1}}=-\mathtt{x}\,\overline{\mathrm{S}_{\mathsf{X}}}+\mathtt{jxx}\,\overline{\mathrm{S}_{\mathsf{XX}}}-w_{c}\,\overline{\mathrm{S}_{\mathsf{\tilde{Z}}}}.

Substituting the operator expressions from Eq.
6.2
in Theorem
6.2
, we obtain

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbbm{H}_{1}}

6.2.5
Transformation via Two Coupled Subcliques

In this section, we reinterpret a single clique as two coupled subcliques and derive the transformation needed to express the partial clique
\mathsf{ZZ}
-coupling.

Consider a clique
c
composed of two subcliques
a
and
b
, with
n_{c}=n_{a}+n_{b}
.
We apply the single-clique analysis from Eq. (
6.7
) independently to each subclique, yielding:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{a}

\displaystyle=\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\right]_{n_{a}}\oplus\underbrace{0\oplus\cdots\oplus 0}_{n_{a}-1},

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{b}

\displaystyle=\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\right]_{n_{b}}\oplus\underbrace{0\oplus\cdots\oplus 0}_{n_{b}-1}.

The low-energy subspace
\mathcal{L}^{c}
of clique

is the restricted subspace within the tensor product of
\mathcal{L}^{a}
and
\mathcal{L}^{b}
.
Projecting onto the low-energy sector of two spin-
\tfrac{1}{2}
systems yields an effective spin-
\tfrac{1}{2}
for the full clique, along with an additional spin-0 component:

{\Pi^{\tiny{\mathsf{ind}}}}\left(\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\right]_{n_{a}}\otimes\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\right]_{n_{b}}\right){\Pi^{\tiny{\mathsf{ind}}}}=\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\right]_{n_{c}}\oplus 0.

We now describe the corresponding Hamiltonians. For simplicity, we drop the time parameter
(t)
.

The Hamiltonians for the effective spin-
\tfrac{1}{2}
block are explicitly:

\mathbb{B}_{a}=\begin{bmatrix}-\left(\mathtt{w}-\tfrac{n_{a}-1}{4}\mathtt{jxx}\right)&-\tfrac{\sqrt{n_{a}}}{2}\mathtt{x}\\[6.0pt]
-\tfrac{\sqrt{n_{a}}}{2}\mathtt{x}&0\end{bmatrix},\quad\mathbb{B}_{b}=\begin{bmatrix}-\left(\mathtt{w}-\tfrac{n_{b}-1}{4}\mathtt{jxx}\right)&-\tfrac{\sqrt{n_{b}}}{2}\mathtt{x}\\[6.0pt]
-\tfrac{\sqrt{n_{b}}}{2}\mathtt{x}&0\end{bmatrix}.

The combined low-energy Hamiltonian is:

\mathbb{D}_{c}={\Pi^{\tiny{\mathsf{ind}}}}\left(\mathbb{B}_{a}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{b}+\mathbb{I}_{a}\otimes\mathbb{B}_{b}+H_{\mathsf{XX}}\right){\Pi^{\tiny{\mathsf{ind}}}},

which takes the explicit

\mathbb{D}_{c}=\begin{bmatrix}-\left(\mathtt{w}-\tfrac{n_{a}-1}{4}\mathtt{jxx}\right)&-\tfrac{\sqrt{n_{a}}}{2}\mathtt{x}&\tfrac{\sqrt{n_{a}n_{b}}}{4}\mathtt{jxx}\\[6.0pt]
-\tfrac{\sqrt{n_{a}}}{2}\mathtt{x}&0&-\tfrac{\sqrt{n_{b}}}{2}\mathtt{x}\\[6.0pt]
\tfrac{\sqrt{n_{a}n_{b}}}{4}\mathtt{jxx}&-\tfrac{\sqrt{n_{b}}}{2}\mathtt{x}&-\left(\mathtt{w}-\tfrac{n_{b}-1}{4}\mathtt{jxx}\right)\end{bmatrix}.

To recover the effective spin-
\tfrac{1}{2}
plus zero-spin structure, we apply the basis transformation:

U_{\mathsf{merge}}=\begin{bmatrix}\sqrt{\tfrac{n_{a}}{n_{c}}}&0&-\sqrt{\tfrac{n_{b}}{n_{c}}}\\[6.0pt]
0&1&0\\[6.0pt]
\sqrt{\tfrac{n_{b}}{n_{c}}}&0&\sqrt{\tfrac{n_{a}}{n_{c}}}\end{bmatrix},

where the columns correspond to the transformed basis states:

\ket{1_{c}}=\sqrt{\tfrac{n_{a}}{n_{c}}}\ket{1_{a}0_{b}}+\sqrt{\tfrac{n_{b}}{n_{c}}}\ket{0_{a}1_{b}},\quad\ket{0_{c}}=\ket{0_{a}0_{b}},\quad\ket{\odot_{q}}=-\sqrt{\tfrac{n_{b}}{n_{c}}}\ket{1_{a}0_{b}}+\sqrt{\tfrac{n_{a}}{n_{c}}}\ket{0_{a}1_{b}}.

In this basis, the Hamiltonian simplifies to:

U_{\mathsf{merge}}^{\dagger}\mathbb{D}_{c}U_{\mathsf{merge}}=\begin{bmatrix}-\left(\mathtt{w}-\tfrac{n_{c}-1}{4}\mathtt{jxx}\right)&-\tfrac{\sqrt{n_{c}}}{2}\mathtt{x}&0\\[6.0pt]
-\tfrac{\sqrt{n_{c}}}{2}\mathtt{x}&0&0\\[6.0pt]
0&0&-\left(\mathtt{w}+\tfrac{1}{4}\mathtt{jxx}\right)\end{bmatrix},

which clearly isolates the effective spin-
\tfrac{1}{2}
subsystem and the spin-0 subsystem, as described at the outset of this section.

7
Analytical Solution for the Bare Subsystem (
\mathtt{dMIC}
)

We define the
bare subsystem
as the system restricted to a given
\mathtt{dMIC}
,
that is, a subgraph consisting of independent cliques
\{\text{Clique}(w_{i},n_{i})\}_{i=1}^{k}
.
A
\mathtt{dMIC}
defines either a critical local minima (
\mathtt{LM}
), or the global minimum (
\mathtt{GM}
) where each clique has size one.
It is a
subsystem
because we consider only the subgraph induced by this
\mathtt{dMIC}
,
and it is
bare
because it is fully decoupled from the rest of the graph.

We refer to the bare subsystem induced by the set of cliques in
L
(generating the local minima
\mathtt{LM}
)
as the
L
-bare subsystem
, denoted by
\mathbbm{H}_{L}^{\mathsf{bare}}
.
Similarly, the bare subsystem induced by the subset
R\subseteq\mathtt{GM}
is called the
R
-bare subsystem
,
denoted by
\mathbbm{H}_{R}^{\mathsf{bare}}
, and referred to as the
GM-bare subsystem
when
R=\mathtt{GM}
.
In our analysis in later sections, we will express the total Hamiltonian
in terms of the
L
- and
R
-bare subsystems together with their coupling.
Throughout, we distinguish bare subsystem operators from their full-system counterparts
by using the superscript
bare
; the same symbol without the superscript
refers to the corresponding operator in the full system.

The bare subsystem admits a fully analytical treatment due to its tensor-product structure across independent cliques. Our analysis proceeds in three steps, and the resulting closed-form solutions will later be used to derive analytical bounds on
J_{\mathsf{xx}}
.

•

First, we decompose the low-energy subspace of the bare subsystem into three types of sectors:
the
same-sign sector

\mathcal{C}^{\mathsf{bare}}
, the
all-spin-zero (AS0) opposite-sign sector

\mathcal{Q}^{\mathsf{bare}}
, and the
intermediate opposite-sign sectors
.
We define corresponding Hamiltonians for each:
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathcal{C}^{\mathsf{bare}}}
,
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathcal{Q}^{\mathsf{bare}}}
, and
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathcal{W}^{\mathsf{bare}}}
, where
\mathcal{W}^{\mathsf{bare}}
denotes a typical intermediate sector.

•

Second, we derive a closed-form solution for the same-sign block
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathcal{C}^{\mathsf{bare}}}
,
including explicit expressions for its eigenvalues and eigenstates.
In the case of uniform clique sizes, we perform a symmetric-subspace reduction based on angular momentum structure,
reducing the Hilbert space dimension from
2^{m}
to
m+1
.

•

Third, the ground-state energies of the blocks are totally ordered.
Depending on the relative energy of the spin-
\tfrac{1}{2}
and spin-
0
components, the ordering takes one of two forms:
either the same-sign sector has the lowest energy and the AS0 opposite-sign sector the highest,
or the order is reversed.

7.1
Low-Energy Structure of the Bare Subsystem: Same-Sign and Opposite-Sign Sectors

Let
G_{\mathsf{mic}}
consist of
m
independent cliques
\mathsf{Clique}(w_{i},n_{i})
,
where
w_{i}\equiv 1
denotes the vertex weight and
n_{i}
the size of the
i
th clique.
This graph admits
\prod_{i=1}^{m}n_{i}
degenerate maximal independent sets of size
m
.

From the single-clique result (Eq. (
6.7
)), the low-energy subspace of each clique decomposes as:

\mathcal{L}_{i}=\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\right]_{n_{i}}\oplus\underbrace{0\oplus\cdots\oplus 0}_{n_{i}-1}.

Hence, the total low-energy subspace
\mathcal{L}
of the bare subsystem is given by:

\mathcal{L}=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m}\mathcal{L}_{i}=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m}\left(\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\right]_{n_{i}}\oplus\underbrace{0\oplus\cdots\oplus 0}_{n_{i}-1}\right)=\bigoplus\left(\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m}\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\text{ or }0\right]\right),

where the direct sum ranges over all tensor products selecting one spin-
\tfrac{1}{2}
and
n_{i}-1
spin-zeros from each clique.

This decomposition yields
\prod_{i=1}^{m}n_{i}
block subspaces. Among them, the block

\mathcal{C}^{\mathsf{bare}}:=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m}\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\right]_{n_{i}}

is composed entirely of spin-
\tfrac{1}{2}
factors and is referred to as the
same-sign sector
.

At the opposite extreme, there are
\prod_{i=1}^{m}(n_{i}-1)
blocks composed entirely of spin-
0
factors:

\mathcal{Q}^{\mathsf{bare}}:=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m}0,

referred to as the
all-spin-zero (AS0) opposite-sign sector
.

The remaining opposite-sign blocks, each containing a mixture of spin-
\tfrac{1}{2}
and spin-
0
components, are called the
intermediate sectors
.
Let
\mathcal{W}^{\mathsf{bare}}
denote one such intermediate sector.

We denote the restriction of the Hamiltonian
\mathbbm{H}_{1}
to each sector by:

\mathbbm{H}_{\mathcal{C}^{\mathsf{bare}}}:=\text{projection of }\mathbbm{H}_{1}\text{ to }\mathcal{C}^{\mathsf{bare}},\quad\mathbbm{H}_{\mathcal{Q}^{\mathsf{bare}}}:=\text{projection of }\mathbbm{H}_{1}\text{ to }\mathcal{Q}^{\mathsf{bare}},\quad\mathbbm{H}_{\mathcal{W}^{\mathsf{bare}}}:=\text{projection of }\mathbbm{H}_{1}\text{ to }\mathcal{W}^{\mathsf{bare}}.

The same-sign block Hamiltonian is given by:

\displaystyle\mathbbm{H}_{\mathcal{C}^{\mathsf{bare}}}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbbm{B}_{i}\left(\mathtt{w^{\text{\tiny eff}}_{i}},\,\sqrt{n_{i}}\,\mathtt{x}\right),

(7.1)

where

\mathtt{w^{\text{\tiny eff}}_{i}}=\left(w_{i}-\tfrac{n_{i}-1}{4}\mathtt{jxx}\right),

and each
\mathbbm{B}_{i}
is a two-level block operator defined as:

\mathbbm{B}_{i}=\mathbbm{I}_{2}\otimes\cdots\otimes\underbrace{\mathbbm{B}}_{\text{$i$th position}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbbm{I}_{2},

with
\mathbbm{B}
being the two-level Hamiltonian from Eq.
5.1
, and
\mathbbm{I}_{2}
the
2\times 2
identity matrix.

The AS0 opposite-sign block Hamiltonian is:

\mathbbm{H}_{\mathcal{Q}^{\mathsf{bare}}}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}-\left(w_{i}+\tfrac{1}{4}\mathtt{jxx}\right),

which evaluates to a single-value offset.

The intermediate block Hamiltonian
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathcal{W}^{\mathsf{bare}}}
can be expressed in terms of a same-sign block Hamiltonian with fewer active

terms, accompanied by an appropriate energy shift.

In the following, we present a closed-form solution for the same-sign block; analogous expressions for the intermediate blocks follow similarly.

7.2
Closed-Form Solution of the Same-Sign Block
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathcal{C}^{\mathsf{bare}}}

Since each
\mathbbm{B}_{i}
in
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathcal{C}^{\mathsf{bare}}}
(in Eq. (
7.1
))
acts on a disjoint spin-
\tfrac{1}{2}
subsystem, the full Hamiltonian
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathcal{C}^{\mathsf{bare}}}
is frustration-free.
Its eigenstates are tensor products of the eigenstates of the individual
\mathbbm{B}_{i}
, and its eigenvalues are additive:

\mathbbm{H}_{\mathcal{C}^{\mathsf{bare}}}\ket{\psi_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\ket{\psi_{m}}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\beta_{k_{i}}^{(i)}\right)\ket{\psi_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\ket{\psi_{m}},

where
\ket{\psi_{i}}\in\{\ket{\beta_{0}^{(i)}},\,\ket{\beta_{1}^{(i)}}\}
are the eigenstates of the
i
th two-level
block
\mathbbm{B}_{i}
, and
\beta_{k}^{(i)}
are the corresponding eigenvalues
(Eqs. (
5.2
), (
5.3
)).

As a result, the full spectrum and eigensystem of the same-sign block
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathcal{C}^{\mathsf{bare}}}
are exactly solvable.

Theorem 7.1

(Exact Spectrum and Ground State of the Same-Sign Block)
.

Let
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathcal{C}^{\mathsf{bare}}}
be given as in Eq. (
7.1
).
Then all eigenvalues and eigenstates of
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathcal{C}^{\mathsf{bare}}}
are exactly solvable.
Each eigenstate is indexed by a bit string
z=z_{1}z_{2}\ldots z_{m}\in\{0,1\}^{m}
, with:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}E_{z}&=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\beta_{{z_{i}}}^{(i)},\\
\ket{E_{z}}&=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m}\ket{\beta_{{z_{i}}}^{(i)}},\end{array}\right.

where
\beta_{k}^{(i)}
and
\ket{\beta_{k}^{(i)}}
, for
k=0,1
, are the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the local two-level subsystem
\mathbbm{B}_{i}
, given by:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\beta_{k}^{(i)}&=-\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\mathtt{w^{\text{\tiny eff}}_{i}}+(-1)^{k}\sqrt{\left[\mathtt{w^{\text{\tiny eff}}_{i}}\right]^{2}+n_{i}\,\mathtt{x}^{2}}\right),\\
\ket{\beta_{0}^{(i)}}&=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{1+\gamma_{i}^{2}}}\left(\gamma_{i}\ket{0}_{i}+\ket{1}_{i}\right),\quad\ket{\beta_{1}^{(i)}}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{1+\gamma_{i}^{2}}}\left(\ket{0}_{i}-\gamma_{i}\ket{1}_{i}\right),\end{cases}

with mixing ratio

\gamma_{i}=\tfrac{\sqrt{n_{i}}\,\mathtt{x}}{\mathtt{w^{\text{\tiny eff}}_{i}}+\sqrt{[\mathtt{w^{\text{\tiny eff}}_{i}}]^{2}+n_{i}\,\mathtt{x}^{2}}}.

In particular, the ground state corresponds to the all-zero bit string
z=0\ldots 0
, and is given by:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\ket{E_{0}}&=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m}\ket{\beta_{0}^{(i)}},\\[4.0pt]
E_{0}&=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\beta_{0}^{(i)}=-\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\mathtt{w^{\text{\tiny eff}}_{i}}+\sqrt{\left[\mathtt{w^{\text{\tiny eff}}_{i}}\right]^{2}+n_{i}\,\mathtt{x}^{2}}\right).\end{cases}

7.3
Uniform Clique Size: Symmetric-Subspace Reduction of the Same-Sign Block

In the case when all cliques have the same size

The same-sign block
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathcal{C}^{\mathsf{bare}}}

Using the same technique of transforming to the total angular momentum basis, this space decomposes into a direct sum of angular momentum sectors.
In particular,

\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\right]_{n_{c}}^{\otimes m}=\underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2}\otimes\cdots\otimes\tfrac{1}{2}}_{m}=\tfrac{m}{2}\oplus\underbrace{\left(\tfrac{m}{2}-1\right)\oplus\cdots\oplus\left(\tfrac{m}{2}-1\right)}_{m-1}\oplus\cdots

\quad\vdots\\[6.0pt]

Each

We interpret

Operators in the Symmetric Subspace.

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}s&0&\cdots&0\\

\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\

Recall that the transverse operator
\mathrm{S}_{\mathsf{X}}=\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathrm{S}_{+}+\mathrm{S}_{-})
where

\mathrm{S}_{\pm}\ket{s,m_{s}}=\sqrt{s(s+1)-m_{s}(m_{s}\pm 1)}\,\ket{s,m_{s}\pm 1}.

Letting
m_{s}=s-a

a
counts the number of spin flips downward, for
a=0,1,\ldots,2s

\braket{s,m_{s}|\mathrm{S}_{\mathsf{X}}|s,m_{s}-1}=\tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{(m-a)(a+1)},

where

Hence,

\displaystyle\mathbbm{C}\mathrm{S}_{\mathsf{X}}(m)=\begin{bmatrix}0&\tfrac{\sqrt{m}}{2}&0&\cdots&0\\
\tfrac{\sqrt{m}}{2}&0&\tfrac{\sqrt{2(m-1)}}{2}&\cdots&0\\
0&\tfrac{\sqrt{2(m-1)}}{2}&0&\cdots&0\\

One can again use CG transformation to obtain the restricted same-sign block to the symmetric subspace, which is also known as spanned by
Dicke states

Explicit Transformation to the Symmetric Subspace.

The symmetric subspace of
m
spin-
\tfrac{1}{2}
particles corresponds to the totally symmetric sector of the full Hilbert space
(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes m}
.
This subspace has dimension
m+1
, and is spanned by the
Dicke states
, which are uniform superpositions over computational basis states with fixed Hamming weight.

Dicke Basis.

For each
k=0,1,\dotsc,m
, define the Dicke state:

\ket{s,m_{s}=k-\tfrac{m}{2}}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{\binom{m}{k}}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\vec{z}\in\{0,1\}^{m}\\
\text{Hamming weight}=k\end{subarray}}\ket{\vec{z}}.

These form an orthonormal basis for the symmetric subspace, where
s=\tfrac{m}{2}
and
m_{s}=-s,\dotsc,s
.

Transformation Matrix.

Let
\mathrm{U}_{\mathsf{Dicke}}\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{m}\times(m+1)}
denote the matrix whose columns are the normalized Dicke states (embedded in the full space).
Then
\mathrm{U}_{\mathsf{Dicke}}^{\dagger}\mathrm{U}_{\mathsf{Dicke}}=\mathbb{I}_{m+1}
, and the following operator reductions hold:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\mathbbm{C}\mathrm{S}_{\mathsf{\tilde{Z}}}(m)&=\mathrm{U}_{\mathsf{Dicke}}^{\dagger}\,\mathrm{S}_{\mathsf{\tilde{Z}}}(m)\,\mathrm{U}_{\mathsf{Dicke}},\\
\mathbbm{C}\mathrm{S}_{\mathsf{X}}(m)&=\mathrm{U}_{\mathsf{Dicke}}^{\dagger}\,\mathrm{S}_{\mathsf{X}}(m)\,\mathrm{U}_{\mathsf{Dicke}},\\
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathcal{C}^{\mathsf{bare}}}^{\mathsf{sym}}&=\mathrm{U}_{\mathsf{Dicke}}^{\dagger}\,\mathbbm{H}_{\mathcal{C}^{\mathsf{bare}}}\,\mathrm{U}_{\mathsf{Dicke}}=-\sqrt{n_{c}}\,\mathtt{x}\,\mathbbm{C}\mathrm{S}_{\mathsf{X}}(m)-\mathtt{w^{\text{\tiny eff}}_{c}}\,\mathbbm{C}\mathrm{S}_{\mathsf{\tilde{Z}}}(m).\end{cases}

Theorem 7.2

(Reduction to the Symmetric Subspace via Dicke Basis)
.

Let
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathcal{C}^{\mathsf{bare}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2^{m}\times 2^{m}}

8
Analysis of Stage 0

Recall that the Hamiltonian for Stage 0 is

\mathbbm{H}_{0}(t)=\mathtt{x}(t)\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{X}}+\mathtt{jxx}(t)\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{XX}}+\mathtt{p}(t)\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{problem}},\quad t\in[0,1],

with

\mathtt{x}(t)=(1-t)(\Gamma_{0}-\Gamma_{1})+\Gamma_{1},\mathtt{jxx}(t)=tJ_{\mathsf{xx}},\mathtt{p}(t)=t.

During this phase, the problem parameters
w_{i}
,
J_{\mathsf{zz}}^{\mathsf{clique}}
, and
J_{\mathsf{zz}}

are gradually ramped to their final values as
\mathtt{p}(t)
increases from 0 to 1,
while
\mathtt{jxx}(t)
increases linearly to its target value
J_{\mathsf{xx}}
.

Definition 8.1
.

Given a Hamiltonian
\mathbbm{H}
acting on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{V}
,
and a subspace
\mathcal{L}\subset\mathcal{V}
with orthogonal projector
\Pi_{\mathcal{L}}
,
we define the
restricted Hamiltonian
on
\mathcal{L}
as

\mathbbm{H}|_{\mathcal{L}}:=\Pi_{\mathcal{L}}\mathbbm{H}\Pi_{\mathcal{L}}.

The main goal of this section is to derive the effective Hamiltonian at the end of Stage 0.
The foundational idea follows
[
KKR2006
]
, which presents a systematic approach to constrained Hamiltonians.
The Projection Lemma in
[
KKR2006
]
shows that when a large penalty is assigned to a subspace,
the lowest eigenvalues of the full Hamiltonian are close to those of the restricted Hamiltonian.
To improve this approximation,
[
KKR2006
]
further derives an effective Hamiltonian
by incorporating perturbative corrections from the high-energy subspace.
We adopt a similar approach: by assigning a large penalty
J_{\mathsf{zz}}^{\mathsf{clique}}
to states involving edges within a clique,
we energetically suppress their contribution and restrict the evolution to a reduced subspace.
We then apply perturbation theory to characterize the resulting effective Hamiltonian.

In the following, we first introduce a decomposition of the Hilbert space based on energy penalties from the problem Hamiltonian.
We then derive the effective Hamiltonian and argue that the spectral gap remains large throughout Stage 0,
justifying the adiabatic elimination of high-energy states.

8.1
Decomposition of the Hilbert Space

Recall the MIS-Ising problem Hamiltonian defined in Eq.
2.3
:

\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{problem}}=\sum_{i\in{\mathsf{V}}(G)}(-w_{i})\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{i}+J_{\mathsf{zz}}^{\mathsf{clique}}\sum_{(i,j)\in{\mathsf{E}}(G_{\mathsf{driver}})}\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{i}\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{j}+J_{\mathsf{zz}}\sum_{(i,j)\in{\mathsf{E}}(G)\setminus{\mathsf{E}}(G_{\mathsf{driver}})}\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{i}\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{j}.

The vertex set of the graph is
{\mathsf{V}}(G)=V_{L}\,\cup\,R
,
where
V_{L}
is the union of all cliques in
L
and
R
is the set of vertices outside these cliques.
The corresponding Hilbert space factors as

\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{V}_{L}\otimes\mathcal{V}_{R},

where
\mathcal{V}_{L}
is the Hilbert space of all vertices in
V_{L}
and

\mathcal{V}_{R}
is that of the vertices in
R
.

The parameter
J_{\mathsf{zz}}^{\mathsf{clique}}
penalizes states that include occupied edges within a clique.
By setting
J_{\mathsf{zz}}^{\mathsf{clique}}
sufficiently large, the Hilbert space
\mathcal{V}_{L}
separates into a low-energy subspace
(spanned by all independent-set states within
L
) and a high-energy subspace (spanned by all dependent-set states within
L
).

We define

\mathcal{L}_{-}:=(\text{low-energy subspace of }\mathcal{V}_{L})\otimes\mathcal{V}_{R},\quad\mathcal{L}_{+}:=(\text{high-energy subspace of }\mathcal{V}_{L})\otimes\mathcal{V}_{R}.

Here
\mathcal{L}_{+}
corresponds to high-energy states—each such state containing at least one intra-clique edge incurring the
J_{\mathsf{zz}}^{\mathsf{clique}}
penalty.

Let
\Pi_{-}
and
\Pi_{+}
denote the orthogonal projectors onto
\mathcal{L}_{-}
and
\mathcal{L}_{+}
, respectively.
With respect to this decomposition, the system Hamiltonian at the end of Stage 0 can be written in block form:

\mathbbm{H}_{0}(1)\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}\mathbbm{H}^{\mathsf{low}}&\mathbbm{V}\\
\mathbbm{V}^{\dagger}&\mathbbm{H}^{\mathsf{high}}\end{pmatrix},

with

{\mathbbm{H}}^{\mathsf{low}}=\Pi_{-}\mathbbm{H}_{0}(1)\Pi_{-},\mathbbm{H}^{\mathsf{high}}=\Pi_{+}\mathbbm{H}_{0}(1)\Pi_{+}.

At the end of Stage 0, the full Hamiltonian takes the form

\mathbbm{H}_{0}(1)=\left(\Gamma_{1}\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{X}}+J_{\mathsf{xx}}\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{XX}}+\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{prob}}^{\mathsf{low}}\right)+\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{prob}}^{\mathsf{high}},

where

\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{problem}}=\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{prob}}^{\mathsf{low}}+\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{prob}}^{\mathsf{high}},

with

\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{prob}}^{\mathsf{low}}=\Pi_{-}\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{problem}}\Pi_{-},\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{prob}}^{\mathsf{high}}=\Pi_{+}\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{problem}}\Pi_{+}.

By construction,
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{prob}}^{\mathsf{high}}
annihilates the low-energy subspace:

\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{prob}}^{\mathsf{high}}\Pi_{-}=\Pi_{-}\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{prob}}^{\mathsf{high}}=0.

Thus

\mathbbm{H}^{\mathsf{low}}=\Pi_{-}\mathbbm{H}_{0}(1)\Pi_{-}=\Pi_{-}(\mathbbm{M}+\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{prob}}^{\mathsf{high}})\Pi_{-}=\Pi_{-}\mathbbm{M}\Pi_{-}
,
where
\mathbbm{M}=\Gamma_{1}\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{X}}+J_{\mathsf{xx}}\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{XX}}+\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{prob}}^{\mathsf{low}}
.

In the following, we will show that by the end of Stage 0, if

J_{\mathsf{zz}}^{\mathsf{clique}}
is chosen sufficiently large, the Stage 1
evolution is governed exactly by the restricted Hamiltonian

\mathbbm{H}_{1}^{\mathsf{eff}}=\mathbbm{H}^{\mathsf{low}}=\Pi_{-}\mathbbm{M}\Pi_{-}
.

8.2
Effective Hamiltonian at the End of Stage 0

Definition 8.2
.

Let
\lambda_{\mathsf{cutoff}}\in\mathbb{R}
be a cutoff energy, and let
\epsilon>0
.
We say that
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{eff}}
is an
\epsilon
-close effective low-energy Hamiltonian of
\mathbbm{H}

if for every eigenstate
\mathbbm{H}\ket{\psi}=\lambda\ket{\psi}
with
\lambda<\lambda_{\mathsf{cutoff}}
,
there exists a state
\ket{\psi^{\prime}}
such that
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{eff}}\ket{\psi^{\prime}}=\lambda^{\prime}\ket{\psi^{\prime}}
, and:

1.

|\lambda-\lambda^{\prime}|=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
(energy closeness),

2.

\|\ket{\psi^{\prime}}-\Pi_{-}\ket{\psi}\|=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
(state closeness).

Intuitively,
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{eff}}
provides an approximation of
\mathbbm{H}

that accurately captures the system’s behavior within the low-energy subspace
\mathcal{L}_{-}
.
This ensures that both the spectrum and eigenstates of
\mathbbm{H}
below
\lambda_{\mathsf{cutoff}}

are preserved up to an error of order
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
.

The following Theorem
8.3
can be derived as a consequence of Theorem 6.2 in
[
KKR2006
]
.
However, we provide a direct derivation based on projection properties,
and explicitly establish the correspondence between eigenstates.

Theorem 8.3
.

Let
\mathbbm{H}=\mathbbm{M}+\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{prob}}^{\mathsf{high}}
, where

\Pi_{-}\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{prob}}^{\mathsf{high}}=\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{prob}}^{\mathsf{high}}\Pi_{-}=0

and all eigenvalues of
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{prob}}^{\mathsf{high}}
are at least

\lambda_{\mathsf{high}}>\lambda_{\mathsf{cutoff}}
.
Furthermore, to ensure the effective Hamiltonian approximation remains within
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
,
we impose the stronger condition:

\lambda_{\mathsf{high}}\geq\tfrac{1}{\epsilon}\|\mathbbm{M}\|^{2}.

Then the projected Hamiltonian

\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{eff}}:=\Pi_{-}\mathbbm{M}\Pi_{-}

is an
\epsilon
-close effective low-energy Hamiltonian of
\mathbbm{H}
, as defined in Definition
8.2
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbbm{H}\ket{\psi}=\lambda\ket{\psi}
be an eigenstate of
\mathbbm{H}
with
\lambda<\lambda_{\mathsf{cutoff}}
.
We express
\mathbbm{H}
in the block basis defined by
\Pi_{-}
and
\Pi_{+}
:

\mathbbm{H}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbbm{H}_{++}&\mathbbm{H}_{+-}\\
\mathbbm{H}_{-+}&\mathbbm{H}_{--}\end{pmatrix},\quad\text{where }\mathbbm{H}_{ab}:=\Pi_{a}\mathbbm{H}\Pi_{b}\text{ for }a,b\in\{+,-\}.

Similarly, we decompose
\mathbbm{M}
as

\mathbbm{M}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbbm{M}_{++}&\mathbbm{M}_{+-}\\
\mathbbm{M}_{-+}&\mathbbm{M}_{--}\end{pmatrix}.

Let
\ket{\psi_{+}}:=\Pi_{+}\ket{\psi}
and
\ket{\psi_{-}}:=\Pi_{-}\ket{\psi}
.
The eigenvalue equation becomes:

\begin{pmatrix}\mathbbm{H}_{++}&\mathbbm{H}_{+-}\\
\mathbbm{H}_{-+}&\mathbbm{H}_{--}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\ket{\psi_{+}}\\
\ket{\psi_{-}}\end{pmatrix}=\lambda\begin{pmatrix}\ket{\psi_{+}}\\
\ket{\psi_{-}}\end{pmatrix}.

Solving for
\ket{\psi_{+}}
, we obtain

\ket{\psi_{+}}=\mathbbm{G}(\lambda)\mathbbm{H}_{+-}\ket{\psi_{-}},

where the Green’s function is defined as

\mathbbm{G}(\lambda):=(\lambda I-\mathbbm{H}_{++})^{-1},

which corresponds to
G_{+}(z)
in
[
KKR2006
]
.

Substituting back, we obtain an effective energy-dependent equation for
\ket{\psi_{-}}
:

\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{eff}}^{\mathsf{exact}}(\lambda)\ket{\psi_{-}}=\lambda\ket{\psi_{-}},

where the exact effective Hamiltonian is given by

\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{eff}}^{\mathsf{exact}}(\lambda):=\mathbbm{H}_{--}+\mathbbm{H}_{-+}\mathbbm{G}(\lambda)\mathbbm{H}_{+-}=\mathbbm{M}_{--}+\mathbbm{M}_{-+}\mathbbm{G}(\lambda)\mathbbm{M}_{+-}.

To approximate this, we expand
\mathbbm{G}(\lambda)
using a resolvent expansion:

\mathbbm{G}(\lambda)=\left(\lambda I-\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{prob}}^{\mathsf{high}}-\mathbbm{M}_{++}\right)^{-1}=\mathbbm{D}^{-1}\left(I-\mathbbm{M}_{++}\mathbbm{D}^{-1}\right)^{-1},

where
\mathbbm{D}:=\lambda I-\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{prob}}^{\mathsf{high}}
, and hence
\|\mathbbm{D}^{-1}\|\leq 1/(\lambda_{\mathsf{high}}-\lambda_{\mathsf{cutoff}})
.

Expanding in powers of
\mathbbm{M}_{++}\mathbbm{D}^{-1}
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{eff}}^{\mathsf{exact}}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{M}_{--}+\mathbbm{M}_{-+}\mathbbm{D}^{-1}\mathbbm{M}_{+-}+\mathbbm{M}_{-+}\mathbbm{D}^{-1}\mathbbm{M}_{++}\mathbbm{D}^{-1}\mathbbm{M}_{+-}+\ldots

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{M}_{--}+\mathbbm{M}_{-+}\mathbbm{D}^{-1}\mathbbm{M}_{+-}+\mathcal{O}\left(\tfrac{\|\mathbbm{M}\|^{3}}{(\lambda_{\mathsf{high}}-\lambda_{\mathsf{cutoff}})^{2}}\right).

Under the condition
\lambda_{\mathsf{high}}\geq\tfrac{1}{\epsilon}\|\mathbbm{M}\|^{2}
,
the leading correction is bounded by
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
, and we may set

\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{eff}}:=\mathbbm{M}_{--}

as an
\epsilon
-close effective low-energy Hamiltonian of
\mathbbm{H}
.

Finally, letting
\ket{\psi^{\prime}}:=\ket{\psi_{-}}
, we see that both the energy
and state closeness conditions in Definition
8.2

are satisfied up to
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
, completing the proof.
∎

Remark 8.4
.

The structure of this proof closely resembles methods commonly associated
with the Schrieffer–Wolff transformation,
a technique originally developed to perturbatively eliminate high-energy subspaces
via a unitary block-diagonalization.
Although the present argument is based on resolvent expansion and projection—rather than an explicit unitary transformation—the resulting effective Hamiltonian is often referred to in the literature, particularly in physics,
as a Schrieffer–Wolff-type reduction; see, e.g.,
[
SWT-2011
]
.

Applying Theorem
8.3
, we have
the effective low-energy Hamiltonian

\mathbbm{H}_{0}(1)^{\mathsf{eff}}=\Pi_{-}\mathbbm{M}\Pi_{-}=\Pi_{-}\left(\Gamma_{1}\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{X}}\right)\Pi_{-}+J_{\mathsf{xx}}\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{XX}}+\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{prob}}^{\mathsf{low}}.

(8.1)

by setting

J_{\mathsf{zz}}^{\mathsf{clique}}=\Omega(\|\mathbbm{M}\|^{2}/\epsilon).

The ground state of
\mathbbm{H}_{0}(1)^{\mathsf{eff}}
is approximately the ground state of the transverse-field term
\Pi_{-}(\Gamma_{1}\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{X}})\Pi_{-}
as
\left\|J_{\mathsf{xx}}\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{XX}}+\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{prob}}^{\mathsf{low}}\right\|\ll\left\|\Pi_{-}(\Gamma_{1}\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{X}})\Pi_{-}\right\|
when

8.3
Spectral Gap Behavior in Stage 0

The initial Hamiltonian
\Gamma_{0}\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{X}}
(with
\Gamma_{0}=2\Gamma_{1}
) has a spectral gap of
2\Gamma_{0}
.
We argue that this spectral gap decreases smoothly from
2\Gamma_{0}
to
2\Gamma_{1}
, without encountering any small-gap regime.

The initial ground state is the uniform superposition over all computational basis states,
corresponding to the ground state of
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{X}}
.
As the system evolves, the problem Hamiltonian
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{problem}}(t)
and the XX-driver
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{XX}}

are gradually turned on, while the transverse field
\mathtt{x}(t)
is smoothly reduced.

Throughout Stage 0, the transverse field remains large, and the suppression of high-energy states in
\mathcal{L}_{+}

helps ensure that the spectral gap decreases in a controlled manner, without abrupt transitions.
This can be understood as follows:

1.

For small
t
, the transverse field term
\mathtt{x}(t)\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{X}}
dominates,
maintaining a large gap between the ground and first excited states.

2.

As
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{XX}}
gradually turns on, it remains small relative to the transverse field.

3.

At intermediate
t
, the increasing energy penalty
t\cdot J_{\mathsf{zz}}^{\mathsf{clique}}

progressively separates the
\mathcal{L}_{+}
subspace from the low-energy dynamics.

4.

For large
t
, the system is effectively projected into
\mathcal{L}_{-}
,
and the spectral gap approaches
2\Gamma_{1}
, determined by the final transverse field strength.

Thus, the gap transitions from
2\Gamma_{0}
to
2\Gamma_{1}
smoothly,
allowing the adiabatic evolution in Stage 0 to proceed efficiently without risk of gap closure.

9.1
Block Decomposition of the Full Hamiltonian: Same-Sign vs. Opposite-Sign Blocks

We begin by expressing the Hamiltonian
\mathbbm{H}_{1}^{\mathsf{eff}}

in the angular momentum basis induced by the
\mathsf{XX}
-driver graph.
This basis is constructed locally within each clique using projection and transformation operators, as shown in Section
6
.
The resulting global basis, denoted
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathsf{ang}}
, yields a block-diagonal decomposition of the full Hamiltonian
into one
same-sign block
and multiple
opposite-sign blocks
.

The angular momentum basis
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathsf{ang}}
is constructed by applying a local transformation within each clique
C_{i}
,
which maps the computational basis on
C_{i}
to an angular momentum basis
{{\mathcal{B}}_{a}^{\prime}}_{i}
.
Because the total Hilbert space is a tensor product,
these local transformations can be applied independently to each clique,
with the identity acting on the remainder of the system.
These local transformations are then combined to define a global transformation on the full system,
preserving the tensor-product structure and yielding a basis constructed from the local angular momentum decompositions of the cliques defined by the
\mathsf{XX}
-driver graph.

The resulting block decomposition emerges hierarchically:
each clique yields one same-sign sector (an effective spin-
\tfrac{1}{2}
) and several opposite-sign sectors (spin-0).
At the next level, the set of cliques in
L
—those forming the
\mathtt{LM}
—induces a bare subsystem with
the
same-sign sector

\mathcal{C}^{\mathsf{bare}}
, the
intermediate opposite-sign sectors

\mathcal{W}^{\mathsf{bare}}
,
and the
all-spin-zero (AS0) opposite-sign sector

\mathcal{Q}^{\mathsf{bare}}
.
Finally, the full same-sign sector
\mathcal{C}
of the system is defined as

\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}^{\mathsf{bare}}\otimes\left(\mathbb{C}^{2}\right)^{\otimes m_{r}},

where
m_{r}=|R|
.
Similarly, the full opposite-sign sectors
\mathcal{W}
and
\mathcal{Q}
are defined by

\mathcal{W}=\mathcal{W}^{\mathsf{bare}}\otimes\left(\mathbb{C}^{2}\right)^{\otimes m_{r}},\quad\mathcal{Q}=\mathcal{Q}^{\mathsf{bare}}\otimes\left(\mathbb{C}^{2}\right)^{\otimes m_{r}}.

Thus,
\mathcal{Q}
is also referred to as the (AS0) opposite-sign sector of the full system.
The full Hamiltonian then decomposes into the same-sign block
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathcal{C}}
and multiple opposite-sign blocks
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathcal{Q}},\mathbbm{H}_{\mathcal{W}}
,
which are either decoupled (in the disjoint case) or coupled (in the shared case).

Notation for Operators in
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathsf{ang}}
.

A bar denotes an operator expressed in the angular momentum basis
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathsf{ang}}
, e.g.,

\overline{\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{X}}}
for the transverse-field term,

\overline{\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{P}}}
for the problem Hamiltonian,
and
\overline{\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{XX}}}
for the
\mathsf{XX}
-driver term.

The effective Hamiltonian in
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathsf{ang}}
is given by:

\overline{\mathbbm{H}_{1}^{\mathsf{eff}}}:=\mathtt{x}\overline{\mathbbm{H}}_{\mathsf{X}}+\mathtt{jxx}\overline{\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{XX}}}+\overline{\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{P}}}.

First, we state the resulting block structure in Theorem
9.1
, illustrated in Figure
12
.
We prove the theorem by deriving
\overline{\mathbbm{H}_{1}^{\mathsf{eff}}}
for the disjoint-structure graph
G_{\mathsf{dis}}
in Section
9.1.1
.
We then describe the modifications required for the shared-structure graph
G_{\mathsf{share}}
in Section
9.1.2
,
highlighting the critical differences introduced by shared connectivity.

Theorem 9.1

(Block Structure of the Transformed Hamiltonian)
.

Under the angular momentum basis transformation,
the effective Hamiltonian
\overline{\mathbbm{H}_{1}^{\mathsf{eff}}}
becomes block-diagonal in the basis
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathsf{ang}}
:

\overline{\mathbbm{H}_{1}^{\mathsf{eff}}}=\mathbbm{H}_{\mathcal{C}}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathbbm{H}_{\mathcal{Q}}+\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{inter\!-\!block}}

where

\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{inter\!-\!block}}\equiv 0
in the disjoint case, while in the shared case

\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{inter\!-\!block}}=J_{\mathsf{zz}}\sum_{(i,j)\in L\times R}\tfrac{\sqrt{n_{i}-1}}{n_{i}}\mathbbm{T}^{\mathsf{cq}}_{i}\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{j},

where the matrix
\mathbbm{T}^{\mathsf{cq}}
(as in Eq. (
9.3
)) is a
3\times 3
off-diagonal operator
that mixes same-sign and opposite-sign components.
The coupling strength in
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{inter\!-\!block}}
depends on
J_{\mathsf{zz}}
and clique size
n_{i}
,
but not on the transverse field
\mathtt{x}
.

In both the disjoint and shared cases, the Hamiltonians
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathcal{C}}
and
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathcal{Q}}
admit unified expressions:

\displaystyle\mathbbm{H}_{\mathcal{C}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in L}\mathbbm{B}_{i}\!\left(\mathtt{w^{\text{\tiny eff}}_{i}},\,\sqrt{n_{i}}\,\mathtt{x}\right)+\sum_{j\in R}\mathbbm{B}_{j}\!\left(w_{j},\,\mathtt{x}\right)+J_{\mathsf{zz}}\sum_{(i,j)\in L\times R}f_{i}^{\mathsf{C}}\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{i}\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{j},

\displaystyle\mathbbm{H}_{\mathcal{Q}}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i\in L}\left(w_{i}+\tfrac{1}{4}\mathtt{jxx}\right)+\sum_{j\in R}\mathbbm{B}_{j}\!\left(-w_{j}+J_{\mathsf{zz}}\sum_{i\in L}f_{i}^{\mathsf{Q}},\,\mathtt{x}\right),

with effective coefficients

\mathtt{w^{\text{\tiny eff}}_{i}}=w_{i}-\tfrac{n_{i}-1}{4}\mathtt{jxx},\quad f_{i}^{\mathsf{C}}=\begin{cases}1&\text{for }G_{\mathsf{dis}},\\
\tfrac{n_{i}-1}{n_{i}}&\text{for }G_{\mathsf{share}},\end{cases}\qquad f_{i}^{\mathsf{Q}}=\begin{cases}1&\text{for }G_{\mathsf{dis}},\\
\tfrac{1}{n_{i}}&\text{for }G_{\mathsf{share}}.\end{cases}

The initial ground state resides in
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathcal{C}}
at the beginning of Stage 1.

Corollary 9.2

(Bare Subsystem Decomposition of
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathcal{C}}
)
.

The same-sign block Hamiltonian
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathcal{C}}
decomposes
into two bare subsystems coupled via
\mathsf{ZZ}
-couplings:

\displaystyle\mathbbm{H}_{\mathcal{C}}=\mathbbm{H}_{L}^{\mathsf{bare}}\otimes\mathbbm{I}_{R}+\mathbbm{I}_{L}\otimes\mathbbm{H}_{R}^{\mathsf{bare}}+\mathbbm{H}_{LR},

(9.1)

where

\mathbbm{H}_{L}^{\mathsf{bare}}=\sum_{i\in L}\mathbbm{B}_{i}^{L}(\mathtt{w^{\text{\tiny eff}}},\,\sqrt{n_{c}}\,\mathtt{x}),\quad\mathbbm{H}_{R}^{\mathsf{bare}}=\sum_{j\in R}\mathbbm{B}_{j}^{R}(w,\,\mathtt{x}),\quad\mathbbm{H}_{LR}=J_{\mathsf{zz}}\sum_{(i,j)\in L\times R}f_{i}^{\mathsf{C}}\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{i}\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{j},

with the effective weight
\mathtt{w^{\text{\tiny eff}}}=w-\tfrac{n_{c}-1}{4}\,\mathtt{jxx}
.

and
\mathbbm{B}_{j}^{R}
indicate the subspace of the tensor product on which each two-level Hamiltonian acts, and are omitted when the context is clear.

9.1.1
The Disjoint-Structure Graph: Block-Diagonal Structure via Clique Contraction

We now turn to the disjoint-structure graph
G_{\mathsf{dis}}
,
illustrated in Figure
3
of Section
3.3
,
which serves as the foundation for the block decomposition stated in Theorem
9.1
.
The key idea is that the local angular momentum transformation within each clique
induces an effective contraction of the graph structure,
allowing us to derive the block Hamiltonians explicitly.

Recall that within the low-energy subspace of a clique of size
n_{c}
,
the transformed spin operators
\overline{\mathrm{S}_{\mathsf{\tilde{Z}}}},\overline{\mathrm{S}_{\mathsf{X}}},\overline{\mathrm{S}_{\mathsf{XX}}}
,
given in Equation (
6.2
) of Theorem
6.2
,
are block-diagonal in the angular momentum basis.

This local transformation naturally induces a
clique contraction
:
each clique, whose vertices interact identically with the rest of the graph,
is collapsed into a single
super-vertex
, carrying the effective operators described above.
Under this contraction, the graph
G_{\mathsf{dis}}
becomes a bipartite graph
G_{\mathsf{contract}}
,
in which each clique is replaced by a super-vertex,
as illustrated in Figure
13
.
Thus,
L
consists of the set of super-vertices.

The Transformed Transverse-Field Term.

For the transverse-field Hamiltonian
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{X}}
,
the transformed operator in
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathsf{ang}}
takes the form:

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbbm{H}}_{\mathsf{X}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in L}\left(\tfrac{\sqrt{n_{i}}}{2}\sigma^{x}_{i}\oplus 0\oplus\cdots\oplus 0\right)+\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i\in R}\sigma^{x}_{i}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{i\in L}\tfrac{\sqrt{n_{i}}}{2}\sigma^{x}_{i}+\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i\in R}\sigma^{x}_{i}\right)\oplus\cdots\oplus\left(\sum_{i\in L^{\prime}}\tfrac{\sqrt{n_{i}}}{2}\sigma^{x}_{i}+\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i\in R}\sigma^{x}_{i}\right)\oplus\cdots\oplus\left(\sum_{i\in L}0+\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i\in R}\sigma^{x}_{i}\right),

where the direct sum runs over the block decomposition induced by
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathsf{ang}}
, and the contributions are grouped as follows:

•

\overline{\mathbbm{H}}_{\mathsf{X}}^{\mathcal{C}}:=\sum_{i\in L}\tfrac{\sqrt{n_{i}}}{2}\sigma^{x}_{i}+\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i\in R}\sigma^{x}_{i}

corresponds to the same-sign block
\mathcal{C}
.

•

\overline{\mathbbm{H}}_{\mathsf{X}}^{\mathcal{Q}}:=\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i\in R}\sigma^{x}_{i}

corresponds to the AS0 opposite-sign block
\mathcal{Q}
, which receives no transverse contribution from
L
.

•

\overline{\mathbbm{H}}_{\mathsf{X}}^{\mathcal{W}}:=\sum_{i\in L^{\prime}}\tfrac{\sqrt{n_{i}}}{2}\sigma^{x}_{i}+\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i\in R}\sigma^{x}_{i}

corresponds to the intermediate opposite-sign blocks
\mathcal{W}
,
where some cliques are in spin-0 sectors and others in spin-
\tfrac{1}{2}
, denoted by the subset
L^{\prime}\subset L
.

The Transformed Problem Hamiltonian.

The Ising interaction term,

\sum_{(i,j)\in{\mathsf{E}}(G)}\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{i}\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{j},

is mapped under clique contraction to:

\sum_{(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\in{\mathsf{E}}(G_{\mathsf{contract}})}\left(\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{i^{\prime}}\oplus 1\oplus\cdots\oplus 1\right)\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{j^{\prime}},

where
{\mathsf{E}}(G_{\mathsf{contract}})=L\times R
.
This yields a block structure:

\displaystyle\sum_{(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\in{\mathsf{E}}(G_{\mathsf{contract}})}\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{i^{\prime}}\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{j^{\prime}}\oplus\cdots\oplus m_{l}\sum_{j\in R}\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{j},

which highlights the distinct contributions of the same-sign and opposite-sign sectors.

The transformed problem Hamiltonian becomes:

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbbm{H}}_{\mathsf{P}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in L}(-w_{i})\left(\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{i}\oplus 1\oplus\cdots\oplus 1\right)+\sum_{i\in R}(-w_{i})\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{i}+J_{\mathsf{zz}}\left(\sum_{(i,j)\in{\mathsf{E}}(G_{\mathsf{contract}})}\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{i}\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{j}\oplus\cdots\oplus m_{l}\sum_{j\in R}\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{j}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{i\in L\cup R}(-w_{i})\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{i}+J_{\mathsf{zz}}\sum_{(i,j)\in{\mathsf{E}}(G_{\mathsf{contract}})}\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{i}\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{j}\right)\oplus\cdots\oplus\left(\sum_{i\in L}(-w_{i})+\sum_{i\in R}(-w_{i}+m_{l}J_{\mathsf{zz}})\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{i}\right).

Thus, the same-sign sector behaves as a standard Ising Hamiltonian on the contracted graph
G_{\mathsf{contract}}
,
while each opposite-sign block contributes an energy shift and modifies only the
R
-part of the spectrum.

The Transformed
\mathsf{XX}
-Driver Term.

The
\mathsf{XX}
-driver modifies the diagonal energies of super-vertices:

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{XX}}}=\sum_{i\in L}\left(\tfrac{n_{i}-1}{4}\right)\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{i}\oplus\left(-\tfrac{1}{4}\right)\oplus\cdots\oplus\left(-\tfrac{1}{4}\right).

(9.2)

This shifts effective vertex-weights as follows:

•

In the same-sign block:

-w_{i}\longmapsto-\left(w_{i}-\tfrac{n_{i}-1}{4}\mathtt{jxx}\right)\quad\text{(energy lifted)};

•

In the AS0 opposite-sign block:

-w_{i}\longmapsto-\left(w_{i}+\tfrac{1}{4}\mathtt{jxx}\right)\quad\text{(energy lowered)}.

Remark 9.3
.

In the disjoint-structure case, the opposite-sign blocks are, in principle, decoupled from the same-sign block and can be ignored.
In contrast, in the shared-structure case, weak to moderate couplings emerge between the same-sign and opposite-sign sectors,
necessitating their inclusion in the analysis.

Because of the block ordering in Section
7.4
, the energy levels of the intermediate opposite-sign blocks are
sandwiched between those of the same-sign block and the AS0 block.
Therefore, for the purpose of analyzing the low-energy spectrum and the dynamical behavior near critical anti-crossings,
it is sufficient to retain only the same-sign block and the AS0 block.
All other opposite-sign blocks can be safely ignored.

Same-Sign Block Hamiltonian.

We denote by
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathcal{C}}^{\mathsf{dis}}
the effective Hamiltonian restricted to the same-sign sector
\mathcal{C}
for the disjoint-structure graph case:

\displaystyle\mathbbm{H}_{\mathcal{C}}^{\mathsf{dis}}

\displaystyle=-\mathtt{x}\overline{\mathbbm{H}}_{\mathsf{X}}^{\mathsf{C}}+\overline{\mathbbm{H}}_{\mathsf{Z}}^{\mathsf{C}}(\mathtt{jxx})+J_{\mathsf{zz}}\sum_{(i,j)\in{\mathsf{E}}(G_{\mathsf{contract}})}\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{i}\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{j},

where

\overline{\mathbbm{H}}_{\mathsf{Z}}^{\mathsf{C}}(\mathtt{jxx}):=\sum_{i\in L}\left(-w_{i}+\tfrac{n_{c}-1}{4}\mathtt{jxx}\right)\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{i}+\sum_{j\in R}(-w_{j})\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{j}

collects all diagonal terms linear in
\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{i}
, including both the bare vertex weights and the
\mathsf{XX}
-induced energy shifts.

AS0 Block Hamiltonian.

We denote by
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathcal{Q}}^{\mathsf{dis}}
the effective Hamiltonian restricted to the AS0 sector
\mathcal{Q}
for the disjoint-structure graph case:

\displaystyle\mathbbm{H}_{\mathcal{Q}}^{\mathsf{dis}}

\displaystyle=-\mathtt{x}\overline{\mathbbm{H}}_{\mathsf{X}}^{\mathsf{Q}}+\overline{\mathbbm{H}}_{\mathsf{Z}}^{\mathsf{Q}}(\mathtt{jxx}),

where

\overline{\mathbbm{H}}_{\mathsf{X}}^{\mathsf{Q}}=\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{j\in R}\sigma^{x}_{j},\quad\overline{\mathbbm{H}}_{\mathsf{Z}}^{\mathsf{Q}}(\mathtt{jxx}):=\sum_{i\in L}\!\left(-w_{i}-\tfrac{1}{4}\mathtt{jxx}\right)+\sum_{j\in R}\!\left(-w_{j}+m_{l}J_{\mathsf{zz}}\right)\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{j}.

The diagonal term
\overline{\mathbbm{H}}_{\mathsf{Z}}^{\mathsf{Q}}
reflects a constant energy shift from the
\mathsf{XX}
-driver applied to
L
,
and a coupling-induced energy shift on the
R
-vertices, scaled by the number of cliques
m_{l}
.
Note that
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathcal{Q}}^{\mathsf{dis}}
contains no coupling,
as the spin-0 components contribute only a scalar constant to the interaction term.
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathcal{Q}}^{\mathsf{dis}}
is analytically solvable.

Remark 9.4
.

The differences between the same-sign block
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathcal{C}}^{\mathsf{dis}}
and the AS0 block
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathcal{Q}}^{\mathsf{dis}}
are:

•

The effective transverse-field contribution from
L
vanishes:

\sqrt{n_{i}}\sigma^{x}\mapsto 0
.

•

The operator
\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{i}
is replaced by the scalar
1

(i.e.,
\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{i}\mapsto 1
), contributing only constant energy shifts.

•

The
\mathsf{XX}
-driver term, which raises the energy of each vertex
i\in L

by
\tfrac{n_{i}-1}{4}J_{\mathsf{xx}}
in the same-sign block,
instead
lowers
the energy in the all-spin-zero block by
\tfrac{1}{4}J_{\mathsf{xx}}
per clique.

This completes the block-level analysis of the disjoint-structure case.
We now turn to the shared-structure case, where weak inter-block couplings emerge and modify this picture.

9.1.2
The Shared-Structure Graph: Modification to the Disjoint Case

The

, shown in Figure

,
differs from the disjoint-structure graph

primarily in its inter-subsystem
\mathsf{ZZ}
-couplings.

In the transformed Hamiltonian, the only modification appears in the
\mathsf{ZZ}
-interaction terms.
Unlike the fully connected case in

n_{c}-1

are coupled to each vertex

The interaction between an external vertex and the internal basis of a clique of size

\mathbbm{T}=\left(\tfrac{n_{c}-1}{n_{c}}\tilde{\sigma}^{z}\right)\oplus\left(\tfrac{1}{n_{c}}\right)+\tfrac{\sqrt{n_{c}-1}}{n_{c}}\mathbbm{T}^{\mathsf{cq}},

-1&0&0\end{bmatrix},

which mixes same-sign and opposite-sign components.
The coupling magnitude is

\tfrac{\sqrt{n_{c}-1}}{n_{c}}\;\approx\;\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{n_{c}+1}},

Modification of
\mathsf{ZZ}
Terms.

The Ising interaction term

\sum_{(i,j)\in{\mathsf{E}}(G)}\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{i}\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{j}=\sum_{j\in R}\sum_{i\in L}\sum_{i_{k}=1}^{n_{i}-1}\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{i_{k}}\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{j}

is modified under contraction due to the partial connectivity between

L

For each

\sum_{i_{k}=1}^{n_{i}-1}\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{i_{k}}\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{j}\;\Longrightarrow\;\left(\mathbbm{T}_{i}\oplus 1\oplus\cdots\oplus 1\right)\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{j}=\mathbbm{T}_{i}\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{j}\oplus\underbrace{\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{j}\oplus\cdots\oplus\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{j}}_{n_{i}-2\text{ times}},

Therefore, the full Ising interaction term is transformed into:

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in R}\sum_{i\in L}\sum_{i_{k}=1}^{n_{i}-1}\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{i_{k}}\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in R}\sum_{i\in L}\mathbbm{T}_{i}\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{j}\oplus\cdots\oplus m_{l}\sum_{j\in R}\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{j}.

The transformed problem Hamiltonian thus becomes:

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbbm{H}}_{\mathsf{P}}^{\mathsf{share}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in L}(-w_{i})\left(\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{i}\oplus 1\oplus\cdots\oplus 1\right)+\sum_{j\in R}(-w_{j})\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{j}+J_{\mathsf{zz}}\left(\sum_{j\in R}\sum_{i\in L}\left(\mathbbm{T}_{i}\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{j}\right)\oplus\cdots\oplus m_{l}\sum_{j\in R}\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{j}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{i\in L\cup R}(-w_{i})\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{i}+J_{\mathsf{zz}}\sum_{(i,j)\in L\times R}\mathbbm{T}_{i}\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{j}\right)\oplus\cdots\oplus\left(\sum_{i\in L}(-w_{i})+\sum_{j\in R}(-w_{j}+m_{l}J_{\mathsf{zz}})\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{j}\right).

In summary, the interaction term
\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{i}\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{j}
is replaced by the effective operator
\mathbbm{T}_{i}\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{j}
.

The full Hamiltonian for the shared-state case is thus:

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbbm{H}_{1}^{\mathsf{eff}}}

\displaystyle=-\mathtt{x}\left(\overline{\mathbbm{H}}_{\mathsf{X}}^{\mathcal{C}}\oplus\ldots\oplus\overline{\mathbbm{H}}_{\mathsf{X}}^{\mathcal{Q}}\right)+\left(\overline{\mathbbm{H}}_{\mathsf{Z}}^{\mathcal{C}}(\mathtt{jxx})\oplus\ldots\oplus\overline{\mathbbm{H}}_{\mathsf{Z}}^{\mathcal{Q}}(\mathtt{jxx})\right)+J_{\mathsf{zz}}\sum_{(i,j)\in L\times R}\left(\tfrac{n_{i}-1}{n_{i}}\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{i}\oplus\tfrac{1}{n_{i}}+\mathbbm{T}^{\mathsf{cq}}_{i}\right)\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{j}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbbm{H}_{\mathcal{C}}^{\mathsf{share}}\oplus\ldots\oplus\mathbbm{H}_{\mathcal{Q}}^{\mathsf{share}}\right)+\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{inter\!-\!block}}

with

\displaystyle\mathbbm{H}_{\mathcal{C}}^{\mathsf{share}}

\displaystyle=-\mathtt{x}\overline{\mathbbm{H}}_{\mathsf{X}}^{\mathcal{C}}+\overline{\mathbbm{H}}_{\mathsf{Z}}^{\mathcal{C}}(\mathtt{jxx})+J_{\mathsf{zz}}\sum_{(i,j)\in{\mathsf{E}}(G_{\mathsf{contract}})}\tfrac{n_{i}-1}{n_{i}}\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{i}\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{j},

\displaystyle\mathbbm{H}_{\mathcal{Q}}^{\mathsf{share}}

\displaystyle=-\mathtt{x}\overline{\mathbbm{H}}_{\mathsf{X}}^{\mathcal{Q}}+\sum_{i\in L}\left(-w_{i}-\tfrac{1}{4}\mathtt{jxx}\right)+\sum_{j\in R}\left(-w_{j}+J_{\mathsf{zz}}\sum_{i\in L}\tfrac{1}{n_{i}}\right)\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{j},

\displaystyle\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{inter\!-\!block}}

\displaystyle=J_{\mathsf{zz}}\sum_{(i,j)\in L\times R}\tfrac{\sqrt{n_{i}-1}}{n_{i}}\mathbbm{T}^{\mathsf{cq}}_{i}\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{j}.

The inter-block coupling magnitude depends on
J_{\mathsf{zz}}
,
\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{n_{i}+1}}
, and
m_{l}
(size of

9.2.1
Two Block Decompositions of
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathcal{C}}

L
-Inner vs.

Physically, the same-sign block Hamiltonian
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathcal{C}}
is symmetric under interchange of the

Mathematically, this corresponds to a permutation similarity transformation: reordering the basis to place

9.3
Two-Stage Evolution and Feasibility Bounds on
J_{\mathsf{xx}}

Our goal is to show that, for an appropriate choice of
J_{\mathsf{xx}}
and
J_{\mathsf{zz}}
,
the system evolves from the initial ground state of
\mathbbm{H}_{1}^{\mathsf{eff}}
to the global minimum (
\mathtt{GM}
) without encountering an anti-crossing.
The correctness of the algorithm critically depends on
four feasibility bounds on
J_{\mathsf{xx}}
(Table
2
),
together with two upper bounds on
J_{\mathsf{zz}}
:
J_{\mathsf{zz}}^{\text{\tiny inter}}
and
J_{\mathsf{zz}}^{\text{\tiny steer}}
.

In Section
9.3.1
, we derive these bounds analytically, and show that for
J_{\mathsf{zz}}\leq J_{\mathsf{zz}}^{\text{\tiny steer}}

there exists a nonempty feasible window for
J_{\mathsf{xx}}
.
To give intuition before the detailed derivations, we briefly summarize how each bound
contributes to maintaining a smooth two-stage evolution.
The Stage-Separation bound together with
J_{\mathsf{zz}}^{\text{\tiny inter}}
ensure that Stage 1 is effectively confined to the same-sign block,
allowing Stage 1 and Stage 2 to be analyzed separately;
the Lifting bound ensures the original anti-crossing is removed;
the Steering bound directs the system smoothly into the
R
-region (bypassing tunneling) during Stage 1,
and Stage 2 is secured by the Sinking bound, which prevents the emergence of a new anti-crossing when the lowest opposite-sign block participates.
The analysis, supported by numerical results, of Stage 1 and Stage 2 is presented in Section
9.3.2
and Section
9.3.3
, respectively.

9.3.1

and the Feasibility Window

Recall that

This effect introduces a fundamental trade-off: if

As a result, the success of the algorithmic design depends critically on choosing an appropriate value of
J_{\mathsf{xx}}

(i) Stage-Separation Upper Bound and
J_{\mathsf{zz}}^{\text{\tiny inter}}

The Stage-Separation requirement explains the rationale behind the two-stage design of the coupling schedule.
The objective is to ensure that the system’s ground state starts and remains confined to the same-sign block during Stage 1,
and involves opposite-sign blocks only during Stage 2.
To achieve this, two conditions are needed:
(a) the lowest energy level of the full system must lie within the same-sign block throughout Stage 1, and
(b) the inter-block coupling between same-sign and opposite-sign blocks must remain weak enough to be neglected.

Condition (a): Crossover within Stage 2.

This motivates a two-stage schedule in which the
\mathsf{XX}
-coupling
\mathtt{jxx}
is held constant during Stage 1
and scaled linearly with the transverse field during Stage 2.
Using the block energy ordering results of the
L
-bare subsystem from Section
7.4
(Figure
11
),
we find that the crossover between the same-sign and opposite-sign blocks occurs at

\mathtt{x}_{c}=\tfrac{4\alpha}{4-\alpha^{2}}.

This lies within Stage 2 (i.e.,
\mathtt{x}_{c}\leq\Gamma_{2}
) if and only if
\alpha\leq\alpha_{\max}(\Gamma_{2})
,
where
\alpha_{\max}(\Gamma_{2})
is defined in Eq.
6.12
.
For
\Gamma_{2}\geq 2
, one has

\alpha_{\max}(\Gamma_{2})\gtrsim\tfrac{2(\Gamma_{2}-1)}{\Gamma_{2}}.

This yields the Stage-Separation bound on the constant
\mathsf{XX}
-coupling during Stage 1:

J_{\mathsf{xx}}^{\text{\tiny sep}}=2(\Gamma_{2}-1).

Condition (b): Weak inter-block coupling.

In addition, recall from Theorem
9.1
that the effective Hamiltonian decomposes as

\overline{\mathbbm{H}_{1}^{\mathsf{eff}}}=\mathbbm{H}_{\mathcal{C}}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathbbm{H}_{\mathcal{Q}}+\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{inter\!-\!block}},

where
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{inter\!-\!block}}=0
in the disjoint case, while in the shared case

\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{inter\!-\!block}}=J_{\mathsf{zz}}\sum_{(i,j)\in L\times R}\tfrac{\sqrt{n_{i}-1}}{n_{i}}\mathbbm{T}^{\mathsf{cq}}_{i}\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{j}.

Here
\mathbbm{T}^{\mathsf{cq}}
(Eq. (
9.3
)) is a
3\times 3
off-diagonal operator mixing same-sign and opposite-sign components.
The strength of this coupling depends on
J_{\mathsf{zz}}
and clique size
n_{i}
, but not on the transverse field
\mathtt{x}
.

Since we take
\Gamma_{2}=m
, and Stage 1 is defined by
\mathtt{x}\in[\Gamma_{2},\Gamma_{1}]
,
the inter-block coupling is negligible whenever
J_{\mathsf{zz}}\ll m
.
We summarize this condition by writing
J_{\mathsf{zz}}\leq J_{\mathsf{zz}}^{\text{\tiny inter}}
.
In particular, this requires that
J_{\mathsf{zz}}
be chosen as a constant (independent of
m
),
so that the condition continues to hold as the problem size grows.
In practice, since
J_{\mathsf{zz}}^{\text{\tiny steer}}\leq J_{\mathsf{zz}}^{\text{\tiny inter}}
by construction,
it suffices to impose
J_{\mathsf{zz}}\leq J_{\mathsf{zz}}^{\text{\tiny steer}}
.

Conclusion.

Together, the Stage-Separation bound
J_{\mathsf{xx}}^{\text{\tiny sep}}
and the inter-block coupling bound
J_{\mathsf{zz}}^{\text{\tiny inter}}

ensure that the low-energy dynamics of Stage 1 are effectively confined to the same-sign block.
This joint condition justifies treating Stage 1 and Stage 2 as separate regimes in the analysis.

(ii) Lifting Lower Bound
J_{\mathsf{xx}}^{\text{\tiny lift}}

Idea:

This bound ensures that the energy associated with
\mathtt{LM}
in the same-sign block is raised high enough,
during Stage 2.
The key idea is to use exact analytical expressions for the bare energy levels,
E^{\mathsf{\tiny LM}}_{0}
and
E^{\mathsf{\tiny GM}}_{0}
,
derived in Section
7
, to approximate the true energies associated with
\mathtt{LM}
and
\mathtt{GM}
.
We then determine the condition under which
E^{\mathsf{\tiny LM}}_{0}
remains strictly above
E^{\mathsf{\tiny GM}}_{0}
throughout Stage 2.

This is a perturbative approximation.
By second-order perturbation theory, the energy associated with
\mathtt{LM}
is shifted upward,
while the energy associated with
\mathtt{GM}
is shifted downward.
Therefore, if the bare energy levels do not cross, the true levels will not have an anti-cross either.

The resulting threshold defines the
Lifting Lower Bound

J_{\mathsf{xx}}^{\text{\tiny lift}}
:
the minimum value of
J_{\mathsf{xx}}
required to ensure that the energy level associated with
\mathtt{LM}
in the same-sign block remains sufficiently lifted throughout Stage 2.
This approximation is further supported numerically.

Derivation:

The (negative) slope magnitude of
E^{\mathsf{\tiny LM}}_{0}(\mathtt{x})
decreases from

\tfrac{\sqrt{n_{c}}}{2}\cdot m\quad\text{(at }J_{\mathsf{xx}}=0\text{)}\quad\text{to}\quad\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\cdot m\quad\text{(at }J_{\mathsf{xx}}=\alpha\Gamma_{2}\text{).}

This is illustrated in Figure
8
(c), and notably, the slope bound is independent of clique size
n_{c}
.
In contrast, the (negative) slope magnitude of
E^{\mathsf{\tiny GM}}_{0}(\mathtt{x})
remains approximately constant:

\tfrac{m_{g}}{2}.

To maintain
E^{\mathsf{\tiny LM}}_{0}(\mathtt{x})>E^{\mathsf{\tiny GM}}_{0}(\mathtt{x})
throughout Stage 2, it suffices to require

\tfrac{m}{\alpha}<\tfrac{m_{g}}{2}
, i.e.
\alpha>2\tfrac{m}{m_{g}}
.
Thus, the bound becomes:

J_{\mathsf{xx}}^{\text{\tiny lift}}=2\cdot\tfrac{m}{m_{g}}\cdot\Gamma_{2}.

(iii) Steering Lower Bound
J_{\mathsf{xx}}^{\text{\tiny steer}}

Idea:

The lower bound
J_{\mathsf{xx}}^{\text{\tiny steer}}
is defined as the minimal value of
J_{\mathsf{xx}}
such that
the diagonal entries of the
R
-inner block decomposition Hamiltonian are
strictly decreasing from top to bottom.
This condition ensures that the system can smoothly localize into the lowest
R
-blocks.

The key idea is that the coupling strength
J_{\mathsf{xx}}
must be large enough
so that any configuration involving vertices from
L
incurs a high energy penalty,
thereby suppressing support on the
L
-blocks throughout Stage 1.
See Figure
15
for an illustration.

Derivation:

Specifically, we require the inequality

-2\,w^{\text{\tiny eff}}>-(w^{\text{\tiny eff}}+m_{r}(1-J_{\mathsf{zz}})),

where
w^{\text{\tiny eff}}=1-\tfrac{n_{c}-1}{4}J_{\mathsf{xx}}.
is the effective vertex weight.
Solving the inequality yields the lower bound:

J_{\mathsf{xx}}^{\text{\tiny steer}}=\tfrac{4}{n_{c}-1}\left[m_{r}(J_{\mathsf{zz}}-1)+1\right].

Remark 9.8
.

Since
J_{\mathsf{xx}}^{\text{\tiny steer}}
depends on
n_{c}
,
a conservative generalization for non-uniform clique sizes is to replace
n_{c}
with
\min_{i}n_{i}
.

(iv) Sinking Upper Bound
J_{\mathsf{xx}}^{\text{\tiny sink}}

Idea:

This is analogous to the Lifting Lower Bound
J_{\mathsf{xx}}^{\text{\tiny lift}}
.
We want to make sure that the AS0 energy level is not dropped too low to form a new anti-crossing.
We use the bare (decoupled) energy levels
E^{\mathsf{\tiny GM}}_{0}
(the ground state energy of
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathtt{GM}}^{\mathsf{bare}}
) and
E^{\mathsf{\tiny AS0}}_{0}
(the ground state energy of
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathcal{Q}}
)
to approximate the true energy levels.
We then impose an upper bound on
J_{\mathsf{xx}}
to ensure that
E^{\mathsf{\tiny AS0}}_{0}>E^{\mathsf{\tiny GM}}_{0}
throughout Stage 2.

By the same perturbative argument applied when the blocks are coupled, while using the decoupled bare levels as reference points,
the true energy level corresponding to
E^{\mathsf{\tiny AS0}}_{0}
is shifted upward, while the true energy level corresponding to
E^{\mathsf{\tiny GM}}_{0}
is shifted downward.
Therefore, as long as
E^{\mathsf{\tiny AS0}}_{0}>E^{\mathsf{\tiny GM}}_{0}
, no anti-crossing will be formed.
This approximation is further supported numerically.

Derivation:

We require that

E^{\mathsf{\tiny AS0}}_{0}(\mathtt{x})>E^{\mathsf{\tiny GM}}_{0}(\mathtt{x}),
during Stage 2,
and in particular, we enforce this at the beginning of Stage 2, with
x=\Gamma_{2}
:

\displaystyle E^{\mathsf{\tiny AS0}}_{0}(\Gamma_{2})

\displaystyle\approx-m\left(1+\tfrac{J_{\mathsf{xx}}}{4}\right)-\tfrac{m_{r}}{2}\left(1-\tfrac{m}{n_{c}}J_{\mathsf{zz}}+\Gamma_{2}\right),

\displaystyle E^{\mathsf{\tiny GM}}_{0}(\Gamma_{2})

\displaystyle\approx-\tfrac{m+m_{r}}{2}(1+\Gamma_{2}).

Solving this inequality yields the upper bound:

J_{\mathsf{xx}}^{\text{\tiny sink}}=2(\Gamma_{2}-1)+\tfrac{2m_{r}}{n_{c}}J_{\mathsf{zz}}.

Remark 9.9
.

Since
J_{\mathsf{xx}}^{\text{\tiny sink}}
depends on
n_{c}
, a conservative generalization for non-uniform clique sizes is to replace
n_{c}
with
\max_{i}n_{i}
.

Having established the four feasibility bounds on
J_{\mathsf{xx}}

and the condition for a nonempty window, we now turn to the detailed analysis of Stage 1 and Stage 2.

9.3.2
Stage 1: Structural Steering

The purpose of Stage 1 is twofold:
(1) to ensure that the evolution is confined to the same-sign block, and
(2) to demonstrate successful
structural steering
within this block.
Confinement is guaranteed by the Stage-Separation bound
J_{\mathsf{xx}}^{\text{\tiny sep}}
together with the requirement
J_{\mathsf{zz}}\leq J_{\mathsf{zz}}^{\text{\tiny inter}}
(in the shared case).

We define

as the mechanism by which the evolving ground state is directed smoothly into the
R
-localized region (the global-minimum-supporting region).
In our design, steering is achieved by ordering the energies of the
R

R
-localized region, rather than first becoming trapped in the
L
-region and only later tunneling into the
R
-region, as happens in the stoquastic annealing case.
Formally, this mechanism is guaranteed by two feasibility bounds: the Lifting bound
J_{\mathsf{xx}}^{\text{\tiny lift}}
, which forces the ground state toward the lowest
R
-blocks; and the Steering bound
J_{\mathsf{xx}}^{\text{\tiny steer}}
, which ensures that this localization proceeds smoothly.
This analytically established behavior is further confirmed by numerical evidence.

9.3.3
Stage 2: Smooth Evolution to
\mathtt{GM}
via an Opposite-Sign Path

The purpose of Stage 2 is to show that the evolution proceeds from the
R
-localized region at the end of Stage 1 to
\mathtt{GM}
without the emergence of any new anti-crossing.
We analyze this stage under two structural cases: the
disjoint-structure graph

G_{\mathsf{dis}}
and the
shared-structure graph

G_{\mathsf{share}}
.

The disjoint-structure case serves mainly for illustration and comparison:
the evolution remains entirely within the same-sign block, and the dynamics proceed smoothly.
In the shared-structure case, the lowest opposite-sign block (AS0) participates in the evolution.
By construction, the Sinking bound
J_{\mathsf{xx}}\leq J_{\mathsf{xx}}^{\text{\tiny sink}}
ensures that no new anti-crossing emerges, so the evolution remains smooth.
Moreover, the dynamics can be explained by
sign-generating interference
, which produces negative amplitudes in the computational basis of the ground state.
Thus the system follows an opposite-sign path, a route not accessible in the stoquastic case.

Shared-Structure Case

We now analyze the shared-structure case in detail.
The goal is to show explicitly how the participation of opposite-sign blocks leads to both positive and negative amplitudes in the ground state.
Our approach is twofold: first we establish the sign pattern mathematically, and then we interpret it physically in terms of
sign-generating quantum interference
.
We begin with the mathematical analysis.

Emergence of Negative Amplitudes

In the angular-momentum basis, Stage 2 involves the lowest
R
-supporting block together with the opposite-sign blocks that couple to it.
We consider the following restricted subspace in the computational basis (transforming back), which consists of two parts:

•

\mathcal{M}
: the set of
\mathtt{GM}
-supporting states containing
R
,

\mathcal{M}\;=\;\{\,M\subset\mathtt{GM}:R\subset M\,\}.

•

\mathcal{D}
: the set of dependent-set states paired to
\mathcal{M}
via
\mathsf{XX}
-couplers.
For each
M\in\mathcal{M}
and each
b\in M\setminus R
, there is a unique partner
a
such that
(a,b)
is an
\mathsf{XX}
-coupled pair.
The corresponding dependent state
D_{M,b}\in\mathcal{D}
is obtained by flipping
(a,b)
.
Thus each
M
has
|M\setminus R|
associated dependent-set states, while each
D_{M,b}
is uniquely coupled to its parent
M
.

Restricting the Hamiltonian to this subspace yields the block form

\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{eff}}^{(\mathcal{M}\mathcal{D})}\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}\mathbbm{H}_{\mathcal{M}}&\mathbbm{V}\\
\mathbbm{V}^{\top}&\mathbbm{H}_{\mathcal{D}}\end{pmatrix},

where
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathcal{M}}
and
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathcal{D}}
are both stoquastic (off-diagonals
\leq 0
),
with
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathcal{D}}
has strictly positive diagonal entries,
and
\mathbbm{V}
consists of only nonnegative entries
\mathtt{jxx}
(as each
D\in\mathcal{D}
is coupled only to one
M\in\mathcal{M}
).

To identify the ground-state structure, we apply the Rayleigh variational principle to a trial vector

(u_{M},u_{D})
,
with
u_{M}\geq 0
on
\mathcal{M}
.
The minimum is attained when

u_{D}\;=\;-\,H_{\mathcal{D}}^{-1}\mathbbm{V}^{\top}u_{M}.

Since
\mathbbm{V}^{\top}u_{M}\geq 0
and
H_{\mathcal{D}}^{-1}\geq 0
entrywise,
the Stage 2 ground state necessarily takes the form

(u_{M},\,u_{D})
, with
u_{M}\geq 0
and
u_{D}\leq 0
.
In other words, the ground state carries positive amplitudes on the
\mathtt{GM}
-supporting sector and negative amplitudes on the dependent-set sector,
establishing the structural origin of negative components in the Stage 2 ground state.
This analytical prediction is confirmed numerically: Figure

plots the total fraction of negative amplitudes as a function of time.

The negative amplitudes identified above can be understood in terms of
constructive and destructive interference between same-sign and opposite-sign components.
This provides the physical mechanism we call
sign-generating quantum interference
.

We illustrate this mechanism in detail using the three-vertex conceptual model (V3) in Section
9.4

which may also serves as a minimal testbed for probing sign-generating interference experimentally.

9.4
Three-Vertex Conceptual Model: Illustrating Quantum Interference

We define
quantum interference
as the superposition of components in a quantum system that project onto the same basis state and either interfere destructively—reducing amplitude through opposite-sign cancellation—or constructively—increasing amplitude through same-sign contributions.

To illustrate this mechanism, we introduce the three-vertex model (V3), a minimal example in which interference arises from basis rotation and occurs in both the stoquastic case (
J_{\mathsf{xx}}=0
) and the non-stoquastic case (
J_{\mathsf{xx}}>0
).
Within this framework, we emphasize the distinctive feature of
sign-generating
interference: it produces negative amplitudes in the computational basis.

This section is organized as follows:

•

Model Description:
Formulation of the full Hamiltonian in both the computational and angular-momentum bases (Section
9.4.1
).

•

Quantum Interference via Basis Rotation:
How interference emerges from a basis change (Section
9.4.2
).

•

Numerical Illustration of Sign-Generating Interference:
Emergence of negative amplitudes in the non-stoquastic case (Section
).

9.4.1

The model consists of three vertices
V=\{a,b,r\}
, with associated weights
w_{a}
,
w_{b}
, and
w_{r}

The graph includes two edges,
\{ab,ar\}
, each associated with a
\mathsf{ZZ}
-coupling, denoted
J_{\mathsf{zz}}^{ab}
and
J_{\mathsf{zz}}^{ar}
.
Additionally, there is an
\mathsf{XX}
-coupling on the edge
ab
, denoted
J_{\mathsf{xx}}
.

We bipartition
V
into
L=\{a,b\}
and
R=\{r\}
.
The set
L
corresponds to the local minima (
\mathtt{LM}
), while
\mathtt{GM}=\{b,r\}
—the maximum independent set—corresponds to the global minimum.

for a visualization of this setup.

.

The V3 model is not meant to demonstrate structural steering in Stage 1. Since the right component contains only a single vertex (

), structrual steering fails, as it requires
m_{r}\geq 2
. The system therefore undergoes a tunneling-induced anti-crossing in Stage 1. At the end of Stage 1, we assume that the ground state has successfully tunneled and is now localized on the state
\{r\}
, which is a subset of the global minimum configuration
\mathtt{GM}=\{b,r\}
.

Hamiltonian and Basis Construction

Each vertex
k\in\{a,b,r\}
is modeled as a spin-
\tfrac{1}{2}
system with transverse field strength proportional to
\sqrt{n_{k}}
. The local Hamiltonian at vertex
k
, in the computational basis
\{\ket{1_{k}},\ket{0_{k}}\}
, is given by

\mathbb{B}_{k}=\begin{bmatrix}-w_{k}&-\tfrac{\sqrt{n_{k}}}{2}\mathtt{x}\\[6.0pt]
-\tfrac{\sqrt{n_{k}}}{2}\mathtt{x}&0\end{bmatrix},\qquad w_{k}=w-\tfrac{n_{k}-1}{4}\mathtt{jxx}.

The
L
-subsystem consists of vertices
a
and
b
, coupled via both ZZ- and XX-terms:

\displaystyle\mathbbm{H}_{L}=\mathbbm{B}_{a}\otimes\mathbbm{I}_{2}+\mathbbm{I}_{2}\otimes\mathbbm{B}_{b}+J_{\mathsf{zz}}^{ab}\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{a}\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{b}+\tfrac{\sqrt{n_{a}n_{b}}}{4}\mathtt{jxx}\sigma_{a}^{x}\sigma_{b}^{x}.

In the computational basis
\{\ket{1_{a}1_{b}},\ket{1_{a}0_{b}},\ket{0_{a}1_{b}},\ket{0_{a}0_{b}}\}
, this becomes:

\mathbbm{H}_{L}=\begin{array}[]{ccccc}&\ket{1_{a}1_{b}}&\ket{1_{a}0_{b}}&\ket{0_{a}1_{b}}&\ket{0_{a}0_{b}}\\
\cline{2-5}\cr\ket{1_{a}1_{b}}&J_{\mathsf{zz}}^{ab}-w_{a}-w_{b}&-\tfrac{\sqrt{n_{b}}}{2}\mathtt{x}&-\tfrac{\sqrt{n_{a}}}{2}\mathtt{x}&\tfrac{\sqrt{n_{a}n_{b}}}{4}\mathtt{jxx}\\
\ket{1_{a}0_{b}}&-\tfrac{\sqrt{n_{b}}}{2}\mathtt{x}&-w_{a}&\tfrac{\sqrt{n_{a}n_{b}}}{4}\mathtt{jxx}&-\tfrac{\sqrt{n_{a}}}{2}\mathtt{x}\\
\ket{0_{a}1_{b}}&-\tfrac{\sqrt{n_{a}}}{2}\mathtt{x}&\tfrac{\sqrt{n_{a}n_{b}}}{4}\mathtt{jxx}&-w_{b}&-\tfrac{\sqrt{n_{b}}}{2}\mathtt{x}\\
\ket{0_{a}0_{b}}&\tfrac{\sqrt{n_{a}n_{b}}}{4}\mathtt{jxx}&-\tfrac{\sqrt{n_{a}}}{2}\mathtt{x}&-\tfrac{\sqrt{n_{b}}}{2}\mathtt{x}&0\\
\end{array}

Assuming the coupling
J_{\mathsf{zz}}^{ab}
is large, we restrict to the low-energy subspace spanned by
\ket{1_{a}0_{b}},\ket{0_{a}1_{b}},\ket{0_{a}0_{b}}
, yielding:

\overline{\mathbbm{H}}_{L}={\Pi^{\tiny{\mathsf{ind}}}}\mathbbm{H}_{L}{\Pi^{\tiny{\mathsf{ind}}}}=\begin{array}[]{cccc}&\ket{1_{a}0_{b}}&\ket{0_{a}1_{b}}&\ket{0_{a}0_{b}}\\
\cline{2-4}\cr\ket{1_{a}0_{b}}&-w_{a}&\tfrac{\sqrt{n_{a}n_{b}}}{4}\mathtt{jxx}&-\tfrac{\sqrt{n_{a}}}{2}\mathtt{x}\\
\ket{0_{a}1_{b}}&\tfrac{\sqrt{n_{a}n_{b}}}{4}\mathtt{jxx}&-w_{b}&-\tfrac{\sqrt{n_{b}}}{2}\mathtt{x}\\
\ket{0_{a}0_{b}}&-\tfrac{\sqrt{n_{a}}}{2}\mathtt{x}&-\tfrac{\sqrt{n_{b}}}{2}\mathtt{x}&0\\
\end{array}

This restriction removes the high-energy state
\ket{1_{a}1_{b}}
, which is energetically penalized by large

J_{\mathsf{zz}}^{ab}
.

To uncover the effective angular momentum structure, we apply the basis transformation:

U_{\mathsf{merge}}=\begin{array}[]{cccc}&\ket{1_{c}}&\ket{0_{c}}&\ket{\odot_{q}}\\
\cline{2-4}\cr\ket{1_{a}0_{b}}&\sqrt{\tfrac{n_{a}}{n_{c}}}&0&-\sqrt{\tfrac{n_{b}}{n_{c}}}\\
\ket{0_{a}1_{b}}&\sqrt{\tfrac{n_{b}}{n_{c}}}&0&\sqrt{\tfrac{n_{a}}{n_{c}}}\\
\ket{0_{a}0_{b}}&0&1&0\\
\end{array}\quad\text{where }n_{c}=n_{a}+n_{b}.
