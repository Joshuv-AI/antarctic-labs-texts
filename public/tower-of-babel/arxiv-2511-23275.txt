Conjugate Generalised Bayesian Inference for Discrete Doubly Intractable Problems
William Laplante †1,2,4 , Matias Altamirano 2 , Jeremias Knoblauch 2 , Andrew Duncan 3 , and François-Xavier Briol 2

Abstract
Doubly intractable problems occur when both the likelihood and the posterior are available only in unnormalised form, with computationally intractable normalisation constants. Bayesian inference then typically requires direct approximation of the posterior through specialised and typically expensive MCMC methods. In this paper, we provide a computationally efficient alternative in the form of a novel generalised Bayesian posterior that allows for conjugate inference within the class of exponential family models for discrete data. We derive theoretical guarantees to characterize the asymptotic behavior of the generalised posterior, supporting its use for inference. The method is evaluated on a range of challenging intractable exponential family models, including the Conway-Maxwell-Poisson graphical model of multivariate count data, autoregressive discrete time series models, and Markov random fields such as the Ising and Potts models. The computational gains are significant; in our experiments, the method is between 10 10 and 6000 6000 times faster than state-of-the-art Bayesian computational methods.

Introduction

An
intractable
model is one whose normalisation constant (partition function) cannot be computed or efficiently approximated.
In discrete settings, statistical models rapidly become intractable due to summations over large, sometimes infinite, state spaces.
These intractable discrete models are prevalent, appearing in spatial statistics
(

(

)
) and multivariate count data
(

;

)
.
They are especially problematic in Bayesian settings since standard sampling methods no longer apply, leading to the well-established and challenging
doubly-intractable problem
, where the posterior as well as the model (or likelihood) are intractable.
Existing approaches provide approximate and often costly solutions, using MCMC with auxiliary variables
(
64
;
63
;
47
;
67
)
, approximations to the normalizing constant
(
50
)
, surrogate or composite likelihoods
(
3
;

;

)
, or variational Bayes methods
(
38
;
81
;

)
.
Alternatively, some assume that although the intractable model cannot be evaluated, it can be simulated from, leading to simulation-based inference methods
(
54
;
73
;
16
)
.
Overall, the computational burden introduced by intractable models is significant, often leading practitioners to
avoid
them in favour of simpler alternatives at the expense of expressiveness.
This leads directly to misspecified models, where unwanted effects are encountered, such as failing to capture data dispersion well or excluding the impact of important covariates.

For continuous domains,
55
introduced a method based on the
kernel Stein discrepancy
(KSD), and generalised Bayesian inference—a framework that extends traditional Bayesian updating, which relies on the likelihood, by enabling updates based on a broader class of loss functions or divergences
(
9
;
43
)
.
The method, called KSD-Bayes, is constructed such that (1) the need to compute the statistical model’s normalisation constant is removed, and (2) a
conjugate
generalised posterior is achieved for intractable models in the exponential family, which led to significant computational savings.

This was then followed up by
56
, who attempted to replicate the approach for discrete domains using the
discrete Fisher divergence
(DFD).
Although DFD-Bayes accomplishes the former property—thus improving on existing methods by enabling the use of out-of-the-box Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) algorithms—it does not yield conjugate posteriors.
This implies DFD-Bayes faces the typical drawbacks of MCMC sampling: It requires tuning and convergence checks, and struggles when the resulting posterior is multi-modal, the model is high-dimensional, or when the number of data points is large.
In contrast, these issues are at the very least mitigated or entirely avoided when a conjugate posterior is obtained.

This paper improves upon DFD-Bayes by crafting a new statistical divergence, called the
log-ratio matching (LRM) divergence
which induces conjugate generalised Bayesian posteriors for intractable exponential family models of discrete data—thus mirroring the developments of KSD-Bayes in continuous domains.
This is possible because the LRM is quadratic in the natural parameters of the exponential family, and conjugacy can therefore be achieved whenever the prior is chosen to be exponentially quadratic, such as a multivariate Gaussian.
Further, under mild conditions, this
LRM-Bayes posterior
satisfies a Bernstein-von-Mises result.
In the cases studied—including a 64-dimensional posterior for a multivariate Conway–Maxwell–Poisson (CMP) graphical model of cancer data, and a Potts model of a satellite image on a
174\times 174
grid—our method is between
10
and
6000
than existing methods, while yielding posterior distributions closely matching those from standard Bayesian method for doubly intractable problems.

2
Background

This section first discusses how generalised Bayesian inference can enable a substantial reduction in computation cost to update belief distributions.
We then discuss discrete divergences, along with their corresponding estimators, and emphasise their shortcomings in producing conjugate posteriors—a highly desirable feature for doubly intractable problems.

2.1
Generalised Bayes For Accelerated Computation

Throughout the remainder, the probability mass function of a statistical model parametrised by some continuous parameter
\bm{\theta}\in\Theta
and defined on data domain
\mathcal{X}
will be denoted as
p_{\bm{\theta}}
.
In the generalised Bayesian inference framework, a loss function
\mathcal{L}:\Theta\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
linking data to parameters is specified.
We concern ourselves with the special case in which
\mathcal{L}
is derived from a statistical divergence
D(q_{0}\|p_{\bm{\theta}})
between the data-generating process
q_{0}
and the model
p_{\bm{\theta}}
, typically up to an additive constant
C(q_{0})
independent of
\bm{\theta}
so that
\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta})=D(q_{0}\|p_{\bm{\theta}})+C(q_{0})

(
41
)
.
Recall that for any two probability mass functions
q
and
p
, a divergence satisfies
D(q\|p)\geq 0
and
D(q\|p)=0
if and only if
q=p
.
In most cases,
\mathcal{L}
cannot be computed directly; it is instead approximated using an estimator of the loss function
\hat{\mathcal{L}}:\Theta\times\mathcal{X}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
dependent on i.i.d. observations
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\sim q_{0}
.
For convenience of notation, we often suppress explicit dependence on the data and define
\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{n}(\bm{\theta}):=\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{\theta},\{\bm{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n})
.
Given a prior
\pi
and scaling parameter

has density
1
1

\pi_{\mathcal{L}}^{\beta}\left(\bm{\theta};\;\{\bm{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\right)\propto\exp\left(-\beta n\,\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{n}(\bm{\theta})\right)\pi(\bm{\theta}),

(1)

With a few additional but standard conditions, this uniform convergence ensures that the generalised posterior concentrates around minimisers of

Importantly, this framework recovers standard Bayesian inference as a special case when

This loss is quadratic for the natural parameters of any exponential family model

p^{\exp}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}):=\exp\left(\bm{\eta}(\bm{\theta})^{\top}\mathbf{T}(\bm{x})+B(\bm{x})-\log Z(\bm{\theta})\right),

Specifically, the loss becomes

\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{KSD}}_{n}(\bm{\theta})=\bm{\eta}(\bm{\theta})^{\top}\bm{\Lambda}_{n}\bm{\eta}(\bm{\theta})+\bm{\eta}(\bm{\theta})^{\top}\bm{\nu}_{n}

\bm{\Lambda}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}

Equation

It was also shown in

These results are strictly applicable to continuous domains.
In contrast, in discrete settings, there is to date no known statistical divergence that yields a quadratic loss for exponential family models, and thus no analogous conjugate generalised posterior.
In the next section, we introduce existing divergences for discrete intractable models, highlighting their shortcomings in achieving conjugacy.

2.2
Divergences for Discrete Intractable Models

Suppose that
p_{\bm{\theta}}
is defined on a discrete domain
\mathcal{X}
and with a normalisation constant that cannot be computed. That is,

p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x})=\frac{\tilde{p}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x})}{Z(\bm{\theta})},\quad Z(\bm{\theta}):=\sum_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\tilde{p}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}),

(3)

where, unlike the intractable
Z(\bm{\theta})>0
,
\tilde{p}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x})
is easily evaluated.
Many statistical divergences for models on discrete domains unfortunately depend on the intractable
Z(\bm{\theta})
, and we therefore focus on divergences that by-pass computation of this constant.

37
first investigated such divergences via an estimation of models of binary data, where
\mathcal{X}=\{-1,1\}^{d}
.
The strategy is to force the ratios
p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x})/p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}_{-i})
and
q_{0}(\bm{x})/q_{0}(\bm{x}_{-i})
to be equal, where
\bm{x}_{-i}=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,-x_{i},\dots,x_{d})
.
For a parametric model
p_{\bm{\theta}}
, the ratio-matching divergence proposed is given by

\displaystyle D^{\operatorname{RM}}(q_{0}\|p_{\bm{\theta}})

\displaystyle:=\quad

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim q_{0}}\Bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{d}\Bigg(g\!\left(\frac{q_{0}(\bm{x})}{q_{0}(\bm{x}_{-i})}\right)-g\!\left(\frac{p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x})}{p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}_{-i})}\right)\Bigg)^{2}+\Bigg(g\!\left(\frac{q_{0}(\bm{x}_{-i})}{q_{0}(\bm{x})}\right)-g\!\left(\frac{p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}_{-i})}{p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x})}\right)\Bigg)^{2}\Bigg]

\displaystyle=\quad

\displaystyle\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim q_{0}}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{d}g^{2}\!\left(\frac{p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x})}{p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}_{-i})}\right)\right]}_{\mathcal{L}^{\operatorname{RM}}(\bm{\theta})}+C^{\operatorname{RM}}(q_{0})

where
g(u)=1/(1+u)
serves as a bounded transformation that prevents numerical instability when probabilities are small; the constant
C^{\operatorname{RM}}(q_{0})\in\mathbb{R}
does not depend on
\bm{\theta}
; and the second line follows from Theorem 1 of
37
.
Importantly,
D^{\text{RM}}
induces a loss
\mathcal{L}^{\text{RM}}
not depending on
Z(\bm{\theta})
and that can be estimated using only samples from
q_{0}
.
Subsequent studies have proposed similar objectives for fitting unnormalised models, including energy-based and generative models
(
51
;
29
;
66
;
58

77

(
84

25

, gradient estimation
(

(

56

51

Assuming an ordering of the domain
\mathcal{X}
(see Definition 1 of
56

x^{\pm}
denoting the previous or next element of
x
in the domain
\mathcal{X}
, and given some
h:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, we define the backward difference operator as
\nabla^{-}h(\bm{x}):=\left(h(\bm{x})-h(\bm{x}^{1-}),\dots,h(\bm{x})-h(\bm{x}^{d-})\right)^{\top}
for
\bm{x}^{j\pm}:=(x_{1},\dots,x_{j}^{\pm},\dots,x_{d})
. Armed with this operator, the DFD is defined as

\begin{split}D^{\operatorname{DFD}}(q_{0}\|p_{\bm{\theta}})&:=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim q_{0}}\left[\left\|\frac{\nabla^{-}p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x})}{p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x})}-\frac{\nabla^{-}q_{0}(\bm{x})}{q_{0}(\bm{x})}\right\|^{2}\right]\\
&=\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim q_{0}}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left(\frac{p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}^{j-})}{p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x})}\right)^{2}-2\left(\frac{p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x})}{p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}^{j+})}\right)\right]}_{\mathcal{L}^{\text{DFD}}(\bm{\theta})}+C^{\operatorname{DFD}}(q_{0}),\end{split}

where
C^{\operatorname{DFD}}(q_{0})
does not depend on
\bm{\theta}
, and where the second equality follows from Proposition 1 of
56
. When constraining the model class to exponential families as in
Equation

2
,
the DFD loss is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{DFD}}(\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim q_{0}}\!\Bigg[\sum_{j=1}^{d}e^{2\bm{\eta}(\bm{\theta})^{\top}\Delta\mathbf{T}_{j}^{-}(\bm{x})+2\Delta B_{j}^{-}(\bm{x})}-2e^{\bm{\eta}(\bm{\theta})^{\top}\Delta\mathbf{T}_{j}^{+}(\bm{x})+\Delta B_{j}^{+}(\bm{x})}\Bigg].

3
Methodology

This section introduces a novel
log-ratio matching divergence
, from which a loss function can be obtained to construct a generalised posterior—the
LRM-Bayes
posterior.
We demonstrate that this posterior is
conjugate
for exponential family models, and discuss its hyperparameter selection.

Before doing so, we briefly introduce notation. The domain
\mathcal{X}
is a countable space, and
2^{\mathcal{X}}
denotes the set of all subsets of
\mathcal{X}
.

\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{X})
denotes the set of probability mass functions (PMFs) on
\mathcal{X}
.
For any
q\in\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{X})
, the set of PMFs whose support is contained in that of
q
is
\mathcal{Q}_{q}(\mathcal{X}):=\{p\in\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{X})\;|\;\operatorname{supp}(p)\subseteq\operatorname{supp}(q)\}
, where
\operatorname{supp}(q):=\{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}:q(\bm{x})>0\}
.
For
r\in\mathbb{N}
, we also denote
L^{r}(q,\mathbb{R}):=\{f:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\;|\;\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim q}\left[|f(\bm{x})|^{r}\right]<\infty\}
the space of functions that are
r
-integrable against
q
.

3.1
Log-Ratio Matching Divergence

To define a divergence between
q\in\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{X})
and

Intuitively, the matching set is a set-valued function that enables

The main properties of

denotes the cardinality of a set.

We are now ready to introduce our
log-ratio matching
(LRM) divergence.
For the regularity conditions that follow, we equip each matching set
M(\bm{x})
with an arbitrary index set
\mathcal{J}

m

M(\bm{x})=\{M_{j}(\bm{x})\}_{j\in\mathcal{J}}

M_{j}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}

Importantly, this indexing is noninformative, does not depend on
\bm{x}
, and does not impose any ordering.
Using these maps

\mathcal{R}_{j}[q](\bm{x}):=\log\frac{q(M_{j}(\bm{x}))}{q(\bm{x})}

For any reference PMF
q\in\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{X})

q
and satisfy an

q

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}^{\operatorname{adm}}_{q}(\mathcal{X}):=\Big\{p\in\mathcal{Q}_{q}(\mathcal{X})\mid\mathcal{R}_{j}[p]\in L^{2}(q,\mathbb{R})\;\forall j\in\mathcal{J}\Big\}.

Definition 3.1

(The LRM Divergence)
.

Suppose
q\in\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{X})
.
The
log-ratio matching (LRM) divergence
between
q,p\in\mathcal{Q}^{\operatorname{adm}}_{q}(\mathcal{X})
is defined as:

\begin{split}D^{\operatorname{LRM}}(q\|p):&=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim q}\left[\frac{1}{|M(\bm{x})|}\sum_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in M(\bm{x})}\left(\log\frac{p(\bm{x}^{\prime})}{p(\bm{x})}-\log\frac{q(\bm{x}^{\prime})}{q(\bm{x})}\right)^{2}\right]\\[5.0pt]
&=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim q}\left[\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\left(\mathcal{R}_{j}[p]-\mathcal{R}_{j}[q]\right)^{2}\right]\end{split}

(4)

D^{\operatorname{LRM}}

Assumption 3.1

(Graph Connectedness)
.

The matching set
M
induces a graph
G:=(\mathcal{X},E)
with edges
E:=\bigcup_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\{(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime}):\bm{x}^{\prime}\in M(\bm{x})\}

which is

\bm{x},\bm{y}\in\mathcal{X}
there exists a

between them: a finite sequence of vertices
(\bm{x}_{0},\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{k})
with
\bm{x}_{0}=\bm{x}
,
\bm{x}_{k}=\bm{y}
, and
(\bm{x}_{i-1},\bm{x}_{i})\in E
for all
i=1,\ldots,k

Theorem 3.2

(LRM is a Statistical Divergence)
.

Suppose
q\in\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{X})
and

p,q\in\mathcal{Q}^{\operatorname{adm}}_{q}(\mathcal{X})

D^{\operatorname{LRM}}(q\|p)=0\Leftrightarrow q=p.

The proof for

We briefly discuss how graph connectedness is achieved in

3.2
Inference with Log-Ratio Matching

From the log-ratio matching divergence
D^{\operatorname{LRM}}
, we will construct a loss function
\mathcal{L}^{\operatorname{LRM}}
and a corresponding estimator
\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}
to use for inference. Throughout, given a data-generating process
q_{0}
, our parametric model of interest will be
\mathcal{P}_{\Theta}(\mathcal{X})
:=
\{p_{\bm{\theta}}\in\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{X}):\bm{\theta}\in\Theta\}
, with parameter space
\Theta\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p}
.
Estimating the loss will require a PMF estimate
\hat{q}
of
q_{0}
, along with assumptions that will be maintained throughout the rest of this paper.

Standing Assumption
.

Inference with log-ratio matching requires the following:

(1)

The domain
\mathcal{X}
is a countable space, and the data
\{\bm{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}q_{0}
for a data-generating process
q_{0}\in\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{X})
such that
q_{0}\in\mathcal{Q}^{\operatorname{adm}}_{q_{0}}(\mathcal{X})
.

(2)

Every model
p_{\bm{\theta}}\in\mathcal{P}_{\Theta}(\mathcal{X})
belongs to the class

\mathcal{Q}^{\mathrm{adm}}_{q_{0}}(\mathcal{X})
.

(3)

For each sample size
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the estimate
\hat{q}
of
q_{0}
satisfies
\hat{q}\in\mathcal{Q}^{\mathrm{adm}}_{q_{0}}(\mathcal{X})
almost surely.

Part
(1)
of the Standing Assumption ensures that the local ratio terms are square-integrable, guaranteeing that all expectations appearing in
D^{\operatorname{LRM}}
are finite.
As previously discussed, this assumption is automatically satisfied for finite sample spaces and is only mildly restrictive in countable discrete settings.
Part
(2)
of the Standing Assumption imposes the same integrability condition for the parametric model family and excludes models that assign zero probability to possible observations.
This strict positivity requirement is met by all models considered in this paper and by many other discrete models.
Similarly, Part
(3)
of the Standing Assumption requires the estimated PMF
\hat{q}
to be strictly positive on the support of
q_{0}
and square-integrable under
q_{0}
.
This condition is typically satisfied by standard PMF estimators such as Laplace smoothing
(
14
)
.

We now present the log-ratio matching loss.

Definition 3.2

(The LRM Loss)
.

The log-ratio matching loss is given by:

\mathcal{L}^{\operatorname{LRM}}(\bm{\theta}):=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim q_{0}}\left[\frac{1}{|M(\bm{x})|}\sum_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in M(\bm{x})}\left(\log\frac{p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}^{\prime})}{p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x})}\right)^{2}-2\log\frac{p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}^{\prime})}{p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x})}\log\frac{q_{0}(\bm{x}^{\prime})}{q_{0}(\bm{x})}\right].

(5)

The loss from
Equation

5
is straightforwardly obtained from
D^{\operatorname{LRM}}
by expanding the square and dropping the term not depending on
\bm{\theta}
.
For some divergence-based losses, such as
\mathcal{L}^{\operatorname{DFD}}
and
\mathcal{L}^{\operatorname{RM}}
,
q_{0}
only appears as the measure over which the loss’ expectation is taken; therefore, these losses can be approximated with samples from
q_{0}
.
However, this is not the case for LRM: estimating
\mathcal{L}^{\operatorname{LRM}}
requires a PMF estimate
\hat{q}
of
q_{0}
, since
q_{0}
also appears inside the log-ratio term.
This reliance on an estimate of
q_{0}
has precedent in works such as
41
and
31
.
With an estimate
\hat{q}
, we can now define the estimator of the log-ratio matching loss as follows:

\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}(\bm{\theta}):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{|M(\bm{x}_{i})|}\sum_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in M(\bm{x}_{i})}\left(\log\frac{p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}^{\prime})}{p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}_{i})}\right)^{2}-2\log\frac{p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}^{\prime})}{p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}_{i})}\log\frac{\hat{q}(\bm{x}^{\prime})}{\hat{q}(\bm{x}_{i})},

(6)

where the dependency on the estimate
\hat{q}
is implied from the hat notation.
In
Section

5
, we discuss the theoretical requirements, in particular those needed for
\hat{q}
to ensure that
\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}(\bm{\theta})\longrightarrow\mathcal{L}^{\operatorname{LRM}}(\bm{\theta})
almost surely pointwise and uniformly in
\bm{\theta}
.
Although not the focus of this paper, we note that the loss from
Equation

6
could also be used in a frequentist setting,
In
Appendix

D
, we study some of the properties of the resulting frequentist estimator.
As we will demonstrate in
Section

3.3
, for exponential family models, the loss function is quadratic, which would lead to a closed-form minimum distance estimator.
Additionally, the loss can be extended to include data-dependent weights, providing robustness against outliers.
We elaborate on this extension in
Appendix

C
.

Finally, the loss
\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}
allows us to build the
log-ratio matching generalised Bayesian (LRM-Bayes) posterior
, presented below, which has desirable theoretical properties that we elaborate on in
Section

5
.

Definition 3.3

(The LRM-Bayes Posterior)
.

The LRM-Bayes posterior has density:

\hat{\pi}_{M}^{\beta}(\bm{\theta};\;\{\bm{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n})\propto\exp\left(-\beta n\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}(\bm{\theta})\right)\pi(\bm{\theta}).

3.3
Conjugate Posterior for Exponential Family Models

Similar to the work of
56
(DFD-Bayes), we develop a generalised posterior for any discrete intractable model.
However, in stark contrast to their approach, when
\mathcal{P}_{\Theta}(\mathcal{X})
is restricted to the exponential family model outlined in
Equation

2
, the LRM-Bayes posterior is
conjugate
.
This is demonstrated in the following
Proposition

3.3
.

Proposition 3.3

(Exponential Family Models and Quadratic Losses)
.

Suppose
p_{\bm{\theta}}\in\mathcal{P}_{\Theta}(\mathcal{X})
is an exponential family, i.e. it is of the form in
Equation

2
.
Then,

\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}(\bm{\theta})=\bm{\eta}(\bm{\theta})^{\top}\bm{\Lambda}_{n}\bm{\eta}(\bm{\theta})-2\bm{\eta}(\bm{\theta})^{\top}\bm{\nu}_{n}+C^{\operatorname{LRM}}(q_{0}),

for a constant
C^{\operatorname{LRM}}(q_{0})
independent of
\bm{\theta}
, and

\begin{split}&\bm{\Lambda}_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{|M(\bm{x}_{i})|}\sum_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in M(\bm{x}_{i})}\left(\mathbf{T}(\bm{x}^{\prime})-\mathbf{T}(\bm{x}_{i})\right)\left(\mathbf{T}(\bm{x}^{\prime})-\mathbf{T}(\bm{x}_{i})\right)^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}\\
&\bm{\nu}_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{|M(\bm{x}_{i})|}\sum_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in M(\bm{x}_{i})}\left(\mathbf{T}(\bm{x}^{\prime})-\mathbf{T}(\bm{x}_{i})\right)\left(\log\frac{\hat{q}(\bm{x}^{\prime})}{\hat{q}(\bm{x}_{i})}-\left(B(\bm{x}^{\prime})-B(\bm{x}_{i})\right)\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{p}.\end{split}

For an exponentially quadratic prior

\pi(\bm{\eta})\propto\exp(-\frac{1}{2}(\bm{\eta}-\bm{\mu})^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}(\bm{\eta}-\bm{\mu})),

with
\bm{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{p},\mathbf{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
, and
\mathbf{\Sigma}
positive definite,
\beta>0
, the LRM-Bayes posterior
\hat{\pi}_{M}^{\beta}
on
\bm{\eta}
simplifies to
\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu}_{n},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n})
for

\bm{\mu}_{n}:=\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n}\left(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{\mu}+2\beta n\bm{\nu}_{n}\right)
and
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n}:=\left(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}+2\beta n\bm{\Lambda}_{n}\right)^{-1}
.

The derivation can be found in
Section

E.2
.
We note that for exponential family models, Part
(2)
of Standing Assumption holds if, for each
M_{j}
(
j=1,\dots,m
): (i) for all
i=1,\dots,p
,
\Delta T_{i,j}:=T_{i}\circ M_{j}-T_{i}\in L^{2}(q_{0},\mathbb{R})
, where
\mathbf{T}(\bm{x}):=(T_{1}(\bm{x}),\dots,T_{p}(\bm{x}))^{\top}
; and (ii)
\Delta B_{j}:=B\circ M_{j}-B\in L^{2}(q_{0},\mathbb{R})
, where
\circ
denotes function composition.

Proposition

3.3
implies significant computational gains over the DFD-Bayes posterior of
56
for exponential family models, as MCMC sampling is no longer required.
Supposing
m\propto d
(for instance, see
Section

4.2
), computing
\bm{\Lambda}_{n}
and
\bm{\nu}_{n}
incurs a naive cost
\mathcal{O}(ndp^{2})
, which includes the computation of
\hat{q}
, typically requiring one pass through all samples.
Obtaining the LRM-Bayes posterior then only requires computing
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n}
and
\bm{\mu}_{n}
, which has complexity
\mathcal{O}(ndp^{2}+p^{3})
.
In contrast, the DFD-Bayes posterior is approximated through MCMC, and the complexity scales with both
p
and the number of MCMC steps
T
, implying a cost
\mathcal{O}(ndpT)
.
For fixed
p
and
n\gg p
, the dominant costs are
\mathcal{O}(nd)
for LRM-Bayes and
\mathcal{O}(ndT)
for DFD-Bayes: LRM-Bayes then effectively reduces computational scaling by a factor of
T
.
This advantage becomes increasingly pronounced as
n
increases, since DFD-Bayes’ cost scales linearly in the number of MCMC iterations
T
.

3.4
Hyperparameter Selection

To construct
\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}
and the LRM-Bayes generalised posterior
\hat{\pi}_{M}^{\beta}
, we require an estimate
\hat{q}
of
q_{0}
.
The empirical PMF
\hat{q}^{\operatorname{emp}}(\bm{x})=\frac{1}{n}C_{n}(\bm{x})
, where
C_{n}(\bm{x}):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta(\bm{x}_{i}=\bm{x})
, is the canonical nonparametric estimator, and can be used for LRM-Bayes; see
Section

A.2
for more details.
However, it can be unstable when
n
is small
(
14
)
, and it does not guarantee strict positivity on all of
\mathcal{X}
, and would therefore break Part
(3)
of the Standing Assumption.
To resolve this, we use
Laplace additive smoothing
as a regularised extension of the empirical PMF.
For
\alpha\in[0,1]
and a base PMF
q^{\dagger}(\bm{x}):=\tilde{q}^{\dagger}(\bm{x})/Z^{\dagger}
, where
Z^{\dagger}:=\sum_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\tilde{q}^{\dagger}(\bm{x})<\infty
, Laplace additive smoothing defines

\hat{q}_{\alpha}(\bm{x}):=\frac{C_{n}(\bm{x})+\alpha\tilde{q}^{\dagger}(\bm{x})}{n+\alpha Z^{\dagger}}.

(7)

This form can be interpreted as the posterior mean of a multinomial likelihood with Dirichlet prior and base PMF
q^{\dagger}
. The PMF
\hat{q}_{\alpha}
satisfies Part
(3)
of the Standing Assumption whenever the base PMF
\tilde{q}^{\dagger}\in\mathcal{Q}^{\operatorname{adm}}_{q_{0}}(\mathcal{X})
.
When
\mathcal{X}
is finite, we take
q^{\dagger}
to be uniform.
When
\mathcal{X}
is countably infinite, we typically select
q^{\dagger}
to be a mixture between a uniform distribution covering the majority of the mass of
q_{0}
and a distribution with the same support as
q_{0}
; see
Section

A.3
for more details.
Finally, there remains to estimate the value of
\beta
.
We achieve this by following the procedure outlined in
80
, which aims to obtain an approximate nominal frequentist coverage probability.
This approach to estimating
\beta
is summarised in
Section

A.4
and adopted throughout the paper.

We investigate LRM-Bayes for intractable models of count data and for Markov random fields on lattices, demonstrating that it achieves results comparable to other generalised and standard Bayes methods while incurring significantly lower computational costs thanks to conjugacy.
In the figures, we refer to LRM-Bayes and DFD-Bayes as
LRM
and

.
We primarily compare our method to DFD-Bayes because, before the work if this paper, it is the most computationally efficient methods for the class of discrete doubly intractable problems. In some context, we also compare to a Bayesian posterior based on the pseudo-likelihood
(
70

, denoted by

Finally, where computationally feasible, we also compare to a standard Bayesian posterior approximated through MCMC as a reference. This will typically based on standard Metropolis-Hastings with a truncated normalisation constant, or auxiliary variable MCMC
(

and

4.1.2
Graphical Model for Breast Cancer Data

Next, we apply a CMP graphical model to a breast cancer gene expression dataset previously studied in
56
and
39
.
This is a substantially more challenging task compared to the univariate case: the data consist of
n=878
patients across
d=10
dimensions, with a parameter space of size
p=64
.
In this regime, standard Bayesian inference is effectively computationally infeasible, since estimating the normalising constant requires truncated summations whose complexity grows combinatorially with
d
(approximately
10^{K}
terms for truncation level
K
).
We show that despite this, LRM-Bayes provides a reliable posterior distribution at very low cost compared to state-of-the-art methods.
The CMP graphical model we consider is given by:

\begin{split}p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x})\propto\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\theta_{i}x_{i}-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{i<j}\theta_{i,j}x_{i}x_{j}-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\theta_{0,i}\log(x_{i}!)\right)\end{split}

where
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
, the parameters
\theta_{i},\theta_{i,j}\in\mathbb{R}
, and
\theta_{0,i}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
.
The dimensionality of
\Theta
scales with
d
as
p=2d+d(d-1)/2
.
The model can be written as an exponential family with
\bm{\eta}(\bm{\theta}):=\left((\theta_{i})_{i=1}^{d},(-\theta_{i,j})_{i<j},(-\theta_{0,i})_{i=1}^{d}\right)^{\top}
,
\mathbf{T}(\bm{x}):=\left((x_{i})_{i=1}^{d},(x_{i}x_{j})_{i<j},(\log(x_{i}!))_{i=1}^{d}\right)^{\top}
, and
B(\bm{x})=0
, where
(\cdot)_{i=1}^{n}
corresponds to vector notation.

Since standard Bayes is infeasible, we compare LRM-Bayes with DFD-Bayes.
For LRM-Bayes, the CMP model must satisfy Part
(2)
of the Standing Assumption, which holds if
\Delta T_{i,j}\in L^{2}(q_{0},\mathbb{R})
for all
i=1,\dots,p
and
j=1,\dots,m
(
B=0
, so
\Delta B\in L^{2}(q_{0},\mathbb{R})
automatically).
In the setting of this experiment, to stabilise inference, we select a larger matching set
M(\bm{x})
with elements
M_{j}
defined as all points obtained by adding offsets
\{-2,-1,1,2\}
to each individual coordinate of
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}
.
Then, for
\Delta T_{i,j}\in L^{2}(q_{0},\mathbb{R})
to hold, we require
q_{0}
to have finite second moment
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim q_{0}}[x_{i}^{2}]<\infty
for all
i=1,\dots,d
.

This last CMP-based experiment investigates a time series model for discrete count data.
Such models have been studied extensively in the literature, with notable applications in econometrics, finance and sociology

For this model class, we highlight the broad applicability of the LRM-Bayes method and demonstrate that it admits partial conjugacy, which can lead to substantial computational benefits for MCMC methods and well beyond closed form computations.
To this end, we focus on a CMP variant of the popular Poisson integer-valued GARCH model

(see
82
;
12
;
33

; the former was also introduced in
33

The model is:

x_{t}\mid x_{1:t-1}\sim\text{CMP}(\lambda_{t},\theta_{3}),\quad\quad\log\lambda_{t}=\theta_{1}+\varphi\log\lambda_{t-1}+\theta_{2}\log(1+x_{t-1})

(8)

for some fixed initial
\lambda_{0}>0
and
t\in[0,T]
.
The complete model
p_{(\bm{\theta},\varphi)}(x_{t}\mid x_{1:t-1})
is
not
in the exponential family class since

\log\lambda_{T}=\varphi^{T}\log\lambda_{0}+\theta_{1}\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}\varphi^{k}+\theta_{2}\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}\varphi^{T-k-1}\log(1+x_{k}):=c_{t}+\theta_{1}b_{t}+\theta_{2}a_{t},

where
c_{t},b_{t},a_{t}
depend on
\varphi
.
However, for fixed
\varphi
,
p_{\bm{\theta}}(x_{t}\mid x_{1:t-1})
is an exponential family model with
\bm{\eta}(\bm{\theta})=(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},-\theta_{3})^{\top}
,
\mathbf{T}(x_{t};x_{1:t-1},\varphi)=(b_{t}x_{t},a_{t}x_{t},\log(x_{t}!))^{\top}
, and
B(x_{t};x_{1:t-1},\varphi)=c_{t}x_{t}
.
This enables partial conjugacy, which in turns allows the usage of algorithms such as Metropolis-within-Gibbs.
In contrast, even for the Poisson variant, standard Bayesian updating with a Gaussian prior does not produce a conjugate posterior.

4.2
Intractable Models on Lattices

We now move to discrete
Markov random fields
, another well-established class of generally intractable models.
We demonstrate that our method performs well in these settings, which are notoriously difficult for standard Bayesian inference reliant on conventional MCMC-based approaches (see
64
;
67
;
59
).
Details for the experiments that follow can be found in
Section

B.2
.

Suppose the domain
\mathcal{X}
is a square lattice made of sites
j=1,\ldots,d
, where at each site, we observe
x_{j}
, which takes values in a finite set of states
\mathcal{S}
.
Therefore,
\bm{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\in\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{S}^{d}
.
By the Markovian assumption, each site is solely dependent on its nearest neighbours.
We denote this with
\text{nb}(x_{j}):=\{x_{j^{\prime}}:j^{\prime}\text{ is a neighbouring site of }j\}
. The Markov random field model is given by:

p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x})=\frac{1}{Z(\bm{\theta})}\exp\left(\theta_{1}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\psi(x_{j})+\theta_{2}\sum_{x_{j^{\prime}}\in\text{nb}(x_{j})}\;\phi(x_{j},x_{j^{\prime}})\right),

(9)

where
\psi:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
denotes a scalar function on the sites and
\phi:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
over any two sites. Here,
\mathbf{T}(\bm{x}):=(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\psi(x_{j}),\sum_{x_{j^{\prime}}\in\text{nb}(x_{j})}\phi(x_{j},x_{j^{\prime}}))^{\top}
and
\bm{\eta}(\bm{\theta})=(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})^{\top}
.
Because
|\mathcal{X}|<\infty
, Part
(2)
of the Standing Assumption is satisfied so long as
\psi,\phi
are bounded.

For this data structure, the matching set of
\bm{x}
is typically obtained by iteratively altering states at each site.
That is,
M(\bm{x}):=\{\bm{x}^{\prime}:\bm{x}^{\prime}_{-j}=\bm{x}_{-j}\;\text{and}\;x^{\prime}_{j}=s\;\text{for}\;s\in\mathcal{S}\setminus\{x_{j}\},\;\text{for}\;j=1,...,d\}
, such that for any
\bm{x}
,
|M(\bm{x})|=d\cdot(|\mathcal{S}|-1)
.
Note that with this construction, the graph connectedness condition of
Theorem

3.2
is directly satisfied.

These models are of particular interest for this paper because
\mathcal{L}^{\operatorname{LRM}}
simplifies greatly when we make similar assumptions as the model
p_{\bm{\theta}}
does.
First, suppose that for
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}
, we have
\bm{x}^{\prime}\in M(\bm{x})
such that
\bm{x}^{\prime}_{-k}=\bm{x}_{-k}
and
x_{k}\neq x^{\prime}_{k}
, following the matching set convention previously defined. Then, the model’s log-ratio reduces to:

\log\frac{p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}^{\prime})}{p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x})}=\theta_{1}\left(\psi(x_{k}^{\prime})-\psi(x_{k})\right)+\theta_{2}\sum_{x_{j}\in\text{nb}(x_{k})}(\phi(x^{\prime}_{k},x_{j})-\phi(x_{k},x_{j})),

which is linear in
\bm{\theta}=(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})
.
We now assume a Markov blanket for
q_{0}
as well, i.e.
q_{0}(x_{k}\mid\bm{x}_{-k})=q_{0}(x_{k}\mid\text{nb}(x_{k}))
.
Then, for
\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime}
defined as before, the log-ratio of
q_{0}
is

\log\frac{q_{0}(\bm{x}^{\prime})}{q_{0}(\bm{x})}=\log\frac{q_{0}(x^{\prime}_{k}\mid\bm{x}_{-k})q_{0}(\bm{x}_{-k})}{q_{0}(x_{k}\mid\bm{x}_{-k})q_{0}(\bm{x}_{-k})}=\log\frac{q_{0}(x_{k}^{\prime}\mid\text{nb}(x_{k}))}{q_{0}(x_{k}\mid\text{nb}(x_{k}))}.

The Markov property for
q_{0}
holds when the model in
Equation

9
is well specified, but may or may not hold for misspecified models.
The model also assumes stationarity, meaning all sites share the same conditional distribution.
These two assumptions are crucial for LRM-Bayes: rather than estimating the full conditional
q_{0}(x_{k}\mid\bm{x}_{-k})
, which becomes exponentially harder as
d
grows and typically requires multiple observed lattices, we can instead target the local conditional
q_{0}(x_{k}\mid\text{nb}(x_{k}))
.
Consequently,
\mathcal{L}^{\operatorname{LRM}}
can be estimated from a single observed lattice (or more, if available).
Further details on this simplification can be found in
Section

B.2.1
.

5
Theoretical Assessment

We provide a concise theoretical analysis regarding posterior consistency and Bernstein–von Mises type behaviour for LRM-Bayes.
Our theory relies on the arguments of
61
, and thus applies equally to the well-specified and misspecified regime.
We confine our presentation to the case of exponential families, since they are the main model class considered in this paper, but

Appendix

D
extends the results to more general settings for completeness.
This appendix also includes proofs and additional technical lemmas that may be of interest for the frequentist study of LRM-based point estimators.
The proofs for the presented theoretical assessment are in
Appendix

E
.

The main results presented hereafter demonstrate that LRM-Bayes enjoys the same asymptotic guarantees as other generalised posteriors
(
55
;
15
;
56
)
.
Here, the technical complication relative to prior work is the LRM estimator’s reliance on the PMF estimate
\hat{q}_{\alpha}
.
For our results to hold, we will require that the estimated log ratios used within LRM-Bayes can be estimated arbitrarily well as
n\to\infty
on every truncated domain where

q_{0}
is bounded away from zero.
The next lemma establishes this property.

Lemma 5.1
.

For
\alpha>0
, for every
\varepsilon>0
, the PMF estimator
\hat{q}_{\alpha}
in (
7
) satisfies:

\displaystyle\sup_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}:q_{0}(\bm{x})\geq\epsilon}\max_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in M(\bm{x})}\left|\log\left(\frac{\hat{q}_{\alpha}(\bm{x}^{\prime})}{\hat{q}_{\alpha}(\bm{x})}\right)-\log\left(\frac{q_{0}(\bm{x}^{\prime})}{q_{0}(\bm{x})}\right)\right|\overset{a.s.}{\longrightarrow}0.

(10)

This allows us to overcome the challenges posed by the PMF estimator
\hat{q}_{\alpha}
. To guarantee the remaining pointwise convergence properties of
\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}
, we impose regularity conditions.

Assumption 5.2
.

One of the following conditions holds:

(i)

|\mathcal{X}|<\infty
;

(ii)

\mathcal{X}
is countably infinite, and
q_{0}
is a subexponential distribution. Moreover, there exists constants
K_{q_{0}}>0
,
\gamma>1
and a sequence
(K_{\hat{q}_{\alpha},n})_{n\geq 1}
of non negative constants with
\limsup_{n\to\infty}K_{\hat{q}_{\alpha},n}<\infty
, such that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}
,
j\in\mathcal{J}
,
|\mathcal{R}_{j}[q_{0}](\bm{x})|\leq K_{q_{0}}(1+\|\bm{x}\|^{\gamma})
and
|\mathcal{R}_{j}[\hat{q}_{\alpha}](\bm{x})|\leq K_{\hat{q}_{\alpha},n}(1+\|\bm{x}\|^{\gamma})

In
5.2
, (i) yields pointwise convergence straightforwardly, and (ii) allows unbounded spaces so long as
q_{0}
does not put too much mass on a large number of states, and that log-ratios do not grow too quickly, the latter being ensured by defining
M
so that each
\bm{x}^{\prime}\in M(\bm{x})
differs from
\bm{x}
by a bounded perturbation. We note that this assumption holds for all experiments in the paper. The discrete Markov random fields all satisfy (i), whilst for the count data models, we have provided experiments with all three model classes where the part of (ii) dependent on
\hat{q}_{\alpha}
holds, and the part of (ii) dependent on
q_{0}
can be verified for all synthetic experiments.

To obtain a Berstein-von Mises theorem and posterior concentration, for non-exponential family models, stronger modes of convergence are required—we refer the reader to
Appendix

D
.
However, for the special case of exponential family likelihoods, these results follow immediately from convexity in the natural parameters.

Theorem 5.3

(Consistency & Bernstein–von Mises)
.

Suppose
p_{\bm{\theta}}
is an exponential family as in
Equation

2
with natural parameter
\eta(\bm{\theta})=\bm{\theta}
. Then
\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}(\bm{\theta})
and
\mathcal{L}^{\operatorname{LRM}}(\bm{\theta})
are convex in
\bm{\theta}
, and therefore
\mathcal{L}^{\operatorname{LRM}}
has a minimiser
\bm{\theta}_{\star}\in\Theta
.
In addition, suppose that
5.2
holds and that the prior
\pi
has a density continuous at
\bm{\theta}_{\star}
with
\pi(\bm{\theta}_{\star})>0
.
Let
B_{\epsilon}(\bm{\theta}_{\star})=\{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta\,:\,\|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{\star}\|_{2}\leq\epsilon\}
. Then, for any
\epsilon>0
,

\int_{B_{\epsilon}(\bm{\theta}_{\star})}\hat{\pi}_{M}^{\beta}(\bm{\theta})d\bm{\theta}\;\xrightarrow{\text{a.s}}\;1.

Let
\tilde{\pi}_{M}
be the p.d.f. of the random variable
\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{n}:=\sqrt{n}(\bm{\theta}-\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n})
for
\bm{\theta}\sim\hat{\pi}_{M}^{\beta}
, viewed as a p.d.f. on
\mathbb{R}^{p}
.
Let
\mathbf{H}_{\star}:=\nabla^{2}_{\bm{\theta}}\,\mathcal{L}_{M}(\bm{\theta})\big|_{\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}_{\star}}
. If
\mathbf{H}_{\star}
is non-singular,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{p}}\left|\tilde{\pi}_{M}(\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{n})-\frac{1}{\det(2\pi\mathbf{H}_{\star}^{-1})^{1/2}}\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\bm{\theta}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_{\star}\bm{\theta}\right)\right|\,d\bm{\theta}\;\xrightarrow{\text{a.s}}\;0.

The above result shows that under mild regularity conditions on the prior the LRM–Bayes posterior is asymptotically Gaussian.
Such results are now considered standard in the generalised Bayes literature, where they have several important uses.
Most immediately, the Gaussian limit provides a convenient approximation to the posterior.
More interestingly, when the underlying model is well specified, discrepancies between the asymptotic covariance matrix
\mathbf{H}_{\star}
and the inverse Fisher information have been used to assess posterior calibration or loss of efficiency relative to standard Bayesian procedures.

6
Conclusion

This paper introduces a new divergence for discrete-valued data we call the log-ratio matching (LRM) divergence.
Combined with generalised Bayesian inference (GBI), this produces LRM-Bayes: a conjugate generalised posterior for discrete intractable exponential family models which is consistent and satisfies a Bernstein-von-Mises theorem.
The resulting procedure is substantially more computationally efficient than standard Bayes and the related GBI-based approach in
56
, with computational gains between
10\times
and
6000\times
while producing posterior distributions that closely match those obtained by other baseline methods.

The scope of LRM-Bayes is already broad, spanning graphical count data models and Markov random fields, but several extensions follow naturally from the present formulation.
One direction concerns settings in which part of the data is continuous, such as regression tasks with discrete responses and continuous covariates
(
32
)
.
The Laplace smoothing estimator used in this paper would not be appropriate in this context without further adaptation, but other strategies could be employed instead in line with ideas introduced by
31
.
More importantly, conditional dependence and regression settings raise non-trivial questions about how the matching set should be defined, and specifically whether it should be constructed jointly across the continuous and discrete components or restricted to the discrete space.

A second direction arises from the fact that, unlike much of the literature on generalised Bayesian inference, this paper has not focused on robustness: the proposed framework is motivated instead by computational considerations—an initiative which has recently gained traction in the generalised Bayes literature
(
55
;
56
;
1
;
45
, e.g., see)
.
The computational gains achieved by LRM-Bayes suggest a broader question of where generalised posteriors designed for tractability might offer similar advantages in other data spaces.
Settings such as simulation-based inference
(
73
;
6
;
16
)
, models on manifolds
(
53
)
, or other likelihood-free problems with complex structure may benefit from constructing posterior distributions in this spirit, provided an appropriate divergence can be specified.
Pursuing such extensions would provide a promising avenue for future work.

A.2
Evaluating the Loss Under the Empirical PMF

When
\hat{q}_{\alpha}
is taken to be the empirical PMF
\hat{q}^{\operatorname{emp}}
(
\alpha=0
), it may assign zero mass to some configurations.
In such cases, the corresponding log–ratio terms in
\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}
are undefined.
In our implementation, we omit these terms from the loss, i.e., we restrict the inner sum in
\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}
to pairs
(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}^{\prime})
for which both
\hat{q}^{\operatorname{emp}}(\bm{x}_{i})
and
\hat{q}^{\operatorname{emp}}(\bm{x}^{\prime})
are positive.
Equivalently,
\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}
is evaluated on the empirical support of the dataset.
This modification is standard when working with empirical measures and preserves the form of the loss on all configurations that are actually observed.

A.3
Selecting the Base PMF for Infinite Support

For infinite but countable
\mathcal{X}
, we use a mixture of a uniform distribution on a subdomain
\tilde{\mathcal{X}}\subset\mathcal{X}
and a PMF
r\in\mathcal{Q}^{\operatorname{adm}}_{q_{0}}(\mathcal{X})
.
Concretely, the base PMF is defined as
q^{\dagger}(\bm{x})=(1-\epsilon)\text{Unif}(\tilde{\mathcal{X}})(\bm{x})+\epsilon r(\bm{x})
, where
\epsilon>0
is taken to be small.
We select this base PMF because (i) it satisfies the requirement of
(3)
and (ii) the uniform component has proven effective for inference and performs well empirically (see
Section

4
).
In practice, the set
\tilde{\mathcal{X}}\subset\mathcal{X}
is chosen to contain all observed values.
The parameter
\alpha
regulates the influence of the base PMF
q^{\dagger}
.
In moderate-sample regimes where the empirical distribution is sufficiently representative of
q_{0}
, positivity violations are negligible for our purposes, and the empirical PMF (
\alpha=0
) is adequate and preferred for simplicity.
When data are sparse or unrepresentative, we set
\alpha>0
to ensure stability and positivity.

A.4
Estimating
\beta

The procedure we use to estimate
\beta
, taken from
80
, is outlined as follows. To simplify the notation, we refer to a dataset
\{\bm{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
as
\mathcal{D}
, and a resampled version of this dataset, that is, a bootstrap sample, as
\mathcal{D}_{b}
, where
b
indexes the resampled sample.

1.

With dataset
\{\bm{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, estimate
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}:=\arg\min_{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta}\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}(\bm{\theta})=\bm{\Lambda}_{n}^{-1}\bm{\nu}_{n}
.

2.

For
\beta>0
and fixed coverage
1-\delta
, do the following:

(a)

We generate bootstrap datasets
\mathcal{D}_{b}
for
b=1,\dots,B
, by sampling with replacement.
We then compute the posteriors
\{\hat{\pi}_{M}^{\beta}(\bm{\theta};\mathcal{D}_{b})\}_{b=1}^{B}
.
Recall that each posterior
\hat{\pi}_{M}^{\beta}(\bm{\theta};\mathcal{D}_{b})
is conjugate when the model is an exponential family; hence, we may only need to compute the means and covariances using
Proposition

3.3
.

(b)

Estimate the
1-\delta
coverage region
\mathcal{C}\left(\hat{\pi}_{M}^{\beta}(\bm{\theta};\mathcal{D}_{b}),\;1-\delta\right)
for
b=1,\dots,B
. When the posteriors
\hat{\pi}_{M}^{\beta}(\bm{\theta};\mathcal{D}_{b})
for
b=1,\dots,B
are Gaussian (from the conjugacy of
Proposition

3.3
), the procedure simplifies. In particular,

\mathcal{C}\left(\hat{\pi}_{M}^{\beta}(\bm{\theta};\mathcal{D}_{b}),1-\delta\right)=\{\bm{\theta}:(\bm{\theta}-\bm{\mu}_{n}(\beta))^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}_{n}(\beta)(\bm{\theta}-\bm{\mu}_{n}(\beta))\leq\chi^{2}_{p,1-\delta}\},

where
\chi^{2}_{p,1-\delta}
is the
1-\delta
quantile of a chi-squared distribution with
p
degrees of freedom.

(c)

Estimate the frequency
1-\hat{\delta}
at which
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{M}\in\mathcal{C}(\hat{\pi}_{M}^{\beta}(\bm{\theta};\mathcal{D}_{b}),1-\delta)
using the bootstrapped datasets.

3.

Repeat 2. for a different
\beta>0
until coverage
\hat{\delta}\approx\delta
.

The outlined procedure above defines a stochastic optimisation objective over
\beta
. That is, we may use a standard black-box optimiser to optimise over
\beta
.
In some cases, specifying a grid of
\beta
values is simpler and sufficient, as one of the
\beta
’s produces a posterior that attains the desired coverage.

B.2.1
LRM for Markov Random Fields for
Section

4.2

We first show how the stationarity and Markovian assumptions can be leveraged for LRM in Markov random fields.
Recall that
\mathcal{S}
is the set of states, and for any
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}
,
m=|M(\bm{x})|=d(|\mathcal{S}|-1)
where
d
is the number of sites, and
|\mathcal{S}|
the number of possible values at each site. The loss
\mathcal{L}^{\operatorname{LRM}}
may be simplified by the Markovian assumption as follows:

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}^{\operatorname{LRM}}(\bm{\theta})&=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim q_{0}}\!\left[\frac{1}{m}\sum_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in M(\bm{x})}\Big(\log\tfrac{p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}^{\prime})}{p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x})}\Big)^{2}-2\log\tfrac{p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}^{\prime})}{p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x})}\log\tfrac{q_{0}(\bm{x}^{\prime})}{q_{0}(\bm{x})}\right]\\[2.0pt]
&=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim q_{0}}\!\left[\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{x_{i}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\Big(\log\tfrac{p_{\bm{\theta}}(x_{i}^{\prime}\mid\text{nb}(x_{i}))}{p_{\bm{\theta}}(x_{i}\mid\text{nb}(x_{i}))}\Big)^{2}-2\log\tfrac{p_{\bm{\theta}}(x_{i}^{\prime}\mid\text{nb}(x_{i}))}{p_{\bm{\theta}}(x_{i}\mid\text{nb}(x_{i}))}\log\tfrac{q_{0}(x_{i}^{\prime}\mid\text{nb}(x_{i}))}{q_{0}(x_{i}\mid\text{nb}(x_{i}))}\right].\end{split}

Next, we use linearity of expectation to get:

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}^{\operatorname{LRM}}(\bm{\theta})&=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim q_{0}}\left[\frac{1}{m}\sum_{x_{i}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\Big(\log\tfrac{p_{\bm{\theta}}(x_{i}^{\prime}\mid\text{nb}(x_{i}))}{p_{\bm{\theta}}(x_{i}\mid\text{nb}(x_{i}))}\Big)^{2}-2\log\tfrac{p_{\bm{\theta}}(x_{i}^{\prime}\mid\text{nb}(x_{i}))}{p_{\bm{\theta}}(x_{i}\mid\text{nb}(x_{i}))}\log\tfrac{q_{0}(x_{i}^{\prime}\mid\text{nb}(x_{i}))}{q_{0}(x_{i}\mid\text{nb}(x_{i}))}\right],\end{split}

and by locality (the integrand depends only on
(x_{i},\text{nb}(x_{i}))
, we marginalise the expectation to
(x_{i},\text{nb}(x_{i}))
. Note that
x^{\prime}_{i}
also depends only on
x_{i}
. Therefore,

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}^{\operatorname{LRM}}(\bm{\theta})&=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}_{(x_{i},\text{nb}(x_{i}))}\left[\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|}\sum_{x_{i}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\Big(\log\tfrac{p_{\bm{\theta}}(x_{i}^{\prime}\mid\text{nb}(x_{i}))}{p_{\bm{\theta}}(x_{i}\mid\text{nb}(x_{i}))}\Big)^{2}-2\log\tfrac{p_{\bm{\theta}}(x_{i}^{\prime}\mid\text{nb}(x_{i}))}{p_{\bm{\theta}}(x_{i}\mid\text{nb}(x_{i}))}\log\tfrac{q_{0}(x_{i}^{\prime}\mid\text{nb}(x_{i}))}{q_{0}(x_{i}\mid\text{nb}(x_{i}))}\right].\end{split}

Finally, by stationarity (identically distributed sites, or equivalently translation invariance), we can collapse the site-average to a single-site expectation:

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}^{\operatorname{LRM}}(\bm{\theta})&=\mathbb{E}_{(x,\text{nb}(x))}\left[\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|}\sum_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\Big(\log\tfrac{p_{\bm{\theta}}(x^{\prime}\mid\text{nb}(x))}{p_{\bm{\theta}}(x\mid\text{nb}(x))}\Big)^{2}-2\log\tfrac{p_{\bm{\theta}}(x^{\prime}\mid\text{nb}(x))}{p_{\bm{\theta}}(x\mid\text{nb}(x))}\log\tfrac{q_{0}(x^{\prime}\mid\text{nb}(x))}{q_{0}(x\mid\text{nb}(x))}\right].\end{split}

Appendix C
Extension: Robustness Through a Weighted LRM-Bayes Posterior

Robustness to outliers with generalised posteriors can be achieved with a weighted loss (for example, see
2
;
21
;
55
;
45
).
We can introduce robustness similarly with a weight function
w:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow(0,\infty)
that downweights unreliable observations, extending the definition of the divergence as follows.
Suppose
q\in\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{X})
and
p\in\mathcal{Q}_{q}(\mathcal{X})
. Then, define the
weighted LRM divergence
as:

D^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{w}(q\|p):=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim q}\left[\frac{1}{|M(\bm{x})|}\sum_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in M(\bm{x})}w(\bm{x})\left(\log\frac{p(\bm{x}^{\prime})}{p(\bm{x})}-\log\frac{q(\bm{x}^{\prime})}{q(\bm{x})}\right)^{2}\right].

This remains a statistical divergence so long as
w(\bm{x})>0
wherever
q(\bm{x})>0
, and some regularity conditions are satisfied.
In particular, with the weighted log-ratio operator
\mathcal{R}^{w}_{j}[q](\bm{x}):=w(\bm{x})\log\frac{q(M_{j}(\bm{x}))}{q(\bm{x})}
, define:

\mathcal{Q}_{q}^{\text{adm}(w)}(\mathcal{X}):=\Big\{p\in\mathcal{Q}_{q}(\mathcal{X})\mid\mathcal{R}^{w}_{j}[q](\bm{x})\in L^{2}(q,\mathbb{R})\;\forall j\in\mathcal{J}\Big\}.

Then, we would require
q,p\in\mathcal{Q}_{q}^{\text{adm}(w)}(\mathcal{X})
and
3.1
to hold to obtain
D_{w}^{\operatorname{LRM}}(q\|p)=0\iff q=p
.
Further, this weighted divergence would yield a loss similar to the one from
Equation

6
, expressed as follows:

\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\text{LRM}}_{w}(\bm{\theta};\{\bm{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{w(\bm{x}_{i})}{|M(\bm{x}_{i})|}\sum_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in M(\bm{x}_{i})}\left(\log\frac{p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}^{\prime})}{p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}_{i})}\right)^{2}-2\log\frac{p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}^{\prime})}{p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}_{i})}\log\frac{\hat{q}(\bm{x}^{\prime})}{\hat{q}(\bm{x}_{i})}.

(11)

A popular choice of weights that has been used frequently in the literature is the inverse multi-quadratic function
(
55
;
74
;
5
, e.g., see)
.
However, the shape of the IMQ is symmetric, and in many cases would not align well with the distribution of count data.
We instead propose an alternative for count data problems: the product of Poisson marginals with a robust statistics parametrizing the densities.
This better reflects the semi-bounded domain of count data than the IMQ.
It is given by:

w(\mathbf{x};\bm{\mu})=\prod_{j=1}^{d}\frac{e^{-\mu_{j}}\mu_{j}^{x_{j}}}{x_{j}!}=\exp\left(-\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mu_{j}\right)\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{d}\frac{\mu_{j}^{x_{j}}}{x_{j}!}

where
\bm{\mu}:=(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{d})
, for
\mu_{j}:=\text{med}(x_{j})
, which denotes the median of
\{x_{j}^{(1)},x_{j}^{(2)},\dots,x_{j}^{(n)}\}
.
In
Figure

9
, we demonstrate the benefits of downweighting.
While the resulting CMP density estimates differ only slightly, the posterior derived from the robust empirical loss
\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{w}
is considerably more stable and reliable.

Figure 9
:
Posterior Fit with CMP Contaminated Data
. We simulate 1000 data points from a CMP model with
(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})=(4,1.25)
and introduce 5% contamination from a high mean Poisson distribution. We obtain a posterior distribution
\pi_{\hat{\mathcal{L}}}
with
\alpha=0
using
\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\text{LRM}}_{n}
(regular) and
\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{w}^{\text{LRM}}
(robust); then, we show estimates of the CMP density with posterior means in the leftmost plot, and the marginals of
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
in the two rightmost plots.

Appendix D
Theoretical Assessment

In this Section, we extend the theoretical guarantees of the LRM estimator to a broader class of models. To do so, additional regularity conditions are required.
The first set of conditions concerns the parameter space and the existence of the empirical minimiser.

Assumption D.1
.

The following regularity conditions apply:

(1)

\Theta\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p}
is open, convex and bounded;

(2)

The minimiser
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}:=\arg\min_{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta}\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}(\bm{\theta})
exists for all
n
large enough and
q_{0}
-almost surely.

A further condition is imposed on the mapping
\bm{\theta}\mapsto p_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot)
to ensure sufficient smoothness. The required strength of this condition varies depending on the specific result.

Assumption D.2
.

Assume that
\bm{\theta}\mapsto p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x})
is thrice continuously differentiable for any
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}
, and that for some
r\in\mathbb{N}
and all
\rho=0,\dots,r
, one has

\max_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in M(\bm{x})}\sup_{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta}\left\|\partial_{\bm{\theta}}^{\rho}\log\left(\frac{p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}^{\prime})}{p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x})}\right)\right\|<K_{\rho}(\bm{x}),

(12)

such that
K_{\rho}\in L^{2}(q_{0},\mathbb{R})
and where we denote
\partial_{\bm{\theta}}^{0}f_{\bm{\theta}}:=f_{\bm{\theta}}
.

These types of assumptions are standard in the literature on divergence-based estimation
(
27
;
5
;
55
;
56
)
. In particular,
D.2
can be viewed as a natural extension of Assumption 2 in
56
, where integrability of the local probability ratios is required. Here, we extend this condition to the entire matching set.
Equipped with these assumptions, we can now derive the following results:

Proposition D.3

(a.s. Pointwise Convergence)
.

Suppose
5.2
,
D.1
and
D.2
for
r=0
hold.
Then,

\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}(\bm{\theta})\overset{\text{a.s.}}{\longrightarrow}\mathcal{L}^{\operatorname{LRM}}(\bm{\theta})
, pointwise for all
\bm{\theta}\in\Theta
.

Proof.

Define
f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})=\log\frac{p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}^{\prime})}{p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x})}
,

g(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})=\log\frac{q_{0}(\bm{x}^{\prime})}{q_{0}(\bm{x})}
, and

\hat{g}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})=\log\frac{\hat{q}_{\alpha}(\bm{x}^{\prime})}{\hat{q}_{\alpha}(\bm{x})}
.
Then

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}(\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{|M(\bm{x}_{i})|}\sum_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in M(\bm{x}_{i})}\!\left[f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}^{\prime})^{2}-2f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}^{\prime})\hat{g}(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}^{\prime})\right],

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\operatorname{LRM}}(\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim q}\!\left[\frac{1}{|M(\bm{x})|}\sum_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in M(\bm{x})}\!\left[f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})^{2}-2f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})g(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})\right]\right].

Write
\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}(\bm{\theta})=A_{n}-2\hat{B}_{n}
, where
A_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{|M(\bm{x}_{i})|}\sum_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in M(\bm{x}_{i})}f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}^{\prime})^{2}
and
\hat{B}_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{|M(\bm{x}_{i})|}\sum_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in M(\bm{x}_{i})}f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}^{\prime})\hat{g}(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}^{\prime})
.

By the SLLN and
(1)
,
A_{n}\xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}}A:=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim q_{0}}\left[\frac{1}{|M(\bm{x})|}\sum_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in M(\bm{x})}f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})^{2}\right]
. For
\hat{B}_{n}
, we add and subtract
B_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{|M(\bm{x}_{i})|}\sum_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in M(\bm{x}_{i})}f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}^{\prime})g(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}^{\prime})
, to obtain
\hat{B}_{n}=B_{n}+R_{n}
with
R_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{|M(\bm{x}_{i})|}\sum_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in M(\bm{x}_{i})}f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}^{\prime})\!\big(\hat{g}(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}^{\prime})-g(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}^{\prime})\big)
. Again by the SLLN,
B_{n}\xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}}B:=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim q_{0}}\left[\frac{1}{|M(\bm{x})|}\sum_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in M(\bm{x})}f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})g(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})\right]
.

To handle the remainder term, we consider separately the two cases in
5.2
: (i) the finite case and (ii) the infinite case.

Finite Case.

If
5.2
(i) holds, let
q_{0}^{\min}>0
and set any
\varepsilon\leq q_{0}^{\min}
. By
Lemma

5.1
,
\hat{\Delta}_{\varepsilon}:=\sup_{q_{0}(\bm{x})\geq\varepsilon}\max_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in M(\bm{x})}|\hat{g}-g|\to 0
a.s., and here
\{q_{0}\geq\varepsilon\}=\mathcal{X}
, so
|R_{n}|\leq\hat{\Delta}_{\varepsilon}\,\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{|M(\bm{x}_{i})|}\sum_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in M(\bm{x}_{i})}|f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}^{\prime})|\xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}}0
, since the average converges a.s. to a finite constant by
D.2
.

Infinite Case.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
and decompose
R_{n}=R_{n}^{(1)}(\varepsilon)+R_{n}^{(2)}(\varepsilon)
, where

\displaystyle R_{n}^{(1)}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}\{q_{0}(\bm{x}_{i})\geq\varepsilon\}\frac{1}{|M(\bm{x}_{i})|}\sum_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in M(\bm{x}_{i})}f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}^{\prime})\big(\hat{g}(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}^{\prime})-g(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}^{\prime})\big),

\displaystyle R_{n}^{(2)}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}\{q_{0}(\bm{x}_{i})<\varepsilon\}\frac{1}{|M(\bm{x}_{i})|}\sum_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in M(\bm{x}_{i})}f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}^{\prime})\big(\hat{g}(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}^{\prime})-g(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}^{\prime})\big).

By
Lemma

5.1
,
\Delta_{\varepsilon}:=\sup_{q_{0}(\bm{x})\geq\varepsilon}\max_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in M(\bm{x})}|\hat{g}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})-g(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})|\xrightarrow{\mathrm{a.s.}}0.
Hence

\displaystyle|R_{n}^{(1)}(\varepsilon)|\leq\Delta_{\varepsilon}\,\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}\{q_{0}(\bm{x}_{i})\geq\varepsilon\}\frac{1}{|M(\bm{x}_{i})|}\sum_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in M(\bm{x}_{i})}|f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}^{\prime})|\xrightarrow{\mathrm{a.s.}}0,

since the average converges a.s. by the SLLN. By
5.2
(ii), for all
\bm{x}
and
\bm{x}^{\prime}\in M(\bm{x})
,
|\hat{g}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})-g(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})|\leq|\hat{g}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})|+|g(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})|\leq(K_{\hat{q}_{\alpha},n}+K_{q_{0}})\,(1+\|\bm{x}\|^{\gamma})
.
Since
\limsup_{n\to\infty}K_{\hat{q}_{\alpha},n}<\infty
, define

K_{\hat{q}_{\alpha}}:=\limsup_{n\to\infty}K_{\hat{q}_{\alpha},n}+1
.
Then, by the definition of the limsup, there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that
for all
n\geq N
,
K_{\hat{q}_{\alpha},n}\leq K_{\hat{q}_{\alpha}}
. Thus, for all
n\geq N
,
|\hat{g}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})-g(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})|\leq(K_{\hat{q}_{\alpha}}+K_{q_{0}})\,(1+\|\bm{x}\|^{\gamma})
.
Hence,

\displaystyle|R_{n}^{(2)}(\varepsilon)|\leq\frac{(K_{\hat{q}_{\alpha}}+K_{q_{0}})}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}\{q_{0}(\bm{x}_{i})<\varepsilon\}(1+\|\bm{x}_{i}\|^{\gamma})\frac{1}{|M(\bm{x}_{i})|}\sum_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in M(\bm{x}_{i})}|f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}^{\prime})|.

Define
H_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}):=(K_{\hat{q}_{\alpha}}+K_{q_{0}})(1+\|\bm{x}\|^{\gamma})\frac{1}{|M(\bm{x})|}\sum_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in M(\bm{x})}|f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})|
. Since
q_{0}
has sub-exponential tails (
5.2
(ii)), it follows that
\mathbb{E}_{X\sim q_{0}}\bigl[\|X\|^{k}\bigl]<\infty
for every integer
k\geq 1
. Therefore,
H_{\bm{\theta}}\in L^{1}(q_{0},\mathbb{R})
.
Hence, by the SLLN,
|R_{n}^{(2)}(\varepsilon)|\xrightarrow{\mathrm{a.s.}}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim q_{0}}\big[\mathbf{1}\{q_{0}(\bm{x})<\varepsilon\}H_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x})\big]
. Since
H_{\bm{\theta}}\in L^{1}(q_{0},\mathbb{R})
and
\mathbf{1}\{q_{0}(\bm{x})<\varepsilon\}\downarrow 0
as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
,
dominated convergence implies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim q_{0}}\big[\mathbf{1}\{q_{0}(\bm{x})<\varepsilon\}H_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x})\big]\xrightarrow[\varepsilon\downarrow 0]{}0.

Choosing a sequence
\varepsilon_{n}\downarrow 0
slowly enough gives

R_{n}^{(1)}(\varepsilon_{n})\to 0
and
R_{n}^{(2)}(\varepsilon_{n})\to 0
a.s., hence

R_{n}\to 0
a.s. In both cases, finite and countably infinite
\mathcal{X}
, we have
A_{n}\to A
,
B_{n}\to B
, and
R_{n}\to 0
almost surely. Therefore

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}(\bm{\theta})=A_{n}-2\widehat{B}_{n}=A_{n}-2(B_{n}+R_{n})\xrightarrow{\mathrm{a.s.}}A-2B=\mathcal{L}^{\operatorname{LRM}}(\bm{\theta}).

∎

Proposition

D.3
establishes almost sure pointwise convergence of the empirical loss under the weakest regularity conditions. Strengthening the assumptions slightly yields uniform convergence, as follows:

Proposition D.4

(a.s. Uniform Convergence)
.

Suppose
5.2
,
D.1
and
D.2
for
r=1
hold. Then,

\sup_{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta}|\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}(\bm{\theta})-\mathcal{L}^{\operatorname{LRM}}(\bm{\theta})|\overset{\text{a.s.}}{\longrightarrow}0
.

Proof.

By
Proposition

D.3
,
\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}\to\mathcal{L}^{\operatorname{LRM}}
pointwise a.s. on
\Theta
.
To upgrade to uniform convergence, it suffices to show that the family

\{\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is strongly stochastically equicontinuous

(
18
, Theorem 21.8)
.

By
18
, the family

\{\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is strongly stochastically equicontinuous
on
\Theta
if there exists a stochastic sequence
\{G_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
, independent of
\theta
, such that
|\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}(\bm{\theta})-\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}(\bm{\theta}^{\prime})|\leq G_{n}\|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}^{\prime}\|_{2}
, for all
\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}^{\prime}\in\Theta
, and
\limsup_{n\to\infty}G_{n}<\infty
almost surely.

Since
\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}
is an algebraic combination and composition of the log-likelihood ratio

\log(p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}^{\prime})/p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}))
and the estimator
\hat{q}_{\alpha}
, and
p_{\bm{\theta}}
is continuously differentiable
in
\bm{\theta}
by
D.2
, it follows that
\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}
is
continuously differentiable in
\bm{\theta}
. Moreover,
\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is convex
by
D.1.1
, so the mean value theorem

(
76
, Theorem 9.19)
yields

\displaystyle|\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}(\bm{\theta})-\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}(\bm{\theta}^{\prime})|\leq\sup_{\vartheta\in\Theta}\|\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}(\vartheta)\|_{2}\cdot\|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}^{\prime}\|_{2},\qquad\forall\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}^{\prime}\in\Theta.

Hence we can define
G_{n}:=\sup_{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta}\|\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}(\bm{\theta})\|_{2}
. It remains to show that
\limsup_{n\to\infty}G_{n}<\infty
almost surely. Recall
\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}(\bm{\theta})=A_{n}(\bm{\theta})-2\widehat{B}_{n}(\bm{\theta})
. Differentiating and using the product/chain rules,

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}A_{n}(\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{|M(\bm{x}_{i})|}\sum_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in M(\bm{x}_{i})}f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}^{\prime})\,\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}^{\prime}),

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\hat{B}_{n}(\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{|M(\bm{x}_{i})|}\sum_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in M(\bm{x}_{i})}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}^{\prime})\,\hat{g}(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}^{\prime}).

Thus

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}(\bm{\theta})=\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{|M(\bm{x}_{i})|}\sum_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in M(\bm{x}_{i})}\Big(f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}^{\prime})\,\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}^{\prime})-\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}^{\prime})\,\hat{g}(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}^{\prime})\Big).

Taking Euclidean norms and applying the triangle inequality,

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}(\bm{\theta})\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{|M(\bm{x}_{i})|}\sum_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in M(\bm{x}_{i})}\big|f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}^{\prime})\big|\;\big\|\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}^{\prime})\big\|_{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{|M(\bm{x}_{i})|}\sum_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in M(\bm{x}_{i})}\big\|\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}^{\prime})\big\|_{2}\;\big|\hat{g}(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}^{\prime})\big|.

By
D.2
, there exist measurable functions
K_{0},K_{1}\in L^{1}(q_{0},\mathbb{R})
such that

\displaystyle\sup_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in M(\bm{x})}\sup_{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta}\big|f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})\big|\leq K_{0}(\bm{x}),\qquad\sup_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in M(\bm{x})}\sup_{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta}\big\|\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})\big\|_{2}\leq K_{1}(\bm{x}).

Hence, for all
\bm{\theta}\in\Theta
,

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}(\bm{\theta})\|_{2}\leq\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}E_{1}(\bm{x}_{i})+\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{E}_{2}(\bm{x}_{i}),

where
E_{1}(\bm{x}):=K_{0}(\bm{x})K_{1}(\bm{x})
and
\hat{E}_{2}(\bm{x}):=\frac{K_{1}(\bm{x})}{|M(\bm{x})|}\sum_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in M(\bm{x})}|\hat{g}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})|
.

Similarly to
Proposition

D.3
, by
5.2
, there exists a constant
K_{\hat{q}_{\alpha}}<\infty
such that for large enough
n
, all
x
, and all
\bm{x}^{\prime}\in M(\bm{x})
,
|\hat{g}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})|\leq K_{\hat{q}_{\alpha}}(1+\|\bm{x}\|^{\gamma})
.
Thus

\displaystyle\widehat{E}_{2}(\bm{x})\leq\frac{K_{1}(\bm{x})}{|M(\bm{x})|}\sum_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in M(\bm{x})}K_{\hat{q}_{\alpha}}(1+\|\bm{x}\|^{\gamma})=K_{1}(\bm{x})K_{\hat{q}_{\alpha}}(1+\|\bm{x}\|^{\gamma}):=J_{2}(\bm{x}),

and we have
\|\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}(\bm{\theta})\|_{2}\leq\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}E_{1}(\bm{x}_{i})+\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}J_{2}(\bm{x}_{i})
,

for all
\bm{\theta}\in\Theta
. By
D.2
,

E_{1},J_{2}\in L^{1}(q_{0},\mathbb{R})
, so by the SLLN,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}E_{1}(\bm{x}_{i})\xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim q_{0}}[E_{1}(\bm{x})],\qquad\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}J_{2}(\bm{x}_{i})\xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim q_{0}}[J_{2}(\bm{x})],

both finite. Therefore

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\sup_{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta}\|\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}(\bm{\theta})\|_{2}\leq 2\big(\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim q_{0}}[E_{1}(\bm{x})]+\mathbb{E}[J_{2}(\bm{x})]\big)<\infty\qquad\text{a.s.}

That is,
\limsup_{n\to\infty}G_{n}<\infty
almost surely.

We have (i) pointwise a.s. convergence
\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}(\bm{\theta})\to\mathcal{L}^{\operatorname{LRM}}(\bm{\theta})
for all
\theta
by
Proposition

D.3
, and (ii) strong stochastic equicontinuity of
\{\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
via the bound on
\{G_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
.
Hence, by
18
,
\sup_{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta}\big|\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}(\bm{\theta})-\mathcal{L}^{\operatorname{LRM}}(\bm{\theta})\big|\xrightarrow{\textnormal{a.s.}}0
.
∎

Our next results establish the consistency and asymptotic normality of the estimator
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}
that minimises the LRM loss.
Common in the literature on M-estimators, and less restrictive than assuming convexity, we assume the existence of empirical minimisers and the uniqueness of the population minimiser as follows:

Assumption D.5
.

There exist minimisers
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}
of
\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and there is a unique
\bm{\theta}_{\star}
such that
\mathcal{L}^{\operatorname{LRM}}(\bm{\theta}_{\star})<\inf_{\{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta:\|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{\star}\|\geq\epsilon\}}\mathcal{L}^{\operatorname{LRM}}(\bm{\theta})
for any
\epsilon>0
.

The uniqueness of
\bm{\theta}_{\star}
holds automatically in the well-specified setting, i.e., when there exists
\bm{\theta}_{0}
such that
p_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}=q_{0}
, since the LRM defines a divergence whenever the matching set
M
induces a connected graph.

Proposition D.6

(Consistency)
.

Suppose
5.2
holds, and
D.1
,
D.2
for
r=1
, and
D.5
. Then,
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}\overset{\text{a.s.}}{\longrightarrow}\bm{\theta}_{\star}
.

Proof.

By
Proposition

D.3
and
D.2
, each

\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}
and
\mathcal{L}^{\operatorname{LRM}}
is continuous on
\Theta
almost surely, and

\sup_{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta}\bigl|\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}(\bm{\theta})-\mathcal{L}^{\operatorname{LRM}}(\bm{\theta})\bigr|\xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}}0.

From
D.5
,
\mathcal{L}^{\operatorname{LRM}}
has a unique and well-separated minimiser

\bm{\theta}_{*}\in\Theta
. That is, for every
\epsilon>0
,

\mathcal{L}^{\operatorname{LRM}}(\bm{\theta}_{*})<\inf_{\{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta:\|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{*}\|\geq\epsilon\}}\mathcal{L}^{\operatorname{LRM}}(\bm{\theta}).

Thus, almost surely:
(1)
\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
is open and bounded,
(2)
\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}
and
\mathcal{L}^{\operatorname{LRM}}
are continuous,
(3)
\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}\to\mathcal{L}^{\operatorname{LRM}}
uniformly on
\Theta
, and
(4)
\mathcal{L}^{\operatorname{LRM}}
has a unique, well-separated minimiser
\bm{\theta}_{*}
.
These are exactly the hypotheses of
55
), therefore any sequence of empirical minimisers
\{\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
with

\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}\in\arg\min_{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta}\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}(\bm{\theta})

for all sufficiently large
n
satisfies
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}\xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}}\bm{\theta}_{*}
, which establishes consistency.

∎

With additional regularity conditions, the asymptotic normality of the estimator can be established.

Proposition D.7

(Asymptotic normality)
.

Suppose Assumption
5.2
holds, together with Assumptions
D.1
,
D.2
for
r=3
, and
D.5
. Let
\mathbf{H}_{\star}:=\nabla^{2}_{\bm{\theta}}\,\mathcal{L}^{\operatorname{LRM}}(\bm{\theta})\big|_{\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}_{\star}}
. Then there exists a function
\varphi_{\bm{\theta}_{\star}}\in L^{2}(q_{0},\mathbb{R})
and
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim q_{0}}[\varphi_{\bm{\theta}_{\star}}(\bm{x})]=\bm{0}
such that

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\,\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}(\bm{\theta}_{\star})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varphi_{\bm{\theta}_{\star}}(\bm{x}_{i})+o_{p}(1).

Let
\mathbf{J}_{\star}:=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim q_{0}}\big[\varphi_{\bm{\theta}_{\star}}(\bm{x})\varphi_{\bm{\theta}_{\star}}(\bm{x})^{\top}\big]
. Then
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}
satisfies

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\,(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}-\bm{\theta}_{\star})\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}\!\left(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{H}_{\star}^{-1}\mathbf{J}_{\star}\mathbf{H}_{\star}^{-1}\right).

Proof.

Since
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}
minimises
\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}
, the first-order condition gives
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n})=\mathbf{0}
. By a second-order Taylor expansion of
\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}
around
\bm{\theta}_{\star}
, there exists
\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{n}
on the line segment between
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}
and
\bm{\theta}_{\star}

such that

\mathbf{0}=\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}(\bm{\theta}_{\star})+\nabla^{2}_{\bm{\theta}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}(\bm{\theta}_{\star})(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}-\bm{\theta}_{\star})+(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}-\bm{\theta}_{\star})\cdot\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}^{3}\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}(\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{n}),

where
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}^{3}\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}
is the third-order derivative tensor. Rearranging,

\sqrt{n}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}-\bm{\theta}_{\star})=-\Big(\nabla^{2}_{\bm{\theta}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}(\bm{\theta}_{\star})+(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}-\bm{\theta}_{\star})\cdot\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}^{3}\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}(\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{n})\Big)^{-1}\sqrt{n}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}(\bm{\theta}_{\star}).

(13)

To obtain the CLT it suffices to show:

(i)

\nabla^{2}_{\bm{\theta}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}(\bm{\theta}_{\star})+(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}-\bm{\theta}_{\star})\cdot\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}^{3}\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}(\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{n})\xrightarrow{p}\mathbf{H}_{\star}
;

(ii)

\sqrt{n}\,\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}(\bm{\theta}_{\star})\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{J}_{\star})
,
for some finite covariance matrix
\mathbf{J}_{\star}
.

Since
\mathbf{H}_{\star}
is symmetric positive definite by
D.5
, invertibility of the matrix in parentheses is ensured for large
n
, and Slutsky’s theorem applied to (
13
) then yields the desired CLT for
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}
.

Write
\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}(\bm{\theta})=A_{n}(\bm{\theta})-2B_{n}(\bm{\theta})-2R_{n}(\bm{\theta})
and
\mathcal{L}^{\operatorname{LRM}}(\bm{\theta})=A(\bm{\theta})-2B(\bm{\theta})
. Differentiating twice,
\nabla^{2}_{\bm{\theta}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}(\bm{\theta})=\nabla^{2}_{\bm{\theta}}A_{n}(\bm{\theta})-2\nabla^{2}_{\bm{\theta}}B_{n}(\bm{\theta})-2\nabla^{2}_{\bm{\theta}}R_{n}(\bm{\theta})
, and analogously
\nabla^{2}_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{L}^{\operatorname{LRM}}(\bm{\theta})=\nabla^{2}_{\bm{\theta}}A(\bm{\theta})-2\nabla^{2}_{\bm{\theta}}B(\bm{\theta})
.

By Assumption
D.2
and
D.1
, the envelopes for
f_{\bm{\theta}},\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}f_{\bm{\theta}},\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}^{2}f_{\bm{\theta}}
are integrable,
so the SLLN yields
\nabla^{2}_{\bm{\theta}}A_{n}(\bm{\theta})\xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}}\nabla^{2}_{\bm{\theta}}A(\bm{\theta})
and
\nabla^{2}_{\bm{\theta}}B_{n}(\bm{\theta})\xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}}\nabla^{2}_{\bm{\theta}}B(\bm{\theta})
, in particular at
\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}_{\star}
.

It remains to show that
\nabla^{2}_{\bm{\theta}}R_{n}(\bm{\theta}_{\star})\to 0
almost surely. By Assumption
D.2
,

\|\nabla^{2}_{\bm{\theta}}f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})\|\leq K_{2}(\bm{x})
with
K_{2}\in L^{2}(q_{0},\mathbb{R})
, giving

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{2}_{\bm{\theta}}R_{n}(\bm{\theta})\|\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}K_{2}(\bm{x}_{i})\frac{1}{|M(\bm{x}_{i})|}\sum_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in M(\bm{x}_{i})}\big|\hat{g}(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}^{\prime})-g(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}^{\prime})\big|.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
and split according to
q_{0}(\bm{x}_{i})\geq\varepsilon
or
q_{0}(\bm{x}_{i})<\varepsilon
:
\|\nabla^{2}_{\bm{\theta}}R_{n}(\bm{\theta})\|\leq T_{n,1}(\varepsilon)+T_{n,2}(\varepsilon)
, with

\displaystyle T_{n,1}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}K_{2}(\bm{x}_{i})\mathbf{1}\{q_{0}(\bm{x}_{i})\geq\varepsilon\}\frac{1}{|M(\bm{x}_{i})|}\sum_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in M(\bm{x}_{i})}\big|\hat{g}(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}^{\prime})-g(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}^{\prime})\big|,

\displaystyle T_{n,2}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}K_{2}(\bm{x}_{i})\mathbf{1}\{q_{0}(\bm{x}_{i})<\varepsilon\}\frac{1}{|M(\bm{x}_{i})|}\sum_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in M(\bm{x}_{i})}\big|\hat{g}(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}^{\prime})-g(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}^{\prime})\big|.

On
\{q_{0}\geq\varepsilon\}
we have the truncated error
\frac{1}{|M(\bm{x})|}\sum_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in M(\bm{x})}\big|\hat{g}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})-g(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})\big|\leq\Delta_{\varepsilon}^{(n)}
, where
\Delta_{\varepsilon}^{(n)}:=\sup_{\{\bm{x}:\,q_{0}(\bm{x})\geq\varepsilon\}}\max_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in M(\bm{x})}\big|\hat{g}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})-g(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})\big|\xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}}0
, by
Lemma

5.1
. Hence

\displaystyle T_{n,1}(\varepsilon)\leq\Delta_{\varepsilon}^{(n)}\,\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}K_{2}(\bm{x}_{i})\mathbf{1}\{q_{0}(\bm{x}_{i})\geq\varepsilon\}\xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}}0.

For the tail term, we use the same technique used in
proposition

D.3
to show that
T_{n,2}(\varepsilon)\to 0
as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
. A diagonal argument then yields
\nabla^{2}_{\bm{\theta}}R_{n}(\bm{\theta}_{\star})\to 0
a.s., and thus
\nabla^{2}_{\bm{\theta}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}(\bm{\theta}_{\star})\xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}}\mathbf{H}_{\star}
.

Finally, Assumption
D.2
with
r=3
provides an integrable envelope
K_{3}
for
\nabla^{3}_{\bm{\theta}}f_{\bm{\theta}}
, so the SLLN yields
\nabla^{3}_{\bm{\theta}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}(\bm{\theta})=O_{p}(1)
uniformly in a neighbourhood of
\bm{\theta}_{\star}
. Consistency of
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}
implies
\|\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}-\bm{\theta}_{\star}\|=o_{p}(1)
, hence
(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}-\bm{\theta}_{\star})\cdot\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}^{3}\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}(\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{n})=o_{p}(1).

Together, this shows
\nabla^{2}_{\bm{\theta}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}(\bm{\theta}_{\star})+(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}-\bm{\theta}_{\star})\cdot\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}^{3}\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}(\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{n})\xrightarrow{p}\mathbf{H}_{\star}
, establishing (i).

Now for (ii) we define for any pmf
p\in\mathcal{Q}^{\operatorname{adm}}_{q_{0}}(\mathcal{X})
,

\displaystyle\ell_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x};p):=\frac{1}{|M(\bm{x})|}\sum_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in M(\bm{x})}\Big(f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})^{2}-2f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})\log\frac{p(\bm{x}^{\prime})}{p(\bm{x})}\Big).

If we freeze the pmf inside the loss at
p=q_{0}
, define
\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\mathrm{LRM}}_{n,\mathrm{dgp}}(\bm{\theta}):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}_{i};q_{0})
,
so that

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\mathrm{LRM}}_{n,\mathrm{dgp}}(\bm{\theta}_{\star})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varphi^{\mathrm{dgp}}_{\bm{\theta}_{\star}}(\bm{x}_{i}).

Since
\bm{\theta}_{\star}
minimises
\mathcal{L}^{\operatorname{LRM}}
, we have
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim q_{0}}[\varphi^{\mathrm{dgp}}_{\bm{\theta}_{\star}}(\bm{x})]=\bm{0}
, and
D.1
and
D.2
imply
\varphi^{\mathrm{dgp}}_{\bm{\theta}_{\star}}\in L^{2}(q_{0},\mathbb{R})
.
Hence the multivariate CLT gives

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varphi^{\mathrm{dgp}}_{\bm{\theta}_{\star}}(\bm{x}_{i})\xrightarrow{d}\ \mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\mathbf{J}_{\star}^{\mathrm{dgp}}),\quad\mathbf{J}_{\star}^{\mathrm{dgp}}:=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim q_{0}}[\varphi^{\mathrm{dgp}}_{\bm{\theta}_{\star}}(\bm{x})\varphi^{\mathrm{dgp}}_{\bm{\theta}_{\star}}(\bm{x})^{\top}].

It remains to incorporate the effect of replacing
g
by
\hat{g}
in the loss. Recall that
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}(\bm{\theta}_{\star})=\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\mathrm{LRM}}_{n,\mathrm{dgp}}(\bm{\theta}_{\star})-2\,\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}R_{n}(\bm{\theta}_{\star})
. Thus

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\,\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}(\bm{\theta}_{\star})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varphi^{\mathrm{dgp}}_{\bm{\theta}_{\star}}(\bm{x}_{i})-2\sqrt{n}\,\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}R_{n}(\bm{\theta}_{\star}).

We now show that the second term on the right-hand side admits a linear representation plus an
o_{p}(1)
remainder.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
and define
S_{\varepsilon}:=\{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}:q_{0}(\bm{x})\geq\varepsilon\}
, which is finite. Define the truncated empirical loss

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\varepsilon}(\bm{\theta}):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}\{\bm{x}_{i}\in S_{\varepsilon}\}\frac{1}{|M(\bm{x}_{i})|}\sum_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in M(\bm{x}_{i})\cap S_{\varepsilon}}\Big(f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}^{\prime})^{2}-2f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}^{\prime})\hat{g}(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}^{\prime})\Big),

and its gradient
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\varepsilon}(\bm{\theta})
. On
S_{\varepsilon}
,
q_{0}(\bm{x})\geq\varepsilon
and
\hat{q}_{\alpha}(\bm{x})\to q_{0}(\bm{x})
a.s.; hence for
n
large we have
\hat{q}_{\alpha}(\bm{x})\geq\varepsilon/2
for all
\bm{x}\in S_{\varepsilon}
, almost surely. In particular, on
S_{\varepsilon}
the map
\hat{q}_{\alpha,\varepsilon}:=\big(\hat{q}_{\alpha}(\bm{x})\big)_{\bm{x}\in S_{\varepsilon}}\mapsto\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\varepsilon}(\bm{\theta}_{\star})
is a smooth function of the finite vector
\hat{q}_{\alpha,\varepsilon}
. Let
q_{\varepsilon}:=(q_{0}(\bm{x}))_{\bm{x}\in S_{\varepsilon}}
be the truncated true mass function and let

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}(\bm{\theta};p_{\varepsilon}):=\sum_{\bm{x}\in S_{\varepsilon}}p(\bm{x})\,\frac{1}{|M(\bm{x})|}\sum_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in M(\bm{x})\cap S_{\varepsilon}}\Big(f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})^{2}-2f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})\log\frac{p(\bm{x}^{\prime})}{p(\bm{x})}\Big)

denote the corresponding truncated population loss. By the assumptions on
f_{\bm{\theta}}
and the truncation, the map
p_{\varepsilon}\mapsto\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}(\bm{\theta}_{\star};p_{\varepsilon})
is continuously differentiable in a neighbourhood of
q_{\varepsilon}
, with bounded Jacobian.

For each
\bm{x}\in S_{\varepsilon}
we have
\sqrt{n}\big(\hat{q}_{\alpha}(\bm{x})-q_{0}(\bm{x})\big)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(\mathbf{1}\{\bm{x}_{i}=\bm{x}\}-q_{0}(\bm{x})\big)+o_{p}(1)
, where the
o_{p}(1)
term comes from the deterministic correction of order
O(n^{-1/2})
. By the multivariate delta method applied at
q_{\varepsilon}
, there exists a vector of coefficients
(c_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{y}))_{\mathbf{y}\in S_{\varepsilon}}
such that

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\,\Big(\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\varepsilon}(\bm{\theta}_{\star})-\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}(\bm{\theta}_{\star};q_{\varepsilon})\Big)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in S_{\varepsilon}}c_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{y})\big(\mathbf{1}\{\bm{x}_{i}=\mathbf{y}\}-q_{0}(\mathbf{y})\big)+o_{p}(1).

But
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}(\bm{\theta}_{\star};q_{\varepsilon})=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim q_{0}}\big[\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\ell_{\bm{\theta}_{\star}}(\bm{x};q_{0})\,\mathbf{1}\{\bm{x}\in S_{\varepsilon}\}\big]
, and by definition,

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\mathrm{LRM}}_{n,\mathrm{dgp}}(\bm{\theta}_{\star})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varphi^{\mathrm{dgp}}_{\bm{\theta}_{\star}}(\bm{x}_{i}).

Hence, for each fixed
\varepsilon>0
,
\sqrt{n}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\varepsilon}(\bm{\theta}_{\star})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varphi_{\bm{\theta}_{\star},\varepsilon}(\bm{x}_{i})+o_{p}(1)
, where

\displaystyle\varphi_{\bm{\theta}_{\star},\varepsilon}(\bm{x}):=\varphi^{\mathrm{dgp}}_{\bm{\theta}_{\star}}(\bm{x})\,\mathbf{1}\{\bm{x}\in S_{\varepsilon}\}+\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in S_{\varepsilon}}c_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{y})\big(\mathbf{1}\{\bm{x}=\mathbf{y}\}-q_{0}(\mathbf{y})\big).

Since
S_{\varepsilon}
is finite and the coefficients
c_{\varepsilon}(y)
are bounded, and since
\varphi^{\mathrm{dgp}}_{\bm{\theta}_{\star}}\in L^{2}(q_{0},\mathbb{R})
, there exists
H_{1}\in L^{2}(q_{0},\mathbb{R})
and a constant
C_{\varepsilon}
such that
\|\varphi_{\bm{\theta}_{\star},\varepsilon}(\bm{x})\|\leq C_{\varepsilon}H_{1}(\bm{x})
for all
\bm{x}
. In particular,
\varphi_{\bm{\theta}_{\star},\varepsilon}\in L^{2}(q_{0},\mathbb{R})
and the multivariate CLT yields

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varphi_{\bm{\theta}_{\star},\varepsilon}(\bm{x}_{i})\xrightarrow{d}\ \mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\mathbf{J}_{\star}(\varepsilon)),\qquad\mathbf{J}_{\star}(\varepsilon):=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim q_{0}}\big[\varphi_{\bm{\theta}_{\star},\varepsilon}(\bm{x})\varphi_{\bm{\theta}_{\star},\varepsilon}(\bm{x})^{\top}\big].

Now, to control the tail, we define
\Delta_{n}(\varepsilon):=\sqrt{n}\Big(\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}(\bm{\theta}_{\star})-\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\varepsilon}(\bm{\theta}_{\star})\Big)
. Only indices with
\bm{x}_{i}\notin S_{\varepsilon}
, i.e.
q_{0}(\bm{x}_{i})<\varepsilon
, contribute to
\Delta_{n}(\varepsilon)
. Using the expressions for the gradients of
\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}
and
\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\varepsilon}
, the polynomial growth of
f_{\bm{\theta}_{\star}}
and
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}f_{\bm{\theta}_{\star}}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\|\Delta_{n}(\varepsilon)\|\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}H_{2}(\bm{x}_{i})\,\mathbf{1}\{q_{0}(\bm{x}_{i})<\varepsilon\}

for some
H_{2}\in L^{2}(q_{0},\mathbb{R})
.
Consequently
\mathbb{E}\big[\|\Delta_{n}(\varepsilon)\|^{2}\big]\leq\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim q_{0}}\big[H_{2}(\bm{x})^{2}\mathbf{1}\{q_{0}(\bm{x})<\varepsilon\}\big]\xrightarrow[\varepsilon\downarrow 0]{}0
by dominated convergence. Hence, for every
\delta>0
,

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\sup_{n}\mathbb{P}\big(\|\Delta_{n}(\varepsilon)\|>\delta\big)=0.

Thus the tail contribution to the score can be made arbitrarily small in probability, uniformly in
n
, by choosing
\varepsilon
sufficiently small.

For
0<\varepsilon^{\prime}\leq\varepsilon
, observe that
\varphi_{\bm{\theta}_{\star},\varepsilon}(\bm{x})=\varphi_{\bm{\theta}_{\star},\varepsilon^{\prime}}(\bm{x})
whenever
q_{0}(\bm{x})\geq\varepsilon
; both are bounded by a constant multiple of
H_{2}(\bm{x})
.
Therefore

\displaystyle\|\varphi_{\bm{\theta}_{\star},\varepsilon}(\bm{x})-\varphi_{\bm{\theta}_{\star},\varepsilon^{\prime}}(\bm{x})\|\leq C\,H_{2}(\bm{x})\,\mathbf{1}\{q_{0}(\bm{x})<\varepsilon\},

and hence
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim q_{0}}\bigl[\|\varphi_{\bm{\theta}_{\star},\varepsilon}(\bm{x})-\varphi_{\bm{\theta}_{\star},\varepsilon^{\prime}}(\bm{x})\|^{2}\bigr]\leq C^{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim q_{0}}\big[H_{2}(\bm{x})^{2}\mathbf{1}\{q_{0}(\bm{x})<\varepsilon\}\big]\xrightarrow[\varepsilon\downarrow 0]{}0
. Thus
\{\varphi_{\bm{\theta}_{\star},\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon>0}
is Cauchy in
L^{2}(q_{0},\mathbb{R})
, and there exists
\varphi_{\bm{\theta}_{\star}}\in L^{2}(q_{0},\mathbb{R})
such that

\displaystyle\varphi_{\bm{\theta}_{\star},\varepsilon}\xrightarrow[\varepsilon\downarrow 0]{L^{2}(q_{0},\mathbb{R})}\varphi_{\bm{\theta}_{\star}}.

In particular,
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim q_{0}}[\varphi_{\bm{\theta}_{\star}}(\bm{x})]=\bm{0}
by continuity of expectation on
L^{2}
. Choose a sequence
\varepsilon_{n}\downarrow 0
slowly enough so that
\Delta_{n}(\varepsilon_{n})\to 0
in probability. Then

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\,\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}(\bm{\theta}_{\star})=\sqrt{n}\,\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\varepsilon_{n}}(\bm{\theta}_{\star})+\Delta_{n}(\varepsilon_{n})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varphi_{\bm{\theta}_{\star},\varepsilon_{n}}(\bm{x}_{i})+o_{p}(1).

Since
\varphi_{\bm{\theta}_{\star},\varepsilon_{n}}\to\varphi_{\bm{\theta}_{\star}}
in
L^{2}(q_{0},\mathbb{R})
and
\{\bm{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are i.i.d., the CLT and Slutsky’s theorem imply

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(\varphi_{\bm{\theta}_{\star},\varepsilon_{n}}(\bm{x}_{i})-\varphi_{\bm{\theta}_{\star}}(\bm{x}_{i})\big)\xrightarrow{p}0,

and
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varphi_{\bm{\theta}_{\star}}(\bm{x}_{i})\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\mathbf{J}_{\star})
, and
\mathbf{J}_{\star}:=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim q_{0}}[\varphi_{\bm{\theta}_{\star}}(\bm{x})\varphi_{\bm{\theta}_{\star}}(\bm{x})^{\top}]
. Combining these, we obtain
\sqrt{n}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}(\bm{\theta}_{\star})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varphi_{\bm{\theta}_{\star}}(\bm{x}_{i})+o_{p}(1)
, which proves (ii) with the stated
\mathbf{J}_{\star}
.

Part (i) showed that
\nabla^{2}_{\bm{\theta}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}(\bm{\theta}_{\star})+(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}-\bm{\theta}_{\star})\cdot\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}^{3}\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}(\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{n})\xrightarrow{p}\mathbf{H}_{\star}
, and part (ii) established
\sqrt{n}\,\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}(\bm{\theta}_{\star})\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{J}_{\star})
. Applying Slutsky’s theorem to the representation (
13
), we conclude that

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\,(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}-\bm{\theta}_{\star})\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{H}_{\star}^{-1}\mathbf{J}_{\star}\mathbf{H}_{\star}^{-1}\right),

which completes the proof.
∎

Given the technical results established for the LRM estimator, we can now derive a posterior consistency and a Bernstein–von Mises theorem for the corresponding LRM generalised posterior. To do so, an additional assumption on the prior is required. This assumption—commonly referred to as the prior mass condition—requires that the prior assigns positive probability mass to the loss minimiser.

Theorem D.8

(Consistency & Bernstein–von Mises)
.

Suppose
5.2
,
D.1
,
D.2
for

Let
\tilde{\pi}_{M}
the p.d.f. of the random variable
\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{n}:=\sqrt{n}(\bm{\theta}-\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n})
for
\bm{\theta}\sim\hat{\pi}_{M}^{\beta}
, viewed as a p.d.f. on
\mathbb{R}^{p}

Proof.

We verify, almost surely, the conditions of

.

1. Prior Mass.

By assumption, the prior
\pi
admits a density continuous at
\bm{\theta}_{\star}
with

. This matches the prior conditions in
61
.

2. Consistency of the minimiser.

Let
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}\in\arg\min_{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta}\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}(\bm{\theta})
denote the empirical minimiser. By

D.6
, we have
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}\xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}}\bm{\theta}_{\star}

3. Local quadratic expansion of

ensures that

is three times continuously differentiable in
\bm{\theta}
on
\Theta
. For
\bm{\theta}
in a neighbourhood of
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}

around

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}(\bm{\theta})=\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n})+\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n})^{\top}(\bm{\theta}-\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n})+\tfrac{1}{2}(\bm{\theta}-\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n})^{\top}\mathbf{H}_{n}(\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{n})\,(\bm{\theta}-\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}),

where
\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{n}
lies on the line segment between
\bm{\theta}
and
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}

a.s., so the remainder is

61

By

and

In

is positive semidefinite. Under the assumption that

5. Separation of minimisers.

By the uniform convergence of

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\Big(\inf_{\{\bm{\theta}:\|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{\star}\|\geq\varepsilon\}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}(\bm{\theta})-\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n})\Big)

1–5 verify, almost surely, the hypotheses of the consistency and Bernstein–von Mises theorem for loss sequences in

E.1
Theorem

Proof.

Suppose
q\in\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{X})
such that
q\in\mathcal{Q}^{\operatorname{adm}}_{q}(\mathcal{X})
and
p\in\mathcal{Q}^{\operatorname{adm}}_{q}(\mathcal{X})
. We first show that if

, then clearly

and so every term in the definition of

.

Now, for the converse, assume

. Since every term in the expectation is non-negative, this implies:

Therefore,
\frac{q(\bm{x})}{q(\bm{x}^{\prime})}=\frac{p(\bm{x})}{p(\bm{x}^{\prime})}

(14)

\bm{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}

3.1
holds; that is,

to emphasise the dependency of the loss on
\bm{\theta}
. Now, if we assume an exponentially quadratic prior
\pi(\bm{\eta})\propto\exp(-\frac{1}{2}(\bm{\eta}-\bm{\mu})^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}(\bm{\eta}-\bm{\mu}))
, the generalised log-ratio matching posterior is obtained as:

\begin{split}\log\pi_{M}&\propto-\frac{1}{2}(\bm{\eta}-\bm{\mu})^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}(\bm{\eta}-\bm{\mu})-\beta n\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}(\bm{\theta})\\
&\propto-\frac{1}{2}(\bm{\eta}^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{\eta}-2\bm{\eta}^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{\mu})-\beta n\left(\bm{\eta}^{\top}\bm{\Lambda}_{n}\bm{\eta}-2\bm{\eta}^{\top}\bm{\nu}_{n}\right)\\
&=-\frac{1}{2}\left(\bm{\eta}^{\top}(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}+2\beta n\bm{\Lambda}_{n})\bm{\eta}-2\bm{\eta}^{\top}(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{\mu}+2\beta n\bm{\nu}_{n})\right),\end{split}

where on the first line, we write the definition of the (log) generalised posterior on
\bm{\eta}
; on the second line we expand the prior, replace the loss by its quadratic form, and absorb into
\propto
constants not depending on
\bm{\eta}
; and on the last line, we group terms. This last line implies
\pi_{M}
is

\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu}_{n},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n})
with
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n}:=\left(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}+2\beta n\bm{\Lambda}_{n}\right)^{-1}
and
\bm{\mu}_{n}:=\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n}\left(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{\mu}+2\beta n\bm{\nu}_{n}\right)
.
∎

E.3
Lemma

5.1

Proof.

For any
\varepsilon>0
, the set

S_{\varepsilon}:=\{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}:\ q_{0}(\bm{x})\geq\varepsilon\}
is finite.
Indeed, since
q_{0}
is a probability mass function,

\displaystyle 1=\sum_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}}q_{0}(\bm{x})\geq\sum_{\bm{x}\in S_{\varepsilon}}q_{0}(\bm{x})\geq\sum_{\bm{x}\in S_{\varepsilon}}\varepsilon=\varepsilon|S_{\varepsilon}|.

Thus
|S_{\varepsilon}|\leq 1/\varepsilon<\infty
. Fix any
\bm{x}\in S_{\varepsilon}
.
By definition,
\hat{q}_{\alpha}(\bm{x})=(C_{n}(\bm{x})+\alpha\tilde{q}^{\dagger}(\bm{x}))/(n+\alpha Z^{\dagger})
and
Z^{\dagger}=\sum_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\tilde{q}^{\dagger}(\bm{x})
.
Since
C_{n}(\bm{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}\{\bm{x}_{i}=\bm{x}\}
,
the strong law of large numbers gives
C_{n}(\bm{x})/n\ \longrightarrow\ q_{0}(\bm{x})
a.s. Dividing numerator and denominator of
\hat{q}_{\alpha}(\bm{x})
by
n
yields

\displaystyle\hat{q}_{\alpha}(\bm{x})=\frac{C_{n}(\bm{x})/n+\alpha\,\tilde{q}^{\dagger}(\bm{x})/n}{1+\alpha Z^{\dagger}/n},

and since
\tilde{q}^{\dagger}(\bm{x})/n\to 0
and
\alpha Z^{\dagger}/n\to 0
, we obtain
\hat{q}_{\alpha}(\bm{x})\rightarrow q_{0}(\bm{x})
a.s. for each
\bm{x}\in S_{\varepsilon}
.

For
\bm{x}\in S_{\varepsilon}
we have
q_{0}(\bm{x})\geq\varepsilon
, hence
q_{0}(\bm{x})\in[\varepsilon,1]
.
Since
\hat{q}_{\alpha}(\bm{x})\to q_{0}(\bm{x})
a.s., there exists a
N_{\bm{x}}

such that for all
n\geq N_{\bm{x}}
,
\hat{q}_{\alpha}(\bm{x})\in[\varepsilon/2,1]
.

Because
S_{\varepsilon}
is finite, we can take
N:=\max_{\bm{x}\in S_{\varepsilon}}N_{\bm{x}}
, which is finite a.s. Thus for all
n\geq N
,
\hat{q}_{\alpha}(\bm{x})\in[\varepsilon/2,1]
,
\forall\bm{x}\in S_{\varepsilon}
. The function
u\mapsto\log u
is continuously differentiable on
[\varepsilon/2,1]

and satisfies
|\log u-\log v|\leq\frac{2}{\varepsilon}|u-v|
for all
u,v\in[\varepsilon/2,1]
. Therefore, for all
\bm{x}\in S_{\varepsilon}
,
\bigl|\log\hat{q}_{\alpha}(\bm{x})-\log q_{0}(\bm{x})\bigr|\leq\frac{2}{\varepsilon}\,|\hat{q}_{\alpha}(\bm{x})-q_{0}(\bm{x})|
, and taking
\max_{\bm{x}\in S_{\varepsilon}}
gives
\max_{\bm{x}\in S_{\varepsilon}}\bigl|\log\hat{q}_{\alpha}(\bm{x})-\log q_{0}(\bm{x})\bigr|\xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}}0
. Fix
\bm{x}\in S_{\varepsilon}
and any
\bm{x}^{\prime}\in M(\bm{x})
. We have

\displaystyle\left|\log\frac{\hat{q}_{\alpha}(\bm{x}^{\prime})}{\hat{q}_{\alpha}(\bm{x})}-\log\frac{q_{0}(\bm{x}^{\prime})}{q_{0}(\bm{x})}\right|\leq\bigl|\log\hat{q}_{\alpha}(\bm{x}^{\prime})-\log q_{0}(\bm{x}^{\prime})\bigr|+\bigl|\log\hat{q}_{\alpha}(\bm{x})-\log q_{0}(\bm{x})\bigr|.

Now take the maximum over
\bm{x}^{\prime}\in M(\bm{x})
, then the supremum over
\bm{x}\in S_{\varepsilon}
.
Since
S_{\varepsilon}
is finite and
M(\bm{x})
is finite for each
\bm{x}
, all maxima are finite, and the right-hand side converges to
0
a.s. Hence

\displaystyle\sup_{\bm{x}\in S_{\varepsilon}}\max_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in M(\bm{x})}\left|\log\frac{\hat{q}_{\alpha}(\bm{x}^{\prime})}{\hat{q}_{\alpha}(\bm{x})}-\log\frac{q_{0}(\bm{x}^{\prime})}{q_{0}(\bm{x})}\right|\xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}}\;0.

This proves the desired truncated convergence.
∎

E.4
Theorem

5.3

Before proving
Theorem

5.3
, we establish the following auxiliary result.

Proposition E.1
.

Suppose
5.2
and
p_{\bm{\theta}}
is an exponential family as in
Equation

2
with natural parameter
\eta(\bm{\theta})=\bm{\theta}
. Let
E\subseteq\Theta
be open and bounded. Then
\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}(\bm{\theta})\overset{\text{a.s.}}{\longrightarrow}\mathcal{L}^{\operatorname{LRM}}(\bm{\theta})
pointwise for all
\bm{\theta}\in E
.

Proof.

We first verify that natural exponential families satisfy
D.2
with
r=0
.

The model is an exponential family of the form
p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}):=\exp\left(\bm{\theta}^{\top}\mathbf{T}(\bm{x})+B(\bm{x})-\log Z(\bm{\theta})\right)
. For any
\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
and
\bm{\theta}\in\Theta
we have

\displaystyle\log\frac{p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}^{\prime})}{p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x})}=\bigl(\log B(\bm{x}^{\prime})-\log B(\bm{x})\bigr)+\bm{\theta}^{\top}\bigl(\mathbf{T}(\bm{x}^{\prime})-\mathbf{T}(\bm{x})\bigr),

since the terms
Z(\bm{\theta})
cancel in the ratio. Fix an open, bounded set
E\subset\Theta
. Because
E
is bounded, there exists a finite constant
C_{E}:=\sup_{\bm{\theta}\in E}\|\bm{\theta}\|<\infty
. Then, for any
\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
and
\bm{\theta}\in E
,

\displaystyle\left|\log\frac{p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}^{\prime})}{p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x})}\right|\leq\bigl|\log B(\bm{x}^{\prime})-\log B(\bm{x})\bigr|+C_{E}\,\|\mathbf{T}(\bm{x}^{\prime})-\mathbf{T}(\bm{x})\|.

Define
K_{0}(\bm{x}):=\max_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in M(\bm{x})}\left(\bigl|\log B(\bm{x}^{\prime})-\log B(\bm{x})\bigr|+C_{E}\,\|\mathbf{T}(\bm{x}^{\prime})-\mathbf{T}(\bm{x})\|\right)
. Then, for all
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}
,
\bm{x}^{\prime}\in M(\bm{x})
and
\bm{\theta}\in E
,
\sup_{\bm{\theta}\in E}\left|\log\frac{p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}^{\prime})}{p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x})}\right|\leq K_{0}(\bm{x})
, which is
D.2
with
r=0
. By
(2)
, the
B
and
T
satisfy conditions ensuring that
K_{0}\in L^{2}(q_{0},\mathbb{R})
. Hence
D.2
holds with
r=0
for natural exponential families on
E
. Therefore, all conditions of
Proposition

D.3
are satisfied on
E
, and we obtain
\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}(\bm{\theta})\xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}}\mathcal{L}^{\operatorname{LRM}}(\bm{\theta})

for all
\bm{\theta}\in E
.
∎

We now prove
Theorem

5.3
by verifying the conditions of
61
a.s.

Proof.

1. Prior mass.

By assumption, the prior
\pi
admits a continuous density on
\bm{\theta}
and
\pi(\bm{\theta}_{\star})>0
. This verifies the required prior mass condition.

2. Pointwise convergence of the empirical loss.

Let
E\subseteq\Theta
be an open and bounded set such that
\bm{\theta}_{\star}\in E
. By
Proposition

E.1
, we have
\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}(\bm{\theta})\xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}}\mathcal{L}^{\operatorname{LRM}}(\bm{\theta})
for all
\bm{\theta}\in E
. Thus, the empirical loss converges pointwise a.s. to its population counterpart in
E
.

3. Regularity of the empirical loss
\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}
.

By
proposition

3.3
, the
\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\operatorname{LRM}}_{n}(\bm{\theta})
is quadratic in
\bm{\theta}
. In particular, it is convex and possesses uniformly bounded third derivatives, in fact, these derivatives are zero. This matches the smoothness requirement of
61
.

4. Regularity of the population loss
\mathcal{L}^{\operatorname{LRM}}
.

Since
\bm{\theta}_{\star}=\arg\min_{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta}\mathcal{L}^{\operatorname{LRM}}(\bm{\theta})
, we have first-order optimality:
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{L}^{\operatorname{LRM}}(\bm{\theta}_{\star})=\mathbf{0}
.
Furthermore, the Hessian at the minimiser,
\mathbf{H}_{\star}:=\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}^{2}\mathcal{L}^{\operatorname{LRM}}(\bm{\theta}_{\star})
, is positive definite.

(1)–(4) verify, almost surely, all hypotheses of the consistency and Bernstein–von Mises result for posteriors based on loss sequences
(
61
, Theorem 5)
.
Consequently, the posterior
\hat{\pi}_{M}^{\beta}
concentrates at
\bm{\theta}_{\star}
:
\int_{B_{\epsilon}(\bm{\theta}_{\star})}\hat{\pi}_{M}^{\beta}(\bm{\theta})\,d\bm{\theta}\xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}}1
for all
\epsilon>0
.

Moreover, the rescaled posterior density
\tilde{\pi}_{M}
converges to a Gaussian distribution with covariance
\mathbf{H}_{\star}^{-1}
:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{p}}\left|\tilde{\pi}_{M}(\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{n})-\frac{1}{\det(2\pi\mathbf{H}_{\star}^{-1})^{1/2}}\exp\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\bm{\theta}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_{\star}\bm{\theta}\right)\right|d\bm{\theta}\xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}}0.

This completes the proof of
Theorem

5.3
.
∎
