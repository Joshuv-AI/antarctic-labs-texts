Inhibition of the AMPK/PGC-1α/Mfn2 Axis is Associated with Mitochondria-ER Dysfunction and Ultrastructural Remodeling in a Rat Model of Myofascial Trigger Points
Authors: Shuangcheng Yu, Wei Huang, Haiqing Su, Fangyan Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMyofascial trigger points (MTrPs) represent the primary pathological basis of myofascial pain syndrome (MPS). However, the exact cellular and molecular mechanisms underlying MTrPs formation and maintenance remain largely unknown, especially regarding mitochondrial function, endoplasmic reticulum (ER) homeostasis, and energy metabolism regulation.MethodsA rat model of MTrPs was established by blunt striking combined with eccentric exercise. ATPase histochemistry, immunofluorescence staining, transmission electron microscopy (TEM), Western blotting, and quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) were used to investigate muscle fiber type conversion, ultrastructural changes, and the expression levels of key molecules involved in mitochondrial energy metabolism.ResultsMuscle fibers in the MTrPs model exhibited a phenotypic switch from type I to type II fibers. Ultrastructurally, the MTrPs region showed abnormalities, including swollen and vacuolar mitochondria with matrix rarefaction, dilated ER cisternae, and significantly increased mitochondria-ER contacts (MERCs). The AMPK/PGC-1α/Mfn2 signaling axis and MEF2 were significantly downregulated at both mRNA and protein levels.ConclusionMitochondrial-ER dysfunction, energy metabolism disturbance, and muscle ultrastructural remodeling are associated with the pathophysiology of MTrPs, and these alterations may be linked to the downregulation of the AMPK/PGC-1α/Mfn2 pathway. These findings provide support for the “energy crisis” hypothesis of MTrPs from both molecular and ultrastructural perspectives, and identify potential therapeutic targets for MPS.

Introduction

Myofascial pain syndrome (MPS) is a highly prevalent chronic musculoskeletal disorder characterized by myofascial trigger points (MTrPs) — focal hypersensitive nodules within taut muscle bands that cause local pain, referred pain, motor dysfunction, and autonomic disturbances.1,2 MPS affects approximately 30–85% of the general population at some point in life and represents one of the most common causes of chronic musculoskeletal pain encountered in clinical practice.3 It is particularly prevalent among patients with spinal disorders, including cervical and lumbar spine pathologies, where MTrPs frequently contribute to and perpetuate pain symptoms.4 Although the clinical prevalence rate of MTrPs is high, the specific molecular and cellular mechanisms underlying their formation remain incompletely unclear.5,6

Multiple hypotheses have been proposed to explain MTrPs pathogenesis, each emphasizing different aspects of the complex pathophysiology. The integrated hypothesis, originally proposed by Simons et al, posits that MTrPs arise from a vicious cycle of excessive acetylcholine release at motor endplates, leading to sustained sarcomere contraction, local hypoxia, and energy depletion — commonly referred to as the “energy crisis” theory.7–10 Motor endplate dysfunction is considered a key initiating event, whereby abnormal acetylcholine release results in persistent depolarization and contracture of muscle fibers.10,11 The Cinderella hypothesis proposes that type I muscle fibers are preferentially recruited during sustained low-level contractions, leading to their gradual degeneration and replacement by type II fibers over time.12,13 Additionally, peripheral and central sensitization mechanisms play important roles in the maintenance and amplification of pain associated with MTrPs, involving nociceptor sensitization, spinal cord plasticity, and altered central pain processing.14 Local tissue congestion, impaired microcirculation, and hypoxia are also thought to contribute significantly to MTrPs formation by creating an ischemic environment that further compromises energy metabolism and promotes tissue damage.9,15 While each hypothesis captures important aspects of MTrPs pathophysiology, none fully explains the complete spectrum of clinical and pathological findings, suggesting that multiple mechanisms likely interact in a complex and dynamic manner.

Mitochondrial dysfunction has emerged as a central theme in MTrPs pathophysiology, consistent with the energy crisis hypothesis. Animal studies have demonstrated reduced mitochondrial content in MTrPs regions, and downregulation of the adenosine monophosphate-activated protein kinase/peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator-1α/sirtuin 3 (AMPK/PGC-1α/SIRT3) signaling axis has been linked to mitochondrial impairment and energy depletion.16,17 However, human studies have yielded conflicting results. A prospective cohort pilot study using high-resolution respirometry found no significant differences in mitochondrial respiratory function between chronic MTrPs and control muscle samples,18 raising important questions about the translational relevance of animal findings. This discrepancy may reflect differences in disease stage, muscle type, sampling location, or the limitations of ex vivo functional assays. Furthermore, most existing studies have been primarily descriptive, and the specific molecular pathways governing mitochondrial dysfunction in MTrPs remain to be fully elucidated.

The AMPK/PGC-1α/ Mitofusin 2(Mfn2) signaling axis represents a compelling pathway for understanding MTrPs pathogenesis for several reasons. First, AMPK serves as the master cellular energy sensor, directly linking energy status to mitochondrial biogenesis through PGC-1α — the principal transcriptional co-activator governing mitochondrial mass and oxidative capacity.19 Impaired AMPK/PGC-1α signaling would logically result in reduced mitochondrial content and compromised energy production, aligning with the energy crisis hypothesis. Second, PGC-1α cooperates with myocyte enhancer factor 2 (MEF2) to regulate skeletal muscle fiber type specification, promoting the slow-twitch oxidative phenotype.20,21 Dysregulation of this axis could explain the fiber type shifts observed in MTrPs. Third, Mfn2 — a downstream target of both AMPK and PGC-1α — is a critical regulator of mitochondrial dynamics and mitochondria-ER contact sites (MERCs).22,23 MERCs are essential for Ca2⁺ homeostasis, lipid exchange, and cell survival,24,25 all of which are potentially relevant to MTrPs. Mfn2 downregulation has been associated with both mitochondrial fragmentation and ER stress,26,27 representing a potential mechanistic link between energy metabolism disturbance and the ultrastructural abnormalities observed in MTrPs. Despite these compelling connections, whether the AMPK/PGC-1α/Mfn2 axis is dysregulated in MTrPs, and how such dysregulation relates to the observed pathological changes, has not been systematically investigated.

MERCs regulate mitochondrial function, Ca2⁺ homeostasis, ER stress, and inflammation, all of which are relevant to MTrPs pathophysiology.24,25 Mfn2 is a key tether protein for MERCs,22 but its role in MTrPs has not been reported. PGC-1α is a master regulator of mitochondrial biogenesis and energy metabolism, controlled upstream by AMPK.19 PGC-1α modulates ER stress via Mfn2 and regulates fiber type through the MEF2/PGC-1α pathway.20,26 MEF2 also regulates basal Mfn2 expression, linking it to mitochondrial function and Ca2⁺ balance.28 Whether this network contributes to MTrPs remains unknown.

Therefore, this study aims to utilize the MTrPs model of rats to explore the association between the dysregulation of the AMPK/PGC-1α/Mfn2 pathway and the ultrastructural changes of mitochondria and endoplasmic reticulum, and to investigate the potential role of MERCS abnormalities in the pathophysiology of MTrPs, in order to provide morphological and molecular evidence related to the energy crisis hypothesis.

Materials and Methods

This study employed a controlled experimental design using a rat model of MTrPs to investigate the association between the AMPK/PGC-1α/Mfn2 signaling axis and mitochondrial-ER ultrastructural alterations. The experimental workflow comprised: (1) establishment of the MTrPs model via blunt striking combined with eccentric exercise; (2) confirmation of MTrPs by electromyography and histological examination; (3) assessment of muscle fiber type composition using ATPase histochemistry and immunofluorescence staining; (4) evaluation of mitochondrial and ER ultrastructure and MERCs by TEM; and (5) quantification of AMPK, PGC-1α, Mfn2, and MEF2 expression at mRNA and protein levels using qRT-PCR and Western blotting, respectively. All procedures were approved by the institutional animal ethics committee and conducted in accordance with established guidelines.

Animals

Twenty-five adult male Sprague Dawley rats (200–250 g) were obtained from the Animal Experimental Center of Guangxi Medical University. Rats were randomly divided into a normal control group (n=10) and a model group (n=15). All animals were housed in a specific pathogen-free facility (22±2 °C, 42% humidity, 12 h light/dark cycle) with ad libitum access to food and water. All procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Guangxi Medical University (No. 202311017) and complied with ARRIVE and NIH guidelines. All animal experiments, anesthesia, and euthanasia were performed in strict accordance with the AVMA Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals: 2020 Edition.29 According to the method described by Huang et al, only male rats were included in this study.30 It may minimize potential confounding effects of estrous cycle-related hormonal fluctuations on muscle metabolism and mitochondrial function, which have been shown to influence pain sensitivity and muscle physiology.31,32 This approach may limit the generalizability of our findings to female subjects.

Modeling Intervention

The MTrPs model was established through 8 weeks of blunt striking combined with eccentric exercise in accordance with previous study.30 Rats in the model group were anesthetized with 30 mg/kg pentobarbital sodium (1% concentration, intraperitoneal injection) and fixed on the percussion plate. A 1200 g stick with a kinetic energy of 2.352 J falling freely from a height of 20 cm was used to bluntly strike the right proximal gastrocnemius area marked on the skin. All injured rats were subjected to 90 minutes of eccentric exercise on a treadmill (SA101B, Jiangsu Saiangsi Biological Technology Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China) at a −16° downhill angle and a speed of 16 m/min on the second day. Subsequently, the remaining 5 days of the week were spent without any intervention. The rats in the model group underwent intervention for 8 weeks and resting for 4 weeks, while rats in the control group received no intervention during this period. At the end of all experiments, all rats were humanely euthanized via an overdose of pentobarbital sodium (150 mg/kg, intraperitoneal injection, 1% concentration) in accordance with AVMA guidelines to ensure complete and painless cessation of vital functions.29

EMG Identification of MTrPs

The active MTrPs that caused clinical pain complaints were identified in the surrounding tissue through the presence of a taut band within the muscle upon palpation, a local twitch response of muscle when adequately stimulated, and spontaneous electrical activity (SEA) as evidenced by electromyography (EMG).30 All rats underwent EMG examination utilizing the NTS-2000 instrument (Nuocheng Medical Co., Ltd., Shanghai). Initially, the right gastrocnemius of each rat was meticulously exposed. The taut band was palpated and marked. Subsequently, an electrode was inserted into the taut band to assess the local twitch response and verify the presence of active MTrPs exhibiting SEA. In contrast, the normal gastrocnemius of rats in the control group showed a resting potential, as observed by EMG.

Western Blotting

Total protein was extracted from rat gastrocnemius muscle, and protein concentration was determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Cat# PC0020, Solarbio Biotech Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.33 Protein samples were separated by 10% SDS‑PAGE (Cat# S1052, Solarbio Biotech Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) and electro‑transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. Membranes were blocked in blocking buffer at room temperature for 1 h and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against AMPK (Cat# K002500P), PGC‑1α (Cat# K007793P), MEF2 (Cat# K009295P), and Mfn2 (Cat# K007793P) (all from Solarbio Biotech Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). After washing, immunoblots were imaged, and protein expression levels were quantified using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Quantitative Real-Time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from gastrocnemius muscle samples using TriQuick Reagent (Cat# R1100, Solarbio Biotech Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions.34 Quantitative real‑time PCR (qRT‑PCR) was performed using ABI Real‑Time PCR System with corresponding software v. 2.4 (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). Muscle Fiber Staining

Alkaline ATPase Histochemical Staining

Alkaline ATPase histochemical staining (modified from Hämäläinen and Pette) was used to distinguish type I and type II muscle fibers in gastrocnemius sections.35 Briefly, 5 g Tris base and 0.499 g calcium chloride were dissolved in 250 mL distilled water, and the pH was adjusted to 10.4 with hydrochloric acid. ATP sodium salt (15 mg/10 mL) was added, mixed, and the pH was further adjusted to 9.4. Frozen sections were incubated in the pH 10.4 solution for 5 min, then in the pH 9.4 solution for another 30 min. Sections were rinsed three times with CaCl2 solution (2 min each), incubated with cobalt nitrate for 5 min, washed with running water for 5 min, treated with ammonium sulfide for 30s, washed again for 5 min, and finally mounted with neutral balsam.

Immunofluorescence Staining

Immunofluorescence staining was performed for muscle fiber type validation. Fresh frozen sections were fixed in cold acetone (−20 °C) for 10 min and washed three times (5 min each) with 0.2% PBS-T (pH 7.4) (Cat# G0002, Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China). Sections were blocked with 10% goat serum and 3% BSA for 30 min, followed by antigen retrieval in 0.01 mol/L sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0) for 30 min. Sections were then incubated with primary antibodies against MyHC-fast and MyHC-slow (1:300 in PBS-T) at 37 °C for 2 h and then at 4 °C for 36 h. After washing, sections were incubated with CY3- and Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:500 in PBS-T) (Cat# GB21303, Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China) at 37 °C for 30 min. All sections were coverslipped with glycerol mounting medium and observed under a fluorescence microscope.

ATPase staining was used to assess muscle fiber composition in MTrPs lesions, as it permits simultaneous visualization of all fiber types and facilitates quantitative and spatial analysis. Fluorescent staining was employed for validation and subcellular localization owing to its high specificity. Samples from five rats per group were alternately allocated to ATPase or immunofluorescence staining to ensure balanced analysis.

Transmission Electron Microscope (TEM)

The ultramorphology of the obtained gastrocnemius muscle was observed using Hitachi HT7700 TEM. For this purpose, all tissue blocks were sectioned into 1 mm3 samples, fixed overnight at 4°C in 2.5% glutaraldehyde, then treated with 1% osmium tetroxide for 2 hours, followed by dehydration in a graded series of ethanol. After embedding in epoxy resin, ultra-thin sections were prepared and stained with 1% uranyl acetate. Ultimately, a comparative analysis of mitochondria, ER, and MERCs between the model and control groups was conducted using TEM.

MERCs were defined as ER–mitochondria membrane contacts with a distance < 30 nm. MERCs frequency was expressed as the percentage of mitochondria involved in MERCs relative to total mitochondria. Five rats per group were randomly selected for TEM observation. Three images per sample were randomly captured near the Z-line at a fixed magnification, and MERCs frequency was quantified accordingly.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 22.0 software (IBM Corp., Chicago, IL, USA). The Shapiro‑Wilk test and Levene’s test were used to evaluate the normality and homogeneity of variance for continuous data, respectively. Continuous variables are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Independent‑samples t‑tests were adopted to compare the expression levels of key proteins and mRNAs, muscle fiber type proportions, and MERCs frequency in gastrocnemius muscle between the MTrPs group and the control group. To mitigate inflated Type I error caused by multiple statistical comparisons, the Holm‑Bonferroni procedure was applied to adjust the P values for all primary outcome indicators to control the familywise error rate. In addition, the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate correction was performed as a supplementary sensitivity analysis.36 A corrected P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The Identification of MTrPs in the Rat Models

During modeling, one rat died of anesthesia-related complications. Finally, 24 rats were included in the present study (model group: n=14; normal control group: n=10). Compared with the normal control group, rats in the MTrPs group showed slight atrophy at and around the blunt trauma site, and palpable taut bands were detected in the gastrocnemius muscle during physical examination. Electromyography revealed spontaneous electrical activity (Figure 1A) and local twitch responses at the hypersensitive nodules within the taut bands during acupuncture. No abnormal activity was observed in the control group, which maintained stable resting membrane potentials (Figure 1B). In addition, H&E staining (Figure 1C) showed locally swollen and contracted muscle fibers in cross-sections of the MTrPs lesion area, whereas muscle fibers in the control group were regular in size and morphology (Figure 1D). These findings confirmed the successful establishment of the active MTrPs rat model. Muscle Fiber Type Proportion in the MTrPs Rat Model

As shown in Figure 2A and B, muscle fiber type composition was determined by ATPase histochemical staining in gastrocnemius muscle sections. For quantification, three fields were randomly selected per section, and at least 200 muscle fibers were analyzed per sample. The percentage of type I muscle fibers was 32.80±1.578 in the normal control group but significantly decreased to 27.38±1.597 in the MTrPs model group (P < 0.05). Accordingly, the proportion of type II muscle fibers was increased in the model group. Immunofluorescence staining (Figure 2C and D) further validated the distribution of muscle fiber types and supported the quantitative data. Downregulation of the AMPK/PGC-1α/Mfn2 Signaling Axis in the MTrPs Rat Model

Given the observed muscle fiber type transition, we further examined the expression of MEF2—a key transcription factor involved in fiber type specification—and its potential association with the AMPK/PGC-1α/Mfn2 signaling pathway. As illustrated in Figure 3A, mRNA levels of AMPK, PGC-1α, Mfn2, and MEF2 were all downregulated in the model group relative to the control group. Western blot analysis (Figure 3B and C) confirmed that the corresponding protein levels were also significantly reduced. Collectively, qRT-PCR and Western blot results demonstrated that the AMPK/PGC-1α/Mfn2 signaling axis and MEF2 were markedly inhibited at both transcriptional and translational levels in the MTrPs model. These molecular changes are consistent with impaired mitochondrial biogenesis and altered energy metabolism, although the correlative nature of these findings precludes definitive conclusions regarding causal relationships. Ultrastructural Changes Under Transmission Electron Microscopy

As shown in Figure 4, TEM observation revealed distinct ultrastructural alterations in mitochondria, ER, and MERCs between the two groups. In the MTrPs model group, most mitochondria displayed mild swelling with sparse, unevenly distributed matrix and occasional vacuolization; meanwhile, the ER was extensively dilated, accompanied by an increased frequency of MERCs (Figure 4A and B). In contrast, the ultrastructure of gastrocnemius muscle was intact, with no obvious swelling or dilation of mitochondria or ER, and only a small number of MERCs were detected in the normal control group (Figure 4C and D). Figure 4E showed that the frequency of MERC formation was significantly higher in the model group than in the control group (P < 0.001). Discussion

This study yielded several novel insights into the pathophysiology of MTrPs. Morphologically, rats in the MTrPs model group exhibited a significantly higher proportion of type II muscle fibers compared with normal controls. Distinct ultrastructural abnormalities were also evident in the model group, including mitochondrial swelling, vacuolization, cristae disruption, mild ER dilation, and an increased frequency of MERCs. Notably, these MERC alterations in MTrPs were firstly documented by TEM, providing direct ultrastructural evidence of dysregulated mitochondria–ER coupling. Furthermore, mRNA and protein expression levels of AMPK, PGC-1α, Mfn2, and MEF2 were all downregulated in the MTrPs group. To our knowledge, this is the first study to systematically investigate the AMPK/PGC-1α/Mfn2 signaling axis in the context of MTrPs, thereby linking energy metabolism regulation to mitochondrial dynamics and MERC abnormalities. Taken together, the pathophysiological changes inferred from these morphological and molecular data point toward mitochondrial dysfunction and disturbed energy metabolism, which may contribute to the pathogenesis of active MTrPs.

Rats in the MTrPs group showed mild atrophy at and around the gastrocnemius blunt injury site. The altered muscle fiber-type profile indicated a phenotypic shift from type I oxidative fibers toward type II glycolytic fibers in the MTrPs model. This shift likely represents an adaptive response of skeletal muscle to insufficient energy supply under chronic hypoxia.37 Type II fibers are characterized by high glycolytic capacity, low mitochondrial density, and weak antioxidant capacity, rendering them more vulnerable to damage induced by metabolic stress.38,39 The consequent reduction in ATP levels leads to decreased muscle endurance and increased fatigue. Furthermore, such fiber-type transformation promotes skeletal muscle atrophy and downregulates key molecules related to mitochondrial biogenesis and oxidative phosphorylation. These observations are consistent with the inhibited AMPK/PGC-1α and MEF2/PGC-1α signaling axes observed in this study and support previous reports of reduced mitochondrial content in MTrPs animal models.16,17 However, it is important to acknowledge that the observed fiber-type shift and mitochondrial changes may not be entirely specific to MTrPs. Chronic muscle injury induced by repeated blunt trauma and eccentric exercise—the very interventions used to establish the MTrPs model—could also contribute to these alterations through non-specific tissue damage and repair processes. Thus, the changes observed in this study may reflect both MTrPs-specific pathophysiology and a more generalized response to chronic muscle injury. Additionally, our results are not fully consistent with the Cinderella hypothesis, which emphasizes the predominant involvement of type I fibers.13 This discrepancy may be explained by differences in muscle types: previous studies mainly focused on postural muscles such as the trapezius and gluteus medius, whereas the present study used the gastrocnemius, a typical locomotor muscle.12,13 Another possible explanation relates to the sampling time point. Similar to other pathological conditions, MPS is a dynamic and progressive disorder, and the local microenvironment of MTrPs undergoes continuous adaptive changes. To date, no studies have provided comprehensive and continuous observations covering the entire natural course of MTrPs.

As a central cellular energy sensor, decreased AMPK activity would be expected to downregulate PGC-1α expression, which not only suppresses mitochondrial biogenesis but also impairs its coordination with downstream regulatory pathways. Inhibition of the PGC-1α/NRF-1/ERRα axis reduces mitochondrial DNA replication, corresponding to the sparse mitochondrial matrix and reduced cristae density observed under electron microscopy.21,40 Moreover, decreased PGC-1α activity weakens its synergistic interaction with MEF2. Inactivated MEF2 reduces transcription of MYH7 (slow myosin) and relieves inhibition of MYH4 (fast myosin), thereby promoting the transition from type I to type II muscle fibers.21,41 In addition, both AMPK and PGC-1α can bind to the MERCs scaffold protein Mfn2 and regulate MERCs formation.23,42 Downregulation of AMPK/PGC-1α inhibits Mfn2 transcription, disturbs mitochondrial outer membrane fusion, and triggers mitochondrial fragmentation, which manifests as mitochondrial swelling and increased volume under TEM.27 The abnormal mitochondrial morphology observed in this study is consistent with previous reports.16 Furthermore, Mfn2 reduction impairs MERCs function. Although increased MERCs frequency was detected in our TEM images, this change likely represents a functionally ineffective compensatory response.43 This phenomenon may be associated with the extensive morphological alterations of mitochondria and ER in MTrPs. Mitochondrial swelling and ER dilation increase the physical contact area between these two organelles; however, sustained Mfn2 downregulation leads to dysfunctional coupling sites and eventually metabolic collapse.27,44 During the early stage of energy stress, cells may enhance MERCs formation to alleviate the functional crisis. Nevertheless, persistent loss of Mfn2 prevents these contacts from exerting normal physiological functions, ultimately leading to decompensation—consistent with the mitochondrial vacuolization and sarcoplasmic reticulum dilation observed in this study. Although the causal directions of these associations still need to be determined through future intervention studies targeting the AMPK/PGC-1α/Mfn2 signaling axis, these findings provide new insights into the potential pathways of the pathogenesis of MTrP.

It is intriguing to speculate that MERCs dysfunction may also contribute to inflammatory processes in MTrPs. MERCs have been proposed to serve as a platform for NLRP3 inflammasome assembly and activation, which can sense mitochondrial damage and initiate cellular stress responses. Under conditions of mitochondrial dysfunction, injured mitochondria may release reactive oxygen species (ROS) and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), including mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) and ATP,45 which could potentially activate the NLRP3 inflammasome at MERCs and trigger secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-8.46 Although the present study did not directly measure DAMP levels or NLRP3 inflammasome activation, previous studies have reported elevated IL-1β and IL-8 expression and local inflammatory signals on magnetic resonance imaging in MTrPs, which is consistent with this hypothetical framework.47,48 Release of pro-inflammatory cytokines may further sensitize nociceptors by upregulating downstream inflammatory mediators. Therefore, therapeutic strategies targeting the NLRP3 inflammasome might represent a promising approach for relieving MTrPs-related pain,49 though this remains speculative and requires direct experimental validation in the MTrPs context.

In this study, extensive mild ER dilation was observed in the MTrPs region at the ultrastructural level, which is a morphological feature suggestive of disordered intracellular calcium recycling. The underlying mechanism may be closely associated with impaired mitochondrial energy metabolism. Specifically, local ischemia and hypoxia may suppress mitochondrial biogenesis, which is further aggravated by inhibition of the AMPK/PGC-1α signaling axis.33 ATP is the primary energy source for sarcoplasmic/endoplasmic reticulum calcium ATPase (SERCA) function.44 ATP deficiency could directly reduce SERCA activity and impair reuptake of cytosolic Ca2⁺ into the sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR).49 Consequently, SERCA dysfunction may lead to insufficient or abnormal Ca2⁺ storage in the SR, which could trigger structural dilation as a compensatory change. These ultrastructural observations, combined with the molecular evidence of AMPK/PGC-1α downregulation and mitochondrial injury, provide subcellular support for the “energy crisis” hypothesis in MTrPs pathophysiology. Notably, these findings are logically consistent with Simons’ theory of excessive acetylcholine (ACh) release at motor endplates, which may represent the initial trigger for the above pathophysiological cascade in MTrPs.7 However, it should be emphasized that our morphological observations cannot direct evidence regarding calcium dysregulation, and direct measurements of intracellular calcium dynamics would be needed to confirm this interpretation.

While our findings align with the energy crisis hypothesis, alternative and complementary mechanisms should also be considered. The integrated hypothesis posits that MTrPs arise from a combination of motor endplate dysfunction, energy deficit, and peripheral sensitization, all of which may interact with the mitochondrial alterations reported in this study. Furthermore, the observed changes could partly represent secondary adaptations to chronic muscle injury, hypoxia, inflammation, or tissue remodeling rather than primary drivers of MTrPs.50 Future studies will be needed to distinguish MTrPs-specific alterations from non-specific injury responses.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, although the MTrPs rat model established by blunt injury combined with eccentric exercise meets commonly accepted diagnostic criteria, it cannot fully recapitulate the complex pathophysiological processes of clinical MTrPs. Second, mechanistic exploration remains preliminary and correlative: we did not directly evaluate mitochondrial functional indicators (eg, oxygen consumption rate, ATP production, membrane potential, or ROS levels) or dynamically monitor intracellular calcium concentrations. Despite the observed increase in MERCs frequency, its functional consequences (eg, calcium transport efficiency, lipid exchange) and detailed regulatory mechanisms remain unclear. Third, this study only observed a single time point rather than the entire progressive course of MTrPs; thus, the results may only reflect the local microenvironmental status at that specific stage. Future studies are warranted to further explore the dynamic mechanisms underlying MTrPs initiation and progression on the basis of this study.

Conclusion

In summary, this rat MTrPs model demonstrated a phenotypic shift from type I to type II muscle fibers, downregulation of the AMPK/PGC-1α/Mfn2 pathway and MEF2, mitochondrial damage, ER dilation, and increased MERCs frequency. These results suggest that mitochondrial–ER dysfunction, energy metabolic disturbance, and ultrastructural remodeling associated with AMPK/PGC-1α/Mfn2 downregulation are implicated in MTrPs pathophysiology. Our findings provide molecular and ultrastructural evidence supporting the “energy crisis” hypothesis and identify potential therapeutic targets. Further functional validation and clinical translation studies are needed to confirm these associations and explore their therapeutic relevance.
