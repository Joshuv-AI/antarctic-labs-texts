Impact of traumatic experiences on obsessive-compulsive disorder: a study of Palestinians in the Gaza Strip – a conflict area
Authors: Nadin Hannoon, Basel El-Khodary, Zhongming Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjective: Prior research has indicated a possible relationship between obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) and trauma that appears to affect symptom severity and comorbidity. The Gaza Strip, a region characterised by ongoing violence and conflict, would benefit from further study to address psychopathology in the context of psycho-traumatology. Considering these circumstances, in this study, we investigated the prevalence of trauma aetiology among OCD patients and its impact on the clinical picture by studying the effects of trauma on three different levels of trauma-aetiology: (1) history of trauma exposure (TE-OCD), (2) individual perception of OCD as trauma-related (TR-OCD), and (3) the onset of OCD within six months post-trauma (PT-OCD).Methods: This cross-sectional study included 103 Gazans diagnosed with OCD, aged 18–56 years. Semi-structured clinical interviews, including self-report measures, were used to collect demographic and diagnostic data.Results: Out of 103 participants, 77.7% reported a history of trauma (TE-OCD), 29.1% perceived their trauma as a cause of OCD (TR-OCD), and 18.4% developed their symptoms within six months of the trauma (PT-OCD). No significant correlation was found between the severity of OCD symptoms and a history of trauma exposure. Statistical differences in symptom severity were found across TR- and PT-OCD levels of trauma-aetiology. However, after controlling for major depressive disorder (MDD), these differences were observed only for obsessions.Conclusions: These findings reveal that trauma is common among OCD patients in Gaza and could be a major predisposing factor. The perception of trauma as a cause and the time between trauma exposure and OCD onset could impact the clinical picture of OCD in terms of symptom severity and increased MDD comorbidity.KEYWORDS: Trauma, OCD, depression, trauma-related OCD, Gaza Strip, conflictHIGHLIGHTS: • Trauma is a common aetiological factor leading to the development of OCD.• The history of trauma exposure (TE) per se has shown no effect on the severity of the OCD symptoms.• The individual perception of trauma and its relation to OCD could play a role in the severity of the symptoms and the development of other comorbidities

Introduction

The history of trauma in general and understanding its diverse dimensions are key aetiological factors in psychopathology (Copeland et al., 2018; Curran et al., 2018). Trauma is defined as having witnessed or being directly or indirectly exposed to actual or threatened death, serious injury, or sexual violence (APA, 2013). Although trauma is most commonly associated with posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) (Kessler et al., 2017), trauma-exposed patients frequently present with additional and varied co-occurring disorders, including obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) (Carbonella, 2018; Rajabi Khamesi et al., 2021), which is characterised by the presence of obsessions, compulsions, or both (APA, 2013). Different dimensions of direct and indirect traumatic experiences play a role in OCD psychopathology as they trigger, aggravate, and/or maintain it (Carr et al., 2013; D’Angelo et al., 2024; Fontenelle et al., 2021; Pinciotti et al., 2021). Furthermore, a history of trauma has been found to be positively associated with the severity of OCD symptoms (Boger et al., 2020; Miller & Brock, 2017), comorbidity (de Araújo et al., 2018; Hofmeijer-Sevink et al., 2013), suicidality (Brakoulias et al., 2017; Rajabi Khamesi et al., 2021), chronicity (Van Oudheusden et al., 2018), and worse treatment outcomes (Boger et al., 2020).

Previous studies suggest that major traumatic events play a role in the development of late-onset OCD (Fontenelle et al., 2012; Frydman et al., 2014) and posttraumatic OCD (PT-OCD) (Nacasch et al., 2011). Furthermore, it was suggested that trauma may be selectively related to a specific OCD loop associated with only obsessions or compulsions (Miller & Brock, 2017). In some cases, OCD symptoms were initially related to trauma but later became generalised and independent (Fostick et al., 2012). This suggests a potential environmental role in the development of OCD following exposure to a traumatic event and a biological link between exposure to trauma and OCD (Fostick et al., 2012). Preliminarily, consistent with Adams et al. (2018b), the literature indicates different trauma-related OCD (TR-OCD) pathways, as it may directly cause the onset (precipitating factor), increase the vulnerability (predisposing factor), or reveal latent excitant morbidity (revealing factor). In this context, two terms have been used in the literature to describe the direct relationship between trauma and OCD: PT-OCD (Murayama et al., 2020b) and TR-OCD (Dykshoorn, 2014). PT-OCD is caused by intense emotional distress related to physiological and psychological changes and shows high comorbidity with PTSD. TR-OCD develops at any time and/or in the context of different moderators, such as anxiety and depression, which in turn have complex relationships with OCD symptoms (Briggs & Price, 2009). The literature lacks precise criteria for distinguishing between the two terms. It mostly depended on the researcher's methodology and preferences, whereas specific elements (e.g. duration between TE and OCD onset) may differently influence the disorder's presentation, as they may have different aetiological mechanisms.

Palestinians in the Gaza Strip have been subjected to persistent conflict for decades, experiencing repeated instances of violence and war (Alibwaini & Thabet, 2019). This results in acute and potentially chronic dysfunction, which most commonly manifests as PTSD (Kessler et al., 2017). Moreover, prolonged exposure to potential danger can increase the risk of trauma and subsequently affect mental health (Norris et al., 2003). Studies on war trauma have found high comorbidity between PTSD and OCD, or at least obsessive-compulsive tendencies (Aldea et al., 2019; Morina et al., 2016). War-related trauma is a common experience among Palestinians in the Gaza Strip, with estimates indicating that between 60% and 93% of individuals have been affected at some point in their lives (Manzanero et al., 2021; Qeshta et al., 2019; Al-Sheikh & Thabet, 2017). According to El-Deeb (2017), around 90% of individuals were found to have symptoms of PTSD, with roughly 36% to 55% meeting the DSM-IV criteria for PTSD, while about 67% had other mental health comorbidities. It is crucial to consider trauma-related sequelae in the development and perpetuation of OCD psychopathology. Given the heightened vulnerability of Gazans to traumatic incidents, particularly the repeated exposure to war-related trauma alongside other forms of trauma, it is essential to address trauma-induced pathologies.

Considering these circumstances, the purpose of this study is to have a rough estimate of the prevalence of trauma exposure in OCD patients in Gaza. The study also aims to investigate how trauma influences the clinical picture of OCD by comparing patients with OCD based on the presence or absence of trauma at three different levels. We hypothesised that trauma could have various impacts on OCD based on: level 1: presence of a history of trauma (Trauma Exposure TE-OCD); level 2: perception of trauma as the cause of OCD (Trauma-Related TR-OCD); or level 3: OCD developed within 6 months of the traumatic event (Posttraumatic PT-OCD).

Methodology

Participants

Participants were recruited from the outpatient psychiatry clinics at the Gaza Community Mental Health Programme (GCMHP) and the Ministry of Health in the Gaza Strip between March 2022 and October 2022. The inclusion criteria were: (1) age between 18 and 65 years; and (2) meeting the diagnostic criteria for obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) as outlined in the DSM-5 through evaluations conducted by psychiatrists at the hosting institutions. Out of 224 patient files, patients with psychotic disorders, substance abuse, a history of neurological illnesses, or a central nervous system disorder were excluded. A total of 165 patients met the criteria to participate; of those, 110 patients provided informed consent, while the limited familiarity with comprehensive scale batteries during routine diagnostic and therapeutic sessions contributed to reluctance among 55 patients. Seven participants dropped out. The final sample consisted of 103 patients between the ages of 18 and 56. Table 1 presents the characteristics of the participants.

Measures

Registered clinical psychologists conducted semi-structured clinical interviews to collect information on patients’ histories, comorbid conditions, and OCD symptoms.

Demographic information

Demographic data, such as gender, age, and level of education, was gathered via a self-report questionnaire.

Clinical diagnosis and comorbid conditions

For a diagnosis of OCD and other comorbid conditions (MDD and PTSD), the adapted symptoms checklist of the Arabic version of the Mini International Neuropsychiatric Interview (M.I.N.I.-AR) was used to obtain clinical information on diagnosis and comorbidity. A recent study showed evidence supporting the validation of modules that covered depression and PTSD in M.I.N.I.-AR (Karnouk et al., 2021).

Yale-Brown obsessive-compulsive scale

Symptom severity was assessed using the Arabic version of the Yale-Brown Obsessive-Compulsive Scale (Y-BOCS), a 21-item scale that provides a severity rating (0–63) (Overduin & Furnham, 2012). In the present study, the Cronbach’s alpha was 0.927.

Beck depression inventory-II

The Arabic version of the Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI-II) (Ghareeb, 2000) was used to assess depressive symptoms over the previous two weeks. It consists of 21 items and provides a severity rating (0–63) for depressive symptoms, with alpha values ranging between 0.82 and –0.93 in a study conducted in Arabic countries, including Palestine (Alansari, 2006). In the present study, the Cronbach’s alpha was 0.963.

Measures of trauma

For traumatic experiences, an abbreviated Arabic version of the Life Events Checklist for DSM-5 (LEC-5), consisting of 10 events, was used (Weathers et al., 2013). For PTSD severity, the PTSD Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5) with Criterion A was used (Blevins et al., 2015). The scale was found to have validity and reliability (α = 0.89) in its Arabic version (Alhalal et al., 2017). To differentiate between OCD diagnoses according to trauma-related aetiology, the researchers designed a structured interview based on the OCD Trauma Timeline Interview (OTTI) (Wadsworth et al., 2021) with proper modifications to cover the research questions. The modified OTTI was developed to investigate symptom overlap between OCD and PTSD. In this study, the relationship between shared origins of trauma and OCD was investigated, even in the absence of PTSD. The OTTI was used to distinguish TR-OCD from non-TR-OCD and PT-OCD. The following questions guided the classification of the participants into groups: Is there a relationship between trauma or posttraumatic stress symptoms (PTSS) onset and OCD onset? If the answer was affirmative, we posed two additional questions: (1) Is there an overlap between obsessions, compulsions, PTSS/trauma-related intrusions, rituals, or both, and avoidance behaviours? (2) Is there a relationship between peri-trauma, post-trauma, and OCD’s main feelings? If the answer was yes to at least one question, the case was defined as TR-OCD. The last question concerned cases classified as TR-OCD and inquired about the duration from TE to OCD onset. If the duration was less than six months, it was defined as PT-OCD (Figure 1).

Guidelines of Classification of OCD patients according to the history of traumatic experiences.

Procedure

TE-OCD was defined as having a history of traumatic experiences at any time during the patient's life. TR-OCD was defined as having the same origin and sharing trauma-related symptoms or elements between TE and OCD at any time (Dykshoorn, 2014). In this study, this was manifested by the patient's perception of symptoms overlap and/or the relation between peri- or post-trauma emotional experience and OCD. PT-OCD was defined as TR-OCD when a TE happened within six months of the onset of OCD. Other authors, like Murayama et al. (2020b), defined PT-OCD as developing OCD within one month of experiencing the trauma.

The 103 participants with OCD in this study were grouped into three levels. Firstly, we divided the patients at level 1 into two groups: TE-OCD (80) and non-TE-OCD (23), based on the presence of a history of trauma. At level 2, we divided patients into two groups based on their perception of trauma as a cause of OCD: TR-OCD (30) and non-TR-OCD (73). Lastly, at level 3, we divided patients into two groups based on the occurrence of OCD within six months of trauma: PT-OCD (19) and non-PT-OCD (84).

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted using SPSS 26.0. Descriptive statistics were used to determine the prevalence rates of trauma-related pathology among participants. Three grouping levels were identified: TE-OCD, TR-OCD, and PT-OCD. Independent t-test and analysis of variance were used to compare the groups’ demographic variables. Pearson correlations between OCD severity, the number of times of exposure to trauma, PTSS severity, and MDD severity were computed to test the basic associations between variables. Independent t-test was used to compare severity across trauma aetiology groups in each level, separately for OCD, including obsessions and compulsions, MDD, and PTSS symptoms severity. No correlations were found between the main variables: OCD and trauma-related variables, while correlations were found with MDD, so an independent analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was conducted to compare severity across trauma aetiology groups in the three levels after controlling for MDD. All significant values were computed at p ≤ .05.

Results

Preliminary analyses based on demographic variables

Age significantly correlated with depressive symptoms (r = .348, p = .001), while there was no significant correlation between age and OCD severity (r = .118, p = .235) or PTSS (r = .055, p = .633).

The results showed that there was a significant difference in depression severity depending on the education level (F(2, 96) = 5.88, p = .004); patients with a primary education reported more depressive symptoms than those with a secondary education. There were no significant gender differences on any of the measures (Table 1).

Prevalence of trauma-aetiology OCD psychopathology

With regard to Level 1, 77.7% (n = 80) reported a history of TE (TE-OCD group), while 23.3% (n = 23) reported no history. In Level 2, 29% (n = 30) reported that their OCD was trauma-related (TR-OCD group), whereas in Level 3, 18.4% (n = 19) of the total sample developed TR-OCD within 6 months of trauma (PT-OCD group) (Figure 2).

Impact of trauma on symptom severity

The results revealed a significant positive correlation between OCD and MDD (p = .001). Similarly, MDD had a significant positive correlation with TE and PTSS (p = .02, p = .001, respectively). Likewise, TE had a significant positive correlation with PTSS (p = .04). However, there was no significant correlation between OCD and TE (p = .18) or PTSS (p = .63) (Table 2).

Discussion

Prevalence of trauma aetiology among OCD patients

The current study was conceptualised to address the impact of trauma on OCD at three different levels (TE-OCD, TR-OCD, and PT-OCD) in the Gaza Strip as a conflict zone with ongoing trauma. Specifically, we hypothesised that the type of perceived relationship between trauma and OCD may differentially impact OCD. To the best of our knowledge, no previous study has addressed the history of trauma in OCD at three different levels.

The results revealed a high prevalence of traumatic experiences (77%) among OCD patients. Approximately 74% of the sample had PTSS, while only 20.4% met the full criteria for PTSD. Interestingly, nearly 29% of the individuals believed that a specific trauma directly caused their OCD (TR-OCD), with some degree of symptom overlap, irrespective of the time interval between TE and OCD onset or the impact of trauma on PTSD comorbidity. Approximately two-thirds of them developed OCD within six months of TE (PT-OCD), which is relatively high (Murayama et al., 2020a; Pinciotti & Fisher, 2022; Rosso et al., 2012). The impact of trauma in a zone of ongoing conflict, where the triggering context of stress is cumulative and unavoidable, could potentially explain these findings (Wispelwey & Jamei, 2020). Unfortunately, except for PTSD, the data on Gazans’ trauma psychopathology is very limited.

The hypothesis that the perception of trauma (Pinciotti & Fisher, 2022) or the real trauma mechanisms (Adams et al., 2018a) play a role in TROCD requires further investigation. As our results showed, the duration was not conditional for all TR-OCD cases, and approximately one-third of the participants who were classified as having TR-OCD developed OCD after six months of TE. However, the reason for this delayed effect requires further investigation. It's unclear whether this is due to residual trauma symptoms or the emergence of hidden mechanisms simulating PTSD's late onset. Previous studies have focused on short durations, regardless of symptom overlap, to address TR-OCD (Murayama et al., 2020a; Pinciotti & Fisher, 2022; Rosso et al., 2012). This study suggests that different trauma-related pathways should be addressed in terms of perception, duration, and symptoms overlap, as they may have different mechanisms and effects on OCD. This may have implications for therapeutic intervention, such as utilising trauma-focused interventions or starting with trauma processing in treating TR-OCD, especially in its early stages.

Impact of trauma on OCD

Unexpectedly, no correlation was found between OCD symptom severity and trauma-related variables (the number of TEs and PTSS severity). Previous studies have reported inconsistent findings, providing more support for the relationship between trauma and OCD (Miller & Brock, 2017; Pinciotti et al., 2021; Rosso et al., 2012). Considering that no significant correlation or differences were found in the same demographic variable context, one reason for the absence of differences between the groups in the first level (TE) could be due to examining the impact of TE regardless of the TE time; variation in symptom severity could be related to the time since the last traumatic experience occurred. To investigate this possibility, future studies need to examine the effect of trauma based on a chronological pattern: the short- and the long-term.

However, consistent with the hypothesis in this study, the severity of obsessions, compulsions, and the total OCD score varied across trauma aetiology groups in the second and third levels, which suggests that experiencing trauma solely does not play a critical role in the severity of OCD symptoms. Instead, the time factor and the relationship between trauma and OCD symptoms, as perceived by the patient and manifested by symptom overlap, may play a significant role. These outcomes were validated by replicating the test in the TE-OCD group (n = 80). This may be explained from a pathophysiological perspective by sharing the same pathogenesis, as trauma has conspicuous effects on corticostriatal and limbic circuitry and can also result in neuronal hypertrophy in the dorsolateral striatum (putamen) and amygdala. These neural abnormalities affect the presentation of obsessive-compulsive disorder as they may contribute to an imbalance between goal-directed and habitual behaviour (Adams et al., 2018b; Gorbis et al., 2024), which may be explicit as symptoms overlap (e.g. excessive reassurance seeking behaviour); thus, the tendency for such trauma aetiology perceptions increases.

This study's inconsistency in Pearson correlations and t-test results highlights a question regarding the factors that may have played a role in these differences between the groups. We found a moderate correlation between MDD severity and OCD severity and weak correlations between MDD severity and both trauma variables (number of TE and PTSS severity). However, after controlling for MDD, we found no association between overall OCD severity and compulsion severity with the three levels of traumatic aetiology, but we did find a relationship between obsessions and different traumatic aetiologies. This is similar to the findings of Morgado et al. (2013), who found that perceived stress was relevant in the context of OCD, particularly for obsessive symptoms. In contrast, some studies have suggested that the association between compulsions and trauma is stronger than the association between obsessions and trauma (Miller & Brock, 2017; Pinciotti & Fisher, 2022). One reason for the current results may be the clear overlap between trauma-related intrusions and OCD obsessions, which was one of the conditions for TR-OCD in this study.

Regarding the role of MDD, which was reported as a common comorbidity in cases of OCD with trauma history (Mundy et al., 2022; Visser et al., 2014), consistent with this, our finding suggesting that MDD comorbidity may develop after exposure to trauma, OCD, or both. Furthermore, it has been suggested that the development and maintenance of comorbid OCD/PTSD/MDD may be explained by the cognitive constructs that the three disorders have in common (Basile et al., 2018; 2014; Despotes et al., 2021; Mancini & Gangemi, 2015). For example, this study suggests that guilt and self-blame among OCD patients could be exaggerated in the case of MDD comorbidity, which could in turn increase the tendency to control obsessions by utilising more excessive compulsive behaviours. In addition, shared maladaptive emotion regulation strategies and abilities, such as experimental avoidance and emotional intolerance (Browning et al., 2022; Visted et al., 2018; Yap et al., 2018), could also play a role in the association between compulsive behaviours in OCD and MDD. To summarise, these findings revealed questions regarding the role and mechanisms of current TR-OCD symptom severity and shared cognitions with MDD. Thus, further research is required in this regard. In the same context, future research may need to investigate other comorbid conditions (e.g. generalised anxiety disorder [GAD]), which were previously considered in the same category as both OCD and PTSD (APA, 2013; APA, 2000), and research supporting shared mediating factors with trauma and OCD, particularly alterations in brain function in areas associated with fear and anxiety processing. This could provide a clearer picture of the unique impact of trauma on OCD symptoms (Shin & Liberzon, 2010).

Limitations and future directions

The limitations of this study include its reliance on a treatment-seeking OCD sample in a special geopolitical context, which was a small sample for epidemiological conclusions regarding prevalence. Moreover, the combination of cumulative trauma and the levels of psychological resilience among Palestinians could limit the generalisability of these findings. Furthermore, the absence of a correlation between variables restricted the use of advanced statistical methods. The current study was strengthened by using a relatively heterogeneous clinical sample, strict exclusion criteria, and structured interviews.

Future research would significantly benefit from replication in larger OCD samples in the same and different contexts. Such research might also include different or additional measures of trauma and OC symptoms, which may provide more precise information regarding the relationship between trauma types and obsessions and compulsions. More qualitative investigations may help in formulating a clear definition of trauma-related psychopathology among OCD patients. In addition, longitudinal research that addresses causal links between trauma exposure and OCD symptomatology, including neural and cognitive alterations, may enhance our understanding of the mechanisms underlying this pathology.

The current study highlights the importance of assessing several types of trauma from different perspectives and their impact on OCD, including symptoms overlap, and considering trauma-focused interventions within certain contexts, which in turn may raise new questions to be addressed in future research regarding the utility and effectiveness of specific trauma-focused interventions.

Conclusion

The results of this study revealed that TR-OCD was relatively common, at approximately 29%, and approximately 70% of the patients with TR-OCD developed OCD within six months after TE. No aetiological impact was found in patients who reported simply experiencing trauma, whereas a perceived overlap in OCD and posttraumatic symptoms or a phenotypic overlap, or both, had a significant impact on symptom severity. Additionally, the results revealed questions related to the impact of trauma on MDD comorbidity and severity and its mutual reflection on OCD severity at different levels. The results highlight the importance of considering the subjective and objective aetiological sequelae of traumatic events. Furthermore, to the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to examine the impact of trauma aetiology on OCD at three levels in an area with ongoing conflict-related traumatic experiences.
