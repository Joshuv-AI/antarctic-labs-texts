Lotka–Volterra-type kinetic equations for interacting species
Andrea Bondesan, Marco Menale, Giuseppe Toscani, Mattia Zanella

Abstract
. In this work, we examine a kinetic framework for modeling the time evolution of size distribution densities of two populations governed by predator–prey interactions. The model builds upon the classical Boltzmann-type equations, where the dynamics arise from elementary binary interactions between the populations. The model uniquely incorporates a linear redistribution operator to quantify the birth rates in both populations, inspired by wealth redistribution operators. We prove that, under a suitable scaling regime, the Boltzmann formulation transitions to a system of coupled Fokker–Planck-type equations. These equations describe the evolution of the distribution densities and link the macroscopic dynamics of their mean values to a Lotka–Volterra system of ordinary differential equations, with parameters explicitly derived from the microscopic interaction rules. We then determine the local equilibria of the Fokker–Planck system, which are Gamma-type densities, and investigate the problem of relaxation of its solutions toward these kinetic equilibria, in terms of their moments’ dynamics. The results establish a bridge between kinetic modeling and classical population dynamics, offering a multiscale perspective on predator–prey systems.



Keywords:
Kinetic theory; Lotka–Volterra dynamics; Fokker–Planck equations; Multiscale modeling; Predator–prey interactions.

Mathematics Subject Classification:
35Q20; 35Q84; 92D25.

1.
Introduction

Lotka–Volterra equations, often referred to as predator–prey equations, are first-order nonlinear differential equations that describe population dynamics within systems where multiple species with distinct characteristics interact. In 1925, in the United States, Alfred Lotka proposed a model to describe a chemical reaction with oscillating concentrations. A year later, in Italy, mathematician Vito Volterra independently arrived at the same set of equations while attempting to explain the observed increase in predatory fish, and the corresponding decrease in prey fish, in the Adriatic Sea during World War I. The model proposed by Lotka and Volterra is considered to be the simplest one describing predator–prey interactions for competing species
Hirsch (1988)
. A step beyond simple growth dynamics, which are typically influenced by the carrying capacity of the habitat, requires analyzing interacting populations and recognizing their mutual influence on growth. These interactions shape their mutual evolution, making it crucial to predict their dynamics to better understand how communities organize and sustain themselves.
In this direction, a rich literature concentrating on species interacting through space-dependent factors have been developed in recent years, see e.g.
Breden et al. (2021)
;
Carrillo et al. (2018)
;
Desvillettes and Soresina (2019)
, in which minimal models for collective behavior may include attraction-repulsion forces between individuals as well as diffusion operators. In fact, cross-interactions can be suitably modeled by nonlinear cross-diffusion factors, which are used to take into account for the population pressure
Choi et al. (2004)
;
Haskovec et al. (2018)
;
Matthes and Parsch (2024)
;
Yamada (2009)
. We also mention selection-type dynamics as generalizations of Lotka–Volterra models
Desvillettes et al. (2008)
;
Mirrahimi et al. (2012)
;
Pouchol and Trélat (2018)
which can be seen as replicator-type dynamics
Ambrosio et al. (2021)
for infinite systems of agents. The study of the evolution of competing species has then tremendously impacted the community investigating adaptation dynamics, see e.g.
Berestycki et al. (2014)
;
Berestycki et al. (2009)
;
Calsina et al. (2013)
;
Cañizo et al. (2013)
;
Choi et al. (2003)
;
Diekmann et al. (2005)
;
Kiss and Kovács (2008)
;
Jabin and Raoul (2011)
. These approaches have been further generalized in
Lorz et al. (2011)
;
Jabin and Liu (2017)
in a nonlocal setting. Moreover, particle dynamics for cross-diffusion systems have been recently introduced in the literature for multicomponent space-dependent interactions
Chen et al. (2021)
;
Chen et al. (2019)
;
Daus et al. (2022)
.

Population dynamics have a significant impact in heterogeneous fields, such as ecological systems, game theory
Cressman and Garay (2003)
and socio-economic sciences
Palomba (1939)
, including for the study of banking systems
Sumarti et al. (2014)
. Additionally, other modeling settings presented in the literature might be considered as mesoscopic approaches inspired by generalized Lotka–Volterra equations
Malcai et al. (2002)
;
Solomon and Richmond (2002)
. In these early approaches, the population size of a system of interacting species is interpreted, within economic systems, with the wealth
of an individual investor or the market value of a traded firm.

Denoting with
X(t)\geq 0
and
Y(t)\geq 0
the densities of preys and predators at time
t\geq 0
, the Lotka–Volterra dynamics is classically described by the pair of differential equations

(1)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}X(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=\alpha X(t)-\beta X(t)Y(t),

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}Y(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=-\delta Y(t)+\gamma X(t)Y(t),

where the evolution of the preys’ population is characterized by a rate of Malthusian growth
\alpha>0
and by a predation rate
\beta>0
, while that of the predators’ population depends on losses due to a death rate
\delta>0
and on a growth rate
\gamma>0
due to predation.

In this work, we introduce a system of Boltzmann-type kinetic equations to study the time evolution of the size distribution densities of two interacting populations following the rules of a predator–prey system. The dynamics of the introduced model is based on a binary interaction scheme between the introduced populations, the number of births and deaths of individuals in both populations is quantified according to a mass preserving linear operator that has been introduced in a cognate field
Bisi et al. (2009)
. In the following, to comply with a statistical description of the dynamics, we will always assume that the number of agents is infinite. Furthermore, extending the results of
Toscani and Zanella (2024)
, we will show that, in a quasi-invariant regime of the parameters, corresponding to a high frequency of interactions balanced by a scaling factor, the Boltzmann model reduces to a system of coupled Fokker–Planck-type equations for the distribution densities of the sizes of the populations, where both the diffusion and the drift operators are characterized by time-dependent coefficients. The dynamics of the mean values of the solutions to this system of Fokker–Planck equations satisfies the Lotka–Volterra model defined in (
1
). Hence, the structure of the latter, along with its relevant parameters, can be justified using a purely microscopic binary interaction scheme, whose aggregate description is formulated assuming the propagation of chaos, through a system of Boltzmann-type equations for the evolution of the particles’ densities
Cercignani et al. (1994)
. In particular, this kinetic description allows for the statistical investigation of the underlying multiagent dynamics and the associated Fokker–Planck model provides a simple framework to explicitly determine the relevant kinetic quasi-equilibrium distributions, which take the form of Gamma-type densities, making possible to investigate the convergence of the system’s solutions toward equilibrium. In this context, we will study the two significant cases of Malthusian and logistic growths for the preys’ population, by deriving the corresponding kinetic models and by analyzing the problem of relaxation to equilibrium in terms of the moments of the solutions. The considered approach is therefore not able to account for the emergence of correlations and possible fluctuations in population sizes near extinction, as shown in
Dobramysl et al. (2018)
;
Täuber (2012)
.

The construction of the general kinetic model of Boltzmann-type will be presented in Section
2
. In Section
3
we will derive the associated coupled Fokker–Planck system, the analysis of which will form the core of this work. The presence of oscillating time-dependent coefficients in both the diffusion and drift operators, unlike the classical case, prevents the formation of uniform-in-time equilibrium solutions. For this reason, we will concentrate our attention on the role of the so-called quasi-equilibrium distributions, namely the time-dependent distributions that are flux-vanishing for the Fokker–Planck system written in divergence form. These distributions turn out to be Gamma-type densities, whose parameters are functions of the mean values of the solutions to the Fokker–Planck system itself, which in turn solve the Lotka–Volterra model (
1
). By studying the main features of these quasi-equilibrium distributions, we show how they relate to the true solutions of the kinetic system, furnishing a number of interesting information about their evolution, at least for sufficiently large times. Finally, in Section
4
we deal with a correction to our kinetic model, switching from a Malthusian-type growth for the preys to a Verhulst-type one. This choice allows to obtain a Fokker–Planck system having still time-dependent drift–diffusion coefficients, but displaying a unique global equilibrium state that represents a large-time attractor for the solutions.

2.
A kinetic model for Lotka–Volterra systems with periodic orbits

We consider an interacting system composed of two populations usually termed as
preys
and
predators
. According to classical kinetic theory, and following the approach developed in
Toscani and Zanella (2024)
, we assume that the relevant microscopic trait characterizing both populations is the size of the interacting groups and that the elementary interactions between groups of preys and predators, which influence the evolution of the overall system, are of binary-type. We suppose in particular that the group sizes can assume all nonnegative real values and we will denote with
x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
y\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
the number of individuals in each interacting group of preys and predators respectively. For future convenience, we also introduce the vector notation
\mathbf{x}=(x,y)
. Note that, within this assumption, also very small sizes of the populations
x,y\ll 1
are allowed, a condition which is not fully justified and that has to be suitably avoided. The evolution over time
t\geq 0
of the two populations of preys and predators can be described by two distribution functions
f_{1}(x,t)
and
f_{2}(y,t)
, depending on their respective sizes
x,y\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. More precisely,
f_{1}(x,t)\mathrm{d}x
indicates the fraction of preys belonging to a group of size in
[x,x+\mathrm{d}x]
at time
t\geq 0
, while
f_{2}(y,t)\mathrm{d}y
corresponds to the fraction of predators belonging to a group of size in
[y,y+\mathrm{d}y]
at time
t\geq 0
. In particular, without loss of generality we can fix the initial distributions
f_{1}(x,0)
and
f_{2}(y,0)
to be probability density functions, i.e.

\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}f_{1}(x,0)\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}f_{2}(y,0)\mathrm{d}y=1.

The main goal is to characterize the evolution over time of the densities
f_{1}(x,t)
and
f_{2}(y,t)
, from which one can determine the dynamics of their main relevant observable quantities, namely the means and the variances of the sizes of the two species, defined by

\displaystyle m_{1}(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}xf_{1}(x,t)\mathrm{d}x,

\displaystyle m_{2}(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}yf_{2}(y,t)\mathrm{d}y,\qquad

\displaystyle v_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}(x-m_{1}(t))^{2}f_{1}(x,t)\mathrm{d}x,

\displaystyle v_{2}(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}(y-m_{2}(t))^{2}f_{2}(y,t)\mathrm{d}y.

In the kinetic approach, the core of the methodology consists in introducing the elementary (binary) interaction rules which are mainly responsible for variations in populations’ sizes, in order to write, like in the classical case of gas-dynamics
Cercignani et al. (1994)
, a kinetic system of Boltzmann-type equations modeling the temporal evolution of the distributions of preys and predators, as well as of the associated macroscopic observables defined in (
).

2.1.
Microscopic dynamics

We start by considering only cross interactions.
These interactions are designed in agreement with the expected behavior of the biological system under consideration: prey–predator interactions should lead to a decrease in the number of preys and, conversely, to an increase in the number of predators, in the presence of a sufficiently large number of preys. Moreover, to take into account possible unpredictable variations, these processes should also be subject to random effects. Following the assumptions in
Toscani and Zanella (2024)
, we define the instantaneous microscopic transitions
x\mapsto x^{\prime}
and
y\mapsto y^{\prime}
of the form

(3)

\begin{split}x^{\prime}&=x-\Phi(y)x+\left(\frac{x}{\bar{x}}\right)^{p}\mathds{1}(x\geq(1-p)s_{0})\eta_{1}(y),\\[5.69054pt]
y^{\prime}&=y+\Psi(x)y+\left(\frac{y}{\bar{y}}\right)^{p}\mathds{1}(y\geq(1-p)s_{0})\eta_{2}(x),\end{split}

where
\mathds{1}(A)
denotes the characteristic function of the set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{+}
, which is needed to guarantee the positivity of the post-interactions sizes
x^{\prime}
and
y^{\prime}
(see Remark
1
). Here, the functions
\Phi(y)
and
\Psi(x)
, which translate deterministic variations within the predator–prey interactions, are assumed as Holling-type II functional responses

(4)

\displaystyle\Phi(y)

\displaystyle=\beta\frac{y}{1+y},

\displaystyle\Psi(x)

\displaystyle=\gamma\frac{x-\mu}{1+x},

These functions model respectively the predation rate, i.e. the pressure of predators on the population of preys, and the predators’ growth rate due to predation. Here,
\beta,\gamma\in(0,1)
, and
\mu\geq 1
are positive constants measuring the maximal predation rate, the maximal growth rate of predators, and the minimal amount of preys that is needed to induce a growth in the predators’ population. Note in particular that the predation and the growth rates remain smaller than one. Moreover, we require that
\gamma
and
\mu
satisfy the upper bound
\gamma\mu<1
, ensuring that even in the absence of preys the number of predators does not collapse to zero in a single interaction. Such type of predator–prey interactions are in agreement with the pertinent literature on the subject (cf.
Holling (1959)
;
Holling (1966)
;
Beddington (1975)
;
Kuno (1987)
;
Abrams and Ginzburg (2000)
). Furthermore, relations (
3
) also involve stochastic variations through the presence of two independent random variables
\eta_{1}
and
\eta_{2}
, having zero mean and variable bounded variances of the form

(5)

\begin{split}\langle\eta_{1}^{2}(y)\rangle&=\sigma_{1}\frac{y}{1+y},\\[5.69054pt]
\langle\eta_{2}^{2}(y)\rangle&=\sigma_{2}\frac{x}{1+x},\end{split}

where we have denoted with
\langle\cdot\rangle
the integration with respect to the probability densities of
\eta_{1}
and
\eta_{2}
, and with
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}
some constant positive parameters. These random effects are activated only when
x
and
y
are greater than
(1-p)s_{0}
, with
0<s_{0}<1
, and encode the diffusion of preys and predators, which is assumed to depend in a nonlinear way from the presence of the other species. One can think for example of preys diffusing as a way to escape from or avoid predators and, conversely, predators diffusing to reach more preys. The choice once again of a Holling-type II form for the variances of
\eta_{1}
and
\eta_{2}
translates the fact that the ability of one species to diffuse decreases with the increase of the other species’ population, up to a maximum value prescribed by the constants
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
. The nonlinearity of such random effects is further modulated by the parameter
p
, satisfying
0<p\leq 1
(note that in the work
Toscani and Zanella (2024)
, only the case
p=1
has been considered), which tells how strong the species’ diffusion is, based on the reference values
\bar{x}
for the preys and
\bar{y}
for the predators. In particular, for
p<1
, these thresholds define positive bounds on the species’ sizes below which the random effects are more evident, while they are mitigated when
x>\bar{x}
and
y>\bar{y}
. Additionally, as already done for existing models with similar formulations
Pareschi and Toscani (2013)
, one can verify that the choice
p<1
leads to formation of long-time equilibrium distributions with thin tails, while the choice
p=1
produces fat-tails equilibrium distributions. For this reason, and without loss of essential generality, in what follows we will consider either
p=1/2
or
p=1
to quantify the prototypical behavior of the tails that characterize the local equilibria of the kinetic system. Moreover, in order to ease the notations in the remaining part, we shall assume from now on that
\bar{x}=\bar{y}=1
.

Remark 1
.

We highlight that the microscopic transitions (
3
) must map positive values into positive values, while the random variables
\eta_{1}(y)
and
\eta_{2}(x)
may have both positive and negative values, since they are chosen to have zero mean. Clearly, the positivity of the updates
x^{\prime}
and
y^{\prime}
is always guaranteed in the case
p=1
considered in
Toscani and Zanella (2024)
, where the indicator functions are not playing a role. Indeed, if
\eta_{1}(y)
satisfies the negative lower bound
\eta_{1}(y)\geq-(1-\beta)\bar{x}
, then

x^{\prime}\geq x\left(1-\beta+\frac{1}{\bar{x}}\eta_{1}(y)\right)\geq 0.

Similarly, the positivity of
y^{\prime}
would follow by assuming that the random variable
\eta_{2}(x)
satisfies the lower bound
\eta_{2}(x)\geq-(1-\gamma\mu)\bar{y}
. On the contrary, the use of the indicator functions inside the interactions (
3
) cannot be avoided when
p<1
, since otherwise the possibility for
\eta_{1}(y)
and
\eta_{2}(x)
to be negative in an interval of strictly positive measure would not allowed. Indeed, in absence of the indicator function, for a given
p<1
, one would compute

x^{\prime}\geq x\left(1-\beta+\frac{1}{x^{1-p}{\bar{x}}^{p}}\eta_{1}(y)\right),

and infer the
x
-dependent bound

\eta_{1}(y)\geq-(1-\beta)x^{1-p}{\bar{x}}^{p}.

This unpleasant fact can be circumvented as soon as
x\geq(1-p)s_{0}
, since in this case
\eta_{1}(y)
would satisfy the uniform lower bound

\eta_{1}(y)\geq-(1-\beta)[(1-p)s_{0}]^{1-p}{\bar{x}}^{p},

whence the introduction of the indicator functions in (
3
) to cut off all random effects below the positive threshold
(1-p)s_{0}
. Notice that the latter has been chosen of this particular form simply to highlight that the restriction is no longer present when
p=1
, while the coefficient
s_{0}<1
is there to ensure that we can reduce the threshold at will.

Next, to take into account expected growth and shrinking factors of the two populations, we proceed as in
Bisi (2017)
;
Bisi et al. (2009)
and introduce a redistribution-type operator encapsulating birth and death processes. The latter processes can be linked to interactions of the two species with an additional surrounding background, that can be thought of as the resources (for example food, shelters, etc.) offered by the environment. If we denote with
z\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
the variable representing the size of these resources, and with
g(z,t)
and
h(z,t)
their corresponding kinetic densities, the sought redistribution operator acts at the particle level as

(6)

\begin{split}x^{\prime\prime}&=x+\alpha\left(z-\chi x\right),\\[5.69054pt]
y^{\prime\prime}&=y+\nu\left(z-\theta y\right),\end{split}

where
\alpha,\nu>0
are constant interaction coefficients, while
\chi,\theta>-1
define some constant rates of variation for the two populations. In particular, we require that these four parameters satisfy the constraints
\alpha\chi<1
and
\nu\theta<1
, in order to ensure that relations (
6
) preserve the positivity of the group sizes. Furthermore, we assume that the means of the densities
g(z,t)
and
h(z,t)
are explicitly given by

(7)

\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}zg(z,t)\mathrm{d}z=(\chi+1)m_{1}(t),\qquad\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}zh(z,t)\mathrm{d}z=(\theta+1)m_{2}(t).

We highlight that since
\alpha
and
\nu
are assumed to be positive, the presence of the environment always ensures some growth (even if, possibly, very small) of the populations of preys and predators, alike. As we shall see, this property is essential to recover well-behaved equilibrium states for the kinetic model. From a modeling perspective, we interpret this assumption by saying that preys tend to reproduce by having access to food or by using shelters to avoid the predators, while the latter may reproduce by feeding on other types of preys or (when handling, for example) by escaping from predation themselves, taking advantage of the environment to hide. Additionally, the assumption on the means of
g
and
h
convey the idea that the environment has not enough (
-1<\chi,\theta<0
), enough (
\chi=\theta=0
) or more than enough (
\chi,\theta>0
) resources to cover the needs, of food or space, of each population.

2.2.
The kinetic model

Being interested in a statistical description of the dynamics, in the following we will always assume that the number of agents/particles is infinite. The analysis that follows is conducted under the ansatz of the propagation of chaos
Cercignani et al. (1994)
.
Starting from the interaction rules (
3
), we define the system of kinetic equations modeling the evolution of the two distribution functions,
f_{1}(x,t)
and
f_{2}(y,t)
. This evolution is provided by the following Boltzmann-type equations
Pareschi and Toscani (2013)
;
Toscani and Zanella (2024)

(8)

\begin{split}\displaystyle\frac{\partial f_{1}(x,t)}{\partial t}=R_{\chi}^{\alpha}(f_{1})(x,t)+Q_{12}(f_{1},f_{2})(x,t),\\[5.69054pt]
\displaystyle\frac{\partial f_{2}(y,t)}{\partial t}=R_{\theta}^{\nu}(f_{2})(y,t)+Q_{21}(f_{2},f_{1})(y,t),\end{split}

where
R_{\chi}^{\alpha}(f_{1})
and
R_{\theta}^{\nu}(f_{2})
are redistribution-type linear operators describing the balance of the microscopic interactions (
6
), whereas
Q_{12}(f_{1},f_{2})
and
Q_{21}(f_{2},f_{1})
are Boltzmann-type bilinear operators accounting for the microscopic interactions defined by (
3
). The Boltzmann system (
8
) can be conveniently recast in weak form, which corresponds to evaluate the action that the solutions to system (
8
) exercises on the observable quantities, which are in general characterized by two smooth test functions
\varphi(x)
and
\psi(y)
. This leads to the system

(9)

\begin{split}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\varphi(x)f_{1}(x,t)\mathrm{d}x&=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}}\big(\varphi(x^{\prime\prime})-\varphi(x)\big)f_{1}(x,t)g(z,t)\mathrm{d}z\mathrm{d}x\\[5.69054pt]
&\hskip 28.45274pt+\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}}\kappa_{1}(x,y)\big\langle\varphi(x^{\prime})-\varphi(x)\big\rangle f_{1}(x,t)f_{2}(y,t)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y,\\[11.38109pt]
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\psi(y)f_{2}(y,t)\mathrm{d}y&=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}}\big(\psi(y^{\prime\prime})-\psi(y)\big)f_{2}(y,t)h(z,t)\mathrm{d}z\mathrm{d}y\\[5.69054pt]
&\hskip 28.45274pt+\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}}\kappa_{2}(x,y)\big\langle\psi(y^{\prime})-\psi(y)\big\rangle f_{1}(x,t)f_{2}(y,t)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y.\end{split}

In system (
9
),
\kappa_{i}(x,y)>0
,
i=1,2
denote the interaction frequencies that prescribe the rate at which the two species encounter each other. They are assumed of the following form

(10)

\begin{split}\kappa_{1}(x,y)&=\kappa(y)=1+y,\\[5.69054pt]
\kappa_{2}(x,y)&=\kappa(x)=1+x.\end{split}

By this choice, in the spirit of the Lotka–Volterra model, the frequency of interactions in the first equation grows with the number of predators, while in the second equation it grows with the number of preys.

2.3.
Evolution of the observable quantities

In particular, if one assumes
\varphi(x)=x^{r}
and
\psi(y)=y^{r}
, for
r\in\mathbb{N}
, the kinetic equations (
9
) provide the time evolution of the
r
th-order moment of the system. For the aim of this paper, we will focus on the
1
st-order moment and the
2
nd-order moments, defined for each of the two species by (
). The former represents the mean, while the latter provides the energy (and thus also the variance) of the system.

At first, we observe that taking
\varphi(x)=1
and
\psi(y)=1
, one deduces from equations (
9
) the evolution of the densities of the two populations, i.e.

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}f_{1}(x,t)\mathrm{d}x=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}f_{2}(y,t)\mathrm{d}y=0,

ensuring the conservation of the total mass of the system. Since
f_{1}(x,t)
and
f_{2}(y,t)
can be chosen to be probability densities at initial time, this conservation property then ensures that, for all
t\geq 0
,

(11)

\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}f_{1}(x,t)\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}f_{2}(y,t)\mathrm{d}y=1,

meaning that the two distributions remain probability densities during the overall evolution of the system. Indeed, notice that the positivity of solutions to system (
8
) can be readily proved by resorting to standard arguments developed for the classical Boltzmann equation
Cercignani et al. (1994)
, owing to the fact that the redistribution operators
R_{\chi}^{\alpha}
and
R_{\theta}^{\nu}
are positivity-preserving transport-like operators and to the specific form of the interaction frequencies (
10
). This choice allows to split the Boltzmann-like operators
Q_{12}
and
Q_{21}
into a gain and a loss part, where the former is positivity-preserving while the latter is a multiplicative-like operator acting linearly on
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
, and depends on the other species’ distribution only through its zeroth- and first-order moments, via integration in
y
and in
x
, respectively, of the quantities
\kappa(y)f_{2}(y,t)
and
\kappa(x)f_{1}(x,t)
. In particular, these moments are positive since from (
11
) the masses of the populations are conserved and their means solve the Lotka–Volterra system (
12
), as we shall observe in what follows.

If now
\varphi(x)=x
and
\psi(y)=y
, from (
9
) we obtain the coupled evolution of the mean values
m_{1}(t)
and
m_{2}(t)
defined in (
). In particular, we get

(12)

\begin{split}\frac{\mathrm{d}m_{1}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}&=\alpha m_{1}(t)-\beta m_{1}(t)m_{2}(t),\\[5.69054pt]
\frac{\mathrm{d}m_{2}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}&=-(\gamma\mu-\nu)m_{2}(t)+\gamma m_{1}(t)m_{2}(t),\end{split}

which, under the identification
\delta=\gamma\mu-\nu
and assuming that
\nu<\gamma\mu
, corresponds to the classical Lotka–Volterra model (
1
). For any initial data
m_{1}(0)
,
m_{2}(0)>0
, a unique vector solution
\mathbf{m}(t)=(m_{1}(t),m_{2}(t))
exists for all times and remains positive (see for example
Iannelli and Pugliese (2014)
). The solution
\mathbf{m}(t)
evolves, as known, in the phase plane, over closed orbits around the unique non-zero equilibrium point

(13)

\mathbf{m}^{*}=\left(\frac{\delta}{\gamma},\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\right).

In particular, the function
H(m_{1}(t),m_{2}(t))=\gamma m_{1}(t)-\delta\log m_{1}(t)+\beta m_{2}(t)-\alpha\log m_{2}(t)
is a conserved quantity of the system, that characterizes the shape of such orbits spanned by the preys and the predators in the phase plane. The solutions
m_{1}(t)
and
m_{2}(t)
are thus periodic and bounded from above and below. For future use, we define these bounds via some positive constants
\underline{c}_{1}
,
\overline{c}_{1}
and
\underline{c}_{2}
,
\overline{c}_{2}
such that for any
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}

(14)

\begin{split}&\underline{c}_{1}\leq m_{1}(t)\leq\overline{c}_{1},\\[5.69054pt]
&\underline{c}_{2}\leq m_{2}(t)\leq\overline{c}_{2}.\end{split}

We conclude by studying the evolution of the variances (
) of
f_{1}(x,t)
and
f_{2}(y,t)
, which are obtained by inserting
\varphi(x)=(x-m_{1}(t))^{2}
and
\psi(y)=(y-m_{2}(t))^{2}
in (
9
). While the means of the two distributions characterize the orbits of the Lotka–Volterra equations (
12
), their variances account for the deviations around these orbits. We distinguish the case
p=1
, for which we obtain

(15)

\begin{split}\frac{\mathrm{d}v_{1}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}&=-[(2\beta-\sigma_{1})m_{2}(t)+2\alpha\chi]v_{1}(t)+\sigma_{1}m_{1}^{2}(t)m_{2}(t)+\alpha^{2}\chi^{2}(v_{1}(t)+m_{1}^{2}(t))\\[5.69054pt]
&\hskip 28.45274pt-2\alpha^{2}\chi(\chi+1)m_{1}^{2}(t)+\alpha^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}z^{2}g(z,t)\mathrm{d}z+\beta^{2}(v_{1}(t)+m_{1}^{2}(t))\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\frac{y^{2}}{1+y}f_{2}(y,t)\mathrm{d}y,\\[11.38109pt]
\frac{\mathrm{d}v_{2}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}&=-[2\gamma(\mu-m_{1}(t))-\sigma_{2}m_{1}(t)+2\nu\theta]v_{2}(t)+\sigma_{2}m_{1}(t)m_{2}^{2}(t)+\nu^{2}\theta^{2}(v_{2}(t)+m_{2}^{2}(t))\\[5.69054pt]
&\hskip 28.45274pt-2\nu^{2}(\theta+1)m_{2}^{2}(t)+\nu^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}z^{2}h(z,t)\mathrm{d}z+\gamma^{2}(v_{2}(t)+m_{2}^{2}(t))\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\frac{(x-\mu)^{2}}{1+x}f_{1}(x,t)\mathrm{d}x,\end{split}

from the case
p=1/2
that gives

(16)

\begin{split}\frac{\mathrm{d}v_{1}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}&=-2(\beta m_{2}+\alpha\chi)v_{1}(t)+\sigma_{1}m_{1}(t)m_{2}(t)+\alpha^{2}\chi^{2}(v_{1}(t)+m_{1}^{2}(t))\\[5.69054pt]
&\hskip 42.67912pt-2\alpha^{2}\chi(\chi+1)m_{1}^{2}(t)+\alpha^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}z^{2}g(z,t)\mathrm{d}z\\[5.69054pt]
&\hskip 71.13188pt+\beta^{2}(v_{1}(t)+m_{1}^{2}(t))\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\frac{y^{2}}{1+y}f_{2}(y,t)\mathrm{d}y+\int_{0}^{s_{0}/2}xf_{1}(x,t)dx,\\[11.38109pt]
\frac{\mathrm{d}v_{2}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}&=-2[\gamma(\mu-m_{1}(t))+\nu\theta]v_{2}(t)+\sigma_{2}m_{1}(t)m_{2}(t)+\nu^{2}\theta^{2}(v_{2}(t)+m_{2}^{2}(t))\\[5.69054pt]
&\hskip 42.67912pt-2\nu^{2}\theta(\theta+1)m_{2}^{2}(t)+\nu^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}z^{2}h(z,t)\mathrm{d}z\\[5.69054pt]
&\hskip 71.13188pt+\gamma^{2}(v_{2}(t)+m_{2}^{2}(t))\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\frac{(x-\mu)^{2}}{1+x}f_{1}(x,t)\mathrm{d}x+\int_{0}^{s_{0}/2}yf_{2}(y,t)dy.\end{split}

It is worth pointing out that the integrals
\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}z^{2}g(z,t)\mathrm{d}z
and
\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}z^{2}h(z,t)\mathrm{d}z
are the
2
nd-order moments of the densities
g(z,t)
and
h(z,t)
characterizing the evolution of the background environment, whose means have been assumed to have the explicit form (
7
). Therefore, these integrals can be computed once also the energies of
g(z,t)
and
h(z,t)
are explicitly assigned. For instance, if the densities were taken to be Dirac delta functions, namely a situation where the resources provided by the environment have zero variance and are thus kept fixed, then, according to hypotheses (
7
) one would have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}z^{2}g(z,t)\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=\left[(\chi+1)m_{1}(t)\right]^{2},

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}z^{2}h(z,t)\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=\left[(\theta+1)m_{2}(t)\right]^{2}.

Therefore, unlike what happens for the evolution of the means
m_{1}(t)
and
m_{2}(t)
, we see from systems (
15
) and (
16
) that it is hard to recover a closed evolution for the variances of the species’ distributions (nor for their higher order moments). Hence, to simplify the study of the behavior of the solutions to the Boltzmann-type system (
8
), in the next section, we propose a reduced complexity approach by resorting to a simplified model of Fokker–Planck-type which allows for an explicit closed evolution of the principal moments, as well as of an explicit evaluation of the local equilibria associated with the kinetic system.

3.
Derivation of a coupled Fokker–Planck system

3.1.
Scaling of the redistribution operators

In this section, we show that for a certain range of the parameters characterizing the kinetic system (
9
), usually referred to as the
quasi-invariant collision regime
, it is possible to derive a reduced model of Fokker–Planck type
Pareschi and Toscani (2013)
. The Fokker–Planck description in many cases facilitates the analytical study of the shape of the emerging equilibrium distributions. We refer for example to
Dimarco et al. (2021)
;
Furioli et al. (2019)
;
Furioli et al. (2017)
for further details on this topic.

Let us first concentrate on the redistribution operators
R_{\chi}^{\alpha}(f_{1})
and
R_{\theta}^{\nu}(f_{2})
, resulting from the particle dynamics (
6
). A particle approach to the redistribution dynamics has been studied in
Bisi (2017)
. By denoting with
\varepsilon\ll 1
a suitable scaling parameter, we may introduce a new timescale of observation
\tau=\varepsilon t
, together with the rescaled distributions
f_{1}^{\varepsilon}(x,\tau)=f_{1}(x,\tau/\varepsilon)
and
f_{2}^{\varepsilon}(x,\tau/\varepsilon)=f_{2}(x,\tau/\varepsilon)
for the two populations, as well as
g^{\varepsilon}(z,\tau)=g(z,\tau/\varepsilon)
and
h^{\varepsilon}(z,\tau)=h(z,\tau/\varepsilon)
for those characterizing the environment, obtaining the temporal evolution

(17)

\begin{split}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\varphi(x)f^{\varepsilon}_{1}(x,\tau)\mathrm{d}x&=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}}\big(\varphi(x^{\prime\prime})-\varphi(x)\big)f_{1}^{\varepsilon}(x,\tau)g^{\varepsilon}(z,\tau)\mathrm{d}z\mathrm{d}x,\\[5.69054pt]
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\psi(y)f^{\varepsilon}_{2}(y,\tau)\mathrm{d}y&=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}}\big(\psi(y^{\prime\prime})-\psi(y)\big)f_{2}^{\varepsilon}(y,\tau)h^{\varepsilon}(z,\tau)\mathrm{d}z\mathrm{d}y.\end{split}

Hence, considering the following scaling of the parameters

\alpha\to\varepsilon\alpha,\qquad\nu\to\varepsilon\nu,

the interactions (
6
) are quasi-invariant and we may thus perform the Taylor expansion

\begin{split}\varphi(x^{\prime\prime})-\varphi(x)=(x^{\prime\prime}-x)\varphi^{\prime}(x)+\frac{1}{2}(x^{\prime\prime}-x)^{2}\varphi^{\prime\prime}(\hat{x}),\\[5.69054pt]
\psi(y^{\prime\prime})-\psi(y)=(y^{\prime\prime}-y)\psi^{\prime}(y)+\frac{1}{2}(y^{\prime\prime}-y)^{2}\psi^{\prime\prime}(\hat{y}),\end{split}

with
\hat{x}\in(\min\{x,x^{\prime\prime}\},\max\{x,x^{\prime\prime}\})
and
\hat{y}\in(\min\{y,y^{\prime\prime}\},\max\{y,y^{\prime\prime}\})
. Plugging the above expansions into (
17
), we then obtain, for any sufficiently smooth test functions
\varphi(x)
and
\psi(y)
,

\begin{split}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\varphi(x)f_{1}^{\varepsilon}(x,\tau)\mathrm{d}x&=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}}\alpha(z-\chi x)\varphi^{\prime}(x)f_{1}^{\varepsilon}(x,\tau)g^{\varepsilon}(z,\tau)\mathrm{d}z\mathrm{d}x+\mathcal{R}_{\varphi}(f_{1}^{\varepsilon},g^{\varepsilon})(x,\tau),\\[5.69054pt]
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\psi(y)f_{2}^{\varepsilon}(y,\tau)\mathrm{d}y&=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}}\nu(z-\theta y)\psi^{\prime}(y)f^{\varepsilon}_{2}(y,\tau)h^{\varepsilon}(z,\tau)\mathrm{d}z\mathrm{d}y+\mathcal{R}_{\psi}(f_{2}^{\varepsilon},h^{\varepsilon})(y,\tau),\end{split}

with the integral remainders
\mathcal{R}_{\varphi}
and
\mathcal{R}_{\psi}
given by

\begin{split}\mathcal{R}_{\varphi}(f_{1}^{\varepsilon},g^{\varepsilon})(x,\tau)&=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}}\alpha^{2}(z-\chi x)^{2}\varphi^{\prime\prime}(\hat{x})f_{1}^{\varepsilon}(x,\tau)g^{\varepsilon}(z,\tau)\mathrm{d}z\mathrm{d}x,\\[5.69054pt]
\mathcal{R}_{\psi}(f_{2}^{\varepsilon},h^{\varepsilon})(y,\tau)&=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}}\nu^{2}(z-\theta y)^{2}\psi^{\prime\prime}(\hat{y})f_{2}^{\varepsilon}(y,\tau)h^{\varepsilon}(z,\tau)\mathrm{d}z\mathrm{d}y.\end{split}

Therefore, observing that

\left|\mathcal{R}_{\varphi}(f_{1}^{\varepsilon},g^{\varepsilon})\right|\underset{\varepsilon\to 0}{\longrightarrow}0,\qquad\left|\mathcal{R}_{\psi}(f_{2}^{\varepsilon},h^{\varepsilon})\right|\underset{\varepsilon\to 0}{\longrightarrow}0,

in the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
the distributions
f^{\varepsilon}_{1}(x,\tau)
and
f^{\varepsilon}_{2}(y,\tau)
formally converge to some
f_{1}(x,\tau)
and
f_{2}(y,\tau)
, which are solutions to the system

\begin{split}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\varphi(x)f_{1}(x,\tau)\mathrm{d}x&=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}}\alpha(z-\chi x)\varphi^{\prime}(x)f_{1}(x,\tau)g(z,\tau)\mathrm{d}z\mathrm{d}x,\\[5.69054pt]
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\psi(y)f_{2}(y,\tau)\mathrm{d}y&=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}}\nu(z-\theta y)\psi^{\prime}(y)f_{2}(y,\tau)h(z,\tau)\mathrm{d}z\mathrm{d}y.\end{split}

Integrating back by parts the resulting equations and recalling (
7
), we recover the following transport equations

(18)

\begin{split}\frac{\partial f_{1}(x,\tau)}{\partial\tau}&=\alpha\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\big((\chi x-(\chi+1)m_{1}(\tau))f_{1}(x,\tau)\big)=\bar{R}_{\chi}^{\alpha}(f_{1})(x,\tau),\\[5.69054pt]
\frac{\partial f_{2}(y,\tau)}{\partial\tau}&=\nu\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\big((\theta y-(\theta+1)m_{2}(\tau))f_{2}(y,\tau)\big)=\bar{R}_{\theta}^{\nu}(f_{2})(y,\tau),\end{split}

coupled with the boundary conditions

\big(\chi x-(\chi+1)m_{1}(\tau)\big)f_{1}(x,\tau)\Big|_{x=0,+\infty}=0,\quad\big(\theta y-(\theta+1)m_{2}(\tau)\big)f_{2}(y,\tau)\Big|_{y=0,+\infty}=0.

We highlight that
\bar{R}_{\chi}^{\alpha}
and
\bar{R}_{\theta}^{\nu}
correspond exactly to the redistribution operators introduced in
Bisi et al. (2009)
. In particular, in view of the above no-flux boundary conditions, one finds

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\bar{R}_{\chi}^{\alpha}(f_{1})(x,\tau)\mathrm{d}x=0,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\bar{R}_{\theta}^{\nu}(f_{2})(y,\tau)\mathrm{d}y=0,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}x\bar{R}_{\chi}^{\alpha}(f_{1})(x,\tau)\mathrm{d}x=\alpha m_{1}(\tau),

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}y\bar{R}_{\theta}^{\nu}(f_{2})(y,\tau)\mathrm{d}y=\nu m_{2}(\tau),

meaning that the redistribution operators preserve the total masses of the two populations, while increasing the mean sizes of the preys by a factor
\alpha>0
and of the predators by a factor
\nu>0
. The new parameters
\chi,\theta>-1
define the nature of the redistribution. In particular, for positive values of these parameters, the redistribution operators promote the growth of small populations and simultaneously penalize very large populations,
while for
-1<\chi,\theta<0
, the redistribution mechanism favors both large and small populations at the expense of medium-sized ones. We can obtain a further insight on this operator by solving the homogeneous equations in (
18
). Starting from the initial conditions
f_{1}(x,0)=f_{1}^{\mathrm{in}}(x)
and
f_{2}(y,0)=f_{2}^{\mathrm{in}}(y)
, with means
m_{1}(0)>0
and
m_{2}(0)>0
,
the solutions to (
18
) read

\begin{split}f_{1}(x,\tau)&=e^{\alpha\chi\tau}f_{1}^{\mathrm{in}}\left(e^{\alpha\chi\tau}x-m_{1}(0)(e^{\alpha(\chi+1)\tau}-1)\right),\\[5.69054pt]
f_{2}(y,\tau)&=e^{\nu\theta\tau}f_{2}^{\mathrm{in}}\left(e^{\nu\theta\tau}y-m_{2}(0)(e^{\nu(\theta+1)\tau}-1)\right).\end{split}

Hence, if
f_{1}^{\mathrm{in}}(x)\equiv 0
, for any
x<0
the corresponding solution
f_{1}(x,\tau)
satisfies

f_{1}(x,\tau)\equiv 0\qquad\textrm{for all}\quad x<m_{1}(0)\frac{e^{\alpha(\chi+1)\tau}-1}{e^{\alpha\chi\tau}},

and similarly, when
f_{2}^{\mathrm{in}}(y)\equiv 0
, for any
y<0
the distribution
f_{2}(y,\tau)
will be such that

f_{2}(y,\tau)\equiv 0\qquad\textrm{for all}\quad y<m_{2}(0)\frac{e^{\nu(\theta+1)\tau}-1}{e^{\nu\theta\tau}},

implying that no populations with sizes below the obtained thresholds can emerge at any given time
\tau\geq 0
.

3.2.
Scaling of the full system

Next, we focus on the derivation of a Fokker–Planck model based on the interaction dynamics (
3
) leading to the Boltzmann system (
9
). Introducing once again a small parameter
\varepsilon\ll 1
and a corresponding timescale
\tau=\varepsilon t
, we consider a quasi-invariant regime of the interactions (
3
), corresponding to very small variations that are obtained by properly rescaling all relevant parameters in terms of
\varepsilon
. More precisely, the parameters
\beta
and
\gamma
characterizing the deterministic variations (
4
) are rescaled as

\beta\to\varepsilon\beta,\qquad\gamma\to\varepsilon\gamma,

while the diffusion constants
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}
and the parameter
s_{0}
appearing in the random effects (
5
) are rescaled as

\sigma_{1}\to\varepsilon\sigma_{1},\qquad\sigma_{2}\to\varepsilon\sigma_{2},\qquad s_{0}\to\varepsilon.

Consequently, from the microscopic interactions (
3
) one computes the following Taylor expansions

\begin{split}\left\langle\varphi(x^{\prime})-\varphi(x)\right\rangle=(x^{\prime}-x)\varphi^{\prime}(x)+\frac{1}{2}(x^{\prime}-x)^{2}\varphi^{\prime\prime}(x)+\frac{1}{6}(x^{\prime}-x)^{3}\varphi^{\prime\prime\prime}(\hat{x}),\\[5.69054pt]
\left\langle\psi(y^{\prime})-\psi(y)\right\rangle=(y^{\prime}-y)\psi^{\prime}(y)+\frac{1}{2}(y^{\prime}-y)^{2}\psi^{\prime\prime}(y)+\frac{1}{6}(y^{\prime}-y)^{3}\psi^{\prime\prime\prime}(\hat{y}),\end{split}

where
\hat{x}\in(\min\{x,x^{\prime}\},\max\{x,x^{\prime}\})
and
\hat{y}\in(\min\{y,y^{\prime}\},\max\{y,y^{\prime}\})
. Plugging the above expansions
into (
9
), we then get

(19)

\begin{split}&\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\varphi(x)f_{1}^{\varepsilon}(x,\tau)\mathrm{d}x=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}}\kappa_{1}(x,y)\big(-\Phi(y)x\varphi^{\prime}(x)\big)f_{1}^{\varepsilon}(x,\tau)f_{2}^{\varepsilon}(y,\tau)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y\\[5.69054pt]
&\hskip 85.35826pt+\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}}\kappa_{1}(x,y)\left[\left(\varepsilon^{2}\beta^{2}\Phi^{2}(y)x^{2}+\varepsilon x^{2p}\frac{\sigma_{1}y}{1+y}\right)\varphi^{\prime\prime}(x)\right]f_{1}^{\varepsilon}(x,\tau)f_{2}^{\varepsilon}(y,\tau)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y\\[17.07164pt]
&\hskip 85.35826pt+\mathcal{R}_{\varphi}(f_{1}^{\varepsilon},f_{2}^{\varepsilon})+\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{\varphi}(f_{1}^{\varepsilon},f_{2}^{\varepsilon}),\end{split}

and

(20)

\begin{split}&\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\psi(y)f_{2}^{\varepsilon}(y,\tau)\mathrm{d}y=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}}\kappa_{2}(x,y)\big(\Psi(x)y\psi^{\prime}(y)\big)f_{1}^{\varepsilon}(x,\tau)f_{2}^{\varepsilon}(y,\tau)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y\\[5.69054pt]
&\hskip 85.35826pt+\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}}\kappa_{2}(x,y)\left[\left(\varepsilon^{2}\gamma^{2}\Psi^{2}(x)y^{2}+\varepsilon y^{2p}\frac{\sigma_{2}x}{1+x}\right)\psi^{\prime\prime}(y)\right]f_{1}^{\varepsilon}(x,\tau)f_{2}^{\varepsilon}(y,\tau)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y\\[17.07164pt]
&\hskip 85.35826pt+\mathcal{R}_{\psi}(f_{1}^{\varepsilon},f_{2}^{\varepsilon})+\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{\psi}(f_{1}^{\varepsilon},f_{2}^{\varepsilon}).\end{split}

In (
19
) and (
20
) the remainder terms
\mathcal{R}_{\varphi}(f_{1}^{\varepsilon},f_{2}^{\varepsilon})
and
\mathcal{R}_{\psi}(f_{1}^{\varepsilon},f_{2}^{\varepsilon})
read

\begin{split}&\mathcal{R}_{\varphi}(f_{1}^{\varepsilon},f_{2}^{\varepsilon})(x,\tau)=\frac{1}{6\varepsilon}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}}\kappa_{1}(x,y)\big\langle(-\Phi(y)x+x^{p}\eta_{1}(y))^{3}\big\rangle\varphi^{\prime\prime\prime}(\hat{x})f_{1}^{\varepsilon}(x,\tau)f_{2}^{\varepsilon}(y,\tau)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y,\\[11.38109pt]
&\mathcal{R}_{\psi}(f_{1}^{\varepsilon},f_{2}^{\varepsilon})(y,\tau)=\frac{1}{6\varepsilon}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}}\kappa_{2}(x,y)\big\langle(\Psi(x)y+y^{p}\eta_{2}(x))^{3}\big\rangle\psi^{\prime\prime\prime}(\hat{y})f_{1}^{\varepsilon}(x,\tau)f_{2}^{\varepsilon}(y,\tau)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y,\end{split}

while the other remainders
\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{\varphi}(f_{1}^{\varepsilon},f_{2}^{\varepsilon})
and
\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{\psi}(f_{1}^{\varepsilon},f_{2}^{\varepsilon})
are defined as

\begin{split}\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{\varphi}(f_{1}^{\varepsilon},f_{2}^{\varepsilon})=&\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}}\kappa_{1}(x,y)\mathds{1}(0\leq x\leq(1-p)\varepsilon)x^{2p}\left\langle\eta_{1}^{2}(y)\right\rangle\varphi^{\prime\prime}(\hat{x})f_{1}^{\varepsilon}(x,t)f_{2}^{\varepsilon}(y,t)dxdy\\[5.69054pt]
&+\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}}\kappa_{1}(x,y)\mathds{1}(0\leq x\leq(1-p)\varepsilon)x^{3p}\left\langle 3\eta_{1}^{2}(y)+\eta_{1}^{3}(y)\right\rangle\varphi^{\prime\prime\prime}(\hat{x})f_{1}^{\varepsilon}(x,t)f_{2}^{\varepsilon}(y,t)dxdy\\[11.38109pt]
\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{\psi}(f_{1}^{\varepsilon},f_{2}^{\varepsilon})=&\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}}\kappa_{2}(x,y)\mathds{1}(0\leq y\leq(1-p)\varepsilon)y^{2p}\left\langle\eta_{2}^{2}(x)\right\rangle\psi^{\prime\prime}(\hat{y})f_{1}^{\varepsilon}(x,t)f_{2}^{\varepsilon}(y,t)dxdy\\[5.69054pt]
&+\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}}\kappa_{2}(x,y)\mathds{1}(0\leq y\leq(1-p)\varepsilon)y^{3p}\left\langle 3\eta_{2}^{2}(x)+\eta_{2}^{3}(x)\right\rangle\psi^{\prime\prime\prime}(\hat{y})f_{1}^{\varepsilon}(x,t)f_{2}^{\varepsilon}(y,t)dxdy,\end{split}

and take into account the presence of the indicator functions in the random effects of the elementary interactions (
3
). These terms disappear in the case
p=1
, but whenever
p<1
they are different from zero on a subset of measure
(1-p)\varepsilon\ll 1
with respect to
(x,y)
.

Now, assuming that the random variables
\eta_{1}(y)
and
\eta_{2}(x)
have bounded third order moments, i.e.
\left\langle|\eta_{1}(y)|^{3}\right\rangle<+\infty
and
\left\langle|\eta_{2}(x)|^{3}\right\rangle<+\infty
, then we can write
\eta_{1}=\sqrt{\dfrac{\sigma_{1}y}{1+y}}\hat{\eta}_{1}
and
\eta_{2}=\sqrt{\dfrac{\sigma_{2}x}{1+x}}\hat{\eta}_{2}
, with
\left\langle\hat{\eta}_{1}\right\rangle=\left\langle\hat{\eta}_{2}\right\rangle=0
and
\left\langle\hat{\eta}_{1}^{2}\right\rangle=\left\langle\hat{\eta}_{2}^{2}\right\rangle=1
. Therefore, observing that

\left|\mathcal{R}_{\varphi}(f_{1}^{\varepsilon},f_{2}^{\varepsilon})\right|\approx\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{2}+\sqrt{\varepsilon},\qquad\left|\mathcal{R}_{\psi}(f_{1}^{\varepsilon},f_{2}^{\varepsilon})\right|\approx\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{2}+\sqrt{\varepsilon},

and also

|\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{\varphi}(f_{1}^{\varepsilon},f_{2}^{\varepsilon})|\approx\varepsilon^{1+2p},\qquad|\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{\psi}(f_{1}^{\varepsilon},f_{2}^{\varepsilon})|\approx\varepsilon^{1+2p},

in the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
all remainder terms vanish and the distributions
f_{1}^{\varepsilon}(x,\tau)
and
f_{2}^{\varepsilon}(y,\tau)
formally converge to some
f_{1}(x,\tau)
and
f_{2}(y,\tau)
which are solutions to the limit system of (
19
)–(
20
), being

\begin{split}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\varphi(x)f_{1}(x,\tau)\mathrm{d}x&=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}}\beta xy\varphi^{\prime}(x)f_{1}(x,\tau)f_{2}(y,\tau)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y\\[5.69054pt]
&\hskip 28.45274pt+\frac{\sigma_{1}}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}}x^{2p}y\varphi^{\prime\prime}(x)f_{1}(x,\tau)f_{2}(y,\tau)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y,\\[11.38109pt]
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\psi(y)f_{2}(y,\tau)\mathrm{d}y&=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}}\gamma(x-\mu)y\psi(y)^{\prime}f_{1}(x,\tau)f_{2}(y,\tau)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y\\[5.69054pt]
&\hskip 28.45274pt+\frac{\sigma_{2}}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}}y^{2p}x\psi^{\prime\prime}(y)f_{1}(x,\tau)f_{2}(y,\tau)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y.\end{split}

Integrating back by parts, the previous formulation writes in strong form as

(21)

\begin{split}\frac{\partial f_{1}(x,\tau)}{\partial\tau}&=\frac{\sigma_{1}m_{2}(\tau)}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}\left[x^{2p}f_{1}(x,\tau)\right]+\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\big(\beta m_{2}(\tau)xf_{1}(x,\tau)\big),\\[5.69054pt]
\frac{\partial f_{2}(y,\tau)}{\partial\tau}&=\frac{\sigma_{2}m_{1}(\tau)}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial y^{2}}\left[y^{2p}f_{2}(y,\tau)\right]+\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left[\gamma(\mu-m_{1}(\tau))yf_{2}(y,\tau)\right]\end{split}

Finally, restoring the time variable
t\geq 0
and merging the contributions (
18
) and (
21
) coming from the redistribution and the Boltzmann operators, we end up with the following system of Fokker–Planck-type equations

(22)

\begin{split}\frac{\partial f_{1}(x,t)}{\partial t}&=\frac{\sigma_{1}m_{2}(t)}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}\left[x^{2p}f_{1}(x,t)\right]+\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\big(((\beta m_{2}(t)+\alpha\chi)x-\alpha(\chi+1)m_{1}(t))f_{1}(x,t)\big),\\[17.07164pt]
\frac{\partial f_{2}(y,t)}{\partial t}&=\frac{\sigma_{2}m_{1}(t)}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial y^{2}}\left[y^{2p}f_{2}(y,t)\right]+\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\big((\gamma(\mu-m_{1}(t))y+\nu\theta y-\nu(\theta+1)m_{2}(t))f_{2}(y,t)\big),\end{split}

for any
0<p\leq 1
, complemented by the no-flux boundary conditions

\begin{split}&\frac{\sigma_{1}m_{2}(t)}{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left[x^{2p}f_{1}(x,t)\right]+((\beta m_{2}(t)+\alpha\chi)x-\alpha(\chi+1)m_{1}(t))f_{1}(x,t)\Big|_{x=0,+\infty}=0,\\[5.69054pt]
&x^{2p}f_{1}(x,t)\Big|_{x=0,+\infty}=0,\\[17.07164pt]
&\frac{\sigma_{2}m_{1}(t)}{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left[y^{2p}f_{2}(y,t)\right]+(\gamma(\mu-m_{1}(t))y+\nu\theta y-\nu(\theta+1)m_{2}(t))f_{2}(y,t)\Big|_{y=0,+\infty}=0,\\[5.69054pt]
&y^{2p}f_{2}(y,t)\Big|_{y=0,+\infty}=0.\end{split}

Well-posedness of the above system can be assessed by applying the results from the work of Le Bris and Lions
Le Bris and Lions (2008)
.

3.3.
Evolution of the observable quantities

It is immediate to show that the evolution of the first order moments of the solutions to system (
22
) is consistent with the one obtained from the Boltzmann-type equations (
9
), so that the means
m_{1}(t)
and
m_{2}(t)
of the two populations are still solutions to the Lotka–Volterra system (
12
). The added value in working with this simplified Fokker–Planck formulation lies in the fact that we can now recover a closed evolution of the higher moments of the solutions to (
22
).
In particular, by concentrating on the two prototypical cases
p=1
and
p=1/2
, we can determine the explicit dynamics of the variances
v_{1}(t)
and
v_{2}(t)
. For
p=1
we obtain

(23)

\begin{split}&\frac{\mathrm{d}v_{1}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}=-2\big(\big(\beta-\sigma_{1}/2\big)m_{2}(t)+\alpha\chi\big)v_{1}(t)+\sigma_{1}m_{1}^{2}(t)m_{2}(t),\\[5.69054pt]
&\frac{\mathrm{d}v_{2}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}=-2\big(\gamma(\mu-(1-\sigma_{2}/2\gamma)m_{1}(t))+\nu\theta\big)v_{2}(t)+\sigma_{2}m_{1}(t)m_{2}^{2}(t),\end{split}

while choosing
p=1/2
leads to

(24)

\begin{split}&\frac{\mathrm{d}v_{1}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}=-2(\beta m_{2}(t)+\alpha\chi)v_{1}(t)+\sigma_{1}m_{1}(t)m_{2}(t),\\[5.69054pt]
&\frac{\mathrm{d}v_{2}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}=-2\big(\gamma(\mu-m_{1}(t))+\nu\theta\big)v_{2}(t)+\sigma_{2}m_{1}(t)m_{2}(t).\end{split}

We highlight that these two systems governing the evolution of the variances for the Fokker–Planck equations (
22
) are in fact coherent with the ones determined from the original Boltzmann equations (
9
), in the quasi-invariant regime of parameters considered. Furthermore, we notice that system (
23
) loses stability compared to system (
24
), due to the diffusion parameter
\sigma_{1}>0
reducing the region of parameters in which the variances remain bounded. A similar effect will be also observed in the following part, where we shall see that the parameter
p
determines the structure of the equilibrium states, producing fat-tails and thin-tails distributions respectively in the cases
p=1
and
p=1/2
. This loss of higher order moments when
p=1
will eventually lead us to consider only the more stable case
p=1/2
.

3.4.
Study of the local equilibria

A further property of the Fokker–Planck description is related to the possibility of obtaining the explicit form of the equilibria, which are determined by setting the left-hand sides of equations (
22
) equal to zero. In the case of the classical Fokker–Planck equations for gases
Furioli et al. (2017)
;
Pareschi and Toscani (2013)
, the knowledge of the uniform-in-time steady state and of the (exponential) rate of relaxation of the solution toward it allows to conclude that the distribution solving the Fokker–Planck system under study is well-represented by the characteristics of its corresponding equilibrium. However, the situation is quite different here, because system (
22
) does not possess stable uniform steady states, but only time-dependent oscillating local equilibria, and it is not clear whether the characteristics of these local equilibria do represent or not a good approximation of the real solutions to the system, even for intermediate and for large times. This is a challenging question which we try to partially answer here, but that it could deserve further investigations. We mention the works
Alasio et al. (2020)
;
Matthes and Parsch (2024)
for related settings in cross-diffusion systems.

The local equilibrium states (or
quasi-equilibria
) of the Fokker–Planck system (
22
) are all vector distribution functions
\mathbf{f}^{\mathrm{eq}}(\mathbf{x},t)=(f_{1}^{\mathrm{eq}}(x,t),f_{2}^{\mathrm{eq}}(y,t))
solving

(25)

\begin{split}&\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\big(x^{2p}f_{1}^{\mathrm{eq}}(x,t)\big)+\frac{2}{\sigma_{1}m_{2}(t)}\big(((\beta m_{2}(t)+\alpha\chi)x-\alpha(\chi+1)m_{1}(t))f_{1}^{\mathrm{eq}}(x,t)\big)=0,\\[5.69054pt]
&\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\big(y^{2p}f_{2}^{\mathrm{eq}}(y,t)\big)+\frac{2}{\sigma_{2}m_{1}(t)}\big((\gamma(\mu-m_{1}(t))+\nu\theta)y-\nu(\theta+1)m_{2}(t))f_{2}^{\mathrm{eq}}(y,t)\big)=0,\end{split}

for any
t>0
and
x,y\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. The above system can be conveniently recast as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\log f_{1}^{\mathrm{eq}}(x,t)=\frac{-\frac{2}{\sigma_{1}m_{2}(t)}((\beta m_{2}(t)+\alpha\chi)x-\alpha(\chi+1)m_{1}(t))-2px^{2p-1}}{x^{2p}},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\log f_{2}^{\mathrm{eq}}(y,t)=\frac{-\frac{2}{\sigma_{2}m_{1}(t)}(\gamma(\mu-m_{1}(t))y-\nu m_{2}(t))-2py^{2p-1}}{y^{2p}},

and, reducing once again to the study of the main representative cases
p=1
and
p=1/2
, by simple computations we find the following explicit form of the equilibrium states, for
p=1

\displaystyle f_{1}^{\mathrm{eq}}(x,t)

\displaystyle=C_{1}(t)x^{-\left(2\frac{(\beta+\sigma_{1}/2)m_{2}(t)+\alpha\chi}{\sigma_{1}m_{2}(t)}+1\right)}\exp\left\{-\frac{2\alpha(\chi+1)m_{1}(t)}{\sigma_{1}m_{2}(t)}\frac{1}{x}\right\},

\displaystyle f_{2}^{\mathrm{eq}}(y,t)

\displaystyle=C_{2}(t)y^{-\left(2\frac{\gamma(\mu-(1-\sigma_{2}/2\gamma)m_{1}(t)+\nu\theta}{\sigma_{2}m_{1}(t)}+1\right)}\exp\left\{-\frac{2\nu(\theta+1)m_{2}(t)}{\sigma_{2}m_{1}(t)}\frac{1}{y}\right\},

and for
p=1/2

(26)

\begin{split}f_{1}^{\mathrm{eq}}(x,t)&=C_{1}(t)x^{\frac{2\alpha(\chi+1)}{\sigma_{1}}\frac{m_{1}(t)}{m_{2}(t)}-1}\exp\left\{-\frac{2}{\sigma_{1}m_{2}(t)}(\beta m_{2}(t)+\alpha\chi)x\right\},\\[5.69054pt]
f_{2}^{\mathrm{eq}}(y,t)&=C_{2}(t)y^{\frac{2\nu(\theta+1)}{\sigma_{2}}\frac{m_{2}(t)}{m_{1}(t)}-1}\exp\left\{-\frac{2}{\sigma_{2}m_{1}(t)}(\gamma(\mu-m_{1}(t)+\nu\theta)y\right\},\end{split}

where
C_{1}(t)
,
C_{2}(t)>0
are normalization coefficients depending on the means
m_{1}(t)
and
m_{2}(t)
. In particular, one recognizes the structure of the inverse Gamma distribution emerging for
p=1
and that of the Gamma distribution for
p=1/2
, provided of course that we appropriately bound the parameters of the problem in order to ensure that the exponents remain positive. However, since an inverse Gamma distribution does not possess finite moments of any order, from now on we shall focus our attention on the more interesting case
p=1/2
, corresponding to thin-tails local equilibrium states.

Let us then rewrite, for
p=1/2
, the equilibrium distributions (
26
) as

f_{1}^{\mathrm{eq}}(x,t)=C_{1}(t)x^{a_{1}(t)-1}e^{-b_{1}(t)x},\qquad f_{2}^{\mathrm{eq}}(y,t)=C_{2}(t)y^{a_{2}(t)-1}e^{-b_{2}(t)y},

where we have denoted with

\begin{split}&a_{1}(t)=\frac{2\alpha(\chi+1)}{\sigma_{1}}\frac{m_{1}(t)}{m_{2}(t)},\qquad b_{1}(t)=\frac{2}{\sigma_{1}m_{2}(t)}(\beta m_{2}(t)+\alpha\chi),\\[5.69054pt]
&a_{2}(t)=\frac{2\nu(\theta+1)}{\sigma_{2}}\frac{m_{2}(t)}{m_{1}(t)},\qquad b_{2}(t)=\frac{2}{\sigma_{2}m_{1}(t)}(\gamma(\mu-m_{1}(t))+\nu\theta),\end{split}

the four exponents of interest. To recover a Gamma distribution, we must then guarantee that the latter are positive. Assuming that we have fixed the main parameters
\alpha
,
\beta
,
\delta
,
\gamma>0
of the Lotka–Volterra system (
12
), together with the interaction coefficient
\nu>0
and the lowest group of preys
\mu\geq 1
which define
\delta=\gamma\mu-\nu
, the positivity of the above exponents depends on the bounds (
14
) for the means
m_{1}(t)
and
m_{2}(t)
. In particular, one needs to further impose that
\chi,\theta\in\mathbb{R}
satisfy the inequalities

(27)

\chi>\max\left\{-1,\ -\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\underline{c}_{2}\right\},\qquad\theta>\max\left\{-1,\ -\frac{\gamma}{\nu}(\mu-\overline{c}_{1})\right\},

Under these bounds, we can exploit the properties of the Gamma distributions to determine an explicit expression for the normalization constants
C_{1}(t)
and
C_{2}(t)
, which read

C_{1}(t)=\frac{b_{1}^{a_{1}}}{\Gamma(a_{1})},\qquad C_{2}(t)=\frac{b_{2}^{a_{2}}}{\Gamma(a_{2})}.

Moreover, by denoting with
\zeta_{1}>0
and
\zeta_{2}>0
the two positive lower bounds for which conditions (
27
) are satisfied, namely
\beta\underline{c}_{2}+\alpha\chi\geq\zeta_{1}
and
\gamma(\mu-\overline{c}_{1})+\nu\theta\geq\zeta_{2}
, one can compute the explicit solutions of system (
24
) as

(28)

\begin{split}v_{1}(t)&=v_{1}(0)e^{-2\int_{0}^{t}(\beta m_{2}(s)+\alpha\chi)\mathrm{d}s}+\sigma_{1}\int_{0}^{t}m_{1}(s)m_{2}(s)e^{-2\int_{s}^{t}(\beta m_{2}(\bar{s})+\alpha\chi)\mathrm{d}\bar{s}}\mathrm{d}s,\\[5.69054pt]
v_{2}(t)&=v_{2}(0)e^{-2\int_{0}^{t}(\gamma(\mu-m_{1}(s))+\nu\theta)\mathrm{d}s}+\sigma_{2}\int_{0}^{t}m_{1}(s)m_{2}(s)e^{-2\int_{s}^{t}(\gamma(\mu-m_{1}(\bar{s}))+\nu\theta)\mathrm{d}\bar{s}}\mathrm{d}s,\end{split}

and consequently infer the following global-in-time
L^{\infty}
controls for the variances
v_{1}(t)
and
v_{2}(t)

(29)

\begin{split}\|v_{1}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}&\leq v_{1}(0)e^{-2\zeta_{1}t}+\frac{\sigma_{1}}{2\zeta_{1}}\overline{c}_{1}\overline{c}_{2}\left(1-e^{-2\zeta_{1}t}\right),\\[5.69054pt]
\|v_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}&\leq v_{2}(0)e^{-2\zeta_{2}t}+\frac{\sigma_{2}}{2\zeta_{2}}\overline{c}_{1}\overline{c}_{2}\left(1-e^{-2\zeta_{2}t}\right),\end{split}

based on the global bounds
\|m_{1}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}=\overline{c}_{1}
and
\|m_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}=\overline{c}_{2}
for the means.

Remark 2
.

We highlight that sharper a priori estimates show that the variances are actually well-defined for ranges of admissible
\chi
and
\theta
that are wider than the ones imposed by conditions (
27
). Indeed, exploiting the integral form of the solutions to the Lotka–Volterra system (
12
), namely

\begin{split}m_{1}(t)&=m_{1}(0)e^{-\int_{0}^{t}(\beta m_{2}(s)-\alpha)\mathrm{d}s},\\[11.38109pt]
m_{2}(t)&=m_{2}(0)e^{-\int_{0}^{t}(\delta-\gamma m_{1}(s))\mathrm{d}s},\end{split}

one can extract these terms from system (
28
) by adding and subtracting respectively
\alpha
and
\nu
inside the integrals that define the exponents on the right-hand sides. In doing so, simple algebraic manipulations allow to recast (
28
) as

\begin{split}v_{1}(t)&=\frac{v_{1}(0)}{m_{1}^{2}(0)}m_{1}^{2}(t)e^{-2\alpha(\chi+1)t}+\sigma_{1}m_{1}^{2}(t)e^{-2\alpha(\chi+1)t}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{m_{2}(s)}{m_{1}(s)}e^{2\alpha(\chi+1)s}\mathrm{d}s,\\[5.69054pt]
v_{2}(t)&=\frac{v_{2}(0)}{m_{2}^{2}(0)}m_{2}^{2}(t)e^{-2\nu(\theta+1)t}+\sigma_{2}m_{2}^{2}(t)e^{-2\nu(\theta+1)t}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{m_{1}(s)}{m_{2}(s)}e^{-2\nu(\theta+1)s}\mathrm{d}s,\end{split}

from which we derive the global-in-time controls

\begin{split}\|v_{1}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}&\leq\frac{v_{1}(0)}{m_{1}^{2}(0)}\overline{c}_{1}^{2}e^{-2\alpha(\chi+1)t}+\frac{\sigma_{1}}{2\alpha(\chi+1)}\frac{\overline{c}_{2}}{\underline{c}_{1}}\left(1-e^{-2\alpha(\chi+1)t}\right),\\[5.69054pt]
\|v_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}&\leq\frac{v_{2}(0)}{m_{2}^{2}(0)}\overline{c}_{2}^{2}e^{-2\nu(\theta+1)t}+\frac{\sigma_{2}}{2\nu(\theta+1)}\frac{\overline{c}_{1}}{\underline{c}_{2}}\left(1-e^{-2\nu(\theta+1)t}\right),\end{split}

implying that the variances remain in fact bounded as long as
\chi>-1
and
\theta>-1
.

3.5.
Asymptotic behavior of the moments

In this part, we analyze how far the solution
\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},t)=(f_{1}(x,t),f_{2}(y,t))
to the Fokker–Planck system (
22
) is from the local Gamma equilibrium
\mathbf{f}^{\mathrm{eq}}(\mathbf{x},t)=(f_{1}^{\mathrm{eq}}(x,t),f_{2}^{\mathrm{eq}}(y,t))
defined by (
26
), in terms of the distances between means and between variances of the two vector-distributions.

Given this equilibrium state
\mathbf{f}^{\mathrm{eq}}(\mathbf{x},t)
, we define the vector
\mathbf{m}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t)=(m_{1}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t),m_{2}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t))
of the corresponding means
\displaystyle m_{1}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}xf_{1}^{\mathrm{eq}}(x,t)\mathrm{d}x
and
\displaystyle m_{2}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}yf_{2}^{\mathrm{eq}}(y,t)\mathrm{d}y
. The explicit form of these means can be determined thanks to well-known formulae for the Gamma distributions, specifically

(30)

\begin{split}m_{1}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t)&=\frac{a_{1}(t)}{b_{1}(t)}=\frac{\alpha(\chi+1)m_{1}(t)}{\beta m_{2}(t)+\alpha\chi},\\[11.38109pt]
m_{2}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t)&=\frac{a_{2}(t)}{b_{2}(t)}=\frac{\nu(\theta+1)m_{2}(t)}{\gamma(\mu-m_{1}(t))+\nu\theta},\end{split}

and one can measure their distance from the solution
\mathbf{m}(t)=(m_{1}(t),m_{2}(t))
of the Lotka–Volterra system (
12
), by estimating the expression

\|\mathbf{m}(t)-\mathbf{m}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t)\|_{l^{\infty}}=\frac{\beta\left|m_{2}(t)-\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\right|}{\beta m_{2}(t)+\alpha\chi}\ m_{1}(t)+\frac{\gamma\left|m_{1}(t)-\frac{\delta}{\gamma}\right|}{\gamma(\mu-m_{1}(t))+\nu\theta}\ m_{2}(t),

where we have used that
\delta=\gamma\mu-\nu
, and the denominators are positive because of the assumptions (
27
) on the parameters
\chi
and
\theta
. To recover explicit lower and upper bounds, we thus need to properly control the numerators. We first observe that since the solution
\mathbf{m}(t)
evolves over closed orbits around the non-zero equilibrium point (
13
), for any given positive initial condition
(m_{1}(0),m_{2}(0))\neq\mathbf{m}^{*}
, the solution
\mathbf{m}(t)
differs from the null vector for any
t\geq 0
, and we thus deduce the existence of a strictly positive lower bound. In particular, for any fixed
(m_{1}(0),m_{2}(0))\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
, the following constants

\displaystyle\underline{c}_{0}:=\min_{H(m_{1}(t),m_{2}(t))=H(m_{1}(0),m_{2}(0))}\|\mathbf{m}(t)-\mathbf{m}^{*}\|_{l^{\infty}},

\displaystyle\overline{c}_{0}:=\max_{H(m_{1}(t),m_{2}(t))=H(m_{1}(0),m_{2}(0))}\|\mathbf{m}(t)-\mathbf{m}^{*}\|_{l^{\infty}},

are well-defined, positive and depend only on
(m_{1}(0),m_{2}(0))
. Recalling once again the bounds (
14
), we may then infer the lower and upper estimates

(31)

0<\underline{c}_{0}\min\left\{\frac{\beta\underline{c}_{1}}{\beta\overline{c}_{2}+\alpha\chi},\frac{\gamma\underline{c}_{2}}{\gamma(\mu-\underline{c}_{1})+\nu\theta}\right\}\leq\|\mathbf{m}(t)-\mathbf{m}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t)\|_{l^{\infty}}\leq\overline{c}_{0}\max\left\{\frac{\beta\overline{c}_{1}}{\beta\underline{c}_{2}+\alpha\chi},\frac{\gamma\overline{c}_{2}}{\gamma(\mu-\overline{c}_{1})+\nu\theta}\right\}.

This proves existence of a positive gap in the distance over time between
\mathbf{m}(t)
and
\mathbf{m}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t)
, preventing any possible relaxation of the solutions to the Lotka–Volterra system (
12
) toward the means of the local equilibrium states (
26
) computed from the Fokker–Planck equations (
22
), thus implying that the distributions
f_{1}(x,t)
and
f_{2}(y,t)
themselves cannot converge to the corresponding local equilibria
f_{1}^{\mathrm{eq}}(x,t)
and
f_{2}^{\mathrm{eq}}(y,t)
prescribed by the kinetic system.

A similar behavior is also observed for the variances
\mathbf{v}(t)=(v_{1}(t),v_{2}(t))
of the solutions
\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},t)
to system (
24
), when compared to the variances
\mathbf{v}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t)=(v_{1}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t),v_{2}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t))
of the corresponding equilibrium state
\mathbf{f}^{\mathrm{eq}}(\mathbf{x},t)
, which are defined by
\displaystyle v_{1}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}(x-m_{1}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t))^{2}f_{1}^{\mathrm{eq}}(x,t)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle v_{2}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}(y-m_{2}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t))^{2}f_{2}^{\mathrm{eq}}(y,t)\mathrm{d}y

\begin{split}v_{1}(t)=\frac{\sigma_{1}m_{1}(t)m_{2}(t)}{2(\beta m_{2}(t)+\alpha\chi)},\qquad v_{2}(t)=\frac{\sigma_{2}m_{1}(t)m_{2}(t)}{2(\gamma(\mu-m_{1}(t))+\nu\theta)},\end{split}

from which follows that the only possible equilibrium point
\mathbf{v}^{*}
for the variances
\mathbf{v}(t)
is the one determined by replacing
\mathbf{m}^{*}
into the above relations. Simple computations show that

(34)

\mathbf{v}^{*}=\left(\frac{\delta\sigma_{1}}{2\beta\gamma(\chi+1)},\frac{\alpha\delta\sigma_{2}}{2\beta\gamma\nu(\theta+1)}\right).

Moreover, by rewriting the variances (

\mathbf{m}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t)

\begin{split}v_{1}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t)&=\frac{\sigma_{1}m_{1}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t)m_{2}(t)}{2(\beta m_{2}(t)+\alpha\chi)},\\[5.69054pt]
v_{2}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t)&=\frac{\sigma_{2}m_{1}(t)m_{2}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t)}{2(\gamma(\mu-m_{1}(t))+\nu\theta)},\end{split}

one easily deduces that
\mathbf{v}^{*}
is also the unique equilibrium point of
\mathbf{v}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t)
, since
\mathbf{m}^{*}
is the only equilibrium point common to both
\mathbf{m}(t)
and
\mathbf{m}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t)
.

Therefore, similar to how the means
\mathbf{m}(t)
and
\mathbf{m}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t)
orbit around the point
\mathbf{m}^{*}
, the variances
\mathbf{v}(t)
and
\mathbf{v}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t)
evolve, at least for large enough times, over different periodic orbits around the common equilibrium point
\mathbf{v}^{*}
. In fact, from the bounds (
29
) on the variances and by Poincaré–Bendixson theorem, one infers that the
\omega
-limit set of
\mathbf{v}(t)
must be a periodic orbit, since the only fixed point
\mathbf{v}^{*}
of system (
24
) is never reached unless also
\mathbf{m}(t)
equals
\mathbf{m}^{*}
. In particular, from the explicit expressions (

and
m_{2}(t)

\mathbf{m}(t)

and

(35)

\mathbf{m}^{*}

which, from relations (

m_{1}(t)
and
m_{2}(t)
of the original variables

and

to the Fokker–Planck system (

Remark 3
.

From the previous observations on the means
\mathbf{m}(t)
and variances
\mathbf{v}(t)
, we may draw similar considerations regarding the relationship between the solution
\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},t)
and the local equilibrium state
\mathbf{f}^{\mathrm{eq}}(\mathbf{x},t)
. In particular, we can infer that the two distributions orbit around a common equilibrium state
\mathbf{f}^{*}(\mathbf{x})
which is the unique uniform-in-time Gamma distribution canceling out the Fokker–Planck fluxes (
25
), given by

\begin{split}f_{1}^{*}(x)&=C_{1}^{*}x^{\frac{2\alpha(\chi+1)}{\sigma_{1}}\frac{m_{1}^{*}}{m_{2}^{*}}-1}\exp\left\{-\frac{2}{\sigma_{1}m_{2}^{*}}(\beta m_{2}^{*}+\alpha\chi)x\right\},\\[11.38109pt]
f_{2}^{*}(y)&=C_{2}^{*}y^{\frac{2\nu(\theta+1)}{\sigma_{2}}\frac{m_{2}^{*}}{m_{1}^{*}}-1}\exp\left\{-\frac{2}{\sigma_{2}m_{1}^{*}}(\gamma(\mu-m_{1}^{*}+\nu\theta)y\right\},\end{split}

where
C_{1}^{*}
,
C_{2}^{*}>0
are normalization coefficients depending on the equilibrium means
m_{1}^{*}
and
m_{2}^{*}
. One then easily checks that the means and variances of this equilibrium state coincide with the equilibrium points
\mathbf{m}^{*}
and
\mathbf{v}^{*}
, namely

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}xf_{1}^{*}(x)\mathrm{d}x=m_{1}^{*},

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}yf_{2}^{*}(y)\mathrm{d}y=m_{2}^{*},\qquad

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}

\displaystyle(x-m_{1}^{*})^{2}f_{1}^{*}(x)\mathrm{d}x=v_{1}^{*},

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}(y-m_{2}^{*})^{2}f_{2}^{*}(y)\mathrm{d}y=v_{2}^{*}.

Remark 4
.

The obtained Fokker–Planck approximation, together with the closure of the moment systems, is inherently limited in capturing the full spectrum of fluctuations near population extinction thresholds, particularly in regimes with finite carrying capacities. Indeed, the modeling assumptions underlying the derivation of the Fokker–Planck system break down in the presence of a finite number of agents, which triggers a regime of rarefied interactions, see
Dobramysl et al. (2018)
;
Täuber (2012)
.

4.
Stabilizing the dynamics with intraspecific interactions

The previous section has highlighted the main challenges encountered in studying the relaxation toward equilibrium for the Fokker–Planck system (
22
), when the underlying macroscopic dynamics (in our case, the Lotka–Volterra ones) exhibit an oscillatory behavior around the equilibrium state. Such a phenomenon appears to render any proof of relaxation toward equilibrium unattainable for the time being. To address this issue, in this section we introduce a correction to our kinetic modeling, switching from a Malthusian-type growth for the preys to a Verhulst-type one, in order to obtain a Lotka–Volterra system with a unique equilibrium state that serves as an attractor for the macroscopic dynamics. This represents another novelty of this work.

Bearing in mind the structure of the Boltzmann equations (
8
), we introduce the following system

(36)

\begin{split}\frac{\partial f_{1}(x,t)}{\partial t}&=R_{\chi}^{\alpha}(f_{1})(x,t)+Q_{11}(f_{1},f_{1})(x,t)+Q_{12}(f_{1},f_{2})(x,t),\\[5.69054pt]
\frac{\partial f_{2}(y,t)}{\partial t}&=R_{\theta}^{\nu}(f_{2})(y,t)+Q_{21}(f_{2},f_{1})(y,t),\end{split}

which accounts for both interspecific predator–preys interactions as well as intraspecific competition among the preys. More precisely, the bilinear operators
Q_{12}(f_{1},f_{2})
and
Q_{21}(f_{2},f_{1})
are now used to describe any binary interaction between individuals of the same or of different species, and the operator
Q_{11}(f_{1},f_{1})
models in particular the intraspecific interactions occurring inside the population of preys. This means that at the microscopic level, the preys are involved in the interactions (
3
) with the predators, and in new intraspecific binary interactions given by

(37)

x^{\prime\prime\prime}=x-\tilde{\Phi}(x_{*})x+\sqrt{x}\mathds{1}(x\geq s_{0}/2)\eta_{1}(x_{*}),

where the variable
x_{*}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
refers to the size of the interacting group within the species of preys, described by
f_{1}(x_{*},t)
, and compared to (
3
) we now solely consider the more interesting case
p=1/2
which leads to thin-tails equilibrium states. Notice that we have once again fixed
\bar{x}=1
for simplicity and we still need to reduce the range of values taken by
x
by using the indicator function
\mathds{1}(x\geq s_{0}/2)
, which ensures the positivity of the post-interactions
x^{\prime\prime\prime}
. For consistency with our previous choices (
4
)–(
5
) on the form of the deterministic and random effects involved in the interactions, we choose once more

\tilde{\Phi}(x_{*})=\frac{\alpha}{K}\frac{x_{*}}{1+x_{*}},

where the new parameter
K>0
denotes the carrying capacity of the preys, and we consider
\eta_{1}(x_{*})
to be an independent random variable of zero mean and bounded variance, given explicitly by

\langle\eta_{1}^{2}(x_{*})\rangle=\sigma_{1}\frac{x_{*}}{1+x_{*}}.

In particular, we assume that the ratio
\alpha/K
satisfies the constraint
\displaystyle\frac{\alpha}{K}<1
.

Then, similarly to the previous section, we consider the weak formulation of system (
36
) which, for all smooth functions
\varphi(x)
and
\psi(y)
, reads as

(38)

\begin{split}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\varphi(x)f_{1}(x,t)\mathrm{d}x&=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}}\kappa_{1}(x,x_{*})\big\langle\varphi(x^{\prime\prime\prime})-\varphi(x)\big\rangle f_{1}(x,t)f_{1}(x_{*},t)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}x_{*}\\[5.69054pt]
&\hskip 28.45274pt+\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}}\big(\varphi(x^{\prime\prime})-\varphi(x)\big)f_{1}(x,t)g(z,t)\mathrm{d}z\mathrm{d}x\\[5.69054pt]
&\hskip 56.9055pt+\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}}\kappa_{1}(x,y)\big\langle\varphi(x^{\prime})-\varphi(x)\big\rangle f_{1}(x,t)f_{2}(y,t)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y,\\[11.38109pt]
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\psi(y)f_{2}(y,t)\mathrm{d}y&=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}}\big(\psi(y^{\prime\prime})-\psi(y)\big)f_{2}(y,t)h(z,t)\mathrm{d}z\mathrm{d}y\\[5.69054pt]
&\hskip 56.9055pt+\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}}\kappa_{2}(x,y)\big\langle\psi(y^{\prime})-\psi(y)\big\rangle f_{1}(x,t)f_{2}(y,t)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y,\end{split}

where we have used the same function
\kappa_{1}(x,x_{*})=\kappa(x_{*})=1+x_{*}
defined in (
10
) to model the frequency of contacts (
37
) among preys, and the integral terms on the right-hand sides of the equations account for the balance of all intraspecific (
37
) and interspecific (
3
) microscopic interactions, as well as of the exchanges with the environment (
6
), recalling the notations
g(z,t)
and
h(z,t)
for the distributions of its resources, that have prescribed means given by (
7
).

In this new setting, choosing
\varphi(x)=x
and
\psi(y)=y
in (
38
), we find that the temporal evolution of the mean values
m_{1}(t)
and
m_{2}(t)
is now described by the system

(39)

\begin{split}\frac{\mathrm{d}m_{1}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}&=\alpha\left(1-\frac{m_{1}(t)}{K}\right)m_{1}(t)-\beta m_{1}(t)m_{2}(t),\\[5.69054pt]
\frac{\mathrm{d}m_{2}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}&=-\delta m_{2}(t)+\gamma m_{1}(t)m_{2}(t),\end{split}

which are the well-known competitive predator–prey Lotka–Volterra equations, involving a logistic growth for the population of preys. It is worth pointing out that this new system differs from the one (
12
) derived in the previous section only in the first equation, as we solely consider intraspecific interactions among preys. Furthermore, we highlight that system (
39
) is characterized by a unique stable equilibrium point of coexistence given by

(40)

\mathbf{m}^{\infty}=\left(\frac{\delta}{\gamma},\frac{\alpha(\gamma K-\delta)}{\beta\gamma K}\right),

which is biologically meaningful provided that
\gamma K-\delta>0
. In particular, under this condition it is possible to prove that any solution
\mathbf{m}(t)
to (
39
) converges asymptotically in time to the equilibrium
\mathbf{m}^{\infty}
, see e.g.
Champagnat et al. (2010)
.

We proceed to derive the corresponding reduced model of Fokker–Planck-type, by considering the following quasi-invariant regime of the parameters

\alpha\to\varepsilon\alpha,\qquad\beta\to\varepsilon\beta,\qquad\nu\to\varepsilon\nu,\qquad\gamma\to\varepsilon\gamma,\qquad\sigma_{1}\to\varepsilon\sigma_{1},\qquad\sigma_{2}\to\varepsilon\sigma_{2},

for some
\varepsilon\ll 1
, where the temporal variable is rescaled accordingly as
\tau=\varepsilon t
. Since the computations do not deviate substantially from the previous ones, we skip the details of such derivation. When
\varepsilon\to 0
, we find that the evolution of the limit distribution functions
f_{1}(x,t)
and
f_{2}(y,t)
is described by the Fokker–Planck system

(41)

\begin{split}\frac{\partial f_{1}(x,t)}{\partial t}&=\frac{\sigma_{1}(m_{1}(t)+m_{2}(t))}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}\left[xf_{1}(x,t)\right]\\[5.69054pt]
&\hskip 65.44142pt+\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left[\left(\left(\beta m_{2}(t)+\frac{\alpha}{K}m_{1}(t)+\alpha\chi\right)x-\alpha(\chi+1)m_{1}(t)\right)f_{1}(x,t)\right],\\[17.07164pt]
\frac{\partial f_{2}(y,t)}{\partial t}&=\frac{\sigma_{2}m_{1}(t)}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial y^{2}}\left[yf_{2}(y,t)\right]+\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\big((\gamma(\mu-m_{1}(t))y+\nu\theta y-\nu(\theta+1)m_{2}(t))f_{2}(y,t)\big),\end{split}

complemented by the no-flux boundary conditions

\begin{split}&\frac{\sigma_{1}(m_{1}(t)+m_{2}(t))}{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left[xf_{1}(x,t)\right]\\[5.69054pt]
&\hskip 48.36958pt+\left(\left(\beta m_{2}(t)+\frac{\alpha}{K}m_{1}(t)+\alpha\chi\right)x-\alpha(\chi+1)m_{1}(t)\right)f_{1}(x,t)\Big|_{x=0,+\infty}=0,\\[5.69054pt]
&xf_{1}(x,t)\Big|_{x=0,+\infty}=0,\\[22.76219pt]
&\frac{\sigma_{2}m_{1}(t)}{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left[yf_{2}(y,t)\right]+(\gamma(\mu-m_{1}(t))y+\nu\theta y-\nu(\theta+1)m_{2}(t))f_{2}(y,t)\Big|_{y=0,+\infty}=0,\\[5.69054pt]
&yf_{2}(y,t)\Big|_{y=0,+\infty}=0.\end{split}

Starting from (
41
), we are now able to determine closed evolution equations for the moments of the system, in particular for the associated means and variances of its solutions
\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},t)
. For the means
m_{1}(t)
and
m_{2}(t)
we find a governing system that is obviously consistent with the one determined from the Boltzmann dynamics, given by the competitive Lotka–Volterra equations (
39
). The evolution of the variances
v_{1}(t)
and
v_{2}(t)
is then prescribed by

(42)

\begin{split}\frac{\mathrm{d}v_{1}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}&=-2\left(\beta m_{2}(t)+\frac{\alpha}{K}m_{1}(t)+\alpha\chi\right)v_{1}(t)+\sigma_{1}m_{1}(t)(m_{1}(t)+m_{2}(t)),\\[11.38109pt]
\frac{\mathrm{d}v_{2}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}&=-2\big(\gamma(\mu-m_{1}(t))+\nu\theta\big)v_{2}(t)+\sigma_{2}m_{1}(t)m_{2}(t),\end{split}

and one can infer global-in-time a priori estimates similar to the ones (
29
) obtained in the previous section.

Moreover, we can carry out the same computations as before to determine the new local equilibrium distribution states
\mathbf{f}^{\mathrm{eq}}(\mathbf{x},t)=(f_{1}^{\mathrm{eq}}(x,t),f_{2}^{\mathrm{eq}}(y,t))
which now read

(43)

\begin{split}f_{1}^{\mathrm{eq}}(x,t)&=C_{1}(t)x^{\frac{2\alpha(\chi+1)}{\sigma_{1}}\frac{m_{1}(t)}{m_{1}(t)+m_{2}(t)}-1}\exp\left\{-\frac{2}{\sigma_{1}(m_{1}(t)+m_{2}(t))}\left(\beta m_{2}(t)+\frac{\alpha}{K}m_{1}(t)+\alpha\chi\right)x\right\},\\[11.38109pt]
f_{2}^{\mathrm{eq}}(y,t)&=C_{2}(t)y^{\frac{2\nu(\theta+1)}{\sigma_{2}}\frac{m_{2}(t)}{m_{1}(t)}-1}\exp\left\{-\frac{2}{\sigma_{2}m_{1}(t)}(\gamma(\mu-m_{1}(t))+\nu\theta)y\right\},\end{split}

for some time-dependent normalization constants
C_{1}(t),C_{2}(t)>0
. The above functions are Gamma-type distributions if we impose (once the parameters
\alpha
,
K
,
\beta
,
\delta
,
\gamma
,
\nu>0
and
\mu\geq 1
of the competitive Lotka–Volterra system (
39
) have been fixed) that
\chi,\theta\in\mathbb{R}
satisfy the conditions

(44)

\chi>\max\left\{-1,\ -\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\underline{c}_{2}-\frac{1}{K}\underline{c}_{1}\right\},\qquad\theta>\max\left\{-1,\ -\frac{\gamma}{\nu}(\mu-\overline{c}_{1})\right\},

for some constants
\underline{c}_{1}
,
\overline{c}_{1}
,
\underline{c}_{2}>0
bounding from below and from above the means
m_{1}(t)
and
m_{2}(t)
, defined similarly to (
14
). Notice in particular that the introduction of the intraspecific competition among preys allows to lessen the restrictions on the range of admissible values for
\chi
, meaning that the local equilibrium
\mathbf{f}^{\mathrm{eq}}(\mathbf{x},t)
is well-defined as a Gamma vector distribution and the corresponding variances
v_{1}(t)
and
v_{2}(t)
remain bounded, for values of
\chi
ranging in an interval wider than the one determined in the previous setting.

By rewriting the equilibrium distributions (
43
) as

f_{1}^{\mathrm{eq}}(x,t)=C_{1}(t)x^{a_{1}(t)-1}e^{-b_{1}(t)x},\qquad f_{2}^{\mathrm{eq}}(y,t)=C_{2}(t)y^{a_{2}(t)-1}e^{-b_{2}(t)y},

where all the exponents

\begin{split}&a_{1}(t)=\frac{2\alpha(\chi+1)m_{1}(t)}{\sigma_{1}(m_{1}(t)+m_{2}(t))},\qquad b_{1}(t)=\frac{2(\beta m_{2}(t)+\frac{\alpha}{K}m_{1}(t)+\alpha\chi)}{\sigma_{1}(m_{1}(t)+m_{2}(t))},\\[11.38109pt]
&a_{2}(t)=\frac{2\nu(\theta+1)m_{2}(t)}{\sigma_{2}m_{1}(t)},\qquad\quad\ b_{2}(t)=\frac{2(\gamma(\mu-m_{1}(t))+\nu\theta)}{\sigma_{2}m_{1}(t)},\end{split}

are positive thanks to the above assumptions on the parameters
\chi
and
\theta
, we deduce explicit expressions for the equilibrium means
\mathbf{m}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t)
, which are given by

(45)

\begin{split}m_{1}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t)&=\frac{\alpha(\chi+1)m_{1}(t)}{\beta m_{2}(t)+\frac{\alpha}{K}m_{1}(t)+\alpha\chi},\\[11.38109pt]
m_{2}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t)&=\frac{\nu(\theta+1)m_{2}(t)}{\gamma(\mu-m_{1}(t))+\nu\theta},\end{split}

and for the equilibrium variances
\mathbf{v}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t)
, which read

(46)

\begin{split}v_{1}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t)&=\frac{\sigma_{1}\alpha(\chi+1)m_{1}(t)(m_{1}(t)+m_{2}(t))}{2\left(\beta m_{2}(t)+\frac{\alpha}{K}m_{1}(t)+\alpha\chi\right)^{2}}=\frac{\sigma_{1}m_{1}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t)(m_{1}(t)+m_{2}(t))}{2\left(\beta m_{2}(t)+\frac{\alpha}{K}m_{1}(t)+\alpha\chi\right)},\\[11.38109pt]
v_{2}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t)&=\frac{\sigma_{2}\nu(\theta+1)m_{1}(t)m_{2}(t)}{2(\gamma(\mu-m_{1}(t))+\nu\theta)^{2}}=\frac{\sigma_{2}m_{1}(t)m_{2}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t)}{2(\gamma(\mu-m_{1}(t))+\nu\theta)}.\end{split}

We can thus investigate the asymptotic behavior of the first two moments of the distributions of preys and predators, in order to evaluate the distance of the solutions
\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},t)
to system (
41
) from the associated local equilibrium states
\mathbf{f}^{\mathrm{eq}}(\mathbf{x},t)

Remark 5
.

Our results on the asymptotic behavior of
\mathbf{m}(t)
and
\mathbf{v}(t)
suggest that the solution
\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},t)
to the Fokker–Planck system (
41
) relaxes over time toward the local equilibrium state
\mathbf{f}^{\mathrm{eq}}(\mathbf{x},t)
defined by (
43
). Moreover, the two distributions are expected to converge toward a common global equilibrium state
\mathbf{f}^{\infty}(\mathbf{x})
which is the unique uniform-in-time Gamma distribution canceling out the right-hand sides of system (
41
), and is given by

\begin{split}f_{1}^{\infty}(x)&=C_{1}^{\infty}x^{\frac{2\alpha(\chi+1)}{\sigma_{1}}\frac{m_{1}^{\infty}}{m_{1}^{\infty}+m_{2}^{\infty}}-1}\exp\left\{-\frac{2}{\sigma_{1}(m_{1}^{\infty}+m_{2}^{\infty})}\left(\beta m_{2}^{\infty}+\frac{\alpha}{K}m_{1}^{\infty}+\alpha\chi\right)x\right\},\\[11.38109pt]
f_{2}^{\infty}(y)&=C_{2}^{\infty}y^{\frac{2\nu(\theta+1)}{\sigma_{2}}\frac{m_{2}^{\infty}}{m_{1}^{\infty}}-1}\exp\left\{-\frac{2}{\sigma_{2}m_{1}^{\infty}}(\gamma(\mu-m_{1}^{\infty}+\nu\theta)y\right\},\end{split}

where
C_{1}^{\infty}
,
C_{2}^{\infty}>0
are normalization coefficients depending on the equilibrium means
m_{1}^{\infty}
and
m_{2}^{\infty}
. In particular, the means and variances of this equilibrium state give exactly the equilibrium points
\mathbf{m}^{\infty}
and
\mathbf{v}^{\infty}
, namely

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}xf_{1}^{\infty}(x)\mathrm{d}x=m_{1}^{\infty},

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}yf_{2}^{\infty}(y)\mathrm{d}y=m_{2}^{\infty},\qquad

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}

\displaystyle(x-m_{1}^{\infty})^{2}f_{1}^{\infty}(x)\mathrm{d}x=v_{1}^{\infty},

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}(y-m_{2}^{\infty})^{2}f_{2}^{\infty}(y)\mathrm{d}y=v_{2}^{\infty}.

It remains an open question to determine the equilibration rate of
\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},t)
toward this global equilibrium state
\mathbf{f}^{\infty}(\mathbf{x})
.

Conclusion

In this work we studied the evolution properties of a system of kinetic equations of Fokker–Planck-type, generalizing an analogous model introduced recently in
Toscani and Zanella (2024)
and justifying its derivation from a Boltzmann-type description. These Fokker–Planck equations describe the time evolution of vector-densities accounting for the distribution of the sizes of two populations interacting along the rules of predator–prey-like dynamics. The main feature of the kinetic system is the presence of time-dependent coefficients of drift and diffusion, which are proportional to the mean values of the solution densities, leading to the formation of quasi-equilibria. The importance of the characteristics of these local equilibria in connection with the true solution of the system have been investigated by showing their possible rule. An interesting by-product of the present analysis is the possibility of extending the classical Lotka–Volterra system, which in this case describes the time evolution of the mean solution to a Fokker–Planck system modeling the sizes of predator and prey populations. Specifically, we derive a closed evolution equation for the variances of the solutions, providing additional information on the study of such biological systems. Determining the equilibration rate for the obtained kinetic dynamics is an open and significant question that deserves further attention. In future research, with the aim of quantifying the convergence rate solutions to the kinetic system toward equilibrium, we plan to design suitable entropy methods following the strategy proposed in
Auricchio et al. (2023)
, where relaxation to equilibrium is shown to occur at least with a polynomial rate. Extensions of the present methods to account for multiple interactions between species, as for example in
Bobylev et al. (2009)
;
Toscani et al. (2019)
, are currently under investigation and will be addressed in future works.
