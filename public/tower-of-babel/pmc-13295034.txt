Lacrimal Drainage Catheter Coated with Mitomycin C to Inhibit Fibrosis after Canalicular Laceration Repair
Authors: Huanhuan Xu, Liang Lu, Xueying Kong, Rui Wu, Hu Meng, Dekui Ren, Jun Gu, Yong Wu, Yirui Ge
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Canalicular lacerations represent a common ocular emergency that can lead to significant complications if not managed promptly and effectively. Silicone stents are the most commonly chosen option due to their favorable characteristics. However, postoperative complications, such as canalicular stenosis, remain prevalent, necessitating the exploration of additional therapeutic strategies. Mitomycin C (MMC) is an effective agent for preventing excessive fibrosis in various ophthalmic surgeries; however, its topical application faces challenges due to rapid clearance and potential side effects. Currently, we have established a dual-layer drug delivery system for MMC, wherein lacrimal drainage catheters (LDC) were coated with agarose loaded with MMC (MA-1, MA-2, and MA-3) and subsequently encapsulated in a layer of poly­(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA). The LDC@MA-1@PLGA, LDC@MA-2@PLGA, and LDC@MA-3@PLGA released final amounts of MMC of 1.72 ± 0.21 μg, 5.32 ± 0.56 μg, and 9.32 ± 0.16 μg during the first 8 days, with corresponding final release rates of 57.43 ± 7.78%, 85.36 ± 10.49%, and 79.06 ± 2.56%, respectively. In vitro assays demonstrated that MMC not only inhibits fibroblast proliferation but also enhances apoptosis, particularly in the fibrotic context induced by TGF-β1. Additionally, in vivo experiments demonstrated that LDC@MA@PLGA effectively reduced the expression levels of α-SMA and collagen deposition. Overall, the findings from our study elucidate the potential of LDC@MA@PLGA as an effective therapeutic strategy in preventing postsurgical fibrosis following canalicular laceration repairs.

Introduction

Canalicular laceration is a common type of ocular emergency accounting for 16% of all eyelid lacerations, and typically results from either blunt or penetrating injuries in the medial canthal region. Canalicular lacerations can occur across all age groups, particularly affecting children and young adults, with the inferior canaliculus often involved. If not treated appropriately, the canaliculus may experience inflammation, scarring, canalicular stenosis, and subsequently lead to lacrimal drainage dysfunction characterized by epiphora. Significant focal areas of subepithelial scarring and fibrosis have been noted around the injury site, which are major contributors to postoperative canalicular stenosis and epiphora. To mitigate these complications, it is essential to repair the lacerated canaliculus promptly and accurately. This restoration is vital for reestablishing the normal anatomy and function of tear drainage, as well as preventing cicatrization of the lacrimal pathway. The management of canalicular laceration varies widely, and there is consensus that immediate anastomosis and stenting are necessary. Silicone tubes are the most used stents due to its advantageous characteristics, such as inert nature, flexibility, and easy availability. By maintaining patency at the anastomosis site, these stents not only support the healing process but also minimize the risk of postoperative complications such as canalicular stenosis and obstruction. However, excessive fibrotic cell proliferation following surgery can result in stenosis or even complete obstruction of the lacrimal canaliculi, negatively impacting the overall surgical outcomes. MMC, an antineoplastic antibiotic, with potent cytotoxic effects during various stages of cell proliferation, is one of the most effective agents to prevent excessive fibrosis in various ophthalmic operations, such as pterygium excision, dacryocystorhinostomy, trabeculectomy, corneal surgeries and lacrimal system obstruction. − Previous research has shown that irrigating MMC (0.2 mg/mL) through the inferior lacrimal punctum for a duration of 3 min is both safe and effective in improving the patency rate of lacrimal systems after the removal of an obstructed nasolacrimal stent. However, the effectiveness of topical MMC application is hampered by its short half-life and rapid clearance from the conjunctival sac due to low viscosity and continuous tear film renewal. Consequently, multiple doses are often required to maintain therapeutic levels, which can complicate patient compliance and treatment effectiveness. Furthermore, application of MMC with high concentration may lead to overinhibiting of fibroproliferation leading to a range of side effects. , Additionally, MMC that enters the lacrimal duct may be quickly diluted and washed away by tears, diminishing its concentration and the duration of contact with the canalicular laceration site. Therefore, there is an urgent need to explore a novel MMC-coated stent that can sustain the release of MMC over an extended period at the site of canalicular laceration.

Currently, various drug delivery systems have been developed to reduce the side effects of MMC, release the appropriate therapeutic dosage of the MMC into the tissues, and provide a safe dose to patients over an extended duration. ,, This can be achieved by the use of chitosan, Cryogels, , nanoparticles, agarose, hydrogels and tannic acid , that are engineered to release MMC in response to specific physiological triggers associated with the healing process. However, we require a material that can be smoothly coated on the surface of the stent and effectively load and release MMC. Among these options, agarose-based delivery systems present a promising solution for attaining the desired drug release profile while meeting our requirements. Agarose, a water-soluble linear polysaccharide derived from seaweed, is widely recognized for its unique properties, which make it an excellent candidate for drug delivery systems. Its special interconnected porous structure, higher surface area, self-gelling and neutral surface properties enable the efficient loading and controlled release of a diverse array of therapeutic agents, including small molecules, proteins, and nucleic acids. However, drug delivery systems based on agarose hydrogels typically exhibit burst release. To address the challenge of high initial drug release, implementing a layer-by-layer assembly of thin-film polymers is an effective approach. Therefore, we require a material that can be wrapped around agarose to further control the release of MMC. PLGA is commonly utilized as a polymeric matrix-forming agent in parenteral controlled drug delivery systems, particularly for microparticles and implants. We aimed to apply a hydrophobic layer of PLGA surrounding the agarose, thereby optimize the release of MMC.

In this study, we reported the application of a dual-layered drug delivery system for MMC delivery and investigate its antifibrotic effects following canalicular laceration repair surgery (Figure ). We established this delivery system by coating LDC with agarose loaded with MMC, followed by encasing it with a layer of PLGA. The inner layer is designed for a controlled, immediate release of MMC to rapidly achieve therapeutic concentrations at the target site. Meanwhile, the outer layer provides a sustained and prolonged release, maintaining effective drug levels over an extended period. The MMC release properties of LDC@MA@PLGA were subsequently evaluated in vitro. The in vitro effect of MMC on the viability of Rabbit primary skin fibroblasts (RPSFs) was investigated and the in vivo antifibrotic effect of LDC@MA@PLGA was investigated by using a rabbit canalicular laceration model. Our results demonstrated that this dual-layered drug delivery system facilitates the sustained release of MMC, effectively reducing fibrotic complications and promoting tissue healing, thereby suggesting its potential as a therapeutic strategy in managing canalicular injuries.

Materials and Methods

Reagents

Agarose (electrophoretic grade) was obtained from Beijing Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Hexafluoroisopropanol (HFIP, ≥99%), PLGA (Mw 38000–54000) were obtained from Aladdin Biochemical Technology co., Ltd. Mitomycin C (MMC, 98%) was obtained from Hanhui Pharmaceuticals CO., Ltd. MMC solutions were prepared by dissolving it in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). RIPA lysis buffer was obtained from Beyotime Biotechnology. Ultrapure water was used to prepare various solutions (Millipore, 18.2 MΩ·cm).

Cell Culture

RPSFs were kindly provided by Wei Ye from the Department of Ophthalmology at Jinling Hospital, Jiangsu, China, and were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, Gibco), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FND500, ExCell Bio, Shanghai, China), in a humidified incubator set at 37 °C with 5%CO2.

Preparation of LDC@MA@PLGA

Agarose powder (1.0 g) was mixed with ultrapure water (50 mL) in a beaker and sonicated at 53 kHz (100%, SK3310HP, KUDOS) for 3 min. The agarose water dispersion was then heated in a microwave oven for 2 min to obtain an agarose solution. The hot agarose solution was kept at 70 °C to prevent gelation. Then, 5 mL agarose solution was mixed with various amounts of MMC (1.5, 3.0, and 6.0 mg). The mixtures were stirred at 70 °C for 5 min to obtain agarose solutions with different concentrations of MMC (MA-1, MA-2, and MA-3). A 10 mm segment of a LDC (Shandong Steve Medical Science & Technology Co., Ltd.) was utilized for further studies, and its weight was recorded. The LDC was then immersed in the MA solutions. Once the solution cooled to 35 °C, the LDC was removed and dried in an oven at 55 °C for 3 h, yielding an MMC/agarose-coated LDC (LDC@MA), with its weight subsequently measured. PLGA (1.0 g) was dissolved in 5 mL HFIP and stirred for 5 min. LDC@MA was immersed in the PLGA solution for 5 min and then placed at room temperature for 60 min to obtain PLGA-coated LDC@MA (LDC@MA@PLGA). LDC@A@PLGA refers to LDC coated with agarose solution without MMC, which was prepared as LDC@A and subsequently immersed in PLGA solution. All procedures were conducted in the dark. All LDC@MA@PLGA samples were sterilized by ethylene oxide fumigation for 8 h in the dark.

Drug Release Assay

The LDC@MA@PLGA samples were placed in a 10 mm quartz colorimetric cuvette, to which 3 mL of normal saline at 37 °C was added. The absorption spectra of the solutions were measured every 24 h using an ultraviolet–visible-near-infrared spectrophotometer (UV-3600, Shimadzu, Japan). The drug release of MMC was calculated based on the drug release profiles and the working curves of MMC in the solution.

Cell Counting Kit-8 Assay

Pretreated cells were incubated with CCK-8 solution at 37 °C for 1 h, and the absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Bio-Tek, USA). The percentage of viable fibroblasts was defined as the number of viable change cells normalized based on untreated control cells.

Flow Cytometry Assay

RPSFs (5 × 105 cells) were exposed to TGF-β1 with or without MMC for a duration of 24 h. Cell apoptosis was evaluated using the FITC Annexin V Apoptosis Detection Kit I (BD Biosciences) following the manufacturer’s guidelines. Data analysis was performed using FlowJo software. The apoptosis rate indicated the overall apoptosis rate, encompassing both early and late stages of apoptosis.

Western Blot Assay (WB)

Pretreated RPSFs were lysed using RIPA lysis buffer that was supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Equivalent protein samples were separated on a 12% polyacrylamide gel to isolate the target proteins, which were then transferred to PVDF membranes. After blocking for 20 min at room temperature in 5% nonfat milk, the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies against α-SMA (1:2000) overnight at 4 °C. Afterward, the membranes were further incubated with secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. Then, the membranes were exposed to an ECL Western Blotting Detection Reagent and visualized using detection system. The expression levels of proteins were normalized to GAPDH, and the gray densities of the blots were quantified using ImageJ software provided by the National Institutes of Health, USA.

Histological Examination

The rabbit canaliculus and surrounding tissue were harvested at 1 month and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded with paraffin, and sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (HE) and Masson trichrome to demonstrate the inflammation and the degree of fibrosis. ImageJ software was used to quantify fibrosis and collagen content.

Immunofluorescence Staining

Each group of pretreated RPSFs were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 20 min, and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin for 20 min. After overnight incubation with primary antibodies, the cells were then incubated with secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. Subsequently, fluorescence images were detected and taken by a Laser Scanning Confocal Microscope (LSCM 980, Carl Zeiss Microscopy GmbH, Germany).

The paraffin section of canalicular tissue was deparaffinized, rehydrated, and antigen heat retrieved. Subsequently, sections were permeabilized using 0.5% Triton X-100. Sections were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, and then with second antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. Last, fluorescence images were observed and recorded on Laser Scanning Confocal Microscope (LSM 980, Carl Zeiss Microscopy GmbH, Germany).

Quantitative Real-Time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA isolation was performed on RPSFs and rabbit canaliculus tissue employing the Trizol method, according to the instructions of the manufacturer. RNA was then reverse-transcribed into cDNA using an RT-PCR kit according to the manufacturer’s guidelines. Quantitative RT-PCR assays were performed on an ABI 7500 instrument (ABI, USA), and the expression levels of the target gene were determined using the standard 2−ΔΔCT methods. The primer sequences for human α-SMA were: Forward, 5′ TGGTGAGATTGGCACTGAGGAG 3′; Reverse, 5′ AGGATGCTGTCGTGGTGAAGG 3′.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed with a GrahpPad Prisms version 9.0 software (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). One-way analysis of variance and Student’s t test were employed to assess statistical differences between groups. All experiments were conducted three independent times, and data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). A P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Preparation of LDC@MA@PLGA

The preparation process of LDC@MA@PLGA is illustrated in Figure a. Briefly, LDC was coated with MA solutions to form LDC@MA, which was subsequently immersed in a PLGA solution to obtain LDC@MA@PLGA. The LDC is a silicon stent, as illustrated in Figure b, with a diameter of 0.8 mm and a length of 10 mm from the head of the LDC indicated between the red arrows were used. As shown in Figure c, the prepared LDC@MA@PLGA appears light purple, which indicates the presence of MMC. The average diameter of prepared LDC@MA@PLGA was 1.11 ± 0.02 mm. We observed that the surface of the prepared LDC@MA@PLGA is smooth and remains intact without tearing or detaching when bent, thereby preventing damage to the lacrimal passage. We next investigated the drug loading performance of LDC@MA@PLGA. As shown in Figure d, the amount of MA loaded on LDC for the different groups was approximately 10 mg. The loading capacities of MMC for LDC@MA-1@PLGA, LDC@MA-2@PLGA, and LDC@MA-3@PLGA were 2.99 ± 0.09 μg, 6.25 ± 0.12 μg, and 11.80 ± 0.53 μg, respectively (Figure e).

In Vitro Drug Release of LDC@MA@PLGA

To investigate the drug release properties of LDC@MA@PLGA, we first measured the time-dependent UV–vis absorption spectra of LDC@MA@PLGA. During the first 6 days, the amount of MMC released from LDC@MA-1@PLGA continuously increased, while the release remained unchanged for the subsequent 3 days, indicating that no further drug release occurred after day 6 (Figure a). In contrast, MMC was continuously released from LDC@MA-2@PLGA and LDC@MA-3@PLGA during the initial 6 days, followed by a slower release over the next 3 days, suggesting that MMC was not completely released within the first 8 days (Figure b and c). Generally, the absorbance at 363 nm increased with time for all samples (Figure d).

(d) The change in absorbance of normal saline at 363 nm after incubation with different samples (n = 3). (e) Time-dependent drug release of LDC@MA@PLGA (n = 3). (f) Time-dependent drug release efficiency of LDC@MA@PLGA (n = 3). (g–i) Higuchi model fitting for LDC@MA-1@PLGA (g), LDC@MA-2@PLGA (h), and LDC@MA-3@PLGA (i) (n = 3). We further calculated the drug release of MMC based on the drug release profiles and working curves of MMC in solution. As shown in Figure e, the release of MMC from LDC@MA-1@PLGA continued during the first 6 days and ceased on day 7, with a final released amount of 1.72 ± 0.21 μg. Approximately 5.32 ± 0.56 μg and 9.32 ± 0.16 μg of MMC were released from LDC@MA-2@PLGA and LDC@MA-3@PLGA during the first 8 days, respectively, indicating that the amount of released MMC can be adjusted by changing the concentration of MMC in MA. The final release rates of LDC@MA-1@PLGA, LDC@MA-2@PLGA, and LDC@MA-3@PLGA were determined to be 57.43 ± 7.78%, 85.36 ± 10.49%, and 79.06 ± 2.56% (Figure f).These findings suggest that there is a threshold concentration of MMC in the agarose matrix beyond which the release rates become comparable across different formulations at a given time point.

To further analyze the release kinetics of MMC from different formulations, we applied several kinetic models commonly used in drug release studies, including the Higuchi and Korsmeyer-Peppas models. Plotting the cumulative MMC release against the square root of time revealed a diffusion-controlled mechanism, characterized by cumulative drug release proportional to the square root of time at equilibrium. The linear regression analysis yielded a high correlation coefficient values (R 2) of 0.93, 0.99, and 0.99 for LDC@MA-1@PLGA (Figure g), LDC@MA-2@PLGA (Figure h), and LDC@MA-3@PLGA (Figure i), respectively. These results indicate that the cumulative MMC release fits the Higuchi model well, demonstrating that diffusion is the dominant mechanism controlling the release of MMC in all formulations.

The Effect of MMC on the Viability of RPSFs

We initially evaluated the impact of MMC on RPSFs by treating the cells with various concentrations of MMC for 24 h and assessing the outcomes using the CCK-8 assay. As shown in Figure a, MMC exhibited a dose-dependent inhibitory effect on the proliferation of RPSFs. A significant decrease in cell viability was noted at a concentration of 2 μg/mL, which was identified as the lowest effective dose. The results indicated that treatment with MMC at concentrations of 2, 4, and 8 μg/mL resulted in reductions in cell viability of 5.0%, 8.2%, and 30.0%, respectively. Importantly, exposure to MMC concentrations above 8 μg/mL led to excessive cell death, indicating cytotoxicity at higher doses. Therefore, 8 μg/mL MMC was selected as the maximum concentration for subsequent experiments to maintain a balance between efficacy and safety.

Next, we aimed to examine the antiproliferative effects of MMC in the context of TGF-β1-induced fibrosis. RPSFs were cotreated with 10 ng/mL TGF-β1 and varying concentrations of MMC (0, 1, 2, 4, and 8 μg/mL) for 24 h. As illustrated in Figure b, TGF-β1 treatment resulted in a significant increase in RPSF proliferation. However, the addition of MMC effectively countered this effect, with concentrations of 2, 4, and 8 μg/mL significantly reducing TGF-β1-induced proliferation in a dose-dependent manner.

To investigate whether MMC could induce apoptosis in TGF-β1-treated RPSFs, we conducted flow cytometry analysis. Cells were cotreated with TGF-β1 and MMC, and the apoptosis rate was measured. The results revealed a significant increase in the apoptosis rate among the cotreated cells in a dose-dependent manner (Figure c and d).

In summary, our findings suggest that MMC not only inhibits fibroblast proliferation but also enhances apoptosis, particularly in the fibrotic context induced by TGF-β1.

MMC Inhibited the Expression of Fibrotic Marker Protein

To further explore the inhibitory mechanism of MMC on RPSFs, we conducted immunofluorescence staining on RPSFs treated with 10 ng/mL TGF-β1, 10 ng/mL TGF-β1 plus DMSO (used as a vehicle control), or 10 ng/mL TGF-β1 combined with 4 μg/mL MMC for 24 h. As illustrated in Figure a, TGF-β1 treatment led to a significant increase in the fluorescence intensity of α-SMA, indicating an upregulation related to fibroblast activation and the fibrotic response. In contrast, treatment with MMC substantially reduced the fluorescence intensity of α-SMA, suggesting that MMC effectively inhibits the TGF-β1-induced transition to myofibroblasts in RPSFs.

Western blot analysis was performed to quantify the levels of E-cadherin, N-cadherin, and α-SMA in RPSFs subjected to the different treatments. The results confirmed that the protein expression levels of α-SMA aligned with the immunofluorescence findings, showing a marked increase in response to TGF-β1 treatment, which was significantly diminished by MMC treatment (Figure b and e). Furthermore, MMC treatment led to an increase in the protein expression levels of E-cadherin while concurrently decreasing the expression levels of N-cadherin (Figure c and d).

Overall, our findings suggest that MMC plays a role in inhibiting the progression of fibrosis.

LDC@MA@PLGA Inhibits Fibrosis after Canalicular Laceration Repairment in Rabbits

To further investigate the therapeutic potential of LDC@MA@PLGA in vivo, we established a rabbit canalicular laceration model. This model serves as an effective platform for assessing the antifibrotic effects of our drug delivery system postsurgery. Thirty male New Zealand White rabbits, each weighing between 2.0 and 2.5 kg, were randomly allocated into six groups (n = 5): surgery control group, surgery group, LDC@A@PLGA group, LDC@MA-1@PLGA group, LDC@MA-2@PLGA group, and LDC@MA-3@PLGA group.

Animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Jinling Hospital and conducted in concordance with the guidelines of Association for Research in Vision and Ophthalmology (ARVO) for the use of animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research. The rabbit canalicular laceration model was established following previously described methodologies. In brief, each rabbit was anesthetized via an intravenous injection of 3% pentobarbital sodium (1 mL/kg). The punctum was located and gently dilated using a punctal dilator (Figure a). After dilation, the lacrimal duct was irrigated with fluorescein dye to ensure the patency of the lacrimal passage (Figure b). The presence of visible fluorescence in the external nostril confirmed the patency of the lacrimal passage (Figure c). Once patency was verified, a lacrimal probe was inserted through the canaliculus, and a point 3 mm from the punctum was measured and marked (Figure d). The canaliculus was then severed at the marked point using a 15-degree blade (Figure e). The ability of the lacrimal probe to pass through the incision indicates successful dissection of the lacrimal passage (Figure f). A prefabricated catheter tail was stitched using 10–0 nylon sutures and subsequently inserted through the punctum (Figure g). The canalicular laceration was repaired with 8–0 absorbable sutures (Figure h), and the catheter tail was secured to the punctum (Figure i).

(d) Insertion of the lacrimal probe and marking of the laceration point. (e) Cutting canaliculus at the marked point. (f) Dissection of the lacrimal passage. (g) Insertion of the lacrimal drainage catheter. (h) Repair the canalicular laceration. (i) Fixation of the lacrimal drainage catheter to the punctum.

A month later, the sutures were removed, the catheter was extubated (Figure a), and the average diameter of the catheter were 1.25 ± 0.19 mm. Although some expansion of the lacrimal duct has been observed following treatment, it remains within the acceptable range, which is a critical consideration in the context of surgical repair and therapeutic interventions. The patency of the lacrimal duct was confirmed as previously described. A lacrimal probe was inserted into the lacrimal sac. A 10 mm skin incision was made along the lower eyelid margin, extending from the inner canthus. The subcutaneous tissue was then dissected to expose the probe and the lacrimal sac. The canalicular and surrounding tissue were harvested via dissection around the lacrimal sac (Figure b). To facilitate the presentation of the canaliculus in sections, an appropriate length of catheter was inserted into the harvested canaliculus, and the two ends of the catheter were secured to the tissue with 8–0 absorbable sutures (Figure c).

Catheter extubation and tissue retrieval. (a) The extubated catheter. (b) Intraoperative image demonstrating the retrieval of canalicular tissue with the probe in place. (c) Harvested canalicular and surrounding tissue.

As shown in Figure a, histological examination of the canalicular tissues from the control group revealed a normal structure, characterized by a well-organized epithelial layer and intact mucosal architecture. However, significant disorganization was observed in the epithelial layer of the surgery and LDC@A@PLGA groups, including subepithelial fibrosis, inflammatory cell infiltration, exfoliation of epithelial cells and disappearance of the mucosal epithelial structure. Such alterations are indicative of aberrant healing processes that can lead to complications in canalicular repair. However, these detrimental changes were significantly reduced in the LDC@MA-1@PLGA, LDC@MA-2@PLGA, and LDC@MA-3@PLGA groups (Figure a), suggesting that our formulations effectively mitigate the adverse effects associated with surgical trauma. Masson’s trichrome staining demonstrated increased collagen fibers (blue) in the surgery and LDC@A@PLGA groups, while collagen content was reduced following treatment with LDC@MA-1@PLGA, LDC@MA-2@PLGA, and LDC@MA-3@PLGA. Interestingly, we observed no significant difference in collagen content between the LDC@MA-2@PLGA and LDC@MA-3@PLGA groups (Figure c), suggesting that both formulations achieve comparable levels of effectiveness in reducing collagen deposition.

Additionally, we conducted immunofluorescence staining to assess the expression levels of α-SMA in the canalicular tissues. The number of α-SMA-positive cells was significantly increased in the surgery group compared to the control group; however, treatment with LDC@MA-1@PLGA, LDC@MA-2@PLGA, and LDC@MA-3@PLGA led to a significant reduction in these cells (Figure b).

To further support our histological observations, we utilized RT-PCR to quantify the mRNA expression levels of α-SMA in the canalicular tissues. In line with our immunofluorescence findings, the mRNA levels of α-SMA were significantly higher in the surgery group than in the control group. Importantly, treatment with LDC@MA@PLGA significantly decreased the mRNA expression levels of α-SMA induced by canalicular laceration, with no significant difference observed between the LDC@MA-2@PLGA and LDC@MA-3@PLGA groups (Figure d). This result suggests that the therapeutic effects of LDC@MA-2@PLGA and LDC@MA-3@PLGA are comparable.

In summary, our results demonstrate that LDC@MA@PLGA effectively inhibits fibrosis following canalicular laceration repair in rabbits.

Discussion

Excessive proliferation and fibrosis following canalicular anastomosis oftend diminish the success of the procedure, potentially resulting in canalicular stenosis or obstruction at the anastomosis site, which may cause lacrimal drainage dysfunction, leading to epiphora. To enhance the success rate of canalicular anastomosis, bicanalicular stent intubation has emerged as a critical advancement in the field of lacrimal duct repair. Furthermore, MMC functions by inhibiting fibroblast proliferation and collagen synthesis, which reduces scar formation at the surgical site. This approach has demonstrated promising results in various ophthalmic procedures. − In this study, we loaded MMC onto the LDC to establish a dual-layered drug delivery system, in which MMC and the LDC were effectively combined to jointly reduce fibrotic complications and promote tissue healing, and minimize the side effects of MMC.

Agarose has been widely utilized for the delivery of various drugs, including proteins, peptides, chemotherapeutics, DNA, and cells, due to its unique advantages. To address this problem, blending and coupling are regarded as simple and effective methods. Fibroblasts are indeed essential components of the wound healing process. Their entry into the provisional matrix after tissue injury is a critical step that significantly influences the healing outcome and the potential development of fibrosis. Moreover, an optimized release profile ensures that an adequate concentration of the drug reaches the target site promptly, providing immediate therapeutic effects while also maintaining sustained release for prolonged efficacy. Therefore, the amount and efficiency of drugs released by our drug delivery systems are critical factors for achieving therapeutic success. Our previous research demonstrated that MMC loaded onto agarose was released rapidly within a few hours. Additionally, we found that controlling drug release from the agarose microbead matrix could be achieved through layer-by-layer assembly of thin-film polymers. In this study, we propose utilizing PLGA to encapsulate agarose, further refining the release profile of MMC. This design integrates the high drug-loading capacity of hydrogels with the precise controlled release capabilities of hydrophobic coatings, representing a widely adopted strategy for long-acting formulations and targeted drug delivery systems. In this study, the release of MMC from our formulation, LDC@MA@PLGA, achieved highly satisfactory results. The potential mechanism by which PLGA inhibits the burst release of MMC is attributed to the physical barrier effect of its hydrophobic coating. Interestingly, we observed that the concentration of MMC within the MA coating plays a critical role in modulating release efficiency. Specifically, formulations with varying MMC concentrations yielded different release rates, for example, LDC@MA-2@PLGA exhibited the highest release rate, while LDC@MA-3@PLGA showed a slightly lower efficiency. By varying the concentration of MMC in the agarose matrix, we can achieve tailored release profiles that may enhance the therapeutic efficacy of the dual-layered drug delivery system. However, there is a threshold concentration of MMC in the agarose matrix beyond which the release rates become comparable across different formulations at a given time point. This variation may arise from factors such as saturation of the PLGA matrix or alterations in drug-polymer interactions at higher MMC concentrations. In summary, our research underscores the potential of utilizing PLGA to encapsulate agarose for enhanced drug delivery systems, demonstrated that careful optimization of drug loading and polymer interactions can significantly influence the release profiles.

TGF-β1 is a critical cytokine involved in various biological processes, particularly in the context of tissue repair and fibrosis. Its role in upregulating α-SMA expression, which is frequently used as a marker for the myofibroblast phenotype, is essential for the differentiation of fibroblasts into myofibroblasts. In our study, we confirmed that MMC exerts its antifibrotic effects on RPSFs by inhibiting the expression of α-SMA, thereby reducing myofibroblast activation in response to TGF-β1. Additionally, MMC enhances E-cadherin expression while decreasing N-cadherin levels, indicating a reversal of the Epithelial-Mesenchymal Transition (EMT) process and a potential restoration of epithelial characteristics in RPSFs. Previous studies have indicated that apoptosis is a form of cell death that does not elicit an inflammatory response, and may therefore play a significant role in the resolution of inflammation and fibrogenesis. MMC has been shown to induce apoptosis in cells, as reported in previous studies. In our study, we confirmed that MMC enhance the pro-apoptotic effects in fibroblasts that have been activated by TGF-β1. The dose–response relationship observed implies that MMC may effectively promote apoptosis in these cells, potentially counteracting the fibrotic effects induced by TGF-β1. Collectively, these results demonstrate that this dual action is crucial as it addresses both the proliferation of fibrotic cells and promotes the programmed cell death necessary for mitigating excessive scarring.

In our study, we demonstrate that LDC@MA@PLGA effectively inhibits fibrosis following canalicular laceration repair in rabbits. The histological, immunofluorescence, and RT-PCR analyses collectively confirm the potential of LDC@MA@PLGA as a promising therapeutic strategy to limit fibrotic complications and promoting organized healing in canalicular laceration. Canalicular anastomosis combined with canalicular stent intubation is an effective method for repairing primary canalicular lacerations. This technique not only restoring a normal anatomical pathway but also enhances the healing process by preventing obstruction and facilitating drainage. Our study utilized MMC-coated LDC, which not only retain the inherent advantages of canalicular stent but also further inhibit fibrosis through the action of MMC. This sustained release pattern is beneficial for therapeutic applications, as it allows for prolonged drug exposure, potentially enhancing the efficacy of treatment while minimizing the frequency of dosing.

However, this study has several limitations that require further consideration. First, this study concentrated exclusively on the degree of fibrosis and did not assess the function of the lacrimal duct. Assessing lacrimal duct function is essential, as successful repair should not only reduce fibrosis but also restore normal tear drainage and overall ocular health. Second, we focused on a single fibrosis marker protein to evaluate the degree of fibrosis. While α-SMA is a well-established indicator of myofibroblast activation and fibrosis, relying solely on a single marker may not fully encompass the spectrum of fibrotic processes. Finally, this study was only observed for one month, which may be insufficient to capture the long-term effects of treatment. By addressing these limitations, future research can provide a more comprehensive evaluation of LDC@MA@PLGA’s therapeutic potential and its implications for clinical practice in managing canalicular lacerations and associated fibrosis.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study presents a significant advancement in the management of canalicular lacerations through the development of the LDC@MA@PLGA drug delivery system. The successful fabrication of this dual-layered drug delivery system, featuring controlled release of MMC, not only enhances the local concentration of the antifibrotic agent but also minimizes systemic exposure, thereby reducing the potential for side effects. In vitro assays demonstrated that MMC not only inhibits fibroblast proliferation but also enhances apoptosis. In vivo experiments demonstrate that LDC@MA@PLGA effectively inhibits fibrosis and enhances healing outcomes following canalicular laceration repair in rabbits. This innovative approach not only addresses the challenges associated with traditional treatments but also opens new avenues for research in ocular therapeutics.
