Interpersonal communication anxiety in adolescents: scale development and the mediating role of cognitive flexibility in the relationship between perfectionism and anxiety
Authors: Mustafa Onur Kan, Sezai Demi̇r
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe present study was planned in two stages. The initial stage entailed the development of an interpersonal communication anxiety scale for adolescents. The subsequent stage involved an examination of the mediating role of cognitive flexibility in the effect of perfectionism on interpersonal communication anxiety in adolescents. In order to achieve the aforementioned objectives, data were collected from a total of 1,643 adolescents (829 girls, 814 boys; age, = 14.8) aged between 12 and 17 years for both studies. In the first stage of the study, data was collected from 1029 adolescents (519 girls, 510 boys; age, =14.9) attending schools in four different residential areas. In the second stage, data obtained from 614 adolescents (age, =14.6), comprising 310 girls (age, =14.4) and 304 boys (age, =14.8), who were different from those in the first stage, were analyzed. In the first stage of the study, the Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) of the Interpersonal Communication Anxiety Scale revealed that the 12-item single factor explained 69.22% of the total variance. The results of the Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) demonstrated that the model exhibited excellent or good fit, and the model fit values were again excellent in terms of measurement invariance by gender. The internal reliability Cronbach’s Alpha coefficient was 0.96. In this respect, it was determined that the Interpersonal Communication Anxiety Scale is a valid and reliable measurement tool for adolescents in Turkish culture, with no variation according to gender. In the second stage of the study, when cognitive flexibility and self-directed perfectionism acted as mediators between adolescents’ interpersonal communication anxiety, the total effect coefficient was significantly reduced (− 0.12; 0.02). In a similar manner, when cognitive flexibility mediated the relationship between socially prescribed perfectionism and interpersonal communication anxiety, the total effect coefficient was found to decrease significantly (− 0.12; 0.00). Consequently, it has been determined that cognitive flexibility in adolescents serves as a pivotal mediating factor between self-oriented perfectionism, socially prescribed perfectionism, and interpersonal communication anxiety.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-025-03583-2.Keywords: Interpersonal communication anxiety, Self-oriented perfectionism, Socially prescribed perfectionism, Cognitive flexibility, Adolescents

Introduction

Adolescence is characterized by rapid emotional development [1, 2] and intense anxiety [3, 4]. One of these concerns in adolescence is communication anxiety [5]. Based on McCroskey’s [6] definition, this type of anxiety is the individual’s level of fear about the expected communication with one or many people. Interpersonal communication anxiety (ICA) is characterized by interconnected phenomena including shyness, timidity, and an aversion to communication [7, 8]. It is important to note that anxiety is not the sole cause of mutual communication anxiety. In certain cases, the individual may harbor a personal aversion to the subject matter they are required to communicate on behalf of another. Another reason for avoiding interpersonal communication is the person’s belief that they will fail in the subject they need to communicate about [9, 10]. Individuals experiencing elevated anxiety levels often demonstrate impaired performance in communication activities. This phenomenon is associated with a cognitive process in which individuals perceive themselves as unsuccessful [7, 9]. The persuasion of the individual to believe in their own success has been demonstrated to be an effective strategy in reducing communication anxiety [7]. Adolescents’ manifest communication anxiety predominantly through withdrawal, reticence, shyness, avoidance of peer and other social interactions, absence of dialogue and unwillingness to participate in public speaking [7, 11]. Communication anxiety, on the other hand, is categorized as situational and trait, person-group, situational, generalized contextual and trait communication anxiety. Whilst person-group and situational communication anxiety is mainly associated with the other person and situations that the person deems significant, generalized contextual communication anxiety is linked to public speaking anxiety. Trait communication anxiety includes interpersonal verbal communication anxiety and is directly related to personality [12]. The etiology of communication anxiety has been identified as being attributable to genetic predisposition [13], reinforcement [11, 14], modelling [15–17] and learned helplessness [12, 18]. As posited by [18] one such reason pertains to the notion of learned helplessness, which is associated with an individual’s cognitive processes. This phenomenon entails the tendency for individuals to consistently orient their cognitive processes towards unfavorable expectations. The phenomenon of experiencing anxiety due to the constant anticipation of unfavorable outcomes is associated with the concept of cognitive flexibility in communication anxiety.

Cognitive flexibility is defined as the ability to adapt to new situations [19] and to modify cognitions in accordance with changing environmental factors [20]. Cognitive flexibility is a widely recognized adaptation skill that an individual develops in response to new situations [21]. It may be hypothesized that cognitive flexibility assumes greater significance in the context of adapting to new situations, a phenomenon that is thought to be precipitated by the augmentation of outward-oriented relationships during adolescence. It has been established that individuals who possess cognitive flexibility regard themselves as being adequately skilled in interpersonal relationships [19]. The hypothesis is that the flexible cognitive structure, which has the capacity to adapt to people and events, functions as a facilitator against anxiety and stress in interpersonal relationships. In addition, it is known that individuals who possess cognitive flexibility have expectations of themselves that are flexible and appropriate to their capacities [22]. Furthermore, individuals who possess cognitive flexibility have been shown to have strong perceptions and to be more open to their social environment, other people, and communication [23]. It has been established that individuals who are receptive to communication have the capacity to adapt their cognitive flexibility in response to challenging circumstances and individuals [24]. Cognitive flexibility has been demonstrated to facilitate adaptability in communication in situations that induce stress and anxiety [25]. Such behaviors manifest through expectations that exceed an individual’s capabilities, the presence of inflexible beliefs and standards, and persistent self-criticism [20].

Perfectionism is defined as the pursuit and maintenance of standards that are considered unrealistic [26] and the attainment of perfection [27]. The concept of perfectionism has also been defined in a variety of forms. It was defined by Hewitt et al. [28] by considering intrapersonal and interpersonal dimensions. The intrapersonal dimension consists of self-oriented perceptions of self-perfection, whereas the interpersonal dimension consists of socially orientated perceptions of others’ perfection. Consequently, perfectionism is widely regarded as a detrimental concept [29]. Furthermore, it has been demonstrated that perfectionism exhibits a strong correlation with the propensity for anxiety, with the pursuit of perfection frequently giving rise to numerous emotional challenges, including feelings of anxiety [30–32]. The negative aspects of perfectionism include shame and excessive sensitivity in interpersonal relationships [33, 34], social stress, and high anxiety [35, 36]. This phenomenon is attributed to a deficiency in cognitive flexibility. Individuals with limited cognitive flexibility are incapable of altering the direction of their thinking by means of constant self-criticism and may consequently exhibit avoidance behavior as a result of their inability to adapt to their environment [20]. Conversely, it has been established that perfectionist young people exhibit a conscience that is associated with diminished flexibility, consequently manifesting heightened levels of neuroticism [37]. The following essay will provide a comprehensive overview of the relevant literature on the subject.

Present study

Communication anxiety, which has been demonstrated to exhibit a robust correlation with social anxiety [38], assumes particular significance during the adolescent phase. Furthermore, this anxiety can also develop during planned or spontaneous conversations with acquaintances or anyone in daily life [39, 40]. It is clear that anxiety developing in interpersonal communication affects peer acceptance, many social characteristics, and academic achievement of students attending university or secondary school [41, 42]. The importance of determining the level of interpersonal communication anxiety, which is effective in the developmental process of adolescents, is evident. In this context, it has been argued that the use of non-test instruments to measure communication anxiety is not conducive to objectivity [43, 44] and therefore disregards individual differences [45]. Consequently, in contrast to observations and personal reports, the necessity of developing a measurement tool for communication anxiety has been emphasized [41, 46]. In the context of speech anxiety, tools have been developed for the assessment of public speaking anxiety, with a focus on undergraduate students [47–50]. It has also been observed that measurement tools developed to assess interpersonal communication anxiety as a subscale are aimed at undergraduate students [41, 51, 52] and the business world [53]. Furthermore, a review of the WoS and Scopus databases reveals that no measurement tool specific to Turkish culture has been developed to assess interpersonal communication anxiety among adolescents. It is understood that interpersonal communication anxiety affects both academic achievement and personality during adolescence. In this context, it is hypothesized that an adolescent-specific communication anxiety scale will contribute to academic achievement, personality development, and the development of close relationships.

It has been established that individuals experiencing communication anxiety tend to exhibit low self-confidence, and that low self-confidence, in turn, can lead to introversion and communication anxiety over time [54, 55]. Furthermore, communication anxiety has been demonstrated to precipitate withdrawal in interpersonal relationships with members of the opposite sex, pedagogues, and acquaintances [13, 56, 57]. It has also been observed that this cycle can result in academic failure [58, 59] and the deferral of matters related to academic achievement [60]. It is known that interpersonal communication anxiety leads to withdrawal and the development of negative beliefs in the cognitive system, which become more prevalent in later years of life [61]. Conversely, it has been documented that social anxiety arising from communication anxiety can result in selective mutism [62] and mood disorders in two-thirds of cases [63]. In this context, the development of a measurement tool for interpersonal communication anxiety and the identification of other variables that influence this concept are considered to be of importance in the design of preventive and intervention studies for students’ academic and emotional problems.

It is widely acknowledged that there exists a correlation between communication anxiety and a multitude of personality traits, including introversion/extroversion, emotional stability, and self-esteem [64–66]. It has been posited that personality traits, particularly perfectionism, should be examined in conjunction with other concepts throughout the educational process [67]. Shimotsu & Mottet [68], observations indicated a favorable correlation between negative perfectionism and interpersonal communication anxiety. In accordance with this theoretical framework, Molnar et al. [69], reported in their study with adolescents that the psychological well-being of socially and self-oriented perfectionist students was lower than that of non-perfectionist students. Barabadi et al. [70], in a study with Iranian high school students, found that students prone to selfish perfectionism were more inclined to communicate because they tended to think less about their mistakes than students prone to socially prescribed perfectionism. In a study conducted on 209 undergraduate students, Kawamura et al. [31], determined that perfectionism demonstrated the strongest correlation with social anxiety among the various types of anxiety. Another variable that has been demonstrated to be effective in this relationship is negative evaluation, which is a cognitive output. In a similar vein, Gautreau et al. [71], has proposed that individuals who exhibit perfectionist tendencies tend to display elevated levels of self-criticism. In addition to perfectionist traits, as discussed in this study, cognitive processes related to personality are also thought to be an important factor in communication anxiety.

As posited by Beatty et al. [72], the experience of anxiety during communication is hypothesized to be influenced by cognitive processes. McCroskey [6], posits that communication anxiety evolves into a persistent cognitive framework stemming from learned helplessness in the context of familial and environmental pressures. The fundamental issue addressed here is the individual’s inflexible beliefs regarding expected negative evaluation and the resulting response [39]. In this specific context, McCroskey [6], reported a high correlation between communication anxiety and dogmatic personality structure. Conversely, adolescents who experience minimal anxiety have been shown to exhibit a capacity for adopting a more rational and flexible cognitive approach [73]. Adolescents facing numerous changes may feel compelled to present themselves as flawless in their social environments [74]. Consequently, adolescents may avoid social interaction due to cognitive errors [75, 76]. It has been emphasized that social anxiety also negatively affects close communication and that it is important to examine the cognitive processes that influence anxiety in communication [77]. It has been asserted that competence is a pivotal factor in communication anxiety, and consequently, it is imperative to address the cognitive processes that underpin competence [78].

Empirical studies and theoretical literature suggest that communication anxiety, which is closely related to social anxiety, may be a contributing factor to withdrawal, academic failure, and the avoidance of close vertical and horizontal relationships during adolescence, a critical period of development. However, during adolescence, self-directed and socially prescribed perfectionism, in conjunction with the cognitive processes of negative self-evaluation and the belief that others will think badly of them, have been demonstrated to exert an influence on social anxiety. It is hypothesized that adolescents will experience communication anxiety due to avoidance and fear of criticism. This study, which developed a communication anxiety scale specific to adolescents, also examines the role of perfectionism, considered cognitively rigid, and cognitive flexibility in the emergence of this anxiety. Consequently, it is anticipated that this study will facilitate a more comprehensive evaluation of the adverse consequences of communication anxiety [38], which is observed in 97% of individuals experiencing social anxiety, among adolescents.

In this context, the study is divided into two stages. In the first stage, the aim is solely to develop a measurement tool for adolescents’ interpersonal communication anxiety. The development of the measurement tool has been further enriched with the objective of determining measurement invariance (MI) according to gender. Subsequently, in the second stage, the aim is to examine the mediating role of cognitive flexibility in the relationship between adolescents’ self-focused and socially prescribed perfectionism and interpersonal communication anxiety. Method

The present study was conceived as a descriptive research study, employing a relational survey model. In the first stage, the objective was to develop a valid and reliable measurement tool that could be used to assess interpersonal communication anxiety in adolescents aged 12–17 years. In the second stage, the mediating role of cognitive flexibility in the effect of self-oriented and socially prescribed perfectionism on interpersonal communication anxiety in adolescents was examined.

Study group and data collection

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Hatay Mustafa Kemal University Social and Human Sciences Ethics Committee (permission dated 04/07/2024 and number 7). Informed consent was obtained from all individual participants included in the study. As the participants were under the age of 18, their parents were informed in writing about the purpose and subject of the study. Written consent forms were obtained from parents regarding their children’s participation in the study. In this research process, a convenience sample type that is suitable for the purpose was selected in order to determine the level of both scale development and the level of relationships between variables. The study group consisted of adolescents residing in four different locations (provinces) in Turkey. Participants in Studies 1 and 2 included adolescents from diverse educational institutions. Therefore, it can be concluded that the adolescents in the Study 1 and Study 2 groups were not the same individuals. For both Study 1 (scale development) and Study 2 (model validation), a letter elucidating the purpose of the study was distributed to the parents of adolescents. Parental consent forms were obtained. In Study 1, data were collected from 1050 adolescents aged 12 to 17 years, comprising 529 girls (age, = 14.8) and 521 boys (age, = 15.0) for the scale development process. Following the exclusion of 21 data sets that contained missing data, the remaining 1050 data sets were analyzed. Focus group interviews were conducted with 13 participants (7 girls, 6 boys; age, = 14.6) from the data collected from 1029 adolescents (519 girls, 510 boys; age, = 14.9). Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was performed on data collected from 471 adolescents (237 girls, 234 boys; age, = 14.5). Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) and measurement invariance by gender were performed on data collected from 267 adolescents (139 girls, 128 boys; age, = 14.6). Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) and multi-group confirmatory factor analysis for measurement invariance by gender were performed on data collected from 168 adolescents (80 girls, 88 boys; age, = 15.5). This data was also used to assess criterion scale validity and test-retest reliability (nine of whom could not participate in the test-retest measurement). Internal consistency coefficients were determined using data collected from 110 adolescents (56 girls, 54 boys; age, = 15.2).

The data for the relational model in Study 2 were collected face-to-face from 646 adolescents aged 12 to 17 years in other schools in two locations not included in Study 1’s study group. After excluding 32 incomplete data sets, 614 data sets were obtained. Correlational analyses were conducted using data collected from a total of 614 adolescents (age, =14.6), comprising 310 girls (age, = 14.4) and 304 boys (age, = 14.8). Therefore, valid data were collected from a total of 1643 adolescents (829 girls, 814 boys; age, = 14.8) through face-to-face interviews for each study, with Study 1 and Study 2 differing.

Data collection tools

Turkish version of cognitive flexibility scale (T-CFS)

The scale was developed by Martin &Rubin [79]; and validity and reliability studies for Turkish culture were conducted by Çelikkaleli [80]. Accordingly, the unidimensional structure of the Turkish form of the scale, whose original form consisted of 12 items, obtained as a result of the exploratory factor analysis, was found to be compatible with the structure obtained by CFA. The T-CFS, a 6-point Likert-type measurement tool, is scored from 1 (strongly disagree) to 6 (strongly agree). In Çelikkaleli’s adaptation study, the internal consistency coefficients in three different samples were obtained as 0.74, 0.73 and 0.75 respectively [80]. The coefficient obtained for the two-half reliability of the scale is 0.77 in all samples. In addition, a correlation of 0.98 was found between the two applications in the test-retest reliability study, and as for language validity, a correlation of 0.88 was obtained between the original English form and the Turkish form [80]. In the present study, the internal reliability of Cronbach’s Alpha coefficient was determined to be 0.96.

Turkish version of child and adolescent perfectionism scale (T-CAPS)

The 22-item Likert-type scale developed by Flett et al. [81]; was adapted into Turkish by Bas &Siyez [82]. The 5-point Likert-type scale is comprised of two dimensions: self-oriented perfectionism and socially prescribed perfectionism. The scale was composed of 18 items following the adaptation process in Türkiye. In the scoring of the scale, only the total score of the sub-dimensions is obtained. It is not possible to obtain the overall total score of the scale. The internal reliability of the scale was determined by calculating the Cronbach’s Alpha coefficient, which yielded a value of 0.85 for the Self-Oriented Perfectionism dimension and 0.89 for the Socially Prescribed Perfectionism dimension [82]. Within the scope of present study, the internal reliability of the scale was 0.91 for the Self-Oriented Perfectionism dimension and 0.96 for the Socially Prescribed Perfectionism dimension.

Turkish version of social anxiety scale for adolescents (T-SAS-A)

The measurement tool was initially developed by LaGreca et al. [83]; to ascertain the level of social anxiety in children, and was subsequently adapted for use with adolescents by LaGreca & Lopez [84]. This adaptation involved the removal of four items deemed to be fillers, resulting in a final version comprising 22 items. The 3-factor structure of the tool encompasses “fear of negative evaluation”, “avoidance and uneasiness in general situations” and “avoidance and uneasiness in new situations”. In the adaptation of the measurement tool into Turkish, it was determined that 22 items and a three-factor structure could be utilized, with internal consistency coefficients of (ODK: 0.83), (G-SHKD: 0.68) and (Y-SHKD: 0.71), and a correlation value with the social phobia scale for children and adolescents (r = 0.75). A study undertaken by Aydın & Tekinav-Sütcü [85] examined the applicability of the half reliability coefficients of a Likert-type scale to Turkish culture. The results demonstrated that the coefficients ranged from 0.67 to 0.85, indicating a relatively strong reliability. The scale utilized a five-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 to 5, with items not reverse-scored. A general total score is obtained from the scale, with the exception of the dimensions. In present study, the internal reliability coefficient of the total score was found to be 0.84.

Interpersonal communication anxiety scale (ICAS)

In order to create the items of the measurement tool developed within the scope of the current research, a literature review and a focus group interview with 13 adolescents (7 girls, 6 boys; age, =14.4) were conducted. As a result, 36 items were written. Subsequently, the opinions of six experts were obtained with regard to the measurement tool in question. This tool was then reduced to 21 items as a consequence of the validation of its content and language. In the ‘Findings of Study 1’ section, validity and reliability analyses of the measurement tool were performed. As a result of these procedures, it was determined that the measurement tool exhibited sufficient validity and reliability to assess interpersonal communication anxiety in adolescents aged 11–17 years. In the Likert-type scale (see Appendix A), which consists of a total of 12 items, there are no reverse-scored items. The responses are collected using a 5-point Likert scale, with options ranging from ‘Never’ to ‘Always’.

Data analysis

For the scale development phase of the present study; firstly, a focus group interview was conducted with adolescents. Following the content analysis, a consensus was reached on the identification of common items. Construct validity was then established by performing the KMO Bartlett test, Horn’s Parallel Analysis, Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA), and Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) in AMOS using the SPSS software. Multi Group Confirmatory Factor Analysis (MGCFA) was conducted to ascertain the measurement invariance of the measurement tool between gender-related groups. Composite Reliability (CR) analyses were performed after the exploratory factor analysis for internal reliability between items, and Average Variance Extracted (AVE) analyses were conducted to determine convergent validity after CFA. Internal reliability values were determined by Cronbach Alpha, McDonalds omega (ω) and Guttman Split Half coefficient. Pearson Product Moment Correlation coefficients were utilized to ascertain criterion scale validity and test-retest reliability.

In order to validate the structural equation model in Study 2, Skewness-Kurtosis coefficients were initially determined to ascertain whether the data were normally distributed. Subsequently, a descriptive analysis was conducted on the average minimum and maximum scores obtained from the scales utilized in the research. Cronbach Alpha internal reliability coefficients were determined for the reliability of the measurement tools to be used in the research. Pearson Product Moment coefficients were calculated for correlational relationships between variables, and Tolerance and VIF values were calculated to reveal whether there was multicollinearity. As indicated in the findings, it is understood that there is no multicollinearity problem. Accordingly, the bootstrapping method was employed for the mediation analysis in the structural model within the scope of the current research through the Bootstrapping [86].

In the bootstrapping method, it is essential to calculate confidence intervals for the relationships between variables. The fact that the confidence interval obtained lies within the same direction as the lowest and highest values, and does not include zero, regardless of the positive or negative values, indicates that the obtained finding is reliable. In the analysis conducted in the present study, the resampling number was determined as 5000 and the confidence intervals were determined as 0.95.

Findings

The findings presented herein are based on the development of the Interpersonal Communication Anxiety Scale (Study 1) and, subsequently, on the examination of the mediating role of cognitive flexibility in the relationship between self-focused perfectionism and socially prescribed perfectionism and interpersonal communication anxiety (Study 2).

Study 1 interpersonal communication anxiety scale (ICAS) validity and reliability findings

The psychometric properties of the Interpersonal Communication Anxiety Scale were determined by conducting validity and reliability analyses.

Validity studies

The initial phase in the development of the measurement tool is item writing. In this context, a literature review was conducted to inform the study. The review focused on attitudes towards social anxiety and communication anxiety in adolescents, as well as the characteristics of adolescents with communication anxiety. Furthermore, research articles pertaining to previously developed scales related to communication and social anxiety were analyzed. Accordingly, it was ascertained that interpersonal communication anxiety is characterized by three distinct dimensions: avoidance, anxiety, and reluctance to communicate [7, 8]. It was understood that these dimensions needed to be expressed in physical, emotional (fear, anxiety) and cognitive (e.g. expectation of humiliation, expectation of criticism) terms in order to possess Likert-type scale characteristics [87]. Consequently, an interview form was meticulously designed to ascertain how adolescents experience reciprocal communication anxiety in this context. A total of 13 adolescents (7 girls and 6 boys; age, = 14.4 years) were interviewed, and their responses were analyzed by the researchers to ascertain whether they represented interpersonal communication anxiety in terms of content validity. In this analysis, expressions which have been reiterated by a least three adolescents have been identified as common items. Consequently, these two processes resulted in the development of a 34-item form. In order for the total of 34 items to adequately represent interpersonal communication anxiety, it was designed to include a brief information note containing the three core components of avoidance, anxiety, and reluctance to engage in the topic [7, 8]. The information note was presented to three faculty members from the Guidance and Psychological Counselling field, one from the Measurement and Evaluation field, and two from the Linguistics field. In order to ascertain whether the draft scale items represented interpersonal communication anxiety in terms of content, they were initially sent to experts in the field of Guidance and Psychological Counselling. During this process, 21 items on which at least two faculty members agreed were identified, and 13 items were removed because, according to the experts’ opinions, they did not represent the structure and were semantically confused with the concepts of self-confidence and self-esteem. Subsequently, the opinions of experts in the field of Measurement and Evaluation were sought in order to determine whether the 21 items contained measurable statements. In addition, experts in the field of Linguistics were consulted with regard to expression and comprehensibility. Consequently, following revisions in terms of measurability, expression, and comprehensibility, 21 items were obtained. The decision was taken that the items would consist of positive statements in a 5-point Likert scale with response options ranging from ‘never’ to ‘always’. Initially, Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA), composite reliability (CR) analysis, Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA), multi-group CFA for measurement invariance, criterion-related validity, and Average Variance Extracted (AVE) analysis were conducted on the items in question.

A normal distribution test was performed on 21 items of data obtained from 471 adolescents (237 girls, 234 boys; age = 14.5) aged between 12 and 17 years. In the absence of conducting a factor analysis, it was observed that the Skewness values (0.221–0.224) and Kurtosis values (− 0.212 and − 0.245) varied between (221-0.224.224.224.224) and the distribution was found to be normal according to the criteria [88] that the Skewness coefficient should be between − 3.3 and + 3.3 and the Kurtosis coefficient should be between − 7 and + 7 in the normality assumption [89]. The KMO and Barlett analyses were performed on a dataset comprising over 21 items, with the objective of assessing the sample size and the multivariate normality assumption. Following the analysis, the KMO value was found to be 0.967 (< 0.80), and the result of the Bartlett Sphericity Test (χ2 = 5200.265; df = 66; p = 0.000; p < 0.001) was obtained. In this particular instance, it was observed that the sample size and the normality assumption were both met (p < 0.001). Criterion scale, average variance extracted (AVE) and composite reliability (CR) analysis validity results

In order to ascertain the criterion scale validity of ICAS, SAS-A, adapted into Turkish by [85], was utilized. Following the collection of data from 168 adolescents (80 girls, 88 boys; age, = 15.5), the Pearson Product Moment Correlation coefficient between ICAS and SAS-A was determined to be 0.82 (p < 0.001). This positive correlation was found to provide criterion scale validity.

Average Variance Extracted (AVE) and Composite Reliability (CR) analyses were needed to determine convergent validity and internal consistency of the scale items, respectively. It is recommended that Composite Reliability (CR) analysis be performed with the factor loadings obtained from exploratory factor analysis before CFA [90]. For this reason, Composite Reliability (CR) was performed on the factor loadings obtained from the exploratory factor analysis and Average Variance Extracted (AVE) analysis was performed after CFA. The analysis yielded a CR value of 0.94 and an AVE value of 0.59. Hair et al. [90] stated that CR ≥ 0.70 and AVE ≥ 0.50 should be met. In light of this evidence, it is concluded that ICAS meets the criteria of convergent validity. Consequently, it was determined that the 12-item structure of ICAS was valid.

Self-oriented and socially prescribed perfectionism explained 37% of the total variance of interpersonal communication anxiety through cognitive flexibility. In conclusion, the findings indicate that cognitive flexibility plays a significant mediating role in the relationship between self-oriented perfectionism and socially prescribed perfectionism and interpersonal communication anxiety in adolescents.

Discussion and conclusion

In the first stage of the current study, the psychometric properties of the Interpersonal Communication Anxiety Scale based on reliability and validity were determined. According to the findings, a unidimensional 12-item measurement tool that can assess interpersonal communication anxiety appropriate for Turkish culture was obtained. To date, the measurement tools capable of quantifying communication anxiety [46, 51–53] encompass dimensions of communication anxiety associated with speaking in groups and public speaking, in addition to one-to-one dialogue. Furthermore, it is evident that no contemporary, valid and reliable measurement tool to assess interpersonal communication anxiety has been developed in the last two decades. The measurement tool obtained as a result of this study is an up-to-date measurement tool with psychometric properties that can only measure one-to-one communication anxiety. In consideration of these characteristics, it is distinguished as the sole contemporary measurement instrument documented in the extant literature.

Existing instruments that can measure interpersonal communication anxiety are generally aimed at university students [51, 52] and adults in the business world [53]. The period of high school, which corresponds with adolescence, has been identified as a time when interpersonal relationships are said to develop [94], academic success is increasingly viewed as a predictor of future success [95], and social anxiety levels rise due to heightened sensitivity [94]. Furthermore, Wang et al. [96], conducted a meta-analysis study to ascertain the factors affecting students’ effective learning processes. The findings of this study indicated that relationships with teachers and friends emerged as the most significant category among the 28 distinct factors influencing learning outcomes. The capacity of adolescents to articulate their thoughts and feelings in an academic setting without experiencing anxiety has been demonstrated to be conducive to their social and emotional development [97, 98]. Furthermore, the visibility of their academic achievements has been shown to positively impact their self-perception and self-esteem [99, 100]. Moreover, the 2002 report of the Centre for Education Statistics in the USA on children’s liking and attendance at school indicates that difficulties in communication are among the primary factors contributing to children’s decision to discontinue their education [101]. In addition, it has been asserted that the age range in which social anxiety is most prevalent is between 11 and 17 years of age [102]. The fact that a measurement tool specific to such a critical period in human life in terms of development and social anxiety has been developed within the scope of the current study has a pioneering feature in terms of the literature.

Adolescents may evade communication with others due to concerns regarding their perceived image in the eyes of others, which is a component of social anxiety. This situation can sometimes be confused with shyness. It is stated that shyness and interpersonal communication anxiety are different concepts [103, 104]. It is evident that the inability to differentiate between concepts may result in the school counsellor employing unsuitable methods and processes in guidance studies. This observation underscores the potential of the interpersonal communication anxiety scale proposed in the present study as a preliminary measurement tool at the point of differentiation. The scale of anxiety in interpersonal communication developed by psychological counselling and grammar experts within the scope of the current study is thought to be an important resource in school guidance studies in terms of determining the factors underlying adolescents’ introversion and avoidance behaviors.

Adolescence is characterized by a period of intense emotional development, with gender-related differences in the experience of this process. Asher & Aderka [105], stated that behaviors such as avoidance in the course of social anxiety do not vary according to gender. However, extant research also indicates that the manifestation of avoidance behaviors in response to social anxiety is significantly more pronounced in girls. While these findings suggest that social anxiety may engender divergent outcomes for girls and boys, it is noteworthy that the extant literature indicates that the impact of social anxiety differs according to gender [56, 105]. In summary, gender is regarded as a significant variable in the measurement of social anxiety and its subcomponent communication anxiety, irrespective of its causative role. It has been demonstrated that the manifestation of anxiety in interpersonal interactions is contingent on social anxiety, rather than on gender, which is a salient variable. ICAS’s measurement invariance according to gender is regarded as a distinctive characteristic. The elimination of measurement bias in studies on interpersonal communication anxiety in adolescents is a key benefit. Consequently, it is hypothesized that the measurement tool yields more reliable results in studies employing ICAS.

The first hypothesis of the present study, ‘the total effect of self-oriented perfectionism on interpersonal communication anxiety in adolescents is positively significant’, was confirmed. A review of the extant literature reveals a paucity of studies that examine the relationship between perfectionist characteristics in adolescents and their tendency towards interpersonal communication anxiety. Adolescents often develop beliefs about academic success and human relations in their inner world due to parental and environmental pressure and anxiety about preparing for the future [106, 107]. These beliefs can be seen as a form of internal pressure that can develop over time, even if they do not originate from external sources. Adolescents who exhibit elevated levels of self-oriented perfectionism may experience an increase in introversion and shyness, though the impact of perfectionism on these behaviors is less pronounced than that of social pressure [108]. It has been posited that cognitive processes related to abstract thinking in adolescents engender a propensity to maintain elevated personal standards, leading to the subsequent development of self-oriented perfectionism [109, 110]. Therefore, this dimension of perfectionism may lead to heightened anxiety in adolescents due to the pressure to avoid making errors and to achieve flawlessness [111]. It is predicted that, given the anxiety’s reflection in social relationships, there may be a positive relationship between self-oriented perfectionism and interpersonal communication anxiety.

The second hypothesis of the study, which was ‘the total effect of socially prescribed perfectionism on interpersonal communication anxiety in adolescents is positively significant’, was confirmed by the findings obtained. It is acknowledged that adolescents exhibit characteristics of social-induced perfectionism, particularly in response to the elevated expectations of their environment [108]. This tendency manifests in their reluctance to communicate, driven by the desire to avoid criticism from their social milieu. A significant body of research has identified a positive correlation between socially prescribed perfectionism and communication anxiety [68] and social anxiety [108, 112] among university students. Given the established correlation between social anxiety and interpersonal communication, it can be concluded that the findings of this study can be extrapolated to a broader context of interpersonal communication anxiety in adolescents. Furthermore, the exclusive focus on adolescents as the study sample, in contrast to the approach adopted by other studies, signifies the pioneering nature of the present study and paves the way for future research in this field.

The hypotheses that the direct effect of socially prescribed and self-oriented perfectionism, which are the dimensions of perfectionism, on cognitive flexibility is positive were confirmed. Akkus-Cutuk [113], found, in a study conducted with university students, that there were significant positive correlations between perfectionism and cognitive flexibility. In the domain of cognitive psychology, the propensity to adopt a rigid, ‘all or nothing’ or ‘ignore the positive’ mindset has been identified as a hallmark feature of perfectionist tendencies [114]. Furthermore, the perfectionist personality structure and the cognitive style unit known as perfectionist attitudes have been shown to be complementary, and as perfectionist attitudes continue, the cognitive style unit becomes more rigid [115]. As can be seen, this is in accordance with the findings of the present study, which demonstrated that perfectionist traits and cognitive flexibility are concepts that support each other. The theoretical underpinnings of this finding are further reinforced by the dearth of alternative studies that offer corroborating evidence, thereby underscoring the study’s originality.

The hypothesis that ‘The direct effect of cognitive flexibility on interpersonal communication anxiety in adolescents is negatively significant’ was confirmed. As posited by [116], cognitive flexibility has been demonstrated to exert a detrimental influence on the social anxiety-based withdrawal behaviors exhibited by adolescents. Adolescents who demonstrate low cognitive flexibility may experience anxiety about communicating with peers or other individuals because they believe that they will be humiliated and ridiculed [117]. Adolescents limited cognitive flexibility, manifesting as rigid self-criticism, may result in feelings of anxiety when communicating with others and subsequent withdrawal. Adolescents who demonstrate low cognitive flexibility may experience anxiety due to the belief that they may be boring and unable to express themselves [114]. Focusing on the negative and thinking of the worst case scenario, which is the result of lack of cognitive flexibility, can cause interpersonal communication anxiety [17]. Despite the paucity of related studies, this finding is theoretically substantiated. Adolescents who harbor inflexible beliefs regarding the likelihood of social ridicule or self-expression difficulties are considered to manifest communication anxiety.

The main and final hypotheses of the study, the indirect effect of self-oriented and socially prescribed perfectionism on interpersonal communication anxiety through cognitive flexibility in adolescents is significant’, were confirmed. It is seen that there are almost no studies related to these findings. Despite the differences in the sample group and the closely related dependent variable. Abdollahi [118], asserted that rumination related to cognitive flexibility plays a mediating role between socially prescribed perfectionism and social anxiety in university students. Sensitivity to being judged negatively by others has been demonstrated to result in cognitive rigidity, which can consequently precipitate elevated levels of anxiety in social interactions. Especially adolescents may criticize themselves mercilessly due to their development (body image, self-centeredness) [1]. This situation is accepted as a result of self-oriented perfectionism. Adolescents’ persistent self-criticism, deficiency in cognitive flexibility and concomitant anxiety result in their reluctance to engage in communication with others [117]. The observation that adolescents seek social support and consequently manifest socially prescribed perfectionist traits is indicative of a lack of cognitive flexibility [111]. It has been argued that adolescents exhibiting these two characteristics experience anxiety about communication in order to avoid being criticized [117]. As a result, cognitive flexibility is regarded as being a consequence of perfectionist personality structure and as a catalyst for interpersonal communication, with outcomes including rumination. As is clear, the finding regarding the mediating role of cognitive flexibility between self-oriented and socially prescribed perfectionism and interpersonal communication anxiety, a dimension of social anxiety, is consistent with the relevant literature.

Conversely, perfectionistic personalities, particularly in the context of the developmental stage of adolescence, have been shown to perceive failure as a deficiency [13, 119]. Perfectionism has been shown to be a contributing factor to the development of social anxiety, which encompasses concerns regarding one’s perception by others and the apprehension of interpersonal communication [109, 120]. The findings of [61], indicate that, due to the interplay between perfectionism and communication anxiety, adolescents are preoccupied with their self-perception in the eyes of others, aspiring to embody the archetype of perfection. The crux of the matter is to instill in adolescents the understanding that perfection and failure are both natural aspects of the learning process. According to the cognitive model of social anxiety, the possibility exists of this occurring through cognitive flexibility towards negative beliefs [117]. In the context of Turkish culture, research has demonstrated a strong correlation between adolescents’ self-identification as perfectionists and their social anxiety regarding how they will be perceived [121]. In particular, it has been stated that the perfectionist aspects of adolescents are reinforced due to the expectations of the society in which they live, and thus adolescents avoid social relationships as a result of the thought that they will be criticized and the effort to hide their mistakes [121]. In the context of Turkish culture, there is a discernible correlation between the success-oriented approach of parents and society, and the emergence of perfectionist tendencies among adolescents [122, 123]. Consequently, this phenomenon often gives rise to social anxiety, precipitated by rigid beliefs, such as the apprehension of negative evaluation [124]. In this study, it is demonstrated that students who are brought to the perfectionist level are susceptible to negative thinking due to the absence of cognitive flexibility in experiencing social anxiety, and consequently, they experience social anxiety. In this context, the importance and mediating role of gaining cognitive flexibility for perfectionist adolescents in Turkish society to experience reduced social anxiety and to communicate free from anxiety becomes evident. The present study’s findings align with those of previous research in this field, which suggests that the elimination of perfectionist beliefs through the cultivation of cognitive flexibility may result in a reduction in communication anxiety. In this context, it is posited that the effect of structured or semi-structured guidance programs for cognitive flexibility is significant for perfectionist adolescents to experience reduced communication anxiety.

The present study is not without its own limitations. Cognitive flexibility has been demonstrated to play a substantial role in the relationship between perfectionist traits and interpersonal communication anxiety in adolescents. A notable limitation of the present study is its cross-sectional nature, which precludes causal inferences. Another limitation of the present study is that it was conducted solely in specific regions of Türkiye. The restriction of the sample group to adolescents constitutes an additional limitation of the study. Suggestions regarding the study’s limitations and findings are outlined below.

It is posited that, given the variability of cognitive flexibility in accordance with life conditions and developmental processes, a longitudinal study of the same model will yield more explanatory results. Furthermore, experimental studies involving cognitive intervention with groups of perfectionist adolescents can be planned to reduce social anxiety or interpersonal communication anxiety. In the event of the study sample being differentiated, a comparative study can be conducted at pre-adolescence, adolescence and childhood levels using the same variables. The findings indicated the full mediating role of cognitive flexibility between perfectionism and interpersonal communication anxiety. In this context, the integration of cognitive behavioral-based activities aimed at enhancing cognitive flexibility within the school guidance program can be a strategy to mitigate the impact of heightened perfectionism on adolescents, thereby reducing their interpersonal communication anxiety. In addition, it is recommended that interventions be made to address interpersonal communication anxiety during the second childhood period. This is a significant period for the establishment of successful identity and the cultivation of horizontal and vertical relationships prior to adolescence.
