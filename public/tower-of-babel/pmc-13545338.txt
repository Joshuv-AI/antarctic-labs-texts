The impact of family interview on the relationship between medical students and their silent mentor
Authors: Po‐Fang Tsai, Mei‐Fang Tseng, Ruei‐Jen Chiou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe gross anatomy laboratory course often triggers significant negative emotions in medical students during dissection. While various pedagogical interventions aim to alleviate psychological burden, the fundamental question of how students' perceptions of donors evolve throughout the course remains underexplored. In our program, students participate in family interviews with donors' family members, which provide a biographical context for the donor. This study investigated the processual nature of medical students' relationship formation with their “silent mentor” and the pivotal role of family interviews in this transformation. A qualitative study was conducted with 12 third‐year medical students. Participants were interviewed at two critical time points: before family interviews (T1) and after course completion (T2). Semi‐structured interviews explored students' perceptions of the silent mentor's body, the student–donor relationship, and the impact of family interviews. Data were analyzed using thematic analysis to trace the evolution of themes across the semester. Students' experiences unfolded in two distinct phases. Phase 1 was characterized by oscillation between viewing the silent mentor as “person” versus “object,” influenced by temporal boundaries and sensory dissonance. Phase 2 revealed the family interview as a transformative catalyst that initiated relationship formation, prompting students to reframe their connection with the silent mentor as teacher, bridge, or companion, while extending reflections to their relationships with living people. The family interview emerges not merely as a humanistic activity but as a critical pedagogical intervention that transforms students' professional identity and compassion through facilitating a profound process of relationship formation with their silent mentor.Keywords: dissection, family interview, gross anatomy laboratory course, relationship formation, silent mentor

INTRODUCTION

The gross anatomy laboratory is a cornerstone of medical education, yet it is widely recognized that medical students often experience significant negative emotions and psychological stress during dissection. This distress can manifest not only during their initial encounter with the human body but may also persist throughout the semester‐long course. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 Prior studies have described both the acute emotional impact of first exposure to the donor 1 , 3 and the continuing burden associated with repeated laboratory work, including anxiety, discomfort, and emotional fatigue across the course. 2 , 4 , 5 , 6 The sources of this emotional burden are multifaceted. On one hand, they stem from students' internal demands for professional performance, including anxiety about making the first incision, concerns over inadequate dissection skills, and fear of falling behind in the curriculum. 7 , 8 , 9 On the other hand, they relate to more profound existential issues, such as the fear and anxiety evoked by the first confrontation with death and a human corpse, which may trigger past personal experiences with mortality. 10 , 11 , 12 Collectively, these emotional challenges can pose a considerable obstacle to learning. Consequently, a key objective for anatomy and medical educators is to transform this potential hindrance into a constructive pedagogical opportunity.

To address this challenge, a common strategy in the literature has been to “alleviate students' psychological burden” through various pedagogical interventions. These have included facilitating small‐group discussions, 13 organizing experience‐sharing sessions with senior students, 14 using preparatory orientation or audiovisual materials before dissection, 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 and conducting formal commencement or memorial activities for donors. 20 , 21 , 22 These are often implemented prior to the dissection, such as facilitating small‐group discussions, organizing experience‐sharing sessions with senior students, conducting formal commencement ceremonies for the donors, or encouraging students to learn about the donors' life stories beforehand. 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 In recent years, multimedia technologies have offered supplementary methods, such as viewing documentaries related to body donation. For instance, the study by Chiou et al. indicated that a “commencement ceremony” and “family interviews” effectively fostered feelings of gratitude among students, thereby significantly reducing their negative emotions. 4 , 21 , 22 However, such interventions often rely on a relatively passive and indirect approach, primarily aiming to mitigate symptoms rather than directly investigating the root causes of these emotions. This approach may overlook a more fundamental question: How do medical students perceive and interpret the donor's body, and how does this perception influence the nature and intensity of their emotional responses?

Another line of research shifts the focus from emotional reactions to the various perspectives students adopt toward the donor's body. The most prevalent discussion in this area revolves around whether students view the donor as a “person” or an “object/specimen” during dissection. 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 This dichotomy is rarely absolute; rather, students often oscillate or shift between these two modes of perception throughout the course. 29 , 30 More recent studies have nuanced this debate. McBain et al. found that when provided with a donor's medical history, students were more inclined to see the donor as a former “patient” rather than merely a “specimen.” 25 Furthermore, Goss et al. suggested that this oscillation between perspectives corresponds to the development of two critical metacognitive skills: the cultivation of empathy and the practice of emotional regulation through professional detachment. 26 While this line of inquiry directly addresses the perceptual vacillation that underlies students' emotional experiences, it also leaves a critical question unanswered: What are the key factors that drive this transformation over the semester‐long course, and what is the trajectory of this dynamic process?

In our institutional context, one distinctive activity intended to address this gap is the family interview, in which students meet the donor's family members and, in some cases, close friends to learn about the donor's life, values, and motivations for body donation. Even though this type of activity was first initiated at Tzu Chi University, literature specifically examining donor‐family interviews remains limited. 31 , 32 , 33 At Taipei Medical University (TMU), this activity was not adopted from a standardized research protocol; rather, it was developed locally and refined over time by frontline faculty, drawing on earlier body donation advocacy and donor‐family engagement practices in Taiwan. The activity was introduced because teachers considered it educationally important for students to understand who the donor was as a person, not only as a body for dissection.

This paper argues that these two research streams are complementary and that their findings converge on the critical importance of a key pedagogical activity: the “family interview.” On the one hand, when viewed as an intervention to reduce psychological burden, the family interview not only appears effective but, more importantly, may shape how students understand the donor across the semester. Positioned between the “commencement ceremony” at the beginning and the midterm, the family interview offers a relational context for learning in the medical humanities. On the other hand, the students' oscillation between viewing the donor as a “person” versus an “object” is not random but is likely influenced by specific stages and events within the course. The family interview, as a powerful informational and emotional catalyst, profoundly influences students' understanding of the donor, thereby modulating their position within this perceptual spectrum. We contend, therefore, that the role of the family visit interview must be re‐evaluated. It should be seen not merely as one of many tools for mitigating negative emotions or as a simple courtesy to the donor's family, but as a profound pedagogical intervention capable of shaping students' professional identity and humanistic compassion.

METHODS AND MATERIALS

Study design and context

This study employed a qualitative design to explore the evolving perceptions of medical students during their gross anatomy laboratory course. The research was situated within the compulsory gross anatomy laboratory course at TMU, a semester‐long (18‐week) module for third‐year medical students. In the 2022 academic year, 155 students were enrolled in the course (90 male and 65 female). Students were organized into nine groups, with approximately 17–18 students assigned to a donor, referred to respectfully within the institution as a “silent mentor.” Within each group, students were further divided into five teams, each responsible for dissecting a specific anatomical region (i.e., head, neck and thorax, upper limb, abdominopelvis, and lower limb). These five teams worked concurrently during each laboratory session rather than sequentially, with all teams progressing through their assigned regions at the same time.

A distinctive feature of this curriculum is the integration of several humanistic activities designed to foster empathy and respect. These include a formal “Silent Mentor Initiation Ceremony” at the start of the course, a “Family Interview” session where students meet the donor's family and friends, and a concluding cremation ceremony. Throughout the semester, students are also encouraged to engage in reflective practices, such as writing personal notes to their silent mentor and compiling their family interview experiences into a commemorative book for the donor's family.

At TMU, the family interview was developed locally as a curricular practice rather than adopted from a formal published protocol. The activity was gradually refined by frontline faculty in response to the educational need to help students understand who the donor was as a person, not only as a body for dissection. Before the family interview, faculty members contacted the family, coordinated the time and location, and provided students with a donor biography form and other background information supplied by the family. Students then prepared possible interview questions in advance, and these were reviewed and revised by faculty. On the day of the visit, the interview was mainly moderated by a faculty member, while students asked most of the questions. Students usually designated several representatives to lead the questioning, after which other students were invited to ask additional questions. The interview was generally open and conversational, but followed a loose structure consisting of faculty introduction, student questions, family responses, closing remarks, and a concluding ritual such as offering flowers, presenting a small gift, and taking a group photograph. These sessions usually lasted 1–2 h. When family members were more talkative, the conversation followed their own narrative rhythm; when they were less forthcoming, faculty took a more active role in guiding the discussion. Family interviews were completed within approximately the first two weeks of the semester, while dissection activities were already underway.

Because the family interviews were centrally arranged by the course faculty, not all students met the family of their own assigned silent mentor. In the 2022 cohort, students in groups 1–3 attended the family interview of another group's donor; students in groups 5–9 attended the family interview of their own donor, and group 4 participated in a visit with a registered donor who was still living. This variation formed an important contextual feature of the present study.

Ethical considerations

This study received full ethical approval from the Taipei Medical University‐Joint Institutional Review Board (TMU‐JIRB No. N202203215). All methods were conducted in strict accordance with the approved protocol and relevant ethical guidelines. Participation was voluntary, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection. Anonymity and confidentiality were maintained throughout the research process.

Participants

Participants were recruited via purposive voluntary sampling from the cohort of third‐year medical students enrolled in the gross anatomy course. The study was first introduced to students during the previous semester, and an announcement was subsequently posted on the university's online learning platform inviting students to participate. Students decided voluntarily whether to join the study.

The purposive sampling strategy was intended to recruit students who were enrolled in the course, willing to participate in both rounds of interviews, and able to provide informed consent, while also capturing variation in sex, dissection region, and family interview experience. The final sample consisted of 12 students (6 male, 6 female) with a mean age of 21.0 ± 0.7 years. The distribution of participants across dissection regions was as follows: head (n = 2), neck and thoracic (n = 2), upper limb (n = 1), abdominopelvic (n = 5), and lower limb (n = 2).

A key aspect of the course structure, and a relevant variable for this study, is that not all students meet the family of their assigned silent mentor. Due to logistical arrangements by the course convenor, some students are scheduled to join the family interviews of other dissection groups. In our cohort, six participants interviewed their own silent mentor, while the other six participated in an interview for a different group's mentor (Table 1).

Data collection

Data were collected through semi‐structured, online focus group interviews. The 12 participants self‐organized into four groups according to their availability, and these groups formed the basis of the first round of focus groups. To capture the dynamic nature of their experiences, two rounds of interviews were conducted with the same participants at critical junctures of the course.

The first round of interviews (T1) was held in July and August 2022, during the summer vacation prior to the family interviews. The second round (T2) took place in January and February 2023, after all course‐related activities, including the family interviews and cremation ceremony, had been completed. Each focus group session lasted approximately two hours, was audio‐recorded with participants' permission, and subsequently transcribed verbatim for analysis. Interview tool

A semi‐structured interview guide was developed to ensure consistency across focus groups while allowing flexibility to explore emergent themes. The protocol was designed to investigate students' perceptions of the silent mentor's body, the student–donor relationship, and the perceived impact of the family interview.

Data analysis

The transcribed data were analyzed using thematic analysis informed by a processual lens. While this approach is grounded in the established methodology of Braun and Clarke, 34 , 35 , 36 , 37 it was used here to examine not only recurrent patterns of meaning but also how those meanings shifted across the two interview time points over the semester. The analysis was primarily inductive, while remaining sensitized by the study questions and relevant literature on anatomy education, donor‐centered learning, and relationship formation.

Analysis proceeded iteratively through familiarization with the transcripts, initial coding, development of candidate themes, review and refinement of themes, and final definition and naming of themes. T1 and T2 transcripts were first read in relation to their respective time points and were then compared across time to explore continuity, change, and turning points in students' narratives. Importantly, the two interview time points were part of the original study design, whereas the broader “phases” reported in the Results were interpretive categories developed during analysis. During the later stages of analysis, 38 the research team engaged in an iterative process of refining the temporal framework. An initial analysis distinguished three discrete time points (initial encounter, dissection, family interview). However, through collaborative discussion, these were consolidated and reconceptualized into two overarching phases, which were then renamed to better reflect the students' journey. We contend that conceptualizing the student experience in terms of these continuous “phases” rather than as isolated “time points” more accurately represents the sustained and dynamic nature of their transformation.

Team reflexivity

This study was informed by an interpretivist epistemology and a constructivist orientation. We approached students' perceptions of the silent mentor not as fixed internal states, but as meanings shaped through lived experience, social interaction, and reflective narration. This stance informed our use of semi‐structured focus group interviews and thematic analysis.

The research team comprised three members from diverse academic backgrounds: a historian (TMF) from the Center for General Education, a sociologist (TPF) from the Institute of Medical Humanities, and an anatomist (CRJ) from the Department of Medicine. This interdisciplinary composition brought diverse perspectives to the research. All members have extensive experience in teaching medical students in their respective capacities and share a long‐standing collaboration at the intersection of anatomy education and the medical humanities. This collaborative ethos was maintained throughout all phases of the study, from design to analysis, through regular discussions and peer debriefing.

The team was acutely aware of the potential for power dynamics to influence participant responses, particularly as some participants were students in CRJ's anatomy course. To mitigate this potential bias, the focus group interviews were mainly led by TMF and TPF, who had no direct instructional or evaluative role over the participants. Subsequently, the data analysis was co‐conducted by TPF with expertise in qualitative analysis and CRJ with essential content‐specific insights, ensuring a balanced and rigorous interpretation of the data. During data analysis, TPF contributed qualitative methodological expertise, and CRJ contributed content‐specific and curricular insight, helping to ensure a balanced and rigorous interpretation of the data.

RESULTS

Our analysis revealed a distinct two‐phase trajectory in students' perceptions and emotional responses. The first phase, spanning from the initial encounter with the “silent mentor” to increasing familiarity with dissection, was defined by cognitive and emotional recalibration. The second phase, extending from the family interview to the completion of the course, was marked by a more explicitly relational understanding of the silent mentor. Many students linked this later shift to the family interview, although their changing perceptions also unfolded through continued dissection, reflective activities, and other course experiences.

Phase 1: From initial encounter to familiar with dissection

The initial phase of the gross anatomy laboratory course was characterized by a profound tension between students' visceral, sensory responses to the donor and the escalating demands of the curriculum. This period was marked by two primary dynamics: first, a cognitive recalibration as students navigated the boundary between the silent mentor's personhood and objecthood, and second, the concurrent development of procedural familiarity and academic pressure.

Navigating perceptual boundaries: Critical junctures and the human form

While students universally described an initial state of ambivalence—oscillating between viewing the silent mentor as a “person” and a “pedagogical tool”, two moments were repeatedly identified as especially difficult: the initial viewing of the silent mentor and the moment of the first incision. Participants identified two critical junctures that precipitated the most intense emotional flux: the initial viewing of the silent mentor and the moment of the first incision. These events were associated with heightened feelings of anxiety, uncertainty, and fear of error. It was predominantly during or immediately after these junctures that the most significant cognitive shift from “person” to “object” occurred, serving as a necessary coping mechanism to proceed with the task.

Students also described comparing the silent mentor's body to their own body or to that of a living person. This comparison brought sensory differences into sharper focus, including the unnatural color, firmness, and odor of the embalmed body. Although these differences were initially unsettling, they also helped create the cognitive distance needed for dissection.

The significance of the silent mentor's holistic “human form” in mediating this perception was a recurring theme. The ability to view the silent mentor as a whole entity often sustained the perception of personhood, whereas focusing on specific anatomical parts facilitated objectification. Participant G articulated this internal conflict:

I guess it was his overall appearance, his entire human form… it was like I couldn't just flip a switch and see him as only a teaching tool. (Participant G)

Participant G's reflection is emblematic of a broader pattern identified in our data. The perception of “personhood” was often linked to the anthropomorphic gestalt, which evoked thoughts of the silent mentor's past life. In contrast, focusing on specific, non‐living sensory cues (e.g., skin turgor, rigidity) facilitated a necessary cognitive shift toward viewing the body as a pedagogical object.

The duality of increasing familiarity and academic pressure

As the semester progressed, students developed a sense of familiarity with the silent mentor. Repetitive, preparatory tasks, such as cleaning adipose and connective tissues, along with prolonged exposure in the lab, helped to desensitize them to the initial emotional shock.

As the course went on, you just got a better feel for… like, the size and thickness of the nerves and vessels. You kind of know where they should be, so you're not as nervous. (Participant C)

This growing familiarity was paralleled by a sharp increase in academic pressure. As the curriculum advanced to complex organ systems, the focus shifted toward the efficient identification of discrete structures like nerves and blood vessels. This instrumental approach, driven by curricular deadlines and performance expectations, compelled students to deconstruct the silent mentor into a series of anatomical components. This pragmatic need for efficiency was often in direct conflict with the humanistic goal of respecting the silent mentor as a former person. This tension was clearly articulated by Participant C:

when you're rushing to keep up, you start to see him [the silent mentor] more like a specimen. (Participant C)

The account highlights how pedagogical pressures can inadvertently undermine humanistic goals. The imperative to “keep pace” forces students to adopt an objectifying lens as a functional necessity. Several participants echoed this sentiment, noting that the silent mentor's face was a powerful, often unavoidable, reminder of their personhood. In contrast, when dissecting other regions under time constraints, the primary cognitive frame shifted from holistic respect to task‐oriented problem‐solving, reinforcing the perception of the silent mentor as a specimen. This created a persistent tension between seeing the silent mentor's form and focusing on finding a specific anatomical structure.

Phase 2: From the family interview to retrospective integration

The second phase of the students' journey was catalyzed by the family interview, a pivotal medical humanities intervention scheduled after students had achieved procedural familiarity but before their final examinations. This event marked a significant departure from earlier, more passive forms of engagement, such as the commencement ceremony or writing gratitude cards. It constituted the students' first direct, dialogic encounter with the silent mentor's personal history, mediated through the narratives of their loved ones. While the subsequent impact on students' dissection practice was varied, the family interview unequivocally functioned as a powerful psychological catalyst, fundamentally reconfiguring the student–silent mentor relationship and enabling a profound retrospective sense of closure at the course's conclusion.

Catalyzing a relationship: A spectrum of emotional responses

The transition from indirectly learning about the silent mentor to hearing directly from their family created a powerful sense of unmediated connection. This newfound intimacy, however, generated a heterogeneous range of emotional and professional responses among the participants. Four distinct reactions illustrate this spectrum:

First, some students felt that learning more about the donor's life increased the emotional weight of dissection:

But when I *do* know about the actual traces of their life, their stories, it really is a heavier psychological burden… But I think it's a double‐edged sword. (Participant A)

Second, some students felt calmer and more grateful after learning the donor's life story:

If I can understand their life story more, I feel like when I'm actually dissecting, I can be calmer and feel even more grateful to this teacher. (Participant G)

Third, some students interpreted the family interview as an affirmation of the educational value of dissection and therefore felt less anxious about making technical mistakes:

I remember during the family interview, the family kept telling us that we should learn as much as we can from the teacher…So after the interview, I was less worried during the dissection… I just felt like as long as I could expose the structures and learn something, it was okay. (Participant L)

Fourth, a minority of students reported little emotional change and maintained a relatively steady professional distance:

During the dissection process, I was just very, very rational. My emotions didn't really change much throughout the whole thing. (Participant K)

In summary, four broad responses were identified. Some students, like Participant A, found that this deeper knowledge introduced a significant emotional weight to their work. In contrast, others, such as Participant G, felt that the connection fostered a sense of peace and gratitude that calmed their anxieties. A third perspective, articulated by Participant L, framed the interview as an empowering endorsement from the family, which alleviated the fear of making technical mistakes. A minority, represented by Participant K, maintained a position of professional detachment, reporting minimal emotional change.

Despite these varied outcomes, a common underlying mechanism was evident: the family interview universally initiated a process of “relationship formation.” The silent mentor was no longer an abstract entity but a person with a social identity and a life story. As Participant A reflected, this knowledge created “connections” that came with a “heavier psychological burden,” yet also made the experience more precious. Participant L felt a shift in professional responsibility, stating, “It's better to make a hundred mistakes on our teacher than to make one wrong cut on a future patient.” Even though this connection was still mediated by family, students began to operate under the profound conviction that a tangible, personal relationship now existed between them and their silent mentor.

The ripple effect: Embodying and defining the new relationship

The establishment of this relationship set in motion two subsequent waves of impact that extended through the remainder of the semester. First, it prompted students to reflect on their relationships with the living. Second, as the course concluded, it drove them to retrospectively define and give a name to the unique bond they had formed with their silent mentor. Importantly, in students' accounts, this ripple effect was the family interview that placed the silent mentor back into a social and biographical context, thereby mediating a shift from encountering a body for dissection to encountering a particular person with family ties, life stories, and relational meaning. In this sense, the family interview occupied a central position in the course by linking earlier bodily and technical experiences with later emotional, ethical, and relational reflection.

This “embodied reflection” manifested in students' professional conduct and personal lives. In the lab, routine tasks like end‐of‐day cleanup and the final suturing of the silent mentor's body were imbued with new meaning, performed with what participants described as heightened reverence and care. As Participant E explained, this new perspective followed from knowing, through the family interview, that the silent mentor had been someone's family member and was therefore no longer experienced merely as an anonymous body:

It's like, it makes the whole procedure more meaningful. (Participant D)

And when we were cleaning up, I'd be thinking, 'The silent mentor was someone's father, after all.' We had to try our best to clean up… at the end of the semester, when we were suturing the skin, I still really wanted to do right by him. (Participant E)

This sense of personal responsibility also extended beyond the laboratory, with some students reporting that they initiated conversations with their own families about mortality and body donation. These responses suggest that the family interview redirected students' attention outward—from the dissected body itself to the donor's relationships with others—and inward, toward their own future roles and personal relationships. In this way, the family interview generated a ripple effect that connected laboratory practice, family reflection, and professional meaning.

This process culminated in a final phase of “retrospective relational framing,” where students actively sought to articulate the nature of their relationship with the silent mentor. For many, this bond defied conventional categories. The most common metaphor was that of a “bridge,” which, as Participant G described, connected lifeless “knowledge on one side” with “life itself” on the other. For others, like Participant B, it was a feeling of inexplicable “closeness” with someone they had never met. These metaphors were significant because they indicate that, after the family interview, students were no longer describing the silent mentor only in anatomical or technical terms; instead, they were trying to name a relationship made possible by learning who the donor had been in life.

I feel like… it's like a bridge. On one side, it's just knowledge without life. But the other side is life itself… I could feel that the knowledge I was learning could be carried over that bridge and applied to a human body. (Participant G)

You start to feel like you know the silent mentor really well. We'd never met, we weren't family or friends, but you know so many of their stories. So during the gross anatomy laboratory course, there was this feeling of closeness with him. (Participant B)

Most profoundly, this retrospective process allowed students to resolve the person‐object ambivalence that characterized the initial phase of the course. The act of suturing and restoring the silent mentor's “human form” became a powerful symbolic moment, cementing a final, integrated identity for the silent mentor. This transformation was vividly captured by Participant D, who had initially viewed the silent mentor as a “teaching tool”: through the family interview, the silent mentor had become identifiable as a particular person, and this made the final restoration of bodily wholeness meaningful in relational rather than merely procedural terms.

Especially when we were working on a detached part… it was really hard to associate it with a person. … when you see the complete form, you really feel he is a person, and our teacher. You feel that student‐teacher relationship because he truly taught us something. (Participant E)

Honestly, from the very first impression, he just seemed like a teaching tool. … My gratitude was for the person who donated his body, not for the body itself. Of course, you have a respectful *attitude* toward the body… but the funny thing is, after it's all over, you get this feeling that you've actually gotten to know Teacher Hsiao‐Wen [the silent mentor's name]… When we sutured him up at the end, he looked like a teacher again. The student‐teacher relationship was definitely there. By the very end, it felt like… he was an old friend you'd walked with for a while. It's that feeling of, 'It's good to see you whole again.' It's a very special kind of relationship, one you probably wouldn't experience anywhere else in life. (Participant D)

In summary, the family interview occupied a central mediating position in the course. It did not simply coexist with the formation of a deeply personal relationship but helped make that relationship possible by transforming the silent mentor from a dissected body into a socially and biographically situated person. From this central shift flowed a ripple effect: students reinterpreted their laboratory practices, reflected on their relationships with living people, and retrospectively re‐described their bond with the silent mentor, ultimately integrating the roles of teacher, mentor, and even friend.

DISCUSSION

This study reveals that medical students engage in a dynamic process of “relationship formation” with their silent mentor over the course of the gross anatomy laboratory course. Recent studies have begun to conceptualize anatomy as a relational process in which donated bodies are understood simultaneously as persons, educational material, and gifts, and in which meaning is constituted through social, material, and memorial practices. 39 Contemporary anatomy education has also been described as moving beyond the mere learning of body parts toward a relational and humanistic ethos that supports contextual learning and early professional identity formation. 40 In this sense, our findings suggest that students' experiences are better understood not only in terms of negative emotions or the binary oscillation between viewing the silent mentor as a “person” versus an “object,” but also in terms of an evolving relational orientation toward the donor. Here, “relationship formation” refers not to a conventional reciprocal social relationship, but to the gradual development of a meaningful connection through which students came to situate the donor within a personal, social, and ethical frame. This process, facilitated by both technical dissection and integrated humanistic activities, offers a more nuanced framework for understanding the student experience. Our findings underscore the profound processual nature of this relationship and highlight the critical interventional role of specific pedagogical activities in shaping this journey.

The processual nature of relationship formation

Prior literature has extensively documented the diverse and intense emotional responses students have upon their initial encounter with a human body. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 However, descriptions of how these emotions evolve over a full semester have often been generalized as a linear progression of “adaptation” or punctuated by moments of “gratitude” triggered by specific events. Our findings suggest a more intricate and relational trajectory. We contend that the key to understanding this emotional evolution lies in the process of relationship formation itself—a journey characterized by distinct, though overlapping, phases of unfamiliarity and comparison, anxiety and habituation, and indirect versus direct knowing. A crucial finding is that regardless of where students situated the silent mentor on the person‐object spectrum during the initial phases, the family interview and final course reflections served as catalysts, providing them with a rich vocabulary to articulate and define the nature of their relationship with their silent mentor.

This process‐oriented interpretation offers two significant contributions to the existing discourse. First, it reframes the student experience beyond the simple metrics of emotional valence, by which we mean the broadly positive or negative tone of students' emotional responses, such as fear, anxiety, gratitude, burden, or calm. Prior studies of anatomy‐related interventions have often evaluated students' experiences in terms of whether such strategies reduce distress or psychological burden. 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 While initial negative emotions are undeniable, a mere “adaptation” model inadequately captures the complex psychological landscape of the latter half of the course. By then, a relationship—forged through frequent, intimate interaction—has been established. Consequently, any lingering negative emotions are qualitatively different; the initial fear of the unknown is often supplanted by performance anxiety or a sense of guilt over perceived technical inadequacies, which are now framed in the context of failing to honor the silent mentor's gift. Our findings, therefore, extend prior work suggesting that ceremonies and family interviews may induce gratitude or reshape emotional responses 4 , 21 , 22 by showing that these changes may also be structured by the emerging relationship between students and their silent mentor. This moral and emotional texture, most pronounced around the family interview, demonstrates that students' emotional states become contingent upon the perceived status of this emergent relationship. Therefore, we argue that an integrated anatomy curriculum should be intentionally designed to support students in navigating this relational journey, from daily routines of care to the symbolic act of restoring the human form during final suturing. 41 , 42 , 43 , 44 , 45 , 46

Second, the process of relationship formation is not confined to the student‐silent mentor dyad, so that the crucial point would be more than merely students' perception of the silent mentor as either a “person” or an “object”. 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 The establishment of this central relationship precipitates a series of re‐evaluations of other social connections. The silent mentor becomes a shared focal point for the dissection group, potentially fostering a more conscientious and collaborative team dynamic. Following the family interview, the silent mentor's family becomes a salient presence in the students' minds, adding another layer of accountability to their work. Furthermore, this intense confrontation with mortality often prompts students to reflect on and engage more deeply with their own intimate relationships with family and friends. This outward ripple effect is initiated by a relationship formation between medical students and their silent mentors, including the other people, from the family of the silent mentor to the student's own family and friends, in the loop. In other words, medical students were inclined to review or revalue their relations with those living people under their relationship formation with the silent mentor.

Furthermore, this phenomenon also catalyzes a profound process of introspection and hypothetical self‐interrogation. In the latter stages of the course, participants described engaging in an inner dialogue with the silent mentor, imagining how their performance might be perceived, projecting the encouragement the silent mentor might offer their future professional selves, and sublimating this imagined relationship into foundational principles of medical ethics and professionalism. 47 , 48 , 49 , 50 , 51 , 52 , 53 Our findings provide empirical granularity to how this sublimation occurs. For instance, the cognitive recalibration students experienced when navigating the boundary between the mentor's personhood and objecthood directly aligns with the ethical “dichotomy of objectification and personification”. 48 Furthermore, while previous studies highlighted the necessity of managing academic pressure and emotional anxiety during early professional identity formation, 49 , 50 our results reveal a specific coping mechanism: the perceived endorsement from the mentor's family and the inner dialogue with the mentor fostered a sense of peace that actively alleviated the fear of technical mistakes. This process humanizes the anatomical experience, a core component of the hidden curriculum,51 prompting students to retrospectively define this unique bond. Ultimately, rather than merely learning the objective rules of ethical dissection, 47 , 52 our students utilized this reflective practice to contemplate “who they shall become,” 53 directly translating their reverence for the dead into an ethical benchmark for their future relationships with the living.

Conversely, students reported using this formative relationship as a benchmark or a schema for calibrating their future clinical encounters, whether with living patients or the corporeal reality of the surgical field. They began to prospectively analyze the similarities and differences between this foundational relationship and those hypothetical future scenarios, sometimes invoking the “first patient” metaphor. 28 In essence, this internal, reflective work prompted by the relationship with the silent mentor is arguably as significant for their professional identity formation as the external relational considerations.

The interventional role of the family interview

Much of the literature on pedagogical interventions in anatomy focuses on mitigating student distress through either direct or mediated means. 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 Some strategies involve preparing students for the practical realities of dissection via preparatory videos or peer‐sharing, 8 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 while others, such as memorial ceremonies and family interviews, aim to provide a positive emotional scaffold during the course. 7 , 21 , 22 , 54 , 55 However, this approach often implicitly positions formal, collective strategies as superior to students' individual, emergent coping mechanisms. Our research offers a different perspective by identifying a near‐universal, self‐directed coping strategy among students: the gradual formation of a relationship with their silent mentor. From this vantage point, the role of pedagogical interventions should be re‐examined not as top‐down solutions, but as catalysts that complement and scaffold this organic process.

Viewed through this lens, the family interview emerges as a uniquely effective intervention. It is strategically timed to occur when students are caught in the tension between procedural habituation and mounting academic pressure. The preparatory activities for the interview—contacting the family, writing notes, formulating questions—directly align with the students' burgeoning desire to know the silent mentor more deeply. The interview itself then triggers a cascade of effects—a more direct connection to the silent mentor, a re‐evaluation of other relationships, and profound internal reflection—that empower students to navigate the pressures and ambiguities of the course. We therefore posit that the family interview is not merely a humanistic add‐on but a critical nexus where individual coping strategies and collective pedagogical design synergize.

In contrast, other formal ceremonies, such as the initiation at the beginning of the semester and the final memorial service, appeared less complementary to this relational process in our study. The initiation ceremony occurs before students have sufficient information or experience to form a meaningful connection. Similarly, while students found daily routines—such as reassembling the silent mentor's body parts to restore the “human form” under the shroud—to be profoundly important for maintaining a sense of personhood, the final ceremonies are summative rather than formative. This suggests that the efficacy of an intervention depends on its alignment with the students' developmental stage in their relationship with the silent mentor.

Furthermore, this analysis extends beyond the role of the family interview to a broader critique of how interventions are evaluated and implemented. The traditional pre‐test/post‐test quantitative model for assessing a single intervention is inadequate for capturing the longitudinal, relational development we observed. 4 , 21 A qualitative, multi‐point assessment, such as the focus group interviews used in this study, is better suited to revealing how interventions like the family interview serve as a linchpin, connecting students' initial states of ambiguity with their final states of relational closure. The relative ineffectiveness of other activities in fostering this relationship may stem from two factors.

First, the design of these activities (e.g., the initiation ceremony), while serving as a collective strategy, may fail to achieve a synergistic balance with students' organic, individual coping strategies. This presents a critical challenge for future curricular designers to develop more holistically integrated approaches. Second, the connection between certain activities (e.g., final memorial events) and the overarching educational journey may be weak if they are not tightly integrated into the curriculum from the outset. As some research notes, ceremonies are often included for their intrinsic symbolic or ritualistic value rather than for a planned pedagogical purpose. 7 , 21 , 22 , 54 , 55 The pedagogical implications and synergistic potential of all such interventions, therefore, remain a critical focus for future research.

LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS

A limitation of this study, which warrants further investigation, concerns the cohort of students who were unable to meet the family of their assigned silent mentor due to logistical or personal circumstances. These students participated in the family interview of other dissection groups. Anecdotally, we observed that this experience appeared to create a “split” relational focus; while they continued to develop a primary relationship with their own silent mentor, they also demonstrated a sustained interest in and sense of connection to the silent mentor whose family they had met. This phenomenon, whether it manifests as checking in on the other group's progress or assisting with their cleanup, suggests a more complex and potentially fragmented process of relationship formation.

However, as the assignment to this condition is not a controllable variable and occurs infrequently, our current sample size was insufficient to conduct a formal comparative analysis. A future longitudinal study, accumulating data over several years, would be necessary to systematically explore how this “vicarious” family interview experience shapes the student–silent mentor relationship. Such research could offer valuable insights into the core mechanisms of relational bonding and the transferability of empathy in medical education.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, this study suggests that medical students' experiences in gross anatomy are shaped by an evolving relationship with the silent mentor rather than by emotional adaptation alone. The family interview appeared to function as an important relational and reflective moment for students by helping them understand the donor within a personal and social context. However, this influence was not uniform and did not occur in isolation. Understanding anatomy learning in this way may help educators design curricula that better support students' emotional, relational, and professional development.
