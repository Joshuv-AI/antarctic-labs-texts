Anisotropic min-max via phase transitions
Antonio De Rosa, Alessandro Pigati

Abstract
We develop a PDE-based approach to the min-max construction of nontrivial integer rectifiable varifolds that are stationary with respect to anisotropic surface energies on closed Riemannian manifolds, in codimension one. Specifically, we study the anisotropic analogue of the Allen–Cahn energy and establish a Modica-type gradient bound for its critical points. Using this in conjunction with certain estimates for stable solutions, we then prove that the energy densities of stable or bounded-Morse-index critical points of its rescalings concentrate along an integer rectifiable varifold that is stationary for the underlying anisotropic integrand. As a consequence, we construct a (possibly singular) anisotropic min-max hypersurface via Allen–Cahn, obtaining an analogue of the result of Hutchinson–Tonegawa in the anisotropic setting.

Introduction

The Allen–Cahn equation has become a central tool in the study of minimal hypersurfaces and related variational problems in geometric analysis. Originally introduced by Allen and Cahn
[
6
]
as a diffuse-interface model for phase transitions, this reaction-diffusion equation reads

\partial_{t}u-\Delta u+\frac{W^{\prime}(u)}{\varepsilon^{2}}=0.

Here
W
denotes a double-well potential with global minima at
\pm 1
, the prototypical example being
W(u)=\frac{1}{4}(1-u^{2})^{2}
. Modica and Mortola
[
44
]
observed that the associated energy functional

\int\left[\varepsilon\frac{|\nabla u|^{2}}{2}+\frac{W(u)}{\varepsilon}\right],

(1.1)

\Gamma
-converges, as the interfacial width parameter
\varepsilon\to 0
, to the area functional of sets of finite perimeter. This observation revealed a deep connection between the Allen–Cahn variational framework and the theory of minimal hypersurfaces in geometric measure theory, as originally suggested by De Giorgi.

In particular, critical points of (
1.1
), that is, functions
u
satisfying

-\varepsilon\Delta u+\frac{W^{\prime}(u)}{\varepsilon}=0,

have been shown to concentrate on minimal hypersurfaces as
\varepsilon\to 0
. This phenomenon dates back to the foundational work of Modica
[
43
]
and Sternberg
[
57
]
, and has since developed into a well-established field. We refer the reader to the surveys
[
46
,
52
,
60
]
for an overview.

Building on this idea and on the works of Ilmanen
[
34
]
, Hutchinson and Tonegawa
[
33
]
, Tonegawa
[
59
]
, Tonegawa and Wickramasekera
[
58
]
, and Wickramasekera
[
61
]
, Guaraco
[
32
]
established an existence theorem for closed minimal hypersurfaces in arbitrary closed Riemannian manifolds by adapting the mountain-pass construction to the Allen–Cahn functional (
1.1
). His work provided a PDE-based alternative to the Almgren–Pitts min-max theory from
[
7
,
51
]
. The Almgren–Pitts theory has had a profound impact on geometric analysis and was later refined by Marques and Neves
[
40
,
41
]
in their proof of the Willmore conjecture, leading also to the recent resolution of Yau’s conjecture by Song
[
54
]
.
On the other hand, the PDE-based min-max theory has since been extended to a variety of settings
and proved capable of achieving more refined results which predated analogues in the Almgren–Pitts framework, such as the multiplicity-one result of
[
14
]
. Collectively, these developments have established the Allen–Cahn framework as a flexible and powerful analytic alternative to the Almgren–Pitts one, allowing geometric-measure-theoretic ideas to be realized within a purely elliptic PDE setting,
with promising analogues in higher codimension:
see
[
38
,
35
,
1
,
39
,
11
,
56
,
13
]
for the Ginzburg–Landau model with no magnetic field and
[
50
,
48
]
for the abelian Higgs model
(as well as
[
55
,
49
]
for slightly different settings).

Parallel to these isotropic developments, a significant body of work has emerged on minimal surfaces with respect to anisotropic surface energies, which naturally arise in crystalline surface tension models, capillarity problems, and in the modeling of interfaces between distinct materials. We refer to the survey
[
26
]
for an overview of the theory of anisotropic minimal surfaces. The anisotropic area functional generalizes the classical area by introducing a convex, one-homogeneous integrand depending on both position and normal direction. While existence and regularity of minimizers for anisotropic energies are by now well understood
[
8
,
9
,
18
,
20
,
25
]
, a satisfactory existence and regularity theory for stationary solutions remains incomplete
[
5
,
23
,
24
]
, due largely to the absence of a monotonicity formula for density ratios
[
4
]
. Allard
[
2
]
conjectured the existence of closed anisotropic minimal hypersurfaces in closed Riemannian manifolds, but an anisotropic counterpart of the Almgren–Pitts min-max theory had remained elusive until recently. In
[
22
,
21
]
De Philippis, the first-named author, and Li have now resolved this problem by developing an anisotropic version of the Almgren–Pitts theory, proving the existence of closed anisotropic minimal hypersurfaces with essentially optimal regularity in any closed Riemannian manifold.

The goal of the present paper is to provide a PDE-based alternative to the latter approach to the anisotropic min-max construction. More precisely, we develop an anisotropic version of the isotropic Allen–Cahn min-max construction by Guaraco
[
32
]
.
Let
(M^{n},g)
be a closed Riemannian manifold with
n\geq 2
. We consider a smooth even function

F:UT(M)\to(0,\infty),\hskip 10.00002ptF(x,v)=F(x,-v),

defined on the unit tangent bundle, and extend
F

1
-homogeneously to the tangent bundle:

F:TM\to[0,\infty),\hskip 10.00002ptF(x,\lambda v)=\lambda F(x,v)\text{ for all }\lambda\geq 0,\ (x,v)\in TM.

We assume that
F
is uniformly convex in directions orthogonal to the radial one, namely

D^{2}F_{x}(v)[w,w]\geq\lambda|w|^{2}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for }v\in UT_{x}(M)\text{ and }w\in T_{x}M,\ w\perp v,

for all
x\in M
, where
F_{x}
denotes the restriction of
F
to
T_{x}M
.
The anisotropic surface area of an embedded hypersurface
\Sigma^{n-1}\subset M^{n}
is defined by

\mathcal{F}(\Sigma):=\int_{\Sigma}F(x,\nu_{x})\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}_{g}(x),

and the anisotropic Allen–Cahn energy of a function
u:M\to\mathbb{R}
by

E_{\varepsilon}(u):=\int_{M}\left[\varepsilon\frac{F(x,\nabla u(x))^{2}}{2}+\frac{W(u(x))}{\varepsilon}\right]\,d\operatorname{vol}_{g}(x).

All the relevant assumptions on the double-well potential
W
will be recalled in (
2.5
)–(
2.6
) in Section
3
. The energy functional
E_{\varepsilon}
has been recently investigated by Cicalese, Nagase, and Pisante
[
16
]
, and the associated parabolic equation has been the subject of an extensive literature
[
27
,
28
,
31
,
37
,
42
]
. Bouchitté
[
12
]
proved in a more general framework that
E_{\varepsilon}

\Gamma
-converges to
c_{W}\cdot\mathcal{F}
, where
c_{W}:=\int_{-1}^{1}\sqrt{2W}
. We provide a shorter proof of this
\Gamma
-convergence in Theorem
4.1
, tailored for
E_{\varepsilon}
. We also remark that in recent years there has been a growing literature on the
\Gamma
-convergence to anisotropic perimeters of heterogeneous variants of the Allen–Cahn energy functional in a periodic medium
[
15
,
17
,
29
]
.

On the contrary, nothing was known in the literature for the convergence of stable (or bounded-Morse-index) critical points for
E_{\varepsilon}
to anisotropic minimal hypersurfaces, as
\varepsilon\to 0
;
actually, as explained in the body of the paper, in order to have a
C^{2}
functional
we need to perturb
F
slightly in the variational construction, although ultimately we still obtain critical points for the original
E_{\varepsilon}
. Establishing this convergence is a crucial step to extend the isotropic min-max construction of Guaraco
[
32
]
to the anisotropic Allen–Cahn framework. The main obstruction is the lack of a suitable monotonicity formula in the anisotropic setting
[
4
]
, which makes it a challenging task to prove that the limiting varifold has no diffuse part, and in particular that it is rectifiable.
For the isotropic Allen–Cahn functional, the monotonicity formula is deduced by a celebrated gradient bound proved by Modica
[
45
]
. Our first main contribution is the following anisotropic version of Modica’s bound (see Theorem
5.1
for the details).

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
u:M\to[-1,1]
be a critical point of
E_{\varepsilon}
. Then

\frac{\varepsilon F^{2}(\nabla u)}{2}\leq\frac{W(u)}{\varepsilon}+C(M^{n},g,F).

(1.2)

Compared to the isotropic counterpart, its proof is much more delicate
due to the appearance of additional terms when the Euler–Lagrange equation is differentiated, which need to be controlled by exploiting convexity and homogeneity of
F
at various places. An additional challenge is
the lack of effective bounds on the Hessian
D^{2}u
away from
\{\nabla u=0\}
, as globally
u
is guaranteed to belong only to
C^{1,\alpha}
for some
\alpha\in(0,1)
; terms involving the Hessian naturally appear in the setting of inherently non-autonomous integrands
F
on closed manifolds.

Theorem
1.1
is somewhat surprising: in the isotropic Allen–Cahn case, the validity of (
1.2
) implies the monotonicity formula
[
34
,
33
]
, which is known to fail in the anisotropic setting
[
4
]
. Hence, while (
1.2
) remains valid, it no longer entails monotonicity.
Nonetheless, Theorem
1.1
serves as a key ingredient in proving our main result.

Theorem 1.2
.

There exists a nontrivial
F
-stationary integral
(n-1)
-varifold
V
in
M
, whose weight
c_{W}\|V\|
arises as the limit, as
\varepsilon\to 0
, of the energy densities

e_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon}):=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}F(\nabla u_{\varepsilon})^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}W(u_{\varepsilon})

of suitable critical points
u_{\varepsilon}
of
E_{\varepsilon}
, constructed via a min-max procedure.

Remark 1.3
.

While we exhibit such
V
from the simplest mountain-pass construction, the analysis contained
in the present paper applies to any min-max scheme. We conjecture that, in fact, the energy density of
any
sequence
(u_{\varepsilon})
of critical points with bounded energy concentrates along an integral
F
-stationary varifold as
\varepsilon\to 0
, up to a subsequence, regardless of stability conditions.

While we do not attempt to develop any regularity theory beyond integrality of
V
,
we hope to do so in a future work, exploiting once again the stability of
u_{\varepsilon}
.
It would also be interesting to investigate analogues in codimension two or higher,
e.g. by devising appropriate anisotropic versions of
[
50
]
.

Below, we briefly discuss the proof strategy of Theorem
1.2
, which is obtained combining Proposition
3.5
with Theorem
7.2
and Theorem
7.10
.

First of all, we construct a family of min-max solutions
(u_{\varepsilon})
satisfying uniform (in
\varepsilon
) lower and upper bounds of
E_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})
and with Morse index
\leq 1
(up to a regularizing approximation of
F
); see Proposition
3.5
. Hence, the energy densities
of
u_{\varepsilon}
will subconverge in the sense of measures to a finite Radon
measure
\mu
on
M
.

To encode the geometry of the level sets of
u_{\varepsilon}
, we introduce the

(n-1)
-varifolds
\tilde{V}_{\varepsilon}
in (
6.6
), which heuristically are weighted averages of the level sets of
u_{\varepsilon}
.
The weight measure
\|\tilde{V}_{\varepsilon}\|(M)
can be bounded above (up to a constant) with
E(u_{\varepsilon})
, and hence we can extract a subsequential limit
\tilde{V}_{0}
(as
\varepsilon\to 0
) with
\|\tilde{V}_{0}\|\leq C\mu
. Moreover, using the Morse index bound of (the approximations of)
u_{\varepsilon}
, we are also able to deduce a local bound on the isotropic first variation of
\tilde{V}_{0}
away from a finite set

: see Corollary
6.6
. To this aim, we derive a diffuse version of the stability inequality for anisotropic minimal hypersurfaces, stated in Theorem
6.4
. This result parallels those obtained in the isotropic case by Padilla and Tonegawa
[
47
]
and Tonegawa
[
59
]
, although in our setting the PDE degenerates at points where
\nabla u_{\varepsilon}=0
, as

is not
C^{2}
at the zero section (unless

is a quadratic norm). To handle this, we perform our estimates first for suitable smooth approximations of

(the same ones used to regularize

By means of the Modica-type estimate (

) in Theorem
1.1
and
another application of stability, we can also control the weight
\|\tilde{V}_{0}\|
from below with

on

and, more importantly, we can prove that
\tilde{V}_{0}
and

are rectifiable: see Theorem
6.7
. A crucial ingredient in the proof of Theorem
6.7
is to show that the upper density
\Theta^{n-1,*}(\|\tilde{V}_{0}\|,x)>0
for
\|\tilde{V}_{0}\|
-a.e.
x
.
This, combined with the local bound on the isotropic first variation of
\tilde{V}_{0}
, provides the rectifiability of
\tilde{V}_{0}
and hence of
\mu
on
M\setminus\mathcal{S}
, by Allard’s rectifiability theorem
[
3
, Section 5]
.

Our next step involves extracting an
F
-stationary varifold
V
and, as a byproduct of the analysis,
showing that
\mu
is a rectifiable measure on all
M
, as stated in Theorem
7.2
. This is achieved by analyzing the stress-energy tensor

T_{\varepsilon}
of
u_{\varepsilon}
defined in (
7.2
), and proving its convergence as
\varepsilon\to 0
to a tensor-valued measure

T_{0}
whose divergence is controlled. A careful analysis of the directions of invariance of the tangent measures of
T_{0}
provides the desired rectifiability of
\mu
, with an argument in the spirit of
[
19
]
.
Also, writing
d\mu=\theta\,d(\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\mathop{\hbox{\vrule height=7.0pt,width=0.3pt,depth=0.0pt\vrule height=0.3pt,width=5.0pt,depth=0.0pt}}\nolimits\Sigma)
, where
\Sigma\subset M
is a rectifiable Borel set with
\sigma
-finite
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
measure and

, we show that the
(n-1)
-dimensional varifold

dV(x,\nu):=\frac{\theta(x)}{F(x,\nu_{x})}\delta_{\nu_{x}}(\nu)\otimes d(\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\mathop{\hbox{\vrule height=7.0pt,width=0.3pt,depth=0.0pt\vrule height=0.3pt,width=5.0pt,depth=0.0pt}}\nolimits\Sigma)(x)

Our candidate varifold claimed in Theorem

: we are just left to prove that
\frac{\theta(x)}{c_{W}F(x,\nu_{x})}

is an integer for
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
-a.e.

.
This is done by a one-dimensional slicing argument in the normal directions to
\Sigma
. Fix

and assume

\nu_{x}=e_{n}
in a chart. On small cylinders of the form

to obtain

F_{x_{0}}
.
Using the convergence of stress-energy tensors, we show in (

\partial_{x_{i}}u_{\varepsilon}
for

sense, that is, the fact that

is almost one-dimensional.
Such smallness, combined with the Modica-type inequality (

that

is close to (

Preliminaries

2.1
Terminology

Throughout the paper, we fix a smooth closed (i.e., compact and without boundary)
n
-dimensional Riemannian manifold
(M^{n},g)
with
n\geq 2
. We note that the fixed background Riemannian metric plays essentially no role in what follows, since it can be absorbed into the integrand. It is however useful in order to fix a background volume form and to identify
n
-dimensional planes with their normals.

We assume the reader to be familiar with the standard notions in geometric measure theory
[
53
]
, and we adopt the following notation and conventions.

•

UT(M)
: the unit tangent bundle of
M
, namely,

UT(M):=\{(x,v)\in TM:\|v\|_{g_{x}}=1\}.

•

G_{n-1}(M)
: the unoriented hyperplane bundle of
M
, namely,

G_{n-1}(M):=\{(x,T):x\in M,\ T\text{ is an $(n-1)$-dimensional linear subspace of }T_{x}M\}.

By means of the background metric, we can identify
G_{n-1}(M)
with
UT(M)/\sim
, where we have set the equivalence relation
(x,v)\sim(x,-v)
.

•

\partial^{*}E
: the reduced boundary of a Caccioppoli set
E
.

•

\mathcal{V}(M)=\mathcal{V}_{n-1}(M^{n})
: the space of
(n-1)
-varifolds on
M
, namely, the space of nonnegative Radon measure on
G_{n-1}(M)
.

•

\llbracket K\rrbracket
: the integral varifold of multiplicity one associated with an
(n-1)
-rectifiable set
K
with
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(K)<\infty
.

•

\|V\|
: the weight of
V\in\mathcal{V}(M)
, i.e., the Radon measure on
M
associated with
V
.

2.2
Anisotropic energies

In the sequel, an
anisotropic integrand
will be a smooth function
F:G_{n-1}(M)\to(0,\infty)
. Note that, according to the identification above, we can consider it as a function
F:UT(M)\to(0,\infty)
, which is an even function in the second variable. When no confusion arises, we will often switch between these viewpoints without further comment. Also, for the fixed background metric
g
, we will just use the symbol
|v|
for
|v|_{g_{x}}=\sqrt{g_{x}(v,v)}
and
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
for
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}_{g}
. It will also be useful to extend
F

1
-homogeneously in the second variable as

F(x,v)=|v|F\left(x,\frac{v}{|v|}\right),\hskip 10.00002ptF(x,0)=0.

We will always assume this extension has been made whenever we consider derivatives of
F
.

We assume that

F
is uniformly convex in directions orthogonal to the radial direction, namely

D^{2}F_{x}(v)[w,w]\geq\lambda|w|^{2}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for }v\in UT_{x}(M)\text{ and }w\in T_{x}M,\ w\perp v

at all points
x\in M
, where
F_{x}
denotes the restriction of
F
to
T_{x}M
.
Note that, by
1
-homogeneity of
F
, the uniform convexity assumption gives

D^{2}F_{x}(v)[w,w]\geq\lambda\frac{|w|^{2}}{|v|}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for }v\in T_{x}M\setminus\{0\}\text{ and }w\in T_{x}M,\ w\perp v

(2.1)

and the
2
-homogeneous function
F^{2}
is automatically uniformly convex, namely

D^{2}(F^{2}_{x})\geq 2\lambda g_{x}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on }T_{x}M\setminus\{0\}

(2.2)

in the sense of quadratic forms at each point
x\in M
, up to possibly decreasing
\lambda>0
.
Possibly further decreasing
\lambda\in(0,1)
, we can also assume that

\lambda|v|\leq F_{x}(v)\leq\frac{|v|}{\lambda}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all }v\in T_{x}M.

(2.3)

For a finite perimeter set
E
, we denote the
anisotropic perimeter
of
E
by

\mathcal{F}(E):=\int_{\partial^{*}E}F(x,\nu_{x})\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x),

where here
\nu_{x}
denotes the exterior measure-theoretic normal of
E
. Actually, the assumption that
F
is even implies that the sign of the unit normal
\nu_{x}
is irrelevant.

For a varifold
V\in\mathcal{V}(M)
, we define its
F
-anisotropic energy
, and respectively its localized energy to a Borel subset
U\subset M
, as

\mathcal{F}(V):=\int_{G_{n-1}(M)}F(x,T)\,dV(x,T),\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{and respectively}\hskip 10.00002pt\mathcal{F}(V;U):=\int_{G_{n-1}(U)}F(x,T)\,dV(x,T),

where
G_{n-1}(U)
is the restriction of the Grassmannian bundle to
U
.
Note that, by the identification of the
(n-1)
-dimensional Grassmanian with the unit sphere, we can equivalently think an
(n-1)
-dimensional varifold as a measure on
UT(M)
invariant under reflection
(x,v)\mapsto(x,-v)
. In that case we will write

\mathcal{F}(V)=\int_{UT(M)}F(x,\nu)\,dV(x,\nu).

The
first variation
of the
F
-anisotropic energy is defined as

\delta_{F}V(X):=\frac{d}{dt}\Bigl|_{t=0}\mathcal{F}\left((\varphi_{t})_{\#}V\right)

where
V\in\mathcal{V}(M)
,
X\in C^{1}(M,TM)
, and
\varphi_{t}
is the flow of
X
(i.e.,
\frac{d\varphi_{t}}{dt}=X(\varphi_{t})
and
\varphi_{0}=\operatorname{id}_{M}
). Referring to
[
19
]
for the general expression, here we record that when
M=\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
and
F(x,\nu)
is autonomous, i.e., independent of the spatial variable

(so that we can view it as an even function

), we have the following formula:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \delta_{F}V(X)&=\int_{UT(M)}[F(\nu)\operatorname{div}X-\langle DF(\nu),DX^{\top}\nu\rangle]\,dV(x,\nu)\\
&=\int_{UT(M)}\langle F(\nu)I-\nu\otimes DF(\nu),DX\rangle\,dV(x,\nu),\end{aligned}

(2.4)

where
DX^{\top}
is the transpose of
DX
. For the general formula of
\delta_{F}V(X)
, one needs to add a term which depends on
D_{x}F
(see
[
19
]
).
Back to the case of closed
M
, a varifold
V\in\mathcal{V}(M)
is said to have
bounded
F
-anisotropic first variation
if there exists
C>0
such that for all
X\in C^{1}(M,TM)

|\delta_{F}V(X)|\leq C\|X\|_{L^{\infty}}.

Equivalently, an
(n-1)
-varifold
V\in\mathcal{V}(M)
has bounded
F
-anisotropic first variation if
\delta_{F}V
is a (
TM
-valued) Radon measure. We will say that
V
is
F
-stationary
if
\delta_{F}V\equiv 0
. In the isotropic setting
F_{x}(v)=|v|
, we will simply write
\delta V
to denote the first variation of a varifold
V
.

2.3
Anisotropic Allen–Cahn

We now generalize the Allen–Cahn energy to the anisotropic setting by letting

E_{\varepsilon}(u):=\int_{M}\left[\varepsilon\frac{F(x,\nabla u(x))^{2}}{2}+\frac{W(u(x))}{\varepsilon}\right]d\operatorname{vol}_{g}(x),

where
W
is a fixed double-well potential vanishing at
\pm 1
;
we assume that
W:\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)
is smooth with

W>0\text{ on }\mathbb{R}\setminus\{\pm 1\},\hskip 10.00002ptW(\pm 1)=0,\hskip 10.00002ptW^{\prime\prime}(\pm 1)>0,\hskip 10.00002pt(\sqrt{W})^{\prime\prime}\leq-c<0\text{ on }(-1,1),

(2.5)

as well as

-Cs\leq W^{\prime}(s)<0\text{ on }(-\infty,-1),\hskip 10.00002pt0<W^{\prime}(s)\leq Cs\text{ on }(1,\infty),\hskip 10.00002ptcs^{2}\leq W(s)\leq Cs^{2}\text{ on }\mathbb{R}\setminus[-2,2],

(2.6)

for two constants
c,C>0
.
Two standard choices are
W(s)=\frac{(1-s^{2})^{2}}{4}
or
W(s)=1+\cos(\pi s)
, suitably modified outside of the interval
[-1,1]
. The requirements (
2.6
) on
\mathbb{R}\setminus[-1,1]
are actually
irrelevant in the construction of critical points
u
, since we can always modify
W
on this set
in order to satisfy them, and the latter will imply that
|u|\leq 1
.

We will denote by

e_{\varepsilon}(u(x)):=\varepsilon\frac{F(x,\nabla u(x))^{2}}{2}+\frac{W(u(x))}{\varepsilon}

(2.7)

the energy density of
u:M\to\mathbb{R}
with respect to
E_{\varepsilon}
.

We observe that, although
F^{2}
is smooth away from the zero section of
TM
, it is in general only
C^{1}
on
TM
. Indeed, if
F_{x}^{2}
is
C^{2}
at
0
, then by Taylor expansion there exists a symmetric matrix
Q
such that

F_{x}^{2}(v)=\langle Qv,v\rangle+o(|v|^{2}).

From the
2

F^{2}_{x}
we obtain

F_{x}^{2}(v)=\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{F_{x}^{2}(tv)}{t^{2}}=\langle Qv,v\rangle.

We deduce that
F^{2}_{x}
is
C^{2}
if and only if
F_{x}
is the Euclidean norm up to linear changes of coordinates. Having a
C^{1}
functional will be enough for first-order considerations and in particular to check the Palais–Smale condition. However, to give precise meaning to stability (or to Morse index bounds) of critical points, it will be useful to consider smooth approximations
F_{\delta}
converging smoothly to
F
away from the zero section of
TM
, as
\delta\to 0
; see also Remark
3.2
below.

To this aim, for each
\delta\in(0,1)
, we define for every
x\in M

G_{\delta}(x,v):=(F_{x}^{2}*\eta_{\delta})(v)-(F_{x}^{2}*\eta_{\delta})(0)\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{and}\hskip 10.00002ptF_{\delta}(x,v):=\sqrt{G_{\delta}(x,v)},

where
\eta_{\delta}
is the standard radial mollifier supported in the ball
B_{\delta}(0)\subset T_{x}M\cong\mathbb{R}^{n}

(using the metric
g
for the latter identification). Note that
G_{\delta}(x,v)\geq 0

as it is convex and even in
v
, which forces
v=0
to be the minimum point of
G_{\delta}(x,\cdot)
.
Hence,
F_{\delta}^{2}
is smooth,
F_{\delta}^{2}\to F^{2}
in
C^{1}_{loc}(TM)
as
\delta\to 0
,
and for all
\delta\in(0,1)

\mbox{$F_{\delta}$ satisfies \eqref{F.square.cvx}--\eqref{bound}, with a smaller $\lambda^{\prime}>0$ uniform in $\delta$ in place of $\lambda$}.

(2.8)

In fact, the validity of (
2.3
) for
F_{\delta}
, namely
(\lambda^{\prime}|v|)^{2}\leq G_{\delta}(x,v)\leq(|v|/\lambda^{\prime})^{2}
, is immediate to check for fixed
\delta

(if

it follows from (
2.3
) for

, while near the origin it follows from
(
2.2
) and smoothness of

), and its uniformity follows from the fact that

G_{\delta}(x,v)=\delta^{2}G_{1}(x,v/\delta)
.
As an immediate consequence of (

), the differential of
(F_{\delta})_{x}^{2}
is monotone, i.e.,

\langle D(F_{\delta})_{x}^{2}(v)-D(F_{\delta})_{x}^{2}(w),v-w\rangle\geq 2\lambda^{\prime}|v-w|^{2}.

(2.9)

We define the anisotropic Allen-Cahn energy associated with
F_{\delta}
as follows:

E_{\varepsilon,\delta}(u):=\int_{M}\left[\varepsilon\frac{F_{\delta}(x,\nabla u(x))^{2}}{2}+\frac{W(u(x))}{\varepsilon}\right]d\operatorname{vol}_{g}.

As in (

), we denote by

the energy density of

with respect to

3
Existence of nontrivial solutions via min-max

The main purpose of this section is to check the Palais–Smale condition for the functional
E_{\varepsilon,\delta}
and to deduce the existence of nontrivial critical points for
E_{\varepsilon}
.

Proposition 3.1
.

The functional
E_{\varepsilon,\delta}
is finite and of class
C^{2}
on
H^{1}(M)
, with

\displaystyle DE_{\varepsilon,\delta}(u)[v]

\displaystyle=\int_{M}\left[\frac{\varepsilon}{2}D(F_{\delta})_{x}^{2}(\nabla u)[\nabla v]+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}W^{\prime}(u)v\right],

\displaystyle D^{2}E_{\varepsilon,\delta}(u)[v,w]

\displaystyle=\int_{M}\left[\frac{\varepsilon}{2}D^{2}(F_{\delta})_{x}^{2}(\nabla u)[\nabla v,\nabla w]+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}W^{\prime\prime}(u)vw\right].

Moreover, it satisfies the Palais–Smale condition: if
u_{k}\in H^{1}(M)
is a sequence such that
E_{\varepsilon,\delta}(u_{k})
is bounded uniformly in
k
and
DE_{\varepsilon,\delta}(u_{k})\to 0
strongly in
H^{1}(M)
(note that
DE_{\varepsilon,\delta}(u_{k})\in H^{1}(M)^{*}\cong H^{1}(M)
), then
(u_{k})
admits a strongly converging subsequence.

Proof.

The finiteness of
E_{\varepsilon,\delta}
follows from the quadratic growth of
W
, while the
C^{2}

regularity and the formulas for its derivatives are standard calculations.
Since by (
2.6
) and (
2.8
)

(F_{\delta})_{x}^{2}(\nabla u)\geq\lambda^{\prime}|\nabla u|^{2},\hskip 10.00002ptW(u)\geq c|u|^{2}-C,

given a sequence
(u_{k})
as in the statement, we immediately deduce that it is bounded in
H^{1}(M)
; by the Rellich–Kondrachov compactness theorem, there exists
u\in H^{1}(M)
such that
u_{k}
converges to
u
, weakly in
H^{1}(M)
and strongly in
L^{2}(M)
, along a subsequence.
We observe that

\begin{split}\lim_{k\to\infty}DE_{\varepsilon,\delta}(u)[u_{k}-u]&=\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{M}\left[\frac{\varepsilon}{2}D(F_{\delta})_{x}^{2}(\nabla u)[\nabla u_{k}-\nabla u]+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}W^{\prime}(u)(u_{k}-u)\right]=0,\end{split}

(3.2)

where for the first term on the right-hand side we use that
D(F_{\delta})_{x}^{2}(\nabla u)\in L^{2}

(as
|D(F_{\delta})_{x}^{2}(v)|\leq C|v|
) and
\nabla u_{k}-\nabla u\rightharpoonup 0
in
L^{2}
, while for the second term we use that
|W^{\prime}(u)|\leq C|u|+C\in L^{2}
by (
2.6
) and
u_{k}\to u
strongly in
L^{2}
.

Since
DE_{\varepsilon,\delta}(u_{k})\to 0
strongly in
H^{1}
and
u_{k}
is bounded in
H^{1}
uniformly in
k
, we deduce that

\lim_{k\to\infty}DE_{\varepsilon,\delta}(u_{k})[u_{k}-u]=0

and hence

DE_{\varepsilon,\delta}(u_{k})[u_{k}-u]-DE_{\varepsilon,\delta}(u)[u_{k}-u]\to 0.

(3.3)

Arguing again as for (
3.2
), we have
W^{\prime}(u_{k})(u_{k}-u)\to 0
in
L^{1}
, hence we deduce that

\int_{M}[D(F_{\delta})_{x}^{2}(\nabla u_{k})[\nabla u_{k}-\nabla u]-D(F_{\delta})_{x}^{2}(\nabla u)[\nabla u_{k}-\nabla u]]\to 0.

Using (
2.9
), this implies that

\int_{M}|\nabla u_{k}-\nabla u|^{2}\to 0,

and hence
u_{k}\to u
strongly in
H^{1}
,
as desired.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

The original functional
E_{\varepsilon}
is of class
C^{1}
and the previous proof still
applies to show that
E_{\varepsilon}
satisfies the Palais–Smale condition.
On the other hand,
E_{\varepsilon}
cannot be used to give an immediate meaning to stability or to a bound on the Morse index
of critical points.

Proposition 3.3
.

Any critical point
u
of
E_{\varepsilon,\delta}
is smooth and satisfies
|u|\leq 1
.
Also, there exists
\alpha\in(0,1)
depending only on
F
such that, for every family of critical points
(u_{\varepsilon,\delta})_{\delta\in(0,1)}
for the perturbed functionals
E_{\varepsilon,\delta}
with
\sup_{\delta}E_{\varepsilon,\delta}(u_{\varepsilon,\delta})<\infty
, along a subsequence
u_{\varepsilon,\delta}
converges in

as

to
a critical point

u_{\varepsilon}=\lim_{\delta\to 0}u_{\varepsilon,\delta}\in C^{1,\alpha}(M)

for
E_{\varepsilon}
, with
E_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})=\lim_{\delta\to 0}E_{\varepsilon,\delta}(u_{\varepsilon,\delta})
.
Moreover,
u_{\varepsilon}
is smooth on the open set
\{|\nabla u_{\varepsilon}|\neq 0\}
.

Proof.

For each
u=u_{\varepsilon,\delta}
the Euler–Lagrange equation for the functional
E_{\varepsilon,\delta}
reads

\operatorname{div}(a(\nabla u))=\varepsilon^{-2}W^{\prime}(u),

(3.4)

where in local coordinates
a(\nabla u)=g^{ij}\partial_{i}((F_{\delta})_{x}^{2})(\nabla u)\partial_{j}
. Note that the vector field

a
is Lipschitz and, by (
2.9
),
a
is also monotone, in the sense that

\langle a(v)-a(w),v-w\rangle\geq\lambda^{\prime}|v-w|^{2},\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{for all }v,w\in T_{x}M.

The bound
u\leq 1
readily follows by testing the integral form of (
3.4
) with
\varphi:=(u-1)^{+}
and using the strict inequality
0<W^{\prime}
on
(1,\infty)
in (
2.6
). The bound
u\geq-1
is obtained analogously. The fact that
u
is smooth follows
by expanding (
3.4
) into a linear second-order elliptic PDE, using the fact
that
F_{\delta}^{2}
is smooth.

We can apply the results of
[
36
, Chapter 4]
to the PDE (
3.4
) in divergence form to get the uniform bound

\|u_{\varepsilon,\delta}\|_{C^{1,\alpha}}\leq C,

where

may depend on
\varepsilon
and the total energy

, but not on
\delta
, as
\sup_{\delta}E_{\varepsilon,\delta}(u_{\varepsilon,\delta})<\infty

By Arzelà–Ascoli, we deduce the existence of a subsequential limit

in

The limit

is thus a critical point of

is smooth away from the zero section of

.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

In fact, any critical point
u_{\varepsilon}
of

is

of

We are now ready to construct nontrivial critical points for

By (

) and (

), we deduce that the isotropic Allen–Cahn energy

\tilde{E}_{\varepsilon}(u):=\int_{M}\left[\varepsilon\frac{|\nabla u|^{2}}{2}+\frac{W(u)}{\varepsilon}\right]

(\lambda^{\prime})^{2}\tilde{E}_{\varepsilon}(u)\leq E_{\varepsilon,\delta}(u)\leq(\lambda^{\prime})^{-2}\tilde{E}_{\varepsilon}(u),\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{for all }u\in H^{1}(M).

(3.5)

Letting

\Gamma:=\{\gamma:[-1,1]\to H^{1}(M)\,:\,\gamma\text{ continuous},\ \gamma(-1)\equiv-1,\ \gamma(1)\equiv 1\}

and defining the mountain-pass values

c_{\varepsilon,\delta}:=\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\max_{t\in[-1,1]}E_{\varepsilon,\delta}(\gamma(t)),\hskip 10.00002pt\tilde{c}_{\varepsilon,\delta}:=\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\max_{t\in[-1,1]}\tilde{E}_{\varepsilon}(\gamma(t))

as in
[
32
]

[

Combining this with (

and a small

.

For
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0})

for

,

The existence of critical points

and Morse index

[

The main contribution of the present work is to show that,

4
\Gamma
-convergence of
E_{\varepsilon}
to
\mathcal{F}

As a preliminary result, in fact not needed in the next sections,
we prove in this section that
E_{\varepsilon}

\Gamma
-converges to
c_{W}\cdot\mathcal{F}
,
where
c_{W}:=\int_{-1}^{1}\sqrt{2W}
is a positive constant depending only on
W
. This was first proved by Bouchitté
[
12
]
in a more general framework. We provide a shorter proof in Theorem
4.1
, tailored to
E_{\varepsilon}
. In the following we denote by
1_{S}
the indicator function of a set
S
, i.e.,
1_{S}=1
on
S
and
1_{S}=0
on
M\setminus S
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Given a sequence
\varepsilon_{k}\to 0
and maps
u_{k}\in H^{1}(M)

with

\liminf_{k\to\infty}E_{\varepsilon_{k}}(u_{k})<\infty
,
there exists a finite perimeter set
S\subseteq M
such that

u_{k}\to 1_{S}-1_{M\setminus S}

pointwise a.e. and in
L^{2}(M)

up to a subsequence, as well as

c_{W}\cdot\mathcal{F}(S)\leq\liminf_{k\to\infty}E_{\varepsilon_{k}}(u_{k}).

Conversely, given a finite perimeter set
S\subseteq M
,
there exists a family of smooth maps
(u_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}

with
|u_{\varepsilon}|\leq 1
,

u_{\varepsilon}\to 1_{S}-1_{M\setminus S}

pointwise a.e. and in
L^{p}(M)
for all
p<\infty
, and

E_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})\to c_{W}\cdot\mathcal{F}(S).

Proof.

Given a sequence
(\varepsilon_{k},u_{k})
as in the statement,
we will write
\varepsilon
and
u_{\varepsilon}
in place of
\varepsilon_{k}
and
u_{k}
, with a slight abuse of notation.
Up to a subsequence, we can assume that the
\liminf
is a limit. Also,
letting
v_{\varepsilon}:=\min\{\max\{u_{\varepsilon},-2\},2\}
, note that by (
2.6
) we have

E_{\varepsilon}(v_{\varepsilon})\leq E_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon}),\hskip 10.00002pt\int_{M}|u_{\varepsilon}-v_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\leq\int_{\{|u_{\varepsilon}|>2\}}|u_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\leq C\int_{M}W(u_{\varepsilon})\leq C\varepsilon.

Thus, we can assume without loss of generality that
|u_{\varepsilon}|\leq 2
, up to replacing
u_{\varepsilon}
with
v_{\varepsilon}
.

Since
|\nabla u_{\varepsilon}|\leq\lambda^{-1}F_{x}(\nabla u_{\varepsilon})
, by Cauchy–Schwarz we have

\int_{M}\sqrt{2W(u_{\varepsilon})}|\nabla u_{\varepsilon}|\leq\lambda^{-1}\int_{M}\sqrt{2W(u_{\varepsilon})}F_{x}(\nabla u_{\varepsilon})\leq\lambda^{-1}E_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})\leq C

for some constant
C>0
independent of
\varepsilon
.
Thus, letting
H(t):=\int_{0}^{t}\sqrt{2W(s)}\,ds
and
w_{\varepsilon}:=H(u_{\varepsilon})
,
we have a uniform
BV
bound:

\int_{M}(|w_{\varepsilon}|+|\nabla w_{\varepsilon}|)\leq C.

By the compact embedding
BV(M)\hookrightarrow L^{1}(M)
,
up to a subsequence we can find
w_{0}\in BV(M)

such that
w_{\varepsilon}\to w_{0}
in
L^{1}
and pointwise a.e., and thus also in
L^{2}(M)
, as
|w_{\varepsilon}|\leq 2
.

Since
H:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is continuous and bijective and
w_{\varepsilon}\to w_{0}
pointwise a.e.,
we have

u_{\varepsilon}=H^{-1}(w_{\varepsilon})\to H^{-1}(w_{0})=:u_{0}\mbox{ pointwise a.e.},

and the limit
u_{0}
takes values in
\pm 1
a.e. since

\int_{M}W(u_{0})\leq\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}W(u_{\varepsilon})=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}O(\varepsilon)=0.

Moreover,
S:=\{u_{0}=1\}=\{w_{0}=H(1)\}
is a set of finite perimeter,
as its indicator function is precisely
\frac{w_{0}-H(-1)}{H(1)-H(-1)}\in BV(M)
.
The desired bound on the
\mathcal{F}
-perimeter follows easily from the convexity of
F
at each
x\in M
: indeed, a straightforward adaptation
of
[
10
, Theorem 2.38]
to the Riemannian case gives

\displaystyle\int_{M}F_{x}\left(\frac{\nabla w_{0}}{|\nabla w_{0}|}\right)\,d|\nabla w_{0}|

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{M}F_{x}(\nabla w_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{M}H^{\prime}(u_{\varepsilon})F_{x}(\nabla u_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{M}\sqrt{2W(u_{\varepsilon})}F_{x}(\nabla u_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}E_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon}).

This gives the desired conclusion, since

\nabla 1_{S}=\nabla\frac{w_{0}-H(-1)}{H(1)-H(-1)}=\frac{\nabla w_{0}}{H(1)-H(-1)}=\frac{\nabla w_{0}}{c_{W}}

and hence

\mathcal{F}(S)=\int_{\partial^{*}S}F_{x}(\nu_{x})\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x)=\frac{1}{c_{W}}\int_{M}F_{x}\left(\frac{\nabla w_{0}}{|\nabla w_{0}|}\right)\,d|\nabla w_{0}|.

Conversely, given a set
S\subseteq M
of finite perimeter, by
[
10
, Theorem 3.42]

we can find a sequence of smooth open sets
S_{k}
such that

\mathcal{H}^{n}(S_{k}\Delta S)\to 0,\hskip 10.00002pt\operatorname{Per}(S_{k})\to\operatorname{Per}(S),

where
\operatorname{Per}
denotes the isotropic perimeter. Thus, by Reshetnyak’s continuity
principle
[
10
, Theorem 2.39]
, we have the convergence of
\mathcal{F}
-perimeters:

\int_{\partial S_{k}}F_{x}(\nu_{x})\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x)\to\int_{\partial^{*}S}F_{x}(\nu_{x})\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x).

Thus, to prove the existence of a recovery sequence, we can just consider the case where
S
is a smooth open set;
the conclusion then follows by a standard diagonal argument. In this case, we consider the
one-dimensional
heteroclinic solution
U:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
such that

U^{\prime}=\sqrt{2W(U)},\hskip 10.00002ptU(0)=0,\hskip 10.00002pt\lim_{t\to\pm\infty}U(t)=\pm 1

and we let

U_{\gamma}(t):=\begin{cases}-1&\text{for }t\leq-2\gamma\\
U(\gamma t/(2\gamma+t))&\text{for }t\in(-2\gamma,-\gamma]\\
U(t)&\text{for }t\in[-\gamma,\gamma]\\
U(\gamma t/(2\gamma-t))&\text{for }t\in[\gamma,2\gamma)\\
1&\text{for }t\geq 2\gamma.\end{cases}

Using the exponential decay of
U^{\prime}
and of
W(U)
at infinity, it is easy to check that, as
\gamma\to\infty
, we have

\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus[-\gamma,\gamma]}[(U^{\prime})^{2}/2+W(U)]=O(e^{-c\gamma}),\hskip 10.00002pt\int_{-\gamma}^{\gamma}[(U^{\prime})^{2}/2+W(U)]=\int_{-\gamma}^{\gamma}\sqrt{2W(U)}U^{\prime}\to c_{W}.

Then, taking
\delta>0
small and writing
B_{\delta}(\partial S)
as a disjoint union of geodesics of the form

\{\ell_{p}(t):=\exp_{p}(t\nu_{p})\mid t\in(-\delta,\delta)\}

as
p
ranges in
\partial S
, we can define

u_{\varepsilon,\gamma}(\ell_{p}(t)):=U_{\gamma}\left(\frac{t}{\varepsilon F_{p}(\nu_{p})}\right).

As long as
2\gamma\cdot\varepsilon\max_{p\in\partial S}F_{p}(\nu_{p})<\delta
,
we can extend this to a smooth map
u_{\varepsilon,\gamma}:M\to[-1,1]
by
u_{\varepsilon,\gamma}:=-1
on
S\setminus B_{\delta}(\partial S)

and
u_{\varepsilon,\gamma}:=1
on
(M\setminus S)\setminus B_{\delta}(\partial S)
.
We observe that

\nabla u_{\varepsilon,\gamma}(\ell_{p}(t))=U_{\gamma}^{\prime}\left(\frac{t}{\varepsilon F_{p}(\nu_{p})}\right)\frac{\ell_{p}^{\prime}(t)}{\varepsilon F_{p}(\nu_{p})}+O(|t|/\varepsilon)=U_{\gamma}^{\prime}\left(\frac{t}{\varepsilon F_{p}(\nu_{p})}\right)\frac{\ell_{p}^{\prime}(t)}{\varepsilon F_{p}(\nu_{p})}+O(\gamma),

where the error term comes from differentiation of
F\circ\nu
at the nearest-point projection
p
;
note that
|U_{\gamma}^{\prime}|\leq C
and that both sides vanish when
|t|>2\gamma\varepsilon F_{p}(\nu_{p})
.
Moreover,

|\nabla u_{\varepsilon,\gamma}|(\ell_{p}(t))\leq C\varepsilon^{-1}e^{-c\gamma}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for }\gamma\varepsilon F_{p}(\nu_{p})<|t|<\delta

and similarly

W(u_{\varepsilon,\gamma})(\ell_{p}(t))\leq Ce^{-c\gamma}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for }\gamma\varepsilon F_{p}(\nu_{p})<|t|<\delta.

Thus, we have

\int_{\{\gamma\varepsilon F_{p}(\nu_{p})<|t|<\delta\}}[\varepsilon F_{\ell_{p}(t)}(\nabla u_{\varepsilon,\gamma})^{2}/2+\varepsilon^{-1}W(u_{\varepsilon,\gamma})](\ell_{p}(t))\,dt=O(e^{-c\gamma}),

for a possibly different
c>0
, as the integrand is nonzero only on an interval of size
O(\gamma\varepsilon)
.
Hence,

E_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon,\gamma})=(1+O(\delta))\int_{\partial S}\int_{\{|t|<\gamma\varepsilon F_{p}(\nu_{p})\}}[\varepsilon F_{\ell_{p}(t)}(\nabla u_{\varepsilon,\gamma})^{2}/2+\varepsilon^{-1}W(u_{\varepsilon,\gamma})](\ell_{p}(t))\,dt\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(p)+O(e^{-c\gamma}).

Since

F_{\ell_{p}(t)}(\nabla u_{\varepsilon,\gamma}(\ell_{p}(t)))=(1+O(\delta))\varepsilon^{-1}U_{\gamma}^{\prime}\left(\frac{t}{\varepsilon F_{p}(\nu_{p})}\right)+O(\gamma)

by the previous expansion, we deduce that

\displaystyle E_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon,\gamma})

\displaystyle=(1+O(\delta))\int_{\partial S}\int_{\{|t|<\gamma\varepsilon F_{p}(\nu_{p})\}}\left[\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}U_{\gamma}^{\prime}\left(\frac{t}{\varepsilon F_{p}(\nu_{p})}\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}W(U_{\gamma})\left(\frac{t}{\varepsilon F_{p}(\nu_{p})}\right)\right]\,dt\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(p)

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+O(\gamma^{2}\varepsilon)+O(e^{-c\gamma}),

as long as
\gamma\varepsilon<1
. By a simple change of variables, we get

E_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon,\gamma})=(1+O(\delta))\int_{\partial S}F_{p}(\nu_{p})\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(p)\cdot\int_{-\gamma}^{\gamma}[(U_{\gamma}^{\prime})^{2}/2+W(U_{\gamma})]+O(\gamma^{2}\varepsilon)+O(e^{-c\gamma}).

This converges to
\mathcal{F}(S)
as we let
\varepsilon\to 0
, then
\gamma\to\infty

and
\delta\to 0
.
By a diagonal argument, the conclusion follows.
∎

5
A generalization of Modica’s bound

The following pointwise bound for critical points
u
of the isotropic Allen–Cahn

\varepsilon\frac{|\nabla u|^{2}}{2}\leq\frac{W(u)}{\varepsilon}

was first proved by Modica
[
45
]
in the Euclidean setting. This is the fundamental tool used in deriving a sharp monotonicity formula
in the isotropic case
[
34
,
33
]
.

In the anisotropic setting, we obtain an analogous bound; as expected, it no longer yields a monotonicity formula. Nonetheless, it will be a crucial ingredient
in the proof of rectifiability of the limit of the energy densities.

From now on up to the end of the paper, given a map
u:M\to\mathbb{R}
, when evaluating
F(x,\nabla u(x))
with a slight abuse of notation we will look at
\nabla u
as a vector field in the tangent bundle
\nabla u:M\to TM
, and use the compact expression
F(\nabla u)
in place of
F(\cdot,\nabla u(\cdot))
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Letting
u:M\to[-1,1]
be a critical point of
E_{\varepsilon}
, we have

F(\nabla u)\leq\varepsilon^{-1}\sqrt{2W(u)}+C,

where
C
depends only on
M^{n},g
and
F
. As a consequence,

\frac{\varepsilon F^{2}(\nabla u)}{2}\leq\frac{W(u)}{\varepsilon}+C

for a possibly different
C=C(M^{n},g,F)
.

Proof.

Recall that, by Proposition
3.3
and Remark
3.4
, away from
\{\nabla u=0\}
,
u
is smooth and we can expand the Euler–Lagrange equation (
3.4
) as

A^{ij}\partial_{ij}u=\varepsilon^{-2}W^{\prime}(u)+O(\nabla u),

where the coefficients
A^{ij}(x)
are essentially the second derivatives of
F_{x}^{2}/2
at
\nabla u
;
more precisely, in any coordinate chart we have

A^{ij}=g^{i\ell}g^{jm}\partial_{\ell m}(F^{2}/2)(\nabla u)=g^{i\ell}g^{jm}[F(\nabla u)\partial_{\ell m}F(\nabla u)+\partial_{\ell}F(\nabla u)\partial_{m}F(\nabla u)],

where when differentiating
F
and
F^{2}
we use the convention that
\partial_{k}F
denotes the
partial derivative along
\partial_{k}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{k}}\in T_{x}M
of
F_{x}=F|_{T_{x}M}
, at any given
x\in M

(and similarly for higher-order derivatives and for
F^{2}
).
The error term is a function
G(x,\nabla u)
, with
G(x,v)
smooth away from
\{v=0\}
and
1
-homogeneous in
v
,
so that
|\frac{\partial^{\alpha+\beta}G}{\partial x^{\alpha}\partial v^{\beta}}|\leq C_{\alpha,\beta}|v|^{1-|\beta|}
.

We would like to show that

F(\nabla u)-\varepsilon^{-1}\sqrt{2W(u)}\leq\Lambda

everywhere, where
\Lambda>0
will be chosen later. To do this, consider a maximum point
\hat{x}
for the difference

\zeta:=F(\nabla u)-\varepsilon^{-1}\sqrt{2W(u)},

and assume by contradiction that
\zeta(\hat{x})>\Lambda
,
so that in particular
\nabla u(\hat{x})\neq 0
, and thus
u
is smooth around
\hat{x}
, as well as
|u(\hat{x})|<1
.
We now choose a coordinate system centered at
\hat{x}
, with
g_{ij}(0)=\delta_{ij}
and
\partial_{k}g_{ij}(0)=0
, as well as

\nabla u(0)=|\nabla u(0)|e_{n}.

Since
A^{ij}
is positive definite, we have

A^{ij}\partial_{ij}[F(\nabla u)-\varepsilon\sqrt{2W(u)}](\hat{x})=A^{ij}\partial_{ij}\zeta(\hat{x})\leq 0.

We now compute that at
\hat{x}=0
we have

\displaystyle\partial_{ij}[F(\nabla u)-\varepsilon^{-1}\sqrt{2W(u)}]

\displaystyle=\partial_{i}[g^{k\ell}\partial_{k}F(\nabla u)\partial_{j\ell}u-\varepsilon^{-1}(\sqrt{2W})^{\prime}(u)\partial_{j}u+O(\nabla u)]

\displaystyle=\partial_{k\ell}F(\nabla u)\partial_{i\ell}u\partial_{jk}u+\partial_{k}F(\nabla u)\partial_{ijk}u-\varepsilon^{-1}(\sqrt{2W})^{\prime}(u)\partial_{ij}u

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt-\varepsilon^{-1}(\sqrt{2W})^{\prime\prime}(u)\partial_{i}u\partial_{j}u+O(\nabla u)+O(D^{2}u),

where the error terms come from differentiating
F
in the spatial variable at least once.

Once we multiply by
A^{ij}
and sum over
i,j
, we get

\displaystyle I+A^{ij}\partial_{k}F(\nabla u)\partial_{ijk}u-\varepsilon^{-3}\frac{W^{\prime}(u)^{2}}{\sqrt{2W(u)}}-\varepsilon^{-1}(\sqrt{2W})^{\prime\prime}(u)F^{2}(\nabla u)

\displaystyle\leq O(\varepsilon^{-1}\nabla u)+O(D^{2}u),

(5.1)

where we omit the sum over
i,j
and we set

I:=A^{ij}\partial_{k\ell}F(\nabla u)\partial_{i\ell}u\partial_{jk}u

and we used the Euler–Lagrange equation and the fact that

A^{ij}\partial_{i}u\partial_{j}u=D^{2}(F^{2}/2)(\nabla u)[\nabla u,\nabla u]=F^{2}(\nabla u),

since
F^{2}
is 2-homogeneous.

We rewrite the second term of (
5.1
) as

\displaystyle\partial_{k}(A^{ij}\partial_{ij}u)\partial_{k}F(\nabla u)-\partial_{k}A^{ij}\partial_{k}F(\nabla u)\partial_{ij}u

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{-2}W^{\prime\prime}(u)\partial_{k}u\partial_{k}F(\nabla u)-\partial_{ij\ell}(F^{2}/2)(\nabla u)\partial_{k\ell}u\partial_{k}F(\nabla u)\partial_{ij}(u)+O(\nabla u)+O(D^{2}u),

where we used again the Euler–Lagrange equation and
we expanded
\partial_{k}A^{ij}
using the chain rule.
Since
F
is 1-homogeneous, at
\hat{x}
we have

\partial_{k}u\partial_{k}F(\nabla u)=F(\nabla u).

Moreover, since
\nabla\zeta(\hat{x})=0
, at
\hat{x}
we have

\partial_{k\ell}u\partial_{k}F(\nabla u)=\partial_{\ell}[F(\nabla u)]+O(\nabla u)=\varepsilon^{-1}\partial_{\ell}\sqrt{2W(u)}+O(\nabla u)=\varepsilon^{-1}(\sqrt{2W})^{\prime}(u)\partial_{\ell}u+O(\nabla u),

giving

\partial_{ij\ell}(F^{2}/2)(\nabla u)\partial_{k\ell}u\partial_{k}F(\nabla u)\partial_{ij}(u)=\varepsilon^{-1}(\sqrt{2W})^{\prime}(u)\partial_{ij\ell}(F^{2}/2)(\nabla u)\partial_{\ell}u\partial_{ij}(u)+O(D^{2}u),

thanks to the fact that
\partial_{ij\ell}(F^{2}/2)
is
(-1)
-homogeneous, so that

|\partial_{ij\ell}(F^{2}/2)(\nabla u)|\leq C|\nabla u|^{-1}
.
Also, since
\partial_{ij}(F^{2}/2)
is
0
-homogeneous, we have

\partial_{ij\ell}(F^{2}/2)(\nabla u)\partial_{\ell}u=0.

In summary, at
\hat{x}
we get

A^{ij}\partial_{k}F(\nabla u)\partial_{ijk}u=\varepsilon^{-2}W^{\prime\prime}(u)F(\nabla u)+O(D^{2}u).

Thus, (
5.1
) becomes

I+\varepsilon^{-2}W^{\prime\prime}(u)F(\nabla u)-\varepsilon^{-3}\frac{W^{\prime}(u)^{2}}{\sqrt{2W(u)}}-\varepsilon^{-1}(\sqrt{2W})^{\prime\prime}(u)F^{2}(\nabla u)\leq O(\varepsilon^{-1}\nabla u)+O(D^{2}u).

Now
I
is nonnegative, since it is the trace of the product of two positive semidefinite matrices; in fact,
by (
2.1
) and the fact that
D^{2}F(v)[v,\cdot]=0
for
v\neq 0
(by
0
-homogeneity of
DF_{x}
),
we have

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle:=A^{ij}\partial_{k\ell}F(\nabla u)\partial_{i\ell}u\partial_{jk}u

\displaystyle\phantom{:}=\operatorname{tr}((D^{2}F)(D^{2}uAD^{2}u))

\displaystyle\phantom{:}\geq\frac{\lambda}{|\nabla u|}\operatorname{tr}((I-e_{n}^{*}\otimes e_{n}^{*})(D^{2}uAD^{2}u))

\displaystyle\phantom{:}\geq\frac{\lambda^{2}}{|\nabla u|}\operatorname{tr}((I-e_{n}^{*}\otimes e_{n}^{*})(D^{2}u)^{2})

\displaystyle\phantom{:}\geq\frac{\lambda^{2}}{|\nabla u|}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}|\partial_{ij}u|^{2},

where we repeatedly used the fact that
\operatorname{tr}(AB)\geq\operatorname{tr}(A^{\prime}B^{\prime})
if
A\geq A^{\prime}\geq 0
and
B\geq B^{\prime}\geq 0
.
Moreover, by the Euler–Lagrange equation, the lower bound
A^{nn}\geq\lambda
, and the estimate
|W^{\prime}|\leq C\sqrt{W}
(as
W^{\prime\prime}(\pm 1)>0
), we have

|\partial_{nn}u|\leq C\sum_{(i,j)\neq(n,n)}|\partial_{ij}u|+O(\nabla u)+C\varepsilon^{-2}\sqrt{W(u)}.

(5.2)

By the assumption that
\varepsilon^{-1}\sqrt{W(u)}<F(\nabla u)
at
\hat{x}
, the estimate (
5.2
) in turn implies that at
\hat{x}

|\partial_{nn}u|\leq C\sum_{(i,j)\neq(n,n)}|\partial_{ij}u|+O(\varepsilon^{-1}\nabla u).

Thus, by Cauchy’s inequality,
we can absorb the term
O(D^{2}u)
, getting

\displaystyle I+O(D^{2}u)\geq-C|\nabla u|-C\varepsilon^{-1}|\nabla u|\geq-C\varepsilon^{-1}F(\nabla u)

at
\hat{x}
, where
C
depends on the implied constant in
O(D^{2}u)
. We deduce that

\varepsilon^{-2}W^{\prime\prime}(u)F(\nabla u)-\varepsilon^{-3}\frac{W^{\prime}(u)^{2}}{\sqrt{2W(u)}}-\varepsilon^{-1}(\sqrt{2W})^{\prime\prime}(u)F^{2}(\nabla u)\leq C\varepsilon^{-1}F(\nabla u).

(5.3)

Since
W^{\prime\prime}=(\sqrt{2W})^{\prime\prime}\cdot\sqrt{2W}+\frac{(W^{\prime})^{2}}{2W}
,
we can rewrite (
5.3
) as

\displaystyle-\varepsilon^{-1}(\sqrt{2W})^{\prime\prime}(u)F(\nabla u)[F(\nabla u)-\varepsilon^{-1}\sqrt{2W(u)}]+\varepsilon^{-2}\frac{(W^{\prime})^{2}}{2W}[F(\nabla u)-\varepsilon^{-1}\sqrt{2W(u)}]\leq-I+O(\varepsilon^{-1}\nabla u)

at
\hat{x}
; recalling that
-(\sqrt{2W})^{\prime\prime}\geq c>0
by (
2.5
),
we reach

c\varepsilon^{-1}F(\nabla u)[F(\nabla u)-\varepsilon^{-1}\sqrt{2W(u)}]+\varepsilon^{-2}\frac{(W^{\prime})^{2}}{2W}[F(\nabla u)-\varepsilon^{-1}\sqrt{2W(u)}]\leq C\varepsilon^{-1}F(\nabla u).

This contradicts the fact that
F(\nabla u)-\varepsilon^{-1}\sqrt{2W(u)}>\Lambda
, once we take
\Lambda
large enough that
c\Lambda\geq C
.
∎

6
Uniform bounds for stable solutions

In this section we derive bounds on the second fundamental form of level sets for stable solutions
u_{\varepsilon,\delta}
with respect to
E_{\varepsilon,\delta}
and consequently for limits
u_{\varepsilon}
thereof. Moreover we obtain lower density bounds for the energy. Since
F^{2}
is not of class
C^{2}
, in many statements
we will replace it with the perturbed integrands
F_{\delta}^{2}
already considered earlier, in order to make sense of stability.
Let us start with a simple observation.

Proposition 6.1
.

There exists
\rho=\rho(M^{n},g,F)>0
large enough
such that the following holds.
Given
x_{0}\in M
, we identify
T_{x_{0}}M\cong\mathbb{R}^{n}
isometrically, so that
the restriction
(F_{\delta})_{x_{0}}=F_{\delta}|_{T_{x_{0}}M}
gives an autonomous integrand
\overline{F}_{\delta}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to[0,\infty)
.
If a constant
\overline{u}\in[-1,1]
is a stable critical point for

\overline{E}_{1,\delta}(u):=\int_{B_{\rho}(0)}[{\overline{F}_{\delta}(\nabla u)^{2}}/{2}+W(u)]

on the Euclidean ball
B_{\rho}(0)\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
then
\overline{u}\in\{-1,1\}
.

Proof.

Indeed, assume by contradiction that
u_{0}\in(-1,1)
. For any
\eta\in C^{1}_{c}(B_{\rho}(0))
, stability gives

\int_{B_{\rho}(0)}[D^{2}(\overline{F}_{\delta}^{2}/2)(0)[\nabla\eta,\nabla\eta]+W^{\prime\prime}(\overline{u})\eta^{2}]\geq 0.

Since
W^{\prime}(\overline{u})=W^{\prime}(\pm 1)=0
and
W^{\prime\prime}(\pm 1)>0
, we must have
|\overline{u}|\leq 1-c
for some
c>0
.
Also, using again the fact that
W^{\prime}(\overline{u})=0
, we have

W^{\prime\prime}(\overline{u})=2(\sqrt{W})^{\prime\prime}(\overline{u})\sqrt{W(\overline{u})}.

Using (
2.5
), we see that
-W^{\prime\prime}(\overline{u})\geq c
for a possibly different
c>0
.
We deduce that

c\int_{B_{\rho}}\eta^{2}\leq\int_{B_{\rho}}D^{2}(\overline{F}_{\delta}^{2}/2)(0)[\nabla\eta,\nabla\eta]\leq C\int_{B_{\rho}}|\nabla\eta|^{2}

(6.1)

for all
\eta\in C^{1}_{c}(B_{\rho})
. This is impossible once we take
\rho>0
large enough.
∎

The following are useful consequences.

Lemma 6.2
.

Given
\gamma>0
,
there exist constants
c>0
and
\varepsilon_{0}>0
, depending on
\gamma
and
(M^{n},g,F)
, such that

\int_{B_{\rho\varepsilon}(p)}\sqrt{2W(u)}|\nabla u|\geq c\varepsilon^{n-1}

whenever
|u(p)|\leq 1-\gamma
,
for any stable critical point
u:B_{\rho\varepsilon}(p)\to\mathbb{R}
of
E_{\varepsilon,\delta}
, provided that

\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0})
.

Proof.

Arguing by contradiction, dilating the domain by a factor
\varepsilon^{-1}
, assume that

\int_{B_{\rho}^{(\varepsilon)}}\sqrt{2W(\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon})}|\nabla\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon}|\to 0

for a sequence of rescaled functions
\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon}
, defined on rescaled geodesic balls
B_{\rho}^{(\varepsilon)}

and stably critical for
E_{1,\delta_{\varepsilon}}
, for some
\delta_{\varepsilon}\in(0,1)
.
Since
\varepsilon\to 0
, the rescaled metrics converge to the Euclidean one.

Since
u_{\varepsilon}
is uniformly
C^{1,\alpha}
on
B_{\rho^{\prime}}^{(\varepsilon)}
, for any
\rho^{\prime}\in(0,\rho)
(see Proposition
3.3
and its proof), once we identify each
B_{\rho}^{(\varepsilon)}
with the Euclidean ball
B_{\rho}(0)
, these solutions
converge in
C^{1}_{loc}
, along a subsequence, to a critical point
\tilde{u}_{0}:B_{\rho}(0)\to[-1,1]

for
E_{1,\delta_{0}}
, where
\delta_{0}:=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\delta_{\varepsilon}\in[0,1]
. Here the limit energy
E_{1,\delta_{0}}
involves the autonomous integrand
F_{p_{0}}
, where
p_{0}=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}p_{\varepsilon}
.
Also,
|\tilde{u}_{0}(0)|=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}|u_{\varepsilon}(p_{\varepsilon})|\leq 1-\gamma
and

\int_{B_{\rho}(0)}\sqrt{2W(\tilde{u}_{0})}|\nabla\tilde{u}_{0}|=0,

so that
\nabla\tilde{u}_{0}
vanishes on the open set
\{\tilde{u}_{0}\in(-1,1)\}
.
We deduce that
\tilde{u}_{0}
is a constant value in
[-1+\gamma,1-\gamma]
.
If
\delta_{0}>0
, then
\tilde{u}_{0}
is also stable and we can immediately apply Proposition
6.1

to reach a contradiction.

If
\delta_{0}=0
, we can still derive an inequality like (
6.1
): namely, for
\varepsilon
small enough, depending on
\operatorname{spt}(\eta)
, we can write

c\int_{B_{\rho}^{(\varepsilon)}}\eta^{2}\leq C\int_{B_{\rho}^{(\varepsilon)}}|\nabla\eta|^{2},

by exploiting the stability of
\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon}
and using the fact that
W^{\prime\prime}(\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon})\to W^{\prime\prime}(\tilde{u}_{0})\leq-c<0

locally uniformly.
In the limit we get
c\int_{B_{\rho}}\eta^{2}\leq C\int_{B_{\rho}}|\nabla\eta|^{2}
,
for all
\eta\in C^{1}_{c}(B_{\rho})
, which is again impossible by our choice of
\rho
in the proof of Proposition
6.1
, making (
6.1
) fail.
∎

Lemma 6.3
.

There exist constants
c>0
and
\varepsilon_{0}>0
, depending on
(M^{n},g,F)
, such that

\int_{B_{2r}(p)}\varepsilon F_{\delta}(\nabla u)^{2}\geq c\int_{B_{r}(p)}\frac{W(u)}{\varepsilon}

for any stable critical point
u:B_{2r}(p)\to\mathbb{R}
of
E_{\varepsilon,\delta}
, provided that

\rho\varepsilon<\min\{r,\varepsilon_{0}\}
.

Proof.

Arguing by contradiction, assume that a stable solution
u_{\varepsilon}
satisfies the reverse inequality for arbitrarily small
c,\varepsilon>0
. Since we can cover
B_{r}(p)
by a family of balls
B_{\rho\varepsilon}(x)\subseteq B_{2r}(p)
with bounded overlap, we can find a sequence of stable solutions

u_{\varepsilon}
(with
\varepsilon\to 0
) and points
x_{\varepsilon}\in M
such that

\int_{B_{2\rho\varepsilon}(x_{\varepsilon})}\varepsilon F(\nabla u_{\varepsilon})^{2}<c_{\varepsilon}\int_{B_{\rho\varepsilon}(x_{\varepsilon})}\frac{W(u_{\varepsilon})}{\varepsilon},

(6.2)

with
c_{\varepsilon}\to 0
.

We can rescale
u_{\varepsilon}
to functions
\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon}
, defined on rescaled geodesic balls
B_{2\rho}^{(\varepsilon)}
converging to the Euclidean ball
B_{2\rho}(0)
.
As in the previous proof, we have
\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon}\to\tilde{u}_{0}
in
C^{1}_{loc}
up to a subsequence.
Since

\int_{B_{2\rho}^{(\varepsilon)}}|\nabla\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\leq C\int_{B_{2\rho}^{(\varepsilon)}}F(\nabla\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon})^{2}<C\cdot c_{\varepsilon}\int_{B_{\rho}^{(\varepsilon)}}W(\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon})\to 0,

in the limit we deduce that
\tilde{u}_{0}
is constant
on
B_{2\rho}(0)
.

If
u_{\varepsilon}
is critical and stable for
E_{\varepsilon,\delta_{\varepsilon}}
with
\delta_{0}:=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\delta_{\varepsilon}>0
,
then
\tilde{u}_{0}
is critical and stable for
E_{1,\delta_{0}}
, so that Proposition

6.1
gives
\tilde{u}_{0}\in\{\pm 1\}
; if instead
\delta_{0}=0
,
we can reach the same conclusion by arguing exactly as in the previous proof.

Now, recalling that
W^{\prime\prime}(\pm 1)>0
, we can fix
\gamma\in(0,1)
such that

-\operatorname{sgn}(s)W^{\prime}(s)(1-|s|)\geq 4c(1-|s|)^{2}\geq(1-s^{2})^{2}\text{ for }|s|\in[1-\gamma,1],

while, for a possibly different
c>0
, we also have

c(1-s^{2})^{2}\leq W(s)\leq C(1-s^{2})^{2}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for }|s|\leq 1.

Since
|\tilde{u}_{0}|=1
, we have
|\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon}|\in[1-\gamma,1]
on
B_{3\rho/2}^{(\varepsilon)}
eventually.

Let us take a cut-off function
\varphi_{\varepsilon}\in C^{1}_{c}(B_{3\rho/2}^{(\varepsilon)})
, equal to
1
on
B_{\rho}^{(\varepsilon)}
and with
|\nabla\varphi_{\varepsilon}|\leq 4\rho^{-1}
.
Assuming for instance that
\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon}\geq 1-\gamma
on the ball
B_{3\rho/2}^{(\varepsilon)}
and
testing the Euler–Lagrange equation (
3.4
) with
\varphi_{\varepsilon}^{2}(1-\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon})
, we find

-\int_{B_{2\rho}^{(\varepsilon)}}\varphi_{\varepsilon}^{2}W^{\prime}(\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon})(1-\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon})=\int_{B_{2\rho}^{(\varepsilon)}}\langle a_{\varepsilon}(\nabla\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon}),\nabla(\varphi_{\varepsilon}^{2}(1-\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon}))\rangle,

for suitable
a_{\varepsilon}:TM\to TM
with
|a_{\varepsilon}(v)|\leq C|v|
.
Applying Young’s inequality, we deduce that

-\int_{B_{2\rho}^{(\varepsilon)}}\varphi_{\varepsilon}^{2}W^{\prime}(\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon})(1-\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon})\leq C(\sigma)\int_{B_{2\rho}^{(\varepsilon)}}|\nabla\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon}|^{2}+\sigma\int_{B_{2\rho}^{(\varepsilon)}}\varphi_{\varepsilon}^{2}(1-\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon})^{2}

for an arbitrarily small
\sigma>0
. Using the fact that
\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon}\in[1-\gamma,1]
on the support of
\varphi_{\varepsilon}
,
we deduce that

c\int_{B_{2\rho}^{(\varepsilon)}}\varphi_{\varepsilon}^{2}(1-\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon}^{2})^{2}\leq C(\sigma)\int_{B_{2\rho}^{(\varepsilon)}}|\nabla\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon}|^{2}+\sigma\int_{B_{2\rho}^{(\varepsilon)}}\varphi_{\varepsilon}^{2}(1-\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon})^{2},

and thus, taking
\sigma:=c/2
, we get

\int_{B_{2\rho}^{(\varepsilon)}}\varphi_{\varepsilon}^{2}(1-\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon}^{2})^{2}\leq C\int_{B_{2\rho}^{(\varepsilon)}}|\nabla\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon}|^{2},

or alternatively

\int_{B_{2\rho}^{(\varepsilon)}}\varphi_{\varepsilon}^{2}W(\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon})^{2}\leq C\int_{B_{2\rho}^{(\varepsilon)}}F(\nabla\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon})^{2}.

Thus, we have

\int_{B_{\rho}^{(\varepsilon)}}W(\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon})^{2}\leq C\int_{B_{2\rho}^{(\varepsilon)}}F(\nabla\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon})^{2},

which contradicts (
6.2
). The case
\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon}\in[-1,-1+\gamma]
is analogous.
∎

We record here a diffuse version of the
stability inequality
for
\mathcal{F}
-stationary hypersurfaces,
similar to the one obtained for the isotropic Allen–Cahn
[
47
]
, first for stable critical points
of
E_{\varepsilon,\delta}
.
We will then let
\delta\to 0
to derive a consequence for critical points
u_{\varepsilon}
of
E_{\varepsilon}
which are limits
u_{\varepsilon}=\lim_{\delta\to 0}u_{\varepsilon,\delta}

of stable critical points of
E_{\varepsilon,\delta}
.

Theorem 6.4
.

Assume that
u
is a stable critical point for
E_{\varepsilon,\delta}
on an open set
U\subseteq M
.
Then we have

\int_{U}\varphi^{2}|\operatorname{II}_{u}|^{2}\cdot\varepsilon F_{\delta}(\nabla u)^{2}\leq C(\varphi)\int_{U}e_{\varepsilon,\delta}(u),

where
\operatorname{II}_{u}(x)
denotes the second fundamental form of the level set
\{u=u(x)\}
if
\nabla u(x)\neq 0
, and it is set to be zero on
\{\nabla u=0\}
.

Proof.

Since
u_{\delta}
is stable on
U
, for any
\varphi\in C^{2}_{c}(U)

a straightforward computation shows that

\int_{U}\left[A^{ij}_{\delta}\partial_{i}\varphi\partial_{j}\varphi+\frac{W^{\prime\prime}(u_{\delta})}{\varepsilon^{2}}\varphi^{2}\right]\geq 0,

where as above
A^{ij}_{\delta}=g^{i\ell}g^{jm}\partial_{\ell m}(F_{\delta}^{2}/2)(\nabla u)
, and
\partial_{\ell m}(F_{\delta}^{2}/2)
denotes the second derivative of
(F_{\delta}^{2}/2)|_{T_{x}M}
along
\partial_{\ell},\partial_{m}\in T_{x}M
, at any given
x\in M
.

Since
|\nabla u|
is Lipschitz, by a standard approximation argument we can plug
\varphi|du_{\delta}|
in place of
\varphi
,
obtaining

\displaystyle\int_{U}\left[\varphi^{2}\left(A^{ij}_{\delta}\partial_{i}|\nabla u|\partial_{j}|\nabla u|+\frac{W^{\prime\prime}(u)}{\varepsilon^{2}}|\nabla u|^{2}\right)+A^{ij}_{\delta}\partial_{i}\varphi\partial_{j}\varphi|\nabla u|^{2}+2A^{ij}_{\delta}\varphi|\nabla u|\partial_{i}\varphi\partial_{j}|\nabla u|\right]\geq 0.

(6.3)

Next, testing criticality with
\varphi^{2}\Delta u
, we have

\int_{U}\left[g^{ij}\partial_{i}(F_{\delta}^{2}/2)(\nabla u)\partial_{j}(\varphi^{2}\Delta u)+\frac{W^{\prime}(u)}{\varepsilon^{2}}\varphi^{2}\Delta u\right]=0.

Writing
\partial_{j}(\varphi^{2}\Delta u)=\varphi^{2}g^{k\ell}\partial_{kj\ell}u+\varphi[O(\nabla u)+O(D^{2}u)]
, with implied constants depending on
\varphi
, and noting that

\partial_{k}[\partial_{i}(F_{\delta}^{2}/2)(\nabla u)]=g^{pq}\partial_{ip}(F_{\delta}^{2}/2)(\nabla u)\partial_{qk}u,

after an integration by parts and relabeling of indices we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{U}\left[\varphi^{2}A^{ij}_{\delta}g^{k\ell}\partial_{ik}u\partial_{j\ell}u+\varphi^{2}\frac{W^{\prime\prime}(u)}{\varepsilon^{2}}|\nabla u|^{2}+\varepsilon^{-2}\langle\nabla(\varphi^{2}),\nabla(W(u))\rangle\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{U}\varphi[O(|\nabla u|^{2})+O(\nabla u)O(D^{2}u)],

and hence

\displaystyle\int_{U}\left[\varphi^{2}A^{ij}_{\delta}g^{k\ell}\partial_{ik}u\partial_{j\ell}u+\varphi^{2}\frac{W^{\prime\prime}(u)}{\varepsilon^{2}}|\nabla u|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{U}[\varepsilon^{-1}\cdot O(e_{\varepsilon}(u))+\varphi\cdot O(\nabla u)O(D^{2}u)].

(6.4)

Subtracting (
6.3
) from (
6.4
), we get

\int_{U}\varphi^{2}A^{ij}_{\delta}[g^{k\ell}\partial_{ik}u\partial_{j\ell}u-\partial_{i}|\nabla u|\partial_{j}|\nabla u|]\leq C(\varphi)\int_{U}[\varepsilon^{-1}e_{\varepsilon}(u)+\varphi|\nabla u||D^{2}u|].

We now absorb the last error term: fixing a point
p\in M
where
\nabla u(p)\neq 0
and choosing a chart centered at
p
,
with
g_{ij}(p)=\delta_{ij}
,
\partial_{k}g_{ij}(p)=0
, and
\nabla u(p)=|\nabla u(p)|\partial_{n}
, as in (
5.2
) we observe that

|\partial_{nn}u(p)|\leq C\sum_{(i,j)\neq(n,n)}|\partial_{ij}u|,

while the integrand in the left-hand side is equal to
\varphi^{2}
times

A^{ij}_{\delta}[\partial_{ik}u\partial_{jk}u-\partial_{in}u\partial_{jn}u]\geq\lambda^{\prime}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}|\partial_{ik}u|^{2}.

On the other hand, the same term equals
A^{ij}_{\delta}\partial_{ik}u\partial_{jk}u\geq\lambda^{\prime}\sum_{i,k=1}^{n}|\partial_{ik}u|^{2}

at a.e. point where
|\nabla u|=0
.
Thus, by Cauchy’s inequality, we reach the bound

\int\varphi^{2}A^{ij}_{\delta}[g^{k\ell}\partial_{ik}u\partial_{j\ell}u-\partial_{i}|\nabla u|\partial_{j}|\nabla u|]\leq C(\varphi)\int_{U}\varepsilon^{-1}e_{\varepsilon}(u),

(6.5)

for a possibly larger
C(\varphi)
.

Let
A_{\delta}
denote the section of
TM\otimes TM
with components
A^{ij}_{\delta}
(in a coordinate chart),
which (using the metric
g
) we can recast as a positive definite
(1,1)
-tensor
A_{\delta}\geq\lambda^{\prime}I
.
On
\{\nabla u\neq 0\}
, letting
\nu:=\frac{\nabla u}{|\nabla u|}
, we observe that the integrand on the left-hand side can be written more compactly as

\varphi^{2}\operatorname{tr}((HA_{\delta}H)(g-\nu^{*}\otimes\nu^{*}))\geq\lambda^{\prime}\varphi^{2}\operatorname{tr}(H^{2}(g-\nu^{*}\otimes\nu^{*})),\hskip 10.00002ptH:=D^{2}u.

Given a point
p
and selecting a coordinate chart as above, writing
e_{i}:=\partial_{i}
, for
i=1,\dots,n-1
we have

|\operatorname{II}_{u}(e_{i})|^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}|\langle\partial_{i}\nu,e_{j}\rangle|^{2}=|\partial_{i}\nu|^{2}

at
p
, as the tangent space of the level set is spanned by
\{e_{1},\dots,e_{n-1}\}
, while
e_{n}=\nu
. Moreover

\partial_{i}\nu=\frac{H(e_{i})-H(e_{i},\nu)\nu}{|\nabla u|},

so that for all
j=1,\dots,n-1
we have

\langle\partial_{i}\nu,e_{j}\rangle=\frac{H(e_{i},e_{j})}{|\nabla u|}.

Thus,

|\operatorname{II}_{u}|^{2}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n-1}\left|\frac{H(e_{i},e_{j})}{|\nabla u|}\right|^{2}\leq\frac{\operatorname{tr}(H^{2}(g-\nu^{*}\otimes\nu^{*}))}{|\nabla u|^{2}}.

We deduce that

\int_{U}\varphi^{2}|\operatorname{II}_{u}|^{2}|\nabla u|^{2}\leq C\varphi^{2}\operatorname{tr}((HA_{\delta}H)(g-\nu^{*}\otimes\nu^{*})).

The claim now follows from (
6.5
).
∎

Now, given a critical point
u_{\varepsilon}
for the energy
E_{\varepsilon}
, we define the
(n-1)
-dimensional varifold

\tilde{V}_{\varepsilon}
to be the measure on the Grassmannian bundle
G:=G_{n-1}(M)
given by

\tilde{V}_{\varepsilon}(f):=\int_{\{\nabla u_{\varepsilon}\neq 0\}}\sqrt{2W(u_{\varepsilon})}|\nabla u_{\varepsilon}|\cdot f(P_{u_{\varepsilon}})\,d\operatorname{vol}_{g},

(6.6)

for any
f\in C^{0}(G)
, where
P_{u_{\varepsilon}}(x):=(\nabla u_{\varepsilon}(x))^{\perp}\in G
is the tangent plane to the level set
\{u_{\varepsilon}=u_{\varepsilon}(x)\}

at
x
. Here we make a slight abuse of notation: we write
f(P_{u_{\varepsilon}}(x))
in place of
f(x,P_{u_{\varepsilon}}(x))
.

Remark 6.5
.

If almost all level sets
\{u_{\varepsilon}=\lambda\}
are regular then, viewing them as the
(n-1)
-varifolds
\llbracket\{u_{\varepsilon}=\lambda\}\rrbracket
,
we have

\tilde{V}_{\varepsilon}=\int_{-1}^{1}\sqrt{2W(\lambda)}\cdot\llbracket\{u_{\varepsilon}=\lambda\}\rrbracket\,d\lambda,

by the coarea formula. In other words, in this case
\tilde{V}_{\varepsilon}
is simply a weighted average of the level sets of
u_{\varepsilon}
.

Further, given a sequence of critical points
(u_{\varepsilon})
with
\varepsilon\to 0
and

\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}E_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})<\infty,

(6.7)

we can assume that the
\liminf
is a limit (up to a subsequence) and define the measure

d\mu=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}e_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})\,d\operatorname{vol}_{g},

namely
\mu
is the limit of the energy densities in duality with
C^{0}(M)
, up to a subsequence.

Corollary 6.6
.

Assuming (
6.7
), up to a subsequence we can extract a limit varifold
\tilde{V}_{0}
, with weight

\|\tilde{V}_{0}\|\leq C\mu.

If moreover each
u_{\varepsilon}=\lim_{\delta\to 0}u_{\varepsilon,\delta}
, for a suitable sequence

(u_{\varepsilon,\delta})_{\delta}
of critical points for
E_{\varepsilon,\delta}
with Morse index
\leq m
independently of
\varepsilon,\delta
,
then there exists a finite set of points
\mathcal{S}
, with
\#\mathcal{S}\leq m
, such that any

p\not\in\mathcal{S}
admits a neighborhood
U
where the
isotropic
first variation
\delta\tilde{V}_{0}
satisfies

|\delta\tilde{V}_{0}|(U)\leq C(U).

The sequence
\delta=\delta_{k}\to 0
used in the limit
u_{\varepsilon}=\lim_{\delta\to 0}u_{\varepsilon,\delta}
is allowed to depend on
\varepsilon
.
In the last part of the statement,
C(U)
denotes a finite constant which may depend on all data (in particular, on
U
).

Proof.

The total weight of
\tilde{V}_{\varepsilon}

on an open set
U\subseteq M
equals

\|\tilde{V}_{\varepsilon}\|=\int_{U}\sqrt{2W(u_{\varepsilon})}|\nabla u_{\varepsilon}|\leq C\int_{U}e_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon}),

by Cauchy–Schwarz and the bound
|\nabla u_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\leq\lambda^{-2}F(\nabla u_{\varepsilon})^{2}
,
showing the first claim.

Now assume that
u_{\varepsilon}=\lim_{\delta\to 0}u_{\varepsilon,\delta}
and that each
u_{\varepsilon,\delta}
is stable on
U
.
Then, recalling that the convergence is in
C^{1,\alpha}(M)
and that the integrand defining
\tilde{V}_{\varepsilon}
contains the weight
|\nabla u|
, which vanishes on the complement
of
\{\nabla u\neq 0\}
, it is straightforward to check that in the sense of varifolds

\tilde{V}_{\varepsilon}=\lim_{\delta\to 0}\tilde{V}_{\varepsilon,\delta}.

Now, since
u_{\varepsilon,\delta}
is smooth, we can apply Remark
6.5

to say that
\tilde{V}_{\varepsilon,\delta}
is a weighted average of its level sets.
By subadditivity of the first variation, we have

\displaystyle|\delta\tilde{V}_{\varepsilon,\delta}|(U)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{-1}^{1}\sqrt{2W(\lambda)}\cdot|\delta\llbracket\{u_{\varepsilon,\delta}=\lambda\}\rrbracket|(U)\,d\lambda

\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{1}\sqrt{2W(\lambda)}\int_{\{u_{\varepsilon,\delta}=\lambda\}\cap U}|H_{u_{\varepsilon,\delta}}|\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\,d\lambda

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{U}|\operatorname{II}_{u_{\varepsilon,\delta}}|\sqrt{2W(u_{\varepsilon,\delta})}|\nabla u_{\varepsilon,\delta}|,

where we denoted by
H_{u_{\varepsilon,\delta}}
the mean curvature of the level set and used again the coarea formula.
Since trivially

\int_{M}\varepsilon^{-1}W(u_{\varepsilon,\delta})\leq E_{\varepsilon,\delta}(u_{\varepsilon,\delta})\leq C,

from Theorem
6.4
and Cauchy–Schwarz we get

|\delta\tilde{V}_{\varepsilon,\delta}|(U)\leq C(U).

By lower semicontinuity of the first variation, we deduce that

|\delta\tilde{V}_{\varepsilon}|(U)\leq\liminf_{\delta\to 0}|\delta\tilde{V}_{\varepsilon,\delta}|(U)\leq C(U),

(6.8)

and thus

|\delta\tilde{V}_{0}|(U)\leq\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}|\delta\tilde{V}_{\varepsilon}|(U)\leq C(U).

The conclusion is standard: for every
\varepsilon,\delta
, let
S_{\varepsilon,\delta,r}
be the set of points
p

such that
u_{\varepsilon,\delta}
is unstable on
B_{r}(p)
. By Vitali’s covering lemma,
there exists a subcollection
S_{\varepsilon,\delta,r}^{\prime}
such that the balls
B_{r}(p)
with
p\in S_{\varepsilon,\delta,r}^{\prime}

are disjoint, giving in particular
\#S_{\varepsilon,\delta,r}^{\prime}\leq m
, and such that

u_{\varepsilon,\delta}
is stable on
B_{r}(q)
unless
q\in\bigcup_{p\in S_{\varepsilon,\delta,r}^{\prime}}B_{5r}(p)
.
The desired set is obtained by taking a limit of
S_{\varepsilon,\delta,r}^{\prime}
in the Hausdorff topology
as
\delta\to 0
, then as
\varepsilon\to 0
, and finally as
r\to 0
(along subsequences).
∎

We are now in a position to prove the following. In fact, later on we will
just use the consequence (
6.10
),
even if the rectifiability of
\tilde{V}_{0}
will simplify the argument. Later,
\tilde{V}_{0}
will be replaced by
a more appropriate varifold
V
, directly tied to the anisotropic first variation; the latter will be shown to be (
c_{W}
times) integer rectifiable on all of
M
.

Theorem 6.7
.

On
M\setminus\mathcal{S}
we have

c\mu\leq\|\tilde{V}_{0}\|\leq C\mu

and the density of
\tilde{V}_{0}
satisfies

\Theta^{n-1}(\|\tilde{V}_{0}\|,x):=\lim_{r\to 0}\frac{\|\tilde{V}_{0}\|(B_{r}(x))}{r^{n-1}}\in(0,\infty)

(6.9)

at
\|\tilde{V}_{0}\|
-almost every
x\in M\setminus\mathcal{S}
(or equivalently at
\mu
-a.e.
x\in M\setminus\mathcal{S}
),
and hence
\tilde{V}_{0}
is rectifiable on
M\setminus\mathcal{S}
.
As a consequence,
\mu
is a rectifiable measure on
M\setminus\mathcal{S}
and

0<\Theta^{n-1}_{*}(\mu,x)\leq\Theta^{n-1,*}(\mu,x)<\infty\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for $\mu$-a.e.}\ x\in M\setminus\mathcal{S},

(6.10)

where
\Theta^{n-1}_{*}(\mu,x):=\liminf_{r\to 0}\frac{\mu(B_{r}(x))}{r^{n-1}}

and
\Theta^{n-1,*}(\mu,x):=\limsup_{r\to 0}\frac{\mu(B_{r}(x))}{r^{n-1}}
.

Proof.

While the upper bound
\|\tilde{V}_{0}\|\leq C\mu
was already obtained (on all of
M
),
we claim that the lower bound
\|\tilde{V}_{0}\|\geq c\mu
on
M\setminus\mathcal{S}
follows from (
6.9
).
Indeed, given
p\not\in\mathcal{S}
, let us fix a ball
B_{2r}(p)\subseteq U
,
where
U
is the neighborhood given by the previous result.
First of all, by Theorem
5.1
, we have

\|\tilde{V}_{\varepsilon}\|(B_{2r}(p))\geq c\int_{B_{2r}(p)}\sqrt{2W(u_{\varepsilon})}F(\nabla u_{\varepsilon})\geq\int_{B_{2r}(p)}[c\varepsilon F(\nabla u)^{2}-C\varepsilon F(\nabla u)]\geq c\int_{B_{2r}(p)}\varepsilon F(\nabla u)^{2}-Cr^{n},

thanks to the uniform
C^{1}
bound
F(\nabla u_{\varepsilon})\leq C\varepsilon^{-1}
, which again follows from Theorem
5.1
.

By Lemma
6.3
, which applies since
u_{\varepsilon,\delta}
is stable on
U
by construction (for
\varepsilon
, and in turn
\delta
, small enough), we deduce that

\|\tilde{V}_{\varepsilon}\|(B_{2r}(p))\geq c\int_{B_{r}(p)}e_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})-Cr^{n},

and hence in the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
we get
\|\tilde{V}_{0}\|(\overline{B}_{2r}(p))\geq c\mu(B_{r}(p))-Cr^{n}
. Approximating
r
from below, we deduce

\|\tilde{V}_{0}\|(B_{2r}(p))\geq c\mu(B_{r}(p))-Cr^{n}.

Assuming that
\|\tilde{V}_{0}\|(B_{r}(p))\geq c(p)r^{n-1}

for any
r>0
small enough, we can then find
r>0
as small as we want and such that

\|\tilde{V}_{0}\|(B_{2r}(p))\geq c(p)r^{n-1},\hskip 10.00002pt\|\tilde{V}_{0}\|(B_{r}(p))\geq 4^{-n}\|\tilde{V}_{0}\|(B_{2r}(p)).

We then have
r^{n}=o(\|\tilde{V}_{0}\|(B_{r}(p)))
, and thus

\|\tilde{V}_{0}\|(B_{r}(p))\geq 4^{-n}\|\tilde{V}_{0}\|(B_{2r}(p))\geq c\mu(B_{r}(p)),

from which the bound
\|\tilde{V}_{0}\|\geq c\mu
follows, thanks to Besicovitch’s differentiation theorem.

To show the second part of the statement, it suffices to show that
\Theta^{n-1,*}(\|\tilde{V}_{0}\|,x)>0
for
\|\tilde{V}_{0}\|
-a.e.
x
:
indeed, we already proved in Corollary
6.6
that the varifold
\tilde{V}_{0}
has locally bounded (isotropic) first variation in
M\setminus\mathcal{S}
, so that here its rectifiability follows by Allard’s classical rectifiability criterion
[
3
, Section 5]
, which gives (
6.9
).
Finally, (
6.10
) is now clear on
M\setminus\mathcal{S}
.

To check that
\Theta^{n-1,*}(\|\tilde{V}_{0}\|,x)>0
for
\|\tilde{V}_{0}\|
-a.e.
x
, let us fix
U
as above, and fix
U^{\prime}\subset U
and
r_{0}>0
such that

B_{2r_{0}}(x)\subseteq U
for all
x\in U^{\prime}
.
Given
\gamma>0
, we let
\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon,\gamma}
denote the set of points
x\in U^{\prime}
such that

|u_{\varepsilon}(x)|\leq 1-2\gamma,\hskip 10.00002pt|\delta\tilde{V}_{\varepsilon}|(B_{r}(x))\leq\frac{\|\tilde{V}_{\varepsilon}\|(B_{r}(x))}{\gamma}

(6.11)

for all
r\in(0,r_{0})
. Since
|\delta\tilde{V}_{\varepsilon}|(U)\leq C(U)
by (
6.8
), a simple application of Besicovitch’s covering lemma gives

\|\tilde{V}_{\varepsilon}\|(U^{\prime}\setminus\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon,\gamma})\leq C(U)\gamma+\int_{U^{\prime}\cap\{|u_{\varepsilon}|>1-2\gamma\}}\sqrt{2W(u_{\varepsilon})}|\nabla u_{\varepsilon}|.

We claim that the last term vanishes in the limit
\varepsilon,\gamma\to 0
. Indeed,
testing the Euler–Lagrange equation (
3.4
) with
\lambda-u_{\varepsilon}
we have

\int_{M}\varepsilon^{-1}W^{\prime}(u_{\varepsilon})(\lambda-u_{\varepsilon})\leq C\int_{M}\varepsilon|\nabla u_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\leq CE_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})\leq C;

choosing
\lambda\in(-1,1)
to be the maximum point of
W|_{[-1,1]}

(recall that
\sqrt{W}
is assumed to be strictly concave on
[-1,1]
),
we have
(\lambda-s)W^{\prime}(s)\geq 0
for all
s\in[-1,1]
, and actually

(\lambda-s)W^{\prime}(s)\geq-c\operatorname{sgn}(s)W^{\prime}(s)\geq c\sqrt{W(s)}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for }|s|\in[1-2\gamma,1]

for
\gamma>0
small enough, as
W^{\prime\prime}(\pm 1)>0
. We infer that

\int_{\{|u_{\varepsilon}|>1-2\gamma\}}\varepsilon^{-1}\sqrt{W(u_{\varepsilon})}\leq C,

and thus, since
\sqrt{W(s)}\leq C(1-|s|)
on
[-1,1]
(again as
W^{\prime\prime}(\pm 1)>0
),

\int_{\{|u_{\varepsilon}|>1-2\gamma\}}\varepsilon^{-1}{W(u_{\varepsilon})}\leq C\gamma.

(6.12)

By Theorem
5.1
and the last two bounds, we have shown that

\int_{\{|u_{\varepsilon}|>1-2\gamma\}}\sqrt{2W(u_{\varepsilon})}|\nabla u_{\varepsilon}|\leq C\int_{\{|u_{\varepsilon}|>1-2\gamma\}}[\varepsilon^{-1}W(u_{\varepsilon})+\sqrt{W(u_{\varepsilon})}]\leq C\gamma+C\varepsilon.

In summary, we deduce that there exists a small
\gamma_{0}>0
such that

\|\tilde{V}_{\varepsilon}\|(U^{\prime}\setminus\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon,\gamma})\leq C(U)(\gamma+\varepsilon)

for all
\gamma\in(0,\gamma_{0})
and
\varepsilon>0
small.

Up to a subsequence, let
\mathcal{G}_{0,\gamma}

denote the Hausdorff limit of the closures

\overline{\mathcal{G}}_{\varepsilon,\gamma}
, along a subsequence
\varepsilon\to 0
depending on
\gamma\in(0,\gamma_{0})
. We claim that

\Theta^{n-1}(\|\tilde{V}_{0}\|,x)>0

at each
x\in\mathcal{G}_{0,\gamma}
.
Indeed, thanks to (
6.11
), we can apply
[
53
, Theorem 17.6]
to the varifold
\tilde{V}_{\varepsilon}

with
\alpha=1
; note that here we use geodesic balls rather than Euclidean ones, but the proof still carries through. Hence we obtain

\frac{\|\tilde{V}_{\varepsilon}\|(B_{r}(x))}{r^{n-1}}\geq c(\gamma)\frac{\|\tilde{V}_{\varepsilon}\|(B_{\rho\varepsilon}(x))}{(\rho\varepsilon)^{n-1}}\geq c(\gamma)>0

for any
x\in\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon,\gamma}
and
r\in(\rho\varepsilon,r_{0})
,
thanks to Lemma
6.2
, which applies as
B_{\rho\varepsilon}(x)\subseteq U
and
|u_{\varepsilon,\delta}(x)|\leq 1-\gamma
for
\delta
small enough, by (
6.11
).
Thus, we also have

\frac{\|\tilde{V}_{0}\|(\overline{B}_{r}(x))}{r^{n-1}}\geq c(\gamma)>0

for any
x\in\mathcal{G}_{0,\gamma}
. Finally, we have

\|\tilde{V}_{0}\|(U^{\prime}\setminus\mathcal{G}_{0,\gamma})\leq\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\|\tilde{V}_{\varepsilon}\|(U^{\prime}\setminus\overline{\mathcal{G}}_{\varepsilon,\gamma})\leq C(U)\gamma.

Since
\|\tilde{V}_{0}\|
has positive density
at all points in
\bigcup_{\gamma\in(0,\gamma_{0})}\mathcal{G}_{0,\gamma}
and we can cover
M\setminus\mathcal{S}

with countably many such
U^{\prime}
, the statement follows. Note that the subsequence
\varepsilon\to 0
defining
\mathcal{G}_{0,\gamma}
depends on
\gamma
,
but this is irrelevant.
∎

7
Stress-energy tensor and integrality of the limit varifold

Given
u_{\varepsilon}:M\to\mathbb{R}
critical for
E_{\varepsilon}
, a straightforward computation using inner variations
shows that, for any vector field
X\in C^{1}(M,TM)
, denoting by
DX
the
(1,1)
-tensor given by the Levi-Civita
connection, we have

\int_{M}\langle T_{\varepsilon},DX\rangle=\int_{M}O(\varepsilon|\nabla u|^{2}|X|),

(7.1)

where
T_{\varepsilon}
is the stress-energy tensor, namely the
(1,1)
-tensor given by

T_{\varepsilon}:=e_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})I-\varepsilon\nabla u_{\varepsilon}\otimes D(F^{2}/2)(\nabla u_{\varepsilon}),

(7.2)

with
D(F^{2}/2)(\nabla u_{\varepsilon})\in T_{x}^{*}M
denoting the differential of
F_{x}^{2}/2=(F^{2}/2)|_{T_{x}M}

(viewed as a function
T_{x}M\to\mathbb{R}
) at
\nabla u(x)
, for any given
x\in M
; the error term comes from the fact that
F_{x}^{2}
depends on
x
.

In the sequel, it is useful to consider the map

C_{F}(\nu):=I-\frac{\nu\otimes DF(\nu)}{F(\nu)},

where
\nu\in TM
is a unit vector.
We observe that

T_{\varepsilon}=[e_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})-\varepsilon F(\nabla u_{\varepsilon})^{2}]I+\varepsilon F(\nabla u_{\varepsilon})^{2}C_{F}(\nu_{\varepsilon}),\hskip 10.00002pt\nu_{\varepsilon}:=\frac{\nabla u_{\varepsilon}}{|\nabla u_{\varepsilon}|}\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{on }\{\nabla u_{\varepsilon}\neq 0\},

while we let
\nu_{\varepsilon}:=0
and
C_{F}(\nu_{\varepsilon}):=0
on
\{\nabla u_{\varepsilon}=0\}
.
We have the trivial bound

|T_{\varepsilon}|\leq Ce_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon}).

Assuming (
6.7
), let
T_{0}
be a limit of the measures
T_{\varepsilon}\,d\operatorname{vol}_{g}
, up to a subsequence.

Proposition 7.1
.

The limit
T_{0}
has the form

dT_{0}=((1-\lambda)I+\lambda A)\,d\mu+d\xi,

where
\lambda:M\to[0,1]
and the measure
\xi
satisfies
|\xi|\leq C\operatorname{vol}_{g}
,
while for
\mu
-a.e.
x
the tensor
A(x)
belongs to the (compact) convex hull

\mathcal{C}_{x}:=\operatorname{co}(\{C_{F}(\nu)\,:\,\nu\in T_{x}M,\ |\nu|=1\}).

Proof.

Indeed, we have

T_{\varepsilon}=[(1-\lambda_{\varepsilon})I+\lambda_{\varepsilon}C_{F}(\nu_{\varepsilon})]e_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})+\xi_{\varepsilon},

where

\lambda_{\varepsilon}:=\min\left\{\frac{\varepsilon F(\nabla u_{\varepsilon})^{2}}{e_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})},1\right\}\in[0,1]

(7.3)

and defined to be zero on
\{e_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})=0\}
, and

\xi_{\varepsilon}=-(\varepsilon F(\nabla u_{\varepsilon})^{2}-e_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon}))^{+}I+(\varepsilon F(\nabla u_{\varepsilon})^{2}-e_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon}))^{+}C_{F}(\nu_{\varepsilon}).

Crucially, by the generalization of Modica’s bound, namely Theorem
5.1
,
we have
\varepsilon F(\nabla u_{\varepsilon})^{2}-e_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})\leq C
pointwise, and thus

|\xi_{\varepsilon}|\leq C.

Since
(1-\lambda_{\varepsilon})I+\lambda_{\varepsilon}C_{F}(\nu_{\varepsilon})\in\operatorname{co}(\{I\}\cup\mathcal{C}_{x})

at any given
x\in M
, it follows that

dT_{0}=B\,d\mu+d\xi,

where
\xi
is the limit of
\xi_{\varepsilon}\,d\operatorname{vol}_{g}
and

B(x)\in\operatorname{co}(\{I\}\cup\mathcal{C}_{x})
a.e., as desired.
∎

Theorem 7.2
.

Assuming that
u_{\varepsilon}=\lim_{\delta\to 0}u_{\varepsilon,\delta}
is a limit of critical points for
E_{\varepsilon,\delta}

with Morse index
\leq m
, then the measures
T_{0}
and
\mu
are rectifiable. In fact, we have

\lambda=0,\hskip 10.00002ptA(x)=C_{F}(\nu_{x})

for a suitable unit vector
\nu_{x}\in T_{x}M
, at
\mu
-a.e.
x\in M
.
Furthermore, writing
d\mu=\theta\,d(\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\mathop{\hbox{\vrule height=7.0pt,width=0.3pt,depth=0.0pt\vrule height=0.3pt,width=5.0pt,depth=0.0pt}}\nolimits\Sigma)
for a suitable rectifiable Borel set
\Sigma\subset M
with
\sigma
-finite
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
measure and
\theta:\Sigma\to(0,\infty)
, the
(n-1)
-dimensional varifold

dV(x,\nu^{\perp}):=\frac{\theta(x)}{F(\nu_{x})}\delta_{\nu_{x}}(\nu)\otimes d(\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\mathop{\hbox{\vrule height=7.0pt,width=0.3pt,depth=0.0pt\vrule height=0.3pt,width=5.0pt,depth=0.0pt}}\nolimits\Sigma)(x)

is rectifiable and
F
-stationary, and
\nu_{x}
is
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
-a.e. the unit normal to
\Sigma

(unique up to sign).

It follows from the formula for
V
that its anisotropic energy is

\mathcal{F}(V)=\mu(M),\hskip 10.00002pt\mathcal{F}(V;U)=\mu(U)\text{ for all }U\subseteq M\text{ Borel}.

Proof.

We observe that
\mu
-a.e.
x\in M
is an approximate continuity point of
\lambda
and
A
,
and moreover by Theorem
6.7
satisfies

0<\Theta^{n-1}_{*}(\mu,x)\leq\Theta^{n-1,*}(\mu,x)<\infty,

(7.4)

provided that
x\not\in\mathcal{S}
.
In this case, thanks to Proposition
7.1
, any blow-up
\tilde{T}_{0}
of
T_{0}
at any such point
x_{0}\in M
will be of the form

d\tilde{T}_{0}=B\,d\tilde{\mu},

where

B=(1-\lambda_{0})I+\lambda_{0}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}C_{F_{x_{0}}}(\nu)\,d\alpha(\nu)

(7.5)

is a constant matrix given by a suitable
\lambda_{0}\in[0,1]
and a probability measure
\alpha
on

, and

is a blow-up of

at

.
Indeed,

Note that a blow-up defined on

.

As guaranteed by (

), the measure

cannot be a constant multiple of the Lebesgue measure
on

, as

. On the other hand, (

|\langle T_{0},DX\rangle|\leq C\int_{M}|X|\,d\mu;

given

, extended to zero outside the domain of
\exp_{x_{0}}^{-1}
, for a fixed orthonormal frame
\{e_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}

defined near
x_{0}
inducing the chosen identification

. Letting

\langle\tilde{T}_{0},DY\rangle=0\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all }Y\in C^{1}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{n}).

Taking

of the form

, we then see that
\tilde{\mu}
is invariant along
B^{\top}v
.
Since
\tilde{\mu}
is not a multiple of the Lebesgue measure, we deduce that

\operatorname{ker}(B)\neq\{0\}.

Let then
\nu_{0}\in\operatorname{ker}(B)
be a unit vector.
Recalling (
7.5
), we see that

0=\langle DF_{x_{0}}(\nu_{0}),B\nu_{0}\rangle=(1-\lambda_{0})F_{x_{0}}(\nu_{0})+\lambda_{0}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\langle DF_{x_{0}}(\nu_{0}),C_{F_{x_{0}}}(\nu)\nu_{0}\rangle\,d\alpha(\nu).

(7.6)

By
1
-homogeneity and convexity of
F_{x_{0}}
, we have that

DF_{x_{0}}(\nu)[\nu]=F(\nu),\hskip 10.00002ptDF_{x_{0}}(\nu)[\nu^{\prime}]=F_{x_{0}}(\nu)+DF_{x_{0}}(\nu)[\nu^{\prime}-\nu]\leq F_{x_{0}}(\nu^{\prime})

and, since

is even, it also holds that
|DF_{x_{0}}(\nu)[\nu_{0}]|\leq F_{x_{0}}(\nu_{0})
and

. Hence, we obtain that

\langle DF_{x_{0}}(\nu_{0}),C_{F_{x_{0}}}(\nu)\nu_{0}\rangle=F_{x_{0}}(\nu_{0})-\frac{DF_{x_{0}}(\nu)[\nu_{0}]}{F(\nu)}DF_{x_{0}}(\nu_{0})[\nu]\geq 0.

(7.7)

Plugging (
7.7

\lambda_{0}=1
. Moreover, since

is strictly convex along non-radial directions, (
7.7
) can be an equality only

.

Thus,
\alpha
is concentrated on
\{\pm\nu_{0}\}
so that, using again that

is even, we obtain

For the same reason,

We cannot have

and the conclusion follows from the rectifiability of

since for generic

\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\mathop{\hbox{\vrule height=7.0pt,width=0.3pt,depth=0.0pt\vrule height=0.3pt,width=5.0pt,depth=0.0pt}}\nolimits\nu_{\Sigma}(x_{0})^{\perp}

The

is equivalent to the fact that

∎

Lemma 7.7

There exist Borel sets

where the error term comes from the ambient metric

We have the following simple result in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, for autonomous

.

Lemma 7.8
.

Given
\Lambda>0
and
\gamma\in(0,1)
, there exists
\delta(\Lambda,\gamma,F)>0
such that
either

|u|\geq 1-C\gamma\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on }\{0\}\times(-R,R)

or the energy on the slice

\frac{1}{F(e_{n})}\int_{\{0\}\times(-R,R)}e_{1}(u)\in\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}}(kc_{W}-Ck\gamma^{2},kc_{W}+Ck\gamma^{2})

(7.13)

for any critical point

u:B_{1}^{n-1}(0)\times(-R,R)\to\mathbb{R}

of the energy
E_{1}
,
provided that
R\in(0,\Lambda]
,
|u|\leq 1
,
F(\nabla u)^{2}\leq W(u)+\gamma^{2}
,

\lim_{t\to\pm R}|u(0,t)|\in[1-\gamma,1],

and

\int_{B_{1}^{n-1}(0)\times(-R,R)}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}|\partial_{j}u|^{2}\leq\delta.

In other words, if
u
is almost constant along the first
n-1
coordinate
directions, then its energy is close to (
F(e_{n})
times) a multiple of the energy
c_{W}=\int_{-1}^{1}\sqrt{2W}
of the heteroclinical solution, unless
u
is essentially constant. In the statement,
C
is a universal constant depending just on
W
, on which
the other constants tacitly depend as well.

Proof.

Assuming for every
\delta>0
we have a counterexample

u^{\delta}:B_{1}^{n-1}(0)\times(-R_{\delta},R_{\delta})\to\mathbb{R}
,
in the limit
\delta\to 0
(up to a subsequence) we get a one-dimensional function

u(x)=U(x^{n})
critical for
E_{1}
, defined on
B_{1}^{n-1}(0)\times(-R,R)
for a limiting
R=\lim_{\delta\to 0}R_{\delta}\in[0,\Lambda]
;
we can assume that
R>0
since otherwise the first conclusion for
u^{\delta}
trivially holds, thanks to the Lipschitz bound on
u^{\delta}
(implied by the assumption
F(\nabla u^{\delta})^{2}\leq W(u^{\delta})+\gamma^{2}
).

Note that we can pass to the limit the Euler–Lagrange equation (
3.4
) and also

\lim_{\delta\to 0}\int_{\{0\}\times(-R_{\delta},R_{\delta})}e_{1}(u^{\delta})=\int_{\{0\}\times(-R,R)}e_{1}(u),

thanks to the same Lipschitz bound and Proposition
3.3
, which upgrades it to convergence in
C^{1,\alpha}_{loc}
.
Since
u
is one-dimensional, it is smooth and satisfies

U:(-R,R)\to\mathbb{R},\hskip 10.00002ptF^{2}(e_{n})U^{\prime\prime}=W^{\prime}(U).

Thus,
\xi:=F^{2}(e_{n})(U^{\prime})^{2}/2-W(U)
is constant; actually, it is bounded by
C\gamma^{2}
in absolute value:
this follows from the uniform convergence
u^{\delta}\to u
and from equicontinuity, which give

\lim_{t\to\pm R}|u(0,t)|=\lim_{\delta\to 0}\lim_{t\to\pm R_{\delta}}|u^{\delta}(0,t)|\in[1-\gamma,1]

and thus
\lim_{t\to\pm R}W(u)(0,t)\leq C\gamma^{2}
, as well as the fact that

F(\nabla u)^{2}\leq W(u)+\gamma^{2}
.

The solution
U
can be extended to all of
\mathbb{R}
and, by classical theory of Newtonian systems
(viewing
-W(U)
as the potential energy), its extension is periodic if
\xi<0
, constant or heteroclinic if
\xi=0
(i.e., either
U\equiv\pm 1
or it is monotone, with limits
\pm 1
at infinity),
or divergent if
\xi>0
(i.e., goes to
\pm\infty
at infinity, since
U^{\prime}=\pm\frac{\sqrt{2W(U)+2\xi}}{F(e_{n})}
with constant sign; recall that the right-hand side is
\simeq\pm|U|
for
|U|\geq 2
).
Here we are assuming that
\gamma
is small enough, so that the steady state in
(-1,1)
,
corresponding to the maximum point for
W|_{(-1,1)}
, is not one of the possibilities.

In the second and third cases,
U
is monotone;
applying the elementary inequality
0\leq a+b-2\sqrt{ab}\leq|a-b|
with
a:=F^{2}(e_{n})(U^{\prime})^{2}/2
and
b:=W(U)
, we see that

\int_{\{0\}\times(-R,R)}e_{1}(u)=\int_{-R}^{R}F(e_{n})\sqrt{2W(U)}|U^{\prime}|+O(\gamma^{2}),

(7.14)

from which the claim follows: either
|U|\geq 1-\gamma
or (
7.13
) holds with
k=1
, thanks to a simple change of variables and the boundary condition

|U(\pm R)|\in[1-\gamma,1]
.

If instead we are in the first case where
\xi<0
, then we can divide
[-R,R]
into

N\geq 1
consecutive intervals
[t_{j},t_{j+1}]
where
U
is monotone, for
j=0,\dots,N-1
,
such that
t_{0}=-R
,
t_{N}=R
, and

U(t_{j})\in W^{-1}(-\xi)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all }j=1,\dots,N-1

(as
U^{\prime}(t_{j})=0
and hence
\xi=-W(U(t_{j}))
for these

).
Using again (
7.14
) and writing

\int_{-R}^{R}F(e_{n})\sqrt{2W(U)}|U^{\prime}|=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\int_{t_{j}}^{t_{j+1}}F(e_{n})\sqrt{2W(U)}|U^{\prime}|,

the claim follows again from the monotonicity of
U
on each interval,
together with the fact that
U(t_{j})\in W^{-1}(-\xi)
forces
|U(t_{j})|\in[1-C\gamma,1]
,
as

and

is nearly quadratic around
\pm 1
.
Thus, the claim must also hold for

eventually, a contradiction.

) to each of the cylinders

B_{\varepsilon}^{n-1}(p_{\varepsilon})\times I_{j},\hskip 10.00002ptj\in\{1,\dots,N-1\},

dilated by a factor
\varepsilon^{-1}
, so that the dilated function becomes critical for
E_{1}
.
The assumptions of this lemma hold eventually, thanks to the bound
\varepsilon^{-1}|I_{j}|\leq\Lambda
, the bound (
7.10
), the fact that

|u_{\varepsilon}(p_{\varepsilon},t)|\in[1-\gamma,1]\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for }t\in\partial I_{j},

and (
7.16
).

We deduce that either
|u_{\varepsilon}(p_{\varepsilon},t)|\in[1-C_{0}\gamma,1]
for all
t\in I_{j}
or

\frac{1}{F_{x_{0}}(e_{n})}\int_{I_{j}}e_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})(p_{\varepsilon},t)\in\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}}(kc_{W}-Ck\gamma^{2},kc_{W}+Ck\gamma^{2}).

In particular, in the second case we have
\frac{1}{F_{x_{0}}(e_{n})}\int_{I_{j}}e_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})(p_{\varepsilon},t)\geq c_{W}/2

(provided
\gamma
is small enough). Since the energy on the full slice
\{p_{\varepsilon}\}\times[-1,1]

is at most
2\theta
, we deduce that there is an upper bound
C
for the number of such intervals
of the second kind,
independent of
\gamma
.

Recalling (
7.17
), we arrive at

\frac{1}{F_{x_{0}}(e_{n})}\int_{-1}^{1}e_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})(p_{\varepsilon},t)\,dt=\frac{1}{F_{x_{0}}(e_{n})}\int_{[-1,1]\setminus(I_{0}\cup I_{N})}e_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})(p_{\varepsilon},t)\,dt=n_{\varepsilon}c_{W}+O(\gamma),

for some
n_{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{N}
, with an implied constant independent of
\varepsilon,\gamma
.
Since

\frac{\theta}{F_{x_{0}}(e_{n})}=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{F_{x_{0}}(e_{n})}\int_{-1}^{1}e_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})(p_{\varepsilon},t)\,dt=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}n_{\varepsilon}c_{W}+O(\gamma),

we obtain that the distance of
\frac{\theta}{F_{x_{0}}(e_{n})}
from
c_{W}\mathbb{N}
is bounded by
O(\gamma)
.
Since
\gamma
was arbitrary, we reach the conclusion.
