Kidney anatomy: A Survey of Historical Illustrations and Methods of Depiction
Authors: Melissa D. Clarkson, Kristen M. Platt
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Kidneys produce and transport urine using an extensive system of blood vessels and a series of tubular structures. Many of these anatomical structures are visible during dissection with the unaided eye or low-power magnification. In this paper we survey the variety of ways gross anatomy of the human kidney has been illustrated in past centuries. These examples demonstrate alternatives to depicting a frontal section of the kidney, a view that is commonly used in contemporary illustrations but provides limited information about the morphology and organization of internal kidney anatomy.

Introduction

Kidneys have complex internal anatomy, presenting challenges for effective visual representation. Because they are the site of urine production and receive approximately 20% of blood flow from the heart (Eaton & Pooler 2023), kidneys are relevant to both the urinary and circulatory systems. Depictions of gross anatomy of kidneys appear in the earliest atlases of human anatomy, but detailed knowledge concerning the structure and function of kidneys required the invention of the microscope and introduction of injection studies in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries (Purkerson & Wechsler 1997; Eknoyan & De Santo 2012).

The majority of contemporary illustrations displaying internal gross anatomy of the kidney depict a frontal (coronal) section (demonstrated in Figure 1A), but this view does not effectively communicate the three-dimensional arrangement of anatomical structures. Historically, depictions of the kidney have employed a variety of methods to communicate the morphology of structures. The purpose of this work is to document the variety of ways in which gross anatomy of the human kidney has been illustrated in the past, with the goal of demonstrating how different views and partitions are effective at communicating different aspects of kidney anatomy.

Anatomical structures of kidneys

Kidneys are bilateral organs, commonly described as bean-shaped. They are positioned in the retroperitoneal space on either side of the vertebral column. To accommodate the liver, the right kidney is typically at a slightly inferior position in the body as compared with the left kidney. An adrenal gland lies on the superior pole of each kidney, a fibrous renal capsule intimately covers each kidney, and the kidneys are densely encased by perirenal adipose tissue.

The renal hilum is a fissure on the medial surface of a kidney that provides an opening to a hallow called the sinus of the kidney. The renal artery, renal vein, and ureter pass through the hilum to the sinus (Figure 1B, left). The renal parenchyma is the functional tissue of the kidney, consisting of approximately a million microscopic nephrons (Bertram 2011). At each nephron, waste and excess salt and fluid are removed from the blood by hydrostatic pressure-driven filtration across a rounded bundle of capillaries, known as a glomerulus, into the proximal part of a nephron, called Bowmanâ€™s capsule. This filtrate moves through a series of looped tubules and into a tree-shaped collecting duct. The collecting ducts, nephron loops and vasa recta aggregate in dense bundles that allow the renal pyramids to be visually distinct in gross dissection. This visible segregation of the parenchyma is known as the medulla (tissue of the pyramids) and cortex (tissue surrounding the pyramids). See Figure 1, parts A and B.

The collecting ducts merge into a small number of openings at the apex of the pyramid, known as the renal papilla (Figure 1D, right). Urine is released from these openings into the lumen of the pelvicalyceal system that transports it to the ureter and then to the urinary bladder. The pelvicalyceal system consists of minor calyces, major calyces, and the renal pelvis. Each minor calyx forms a funnel around a renal papilla (Figure 1D, left). Minor calyces join to form a major calyx, and major calyces join to form the renal pelvis, which is continuous with the ureter (Figure 1C).

A kidney typically has eight renal pyramids, although those at the superior and inferior poles tend to have two or three lobes that fuse at a single renal papilla and may be considered to be multiple pyramids (increasing the number count of pyramids to approximately fourteen) (Brödel 1901, Fine & Keen 1976). The renal pelvis shows substantial morphological variation, with differences in width (from wide to narrow), variable location relative to the hilum (completely internal to the sinus or spanning the hilum), and shape (a single pelvis or divided pelvis with separate branches for the upper and middle calyces) (Sampaio & Mandarim-De-Lacerda 1998, Takazawa 2018).

The left and right renal arteries branch from the abdominal aorta to supply the left and right kidneys. These arteries provide oxygen to the organ and the supply of blood to be filtered. Each renal artery divides into four or five branches at the hilum, passing anteriorly and posteriorly across the sinus. The intrarenal arterial trees send off fine branches in the region of the pyramids. The intrarenal veins pass around the pyramids, progressively merging to form the renal vein that returns blood to the inferior vena cava (Brödel 1901, Graves 1986). Variation is common in branching of the intrarenal vessels. In addition, branches of vessels occasionally pass through the parenchyma near the hilum or at the poles, rather than through the hilum.

Methodology

Identification and preparation of historic illustrations

Two methods were used to identify publicly-available historical illustrations of kidney anatomy. The first was searching within anatomy books from online repositories. These repositories included the Biodiversity Heritage Library (https://www.biodiversitylibrary.org), National Library of Medicine Digital Collections (https://collections.nlm.nih.gov), Gallica Digital Library (https://www.bnf.fr/en/gallica-bnf-digital-library), Thomas Fisher Rare Book Library Digital Collections of the University of Toronto (https://collections.library.utoronto.ca), The Wellcome Collection (https://wellcomecollection.org), and HathiTrust (https://www.hathitrust.org). The second method was to perform searches using the Google web browser (https://www.google.com) and identify relevant illustrations from the search results. Because images were often used without citing the original source, Google Images (https://images.google.com) was used to assist with identification of sources.

The organizations providing online access to the books and articles that were sources of these illustrations believe them to be in the public domain. The illustrations shown in this paper were published between 1698 and 1919.

Documents were downloaded and illustrations cropped from pages using Adobe Photoshop. If illustrations were originally produced in black and white or using only gray tones, they were converted to grayscale files. Illustrations were adjusted for brightness, contrast, and color in an effort to lessen effects of discoloration of the paper, aging, and loss of contrast when scanning. Areas around illustrations were converted to white.

We limited this investigation to depictions of gross anatomy of isolated kidneys. Neither illustrations of kidneys within the context of the body nor microanatomy (nephrons) are included here. We also excluded representations of pathological kidneys. When we identified the same illustration in multiple publications, the image and source presented here is the earliest instance (except for a limited number of cases of poor image quality of the scan of the oldest available version).

Results

Our survey documented how gross anatomy of the kidney has been represented as surface views, different types of partitions through the organ, and isolated structures.

Representations of surface anatomy

Examples of illustrations of surface anatomy are shown in Figure 2. Some depictions include the adrenal gland residing on the superior pole of the kidney; vasculature serving the adrenal gland tends to be omitted even though vasculature for the kidney is included. Across all representations, truncated ureter and blood vessels are typically shown emerging from the hilum of the kidney.

The appearance of the anterior and posterior sides of a kidney are similar. The hilum may be offset slightly in either an anterior or posterior direction. One role of illustrations is to help the viewer distinguish the anterior and poster surfaces by clarifying the relative positions of the vasculature and the ureter (with the ureter residing posteriorly). Illustrators have communicated this distinguishing feature using multiple views of the kidney, showing an unobscured ureter and renal pelvis in the posterior view.

Fetal kidneys develop with distinct lobes, and these lobes lose their distinctiveness in the adult to a variable degree. Illustrations ranged from no indication of lobes (example C of Figure 2) to traces of at least nine lobes (example D of Figure 2).

Two types of tissue are generally omitted from illustrations for clarity. Adipose fills the space of the sinus and hilum and extends into a dense layer of perirenal fat that cushions the kidneys. This tissue must be removed during dissection and is rarely illustrated. One of the few examples of adipose depicted in the area of the hilum is shown in example D of Figure 2. A layer of tough fibrous tissue, known as the renal capsule, adheres to the surface of the kidney, and examples F and G of Figure 2 demonstrate this layer by showing a portion of it cut away to reveal the underlying parenchyma.

A key challenge in studying gross anatomy of the kidney is understanding the relationship between internal structures and external morphology. Max Brödel developed sets of illustrations showing multiple views of the kidney with partially transparent surfaces for the purpose of demonstrating arrangement of the calyces and pyramids (examples in Figure 3). These illustrations were developed as part of anatomical documentation to guide surgeons in making an incision in the parenchyma to open the renal pelvis while avoiding major arteries (Brödel 1901).

Representation of partitions

The function of kidneys in collecting and draining urine has its basis in the morphology of renal papillae surrounded by minor calyces and the calyces uniting to form the renal pelvis. Frontal sections are typically used to reveal these structures (Figure 4, A-M). Because the calyces form a tree branching in three dimensions, rather than a flat structure than could be easily exposed by sectioning on a plane, use of a frontal section is not an ideal solution. Illustrators have addressed this by artificially arranging the minor calyces in a plane (A and B of Figure 4), depicting apex of papilla emerging from the wall of the pelvicalyceal system (such as E of Figure 4), and drawing much of the pelvicalyceal system intact, with only regions near the papilla excised (J, K, L of Figure 4). We also found four examples of isolated renal papilla, depicted either with or without a portion of the minor calyx (N-Q of Figure 4).

We identified three types of sections that are alternatives to frontal sections. Sagittal sections (dividing into left and right) are well-suited to clarifying the arrangement of pyramids and displaying structures at the hilum (A-B of Figure 5). Transverse sections (dividing into superior and inferior), particularly through the pelvis, explain the relative arrangement of the pelvis, vasculature, and pyramids (C-H of Figure 5). Two examples show one pole intact, presumably to bring context to the remaining structures (I-J of Figure 5). These alternate views demonstrate anatomical features not evident in frontal sections. This is a relatively avascular region between the anterior and posterior branches of the renal artery that is used as a site of surgical access to the pelvicalyceal system.

Additional approaches to representation

Kidneys show great morphological variation. Examples depicting variation are shown in Figure 9. A typical approach is to place alternative morphologies next to each other for comparison (A-B of Figure 9). But to show common entry points of accessory arteries, variants have been combined into composite drawings of anterior and posterior views (C of Figure 9).

Illustrations depicting variation. (A) Variation in shape of kidney, posterior views (Cruveilhier & Sée 1877, fig 194, p 316, vol 2). (B) Variation in morphology of pelvicalyceal system, drawn from casts and labeled with capacity of urine (Kelly & Burnam 1914, fig 250, p 521). (C) Composite illustration showing common points of entry of accessory arteries. Right kidney, shown as anterior and posterior views (Kelly & Burnam 1914, fig 204, p 428).

Anatomical books with papers flaps designed to reveal layers of anatomy were first introduced in the sixteenth century (On 2025). An example of a kidney depicted in this manner is shown in Figure 10. The unopened flap shows the anterior surface of a kidney and adrenal gland, with a portion of the renal capsule removed. When opened, the back surface of the flap displays vasculature and an array of pyramids, while the surface of the printed page shows the pyramids with the pelvicalyceal system.

Discussion

The goal of this work is to help medical illustrators consider how alternative views and partitions of anatomical structures communicate different types of information. We have demonstrated the wide variety in approaches to illustrating kidney anatomy in historical work and described how these different views and partitions demonstrate different components of internal structure and their relations. This survey should help illustrators to consider whether contemporary depictions of anatomy, which tend to be more homogeneous in their depictions, are chosen because they are the best form for communication or because they are the most familiar.

The source publications of these examples describe some illustrations as depictions of individual specimens (including example B of Figure 8 and A and B of Figure 9, which are based on casts). Others are highly schematic or fanciful depictions (A of Figure 4, I of Figure 5). Authors occasionally described the methods underlying their representations, such as the explanation by Dr. Johannes Sobotta that "Photography has also been made the basis for all the original drawings and has been uniformly utilized for the general lines of the illustrations." (Sobotta 1914, preface) But for most work, it is unclear whether the illustration is based on a single specimen or is drawn as a synthesis intended to demonstrate typical anatomy.

These illustrations were produced for a variety of purposes - from documenting new scientific knowledge to serving as simplified representations for books written for general audiences. A passage from the English translation of the introduction to Traité complet de l'anatomie de l'homme, written by Jean Marc Bourgery in 1830, speaks to the purposes of medical illustrations to document new findings and to serve both students of anatomy and medical practitioners (Bourgery 2017):... for a work of this kind to satisfy all its potential uses, not only has the science to be presented in its most advanced state, it also has to appear with all its applications. Therefore we must not slavishly copy a previous work, as none exists to which new facts could not be added; but, above all, it is essential for the plates of such a work, created with new intentions, to be drawn from nature, whilst using as guides renowned figures amongst those that have been published to date.During this project we discovered both reuse of illustrations and plagiarism of illustrations. Examples of illustrations that are clearly plagiarized are shown in Figure 11. The original illustrations tended to be highly detailed and reproduced using the best technology of the time (such as lithography for example, A and D of Figure 11), while the plagiarized versions were copied with less detail or reproduced using inferior methods. These historical examples may be of interest in contemporary conversations about plagiarism and adaption of medical illustrations (Cornwall 2025).
