Impact of the 2023-armed conflict on routine malaria imapact indicators in Sudan (January 2020–March 2025): a mixed-methods analysis of surveillance data
Authors: Khlood Fathi Alnaeem, Unnati Rani Saha, Hewida Fathalrhman Ahmed, Christina Mergenthaler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMalaria constitutes a major public health burden in Sudan, accounting for most outpatient visits and hospital admissions across approximately 80% of the states. The armed conflict beginning in April 2023 severely disrupted an already fragile health system, affecting the surveillance system infrastructure. No prior studies have assessed the impact of conflict on routine malaria surveillance data reported through District Health Information Software 2 (DHIS2). This study evaluated the effects of conflict on completeness and reporting of malaria impact indicators data across Sudanese states.MethodsA mixed-methods design combined quantitative analysis of quarterly DHIS2 data (January 2020–March 2025) from 17 states with qualitative exploration of surveillance system functionality. Quantitative analysis included descriptive analyses and interrupted time series analysis (ITSA) of three malaria impact indicators: quarterly reported malaria cases (presumed and confirmed) per 100,000 state population, test positivity rate (RDT + microscopy), and quarterly inpatient malaria deaths per 100,000 state population. Data completeness was quantified as the proportion of missing quarterly reports per state.Descriptive analysis graphs illustrate pre- and post-conflict trends and missing data patterns. ITSA was conducted for 11 states with complete post-conflict time series; six states with incomplete data were excluded. Three key informant interviews with national- and state-level malaria programme managers, selected from severe and less severe conflict-affected states, provided contextual insights. Qualitative data were analyzed using a deductive framework approach.ResultsMissing DHIS2 reporting increased substantially after April 2023. Inter-state variation was observed: western and southern states (except North Kordofan) experienced persistent data gaps, whereas northern and eastern states maintained relatively continuous reporting despite declining trends. Qualitative findings indicated stronger surveillance functionality in less-affected states by conflict. ITSA showed a statistically significant decline in quarterly reported malaria case rates per 100,000 state population at conflict quarter (p = 0.02), with no significant post-conflict trend change. Key informants identified health facility destruction, workforce shortages, unpaid salaries, and communication breakdowns as major barriers.ConclusionThe conflict coincided with widening disparities in malaria surveillance across states, reflecting underlying inequalities in health system capacity. Strengthening states’ surveillance systems is critical in conflict-affected settings. Future research should examine locality-level impacts to better capture subnational variation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12936-026-05914-z.

Introduction

Sudan is a low-income country [1]. It is administratively divided into 18 states, each of which is further subdivided into localities, resulting in a total of 189 localities nationwide [2]. Sudan is characterized by considerable environmental heterogeneity, shaped largely by the north-to-south variation in annual rainfall [2]. This environmental heterogeneity directly influences the variable dynamics of malaria transmission and endemicity across the country [3].

Sudan is a major contributor to the malaria burden in the World Health Organization Eastern Mediterranean Region (WHO EMRO). In 2023, the country accounted for approximately 41% of all malaria cases and 49% of malaria-related deaths in the region [4]. According to the WHO World Malaria Report 2024, Sudan ranks second-highest among WHO EMRO countries in malaria cases [5].

Malaria is a critical public health threat in Sudan, consistently ranking as the nation's most prevalent and fatal disease. In 2021, it was the primary cause of outpatient visits in 15 of the 18 states [6]. The Annual Health Statistical Report 2021 states that nationally, malaria accounted for 17% of outpatient consultations (a prevalence of 83.5 cases per 1,000 population) and 14.7% of all inpatient discharges [6]. Most notably, it was the leading cause of in-hospital mortality, representing 8.4% of total deaths [6]. These figures underscore the disease's pervasive burden across the healthcare system.

Malaria control is led by the National Malaria Control Programme (NMCP). According to the Global Technical Strategy for Malaria 2016–2030, surveillance is a core intervention to control the disease [7]. Malaria indicators data are essential for informed decision-making and programme planning, as they serve as crucial measures of the effectiveness of malaria control interventions [7].

However, malaria data collection in Sudan is fragmented. It is collected from three main sources.

Firstly, for malaria outbreak detection, the collection of weekly disease surveillance data and epidemic preparedness fall under the mandate of the Health Emergency and Epidemic Control (HEEC) General Directorate, rather than the NMCP. The weekly aggregate surveillance data are collected from selected sentinel sites through the Integrated Disease Surveillance and Response (IDSR) system. Weekly data are shared with the NMCP and the vector control departments to join efforts in managing the outbreaks [8].

The second source is routine malaria data collected through the Health Management Information System (HMIS). HMIS collects monthly programme data through the District Health Information System 2 (DHIS2) through reports designed by the NMCP, which obtain programme impact indicators for malaria control programme planning purposes [8].

The DHIS2 platform has been implemented in 144 of Sudan’s 189 localities. Malaria data are routinely collected at all levels of the health system, including primary, secondary, and tertiary facilities. Initially, data are recorded on paper at the facility level and then aggregated at the locality level. These aggregated data are subsequently digitized at localities and submitted to the state level. Finally, data from all states are compiled and stored in a structured, digital national database [8, 9]. Finally, regular monitoring surveys are conducted to assess malaria-related threats, such as drug resistance and emerging vectors, as well as the quality of care [8]. This fragmented surveillance structure leads to coordination challenges, resulting in suboptimal data utilization for decision-making.

Malaria data collection depends on a well-functioning surveillance system; however, such systems are highly vulnerable to disruption during conflict, often leading to incomplete or inconsistent reporting. The integrity of the surveillance system in Sudan was most recently challenged by the April 15, 2023, conflict between the Sudanese Armed Forces (SAF) and the Rapid Support Forces (RSF). The conflict simultaneously erupted in Khartoum and the Darfur states before subsequently extending to other states of Sudan [10].

This crisis has significantly exacerbated the existing health infrastructure and the surveillance system of malaria [9], a fragility stemming from a long history of political instability [11]. Because different states were attacked at different times and intensities, the impact on data collection has been uneven and complex, as different states were attacked at different times and with varying intensities.

The conflict resulted in widespread destruction of health facilities and essential infrastructure. It also fragmented health governance structures and severely disrupted disease control programmes, including the national health information system [12]. The conflict precipitated the world's largest Internally Displaced Persons (IDP) crisis, estimated at 10.07 million individuals [13].

The humanitarian crisis was further compounded by outbreaks of endemic infectious diseases, including malaria, that resurged and intensified amid the conflict's disruption of health services and mass displacement [14]. Due to the current conflict context, the real estimates of malaria burden and deaths are underreported [15].

There is a significant gap in studies exploring how the ongoing 2023 conflict has affected the malaria surveillance system. This article examines the system's functionality during the 2023 conflict using three key Monitoring and Evaluation (M&E) impact indicators adopted from Sudan’s NMCP's latest strategic plan (2021–2026) [2, 16, 17].

Methodology

This study adopted a mixed-methods research design, integrating both quantitative analysis of malaria impact indicators and qualitative exploration of the routine surveillance system functionality.

Quantitative component

Study area

The study covered 17 of the 18 states of Sudan that routinely report malaria surveillance data to the national DHIS2 platform. Khartoum State was excluded because malaria programme data are reported through a separate surveillance system rather than DHIS2.

Although all states experienced some degree of conflict-related disruption, the extent of exposure varied from localized clashes affecting specific localities in Red Sea, Kassala, Northern, White Nile, Blue Nile, and Gedaref states to more widespread and sustained disruption in other states [16], as shown in Fig. 2.

For descriptive purposes, states where conflict remained largely localized are referred to as less-affected states, whereas states that experienced more widespread or prolonged conflict are referred to as severely affected states. This classification is intended to reflect observed patterns of instability and does not represent a formal quantification of conflict intensity.

The remaining states experienced pronounced effects as the conflict spread from Khartoum and Darfur to the Kordofan states [17–19]. The five Darfur states have experienced protracted insecurity since 2003, with further escalation after April 2023 [20]. Gezira and Sennar states also experienced temporary disruption after periods of RSF control (December 2023 and June 2024, respectively) [21, 22], before entering recovery phases following restoration of government control in late 2024 [23, 24].The administrative control became fragmented across Sudan, with some states remaining under the authority of the SAF, while others experienced varying degrees of control or influence by the RSF [25].

Study population

The study population comprises of all laboratory-confirmed and clinically diagnosed malaria cases reported as malaria cases in the DHIS2 platform who attended health facilities in any of the 17 states during the study period.

DHIS2 was chosen as the primary data source because it functions as the country’s NMCP routine surveillance platform. Although data are available at the health facility, locality, and state levels, this study focused on state-level aggregates to provide a clear nationwide overview of conflict-related impacts. Lower-level data were available but highly granular, making it difficult to visualize overall trends.

Data sources and study design

State-level monthly malaria aggregates data were extracted from DHIS2 for the period January 2020 to March 2025. Data beyond the first quarter of 2025 were excluded due to incomplete reporting at the time of analysis. Reporting completeness was not selected as the primary outcome due to the absence of a reliable denominator for the number of active reporting facilities during the conflict period. Health facilities were frequently damaged, closed, or displaced without systematic updates to facility registries, limiting the ability to determine which facilities were expected to report at any given time. Consequently, facility-level completeness measures were considered prone to substantial measurement error. To address this limitation we analyzed three malaria impact indicators as complementary which are: (1) quarterly reported malaria cases (presumed and confirmed) per 100,000 state population, (2) malaria test positivity rate (rapid diagnostic test (RDT) + microscopy), which are crucial measures of the disease burden, and (3) quarterly inpatient malaria deaths per 100,000 state population, a key indicator of malaria mortality.

These indicators were selected for analysis based on their strategic relevance, routine availability in DHIS2, and alignment with both national and international monitoring and evaluation frameworks [2, 29, 30]. However, this approach does not fully distinguish between reporting disruptions and true epidemiological changes.

Two levels of analysis were conducted: a descriptive analysis including all 17 eligible states, and an analytic analysis restricted to 11 states that met the defined inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Sample size

The analytic part did not involve any sampling; instead, it utilized the complete datasets of malaria indicators reported in DHIS2 that met the inclusion criteria. All data were extracted on the 5th of July 2025 from the national DHIS2 website.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

All states reporting to DHIS2 were included in the descriptive analyses regardless of data completeness. For the interrupted time series analysis, six states (South Darfur, Central Darfur, South Kordofan, North Darfur, West Darfur, and West Kordofan) were excluded due to substantial missing data in the post-conflict period, which limited the reliability of model estimation.

Data management

Data were managed and processed using the R program version R 4.5.0. Indicator data were aggregated into three-month calendar quarters for completeness assessment and subsequent descriptive and analytical analyses. Quarters were defined as Q1 (January–March), Q2 (April–June), Q3 (July–September), and Q4 (October–December). The study period spanned 21 consecutive quarters from the first quarter of 2020 to the first quarter of 2025, yielding 1,071 expected state-quarter data points.

The data was divided into two periods: a pre-conflict period and a post-conflict period, with the onset of conflict defined as the April–June 2023 quarter [31]. The terms “pre-” and “post-conflict” were used to indicate the conflict onset as the reference point for analyses. This distinction was applied consistently in the analytical components, while vertical lines in the descriptive plots were used to illustrate pre- and post-conflict periods. In this study, “post-conflict” refers to the period following the onset of conflict, rather than the cessation of hostilities. A total of 13 quarters (Q1 2020–Q1 2023) were classified as the pre-conflict period and 8 quarters (Q2 2023–Q1 2025) as the post-conflict period, with the April–June 2023 quarter included in the post-conflict period.​

Missing values were defined as blank cells in the DHIS2 dataset. Malaria is endemic across all study states in Sudan [3], and programmatic guidance from the NMCP of Sudan indicates that true zero malaria case counts at the state level are not expected under routine transmission conditions. Consistent with this expectation, no zero values were observed in the extracted dataset across all states and time periods. Increases in blank entries coincided temporally with conflict-related disruptions to health service delivery and reporting. Blank observations were therefore interpreted as missing reporting rather than true zeros and treated as missing not at random (MNAR).

For data management purposes, blank entries were labeled as “not available”. However, this labeling did not affect the analysis; all such observations were treated as missing in descriptive analysis with no imputation, using available data. Data completeness was assessed at the state–quarter level. Facility-level reporting completeness could not be reliably calculated because the total number of active reporting facilities was uncertain during the conflict period.

Data completeness was assessed for each malaria indicator across pre-conflict and post-conflict periods. The proportion of missing quarters was calculated as the number of quarters with empty data cells divided by the total number of quarters in each period, providing a measure of national-level reporting patterns (rather than state-specific completeness). For the descriptive analysis, we have downloaded the absolute counts of these two indicators, reported malaria cases and inpatient malaria deaths, directly from DHIS2.

To compute the rates allowing for the comparability between different states, the absolute count indicators were standardized to the states’ population by dividing the indicators’ count by the state population and multiplying by 100,000. The total population data for each state for the study years were sourced from the Expanded Immunization Programme records [2]. Annual population estimates were used as denominators for all reporting periods.

The mathematical formula is given below.

Reported malaria cases (presumed and confirmed) rate per 100,000 = (Total reported malaria cases (presumed and confirmed) for the study year/State population for this specific study year) × 100,000

Inpatient malaria deaths rate per 100,000 population = (Number of inpatient malaria deaths for study year/ State population for this specific study year) × 100,000 [30]

Following the above formulas, we have calculated the reported malaria cases and inpatient malaria deaths rates separately for each study year.

The malaria test positivity rate (RDT + Microscopy) indicator was expressed as a percentage in DHIS2 and thus did not require standardization.

Data analysis

A two-stage analytical approach was employed. First, a descriptive analysis was conducted on the three selected malaria impact indicators. These analyses were performed to explore national reporting trends and patterns of missing values.

The descriptive analysis was performed not to estimate the true malaria burden, but to visualize patterns of missing data and interpret these patterns as proxy measures of DHIS2 surveillance system performance [32].Quarters with missing observations were represented in the plots using a graphical placeholder at the zero-level highlighted in red to preserve the temporal continuity of trends. These placeholders indicate missing data and do not represent true zero values.

Due to the large fluctuations between states, the y-axis scales were adjusted independently across facets. This approach was selected so that meaningful changes, particularly in states with low indicator rates, are not visually compressed. The time-trend graphs using a unified scale are provided in the Additional files.

In the second stage, an analytical assessment was undertaken to evaluate the effect of the 2023 armed conflict on the selected indicator: quarterly reported malaria cases (presumed and confirmed) per 100,000 state population, providing an inferential perspective that complemented the descriptive analyses. The indicator was selected due to its strategic relevance to the NMCP.

Changes in this indicator pre and post conflict were modelled using Interrupted Time Series Analysis (ITSA), with the Q2 of 2023 identified as the intervention point.

Quarters from Q1 2020 to the last quarter in the dataset were ordered chronologically and assigned a numeric variable (time) representing consecutive quarters. A binary variable (post) was created to distinguish the pre- and post-conflict periods: coded as 0 for the pre-conflict period, and 1 from Q2 2023 (the conflict quarter) onward. In addition, a numeric variable (post_time) was created to represent the number of quarters since Q2 2023: 0 pre-conflict, 1 in the conflict quarter, increasing by one in each subsequent quarter [33].

Reported malaria case rates were aggregated across states with sufficient post-conflict data, excluding states with substantial missing observations. A segmented linear regression model was then fitted to estimate the underlying pre-conflict period trend, the immediate level change at the conflict quarter, and the change in trend in the post-conflict period.

Qualitative component

Conceptual framework

A conceptual framework adapted from the public health surveillance model developed by Eric Wiesen et al. 2022 was used to guide the qualitative component of this study [34]. The framework was contextualized to malaria surveillance and informed the development of the semi-structured key informant interview guide. Interview domains explored the operational context, inhibiting factors, enabling factors, and adaptive or innovative strategies influencing malaria surveillance in the post-conflict period [34]. The framework was also used to structure the thematic analysis and interpretation of qualitative findings. Additionally, the qualitative approach was informed by the mixed-methods study conducted by Yenew Mulatu and Alamneh Yenew (2021) [35], which provided methodological insights for examining interstate malaria data reporting variations.

Study sampling

Key informants (KIs) were selected through purposive sampling to provide insights at both the national and state levels. The first KI was the NMCP’s Monitoring and Evaluation (M&E) manager, provided a national-level perspective, while the remaining KIs were state-level malaria program and M&E managers. The NMCP M&E Manager was referred to as N-M&E, the state malaria programme manager as S-MP and the the state M&E manager as S-M&E.

These state-level KIs were purposively selected from states representing different degrees of conflict impact: one from a severely affected state, and one from a less affected state, which experienced large internal displacement influxes and disruptions to electricity and communication services. State names have been withheld to preserve confidentiality. The selection of states was limited to those with reliable communication due to connectivity issues.

Data collection

Key informant interviews (KIIs) were conducted using the developed semi-structured guide. The guide was first translated into Arabic, then conducted via recorded online Google Meet sessions. The resulting audio recordings were then transcribed using the TurboScribe online site [36], manually proofread and subsequently translated into English.

Data analysis

A coding framework was deductively developed using the established themes from the Eric Wiesen et al. (2022) conceptual framework. The interview data were then manually analyzed according to these pre-defined themes. To safeguard participant anonymity, all identifying information was pseudonymized by assigning identifier codes to each key informant.

Ethical consideration

Ethical approval was obtained from the KIT Institute Ethical Review Committee. The quantitative component used routinely collected DHIS2 surveillance data that were anonymized prior to analysis. Permission to access and use the data was granted by the Sudan NMCP. In accordance with national regulations governing secondary analysis of anonymized surveillance data, additional institutional ethical review within Sudan was not required.

The qualitative component included key informant interviews with malaria programme managers in their professional roles. Administrative permission for the interviews was obtained from the NMCP, and recorded verbal informed consent was obtained from all participants. Participants were informed of the study objectives, the voluntary nature of participation, their right to withdraw at any time, and the measures taken to protect confidentiality. No personal identifiers were collected, and all findings are reported in anonymized form.

Results

Missing malaria surveillance quarters in DHIS2 increased after the onset of the April 2023 conflict across all three indicators. Prior to the conflict, the proportion of missing data in the dataset across all 17 states ranged between 0% and 11.8%. Trends in Darfur and Kordofan States

Figure 3 shows that conflict-related instability substantially affected data reporting in western and southern states, which historically experienced high levels of violence [37]. Reporting largely ceased across all Darfur states except East Darfur and was interrupted in West Kordofan for most of the post-conflict period, despite being high-burden malaria states. In contrast, North Kordofan maintained reporting but showed a marked decline in the trend of reported malaria cases in the post-conflict period.

Trends in the Northern and Eastern States

In contrast, in states where reporting was maintained, distinct epidemiologic patterns were observed. In low-transmission northern and eastern states, including Northern State, River Nile, and Red Sea, reporting continued throughout the conflict. These states exhibited a sustained upward trend in the indicator-reported malaria cases (Fig. 3), temporally coinciding with substantial influxes of IDPs. However, population denominators were based on pre-conflict projections and did not account for newly arrived displaced populations, likely leading to overestimation of the rates. Consequently, the observed increase should be interpreted cautiously, due to denominator-related bias.

Trends in other states

The remaining states continued to report in the post-conflict period, however the reported malaria cases indicator exhibited a declining trend (Fig. 3).

In contrast, northern and eastern states maintained reporting, although temporary interruptions occurred in Sennar during periods of active armed clashes. Notably, Gezira State continued reporting despite exposure to armed clashes.

Qualitative results

The qualitative findings are organized by the pre-defined thematic areas.

Theme 1: context and variation in the impact of conflict across states

The impact of the conflict varied among states, as some suffered direct consequences of the conflict, while others faced indirect effects on the health system and malaria surveillance.

“Darfur states were directly affected by the conflict, which resulted in destruction of the infrastructure, electricity, internet, and connection, displacement of the health care providers and malaria programme personnel, health care facilities going out of service, and that resulted in complete absence of reporting” reported N-M&E.

In contrast, states in Northern and Eastern Sudan did not experience severe disruption due to conflict but were indirectly affected by large influxes of IDPs seeking safety.

Theme 2: post-conflict data reporting challenges (inhibitors)

Four main challenges emerged in data collection:

Sub-theme: health system infrastructure damage

It emerged as a major challenge, severely disrupting malaria surveillance. Numerous health facilities, including key reporting units and laboratories, became nonfunctional due to destruction or looting, resulting in a collapse of service delivery and absence of case reporting.

Sub-theme: human resource challenges

The lack of trained malaria programme and surveillance officers, due to mass displacement and migration, substantially weakened system functionality.

“The lack of reporting is not due to decreases in the number of malaria cases but rather due to population movement, lack of health care providers, and the presence of minimally trained health care providers.” N-M&E clarified

Additionally, the suspension of salaries and transport allowances following shifts in national financing priorities further demotivated the remaining health workers, contributing to reduced performance:

“Programme officers and statisticians did not have the money for transportation to go to work.” S-M&E

Sub-theme: data collection constraints

Conflict-related telephone and internet service outages, combined with electricity interruptions and the theft of computers used for data entry, severely limited the ability to collect and transmit data to DHIS2.

“My computer at the state malaria programme was stolen” S-M&E

Additionally, the lack of printed surveillance booklets at the health facility levels, as printing had halted with the onset of the conflict, further exacerbated the problem.

The insecurity in localities and states led to interrupted reporting and supervision activities. As a result, reporting timeliness was a major challenge in conflict-affected states.

“Due to the conflict, one locality saw massive population displacement despite our officers being present; entering and leaving that locality remained dangerous” S-M&E described.

Sub-theme: impact of internal displacement

The movement of IDPs disrupted malaria transmission dynamics, affecting both displaced and host populations. Routine surveillance identified malaria cases among community-based IDPs presenting to health facilities; however, those in camps were initially excluded, as the camps were not designated as official surveillance sites.

As the state malaria M&E officer explained:

“Many malaria cases at the IDP camps went undocumented; they came to our attention when the camp representatives requested mosquito nets and residual spraying services from the state malaria programme.” S-M&E

Theme 3 enabling factors

The national M&E manager at the NMCP noted that malaria surveillance capacity appeared to strengthen in some relatively less affected states during the conflict, which they attributed to the displacement of experienced federal malaria programme personnel who provided hands-on training to state malaria officers and facilitated access to shared resources. According to the key informant, these shared resources included financial and nonfinancial support, such as stipends, transportation allowances, and direct supervisory feedback, which helped less affected states teams maintain engagement and support routine reporting during challenging conditions.

Another enabling factor was the provision of dedicated, complimentary telephone lines for M&E personnel, which facilitated data accessibility and reporting despite limited infrastructure.

Theme 4: innovative surveillance methods

According to the national M&E manager, the General Directorate of HEEC established the Early Warning, Alert, and Response System (EWARS), a platform that enables health facilities to collect and transmit data directly to a centralized dashboard for notifiable diseases, including malaria and dengue fever. In addition, malaria-related data from various state-level Ministry of Health (MOH) departments are consolidated and shared through a single WhatsApp group, enhancing coordination and real-time information exchange across departments.

Discussion

To the authors’ knowledge, this is the first study to assess the effects of the 2023 Sudan conflict on malaria programme impact indicators reported data through the national DHIS2 platform. Descriptive analyses revealed marked variations in data completeness across states in the three malaria impact indicators during pre and post-conflict time periods, which were corroborated by the qualitative findings. At the national level, the interrupted time series analysis was statistically significant for the indicator, quarterly reported malaria cases (presumed and confirmed) per 100,000 state population at the intervention point corresponding to the conflict quarter.

State-level variation in quarterly malaria reporting pre- and post-conflict

Effective disease surveillance relies on a range of factors, including health infrastructure, adequate laboratory capacity, and trained staff, statisticians, and malaria officers skilled in digital systems [38].

The variations among Sudanese states reflect disparities rooted in the longstanding uneven distribution of resources and health infrastructure across states. As Mohamed Ismail (2020) highlighted, northern and central states were historically better resourced in terms of health facilities and qualified personnel compared to Darfur and Kordofan states [39].

In Darfur and Kordofan, the combination of pre-existing constraints in health infrastructure and workforce [39], and the disproportionate severity of the 2023 conflict may have amplified disruptions in malaria surveillance reporting. These disruptions likely reflect a combination of factors, including population displacement, reduced access to health services, and challenges in maintaining routine surveillance, a pattern that was less evident in the better-resourced states. The sustained conflict violence in some states might have hindered surveillance officers’ physical access to data collection sites, likely impairing their routine malaria surveillance activities [40]. This pattern of missing data due to conflict was previously reported in South Kordofan state from June to July 2018 [41]. Violence as a barrier to data reporting has also been documented in Cameroon, where the destruction of health facilities directly contributed to missing data [42]. Comparable patterns were observed in Colombia, where prolonged conflict disrupted health system continuity and disease surveillance [43].

These states have endured prolonged historical conflicts, which may have hindered the implementation of health development programmes. Reflecting similar challenges, South Sudan’s efforts to strengthen its health system have been severely constrained by insecurity and the degradation of healthcare infrastructure [44].

This discussion highlights that the current breakdown in malaria surveillance data might not be attributed merely to a consequence of the 2023 conflict, but also to the culmination of decades-long systemic inequities [45, 46]. Addressing these challenges will require long-term commitments to innovative and equitable development and strengthening of health surveillance systems across affected states.

In contrast, the northern and eastern states continued to report malaria data without disruption. The DHIS2 dataset indicates no missing records, even in Al Jazirah and Sennar states, which were severely affected by conflict. This reporting continuity might reflect the comparatively higher density of healthcare facilities in these areas, which might have allowed parts of the surveillance network to remain functional despite disruptions [39]. Furthermore, the presence of a larger health workforce could have enabled continued documentation and data submission, even amid population displacement. The 2023 conflict in these states were also relatively less intense and of shorter duration, and both have since entered a recovery phase [47].

Our qualitative findings suggest that the forced relocation of Federal MOH departments to State MOH in the northern and eastern states, particularly River Nile, Red Sea, Kassala, and Gedaref, following the destruction of the capital, might have contributed to enhanced malaria surveillance performance in these areas.

This relocation may represent an unintended yet positive shift in health system governance. It aligns with the framework described by Lewis and Pettersson [48], who emphasize standards, incentives, information, and accountability as core drivers of improved institutional performance.

Displaced but highly skilled federal staff worked alongside state-level teams, which have facilitated capacity transfer and coordination. Targeted DHIS2 training workshops and on-the-job mentoring might have clarified surveillance standards and strengthened data quality practices. Our qualitative findings suggest that, in some less affected states, access to both financial and non-financial support was crucial in enabling state malaria teams to sustain routine malaria surveillance during the conflict. These findings suggest that in less affected states, combining technical support with targeted incentives may strengthen health surveillance in challenging contexts.

In line with the governance framework, the shift in responsibilities to state-level teams and their greater involvement in surveillance activities may have created more favorable conditions for accountability around surveillance outcomes, although we did not directly measure accountability mechanisms in this study. The variation in malaria indicator reporting across Sudanese states appears to reflect the critical role of governance in maintaining disease surveillance during crises. Our results showed that states under SAF (northern and eastern regions) demonstrated more consistent reporting across quarters compared with states under RSF control (primarily in western and southern regions), where lower reporting completeness was observed [49]. This pattern suggests that fragmented governance may hinder the continuity of surveillance systems. Similar patterns have been observed in other conflict-affected settings. For instance, in northern Syria, access to health services depended largely on the governing authority controlling each area, resulting in uneven service delivery [50].

Similarly, in Colombia, fragmented local governance structures and institutional weaknesses led to inconsistent health responses [43].

ITSA results and interpretation

At the nationwide level, the significant reduction in quarterly reported malaria cases per 100,000 state population observed in the intervention (conflict) quarter is best explained by insights from our qualitative results, particularly those captured under the theme of "surveillance post-conflict challenges (inhibitors)".

The surveillance officers experienced financial constraints that limited their mobility to conduct routine surveillance activities. In Addition, the collapse of national communication networks might further hinder the timely transmission of data to the DHIS2 platform, potentially delaying or preventing the reporting of malaria data from health facilities.

Respondents also reported nationwide suspension of surveillance register printing, which may have compromised record accuracy and completeness. Together, these disruptions to routine surveillance operations may have contributed to underreporting malaria cases during the study period rather than reflecting a true reduction in malaria incidence. Our qualitative findings also indicated that the lack of trained malaria programme and surveillance officers, resulting from staff displacement and migration, substantially weakened surveillance system functionality. These observations are supported by the literature, which documents that mass migration of skilled health personnel in conflict-affected settings can lead to critical gaps in disease surveillance and programme implementation [51].

Added to the burden, laboratory confirmation of malaria was further compromised by the destruction of diagnostic infrastructure and the disruption of health supply chains during the conflict. Widespread electricity outages, the relocation of the national supply warehouse, and increased distances to peripheral facilities likely affected the availability and distribution of both RDTs and microscopy materials [51–53]. Inadequate storage conditions for RDTs during this period may have also impaired test performance. Together, these factors likely contributed to the observed reduction in laboratory-confirmed malaria cases documented during the study period.

Conversely, the decline in reported malaria cases in the selected states may partly reflect a genuine reduction in disease burden, possibly resulting from the sustained effectiveness of malaria control interventions. This interpretation is consistent with experiences from Sri Lanka, where malaria elimination was achieved despite prolonged civil conflict through decentralized leadership and strong stakeholder collaboration [54].

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the analysis relied on a single, fragmented routine data source accessed through the dynamic DHIS2 online platform, which may affect reproducibility and data consistency over time. In addition, the analysis was conducted at the state level, which may have masked important heterogeneity at the locality level. Sudan’s DHIS2 system includes 189 localities, and the effects of conflict were often unevenly distributed, with some localities within a state more affected than others. Aggregating data to the state level may therefore have obscured localized variation in malaria burden and the differential effects of conflict on health services [55]. A locality-level analysis would offer a more granular understanding of these patterns, but this was beyond the scope of the present study and should be considered in future research.

Second, the conflict likely affected the completeness and reliability of the routine DHIS2 data used in this study. Extensive damage to health facilities and reporting systems may have introduced systematic bias into the indicator estimates, leading to either overestimation or underestimation. Reliable information on the number of active reporting facilities was not available in the post conflict period within the study, which prevented calculation of facility-level reporting completeness. Because health facilities were frequently damaged, closed, displaced, or temporarily non-functional without systematic updates to facility registries, it was difficult to determine which facilities were expected to report in each period. This makes it challenging to distinguish true changes in malaria burden from reporting disruptions.

Third, population displacement during the conflict likely affected the state-level denominators used to calculate rates. Internally displaced populations were not systematically included in the population estimates, particularly at the beginning of the conflict, and IDP camps were not consistently integrated into the surveillance system. As a result, malaria reported cases and in patient death rates per 100,000 population should be interpreted with caution, as they may underestimate the burden among displaced populations. In addition, population movement during the conflict may have altered malaria transmission and reporting patterns, but these factors were not explicitly modeled because reliable time-series population data were unavailable.

Fourth, the interrupted time series analysis was conducted using quarterly rather than monthly data because of substantial missing monthly reporting in the DHIS2 dataset. Although monthly data would have provided greater temporal resolution, quarterly aggregation was used to reduce the influence of irregular reporting and improve the stability of the time series estimates.

Finally, the qualitative component was based on a small number of key informant interviews (n = 3), largely because of limited accessibility to potential participants and the constraints of conducting research in a post-conflict setting. Although the sample was small, the interviews provided useful contextual insights that helped interpret the quantitative findings. We also acknowledge the absence of formal local ethical approval, beyond permission from the NMCP, as a limitation and have clarified this context in the manuscript.

Recommendations

These findings point to several priorities for strengthening malaria surveillance and response in Sudan. First, the national and state Ministries of Health should prioritize the development of an integrated national malaria surveillance platform that fully incorporates all three major reporting streams, and include data from Khartoum, within DHIS2. Such integration would improve the completeness and consistency of routine surveillance data. Strengthening state-level malaria programme management, alongside clearer operational autonomy, may also help improve responsiveness in conflict-affected settings.

Second, policy efforts should support state-level surveillance systems through targeted technical assistance, supervision, and incentives that help sustain routine reporting during periods of instability. Building state capacity in data management, analysis, and use could strengthen accountability while maintaining coordination with national oversight structures. This balance may be particularly important in contexts where central systems are disrupted, but subnational structures remain functional.

Third, future research should examine locality-level variation to better capture the uneven effects of conflict within states. Studies should also incorporate IDPs into denominators and surveillance frameworks, as this would improve the accuracy of malaria burden estimates and better reflect the needs of affected populations. Such work would support the design of more resilient, equitable, and conflict-sensitive malaria surveillance systems.

Conclusion

The April 2023 Sudan conflict substantially increased missing DHIS2 malaria surveillance data across Sudan, with marked state-level variations in trends and completeness. Interrupted time series analysis revealed a significant decline in the intervention (conflict) quarter for the indicator reported malaria cases (presumed and confirmed) per 100,000 state population, while qualitative findings identified healthcare infrastructure destruction, supply chain disruptions, and human resource shortages as primary surveillance breakdown mechanisms. These patterns reflect pre-existing health system disparities exacerbated by differential conflict intensity, underscoring the urgent need for targeted investment in state-level capacity building and strengthening state-level malaria surveillance.
