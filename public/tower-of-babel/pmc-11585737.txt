Therapeutic potential of the probiotic Lactiplantibacillus plantarum BX 62 and its postbiotics in alleviating rheumatoid arthritis in mice
Authors: Qi Qin, Gang Hu, Xiaoni Zhou, Ruizhe Zhu, Jianying Chen, Kangtai Zeng, Jingwu Wu, Jing Wei, Tingtao Chen, Xusheng Guo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study comprehensively investigated the probiotic potential of Lactiplantibacillus plantarum and its derived postbiotics in alleviating rheumatoid arthritis (RA) symptoms in mice. In vitro analyses established the probiotic characteristics of L. plantarum, highlighting its acid tolerance, bile salt resistance, and adhesion abilities. Subsequent in vivo experiments demonstrated that administering live L. plantarum significantly reduced arthritis scores, paw oedema, and intestinal permeability in RA mice, indicating its therapeutic potential. Furthermore, the study explored the effects of L. plantarum-derived postbiotics, including both the cellular remnants and supernatant, on RA. Notably, both the inactivated body of L. plantarum and its supernatant effectively reduced osteoarthritic damage, intestinal pathological changes, autoantibody levels, cytokine imbalances, and oxidative stress in RA mice. Additionally, qPCR analysis revealed that L. plantarum interventions restored gut microbiota composition towards a healthier profile, resembling that of normal mice. Collectively, these findings underscore the therapeutic benefits of L. plantarum and its postbiotics in ameliorating RA symptoms, emphasising their potential as novel therapeutic agents for managing RA.Keywords: Rheumatoid arthritis, Lactiplantibacillus plantarum, Osteoarthritis, Postbiotic

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic, systemic autoimmune disease characterised by synovial hyperplasia, synovitis, and erosion of bone and cartilage in the joints. These changes lead to joint swelling, symmetrical pain, and stiffness, ultimately resulting in joint deformities, restricted mobility, and a significant reduction in quality of life (Liu and Zhang, 2024). RA affects approximately 1% of the global population, with an age-standardised incidence rate in China of approximately 13.7% (Golmohammadi et al., 2024; Gu et al., 2024). Current RA treatments can be broadly classified into four categories: glucocorticoids, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), disease-modifying anti-rheumatic drugs (DMARDs), and biological agents (Gu et al., 2024). Although these medications are effective in alleviating RA symptoms, their side effects are a considerable concern. For example, some NSAIDs can cause stomach ulcers and gastrointestinal bleeding, while glucocorticoids can disrupt hormonal regulation, leading to increased body fat. Biological agents, despite their efficacy, are often costly and have a limited range of application. Therefore, the development of a treatment with minimal side effects, broad applicability, and high efficacy is of substantial clinical importance (Laha et al., 2024).

Recent studies have identified a significant relationship between gut microbiota and the development and progression of RA (Balasundaram et al., 2024; Shan et al., 2024). The gut microbiota can influence the immune system, potentially triggering or exacerbating RA through various mechanisms (Balasundaram et al., 2024). Dysbiosis, an imbalance in the gut microbiota, is frequently observed in individuals with RA and is linked to increased intestinal permeability, which leads to systemic inflammation. This dysbiosis can alter immune responses, promote the production of inflammatory cytokines, and activate inflammatory pathways, such as the NF-κB and MAPK pathways (Langan et al., 2021). Consequently, the gut microbiota can modulate inflammatory factors, contributing to the pathogenesis and exacerbation of RA.

Probiotics, which are beneficial live microorganisms, have shown promise in the management of RA (Levenson et al., 2024). Numerous studies have demonstrated that specific probiotic strains, such as Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium, can alleviate RA symptoms and improve patient outcomes (Fan et al., 2021; Li et al., 2023). These probiotics help restore the balance of gut microbiota, enhance gut barrier function, and modulate the immune system. Clinical studies have shown that patients with RA receiving probiotic supplements experienced reductions in inflammatory markers, such as C-reactive protein and tumour necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), along with improvements in clinical symptoms. The evidence suggests that probiotics may serve as an adjunct therapy for RA, helping to manage inflammation and improve the overall disease prognosis. The beneficial effects of probiotics in RA are associated with changes in various inflammatory pathways (Blenkinsopp et al., 2024).

Postbiotics, the bioactive metabolites and cellular components derived from bacterial fermentation or the non-viable state of probiotics, have attracted significant attention for their potential therapeutic effects in various inflammatory conditions, including RA (Ying et al., 2023). Among these, postbiotics derived from Lactiplantibacillus plantarum have emerged as promising candidates because of their ability to modulate immune responses and alleviate inflammatory processes. Recent evidence suggests that L. plantarum and its derived postbiotics can effectively downregulate proinflammatory pathways and upregulate anti-inflammatory responses in RA (Gohil et al., 2018; Fan et al., 2020). Furthermore, L. plantarum postbiotics have been shown to suppress proinflammatory cytokines, including C-reactive protein and TNF-α (Fan et al., 2020). These alterations in the inflammatory environment contribute to reduced systemic inflammation, diminished joint damage, and improved clinical outcomes in individuals with RA.

This study was performed to elucidate the specific beneficial effects of L. plantarum and its derived postbiotics on the clinical symptoms of RA, as well as their impact on the gut microbiota, which plays a crucial role in modulating immune responses and inflammation. By exploring the underlying molecular mechanisms of these therapeutic effects, the study seeks to provide a robust theoretical framework and practical guidance for the development of functionally tailored, highly effective postbiotic interventions for the management of RA in the future.

Materials and methods

Animals, probiotics, and reagents

The study utilised male DBA/1 mice, aged 6–8 weeks, obtained from SuperBio (Beijing) Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). The L. plantarum strain, identified as L. plantarum BX62, was originally isolated from traditional fermented yak yogurt from the Tibetan Plateau by the Probiotics and Life Health Institute of Lanzhou University (Lanzhou, Gansu Province, China). This strain was manufactured by Harbin Meihua Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Harbin, China) and is preserved at the China General Microbiological Culture Collection Center under accession number 15779.

The L. plantarum strain was cultivated using Man, Rogosa, and Sharpe (MRS) medium supplied by Qingdao Haibo Biology (Qingdao, China). Various reagents were used in the study, including Fluo-4AM and Pluronic F-127 (Molecular Probes, Eugene, OR, USA), along with a selection of antibiotics—ampicillin, metronidazole, neomycin, and vancomycin—obtained from Beijing Solarbio (Beijing, China). For protein-related experiments, biochemical agents from Beijing Solarbio were employed. These included bovine serum albumin, radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer, tris(hydroxymethyl)aminomethane (Tris), N,N,N′,N′-tetramethylethylenediamine (TEMED), sodium dodecyl sulphate, glycine, and ammonium persulfate. Additionally, skim milk (Becton, Dickinson and Company, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) and a protease inhibitor cocktail (MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA) were included to enrich growth media and preserve protein stability, respectively.

Immunoblotting analyses were performed using antibodies specific to β-actin and targeted proteins, including occludin and ZO-1, which were sourced from ABclonal (Woburn, MA, USA). For detection purposes, horseradish peroxidase-labelled sheep anti-mouse IgG and sheep anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibodies (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA) were used. Protein concentrations were measured using a bicinchoninic acid protein concentration determination kit (Cell Signaling Technology). Molecular analysis was facilitated by a reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) kit from TAKARA (Kusatsu, Japan). To quantify levels of anti-C II IgG, interleukin-4 (IL-4), IL-10, IL-17 A, IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays were conducted using kits from 4 A Biotech (Beijing, China). Finally, bacterial DNA extraction was performed using the TIANamp Bacteria DNA kit from Tiangen Biotech (Beijing, China).

Culture of bacteria and probiotic experiment in vitro

The experiments were conducted using liquid MRS medium, with its pH adjusted to 5.0–5.5 using glacial acetic acid to resuscitate L. plantarum BX62. The preparation of MRS medium involves dissolving glucose, lactose, yeast extract, a nitrogen source (such as broth), and sodium propionate in distilled water. The pH is adjusted to the appropriate range, followed by filtration, aliquoting, and sterilization through autoclaving. This process yields a clear and transparent medium suitable for the cultivation and enumeration of L. plantarum BX62. The culture conditions involved an overnight incubation in an anaerobic incubator at 37 °C, followed by two subsequent activation steps.

The spot plate counting method is a precise microbial enumeration technique. The process begins with the preparation of a homogeneous sample followed by serial dilutions to ensure the presence of a countable number of colonies on the plates. MRS agar is then poured into sterile petri dishes, and aliquots from each dilution are spread evenly onto the surface. The inoculated plates are incubated at a constant temperature until visible colonies form. Plates with colony counts within the appropriate range are selected for enumeration. The total viable cell count in the original sample is calculated based on the dilution factor and the colony counts. The experimental results are then recorded and analysed.

The probiotic investigation included several key assessments as described below.

Acid tolerance assessment

To evaluate acid tolerance, the resuscitated L. plantarum BX62 culture, following the prescribed incubation protocol, was centrifuged at 4000 rpm (R = 13.5 cm) for 3 min after serial dilutions (101 to 106). The cells were then exposed to phosphate-buffered saline buffers with pH values of 3.0–7.0 for 4 h, followed by plating on MRS solid plates and cultivation at 37 °C for 36 h to determine viable bacterial counts.

In vitro antioxidant capacity of L. plantarum

The 1,1-diphenyl-2-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) free radical scavenging ability was assessed by incubating the bacterial culture supernatant with a DPPH methanol solution and measuring the absorbance at 517 nm using a spectrophotometer. The hydroxyl radical (OH) scavenging activity was evaluated by incubating the culture with FeSO4, H2O2, and salicylic acid, with absorbance measured at 510 nm. The superoxide radical (O2−) scavenging was determined by reacting the bacterial supernatant with Tris-HCl and pyrocatechol solutions, measuring absorbance at 330 nm. The Fe2+ chelating potential was analysed spectrophotometrically at 546 nm following the reaction with ferrous sulphate, vitamin C, NaOH, trichloroacetic acid, and o-diazophenol. The total reducing power was measured at 700 nm after incubation with K3 [Fe(CN)6], trichloroacetic acid, FeCl3, and deionised water.

Antibacterial and antifungal properties of L. plantarum

The antibacterial and antifungal activity of L. plantarum BX62 was assessed by inoculating LB plates with various bacterial strains (1 × 10³ CFU/mL, 20 μL). Oxford cups containing the L. plantarum BX62 (1 × 106 CFU/mL) culture supernatant (250 μL) were placed on the plates, which were then incubated at 37 °C for 8 h. The inhibitory zones were observed and measured periodically.

Cell adhesion potential of L. plantarum

HT29 cells were cultured in DMEM medium supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum. Lactiplantibacillus plantarum BX62 was cultured until it reached an optical density at 600 nm (OD600) of 1.0, indicating the end of growth. Subsequently, HT29 cells were co-cultured with L. plantarum BX62 for 2 h. Following fixation with methanol and staining with 0.1% crystal violet solution, the extent of cell adhesion was observed under a microscope.

Establishment of arthritis model in DBA/1 mice

In total, 100 male DBA/1 mice aged 6–8 weeks were randomly allocated into 2 experimental groups of 50 mice each. The first group was further divided into five subgroups: the control group (Group C), the model group (Group M), the model group treated with methotrexate (MD), the model group treated with a low concentration (107 CFU) of L. plantarum (ML), and the model group treated with a high concentration (109 CFU) of L. plantarum (MH). Similarly, the second group was randomly divided into five subgroups: the control group (Group C), the model group (Group M), the model group treated with a high concentration (109 CFU) of L. plantarum (MH), the model group treated with the supernatant of L. plantarum (MS), and the model group treated with the inactivated body of L. plantarum (MB). After a 1-week acclimatisation period, the mice were prepared for construction of the animal models.

Histopathological examination

Safranin-O/Fast Green staining

Following the ethical euthanasia of male DBA/1 mice (aged 6–8 weeks) under controlled isoflurane anaesthesia, bone and joint samples were promptly harvested, thoroughly rinsed in phosphate-buffered saline, and carefully freed from the surrounding soft tissue and bone marrow. To visualise the cartilage matrix and bone architecture, Safranin-O/Fast Green staining was employed. This staining technique vividly highlights cartilage proteoglycans in red, while bone matrix and collagenous structures appear green, enabling a contrast-rich assessment of bone–cartilage interfaces and any degenerative changes.

The tissue samples were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin, followed by a gradual dehydration process through ascending concentrations of ethanol. After clearing in xylene and embedding in paraffin, thin sections (4 μm) were cut and subjected to dewaxing using xylene and a graded ethanol series. The sections were then stained with Safranin-O and counterstained with Fast Green. Following the staining procedure, the sections were cleared, mounted with an appropriate mounting medium, and examined under a microscope at 40 × and 200 × magnifications for detailed histological analysis and documentation.

Tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) staining

TRAP staining was conducted to assess osteoclast activity and bone resorption within the bone–joint specimens of the euthanised male DBA/1 mice. This technique specifically targets TRAP, an enzyme highly expressed in osteoclasts, allowing for the identification and quantification of these bone-resorbing cells. After standard tissue processing, including fixation, dehydration, clearing, and embedding, thin sections were prepared and dewaxed. The sections were then incubated with a TRAP staining solution, which stains osteoclasts in a distinctive purple or red colour against the background tissue. Following rinsing and mounting, the slides were examined microscopically at high magnification (200 × ) to evaluate the osteoclast distribution, morphology, and activity levels.

Goldner's staining

Goldner's staining was used to perform a detailed evaluation of the bone tissue composition and mineralisation patterns in the bone–joint samples of the euthanised mice. This staining method differentially colours osteoid tissue (orange-red) and mineralised bone matrix (green), providing a visual representation of bone formation and remodelling processes. Following tissue preparation and sectioning according to standard protocols, the slides were subjected to Goldner's stain, involving the sequential application of various reagents to achieve the desired colouration. After thorough rinsing and mounting, the stained sections were analysed microscopically at various magnifications, allowing for assessment of the bone microstructure, mineral deposition patterns, and relative proportions of different bone components.

Haematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining

HE staining was performed on intestinal tissue samples from the euthanised mice to assess the histological features of the intestinal mucosa, including the epithelial cell architecture, crypt–villus morphology, inflammatory cell infiltrates, and overall tissue integrity. Following tissue harvest, rinsing, and fixation, the intestinal samples underwent dehydration, clearing, and embedding. Thin sections were then prepared, dewaxed, and stained with haematoxylin (for nuclear staining) and eosin (for cytoplasmic and extracellular matrix staining). After staining, the sections were mounted, and microscopic examination at high magnifications provided insights into the structural and pathological changes, if any, within the intestinal tissue.

RT-PCR

Faecal specimens collected from the mice underwent quantitative RT-PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis to quantify the expression profiles of Bacteroides, Bifidobacterium, Enterococcus, Enterobacteriaceae, Clostridium, Lactobacillus, Faecalibacterium, Akkermansia, and Prevotella. The primer sequences used for this analysis are detailed in the supplementary material (Supplementary Table 1). Initial processing involved homogenising 0.1 g of tissue in Trizol, followed by RNA isolation through chloroform-mediated extraction and subsequent precipitation with isopropanol. After washing with ethanol and air-drying, the concentration and purity of the extracted RNA were assessed using ultraviolet spectrophotometry.

The reverse transcription process included the removal of genomic DNA contamination using a specific cocktail (comprising 5× gDNA Eraser Buffer, 2.0 μL; gDNA Eraser, 1.0 μL; total RNA, 1.0 μL; and RNase-free ddH2O, 6 μL) and the subsequent synthesis of cDNA (facilitated by 5 × PrimeScript Buffer 2, 4.0 μL; PrimeScript RT Enzyme Mix I, 1.0 μL; RT Primer Mix, 1.0 μL; and RNase-free ddH2O, 4.0 μL).

The qRT-PCR analysis was conducted using the synthesised cDNA as a template, following a carefully calibrated thermal cycling protocol (initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 5 s, annealing at 60 °C for 34 s, and extension at 72 °C for 60 s). To confirm the specificity of the amplification products, a melting curve analysis was performed. The complete list of primer sequences used in this study is provided in the supplementary material for reference.

Western blot

The extraction of intestinal tissue proteins began with an initial washing step using sterile, deionised water containing 1 mM phenylmethanesulfonyl fluoride to remove any residual blood components. This was followed by homogenisation of the tissue in 1 mL of lysis buffer, carried out on ice to maintain a chilled environment. The homogenate underwent a series of vigorous shaking and cooling cycles before being centrifuged at 12,000 rpm (R = 13.5 cm) for 15 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was carefully stored at −80 °C for later use. Denaturation of the protein samples was achieved by heating, facilitated by the addition of 5 × loading buffer.

For preparation of the electrophoresis apparatus, a rubber plate was assembled by mixing and solidifying the separation gel, followed by thorough washing with isopropyl alcohol and flattening to ensure a smooth surface. Electrophoresis was then conducted on the gel plate, allowing for the loading and subsequent separation of the protein samples under constant pressure conditions. The transfer of proteins to a polyvinylidene fluoride membrane was carried out under low-temperature, wet conditions to ensure optimal transfer efficiency.

The membrane was then blocked with a 5% skim milk Tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 solution to minimize non-specific binding, followed by overnight incubation with the primary antibody at 4 °C. The membrane was then incubated with the secondary antibody at room temperature. Protein bands were developed by exposure to a developing solution in a light-protected environment, and the resulting images were recorded for subsequent analysis.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 for clinical data and GraphPad version 9.0 for graphical representations. Measurement data following a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. For data that did not conform to a normal distribution, mathematical transformations, such as logarithmic transformation, were applied to achieve normality before being represented in the mean ± standard deviation format. Depending on the distribution pattern, either t-tests or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were used for statistical comparisons. For categorical data, chi-square tests were applied.

For analysing arthritis scores and paw swelling in mice, two-way ANOVA was employed to thoroughly evaluate the data. For microbiota sequencing data, a distinction was made between normally distributed and non-normally distributed data. Normally distributed data were analysed using one-way ANOVA for unpaired comparisons, while non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis tests were used for non-normally distributed data. To identify significant differences among multiple groups, post hoc comparisons were conducted using either Tukey's test (for parametric data) or Dunn's test (for non-parametric data), both with a two-tailed significance level. The threshold for statistical significance was set at P < 0.05 unless otherwise specified.

Results

Probiotic characteristics of L. plantarum in vitro

The growth performance of the selected strain, L. plantarum BX62, was assessed by monitoring its OD600 and enumerating viable bacterial counts. The results indicated a stable growth phase occurring after 24 h, with a logarithmic growth period observed between 6 and 18 h (Fig. 1A). Evaluation of in vitro antioxidant activity revealed substantial scavenging capacities against DPPH, OH, O2− radicals, and Fe2+, with respective percentages of 29.0%, 56.0%, 62.0%, and 10.1%, indicating robust antioxidant potential (Fig. 1B).

Acid tolerance testing demonstrated stable growth and maintenance of colony counts exceeding 107 CFU/mL across a pH range of 3.0–7.0, with optimal growth observed at pH 7.0, suggesting resilience to acidic conditions (Fig. 1C). Testing for bile salt resistance showed that the viable concentration of L. plantarum BX62 was robustly maintained at 107 CFU/mL, even within a bile salt concentration range of 0.0%–0.3% (Fig. 1D). This finding highlights the exceptional resistance of L. plantarum BX62 to bile salts, attesting to its resilient nature.

Adhesion assays with the HT29 cell line revealed substantial attachment of L. plantarum BX62, with approximately 7000 CFU per 100 cells, without noticeable effects on cell morphology or number (Fig. 1E). This demonstrated its strong cell adhesion capabilities with no detrimental impact on host cell viability or growth.

In inhibitory assays targeting common pathogens, including Salmonella typhimurium, Salmonella enterica serovar Enteritidis, Shigella dysenteriae 301, Shigella flexneri, Listeria monocytogenes, Escherichia coli O157, Staphylococcus aureus Cowan1, Candida albicans SC531, and β-haemolytic streptococcus, L. plantarum BX62 displayed significant inhibitory effects, with average inhibitory zone diameters exceeding 22.3 mm (Fig. 1F). These findings affirmed the strain's efficacy in mitigating these reproductive tract pathogens.

Intervention effects of L. plantarum on RA mice

In Experiment 1, a comprehensive experimental design comprising five distinct groups was implemented to investigate the efficacy of different intervention strategies administered post-modelling in mice. An experimental flowchart provides a detailed overview of these groups (Fig. 2A). Analysis of arthritis scores and paw oedema indices revealed statistically significant differences between Group M (the untreated RA model group) and both Group MD (the drug-treated group) and Group MH (high concentrations of L. plantarum, 109 CFU) (Fig. 2B and C), highlighting the potential of high concentrations of L. plantarum in alleviating clinical manifestations of RA in mice. Additionally, evaluation of the expression of intestinal tight junction proteins occludin and ZO-1 showed statistically significant variations between Group M and Group MH (Fig. 2D–F), indicating the effectiveness of high concentrations of L. plantarum in reducing intestinal permeability in RA mice.

Histopathological examinations, including Safranin-O/Fast Green, TRAP, and Goldner staining of bone joints and HE staining of intestinal tissues, consistently showed that high concentrations of L. plantarum effectively reduced both osteoarthritic damage and intestinal pathological changes in RA mice (Fig. 2G).

High-throughput sequencing of mouse faecal samples provided insights into microbial diversity. Alpha diversity analysis, measured by the Shannon index and Pielou_e index, showed overall variations among groups, with Group MH displaying indices closer to those of Group C (the control group) (Fig. 3A). These findings suggest that L. plantarum mitigates the alpha diversity disturbances associated with RA. Principal coordinates analysis further supported these findings, positioning Groups MD, ML (low concentrations of L. plantarum, 109 CFU), and MH closer to Group C than Group M (Fig. 3B). This emphasises the beneficial effects of both pharmacological and probiotic interventions, particularly at higher concentrations, on beta diversity.

Venn diagram analysis highlighted the unique operational taxonomic units (OTUs) of specific microbiota among Groups C, M, MD, ML, and MH, with counts of 129, 278, 322, 257, and 208, respectively, and a shared OTU count of 301 (Fig. 3C). Regarding microbiota composition, the most abundant phyla across all groups were Firmicutes, Proteobacteria, Bacteroidetes, and Deferribacteres. The primary phyla showing differences between Group M and Group C were Proteobacteria and Deferribacteres (Fig. 3D–H). Notably, Group MH reversed these trends, highlighting the capability of L. plantarum intervention to correct the microbial imbalances caused by RA.

A detailed metabolomic assessment of mouse faecal specimens was also conducted. Principal component analysis clearly discriminated between the metabolite profiles of Groups M and C, with Groups MD, ML, and MH showing profiles similar, albeit with some differences, to those of Group C (Fig. 4A).

Heatmap analysis of the metabolomic data revealed significant similarities in metabolite composition among Groups MD, ML, and MH, which formed a stark contrast with Group M (Fig. 4B), emphasising the efficacy of the interventions in homogenising the metabolic landscape. Pairwise comparative analyses of metabolite differential expression patterns between groups revealed a general downregulation of metabolites in Group M relative to Groups MD, ML, and MH (Fig. 4C), supporting the notion of enhanced metabolic status conferred by these interventions. Venn diagram analysis (Fig. 4D) indicated that the overlap in metabolite counts between any two groups ranged from 4 to 12, with no single metabolite shared across all groups. This suggests that the metabolic perturbations induced by the various treatments were specific and non-overlapping, highlighting their unique effects.

To gain deeper insights into the specific metabolic alterations in RA mice mediated by L. plantarum at varying concentrations, a correlation analysis between Group M and Group MH revealed significant disparities in the expression of multiple metabolites (Fig. 4E). These findings suggest that the ameliorative effects of L. plantarum on RA may be closely linked to these metabolites or their downstream derivatives, offering a potential mechanistic basis for its therapeutic potential.

Additionally, the study assessed serum autoantibody levels (anti-C II IgG and anti-CCP IgG), cytokines (IFN-γ, IL-4, IL-10, IL-17 A, IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α), and oxidative factors in mice (Fig. 5). Group M showed marked differences in these parameters compared to Group C, whereas Group MH effectively mitigated the changes induced by the RA model. These findings indicate that L. plantarum intervention positively influences serological alterations associated with RA, including autoantibody production, inflammatory status, and oxidative stress.

These findings highlight the potential of L. plantarum-derived postbiotics, including the cellular remnants, in mitigating the clinical manifestations of RA in mice.

In Experiment 2, blood samples from mice were also analysed for autoantibody levels (anti-C II IgG and anti-CCP IgG) (Fig. 7A and B), cytokines (IFN-γ, IL-4, IL-10, IL-17 A, IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α) (Fig. 7C–I), and oxidative factors (Fig. 7J–L). These observations suggest that L. plantarum interventions contribute to the amelioration of serological alterations associated with RA in mice, with the underlying mechanisms likely linked to the functional properties of L. plantarum-derived postbiotics, including both the supernatant and the body of inactivated L. plantarum.

Discussion

The management of RA, a complex autoimmune disease, remains a significant challenge despite substantial therapeutic advancements (Golmohammadi et al., 2024) Traditional therapies, including glucocorticoids, NSAIDs, DMARDs, and biological agents, have demonstrated varying degrees of efficacy but are often associated with adverse effects (Laha et al., 2024). In this context, probiotics have emerged as a promising adjunctive therapy, offering a non-toxic, long-term solution with minimal side effects by restoring gut microbiota balance, enhancing gut barrier function, and modulating the immune system (Levenson et al., 2024). Evidence indicates that probiotics can reduce inflammation, alleviate RA symptoms, and improve patient outcomes, effectively complementing conventional therapies (Fan et al., 2021; Li et al., 2023; Blenkinsopp et al., 2024). This study specifically focuses on L. plantarum BX62, a strain with exceptional characteristics that hold significant promise in the management of RA.

Lactiplantibacillus plantarum has emerged as a promising probiotic candidate for RA management because of its antioxidant effects, acid/bile tolerance, and immunomodulatory properties that target gut microbiota and immune inflammation (Gohil et al., 2018). Its postbiotics, which are bioactive metabolites, further amplify these benefits through potent anti-inflammatory effects. The L. plantarum BX62 strain exhibits superior probiotic characteristics, surpassing others in antioxidant activity, bile tolerance, and cell adhesion. These attributes are likely due to its unique genetic and metabolic profile. Comparative analyses with other L. plantarum strains reported in the literature indicate that this strain has distinct advantages, particularly in terms of higher antioxidant activity and bile salt tolerance, possibly resulting from its specific genetic background and metabolic pathways (Requena et al., 2024). Additionally, the L. plantarum BX62 strain's enhanced cell adhesion capacity, compared to certain other strains, may be linked to the unique composition or structure of its surface proteins, which are crucial for intestinal colonisation and immunomodulation (Gao et al., 2020). These strengths underscore the potential of the L. plantarum BX62 strain in RA therapy, highlighting the need for further exploration of its mechanisms and the development of targeted postbiotic interventions.

This study, by establishing an RA animal model and introducing a high-concentration L. plantarum intervention, has demonstrated significant improvements in the clinical symptoms and pathological alterations associated with RA. This finding is consistent with existing literature while also expanding our understanding by elucidating the mechanisms through which L. plantarum modulates serum autoantibody titres, inflammatory cytokines, and antioxidant factors (Gohil et al., 2018; Saghir et al., 2024). Notably, L. plantarum stands out among other probiotics, such as Lactobacillus casei and Bifidobacterium longum, in its RA-supportive intervention, showing stronger immunomodulatory effects and broader health benefits (Fan et al., 2021; Li et al., 2023). However, the potential for strain-specific variations in efficacy must be acknowledged, underscoring the necessity for further research to clarify the roles and underlying mechanisms of different L. plantarum strains in RA therapy. Investigating the strain's specific interactions with gut-associated lymphoid tissue, its influence on T-cell subset modulation (e.g., Th17/Treg balance), and cytokine production (such as IL-10 and TNF-α) could provide valuable insights into its therapeutic potential (Sakyi et al., 2020). Additionally, exploring the role of gut–brain axis modulation in reducing RA symptoms could open new therapeutic possibilities.

The variability in efficacy among L. plantarum strains underscores the critical importance of strain specificity in probiotic-based therapies. Future efforts should aim to identify biomarkers that predict individual responses to specific strains, facilitating the development of personalised probiotic interventions tailored to patients’ unique gut microbiomes and immune profiles. This approach has the potential to significantly improve treatment outcomes while reducing the risk of adverse effects. Given the complementary nature of probiotics and conventional RA treatments, examining the synergistic effects of L. plantarum in combination with glucocorticoids, DMARDs, or biological agents could lead to more effective therapeutic strategies (Pružinská et al., 2022). Research into how such combinations influence disease progression, medication dosage requirements, and adverse effect profiles would be highly valuable.

This study also contributes significantly to the field by investigating the therapeutic benefits of L. plantarum-derived postbiotics. Postbiotics, as the non-viable components of probiotics, have attracted considerable attention for their potential health benefits, and this study highlights their promising application in RA management. These findings pave the way for developing targeted postbiotic interventions that avoid concerns related to live microorganisms. Future research should focus on identifying the specific bioactive molecules, such as short-chain fatty acids, peptides, or polysaccharides, responsible for these effects (Wang et al., 2024). Understanding their mechanisms of action, including direct anti-inflammatory effects, gut barrier function modulation, and interactions with the gut microbiota, could advance the creation of enhanced, targeted postbiotic formulations.

Additionally, characterising the bioactive molecules within postbiotics and deciphering their mechanisms of action are essential steps toward developing formulations with improved efficacy (Cabello-Olmo et al., 2021). High-throughput sequencing and metabolomics techniques will be pivotal in this effort, enabling the identification and functional analysis of key metabolites and bioactive molecules (Zhang et al., 2024). Interestingly, in our study, Zingerone and Curcumin exhibited notable differences between the M and MH groups. Zingerone and Curcumin have attracted considerable attention in recent studies due to their potent anti-inflammatory properties, positioning them as promising candidates for therapeutic applications. Zingerone, a bioactive compound derived from ginger, has been shown to inhibit key inflammatory pathways, including the nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) signaling cascade, as well as the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 (Yilmaz et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024). These actions contribute to its ability to reduce oxidative stress and attenuate inflammation in various experimental models of chronic diseases, including arthritis and colitis. Similarly, Curcumin, the primary active constituent of turmeric, exhibits broad anti-inflammatory effects by modulating a wide range of molecular targets, including NF-κB, cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2), and inflammatory cytokines (Cheng et al., 2024; Mladenov et al., 2022). Curcumin's anti-inflammatory efficacy has been validated in both preclinical and clinical settings, particularly in diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis, inflammatory bowel disease, and neurodegenerative disorders (Pourhabibi-Zarandi et al., 2024; Ben-Horin et al., 2024). Its ability to regulate oxidative stress and support cellular homeostasis further enhances its therapeutic potential. Given their anti-inflammatory mechanisms, both Zingerone and Curcumin hold significant promise as adjunct therapies in the management of inflammatory diseases, offering natural, low-toxicity alternatives to conventional medications. However, translating these findings into clinical practice will require well-designed human clinical trials, evaluating not only short-term benefits but also long-term safety, tolerability, and sustainability.

As the field of probiotics and postbiotics evolves, addressing regulatory and ethical considerations related to their development and use is critical. Establishing clear guidelines for product safety, efficacy testing, and labelling is necessary to ensure public confidence and encourage widespread adoption. Furthermore, equitable access to these innovative therapies should be prioritised, particularly for underrepresented populations.

In conclusion, this study provides compelling evidence for the therapeutic potential of L. plantarum BX62 and its postbiotics in RA management. However, the study also has some limitations, and further research is needed to unravel the intricate mechanisms underpinning their effects, explore strain specificity and personalised medicine approaches, assess their synergy with conventional therapies, characterise postbiotic bioactive molecules, and translate these findings into clinical practice. By pursuing these avenues, we can fully harness the potential of probiotics and postbiotics to improve the lives of millions living with RA worldwide.
