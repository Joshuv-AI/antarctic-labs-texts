Association between postpartum depression and maternal skeletal muscle mass evaluated using bioelectrical impedance analysis: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Kentaro Nakanishi, Yasuaki Saijo, Yuta Ishikawa, Akiko Yoshizawa, Asako Kanai, Yuko Yokohama, Yasuhito Kato

Background

Maternal mental health issues including depression have become increasingly critical worldwide [1]. The risk of suicide is significantly higher among women with depression during the postpartum period than in those without. Suicide is a leading cause of death during the perinatal period [2]. Although Japan has one of the lowest maternal mortality rates globally, a recent study has revealed a disproportionately high number of maternal deaths due to suicide [3]. An estimated 80–90 suicides occur annually among women from pregnancy to 1 year postpartum, making suicide the leading cause of maternal death in Japan, as in other high-income countries [3]. Maternal depression has been recognized as a leading cause of maternal suicide, with prenatal and postpartum depression affecting 7–10% of women [1]. Risk factors strongly associated with postpartum depression include domestic violence, previous abuse, negative life events, low social support, low partner support, marital difficulties, depression or anxiety in pregnancy, and a history of depression [4, 5], while the COVID-19 pandemic further exacerbated maternal mental health risks by increasing isolation and reducing support systems during pregnancy and the postpartum period [6].

Recent research, mainly among older adults with sarcopenia, suggests that a decrease in skeletal muscle mass is associated with the deterioration of mental health, including depression [7–9]. Sarcopenia is characterized by the loss of muscle mass, strength, and performance [10]. It is associated with falls, fractures, physical disability and mortality [11]. Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA) and bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) are commonly used to measure appendicular skeletal muscle mass, which is necessary for the diagnosis of sarcopenia [11]. BIA is a non-invasive method that relies on assumptions and relationships between the electrical properties of biological tissues [12, 13]. Because of its ease of measurement and non-invasive nature, BIA has been used to measure the body composition of pregnant individuals in numerous recent studies [13–17].

The findings of these studies indicate that promoting physical health, particularly maintaining muscle mass, may play a crucial role in supporting psychological well-being. Population-based studies in China have not been limited to sarcopenia, and have also reported that low muscle mass was associated with depressive symptoms [18]. However, maternal mental health care has traditionally focused on psychological or pharmacological treatments and psychosocial interventions [4], with a tendency to overlook the potential impact of maternal physical health indicators such as skeletal muscle mass. Although a few reports have stated that physical activity during pregnancy improves perinatal depression [19–21], no reports have been published on maternal skeletal muscle mass during pregnancy and postpartum, and its association with postpartum depression.

This study addresses a critical gap in the literature by examining whether reduced maternal skeletal muscle mass during the perinatal period is associated with postpartum mental health deterioration. Specifically, we aimed to evaluate the association between maternal skeletal muscle mass and postpartum depressive symptoms using BIA and the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS), a globally recognized screening tool [22].

Methods

Study design and participants

This prospective cohort study was conducted at Asahikawa Medical University Hospital, Hokkaido, Japan, between May 2022 and July 2025. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Asahikawa Medical University (No.21191). It was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all individuals before body composition was measured. The participants were recruited by obstetricians at the Obstetrics Outpatient Clinic of Asahikawa Medical University Hospital. All pregnant women who participated in this study provided written informed consents. Pregnant Japanese women (including some East Asians) were recruited during the first trimester (before 14 weeks of gestation), provided that they planned to deliver at our hospital. Patients who had miscarriages, artificial abortions, or stillbirths, or who were participating in this study for the second or more time, had missing initial measurements, delivered at another hospital, or had psychiatric complications under treatment, were excluded.

Maternal body composition measurement

Although various methods are used to measure body composition, this study used BIA, which is noninvasive, reliable, and safe for pregnant women [13, 23, 24]. Maternal body composition was measured using a multifrequency bioelectrical impedance analyzer (MC-780 A-N; TANITA Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). TANITA bioelectrical impedance analyzers have been used in several studies on pregnant women [16, 24–26]. The participants were asked to urinate before the measurement and to stand barefoot on metal footplates while holding the hand electrodes. The participants also removed their thick coats and wore light clothing. Body weight was calculated by subtracting 1 kg from their weight measurements. We corrected the data for fat mass, body fat rate, free fat mass, muscle mass, appendicular lean mass (ALM), skeletal muscle mass index (SMI), total body water, and total body water rate using BIA. SMI was calculated by dividing the appendicular skeletal muscle mass (kg) by the square of the height (m²). Maternal body composition was measured at four time points: T1, the first trimester (before 14 weeks of gestation); T2, the second trimester (24–26 weeks of gestation); T3, the third trimester (34–37 weeks of gestation); and T4, 1 month postpartum. In this study, we focused on the SMI because body composition analyzers are not designed for pregnant individuals, and we speculated that the SMI would be less affected by the uterus, fetus, amniotic fluid, and placenta at earlier gestational ages.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the EPDS score at 1 month postpartum. The secondary outcome was the proportion of EPDS scores of ≥ 9 at 1 month postpartum. We usually assessed the postpartum depressive symptoms in postpartum women at the 1 month postpartum visit using the EPDS. The EPDS is a self-administered questionnaire developed by Cox et al. as a screening tool for postpartum depression [22, 27]. The EPDS consists of 10 items, each of which is evaluated on a 4-point scale from 0 to 3 points, for a total of 30 points. The cut-off for depression screening was defined as 13 or higher in the original study [22], and this cut-off has since been used internationally. The Japanese version of the EPDS was created by Okano et al., and the cut-off for depression screening in Japan was defined as ≥ 9 (with a 75% sensitivity and 93% specificity) [28].

Maternal and neonatal baseline information

The following maternal and neonatal baseline information was collected from medical records: age at delivery; parity; assisted reproductive technology (ART); multiple pregnancies; pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI); medical history of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, psychiatric disease, hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (HDP), and gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM); delivery mode; gestational weeks at delivery; neonatal birth weight; and neonatal sex. Parity was categorized as 0, 1, 2, or more. Maternal height and pre-pregnancy weight were obtained from the self-reports of the participants at the first visit. Pregnant individuals were categorized based on their pre-pregnancy BMI as follows: underweight (BMI < 18.5 kg/m2), normal weight (BMI 18.5–24.9 kg/m2), and overweight (BMI ≥ 25.0 kg/m2). HDP was defined as the development of hypertension during pregnancy, after 20 weeks of gestation. GDM was diagnosed using a 75 g oral glucose challenge test during the first and second trimesters.

We also collected maternal baseline information which included the mental status during the second trimester and physical activity before pregnancy and during the second trimester. Maternal mental status was evaluated using the Japanese version of the patient health questionnaire-9 (J-PHQ-9). The PHQ-9 is a 9-item depression assessment scale extracted from all items of the PHQ and consists of 9 criteria used as diagnostic criteria for depression [29]. The original version of the PHQ-9 has been translated into multiple languages and validated in many studies [30]. The J-PHQ-9 was validated by Muramatsu et al., as a useful tool for assessing major depressive disorders (MDD) in primary care in Japan [31, 32]. The J-PHQ-9 consists of 9 items, each of which is evaluated on a 4-point scale from 0 to 3 points, for a total of 27 points. If the J-PHQ-9 score is ≥ 10, further detailed examination or consultation with a psychiatrist is recommended, as there is a possibility of MDD [32]. To evaluate maternal physical activity, we used a questionnaire from a Japan Public Health Center-based (JPHC) study [33]. The JPHC study was a large-scale epidemiological study that validated questionnaires on physical activity. In the JPHC study, participants were asked about the average time spent per day on three types of physical activity: heavy physical work or strenuous exercise (none, < 1 h, ≥ 1 h), sedentary activity (< 3 h, 3–8 h, >8 h), and walking and standing (< 1 h, 1–3 h, and >3 h). The following values were assigned as time scores for each activity; heavy physical work or strenuous exercise: 0 for none, 0.5 for < 1 h, 3 for ≥ 1 h; sedentary activity: 1.5 for < 3 h, 5.5 for 3–8 h, and 7.5 for ≥ 8 h; and walking or standing: 0.5 for < 1 h, 2 for 1–3 h, and 8.5 for ≥ 3 h. Metabolic equivalent (MET) h/day was estimated by multiplying the time score spent on each activity/day by its MET intensity: heavy physical work or strenuous exercise (4.5), walking or standing (2.0), sedentary (1.5), and sleep or others (1.5) [33]. The total METs/day scores for the four groups in the JPHC study, namely the lowest, second, third, and highest, were 26.10, 31.85, 34.25, and 42.65, respectively, in middle-aged and older adult Japanese women.

Maternal body composition measurements, EPDS, J-PHQ-9, and maternal physical activity data were collected at Asahikawa Medical University Hospital’s obstetrics outpatient clinic, where trained midwives explained the procedures to the participants and collected the data. The study participants completed the EPDS, J-PHQ-9, and a physical activity questionnaire personally using designated forms. Maternal body composition measurements, J-PHQ-9, and maternal physical activity were assessed by obstetricians, whereas the EPDS was assessed by midwives. As the J-PHQ-9 score was treated as a reference value for observational studies, routine care was provided in the absence of depressive symptoms. Participants with high scores on the EPDS at 1 month postpartum were referred for intervention by community public health nurses or visiting nursing care. Participants with suspected postpartum depression were referred to a psychiatric outpatient clinic.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as medians and ranges or interquartile ranges. Binary variables are presented as medians and proportions. We compared the clinical characteristics of the two groups with EPDS scores of ≥ 9 and < 9 points. Multiple linear regression analysis was used to assess the association between EPDS scores and maternal SMI. We also evaluated the association between EPDS scores and ΔSMI, which was defined as the change in SMI for each measurement period. We also categorized maternal SMI into quartile ranges and analyzed their association with EPDS scores. Using the lowest SMI quartile range as the reference group, we performed multiple linear regression analyses. The adjusted variables set two models in the statistical analysis to clarify the total and direct effects between maternal SMI and EPDS scores. The main model included maternal age, parity, pre-pregnancy BMI, history of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, psychiatric disease, and physical activity before pregnancy. The additional model included intermediate factors and comprised multiple pregnancies, HDP, GDM, the J-PHQ-9 score in the second trimester, physical activity during pregnancy, and confounders in the main model. The analysis results using the main model are presented as the main findings, whereas the analysis results, including intermediate factors, are shown in the supplementary tables. We also created a directed acyclic graph to justify confounding factors related to maternal SMI and postpartum depression. (Supplemental figure) We calculated the variance inflation factor (VIF) to confirm multicollinearity. Variables with a VIF of ≥ 10 indicated multicollinearity. Statistical significance was set at a two-tailed P < 0.05. All analyses were performed using STATA version 17.0, for Windows (Stata Corporation, College Station, TX, USA).

Between May 2022 and July 2025, 375 pregnant women were enrolled in this study. After exclusion, 318 pregnant women were included in the analysis (Fig. 1). The number of pregnant women available for analysis was 318 at T1, 314 at T2, 295 at T3, and 311 at T4 (Fig. 1).

The clinical characteristics of the pregnant women with EPDS scores of ≥ 9 are shown in Table 2. The pre-pregnancy BMI was identified as a characteristic variable between the two groups. The median pre-pregnancy BMI was 25.8 kg/m2 in the group with an EPDS score of ≥ 9 or higher, which was higher than that in the group with an EPDS score of < 9. Additionally, the proportion of women with a history of psychiatric disease and a high J-PHQ-9 score tended to be higher in the group with an EPDS score of ≥ 9 than in the group with an EPDS score of < 9. The SMI also tended to be higher in the group with an EPDS score of ≥ 9 at all measurement points.

The details of maternal body composition are shown in Table 3. The fat mass, lean mass, muscle mass, ALM, and body water tended to increase with increasing gestational age and decrease during the postpartum period. Similarly, the body fat percentage and SMI tended to increase with increasing gestational age and decrease during the postpartum period. Conversely, the body water percentage decreased with increasing gestational age and returned to normal after delivery (Table 3).

Discussion

This prospective cohort study did not find a significant association between maternal skeletal muscle mass and postpartum depression. Changes in skeletal muscle mass during pregnancy and the postpartum period were also not associated with postpartum depression. To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the association between maternal skeletal muscle mass and postpartum depression.

Although an association was not observed in the current study, a relationship between skeletal muscle mass and depression has been reported in previous studies. A population-based study by Cho et al. demonstrated an association between low muscle mass and depression in asymptomatic adults [18]. Low muscle mass was significantly associated with depressive symptoms in both men and women, although this tendency was stronger in men. A cross-sectional cohort study by Kim et al. reported that, in Korean older adults, depression is associated with low body mass and sarcopenia, particularly in men [34]. A prospective study by Gariballa et al. described a relationship between low muscle mass and poor mental health in hospitalized older patients [7]. A systematic review and meta-analysis by Chang et al. revealed that sarcopenia was positively associated with depression and that this relationship was not influenced by adjusting for relevant covariates [8]. They also reported that the stratified analysis demonstrated that studies using BIA for the measurement of body composition tended to show a stronger association than those using DEXA or equation estimation. A cross-sectional study conducted in Japan detected an association between depressive mood and sarcopenia in community-dwelling older adults [35]. In addition, a systematic review and meta-analysis by Sánchez-Polán et al. reported that supervised exercise during pregnancy may be useful in the prevention or reduction of prenatal depression and depressive symptoms [19]. Physical activity and skeletal muscle mass are significantly associated in women of reproductive age and the postpartum period, suggesting that physical activity and skeletal muscle mass may be related to depression onset [36, 37]. In contrast, Qiu et al. observed a significant interaction between ALM and depression in a non-obese male population, but not in the female population [38]. Various risk factors are associated with postpartum depression, and skeletal muscle mass may be less influential in pregnant women than in the general adult population. We believe that our results will lead to further research in this field. If maternal skeletal muscle mass influences postpartum depression, interventions to promote physical fitness before and during pregnancy may represent a new avenue for maternal mental health care.

There is no established evidence of a biological mechanism for the relationship between skeletal muscle mass and depression, although several hypotheses have been proposed. A systematic review by Wu et al. described a few factors associated with this relationship, such as serotonin levels, brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) levels, and the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis [39]. Low serotonin levels are associated with the onset of depression. Maintaining appropriate serotonin levels is crucial for optimal muscle function [39]. BDNF promotes neuronal growth and regeneration, regulates neuronal survival, and enhances synaptic function. BDNF also plays an important role in the development and treatment of depression [40]. A reduction in BDNF levels impairs muscle growth and mood regulation. The HPA axis is a neuroendocrine system that regulates many bodily activities, including stress response, immunity, eating, and sleep. Dysregulation of the HPA-axis is strongly linked to depression and increased glucocorticoid secretion, which are associated with muscle atrophy [41]. It is assumed that these factors are also associated with postpartum depression; however, further research is required to determine the mechanism of pathogenesis, as psychological and social factors are also involved.

One strength of this study lies in its prospective cohort design. We collected the data to address our clinical questions. We also examined maternal mental status during pregnancy and physical activity before and during pregnancy as confounding factors, which allowed for more accurate statistical analysis. Another strength of this study is the use of SMI. Previous studies have used absolute measures, such as fat mass and lean mass, as variables but have not considered weight or height. This is similar to the use of weight alone rather than BMI.

This study also has several limitations. First, the multi-frequency bioelectrical impedance analyzers were not designed for pregnant individuals. TANITA’s body composition analyzers (MC-780 A-N) are designed based on Japanese data and are used in government statistics and clinical studies because they use the 4-compartment model method to more accurately measure body composition. However, the effects of the uterus, fetus, amniotic fluid, and placenta cannot be excluded; therefore, body composition measurements may become more inaccurate as gestation progresses. Second, maternal height and pre-pregnancy weight were self-reported. Self-reported body weight may be underestimated for underweight individuals and overestimated for obese individuals [42, 43]. Third, most of the participants had low EPDS scores. The number of outcomes may be too low to establish whether postpartum depression was relevant, because the median EPDS score was 1, and only 4.4% had a score of ≥ 9, which is indicative of postpartum depression. The reason for the low proportion of EPDS scores ≥ 9 in this study remains unclear. Research indicates that home visits can prevent postpartum depression [44], and we speculate that the care provided by our midwives to pregnant and postpartum women with high risk of depression may improve the mental state of mothers. In the analysis of ΔSMI (T4-T1), which had the lowest p-value in the multiple linear regression analysis, the post-hoc power calculation also yielded only 21.1%. Therefore, the present study had a small sample size, and to determine whether there is no true effect, further analysis with a larger sample size is crucial. Fourth, this study was conducted at a single university hospital, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to the broader population of pregnant women in Japan.

Conclusion

We examined the association between maternal skeletal muscle mass and postpartum depression using BIA. Maternal SMI during pregnancy and after birth was not significantly associated with EPDS scores. Although the present study did not provide any new interventions for postpartum mental health care, further research and case accumulation are warranted to verify our findings as many women in this study had low EPDS scores.
