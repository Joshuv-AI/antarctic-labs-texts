In vivo CAR-T therapy: latest updates from the 2025 ASH annual meeting
Authors: Shule Wang, Ran Kong, Guangcai Zhong, Xinyu Yang, Xiangxiang Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)–T cell therapy has significantly improved outcomes in hematologic malignancies but remains constrained by complex ex vivo manufacturing and lymphodepleting chemotherapy. In vivo CAR-T therapy enables direct genetic programming of endogenous T cells through systemic delivery of CAR-encoding vectors, thereby bypassing ex vivo manipulation and simplifying treatment workflows. This strategy has the potential to reduce production timelines, lower costs, and broaden patient accessibility. In addition, in vivo CAR-T approaches may preserve more physiological T-cell states and offer improved scalability. Importantly, however, current in vivo CAR-T platforms differ substantially in delivery modality, persistence, reversibility, safety profile, and translational feasibility. Here, we summarize the latest advances in major preclinical approaches and early clinical studies presented at the 2025 ASH annual meeting, with emphasis on the comparative strengths, limitations, and translational trade-offs of viral vector-based, RNA/lipid nanoparticle-based, non-viral DNA, and gene-writing strategies.

To the editor

Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)–T cell therapy has achieved remarkable clinical success in hematologic malignancies [1, 2]. However, its broader application is constrained by complex personalized preparation processes and the requirement for lymphodepleting chemotherapy. In vivo CAR-T therapy has emerged as a fundamentally distinct paradigm, which can directly “edit” T cells within the human body, offering potential advantages in scalability, accessibility, and treatment simplicity by enabling direct generation of CAR-T cells within patients [3]. Here, we highlight the latest preclinical and early clinical advances in in vivo CAR-T cell therapy presented at the 2025 ASH annual meeting and provide a platform-based analysis of this rapidly evolving field.

Preclinical investigations

The core challenge of in vivo CAR-T therapy is to generate functional CAR-T cells directly in patients while balancing target-cell specificity, expression durability, redosing feasibility, safety, and manufacturability. No single system simultaneously optimizes target-cell specificity, expression durability, safety, and manufacturability, necessitating indication-specific platform selection. Based on delivery modality and expression characteristics, the preclinical studies presented at ASH 2025 can be broadly grouped into four technological classes: viral vector-based approaches, transient RNA/lipid nanoparticle (LNP) systems, non-viral DNA/minicircle platforms, and gene-editing or gene-writing strategies.

Viral vector–based approaches, particularly engineered lentiviral systems, enable efficient T-cell transduction together with relatively durable CAR expression. IASO Bio reported an engineered lentiviral platform capable of delivering BCMA-, CD19-, and CD20-directed CAR constructs through T-cell-targeted transduction and T-cell-restricted promoter control, with antitumor activity reported in humanized mice and myeloma models [4] (Table 1). Qihan Bio further optimized viral entry using binder-fusogen combinatorics to functionally separate transduction from early T-cell activation, thereby maintaining a more quiescent metabolic state during the transduction phase and supporting antitumor activity in animal models [5] (Table 1). Similarly, Russell et al. showed that a single intravenous administration of the engineered lentiviral vector (LV-169) selectively delivered a functional BCMA-CAR transgene to niche-resident, resting T cells and eliminated disseminated myeloma in major histocompatibility complex(MHC)-deficient NOD scid gamma (NSG) mice [6] (Table 1). While these systems support robust expansion and persistence, they raise concerns regarding insertional mutagenesis, off-target transduction, immunogenicity, and limited redosing potential.

RNA/LNP-based delivery systems offer a non-integrating alternative characterized by transient expression and improved reversibility. Orna Therapeutics reported a circular RNA (oRNA®) panCAR platform targeting CD19 that induces controllable B-cell depletion in animal models, reduced autoantibody titers in a lupus model, and favored recovery of naïve B-cell compartments [7] (Table 1). Aera Therapeutics presented a targeted LNP strategy that rapidly generated transient CAR-T cells in vivo without ex vivo preparation or lymphodepletion, resulting in reversible B-cell depletion and immune remodeling in preclinical models [8] (Table 1). These platforms are particularly attractive for autoimmune indications or settings requiring controllable activity; however, limited persistence may reduce efficacy in malignancies requiring sustained immune pressure.

Non-viral DNA and programmable gene-engineering platforms expand the design space further. Velvet Therapeutics described a minicircle DNA-based STAR-CAR platform that supported proof-of-concept systemic immune cell reprogramming with the potential for repeated administration [9] (Table 1). In parallel, targeted LNP-mediated RNA delivery and gene-writing systems from Tessera Therapeutics enabled in vivo gene editing in hematopoietic stem cells and T cells, as well as generation of functional CAR-T cells without conventional ex vivo manufacturing [10, 11] (Table 1). These approaches are especially notable because they seek to combine the manufacturing simplicity of in vivo delivery with more programmable and potentially durable cell engineering. Nevertheless, questions remain regarding editing precision, biodistribution, ectopic expression in non-target cells, and the regulatory complexity of permanently rewritten cells.

Collectively, these platforms reflect trade-offs rather than a single optimal solution. Viral vectors favor persistence and potency but raise integration risks; RNA/LNP systems enhance reversibility but require repeat dosing; DNA-based platforms improve safety and flexibility but may reduce durability; and gene-writing approaches offer programmability at the cost of increased biological and regulatory complexity. Future progress will depend on improving cell-type specificity, minimizing off-target delivery, controlling CAR expression kinetics, and enabling scalable manufacturing.

Clinical trials

Clinical translation remains at a very early stage, and the currently available evidence is limited to small phase 1 cohorts reported in conference abstracts. KLN-1010 (inMMyCAR) is an envelope-modified, replication-incompetent lentiviral vector designed to generate anti-BCMA CAR-T cells in vivo. In the first three heavily pretreated patients with relapsed/refractory multiple myeloma (RRMM), KLN-1010 induced peripheral CAR-T expansion and persistence with a memory phenotype. The reported best responses were partial responses that deepened to very good partial responses, with MRD-negative assessments in all three patients. Toxicities were reported as manageable, including grade 2 cytokine release syndrome in two patients, no ICANS, and transient hematologic adverse events [12] (Table 2). In a phase 1 investigator-initiated study of GT801 for relapsed/refractory CD19-positive B-cell lymphoma, two patients with non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL) received treatment without prior lymphodepletion. High CAR expression was detected in peripheral blood T cells, and reproducible CAR-T cell expansion observed after each dose. The abstract further stated that no CAR signal was detected in non-T-cell PBMC populations, suggesting limited off-target delivery in this preliminary dataset. By week 4, both patients achieved partial response [13] (Table 2).

In vivo CAR-T therapy still entails several critical biological and translational safety concerns that warrant close attention, including off-target transduction, ectopic CAR expression in non-T cells, biodistribution, and vector shedding. The current main challenges are how to establish a reliable biological distribution monitoring system, extend follow-up time, formulate re-administration strategies, and integrate controllable systems (such as inducible promoters or safety switches).
