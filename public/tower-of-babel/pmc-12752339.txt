Exploring the public attitudes toward euthanasia legalization in China, especially among individuals with mental disorders: a national cross-sectional study
Authors: Xinyao Yi, Yan He, Zhiguang Huang, Ming Liu, Ginenus Fekadu, Xinchang Liu, Zhenjie Yu, Hei Hang Edmund Yiu, Jian Huang, Yibo Wu, Wai-kit Ming
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe legalization of euthanasia remains a highly contentious issue in China, with no comprehensive cross-sectional studies conducted in the country to date. However, in end-of-life decision-making among terminally ill patients, psychosocial factors demonstrate significantly greater predictive power than somatic determinants.MethodsA national cross-sectional study based on multistage random sampling was conducted from June 20 to August 31, 2022, in China. We employed a stratified random sampling method, collecting a total of 31,449 questionnaires of Chinese residents aged above 18. We employed a multivariate ordinal logistic regression model, considering various factors. Additionally, we conducted subgroup analyses to address potential confounding factors and fitted the same model to obtain the corresponding coefficients, odds ratios, 95% confidence intervals, and P-values, which were used to assess the results.ResultsSeveral factors were found to influence the attitude of euthanasia legalization in China. Lower age, higher educational level, and higher family monthly income showed positive attitudes toward euthanasia legalization. Comparative analysis revealed that females were more likely to support euthanasia legalization than males. Moreover, adherents of Buddhism or Islam demonstrated significantly lower support for euthanasia legalization compared to non-affiliated respondents. Individuals majored in medicine were more likely to support euthanasia legalization than other academic disciplines. Notably, individuals living with mental disorders or other chronic diseases showed a greater tendency to support euthanasia legalization. Compared to individuals who did not encounter any serious events, those who had experienced the death of a close relative had higher scores supporting euthanasia legalization.ConclusionsBasic demographic information, economic status, physical health status and lived experience are associated with attitudes toward euthanasia legalization. This study can contribute towards promoting more holistic and balanced legislation and policy implementation on euthanasia, reconciling considerations of individual autonomy, medical ethics, and broader societal interests.Trial registrationNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13690-025-01769-z.Keywords: Euthanasia legalization, Chinese public, Mental disorders, Cross-sectional study

Introduction

China’s definition of euthanasia refers to patients suffering from incurable diseases in a state of dying, due to extreme mental and physical pain, at the request of the patient and relatives, approved by the doctor, human methods to make the patient in a pain-free state to end the process of life. The practice of euthanasia remains a highly contentious and debated issue within China [1] due to the complexities in verifying whether the patient has provided genuine consent, as well as the ethical considerations and the ambiguous distinction between euthanasia and indirect homicide. In contrast, developed nations have legalized or decriminalized the practice of euthanasia as a permissible end-of-life option like the Netherlands and Belgium [2, 3].

Euthanasia legalization can respect the autonomy of terminally ill patients by allowing them to choose a peaceful death and avoid prolonged suffering [4]. Furthermore, it may ease the burden on caregiving families and improve healthcare resource efficiency [5]. However, there are risks of abuse, where patients could be coerced or misled [6]. It also conflicts with the medical professional’s role of preserving life [7, 8]. Religiously and culturally, euthanasia remains a contentious moral issue. The lack of comprehensive regulations could lead to inconsistencies in practice. Previous studies have illustrated various influencing factors related to public attitudes of euthanasia legalization, including the demographics profile, economic status, physical health status and lived experience [9–11]. Furthermore, research consistently indicates that psychological determinants, especially mental disorders tend to exert a stronger influence on terminally ill patients’ end-of-life decisions compared to somatic factors like pain intensity [12, 13]. Overall, the debate on legalizing euthanasia requires carefully balancing individual rights, ethics, and societal interests.

This cross-sectional study conducted in China aims to investigate the public attitudes and influencing factors toward euthanasia legalization. Moreover, subgroup analyses are performed aiming to explore new perspectives among groups like age and gender, especially comparing the attitudes toward euthanasia legalization between individuals without chronic diseases and subgroups living with physician-diagnosed mental disorders. The findings from this research will provide insights into the significance and ethical considerations surrounding this contentious issue, which can inform more comprehensive policy development and management in this domain. Furthermore, this research can serve to facilitate broader societal discussions on the ethical challenges surrounding euthanasia, enhance public understanding and evolve attitudes on this complex issue. It can contribute towards promoting more holistic and balanced legislation and policy implementation on euthanasia, reconciling considerations of individual autonomy, medical ethics, and broader societal interests. Additionally, the study provides references to the public attitudes and influencing factors for developing countries on this issue. The exploratory nature of this study is necessitated by the limited existing research in this specific cultural and ethical context. It examines the factors correlated with attitudes toward euthanasia legalization in China within a theoretical framework, establishing a foundation for subsequent confirmatory research.

Methods

Survey description and participants

This population-based cross-sectional investigation was implemented across China between June 20 and August 31, 2022. Employing a multistage hybrid sampling framework, the study encompassed 23 provinces, 5 autonomous regions, and 4 directly administered municipalities. The sampling architecture was comprised of four administrative tiers. These included 148 prefecture-level cities, 202 districts or counties, 390 townships or subdistricts, and 780 communities or villages. Provincial administrations and primary community or village units were selected through probability sampling and equal probability sampling methodology, while subsequent community/village entities and individual participants underwent non-equal probability sampling (quota sampling) processes. Specifically, the minimum sample size required for each province-level division was determined based on population proportions from the Seventh National Census [14]. Stratified sample size thresholds were set at 500, 1000, 1500, 2000, and 2500 participants, corresponding to different population strata. At the individual level, non-proportional quota sampling was employed to ensure adequate representation of key demographic subgroups defined by gender and age. The research protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Health Culture Research Centre of Shaanxi, China and has been officially registered in the China Clinical Trial Registry (Registration No: ChiCTR2200061046) [15].

The survey was conducted anonymously. To facilitate participant enrollment, investigators utilized a combination of print-based and digital methods, involving the strategic placement of flyers and the broad distribution of electronic communications. Investigators verified the identity of respondents and confirmed their eligibility based on the study’s inclusion and exclusion criteria. They conducted one-on-one, face-to-face administration of electronic questionnaires to participants. We utilized two-pronged approach to distribute the questionnaires. Participants can access the questionnaire designed on Wenjuanxing (http://www.wjx.cn/) platform by scanning a QR code or clicking a link. Submitted questionnaire data were automatically collected and stored on a secure backend server. For respondents who retained cognitive capacity but lacked sufficient physical ability to self-complete questionnaires, investigators conducted one-on-one interviews and recorded responses on their behalf in accordance with their verbal inputs. If face-to-face interviews were not feasible due to personal reasons, such as respondents being isolated at home due to SARS-CoV-2 or being unable to complete the questionnaire independently due to physical limitations, the investigators employed online communication platforms to carry out video-based surveys.

Prior to data collection, informed consent was obtained from all respondents. The informed consent explicitly stated: (1) aims of this study, (2) estimated time commitment, (3) the voluntary nature of participation, emphasizing that participants can skip any questions or withdraw from the study at any time without facing discrimination or retaliation, (4) the inclusion and exclusion criteria, (5) the potential risks associated with participation, (6) the benefits to participate in this study, (7) the procedures for data anonymization and secure storage, in compliance with China’s Statistics Law, assuring participants that data will be used solely for academic purposes and that no personally identifiable information will be published, (8) the right of participants to ask questions, with assurance that all queries will be addressed satisfactorily.

The survey questionnaire used in this study underwent consultations of 38 authoritative experts with senior professional titles invited nationwide. They refined and reviewed the questionnaire with selection criteria emphasizing both geographical coverage and disciplinary representation. They also come from various medical-related fields (including social medicine, behavioral epidemiology, health education, health statistics, health management, clinical medicine, pharmacy, and nursing) and cross-disciplinary domains (such as psychology, medical humanities, sociology, philosophy, and communication studies). This comprehensive approach ensured the research instrument demonstrated robust theoretical validity and interdisciplinary consensus. Moreover, 3 pre-surveys were performed to guarantee the results’ validity.

Of the 31,449 questionnaires collected, 30,505 met the qualification criteria after logical checks (Note S1), removing duplicate questionnaires, incomplete questionnaires, also questionnaires where all selected options are identical or exhibit a pattern. 21,916 remained after adjustment using quota sampling based on Chinese demographic characteristics. After further eliminating the contradictory records and records exceeding the allowed limits in the questionnaire, 18,902 remained. Above all, although the initial data collection encompassed individuals aged 12 and above, this study ultimately only analyzed data from participants aged 18 and above considering the legal relevance focus, that current euthanasia legislation debates globally center on competent adults as the target population. Moreover, minors may be more susceptible to the emotional impact of the research topics and discussing death and ending life could have unpredictable effects on the psychological and emotional development of minors, making their exclusion a standard protective measure. They may not yet possess fully mature cognitive abilities to comprehend the legal, medical, ethical, and philosophical complexities involved in euthanasia, therefore, complex decisions, particularly those made under emotional arousal or social pressure, remain challenging for them [16, 17]. The final record remaining 17,326 participants (Fig. 1).

Therefore, the survey finally included participants who met the following inclusion criteria: (1) aged 18 and above, (2) Chinese nationality, (3) permanent resident of China (out of country for no more than 1 month per year), (4) voluntarily agreed to participate in the study and signed an informed consent form, (5) exhibited adequate operational competence for digital survey implementation, whether executed independently or via investigator-mediated support systems, and (6) demonstrated sufficient cognitive capacity to interpret all survey components with semantic accuracy. The exclusion criteria comprised the following: (1) having acute disturbances of consciousness or active psychotic disorders, (2) having neurodegenerative or acquired cognitive dysfunction, (3) participation in other similar research projects, and (4) unwillingness to fill out the questionnaire.

Measurement exposures and outcome

The primary outcome measured in this study is the support score for euthanasia legalization, quantified on a scale ranging from 1 to 100, with 100 score indicating fully supportive and 0 score reflecting completely opposed to euthanasia legalization. Since the design of the primary outcome in our questionnaire was based on slider, the choices made by the participants were all integers. The definition of euthanasia listed in our questionnaire refers to patients suffering from incurable diseases in a state of dying, due to extreme mental and physical pain, at the request of the patient and relatives, approved by the doctor, human methods to make the patient in a pain-free state to end the process of life.

Influencing factors of the support score towards euthanasia legalization were selected based on a review of the relevant literature from various domains, including the demographics profile, economic status, physical health status and lived experience, as well as insights from subject matter experts [9–11]. We first selected the basic personal information including age, gender, education level, major (individuals with a technical secondary school education or above) and religious affiliation. Notably, religious affiliation was included as a factor of interest, as some religious beliefs and concepts regarding the afterlife may influence perceptions towards euthanasia as a means of transitioning to the next phase of existence. The majors of the participants are categorized according to national criteria, comprising 14 distinct academic disciplines. This variable is included to investigate whether individuals with a medical background exhibit different attitudes towards euthanasia legalization compared to those from other academic backgrounds. Economic status was measured with family monthly income, categorized into 10 levels, with increments of 1,000 RMB ranging from 0 to 15,000 RMB. We also focused on physical health status, specifically the presence of self-reported chronic diseases with verification provided by physician assessment. Chronic conditions were assessed with ongoing disease status. Particular attention will be given to participants with mental disorders according to the definition of DSM-5 [18], in order to better understand their degree of support or opposition to euthanasia as a potential end-of-life option for themselves.

Moreover, the severity of self-reported depressive symptoms was assessed utilizing the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 scale (PHQ-9). The PHQ-9 has demonstrates reliability and validity as a measure of depression severity and constitutes a valid and efficient screening tool for depression within the general Chinese population [19, 20]. This instrument employs a scale ranging from 0 to 27, where elevates scores denote increased depression severity. Symptom severity is categorized as follows: no depression (0–4), mild depression (5–9), moderate depression (10–14), moderately severe depression (15–19), and severe depression (20–27). Anxiety symptom severity was assessed performing Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 scale (GAD-7). This instrument utilizes a scoring range from 0 to 21, with higher scores indicating increased anxiety severity. Symptom severity is classified into four clinical tiers: no anxiety (0–4), mild anxiety (5–9), moderate anxiety (10–14), and severe anxiety (15–21). The GAD-7 is also validated as a reliable and efficient screening tool for anxiety within the general Chinese population [21, 22]. The variable for lived experience (significant life events experienced) variable was chosen to assess whether individuals experience significant events within past year like the death of a close relative or suffering from severe illness which may shape their attitudes towards euthanasia legalization. The exposed variables and outcome were partially extracted from the questionnaire developed by the “2022 Psychology and Behavior Investigation of Chinese Residents (PBICR)” project (https://www.x-mol.com/groups/pbicr/research) [23–25]. An English version of the relevant questionnaire sections used in our research is provided in Table S1.

Data preprocessing procedures

After data collection and quota sampling, the dataset was reviewed for any noted erroneous values. To ensure the integrity of the analysis, records with conflicted and exceeding the allowed limits were removed. Furthermore, individuals under the age of 18 were excluded.

Regarding the religious affiliation, for participants who did not specify a religion, the value was recoded as “No religion”. For those who reported a religious denomination, responses were standardized into a consistent format. Specifically, all forms of “Tibetan Buddhism” were consolidated under the broader “Buddhism” category. Any religious affiliations that could not be clearly classified were recorded as “Have religion but unknown”. For the remaining variables, values deemed likely to be erroneous typos were excluded from the final dataset. For the presence of chronic diseases, we categorized them to four different groups: mental disorders, mental disorders and other diseases, other diseases, and no disease, to focus more on mental disorders. Furthermore, for significant life events experienced, we set up four categories: severe illness yourself, death of a close relative, both severe events and no event.

Statistical analysis

Multicollinearity among the exposure variables were assessed using Variance Inflation Factor (VIF). Any variables exhibiting high multicollinearity (VIF > 10) were removed if they had no significant impact on the overall model. Subsequently, a multivariate ordinal logistic regression model was constructed to examine the influence of the selected factors on the support score for euthanasia legalization. Additionally, an ANOVA test was conducted to compare the fit of the full model against the baseline model. The baseline characteristics of the study population were then described using Chi-Square test statistical methods. Finally, regression coefficients, significance levels, odds ratios, and corresponding confidence intervals were reported for the final model. Sensitivity analysis and subgroup analysis were performed to further validate the robustness of the findings. Sensitivity analysis was performed to assess the robustness of our findings. Threshold of P value was set up as 0.05.

All statistical analyses were performed using R version 3.5.0. The multivariate ordinal logistic regression models were estimated using clm function from the ordinal package. The standard multivariate ordinal logistic regression and the parallel assumption test were conducted using polr function from the MASS package and the brant function, respectively.

Results

Baseline characteristics

This study included 17,326 participants, whose mean age is 43.12 (SD = 17.51). There are 8,987 (51.87%) females and 8,339 (48.13%) males. The majority of participants held a bachelor’s degree (5,460, 31.51%), while those with PhD, master degree, and individuals with no formal education have proportions of 0.61%, 3.54% and 6.24% among all the participants. A significant majority of participants identified as having no religion (16,653, 96.12%), while those with religion affiliation, included Buddhism, Islam, Christianity, Catholicism, and others in decreasing order of prevalence. Among individuals with a technical secondary school education or above, 3.24% of participants majored in medicine. Family monthly income is range from 0 to 15,000 RMB. The highest proportion for individuals earning between 3,000 and 4,000 RMB (17.21%) and the least in the 12,000 to 15,000 RMB category (3.31%).

126 participants reported mental disorders (0.73%), while 111 (0.64%) reported both mental disorders and other diseases. A total of 12,395 participants (71.54%) reported no chronic diseases, and 4,694 (27.09%) reported other diseases. We also assessed significant life events experienced within the past year, 950 individuals (5.48%) reported the death of a close relative, while 16,163 (93.29%) reported no significant events. A total of 193 (1.11%) participants reported experiencing severe illness themselves, and 20 (0.16%) participants reported both severe events. The majority of participants reported no depressive symptoms (7,155, 41.30%), while individuals with mild depression, moderate depression, moderately severe depression and severe depression have proportions of 32.86%, 12.43%, 5.11% and 1.59% respectively. Similarly, the majority of participants reported no anxious symptoms (9,380, 54.14%), while individuals with mild anxiety, moderate anxiety and severe anxiety have proportions of 33.09%, 10.11% and 2.66% respectively (Table 1).

Factors associated with support score for euthanasia legalization

Regression analysis indicated that for every one-year increase in age, the support score of euthanasia legalization decreased by 0.002 significantly, after adjusting for other factors (OR = 0.997 [95% CI 0.995 to 1.00]). These results suggested a slight increase in support for euthanasia legalization with declining age. Females were more likely to support euthanasia legalization than males, with an increase of 0.17 in the score after adjusting for other factors (OR = 1.19 [95% CI 1.13 to 1.25]). Additionally, compared to individuals with no formal education, those with a master’s degree, bachelor’s degree, junior college, and high school education had significantly higher scores supporting the euthanasia legalization (OR = 1.37 [95% CI 1.14 to 1.65]; OR = 1.30 [95% CI 1.14 to 1.48]; OR = 1.30 [95% CI 1.13 to 1.49]; OR = 1.21 [95% CI 1.06 to 1.39]). However, differences for individuals with a PhD degree, technical secondary school, and primary school education, were not statistically significant. The results also indicated that compared to individuals without any religious affiliation, those who identified with Buddhism or Islam were less likely to support euthanasia legalization, and this difference was statistically significant (OR = 0.70 [95% CI 0.57 to 0.85]; OR = 0.65 [95% CI 0.50 to 0.83]). In contrast, other religious affiliations did not show significant differences in support euthanasia legalization.

The analysis further revealed that compared to individuals with a family monthly income less than 1000 RMB, those with higher income level had significantly higher scores supporting euthanasia legalization, except for 1001–2000 and 3000–4000 RMB categories. This suggested that higher-income individuals tend to have a more favorable attitude toward euthanasia legalization. The results also indicated that compared to individuals majored in medicine, those majored in engineering and economics had significantly lower scores supporting euthanasia legalization (OR = 0.80 [95% CI 0.67 to 0.97]; OR = 0.71 [95% CI 0.54 to 0.93]).

The analysis revealed that individuals with mental disorders, other diseases, both mental disorders and other diseases were more likely to support euthanasia legalization, compared to those who did not have chronic disease. Although individuals with mental disorders were marginally significant when P value set up to 0.1. This finding was consistent with the expected relationships between physical health status and attitudes towards euthanasia legalization (OR = 1.56 [95% CI 1.12 to 2.18]; OR = 1.15 [95% CI 1.08 to 1.23]; OR = 1.36 [95% CI 0.99 to 1.87]). Furthermore, an interesting phenomenon was observed regarding PHQ-9 and GAD-7 scales. The results demonstrated that compared to individuals without depression, those reported mild or moderate depression were less likely to support euthanasia legalization. However, individuals with severe depression were more likely to support euthanasia legalization significantly (OR = 0.92 [95% CI 0.85 to 0.99]; OR = 0.84 [95% CI 0.76 to 0.93]; OR = 1.32 [95% CI 1.02 to 1.72]). A similar trend was observed in anxiety levels. Compared to participants without anxiety, those with moderate anxiety showed less support for euthanasia legalization, whereas those reported severe anxiety were more inclined to support it (OR = 0.81 [95% CI 0.71 to 0.91]; OR = 1.42 [95% CI 1.13 to 1.78]).

The study also examined the influence of significant life events experienced within the past year on the scores supporting euthanasia legalization. The results indicated that compared to individuals who did not encounter any serious events, those who had experienced the death of a close relative had significantly higher scores supporting euthanasia legalization (OR = 1.22 [95% CI 1.08 to 1.37]). However, the other life events assessed did not have a significant influence on the scores (Table S2, Fig. 3).

Sensitivity analysis

Given the inherent subjectivity in the operationalization of mental disorder status and family monthly income, we conducted sensitivity analysis to assess the robustness of the findings related to these two variables. For mental disorder status, comparisons were made using PHQ-9 and GAD-7 scales to evaluate self-reported and physician-diagnosed depression. The results consistently demonstrated that individuals with severer depression status were more likely to support euthanasia legalization (Table S2). For family monthly income, we recategorized the variable into four groups: <4,000 RMB, 4,001–9,000 RMB, 9,001–12,000 RMB, and ≥ 12,000 RMB. The findings revealed a trend aligns with original analysis that utilized 10 income categories with increments of 1,000 RMB (ranging from 0 to 15,000 RMB). Specifically, higher family monthly income was associated with greater support for euthanasia legalization (Table S3).

Age-specific analysis with euthanasia legalization

The overall analysis revealed that age had slight but significantly effective size on support score of euthanasia legalization. To further explore this relationship, we conducted separate multivariate ordinal regression analyses for different age subgroups. We categorized age to 18–29, 30–44, 45–64, 65–74, 75–84, 85 and above groups based on their life-course dynamics of attitude formation, transformations in social roles, shifting health priorities, and intergenerational value discrepancies [26–28]. The results indicated females were more likely to support euthanasia legalization under 44 years old, while no significant trend was found for females above 44 years old compared to males. Furthermore, individuals affiliate to Christian were more likely to support euthanasia legalization in 30–44, 45–74, and ≥ 85 age groups. Conversely, individuals affiliate to Islam and Buddhism were less likely to support euthanasia legalization among all age groups. Education level indicated that those with higher education level were more likely to support euthanasia legalization in 18–29 age group significantly. Moreover, individuals with higher family monthly income were more likely to support euthanasia legalization in younger age groups under 44. Comparing with individuals majored in medicine, those majored in economics were less likely to support euthanasia legalization in 18–29 age group.

The study also examined the influence of significant life events experienced within the past year on support scores of euthanasia legalization. It indicated individuals went through death of a closed relative were more likely to support euthanasia legalization in age groups of 18–29, 30–44, and 45–64. This trend also obvious for individuals with serious illness themselves in 30–44 age group. Comparing to individuals without chronic disease, those with mental disorders were more likely to support euthanasia legalization in 18–29 age group, while individuals with other chronic diseases besides mental disorder were more likely to support euthanasia legalization in 30–64 age group. For self-reported depression and anxiety status utilizing PHQ-9 and GAD-7 scales, similar trends were found that compared to individuals without depression and anxiety, people with mild depression and anxiety were less likely to support euthanasia legalization, but the opposite trend was observed in individuals with severe depression and anxiety (Fig. 4, Table S4).

Gender-specific analysis with euthanasia legalization

The overall analysis revealed that gender significantly influenced the level of support for euthanasia legalization. To further explore this relationship, we conducted separate multivariate ordinal regression analyses for different gender subgroups (Table S5). The findings indicated that as age increased, the level of support for euthanasia legalization decreased significantly in females. The analysis further revealed interesting differences regarding the influence of chronic diseases on euthanasia legalization support. Among male participants, the presence of mental disorders was associated with a significant increase in support euthanasia legalization. However, this relationship was not statistically significant for female participants. Conversely, among female participants, the presence of mental disorders and other chronic diseases led to a significant increase in support for euthanasia legalization, but this pattern was not observed among male participants. Individuals with other chronic diseases (excluding mental disorders) were more likely to support euthanasia legalization in both groups. Furthermore, for self-reported depression and anxiety status, males who reported severe depression were more likely to support euthanasia legalization, while females living with severe anxiety showed the similar trend.

Religious affiliation also played a crucial role in shaping attitudes toward euthanasia. The results indicated that female participants who adhered to Buddhism and Islam demonstrated significantly lower support for euthanasia legalization compared to those without religious affiliation. But this trend was not obvious in male group. For education level, compared to individuals with no formal education, those with higher education levels (master’s degrees, bachelor’s degrees, junior college, or even high school) exhibited significantly greater support for euthanasia legalization in female group. However, this pattern was not observed among male participants, as their educational level have no significant relationship with support of euthanasia legalization.

The influence of family monthly income also revealed gender differences. Among males, those with a family monthly income higher than 2000 RMB showed a significant increase in their support for euthanasia legalization. In contrast, this association was attenuated among female participants and was only significant in the highest income bracket compared to less than 1000 RMB family monthly income group. Moreover, for other majors compared with medicine, females majored in economics were less likely to support euthanasia legalization, while no significant trend was found in males. Furthermore, the analysis of the impact of recent significant life events revealed no gender differences, the experience of the death of a close relative within the past year was associated with a significant increase in both groups. Chronic diseases (mental disorders): specific analysis with euthanasia legalization

The overall regression analysis revealed that individuals with mental disorders and other chronic diseases exhibited a significant increase in their support level of euthanasia legalization compared to those without any reported chronic diseases. To further explore this relationship, we conducted separate regression analyses based on the participants’ disease status (Table S6). The age variable did not exhibit a significant effect on disease status. Meanwhile, females were more likely to support euthanasia legalization in no disease and other diseases groups. Additionally, no statistically significant differences were observed across the distinct educational levels of individuals with mental disorder and other chronic diseases. But compared to individuals with no formal education, those with master degree, bachelor degree, junior college degree, even high school, were more likely to support euthanasia legalization for individuals without chronic disease. Compared to individuals majored in medicine, participants majored in economics exhibited statistically significant decrease in supporting euthanasia legalization in no chronic disease group.

The variable of family monthly income demonstrated discrete significance. Discussion

In this cross-sectional study, we found that age was significantly associated with the level of support for euthanasia legalization, with the support score decreased as age increased, which was consistent with previous research [29, 30]. Considering odds ratio is very close to 1, which indicated minor effectively size in our large-sample study, so age considered slight influence on the support score of euthanasia legalization. However, the importance of certain small effects may lie in their cumulative nature or their value at a population level. For example, a small average decrease per year, when apply to an entire population, may still reflect an important societal shift in attitudes across generations. This finding may be attributed to a perceived sense of vulnerability or concerns among the elderly in China regarding the quality of care they may receive as they grow older. They are profoundly influenced by multiple factors such as traditional filial piety, the worldview of “valuing life and fearing death”, the family-oriented collective decision-making model, and concerns about potential misuse risks and institutional safeguards in Chinese culture. Previous research had also identified age as a key factor influencing the acceptance of euthanasia legalization. This highlights the importance of developing policies and support systems that address the specific needs and concerns of the elderly population in this context.

The existing literature has consistently demonstrated an association between gender and support for euthanasia legalization [31, 32]. Our findings aligned with prior research, indicating that female participants were more likely to support euthanasia legalization compared to males [33]. From the specific ethical and cultural framework of Chinese society, women’s greater tendency to support euthanasia legalization is not a simple departure from traditional culture but rather a complex choice made based on their roles as primary family caregivers, bearers of emotional labor, and members of a relatively vulnerable social group. These roles driven by empathy, lived experience, and a desire for autonomy. They may reinterpret “filial piety” by emphasizing compassion and relief, voice their call for personal autonomy within the “family-oriented ethos,” and redefine the meaning of a “good death” through their experiences of empathy and caregiving [34, 35]. However, some studies have reported that male medical students and physicians tend to be more inclined to support euthanasia legalization [36].

Moreover, the findings of this study indicated that individuals affiliated with the religious faiths of Buddhism and Islam exhibit a lower propensity to support euthanasia legalization. This observed relationship may be attributable to the core tenets of these religions, which emphasize the universal responsibility to preserve all sentient life, and thus preclude the acceptance of practices that could hasten the death of individuals with the potential to survive. This inverse association between religious affiliation and support for euthanasia legalization is corroborated by prior research, which is further substantiated by the inclusion of a broader range of religious denominations in the current investigation [37–45]. “A good death” is placed a great importance in Chinese culture. Buddhism seeks a peaceful mind at death and rebirth in a higher realm while Islam emphasizes submission to Allah’s will and accepting natural death. Additionally, Confucianism values having children and grandchildren present to fulfill filial duties by accompanying parents in their final moments. These perspectives inherently conflict with the notion of artificially hastening the end of life [46].

This study found that individuals with higher educational level were associated with increased support for euthanasia legalization [29]. This trend may be attributed to the more open-minded attitudes and enhanced ethical awareness fostered by higher education. Furthermore, individuals from families with higher income levels displayed higher acceptance of euthanasia legalization, which may be linked to their increased likelihood of individuals from more affluent backgrounds obtaining higher levels of education [30]. This suggests that higher-income individuals tend to have a more open and favorable attitude towards euthanasia legalization. It also revealed that individuals with educational backgrounds in engineering and economics exhibited lower support for euthanasia legalization, compared to those with medical training. This suggests that individuals majored in medicine exhibited a more favorable and specific attitude towards euthanasia legalization compared to their counterparts in other academic disciplines. Further research will be warranted to elucidate the underlying factors contributing to these observed disciplinary differences in attitudes towards euthanasia legalization.

The findings further demonstrated that the presence of chronic illnesses, as well as the experience of significant life events within the preceding year, were associated with increased levels of support for euthanasia legalization. This aligns with the conclusions drawn from prior studies, which have suggested that the debilitating physical and emotional impact of chronic conditions, including mental disorders may contribute to a heightened desire for euthanasia as a means of relieving suffering [47, 48]. Accordingly, providing enhanced care and support for the daily lives and work of individuals affected by chronic ailments may be a crucial factor in mitigating suicidal ideation and bolstering acceptance of alternative end-of-life options. Notably, some previous research has indicated that mental disorders in particular may be a more salient predictor of support for euthanasia legalization [49]. We also propose that lived experience with psychological pain may foster greater empathy for the desire to escape intractable suffering, and we importantly raise the point that such support might be interpreted as a plea for improved care rather than solely an endorsement of hastened death. In China, mental illness continues to be widely misunderstood and socially marginalized. Individuals often face not only the direct burden of their condition but also prejudice. Such societal attitudes can amplify their distress, often resulting in withdrawal from social contact and a profound loss of self-worth, including feelings of being a burden to others. For some individuals, this leads to the perception that death might be an easier alternative to life.

Our findings must not be misconstrued as devaluing the lives of individuals with mental disorders. We emphasize that the higher level of support for euthanasia legalization within this population should rather be interpreted as a profound expression of unmet needs and a call for better psychosocial support and palliative care options. Consideration of euthanasia in cases of psychological suffering would require exceptional safeguards to ensure true voluntariness and irremediability, far beyond what might be required for terminal physical illnesses.

Therefore, reassuring that any legislative considerations should be careful not to reinforce negative stereotypes about mental illness and understanding the perspective of persons with mental illness to ensure they receive the care they need. We could refer to the relevant legalization from developed countries where multiple medical consultations and independent medical assessment are performed. For example, two independent physicians (usually specialists, including psychiatrists) with no conflict of interest must conduct separate rigorous evaluations of the patient’s condition. We should understand that despite these clause, extremely high rejection rate and prolonged evaluation process always occur. This underscores the critical need to provide enhanced emotional support and care for these population who may be vulnerable in certain situations, which may in turn help mitigate suicidal ideation. Furthermore, the study found that the death of a close relative was also associated with increased support for euthanasia legalization. This finding may be attributed to the direct observation of the pain and suffering experienced by one’s loved ones, which can engender a desire to alleviate such distress and provide a more comfortable end-of-life experience [50–52].

Several limitations of this study should be considered. Firstly, the research was conducted solely in community settings and did not include hospitalized patients, which may constrain the generalizability of the findings. Secondly, the self-reported nature of the disease data raises the possibility that some participants may not have sought verified medical confirmation, potentially introducing uncertainty. Thirdly, as a cross-sectional study, the design precludes the establishment of causal relationships, and instead only permits the examination of relative patterns. Fourthly, admittedly, there may be some inevitable confounding factors. Finally, the study did not investigate public perceptions of how euthanasia policies should be operationalized within healthcare systems. Specifically, we did not explore attitudes toward decision-making frameworks (e.g., who should qualify, what safeguards should exist, or how abuses might be prevented). This omission is critical, as support for euthanasia may depend heavily on trust in regulatory systems, even individuals broadly favoring the option might oppose its implementation if they perceive risks of misuse due to inadequate oversight. These limitations highlight areas for refinement in future research.

While this study does not measure multidimensional aspects of religiosity (e.g., frequency of spiritual practices, beliefs about afterlife, or interpretations of suffering), which prior literature identifies as stronger predictors of end-of-life attitudes than nominal affiliation alone [53]. Future research can incorporate validated religiosity scales to capture this complexity by applying scales of Chinese religiousness. Furthermore, we may consider the question scale in this survey in future studies, considering the detailed categories of mental disorders. Specifically, by incorporating more detailed categories and subtype-specific criteria of mental disorders, such as differentiating between anxiety disorders or mood disorders, we could better capture the nuanced attitudes and experiences unique to each clinical population. Moreover, the single-item measure of euthanasia legalization cannot deconstruct attitudinal dimensions, although it aligns with the preliminary nature of euthanasia awareness in contemporary Chinese society. Therefore, future studies can develop localized multidimensional scales (e.g., adapted EAS-China) for deeper mechanistic analysis.

Conclusions

This cross-sectional study examined the factors influencing support for euthanasia legalization in China. Key characteristics, such as age, gender, religious affiliation, educational level, family income, academic discipline, as well as the presence of chronic illnesses and significant life events, all exerted notable impacts on attitude toward euthanasia legalization. Notably, individuals with mental disorders showed higher acceptance for euthanasia legalization. Overall, the multifaceted findings of this cross-sectional investigation underscore the complex interplay of individual, socioeconomic, and health-related factors in shaping attitudes towards euthanasia legalization in China. Policymakers must reconcile ethical, cultural, and legal complexities to protect vulnerable populations while advancing equitable. Moreover, they may propose dignity-driven frameworks that prioritize mental health support, cultural inclusivity, and safeguards against systemic abuse, such as mandatory psychiatric evaluations, waiting periods, second opinions to ensure thorough consideration in any euthanasia decision.
