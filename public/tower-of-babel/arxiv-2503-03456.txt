Mixed-precision algorithms for solving the Sylvester matrix equation Thanks: Version of August 24, 2026. The work of Andrii Dmytryshyn was supported by the Swedish Research Council (VR) under grant 2021-05393.
Andrii Dmytryshyn, Massimiliano Fasi, Nicholas J. Higham, Xiaobo Liu

Abstract
We consider the solution of the Sylvester equation A ​ X + X ​ B = C AX+XB=C in mixed precision. We derive a new iterative refinement scheme to solve perturbed quasi-triangular Sylvester equations; our rounding error analysis provides sufficient conditions for convergence and a bound on the attainable relative residual. We leverage this iterative scheme to solve the general Sylvester equation. The new algorithms compute the Schur decomposition of the coefficient matrices A A and B B in lower than working precision, use the low-precision Schur factors to obtain an approximate solution to the perturbed quasi-triangular equation, and iteratively refine it to obtain a working-precision solution. In order to solve the original equation to working precision, the unitary Schur factors of the coefficient matrices must be unitary to working precision, but this is not the case if the Schur decomposition is computed in low precision. We propose two effective approaches to address this: one is based on re-orthonormalization in working precision, and the other on explicit inversion of the almost-unitary factors. The two mixed-precision algorithms thus obtained are tested on various Sylvester and Lyapunov equations from the literature. Our numerical experiments show that, for both types of equations, the new algorithms are at least as accurate as existing ones. Our cost analysis, on the other hand, suggests that they would typically be faster than mono-precision alternatives if implemented on hardware that natively supports low precision.

Introduction

A Sylvester matrix equation has the form

AX+XB=C,

(1.1)

where the coefficients
A\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
and
B\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
and the
right-hand side
C\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
are given, whilst
X\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
is the unknown.
The matrix equation (
1.1
) can be recast as the
mn\times mn
linear system

M_{f}\vvec(X)=\vvec(C),\quad M_{f}:=I_{n}\otimes A+B^{T}\otimes I_{m},

(1.2)

where
I_{k}\in\mathbb{C}^{k\times k}
denotes the identity matrix of order
k
,

\otimes
is the infix operator that computes the Kronecker product,
and
\vvec
is the operator that stacks the columns of an
m\times n

matrix into a vector of length
mn
.
The subscript
f
stands for “full” and stresses that the Kronecker matrix in (
1.2
) corresponds to the full Sylvester equation—in later sections we will use the subscript
t
for “triangular” to denote the Kronecker matrix of a triangular equation.
A special case of (
1.1
) is the continuous Lyapunov equation, which has the form

AX+XA^{*}=C.

(1.3)

The matrix equations (
1.1
) and (
1.3
) have both been extensively investigated in the literature, and theoretical results, as well as algorithms and software tools, are available.
This is motivated by the key role that these two equations play in both theory and applications.
Theoretically, they are essential in block diagonalization
[
27
, sect. 7.6.3]
and
perturbation theory for matrix functions
[
33
, sects. 5.1 and 6.1]
.
In applications, they are employed in signal
processing
[
16
]
,
[
47
]
,
system balancing
[
38
]
,
control
[
20
]
,
[
21
]
,
model reduction
[
7
]
,
[
38
]
,

[
44
]
,
[
46
]
,
machine learning
[
19
]
,
numerical methods for matrix functions
[
22
]
,
[
28
]
,
and matrix differential Riccati equations
[
9
]
.
We refer the reader to the survey by Bhatia and Rosenthal
[
14
]
for a historical perspective and a review of theoretical results, and to the work of Simoncini
[
45
]
for a discussion on computational methods.

Given the availability of new mixed-precision hardware, there has been a
renewed interest in mixed-precision algorithms.
Such algorithms have been studied and developed for a number of applications.
For a discussion of mixed-precision methods for numerical linear algebra problems, we refer the reader to the surveys by Abdelfattah et al.
[
1

and by Higham and Mary
[

.
In this work, we design mixed-precision numerical methods for the solution
of (

) and (

The new algorithm are based on the Schur factorization and, therefore, they are mainly of interest when solving equations with dense coefficients of moderate size.
We focus on the mixed-precision framework where

two
floating-point arithmetics are involved.

In the next section, we briefly recall the relevant preliminary results on these matrix equations and summarize the classical algorithms used to solve them.

In

In

In

In
Section

Conclusions are drawn in
Section

As is customary, computed quantities wear a hat in our error analysis.
Many bounds will feature the constants

We denote the spectrum of a square matrix

\Lambda(A)
, and its spectral radius by
\rho(A)

We denote by
\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert

For
A\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}

\lVert A\rVert_{F}:=\biggl(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\lvert a_{ij}\rvert^{2}\biggr)^{1/2},

\lVert A\rVert_{p}:=\max_{x\in\mathbb{C}^{n}}\frac{\lVert Ax\rVert_{p}}{\lVert x\rVert_{p}},\qquad\lVert x\rVert_{p}:=\biggl(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lvert x_{i}\rvert^{p}\biggr)^{1/p},\quad 1\leqslant p<\infty.

(2.2)

\lVert A\rVert_{\infty}:=\lim_{p\to\infty}\lVert A\rVert_{p}=\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\lvert a_{ij}\rvert.

\kappa_{p}(A):=\lVert A\rVert_{p}\cdot\lVert A^{-1}\rVert_{p},

and componentwise condition numbers

\cond(A):=\lVert\lvert A^{-1}\rvert\lvert A\rvert\rVert_{\infty},\quad\cond(A,x):=\frac{\lVert\lvert A^{-1}\rvert\lvert A\rvert\lvert x\rvert\rVert_{\infty}}{\lVert x\rVert_{\infty}},

where the absolute value of a matrix is to be understood componentwise.
Similarly, inequalities between vectors or matrices are to be interpreted componentwise.

2.1
Error analysis

The perturbation analysis by Higham
[
30
]
shows
that, for an approximate solution to (
1.1
), the (normwise) backward
error
[
32
, eq. (16.10)]

can be arbitrarily larger than the (normwise) relative residual, which is
defined by

\frac{\lVert AX+XB-C\rVert}{\lVert C\rVert+\lVert X\rVert\bigl(\lVert A\rVert+\lVert B\rVert\bigr)}.

(2.3)

This is in stark contrast with the case of linear systems, for which
the two quantities are one and the same
[
32
, Chap. 7]
.
For two matrices
A\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
and
B\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
, the separation is defined as

\sep(A,-B):=\min_{\lVert X\rVert\neq 0}\frac{\lVert AX+XB\rVert}{\lVert X\rVert}.

(2.4)

Varah
[
48
]
shows that by taking the Frobenius norm
in (
2.4
) we have

\sep_{F}(A,-B)=\left\lVert(I_{n}\otimes A+B^{T}\otimes I_{m})^{-1}\right\rVert_{2}^{-1}=\sigma_{\min}(I_{n}\otimes A+B^{T}\otimes I_{m}),

which implies that

\lVert X\rVert_{F}\leq\frac{\lVert C\rVert_{F}}{\sep_{F}(A,-B)}.

In our mixed-precision algorithms, the Schur decomposition of
A
and
B
are computed by using the QR algorithm
[
27
, p. 391]
in the low precision
u_{\ell}
. We have

A\approx\widehat{U}_{A}\widehat{T}_{A}\widehat{U}_{A}^{*},\qquad B\approx\widehat{U}_{B}\widehat{T}_{B}\widehat{U}_{B}^{*},

(2.5)

where the computed upper quasi-triangular factors
\widehat{T}_{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
and
\widehat{T}_{B}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
satisfy

\displaystyle U^{*}(A+\varDelta A)U=\widehat{T}_{A},

\displaystyle\lVert\varDelta A\rVert_{2}\approx u_{\ell}\lVert A\rVert_{2},

(2.6)

\displaystyle V^{*}(B+\varDelta B)V=\widehat{T}_{B},

\displaystyle\lVert\varDelta B\rVert_{2}\approx u_{\ell}\lVert B\rVert_{2},

for some unitary matrices
U\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
and
V\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
, and the computed unitary factors
\widehat{U}_{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
and
\widehat{U}_{B}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
satisfy

\left\lVert\widehat{U}_{A}^{*}\widehat{U}_{A}-I_{m}\right\rVert_{2}\approx u_{\ell},\qquad\left\lVert\widehat{U}_{B}^{*}\widehat{U}_{B}-I_{n}\right\rVert_{2}\approx u_{\ell}.

2.2
Numerical algorithms

When working with only one precision, a well-conditioned Sylvester equation can
be solved accurately and efficiently using the algorithm of Bartels and
Stewart
[
6
]
, of which several implementations are available.
The algorithm simplifies in the case of the Lyapunov equation (
1.3
), where the coefficient matrices are adjoints of each other.

We now recall the Bartels–Stewart algorithm and a cheaper variant that can be derived if the coefficients
A
and
B
are Hermitian.

2.2.1
The Bartels–Stewart algorithm

The algorithm requires three steps.
First, we compute the Schur decompositions
A=:U_{A}T_{A}U_{A}^{*}
and

B=:U_{B}T_{B}U_{B}^{*}
, where
T_{A}
and
T_{B}
are upper quasi-triangular
matrices and
U_{A}
and
U_{B}
are unitary matrices.
Next, we multiply (
1.1
) by
U_{A}^{*}
on the left and by
U_{B}
on the right to obtain

T_{A}Y+YT_{B}=\widetilde{C},\qquad\widetilde{C}:=U_{A}^{*}CU_{B},

(2.7)

where the coefficients
T_{A}
and
T_{B}
are upper quasi-triangular.
Finally, we solve (
2.7
) with a recurrence.
The result is obtained by observing that if
Y
satisfies (
2.7
), then
X:=U_{A}YU_{B}^{*}
satisfies (
1.1
).

Computing the Schur decomposition of
A
and
B
requires approximately
25m^{3}
and
25n^{3}
flops
[
27
, p. 391]
, respectively,
2mn(m+n)
flops are needed to compute the updated right-hand side
\widetilde{C}
, solving the Sylvester equation with triangular coefficients requires
mn(m+n)
flops
[
27
, p. 398]
, and recovering the solution involves two matrix multiplications for
2mn(m+n)
additional flops.
Overall, the algorithm requires
25(m^{3}+n^{3})+5mn(m+n)
flops.

For the Lyapunov equation (
1.3
), the algorithm can be simplified, as only one Schur decomposition is needed.
As
m=n
in this case, the Lyapunov equation can be solved with only

35n^{3}
flops if the Bartels–Stewart algorithm is used.

2.2.2
Hermitian matrix coefficients

If the coefficients
A
and

B
in (
1.1
) are both Hermitian, the Bartels–Stewart
algorithm can be simplified—and its computational cost can be
significantly reduced—by exploiting the fact that the quasi-triangular
Schur factor of a Hermitian matrix is diagonal.
In fact, once the decompositions
A=:U_{A}D_{A}U_{A}^{*}
and
B=:U_{B}D_{B}U_{B}^{*}
are computed, solving the matrix equation

D_{A}Y+YD_{B}=\widetilde{C},\qquad\widetilde{C}:=U_{A}^{*}CU_{B},

amounts to applying the formula

Y_{ij}=\frac{\widetilde{C}_{ij}}{(D_{A})_{ii}+(D_{B})_{jj}},

and then retrieving the solution as

X=U_{A}YU_{B}^{*}.

(2.8)

Computing the eigendecomposition of an
n\times n
matrix requires

9n^{3}

flops, determining
Y
requires only
2n^{2}
flops,
and
computing the new right-hand side
\widetilde{C}
and computing
X
as
in (
2.8
) have the same
cost as in the non-Hermitian case.
Therefore, the algorithm for Hermitian matrices asymptotically requires

26n^{3}
flops—the Bartels–Stewart method would need about
60n^{3}
flops when
m=n
.

3
Iterative refinement for perturbed quasi-triangular equations

Consider the perturbed quasi-triangular Sylvester equation

(T_{A}+\varDelta T_{A})Y+Y(T_{B}+\varDelta T_{B})=\widetilde{C},

(3.1)

where
T_{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
and
T_{B}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
are upper quasi-triangular, and

\varDelta T_{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
and
\varDelta T_{B}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
are
unstructured
matrices.
The perturbed Sylvester operator can be split into a dominant term
\mathcal{D}:\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}\mapsto\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
and a perturbation term
\mathcal{P}:\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}\mapsto\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
such that

\mathcal{D}(Y)+\mathcal{P}(Y)=\widetilde{C},\qquad\mathcal{D}(Y):=T_{A}Y+YT_{B},\qquad\mathcal{P}(Y):=\varDelta T_{A}Y+Y\varDelta T_{B}.

Thus, we obtain the fixed‐point iteration

\mathcal{D}(Y_{i+1})=\widetilde{C}-\mathcal{P}(Y_{i}),

where at each step we solve

T_{A}Y_{i+1}+Y_{i+1}T_{B}=\widetilde{C}-(\varDelta T_{A}Y_{i}+Y_{i}\varDelta T_{B}).

Equivalently, by setting
Y_{i+1}:=Y_{i}+D_{i}
and rearranging the terms, we can solve the triangular Sylvester equation

T_{A}D_{i}+D_{i}T_{B}=\widetilde{C}-(T_{A}+\varDelta T_{A})Y_{i}-Y_{i}(T_{B}+\varDelta T_{B})

(3.2)

for the correction
D_{i}
.
Using (
3.2
) repeatedly yields a classical fixed‑point iteration with regular splitting, which represents the foundation of the Alternating Direction Implicit (ADI) method
[
40
]
,
[

]

This assumption on the unstructured perturbation is a consequence of the equivalence between (

) and (

), together with (

are computed in low precision.
As the bound (

to account for the shift of norm.
A sufficient condition for the spectral radius bound in (

. This bound will play a crucial role in our characterization of the forward and backward errors (see

3.3
Normwise forward error analysis

We begin by analyzing the behavior of the forward error of
\widehat{x}_{i}
, the approximated solution produced by

at the

For the computation of

on
Algorithm

3.2
of

[

\widehat{r}_{i}=b-M\widehat{x}_{i}+\varDelta r_{i},\quad\lvert\varDelta r_{i}\rvert\leqslant\gamma_{s+1}^{h}\big(\lvert b\rvert+\lvert M\rvert\lvert\widehat{x}_{i}\rvert\big)\leqslant\gamma_{s+1}^{h}\big(\lvert M\rvert\lvert x-\widehat{x}_{i}\rvert+2\lvert M\rvert\lvert x\rvert\big).

We note that

(M-\varDelta M)^{-1}\widehat{r}_{i}\approx(I+M^{-1}\varDelta M)M^{-1}\widehat{r}_{i}=(I+M^{-1}\varDelta M)(x-\widehat{x}_{i}+M^{-1}\varDelta r_{i}),

where we have used the approximation

(3.10)

Equation (

) is obtained by ignoring higher-order terms in the Neumann expansion of
(I-M^{-1}\varDelta M)^{-1}
, which is guaranteed to converge in view of (
3.7
).
It follows, by using (

), that the solver on
Algorithm

3.2
satisfies

\widehat{d}_{i}-(M-\varDelta M)^{-1}\widehat{r}_{i}=u_{h}G_{i}(I+M^{-1}\varDelta M)(x-\widehat{x}_{i}+M^{-1}\varDelta r_{i}).

(3.11)

By substituting (
3.8
) into (
3.11
), we obtain

\displaystyle\lvert\widehat{d}_{i}-(M-\varDelta M)^{-1}\widehat{r}_{i}\rvert\leqslant

\displaystyle u_{h}\lvert G_{i}\rvert\lvert I+M^{-1}\varDelta M\rvert\big(\lvert\widehat{x}_{i}-x\rvert+\gamma_{s+1}^{h}\lvert M^{-1}\rvert\lvert M\rvert(\lvert x-\widehat{x}_{i}\rvert+2\lvert x\rvert)\big)

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle u_{h}\lvert G_{i}\rvert\lvert I+M^{-1}\varDelta M\rvert(I+\gamma_{s+1}^{h}\lvert M^{-1}\rvert\lvert M\rvert)\lvert\widehat{x}_{i}-x\rvert

\displaystyle+2u_{h}\gamma_{s+1}^{h}\lvert G_{i}\rvert\lvert I+M^{-1}\varDelta M\rvert\lvert M^{-1}\rvert\lvert M\rvert\lvert x\rvert.

(3.12)

Following the modified standard model of floating-point arithmetic
[
32
, eq. (2.5)]
, we see that the solution update on
Algorithm

3.2
satisfies

\widehat{x}_{i+1}=\widehat{x}_{i}+\widehat{d}_{i}+\varDelta{x}_{i},\quad\lvert\varDelta{x}_{i}\rvert\leqslant u_{h}\lvert\widehat{x}_{i+1}\rvert.

(3.13)

By substituting (
3.9
) into (
3.13
), we arrive at

\displaystyle\widehat{x}_{i+1}

\displaystyle=\widehat{x}_{i}+(M-\varDelta M)^{-1}\widehat{r}_{i}+\big(\widehat{d}_{i}-(M-\varDelta M)^{-1}\widehat{r}_{i}+\varDelta{x}_{i}\big)

\displaystyle\approx x+M^{-1}\varDelta r_{i}+M^{-1}\varDelta M(x-\widehat{x}_{i}+M^{-1}\varDelta r_{i})+\big(\widehat{d}_{i}-(M-\varDelta M)^{-1}\widehat{r}_{i}+\varDelta{x}_{i}\big),

(3.14)

and, therefore, from (
3.12
) and (

), we conclude that

\displaystyle\lvert\widehat{x}_{i+1}-x\rvert

\displaystyle\lesssim\lvert M^{-1}\rvert\lvert I+\varDelta MM^{-1}\rvert\cdot\gamma_{s+1}^{h}(\lvert M\rvert\lvert x-\widehat{x}_{i}\rvert+2\lvert M\rvert\lvert x\rvert)+\lvert M^{-1}\varDelta M\rvert\lvert\widehat{x}_{i}-x\rvert

\displaystyle\phantom{\leqslant 3}+u_{h}\lvert G_{i}\rvert\lvert I+M^{-1}\varDelta M\rvert(I+\gamma_{s+1}^{h}\lvert M^{-1}\rvert\lvert M\rvert)\lvert\widehat{x}_{i}-x\rvert

\displaystyle\phantom{\leqslant 3}+2u_{h}\gamma_{s+1}^{h}\lvert G_{i}\rvert\lvert I+M^{-1}\varDelta M\rvert\lvert M^{-1}\rvert\lvert M\rvert\lvert x\rvert+u_{h}\lvert\widehat{x}_{i+1}\rvert

\displaystyle=:F_{i}\lvert\widehat{x}_{i}-x\rvert+f_{i},

where

\displaystyle F_{i}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{s+1}^{h}\lvert M^{-1}\rvert\lvert I+\varDelta MM^{-1}\rvert\lvert M\rvert+\lvert M^{-1}\varDelta M\rvert+u_{h}\lvert G_{i}\rvert\lvert I+M^{-1}\varDelta M\rvert(I+\gamma_{s+1}^{h}\lvert M^{-1}\rvert\lvert M\rvert),

\displaystyle f_{i}

\displaystyle=2\gamma_{s+1}^{h}\bigl(\lvert M^{-1}\rvert\lvert I+\varDelta MM^{-1}\rvert+u_{h}\lvert G_{i}\rvert\lvert I+M^{-1}\varDelta M\rvert\lvert M^{-1}\rvert\bigr)\lvert M\rvert\lvert x\rvert+u_{h}\lvert\widehat{x}_{i+1}\rvert.

To shorten the notation in the equations in this and the following section, we define

\psi_{M}:=1+c_{3}u_{\ell}\kappa_{\infty}(M)

(3.15)

By using the bound
\gamma_{s+1}^{h}\leqslant(s+1)u_{h}
and (
3.7
), it is straightforward to show that

\displaystyle\lVert F_{i}\rVert_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leqslant(u_{h}\lVert G_{i}\rVert_{\infty}+1)\psi_{M}(1+(s+1)u_{h}\cond(M))-1

\displaystyle\less 2\psi_{M}(1+(s+1)u_{h}\cond(M))-1

and that

\displaystyle\lVert f_{i}\rVert_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leqslant 2(s+1)u_{h}\bigl(\psi_{M}+u_{h}\lVert G_{i}\rVert_{\infty}\psi_{M}\bigr)\lVert\lvert M^{-1}\rvert\lvert M\rvert\lvert x\rvert\rVert_{\infty}+u_{h}\lVert\widehat{x}_{i+1}\rVert_{\infty}

\displaystyle\less 4(s+1)u_{h}\psi_{M}\lVert\lvert M^{-1}\rvert\lvert M\rvert\lvert x\rvert\rVert_{\infty}+u_{h}\lVert\widehat{x}_{i+1}\rVert_{\infty}.

.

(

The

3.4
Normwise backward error analysis

Now we turn our focus to the behavior of the normwise backward error, that is, the normwise residual.

Multiplying (

) and then (

\displaystyle M\widehat{x}_{i+1}-b

\displaystyle\approx\varDelta r_{i}+\varDelta M(x-\widehat{x}_{i}+M^{-1}\varDelta r_{i})+M\widehat{d}_{i}-M(M-\varDelta M)^{-1}\widehat{r}_{i}+M\varDelta{x}_{i}

Let

), (

and two invocations of (

\displaystyle\lVert b-M\widehat{x}_{i+1}\rVert_{\infty}\lesssim

\displaystyle\gamma_{s+1}^{h}\big(\lVert b\rVert_{\infty}+\lVert M\rVert_{\infty}\lVert\widehat{x}_{i}\rVert_{\infty}\big)+u_{h}\frac{c_{4}\kappa_{\infty}(M)+c_{2}}{1-c_{4}\kappa_{\infty}(M)u_{h}}\lVert\widehat{r}_{i}\rVert_{\infty}

\displaystyle+u_{h}\lVert M\rVert_{\infty}\lVert\widehat{x}_{i+1}\rVert_{\infty}+c_{3}u_{\ell}\kappa_{\infty}(M)(1+u_{h}\lVert G_{i}\rVert_{\infty})\psi_{M}\lVert\widehat{r}_{i}\rVert_{\infty},

\displaystyle:=u_{h}\frac{c_{4}\kappa_{\infty}(M)+c_{2}}{1-c_{4}\kappa_{\infty}(M)u_{h}}+c_{3}u_{\ell}\kappa_{\infty}(M)(1+u_{h}\lVert G_{i}\rVert_{\infty})\psi_{M},

4
Mixed-precision algorithms for the Sylvester equation

We can use the iterative refinement scheme
solve_pert_sylv_tri_stat
in

With the notation in (
2.5
), let

\widehat{\varDelta A}=A-\widehat{U}_{A}\widehat{T}_{A}\widehat{U}_{A}^{*}\quad\text{ and }\quad\widehat{\varDelta B}=B-\widehat{U}_{B}\widehat{T}_{B}\widehat{U}_{B}^{*}

denote the errors in the Schur decompositions of

and

One might be tempted to use the function
solve_pert_sylv_tri_stat
to solve

\bigl(\widehat{T}_{A}+\widehat{U}_{A}^{*}\widehat{\varDelta A}\widehat{U}_{A}\bigr)Y+Y\bigl(\widehat{T}_{B}+\widehat{U}_{B}^{*}\widehat{\varDelta B}\widehat{U}_{B}\bigr)=\widehat{U}_{A}^{*}C\widehat{U}_{B}

in precision
u_{h}
and then recover the solution of (

) as
X\leftarrow\widehat{U}_{A}Y\widehat{U}_{B}^{*}
.
This would not work, because
\widehat{U}_{A}
and
\widehat{U}_{B}
are only unitary to precision
u_{\ell}
.
To recover the solution to (
1.1
) from a solution to (
2.7
),
U_{A}
and

must the unitary to precision
u_{h}
, and this is not the case if the Schur decomposition is only computed in precision

In

and

The former is based on re-orthonormalization in high precision, the other is based on explicit inversion of the almost-unitary factors.

4.1
Orthonormalization of the unitary factors in high
precision

Algorithm 4.1

(
Orthonormalization-based mixed-precision Sylvester solver.
)
.

Input:

,

,

,

and

in precision

.

3

in precision

.

, computed in precision

.

6

, computed in precision

u_{h}
.

7

, computed in precision
u_{h}
.

8

\widehat{T}_{A}Y_{0}+Y_{0}\widehat{T}_{B}=F

.

9

(

,

,
\widehat{T}_{B}
,

,

in precision

u_{h}

Q_{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
and

be the matrices obtained by

u_{h}
,
\widehat{U}_{A}
and
\widehat{U}_{B}
,

This can be done by using, for example, the modified Gram–Schmidt (MGS) algorithm, so we have
[
32
, Thm. 19.13]
, for some orthogonal matrices (in exact arithmetic)

and

, that

\displaystyle E_{A}:

\displaystyle=Q_{A}-Q_{1},\quad\lVert E_{A}\rVert_{2}\lesssim d_{1}u_{h}\kappa_{2}(\widehat{U}_{A})\approx d_{1}u_{h},

(4.3)

\displaystyle E_{B}:

\displaystyle=Q_{B}-Q_{2},\quad\lVert E_{B}\rVert_{2}\lesssim d_{2}u_{h}\kappa_{2}(\widehat{U}_{B})\approx d_{2}u_{h},

and

\lVert Q_{A}^{*}Q_{A}-I\rVert_{2}\lesssim d_{3}\kappa_{2}(\widehat{U}_{A})u_{h}\approx d_{3}u_{h},\quad\lVert Q_{A}^{*}Q_{A}-I\rVert_{2}\lesssim d_{4}\kappa_{2}(\widehat{U}_{A})u_{h}\approx d_{4}u_{h},

for some constants
d_{1}\equiv d_{1}(m)
,
d_{2}\equiv d_{2}(n)
,
d_{3}\equiv d_{3}(m)
, and
d_{4}\equiv d_{4}(n)
.
We used the MGS algorithm because it admits simple and explicit bounds on the loss of orthogonality, Alternatives such as Householder QR and Cholesky-based QR could also be of interest
[
32
, Chap. 19]
; in practice, Householder QR is often more robust, while Cholesky-based methods can better exploit BLAS-3 operations and be more efficient when the input matrix is nearly unitary.
Note that
\widehat{U}_{A}
and
\widehat{U}_{B}
in (

) are well conditioned in precision
u_{h}
, which can be assumed since they are orthonormal to precision

.

\lVert\widehat{U}_{A}-Q_{A}\rVert_{2}\leqslant\lVert\widehat{U}_{A}-Q\rVert_{2}+\lVert Q-Q_{A}\rVert_{2}\lesssim u_{\ell}+d_{1}u_{h}
, we have

\displaystyle G_{A}:=Q_{A}^{*}\widehat{U}_{A}-I,\quad\lVert G_{A}\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle=\lVert Q_{A}^{*}(\widehat{U}_{A}-Q_{A}+Q_{A})-I\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\lVert Q_{A}^{*}Q_{A}-I\rVert_{2}+\lVert Q_{A}^{*}\rVert_{2}\lVert\widehat{U}_{A}-Q_{A}\rVert_{2}\lesssim u_{\ell}+(d_{1}+d_{3})u_{h}.

(4.4)

Similarly, we can show
\lVert Q_{B}^{*}\widehat{U}_{B}-I\rVert_{2}\lesssim u_{\ell}+(d_{2}+d_{4})u_{h}
.
It requires asymptotically
2m^{3}
or
2n^{3}
flops to compute a matrix
Q_{A}
or
Q_{B}
. Once
Q_{A}
and
Q_{B}
are obtained, we can use

in

Algorithm

to solve in precision

u_{h}
the perturbed equation

\bigl(\widehat{T}_{A}+(Q_{A}^{*}AQ_{A}-\widehat{T}_{A})\bigr)Y+Y\bigl(\widehat{T}_{B}+(Q_{B}^{*}BQ_{B}-\widehat{T}_{B})\bigr)=Q_{A}^{*}CQ_{B},

(4.5)

and then recover the solution as
Q_{A}YQ_{B}^{*}
.
This approach is summarized in

.

To see why
Algorithm

4.1
computes an approximate solution to the Sylvester equation (
1.1
), one first should note that (
4.5
), the equation being solved, is mathematically equivalent to

(Q_{A}^{*}AQ_{A})Y+Y(Q_{B}^{*}BQ_{B})=Q_{A}^{*}CQ_{B}.

(4.6)

On the other hand,
with
Y\equiv Q_{A}^{*}XQ_{B}
(so
X\equiv Q_{A}^{-*}YQ_{B}^{-1}
), the Sylvester equation
(
1.1
)
is mathematically equivalent to

(Q_{A}^{*}AQ_{A}^{-*})Y+Y(Q_{B}^{-1}BQ_{B})=Q_{A}^{*}CQ_{B}.

(4.7)

From (
4.3
) we have

\displaystyle\lVert Q_{2}^{-1}-Q_{B}^{-1}\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle=\lVert Q_{2}^{-1}-(Q_{2}+E_{B})^{-1}\rVert_{2}=\lVert Q_{2}^{-1}(I-(I+E_{B}Q_{2}^{*})^{-1})\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle=\lVert I-(I+E_{B}Q^{*})^{-1}\rVert_{2}=\lVert E_{B}Q_{2}^{*}-(E_{B}Q_{2}^{*})^{2}+\cdots\;\rVert_{2}\approx\lVert E_{B}\rVert_{2}.

and hence

\lVert Q_{B}^{*}-Q_{B}^{-1}\rVert_{2}=\lVert Q_{B}^{*}-Q_{2}^{*}+Q_{2}^{*}-Q_{B}^{-1}\rVert_{2}\leqslant\lVert Q_{B}-Q_{2}\rVert_{2}+\lVert Q_{2}^{-1}-Q_{B}^{-1}\rVert_{2}\approx 2\lVert E_{B}\rVert_{2}\leqslant 2d_{2}u_{h}.

In a similar way we can show that

.

in the coefficient matrices on the left-hand side.

in norm.
The whole process amounts to solving in precision

a nearby equation to (

perturbations in the coefficient matrices and in the solution itself.

\displaystyle\lVert\varDelta T_{A}\rVert

\displaystyle=\lVert(I+G_{A})\widehat{T}_{A}(I+G_{A}^{*})-\widehat{T}_{A}+Q_{A}^{*}\widehat{\varDelta A}Q_{A}\rVert

\displaystyle\lesssim\bigl(\lVert G_{A}\rVert+\lVert G_{A}^{*}\rVert\bigr)\lVert\widehat{T}_{A}\rVert+\kappa(Q_{A})\lVert\widehat{\varDelta A}\rVert\lesssim u_{\ell}\big(2(d_{1}+d_{3}+1)\lVert\widehat{T}_{A}\rVert+\kappa(Q_{A})\lVert A\rVert\big).

The results suggest that, for Sylvester equations, the mixed-precision algorithms can reduce the computational cost by up to 60%, when the cost of low-precision flops becomes negligible. When

6
GMRES-IR for the Sylvester matrix equation

It is possible to solve the Sylvester equation (
1.1
) by applying an iterative algorithm to the equivalent formulation (
1.2
).
In a mixed-precision setting, one might derive a variant of the GMRES-IR algorithms
[
2
]
,
[
17
]
,
[
18
]
tailored to the Sylvester equation.
Good preconditioners are often necessary for such algorithms to be efficient.

In the setting where the Schur decompositions
A=U_{A}T_{A}U_{A}^{*}
and
B=U_{B}T_{B}U_{B}^{*}
are computed in low precision, one can implicitly apply the obvious preconditioner
M_{f}^{-1}=(I_{n}\otimes A+B^{T}\otimes I_{m})^{-1}
, which can yield an efficient algorithm if the GMRES solver converge sufficiently quickly.
However, since applying the preconditioner involves solving Sylvester equations—which must necessarily be done in a precision lower than the working precision for this approach to be computationally sensible—the overall Schur-preconditioned GMRES-IR algorithm only converges for problems whose condition numbers are well bounded, depending on the unit roundoff of the precision at which the preconditioner is formed and applied.
A detailed discussion is beyond the scope of this manuscript.

8
Conclusions

We have derived two algorithms for solving the Sylvester equation using two floating-point precisions.
The main building block is the stationary iteration in
Algorithm

3.1
, which iteratively refines a solution to the perturbed quasi-triangular Sylvester equation.
We have analyzed the convergence of this method and its attainable residual in a two-precision setting.

This iteration can be used, for example, to refine an approximate solution obtained using the quasi-triangular factors of low-precision Schur decompositions, so that it is accurate to high precision.
This observation has allowed us to develop two new approaches to solve the Sylvester and Lyapunov equation in two precisions.
The two algorithms we have proposed leverage either orthonormalization (
Algorithm

4.1
) or inversion (
Algorithm

4.2
) to obtain high-precision unitary matrices that can be used to recover the accurate full solution from the accurate quasi-triangular solution obtained via iterative refinement.

We have proposed a model to compare the flop count of mixed-precision and mono-precision algorithms.
This model is then employed to compare the computational cost of our new algorithms with the cost of the Bartels–Stewart algorithm run entirely in high precision.
Our numerical experiments, run on Sylvester and Lyapunov equations from the literature, show that the accuracy of the new approaches is comparable with that of the high-precision Bartels–Stewart algorithm.
The experiments also suggest that, for Sylvester equations, a performance gain should be expected from high-performance implementations on existing hardware.
Such implementations should target hardware for which a low-precision implementation of the QR algorithm is available, so that the performance gain of low precision can be assessed on real hardware.
For Lyapunov equations, on the other hand, our experiments suggests that a performance gain should only be expected for well-conditioned equations, where one or two iterations are sufficient to achieve convergence.

An open question is the performance of the mixed-precision algorithms on Sylvester equations with unbalanced coefficients (
m\gg n
or
n\gg m
), where the high-precision stationary iteration in
Algorithm

3.1
becomes relatively much cheaper, potentially changing the overall cost landscape.
We will also consider whether extending our approach to more than two precisions has the potential for further acceleration.
Finally, we will examine whether the results discussed here can be applied to T- and
\star
-Sylvester matrix equations
[
25
]
, as well as to systems consisting of T-,
\star
-, and Sylvester matrix equations
[
23
]
,
[
36
]
.
