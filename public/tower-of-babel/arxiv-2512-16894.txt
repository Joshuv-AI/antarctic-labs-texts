Growing Self-Similar Markov Trees
Nicolas Curien, William Fleurat, & Adrianus Twigt

Abstract
Can we obtain a Brownian CRT of mass 1 / 2 1/2 from a CRT of mass 1 1 by cutting certain branches? In this paper, we will answer that question in the much more general setting of self-similar Markov trees. Self-similar Markov trees (ssMt) are random decorated trees that encode the genealogy of a system of particles carrying positive labels, and where particles undergo splitting and growth depending on their labels in a self-similar fashion. Introduced and developed in the recent monograph [ BCR24 ] , they provide a broad generalization of Brownian and stable continuum random trees and arise naturally in various models of random geometry such as the Brownian sphere/disk. The law of a ssMt is characterized by its quadruplet ( a , σ 2 , 𝚲 , α ) (\mathrm{a},\sigma^{2},\boldsymbol{\Lambda};\alpha) , which specifies the features of the underlying growth–fragmentation mechanism, together with the initial decoration x > 0 x>0 . In this work, we focus on special cases of ssMt in which the trees started from different initial values x > 0 x>0 can be coupled into a continuous, increasing family of nested subtrees. In the case of the Brownian and stable continuum random trees, this yields surprisingly simple novel dynamics corresponding to the scaling limit of the leaf-growth algorithms of Łuczak–Winkler and Caraceni–Stauffer.

Introduction

Self-Similar Markov trees.

In a nutshell, self-similar Markov trees (ssMt), are the branching extension of the famous positive self-similar Markov processes (pssMp) of John Lamperti. As for pssMp, it is expected that they are characterized by their Markov and scaling property and that they form all the possible scaling limits of multi-type Galton–Watson trees with types in
\mathbb{Z}_{+}
. They encompass and unify various models such as Aldous’ Brownian Continuum Random tree
[
Ald91
]
and its stable generalizations
[
DL02
]
as well as the Brownian Cactus
[
CLM13
]
sitting inside the Brownian sphere
[
LR20
,
BCK18
]
, or the related scaling limits of peeling or parking trees
[
Ber+18
,
CC25
]
. Formally, a self-similar Markov tree is actually a family of laws
(\mathbb{Q}_{x}:x>0)
of decorated random trees
\mathtt{T}=(T,d_{T},\rho,g)
where
\rho
is the root of the tree and
g:T\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is a decoration which is upper semi-continuous (usc) on
T
and positive on its skeleton. They can be defined through their characteristic quadruplets
(\mathrm{a},\sigma^{2},\boldsymbol{\Lambda};\alpha)
, which through a Lamperti transformation encapsulate the law of their underlying branching Lévy processes. In particular, the so-called generalized Lévy measure
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
is a (possible infinite) measure on the space
\mathcal{S}=\{\mathbf{u}=(u_{0},u_{1},...):u_{0}\in\mathbb{R}\mbox{ and }u_{1}\geqslant u_{2}\geqslant...\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}\}
which describes the splitting probabilities. In this work we shall mainly work with the push-forward
\boldsymbol{\Xi}
of
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
by the exponential map, which is now a (generally infinite) measure on

\mathcal{E}=\exp(\mathcal{S})=\{\mathbf{y}=(y_{0},y_{1},...):y_{0}>0\mbox{ and }y_{1}\geqslant y_{2}\geqslant...\geqslant 0\},

and is called the
splitting measure
. Informally, an individual of decoration
x>0
sees its decoration instantaneously moved to
y_{0}\cdot x
while giving rise to a family of particles with decorations
x\cdot y_{1},x\cdot y_{2},...
(which are interpreted as the birth of new individuals)

\displaystyle x\to(x\cdot y_{0},(x\cdot y_{1},x\cdot y_{2},\cdots))\quad\mbox{ at a rate }\quad x^{-\alpha}\cdot\boldsymbol{\Xi}(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}),

(1)

where
\alpha>0
is the self-similarity parameter. The coefficient
\mathrm{a}\in\mathbb{R}
encodes the drift term while
\sigma^{2}
controls the Brownian part of the evolution of the decoration along branches. See Section
3.1
or
[
BCR24
]
for details. When the starting decoration
x>0
is fixed, the law
\mathbb{Q}_{x}
is the distribution of the ssMt with initial decoration
x
. The self-similarity property of ssMt entails that the law of the rescaled tree
(T,x^{\alpha}\cdot d_{T},\rho,x\cdot g)
under
\mathbb{Q}_{1}
is precisely
\mathbb{Q}_{x}
, but one can wonder whether a tree of law
\mathbb{Q}_{x}
can be obtained by “cutting branches” from a tree of law
\mathbb{Q}_{1}
when
0<x<1
. This natural question is already non-trivial for the Brownian Continuum Random tree (CRT)
[
Ald91
]
where the decoration represents the mass of the fringe subtrees:

Can we obtain a Brownian CRT of mass
1/2
from a CRT of mass
1
by cutting certain branches?

In this paper, we will answer the above question positively and construct in particular continuous and increasing couplings of Brownian and stable CRT and of many more self-similar Markov trees.

1.1
The growing theorem

Growing condition.

The main idea behind our construction is to require a certain property of
(\boldsymbol{\Xi};\alpha)
which amounts heuristically to the following: For any
0<x^{\prime}<x
, one can use the splitting of a particle of decoration
x>0
to emulate the splitting of a particle of smaller decoration
x^{\prime}>0
which we view as “sitting inside” the larger particle. This is done formally by requiring the existence of a family of functions
G_{x}(\cdot):\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{E}
for
x>0
such that

\displaystyle(G_{x})_{\sharp}\ \boldsymbol{\Xi}=x^{-\alpha}\cdot\boldsymbol{\Xi}.

(2)

Recalling (
1
), when such a function exists, one can indeed use the splittings of a particle of decoration
x
, after mapping by
G_{x^{\prime}/x}(\cdot)
, to emulate the splitting of a particle of decoration
x^{\prime}
. In order to keep a geometric meaning to this, we shall furthermore require that when
0<x^{\prime}<x
, the offspring particles of
x^{\prime}
naturally sit inside those of
x
, that is we have coordinate-wise

\displaystyle x\cdot G_{x}(\mathbf{y})\leqslant\mathbf{y},\quad\mbox{ for }x\in(0,1).

(3)

Those conditions are depicted schematically in Figure

Except in the special case where the support of

(Growing self-similar Markov trees, informal)

If the characteristic quadruplet

(i)

(ii)

Monotonicity.
The application

(iii)

(iv)

\mathtt{T}_{x}

x^{\prime}<x

Let us make some comments about the result (see Theorem
3

•

We say above that
\mathtt{T}^{\prime}\subset\mathtt{T}
if the decorated tree
\mathtt{T}^{\prime}
can be realized inside
\mathtt{T}
, formally it means that there exists
g:T\to\mathbb{R}

g^{\prime}\leqslant g
and where
\mathtt{T}^{\prime}
is isomorphic to the hypograph of
g^{\prime}
on the closure of
\{x\in T:g^{\prime}(x)>0\}

g^{\prime}

•

The continuity of the application

holds with respect to the hypograph metric introduced in
[
BCR24
]
which is stronger that Gromov–Hausdorff metric, see Section
3.1
for details.

•

The sequence of points
(p_{x}(i):i\geqslant 0)
is part of the construction and is obtained as the ends of the branches in the gluing construction of
\mathtt{T}_{x}
from the characteristics
(a,\sigma^{2},\boldsymbol{\Xi};\alpha)
, see Section
3.1
for background on the construction of ssMt. In particular, those points depend in a crucial way on the characteristics
(a,\sigma^{2},\boldsymbol{\Xi};\alpha)
and not only on the (law of) the ssMt. Indeed, recall from
[
BCR24
, Chapter 5]
that several characteristic quadruplets may yield to the same law of ssMt.

Two different quadruplets
(\mathrm{a},\sigma^{2},\boldsymbol{\Xi};\alpha)
and
(\mathrm{a}^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime 2},\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\prime};\alpha)
yielding the same laws
(\mathbb{Q}_{x})_{x>0}
are called
bifurcators
of one another. In this case, the splitting measures
\boldsymbol{\Xi}
and
\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\prime}
are simply obtained by choosing another particle to follow in some splitting event, and they coincide up to an event of finite measure. The remaining parameters
\mathrm{a},\mathrm{a}^{\prime},\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}
are simple functions of one-another, see
[
BCR24
, Theorem 5.13]
for details. Perhaps surprisingly, different bifurcators will yield to different growing mechanisms for the same ssMt! The worried reader may first concentrate on the so-called
locally largest bifurcator
which consists in following the largest particle at each splitting event. In this case, the splitting measure
\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\ell\ell}
is supported on
\mathcal{E}^{\downarrow}:=\{y_{0}\geqslant y_{1}\geqslant y_{2}\geqslant\cdots\geqslant 0\}
. See
[
BCR24
, Remark 1.20]
. In the case of the locally largest bifurcator, our growing theorem is intrinsic, i.e. the growing mechanism only depends on the geometry of the decorated tree (whereas in general it depends on the way to explore it using bifurcators).

The Brownian CRT

As a first application of our main result Theorem

As mentioned above, the Brownian CRT decorated with the mass of fringe subtrees can be obtained as the ssMt with characteristics

1.4
Analytical conditions and generators

The previous examples of growing characteristic quadruplets were eventually relying on a (magical) explicit and simple computation. To check if
(\boldsymbol{\Xi};\alpha)
is growing, one should
a priori
find the family of function
(G_{x}:x>0)
satisfying the conditions of Definition
2.1

(G_{x}:x>0)
can generically be described by a single function: if we denote by
\mathtt{G}_{t}=G_{\mathrm{e}^{-t}}
then in smooth situations
\mathtt{G}
is the flow of solution of a (infinite dimensional) differential equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathtt{G}_{t}=\mathtt{V}(\mathtt{G}_{t}),\quad\mbox{with}\quad\mathtt{G}_{0}=\mathrm{Id},

(9)

where the operator
\mathtt{V}:\mathcal{E}\to\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
is called the
generator
. The monotonicity condition (
3
) is then easily re-interpreted in terms of the generator and amounts to require that
\mathtt{V}\leqslant\mathrm{Id}
. Since
\mathtt{G}
is the flow of solutions to (
9
), the measures
\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{t}=(\mathtt{G}_{t})_{\sharp}\,\boldsymbol{\Xi}
are obtained by moving the mass of
\boldsymbol{\Xi}
along the vector field
\mathtt{V}
. In PDE theory, under reasonable regularity conditions, the evolution of
(\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{t})_{t}
is then dictated by the
continuity equation
:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{t}+\mathrm{div}(\mathtt{V}\,\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{t})=0,

understood by duality against test functions, see
[
AGS05
]
. In our setting, we shall then require that
\mathtt{V}
obey the divergence PDE

\displaystyle\mathrm{div}(\mathtt{V}\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{t})=-\alpha\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{t}

(10)

so that the preceding (measure-valued) ODE becomes
\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{t}=\alpha\,\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{t}
and thus
\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{t}=\mathrm{e}^{\alpha t}\,\boldsymbol{\Xi}
for
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

In particular, when the support of
\boldsymbol{\Xi}
is one-dimensional, then the above differential equation fixes the generator and we have at most one growing family of functions for
(\boldsymbol{\Xi};\alpha)
. This uniqueness is lost in the multi-dimensional case, see Example
13
. These analytical conditions on the generator are much easier to check and enables us to compute
\alpha_{c}(\boldsymbol{\Xi})
in many situations, although the family
G
extracted from (
9
) is usually not explicit. As an example, for
\gamma>0
consider the binary conservative locally largest splitting measures

\int F({y_{0}},{y_{1}},...)\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\ell\ell}_{\gamma\text{-bin}}(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y})\propto\int_{1/2}^{1}\frac{\mathrm{d}s}{(s(1-s))^{\gamma}}F(s,1-s,0,0,0...).

In this case the generator is explicit in terms of Euler’s incomplete beta-function and
\alpha_{c}(\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\ell\ell}_{\gamma\text{-bin}})\equiv\alpha_{c}(\gamma)
can be computed: When
\gamma=3/2
we have already seen that
\alpha_{c}(3/2)=1/2
so that the growing ability of the Brownian CRT is a critical case. When
\gamma=\frac{5}{2}
, which corresponds to the generalized Lévy measure of the Brownian growth-fragmentation tree
[
BCR24
, Chapter 3, Example 3.11]
, then
\alpha_{\mathrm{c}}(5/2)
is the solution
x\approx 1.21
of the equation

256-162x-42x^{2}+x^{3}=0.

In particular, if
\alpha=3/2
then the associated ssMt is not growing, whereas it is growing if the self-similarity parameter is
\alpha=1/2
. Interestingly, the ssMt corresponds in this case with the Brownian Cactus tree sitting inside the Brownian disk,
[
CLM13
,
BCK18
,
LR20
]
. Various other examples are discussed in Section
4
and
5
.

2
Growing pssMp

In this section we show that under the growing condition it is possible to couple positive self-similar Markov processes, and their extensions the decoration-reproduction processes, which serves as the basis in the construction of the ssMt. The main idea is to use a construction of pssMp via stochastic differential equations with jumps. Before we start the construction of our coupling, let us first recall the construction of those objects from
[
BCR24
]
.

2.1
Decoration-reproduction processes

We provide a shortened version of some of the essential definitions and constructions which we will use. For this, we use the same notation as in Section 2.2 of
[
BCR24
]
to which we refer for details.

Let us consider the space
\mathcal{S}
of real-numbered sequences non-increasing after the first term
\mathbf{u}=(u_{0}\in\mathbb{R},(u_{1}\geqslant u_{2}\geqslant\dots\geqslant-\infty))
with
\lim_{i\to\infty}u_{i}=-\infty
.
Applying the exponential function to all elements, we get the space
\mathcal{E}=\exp(\mathcal{S})
made of sequences starting with a positive term, then non-increasing tending to
0
.

Borrowing the supremum norm induces a distance on
\mathcal{E}
that makes it Polish. Next, we consider a
generalized Lévy measure

\boldsymbol{\Lambda}=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{u})=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(\mathrm{d}u_{0}\mathrm{d}u_{1}...)
on
\mathcal{S}
and we denote
\Lambda_{0}
and
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{1}
the push-forward images by the projection on the first coordinate
(u_{0},u_{1},...)\mapsto u_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
and by the projection on the remaining coordinates
(u_{0},u_{1},...)\mapsto(u_{1},u_{2},...)
. The push-forward of
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
on
\mathcal{E}
by the exponential is denoted by
\boldsymbol{\Xi}
and called
the splitting measure
. Denote equally
\Xi_{0}
and
\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{1}
the push-forward measures on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
\mathcal{E}_{1}
of
\Lambda_{0}
and
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{1}
respectively. We suppose that
\boldsymbol{\Xi}
satisfies the conditions

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(1\wedge u^{2})\Lambda_{0}(\mathrm{d}u)<\infty\hskip 5.69054pt\text{ and }\hskip 5.69054pt\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{1}\big(\{(y_{1},y_{2},\dots)\in\mathcal{E}^{\downarrow}:y_{1}>\varepsilon\}\big)<\infty\hskip 8.53581pt\text{ for all }\varepsilon>0.

Furthermore, to be able to compensate all jumps and to get more compact formulas, we suppose in this paper that

\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}(y_{0}-1)^{2}\,\Xi_{0}(\mathrm{d}y_{0})<+\infty

(11)

We stress that for Theorem
2
and Theorem
3
to hold, condition (
11
) is superfluous. The changes that need to be implemented are discussed in Section
6.1
.

Next, consider a quadruplet
(a,\sigma^{2},\boldsymbol{\Lambda};\alpha)
, with
a\in\mathbb{R}
a drift coefficient,
\sigma^{2}\geqslant 0
a diffusion coefficient,
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
a generalized Lévy measure and
\alpha>0
the index of self-similarity. We call
(a,\sigma^{2},\boldsymbol{\Lambda};\alpha)
the
characteristic quadruplet
. Notice that compared to
[
BCR24
]
, we impose here that the killing rate
\mathtt{k}:=\Lambda_{0}(\{-\infty\})
is zero. See Section
6.2
for a discussion on it. In the language of
[
BCR24
, Assumption 2.8]
, the corresponding
cumulant function
is defined by

\displaystyle\kappa(\gamma)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\gamma^{2}+\mathrm{a}\gamma+\int_{\mathcal{S}}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{u})\left(\mathrm{e}^{\gamma u_{0}}-1-\gamma u_{0}\mathbf{1}_{|u_{0}|\leqslant 1}+\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{\gamma u_{i}}\right),

(12)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\gamma^{2}+\mathrm{a}\gamma+\int_{\mathcal{E}}\boldsymbol{\Xi}(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y})\left(y_{0}^{\gamma}-1-\gamma\log y_{0}\mathbf{1}_{|\log y_{0}|\leqslant 1}+\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}y_{i}^{\gamma}\right),\qquad\gamma>0.

Its support
\mathrm{supp}(\kappa)
is the set of
\gamma>0
such that
\kappa(\gamma)<\infty
.
We shall henceforth suppose that we are in the
(sub)critical
case, that is there exists
0<\gamma_{0}<\gamma_{1}<\infty
such that
\kappa(\gamma_{1})<\infty
and

\displaystyle\kappa(\gamma_{0})\leqslant 0.

(13)

Equipped with this quadruplet, let
(W_{s})_{s\geqslant 0}
be a standard Brownian motion and
\textbf{N}:=\textbf{N}(\mathrm{d}t,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{u})
a Poisson random measure on
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathcal{S}
with intensity measure
\mathrm{d}t\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{u})
.
We suppose that
W
and
N
are independent. We denote
\textbf{N}_{0}
the first projection of
N
onto
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}
, which, by the mapping theorem, is a Poisson point process with intensity
\mathbbm{1}_{u\in\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{d}t\Lambda_{0}(\mathrm{d}u)
. Denote also

\tilde{\textbf{N}}_{0}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}u)=\textbf{N}_{0}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}u)-\mathrm{d}s\Lambda_{0}(\mathrm{d}u)

the compensated Poisson measure. Then, for
t\in[0,\infty)
, we define the Lévy process

\xi_{t}=at+\sigma B_{t}+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{|u|>1}u\hskip 2.84526pt\textbf{N}_{0}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}u)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{|u|\leqslant 1}u\hskip 2.84526pt\tilde{\textbf{N}}_{0}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}u),

and its shifted version
\xi^{(x)}:=\xi+\log x
. In particular, the Lévy–Khintichine exponent of
\xi
is given by
\mathbb{E}[\exp(\gamma\xi_{t})]=\exp(t\psi(\gamma))
where

\displaystyle\psi(\gamma)=\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\gamma^{2}+\mathrm{a}\gamma+\int_{\mathbb{R}_{*}}\big(\mathrm{e}^{\gamma u}-1-\gamma u\mathbbm{1}_{|u|\leqslant 1}\big)\Lambda_{0}(\mathrm{d}u).

(14)

We now introduce the necessary definitions and quantities for the Lamperti transformation. Consider the following exponential functionals:

\vartheta(t):=\int_{0}^{t}\exp{(\alpha\xi_{s})}\mathrm{d}s\hskip 14.22636pt\text{for }0\leqslant t<\infty,\hskip 14.22636ptz:=\vartheta(\infty)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp{(\alpha\xi_{s})}\mathrm{d}s<\infty\hskip 8.53581pt\text{a.s.}

Noting that
\vartheta:[0,\infty]\to[0,z]
is an increasing bijection a.s., we can define
\theta
as the reciprocal bijection, which gives

\int_{0}^{\theta_{t}}\exp{(\alpha\xi_{s})}\mathrm{d}s=t,\hskip 14.22636pt\text{ for all }0\leqslant t<z.

Then the famous Lamperti transformation of
\xi
,

X_{t}=\exp{(\xi(\theta_{s}))},\hskip 14.22636pt\text{for all }0\leqslant t<z,

is both Markovian and self-similar with exponent
\alpha>0
. More precisely,
X_{0}=1
and for every
x>0
, the rescaled process

\tilde{X}_{t}:=xX_{x^{-\alpha}t},\hskip 14.22636pt\text{for all }0\leqslant t<x^{\alpha}\cdot z,

is a version of
X
started from
x>0
, meaning that the law of
\tilde{X}
equals that of the the Lamperti transformation of the shifted Lévy process
\xi^{(x)}
. The lifetime
z
of
X
is finite almost-surely iff
\lim_{t\to\infty}\xi_{t}=-\infty
almost surely, which is always the case in the subcritical case (
13
) which we supposed. We then manually set
X_{t}:=0
for all
t\geqslant z
, so that
X
is a positive process on the segment
[0,z]
and killed/ absorbed at
0
at time
z
. The quadruplet
(\mathrm{a},\sigma^{2},\Lambda_{0};\alpha)
will be called the
characteristics
of
X
in what follows.

Next, we define the reproduction process
\eta
. First, we define
\hat{\eta}
on
[0,\infty)
. The second projection of
N
is denoted by
\textbf{N}_{1}:=\textbf{N}_{1}(\mathrm{d}t,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{u})
. We then
expand
each atom
(s,\mathbf{u})
as a sequence
(s,u_{i})_{i\geqslant 1}
in
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times[-\infty,\infty)
. Define the point process
\hat{\eta}
on
[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
as

\hat{\eta}:=\sum_{(s,\mathbf{u})\in\textbf{N}_{1}}\sum_{i\geqslant 1}\delta_{(s,u_{i})},

which, after the push-forward by the Lamperti time change, yields

\eta:=\sum_{(s,\mathbf{u})\in\textbf{N}_{1}}\sum_{i\geqslant 1}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\vartheta(s)<z\}}\delta_{(\vartheta(s),X_{\vartheta(s)-}\cdot\exp{(u_{i})})},

making it a point process on
[0,z)\times[0,\infty)
.

We define the law of the couple
(X,\eta)
as
P=P_{1}
and for all
x>0
, we denote
P_{x}
the law of the transformed process
(X^{(x)},\eta^{(x)})
, where the scaling for
\eta
is done by transforming the atoms
\delta_{(s,u)}
into
\delta_{(x^{\alpha}s,xu)}
. The kernel
(P_{x})_{x>0}
is called the
self-similar Markov decoration-reproduction kernel
associated to the characteristic quadruplet
(a,\sigma^{2},\boldsymbol{\Lambda};\alpha)
.

2.2
Growing hypothesis

We make here precise what we mean by the growing assumption.
Fix a splitting measure
\boldsymbol{\Xi}
and a self-similarity parameter
\alpha>0
. Recall that we supposed that
\boldsymbol{\Xi}
has no killing term
\Xi_{0}(\{{0}\})=0
.

Definition 2.1

(
G
-Growing)
.

We say that
(\boldsymbol{\Xi};\alpha)
is growing if there exists a measurable
\mathcal{E}_{0}\subset\mathcal{E}
with
\boldsymbol{\Xi}(\mathcal{E}\setminus\mathcal{E}_{0})=0
, and a family
G=(G_{x}:x>0)
of mappings
\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{E}
verifying the following assumptions:

•

Semi-group property.
The function
G_{1}
is the identity and for all
x,x^{\prime}>0
we have

G_{x}\circ G_{x^{\prime}}=G_{x\cdot x^{\prime}}.

•

Quasi-preservation of the measure.
For all
\phi\in C_{b}(\mathcal{E})
, the set of continuous bounded functions from
\mathcal{E}
to
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, and for all
x>0
, we have

\int_{\mathcal{E}}\phi\left(G_{x}(\mathbf{y})\right)\boldsymbol{\Xi}(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y})=x^{-\alpha}\int_{\mathcal{E}}\phi\left(\mathbf{y}\right)\boldsymbol{\Xi}(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}).

(15)

•

Monotonicity.
For each
\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{E}_{0}
the map

\displaystyle x\mapsto x\cdot G_{x}(\mathbf{y})\quad\mbox{is increasing (coordinate wise)},

(16)

so that in particular for all
x\in(0,1]
, we have
x\cdot G_{x}(\mathbf{y})\leqslant\mathbf{y}
.

•

Regularity of
G
.
The mapping
(x,\mathbf{y})\mapsto G_{x}(\mathbf{y})
is continuous from
(0,\infty)\times\mathcal{E}_{0}
to
\mathcal{E}_{0}
when
\mathcal{E}_{0}\subset\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\mathbb{N}}
is equipped with the product topology. Moreover, if
G^{(0)}
is the first coordinate of
G
, then for every compact set
K\subset(0,\infty)
, there exists a constant
C_{K}>0
such that for all
x,x^{\prime}\in K
,

\int_{\mathcal{E}}\left(x\left(G_{x}^{(0)}(\mathbf{y})-1\right)-x^{\prime}\left(G_{x^{\prime}}^{(0)}(\mathbf{y})-1\right)\right)^{2}\boldsymbol{\Xi}(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y})\leqslant C_{K}\cdot(x-x^{\prime})^{2}.

(17)

When
(\boldsymbol{\Xi};\alpha)
is growing for some specific family
G
, we say that it is
G
-growing.

Let us make a few comments on this definition:

•

Dependence in
\alpha
.
The existence of
G_{x}
satisfying the semi-group and the quasi-preservation of the measure is true in most of the cases, however the monotonicity assumption generally puts a constraint on the self-similarity parameter
\alpha
in function of
\boldsymbol{\Xi}
.
Lemma
1
and
2
below give quantitative restrictions on the values that
\alpha
can take and show it must be an interval, implying that there must be a maximal
\alpha_{c}
for which
(\boldsymbol{\Xi};\alpha)
is growing.

•

Atoms.
Note that the quasi-preservation of the measure forces the function
G_{x}
to “continuously shift” the mass of the splitting measure
\boldsymbol{\Xi}
, for different values of
x>0
. In particular, such a family of functions cannot exists if
\boldsymbol{\Xi}
has an atom in
\mathcal{E}
. See Section
6.2
for a discussion about the re-incorporation of a killing term.

•

Infinite intensity.
For
(\boldsymbol{\Xi};\alpha)
to grow, one must necessarily have
\boldsymbol{\Xi}(\mathcal{E})=\infty
thus excluding the finite intensity ssMt.

•

Interpretation when
x>1
.
The “geometric” interpretation made in Figure
2
of
G_{x}
when
x>1
does not hold anymore. However, if
G_{x}
is defined for all
x\in(0,1)
, we can just set
G_{x^{-1}}=(G_{x})^{-1}
which is well defined on the support of
\boldsymbol{\Xi}
since
G_{x}
is supposed to be bijective on the support of
\boldsymbol{\Xi}
.

•

Weaker assumption.
Whenever condition (
11
) does not hold, the condition (
17
) needs to be weakened by replacing
\mathcal{E}
with the set
\mathcal{E}_{0}=\{(y_{0},\mathbf{y})\in\mathcal{E}:y_{0}\in[e^{-1},e]\}
. We refer to Section
6.1
to see what changes need to be made in the proof of Theorem
2
under this weaker assumption.

Lemma 1

(Upper bound on
\alpha
)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\Xi}
be a splitting measure such that
\boldsymbol{\Xi}(\{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{E}:y_{1}=0\})=0

and let
\alpha>0
. Then, for the pair
(\boldsymbol{\Xi};\alpha)
to be
G
-growing, it is necessary that

\displaystyle\alpha\leqslant\inf\left\{\gamma>0\colon\int_{\mathcal{E}}y_{1}^{\gamma}\,\boldsymbol{\Xi}\bigl(\mathrm{d}(y_{0},y_{1},\dots)\bigr)<\infty\right\}.

(18)

In particular, if
(\mathrm{a},\sigma^{2},\boldsymbol{\Xi};\alpha)
is a non-trivial characteristic quadruplet such that
(\boldsymbol{\Xi};\alpha)
is
G
-growing, then
\alpha
is at most the infimum of the support of the corresponding cumulant function
\kappa
defined in (
12
).

Proof.

Suppose that
(\boldsymbol{\Xi};\alpha)
is
G
-growing, and let
(G_{x},x>0)
be a family of mappings
\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{E}
satisfying the conditions in Definition
2.1
.
For
z>0
, we let:

\displaystyle{E}_{z}=\left\{\mathbf{y}=(y_{0},y_{1},\dots)\in\mathcal{E}\colon y_{1}\geqslant z\right\}.

The assumption on the support of
\boldsymbol{\Xi}
gives that there is some
z_{0}>0
such that
\boldsymbol{\Xi}(E_{z_{0}})>0
.
The mapping
z\mapsto z\cdot{G_{z}(\mathbf{y})}
is coordinate-wise non-decreasing, so that for all
z\geqslant z_{0}
we have
G_{z}(E_{z_{0}})\subset E_{z_{0}/z}
, and thus
E_{z_{0}}\subset G_{z}^{-1}(E_{z_{0}/z})
. Hence, by the “quasi-preservation of the measure”, we have the inequality

\boldsymbol{\Xi}(E_{z_{0}})\leqslant\boldsymbol{\Xi}\bigl(G_{z}^{-1}(E_{z_{0}/z})\bigr)=z^{-\alpha}\boldsymbol{\Xi}(E_{z_{0}/z}).

In terms of
x=z_{0}/z\in(0,1]
, this entails that:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Xi}(E_{x})\geqslant C_{0}x^{-\alpha},\qquad x\in(0,1],

where
C_{0}:=z_{0}^{\alpha}\boldsymbol{\Xi}(E_{z_{0}})
is positive since
\boldsymbol{\Xi}(E_{z_{0}})>0
.
This estimate suffices to conclude. Indeed, for all
\gamma>0
, we have by the Fubini–Tonnelli theorem and the preceding estimate:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{E}}\boldsymbol{\Xi}\bigl(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\bigr)\,y_{1}^{\gamma}=\int_{\mathcal{E}}\boldsymbol{\Xi}\bigl(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\bigr)\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}x\,\mathbf{1}_{\{x\leqslant y_{1}^{\gamma}\}}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\boldsymbol{\Xi}\bigl(E_{x^{1/\gamma}}\bigr)\mathrm{d}x\geqslant\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}x\,C_{0}x^{-\alpha/\gamma}.

Since
C_{0}>0
, if the left-hand side is finite, then we have
\alpha<\gamma
. The conclusion follows. The consequence in terms of the support of
\kappa
is immediate from the definition (
12
).
∎

Lemma 2

(Critical self-similarity)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\Xi}
be a splitting measure. Then the set of
\alpha>0
such that
(\boldsymbol{\Xi};\alpha)
is
G
-growing is either empty, or an interval of the form
(0,\alpha_{c})
or
(0,\alpha_{c}]
for some
\alpha_{c}=\alpha_{c}(\boldsymbol{\Xi})>0
.

Proof.

It suffices to show that if
(\boldsymbol{\Xi};\beta)
is
G
-growing, then so is
(\boldsymbol{\Xi};\alpha)
when
0\leqslant\alpha\leqslant\beta
.
We therefore assume the former and let
(G_{x},x>0)
be a family of mappings
\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{E}
satisfying the conditions of Definition
2.1
for the pair
(\boldsymbol{\Xi};\beta)
. We set:

\displaystyle\widetilde{G}_{x}=G_{x^{\alpha/\beta}},\qquad x>0.

It is then straightforward to check that
(\widetilde{G}_{x},x>0)
satisfies the conditions of Definition
2.1
for the pair
(\boldsymbol{\Xi};\alpha)
. Indeed, the semi-group property and “quasi-preservation of the measure” are readily verified. The monotonocity condition follows by observing that for all
\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{E}
, the mapping
x\mapsto x\cdot{\widetilde{G}_{x}(\mathbf{y})}
is the product of the non-decreasing function
x\mapsto x^{1-\alpha/\beta}
with the coordinate-wise non-decreasing mapping
x\mapsto x^{\alpha/\beta}\cdot{G_{x^{\alpha/\beta}}(\mathbf{y})}
.
Lastly, we check (
17
). Let
0<z\leqslant x
in a compact
K\subset(0,+\infty)
. Then a straightforward calculation shows, using a varying constant
C_{K}
,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{E}}\left(x\left(\tilde{G}_{x}^{(0)}(\mathbf{y})-1\right)-z\left(\tilde{G}_{z}^{(0)}(\mathbf{y})-1\right)\right)^{2}\boldsymbol{\Xi}(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle\leqslant C_{K}\int_{\mathcal{E}}\left(x^{\alpha/\beta}\left(G_{x^{\alpha/\beta}}^{(0)}(\mathbf{y})-1\right)-z^{\alpha/\beta}\left(\tilde{G}_{z^{\alpha/\beta}}^{(0)}(\mathbf{y})-1\right)\right)^{2}\boldsymbol{\Xi}(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle+C_{K}\int_{\mathcal{E}}\left(z^{\alpha/\beta}-x^{(\alpha/\beta)-1}z\right)^{2}\left(G_{x^{\alpha/\beta}}^{(0)}(\mathbf{y})-1\right)^{2}\boldsymbol{\Xi}(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle\leqslant C_{K}\left(x^{\alpha/\beta}-z^{\alpha/\beta}\right)^{2}+C_{K}\left(z-x\right)^{2}\int_{\mathcal{E}}(y_{0}-1)^{2}\Xi_{0}(\mathrm{d}y_{0})\leqslant C_{K}(z-x)^{2},

where we used that
x,z\in K
in the first inequality and conditions (
17
) and (
11
) in the last inequality. The last condition for
(\boldsymbol{\Xi};\alpha)
to be
G
-growing is thus verified.
∎

Remark
.

We expect that in most reasonable cases, the set of self-similarity parameters described in Lemma
2
is in fact of the form
(0,\alpha_{c}]
, by taking suitable limits of families
(G_{x}:x>0)
satisfying the conditions of Definition
2.1
for pairs
(\boldsymbol{\Xi};\alpha_{n})
with
\alpha_{n}\to\alpha_{c}
from below.

2.3
Coupling decoration-reproduction processes

We now present the technical crux of the paper which consists in a pssMp version of Theorem
1
:

Theorem 2

(Baby version of Theorem
1
for a single branch)
.

Let
(\mathrm{a},\sigma^{2},\boldsymbol{\Xi};\alpha)
be a characteristic quadruplet with subcritical cumulant
\kappa
and without killing such that
(\boldsymbol{\Xi};\alpha)
is
G
-growing. Then, on a common probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
, one can construct a family of decoration-reproduction processes

\left\{\left(X^{(x)},\eta^{(x)}\right):X_{0}^{(x)}=x\right\}_{x>0}\quad\mbox{absorbed at $0$ at time $z^{(x)}$ }

for which the following properties hold
\mathbb{P}
-almost surely:

(i)

(
Law
P_{x}
) For every
x>0
, the process
\left((X_{t}^{(x)})_{t\geqslant 0},\eta^{(x)}\right)
has law
P_{x}
.

(ii)

(
Monotonicity
) For every
0<x^{\prime}\leqslant x
, and for all
t\geqslant 0
,

X^{(x^{\prime})}_{t}\leqslant X^{(x)}_{t}.

(iii)

(
Synchronized and monotone reproduction
) For every
\,0<x^{\prime}\leqslant x
and for all
t\geqslant 0
, if
X^{(x^{\prime})}_{t-}>0
, then
\Delta X^{(x^{\prime})}_{t}>0\iff\Delta X^{(x)}_{t}>0
, i.e. processes have the same jump times, whenever both are positive. Also, for every unexpanded atom
\left(s,\mathbf{y}^{(x^{\prime})}\right)
of
\eta^{(x^{\prime})}
, there is exactly one corresponding atom
\left(s,\mathbf{y}^{(x)}\right)
of
\eta^{(x)}
, and we have coordinate-wise,

\mathbf{y}^{(x^{\prime})}\leqslant\mathbf{y}^{(x)}

This means that at every birth event, the labels of all the children of the process
\left(X^{(x^{\prime})},\eta^{(x^{\prime})}\right)
are always smaller than those of the corresponding children of
\left(X^{(x)},\eta^{(x)}\right)
.

(iv)

(
Continuity
) The process

The high-level idea of the proof is to represent the decoration (and reproduction) processes

as flows to a stochastic differential equation using a common Brownian motion and Poisson measure. The fact that pssMp can be written as solution to SDEs is well-known, see in particular the recent work of Döring & Barczy

who proved the existence and uniqueness of pathwise strong solutions to an SDE yielding quite general pssMp. They use it to (re)start the pssMp at zero, which is a well-known subtle problem. The SDE they used to construct a pssMp

with characteristics

\displaystyle X_{t}=x+\mathrm{a}\int_{0}^{t}X_{s}^{1-\alpha}\mathrm{d}s+\sigma\int_{0}^{t}X_{s}^{1-\alpha/2}\mathrm{d}B_{s}

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{|u|>1}\mathbbm{1}_{\{rX_{s-}^{\alpha}\leqslant 1\}}X_{s-}(e^{u}-1)\mathcal{N}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}r,\mathrm{d}u)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{|u|\leqslant 1}\mathbbm{1}_{\{rX_{s-}^{\alpha}\leqslant 1\}}X_{s-}(e^{u}-1)\tilde{\mathcal{N}}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}r,\mathrm{d}u)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbbm{1}_{\{rX_{s-}^{\alpha}\leqslant 1\}}X_{s-}\hskip 2.84526pt\mathcal{M}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}r),

Here

\displaystyle\left(X^{(x^{\prime})}-X^{(x)}\right)_{\tilde{t}}^{+}=(x^{\prime}-x)^{+}+\int_{0}^{\tilde{t}}\mathbbm{1}_{X^{(x^{\prime})}_{s}>X^{(x)}_{s}}\left(\beta(X^{(x^{\prime})}_{s})-\beta(X^{(x)}_{s})\right)\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{\tilde{t}}\int_{\mathcal{E}}\left(X^{(x^{\prime})}_{s-}-X^{(x)}_{s-}+h(X^{(x^{\prime})}_{s-},u)-h(X^{(x)}_{s-},u)\right)^{+}-\mathbbm{1}_{X^{(x^{\prime})}_{s}>X^{(x)}_{s}}\left(X^{(x^{\prime})}_{s-}-X^{(x)}_{s-}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 42.67912pt-\mathbbm{1}_{X^{(x^{\prime})}_{s}>X^{(x)}_{s}}\left(h(X^{(x^{\prime})}_{s-},u)-h(X^{(x)}_{s-},u)\right)\mathcal{N}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}u).

4.1
On bifurcators

Recall from
[
BCR24
, Section 5.3]
that two splitting measures
\boldsymbol{\Xi}
and
\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\prime}
are bifurcators of one another if

\displaystyle\mathrm{ord}_{\sharp}\,\boldsymbol{\Xi}=\mathrm{ord}_{\sharp}\,\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\prime},

where
\mathrm{ord}(\mathbf{y})
denotes the non-increasing re-arrangment of the vector
\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{E}
. Given a splitting measure, its locally largest bifurcator
\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\ell\ell}
is defined as
\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\ell\ell}=\mathrm{ord}_{\sharp}\,\boldsymbol{\Xi}
. If
\boldsymbol{\Xi}
is a bifurcator of
\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\ell\ell}
, its conditional law given its non-increasing re-arrangment is described by a family
\mathfrak{p}=(p_{i})_{i\geqslant 0}
of mappings
\mathrm{supp}(\boldsymbol{\Xi})\to[0,1]
with

\displaystyle\sum_{i}p_{i}(\mathbf{y})=1,\qquad\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{E}.

More precisely,
\boldsymbol{\Xi}
has the same “law” as the bifurcator
\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\mathfrak{p}}
, in which
p_{i}(\mathbf{y})
,
i\geqslant 0
encode the probability to pick the
i
-th largest coordinate of
\mathbf{y}
to be singled out — in the ssMt construction, this corresponds to the particle which is “followed” by the individual. Formally it reads

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{E}}\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\mathfrak{p}}(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y})F(y_{0};y_{1},y_{2},\dots):=\int_{\mathcal{E}}\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\ell\ell}(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y})\sum_{i\geqslant 0}p_{i}(\mathbf{y})F(y_{i};y_{0},\dots,y_{i-1},y_{i+1},y_{i+2},\dots).

(35)

In particular, the bifurcator associated to
\mathfrak{p}^{\ell\ell}\colon\mathbf{y}\mapsto(1,0,0,\dots)
is the locally largest one. Whenever
\boldsymbol{\Xi}
is supported on summable sequences, one can also define the size-biased bifurcator
\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{*}
, obtained by taking
\mathfrak{p}^{*}=(p_{i}^{*},i\geqslant 0)
, where

\displaystyle p_{i}^{*}(\mathbf{y})=\frac{y_{i}}{\sum_{j}y_{j}},\qquad i\geqslant 0,\qquad\mathbf{y}\in\mathrm{supp}(\boldsymbol{\Xi}).

(36)

Notice also, that not all bifurcators
\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\mathfrak{p}}
are splitting measures: the first coordinate should in particular produce a Lévy measure, this is generically not the case for
\mathfrak{p}:\mathbf{y}\mapsto(0,1,0,0,...)
.

4.2
A “magic” function

A single magic family of functions
\mathsf{m}
can be used to show that many natural ssMt are growing. This family is simply given by

\mathsf{m}_{x}(y)=\frac{xy}{xy+(1-y)},\qquad x,y\in[0,1].

(37)

One verifies by a straightforward computation that
\mathsf{m}_{x}\circ\mathsf{m}_{x^{\prime}}=\mathsf{m}_{x\cdot x^{\prime}}
for all
x,x^{\prime}>0
. For many natural splitting measure
\boldsymbol{\Xi}
, (after a suitable choice of bifurcator) the first marginal of
\boldsymbol{\Xi}
has the form
\nu_{\gamma}
for some
\gamma\neq 0
where

\displaystyle\nu_{\gamma}(\mathrm{d}y)=\mathbbm{1}_{[0,1]}(y)\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{y^{1-\gamma}(1-y)^{1+\gamma}}.

We will then see below that
(\mathsf{m}_{x})_{\sharp}\ \nu_{\gamma}=x^{-\gamma}\cdot\nu_{\gamma}
so that the family
\mathsf{m}
can be used to build growing family of functions. More precisely, the splitting measures that we will consider have one of the two following forms:

•

“Mass form”:
For
\gamma>0
, a splitting measure
\boldsymbol{\Xi}
is said to have form
\mathsf{M}_{\gamma}
if there is a non-trivial Borel measure
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
on
\mathcal{E}
such that for all Borel
F\colon\mathcal{E}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
,

\int_{\mathcal{E}}\boldsymbol{\Xi}(\mathrm{d}y_{0},\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y})\,F(y_{0},\mathbf{y})=\int_{0}^{1}\nu_{\gamma}(\mathrm{d}y_{0})\int_{\mathcal{E}^{\downarrow}}\boldsymbol{\Theta}(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y})\,F\bigl(y_{0},(1-y_{0})\mathbf{y}\bigr).

(38)

•

“Height form”:
For
\gamma>0
, a splitting measure
\boldsymbol{\Xi}
is said to have form
\mathsf{H}_{\gamma}
if there is a non-trivial Borel measure
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
on
\mathcal{E}
such that for all Borel
F\colon\mathcal{E}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
,

\int_{\mathcal{E}}\boldsymbol{\Xi}(\mathrm{d}y_{0},\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y})\,F(y_{0},\mathbf{y})=\int_{0}^{1}\nu_{-\gamma}(\mathrm{d}y_{0})\int_{\mathcal{E}^{\downarrow}}\boldsymbol{\Theta}(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y})\,F\bigl(1,y_{0},y_{0}\cdot\mathbf{y}\bigr).

(39)

The above discussion is then formally encapsulated in the following proposition.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let

\displaystyle G^{\mathsf{M}}_{x}(y_{0},\mathbf{y})=\Bigl(\mathsf{m}_{x}(y_{0}),\frac{1-\mathsf{m}_{x}(y_{0})}{1-y_{0}}\cdot\mathbf{y}\Bigr),\qquad(y_{0},\mathbf{y})\in\mathcal{E},\quad x>0.

\boldsymbol{\Xi}
has form

then
(\boldsymbol{\Xi},\gamma)
is

\displaystyle G^{\mathsf{H}}_{x}(1,y_{1},\mathbf{y})=\Bigl(1,\mathsf{m}_{x^{-1}}(y_{1}),\mathsf{m}_{x^{-1}}(y_{1})\cdot\mathbf{y}\Bigr),\qquad(1,y_{1},\mathbf{y})\in\mathcal{E},\quad x>0

and

Proof.

Let us check the conditions of Definition
2.1

•

This readily follows by the same property of

First, let us remark the function

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{E}}\boldsymbol{\Xi}(\mathrm{d}y_{0},\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y})\,F\left(G_{x}^{\mathsf{M}}(y_{0},\mathbf{y})\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\nu_{\gamma}(\mathrm{d}y_{0})\int_{\mathcal{E}}\boldsymbol{\Theta}(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y})\,F\bigl(\mathsf{m}_{x}(y_{0}),(1-\mathsf{m}_{x}(y_{0}))\mathbf{y}\bigr)

\displaystyle=x^{-\gamma}\int_{0}^{1}\nu_{\gamma}(\mathrm{d}y_{0})\int_{\mathcal{E}}\boldsymbol{\Theta}(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y})\,F\bigl(y_{0},(1-y_{0})\mathbf{y}\bigr)

5
The generator

Given
(\boldsymbol{\Xi};\alpha)
, the
G
-growing condition of Definition
2.1
may seem hard to verify, since we have to construct the family of functions
(G_{x}:x>0)
beforehand. However, due to the required semi-group property of these functions, under mild differentiability conditions, these are specified by a so-called
generator
, namely a “vector field” on
\mathcal{E}
which describes the infinitesimal action of the family
G_{\cdot}
. In smooth cases, one can then translate the requirements of Definition
2.1
in terms of the generator. Specifically, we first reparametrize a family of growing functions
(G_{x}:x>0)
satisfying the requirements of Definition
2.1
using

\mathtt{G}_{t}=G_{\mathrm{e}^{-t}},\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

(48)

so that
\mathtt{G}
satisfies the additive semi-group property
\mathtt{G}_{t}\circ\mathtt{G}_{s}=\mathtt{G}_{t+s}
for
s,t\in\mathbb{R}
. We can now state the definition of the
generator
. Below,
differentiation
and
integration
of function
\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{\mathbb{R}}
are understood as
coordinate-wise
.

Definition 5.1

(The generator)
.

Let
(\mathtt{G}_{t}\colon t\in\mathbb{R})
be a one-parameter group of mappings
\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{E}
. We say that
(\mathtt{G}_{t}\colon t\in\mathbb{R})

admits
\mathtt{V}
as generator
if for all
\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{E}
, the coordinate functions of the mapping
t\mapsto\mathtt{G}_{t}(\mathbf{y})
are differentiable at
t=0
and

\displaystyle\mathtt{V}\colon\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{E}&\longrightarrow&\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}\\
\mathbf{y}&\longmapsto&\partial_{t}\mathtt{G}_{t}(\mathbf{y})\bigl.\bigr|_{t=0}\end{pmatrix}.

In particular, if
t\mapsto\mathtt{G}_{t}
is differentiable then by (
48
) we deduce that
\mathtt{G}_{t}
is a flow for the autonomous differential equation i.e.

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathtt{G}_{t}=\mathtt{V}(\mathtt{G}_{t}),\quad\mathtt{G}_{0}=\mathrm{Id}.

(49)

Since
\mathcal{E}
is infinite dimensional, existence and uniqueness of the above type of differential equations is not
a priori
granted and we shall impose further regularity assumptions to ensure that the family
\mathtt{G}
is characterized by its generator. Before that, let us treat the case of binary conservative splitting measure for which the growing family
G
is unique if it exists.

5.1
The binary conservative case

In order to build intuition on the generator, let us first restrict our attention to the case of a binary conservative splitting measure
\boldsymbol{\Xi}
with a continuous positive density
\lambda
on
(0,1)
, i.e.

\int_{\mathcal{E}}\boldsymbol{\Xi}(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y})F(y_{0},y_{1},\ldots)=\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}s\lambda(s)F(s,1-s,0,\cdots),

and recall from the remarks below Definition
2.1
that we restrict to the case where
\int_{0}^{1}\lambda=\infty
otherwise the splitting measure is not growing. In this case, there is a unique continuous family of functions that satisfies the quasi-preservation of the measure:

Proposition 5.1

(Quasi-preservation fixes the growing family)
.

Assume that
\lambda
is positive and continuous on
(0,1)
, with
\int_{0}^{1}\lambda(s)\mathrm{d}s=+\infty
. Then there exists a unique family
\mathtt{G}_{t}=G_{\mathrm{e}^{-t}}
of continuous bijections of
\{(y,1-y):y\in[0,1]\}
quasi-preserving
\boldsymbol{\Xi}
as in
(
15
). It is given by
\mathtt{G}_{t}=(\mathtt{f}_{t},1-\mathtt{f}_{t})
where
\mathtt{f}_{t}:[0,1]\to[0,1]
are prescribed by

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\mathtt{f}_{0}=\mathrm{Id}\\
\partial_{t}\mathtt{f}_{t}=\mathtt{v}(\mathtt{f}_{t}),\end{array}\right.\quad\mbox{where}\quad\mathtt{v}(s)=-\alpha\frac{\int_{0}^{s}\mathrm{d}u\lambda(u)}{\lambda(s)}.

Proof.

Notice that since
\mathtt{G}_{t}
is continuous and bijective on
\{(y,1-y):y\in[0,1]\}
the same is true for
\mathtt{f}_{t}
. By continuity in
t
and the initial condition
\mathtt{f}_{0}=\rm Id
we deduce that
\mathtt{f}_{t}
are increasing continuous bijections
[0,1]\to[0,1]
for every
t\geqslant 0
. Specifying (
2
) to
[0,\mathtt{f}_{t}(s)]
we deduce that

I(\mathtt{f}_{t}(s))=\mathrm{e}^{-\alpha t}I(s),\quad\mbox{where}\quad I(s)=\int_{0}^{s}\mathrm{d}u\lambda(u).

Since
I
is a
\mathcal{C}^{1}
diffeomorphism of
[0,1)\to[0,\infty)
we deduce that

\mathtt{f}_{t}(s)=I^{-1}(\mathrm{e}^{-\alpha t}I(s)),

(53)

which after differentiation is easily seen to be equivalent to (
5.1
).
∎

In the above binary conservative situation, the generator of
\mathtt{G}
is

\mathtt{V}:\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}\{(s,1-s):s\in[0,1]\}&\to&\mathbb{R}^{2}\\
s&\mapsto&(\mathtt{v}(s),-\mathtt{v}(s)).\end{array}\right.

It is clear from the flow property of (
5.1
) that the family of homeomorphisms
\mathtt{G}
satisfies the additive version of the semi-group property. One can now check the other assumptions of Definition
2.1
using the generator, and foremost the monotonicity property:

Proposition 5.2

(Monotonicity via the generator)
.

The family of functions
\mathtt{G}
produced in Proposition
5.1
satisfies the (reparametrized) monotonicity property (
16
) if and only if
\mathtt{v}(s)\geqslant s-1
, for all
s\in[0,1)
, or equivalently with the above notation that

\mathtt{V}\leqslant\mathrm{Id}.

Proof.

In the reparametrization (
48
) the monotonicity condition (
16
) reduces to checking

\mathrm{e}^{-t}\mathtt{f}_{t}(s)\leqslant s\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mathrm{e}^{-t}(1-\mathtt{f}_{t}(s))\leqslant 1-s.

Given that
\mathtt{f}_{0}(s)=s
, the above inequalities are granted if their derivatives with respect to
t
are always non-positive, that is if

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}(\mathrm{e}^{-t}\mathtt{f}_{t}(s))\underset{\eqref{eq:edoconservatif}}{=}\mathrm{e}^{-t}(\mathtt{v}(\mathtt{f}_{t}(s))-\mathtt{f}_{t}(s))\leqslant 0\\
\partial_{t}(\mathrm{e}^{-t}(1-\mathtt{f}_{t}(s))\underset{\eqref{eq:edoconservatif}}{=}\mathrm{e}^{-t}(-\mathtt{v}(\mathtt{f}_{t}(s))-(1-\mathtt{f}_{t}(s)))\leqslant 0.\end{array}\right.

Since
\mathtt{f}_{t}([0,1])=[0,1]
, this is again equivalent to

\sup_{0\leqslant s<1}\frac{\mathtt{v}(s)}{s}\leqslant 1\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\sup_{0\leqslant s<1}\frac{-\mathtt{v}(s)}{1-s}\leqslant 1.

Since
\mathtt{v}\leqslant 0
only the second condition is relevant and it is actually necessary by looking at
t=0
. ∎

Similarly, the condition (
17
) in Definition
2.1
can be checked directly on the generator:

Proposition 5.3

(Integrated Lipschitz condition via generator)
.

A necessary and sufficient condition for the family of functions
\mathtt{G}
produced in Proposition
5.1
to satisfy (
17
) is that
\mathtt{v}\in\mathbb{L}^{2}(\boldsymbol{\Xi})
.

Proof.

In this proof we use
f_{x}=\mathtt{f}_{-\log x}
. We first assume that
\mathtt{v}\in\mathbb{L}^{2}(\boldsymbol{\Xi}
. Fix
0<x^{\prime}\leqslant x
in a compact
K\subset(0,\infty)
so that we need to verify that

\int_{0}^{1}\left(x({f}_{x}(s)-1)-x^{\prime}(f_{x^{\prime}}(s)-1)\right)^{2}\lambda(s)\mathrm{d}s\leqslant C_{K}(x-x^{\prime})^{2}.

(54)

Introducing the function
\Theta(x,s)=x(f_{x}(s)-1)
with derivative
\partial_{x}\Theta(x,s)=x\left(\mathtt{v}({f}_{x}(s))-({f}_{x}(s)-1)\right)
the LHS is equal to

\displaystyle LHS

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}s\lambda(s)\left(\int_{x}^{x^{\prime}}\mathrm{d}u\ \partial_{x}\Theta(u,s)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}s\lambda(s)\left(\int_{x}^{x^{\prime}}\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{x-x^{\prime}}\cdot(x-x^{\prime})\cdot u\left(\mathtt{v}({f}_{u}(s))-({f}_{u}(s)-1)\right)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\underset{\mathrm{Jensen}}{\leqslant}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}s\lambda(s)\int_{x}^{x^{\prime}}\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{x-x^{\prime}}\left((x-x^{\prime})\cdot u\left(\mathtt{v}({f}_{u}(s))-({f}_{u}(s)-1)\right)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\underset{\rm Fubini}{=}

\displaystyle(x-x^{\prime})\int_{x}^{x^{\prime}}\mathrm{d}u\ u^{2}\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}s\lambda(s)\left(\mathtt{v}(f_{u}(s))-({f}_{u}(s)-1)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\underset{\mathrm{quasi-preservation}}{=}

\displaystyle(x-x^{\prime})\int_{x}^{x^{\prime}}\mathrm{d}u\ u^{2}u^{-\alpha}\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}s\lambda(s)\left(\mathtt{v}(s)-(s-1)\right)^{2}.

Since
\boldsymbol{\Xi}
integrates both
\mathtt{v}(s)
and
(s-1)^{2}
near
1
, the above integral is bounded by
C_{K}(x-x^{\prime})^{2}
for some constant
C_{K}>0
.
Conversely, if for every compact
K\subset(0,\infty)
there exists a constant
C_{K}>0
such that (
54
) holds, then we take some compact
K
having
1
in its interior and use Fatou’s lemma:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}s\lambda(s)\left(\mathtt{v}(s)-(s-1)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}s\lambda(s)\,\lim_{x\to 1}\left(\frac{\Theta(x,s)-\Theta(1,s)}{x-1}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\liminf_{x\to 1}\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}s\lambda(s)\,\left(\frac{\Theta(x,s)-\Theta(1,s)}{x-1}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant C_{K}.

Since
\boldsymbol{\Xi}
integrates
(s-1)^{2}
, this gives that
\mathtt{v}\in\mathbb{L}^{2}(\boldsymbol{\Xi})
as needed.
∎

5.2
General case: nicely growing splitting measures

We shall now extend Propositions
5.1
,
5.2
and
5.3
to the general case. As said above, the first issue is the possible non-existence/uniqueness of solutions to differential equations of the type
\mathtt{G}_{t}=\mathtt{V}(\mathtt{G}_{t})
. A second issue is that in the general case, even in smooth cases the generator
\mathtt{V}
may not be unique and several families of growing functions could make the pair
(\boldsymbol{\Xi};\alpha)
grow. However, we shall see that
\mathtt{V}
is solution to a partial differential equation (PDE) involving
\boldsymbol{\Xi}
and
\alpha
thus restricting considerably the possibilities.
We fix some
q\in[1,\infty]
once and for all, to be chosen depending on convenience in applications. The
\ell_{q}
-norm will be denoted by
|\cdot|
.

Definition 5.2

(Nicely growing)
.

We let
\boldsymbol{\Xi}
be a splitting measure supported in
\mathcal{E}\cap\ell_{q}
and
\alpha>0
. We say that
(\boldsymbol{\Xi};\alpha)
is nicely growing if there exists

\mathtt{V}:\mathcal{O}\subset\ell_{q}\to\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}},

where
\mathcal{O}
is an open subset of
\ell_{q}
such that
\boldsymbol{\Xi}(\mathcal{E}\setminus\mathcal{O})=0
, satisfying the following properties:

•

Inequalities.
We have
\mathtt{V}(\mathbf{y})\leqslant\mathbf{y}
coordinate-wise for
\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{O}\cap\mathrm{Supp}(\boldsymbol{\Xi})
.

•

Divergence PDE.
We have
\mathrm{div}(\mathtt{V}\,\boldsymbol{\Xi})=-\alpha\boldsymbol{\Xi}
, in the sense that for every cylindrical test function
F\colon\mathcal{O}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{E}}\langle\mathtt{V},\nabla F\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\Xi}=\alpha\int_{\mathcal{E}}F\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\Xi}.

•

L^{2}
condition.
The
\ell_{q}
-norm
|\mathtt{V}|
of the generator is in
L^{2}(\boldsymbol{\Xi})
.

•

Regularity of
\mathtt{V}
.
With respect to the
\ell_{q}
-norm,
\mathtt{V}
is Lipschitz and
C^{1}
-smooth.
12
12

12

The latter means that: (i) for all
\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{O}
, there is a bounded linear operator
\mathtt{D}\mathtt{V}(\mathbf{y})\colon\ell_{q}\to\ell_{q}
, the differential of
\mathtt{V}
, satisfying that

\bigl|\mathtt{V}(\mathbf{y}+t\mathbf{z})-\mathtt{V}(\mathbf{y})-\mathtt{D}\mathtt{V}(\mathbf{y})\cdot\mathbf{z}\bigr|=o\bigl(|\mathbf{z}|\bigr)

as
|\mathbf{z}|\to 0
, with
|\cdot|
the
\ell_{q}
-norm; and (ii), the mapping
\mathbf{y}\mapsto\mathtt{D}\mathtt{V}(\mathbf{y})
is continuous in operator norm.

•

No escape from
\mathcal{O}
.
There is
\varepsilon>0
such that for all
\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{O}
, we have
\mathbf{y}+t\mathtt{V}(\mathbf{y})\in\mathcal{O}
for all
|t|<\varepsilon
.

These conditions are sufficient for the purpose of this paper, and in particular will allow us to verify the
G
-growing condition in the important case of the mass fragmentation of
\beta
-stable trees, seen through the lens of the
locally largest
bifurcator, see Example
6
.
Note that we did not pursue optimal conditions and that the theory can certainly be extended: in particular, we expect that the
regularity
and
no escape
conditions can be weakened.
The above terminology is justified by the following proposition:

Proposition 5.4

(Nicely growing
\Rightarrow
growing)
.

If
(\boldsymbol{\Xi};\alpha)
is nicely growing in the sense of Definition
5.2
then it is
G
-growing in the sense of Definition
2.1
, by setting
\mathcal{E}_{0}=\mathcal{O}\cap\mathrm{Supp}(\boldsymbol{\Xi})
and
G=(G_{x}:x>0)
the family of mappings
\mathcal{O}\to\mathcal{O}
characterized by the following ODE for all
\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{O}

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{x}{G}_{x}(\mathbf{y})=-\frac{1}{x}\mathtt{V}\bigl({G}_{x}(\mathbf{y})\bigr),&x>0,\\
G_{1}(\mathbf{y})=\mathbf{y}.\end{cases}

Proof.

Let us equivalently define
\mathtt{G}_{t}=G_{\mathrm{e}^{-t}}
, satisfying for all
\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{O}
the ODE

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\mathtt{G}_{t}(\mathbf{y})=\mathtt{V}\bigl(\mathtt{G}_{t}(\mathbf{y})\bigr),&t\in\mathbb{R},\\
\mathtt{G}_{0}(\mathbf{y})=\mathbf{y}.\end{cases}

Since
\mathtt{V}:(\mathcal{O},\ell_{q})\to(\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}},\ell_{q})
is supposed to be Lipschitz, with
\mathcal{O}
open in
\ell_{q}
, the Cauchy–Lipschitz theorem ensures for all
\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{O}
the existence and uniqueness of a solution
t\mapsto\mathtt{G}_{t}(\mathbf{y})
defined on a maximal interval
I(\mathbf{y})\subset\mathbb{R}
.
Let
\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{O}
, and let us show that
I(\mathbf{y})=\mathbb{R}
. The
no escape
condition applied iteratively with the given global
\varepsilon
ensures that for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
, the Euler scheme
S_{n,0}=\mathbf{y}
and
S_{n,k+1}=S_{n,k}+\frac{t}{n}\mathtt{V}(S_{n,k})
stays inside
\mathcal{O}
as soon as
n\geqslant|t|/\varepsilon
. Together with the Lipschitz assumption on
\mathtt{V}
, this entails that the Euler scheme approximation
(S_{n,\lfloor ns/t\rfloor}\colon-t\leqslant s\leqslant t)
converges uniformly to a solution of the ODE
\partial_{t}\mathbf{y}_{t}=\mathtt{V}(\mathbf{y}_{t})
with
\mathbf{y}_{0}=\mathbf{y}
. Hence
I(\mathbf{y})\supset(-t,t)
for all
t>0
, and thus
I(\mathbf{y})=\mathbb{R}
.
Hence
\mathtt{G}
is now well-defined as a mapping
\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{O}\to\mathcal{O}
, which is in fact continuous by a classical application of the Grönwall lemma, using again the Lipschitz assumption on
\mathtt{V}
.
We still have to verify the
G
-growing conditions.

First, by differentiation with respect to
t
with
t^{\prime}
fixed, the semi-group property
\mathtt{G}_{t+t^{\prime}}=\mathtt{G}_{t}\circ\mathtt{G}_{t^{\prime}}
directly follows from the ODE
\partial_{t}\mathtt{G}_{t}(\mathbf{y})=\mathtt{V}(\mathtt{G}_{t}(\mathbf{y}))
with initial condition
\mathtt{G}_{0}=\mathrm{id}
.
Deriving the monotonicity property of
G_{x}
(or of the reparametrized
\mathtt{G}_{t}
) functions from the inequalities
\mathtt{V}(\mathbf{y})\leqslant\mathbf{y}
on
\mathcal{E}_{0}
is mutatis mutandis the same proof as Proposition
5.2
— we just need to check that
\mathtt{G}_{t}
sends
\mathcal{E}_{0}=\mathcal{O}\cap\mathrm{Supp}(\boldsymbol{\Xi})
to itself (since the inequalities are only assumed there), which can be done as follows: (i) the above Euler scheme argument proves that
\mathtt{G}_{t}(\mathcal{O})\subset\mathcal{O}
, and (ii) the quasi-preservation assumption that we verify below implies further
\mathtt{G}_{t}(\mathcal{O}\cap\mathrm{Supp}(\boldsymbol{\Xi}))\subset\mathrm{Supp}(\boldsymbol{\Xi})
.
Then, verifying (
17
) from the
L^{2}
-condition is parallel to the proof of Proposition
5.3
.

Lastly, we still need to verify the quasi-preservation assumption,
i.e.
that the measures
\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{t}=(\mathtt{G}_{t})_{\sharp}\boldsymbol{\Xi}
for
t\in\mathbb{R}
, are actually given by
\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{t}=\mathrm{e}^{\alpha t}\,\boldsymbol{\Xi}
. First, for all cylindrical test functions
F
on
\mathcal{O}
, we have:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\mathcal{O}}F(\mathtt{G}_{t}(\mathbf{y}))\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\Xi}=\int_{\mathcal{O}}\langle\nabla F(\mathtt{G}_{t}),\mathtt{V}(\mathtt{G}_{t})\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\Xi},

which is simply obtained by exchanging derivation and integration.
It follows from the ODE defining
\mathtt{G}
and the
C^{1}
-smoothness of
\mathtt{V}
that
\mathtt{G}_{t}
is itself
C^{1}
-smooth for all
t
, with differential
\mathtt{D}\mathtt{G}_{t}
satisfying the ODE
\partial_{t}\left(\mathtt{D}\mathtt{G}_{t}(\mathbf{y})\right)=\mathtt{D}\mathtt{V}(\mathtt{G}_{t}(\mathbf{y}))\cdot\mathtt{D}\mathtt{G}_{t}(\mathbf{y})
.
We now claim that
\mathtt{D}\mathtt{G}_{t}\cdot\mathtt{V}=\mathtt{V}\circ\mathtt{G}_{t}
, an identity which expresses that
\mathtt{V}
is preserved when transported along its own flow.
Temporarily taking this identity for granted, the RHS of the last display becomes

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{O}}\langle\nabla F(\mathtt{G}_{t}),\mathtt{D}\mathtt{G}_{t}\cdot\mathtt{V}\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\Xi}=\int_{\mathcal{O}}\langle(\mathtt{D}\mathtt{G}_{t})^{\top}\nabla F(\mathtt{G}_{t}),\mathtt{V}\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\Xi}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{O}}\langle\nabla(F\circ\mathtt{G}_{t}),\mathtt{V}\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\Xi}

\displaystyle=\alpha\int_{\mathcal{O}}F\circ\mathtt{G}_{t}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\Xi},

where the last identity uses that
\mathtt{V}
satisfies the divergence PDE.
The two preceding displays together entail that for all cylindrical test functions
F
, we have:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\mathcal{O}}F\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{t}=\alpha\int_{\mathcal{O}}F\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{t}\qquad\text{for all $t\in\mathbb{R}$}.

This gives that
\int_{\mathcal{O}}F\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{t}=\mathrm{e}^{\alpha t}\int_{\mathcal{O}}F\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\Xi}
for all
t
and thus the result.

It remains to justify our claim that
\mathtt{D}\mathtt{G}_{t}\cdot\mathtt{V}=\mathtt{V}\circ\mathtt{G}_{t}
for all
t
. For a fixed
\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{O}
, it follows from the chain rule that both
t\mapsto\mathtt{D}\mathtt{G}_{t}(\mathbf{y})\cdot\mathtt{V}(\mathbf{y})
and
t\mapsto\mathtt{V}(\mathtt{G}_{t}(\mathbf{y}))
are solutions in the indeterminate
(\mathbf{u}_{t})_{t}\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R},\ell_{q})
of the ODE given by
\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}_{t}=\mathtt{D}\mathtt{V}(\mathtt{G}_{t}(\mathbf{y}))\cdot\mathbf{u}_{t}
, with initial condition
\mathbf{u}_{0}=\mathtt{V}(\mathbf{y})
. Notice that
(t,\mathbf{y})\mapsto\mathtt{D}\mathtt{V}(\mathtt{G}_{t}(\mathbf{y}))
is continuous in both coordinates and locally Lipschitz in its second coordinate — by composition of a bounded linear operator with a
C^{1}
-smooth function — so that the Cauchy–Lipschitz theorem ensures that solutions of the preceding ODE with a fixed initial condition are unique. Hence
\mathtt{D}\mathtt{G}_{t}\cdot\mathtt{V}=\mathtt{V}\circ\mathtt{G}_{t}
, thus proving the claim and finishing our proof.
∎

Proposition 5.5

(From bifurcator to locally largest)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\ell\ell}
be a locally largest splitting measure, and let
\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\mathfrak{p}}
be a bifurcator specified by a family
\mathfrak{p}=(p_{i})_{i}
as in (
35
), but defined on an open subset
\mathcal{O}\subset\ell_{q}
. Let
\alpha>0
and suppose that
(\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\mathfrak{p}};\alpha)
is nicely growing with generator
\mathtt{V}\colon\mathcal{O}\to\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
. If we define the generator
\mathtt{V}^{\ell\ell}
by

\displaystyle\mathtt{V}^{\ell\ell}(\mathbf{y})=\sum_{i\geqslant 0}p_{i}(\mathbf{y})\,\sigma_{i}^{-1}\mathtt{V}(\sigma_{i}\mathbf{y}),\qquad\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{O},

(55)

where
\sigma_{i}\colon(y_{0},(y_{1},\dots))\mapsto(y_{i},(y_{0},\dots,y_{i-1},y_{i+1},\dots))
puts the
i
-th coordinate to the front, then
\mathtt{V}^{\ell\ell}
satisfies the nicely growing conditions for
(\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\ell\ell};\alpha)
except possibly the
regularity
condition.
It thus suffices to check the latter condition to prove that
(\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\ell\ell};\alpha)
is nicely growing.

Proof.

Let us inspect the required conditions on
\mathtt{V}^{\ell\ell}
.

•

Inequalities.

For
\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{O}\cap\mathrm{Supp}(\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\ell\ell})
, we have
\mathtt{V}^{\ell\ell}(\mathbf{y})=\sum_{i\geqslant 0}p_{i}(\mathbf{y})\,\sigma_{i}^{-1}\mathtt{V}(\sigma_{i}\mathbf{y})\leqslant\sum_{i}p_{i}(\mathbf{y})\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{y}
, coordinate-wise, where we used that the inequalities satisfied by
\mathtt{V}
include
\mathtt{V}(\sigma_{i}\mathbf{y})\leqslant\sigma_{i}\mathbf{y}
.

•

Divergence PDE.
Since the sequences
\mathbf{y}
in the support of
\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\ell\ell}
are ordered, we only need to chack the divergence PDE against cylindrical test functions
F\colon\mathcal{O}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
which are permutation invariant. For such an
F
, we have:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{O}}\langle\mathtt{V}^{\ell\ell},\nabla F\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\ell\ell}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{O}}\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\ell\ell}(\mathrm{d}y)\sum_{i}p_{i}(\mathbf{y})\langle\sigma_{i}^{-1}\mathtt{V}(\sigma_{i}\mathbf{y}),\nabla F(\mathbf{y})\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{O}}\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\ell\ell}(\mathrm{d}y)\sum_{i}p_{i}(\mathbf{y})\langle\mathtt{V}(\sigma_{i}\mathbf{y}),\nabla F(\sigma_{i}\mathbf{y})\rangle

\displaystyle\underset{\eqref{eq:defbifurcatorp}}{=}\int_{\mathcal{E}}\boldsymbol{\Xi}(\mathrm{d}y)\langle\mathtt{V}(\mathbf{y}),\nabla F(\mathbf{y})\rangle

\displaystyle=\alpha\int_{\mathcal{O}}F\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\Xi}

\displaystyle=\alpha\int_{\mathcal{O}}F\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\ell\ell}.

The second equality follows by re-indexation of the sum defining the scalar product, and the easily verified identity
\sigma_{i}\nabla F=\nabla(F\circ\sigma_{i})
, while the last equality uses that
\boldsymbol{\Xi}
is permutation-invariant.

•

L^{2}
condition.
By Jensen’s inequality and the permutation-invariance of the
\ell_{q}
-norm, we have:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{O}}\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\ell\ell}(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y})\,|\mathtt{V}^{\ell\ell}|^{2}\leqslant\int_{\mathcal{O}}\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\ell\ell}(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y})\,\sum_{i}p_{i}(\mathbf{y})\,\left|\sigma_{i}^{-1}\mathtt{V}(\sigma_{i}\mathbf{y})\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{O}}\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\ell\ell}(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y})\,\sum_{i}p_{i}(\mathbf{y})\,\left|\mathtt{V}(\sigma_{i}\mathbf{y})\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{O}}\left|\mathtt{V}\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\Xi},

which is finite since
\mathtt{V}
is in
L^{2}(\boldsymbol{\Xi})
by assumption.

•

No escape from
\mathcal{O}
.

The
no escape
condition for
\mathtt{V}^{\ell\ell}
follows from the expression

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}+t\cdot\mathtt{V}^{\ell\ell}(\mathbf{y})=\sum_{i}p_{i}(\mathbf{y})\sigma_{i}^{-1}\left(\sigma_{i}\mathbf{y}+t\cdot\mathtt{V}(\sigma_{i}\mathbf{y})\right),\qquad\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{O},\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

(56)

the identity
\sum_{i}p_{i}=1
, and the the no escape condition for
\mathtt{V}
.

This proves that the
nicely growing
conditions on
\mathtt{V}^{\ell\ell}
with respect to
(\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\ell\ell},\alpha)
hold, except maybe the
regularity
condition.
∎

\displaystyle\mathtt{V}(\mathbf{y})=-\partial_{x}(G_{x}(\mathbf{y}))\bigl.\Bigr|_{x=1},\qquad\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{O},

then

satisfies
Inequalities

Proof.

For

Example 13

.

There is generically no uniqueness of the growing family of functions when the support of the splitting measure is genuinely multi-dimensional. This follows from the fact that there are typically

gives rise to a divergence-free vector field by taking the gradient rotated by a quarter-turn as follows:

\nabla^{\perp}\varphi:=(-\partial_{y}\varphi,\partial_{x}\varphi).

Without going into formal details, we exemplify in our context as follows.

For the locally largest bifurcator of the Haas–Stephenson

[

, see Example
8
, one can solve for a generator

-family using Proposition

. In the case

, the support of the splitting measure is a simplex which can be identified with the domain

6.2
In presence of killing

Throughout the paper we did not consider a possible killing term
\mathtt{k}=\boldsymbol{\Xi}(\mathbf{0})
, even though there exist various models of ssMt that have one (see for example
[
BCR24
, Example 3.11]
). Here we discuss what changes need to be adopted in order to integrate a killing term in our framework and we shall see that this alters some of the results, notably the continuity of
x\mapsto\underline{\mathtt{T}}_{x}
.

Starting with pssMp, we first produce solutions
(\hat{X}^{(x)},\hat{\eta}^{(x)})
as in Section
2.1
by considering the same Ikeda-Watanabe argument as in Proposition
2.1
. The only difference is that now the processes
\hat{X}^{(x)}
may not touch
0
since their underlying Lévy processes may drift to
\infty
. We then reintegrate the killing as follows: Consider
\mathcal{E}
an independent exponential variable of mean
\frac{1}{\mathtt{k}}
and define the decoration-reproduction process
(X^{(x)},\eta^{(x)})
by killing
(\hat{X}^{(x)},\hat{\eta}^{(x)})
at

z^{(x)}\quad\mbox{ such that }\quad\int_{0}^{z^{(x)}}\frac{\mathrm{d}s}{(X_{s}^{(x)})^{\alpha}}=\mathcal{E}.

It is easily seen using the inverse Lamperti transformation (
21
) that
z^{(x)}
is almost surely finite, and that
({X}^{(x)},\eta^{(x)})
indeed has law
P_{x}
. Furthermore, since
x\mapsto X^{(x)}
is increasing, it follows that
x\mapsto z^{(x)}
is also increasing. It is not hard to see that the monotonicity and synchronicity of the jumps of Theorem
2
still hold. Furthermore, the presence of killing does not compromise the measurability property. There is almost surely no reproduction at the killing time, since
\mathcal{E}
is independent of
\mathcal{N}
and since
z^{(x)}
is previsible conditionally on
\mathcal{E}
. By the same method, this yields the measurability property. On the contrary, the continuity of
x\mapsto(X^{(x)},\eta^{(x)})
is not guaranteed anymore.

Loss of continuity of
x\mapsto\mathtt{T}_{x}
in presence of killing.

The absorption time
x\mapsto z^{(x)}
is almost surely continuous, this can be seen directly from its definition. Similarly, the resulting process
x\mapsto X^{(x)}
remains a.s. continuous for the Skorokhod distance. One might then be tempted to think that the resulting process
x\mapsto\mathtt{T}_{x}
remains continuous as well. However, the problem is that the reproduction process
x\mapsto\eta^{(x)}
ceases to be continuous. With positive probability, there is a time
\tau
such that
\mathcal{N}
has an atom with
|y_{1}|\geqslant\eta
for some
\eta>0
and let
\chi
a label such that
z^{(\chi)}=\tau
. Since
X
is almost surely positive at this time, i.e.
J:=X^{(\chi)}_{z^{(\chi)}-}>0
, the reproduction process
\eta^{(\chi)}
has an atom at time
\tau
with vertical coordinate
J\cdot G_{J}(y_{1})>0
. However, for any
\varepsilon>0
, the process
\eta^{(\chi-\varepsilon)}
does
not
have this atom. The big difference with the situation without killing is the fact that
xG_{x}(y_{1})\to 0
for
x\to 0
, which is the case when
X^{(x)}
is continuously absorbed at
z^{(x)}
instead of killed, i.e.
X^{(x)}_{z^{(x)}-}=0
. On the resulting tree
\mathtt{T}_{x}
, one can see this as branches that slowly start growing in the case without killing versus large branches that suddenly appear in the case with killing. The resulting dynamic
x\mapsto\mathtt{T}_{x}
is thus not continuous, but we stress the fact that the rest of Theorem
3
still holds.
