Gradient-Based Non-Linear Inverse Learning
Abhishake, Nicole Mücke, Tapio Helin

Abstract
We study statistical inverse learning in the context of nonlinear inverse problems under random design. Specifically, we address a class of nonlinear problems by employing gradient descent (GD) and stochastic gradient descent (SGD) with mini-batching, both using constant step sizes. Our analysis derives convergence rates for both algorithms under classical a priori assumptions on the smoothness of the target function. These assumptions are expressed in terms of the integral operator associated with the tangent kernel, as well as through a bound on the effective dimension. Additionally, we establish stopping times that yield minimax-optimal convergence rates within the classical reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) framework. These results demonstrate the efficacy of GD and SGD in achieving optimal rates for nonlinear inverse problems in random design.

Introduction

Nonlinear inverse problems arise across a wide range of scientific, engineering, and medical contexts, where the goal is to determine underlying parameters, structures, or causes from indirect, incomplete, or noisy measurements. Examples include modeling physical phenomena governed by partial differential equations, electrical impedance tomography, and detecting cracks or flaws in materials using ultrasonic, thermal, or electromagnetic signals, which often involve nonlinear wave propagation.

Statistical inverse learning in random design is
a subfield of statistical learning that focuses on solving inverse
problems where the design (input) points are randomly sampled from some unknown distribution. More specifically, the observations
follow a model

Y=(Af^{\dagger})(X)+\epsilon\;,

where
A:{\mathcal{H}}_{1}\to{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
is a linear or non-linear operator that maps between two Hilbert spaces
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
. Here,
(X,Y)

is randomly sampled from an unknown probability distribution, rather than being fixed or chosen in a deterministic way.
The noise
\epsilon
is random as well and centered, conditioned on the design points.
The (potentially ill-posed) inverse problem involves determining the unknown function
f^{\dagger}
. The randomness can
introduce additional complexity into the inverse problem, as the statistical properties of the random
inputs need to be taken into account.

The deep connection between statistical learning and linear inverse problems has been made evident in a variety of pioneering works,
starting with
De Vito et al. 2006
;
De Vito et al. 2005
, where the benefit of regularization techniques from inverse problems
Engl et al. 1996
,
such as Tikhonov regularization, in machine learning is highlighted, helping to control overfitting.
These techniques add additional information or constraints to the learning problem, guiding the solution towards a
more reasonable and stable outcome. The statistical properties of those algorithms in the context of
prediction and kernel regression have subsequently been analyzed in many works,
e.g.
Bauer et al. 2007
;
Lo Gerfo et al. 2008
;
Steinwart et al. 2009
;
Mendelson and Neeman 2010
;
Caponnetto and De Vito 2007
;
Blanchard and Mücke 2020
;
Lin et al. 2020
,
to mention just a few.

Solving linear inverse learning problems with general spectral regularization methods is investigated in
Blanchard and Mücke 2017
,
further developing the original ideas from
De Vito et al. 2005
from prediction to reconstruction and providing minimax optimal bounds.
Regularization in Hilbert scales is analyzed in
Rastogi and Mathé 2023
, extending the classical analysis to the inverse learning setting.
These ideas have been further advanced for regularization by projection
Helin 2024
, showing that
coupling the number of random point evaluations with the choice of projection dimension, one can
derive probabilistic convergence rates for the reconstruction error of the maximum likelihood estimator.
In
Bubba et al. 2023
, Tikhonov regularization for inverse learning is extended to general convex regularization
with
p
-homogeneous penalty functionals. Concentration rates of
the regularized solution to the ground truth, measured in the symmetric Bregman distance
induced by the penalty functional, are derived.

While the statistical analysis of regularization approaches for linear inverse problems is mostly well understood,
the analysis for non-linear problems in the context of inverse learning is rather scarce. The first attempts at
analyzing Tikhonov regularization in the non-linear case are made in
Rastogi et al. 2020
. A theoretical analysis is
developed, and optimal rates of convergence are discussed. This analysis is then extended in
Rastogi 2020
;
Rastogi 2024

to the settings of Tikhonov regularization in Hilbert scales and with oversmoothing penalty. However, the drawback of
these approaches lies in their lack of practical implementability. The minimizer of the Tikhonov functional has no explicit solution in the non-linear case and thus has to be approximated, preferably with an iterative method that is efficient to implement.

In the context of nonlinear inverse problems with a fixed design, where the data are considered deterministic, iterative methods such as Landweber iteration, Newton-type methods, or multilevel methods are analyzed with respect to their convergence properties under specific assumptions on the nonlinear operator to be inverted (e.g., Fréchet differentiability); see, for instance,
Hanke et al. 1995
and
Kaltenbacher et al. 2008
. However, the fixed design setting fails to properly incorporate the random nature of the data. A random design setting, on the other hand, allows for the incorporation of the stochastic nature of data, enabling the modeling of uncertainty and variability inherent in real-world measurements. This can lead to more robust and generalizable solutions, as it facilitates statistical analysis, regularization techniques, and error estimation that account for noise and randomness. For a recent overview and more details, we refer the reader to
Abhishake et al. 2024
.

Our Contribution.

We follow the framework of statistical nonlinear inverse learning introduced in
Rastogi et al. 2020
. Extending this approach, we present gradient descent (GD) and stochastic gradient descent (SGD), both with constant step sizes and mini-batching for SGD, providing gradient-based iterative algorithms that are numerically implementable. To efficiently achieve this, we introduce the
tangent kernel
, derived from the linearization of the nonlinear operator to be inverted. This kernel is used to formulate
a priori
smoothness assumptions on the objective function in terms of the associated integral operator.

For GD, we demonstrate that the algorithm is indeed a descent method with high probability, up to a specific threshold in the number of iterations. This threshold serves as the optimal stopping time, ensuring a fast convergence rate.

For mini-batch SGD, we derive convergence rates under different assumptions regarding the step size, stopping time, and mini-batch size, analyzing how these choices influence the algorithm’s performance and providing deeper insights into the trade-offs inherent in practical implementations. Our results explicitly quantify the convergence rate in terms of the number of passes over the training data.

All our derived convergence rates are known to be minimax optimal in the classical framework of nonparametric regression in reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces and match those of linear inverse problems in random design.

2
Statistical Non-Linear Inverse Learning

2.1
Setting

Statistical Model and Noise Assumption.
Statistical inverse learning involves inferring an
unknown function
f^{\dagger}
using statistical methods applied to point evaluations of a related
function
g^{\dagger}
. These evaluations may be sparse or noisy, and the relation
between
f^{\dagger}
and
g^{\dagger}
is established through the model

A(f^{\dagger})=g^{\dagger},\quad\text{for }f^{\dagger}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{1}\text{ and }g^{\dagger}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{2},

(1)

where
A
is a nonlinear operator between real separable Hilbert spaces
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
.

In
supervised inverse learning
, it is assumed that the image space
{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
consists of functions
g:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
.
Here, we assume the input space
\mathcal{X}

to be a Polish space and the output space
\mathcal{Y}
to be a separable Hilbert space.
Moreover, we assume that function evaluation is continuous. Consequently, for any

x\in\mathcal{X}
, the values
g(x)=[A(f)](x)
are well-defined elements in
\mathcal{Y}
.

We focus on random design regression in inverse problems, where the data

((x_{1},y_{1}),\ldots,(x_{n},y_{n}))\in(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})^{n}
emerge due to random observations

y_{j}=g^{\dagger}(x_{j})+\epsilon_{j}\;.

(2)

Here, the outputs
y_{j}\in\mathcal{Y}
are noisy observations
of
g^{\dagger}(x_{j})
at random inputs
x_{j}\in\mathcal{X}
,
j=1,\ldots,n
.
The noise variables
\epsilon_{j}
are independent and centered with
\mathbb{E}[\epsilon_{j}|X=x_{j}]=0
.

The data
(x_{1},y_{1}),\ldots,(x_{n},y_{n})
are realizations of a pair of random variables
(X,Y)\sim\rho
, with
\rho

being an unknown probability measure on the Borel
\sigma
-field of
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
.
We denote by
\nu
the marginal distribution on
\mathcal{X}
and by
\rho(\cdot|x)
the conditional
distribution on
\mathcal{Y}
with respect to
x\in\mathcal{X}
, whose existence is assumed.

The expected square loss (or risk)

{\mathcal{E}}(f)=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert[A(f)](X)-Y}}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}}}\right]=\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert[A(f)](x)-y}}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}\;d\rho(x,y)

(3)

quantifies the discrepancy between the predicted values and the true labels,
with pairs
(x,y)
that are more likely to be sampled contributing more significantly to
the overall error. We are interested in providing convergence rates for the reconstruction and prediction
error for the unknown
f^{\dagger}
by minimizing
{\mathcal{E}}
.
The minimizers of the expected risk typically coincide with the classical minimum norm solutions of inverse problems with the difference that
data fidelity is weighted by the design measure, see
Abhishake et al. 2024
.

In what follows, we state our assumptions on the solution
f^{\dagger}
of the inverse
problem (
1
) and
the noise in (
2
).

Assumption 2.1

(True solution
f^{\dagger}
)
.

The conditional expectation of
Y
given
X
exists a.s. and there exists an
f^{\dagger}\in\mbox{int}({\mathcal{D}}(A))\subset{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
such that

[A(f^{\dagger})](x)=\int_{\mathcal{Y}}y\;\rho(dy|x)\;,\quad\nu\mbox{-almost surely}\;.

It can be easily observed that
f^{\dagger}
is also the minimizer of the expected risk.

We will need two different assumptions on the outputs. For our analysis of stochastic gradient descent, we require the output variable to be bounded in norm:

Assumption 2.2

(Bounded outputs)
.

Assume that
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert Y}}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{Y}}\leq\widetilde{M}
almost surely, for some
\widetilde{M}>0
.

However, for our analysis of gradient descent, we can relax this assumption to:

Assumption 2.3

(Noise condition)
.

There exist some constants
M,\Sigma
such that for almost all
x\in X
and
l\geq 2
,

\int_{Y}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert y-A(f^{\dagger})(x)}}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{Y}}^{l}d\rho(y|x)\leq\frac{1}{2}l!\Sigma^{2}M^{l-2}.

(4)

This assumption is usually referred to as a
Bernstein-type assumption
. It describes the characteristics of the noise influencing the output
y
.
This condition holds true in multiple scenarios, such as when the noise
\epsilon
is either
bounded or follows a sub-Gaussian distribution with a zero mean, independent of
x

(see
Vaart and Wellner 1997
). However, note that this does not apply to
Gaussian white noise in infinite-dimensional spaces.

It is straightforward to observe that Assumption
2.2
implies the inequality (
4
).
For
l\geq 2
, we have almost surely:

\displaystyle\int_{Y}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert y-A(f^{\dagger})(x)}}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{Y}}^{l}d\rho(y|x)\leq

\displaystyle 2^{l-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{Y}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert y}}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{Y}}^{l}d\rho(y|x)+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert A(f^{\dagger})(x)}}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{Y}}^{l}}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2^{l-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\widetilde{M}^{l}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\kappa_{0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert A(f^{\dagger})}}\right\rVert}}\right)^{l}}}\right)\leq 2^{l}M^{l}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}l!\Sigma^{2}M^{l-2},

with
M:=\max\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\widetilde{M},\kappa_{0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert A(f^{\dagger})}}\right\rVert}}\right)
and
\Sigma:=2M
. This implies the inequality (
4
).

RKHS Structure.
We endow the space
{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
with an additional structure that allows us to
apply kernel methods, making practical solutions amenable.
We concentrate on Hilbert spaces of vector-valued functions that possess a
reproducing kernel
Alvarez et al. 2012
;
Carmeli et al. 2006
;
Carmeli et al. 2010
.
These spaces have garnered significant
attention in recent years within machine learning theory, owing to the successful
application of kernel-based learning methods to complex data.

Assumption 2.4
.

Let
{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
be a vector-valued
reproducing kernel Hilbert space of functions
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
corresponding to an operator-valued positive semi-definite kernel
K:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{Y})
. We define the linear function
K_{x}:\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow{\mathcal{H}}_{2}:y\mapsto K_{x}y
, where
K_{x}y:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}:x^{\prime}\mapsto(K_{x}y)(x^{\prime})=K(x^{\prime},x)y
for
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
and
y\in\mathcal{Y}
. Then,

(i)

K_{x}:\mathcal{Y}\to{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
is a Hilbert-Schmidt operator for
x\in\mathcal{X}
with

\kappa_{0}^{2}:=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert K_{x}}}\right\rVert_{HS}^{2}={\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mathop{\rm Tr}\limits(K_{x}^{*}K_{x})}<\infty.

(ii)

For
y,y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}
, the real-valued
function
\varsigma:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}:(x,x^{\prime})\mapsto\langle{K_{x}y},{K_{x^{\prime}}y^{\prime}}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}_{2}}
is measurable.

2.2
Linearization and Tangent Kernel

In order to minimize (
3
) we apply gradient methods. This requires at least
the existence of the derivative of the operator
A
in a vicinity around
f^{\dagger}
.

Assumption 2.5

(Fréchet derivative)
.

The operator
A
is Fréchet differentiable with derivative at
f
denoted by
A^{\prime}(f)
.
Moreover,
A^{\prime}(f)
is bounded in a ball
{\mathcal{B}}_{d}(f^{\dagger})
of radius
d
centered at
f^{\dagger}
with

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert A^{\prime}(f)}}\right\rVert\leq{\rm Lip}\;,\quad f\in{\mathcal{B}}_{d}(f^{\dagger})\cap{\mathcal{D}}(A)\;,

for some
{\rm Lip}>0
.

In what follows, we show that the gradient-based dynamics is guided by a different kernel, which we call the

tangent kernel
, that is related to
A^{\prime}(f^{\dagger})
. To this end, we need an
additional assumption about point evaluations:

Assumption 2.6
.

For any
x\in\mathcal{X}
, we let
S_{x}:{\mathcal{H}}_{2}\to\mathcal{Y}
denote the point evaluation.
The evaluation functional

\displaystyle B_{x}:=S_{x}\circ A^{\prime}(f^{\dagger})

\displaystyle:\mathcal{H}_{1}\to\mathcal{Y}

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle\mapsto B_{x}(f)=[A^{\prime}(f^{\dagger})f](x)

at
x\in X
is uniformly bounded, i.e., there exists a constant
\kappa_{1}<\infty
such that for
any
x\in X
:

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert B_{x}(f)}}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{Y}}\leq\kappa_{1}\;\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert f}}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}.

Note that we have

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert B_{x}(f)}}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{Y}}\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert S_{x}}}\right\rVert\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert A^{\prime}(f^{\dagger})}}\right\rVert\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert f}}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}\leq\kappa_{0}\cdot{\rm Lip}\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert f}}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}.

Hence, we may choose
\kappa_{1}=\kappa_{0}\cdot{\rm Lip}
.

This assumption ensures that the range of
A^{\prime}(f^{\dagger})
is a vector-valued reproducing kernel Hilbert
space
{\mathcal{H}}_{G}
, see e.g.
Carmeli et al. 2010
, with kernel given by

G_{f^{\dagger}}=G:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{Y})\;,\quad G(x,x^{\prime})=B_{x}\circ B_{x^{\prime}}^{*}\;.

(5)

Indeed, we have

B_{x}^{*}y=G(\cdot,x)y

and for
g\in Ran(A^{\prime}(f^{\dagger}))
, the
reproducing property

\langle g(x),y\rangle_{\mathcal{Y}}=\langle g,G(\cdot,x)y\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}_{G}}\;,\quad x\in\mathcal{X},y\in\mathcal{Y},

holds. Moreover,
A^{\prime}(f^{\dagger})
is a partial isometry between
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
and
Ran(A^{\prime}(f^{\dagger}))
, see

Carmeli et al. 2010
, Proposition 1.

Definition 2.7

(Tangent Kernel)
.

The kernel
G_{f^{\dagger}}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{Y})
defined in (
5
)
is called the
tangent kernel at
f^{\dagger}
. The RKHS generated by
G_{f^{\dagger}}
is denoted by
{\mathcal{H}}_{G}
.

We will investigate how the tangent kernel
G_{f^{\dagger}}
at
f^{\dagger}

influences the learning properties of gradient-based inverse learning.
More specifically, if
T:{\mathcal{H}}_{1}\to{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
denotes the associated kernel integral operator, we express
a priori
smoothness assumptions on
f^{\dagger}
in terms of powers of
T
; see Assumption
3.2
. This integral operator determines the convergence rates of our gradient-based estimators. Furthermore, all hyperparameters, such as stopping time, step size, and batch size, depend on those powers. We also express our error bounds using the interpolation norms
||T^{u}(\cdot)||_{{\mathcal{H}}_{1}}
for
u\in\{0,1/2\}
; see Section
3
. Finally, the feature map
x\mapsto B_{x}=S_{x}\circ A^{\prime}(f^{\dagger})
enters directly into the algorithms (
6
) and (
7
), enabling direct practical implementation.

2.3
Gradient Descent and Stochastic Gradient Descent

In what follows, we define the algorithms we are going to analyze. Gradient descent and stochastic gradient descent
are based on empirical risk minimization and both perform an implicit regularization.
We are given a sample
((x_{1},y_{1}),\ldots,(x_{n},y_{n}))\in(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})^{n}
, drawn independently and identically
from the probability measure
\rho
. We denote by
{\mathbf{y}}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})^{T}\in\mathcal{Y}^{n}
the vector of outputs
and by
\hat{S}:{\mathcal{H}}_{2}\to\mathcal{Y}^{n}

the empirical operator
\hat{S}g=(g(x_{1}),\ldots,g(x_{n}))^{T}
. Moreover, we define the empirical norm

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert{\mathbf{y}}}}\right\rVert^{2}_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert y_{j}}}\right\rVert^{2}_{\mathcal{Y}}
. With this notation, the empirical risk is given by

\widehat{\mathcal{E}}(f):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert[A(f)](x_{j})-y_{j}}}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert(\hat{S}\circ A)(f)-{\mathbf{y}}}}\right\rVert_{n}^{2}\;.

Gradient Descent.
Given a stepsize
\eta>0
, we initialize at some
g_{1}\in\mathcal{D}(A)
.
The gradient descent iterates are then given by

g_{t+1}=g_{t}-\eta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\hat{S}\circ A^{\prime}(g_{t})}}\right)^{*}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left((\hat{S}\circ A)(g_{t})-\mathbf{y}}}\right)\;,

(6)

for any
t>1
.

Assumption 2.8

(GD Constant Step Size)
.

We assume that the stepsize is bounded:
\eta<1/\kappa^{2}_{1}=1/(\kappa_{0}{\rm Lip})^{2}
.

Stochastic Gradient Descent with mini-batching.
A variant of Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) involves processing multiple data points at each
iteration, a technique known as mini-batching. Given a batch-size
b\in[n]
, the mini-batch SGD update
rule is defined as follows: We chose a stepsize
\eta>0
and initialize at some
f_{1}\in\mathcal{D}(A)
.
The consecutive iterates are given by

f_{t+1}=f_{t}-\frac{\eta}{b}\sum\limits_{i=b(t-1)+1}^{bt}(S_{x_{j_{i}}}\circ A^{\prime}(f_{t}))^{*}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S_{x_{j_{i}}}\circ A}}\right)(f_{t})-y_{j_{i}}),

(7)

for any
t>1
. Here,
j_{1},\ldots,j_{b}
are independent and identically distributed random variables,
each drawn from a uniform distribution over
[n]
. After
t
iterations, the number of passes
over the data is
\lceil bt/n\rceil
. Note that with
b=n
, we obtain batch gradient descent.

Main Results

Assumption 3.1
.

The following conditions hold:

(i)

There exists an
\alpha\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
such that for any
f,\tilde{f}\in{\mathcal{B}}_{d}(f^{\dagger})\cap{\mathcal{D}}(A)
,

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert A(f)-A(\tilde{f})-A^{\prime}(\tilde{f})(f-\tilde{f})}}\right\rVert_{{L}^{2}}\leq\alpha\;\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert A(f)-A(\tilde{f})}}\right\rVert_{{L}^{2}}

(8)

(ii)

There exists a family of uniformly bounded operators
\{R_{f}\}_{f}
,
R_{f}:L^{2}(\mathcal{X},\nu)\to L^{2}(\mathcal{X},\nu)

such that for any

f\in{\mathcal{B}}_{d}(f^{\dagger})\cap{\mathcal{D}}(A)
,

SA^{\prime}(f)=R_{f}SA^{\prime}(f^{\dagger})\;,

(9)

with

\sup_{f\in{\mathcal{B}}_{d}(f^{\dagger})\cap{\mathcal{D}}(A)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert R_{f}}}\right\rVert\leq\tilde{C}_{R}\;,\quad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert R_{f}-I}}\right\rVert\leq C_{R}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert f-f^{\dagger}}}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}\;,

(10)

for some
\tilde{C}_{R}>0
and
C_{R}>0
.

Note that (
8
) implies by the triangle inequality the bound

\frac{1}{1+\alpha}\;\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert A^{\prime}(\tilde{f})(f-\tilde{f})}}\right\rVert_{{\mathcal{H}}_{2}}\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert A(f)-A(\tilde{f})}}\right\rVert_{{\mathcal{H}}_{2}}\leq\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\;\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert A^{\prime}(\tilde{f})(f-\tilde{f})}}\right\rVert_{{\mathcal{H}}_{2}}\;,

(11)

for all
f,\tilde{f}\in{\mathcal{B}}_{d}(f^{\dagger})\cap{\mathcal{D}}(A)
. Together with Assumption
2.5
we have

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert A(f)-A(\tilde{f})}}\right\rVert_{{\mathcal{H}}_{2}}\leq\frac{{\rm Lip}}{1-\alpha}\;\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert f-\tilde{f}}}\right\rVert_{{\mathcal{H}}_{1}}\;.

(12)

Condition (
8
) is strong enough to ensure at least local convergence to a solution.
It also guarantees that all iterates
f_{t}
remain in
{\mathcal{D}}(A)
for all
t\in[T_{n}]
, which makes the
iteration (
6
) well defined.
Otherwise, it would be necessary to project the iterates onto
{\mathcal{D}}(A)
in each step, see
Hanke et al. 1995
.

Example (Parameter identification).
A prominent example of a non-linear inverse problem comes from parameter identification, see
Hanke et al. 1995
;
Kaltenbacher et al. 2008

a
in

\displaystyle-(a(s)u_{s}(s))_{s}

\displaystyle=f(s),\quad s\in(0,1),

(13)

\displaystyle u(0)

\displaystyle=0=u(1),

(14)

where
f\in L^{2}[0,1]
. The subscript
s
denotes the derivative with respect to
s
.

In this example,
A
is defined as the parameter-to-solution mapping

\displaystyle A:{\mathcal{D}}(A):=

\displaystyle\{a\in H^{1}[0,1]:a(s)\geq\underline{a}>0\}\to H^{2}[0,1],

\displaystyle a\mapsto F(a):=u(a),

where
u(a)
is the solution of (
13
). One can prove that
A
is Fréchet-differentiable with

A^{\prime}(a)h=D(a)^{-1}[(hu_{s}(a))_{s}],

A^{\prime}(a)^{*}w=-F^{-1}[u_{s}(a)(A(a)^{-1}w)_{s}],

where

D(a):H^{2}[0,1]\cap H^{1}_{0}[0,1]\to L^{2}[0,1],

u\mapsto D(a)u:=-(au_{s})_{s},

and

F:{\mathcal{D}}(F):=\{\psi\in H^{2}[0,1]:\psi^{\prime}(0)=\psi^{\prime}(1)=0\}\to L^{2}[0,1],

\psi\mapsto B\psi:=-\psi^{\prime\prime}+\psi.

Note that
F^{-1}
is the adjoint of the embedding operator from
H^{1}[0,1]
into
L^{2}[0,1]
.
It is shown in
Hanke et al. 1995
;
Kaltenbacher et al. 2008
that Assumption
3.1
is fulfilled for this example. For more examples, we refer to
Hanke et al. 1995
;
Kaltenbacher et al. 2008
.

We move on with introducing more structural assumptions.

Assumption 3.2

(Source Condition)
.

There exist some
r>0
,
D>0
and
g\in\mathcal{H}_{1}
such that

f^{\dagger}-f_{1}=T^{r}g\;,\quad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert g}}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}\leq D\;.

Our Hölder source condition describes the smoothness assumption on the objective function
f^{\dagger}
in relation to the RKHS
{\mathcal{H}}_{G}
generated by the tangent kernel
G_{f^{\dagger}}
, see Definition
2.7
. It specifies how well the true function
f^{\dagger}
can be approximated by the RKHS, as determined by the tangent kernel and the spectral properties of the associated operator
T
. This condition is critical for deriving convergence rates for our gradient-based estimators.
The parameter
r>0
quantifies how smooth
f^{\dagger}
is relative to the RKHS determined by
G_{f^{\dagger}}
. Larger values of
r
indicate higher smoothness, implying that
f^{\dagger}
can be well-approximated using the RKHS
{\mathcal{H}}_{G}
. For specific kernels like Sobolev kernels, the Hölder source condition links the smoothness of
f^{\dagger}
to classical function spaces, such as Sobolev or Besov spaces. Note, however, that for (non-)linear inverse learning, the kernel
G_{f^{\dagger}}
depends on the operator to be inverted, specifically on the Fréchet derivative at
f^{\dagger}
, see, e.g.,
Blanchard and Mücke 2020
for a similar setting in the linear case.

Assumption 3.3

(Polynomial decay condition)
.

Let
\lambda>0
. Assume that for some
0<\nu<1
, there exists some positive constant
C_{\nu}>0
such that

\mathcal{N}(\lambda):=\mathop{\rm Tr}\limits\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left((L+\lambda I)^{-1}L}}\right)\leq C_{\nu}^{2}\lambda^{-\nu}.

The effective dimension quantifies the complexity of the function space relative to the data and plays a crucial role in understanding the trade-offs between model complexity, estimation accuracy, and computational efficiency. It is defined via spectral calculus

{\mathcal{N}}(\lambda)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{\sigma_{j}}{\sigma_{j}+\lambda}\;,

where
\sigma_{j}
are the eigenvalues of the integral operator
L
and
\lambda>0
is typically some regularization parameter that controls the trade-off between bias and variance. The effective dimension measures the number of degrees of freedom of the regression problem that are effectively being used given the regularization level
\lambda>0
. Smaller
\lambda>0
values allow more eigenvalues
\sigma_{j}
to contribute, resulting in higher complexity.
The effective dimension captures the impact of the data distribution on the problem’s complexity. It often appears in generalization bounds for kernel ridge regression and similar methods, influencing the convergence rates of estimators,
see e.g.
Caponnetto and De Vito 2007
;
Blanchard and Mücke 2017
. The rate at which the eigenvalues
\sigma_{j}
decay
determines the behavior of
{\mathcal{N}}(\lambda)
. If the eigenvalues decay as
\sigma_{j}\lesssim j^{-1/\nu}
, then

\mathcal{N}(\lambda)\lesssim\lambda^{-\nu}
.

4
Discussion of Results

In this section, we provide a more detailed comparison of our results with existing ones in the context of inverse learning.

GD for linear inverse problems.

Under model (
1
), when
A:{\mathcal{H}}_{1}\to{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
is linear, a convergence analysis of GD with a constant step size has been established in
Blanchard and Mücke 2017
and
Rastogi and Sampath 2017
under our given assumptions (noise model, Hölder source condition, and polynomial eigenvalue decay). Interestingly, our convergence rate in Corollary
3.5
with the chosen stopping time
T_{n}
matches those given in these references, which are known to be minimax optimal. In Theorem
3.4
, we extend these results by showing that GD is a descent algorithm even in the random design setting, with high probability, meaning that the error decreases with each consecutive GD step until a threshold
T_{n}
for the number of steps is reached. Notably, this threshold is identified in the linear case as the optimal stopping time, thereby ensuring optimality.

SGD for linear inverse problems.
In
Lu and Mathé 2022
, the authors analyze a minibatch version of SGD for solving linear inverse problems in a fixed design setting (i.e., where the samples are deterministic). They propose a projected version of SGD to discretize an original infinite-dimensional linear operator
A
, where the use of randomly chosen minibatches introduces noise fluctuations. Their results derive error bounds that account for factors such as discretization levels, the decay rate of the step size, and the smoothness of the problem, characterized by general source conditions. The analysis accommodates a wide range of step size schedules, requiring them to be non-increasing with
\eta_{t}\log(t)\to 0
and
\eta_{t}t\to\infty
as
t\to\infty
, including power-type decays
\eta_{t}\sim t^{-\alpha}
for
\alpha>0
. Since the fixed design framework and the obtained results differ significantly from our random design setting, we only attempt to qualitatively compare their findings with ours.

A key insight in
Lu and Mathé 2022
is that the convergence rate of mini-batch SGD is independent of the batch size. However, the a priori chosen stopping time
T^{*}
does depend on the batch size. Specifically, larger batch sizes correspond to smaller values of
T^{*}
for the same stepsize schedule. While our rate of convergence is also independent of the chosen batch size, the observation in
Lu and Mathé 2022
is fundamentally different from our findings in Corollary
3.7
. Cases
(b)
and
(c)
reveal that for a constant stepsize with an optimal stopping time
T_{n}
, increasing the batch size beyond the threshold given in
(b)
- up to a batch size of
b_{n}=n
(full-batch GD) - offers no additional benefit. In fact, it even increases the computational complexity as the number of passes over the data grows. The same observation is made in
Mücke et al. 2019
(Corollary 1) for minibatch tail-averaging SGD for linear prediction problems (forward problem) in random design. However, while in

Mücke et al. 2019
, one pass over the data is possible (case
(c)
in Corollary 1), we require at least
{\mathcal{O}}(n^{\frac{1}{\min\{2r,1\}+\nu+1}})
passes to obtain optimality.

Nonlinear inverse problems.
The convergence analysis of algorithms for nonlinear inverse problems in random design is, to the best of our knowledge, restricted to Tikhonov regularization (see the stream of works
Rastogi et al. 2020
,
Rastogi 2020
,
Rastogi 2024
). The estimator is defined as the minimizer of a Tikhonov functional, which consists of the sum of a data misfit term and a quadratic penalty term. In these works, a theoretical analysis of the minimizer of the Tikhonov regularization scheme using the concept of reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces is developed. In
Rastogi et al. 2020
, upper rates are presented for smoothness
r\in[1/2,1]
; however, there is no restriction on the range of
r
for lower rates. In contrast, we derive matching upper bounds for a range of smoothness with
r\in(0,1/2]
, showing that GD and SGD seem to suffer from saturation in the nonlinear setting. This stands in contrast to linear inverse problems, where GD and SGD with tail-averaging do not experience this phenomenon
Blanchard and Mücke 2017
,
Mücke et al. 2019
.

Despite its good theoretical performance, Tikhonov regularization in the nonlinear setting has the drawback of being impossible to implement, as it lacks a closed-form solution, unlike in the linear case. As a result, an approximate estimator for
f^{\dagger}
must be derived, which can also be implemented numerically. We extend the established theory of Tikhonov regularization and propose two distinct iterative algorithms to address this limitation.

Iterative regularization approaches for nonlinear inverse problems in Hilbert spaces in the fixed design setting are better understood. Despite the differing settings, we compare our results for GD with a constant step size to the convergence results in
Hanke et al. 1995
;
Kaltenbacher et al. 2008
for constant step size GD. Regarding smoothness, those earlier works also require
r\in(0,1/2]
, which aligns with our findings. The reconstruction error is then bounded by

||g_{t}-f^{\dagger}||_{{\mathcal{H}}_{1}}\leq C_{\star}\;t^{-r}\;,

(16)

for some
C_{\star}<\infty
and for all
t\leq T^{\star}
, where
T^{\star}
is an early stopping time derived from the discrepancy principle. Notably, this bound coincides with our bound in Theorem
3.4
and holds under the same Assumption
3.1
on the nonlinear operator.

A convergence analysis of SGD in fixed design with batch size one and decaying stepsize
\eta_{t}=\eta_{0}t^{-\alpha}
with
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
\eta_{0}
sufficiently small is conducted in

Jin et al. 2020
. Under Assumption
3.1
, the reconstruction error is bounded by

\mathbb{E}[||f_{t}-f^{\dagger}||_{{\mathcal{H}}_{1}}]\leq C_{\star}\;t^{-min\{r(1-\alpha),\alpha-\epsilon\}}\;,

(17)

for some
\epsilon\in(0,\alpha/2)
and
C_{\star}<\infty
. Again, this bound holds for all
t
up to a threshold
T^{*}
(depending on the noise level) and is even slightly better than our result in Corollary
3.7
, case
(d)
.

To sum up, both results (
16
) and (
17
) also show the descent property of GD and SGD (in expectation), similarly to our findings in Theorem
3.4
and Theorem
3.6
, respectively.
