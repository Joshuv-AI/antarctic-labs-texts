Iron-Coordinated Thiourea Formaldehyde Metal–Organic Framework for Efficient Water Splitting
Authors: Gowtham Kanagaraj, Pandian Ganesan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A key challenge in the oxygen evolution reaction (OER) is the development of efficient and durable catalysts for applications such as water electrolyzers and metal-air batteries. Due to sluggish reaction kinetics, the OER typically requires an overpotential significantly higher than the thermodynamic potential of 1.23 V vs RHE, which limits the overall efficiency of electrochemical energy systems. In this work, we report the design and synthesis of an iron-coordinated thiourea–formaldehyde framework, denoted as (Fe-TF) n , as a nonprecious metal-based electrocatalyst for the OER. Its significance lies in the integrated coordination of iron with sulfur and nitrogen atoms within the thiourea–formaldehyde framework. This coordination enables strong dπ–pπ interactions, which enhance electron delocalization and improve electronic conductivity, thereby boosting the catalytic activity of the material. When supported on Ketjen Black, (Fe-TF) n exhibited an OER potential (E j10) of 1.53 V vs RHE at a current density of 10 mA/cm2 and demonstrated stability over 300 h of continuous operation. Furthermore, to evaluate its practical viability, the catalyst was incorporated into a membrane electrode assembly (MEA), and achieved a current density of 70 mA/cm2 at 2.1 V in a water electrolyzer.

Introduction

The Oxygen Evolution Reaction (OER) is a key process in electrochemical water splitting and rechargeable metal–air batteries; however, it remains kinetically sluggish due to its complex four-electron mechanism. − RuO2 and IrO2 are considered benchmark catalysts because of their excellent activity; however, their high cost, limited availability, and poor long-term stability have prompted the search for abundant, durable, and nonnoble alternatives with similar performance. − Among nonnoble metals, nickel (Ni) and cobalt (Co)-based materials, particularly their sulfides (e.g., Ni3S2, Co9S8) and phosphides (e.g., Ni2P, CoP), have gained significant attention due to their impressive OER activity, structural stability, and ability to form high-valent species under oxidative conditions.

Iron (Fe), the most common transition metal in the Earth’s crust (≈5%), has poor catalytic stability in sulfide and phosphide forms under OER conditions, in contrast to nickel (≈0.009%), platinum (10–6), and cobalt (≈0.0025%). Iron sulfides (such as FeS and FeS2) are easily oxidized and leached because iron cannot remain in stable high-valent forms in oxidative environments. , The formation of multiple phases in Fe–S coordination is an unsolved problem. Unlike the robust metal–sulfur interactions in Ni–S and Co–S systems, the Fe–S frameworks tend to break down because Fe3+–S bonds are not very robust. , Together, these challenges, poor sulfur bonding and oxidative instability, greatly limit the durability of Fe-based catalysts for the oxygen evolution reaction.

However, ensuring the long-term stability of iron in sulfur coordination environments remains a major challenge. Therefore, designing iron-based catalysts with reinforced structural frameworks that can stabilize iron in higher oxidation states while preserving Fe–S coordination is critical for unlocking the full potential of iron in OER applications.

A promising approach to stabilize iron-coordinated sites and enhance their OER performance involves the use of framework-based catalysts, which can offer tunable coordination environments, enhanced dispersion, and mechanical robustness. However, recently reported MOFs synthesized from carboxylate (1,4-benzenedicarboxylic acid, 2,5-dihydroxyterephthalic acid, 3,4,9,10-perylenetetracarboxylic dianhydride, terephthalic acid), and N-donor (triethylenediamine, 2-methylimidazole) ligands were reported for the OER with heat treatment at higher temperatures. These ligands typically rely on localized M–N or M–O bonding (where M = Fe, Ni, etc.), which limits electron transfer and leads to reduced stability. ,

To overcome these limitations, we propose the use of thiourea without calcination or heat treatment, which acts as a dual heteroatom ligand providing sulfur and nitrogen coordination sites that are absent in conventional MOF ligands. The Fe–S/Fe–N environment stabilizes Fe2+/Fe3+ redox states and enhances charge transfer through C = S, leading to superior adsorption of oxygen intermediates (−OH, −OOH) and higher catalytic activity than carboxylate- or imidazole-based frameworks.

In this study, we designed and synthesized an iron-coordinated thiourea formaldehyde polymer (denoted as (Fe-TF)n) through a two-step process involving the preparation of an iron–thiourea complex followed by condensation. To synthesize the catalyst, (Fe-TF) n was combined with various carbon supports, including Ketjen Black, multiwalled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs), Vulcan, and Super P. The proposed structure of the iron–thiourea formaldehyde polymer (Fe-TF) n was elucidated using a combination of characterization techniques, including 13C NMR, FT-IR, XPS, and CHNSO analysis. The synthesized (Fe-TF) n polymer showed superior OER performance. Among the prepared catalysts, (Fe-TF) n combined with Ketjen Black was used as the anode in a water electrolyzer.

Experimental Section

Materials and Methods

Thiourea and FeCl2·4H2O were procured from Sisco Research Laboratories Pvt. Ltd. n-Butanol, formaldehyde, potassium hydroxide, and N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF) were obtained from Avara Synthesis Pvt. Ltd. Nafion solution, Vulcan carbon, Ketjen Black EC330J, and Super P were purchased from The Electrode Store (TES), while multiwalled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs) and nickel foam were obtained from Phoenix Science Industries. The synthesized catalyst was characterized using solid-state 13C NMR spectroscopy (Bruker AVANCE HD 500 MHz FT-NMR Spectrometer), Fourier Transform Infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy (Shimadzu Corporation), X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS, Physical Electronics), and elemental analysis (CHNS/O) using a Flash Smart V CHNS/O analyzer. The surface morphology of the catalyst was examined by using a field emission scanning electron microscope (FEG-SEM, JSM-7600F). The surface area and pore size distribution of the synthesized polymer (Fe-TF) n and carbon-supported catalyst were determined using a BELSORP Max surface area analyzer.

Synthesis of Iron–Thiourea (Fe-TU) Complex

As illustrated in Scheme , the iron–thiourea complex was synthesized by dissolving thiourea (0.07 mol) in 200 mL of n-butanol, to which FeCl2·4H2O (0.03 mol) was added slowly. The mixture was then refluxed at 60 °C for 4h under an argon atmosphere. After overnight cooling in an ice bath, the brownish-green precipitate was filtered, dried, and recrystallized with ethanol to obtain a green iron–thiourea (Fe-TU) complex with an 80% yield.

Synthesis of Iron Thiourea–Formaldehyde Polymer (Fe-TF) n

The iron–thiourea complex (0.01 mol) was dissolved in 80 mL of water. Then, 40 mL of formaldehyde was added, and then acetic acid was added to maintain the pH at 3. The mixture was refluxed for 10 h at 90 °C under an argon atmosphere. The product was filtered, washed with deionized water and methanol, and dried at 30 °C for 24h to obtain a brown-colored compound (Fe-TF) n .

Fabrication of (Fe-TF) n /Carbon Support

The carbon supports (Ketjen Black, MWCNT, Vulcan, and Super P) (50 mg) were dispersed in 40 mL of DMF to which the synthesized (Fe-TF) n was added in different quantities and then dispersed by sonication for 4h, followed by filtration to obtain a product, which was washed with methanol and then dried at 30 °C overnight. The eight synthesized catalysts are listed in Table S1.

Electrochemical Measurements

The electrochemical performance (OER) of the catalysts was measured using linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) in 1 M KOH at a scan rate of 10 mVs–1. A three-electrode system with glassy carbon as the working electrode, platinum wire as the counter electrode, and mercury/mercurous oxide (Hg/HgO) as the reference electrode was used. A 5 mg of the catalyst was dispersed in a solution containing 200 μL of DI water, 185 μL of isopropyl alcohol, and 15 μL of Nafion solution by sonication for 1 h. A 6 μL ink was distributed evenly on a glassy carbon electrode with a surface area of 0.071 cm2 and then dried at room temperature. The OER performance was measured within a potential range of 0.9–1.8 V vs RHE at a rotation speed of 1600 rpm in 1 M KOH. All potential values were converted to the reversible hydrogen electrode scale using the relation E RHE = E Hg/HgO + 0.906 V in 1 M KOH. The potentials of all the electrodes were measured using a calibrated Hg/HgO reference electrode (Figure S1).

Preparation of MEA

We used (Fe-TF) n /ketjen (w/w = 1:5) and RuO2 as anodes, which were spray-coated on an anion exchange membrane. Platinum (Pt) was deposited on the membrane from chloroplatinic acid by a wet impregnation method using sodium borohydride, and subsequently used as the cathode material. A platinum-coated titanium mesh was used as the current collector. The anion exchange membrane (AEM), sourced from The Electrode Store, was pretreated with potassium hydroxide for 1 day and then thoroughly washed with deionized (DI) water. To prepare the catalyst ink, 10 mg of the catalyst was dispersed in a mixture of isopropyl alcohol (400 μL), water (370 μL), and Nafion solution (30 μL), followed by sonication for 1 h. The resulting ink was uniformly spray-coated onto the AEM and allowed to dry at room temperature for 1 h.

The S 2p spectra, as shown in Figure a, exhibit a binding energy shift from 161.8 eV in (TF) n and (Fe-TF) n , corresponding to the S2–(CS) species present in the polymer chain. , The peak at 160.7 eV corresponds to sulfur coordinated to the Fe centers (Fe–S). In the N 1s spectrum of (Fe-TF) n (Figure b), a shift of 1.0 eV compared to that of (TF) n is observed, indicating the formation of iron–nitrogen coordination. In the C 1s region, (TF) n (Figure d) shows the binding energy peaks at 284.9 and 287.0 eV, attributed to sp3 carbon (C–C) and characteristic of the CS bond, respectively. Thermal Stability

Differential thermal analysis (DTA) of (Fe-TF) n (Figure b) shows an endothermic peak near 120 °C, indicating the presence of water molecules coordinated to iron through hydrogen-bonding interactions. This is further supported by the FT-IR spectrum of (Fe-TF) n , which displays a broad absorption band at 3426 cm–1, characteristic of −OH groups involved in hydrogen-bonding. Figure a shows the thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) of (Fe-TF) n and (TF) n . (Fe-TF) n showed an initial weight loss of 8%, attributed to the loss of adsorbed water, gas, and solvent molecules. This was followed by gradual decomposition, with a 58% weight loss, indicating the breakdown of noncoordinated ligands, followed by the decomposition of metal-coordinated ligands. In contrast, (TF) n exhibited an initial weight loss of 12% up to 200 °C, likely due to the release of adsorbed gases and water molecules. The decomposition rate was significantly more rapid for (TF) n , with a 76% weight loss between 200 and 300 °C, corresponding to the breakdown of the polymeric chain into low molecular weight compounds, such as CS2, CH4, N2, and H2O. At 540 °C, nearly all the (TF) n had decomposed. (TF) n underwent complete decomposition between 400 and 500 °C, while (Fe-TF) n retained 20% of its weight, indicating that only 80% of (Fe-TF) n was lost in this temperature range. This result reveals that the coordination of iron to the (TF) n polymeric chain enhances the thermal stability of (Fe-TF)n compared to that of (TF) n . The differential thermal analysis (DTA) of (TF) n and (Fe-TF)n was performed to examine the presence of coordinated water molecules.

Electrochemical Analysis

Linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) was performed to evaluate the OER performance; the LSV was performed between 0.9 and 1.8 V vs RHE at 1600 rpm with a scan rate of 10 mV/s, and the results are shown in Figure a and Table . The OER potentials at 10 mA/cm2 for (Fe-TF) n /ketjen (w/w = 1:5) and (Fe-TF) n /MWCNT (w/w = 1:5) were 1.53 and 1.66 V, respectively, and the other two carbon supports, i.e., Vulcan and Super P, were inactive (Figure S3). Based on the OER results, ketjen shows superior performance as a carbon support compared to other carbon supports. Next, we examine the effect of different weight ratios of (Fe-TF) n to ketjen, specifically (Fe-TF) n /ketjen (w/w = 1:10), (Fe-TF) n /ketjen (w/w = 1:2), and (Fe-TF) n /ketjen (w/w = 2:5). Additionally, the 1:10 ratio was compared using MWCNT as the support to evaluate the impact of changing the carbon support from ketjen (Figure S4a). As shown in Figure a, the OER activity of (Fe-TF) n /ketjen (w/w = 1:5) was superior to that of the other prepared ratios. The OER mass activities at 1.6 V for various (Fe-TF) n /carbon composites were evaluated. Specifically, (Fe-TF) n /ketjen (w/w = 1:5), (Fe-TF) n /ketjen (w/w = 1:2), (Fe-TF) n /ketjen (w/w = 1:10), and (Fe-TF) n /ketjen (w/w = 2:5) showed activities of 2.2, 0.74, 0.66, and 0.67 mA mg–1, respectively (Figure b). Among these, the (Fe-TF) n /ketjen (w/w = 1:5) composite exhibited the highest OER mass activity, indicating that this is the optimal weight ratio for the (Fe-TF) n and carbon support among the catalysts. The (Fe-TF) n -to-carbon support ratio significantly impacts the OER performance due to the polymer’s agglomerated structure, as supported by the SEM images of (Fe-TF) n and (Fe-TF) n /ketjen (w/w = 1:5), as shown in Figure . Altering the weight ratio of (Fe-TF) n to the carbon support can affect agglomeration, which in turn decreases the surface area and active sites of the catalyst. The (Fe-TF) n /ketjen (w/w = 1:5) composition is the optimal balance, minimizing agglomeration and maximizing catalytic efficiency. This can be attributed to the ketjen support’s high surface area (1270 m2g), which provides numerous catalytic sites, along with its unique structure comprising both sp2- and sp3-hybridized carbon, superior conductivity, and narrow pore diameter. − , These features make ketjen an excellent support for catalytic activity. OER Performance of the Synthesized Catalysts

As depicted in Figure a, the durability of (Fe-TF) n /ketjen (w/w = 1:5) for the OER was evaluated using the chronopotentiometry technique at a current density of 10 mA/cm2. After a continuous run of 300 h, the catalyst exhibited a 13.1% potential loss, demonstrating excellent OER durability in alkaline conditions. During the stability test, (Fe-TF) n /ketjen (w/w = 1:5) started peeling off from the glassy carbon electrode because of weak bonding to its smooth, flat surface. This poor adhesion made the catalyst layer unstable during operation. To solve this, we switched to nickel foam, which has a 3D porous structure that offers better grip and support, ensuring stronger attachment and improved stability. Figure b compares the OER performance with that of bare nickel foam with nickel foam coated with (Fe-TF) n /ketjen (w/w = 1:5). For bare nickel foam, an anodic peak at 1.35 V is observed, attributed to the oxidation of Ni2+ to Ni3+, and is also evident for the nickel foam supported with the (Fe-TF) n /ketjen (w/w = 1:5) catalyst. The OER performance of (Fe-TF) n /ketjen (w/w = 1:5) was consistent across both glassy carbon and nickel foam substrates.

Figure a–f present the Fe 2p, S 2p, and N 1s XPS spectra of (Fe-TF) n /ketjen (w/w = 1:5) before and after durability testing, respectively. The Fe 2p spectra exhibit binding energies and oxidation states comparable to those of pristine (Fe-TF) n , indicating that the iron coordination environment is the same in (Fe-TF) n /ketjen (w/w = 1:5). In contrast, the S 2p region shows two distinct peaks arising from strong (Fe-TF) n interactions and interfacial charge transfer induced by ketjen black, which alters the electronic environment of the sulfur species. The low-binding energy S 2p3/2 peak at 159.5 eV corresponds to Fe–S. The higher-binding energy peak at 164.8 eV corresponds to noncoordinated CS species from the thiourea–formaldehyde framework, while a peak at 168.5 eV is attributed to the oxidized sulfur species (−SO x −). The N 1s spectrum displays three distinct peaks at 399.2, 400.1, and 403.3 eV, which are assigned to the −NH– groups of the thiourea–formaldehyde framework, Fe–N coordination, and oxidized nitrogen species (−NO2−), respectively. After 300 h of durability, the Fe 2p spectra retain characteristic Fe 2p1/2 and Fe 2p3/2 peaks and their associated satellite peaks, confirming the presence of Fe2+/Fe3+ species. The S 2p region shows the Fe–S (160.6 eV) and CS (164.8 eV) peaks with reduced intensity compared to the durability spectra before, indicating that the metal centers and framework remain stable, and the peak at 169.0 eV corresponds to the oxidized sulfur species (−SO x –). ,− The N 1s spectrum shows two peaks at 399.8 and 404.2 eV, corresponding to Fe–N-coordinated nitrogen and oxidized nitrogen species (−NO2−), respectively. The retention of Fe–S and Fe–N coordination bonds demonstrates the structural integrity of (Fe-TF) n after extended durability. These results highlight that Fe–S and Fe–N bonds play a crucial role in facilitating electron transfer to OH– ions through dπ–dπ interactions between the iron d-orbitals and sulfur orbitals, along with p-orbital interactions with nitrogen. These bonding not only promotes efficient electron transfer to the iron center but also help to maintain the (Fe-TF) n framework, stabilizing the redox behavior of iron over prolonged operation. ,

The electrochemically active surface area (ECSA) was assessed using the double-layer capacitance (C dl) method, which depends on the specific capacitance of a perfectly flat electrode surface. In this work, the catalysts are based on an iron thiourea formaldehyde framework supported on ketjen. Since the carbon support remains the same across all samples, the specific capacitance can be considered constant. The electron and ionic transport resistances significantly influence the performance of the OER. To evaluate the impact of resistance on these processes, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was conducted at open-circuit voltage (OCV), and the results were recorded and analyzed as Nyquist plots. The Randles-equivalent circuit model (Figure b) was applied to extract the values for the ionic resistance and charge transfer resistance in a 1 M KOH solution. In this context, R s represents the solution resistance, R ct denotes the charge transfer resistance, and C dl corresponds to the double-layer capacitance of the catalyst. The frequency range for the measurements was extended from 1 Hz to 500 kHz.

At higher frequencies, (Fe-TF) n /ketjen (w/w = 1:5) exhibited a low R s value of 20.7 Ω. At lower frequencies, the charge transfer resistance and double-layer capacitance were determined by fitting an equivalent circuit model. For (Fe-TF) n /ketjen (w/w = 1:5), as shown in Figure a, the EIS results indicated a charge transfer resistance (R ct) of 0.72 Ω, the lowest among the tested catalysts, and a capacitance of 18.4 μF. These impedance results indicate that (Fe-TF) n /ketjen (w/w = 1:5) exhibits a reduced overall resistance, leading to improved OER kinetics. In contrast, the (Fe-TF) n /ketjen (w/w = 1:2) catalyst exhibited a high R ct value of 1.67 Ω and a low C dl of 5.56 μF. This increase in resistance and decrease in capacitance may be attributed to the reduced ketjen content, which limits the conductive pathways and increases the relative exposure of the polymer, thereby hindering electron transfer and overall catalytic performance. Based on the analysis of all electrochemical characterizations, the oxygen evolution reaction (OER) mechanism, involving an iron-based catalyst coordinated with sulfur (CS), nitrogen (−NH2), and hydroxyl (−OH) groups, proceeds through a sequential series of steps under alkaline conditions, as illustrated in Figure . Initially, Fe coordinates to a hydroxyl group and is stabilized by the surrounding ligand environment. Upon the first interaction with hydroxide ions (OH–) from the electrolyte, an electron is abstracted, and the Fe–OH is converted into an oxo species (step 1) while releasing a molecule of water (H2O) and an electron. This oxo species is highly reactive and undergoes a second nucleophilic attack by another OH–, forming a hydroperoxo intermediate (−OOH) (step 2), along with the release of a second electron. This oxo species is highly reactive and extracts hydrogen from a water molecule, resulting in the formation of a surface hydroperoxo species and hydroxyl group (step 3). In the next step, the hydroperoxo species receives a hydrogen from the water molecule, which stabilizes O–O bond formation (step 4). This is followed by the coordination of water molecules around Fe-OOH, enhancing the stabilization of the peroxo species (step 5). At this stage, the O–O bond is sufficiently formed and primed for the release of molecular oxygen (O2, step 6). , The desorption of O2 regenerates the Fe active site, completing the catalytic cycle. The overall reaction is 4OH−→2H2O+O2+4e−

Anion Exchange Membrane (AEM) Water Electrolyzer Performance

Based on the three-electrode (half-cell) studies, the (Fe-TF) n /ketjen (w/w = 1:5) composite demonstrated the best OER performance among the synthesized catalysts. Therefore, two types of catalyst-coated membrane electrode assemblies (MEAs) were prepared and evaluated. MEA-1 consisted of a PtTi cathode and an (Fe-TF) n /ketjen (w/w = 1:5) anode, assembled as PtTi//Pt//AEM//(Fe-TF) n /ketjen (w/w = 1:5)//PtTi, where Pt acts as the cathode for the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) and the composite functions as the anode for the oxygen evolution reaction (OER). Figure highlights the performance difference between the synthesized catalyst and the benchmark noble metal-based catalysts. MEA-1, which includes (Fe-TF) n /ketjen (w/w = 1:5) as the OER catalyst, achieved a current density of 70 mA/cm2 at 2.1 V. In contrast, MEA-2, which uses state-of-the-art metal catalysts (Pt/C for the HER and RuO2 for the OER), reached a higher current density of 130 mA/cm2 at the same voltage. This indicates that while the (Fe-TF) n -based catalyst exhibits promising electrocatalytic activity, especially given its nonnoble composition, it still lags behind the commercial benchmark in terms of overall water-splitting performance.

Conclusion

The polymerized (Fe-TF) n catalyst demonstrated promising oxygen evolution reaction (OER) activity, attributed to its metal–organic framework structure. Among the various compositions, (Fe-TF) n /ketjen (w/w = 1:5) exhibited a low OER potential with improved stability, making it a viable candidate for sustainable electrochemical applications. The strong interaction between Fe and the sulfur and nitrogen of the thiourea–formaldehyde framework contributed to the enhanced performance and durability. In a water electrolyzer, the (Fe-TF) n -based MEA showed competitive performance comparable to that of noble-metal catalysts. These results highlight (Fe-TF) n as a cost-effective and efficient alternative, offering real potential for large-scale and sustainable water-splitting technology.
