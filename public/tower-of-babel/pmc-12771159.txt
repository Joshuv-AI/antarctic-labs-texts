Manufacture of Kaolin Supported-Palladium Catalysts via Electrospinning and Utilization in Heck and Sonogashira Reactions
Authors: Yinjiao Ma, Xiaojie He, Jialing Feng, Terigele Zhao, Chaolumen Bai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
A Pd nanoparticle/carbon nanofiber (Pd NP/CNF) catalyst was fabricated from modified kaolin via electrospinning. The catalyst was characterized using SEM, TEM, XPS, XRD, and FT-IR. Pd nanoparticles were uniformly distributed on the CNFs, with several particles evenly incorporated inside the carbon fiber matrix, indicating a uniform particle distribution. Thereafter, the catalyst was used in Heck-Sonogashira coupling to evaluate its catalytic efficacy and reusability. Results indicated that the catalyst displayed exceptional stability and recyclability, maintaining its catalytic activity with minimal loss over a minimum of 10 consecutive usage cycles. This method of synthesizing a Pd-loaded nanofiber catalyst with modified kaolin is efficient and ecofriendly, presenting an innovative strategy for catalyst design in C–C coupling, with great potential for practical applications.

Introduction

Transition metal catalysts, with their unique electronic structures and activities, efficiently promote organic reactions and exhibit good selectivity (chemical, regio-, and stereoselectivity). − Palladium (Pd)-catalyzed cross-coupling processes involving transition metals are vital instruments in organic synthesis, and they have received considerable attention since the awarding of the 2010 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. − The Suzuki–Miyaura, Stille, Sonogashira, and Heck reactions are extensively used in the synthesis of natural products, medicines, and polymers. The use of homogeneous catalysts for these processes increases reaction rates, selectivity, and yields. Nonetheless, their extensive implementation is hindered by issues related to separation, high cost, inadequate stability, and possible environmental contamination.

Heterogeneous catalysis has attracted considerable attention because of its reusability and compatibility. In recent years, electrospinning has garnered considerable attention because of its multifunctionality, simplicity, cost-effectiveness, and continuous manufacturing of nano/microfiber catalysts. This method involves the expulsion and elongation of charged polymer solutions or melts by electrostatic forces. After preoxidation and high-temperature calcination, nanofibers can be converted into nano-/microscale carbon fibers. Magnetic PdOx/NiFe2O4 nanofibers have been utilized as catalysts for Suzuki coupling reactions (Scheme A, path a), while Pd/TiO2 nanofibers have been used for Heck coupling reactions (Scheme A, path b). In addition, Pd/PAN composite carbon nanofibers (CNFs) have been utilized in Sonogashira coupling reactions (SCR) (Scheme A, path c), while PANETA/CuI nanofibers have been used for alkyne homocoupling reactions (Scheme A, path d). Moreover, Pd/CFs have been proven effective in Heck coupling processes (Scheme A, path e). These catalysts have also exhibited exceptional catalytic efficacy in their specific applications.

Nano/microfiber materials produced through electrospinning, which were utilized as catalyst supports, have shown great application potential because of their distinctive benefits, such as a substantial specific surface area, elevated porosity, high aspect ratio, and lightweight properties. −

Kaolin (KL), with the chemical formula [Al4Si4O10(OH)8], is a 1:1 dioctahedral layered silicate composed of layers interconnected by hydrogen bonds, each consisting of SiO4 tetrahedra and AlO6 octahedra. , Given its physical and chemical properties, crystal structure, and surface chemistry, KL, which is a type of clay mineral, has been extensively studied and utilized in environmental engineering, cosmetics, and pharmaceuticals. − Literature indicates that virgin KL lacks the anchoring sites necessary for active metals; therefore, functional groups or anchoring sites must be introduced to its surface to immobilize these metals. , Organic modifications, including quaternary ammonium salts and silane coupling agents, are commonly used to load metal nanoparticles such as Au, Ag, Pd, and Pt. Numerous studies on KL-based nanometal catalysts have been published. Iron nanoparticle–loaded KL catalysts (Scheme B, path a), Pd-Kaolin/NiFeO4 nanocatalysts (Scheme B, path b), Kaolin@CS-starch-Pd (Scheme B, path c), and Kaolin-PDA-PdAg (Scheme B, path d) are some examples of synthesized catalysts. Organic coupling processes such as catalytic redox reactions and photocatalysis have been thoroughly investigated using KL-based composite materials.

The Bao Yongsheng group reported a heterogeneous catalyst system based on immobilizing Pd0 nanoparticles on organically functionalized kaolinite to introduce a new synthetic route for the directed C–H functionalization of aryl pyrazoles. Hence, Pd nanoparticle/carbon nanofiber (Pd NP/CNF) catalysts were prepared from modified kaolinite by using electrospinning. Consequently, a novel, highly efficient, and easily recyclable catalyst was successfully developed. The catalytic activity and reusability of this catalyst were systematically evaluated through Heck and Sonogashira reactions (Scheme C).

Experimental Section

Materials

Polyacrylonitrile (PAN, Mw = 1/85,000), N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF, C3H7NO, AR, 99.5%), triethylamine (Et3N, C6H15N, AR, 99.0%), 2-iodothiophene (C4H3IS, 97%, copper stabilizer), phenylacetylene (C8H6, 97%), styrene (C8H8, 99%), 3-aminopropyl triethoxysilane (APTES, C9H23NO3Si, 99%), and trimethyloxy phenyl silane (C9H14O3Si, 95%) were obtained from Shanghai Alding Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. Kaolin was purchased from Inno Biological Chem. N-heptane (C7H16, AR, 98.5%) and anhydrous ethanol (C2H6O, AR, 99.7%) were provided by Tianjin Zhiyuan Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. Palladium chloride (PdCl2, Pd 59.8%) was purchased from Hebei Bailingwei Hyperfine Materials Co., Ltd. All reagents were used without further treatment.

Catalyst Preparation

Synthesis of Organofunctionalized Kaolin (APTE-BTMO-KL)

After 1 g of the Inner Mongolia KL sample was dissolved in 30 mL of n-heptane solution, 1.2 mmol of 3-aminopropyl triethoxysilane (APTE) and 1.2 mmol of phenyl trimethoxysilane (BTMO) were added. After 6 h of stirring at room temperature, the suspension was left in the dark for 12 h, and the solvent was extracted by using centrifugation. Afterward, the functionalized KL was cleaned with n-heptane and vacuum-dried for 12 h. The Heck and Sonogashira reactions were used to assess the APTE-BTMO-KL material.

Preparation of Pd/APTE-BTMO-KL Carbon Nanofiber Membrane (Pd/APTE-BTMO-KL CNFs)

After 0.5 g of PAN was dissolved in 4.5 g of DMF and thoroughly stirred, 0.0094 g of PdCl2 was added, and the mixture was continuously stirred. After thorough agitation, 0.0709 g of APTE-BTMO-KL was added, and the solution was stirred continuously until homogeneity was achieved. The preparation of the precursor solution is now complete. Subsequently, the precursor solution was introduced into the spinning tube and electrospun at 18 kV, maintaining a gap of 18 cm between the electrode and the foil. Consequently, the Pd/APTE-BTMO-KL nanofiber membrane was synthesized. The entire process was conducted in an ambient environment. The Pd/APTE-BTMO-KL nanofiber membrane was heated at 250 °C, 300 °C, and 350 °C at a rate of 5 °C/min and maintained in air for 2 h to produce the catalyst Pd/APTE-BTMO-KL carbon nanofiber membrane.

Preparation of Pd Carbon Nanofiber Membrane Catalysts

In general, 0.5 g of PAN was dissolved in 4.5 g of DMF. After thorough stirring, 0.0094 g of PdCl2 was included to maintain stirring. The precursor solution was prepared following the thorough agitation of the aforementioned solution. Subsequent electrospinning and high-temperature calcination were performed as described in Section .

Characterization Techniques

Scanning electron microscopy was performed using a Zeiss Gemini 300. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images were obtained using a Thermo TALOS FEI F200X. X-ray diffraction (XRD) spectra were acquired using a Rigaku Ultima IV. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) data were acquired using a Thermo Fischer ESCA LAB 250 spectrometer, which utilizes a monochromatic Al Kα X-ray source. Fourier transform infrared spectra were acquired by using a Thermo-Fisher IS5 instrument. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) data were obtained using a Mettler Toledo TGA/DSC 3+ instrument from Switzerland. Nuclear magnetic resonance (1HNMR, 13CNMR) data were acquired using a Bruker AvanceIII600 instrument from Germany.

Activity Test

General Procedure for the Heck Coupling Reaction

2-Iodothiophene (0.2 mmol), styrene (0.3 mmol), Et3N (0.6 mmol), and the catalyst (25 mg) were introduced into a 20 mL tube reactor containing 1 mL of DMF solvent. The reaction mixture was heated at 120 °C in an air atmosphere for 24 h. After cooling to ambient temperature, the reaction mixture was purified using thin-layer chromatography (ethyl acetate/petroleum ether = 1:30) to isolate the intended product. The products were validated by comparison with 1H NMR and 13C NMR data from authentic samples.

General Procedure for Sonogashira Coupling Reactions

2-Iodothiophene (0.2 mmol), phenylacetylene (0.3 mmol), triethylamine (0.6 mmol), and catalyst (25 mg) were added to a 20 mL tubular reactor containing 1 mL of solvent (DMF). The reaction mixture was heated to 90 °C under atmospheric conditions for 24 h. Upon reaching room temperature, the reaction solution reached room temperature, and it was purified using thin-layer chromatography (ethyl acetate/petroleum ether = 1:30) to isolate the intended product. The products were validated by comparing them with the 1H NMR and 13C NMR data of authentic materials.

Catalyst Recycle Experiment

After each reaction cycle, the reactants, solvent, and products were eliminated via centrifugation. After separation, the catalyst was carefully washed with ethanol and dried at 80 °C overnight. The retrieved catalyst was used for the subsequent cycle.

Hot Filtration Test

1a (0.2 mmol), 2a (0.2 mmol), 3a (0.3 mmol), the catalyst (25 mg), Et3N (0.6 mmol), and DMF (N,N-dimethylformamide, 1 mL) were separately added into two reaction tubes. One reaction was stopped after 4 h, and 1a was isolated to calculate the turnover number (TON). For the other reaction, the reaction mixture was filtered through a preheated Celite filter cake after 4 h. Then, the filtered reaction solution was allowed to continue reacting under normal conditions for another 4 h, and 1a was isolated to calculate the TON.

Results and Discussion

Characterization of Catalysts and the Support

Figure a and b shows that freshly synthesized nanofibers have consistent diameters without remarkable aggregation. The nanofibers exhibited no cross-linking. The nanofiber surface was smooth without prominent protrusions or fissures. Figure c, b, d, and f shows that the catalyst fibers used once and 10 times exhibited no remarkable cross-linking phenomena. The catalyst recovery experiment showed that the catalyst exhibited stability and a consistently high yield.

SEM images of (a, b) the fresh catalyst and (c, d) the catalyst used once and 10 times (e, f).

TEM images of the catalyst (Figure ) revealed that the synthesized nanofibers had a 1D structure and displayed relative uniformity. In addition, the Pd particles were relatively distributed without agglomeration. The nanoparticle size was determined to be 3.45 nm. The TEM scans of the recovered catalyst indicated no remarkable particle aggregation, with an average particle size of 4.85 nm. Elemental mapping revealed a homogeneous distribution of C, O, N, and Pd components across the catalyst. The catalyst comprises 101 crystal planes with a crystal plane spacing of 0.0246 nm, which has been computed and evaluated using GMS 3. We also compared the catalysts prepared under other electrospinning parameters (Figure S3). The results show that at a voltage of 18 kV, the catalyst performs best in all aspects when the feeding speed is 3.2.

The chemical composition and electronic state of the catalyst were characterized by using XPS. Figure a and b shows that the XPS full spectra of KL and APTE-BYMO-KL are almost identical, with differences in the peak heights of the N 1s peaks, indicating that the silane coupling agent successfully modified kaolinite, which is consistent with the TGA results. The XPS full spectrum of the catalyst shows peaks for Pd 3d, but no peaks for Al 2p were found in the previous supports. This difference is probably due to the limited detection depth of XPS for thin film samples, with the outer surface of the fibers being composed of Si–O tetrahedra and the inner surface being composed of Al–O octahedra. As shown in Figure c, the C 1s peaks of the catalyst at 281.4, 282.9, 285.6, and 287.1 eV correspond to the C–C/C–H, C–C, CC, and C–O bonds, respectively. The presence of the CC bond indicates the existence of the BTMO coupling agent. , The N 1s peak of the catalyst at 396.5 eV (Figure d) corresponds to N–H. − The O 1s peak of the catalyst (Figure e) at 529.7 and 532.7 eV corresponds to Al–OH and Si–O, respectively. As shown in Figure f, the Si 2p peaks of the catalyst at 100 and 532.798 eV correspond to the Si–O–Si and Si–O bonds, respectively. The presence of the Si–O–Si bond indicates that kaolinite has been successfully modified by the silane coupling agent. − As shown in Figure g, this is basically consistent with the modified kaolinite, with only an additional peak for Pd 3d. As shown in Figure h, the XPS spectrum of Pd 3d consists of two doublets located at binding energies of 343.2 and 338.0 eV, corresponding to Pd 3d3/2 and Pd 3d5/2 of Pd2+, respectively. After one cycle of use, the XPS spectrum consists of four peaks located at binding energies of 337.4, 339.9, 338.2, and 343.3 eV, corresponding to Pd 3d3/2 and Pd 3d5/2 of Pd2+ and Pd 3d3/2 and Pd 3d5/2 of Pd0, respectively. After 10 cycles, the oxidation state of Pd was found to be the same as that after one cycle. The oxidation state of Pd fluctuates between Pd2+ and Pd0, indicating that the Pd/APTE-BTMO-KL CNF catalyst facilitates Heck and cross-coupling reactions via a catalytic cycle that oscillates between the Pd2+ and Pd0 states.

Figure presents a comparative analysis of the XRD patterns of the fresh catalyst and kaolinite. The presence of overlapping peaks at approximately 12.58°, 21.2°, and 24.8° confirms the existence of kaolinite. This observation is consistent with the TGA results of kaolinite, which shows excellent thermal stability. The peaks associated with Pd were not detected in this figure because of its low concentration. The XRD pattern of the catalyst is shown in Figure b. The peaks at 2θ = 33.6°, 33.8°, 41.9°, 54.8°, and 71.5° correspond to the (002), (101), (110), (112), and (211) planes of the cubic structure of PdO (JCPDS 43–1204), respectively. As mentioned previously, the catalyst exhibits a well-defined crystalline structure.

XRD pattern (a, b) of KL and the fresh catalyst.

Figure shows a pronounced absorption peak at 3685 cm–1 corresponding to the hydroxyl stretching vibration of the inner surface between neighboring KL layers as well as the hydroxyl stretching vibration peak in the Al–O octahedron and Si–O tetrahedron situated between the KL layers. The pronounced absorption peak at 1033 cm–1 indicates the asymmetric stretching vibration of Si–O–Si, whereas the absorption peaks at 934 and 801 cm–1 correspond to the stretching vibration of Al–OH. In addition, the two distinct absorption peaks at 538 and 475 cm–1 denote the bending and stretching vibrations of the Si–O–Si bond and the stretching vibration of the Si–O–Al bond, respectively. This finding confirms the successful modification of KL. The introduction of Pd did not destroy the basic structure of KL. The two absorption peaks of KL at 1586 and 1368 cm–1 may be attributed to the hydrated structure H–O–H present in the sample. The high-temperature carbonization of the catalyst may lead to water evaporation, resulting in the absence of an absorption peak at this point for the fresh and recovered catalysts.

Characterization

Initially, the efficacy of the catalyst was examined in the Heck reaction involving 2-iodothiophene and styrene as well as in the Sonogashira reaction between 2-iodothiophene and phenylacetylene. Temperature, base, solvent, and duration were systematically altered in a series of reactions to enhance the reactivity of the Heck and Sonogashira reaction. As shown in Table , the catalyst exhibited exceptional catalytic efficacy in the Heck reaction and Sonogashira reaction across diverse circumstances. At 120 °C, catalyst-250, catalyst-300, and catalyst-350 were evaluated, revealing that catalyst-300 exhibited the highest yield, achieving 88% for the 4aa reaction and 74% for the 5aa reaction (Table , entries 1–3). The yield of 4aa remained unchanged with temperature variations, whereas the yield of 5aa attained 91% at 90 °C (Tables S1 and S2). Among the several bases, triethylamine produced the highest yield (Table , entries 4–7). Hence, triethylamine was selected as the foundation for the subsequent research. The solvent markedly influenced the coupling reaction (Table , entries 8–11). The yield of the 4aa and 5aa products was optimal when DMF was used as the solvent. The assessment of the reaction time indicated that 24 h yielded the optimal response (Table , entries 12–16). Therefore, the reaction conditions of item 2 (Table ) were suitable for the succeeding Heck coupling process, whereas those of item 15 (Table ) were optimal for the subsequent Sonogashira coupling reaction. Furthermore, catalyst-300 was evaluated against the catalyst under the second and 15th conditions, revealing its superior catalytic efficacy. The PdO catalyst facilitated the reaction between 2-iodothiophene and styrene, yielding 43%, whereas the same catalyst promoted the reaction between 2-iodothiophene and phenylacetylene, yielding 63% (Table , entry 20). This finding also indicates that catalyst-300 can facilitate the aforementioned process.

Reaction Scope

After the optimal conditions were determined, the Heck and Sonogashira reactions were carried out on thiophene as well as on the derivatives of styrene and phenylacetylene to investigate the efficiency of the catalyst toward the substrate structure (Table ). As a result, various styrenes and phenylacetylenes with electron-donating groups (OMe and Me) (4ab–4ad, 5ab–5ae) and electron-withdrawing groups (Br, –COOR, and F) (4ae–4ai, 5af–5ah) exhibited good compatibility and yields. The geometric position of the functional group (ortho, meta, and para) (4ab–4ad, 5af–5ah) had no significant effect on the yield of styrene and phenylacetylenes. However, it is worth noting that for o-bromostyrene, we did not obtain a clean spectrum. Heterocycles and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons were also attempted, affording moderate yields (4aj–4ak, 5ai). Similarly, various thiophenes with aromatic and aliphatic substituents showed good compatibility and yields under the optimized conditions (4ba–4ea, 5ba–5da). Moreover, the intermediate 5ea was used to develop anticancer drugs targeting the RhoA/ROCK signaling pathway, which helps to inhibit the spread of tumor cells.

Catalyst Recycling Experiment

The reusability of a catalyst is a crucial aspect of its practicality. In this study, the reusability of catalyst-300 was examined. As shown in Figure , the catalyst was used continuously for 10 cycles, achieving a yield exceeding 75% in the Heck reaction for 2-iodothiophene and styrene. Figure illustrates the assessment of the recycling efficacy of catalyst-300 in the Sonogashira process involving 2-iodothiophene and phenylacetylene. The catalyst can be applied for up to 10 cycles, achieving a yield exceeding 70%. The results indicated the outstanding cycling characteristics of the catalyst. In comparative experiments, commercial palladium on carbon (Pd/C) and Pd supported on γ-Al2O3 were used as catalysts. Pd/C exhibited relatively low yields in the reactions and unsatisfactory recycling efficiency in the Sonogashira reaction. Palladium supported on γ-Al2O3 showed better recycling efficiency in both reactions, but it had excessively low yields.

Conclusion

In this study, a Pd/APTE-BTMO-KL carbon nanofiber membrane catalyst was synthesized by electrospinning and carbonization. The shape, structure, and alterations in atomic valency during the catalytic process were elucidated by using several characterization techniques. Pd nanoparticles anchored on carbon nanofibers were proven to be effective, stable, and highly reusable catalysts for the Heck and Sonogashira reactions using phenylolefin and phenylalkyne derivatives, respectively. This method provides high yield, broad applicability, and straightforward operation, presenting a novel approach for catalyst selection in C–C coupling processes.
