Vaping Versus Smoking: Effects on Attention and Executive Functioning in Young Adults
Authors: Nandini Lahoty, Srishty Yadav, Dinesh Chhabra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTobacco smoking remains a major global public health concern, with approximately 1.25 billion adults using tobacco worldwide. Electronic cigarettes (e-cigarettes or vapes) have gained popularity among an estimated 82 million users globally. Emerging evidence suggests that both cigarette smoking and vaping are associated with alterations in cognitive functioning; however, comparative evidence regarding their effects on attention and executive functioning remains limited.PurposeThe present study aimed to compare selective attention and executive functioning among cigarette smokers, e-cigarette smokers and non-smokers.MethodsA non-experimental, cross-sectional between-groups design was employed. Using purposive and snowball sampling, 90 participants were recruited and categorised into three groups: cigarette smokers (Mage = 21.73), e-cigarette smokers (Mage = 21.77) and non-smokers (Mage = 21.90), with 30 participants (15 males and 15 females) in each group. Selective attention was assessed using the online Stroop Test administered via PsyToolkit, while executive functioning was measured using the Behaviour Rating Inventory of Executive Function–Adult Version, including the Behavioural Regulation Index, Metacognition Index and Global Executive Composite (GEC).ResultsE-cigarette smokers demonstrated comparatively stronger performance in attentional control, self-monitoring and working memory than cigarette smokers. Cigarette smokers exhibited the fastest Stroop performance and showed stronger inhibitory control and emotional regulation. Non-smokers outperformed both smoking groups on cognitive flexibility (shifting), planning and organisation, metacognitive abilities and overall executive functioning as reflected by GEC scores.ConclusionDistinct patterns of cognitive functioning were observed across cigarette smokers, e-cigarette smokers and non-smokers. While vaping was associated with relatively better performance in selected executive domains than cigarette smoking, non-smokers consistently demonstrated superior overall executive functioning. These findings contribute to the growing evidence on the cognitive correlates of smoking behaviours and underscore the need for longitudinal research to examine their long-term neurocognitive consequences.Keywords: E-cigarette smoking, cigarette smoking, vaping, selective attention, inhibitory control, executive functioning, cognitive functioning

Introduction

Tobacco use is rising, with 28.6% of the global population (267 million adults) consuming it daily (Global Adult Tobacco Survey (GATS), 2017). 1 The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and the World Health Organization (WHO) identify cigarette smoking as a major mortality risk.2, 3 Globally, smoking prevalence has dropped by 27.2% in men and 37.9% in women since 1990. 4 Despite this decline, e-cigarettes have gained popularity, particularly for smoking cessation. 5 By 2028, the global e-cigarette market is projected to reach $47.5 billion. 6 Beyond physical health outcomes, understanding the cognitive consequences of tobacco and e-cigarette use has important implications for academic performance, occupational functioning, and long-term mental health.

Growing Use of E-cigarettes/Vapes

E-cigarettes (vapes) are electronic nicotine delivery systems (ENDS) that produce an aerosol containing nicotine, flavours and chemicals. 7 Due to the well-documented risks of cigarette smoking, e-cigarettes were introduced in 2004 as a safer alternative, allowing nicotine users to satisfy cravings without harmful tobacco-related toxins. 8 In 2023, 4.6% of middle schoolers and 10% of high schoolers, about 2.13 million students, used e-cigarettes. 9 Among adults, usage peaked at 9.3% in the 18–24 age group, with 56% never having smoked cigarettes. 10 The global landscape is rapidly evolving with the rise of e-cigarettes. By 2023, the e-cigarette market was led by North America, accounting for approximately 43.9% of global revenue (around $8.5 billion), followed by Europe, which held the largest regional revenue share, and Asia-Pacific, where China alone contributed around 31.1% of the regional market. 11

Physical and Mental Health Effects of E-cigarettes and Cigarettes

Cigarette smoking causes lung cancer, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), cardiovascular disease, stroke and respiratory infections, while also reducing lung function and sperm count, impairing pregnancy outcomes and increasing the risk of asthma and infections.12, 13 E-cigarettes trigger acute lung inflammation, influenced by device type and e-liquid composition. Short-term studies show biomarker and respiratory changes, but chronic effects remain unclear.14, 15 Emerging research suggests long-term lung inflammation risks. 15 E-cigarettes function as toxic substance delivery systems and may increase the risk of chronic diseases. 16 Reviews link inhaled chemicals, oxidative stress, and cellular damage to chronic respiratory conditions.16, 17

Research indicates that cigarettes and e-cigarettes also impact mental health. 18 Depression and anxiety are positively associated with smoking behaviour. 19 Compared to non-smokers, smokers are more likely to experience psychosis, anxiety and mood disorders. 20 Smoking cessation is associated with reduced depression, anxiety and stress, as well as improved mood and quality of life. 21 These effects are comparable to or greater than antidepressant treatments, even in individuals with psychiatric conditions.

E-cigarette use is linked to mood and anxiety disorders, suicidal ideation and depressive symptoms. 22 Smoking also contributes to cognitive decline, impairing attention and distraction filtering.23, 24 Adolescent e-cigarette use is associated with decreased attention, impulsivity and poor academic performance. 25 Chronic smoking reduces cognitive flexibility and attention, while heavy smokers struggle with updating tasks and attention-switching.23, 26, 27 It affects working memory, attention and executive function, though processing speed remains unchanged. 28 Early and prolonged smoking correlates with poorer cognitive outcomes. 29

Rationale

Despite global recognition of the cognitive effects of tobacco and e-cigarettes, research on their impact, particularly on attentional control (selective attention and inhibitory control simultaneously) and executive functioning, remains limited. Attentional control enables selective focus while ignoring irrelevant inputs and is crucial for sustained tasks, closely linked to working memory and cognitive load management.30, 31 Moreover, no study has directly compared cigarette smokers, e-cigarette smokers and non-smokers in these cognitive domains. The present study extends previous research by directly comparing these three groups within the same design using standardised measures of attentional control and executive functioning. Many studies focus on specific aspects rather than a comprehensive review of executive functioning. Additionally, differences in sample characteristics such as age, smoking duration and sociocultural factors limit generalisability. Most studies also overlook puffs per day frequency and intensity, leaving gaps in understanding their cognitive impact.

With e-cigarette use rising among youth, it is essential to assess whether smoking or vaping impairs cognitive and executive functions, potentially hindering academic performance and professional development. Young adults are critical to study, as these functions peak during this stage. Understanding smoking’s cognitive impact will help determine whether so-called ‘better’ alternatives, such as e-cigarettes, still pose significant risks to brain and cognitive health. This study rigorously examines whether cigarette smokers, e-cigarette smokers and non-smokers differ in attentional control (selective attention and inhibitory control simultaneously) and executive function. By doing so, it aims to provide vital insights to inform public health strategies and interventions for cognitive health in Indian youth and at a global level.

Prior research has suggested that cigarette smoking and nicotine exposure may be associated with alterations in attention, inhibitory control and executive functioning, although findings regarding e-cigarette use remain mixed.27, 28, 30, 31 Accordingly, directional hypotheses were formulated regarding expected group differences across cigarette smokers, e-cigarette smokers and non-smokers. In light of the aforementioned considerations, the following objectives were formulated:

To study the differences among cigarette smokers, e-cigarette smokers and non-smokers with respect to their attentional control (selective attention and inhibitory control simultaneously).

To study the differences among cigarette smokers, e-cigarette smokers and non-smokers with respect to their attentional control (selective attention and inhibitory control simultaneously).

To study the differences among cigarette smokers, e-cigarette smokers and non-smokers with regard to their executive functioning.

To study the differences among cigarette smokers, e-cigarette smokers and non-smokers with regard to their executive functioning.

To study the differences among cigarette smokers, e-cigarette smokers and non-smokers with respect to their reaction time on the Stroop test.

To study the differences among cigarette smokers, e-cigarette smokers and non-smokers with respect to their reaction time on the Stroop test.

The present study tested the following hypotheses:

H1: Cigarette smokers, e-cigarette smokers and non-smokers would differ significantly in selective attention and inhibitory control, with non-smokers expected to demonstrate relatively stronger attentional control compared to the smoking groups.

H2: Cigarette smokers, e-cigarette smokers and non-smokers would differ significantly in reaction time on the Stroop Test, with non-smokers expected to demonstrate relatively faster reaction times compared to the smoking groups.

H3: Cigarette smokers, e-cigarette smokers and non-smokers would differ significantly in executive functioning, with non-smokers expected to demonstrate relatively stronger executive functioning compared to the smoking groups.

Methods

Participants

The study employed a non-experimental cross-sectional design and focused on young people aged 18–25 years, predominantly from the Delhi National Capital Region (NCR), India. The participants were chosen via a combination of purposive (participants who smoke cigarettes and e-cigarettes were specifically chosen) and snowball sampling (smokers referred other smokers for participation) techniques. The sample consisted of cigarette smokers, e-cigarette smokers and non-smokers, with average ages of approximately 21.73 years, 21.77 years and 21.90 years, respectively. The participants in both smoking groups had at least 1 year of smoking experience. Inclusion criteria were age between 18 and 25 years and classification as a cigarette smoker, e-cigarette smoker or non-smoker. For the smoking groups, participants were required to have at least 1 year of regular cigarette or e-cigarette use. Exclusion criteria included a self-reported history of neurological disorders, major psychiatric conditions, substance dependence other than nicotine or current use of medications known to affect cognitive functioning. All individuals provided informed consent before participating in the study. Cigarette smokers had a higher number of puffs per day (Mpuffs = 34.67) than e-cigarette smokers did (Mpuffs = 24.83). Cigarette smokers had an average smoking duration of 2.63 years, whereas e-cigarette smokers had an average duration of 2.5 years. Although the study of gender differences was not an objective, equal numbers of males and females in all groups were included to maintain homogeneity. Additionally, demographic characteristics were examined descriptively across groups to evaluate overall sample comparability. The majority of participants were from the middle (43.33%–46.67%) and upper-middle classes (26.67%–36.67%). The majority lived in urban areas (73.33%–90%) and were mostly students (46.67%–56.67%). The sample size was determined based on feasibility and consistency with prior studies examining cognitive differences among smoking and non-smoking groups, rather than an a priori power analysis.

After the data were collected, statistical analysis was carried out using descriptive statistics for age, puff frequency and duration of smoking. Measures

The survey collected sociodemographic information such as age, gender, geographical location and employment status. The level of attentional control (selective attention and inhibitory control simultaneously) was evaluated via the Stroop task. The Behaviour Rating Inventory of Executive Function–Adult (BRIEF-A) was used to assess executive function.

BRIEF-A: The BRIEF-A assesses executive functioning in adults aged 18–90 years. 32 It contains 75 items and generates two indices: the Behavioural Regulation Index (BRI) and the Metacognition Index (MI), as well as the Global Executive Composite (GEC). Combining the Inhibit, Shift, Self-monitor and Emotional Control subdomains results in an additional composite BRI. The MI consists of five subdomains: Initiate, Working Memory, Plan/Organise, Organisation of Materials and Task Monitor. 32 The self-reported normative sample demonstrated moderate to high internal consistency (α = 0.73–0.90 for clinical scales/subdomains and 0.93–0.96 for the indices and the GEC).

Stroop Test: The Stroop Test assesses cognitive processing speed and the ability to regulate interference. 33 Participants are required to identify the colour of the ink in which words are printed, while ignoring the word itself. The test measures reaction time and accuracy to evaluate attentional control and cognitive inhibition. The primary index, known as the interference score, is calculated by subtracting reaction times in congruent trials (word and colour match) from those in incongruent trials (word and colour mismatch). The Stroop C demonstrates good psychometric properties, with a test–retest reliability of 0.88 for individuals aged 18–49 and 0.81 for those over 50; its internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha) is .77, with a standardised alpha of .86. 34 In the present study, the task was administered via PsyToolkit, an open-source platform for conducting cognitive experiments, known for its precision in measuring reaction times in both online and lab-based settings.

Procedure

The survey used PsyToolkit (version 3.4.4) to capture sociodemographic data such as age, gender, geographic location and work status. This study sought to examine the cognitive and behavioural impacts of cigarette smoking versus e-cigarette smoking. The participants were divided into three categories: cigarette smokers, e-cigarette smokers and non-smokers. Testing took place in a controlled, offline environment for approximately 10–15 min. Informed consent was sought to protect participant anonymity and voluntary participation. Participation was voluntary, and self-selection into the study occurred. Data were collected from participants residing in the Delhi National Capital Region (NCR) (India), and the data were methodically structured in an Excel sheet for later analysis. The study protocol adhered to ethical standards for human participants outlined by the American Psychological Association and the Declaration of Helsinki.35, 36 A written informed consent form was presented to the participants, and they signed it. Standardised instructions were provided to all participants to ensure uniform administration of the tasks. Given the non-experimental nature of the study, no masking or blinding procedures were implemented.

Data Analysis

The data were analysed with IBM SPSS (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences, Windows) (Version 25.0) for both descriptive statistics and inferential statistics. 37 This study sought to compare executive function and attentional control (selective attention and inhibitory control simultaneously) among the three groups via one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). The Levene test was used to assess the homogeneity of variance during variance analysis. One-way ANOVA was utilised for the homogeneity of variance and to examine the parameters. One-way ANOVA is the most appropriate method for comparing non-smokers, cigarette smokers and e-cigarette smokers since it rapidly tests for significant mean differences among these independent groups; the Welch test was used when variance homogeneity was not achieved. The descriptive statistics included the mean and standard deviation values. p < .05 was considered statistically significant. Before analysis, data were screened for missing values, outliers and violations of statistical assumptions. No cases were excluded following data screening. Assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance were evaluated before conducting inferential analyses.

No covariates were included in the primary analyses because the study aimed to examine overall group differences across naturally occurring smoking-status groups within a relatively homogeneous young adult sample. Demographic and smoking-related characteristics were examined descriptively to evaluate group comparability. However, the absence of statistical controls should be considered when interpreting the findings, as unmeasured confounding variables may have influenced the observed group differences.

Results

A total of 90 participants were recruited and allocated equally across three groups: cigarette smokers, e-cigarette smokers and non-smokers. All participants who consented to participate completed the study and were included in the final analyses. No participants were excluded following data screening. The results of the study provide a comparative analysis of attentional control and executive functioning across cigarette smokers, e-cigarette smokers and non-smokers, using both performance-based and self-report measures. Attentional control, reaction time, and executive functioning constituted the primary outcomes.

No missing data were observed for the primary and secondary outcome variables, and all cases were included in the analyses. After data collection, statistical analysis was conducted using descriptive and inferential statistics for cigarette smokers, e-cigarette smokers and non-smokers. Discussion

With respect to the study hypotheses, H1, which predicted group differences in attentional control, was supported. H2, which predicted differences in reaction time on the Stroop task, was not supported. H3, which predicted differences in executive functioning (behavioural regulation, metacognition, and overall executive functioning), was not supported, as no statistically significant group differences were observed across these domains. Attentional control and executive functioning were treated as primary outcomes, whereas reaction time on the Stroop task was examined as a secondary outcome.

Given the cross-sectional and non-experimental nature of the present study, the findings should be interpreted as associative rather than causal. Although group differences were observed across certain cognitive domains, it cannot be concluded that cigarette smoking or e-cigarette use directly caused these differences, as pre-existing individual differences and unmeasured confounding variables may also have contributed to the observed patterns.

This study contributes to the literature by examining the cognitive effects of cigarette and e-cigarette smoking among young adults (18–25 years) in India, considering daily puffs per day. It is the first to compare both smoker groups with non-smokers regarding attentional control (selective attention and inhibitory control) and executive functioning. While smoking’s health risks are well known, its cognitive effects, especially those of vaping, remain understudied in India. Given the rising use of ENDS like vapes and e-cigarettes, assessing their cognitive impact is essential.38, 39 Both contain nicotine, which can impair cognition, particularly in youth. The effects of vaping on cognition may depend on factors such as puffs per day, accentuating the need for further research. 40 The acute effects of e-cigarette smoking have been researched, illustrating how nicotine delivery via e-cigarettes may alter cognitive functions such as attentional control (simultaneous selective attention and inhibitory control) and executive functioning, providing context for the findings of this study. 41

The sample consisted of cigarette smokers, e-cigarette users and non-smokers residing in the Delhi NCR region, with variations observed in age, frequency and duration of smoking, gender distribution, socioeconomic background, educational levels, geographic location and employment status. Notably, e-cigarette users reported a lower average number of daily puffs compared to cigarette smokers, indicating differences in consumption patterns between the two groups.

Analysis revealed notable differences in attentional control across the three groups. E-cigarette users showed relatively higher levels of attentional control, encompassing selective attention and inhibitory control, compared to both cigarette smokers and non-smokers. This suggests that individuals who use e-cigarettes may possess a greater capacity to focus and filter out distractions than their counterparts. The observed differences were statistically significant, indicating a meaningful variation in cognitive performance related to attentional control among the groups. The relatively higher attentional control observed among e-cigarette users may reflect differences in nicotine consumption patterns, attentional engagement with vaping-related cues or other behavioural and contextual factors discussed in previous research. 42 However, given the cross-sectional and comparative nature of the present study, these findings should be interpreted as associative rather than causal, and it cannot be determined whether e-cigarette use itself contributed to the observed differences in attentional control.

The cognitive dimensions examined included reaction time on the Stroop Test, as well as executive functions such as inhibition, shift, emotional control, self-monitoring, the BRI, working memory, plan/organise, task monitor, organisation of material, initiate, the MI and the GEC. The results revealed no significant group differences across these characteristics, and the variation explained by these differences was minimal. Accordingly, any numerical differences observed across executive functioning domains should be interpreted as descriptive trends rather than evidence of meaningful group differences. No exploratory or post hoc analyses were conducted; therefore, the reported findings are limited to the planned comparisons.

Differences in reaction times on the Stroop Test were modest across the groups, indicating that the speed at which participants processed and responded to cognitive interference was generally similar. E-cigarette smokers completed the test faster than cigarette smokers and non-smokers. Elderly individuals may have trouble with the Stroop Test due to increased cognitive load, suggesting that shorter tests could help ease these difficulties. 43 Although e-cigarette users showed relatively faster reaction times, these differences were not statistically significant. Just as shorter tests benefit older adults, cognitive advantages linked to e-cigarette use may facilitate faster task completion, highlighting the need for tailored cognitive assessments.

Across the executive functioning domains, no statistically significant group differences were observed. Although some numerical differences were evident, non-smokers generally showed relatively higher scores on cognitive flexibility, planning and organisation, organisation of materials, the MI and the GEC, whereas cigarette smokers showed relatively higher scores on inhibition, emotional control and the BRI, and e-cigarette users showed relatively higher scores on self-monitoring, working memory and task monitoring. Previous research has associated nicotine and tobacco exposure with difficulties in executive functioning, cognitive flexibility and self-regulatory processes.38, 39, 42 However, the small and non-significant differences observed in the present study may partly reflect the developmental characteristics of the young adult sample, as executive functions continue to undergo development and consolidation during this period, with considerable individual variability in their expression. Furthermore, executive functioning is influenced by multiple factors, including education, lifestyle, sleep, stress, individual cognitive differences and the frequency and duration of nicotine exposure, which may have contributed to variability within and across groups. Therefore, the numerical variations observed across domains are best interpreted as descriptive patterns rather than as evidence of meaningful differences in executive functioning between cigarette smokers, e-cigarette users and non-smokers.

Previous research has reported associations between tobacco or nicotine exposure and several aspects of executive functioning, including inhibitory control, cognitive flexibility, working memory and self-regulation.40–42 However, the absence of statistically significant differences in the present study suggests that these associations may not be consistently evident across executive-function domains among young adult cigarette smokers and e-cigarette users. One possible explanation is that the relationship between nicotine use and executive functioning may be complex rather than uniformly detrimental. Nicotine can produce different cognitive effects depending on the timing of use and the individual’s immediate state, while regular users may also experience changes related to nicotine withdrawal or relief of withdrawal symptoms. These opposing influences may have contributed to relatively similar performance across the groups at the time of assessment. Moreover, executive-function performance may remain relatively stable despite differences in tobacco or e-cigarette use, particularly when cognitive demands during assessment are not sufficiently challenging to reveal subtle differences. Thus, the small numerical variations observed in the present study may reflect the complex and context-dependent relationship between nicotine use and executive functioning rather than clear differences between the groups. Further research using larger samples, longitudinal designs and assessments conducted across different levels of cognitive demand may help clarify these relationships.

Conclusion

This study represents the first to concurrently examine the effects of both e-cigarettes and cigarettes on non-smokers on attentional control (selective attention and inhibitory control simultaneously) and various dimensions of executive functioning. The results indicated that e-cigarette users showed relatively higher mean attentional control scores than cigarette smokers and non-smokers, whereas non-smokers showed relatively higher scores on several executive functioning dimensions. E-cigarette use also showed relatively higher mean scores for self-monitoring and working memory. They did, however, fall behind non-smokers in terms of shifting, planning and organisation of material, as well as having weaker emotional control and BRI than cigarette smokers did. Despite higher puff frequency (per day), cigarette users demonstrated stronger inhibitory control and emotional regulation but fared poorly in shifting, planning and organisation of material. Non-smokers, on the other hand, demonstrated relatively higher scores in these areas, as well as in MI and the GEC. Given the cross-sectional design of the study, these findings should be interpreted as associations rather than evidence of causal effects of cigarette smoking or e-cigarette use on cognitive functioning. Future research could examine these relationships longitudinally and investigate additional factors that may contribute to observed differences in attentional control and executive functioning.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Using both self-report questionnaires and performance-based tests introduces methodological diversity, potentially causing inconsistencies due to self-report biases. These questionnaires rely on participants’ ability to recall and reflect accurately, which is problematic in cognitive tests where memory and self-perception may be compromised. The sample size was modest, consisting only of young adults from Delhi NCR, limiting generalisability. Key confounding factors were not statistically controlled for, including cigarette and e-cigarette usage history, physical health, nicotine exposure, sleep quality, caffeine intake, anxiety, depression and negative childhood experiences, all of which may independently influence cognitive functioning. Negative childhood experiences have also been linked to e-cigarette use and cognitive difficulties.43, 44 Consequently, the observed group differences cannot be attributed exclusively to smoking or vaping status, and the findings should be interpreted as associative rather than causal or fully controlled comparisons. The study also overlooked e-cigarette flavours, despite evidence that sweet and fruity flavours influence vaping choices, satisfaction and sensory experiences.45, 46 The cross-sectional design limits conclusions about long-term effects. The timing of cognitive assessments relative to nicotine use was unspecified, meaning acute withdrawal symptoms could have affected results. Finally, anxiety and depression, which independently impact cognition, were not adequately considered. Accordingly, the findings are most generalisable to urban Indian young adults aged 18–25 years and should be interpreted cautiously when applied to other age groups, cultural contexts or rural populations.

Practical Implications

This study highlights cognitive trade-offs in smoking and vaping. While e-cigarette users may show better attentional control (selective attention and inhibitory control), they exhibit deficits in planning and emotional regulation. These findings underscore the need for further research on nicotine’s impact on decision-making and self-regulation. Addressing cognitive vulnerabilities alongside physical health risks is crucial in health messaging and interventions. 47 Tailored interventions, such as workplace wellness programmes and support groups, can enhance mental health and productivity. The perception of e-cigarettes as safer alternatives contradicts their cognitive risks. Public health campaigns must address these concerns, and legislation should mandate cognitive impairment warnings on e-cigarette packaging, similar to cigarettes. Research on nicotine’s effects on executive function circuits could inform neuro-targeted treatments. Additionally, nicotine-induced cognitive deficits may serve as early biomarkers for neurodegenerative diseases, aiding early diagnosis and psychotherapy-based interventions.
