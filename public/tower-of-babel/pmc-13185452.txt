Coverage and determinants of childhood immunization in Somalia: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Ahmed Mohamed Dirie, Nur Rashid Ahmed, Shafie Abdirahman Dirie, Osman Abubakar Fiidow, Kassim Abdi Jimale, Abdirahman Mohamud Mohamed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTChildhood immunization is a cost-effective public health intervention, yet coverage remains suboptimal in Somalia. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to pool childhood immunization coverage and its determinants in Somalia. The electronic database search strategy yielded 11 eligible cross-sectional studies conducted between 2010 and 2025. Using Comprehensive Meta-Analysis Version 5 (Biostat, Englewood, NJ, USA), the pooled coverage for receiving at least one vaccine was 57.9%, while completion of the four basic vaccines was 16.1%, Full immunization coverage was 42.2%. Children born in health facilities were more likely to be immunized than home births (AOR = 2.10; 95% CI: 1.87–2.35). Unexpectedly, mothers with ≥4 ANC visits had lower odds of child immunization (AOR = 0.27; 95% CI: 0.24–0.30). Maternal education increased immunization likelihood (AOR = 1.14; 95% CI: 1.09–1.20). Children in rural areas were likely to be immunized than urban counterparts (AOR = 1.55; 95% CI: 1.49–1.61). These findings highlight the urgent need to strengthen integrated maternal and child health services.KEYWORDS: Childhood immunization, vaccination coverage, determinants, Somalia, systematic review and meta-analysis

Introduction

A vaccine is a harmless form of an antigen that triggers the body’s immune system to produce antibodies, providing protection against future infections.1 It is one of the most affordable and effective public-health measures for controlling and eliminating life-threatening infectious diseases.2,3 The World Health Organization (WHO) introduced the Expanded Programme on Immunization (EPI) in 1974 for all member countries. Its goal was to eliminate vaccine-preventable diseases (VPDs) including diphtheria, measles, pertussis, tetanus, polio, and tuberculosis.4–7 WHO estimates that current vaccines could prevent about 29% of deaths in children under five, saving roughly 2 to 3 million lives worldwide every year.8 Globally, under-five mortality dropped from 96 to 41 deaths per 1,000 live births between 1990 and 2018. During that same period, Sub-Saharan Africa saw a decline from 189 to 83 deaths per 1,000 live births.9 The major causes of death in children under five include pneumonia, diarrhea, measles, and various newborn-related conditions.9

Of all unvaccinated children, 60% were concentrated in just ten countries: Angola, Brazil, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Ethiopia, India, Indonesia, Nigeria, Pakistan, the Philippines, and Vietnam.10 The problem is especially acute in Sub-Saharan Africa, where numerous countries struggle to meet international vaccination benchmarks.11,12

In Somalia, infant mortality remains extremely high, around 81 deaths per 1,000 live births due to preventable diseases such as measles, which alone accounts for about 30% of deaths among young children.13 Recent studies conducted between 2020 and 2023 in different parts of Somalia shows that only about 20%–55% of children are fully vaccinated, and diseases like measles and polio remain endemic in some areas.14,15 Another study reported in Somalia, immunization coverage is estimated at only 30–40% against 6 major childhood diseases tuberculosis, diphtheria, pertussis, tetanus, polio, and measles, whereas the Somali Demographic and Health Survey (SDHS) declared only 13% of the Somali children had a chance to receive the routine immunizations.16,17 Multiple studies have linked mothers’ literacy to higher vaccination uptake.14,15,18,19 Despite continuous efforts by the Ministry of Health and support from international partners to boost vaccination coverage, many Somali children are still exposed to vaccine-preventable diseases, resulting in persistently high levels of illness and death.20,21

Overall, Somalia’s true immunization coverage remains unclear, with only limited estimates available from organizations such as WHO and UNICEF.15 Given these persistent challenges, along with concerns about the quality and completeness of available data there is a clear need for a systematic and comprehensive review of publicly accessible evidence on immunization coverage in Somalia. Thus this study aims to assess pooled coverage and determinants of childhood immunization in Somalia. The findings of this study have important public health implications. First, identifying the overall pooled immunization coverage will provide a more reliable national estimate that can guide policymakers in evaluating progress toward national and global immunization targets. Second, understanding the key determinants of incomplete or delayed vaccination will help to identify high-risk groups and structural barriers.

Methods

Study selection and eligibility criteria

The systematic review followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines22 and was registered in the PROSPERO database (registration number: CRD420251244101). Facility and community-based studies that reported numerical data or estimates on childhood immunization coverage and its determinants across different communities, settings, towns, or cities in Somalia were included. Studies focusing on Somali populations living outside the country were excluded. Additionally, qualitative based studies, reviews, letters, and editorials were excluded.

Search strategy

All relevant studies were systematically searched from databases using key terms such as immunization, pediatric immunization, childhood immunization, vaccine, vaccination coverage, predictors, determinants, associated factors, risk factors, children, and Somalia. The databases searched included, PubMed, Cochrane Library, Science direct, Web of science, Google Scholar, Medline, CINAHL, EMBASE, HINARI, and Google, covering studies conducted in Somalia between 2010 and 2025. The search strategy was as follows: “Child” OR “Children” OR “Pediatric” AND “Vaccine” OR “Immunization” AND “Childhood immunization” OR “pediatric immunization” AND “Vaccination coverage” “Immunization Coverage” AND “Predictors” OR “Determinants” OR “Associated factors” OR “Risk factors” AND “Somalia.”

Data extraction

Data extraction was conducted by Nur Rashid Ahmed (NRA) and Shafie Abdirahman Dirie (SHAD), who recorded details such as the first Author last name, Date, Study area, Sample size, immunization coverage and predictors. A third author Ahmed Mohamed Dirie (AMD) mediated disagreements between the two primary authors and provided the final decision when consensus was required. The overall screening and selection process for the included articles was documented and presented using a PRISMA flow diagram.

Study quality assessment

A modified Newcastle–Ottawa Quality Assessment Scale for cross-sectional studies was used to evaluate the methodological quality of the studies included in this review.23 The scale has a maximum score of nine stars, with scores ranging from zero to nine. Studies scoring 7 out of 9 stars or higher were classified as high quality, while those scoring 5 out of 9 stars were considered of moderate quality and those studies with less than 5 out of 9 considered as low quality (Table 1).

Outcome measures and statistical analysis

Immunization coverage (%) and determinants of immunization coverage (odds ratios) were the two primary outcome measures in this study. Immunization coverage was defined as the prevalence of sampled children who had received childhood immunization. Determinants of immunization referred to factors associated with immunization coverage or uptake. A random-effects meta-analysis was conducted to pool individual study estimates of immunization coverage and odds ratios, accounting for between-study variability. Heterogeneity among studies was assessed using the I-squared (I2) statistic, and subgroup analyses were performed to explore potential sources of heterogeneity where appropriate. All statistical analyses were conducted using Comprehensive Meta-analysis (CMA) version 5.

Results

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted to synthesize available evidence on the coverage and determinants of childhood immunization in Somalia. Specifically, the study aimed to (i) estimate the pooled coverage of routine childhood immunization across different regions and vaccine antigens, and (ii) identify key maternal, child, and health system–related factors associated with full or incomplete immunization among children under five years of age.

Study selection

A total of 36 records were identified through database searching. After removal of 7 duplicate records, 29 studies remained for title and abstract screening. Of these, 11 records were excluded due to irrelevance, leaving 18 full-text articles assessed for eligibility. Following full-text review, 7 articles were excluded because they lacked quantitative estimates of immunization coverage or focused other outcomes. Ultimately, 11 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the systematic review and meta-analysis (Figure 1).

Flow chart on the article selection strategy on coverage and determinants of childhood immunization in Somalia.

Characteristics of included studies

The 11 included studies were cross-sectional design, published between 2011 and 2025. Conducted across different regions of Somalia, including Somaliland, Mogadishu, Galkayo, and national-level surveys. Sample sizes ranged from 174 to 9,290 children. Immunization status was assessed using vaccination cards, caregiver or maternal recall, or both, although some studies did not explicitly specify the verification method.

The included studies reported varying definitions of immunization status, including receipt of at least one vaccine dose, completion of four basic vaccines, and full immunization. Considerable variability in immunization coverage was observed across studies (Table 1).

Pooled coverage of childhood immunization

At least one vaccine dose

Three studies reported coverage for receipt of at least one vaccine dose. Individual study estimates ranged from 34.0% to 80.0%. The pooled prevalence of children who received at least one vaccine was 57.9% (95% CI: 26.7%–83.9%), indicating that slightly more than half of Somali children had initial contact with immunization services.

Completion of the four basic vaccines

Four studies reported coverage for completion of the four basic vaccines (BCG, DPT, polio, and measles). Coverage ranged from 6.3% to 39.8% across studies. The pooled prevalence of children who completed the four basic vaccines was 16.1% (95% CI: 8.6%–28.3%), representing the lowest coverage among the immunization categories assessed.

Determinants of childhood immunization

The analysis showed several factors associated with childhood immunization coverage. Two studies15,17 reported that children born in a health facility were more than twice as likely to be immunized compared to those born at home (AOR = 2.10; 95% CI: 1.87–2.35; I2 = 80.8%).Unexpectedly two studies18,27 reported mothers with ≥4 ANC visits had lower odds of child immunization compared with mothers who attended fewer visits (AOR = 0.27; 95% CI: 0.24–0.30; I2 = 66.7%). This finding may be due to differences in variable coding, study heterogeneity, or contextual factors, and should be interpreted with caution Maternal education was reported in 3 studies15,25,27; with children of mothers who attained primary education being more likely to be immunized than those whose mothers had no formal education (AOR = 1.14; 95% CI: 1.09–1.20; I2 = 99.5%). Place of residence18–23,25,27; children residing in rural areas were likely to have immunization compared with those living in urban areas (AOR = 1.55; 95% CI: 1.49–1.61; I2 = 99.4%) (Table 2).

Full immunization coverage

Four studies reported estimates of full immunization. Individual prevalence estimates ranged from 20.0% to 55.0%. The pooled prevalence of full immunization was 42.2% (95% CI: 28.6%–57.2%), indicating that fewer than half of children included in these studies had completed the recommended immunization schedule (Figure 2).

Pooled coverage of childhood immunization in Somalia.

Synthesis of results

Overall, the findings indicate that childhood immunization coverage in Somalia remains low and inconsistent. The pooled prevalence of children receiving at least one vaccine was 57.9%, suggesting moderate initial contact with immunization services. However, completion of the four basic vaccines was substantially lower at 16.1%, indicating a pronounced dropout between initiation and completion of the vaccination schedule. The pooled prevalence of full immunization was 42.2%, with considerable variability observed across studies. Immunization coverage ranged widely, from as low as 6.3% in some national-level studies to over 50% in others, with relatively higher coverage reported in urban settings such as Mogadishu. Across all outcomes, a consistent pattern of higher initiation but poor completion was evident, accompanied by substantial heterogeneity. Key determinants associated with immunization coverage included maternal education, place of residence, and place of delivery, with improved uptake observed among children of educated mothers and those born in health facilities.

Discussion

This study is a systematic review and meta-analysis conducted in Somalia to assess the coverage of childhood immunization and to synthesize evidence on its associated determinants. Approximately 65.2% of children under five in Somalia had not received any vaccinations.27 Similarly, a previous study among Somali children aged 12–23 months reported that more than 60.2% were zero-dose, having received none of the four essential routine immunization doses.30

The pooled prevalence of children who had received at least one vaccine was 57.9%, suggesting that more than half of children had initial contact with routine immunization services. The current finding is considerably lower compared to that in neighboring Kenya (77%),31 However it is slightly higher than the coverage reported in Uganda (57.4%) and Rwanda (56.1%).32 Although some individual studies reported relatively higher coverage, the pooled estimate reflects limited overall access to vaccination services. This finding points to barriers such as poor outreach, limited availability of health facilities, population displacement, and low caregiver awareness, which continue to hinder early vaccination uptake.

Completion of the four basic vaccines (BCG, DPT, Polio, and Measles) showed a markedly low pooled prevalence of 16.1%, representing the lowest coverage among the three immunization categories assessed. This finding is consistent with a previous study in Somalia, which reported a slightly lower coverage of 17.04% among children under five.27 This substantial decline between initiation and completion indicates a high dropout rate within the immunization program. Factors such as missed appointments, vaccine stock-outs, insecurity, and competing household priorities may contribute to poor completion of the basic immunization schedule.

The pooled prevalence of full immunization in this review was 42.2%, which is higher than findings from Ethiopia, where the 2016 Demographic and Health Survey reported 38.0% coverage,33 and a study in Jigjiga District, Somali National Regional State, found 36.6% coverage.34 While this similarity may appear unexpected, it likely reflects heterogeneity in study definitions, age ranges, and measurement approaches used across included studies. Some studies defined full immunization based on receipt of all recommended vaccines, while others relied on proxy indicators or incomplete schedules. Considerable variability in immunization coverage was observed across individual studies, with full immunization ranging from as low as 6.3% in some national studies to over 50% in others. Studies conducted in Mogadishu generally reported higher full immunization coverage compared to those conducted in other regions of Somalia. Suggesting regional disparities in access to immunization services. These differences may be explained by better health infrastructure, higher health facility density, and improved service availability in urban areas.

Our analysis identified several factors significantly associated with childhood immunization coverage in Somalia. Maternal education also positively influenced immunization, with children of mothers who attained primary education being 14% more likely to be fully vaccinated compared to children of uneducated mothers. Similarly, previous studies have indicated that maternal education is a predictor of childhood immunization.35–38 This may be due to the educated mothers having better knowledge of vaccine-preventable diseases and can recognize the importance of vaccination in their children.

According to the current review, children living in rural areas are likely to be immunized compared to urban children. It contradicts the common trend reported in the following studies.39–43 However, our finding aligns studies conducted in Kenya, Uganda and Ghana, which reported that rural children are more likely to be fully immunized.44–46 This finding may be explained by fewer rural immunization programs and outreach efforts in some regions, which can result in lower immunization rates among rural children.

Children born in a health facility are about twice as likely to be fully immunized compared to those born at home in our review which is consistent in previous studies conducted in Ethiopia and Kenya.34,43,47,48

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, although rigorous selection criteria and quality assessment were applied, substantial heterogeneity was observed across the included studies. This heterogeneity may be attributed to differences in study design, population characteristics, geographic settings, and variations in the measurement of immunization coverage and associated factors. Second, the review included only 11 studies, reflecting the limited availability of research on childhood immunization in Somalia. This relatively small number of studies may affect the precision and generalizability of the pooled estimates and limits the ability to conduct more detailed subgroup analyses. Third, most included studies relied on vaccination cards combined with caregiver recall, which may introduce recall bias and potentially lead to overestimation or underestimation of immunization coverage.

This systematic review and meta-analysis provides one of the most comprehensive syntheses of evidence on childhood immunization coverage and its determinants in Somalia to date. By systematically identifying, appraising, and pooling data from multiple studies, the review increases statistical power and provides more precise pooled estimates than individual studies alone. The inclusion of a large cumulative sample of children enhances the robustness and generalizability of the findings within the Somali context. Additionally, the study identifies key maternal, child, and health system–related determinants of immunization uptake, offering valuable insights for programmatic decision-making. The use of rigorous methodological approaches, including predefined inclusion criteria and meta-analytic techniques, strengthens the credibility of the evidence generated and provides a useful baseline for future research and policy planning.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrates that childhood immunization coverage in Somalia remains low, with substantial variation across regions and immunization indicators. Although uptake of at least one vaccine dose was moderate, completion of the basic immunization schedule was markedly poor. Place of delivery, antenatal care attendance, maternal education, and residence were significant determinants of immunization status. Despite high heterogeneity among studies, the findings provide robust evidence of persistent gaps in routine immunization services. Strengthening integrated maternal and child health services, improving service continuity, and addressing inequities in access are essential to improving childhood immunization coverage in Somalia.
