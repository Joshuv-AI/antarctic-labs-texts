African-ancestry-specific variant IKKβ p.Glu502Lys confers high lupus risk
Authors: Gudny Ella Thorlacius, Erna V Ivarsdottir, Saedis Saevarsdottir, Kristjan H S Moore, Sigurjon A Gudjonsson, Bjarni V Halldorsson, Hilma Holm, Aslaug Jonasdottir, Frosti Jonsson, Snaedis Kristmundsdottir, Gisli Masson, Olafur T Magnusson, Gudmundur L Norddahl, Asmundur Oddsson, Thorunn A Olafsdottir, Pall I Olason, Aron Skaftason, Lilja Stefansdottir, Arni Sturluson, Unnur Thorsteinsdottir, Gudmar Thorleifsson, Valentin Y Walker, Florian Zink, Eirikur Steingrimsson, Daniel F Gudbjartsson, Ingileif Jonsdottir, Agnar Helgason, Patrick Sulem, Kari Stefansson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCutaneous lupus erythematosus (CLE) is an autoimmune disease of the skin, occurring with or without systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE). People with African ancestry have a higher risk than people with other ancestries of developing lupus1 but have been underrepresented in genetic studies. We whole-genome-sequenced 27,820 Americans with genetically inferred African ancestry from the Diverse Ancestry Cohort, including people with CLE (n = 211) and/or SLE (n = 574). We discovered an association with a rare missense variant in IKBKB, rs115698972G>A, IKKβE502K, exclusive to people with African ancestry, conferring an odds ratio (OR) of 5.4 for CLE and 3.3 for SLE. These associations replicated in the All of Us and VA Million Veteran Research Programs for CLE (ORmeta = 3.8, Pmeta = 5.3 × 10−20, n = 1,243) and SLE (ORmeta = 3.2, Pmeta = 1.0 × 10−19, n = 1,697). In this cohort, IKKβE502K accounts for 10.4% of CLE cases and 6.4% of SLE cases, confers a high lupus risk, and contributes substantially to the disease prevalence among people with African ancestry. This highlights the value of including diverse ancestries in genetic association studies.Subject terms: Genome-wide association studies, Systemic lupus erythematosus

Main

There is substantial overlap between those who receive a CLE and SLE diagnosis, but SLE patients can present without cutaneous involvement and CLE patients can present without systemic manifestations. Lupus erythematosus can manifest in several ways that vary greatly among affected patients. Both the prevalence and presentation of CLE and SLE differ between people with African and European ancestries2–5. Severe manifestations such as lupus nephritis have a higher prevalence in Americans with African ancestry than in people with European ancestry1,6, and discoid lupus, one of the most severe forms of CLE, is over ten times more prevalent in people with African ancestry5.

Despite this, only 1 of the 33 studies on SLE presently documented in the genome-wide association studies (GWAS) Catalog (www.ebi.ac.uk/gwas) included people with African ancestry7. That study described six loci outside the human leukocyte antigen (HLA) region to associate with SLE in their African ancestry cohort subset: IRF5, BLK, ITGAM, two near PTTG1 and the PLAT-IKBKB locus. All but the PLAT-IKBKB association have been reported to associate with SLE in populations without African ancestry8. The GWAS Catalog contains just one study of CLE that included only people of European ancestry9 and reported associations with variants in the HLA region and ITGAM10. People with African ancestry are underrepresented in genetic studies on lupus, despite carrying the greatest disease burden. Therefore, we performed GWAS for CLE and SLE in a subset of the Diverse Ancestry Cohort (DAC) that includes Americans with genetically inferred African ancestry (Methods).

To search for African-ancestry-specific sequence variants that associate with CLE, we analyzed 340 million sequence variants identified through whole-genome sequencing (WGS) of 27,820 people from the DAC collected by the Alliance for Genomic Discovery11,12 (AGD) (www.nashbio.com) with genetically inferred African ancestry, hereafter referred to as Americans with African ancestry. The DAC dataset consists of patients from Vanderbilt University Medical Center (VUMC) health system in Nashville and primary-care clinics in Tennessee, USA. We identified a rare missense variant, rs115698972G>A, unique to populations with African ancestry (minor allele frequency (MAF) = 1.05%, European ancestry-MAF = 0.0005% (ref. 13)), to associate with increased risk of CLE (OR = 5.4, 95% confidence interval (CI) = (3.2; 9.3), P = 6.9 × 10−10) in 211 patients compared to 25,360 controls (Table 1 and Fig. 1a,b). This variant causes a substitution Glu502Lys (IKKβE502K) in exon 14 of the IKBKB gene encoding the inhibitor of nuclear factor kappa-B kinase (NFκB), subunit beta (IKKβ) and was the variant associated most significantly at the locus after weighted Bonferroni correction. No other variant at the locus was associated significantly after adjusting for IKKβE502K (Supplementary Table 1).

To determine whether the CLE-associated IKKβE502K also associates with SLE, we assessed the 574 SLE patients in the DAC and found a significant association (OR = 3.3 (2.2; 4.9), P = 5.3 × 10−9; Table 1, Fig. 1c,d and Supplementary Tables 2 and 3). The effect was present in both the isolated CLE and SLE patient populations, even when excluding people with both diagnoses (CLE and SLE; Supplementary Table 4). Strikingly, 11.1% of those with only CLE and no SLE diagnosis on record, and 10.3% of those with a combination of CLE and SLE, were carriers of this rare variant, compared to 2.0% of the controls (Fig. 2a). The penetrance of IKKβE502K was 7.1% for the combined CLE and SLE patient group. Of the quantitative traits available, IKKβE502K associated with reduced lymphocyte counts (effect = −0.21 s.d., P = 3.1 × 10−5; Supplementary Table 5). After accounting for multiple testing, IKKβE502K was not associated with other diseases available in the DAC dataset (Supplementary Table 6). As this cohort is captured via healthcare registries with limited options for clinical verification, it is possible that the effect of the IKKβE502K variant on lupus risk could be underestimated; however, the effect of this variant on CLE risk is currently, to our knowledge, the largest documented.

IKKΒE502K increases the risk of CLE fivefold in the DAC dataset. Resources with large-scale genotype and phenotype data derived from people with African ancestries are few, but people with this ancestry are necessary to replicate this association. Currently, the All of Us Research Program14 and the VA Million Veteran Program (MVP)15 are two of the largest studies available with data from approximately 23,000 and 121,000 people, respectively. The IKKβE502K association with CLE and SLE replicated in both datasets (Table 1). Meta-analyzing all three datasets strengthened the association of IKKΒE502K with CLE (ORmeta = 3.8 (2.9; 5.1), Pmeta = 5.3 × 10−20, ncases = 1,243, ncontrols = 169,847; Table 1) and SLE (ORmeta = 3.2 (2.5; 4.1), Pmeta = 1.0 × 10−19, ncases = 1,697, ncontrols = 169,574; Table 1). There was no significant heterogeneity between cohorts (Table 1).

To assess the influence of IKKΒE502K on disease presentation, we analyzed its effect on age at diagnosis, using age at first recorded instance of CLE/SLE codes (Fig. 2b). IKKΒE502K carriers received their first registered diagnosis 6.9 years earlier (95% CI = 1.9, 11.9) than noncarriers (mean age: 32.5 years in carriers, 39.4 years in noncarriers (P = 0.0069; Fig. 2b); 24.4% of carriers were diagnosed with lupus during childhood (<18 years old) compared to 11.6% of noncarriers (OR = 2.5 (1.1; 5.7), P = 0.029).

Both CLE and SLE are more prevalent in women than men16, and 90% of the lupus cases in the DAC were biologically female. Whereas the effect of IKKβE502K on the risk of SLE/CLE was similar for both sexes (Supplementary Table 7), the effect on age at diagnosis was greater in men than in women (P = 0.045; Fig. 2c and Supplementary Table 8). All affected male IKKΒE502K carriers (n = 4) had early-onset disease (16–23 years of age), whereas the average age at diagnosis for male noncarriers was 42.3 years (Fig. 2c and Extended Data Fig. 1). Most participants in the VA MVP Research Program are male; however, the difference in sex ratio did not affect the association with IKKβE502K.

Whereas CLE alone is often isolated to cutaneous involvement, SLE can present with a range of clinical and hematological manifestations. To assess whether IKKΒE502K influenced disease severity, we compared the frequency of clinical manifestations of SLE, including 16 relevant hematological traits available in the DAC, in IKKβE502K carriers to noncarriers (Fig. 3a–c). IKKβE502K carriers diagnosed with SLE were more likely to also receive a CLE diagnosis than noncarriers (OR = 2.3 (1.2; 4.5), P = 0.017; Fig. 3a). Furthermore, IKKβE502K carriers were more often diagnosed with glomerular disease (ICD-10 code M32.14) than noncarriers (OR = 2.3 (1.2; 4.7), P = 0.017; Fig. 3b), and they also had more protein in urine (effect = 0.45 s.d. (0.04; 0.86), P = 0.032; Fig. 3c). Among SLE patients, IKKΒE502K carriers had, on average, lower leukocyte, lymphocyte and reticulocyte counts than noncarriers, indicating more hematological manifestations (Fig. 3c), but with no significant difference in other SLE subtypes or secondary diagnoses (Supplementary Table 9). These results indicate that SLE patients carrying IKKΒE502K have more cutaneous, renal and hematological manifestations than noncarriers with SLE.

Although the associations with variants in IKBKB reported here are new, previous studies have found variants at the IKBKB locus to associate with SLE in populations of other non-European ancestries. A meta-analysis on cohorts of people with reported East Asian ancestry collected in mainland China, Japan and Korea described a missense variant in IKBKB, IKKβR526Q (rs2272736_A) to associate with reduced risk of SLE (OR = 0.82, P = 6.4 × 10−11)8. IKKβR526Q is common in the East Asian cohort studied (MAF = 10.5%) but effectively absent in populations with mainly European or African ancestry (MAF = 0–0.1% in gnomAD).

Variants in the PLAT-IKBKB region have been described to associate with SLE in a study that included Americans with African ancestry; however, IKBKB was not identified as a potential causal gene7. Using the Immunochip array, Langefeld et al.7 found a stop-gain variant, rs1804182_A, in the IKBKB-neighboring gene PLAT to associate with SLE risk (OR = 1.94, P = 3.5 × 10−8) specifically in the study subset defined as African American (MAF = 1.93%, MAFEUR = 0.00%). In the current DAC cohort, that variant, rs1804182_A, is associated with SLE (OR = 2.8, P = 2.1 × 10−5) and correlated with IKKβE502K (r2 = 0.44). Conditional analysis adjusting for IKKβE502K showed that the association between rs1804182_A and SLE is explained completely by IKKβE502K (ORadjusted = 0.78, Padjusted = 0.56), showing that IKKβE502K is driving the association at the locus.

NFκB signaling is a multistep pathway involved in numerous important processes in the immune system and beyond. IKKβ is one of three components of the IKK complex, which also includes IKKα and IKKγ. Upon activation of the canonical NFκB pathway, the IKK complex phosphorylates the inhibitory IκB proteins, leading to their ubiquitination and degradation by the proteasome. This allows the NFκB transcription factor to translocate to the nucleus and activate target gene expression17.

Rare coding-sequence variants in IKBKB have been reported to cause severe combined immunodeficiencies (SCID), including immunodeficiency syndromes 15 A and 15B. The presentation of these syndromes includes susceptibility to infection, abnormal lymphocyte count and function18,19, and a range of skin manifestations20.

The CLE-associated IKKβE502K variant represents a G>A change in exon 14 of 24 in the IKBKB gene that leads to a glutamic acid to lysine change at a conserved position 502 in a turn near the C-terminal NEMO-binding end of the IKKΒ protein21 (Extended Data Fig. 2a) within the annotated scaffold dimerization domain (SDD) that is thought not to participate in IKKΒ kinase activity22. Variants in the IKKβ kinase domain have been associated with SCID18, and variants in the IKKβ ubiquitin-like domain have been reported to cause B and T cell functional defects23. However, the functional effects of variants in the SDD (Extended Data Fig. 2b) such as IKKΒE502K are harder to predict. A case report of another IKKβ-SDD domain variant, IKKβT559M, described a gain-of-function effect, with increased phosphorylation in IKKβT559M cells24.

IKKβE502K is associated with reduced lymphocyte counts (effect = −0.21 s.d., P = 3.1 × 10−5; Supplementary Table 5) but shows no significant associations with infectious phenotypes (Supplementary Table 10), indicating that IKKΒE502K does not result in measurable immunodeficiencies. However, further studies are needed to elucidate the impact of variants in the IKKβ-SDD on protein function.

IKKβE502K has a frequency of 1.09% in reference datasets derived from people labeled as African American and can be detected in other groups reported previously to carry African genomic ancestry25, but is effectively absent from people labeled as European or Asian (PAGE study/gnomAD; Fig. 4a). The variant has a frequency of 1.0–1.2% in the study populations included in the present study. Within the UK Biobank, IKKβE502K reaches its highest frequency of 1.7% among people born in West and Central Africa (Fig. 4b), consistent with IKKβE502K being an African-ancestry-specific variant.

All loci described by Langefeld et al.7 to associate with SLE in the subset defined as African American replicated in our study of the DAC lupus cohort (Supplementary Table 11). Outside the IKBKB locus, reported lupus variants did not exhibit sufficient difference in frequency between ancestry groups to account for the observed differences in disease prevalence. We estimate the population attributable risk of the IKKβE502K variant to be 4.3% for SLE and 8.0% for CLE and, by leveraging reported incidence rates for SLE and CLE in the Southeastern USA26,27, we estimate that the IKKβE502K variant alone explains approximately 6.4% of the observed difference in SLE and 11.0% of the difference in CLE incidence rates between people of African and European ancestry.

Despite the robustness of the reported associations, this study has limitations. All three datasets included in the meta-analysis identified CLE and SLE patients using diagnostic codes in clinical records. To minimize potentially misdiagnosed cases, a minimum of two separate instances of diagnostic codes on file were required for inclusion in the discovery cohort. Furthermore, the data available for the replication studies were limited to summary statistics. Thus, it was not possible to assess potential overlap between the cases included in these studies. As the association with the IKKβE502K variant was significant in more than one cohort before meta-analysis, we believe that these limitations do not impact the findings. Furthermore, we acknowledge that the relatively small number of cases in this study may limit the power to detect additional associated variants, and further studies with larger case cohorts are needed to uncover the full genetic contribution to this disease.

Increasing diversity in genetic studies will increase variant discovery, particularly variants with large effects, which are more likely to be population specific28, where IKKβE502K is a striking example. Despite having the largest effect of all known non-HLA sequence variants on lupus risk, this association was undetectable in previous studies conducted in populations of European and East Asian ancestry, as it is found almost exclusively in populations with African ancestry, who have the greatest diversity of sequence variants29,30. Efforts such as the DAC, All of Us and MVP as well as the upcoming Our Future Health Research Program will be instrumental in improving our understanding of the genetic landscapes in diverse ancestries and their influence on disease.

Our discovery of an ancestry-specific variant that greatly increases the risk of lupus in a cohort of Americans with African ancestry highlights the importance of expanding GWAS to more populations with African ancestry31, and provides a tangible example of what can be learned by including hitherto neglected populations in genetic studies.

Methods

Ethics

The research conducted using the dataset generated by the AGD was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of VUMC. Data included from the VA MVP and the All of Us Research Program were limited to summary statistics. We confirm that our research complies with all relevant regulations regarding the use of data from human participants and was conducted in accordance with the criteria set by the Declaration of Helsinki. Participants received no compensation.

Cohort description

Nashville Biosciences (NashBio) is a data and analytics provider wholly owned by VUMC. NashBio harnesses the extensive genomic and bioinformatics resources of VUMC, including its biobank collection BioVU. BioVU includes a collection of deidentified DNA samples that are linked to deidentified version of the VUMC’s electronic health records, referred to as the Synthetic Derivative (SD) database. The SD contains longitudinal clinical data from more than 3.6 million patients cared for at the VUMC’s hospitals and primary-care clinics since 2001. Germline DNA samples from more than 307,000 patients have been collected and, thereof, 250,000 samples were selected for WGS under the AGD, which is a collaboration between NashBio/VUMC and Illumina on one hand, and eight pharmaceutical companies (including Amgen/deCODE) on the other. The aim of the AGD is to accelerate development of therapeutics through large-scale genomics and to establish a preeminent clinical genomic resource for diverse ancestries. The DAC included in this study is a subset of the BioVU cohort, consisting of 35,080 WGS people of diverse ancestries, primarily African ancestry. We further restricted our study sample set to 27,871 study participants that we assessed as carrying African genomic ancestry by analyzing their genotypes with supervised ADMIXTURE (v.1.23)32 using the 1000G (1000 Genomes) populations CEU (Utah Caucasian), CHB (Beijing Han), ITU (Indian Telugu), PEL (Peruvian) and YRI (Nigerian Yoruba) as training samples33. We included people who were modeled by ADMIXTURE as either (1) carrying >90% YRI-like ancestry or (2) carrying >30% YRI-like ancestry, >2% CEU-like ancestry and YRI-like plus CEU-like ancestry proportions summing to >90% (Supplementary Fig. 2). Both inhouse and previously published analyses of human diversity datasets such as Human Origins34 indicate that this filtering enriches for genomes similar to those carried by reference dataset samples labeled as African American (for example, 1000G ‘African Ancestry in Southwest USA’) or Afro-Caribbean (for example, 1000G ‘African Caribbean in Barbados’) as well as those of West African origin (for example, 1000G ‘Yoruba in Ibadan, Nigeria’) whose genomic ancestry is related to the African genomic ancestry in African Americans and Afro-Caribbeans. We note that our subset was defined solely on the basis of genomic ancestry.

All participants consented to their residual samples from routine clinical testing and data being contributed to BioVU. BioVU extracts and banks germline DNA samples that are deidentified and linked to the SD only through a randomly assigned unique identifier, not back to the patient or their underlying medical record. The use of BioVU is classified as nonhuman subject research by VUMC’s IRB, and the research conducted using the dataset generated by the AGD was approved by VUMC’s IRB. The overall biobanking program is reviewed annually by the IRB to maintain this determination and make decisions about patient protections, privacy and ethical issues. Each individual study seeking to use the SD database and BioVU biobank is filed with VUMC’s IRB to maintain this nonhuman subject determination and appropriate use of the data. NashBio takes steps to protect client confidentiality during this submission.

For association analyses, CLE cases were defined as those with two or more instances of the ICD-10 code L93.0 or any subcode, or ICD-9 695.4 recorded. SLE cases were defined as those with two or more instances of the ICD-10 code M32 or subcode, or ICD-9 710.0 recorded. Average year of birth for the CLE cases was 1975, and 89.6% were female. Average year of birth for the SLE cases was 1972, and 90.2% were female. People with a record of the following ICD-10 codes (or corresponding ICD-9) and subcodes were excluded from the control population: L93, M03, M05, M30, M32 and M36.

Whole-genome sequencing

DNA samples

DNA samples from NashBio were received on dry-ice in two-dimensional barcoded Matrix tubes (Thermo Scientific) in 96-well racks in a volume of 70 µl and a target concentration of >20 ng µl−1. Samples and plates were registered in our inhouse database and stored at −20 °C in an automated Hamilton Verso freezer until use. Samples were measured for absolute concentration using the DropSense 96/LabChip DS method for ultraviolet–visible spectrophotometry analysis of small droplets. Samples were diluted as needed and transferred to 96-well PCR plates in a volume of 25 µl at a concentration of 20 ng µl−1 (500 ng) for generating sequencing libraries.

Sample preparation

Sequencing libraries were generated using the Illumina DNA PCR-free method from Illumina with an input of 500 ng per sample (https://www.illumina.com/products/by-type/sequencing-kits/library-prep-kits/dna-pcr-free-prep.html). This method is based on ‘tagmentation’ of gDNA, which involves using Illumina’s bead-linked transposome technology for DNA fragmentation, library preparation and library normalization (https://www.illumina.com/techniques/sequencing/ngs-library-prep/tagmentation.html). The procedure was performed using a fully automated workflow on the Hamilton NGS STAR with the employment of a 96-well head and an on-board thermal cycler with a total run time of 1.5 h per 96-well sample plate. Samples were indexed using the DNA UD dual index adapter sets A, B, C or D from Illumina. Quality (concentration and insert size) of sequencing libraries was determined using the LabChip GX (96-samples) instrument (Perkin Elmer).

Sequencing libraries were pooled appropriately using Hamilton STARlet liquid handlers. Paired-end sequencing on the S4 flowcell (v.1.5 chemistry) was performed with a read length of 2 × 151 cycles of incorporation and imaging, in addition to 2 × 10 index cycles. Real-time analysis involved conversion of image data to base-calling in real time. Run quality control monitoring involved the use of flowcells and/or flowcell lanes that exceeded the quality threshold of %Q30 ≥ 85%. All steps in the workflow were monitored using an inhouse laboratory information management system with barcode tracking of all samples, plates and reagents.

Duplicated samples were discarded based on sequencing yield, and only samples with a genome-wide average coverage of 20× and higher were included, resulting in 34,427 samples. The average genome-wide sequencing coverage was 33.9× (s.d. 3.5; min, 28.7×; max, 66.4×), and joint variant calling was performed using GraphTyper35 (v.2.7.5). All variants tested had sequencing information over 0.8. Variant Effect Predictor36 was used to assign predicted consequences to the sequenced variants.

Association analysis

We used logistic regression to test for association between sequence variants and binary traits, regressing trait status against expected genotype count. We adjusted for sex, age (first and second order terms) and sequencing batch by including these variables in the logistic regression model. Furthermore, associations were adjusted for population structure using the top seven principal components (PCs) calculated by bigsnpr37. For PC calculation, we included variants with MAF > 0.5% LD-pruned by PLINK38–indep-pairwise 60000 6000 0.4, and samples without third-degree or closer relatives according to KING39 –ibdseg, using bigsnpr’s OADP-projected coordinates for samples with relatives. To better account for ancestral diversity, all samples regardless of assigned YRI-like ancestry proportion were used to calculate PCs.

The 92 quantitative traits available in the DAC dataset were rank-based inverse normal transformed to a standard normal distribution for each sex and adjusted for age using a generalized additive model. We used a linear mixed regression model implemented in BOLT-LMM40 to test for association between sequence variants and quantitative traits.

To account for multiple testing, we used a weighted Bonferroni adjustment using as weights the enrichment of variant classes with predicted functional impact among association signals41. This yielded significance thresholds of 8.0 × 10−8 for loss-of-function variants, 1.6 × 10−8 for moderate-impact variants, 4.9 × 10−9 for low-impact variants, 7.2 × 10−10 for other variants within DHS sites and 2.4 × 10−10 for remaining variants.

Population attributable risk

We estimated the population attributable risk of the IKKβE520K variant for SLE/CLE as PAR=f(OR−1)/(1+f(OR−1)), where f=0.020 is the carrier frequency in the control group and OR is the estimated OR from the association between IKKβE520K and SLE/CLE. We estimate the contribution of IKKβE520K to the difference in SLE/CLE incidence rates (IR) between people of African and European ancestry as, IR1PAR/(IR1 − IR2), where IR1 are reported incidence rates for people of African ancestry and IR2 are reported incidence rates for people of European ancestry. For SLE, we used IR1 = 10.9 and IR2 = 3.6 (ref. 27), and for CLE, we used IR1 = 6.2 and IR2 = 1.7 (ref. 26).

Replication analysis

Replication of the detected association was attempted on the following independent datasets that include Americans with African ancestry collected in the USA.

The All of Us research program

The All of Us research program14 (https://allofus.nih.gov/) is a nationwide research initiative in the USA aiming to collect and study data from people of varied ancestries. Replication of the association of IKKβE502K with CLE (phecode 695.4) and SLE (phecode 695.42) was performed at the University of Iceland BioMedical Center at the laboratory of E. Steingrimsson and colleagues who have controlled tier access to data from the All of Us research program as part of their approved study with the project title: ‘A multi-ancestry analysis of the genetic architecture of complex diseases.’

The VA MVP

The MVP (https://www.research.va.gov/MVP/research.cfm), from the US Department of Veteran Affairs, is one of the largest biobanks in the USA where people with diverse ancestries have systematically been included. The MVP has used electronic health records to create a large collection of binary and quantitative phenotypes that have been tested with genetic markers to generate genome-wide associations for vast lists of phenotypes in groups of people of previously understudied ancestries (genetically similar to African, Admixed American and East Asian reference populations). Following the publication of a study by Verma et al.15 on the diversity and scale of the VA MVP, the summary statistics for a large set of markers and phenotypes within those populations have been made available via dbGAP (accession number phs002453). The summary statistics for the association of the IKKβE502K variant with CLE (phecode 695.4) and SLE (phecode 695.42) were downloaded and included in the meta-analysis.

Meta-analysis

We used a fixed-effects inverse variance method42 based on effect estimates and s.e. from the DAC, the All of Us study and the VA MVP research program (see above) to meta-analyze the three cohorts together (see Aegisdottir et al.43 for more information on the applied methodology).

Variant frequency map

Data from the UK Biobank were utilized to create a frequency map (Fig. 4). UK Biobank participants were first grouped by birth country. We then defined regional ancestry groups with the aims that they be representative of the region’s current population, be homogeneous by genetic ancestry, and have at least 200 people (for accurate estimation of variant frequencies).

We assessed the current genetic ancestry profiles of regions by: comparing our ancestry analyses44 to inhouse and published results of human genome diversity datasets such as Human Origins34 and HGDP45; comparing genetic ancestry results across neighboring countries; surveying country demographics through resources such as The World Factbook46; and examining participants’ self-reported ethnicity information and UK census data47 to determine the extent to which people who migrated to the UK were representative of the source countries’ current demographic composition.

In some cases, we split off ancestry-based groupings representing distinct populations or unrepresentative migrant communities (for example ‘South Asian ancestry born in Africa and West Asia’) to achieve homogeneous birthplace-based groupings. Groups depicted on map figures are those best representing the current demographic majority. If countries had fewer than 200 participant birthplaces, we merged them with neighboring countries with similar assessed ancestry profiles. Map geometries were obtained via R package maps48 and manipulated with sf49.
