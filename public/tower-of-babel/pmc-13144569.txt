Reply to ‘Preoperative anxiety and chronic postsurgical pain: a signal worth acting on, but clinical translation needs greater nuance’. BJA Open 2026; 18: 100558
Authors: Mirjam Bakker-Bons, André P. Wolff, Remko Soer
Published: 2026

Editor

We thank Drs Mistry and Nair for their interest1 in our systematic review and meta-analysis.2 We acknowledge the importance of maintaining a critical perspective regarding the clinical interpretation of the results and appreciate the time taken to critically evaluate our article. The authors have raised several questions in their commentary, to which we would like to respond.

First, the authors challenge the restriction to general anaesthesia alone, as this limits generalisability. Our review focused exclusively on the impact of preoperative anxiety on chronic postsurgical pain (CPSP). This choice was deliberate because we assumed that including patients who are awake during surgery might experience intraoperative anxiety, which could bias our results. We agree that studying the effect of intraoperative anxiety, as well as other factors, on the development of CPSP would also be valuable, as we have mentioned in the discussion section of our article.

Second, the authors question the explanation for the observed high heterogeneity and suggest conducting an individual participant data (IPD) analysis instead. We agree that a subgroup analysis of surgical, analgesic, and psychosocial factors would improve the results. However, as outlined in our article, this was not feasible due to the high heterogeneity within the dataset. While using IPD could indeed clarify this issue, the task would be nearly impossible for our group, given the 23 studies included. Furthermore, in many cases, an IPD meta-analysis yields results and conclusions similar to those of an aggregate-data meta-analysis, as shown in the review by Tudur Smith and colleagues.3 As a result, we restricted subgroup analyses to confounders for which sufficient data were available. The authors also highlight that results should only be interpreted as a population-level association rather than for the individual. We agree with this suggestion, which was part of our original conclusion (page 9: ‘Given the small effect size observed in this meta-analysis, it is not possible to draw firm conclusions about the clinical implications at the individual level’).

Third, the authors note that conducting a formal Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) assessment would have improved transparency. However, the current GRADE method is not suitable when only observational studies are included. The Risk Of Bias In Non-randomised Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tool is considered an appropriate alternative to replace GRADE in such cases.4

Fourth, the authors challenge the methodology for translating the outcome into a clinically relevant scale and caution against its interpretability. We believe that the methodology we used for this translation is correct, as it followed the current scientific recommendations from the Cochrane Group.5 We agree with the authors that translating the outcome to a more clinically relevant scale should be interpreted cautiously; we have noted this at several points in the discussion (e.g. ‘given the high amount of heterogeneity in the reviewed literature, the magnitude of clinical relevance on the observed effect is unknown’, ‘it is not possible to draw firm conclusions about the clinical implications’, and ‘more research is needed, particularly regarding the impact of anxiety reduction on CPSP’).

Fifth, the authors note that the resulting significant association between preoperative anxiety and CPSP does not indicate that treatment of anxiety would reduce the incidence of chronic pain. In discussing the clinical implications of our results, we did not imply this; instead, we advised that more research is needed to clarify the matter, especially given the high heterogeneity observed (see also point four for examples).

Sixth, the authors add a practical point concerning the exclusion of papers due to incomplete data. We agree that it would be better to obtain raw data, but this was not feasible in our case. The authors furthermore challenge the interpretation of funnel plot asymmetry, which we also questioned in our discussion (page 9: ‘Although the funnel plot showed an asymmetric distribution, this might not be attributable to publication bias, but rather a result of baseline risk or sample size. In this meta-analysis, we used SMDs with continuous data; however, there is currently no reliable or valid method to determine the presence of publication bias for these type of data’).

Finally, the authors add that standardisation of assessment timing and alignment with the International Association for the Study of Pain (IASP) is essential for reproducibility. We agree with this, as outlined in our discussion (page 8: ‘For future research, it is recommended to use standardised methods to increase comparability’).

In conclusion, we agree with the authors that our manuscript supports an association between preoperative anxiety and CPSP following general anaesthesia. We have carefully highlighted the limitations regarding the possible clinical implications of our findings in the manuscript. Further research is needed to determine whether preventing anxiety could reduce the incidence of CPSP. However, we believe that future research should focus on patients at higher risk for both preoperative anxiety and CPSP, which could enable the development of more targeted therapies to decrease the occurrence of CPSP.
