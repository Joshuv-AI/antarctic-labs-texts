Curative Locoregional Therapy following Objective Response to Atezolizumab plus Bevacizumab in Unresectable Hepatocellular Carcinoma
Authors: Yu-Yun Shao, San-Chi Chen, Kun-Ming Rau, Teng-Yu Lee, Stephen L Chan, Ken Liu, Hsueh-Chou Lai, Jacob George, Cameron Gofton, Siraphong Putraveephong, Yin-Hsun Feng, Ming-Yang Lee, Jie XinJoycelyn Lee, David Tai, Suat Ying Lee, Yen-Hao Chen, Tsung-Che Wu, Lein-Ray Mo, Wan Zamaniah WI, Cosmas Rinaldi A Lesmana, Cheng Vai Hui, Shu Sen Sam Li, Irsan Hasan, Landon L Chan, Melinda Nguyen, Madhawa De Silva, Chien-Huai Chuang

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAtezolizumab plus bevacizumab (Atezo-Bev) is a standard first-line systemic therapy for unresectable hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). We aimed to explore the benefits of adding curative locoregional therapy (LRT) to patients who achieve a partial response (PR) to Atezo-Bev treatment.MethodsThis multinational retrospective study enrolled patients who received Atezo-Bev as first-line systemic therapy for unresectable HCC and reached complete response (CR) or PR. Patients were categorized into three cohorts: patients who underwent curative LRT after PR, patients with PR but did not receive curative LRT, and patients achieving CR without LRT. Curative LRT included surgery, ablation, and definitive radiotherapy with imaging evidence showing disease-free status afterward.ResultsA total of 243 patients were enrolled from 8 countries or regions; 31 patients received curative LRT after PR, 180 patients obtained PR but did not receive curative LRT, and 32 patients achieved CR without LRT. The curative-LRT group, compared with patients who did not receive curative LRT after PR, exhibited significantly longer PFS (median, 44.4 months vs. 18.2 months, p = 0.004) and overall survival (OS) (median, not reached vs. 34.3 months, p < 0.001). The differences remained significant after inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) adjustment. By contrast, whether before or after IPTW adjustment, the curative-LRT group did not exhibit statistically significant difference in PFS, OS, and recurrence-free survival from patients who obtained CR without curative LRT.ConclusionCurative LRT may consolidate the efficacy of Atezo-Bev in patients achieving a PR, potentially prolonging survival compared to systemic therapy alone.Keywords: Ablation, Targeted therapy, Immunotherapy, PD-L1, Surgery

Introduction

Unresectable hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) remains a formidable clinical challenge. For over a decade, multikinase inhibitors such as sorafenib served as the primary standard of care for patients ineligible for locoregional therapy (LRT), though they offered modest objective response rates ranging from 3 to 18% based on RECIST version 1.1 [1–3]. The therapeutic landscape underwent a paradigm shift with the results of the IMbrave150 trial, which established the combination of atezolizumab (a programed death-ligand 1 inhibitor) and bevacizumab (a vascular endothelial growth factor inhibitor) as the preferred first-line systemic therapy [4, 5]. This combination demonstrated significant improvements in overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) compared to sorafenib, achieving an objective response rate of approximately 30%. Subsequently, other dual immunotherapy combinations have also shown superiority over multikinase inhibitors [6, 7], solidifying combination immunotherapy as the global standard of care for advanced HCC regardless of patient demographics [8].

However, achieving a complete radiological response (CR) remains relatively rare, occurring in only 8–12% of patients treated with atezolizumab plus bevacizumab (Atezo-Bev). The vast majority of responders achieve a partial response (PR) or stable disease. While PR is a strong prognostic indicator for improved survival [9–13], relying solely on maintenance systemic therapy is often insufficient. Viable tumor cells frequently persist within these partial responders [14], eventually leading to secondary drug resistance and disease progression. This clinical limitation has fueled interest in conversion therapy – a strategy utilizing potent systemic regimens to downstage unresectable tumors to a point where curative interventions, such as surgical resection or radiofrequency ablation, become feasible.

Despite emerging evidence suggesting that conversion surgery may prolong survival [15–17], there is a distinct lack of data specifically evaluating the efficacy of consolidative curative LRT for patients who have already achieved a PR to Atezo-Bev [18, 19]. It remains unclear whether proactive intervention with curative LRT during the “window of response” provides a significant survival advantage over continuous systemic therapy or if it can yield outcomes comparable to those of patients who achieve a CR spontaneously. While the TALENTop study recently explored surgery versus continued therapy in partial responders, the exclusion of patients with distant metastasis and the inclusion of both PR and stable disease patients – groups with potentially distinct biological behaviors – leaves critical questions unanswered for a broader clinical population [20].

To address this knowledge gap, we conducted a multinational study to analyze the survival outcomes of patients with unresectable HCC who achieved a PR to Atezo-Bev and subsequently underwent curative LRT. We compared their long-term clinical outcomes against two distinct cohorts: patients who achieved a PR but continued systemic therapy without curative LRT and patients who achieved a CR through systemic therapy alone.

Patients and Methods

Study Design and Study Population

This multinational retrospective study enrolled patients with histologically or clinically diagnosed HCC who received Atezo-Bev containing treatment as first-line systemic therapy and obtained CR or PR, according to RECIST version 1.1. Patients with fibrolamellar carcinoma or mixed hepatocellular cholangiocarcinoma and patients who received anti-CTLA4 antibodies simultaneously with Atezo-Bev were excluded. Patients must have initiated Atezo-Bev treatment before September 30, 2024, to ensure an adequate follow-up period. We also excluded patients who received combination LRT simultaneously with Atezo-Bev treatment, such as transarterial chemoembolization, hepatic arterial infusion of chemotherapy, transarterial radioembolization, radiofrequency ablation, and microwave ablation before obtaining objective tumor response to Atezo-Bev treatment.

Patients were divided into three cohorts. The main study cohort consisted of patients who received curative LRT, including surgery, ablation, and definitive radiotherapy, after obtaining PR to Atezo-Bev treatment. Before curative LRT, the patient’s disease must remain in PR without new lesions. After curative LRT, there must be imaging evidence using computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging to demonstrate disease-free status, according to RECIST version 1.1. The other two cohorts were comparative cohorts. One consisted of patients who obtained PR but did not receive curative LRT, but palliative LRT after PR status was allowed. The other comparative cohort comprised patients who obtained CR due to Atezo-Bev treatment alone.

Study Endpoints

The primary endpoint is the recurrence-free survival (RFS) of the curative-LRT cohort, which could be compared with that of the CR-without-LRT cohort. RFS was calculated from the date of curative LRT (in the curative-LRT cohort) or the date of reaching CR on imaging assessment (the CR-without-LRT cohort) to the date of disease recurrence or death, whichever came first. Secondary endpoints included the OS and PFS of all patients. OS was calculated from the date of initiation of Atezo-Bev treatment to death of all causes. PFS was calculated from the date of initiation of Atezo-Bev treatment to disease recurrence, disease progression, or death, whichever came first.

We reviewed the patients’ medical records to collect data regarding demographics, disease etiology, tumor extent, liver function reserve, treatment details and response, and survival outcomes. This study was approved by the Research Ethical Committee of National Taiwan University Hospital, Taiwan, and all the other participating hospitals and was registered at clinicaltrials.gov (NCT07091942).

Statistical Methods

The study utilized a convenience sampling method to select all patients who meet the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Continuous variables are summarized as mean (SD), and categorical variables as counts and percentages. To improve comparability between (1) the curative-LRT and CR-without-LRT groups and (2) curative-LRT and PR-without-LRT groups, we applied propensity score-based inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) in each treatment comparison for both the primary and sensitivity analyses, respectively [21]. IPTW was selected to estimate average treatment effects for the overall study population [22]. The propensity scores were derived from logistic regression models incorporating baseline characteristics, including age, sex, HCC etiology, tumor burden, liver function reserve, α-fetoprotein level, and performance status, listed in Table 1. Covariate balance after weighting was evaluated using absolute standardized mean differences, with values less than 0.10 indicating adequate balance. Any residual imbalances in baseline characteristics after IPTW were further adjusted in multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models (Firth’s penalized Cox model if suitable) to estimate adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs.

Time-to-event outcomes were analyzed using the Kaplan-Meier method, with between-group differences assessed by the log-rank test. The group other than the curative-LRT group served as the reference group in each treatment comparison. All statistical tests were 2-sided, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Analyses were conducted using SAS software, version 9.4 (SAS Institute). For statistically significant associations in the main analyses, we calculated E-values to evaluate the potential impact of unmeasured confounding. The E-value defines the minimum strength of association that an unmeasured confounder would need to have with both the exposure and the outcome to fully explain the observed significant effect.

To mitigate potential immortal time bias, where patients in the curative-LRT group must survive long enough to receive the intervention, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using a 60-day, 90-day, and 120-day landmark approach [23]. In this analysis, only patients who survived and remained progression-free for at least 60, 90, and 120 days, respectively, after achieving PR were included. Patients who received curative LRT before the landmark time were classified into the curative-LRT group; otherwise, patients were classified into the PR-without-LRT group. To conduct a sensitivity analysis among patients who achieved a PR, we replicated the IPTW analyses by treating curative LRT as a time-dependent variable. To account for this time-varying exposure, the follow-up period accumulated prior to the initiation of curative LRT was classified as the no-LRT group; patients crossed over to the LRT group only from the exact date of their LRT procedure.

Results

Patient Demographics

Twenty hospitals joined this study. A total of 243 patients were enrolled from Taiwan (n = 147; 11 hospitals), Australia (n = 37; 3 hospitals), Singapore (n = 21; 1 hospital), Hong Kong (n = 16; 1 hospital), Thailand (n = 8; 1 hospital), Macau (n = 5; 1 hospital), Indonesia (n = 5; 1 hospital), and Malaysia (n = 4; 1 hospital). Before starting Atezo-Bev treatment, the mean age was 66.3 years (SD 10.8), and the majority of patients were male (83.5%) (Table 1). Hepatitis B was the most prevalent risk factor (42.4%), followed by hepatitis C (23.5%), alcohol-related liver disease (11.5%), and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (9.9%). The vast majority of patients (92.2%) had preserved liver function as Child-Pugh class A and more than half (56.0%) were classified as ALBI grade 1. In terms of tumor burden, 75.3% of patients were classified as Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) stage C, with 43.2% presenting with macrovascular invasion and 42.0% having extrahepatic metastasis. The performance status was Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) 0 or 1 in 99% of patients, and 58.9% of the cohort had received prior treatment for HCC.

The patients were categorized into three groups. The main cohort consisted of patients who received curative LRT after PR (n = 31). The two comparative cohorts were patients who achieved PR but did not receive curative LRT (n = 180) and patients who achieved CR without LRT (n = 32). The curative-LRT group, when compared with the PR-without-LRT group, demonstrated significantly better baseline performance status (ECOG 0: 87.1% vs. 57.2%, p = 0.007) and a significantly lower proportion of patients with a Cancer of the Liver Italian Program (CLIP) score ≥2 (35.5% vs. 54.4%, p = 0.041). Comparisons between the curative-LRT group and the CR-without-LRT group showed generally similar demographics and tumor characteristics. However, the CR-without-LRT group had a significantly higher rate of prior HCC treatment compared to the curative-LRT group (84.4% vs. 51.6%, p = 0.005).

PFS and OS Outcomes

For the entire cohort, the median PFS was 25.5 months (95% CI, 18.1–32.9). The 24-month, 36-month, and 48-month PFS rate was 52.4%, 41.9%, 36.9%, respectively (Fig. 1a). The median OS was not reached. The 24-month, 36-month, and 48-month OS rate was 78.5%, 59.0%, 53.0%, respectively (Fig. 1b). These results were similar to previously published results demonstrating the prolonged treatment benefits for patients who obtained an objective tumor response to Atezo-Bev treatment [10, 11].

Progression-free survival (PFS) (a) and overall survival (OS) (b) of all enrolled patients; PFS (c) and OS (d) of patients grouped by their tumor response and whether curative locoregional therapy (LRT) was performed. p values conducted using log-rank test. CR, complete response; PR, partial response.

The three groups demonstrated significantly different PFS outcomes (p < 0.001; Fig. 1c). Patients who received curative LRT demonstrated superior PFS outcomes (median 44.4 months, 95% CI: 19.1–69.8) compared with patients who obtained PR but did not receive LRT (median, 18.2 months, 95% CI: 14.4–22.1) (p = 0.004). The PFS rate at 24 months, 36 months, and 48 months was 68.1, 68.1, and 36.3%, respectively, for the curative-LRT group and 44.6, 31.9, and 31.9%, respectively, for the PR-without-LRT group. When comparing the curative-LRT group to the CR-without-LRT group (median PFS: not reached), the curative-LRT group showed no significantly different PFS outcomes (p = 0.681). The 36-month PFS rates were 68.1 and 67.6%, respectively.

Similarly, the three groups demonstrated significantly different OS outcomes (p < 0.001; Fig. 1d). Patients who received curative LRT demonstrated superior OS outcomes (median not reached) compared with patients who obtained PR but did not receive LRT (median, 34.3 months, 95% CI: 19.7–49.0) (p < 0.001). The 24-month, 36-month, and 48-month OS rates were all 100% for the curative-LRT group, compared to 72.0, 47.9, and 40.2%, respectively for the PR-without-LRT group. Comparatively, the difference in OS between the curative-LRT group and the CR-without-LRT group did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.091), although the curative-LRT group maintained a 100% survival rate throughout the observation period while the CR group showed a 36-month and 48-month OS rate of 79.5 and 79.5%, respectively.

IPTW Adjustment and Sensitivity Analyses

Among patients who obtained PR, after IPTW adjustment (online suppl. Table S1; for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/lic/admag004; online suppl. Fig. S1 and S2), those who received curative LRT, compared with those who did not, exhibited significantly better PFS (p = 0.004; Fig. 2a), with an adjusted HR of 0.242 (95% CI, 0.082–0.713; E-value: 7.73). They also had significantly better OS (p < 0.001; Fig. 2b). The HR for OS was not estimable by the traditional Cox’ model because no deaths occurred in the curative-LRT group but can be calculated as 0.059 (95% CI, 0.004–0.993; E-value: 33.39) using the Firth’s penalized Cox model. Restrictive mean survival time also consistently showed better OS among patients who received curative LRT (online suppl. Table S2).

We performed multiple sensitivity analyses. To mitigate potential immortal time bias, we performed 60-day, 90-day, and 120-day landmark analyses after IPTW adjustment among patients who achieved PR, comparing those who did and did not receive curative LRT. The PFS advantages favoring curative LRT remained (online suppl. Table S3). Another sensitivity analysis using curative LRT as a time-dependent variable in the Cox proportional hazards model reported pointed estimates of HRs favoring curative LRT for prolonged PFS (HR 0.498, 95% CI 0.234–1.057) and OS (HR 0.054, 95% CI, 0.002–1.178). However, the wide confidence intervals reflected limited statistical power.

We also compared patients who received curative LRT after PR with those who achieved CR using IPTW (online suppl. Table S4; online suppl. Fig. S3 and S4). After IPTW and the multivariate adjustment, the two groups showed no statistically significant difference in PFS (p = 0.681; Fig. 2c) and OS (p = 0.091; Fig. 2d). Restrictive mean survival time was reported on online supplementary Table S5.

We calculated RFS for the curative-LRT group and the CR group, both of which reached disease-free status. There was no significant difference in RFS between the two groups (p = 0.255; Fig. 3a). The RFS rate at 24 months was 57.5% for the curative-LRT group and 66.1% for the CR group. After IPTW adjustment (online suppl. At the time of receiving curative LRT, all patients maintained Child-Pugh class A liver function, and the majority (74.2%) were classified as ALBI grade 1. The median time from the initiation of Atezo-Bev treatment to curative LRT was 8.1 months (range: 2.2–42.6), while the median time from achieving PR to LRT was 4.4 months (range: 0.1–35.8). Following the intervention, 19 (61.3%) patients resumed Atezo-Bev treatment, with a median interval from LRT to therapy resumption of 0.72 months.

We performed exploratory subgroup analyses to identify potential predictors of RFS among patients who received curative LRT. No significant differences were observed when stratifying by characteristics before LRT such as ALBI grade (p = 0.181), ECOG performance status (p = 0.376), or α-fetoprotein levels (p = 0.264) (Fig. 4). There were no significant differences in RFS based on the type of liver-directed LRT (hepatectomy vs. ablation vs. radiotherapy; p = 0.758) or whether the LRT was metastasectomy (p = 0.834). Timing of the intervention also did not significantly stratify outcomes; neither the time from treatment initiation to LRT (p = 0.689) nor the time from PR to LRT (p = 0.433) was associated with RFS. Finally, the resumption of Atezo-Bev after LRT did not result in a statistically significant difference in RFS (p = 0.380).

Discussion

Our study demonstrates that consolidation curative LRT was associated with favorable outcomes in patients who achieve a PR from Atezo-Bev treatment, yielding results not statistically significantly different from those who achieve a CR through systemic therapy alone. These findings suggest that integrating LRT into the management of partial responders can effectively consolidate the benefits of frontline immunotherapy. While previous studies have highlighted the potential of conversion therapy [15–17], especially for intermediate stage [24, 25], our analysis specifically targets the PR population, providing a clearer rationale for intervening during this optimal therapeutic window.

The survival advantage observed with curative LRT likely stems from the eradication of microscopic residual disease and drug-resistant clones that persist despite radiographic shrinkage. In our cohort, pathological analysis of tissue samples obtained during LRT revealed viable tumor cells in nearly half of the cases (8 of 18). While systemic therapy exerts selective pressure that may eventually trigger secondary resistance, curative LRT resets the disease burden. This transition from a strategy of disease control to one of surgical or ablative eradication may facilitate a true drug-free cure. This is particularly relevant given that even complete responders may face recurrence upon treatment discontinuation [11]; consolidative LRT offers a definitive local control mechanism that systemic therapy alone cannot guarantee.

Interestingly, our findings highlight a significant discordance between radiological and pathological responses. Among the 18 tissue samples analyzed from patients in radiological PR, 10 revealed a complete pathological response (no viable tumor cells). This mirrors findings in other HCC cohorts where a subset of patients with residual imaging abnormalities actually harbor only necrotic or fibrotic tissue [26]. Conversely, existing literature suggests that 30–40% of patients in radiological CR may still harbor viable microscopic disease. This discrepancy underscores a critical clinical insight: the decision to perform curative LRT in partial responders should perhaps be guided more by the technical feasibility of the intervention and the patient’s fitness rather than a strict reliance on achieving a perfect radiological response. The potential for advanced functional imaging, such as positron emission tomography, to better differentiate viable malignancy from treatment-related necrosis warrants further prospective investigation to refine this selection process.

However, the impact of patient selection cannot be overlooked. Our data indicate that patients who underwent curative LRT generally had superior baseline liver function and performance status compared to those in the PR-only group. While our IPTW matching and multivariate analyses were used to account for these imbalances, the superior survival in the LRT group likely reflects a synergy between the therapeutic intervention and favorable underlying tumor biology. Not every partial responder is a candidate for conversion; careful selection based on hepatic reserve and performance status is essential to replicate these favorable outcomes.

There are several limitations to this study. Its retrospective nature introduces inherent selection biases, as discussed regarding baseline characteristics. Notably, despite the application of IPTW matching, residual imbalances remained for critical clinical variables, such as ECOG performance status and Child-Pugh class. Although we adjusted for these specific residual imbalances using multivariable Cox proportional hazards models to calculate the final HRs, residual treatment-selection bias and unmeasured confounders cannot be entirely excluded. Furthermore, although this was a multinational effort, the sample size of the curative-LRT cohort remains relatively modest, potentially limiting the statistical power to detect subtle differences in RFS compared to the CR group. The limited number of cases may also restrict the ability to identify independent predictors of RFS. The heterogeneity of the LRT modalities employed, ranging from surgical resection to ablation and radiotherapy, reflects real-world clinical practice but precludes identification of a single optimal intervention. Nevertheless, our exploratory sub-analysis did not reveal significant differences in RFS across these varied modalities. In addition, there were no deaths among patients who received curative LRT at the last follow-up date, indicating a longer follow-up is needed to validate our findings and that immortal time bias might persist.

In conclusion, this study provides evidence that curative LRT may consolidate the efficacy of Atezo-Bev in patients achieving a PR, potentially offering survival outcomes superior to systemic therapy alone and not significantly different from Atezo-Bev complete responders. For selected patients with preserved liver function, achieving a PR should be recognized as a vital window of opportunity for curative conversion and the potential attainment of long-term survival benefits. Future prospective trials are warranted to further refine patient selection and the timing of these consolidation interventions.
