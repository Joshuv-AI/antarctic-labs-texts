Reductions in the white–gray functional connectome in preclinical Alzheimer's disease and their associations with amyloid and cognition
Authors: Lyuan Xu, Yu Zhao, Soyoung Choi, Muwei Li, Kurt G Schilling, Zhongliang Zu, Baxter P Rogers, Zhaohua Ding, Adam W Anderson, Bennett A Landman, John C Gore, Yurui Gao, for the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDThe magnitudes and patterns of alterations of the white–gray matter (WM–GM) functional connectome in preclinical Alzheimer's disease (AD), and their associations with amyloid and cognition, remain unclear.METHODSWe compared regional WM–GM functional connectivity (FC) and network properties in subjects with preclinical AD (or AD dementia) and controls (total n = 344). BACKGROUND

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is commonly perceived as a gray matter (GM) disease, evident from the loss of cortical tissue and associated cognitive impairment. 1 The classic amyloid cascade hypothesis implicates that amyloid beta (Aβ) accumulation triggers critical pathological processes leading to ultimate neurodegeneration in AD. 2 These pathological processes may also detrimentally affect white matter (WM) regions, as evidenced by abnormalities such as axonal damage, myelin loss, oligodendrocyte injury, reactive astrocytic gliosis, vascular stenosis, and sparse distribution of macrophages. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 These WM abnormalities can manifest early in preclinical AD, a stage defined by positive amyloid deposition without significant cognitive impairment in the National Institute on Aging–Alzheimer's Association framework, 8 compromising neural activity transmission between WM and GM, and contributing to functional deficits in brain networks even before clinical symptoms appear. 5 , 9 , 10

Recent advances in functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) methodology offer promise for evaluating the WM–GM functional connectome and its alterations in brain disorders. The WM–GM functional connectome is composed of a set of measures of WM–GM functional connectivity (FC) obtained from estimates of the temporal correlations of fMRI blood oxygen‐level–dependent (BOLD) fluctuations between WM and GM regions. As such, the WM–GM connectome differs from conventional fMRI network analyses and explicitly incorporates indicators of WM functional activities into brain network models, potentially capturing changes in the functional integrity of brain circuits not detectable by other means. 11 , 12 , 13

While previous studies have reported alterations in regional WM–GM FC in the prodromal and demented phases of AD, as well as their correlations with cognitive performances, 14 their occurrence in preclinical AD remains unclear. Moreover, systemic characteristics of WM–GM FC networks, particularly their global efficiencies and global clustering coefficients, which reflect network integration and segregation, have yet to be explored in the context of AD, especially in the preclinical stage. Assessing these network properties poses challenges due to the unconventional topology of WM–GM FC networks. We recently proposed a bipartite model to address this challenge and observed a widespread decrease in the global efficiency of WM–GM FC networks during healthy aging, 15 providing both methodology and motivation for the current study.

We aimed to explore changes in the WM–GM functional connectome in preclinical AD, focusing on both regional and systemic connectomic metrics, including pairwise WM–GM FC and network‐wise properties global efficiency and global clustering coefficient. We hypothesized that disruptions in FC of specific WM–GM pairs, and in properties of specific networks, would be significant and detectable in preclinical AD subjects. Additionally, we examined the associations of these metrics with amyloid burden measured by positron emission tomography (PET), and cognitive performance, demonstrating their relevance and correlation with other indicators of pathology. Furthermore, we extended our investigation into AD dementia to provide a comprehensive understanding of how the WM–GM connectome evolves across the AD continuum, and to confirm that the changes detected in preclinical AD manifest as more widespread disruptions in dementia.

METHODS

Participants

Data were sourced from the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) stages 2 and 3 (adni.loni.usc.edu; April 2012–July 2021). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and the research was conducted following protocols approved by the institutional review board at each participating site. The secondary analyses in the present study received approval from the institutional review board at Vanderbilt University Medical Center.

We initially included all the participants with baseline resting state fMRI (rs‐fMRI) scans from three groups: normal controls (NC; clinically unimpaired cognition and negative Aβ), preclinical AD (clinically unimpaired cognition and positive Aβ), and AD dementia. Clinically unimpaired cognition was primarily determined by a Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) of 0 and a Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE) of 24 to 30. Aβ positivity was identified by meeting at least one of the following four biomarker‐dependent criteria: (1) cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) Aβ < 459 pg/mL, 16 (2) standardized uptake value ratio (SUVR) of florbetapir (AV‐45) PET > 1.22, 17 (3) SUVR of Pittsburgh compound B (PiB) PET > 1.42, 18 and (4) SUVR of florbetaben (FBB) PET > 1.478. 19 We adopted this relatively loose inclusion condition with the purpose of including as many preclinical AD participants as possible in our major study. Inclusion criteria for AD dementia aligned with ADNI's definitions of AD dementia. 20

MRI

Image acquisition, preprocessing, and quality assurance

The MRI images were collected on 3T scanners of multiple models (vendors: GE, Philips, and Simens) distributed across 53 sites with a consistent imaging protocol. The acquisition parameters for rs‐fMRI image included an isotropic voxel size of 3.3 or 3.4 mm, repetition time (TR) of 3 seconds, echo time (TE) of 30 ms, and matrix size of 64 × 64 × 48. The parameters for T1 weighted (T1w) image acquisition include an in‐plane voxel size of 1 mm × 1 mm and a slice thickness of 1 or 1.2 mm. Please see Table S1 in supporting information for specific acquisition parameters for each site.

All MRI images underwent preprocessing using a robust WM‐tailored analysis pipeline detailed in a previous report. 21 Briefly, the resting state (rs‐fMRI) images were first corrected for slice timing and head motion effects. Then, 24 motion‐related parameters and the mean CSF signal were regressed out. 22 The resultant data were detrended and temporally filtered (passband = 0.01–0.1 Hz). These preprocessing steps were executed using customized modules within the Data Processing Assistant for Resting‐State fMRI toolbox. 23 Meanwhile, tissue probability maps (TPM) of GM, WM, and CSF were generated through segmenting T1w images using the Computational Anatomy Toolbox (CAT12). 24 Subsequently, the filtered rs‐fMRI data and TPMs were normalized to Montreal Neurological Institute space. Notably, to prevent partial volume influences between WM and GM, spatial smoothing was omitted during preprocessing.

The participants with too much head motion (rotation > 2° or translation > 2 mm) were removed from further analyses.

Evaluation of pair‐wise WM–GM FC and harmonization

For each participant, a WM–GM functional connectivity matrix was evaluated by calculating Pearson correlation coefficients between the regional averaged time series from 46 WM bundles (Figure 1A; Table S2 in supporting information) and 200 GM parcels (Figure 1B; Table S3 in supporting information), which were initially defined by the ICBM‐DTI‐81 WM atlas and Schaefer–Kong cortical atlas, 25 , 26 and further constrained within the participant's whole‐brain WM and GM masks, respectively. These masks were created by binarizing the participant's WM and GM TPMs with a highly conservative threshold (0.8) to minimize possible signal contamination between WM and GM.

To mitigate acquisition site effects, we applied the ComBat harmonization procedure to the FC values using the neuroComBat R package, with age, sex, and years of education included as covariates to account for variations. 27

Evaluation of network‐wise global efficiency and global clustering coefficient

To assess the network properties of the WM–GM FC, we adopted a novel analysis derived from graph theory which was described in our recent report (Figure 1C,D). 28 Unlike a traditional GM network, a WM–GM FC network is topologically a “bipartite” graph formed by two distinct groups of nodes (i.e., WM bundles and GM parcels) with exclusively between‐group edges (i.e., WM–GM FC), thereby intrinsically lacking closed triangular connection pathways necessary for conventional assessments of network properties. The adopted model addresses this challenge by projecting the bipartite graph to a weighted directional unipartite graph in which only GM nodes explicitly exist, with transformed WM–GM FC as edges, creating a WM‐mediated GM–GM FC network. where bki is the FC between the kth WM node and the ith GM node, and aij is the WM‐mediated FC from the ith to the jth GM nodes. This equation indicates that two GM nodes in the projected network are considered connected only if they share at least one common WM bundle in the original WM–GM bipartite network, and the strength of the connection from the starting GM node to the target GM node is quantified as the sum of WM–GM FC between the starting GM node and all the WM nodes shared by the two GM nodes. This projection allows for the assessment of WM–GM FC network properties.

The whole‐brain network included all 46 WM nodes and 200 GM nodes. We split the whole‐brain biadjacency matrix (size = 46 × 200) into 17 submatrices, representing 17 canonical functional networks including default A/B/C, language, control A/B/C, dorsal attention A/B, salience/ventral‐attention A/B, and limbic A/B, auditory, somatomotor A/B, and visual A/B/C networks (Figure 1E). 29 For each functional network or the whole‐brain network, we projected the bipartite graph to a unipartite graph and assessed the global efficiency and global clustering coefficient of the projected graph using the Brain Connectivity Toolbox (https://sites.google.com/site/bctnet). 30 Note that previous studies have indicated spatially heterogeneous alterations of FC within large functional networks in preclinical AD. 31 Thus, we chose the division strategy of 17 smaller functional networks instead of 7 larger networks to capture more subtle and consistent within‐network changes in preclinical AD.

After comparing the network properties between groups (details provided in the following statistics in section 2.5), we calculated an index to estimate the contribution of each WM bundle to the difference observed in network properties between groups. Further details are outlined in the supporting information.

Amyloid PET

Amyloid PET images were acquired using AV‐45 (18F‐florbetapir) and the detailed protocol has been previously described. 32 The AV‐45 measurements were represented as a cortical summary SUVR across a composite of lateral and medial frontal, anterior and posterior cingulate, lateral parietal, and lateral temporal regions, while the whole cerebellum served as the reference region.

Statistical analysis

Alterations of connectomic metrics in preclinical AD and dementia

Primary analyses compared the regional and network metrics of the WM–GM functional connectome between preclinical AD (n = 78) and NC (n = 217), and as an extension, between AD dementia (n = 49) and NC.

First, WM–GM FC values were statistically compared between groups (disease vs. control) at a significance level of P < 0.05 using network‐based statistics (NBS; https://sites.google.com/site/bctnet/network‐based‐statistic‐toolbox). 35 NBS is a validated approach for statistical analyses on large networks, calculating a family‐wise error rate (FWER) P value for each component (i.e., t value thresholded topological cluster). Specifically, to fit the input of this method, we constructed a “virtual” square symmetric matrix (size = 246 × 246), combining both WM nodes (n = 46) and GM nodes (n = 200) as virtual nodes. In this matrix, GM–GM FC and WM–WM FC were set to zero. A two‐sample t test, with a t value threshold of 3.1, was set for pairwise FC comparisons, generating initial component(s). Then, the FWER‐corrected P value was computed for each component using permutation testing (5000 permutations; significance level = 0.05). Using this NBS method, we identified WM–GM pairs with significantly disrupted FC values in the test group relative to the NC group. To further summarize these changes on a network basis, we divided the number of altered WM–GM pairs within the network by the total number of GM nodes of the network, a measure that indicates the degree of disruption of the network.

Second, mean global efficiency and global clustering coefficients for each network were calculated, and between‐group comparisons were conducted through permutation tests (10,000 permutations). The potential influences of covariates, including age, sex, and years of education, were controlled via multiple linear regression for all the network measures. Corrections for multiple comparisons were performed using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure.

The effect size (Cohen d) was evaluated for each of the above‐mentioned comparisons. Only P values that passed the FDR correction are presented in the text, but all uncorrected P values are indicated in the tables. The criterion for statistical significance was P < 0.05.

In addition to the primary analyses, we conducted further comparisons of WM–GM FC pairs and network properties between preclinical AD and AD dementia, following the same procedures mentioned above.

Associations of connectomic metrics with Aβ deposits and mPACC

Following inter‐group comparisons, complementary analyses were performed to determine whether the detected connectomic changes were related to the presence and extent of Aβ deposits (measured by PET AV‐45 SUVR) and cognitive performance (assessed using mPACC).

We first evaluated the correlations of WM–GM FC with Aβ level and mPACC score for each preclinically disrupted WM–GM pair among asymptomatic participants using general linear models (GLMs) controlled for age, sex, and years of education. Similarly, we assessed the linear correlations of network properties with Aβ level and mPACC score for each disrupted network. Multiple comparisons were corrected with FDR. In the mPACC correlation analyses, “asymptomatic participants” refer to those without clinical cognitive symptoms, specifically participants in the preclinical AD and NC groups (n = 295). For the Aβ correlation analyses, only these asymptomatic participants with available PET AV‐45 measures were included (n = 212). Moreover, we performed the same correlation analyses among all participants, including both asymptomatic participants and AD dementia patients. The mPACC correlation analyses included 344 participants, and the Aβ correlation analyses included 252 participants with PET AV‐45 measures.

Besides the correlation analyses, we also conducted dichotomy experiments to investigate the associations of network properties with Aβ level. We first categorized participants into two groups: Aβ negative (Aβ−) and Aβ positive (Aβ+). Given our relatively lenient inclusion criteria for preclinical AD, which required compliance with only one of the four conditions corresponding to four amyloid biomarkers, there was an overlap in PET AV‐45 SUVR values between the NC and preclinical AD groups. To ensure rigorous categorization of asymptomatic participants into Aβ− or Aβ+ categories for subsequent analysis, we defined participants with PET AV‐45 SUVR values below 1.09 as belonging to the Aβ− group (n = 113), while those with values above 1.25 (n = 55) were classified as the Aβ+ group. Subsequently, for each network and each network property, differences between the Aβ− and Aβ+ groups were examined using a permutation test (10,000 permutations), controlling for age, sex, and years of education, followed by FDR correction.

Linear correlation between Aβ level and mPACC score was also evaluated among the asymptomatic participants (n = 212) for the purpose of interpretation, adjusted for age, sex, and years of education.

RESULTS

Data from a total of 344 participants were analyzed: 217 NC participants (75.2 ± 7.4 years; 121 [56%] female), 78 patients with preclinical AD (77.0 ± 6.8 years; 64% female), and 49 patients with AD dementia (73.8 ± 8.6 years; 45% female). Additional demographic, genetic, Aβ level, and cognitive characteristics are summarized in Table 1.

Reduction of WM–GM FC in preclinical AD

We investigated differences in WM–GM FC across 46 WM bundles and 200 GM parcels and detected significant reductions in 166 WM–GM pairs in preclinical AD compared to NC (FWER‐corrected P = 0.009; Figure 2A left panel). These disrupted WM–GM pairs involved 22 out of 46 (48%) WM bundles and 83 out of 200 (42%) GM parcels. Categorizing these pairs into the 17 canonical functional networks according to the GM's classification, we observed the average counts of disturbed pairs within a network were higher (> 1) in somatomotor A/B, visual B, control C, and dorsal attention B in preclinical AD (Figure 2B), indicating uneven disruption across brain networks in preclinical AD.

In contrast, AD dementia exhibited substantially wider‐spread reductions relative to NC, with 516 WM–GM pairs significantly affected (FWER‐corrected P < 0.001) involving 40 (87%) WM bundles and 141 (71%) GM parcels (Figure 2A right panel). In addition to the networks disrupted in preclinical AD, many other networks had larger average counts of disturbed pairs (> 1) in dementia (Figure 2B).

Effect sizes (Cohen d) for these significant FC differences were predominantly in the lower moderate range for preclinical AD and the higher moderate range for AD dementia (Figure 2C), suggesting WM–GM FC differences have practical significance.

In the supplementary comparisons between preclinical AD and AD dementia, we found lower FC in 174 WM–GM pairs in dementia relative to preclinical AD (Figure S1 in supporting information).

Reduction of network properties in preclinical AD

Considering a WM–GM functional network as a cohesive system, the global efficiency and global clustering coefficient of the network indicate the degrees of integration and segregation of the system, respectively. To identify systemic disruptions in preclinical AD, permutation tests were conducted on these two network properties for each of the 17 WM–GM functional networks as well as the whole‐brain network.

After FDR correction, no significant changes in global efficiency persisted across all 18 examined networks at the preclinical stage (Figure 3A; Table S4 in supporting information). Conversely, substantially lower global efficiency was observed at the dementia stage in networks of default A (d = −0.30; P = 0.02); control B (d = −0.28; P = 0.04) and C (d = −0.31; P = 0.02); salience/ventral attention A (d = −0.42; P = 0.004) and B (d = −0.34; P = 0.02); dorsal attention A (d = −0.34; P = 0.01) and B (d = −0.49; P = 0.002); auditory (d = −0.43; P = 0.004); somatomotor A (d = −0.41; P = 0.005) and B (d = −0.46; P = 0.001); visual A (d = −0.36; P = 0.01), B (d = −0.43; P = 0.003), and C (d = −0.40; P = 0.006); and whole‐brain networks (d = −0.37; P = 0.01), suggesting a later onset of reduced integration in these networks.

These findings indicate that network segregation starts to be reduced in the preclinical AD stage, and spreads to more networks as the disease progresses.

In the supplementary comparisons, we found no significant changes in global efficiency and global clustering coefficient in AD dementia relative to preclinical AD (Figure S2 in supporting information).

Associations of reduced network properties with elevated Aβ level and reduced mPACC scores

Given the observed reductions of the global clustering coefficient present in four networks at the preclinical stage, we further investigated whether these reductions were associated with amyloid accumulation and impaired cognition. Among asymptomatic participants with PET AV45 scans (n = 212), we observed slight, albeit non‐significant, negative correlations in the four examined networks following FDR correction (Table S6 in supporting information). However, dichotomy analyses revealed that the Aβ+ group exhibited significantly lower global clustering coefficients in the control C (P = 0.01), dorsal attention B (P = 0.02), somatomotor A (P = 0.01) and B (P = 0.001) networks compared to the Aβ− group (Figure 5), suggesting an association between reduced network segregation and elevated Aβ level. Additionally, we did not find significant linear correlations between global clustering coefficient and mPACC in the four networks among the asymptomatic cohort (Table S6).

As both global efficiency and clustering coefficient were reduced at the AD dementia stage, we investigated the associations of these two network properties with amyloid level and cognitive performance among all participants. Across all participants with PET AV45 scans (n = 252), there were slight but non‐significant linear correlations found between global efficiency and Aβ level (Table S7 in supporting information). Meanwhile, the global clustering coefficient negatively correlated with Aβ in the networks of somatomotor A (R = −0.17, P = 0.007) and B (R = −0.18, P = 0.004), and visual B (R = −0.20, P = 0.002; Table S8 in supporting information). Additional dichotomy analyses showed that lower global efficiency and global clustering coefficient in the Aβ+ group extended to more networks (Figures S3 and S4 in supporting information). Moreover, among all participants, mPACC had non‐significant correlations with global efficiency but positive correlations with global clustering coefficient in eight networks, including language (R = 0.12, P = 0.03), control B (R = 0.14, P = 0.01) and C (R = 0.11, P = 0.03), salience/ventral attention A (R = 0.15, P = 0.004) and B (R = 0.12, P = 0.03), dorsal attention A (R = 0.14, P = 0.01), auditory (R = 0.13, P = 0.02), and somatomotor B (R = 0.12, P = 0.02; Table S8).

In addition, a linear correlation between Aβ and mPACC across the asymptomatic participants was observed (R = −0.25, P < 0.001).

DISCUSSION

We present new evidence of reductions in both regional and systemic metrics of the WM–GM functional connectome in preclinical AD, along with their associations with amyloid accumulation, as well as cognitive impairment. Specifically, substantially lower WM–GM FC and reduced network segregation (i.e., global clustering coefficient) were observed in control, dorsal attention, and somatomotor networks in preclinical AD relative to normal controls. A subset of the reduced FC and network segregation were associated with elevated amyloid and impaired cognitive performance. Furthermore, AD dementia exhibited broader reductions and stronger associations. These findings suggest that WM–GM functional connectome metrics are sensitive to early brain changes associated with AD, potentially serving as novel neuroimaging biomarkers for the early characterization of AD.

Dysfunction of cognitive networks in preclinical AD

The global clustering coefficients, indicative of network segregation, of the control C and dorsal attention B networks were reduced in preclinical AD (Figure 3B), suggesting a more diffuse or less specialized functional organization of these WM‐mediated networks present prior to the onset of symptoms. 30 These systematic degradations may be attributed to the greater severity of regional WM–GM disconnections within the networks, as indicated by the top rankings of the counts of disconnected WM–GM pairs within the networks relative to the total number of GM nodes within the networks (Figure 2B). Moreover, the control C and dorsal attention B networks in this study (Figure 1B) primarily harbor the anterior/posterior cingulate cortex and portion of medial frontal cortex (e.g., parcels labeled “ControlC_CingP” and “ControlC_PFCm” in Table S3), lateral superior parietal cortex (e.g., parcel labeled “DorsAttenB_SPL”), and a small portion of precuneus (e.g., parcel labeled “ControlC_pCun”). These regions are consistently considered hubs of structural connections assessed by diffusion MRI tractography, and their high level of centrality renders them susceptible to disconnection and dysfunction in neurodegenerative disorders, 36 , 37 including AD and preclinical AD. 38 , 39 Future multimodal studies that compare structural connectome and WM–GM functional connectome data may help further interpret our results.

While the default network has been consistently reported with reduced cortical FC and lower clustering coefficient in preclinical AD, 38 , 40 , 41 , 42 complex changes within the default network have been observed in both spatial and temporal dimensions. 43 , 44 For instance, Mormino et al. showed both decreases and increases in different cortical regions within the default network as Aβ accumulated in cognitively normal elderly subjects, speculating that the increase may represent compensatory activity. 31 Schultz et al. found a hyperconnectivity phase followed by a hypoconnectivity phase in the course of preclinical AD. 45 Combined, it is not surprising that we did not observe a substantial reduction in areas engaged in the default networks at the early stage but rather in the dementia stage.

Although our earlier study found that global efficiency in default mode, fronto‐parietal, limbic, attention, and somatomotor networks decreases with age during adulthood, 15 the current study did not find strong evidence for significantly lower global efficiency in the 17 finer‐grained networks in individuals with preclinical AD relative to controls after controlling for age. This suggests that the impact of preclinical AD on network integrations may not be as pronounced as the impact of aging.

In addition, the observed differences between preclinical AD and NC (Figures 2A and 3) as well as between AD and preclinical AD (Figures S1 and S2) suggest disruptions in WM–GM pairs and network properties occur in different temporal patterns.

Dysfunction of somatomotor networks in preclinical AD and contributions of motor‐related bundles

A pronounced preclinical reduction was observed in both regional FC and systemic segregation in the somatomotor networks, with motor‐related WM bundles (e.g., anterior limb of internal capsule, posterior limb of internal capsule, and superior cerebellar peduncle) contributing relatively more to these reductions (Figure S5). Conventional GM–GM FC studies have suggested that the somatomotor network tends to maintain the intra‐network connections at the preclinical stage 42 whereas diffusion MRI studies found reduced structural connectivity linked to the precentral gyrus, indicating early‐stage structural damage to motor‐connected fibers. 46 Collectively, these findings indicate that the abnormalities observed through WM–GM FC networks are not a complete replica of the GM–GM FC findings. Instead, there is a noticeable shift toward emphasizing functional abnormalities originating from relevant WM pathways, enabled by the explicit incorporation of WM into the functional connectome.

The observed functional disruption of the somatomotor network in preclinical AD has significant clinical implications, advancing our understanding of motor dysfunction's role in the early stage of AD, an area of increasing interest. 47 Recent evidence indicates that motor dysfunction (e.g., slow gait and decreased grip strength) may precede cognitive impairments in AD by several years and may signal an increased risk of AD. 48 , 49 , 50 Histopathological and diffusion MRI studies demonstrate that the structure of motor cortex along with its connected WM pathways are affected by AD pathology. 51 , 52 , 53 , 54 Even AD risk genes have been associated with several motor symptoms in AD. 55 , 56 Our findings provide the first fMRI evidence showing manifest functional degeneration of the somatomotor network at the preclinical AD stage, offering opportunities to link upstream factors (gene risk, pathology, structural damage) to downstream behaviors (motor symptoms).

Association with amyloid deposit

The associations of WM–GM FC and global clustering coefficient with Aβ levels among asymptomatic participants suggest that amyloid accumulation may account for the degradation of regional FC and network segregation at the preclinical stage, impacting both cognitive and other networks. In a large‐scale amyloid PET study, Aβ was found to preferentially accumulate early in the precuneus, medial orbitofrontal, and posterior cingulate cortices, spatially overlapping mostly with cognitive networks (default, control, and attention) and less with non‐cognitive networks (motor and visual). 57 However, our observations indicate that the most pronounced changes occurred in somatomotor networks. Several explanations may account for the inconsistency. For instance, histopathological evidence highlights that Aβ may first appear in sensory‐association areas in non‐demented patients, extending to the striatum, thalamus, and other motor‐related region. 58 Moreover, WM‐related impairments, such as myelin loss, 59 may moderate these associations observed in motor networks, particularly given the documented negative association of myelin water fraction with Aβ in preclinical AD 5 and the myelin being most heavily distributed on motor and visual pathways in the healthy population. 60

Association with mPACC

In our study, although the mPACC was statistically lower in the preclinical AD group relative to the NC group (P = 0.046; Table 1), the effect size of the difference was small (Cohen |d| = 0.27). This is because of the significant overlap in the mPACC of the two groups, as indicated by their large standard deviations relative to the means. Thus, it is not surprising that the mPACC showed a significant linear correlation with only one WM–GM FC after FDR correction across the asymptomatic participants. A recent study indicates that baseline GM–GM FC within cognitive networks presage longitudinal PACC decline in preclinical AD, 42 highlighting the need for similar longitudinal studies on the WM–GM functional connectome in the future.

When AD dementia patients were included in the analysis cohort, almost all reduced pairs and more than half of the reduced networks in dementia were correlated with mPACC, suggesting that WM–GM connectomic metrics may predict performance in episodic memory, timed executive function, and global cognition. 33 This progression from asymptomatic to symptomatic stages is consistent with previous findings in GM–GM FC networks detected by fMRI and electroencephalography. 61

Moreover, it is noted that the relationship between Aβ pathology and AD clinical symptoms is complex, especially given that preclinical AD, determined by the core biomarker—Aβ, does not always progress to clinical cognitive decline. 8 , 62 When considering tau pathology and subjective memory concerns (SMC), the decade‐long preclinical AD stage can be further divided into three substages: the first two without SMC and the third with SMC. A recent meta‐analysis study suggests that the risk of progression is ≈ 20% to 38% for the first two substages and 73% for the third substage. 63 Out of the preclinical AD cohort in our study, 73% (n = 57) had SMC and 27% (n = 21) had no SMC, leading to ≈ 53% to 67% risk of progression, which may account for the low variation of cognitive decline explained by Aβ in our study. Additionally, the correlation between Aβ and mPACC among the asymptomatic participants is consistent with previous findings indicating that amyloid deposition lacks a strong association with cognitive impairment before the onset of clinical symtoms, 64 whereas the significant alterations of the WM–GM connectomic metrics in preclinical AD suggest the potential clinical values of the WM–GM functional connectome.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, we relied on whole‐brain Aβ burden to associate with functional measures, whereas the regional amyloid load may offer spatially specific findings. Second, the structural complexity of WM bundles may contribute to functional complexity, suggesting that voxel‐wise analysis of WM may identify more subtle alterations in WM–GM FC. An additional limitation is the imbalance in the sample sizes across different groups, which may lead to biased estimates of group differences. To mitigate this effect, we chose permutation tests, which are non‐parametric and robust to unequal group sizes, to assess the differences between groups. Finally, this cross‐sectional study examined the associations of functional measures with pathology and cognition; these findings should be validated in longitudinal samples in the future to evaluate the time courses of these relationships.
