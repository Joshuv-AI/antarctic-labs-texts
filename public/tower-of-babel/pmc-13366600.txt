Biomechanical advantage of anterior over central cage placement in XLIF with bilateral pedicle screw fixation: a finite element analysis
Authors: Liang Wu, Jingxuan Feng, Ruijue Zhu, Peng Cui, Wei Zhang, Guangbao Li, Shenghui Huang, Xianhua Cai, Wenbin Wu, Lei He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis finite element study aimed to compare the biomechanical differences between anterior and central cage placement in L3-L4 XLIF supplemented with bilateral pedicle screw fixation, to evaluate the biomechanical impact of cage positioning on minimizing cage subsidence risk.MethodsThree L1-L5 finite element models were developed from healthy volunteer CT data: intact (Group A), L3-L4 XLIF with anterior cage placement (Group B), and central cage placement (Group C). Loads simulating flexion, extension, lateral bending, and rotation were applied. Segmental stability and cage stress distribution were analyzed.ResultsBoth surgical groups markedly decreased range of motion (ROM) at L3-L4 compared to Group A. The peak stress on the cage was consistently lower in Group B than in Group C across all motions. The screw-rod system stress in Group B was higher than in Group C in all motions except flexion. Facet joint stress in adjacent segments was higher in Group C than in Groups A and B.ConclusionIn this finite element model, anterior cage placement was associated with lower cage stress and higher posterior instrumentation stress than central placement, while segmental stability remained similar. These results indicate a different load-sharing pattern and suggest potential biomechanical differences in adjacent segment loading. Further research is needed to clarify the clinical implications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13018-026-06901-5.

Introduction

Extreme Lateral Interbody Fusion (XLIF) is a minimally invasive technique that achieves indirect decompression by restoring disc height through large cage placement [1, 2]. However, cage subsidence remains a critical complication affecting long-term outcomes [3]. The optimal cage position to minimize subsidence is controversial: while some studies suggest posterior placement reduces subsidence risk [4], others advocate anterior placement, leveraging the stronger mechanical support of the anterior bony endplate and apophyseal ring [5, 6]. Anterior positioning may also better facilitate lumbar lordosis restoration without compromising indirect decompression [7].

Supplemental bilateral pedicle screw fixation is widely used to enhance stability and reduce subsidence risk, especially in osteoporotic patients [8]. Finite element analysis (FEA) provides a powerful tool to evaluate biomechanical performance under physiological loads [9, 10]. This study used finite element analysis (FEA) to compare the biomechanical effects of anterior versus central cage placement in XLIF with posterior fixation, focusing on subsidence-related mechanical factors and adjacent segment biomechanics.

Materials and methods

The finite element model was developed using imaging data from a 26-year-old healthy male volunteer (height 178 cm, weight 75 kg) with no history of lumbar trauma or surgery. Prior to the collection of CT imaging data, the volunteer was fully informed of the study purpose, data usage scope, and potential risks, and provided written informed consent in accordance with the requirements of the Ethics Committee of The Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (approval No. [2016] 2-104, Ethics Committee of the Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University). Continuous thin-layer CT images (slice thickness 0.625 mm) of the L1 to L5 vertebrae were acquired using a Philips 64-slice spiral CT scanner and stored in DICOM format.

The hardware and software utilized in this study included: a computer equipped with an Intel Core i7—8565U processor and 16 GB RAM (running the Windows 10 operating system); Mimics 17.0 (Materialise, Belgium); Geomagic 12.0 (3D Systems, USA); SolidWorks 2015 (Dassault Systèmes, France); and Ansys Workbench 16.2 (Ansys Inc., USA).

Model establishment and grouping

3D model reconstruction

The obtained DICOM format CT files were imported into Mimics 17.0 software. Threshold segmentation and mask processing were used to reconstruct the 3D outline of the lumbar vertebrae, which was output as an STL format file. The STL file was then imported into Geomagic 12.0 software for reverse engineering processing, including noise reduction and smoothing, ultimately resulting in a refined solid model exported in IGES format. Subsequently, in Solidworks 2015 software, intervertebral discs (nucleus pulposus to annulus fibrosus volume ratio 2:3), 1 mm thick bony endplates, 1 mm thick cortical bone shell, and a complete ligament system (including anterior longitudinal ligament, posterior longitudinal ligament, ligamentum flavum, etc.) were added to the optimized lumbar model. This process established a normal 3D L1-L5 lumbar model with complete anatomical structures. (See Supplementary Fig. 1A-D for key steps of model processing and the intact model).

Model grouping and meshing

Three finite element model groups were established (Supplementary Fig. 1E):

Group A (Normal control group): The complete normal L1–L5 lumbar model.

Group B (Anterior group): Based on Group A, XLIF surgery at the L3–L4 segment was simulated. The interbody cage was placed in the anterior third of the disc space and supplemented with bilateral pedicle screw–rod fixation.

Group C (Central group): Based on Group A, XLIF surgery at the L3–L4 segment was simulated. The interbody cage was placed in the central third of the disc space and supplemented with bilateral pedicle screw–rod fixation.

The assembled solid models for all three groups were imported into Ansys Workbench 16.2 software for meshing. Vertebral bodies, intervertebral discs, and internal fixation components were meshed using C3D10 tetrahedral elements (10-node linear tetrahedral structural elements in ANSYS) (Supplementary Fig. 1F).

Material properties of the finite element model

Specifically regarding the surgical site, the contact interface between the interbody cage and the adjacent vertebral endplates was defined as bonded. This assumption was adopted to approximate an idealized condition of immediate postoperative fixation and to standardize the interface behavior for comparative analysis of anterior and central cage placement [10, 11].

Loading conditions and boundary constraints

In all models, the inferior surface of the L5 vertebral body was fully fixed. A compressive axial load of 500 N was applied to the superior surface of the L1 vertebral body to simulate gravitational loading during upright standing. Additionally, a pure moment of 10 N m was applied separately in six physiological directions: flexion, extension, left lateral bending, right lateral bending, left axial rotation, and right axial rotation.

Outcome measures

Evaluation of lumbar stability

Lumbar stability was assessed by measuring the range of motion (ROM) at the surgical segment (L3–L4) and adjacent segments (L2–L3 and L4–L5) under various loading conditions. The segmental ROM was defined as the angular change between two adjacent vertebrae before and after application of the load, as illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 2.

Stress distribution evaluation

The maximum equivalent (von Mises) stress on the interbody cage, screw-rod system, facet joints, and adjacent segment intervertebral discs was measured under each of the six motion conditions to evaluate the biomechanical risk of the cages and related tissues.

Discussion

Key findings and biomechanical rationale

The findings from this finite element analysis indicate that anterior cage placement was associated with lower mechanical stress on the interbody cage compared with central placement under all simulated motion conditions. This reduction in stress may be biomechanically relevant, as excessive loading of the interbody cage has been linked to an increased risk of endplate subsidence in previous studies [14]. This discrepancy in stress distribution may be related to the structural properties of the anterior vertebral endplate and the apophyseal ring. The anterior and lateral portions of the endplate are reinforced by a denser cortical bone shell and the apophyseal ring, providing a more rigid support foundation than the central region predominated by cancellous bone [15]. This finding is consistent with the surgical rationale that cage positioning is a critical modifiable factor influencing construct stability and clinical outcomes [16, 17].

Compared with previous finite element studies on lumbar interbody fusion, the present study provides several additional insights. Prior investigations have primarily focused on the development and validation of detailed spine models or the biomechanical assessment of fixation systems [10, 13], often without systematically comparing the biomechanical impact of different cage positions under identical posterior fixation conditions. In contrast, the present study directly compares anterior and central cage placement within the same validated model and evaluates the stress distribution of both the interbody cage and the posterior screw–rod system. This integrated analysis allows for a more comprehensive understanding of load-sharing mechanisms associated with different cage positions, which has not been fully addressed in previous studies.

Concurrently, the elevated stress observed in the posterior pedicle screw–rod construct with anterior cage placement suggests that load may be redistributed from the anterior column to the posterior instrumentation under the simulated biomechanical conditions. While such load redistribution may decrease mechanical demand on the cage, with peak stresses remaining within ranges reported in previous finite element studies, it may also increase mechanical demand on the posterior instrumentation. Over time, this may have implications for the fatigue behavior of the posterior instrumentation under repetitive physiological loading conditions. From a biomechanical perspective, increased implant stress may be associated with an elevated risk of fatigue-related complications, including screw loosening or rod fracture under long-term cyclic loading. However, it should be emphasized that the present study was conducted using a static finite element model and did not simulate cyclic loading or biological remodeling processes. Accordingly, these findings should be interpreted with caution, and further in vitro fatigue testing and clinical follow-up studies are needed to determine whether the observed increase in stress translates into a clinically meaningful risk of implant failure.

Therefore, although anterior cage placement appears to alter load-sharing characteristics within the construct, the potential long-term mechanical durability of the posterior instrumentation warrants further investigation [18].

Regarding adjacent segment biomechanics, central cage placement was associated with elevated stress concentrations within the facet joints at adjacent levels across all simulated motion conditions. This altered loading pattern may increase mechanical stimuli associated with facet joint degeneration, a known contributor to adjacent segment disease [19, 20]. In contrast, anterior cage placement generated adjacent segment stress distributions that were closer to the intact condition. However, it should be emphasized that these observations represent biomechanical tendencies identified within a computational model, and no direct conclusions can be drawn regarding the development of adjacent segment disease. The biomechanical impact of fusion constructs on adjacent levels remains an important consideration in surgical planning [21]. Accordingly, the present finite element study compared the biomechanical performance of anterior and central cage placement in XLIF with bilateral pedicle screw fixation to evaluate their influence on cage stress distribution and adjacent segment mechanical behavior.

Limitations

This study has limitations inherent to finite element methodology. The cage–endplate interface was modeled as a bonded contact, which may not fully represent the micromotion and frictional interactions at the bone–implant interface in vivo and may lead to an overestimation of construct stability. The material properties assigned to biological tissues were simplified, assuming isotropy and linear elasticity. Furthermore, this model was constructed based on CT data from a single healthy subject, which may limit the generalizability of the findings due to inter-individual anatomical variability. In addition, the analysis captures immediate postoperative biomechanics only and cannot predict long-term adaptive processes such as bone remodelling or ligamentous relaxation [12, 22]. Future studies integrating patient-specific modelling and validation against long-term clinical follow-up data are warranted to confirm these findings.

Moreover, the present finite element model does not include active muscle forces, which play an important role in maintaining spinal stability in vivo. The absence of muscle stabilization may influence load distribution patterns and potentially affect the magnitude of stress predicted in the interbody cage and posterior instrumentation. However, this simplification is commonly adopted in finite element studies to allow controlled comparisons between different surgical conditions.

Conclusion

From a biomechanical perspective and within the constraints of this finite element model, anterior cage placement was associated with lower cage stress and a different load distribution pattern compared with central placement, while maintaining similar segmental stability. Differences in adjacent facet joint loading were also observed under simulated motion conditions. These findings suggest that anterior positioning may be relevant to subsidence-related mechanical considerations. Whether these biomechanical differences translate into improved long-term clinical outcomes remains to be determined [23].
