Contrasting structural and functional changes in enzymatically pretreated coffee peel insoluble dietary fiber induced by alkaline hydrogen peroxide and ultrasonication
Authors: Huihua Tang, Jie Ni, Biqin Liu, Qiao Shi, Qi Wang, Wanrong Zhu, Hong Li, Yongjin Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Native coffee peel insoluble dietary fiber (N-IDF) was enzymatically treated to obtain a common precursor (E-IDF), which was subsequently modified by alkaline hydrogen peroxide (EA-IDF) or ultrasonication (EU-IDF) to enable a direct comparison of the two modification routes. All treatments significantly reduced particle size (p < 0.05); EU-IDF-300 had the smallest D50 (40.90 μm) and the highest specific surface area (308.47 m2/kg). EA-IDF exhibited a more open matrix and greater improvements in hydration and in vitro adsorption. EA-IDF-1.0% showed the highest swelling capacity (17.67 mL/g) and glucose adsorption capacity (5.24 mmol/g at 200 mmol/L), while both EA-IDF samples had higher cholesterol adsorption capacity than N-IDF and E-IDF at pH 7.0 (p < 0.05). XRD and monosaccharide profiling revealed structural and compositional differences between treatments, whereas FTIR detected no new functional groups. The contrasting responses of EA-IDF and EU-IDF provide a basis for selecting modification routes for specific technological applications.

Introduction

Coffee processing generates large quantities of by-products, including peel, husk, and parchment. Coffee peel (CP), consisting of the skin and adhering pulp removed during wet processing, accounts for approximately 40–50% of the fresh weight of coffee cherries. Much of this material is discarded or used in low-value applications, raising concerns about waste disposal and resource use (Esquivel & Jiménez, 2012). CP contains substantial dietary fiber and other potentially valuable components, and its conversion into food-grade fiber ingredients could provide a practical route for the utilization of coffee processing residues (Iriondo-DeHond et al., 2019).

Insoluble dietary fiber (IDF) is a major structural component of many plant residues. Its compact lignocellulosic matrix, however, often restricts water penetration, oil retention, and interactions with other components in food systems (Liu et al., 2021). Enzymatic hydrolysis can selectively cleave accessible polysaccharide regions and loosen the fiber matrix. Alkaline hydrogen peroxide (AHP) can disrupt regions associated with lignin and hemicellulose and increase surface accessibility, whereas ultrasonication can fragment particles and reorganize the matrix through cavitation and shear forces (Gan et al., 2021; Huang et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2020).

Enzymatic, alkaline, and ultrasonic treatments have been widely studied for plant dietary fibers (Gan et al., 2021), but most studies have examined individual modification methods or different fiber sources. Direct comparisons under the same experimental conditions therefore remain limited. Research on coffee peel dietary fiber has focused mainly on extraction methods, soluble dietary fiber, and selected physicochemical properties, whereas sequential modification of the insoluble fraction has received less attention (Bobková et al., 2022; Dong et al., 2020; Yu et al., 2025). The effects of AHP and ultrasonication after the same enzymatic pretreatment remain unclear, particularly regarding whether particle fragmentation and matrix opening are associated with different functional responses. In this study, N-IDF was enzymatically treated to obtain a common precursor (E-IDF), which was subsequently subjected to AHP treatment or ultrasonication to produce EA-IDF and EU-IDF. This common-precursor design enabled a controlled comparison of the two modification routes while minimizing variation arising from the raw material and pretreatment history. The samples were evaluated for particle characteristics, microstructure, crystallinity, monosaccharide composition, physicochemical properties, and in vitro adsorption capacities, and multivariate analyses were used to examine structure–function associations. The study aimed to clarify the route-dependent structural and functional responses to AHP and ultrasonication and to provide a basis for selecting modification routes according to the desired technological functions of coffee peel IDF.

Materials and methods

Materials

Fully ripe Arabica coffee cherries (Coffea arabica) were collected from Huke Coffee Estate (Baoshan, Yunnan, China). The skin and adhering pulp recovered during wet processing were collectively designated as coffee peel. The material was dried with hot air to a moisture content of approximately 10%, ground, and passed through an 80-mesh sieve. Cellulase (10,000 U/g), neutral protease (60,000 U/g), amyloglucosidase (100,000 U/g), α-amylase (40,000 U/g), and monosaccharide standards (rhamnose, arabinose, galactose, glucose, xylose, mannose, glucuronic acid, and galacturonic acid) were purchased from Beijing Solarbio Bioscience & Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). All other chemicals were obtained from Sinopharm Chemical Co., Ltd. (China) and were of analytical grade.

IDF extraction from CP

N-IDF was extracted from CP using an adapted procedure (Ma & Mu, 2016). CP flour was suspended in distilled water at 1:20 (w/v) and heated at 90 °C for 10 min. Sequential hydrolysis was then conducted at 60 °C with 0.5% α-amylase (w/w, dry basis; pH 6.0; 30 min), 0.3% amyloglucosidase (w/w, dry basis; pH 4.5; 60 min), and 1.0% neutral protease (w/w, dry basis; pH 7.0; 60 min). The solid residue was washed repeatedly with distilled water to neutral pH, centrifuged at 5000 ×g for 10 min, and freeze-dried. The resulting material was designated N-IDF. Its purity was 90.56%, as determined according to the Chinese national standard GB 5009.88–2023.

Modification of N-IDF

Treatment levels were selected based on preliminary screening results (Table S1). AHP concentrations of 0.5%, 1.0%, and 2.0% (w/v) and ultrasonication powers of 100, 300, and 500 W were evaluated using yield, water retention capacity (WRC), oil-holding capacity (OHC), and swelling capacity (SC) as screening responses. The 0.5% AHP treatment produced the highest yield, whereas 1.0% AHP provided the strongest overall hydration performance. Increasing the concentration to 2.0% did not provide further improvements in the measured functional properties and substantially reduced the sample yield. Ultrasonication at 500 W increased WRC but did not consistently improve OHC, SC, or yield relative to the lower powers. Consequently, AHP treatments at 0.5% and 1.0% and ultrasonication at 100 and 300 W were retained for detailed characterization.

Enzymatic modification

N-IDF (10 g) was dispersed in distilled water (200 mL). Cellulase was added at 0.24% (w/w, dry basis), and hydrolysis was conducted at pH 4.5 and 50 °C for 2 h. The solid residue was then washed repeatedly with distilled water to neutral pH, centrifuged at 5000 ×g for 10 min, and freeze-dried to obtain E-IDF.

Alkaline hydrogen peroxide treatment

E-IDF (10 g) was dispersed in distilled water (200 mL) and treated with AHP at a final concentration of 0.5% or 1.0% (w/v). The pH was adjusted to 11.0, and the suspension was stirred at 60 °C for 2 h. After treatment, the solid residue was washed repeatedly with distilled water to neutral pH, centrifuged at 5000 ×g for 10 min, and freeze-dried. The products were designated EA-IDF-0.5% and EA-IDF-1.0%, respectively.

Ultrasonication treatment

E-IDF (10 g) was dispersed in distilled water (200 mL) and treated using an ultrasonic processor (KH5200DE, Kunshan Hechuang Ultrasonic Instrument Co., Ltd., Kunshan, China). Ultrasonication was performed at a frequency of 40 kHz and powers of 100 or 300 W for 30 min in pulsed mode (2 s on and 4 s off). The solid residue was subsequently washed repeatedly with distilled water to neutral pH and was then freeze-dried. The products were designated EU-IDF-100 and EU-IDF-300, respectively.

Color analysis

Color was measured using an UltraScan PRO colorimeter (HunterLab, USA) and expressed as CIE L*, a*, and b* values. Total color difference (ΔE) was calculated relative to N-IDF, and whiteness index (WI) was calculated using the following equations:ΔE=L∗−L0∗2+a∗−a0∗2+b∗−b0∗2WI=100−100−L∗2+a∗2+(b∗)2where L*, a*, and b* are the color values of each sample, and L₀*, a₀*, and b₀* are the corresponding values for N-IDF.

Particle size and zeta potential

Particle size distributions and zeta potentials were measured using a Mastersizer 3000 and a Zetasizer Nano ZSE (Malvern Instruments Ltd., UK), respectively. Before measurement, the samples were dispersed or diluted in distilled water to concentrations suitable for the respective instruments. Refractive indices of 1.470 and 1.330 were used for dietary fiber and water, respectively (Yu et al., 2025). Specific surface area (SSA) was estimated from the particle size distribution obtained by laser diffraction.

Structural analysis

Sample structure was characterized by scanning electron microscopy (SEM), X-ray diffraction (XRD), and Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR). Gold-coated samples were examined using a Nova NanoSEM 450 microscope (FEI, USA) at 5–10 kV and magnifications of 500×, 1000×, and 2000×. XRD patterns were recorded using a Philips X-Pert diffractometer (Philips, Netherlands) with Cu Kα radiation over a 2θ range of 5–70° at 40 kV and 30 mA. The patterns were processed in JADE 6.0 after baseline correction and smoothing. Peak deconvolution was performed, and the crystallinity index (CI) was calculated as the crystalline peak area divided by the total fitted area. FTIR spectra were acquired using a Nicolet iS50 spectrometer (Thermo Electron Corporation, USA) in attenuated total reflectance mode over 4000–400 cm−1 at 4 cm−1 resolution with 32 scans per sample.

Thermogravimetric analysis

Thermogravimetric behavior was evaluated using a TGA 2 analyzer (Mettler-Toledo, Switzerland). Samples were heated from 35 to 600 °C at 15 °C/min under a nitrogen atmosphere.

Monosaccharide composition analysis

Monosaccharide composition was determined by 1-phenyl-3-methyl-5-pyrazolone (PMP) derivatization followed by high-performance liquid chromatography with diode-array detection (HPLC-DAD). Each IDF sample (10 mg) was hydrolyzed with 2 mol/L trifluoroacetic acid at 110 °C for 2 h under nitrogen. Residual acid was removed by repeated evaporation with methanol under a nitrogen stream, and the dried hydrolysate was redissolved in 0.3 mol/L NaOH. The hydrolysate was derivatized with 0.5 mol/L PMP in methanol at 70 °C for 2 h. After cooling, the reaction mixture was neutralized with 0.3 mol/L HCl, extracted twice with chloroform, and filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane. PMP derivatives were analyzed using an Agilent 1100 HPLC-DAD system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) with detection at 250 nm.

Physicochemical properties

Measurement of WRC and OHC

WRC and OHC were measured using a previously reported method with minor modifications (Tian et al., 2024). Each sample (0.5 g) was mixed with 35 mL of distilled water or peanut oil and agitated at room temperature for 2 h. The mixtures were centrifuged at 5000 ×g for 10 min, and the hydrated or oil-retaining residue was collected and weighed. WRC and OHC were calculated using the following equation:WRC/OHCg/g=W1−W/Wwhere W is the dry sample mass and W1 is the mass of the residue after centrifugation.

Measurement of SC

SC was determined by adding 0.1 g of sample to 5 mL of distilled water and allowing the mixture to stand at room temperature for 24 h. The sample volume was recorded before and after hydration, and SC was calculated using the following equation:SCmL/g=V1−V0/Wwhere V0 and V1 are the initial and final sample volumes, respectively, and W is the dry sample mass.

In vitro adsorption capacities

Measurement of nitrite ion adsorption capacity (NIAC)

NIAC was measured using a previously reported method with minor modifications (Lyu et al., 2021). Each IDF sample was dispersed in 10 mmol/L sodium nitrite solution to obtain a 1% suspension. The pH was adjusted to 2.0 or 7.0, and the suspension was shaken at 37 °C for 2 h. 2 mL of the supernatant was mixed with 2 mL of p-aminobenzenesulfonic acid solution and 1 mL of N-(1-naphthyl)ethylenediamine dihydrochloride solution. The mixture was kept in the dark for 15 min, and absorbance was measured at 538 nm. NIAC was calculated using the following equation:NIACmmol/g=C0−C1/Wwhere C0 and C1 are the nitrite amounts before and after incubation, respectively, and W is the dry sample mass.

Measurement of cholesterol adsorption capacity (CAC)

CAC was determined using a previously reported method with slight modifications (Huang et al., 2024). Egg yolk was diluted 1:9 (v/v) with distilled water and homogenized to form an emulsion. Each IDF sample (1.0 g) was mixed with 50 g of the emulsion. The pH was adjusted to 2.0 or 7.0, and the mixture was shaken at 37 °C for 2 h. 0.04 mL of supernatant was analyzed for cholesterol using the o-phthalaldehyde method, with absorbance measured at 550 nm. CAC was calculated using the following equation:CACmg/g=C0−C1/Wwhere C0 and C1 are the cholesterol amounts before and after incubation, respectively, and W is the dry sample mass.

Measurement of glucose adsorption capacity (GAC)

GAC was measured by dispersing each sample in glucose solutions containing 50, 100, or 200 mmol/L glucose to obtain a 1% suspension. The mixtures were stirred at 37 °C for 6 h. The supernatant was collected and its glucose concentration was measured using the 3,5-dinitrosalicylic acid method. GAC was calculated using the following equation:GACmmol/g=C0−C1/Wwhere C0 and C1 are the glucose amounts before and after incubation, respectively, and W is the dry sample mass.

Statistical analysis

Data processing and visualization were performed using Origin 2018 (OriginLab, USA), and statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS Statistics 22.0 (IBM, USA). Each treatment was independently prepared three times, and the resulting samples were analyzed separately. Results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD, n = 3). Differences among treatment groups were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey's multiple comparison test. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results and discussion

N-IDF had a dark reddish-yellow color typical of coffee peel. Compared with N-IDF, E-IDF had higher L* and WI values and a lower a* value (p < 0.05), corresponding to greater lightness and lower redness. These changes coincided with the reduction in particle size and the partial removal of water-soluble colored constituents during enzymatic treatment and washing. The larger changes at 1.0% AHP were consistent with a stronger bleaching effect associated with the oxidation or removal of chromophoric components under alkaline peroxide conditions. Particle size distribution and SSA analysis

Particle size distribution and SSA were used to assess particle fragmentation and surface exposure. N-IDF had a broad particle size distribution, with a D50 of 207.73 μm (Table 2). Enzymatic treatment shifted the distribution toward smaller sizes and increased SSA from 79.80 to 136.70 m2/kg. Cellulase hydrolysis of accessible cellulose regions can loosen the compact fiber network and promote the release of smaller particles (Zheng et al., 2021). Subsequent AHP treatment and ultrasonication caused further reductions in particle size. Within the AHP groups, EA-IDF-1.0% had a lower D50 than EA-IDF-0.5%. This difference was consistent with greater weakening of the fiber matrix at the higher AHP concentration. EU-IDF-300 had the smallest D50 among all samples (40.90 μm) and the highest SSA (308.47 m2/kg). The higher SSA may provide a larger contact area between the fiber particles and the surrounding medium. This pronounced particle size reduction was consistent with the disintegration of fiber aggregates by cavitation and shear forces during ultrasonication. Structural analysis

SEM

The SEM images revealed clear morphological differences among the IDF samples following treatment (Fig. 1A). N-IDF consisted mainly of large aggregates with compact, uneven surfaces, a morphology typical of a dense native matrix. Enzymatic treatment reduced aggregation and produced smaller clusters with rougher surfaces, consistent with partial disruption of the original fiber matrix. Similar structural loosening after cellulase treatment has been associated with the hydrolysis of readily accessible cellulose-rich regions (Xu et al., 2022). Compared with N-IDF and E-IDF, the AHP-treated samples had a more open matrix. At 2000×, EA-IDF exhibited irregular surfaces, visible cavities, and separated lamellar regions, especially in EA-IDF-1.0%. Comparable morphological changes after alkaline peroxide treatment have been attributed to the disruption of regions associated with lignin and hemicellulose, resulting in a less compact fiber matrix (Jiang et al., 2021). EU-IDF consisted of irregular flaky fragments with fractured surfaces rather than the open matrix observed in EA-IDF. Fragmentation was more pronounced in EU-IDF-300, in agreement with its smaller particle size. Similar fragmentation and surface damage after ultrasonication have been reported in soluble dietary fiber from pomelo peel and dietary fiber from coffee peel (Ye et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2025).Fig. 1Structural characterization of native and modified IDF samples: (A) SEM images at magnifications of 500×, 1000×, and 2000×; (B) XRD patterns; and (C) FTIR spectra.Fig. 1

XRD

All IDF samples exhibited two main diffraction peaks at approximately 16° and 21° (Fig. 1B), consistent with the cellulose I structure. The peak positions remained largely unchanged after modification, indicating that the basic crystalline form of cellulose was preserved. The crystallinity index (CI) varied among treatments because of differences in the relative proportions of ordered and amorphous regions. CI decreased from 23.37% in N-IDF to 11.49% in E-IDF, accompanied by broadening of the peak near 16.6°. These changes were associated with lower structural order after enzymatic treatment. Cellulase hydrolysis may have loosened the polysaccharide network and disrupted accessible ordered regions of cellulose, increasing the proportion of amorphous material (Gao et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2024). Subsequent AHP treatment and ultrasonication both increased CI relative to E-IDF, although the increase differed among samples. The increase in CI after AHP treatment was consistent with preferential removal of amorphous material associated with lignin and hemicellulose. Similar increases in crystallinity have been reported for soybean fiber and lotus root residue dietary fiber after alkaline or alkaline peroxide treatment (Gu et al., 2025; Zhang, 2023). In EU-IDF, fragmentation and matrix rearrangement during ultrasonication may have altered polysaccharide chain packing. Similar increases in crystallinity after ultrasound-assisted enzymatic treatment have been associated with mechanical disruption and redistribution of crystalline and amorphous regions (He et al., 2020).

FTIR

The FTIR spectra of all IDF samples contained similar major absorption bands, and no new characteristic bands appeared after modification (Fig. 1C). The main polysaccharide functional groups therefore remained largely unchanged (Jiang et al., 2024). The broad O—H stretching band around 3400 cm−1 had a lower relative intensity in the modified samples, a change associated with altered hydrogen bonding and polysaccharide network organization (Zhang et al., 2020). Zeta potential analysis

Zeta potential was used to evaluate the surface charge and electrostatic stability of the IDF dispersions. N-IDF had a zeta potential of −13.88 mV (Table 2), consistent with relatively weak electrostatic repulsion. After enzymatic treatment, the value shifted to −28.56 mV (p < 0.05), representing a substantial increase in the magnitude of the negative surface charge. This shift coincided with the reduction in particle size and the increase in SSA and was consistent with greater exposure of existing charged groups following disruption of the fiber matrix (Cheng et al., 2025; Gao et al., 2022). The zeta potentials of EA-IDF-0.5% and EA-IDF-1.0% were − 34.50 and − 35.57 mV, respectively. Absolute zeta potential values above 30 mV are generally associated with stronger electrostatic repulsion and greater dispersion stability. Similar increases in negative surface charge have been reported for insoluble soybean fiber after combined alkaline treatment and homogenization (Cai et al., 2021). The corresponding values for EU-IDF-100 and EU-IDF-300 were − 25.42 and − 13.76 mV, respectively. Despite its much smaller particle size, EU-IDF-300 had a value close to that of N-IDF; particle fragmentation alone was therefore insufficient to enhance electrostatic stabilization.

Thermogravimetric analysis

The thermogravimetric curves in Fig. 2 followed broadly similar degradation patterns across all samples. Below 250 °C, the samples lost approximately 8% of their mass, mainly owing to the loss of free and bound water. The main decomposition stage occurred at approximately 220–400 °C and was primarily associated with the thermal degradation of hemicellulose and cellulose (Liu et al., 2025). During this stage, mass loss was 55.25% for N-IDF, 56.82% for E-IDF, 60.69% for EA-IDF-0.5%, 58.56% for EA-IDF-1.0%, 57.10% for EU-IDF-100, and 57.83% for EU-IDF-300. Distinct DTG peaks appeared within this temperature range, each representing the maximum degradation rate of a sample. The DTG peak temperature increased from 305.17 °C in N-IDF to 317.84, 323.49, 308.61, 318.25, and 317.56 °C in E-IDF, EA-IDF-0.5%, EA-IDF-1.0%, EU-IDF-100, and EU-IDF-300, respectively. All modified samples therefore reached their maximum degradation rates at higher temperatures than N-IDF. A similar shift toward higher peak degradation temperatures has been reported for red bean dietary fiber after enzymatic hydrolysis and ultrasound-assisted modification (Xu et al., 2022). Monosaccharide composition

Table 3 summarizes the monosaccharide composition of the native and modified IDF samples. Arabinose and glucose were the predominant monosaccharides in all samples. Modification reduced the relative proportion of glucose, while arabinose and xylose generally increased. EA-IDF-1.0% had the highest xylose proportion, consistent with a greater contribution from hemicellulose-derived residues. Galacturonic acid was more abundant in EU-IDF than in E-IDF, corresponding to a greater contribution from pectin-associated residues. Physicochemical properties

WRC and SC

Water retention capacity (WRC) describes the ability of the fiber matrix to retain water, whereas swelling capacity (SC) represents its volume expansion during hydration. All modified samples had higher WRC and SC than N-IDF (Fig. 3A and B). EA-IDF-1.0% reached the highest values, with a WRC of 14.66 g/g and an SC of 17.67 mL/g, compared with 4.79 g/g and 4.75 mL/g for N-IDF. The enhanced hydration of EA-IDF coincided with its open morphology, visible cavities, and separated lamellar regions observed by SEM, as well as changes in the O—H band detected by FTIR. These structural features could facilitate water penetration and provide more space for water retention and fiber swelling (Rezvani & Goli, 2023; Zhang et al., 2020). Similar improvements in water-holding capacity have been reported for buckwheat straw IDF after AHP treatment and were attributed to a looser structure and greater exposure of hydrophilic regions (Meng et al., 2019). Unlike the larger increases reported for ultrasonically treated coffee peel soluble dietary fiber (SDF), ultrasonication produced only modest changes in WRC and SC in coffee peel IDF (Yu et al., 2025). The different responses may reflect differences in fiber fraction and processing conditions, because soluble and insoluble dietary fibers differ substantially in polysaccharide composition and matrix organization. EU-IDF had lower WRC and SC than EA-IDF despite its smaller particle size and greater SSA. This comparison highlights the importance of matrix opening in hydration performance, rather than particle size reduction or surface area expansion alone.Fig. 3Physicochemical properties of native and modified IDF samples: (A) WRC; (B) SC; and (C) OHC. OHC

Oil-holding capacity (OHC) reflects the ability of dietary fiber to retain oil within its matrix. All modified samples had significantly higher OHC than N-IDF (p < 0.05; Fig. 3C). Compared with E-IDF, ultrasonication produced little additional change in OHC, whereas AHP treatment caused a marked increase. The greater OHC of EA-IDF was associated with its open matrix and visible cavities, which could provide additional space for the physical entrapment of oil. Improved OHC has also been reported for bagasse insoluble dietary fiber after AHP-assisted hydrothermal-pressure modification, together with changes in surface morphology, pore structure, crystallinity, and functional groups (Luo et al., 2023). However, the OHC of EA-IDF-1.0% (3.73 g/g) was considerably lower than that reported for the bagasse IDF (11.12 g/g). This difference may reflect variations in botanical source and processing conditions. In particular, the additional hydrothermal-pressure treatment applied to the bagasse fiber could have contributed to differences in pore structure and the accessibility of oil-binding regions.

In vitro adsorption capacities

NIAC

NIAC was evaluated at pH 2.0 and 7.0 to compare nitrite adsorption under acidic and neutral conditions. All samples had higher NIAC at pH 2.0 than at pH 7.0 (Fig. 4A). Under acidic conditions, a larger fraction of nitrite is present as nitrous acid (HNO2), which has been reported to interact more readily with fiber matrices (Jiang et al., 2021; Tian et al., 2024). At pH 2.0, NIAC reached 1.00 mmol/g for EA-IDF-1.0% and 0.98 mmol/g for EU-IDF-100, compared with 0.80 mmol/g for E-IDF. The higher values were associated with greater accessibility of polar adsorption sites following matrix opening in EA-IDF and structural disruption in EU-IDF-100. Increased NIAC has also been reported for bagasse IDF modified with AHP and Rosa roxburghii pomace IDF modified by ultrasonication and cellulase treatment (Huang et al., 2024; Luo et al., 2023). At pH 7.0, increasing the ultrasonic power from 100 to 300 W produced no further increase in NIAC. A similar response was reported for coffee peel dietary fiber treated at ultrasonic powers ranging from 100 to 500 W (Yu et al., 2025).Fig. 4In vitro adsorption capacities of native and modified IDF samples: (A) NIAC; (B) CAC; and (C) GAC. CAC

Cholesterol adsorption capacity (CAC) was measured at pH 2.0 and 7.0 to compare in vitro cholesterol retention by the IDF samples. All samples had higher CAC at pH 7.0 than at pH 2.0 (Fig. 4B). Previous studies have associated this pH dependence with hydrophobic interactions and the physical entrapment of cholesterol within the fiber matrix (Huang et al., 2024). At pH 2.0, CAC increased with AHP concentration and reached 17.58 mg/g in EA-IDF-1.0%, 4.48 times the value of N-IDF. At pH 7.0, both EA-IDF samples had the highest CAC values. The greater OHC and more open matrix of EA-IDF could favor hydrophobic association and the physical entrapment of cholesterol. EU-IDF-300 also had higher CAC than N-IDF and E-IDF, reaching 45.05 mg/g, compared with 36.83 mg/g for N-IDF and 40.43 mg/g for E-IDF. This value was higher than those reported for ultrasonically modified dietary fiber-rich polysaccharides derived from adzuki bean cooking liquid (29.47 mg/g) and bamboo shoot IDF (10.82 mg/g) (Wu et al., 2020; Xu et al., 2022).

GAC

The in vitro glucose adsorption capacity (GAC) of the IDF samples was measured at different initial glucose concentrations. GAC increased with glucose concentration in all samples (Fig. 4C). At 200 mmol/L, EA-IDF-1.0% reached the highest GAC of 5.24 mmol/g, 3.12 times the value of N-IDF. This result coincided with its strong hydration capacity, which could increase contact between the glucose solution and the fiber matrix. EU-IDF-300 exceeded EU-IDF-100 but remained below both AHP-treated samples. The response of the AHP-treated samples was more closely associated with matrix openness and hydration than with particle size reduction alone. The maximum value obtained for EA-IDF-1.0% was higher than the reported ranges for black bean insoluble fiber (0.24–0.979 mmol/g) and rice bran insoluble fiber (1.43–3.05 mmol/g) (Liu et al., 2025; Qi, 2016).

Multivariate analysis and structure–function relationships

Principal component analysis (PCA) and Pearson correlation analysis were used to examine associations among the structural, compositional, physicochemical, and in vitro adsorption variables of the IDF samples. PC1 and PC2 accounted for 57.80% and 28.20% of the total variance, respectively, explaining a cumulative 86.00% (Fig. 5A). The two EA-IDF samples clustered together in the score plot, whereas the EU-IDF samples were separated from the AHP-treated samples. In the loading plot (Fig. 5B), WRC, SC, OHC, CAC, GAC, and SSA loaded in a similar direction, while D10, D50, D90, glucose, and mannose appeared on the opposite side. The correlation heatmap showed negative correlations between D50 and WRC, SC, OHC, CAC, GAC, and NIAC, whereas SSA was positively correlated with these properties (Fig. 5C). Xylose was positively correlated with WRC, SC, and OHC, while glucose was negatively correlated with several adsorption variables. EU-IDF-300 had the smallest particle size and the highest SSA but did not exhibit the strongest hydration, oil retention, or adsorption properties. EA-IDF had a more open matrix and generally higher WRC, SC, OHC, GAC, and CAC than EU-IDF. Because both routes were applied to the same E-IDF precursor, the comparison revealed two route-dependent response patterns: ultrasonication was characterized mainly by particle fragmentation and an increase in SSA, whereas AHP treatment was associated with matrix opening and broader functional improvements. These results suggest that extensive particle size reduction did not necessarily translate into stronger functional performance. Matrix openness therefore appeared to be an important structural factor in addition to particle size and apparent surface exposure. The hydration and oil retention properties of EA-IDF warrant further evaluation in food systems requiring water and lipid retention, whereas the smaller particle size of EU-IDF may be advantageous in formulations requiring fine particle dispersion.

Conclusion

Using E-IDF as a common precursor, this study directly compared AHP and ultrasonication as subsequent modification routes for coffee peel IDF. Ultrasonication mainly reduced particle size and increased SSA, whereas AHP produced a more open matrix and broader improvements in hydration, oil retention, and in vitro adsorption. Notably, the most extensive particle fragmentation and the greatest increase in SSA did not coincide with the strongest functional performance, indicating that particle size reduction alone was insufficient to explain the observed differences. These contrasting responses provide a basis for selecting the modification route according to the intended technological function. The use of coffee peel as a dietary fiber source may also contribute to the valorization of coffee processing residues. For scale-up, AHP treatment involves chemical use, washing, and effluent management, whereas ultrasonication depends on energy input and equipment capacity. Further studies should evaluate formulation performance, sensory quality, processing stability, and storage behavior in food products. Changes in total dietary fiber (TDF), insoluble dietary fiber (IDF), and SDF contents, including possible solubilization of the insoluble fraction, and the fate of native coffee peel bioactive compounds during modification also require investigation.
