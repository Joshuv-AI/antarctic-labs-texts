Global Economic and Social Burden of Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease: A Systematic Review (2020–2024)
Authors: Celia Gutiérrez-Villegas, Manuel Herrero-Montes, Luis Manuel Fernández Cacho, Carlos Antonio Amado-Diago, Valentín Perales-García, Andrea Marcela Aceros Lora, María Paz-Zulueta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide and imposes a substantial economic and social burden on healthcare systems and society.ObjectiveTo analyse recent evidence (2020–2024) on the economic and social burden of COPD, including direct, indirect, and intangible costs.MethodsA systematic review was conducted to synthesise recent evidence on the economic and social burden of COPD published between January 2020 and December 2024. Searches were performed in PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, and Web of Science. Original studies reporting direct healthcare costs, direct non-healthcare costs, indirect costs or intangible costs related to health-related quality of life (HRQoL) were included.ResultsThirty studies from 15 countries were included. Direct healthcare costs represent the main component of expenditure, largely driven by hospitalisations and exacerbations. Annual costs ranged from €3500 to €10,000 per patient in Europe and from US$10,000 to US$17,000 in the United States. Although absolute costs were lower in Asia, the relative financial burden on patients was considerable. Direct non-healthcare costs, including out-of-pocket expenses and long-term care, were particularly relevant in severe disease. Indirect costs related to productivity losses and unemployment were substantial and, in some middle-income countries, exceeded healthcare costs. Intangible costs included anxiety, depression, caregiver distress, impaired quality of life, and perceived financial toxicity, with high overall prevalence.ConclusionCOPD creates a considerable economic and social burden. Although direct costs are predominant, indirect, and intangible costs are decisive, particularly in contexts with lower healthcare coverage. Clinical factors (severity, exacerbations, and comorbidities) and socio-economic factors (educational level, income, and employment) explain much of the variability. A holistic approach that addresses health, social, and emotional dimensions is required for a more equitable and sustainable management.Keywords: chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, COPD, direct healthcare cost, indirect cost, intangible cost, health-related quality of life, HRQoL, socioeconomic impact

Introduction

According to the Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD),1 COPD is a heterogeneous lung condition characterised by chronic respiratory symptoms (dyspnoea, cough, sputum production and/or exacerbations) due to airway and/or alveolar abnormalities causing persistent, often progressive airflow obstruction.

Globally, the disease is estimated to affect more than 390 million people and it is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality, with a significant and growing health and economic burden.2,3

In addition to its clinical impact, COPD imposes a considerable economic burden on both health systems and society as a whole.4,5 In addition to direct and indirect economic costs, COPD also entails a substantial intangible burden, including impaired health-related quality of life, psychological distress, and caregiver burden. While economic burden generally refers to quantifiable financial costs (direct and indirect), social burden encompasses broader impacts on daily functioning, well-being, social participation, and family dynamics. Distinguishing between these dimensions is essential to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the overall impact of COPD.1

In the European Union, the total direct costs of respiratory diseases are estimated at around 6% of the total annual healthcare budget, with COPD accounting for 56% (38.6 billion euros) of the cost of respiratory diseases.6 In the US, costs attributable to COPD are expected to increase over the next 20 years, with projected costs of US$40 billion per year.7

A previous systematic review conducted by our group (2015–2020)8 identified high methodological heterogeneity and an uneven distribution of studies on COPD costs, with a predominance of data from high-income countries. In line with this work, this review updates the analysis for the period 2020–2024, capturing the most recent evidence available in the literature. Although many of the included studies analyse data collected before the COVID-19 pandemic, their publication in the post-pandemic context reflects evolving research priorities and provides an updated perspective on the economic and social burden of COPD. This approach also allows the identification of emerging trends, particularly in relation to indirect and intangible costs, and offers a more current overview of inequalities across different international settings. Compared with our previous review (2015–2020),8 this study nor only updates the timeframe but also expands the scope by incorporating emerging evidence on indirect and intangible costs, as well as providing a more comprehensive analysis of the social burden of COPD and its socioeconomic determinants across different settings.

Objective

The overall objective is to update and synthesise available scientific evidence on the global economic and social burden of COPD in the period between 2020 and 2024, integrating direct healthcare and non-healthcare costs, indirect costs, and intangible costs, to identify trends, determinants, and inequalities in different international contexts.

Methods

Study Design and Reporting Standards

This systematic review was conducted and reported following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA)9 2020 guidelines. The study protocol is part of a broader economic research project on COPD costs approved by the Cantabria Research Ethics Committee (CEIM) (Spain). Internal code: 2024.039.

Search Strategy

A systematic literature search was performed in PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, and Web of Science to identify studies published between 1 January 2020 and 20 December 2024. Although many studies analyse data collected in previous decades, their recent publication reflects updated methodologies and perspectives. The search strategy combined terms related to COPD and economic burden, including direct healthcare costs, direct non-healthcare costs, indirect costs, and socioeconomic impact.

Both controlled vocabulary (MeSH terms) and free-text keywords were used, and the search strategy was adapted for each database. The full search strategy is presented in Table 1.

Bibliographic Search Strategy

All records retrieved from the three databases were imported into Zotero, where duplicates were identified and removed. In addition, reference lists of the selected articles were manually screened to identify further relevant studies that might have been missed in the initial search.

Conference abstracts, editorials, letters, and notes were excluded.

Eligibility Criteria

Studies were considered eligible if they met the following inclusion criteria: (1) Original research articles published between 2020 and 2024, (2) studies reporting economic costs associated with COPD, (3) studies conducted from any economic perspective (healthcare system, patient, or societal perspective), (4) studies conducted in any country or region and (5) articles published in any language.

Studies were excluded if they: (1) Did not specifically report costs attributable to COPD, (2) did not provide quantifiable economic or health-related HRQoL data and/or (3) were systematic reviews, editorials, letters to the editor or conference abstracts.

Study Selection

Titles and abstracts were independently screened by two reviewers (C.G.V. and L.M.F.C.), who classified the records as included, excluded or uncertain.

Full texts of potentially eligible studies were subsequently assessed. Disagreements were resolved through discussion, and when necessary, by consultation with a third reviewer (M.H.M).

The final set of included studies was determined by consensus among the three reviewers. The PRISMA flow diagram (Figure 1) summarises the study selection process and the reasons for exclusion.

Data Extraction

A structured data extraction form was developed for this review. A pilot extraction was first conducted on a sample of studies to ensure consistency.

Two reviewers (C.G.V. and M.H.M.) independently extracted the data from all included studies and compared their results to ensure accuracy. Any discrepancies were resolved by consensus.

Risk of Bias and Quality Assessment

The methodological quality of the included studies was independently assessed by two reviewers (C.G.V. and M.H.M.) using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) for observational studies.10 This tool evaluates study quality across three domains: selection of study groups, comparability of groups, and ascertainment of outcomes or exposures.

Each study was assigned a score ranging from 0 to 9 points, with higher scores indicating better methodological quality and lower risk of bias. Studies scoring 7–9 points were considered high quality, those scoring 4–6 points moderate quality, and those scoring ≤3 points low quality.

Discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached. The results of the quality assessment were used to interpret the robustness of the findings but did not constitute exclusion criteria.

Data Synthesis

Due to methodological heterogeneity across studies in terms of study design, economic perspective, cost components, and currencies reported, a quantitative meta-analysis was not feasible. Therefore, the findings were synthesised using a structured narrative approach, grouping studies according to the type of economic outcomes reported. No standard effect measures were applied, and economic outcomes were reported as described in the original studies.

30 studies were eventually analysed. Methodological heterogeneity precluded the possibility of conducting a meta-analysis, so a narrative synthesis was chosen. General Characteristics of the Studies

30 original studies published between 2020 and 2024 were included, from 15 countries and representing all World Health Organization (WHO) regions. Following the World Bank’s classification of countries by income level,41 most of the research was conducted in health systems in high-income countries: US (8 studies),15,18,19,21,30,37,38,40 mostly with a healthcare system perspective (Medicare/claims), China (5 studies)16,31,32,34,39 reporting results on hospital costs and epidemiological trends, and Western Europe (10 studies) (Italy,23 Spain,20,22 France,12 England,28,33 Denmark,25 Sweden,29 Ireland,26 and Austria13), mainly focusing on clinical burden, quality of life, and costs in universal systems. Fewer studies came from upper-middle income countries: Brazil17 and Malaysia,27 focusing mainly on economic impact, quality of life and inequalities, and lower-middle income countries: Pakistan,24 the Philippines36 and Egypt35 focused their studies on the psychosocial impact (anxiety, depression, unemployment, and productivity) of COPD in their regions.

Most of the included studies have retrospective observational designs, mainly cohorts based on administrative and healthcare cost databases. Cross-sectional studies were used to a lesser extent, and they focused on assessing intangible costs (quality of life, emotional or financial burden) and out-of-pocket spending. In turn, a limited number of prospective studies addressed specific costs, such as those associated with intensive care unit care, loss of working productivity or long-term economic projections. In terms of the economic perspective analysed, there is a predominance of analyses from the health/insurance system (17 studies) that analyse direct costs: hospitalisations, exacerbations, or drug therapy. Studies focusing on the patient’s perspective were less frequent (10 investigations), they focus on HRQoL, out-of-pocket expenses or financial burden. Finally, some studies focus on the societal perspective (7 investigations). These mainly consider intangible and indirect costs of the disease (loss of productivity, unemployment, or inequality). Some of them complemented two perspectives.

The objectives were heterogeneous, ranging from estimations of direct hospitalisation and drug costs, quantification of indirect costs accounting for loss of productivity or analyses of intangible costs using quality of life scores.

The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS). Most studies showed moderate to high methodological quality. Overall, most studies scored between 6 and 8 points, indicating a generally acceptable risk of bias. The most common limitations were related to the use of administrative databases, limited clinical information, and restricted geographical representativeness.

Direct Healthcare Costs

Most studies (23 of the total 30) included at least one component accounting for direct healthcare costs. Hospitalisation costs and ICU stay were the main determinants of acute exacerbations of COPD (AECOPD) spending.

The main direct cost driver is exacerbation hospitalisation.12,21,22,31 Drugs represent an important component in the stable phase (40–60%), whereas hospital spending dominates in exacerbations.22,23,31

There is enormous geographic variability in the annual cost per COPD patient. In the US, Medicare-based studies accounted for annual costs between $10,000–17,000/year with specific very costly subgroups (>$40,000).18,19,38,40 In Europe, hospital costs ranged from €3500 to €10,000 per patient/year,11,22,23,25 while in Asia, figures ranged from $139/exacerbation in Pakistan,14 $507/year in Malaysia,27 $500–900 per hospitalisation in the Philippines,36 to $2000–3000 per admission in China.16,31,34,39

Clinical severity and number of co-morbidities are key predictors of increased costs. For example, in Italy, patients with no comorbidities reported a cost of €1158/year vs patients with 6 or more comorbidities reporting a total of €9667/year.11 In Denmark, the cost per year increases from €8766 in GOLD A to €17,749 in GOLD D.25 In England, patients with 1 severe exacerbation reach £4095, over twice as high as those who do not exacerbate.33 Finally, in the US, the adjusted costs rise from $25,084 in category A (no exacerbations) to $26,633 in category E (two or more exacerbations/year).30

Time trends show a progressive increase in hospital and pharmacological costs. In China, the hospital cost per capita rose from CNY 23,716 in 2009 to CNY 31,000 in 2017.16 In the US, the national attributable cost has been projected from $31.3 billion in 2019 to $60.5 billion in 2029.38

In conclusion, the direct healthcare cost of COPD is very high and, on the rise, mainly explained by exacerbations and hospitalisations12,21,22,31 and increased by comorbidities and GOLD severity.11,25,30,33 The magnitude of these costs varies considerably across regions and socioeconomic contexts.

Direct Non-Healthcare Costs

The direct non-healthcare cost is less quantified than the healthcare cost. It is only reported in 4 studies.14,25,27,32 Includes out-of-pocket spending for exacerbations, transportation and consumables, and long-term and residential care. Transportation and consumables are relevant in lower-middle income countries14,27 while long-term care and residential care is a key component in countries with stronger social systems, especially for severe patients.25 Socio-economic inequalities determine out-of-pocket spending and access to informal care services.32

According to Lokke et al,25 the relative magnitude of these costs is usually <10% of the total cost, except for prolonged non-medical care, which may exceed hospital costs in advanced COPD.

In conclusion, although in most countries direct non-healthcare costs are a smaller proportion of total costs, in acute and long-term care settings they can be as or more relevant than direct healthcare costs.

Indirect Costs

The studies that quantified the indirect cost were 9.14,22,25,27,29,32,35,37,40 They were manifested as loss of productivity, reduced income, unemployment, and work impairment.

In high-income countries such as Spain, Alcázar-Navarrete et al22 estimated a total indirect cost of €1020/patient/year. In Denmark, Lokke et al25 estimated it at €6773 patient/year, and in Sweden, the indirect cost according to Larsson et al29 amounts to €7000 to €12,500 patient/year.

In upper-middle and lower-middle income countries, the indirect cost may exceed the direct healthcare care cost as reflected in Malaysia27 (estimated at $1700 patient/year) and Egypt,35 who report much higher productivity losses and activity impairment compared with the direct healthcare cost.

Even in mild stages of the disease, there is a significant indirect economic impact, lower wages and higher unemployment.29

Different authors describe a series of determinants related to the increased indirect costs associated with COPD such as: severity (GOLD stage),25 comorbidities and exacerbations22 and socio-economic variables such as educational level, type of employment and rural residence.35,40

Intangible Costs

8 studies that assessed HRQoL as an intangible cost component were included.13,17,20,22,24,26,37,40 The intangible costs of COPD are enormous and cross-cutting: anxiety, depression, distress, poor quality of life, and caregiver burden. Although they are not quantified monetarily, they have a direct impact on the well-being of the patient, their family, and on the use of healthcare resources.

Anxiety and depression are highly prevalent in COPD. Lima et al17 reported 100% severe anxiety in hospitalised COPD patients in Brazil and in Pakistan, Husain et al24 reported 51% prevalence of depression and 20% prevalence of anxiety with this disease.

Quality of life in COPD patients is always lower than in the general population without the disease. This is reported in studies carried out in Austria,13 where a poorer quality of life score is associated with patients with a higher number of exacerbations, in Spain,20 where the EQ-5D-5L questionnaire was used to assess HRQoL and reports an average HRQoL of 0.6 in COPD vs 0.91 in the general population, and in Ireland,26 where multiple physical and psychological symptoms in COPD patients are reported to reduce their functional performance.

Soto-Rubio et al20 also reflect that emotional distress affects not only the patient, but also the family environment, with a bidirectional distress between the patient and the informal caregiver.

Several studies indicate that intangible symptoms are associated with worse outcomes and higher resource utilisation. Factors such as depression, anxiety and severe dyspnoea predict poorer quality of life and an increased number of hospitalisations.17,24,26

Finally, a time trend is identified in which studies published in 2020–202113,17,20,22,24,26 focus on aspects such as anxiety, depression, or symptom burden, in 2022–2023 there are no publications specific to intangible costs, and finally, in 2024 the link between perceived economic burden and emotional well-being37,40 emerges.

Reporting Biases and Certainty of Evidence

Due to the heterogeneity of study designs, economic perspectives, cost components, and outcome reporting across the included studies, as well as the absence of a quantitative meta-analysis, a formal assessment of reporting bias was not performed.

Discussion

Our results show that COPD is a chronic disease with a very high economic and social burden, the impact of which is spread across several dimensions. In line with international literature, direct healthcare costs are the best documented and it accounts for the largest share of spending, mainly driven by hospitalisations and AECOPD.12,21,22,31 This pattern is observed both in European countries11,23 as well as in the United States, where annual costs per patient are even higher.38 In Asia, while absolute costs are lower, hospitalisations once again account for the largest proportion of the spending.16,34

In contrast, the direct non-healthcare cost has been less explored, although it becomes relevant in certain contexts. For example, out-of-pocket spending in lower-middle income countries can represent a considerable burden for patients,14,27 whereas in European settings, long-term and residential care is a key component in the advanced stages of the disease, where dependency increases.25 This finding highlights the need to systematically include this dimension in economic analyses, as it reflects inequalities in access and financial burden beyond the healthcare system.32

Indirect cost represents another critical axis of the COPD burden, reflecting productivity losses and reduced income. Its relative weight varies according to the economic context: in high-income countries, although relevant, it is overshadowed by direct healthcare costs.22,25,40 However, in lower-middle income countries, the indirect cost may exceed the direct cost.27,35

Lastly, the intangible cost emerges as an extremely important dimension. The high prevalence of anxiety and depression,17,24 the noticeable decrease in HRQoL,13,20,26 and the interrelationship of distress among patients and informal caregivers20 reveal an impact that transcends the economic and has repercussions on the overall wellbeing of patients and their family environment. More recently, the perception of financial toxicity has been incorporated as an additional intangible cost, linked to worse emotional and physical outcomes.37,40

The burden of COPD cannot be explained by direct healthcare costs alone. The results emphasize the need for comprehensive approaches that include socio-economic and psychosocial dimensions, following the current trend towards more comprehensive disease assessments.

Our results consistently replicate the findings of the previous systematic review,8 in which most studies included direct healthcare costs, a smaller proportion included indirect costs, and only a minority addressed non-healthcare or intangible costs. In both studies, AECOPD and increased disease severity were identified as the main determinants of increased spending, with hospitalisations and drug therapy accounting for a predominant share of the total cost.

This review has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, there was substantial methodological heterogeneity among the included studies in terms of study design, economic perspective, cost components assessed, currencies used, and years of cost estimation. This variability limited the comparability of results across studies and precluded the possibility of conducting a quantitative meta-analysis. Second, an important limitation of this review is the unequal regional representation of the available evidence, with a predominance of studies conducted in high-income countries. This imbalance may limit the generalisability of the findings at a global level and may underestimate the economic and social burden of COPD in low- and middle – income settings, where healthcare access is more limited and out-of-pocket expenditure is often higher. Further research is these regions is needed to provide a more comprehensive and equitable understanding of the global burden of COPD. Third, several studies relied on administrative databases, insurance claims, or self-reported information, which may introduce potential sources of bias, such as coding inaccuracies, incomplete clinical information, or recall bias. Fourth, cost estimates were not adjusted for inflation or standardised across currencies, as data were reported as presented in the original studies to preserve methodological consistency. This may limit comparability across studies and time periods and should be considered when interpreting or extrapolating the findings. Fifthly, intangible costs, including psychological burden and health-related quality of life, were often measured indirectly using HRQoL instruments rather than through formal economic valuation methods. As a result, the full societal impact of COPD may still be underestimated in the current literature. Finally, although most included studies were based on pre-pandemic data, the post-COVID-19 context may have influenced healthcare utilisation, access to services, and the economic burden of chronic diseases such as COPD. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted with caution in relation to the current context.

Conclusion

This systematic review of 30 studies from 15 countries confirms that hospitalizations and acute exacerbations remain the primary drivers of direct economic expenditure in COPD, particularly in high-income settings. Furthermore, our findings underscore that while direct costs are predominant, indirect, and intangible costs—such as loss of productivity and psychological distress—represent a decisive, though often under-measured, component of the overall burden. The identified variability in costs is largely explained by clinical severity and socioeconomic determinants, highlighting the need for holistic management strategies that address both medical and psychosocial dimensions to ensure healthcare sustainability.
