Engineered macrophage membrane-mimicking nanodrugs activate cGAS/STING pathway to reverse tumor immune suppression after incomplete radiofrequency ablation
Authors: Wei-Hua Zhang, Lei Chen, Lin Gao, Ye-Ming Wu, Zhi-Cheng Jin, Jian-Jian Chen, Yan-Li An, Gao-Jun Teng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIncomplete radiofrequency ablation (iRFA) often results in tumor recurrence and therapeutic resistance, presenting significant clinical challenges. Tumors subjected to sublethal thermal injury activate the HSP70/ NQO1 antioxidant pathway and secrete elevated levels of CCL2, which recruits macrophages and fosters an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. To address these issues, this study engineered copper-doped ZIF-8 nanoparticles that co-deliver the hypoxia-activated prodrug TH-302 and the NQO1-targeting quinone β-lapachone, encapsulated within genetically engineered M1 macrophage membranes overexpressing CCR2 (CCR2-M). This innovative CCR2-M biomimetic coating enhances tumor targeting by sequestering excess CCL2, effectively reducing pro-tumoral macrophage infiltration. β-lapachone selectively targets NQO1-overexpressing tumor cells, elevating intracellular H2O2 levels, while copper-doped ZIF-8 catalyzes Fenton-like reactions to generate cytotoxic hydroxyl radicals. Simultaneously, TH-302, responsive to hypoxia, complements β-lapachone by inducing cell death in hypoxic regions, thereby mitigating β-lapachone’s oxygen dependence. This synergistic ROS burst effectively suppressing tumor growth, activating the cGAS-STING pathway and enhancing tumor antigen presentation. This synergistic ROS burst effectively suppresses tumor growth, activates the cGAS-STING pathway and enhances tumor antigen presentation. This cascade recruits dendritic cells and cytotoxic CD8+ T cells, ultimately reversing the immunosuppressive microenvironment induced by iRFA. No drug-related toxicity was observed. Thus, this rationally designed nanotherapeutic strategy significantly curtails residual tumor growth and offers a promising immunomodulatory approach to overcoming therapeutic resistance in cancer treatment after iRFA. Introduction

Radiofrequency ablation (RFA) is a widely employed radical treatment for various tumors, with fewer complications compared to surgical resection [1–3]. However, emerging evidence indicates that recurrence rates following thermal ablation significantly exceed those after surgical resection, limiting its broader clinical application [4]. Following incomplete ablation, tumors exhibit a significant increase in proliferation and metastatic potential [5]. Hypoxia within the residual tumor tissue becomes more pronounced [6]. Moreover, the remaining tumor recruits additional myeloid-derived immunosuppressive cells, which hinder the effectiveness of PD-1 antibody therapy [7].

Following thermal stress, tumors exhibit a marked increase in HSP70 expression, thereby diminishing the effectiveness of conventional chemotherapy such as cisplatin [8]. However, the underlying mechanisms driving this acquired resistance remain incompletely understood. This study reveals that sublethal thermal injury significantly activates the HSP70/NQO1 antioxidant signaling axis, suggesting its potential role in post-ablation drug resistance. In response, β-lapachone, a chemotherapeutic agent, selectively targets NQO1-overexpressing tumors, generating hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) through NQO1, inducing tumor cell death in an oxygen-dependent manner. However, hypoxic tumor microenvironments hinder this process, and the low cytotoxicity of H2O2 limits therapeutic efficacy. Therefore, strategies to enhance β-lapachone’s efficacy are essential [9].

Chemodynamic therapy (CDT) has emerged as a promising cancer treatment, leveraging metal ions to convert hydrogen peroxide into highly toxic hydroxyl radicals [10]. Divalent copper ions, in particular, effectively reduce glutathione (GSH) levels while converting into cuprous ions, promoting hydroxyl radical generation within tumor cells with minimal toxicity to normal tissues [11, 12]. Despite their potential, copper-based nanomaterials face challenges such as inadequate exposure of ion-active sites and non-biodegradability [13]. This study employed copper-doped zeolitic imidazolate framework-8 (Cu-Z) as a solution to the controlled ion release challenge, preserving the carrier properties of ZIF-8. Cu-Z provides a significant number of exposed active sites to enhance catalytic activity, reduces premature copper ion leakage, and improves biocompatibility.

Biomimetic membrane coatings derived from immune cells have become a powerful approach to enhance nanoparticle targeting and therapeutic efficacy [14]. Membranes from macrophages, particularly pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages, exhibit inherent tumor-tropic properties mediated by integrins and can reprogram tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) towards an anti-tumor phenotype [15]. However, the targeting efficiency of traditional macrophage membrane-coated nanoparticles is often suboptimal. Genetically modifying macrophages to specifically respond to tumor-derived chemokines could significantly improve nanoparticle homing [16]. Given that residual tumors after incomplete RFA (iRFA) secrete high levels of CCL2 , it is hypothesized that engineering macrophage membranes to overexpress the cognate receptor CCR2 (CCR2-M) could substantially enhance targeted nanoparticle delivery while concurrently disrupting tumor-mediated macrophage recruitment.

In this study, a multifunctional nanotherapeutic was developed, consisting of copper-doped ZIF-8 nanoparticles co-loaded with the hypoxia-activated prodrug TH-302 and β-lapachone, subsequently cloaked with genetically engineered CCR2-overexpressing M1 macrophage membranes. These CCR2-overexpressing macrophage membranes are effectively recruited by CCL2 in residual tumors while simultaneously reducing TAM recruitment, thus acting as a “nano decoy.” Within the tumor microenvironment, β-lapachone generates intracellular ROS through NQO1-mediated H2O2 production. Simultaneously, TH-302, also known as evofosfamide, is activated by hypoxia and complements the action of β-lapachone by targeting hypoxic tumor regions, thus overcoming the limitations caused by tumor oxygenation heterogeneity. The resulting oxidative stress and DNA damage induce immunogenic cell death (ICD) and activate the cGAS-STING signaling pathway, which promotes the recruitment of cross-presenting dendritic cells (cDC1) and enhances tumor antigen presentation. This process stimulates cytotoxic CD8+ T cell responses and reverses the immunosuppressive post-ablation environment (Scheme 1).

Results and discussion

Incomplete ablation alters the tumor microenvironment, enhancing tumor malignancy and promoting residual tumor growth

Following prior research protocols, iRFA was simulated by subjecting Hepa1-6 and MC38 cells to a 45 °C water bath for 10 min in vitro [8]. Initially, the proliferation and metastatic potential of mouse tumor cell lines post-ablation were assessed. A significant increase in PCNA expression was observed in both cell lines after the thermal treatment (Fig. 1A and Fig. S1A), and the migration assay revealed enhanced migration to the lower chamber in the iRFA group (Fig. 1B and Fig. S1B). Next, the anti-proliferative effects of sorafenib, a first-line tyrosine kinase inhibitor for hepatocellular carcinoma, were evaluated in Hepa1-6 cells with or without iRFA. The results indicated that tumor cells exhibited increased resistance to the TKI following sublethal thermal injury (Fig. S1D). HSP70, a well-characterized heat shock protein, is significantly upregulated following iRFA, thereby promoting the growth of residual tumor cells [17]. Additionally, HSP70 play a crucial role in cellular antioxidant defense by preventing protein aggregation and enhancing the stability of NAD(P)H: quinone oxidoreductase 1 (NQO1), which detoxifies reactive quinones to reduce reactive oxygen species (ROS) and strengthen resistance to oxidative and chemotherapeutic stress [18]. The HSP70/NQO1 antioxidant axis is strongly induced after photothermal ablation [19]. However, it remains unclear whether this pathway is directly activated by sublethal thermal injury. To explore this, the expression levels of key proteins in this pathway were examined, revealing significant upregulation of HSP70 and NQO1 in both MC38 and Hepa1-6 cell lines (Fig. 1C and Fig. S1C), confirming the activation of antioxidant pathways following sublethal thermal injury.

CCL2 levels increase substantially in residual tumors after iRFA, leading to macrophage recruitment [7]. To further investigate the relationship between sublethal thermal injury and CCL2-mediated macrophage recruitment, a migration model was employed in which tumor cells were seeded in the lower chamber and macrophages in the upper chamber. After sublethal thermal injury, both tumor cell lines recruited more macrophages (Fig. 1D and Fig. S1E). A significant upregulation of CCL2 mRNA expression and an increase in CCL2 release in the supernatants were observed after thermal injury (Fig. 1E, Fig. S1F), indicating that tumor cells secrete more CCL2 post-ablation to recruit macrophages. To investigate the effect of sublethal thermal injury on macrophage polarization, RAW264.7 macrophages were co-cultured with tumor cells, as depicted in Fig. 1F. iRFA induced increased expression of M2 macrophage markers, such as IL-10 and TGF-β1, while reducing the expression of M1 polarization markers, including IL-12 and TNF-α (Fig. 1G). These results suggest that tumor cells not only recruit more macrophages but also promote M2 polarization following sublethal thermal injury [20].

iRFA was performed in an MC38 subcutaneous tumor model. H&E staining and the TUNEL assay revealed considerable tumor apoptosis in the necrotic areas (Fig. 2K and L). However, tumors initially exhibited slight shrinkage post-ablation, followed by faster growth (Fig. 1I). The final tumor volume and weight were significantly higher than those in the non-ablation group (Fig. 1H and J), consistent with previous reports in the literature [21, 22]. Ki67 immunohistochemistry revealed stronger Ki67 expression at the tumor boundary (Fig. 1M). These results suggest that after iRFA, tumors undergo slight necrosis, followed by a significant increase in proliferative capacity. The expression of HSP70 is significantly upregulated in the tumor region after iRFA, suggesting the activation of the heat shock response (Fig. 1N). Additionally, higher expression of the M2 macrophage marker CD206 was observed at the tumor boundary (Fig. 1O), further confirming that after sublethal thermal ablation, tumors recruit more macrophages and polarize them towards the M2 phenotype.

Synthesis and characterization of the nanoparticle

To overcome these challenges, copper-doped ZIF-8 nanoparticles co-loaded with TH-302 and β-lapachone, encapsulated in M1 macrophage membranes overexpressing CCR2, were specifically engineered to target residual tumors following iRFA. The copper-doped ZIF-8 nanoparticles loaded with TH-302 and β-lapachone (TLCu-Z) were synthesized via a one-pot method in methanol, followed by ultrasound and membrane extrusion to coat the surface with macrophage membranes. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) confirmed that the particles exhibited a uniform size distribution and polyhedral morphology, with the macrophage membrane coating clearly visible on the surface (Fig. 2 A and B, S2A). Elemental analysis verified the uniform distribution of zinc and copper within the nanoparticles and confirmed successful drug loading of TH-302 by detecting bromine. Additionally, phosphorus and sulfur elements were detected, indicating successful macrophage membrane encapsulation (Fig. 2C, S2B). ICP-MS results showed that the copper content accounted for 2.67 ± 0.26% of the total TLCu-Z content, and the zinc content accounted for 13.75 ± 0.43% of the total TLCu-Z content, which was lower than the Cu/Zn ratios used in this experiment (Table S1). Dynamic light scattering measurements revealed an average particle size of 203.9 ± 6.04 nm for TLCu-Z, with a polydispersity index (PDI) of 0.12, slightly larger than the Cu-Z nanoparticles (189.6 ± 2.43 nm, PDI = 0.07). Following macrophage membrane coating, the size further increased to 235.1 ± 3.41 nm, with a PDI of 0.14 (Fig. 2D), confirming the successful encapsulation of macrophage membranes. The Zeta potentials of Cu-Z, TLCu-Z, and TLCu-Z@CM were 41.2 mV, 8.5 mV, and − 13.3 mV, respectively (Fig. 2E).

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy confirmed the successful incorporation of copper ions into TLCu-Z, with Cu 2p3/2 and Cu 2p1/2 peaks observed at 933.55 eV and 952.75 eV, respectively, indicating that Cu was in the + 2-valence state (Fig. 2F). X-ray diffraction analysis showed that copper ion doping and drug loading did not alter the ZIF-8 crystal structure, maintaining its integrity (Fig. 2G), consistent with previous reports [23]. Cu2+ doping enables ZIF-8 to efficiently deplete GSH, and in mildly acidic environments, Cu+ catalyzes Fenton-like reactions to generate highly reactive hydroxyl radicals (•OH). As demonstrated in previous studies [24, 25], the production of •OH was further confirmed by a decrease in MB absorbance with increasing TLCu-Z@CM concentration, and by ESR imaging, which revealed a characteristic 1:2:2:1 doublet signal, confirming the catalytic production of •OH (Fig. S2E, S2F). These results indicate that copper doping facilitates Fenton-like reactions, enhancing the generation of potent hydroxyl radicals.

Quantitative PCR (qPCR) and flow cytometry analyses were performed to evaluate the efficiency of CCR2 overexpression in RAW264.7 macrophages. After transduction with a CCR2-overexpressing lentiviral vector, both CCR2 mRNA and protein levels were significantly elevated (Fig. 2H and I, Fig. S2D). Membrane vesicles were then isolated from these CCR2-overexpressing M1-polarized macrophages (CCR2-M) and coated onto the surface of TLCu-Z nanoparticles. SDS-PAGE analysis revealed that the protein composition of TLCu-Z@CM closely mirrored that of CCR2-M, and Western blot analysis confirmed the presence of key membrane proteins, including CCR2, on the surface of TLCu-Z@CM (Fig. S2C, Fig. 2J). The drug loading capacity for β-lapachone and TH-302 were 9.61 ± 0.22 and 11.69 ± 0.27%, respectively, and the standard curve was shown in Fig. S2 and S3, as determined by HPLC analysis, similar to that of ZIF-8 in previous reports. (Fig. S2G, S2H) [26, 27].To evaluate the acid responsiveness of TLCu-Z, the nanoparticles were incubated at different pH values, and drug release was monitored. At pH 6.5, both drugs exhibited significantly higher release compared to pH 7.4 (Fig. 2K, L). TEM images confirmed that after 4 h of incubation at pH 6.5, the nanoparticles underwent acid degradation, providing in vitro evidence of drug release in the acidic tumor microenvironment (Fig. 2M) [28]. In order to further verify the stability of the nanomaterials under physiological conditions, we incubated the nanomaterials in DMEM containing 10% FBS and measured their particle size at different time points. The results showed that the nanomaterials maintained stable particle size for up to 7 days (Fig. S2I).

Nanoparticle uptake, macrophage recruitment and polarization, and tumor cell treatment effectiveness

Four distinct nanoparticles—Cu-Z/IR780, Cu-Z@M/IR780, and Cu-Z@CM/IR780—were incubated with MC38 tumor cells to assess their behavior. CCR2-M significantly promoted nanoparticle accumulation in tumor cells compared to macrophage membranes without CCR2 overexpression (Fig. 3A and B). Additionally, intracellular GSSG levels were measured under various treatments, revealing that the Cu-Z@CM group facilitated the entry of copper ions into the tumor, leading to the oxidation of GSH to GSSG (Fig. S3A), which in turn enhanced ROS-induced cytotoxicity [29].

A transwell system was used to evaluate the impact of CCR2 overexpression on macrophage recruitment. Compared to the Cu-Z@M group, Cu-Z@CM intervention notably reduced macrophage migration to the tumor site, achieving only 54% migration (Fig. 3C and D), demonstrating that CCR2-M can effectively suppress macrophage migration toward tumor cells. Further analysis of M1 and M2 macrophage marker mRNA expression revealed that Cu-Z significantly upregulated TNF-α and IL-12 expression while downregulating TGF-β1 and IL-10 expression. This effect was enhanced when M1 macrophage membranes and CCR2-M were incorporated (Fig. S3C, S3D, S3E, S3F), confirming that Cu-Z and M1 macrophage vesicles promote M1 polarization, with CCR2 overexpression further intensifying this effect [30].

Next, the tumor-killing efficacy of the various nanomaterials loaded with chemotherapeutic agents was assessed in vitro. The Calcein AM/PI assay indicated that Cu-Z@M induced tumor cell death, and CCR2 overexpression enhanced the tumor-killing effect of Cu-Z@M. Co-loading two distinct chemotherapeutic agents led to the most significant tumor cell death (Fig. 3E and F). ROS production was subsequently analyzed in tumor cells using immunofluorescence and flow cytometry, revealing that TLCu-Z@CM significantly enhanced ROS generation (Fig. 3G H, 3I, and Fig. S3B). Additionally, an increase in JC-1 monomers and a decrease in JC-1 aggregates were observed in the TLCu-Z@CM group (Fig. S3G, S3H). These results indicate that nanoparticle cytotoxicity is significantly enhanced under hypoxic conditions after loading β-lapachone, with the combination of TH-302 resulting in the highest ROS production and the most pronounced tumor cell death. This demonstrates the successful synergy of normoxic chemotherapy drugs and hypoxia-activated chemotherapy agents for tumor treatment [31].

To investigate the mechanisms underlying tumor cell death, we conducted rescue treatments with necrostatin-1 (a necroptosis inhibitor), emricasan (an apoptosis inhibitor), and ferrostatin-1 (a ferroptosis inhibitor). CCK-8 assay results demonstrated that all three inhibitors partially rescued tumor cells from death, indicating that TLCu-Z@CM treatment induced multiple cell death pathways, including necroptosis, apoptosis, and ferroptosis (Fig. 3J). Next, this study examined whether the tumor cell death induced by TLCu-Z@CM through various mechanisms also caused DNA damage and promoted the release of free dsDNA. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed a significant increase in cytosolic dsDNA per cell, indicating DNA damage in tumor cells and the release of DNA into the cytoplasm (Fig. 3K and L). This suggests that TLCu-Z@CM induces complex cytotoxic effects in tumor cells, promoting the generation of free dsDNA in the cytoplasm and establishing the conditions for the occurrence of various cell death pathways.

In vitro induction of ICD, activation of the cGAS-STING pathway, and immune cell activation

cGAS, a critical cytosolic sensor, binds to cytoplasmic dsDNA and activates the STING pathway, which can trigger various forms of cell death in tumor cells, such as pyroptosis and necroptosis [32]. Additionally, the secretion of IFN-β plays a pivotal role in recruiting cDC1 cells to induce antitumor immunity [33]. To explore this, the phosphorylation levels of key molecules were first examined via Western blot. The results revealed a significant increase in phosphorylated TBK1 (P-TBK1) and phosphorylated STING (P-STING) in the TLCu-Z@CM group (Fig. 4A and Fig. S4A). Immunofluorescence of P-IRF3 demonstrated that TLCu-Z@CM treatment not only increased cytoplasmic P-IRF3 levels but also notably enhanced nuclear P-IRF3 expression (Fig. 4I and Fig. S4D). Consistent with the Western blot results, mRNA expression levels of Ifnb1, ISG15, and CXCL10 were significantly upregulated in the TLCu-Z@CM group (Fig. 4B, C and D). ELISA results further confirmed that both cGAMP and IFN-β were significantly elevated in the TLCu-Z@CM group, providing additional evidence of cGAS-STING pathway activation (Fig. 4E and F). Notably, Cu-Z@M was also effective in promoting cGAS-STING pathway activation, likely due to copper ions facilitating hydroxyl radical production and zinc ions enhancing cGAS enzymatic activity. The encapsulation of CCR2-M may further amplify this effect by increasing the accumulation of Cu-Z.

Since cGAS-STING activation upregulates MHC-I expression and antigen presentation in tumor cells [34], MC38-OVA cells were treated with various formulations, and a significant increase in the expression of SIINFEKL bound to H2Kb was observed on the cell surface. The most pronounced increase was observed in the TLCu-Z@CM group, indicating enhanced tumor cell surface antigen-MHC-I complex expression (Fig. 4G and H). Furthermore, the ability of these treatments to induce ICD, a crucial process for promoting DC recruitment and maturation, was evaluated. MC38 cells treated with TLCu-Z@CM following sublethal heat shock showed a significant increase in calreticulin (CRT) expression on the cell surface and substantial ATP and HMGB1 release into the supernatant (Fig. 4J and Fig. S4B, S4C, S4D, S4E, S4F).

The impact of tumor cells treated with TLCu-Z@CM on DC maturation and antigen presentation was further assessed. Flow cytometric analysis revealed that, after TLCu-Z@CM treatment, the highest proportion of DCs co-expressed CD86 and MHC II (Fig. 4K, Fig. S4G). Furthermore, the surface expression of SIINFEKL bound to H2Kb on BMDCs was significantly upregulated after co-culture with MC38-OVA cells treated with TLCu-Z@CM (Fig. S4H, S4I), compared to other groups. This demonstrates that tumor cells treated with TLCu-Z@CM not only promote BMDC maturation but also enhance antigen presentation.

Activation of the cGAS-STING pathway, along with the induction of ICD markers, significantly promotes DC maturation, enabling DCs to present antigens to naïve T cells. This process facilitates T cell differentiation into cytotoxic T cells, thereby triggering a robust T cell–mediated immune response [35]. To investigate this, a three-cell co-culture system was established, consisting of MC38 cells (pretreated with different agents), immature DCs, and spleen-derived CD8+ T cells, which were incubated together for 48 h. IFN-γ and CD69 expression in T cells was then analyzed. The results showed that TLCu-Z@CM significantly increased IFN-γ and CD69 levels in CD8+ T cells, indicating pronounced T cell activation and enhanced tumor cell killing through the DC–T cell axis (Fig. 4L and M, Fig. S4J, S4K).

In vivo anti-tumor effect

To evaluate whether CCR2 overexpression on macrophage membranes enhances the delivery of membrane vesicle-coated nanodrugs, partial RFA was performed on subcutaneous MC38 tumor-bearing mice. Twelve hours post-ablation, either Cu-Z@M/IR780 or Cu-Z@CM/IR780 was administered, and drug localization was quantified using near-infrared imaging at 1, 6, 12, and 24 h after injection. Both formulations, Cu-Z@CM/IR780 and Cu-Z@M/IR780, exhibited peak accumulation at 12 h, followed by a decrease at 24 h. However, Cu-Z@CM/IR780 showed greater and more sustained accumulation at the tumor site compared to Cu-Z@M/IR780, suggesting that CCR2-M is recruited by the overexpressed CCL2 in the residual tumor tissue (Fig. 5A and Fig. S5A).

Building on these findings, the therapeutic impact of CCR2-M was examined following iRFA. Twelve hours after ablation, mice were randomized into six groups, each receiving a distinct nanoparticle formulation (Fig. 5B). Tumor progression diverged notably across groups: while tumor growth continued in five groups, only the TLCu-Z@CM group exhibited a significant reduction in tumor volume. When the formulation combined chemotherapeutic agents, the tumor inhibitory effect was maximized, yielding the smallest tumor volume and weight, as well as the longest survival time (Fig. 5C, D, E and F). Notably, no significant body weight loss was observed in any group, and liver function (ALT and AST levels) remained within normal limits, with no statistical differences. Histological analysis of heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney tissues post-treatment revealed no evidence of cell death, confirming a favorable overall safety profile (Fig. 5G, Fig. S5B, S5C, S5D).

Subsequent pathological evaluations provided deeper insights into the mechanisms underlying tumor reduction. H&E staining revealed extensive necrosis in tumors treated with TLCu-Z@CM, indicative of a strong cytotoxic effect (Fig. 5H). Ki67 immunohistochemistry in residual tumors showed a marked reduction in proliferating tumor cells, while TUNEL assays demonstrated the highest apoptotic index in tissues treated with TLCu-Z@CM, signaling significant activation of cell death (Fig. 5I and J). Concurrent assessment of CD206 and CD86 expression showed that Cu-Z@M reduced CD206 and increased CD86 expression at the tumor boundary, with a more pronounced effect in the Cu-Z@CM group (Fig. 5K and L). The addition of two chemotherapeutic agents further enhanced these effects, supporting the role of drug-loaded nanoparticles in repolarizing TAMs and promoting antitumor immunity [36]. In alignment with in vitro findings, CCR2-M facilitated nanoparticle accumulation in residual tumors, enhancing drug cytotoxicity and concurrently reducing TAM recruitment. The combined administration of both chemotherapeutic agents amplified the tumor suppression and TAM reduction effects. This suggests that, within the tumor’s hypoxic microenvironment, β-lapachone treatment remains highly effective. The use of hypoxia-activated prodrugs can precisely target and eliminate tumors in hypoxic regions, working synergistically with β-lapachone to inhibit tumor growth.

In vivo antitumor immune response activation

Previous in vitro studies have demonstrated that TLCu-Z@CM not only directly targets tumor tissue but also modulates the immune microenvironment. To systematically evaluate the impact of TLCu-Z@CM on the immune landscape within residual tumors, flow cytometry was employed to profile tumor-infiltrating immune cells after two treatments (Fig. S6A). The results revealed that Cu-Z coated with membrane vesicles derived from M1 macrophages significantly reduced the population of CD206-high macrophages within tumors, driving the reprogramming of M2 macrophages—characterized by high CD206 expression—into an M1 phenotype marked by elevated CD86 levels. This macrophage polarization effect was notably more pronounced in the Cu-Z@CM group, with the therapeutic potential further enhanced upon incorporation of chemotherapeutic agents into the nanoparticle formulation (Fig. 6A and B).

Additionally, flow cytometric analysis showed substantial increases in DC maturation markers, including CD86 and MHC-II, following TLCu-Z@CM treatment (Fig. 6C and D). Among DC subsets, cDC1, recognized for their superior cross-presentation abilities, were notably recruited to tumor tissues after TLCu-Z@CM treatment (Fig. 6E and F). This suggests that TLCu-Z@CM enhances the antitumor activity of DCs, corroborating our in vitro findings. Further analysis of IFN-γ-positive cell populations in CD8+ T cells (Fig. 6G and H) and natural killer (NK) cells (Fig. 6I and J) within tumor tissues revealed that Cu-Z@M significantly boosted IFN-γ secretion in both cell types. This immunostimulatory effect was notably amplified by CCR2 overexpression in macrophage membranes, with the TLCu-Z@CM group showing the most substantial enhancement of IFN-γ production. Moreover, TLCu-Z@CM treatment significantly reduced the proportion of CD25+FOXP3+ cells in CD4+ T cells (Fig. 6K and L), markers of Tregs. This suggests that TLCu-Z@CM not only enhances cytotoxic T lymphocyte activity but also alleviates immune suppression following iRFA.

Immunohistochemical staining was performed to validate immune activation at the histological level. The analysis revealed a significant increase in CD103+ cells surrounding CD31+ vascular structures within the tumor microenvironment, along with a marked reduction in CD31+ cells (Fig. 6M). This observation likely reflects the targeted activity of TH-302-loaded nanoparticles in hypoxic regions of tumor tissues [37]. Additionally, since IFN-γ production may also originate from exhausted CD8+ T cells [38], the infiltration and activation status of these cells was evaluated by analyzing TNF-α expression. Treatment with TLCu-Z@CM significantly enhanced both CD8+ T cell infiltration and TNF-α production within tumor tissues, providing further evidence of effective immune activation and therapeutic efficacy (Fig. 6N). Finally, the levels of antitumor cytokines, TNF-α and IFN-γ, were measured within the tumors. Consistent with the flow cytometry and immunofluorescence results, the cytokine levels in tumors of mice treated with TLCu-Z@CM were the highest, further confirming that TLCu-Z@CM elicited the most potent antitumor immune response (Fig. S6D, S6E).

Transcriptomic analysis of MC38 tumor tissues after TLCu-Z@CM treatment

To assess the transcriptional landscape of tumor tissues following TLCu-Z@CM treatment, in vivo transcriptomic profiling was performed. Tumor samples from both PBS- and TLCu-Z@CM-treated groups were harvested for RNA sequencing. A Venn diagram showed that 418 genes were uniquely expressed in the TLCu-Z@CM treatment group (Fig. 7A). A total of 1,274 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (fold change ≥ 1.5, P-value < 0.05) were identified, with 589 genes upregulated and 685 downregulated (Fig. 7B). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed significant enrichment in pathways related to metal ion response, notably the upregulation of metallothioneins MT1 and MT2, which are involved in zinc and copper ion transport. When the intracellular concentrations of copper and zinc ions increase, MT1/2 expression is upregulated through a transcriptional activation mechanism. Its cysteine-rich domain specifically chelates the excess metal ions, forming metal-MT complexes, which negatively feedback to reduce the toxicity and immune response of copper and zinc [39]. This suggests that the nanoparticles effectively targeted tumor tissues and triggered a corresponding molecular response. Additionally, GO analysis highlighted significant enrichment in immune response and cytokine-related processes, indicating that TLCu-Z@CM modulates the tumor immune microenvironment (Fig. 7C and E).

Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis further corroborated these findings, identifying several immune-related pathways enriched following TLCu-Z@CM treatment. Given that cGAS functions as a cytosolic DNA sensor by binding free dsDNA and activating downstream signaling through IFN-β production [40], transcriptional changes in the cytosolic DNA-sensing pathway were examined (Fig. 7D and F). The results demonstrated a significant increase in the expression of Ifnb1 and CXCL10, aligning with our in vitro findings. The upregulation of CXCL10 and IFN provides strong evidence for the activation of the cGAS-STING pathway. Specifically, the significant increase in CXCL10, a T-cell chemoattractant, along with the enhanced expression of Ifnb1, confirms the activation of the STING pathway and its role in initiating immune responses. Moreover, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) revealed upregulation of genes in the “Cellular response to metal ion” and “Cytosolic DNA-sensing pathway” categories, further supporting the activation of immune responses in tumors following TLCu-Z@CM treatment. Additionally, GSEA indicated significant upregulation of DNA damage and TNF-α pathways in the TLCu-Z@CM group, suggesting that TLCu-Z@CM induces DNA damage, activates the cGAS-STING pathway, and thereby enhances antitumor immunity (Fig. 7G and H, Fig. S7A, S7B).

To further confirm the activation of the cGAS-STING pathway in vivo following TLCu-Z@CM treatment, tumor samples were collected from MC38 tumor-bearing mice treated with different formulations. Western blot, qPCR, and ELISA assays were performed to assess the expression of key cGAS-STING pathway-related genes and proteins. Phosphorylation levels of P-TBK1, P-IRF3, and P-STING were first analyzed. Consistent with previous findings, Cu-Z@M significantly promoted the phosphorylation of these proteins. Among all groups, the TLCu-Z@CM group showed the most prominent phosphorylation, indicating the strongest activation of the cGAS-STING pathway (Fig. 7I, Fig. S7C). Next, qPCR was performed to measure the mRNA expression of Ifnb1 and CXCL10. The results showed the highest increase in Ifnb1 and CXCL10 mRNA expression in the TLCu-Z@CM group. Additionally, ELISA results were consistent with the qPCR findings, showing significantly higher IFN-β levels in the TLCu-Z@CM group (Fig. 7J, K and L). These results, in line with in vitro findings and in vivo transcriptomic data, demonstrate that TLCu-Z@CM treatment effectively promotes cGAS-STING pathway activation, leading to enhanced immune activation.

In vivo anti-tumor efficacy and anti-tumor immune activation of TLCu-Z@CM combined with PD-L1 mAb

Although the activation of the cGAS-STING pathway effectively recruits cDC1 and CD8+ T cells, thereby inducing a robust antitumor immune response, the activation of IFN-β and IFN-γ can upregulate immune checkpoint genes such as PD-L1, leading to an “immune brake” response [41, 42]. This provides a rationale for combining nanoparticle-based therapy with PD-L1 blockade [43]. To investigate this, a subcutaneous Hepa1-6 OVA tumor model was established. Once the tumor volume reached 350 mm³, mice underwent iRFA followed by treatment with PBS, TLCu-Z@CM, PD-L1 monoclonal antibody (mAb), or a combination of TLCu-Z@CM and PD-L1 mAb.

First, the direct antitumor effects were evaluated by monitoring tumor growth curves and tumor volume. The combination treatment group exhibited the most potent antitumor effects, with the smallest tumor volume and weight, along with the longest median survival time of 57 days compared to single-agent treatments (Fig. 8A, B, C and D). Next, the proportion of cDC1 and IFN-γ-positive cells in CD8+ T cells within the tumor was assessed. Consistent with previous results in MC38 tumors, TLCu-Z@CM treatment significantly increased the proportion of cDC1 and IFN-γ-positive CD8+ T cells in the tumor. Notably, when combined with PD-L1 mAb, this increase was further amplified, with a 0.3-fold increase in cDC1 cells and a 0.4-fold increase in IFN-γ-positive CD8+ T cells (Fig. 8E and F).

Given that OVA was overexpressed as a tumor-associated antigen in Hepa1-6 cells, the antigen presentation capacity of T cells was evaluated. After TLCu-Z@CM treatment, the proportion of CD8+ T cells positive for SIINFEKL bound to H-2Kb significantly increased, with further enhancement observed upon adding PD-L1 mAb (Fig. 8G and J). Subsequently, CD69 expression on CD8+ T cells was examined. TLCu-Z@CM treatment led to a significant increase in CD69 expression on CD8+ T cells. Furthermore, when combined with PD-L1 mAb, CD69 expression on CD8+ T cells was further increased (Fig. 8H and K). These results suggest that the combination of TLCu-Z@CM with PD-L1 mAb not only enhances the antitumor efficacy of the nanoparticles but also strengthens the activation of antitumor immunity.

Additionally, analysis of the proportion of CD8+CD44+ memory T cells in tumor tissues revealed a significant increase in their percentage within the tumor microenvironment following TLCu-Z@CM treatment, indicating robust immune surveillance. When combined with PD-L1 mAb, the proportion of memory T cells was further elevated (Fig. 8I and L). Subsequently, the proportions of central memory T cells (CD44highCD62Lhigh) and effector memory T cells (CD44highCD62Llow) in the spleens of treated mice were evaluated. The combination of TLCu-Z@CM and PD-L1 mAb resulted in the most significant increase in both central memory and effector T cells compared to other groups (Fig. 8M, N and O). These results collectively demonstrate that TLCu-Z@CM, in combination with PD-L1 mAb, not only improves antigen-presentation capabilities but also enhances immune activation, synergistically promoting a more potent antitumor immune response.

Therapeutic evaluation of TLCu-Z@CM combined with PD-L1 mAb in orthotopic HCC after iRFA

To comprehensively assess the therapeutic potential and immunomodulatory effects of TLCu-Z@CM combined with PD-L1 mAb in residual HCC RFA, we established an orthotopic HCC model by implanting Hepa1-6 cells into murine livers. Following RFA intervention, animals were randomized into different treatment cohorts (Fig. 9A). Longitudinal MRI monitoring demonstrated that both TLCu-Z@CM monotherapy and PD-L1 mAb alone effectively suppressed tumor progression, mirroring observations from the subcutaneous tumor model. Strikingly, combinatorial therapy achieved superior tumor growth inhibition, providing compelling evidence for TLCu-Z@CM-mediated potentiation of PD-L1 mAb efficacy (Fig. 9B, C and D). This synergistic effect was histologically validated by Ki67 immunostaining showing minimal proliferative activity in the combination group (Fig. 9E).

Further investigation into TAMs revealed distinct phenotypic alterations. The combined treatment group demonstrated the highest density of M1-polarized macrophages coupled with the most pronounced reduction in M2-subtype infiltration, providing direct evidence for TLCu-Z@CM-driven TAM repolarization (Fig. 9F and G). Parallel analysis demonstrated enhanced cross-talk between cDC1 and CD8 + T cells in the combination group, indicating robust activation of adaptive antitumor immunity - a finding consistent with subcutaneous model outcomes (Fig. 9H and I).

Given the relevance of RFA-induced metastatic progression in HCC, we systematically evaluated pulmonary metastasis. Macroscopic examination of lung specimens showed TLCu-Z@CM monotherapy significantly reduced both metastatic burden and lesion size. The combinatorial regimen achieved maximal suppression of metastatic dissemination, as quantified by histopathological analysis revealing minimal tumor nodules in H&E-stained lung sections from this group (Fig. 9J).

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that residual tumors following iRFA exhibit significant activation of the HSP70/NQO1 pathway. M1 macrophage membranes overexpressing CCR2 can effectively enhance targeting efficiency of the nanoparticles to the residual tumor. The combination of β-lapachone with TH-302 not only targets the HSP70/NQO1 axis to counteract tumor resistance to conventional therapies but also synergistically targets both normoxic and hypoxic tumor regions, addressing the limitations of single-agent therapies. Additionally, the inclusion of copper facilitates the conversion of hydrogen peroxide into potent hydroxyl radicals, which activate the cGAS-STING pathway. This activation enhances antigen presentation and immune cell activation, reversing the immunosuppressive microenvironment induced by iRFA. When combined with PD-L1 mAb, this nanoparticle system significantly improves tumor suppression, immune activation, and the generation of memory immunity in the residual tumor, presenting a promising strategy for future cancer therapies.

However, further optimization of the nanoparticles, a deeper investigation into the role of zinc ions, and a more comprehensive understanding of tumor cell death mechanisms are required. Additional animal studies are necessary to evaluate the clinical safety and efficacy of this approach.

Methods and materials

Materials and reagents

Zinc nitrate was sourced from Sigma-Aldrich (USA). 2-Dimethylimidazole was obtained from Aladdin Reagent (Shanghai, China), and copper nitrate was purchased from Aladdin Reagent (Shanghai, China). β-lapachone and TH-302 (evofosfamide) were acquired from Selleck Chemicals (Houston, USA). IR780 was obtained from Merck (Germany). The CCK-8 assay kit was from Selleck Chemicals (Houston, USA), and DCFH-DA was acquired from MedChemExpress (New Jersey, USA). The JC-1 assay kit was purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China), and DAPI was also obtained from Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). P-TBK1, TBK1, P-IRF3, P-STING, STING, and β-actin antibodies were supplied by Cell Signaling Technology (CST, Danvers, USA). The dsDNA antibody was purchased from Santa Cruz Biotechnology (Dallas, USA), and the GSSG assay kit was acquired from Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). transwell plates were sourced from Corning (New York, USA).

Synthesis of TH302/β-lapachone@Cu-Z

The synthesis of TH302/β-lapachone@Cu-Z (TLCu-Z) nanoparticles followed previously published methods with modifications [13]. Briefly, 1 mg of TH-302, 1 mg of β-lapachone, and 12 mg of Zn(NO3)2·H2O were dissolved in 333 µL methanol. A separate solution of 2.4 mg of Cu(NO3)2·3H2O and 33 mg of 2-dimethylimidazole was prepared in 333 µL methanol. The two solutions were mixed, stirred for 20 min, and then centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 10 min. The resulting precipitate was washed twice with ethanol and water and then freeze-dried to obtain the TLCu-Z nanoparticles.

For the preparation of TH302@Cu-ZIF-8 (TCu-Z) and β-lapachone@Cu-Z (LCu-Z) nanoparticles, the procedure was identical to that for TLCu-Z, but without the inclusion of TH-302 or β-lapachone. The drug loading capacity of TH-302 and β-lapachone was determined using HPLC. The drug loading capacity (DL%) was calculated based on the difference in concentration before and after drug loading.

The synthesis of Cu-Z/IR780 involved a two-step procedure: first, Cu-ZIF-8 was synthesized; subsequently, 5 mg of Cu-Z was incubated with 1 mg of IR780 at room temperature for 1 h to achieve IR780 loading.

Preparation of cell Membrane-Coated nanoparticles (TLCu-Z@CM)

RAW264.7 macrophages were transfected with CCR2-overexpressing lentivirus and selected with puromycin. The cells were expanded and polarized into M1 macrophages using LPS. Membrane proteins were extracted using a membrane protein extraction kit. Briefly, RAW264.7 cells were collected using a cell scraper, washed with PBS by centrifugation, and dispersed in membrane protein extraction buffer. The cells were rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen and thawed at room temperature three times. The suspension was then centrifuged at 700 g for 10 min at 4 °C, followed by further centrifugation at 14,000 g for 30 min to collect the supernatant. The resulting cell membrane was freeze-dried for further use. The cell membranes were mixed with an equal volume of nanoparticles and sonicated for 30 s. The mixture was extruded 11 times through a 400 nm polycarbonate membrane. Remaining cell membranes were discarded by centrifugation at 1000 g for 10 min, and the final TLCu-Z@CM was resuspended in 0.5 mL of water for further use.

The size and Zeta potential of the nanoparticles were determined using a ZetaSizer Nano ZS90 (Malvern, UK). ICP-MS measurements were performed on a ThermoScientific Xseries II inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometer. The drug loading (DL%) was calculated using the following formulas:

Extracellular •OH generation detection

To evaluate the in vitro generation of hydroxyl radicals (•OH), TLCu-Z nanoparticles at concentrations of 0, 10, 20, and 30 µg/mL were incubated with methylene blue (MB, 10 µg/mL), GSH (10 mM), and H2O2 (30 mM) in PBS (pH 6.5) for 1 h. The degradation of MB was monitored by measuring the absorbance at 665 nm using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer. Additionally, the reaction mixture containing 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline-N-oxide (DMPO) was transferred into a quartz capillary, and electron spin resonance (ESR) spectroscopy was performed to detect the characteristic •OH signal.

Cell culture

MC38, Hepa1-6 and RAW264.7 were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (CCAS, China). Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% (v/v) penicillin–streptomycin. Stable OVA-expressing Hepa1-6 and MC38 cell lines (Hepa1-6 OVA and MC38 OVA) were generated via lentiviral transduction. All cells were grown in a humidified incubator at 37 °C and 5% CO2.

Live/Dead cell staining assay and JC-1 staining

MC38 cells were seeded in a 6-well plate at a density of 5 × 105 cells per well and cultured in 2 mL DMEM. The following day, cells were subjected to a 10-minute 45 °C water bath to simulate sublethal thermal injury. Cells were then treated with PBS or 20 µg/mL of different drug formulations: Cu-Z@M, Cu-Z@CM, LCu-Z@CM, TCu-Z@CM, or TLCu-Z@CM. After incubating for 12 h under 5% hypoxic conditions, cells were washed with PBS and stained using Calcein-AM/PI staining kits. Fluorescence microscopy was used to visualize cell viability. For JC-1 staining, cells were treated with the same drug formulations for 6 h, followed by staining with a JC-1 assay kit to evaluate mitochondrial membrane potential. Fluorescence microscopy was used to analyze the JC-1 staining patterns.

DCFH-DA fluorescence and flow cytometry

MC38 cells were seeded in a 6-well plate at a density of 5 × 105 cells per well in 2 mL DMEM. After the 45 °C water bath and subsequent drug treatments (PBS, Cu-Z@M, Cu-Z@CM, LCu-Z@CM, TCu-Z@CM, TLCu-Z@CM), cells were incubated under 5% hypoxic conditions for 6 h. After washing with PBS, cells were stained with DCFH-DA according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Flow cytometry was used to quantify ROS levels in the cells.

Cellular uptake and intracellular GSSG detection

MC38 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 5 × 105 cells per well in 2 mL of DMEM. The next day, cells were subjected to a 45 °C water bath for 10 min to simulate sublethal thermal injury. Subsequently, cells were treated with PBS or 20 µg/mL of various formulations (Cu-Z/IR780, Cu-Z@M/IR780, or Cu-Z@CM/IR780) and incubated under 5% hypoxic conditions for 6 h. Cells were then fixed, stained with DAPI, and imaged using confocal laser scanning microscopy to assess nanoparticle uptake.

For GSSG analysis, MC38 cells were treated under the same conditions, and intracellular oxidized glutathione (GSSG) levels were measured using a GSSG assay kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Transwell assay

For tumor cell migration, 30,000 tumor cells were seeded in the upper chamber of a transwell plate. The following day, cells were subjected to a 10-minute 45 °C water bath, followed by treatment with PBS or 20 µg/mL of the drug formulations. After 24 h, the cells were fixed and stained with crystal violet for imaging.

For macrophage recruitment, 50,000 RAW264.7 cells were seeded in the upper chamber, and MC38 cells were seeded in a 12-well plate. After overnight incubation, the tumor cells were treated according to the same protocol. The medium in the RAW264.7 wells was replaced with media containing 2% FBS, and both groups were co-cultured for 24 h. After fixation and crystal violet staining, images were captured for analysis.

Immunofluorescence staining

MC38 cells were cultured on confocal dishes, subjected to thermal injury, and treated with different drug formulations. After incubation under 5% hypoxic conditions, cells were stained with dsDNA, P-IRF3, or CRT antibodies, incubated overnight at 4 °C, washed, and stained with secondary antibodies. The samples were counterstained with DAPI and visualized using confocal microscopy.

Western blot analysis

Tumor cells or tissues were lysed in RIPA buffer, and protein content was quantified. Proteins were separated using SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were incubated with primary antibodies and then secondary antibodies. Chemiluminescence imaging was used to visualize protein expression, and ImageJ software was used for quantification.

Quantitative PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted using Trizol or Direct-zol™ RNA Kits, and cDNA was synthesized using the PrimeScript RT reagent Kit. qPCR was performed using SYBR® Premix Ex Taq™. The relative mRNA expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method, with β-actin as the internal reference gene.

ELISA analysis

For in vitro cytokine analysis (IFN-β, HMGB1, and cGAMP), MC38 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 5 × 105 cells per well in 2 mL of DMEM. After a 10-minute 45 °C water bath to simulate sublethal thermal injury, cells were treated with various formulations (PBS, Cu-Z@M, Cu-Z@CM, LCu-Z@CM, TCu-Z@CM, and TLCu-Z@CM) and incubated under 5% hypoxic conditions for 6 h. For in vivo analysis of IFN-β, tumor tissues from each group were harvested, mechanically dissociated, and suspended in PBS. After centrifugation, the supernatants were collected. Cytokine concentrations in both cell culture media and tissue supernatants were quantified using ELISA kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

ICD Induction, dendritic cell (DC) maturation and T cell activation

MC38 cells were cultured on confocal dishes, subjected to thermal injury, and treated with different drug formulations. After incubation under 5% hypoxic conditions, the cells were collected, and the expression of cell surface CRT, along with the release of ATP and HMGB1, was detected.

BMDCs were isolated from 6-week-old male C57BL/6J mice. The femurs and tibias were collected, and bone marrow cells were extracted by flushing with PBS, followed by red blood cell lysis. The remaining cells were washed twice with PBS and cultured in complete RPMI 1640 medium containing recombinant mouse GM-CSF (20 ng/ml) and IL-4 (10 ng/ml) for 6 days to generate immature DCs. On day 7, these immature DCs were co-cultured with MC38-OVA cells treated with various agents. After 48 h of co-culture, DCs were analyzed by flow cytometry.

CD8+ T cells were isolated from C57BL/6J mice using the EasyStep Mouse CD8+ T Cell Isolation Kit. The immature BMDCs were co-cultured with pre-treated tumor cells for 24 h, followed by the addition of CD8+ T cells. The co-culture was continued for another 48 h, and T cell activation was assessed by flow cytometry.

In vivo tumor models and treatment

In the MC38 tumor model, 1 × 106 MC38 cells were injected subcutaneously into the right flank of C57BL/6 mice. When the tumor volume reached approximately 450 mm³, incomplete ablation was induced by placing the radiofrequency ablation needle in a paracentral region of the tumor with the tip temperature set at 70 °C, followed by iRFA for 3 minutes [44]. For in vivo distribution analysis, Cu-Z@M/IR780 and Cu-Z@CM/IR780 were injected intravenously into the mice, and drug localization was monitored at various time points using IVIS imaging. To evaluate anti-tumor efficacy, the mice were divided into six groups, with six mice per group. Mice were injected intravenously with PBS, Cu-Z@M, Cu-Z@CM, LCu-Z@CM, TCu-Z@CM, or TLCu-Z@CM (20 mg/kg) every three days for a total of five treatments. Tumor volumes were measured with calipers, and body weight was recorded.

In the Hepa1-6 OVA tumor model, mice were treated with PBS, TLCu-Z@CM, PD-L1 mAb, or a combination of TLCu-Z@CM and PD-L1 mAb (10 mg/kg) every three days for a total of five treatments. Tumor volumes were measured with calipers, and body weight was monitored.

In the orthotopic liver cancer model, 5 × 10⁶ Hepa1-6 cells were injected into the livers of mice, and tumor growth was monitored using MRI. When the tumor volume reached approximately 450 mm³, incomplete ablation was performed on the liver. Mice were treated with PBS, TLCu-Z@CM, PD-L1 mAb, or a combination of TLCu-Z@CM and PD-L1 mAb (10 mg/kg) every three days for a total of four treatments. All the experiments are approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Medical School of Southeast University.

Flow cytometry

Following tumor sample collection on day 9 (Fig. S6A), the tissues were digested using collagenase and hyaluronidase. The digested cells were filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer and treated with red blood cell lysis buffer. For IFN-γ detection, cells were activated in a leukocyte activation solution for 4 h. As shown in Fig. S6B and S6C, cell viability was assessed using live/dead staining, and cells were incubated with Fc receptor-blocking solution for 10 min. Cell surface markers were stained first, followed by fixation and permeabilization, and intracellular markers (IFN-γ, FOXP3, and CD206) were stained afterward.

To analyze macrophages and DCs, the following antibodies were used for staining: L/D-APCCY7, CD45-bv510, CD11b-FITC, CD11c-PE, MHCII-percp-cy5.5, F4/80-BV421, CD86-PECY7, CD206-APC, and CD103-BV650. For NK and T cells, the following antibodies were used:

L/D-APC-CY7, CD45-RB705, CD3-FITC, CD4-PE-CY7, CD8-BV510, CD25-BV421, FOXP3- APC, IFN-γ-PE, and NK1.1-BV650. For T cell functionality, the following antibodies were used: L/D-APC-CY7, CD45-RB705, CD3-FITC, CD8-BV510, OVA257-264 (SIINFEKL) peptide bound to H-2Kb-BV650, CD44-BV605, CD62L-APC, and CD69-PE-CY7.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SD. Statistical differences between two groups were evaluated using a two-tailed t-test, while differences among multiple groups were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
