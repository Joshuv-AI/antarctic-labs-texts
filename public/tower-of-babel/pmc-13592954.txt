Estimated prevalence of osteoporosis in postmenopausal women in Saudi Arabia
Authors: Noor A. Hakim, Khulood S. Hussein
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Although osteoporosis is a major public health concern, limited and outdated research in Saudi Arabia highlights the need to investigate its current prevalence and risk factors among postmenopausal women in Jeddah. This study aimed to determine the prevalence of osteoporosis in postmenopausal women in Saudi Arabia and identify factors associated with bone mineral density (BMD). A total of 450 healthy postmenopausal women aged ≥50 years were randomly chosen to participate in this cross-sectional study. The study collected demographic information and performed dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry scans of the lumbar spine and femoral neck. The overall prevalence of osteoporosis and osteopenia was 12.2% and 42.9%, respectively. Women with osteoporosis were older, had been menopausal for longer, and had a lower body mass index (BMI) than those with osteopenia or normal BMD (P < .001). Compared with those in the other 2 groups, women with osteoporosis also had fewer deliveries and a shorter breastfeeding duration (P = .01 and P = .003, respectively). Multiple linear regression analysis revealed that age, BMI, and duration of lactation were independent predictors of lumbar spine BMD (P = .007, P = .001, and P = .01, respectively), whereas menopausal duration and BMI were independent predictors of femoral neck BMD (P = .008 and P = .001, respectively). Osteoporosis and osteopenia are prevalent among postmenopausal women in Saudi Arabia. Older age and longer menopausal duration were associated with lower BMD, whereas higher BMI and longer lactation duration were positively associated with BMD. Given the cross-sectional design, these associations should not be interpreted as causal. Early identification and appropriate assessment of postmenopausal women according to their clinical and demographic risk factors may help support osteoporosis prevention.

1. Introduction

Osteoporosis, a systemic bone disease involving reduced bone mass and degeneration of bone microarchitecture, is linked to increased bone fragility and the risk of fractures of the spine and hip.[1] Osteoporosis, along with its resultant fractures, is chiefly observed in postmenopausal women, although it can also occur in older men[1] and individuals with other comorbidities.[2] Osteoporosis-induced fractures lead to substantial morbidity, mortality, and financial burdens.[3] A bone mineral density (BMD) value of 2.5 standard deviations (SDs) or more below the mean of young healthy adults (T-score ≤ −2.5) is used to diagnose osteoporosis.[4] Although effective measures exist to prevent and treat osteoporosis, it is regarded as undertreated, particularly in older people and those with existing comorbidities.[2]

Among the major risk factors for osteoporosis, female sex and older age are particularly important.[1] Therefore, osteoporosis is typically referred to as a disorder affecting postmenopausal women.[1] The reduction in bone density in postmenopausal women stems mainly from the consequent reduction in estrogen levels in this population.[5] Premature osteoporosis is observed in women whose ovaries are removed before menopause and in those with premature ovarian failure.[6]

The body mass index (BMI) is another risk factor for osteoporosis. After menopause, estrogen is generated within adipose tissue, where it is converted from androgen, which may partly explain the association between higher adiposity and greater BMD in postmenopausal women.[7] In addition, dietary factors, especially low calcium and vitamin D consumption, play a role in the development of osteoporosis.[1] Some lifestyle factors, including physical inactivity, smoking, and alcohol consumption, are risk factors for this disease.[3] The prolonged use of glucocorticoids is also strongly linked to the loss of BMD, and individuals receiving long-term treatment with corticosteroids require monitoring for osteoporosis.[8]

Worldwide, approximately 1 in 3 women aged 50 years and older will experience an osteoporosis-related fragility fracture during their remaining lifetime.[9] As longevity increases, the burden of osteoporosis and fragility fractures is expected to increase.[1] The prevalence of osteoporosis is generally higher in Western countries than in the rest of the world. A recent study on fragility fractures conducted across 6 European countries (France, Germany, Italy, Spain, Sweden, and the UK) reported an increase in the anticipated number of people who will develop low BMD from 2.7 to 3.3 million in 2030.[10] Large prospective studies have indicated an increase in almost all types of fractures in individuals with a low BMD. Furthermore, individuals with a previous fracture have a substantially increased risk of subsequent fractures, with approximately 1.8-fold higher risks of subsequent clinical, osteoporotic, major osteoporotic, and hip fractures.[11]

Since people with osteoporosis usually do not present with clinical symptoms before a fracture occurs, fragility fractures are the chief clinical presentation. Osteoporotic fractures incur considerable financial and social burdens, both directly and indirectly. Hip fractures, the most severe complication of osteoporosis, result in the highest costs for hospitalization and long-term care, and are associated with significant reductions in quality of life, increased disability, and increased mortality rates.[10]

Given that osteoporosis is not usually diagnosed until a fragility fracture occurs, the main goal of osteoporosis treatment should be to prevent the first fragility fracture. Therefore, early detection and prompt treatment of osteoporosis are critical. The 2024 UK clinical guideline provides updated recommendations for the assessment and management of osteoporosis and the prevention of fragility fractures in postmenopausal women and men aged 50 years and older.[12] The 2023 Saudi Osteoporosis Society guidelines recommend osteoporosis screening for Saudi women and men aged 60 years and older, as well as for younger postmenopausal women with specific risk factors, including a history of fragility fracture, premature menopause, prolonged secondary amenorrhea, or long-term glucocorticoid therapy.[13] Although osteoporosis is a major public health concern, research on its prevalence in Saudi Arabia is limited, with most available prevalence estimates based on older, hospital-based studies and relatively little community-based data.

This study aimed to establish the prevalence of osteoporosis in postmenopausal Saudi women living in Jeddah, Saudi Arabia, and to identify the risk factors for low BMD in this population.

2. Material and methods

2.1. Participants and study design

This cross-sectional study targeted healthy postmenopausal Saudi women aged ≥50 years living in Jeddah, a city in the western region of Saudi Arabia, from January 2017 to December 2019. A separate previous study investigated the influence of vitamin D intake on BMD among Saudi women who were not taking vitamin D supplements.[14] Participants were recruited from primary health care centers (PHCCs) across Jeddah to obtain participants from different sectors of the city. A multistage sampling approach was employed. In the first stage, 1 PHCC was chosen from each of the 7 sectors of Jeddah. In the second stage, randomly selected patient files from these PHCCs were reviewed with a focus on women in the target age group. The number of files selected from the PHCC was proportional to the number of female patients registered at each center. In the third stage, women aged ≥50 years were contacted for potential participation based on the predefined inclusion criteria. The required sample size was calculated to be 384 participants, assuming a 50% prevalence, a 5% margin of error, and a 95% confidence level. An additional 15% was added to account for potential nonresponse or incomplete data, resulting in a target sample size of 450 participants. After multiple exclusion stages (Fig. 1), a final sample size of 450 was included in this study.

A total of 761 women were approached, and 450 eligible women were ultimately included in the study. Participants were excluded because of refusal to participate or according to their medical and medication history.

Ethical approval was obtained from the Human Ethics Research Committee of the King Fahad Medical Research Center at King Abdulaziz University, Jeddah (reference number 08/003, January 2017). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before their inclusion in the study. For illiterate participants, the study procedures and consent form were read aloud in the presence of a witness, and verbal informed consent was obtained and documented.

2.2. Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Healthy postmenopausal women aged ≥50 years who were free from orthopedic conditions were eligible for this study. Participants were classified as postmenopausal if they had experienced no menstruation for 12 consecutive months in the absence of other physiological/pathological factors or clinical intervention.

Women were excluded from the study if they had any of the following conditions: a prior diagnosis of osteoporosis, secondary causes of osteoporosis, or a history of osteoporosis treatment; were premenopausal or experienced menopause before the age of 40 years; had undergone hysterectomy or oophorectomy; had conditions that could lead to decreased BMD, such as rheumatoid arthritis, sickle cell anemia, any level of renal insufficiency, or disorders affecting the thyroid, parathyroid, or adrenal glands; had a history of conditions that increased fall risk, including dementia, Parkinson disease, visual impairment, and vertigo; had a history of malignancies; were on medications that might affect bone metabolism; were receiving hormone replacement therapy; or had unavailable requested data.

2.3. Data collection and variables

Data were collected over a period of 3 years. The participants’ age, marital status, level of education, and occupation were collected as demographic data. Lifestyle data, such as smoking status and level of physical activity, were also recorded. All questionnaire-based information was collected through interviews conducted personally by the researcher with each participant, and the responses were recorded during the interview. Participants were asked about the type of physical activity they practiced, the duration of activity per day or week, and the number of days per week they were physically active. Physical activity was classified as positive if the participant reported engaging in any moderate-intensity aerobic physical activity during leisure time for at least 30 minutes on 2 or more days per week. Anthropometric measurements, including weight and height, were obtained by the physician and documented in the medical records. The recorded variables included weight (kg) and height (cm). BMI was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared (kg/m2). In addition, reproductive data, including parity, lactation duration, and menopause duration, were recorded.

2.4. BMD measurement and interpretation

BMD measurements at the lumbar spine and femoral neck were obtained using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA) with a General Electric Lunar Prodigy Model (Lunar Corp.). All BMD scans were conducted by trained technicians who ran daily calibrations of the machine, following the manufacturer’s instructions. BMD measurements were interpreted according to the World Health Organization criteria: a T score of −2.5 or below (≤−2.5) indicated a diagnosis of osteoporosis, a T score between −1.0 and −2.5 indicated osteopenia, and a T score of −1.0 or above was considered normal.[4] In cases of discrepancy between lumbar BMD and femoral neck BMD, the lowest score was used to categorize participants into normal, osteopenia, or osteoporosis groups.

2.5. Efforts to address potential sources of bias

Several measures were implemented to minimize potential sources of bias. Strict inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied to reduce confounding from medical conditions, medications, or treatments known to influence bone metabolism. All BMD measurements were performed using standardized procedures and the same calibrated DEXA device to minimize measurement bias.

Data collection followed a standardized protocol, and anthropometric and clinical data were obtained from documented medical records to reduce reporting and recall bias. In addition, the use of objective measurements (DEXA-derived BMD values) minimized the risk of subjective assessment bias. Statistical analyses included multivariable regression models to control for potential confounders and identify independent predictors of BMD.

2.6. Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 20 (SPSS Inc.). The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to assess the distribution of continuous variables. Numerical parametric results are reported as mean ± SDs, while descriptive results are shown as percentages of the total sample. The chi-square test was used to compare the proportions between categorical variables, and the Kruskal–Wallis test was applied to continuous variables across different diagnostic categories (normal, osteopenia, and osteoporosis). Statistical significance was set at P ≤ .05. Missing data varied by variable. Among postmenopausal participants, lumbar spine BMD and T-score were missing for 6 participants (1.3%), while femoral neck BMD and T-score were missing for 4 participants (0.9%). Serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D (25(OH)D) measurements were unavailable for 94 participants (20.9%). For analyses involving a specific variable, participants with missing values for that variable were excluded from the respective analysis. No imputation was performed. Multiple linear regression analyses were performed to identify independent factors associated with lumbar spine and femoral neck BMD. Relevant demographic, anthropometric, reproductive, and lifestyle variables were considered in the multivariable analyses based on their potential biological relevance to BMD. Regression assumptions were assessed by examining residual-versus-predicted-value plots for linearity and homoscedasticity, histograms and normal P–P plots for the distribution of residuals, variance inflation factor and tolerance values for multicollinearity, and Cook distance, leverage, and standardized residuals for potentially influential observations. Results were reported as regression coefficients, 95% confidence intervals, and P values.

3. Results

The general characteristics of the study participants are presented in Table 1. A total of 450 women met the inclusion criteria and were included in this study. Of the 450 participants, complete data were available for the core demographic, anthropometric, reproductive, and lifestyle variables. The age of the participants ranged from 50 to 62 years, with a mean of 52.1 (SD = 2.3) years. The majority of women (83.4%) were aged between 50 and 55 years. The mean body weight of the participants was 74.8 kg (SD = 13.9), and the mean BMI was 31.5 (SD = 5.7) kg/m2. Almost one-third (30.1%) of the participants were overweight, and 38.5% were obese. The mean parity was 6.51 (SD = 3.1), and the mean duration of lactation was 11.35 (SD = 7.1) months. Among the 450 study participants, 33 (7.3%) were smokers, and 71 (15.8%) were physically active (Table 1). Sun exposure was reported by 112 participants (24.9%), while 23 (5.1%) reported sunscreen use, and 108 (24.1%) reported vitamin D supplementation. The mean serum 25(OH)D concentration was 35.01 ± 22.08 nmol/L, and the mean serum calcium concentration was 2.54 ± 0.21 mmol/L (Table 1).

4. Discussion

In this multicenter cross-sectional study of healthy postmenopausal Saudi women, osteoporosis was identified in 9.1% and 4.2% of participants based on lumbar spine and femoral neck BMD, respectively, while osteopenia was present in 43.6% and 42.2%, respectively. Older age and longer duration of menopause were associated with lower BMD, whereas higher BMI was associated with higher BMD. Longer lactation duration was positively associated with lumbar spine BMD, although this finding should be interpreted cautiously. These findings provide information on the prevalence of low BMD and associated factors among relatively young, healthy postmenopausal women in Jeddah, Saudi Arabia.

The prevalence estimates in our study were lower than those reported in some other Saudi studies and varied considerably according to the study population, age distribution, and clinical setting. A recent study conducted among women attending a DEXA scanning center in Buraidah reported osteoporosis in 10% of participants and osteopenia in 42%.[15] A large retrospective study of Saudi postmenopausal women aged ≥60 years in Riyadh reported osteoporosis in 24.7% and osteopenia in 58.8% of participants based on BMD T-scores.[16] A recent hospital-based study in Al-Khobar reported osteoporosis in 58.4% of postmenopausal women.[17] The higher prevalence observed in the Al-Khobar study may partly reflect differences in study setting and participant characteristics, as that study was hospital-based, whereas the present study specifically included apparently healthy postmenopausal women. Differences in age distribution and the inclusion or exclusion of women with comorbidities or previously diagnosed osteoporosis may also contribute to the variation in prevalence estimates across Saudi studies.

Only 5 women in our study had osteoporosis at both the lumbar spine and femoral neck. This relatively low number may partly reflect the study population, which consisted of relatively young, healthy postmenopausal women, together with the exclusion of women with previously diagnosed osteoporosis and conditions or medications known to affect bone metabolism. These selection criteria may have reduced the number of women with osteoporosis in our sample and may partly explain the relatively low prevalence of osteoporosis at both skeletal sites.

The prevalence of osteoporosis varies across regions. A recent multicenter study in Bahrain reported osteoporosis in 22.3% of postmenopausal women.[18] A national cross-sectional study in Jordan reported a prevalence of 19.8% among postmenopausal women aged ≥50 years.[19] In Turkey, a cross-sectional study of 1148 postmenopausal women reported osteoporosis in 20.5% of participants.[20] A large national study in China reported osteoporosis in 32.1% of postmenopausal women.[21] The differences in these results may be due to variations in sample size, participant selection criteria, study design, and diagnostic methods. Participant-related factors, including age, genetics, nutrition, and physical activity, may also contribute to differences in osteoporosis prevalence.

The high prevalence of osteopenia in our sample is also noteworthy. Osteopenia was identified in 43.6% and 42.2% of women based on lumbar spine and femoral neck BMD, respectively. These findings are comparable to recent data from Bahrain, where 42.4% of postmenopausal women had osteopenia.[18] Differences in age distribution, study setting, participant characteristics, and the skeletal sites used for classification may contribute to variations in prevalence estimates across populations.

The progression of osteopenia to osteoporosis also varies according to baseline BMD and other characteristics. A recent longitudinal study of older Asian adults found that, among women, transition rates to osteoporosis over 2 to 5 years were 7.8%, 29.9%, and 45.9% among those with mild, moderate, and severe osteopenia, respectively.[22] These findings highlight the heterogeneity of progression among individuals with osteopenia and suggest that the risk of progression varies according to the severity of baseline bone loss. These findings also highlight the importance of identifying women at increased risk of low BMD and support consideration of appropriate osteoporosis assessment in accordance with established clinical guidelines.

In this study, we found significant associations between osteoporosis and several variables, including age, BMI, parity, duration of lactation, and duration of menopause. However, no significant association was found between lifestyle factors, such as smoking and physical activity, and osteoporosis.

The inverse association we found between age and BMD is consistent with the established effects of aging on bone remodeling, including increased bone resorption and reduced bone formation.[23] The regression analysis conducted in our study revealed that older age was negatively associated with lumbar spine BMD. With the wealth of empirical data pointing to a negative association between age and BMD, it is clear that measures for early detection and prevention are essential to address women’s bone health as they age. However, the regression models explained 24.5% and 27.5% of the variability in lumbar spine and femoral neck BMD, respectively, indicating that a substantial proportion of BMD variability remained unexplained and may reflect other determinants that were not included in the models.

The association between longer menopausal duration and lower BMD in our study is in line with previous research showing progressive bone loss with increasing time since menopause.[24,25] Menopausal duration has also been found to be a significant independent predictor of osteoporosis, regardless of whether an individual underwent hormonal therapy or took calcium and vitamin D supplements.[24] The association between menopausal duration and osteoporosis has also been reported among Arab women, including postmenopausal women in Jordan.[25] In our study, longer menopausal duration was significantly associated with lower femoral neck BMD and remained an independent predictor in the multiple linear regression analysis. This finding is consistent with the progressive nature of postmenopausal bone loss and highlights the importance of considering years since menopause when assessing osteoporosis risk.

Our results also indicated that overweight and obesity were associated with a lower risk of osteoporosis. This finding was consistent with the results of several studies.[26,27] This association may be explained by the role of adipose tissue in the conversion of androgens to estrogen and greater mechanical loading on bones.[28] A recent systematic review and meta-analysis involving postmenopausal women reported a positive association between BMI and BMD at several skeletal sites, including the lumbar spine and femoral neck.[26] Skrzek et al reported the lowest risk of osteoporosis in postmenopausal women with a BMI in the 26 to 27.9 kg/m2 range.[29]

The results of the present study showed a positive association between longer lactation duration and lumbar spine BMD; however, this finding should be interpreted cautiously. Women in the osteoporosis group had a significantly shorter duration of lactation than participants in the other 2 groups (P = .003). Regression analysis also showed a significant positive association between longer lactation duration and lumbar spine BMD in our study population. However, this association should not be interpreted as a causal relationship, particularly given the cross-sectional design and the possibility of residual confounding by factors related to reproductive history, nutrition, and lifestyle. The association between postmenopausal osteoporosis and total duration of lactation is complex, and the results of studies in different cultures have varied. In a study of healthy Saudi women, Ghannam et al found no significant association between total duration of lactation and lumbar spine BMD.[30] In Korea, longer breastfeeding duration was associated with lower BMD among postmenopausal women.[31] In contrast, a Canadian study found no significant differences in baseline BMD according to breastfeeding history, although some differences in the rate of bone loss were observed during follow-up.[32] A study of postmenopausal women in Turkey also reported an association between longer total breastfeeding duration and lower BMD.[33] A systematic review and meta-analysis found that BMD decreases during pregnancy and lactation and generally recovers after weaning, although the evidence regarding the effect of lactation duration remains inconclusive.[34] One possible explanation for this finding may be related to differences in dietary and lifestyle patterns among the women in our study. The participants belonged to an older generation in which prolonged breastfeeding was common, and dietary patterns may have differed from those of younger generations. However, because dietary intake during the breastfeeding period was not specifically examined in relation to lactation duration in the present analysis, this explanation remains speculative.

The relatively narrow age distribution of the study population should be considered when interpreting the findings. Although the study targeted healthy postmenopausal women aged 50 years and older, most participants were relatively young, and women older than 62 years were not represented. Therefore, the findings may not be generalizable to older postmenopausal women, who have a higher risk of osteoporosis and fragility fractures.

The strengths of the present study include its relatively large sample size, community-based setting, and objective assessment of BMD using DEXA. However, the exclusion of women with conditions or medications known to affect BMD, including those with orthopedic complaints or previous osteoporosis treatment, may have introduced selection bias and resulted in an underestimation of osteoporosis prevalence compared with the broader postmenopausal population. Therefore, the prevalence observed in this study should be interpreted within the context of the specific eligibility criteria and should not be considered representative of all postmenopausal women in Saudi Arabia.

A broad range of demographic, anthropometric, reproductive, and lifestyle factors were assessed and considered in the multivariable analyses based on their potential biological relevance to BMD. Nevertheless, residual confounding cannot be completely excluded because some potentially relevant factors, including dietary calcium and protein intake, family history of osteoporosis, previous fragility fractures, socioeconomic factors, glucocorticoid exposure, and chronic diseases affecting bone metabolism, were not comprehensively evaluated. Serum 25(OH)D, serum calcium, sun exposure, and vitamin D supplementation were assessed and are presented in Table 1. Physical activity was based on a simple self-reported classification rather than a validated questionnaire; therefore, some exposure misclassification cannot be excluded because resistance exercise, occupational activity, and lifetime physical activity were not specifically assessed.

Additional analyses stratified by age, BMI, smoking, physical activity, and duration of menopause may provide further insight into potential differences in osteoporosis risk across subgroups. However, such analyses were beyond the scope of the present study and should be explored in future studies with appropriate sample sizes. Future studies involving larger and more diverse populations are also needed to determine whether the observed associations differ across clinically relevant subgroups.

5. Conclusions

Osteoporosis and osteopenia are prevalent among postmenopausal Saudi Arabian women. Awareness of the factors that may lead to this disease is crucial for the prevention of complications, particularly fragility fractures. Raising community awareness about the risk factors for osteoporosis, especially those that are modifiable, may help reduce the financial and social burden of the disease.

Given the high prevalence of osteopenia in nearly half of our study population, early identification and appropriate assessment of postmenopausal women according to their clinical and demographic risk factors may help identify those at increased risk of progression and complications.

Further studies with larger sample sizes would be useful for determining changes in the prevalence of osteoporosis and osteopenia over time. Longitudinal studies could also help determine whether these prevalence rates are influenced by increased awareness, preventive strategies, and advances in diagnostic and treatment methods.
