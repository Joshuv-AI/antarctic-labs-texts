The anticoagulant effects of milvexian, a novel small molecule factor XIa inhibitor, are neutralized by activated prothrombin complex concentrates and recombinant factor VIIa in human plasma and whole blood in vitro
Authors: Matthew Bunce, Zheng Huang Devine, Madhu Chintala
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe development of anticoagulants that provide antithrombotic efficacy without a concomitant bleeding risk remains an unmet clinical need in thrombosis. Although direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) have a reduced incidence of major bleeding compared with warfarin, they still carry a bleeding risk, resulting in a suboptimal therapeutic index. Epidemiologic data suggest that inhibiting activated factor XI (FXIa) may offer an improved safety profile with respect to bleeding risk compared with current-generation DOACs. Additionally, a phase II trial of milvexian in patients undergoing elective total knee replacement demonstrated robust dose-dependent efficacy with no statistically significant increase in bleeding. Nevertheless, the ability to rapidly and effectively correct FXIa inhibitor–induced anticoagulation may still be important in situations where patients experience uncontrolled bleeding or require emergency surgery.ObjectivesWe assessed the ability to normalize the anticoagulant effects of the novel small-molecule FXIa inhibitor milvexian (BMS-986177/JNJ-70033093) in vitro using commercially available prohemostatic agents.MethodsMilvexian-associated anticoagulation and correction was evaluated in activated partial thromboplastin time clotting assays, thromboelastography, and kaolin-initiated thrombin generation assays.ResultsActivated prothrombin complex concentrates (PCCs) and recombinant factor (rF)VIIa corrected the anticoagulant effects of milvexian in activated partial thromboplastin time clotting assays, thromboelastography, and kaolin-initiated thrombin generation assays. In contrast, other agents, including PCCs, rFIX, and rFVIII, demonstrated either modest or no correction of milvexian-associated anticoagulation.ConclusionThis study demonstrated that currently available activated PCCs and rFVIIa normalize the anticoagulation induced by milvexian in vitro. The clinical utility of these agents remains to be established.Keywords: anticoagulant, antithrombotic, FXIa, milvexian, thrombosis

Introduction

Over the past decade, direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) have overtaken vitamin K antagonists (VKAs) as the standard of care for preventing thrombosis [1,2]. The primary improvement of DOACs over VKAs is their reduced incidence of major bleeding, including a reduced rate of fatal bleeding and lower case-fatality rate [[3], [4], [5]]. Additional benefits include standardized dose regimens, no need for regular international normalized ratio monitoring, and lower risk for food or drug–drug interactions. Yet despite their overall improved bleeding profile, DOACs still incur a 3% to 4% rate of major bleeding and a case-fatality rate of 7.6% [6]. Although both specific and nonspecific reversal strategies have been developed for DOACs, a substantial number of patients remain either untreated or undertreated due to concerns over bleeding risk [7].

Due in part to these limitations with current-generation DOACs, there is a continuing need to develop new antithrombotic drugs with an improved benefit/risk profile. The contact activation pathway protease activated factor XI (FXIa) is currently a target of interest. Epidemiologic studies have demonstrated a direct correlation between FXI levels and risk of thrombosis [[8], [9], [10]], suggesting that FXIa inhibition could be an effective approach for thromboprophylaxis. Furthermore, FXI deficiency is associated with a heterogeneous but typically mild bleeding diathesis that manifests primarily in provoked situations [11]. Together these observations suggest that inhibiting FXIa may offer a substantially wider therapeutic index than current-generation DOACs, with respect to bleeding risk.

Milvexian (BMS-986177/JNJ-70033093) is a novel small-molecule FXIa inhibitor currently in phase III clinical trials. Based on FXI epidemiologic data, milvexian offers the potential for reduced bleeding risk compared with currently approved DOACs. Additionally, a phase II trial of milvexian in patients undergoing elective total knee replacement demonstrated robust antithrombotic efficacy with no statistically significant increase in bleeding [12]. Nevertheless, in situations where anticoagulated patients experience uncontrolled bleeding or require emergency surgery, there may be a need for rapid correction of anticoagulation. It is therefore prudent to establish reversal strategies that will be readily available should a patient require rapid correction of their anticoagulation. Prior to the development of specific reversal agents (RAs) for direct thrombin and FXa inhibitors, reversal strategies relied on procoagulant agents which provide either a specific clotting factor or a combination of several clotting factors, depending on the agent. These include fresh frozen plasma, prothrombin complex concentrates (PCCs), activated prothrombin complex concentrates (aPCCs), recombinant factor (rF)VIIa, and antifibrinolytic agents [13,14]. Importantly, many of these agents stimulate thrombin generation downstream of FXIa in the coagulation cascade, suggesting that they could effectively bypass FXIa inhibition by milvexian in order to restore thrombin generation and hemostasis. In the current study, we therefore tested a panel of commercially available procoagulant agents to assess correction of milvexian-induced anticoagulation in activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT) clotting assays, thrombin generation assays (TGAs), and thromboelastography (TEG).

Materials and Methods

Materials

Citrated normal human platelet-poor plasma was purchased from George King Bio-medical, Inc. Citrated kaolin (CK) Prest aPTT reagent, 25 mM CaCl2, Start4 accessories, FluCa reagent, platelet-poor plasma reagents, and TGA calibrator were purchased from Diagnostica Stago. TEG cups, kaolin vials, 0.2 M CaCl2, and TEG biologic controls were purchased from Haemonetics Corporation. Procoagulant agents (PCCs [FII, FVII, FIX, and FX, as well as antithrombotic proteins C and S (Kcentra) and FIX (Profilnine)], rFIX [Benefix], aPCC [FII, FVII, FIX, FX: zymogen + activated factors (FEIBA)], rFVIIa [NovoSeven], and rFVIII [NovoEight]) were purchased from Myoderm. Dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO), phosphate-buffered saline, Hepes, and NaCl were purchased from Sigma Aldrich. Milvexian used in these studies was synthetized by our codevelopment partner Bristol Myers Squibb. The concentrations of milvexian used in this study span the peak concentrations in our phase I dose-ranging clinical trials in healthy volunteers. The concentrations of the procoagulant agents were based on the recommended doses in their respective labels and expected plasma exposure.

aPTT clotting assays

aPTT assays were performed on a Start4 coagulation analyzer (Diagnostica Stago) with platelet-poor plasma from healthy donors (n = 4-6). Milvexian or DMSO (vehicle control) was added to plasma at 0.5% (v/v) and incubated for 10 minutes at 25 °C. The indicated prohemostatic agent or hydrostatically balanced system (HBS) vehicle controls were added at a final ratio of 1:24 (agent:platelet-poor plasma) and incubated for another 5 minutes at 25 °C; 50 μL of plasma was added in triplicate to clotting cuvettes containing 50 μL CK Prest and incubated for 3 minutes at 37 °C. Further, 50 μL of 25 mM CaCl2 was added to each cuvette, and clotting times were measured by the analyzer. Average clotting times from the triplicate measurements are reported as ratios relative to untreated vehicle control.

Thromboelastography

CK TEG was performed using TEG-5000 hemostasis analyzers (Haemonetics Corporation). Blood from healthy volunteers (n = 4-6) was collected into sodium citrate Vacutainer tubes (BD Biosciences) following informed consent. Measurements were performed within 4 hours of collection. Milvexian or DMSO was added to the citrated blood at a final dilution of 0.5% (v/v) and incubated for 10 minutes at 25 °C. The indicated RAs or HBS vehicle controls were added at a final ratio of 1:24 (RA:blood) and incubated for another 5 minutes at 25 °C. Samples (1 mL) were transferred into kaolin vials and mixed, and 340 μL was pipetted into TEG cups containing 20 μL of 0.2 M CaCl2. Sample measurements were started immediately following the CK TEG protocol and continued until maximum amplitude was established.

Thrombin generation assays

Kaolin-initiated TGAs were performed using a modification of the Thrombinoscope TGA protocol (Stago). Platelet-poor plasma was prepared with the indicated concentrations of milvexian, RAs, or vehicle controls similarly to samples described for aPTT clotting assays. Kaolin-initiating reagent was prepared by diluting the CK Prest Reagent 1 kaolin slurry 1:10 000 and rehydrated cephalin 1:20 into HBS (20 mM Hepes, 150 mM NaCl, pH 7.4). Plasma samples were dispensed in triplicate into clear round-bottom 96-well plates containing 20 μL of kaolin-initiating reagent. Samples were incubated for 10 minutes at 37 °C in a Fluoroskan Ascent plate reader (Thermo), and 20 μL of FluCa reagent per well was injected to start the reaction. Samples were measured every minute for 90 minutes with 360-nm/460-nm excitation/emission wavelengths. Results were calculated by the Thrombinoscope software and exported to Excel. Samples for each donor were normalized to the vehicle control, and results are reported as mean ± SEM for 4 to 6 donors.

Ethics approval

Blood was collected from healthy human volunteers on site in Spring House, Pennsylvania, by the nursing staff at the health center under an approved blood-collection protocol for investigational use.

Results

In vitro reversal in aPTT clotting assays

Milvexian spiked into the plasma from healthy donors produced a dose-dependent prolongation of aPTT clotting time (Figure 1). Adding 2 U/mL aPCC reversed the aPTT prolongation, reducing the clotting time from 4.4-fold to 1.4-fold above baseline at 10 μM milvexian (Figure 1A). rFVIIa was first tested at 2 μg/mL to approximate plasma exposure following an infusion of 90 μg/kg. At this dose, rFVIIa partially reversed the prolonged aPTT to 2.1-fold above baseline (Figure 1B). Increasing rFVIIa to 6 μg/mL further reduced the aPTT to 1.6-fold above baseline. Other agents demonstrated less ability to correct milvexian-associated aPTT prolongation: the PCCs and rFIX had no effect, while rFVIII produced a modest correction in the aPTT (Figure 2).

In vitro reversal in TEG

Milvexian induced a dose-dependent effect on the primary TEG end points: lag time (R) and clot time (k) prolonged, while clot angle (α) and maximum amplitude (MA) reduced (Figure 3). Both aPCC and rFVIIa fully corrected the effects of milvexian in CK TEG assays. Consistent with the results in aPTT assays, limited or no correction was observed with other agents, including PCCs, rFIX, or rFVIII, in CK TEG (Figure 4).

In vitro reversal in thrombin generation assays

Milvexian produced a dose-dependent prolongation of lag time with concomitant reductions in peak thrombin and endogenous thrombin potential (ETP) (Figure 5). aPCC fully corrected the changes in all 3 primary TGA end points, while rFVIIa corrected lag time and ETP but not peak thrombin. Of the other agents tested, only rFVIII had a demonstrable effect, fully correcting lag time and partially correcting peak thrombin (Figure 6).

Discussion

Current interest in FXIa as a target for next-generation anticoagulants is predicated on the hypothesis that epidemiologic data will translate to efficacy in preventing thrombosis with a lower risk of bleeding than current-generation DOACs (ie, an improved benefit/risk profile). Yet, even if clinical experience confirms this hypothesis, there may still be rare scenarios of bleeding or emergency invasive procedures for which the rapid and sustained reversal of the anticoagulant effects of FXIa inhibition is required. In this study, we evaluated the ability of several commercially available procoagulant agents to correct the anticoagulant effects of milvexian in vitro. The assays chosen were those that were sensitive to the pharmacology of milvexian as the goal was to assess the impact of RAs on pharmacology. We acknowledge that additional assays (eg, tissue factor–mediated thrombin generation) that evaluate the extrinsic pathway and hemostasis would have been beneficial but were not evaluated in the present study. In pilot studies using tissue factor (5 pM), we observed only modest effects on the inhibition of thrombin generation with milvexian, even at very high concentrations. Due to this modest effect on inhibition of tissue factor–mediated thrombin generation, we did not pursue experiments to assess reversal by milvexian. While thrombin generation with 5 pM tissue factor is sensitive to deficiencies in the extrinsic and common pathway, it may not be sensitive to deficiencies of FVIII, FIX, or FXI. Thus, lower tissue factor concentrations (≤1 pM), a pathway considered critical for hemostasis, may be required for FXI-sensitive thrombin generation, but was not assessed in the present study.

Based on the position of FXIa upstream in the intrinsic pathway of the clotting cascade, we anticipated that PCCs and aPCC would effectively normalize milvexian-induced anticoagulation. aPCC consistently demonstrated efficacy in our panel of in vitro assays. In contrast, PCC (Kcentra) failed to reverse milvexian-associated anticoagulation in any of our in vitro assays. We hypothesize that this difference between the in vitro effects aPCC and PCC (Kcentra) is attributable to trace amounts of activated clotting factors, including FVIIa, in aPCC that confer increased prohemostatic potency compared with PCCs, as well as paradoxically to the trace amounts of heparin and antithrombin III found in the formulation of PCC (Kcentra). Although PCCs have successfully corrected DOAC-associated bleeding in clinical reports, they have largely failed to reverse the anticoagulant effects of DOACs in traditional in vitro assays [15,16]. A lack of sensitivity of traditional coagulation assays to DOACs has further confounded attempts to evaluate their reversal in vitro [17]. Since milvexian produces a clear dose response in aPTT assays, TEG, and kaolin-initiated TGA, we speculated that PCCs might produce an observable reversal of milvexian in these assays. Nevertheless, we observed no corrective effect with PCC (Kcentra), despite confirming its procoagulant activity in warfarin (Coumadin) plasma (data not shown). This lack of in vitro effect was also observed with a second PCC (Profilnine). These results are in line with in vitro studies using direct FXa inhibitors and further support the conclusion that in vitro assays may not reliably predict in vivo efficacy for PCCs. Interestingly, although PCCs tended to show partial to complete correction of ETP in the TGA [16], we saw no sign of correction of ETP in our assays. This discrepancy could potentially be attributed to the different enzymes being inhibited (FXa vs FXIa) or to differences in the TGA protocols (eg, initiating with tissue factor vs kaolin).

Limited preclinical studies with rFVIIa have demonstrated correction of bleeding time in animal models [13], and rFVIIa has been successfully used to manage surgical bleeding in FXI-deficient patients with inhibitory antibodies [[18], [19], [20], [21]]. These results suggest that rFVIIa may also be effective as a reversal strategy for prophylactic FXIa inhibitors like milvexian. Accordingly, we observed reversal of milvexian with rFVIIa in our panel of in vitro assays. Complete correction required 6 μg/mL of rFVIIa, which corresponds to a dose that is approximately 3 times the standard clinical dose of 90 μg/kg. High-dose rFVIIa has been associated with increased risk of arterial thrombosis [22]; therefore, to better define the efficacious reversal dose of rFVIIa and understand the potential benefit/risk ratio of its use, further studies to evaluate FXIa inhibitor reversal with rFVIIa may be warranted in in vivo models in animals and in humans. Additionally, it is also likely that full reversal of the anticoagulation of milvexian in these assays may not be required to restore hemostasis and stop bleeding as these RAs participate in and activate clotting downstream to FXIa inhibition by milvexian. For instance, rFVIIa at 20 to 30 μg/kg is typically used to stop bleeding in FXI-deficient patients.

In summary, our study provides insight into the potential application of nonspecific reversal strategies for the emerging field of direct FXIa inhibitors. This in vitro study demonstrates the ability to correct anticoagulation by milvexian with aPCC and rFVIIa in vitro. Although PCCs failed to correct anticoagulation in vitro, preclinical and clinical data demonstrating correction of direct FXa inhibitor–associated bleeding supports further evaluation of PCCs as a milvexian reversal strategy. Additionally, the assays used in this study do not fully elucidate the impact of these RAs on the extrinsic pathway, which is considered critical for hemostasis; this needs to be explored further.
