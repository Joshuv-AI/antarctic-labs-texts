Regularity estimates to quasi-linear parabolic equations in non-divergence form with non-homogeneous signature
by, João Vitor da Silva, & \&, Ginaldo de Santana Sá

Abstract
In this manuscript, we establish the existence and sharp geometric regularity estimates for bounded solutions of a class of quasilinear parabolic equations in non-divergence form with non-homogeneous degeneracy. The model equation in this class is given by ∂ t u = ( | ∇ u | 𝔭 + 𝔞 ( x , t ) | ∇ u | 𝔮 ) Δ p N u + f ( x , t ) in Q 1 = B 1 × ( − 1 , 0 ] , \partial_{t}u=\left(|\nabla u|^{\mathfrak{p}}+\mathfrak{a}(x,t)|\nabla u|^{\mathfrak{q}}\right)\Delta_{p}^{\mathrm{N}}u+f(x,t)\quad\text{in}\quad Q_{1}=B_{1}\times(-1,0], where p ∈ ( 1 , ∞ ) p\in(1,\infty) , 𝔭 , 𝔮 ∈ [ 0 , ∞ ) \mathfrak{p},\mathfrak{q}\in[0,\infty) , and 𝔞 , f : Q 1 → ℝ \mathfrak{a},f:Q_{1}\to\mathbb{R} are suitably defined functions. Our approach is based on geometric tangential methods, incorporating a refined oscillation mechanism, compactness arguments, “alternative methods,” and scaling techniques. Furthermore, we derive pointwise estimates in settings exhibiting singular-degenerate or doubly singular signatures. To some extent, our regularity estimates refine and extend previous results from [ 14 ] through distinct methodological advancements. Finally, we explore connections between our findings and fundamental nonlinear models in the theory of quasilinear PDEs, which may be of independent interest. Keywords : Regularity estimates, Quasi-linear parabolic PDEs, Doubly degenerate models. AMS Subject Classification: Primary 35K65; 35B65; 35K92; Secondary 35D40

Introduction

In this work, we will establish geometric regularity estimates for bounded viscosity solutions of certain quasi-linear parabolic equations with non-homogeneous degeneracy as follows

(1.1)

\partial_{t}u(x,t)=\mathscr{H}(x,t,\nabla u)\Delta_{p}^{\mathrm{N}}u(x,t)+f(x,t)\quad\text{in}\quad Q_{1}=B_{1}\times(-1,0],

where
p\in(1,\infty)
and
u\mapsto\Delta_{p}^{\mathrm{N}}u
denotes the normalized

-Laplace operator defined by

\Delta_{p}^{\mathrm{N}}u(x,t)=\Delta u+(p-2)\left\langle D^{2}u\frac{\nabla u}{|\nabla u|},\frac{\nabla u}{|\nabla u|}\right\rangle.

The vector field
\mathscr{H}:Q_{1}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is defined by

\mathscr{H}(x,t,\xi)=|\xi|^{\mathfrak{p}}+\mathfrak{a}(x,t)|\xi|^{\mathfrak{q}},\quad\text{for}\quad(x,t,\xi)\in Q_{1}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}

where

(H1)

0\leq\mathfrak{p}\leq\mathfrak{q}<\infty
;

and the modulating coefficient
(x,t)\mapsto\mathfrak{a}(x,t)
satisfies:

(H2)

\displaystyle 0<\mathfrak{a}^{-}:=\inf_{Q_{1}}\mathfrak{a}(x,t)\leq\mathfrak{a}(x,t)\leq\mathfrak{a}^{+}:=\sup_{Q_{1}}\mathfrak{a}(x,t)<\infty
;

(H3)

\mathfrak{a}\in C^{1}_{x}(Q_{1})\cap C^{1}_{t}(Q_{1})\quad\text{and}\quad\mathfrak{A}_{0}:=\|D_{x,t}\mathfrak{a}\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{1})}<\infty
;

Moreover, the source term
f:Q_{1}\to\mathbb{R}
fulfills

(H4)

f\in L^{\infty}(Q_{1})\cap C^{0}(Q_{1})
.

State-of-the-art and mathematical motivations

1.1.1
Game theoretic methods to
p
-parabolic PDEs: A motivational study

In the past two decades, a connection between stochastic Tug-of-War Games and nonlinear partial differential equations of
p
-Laplacian type, initiated by the pioneering papers of Peres
et al.

[
26
]
and Peres–Sheffield
[
27
]
, has garnered significant attention and provided new trends in the area of game theoretical methods in PDE. Particularly, in the parabolic setting, Manfredi
et al.

[
23
]
demonstrated that the viscosity solutions to

(1.3)

(n+p)\partial_{t}u(x,t)=\Delta_{p}^{\mathrm{N}}u(x,t)

can be obtained as the limits of value functions for Tug-of-War Games with noise as the parameter controlling the step size approaches zero. Precisely, these are distinguished by the asymptotic mean value formula:

u(x,t)=\frac{\alpha}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{t-\varepsilon^{2}}^{t}\left(\max_{y\in\overline{B_{\varepsilon}(x)}}u(y,s)+\min_{y\in\overline{B_{\varepsilon}(x)}}u(y,s)\right)ds+\frac{\beta}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{t-\varepsilon^{2}}^{t}\mathchoice{\mathop{\vrule width=6.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.5pt\kern-8.0pt\intop}\nolimits_{\kern-6.0ptB_{\varepsilon}(x)}}{\mathop{\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.6pt\kern-6.0pt\intop}\nolimits_{B_{\varepsilon}(x)}}{\mathop{\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.6pt\kern-6.0pt\intop}\nolimits_{B_{\varepsilon}(x)}}{\mathop{\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.6pt\kern-6.0pt\intop}\nolimits_{B_{\varepsilon}(x)}}u(y,s)\,dy\,ds+\text{o}(\varepsilon^{2}),

as
\varepsilon\to 0
, which should hold in the viscosity sense, where
\alpha
and
\beta
are defined by

\alpha=\frac{p-2}{p+n}\quad\text{and}\quad\beta=\frac{2+n}{p+n}.

Additionally, such mean value formulas are associated with the Dynamic Programming Principle (DPP for short), which is fulfilled by the value functions of parabolic tug-of-war games with noise. By way of clarification, the DPP corresponds exactly to the mean value formula without the correction term
o(\varepsilon^{2})
.

Alternatively, Parviainen in
[
24
]
also proposes that the game values
u_{\varepsilon}
to (
1.3

g
. Specifically, we have:

(1.4)

u_{\varepsilon}(x,t)=\frac{\alpha}{2}\left\{\sup_{y\in B_{\varepsilon}(x)}u_{\varepsilon}\left(y,t-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\right)+\inf_{y\in B_{\varepsilon}(x)}u_{\varepsilon}\left(y,t-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\right)\right\}+\beta\mathchoice{\mathop{\vrule width=6.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.5pt\kern-8.0pt\intop}\nolimits_{\kern-6.0ptB_{\varepsilon}(x)}}{\mathop{\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.6pt\kern-6.0pt\intop}\nolimits_{B_{\varepsilon}(x)}}{\mathop{\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.6pt\kern-6.0pt\intop}\nolimits_{B_{\varepsilon}(x)}}{\mathop{\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.6pt\kern-6.0pt\intop}\nolimits_{B_{\varepsilon}(x)}}u_{\varepsilon}\left(y,t-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\right)\,dy,

for every
(x,t)\in\Omega\times(0,\infty)

u_{\varepsilon}(x,t)=g(x,t)
, for every
(x,t)\in\Gamma_{\varepsilon}^{\text{par}}
, which denotes the parabolic boundary strip given by

\Gamma_{\varepsilon}^{\text{par}}=\left(\Gamma_{\varepsilon}\times\left(-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2},\infty\right)\right)\cup\left(\Omega\times\left(-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2},0\right]\right),

where

\Gamma_{\varepsilon}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega:\operatorname{dist}(x,\Omega)\leq\varepsilon\}.

Precisely, Parviainen addresses the following convergence of value functions to a Normalized
p
-parabolic problem:

Theorem 1.4

(
[
24

).

Let
\Omega
be a smooth domain,
g:\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus\Omega_{\mathrm{T}}\to\mathbb{R}

p
-parabolic boundary value problem:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}(n+p)\partial_{t}u=\Delta_{p}^{\mathrm{N}}u&\text{in }\Omega\times(0,\mathrm{T}],\\

Furthermore, let

Remember that the operator

\Delta_{p}^{\mathrm{N}}u
is nowadays labeled the normalized
p
-Laplacian (with
1<p<\infty
), which admits a non-variational nature in contrast with its variational counterpart. Moreover, we note that the normalized

-Laplacian can be viewed as the 1-homogeneous version of the standard
p
-Laplacian, or as a convex combination of the Laplacian and the normalized infinity-Laplacian operators:

\Delta_{p}^{\mathrm{N}}u=\frac{1}{p-1}|\nabla u|^{2-p}\text{div}(|\nabla u|^{p-2}\nabla u)=\frac{1}{p-1}\Delta u+\frac{p-2}{p-1}\Delta_{\infty}^{\mathrm{N}}u

where

\Delta_{\infty}^{\mathrm{N}}u\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=|\nabla u|^{-2}\langle D^{2}u\cdot\nabla u,\nabla u\rangle,

Regarding qualitative studies on evolution equations involving the normalized
p
-Laplacian using game theoretic methods, Does in

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcrcl}\partial_{t}u+\mathcal{A}_{p}(u)&=&0&\text{in }&\Omega\times(0,\mathrm{T})\\

where

\mathcal{A}_{p}(u)=-\frac{1}{p}\text{tr}(D^{2}u)-\frac{p-2}{p}\frac{\langle D^{2}u\,\nabla u,\nabla u\rangle}{|\nabla u|^{2}},

In this setting, the author establishes the existence and uniqueness of a viscosity solution that is globally Lipschitz continuous at

.

Regards universal local estimates, in
[
25
]
Parviainen-Ruostenoja examined the local regularity of value functions arising from time-dependent tug-of-war games, which are associated with the normalized
p

\partial_{t}u=|\nabla u|^{2-p}\,\text{div}(|\nabla u|^{p-2}\nabla u)\equiv\Delta u+(p(x,t)-2)\Delta^{\mathrm{N}}_{\infty}u.

Precisely, the value functions of this particular two-player zero-sum game satisfy the so-called dynamic programming principle:

(1.5)

u_{\varepsilon}(x,t)=\frac{\alpha(x,t)}{2}\left(\sup_{B_{\varepsilon}(x)}u\left(y,t-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\right)+\inf_{B_{\varepsilon}(x)}u\left(y,t-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\right)\right)+\beta(x,t)\int_{B_{\varepsilon}(x)}u\left(y,t-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\right)\,dy,

which may arise, for instance, from stochastic games or discretization schemes, and

\alpha(x,t)=\frac{p(x,t)-2}{p(x,t)+n}\quad\text{and}\quad\beta(x,t)=\frac{2+n}{p(x,t)+n}.

In a game theory perspective, the equation (
1.5
) can be heuristically interpreted as a weighted sum of the three possible outcomes of a game round, with the respective
(x,t)
-dependent probabilities. The step duration corresponds to
\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}
units of time.

In the case of constant
p
, the authors established local Lipschitz continuity, while for the more general setting, Hölder continuity and Harnack estimates are derived.

Additionally, one of the main contributions of
[
25
]
lies in presenting a local argument rooted in probabilistic game theory, inspired by Peres et al.’s pioneering work
[
26
]
. Specifically, the problem is formulated using a suitable Tug-of-War game framework rather than directly analyzing the associated PDEs. Furthermore, for both the initial boundary value problem and the Hölder and Harnack estimates, the authors also addressed the case of non-constant
p=p(x,t)
.
The methods in this work differ from usual PDE techniques, offering an alternative perspective for studying the associated equations.

In 2020, Han in
[
15

investigated functions satisfying the following dynamic programming principle:

(1.6)

\begin{array}[]{rcr}u_{\varepsilon}(x,t)&=&\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sup_{\nu\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\left\{\alpha u_{\varepsilon}\left(x+\varepsilon\nu,t-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\right)+\beta\mathchoice{\mathop{\vrule width=6.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.5pt\kern-8.0pt\intop}\nolimits_{\kern-6.0ptB_{\nu}^{\varepsilon}}}{\mathop{\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.6pt\kern-6.0pt\intop}\nolimits_{B_{\nu}^{\varepsilon}}}{\mathop{\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.6pt\kern-6.0pt\intop}\nolimits_{B_{\nu}^{\varepsilon}}}{\mathop{\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.6pt\kern-6.0pt\intop}\nolimits_{B_{\nu}^{\varepsilon}}}u_{\varepsilon}\left(x+h,t-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\right)\,d\mathcal{L}^{n-1}(h)\right\}\\

In this scenario, Han demonstrated an interior (in the parabolic sense) asymptotic Lipschitz-type regularity for value functions

|u_{\varepsilon}(x,t)-u_{\varepsilon}(z,s)|\leq\mathrm{C}_{\text{Lip}}\left(|x-z|+|t-s|^{\frac{1}{2}}+\varepsilon\right),

Han’s motivation for studying such a DPP partly arises from its connection to stochastic games, namely game values of the time-tracking tug-of-war with noise. Additionally, Han’s work is linked to the normalized parabolic

\partial_{t}u=\Delta_{p}^{\mathrm{N}}u=\Delta u+(p-2)\Delta_{\infty}^{\mathrm{N}}u.

1.1.2
Quasi-linear parabolic PDEs in the non-divergence form: regularity theory

Concerning higher regularity estimates to quasi-linear evolution models in the non-divergence form we must stress that Jin-Silvestre in their seminal work
[
19
]
established interior Hölder estimates for the spatial gradient of viscosity solutions to the parabolic homogeneous
p
-Laplacian equation:

\partial_{t}u-|\nabla u|^{2-p}\operatorname{div}\left(|\nabla u|^{p-2}\nabla u\right)=0,

where
1<p<\infty
. Observe that such an equation can also be expressed as

(1.8)

\partial_{t}u=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\left(\delta_{ij}+(p-2)|\nabla u|^{-2}u_{x_{i}}u_{x_{j}}\right)u_{x_{i}x_{j}}=\Delta_{p}^{\mathrm{N}}u,

which is a parabolic equation in non-divergence form and is fundamentally distinct from its divergence counterpart, namely the parabolic
p
-Laplacian equation:

\partial_{t}u-\operatorname{div}\left(|\nabla u|^{p-2}\nabla u\right)=0,

which enjoys either degenerate or singular signature when
p\neq 2
.

We highlight that to achieve their main result (namely
[
19
, Theorem 1.1]
), the authors primarily employ tools from the theory of equations in non-divergence form, which include Maximum Principles and geometric approaches. Initially, they approximate equation (
1.8
) with a regularized problem to overcome the lack of smoothness in viscosity solutions. Then, they establish uniform Lipschitz estimates for the solutions of the regularized problem. Furthermore, a crucial insight is that
\varphi=(|\nabla u|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2})^{p/2}
serves as a subsolution to the uniformly parabolic equation:

\partial_{t}u=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\left(\delta_{ij}+(p-2)\frac{u_{x_{i}}u_{x_{j}}}{|\nabla u|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}}\right)u_{x_{i}x_{j}}.

Finally, the authors derive uniform Hölder estimates for the spatial gradient of the approximating solutions via a stability procedure. Specifically

Theorem 1.5

(
[
19
, Theorem 1.1]
).

Let
u
be a viscosity solution of (
1.8
) in
Q_{1}
, where
1<p<\infty
. Then, there exist constants
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
\mathrm{C}_{0}>0
, both depending only on
n
and
p
, such that

\|\nabla u\|_{C^{\alpha}(Q_{1/2})}\leq\mathrm{C}_{0}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{1})}.

Furthermore, it holds that

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}(x,t),(x,s)\in Q_{1/2}\\
t\neq s\end{subarray}}\frac{|u(x,t)-u(x,s)|}{|t-s|^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}}\leq\mathrm{C}_{0}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{1})}.

Here,
Q_{r}=B_{r}\times(-r^{2},0]
denotes the standard parabolic cylinder, where
r>0
and
B_{r}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the ball of radius
r
centered at the origin. Finally, we have

|u(y,s)-u(x,t)-\nabla u(x,t)\cdot(y-x)|\leq\mathrm{C}_{0}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{1})}\big(|y-x|+\sqrt{|t-s|}\big)^{1+\alpha}

for all
(y,s),(x,t)\in Q_{1/2}
.

Subsequently, Attouchi-Parviainen in
[
3

studied an evolution equation involving the normalized
p
-Laplacian and a bounded, continuous source term, namely

(1.9)

\partial_{t}u-\Delta_{p}^{\mathrm{N}}u=f(x,t)\quad\text{in }\quad Q_{1},

In this scenario, they established local
C^{\alpha,\frac{\alpha}{2}}
-regularity for the spatial gradient of viscosity solutions:

Theorem 1.6

(
[
3

Assume that
p>1

and

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}(x,t),(x,s)\in\Omega^{\prime}\times(\varepsilon,T-\varepsilon)\\

can be approximated by an affine function within a cylinder

, then it is possible to achieve a more refined approximation in a smaller cylinder, and this process can be iterated. Specifically, they demonstrated by induction that for some
\rho,\alpha\in(0,1)
and
\mathrm{C}=\mathrm{C}(p,n)
, there exists a sequence
(\vec{q}_{k})
such that

\operatorname{osc}_{Q_{\rho^{k}}}(u(x,t)-\vec{q}_{k}\cdot x)\leq\mathrm{C}\rho^{k(1+\alpha)}.

The inductive step relies on establishing an improvement of flatness for the rescaled function

w_{k}(x,\cdot)=\rho^{-k(1+\alpha)}\big(u(\rho^{k}x,\cdot)-\vec{q}_{k}\cdot(\rho^{k}x)\big).

Therefore, the core objective is to analyze the equation satisfied by the deviation of
u
from its linear approximation, denoted as
w(x,t)=u(x,t)-\vec{q}\cdot x
, and to derive a local
C^{\beta,\beta/2}
estimate for
w
that is independent of
\vec{q}
.

Applications in nonlinear models with
(p\&q)
-type growth

As a consequence of our results, by combining the equivalence established earlier with Theorem
1.1
, we investigate the regularity of weak solutions for nonlinear problems with
(p,q)
-growth, described as follows:

(1.16)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u-\operatorname{div}\left((|\nabla u|^{p-2}+\mathfrak{a}(x,t)|\nabla u|^{q-2})\nabla u\right)=f(x,t),

where
2\leq p\leq q<\infty
and
\mathfrak{a}(x,t)\equiv\mathfrak{a}_{0}
for some positive constant
\mathfrak{a}_{0}
.

In this context, the operator is given by

\mathcal{L}u:=\operatorname{div}\left((|\nabla u|^{p-2}+\mathfrak{a}_{0}|\nabla u|^{q-2})\nabla u\right),

which can be expressed in its non-variational form as:

\mathcal{L}u=\mathscr{H}_{p,q}(x,t,\nabla u)\Delta_{p}^{\rm{N}}u+\mathfrak{a}_{0}(q-p)|\nabla u|^{q-2}\Delta_{\infty}^{\rm{N}}u.

Here,

\mathscr{H}_{p,q}(x,t,\xi)=|\xi|^{p-2}+\mathfrak{a}_{0}|\xi|^{q-2},\quad\text{and}\quad\Delta_{\infty}^{\mathrm{N}}u=\left\langle D^{2}u\frac{\nabla u}{|\nabla u|},\frac{\nabla u}{|\nabla u|}\right\rangle

is the normalized
\infty
-Laplacian operator.

Under these conditions, and under suitable extra assumptions, we obtain the following result as a direct application of Theorem
1.1
:

Corollary 1.10
.

Let
u\in C^{0}(Q_{1})
be a bounded viscosity solution of (
1.16
) in
Q_{1}
. Assume one of the following conditions holds:

(i)

\mathcal{G}(D^{2}u,\nabla u):=|\nabla u|^{q-2}\Delta_{\infty}^{\mathrm{N}}u\in L^{\infty}(Q_{1})
and
f\in L^{\infty}(Q_{1})
.

(ii)

\Delta_{\infty}^{\mathrm{N}}u,f\in L^{\infty}(Q_{1})
,
u\in C^{0,1}_{x}(Q_{1})
, and
q\geq 2
.

Then,
u\in C^{1,\alpha,\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}_{loc}(Q_{1})
for some
\alpha\in\left(0,\frac{1}{p-1}\right)
. Moreover, the following estimates hold:

\sup_{(x,t),(y,s)\in Q_{1/2}\atop{(x,t)\neq(y,s)}}\frac{|\nabla u(x,t)-\nabla u(y,s)|}{|x-y|^{\alpha}+|t-s|^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}}\leq\mathrm{C}\left[\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{1})}+\left(\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{1})}+\|\mathcal{G}\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{1})}\right)^{\frac{1}{1+\mathfrak{p}}}\right],

and

\sup_{(x,t),(x,s)\in Q_{1/2}\atop{t\neq s}}\frac{|u(x,t)-u(x,s)|}{|s-t|^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}}\leq\mathrm{C}\left[\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{1})}+\left(\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{1})}+\|\mathcal{G}\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{1})}\right)^{\frac{1}{1+\mathfrak{p}}}\right].

Preliminaries

Throughout this work, we will adopt the following notation: For
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
, and
r>0
, we denote the Euclidean ball by

B_{r}(x_{0})=B(x_{0},r):=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:|x-x_{0}|<r\},

and the parabolic cylinder by

Q_{r}(x_{0},t_{0}):=B_{r}(x_{0})\times(t_{0}-r^{2},t_{0}].

Additionally, we define the re-scaled (or intrinsic) parabolic cylinders as

Q_{r}^{\lambda}(x_{0},t_{0}):=B_{r}(x_{0})\times(t_{0}-r^{2}\lambda^{-\mathfrak{p}},t_{0}],

which are appropriately adjusted to account for the degeneracy of (
1.1
). When
x_{0}=0
and
t_{0}=0
, we omit the centers in the above notations.

We also introduce the intrinsic
\theta-
parabolic cylinder

(2.1)

Q_{\rho,\theta}(x_{0},t_{0})\coloneqq B_{\rho}(x_{0})\times I_{\rho,\theta}(t_{0}),

where
I_{\rho,\theta}(t_{0})\coloneqq(-\rho^{\theta}+t_{0},t_{0}]
with
\theta>1
. We also denote
Q_{1}\coloneqq Q_{1,\theta}
.

Consider the set
\Omega_{\mathrm{T}}=\Omega\times(-\mathrm{T},0]
for some
\mathrm{T}>0
and
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
an open set, we define

\partial_{par}\Omega_{\mathrm{T}}=(\partial\Omega\times[-\mathrm{T},0))\cup(\Omega\times\{t=-\mathrm{T}\})

represents its parabolic boundary. Moreover, we define the parabolic distance between the points
\mathrm{P}_{1}=(x,t),\mathrm{P}_{2}=(y,s)
in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
by

d(\mathrm{P}_{1},\mathrm{P}_{2})=|x-y|+|t-s|^{\frac{1}{2}}.

We will use the following notations for the parabolic Hölder spaces: the semi-norms for
\alpha\in(0,1]
,

[u]_{C^{\alpha,\alpha/2}(Q_{r})}:=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}(x,t),(y,s)\in Q_{r}\\
(x,t)\neq(y,s)\end{subarray}}\frac{|u(y,s)-u(x,t)|}{|x-y|^{\alpha}+|t-s|^{\alpha/2}},

[u]_{C^{0,\alpha}_{x}(Q_{r})}:=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}(x,t),(y,t)\in Q_{r}\\
x\neq y\end{subarray}}\frac{|u(y,s)-u(x,t)|}{|x-y|^{\alpha}}

and the norm

\|u\|_{C^{\alpha,\alpha/2}(Q_{r})}:=\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{r})}+[u]_{C^{\alpha,\alpha/2}(Q_{r})}.

The space
C^{1+\alpha,(1+\alpha)/2}(Q_{r})
is defined as the set of all functions with finite norm

\|u\|_{C^{1+\alpha,(1+\alpha)/2}(Q_{r})}:=\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{r})}+\|\nabla u\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{r})}+[u]_{C^{1+\alpha,(1+\alpha)/2}(Q_{r})},

where

[u]_{C^{1+\alpha,(1+\alpha)/2}(Q_{r})}:=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}(x,t),(y,s)\in Q_{r}\\
(x,t)\neq(y,s)\end{subarray}}\frac{|\nabla u(x,t)-\nabla u(y,s)|}{|x-y|^{\alpha}+|t-s|^{\alpha/2}}+\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}(x,t),(x,s)\in Q_{r}\\
t\neq s\end{subarray}}\frac{|u(x,t)-u(x,s)|}{|t-s|^{(1+\alpha)/2}}.

Definition 2.1

(
Viscosity solution -
[
2
]
,
[
3
]
and
[
11
]
).

A function
u
that is locally bounded and upper semi-continuous in
Q_{1}
is referred to as a viscosity subsolution of (
1.1
) if, for any point
(x_{0},t_{0})\in Q_{1}
, one of the following conditions is satisfied:

1.

Either for every
\phi\in C^{2,1}(Q_{1})
, such that
u-\phi
attains a local maximum at
(x_{0},t_{0})
and
\nabla\phi(x_{0},t_{0})\neq 0

\partial_{t}\phi(x_{0},t_{0})-\mathscr{H}(x_{0},t_{0},\nabla\phi(x_{0},t_{0}))\Delta_{p}^{\mathrm{N}}\phi(x_{0},t_{0})\leq f(x_{0},t_{0}).

2.

Or if there exist
\delta_{1}>0
and
\phi\in C^{1}((t_{0}-\delta_{1},t_{0}+\delta_{1}))
such that

\begin{cases}\phi(t_{0})=0,\\
u(x_{0},t_{0})\geq u(x_{0},t)-\phi(t),\quad\forall t\in(t_{0}-\delta_{1},t_{0}+\delta_{1}),\\
\displaystyle\sup_{t\in(t_{0}-\delta_{1},t_{0}+\delta_{1})}(u(x,t)-\phi(t))\text{ is constant in a neighborhood of }x_{0},\end{cases}

then

\phi^{\prime}(t_{0})\leq f(x_{0},t_{0}).

Similarly, a function
u
that is locally bounded and lower semi-continuous in
Q_{1}
is called a viscosity supersolution of (
1.1
) if, for any point
(x_{0},t_{0})\in Q_{1}
, one of the following conditions is fulfilled:

1.

Either for every
\phi\in C^{2,1}(Q_{1})
, such that
u-\phi
attains a local minimum at
(x_{0},t_{0})
and
D\phi(x_{0},t_{0})\neq 0
, it holds that

\partial_{t}\phi(x_{0},t_{0})-\mathscr{H}(x_{0},t_{0},\nabla\phi(x_{0},t_{0}))\Delta_{p}^{\mathrm{N}}\phi(x_{0},t_{0})\geq f(x_{0},t_{0}).

2.

Or if there exist
\delta_{1}>0
and
\phi\in C^{1}((t_{0}-\delta_{1},t_{0}+\delta_{1}))
such that

\begin{cases}\phi(t_{0})=0,\\
u(x_{0},t_{0})\leq u(x_{0},t)-\phi(t),\quad\forall t\in(t_{0}-\delta_{1},t_{0}+\delta_{1}),\\
\displaystyle\inf_{t\in(t_{0}-\delta_{1},t_{0}+\delta_{1})}(u(x,t)-\phi(t))\text{ is constant in a neighborhood of }x_{0},\end{cases}

then

\phi^{\prime}(t_{0})\geq f(x_{0},t_{0}).

Finally, a continuous function

is called a viscosity solution of (

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{J}}^{\pm}(v)(x,t)=\left\{(\tau,\xi,\mathrm{X})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\text{Sym}(n)\,|\,\exists(x_{k},t_{k})\to(x,t),\right.

Lemma 3.1

(

Let
u_{i}

for
i=1,\ldots,k

\varphi

(B_{1})^{k}\times(-1,0)

(x_{1},\dots,x_{k},t)\to\varphi(x_{1},\dots,x_{k},t)

and twice continuously differentiable in
(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\in(B_{1})^{k}

w(x_{1},\dots,x_{k},t):=u_{1}(x_{1},t)+\cdots+u_{k}(x_{k},t)-\varphi(x_{1},\dots,x_{k},t)

attains a local maximum at
(\bar{x}_{1},\dots,\bar{x}_{k},\bar{t})\in(B_{1})^{k}\times(-1,0)
. Assume, moreover, that there exists an
r>0
such that for every
\mathrm{M}_{\star}>0
there is a constant
\mathrm{C}_{\star}
such that for
i=1,\ldots,k

b_{i}\leq\mathrm{C}_{\star}\quad\text{whenever }(b_{i},\vec{q}_{i},X_{i})\in\mathcal{J}^{+}u_{i}(x_{i},t),

|x_{i}-\bar{x}_{i}|+|t-\bar{t}|\leq r,\quad\text{and}\quad|u_{i}(x_{i},t)|+|\vec{q}_{i}|+\|\mathrm{X}_{i}\|\leq\mathrm{C}_{\star}.

Then for each
\varepsilon>0
, there exist
\mathrm{X}_{i}\in\mathrm{Sym}(n)
such that:

(i)

(b_{i},D_{x_{i}}\varphi(\bar{x}_{1},\ldots,\bar{x}_{k}),\mathrm{X}_{i})\in\overline{\mathcal{J}}^{+}u_{i}(\bar{x}_{i},\bar{t})
for
i=1,\dots,k
,

(ii)

-\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}+\|\mathrm{A}\|\right)\mathrm{Id}_{n}\leq\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{X}_{1}&&0\\
&\ddots&\\
0&&\mathrm{X}_{k}\end{pmatrix}\leq\mathrm{A}+\varepsilon\mathrm{A}^{2}
,

(iii)

b_{1}+\cdots+b_{k}=\varphi_{t}(\bar{x}_{1},\ldots,\bar{x}_{k},\bar{t}),

where
\mathrm{A}=D^{2}\varphi(\bar{x}_{1},\ldots,\bar{x}_{k},\bar{t})
.

The next result is the classical Comparison Principle for the problem (
1.1
), whose ideas were inspired by
[
14
, Proposition 5.1]
and
[
11
, Theorem 1]
. We must emphasize that the proof holds for a more general range of exponents, namely
-1<\mathfrak{p}\leq\mathfrak{q}<\infty
.

Theorem 3.2

(
Comparison Principle).

Assume that assumptions
\mathrm{(H1)-(H4)}
are satisfied. Suppose that
u
and
v
are, respectively, a viscosity subsolution with source term
f
, and a locally uniformly Lipschitz in spatial variable viscosity supersolution with source term
g
of (
1.1
) in
\Omega_{\mathrm{T}}
. Moreover, assume that
f\leq g
in
\Omega_{\mathrm{T}}
. If
u\leq v
on
\partial_{par}\Omega_{\mathrm{T}}
, then
u\leq v
in
\Omega_{\mathrm{T}}
.

Proof.

First, we can assume without loss of generality that
v
is a strict viscosity supersolution. More precisely, we assume that

(3.1)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v-\mathscr{H}(x,t,\nabla v)\Delta_{p}^{\rm{N}}v>g(x,t)\,\,\,\text{in}\,\,\,\Omega_{\mathrm{T}},

in the viscosity sense. Indeed, for
\varepsilon>0
, define
v_{\varepsilon}(x,t)=v(x,t)-\frac{\varepsilon}{\mathrm{T}+t}
. Now, let
\psi\in C^{2,1}(\Omega_{\mathrm{T}})
be such that
v_{\varepsilon}-\psi
attains a local minimum at
(x_{0},t_{0})\in\Omega_{\mathrm{T}}
and
\nabla\psi(x_{0},t_{0})\neq 0
. Hence, the function
\phi(x,t)=\psi(x,t)+\frac{\varepsilon}{\mathrm{T}+t}
also lies in
C^{2,1}(\Omega_{\mathrm{T}})
. Moreover,
v-\phi
attains a local minimum at
(x_{0},t_{0})
, and
\nabla\psi(x_{0},t_{0})=\nabla\phi(x_{0},t_{0})
. Thus, since
v
is a viscosity supersolution and
\varepsilon>0
, we have that

\displaystyle g(x_{0},t_{0})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi(x_{0},t_{0})-\mathscr{H}(x_{0},t_{0},\nabla\phi(x_{0},t_{0}))\Delta_{p}^{\rm{N}}\phi(x_{0},t_{0})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\psi(x_{0},t_{0})-\frac{\varepsilon}{(\mathrm{T}+t_{0})^{2}}-\mathscr{H}(x_{0},t_{0},\nabla\psi(x_{0},t_{0}))\Delta_{p}^{\rm{N}}\psi(x_{0},t_{0})

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\psi(x_{0},t_{0})-\mathscr{H}(x_{0},t_{0},\nabla\psi(x_{0},t_{0}))\Delta_{p}^{\rm{N}}\psi(x_{0},t_{0}).

On the other hand, suppose that there exist
\delta_{1}>0
and
\psi\in C^{1}((t_{0}-\delta_{1},t_{0}+\delta_{1}))
such that

\begin{cases}\psi(t_{0})=0,\\
w(x_{0},t_{0})\leq w(x_{0},t)-\psi(t),\quad\forall t\in(t_{0}-\delta_{1},t_{0}+\delta_{1}),\\
\displaystyle\inf_{t\in(t_{0}-\delta_{1},t_{0}+\delta_{1})}(w(x,t)-\psi(t))\text{ is constant in a neighborhood of }x_{0}.\end{cases}

Now, define

\phi(t)=\psi(t)-\frac{\varepsilon}{\mathrm{T}+t_{0}}+\frac{\varepsilon}{\mathrm{T}+t}.

Observe that
\phi\in C^{1}((t_{0}-\delta_{1},t_{0}+\delta_{1}))
, and by the properties of
\psi
, we deduce that

\begin{cases}\phi(t_{0})=\psi(t_{0})=0,\\
v(x_{0},t_{0})\leq v(x_{0},t)-\phi(t),\quad\forall t\in(t_{0}-\delta_{1},t_{0}+\delta_{1}),\\
\displaystyle\inf_{t\in(t_{0}-\delta_{1},t_{0}+\delta_{1})}(v(x,t)-\phi(t))\text{ is constant in a neighborhood of }x_{0}.\end{cases}

Thus, since
v
is a viscosity supersolution, we obtain that

g(x_{0},t_{0})\leq\phi^{\prime}(t_{0})=\psi^{\prime}(t_{0})-\frac{\varepsilon}{(\mathrm{T}+t_{0})^{2}}<\psi^{\prime}(t_{0}).

Consequently, by the arbitrariness of the point
(x_{0},t_{0})
and the function
\psi
described above, the claim (
3.1
) follows.

Now proceed to prove the theorem’s conclusion. We will establish this result using
reductio ad absurdum
. Consequently, define

\mathrm{M}_{0}=\sup_{(x,t)\in\overline{\Omega_{T}}}\left\{u(x,t)-v(x,t)\right\}>0,

where
\mathrm{M}_{0}
is attained at the point
(\bar{x},\bar{t})\in\Omega\times(-\mathrm{T},0)
.

Now, for
j
and
\mathfrak{m}>\max\left\{2,\frac{\mathfrak{p}+2}{\mathfrak{p}+1},\frac{\mathfrak{q}+2}{\mathfrak{q}+1}\right\}
, we define the auxiliary function

\phi_{j}(x,y,t,s)=u(x,t)-v(y,s)-\psi_{j}(x,y,t,s),

where

\psi_{j}(x,y,t,s)=\frac{j}{\mathfrak{m}}|x-y|^{\mathfrak{m}}+\frac{j}{2}(t-s)^{2}.

Under these conditions,
\phi_{j}
achieves its maximum at the point
(x_{j},y_{j},t_{j},s_{j})\in\Omega\times\Omega\times(-\mathrm{T},0)\times(-\mathrm{T},0)
for sufficiently large
j
, and
(x_{j},y_{j},t_{j},s_{j})\to(\bar{x},\bar{x},\bar{t},\bar{t})
as
j\to\infty
(cf.
[
11
, Theorem 1]
and
[
13
, Proposition 5.1]
).

Claim:

x_{j}\neq y_{j}
for
j\gg 1
.

To prove this, suppose that
x_{j}=y_{j}
. Then the function

\hat{\phi}(y,s)=v(y_{j},s_{j})-\frac{j}{\mathfrak{m}}|x_{j}-y|^{\mathfrak{m}}-\frac{j}{2}(s-t_{j})^{2}+\frac{j}{2}(t_{j}-s_{j})^{2}

touches
v
from below at
(x_{j},s_{j})
. By
[
11
, Lemma 1]
, it follows that

(3.2)

\displaystyle j(t_{j}-s)>g(x_{j},s_{j}).

On the other hand, we observe that

\Psi(x,t):=\psi_{j}(x,y_{j},t,s_{j})-\psi_{j}(x_{j},y_{j},t_{j},s_{j})+u(x_{j},t_{j})

is a test function for
u
at
(x_{j},t_{j})
. Similarly to the above case for
v
, it holds that

(3.3)

\displaystyle j(t_{j}-s_{j})\leq f(x_{j},t_{j}).

Combining (
3.2
) and (
3.3
), we deduce that

0=j(t_{j}-s_{j})-j(t_{j}-s_{j})>g(x_{j},s_{j})-f(x_{j},t_{j})\geq\liminf_{j\to\infty}(g(x_{j},s_{j})-f(x_{j},t_{j}))=0,

which is a contradiction. Therefore, we conclude that
x_{j}\neq y_{j}
for
j\gg 1
, as claimed.

In this scenario, we arrive at a contradiction similar to the one presented in
[
13
, Proposition 5.2]
for the non-homogeneous case, arising from the continuity of the source terms
f
and
g
, as well as the previously mentioned convergences. Thus, the proof is complete.
∎

). The proof follows standard techniques, which rely on the Comparison Principle above (cf.
[
11
]
and
[
18
]
). For brevity, the detailed proof is omitted.

Theorem 3.3

(
Existence and Uniqueness of Solutions).

Assume that
-1<\mathfrak{p}\leq\mathfrak{q}<\infty
,
\mathfrak{a}\in C^{0}(Q_{\mathrm{T}})
,
f\in L^{\infty}(Q_{\mathrm{T}})\cap C^{0}(Q_{\mathrm{T}})
, and
g\in C^{0}(\partial_{par}Q_{\mathrm{T}})
for
\mathrm{T}>0
. Then, there exists a unique viscosity solution
u
to the problem

(3.4)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rclcl}\partial_{t}u(x,t)-\mathscr{H}(x,t,\nabla u)\Delta_{p}^{\mathrm{N}}u(x,t)&=&f(x,t)&\mbox{in}&Q_{\mathrm{T}},\\
u(x,t)&=&g(x,t)&\mbox{on}&\partial_{par}Q_{\mathrm{T}}.\end{array}\right.

The idea of the proof follows Perron’s method. Specifically, considering
u_{\star}
and
u^{\star}
, respectively, a viscosity subsolution and a viscosity supersolution of (
3.4
), then

u(x,t)=:\sup_{\mathcal{S}}v(x,t)

is a viscosity solution of (
3.4
), where

\mathcal{S}=\{v\in C^{0}(\overline{Q_{\mathrm{T}}}):u_{\star}\leq v\leq u^{\star}\,\,\,\text{and}\,\,\ v\,\text{is a subsolution of}\,\eqref{Dirichletprob}\}.

The main goal of this section is to establish compactness results for the solutions of (

Lemma 4.1

(
Hölder estimates in spatial variables).

Let

\rm{(H2)}
and
\rm{(H3)}

\displaystyle|u(x,t)-u(y,t)|\leq\mathrm{C}\left(\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{1})}+\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{1})}^{\frac{1}{1+\mathfrak{p}}}+\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{1})}^{\frac{1}{1+\mathfrak{p}}}\right)|x-y|^{\mu}.

Proof.

We fix
x_{0},y_{0}\in B_{\frac{15}{16}}
and
t_{0}\in\left(-\left(\frac{15}{16}\right)^{2},0\right)

\mathfrak{L}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{L}_{2}

\displaystyle\phi(x,y,t):=u(x,t)-u(y,t)-\mathfrak{L}_{2}\omega(|x-y|)-\frac{\mathfrak{L}_{1}}{2}|x-x_{0}|^{2}-\frac{\mathfrak{L}_{1}}{2}|y-y_{0}|^{2}-\frac{\mathfrak{L}_{1}}{2}(t-t_{0})^{2},

where the function
\omega
is defined by
\omega(s)=s^{\mu}
. We assert that
\phi\leq 0
in
\overline{Q_{\frac{15}{16}}}
. We will proceed with a
reductio ad absurdum
argument. Assume, for contradiction, that there exists a maximum point

\phi
is positive. Since

\bar{x}\neq\bar{y}
. Now, choosing,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{L}_{1}\geq\frac{32\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{1})}}{(\min\{\mathrm{dist}((x_{0},t_{0}),\partial Q_{15/16}),\mathrm{dist}((y_{0},t_{0}),\partial Q_{15/16})\})^{2}}=\mathrm{C}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{1})},

we have that

\displaystyle|\bar{x}-x_{0}|+|\bar{t}-t_{0}|\leq\frac{\mathrm{dist}((x_{0},t_{0}),\partial Q_{15/16})}{2}

and

\displaystyle|\bar{y}-y_{0}|+|\bar{t}-t_{0}|\leq\frac{\mathrm{dist}((y_{0},t_{0}),\partial Q_{15/16})}{2}.

With this choice and the two inequalities above, we can conclude that
\bar{x},\bar{y}\in B_{\frac{15}{16}}
and
\bar{t}\in\left(-\left(\frac{15}{16}\right)^{2},0\right)
. Furthermore, if
\mathfrak{L}_{2}\gg 1
, then

(4.1)

\displaystyle|\bar{x}-\bar{y}|\leq\left(\frac{2\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{1})}}{\mathfrak{L}_{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\mu}}\ll 1.

Having made these observations, we apply Jensen-Ishii’s Lemma
3.1
with the functions

\displaystyle\left(\tau+\mathfrak{L}_{1}(\bar{t}-t_{0}),\varsigma_{1},\mathrm{X}+\mathfrak{L}_{1}\mathrm{Id}_{n}\right)\in\overline{\mathcal{J}}^{+}(u)(\bar{x},\bar{t})\quad\text{and}\quad\left(\tau,\varsigma_{2},\mathrm{Y}-\mathfrak{L}_{1}\mathrm{Id}_{n}\right)\in\overline{\mathcal{J}}^{-}(u)(\bar{y},\bar{t}).

Here,

\displaystyle\varsigma_{1}=\mathfrak{L}_{2}\omega^{\prime}(|\bar{x}-\bar{y}|)\frac{\bar{x}-\bar{y}}{|\bar{x}-\bar{y}|}+\mathfrak{L}_{1}(\bar{x}-x_{0})\quad\text{and}\quad\varsigma_{2}=\mathfrak{L}_{2}\omega^{\prime}(|\bar{x}-\bar{y}|)\frac{\bar{x}-\bar{y}}{|\bar{x}-\bar{y}|}-\mathfrak{L}_{1}(\bar{y}-y_{0}).

Imposing the condition that

(4.2)

\displaystyle 2\mathfrak{L}_{2}\mu|\bar{x}-\bar{y}|^{\mu-1}\geq|\varsigma_{i}|\geq\frac{\mathfrak{L}_{2}}{2}\mu|\bar{x}-\bar{y}|^{\mu-1},\,i=1,2.

Furthermore, by Jensen-Ishii’s Lemma

0&-\mathrm{Y}\end{pmatrix}\leq\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{Z}^{\kappa}&-\mathrm{Z}\\
-\mathrm{Z}&\mathrm{Z}^{\kappa}\end{pmatrix},

where

\displaystyle\mathrm{Z}

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{L}_{2}\omega^{\prime\prime}(|\bar{x}-\bar{y}|)\frac{\bar{x}-\bar{y}}{|\bar{x}-\bar{y}|}\otimes\frac{\bar{x}-\bar{y}}{|\bar{x}-\bar{y}|}+\mathfrak{L}_{2}\omega^{\prime}(|\bar{x}-\bar{y}|)\left(\mathrm{Id}_{n}-\frac{\bar{x}-\bar{y}}{|\bar{x}-\bar{y}|}\otimes\frac{\bar{x}-\bar{y}}{|\bar{x}-\bar{y}|}\right)

Taking

Writing

\displaystyle 2\mathfrak{L}_{2}\mu|\bar{x}-\bar{y}|^{\mu-2}\left(\langle\xi,\xi\rangle-2\frac{2-\mu}{3-\mu}\xi\xi^{T}\xi\right)

\displaystyle 2\mathfrak{L}_{2}\mu|\bar{x}-\bar{y}|^{\mu-2}\langle\xi,\xi\rangle\left(1-2\frac{2-\mu}{3-\mu}\right)

since

. Furthermore, by inequality (

Combining these two inequalities, it follows that

\displaystyle\mathfrak{L}_{1}(\bar{t}-t_{0})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}(\bar{x},\bar{t},\varsigma_{1})\operatorname{tr}(\mathfrak{A}(\varsigma_{1})(\mathrm{X}+\mathfrak{L}_{1}\mathrm{Id}_{n}))

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}(\bar{y},\bar{t},\varsigma_{2})\operatorname{tr}(\mathfrak{A}(\varsigma_{2})(\mathrm{Y}-\mathfrak{L}_{1}\mathrm{Id}_{n}))+2\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{1})}.

(4.5)

\displaystyle 0

Remark 6.1
.

We say that an operator
\mathcal{L}:\textrm{Sym}(n)\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}\times Q_{\mathrm{T}}\to\mathbb{R}
, in the form

\mathcal{L}(\mathrm{X},\xi,s,x,t)=s-\mathcal{G}(\mathrm{X},\xi,x,t),

where
\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{G}(\mathrm{X},\xi,x,t)
is a fully nonlinear operator, has the
Strong Minimum Principle
property
(\text{for short}\,\,\textbf{S.M.P.})
if, for every bounded solution
u
of the problem
\mathcal{L}u=0
such that
u\geq 0
and
u
admits a local minimum, it follows that
u\equiv 0
.

Now, consider the following class of operators:

\mathfrak{S}=\left\{\mathcal{L}:\textrm{Sym}(n)\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}\times Q_{1}\to\mathbb{R}:\mathcal{L}\text{ satisfies the {S.M.P.} property}\right\}.

From the proof above, we observe that if the limiting operator
\mathcal{L}u=\partial_{t}u-\kappa|\nabla u|^{\mathfrak{p}}\Delta_{p}^{\mathrm{N}}u
belongs to the class
\mathfrak{S}
(for a general exponent
\mathfrak{p}>0
), then the optimal regularity holds at the critical points, namely

\sup_{Q_{r,\theta}(0,0)}|u(x,t)-u(x_{0},t_{0})|\leq\mathrm{C}_{0}r^{1+\frac{1}{1+\mathfrak{p}}}.

Finally, we present an example demonstrating the sharpness of the regularity exponent in Theorem
1.2
.

Example 6.2
.

Consider the function
u:Q_{1}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

u(x,t)=|x|^{1+\frac{1}{1+\mathfrak{p}}}+t.

Note that
u
satisfies (
1.1
) in the viscosity sense with

f(x,t)=1-\left[n-1+\frac{p-1}{1+\mathfrak{p}}\right]\cdot\left[\left(\frac{2+\mathfrak{p}}{1+\mathfrak{p}}\right)^{1+\mathfrak{p}}+|x|^{\frac{\mathfrak{q}-\mathfrak{p}}{1+\mathfrak{p}}}\left(\frac{2+\mathfrak{p}}{1+\mathfrak{p}}\right)^{1+\mathfrak{q}}\right],

\mathfrak{a}\equiv 1
and
\mathfrak{p}=p-2
. Note that
f\in L^{\infty}(Q_{1})\cap C^{0}(Q_{1})
.

Furthermore,
u(0,0)=0
,
\nabla u(0,0)=\vec{0}
, and the solution satisfies

\sup_{Q_{r,\theta}(0,0)}|u(x,t)|\leq 2r^{1+\frac{1}{1+\mathfrak{p}}}.

Therefore, this example demonstrates the sharpness of the regularity exponent in Theorem
1.2
, when
\mathfrak{p}=p-2>0
.

7
Non-degeneracy of solutions: Proof of Theorem
1.3

This section will be devoted to establishing the non-degeneracy of solutions to problem (
1.1
) along local extremum points. Morally, the idea of the proof of Theorem
1.3
is to construct a barrier function that serves as a supersolution to (
1.1
), such that at some point on the parabolic boundary of a cylinder centered at the local extremum point, it lies below the solution of the problem.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Consider a local extremum point
(x_{0},t_{0})\in Q_{1}
. Without loss of generality, we assume
(x_{0},t_{0})=(0,0)
, and that it is a local maximum point. Since the right-hand side of (
1.1
) does not depend on
u
, we may assume
u(0,0)>0
. Now, we define the following comparison (barrier) function:

\Phi(x,t)=\mathfrak{c}\left[|x|^{1+\alpha}+(-t)^{\frac{1+\alpha}{\theta}}\right],

where
\alpha\in(0,1]
,
\theta>0
, and
\mathfrak{c}>0
will be chosen
a posteriori
. We claim that, for a suitable
\mathfrak{c}
, the following inequality holds:

\partial_{t}\Phi(x,t)-\mathscr{H}(x,t,\nabla\Phi)\Delta_{p}^{\mathrm{N}}\Phi(x,t)\geq\sup_{Q_{1}}f(x,t)\,\,\,\text{in}\,\,\,Q_{r,\theta},

for all
r>0
such that
Q_{r,\theta}\subset Q_{1}
. To establish this, we can verify that:

•

\partial_{t}\Phi=-\mathfrak{c}\left(\frac{1+\alpha}{\theta}\right)(-t)^{\frac{1+\alpha-\theta}{\theta}}
,

•

\nabla\Phi=\mathfrak{c}(1+\alpha)|x|^{\alpha-1}x
,

•

D^{2}\Phi=\mathfrak{c}(1+\alpha)|x|^{\alpha-1}\left(\mathrm{Id}_{n}-(1-\alpha)\frac{x}{|x|}\otimes\frac{x}{|x|}\right)
.

Using these expressions, we can estimate
\mathscr{H}(x,t,\nabla\Phi)\Delta_{p}^{\mathrm{N}}\Phi
as follows:

(7.1)

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}(x,t,\nabla\Phi)\Delta_{p}^{\mathrm{N}}\Phi

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathfrak{c}^{\mathfrak{p}}(1+\alpha)^{\mathfrak{p}}|x|^{\alpha\mathfrak{p}}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\mathfrak{a}^{+}\mathfrak{c}^{\mathfrak{q}}(1+\alpha)^{\mathfrak{q}}|x|^{\alpha\mathfrak{q}})\mathfrak{c}(1+\alpha)|x|^{\alpha-1}(n-1+\alpha(p-1))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1+\alpha)^{1+\mathfrak{q}}|x|^{\alpha(1+\mathfrak{q})-1}(\mathfrak{c}^{1+\mathfrak{p}}+\mathfrak{a}^{+}\mathfrak{c}^{1+\mathfrak{q}})(n-1+\alpha(p-1))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2^{1+\mathfrak{q}}|x|^{\alpha(1+\mathfrak{q})-1}(\mathfrak{c}^{1+\mathfrak{p}}+\mathfrak{a}^{+}\mathfrak{c}^{1+\mathfrak{q}})(n-1+\alpha(p-1)),

for all
\mathfrak{c}\in(0,1]
.

Now, we make the following choices:

\alpha=\frac{1}{1+\mathfrak{p}}\quad\text{and}\quad\theta=2-\mathfrak{p}\alpha.

With this choice of
\alpha
and
\theta
, we observe that
1+\alpha=\theta
,
\alpha(1+\mathfrak{q})-1=\frac{\mathfrak{q}-\mathfrak{p}}{1+\mathfrak{p}}\geq 0
(by condition (H1)), and
\partial_{t}\Phi=-\mathfrak{c}
. Substituting into (
7.1
), we obtain:

\mathscr{H}(x,t,\nabla\Phi)\Delta_{p}^{\mathrm{N}}\Phi\leq 2^{1+\mathfrak{q}}\mathfrak{c}(1+\mathfrak{a}^{+})(n-1+\alpha(p-1)),

since
|x|^{\alpha(1+\mathfrak{q})-1}\leq 1
and
\mathfrak{c}^{1+\mathfrak{p}}\leq 1
. Consequently,

\partial_{t}\Phi-\mathscr{H}(x,t,\nabla\Phi)\Delta_{p}^{\mathrm{N}}\Phi\geq-\mathfrak{c}\left[1+2^{1+\mathfrak{q}}(1+\mathfrak{a}^{+})(n-1+\alpha(p-1))\right].

Thus, we can take:

\mathfrak{c}\leq\frac{\mathfrak{c}_{0}}{1+2^{1+\mathfrak{q}}(1+\mathfrak{a}^{+})(n-1+\alpha(p-1))},

and conclude the initial statement.

Therefore,
\Phi
is a supersolution to (
1.1
). Now, for any intrinsic cylinder
Q_{r,\theta}\subset Q_{1}
, we claim that there exists
(x_{r},t_{r})\in\partial_{par}Q_{r,\theta}
such that
u(x_{r},t_{r})\geq\Phi(x_{r},t_{r})
. If this does not hold, then
u\leq\Phi
on
\partial_{par}Q_{r,\theta}
. By applying the Comparison Principle
3.2
, we conclude
u\leq\Phi
in
Q_{r,\theta}
. However, this leads to a contradiction since
u(0,0)>0=\Phi(0,0)
.

Finally, we conclude:

\sup_{\partial_{par}Q_{r,\theta}}u\geq u(x_{r},t_{r})\geq\Phi(x_{r},t_{r})=\mathfrak{c}\left[|x_{r}|^{1+\alpha}+(-t_{r})\right].

Furthermore, since
(x_{r},t_{r})\in\partial_{par}Q_{r,\theta}
, there are two cases:

•

(x_{r},t_{r})\in\partial B_{r}\times[-r^{\theta},0)
: In this case,

\Phi(x_{r},t_{r})=\mathfrak{c}(r^{1+\alpha}-t_{r})\geq\mathfrak{c}r^{1+\alpha}.

•

(x_{r},t_{r})\in B_{r}\times\{-r^{\theta}\}
: Here,
t_{r}=-r^{\theta}
. Thus,

\Phi(x_{r},t_{r})=\mathfrak{c}(|x_{r}|^{1+\alpha}-(-r^{\theta}))\geq\mathfrak{c}r^{\theta}=\mathfrak{c}r^{1+\alpha},

since
1+\alpha=\theta
.

This concludes the proof.
