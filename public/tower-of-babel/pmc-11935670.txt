Following the Dynamic Changes of Coronary Atherosclerosis: An Uphill Battle
Authors: Daniel Chamié, Steven Pfau
Published: 2024

Accumulating evidence proposes that the occurrence of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) is related to the progression and destabilization of atherosclerosis rather than flow‐limiting stenoses and myocardial ischemia. 1 , 2 Advancements in both noninvasive and intravascular imaging now provide quantification of atheroma burden and high‐fidelity characterization of coronary plaque compositional features. In observational studies, patients with specific plaque phenotypes are at higher risk of MACE. 3 , 4 , 5 These studies imaged patients at a baseline and followed them for clinical events over variable times. Single time point imaging inherently fails to capture the dynamic nature of atherosclerosis, assuming the estimated baseline risk is static. Serial imaging studies were an intuitive solution to understanding plaque risk over time and have been used successfully to demonstrate the effects of pharmacotherapy on atherosclerosis. 6 , 7

In this issue of the Journal of the American Heart Association (JAHA), the PREDICTOR (Predicting Long‐Term Clinical Outcomes by OCT Assessment of Plaque Characteristics in the Non‐Revascularized Coronary Artery) investigators 8 studied the serial evolution of atherosclerosis in nonculprit coronary segments and related late clinical outcomes. They included 164 patients who underwent percutaneous coronary intervention and had an additional nonculprit vessel region (n=172) with angiographically intermediate (30%–90% diameter stenosis) stenosis imaged with optical coherence tomography (OCT). At 1‐year follow‐up, planned invasive angiography and OCT were performed in 134 nonculprit vessels from 138 patients, which entered the serial OCT analysis. The primary endpoint was a change in the mean lumen areas within the imaged regions from baseline to 1 year. OCT cross‐sectional images within the matched time points were characterized as normal vessel, adaptive intimal thickening, fibrous plaque, fibrocalcific plaque, calcified nodules (eruptive and noneruptive), mixed plaque, thick‐cap fibroatheroma, and thin‐cap fibroatheroma (TCFA; the most common phenotype associated with MACE). While the imaging observations were separated by 12 months, clinical events were accumulated over 3 years; the rates of all‐cause death, cardiac death, nonfatal myocardial infarction, and ischemia‐driven revascularization comprised the secondary endpoints.

During the 1‐year follow‐up, the investigators found an average reduction of 0.18 mm2 in the mean lumen area at the imaged regions. The magnitude of mean lumen area reduction in regions classified as TCFAs (−0.23 mm2) was similar to those in thick‐cap fibroatheromas (−0.21 mm2), mixed plaques (−0.15 mm2), and fibrous plaques (−0.30 mm2). Regions with fibrocalcific plaques, adaptive intimal thickening, and normal vessels had no or minimal mean lumen area change. A total of 21 873 OCT cross‐sections were analyzed along the matched imaged regions. The proportion of cross‐sections categorized as thick‐cap fibroatheromas and normal vessels decreased by 2% and 0.9%, respectively, while the proportion of cross‐sections categorized as calcified, mixed, and fibrocalcific plaques increased by 3%, 2%, and 0.9%, respectively. While small in absolute terms, all these changes reached statistical significance. Those labeled as TCFA and adaptive intimal thickening did not significantly change over 1 year. At the end of 3 years, patients with TCFA had an excess of 7 revascularizations in the imaged region compared with patients without TCFA. There was no difference in the rates of all‐cause and cardiovascular mortality or myocardial infarction in patients with versus without TCFA.

The study is unique in providing serial OCT observations of nonculprit, intermediate coronary stenoses in a predominantly stable population with chronic coronary syndrome. The investigators should be praised for managing the associated risks, logistical challenges, ethical concerns, and costs of conducting repeated invasive assessments of typically asymptomatic patients. Nonetheless, serial imaging studies generate complex data, and many challenges persist in relating the imaging findings with clinical outcomes.

Atherosclerosis is a chronic disease marked by alternating periods of activity and dormancy. The disease phases can be modified, and its progression delayed or even halted by the implementation of secondary preventative measures, their intensity, and the patient's long‐term compliance with them. Likewise, exacerbations can occur earlier in patients who fail to implement such measures. Consequently, determining the optimal time points for conducting observational serial imaging studies is inherently difficult. This dynamic behavior is partly reflected in the patient‐level analysis of PREDICTOR. Of the 42 patients with a TCFA at baseline, 32 persisted with TCFA in the matched vascular region at 1 year, and 10 transitioned to a more stable plaque phenotype. By the same token, 8 patients with stable plaque phenotypes at baseline developed a TCFA at 1 year.

Although atherosclerosis is an inflammatory disease with systemic manifestations, TCFA usually has a limited and focal distribution. In a pathologic work of 50 whole hearts analyzing a total of 10.9 m of coronary tissue, TCFA accounted for only 1.6% of the total coronary artery length in 33 patients dying of cardiovascular causes. Most of the TCFAs and ruptured plaques localized in the proximal third of the major coronary arteries, and in 92% of cases, these lesions clustered within 2 or fewer nonoverlapping 20‐mm segments. 9 This focal distribution affects the ability of studies that image randomly short coronary segments in single vessels to identify vulnerable plaques. In PREDICTOR, the matched imaged segments between baseline and 1‐year follow‐up measured only 33.2 mm and were in nontarget vessels or within the treated vessel but at least 5 mm apart from the culprit lesion.

The combination of the highest available resolution, the possibility to characterize various plaque morphologies (including TCFA), and the ability to resolve structural changes in the fibrous cap justifies the use of OCT by the PREDICTOR investigators. The OCT technology used in the current study provides a maximum of 75‐mm long pullback at a speed of 36 mm/s. Thus, acquiring adequate images requires at least 2.1 s of continuous blood clearance. To account for the contrast volume necessary to initiate blood clearance in the most distal part of the target region, it is typically necessary to use 10–12 mL of contrast per OCT acquisition. 10 The relatively short pullback length and concerns with contrast volume add difficulties in conducting full‐length, 3‐vessel serial OCT studies. Newer‐generation OCT systems able to scan longer vascular segments (100–110 mm) with faster speeds (100 mm/s) with minimal or no contrast use can potentially improve the feasibility of such studies. 11 , 12

The primary endpoint in PREDICTOR was the change in mean lumen area between baseline and 1 year. In this study, coronary segments with TCFA had small reductions in the primary endpoint of similar magnitude to the mean lumen area reductions seen in regions with more stable plaque phenotypes. This contrasts with patients with TCFA experiencing a 2.4‐fold increased risk of ischemia‐driven revascularization at the end of 3 years without increased risks of spontaneous myocardial infarctions or cardiac deaths. Whether TCFA lesions at baseline were at higher risk of developing flow‐limiting stenoses associated with anginal symptoms at follow‐up cannot be fully determined by the chosen primary endpoint; quantification of the mean lumen areas along the 33‐mm imaged segment underestimates the severity of focal stenoses that could have developed during follow‐up. These would be better captured by changes in the minimum lumen area. In this unblinded registry, the influence of prior knowledge of the underlying plaque morphology on subsequent revascularization decisions cannot be ignored. To complicate matters further, the number of patients with baseline TCFA who transitioned to a stable plaque phenotype was similar to patients with stable plaques at baseline who transitioned to TCFA during the 1‐year interval. The dynamic quantitative, morphological, and compositional changes that occurred from 1 to 3 years cannot be determined. Thus, it is unsurprising that despite many studies identifying high‐risk atherosclerotic plaques at 1 imaging time point, the estimated positive predictive value for the occurrence of a lesion‐level MACE is only modest. 13

Although lumen reduction can influence the rates of ischemia‐driven revascularization, plaque can progress with minimal lumen compromise due to adaptive vascular remodeling. Even in the absence of plaque progression, the same lesion can undergo compositional changes leading to more vulnerable or stable phenotypes. Thus, serial changes in plaque characteristics rather than lumen reduction can better inform the risk of MACE over time. In this regard, the thickness of a fibrous cap overlying a lipid pool/necrotic core is a key morphometric determinant of a future plaque rupture and has been used as the primary endpoint of serial OCT studies that assess the effects of pharmacotherapy on plaque stabilization. 6 , 7 Changes in the length of lipid‐rich plaques, the circumferential arc of lipids, and the density of macrophage accumulation and microvessels complement the risk assessment of lipid‐rich plaques by OCT. In the present study, the only morphometric parameter reported for TCFA lesions was a significant reduction in the lipid arc by an average of 20 degrees.

No single imaging modality provides all answers. OCT has limited vascular penetration and fails to quantify the atheroma burden, another strong determinant of clinical events. 14 , 15 Greyscale intravascular ultrasound is a better tool to quantify the atheroma burden but is imprecise for plaque compositional analysis. Near‐infrared spectroscopy can identify coronary lipids and quantify the lipid core burden index but does not resolve the overlying fibrous cap. While serial multimodality imaging studies are feasible, they bring technical and methodological challenges and add time and cost to the procedures. Newer‐generation systems combining more than 1 imaging modality in the same imaging catheter (eg, near‐infrared spectroscopy‐intravascular ultrasound, intravascular ultrasound‐OCT, and OCT‐intravascular ultrasound) are attractive techniques for a more comprehensive assessment of coronary atherosclerosis in future serial studies.

Vascular regions with low endothelial shear stress are known to stimulate proinflammatory, pro‐atherogenic, and prothrombotic properties, and are associated with plaque formation and progression. 16 , 17 A combination of plaque burden, endothelial shear stress, and plaque phenotype provides incremental value for predicting coronary plaque progression and increased plaque vulnerability in patients with nonobstructive coronary artery disease. 18

We are hopeful that advancements in multimodality, multiparameter imaging integrating quantitative, morphological, compositional, and mechanical information of atherosclerosis, with low‐risk, 3‐vessel scanning will dovetail with progress in genomics, artificial intelligence, and imaging processing techniques to generate a comprehensive picture of lesions and individuals at greatest risk. Imaging the dynamic changes of atherosclerosis and linking lesion‐level findings to quantifiable adverse clinical outcomes remains an uphill battle.
