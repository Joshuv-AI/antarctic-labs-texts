Functional Radiation Medicine: Rediscovering the Full Potential of Radiation Therapy
Authors: Jan Kriz, Ralph Mücke, Oliver Micke
Published: 2026

Radiation therapy (RT) was never intended exclusively for the treatment of cancer. In the years following Wilhelm Conrad Röntgen’s discovery of x-rays in 1895,1 physicians rapidly recognized that ionizing radiation possessed a variety of biological effects extending far beyond tumor destruction. During the first half of the twentieth century, RT became an established treatment for a remarkably broad spectrum of nonmalignant diseases, including inflammatory disorders, degenerative musculoskeletal conditions, dermatologic diseases, vascular malformations, endocrine disorders, and various functional abnormalities.2,3 At that time, radiation oncology was not divided into malignant and benign RT indications. Rather, radiation represented a versatile therapeutic tool whose application was guided by an experimental approach, clinical observation, and therapeutic success.

Beginning in the late 1950s, however, the landscape changed profoundly.4 The extraordinary progress achieved in cancer treatment, together with increasing fear of radiation-induced late effects and some developments of effective pharmacological alternatives, led to the gradual disappearance of many benign indications from routine clinical practice. In several countries, low-dose RT survived in specialized centers, whereas in others, it virtually disappeared. Nevertheless, decades of clinical experience and scientific observation were never lost.2

This therapeutic tradition was preserved particularly in Central Europe, where radiation oncologists continued to refine treatment concepts for degenerative and inflammatory disorders.5,6 During the past decade, however, functional radiation medicine has undergone a remarkable renaissance.7,8 North American centers have increasingly begun to investigate low-dose RT for benign diseases, especially osteoarthritis or tendinopathies,9 generating new clinical evidence and stimulating multidisciplinary collaboration.10,11 What was once regarded largely as a European field has now become an international area of scientific interest.12

This growing momentum has also become evident at the last ASTRO Annual Meeting. In 2025, the scientific program included dedicated educational sessions, scientific symposia, and presentations focusing on RT for nonmalignant diseases. The 2025 Presidential Symposium, held under the theme “Rediscovering Radiation Medicine and Exploring New Indications,” highlighted the expanding role of RT beyond cancer and emphasized musculoskeletal, neurologic, and cardiac applications. Building on this momentum, the 2026 ASTRO Annual Meeting will offer a full-day Functional Radiation Medicine Workshop and additional educational activities dedicated to nonmalignant diseases. Together, these developments illustrate that functional radiation medicine has become an established priority within the international radiation oncology community.8,12

The publication of this Special Issue in Advances in Radiation Oncology represents another landmark in this evolution by recognizing that these indications deserve the same scientific rigor, prospective evaluation, and multidisciplinary collaboration that characterize modern oncologic research. This Special Issue represents more than just a collection of individual studies. It reflects the growing international recognition of functional radiation medicine as an emerging scientific field within contemporary radiation oncology.8,12

Advances in imaging, treatment planning, image guidance, radiobiology, and radiation physics have fundamentally changed the therapeutic ratio of RT. An improved understanding of the anti-inflammatory, immunomodulatory, antifibrotic, and tissue-remodeling effects of irradiation has provided a biological explanation for many clinical observations that were once largely empirical.13,14 Functional radiation medicine is therefore not a return to historical practice. It is the application of twenty-first-century radiation oncology to an expanding set of indications.15

The issue opens with several important contributions addressing one of the most rapidly evolving indications for low-dose RT: osteoarthritis. Low-dose radiation therapy for peripheral joint osteoarthritis: Early U.S. community experience with pain, function, and medication outcomes presents encouraging real-world data from the United States, documenting improvements in pain, functional capacity, and medication use. Together with Low-Dose Radiation Therapy (LDRT) for Osteoarthritis: A Retrospective Study of 94 Patients and 136 Joints Treated, these investigations strengthen the growing international evidence supporting low-dose irradiation for degenerative joint disease.

The continuing refinement of treatment techniques is reflected in Low-Dose Radiotherapy for Osteoarthritis: Analysis of Preliminary Outcomes at Scripps Clinic and CT-Based Target Volume Delineation and Clinical Outcomes of Low-Dose Radiotherapy for Knee Osteoarthritis. These studies demonstrate how contemporary imaging, computed tomography-based target delineation, and modern treatment planning have transformed a century-old therapeutic concept into a modern precision treatment.

The musculoskeletal section extends beyond osteoarthritis. Total Knee Revision for Arthrofibrosis Using Modern Rotating Hinged Knee Arthroplasty Augmented with Preoperative RT explores the prevention of excessive fibrosis after revision arthroplasty and illustrates the expanding role of RT in modulating pathological wound healing and tissue remodeling.

Dermatologic and connective tissue disorders are also represented by several equally important contributions. Low-Dose Radiotherapy in Treatment of Recalcitrant Hidradenitis Suppurativa addresses one of the most challenging chronic inflammatory diseases encountered in clinical practice. External Beam Radiotherapy for Granuloma Faciale: A Contemporary Approach and Clinical Lessons from a Refractory Case reminds us that carefully delivered RT may provide effective treatment for rare disorders when conventional therapies fail. Likewise, Quality of Life and Toxicities Following Whole-Palm Versus Involved-Field Radiation Therapy for Dupuytren’s Disease emphasizes patient-reported outcomes and quality of life, outcomes that have become increasingly important in modern RT. Complementing these studies, RT for Sialorrhea in Patients with Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS): A Case Series and Critical Review demonstrates the potential of RT to improve quality of life in patients with progressive neurological disease.

The central nervous system contributions showcase the expanding role of stereotactic techniques in benign disease. Stereotactic Body Radiotherapy for Symptomatic Benign Spinal Tumors demonstrates the effectiveness of highly precise radiation delivery for benign neoplasms. Evaluation of Hemorrhage Risk and Predictors Following Stereotactic Radiosurgery for Cerebral Arteriovenous Malformations and Clinical Outcomes of Fractionated RT for Spinal Intramedullary Arteriovenous Malformations provide valuable data for optimizing patient selection and treatment strategies in these rare vascular disorders. Accelerated Volume-Staged Stereotactic Radiosurgery for Large Cerebral Arteriovenous Malformations further illustrates how technological innovation continues to expand therapeutic possibilities for complex lesions.

RT is also entering fields that until recently seemed far removed from traditional radiation oncology. Stereotactic Arrythmia Radiotherapy in a Second: Ultra-High Dose Rate Spread-out Bragg Peak Proton Therapy for Breath-Hold Stereotactic Arrhythmia Radioablation represents the convergence of stereotactic body RT, proton therapy, FLASH Radiotherapie concepts, and cardiac electrophysiology. Similarly, Intravascular Radiation Delays Recurrence of Recalcitrant Coronary In-Stent Restenosis reminds us that vascular RT continues to provide valuable therapeutic options for carefully selected patients.

An additional manuscript broadens the scientific perspective even further. Endobronchial High Dose rate Brachytherapy for Post-Transplant Associated Bronchial Stenosis explores an innovative application of brachytherapy in complex airway disease. Finally, Can LDRT Aid in Chronic Subdural Hematoma Management? A Historical Review reminds us that history often serves as a catalyst for innovation. Many therapeutic concepts considered novel today have historical precedents that deserve renewed evaluation using contemporary technology and modern scientific methodology.

The future of functional radiation medicine will depend upon continued scientific collaboration. Because several benign diseases are uncommon at individual institutions, multicenter studies, international registries, harmonized treatment protocols, and long-term follow-up will be essential to generate the level of evidence required for future clinical guidelines.12 Functional radiation medicine has reached a stage at which international cooperation is no longer desirable but indispensable.

This Special Issue documents the remarkable convergence of historical experience, technological innovation, biological understanding, and international collaboration. More importantly, it reflects a growing recognition that radiation oncology possesses therapeutic potential extending far beyond the treatment of cancer. By bringing together experts from multiple disciplines and countries, Advances has provided an important platform for a field whose time has truly come. Functional radiation medicine is no longer rediscovering its past. It is redefining an important part of the future of radiation oncology.2,8
