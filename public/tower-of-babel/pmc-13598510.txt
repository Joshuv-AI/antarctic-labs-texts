Platelet-rich plasma in the treatment of spinal cord injury: Mechanisms, efficacy, and clinical translation
Authors: An-tong Hu, Zhi-jiang Chen, Xin Zhang, Yue Yu, Wen-jun Zhang, Liu Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSpinal cord injury (SCI) is a devastating condition of the central nervous system characterized by complex pathophysiological processes, including primary mechanical damage and secondary injury cascades, ultimately leading to permanent neurological deficits. Current standard treatments—including surgical decompression and high-dose methylprednisolone—offer limited therapeutic benefits and fail to effectively promote neuroregeneration. Platelet-rich plasma (PRP), an autologous biological derivative enriched with multiple growth factors, has demonstrated remarkable potential in tissue repair and has recently emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy for SCI. This comprehensive review systematically examines the pathophysiological characteristics of SCI and the limitations of existing treatments, with particular emphasis on the multifaceted mechanisms of PRP in SCI repair. These mechanisms include anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects through activation of the Nrf2 signaling pathway, regulation of apoptosis, promotion of angiogenesis, and stimulation of axonal regeneration. Preclinical studies and preliminary clinical observations have consistently demonstrated that PRP administration significantly improves neurological functional recovery following SCI. However, substantial challenges impede clinical translation, including a lack of standardization in PRP preparation, an incomplete understanding of underlying mechanisms, limited efficacy of monotherapy, and a scarcity of high-quality clinical evidence. Future directions emphasize the development of combination strategies integrating PRP with biomaterials such as hydrogels or stem cells, which may represent the key to successful clinical implementation.Keywords: SCI, PRP, Neuroregeneration, Growth factors, Hydrogel, Clinical translation

Introduction

SCI constitutes one of the most devastating neurological conditions, affecting approximately 0.9 million individuals worldwide annually and imposing substantial physical, psychological, and socioeconomic burdens on patients, families, and healthcare systems [1]. The pathological sequelae of SCI involve a complex, dynamic cascade encompassing primary mechanical disruption of neural tissue followed by secondary injury mechanisms—including ischemia, excitotoxicity, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, inflammatory infiltration, and glial scar formation—that collectively exacerbate tissue damage and create an inhibitory microenvironment hostile to spontaneous regeneration [2]. Recent single-cell transcriptomic analyses have further revealed the cellular heterogeneity and dynamic molecular changes that occur during the acute and subacute phases of SCI, providing new insights into potential therapeutic targets [3].

Despite decades of research and advances in clinical management, current therapeutic strategies remain largely palliative rather than restorative. Contemporary standard care focuses on early surgical decompression to stabilize the vertebral column and alleviate mechanical compression, accompanied by pharmacological interventions such as high-dose methylprednisolone administered within a narrow therapeutic window [4]. However, the use of methylprednisolone has large side effects, making its routine use increasingly controversial [4]. Subsequent rehabilitation programs, while essential for maximizing residual function and preventing secondary complications, cannot fundamentally address the underlying failure of axonal regeneration and synaptic reconnection. This therapeutic gap underscores an urgent need for innovative approaches capable of actively modulating the injury microenvironment and promoting endogenous repair mechanisms [5].

In this context, regenerative medicine strategies leveraging bioactive factors have garnered considerable attention [5]. Autologous PRP is prepared by whole blood density gradient centrifugation, and the platelet concentration in the preparation can reach 3 to 5 times the baseline level of peripheral venous blood. In the clinical application stage, calcium chloride or thrombin is generally used as an activator to promote the degranulation of platelet alpha particles and release a large number of cytokine and active protein complexes stored in the cell. This active mixture can regulate core physiological processes such as cell proliferation, directed migration, differentiation phenotype and extracellular matrix synthesis. With its multiple biological effects, PRP has now become a biological therapeutic with great application potential in the field of tissue damage repair. The clinical utility of PRP has been well established across diverse fields including orthopedics, sports medicine, oral and maxillofacial surgery, and chronic wound management [6]. More recently, the potential application of PRP in central nervous system repair—particularly following SCI—has emerged as a focus of intensive investigation [7].

Pathophysiology of SCI and limitations of current therapeutic approaches

Primary and secondary injury mechanisms

The pathological evolution of SCI unfolds as a biphasic process comprising primary and secondary injury phases. Primary injury results from instantaneous mechanical forces—including compression, contusion, transection, or distraction—that directly disrupt neuronal membranes, axonal continuity, and the microvascular architecture, causing immediate hemorrhage and tissue necrosis at the lesion epicenter [2].

The secondary injury phase, which evolves over hours to weeks, represents a complex biochemical cascade that progressively expands the initial lesion and constitutes the primary target for therapeutic intervention [2]. This multifaceted process encompasses a series of interrelated pathological events that collectively exacerbate tissue damage and create an inhibitory microenvironment hostile to spontaneous regeneration. Emerging concepts such as liquid–liquid phase separation have been implicated in the aggregation of pathological proteins and the dysregulation of cellular signaling after SCI, adding another layer of complexity to the secondary injury cascade [8].

Vascular dysfunction represents an early and critical component of secondary injury. Disruption of the microvasculature precipitates local ischemia, edema, and breakdown of the blood-spinal cord barrier (BSCB), thereby perpetuating a hypoxic microenvironment and facilitating infiltration of inflammatory cells [9]. This vascular compromise not only deprives neural tissue of essential oxygen and nutrients but also permits the entry of blood-derived factors that amplify local inflammation.

Excitotoxicity subsequently occurs through excessive release of excitatory amino acids, particularly glutamate, which triggers sustained activation of NMDA and AMPA receptors. This pathological overstimulation leads to dysregulated calcium influx, mitochondrial calcium overload, and activation of proteolytic enzymes, ultimately culminating in neuronal death [10].

Concurrently, oxidative stress emerges as a dominant pathological mechanism. The generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and reactive nitrogen species (RNS) overwhelms endogenous antioxidant defenses, causing lipid peroxidation, protein carbonylation, and DNA damage that propagate cellular injury [11]. This oxidative damage is closely intertwined with inflammatory responses, wherein recruitment and activation of neutrophils, microglia, and macrophages release pro-inflammatory cytokines—including interleukin-1β (IL-1β), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and interleukin-6 (IL-6)—that amplify tissue damage while simultaneously initiating repair processes [12]. Advanced single-cell transcriptomic studies have mapped the temporal dynamics of immune cell populations and their gene expression profiles, revealing novel subsets of microglia and macrophages that may serve as therapeutic targets [3].

These pathological insults collectively trigger apoptosis, a form of programmed cell death mediated by activation of caspase cascades and mitochondrial release of cytochrome c. Apoptosis eliminates oligodendrocytes and neurons in regions remote from the primary injury site, contributing to delayed neurodegeneration and demyelination that extends well beyond the initial lesion boundaries [13]. Mitophagy serves as a key mitochondrial quality control mechanism that determines neuronal survival following traumatic spinal cord insults [14]. Related studies have confirmed that FUNDC1-mediated mitophagy can effectively protect spinal cord neurons from secondary injury [15].

Ultimately, reactive astrocytes proliferate and undergo morphological changes, forming a dense physical barrier—the glial scar—that sequesters the lesion while simultaneously impeding axonal regeneration. Concomitantly, upregulation of chondroitin sulfate proteoglycans (CSPGs) and myelin-associated inhibitors (Nogo-A, MAG, OMgp) creates a potent chemical barrier to axonal sprouting and elongation, further compromising the regenerative capacity of the injured spinal cord [16]. Specific molecular regulators such as SIX1 have been shown to promote pro-inflammatory microglial polarization and exacerbate secondary injury progression [17]. Beyond glial scarring, TGFβ-driven fibrotic scar formation presents an additional physical barrier that severely limits axonal regeneration and functional recovery [18].

Imitations of current clinical management

Contemporary clinical management of SCI remains constrained by fundamental limitations that underscore the inadequacy of existing therapeutic paradigms. Surgical decompression, while essential for spinal stabilization and prevention of ongoing mechanical injury, cannot reverse established neurochemical damage or promote regeneration of disrupted neural circuits [4]. The procedure addresses mechanical compression but leaves the complex molecular pathology of secondary injury unmitigated, representing a necessary but insufficient component of comprehensive SCI care.

Pharmacological interventions, notably high-dose methylprednisolone administered according to the National Acute Spinal Cord Injury Study (NASCIS) protocols, have demonstrated modest efficacy within a restricted 8-h therapeutic window. However, these marginal neuroprotective benefits are counterbalanced by significant risks, including immunosuppression, hyperglycemia, myopathy, and gastrointestinal hemorrhage, leading to widespread controversy regarding their risk-benefit profile and a lack of consensus on routine clinical adoption [4]. The narrow therapeutic window and substantial adverse effects have limited the translational impact of even the most extensively studied neuroprotective agent.

Rehabilitation strategies, while invaluable for functional optimization and prevention of secondary complications, operate through mechanisms of neuroplasticity and compensatory adaptation rather than true regeneration, cannot address the fundamental pathological substrate of neuronal loss and axonal disconnection [4]. Consequently, patients are left with permanent neurological deficits despite optimal rehabilitative care.

Collectively, these therapeutic limitations highlight a critical gap in the current clinical armamentarium: the absence of interventions capable of actively modulating the inhibitory microenvironment, promoting neuroregeneration, and restoring disrupted neural connectivity. This unmet need provides the rationale for exploring biologically based therapeutic strategies, such as PRP, that target the multifaceted pathophysiology of secondary injury and promote endogenous repair mechanisms.

PRP: overview and applications in tissue repair

Preparation and characterization

PRP is obtained through differential centrifugation of autologous whole blood, a process that exploits density differences to separate cellular components based on their sedimentation rates. The standard preparation protocol typically involves two centrifugation steps: an initial “soft spin” to separate erythrocytes and leukocytes from platelet-containing plasma, followed by a “hard spin” to concentrate platelets at the bottom of the tube, yielding a platelet pellet that can be resuspended in a smaller plasma volume to achieve the desired platelet concentration [19].

Based on cellular composition, PRP preparations are classified into several distinct categories. Pure platelet-rich plasma (P-PRP) contains minimal leukocytes, while leukocyte-rich platelet-rich plasma (L-PRP) retains significant white blood cell content. Additionally, platelet-rich fibrin (PRF) preparations incorporate a fibrin matrix that provides sustained growth factor release and serves as a three-dimensional scaffold [19]. This compositional heterogeneity has profound implications for biological activity, as leukocytes contribute both beneficial antimicrobial effects and potentially detrimental pro-inflammatory mediators that may influence therapeutic outcomes depending on the clinical context [6].

The therapeutic efficacy of PRP derives primarily from the concentrated growth factors released upon platelet activation. Following exposure to activators such as calcium chloride, thrombin, or endogenous collagen, platelets undergo degranulation, releasing stored α-granule contents that orchestrate key regenerative processes [19]. These bioactive molecules include platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), which promotes cell proliferation, chemotaxis, and angiogenesis; transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), which regulates inflammation, extracellular matrix synthesis, and scar formation; vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), which stimulates angiogenesis and modulates vascular permeability; insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1), which supports cell survival, metabolism, and neurite outgrowth; epidermal growth factor (EGF), which promotes epithelialization and cellular proliferation; fibroblast growth factor (FGF), which stimulates angiogenesis and fibroblast proliferation; and hepatocyte growth factor (HGF), which modulates inflammation and tissue regeneration. The synergistic action of these growth factors enables PRP to exert pleiotropic effects on cellular behavior and tissue microenvironment, forming the biological basis for its therapeutic applications.

Established applications in tissue repair

The robust biological activities of PRP have supported its widespread clinical application across multiple domains of tissue repair. In orthopedics and sports medicine, PRP has been extensively employed for treatment of tendinopathies—including lateral epicondylitis, patellar tendinopathy, and Achilles tendinopathy—with randomized controlled trials demonstrating significant improvements in pain and functional outcomes compared to placebo [6]. Intra-articular PRP injections for knee osteoarthritis have shown superior efficacy compared to hyaluronic acid in meta-analyses, particularly in younger patients with milder degenerative changes [5].

In oral and maxillofacial surgery, PRP accelerates bone regeneration in alveolar defects, enhances integration of dental implants, and promotes healing following tooth extraction [19]. Chronic wound management represents another well-established application, with PRP gel stimulating granulation tissue formation, epithelialization, and neovascularization in diabetic foot ulcers, pressure ulcers, and venous leg ulcers [4]. Notably, a randomized study in SCI patients demonstrated that autologous PRP significantly improved epithelization, granulation, and neovascularization of pressure ulcers compared to conventional hydrogel dressing, confirming PRP's pro-reparative capacity even in the context of SCI [20].

These diverse applications collectively validate the fundamental principle that localized delivery of concentrated autologous growth factors can effectively modulate tissue microenvironment and promote cellular repair processes, providing a rationale for extending this approach to central nervous system injuries [7].

Therapeutic mechanisms and efficacy of PRP in SCI

In the context of SCI, PRP functions not merely as a growth factor delivery system but as a multifaceted biological therapeutic capable of simultaneously addressing multiple pathological processes characteristic of secondary injury (Fig. 1). Extensive preclinical investigations have elucidated the complex mechanisms through which PRP exerts its reparative effects, supported by functional evidence of neurological recovery [5].

Functional mechanism of PRP in SCI repair

Anti-inflammatory regulatory effect: PRP enriches potent anti-inflammatory factors such as IL-10 and TGF -β, which can inhibit the excessive inflammatory cascade reaction after SCI, reduce the release of pro-inflammatory factors, alleviate the secondary inflammatory damage of neurons and glial cells in the damaged area, and lay a stable local microenvironment for nerve repair.

Promoting angiogenesis and BSCB protective effects: Vasoactive factors such as VEGF in PRP can promote the proliferation of spinal cord endothelial cells, induce neovascularization in the injured area, rebuild local microcirculation, improve the ischemic and hypoxic state of damaged spinal cord tissue, protect BSCB, and provide basic support for nerve repair.

Neurotrophic effects: PRP contains various neurotrophic proteins such as NGF and BDNF, which act on neurons, improve the survival rate of damaged spinal cord neurons, inhibit neuronal apoptosis, promote axonal regeneration and extension, and prevent secondary neurodegenerative changes after SCI.

Organizational repair and remodeling effects: Various growth factors released by platelets induce targeted differentiation of endogenous stem cells at the site of injury, regulate extracellular matrix remodeling, inhibit excessive proliferation of glial scars, and accelerate the repair and structural reconstruction of parenchymal tissue in the SCI area.

The above four pathways work together to achieve neuroprotection, microcirculation reconstruction, and damaged tissue repair, forming the key core functional role of PRP in treating SCI.

PRP exerts pleiotropic therapeutic effects in SCI through coordinated modulation of multiple pathological processes characteristic of secondary injury, operating in a temporal and spatial hierarchy to collectively establish a microenvironment conducive to tissue preservation and neural repair. The anti-inflammatory and antioxidant actions of PRP represent an early and critical mechanism, as studies demonstrate that PRP treatment significantly reduces levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and TNF-α while elevating anti-inflammatory mediators including IL-10 [4]. Specialized pro-resolving lipid mediators actively promote the resolution of inflammation and may act synergistically with PRP to create a pro-regenerative microenvironment [21]. In a mouse model of SCI, PRP administration increased superoxide dismutase and reduced glutathione activities while decreasing malondialdehyde content, indicating enhanced antioxidant capacity [11]. Mechanistically, these effects are mediated through activation of the nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) signaling pathway, a master regulator of antioxidant response, with PRP treatment upregulating Nrf2 nuclear translocation and downregulating its cytosolic inhibitor Keap1, leading to transcriptional activation of antioxidant enzyme genes [22]. Additionally, PRP modulates the TGF-β/Smad pathway to attenuate pathological astrocyte activation, thereby reducing the contribution of reactive gliosis to the inhibitory microenvironment [23].

Following this initial modulation of inflammation and oxidative stress, PRP exerts significant cytoprotective effects on neurons and oligodendrocytes within the injury penumbra, preserving cellular substrates essential for functional recovery. Human umbilical cord blood-derived PRP administration in SCI rats downregulated the apoptosis executioner caspase-3 while modulating expression of heat shock protein 70, a molecular chaperone with neuroprotective functions [4]. Studies have further demonstrated that PRP-derived exosomes can modulate the Bax/Bcl 2 ratio, reducing apoptosis and enhancing neuronal survival [24]. MicroRNA-mediated mechanisms, such as miR-130a-3p targeting PTEN, have also been shown to regulate neural stem cell differentiation and promote functional recovery, suggesting potential synergistic pathways that could be harnessed in combination with PRP [25].

Concurrently with neuroprotection, PRP promotes angiogenesis, a critical prerequisite for effective neural repair, as restoration of vascular perfusion delivers oxygen and nutrients while removing metabolic waste from the injury site. Through its rich content of VEGF and PDGF, PRP potently stimulates neovascularization within the injured spinal cord via activation of the hypoxia-inducible factor-1α/VEGF signaling pathway, driving endothelial cell proliferation, migration, and tube formation to establish new vascular networks that support tissue survival and regeneration [26]. Furthermore, PRP-derived exosomes have been shown to promote BSCB repair and reduce vascular permeability, thereby limiting the extravasation of inflammatory cells and neurotoxic factors [24]. Mechanotransduction pathways involving Piezo 1 have recently been identified as key regulators of angiogenesis after SCI, and targeting these pathways may enhance the pro-angiogenic effects of PRP [27].

The establishment of a vascularized and protected microenvironment sets the stage for PRP-mediated axonal regeneration and remyelination. PRP creates a permissive environment for axonal growth through multiple molecular mechanisms that both relieve inhibition and promote intrinsic growth capacity, downregulating expression of axonal growth inhibitors including myelin-associated glycoprotein and pathological tau isoforms while modulating signaling through GSK3β and the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway, a central regulator of cell growth and protein synthesis [5]. Concurrently, PRP-derived growth factors support survival and differentiation of oligodendrocyte precursor cells, promoting remyelination of spared axons and restoration of saltatory conduction necessary for functional neurological recovery.

Finally, when combined with biomaterial scaffolds, PRP demonstrates the capacity to regulate reactive astrogliosis in a manner that balances containment with permissiveness. By attenuating pathological astrocyte activation and modulating CSPG deposition, PRP-based therapies may reduce the physical and chemical barrier properties of the glial scar while preserving its beneficial function in lesion containment and inflammatory isolation [23] (Fig. 2 and Table 1). This nuanced modulation of scar formation represents a significant advantage over approaches that completely abrogate astrogliosis, which may compromise tissue integrity and exacerbate inflammation, and exemplifies the multifaceted therapeutic profile of PRP in SCI.

Therapeutic efficacy: from preclinical models to clinical observations

The molecular and cellular effects of PRP consistently translate into significant functional recovery in animal models of SCI. Multiple studies employing standardized locomotor assessments—including the Basso, Beattie, Bresnahan (BBB) scale for rats and Basso Mouse Scale (BMS) for mice—have demonstrated that PRP-treated animals exhibit substantially improved hindlimb motor function compared to untreated controls [5]. Functional improvements typically become evident by 2–4 weeks post-injury and persist throughout observation periods extending to 8 weeks or longer.

A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis comprehensively evaluated preclinical evidence for PRP efficacy in SCI [28]. Pooled analysis of seven studies demonstrated that PRP administration significantly improved motor function in animals with SCI (standardized mean difference [SMD] = 1.5; 95% CI, 0.9–2.1%; P < 0.0001). Subgroup analysis revealed that PRP efficacy was more pronounced in moderate injuries compared to severe injuries (meta-regression coefficient = −1.36; P < 0.035), suggesting that injury severity may influence therapeutic responsiveness. Importantly, PRP derived from both peripheral blood and umbilical cord blood demonstrated efficacy, with no significant difference between autologous and allogeneic sources [28]. Alternative autologous biotherapies, such as major ozonated autohemotherapy, have also shown promise in preclinical SCI models by inhibiting oxidative stress and inflammation, highlighting the potential of blood-derived treatments [29].

Preliminary clinical observations have provided encouraging signals supporting the translation of PRP therapy to human SCI patients. Studies on pressure ulcer management in SCI patients have demonstrated that local PRP application significantly improves wound healing parameters, including epithelization, granulation tissue formation, and neovascularization, compared to conventional dressings [20]. A 2024 case report described a patient with cervical SCI who received two intrathecal injections of PRP combined with adipose-derived stem cells at a 15-day interval [30]. Following treatment, the patient reported enhanced lower extremity control, achieved unsupported standing, and ultimately regained independent ambulation. While limited to a single case, this report provides proof-of-concept that PRP-based approaches may promote clinically meaningful neurological recovery in humans. Cord blood-derived PRP (CB-PRP) has also attracted attention due to its unique growth factor profile, reduced immunogenicity, and ready availability from banked sources, though clinical experience remains limited [31].

Combination therapy using PRP and adipose-derived stem cells elicits synergistic effects on tissue repair and wound healing [32]. PRP enhances the viability, proliferation, and angiogenic potential of adipose-derived stem cells both in vitro and in vivo [33]. A study on the safety and functional outcomes of the combination of autologous PRP and bone marrow aspirate concentrate in the treatment of patients with SCI. The Oswestry Disability Index significantly decreased in 7 patients with SCI compared to pre-treatment levels, with improved daily activity capabilities. Among them, some patients with incomplete spinal cord injuries showed marked improvements in motor and sensory levels, as well as enhanced lower limb muscle strength, bowel and bladder function, and alleviation of neuropathic pain. The study revealed that intrathecal/intravenous combined administration is safe and feasible, with no significant complications and potential clinical benefits, but larger-scale clinical trials are needed to further evaluate this treatment approach [34]. As a second-generation platelet concentrate, platelet-rich fibrin provides a natural fibrin scaffold for sustained growth factor delivery and has been widely adopted in regenerative medicine applications [35]. After 4 weeks of intervention with autologous platelet rich fibrin (PRF) membrane in patients with incomplete SCI combined with chronic refractory pressure injury in the sacrococcygeal region, the patient's refractory pressure ulcer achieved complete epithelialization healing, vigorous granulation tissue growth, complete wound closure, and no adverse reactions. It reveals that autologous PRF fibrin membrane can be used as a new biological dressing for refractory pressure sores in patients with SCI, significantly accelerating the repair of chronic wounds [36] (Table 2).

Research on PRP in clinical trials of SCI and its complications.

Novel 3D-printed scaffolds have been developed to support structural repair in the injured spinal cord [38]. Multifunctional hydrogels incorporating polydatin show enhanced antibacterial and neuroregenerative properties for SCI treatment [39]. Injectable hydrogels based on platelet-rich fibrin and hyaluronic acid offer a promising platform for minimally invasive therapy [40].

Although these studies have verified the potential benefits of PRP treatment, the results obtained between different studies are currently heterogeneous. The core source is the high heterogeneity of PRP preparation and dosing schedules, as well as platelet and leukocyte composition, activation method and timing of administration. All of these may affect treatment outcomes and are key reasons limiting horizontal pooled comparisons of different studies. First, prepare for differences in centrifugation schemes. The secondary centrifugation speed, duration, and commercial separation kits used in different studies are not uniform, and the platelet enrichment factor spans 2 to 8 times that of basic peripheral blood. Too high a concentration of platelets will release a large number of pro-inflammatory mediators and aggravate the inflammatory response in the injured area. If insufficient enrichment, the total amount of growth factors will not reach the treatment threshold, directly causing differentiation of efficacy. Second, white blood cell component differences (LR-PRP/LP-PRP). Leukocyte-rich PRP (LR-PRP) contains a large number of granulocytes, which can amplify secondary inflammatory damage during the acute phase of SCI. Leukocyte-depleted PRP (LP-PRP) has warmer anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective effects. The applicability of the two in acute and chronic SCI is completely different, which is an important reason for the inconsistent results of similar studies. Third, the activation method is different. The mainstream solutions are divided into in vitro thrombin/calcium chloride pre-activation and in situ activation of tissue collagen in vivo. In vitro activation can release cytokines instantaneously, but active factors degrade rapidly, and in situ activation can achieve long-term sustained release of growth factors. The two have different time-effectiveness, resulting in inconsistent short-term and long-term follow-up indicators. Fourth: The timing of drug administration and intervention is inconsistent. Existing research intervention windows cover the hyperacute period (within 24 h), acute period (1 - 7 days), subacute period (1 - 4 weeks), and old chronic spinal cord injuries. PRP mainly suppresses secondary SCI in the acute phase, and only plays a role in repairing nerve axons in the chronic phase. The intervention timing is not uniform and cannot be combined to compare the efficacy. Fifth, the difference in route of administration. Administration methods include intrathecal injection, local injection, ultrasound-targeted injection and intravenous systemic administration. Intrathecal administration can reach spinal cord lesions directly, with the highest local concentration. Peripheral injection only relies on systemic circulation to take effect, and differences in administration routes cause significant differences in effect size.

Based on the above dimensions, there is still a lack of unified standardized operating procedures for PRP in the field of SCI at present, and there are huge differences in efficacy under different trial schemes. Clinical applications cannot simply copy a single research conclusion. In the future, there will be a need for randomized controlled trials with large sample sizes, unified preparation protocols, standardized two-step centrifugation preparation, LR-PRP/LP-PRP selection criteria, and uniform timing and route of administration to eliminate protocol heterogeneity, further clarify the applicable population and optimal intervention protocol for PRP, and improve the reproducibility of subsequent clinical trials.

Clinical challenges and future directions

Despite compelling preclinical evidence and promising preliminary clinical observations, the widespread clinical application of PRP for SCI faces substantial challenges that must be addressed through coordinated research efforts.

Current challenges

The clinical translation of PRP therapy for SCI is impeded by several interrelated challenges that must be addressed before widespread adoption becomes feasible.

PRP clinical operation plan lacks a unified standardized system

The lack of a standardized protocol for PRP preparation and identification is the most fundamental obstacle because of the substantial differences between studies in centrifuge parameters, final platelet concentration, white blood cell content, activation method and final formulation [6]. This variability produces PRP preparations with widely different biological compositions and activities, seriously compromising the comparability of studies and the repeatability of treatment effects. At present, there is no standardized protocol for PRP preparation and intervention for traumatic SCI in the industry, which is the core reason why it is difficult to replicate clinical efficacy. At the preparation level, one-step centrifugation and two-step gradient centrifugation schemes are used in parallel, and the range of centrifugation speed, centrifugation time, anticoagulant selection, and platelet enrichment ratio is extremely large [41]. There is a significant gap in cell enrichment efficiency between commercial preparation kits. At the clinical intervention level, the selection of drug administration intervention time window, in vitro platelet activation method, intrathecal/paravertebral/venous administration routes, etc. depends on clinical experience. LP-PRP is suitable for acute SCI and LR-PRP is suitable for chronic repair period. There is no industry consensus. At the same time, the neurological function evaluation indicators and follow-up time nodes selected in various studies are not uniform, making it impossible to form a unified clinical efficacy database, which greatly limits the promotion of clinical plans. Therefore, in view of this lack of standardization, it is necessary to standardize the establishment of a special two-step gradient centrifugation preparation for SCI, and clarify the criteria for platelet enrichment fold and LP-PRP/LR-PRP staging selection. Match the corresponding in vitro activation pattern and targeted drug delivery path according to the duration of injury; uniformly use the ASIA neurological function score as the core endpoint indicator, and set standardized follow-up nodes to eliminate trial heterogeneity and improve the replicability and promotion value of clinical plans.

Lack of full-process quality control system and insufficient stability of preparation efficacy

The individualized properties of autologous PRP preparations pose quality control problems. The patient's own underlying diseases (such as diabetes, advanced age, anti-platelet drugs, hyperlipidemia) directly affect the activity of autologous platelets. With the same preparation scheme, the concentration of PRP factor in different patients varies greatly, and there is no personalized quality control threshold. These reasons can lead to a significant decrease in patients ‘ability to degranulation and release growth factors, which cannot offset fluctuations in efficacy caused by individual differences. Most medical institutions do not carry out ex-factory testing of PRP, lack platelet concentration, white blood cell count, and core growth factor (such as BDNF, TGF-β, and VEGF) content testing indicators, there are no pass/fail release standards, and the qualification of preparations cannot be quantified.

In addition, PRP is an open in vitro preparation. Intrathecal injection requires extremely high levels of sterility. Aseptic preparation specifications have not yet been implemented. The open preparation model poses a risk of central infection. Currently, there is no unified sterile operation specification for PRP for central injection. There is a lack of unified standards for the storage temperature and effective time of activated PRP, which further increases the risk of clinical application. Therefore, establishing a complete safety monitoring plan, performing preoperative sterility testing, and monitoring headache, fever, and meningeal irritation after surgery to eliminate aseptic inflammation will help reduce risk factors.

Policies for regulatory supervision and clinical application

The use of PRP in SCI faces multiple regulatory barriers. First of all, the approved drug regulatory indications for all commercial PRP centrifugal preparation medical devices on the market are limited to peripheral tendon, joint and soft tissue repair. The use of it for intrathecal injection to intervene in SCI is not protected by medical device approval regulations, and clinical application has clear medical compliance risks. Secondly, PRP preparations themselves are not controlled according to drugs or cell therapy products, and lack corresponding regulatory rules on drug dose, toxicology, and administration methods, making it difficult to form unified clinical application specifications. In addition, the characteristics of autologous PRP prepared in a single person make it impossible to achieve drug-based batch sample retention and quality traceability. It lacks an independent pharmacovigilance system, and long-term safety cannot be included in normalized supervision.

Therefore, clinical compliance risks can be avoided by improving the filing of off-label drugs, upgrading the preparation environment, and establishing an individualized patient traceability monitoring system. Introduce special toxicological control rules and unify regulatory standards. Clinical trials adopt a central ethical filing model and a stepped research design to reduce the difficulty of implementation. In the long term, through the development of allogeneic standardized PRP preparations, the regulatory shortcomings caused by the individualized preparation of autologous PRP will be completely broken down and the standardized clinical transformation of this therapy will be promoted.

The existing clinical trial evidence level is insufficient, and there are obvious shortcomings in research design

The current human clinical trial system supporting clinical transformation has multiple structural flaws. The number of PRP clinical trials targeting spinal parenchymal nerve repair is scarce, and most studies only focus on peripheral complications such as rotator cuff injury and pressure ulcers secondary to SCI, rather than core central nervous function reconstruction. The evidence base for PRP treatment is still mainly preclinical. Human studies are mainly based on single-center small sample trials and retrospective case series, but the data is insufficient to draw clear conclusions. There is no multi-center, large-sample Phase III randomized controlled double-blind trial, and the overall level of evidence-based medicine evidence is low, which is not enough to form clinical guideline-level recommendations.

Existing studies generally have problems with confusing enrollment criteria. For example, subjects with acute phase, chronic phase, complete and incomplete SCI are enrolled in the mixed enrollment. There is no hierarchical design based on injury time and ASIA classification. Baseline confounding variables are not strictly matched. External interference factors such as rehabilitation intervention are difficult to eliminate. There were huge differences in PRP preparation scheme, route of administration, activation method, and frequency of intervention between trials, and the heterogeneity between studies was extremely high, making it impossible to carry out pooled analysis. At the same time, the efficacy endpoint indicators selected by each study are not uniform, the follow-up period spans a large span, the proportion of subjective scale evaluations is too high, and there is a lack of unified and objective quantitative imaging indicators. The current follow-up cycle is generally less than 1 year, and there is a lack of long-term complication monitoring plans as long as several years.

In addition, most clinical interventions use a composite plan of PRP combined with stem cells and rehabilitation training, which cannot strip off the synergistic intervention effect. Clinical trials that separately demonstrate the efficacy of PRP itself are scarce, further limiting the accurate determination of the clinical value of this therapy. Therefore, a unified efficacy evaluation system includes ASIA injury grading, long-term follow-up motor/sensory scores, residual urine volume in the bladder (neurogenic bladder), inflammatory factors (IL-10/TNF-α) and quantitative imaging evidence. Strict design of randomized controlled trials, sufficient sample size, appropriate control groups, standardized outcome indicators and long-term follow-up are crucial for clinical efficacy judgment.

Future directions and strategies

Current PRP monotherapy has limited efficacy and lacks combination treatment options. In animal experiments, PRP combined with stem cells or biological scaffolds is significantly better than PRP alone. Combining PRP with stem cells leverages complementary mechanisms of neuroprotection and regeneration [30]. PRP combined with adipose-derived stem cells effectively improves pressure injury healing [32].

The integration of PRP with biomaterial scaffolds represents the most extensively investigated approach for overcoming the inherent limitations of PRP monotherapy. Hydrogels enable sustained growth factor release, conformal lesion filling, and structural support for axonal regeneration [42]. Injectable alginate–chitosan hydrogels demonstrate significant promise for acute SCI repair [43]. Chitosan-based biomaterials are widely investigated for neural tissue engineering and SCI repair due to their excellent biocompatibility [44]. Advanced tissue engineering strategies including aligned scaffolds and functionalized hydrogels continue to evolve toward clinical translation [45]. Longitudinal preclinical studies have established optimal therapeutic windows and combination ratios for PRP with stem cells and biomaterials [[46], [47], [48]]. Clinical feasibility studies have confirmed safety profiles and established standardized administration protocols for human translation [[49], [50], [51]]. Comparative effectiveness research has delineated indications for PRP monotherapy versus combination strategies across injury severity spectrums [[52], [53], [54]].

Elucidation of BSCB dynamics has guided the development of barrier-protective adjuvants that enhance PRP retention and efficacy at the injury site [[55], [56], [57], [58]]. Molecular dissection of glial scar formation has identified combinatorial targets to mitigate inhibition while preserving tissue integrity [[59], [60], [61], [62], [63]]. Mechanistic insights into apoptotic cascades and mitochondrial homeostasis have optimized PRP dosing and delivery windows to maximize neuroprotection [[64], [65], [66]]. Emerging biomimetic platforms, including extracellular vesicle engineering and cell membrane coating technologies, have expanded the translational toolkit for PRP delivery [[67], [68], [69]]. PRP-derived extracellular vesicles offer enhanced stability, targeted delivery, and reduced immunogenicity compared with bulk PRP [24]. Translational pipelines integrating omics profiling and functional imaging have enabled precision design of next-generation PRP therapeutics [[70], [71], [72]]. Collectively, these advances position PRP-based combinatorial therapies as a viable clinical strategy to address unmet needs in SCI repair [37,[73], [74], [75], [76]]. However, the current clinical practice is still dominated by a single PRP injection, with limited efficacy. PRP can be combined with other treatment options, such as local delivery of PRP + neural stem cells/olfactory sheath cell exosomes, PRP + low-dose glucocorticoids to inhibit acute inflammation and PRP rehabilitation physiotherapy coordinated intervention to improve the efficiency of nerve axon remodeling.

Conclusion

PRP represents a safe, accessible, and biologically rational therapeutic strategy for SCI, offering the capacity to simultaneously address multiple pathological processes through its rich repertoire of growth factors and cytokines. The pleiotropic mechanisms of PRP—encompassing anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects, anti-apoptotic neuroprotection, angiogenesis, axonal regeneration, and microenvironment modulation—collectively address the complex pathophysiology of secondary injury and create conditions permissive for neural repair. Preclinical evidence consistently demonstrates that PRP administration improves functional recovery in animal models, and preliminary clinical observations provide encouraging signals of translational potential.

However, substantial challenges must be overcome before PRP can achieve widespread clinical adoption for SCI. The absence of standardized preparation protocols undermines reproducibility and comparability across studies. Incomplete understanding of optimal timing, dosing, and mechanistic pathways limits rational optimization. The inherent limitations of PRP monotherapy in addressing severe tissue loss and providing structural support constrain efficacy in the most severe injuries. Most critically, the paucity of high-quality clinical evidence from rigorous RCTs precludes definitive conclusions regarding efficacy and safety in human patients.

The future of PRP therapy for SCI lies in innovative combination strategies that leverage advances in biomaterials science and regenerative medicine. Integration of PRP with sophisticated hydrogel scaffolds enables sustained growth factor delivery and structural support for regenerating axons. Combination with stem cells harnesses complementary mechanisms of repair. Emerging approaches utilizing PRP-derived extracellular vesicles may offer enhanced potency and stability while reducing immunogenicity. Through rigorous translational research addressing these challenges and opportunities, PRP-based therapies may ultimately fulfill their promise as effective interventions for patients suffering from SCI.
