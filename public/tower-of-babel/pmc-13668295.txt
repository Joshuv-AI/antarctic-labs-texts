Hepatocyte senescence in metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD)
Authors: Xinyue Pang, Nan Geng, Chenqi Lu, Qianwen Zhao, Junping Shi, Yali Xiong, Wenhao Li, Jie Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) has emerged as a major global health challenge, with particularly high morbidity and disease burden. Cellular senescence has been identified as one of the core mechanisms driving the onset and progression of MASLD. Hepatocytes, the primary functional cells of the liver, can undergo a senescent state under metabolic stress. These senescent hepatocytes secrete a series of pro-inflammatory and profibrotic factors, directly exacerbating hepatic inflammation and activating hepatic stellate cells, thereby leading to pathological fibrosis. The purpose of this review is to explore the key molecular pathways of hepatocyte senescence and their impact on MASLD progression. Finally, we discuss relevant intervention strategies that can be used for the prevention and treatment of MASLD.

INTRODUCTION

Metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) has become the most prevalent chronic liver disease worldwide, affecting over a quarter of the global population.12 MASLD, formerly known as NAFLD, was first introduced in 1986 and defined as the presence of hepatic steatosis in the absence of secondary aetiologies or significant alcohol consumption. In 2023, MASLD replaced the term NAFLD and is defined as the presence of at least one cardiometabolic risk factor and alcohol consumption <20 g per day for females and 30 g per day for males. This change emphasizes that cardiometabolic risk factors, such as insulin resistance, play a core role in the occurrence and development of the disease.3 The disease spectrum of MASLD ranges from simple steatosis to metabolic dysfunction–associated steatohepatitis (MASH), a more progressive form with increased risks of fibrosis, cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). Despite the growing prevalence and substantial disease burden of MASLD, effective pharmacotherapeutic options have remained limited until recently.4 Therefore, a deeper understanding of MASLD pathogenesis is essential for developing interventions that can prevent, arrest, or reverse the progression of MASLD.

MASLD is increasingly recognized as an age-associated disorder in which chronic nutrient excess, lipotoxicity, and mitochondrial dysfunction drive hepatocyte senescence. Aging is a primary risk factor for most chronic diseases and can be categorized into 2 distinct processes: chronological aging, defined by the passage of time, and biological aging, which reflects progressive functional decline and structural degeneration at molecular, cellular, tissue, and organ levels, reflecting the body’s actual physiological and metabolic state.56 MASLD intrinsically combines these 2 aging processes: it is an age-associated disease, and its core metabolic dysfunction directly accelerates biological aging, thereby promoting liver cell functional impairment and inflammation. Multiple prospective cohort studies have demonstrated that chronological aging is a classical predictor of liver fibrosis and all-cause mortality, while accelerated biological aging independently predicts the onset of MASLD and the severity of fibrosis.7

Hepatocytes, the major parenchymal cells of the liver, perform essential metabolic, synthetic, detoxification, and storage functions crucial for systemic homeostasis.8 In MASLD, however, hepatocytes are subjected to sustained metabolic stress, which not only impairs normal hepatocyte function but also induces cellular senescence, thereby weakening the liver’s capacity to maintain homeostasis, increasing its susceptibility to injury, and promoting disease progression.9 Unlike classical replicative aging driven by telomere shortening, hepatocyte senescence in MASLD is primarily a form of biogenic aging triggered by metabolic stress. Although both manifest as cell cycle arrest, the latter bypasses telomere-dependent timing mechanisms and is directly driven by metabolic dysregulation—such as lipotoxicity, oxidative stress, and insulin resistance—exhibiting unique stress-induced characteristics.10 Accumulating evidence confirms that metabolic stress can directly induce hepatocyte senescence. Furthermore, clinical studies have shown a significant correlation between hepatocyte telomere length and the severity of hepatitis and fibrosis, suggesting that biological aging and telomere attrition may coexist and synergistically exacerbate liver damage during the course of MASLD.11

Based on the above observations, this review explores the mechanisms underlying hepatocyte senescence under conditions of metabolic stress, examines how senescent hepatocytes drive disease progression, and discusses the novel therapeutic strategies targeting senescent hepatocytes in the context of liver disease.

MECHANISMS ASSOCIATED WITH HEPATOCYTE SENESCENCE IN MASLD

Hepatocytes are particularly susceptible to a range of senescence-inducing stressors, including exogenous substances, lipotoxicity, and microbial infections, due to their high metabolic activity and continuous exposure to toxins and inflammatory mediators. These stressors trigger persistent DNA damage signaling pathways that ultimately activate cell cycle checkpoints and initiate the senescence program.12

Telomere attrition and oxidative stress

Telomeres are protective structures located at the ends of chromosomes, responsible for maintaining genomic stability. With each cell division, telomeres gradually shorten in a process known as telomere attrition. In the liver, hepatocytes subjected to oxidative stress exhibit significantly accelerated telomere attrition. When telomeres shorten to a critical length, they trigger a DNA damage response (DDR) cascade, which initiates cell cycle arrest.13 This signal activates the p53 pathway by CHK2/1-mediated phosphorylation of p53, thereby promoting downstream p21 expression and inhibiting cyclin E/A-CDK2 activity, leading to irreversible cell cycle arrest.14 In MASLD, telomere attrition is prevalent in hepatocytes and is closely correlated with disease severity. A study involving 156 patients with MASLD diagnosed by liver biopsy showed that the telomere length significantly shortened with increasing liver inflammation (A1–A3 vs. A0) and fibrosis progression (F3–F4 vs. F0–F2).15 Moreover, shorter telomeres correlate with higher grades of steatohepatitis, suggesting that telomere attrition serves as a “timer” linking chronic lipid injury to histological progression.16

Oxidative stress is a pathological state characterized by an imbalance between the production and clearance of reactive oxygen species (ROS) within cells, leading to excessive ROS accumulation.17 Within hepatocytes, mitochondria serve as the core site of lipid oxidation. Under metabolic stress, increased lipid oxidation directly drives mitochondrial ROS (mtROS) production, which in turn induces oxidative stress.18 Compared with other regions, telomeric DNA is highly sensitive to oxidative stress due to its high guanine content (TTAGGG repeat sequences). Under the oxidative burden caused by MASLD, ROS specifically target these sites, making telomeric DNA highly susceptible to oxidation by ROS, resulting in the formation of 8-oxoG;19 moreover, its repair enzymes are poorly accessible. Following 8-oxoG excision, it often evolves into single-strand breaks rather than precise repair, leading to irreversible telomeric fragment loss and further accelerating shortening rates.20 Concurrently, ROS directly attack nuclear DNA, causing nontelomeric damage like double-strand breaks (DSBs) and base deoxypurination. These “acute strikes” are also recognized by the MRN complex, activating the ATM–p53–p21 axis, and inducing “premature aging.”21 Notably, the maintenance and progression of “premature aging” are often accompanied by the activation of the p16INK4a pathway. High expression of p16INK4a typically signifies the establishment of an irreversible state of cell senescence. In MASLD, the accumulation of p16INK4a is primarily confined to non-parenchymal liver cells, such as sinusoidal endothelial cells and macrophages, driving the persistence of fibrosis and inflammation through the SASP.22 Thus, oxidative stress not only indirectly accelerates aging by “speeding up the telomere clock” but also directly initiates DDR independently of telomeres, delivering a dual blow.

Regardless of the damage source, DDR signals ultimately converge on the p53–p21 pathway. Sustained p53 phosphorylation enables it to evade MDM2-mediated degradation. Accumulated p53 simultaneously upregulates p21, allowing it to bind and inhibit multiple CDK–cyclin complexes, leading to RB dephosphorylation and resulting in cell cycle arrest, while also transcribing pro-apoptotic genes like PUMA and Bax.23 However, MASLD hepatocytes exhibit “apoptosis resistance” due to lipid toxicity, making cellular senescence the predominant fate.

Mitochondrial dysfunction

Mitochondrial dysfunction serves both as a trigger for hepatic cell senescence and as an amplifying factor downstream of this process. Mitochondrial dysfunction initiates oxidative stress. In hepatocytes with lipid accumulation, the compensatory enhancement of fatty acid β-oxidation exceeds the mitochondrial processing capacity, leading to the accumulation of acetyl-CoA and NADH. This forces the electron transport chain (ETC) to “slow down,” causing a surge in the reverse electron transport (RET) and massive superoxide production.24 Simultaneously, mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) becomes highly susceptible to base modifications and strand breaks. This further suppresses the transcription of ETC-encoding genes, thereby generating substantial ROS.25 Excessively accumulated ROS not only oxidize mtDNA but also diffuse into the nucleus, causing double-strand breaks in the nuclear DNA—a classic manifestation of mitochondrial-to-nuclear retrograde signaling. Such nuclear DNA damage stably activates tumor suppressor pathways like p53, inducing cell cycle arrest and senescence phenotypes, thereby further intensifying aging signals.

NAD+ metabolism, as a key pathway linking energy metabolism and aging signals, plays a crucial role in the aforementioned processes.26 In MASLD, it is both excessively consumed as a vital coenzyme for energy metabolism and depleted as a substrate for the DNA damage repair enzyme PARP1. This dual depletion leads to a drastic decline in the intracellular NAD+ levels.27 NAD+ depletion causes the loss of deacetylase activity in the SIRT family. The inactivation of nuclear SIRT1 leads to epigenetic dysregulation and impaired mitochondrial biogenesis, while the reduced activity of mitochondrial SIRT3 causes the collapse of the antioxidant defense system.28 This further exacerbates oxidative stress within the mitochondria, triggering metabolic disorders.

Notably, mitochondrial dysfunction and NAD+ depletion form a vicious cycle: dysfunctional mitochondria generate excessive ROS, activating NAD+ consuming enzymes (such as PARP1, CD38), while declining NAD+ levels further impair the activity of the SIRT family and PGC-1α-mediated mitochondrial biogenesis. Under the context of MASLD, the expression of CD38 is often upregulated by inflammatory cytokines produced by SASP, which also links mitochondrial dysfunction to the liver immune microenvironment.29 In addition, aging itself can lead to mitochondrial autophagy, and this damage allows ROS-producing defective mitochondria to persist within cells, further exacerbating oxidative stress and NAD+ depletion. This self-perpetuating cycle of “mitochondrial dysfunction-NAD+ depletion-epigenetic dysregulation” continuously drives cellular aging and functional decline in the organism.

Inflammatory signals

Lipotoxicity refers to the excessive accumulation of lipids in nonadipose tissues due to abnormal lipid metabolism, leading to cellular damage and inflammatory responses. Under lipotoxicity, intracellular lipid metabolites such as free fatty acids (FFAs) and cholesterol crystals can act as damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), recognized by the pattern recognition receptor TLR4.30 The activation of TLR4 initiates downstream MyD88-dependent signaling pathways. The MyD88-dependent pathway recruits molecules such as TRAF6 and IRAK1/4 to activate NF-κB. Activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, in addition to NF-κB-mediated pathways, can be triggered by various stimuli such as ROS and potassium ion efflux. This leads to NLRP3 oligomerization and apoptosis-associated speck-like protein containing a CARD (ASC) recruitment, subsequently activating caspase-1. This enzyme cleaves pro-IL-1β and pro-IL-18 into mature IL-1β and IL-18, thereby inducing an inflammatory response.2021 The highly activated NLRP3 inflammasome can trigger pyroptosis, but its sublethal or chronic low-level activation can maintain a persistent low-grade inflammation without triggering cell lysis, and becomes one of the main factors driving SASP.31

Research has revealed that NF-κB activation not only promotes the production of inflammatory cytokines but also stabilizes p16 expression through multiple mechanisms. On one hand, NF-κB can directly or indirectly enhance the transcription of the p16 gene; on the other hand, inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and IL-8 can act on the surrounding cells via paracrine or autocrine pathways, activating signaling pathways like JAK/STAT to further amplify p16 expression.32 Upregulated p16 converts retinoblastoma protein (pRB) into a non-functional, hypophosphorylated, and dephosphorylated state. Hypophosphorylated pRB binds to the transcription factor E2F, preventing E2F from activating the expression of genes essential for the cell cycle.33 This causes the cells to arrest in the G0/G1 phase, prompting them to permanently exit the cell cycle and thus enter an irreversible state of cellular senescence.34

Inflammatory signals are not only a consequence of aging, but also its driving force. Metabolic stressors such as lipotoxicity activate NF-κB through the TLR4 and NLRP3 inflammasome pathways, promoting the production of inflammatory cytokines. Furthermore, in the context of MASLD, the cGAS–STING signaling pathway triggered by mtDNA damage can also act as an upstream activator of NF-κB, thereby further driving the onset of the inflammatory response.35 These cytokines stabilize the expression of aging markers like p16 via paracrine or autocrine mechanisms, trapping cells in a senescent state. This mechanism confirms that intervening in inflammatory signaling pathways may represent a novel strategy for delaying aging and treating age-related diseases.

Apoptosis resistance

Damaged hepatocytes acquire resistance to apoptosis, which is one of the key mechanisms enabling their persistent survival in vivo and the secretion of SASP, driving disease progression to more severe stages. This resistance also serves as a prerequisite for the accumulation of aging-related pathologies. Within the MASLD pathological environment, multiple molecular mechanisms collectively contribute to hepatocyte resistance to apoptosis. Abnormal lipid accumulation significantly upregulates the expression of hepatic antiapoptotic proteins (such as Bcl-2, Bcl-xL, Mcl-1).36 Simultaneously, the function or expression of pro-apoptotic proteins (eg, Bax, Bak, Bid) may be suppressed. This tilt of the “survival balance” toward the antiapoptotic side effectively blocks the mitochondrial apoptosis pathway, sparing cells from death, and conferring an additional survival advantage to the senescent hepatocytes. Theoretically, these senescent hepatocytes should be promptly cleared by the immune system; however, their resistance to apoptosis enables them to evade immune surveillance.37 With advancing age, declining immune function further exacerbates this phenomenon, leading to the persistent accumulation of senescent cells (Figure 1).

These apoptosis-resistant senescent cells persistently survive and secrete SASP factors, inducing neighboring cells to become senescent through paracrine effects, promoting chronic inflammation, and disrupting tissue homeostasis.

Histological hallmarks of hepatocyte senescence, such as telomere shortening, DNA damage, and resistance to apoptosis, have been extensively reported in MASLD and other chronic liver diseases.38 However, these alterations lack specificity and fail to capture the unique heterogeneity of senescent hepatocytes. Recent studies have developed a hepatocyte-specific senescence gene signature (SHGS) that captures the core transcriptional features of senescent hepatocytes.9 Analysis reveals that SHGS+ hepatocytes enrich a large number of genes associated with cellular senescence and SASP, and further indicates that these cells can influence neighboring hepatocytes through paracrine signaling, thereby amplifying MASLD disease progression.39 This signature plays a crucial role in assessing disease severity and targeting intervention therapies. However, this approach has limitations. SHGS+ hepatocytes primarily originate from p21+ cells, lacking specificity for p21− or other signaling-induced senescent cells.

There is currently no universally accepted gold standard for diagnosing hepatocyte aging, and diagnosis continues to rely on the functional validation of combinations of various biomarkers. In the MASLD, SHGS+ has emerged as the current standard; however, there is a need to develop more specific noninvasive biomarkers in the future.

THE ROLE OF SENESCENT HEPATOCYTES IN THE PROGRESSION OF MASLD

These molecular mechanisms not only lead to hepatic cell dysfunction but also influence the liver microenvironment through SASP, triggering pathological reprogramming of other liver cells, propagating senescence to surrounding non-senescent hepatocytes, promoting HSC fibrosis, and macrophage activation. Consequently, the liver microenvironment is restructured, accelerating the progression of MASLD.

Heterogeneity in hepatocyte senescence

In MASLD, hepatocyte senescence is not a uniform phenomenon but exhibits significant spatial, temporal, and subtype heterogeneity. At the spatial level, studies indicate that senescent hepatocytes are not randomly distributed but preferentially aggregate around central veins and in areas with the most severe steatosis. These regions exhibit high oxidative stress burdens, suggesting that the local metabolic microenvironment influences susceptibility to senescence.40 Temporal heterogeneity is manifested by the predominance of reversible senescence in the early stages of the disease, where senescent cells can be cleared or regain proliferative capacity once the injury is resolved; however, as MASLD progresses, senescence gradually becomes irreversible, with senescent cells accumulating and forming a fibrogenic microenvironment.41

At the phenotypic level, different subpopulations of senescent hepatocytes exhibit distinct pathogenic potentials. p21-positive senescent cells are associated with fibrosis, driving the transition of MASH to fibrosis. And the lipotoxic subtype is characterized by mitochondrial dysfunction and the accumulation of lipid peroxides, which directly exacerbate metabolic stress. The immune evasion subtype upregulates BCL-2 family proteins to resist immune clearance, leading to the long-term persistence of senescent cells.4243 These various pathogenic subtypes synergistically promote the development of liver fibrosis, hepatocyte dysplasia, and even HCC.

Lipid metabolic reprogramming

Aging induces the redistribution of body fat, increasing the risk of metabolic diseases.44 Preclinical study demonstrates that the induction of hepatocyte senescence promotes hepatic steatosis, whereas the clearance of these senescent cells ameliorates the liver lipid content.45 Conversely, accumulated lipids themselves can also induce hepatocyte senescence, establishing a self-regulating loop in which aging and steatosis mutually exacerbate each other. This phenomenon, often termed “metabolic senescence,” indicates a close bidirectional relationship between senescence and steatosis, thereby promoting the onset and progression of MASLD.

The mechanism of lipid accumulation in senescent hepatocytes is linked to the mitochondrial dysfunction occurring during senescence, including impaired oxidative phosphorylation and increased ROS production.46 This dysfunction results in a decreased capacity of the hepatocytes to effectively catabolize lipids, suppressing the lipolysis process, and leading to the accumulation of lipids in the cytosol, forming a vicious cycle of “lipid droplet accumulation–mitochondrial damage.”45 Concurrently, senescent hepatocytes secrete a series of SASP factors, including IL-6, TNF-α, IL-1β, and others. These factors not only promote inflammation but also directly interfere with insulin-signaling pathways. SASP factors such as TNF-α induce insulin resistance by inhibiting IRS-1 phosphorylation and activating the JNK/NF-κB pathway, thereby suppressing the negative regulation of lipid synthesis by insulin and leading to enhanced lipid production.47 Notably, this insulin resistance cannot suppress de novo lipogenesis in the liver. The differential response of this signaling pathway forms the metabolic paradox of selective insulin resistance, that is, while the liver shows resistance to glucose metabolism, lipid synthesis metabolism is abnormally hyperactive, leading to dysfunction of adipose tissue and progressive decline in peripheral insulin sensitivity, further exacerbating the vicious cycle between lipids and aging. In addition, SASP factors may influence the neighboring non-senescent hepatocytes via paracrine mechanisms, amplifying the scope of lipid metabolism disorders. Relevant studies have shown that under inflammatory stress, SASP factors, such as IL-6, upregulate the expression of CD36 in adjacent healthy hepatocytes at the translational level through the mTOR signaling pathway, endowing these cells with a stronger capacity for fatty acid uptake, and accelerating intracellular lipid accumulation and further expanding the extent of the pathological changes.48

Amplification of the SASP inflammatory

Senescent hepatocytes establish a persistent inflammatory microenvironment through their unique SASP, serving as the key “inflammatory hub” perpetuating chronic liver inflammation in MASLD. The inflammatory mediators secreted by senescent hepatocytes are dominated by IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α, and chemokines CCL2 and CXCL8.49 These secreted factors exert diverse paracrine effects on the neighboring cells. They not only directly activate the NF-κB signaling pathway in hepatocytes, inducing an increased expression of inflammatory mediators in both themselves and the adjacent hepatocytes, but also continuously recruit Ly6C+ monocytes/macrophages and activate Kupffer cells. While concurrently upregulating ICAM-1 and VCAM-1 to enhance immune cell adhesion. Activated macrophages further produce IL-6 and IL-1β, forming an “senescence–inflammation” positive feedback loop that sustains chronic low-grade inflammation in MASLD.50 This process plays a crucial role in mediating cellular communication and influencing several pathological processes. In animal models, the selective depletion of p16-senescent hepatocytes or systemic knockout of p21 both reduced hepatic macrophages by 40%–50% and significantly decreased the serum ALT and IL-6 levels.51

In addition, GDF15 has emerged as one of the new biomarkers of aging. It is specifically secreted by senescent hepatocytes; its serum levels correlate with disease severity.9 Notably, a recent study has shown that p21 expression initiates a p21-associated secretory phenotype (PASP) rich in immune recruitment factors. This finding reveals that p21+ hepatocytes may secrete a unique spectrum of factors distinct from SASP. This may serve as a precursor to true SASP and plays a crucial role in both liver regeneration and pathological processes.52

Interactions between hepatocytes and hepatic stellate cells

Senescent hepatocytes act as the key initiators of MASLD liver fibrosis by directly inducing hepatic stellate cell (HSC) activation, remodeling the hepatic microenvironment, and promoting abnormal extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition.53 Regarding activation mechanisms, senescent hepatocytes release factors such as TGF-β, PDGF, and CTGF, which directly activate quiescent HSCs, transforming them into collagen-producing myofibroblasts, and leading to ECM deposition.54 Among these, PDGF is the most potent mitogen for HSCs, driving their proliferation and migration. Studies have shown that PDGF and its receptors are significantly upregulated in both experimental rat models of fibrosis and human fibrotic livers. TGF-β1, meanwhile, is the primary driver of HSC transdifferentiation into myofibroblasts. Senescent hepatocytes release TGF-β1 via autocrine and paracrine pathways. After binding to the corresponding receptors on the surface of HSCs, it can activate the Smad2/3 signaling pathway in the cells. Phosphorylated Smad2/3 forms a complex with Smad4 and translocates into the nucleus, inducing the transformation of HSCs from a quiescent state into fibroblast-like cells, upregulating the expression of alpha-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) and type I collagen (Col1a1), ultimately leading to excessive ECM deposition within the liver and initiating the process of hepatic fibrosis.55 Studies on liver tissue samples from patients with hepatic fibrosis have revealed a positive correlation between the TGF-β1 expression levels and the number of senescent hepatocytes. TGF-β1 is predominantly localized in the perinuclear regions of senescent hepatocytes, conclusively demonstrating the role of senescent hepatocyte-secreted TGF-β1 in initiating liver fibrosis.56 Clinical studies have shown that, in human liver biopsy specimens, p21/p16-positive cells colocalize with α-SMA-positive cells and exhibit a strong correlation with the thickness of fibrous septa. Relevant animal experiments have shown that administering antioxidants or drugs that inhibit inflammatory signaling pathways can effectively reduce the oxidative stress and inflammatory responses triggered by senescent hepatocytes, decrease the activation level of HSCs, and slow down the initiation of liver fibrosis, which further demonstrates that senescent hepatocytes play a pivotal role in promoting liver fibrosis and influencing the prognosis of MASLD.51

In addition to paracrine effects, HSCs activation depends on multiple mechanisms: HSCs themselves can establish activation loops independent of external signals through the NTF3–NTRK3 autocrine axis and SERPINE1-mediated autonomous signaling;57 extracellular vesicles released by liver sinusoidal endothelial cells (LSECs) can regulate the activation state of HSCs after being taken up by them;58 metabolites such as saturated fatty acids and cholesterol can directly act on HSCs, inducing their transition to a fibrotic phenotype. These mechanisms act synergistically to drive HSC activation and the progression of liver fibrosis.

Immune surveillance and evasion of senescent hepatocytes

Although aging is generally regarded as an antitumor mechanism, senescent hepatocytes in the MASH context may promote the development of HCC through senescence escape mechanisms. Mechanisms such as DNA damage and metabolic stress induce hepatocytes to enter a senescent state characterized by elevated p53–FBP1 expression. Senescent cells prevent the further proliferation of damaged cells through permanent cell cycle arrest, thereby inhibiting mutation accumulation and malignant transformation. However, persistent chronic inflammation creates a tumor-promoting microenvironment. Under this selective pressure, some senescent cells escape senescence by silencing FBP1 and activating pathways such as NRF2/AKT pathways to overcome cell cycle arrest. These escaped senescent cells become hepatocellular precancerous cells (HcPCs).59 Due to disrupted epigenetic regulation and increased replication stress under sustained proliferative pressure, escaped cells develop genomic instability, forming the core of precancerous lesions that ultimately drive HCC development (Figure 2).

Relevant studies have found that during the transformation of MASLD to HCC, senescence markers such as FBP1 and p53 are upregulated in hepatocytes but downregulated in precancerous lesions and HCC, suggesting that the senescent state is reversed. In MUP-uPA mice fed a high-fructose, high-fat diet, the HCC incidence rate increased from 20% to 80% over 32 weeks, confirming that the “escape” of senescent hepatocytes serves as a critical switch linking MASH fibrosis to malignant transformation.60

THERAPEUTIC STRATEGIES TARGETING HEPATOCYTE SENESCENCE

As a key driver of MASLD, the targeted treatment of hepatocyte senescence has become a primary focus in clinical research.61 Targeted aging therapies delay the progression of MASLD by intervening in hepatocyte senescence; however, their core mechanism does not involve reversing hepatocyte senescence, but rather selectively eliminating senescent hepatocytes that have accumulated in a pathological state and suppressing their harmful SASP, thereby breaking the senescence–inflammation cycle, halting fibrosis, and delaying disease progression through improved metabolic function.

Senolytic therapy, which mitigates disease progression by specifically inducing senescent cell apoptosis and reducing the senescent cell burden, represents the most direct intervention currently available for targeting hepatocyte senescence. Its core advantage lies in precisely eliminating dysfunctional senescent cells while sparing normal proliferating cells. Widely used antisenescence drugs dasatinib (D) and quercetin (Q) have been shown to reduce p21, PAI-1, and BCL-xl in senescent cells, and the first clinical trial evaluating senolytic therapy in MASLD patients with fibrosis using the D+Q is currently underway (NCT05506488).62 Bcl-2 inhibitor ABT-263/737 and HSP90 inhibitor 17-DMAG cleared senescent hepatocytes by attenuating antiapoptotic signaling or disrupting HSP90-AKT activation, respectively, demonstrating antifibrotic and antioxidant effects in acute-chronic alcoholic and chemically-induced liver injury models.6364 Although ABT-263 has demonstrated efficacy in xenograft models of HCC, its application in liver disease remains under-explored, and its safety profile requires rigorous evaluation. In addition, novel strategies, PROTAC degraders, such as 753b and ARV-825, which promise higher cell-type specificity and reduced off-target toxicity, are currently under preclinical development.6566 It is important to recognize that these interventions lack selectivity for senescent cells, potentially leading to adverse effects such as thrombocytopenia and bone marrow suppression or demonstrating ineffectiveness in certain models. For instance, D+Q exerts effects on various types of senescent cells (endothelial cells, immune cells, and astrocytes) rather than acting specifically on hepatocytes. It failed to reduce hepatocyte senescence in an acetaminophen-induced acute liver injury model and even exacerbated disease progression in a DEN/HFD-induced liver disease model.67 Therefore, research on antiaging drugs must focus on clearly targeting senescent cells based on the specific disease or etiology.

Senomorphic approaches do not directly eliminate senescent cells, but mitigate their pathogenic effects by suppressing the SASP and improving the senescent microenvironment. This strategy is particularly suitable for patients with a lower burden of senescent cells or those intolerant to senolytics. Senomorphic approaches can directly or indirectly attenuate SASP by inhibiting transcription factors such as NF-κB, the JAK–STAT signaling pathway, the serine/threonine protein kinase mTOR, targets associated with mitochondrial complex I or IV, or other pathways involved in inducing and maintaining SASP.686970 For example, metformin significantly downregulates the pro-inflammatory factors such as IL-6 and TNF-α by activating AMPK and subsequently inhibiting NF-κB, and has been demonstrated to delay fibrosis progression in multiple MASLD cohorts.71 Rapamycin suppresses IL-1α, leading to decreased NF-κB activity and downregulation of pro-inflammatory factor expression.72 Combined with MAPKAPK2 blockade and mTOR-related mRNA stabilization mechanisms, it significantly attenuates SASP. STAT3 inhibitors can block the JAK/STAT signaling axis, markedly reducing pro-inflammatory factors and thereby inhibiting senescent cells’ inflammatory remodeling of the microenvironment.73 Compared with senolytics that directly eliminate senescent cells, senomorphics do not trigger apoptosis pathways, resulting in lower toxicity and higher safety. However, due to the persistent presence of senescent cells, SASP suppression requires long-term maintenance, potentially necessitating regular and prolonged use. This increases the risk of drug dependency and unforeseen adverse effects from long-term medication. Therefore, the development of a new generation of senomorphic compounds to reduce drug side effects such as nephrotoxicity, immunosuppression, and inflammation, while simultaneously improving drug efficacy and pharmacokinetics, remains a primary focus of current research.

The pathways of senescence are complex, and single-target interventions often struggle to simultaneously achieve “clearance” and “suppression.” Combination strategies, designed based on complementary mechanisms, can concurrently reduce the senescent cell burden and block the persistent damage caused by residual SASP. The combined use of senolytic and senomorphic drugs may exhibit synergistic effects, including the elimination of senescent cells and suppression of their harmful secretory activity. For example, the rapid clearance of high-SASP senescent populations with D+Q followed by maintenance of a low-inflammatory environment with metformin/rapamycin significantly reduced p16 and p21 expression and collagen deposition in liver tissue compared with monotherapy in animal studies.74

In addition, immunotherapy, particularly CAR-T cells targeting uPAR (uPAR-CAR-T), has demonstrated a novel and promising mechanism of action in the treatment of MASLD.66 Its core lies in precisely eliminating senescent hepatocytes and senescence-associated myofibroblasts, thereby blocking the progression of fibrosis and improving metabolic homeostasis. uPAR is significantly upregulated on the surface of senescent hepatocytes and myofibroblasts derived from HSCs, making it an ideal target for CAR-T cells.75 uPAR-CAR-T cells precisely recognize and eliminate these cells via their chimeric antigen receptor, minimizing damage to normal cells. Multiple studies confirm that uPAR-CAR-T cells persist in aged or high-fat diet mice, sustainably suppressing senescent cell accumulation without significant toxicity.76 However, clinical application faces challenges: uPAR expression is not universal among senescent cells nor exclusive to them, potentially leading to unintended damage. Further evaluation is needed for long-term immunotoxicity, cytokine release syndrome, and other risks.

Beyond targeted pharmacological therapies (Table 1), the treatment approaches focusing on lifestyle interventions to counteract hepatic aging are also under investigation. In the absence of effective and safe drug treatments, dietary control and lifestyle modifications represent the primary therapeutic strategy for MASLD.77 Weight reduction is observed to be the most effective treatment for MASLD. In patients with MASLD, exercise enhances insulin sensitivity, reduces oxidative stress, and accelerates lipid oxidation, thereby delaying MASLD progression. The latest animal experiments have found that aerobic exercise intervention for 8 months on 18-month-old rats directly inhibits liver cell senescence through the miRNA–P38MAPK signaling pathway. In addition, exercise can improve insulin sensitivity through metabolic epigenetic remodeling, reducing the pro-senescence pressure on liver cells caused by hyperinsulinemia and lipotoxicity. Both aerobic and resistance training improve hepatic steatosis, with resistance training requiring less energy expenditure and being more suitable for individuals with poor cardiopulmonary function or those who are overweight and unable to tolerate aerobic exercise.78 Regarding diet, the Mediterranean diet is currently recommended as this dietary pattern is rich in plant-based foods, olive oil, and nuts, while including moderate amounts of red meat and wine.79 Weight loss induces histological improvement in patients with MASLD. Studies indicate that a weight reduction of at least 3%–5% is necessary for improving steatosis. A weight loss exceeding 10% is associated with the resolution of steatosis in nearly all patients and improvement of fibrosis in ~80% of cases.8081

Therapeutic strategies targeting hepatocyte senescence

CHALLENGES AND PROSPECTS

Despite the increasingly recognized central role of hepatocyte senescence in the development of MASLD and the emergence of senescent cell targeting as a highly promising therapeutic strategy, its clinical translation still faces a series of significant challenges. Currently, there remains a lack of a standardized in vivo definition of senescence and noninvasive diagnostic tools to identify “harmful senescence” that genuinely requires intervention. Secondly, the optimal intervention window, long-term safety, and individualized efficacy differences of senolytics remain unresolved core issues. The key questions include: how to precisely determine the progression stages of MASLD and identify the optimal timing for drug intervention; how to ensure drug safety during long-term use; and whether to select or combine different SASP inhibitors or senolytics based on patient heterogeneity. Future research must integrate multi-omics biomarkers to establish a scalable “senescence scoring system,” validate the senescence biomarkers in humans, and design long-term clinical trials to explore the efficacy and safety of senolytics across different disease stages and populations. This will drive the active development of next-generation targeted therapies with higher specificity and lower toxicity.
