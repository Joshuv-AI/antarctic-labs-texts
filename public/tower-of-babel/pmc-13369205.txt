Performance and statistical evaluation of animal dung–driven microbial fuel cells for simultaneous bio electrochemical conversion and bioremediation of toxic metal-contaminated wastewater
Authors: Mustapha Omenesa Idris, Abdulrahman Itopa Suleiman, Claudia Guerrero-Barajas, Mohamad Nasir Mohamad Ibrahim, Nur Asshifa Md Noh, Dahiru Umar Lawal, Pankaj Sah, Syed Najmul Hejaz Azmi, Mohd Tariq, Parwiz Niazi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The choice of substrates and their composition play a crucial role in supporting the growth of microorganisms and enhancing overall microbial fuel cells (MFCs) performance. Traditionally used substrates, such as glucose and acetate, are now being replaced by innovative alternatives like biomass materials and food waste. Consequently, this research focuses on the use of cow dung as an organic substrate in MFCs, examining its role in microbial metabolism, bioenergy production, and the bioremediation of toxic metals. The MFC processes were conducted over 40 days, during which a peak voltage output of 485 mV was recorded on day 26. The MFC cycle generates maximum metal bioremediation efficiencies of 89.16 ± 0.04% for Al3+, 89.17 ± 0.46% for Cu2+, 92.86 ± 0.11% for Pb2+, 91.37 ± 0.4% for Zn2+, and 92.01 ± 0.16% for Hg2+, respectively indicating its potential in ameliorating toxic metal levels from wastewater. Two-way ANOVA revealed that bioremediation efficiency (%) was significantly influenced by metal ion type (p < 0.001) and operational duration (p < 0.001), with a significant Metal × Operational Days interaction (p < 0.001), indicating metal-specific temporal responses. Post-hoc Tukey HSD analysis confirmed significant differences among most metal–day combinations, demonstrating progressive, time-dependent enhancement of bioremediation efficiency. The substrate was applied to the anodic region constantly throughout the experiment. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) revealed distinct redox peaks, confirming active bioelectrochemical processes, while electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) indicated reduced internal resistance and enhanced electron transfer efficiency. SEM-EDX revealed the extent of biofilms and toxicity level of the operation. Furthermore, microbial analysis was investigated on the anode electrode after MFC operation. The probable bacterial species identified were Bacillus subtilis, Providencia rettgeri, Escherichia coli, Serratia marcescens, Staphylococcus aureus, Morgarella morganii and Providencia alcalifaciens. This study has shown that cow dung is a potential substrate for generation of electric current and bioremediation of toxic metals in MFC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-50415-3.

Introduction

The heavy dependence on fossil fuels during the past century has been crucial for the advancement of industrial and commercial development1,2. Due to environmental pollution, a growing population, depleting natural resources, and climate change, humanity has recently faced a scarcity of both energy and water3. The significant costs linked to maintaining energy generation, the lack of clean energy options, occurrences of incidents and natural disasters (which are increasing in frequency due to climate change), conflicts, and inadequate energy storage all contribute to the severity of the crisis4. Microbial fuel cells (MFCs) can be used to process wastewater in a more cost-effective and environmentally sustainable way to address this inefficiency5.

MFCs are considered one of the most promising solutions for replacing conventional fuel sources by providing a sustainable and eco-friendly approach to energy generation. They harness microbes to transform organic matter into bioelectricity, offering a greener energy source with a remarkably reduced greenhouse gas compared to conventional fossil fuels such as petroleum, oil, coal, and natural gas. Given that fossil fuels are limited resources and significant contributors to carbon footprint and pollution, there is an urgent necessity to transition to greener energy options. MFCs not only mitigate waste and pollution but also have the capability for decentralized energy production, making them well-suited for use in remote locations and small-scale applications6.

In the process of wastewater treatment, MFCs harness bio-residues to generate energy while avoiding the discharge of harmful byproducts. The benefits include being environmentally friendly, sustainable, and providing effective and efficient electrical energy7. MFCs have the ability to use any type of wastewater to serve as electrolyte in generating electrons and protons around anode area, which facilitates the bacterial breakdown of organic materials via anaerobic process. The electrons are transferred through the external circuit connecting the anode to the cathode, where terminal reduction of protons occurs, which is regarded as the fundamental process of MFCs8.

MFC represents a state-of-the-art technique that has been proposed as a vital remedy for various energy and environmental challenges associated with generating power through bioconversion. This supplemental energy input allows for the proton reduction thereby to produce hydrogen gas. MFCs can serve as a power source forming a control system in which the bioelectricity generated by MFCs is employed to produce hydrogen9. This combination improves overall energy efficiency and offers a flexible method for converting and bioresource, showcasing the extent in which technology can collaborate to support green energy solutions. Strengthening the characteristics of MFC will undoubtedly boost hydrogen production through MFC10. Despite their promise, several obstacles impede the potential uses of MFCs, including issues with efficiency, scalability, and cost-effectiveness. Continued research and technological improvements in MFCs may present a significant role in the overall transition to sustainable bioenergy sources, enhancing other green energy solutions and aiding efforts to combat greenhouse effect11.

The factors related to improving the performance of MFCs involve the improvement of exoelectrogenic species, the creation of advanced congenial electrodes, and the development of modified exchange membranes. However, innovative MFC technologies have recently been implemented that involve expensive, rapid, and intense techniques designed to operate without human intervention. Miniaturized MFCs, or microscale MFCs, include components at the microscale level such as chambers, electrode sizes, MFC geometries, and surface modifications12. Given that the scales operate on a small-scale, the MFCs require minimal energy and have a high surface area, coupled with compatible integration systems13.

The use of MFC technology in wastewater treatment has been shown to reduce chemical oxygen demand (COD) and various pollutants, making it a trustworthy alternative to traditional treatment methods14,15. Moreover, MFCs can be utilized to process various kinds of wastewater, including industrial effluents, by leveraging the microbial populations and operational conditions16. The ability of MFCs to generate renewable energy while also treating wastewater positions them as an effective option for sustainable waste management and energy recovery17. The ability of MFCs to generate renewable energy while also treating wastewater positions them as an effective option for sustainable waste management and energy recovery18. Hence, different organic substrates, such as potato waste19,20 domestic waste21, urine22 respectively, were used in MFCs for bio-electricity production.

Cow dung is a nutrient-rich substance, comprising essential minerals like nitrogen, potassium, and smaller amounts of sulfur, magnesium, iron, copper, manganese and cobalt. Additionally, it harbors a diverse microbial community that includes bacteria, protozoa, and yeast, contributing to its ecological significance23. Human feces typically contain about 50 to 70 g of COD per capita per day24. Similar to cow dung, human feces serve as a source of microorganisms. The bacteria present in these wastes can biodegrade the chemical energy found in the organic matter and convert it into electrical energy using MFC technology25. Numerous researchers have explored the feasibility of utilizing cow dung in MFCs for bioelectricity production26–28. Additionally, researchers have explored various MFC configurations, including two-chamber setups, to investigate the viability of utilizing human feces as a fuel source for electricity generation and simultaneous wastewater purification in MFC29. Castro et al. created a new, three-chamber MFC sink that treats human waste and generates treated effluent, compost, and bioelectricity30. Furthermore, human faeces were tested in a multistage MFC for power generation following waste treatment31. Much of the research performed on the monitoring of OCV values for MFCs using cow dung32 and pig dung33 have considered the wastes separately. Some monitored the OCV values for a few minutes or days32 while others monitored for many days33. In MFC, any form of organic matter, including chocolate, wine, wastewater, acetate, glucose, and dung from various animals, can be used as a substrate34. This study utilizes cow dung as substrate in MFCs to promote microbial growth leading to bioenergy generation and removal of heavy metals from wastewater supplemented with toxic metals such as aluminum (Al3+), copper (Cu2+), lead (Pb2+), mercury (Hg2+) and zinc (Zn2+).

Materials and methods

Cow dung collection

The cow dung was collected around Tauran farms in Felele, Lokoja into a polythene bag and then transferred to the laboratory for further preparation and analysis. Before collecting samples, we confirmed that the cows at Tauran Farms were routinely fed a daily diet of grass, hay, grains, and legumes. Additionally, the cows were treated for worms with oxfendazole tablets and vaccinated according to the farm’s schedule. For the inoculum, microbial enrichment was performed for 20 days in an anaerobic environment at 28 °C using 5-liter reagent bottles.

Biochemical analysis of the dung

The cow dung samples were spread out on brown paper and air-dried at room temperature for seven days until they achieved a free-flowing consistency. After drying, the samples were reduced in particle size and stored in labeled polyethylene bags. The following analyses were conducted on the dung samples using standard methods35. Proximate composition, pH, organic carbon content, elemental analysis, effective cation exchange capacity, exchangeable acidity, total hydrocarbon content, base saturation, sodium absorption ratio, and exchangeable bases. Furthermore, the bacterial and fungal composition of cow dung was analysed using culture-dependent methods. Approximately 2 g of bovine faeces were suspended in sterile saline solution and subjected to repeated dilution. Aliquots of suitable dilutions were spread-plated on nutritional agar for bacterial isolation and on dextrose agar for fungus isolation. Bacterial plates were incubated at 30–37 °C for 24–48 h, and fungal plates were incubated at 25–30 °C for 48–72 h. Colony-forming units (CFU/g) were computed to assess microbial burden. Bacterial isolates were identified using Gram staining and routine biochemical assays, while fungal isolates were identified via colony morphology and microscopic features36,37.

Inoculation sources

Wastewater samples were collected from a local pond near Federal University Lokoja. The collected wastewater was filtered to remove large debris and subsequently spiked with Al³⁺, Cu²⁺, Zn²⁺, Hg²⁺, and Pb²⁺ salts to achieve a concentration of 50 ppm for each metal ion, thereby preparing synthetic heavy metal-contaminated wastewater. Cow dung was used as both the substrate and microbial inoculum. Physicochemical parameters such as conductivity, dissolved oxygen, organic matter, phosphate, total solids, total volatile solids, biological oxygen Demand (BOD) and Chemical Oxygen Demand (COD) were determined according to standard laboratory methods20,23. The physicochemical characteristics of both fresh and synthetic wastewater are presented in Table 1. Each metal ion (Al³⁺, Cu²⁺, Zn²⁺, Hg²⁺, and Pb²⁺) was introduced at a uniform concentration of 50 ppm to enable direct comparison of removal efficiencies under identical operating conditions. The use of equal concentration ensured controlled assessment of metal-specific remediation performance without concentration-driven variability. Following the biochemical analysis procedure, the cow dung was placed directly into the anodic chamber of the MFC20. The prepared cow dung substrates were kept in room condition where the daily substrate amount is been collected.

Configuration and operation of MFC

Two 1.2 L plastic containers served as the anode and cathode chamber. A hole was drilled 4 cm from the base of the container for fitting of the 1/2-inch PVC pipe with an electric soldering iron. A hole was also drilled on the lid of the container for passage of copper wire. The set up was coupled by joining the two chambers together using the salt bridge which serve as a membrane. An epoxy gum was used to seal the edges of contact between the hole and salt bridge and was allowed to dry. The set up was tested for leakage by pouring sterile water into the chambers and observed for 30 min. The anode and cathode chambers were cleaned with 95% ethyl alcohol to rule out any form of contamination. The electrodes used were carbon rod and aluminium mesh. The carbon rod electrode has the following measurements: length 8.0 cm and diameter 1.30 cm. The geometric surface area was calculated, yielding an effective surface area of approximately 35.33 cm². The aluminium mesh with length 20 cm and width 5 cm was carefully folded into four and the naked end of the copper wire was tightly passed through the folded mesh. 5 g of cow dung was weighed dissolved in distilled water and added to the MFC set up. Conductivity was measured periodically on days 1, 5, 10, 20, 30, 35, 37, and 40 to monitor variations in ionic strength during the operation of the MFC. These measures aimed to assess the dynamics of the electrolyte and their potential influence on bioelectricity generation and heavy metal removal efficiency. Additionally, the pH of the system was monitored during this period. Copper wire is utilized to merge the anode to the cathode electrodes with 1 kΩ resistor added to connect the MFC to the external circuit as shown in Fig. 1. The MFC cycle was carried out under room temperature for a duration of 40 days, through which the voltage generated was continuously observed and taken23. The MFC was conducted in batch mode for a duration of 40 days without media replacement. The cow dung functioned as both inoculum and substrate, remaining unaltered throughout the duration of the study to provide consistent microbial acclimatisation and electrochemical stability. Heavy metal concentrations were assessed every 10 days to determine bioremediation efficacy, while voltage production was measured every 24 h with a digital multimeter connected across an external resistor.

Microbiological analysis

A sterile scalpel was used to gently scrape a section of the anode surface to get the microbial samples, which was transferred to a separate Falcon conical tube. To isolate the bacteria, the sample was spread onto sterile nutrient agar plates and incubated for 24 h at the suitable temperature. Bacterial colonies exhibiting distinct morphologies were selected and purified through serial sub-culturing in nutrient broth20. The biochemical tests were used to analyze bacterial isolates.

Statistical analysis

All the data were collected in triplicates and analyzed for descriptive and robust inferential statistical analysis such as Mean ± SE, two-way ANOVA, Tukey’s Post-Hoc analysis, violin plots and Kernel Density Estimation (KDE). The analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for windows, version 20.0. Armonk, NY: IBM Corp., JASP Team (2024) and Python (version 3.x) with the pandas, NumPy, matplotlib, and seaborn libraries.” A p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results and discussion

Substrate composition used in the study and its metabolism

Cow dung comprises a varied microbial community, encompassing bacteria and fungus. Fungal species are essential in hydrolysing complex organic polymers like cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin by secreting extracellular enzymes. The enzymatic breakdown yields simpler metabolites, such as volatile fatty acids and soluble sugars, which can act as electron donors for electrogenic bacteria in the anode biofilm. While fungi are not usually predominant exoelectrogenic microbes, their synergistic interaction with bacterial communities can improve substrate bioavailability, stabilise microbial consortia, and indirectly enhance electron transfer efficiency. Consequently, the presence of fungi in bovine excrement may enhance bioelectricity production and overall MFC performance41,42.

During the MFC operation, the organic substrates were metabolized by electrogenic microbes, producing high amount of voltage initially which later drop as result of substrate depletion with the microbes approaching decline phase43.

Voltage generation and polarization studies

The reaction in the MFCs lasted 40 days, utilizing 5 g of cow dung daily as organic substrate supplement for microbial species in the MFC. Briefly, Fig. 2a depicts the result of voltage generation with peak voltage production of 485 mV observed on day 26. Nonetheless, the voltage declines expeditiously to 50 mV at 40th day, reaching the end of the operation period. The recorded decrease in the performance by voltage might be as a result of depletion of bacterial biomass since the microbes may utilize the available nutrients and had attained the peak of their maturing phase38. Voltage output initially remained low but showed a gradual increase up to day 28, after which it started to decline. Voltage generation may also decline as exoelectrogens’ reduced metabolic efficiency due to substrate depletion, signaling bioremediation nearing completion. The peak voltage on day 26 could also indicate increased solubility of the toxic metal as reported in the prior study44. A polarization study examined how different levels of external resistance (ranging from 3.5 kΩ to 100 Ω) affect the efficiency of MFCs. The results, shown in Fig. 2b, indicated that high resistance hindered electron flow and reduced voltage stability. In contrast, low resistance facilitated faster electron transfer but negatively impacted stability by causing an increase in current. The progressive increase in voltage during the initial operational phase suggests microbial acclimatization and biofilm development on the anode surface, which enhances extracellular electron transfer efficiency. The stable conductivity observed throughout the experiment likely contributed to reduced internal resistance and improved electrochemical performance. Additionally, heavy metal removal may have occurred through a combination of microbial biosorption, bioaccumulation, and electrochemical reduction processes, highlighting the dual functionality of the system for both energy recovery and bioremediation. While the gradual decline may be due to reduced microbial activity, accumulation of metabolic by-product and depletion of nutrient availability.

On the day of highest electric current and voltage generation (day 8 and 9), the whole MFC set up were connected in series excluding the control and the set up was able to lit a green LED bulb. The pH showed a sudden rise followed by a gradual decrease which is similar to the report of45. It was observed that the pH had an impact on the amount of voltage and current obtained. This is because the pH has the ability to affect microbial activity and disturb electron and proton generation mechanism.

Conductivity and pH analysis

To assess electrolyte conductivity during MFC reaction, measurements were collected at certain time periods over a 40-day reaction. Figure 3a indicate that conductivity gradually rose from 150 µS/cm on day 1 to 500 µS/cm on day 40, indicating an increase in ionic strength. After day 10, conductivity increased significantly, reaching 500 µS/cm by the end of the MFC cycle. The highest conductivity as seen on the 40th day could have occurred as a result of rapid electrogenicity at the period. Nonetheless, the ensuing fall in conductivity could be a result of factors such as pH, redox potential, organic substrate availability, temperature, and bacterial durability may function in edging the MFC’s effectiveness46. Electrical conductivity in this study is a measure of the cow dung’s ability to facilitate the flow of electric current, which is crucial for its potential use in bioelectrochemical applications.

A trend in pH levels over a period of 40 days is illustrated in Fig. 3b. The starting pH was 6.12, which is close to neutral and conducive for microbial activity. By the end of day 40, the pH had declined to 5.40, reflecting a steady decrease throughout the duration of the process. This drop in pH may be attributed to the anodic microbes responsible for the oxidation of cow dung, which likely produced acidic metabolites. As these microbes carry out their metabolic processes, they generate organic acids, leading to a reduction in pH levels. There is a interdependence between the decreasing pH and the electron generation. Acidic conditions can enhance the electrochemical activeness of certain bacteria, that subsequently enhance electron transport to the anode47. The progressive decline in pH from 6.12 to 5.40 during operation is likely linked to proton buildup and the production of organic acids due to microbial metabolism. While the isolated bacterial strains (E. coli and B. subtilis) often demonstrate optimal development at circumneutral pH levels, they can withstand moderately acidic circumstances. Nonetheless, changes from the optimum pH can affect enzyme activity, metabolic rates, and extracellular electron transport mechanisms48–50. Furthermore, pH is pivotal in determining heavy metal speciation and solubility, thereby affecting biosorption and bioaccumulation efficacy. The detected acidity may have therefore influenced the overall bioremediation efficacy of the system. This indicates a possible constraint of the existing configuration, implying that pH control tactics may improve system stability and removal efficiency in subsequent research51. The spirited changes in pH reflect bacteria activeness and cow dung utilization in the MFCs. Hence, this underscores the importance of maintaining optimal pH levels to clinch potent electron production and bioremediation of contaminants52.

Cyclic voltammetry and EIS

The findings from the CV tests, collected over ranges of days, are presented in Fig. 4a. The graph includes datum for days 5, 10, 20, 30, and 40, plotting current (mA) against potential (V) with respect to an Ag/AgCl reference electrode. It displays distinct peaks related with redox processes, with the current response varying depending on the pertained voltage, signaling the presence of active redox molecules. In the positive potential range, oxidation peaks are observed, with a prominent peak occurring on day 5 at approximately 0.2 V. The current peak of oxidation increases throughout time, maximizing on day 30. This points to either an increase in the amount of electroactive organisms or enhanced electron mobility kinetics. However, by day 40, the peak current has dropped marginally, indicating a probable decrease in electroactive organism activity or degradation of substrate. Reduction peaks are seen in the negative potential range, including a noteworthy peak at −0.2 V on 5th day. Similar to the oxidation peaks, the reduction peak current climbs over period, reaching around day 30, then slightly decreasing by day 40. This pattern resembles that of the oxidation peaks. In the course of the oxidation process, electrons are transited from electroactive species to electrodes, resulting in positive current peaks on the CV spectra. It was found that increasing oxidation peak currents over time suggests increased microbial activity and improved electron transport53. In the reduction phase, electrons flow from the electrode to electroactive compounds, evidenced by negative peaks in the CV curve. Peak positions and shapes reveal electrochemical reaction kinetics: sharp peaks signify fast electron transfer, whereas broad peaks suggest slower kinetics54. Peak potential shifts during MFC operation can be caused by variations in electroactive species concentrations as well as local environmental conditions. Heavy metal remediation in bioelectrochemical systems correlates with current output, as indicated by distinct redox peaks related to toxic metal oxidation and reduction55. This method leverages microbes with electrocatalytic properties, which facilitate electron exchange. It shares similarities with MFC operations that use organic matter, such as animal dung, as substrates to promote bioelectrochemical reactions.

Specific capacitance is a crucial measurement in electrochemistry applied to evaluate biofilm development on the surface of anode of MFCs. Since its biofilm-based system, this approach enhances the anode’s ability to store and transport charge, which is indicated by higher capacitance values. The increasing pattern in specific capacitance as time elapses often signifies an active microbial community and improved substrate utilization56. This measurement also complements SEM and EDX analyses by providing a quantifiable assessment of the electrochemical activity associated with biofilms. Table 3 shows capacitance values measured using CV, indicating an active pattern during a 40-day period. An increase is seen in specific capacitance from 0.00003 F/g on day 5 to 0.00009 F/g on day 20, indicating enhanced electrogenicity along with biofilm formation. A decrease to 0.00007 F/g on day 30 shows a transitory stage in biofilm growth. By day 40, the specific capacitance rises to 0.00013 F/g, showcasing a stable biofilm with improved electron transfer efficiency. The development of a biofilm begins with bacteria adhering to the electrode surface. This initial attachment leads to the fabrication of multi-colonies and the production of extracellular polymeric substances, that enhance the structure of the biofilm. When biofilms develops, it improves in thickness and conductivity, which improves the efficiency of electron transport57.

The findings from the EIS analysis using cow dung serving as organic substrate are recorded over several days, as shown in Fig. 4b. EIS represents efficient method for analysing internal resistance and electrobiological mechanisms in MFCs. The plot depicts impedance against actual impedance (Z’) for days 15, 30, and 40. Initially, the impedance values became high, signaling significant resistance within the MFCs. By 30th day, the impedance starts to deflate, reflecting improved electron mobility and reduced internal resistance. This pattern prevails, with the lowest impedance observed on day 40, indicating maximal electrochemical efficiency. The reduction in impedance over time can be attributed to the development of the biofilm evolving around the electrodes. While the biofilm proliferates with increased conductivity, it facilitates efficient electron transfer, thereby lessening the overall resistance58. The oxidation MFCs involves the transition of electrons from utilized organic substrate, such as cow dung, to the anode. This approach is marked by a decrease in impedance, which indicates enhanced electron mobility kinetics. The rate of reduction influences the transition of electrons from the cathode to the electrogenic organisms, thus accomplishing the redox process. Improved electron transport rates increase the general efficiency of the MFCs59.

Bioremediation efficiency of the heavy metals

Bioremediation of toxic metals in MFCs is a crucial operating parameter studied during the recent time due to the dual ability to generate bioenergy and reduce contaminations. In the current study, the animal dung-based substrate was used for achieving these objectives. Toxic metal removal results observed in the study are illustrated in Table S1. The optimal 50 ppm concentration was earlier reported to be effective in heavy metal bioremediation in previous MFC system studies60. Their study determined the concentrations of Pb2+ and Cr6− and achieved the maximum metal removal effectiveness without harming the bacterial community. This indicates an excellent balance between remediation and microbial well-being. Metal ion concentrations declined steadily over the 40-day trial. By day 10, removal efficiency began to improve, resulting in around 70% reduction in all metal concentrations by day 20, demonstrating the system’s long-term effectiveness.

Statistical analysis of bioremediation efficacy

Various inferential statistical techniques were employed to understand the bioremediation of toxic metal ions with time. The details of statistical analysis are discussed as below.

Mean ± SE for bioremediation efficacy (%)

The interaction plot (Fig. 5) and Table S1 illustrate the mean bioremediation efficiency (%) ± standard error (SE) for five metal ions (Al³⁺, Cu²⁺, Hg²⁺, Pb²⁺, Zn²⁺) measured across four operational durations (10, 20, 30, and 40 days). Each line represents a different metal ion, allowing direct visualization of metal-specific temporal trends. It was observed that for all metal ions, bioremediation efficiency increased progressively from day 10 to day 40, indicating that operational duration is a dominant driver of metal removal. This pattern is consistent with well-established bioremediation kinetics, where longer contact time allows greater microbial adaptation and acclimation, increased biomass and biofilm formation along with Extracellular Polymeric Substances (EPS) resulting in enhanced sorption, complexation, and transformation of metal ions61–63.

Figure 5 also revealed clear differences among metals at each time point such as Pb²⁺ exhibits the highest removal efficiency throughout the experiment, reaching > 90% by day 40. The Cu²⁺ and Al³⁺ show strong and steady increases with time. However, Hg²⁺ and Zn²⁺ display comparatively lower efficiencies at early stages (days 10–20), followed by marked improvement at later stages (days 30 to 40). These differences reflect metal-specific chemical properties, including ionic radius, electronegativity, hydrolysis behavior, and affinity for microbial functional groups (–COOH, –OH, –NH₂, –PO₄). Pb²⁺ is known to bind strongly to biomass and EPS, whereas Hg²⁺ and Zn²⁺ often require longer exposure times due to toxicity effects and slower cellular uptake mechanisms63,64.

Analysis of variance (ANOVA two-way)

We wanted to understand the interaction between metal ions and operational days for bioremediation efficacy. Two-way ANOVA (Type III) revealed that bioremediation efficiency was significantly affected by metal ions (F₄,₄₀ = 821.06, p < 0.001) and operational days (F₃,₄₀ = 13093.10, p < 0.001) (Fig. 6). A significant interaction between metal ions and operational days was also observed (F₁₂,₄₀ = 232.31, p < 0.001), indicating that temporal changes in removal efficiency varied among metals. Such metal and time-dependent behavior is consistent with established heavy-metal bioremediation mechanisms, including adsorption/biosorption kinetics, biofilm maturation, and metal-specific toxicity and binding affinities65. The role of contact time and operational days in effective bioremediation is also well established. It has been found that contact time is a core driver of heavy-metal removal kinetics in biosorption/bioremediation, and biofilm growth and structure that can markedly enhance pollutant removal over time66,67.

Tukey’s post-hoc analysis

The post-hoc Tukey HSD analysis was performed following a significant two-way ANOVA68 to identify which specific combinations of metal ions and operational days differed significantly in bioremediation efficiency (%).

The results show that most pairwise comparisons between metal–day combinations are statistically significant (p < 0.001) (Table S2), indicating strong and consistent differences in removal efficiency across both metal type and treatment duration. Comparisons across increasing operational days generally exhibit positive mean differences, confirming that bioremediation efficiency improves significantly with time. At a given operational day, metal ions also differ significantly in efficiency, reflecting metal-specific removal mechanisms and affinities. In contrast, a small number of non-significant comparisons (p > 0.05) occur primarily between closely related treatments, such as adjacent operational days or metals with similar chemical behavior, indicating comparable efficiencies under those specific conditions.

It was found that time effect has huge impact on metal bioremediation. For example, the Table S2 revealed that at day 10, (Al³⁺ 10) vs. (Cu²⁺ 10) Mean Difference = − 3.147 ± 0.538, p<0.001. It means that Al³⁺ at day 10 has around 3.15% lower efficiency than Cu²⁺, and this difference is statistically significant after Tukey adjustment. Further comparison with increasing number of days confirmed the similar pattern of increasing Al³⁺ bioremediation with time. (Al³⁺ 10) vs. (Al³⁺ 20) Mean Difference = − 24.133 ± 0.538, p<0.001, (Al³⁺ 10) vs. (Al³⁺ 30) Mean Difference = − 38.127 ± 0.538, p<0.001, (Al³⁺ 10) vs. (Al³⁺ 40) Mean Difference = − 46.293 ± 0.538, p<0.001. Since the difference is negative, it means that bioremediation efficiency at day 10 is much lower than at later days (20, 30, 40). This supports the ANOVA result that Operational days strongly affects bioremediation efficiency.

Another pattern was that metal differences depend on the day emphasizing the importance of interaction. For example, on day 10, some metals were statistically similar while others differ. It was observed that (Al³⁺ 10) vs. (Zn²⁺ 10) Mean Difference = − 0.800 ± 0.538, p = 0.992 (No statistically significant difference). Whereas (Zn²⁺ 10) vs. (Hg²⁺ 10) Mean Difference = − 2.187 ± 0.538, p < 0.05, and (Al³⁺ 10) vs. (Pb²⁺ 10) Mean Difference = − 10.400 ± 0.538, p<0.001.

In brief, the Metal × Day interaction showed that bioremediation efficiency increased significantly with operational time for all metals (e.g., day 10 vs. day 20 to 40, all p < 0.001). Metal-specific differences were most evident at early operational days (e.g., Pb²⁺ at day 10 differed from Al³⁺, Cu²⁺, Zn²⁺, and Hg²⁺; p < 0.001), whereas by day 40 several metal pairs were not significantly different (e.g., Al³⁺ vs. Cu²⁺; p = 1.000), indicating convergence of efficiencies at later stages.

Overall, the post-hoc results corroborate the significant Metal × Operational Days interaction, demonstrating that temporal improvements in bioremediation efficiency are metal dependent. By day 40, the bioremediation system had likely reached a stage where efficiency levels converge across several metals (or variability is small), so many pairs are statistically indistinguishable (p > 0.05).

SEM and EDX analysis of anode biofilms

In a typical MFC, it is expected that a significant number of microbes will be located around the anode electrodes, while few will be found at the cathode. This expectation has been confirmed by our scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images in Fig. 9a,b. The SEM images illustrate the diversity and amplitude of the bacterial counts surrounding the anode electrode, indicating successful colonization supported by sufficient cow dung, which facilitates their development and metabolic activities77. Furthermore, the SEM-EDX analysis (Fig. 9c) confirmed the absence of toxicity, providing proof that the MFC system can sustain an operable microbial community for the bioelectrochemical transformation of organic substrates. The cow dung serving as organic substrate is a crucial element in the MFCs because it mainly supports microbe growth and provides electrons for power production. The higher bacterial density on the anode, contrasted with lesser colonies on the cathode, supports the traditional notion that microbial electrogenesis predominantly occurs at the anode. SEM observations of rod-shaped appendages corroborate previous studies, suggesting these structures are indicative of pili-producing bacteria that facilitate electron conduction78.

In addition, the biofilm was analysed using EDX to see if there were any adsorption effects on the anode’s surface. The research revealed that no harmful elements were found on the facet of the biofilm, implying that the system was free of toxic substances. Furthermore, no hazardous metals were found, indicating that no adsorption tendencies had taken places. Minor amounts of sulphur and copper were also discovered, most likely as a result of copper wire adsorption during MFC setup. These results (see Fig. 9c) show that the biofilm formed unconstrained by harmful substances, most as a result of lack of adsorption effects.

Challenges and future prospects

MFCs have the potential to serve as bioelectrochemical systems for sustainable energy generation and wastewater bioremediation. However, their effectiveness is currently hindered by several incessant challenges (as illustrated in Fig. S1). Recent research has identified a number of issues that need to be addressed to enable large-scale applications of MFCs81–84. These include optimizing electron transport and generation, as well as reducing setup costs. Additionally, conventional organic substrates have proven insufficient for MFC performance, leading to a shift toward using biomass waste materials and food wastes. While this shift has improved performance, achieving commercial viability for MFCs through this approach has yet to be fully realized. Several critical factors, such as anode materials, configurations, PEM materials, and the impact of external resistance, must be explored to optimize MFC performance. To address some of these issues in the future, developing an organic substrate that offers dual high stability and a rich metabolic constituent for microbial creation would be valuable85,86. A limitation of the present study is the absence of a control system without electrodes or an external electrical circuit. While active voltage generation and impedance studies indicates the existence of bioelectrochemical processes, it remains indeterminate what proportion of the observed heavy metal removal was exclusively attributable to electrochemical mechanisms as opposed to passive biosorption or physicochemical interactions with the organic matrix. Further research should include abiotic and open-circuit controls to quantitatively distinguish between adsorption-driven and electrochemically mediated remediation mechanisms. This research does not have detailed microbial analysis; future studies should integrate advanced characterisation techniques for isolated strains and molecular identification methods (e.g., 16 S rRNA sequencing) to enhance microbial characterisation and reinforce species-level confirmation. Additionally, the choice of electrode materials presents a significant challenge in MFC technology. Using inefficient electrode materials can lead to low energy production, making the anode particularly significant for electron transfer and biofilm proliferation. Current studies emphasize the potential of electrodes made from waste materials, which offer an economical and efficient option. These waste-based electrodes demonstrate promising performance, making them an attractive choice for sustainable applications87–89.

Conclusion

This work reveals that animal dung, primarily cow dung, provides an effective and sustainable substrate for improving the dual functioning of MFCs in bioelectrochemical energy generation and toxic metal bioremediation. The MFC system worked for 40 days and produced a peak voltage of 485 mV, demonstrating that cow dung could sustain microbial metabolism and the electron transfer mechanisms required for stable electrical power production. The system recorded high removal efficiencies for metal contaminants: 89.16 ± 0.04% for Al3+, 89.17 ± 0.46% for Cu2+, 92.86 ± 0.11% for Pb2+, 91.37 ± 0.4% for Zn2+, and 92.01 ± 0.16% for Hg2+. The study demonstrates that bioremediation efficiency is strongly governed by both metal identity and operational duration. Two-way ANOVA revealed significant effects of metal ions and operational days, as well as a significant Metal × Day interaction (p < 0.001), indicating metal-specific temporal responses. Progressive increases in removal efficiency with time highlight the importance of sufficient operational duration for effective bioremediation, while differences among metals reflect distinct removal kinetics and interactions with microbial biomass and extracellular polymeric substances. These findings provide quantitative evidence for optimizing time-dependent bioremediation strategies tailored to specific metal contaminants. These results also demonstrate the system’s strong capacity to remediate metal-contaminated wastewater. Microbial analysis revealed a broad group of probable electroactive and biologically adaptive bacteria, comprising Bacillus subtilis, Providencia rettgeri, Escherichia coli, Serratia marcescens, Staphylococcus aureus, Morganella morganii, and Providencia alcalifaciens. All these microorganisms helped to degrade the substrate and generate electrons. The results indicate that cow dung is not only an easily accessible and low-cost bioresource, but also an effective substrate for operating MFCs. The integration of waste-to-energy conversion with simultaneous metal removal highlights the potential of dung-based MFCs as a scalable and environmentally friendly technology for decentralized wastewater treatment, environmental restoration, and renewable energy production in resource-limited areas.
