Association Between Health Insurance Type and Survival Outcomes: Insight Into the Impact of Socioeconomic Disparities in the Postoperative Outcomes of Abdominal Aortic Aneurysm in Korea
Authors: Hong-Jae Choi, Joon Seo Lim, Won Mo Jang, Se Jin Oh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAbdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) is associated with a high mortality rate if left untreated, and medically vulnerable populations experience significant disparities in treatment and outcomes. We investigated the characteristics and treatment outcomes of AAA according to the insurance type (i.e., National Health Insurance [NHI] and Medical Aid) in South Korea using the nationwide health insurance database.MethodsFrom 2002 to 2019, a total of 15,065 patients underwent either open aneurysm repair (OAR) or endovascular aneurysm repair (EVAR). The primary outcome was AAA-related mortality, with follow-up conducted until death or December 31, 2019, for those without mortality records. Mortality was evaluated using Kaplan-Meier curves and an adjusted Cox proportional hazards model to identify independent risk factors for AAA-related mortality. Propensity score matching (PSM) was employed using relevant covariates to achieve a 1:10 match between the NHI and Medical Aid groups.ResultsAmong the EVAR patients, the rate of AAA-related mortality was significantly higher in the Medical Aid group compared to the NHI group (P < 0.001). After adjusting for covariates, the difference in AAA-related mortality rates between the two insurance groups remained statistically significant. No significant difference was found between the two groups among OAR patients (P = 0.727). Factors such as age, insurance type, hypertension, diastolic blood pressure, hemoglobin levels, and glomerular filtration rate were significantly associated with AAA-related mortality in the unadjusted multivariable analysis among the EVAR patients. Specifically, Medical Aid patients had a 1.87-fold higher risk of AAA-related mortality compared to NHI patients. Notably, in the PSM cohort, multivariable analysis confirmed that insurance type remained a significant factor for AAA-related mortality in EVAR patients.ConclusionIn patients undergoing EVAR for AAA, insurance type was independently associated with postoperative AAA-related mortality, with Medical Aid patients experiencing worse outcomes. In contrast, no significant difference in AAA-related mortality was observed between insurance types among patients undergoing OAR. These findings highlight the need to improve postoperative care and address outcome disparities in vulnerable populations undergoing EVAR.Keywords: Abdominal Aortic Aneurysms, Health Insurance, Healthcare Disparities, Open Aneurysm Repair, Endovascular Aneurysm Repair, Mortality

INTRODUCTION

Abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) is a pathological dilation of the abdominal aorta, which has a high mortality rate of up to 80% upon rupture,1,2,3 Surgical is the primary treatment method for AAA,1,4,5 and the main surgical options include open aneurysm repair (OAR) and endovascular aneurysm repair (EVAR).6 The outcomes of AAA treatment are influenced by patient characteristics, comorbidities, and healthcare accessibility.7,8,9,10,11 Low income and education levels are also associated with poorer health outcomes in AAA.12 Consequently, insurance type is a crucial factor that determines both healthcare accessibility and quality, thereby impacting surgical outcomes.10 Accordingly, studies have shown that compared to privately insured patients, low-income patients have a 47% higher mortality rate after AAA surgery and uninsured patients have a 102% higher mortality rate.11

Previous studies have indicated that low-income groups are more than twice as likely to develop AAA compared to high-income groups.13 These vulnerable populations have higher prevalence rates of comorbidities such as hypertension and cardiovascular diseases, which increase the likelihood of developing AAA.3,14,15 Moreover, vulnerable populations typically have higher rates of chronic diseases and lower healthcare accessibility.12 The diagnosis and treatment processes for AAA in these populations revealed significant healthcare disparities, primarily due to a lack of regular health check-ups or imaging examinations that could detect AAA in its early stages.13 Vulnerable patients are less likely to have AAA detected early or to receive appropriate follow-up care,13 often resulting in late-stage diagnoses after the rupture of the aneurysm.15,16 These delays in diagnosis and treatment not only postpone the identification of the condition but also increase the risk of complications and mortality after surgery.2,3,16

While many studies have reported lower rates of early management and higher rates of all-cause mortality in vulnerable populations,11,17 these studies often have limitations as they were limited to the Medicare population,9,18,19 focused on a single procedure rather than a head-to-head comparison,9 reported relatively short-term outcomes,10,11,19 or predominantly concentrated on western populations.9,10,11,18,19,20 Therefore, this study investigated the impact of insurance type on AAA-related mortality following OAR or EVAR by using data from the National Health Insurance (NHI) Service database, which encompassed the entire population of South Korea (hereafter referred to as Korea), including the Medical Aid population.

METHODS

Data source

The Korean National Health Insurance System (NHIS) consists of two major healthcare programs that provide universal coverage to all residents in South Korea: NHI and Medical Aid. Approximately 97% of the population is enrolled in NHI, while the remaining 3% are covered by Medical Aid. The NHIS offers biennial health examinations to all Koreans and maintains a comprehensive database that includes demographic information, disease diagnoses based on International Classification of Diseases-10 (ICD-10) codes, treatments, and prescribed medications. Data for this study were extracted from the NHIS database, covering the period from 2002 to 2019.

Data are available from the Korea National Health Insurance Sharing Service Institutional Data Access/Ethics Committee (https://nhiss.nhis.or.kr/bd/ay/bdaya001iv.do) for researchers who meet the criteria for access to confidential data. Researchers can apply for the NHI data-sharing service upon approval of the Institutional Review Board of their institution. After review by the Korea National Health Insurance Sharing Service Institutional Data Access/Ethics Committee, authors are required to pay a data access fee and confirm that other researchers will be able to access the data in the same manner as the authors.

Sampled patients

We extracted data from the NHIS database on all patients with newly diagnosed aortic aneurysms from 2002 to 2019 (n = 145,672). Patients without AAA (n = 24,344) and those with ruptured AAA (n = 8,357) were excluded. We focused on patients with AAA codes who underwent surgical treatment and excluded those who did not receive (n = 97,125). Surgical treatments were classified based on the initial surgery performed, identified by the codes for and EVAR; for example, if a patient underwent EVAR followed by OAR, the treatment was categorized as EVAR. The code definition was presented Supplementary Table 1. Patients under 18 years old (n = 19) and those with data errors (n = 762) were also excluded, resulting in a final cohort of 15,065 patients. Among them, 2,753 underwent OAR and 12,312 underwent EVAR (Fig. 1).

Data collection and definition

From the NHIS database, we collected the following patient characteristics: age, sex, residential area, smoking status, physical activity, body mass index, waist circumference, and family history of hypertension (HTN) or diabetes mellitus (DM). We also gathered information on clinical characteristics (i.e., systolic and diastolic blood pressure [DBP], the duration from diagnosis to surgery, and comorbidities such as HTN, DM, dyslipidemia, coronary artery disease, and chronic kidney disease) and laboratory findings (i.e., hemoglobin, fasting blood sugar, total cholesterol, aspartate transaminase, alanine transaminase, triglycerides, gamma-glutamyltransferase [GGT], high-density lipoprotein, low-density lipoprotein, creatinine, and glomerular filtration rate [GFR]). Residential area was classified as urban or rural. Physical activity was classified based on whether the individual engaged in moderate exercise ≥ 5 days/week or vigorous exercise ≥ 3 days/week. Lifestyle and laboratory data were derived from national health screenings. Comorbidities were defined using the ICD-10 codes (Supplementary Table 1).

Outcomes

The primary outcome was AAA-related mortality, and secondary outcomes included all-cause mortality, cost, length of hospital stay, follow-up times, and follow-up computed tomography (CT) scans. AAA-related mortality was defined using specific cause-of-death codes from death records, as listed in Supplementary Table 1.21 AAA-related mortality was primarily defined as cases where the cause of death diagnosis included the I71 code, which represents aortic aneurysm and dissection. Additionally, AAA-related mortality included cases where at least one of the following associated diagnosis codes was reported, as these represent major complications linked to AAA-related mortality: K66 (including intraperitoneal hemorrhage), R58 (hemorrhage not elsewhere classified [NOS]), R57 (shock, unspecified), and I77 (including rupture of an artery). Costs were defined as the total reimbursed medical expenses incurred during the hospitalization period for surgery, following a review by the Health Insurance Review and Assessment Service. Outpatient follow-up was confirmed through visits associated with AAA diagnosis codes, and CT follow-up was verified using the relevant treatment codes (Supplementary Table 1). Patients without death records were monitored until December 31, 2019.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are expressed as means and standard deviations (SDs), and categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages. Group comparisons were performed using independent t-tests for continuous variables and χ2 tests for categorical variables. A multivariate Cox proportional hazards (PHs) regression model was used to evaluate the association between AAA-related mortality among patients who underwent surgical treatment. Hazard ratios and 95% confidence intervals were calculated. Covariates with a P value < 0.05 in the univariate Cox PH analysis (i.e., age, sex, insurance type, lifestyle factors, comorbidities, and laboratory values) were included in the multivariate Cox PH model. Cumulative outcomes for AAA patients were evaluated using Kaplan-Meier curves, and differences between groups were analyzed using the log-rank test. To account for potential confounders (e.g., age, sex, GFR, and hemoglobin levels), an adjusted Cox PHs model was applied. A propensity score was generated using logistic regression, incorporating age, sex, GFR, and hemoglobin levels as variables. One-to-ten propensity score matching (PSM) was performed using the nearest neighbor method, with Medical Aid patients matched to NHI patients at a 1:10 ratio. The balance of the matched cohort was evaluated using standardized mean differences and statistical significance tests. A standardized difference between −0.10 and 0.10 was deemed indicative of appropriate balance, confirming that both groups were well-balanced and homogeneous. Statistical significance was set at a P value < 0.05 with two-sided testing. The relative importance of variables was determined through a leave-one-out analysis, which involves assessing the difference in the model’s performance metric (e.g., C-index) by sequentially removing each covariate from the full model.22 All statistical analyses were performed using SAS Enterprise Guide 7.15 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) or Python 3.8.16 (Python Software Foundation, Beaverton, OR, USA).

Ethics statement

The study was conducted according to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Seoul Metropolitan Government-Seoul National University (SMG-SNU) Boramae Medical Center (IRB No. 07-2023-33). Due to the retrospective nature of the study, the informed consent was waived by the ethics committee of SMG-SNU Boramae Medical Center. All data were anonymous and de-personalized.

RESULTS

Participant characteristics

From 2002 to 2019, a total of 15,065 patients underwent either OAR (n = 2,753) or EVAR (n = 12,312) for AAA. The number of AAA surgeries showed an overall increasing trend during the study period. OAR procedures peaked at 549 cases in 2008, while EVAR procedures steadily increased, reaching 1,546 cases in 2018. Analysis of surgery types based on insurance status revealed that 81.3% of the NHI group and 87.8% of the Medical Aid group underwent EVAR; within the Medical Aid group, the majority (87.8%) of the patients underwent EVAR and only 12.2% underwent OAR (Fig. 2).

Outcomes of AAA patients after surgery

All-cause mortality and AAA-related mortality in the overall cohort

The median survival time was significantly longer in the OAR group (12.4 years) compared to the EVAR group (7.3 years; P < 0.001). Initially, the OAR group exhibited higher mortality; however, after 276 days, the EVAR group showed higher mortality rates (Fig. 3A). In contrast, after adjusting for age, the survival difference between the OAR and EVAR groups was no longer statistically significant (P = 0.307) (Fig. 3B). When analyzed according to insurance type, the median survival time was 9.2 years for the NHI group and 5.5 years for the Medical Aid group (P < 0.001) (Fig. 3C). AAA-related mortality also differed significantly according to the insurance type (P < 0.001) (Fig. 3E). After age adjustment, all-cause mortality (P < 0.001) (Fig. 3D) and AAA-related mortality (P = 0.010) (Fig. 3F) remained statistically significant according to insurance type.

Additionally, PSM was performed at a 1:10 ratio, with Medical Aid patients matched to NHI patients while adjusting for age, sex, GFR, and hemoglobin levels. The balance of the matched cohort was assessed using standardized mean differences, which were measured at ≤ 0.1. Additionally, no statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups, confirming that both groups were well-balanced and homogeneous (Supplementary Table 3). In the PSM cohort, AAA-related mortality remained significantly different between the insurance types among patients who underwent EVAR (P = 0.005; Supplementary Fig. 2B).

In contrast, among the OAR patients, there was no significant difference in AAA-related mortality between the two insurance groups (P = 0.727) (Fig. 4C), while the median survival time for all-cause mortality was significantly different between the two groups (12.7 vs. 6.9 years; P < 0.001) (Fig. 4D). Supplementary Fig. 3 shows the survival curves for AAA-related mortality in the OAR cohort following stepwise adjustments for age and sex; none of the models indicated statistically significant differences between the insurance types.

Outcomes according to insurance type

Table 3 compares the postoperative outcomes of patients based on the insurance type. The overall AAA-related mortality was 7.1% (EVAR, 6.0% vs. OAR, 12.1%). The overall all-cause mortality was 37.9% (34.5% vs. 53.0%). Among the EVAR patients, the AAA-related mortality rate was 5.8% for the NHI group and 8.3% for the Medical Aid group (P = 0.004). All-cause mortality also differed significantly by insurance type (P < 0.001). The length of hospital stay was significantly shorter in the NHI group compared to the Medical Aid group (11.6 vs. 14.4 days; P < 0.001). The average annual number of outpatient visits was 3.39 in the NHI group and 3.75 in the Medical Aid group (P = 0.083).

DISCUSSION

This study utilized nationwide data to compare the postoperative outcomes of AAA patients according to the insurance type. The results indicated significant differences in surgical treatment methods and AAA-related mortality according to the insurance type. Contrary to trends in other settings where more invasive treatments like OAR are more prevalent among Medical Aid patients, Korean AAA patients who were on Medical Aid had a high proportion of those who underwent EVAR (87.8%). Among patients who underwent EVAR, the Medical Aid group showed higher rates of AAA-related mortality. Multivariable analysis suggested that insurance type may be associated with an increased risk of AAA-related mortality among those who underwent EVAR.

Previous studies have mostly reported higher mortality rates in the Medical Aid group, regardless of the surgical method for treating AAA.11,23 These differences could partially be due to the fact that while EVAR requires long-term follow-up and regular imaging to manage complications such as endoleaks,24,25 financial constraints could hinder adequate long-term follow-up in Medical Aid patients.11,26,27 We found that Medical Aid patients had longer hospital stays compared to NHI patients in both surgical groups, likely reflecting the severity of comorbidities in this population. Among patients who underwent EVAR, AAA-related mortality was significantly higher in Medical Aid patients, whereas among those who underwent OAR, there was no significant difference in AAA-related mortality between the two insurance groups. In the OAR cohort, multivariable analysis identified age as the only significant risk factor for AAA-related mortality. Although there was a difference in age between the two insurance groups (63.0 vs. 66.4 years), it was not substantial enough to have a significant impact on AAA-related mortality. Multivariable Cox PH analysis further identified insurance type as an independent risk factor for AAA-related mortality in EVAR patients, emphasizing the critical role of insurance type in patient outcomes post-EVAR.

Unexpectedly, our study found no significant difference in outpatient follow-up frequency between Medical Aid and NHI groups. In fact, although not statistically significant, Medical Aid patients had more frequent annual outpatient visits compared to NHI patients. Garg et al.9 also found no significant association between follow-up visit frequency and AAA-related mortality among Medicare patients, which is in line with our findings. Additionally, although most patients did not undergo regular imaging follow-up, there was no significant difference in the imaging frequency between the two insurance groups in our study.

The disparities in AAA-related mortality based on insurance type may be explained from three perspectives: 1) healthcare utilization behavior, 2) health behavior, and 3) socioeconomic factors. First, while Korea provides universal health insurance,28 disparities in “amenable mortality” (i.e., mortality that could be prevented with timely and appropriate healthcare), may persist due to differences in healthcare utilization levels.29,30 A study evaluating the effects of Korea's 2017 NHI coverage expansion found that, despite increased coverage and reduced financial burden, regional disparities in amenable mortality were not significantly reduced.29,30 Moreover, a lack of health literacy and medical knowledge,17 as well as differences in healthcare utilization (e.g., management of chronic conditions) may still contribute to differences in mortality outcomes based on insurance type. Previous research has indicated that patients with AAA may not understand up to 30% of medical terminology and are less likely to comprehend AAA risk factors, treatment options, and outcomes.31

Second, Medical Aid patients are more likely to exhibit unfavorable health behaviors.7 While many patients attempt to quit smoking after cardiovascular surgery,32,33 those with lower levels of education find it more difficult to quit33 and are more prone to relapses.32 Medical Aid patients may also find it difficult to adhere to post-surgery health behaviors due to low health literacy, particularly in areas such as weight management.34 Moreover, these patients may have difficulties in procuring funds related to gym memberships, healthy food, and other health-promoting activities.34 While our study adjusted for some health behaviors, it did not account for the intensity or severity of these behaviors.

Third, disparities in mortality may also be influenced by broader socioeconomic factors, including health literacy, access to continuous care, and the ability to effectively engage with the healthcare system.35 Socioeconomic factors such as material resources, psychosocial stressors, and behavioral patterns significantly influence how individuals engage with healthcare.35 These material, psychosocial, and behavioral factors play critical roles in explaining disparities in “preventable mortality” (i.e., mortality that could be averted with effective public health efforts), particularly among socioeconomically disadvantaged groups.35 It is possible that differences in AAA-related mortality by insurance type are influenced by these factors, which were not fully captured in the analysis. Variables such as income, education level, and psychosocial stressors could also significantly impact patient outcomes. Ultimately, the observed differences in AAA-related mortality can be interpreted as stemming from disparities in avoidable mortality.29 Our findings highlight that disparities in AAA-related mortality among Medical Aid patients cannot be solely attributed to healthcare utilization metrics such as outpatient visit frequency, medical expenditures, or hospital length of stay. Instead, these differences should be understood within the broader context of health system inequities and socioeconomic vulnerabilities.

Notably, the surgical treatment trends for Medical Aid patients with AAA in the current Korean cohort differed from those reported in previous studies.10,11 Globally, Medical Aid patients generally undergo invasive surgeries like OAR10,11 due to the higher rate of ruptured AAAs15,16 and lower cost.36 However, in our study, 87.8% of Medical Aid patients underwent EVAR; this can be attributed to Korea's unique health insurance system, where the government covers most medical costs for Medical Aid patients.28 Despite the higher total cost of EVAR compared to OAR, the patient's share is only 0% to 15% of the total cost,28,37 reducing cost-related constraints in choosing EVAR.

Although it may be possible that excluding ruptured AAA patients from the study cohort could have led to the differences between our study and previous research, the surgical trends—including those involving ruptured AAA patients who underwent surgery—did not show notable differences (Supplementary Fig. 4). When deciding surgical treatment plans, key factors such as patient age, comorbidities, hospital stay duration, and potential complications are considered.38 Our study results showed that among patients receiving EVAR, those who were on Medical Aid had a higher risk of AAA-related mortality. Further research is needed to explore how other aspects of healthcare utilization, such as continuous chronic disease management and public health initiatives, contribute to the observed disparities in AAA survival. Understanding these factors may help address and reduce the impact of insurance type on long-term AAA-related mortality.

There are several limitations to this study. First, as a retrospective study based on nationwide population data, it is subject to various biases, including selection bias. We therefore included all available data to minimize these biases. Second, identifying AAA and other risk factors solely based on diagnostic codes may have introduced inaccuracies. Additionally, as the majority of patients were classified under AAA codes, and only a small proportion had codes that could include thoracoabdominal aortic aneurysm, their impact on the overall findings is expected to be limited. There is also a potential overestimation of AAA-related mortality due to the reliance on cause-of-death codes that lacked detailed clinical information. Nevertheless, the majority of deaths were classified under I71 codes, which is consistent with previous definitions of AAA-related mortality. Third, this study is subject to the inherent limitations of administrative data, as clinical information on AAA characteristics—such as diameter and endoleak status—was not available, nor were psychosocial factors like stress. Fourth, changes in the exercise survey format from 2009 onward may have introduced response bias. As EVAR patients were more likely to respond to the revised questionnaire, their answers may have been reported with greater completeness compared to those of OAR patients in earlier years. Lastly, since the study population consists entirely of Koreans, the results may not be generalizable to other racial or ethnic groups.

We found significant differences in treatment methods and postoperative mortality rates in AAA patients based on insurance type. Notably, Medical Aid patients underwent EVAR more frequently and experienced significantly higher AAA-related mortality compared to NHI patients. Conversely, there was no significant difference in AAA-related mortality between insurance groups for patients who underwent OAR. Multivariable analysis identified insurance type as an independent risk factor for AAA-related mortality in EVAR patients. These results highlight the importance of considering the patient's insurance type when planning surgical treatment for AAA to ensure optimal outcomes and effectively address healthcare disparities.
