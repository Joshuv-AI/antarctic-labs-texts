Dynamics of interfaces in the two-dimensional wave-pinning model
Shunsuke Kobayashi, Koya Sakakibara, Taikei Uechi

Abstract
We study the mass-conserved reaction-diffusion system known as the wave-pinning model, which serves as a minimal framework for describing cell polarity. In this model, the interplay between reaction kinetics and slow diffusion forms a sharp interface that partitions the domain into high- and low-concentration regions. We perform a detailed asymptotic analysis and derive higher-order approximation equations governing the motion of this interface. Our results show that on a fast timescale, the interface evolves via propagating front dynamics, whereas on a slow timescale, it evolves as an area-preserving mean curvature flow. Furthermore, using the derived free boundary problem, we demonstrate that on a significantly slower timescale, an interface whose endpoints lie on the domain boundary drifts along the boundary toward regions of higher curvature. In summary, our analysis reveals that the interface dynamics in the wave-pinning model exhibit a hierarchy of three distinct timescales: wave propagation on a fast timescale, curvature-driven area-preserving evolution on a slow timescale, and motion along the boundary on a significantly slower timescale.

In this paper, we study the wave-pinning model, which has emerged as a minimal and analytically tractable framework for explaining how cells achieve polarity
[

,

Biologically, this model captures the dynamics of Rho GTPases, which cycle between active membrane-bound and inactive cytosolic forms.
These dynamics are distilled into a mass-conserved reaction-diffusion system on a bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
:

\displaystyle\varepsilon\partial_{t}u=\varepsilon^{2}\Delta u+f(u,v),

\displaystyle\quad\text{in }\Omega\times(0,\infty),

(1.1a)

\displaystyle\varepsilon\partial_{t}v=D\Delta v-f(u,v),

\displaystyle\quad\text{in }\Omega\times(0,\infty),

(1.1b)

\displaystyle\partial_{\nu}u=\partial_{\nu}v=0,

\displaystyle\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega\times(0,\infty),

The functions

Detailed assumptions for this model are given in Section 2.
The generation of polarity in this framework hinges on three essential properties: (i) a large disparity in diffusion coefficients (

To understand the mechanism intuitively, imagine a local increase in the active form

To elucidate the detailed dynamics of this wave-pinning process, formal asymptotic analysis has been employed to characterize the front behavior as
\varepsilon

[

Complementing these formal results, Gomez, Lam, and Mori rigorously demonstrated that the front solution to the shadow wave-pinning system converges to this sharp interface limit

Beyond these fundamental analyses, the model has been extended to incorporate various biological factors, such as source and sink terms

In addition, the model has been applied to studies of cell motility

Here, we focus on the detailed dynamics of the interface after the initial front formation.
The interface equations derived in previous studies, such as

As noted by

Incorporating such higher-order corrections is expected to regularize the dynamics and reveal that the interface evolves on a slow timescale analogously to volume-preserving mean curvature flow, as suggested by the literature

Similarly, for the bulk-surface coupled wave-pinning model, Miller et al. employed timescale separation techniques to demonstrate that the interface evolves as an area-preserving geodesic curvature flow

However, for the original wave-pinning model, a rigorous analysis of the slow dynamics is lacking.
Consequently, the derivation of a unified interface equation that remains valid across multiple timescales is an open problem.

Through numerical simulations, another intriguing phenomenon regarding the interface motion of the wave-pinning model has been identified: the “shape-sensing phenomenon”

In this process, high-activity domains drift very slowly along the boundary of the domain

Notably, the timescale of this phenomenon is distinct from, and typically slower than, the curvature flow regime described above.
Such geometry-dependent slow motion is not unique to the wave-pinning model; it has been observed in various reaction-diffusion systems, including the Allen-Cahn equation, suggesting it is a universal behavior driven by the interaction between the interface and the boundary.
This type of geometry-dependent slow evolution is classically referred to as “slow dynamics” or “metastability”, which has been extensively studied in the mathematical analysis of reaction-diffusion systems

Biologically, shape-sensing is a well-observed phenomenon; this is summarized in

In their model, the migration of the high-activity domain along the cell boundary is essential for driving rotational cell motion.
Therefore, providing a theoretical understanding of the internal wave-pinning dynamics—specifically, how the interface interacts with the domain geometry—would offer valuable insights.

To the best of our knowledge, the derivation of the interface equations that approximate dynamics occurring across multiple timescales has not been established in the literature, with the notable exception of the wave-pinning model. We highlight this as a key mathematical novelty of the present work.

Furthermore, based on the derived free boundary problem, we investigate the behavior of an interface whose endpoints lie on the domain boundary.
We demonstrate that such an interface drifts along the boundary toward regions of higher curvature with a speed of
O(\varepsilon^{2})
.
Specifically, assuming the interface can be approximated by a sufficiently small circular arc, we represent its center using the arc-length parametrization
X^{0}(s)
of the boundary
\partial\Omega

We then show that the evolution of the position

is governed by the following approximation:

\frac{d}{dt}s(t)=\frac{4\varepsilon^{2}}{3\pi}K_{s}(s(t)),

denotes the derivative of the curvature

In summary, the interface evolves as a propagating front on the

timescale.
This hierarchy corresponds to a biological narrative of polarity establishment: First, the cell rapidly establishes a polarized front (fast timescale). Second, the polarized domain relaxes its shape to minimize interfacial tension while preserving its size (slow timescale). Finally, on a much longer timescale, the cell “senses” its geometric confinement, drifting toward regions of high curvature to stabilize its orientation (very slow timescale).

Formal derivation of the interface equation

In this section we present a formal derivation of the free boundary problem
eq.

1.2
for the wave-pinning model
eq.

1.1
.
We perform the subsequent analysis under the following three assumptions for the reaction term
f
, adopting the formulation by
[
28
]
:

Bistability condition.

For any given
v
in a certain range
[v_{\mathrm{min}},v_{\mathrm{max}}]
, the function
f(\cdot,v)
is bistable.
This means that the equation
f(u,v)=0
for
u
has three roots
u_{-}(v)<u_{m}(v)<u_{+}(v)
for each
v\in[v_{\mathrm{min}},v_{\mathrm{max}}]
, satisfying

f_{u}(u_{\pm}(v),v)<0,\quad f_{u}(u_{m}(v),v)>0.

(2.1)

Homogeneous stability condition.

The homogeneous states
(u,v)\equiv(u_{\pm}(v),v)
for
v\in[v_{\mathrm{min}},v_{\mathrm{max}}]

are stable equilibria for the system
eq.

1.1
. In their linear stability analysis, Mori, Jilkine, and Edelstein-Keshet
[
28
]
observed that this stability condition can be reduced to the following:

f_{u}(u_{\pm}(v),v)-f_{v}(u_{\pm}(v),v)<0.

(2.2)

Velocity sign condition.

For the function

I(v)=\int_{u_{-}(v)}^{u_{+}(v)}f(u,v)du,

(2.3)

there exists a unique
v_{c}\in(v_{\mathrm{min}},v_{\mathrm{max}})
such that
I(v_{c})=0
, with

being positive for
v>v_{c}
, negative for

. In addition, it holds that the derivative of

at

is strictly positive.

The velocity sign condition will be used in Section 3.
These conditions are indispensable kinetic requirements for the wave-pinning phenomenon.
Another key component for the wave-pinning model is the conservation of the total mass:

\int_{\Omega}(u+v)dx=M,

(2.4)

where

M=\int_{\Omega}(u(x,0)+v(x,0))dx.

Let
(u^{\varepsilon},v^{\varepsilon})
be a solution to

1.1

On the fast timescale =

3
Slow interface motion along the boundary

In this section, we show that the interface, which intersects the boundary orthogonally and evolves according to
eq.

2.42
,
moves toward a local maximum of the boundary curvature.
Our analysis employs a perturbation method for an interface enclosing a small area, following the approach of Alikakos et al.
[
3
]

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a bounded domain with smooth boundary
\partial\Omega
of class
C^{4}
.
We consider a family of smooth simple curves
\{\Gamma(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
, which evolve according to the interface equation
eq.

2.42

Each curve
\Gamma(t)
intersects the boundary
\partial\Omega
orthogonally at its endpoints, and separates the domain into
\Omega_{+}(t)
and
\Omega_{-}(t)
.
We assume that the evolution of the interface is in a quasi-static regime. More precisely, we consider the case where the interface evolves slowly with a speed of
O(\varepsilon)
, or equivalently, the magnitude of the leading-order velocity
c(v_{0})
is
O(\varepsilon)
.
In such a slow regime, the interface tends to remain close to a stable equilibrium state.
Indeed, numerical simulations (Fig.
2
) suggest that the interface evolves while maintaining a shape sufficiently close to a circular arc.
Based on this observation, we assume that
\Gamma(t)
is close to a circular arc.
To localize our analysis, we also assume that the size of the nearly circular interface is sufficiently small; specifically, we consider the case where the interface length
L
is of order
\varepsilon
.
Under these assumptions, the free boundary problem
eq.

1.2
reduces to the following simplified form, independent of
v_{1}
.

\displaystyle V_{n}=c(v_{0})-\varepsilon(\kappa-\langle\kappa\rangle)+O(\varepsilon^{3})

\displaystyle\text{on }\Gamma(t),\ t>0,

(3.1a)

\displaystyle u_{+}(v_{0})|\Omega_{+}(t)|+u_{-}(v_{0})|\Omega_{-}(t)|+v_{0}|\Omega|=M

\displaystyle t\geq 0.

(3.1b)

Here,
v_{1}
is assumed to be small and is absorbed into the
O(\varepsilon^{3})
term.
While a rigorous derivation of the
L^{\infty}
bounds for
v_{1}
requires a detailed analysis of the transmission problem with jump condition
eq.

2.39
, the scaling behavior can be understood as follows. Recall that
v_{1}
satisfies the Poisson equation
eq.

2.29
, where the source term is proportional to
c(v_{0})L
. Given the assumptions that
c(v_{0})
and
L
are of order
\varepsilon
, the product
c(v_{0})L
scales as
O(\varepsilon^{2})
. Thus, standard elliptic regularity estimates suggest that the magnitude of
v_{1}
is controlled by this source term, justifying the ansatz

.
For simplicity, we rename
T
back to
t
and hereafter identify
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with
\mathbb{C}
.

In what follows, we introduce a conformal mapping that flattens the curved boundary, thereby transferring our analysis to the complex upper half-plane. In this setting, we treat the interface as a perturbation of a semi-circle and compute the relevant geometric quantities, such as the normal velocity and mean curvature. Finally, applying these to the reduced free boundary problem, we demonstrate that the interface moves along the boundary driven by the gradient of the boundary curvature, and we establish the stability of the near-circular configuration.

3.1
Conformal mapping

Analyzing interface motion near a general curved boundary is analytically challenging due to the complexity of the Neumann boundary condition. To overcome this, we employ a conformal mapping technique. This allows us to “flatten” the boundary geometry, transforming the complex domain interactions into a tractable perturbation problem on the upper half-plane.

Let
X^{0}(s),\,s\in\mathbb{R}/L\mathbb{Z}
, be an arc-length parametrization of
\partial\Omega
, where
L
is the length of
\partial\Omega
.
Since the boundary
\partial\Omega
is of class
C^{4}
,
X^{0}
is also in
C^{4}(\mathbb{R}/L\mathbb{Z})
.
Let
z_{0}\in\Omega
be an arbitrary point. We denote the upper half-plane of
\mathbb{C}
as
\mathbb{H}
.
Then, there exists a unique conformal diffeomorphism
\Psi\colon\overline{\mathbb{H}}\times\mathbb{R}/L\mathbb{Z}\to\overline{\Omega}

satisfying the following properties for every
s\in\mathbb{R}/L\mathbb{Z}
:

•

\Psi(\cdot,s)
maps the real line onto
\partial\Omega
and a point on the positive imaginary axis to the point
z_{0}
.

•

\Psi(0,s)=X^{0}(s)
,
\Psi_{\zeta}(0,s)=\tau(s):=X_{s}^{0}(s)
.

This conformal mapping is also of class
C^{4}
, and its existence and uniqueness are guaranteed by the Riemann mapping theorem.
The overall approach for this mapping, including the derivation of its properties, is detailed in Alikakos et al.
[
3
]
.

For any fixed
s_{0}\in\mathbb{R}/L\mathbb{Z}
, let
g(s,s_{0})
be the real-valued smooth function satisfying

X^{0}(s+s_{0})=\Psi(g(s,s_{0}),s_{0}),\qquad s\in(-L/4,L/4).

(3.2)

From the definition of
\Psi
, we have
g(0,s_{0})=0
. The Taylor expansion of
\Psi(g(s,s_{0}),s_{0})
around
s=0
yields

\displaystyle\Psi(g(s,s_{0}),s_{0})=

\displaystyle X^{0}(s_{0})+\Psi_{\zeta}g_{s}s+\frac{1}{2}\left(\Psi_{\zeta\zeta}g_{s}^{2}+\Psi_{\zeta}g_{ss}\right)s^{2}

(3.3)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{6}\left(\Psi_{\zeta\zeta\zeta}g_{s}^{3}+3\Psi_{\zeta\zeta}g_{ss}g_{s}+\Psi_{\zeta}g_{sss}\right)s^{3}+O(s^{4}),

where all the derivatives are evaluated at
\zeta=0
and at
s=0
.
The Taylor expansion of
X^{0}(s+s_{0})
around
s=0
also yields

X^{0}(s+s_{0})=X^{0}(s_{0})+\tau s+\frac{1}{2}iK\tau s^{2}+\frac{1}{6}\left(iK_{s}-K^{2}\right)\tau s^{3}+O(s^{4}),

(3.4)

where we have used the relations
X_{s}^{0}=\tau
,
X_{ss}^{0}=\tau_{s}=-K\nu=iK\tau
, and
X_{sss}^{0}=i(K\tau)_{s}=i(K_{s}\tau+iK^{2}\tau)
.
Here,
K
is the curvature and
\nu
is the unit outward normal vector of the boundary
\partial\Omega
.
i
is the imaginary unit.
All terms on the right-hand side of
eq.

3.4
are evaluated at
s=0
.

Comparing the coefficients of the expansions

3.3
and
eq.

3.4
according to
eq.

3.2
, we deduce that

g_{s}(0,s_{0})=1
, and

\displaystyle\Psi_{\zeta\zeta}(0,s_{0})

\displaystyle=\left(iK(s_{0})-g_{ss}(0,s_{0})\right)\tau(s_{0})=:a(s_{0})\tau(s_{0}),

\displaystyle\Psi_{\zeta\zeta\zeta}(0,s_{0})

\displaystyle=\left(i(K_{s}(s_{0})\!-\!3K(s_{0})g_{ss}(0,s_{0}))+3g_{ss}^{2}(0,s_{0})\!-\!g_{sss}(0,s_{0})\!-\!K^{2}(s_{0})\right)\tau(s_{0})

\displaystyle=:b(s_{0})\tau(s_{0}).

Since
\Psi
is of class
C^{4}
up to the real axis, for sufficiently small

, we conclude that

\Psi(\zeta,s)=X^{0}(s)+\tau(s)\left(\zeta+\frac{1}{2}a(s)\zeta^{2}+\frac{1}{6}b(s)\zeta^{3}+O(\zeta^{4})\right).

Writing
a(s)=a_{1}(s)+ia_{2}(s)
and
b(s)=b_{1}(s)+ib_{2}(s)
, where
a_{k}
and
b_{k}
are real-valued function for

we see that

\displaystyle a_{1}(s)

\displaystyle=-g_{ss}(0,s),

\displaystyle a_{2}(s)

\displaystyle=K(s),

\displaystyle b_{1}(s)

\displaystyle=3g_{ss}^{2}(0,s)-g_{sss}(0,s)-K^{2}(s),

\displaystyle b_{2}(s)

\displaystyle=K_{s}(s)-3K(s)g_{ss}(0,s).

3.2

As mentioned at the beginning of this section, we consider the situation in which the interface
\Gamma(t)
is sufficiently close to a circular arc with a perimeter of order

\partial\Omega

Specifically, we parametrize the interface

where the unknown

and the unknown

is smooth and describes the center of the semicircle.
This parametrization effectively reduces the dynamics of

Since the interface touches the boundary orthogonally, we expand the perturbation

The

.
In addition, we have

\displaystyle F_{R_{0}}

\displaystyle=u_{+}(v_{0})\partial_{R_{0}}|\Omega_{+}(t)|+u_{-}(v_{0})\partial_{R_{0}}(|\Omega|-|\Omega_{+}(t)|)

\displaystyle=(u_{+}(v_{0})-u_{-}(v_{0}))(\pi\varepsilon^{4}+O(\varepsilon^{5}))>0,

leading to

v_{0}^{\prime}(R_{0})=-\frac{F_{R_{0}}}{F_{v_{0}}}<0.

(3.7)

Here, the prime (
′
) also denotes the derivative with respect to
R_{0}
.

To be consistent with the assumption that
c(v_{0})
is of order
\varepsilon
, we introduce a function
w_{\varepsilon}(R_{0})
of order
\varepsilon^{0}
such that

v_{0}(R_{0})=v_{c}+\varepsilon w_{\varepsilon}(R_{0}),

(3.8)

where
v_{c}
is a constant satisfying
c(v_{c})=0
and
c^{\prime}(v_{c})>0
(see the velocity sign condition).
Accordingly, We expand
c(v_{0})
into a Taylor series to obtain:

c(v_{0})=\varepsilon c^{\prime}(v_{c})w_{\varepsilon}(R_{0})+O(\varepsilon^{2}w_{\varepsilon}^{2}).

(3.9)

It is clear that
w_{\varepsilon}(R_{0})
also satisfies
w_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(R_{0})<0
.

3.4
Geometric quantities

We now calculate the normal velocity
V_{n}
and curvature
\kappa
of the interface
\Gamma(t)
, which is parametrized by
eq.

3.5
.
We denote a point on the interface by
x(\theta,t)+iy(\theta,t)=z(\theta,t)=\Psi(\zeta,s(t))
, where
\zeta=\varepsilon e^{\varepsilon^{2}R(\theta,t)+i\theta}
.
The unit normal vector is taken to point from the droplet region
\Omega_{+}(t)
to
\Omega_{-}(t)
.

First, we calculate the normal velocity
V_{n}
. The normal velocity is given by

\displaystyle V_{n}(\theta,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{x_{t}y_{\theta}-x_{\theta}y_{t}}{|z_{\theta}|}=-\frac{\mathrm{Im}(z_{t}\bar{z}_{\theta})}{|z_{\theta}|}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mathrm{Im}((\Psi_{\zeta}\zeta_{t}+\Psi_{s}\dot{s})\bar{\Psi}_{\zeta}\bar{\zeta}_{\theta})}{|\Psi_{\zeta}\zeta_{\theta}|}=-\frac{\mathrm{Im}((|\Psi_{\zeta}|^{2}\zeta_{t}+\Psi_{s}\bar{\Psi}_{\zeta}\dot{s})\bar{\zeta}_{\theta})}{|\Psi_{\zeta}\zeta_{\theta}|}.

Here, the dot (
\dot{}
) denotes the derivative with respect to time
t
.
We see that

\displaystyle\Psi_{\zeta}

\displaystyle=\tau+O(\zeta),

\displaystyle\Psi_{s}

\displaystyle=\tau+O(\zeta),

\displaystyle\zeta_{\theta}

\displaystyle=(\varepsilon^{2}R_{\theta}+i)\zeta,

\displaystyle\dot{\zeta}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{2}\dot{R}\zeta.

Substituting these into the formula for
V_{n}
yields

V_{n}(\theta,t)=\dot{s}\left(\cos\theta+\varepsilon B_{1}^{\varepsilon}\right)+\varepsilon^{3}\dot{R}\left(1+\varepsilon B_{2}^{\varepsilon}\right)

(3.10)

where
B_{i}^{\varepsilon}
is a function of order
\varepsilon^{0}
for
i=1,2
.

Next, we calculate the curvature
\kappa
of
\Gamma(t)
. The curvature is given by

\kappa(\theta,t)=\frac{y_{\theta\theta}x_{\theta}-x_{\theta\theta}y_{\theta}}{|z_{\theta}|^{3}}=\frac{\mathrm{Im}(z_{\theta\theta}\bar{z}_{\theta})}{|z_{\theta}|^{3}}=\frac{\mathrm{Im}(\zeta_{\theta\theta}\bar{\zeta}_{\theta})}{|\Psi_{\zeta}||\zeta_{\theta}|^{3}}+\frac{\mathrm{Im}(\Psi_{\zeta\zeta}\bar{\Psi}_{\zeta}\zeta_{\theta})}{|\Psi_{\zeta}|^{3}|\zeta_{\theta}|}.

Taking
\zeta=O(\varepsilon)
into account, each component can be calculated as follows:

\displaystyle|\Psi_{\zeta}|^{2}

\displaystyle=|1+a\zeta+b\zeta^{2}/2+O(\zeta^{3})|^{2}

\displaystyle=1+2\mathrm{Re}(a\zeta+b\zeta^{2}/2)+|a\zeta|^{2}+O(\varepsilon^{3}),

\displaystyle|\zeta_{\theta}|

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1+\varepsilon^{4}R_{\theta}^{2}}|\zeta|,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Im}(\zeta_{\theta\theta}\bar{\zeta}_{\theta})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Im}\left((\varepsilon^{4}R_{\theta}^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}R_{\theta\theta}-1+2i\varepsilon^{2}R_{\theta})(\varepsilon^{2}R_{\theta}-i)\right)|\zeta|^{2}

\displaystyle=(1-\varepsilon^{2}R_{\theta\theta}+O(\varepsilon^{4}))|\zeta|^{2},

\displaystyle\mathrm{Im}(\Psi_{\zeta\zeta}\bar{\Psi}_{\zeta}\zeta_{\theta})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Im}\left((a+b\zeta+O(\zeta^{2}))\overline{(1+a\zeta+b\zeta^{2}/2+O(\zeta^{3}))}(\varepsilon^{2}R_{\theta}+i)\zeta\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Re}(a\zeta)+|a\zeta|^{2}+\mathrm{Re}(b\zeta^{2})+O(\varepsilon^{3}).

We then obtain that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{Im}(\zeta_{\theta\theta}\bar{\zeta}_{\theta})}{|\zeta_{\theta}|^{3}|\Psi_{\zeta}|}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left[1\!-\!\mathrm{Re}(a\zeta)\!-\!\varepsilon^{2}R_{\theta\theta}\!-\!\frac{1}{2}(\mathrm{Re}(b\zeta^{2})\!+\!|a\zeta|^{2}\!-\!3\mathrm{Re}(a\zeta)^{2})\!-\!\varepsilon^{2}R\!+\!O(\varepsilon^{3})\right],

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{Im}(\Psi_{\zeta\zeta}\bar{\Psi}_{\zeta}\zeta_{\theta})}{|\zeta_{\theta}||\Psi_{\zeta}|^{3}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\big[\mathrm{Re}(a\zeta)+|a\zeta|^{2}+\mathrm{Re}(b\zeta^{2})-3\mathrm{Re}(a\zeta)^{2}+O(\varepsilon^{3})\big].

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\kappa

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\bigg[1-\varepsilon^{2}(R_{\theta\theta}+R)+\frac{1}{2}\big(|a\zeta|^{2}+\mathrm{Re}(b\zeta^{2})-3\mathrm{Re}(a\zeta)^{2}\big)+O(\varepsilon^{3})\bigg]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\bigg[1-\varepsilon^{2}(R_{\theta\theta}+R)+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\big(|a|^{2}+\mathrm{Re}(be^{2i\theta})-3\mathrm{Re}(ae^{i\theta})^{2}\big)+O(\varepsilon^{3})\bigg].

Now we use the facts

\sin(2\theta)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{8}{\pi(3-4k-4k^{2})}\cos((2k+1)\theta),\qquad b_{2}-3a_{1}a_{2}=K_{s},

then we obtain that

\kappa=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}-\varepsilon\left(R_{0}+\sum_{n\geq 2}(1-n^{2})R_{n}\cos(n\theta)\right)-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\sum_{n\geq 0}e_{n}(s)\cos(n\theta)+O(\varepsilon^{2}),

(3.11)

where

e_{n}(s)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{2}(a_{1}^{2}(s)+a_{2}^{2}(s)),&n=0,\\[10.00002pt]
-b_{1}(s)+\dfrac{3}{2}(a_{1}^{2}(s)-a_{2}^{2}(s)),&n=2,\\[10.00002pt]
\dfrac{8K_{s}(s)}{\pi(3-4k-4k^{2})},&n=2k+1,k\geq 0,\\[10.00002pt]
0&n=2k,k\geq 2.\end{cases}

This expression implies that

\langle\kappa\rangle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}-\varepsilon R_{0}-\frac{\varepsilon}{4}(a_{1}^{2}+a_{2}^{2})+O(\varepsilon^{2}),

(3.12)

that is,
\langle\kappa\rangle
extracts
0
-mode from
\kappa
.

3.5
Motion along the boundary

Substituting
eqs.

3.9
,
3.10
,
3.11
and
3.12
into
eq.

3.1a
, we obtain

\displaystyle\dot{s}(\cos\theta+\varepsilon B_{1}^{\varepsilon})+\varepsilon^{3}\dot{R}(1+\varepsilon B_{2}^{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varepsilon c^{\prime}(v_{c})w_{\varepsilon}(R_{0})+\varepsilon^{2}C_{1}^{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon^{2}\sum_{n\geq 2}(1-n^{2})R_{n}\cos(n\theta)+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\sum_{n\geq 1}e_{n}\cos(n\theta)+\varepsilon^{3}B_{3}^{\varepsilon},

where
C_{1}^{\varepsilon}
represents the
0
-mode terms of order
w_{\varepsilon}^{2}
, and
B_{3}^{\varepsilon}
collects the terms without the
0
-mode.
Dividing both sides by
1+\varepsilon B_{2}^{\varepsilon}
yields

\displaystyle\varepsilon\left(\varepsilon^{2}\dot{R}_{0}-c^{\prime}(v_{c})w_{\varepsilon}(R_{0})\right)+\left(\dot{s}-\frac{4\varepsilon^{2}}{3\pi}K_{s}\right)\cos\theta+\varepsilon^{2}\sum_{n\geq 2}E_{n}(s,t)\cos(n\theta)

(3.13)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{2}C_{2}^{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon B_{5}^{\varepsilon}\dot{s}+\varepsilon^{3}B_{6}^{\varepsilon},

where
C_{2}^{\varepsilon}
denotes the
0
-mode terms of order
w_{\varepsilon}^{2}
,
B_{5}^{\varepsilon}
and
B_{6}^{\varepsilon}
are composed of terms without a
0
-mode,
and
E_{n}(s,t)
is defined by

\displaystyle E_{n}(s,t)=\varepsilon\dot{R}_{n}(t)+(n^{2}-1)R_{n}(t)-\frac{e_{n}(s)}{2}.

Let
B_{i,n}^{\varepsilon}
denote the
n
-th Fourier coefficient of
B_{i}^{\varepsilon}
for
i=5,6
.
By projecting the equation onto each Fourier mode, we conclude that

\begin{cases}\dot{s}=\dfrac{4\varepsilon^{2}}{3\pi}K_{s}+\varepsilon B_{5,1}^{\varepsilon}\dot{s}+\varepsilon^{3}B_{6,1}^{\varepsilon},\\[8.50006pt]
\varepsilon^{2}\dot{R}_{0}=c^{\prime}(v_{c})w_{\varepsilon}(R_{0})+\varepsilon C_{2}^{\varepsilon},\\[8.50006pt]
\varepsilon\dot{R}_{n}=-(n^{2}-1)R_{n}+\dfrac{e_{n}}{2}+\varepsilon^{-1}B_{5,n}^{\varepsilon}\dot{s}+\varepsilon B_{6,n}^{\varepsilon},\quad n\geq 2.\end{cases}

(3.14)

For the first equation, we solve for
\dot{s}
by collecting the terms and dividing by
1-\varepsilon B_{5,1}^{\varepsilon}
. This reveals that
\dot{s}=O(\varepsilon^{2})
. Furthermore, assuming that
w_{\varepsilon}
is of order
\varepsilon^{l}
with
l\geq 0
, and setting
w(R_{0})=\varepsilon^{-l}w_{\varepsilon}(R_{0})
, we arrive at the following system:

\begin{cases}\dot{s}=\dfrac{4\varepsilon^{2}}{3\pi}K_{s}+\varepsilon^{3}\tilde{B}_{6,1}^{\varepsilon},\\[8.50006pt]
\varepsilon^{2-l}\dot{R}_{0}=c^{\prime}(v_{c})w(R_{0})+\varepsilon^{1-l}C_{2}^{\varepsilon},\\[8.50006pt]
\varepsilon\dot{R}_{n}=-(n^{2}-1)R_{n}+\dfrac{e_{n}}{2}+\varepsilon\tilde{B}_{6,n}^{\varepsilon},\quad n\geq 2,\end{cases}

(3.15)

where
\tilde{B}_{6,n}^{\varepsilon}
represents the remainder terms. Specifically, for
n=1
,
\tilde{B}_{6,1}^{\varepsilon}
accounts for the higher-order terms generated by the division by
1-\varepsilon B_{5,1}^{\varepsilon}
. For
n\geq 2
,
\tilde{B}_{6,n}^{\varepsilon}
incorporates the corrections resulting from the substitution of
\dot{s}
, which is of order
\varepsilon^{2}
.
The first equation of the system
eq.

3.15
indicates that the leading-order dynamics of the interface position
s(t)
are governed by

\dot{s}=\frac{4\varepsilon^{2}}{3\pi}K_{s}.

This relationship provides a rigorous mathematical basis for the “shape-sensing”. It implies that the interface is not passive but actively seeks out geometric features; specifically, the polarized domain is attracted to protrusions (local curvature maxima) and repelled from indentations. This mechanism allows the cell to orient itself purely based on the geometry of its environment, without external chemical gradients.

Finally, we investigate the stability of the equilibria for the problem
eq.

3.15
.
Letting
R=(R_{0},R_{2},R_{3},\dots)
by abuse of notation, we establish the following theorem concerning the existence and stability of the stationary solutions to
eq.

3.15
.

{theorem*}

Let
s^{0}
be a non-degenerate critical point of the boundary curvature
K(s)
, i.e.,
K_{s}(s^{0})=0
and
K_{ss}(s^{0})\neq 0
.
Then, for sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a unique equilibrium solution
(s^{\varepsilon},R^{\varepsilon})
to the system
eq.

3.15
in a neighborhood of the unperturbed equilibrium
(s^{0},R^{0})
.
Moreover, the linear stability of
(s^{\varepsilon},R^{\varepsilon})
is determined by the local geometry of the boundary at
s^{0}
:

(1)

If
s^{0}
is a local maximum of
K(s)
(i.e.,
K_{ss}(s^{0})<0
), the equilibrium is asymptotically stable.

(2)

If
s^{0}
is a local minimum of
K(s)
(i.e.,
K_{ss}(s^{0})>0
), the equilibrium is unstable.

Proof.

Let
P(s,R,\varepsilon)
denote the vector field given by the right-hand side of the system
eq.

3.15
, rescaled such that the leading-order terms are of
O(1)
.
Namely,
P(s,R,\varepsilon)=(P_{0},P_{1},P_{2},\dots)
where

\displaystyle P_{0}(s,R,\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{3\pi}K_{s}(s)+\varepsilon\tilde{B}_{6,1}^{\varepsilon},

\displaystyle P_{1}(s,R,\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=c^{\prime}(v_{c})w(R_{0})+\varepsilon^{1-l}C_{2}^{\varepsilon},

\displaystyle P_{2}(s,R,\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=-(2^{2}-1)R_{2}+\frac{e_{2}}{2}+\varepsilon\tilde{B}_{6,2}^{\varepsilon},

\displaystyle P_{n}(s,R,\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=-(n^{2}-1)R_{n}+\frac{e_{n}}{2}+\varepsilon\tilde{B}_{6,n}^{\varepsilon},

An equilibrium
(s^{0},R^{0})
of the unperturbed system (
\varepsilon=0
) is a solution to
P(s,R,0)=0
,
which must satisfy

\displaystyle K_{s}(s^{0})=0,

\displaystyle w(R_{0}^{0})=0,\text{ i.e., }v_{0}(R_{0}^{0})=v_{c},

\displaystyle R_{n}^{0}=\frac{e_{n}(s^{0})}{2(n^{2}-1)}=0\text{ for }n\geq 2.

We now examine the unique existence and stability of an equilibrium for the perturbed system (
\varepsilon>0
) near
(s^{0},R^{0})
.
The Jacobian matrix of
P
with respect to
(s,R)
evaluated at
(s^{0},R^{0},0)
forms a lower triangular matrix:

\begin{pmatrix}\dfrac{4}{3\pi}K_{ss}(s^{0})&0&0&\cdots&0&\cdots\\[10.00002pt]
0&c^{\prime}(v_{c})w^{\prime}(R_{0}^{0})&0&\cdots&0&\cdots\\[10.00002pt]
\dfrac{e_{2}^{\prime}(s^{0})}{2}&0&-3&\cdots&0&\cdots\\[10.00002pt]
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\ddots\\[10.00002pt]
\dfrac{e_{n}^{\prime}(s^{0})}{2}&0&0&\cdots&-(n^{2}-1)&\cdots\\[10.00002pt]
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\ddots\\[10.00002pt]
\end{pmatrix}.

The eigenvalues of the above matrix are given by the diagonal entries:
\frac{4}{3\pi}K_{ss}(s^{0})
,
c^{\prime}(v_{c})w^{\prime}(R_{0}^{0})
, and
-(n^{2}-1)
for
n\geq 2
.
Since we have
c^{\prime}(v_{c})w^{\prime}(R_{0}^{0})<0
(see the defenition of
w
and Subsection
3.3
), the Implicit Function Theorem guarantees
that if
K_{ss}(s^{0})\neq 0
, a unique solution to
P(s,R,\varepsilon)=0
exists in a neighborhood of
(s,R)=(s^{0},R_{0}^{0})
for sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
.
Furthermore, the stability of this solution for
\varepsilon>0
is the same as that of
(s^{0},R^{0})
and is therefore determined by the
sign of
K_{ss}(s^{0})
.
Namely, the equilibrium for
\varepsilon>0
is stable if
K_{ss}(s^{0})<0
, and is unstable if
K_{ss}(s^{0})>0
.

4
Concluding Remarks

In this paper, we have derived a free boundary problem approximating the front behavior of the wave-pinning model valid up to the
O(\varepsilon^{-1})
timescale via matched asymptotics.
Our results analytically corroborate the emergence of area-preserving mean curvature flow on the
O(\varepsilon^{-1})
timescale, a behavior that has been suggested in previous studies.
Furthermore, we demonstrated that the interface undergoes a slow drift along the domain boundary on the
O(\varepsilon^{-2})
timescale, driven by the interaction between curvature flow and boundary geometry.

While these findings provide theoretical insights into the long-time behavior of the wave-pinning model, several mathematical challenges remain.
A rigorous convergence analysis of the original system to the derived interface equations as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
is required.
For such rigorous treatments of reaction-diffusion systems and their interfaces, see
[
18
]
and references therein.
In Section 3, we utilized a scaling argument to justify the assumption that the first-order correction
v_{1}
is of order
\varepsilon^{2}
; however, verifying this assumption requires examining the properties of the transmission problem for
v_{1}
, which necessitates a more delicate argument.
Verifying this assumption would likely require establishing
L^{\infty}
estimates for the transmission problem on the perturbed domain, which involves subtle regularity issues at the boundary contact points. While we leave this rigorous proof for future work, the consistency of our asymptotic results with numerical simulations strongly supports the validity of this scaling ansatz.
In addition to these analytical challenges, the numerical treatment of the
v_{1}
term presents significant difficulties.

These complexities associated with
v_{1}
lead us to consider a simplified model in which the
v_{1}
term is neglected.
This simplified model retains a sufficient mechanism for wave-pinning.
Consequently, this simplified model serves not only as a valid approximation of the full wave-pinning model but also as a minimal framework that distills its essence, providing a tractable mathematical description suitable for investigating long-time behaviors.
Detailed analysis of this simplified model also remains an interesting subject for future research.

We discuss the implications of our results for understanding cell motility models, specifically the model proposed by Camley et al.
[
9
]
.
Their model, which couples the wave-pinning reaction-diffusion system with phase-field mechanics, exhibits a transition between straight and rotating motion depending on parameter values.
Camley et al. hypothesized that “shape sensing” inherent to the wave-pinning kinetics plays a crucial role in the mechanism of rotation.
Our derivation of the drift effect toward high-curvature regions provides analytical validation for this hypothesis.
In the context of rotating motion, the mechanism can be interpreted as a mismatch between mechanical and chemical stable states.
While mechanical forces driven by protrusion tend to deform the cell into a bilaterally symmetric shape (typically symmetric about the short axis), the internal reaction-diffusion dynamics drives the Rho GTPase interface toward regions of higher curvature (i.e., the tips of the long axis), as shown in our
O(\varepsilon^{-2})
analysis.
This continuous breaking of symmetry likely induces the rotational behavior observed in numerical simulations.
Conversely, the existence of straight motion presents a mathematically intriguing phenomenon.
In a migrating cell, the polarity is typically localized at the leading edge, which corresponds to a region of relatively low curvature compared to the sides.
According to our analysis, such a configuration should be unstable, as the interface naturally drifts toward higher curvature.
The persistence of straight motion, therefore, suggests that the kinetics of the moving boundary (protrusion forces) exert a stabilizing effect that counteracts the geometric drift.
In other words, the coupling with boundary mechanics stabilizes a configuration that is inherently unstable within the pure wave-pinning framework.
Unraveling the mathematical structure of this competition—specifically, proving the existence and bifurcation of straight versus rotating solutions in the coupled system—remains a challenging and significant subject for future research.

Ultimately, our analysis suggests that cell polarity is a robust phenomenon where geometry acts as a governing cue. By reducing the complex reaction-diffusion system to a set of geometric evolution laws, we provide a theoretical tool that can be used to predict cell behavior in engineered micro-environments, bridging the gap between mathematical theory and experimental cell biology.
