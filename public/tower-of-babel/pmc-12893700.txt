Assessing survival and learning curves for EVD procedures in hemorrhagic stroke patients in a New York City hospital
Authors: US Govindarajulu, D Rivera, C Brown, E Reynolds, J Zhang, D Cohen, A Schupper
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Types of hemorrhagic stroke and other forms of bleeding within the cranial vault, account for a significant portion of neurological emergencies. External ventricular drain (EVD) placement is a common, life-saving intervention for patients presenting with acute hydrocephalus following intracranial hemorrhage. EVDs reduce intracranial pressure (ICP) by draining cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), minimizing the risk of secondary brain injury. Additionally, EVDs allow for continuous ICP monitoring, providing critical data to guide decisions. However, post-procedural complications may occur following EVD placement. While previous studies have examined EVD outcomes using traditional statistical methods, the impact of provider heterogeneity and resident learning curves on these outcomes remains poorly understood. Understanding the impact of these complications is crucial for improving surgical quality and outcomes.Methods:To address these critical gaps and advance our understanding of EVD outcomes, we reviewed medical records of patients with subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH), intracranial hemorrhage (ICH), or interventricular hemorrhage (IVH) who received EVDs for ICP at the Mount Sinai Health System in New York City from 2019 to 2022. We evaluated surgical complications and patient outcomes following EVD placement by neurosurgeons at different stages of their training. Random effect survival models were implemented to account for heterogeneity between providers. This novel methodological approach integrated provider heterogeneity and learning curves into EVD outcome analysis, extending beyond traditional statistical methods used in previous studies.Conclusions:We found that in time-to-death Cox models, age was a significant predictor while for time-to-complication, device type and pre-operative modified Rankin score were significant predictors. A Cox frailty model did not show significant predictors, but our unique evaluation of restricted mean survival time with frailty showed that smoking was a significant predictor of survival. Furthermore, we also modeled learning curves amongst neurosurgeons performing EVDs to improve surgical proficiency. We found that employing the frailty modeling improved the learning curve fit.Keywords: EVD procedure, Neurosurgery, Survival analysis, Frailty model, Random survival forest, Learning curves

Introduction

External ventricular drainage (EVD) has been one of the most commonly performed neurosurgical procedures today. Originally described in 1744 by French surgeon Claude Nicolas Le Cat [1], EVD has evolved into a life-saving treatment for patients presenting with acute hydrocephalus, particularly in stroke cases including subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH), intracranial hemorrhage (ICH), and intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH). The fundamental therapeutic mechanism of EVD involves draining cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), which alleviates pressure on surrounding brain tissue, thereby reducing the risk of secondary brain injury and improving clinical outcomes. Additionally, EVD allows for intermittent monitoring of intracranial pressure (ICP) when the system is closed to drainage. Although not a suitable option for permanent or long-term drainage, EVDs still offer valuable insights into ICP trends, providing critical data to guide therapeutic decisions and clinical interventions [2].

While numerous studies have examined EVD outcomes, most have relied on conventional statistical approaches that do not account for provider heterogeneity or learning effects. Furthermore, comparative effectiveness data between traditional and newer EVD catheter types remains limited. While technically straightforward and often performed at the bedside, EVD placement carries notable risks such as infections (e. g., ventriculitis), tract hemorrhage, catheter malposition and catheter obstruction requiring readjustment or replacement [3,4]. Despite these risks, EVD has remained a crucial tool for neurosurgeons and neurocritical care physicians in managing patients with elevated ICP, especially in stroke cases involving ICH, SAH, and/or IVH. In ICH cases, EVD has been valuable for managing elevated ICP caused by hematoma mass effect, helping to decompress the brain and minimize the risk of herniation while promoting better cerebral perfusion [4]. For SAH patients, EVD placement addressed acute hydrocephalus resulting from blood accumulation in the subarachnoid space, which can lead to rapidly elevated ICP, brain herniation, and severe neurological deficits. By inserting an EVD, this pressure can be quickly relieved, potentially preventing further damage and allowing for better neurological recovery [3]. Since the focus of this study was on EVD- related complications and modeling, the ICP values for this patients was not reported nor was a separate analysis on intercranial hypertension conducted.

Intraventricular hemorrhage, often secondary to ICH or SAH, has presented unique challenges due to blood presence within the ventricular system. EVD has been an invaluable tool in preventing and treating hydrocephalus caused by blood obstructing the ventricular system. Draining blood-tinged CSF reduces ICP and may decrease the risk of additional neurological damage. Moreover, EVD can facilitate the administration of intraventricular medications aimed at dissolving clots, such as alteplase, potentially enhancing patient recovery [5]. The efficacy of this approach has been investigated in clinical trials such as MISTIE (Minimally Invasive Surgery Plus rt-PA for ICH Evacuation), which explored the use of minimally invasive surgery with thrombolysis for ICH [6]. Additionally, recent studies like the IRRAflow trials have investigated the use of self-irrigating EVD systems for IVH, potentially offering improved clot clearance and reduced catheter occlusion rates [5]. Despite the extensive literature on EVD outcomes, several critical knowledge gaps persist. First, the impact of provider experience and learning curves on EVD outcomes has not been rigorously analyzed using advanced statistical methods. Second, the role of provider heterogeneity in EVD outcomes remains poorly understood. Third, there is limited evidence comparing outcomes between different EVD catheter types in real-world clinical settings.

In summary, EVD use in stroke patients, particularly those with SAH, ICH, and IVH, has been a critical component of neurocritical care. It has provided a means to manage life-threatening increases in ICP, which allows for continuous monitoring and treatment adjustments, and offers therapeutic options that directly address complications associated with these severe types of stroke. This study uniquely investigated time-to-event outcomes through both standard survival techniques and novel approaches accounting for provider heterogeneity. Furthermore, we pioneered the use of frailty modeling in EVD outcome analysis and employed sophisticated learning curve analyses to better understand how case volume affects procedural success, addressing critical gaps in current EVD literature.

Methods

Our study included a total of 160 eligible patients who underwent external ventricular drain (EVD) placement for SAH, ICH, or IVH between 2019–2022 and were greater than 18 years old. We first conducted summary statistics of the variables collected such as the number of operators/physicians, number of cases/operator, prior history of stroke and cardiovascular disease (CVD), patient gender, diabetes status, pre- and post-operative modified Rankin score (mRS), and complications. As of note, post mRS was not reported in this paper due to a lot missingness or unreliable reporting. We also assessed means/standard deviations, median, min and max values of patient age, survival time from procedure date to death or last follow-up, and survival time from time of procedure to time to complication.

For survival analysis, we first assessed time to event (as complication or death) analysis by a non-parametric Kaplan-Meier (KM) analysis by showing the KM plots of the survival curves. We also assessed complication and death rates and obtained proportions of these rates. We then used the standard Cox proportional hazard modeling with proportional hazards assumptions met to model time to event, either time to complication or time to death along with physician clustering, and adjusted by EVD type, age, gender, anticoagulant use, history of stroke, history of diabetes (yes or no), history of CVD, history of drug use, history of hypertension, history of smoking, and ICH or IVH or SAH diagnosis. We also performed a Cox frailty model adjusted by these same predictors for time to death with frailty on the physician operators.

In our new development, we were also interested in a frailty analysis to discern heterogeneity between providers and conducted a Cox frailty model analysis. We also used restricted mean survival time (RMST) [7] to estimate survival probabilities instead of a Cox model or machine learning method, adjusted by covariates, along with frailty in a new development, to handle unexplained heterogeneity.

We used our learning curve methods[8] to adjust the time to event by a specific form of a parametric shape (exponential, power series, logarithmic,and log-normal), and then ran the models with learning, by including procedure in the Cox model or other method. The survival times would be adjusted or updated for the physician learning curve and then new survival probabilities would be calculated and plotted against procedural volume. We also utilized random survival forests [9] (RSF) instead of a Cox model to estimate survival in order to model learning curves between survival probability and procedure volume.

Results: Regular time-to-event analyses

The majority of procedures were performed in the Mount Sinai Hospital system at the main location (56.9 %) and as well as another location (30.0 %). The patient population was diverse, with Black or African-American patients comprising 26.3 %, Hispanic patients 23.8 %, White patients 20.6 %, and Asian patients 10.6 % of the cohort. There was a near-equal distribution of male (50.6 %) and female (48.8 %) patients. The mean age of patients was 57.5 years (SD 14.5), and the average BMI was 29.2 (SD 12.2), indicating a generally overweight population.

Patient demographics and clinical characteristics.

Regarding comorbidities and risk factors, 12.5 % of patients had diabetes, 11.3 % had a history of stroke, and 34.4 % had a history of hypertension. Notably, 27.5 % of patients were on anticoagulant therapy, and 48.8 % were smokers. Cardiovascular disease was present in 15.0 % of the cohort. The mean pre-operative mRS score was 2.93 (SD 1.67), suggesting moderate disability in many patients prior to the procedure.

The primary diagnoses leading to EVD placement were SAH in 52.5 % of cases, ICH in 46.9 %, and IVH in 40.6 %. Regarding the type of EVD device used, bactericidal catheters were employed in 64.4 % of cases, while Irraflow/Cerebroflo devices were used in 35.6 %. In terms of outcomes, the overall mortality rate was 25.6 %, with a mean time to death of 31.8 days (SD 30.4). Complications occurred in 16.9 % of cases, with a mean time to complication of 27.2 days (SD 29.9). To note, mean time of insertion of the EVD was not computed by the medical team since EVD insertion is a very emergent procedure.

Time to death

Stratified KM plots

In the Kaplan-Meier time from procedure to death unadjusted as well as adjusted plots (Fig. 1) we observed no statistically significant difference in survival between patients with and without anticoagulant use in both unadjusted (p = 0.68) and adjusted (p = 0.119) models. However, the adjusted model showed a trend towards improved survival in patients using anticoagulants, though this did not reach statistical significance. Regarding EVD device type, we found no significant difference in survival between Irraflow/Cerebroflo and Bactericidal devices in either unadjusted (p = 0.132) or adjusted (p = 0.622) analyses.

Cox proportional hazard modeling of time to death

In our Cox proportional hazards regression analysis for time from procedure to death (Table 2), we found that age remained a statistically significant risk factor in both univariate and multivariate models with physician clusters (adjusted HR 1.04, 95 % C.I.: 1.01–1.07, p = 0.015). However, several factors that were initially statistically significant in the univariate models became borderline or not statistically significant in the multivariable model. These included pre-operative mRS (adjusted HR 1.24, 95 % C.I.: 0.98–1.56, p = 0.074), history of hypertension (adjusted HR 0.93, 95 % C.I.: 0.37–2.39, p = 0.9), and history of smoking (adjusted HR 1.83, 95 % C.I.:0.84–4.02, p = 0.13). Notably, the type of EVD device (Irraflow/Cerebroflo vs. Bactericidal) did not significantly affect time to death in either univariate (HR 1.26, 95 % C.I.: 0.70–2.29, p = 0.4) or multivariate (HR 1.11, 95 % C.I.: 0.47–2.62, p = 0.8) analyses. Regarding different diagnoses, neither SAH (adjusted HR 2.25, 95 % C.I.: 0.59–8.53, p = 0.2), IVH (adjusted HR 1.10, 95 % C.I.: 0.51–2.35, p = 0.8), nor ICH (adjusted HR 2.39, 95 % C.I.: 0.68–8.43, p = 0.2) showed statistically significant associations with time to death in the multivariate model. Anticoagulant use, while not significant in the univariate model (HR 0.64, 95 % C.I.: 0.34–1.22, p = 0.2), showed a trend towards reduced risk of death in the multivariate model, although this did not reach statistical significance (adjusted HR 0.45, 95 % C.I.: 0.18–1.12, p = 0.087).

Time to complication

Stratified Kaplan-Meier plots

For time of procedure to time to complication (Fig. 2), the Kaplan-Meier plots revealed no significant difference in complication-free survival between patients with and without anticoagulant use in both unadjusted (p = 0.731) and adjusted (p = 0.807) analyses. However, we observed a significant difference in complication-free survival between EVD device types. The Bactericidal EVDs showed a higher rate of complication-free survival compared to Irraflow/Cerebroflo devices in both unadjusted (p = 0.0301) and adjusted (p = 0.0212) models. This finding suggests that the choice of EVD device may have a substantial impact on the likelihood of complications following the procedure.

Standard Cox proportional hazard modeling

In terms of time of procedure to time of complication (Table 3), we found that EVD type remained as a significant predictor of complication (p = 0.008) in either univariate or multivariate (p = 0.010) Cox regression. Interestingly, pre-operative mRS was initially significant in the univariate analyses (p = 0.010) but became less significant in the multivariate analyses (p = 0.060). Other risk factors like anti-coagulant use, smoking and history of stroke were not statistically significant in either univariate or multivariate analyses. These results underscore the complex interplay of factors affecting outcomes in EVD procedures and highlight the potential importance of device selection in minimizing complications.

Learning curve time-to-event analyses

Our analysis of learning curves in EVD placement revealed important insights into the role of physician/operator expertise with procedural success. The learning curve generated from the predicted probabilities from the standard Cox model with physician clustering (Fig. 3) suggested a complex pattern of skill development. Interestingly, we observed a consistent dip in performance around the 40-case mark across all modeling approaches, followed by an improvement. This pattern may indicate a critical point in the learning process where physicians encounter more challenging cases or begin to take on more complex patients, leading to a temporary decrease in success rates before further improvement occurs.

Learning curves derived from the standard Cox model with physician clustering, showing the probability of procedural success versus the number of cases performed. Curves are presented for four different mathematical shapes: exponential, power series, logarithmic, and log-normal.

The learning curve fit from the Cox frailty model (Fig. 4) demonstrated a more consistent upward trend in success probability as case volume increased. This finding has aligned with the expected learning curve in medical procedures, suggesting that overall, physicians’ proficiency in EVD placement improves with experience. This trend has underscored the importance of continued practice and exposure to maintain and enhance skills in this critical neurosurgical procedure.

Learning Curves – Frailty Cox Model. Learning curves obtained from the Frailty Cox model, illustrating the probability of procedural success as a function of the number of cases performed. Curves are shown for exponential, power series, logarithmic, and log-normal shapes.

The learning curves fit from the predictions from the RSF (Fig. 5) did not show a clear trend. However it provided the best fit to the observed data based on mean-squared error calculations (Table 4). This suggests that the relationship between experience and success in EVD placement may be more complex than a simple linear improvement, potentially influenced by factors such as case complexity, patient characteristics, or specific aspects of the procedure that vary from case to case.

The clustering of data points in the lower case order highlighted a potential challenge in achieving proficiency, as many physicians may not reach the higher case volumes where more consistent success is observed. This finding has implications for training programs and hospital policies, suggesting that concentrated experience in EVD placement during residency or fellowship may be beneficial for achieving optimal outcomes.

These learning curve analyses can provide valuable insights for structuring training programs, setting benchmarks for competency, and potentially informing decisions about when less experienced physicians should seek additional support or supervision for EVD procedures. Furthermore, understanding these patterns could help in developing targeted interventions to support physicians during the critical phases of their learning process, ultimately leading to improved patient outcomes.

Conclusion:

In this retrospective cohort study of hemorrhagic stroke patients who received EVD procedures at the Mount Sinai Health System from 2019 to 2022, we evaluated surgical complications, patient outcomes, and learning curves among neurosurgeons at different training stages. Our findings contribute to the growing body of literature on EVD placement and outcomes

We found that age was a significant risk factor for time to death in both unadjusted and adjusted Cox proportional hazard regression models. This aligns with previous studies, such as the work by Sorinala et al. (2019) [10], which identified advanced age as an independent predictor of poor outcomes in patients with intracerebral hemorrhage requiring EVD placement. However, contrary to some earlier findings [11], we did not find EVD device type or anticoagulant use to be significant predictors of mortality.

The pre-operative mRS was initially a significant predictor of survival time, becoming borderline significant after adjustment. This partially corroborates findings by Hwang et al. (2012) [12], who reported that preoperative functional status significantly influenced outcomes in EVD patients, although their study focused on hydrocephalus rather than hemorrhagic stroke.

Discussion

Regarding complications, we observed a statistically significant difference between EVD device types, with bactericidal catheters showing higher complication-free survival rates than Irraflow devices. This finding contributes to the ongoing debate about optimal EVD catheter selection, as highlighted in the systematic review by Jamjoom et al. (2017) [13], which found mixed evidence for the superiority of antibiotic-impregnated catheters.

Our analysis of learning curves among neurosurgeons performing EVD placements revealed that procedural success rates improved with case volume, consistent with findings from other neurosurgical procedures [14]. The incorporation of frailty models to account for provider heterogeneity improved our ability to model these learning curves, an approach that builds upon traditional learning curve analyses in medical education research [15]. This modeling then showed that smoking status instead of age was a significant risk factor and using the restricted mean survival time modeling improved this analysis as well with not having to rely on traditional Cox model assumptions.

The observed dip in performance around the 40-case mark, followed by improvement, suggests a more complex learning process than typically described in the literature. This pattern may reflect the point at which trainees begin to encounter more challenging cases, similar to the concept of “intermediate-level adverse events” described by Patel et al. (2020) [16] in their study of surgical training.

Our study’s mortality rate of 25.6 % is within the range reported in the literature for EVD placement in hemorrhagic stroke patients [17], though direct comparisons are challenging due to variations in patient populations and institutional practices. These findings contribute to our understanding of factors influencing EVD outcomes and the development of surgical expertise. They underscore the need for tailored training programs and careful patient selection, particularly for less experienced surgeons. Future research should focus on validating these findings in larger, multi-center cohorts and exploring interventions to optimize the learning process and improve patient outcomes.
