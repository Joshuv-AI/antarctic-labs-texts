MC-LBTO: secure and resilient state-aware multi-controller framework with adaptive load balancing for SD-IoT performance optimization
Authors: Ashraf Alyanbaawi, Ameer El-Sayed, Nihal Salah, Wael Said, Mahmoud Elmezain, Osama Elkomy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The rapid expansion of the Internet of Things (IoT) has intensified demands for scalable traffic management, real-time visibility, and resilient control in large, heterogeneous networks. Conventional Software-Defined Networking (SDN) architectures, typically based on centralized control and static forwarding, struggle to address the dynamic, data-intensive behavior of Software-Defined IoT (SD-IoT) systems. To overcome these limitations, this paper proposes MC-LBTO, a modular multi-controller framework that integrates programmable data plane intelligence with adaptive, secure coordination among distributed controllers to optimize load balancing and network efficiency. MC-LBTO comprises three cooperative modules: PDSM (P4-enabled Dynamic State Monitoring) for in-switch traffic observation and analytics; PALB (P4-based Adaptive Load Balancer) for latency-aware and fair traffic distribution; and STAM (Secure Trusted Adaptive Multi-Control) for consistent, fault-tolerant inter-controller operation. Experimental evaluations demonstrate that PDSM reduces controller CPU utilization by 35.7%, enhances flow-state detection accuracy to 98.3%, and lowers monitoring latency by 22.6% compared with OpenFlow-based monitoring. PALB achieves a 36% reduction in request latency, a 25% throughput increase, and a load distribution variance of only 5.5%, outperforming both traditional and modern probabilistic baselines. STAM enhances control-plane robustness with a Mean Time to Recovery (MTTR) of 75 ms, a 70% reduction in packet loss, and state consistency and security indices (SCI/SLI ≥ 9) under failure conditions. Collectively, these results confirm that MC-LBTO enables a scalable, secure, and self-adaptive SD-IoT architecture that maintains low overhead, balanced resource utilization, and fast recovery, offering a technically grounded framework for dependable and high-performance IoT networking.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-31216-6.

Introduction

The proliferation of the Internet of Things (IoT) has irrevocably altered the digital landscape, weaving a complex fabric of interconnected devices into the core of our personal and industrial worlds1. This paradigm shift extends far beyond simple connectivity, heralding an era of unprecedented data generation—a veritable “digital deluge”2,3. However, the data generated by IoT ecosystems are not monolithic. They are characterized by extreme heterogeneity, ranging from low-volume, delay-tolerant telemetry from environmental sensors to high-burst, latency-sensitive streams from industrial control systems, autonomous vehicles, and real-time health monitors4. This diversity in traffic profiles, combined with the sheer scale of deployment, imposes extreme demands on the underlying network infrastructure, pushing traditional networking models to their breaking point5,6.

Conventional network architectures, built on rigid protocols, manual configuration, and vertically integrated hardware, are fundamentally ill-equipped for the dynamic nature of IoT7. Their inability to provide granular visibility, adapt to fluctuating traffic loads in real-time, or scale efficiently renders them inadequate for supporting next-generation IoT applications8,9. In response to these limitations, Software-Defined Networking (SDN) emerged as a revolutionary paradigm. By decoupling the network’s control plane (which dictates traffic policy) from the data plane (which forwards packets), SDN promised to centralize network intelligence, simplify management through programmability, and provide a global view of the network state10.

While this centralization has been instrumental in advancing network automation, the initial SDN model, often reliant on a single controller, introduced its own set of challenges, particularly within the demanding context of Software-Defined IoT (SD-IoT)11. A centralized controller can quickly become a performance bottleneck and a single point of failure, struggling to process the immense volume of flow setup requests and telemetry data emanating from a dense IoT environment12. This control channel saturation can lead to increased latency and degraded reliability, undermining the very agility SDN aims to provide13.

The programmable data plane: pushing intelligence to the network edge

The limitations of early SDN architectures highlighted a critical need: the ability to execute more intelligent functions directly within the network’s data plane. The advent of programmable data planes, epitomized by the P4 programming language, represents a transformative leap in this direction14. P4 signifies a paradigm shift away from the fixed-function Application-Specific Integrated Circuits (ASICs) that have long dominated network hardware15. It empowers network engineers and operators to define precisely how their switches process packets, moving far beyond the predefined match-action rulesets of protocols like OpenFlow16. This programmability enables the implementation of sophisticated, stateful operations directly within the network fabric at line rate17.

Stateful data planes can maintain per-flow context, such as packet counts, byte volumes, and timestamps, without constant interaction with a remote controller. This capability is the cornerstone for offloading critical network functions that were previously confined to the control plane or middlebox appliances18. For instance, fine-grained, real-time traffic monitoring can be embedded into the switch’s processing pipeline, allowing for the immediate detection of anomalies or performance degradation. Similarly, adaptive load balancing logic can be executed locally, enabling switches to make intelligent forwarding decisions based on real-time network conditions and server health, rather than relying on static hashing or delayed instructions from a controller19,20. By pushing this intelligence to the network edge, programmable data planes drastically reduce the processing burden on SDN controllers, minimize the latency of decision-making, and create a more responsive, efficient, and resilient network infrastructure—an essential foundation for the high-performance demands of SD-IoT21.

The multi-controller imperative and the coordination problem

As SD-IoT networks expand in scale and geographic distribution, the single-controller model becomes untenable. The logical evolution is toward a distributed control plane, where multiple SDN controllers work in concert to manage the network22. This multi-controller architecture is imperative not only for enhancing scalability and fault tolerance but also for reducing latency by placing control logic closer to the devices it manages23. However, this distribution introduces a new and formidable layer of complexity: the multi-controller coordination conundrum. To realize the benefits of a distributed control plane, several fundamental challenges must be addressed.

First and foremost is the issue of state consistency. Each controller must maintain a view of the network that is consistent with its peers. A lack of a unified, accurate network state can lead to catastrophic failures, such as routing loops, policy conflicts, or black-holing of traffic. Ensuring that topology changes, flow states, and security policies are synchronized efficiently and correctly across all controllers is a non-trivial problem24.

Second is the challenge of security and trust. In a distributed environment, controllers must be able to authenticate one another and establish secure communication channels to prevent man-in-the-middle attacks, eavesdropping, or the injection of malicious control messages. A compromised controller could wreak havoc on the entire network if trust and security are not explicitly designed into the architecture.

Finally, the system must address controller load balancing and adaptation25. The workload of managing switches and processing network events must be distributed equitably among the controllers26,27. The system must also be able to adapt dynamically if a controller becomes overloaded or fails, redistributing its responsibilities to ensure continuous, high-performance operation28. Addressing this coordination conundrum is fundamental to unlocking the full potential of scalable and resilient SD-IoT networks.

Bridging the gaps: motivation and research questions

While programmable data planes offer unprecedented power at the switch level and multi-controller architectures promise scalability and resilience, a critical integration gap persists in the existing literature. Most solutions address these domains in isolation, focusing either on optimizing data plane functions or on solving inter-controller coordination, but rarely on creating a framework where these two domains work in synergistic harmony. There is a notable lack of a unified architecture that cohesively integrates advanced, P4-based data plane functions for monitoring and load balancing with a robust, secure, and adaptive multi-controller coordination scheme tailored specifically for the dynamic SD-IoT environment. This research is motivated by the need to bridge this gap. We seek to develop an integrated framework that leverages data plane intelligence to not only optimize traffic flow but also to inform and enhance control plane coordination. This goal leads to a series of critical research questions:

Research contributions

To answer these questions, this paper introduces the MC-LBTO (Multi-Controller Load Balancing and Traffic Optimization) framework—a novel, state-aware architecture that integrates established but complementary concepts in a unified, closed-loop design for advanced traffic management in SD-IoT. As illustrated in Fig. 1, MC-LBTO is built on the synergy of three core modules. At the data plane, the PDSM (P4-enabled Dynamic State Monitoring) module provides in-switch, real-time traffic analytics, which directly feeds into the PALB (P4-based Adaptive Load Balancer) module for intelligent, low-latency traffic distribution. Governing these data plane functions is the STAM (Secure Trusted Adaptive Multi-Control) module, which operates at the control plane to ensure consistent, secure, and resilient coordination among distributed SDN controllers. While each of the individual modules in MC-LBTO—namely PDSM, PALB, and STAM—draws upon well-established foundations in P4-based telemetry, adaptive load balancing, and multi-controller SDN coordination, the novelty of this work lies in their synergistic integration into a unified, closed-loop architecture. Unlike prior studies that treat these layers independently, MC-LBTO creates continuous feedback between the data and control planes, where real-time monitoring (PDSM) directly informs load balancing (PALB) and adaptive controller decisions (STAM). This co-design enables end-to-end optimization of efficiency, reliability, and security simultaneously—an integration not previously realized in SD-IoT frameworks.

Literature review

In the context of SD-IoT networks, achieving intelligent and scalable traffic management remains a central research challenge due to the inherent heterogeneity and burstiness of IoT workloads. Ensuring real-time visibility with minimal overhead has led to an increasing reliance on programmable data planes, particularly those based on P4, to offload monitoring and pre-processing from the control plane. Traditional sampling mechanisms such as sFlow and NetFlow provide coarse-grained insights and struggle with accuracy at low sampling rates, introducing control-plane congestion and limited adaptability in dynamic IoT conditions17,29.

To overcome these limitations, modern monitoring systems leverage in-band network telemetry (INT) on P4 switches to achieve fine-grained visibility. Suh et al.30 proposed FS-INT, a flexible sampling mechanism combining rate- and event-driven telemetry, effectively reducing INT overhead. Shao et al.31 employed a Markov Decision Process (MDP) to balance sampling accuracy with device capability, offering adaptive monitoring under varying load conditions. However, these approaches remain controller-dependent and do not link telemetry data to adaptive forwarding. Ding et al.32 addressed limited flow visibility by hybridizing programmable and fixed-function switches but at the cost of higher P4 register complexity.

At the network edge, machine-learning-assisted telemetry systems such as DeepMonitor by Nguyen et al.5 apply deep reinforcement learning to detect fine-grained traffic variations. While effective, such schemes impose heavy computational loads unsuitable for resource-constrained IoT devices. Similarly, FlowSpy33 and the model of Jisi et al.34 integrate ML-driven telemetry and flow optimization but face synchronization and control overhead in multi-switch environments. Aleisa35 further enhanced classification accuracy through a fused neural architecture (SMRFO), achieving over 99% QoS-aware flow categorization, yet its centralized inference pipeline limits real-time responsiveness.

In contrast, MC-LBTO’s PDSM module embeds state-awareness directly into the P4 data plane, generating adaptive telemetry without controller intervention. This achieves line-rate monitoring precision while preserving scalability — a capability absent from most prior telemetry frameworks.

Complementary to visibility, adaptive load balancing is essential for maintaining QoS and avoiding congestion in SD-IoT infrastructures. Traditional software-based or controller-centric load balancers exhibit latency and scaling bottlenecks, motivating transitions to programmable and AI-driven architectures36,37. Wang et al.38 distributed load across multiple controllers, improving responsiveness but without real-time feedback from data-plane states. PrePass39 dynamically manages large flows using wildcard rules, but dependency on reactive classification introduces latency. Similarly, Yaghma et al.40 employed an ILP-based heuristic for QoS-aware assignment, and Chien et al.41 utilized KKT optimization for service-function chaining; however, both rely on offline or controller-level computation and lack real-time adaptability. Qi et al.42 adopted multi-path routing for bandwidth efficiency but without integrating delay or congestion feedback.

The PALB module of MC-LBTO, through its PAWR algorithm, directly addresses these gaps by embedding adaptive weighted balancing into the P4 pipeline, enabling real-time flow redistribution based on instantaneous telemetry metrics rather than delayed control-plane updates. This distinguishes PALB from prior reactive or heuristic schemes that operate outside the data plane.

Balancing traffic among distributed controllers has been another research focus. Chaudhary et al.43 proposed the LOAD scheme to reduce migration overhead, while Xu et al.44 introduced BalCon/Plus for dynamic switch migration. Although these methods enhance cluster-level performance, they lack secure synchronization and telemetry integration. Hsu et al.45 developed a P4-based hash-splitting scheme, improving update latency but risking packet reordering. Shahrbabaki et al.46 and Kazemiesfeh et al.47 refined controller load balancing through multi-level thresholds and multi-criteria decision-making (MCDM) models but evaluated only a limited set of metrics.

In contrast, MC-LBTO’s STAM component extends beyond these methods by incorporating secure, trust-aware multi-controller coordination with cryptographically verifiable state synchronization, achieving resilient coordination without sacrificing latency or scalability — a combination not achieved by previous controller migration or clustering frameworks.

Recent advances in production-grade load-balancing systems further illustrate the evolution of this field. Google’s Maglev48 and Facebook’s Katran (Consistent Hash + EWMA)49 achieve high scalability and flow stickiness via hash-based mapping but depend on centralized table management, making them unsuitable for distributed IoT topologies that require dynamic feedback. Queue-aware algorithms such as Least Outstanding Requests (LOR)50 and probabilistic schemes like Power-of-Two-Choices (P2C)51 improve fairness and throughput under bursty traffic but rely on continuous backend feedback loops, increasing control overhead.

Additionally, DOLPHIN52 presents a dynamically optimized load-balancing path selection framework for inter-domain SDN communication, improving flow completion times across 5G and data center environments through multi-link utilization. An SDN-based UAV control framework53 extends software-defined networking to aerial systems, balancing load while conserving UAV battery life. However, both systems remain single-controller or domain-specific and do not address multi-controller trust management or cross-layer telemetry integration.

The proposed MC-LBTO framework

This section introduces the architecture and integrated operational model of the proposed MC-LBTO (Multi-Controller Load Balancing and Traffic Optimization) framework, a holistic solution tailored to the traffic management and coordination demands of SD-IoT networks. MC-LBTO is designed as a modular system, composed of three synergistic components: PDSM (P4-enabled Dynamic State Monitoring), PALB (P4-based Adaptive Load Balancer), and STAM (Secure Trusted Adaptive Multi-Control). Each module contributes to a different functional layer of the framework—PDSM and PALB operate within the programmable data plane, while STAM governs the distributed control plane logic. These modules collectively enable real-time traffic visibility, intelligent in-switch load balancing, and resilient coordination among multiple SDN controllers. The design philosophy of MC-LBTO is rooted in decoupling and distributing intelligence across layers: pushing state-aware decision-making closer to the packet path via P4-programmable switches while securing and scaling controller interactions through trusted coordination protocols.

Although MC-LBTO integrates three custom modules—PDSM, PALB, and STAM—within a unified architecture, this coupling is deliberate rather than monolithic. The design ensures a closed-loop exchange of telemetry, load state, and control feedback that cannot be achieved through loosely linked, standard components such as INT and Raft. In contrast to a modular combination of existing solutions, the integrated design minimizes inter-layer signaling latency, eliminates redundant state synchronization, and allows P4-based data plane logic to react directly to control plane events in real time. Each module remains functionally decoupled through standardized APIs and gRPC interfaces, maintaining modularity while achieving the responsiveness and cross-plane awareness required for dynamic SD-IoT networks.

Figure 1 provides a high-level architectural overview of the MC-LBTO framework, depicting the vertical layering of its three primary components. At the bottom layer, the data plane consists of P4-enabled switches embedded with the PDSM and PALB modules. Incoming traffic flows are intercepted and analyzed by PDSM, which extracts fine-grained flow features such as byte rate, DSCP markings, idle time, and queue depth. This real-time monitoring stream is locally available to the PALB module in the same switch, allowing it to make responsive, state-aware load balancing decisions. Each flow is mapped to an optimal backend server based on dynamic server health status, traffic distribution fairness, and policy constraints. The switches themselves handle flow assignment without requiring round trips to the control plane, minimizing latency and offloading centralized components. This intra-switch interaction is illustrated in Fig. 1 by the internal arrows linking PDSM to PALB and the external arrows showing IoT traffic traversing the system. Elevating to the control plane, the STAM module—as shown in the top tier of Fig. 1—resides within multiple distributed SDN controllers. STAM’s key responsibility is to maintain consistent network state across all controllers via Trusted Communication Channels (TCC), enabling secure synchronization of flow tables, topology views, and policy configurations. Figure 1 visualizes these interactions as bidirectional coordination links connecting peer controllers. Moreover, the figure shows how STAM receives event-driven feedback from the data plane (via digest messages generated by PDSM or reconfiguration alerts from PALB) and uses this intelligence to adapt control policies in real time.

PDSM: P4-Enabled dynamic state monitoring module

The PDSM (P4-Enabled Dynamic State Monitoring) module is the primary component responsible for network observation within the MC-LBTO framework. Executed within the programmable data plane of P4 switches, PDSM provides the real-time, fine-grained traffic visibility necessary for the effective operation of the framework’s adaptive load balancing (PALB) and multi-controller coordination (STAM) modules. The P4 implementation utilizes standard constructs like programmable parsers, match-action tables, and deparsers to execute the PDSM logic within the switch, as conceptually illustrated in Fig. 2. Rules within the match-action pipeline (step 3 in Fig. 2) handle flow identification and trigger actions. While current validation employs IP-based traffic for consistency with SDN/P4 environments, MC-LBTO’s modular P4 data-plane design allows extension to non-IP IoT protocols by customizing header parsers and metadata extraction rules.

The PDSM module provides the essential network monitoring capabilities for the MC-LBTO framework, operating within the P4 data plane. Its function is to capture detailed, real-time information about network traffic, which informs the adaptive behaviors of the PALB and STAM modules. Table 2 presents an expanded view of representative features that PDSM can derive from network packets using P4 capabilities. This includes not only basic flow identification tuples (FlowSig, SrcAddr, DstAddr, etc.) and packet attributes (PktLenBytes, IngressPortID), but also vital statistics (AccBytes, AccPkts), detailed timing information (FirstSeenTS, LastSeenTS, IdleTime), Quality of Service indicators (DSCPVal), protocol-specific flags (TCPFlagsVal), and calculated performance metrics like packet/byte rates (PktRatePPS, ByteRateBPS) and delay variation (PktDelayVar). Capturing this comprehensive feature set allows for a nuanced understanding of network conditions.

The operational logic of the PDSM module, executed for each packet within the P4 data plane, is detailed in Algorithms 1.1 and 1.2 (Supplementary Material). The process begins (Step 1) with parsing the incoming packet (pkt) headers and extracting the relevant baseline features defined in Table 2. This includes essential data like IP addresses, port numbers, protocol identifiers, and packet length. A unique flow signature (flow_sig) is generated from these features to track the packet’s corresponding flow. Basic error checking is performed during parsing to handle malformed packets appropriately.

Following feature extraction, the algorithm proceeds to state management (Steps 2–4). It attempts to locate an existing state record for the flow_sig within the switch’s persistent state storage (flow_state_db). Using conditional logic (IF-ELSE), PDSM handles both existing and new flows. If a state record is found, the Update_Existing_State function is called to update metrics such as accumulated byte and packet counts (AccBytes, AccPkts) and the last seen timestamp (LastSeenTS). This function also checks if the update constitutes a significant state change based on configured thresholds, setting the state_change_flag accordingly. If no record exists, Initialize_New_State creates a new entry, setting initial values for counters and timestamps, and the state_change_flag is set to true.

Subsequent steps refine the collected information and prepare for packet handling. Runtime calculations (Step 5) can be performed using the updated state to compute derived metrics like FlowDuration, IdleTime, or estimated packet/byte rates (PktRatePPS, ByteRateBPS), providing richer insights than raw counters alone. Optional local decisions (Step 6) allow for simple policies (e.g., marking or rate-limiting specific flows based on monitored state) to be applied directly within PDSM, although complex actions are typically deferred. The primary packet action, usually forwarding, is determined (Step 7).

A key function of PDSM is state reporting (Steps 8–9). The algorithm checks if a report should be triggered based on either a periodic timer (ReportingTimer) expiring or a significant state change being detected (state_change_flag = = TRUE). If a report is triggered, relevant data from the current_flow_state is aggregated. PDSM then conceptually prepares this data (Prepare_Data_For_STAM) for export, making it available to the PALB module for load balancing computations and to the STAM module for control plane coordination and potential secure transmission to relevant controllers.

Finally, the algorithm executes the determined packet action (Step 9), such as forwarding the packet towards its destination via the appropriate output port or dropping it if mandated by a local decision or external policy (though complex policy enforcement usually involves STAM/controllers). By structuring the monitoring process in this manner within the P4 data plane, PDSM efficiently generates detailed, real-time state information for network flows. This data is fundamental to the MC-LBTO framework, enabling the adaptive load balancing performed by PALB and the coordinated control actions orchestrated by STAM, all while minimizing direct dependence on the control plane for basic monitoring tasks.

PALB: P4-Enabled adaptive load balancer module

The PALB (P4-Enabled Adaptive Load Balancer) module operates within the data plane of the MC-LBTO framework, working in concert with the PDSM module to intelligently distribute incoming traffic across available backend resources, such as IoT application servers. Its primary goal is to enhance network performance and resource utilization by dynamically adapting traffic distribution based on real-time network conditions, rather than relying on static distribution rules. This adaptive capability is powered by the PAWR (P4 Adaptive State-Aware Round-Robin) algorithm, detailed below. Table 3 summarizes key symbols and definitions used in the PALB algorithm description.

The core logic of PALB, implementing the PAWR method, is presented in Algorithm 2 (Supplementary Material), and visually illustrated in Fig. 3. The process begins after a packet (pkt) has passed initial ingress checks. The first crucial step (Algorithm 2, Step 1) involves calculating weights for each potential backend server listed in the server_status_db. This database contains essential, up-to-date information about each server’s operational status (s.Health) and available resources (s.SrvRes), which is maintained accurately (updated via the STAM coordination module). The CalculateWeight function determines each server’s weight (s.Weight); importantly, servers marked as DOWN receive a weight of zero. This weight calculation is adaptive – it primarily reflects the server’s available resources (s.SrvRes) but can also incorporate real-time network metrics provided by PDSM (PDSM_data), such as relevant input queue depths (InQueueDepth), allowing for sensitivity to immediate network congestion near the server’s ingress. The TotalWeight of all currently available servers is computed. If no servers are available (TotalWeight < = 0), the packet is dropped.

Step 2 performs the weighted server selection. A random value (RandomVal) is generated within the range of the TotalWeight. The algorithm then iterates through the servers, accumulating weights (CumulativeWeight). The first server whose weight causes the CumulativeWeight to exceed the RandomVal is chosen as the SelectedServer. This weighted random selection ensures that servers with higher weights (indicating more available resources or better health) receive a proportionally larger share of the traffic over time.

To handle potential race conditions where a server’s status might change between the weight calculation/selection phase and the actual packet forwarding, Step 3 includes a post-selection health check. It re-verifies if the SelectedServer is still UP. If it has gone DOWN unexpectedly, the algorithm attempts to find the next available server using a simple round-robin approach (Find_Next_Available_Server_RR), maintaining resilience. If no alternative is found, the packet is dropped. The LastServerIndex is used to maintain context for this round-robin fallback mechanism.

Once a viable SelectedServer is confirmed, Step 4 involves modifying the packet’s headers (Modify_Pkt_Headers) to redirect it to the chosen server by updating the destination MAC and IP addresses accordingly. Conceptually, the algorithm also notes the resource usage on the selected server, although the definitive tracking and updating of s.SrvRes might be managed by the control plane (via STAM) based on periodic reports or other metrics to maintain consistency across switches.

Finally, in Step 5, the packet is forwarded out of the appropriate switch port (SelectedServer.Port) towards the chosen backend server. By executing this PAWR logic within the data plane, PALB achieves adaptive load balancing that responds to dynamic server resource availability and potentially other real-time metrics from PDSM. This distribution method optimizes resource utilization, minimizes response times, and enhances the overall efficiency and resilience of traffic management within the MC-LBTO framework.

Framework integration and synergy

The effectiveness of the MC-LBTO framework stems from the synergistic integration of its three core modules: PDSM, PALB, and STAM. While the preceding sections detailed their individual designs, this section elucidates how they collaborate across the data and control planes to achieve optimized, adaptive, and resilient traffic management in multi-controller SD-IoT environments. The conceptual integration and primary information flows are illustrated in Fig. 5. Unlike conventional modular frameworks that loosely couple monitoring, balancing, and coordination, MC-LBTO establishes a closed-loop feedback system where each module continuously informs and refines the others. This integration transforms the architecture from a set of isolated functions into a unified intelligence layer that spans both data and control planes.

The integration begins in the data plane, where the PDSM module (Sect. “PDSM: P4-Enabled dynamic state monitoring module”) acts as the framework’s sensory component. Executing within the P4 switch core (Fig. 5, step 2), PDSM processes incoming packets, extracts detailed features (Table 2), and maintains real-time flow state in connected state tables (Fig. 5, step 1). This granular network visibility is the foundational input for the other modules.

The monitoring insights generated by PDSM directly inform the PALB module (Sect. “PALB: P4-Enabled adaptive load balancer module”), also operating in the data plane (Fig. 5, step 3). PALB consumes real-time state information exported by PDSM, such as ingress queue depth (InQueueDepth) or flow statistics (AccBytes, PktRatePPS), along with server status information. It uses this data within its PAWR algorithm (Algorithm 2) to make intelligent, adaptive load balancing decisions, distributing traffic efficiently based on current conditions rather than static rules. PALB also maintains its own synchronized state related to load balancing decisions (Fig. 5, steps 5, 6).

Simultaneously, monitoring data from PDSM is exported towards the control plane, where the STAM module (Sect. “STAM: secure trusted adaptive multi-control module”) utilizes it for coordination and adaptive control (Fig. 5, step 4). STAM receives aggregated reports and critical alerts derived from PDSM’s analysis. This allows the distributed controllers, orchestrated by STAM, to maintain a consistent network view, evaluate controller load, and detect network-wide anomalies or specific threats (Fig. 5, step 7).

Based on this comprehensive view, STAM facilitates trusted coordination (Fig. 5, step 8), potentially disseminating updated policies or configurations via its Trusted Communication Channel (TCC). These directives can influence data plane behavior, for instance, by providing updated server health/resource information to the server_status_db used by PALB, or even adjusting PALB’s weighting parameters in response to security events.

MC-LBTO performance evaluation

This section evaluates the performance and effectiveness of the proposed MC-LBTO framework and its constituent modules: PDSM, PALB, and STAM. Through simulation-based experiments mimicking SD-IoT network environments, we assess the framework’s capabilities against key performance metrics. The evaluation aims to validate the design choices, quantify the benefits derived from the integrated approach, and demonstrate the specific contributions of each module. The following subsections present the evaluation setup, scenarios, results, and analysis for the PDSM, PALB, and STAM modules individually.

All experiments were repeated three times using identical traffic traces and topology configurations, with results reported as averaged values. Standard deviation across runs remained below 3% for all primary metrics, confirming statistical stability of the observed trends.

To facilitate reproducibility and practical adoption, we have made the full source code of the MC-LBTO framework publicly available at50. The repository includes modular implementations of all core components: PDSM, PALB, and STAM for both Java-based and Python-based SDN controllers. It also provides emulation-ready scripts for Mininet-WiFi and BMv2, topology definitions, P4Runtime controller applications, server monitoring utilities, automated traffic generation tools, and a unified deployment launcher. Comprehensive documentation is included to assist users in replicating the experiments and adapting the framework to real-world SD-IoT testbeds.

Evaluation of PDSM module performance

To comprehensively assess the proposed PDSM (P4-Enabled Dynamic State Monitoring) module within the MC-LBTO framework, we conducted rigorous emulation-based evaluations in SD-IoT environments. The evaluation spans multiple performance dimensions, including accuracy, latency, scalability, and resource efficiency, comparing PDSM against a traditional OpenFlow-based monitoring approach. The aim is to validate the technical benefits of offloading stateful logic to programmable switches, ensuring both responsiveness and scalability in edge-centric IoT networks.

The experimental environment is constructed using Mininet-WiFi, configured to emulate a heterogeneous IoT network with multiple P4-enabled virtual switches (specifically, BMv2 simple_switch_grpc targets), connected in a hierarchical topology reflecting core-edge device distribution. The network is controlled via the ONOS controller equipped with P4Runtime support, enabling seamless integration of custom P4 programs. We populated the environment with 100 emulated SDN/IoT devices, varying from personal computers, tablets, sensors transmitting periodic data to smart devices with bursty traffic, to reproduce realistic workloads. For comprehensive testing, we incorporated background flows and malicious patterns, such as stealthy low-rate DDoS attacks and scanning probes, generated via iperf3, hping3 and custom traffic scripts.

The baseline OpenFlow setup uses version 1.3+, implementing periodic controller polling for flow statistics using OFPFlowStatsRequest messages. OpenFlow meters and group tables are leveraged to approximate similar monitoring logic. A controller-side anomaly detection logic is deployed to mirror the detection goals of PDSM, enabling fair comparative benchmarking.

Monitoring accuracy was evaluated in terms of true positive rate under varying traffic conditions. The PDSM achieved a consistent detection rate of 98.3%, outperforming the OpenFlow baseline, which reached only 91.4% under identical input conditions. This significant difference arises from the granular, flow-specific state maintained directly in the P4 pipeline, which allows PDSM to differentiate between normal fluctuations and true anomalies. In contrast, OpenFlow’s batch-style monitoring via periodic polling introduces temporal granularity gaps that delay anomaly recognition.

In terms of event detection latency, PDSM demonstrated a 22.6% average reduction in response time to anomalous events. Since PDSM processes and flags abnormal patterns within the data plane itself, event propagation and processing delays are minimized. The OpenFlow baseline, relying on a controller-based processing loop, incurred an average latency of 410ms, compared to PDSM’s 317ms. This latency reduction is critical in latency-sensitive SDN/IoT contexts such as health monitoring or autonomous systems.

The resource efficiency dimension is particularly vital in SDN/IoT edge environments. Measurements of CPU utilization on switches showed that PDSM reduced processor overhead by 24.1%, as stateful operations are executed via match-action tables rather than controller-induced computations. Similarly, memory footprint on switches was reduced by 17.3%, thanks to efficient in-pipeline state storage in P4 registers and counters. Controller-side metrics further reinforce the scalability advantage: bandwidth usage due to monitoring dropped by 29.4%, and controller CPU load fell by 35.7%, as it was freed from repetitive polling and flow state reconstruction tasks.

Additional metrics were recorded to evaluate the scalability and robustness of the PDSM module. As illustrated in Fig. 6, when the network scale increased from 100 to 400 devices, PDSM maintained stable detection accuracy with only a 4.2% drop, whereas OpenFlow’s accuracy degraded by 11.6%, primarily due to increased polling delays and controller queue saturation. PDSM’s performance under concurrent anomaly injection scenarios (e.g., multiple low-rate DDoS flows) remained robust, exhibiting only marginal increases in latency and no degradation in precision.

The PDSM module also demonstrated strong adaptability and programmability. Through minor updates to the P4 program, we successfully reconfigured detection thresholds and flow classification rules, validating the framework’s extensibility to future traffic patterns and threat models without requiring hardware changes or controller-side overhauls. This dynamic configurability contrasts with the rigidity of OpenFlow-based mechanisms, which often require extensive rule updates or external processing pipelines.

In summary, the experimental findings confirm that PDSM delivers significantly higher detection accuracy, lower latency, and superior resource efficiency compared to traditional OpenFlow methods, as depicted in Fig. 6. Its in-data-plane design not only improves responsiveness but also ensures scalability and adaptability, making it highly suitable for dynamic and resource-constrained SD-IoT environments.

Based on the evaluation, we recommend PDSM deployment in topologies with ≥ 3-tier edge-core hierarchies, P4 switches with at least 8 MB memory allocation for registers, and controller configurations optimized for asynchronous event handling rather than synchronous polling, to fully leverage PDSM’s advantages in operational environments.

While the preceding analysis establishes PALB’s superiority over conventional approaches such as Round Robin, Connection Hash, and SHELL, the evaluation is further extended to include a broader set of modern, state-of-the-art load-balancing algorithms widely adopted in both research and production environments. This extension enables a more rigorous assessment against techniques that employ dynamic feedback, probabilistic decision-making, and consistent-hash mechanisms for large-scale and latency-sensitive network environments.

The newly considered baselines encompass four advanced algorithms representing the current evolution of load-balancing design. Maglev, a production-grade consistent-hash system developed by Google48, ensures minimal packet remapping and highly stable flow distribution through deterministic permutation tables. Least Outstanding Requests (LOR)50 adopts a queue-aware strategy that always assigns new flows to the backend with the fewest active requests, optimizing responsiveness under fluctuating load conditions. Power-of-Two-Choices (P2C)51 applies a probabilistic sampling process, selecting the lighter-loaded of two randomly chosen servers, thereby achieving near-optimal balance with low computational overhead. Finally, Consistent Hash with Exponentially Weighted Moving Average (CHE)49—implemented in Facebook’s Katran—extends consistent hashing by dynamically adjusting backend weights according to latency trends, promoting smooth adaptation to server performance changes.

ONOS, for example, often shows strong performance attributable to its inherent design for efficient inter-controller communication (leveraged by STAM), high scalability, and robust fault tolerance mechanisms. The distributed control plane enabled by STAM allows for more localized decision-making based on PDSM inputs potentially closer to the source, reducing latency, and improves overall network performance by distributing the processing load associated with PDSM data analysis and PALB policy management.

Integrated framework evaluation

While the preceding sections validated the distinct performance advantages of the PDSM, PALB, and STAM modules, the defining strength of the MC-LBTO framework emerges from their synergistic interaction. This section evaluates the integrated framework’s performance under several dynamic scenarios specifically designed to stress these inter-module collaborations and demonstrate the resulting benefits in system adaptability, resilience, policy enforcement, and overall efficiency. The evaluation utilizes the established multi-controller simulation setup, allowing observation of the complete data-and-control plane workflow coordinated by STAM, informed by PDSM, and acted upon by PALB. Baseline comparisons reference systems lacking this tight integration (e.g., using static load balancing or non-adaptive/uncoordinated control). Table 11 summarizes the integration evaluation results across five different scenarios, while Fig. 11 provides a visual comparison of these performance results with and without MC-LBTO across five operational conditions—traffic surge, server failure, QoS enforcement, DDoS mitigation, and controller overload—demonstrating the synergistic adaptability, resilience, and stability achieved through the coordinated interaction of PDSM, PALB, and STAM modules.

Scenario 1: Rapid Traffic Surge Adaptation

This scenario evaluates the framework’s responsiveness to sudden traffic load imbalances, primarily testing the PDSM-PALB feedback loop. A baseline traffic load was established across the five backend servers before introducing a high-volume traffic burst (3x baseline rate) directed towards the service IP, initially overloading specific servers under a non-adaptive scheme. PDSM’s real-time monitoring capabilities immediately detected the surge through rapidly increasing flow rates (PktRatePPS) and ingress queue levels (InQueueDepth) on affected paths. This state information was promptly made available to PALB. Leveraging these inputs, PALB’s PAWR algorithm dynamically recalculated server weights, significantly down-weighting the congested servers. Within approximately 500ms, PALB effectively redistributed the incoming surge traffic across servers with available capacity (s.SrvRes). Numerically, the Load Distribution Variance (LDV), which spiked momentarily, quickly settled back to ~ 6.5%, demonstrating effective re-balancing. In contrast, baseline systems using static Round Robin or Connection Hash exhibited sustained high LDV (> 18%) throughout the surge. Consequently, while MC-LBTO experienced only a transient increase in Average Request Latency (ARL) peaking at ~ 25ms before recovering to near-baseline (16ms), the baseline systems suffered from persistently elevated ARL (> 30ms) due to continued server overload. Furthermore, Packet Loss Ratio (PLR) under MC-LBTO remained minimal (< 2.5%), whereas the baselines showed significantly higher losses (> 8%) as server queues overflowed. This scenario clearly demonstrates the PDSM-PALB synergy enabling rapid, data-plane-driven adaptation to load bursts.

Scenario 2: Backend Server Failure and Recovery

This test assesses the resilience imparted by the integrated framework, focusing on the STAM-PALB interaction informed by health status updates. During steady-state operation, one of the five backend servers was abruptly terminated. Failure detection occurred rapidly through STAM-coordinated health checks and PDSM detecting persistent timeouts, triggering an alert. STAM securely propagated the server ‘DOWN’ status update via its TCC to all relevant controllers and updated the shared server_status_db within ~ 80ms. Upon reading the updated status, PALB’s PAWR algorithm immediately excluded the failed server from its selection pool (weight became zero), preventing further traffic dispatch to the unavailable resource. This swift exclusion resulted in only a momentary increase in Server Selection Failure Rate (SSFR) before stabilizing. Load was automatically redistributed by PALB across the remaining four servers, causing a moderate, temporary rise in ARL to ~ 20ms. Crucially, the system stabilized quickly; the Mean Time To Recovery (MTTR) for performance metrics (ARL/NTH returning to stable levels for the four-server configuration) was measured at approximately 150ms post-failure detection and state update. When the server was later restored, STAM coordinated the ‘UP’ status update, allowing PALB to seamlessly reintegrate the server back into the active pool, restoring baseline performance. This coordinated state management via STAM ensures significantly faster and more graceful failure handling than systems relying on slower, uncoordinated detection or manual intervention.

Scenario 3: QoS-Aware Balancing via STAM Policy

This scenario evaluates the framework’s capability to enforce control-plane policies within the data plane, testing the STAM-PDSM-PALB interaction path. Mixed traffic containing both best-effort flows and high-priority flows (identified by a specific DSCP value) was directed to the server pool. A QoS policy was then dynamically deployed via STAM, instructing the system to prioritize the high-priority traffic, by directing it towards servers identified (via PDSM metrics processed by STAM) as having lower current latency or higher available resources. PDSM successfully identified the priority packets using the DSCPVal feature within the data plane. STAM disseminated the policy context to the P4 switch via the controller. PALB’s PAWR logic (specifically the CalculateWeight function and associated P4 actions) incorporated this policy context. When processing high-priority flows, PALB assigned preferential weighting towards servers designated as suitable by the policy and current state. Measurement confirmed successful prioritization: the ARL for high-priority flows averaged 12ms, substantially lower than the 20ms ARL observed for best-effort traffic under the same moderate load conditions. Baseline systems lacking this integrated policy enforcement mechanism showed no significant ARL difference between traffic classes (~ 28-30ms for both). This demonstrates MC-LBTO’s ability to translate centralized policy directives (via STAM) into differentiated data plane treatment (via PALB) using PDSM’s fine-grained identification features.

Scenario 4: Coordinated DDoS Mitigation

Here, we tested the integrated security response capabilities involving PDSM’s detection and STAM’s coordination. A low-rate DDoS attack was simulated from multiple sources targeting a single backend server amidst legitimate traffic. PDSM’s stateful tracking algorithm detected the anomalous traffic pattern (e.g., unusual connection rates/durations) within ~ 2 seconds and generated an alert report exported to the STAM coordination layer. STAM received this alert securely via its TCC and disseminated it to relevant control instances. A coordinated mitigation response was initiated within ~ 200ms, involving STAM instructing controllers to push P4 rules via P4Runtime. These rules specifically matched the attack signature (identified using features provided in PDSM’s alert) and enforced a ‘drop’ and ‘rate-limit’ action directly in the data plane. Consequently, the Packet Loss Ratio (PLR) for identified attack traffic rapidly approached 100%. Importantly, the performance for legitimate traffic remained largely unaffected during the mitigated attack, with ARL stable around ~ 18ms and NTH near ~ 900 Mbps. In contrast, allowing the unmitigated low-rate attack to continue in baseline tests led to resource exhaustion on the target server, causing legitimate traffic ARL to climb above 70ms and PLR to exceed 10%. This scenario highlights the critical value of integrating PDSM’s data plane detection with STAM’s rapid, secure alert handling and coordinated mitigation deployment.

Scenario 5: Controller Overload and STAM Adaptation

This scenario evaluates STAM’s ability to maintain control plane stability under stress, leveraging its adaptive features. A high rate of new, short-lived flows was injected into the network, maximizing the frequency of PDSM state reports and stressing the processing capacity of the distributed controllers. As STAM monitored individual controller states (CPUU), it detected when utilization exceeded a predefined threshold (e.g., 70%). The dynamic adaptation trigger within STAM (Algorithm 3, Phase 5) activated within ~ 500ms of sustained high load detection. STAM then coordinated adaptive responses (Phase 6) across the controllers via the TCC; in this test, it involved instructing controllers to signal PDSM (via P4Runtime updates) to temporarily increase its reporting interval for routine flow statistics, thereby reducing the telemetry load on the controllers. Critical alerts, however, continued to be transmitted without delay. This adaptation allowed individual controller CPUU to stabilize at a more manageable level (~ 45–60%), preventing overload, while only causing a minor, transient increase in ARL (~ 17ms). State consistency (SCI) remained high throughout the adaptation process. This validates STAM’s capability to dynamically adjust control plane parameters based on system load, ensuring stability and responsiveness even under stress.

Scenario 6: Stochastic and Adversarial IoT Workload Robustness

To examine MC-LBTO’s stability under unpredictable IoT workloads, this scenario emulates a heterogeneous and time-varying environment composed of 10,000 concurrent IoT devices generating a mix of periodic telemetry, bursty event-driven traffic, and low-rate adversarial probes. The traffic patterns follow a Poisson inter-arrival process with variable burst durations (exponentially distributed) and include malformed or out-of-spec control packets to simulate benign noise and lightweight adversarial interference. PDSM dynamically adapts its telemetry sampling to focus on volatile flows exhibiting sudden deviations in rate or entropy. Meanwhile, PALB’s PAWR algorithm continuously re-evaluates backend weights based on evolving latency and congestion data supplied by PDSM. STAM monitors inter-controller synchronization delay and CPU utilization to ensure stable operation even when feedback events occur asynchronously. Across 60 simulation cycles, MC-LBTO maintained throughput stability within ± 4.5% of baseline conditions, with average ARL fluctuation of only ± 2.3 ms despite randomized bursts. The State Consistency Index (SCI) remained above 0.93 throughout, confirming sustained synchronization even under irregular telemetry input. Importantly, transient CPU utilization spikes at controllers never exceeded 65%, validating STAM’s adaptive feedback mechanism. In contrast, a non-integrated baseline (traditional SDN with static load balancing) exhibited far higher variability—up to ± 18% throughput deviation and frequent temporary policy inconsistencies between controllers due to delayed updates. This scenario confirms that MC-LBTO sustains high stability and fairness even under stochastic and partially adversarial workloads, demonstrating its robustness and adaptability in real-world, unpredictable IoT conditions.

In conclusion, these integrated evaluation scenarios effectively demonstrate the synergistic benefits of the MC-LBTO framework. The tight coupling between PDSM’s real-time data plane monitoring, PALB’s adaptive load balancing, and STAM’s secure, adaptive multi-controller coordination enables the system to handle dynamic network events, enforce complex policies, mitigate threats, and maintain stability far more effectively than systems lacking this level of integration.

Hardware feasibility, algorithmic complexity, and deployment considerations

While the evaluation of MC-LBTO was conducted using Mininet-WiFi and BMv2 (simple_switch_grpc) emulators to ensure reproducibility and control, practical deployment on real P4-enabled hardware introduces additional considerations related to scalability, computational complexity, cost, and operational feasibility. This section analyzes the transition from the emulated setup to hardware environments, quantifies expected performance deltas, and provides formal scalability insights for each module.

Hardware Feasibility and Resource Footprint

Hardware Feasibility and Resource Footprint

Hardware Feasibility and Resource Footprint

The MC-LBTO framework was synthesized and tested using the P4 Studio SDE 2024.2 compiler targeting the Intel Tofino 1 (32 × 100 GbE) architecture. The compiled pipeline consumed approximately 4.8% of total match-action stages, 7.3% of register memory, and under 5% of metadata buses, confirming that MC-LBTO modules—PDSM (monitoring), PALB (load balancing), and STAM (coordination)—can coexist with standard forwarding logic. The per-packet processing latency remained below 900 ns, compared with 35–50 µs in BMv2, implying a 40×–50× latency reduction at line rate. PALB’s measured throughput of 920 Mbps in emulation scales proportionally to roughly 35–40 Gbps on Tofino hardware under similar traffic, limited mainly by interface bandwidth.

(2) Performance and Cost Implications

Performance and Cost Implications

Transitioning to hardware results in measurable performance gains and moderate costs. Modern P4 ASICs sustain 6.4–12.8 Tbps aggregate capacity, supporting large-scale IoT aggregation at true line rate. For example, 10 IoT gateways each producing 1 Gbps peak telemetry would occupy < 0.2% of a single Tofino 1’s capacity. CPIR (< 1 op/s in emulation) is expected to drop further on hardware because of atomic counter updates and in-pipeline statistics. Research-grade P4 switches such as EdgeCore Wedge 100BF-32X or NoviSwitch 4000 cost USD 8 000–18 000 per unit, making a five-node prototype feasible at about USD 50 000, a reasonable investment for deterministic performance and robust coordination.

Limitations and future works

While the MC-LBTO framework presents a robust approach for coordinated traffic management in SD-IoT networks, certain functional aspects represent limitations in the current scope and offer avenues for future enhancement. Although the current evaluation relies on Mininet-WiFi and BMv2 emulation, minor quantitative differences are expected when deploying MC-LBTO on physical P4 hardware operating at true line rate. Hardware-based forwarding typically yields lower absolute latency and higher throughput due to ASIC acceleration, but also introduces configuration overheads and cost considerations (see Sect. “Hardware Feasibility, algorithmic Complexity, and deployment considerations”). Future work will extend validation to hybrid testbeds combining BMv2 and Tofino-based switches to empirically verify scalability, latency margins, and synchronization efficiency under production-scale IoT workloads.

From a functional perspective, although the STAM module incorporates important security features like controller authentication and trusted communication channels, the framework in its present form does not include comprehensive IDS designed to operate within the data plane. Addressing the full spectrum of sophisticated cyber threats targeting IoT ecosystems necessitates specialized security mechanisms beyond the foundational security provided by STAM. Furthermore, the current evaluation primarily considered IP-based traffic flows; extending the framework’s applicability to seamlessly handle the diverse range of potentially non-IP heterogeneous communication protocols commonly found in real-world IoT deployments requires further adaptation and research.

Building upon the existing MC-LBTO architecture, several future research directions are identified to address these limitations and further enhance the framework’s capabilities. Integrating advanced security features is a high priority. Future work should involve the development and integration of dedicated P4-based IDS/IPS modules. Such modules could leverage the rich state insights provided by PDSM to detect a wider array of attacks, including sophisticated reconnaissance or malware propagation attempts, directly within the data plane for rapid response. Exploration of Machine Learning (ML) or Artificial Intelligence (AI) techniques, potentially executed or coordinated via the STAM module, presents a future direction for enabling real-time threat classification and guiding automated mitigation actions. Enhancing the security and verifiability of the inter-controller communication managed by STAM, perhaps through lightweight distributed ledger technologies, could also be investigated.

Another significant avenue for future work lies in incorporating machine learning for adaptive optimization more deeply within the framework. ML models could be trained using historical and real-time data gathered by PDSM to predict traffic patterns or network state evolution. These predictions could enable preemptive adjustments in PALB’s load balancing strategies (e.g., anticipating server load changes) or facilitate dynamic tuning of PDSM’s monitoring parameters and thresholds for improved accuracy and efficiency. Within the STAM module, ML or techniques like reinforcement learning could be employed to further optimize controller load distribution, refine adaptive communication strategies between controllers, and develop more sophisticated, data-driven network policy refinement based on long-term performance feedback and objectives. Pursuing these directions can significantly enhance the intelligence, security, and applicability of the MC-LBTO framework.

Conclusion

This work presented MC-LBTO, a unified and modular framework that bridges real-time visibility, adaptive load balancing, and secure multi-controller coordination in SD-IoT networks. Rather than treating these functions separately, MC-LBTO establishes a closed-loop interaction between the data and control planes, where local switch intelligence, dynamic resource optimization, and trusted coordination continuously reinforce each other to sustain network efficiency and resilience.

Through its three modules—PDSM for in-switch telemetry, PALB for adaptive state-aware balancing, and STAM for secure coordination—MC-LBTO demonstrates that programmable data planes and distributed control can be co-designed as a synergistic ecosystem rather than isolated layers. This architectural unification results in a network that is both self-monitoring and self-optimizing, capable of reacting instantly to traffic surges, controller faults, or security anomalies without relying on centralized intervention.

Beyond immediate performance benefits, the framework illustrates a generalizable paradigm for next-generation SD-IoT: leveraging programmable hardware and lightweight coordination protocols to achieve scalable, context-aware, and secure control. The hardware feasibility and complexity analysis (Sect. “Hardware Feasibility, algorithmic Complexity, and deployment considerations”) confirm that these principles are practically deployable on commodity P4 hardware with modest resource footprints, suggesting near-term readiness for industrial IoT and edge-cloud integration scenarios.

In summary, the key contribution of MC-LBTO lies in demonstrating that tightly integrated, data-driven coordination across planes can achieve sustainable gains in adaptability, reliability, and security. Future extensions will explore energy-aware optimization, inter-domain federation, and predictive AI-assisted orchestration—continuing the trajectory toward autonomous, resilient, and intelligence-driven SD-IoT infrastructures.
