Compressive Coronary Hematoma Treated With Stenting
Authors: Kaïs Ouerghi, Clément Fabre, Tahar Lazizi, Victor Mateus, Alain Furber, Serge Willoteaux, Loïc Bière, Fabrice Prunier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSpontaneous coronary artery dissection (SCAD) may require revascularization when severe ischemia results from compressive intramural hematoma.Case SummaryA 38-year-old woman presented with anterior ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction caused by a long compressive intramural hematoma of the left anterior descending artery. Optical coherence tomography (OCT) demonstrated a 41-mm Type 2a SCAD with severe true lumen compression (minimal lumen area 0.6 mm2). Percutaneous coronary intervention with a total stented length of approximately 60 mm restored TIMI flow grade 3. At 1 month, OCT showed proximal stent malapposition measuring 1.2 mm in depth over a 12-mm segment with positive vessel remodeling (minimal lumen area 7.41 mm2; diameter 3.06 mm). At 2 months, repeat OCT demonstrated partial spontaneous correction (600 μm over 8 mm), followed by OCT-guided postdilation restoring full stent apposition. Two years later, a Type 1 left main SCAD required stenting. Multimodality imaging revealed fibromuscular dysplasia. At 9-year follow-up, no recurrent coronary events occurred and left ventricular ejection fraction improved to 60%.DiscussionSerial OCT demonstrates dynamic vessel remodeling after compressive SCAD and highlights the value of imaging-guided management when long-segment stenting is required.

Spontaneous coronary artery dissection (SCAD) is an increasingly recognized cause of acute coronary syndrome in young women without traditional cardiovascular risk factors. Conservative management is recommended when coronary flow is preserved, but revascularization may be required when intramural hematoma causes severe true lumen compression and myocardial ischemia. The long-term vascular consequences of long-segment stenting in this setting remain poorly described. This case illustrates the dynamic healing process following long metallic scaffolding for compressive SCAD over 9 years of follow-up.

Case Presentation

A 38-year-old woman without cardiovascular risk factors presented with acute chest pain and 2-mm ST-segment elevation in leads V2 to V4. Transthoracic echocardiography showed a left ventricular ejection fraction of 50% with apical hypokinesia.

Coronary angiography demonstrated a long, severe, filiform narrowing of the mid and distal left anterior descending artery (LAD) with occlusion of the second diagonal branch (Video 1). The circumflex and right coronary arteries were normal. A conservative strategy was initially adopted, given suspicion of spontaneous compressive intramural hematoma. A myocardial bridging segment was identified proximal to the dissected LAD, potentially contributing to local mechanical stress.

Six hours later, recurrent chest pain with 12-mm ST-segment elevation (Figure 1A) prompted urgent repeat angiography, which demonstrated a filiform narrowing of the mid-LAD with preserved TIMI flow grade 3, suggestive of a compressive intramural hematoma (Figure 1B, Video 1). Optical coherence tomography (OCT) confirmed a 41-mm intramural hematoma consistent with Type 2a SCAD according to the European Society of Cardiology position paper1 causing severe true lumen compression, with a minimal lumen area of 0.6 mm2 and a 360° arc of compression (proximal minimal lumen area [MLA] 0.6 mm2; mid-MLA 0.6 mm2; distal MLA 0.7 mm2) (Figures 1C–1E, Video 2). Percutaneous coronary intervention was performed with implantation of a 2.75 × 38-mm XIENCE ALPINE drug-eluting stent (Abbott) covering the main compressed segment of the LAD. Due to proximal and distal propagation of the intramural hematoma, additional stents were implanted: a 2.75 × 12-mm XIENCE ALPINE stent distally and a 3.00 × 18-mm XIENCE ALPINE stent proximally, resulting in a total stented length of approximately 60 mm (as measured by OCT). Final angiography demonstrated TIMI flow grade 3 (Video 3, Figure 1F), and OCT confirmed satisfactory acute stent expansion with only limited incomplete apposition and persistent visualization of the intramural hematoma behind the stent struts (proximal segment: MLA 6.66 mm2, diameter 2.92 mm, stent apposition 126%; mid segment: MLA 6.29 mm2, diameter 2.83 mm; distal segment: MLA 6.31 mm2, diameter 2.83 mm) (Figures 1G to 1I, Video 4).Figure 1Acute Presentation, Intracoronary Imaging, Primary Percutaneous Coronary Intervention, and 1-Month Angiographic and OCT Follow-Up(A) Electrocardiogram showing >12-mm ST-segment elevation in leads V2 to V4 with reciprocal changes. At 1-month follow-up, systematic angiography (Figure 1J, Video 5) and OCT revealed proximal stent malapposition measuring 1.2 mm in depth and extending over approximately 12 mm, associated with marked positive vessel remodeling (proximal vessel diameter >4 mm; OCT measurements: MLA 7.41 mm2, diameter 3.06 mm, stent apposition 90%) (Figures 1K to 1M, Video 6). No postdilation was performed at 1 month because of the large proximal vessel diameter (∼4 mm), vascular fragility, and the potential risk of procedural complications. Dual antiplatelet therapy with aspirin and ticagrelor was maintained, and repeat angiographic and OCT evaluation was scheduled 1 month later to allow further hematoma resorption before considering postdilation.

At 2 months, repeat angiography showed preserved TIMI flow grade 3 (Figure 2A, Video 7). Ensuring that the guidewire re-entered the stented lumen was challenging because of proximal malapposition. The wire was advanced with a marked double J–shaped wire configuration within the proximal stent segment. OCT demonstrated persistent but reduced proximal stent malapposition measuring approximately 600 μm in depth and extending over about 8 mm (MLA 6.0 mm2, diameter 2.76 mm) with near-complete endothelialization (Figures 2B to 2D, Video 8). Correct intrastent positioning was confirmed by OCT before postdilation to avoid stent deformation. OCT-guided postdilation was then performed using a 4.0 × 12-mm EUPHORA balloon (Medtronic), achieving optimal stent expansion and complete apposition (postdilation OCT: MLA 7.38 mm2, diameter 3.06 mm, stent apposition 106%) (Figures 2E to 2H, Video 9).Figure 22-Month OCT-Guided Optimization and LAD Stent Assessment During the 2-Year Left Main Event(A) Repeat coronary angiography at 2 months showing preserved TIMI flow grade 3. In contrast to the initial LAD lesion, which was classified as Type 2a SCAD, the left main lesion was consistent with Type 1 SCAD.1

At 2-year follow-up, OCT assessment of the previously implanted LAD stents demonstrated good apposition with complete endothelialization (Video 11). In the proximal LAD–stented segment, MLA was 17.9 mm2 (diameter 4.77 mm), in the mid segment, MLA was 10.05 mm2 (diameter 3.58 mm), and in the distal segment, MLA was 6.62 mm2 (diameter 2.9 mm) (Figures 2J to 2L).

In this case, SCAD was the index clinical presentation that led to the subsequent diagnosis of systemic fibromuscular dysplasia.

Systematic vascular imaging performed during follow-up revealed bilateral arterial abnormalities involving both renal arteries and supra-aortic trunks, characterized by irregular arterial dilatations and dissections consistent with fibromuscular dysplasia (Figures 4A to 4F and 4H). During one hospitalization, computed tomography imaging performed for acute symptoms demonstrated diffuse arterial dissections involving renal and visceral arteries, which were managed conservatively.

At 9-year follow-up, no recurrent coronary events have occurred since LAD and left main stenting. Exercise echocardiography remains negative for inducible ischemia. Left ventricular ejection fraction improved to 60%, with only limited residual apical wall motion abnormality.

Discussion

SCAD represents an increasingly recognized cause of acute coronary syndrome, particularly in women without traditional cardiovascular risk factors.1, 2, 3 Current recommendations favor a conservative strategy whenever coronary flow is preserved because spontaneous healing occurs in most cases.3, 4, 5 However, revascularization becomes necessary when a compressive intramural hematoma causes flow-limiting myocardial ischemia.

In such situations, contemporary interventional strategies increasingly favor fenestration techniques using cutting or scoring balloons to decompress the hematoma while minimizing long metallic implantation in fragile vessels.5, 6, 7 Nevertheless, when the intramural hematoma is extensive and associated with near-complete lumen collapse—as in the present case with a 41-mm lesion and MLA of 0.6 mm2— stenting may be unavoidable to restore coronary flow. In our patient, proximal and distal propagation of the hematoma required total stented length of approximately 60 mm (as measured by OCT).

Intracoronary imaging played a central role in both procedural guidance and follow-up. OCT confirmed the diagnosis, guided stent sizing, and provided insight into the healing process. At 1 month, progressive hematoma resorption was associated with proximal stent malapposition extending over approximately 12 mm due to positive vessel remodeling. Serial OCT demonstrated progressive reduction in malapposition from 1.2 mm in depth over a 12-mm segment at 1 month to 600 μm over an 8-mm segment at 2 months. OCT-guided postdilation subsequently restored complete stent apposition, as confirmed by OCT at 2-year follow-up.

These findings highlight that acquired stent malapposition after SCAD healing should not necessarily be interpreted as procedural failure but rather as a manifestation of vessel remodeling following intramural hematoma resorption. Serial intracoronary imaging is therefore particularly valuable when long-segment stenting is required.

Furthermore, the presence of myocardial bridging proximal to the LAD SCAD provides additional pathophysiological insight. Although systemic fibromuscular dysplasia was the main predisposing factor in this patient, the bridge may have acted as a local trigger. Cyclic compression and altered shear stress at the transition between the intramyocardial and epicardial LAD segments may have contributed to the development of SCAD.8

Multimodality imaging also allowed identification of systemic fibromuscular dysplasia involving renal and supra-aortic arteries, a condition strongly associated with SCAD.9,10 Over long-term follow-up, no recurrent coronary events occurred after LAD and left main stenting, suggesting that long-segment stenting may provide durable coronary stabilization in selected high-risk presentations of compressive SCAD.

Conclusions

Long-segment stenting for compressive SCAD may be justified in patients with ongoing myocardial ischemia caused by a flow-limiting intramural hematoma. Serial intracoronary imaging is essential to understand vessel remodeling and guide reintervention.
