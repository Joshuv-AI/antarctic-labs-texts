Association between body composition, knee synovial effusion, and serum glutamate in middle-aged women with knee osteoarthritis: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Kyota Ishibashi, Eiji Sasaki, Ryo Tomita, Hikaru Kristi Ishibashi, Yukiko Sakamoto, Yuka Kimura, Eiichi Tsuda, Mizuri Ishida, Yasuyuki Ishibashi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This cross-sectional study investigated the association between knee synovial effusion, body composition, and serum glutamate levels in middle-aged women with knee osteoarthritis. Female participants (n = 241) from a health promotion project were classified into control (n = 165), early (n = 34), and definitive (n = 42) knee osteoarthritis groups. Synovial effusion was assessed based on the ultrasonographic effusion area, body composition via bioelectrical impedance, and serum glutamate from fasting blood samples. Muscle mass was higher in the definitive group than in the control (p = 0.009). Fat mass increased from normal to definitive, showing a significant difference between the control and definitive groups (p = 0.047). The effusion area correlated with muscle mass (r = -0.122, p < 0.05) and glutamate levels (r = 0.154, p < 0.001). Logistic regression identified fat mass as an associated factor of early (OR = 1.089, p = 0.004) and definitive (odds ratio = 1.063, p = 0.005) knee osteoarthritis, suggesting a potential association between adiposity and knee osteoarthritis. A weak correlation was observed between serum glutamate and synovial effusion. These findings underscore the importance of body composition optimization as a potential approach in the context of knee osteoarthritis management.

Introduction

Knee osteoarthritis (KOA) is a common degenerative joint disorder characterized by cartilage degradation, synovial inflammation, and structural changes1–3. Synovitis is pivotal in the onset and progression of KOA during the early phase4,5. Radiographic structural changes in KOA are irreversible and typically manifest during advanced stages. Recently, early KOA (EKOA), the stage before radiographic abnormalities become evident, has garnered interest as a target for preventing KOA progression6,7.

Body composition, including fat and muscle mass, significantly influences the development and progression of KOA8. Muscle is essential for maintaining joint stability and absorbing mechanical stress during movement, whereas excess fat, particularly visceral fat, is associated with systemic inflammation and the release of proinflammatory cytokines5,9,10. These cytokines may exacerbate synovial inflammation, thereby accelerating the degenerative processes in KOA. However, the precise relationship between synovitis severity and body composition has not been fully elucidated.

Glutamic acid (glutamate), a key excitatory amino acid, has been implicated in the pathophysiology of KOA11, with elevated levels in synovial fluid associated with inflammation and cartilage degradation in patients with arthritis12,13. However, studies examining systemic glutamate levels in relation to KOA remain scarce. As serum glutamate may reflect broader metabolic and inflammatory states, it may serve as an exploratory surrogate indicator of glutamatergic activity, although it should be acknowledged that serum levels may not directly reflect intra-articular conditions. Given this gap in the literature, this study explored the potential association between serum glutamate levels and synovial effusion as a preliminary investigation into the systemic dimension of glutamate biology in KOA.

This study aimed to investigate the association between knee synovial effusion, body composition, and serum glutamate levels in patients with KOA. Additionally, we examined differences across age groups in fat and muscle masses in middle-aged women. Understanding these associations may offer preliminary insights into potential approaches for managing KOA, such as body composition optimization through muscle-strengthening exercises, weight management, and nutritional support, although longitudinal studies are needed to confirm these implications.

Results

The participants (n = 241) were categorized into control (n = 165), EKOA (n = 34), and definitive KOA (DKOA) (n = 42) groups (Table 1). Body mass index (BMI) was significantly higher in the DKOA group than in the control group (23.7 ± 3.7 vs. 21.8 ± 3.1 kg/m², p = 0.005). The effusion area was significantly larger in both EKOA and DKOA groups than in the control group (EKOA: 70.2 ± 73.6 mm²; DKOA: 92.1 ± 76.1 mm²; control: 43.7 ± 24.9 mm², p < 0.001). Muscle mass was significantly higher in the DKOA group compared to the control group (37.5 ± 2.8 kg vs. 36.1 ± 3.1 kg, p = 0.009). Fat mass exhibited a gradual increase across groups from control to DKOA, with a statistically significant difference identified between the control and DKOA groups (16.2 ± 5.8 kg vs. 19.5 ± 6.5 kg, p = 0.047).

In terms of differences across age groups, muscle mass declined with increasing age across all groups, while fat mass exhibited variable trends across age groups. Significantly higher fat mass was observed in the EKOA group in their 40 s and in the DKOA groups in their 50 s and 60 s compared to age-matched control groups. Notably, the highest fat mass was observed in the EKOA group among participants in their 40 s (Fig. 1).

The effusion area (per 10 mm2) was also significantly associated with the presence of both EKOA (OR = 1.148, p = 0.033) and DKOA (OR = 1.177, p = 0.010). However, muscle mass was not significantly associated with either condition. Serum glutamate levels were not independently associated with either EKOA or DKOA (Table 3).

Discussion

The principal findings of this study indicate that increased fat mass is significantly associated with classification as EKOA or DKOA, whereas muscle mass and serum glutamate levels do not exhibit independent associations with either condition. Additionally, knee synovial effusion demonstrated a negative correlation with muscle mass. These results suggest a potential interplay among adiposity, metabolic factors, and KOA.

The association between high BMI and KOA is well-documented in genetic, epidemiological, and mechanistic studies14–16. Excess fat mass contributes to OA via mechanical loading and systemic inflammation17. Adipose tissue secretes proinflammatory cytokines and adipokines, which accelerate cartilage degradation and synovial inflammation, while increased body weight exacerbates joint stress, particularly during weight-bearing activities9,17,18. In our study, although fat mass was significantly associated with KOA, no significant correlation with effusion area was identified. This suggests that joint effusion, an indicator of synovial inflammation, may be influenced by factors other than adiposity.

Reduced muscle strength, particularly knee extensor strength19, has been reported as associated with increased risk of symptomatic OA and joint space narrowing, underscoring the importance of neuromuscular function rather than absolute muscle mass alone20,21. In the present study, muscle mass was higher in the DKOA group compared to the control group. This may reflect the influence of overall body size and fat mass on absolute muscle mass measurements, as evidenced by the moderate positive correlation between fat mass and muscle mass (r = 0.447, p < 0.001). Although a weak negative correlation was observed between muscle mass and synovial effusion area (r = −0.122), this association was modest and should be interpreted with caution, as it may not reflect a true functional muscle capacity such as strength or neuromuscular control. Future studies incorporating muscle strength measurements, such as knee extensor strength, would provide more meaningful insights into the relationship between neuromuscular function and synovial effusion in KOA.

While elevated glutamate levels in synovial fluid are associated with inflammation and cartilage degradation via intra-articular signaling pathways13, it is noteworthy that serum glutamate represents a distinct biological compartment and may not directly reflect intra-articular glutamatergic activity. In this study, no significant association was observed between serum glutamate levels and the presence of EKOA or DKOA. Although a weak positive correlation was observed between serum glutamate levels and synovial effusion area (r = 0.154), this finding should be interpreted with caution given its modest effect size. These results may nonetheless suggest a potential systemic metabolic dimension to glutamate biology in KOA, warranting further investigation with joint-specific measurements such as synovial fluid analysis.

This study has some limitations. First, its cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and longitudinal studies are required to establish temporal relationships. Additionally, the sample size of this study was relatively small, and the adjustment for potential confoundin.

g factors in the multivariable analyses was insufficient. In particular, physical activity level was not included in the logistic regression model, as comprehensive physical activity data were not systematically collected in the current dataset. This limitation may reduce the reliability and robustness of the observed associations. Future studies with larger sample sizes and more comprehensive assessment of confounding variables, including physical activity level, metabolic comorbidities, and menopausal status, are warranted to confirm and extend the present findings. Second, the study focused solely on middle-aged women. This decision was based on the well-established epidemiological evidence that EKOA is disproportionately prevalent in this demographic, as demonstrated in our previous large-scale cohort study14. Furthermore, given the known mechanisms influencing the sex differences in KOA pathophysiology, including hormonal influences, body composition characteristics, and biomechanical factors, we considered it methodologically appropriate to analyze this population separately. Nevertheless, this approach limits the generalizability of our findings to men and other age groups. Future studies including both sexes and employing sex-stratified analyses are warranted to determine whether the associations observed in the present study are sex-specific or more broadly applicable. Third, the analyses in this study were performed using a person-based approach, as KOA severity was classified based on the more severely affected knee, and serum glutamate levels represent a systemic measurement that cannot be attributed to a specific joint. Future studies incorporating joint-specific imaging and biomarkers may enable joint-based analyses, which could provide more granular insights into the relationship between local synovial effusion and joint-level pathology. Fourth, important potential confounders, including physical activity level, metabolic comorbidities, and menopausal status, were not included in the multivariable model, as these data were not systematically available in the present dataset. Additionally, while EKOA and DKOA were analyzed as separate outcomes to reflect their distinct diagnostic criteria, the generalizability of these models may be limited by the relatively small sample sizes within each subgroup. Fifth, although sensitivity analyses adjusting for KL grade were considered, this approach was deemed inappropriate given that KL grade constitutes a defining criterion for both EKOA and DKOA classification. Including KL grade as a covariate in models where these conditions are outcomes would introduce circularity into the analysis. Finally, serum glutamate levels were measured as a proxy for joint-specific glutamate activity, which may not fully reflect intraarticular changes.

While the association between adiposity and KOA has been extensively documented, the present study extends existing knowledge by simultaneously evaluating body composition, synovial effusion, and serum glutamate levels in middle-aged women across the full spectrum of KOA severity, including the early stage. Notably, the association between fat mass and serum glutamate levels observed in this study suggests a potential metabolic link between adiposity and synovial inflammation. This may offer additional insights into the pathophysiology of KOA beyond mechanisms reported in prior studies focusing solely on mechanical loading or systemic inflammation. Interventions targeting reduction of adiposity, such as lifestyle modifications, dietary management, and physical activity may help mitigate joint inflammation and delay OA progression. Longitudinal studies are required to determine whether changes in body composition influence the onset and progression of EKOA and DKOA.

This study demonstrated that fat mass was significantly associated with the presence of EKOA and DKOA, suggesting that adiposity may be significantly associated with KOA. The weak correlation between serum glutamate levels and synovial effusion highlights a potential metabolic link between adiposity and joint inflammation, which warrants further investigation.

These findings suggest that body composition, particularly fat mass, may be an important associated factor of KOA. However, given the cross-sectional study design, these results should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Longitudinal studies are needed to determine whether targeted modifications in body composition translate into meaningful clinical benefits in KOA management.

Methods

Participants

All participants were volunteers from a health promotion project, a community-based preventive medicine initiative aimed at improving life expectancy through general health checkups and proactive interventions, as previously described22–25. The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki (1964) and its subsequent amendments, and was approved by the ethics committee of our institution (approval number: 2016-028). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrollment.

This study analyzed data from the health promotion project cohort. Participants completed comprehensive questionnaires addressing their medical history, lifestyle habits, occupation, family history, health-related quality of life, and disease-specific information. Our analysis focused on middle-aged women (50–70 years old) because EKOA is particularly prevalent in this demographic14. Of the 1,148 participants, we excluded 459 men, 62 individuals with incomplete ultrasonography data, 11 with missing demographic information, seven with incomplete radiographic data, one without Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (KOOS) responses, 14 diagnosed with rheumatic disease, and seven who underwent knee surgery. After applying these exclusion criteria, 241 middle-aged women were included in the final analysis (Fig. 2).

Assessment of KOA

Participants underwent radiographic examinations of both knees in a weight-bearing, full extension, anteroposterior view with footmap positioning using a CXDI-40EG (Canon Inc., Tokyo, Japan). The beam was aligned parallel to the floor and targeted at the joint space. Sequencing was set at 60 kV, 50 mA, and 80 ms for all participants. DKOA was defined as having a KL grade ≥ 226. Two orthopedic surgeons evaluated all joints, and any discrepancies were resolved through mutual consultation until consensus was reached.

EKOA was diagnosed based on the classification criteria proposed by Luyten et al.27, which include a KOOS28≤ 85% in at least two of the following subscales: pain (nine items), symptoms (seven items), activities of daily living (short version, seven items), and knee-related quality of life (four items); clinical examination revealing at least one of the following findings: joint line tenderness or knee crepitus; and radiographic evaluation with KL grades of 0 or 1 in standing, fixed-flexion, and weight-bearing positions. Participants were classified as control, EKOA, or DKOA based on the presence and severity of KOA.

Assessment of synovial effusion using ultrasonography

Synovitis was evaluated by measuring the total suprapatellar effusion area in both knees using ultrasonography, as previously described4,29. Participants were examined while in the supine position with both knees semi-flexed and feet in a neutral position. The semi-flexed position was achieved by placing a pillow in both popliteal regions. Longitudinal ultrasound scans of the suprapatellar region were obtained using a 12 MHz linear transducer (Viamo™, Toshiba Medical Systems Corp., Otawara, Japan), carefully positioned at the center and proximal poles of the patella. The echo-free space in the suprapatellar region was delineated and automatically calculated to determine the effusion area (mm²). The total effusion areas of both knees were calculated. This study focused solely on the echo-free space and did not evaluate the hypertrophic synovial soft tissue. All ultrasound images were traced by an orthopedic surgeon with 10 years’ experience in musculoskeletal ultrasonography. To ensure reliability, 50 randomly selected ultrasonography images were independently traced and measured by another orthopedic surgeon at separate time points. The intra- and inter-observer reliabilities were excellent, with values of 0.99 (95% CI: 0.987–0.996) and 0.94 (95% CI: 0.875–0.976), respectively.

Assessment of body composition

Muscle and fat masses were assessed using bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) with the Body Composition Analyzer MC-190 (Tanita Corp., Tokyo, Japan)30. This device measures whole-body impedance by applying electrical currents at frequencies of 5, 50, 250, and 500 kHz through an ipsilateral foot-to-hand pathway. Participants were instructed to adhere to standardized BIA measurement protocols, which included (1) fasting for at least 4 h and avoiding alcohol for 8 h preceding measurement, (2) emptying the bladder beforehand, and (3) refraining from exercising for at least 8 h before testing.

Assessment of serum glutamate

Blood samples were obtained from all the participants in the early morning, before breakfast, following a fasting period of at least 10 h. The serum concentrations of glutamate (µmol/L) were subsequently measured.

Statistical analysis

Demographic data, effusion area, and serum glutamate levels were expressed as mean±standard deviation and compared using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Tukey’s post hoc test. Body composition across age groups were also evaluated using ANOVA and Tukey’s tests.

Spearman’s rank correlation was employed to assess the correlation between effusion area, body composition, and serum glutamate levels. To investigate the association between EKOA and DKOA occurrence, multiple logistic regression analysis was conducted including EKOA and DKOA occurrence as dependent variables, and age, effusion area, body composition, and serum glutamate level as independent variables. To assess multicollinearity among independent variables, variance inflation factors (VIFs) were calculated. All VIF values were < 2.0 (age: 1.11; effusion area: 1.07; fat mass: 1.94; muscle mass: 1.56; serum glutamate: 1.33), indicating that multicollinearity did not substantially influence the regression estimates. Separate logistic regression models were constructed for EKOA and DKOA as distinct outcome categories, reflecting their different diagnostic constructs within the KOA continuum. Data input and analysis were performed using SPSS Statistics for Windows (version 29.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.
