The silent normalization of biological exposures in operating rooms: an overlooked occupational crisis
Authors: Majid Bagheri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOperating room personnel are exposed to biological hazards such as needlestick injuries and blood and body fluid exposure, which pose risks of bloodborne infections. However, many of these incidents remain unreported. Underreporting is driven by normalization of exposure, fear of blame, workload, and limited knowledge of reporting procedures. This leads to underestimation of occupational risk and weakens prevention strategies. Improving reporting systems, education, and institutional support is essential to enhance occupational safety in perioperative settings.Keywords: Biological exposures, Operating room, Underreporting, Needlestick injury, Occupational health

Dear Editor,

Operating room (OR) personnel are continuously exposed to a range of biological occupational hazards, including sharps injuries, blood and body fluid exposure, and splashes of potentially infectious materials. These exposures are associated with significant risks of transmission of bloodborne pathogens such as hepatitis B virus, hepatitis C virus, and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV). Despite their clinical relevance, a substantial proportion of these incidents remain unreported in routine practice [1, 2].

Underreporting of biological exposures remains a persistent and under-recognized issue in perioperative settings. In many cases, nurses and students perceive such incidents as an inevitable and routine component of surgical care, leading to their normalization and lack of formal reporting. Contributing factors include fear of blame, insufficient knowledge of reporting procedures, time constraints, perceived futility of reporting, and inadequate institutional feedback. Previous studies have also shown that needlestick injuries and blood exposure incidents remain common among healthcare workers, particularly in surgical environments [3, 4].

Beyond individual determinants, a broader cultural issue appears to be involved: the normalization of biological risk in everyday clinical practice. Clinical observations suggest that exposure incidents are sometimes not followed by appropriate reporting or occupational health evaluation. In some cases, healthcare personnel may continue their duties even after direct blood exposure without seeking formal assessment. Similar behaviors may be observed among students experiencing sharps injuries, even when their immunological protection (e.g., hepatitis B antibody titers) is insufficient. Such patterns may reflect a growing desensitization to occupational hazards in clinical environments.

The consequences of underreporting extend beyond individual health risks. Failure to report biological exposures leads to underestimation of the true burden of occupational hazards, resulting in incomplete surveillance data and potentially ineffective preventive strategies. Moreover, it may delay timely post-exposure interventions and compromise occupational safety programs. Evidence suggests that measurement error and under-ascertainment of exposure events can substantially bias epidemiological estimates of occupational risk [5].

An additional concern is the historical lack of standardized, psychometrically validated instruments for assessing biological exposures among operating room personnel. This gap itself may reflect a broader underestimation of the importance of systematically measuring such risks. Recently, efforts have been made to develop and validate tools for assessing biological hazard exposure among operating room nurses, which may contribute to more accurate identification and monitoring of occupational risks [6].

In conclusion, the silent normalization and underreporting of biological exposures in operating rooms may obscure the true magnitude of occupational risk and undermine preventive efforts. Without accurate reporting and systematic monitoring, the real burden of biological hazards will remain underestimated.
