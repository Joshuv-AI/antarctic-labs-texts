Folic acid attenuates maternal sertraline-induced hepatic injury in rat offspring
Authors: Ayman A Refai, Mohammad I Jumaa, Safaa M Hanafy, Ahmed Shaban Abdel Monsef, Motaz Talaat Elghnam, Mahmoud Elodemi, Mohamed Rafat Elkabary, Einas M Yousef
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSertraline, a selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor, is commonly prescribed during pregnancy. While its adverse effects on pregnant mothers are documented, its impact on fetal organ development, particularly the liver, is less understood. Folic acid is a standard prenatal supplement, possessing antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anti-apoptotic effects. This study aimed to investigate the effects of maternal exposure to sertraline during pregnancy alone, as well as during both pregnancy and lactation, on the offspring liver and to investigate the potential role of concurrent folic acid supplementation. Pregnant Sprague-Dawley albino rats were divided into four groups: Control, Folic acid-supplemented, Sertraline-treated, and Sertraline + folic acid-treated. Treatments were administered to dams by oral gavage once daily during pregnancy alone, as well as during both pregnancy and lactation. Male offspring were sacrificed at birth (0-Day) and 14 days postpartum (14-Day). Offspring livers were processed for histopathological (H&E and Masson trichrome staining), immunohistochemical evaluation of cleaved Caspase-3 expression, and morphometric analysis. Offspring from sertraline-treated dams (Sertraline 0-Day, prenatal exposure only) exhibited marked hepatocellular vacuolation, vascular congestion, and inflammatory infiltration in portal areas. In the sertraline 14-Day group (combined prenatal and lactational exposure), partial structural recovery was observed; however, liver damage persisted but was less severe, showing diffuse vacuolation. Strong cleaved Caspase-3 immunoreactivity was observed in the sertraline-treated group, indicating increased apoptosis. Conversely, offspring from the Sertraline+ folic acid groups (0-Day and 14-Day) showed apparently normal hepatic architecture at both time points, with minimal histopathological changes and significantly weaker cleaved Caspase-3 expression, comparable to that of the control and folic acid groups. Maternal sertraline exposure during pregnancy, with or without continuation into early lactation, induces significant structural damage and increases apoptosis in the offspring’s liver. Concurrent maternal folic acid supplementation effectively mitigates these effects at both time points, although the extent of protection may vary depending on the exposure window (prenatal vs. combined prenatal and lactational exposure). Introduction

Anxiety and mood disorders are common during pregnancy, affecting approximately one in every five pregnant women, with an estimated prevalence of perinatal depression reaching 26.3%1,2. These disorders can have a profound impact on both maternal well-being and fetal development2. Many therapeutic approaches are implemented, including the use of some drugs to prevent or at least mitigate these associated consequences.

Selective Serotonin Reuptake Inhibitors (SSRIs) were the most commonly used antidepressant during pregnancy, with an international prevalence estimate of 3.01%3. Sertraline, a widely prescribed SSRI, is commonly used for major depression, anxiety disorders, and several other psychiatric conditions, and has a relatively good safety profile4–6. However, increasing evidence demonstrates that sertraline crosses the placental barrier in moderate amounts, leading to fetal exposure to this medication7. Sertraline is primarily metabolized in the liver via the cytochrome P450 system, and its metabolism may generate reactive intermediates that can trigger hepatocellular injury8,9, evidenced by elevated liver enzymes, histopathological alterations, and apoptosis10,11. These effects are largely mediated through oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and inflammatory signaling pathways, which play central roles in drug-induced liver injury. Pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6 stimulate pathways such as JAK/STAT, which exacerbate hepatocellular damage, whereas reduced anti-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-4 and IL-10) exacerbate inflammation12–14. In addition, drug-induced toxicity is frequently associated with oxidative stress and apoptosis, which are key mechanisms underlying cellular injury in various tissues15–17. Apoptosis plays a central role in drug-induced tissue injury and has been widely reported as a key mechanism in toxicological studies15,18. Therefore, understanding the role of apoptosis in sertraline-induced hepatic damage is essential, particularly in the context of fetal exposure. While previous experimental studies have investigated the effects of sertraline on maternal hepatic profile and histopathological patterns in animal models19, the effects of maternal sertraline administration on fetal liver structure and function remain poorly explored.

Given the central role of oxidative stress in drug-induced hepatotoxicity, enhancing endogenous antioxidant defenses has emerged as a potential strategy to reduce hepatocellular injury20. Folic acid is one of the essential vitamins (vitamin B9) that cannot be synthesized by the body, but must be obtained from the diet or supplementation21. Routine Folic acid supplementation is recommended before conception and in early pregnancy to support normal fetal development and prevent neural tube defects22. At the molecular level, folic acid is essential for DNA synthesis, repair, and methylation processes that are crucial for normal cellular proliferation and differentiation21. Folic acid has additional antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, including reducing homocysteine levels, enhancing glutathione synthesis, and inhibiting pro-inflammatory pathways such as NF-κB23–29. Recent studies have further reinforced the role of folate in modulating hepatic lipid metabolism and attenuating drug-induced liver injury30. Collectively, these mechanisms suggest that folic acid may serve as a safe, routinely administered, potential hepatoprotective agent.

Accordingly, we hypothesized that maternal exposure to sertraline during pregnancy induces significant histopathological and apoptotic alterations in the offspring livers, and that the concurrent folic acid supplementation mitigates these effects through its anti-inflammatory and anti-apoptotic actions. The current study was designed to investigate the effects of maternal sertraline administration on the fetal liver at the histopathological and molecular levels, with a specific focus on hepatocellular apoptosis. Moreover, we sought to evaluate the potential protective or mitigating effects of folic acid co-administration against the sertraline-induced hepatic alterations.

Materials and methods

Drug preparation and dosing

Sertraline hydrochloride tablets (Zoloft®, Pfizer) and folic acid tablets (Nile Pharmaceutical Company) used in this study were purchased from a commercial source. For experimental use, sertraline was dissolved in distilled water and administered at a dose of 3.6 mg/kg/day via gavage in a volume of 0.5 ml. This dose is calculated using standard body surface area conversion to be equivalent to a human therapeutic dose of 50 mg/day for an average human body (with a body weight of 60 kg and body surface area of 1.62m231,32. Folic acid was similarly dissolved in distilled water and administered at a dose of 0.3 mg/kg/day via gavage in a volume of 0.58 ml. This was also equivalent to the recommended dose for a pregnant human patient receiving antidepressant drugs (4 mg/day)19,31,33.

Ethical approval

This study was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Faculty of Medicine, Menoufia University, Egypt (Approval No: MUFHE/F/NFS/51/25). All procedures followed the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Animals and experimental design

Mature male and female Sprague-Dawley Albino rats (3–4 months old, weighing 200–250 g.) were obtained from the Experimental Animal House, Faculty of Medicine, Menoufia University, Egypt. The animals were housed under standard conditions (21 ± 2 °C, 50% ± 10% humidity, 12 h light/dark cycle) with free access to water and ad libitum standard rodent chow containing (14% crude protein, 2% fat, 15% fiber). According to information provided by the manufacturer, the diet was not fortified with added folic acid; background folate was derived only from the natural feed ingredients. Animals were maintained under strict hygienic conditions. Sterile wood shavings (5–6 cm depth) were used as bedding and were changed twice weekly, while PVC pipes were used for environmental enrichment. Animals were kept for one week before the start of the experiment for acclimatization. Pregnancy was confirmed by the presence of a vaginal plug, which was considered gestational day 0. Pregnant rats were randomly allocated into four main groups (10 dams per group)19:

Group I (Control)

Received 0.5–0.6 mL of distilled water using oral gavage daily.

Group II (Folic acid-treated)

Received folic acid (0.3 mg/kg/day) using oral gavage daily.

Group III (Sertraline-treated)

Received sertraline (3.6 mg/kg/day) using oral gavage daily.

Group IV (Sertraline + folic acid-treated)

Received both folic acid (0.3 mg/kg/day) and sertraline (3.6 mg/kg/day). Folic acid was administered 30 min before sertraline to allow adequate time for absorption and systemic availability, thereby enabling its potential protective effects at the time of sertraline exposure34.

The oral gavage volume was kept constant across all experimental groups. Treatments started on the first day of confirmed pregnancy (gestational day 0) and continued throughout pregnancy and the first 14 days postpartum. The dam (litter) was considered the experimental unit. A total of 10 pregnant dams (litters) were included per group, equally divided between the two time points, with five litters allocated to the 0-day subgroup (sacrificed immediately after birth) and five litters allocated to the 14-day subgroup (sacrificed at 14 days postpartum).To avoid litter-effect bias, one male offspring was randomly selected from each litter for subsequent analyses. Accordingly, each subgroup comprised offspring from independent litters (n = 5 per subgroup), thereby avoiding repeated sampling from the same litter. Only male offspring were included to minimize biological variability and avoid potential confounding effects associated with sex-related hormonal differences in hepatic development and drug metabolism35,36. Offspring sex was determined immediately after birth by anogenital distance and inspection of external genitalia, following standard neonatal sexing criteria in rats37. Throughout the study, no maternal mortality, pregnancy complications, or fetal resorptions were observed. All pregnant dams successfully completed gestation, and all litters were included in the analysis; therefore, no animals were excluded.

Sample size was determined based on Mead’s resource equation, a commonly used approach in experimental animal studies when reliable prior data from effect-size based power calculations are unavailable. This method helps balance study feasibility with the ethical principle of minimizing unnecessary animal use38. In addition, the selected sample size (n = 5 litters per subgroup) is comparable to previous similar experimental studies using similar histological analyses39.

At the end of the experiment (0-day or 14-day time points), thiopental sodium (40 mg/kg body weight) was injected intraperitoneally40. Animals were carefully monitored for depth of anesthesia, including loss of spontaneous movement, absence of response to tactile stimulation, and loss of reflex activity. Cervical dislocation was performed only after confirmation of adequate anesthesia and loss of consciousness. Livers were then collected from all offspring. All procedures were conducted by trained personnel in accordance with institutional guidelines and ARRIVE recommendations.

Histological study

The left lateral lobe of the liver of each offspring was fixed immediately in 10% neutral-buffered formalin for 24 to 48 h. The tissues were then dehydrated in an ascending series of alcohol, cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin blocks. Sections of 5 μm thickness were cut using a rotary microtome (Leica, Germany). Sections were stained with Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) according to the standard histological protocol41. Masson’s trichrome stain was used to assess collagen fiber deposition in hepatic tissue.

A slightly modified semiquantitative scoring approach, as described by Abdelkader et al., was used to evaluate the severity of hepatic tissue lesions across the experimental groups42. Two independent histopathologists, blinded to the experimental group, analyzed five high-power fields from each animal using a light microscope (Olympus BX53, Tokyo, Japan). Slides were coded prior to evaluation to ensure blinding. The assessed parameters were hepatoportal and sinusoidal congestion, cloudy swelling and hydropic degeneration, cellular necrosis, inflammatory cellular infiltrate, and cholestasis. A semiquantitative scoring system from 0 to 3 was used, where 0 indicates the absence of histopathological alterations, 1 + represents pathological changes in less than 20% of the examined fields, 2 + indicates histopathological changes in 20% to 60% of the assessed fields, and 3 + indicates histopathological changes in over 60% of the fields assessed. The score for each parameter was determined within each field, and the mean score across the five examined fields was calculated to obtain a single score per animal. The final score per animal was determined by the agreement between the two pathologists. When the scores differed by > 1 grade, a third blinded pathologist was consulted. Inter-observer agreement was assessed using Cohen’s kappa (κ) coefficient. The final group score represents the mean of individual animal scores derived from independent litters (n = 5 per subgroup), with each animal representing one litter.

Immunohistochemical study

Immunohistochemical staining was performed according to established protocols to assess apoptotic activity by detecting cleaved Caspase-3 expression, a key enzyme involved in apoptosis43. Paraffin-embedded tissue Sects. (4–5 μm thick) were deparaffinized and rehydrated. Antigen retrieval was performed using heat-induced epitope retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0). Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked by incubating the sections with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 10 min. The sections were then blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin and incubated overnight at 4 °C with a rabbit polyclonal anti-cleaved caspase-3 antibody (catalog number GB115733-100, Servicebio, Wuhan, China; 1:500 dilution, specific for cleaved caspase-3 expression). After washing, sections were incubated with a biotinylated secondary antibody (Vector Laboratories Inc, Burlingame, CA, USA) for 30 min, followed by streptavidin–horseradish peroxidase (Vector Laboratories Inc, California, USA). Immunoreactivity was visualized using 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) chromogen (Dako, Agilent Technologies Company, Denmark), which produced a brown color at sites of positive staining. Finally, the sections were counterstained with Mayer’s hematoxylin, dehydrated, cleared, and mounted. Positive control (rat lymph node) and negative control (omission of primary antibody) were included in each staining run to ensure the specificity and reliability of the results.

Morphometric analyses

Quantitative assessment of cleaved caspase-3 immunoreactivity was performed using ImageJ software (version 1.53, NIH, USA). For each animal, five non-overlapping high-power fields (HPF, 400x magnification) were randomly selected from comparable hepatic regions. Images were captured under identical illumination and acquisition settings. Fields were selected using systematic random sampling within comparable hepatic regions, ensuring consistent representation of lobular zones (including periportal and pericentral areas) across all experimental groups. Image analysis was performed in a blinded manner using coded images to minimize observer bias.

To quantify cleaved caspase-3-positive cells, images were first converted to 8-bit grayscale, followed by color deconvolution using the H DAB plugin to separate the DAB chromogen (brown staining) from hematoxylin (nuclear staining). The DAB channel was used to identify positively stained cells. A standardized threshold was applied to the DAB channel to detect positively stained cells. The number of positive cells was quantified using the Analyze Particles function with consistent size and circularity parameters applied across all samples. For total cell count, nuclei were identified from the hematoxylin channel using similar thresholding and particle analysis settings. The percentage of cleaved caspase-3–positive cells was calculated for each field as follows: % positive cells = (number of positive cells/total number of cells) ×100. The mean value from the five analyzed fields was calculated for each animal, and the average of animals within each litter was used to represent the experimental unit for statistical analysis. Data are presented as mean ± SD, with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) calculated to reflect estimate precision.

Additionally, quantitative assessment of collagen deposition in Masson’s trichrome-stained sections was conducted by measuring the percentage area of blue-stained collagen fibers relative to the total tissue area using ImageJ software. All images were captured under identical illumination, exposure time, and white balance settings to ensure consistency across all samples. Importantly, image analysis was performed in a blinded manner, with coded images to prevent observer bias. Color deconvolution (RGB channel separation) was used to enhance signal detection prior to thresholding. Collagen segmentation was performed using a standardized color threshold in ImageJ, determined from representative images and applied uniformly across all samples without further adjustment. The selected threshold was visually validated by two independent observers to ensure accurate detection of collagen fibers and exclusion of background or non-specific staining. Segmentation accuracy was further confirmed by comparing threshold images with the corresponding original images. Background subtraction was performed prior to analysis to minimize non-specific signal, and all processing parameters were kept constant throughout the analysis.

Statistical analysis

The collected data for Caspase-3% area were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), with the litter considered the statistical unit (n = 5 per subgroup), and each value representing one male offspring per litter. As the data were derived from independent litters, relatively low variability may be observed due to controlled experimental conditions. In addition to the mean ± SD, 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) were calculated to estimate the precision of the mean values. Normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was evaluated using Levene’s test prior to ANOVA. Differences between the four main offspring treatment groups (Control, Folic acid, Sertraline, Sertraline + Folic acid) at each time point were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s honest significant difference (HSD) post-hoc test for multiple comparisons. Post hoc multiple comparisons were performed separately for each time point. In addition, a two-way ANOVA test was used to assess the interaction between treatment and time point. Individual data points from independent litters were visualized as dot plots to illustrate the distribution and variability of the data. All statistical analyses were performed using the statistical package for the Social Science (SPSS) software (Version 22.0, IBM Corp., USA). A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Histological analysis (H&E staining)

Histological assessment of liver sections from offspring of different experimental groups revealed distinct morphological alterations that depended on the treatment administered to the mother and on postnatal age.

Histopathological alterations in offspring liver at birth (0-day)

In both control and folic acid-treated offspring, histological assessment demonstrated normal hepatic architecture. Hepatocytes were polygonal with centrally located, vesicular nuclei and appeared as irregular groups around the central veins. Portal tracts appeared well-defined with normal hepatic triads (Fig. 2A-B). In contrast, hepatic sections of offspring of dams treated with sertraline (0-day), demonstrated marked histopathological changes. Hepatic cords appeared disorganized, and many hepatocytes showed cytoplasmic rarefaction. Hepatocytes show marked cytoplasmic vacuolation and cellular swelling, indicating microvesicular steatosis and hydropic degeneration. Focal areas of hepatocyte necrosis were also observed, including nuclear changes in affected cells (pyknotic or absent nuclei in some cells). Moreover, central and portal veins appeared severely dilated and congested, indicating vascular disturbance (Figs. 2C-D; Table 1). Examination of portal areas showed marked mononuclear inflammatory cell infiltration, indicating acute hepatic injury (Figs. 2E-F; Table 1).

Histopathological alterations in offspring liver 14-day postpartum

Histological examination of liver sections from offspring euthanized at 14 days after birth demonstrated structural alterations dependent on the administered treatment (Fig. 4). The control and folic acid-treated groups showed normal hepatic architecture (Fig. 4A and B). In contrast, liver sections from offspring of sertraline-treated dams showed marked histopathological alterations. The hepatic cords were markedly disorganized, and many hepatocytes showed diffuse cytoplasmic vacuolation, pyknotic nuclei, and hydropic degeneration, indicating persistent hepatocellular injury. The portal and periportal regions demonstrated inflammatory cell infiltration, along with congestion and dilatation of blood vessels (Fig. 4C–F; Table 1). These findings indicate that maternal exposure to sertraline during pregnancy induces sustained hepatic injury in offspring that persists into the postnatal period. Inter-rater agreement analysis was excellent (Cohen’s κ = 0.86).

In the sertraline and folic acid-treated group (14-day), a marked improvement in hepatic architecture was observed compared with the sertraline-treated group. The liver lobules were mostly normal, with hepatocytes arranged in regular cords around central veins (Fig. 5A, B). Most hepatocytes appeared normal, with polygonal shapes, vesicular nuclei, and slightly granular cytoplasm; only a few showed minimal cytoplasmic vacuolation. In the periportal regions, there was limited hepatocellular vacuolation, mild inflammatory cell infiltration, and slight congestion of blood vessels (Fig. 5C; Table 1). However, some small areas showed hepatocytes with cytoplasmic vacuolation (Fig. 5D). Overall, folic acid administered during pregnancy provided a sustained mitigating effect against sertraline-induced hepatocellular injury during the postnatal period.

Histological and morphometric analyses (Masson trichrome staining)

Masson’s trichrome staining demonstrated time-dependent alterations in hepatic collagen deposition among the experimental groups (Table 2). At postnatal time points (0-Day and 14-Day), both the control and folic acid groups exhibited minimal collagen fibers, confined to typical perivascular and portal regions (Figs. 6A and 7A). In contrast, the sertraline-treated group (0 day) demonstrated minimal collagen deposition in the perivascular regions (Fig. 6B), with representative sections showing more prominent staining around portal tracts and blood vessels than around central veins (Fig. 6C & D). This was also supported by the quantitative analysis, which demonstrated that collagen deposition was significantly elevated in the sertraline-treated group compared with the control and folic acid-treated groups (Table 2). Co-administration of folic acid with sertraline significantly mitigated these changes, resulting in lower collagen deposition compared to the sertraline-only group (Fig. 6E, F) and (Table 2). By the 14th postnatal day, collagen deposition remained elevated in the sertraline group, though to a lesser extent than at day 0, suggesting partial spontaneous remodeling (Fig. 7B-D). Offspring from the sertraline and folic acid group exhibited nearly normal collagen distribution, underscoring the protective effect of folic acid against sertraline-induced alteration in collagen deposition in the liver during the early postnatal period (Fig. 7E, F) and (Table 2). Two-way ANOVA revealed a highly significant main effect of treatment (F(3,32) = 645.12, p < 0.001) and a smaller but significant effect of timepoint (F(1,32) = 49.68, p < 0.001). In addition, a significant interaction between treatment and time point was observed (F(3,32) = 35.07, p < 0.001), indicating that the effect of treatment on collagen deposition varied across the two time points. Taken together, both qualitative and quantitative findings from Masson trichrome assessment confirm the protective effect of folic acid against sertraline-induced collagen accumulation in the liver during early postnatal development.

Discussion

The present study provides experimental evidence that maternal sertraline exposure during pregnancy was associated with structural alterations in the offspring liver. These changes included marked histopathological changes, increased collagen deposition, and enhanced apoptotic activity, as confirmed by increased cleaved caspase-3 expression. Moreover, concurrent administration of folic acid during pregnancy ± lactation mitigated sertraline-induced hepatic injury at both assessed postpartum time points (0-Day and 14-Day).

Histological examination of liver tissue from the sertraline-treated group demonstrated marked hepatocellular vacuolation, vascular congestion, especially at the portal areas, and inflammatory cellular infiltration. These results indicate hepatocellular stress and injury and are consistent with features of acute drug-induced liver injury44. The observed vascular congestion may be at least in part due to the disruption of endothelial structural integrity. Damage to the endothelial glycocalyx, which plays a key role in maintaining vascular permeability and mechanotransduction, can lead to increased vascular leakage, fluid extravasation, and enhanced inflammatory responses, thereby contributing to vascular congestion45.

The hepatotoxic effects of sertraline are likely mediated through multiple interconnected mechanisms. Previous studies have demonstrated that sertraline induces mitochondrial dysfunction, including uncoupling of oxidative phosphorylation, inhibition of respiratory chain complexes (I and V), and rapid ATP depletion in hepatocytes, leading to loss of membrane integrity and cell death46. In addition, sertraline has been shown to modulate inflammatory signaling pathways, such as p38 MAPK activation and PI3K inhibition, resulting in altered cytokine production and impaired cell survival47. These molecular alterations contribute to oxidative stress and inflammatory injury, which are well-established drivers of hepatocellular damage. Similar mechanisms have been reported in experimental models of chemically induced liver injury, where oxidative stress, lipid peroxidation, and dysregulation of signaling pathways, including PI3K/AKT, Ras-MAPK, and HIF-1, collectively contribute to hepatic injury48.

Beyond these mechanisms, the heightened vulnerability of the developing liver may also be attributed to immature detoxification pathways. These findings can be explained by immature detoxification pathways and reduced antioxidant capacity during fetal and early neonatal life49. In addition, potential pharmacokinetic differences between maternal and neonatal sertraline metabolism may significantly influence fetal and neonatal drug exposure. In pregnant women, sertraline is extensively metabolized by hepatic cytochrome P450 enzymes4, the activity of which may be altered during pregnancy, thereby affecting drug disposition50. Reduced plasma protein levels, including albumin and α1-acid glycoprotein, may also increase the free fraction of the drug5,51. Although sertraline crosses the placenta, fetal concentrations are generally lower than maternal levels13,51. In contrast, neonates exhibit immature hepatic enzyme systems with reduced metabolic capacity, which can decrease drug clearance and prolong half-life4. Furthermore, differences in neonatal plasma protein profiles, including lower α1-acid glycoprotein levels, affect drug distribution and elimination5,13. These developmental differences may predispose neonates to increased drug accumulation and greater susceptibility to adverse hepatic effects. Additionally, hepatocellular vacuolation is a classic sign of acute metabolic insult and is associated with lipid accumulation due to mitochondrial dysfunction induced by sertraline and subsequent ATP depletion44,46. Furthermore, the detected inflammatory infiltration reflects an active immune response to hepatocellular injury, which can be explained by the fact that sertraline-induced liver injury involves cytokine-mediated inflammation44,52.

Notably, the liver tissue from offspring exposed to intrauterine sertraline showed partial spontaneous recovery 14 days after delivery. This recovery may be attributed to the remarkable regenerative capacity of the rodent liver44,53, as well as to the cessation of transplacental sertraline exposure and to sertraline clearance via hepatic metabolism9. However, despite this partial recovery, residual hepatocellular vacuolation and inflammation were also detected at this time point (14-Day). This might indicate that more time is needed for complete hepatic recovery or that early-life sertraline exposure may have long-lasting consequences on liver structure and function.

Masson’s trichrome staining demonstrated significantly increased collagen deposition in the offspring of the sertraline-treated groups, with representative sections showing prominence around portal tracts. However, formal zonal quantification was not performed, and this distribution should therefore be interpreted cautiously. These results are consistent with those of Almansouri et al., who reported increased collagen fiber deposition in the portal area following chronic sertraline exposure54. These findings suggest an early increase in collagen fiber deposition in the offspring livers, possibly secondary to inflammation- and oxidative stress-related extracellular matrix remodeling55. Differences in local vascular supply and microenvironment may contribute to this pattern of collagen deposition56. Interestingly, despite reduced collagen deposition in the offspring of the sertraline-treated groups (14-Day), it remained significantly elevated compared to the control group. This indicates that a longer recovery period may be required for complete hepatic restoration.

In addition to structural and increased collagen deposition, programmed cell death also plays a critical role in sertraline-induced hepatic injury. Apoptosis has been widely recognized as a fundamental mechanism underlying toxicant-induced cellular injury across different tissues, including reproductive and hepatic systems57,58. In the current study, a significant increase in cleaved caspase-3 expression was observed in hepatocytes of offspring of sertraline-treated dams compared to the control and folic acid-treated groups, confirming enhanced apoptotic activity in sertraline-induced hepatic injury. Hepatic injury is mediated by multiple programmed cell death pathways, including apoptosis, autophagy, and emerging mechanisms such as pyroptosis. While apoptosis is a classical and relatively non-inflammatory process, pyroptosis involves caspase-mediated gasdermin activation, leading to membrane pore formation and release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, thereby exacerbating tissue damage. In addition, ferroptosis is a glutathione-dependent process characterized by lipid peroxide accumulation and iron-dependent membrane damage, further exacerbating oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction59,60.

The observed increase in cleaved caspase-3 immunoreactivity is consistent with a previous study, which reported that sertraline-induced apoptosis in hepatic cells through the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway11. Mechanistically, caspase-3 activation is driven by oxidative stress-induced mitochondrial dysfunction. Excess ROS disrupts mitochondrial integrity, leading to loss of membrane potential, opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP), and release of cytochrome c, which subsequently activates the apoptotic cascade. This process is amplified by mitochondrial calcium overload, leading to enhanced hepatocellular injury61. Other in vivo and in vitro studies proposed additional mechanisms of sertraline-induced apoptosis in hepatic tissue, including mitochondrial dysfunction, endoplasmic reticulum stress, and DNA damage10,46,62. Notably, cleaved caspase-3 immunoreactivity was highest at birth (0-Day), and showed a partial decrease in its expression by day 14 postpartum. These results highlight a delayed recovery process, which can be explained by the persistence of sertraline in the offspring blood through lactation or its incomplete clearance due to the immature detoxification capacity of the neonatal liver63,64.

A comparison with previous studies in adult liver models highlights both similarities and key differences in sertraline-induced hepatotoxicity. The present findings demonstrate that sertraline was associated with hepatotoxic changes in developing liver tissue through mechanisms similar to those reported in adult models, including oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, inflammation, and apoptosis46,47. However, the developing liver appears more vulnerable, likely due to its immature metabolic capacity and reduced antioxidant defenses, resulting in more pronounced injury and delayed recovery49. In contrast to adult studies, where fibrosis is commonly reported after chronic exposure, increased collagen deposition was observed early in the current study. This may suggest increased susceptibility of the developing liver to extracellular matrix remodeling following exposure54,55. Furthermore, apoptotic activity (based on cleaved caspase-3 expression) was more prominent at early stages, indicating increased sensitivity of developing hepatocytes10,46,62.

Given these mechanisms of injury and increased vulnerability, the potential protective role of folic acid becomes relevant. In this study, co-administration of folic acid with sertraline during pregnancy ± lactation was associated with improved hepatic architecture, reduced collagen deposition, reduced inflammation, and suppressed cleaved caspase-3 expression in hepatocytes. These protective effects were observed at both time points, with greater improvement noted at 14 days postpartum. This pattern indicates that folic acid may reduce the severity of sertraline-induced hepatic injury not only at birth but also throughout early postnatal life. This finding can be attributed to the transfer of folic acid into breast milk during lactation, potentially prolonging its protective effect in early neonatal life65. Our findings are in agreement with our previous study, which demonstrated the beneficial effects of folic acid administration in the livers of mothers treated with sertraline19. Moreover, several human and animal studies have similarly reported the hepatoprotective effect of folic acid administration in different animal models66–68.

The mitigating effects of folic acid on hepatic alteration can be explained by several mechanisms. Folic acid exerts antioxidant effects by reducing homocysteine accumulation and enhancing glutathione synthesis, thereby limiting reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation69–71. It also modulates the NF-κB pathway, thereby decreasing pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6, which significantly contribute to hepatocyte apoptosis, as reflected by reduced cleaved caspase-3 expression in the present study30,47,70,72. This can also help explain the observed reduction in inflammatory infiltration and collagen fiber deposition in folic acid co-treated groups in this study. Furthermore, emerging evidence suggests that folic acid supplementation improves mitochondrial function and viability and inhibits mitochondrial cytochrome C release into the cytoplasm, both of which can help prevent hepatocyte apoptosis73–75. Folic acid is crucial for thymidine and purine synthesis, which are pivotal for competent DNA replication and repair, supporting cellular proliferation during liver development75,76. Recent research reported that folic acid supplementation can protect against liver injury by preserving normal methylation patterns of principal genes involved in metabolism (e.g., PPARα, CYP2E1) and apoptosis26,70,77. The beneficial effects of folic acid observed in this study are consistent with prenatal antioxidant models, where Docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) normalized glutathione-related gene expression in fetal liver under ethanol exposure78, and Resveratrol reduced oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation in fetal liver79. However, while these antioxidants primarily act via redox regulation, folic acid may provide broader beneficial effects by additionally suppressing apoptosis and supporting cellular proliferation.

From a clinical perspective, this study highlights the need for further investigation into whether routine folic acid supplementation may influence outcomes in sertraline-exposed pregnancies. Despite our valuable insights in the present study, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the evaluation of offspring was restricted to 14 days postpartum, limiting conclusions about long-term hepatic outcomes. Second, offspring examined on postnatal day 14 were exposed to sertraline via both transplacental and lactational routes, making it difficult to isolate the specific impact of postnatal (lactational) exposure. In addition, only single doses of sertraline and folic acid were tested, limiting dose-response analysis or optimal dosing. Moreover, biochemical markers of oxidative stress and inflammatory mediators were not assessed, which would have strengthened the mechanistic understanding of the observed histological and immunohistochemical changes. Another limitation is that only male offspring were studied. Consequently, our findings may not fully explore potential sex-specific differences in sertraline-induced hepatotoxicity or in the mitigating effects of folic acid. In addition, collagen deposition was assessed only by histological staining, without confirmation using additional molecular markers related to fibrogenesis, such as α-SMA or COL1A1. Finally, differences between rodents and humans in hepatic regenerative capacity, pharmacokinetics, and placental drug transfer limit direct clinical translation. Future studies should therefore extend the follow-up period into puberty and adulthood to assess the long-term structural and functional effects on the liver. Additionally, dose-response studies using multiple concentrations of sertraline and folic acid, together with molecular, biochemical, and functional analyses, would provide deeper mechanistic insight. Well-designed observational studies in humans are also needed to validate these findings and clarify the potential protective role of routine folic acid supplementation in sertraline-exposed pregnancies.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated histopathological and apoptotic alterations in the offspring liver following maternal sertraline exposure in rats. These changes were evident at birth, reflecting prenatal exposure, and persisted at 14 days postpartum, representing combined prenatal and lactational exposure to sertraline. Moreover, folic acid supplementation was associated with reduced histopathological and apoptotic alterations in this rat model, suggesting a possible mitigating role. However, the study is limited by its use of an animal model, short follow-up duration, and lack of detailed molecular and pharmacokinetic analyses. Future studies incorporating long-term evaluation, dose-response analysis, and clinical investigations are required to clarify the translational relevance of these findings in humans.
