Multimaterial 3D printed soft robots with embedded actuation and sensing
Authors: Zixiao Zhu, Dong Wang, Mengjie Zhang, Le Dong, Qinghua Yu, Chengru Jiang, Xiaoyang Zhu, Mei Chen, Kun Zhou, Guoying Gu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Soft robots require the seamless integration of actuation and sensing units to achieve autonomy and adaptability behaviors. This demands conformal, stretchable, and spatially distributed electronic components—an unmet challenge with conventional design and fabrication methods due to complex three-dimensional (3D) geometries, multimaterial integration, and mechanical-electrical mismatches between rigid electronics and soft bodies. Here, we present a design and fabrication framework for autonomous soft robots with embedded actuation and sensing. We develop an integrated digital light processing and direct ink writing 3D printing technology to fabricate soft robots with embedded and conformal 3D electronics in an automated manner. To ensure both electrical and mechanical stability under large deformation, we introduce a structural design strategy incorporating lattice metamaterials, wavy interconnects, and discretized printed circuit boards. By combining the fabrication and design methods, we demonstrate soft robots with multimodal actuation, real-time tactile sensing, wireless communication, tactile-to-visual feedback, and autonomous obstacle avoidance. Our approach paves the way for the development of electronics-integrated autonomous soft robots.

INTRODUCTION

Autonomous navigation in unstructured environments is a central challenge in robotics, with direct relevance to search-and-rescue missions and inspection or manipulation in complex industrial settings. Soft robots are well suited to such tasks because their compliant materials can withstand unexpected mechanical disturbances that can disable rigid systems (1, 2). Autonomous soft robots with embedding sensing and control enable rapid and stable operation despite disturbances (3–5). To realize autonomous soft robots, various methods have been explored. One approach involves integrating smart materials, such as thermally driven (6–9), light-responsive (10–15), magnetically actuated (16–21), or electrothermally actuated soft robots (22, 23). Substantial efforts have also been devoted to developing untethered soft actuators (24, 25). However, smart materials often suffer from slow response times and restricted actuation magnitudes. Another approach focuses on designing entirely soft robots, such as the soft octobot (26), twisted liquid crystal elastomer (LCE) based robot (8), three-dimensionally (3D) printed insect-scale robot (27), and growth soft robot (28). While these designs achieve excellent deformability and adaptability, critical components such as soft computational units and signal transmission systems remain underdeveloped, which are essential for autonomous functionality. A promising solution lies in the hybrid integration of soft robotics with traditional rigid electronics. In this strategy, soft robotic components are responsible for actuation and sensing, while rigid electronics handle computation, signal processing, and decision-making. Toward this goal, progress has been made recently, including soft robots with redesign Arduinos (29), octopus-inspired tentacles (30), soft robots for deep-sea exploration (31), 3D stretchable electronics (32), and soft electrically actuated quadrupeds (33).

Despite these advances, the integration of the rigid electronics into the soft materials poses challenges on design and fabrication. Autonomous soft robots require the integration of soft actuators, soft sensors, flexible circuits, energy supply units, control units, and signal transmission components. From a design perspective, embedding rigid electronics into a soft matrix causes stress concentration and interface delamination, leading to mechanical and electrical failure. Various lattice metamaterials and rigid-soft interconnect strategies for reducing the mechanical and electrical failure have been reviewed (34). Moreover, autonomous navigation requires the structural design of soft actuators for multimodal locomotion, soft sensors for force sensing, discretized printed circuit boards (PCBs) for compact integration, 3D conformal circuits for connections, and an impact-resistant soft body. From a fabrication perspective, various methods have been used recently to fabricate soft robots, including molding (35–37), assembling (38–41), and 3D printing (27, 42–46). Parameters such as resolution, process time, material compatibility, and system integration capability are compared in table S1. Among them, molding and assembling are often time-consuming and prone to manufacturing errors. Many pioneering studies have explored the monolithic integration of rigid and soft components, stretchable electronics, and the integration of actuation and sensing functions (47–49). However, these integrations and the resulting robotic systems are primarily demonstrated on planar or thin-film structures. The fabrication of complex three-dimensional architectures that simultaneously incorporate actuators, sensors, and multiple materials remains a substantial challenge.

3D printing, especially digital light processing (DLP) and direct ink writing (DIW), has become widely used in soft robot fabrication. DLP has the advantage of creating complex 3D geometries, whereas DIW is compatible with a wide range of functional materials. But realizing autonomous soft robots remains challenging with a single 3D printing technique as it demands the integration of soft and rigid materials, passive and functional components, and complex architectures within the limited space of a small-scale soft robot. These demands typically necessitate combining multiple 3D printing methods. A notable prior approach combined top-down DLP with DIW for functional devices (50), enabling the development of various functional structures and devices. Nevertheless, the top-down method still encounters difficulties in producing complex structures. Key challenges still require further investigation including compatibility between the two techniques and structural fidelity when integrating rigid, soft, and conductive elements.

Herein, we present a design and fabrication framework for autonomous soft robots with embedded actuation and sensing. We developed an integrated multimaterial DLP and DIW 3D printing technology, enabling the seamless integration of soft and rigid materials, conformal 3D electronics, and complex architectures within a small-scale soft robot. The DLP uses the bottom-up method, which enables fabrication of more complex structures. The system achieves 82-μm lateral and 50-μm vertical resolution in DLP printing and 270-μm resolution when printing silicones via DIW. It can print a soft robot integrating soft actuator, soft sensor, and flexible circuit in 30 min and requires no postassembling. To ensure mechanical and electrical functionality under large deformation, we introduce a structural design strategy incorporating lattice metamaterials, wavy interconnects, and discretized PCBs. The resulting soft robots—integrating soft actuators, soft sensors, flexible circuits, and wireless communication—weigh ~120 g and can be remotely controlled to move forward, backward, and laterally; provide tactile-to-visual feedback; and autonomously navigate environments without human intervention (Fig. 1A). Our approach paves the way for the development of electronics-integrated autonomous soft robots.

RESULTS

Integrated 3D printing of the soft robot

An integrated 3D printing approach combining DLP and DIW technologies is developed (Fig. 1, B to F, and movie S1), which leverages the strengths of both methods: DLP constructs complex 3D structures, while DIW deposits functional materials such as conductive inks. The fabrication process involves four key steps.

Step 1: Base structure fabrication (Fig. 1B). The soft body is fabricated using the DLP process with a hybrid resin [TangoPlus and epoxy aliphatic acrylate (TEAA)], composed of a 6:4 mixture of the commercial ultraviolet (UV)–curable resin TangoPlus and epoxy aliphatic acrylate (EAA). Channels for conductive circuits and spaces for embedded electronic components are left unfilled.

Step 2: Embedding electronics (Fig. 1, C and D). The printing platform is rotated 180° to position the printed platform facing upward. Discretized PCBs and electronic components are embedded into designated spaces within the soft structure to accommodate the small size.

Step 3: Printing conductive circuits (Fig. 1E). The DIW process deposits conductive silicone (Kingstar K867) into reserved channels for flexible circuits due to their relatively lower resistance. The resistive silicone (Kingstar K868) is used as strain-sensitive circuits owing to their larger resistance. The flexible circuits connect discretized electronic components. Vertical or tilted conformal circuits are achieved by adjusting the nozzle trajectories.

Step 4: Packaging (Fig. 1F). The printing platform is rotated back to its original position, and additional layers were printed using the DLP process to encapsulate the electronic components, conductive and resistive circuits. This packaging enhances structural integrity and electronic stability.

To prevent cross-contamination between the two printing processes, an air knife is introduced to remove residual resin. When the cured structure is lifted out of the resin and prepared for DIW printing, the residual resin on its surface can hinder the adhesion of DIW inks. This issue is particularly critical for embedded PCBs, which must be electrically connected via DIW-printed conductive silicone. If resin remains on the conductive pads of the PCBs, then the conductive silicone cannot properly adhere, leading to connection failure. To address this, an air knife is introduced to remove residual resin during each transition from DLP to DIW printing.

By iteratively repeating the steps above, small-scale soft robots with passive and functional materials, complex structures, 3D conductive circuits, and electronic components are fabricated. The integrated 3D printing technology allows for the precise fabrication of soft robots with intricate structures and 3D electronic devices through automated processes. These complex structures enable the design of soft actuators for multimodal motion and programmable mechanical behaviors. Additionally, the 3D electronic devices optimize vertical space utilization, reducing the overall size of the robot. Movie S1 demonstrates the complete fabrication process of a soft robot, in which all components, including the soft actuator, soft sensor, and embedded PCBs, are fabricated in 30 min and require no postassembling.

Fabrication resolution characterization

Experiments were conducted to quantitatively measure the fabrication resolutions of the 3D printers, including the printing resolution of DLP, DIW, and the matching between DLP and DIW technique. The printing resolution of the DIW process was first evaluated. Conductive silicone (K867) was extruded through a nozzle with an inner diameter of 0.55 mm, with the printing height set equal to the nozzle diameter. Straight lines of 50 mm in length were printed while varying the printing speed from 2 to 10 mm/s and the air pressure from 150 to 350 kPa. As shown in Fig. 1G, the printed linewidth decreases with increasing printing speed but increases with higher air pressure. By appropriately adjusting these two parameters, linewidths ranging from 0.3 to 1.3 mm were successfully achieved. To demonstrate the printability, a lattice structure was printed by DIW with K867 (Fig. 1H). The size is 5 mm by 5 mm, with a width of around 320 μm.

The resolution of the DLP 3D printing process was then evaluated. The optical resolution of the projector used was 56 μm by 56 μm per pixel. To visualize the printed resolution in the thickness (z) direction, a U-shaped structure was fabricated, with the corresponding design images shown in the inset of Fig. 1I. TEAA resin was used for the printing process. The experimental procedure involved curing 10 base layers, followed by printing two pillars with 20 layers each. A single layer between the pillars was cured with varying exposure times from 1 to 3 s, and its thickness was subsequently measured. As shown in Fig. 1I, the layer thickness increased with exposure time, ranging from 312 to 907 μm.

To characterize the printing resolution in the xy plane, both the positive and negative resolution methods were used (51). Figure 1J shows the designed and printed structures obtained using these two methods. In the positive resolution method, a series of line models with different widths were printed using DLP, and the minimum achievable linewidth was 82 μm. The negative resolution method measured the minimum printable channel diameter, which was found to be 205 μm.

The compatibility between the DIW and DLP methods were then evaluated. To deposit the DIW-printed conductive ink (K867) onto the DLP-printed structures (TEAA), a cavity structure was designed to accommodate the ink (Fig. 1K). Three cavities with different widths were fabricated, corresponding to w = d, 1.5d and 2d, where d is the nozzle’s inner diameter. A larger nozzle diameter of d = 1 mm was selected to ensure stable extrusion, with a printing speed of 5 mm/s and an air pressure of 150 kPa. The cavity height was set to h = d. Cross-sectional microscopic images revealed that cavity width affects ink filling. The ink fully filled the cavity when w = d; however, for larger widths, incomplete filling was observed. Movie S7 shows the match between DIW and DLP printing on the plane.

Design for soft and rigid material interaction

Mechanical failure and electrical instability typically occur at the interface between soft and rigid materials, especially under large deformations. We design wavy patterns for the conductive circuit, discretize the PCB, and design lattice structures to withstand large deformations without compromising functionality. Figure 2A illustrates a two-layer light-emitting diode (LED) circuit, where layer 1 houses a serpentine circuit connected to the power supply, and layer 2 contains circuits linked to an LED. The layers are electrically connected via a vertical circuit (fig. S3A). The serpentine design enhances flexibility, allowing the circuit to accommodate large deformations of soft materials (Fig. 2B).

We further discretize the PCB to accommodate the small sizes and reduce the stress concentration. Figure 2C shows a device with two discretized PCBs, interconnected by DIW 3D printed curved conductive circuits to match the stretchability of the soft robot. The larger PCB is powered directly by a 3.7-V lithium battery, while the smaller PCB is powered through the 3D printed circuit. LEDs are embedded in both PCBs to verify connectivity and remain lit under compression, stretching, torsion, and bending (Fig. 2D). Experimental and finite element (FE) simulated tensile stress-strain curves show that the flexible circuit can withstand up to 40% strain (blue curve and markers), with both LEDs staying illuminated during stretching (Fig. 2E and movie S2). The simulated maximum strain at the interface between the soft matrix and rigid PCBs occurs at the PCB corners, reaching ~20%, which is not sufficient to cause substantial delamination.

Soft robots are often exposed to external forces or impacts, which can damage internal electronic components. Lattice structures are designed using integrated 3D printing technology to reduce impact on rigid electronic devices. Figure 2F presents a lattice structure with embedded LED circuits, which maintain functionality under compression (Fig. 2G and movie S3). The lattice design can be tailored to achieve specific stress-strain behaviors. Simulated stress-strain curves and deformed shapes agree well with the experimental results (Fig. 2H). Cyclic compression tests at 45% axial compression are conducted, showing that the lattice LED circuit performs reliably throughout the cyclic tests. By incorporating curved circuits, optimizing PCB design, and using lattice structures, the electronic units maintain reliable performance under large deformations.

Structural design of soft actuator

The soft actuator is designed to exhibit multimodal motion by controlling the actuation sequences. The soft actuator comprises two rows and three columns of feet (Fig. 3A), with each column controlled by pneumatic channels independently. Each foot has a triangular structure with a different thickness, with the left wall measuring 1.1 mm and the right wall measuring 0.8 mm (Fig. 3B). Reinforcing bars are added to the left wall additionally to further constrain its deformation. When a negative pressure is applied, the tip of the foot bends toward the right wall (Fig. 3C). The bending angle θ increases with the applied pressure and can reach 45° at −62 kPa.

(C) Experimental snapshots showing the bending shapes under different negative pressures, along with the dependence of the bending angle θ on the negative pressure. (D) Forward, backward, left turn, and right turn movements are achieved by independently controlling the actuation sequences of the three air tubes. Forward, backward, left-turn, and right-turn movements are achieved by independently controlling the actuation sequences of the three air tubes (Fig. 3D). For forward motion, channels 1 and 3 are actuated in a synchronized sequence, 1/4 cycle slower than the sequence of channel 2. Backward motion is realized by altering the signal sequence to reverse the progression of actuation. For right-turn motion, pressure channels 2 and 3 switch in the same sequence as forward motion, while channel 1 is 1/2 cycle faster relative to forward motion. The left-turn motion is 1/2 cycle faster than the right-turn motion. Figure 3 (E and F) shows experimental snapshots during the forward and right turning motions, respectively.

Design of soft sensors

Soft sensors are crucial for enabling feedback control in soft robots, allowing them to perceive their environment and adapt their behavior accordingly. A 3/4-circle-shaped soft sensor is designed for touch detection and fabricated using the integrated DLP and DIW 3D printer (Fig. 4A). The soft shell of the sensor is DLP 3D printed, while the resistive circuits are DIW 3D printed. A microcontroller unit (MCU) module and a Bluetooth module are embedded in the soft sensor to handle signal processing and transmission. A connected battery supplies the necessary voltage to the MCU. The detailed computer-aided design (CAD) model and PCB design are shown in figs. S3E and S4A.

The 3/4-circle-shaped soft sensor contains resistive silicone printed by DIW. The resistance of the silicone changes under deformation. The 3/4 circle of the sensor is evenly divided into three sections, which are arranged on the left side, the front side and the right side, respectively. After being divided into three sections (Fig. 4B), the sensor has four pins, and a fixed voltage of 3.3 V is applied between the two pins at the end of the 3/4 circle. The MCU on the PCB, which is embedded in the soft sensor, reads the voltage values on the two middle pins through the analog-to-digital converter channel. Once the resistance of any one of the three sections changes, the voltage values U1 and U2 at the two middle pins change accordingly. The Bluetooth on the PCB returns the values of U1 and U2 in real time (movie S10). The section under touch can be identified. The directional inference depends on the comparative analysis between the two voltages. The algorithm is clarified in section S14. To provide visual feedback, LEDs are integrated into a 3D printed remote controller, lighting up in different colors to indicate the touch location—red for left, green for front, and blue for right (Fig. 4C and movie S4).

Figure 4D demonstrates the voltage readings (top plot) and the relative voltage changes (bottom plot) for U1 and U2 under various touch positions. The relative voltage change is calculated as the difference between the current voltage and the previous sampling point. Specifically, when the left side is touched, both U1 and U2 decrease. For the front side, U1 increases while U2 decreases, and, when the right side is touched, both U1 and U2 increase. Twenty cycles of 2-mm deformations are applied over 30 s for the left, front, and right sections to assess the repeatability of the sensor (Fig.4, E to G). A sampling interval of 0.025 s is set, and a low-pass filtering is applied to eliminate high-frequency noise and improve data reliability. A threshold (±0.01 V) is set to judge the touch.

Demonstration of remote control

With the rapid development of wearable flexible devices, they are increasingly being used to enhance human-machine interaction. Pioneering examples include self-powered electrotactile gloves (52, 53) and a closed-loop drone interaction system integrating vision, vibration, and electrical stimulation feedback (54). We design a soft robot that can be remotely controlled via finger gestures and provide tactile-to-visual feedback. The soft robot consists of a pneumatic actuator with 3 × 2 feet and a 3/4-circle-shaped soft sensor. A PCB is embedded in the soft robot, integrated with MCU, Bluetooth, and a battery. The MCU continuously measures the voltage changes from the soft sensor, while Bluetooth transmits the signals to a computer for further processing.

A finger-worn remote controller is designed (Fig. 5A). It contains two interconnected PCBs (fig. S4, B and C): one with an inertial measurement unit (IMU) for detecting orientation and the other with a MCU and Bluetooth module. The MCU processes the pitch, roll, and yaw data measured by IMU, which is then wirelessly transmitted to the computer via Bluetooth. The remote controller allows users to control the robot’s movements through specific gestures. Figure 5C shows four basic gestures: forward, backward, left, and right. Moving the finger upward or downward drives the robot to move forward or backward, respectively, while rotating the finger controls left or right movement. Experiments confirm that the robot can be remotely controlled with finger gestures, moving freely in any direction on a plane (Fig. 5C and movie S5).

Beyond remote control, the soft robot can send its sensor data back to the remote controller via Bluetooth, providing tactile-to-visual feedback (Fig. 5B and movie S5). When the robot encounters an obstacle, the soft sensor sends voltage signals back to computer, which identifies the deformed section (left, front, or right). The controller then triggers different LED colors to provide visual feedback to the user.

As shown in Fig. 5D and movie S10, the stretchable connector allows the fingers to move freely, including bending and separation, while maintaining stable signal transmission. The plotted data in Fig. 5D illustrate the acceleration and orientation angles recorded by the IMU embedded in the soft remote controller during different control gestures. The red-highlighted section in Fig. 5D indicates that the fingers can move quickly and naturally without discomfort, and the signal remains stable throughout these deformations. This demonstrates that the stretchable connector not only enhances mechanical comfort but also ensures robust signal reliability during dynamic finger motions. The wearable controller attached to the finger is mainly designed as a concise and intuitive control interface, analogous to teleoperation. Its purpose is to replace the conventional remote controller with a more direct control approach using finger motion. The control of the robot may become less stable and efficient during finger separation and bending, and that a rigid design could better ensure reliable signal transmission for robot control.

The lattice structure (fig. S3F) enables the soft robot to maintain its functionality even after undergoing large deformation. Figure 5E and movie S11 demonstrate the robot’s performance under such conditions. Following large deformations, including pressing, slapping and punching, both the multimodal motion and the tactile-to-visual feedback remain functional.

Demonstration of automatic obstacle avoidance

The ability of the soft robot to navigate environments without human intervention is demonstrated. The control panel includes a customized mini-PCB (20 mm by 15 mm by 1 mm) with a Bluetooth module, an IMU and three time-of-flight (TOF) sensors (Fig. 6A and fig. S4D). The IMU tracks the yaw angle and acceleration to monitor movement direction and status. The TOF sensors, mounted on the front, left, and right, measure obstacle distances, with a 60-mm threshold to prevent collisions. The Bluetooth module enables real-time path planning via computer communication.

Figure 6 (B and C) illustrates the robot’s obstacle avoidance logic in different scenarios. In Fig. 6B, the robot moves forward until the front-facing TOF sensor detects an obstacle. It then turns 90° left, with the IMU ensuring accurate rotation (i). The robot proceeds forward until it clears the obstacle (ii), then turns 90° right (iii), and continues in another direction (iv). Figure 6C depicts a dead-end scenario where obstacles block three sides. The robot reverses until it is no longer obstructed (i), turns 90° left (ii), moves forward (iii), and then turns 90° right (iv) to resume its path (v).

To validate the robot’s autonomous decision-making capabilities, two obstacle mazes are designed (Fig. 6, D and E, and movie S6). In Fig. 6D, the soft robot starts at the top left and navigates an S-shaped maze with two obstacle bars. It successfully detects and avoids them to reach the target at the bottom right. In Fig. 6E, the soft robot starts at the bottom left and encounters two dead ends before reaching the target at the bottom right. The results confirm the robot’s ability to recognize and navigate dead ends without getting stuck.

DISCUSSION

We have introduced a general design and manufacturing method for autonomous soft robots, which integrates soft actuators, sensors, flexible circuits, discretized PCBs, signal transmission components, processing units, and batteries into a small-scale structure. In contrast to traditional manual fabrication methods, such as molding and assembly, which are often time-consuming and prone to manufacturing errors, the integrated 3D printing method allows for precise, automated production. This approach not only enhances the fabrication accuracy but also enables the creation of complex geometries, made possible by the DLP components, and the incorporation of functional materials through DIW, offering capabilities that current methods cannot achieve. The developed DLP-DIW 3D printing platform enables the automatic fabrication of complex structures composed of multiple materials. This system provides a versatile platform for the monolithic fabrication of soft robots with diverse structural and material configurations.

The advancement of fabrication technologies improves the design phase as well. We have discretized PCBs and designed lattice structures and curved conductive circuits to address the strain mismatch between rigid and soft materials. The discretized PCBs help alleviate stress concentrations at the interface between rigid and soft components. Note that rigid PCBs were used in this work. Flexible PCBs, with their compliant substrates, could further improve the robot’s flexibility by better accommodating bending and twisting deformations. Various lattice structures with tailorable mechanical performance can be designed to absorb energy and resist large deformations. The curved design of the circuits enhances their flexibility, enabling them to better accommodate large deformations of soft robots.

The maximum size is constrained by the DLP projector’s printing area, which is around 108.5 mm by 61 mm. If larger size is required, then the printing area of the DLP projector should be increased. If larger dimensions are required, then the printing area of the DLP projector can be increased in several ways: (i) replacing the DLP projector or optical lenses to enlarge the printing area—although this may come at the cost of reduced resolution; and (ii) applying splicing techniques in DLP 3D printing to achieve large-format fabrication, although this introduces challenges such as seam alignment accuracy and ensuring consistent mechanical properties across joints.

In this work, lattice and wavy structures are used to reduce failure in soft robots subjected to large deformations. Another monolithic integration approach is the use of gradient-stiffness–programmed circuit boards, achieved through spatially controlled phase transitions of supercooled hydrogels, which can offer enhanced electromechanical stability for stretchable electronics integration (55). The presence of pneumatic pumps and tubing restricts the mobility of soft robots. To mitigate their effects, only one pump is used for the three actuations by using three two-position, three-way valves (5 g each). Moreover, a miniature pump is used to reduce the weight. As a result, the total hardware system weight was reduced to 100 g (fig. S1B). Recently, several studies have explored untethered pneumatic soft robots, such as integrating pumps directly into the robots (56, 57) and developing untethered soft pumps based on phase transition mechanisms (58), which offer promising approaches for untethered soft robots.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Material preparation

The hybrid resin TEAA is a mixture of the commercial UV-curable resin Tango and EAA. The UV-curable resin Tango was purchased from Stratasys (MN, USA) and chosen to be the base material. The EAA was purchased from Allnex (Germany). The UV-curable resin TEAA was prepared by mixing the Tango and EAA at the 6:4 weight ratio. The hybrid resin was mixed at 2000 rpm for 1.5 min and then centrifugated at 2200 rpm for 1 min, using the planetary centrifugal mixer (ARE-310, Thinky, USA). The conductive silicone (K867) and resistive silicone (K868) were purchased from Kingstar (China). The electrical conductivity of conductive silicone is 90 S/cm, and the one of resistive silicone is 26 S/cm. To measure the electrical conductivity of these two materials, we designed a rectangular mold (15 mm by 5 mm by 2 mm), where the silicone was poured and cured. We then used a multimeter to measure the resistances of the silicone samples along the length. The electrical conductivity σ of the silicone samples was calculated asσ=LR×W×T(1)where R is the resistance of the sample, L is the distance between the two electrodes that measure the resistance, W is the width of the sample, and T is the thickness of the sample (59).

Pneumatic control system

The soft robot was actuated by three pneumatic channels to enable multimodal motion. The whole pneumatic control system includes a control board, a battery, a Bluetooth module, an air pump, and three two-position, three-way valves (fig. S1A). Each valve connects to the air pump, the soft actuator, and atmospheric pressure. The entire system weighs ~100 g (fig. S1B). When a valve is open, it links the soft actuator to the pump, generating negative pressure to deform the feet. When closed, the channel connects to the atmosphere, and the feet restore to their original shape. This mechanism enables a single air pump to control all three channels efficiently. A universal asynchronous receiver-transmitter (UART) serial port is included on the control board (fig. S1C) to connect a Bluetooth module. The air pump and valves are controlled via four metal-oxide-semiconductor field-effect transistor switches instead of conventional electromagnetic relays, further reducing weight. The PCB design of the control board is shown in fig. S1D.

Compression test of the lattice with embedded circuit

Compression tests were conducted using a universal material testing machine (Instron 68SC-2, USA) at a speed of 40 mm/min. The lattice structure had a height of 15 mm, and a 7-mm compression was applied for 10 cycles. At a maximum compression of ~45%, the force exerted on the lattice with embedded circuits reaches around 95 N. During the test, a 6-V voltage was applied to the LED circuit, which operated reliably throughout the compression cycles (Fig. 3H).

Stretching test of flexible electronics embedded with PCBs

The stretching tests were conducted using the universal material testing machine at a speed of 10 mm/min. The flexible electronics were powered by a 3.7-V lithium battery, with the larger PCB powered directly by the battery and the smaller one powered through the printed circuits. After embedding the PCBs, the stretching fracture strain of the structure is ~40%, showing negligible difference from the fracture strain of the structure without PCBs (Fig. 3E).

Finite element simulations

Finite element simulations were conducted using the commercial software ABAQUS (3D Systems, Rock Hill, SC, USA) to analyze the mechanical behavior of 3D printed structural circuits (figs. S8 to S10 and movie S9). The printable material properties were characterized by the Neo-Hookean model, with material constants set as c1 = 0.7 MPa for TEAA and c1 = 0.82 MPa for conductive silicone. The strain energy density function was given by W = c1(IC − 3), where IC denotes the first invariant of the right Cauchy-Green tensor. The PCBs were modeled as rigid bodies. The simulations used 10-node quadratic tetrahedral elements (C3D10M), with refined meshing to ensure computational accuracy. Mechanical testing was simulated under both tensile and compressive loading conditions. For tensile loading, quasistatic simulations were performed using the Abaqus/Standard solver, where the left surface of the structure was fixed, and displacement boundary conditions were applied to the right surface. For compressive loading, dynamic simulations were conducted using the Abaqus/Explicit solver. The circuit was placed between a fixed supporting plate and a downward-moving loading plate, which was constrained in all other degrees of freedom. The loading rate was set to 1 mm/s. Contact interactions were modeled using the general penalty contact scheme with a friction coefficient of 0.2.
