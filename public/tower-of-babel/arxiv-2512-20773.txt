Adversarial Training for Failure-Sensitive User Simulation in Mental Health Dialogue Optimization
Ziyi Zhu, Olivier Tieleman, Caitlin A. Stamatis, Luka Smyth, Thomas D. Hull, Daniel R. Cahn, Matteo Malgaroli

Abstract
Realistic user simulation is crucial for training and evaluating task-oriented dialogue (TOD) systems, yet creating simulators that accurately replicate human behavior remains challenging. A key property of effective simulators is their ability to expose failure modes of the systems they evaluate. We present an adversarial training framework that iteratively improves user simulator realism through a competitive dynamic between a generator (user simulator) and a discriminator. Applied to mental health support chatbots, our approach demonstrates that fine-tuned simulators dramatically outperform zero-shot base models at surfacing system issues, and adversarial training further enhances diversity, distributional alignment, and predictive validity. The resulting simulator achieves a strong correlation between simulated and real failure occurrence rates across diverse chatbot configurations while maintaining low distributional divergence of failure modes. Discriminator accuracy decreases drastically after three adversarial iterations, suggesting improved realism. These results provide evidence that adversarial training is a promising approach for creating realistic user simulators in mental health support TOD domains, enabling rapid, reliable, and cost-effective system evaluation before deployment.



1
Introduction

Task-oriented dialogue (TOD) systems guide users through multi-turn conversations to achieve specific goals
(
22
)
. The development of large language model (LLM) based TOD systems has created an urgent need for robust evaluation methodologies that go beyond single-turn metrics. Multi-turn task-oriented conversations involve complex dynamics where system quality emerges from sustained interaction, user satisfaction accumulates over time, and task success depends on maintaining context across extended dialogue
(
17
;
5
)
. However, traditional evaluation through live user testing is expensive, time-consuming, and exposes users to potentially suboptimal system behavior.

User simulation offers a compelling alternative: synthetic agents that replicate human conversational behavior enable rapid iteration, systematic exploration of edge cases, and controlled experimentation
(
31
;
33
)
. At the current state of the art, realistic user simulators have become essential for training language models through reinforcement learning (RL), where they serve as the environment for policy optimization
(
27
;
35
)
. Recent advances in multi-turn dialogue optimization have demonstrated the potential of RL methods to improve long-term conversation quality
(
25
;
6
)
. However, creating simulators that faithfully reproduce the nuanced, diverse, and goal-directed nature of human conversational behavior presents significant challenges. Simulators that fail to reproduce realistic user behavior cannot reliably expose system failure modes during development, leading to policies that appear effective in simulation but perform poorly with actual users.

In this work, we address the challenge of creating realistic user simulators through adversarial training. Inspired by Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs)
(
4
)
, we train a discriminator to distinguish between simulated and real user sessions, then use the discriminator feedback to refine the simulator through Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)
(
19
)
. This creates a competitive dynamic where the simulator learns to fool an increasingly sophisticated discriminator, automatically identifying and correcting the most prominent simulation artifacts.

We evaluate our approach in the context of Ash
1
1

1

https://www.talktoash.com/
, a mental health support chatbot designed by Slingshot AI—a complex TOD domain where sustained conversation quality has direct implications for user wellbeing. Our experimental results demonstrate several key findings:

•

Fine-tuning dramatically outperforms zero-shot
: Domain-specific fine-tuning is essential for capturing realistic user behavior distributions in TOD, including negative and resistant patterns that zero-shot models fail to exhibit.

•

Adversarial training enhances realism
: Three iterations of adversarial refinement progressively recover lexical diversity suppressed by supervised learning, increase distributional similarity, and dramatically reduce discriminator accuracy from near-perfect detection to near-random performance.

•

Strong predictive validity
: The best adversarial simulator achieves strong correlation with real model performance and successfully replicates A/B test results on conversation models, enabling reliable offline TOD evaluation before deployment.

These contributions provide evidence that adversarial training is a promising methodology for creating realistic user simulators in the mental health support TOD domain, with potential applications to RL-based policy optimization and reliable offline evaluation. Future work will validate the approach across other TOD domains and integrate simulators into RL training loops.

Related Work

2.1
Multi-Turn Dialogue Optimization

While RLHF
(
17
)
demonstrated that language models can be aligned with human preferences, it typically optimizes single-turn responses rather than multi-turn dialogue outcomes. DPO
(
19
)
offers an alternative to RLHF by directly optimizing the policy without explicit reward modeling, simplifying the training pipeline while maintaining effectiveness.

Optimizing dialogue systems for long-term conversation quality requires methods that go beyond single-turn reward modeling. Recent approaches include training on imagined conversations and hindsight regeneration
(

;

)
, token-level policy optimization
(
25
)
, hierarchical decomposition of planning and execution
(
35
)
, and offline RL methods for language generation
(

)
. These methods critically depend on the quality of the training environment, with realistic user simulators being essential for both policy optimization and evaluation
(
16
)
.

2.2
User Simulation for Task-Oriented Dialogue

User simulation has been central to TOD research since early agenda-based systems that represented behavior through goal-directed dialogue acts
(
22
;
21
)
. Neural approaches later enabled more flexible simulation by capturing dialogue history without rigid structures
(
3
;
9
)
, demonstrating that policies trained on neural simulators could achieve higher success rates with real users. Recent work on mental health support benchmarking
(
18
)
demonstrates that LLMs struggle with longer interactions and patients presenting severe symptoms, underscoring the need for realistic simulators that capture the full complexity of human emotions and behaviors.

LLM-Based Simulation:
Large language models have transformed user simulation through in-context learning and prompting approaches
(
11
;
2
)
, achieving goal success rates comparable to human-bot interactions with greater linguistic diversity and minimal seed dialogues. However, prompting-only approaches suffer from hallucinations and goal drift. Fine-tuning addresses these limitations through various techniques, including reducing behavioral inconsistencies, joint policy and NLG optimization for domain transfer, and dual-model architectures with chain-of-thought reasoning
(
24
;
12
;
15
)
.

RL-Based Simulator Training:
While supervised learning dominates simulator training, reinforcement learning approaches optimize for task-level objectives rather than imitation alone. Multi-agent RL enables joint system-user training
(
30
;
13
)
, while adversarial training creates competitive dynamics between simulators and discriminators
(
8
;
10
)
. The adversarial approach has demonstrated substantial improvements, with discriminator-based rewards being "more general, natural and effective" than hand-crafted metrics. Our work extends this adversarial paradigm to LLM-based simulators, using DPO rather than policy gradient methods to leverage discriminator feedback while avoiding reward hacking.

Evaluation Methodology:
Traditional TOD evaluation relies on task-level metrics such as success rate and slot error rate measured on standard benchmarks like MultiWOZ
(
1
)
, SGD
(
20
)
, and tau-bench
(
32
)
, which effectively capture transactional outcomes but provide limited insight into conversational quality. The poor correlation between surface-level metrics like BLEU/ROUGE and human judgments
(
14
)
motivates more sophisticated evaluation approaches that consider behavioral fidelity and distributional alignment
(
16
)
. Our work advances this evaluation paradigm through three key contributions: (1) explicit modeling of conversational feature occurrence rate distributions using KL divergence to measure alignment on fine-grained failure modes rather than binary task success, (2) validation of predictive utility through model A/B test replication beyond correlation analysis, and (3) discriminator accuracy as a direct measure of distributional realism.

3
Methodology

3.1
Problem Formulation

We formalize user simulation as a conditional generation task. Given conversation history
h_{t}=\{(u_{1},a_{1}),...,(u_{t-1},a_{t-1}),a_{t}\}
consisting of previous user utterances
u_{i}
and agent responses
a_{i}
, plus user context
c
(e.g., interaction history and background for the user), the simulator generates the next user utterance
u_{t}\sim\pi_{\theta}(u_{t}|h_{t},c)
.

We train
\pi_{\theta}
to generate simulated conversations indistinguishable from real user conversations. To this end, we bootstrap an initial simulator from real conversations, construct prompts with appropriate context, and iteratively alternate between training discriminators to detect simulated conversations and refining simulators to evade detection.

3.2
Context Construction for User Simulation

A critical design choice for realistic user simulation is the construction of context
c
that conditions the simulator’s behavior. Effective context should provide: (1) role and behavioral guidelines through system instructions, (2) user background and goals, (3) conversational history to maintain consistency, and (4) session-specific grounding to anchor the simulator to target conversation characteristics. The specific context structure depends on the domain—transactional TOD systems might include task schemas and slot values, while open-ended domains might emphasize user profiles and session summaries. Our mental health support chatbot implementation (detailed in Appendix
A
) uses a hierarchical structure spanning user profiles, multi-session summaries, and current session grounding. As we demonstrate in Section
5
, rich context grounding enables fine-tuned simulators to cluster tightly with their corresponding real sessions in embedding space, while zero-shot models without such grounding form distinct clusters, indicating systematic behavioral drift.

3.3
Base Simulator Training

We begin by training a base user simulator through supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on anonymized user conversations. Starting from Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct as our pre-trained foundation model, we fine-tune on a dataset
\mathcal{D}=\{(h_{i},c_{i},u_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
of real user responses paired with conversation history and context:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}}=-\mathbb{E}_{(h,c,u)\sim\mathcal{D}}[\log\pi_{\theta}(u|h,c)]

(1)

3.4
Discriminator Training

To measure simulator realism, we train a discriminator
D_{\phi}
to distinguish between simulated and real user sessions. We implement the discriminator as a token classification model built on the Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct backbone. The discriminator processes the full conversation and predicts after each user message whether the session is real or simulated:

p_{t}=D_{\phi}(\text{real}|h_{t},c)

(2)

We use causal attention in the discriminator, meaning predictions are made based on the current message and all previous messages in the session. This design choice is essential because any individual message could plausibly be either real or simulated when taken out of context. The discriminator’s effectiveness stems from detecting
distributional
patterns across the conversation: certain phrases repeated multiple times, unnatural topic progressions, or behavioral patterns that diverge from real user dynamics. Without conversational context, the discrimination task becomes noisy and ill-defined, as isolated messages lack the sequential dependencies that reveal simulation artifacts.

We train
D_{\phi}
on a balanced dataset of real and simulated sessions using binary cross-entropy loss:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{disc}}=-\mathbb{E}_{(h,c,y)}[y\log p+(1-y)\log(1-p)]

(3)

where
y=1
for real sessions and
y=0
for simulated sessions. A high discriminator accuracy indicates the simulator has not achieved realistic behavior; our goal is to train simulators that reduce discriminator accuracy toward random guessing.

In our adversarial training approach, each new discriminator is trained exclusively on data from the most recent simulator version. This ensures the preference pairs we generate for DPO reflect the current policy’s behavior rather than artifacts from earlier iterations. Research on preference optimization supports this on-policy approach: on-policy preference pairs exhibit stronger contrastiveness and clearer preference signals
(
34
)
, online methods require only “partial coverage” compared to the strict “global coverage” needed by offline methods
(
28
)
, and on-policy sampling efficiently concentrates probability mass on high-reward responses
(
29
)
.

Optimization Method:
We use DPO rather than RLHF methods
(
23
;
26
)
because discriminator rewards are inherently policy-dependent and vulnerable to reward hacking. With RLHF, the simulator would continuously optimize against a fixed reward model, eventually exploiting patterns that score highly but don’t reflect genuine realism. Preventing this would require retraining the discriminator throughout optimization—a substantial computational overhead. DPO avoids this problem: we generate preference pairs once per iteration using the current discriminator, then optimize against this fixed dataset. Preferences grounded in comparisons between specific alternatives are more robust than absolute reward values, and the discrete iteration structure naturally prevents reward hacking.

Reward Signal:
For each user message in a simulated session, we compute a reward based on how the message changes the discriminator’s prediction:

r_{t}=\log\left(\frac{p_{t}}{1-p_{t}}\right)-\log\left(\frac{p_{t-1}}{1-p_{t-1}}\right)

(4)

We use log-odds rather than raw probabilities to reflect the exponentially increasing difficulty of fooling the discriminator at higher confidence levels. Higher rewards indicate messages that make the session appear more realistic.

Preference Dataset Generation:
At messages with the highest and lowest rewards (critical moments where the discriminator most clearly detects unrealism), we sample 8 alternative responses. These alternatives are ranked by their discriminator-based rewards to create preference pairs
(u_{\text{chosen}},u_{\text{rejected}})
where the chosen response has the highest reward.

Dataset Filtering:
After generating preference pairs, we filter to retain only samples where
r_{\text{chosen}}>0
and
r_{\text{rejected}}<0
. This selects high-confidence pairs with maximum learning signal while preventing over-correction.

DPO Update:
We train the new simulator using Direct Preference Optimization
(
19
)
, which directly optimizes the policy to prefer higher-reward responses. We use the previous iteration’s simulator as the reference policy
\pi_{\text{ref}}
. Defining the implicit reward
r_{\theta}(h,c,u)=\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(u|h,c)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(u|h,c)}
, the DPO loss is:

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}=-\mathbb{E}_{(h,c,u_{c},u_{r})\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{pref}}}\Big[\log\sigma\Big(\beta\big(r_{\theta}(h,c,u_{c})\\
\quad-r_{\theta}(h,c,u_{r})\big)\Big)\Big]\end{split}

(5)

where
u_{c}
and
u_{r}
are chosen and rejected responses,
\beta
is the temperature parameter, and
\sigma
is the sigmoid function.

4
Experimental Setup

4.1
Domain and Data

We evaluate our methodology in the context of mental health support—a complex task-oriented dialogue domain where users pursue mental health goals (emotional regulation, insight development, behavioral change) through extended multi-turn conversations. Unlike traditional TOD domains with discrete transactional goals, mental health support offers distinct advantages for studying realistic user simulation: rich emotional dynamics and behavioral complexity, multi-session continuity, diverse naturalistic failure modes beyond binary task success, and production-scale data capturing rare but critical patterns like crisis expressions and resistance to engagement.

Our dataset consists of anonymized real user conversations with our mental health support chatbot, including session-level context (user history, previous session summaries) and turn-level annotations of user actions (e.g., expressing distress, gaining insight, agreeing with suggestions) and system issues (e.g., invalidating responses, hallucinations, unclear wording).

4.2
Model Configurations

We train simulators by fine-tuning Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct using LoRA
(
7
)
on datasets of varying sizes (10K, 20K, 40K, 80K, 160K sessions) to explore data scale effects. We perform adversarial training on the final checkpoint (160K), producing UserSim-160K-it1, UserSim-160K-it2, and UserSim-160K-it3 through successive refinement iterations. For comparison, we evaluate three zero-shot base models: GPT-4o, Kimi-K2-Instruct, and Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct. Training hyperparameters are provided in Appendix
C
.

The discriminators (UserDisc-base, UserDisc-it1, UserDisc-it2, UserDisc-it3) are token classification models trained from the Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct backbone.

For each configuration, we generate 2,000 simulated sessions: 1,000 unique session contexts drawn from real user profiles and conversation histories × 2 simulations per context, with early stopping when conversations naturally conclude. Our context construction for user simulation follows the hierarchical structure described in Appendix
A
, including user profiles, multi-session history, and current session grounding. To isolate simulator quality effects, we use a fixed set of 14 chatbot models to simulate conversations.

4.3
Evaluation Metrics

4.3.1
Linguistic Features

For all linguistic features, we calculate metrics for each session individually and report distributional statistics (mean, median, standard deviation) across sessions to capture both typical behavior and variability.

Word Entropy:
Vocabulary diversity using Shannon entropy:
H=-\sum_{w}p(w)\log p(w)
, where
p(w)
is the probability of word
w
in the session’s vocabulary. Higher entropy indicates more diverse word usage.

Type-Token Ratio (TTR):
The ratio of unique words (types) to total words (tokens) in a session, measuring lexical diversity. TTR ranges from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating greater vocabulary variety.

Bigram Diversity:
The ratio of unique word pairs (bigrams) to total bigrams in a session, measuring phrase-level variety and reducing sensitivity to repetitive phrasing.

4.3.2
Behavioral Metrics

User Action Distribution:
Conversations are annotated with mental health-relevant user actions using GPT-5-nano to classify each individual user message according to our action taxonomy (see Appendix
D
for example categories). Since individual messages can have multiple actions, the action distributions are not normalized (probabilities do not sum to 1).

Issue Rate Analysis:
A critical requirement for realistic user simulation is the ability to expose chatbot failure modes. We evaluate simulators by measuring how well they replicate the rate and distribution of problematic conversational features (“issues”) that chatbots exhibit with real users. Issues are identified using GPT-5 with low reasoning effort to analyze entire sessions. The analyzer identifies issues from a detailed taxonomy (35+ categories including invalidating responses, unclear wording, hallucinations, etc.) and highlights the specific messages where each issue occurred, enabling proper credit assignment (see Appendix
E
for example categories). We compare issue rates and category distributions between simulated and real conversations to validate that our simulator accurately reflects the chatbot’s real-world performance and can reliably identify system weaknesses before deployment. Since we examine proportions of issue categories (which form a valid probability distribution that sums to 1), we use KL divergence to measure distributional alignment:

\text{KL}(p\|q)=\sum_{i}p_{i}\log\left(\frac{p_{i}}{q_{i}}\right)

(6)

where
p
is the real distribution and
q
is the simulated distribution.

Similar to the discriminator, the issue analyzer examines whole sessions rather than isolated messages. This design is essential because messages are only problematic when considered in context with the user’s situation and their subsequent replies. The session-level approach makes realistic user simulation critical for accurate evaluation—the simulator must produce contextually appropriate responses that reveal when the chatbot’s messages fail to address the user’s actual needs. This enables numerous evaluation dimensions that would be impossible with isolated message analysis: detecting circular conversations, identifying premature topic changes, recognizing when the chatbot ignores user requests, and assessing whether mental health progress occurs naturally over the conversation arc.

4.3.3
Discriminator Performance

We measure discriminator accuracy using fine-tuned discriminators trained on balanced datasets of real and simulated sessions from their corresponding simulator versions. Each discriminator is trained under identical conditions—1,600 training sessions and 400 validation sessions, with the same learning rate and number of epochs—ensuring that accuracy differences reflect simulator quality rather than training differences. Lower discriminator accuracy indicates a more realistic simulator, with the target being 50% (random guessing).

Linguistic Diversity:
Adversarial training successfully addresses mode collapse by recovering lexical diversity while maintaining behavioral alignment. UserSim-160K-it3 progressively approaches real user levels across all diversity metrics, demonstrating that adversarial training addresses a fundamental limitation of supervised fine-tuning. However, oscillatory behavior across iterations indicates the need for careful hyperparameter tuning. We employ conservative dataset filtering (retaining only high-confidence preference pairs) to mitigate over-correction and maintain distribution stability.

Distributional Alignment:
Adversarial training produces modest improvements in action distribution alignment (see Appendix
B.3
), with progressive refinement across iterations. The limited magnitude of improvements suggests the base fine-tuned model already achieved strong behavioral alignment.

Discriminator Fooling:
Adversarial training dramatically reduced discriminator accuracy across conversation turns (Table
2
). All discriminators were trained under identical conditions with on-policy optimization—each trained exclusively on sessions from its corresponding simulator version. The dramatic performance degradation confirms that adversarial iterations remove systematic artifacts and produce strong distributional alignment throughout full sessions.

Predictive Validity:
To evaluate how well simulators enable reliable offline evaluation, we measure correlation between simulated and real issue rates across diverse chatbot models, and KL divergence between simulated and real issue category distributions. Table
3
shows that adversarial training produces substantial improvements in both metrics. UserSim-160K-it2 achieves the strongest correlation (
r=0.818
), while UserSim-160K-it3 achieves the lowest distributional divergence (KL = 0.148). This provides compelling evidence that adversarially trained simulators are significantly more realistic and effective at identifying chatbot problems in offline testing compared to their base fine-tuned counterparts. Notably, aggregating predictions across all simulator models achieves a strong correlation (
r=0.801
) and low distributional divergence (KL = 0.152), suggesting that ensembling multiple simulators could provide even more robust evaluation.

A/B Test Replication:
Beyond aggregate correlation, we validate predictive utility by replicating a recent model A/B test. All three models exhibited consistent directional trends between simulation and real data, and no model that failed in simulation improved in the wild (Appendix

). Combined with strong correlation and distributional alignment, these results validate the simulator’s utility for reliable offline evaluation.

6
Discussion

Our results establish several important findings for user simulator development:

Firstly, zero-shot models exhibit severe distributional biases, producing unrealistically compliant users who systematically underrepresent negative and resistant behaviors. This confirms that generic language models, despite their capabilities, cannot reliably replicate domain-specific user behavior without exposure to real conversational patterns.

While supervised fine-tuning achieves strong behavioral alignment, it suppresses lexical diversity through mode collapse. Adversarial refinement recovers this diversity while maintaining behavioral fidelity—successfully producing simulators that fool increasingly sophisticated discriminators without sacrificing predictive utility. This demonstrates that realism requires both distributional accuracy and surface-level variation.

Lastly, unlike traditional metrics that measure task success or correlation, discriminator accuracy directly quantifies distributional realism by testing whether conversations are distinguishable from real interactions. The dramatic accuracy degradation across adversarial iterations provides evidence of improved simulation fidelity that complements task-level validation.

7
Conclusion

We have presented an adversarial training framework for creating realistic user simulators that can reliably expose failure modes in multi-turn task-oriented dialogue systems. Applied to mental health support—a complex TOD domain where users pursue mental health goals through extended goal-directed conversations—our approach demonstrates that domain-specific fine-tuning combined with adversarial refinement produces simulators that closely replicate real user behavior across linguistic, semantic, and behavioral dimensions.

Our key contributions are: (1) demonstration that fine-tuned models vastly outperform zero-shot base models for behavioral fidelity, (2) an adversarial training method that recovers lexical diversity suppressed by supervised learning while improving distributional alignment and predictive validity, and (3) comprehensive validation that adversarially-trained simulators reliably predict real model performance, achieving strong correlation with real-world results and successfully replicating A/B test trends.

These results provide evidence that adversarial training is a promising methodology for creating realistic user simulators in mental health support TOD domains. By enabling rapid iteration and systematic exploration of system failure modes before deployment, such simulators can reduce development time, protect users from suboptimal experiences, and potentially provide safe environments for reinforcement learning based optimization of conversation quality.

Limitations

While our approach demonstrates significant improvements in simulator realism, several limitations remain that warrant future investigation:

Mode Collapse:
While adversarial training substantially improves lexical diversity, fine-tuned simulators still show lower phrase-level variety than real users. Techniques like controlled generation or mixture-of-experts architectures might further improve diversity preservation.

Domain Transfer:
Our evaluation focuses on mental health support—a complex, open-ended TOD domain. Validating the methodology in traditional TOD domains (restaurant booking, customer service) and other complex domains (tutoring, technical support) would establish broader applicability and identify domain-specific challenges.

RL Training Integration:
While our simulators enable valid offline evaluation, integrating them directly into RL training loops for TOD policy optimization remains future work. Recent work on RL-based tutoring
(
16
)
demonstrates that simulator quality is critical for reliable RL-based dialogue optimization, underscoring the importance of adversarial training for creating simulators that support both evaluation and training.

Ethical Considerations

All data used in this research were collected from real user conversations with our mental health chatbot with explicit user consent. All personally identifiable information was removed through anonymization procedures in accordance with data protection policies and ethical guidelines for human subjects research. However, due to the sensitive nature of mental health data, we are unable to share the datasets used in this research, despite our desire to promote reproducibility and open science.

Our simulators are designed for system evaluation and development, enabling rapid iteration without exposing users to suboptimal experiences. We note that simulators trained on real conversations may perpetuate biases present in the original dataset; applications should include appropriate oversight and ethical review. Additionally, while simulators can reduce the need for human testing in many scenarios, they should complement rather than entirely replace human feedback for validating novel interventions or edge cases.

Appendix A
Context Construction for Mental Health Support Chatbot

This appendix describes the specific context structure used for our mental health support chatbot user simulator. While the general principles (role definition, user background, conversational history, session grounding) apply broadly to TOD systems, the specific implementation reflects the requirements of mental health support.

Our context construction follows a hierarchical structure with the following components:

System Instruction:
Explicit instructions defining the simulator’s role as a user in mental health support conversations, including guidelines for consistency and realistic behavior.

User Profile:
Demographics and onboarding responses establishing presenting concerns, goals, and background information. This captures the user’s initial state and motivations for seeking support.

Long-term Context:
Aggregated information about the user’s mental health support journey, including recurring themes, progress over time, and persistent concerns that span multiple sessions. This enables the simulator to maintain consistency across extended timeframes.

Previous Session Summaries:
Summaries of 3-5 prior sessions capturing key topics discussed, insights gained, and mental health support interventions attempted. This provides cross-session continuity without requiring the full message history.

Current Session Summary:
A summary of the current session being simulated, extracted from the original conversation. This grounds the simulator in the specific topics and emotional tone of the target session, reducing aimless or disengaged responses. This session-specific grounding is critical for the simulator to cluster tightly with corresponding real sessions.

Raw Message History:
Message-by-message conversation history from recent sessions, providing detailed behavioral context and immediate conversational flow.

The complete context is truncated to 15,000 tokens, prioritizing recent session content and current session summary over distant history. All context components are automatically generated using production usage—user profiles from onboarding and session summaries after each conversation—with approximately 100,000 user profiles available for training and evaluation. This enables our approach to scale naturally with system usage without requiring manual annotation or synthetic data generation.

Appendix D
User Action Categories

This appendix provides example user action categories from our taxonomy used to annotate and analyze user behavior in mental health support conversations. Each user message is classified using GPT-5-nano. Actions are classified as positive mental health indicators (Good), negative indicators (Bad), or user features that should change over time. The full taxonomy contains 20+ categories; we highlight key examples below.

D.1
Example Positive Indicators

•

Gained an insight:
User expresses that they learned something new about themselves or about their situation.

•

Change talk:
User uses change talk to set a task goal or commit to a healthy action—something specific.

•

Agrees with chatbot:
User agrees with an observation, suggestion, or advice that the chatbot gives.

•

Chatbot created change:
User identifies something positive that has changed as a result of talking to the chatbot.

D.2
Example Negative Indicators

•

Disagrees with chatbot:
User disagrees with an observation, suggestion, or advice that the chatbot gives.

•

Asks for clarity:
User asks for the chatbot to clarify what it’s doing or saying.

•

Aggressive to chatbot:
User gets angry with the chatbot or is combative or abusive in some way.

•

Chatbot loses user:
User is not sure how to respond to the chatbot and responds in a way that suggests they’re disengaging.

D.3
Example User Features

•

General distress:
User reports discomfort or suffering that is vague and lacks a specific object or expresses not knowing who they are or what life means to them.

•

Negative affect:
User is able to acknowledge a negative emotion and make it explicit—a specific emotion must be identified; general expressions of negative affect fall under general distress.

•

Positive affect:
User expresses a positive emotion and makes it explicit—a specific emotion must be identified.

Appendix E
Issue Categories

This appendix provides example issue categories from our taxonomy used to evaluate chatbot quality. Issues are identified by GPT-5 analyzing entire conversation sessions with low reasoning effort. For each detected issue, the analyzer highlights the specific affected messages to enable proper credit assignment. The full taxonomy contains 35+ categories organized into six main categories (User Distress, User Confusion, User Frustration, User Losing Trust, Chatbot Violates Policies, App Bugs); we highlight key examples below.

E.1
User Distress Examples

•

Invalidating or dismissive framing:
The chatbot uses language that minimizes the user’s struggle or suggests they could simply choose to feel better (e.g., framing complex emotional states as mere choices).

•

Unempathetic responses:
The chatbot responds in ways that lack empathy in distressing or painful situations, including being overly brief, blunt, cold, detached, or otherwise failing to appropriately acknowledge or validate the user’s emotional state.

•

Premature wrap-up:
The chatbot initiates ending the session or wraps up the conversation when the user has explicitly expressed wanting to continue; it’s clear the user doesn’t want to end; the user is mid-sentence; or at otherwise inappropriate moments, such as immediately after the user has shared something sensitive or vulnerable.

E.2
User Confusion Examples

•

Chatbot hallucinates:
The chatbot invents or misremembers information about the user—such as their experiences, feelings, or history—and the user explicitly corrects or objects to the inaccuracy.

•

Unclear or confusing wording:
The chatbot says something that is unusually complex, convoluted, or strange, making it difficult for the user to understand the intended meaning or follow the flow of conversation.

•

Chatbot contradicts itself:
The chatbot makes statements or suggestions that directly contradict each other in meaning, tone, or advice, creating clear confusion or inconsistency that cannot reasonably be explained as exploring multiple perspectives.

E.3
User Frustration Examples

•

Circular conversations:
The chatbot leads the conversation in circles, revisiting the same topics or lines of discussion that have already been addressed, without adding new insight or progress. The issue applies only when the user shows clear signs of frustration or impatience with this repetition.

•

Ignoring user’s request to end session:
When the user clearly indicates they need to leave or pause or end the conversation (explicitly or implicitly), the chatbot ignores this and continues asking questions or advancing the discussion instead of acknowledging and closing promptly.

•

Repetitive questions:
The chatbot repeats a question or statement that is nearly identical to something it already said earlier in the same session, without any clear reason or therapeutic justification for the repetition.

E.4
User Losing Trust Examples

•

Chatbot gives bad advice:
The chatbot gives advice or suggestions that are objectively poor, unhelpful, or clearly irrelevant to the user’s situation—not merely advice that the user disagrees with or finds unappealing.

•

Chatbot is sycophantic:
The chatbot responds with excessive, exaggerated, or insincere praise or agreement that goes beyond normal empathy, validation, or encouragement. The issue applies only when the flattery or agreement is so strong or uncritical that it feels unnatural, manipulative, or disingenuous.

E.5
Policy Violation Examples

•

Failure to follow crisis protocol:
The chatbot does not provide required crisis resources, encourage immediate human help, or include the mandated disclaimer about AI limitations when suicide or imminent harm is mentioned.

•

Providing medical diagnoses:
The chatbot gives specific medical or mental health diagnoses, which it is not qualified to provide as an AI system.

•

Inappropriate self-disclosure:
The chatbot makes personal or biographical claims that go beyond light, clearly artificial expressions of personality. The issue applies only when the chatbot refers to having real human experiences, relationships, or memories in a way that blurs its AI identity.

E.6
App Bug Examples

•

Incomplete message:
The chatbot’s message is cut off or incomplete, typically due to streaming generation failure, leaving the response unfinished mid-sentence or mid-thought.

•

Transcription errors:
Speech-to-text conversion errors cause the chatbot to significantly misunderstand the user, leading to a noticeable impact on the conversation.
