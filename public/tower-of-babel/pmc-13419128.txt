P‐CAB vs. PPI for Upper Gastrointestinal Bleeding Prevention in Patients With Atherothrombotic Disease on Antithrombotic Therapy: A CDM Cohort Study
Authors: Min Joo Yoon, Soyoung Lee, Hye‐Kyung Jung, Min‐ho Kim, Suna Yu, Eui Sun Jeong, Chung Hyun Tae
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundRandomised trials have suggested the benefit of potassium‐competitive acid blockers (P‐CABs) is superior to proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) for ulcer recurrence in high‐risk aspirin users. However, real‐world comparative effectiveness across diverse antithrombotic regimens remains poorly defined.ObjectiveWe evaluated P‐CABs versus PPIs for preventing upper gastrointestinal (GI) bleeding in patients with acute atherothrombotic disease and using antithrombotic therapy.DesignThis retrospective cohort study utilised hospital‐based Common Data Model data (2018–2024). Patients with acute cardiovascular or cerebrovascular disease receiving antithrombotic therapy who initiated a PPI or P‐CAB were included. Drug exposure was modelled as a time‐varying variable to mitigate immortal‐time bias. The primary outcome was upper GI bleeding, analysed via time‐dependent Cox regression adjusted for age, sex, comorbidities, and concomitant medications.ResultsAmong 2255 patients (PPI: 1726; P‐CAB: 529) in which 53 upper GI bleeding events occurred during a median follow‐up of 637 days. P‐CAB use was associated with a significantly lower risk of upper GI bleeding than PPIs (incidence rate 5.7 vs. 25.8 per 1000 person‐year; adjusted hazard ratio [HR] 0.22, 95% CI 0.06–0.75, p = 0.016). P‐CABs showed a profound reduction in moderate‐to‐severe upper GI bleeding (HR 0.11, 95% CI 0.02–0.60; p = 0.011). Notably, no bleeding events occurred in P‐CAB users with high antithrombotic burden (≥ 2 agents).ConclusionsIn patients receiving antithrombotic therapy, P‐CABs are associated with a significantly lower risk of clinically significant GI bleeding compared to PPIs. These findings support P‐CABs as a potent acid‐suppressive strategy for gastroprotection in high‐risk populations.Keywords: cardiovascular diseases, cerebrovascular disorders, gastrointestinal Haemorrhage, potassium competitive acid blockers, proton pump inhibitors

Introduction

Cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases remain among the leading causes of morbidity and mortality worldwide, accounting for approximately one‐third of all deaths globally. In 2022, an estimated 19.8 million deaths were attributed to cardiovascular diseases, with more than 80% resulting from myocardial infarction and stroke [1, 2]. For secondary prevention in patients with established cardiovascular or cerebrovascular disease, long‐term antithrombotic therapy is essential to reduce the risk of recurrent ischaemic events [3]. However, the clinical utility of these agents is frequently compromised by an increased risk of upper gastrointestinal (GI) bleeding—a complication associated with treatment interruption, poor adherence, and increased cardiovascular mortality [4]. Current clinical guidelines recommend prophylactic proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) therapy in patients at high risk of upper GI bleeding who are receiving antithrombotic treatment [5]. Major US. cardiology and gastroenterology guidelines recommend PPI co‐therapy in patients receiving aspirin or dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) who are at increased risk of GI complications, including a history of peptic ulcer disease (PUD) or upper GI bleeding, older age, concomitant use of anticoagulants, nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), or corticosteroids, and Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) infection. In addition, expert consensus statements from the ACC (American College of Cardiology), ACG (American College of Gastroenterology), and AHA (American Heart Association) recommend considering PPI prophylaxis in patients receiving two or more antithrombotic agents to mitigate upper GI bleeding risk [5, 6]. Adequate intragastric acid suppression is vital in these patients because platelet aggregation and fibrin clot stability are markedly impaired in acidic environments. While PPIs irreversibly inhibit the H+/K+‐ATPase, their pharmacodynamic effect is limited by acid‐mediated activation and the need for active proton pumps, often resulting in delayed onset and inconsistent nocturnal acid control [7].

Potassium‐competitive acid blockers (P‐CABs) address these limitations by providing rapid, potent, and sustained acid suppression. Unlike PPIs, P‐CABs do not require acid activation and inhibit the H+/K+‐ATPase in a reversible, K+‐competitive manner independently of pump activation. These properties suggest that P‐CABs may offer superior gastroprotection over PPIs by maintaining a more stable intragastric pH, thereby facilitating mucosal healing and clot stabilisation [8]. These pharmacological properties suggest that P‐CABs may offer theoretical advantages over PPIs in high‐risk patients receiving antithrombotic therapy by promoting mucosal healing and clot stabilisation, thereby potentially reducing the risk of upper GI bleeding.

Evidence regarding the comparative effectiveness of P‐CABs and PPIs remains inconclusive. While randomised trials have suggested the superiority of P‐CABs in preventing aspirin‐induced peptic ulcer recurrence [9], large‐scale real‐world data have yet to consistently demonstrate a superior benefit in broader populations, such as those receiving diverse antithrombotic regimens after myocardial infarction or stroke [10].

In this study, we utilised a hospital‐based Common Data Model (CDM) to compare the gastroprotective efficacy of P‐CABs and PPIs in a cohort of patients with acute cardio‐ or cerebrovascular diseases. By employing a time‐dependent analytical framework to accurately reflect real‐world medication transitions, we aimed to provide robust evidence on the optimal acid‐suppressive strategy for mitigating upper GI bleeding risk in these high‐risk populations.

Methods

Data Source and Ethical Considerations

This study utilised de‐identified electronic health record (EHR) data from a single‐centre database converted into the Observational Medical Outcomes Partnership (OMOP) Common Data Model (CDM). The CDM framework ensures standardised definitions for diagnoses, procedures, and prescriptions. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Ewha Womans University Mokdong Hospital (IRB No. 2025–06‐023), which waived the requirement for informed consent due to the retrospective nature of the study and the use of anonymised data.

Study Population and Design

We conducted a retrospective cohort study of patients with cardiovascular or cerebrovascular diseases receiving antithrombotic therapy for secondary prevention between February 2018 and December 2024. Eligible patients were those who initiated either a PPI or a P‐CAB following their primary diagnosis and the commencement of antithrombotics. The index date was defined as the date of the first prescription of a PPI or P‐CAB. To focus on new users and ensure a robust baseline, we excluded patients with:

A history of cardio/cerebrovascular disease or antithrombotic use within one year prior to the index date (wash‐out period)

A history of cardio/cerebrovascular disease or antithrombotic use within one year prior to the index date (wash‐out period)

Total antithrombotic therapy duration of < 90 days to ensure clinical relevance of the exposure‐outcome association.

Total antithrombotic therapy duration of < 90 days to ensure clinical relevance of the exposure‐outcome association.

Less than one year of follow‐up to provide adequate time for outcome assessment

Less than one year of follow‐up to provide adequate time for outcome assessment

Exposure Assessment and Time‐Dependent Modelling

Exposure was defined by the prescription of PPIs (dexlansoprazole, ilaprazole, lansoprazole, omeprazole, pantoprazole, rabeprazole, esomeprazole, and s‐pantoprazole) or P‐CABs (fexuprazan, tegoprazan, and zastaprazan). Crucially, to reflect real‐world clinical transitions and mitigate immortal‐time bias, medication exposure was modelled as a time‐varying covariate. Patients were allowed to switch between PPI and P‐CAB cohorts during the follow‐up period based on their actual prescription records. For patients who experienced the primary outcome, the exposure was classified according to the specific agent being administered at the time of the event.

Definition of Clinical Outcome

The primary clinical outcome was upper GI bleeding, identified using ICD‐10 diagnosis codes for non‐variceal upper GI bleeding, including: peptic ulcer with haemorrhage (K25‐K28), haemorrhagic gastritis or duodenitis (K29), and unspecified gastrointestinal haemorrhage (K92x). Oesophageal or gastric variceal bleeding (I85x, I86.4) was excluded as it is not acid‐related.

Bleeding events were stratified by severity:

Severe: Requiring hospitalisation/ER (emergency room) visit with haemoglobin < 7.0 g/dL or systolic blood pressure < 90 mmHg.

Severe: Requiring hospitalisation/ER (emergency room) visit with haemoglobin < 7.0 g/dL or systolic blood pressure < 90 mmHg.

Moderate: Requiring hospitalisation/ER visit but not meeting severe criteria

Moderate: Requiring hospitalisation/ER visit but not meeting severe criteria

Mild: Managed without hospitalisation or transfusion.

Mild: Managed without hospitalisation or transfusion.

Statistical Analysis

Incidence rates (IRs) were calculated per 1000 person‐years (PY). Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated using a multivariable time‐dependent Cox proportional hazards model. To assess and ensure the generalised comparability of systemic comorbidities between the two cohorts, the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) was calculated for each patient. The model was further adjusted for age, sex, comorbidities (eg., prior PUD, CKD), and concomitant medications (NSAIDs, steroids, anticoagulants).

Sensitivity and Robustness Analyses: Propensity score matching (PSM) was intentionally omitted to preserve statistical power and avoid excessive loss of observations, particularly within the smaller P‐CAB cohort. Instead, we performed severity‐stratified analyses (moderate‐to‐severe vs. mild) and subgroup analyses based on antithrombotic burden (single vs. dual or more) to ensure the consistency of the findings. All analyses were performed using SAS (version 9.4) and R (version 4.3.1).

Ethical Considerations

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Ewha Womans University Mokdong Hospital (IRB No. 2025–06‐023). Informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study and the use of de‐identified data derived from the CDM database. All research procedures adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Baseline Clinical and Demographic Characteristics

A total of 15,716 patients admitted with cardiovascular or cerebrovascular diseases between February 1, 2018, and December 31, 2024, were screened. Among them, 5378 patients were cardiovascular and 10,884 were cerebrovascular patients. After applying inclusion criteria—specifically new initiation of acid‐suppressive therapy and at least 90 days of antithrombotic use—2255 patients were included in the final analysis (Figure 1). The median age was 67.5 ± 12.8 years, and 66.1% were male.

Study Population Flowchart. Selection process for the study cohort. Of 15,716 patients admitted with cardio‐ or cerebrovascular diseases, 2270 met the inclusion criteria of ≥ 90 days of antithrombotic therapy and concurrent PPI or P‐CAB prescription. A final analysis was performed on 53 upper GI bleeding events occurring within the one‐year index period.

Baseline profiles revealed that there was no significant difference in the mean CCI between the PPI and P‐CAB groups (2.3 ± 1.7 vs. 2.2 ± 1.5, p = 0.639), indicating that the overall systemic comorbidity burden was well‐balanced at baseline. However, the PPI group (n = 1726) carried a significantly higher risk for GI complications compared to the P‐CAB group (n = 529). Specifically, the PPI group had a higher prevalence of prior upper GI bleeding (8.2% vs. 3.6%, p < 0.001), previous PUD (8.7% vs. 4.9%, p = 0.005), and DAPT usage (45.6% vs. 27.7%, p < 0.001). Risk of Upper GI Bleeding: P‐CAB vs. PPI

During a median follow‐up of 637 days, 53 upper GI bleeding events were observed. The IR was significantly lower in the P‐CAB group (5.7 per 1000 PY; 95% CI: 1.54–14.46) than in the PPI group (25.8 per 1000 PY; 95% CI: 19.1–34.1). In the time‐dependent Cox regression analysis, P‐CAB use was associated with a 78% reduction in the risk of upper GI bleeding after adjusting for baseline confounders (adjusted HR: 0.22; 95% CI: 0.06–0.75; p = 0.016; Figure 2).

Subgroup analyses by primary diagnosis (cardiovascular vs. cerebrovascular) consistently showed numerically lower incidence rates in the P‐CAB group, although these did not reach statistical significance, likely due to the limited number of events in the P‐CAB cohort (Table 2).

Upper GI Bleeding Risk Stratified by Severity

The protective effect of P‐CABs was most pronounced in clinically significant bleeding events. For moderate‐to‐severe bleeding, the IR was 18.4 per 1000 PY in the PPI group versus 2.8 per 1000 PY in the P‐CAB group. P‐CAB therapy was associated with an 89% risk reduction for moderate‐to‐severe events (HR 0.11; 95% CI: 0.02–0.60; p = 0.011). In contrast, no significant difference was observed for mild bleeding (HR 0.63; 95% CI: 0.09–4.61, p = 0.653), suggesting that the benefit of P‐CABs is primarily driven by the prevention of severe clinical outcomes (Figure 3).

Subgroup Analysis by Antithrombotic Burden

The association between P‐CAB use and reduced upper GI bleeding risk remained consistent across levels of antithrombotic intensity. In patients on single antithrombotic therapy, the point estimate favoured P‐CAB (HR 0.44; 95% CI 0.03–5.73). Notably, among patients receiving dual or multiple antithrombotic agents—a subgroup at the highest risk for haemorrhage—no bleeding events occurred in the P‐CAB group (0/254), whereas the PPI group maintained an IR of 22.2 per 1000 PY (Table 3).

Discussion

In this hospital‐based cohort of patients with cardio‐ or cerebrovascular diseases receiving long‐term antithrombotic therapy, P‐CAB use was associated with a significantly lower risk of upper GI bleeding compared with PPIs. This protective association was consistently observed across multiple analytical approaches, including time‐dependent Cox regression, severity‐stratified analyses, and subgroup analyses according to antithrombotic burden.

Our study showed that P‐CAB therapy was associated with a 78% lower risk of overall upper GI bleeding compared with PPIs. This risk reduction was primarily driven by a marked decrease in moderate‐to‐severe bleeding events, whereas no statistically significant difference was observed for mild bleeding. These findings suggest that P‐CABs may be particularly effective in preventing clinically significant bleeding—events that carry the greatest impact on patient prognosis, healthcare utilisation, and the necessity of interrupting essential antithrombotic therapy. Interestingly, the incidence of upper GI bleeding in the PPI group was substantially higher despite a shorter cumulative person‐time in the P‐CAB group, further reinforcing the strength of the observed association.

Historically, PPIs have been the cornerstone of prophylaxis for upper GI bleeding in patients on antithrombotic agents [11, 12]. However, emerging evidence suggests that P‐CABs may offer superior mucosal protection through more potent and sustained acid suppression. Pharmacodynamic studies have demonstrated that P‐CABs achieve faster onset and more consistent intragastric pH control than PPIs, including during nighttime periods and in CYP2C19 extensive metabolizers, which may be a critical factor for high‐risk populations [13].

Several observational studies and post hoc analyses have reported lower rates of peptic ulcer recurrence and upper GI bleeding with P‐CABs compared with PPIs, especially among patients receiving aspirin or DAPT [14, 15]. In these two prior studies, use of either PPIs or P‐CABs was associated with a lower risk of upper GI bleeding compared with no acid‐suppressive therapy among patients with acute coronary syndrome undergoing stent implantation and receiving antithrombotic treatment, with no significant difference observed between PPIs and P‐CABs. In contrast, our study is the first, to our knowledge, to demonstrate a superior protective effect of P‐CABs over PPIs against upper GI bleeding in a broader population of patients with acute coronary syndrome and acute ischaemic stroke. This difference appears to be primarily driven by a significant reduction in moderate to severe upper GI bleeding events associated with P‐CAB use. This observation is of particular interest and aligns with previous findings in gastroesophageal reflux disease, whereby P‐CABs demonstrate similar efficacy to PPIs in mild reflux esophagitis, while providing superior efficacy in severe reflux esophagitis [16, 17]. From a clinical perspective, preventing moderate‐to‐severe bleeding is paramount, as these events often necessitate the cessation of antithrombotic therapy, thereby increasing the risk of major adverse cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events.

The validity of our findings is further strengthened by the consistency observed across antithrombotic regimens. Although statistical significance was not reached in the single‐agent subgroup, the direction of effect consistently favoured P‐CABs. Most notably, exceptionally low bleeding rates were observed in P‐CAB users receiving dual or multiple antithrombotic agents—a subgroup traditionally at the highest risk. While the low event rate in this group precluded formal hazard ratio estimation, the trend suggests that P‐CABs may be particularly beneficial for patients with a high antithrombotic burden.

This study has several methodological strengths. First, the application of a time‐dependent analytical framework mitigated immortal‐time bias and captured the dynamic nature of acid‐suppressive therapy. Second, the use of a hospital‐derived CDM allowed for granular assessment of GI bleeding severity and comprehensive capture of co‐medications, including anticoagulants and NSAIDs.

However, several limitations warrant consideration. First, confounding by indication is possible, as patients in the PPI group had a higher baseline prevalence of prior upper GI bleeding and PUD. While the overall systemic comorbidity burden, as measured by the CCI, was comparable between the two groups, the PPI group represented a clinically higher‐risk cohort specifically regarding gastrointestinal outcomes. This disparity likely reflects real‐world prescription patterns, where clinicians may favour established PPIs for patients perceived to have a higher GI‐specific risk. Similarly, although the use of concomitant medications such as steroids and NSAIDs was accounted for, we chose not to exclude these patients to better reflect the complexity of real‐world cohorts. However, despite the PPI group having a more unfavourable baseline profile (higher rates of DAPT and prior ulcers), the protective effect of P‐CABs remained robust. To ensure a rigorous comparison, we included these potent risk factors—prior PUD and upper GI bleeding history, and the use of steroids and NSAIDs—as covariates in our multivariable Cox regression models. The finding that P‐CABs remained significantly effective even after adjusting for these baseline imbalances and co‐medications further reinforces the robustness of our results. This is particularly evident in the model‐based survival analysis, where P‐CABs demonstrated a significantly higher estimated survival probability over 70 months of follow‐up (HR 0.22; 95% CI 0.06–0.75; p = 0.016). Our finding that the risk reduction was specifically pronounced in clinically significant (moderate‐to‐severe) events provides a compelling rationale for prioritising P‐CABs in high‐risk patients. Finally, as an observational study, residual confounding—including H. pylori status, medication adherence, and over‐the‐counter drug use—cannot be entirely excluded.

In conclusion, P‐CABs offer superior protection against clinically significant upper GI bleeding compared with PPIs in patients receiving long‐term antithrombotic therapy. Our results suggest that P‐CABs should be considered a potent alternative acid‐suppressive strategy for high‐risk patients, particularly those with a history of peptic ulcer disease or upper gastrointestinal haemorrhage, or high antithrombotic burden. Randomised controlled trials are warranted to confirm these findings and refine the role of P‐CABs in GI prophylaxis.
