Rapidly progressive spinal epithelioid malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumor following subtotal resection surgery: a case report
Authors: Baiyi Liu, Yaoyao Liu, Tengyu Wang, Yi Yang, Xiang Yin
Published: 2025

Background

Epithelioid malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumor (EMPNST) is a rare subtype of malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumor (MPNST), accounting for less than 5% of all MPNST cases. EMPNST can affect patients across a wide age range, from 6 to 80 years, with a mean age of 44 years. The most commonly affected sites for EMPNST are the lower limbs and trunk, with the majority of tumors located superficially [1]. Spinal EMPNST is exceedingly rare, with only a limited number of cases reported in the literature [2, 3].

The diagnosis of EMPNST is challenging and primarily relies on morphological features and immunohistochemical findings. Morphologically, EMPNST is characterized by a relatively uniform yet distinctly atypical population of epithelioid cells [1]. Immunohistochemically, most EMPNST cases express S-100 protein and SOX10 [4]. However, there have been reported cases that lack immunoreactivity for both S-100 and SOX10 [5]. Additionally, the loss of SMARCB1/INI1 expression is a significant immunohistochemical characteristic of EMPNST, observed in up to 70% of cases [6]. Herein, we present a rare case of spinal EMPNST that was negative for both S-100 protein and SOX10, while retaining expression of SMARCB1/INI1. Surprisingly, he experienced rapid tumor progression just 10 days after the initial surgery. Despite undergoing a second surgery, intraoperative radiotherapy (IORT) and postoperative radiotherapy, the patient unfortunately passed away soon after treatment.

Case presentation

A 67-year-old male patient presented to our hospital due to the right shoulder’s pain at July 8, 2023. The laboratory tests showed that the patient has high levels of alpha-fetoprotein (9.05 ng/ml), Cyfra21-1 (6.17 ng/ml), and carbohydrate antigen 199 (54.70 U/ml). Thoracic magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) (Fig. 1A and C) and computed tomography (CT) (Fig. 1D and E) revealed a T2 vertebral bone destruction and the presence of a paravertebral soft tissue mass; besides, the degree of vertebral compression was approximately 1/2, with spinal canal narrowing at the same level and spinal cord compression, suggesting a space-occupying lesion at the T2 vertebra. A 18 F-fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT) showed bone changes at the T2 vertebra and surrounding soft tissue formation, with increased FDG metabolism (SUVmax: 18.11, SUVavg: 4.52), raising suspicion of a malignant tumor (Fig. 1F).

Considering that most spinal tumors are metastatic and the patient had shown signs of spinal cord compression, a multidisciplinary consultation concluded that a combined strategy of subtotal resection surgery and adjuvant radiotherapy would be implemented. At July 18, the patient underwent T2 vertebral tumor subtotal resection, spinal canal decompression, and C7-T4 percutaneous pedicle screw fixation. Postoperatively, a biopsy of the T2 vertebral lesion was performed for H&E staining, which revealed numerous malignant tumor cells. Over 50% of these cells exhibited an epithelial-like appearance, characterized by abundant, clear cytoplasm and prominent nucleoli. Additionally, some local cells were short and spindle-shaped; Approximately 35 mitotic figures per 10 high-power fields (HPF) were identified (Fig. 2A). Immunohistochemically, the tumor cells were TP53 mutation (Fig. 2B), weak positive for cytokeratin (Fig. 2C), retained expression of H3K27me3 (Fig. 2D) and SMARCB1/INI1 (Fig. 2E), and the Ki-67 labeling index was about 80% (Fig. 2F). They were negative for HMB-45, ALK-1, CD23, CD30, CD34, desmin, epithelial membrane antigen (EMA), S-100, smooth muscle actin (SMA), Stat6 and Sox-10. Based on the imaging, pathological, and immunohistochemical results, the patient was diagnosed as EMPNST.

At July 21, digital X-ray radiography was performed to assess the position of the internal fixation, spinal stability, and potential early complications. No abnormities were observed (Figures are not shown). The patient had a good recovery and was discharged one week after surgery. Following discharge, oral cefuroxime axetil tablets (125 mg, bid) were prescribed to prevent infection. The patient was initially scheduled to receive adjuvant radiotherapy two weeks post-surgery; however, on the 10th day after the procedure, July 28, he suddenly noticed swelling at the surgical incision on his back, which was not accompanied by significant local tenderness but was associated with a significant fluctuating sensation upon palpation. He promptly sought treatment at our hospital’s spinal surgery outpatient department, where the swelling was initially suspected to be chylous fluid, cerebrospinal fluid leakage, or a hematoma. Dressing changes and fluid drainage for culture were performed (the bacterial culture returned negative). Approximately 300 ml of serosanguineous fluid was drained, with no purulent secretion observed. Urgent blood tests revealed a white blood cell (WBC) count of 19.38 × 109/L, a neutrophil percentage of 79.6%, a total neutrophil count of 15.43 × 109/L, a hypersensitive C-reactive protein (CRP) level of 96.45 mg/L, and an interleukin-6 (IL-6) level of 147.00 pg/ml. No other significant abnormalities were noticed. Oral cefuroxime axetil was continued.

At August 3, the patient developed swelling at the back incision and was readmitted to our hospital. The physical examination revealed a change in the nature of the swelling, transitioning from a fluctuating mass to one with reduced mobility and partially solid on palpation. On the same day, a distal drainage tube was placed at the surgical site for drainage (bacterial culture was negative). About 100 ml of serosanguineous fluid was drained, with no purulent secretion observed. Blood tests demonstrated the infection markers were decreased compared to the last test (WBC: 5.71 × 109/L; neutrophil percentage: 67.7%; CBP: 43.87 mg/L; and IL-6: 55.5pg/ml). At this moment, infection and cerebrospinal fluid leakage were suspected. Based on the consideration of preventing bacteria from crossing the blood-brain barrier and causing intracranial infection, vancomycin hydrochloride (1 g, q12h) combined with ceftriaxone (1 g, q24 h) was used for anti-infection. After 10 days of anti-infection treatment, the patient’s infection indicators were improved, and he was transferred to the oncology department. Three days later at August 16, a PET/CT scan demonstrated increased FDG metabolism (SUVmax: 24.82, SUVavg: 12.84) in the surgical area and surrounding soft tissues, suggesting a possibility of rapid tumor growth or postoperative infection (Fig.3A).

At August 17, the patient experienced fecal incontinence and progressive weakness in both lower limbs. Symptomatic treatments for dehydration and inflammation showed no significant relief. Enhanced spinal MRI (Fig. 3B and D) and CT (Fig.3E and G) were performed, suggesting tumor progression accompanied by spinal cord compression. Therefore, the patient was transferred back to the spinal surgery department for resection of the recurrent tumor and decompression of the spinal canal. Under three-dimensional navigation guidance, the Intrabeam applicator was positioned at the target site, delivering IORT at 8 Gy (5 mm depth, 26 s) to ensure oncological efficacy while preserving neurological safety. During the second surgery, we explored through the original incision and found extensive formation of a large amount of gelatinous, loose neoplasm in the subcutaneous tissue, accompanied by a small accumulation of serosanguineous fluid. This was different from the soft, mass-like soft tissue tumor excised during the first surgery. The new growth aggressively spread laterally along the area cleared during the first surgery of the T2 vertebral lesion, extending directly to the subcutaneous tissue, leading to extensive encasement and compression of the corresponding segments of the spinal cord. During the procedure, biopsies were taken from both the subcutaneous neoplasm and the T2 vertebral region. Postoperative pathology confirmed that both were neoplastic tissues (Fig.4A and B). The immunohistochemical results at this time were consistent with those from the first surgery (Figures are not shown). Postoperatively, the muscle strength in the right lower limb improved from grade 1 to grade 3, while there was no significant change in the left lower limb, which remained at grade 1.

At August 23, the patient was transferred to the oncology department, where the muscle strength in the right lower limb decreased again to grade 1, and the left lower limb was at grade 0. From August 23 to September 1, the patient received additional stereotactic radiosurgery treatments (Gross target volume: 44 Gy delivered over 8 fractions; Clinical target volume: 24 Gy delivered over 8 fractions) in conjunction with other symptomatic therapies; however, there was no improvement in muscle strength. Ultimately, the patient opted to discontinue further treatment and passed away at the end of September 2023. The timeline of relevant diagnoses and treatments is showcased in Fig.4C.

Discussion and conclusions

The overlapping features shared with other tumors that exhibit epithelioid cytomorphology pose significant diagnostic challenges for EMPNST. The differential diagnosis includes, but is not limited to, malignant melanoma, epithelioid sarcoma, myoepithelial carcinoma, and malignant rhabdoid tumor (MRT). Malignant melanomas can morphologically resemble EMPNST; however, they typically exhibit greater cytological atypia, characterized by large, prominent nucleoli that often appear reddish and frequently involve the epidermis. Both tumor types may express S-100 protein, but malignant melanomas usually demonstrate positive expression of MelanA and/or HMB45, which are generally absent in EMPNST [1, 7, 8]. Epithelioid sarcoma occasionally expresses S-100 and is characterized by more granulomatous structures and interstitial collagen fibers, features not observed in EMPNST. Immunohistochemically, epithelioid sarcoma expresses CK (AE1/AE3), EMA, and Vimentin, with some cases also showing expression of CD34 and CD31. In contrast, EMPNST typically does not express these markers [2, 4, 8]. Distinguishing myoepithelial carcinoma from EMPNST can be challenging, as myoepithelial carcinoma is also S-100 protein positive and may exhibit loss of SMARCB1/INI1 expression. However, myoepithelial carcinoma tends to display some cytokeratin and EMA positivity, in addition to expression of smooth muscle markers, which can aid in the diagnosis [1, 4, 7, 8]. MRT predominantly affects infants and children under the age of three, although it can occasionally occur in adults. This tumor primarily involves the neck, shoulders, trunk, paraspinal region, and limbs. Histologically, MRT is characterized by the presence of polygonal rhabdomyoid cells that exhibit poor intercellular adhesion. These cells typically have abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm, deeply stained large vacuolar nuclei, prominent nucleoli, and frequent mitotic figures. Although the morphological and immunohistochemical features (such as loss of SMARCB1/INI1) of MRT are similar to those of EMPNST, patient’s age and cytokeratin expression are helpful to differentiate MRT from EMPNST because the former usually occurs in infants and children and marks with cytokeratins [2, 4]. In the present case, immunohistochemical analysis of the tumor cells showed weak positivity for cytokeratin, with retained expression of H3K27me3 and SMARCB1/INI1, while being negative for S-100, Sox-10, HMB-45, ALK-1, CD23, CD30, CD34, Desmin, EMA, SMA, and Stat6. Based on all clinical features, imaging findings, epithelioid morphological characteristics, and immunohistochemical results, a diagnosis of EMPNST was ultimately established.

Currently, radical surgical resection of the tumor is regarded as the optimal treatment option for EMPNST, given the tumor’s aggressive infiltrative nature and potential for distant metastasis [9]. However, in cases of spinal EMPNST, radical surgical resection is not always feasible because of anatomical constraints. In such situations, partial resection followed by adjuvant radiotherapy may serve as a valuable alternative. In this case, the patient had exhibited signs of spinal cord compression prior to the diagnosis of EMPNST. Consequently, a combined approach involving subtotal resection and adjuvant radiotherapy was planned. Unfortunately, the patient experienced rapid tumor progression just 10 days after the initial surgery. Prior to the secondary surgery, a multidisciplinary consultation was conducted, leading to the establishment of a comprehensive plan that integrated surgical resection with IORT and postoperative radiotherapy.

IORT is an emerging therapeutic approach that can be precisely administered during surgery and has shown significant potential in the treatment of spinal tumors [10] and MPNST [11, 12]. The seamless integration of IORT with surgical intervention enables the delivery of a single high-dose irradiation directly to the tumor site, effectively targeting tumor cells that may be overlooked in the tumor cavity. The combination of surgery and IORT has been shown to significantly enhance neurological function in patients with spinal tumors, with approximately 90% of patients demonstrating marked improvement in neurological status within a week following IORT [13]. The Intrabeam 600 IORT system used in the present case is a low-energy (50 kV) photon therapy system. The RBE of 50 kV X-ray irradiation is 1.3 times that of 6 MV X-ray irradiation, meaning that a dose of 8 Gy from 50 kV X-ray irradiation is approximately equivalent to 10 Gy from 6 MV X-ray irradiation. Previous studies have found that exposure of the spinal cord to a single dose of MV-level X-rays exceeding 10 Gy poses a risk of radiation-induced spinal cord injury [14], while a prescribed dose of 8 Gy for IORT is effective in tumor control [15]. Therefore, an 8 Gy prescribed dose using the Intrabeam system for IORT was implemented for this patient.

For high-risk patients with incomplete tumor resection, postoperative radiotherapy is a crucial strategy to address the limitations of surgery and mitigate the risk of local recurrence. According to the NCCN Soft Tissue Sarcoma Radiation Therapy Guidelines, it is recommended to administer conventional fractionated radiotherapy after incomplete tumor resection, with a prescribed dose of 50 Gy delivered in 25 fractions (2 Gy per fraction) or 50.4 Gy in 28 fractions (1.8 Gy per fraction). An additional 20–25 Gy may be considered based on tumor type and the risk assessment of adjacent organs for any residual lesions. In this case, the patient had previously received a single dose of 8 Gy of IORT, equivalent to a biological dose of 14.4 Gy. Therefore, a larger fractionation scheme for postoperative radiotherapy utilizing the stereotactic body radiation therapy technique was prescribed, delivering 44 Gy to the gross target volume over 8 fractions and 24 Gy to the clinical target volume over the same number of fractions to achieve effective local control. Although previous evidence has indicated that adjuvant radiotherapy may beneficial for MPNST [16] and EMPNST [8], our case presents a contrasting outcome. The patient underwent IORT and postoperative radiotherapy during the second surgery, yet the results were disappointing, as evidenced by the lack of improvement in muscle strength, and the patient ultimately succumbed to the disease shortly after treatment.

To enhance our understanding of the clinical, immunohistochemical and treatment-related information for patients with spinal EMPNST, we searched the key terms “spine” or “spinal”, “epithelioid malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumor” and “case report” or “case series” in PubMed, Google Scholar and the Web of Science databases. Finally, only two cases of spinal EMPNSTs were reported in the literature. The information regarding these two reported cases, along with the current case, is summarized in Table 1.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. Firstly, drawing generalizable conclusions from a single case report is challenging. Secondly, we cannot rule out the potential influence of local infection or inflammation on tumor recurrence and treatment response. This limitation restricts the applicability of our conclusions to other spinal EMPNST patients with varying characteristics.

In summary, we present a rare case of spinal EMPNST that was negative for both S-100 protein and SOX10, while retaining expression of SMARCB1/INI1. Notably, the patient did not benefit from IORT and postoperative radiotherapy because these treatments failed to stop the fast tumor growth or enhance neurological results in this particular situation.
