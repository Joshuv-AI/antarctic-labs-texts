Trainee nurses and midwives’ perception and experiences of psychological safety and interactive communication in the classroom and clinical settings in Ghana
Authors: Olivia Nyarko Mensah, Elizabeth Boatemaa, Anita Fosuaa, Daprim Samuel Ogaji, Egya Gyanzah Eshun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPsychological safety is essential for effective learning, communication, and professional development in nursing and midwifery education. Environments lacking psychological safety hinder student engagement, reduce confidence, and negatively affect learning outcomes. Despite its importance, the concept remains underexplored in African educational and clinical contexts. This study examined trainee nurses’ and midwives’ perceptions and experiences of psychological safety and interactive communication in classroom and clinical settings in Ghana.MethodsAn embedded mixed-methods cross-sectional design guided by Good Reporting of a Mixed-Method Study (GRAMMS) checklist and Edmondson’s Psychological Safety Theory was employed. A total of 169 students were recruited using purposive and convenience sampling. Data were collected using a structured questionnaire adapted from Edmondson’s psychological safety scale (α = 0.79) with embedded open-ended questions. Quantitative data were analyzed using SPSS version 27, while qualitative data were analyzed using Braun and Clarke’s Thematic Analysis. Psychological safety levels were categorized as low (1.0–2.9), moderate (3.0–3.9), and high (≥ 4.0).Results/ findingsStudents reported moderate psychological safety in classroom settings (mean = 3.42, SD = 0.79) and lower psychological safety in clinical settings (mean = 3.05, SD = 0.86). Approximately 70% felt comfortable asking questions in class, while 49% reported fear of public reprimand during clinical training. Respectful communication, supportive supervision, and peer collaboration enhanced psychological safety, whereas hierarchical relationships, negative feedback practices, and fear of embarrassment hindered it.ConclusionPsychological safety varies across learning environments, with clinical settings presenting greater challenges. Institutional strategies should prioritize mentorship, supportive supervision, and communication training to improve learning outcomes and professional development.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09420-3.

Introduction

Psychological safety refers to an individual’s perception of being able to express ideas, ask questions, and admit mistakes without fear of embarrassment or retribution [1, 2]. In educational and clinical settings, its presence fosters open communication, engagement, and collaborative learning, while its absence results in fear, disengagement, and reduced performance [2].

In nursing and midwifery education, psychological safety is particularly important due to the dual learning environments, which are the classrooms and clinical settings, where students must actively engage, practice skills, and communicate effectively [3–5].

Additionally, when psychological safety is present, students demonstrate improved participation, confidence, and clinical competence [6]. Conversely, environments characterized by intimidation, fear of criticism, and hierarchical communication hinder learning and reduce professional growth.

Globally, psychological safety has been associated with improved learning outcomes, patient safety, and teamwork [7]. However, in many African contexts, including Ghana, the concept remains underexplored. Cultural hierarchies, power distance, and communication norms may influence how students experience psychological safety, particularly in clinical training environments [8, 9].

Understanding these dynamics is critical for improving nursing and midwifery education. This study, therefore, examines trainee nurses’ and midwives’ perceptions and experiences of psychological safety and interactive communication in classroom and clinical settings in Ghana.

Edmondson’s theory of psychological safety

The study is based on Edmondson’s theory of psychological safety, which posits that safe environments encourage interpersonal risk-taking and better interactive communications, thereby enhancing learning and innovation [10].

When interactive communication among trainees (students) and trainers is positive, it enhances psychological safety as a determinant of effective learning among nursing students, as it promotes confidence and reduces fear of humiliation [11–15]. However, when psychological safety was absent, students described disengagement, low confidence, and hesitancy in clinical practice, consistent with the observations.

Methods

Study design

This study employed an embedded mixed-methods cross-sectional design, guided by the Good Reporting of a Mixed-Method Study (GRAMMS) checklist [16] and underpinned by Edmondson’s Psychological Safety Theory. The design involved the concurrent collection of quantitative and qualitative data using a single integrated instrument, with qualitative responses embedded within the quantitative survey.

The embedded mixed-methods design was selected because psychological safety is a complex, context-dependent construct that requires both numerical measurement and experiential interpretation. Quantitative data provide measurable indicators (e.g., levels of psychological safety), while qualitative data offer deeper insights into students’ perceptions, emotions, and contextual realities. The embedded approach enhances complementarity, allowing qualitative findings to explain and enrich quantitative results. A cross-sectional design was appropriate as the study aimed to capture current perceptions and experiences at a specific point in time, across both classroom and clinical environments [17–19]. This approach allows for corroborating and supplementing findings, thereby increasing validity and depth. The study was guided by Amy Edmondson’s Psychological Safety Theory [10] and with added qualitative questions.

Study setting and population

The study was conducted at the Kwame Nkrumah University of Science and Technology (KNUST), Obuasi Campus, and three affiliated clinical training facilities: Obuasi Government Hospital, AngloGold Ashanti Health Foundation Hospital, and Bryant Mission Hospital. These sites serve as primary academic and clinical training environments for nursing and midwifery students. The study population comprised second, third, and fourth year nursing and midwifery students who had completed both classroom instruction and clinical placements. First-year students were excluded. The inclusion of both academic and clinical training sites ensured a comprehensive evaluation of psychological safety across learning contexts. Selecting students who had experienced both environments allowed meaningful comparison of perceptions. First-year students were excluded because they had not yet engaged in clinical training, which is central to the study objectives.

Sampling and sample size

Participants were recruited using a combination of purposive and convenience sampling techniques. A total of 169 students participated in the study. The sample size was determined using Cochran’s formula for proportions. Purposive sampling ensured that participants were information-rich, possessing relevant experience in both classroom and clinical settings. Convenience sampling facilitated efficient recruitment within the available timeframe and institutional structure. The use of Cochran’s formula [20] ensured that the sample size was statistically adequate to generate reliable estimates while maintaining feasibility.

Data collection instruments

Data were collected using a structured questionnaire adapted from Edmondson’s psychological safety scale [18]. The instrument consisted of both closed-ended Likert-scale items and embedded open-ended questions. The questionnaire comprised four sections: Section A: Demographic characteristics, Section B: Perceptions of psychological safety, Section C: Experiences of psychological safety, and Section D: Factors influencing psychological safety.

Responses were measured on a five-point Likert scale ranging from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (5). The reliability of the instrument was confirmed (Cronbach’s α = 0.79), which is above Cronbach’s α = 0.70. The use of a validated instrument enhances reliability and comparability with existing literature. The inclusion of open-ended questions allowed participants to articulate nuanced experiences, capturing dimensions of psychological safety that may not be reflected in quantitative scales. The integrated design minimized respondent burden and improved response completeness. The questionnaire [18] is provided as a supplementary file to enhance transparency and reproducibility. This measure assesses the extent to which a member of an organization feels psychologically safe to take risks, speak up, and discuss issues openly. With permission to use Edmonson’s original questionnaire was modified to suit a mixed-method approach with an embedded quantitative and qualitative components. The questionnaire was divided into sections, with each section having both closed-ended and open-ended questions.

Section A: Demographic Characteristics of Participants; Section B1: Psychological Safety in the Classroom; Section B2: Psychological Safety in the Clinical Environment; Section C: Experiences (Fear & Communication); Section D: Factors Influencing Psychological Safety; and Section E: Overall Experience.

Participants responded via a 5-point Likert scale that varied from “Strongly Disagree” (1), Disagree (2), Neutral (3), Agree (4), “Strongly Agree” (5). Hence, the data collection tool was also deemed reliable for our study. The questionnaire was put into Google Forms, which provided a user-friendly interface, easy distribution, and automatic data organization. A link to the questionnaire was shared with eligible participants through institutional WhatsApp groups, email, and other commonly used digital platforms. This approach increased response rates and ensured broader accessibility.

Pre-testing and validity

The questionnaire was pre-tested among a small group of student nurses who were not included in the main study. Feedback was used to refine clarity, wording, and structure. Pre-testing ensured content validity, clarity, and appropriateness of the instrument, reducing ambiguity and improving data quality.

Data collection procedure

Data collection for this study was conducted between 24th July 2025 and ended on the 25th August 2025 using online self-administered questionnaires. The method was chosen by the research team for its flexibility, cost-effectiveness, and ability to reach a wide sample within a short period. This method was deemed appropriate for student nurses and midwives who were distributed across different clinical and academic settings within the Obuasi Municipality.

The questionnaire was put into a Google Form by EB and AF under the supervision of ONM EB. A.F. collected the data, supported by ONM, which provided a user-friendly interface, easy distribution, and automatic data organization. A link to the questionnaire was shared with eligible participants through institutional WhatsApp groups, email, and other commonly used digital platforms only for student nurses and midwives in years 2, 3, and 4. This approach increased response rates and ensured broader accessibility.

Mixed‑methods integration

Although the quantitative and qualitative data sets were collected using a single instrument where one was embedded in the other, the analysis was done separately. Integration occurred during the interpretation stage, where qualitative findings were used to explain, expand, or contrast the quantitative results.

Data analysis

The quantitative data collected were analyzed using IBM SPSS version 27 with the effort and advice of the research team. Descriptive statistics in the form of frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations were computed to summarize responses. The qualitative responses were subsequently exported by EB, AF, EGE, and ONM after data collection using the Google Forms, which “creates a spreadsheet” option, which automatically produced a Google Sheets spreadsheet of all the responses of the participants. This helped to see all the open-ended responses within one row with their related respondents, as the team agreed that ID and pseudonyms were not needed due to the large numbers.

The spreadsheet was then exported into Excel format and sorted in order for the items of qualitative nature to be separated from the quantitative responses. All narrative commentaries were pooled into one qualitative dataset, screened to ensure its completeness.

The process of analysis used the Braun and Clarke [21, 22] six-step thematic analytical framework. The analytical process began with familiarization and immersion within the data, and initial codes were generated fluidly. Initial codes were logged, with records of how each theme evolved through discussion and refinement. Data obtained through a Google form were transferred to Excel and then imported to NVivo 14 (QSR International), a software for managing qualitative data, to facilitate the organization and analysis of narrative responses.

The data was systematically stored using NVivo 14, codes (nodes) were created, and the emerging ideas were sorted into meaningful categories. Preliminary coding was done by highlighting the important phrases and labelling them with words that summarized the experience of the participants. Subthemes and broader themes were formed by reviewing the codes, combining them, and broadening the themes in line with Braun and Clarke’s [21, 22] method of thematic analysis.

The NVivo tools, like node hierarchies, coding queries, and memo writing, helped in the refinement of themes, and they provided a clear audit trail during the analysis. The final themes were checked with the raw data to guarantee credibility and proper demonstration of the views of the participants.

Regular team meetings were held to review and challenge coding decisions, ensuring that the analysis was grounded in the data while allowing for flexibility in interpretation. Any discrepancies in theme development were resolved through collaborative discussion in the research team, ensuring that final themes were rigorous and reflexive.

Ethical considerations

Our study was conducted according to the guidelines and ethical principles for medical research involving human subjects, established by the World Medical Association (WMA) [23] in the Declaration of Helsinki (75th WMA General Assembly, Helsinki, Finland). Before the commencement of data collection, an introductory letter and supervisors’ support letters were obtained from the School of Nursing, respectively. Ethical clearance was sought from the Committee on Human Research, Publications and Ethics (CHRPE) of the Kwame Nkrumah University of Science and Technology (KNUST). The ethical clearance reference number issued is CHRPE/AP/635/25.

Information on the research and informed consent were embedded in the questionnaire. Informed consent was obtained before respondents answered the questionnaire. Participants were informed of their right to withdraw at any stage without repercussions. Qualitative data were anonymized without names and pseudonyms. They were stored securely, per institutional data protection policies, to protect participant identity and confidentiality. Researching trauma-related topics requires careful ethical handling, and efforts were made to create a psychologically safe space where participants could share their experiences without fear of judgment or harm.

Participants' narratives revealed a spectrum of experiences ranging from highly supportive and respectful environments to those where fear and hesitancy were prevalent. Through a rigorous thematic analysis process, these responses were examined and clustered into recurring patterns to highlight key barriers and enablers of psychological safety. The themes reinforced the quantitative findings: psychological safety was stronger in classrooms than in clinical settings and depended largely on interpersonal relationships. Theme 1: uneven psychological safety

A dominant theme emerging from the data was the inconsistency of psychological safety across different lecturers, supervisors, and learning settings. Students often reported that their willingness to participate was heavily dependent on the personality, approach, or perceived temperament of the educator leading the session. Each subtheme had excerpts of qualitative shared experiences from the respondents from different levels.

Subtheme 1.1: comfort in supportive environments

Participants highlighted that when tutors were approachable and clinical instructors created a non-judgmental atmosphere, they felt encouraged to ask questions and engage in discussions. This comfort fostered confidence and a stronger learning experience. The narrations below illustrate the views of some participants of the study:

“When my clinical instructor or preceptor smiled and told us we could always ask for help, I felt relaxed, and I knew then that I could do my best.”

“When my clinical instructor or preceptor smiled and told us we could always ask for help, I felt relaxed, and I knew then that I could do my best.”

Another response was that;

“It helps when lecturers show that they understand that mistakes happen. It makes you want to try again or take a risk of performing a skill that has been taught with supervision without any fear.”

“It helps when lecturers show that they understand that mistakes happen. It makes you want to try again or take a risk of performing a skill that has been taught with supervision without any fear.”

Sub-theme 1.2: fear and uncertainty in hierarchical settings

Some participants described clinical environments as tense and intimidating, especially where hierarchy was strongly emphasized. This led to hesitation in expressing uncertainty or seeking clarification. Below are some views of participants:

“Sometimes you want to ask, but the teacher’s tone scares you. You just want keep quiet but only for a moment but forever.”

“Sometimes you want to ask, but the teacher’s tone scares you. You just want keep quiet but only for a moment but forever.”

Another participant lamented in her write-up that;

“It’s like you can’t speak freely because they will think you’re not serious.”

“It’s like you can’t speak freely because they will think you’re not serious.”

Theme 2: fear of embarrassment

A second major barrier was the fear of public humiliation, particularly during clinical training where both staff and patients may be present. Students described how being corrected harshly or criticized openly in front of peers deterred them from asking questions or seeking clarification.

Sub-theme 2.1: anxiety in clinical settings

Students recalled feeling nervous about being corrected in front of peers or patients. Such experiences reduced their willingness to engage. Below are some views of participants:

“I become nervous whenever a lecturer is around. I don’t want to make a mistake and be shouted at.”

“I become nervous whenever a lecturer is around. I don’t want to make a mistake and be shouted at.”

Sub-theme 2.2: suppressed contributions due to perceived judgment

The perception that mistakes would attract ridicule made students avoid participation. Below is a view of a participant of the study:

“Sometimes, some of the clinical preceptors laugh and, in a way, mock us, especially when someone answers wrongly, so I prefer to learn as I watch a skill done and not to talk at all.”

“Sometimes, some of the clinical preceptors laugh and, in a way, mock us, especially when someone answers wrongly, so I prefer to learn as I watch a skill done and not to talk at all.”

Students repeatedly emphasized the importance of respectful communication and approachable faculty in creating psychologically safe learning spaces. Faculty who actively invited participation, listened attentively, and responded with patience were perceived as fostering confidence and openness.

Participants appreciated when tutors used encouraging words and demonstrated patience.

Frankly, that alone motivated me.”

Feedback given in a constructive tone helped students improve without feeling belittled.

Students described two contrasting approaches to handling mistakes: constructive feedback that fostered learning versus punitive or humiliating correction that discouraged participation.

Participants valued tutors who corrected errors gently and provided guidance on improvement.

“When they explain what you did wrong and show you the right way, it helps a lot.”

Conversely, scolding or public criticism led to feelings of humiliation and reduced participation.

Another important enabler of psychological safety was the degree to which students were included in decision-making, care activities, and academic discussions.

Supportive instructors encouraged open dialogue, enhancing students’ sense of belonging.

Discussion

Psychological safety is increasingly recognized as a critical factor influencing learning, communication, and professional development in nursing and midwifery education. The present findings align with existing evidence indicating that students’ experiences of psychological safety vary across learning environments, with relatively higher levels reported in classroom settings compared to clinical placements. This suggests that contextual factors, including teaching approaches and interpersonal dynamics, play an important role in shaping students’ engagement and participation.

Consistent with prior studies [24–28], the findings highlight that respectful communication and approachable educators are key enablers of psychological safety. In contrast, fear of embarrassment, perceived negative feedback, and hierarchical interactions may limit students’ willingness to speak up, particularly in clinical settings [15]. Some participants described communication styles that involved raised voices or limited opportunities for dialogue, which appeared to influence their confidence in asking questions or seeking clarification. Interactions with experienced staff were sometimes perceived as intimidating, reflecting broader concerns in the literature about power dynamics within clinical training environments.

Interpreted through Edmondson’s Psychological Safety Theory, these findings emphasize the importance of mutual respect, trust, and inclusive communication in promoting effective learning behaviours. Lower psychological safety in clinical settings may be associated with challenges in interpersonal communication and limited opportunities for active participation. This is consistent with findings by Muliira and Natarajan [9], who reported that power distance negatively influences student engagement, as well as Fadana and Vember [29], who found that staff attitudes significantly shape students’ learning experiences.

The study also suggests that limited opportunities for open dialogue may affect students’ ability to actively engage in learning processes, potentially influencing their communication skills and clinical competence. In contrast, environments characterized by constructive feedback and inclusive practices appear to support greater student participation and confidence.

Mentorship and supervision emerged as important factors in enhancing psychological safety. Evidence from Van Schaik, Plant, and O’Brien [7] indicates that students supervised by trained preceptors report greater confidence and a stronger sense of safety in clinical settings. Similarly, the present findings suggest that structured mentorship can help mitigate hierarchical barriers and improve learning experiences.

In addition, peer support was identified as a key contributor to psychological safety, indicating that the learning environment extends beyond educator–student interactions. Positive peer relationships can foster a sense of belonging, encourage participation, and enhance students’ confidence in expressing themselves.

Overall, the findings underscore the need for continuous professional development for educators and clinical staff to promote empathetic communication, inclusive teaching practices, and supportive supervision. Integrating psychological safety into educational curricula and institutional policies may enhance student engagement, learning outcomes, and professional development in both classroom and clinical settings.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the study included only trainee nurses and midwives; therefore, the perspectives of lecturers, clinical educators, and preceptors were not captured. This may limit a comprehensive understanding of psychological safety and interactive communication within the broader teaching and clinical supervision context.

Second, although the study employed an embedded mixed-methods design, modifying the quantitative Edmondson’s Psychological Safety Scale to incorporate qualitative components presented methodological challenges. In particular, the integration of open-ended responses within a structured survey limited opportunities for follow-up clarification, and it was not feasible to conduct member checking to fully validate participants’ interpretations and meanings.

Third, the study did not compare psychological safety across institutions or clinical sites, which may limit contextual interpretation of the findings. Differences between settings may therefore not have been fully explored.

Additionally, the reliance on self-reported data introduces the possibility of social desirability bias, where participants may provide responses they perceive as acceptable rather than reflecting their true experiences. Finally, the cross-sectional design of the study limits the ability to establish causal relationships between psychological safety and learning outcomes.

Despite these limitations, the study provides valuable insights into the perceptions and experiences of trainee nurses and midwives, highlighting important areas for improvement in both classroom and clinical learning environments.

Conclusion

The study has indicated that the right environment must be created in the classrooms and also at the clinical sites where student nurses and midwives are trained. It has also been established that ensuring psychological safety in the classroom and the clinical sites has a larger influence on the achievement of learning outcomes. Promoting psychological safety should therefore be a strategic educational and institutional priority to improve student professional development. Based on the findings of the study, it is recommended that open dialogue be encouraged in classrooms and also at the clinical sites, and training in emotional intelligence be encouraged among educators and clinical facilitators.
