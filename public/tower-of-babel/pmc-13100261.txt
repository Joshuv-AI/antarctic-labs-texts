Preoperative Diagnostic Factors Associated With Early MCID Achievement in BCTQ Outcomes Following Carpal Tunnel Release
Authors: Brenda Iglesias, Jacob Weinberg, Christopher Gonzalez, Adway Gopakumar, Kikelola Afolabi-Brown, Xinyi Dan, Yan Ma, John Fowler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo evaluate whether commonly obtained preoperative diagnostic findings are associated with early clinically meaningful improvement in patient-reported outcomes after carpal tunnel release (CTR) and to assess the durability of any observed associations across early postoperative follow-up.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included patients who underwent CTR between 2012 and 2020 and completed standardized preoperative evaluation with carpal tunnel syndrome-6 scoring, ultrasound, and electrodiagnostic testing, along with Boston Carpal Tunnel Questionnaire assessments before surgery and at 2 and/or 6 weeks after surgery. Clinically meaningful improvement was defined using established minimal clinically important difference (MCID) thresholds for the Symptom Severity Scale and Functional Status Scale. Associations among preoperative diagnostic findings, demographic variables, and postoperative outcomes were evaluated using univariate, exploratory multivariable, and longitudinal generalized estimating equation analyses.ResultsNinety-nine patients were included. Boston Carpal Tunnel Questionnaire symptom and function scores improved at all postoperative timepoints, with more than half of patients achieving MCID by 6 weeks. At 2 weeks, abnormal sensory nerve action potential amplitude was associated with smaller functional improvement, whereas select demographic factors were associated with MCID achievement. At 6 weeks, prolonged distal sensory latency and distal motor latency were associated with higher odds of achieving Symptom Severity Scale MCID, and prolonged distal sensory latency was associated with greater symptom and functional score reductions. However, associations varied by outcome domain and timepoint, and no variable demonstrated a consistent longitudinal association with clinically meaningful improvement.ConclusionsEarly recovery after CTR is characterized by substantial symptomatic and functional improvement but marked interpatient variability. Although select preoperative diagnostic and demographic factors demonstrated time-limited associations with early outcomes, when treated as cutoff values, no variable being abnormal reliably predicted clinically meaningful improvement in this cohort across early follow-up. These findings support the use of preoperative diagnostic testing cutoff thresholds primarily for diagnostic confirmation and clinical decision-making rather than for prognosticating early postoperative recovery.Type of study/level of evidencePrognostic IV.Key words: Boston Carpal Tunnel Questionnaire, Carpal tunnel syndrome, Electrodiagnostic testing, Minimal clinically important difference, Prognostic factors

Carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS) is the most common peripheral compressive neuropathy, affecting an estimated 1% to 5% of the population.1,2 Carpal tunnel release (CTR) is the standard surgical treatment, yet postoperative outcomes vary substantially; although many patients achieve complete symptom resolution, others continue to experience persistent or recurrent symptoms.3 Multiple preoperative diagnostic tools, each widely used in clinical practice, exist to aid in diagnosing CTS. The CTS-6 clinical scoring system integrates symptom characteristics and physical examination findings.4 Ultrasound provides cross-sectional area (CSA) measurements of the median nerve to evaluate nerve swelling, and electrodiagnostic testing, including distal sensory latency (DSL), distal motor latency (DML), sensory nerve action potential (SNAP), and compound muscle action potential (CMAP), quantifies the degree and nature of nerve conduction impairment.5 These modalities are well validated for diagnostic use; however, evidence regarding their prognostic utility for predicting postoperative functional recovery remains limited.6

Postoperative outcomes are commonly assessed using the Boston Carpal Tunnel Questionnaire (BCTQ), which includes the Symptom Severity Scale (SSS) and Functional Status Scale (FSS). In addition to absolute score reductions, thresholds for minimal clinically important difference (MCID) provide a patient-centered benchmark for meaningful improvement. One validated method defines MCID as a reduction of ≥0.46 × baseline SSS and ≥0.28 × baseline FSS, thereby scaling the threshold to individual preoperative severity.7,8 This approach acknowledges that patients with more severe baseline symptoms require a larger absolute improvement to perceive meaningful benefit.

Although CTS-6, ultrasound, and electrodiagnostic tests have been extensively studied for diagnostic purposes, few investigations have examined their ability to predict clinically notable postoperative improvement. This gap limits clinicians’ prognostic ability to provide individualized counseling based on preoperative findings. Therefore, the aim of this study was to evaluate whether preoperative diagnostic test results predict short-term postoperative outcomes as measured by BCTQ scores and MCID achievement. We hypothesized that abnormal preoperative test results would be associated with higher odds of achieving clinically meaningful improvement at early postoperative time points.

Materials and Methods

Study cohort

A retrospective cohort analysis was performed of patients who underwent CTR for clinically diagnosed CTS at a single tertiary academic center between 2012 and 2020. Patients were identified from a prospectively maintained institutional database in which patients underwent standardized preoperative evaluation, including CTS-6 clinical scoring, ultrasound assessment of median nerve CSA, electrodiagnostic testing, and baseline BCTQ assessment.

Patients were included if they completed preoperative diagnostic testing and had at least 1 postoperative BCTQ assessment at 2 and/or 6 weeks. Patients were excluded if they underwent revision CTR, had prior ipsilateral wrist surgery, were missing required preoperative diagnostic data, or had no postoperative outcome data. This study was approved by the University of Pittsburgh Institutional Review Board (STUDY20070308) with waiver of informed consent and Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act authorization. The investigation was conducted in accordance with Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines.

Preoperative diagnostic assessments

Preoperative evaluation incorporated clinical, imaging, and electrodiagnostic testing. Carpal tunnel syndrome-6 scores of 12 or greater were considered abnormal. Ultrasound was used to measure median nerve CSA at the carpal tunnel inlet, with values of 10 mm2 or greater classified as abnormal. Electrodiagnostic testing included assessment of DML, DSL, SNAP, and CMAP; however, not all electrodiagnostic parameters were available for every patient. Abnormal thresholds were defined as DML ≥4.0 ms, DSL ≥3.5 ms, SNAP amplitude ≤6.0 μV, and CMAP amplitude ≤10.0 μV.

Outcomes

Outcomes were derived from the BCTQ, including the SSS and FSS, collected before surgery and at 2 and 6 weeks after surgery. Longer-term follow-up was not analyzed because of substantial attrition.

Postoperative improvement was evaluated using 2 complementary outcome definitions. Clinically meaningful improvement was assessed using established MCID thresholds scaled to baseline severity (≥0.46 × baseline SSS and ≥0.28 × baseline FSS) as described by Deekleermaker, where they suggest that values for MCID derived from baseline SSS or FSS scores are more accurate than a fixed cutoff value for MCID.7 Continuous change in SSS and FSS scores from baseline was analyzed as a secondary outcome to characterize the magnitude and direction of early postoperative symptom and functional improvement.

Statistical analysis

Patient demographics, including sex as documented in the electronic medical records, baseline characteristics, and diagnostic findings were summarized using descriptive statistics. Continuous variables were reported as means with standard deviations (SDs) and categorical variables as counts with percentages. All preoperative diagnostic tests, including electrodiagnostic parameters, ultrasound CSA, and CTS-6 score, were analyzed as dichotomous clinical variables categorized as normal or abnormal based on predefined clinical cutoff thresholds, and analyses evaluating diagnostic severity as continuous predictors were not performed. Minimal clinically important difference achievement was considered the primary outcome, whereas continuous score reduction was analyzed as a secondary outcome to contextualize improvement magnitude given the baseline severity–dependent nature of MCID thresholds.

Associations between baseline variables and postoperative outcomes were evaluated using regression-based methods. Univariate logistic regression was used for MCID outcomes and univariate linear regression for continuous score changes, with results reported as odds ratios or regression coefficients with 95% CIs.

Exploratory multivariable analyses were performed using forward stepwise selection (P = 0.05), incorporating preoperative diagnostic measures and demographic covariates. Given the limited sample size and correlation among variables, these models were considered exploratory and not intended to establish predictive models.

Longitudinal associations across 2 and 6-week follow-up were evaluated using generalized estimating equations with an exchangeable working correlation structure to account for within-patient correlation of repeated postoperative measurements. These models allowed the inclusion of patients with observations at 1 or more postoperative timepoints. All statistical tests were 2-tailed, and statistical significance was defined as P < .05.

Results

Demographic data

A total of 680 patients were evaluated before surgery for CTS within a prospectively maintained institutional database. Among these, 288 patients underwent CTR and had at least 1 documented postoperative follow-up visit. Application of predefined eligibility criteria, including complete preoperative diagnostic testing and at least 1 postoperative BCTQ assessment at 2 and/or 6 weeks, yielded 148 patients with diagnostic data and a final analytic cohort of 99 patients. Clinical exclusions, including revision CTR and prior ipsilateral wrist surgery, were applied as defined in the methods section (Table 1). The mean age was 54.6 years, 71% of patients were women, and the mean body mass index was 33.9 kg/m2. Mean baseline BCTQ scores were 3.14 for the SSS and 2.63 for the FSS.

BCTQ score reductions and MCID achievement

Postoperative BCTQ data were available for 78 patients (79%) at 2 weeks and 49 patients (49%) at 6 weeks (Table 2). Forty-two of 78 patients (54%) achieved MCID derived from baseline scores for SSS, and 37 of 77 patients (48%) achieved MCID for FSS. At 6 weeks, MCID was achieved by 28 of 49 patients (57%) for SSS and 30 of 49 patients (61%) for FSS. Mean SSS reduction was 1.43 ± 0.89 at 2 weeks and increased to 1.60 ± 0.86 at 6 weeks. Mean FSS reduction was 0.58 ± 1.38 at 2 weeks and 1.08 ± 1.08 at 6 weeks.

Discussion

Carpal tunnel release results in substantial early symptomatic and functional improvement, yet recovery trajectories vary across patients. In this study, we evaluated whether commonly obtained preoperative diagnostic findings and patient characteristics are associated with early clinically meaningful improvement after CTR, using both MCID attainment and raw changes in BCTQ symptom and function scores. Preoperative diagnostic variables were analyzed using clinically defined abnormal versus normal cutoff thresholds rather than continuous measures of diagnostic severity. Although several baseline variables demonstrated statistically significant associations with early postoperative outcomes, these relationships were time-limited and domain-specific. No preoperative diagnostic or demographic variable demonstrated a consistent longitudinal association with clinically meaningful improvement across early follow-up in this limited cohort with mixed follow-up timing.

Prognostic value of electrodiagnostic testing

Electrodiagnostic findings demonstrated time-dependent associations with early postoperative recovery. At 2 weeks, abnormal SNAP amplitude was associated with smaller improvements in functional status, suggesting delayed early functional recovery in patients with sensory axonal involvement. However, this association was confined to a single functional outcome, did not extend to symptom severity or MCID attainment, and did not persist at later follow-up.

In contrast, electrodiagnostic parameters were more frequently associated with outcomes at 6 weeks, particularly for symptom-based measures. Prolonged DSL and DML were each associated with higher odds of achieving symptom-based MCID, and prolonged DSL was also associated with greater reductions in both symptom and functional scores. Similar findings have been reported in select cohorts, including Dennerlein et al, who observed greater symptom improvement among patients with longer distal motor and sensory latencies.9 Other studies have suggested that reduced sensory amplitudes may be associated with slower or less complete recovery.10

Despite these observations, electrodiagnostic measures did not demonstrate consistent longitudinal relationships with postoperative improvement. The broader literature has generally failed to demonstrate reproducible prognostic associations between preoperative nerve conduction abnormalities and postoperative patient-reported outcomes after CTR.11,12 Collectively, these results suggest that electrodiagnostic measures may capture aspects of disease severity or recovery timing, but when treated as a diagnostic cutoff, do not provide reliable prognostic discrimination for clinically meaningful postoperative improvement.

Ultrasound and clinical diagnostic measures

Preoperative ultrasound findings did not demonstrate consistent associations with either MCID attainment or the magnitude of early postoperative improvement. This aligns with prior work showing that median nerve CSA is valuable for diagnostic confirmation but does not reliably predict postoperative symptoms or functional recovery.13 Similarly, CTS-6 clinical scores were not associated with early postoperative outcomes in adjusted or longitudinal analyses. Given the conceptual overlap between CTS-6 and BCTQ symptom constructs, CTS-6 may provide limited incremental prognostic information in this context.

Demographics

Demographic variables demonstrated limited, time-dependent associations with early postoperative recovery. At 2 weeks, male sex was associated with lower odds of achieving symptom-based MCID and smaller SSS reductions. This finding was confined to symptom outcomes, was not observed at 6 weeks or longitudinally, and contrasts with much of the existing literature, raising the possibility of residual confounding or baseline severity effects.14, 15, 16

Increasing age was associated with lower odds of achieving FSS MCID at 2 weeks, consistent with prior work suggesting slower early recovery among older patients.14,17,18 However, this association was not observed at 6 weeks or longitudinally, suggesting age may influence early recovery tempo rather than overall benefit.

At 6 weeks, higher BMI was associated with symptom-based MCID achievement and greater SSS reduction, whereas diabetes mellitus was associated with greater SSS reduction only. Prior studies evaluating obesity and diabetes as prognostic factors after CTR have reported mixed findings, and associations in the present cohort were symptom-specific and time-limited.15,16,19

Socioeconomic factors and access to care may influence disease severity at presentation and postoperative outcomes. Prior studies have shown that socioeconomically disadvantaged patients often present with more advanced disease and worse baseline and postoperative patient-reported outcomes, potentially reflecting delays in diagnosis, referral, or treatment.20, 21, 22 The present study was not designed to evaluate socioeconomic or insurance-related disparities, as these variables were not consistently available. Future studies incorporating standardized measures of socioeconomic status and access to care may better contextualize postoperative recovery.

Clinical implications

Preoperative clinical, ultrasound, and electrodiagnostic testing remain essential for confirming the diagnosis and characterizing disease severity in CTS. However, their utility for predicting early clinically meaningful improvement after CTR appears limited. Although several baseline diagnostic variables demonstrated statistically significant associations with early postoperative outcomes, these relationships were inconsistent across outcome domains and follow-up timepoints, and no variable demonstrated a consistent longitudinal association with clinically meaningful improvement. This pattern mirrors prior literature reporting mixed and largely nonreproducible prognostic associations between preoperative diagnostic findings and postoperative patient-reported outcomes after CTR.16,23,24 Clinically, these findings support the use of preoperative diagnostic tests primarily for diagnostic confirmation and surgical decision-making rather than for forecasting early postoperative improvement. Prognostic counseling should emphasize the high likelihood of symptomatic benefit following CTR while acknowledging interpatient variability in early recovery.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. High attrition at later follow-up intervals limited statistical power for longitudinal analyses and reduced precision for detecting modest associations. As a retrospective analysis, no a priori power calculation was performed, and nonsignificant findings should be interpreted cautiously.

Several electrodiagnostic associations were accompanied by wide CIs, reflecting limited precision related to sample size and event frequency; these findings should be interpreted as exploratory. The single-center design may limit generalizability, and multivariable analyses employing forward stepwise selection are sensitive to sample size and correlated predictors.

An additional limitation relates to the dichotomization of preoperative diagnostic variables using clinically defined cutoff thresholds. This approach emphasizes diagnostic classification rather than the degree of abnormality and may obscure associations between diagnostic severity and postoperative recovery. In particular, the CTS-6 threshold functions as a clinical indicator rather than a strict diagnostic boundary. Analyses evaluating diagnostic severity gradients were not performed.

Differential follow-up patterns may also introduce selection bias. Although a 2-week follow-up is standard after CTR, a 6-week follow-up is often performed on an as-needed basis; patients returning at 6 weeks may represent nonrandom subsets. Finally, socioeconomic status and insurance coverage could not be adequately assessed because of inconsistent documentation, limiting the evaluation of access-related disparities.
