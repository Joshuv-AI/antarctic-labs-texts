Mortality in Patients with Acquired Perforating Dermatosis: Results from the First Multicenter Case-Control Study
Authors: Sebastián Reyes-García, Francisco Vázquez-López, Cristina Galache-Osuna, Blanca Vivanco-Allende, Carmen González-Vela, Jorge Santos-Juanes, Marcos A. González-López
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionMortality data in patients with acquired perforating dermatosis (APD) are limited.ObjectivesTo assess, for the first time, the risk of all-cause mortality in adult patients with APD and to explore associated systemic comorbidities.MethodsThis retrospective multicenter case-control study included adult patients diagnosed with APD and age-, sex-, and year-matched controls. We evaluated overall survival and the prevalence of seven major comorbidities: systemic malignancy, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), severe liver disease, diabetes mellitus, arterial disease, hypertension, and dyslipidemia. Statistical analyses included univariate comparisons (Fisher’s exact test and logistic regression), Kaplan–Meier survival estimates with log-rank tests, and multivariate Cox proportional hazards models.ResultsFifty-one patients with APD and 102 controls (1:2 ratio) were finally included in this investigation. Patients with APD had a significantly increased risk of mortality compared to controls ( log-rank test, P<0.001). In the multivariate Cox regression analysis, arterial disease emerged as the sole independent predictor of mortality (adjusted hazard ratio [HR]:7.716; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 2.774–21.464; P<0.001).ConclusionsThis is the first study to identify a potential association between APD and increased allcause mortality, primarily driven by coexisting arterial disease. These findings highlight the importance of comprehensive management in APD patients and emphasize the dermatologist’s role in multidisciplinary care aimed at improving long-term outcomes.

Introduction

Acquired perforating dermatoses (APD) comprise a pruritic spectrum of benign cutaneous disorders that remain poorly studied. Uncontrolled studies have consistently associated APD with several systemic comorbidities, most notably diabetes mellitus (DM), advanced chronic kidney disease (CKD), or both [1–14], and one controlled study has also suggested a link with malignancy [15]. It has been hypothesized that patients with APD may be at increased risk of mortality due to complications arising from these systemic conditions. However, this assumption is supported only by data from a limited number of uncontrolled case series [2–4].

To date, no systematic investigation has been conducted to determine whether comorbid diseases influence survival in patients with APD, despite its potential clinical implications. To address this knowledge gap, we conducted a multicenter retrospective case-control study to investigate, for the first time, the survival outcomes of patients diagnosed with APD. In addition, we evaluated the prevalence of seven clinically relevant systemic comorbidities: systemic cancer, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), severe liver disease, diabetes mellitus, arterial disease, hypertension, and dyslipidemia

Objectives

To assess, for the first time, the risk of all-cause mortality in adult patients with APD and to examine associated systemic comorbidities.

Patients and Methods

This retrospective case-control study was conducted at two tertiary referral centers in northern Spain: Hospital Universitario Central de Asturias (HUCA, Oviedo, Asturias) and Hospital Universitario Marqués de Valdecilla (Santander, Cantabria). Adult patients with a clinical and histopathological diagnosis of acquired perforating dermatosis (APD) were identified and compared with exactly matched controls (1:2 ratio), randomly selected from the histopathology registry of patients diagnosed with seborrheic keratosis (SK) at the same institutions between 01 January 2000 and 01 January 2023. Controls were matched by sex, age (±2 years), and year of diagnosis (±2 years).

Following the classification proposed by Rapini et al., APD in this study included acquired reactive perforating collagenosis (RPC), Kyrle disease (KD), and elastosis perforans serpiginosa (EPS), while perforating folliculitis (PF) was excluded [12,13].

The analysis focused on nontraumatic mortality and the presence of seven predefined systemic comorbidities, each confirmed by clinical diagnosis and treatment. These comorbidities included: systemic (non-cutaneous) cancer, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) or asthma, severe liver disease (SLD), diabetes mellitus (DM), arterial disease (AD), encompassing coronary artery disease, cerebrovascular disease, aortic aneurysm, peripheral artery disease, carotid artery stenosis, lower extremity artery disease, and multifocal arterial disease, hypertension (HT), and dyslipidemia (DL). Outcomes were assessed through the date of death, last available follow-up, or study end date (01 January 2023). Patients with known cancer-predisposing syndromes were excluded. Univariate results on systemic cancer frequency in this cohort have been previously reported [15].

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 27.0 (Armonk, NY, USA). Analyses included Fisher’s exact test, Mann–Whitney U test, univariate logistic regression, Kaplan–Meier survival analysis with log-rank test, and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression. Variables included in the multivariate model were those found to be significant in univariate analysis as well as APD diagnosis, sex, and age at diagnosis. Results are reported as odds ratios (ORs) or hazard ratios (HRs), with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A two-tailed p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. The use of SK-diagnosed individuals as controls has been previously validated in a comparable context [16].

Results

Table 1 summarizes the demographic characteristics, prevalence of comorbidities, and mortality outcomes of patients with APD and their matched controls. First, and notably, a significantly higher rate of nontraumatic mortality was observed in the APD group (19/51; 37.25%) compared to controls (11/102; 10.78%) (univariate analysis: P<0.001; odds ratio [OR]: 4.912; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 2.110–11.432). Kaplan–Meier survival analysis confirmed a significantly reduced overall survival in patients with APD (log-rank test, P<0.001) (Figure 1). Secondly, in terms of comorbidity burden, univariate analysis revealed significant associations between APD and several systemic conditions, including DM, CKD, HT, DL, and AD (Table 1). Finally, as shown in Table 2, several comorbidities were significantly associated with mortality in both univariate and multivariate analyses. In the adjusted Cox proportional hazards model, AD was the only comorbidity independently associated with increased mortality risk (adjusted HR: 7.716; 95% CI: 2.774–21.464; P<0.001).

Discussion

Our findings may have important implications for the counseling, prevention, and clinical management of patients with APD. Notably, this is the first study to demonstrate that patients with APD have significantly reduced survival compared to a matched control population (univariate analysis: P<0.001; OR: 4.912; 95% CI: 2.110–11.432; log-rank test: P<0.001). As shown in Table 3, our results are consistent with three previously published uncontrolled case series that suggested an association between APD and increased mortality [2–4]. However, to our knowledge, no prior case-control study has formally evaluated survival outcomes in this patient population. APD remains an underexplored entity; although case reports have become more frequent in recent years, few case series have been published. In fact, only nine studies to date have included more than 20 patients, and most of them lacked control groups (Table 3). Controlled investigations of APD are exceedingly rare. To our knowledge, the only published studies of this nature include one identifying clinical predictors of APD among patients undergoing hemodialysis [17] and another from our group reporting an association between APD and malignancy within this same cohort [15].

Primary perforating dermatoses (PPDs) represent a heterogeneous group of disorders characterized by transepidermal elimination of dermal components. Since the original description by Kyrle in 1916 of a female patient with what would later be termed Kyrle’s disease, four classical reactive subtypes have been identified: reactive perforating collagenosis (RPC), in which collagen is eliminated; elastosis perforans serpiginosa (EPS), involving elastin; Kyrle’s disease (KD), associated with abnormal keratin elimination; and perforating folliculitis (PF), which affects hair follicles. RPC is generally considered the most common subtype, while EPS is relatively rare. To address the overlapping clinical and histopathological features of these entities, Rapini et al. proposed the unifying term acquired perforating dermatosis (APD) for all PPDs arising in adulthood, irrespective of the specific dermal material being eliminated [12,13]. They excluded PF from this classification, arguing that follicular perforation is a nonspecific finding that may occur in various inflammatory follicular disorders triggered by trauma, medications, infections, or other mechanisms [12,13]. We adopted this classification in our study, although some authors continue to include PF within the APD spectrum [10]. More recently, perforating calcific elastosis (PCE), most commonly reported in adult black females, has been proposed as a distinct subtype within the PPD group [13]. In addition to the classical and acquired forms, other facultative variants of perforating dermatoses have been described, including perforating granuloma annulare, calcinosis cutis, necrobiosis lipoidica, and chondrodermatitis nodularis helicis [13]. Although the exact prevalence of APD remains unknown, it is considered rare, with an estimated incidence of 2.53 cases per 100,000 inhabitants per year [5]. This figure may be an underestimate due to frequent misdiagnosis. Notably, APD has been reported in up to 10% of patients undergoing hemodialysis [17,18], highlighting a possible association with chronic renal impairment.

Clinically, APD typically presents with pruritic, umbilicated, hyperkeratotic papules or plaques, often exhibiting a central keratotic plug or crateriform morphology. Lesions predominantly affect the trunk and extensor surfaces, particularly the lower extremities [1–5]. Depending on the subtype, lesions may appear erythematous or non-erythematous and may be follicular or non-follicular. The Koebner phenomenon, excoriations, and crusting are frequently observed. Dermoscopic features have also been described and may assist in diagnosis [2,19,20]. APD most commonly occurs in the fifth or sixth decade of life. Among the largest published case series (Table 3), some report a female predominance [3,5], while others do not [4]. Notably, a male predominance (4:1) has been specifically documented in cases of elastosis perforans serpiginosa (EPS) [13]. Recently, a consensus group proposed a classification system for perforating disorders based on combined clinical and histopathologic criteria [1,21]. Histologically, APD is characterized by a cup-shaped epidermal invagination filled with cellular debris, neutrophils, and degenerated connective tissue fibers undergoing transepidermal elimination. Adjacent epidermal hyperplasia is often present, and the underlying dermis typically shows a mild superficial inflammatory infiltrate composed of lymphocytes, neutrophils, and histiocytes [9–13]. Although transepidermal elimination is considered a histopathological hallmark of APD, it may be absent in early or superficial biopsy specimens, requiring serial sectioning or repeat biopsy for confirmation [12]. Overlapping features with prurigo nodularis and foreign body-type granulomatous reactions have also been reported [22]. Management of APD remains challenging. A therapeutic algorithm has been proposed [5], but there is no standardized approach. Topical corticosteroids and oral antihistamines are commonly used to alleviate symptoms. Broadband or narrowband ultraviolet B (UVB) phototherapy has demonstrated clinical benefit in selected cases (23). More recently, dupilumab has emerged as a potential treatment for APD associated with atopic dermatitis [24]. Optimizing control of underlying systemic comorbidities may also contribute to improved dermatologic outcomes [5].

Investigating morbidity and mortality in dermatological conditions presents inherent challenges. In the present study, in addition to evaluating overall survival, we assessed the burden of seven clinically relevant systemic comorbidities in patients with APD. There is broad consensus that DM, CKD, particularly in patients undergoing dialysis, or both, represent the most frequently associated comorbidities in APD [1]. However, this consensus is largely derived from low-level evidence, primarily case reports and small case series [1–14]. Our study provides the first case-control evidence demonstrating significant associations between APD and several systemic conditions, including DM, advanced CKD, HT, DL, AD, and cancer. We had previously reported the association with malignancy in this same cohort, noting that the association was more pronounced for hematological cancers than for solid tumors [15]. Other systemic conditions and causes of chronic pruritus frequently mentioned in association with APD (25–30), such as infectious diseases (e.g., HIV, scabies, insect bites, hepatitis), atopy, and psychiatric, cardiovascular, pulmonary, hepatobiliary, rheumatologic, metabolic, and neurological disorders as well as pregnancy, were not included in the scope of this study. Hypothyroidism and hyperparathyroidism have been cited in case reports, although often in the presence of established risk factors such as diabetes or nephropathy. APD lesions have also been reported to occur at sites of previous trauma, including healing herpes zoster lesions (30). In addition, several medications have also been implicated as potential triggers of perforating dermatoses, including epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) inhibitors (e.g., gefitinib, panitumumab), TNF inhibitors, sirolimus, kinase inhibitors (e.g., nilotinib), antivirals (e.g., telaprevir, indinavir), DPP-4 inhibitors, and various monoclonal antibodies (e.g., atezolizumab, bevacizumab) [13,31]. According to Rapini, many of these drug-induced eruptions could represent acneiform reactions involving disruption of pilosebaceous units rather than true perforating dermatoses [13]. Conversely, APD can occasionally develop in otherwise healthy individuals with no identifiable systemic comorbidity [13], underscoring the clinical heterogeneity and complexity of this condition.

It is also noteworthy that, in this cohort, nontraumatic mortality was significantly associated with DM, cancer, AD, CKD, HT, DL, APD diagnosis, and older age at diagnosis, according to univariate analysis. However, in the multivariate Cox regression model, only arterial disease remained independently associated with increased mortality (adjusted HR: 7.716; 95% CI: 2.774–21.464; P<0.001). These findings highlight the need for future research to elucidate the molecular mechanisms linking APD, systemic comorbidities, particularly AD, and reduced survival.

According to Gambichler et al., the pathogenesis of APD involves trauma, delayed wound healing, and microangiopathy [32]. APD is believed to result from an acquired defect in dermal wound repair, typically triggered by mechanical trauma or scratching, leading to necrotic lesions in predisposed skin [9–13]. Lesions are more frequently observed in trauma-prone areas and have even been experimentally induced [22]. Systemic comorbidities may enhance susceptibility to APD through several pathophysiological mechanisms, including microangiopathy, tissue hypoxia, impaired wound healing, dysregulated inflammation, oxidative stress, upregulation of matrix metalloproteinases, fibronectin-driven epithelial proliferation and migration, abnormal tissue remodeling via TGF-β signaling, and reduced numbers of epidermal Langerhans cells [33–37]. A central pathogenic factor may be the dermal accumulation of advanced glycation end-products (AGEs), highly oxidizing molecules that accumulate in patients with diabetes and CKD. AGEs interfere with collagen integrity, extracellular matrix remodeling, and keratinocyte migration, ultimately impairing wound healing. In vitro studies suggest that mechanical trauma, such as scratching, exposes keratinocytes to AGE-modified collagen, which activates CD36 receptors and promotes metalloproteinase expression, facilitating collagen extrusion through the epidermis [33]. Importantly, CKD and uremia are also associated with AGE accumulation, even in the absence of diabetes.

Cardiovascular disease is the leading cause of death in patients with diabetes and is strongly linked to AGE-related vascular damage. Indeed, AGE accumulation is a well-established pathogenic mechanism in the development of both microvascular and macrovascular complications in diabetes [38]. In diabetic patients, AGEs have been independently associated with increased risk of retinopathy, nephropathy, and neuropathy [38]. It can be hypothesized that the observed link between APD, arterial disease, and reduced survival in our study may, therefore, be mediated, at least in part, by diabetic angiopathy and AGE-induced tissue damage. Furthermore, the coexistence of hypertension and diabetes is known to significantly increase the risk of cardiovascular complications by three- to sixfold compared to either condition alone. These observations underscore the importance of tailored management strategies for patients with APD, taking into account multimorbidity profiles to improve both dermatological and systemic outcomes. With regard to the association between APD and cancer, it remains unclear whether this link is causal. Possible explanations include indirect relationships via diabetes, the role of chronic pruritus and scratching in tumor detection, or even a paraneoplastic phenomenon [15,39]. Additionally, it should also be noted that other facultative variants of perforating dermatoses APD, such as chondrodermatitis nodular helicis, have also been associated with an increased risk of multimorbidity and mortality in middle-aged individuals [40].

The strengths of our study include the histopathological confirmation of all APD cases and its multicenter, case-control design. This is the first study to evaluate survival outcomes in this patient population. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. These include the retrospective nature of the study, the potential for unmeasured confounding, missing clinical data, limited generalizability beyond the study population, the absence of socioeconomic or behavioral information, and the lack of centralized histopathological review. Despite the relatively small sample size, this is one of the largest APD cohorts reported to date and the only study to date featuring a controlled analysis of both morbidity and mortality. To our knowledge, only three previously published case series have included more than 50 APD patients, and all were uncontrolled investigations (Table 3).

Conclusions

In conclusion, this retrospective, multicenter epidemiological study provides the first controlled evidence that patients with acquired perforating dermatosis experience significantly reduced survival compared to matched controls. This excess mortality appears to be primarily driven by underlying arterial disease, as demonstrated in the multivariate analysis. Prospective studies are needed to validate these findings, but other studies suggest that dermatologists, as members of multidisciplinary care teams, may play a key role in implementing preventive and therapeutic strategies aimed at improving long-term survival in patients with APD.
