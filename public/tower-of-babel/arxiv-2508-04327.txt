Matrix Rosenthal and Concentration Inequalities for Markov Chains with Applications in Statistical Learning
Yang Peng, Yuchen Xin, Zhihua Zhang

Abstract
In this paper, we study moment and concentration inequalities of the spectral norm for sums of dependent random matrices. We establish novel Rosenthal-Burkholder inequalities for matrix martingales, as well as matrix Rosenthal, Hoeffding, and Bernstein inequalities for ergodic Markov chains. Compared with previous work on matrix concentration inequalities for Markov chains, our results do not require the assumptions of a non-zero absolute spectral gap and bounded matrix functions. Furthermore, our results have leading terms that match the Markov chain central limit theorem, rather than relying on variance proxies. We also give dimension-free versions of the inequalities, which are independent of the ambient dimension d d and relies on the effective rank of some matrix instead. This enables the generalization of our results to linear operators in infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces. Our results have extensive applications in statistics and machine learning; in particular, we obtain improved bounds in covariance estimation and principal component analysis on Markovian data.

Introduction

Concentration inequalities for sums of random matrices
(
Wigderson and Xiao 2005
;
Tropp 2015
)
are of great significance for statistics, machine learning, theoretical computer science, signal processing, etc.
Common examples include covariance matrix estimation
(
Chen et al. 2012
)
, principal component analysis (PCA)
(
Kumar and Sarkar 2023
)
, and reinforcement learning
(
Duan and Wainwright 2022
;
Wu et al. 2025
;
Mou 2025
)
.
Although matrix concentration inequalities have been thoroughly studied in the literature
(
Tropp 2011
;
Tropp 2012
;
Mackey et al. 2014
;
Tropp 2015
;
Minsker 2017
;
Garg et al. 2018
;
Klochkov and Zhivotovskiy 2020
;
Qiu et al. 2020
;
Huang and Tropp 2021
;
Van Werde and Sanders 2023
;
Bandeira et al. 2023
;
Neeman et al. 2024
;
Kroshnin and Suvorikova 2024
;
Jirak et al. 2024
;
Wang and Ramdas 2024
;
Wu et al. 2025
)
, to the best of our knowledge, the Rosenthal-Burkholder inequality for matrix martingales and matrix Rosenthal inequalities for Markov chains remain absent.

Furthermore, while some existing work
(
Garg et al. 2018
;
Qiu et al. 2020
;
Neeman et al. 2024
;
Wu et al. 2025
)
has derived matrix Hoeffding and Bernstein inequalities for Markov chains with a non-zero absolute spectral gap via the multi-matrix Golden-Thompson inequality
(
Sutter et al. 2017
;
Garg et al. 2018
)
and the León-Perron argument
(
León and Perron 2004
)
, these studies suffer from the following limitations.
First, for non-reversible Markov chains, geometric ergodicity does not imply a non-zero absolute spectral gap
(
Kontoyiannis and Meyn 2012
, Theorem 1.4)
, making these results inapplicable to general non-reversible Markov chains, which occur in reinforcement learning
(
Duan and Wainwright 2022
)
and PCA
(
Kumar and Sarkar 2023
)
problems with Markovian data.
Second, these works are confined to uniformly geometrically ergodic Markov chains and bounded matrix functions, thus cannot be extended to unbounded matrix functions.
Third, the leading terms of these results do not match the Markov chain central limit theorem (CLT)
(
Jones 2004
, Corollary 5)
, relying instead on variance proxies.
Finally, due to the use of the multi-matrix Golden-Thompson inequality
(
Garg et al. 2018
, Theorem 1.5)
, this incurs an additional dependence on the dimension
d
, making it impossible to generalize these results to a dimension-free version.
Accordingly, these results cannot be applied to linear operators in infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces.

The goal of this paper is to bridge the gaps mentioned earlier.
Specifically, we first give a Rosenthal-Burkholder inequality
(
Rosenthal 1970
;
Burkholder 1973
;
Hitczenko 1990
)
for matrix martingales in Theorem
2.1
. That is, for an
({\mathscr{F}}_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
-adapted
d
-dimensional self-adjoint matrix martingale
({\bm{M}}_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
, letting martingale differences
{\bm{X}}_{k}={\bm{M}}_{k}-{\bm{M}}_{k-1}
and quadratic variation
\left\langle{{\bm{M}}}\right\rangle_{\infty}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}{\mathbb{E}}[{\bm{X}}_{k}^{2}|{\mathscr{F}}_{k-1}]
, one has that

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:intro_{R}osenthal_{b}urkholder_{i}neq}\left\|{\sup_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\|{\bm{M}}_{k}\|}\right\|_{p}\leq C_{R,1}\ \sqrt{p\vee\log d}\Big\|\left\|{\left\langle{{\bm{M}}}\right\rangle_{\infty}}\right\|^{1/2}\Big\|_{p}+C_{R,2}\ (p\vee\log d)\left\|{\sup_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\|{\bm{X}}_{k}\|}\right\|_{p}.

(1)

Here,
\|\cdot\|
is the spectral norm of the matrix,
\|\cdot\|_{p}
is the
L^{p}
norm of random variables for any
p\geq 2
, and
C_{R,1},C_{R,2}>0
are universal constants.
This result matches the classic Hilbert space-valued Rosenthal-Burkholder inequality
(
Pinelis 1994
, Theorem 4.1)
, with the only difference that an additional
\log d
term appears.
This is an unavoidable price to pay when extending Hilbert-space-valued probabilistic inequalities to matrix versions.

Based on the Rosenthal-Burkholder inequality in Eqn. (
1
), we further derive matrix Rosenthal inequalities for Markov chains by leveraging the approach of Poisson equations (see Eqn. (
12
))
(
Durmus et al. 2023
)
, which allows us to overcome the limitations mentioned above.
In particular,
we consider the stationary Markov chain
(Z_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
in a measurable space
{\mathcal{Z}}
with Markov kernel
{\mathrm{Q}}
and stationary distribution
\pi
, and a self-adjoint matrix function
{\bm{F}}\colon{\mathcal{Z}}\to{\mathbb{H}}^{d}
satisfying
\pi({\bm{F}}):=\int_{{\mathcal{Z}}}{\bm{F}}(z)\pi(dz)=\bm{0}_{d\times d}
.
Our goal is to give an upper bound for the spectral norm of the summation
{\bm{S}}_{n}:=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}{{\bm{F}}}(Z_{k})
.
By the Markov chain CLT
(
Jones 2004
, Corollary 5)
, under some regularity conditions, we have

converges weakly to a Gaussian distribution and satisfies
\lim_{n\to\infty}n^{-1}{\mathbb{E}}[{\bm{S}}_{n}^{2}]={\bm{\Sigma}}_{\pi}({\bm{F}})

is the long-run variance defined as

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:long_{t}erm_{v}ariance}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\pi}({\bm{F}}):=\pi\left({{{\bm{F}}}^{2}}\right)+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\pi\left({{{\bm{F}}}{\mathrm{Q}}^{k}{{\bm{F}}}+\left({{\mathrm{Q}}^{k}{{\bm{F}}}}\right){{\bm{F}}}}\right),

(2)

with
({\mathrm{Q}}{\bm{F}})(\cdot)=\int_{{\mathcal{Z}}}{\bm{F}}(z){\mathrm{Q}}(\cdot,dz)
.
Under the uniform geometric ergodicity assumption (Assumption
2.1
) and the geometric

-ergodicity assumption with Lyapunov function

2.2
), in Theorem
2.2

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:intro_{r}osenthal_{m}arkov}\frac{1}{n}\left\|{\sup_{k\leq n}\|{\bm{S}}_{k}\|}\right\|_{p}\leq C_{R,1}\sqrt{\frac{(p\vee\log d)\left\|{{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\pi}({\bm{F}})}\right\|}{n}}+O\left({\frac{1}{n^{3/4}}}\right).

(3)

The first term of the r.h.s. of Eqn.(

By Markov inequality, in Corollary

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:intro_{h}oeffding_{m}arkov}\frac{1}{n}\sup_{k\leq n}\|{\bm{S}}_{k}\|\leq\frac{16}{3}C_{R,1}\sqrt{\frac{e^{2}\log(\delta^{-1}\vee d)t_{\operatorname{mix}}\left\|{\left\|{{\bm{F}}}\right\|}\right\|_{\infty}^{2}}{n}}+O\left({\frac{1}{n}}\right),

(4)

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:intro_{b}ernstein_{m}arkov}\frac{1}{n}\sup_{k\leq n}\|{\bm{S}}_{k}\|\leq C_{R,1}\sqrt{\frac{e^{2}\log(\delta^{-1}\vee d)\left\|{{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\pi}({\bm{F}})}\right\|}{n}}+O\left({\frac{1}{n^{3/4}}}\right),

(5)

where
t_{\operatorname{mix}}
is the mixing time and
t_{\operatorname{mix}}\left\|{\left\|{{\bm{F}}}\right\|}\right\|_{\infty}^{2}
is a variance proxy, which is an upper bound of the long-run variance

.

Neeman et al. 2024
gave a Bernstein inequality with a leading term relying on the variance proxy

which is also an upper bound of
\|{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\pi}({\bm{F}})\|
, where

is the absolute spectral gap.
Hence our results are sharper in the leading term than the previous work on the matrix concentration inequalities for Markov chains
(
Garg et al. 2018
;
Qiu et al. 2020
;
Neeman et al. 2024
;
Wu et al. 2025
)
if we consider time-homogeneous matrix functions.

All of the above inequalities (
1
), (
3
),
(
4
),
(

) can be extended to their respective dimension-free versions, where the ambient dimension
d
is replaced by the effective rank of some matrix that is an upper bound of the variance term.
As a result, our results can be applied to linear operators in infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces.

We apply our results in covariance estimation, PCA
with Markovian data, and obtain improved bounds.

2
Main Results

In this section, we present our main results: the Rosenthal-Burkholder inequality for matrix martingales, as well as matrix Rosenthal, Hoeffding, and Bernstein inequalities for ergodic Markov chains.
We will also give dimension-free versions of these results.

We find that the dimension-free results can be applied to linear operator-valued martingales in infinite-dimensional separable Hilbert spaces.
For brevity, we will not change the notation, and our dimension-free results still hold if
{\mathbb{H}}^{d}
is replaced by the space of all self-adjoint operators on the separable Hilbert space
{\mathcal{X}}
, and
{\mathbb{C}}^{d_{1}{\times}d_{2}}
is replaced by the space of all bounded linear operators from the separable Hilbert space
{\mathcal{X}}_{1}
to the separable Hilbert space
{\mathcal{X}}_{2}
.

2.1
The Rosenthal-Burkholder Inequality for Matrix
Martingales

Let
(\Omega,{\mathscr{F}},{\mathbb{P}})
be a probability space and
\{\emptyset,\Omega\}={\mathscr{F}}_{-1}\subseteq{\mathscr{F}}_{0}\subseteq{\mathscr{F}}_{1}\subseteq\ldots\subseteq{\mathscr{F}}
be the filtration.
We denote by
{\mathbb{E}}_{k}
the expectation conditioned on
{\mathscr{F}}_{k}
.
We consider the
d
-dimensional self-adjoint matrix martingale
({\bm{M}}_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
that is adapted to the filtration
({\mathscr{F}}_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
.
The matrix martingale
({\bm{M}}_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
satisfies

{\mathbb{E}}_{k-1}{\bm{M}}_{k}={\bm{M}}_{k-1}\quad\text{ and }\quad{\mathbb{E}}\left\|{{\bm{M}}_{k}}\right\|<\infty\quad\text{ for }\ k\in{\mathbb{N}}.

We denote by
({\bm{X}}_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
the martingale difference sequence, that is,
{\bm{X}}_{k}={\bm{M}}_{k}-{\bm{M}}_{k-1}\in{\mathbb{H}}^{d}
(
{\bm{M}}_{-1}:=\bm{0}_{d{\times}d}
).
And we denote by
(\left\langle{{\bm{M}}}\right\rangle_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
the quadratic variation sequence, that is,
\left\langle{{\bm{M}}}\right\rangle_{k}=\sum_{j=0}^{k}{\mathbb{E}}_{j-1}{\bm{X}}_{j}^{2}\in{\mathbb{H}}^{d}_{+}
.
Throughout this section, we assume that
\left\langle{{\bm{M}}}\right\rangle_{\infty}:=\lim_{k\to\infty}\left\langle{{\bm{M}}}\right\rangle_{k}<\infty
element-wisely almost surely.
As a result, by the martingale convergence theorem
(
Durrett 2019
, Theorem 4.5.2)
,
\lim_{k\to\infty}{{\bm{M}}}_{k}={\bm{M}}_{\infty}\in{\mathbb{H}}^{d}
almost surely.

Rectangular Case:
For a
d_{1}{\times}d_{2}
rectangular matrix martingale
({\bm{W}}_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
adapted to
({\mathscr{F}}_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
, we denote by
{\bm{D}}_{k}={\bm{W}}_{k}-{\bm{W}}_{k-1}
the martingale difference and
\|\langle{\bm{W}}\rangle_{\infty}\|:=\|\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{k-1}[{\bm{D}}_{k}{\bm{D}}_{k}^{*}]\|\vee\|\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{k-1}[{\bm{D}}_{k}^{*}{\bm{D}}_{k}]\|
the proxy of the quadratic variation.
In this case, we also assume that

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{k-1}[{\bm{D}}_{k}{\bm{D}}_{k}^{*}]<\infty\mbox{ and }\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{k-1}[{\bm{D}}_{k}^{*}{\bm{D}}_{k}]<\infty\quad\mbox{element-wisely almost surely}.

Dimension-Free:
As mentioned in
Minsker 2017

d
of the ambient space.
To this end, we resort to the notion of effective rank
(
Koltchinskii and Lounici 2016
)
.
For any
{\bm{\Upsilon}}\in{\mathbb{H}}^{d}_{+}
, its effective rank is defined as

r({\bm{\Upsilon}})=\frac{\operatorname{tr}({\bm{\Upsilon}})}{\left\|{{\bm{\Upsilon}}}\right\|}.

One can check that
r({\bm{\Upsilon}})\leq d
and
r({\bm{\Upsilon}})
can be much smaller than
d
if the eigenvalues of
{\bm{\Upsilon}}
rapidly decay to
0
.

Now, we are ready to state one of the main results, the Rosenthal-Burkholder inequality for matrix martingales in the following theorem.

(Rosenthal-Burkholder Inequality)

For any

\quad\Big\|\left\|{\left\langle{{\bm{M}}}\right\rangle_{\infty}}\right\|^{1/2}\Big\|_{p}<\infty\quad\text{ and }\quad\left\|{\sup_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\|{\bm{X}}_{k}\|}\right\|_{p}<\infty.

Then it holds that

\displaystyle\left\|{\sup_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\|{\bm{M}}_{k}\|}\right\|_{p}\leq C_{R,1}\sqrt{p\vee\log d}\Big\|\left\|{\left\langle{{\bm{M}}}\right\rangle_{\infty}}\right\|^{1/2}\Big\|_{p}+C_{R,2}(p\vee\log d)\left\|{\sup_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\|{\bm{X}}_{k}\|}\right\|_{p},

(6)

where

.
When

Discussion.

In the i.i.d. case,

;
Mackey et al. 2014
provided a matrix Rosenthal inequality,
and

We extend their results from the i.i.d. setting to the martingale setting.
See also

;

;

;

Rosenthal-Burkholder inequality and other probability inequalities for noncommutative martingales.

We also present another version of the Rosenthal-Burkholder inequality:

, which can be found in Section

2.2
Matrix Rosenthal and Concentration Inequalities for Markov Chains

Consider the Markov kernel
{\mathrm{Q}}\colon{\mathcal{Z}}\times{\mathscr{Z}}\to[0,1]
on the measurable space
({\mathcal{Z}},{\mathscr{Z}})
with a unique stationary distribution
\pi\in{\mathcal{P}}
, where
{\mathcal{P}}
is the set of all probability distributions on
({\mathcal{Z}},{\mathscr{Z}})
.
For any
\xi\in{\mathcal{P}}
, we denote by
{\mathbb{P}}_{\xi}
(resp.
{\mathbb{E}}_{\xi}
) the probability (resp. expectation) of the canonical process
(Z_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
with initial distribution
\xi
, and
({\mathscr{F}}_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
the nature filtration where
{\mathscr{F}}_{k}=\sigma(Z_{0},\ldots,Z_{k})
.
That is,
(Z_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is a stochastic process in
({\mathcal{Z}}^{\mathbb{N}},{\mathscr{Z}}^{\mathbb{N}},{\mathbb{P}}_{\xi})
adapted to
({\mathscr{F}}_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
, which satisfies
{\mathbb{P}}_{\xi}(Z_{0}\in A)=\xi(A)
,
{\mathbb{P}}_{\xi}(Z_{k+1}\in A\mid{\mathscr{F}}_{k})={\mathbb{P}}_{\xi}(Z_{k+1}\in A\mid Z_{k})={\mathrm{Q}}(Z_{k},A)

{\mathbb{P}}_{\xi}
-almost surely for any
A\in{\mathscr{Z}}
and
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
Thus,
(Z_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is an Markov chain.

For any
p\geq 1
,
\xi\in{\mathcal{P}}
and random variable
X
defined on
({\mathcal{Z}}^{\mathbb{N}},{\mathscr{Z}}^{\mathbb{N}},{\mathbb{P}}_{\xi})
, we denote by
\|X\|_{\xi,p}=({\mathbb{E}}_{\xi}[|X|^{p}])^{1/p}
the
L^{p}
norm of
X
under the initial distribution
\xi
.

For any measurable function
f
in
({\mathcal{Z}},{\mathscr{Z}})
and distribution
\xi\in{\mathcal{P}}
, we denote
\left\|{f}\right\|_{\infty}=\sup_{z\in{\mathcal{Z}}}\left|{f(z)}\right|
(
f
is real-valued),
\xi(f)=\int_{\mathcal{Z}}f(z)\xi(dz)
,
(\xi{\mathrm{Q}})(\cdot)=\int_{{\mathcal{Z}}}{\mathrm{Q}}(z,\cdot)\xi(dz)
, and
({\mathrm{Q}}f)(\cdot)=\int_{{\mathcal{Z}}}f(z){\mathrm{Q}}(\cdot,dz)
.

Now, we introduce the ergodicity assumptions we consider in this paper, we will only consider the uniformly geometrically ergodic Markov chains in this section, and the more general geometrically
V
-ergodic Markov chains in Section
2.2.1
.
Under other ergodicity condition, such as geometrically ergodic Markov chains with respect to the Wasserstein semimetric
(
Durmus et al. 2023
, Section 2.3)
, similar results can be derived by using the same techniques, so we omit them in this paper.

Assumption 2.1

(Uniformly Geometrically Ergodic Markov Chains)
.

There exists a
t_{\operatorname{mix}}\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that for any
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

\sup_{z\in{\mathcal{Z}}}\left\|{{\mathrm{Q}}^{k}(z,\cdot)-\pi}\right\|_{\textup{TV}}\leq 2\left({1/4}\right)^{\lfloor k/t_{\operatorname{mix}}\rfloor},

where
\left\|{\xi}\right\|_{\textup{TV}}:=\left|{\xi}\right|({\mathcal{Z}})
is the total variation norm of the signed measure
\xi
on
({\mathcal{Z}},{\mathscr{Z}})
.

We consider a measurable self-adjoint matrix function
{\bm{F}}\colon{\mathcal{Z}}\to{\mathbb{H}}^{d}
.
We assume
\pi({\bm{F}})=\int_{{\mathcal{Z}}}{\bm{F}}(z)\pi(dz)=\bm{0}_{d{\times}d}
and
\|\|{\bm{F}}\|\|_{\infty}=\sup_{z\in{\mathcal{Z}}}\|{\bm{F}}(z)\|<\infty
.
Our goal is to give counterparts to matrix Rosenthal, Hoeffding, Bernstein inequalities for the spectral norm of the summation
{\bm{S}}_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}{{\bm{F}}}(Z_{k})
.
We observe that the boundedness condition is indispensable.
As argued by
Fan et al. 2021
, even for scalar unbounded functions, it is impossible to obtain Hoeffding inequalities for
{\bm{S}}_{n}
under Assumption
2.1
, whereas our Rosenthal inequality implies the Hoeffding-typ inequality for
{\bm{S}}_{n}
.

Recall that the Markov chain CLT
(
Jones 2004
, Corollary 5)
says that
n^{-1/2}{\bm{S}}_{n}
converges weakly to a Gaussian distribution and satisfies
\lim_{n\to\infty}n^{-1}{\mathbb{E}}_{\xi}[{\bm{S}}_{n}^{2}]={\bm{\Sigma}}_{\pi}({\bm{F}})
for any
\xi\in{\mathcal{P}}
, where
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\pi}({\bm{F}})
is the long-run variance defined as

{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\pi}({\bm{F}}):=\pi\left({{{\bm{F}}}^{2}}\right)+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\pi\left({{{\bm{F}}}{\mathrm{Q}}^{k}{{\bm{F}}}+\left({{\mathrm{Q}}^{k}{{\bm{F}}}}\right){{\bm{F}}}}\right).

We aim to obtain a Rosenthal inequality whose leading term is given by the long-run variance
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\pi}({\bm{F}})
, matching the Markov chain CLT.

Dimension-Free:
When we address dimension-free results, we need to make an additional assumption, that is, there exists a
{\bm{\Upsilon}}\in{\mathbb{H}}^{d}_{+}
such that
{\bm{F}}^{2}(z)\preccurlyeq{\bm{\Upsilon}}
for all
z\in{\mathcal{Z}}
. Note that the effective rank
r({\bm{\Upsilon}})
could be much smaller than the ambient dimension
d
.

Rectangular Case:
In this case, we consider a rectangular matrix function
{\bm{R}}\colon{\mathcal{Z}}\to{\mathbb{C}}^{d_{1}{\times}d_{2}}
that satisfies
\pi({\bm{R}})=\int_{{\mathcal{Z}}}{\bm{R}}(z)\pi(dz)=\bm{0}_{d_{1}{\times}d_{2}}
. We denote by
{\bm{V}}_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}{\bm{R}}(Z_{k})
the summation and

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:long_{t}erm_{v}ariance_{r}ect}\left\|{{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\pi}({\bm{R}})}\right\|:=\left\|{\pi\left({{\bm{R}}{\bm{R}}^{*}}\right){+}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\pi\left({{{\bm{R}}}{\mathrm{Q}}^{k}{{\bm{R}}^{*}}{+}\left({{\mathrm{Q}}^{k}{{\bm{R}}}}\right){{\bm{R}}^{*}}}\right)}\right\|\vee\left\|{\pi\left({{\bm{R}}^{*}{\bm{R}}}\right){+}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\pi\left({{{\bm{R}}^{*}}{\mathrm{Q}}^{k}{{\bm{R}}}{+}\left({{\mathrm{Q}}^{k}{{\bm{R}}^{*}}}\right){{\bm{R}}}}\right)}\right\|,

(7)

called the proxy of the long-run variance.

Now we state one of our main results, the matrix Rosenthal inequality for uniformly geometrically ergodic Markov chains.

Theorem 2.2

(Matrix Rosenthal Inequality for Uniformly Geometrically Ergodic Markov Chains)
.

Under Assumption
2.1
and
\|\|{\bm{F}}\|\|_{\infty}<\infty
,
we have that for any
p\geq 2
,
n\geq(p\vee\log d)t_{\operatorname{mix}}
, and
\xi\in{\mathcal{P}}
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\left\|{\sup_{k\leq n}\left\|{{\bm{S}}_{k}}\right\|}\right\|_{\pi,p}\leq

\displaystyle C_{R,1}\sqrt{\frac{(p\vee\log d)\left\|{{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\pi}({\bm{F}})}\right\|}{n}}+D_{1}t_{\operatorname{mix}}^{1/2}\left({\frac{(p\vee\log d)t_{\operatorname{mix}}}{n}}\right)^{3/4}\left\|{\left\|{{\bm{F}}}\right\|}\right\|_{\infty},

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\left\|{\sup_{k\leq n}\left\|{{\bm{S}}_{k}}\right\|}\right\|_{\xi,p}\leq\frac{1}{n}\left\|{\sup_{k\leq n}\left\|{{\bm{S}}_{k}}\right\|}\right\|_{\pi,p}+D_{2}\frac{pt_{\operatorname{mix}}}{n}\left\|{\left\|{{\bm{F}}}\right\|}\right\|_{\infty},

(8)

where
C_{R,1}
is defined in Theorem
2.1
and
D_{1},D_{2}>0
are universal constants, which can be traced in the proof.

Furthermore, the following extensions hold:

(a)

Rectangular Matrix:
the above inequality also holds for the rectangular case if we further assume
\|\|{\bm{R}}\|\|_{\infty}<\infty
and replace
{\bm{S}}
by
{\bm{V}}
,
{\bm{F}}
by
{\bm{R}}
, and
d
by
d_{1}+d_{2}
.

(b)

Dimension-Free:
in the self-adjoint case, if we further assume that there exists a
{\bm{\Upsilon}}\in{\mathbb{H}}^{d}_{+}
such that
{\bm{F}}^{2}(z)\preccurlyeq{\bm{\Upsilon}}
, then the above inequality also holds when we replace the
d
in the first term by
e\cdot r([(64/9)t_{\operatorname{mix}}^{2}{\bm{\Upsilon}}]\wedge[(1/n)\|\|\left\langle{{\bm{M}}}\right\rangle_{n}\|\|_{\pi,{p}/{2}}])
, and replace the
d
in the second term by the dimension factor defined after Eqn. (
17
), and double the right-hand side.

In the rectangular case (a), under similar assumptions, analogous dimension-free results hold, and we omit the details.

The proof strategy of Theorem
2.2
is to leverage the Poisson equation
{\bm{G}}-\left({{\mathrm{Q}}{\bm{G}}}\right)={\bm{F}}
to construct a matrix martingale
{\bm{M}}_{k}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}{\bm{G}}(Z_{j})-({\mathrm{Q}}{\bm{G}})(Z_{j-1})
, and then we apply our Rosenthal-Burkholder inequality (Theorem
2.1
).
This method is first introduced by
Durmus et al. 2023
, who derived a Hilbert space-valued Rosenthal inequality for Markov chains.
A detailed outline
of the proof can be found in Section
4.2
.

For the dimension-free results, as
n\to\infty
, we can show that
d_{n,p}\to e\cdot r([(64/9)t_{\operatorname{mix}}^{2}{\bm{\Upsilon}}]\wedge\|{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\pi}({\bm{F}})\|)
, which is a natural dimension factor in this problem.

We note that the dependence of the second term in Theorem
2.2
on
t_{\operatorname{mix}}
is suboptimal.
If we want the first term to dominate the second term, we need to take
n\gtrsim(p\vee\log d)t_{\operatorname{mix}}^{3}
, which is not linear in
t_{\operatorname{mix}}
.
In
Durmus et al. 2023
, they used dyadic descent argument to derive a Hilbert space-valued Rosenthal inequality with residual terms that have a sharp dependence on
t_{\operatorname{mix}}
.
However, this argument does not work for the matrix case, because under the Löwner order, the square function is not operator monotone, and the
p
-th power function (
p>2
) is not operator convex, making the core inequality (23) in the dyadic descent argument of
Durmus et al. 2023
invalid in our matrix case.

Fortunately, if we do not insist on obtaining the result with a leading term matching the Markov chain CLT, we can still derive results with the sharp dependence on
t_{\operatorname{mix}}
by using the blocking argument.
In the following theorem, we present another version of the Rosenthal inequality, whose leading term is given by the variance proxy
t_{\operatorname{mix}}\|\|{\bm{F}}\|\|_{\infty}^{2}
, which is an upper bound for the long-run variance
\|{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\pi}({\bm{F}})\|
.

Theorem 2.3
.

Under Assumption
2.1
and
\|\|{\bm{F}}\|\|_{\infty}<\infty
, we have that for all
p\geq 2
and
n\geq 2
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\left\|{\sup_{k\leq n}\left\|{{\bm{S}}_{k}}\right\|}\right\|_{\pi,p}\leq

\displaystyle\frac{16e}{3}C_{R,1}\sqrt{\frac{(p\vee\log d)t_{\operatorname{mix}}\left\|{\left\|{{\bm{F}}}\right\|}\right\|_{\infty}^{2}}{n}}+\left({\frac{16e}{3}C_{R,2}(p\vee\log d)+\frac{19}{3}}\right)\frac{t_{\operatorname{mix}}\left\|{\left\|{{\bm{F}}}\right\|}\right\|_{\infty}}{n}.

For brevity, we omit the inequality for the rectangular case, and defer the dimension-free results as well as the proofs to Lemma
4.3
(a).

By our Rosenthal inequalities and applying Markov inequality to

(Matrix Hoeffding Inequalities for Uniformly Geometrically Ergodic Markov Chains)

Under Assumption
2.1
and
\|\|{\bm{F}}\|\|_{\infty}<\infty

for any

and
n\geq 2

-probability at least

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sup_{k\leq n}\left\|{{\bm{S}}_{k}}\right\|\leq

where
D_{3}>0
is a universal constant, which can be taken as
6eC_{R,2}+eD_{2}
.

Corollary 2.2

(Matrix Bernstein Inequalities for Uniformly Geometrically Ergodic Markov Chains)

Under Assumption
2.1

\|\|{\bm{F}}\|\|_{\infty}<\infty

for any

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sup_{k\leq n}\left\|{{\bm{S}}_{k}}\right\|\leq

For the above concentration inequalities, the inequalities for both the rectangular case and the dimension-free version (where one only needs to replace
p
by
\log(1/\delta)
in the dimension factor) still hold.
We omit them here.

Discussion.

Garg et al. 2018
;
Qiu et al. 2020
;
Neeman et al. 2024
;
Wu et al. 2025
derived matrix concentration inequalities for Markov chains with a non-zero absolute spectral gap.
Instead of the multi-matrix Golden-Thompson inequality
(
Sutter et al. 2017
;
Garg et al. 2018
)
and the León-Perron argument
(
León and Perron 2004
)
used in these works, we leverage the Poisson equation approach
(
Durmus et al. 2023
)
, which allows us to overcome several limitations of the previous results:
First, our results can be applied to general non-reversible Markov chains without assuming a non-zero absolute spectral gap.
Second, our results can be extended to more general ergodicity assumptions, under which we do not need to assume the boundedness of functions, as detailed in Section
2.2.1
.
Third, the leading term of our results matches the Markov chain CLT, rather than relying on a variance proxy, e.g.
t_{\operatorname{mix}}\|{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi}[{\bm{F}}^{2}(Z_{0})]\|
or
t_{\operatorname{mix}}\|\|{\bm{F}}\|\|^{2}
.
Finally, under further assumptions, we obtain dimension-free results, which the previous works, which used the multi-matrix Golden-Thompson inequality, fail to achieve them.
Applying the dimension-free results, we can also obtain the concentration inequalities for linear operators in infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces.

Our results have the following limitations: As mentioned before, the residual term in the results where the leading term matches the Markov chain CLT (Theorem
2.2
, Theorem
2.2
, Theorem
2.4
) has a suboptimal dependence on
t_{\operatorname{mix}}
, failing to achieve the sharp dependence on
t_{\operatorname{mix}}
in the residual term as in the Hilbert space-valued version of the inequality
(
Durmus et al. 2023
)
.
We leave the improvement to the sharp dependence on
t_{\operatorname{mix}}
as future work.
Another limitation is that the Poisson equation approach is only applicable to time-homogeneous functions (additive functionals of Markov chains), whereas the results in
Neeman et al. 2024
apply to time-inhomogeneous functions.
This makes our results inapplicable to problems such as stochastic approximation with Markovian data
(
Li et al. 2023
;
Mou et al. 2024
;
Huo et al. 2024
;
Samsonov et al. 2025
)
.
We also leave the extension to time-inhomogeneous functions as future work.

3
Applications

In this section, we apply our results to covariance estimation and principal component analysis (PCA) problems
on Markovian data, to showcase their effectiveness.

We use the same notation as in Section
2.2
.
Suppose
(Z_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is a uniformly geometrically ergodic stationary Markov chain (Assumption
2.1
) and
\bm{f}\colon{\mathcal{Z}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is a bounded vector-valued function.
We first consider the problem of covariance estimation on Markovian data, which is also considered in
Fan et al. 2021
.
That is, we are interested in analyzing the spectral norm error in estimating the covariance matrix
{\bm{\Sigma}}=\pi(\bm{f}\bm{f}^{\top})={\mathbb{E}}_{\pi}[\bm{f}(Z_{0})\bm{f}(Z_{0})^{\top}]
using the sample covariance matrix
\hat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{n}=n^{-1}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\bm{f}(Z_{k})\bm{f}(Z_{k})^{\top}
.
Let
{\bm{F}}(z):=\bm{f}(z)\bm{f}(z)^{\top}-{\bm{\Sigma}}
. Then
{\bm{F}}
satisfies
\pi({\bm{F}})=\bm{0}_{d{\times}d}
and
\|\|{\bm{F}}\|\|_{\infty}\leq\|\|\bm{f}\|\|_{\infty}^{2}
.
Using the matrix Bernstein inequality for Markov chains (Corollary
2.2
), we obtain that for any
\delta\in(0,1)
, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:cov_{e}rror}\frac{1}{n}\sup_{k\leq n}\left\|{\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\left({\bm{f}(Z_{j})\bm{f}(Z_{j})^{\top}-{\bm{\Sigma}}}\right)}\right\|\leq C_{R,1}\sqrt{\frac{e^{2}\log(\delta^{-1}\vee d)\left\|{{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\pi}({\bm{F}})}\right\|}{n}}+O\left({\frac{1}{n^{3/4}}}\right),

(9)

where
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\pi}({\bm{F}})
is the long-run variance of
{\bm{F}}
defined in Eqn. (
2
).

Next, we consider the problem of principal component analysis on Markovian data, which is also considered in
Neeman et al. 2024
;
Kumar and Sarkar 2023
.
We denote by
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\ldots\geq\lambda_{d}\geq 0
the eigenvalues of
{\bm{\Sigma}}
, and
{\bm{v}}_{1},{\bm{v}}_{2},\ldots,{\bm{v}}_{n}
the corresponding eigenvectors.
We assume
\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}>0
.
In the PCA problem, we are interested in analyzing the
\sin^{2}
error in estimating the leading eigenvector
{\bm{v}}_{1}
of
{\bm{\Sigma}}
using the leading eigenvector
\hat{{\bm{v}}}_{1,n}
of
\hat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{n}
.
By Eqn. (
9
) and Wedin’s Theorem
(
Wedin 1972
)
, we immediately have

1-\left({{\bm{v}}_{1}^{\top}\hat{{\bm{v}}}_{1,n}}\right)^{2}\leq C\frac{\log(\delta^{-1}\vee d)\left\|{{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\pi}({\bm{F}})}\right\|}{(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})^{2}n}+O\left({\frac{1}{n^{3/2}}}\right),

where
C>0
is a universal constant.

Under further assumptions on
\bm{f}
, we can obtain dimension-free results.
Let
{\bm{\Upsilon}}\in{\mathbb{H}}_{d}^{+}
be a real PSD matrix with spectral decomposition
{\bm{\Upsilon}}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mu_{i}{\bm{u}}_{i}{\bm{u}}_{i}^{\top}
such that
\mu_{1}\geq\mu_{2}\geq\ldots\geq\mu_{d}\geq 0
, and
\bm{f}(z)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}(z){\bm{u}}_{i}
—the representation of
\bm{f}
on the orthonormal basis
\{{\bm{u}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{u}}_{d}\}
.
Suppose that
\sup_{z\in{\mathcal{Z}}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}^{2}(z)/\mu_{i}\leq 1
(
0/0:=0
and
1/0:=\infty
).
Then by the Schur complement lemma
(
Zhang 2006
)
, one has that
\bm{f}(z)\bm{f}(z)^{\top}\preccurlyeq{\bm{\Upsilon}}
for any
z\in{\mathcal{Z}}
.
Furthermore,
{\bm{F}}^{2}(z)=(\bm{f}(z)\bm{f}(z)^{\top}-{\bm{\Sigma}})^{2}\preccurlyeq 2[\bm{f}(z)\bm{f}(z)^{\top}]^{2}+2{\bm{\Sigma}}^{2}\preccurlyeq 2\|\|\bm{f}\|\|_{\infty}^{2}[\bm{f}(z)\bm{f}(z)^{\top}+{\bm{\Sigma}}]\preccurlyeq 4\|\|\bm{f}\|\|_{\infty}^{2}{\bm{\Upsilon}}
.
Using the dimension-free results, we can replace the ambient
d
in the bounds by the effective rank
e\cdot r([(64/9)t_{\operatorname{mix}}^{2}\|\|\bm{f}\|\|_{\infty}^{2}{\bm{\Upsilon}}]\wedge[(1/n)\|\|\left\langle{{\bm{M}}}\right\rangle_{n}\|\|_{\pi,(1/2)\log(1/\delta)}])
.
Similar results hold for the more general geometrically
V
-ergodic Markov chains and unbounded function
\bm{f}
, and we omit them for brevity.

Compared with the results in
Kumar and Sarkar 2023
;
Neeman et al. 2024
, our results do not require the Markov chain to have an absolute spectral gap, while obtaining a leading term that matches the Markov chain CLT and gaining dimension-free results. Moreover, our results can be extended to more general ergodicity assumptions and unbounded functions.
These aspects demonstrate the advantages of our results over the previous ones as we discussed earlier.

4
Proofs

In this section, we outline the proofs of our main results.

4.1
Proof of the Rosenthal-Burkholder Inequality for Matrix Martingales

Based on the Bennett inequality for matrix martingales (Lemma
A.1
), we can obtain the following good
\lambda
inequality for conditionally symmetric matrix martingales, which is a matrix version of
Pinelis 1994
.
Before that, we need to introduce the concept of conditionally symmetric martingales.

Definition 4.1

(Conditionally Symmetric Martingales)
.

The martingale
({\bm{M}}_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is said to be conditionally symmetric if the conditional distributions of
{\bm{X}}_{k}
and
-{\bm{X}}_{k}
given
{\mathscr{F}}_{k-1}
coincide almost surely for any
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

With the assumption that the martingale
({\bm{M}}_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is conditionally symmetric, the analysis can be greatly simplified, because the truncated martingale remains a martingale.
Later on, we will introduce how to perform symmetrization to extend the conclusion to general martingales.

Theorem 4.1

(Good
\lambda
Inequality)
.

Suppose that
({\bm{M}}_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is conditionally symmetric.
For any
\lambda,\delta_{1},\delta_{2}>0
and
\beta>1+\delta_{2}
, letting
N=[\beta-(1+\delta_{2})]/{\delta_{2}}
, we have that

{\mathbb{P}}\left({\sup_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\|{\bm{M}}_{k}\|>\beta\lambda,\ \left\|{\left\langle{{\bm{M}}}\right\rangle_{\infty}}\right\|^{1/2}\leq\delta_{1}\lambda,\ \sup_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\|{\bm{X}}_{k}\|\leq\delta_{2}\lambda}\right)\leq 2d\left({\frac{e}{N}\frac{\delta_{1}^{2}}{\delta_{2}^{2}}}\right)^{N}{\mathbb{P}}\left({\sup_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\|{\bm{M}}_{k}\|>\lambda}\right).

Furthermore, if there exists a
{\bm{\Upsilon}}\in{\mathbb{H}}^{d}_{+}
such that
\langle{\bm{M}}\rangle_{\infty}\preccurlyeq{\bm{\Upsilon}}
almost surely, then the above inequality also holds in which
d
is replaced by
e\cdot r({\bm{\Upsilon}}\wedge(\delta_{1}^{2}\lambda^{2}))
.

The proof is similar to
Pinelis 1994
and can be found in Appendix
A.1
.
The key ingredient is the Bennett inequality for matrix martingales (Lemma
A.1
).
Roughly speaking, this good
\lambda
inequality indicates that if the supremum of a conditionally symmetric martingale is sufficiently large, then with high probability, both the quadratic variation and the supremum of the martingale difference will also be large.

We observe that the dimension-free factor
r\left({{\bm{\Upsilon}}\wedge(\delta_{1}^{2}\lambda^{2})}\right)
in Theorem
4.1
increases to
d
as
\lambda
decreases to
0
.
Therefore, to obtain a valid dimension-free result, it is necessary to truncate the error level
\lambda
before applying the good
\lambda
inequality.

The following lemma, the truncated
\Phi
inequality, translates a truncated good
\lambda
inequality into a moment inequality, which is an extension of
Burkholder 1973
.

Lemma 4.1

(Truncated
\Phi
Inequality)
.

Suppose that

\Phi\colon{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
is non-decreasing continuous satisfying
\Phi(\beta\lambda)\leq\gamma\Phi(\lambda)
for all
\lambda\geq 0
, where
\beta>1
and
\gamma>0
are some constants; and
Y,Z
are nonnegative random variables satisfying
the following good
\lambda
inequality

{\mathbb{P}}\left({Y>\beta\lambda,Z\leq\lambda}\right)\leq\epsilon{\mathbb{P}}\left({Y>\lambda}\right),\quad\forall\lambda\geq\frac{a}{\beta}

for some constants
a\geq 0
and
\epsilon\in(0,1/\gamma)
.
Then we have that

{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\Phi(Y)}\right]\leq\frac{1}{1-\gamma\epsilon}\Phi(a)+\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma\epsilon}{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\Phi(Z)}\right].

The proof can be found in Appendix
A.2
.

Now, we are ready to give a full proof of the Rosenthal-Burkholder inequality (Theorem
2.1
).
As in the proof of the scalar Rosenthal-Burkholder inequality
(
Hitczenko 1990
;
Pinelis 1994
)
, we first deal with conditionally symmetric martingales, and then generalize the results to general martingales.

Step 1: Conditionally Symmetric Martingales.

Assume that
({\bm{M}}_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is conditionally symmetric.
We start with the dimension-dependent case.
Applying the good
\lambda
inequality (Theorem
4.1
) and the truncated
\Phi
inequality (Lemma
4.1
) with
Y=\sup_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\|{\bm{M}}_{k}\|
,
Z=\delta_{1}^{-1}\left\|{\left\langle{{\bm{M}}}\right\rangle_{\infty}}\right\|^{1/2}+\delta_{2}^{-1}\sup_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\|{\bm{X}}_{k}\|
,
\Phi(x)=x^{p}
,
\beta>1
to be determined,
\gamma=\beta^{p}
,
a=0
, and

\epsilon=2d\left({\frac{e}{N}\frac{\delta_{1}^{2}}{\delta_{2}^{2}}}\right)^{N},

we have the following inequality

\left\|{\sup_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\|{\bm{M}}_{k}\|}\right\|_{p}\leq\left({\frac{1}{1-\gamma\epsilon}}\right)^{1/p}\beta\left({\delta_{1}^{-1}\left\|{\left\|{\left\langle{{\bm{M}}}\right\rangle_{\infty}}\right\|^{1/2}}\right\|_{p}+\delta_{2}^{-1}\left\|{\sup_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\left\|{{\bm{X}}_{k}}\right\|}\right\|_{p}}\right),

provided that

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:rosenthal_{p}arameter_{i}neq}\gamma\epsilon=2d\left({\frac{e}{N}\frac{\delta_{1}^{2}}{\delta_{2}^{2}}}\right)^{N}\beta^{p}<1,\quad\text{ where }\ N=\frac{\beta-(1+\delta_{2})}{\delta_{2}}.

(10)

We can choose the following parameters such that
\beta>1+\delta_{2}
and the inequality (
10
) holds:

\beta=1+e^{-p/c}+\frac{1}{c\vee\log d},\quad\delta_{1}=\frac{1}{5\sqrt{c\vee\log d}\ e^{p/c}},\quad\delta_{2}=\frac{1}{5(c\vee\log d)},

where
c\in[1,p]
is a parameter to be determined.
Now, we check that inequality (
10
) indeed holds.
First note that
e^{-p/c}\geq e^{-1}
and then

\displaystyle N=\frac{\beta-1-\delta_{2}}{\delta_{2}}=4+5(c\vee\log d)e^{-p/c}\geq 4+5e^{-1}\log d,

\displaystyle\beta^{p}=\left[{1+(N+1)\delta_{2}}\right]^{p}\leq e^{2pN\delta_{2}}=e^{2pN/[5(c\vee\log d)]}\leq e^{2pN/(5c)},

\displaystyle\frac{e}{N}\frac{\delta_{1}^{2}}{\delta_{2}^{2}}=\frac{e}{4e^{2p/c}(c\vee\log d)^{-1}+5e^{p/c}}\leq\frac{e}{5}e^{-p/c},

hence

\displaystyle\gamma\epsilon=2d\left({\frac{e}{N}\frac{\delta_{1}^{2}}{\delta_{2}^{2}}}\right)^{N}\beta^{p}

\displaystyle\leq 2d\left({\frac{e}{5}}\right)^{4+5e^{-1}\log d}e^{-21p/(5c)}<2(e/5)^{4}e^{-21/5}<1,

which is the desired conclusion.
Here we used the fact
(e/5)^{5/e}<e^{-1}

and
2(e/5)^{4}<1/2
.
Then we have

\left({\frac{1}{1-\gamma\epsilon}}\right)^{1/p}<\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-2(e/5)^{4}e^{-21/5}}}<1.002.

Up to this point, we have shown that for the conditionally symmetric martingale
\{{\bm{M}}_{k}\}_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
, the following inequality holds:

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:rosenthal_{b}urkholder_{s}ymmetric}\left\|{\sup_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\|{\bm{M}}_{k}\|}\right\|_{p}\leq 5.01\!\left({1{+}e^{-p/c}{+}\frac{1}{c{\vee}\log d}}\right)\left({\sqrt{c{\vee}\log d}\ e^{p{/}c}\left\|{\left\|{\left\langle{{\bm{M}}}\right\rangle_{\infty}}\right\|^{1{/}2}}\right\|_{p}{+}(c{\vee}\log d)\left\|{\sup_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\left\|{{\bm{X}}_{k}}\right\|}\right\|_{p}}\right).

(11)

Setting
c=p
, we obtain the inequality in the form given in Theorem
2.1
.
Alternatively, we can choose
c
as the unique solution to equation
\sqrt{c}=e^{p/c}
, which yields another common form of the Rosenthal-Burkholder inequality
\|\sup_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\|{\bm{M}}_{k}\|\|_{p}\lesssim[(p/\log p)\vee\log d](\|\left\|{\left\langle{{\bm{M}}}\right\rangle_{\infty}}\right\|^{1/2}\|_{p}+\|\sup_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\|{\bm{X}}_{k}\|\|_{p})
.
We shall not delve into this form in further detail in this paper.

Dimension-Free:
To extend the result to a dimension-free version, we only need to replace
a=0
with

\tilde{a}=\beta\left({\delta_{1}^{-1}\left\|{\left\|{\left\langle{{\bm{M}}}\right\rangle_{\infty}}\right\|^{1/2}}\right\|_{p}+\delta_{2}^{-1}\left\|{\sup_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\left\|{{\bm{X}}_{k}}\right\|}\right\|_{p}}\right),

and
d
with

\tilde{d}=e\cdot r\left({{\bm{\Upsilon}}\wedge\left[{\left\|{\left\|{\left\langle{{\bm{M}}}\right\rangle_{\infty}}\right\|}\right\|_{\frac{p}{2}}}\right]}\right).

And we need to double the right-hand side of the inequality (
11
) due to the additional appearance of
\Phi(a)
, that is, if
\langle{\bm{M}}\rangle_{\infty}\preccurlyeq{\bm{\Upsilon}}
almost surely, then it holds that

\left\|{\sup_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\|{\bm{M}}_{k}\|}\right\|_{p}\leq 10.02\!\left({1{+}e^{-p/c}{+}\frac{1}{c{\vee}\log\tilde{d}}}\right)\left({\sqrt{c{\vee}\log\tilde{d}}\ e^{p{/}c}\left\|{\left\|{\left\langle{{\bm{M}}}\right\rangle_{\infty}}\right\|^{1{/}2}}\right\|_{p}{+}(c{\vee}\log\tilde{d})\left\|{\sup_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\left\|{{\bm{X}}_{k}}\right\|}\right\|_{p}}\right).

Step 2: Generalize to Arbitrary Martingales

In order to generalize the result under the conditionally symmetry assumption to arbitrary martingales, in the special case of independence increment, it suffices to introduce i.i.d. Rademacher random variables; while in the case of martingales, a more sophisticated technique, named decoupling tangent sequence
(
Kwapien and Woyczynski 1989
;
Hitczenko 1988
;
De la Pena and Giné 2012
)
, is required.
The proof in this part is similar to those of the scalar Rosenthal-Burkholder inequality
(
Hitczenko 1990
)
, and we defer the presentation to Appendix
A.3
.

4.2
Proof of the Matrix Rosenthal Inequality for Markov Chains

We consider the Poisson equation with the matrix forcing function
{\bm{F}}
:

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:Poisson_{e}quation}{\bm{G}}-\left({{\mathrm{Q}}{\bm{G}}}\right)={\bm{F}}.

(12)

In the following lemma, we show that
{\bm{G}}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}{\mathrm{Q}}^{k}{\bm{F}}
is a solution to the Poisson equation under different assumptions, and provide several upper bounds for
{\bm{G}}
. The proof can be found in Appendix
B.1
.

Lemma 4.2

(Solution to the Poisson Equation)
.

Consider the matrix Poisson equation (
12
). Then

(a)

Under Assumption
2.1
and
\|\|{\bm{F}}\|\|_{\infty}<\infty
, we have that
{\bm{G}}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}{\mathrm{Q}}^{k}{\bm{F}}
is a solution to the Poisson equation and satisfies
\|\|{\bm{G}}\|\|_{\infty}\leq(8/3)t_{\operatorname{mix}}\|\|{\bm{F}}\|\|_{\infty}
.
Furthermore, if there exists an
{\bm{\Upsilon}}\in{\mathbb{H}}^{d}_{+}
such that
{\bm{F}}^{2}(z)\preccurlyeq{\bm{\Upsilon}}
for all
z\in{\mathcal{Z}}
, then
{\bm{G}}^{2}(z)\preccurlyeq(64/9)t_{\operatorname{mix}}^{2}{\bm{\Upsilon}}
for all
z\in{\mathcal{Z}}
.

(b)

Under Assumption
2.2
and
\|\|{\bm{F}}\|\|_{V^{\alpha}}<\infty
for some
\alpha\in(0,1]
, we have that
{\bm{G}}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}{\mathrm{Q}}^{k}{\bm{F}}
is a solution to the Poisson equation and satisfies
\|{\bm{G}}(z)\|\leq[\varkappa^{\alpha}2^{3-\alpha}/(3\alpha)](\pi(V)+V(z))^{\alpha}t_{\operatorname{mix}}\|\|{\bm{F}}\|\|_{V^{\alpha}}
for any
z\in{\mathcal{Z}}
.

Using the Poisson equation Eqn. (
12
), we decompose the summation into a martingale, which is the dominant term, plus a remainder term. That is,

{\bm{S}}_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}{{\bm{F}}}(Z_{k})=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\left[{{\bm{G}}(Z_{k})-({\mathrm{Q}}{\bm{G}})(Z_{k})}\right]=\sum_{k=1}^{n}{\bm{X}}_{k}+{\bm{G}}(Z_{0})-{\bm{G}}(Z_{n})={\bm{M}}_{k}+{\bm{G}}(Z_{0})-{\bm{G}}(Z_{n}),

where
{\bm{X}}_{k}={\bm{G}}(Z_{k})-({\mathrm{Q}}{\bm{G}})(Z_{k-1})
is a
({\mathscr{F}}_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
-adapted martingale difference sequence, and we denote
{\bm{M}}_{k}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}{\bm{X}}_{j}
as the related martingale and
\langle{\bm{M}}\rangle_{k}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}{\mathbb{E}}_{j-1}[({\bm{X}}_{j})^{2}]
as the quadratic variation.
We define
{\bm{M}}_{0}={\bm{X}}_{0}=\bm{0}_{d\times d}
.

As in the proof of the Hilbert space-valued Rosenthal inequality for ergodic Markov chains
(
Durmus et al. 2023
)
, we first deal with stationary Markov chains, and then generalize the results to non-stationary chains.

Step 1: Stationary Markov Chains.

We assume
(Z_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is a stationary Markov chain, namely
Z_{0}\sim\pi
.
By Minkowski inequality and Lemma
4.2
, for all
p\geq 2
, we have

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:markov_{r}osenthal_{p}roof_{1}}\left\|{\sup_{k\leq n}\left\|{{\bm{S}}_{k}}\right\|}\right\|_{\pi,p}\leq\left\|{\sup_{k\leq n}\left\|{{\bm{M}}_{k}}\right\|}\right\|_{\pi,p}+2\left\|{\left\|{{\bm{G}}(Z_{0})}\right\|}\right\|_{\pi,p}.

(13)

We start with the dimension-dependent results.
For the martingale term, by the Rosenthal-Burkholder inequality (Theorem
2.1
), we have

\displaystyle\left\|{\sup_{k\leq n}\left\|{{\bm{M}}_{k}}\right\|}\right\|_{\pi,p}\leq

\displaystyle C_{R,1}\sqrt{p\vee\log d}\Big\|\left\|{\left\langle{{\bm{M}}}\right\rangle_{n}}\right\|^{1/2}\Big\|_{\pi,p}+C_{R,2}(p\vee\log d)\left\|{\sup_{k\leq n}\|{\bm{X}}_{k}\|}\right\|_{\pi,p},

(14)

where the quadratic variation term is dominant, because the second term is independent of
n
.

Now, we aim to give an upper bound for
\|\|\langle{\bm{M}}\rangle_{n}\|^{1/2}\|_{\pi,p}
.
Note that
\langle{{\bm{M}}}\rangle_{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}{\mathbb{E}}_{k-1}[({\bm{X}}_{k})^{2}]
and

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{k-1}\left[{\left({{\bm{X}}_{k}}\right)^{2}}\right]=

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\mathbb{E}}_{k-1}\left[{\left({{\bm{G}}(Z_{k})-({\mathrm{Q}}{\bm{G}})(Z_{k-1})}\right)^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left({{\mathrm{Q}}{\bm{G}}^{2}}\right)(Z_{k-1})-\left[{\left({{\mathrm{Q}}{\bm{G}}}\right)(Z_{k-1})}\right]^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\bm{H}}(Z_{k-1}),

where we defined
{\bm{H}}(z)=({\mathrm{Q}}{\bm{G}}^{2})(z)-[({\mathrm{Q}}{\bm{G}})(z)]^{2}
for any
z\in{\mathcal{Z}}
.
According to the Poisson equation Eqn. (
12
) and the definition of
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\pi}({\bm{F}})
(Eqn. (
2
)), we have

\displaystyle\pi\left({{\bm{H}}}\right)=

\displaystyle\pi\left({{\mathrm{Q}}{\bm{G}}^{2}-({\mathrm{Q}}{\bm{G}})^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\pi\left({{\bm{G}}^{2}-\left({{\bm{G}}-{\bm{F}}}\right)^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\pi\left({{{\bm{F}}}{\bm{G}}+{\bm{G}}{\bm{F}}-{{\bm{F}}}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\pi\left({{{\bm{F}}}^{2}}\right)+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\pi\left({{{\bm{F}}}{\mathrm{Q}}^{k}{{\bm{F}}}+\left({{\mathrm{Q}}^{k}{{\bm{F}}}}\right){{\bm{F}}}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}}_{\pi}({\bm{F}}).

By Minkowski’s inequality and the inequality
\sqrt{x+y}\leq\sqrt{x}+\sqrt{y}
, we have that

\displaystyle\Big\|\left\|{\left\langle{{\bm{M}}}\right\rangle_{n}}\right\|^{1/2}\Big\|_{\pi,p}=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\left\|{\left\|{\sum_{k=1}^{n}{\bm{H}}(Z_{k-1})}\right\|}\right\|_{\pi,p/2}}

(15)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sqrt{\left\|{n\left\|{\pi({\bm{H}})}\right\|+\left\|{\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left({{\bm{H}}(Z_{k-1})-\pi({\bm{H}})}\right)}\right\|}\right\|_{\pi,p/2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sqrt{n\left\|{{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\pi}({\bm{F}})}\right\|+\left\|{\left\|{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\bar{\bm{H}}(Z_{k})}\right\|}\right\|_{\pi,p/2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\left\|{{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\pi}({\bm{F}})}\right\|^{1/2}+\left\|{\left\|{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\bar{\bm{H}}(Z_{k})}\right\|}\right\|_{\pi,p/2}^{1/2},

where we denoted
\bar{{\bm{H}}}={{\bm{H}}}-\pi({\bm{H}})
.

Combining the upper bounds provided in Eqn. (
13
), Eqn. (
14
), Eqn. (
15
), we obtain

\displaystyle\left\|{\sup_{k\leq n}\left\|{{\bm{S}}_{k}}\right\|}\right\|_{\pi,p}\leq

\displaystyle C_{R,1}\sqrt{n(p\vee\log d)}\left\|{{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\pi}({\bm{F}})}\right\|^{1/2}+C_{R,1}\sqrt{p\vee\log d}\left\|{\left\|{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\bar{\bm{H}}(Z_{k})}\right\|}\right\|_{\pi,p/2}^{1/2}

(16)

\displaystyle+C_{R,2}(p\vee\log d)\left\|{\sup_{k\leq n}\|{\bm{X}}_{k}\|}\right\|_{\pi,p}+2\left\|{\left\|{{\bm{G}}(Z_{0})}\right\|}\right\|_{\pi,p},

where the first term is the leading term of order
\sqrt{n}
, and the second term is of order
n^{1/4}
because it involves the square root of the sum of
n
zero-mean functions of the Markov chain. The remaining two terms are independent of
n
.
Thus, the sum of these three residual terms is of order
O(n^{1/4})
.

Dimension-Free:
To get a dimension-free counterpart of the inequality (
16
) under Assumption
2.1
, the only difference is that we need to apply the dimension-free Rosenthal-Burkholder inequality to get a dimension-free counterpart of the inequality (
14
).
To this end, we need an upper bound for the quadratic variation:

\langle{{\bm{M}}}\rangle_{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}{\bm{H}}(Z_{k-1})=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left\{{\left({{\mathrm{Q}}{\bm{G}}^{2}}\right)(Z_{k-1})-\left[{\left({{\mathrm{Q}}{\bm{G}}}\right)(Z_{k-1})}\right]^{2}}\right\}\preccurlyeq\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left({{\mathrm{Q}}{\bm{G}}^{2}}\right)(Z_{k-1}).

If we assume Assumption
2.1
and
{\bm{F}}^{2}(z)\preccurlyeq{\bm{\Upsilon}}
, then by Lemma
4.2
(a), it holds that
\left({{\mathrm{Q}}{\bm{G}}^{2}}\right)(Z_{k-1})\preccurlyeq(64/9)t_{\operatorname{mix}}^{2}{\bm{\Upsilon}}
, thus
\langle{{\bm{M}}}\rangle_{n}\preccurlyeq(64/9)nt_{\operatorname{mix}}^{2}{\bm{\Upsilon}}
.
By Theorem
2.1
(b), the inequality (
14
) still holds if we replace
d
by
d_{n,p}=e\cdot r([(64/9)t_{\operatorname{mix}}^{2}{\bm{\Upsilon}}]\wedge[(1/n)\|\|\left\langle{{\bm{M}}}\right\rangle_{n}\|\|_{\pi,\frac{p}{2}}])
, and double the right-hand side.
As
n\to\infty
, by ergodicity and the fact that
\pi({\bm{H}})={\bm{\Sigma}}_{\pi}({\bm{F}})
, we find that
d_{n,p}\to e\cdot r([(64/9)t_{\operatorname{mix}}^{2}{\bm{\Upsilon}}]\wedge\|{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\pi}({\bm{F}})\|)
, which is a natural dimension factor.

Residual Terms:

Next, we provide the specific forms of the upper bounds for these three residual terms under different assumptions.
For the term of order
n^{1/4}
, we need the following lemma, which is a crude version of the Rosenthal inequality for uniformly geometrically ergodic Markov chains.

.

(a)

Under Assumption

and
\|\|{\bm{F}}\|\|_{\infty}<\infty
, we have that for any

,

\displaystyle\left\|{\sup_{k\leq n}\left\|{{\bm{S}}_{k}}\right\|}\right\|_{\pi,p}\leq

\displaystyle\frac{16}{3}C_{R,1}\sqrt{(p\vee\log d)nt_{\operatorname{mix}}}\left\|{\left\|{{\bm{F}}}\right\|}\right\|_{\infty}+\left({\frac{16}{3}C_{R,2}(p\vee\log d)+\frac{19}{3}}\right)t_{\operatorname{mix}}\left\|{\left\|{{\bm{F}}}\right\|}\right\|_{\infty},

Under Assumption

Step 2: Generalize to Non-Stationary Markov Chains

In order to generalize the result to non-stationary Markov chains, we need to leverage the maximal exact coupling argument

.

(

\left\|{\left\|{{\bm{F}}}\right\|}\right\|_{\infty}<\infty

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\left\|{\sup_{k\leq n}\left\|{{\bm{S}}_{k}}\right\|}\right\|_{\xi,p}\leq\frac{1}{n}\left\|{\sup_{k\leq n}\left\|{{\bm{S}}_{k}}\right\|}\right\|_{\pi,p}+\frac{2}{n}\left({1+128(t_{\operatorname{mix}}/\log 4)^{p}(p+1)^{p+1/2}e^{-p}}\right)^{1/p}\left\|{\left\|{{\bm{F}}}\right\|}\right\|_{\infty}.

5
Conclusions

In this work, we have studied probability bounds for the spectral norm of sums of dependent random matrices.
First, we have proved a Rosenthal-Burkholder inequality for matrix martingales using the extrapolation method (good
\lambda
inequality)
(
Hitczenko 1990
;
Hitczenko 1994
;
Pinelis 1994
)
and matrix Bennett inequality
(
Tropp 2011
;
Kroshnin and Suvorikova 2024
)
.
Furthermore, combining this martingale inequality with the Poisson equation approach
(
Durmus et al. 2023
)
, we have derived matrix Rosenthal inequalities for Markov chains under both the uniform geometric ergodicity assumption and the geometric
V
-ergodicity assumption.
By Markov inequality, these moment inequalities can be further translated into concentration inequalities including Hoeffding and Bernstein inequalities.
Through more careful analysis, we have also established dimension-free versions of the above inequalities.

Compared with previous results based on the León-Perron argument
(
León and Perron 2004
)
, our results yield a leading term that matches the Markov chain CLT, rather than relying on a variance proxy (
t_{\operatorname{mix}}\|{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi}[{\bm{F}}^{2}(Z_{0})]\|
or
t_{\operatorname{mix}}\|\|{\bm{F}}\|\|^{2}
). And our results do not require the assumption of a non-zero absolute spectral gap.
This expands the applicability to a broader range of problems, such as policy evaluation in reinforcement learning
(
Duan and Wainwright 2022
)
and PCA
(
Kumar and Sarkar 2023
)
with Markovian data.
In contrast to prior works relying on the multi-matrix Golden-Thompson inequality
(
Sutter et al. 2017
;
Garg et al. 2018
)
, our results can be extended to dimension-free versions, enabling applications to infinite-dimensional settings.
Moreover, our approach is not restricted to the uniform geometric ergodicity assumption, so we can derive the results under more general ergodicity assumptions, and in some cases we do not require the functions to be bounded.
The advantages of our results over previous works are demonstrated in the covariance estimation and PCA problems on Markovian data.

One direction for future work is to explore whether we can obtain residual terms with sharp dependence on
t_{\operatorname{mix}}
.
This might require more sophisticated tools from matrix analysis, beyond the properties of self-adjoint matrices related to the Löwner order.
Another promising direction is to extend our results to time-inhomogeneous functions while retaining the advantages mentioned above.
For time-inhomogeneous functions, the standard Poisson equation approach no longer works, and more advanced tools from Markov chain theory would be necessary.

\displaystyle\leq
