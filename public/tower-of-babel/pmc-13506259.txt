Progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy in an untreated chronic lymphocytic leukemia patient: Case report and review of the literature
Authors: Samin Khoei, Michael Marshall, Eric Basappa, Santhosh Gaddikeri, Miral Jhaveri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy (PML), caused by John Cunningham virus (JCV), is a rare opportunistic infection in patients with chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) after immunosuppressive therapy, with only a handful of cases reported in treatment-naïve patients. Herein we present a 71-year-old man with treatment-naïve CLL who developed rapidly progressive left facial weakness, hemiparesis, dysarthria, and urinary incontinence. Brain magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated characteristic imaging findings of PML, including multifocal white matter lesions involving the subcortical U-fibers with the Milky Way sign and rim-and-core diffusion restriction, as well as atypical findings of internal perivascular enhancement ultimately raising suspicion for JCV infection despite the absence of prior CLL-directed therapy. The diagnosis was confirmed by cerebrospinal fluid analysis and stereotactic brain biopsy demonstrating JCV-associated PML. Despite supportive management, the patient experienced rapid neurological decline and died approximately 1 month after symptom onset. This case highlights that PML should be considered in untreated patients with advanced CLL and emphasizes the importance of recognizing characteristic MRI findings to facilitate timely diagnosis of this rare but devastating complication.

Introduction

Chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) is the most common leukemia in developed countries, with an age-adjusted incidence of 4-5 per 100,000. The underlying pathology consists of clonal proliferation and accumulation of malignant B lymphocytes in bone marrow, vascular, and lymphoid tissues. Infection is a frequent cause of death in these patients; encapsulated bacteria are the major pathogens in untreated CLL subjects, and opportunistic infections are mainly encountered after starting immunosuppressant treatment [1].

Progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy (PML) is a rare demyelinating disease caused by the reactivation of the dormant opportunistic John Cunningham Virus (JCV) in oligodendrocytes. Due to modern immunosuppressive therapeutic techniques, PML is almost exclusively seen in patients with hematologic malignancies, autoimmune diseases, or transplant recipients. Although the overall incidence of PML in CLL patients is 11.1 per 100,000 person-years, its occurrence in treatment-naïve patients remains very uncommon, with only 5 cases reported since 1984 [2,3].

Given its rapidly progressive nature, early detection of PML is essential and linked to better outcomes. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is crucial in the workup, and confirmation can be achieved through CSF analysis and/or brain biopsy [3,4].

Here, we present a case of PML in an untreated CLL patient with a rapid disease course and highlight key radiologic and immunologic findings.

Case presentation

A 71-year-old male with lifestyle-controlled type 2 diabetes mellitus was diagnosed with CLL in 2023 after a work-up of incidentally encountered pancytopenia. Multisystemic CT scans revealed diffuse lymphadenopathy and splenomegaly. Despite informed discussion and meeting criteria for chemotherapy, the patient declined conventional CLL treatment and opted for a more holistic approach. Unfortunately, the disease gradually progressed, and he eventually required frequent blood transfusions, developed chronic renal insufficiency, and suffered recurrent infections.

In 2025, the patient presented to the hospital with 3 days of left-sided facial droop and urinary incontinence, with progression to left-sided weakness and dysarthria. Vitals were normal, and examination showed left hemisensory loss and hemiparesis. CBC revealed mild leukopenia, and serum immunofixation electrophoresis and urine protein electrophoresis resulted in a polyclonal gammopathy with normal serum IgA and IgM, and markedly elevated IgG (6678 mg/dL) without evidence of paraproteinemia. Initial noncontrast brain CT (Fig. 1) demonstrated asymmetric, subcortical hypoattenuation in the right frontal lobe without mass effect or cortical involvement. A subsequent brain MRI (Fig. 2A-D) depicted a corresponding region of right frontal T2/FLAIR hyperintensity involving the subcortical and deep white matter with extension along subcortical U-fibers (Fig. 2A) and across the corpus callosum (Fig. 2B). Stippled peripheral nodules of FLAIR hyperintensity were also present about the dominant focus, producing a milky way sign. Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) predominantly showed a central core of low diffusion signal and rim of high signal in a rim-and-core pattern, (Fig. 2C) with minimal ADC hypointensity on the posterior edge (Fig. 2D). At this point, infarction was the primary concern, so head and neck MR angiograms (not included) were performed and found to be unremarkable. Further stroke work-up with echocardiography revealed a small mobile mass attached to the ventricular left coronary cusp, and a blood culture resulted in no growth. Thus, broad spectrum treatment with vancomycin and ceftriaxone was initiated for presumed culture-negative infectious endocarditis. However, because the patient continued to deteriorate clinically after 2 weeks of antibiotic therapy a new etiology was sought after. A follow-up brain MRI with contrast (Fig. 3A-D) demonstrated worsening diffusion and T2/FLAIR signal abnormalities, with multiple internal foci of nodular enhancement in the right greater than left corona radiata/centrum semiovale. Interestingly, 10-color flow cytometric analysis did not show conclusive evidence of lymphoma, but note was made of a slightly degraded sample. Discussion

Treatment-associated immune dysregulation is purported as the main causative pathology of PML in CLL patients [1]. Our case illustrates that advanced CLL alone may provide sufficient immunosuppression to permit PML via JCV reactivation. MRI plays a key diagnostic role, and our case further emphasizes the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion necessary for the diagnosis of PML.

Since the introduction of Highly Active Retroviral Therapy (HAART) in 1997 and concurrent use of purine analogs for lymphoproliferative disorders, PML has been primarily observed in patients with hematologic/solid malignancies or autoimmune disease. JCV is a DNA, non-enveloped polyomavirus that infects more than half of the world population. Initial contamination with the archetypal virus occurs via the respiratory and fecal-oral route, after which it is hematogenously spread and becomes latent within multiple organ systems such as bone marrow and lymphoid tissues. Components of the immune system involved in JCV clearance include CD4+ and CD8+ T lymphocyte interaction and B lymphocytes, which release JCV-specific antibodies and present antigens to T cells. When these immune systems are compromised, JCV can rearrange its genome to become neurotropic. The virus is then transported by B lymphocytes to the CNS, where it replicates within oligodendrocytes, astrocytes, and cerebellar granule cells, leading to demyelination. Infrequently, virus reactivation presents as encephalitis, meningitis, and/or granule cell neuropathy [[2], [3], [4]].

Our case, along with 5 similar published cases (Table 2), highlights the emergence of PML in untreated CLL patients. In this situation, it is believed that CLL can alter gene expression within T lymphocytes, resulting in upregulation of the exhausted phenotype and impairment of CD4+ cell differentiation, ultimately leading to hypogammaglobulinemia. Not all CLL patients show T lymphocyte deficiencies, however. Our patient demonstrated elevated immunoglobulin levels, making hypogammaglobulinemia an unlikely contributor to the development of PML. As previously mentioned, expression of the exhausted phenotype is increased in the CD4+ and CD8+ T cell populations in PML, which could explain the emergence of PML in our treatment-naïve patient, though no data on the patient’s CD4/8+ count is available. In addition, as in our patient, polyclonal gammopathy has been reported in patients with CLL and is posed to result from the release of cytokines and stimulation of overactive, dysfunctional immunoglobulins from CLL cells [6].

On imaging, lesions are mostly in the supratentorial subcortical and periventricular white matter, especially in the frontal and parietal lobes, followed by the cerebellum and brainstem. They are typically asymmetric and bilateral, can involve subcortical U-fibers, and usually do not show mass effect unless of a specific subtype (discussed below). T2-weighted and FLAIR sequences reveal heterogeneous hyperintensity and may reveal characteristic intralesional vacuoles. A well-known imaging pattern is the “milky way sign,” which consists of a dominant T2/FLAIR hyperintense focus with peripheral punctate foci that likely represent the perivascular inflammatory infiltrate. In the infratentorial compartment, lesions often affect the middle cerebellar peduncles and cerebellar white matter. A “shrimp sign,” where the T2/FLAIR hyperintensity of the cerebellar white matter approaches but spares the dentate nuclei, has been documented. On T1-weighted sequences, lesions are isointense to white matter early on and become more hypointense as the disease progresses. On DWI, active or new lesions typically show a leading edge of restricted diffusion with central hypointensity in a “rim and core pattern,” while chronic lesions are unrestricted. Although nonspecific, SWI can show a linear hypointensity at the gray–white matter junction that may extend to the deep grey matter, substantia nigra, and dentate nucleus in later stages. This may be due to iron deposition in macrophages, disruption of the blood–brain barrier, or cortical laminar necrosis. Regarding contrast enhancement, 3 distinct subgroups of PML are explained in the literature: Classical PML (cPML), Inflammatory PML (iPML), and PML-IRIS (Immune Reconstitution Inflammatory Syndrome). Classical is the most frequent subtype, and the lesions do not enhance. This category is featured with rapid lesional progression, poor prognosis and is believed to occur in the absence of a significant immune response to the disease. PML-IRIS is the most extreme form of inflammatory response to the PML lesions and shows prominent contrast enhancement, edema, and mass effect. This subgroup was described in HIV patients after initiation of HAART, featured by a rapid clinical deterioration of the disease, and is thought to be due to restoration of the immune system. It is important to note that there are 2 subtypes of PML-IRIS (PML-s-IRIS and PML-d-IRIS), which differ based upon whether PML was present before immune reconstitution (-d-) or not (-s-). A new, more recently described pattern is iPML, which was historically observed in MS-targeted Natalizumab-treated patients and encountered even in the early stages of PML, when Natalizumab was not yet discontinued. It shows a punctate perivascular distribution of enhancement and may be a favorable outcome indicator due to partial and incomplete immune suppression in most studies (although in a study performed by Zeiner et al. [9], patients with classical and inflammatory PML did not show a significant difference in their survival rates). This pattern of enhancement corresponds to perivascular infiltrate of T lymphocytes and plasma cells in tissue specimens. However, to date, such a pattern has not been mentioned in PML in the context of hematologic malignancies.

Although our patient was not clinically stable enough to undergo advanced imaging (eg, MR perfusion, MR spectroscopy, and tractography), use for functional imaging has emerged as a potential adjunctive tool to help better characterize PML and its differential. Briefly, limited data on MR spectroscopy has shown significantly elevated choline and lipase, and significantly reduced NAA. MR perfusion has demonstrated predominant reduced perfusion (low rCBV and elevated MTT) with infrequent elevated perfusion on the leading edge, reflecting the absence of angiogenesis and destructive demyelination. Although perfusion MRI can aid differentiation from neoplastic processes, overlap with ischemic lesions limits its ability to distinguish PML from infarction in isolation. Diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) has demonstrated disruptions in fractional anisotropy and white matter microstructure in PML patients, suggesting potential use for characterizing early demyelination and disease evolution. Theoretically, DTI would demonstrate preferential involvement along certain fiber tracts and extension beyond FLAIR abnormality. However, current evidence remains limited to small case series and reports, and DTI is not routinely used for diagnosis [[7], [8], [9]].

Our patient presented with supratentorial and callosal lesions with the “milky way” sign on T2/FLAIR and the “rim and core” pattern on DWI, as well as lack of mass effect on the first MRI. Interestingly, the second MRI showed a new nodular-perivascular pattern of enhancement in the right-sided component, which is most compatible with iPML and correlates with the CD3+ T lymphocyte perivascular infiltrates observed in the histopathology. PML-IRIS was felt to be unlikely given that the patient did not have an immune response to mount and was not treated with anti-retrovirals, chemotherapy or immunosuppressants.

The imaging differential diagnosis includes HIV encephalitis, acute disseminated encephalomyelitis (ADEM), opportunistic infection (eg, CMV), and CNS lymphoma. If contrast enhancement is present, the differential also includes opportunistic infectious agents such as cryptococcus, toxoplasmosis, and TB. In TB, one may also see meningeal involvement, which is atypical of all PML groups. In our patient, lack of mass effect, non-vascular territorial distribution, and presence of the “rim and core” pattern on DWI all argue against infarction. Additionally, the patient tested negative for HIV and other opportunistic infections and did not have any recent vaccination/infection to suggest ADEM (which can also involve the gray matter and is typically monophasic). HIV Encephalitis is also usually symmetric with a component of atrophy, and CMV has been associated with other findings such as ventriculitis.

The final diagnosis is made through a combination of clinical assessment, imaging, CSF analysis, and, at times, brain biopsy. It is important to note that in 5%-25% of cases, CSF PCR for JCV results in a false negative. Thus, radiologists' familiarity with PML’s imaging features is essential for accurate clinical guidance, particularly when initial results are false negative. Notably, Isidoro et al. [10] reported a case of PML in a CLL patient, in whom the initial brain CT, CSF analysis, and brain biopsy results were all negative, and the correct diagnosis was established only after a follow-up brain MRI prompted a third, repeat CSF PCR test [[1], [2], [3], [4],10].

No direct antiviral agent exists for JCV, and treatment focuses on restoring immune response. This can be challenging in hematologic malignancies, as restoration may lead to underlying neoplastic progression. Consequently, PML carries a 90% 2-month mortality rate in this population. While various documented experimental therapies have generally failed to show statistically significant benefit, a recent study by Olivieri et al. [11] described using Venetoclax and Pembrolizumab in a relapsed CLL patient with PML. Over a 2-year follow-up, the patient achieved CSF viral clearance, MRI lesion reduction, and clinical improvement. Nevertheless, more data with a larger patient population is necessary to support these findings [[2], [3], [4], [5],11]. In our patient, initiating Pembrolizumab was of unclear benefit as the underlying CLL was untreated and the overall prognosis was poor. As a result, the patient and clinical team together decided against PML-targeted treatment.

Conclusion

We present a rare case of PML in a treatment-naïve CLL patient and emphasize the radiological findings, differential diagnosis, and immunologic changes that allow CLL to act as the sole cause of immunosuppression. Recognizing characteristic radiological signs and maintaining a high index of suspicion are essential for timely diagnosis of this rapidly progressive disease.
