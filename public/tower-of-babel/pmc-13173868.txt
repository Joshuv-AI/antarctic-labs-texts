Advanced glycation end-products and sclerostin are elevated across diabetes phenotypes and associated with vertebral fractures in type 2 diabetes
Authors: Sevdenur Taskin Kursun, Eren Imre, Dilek Gogas Yavuz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo investigate the associations of circulating advanced glycation end-products (AGEs; carboxymethyllysine and pentosidine) and sclerostin with bone mineral density (BMD) and vertebral fractures in adults with type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM), type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), and latent autoimmune diabetes in adults (LADA).MethodsIn this cross-sectional case–control study, 76 patients with T1DM, 91 with T2DM, and 40 with LADA were compared with 85 non-diabetic controls. Anthropometric variables, biochemical parameters, and bone turnover markers (osteocalcin and β-CTX) were assessed. Serum AGEs and sclerostin were quantified by ELISA. Areal BMD was measured by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) at the lumbar spine (L1–L4) and femoral neck. Vertebral fractures were identified on spine radiographs using Genant’s semiquantitative method. Multivariable analyses were performed to determine independent predictors of vertebral fractures.ResultsSerum carboxymethyllysine, pentosidine, and sclerostin concentrations were significantly higher, while osteocalcin and β-CTX were significantly lower, in all diabetes groups versus controls (all p < 0.001). Vertebral fractures were present in 14.49% of participants with T1DM, 34.2% with T2DM, and 27.5% with LADA. In fracture-stratified analyses within the T2DM group, participants with vertebral fractures were older and had higher carboxymethyllysine levels than those without fractures. In multivariable analysis restricted to the T2DM group, age (AOR 1.079; 95% CI 1.019–1.143; p = 0.009) and pentosidine (AOR 1.848; 95% CI 1.030–3.316; p = 0.040) were independently associated with vertebral fractures, whereas carboxymethyllysine was not.ConclusionElevated AGEs and sclerostin together with suppressed bone turnover markers were observed across diabetes phenotypes and are consistent with altered bone metabolism. In T2DM, age and pentosidine were independently associated with prevalent vertebral fractures; however, given the cross-sectional design, these associations should not be interpreted as evidence of a direct mechanistic pathway. Longitudinal studies are needed to clarify whether these biomarker patterns reflect diabetes severity, skeletal fragility, or both.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Diabetes mellitus, LADA, Advanced glycation end-products, Sclerostin, Bone mineral density, Vertebral fractures, Osteoporosis

Background

The detrimental effects of diabetes on the skeletal system are increasingly recognized as part of its systemic metabolic complications. Skeletal fragility has emerged as a frequent and clinically significant complication of diabetes, particularly in older adults [1].

In patients with type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM), the lifetime risk of hip fractures is up to seven-fold higher than in non-diabetic individuals, with fractures often occurring at younger ages—reflecting the absence of insulin’s anabolic effects on bone formation [2, 3]. In type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), fracture risk is nearly twofold higher despite normal or even increased bone mineral density (BMD) compared with age-matched non-diabetic individuals [4]. Although both T1DM and T2DM share common pathogenetic mechanisms such as low bone turnover, their skeletal characteristics differ. In T2DM, increased cortical thickness and porosity, reduced trabecular density, longer disease duration, and the use of certain antidiabetic agents (e.g., thiazolidinediones, sulfonylureas, insulin), along with a higher propensity for falls, have all been associated with elevated fracture risk [5, 6].

Several mechanisms contribute to the pathogenesis of diabetic bone disease. One key mechanism involves the accumulation of advanced glycation end-products (AGEs), which cross-link collagen fibers, stiffen the bone matrix, and impair both osteoblast and osteoclast function, ultimately reducing bone material strength [7–9]. In T2DM patients with hip fractures, femoral head AGE concentrations were approximately twofold higher than in non-diabetic controls and were inversely correlated with bone microarchitectural parameters [10].

Another important factor is sclerostin, a glycoprotein secreted by osteocytes that inhibits Wnt/β-catenin signaling and suppresses bone formation. Elevated circulating sclerostin levels have been consistently reported in T2DM and are associated with an increased risk of vertebral fractures [11, 12]. In contrast, patients with latent autoimmune diabetes in adults (LADA) display bone resorption patterns similar to those seen in T2DM, yet elevated sclerostin levels appear confined to T2DM, suggesting subtype-specific mechanisms of skeletal fragility [13].

Increased AGE accumulation, either directly or through RAGE (receptor for advanced glycation end-products) activation, promotes osteocyte apoptosis and upregulates SOST gene expression, leading to higher sclerostin secretion and inhibition of bone formation [14]. This AGE–sclerostin interaction has been proposed as a possible molecular link between hyperglycemia and diabetic bone fragility, although the present findings do not establish a direct mechanistic pathway.

The present study therefore aimed to evaluate serum AGEs, sclerostin levels, bone mineral density, and vertebral fracture prevalence across diabetes subtypes—T1DM, T2DM, and LADA—and to investigate the relationships between these biochemical markers, BMD, and vertebral fragility fractures.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional case–control study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Marmara University (Protocol No. 09.2022.1402; Approval Date: 09 November 2022) and supported by the Marmara University Scientific Research Projects Commission (BAPKO, Project ID: 10980). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment.

Patients with diabetes who were followed at the outpatient Endocrinology and Metabolism Clinics of Marmara University between January and September 2023 were screened for eligibility. The study included 76 patients with type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM), 91 with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), and 40 with latent autoimmune diabetes in adults (LADA). An additional 85 non-diabetic healthy individuals were enrolled as the control group. Controls were recruited during the same study period and were included as a non-diabetic comparison group.

Inclusion criteria were a confirmed diagnosis of T1DM for at least 5 years or T2DM/LADA for at least 5 years, and age ≥ 18 years. Exclusion criteria included a history of chronic inflammatory or infectious disease, malignancy, renal failure, pregnancy, lactation, current or prior use of glucocorticoids or anti-osteoporotic medications, and previous diagnosis or treatment of osteoporosis.

Data collection

Demographic and clinical data—including age, sex, menopausal status, diabetes type and duration, comorbidities, diabetes-related complications, and medication use—were extracted from hospital medical records. Anthropometric measurements (height, weight, body mass index [BMI]) and blood pressure were obtained during outpatient visits.

Venous blood samples were collected after an overnight fast. Serum samples were specifically obtained for the measurement of sclerostin, carboxymethyllysine (CML), and pentosidine, and stored at − 80 °C until analysis. Routine biochemical and hormonal parameters—including fasting plasma glucose, HbA1c, creatinine, lipid profile, uric acid, calcium, phosphorus, alkaline phosphatase (ALP), 25-hydroxyvitamin D [25(OH)D], parathyroid hormone (PTH), osteocalcin, and β-CrossLaps (β-CTX)—were retrieved from patient medical records within the preceding three months.

Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg)/height² (m²). Blood pressure was measured after at least 10 min of rest, with the subject seated, using an Omron M2 sphygmomanometer.

Fasting plasma glucose (mg/dL) was determined by the enzymatic hexokinase method, and HbA1c (%) by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) (Trinity Biotech). Serum creatinine was measured using the Jaffe method, and lipid parameters (total cholesterol, high density lipoprotein- cholesterol (HDL-C), triglycerides, uric acid) were determined enzymatically on a Beckman Coulter AU5800 analyzer. Low density lipoprotein- cholesterol (LDL-C) (mg/dL) was calculated using the Friedewald equation, and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was estimated using the CKD-EPI formula.

Serum PTH was measured by chemiluminescence immunoassay (Access Immunoassay Systems, Beckman Coulter UniCel DxI 800). Serum 25(OH)D, ALP, β-CTX, and osteocalcin were analyzed by electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (ECLIA) (Modular Analytics E170, Roche Diagnostics). Serum calcium and phosphorus were determined by photometric colorimetry (Beckman Coulter).

Serum sclerostin was quantified using a Human Sclerostin (SOST) ELISA kit (Sunred Biotechnology, Shanghai, China; sensitivity 0.175 ng/mL; intra-assay CV < 12%; inter-assay CV < 10%). Carboxymethyllysine (CML) and pentosidine, representative AGE biomarkers, were measured using Human CML and Human Pentosidine ELISA kits (Sunred Biotechnology, Shanghai, China; sensitivities 8.013 ng/mL and 0.125 ng/mL, respectively), in accordance with manufacturers’ protocols.

Bone mineral density and vertebral fracture assessment

Bone mineral density (BMD) was measured at the lumbar spine (L1–L4), femoral neck, and trochanteric region using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) (GE Lunar Prodigy Primo). Results are reported as g/cm², T-scores, and Z-scores. For postmenopausal women and men aged ≥ 50 years, densitometric categories were defined using WHO T-score criteria (osteoporosis: T-score ≤ − 2.5; osteopenia/low bone mass: −1.0 to − 2.5; normal: ≥ −1.0 at either site). For premenopausal women and men aged < 50 years, Z-scores were interpreted in accordance with ISCD positions; a Z-score ≤ − 2.0 was classified as “below expected range for age (low BMD for chronological age)” and was not termed “osteoporosis” based on BMD alone [15]. Accordingly, categorical prevalence estimates were reported using the appropriate diagnostic framework (T-score-based for older/postmenopausal participants; Z-score-based for younger/premenopausal participants), and between-group comparisons of categorical “osteoporosis” prevalence should be interpreted with caution because to framework heterogeneity.

Lateral thoracolumbar spine radiographs were obtained for all diabetic participants to assess vertebral fractures. Vertebral deformities were evaluated semiquantitatively using Genant’s method [16], grading deformities as follows: Grade 0: normal (no deformity), Grade 1: mild (≈ 20–25% height reduction), Grade 2: moderate (≈ 25–40% height reduction), Grade 3: severe (> 40% height reduction).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 30.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and median values, and categorical variables were expressed as number (n) and percentage (%).

Group differences in categorical variables were evaluated using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Continuous variables among the four study groups (Type 1 diabetes mellitus, Type 2 diabetes mellitus, LADA, and control) were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis test. When an overall significant difference was detected, post-hoc pairwise comparisons were performed with Bonferroni correction.

Bone mineral density and bone metabolism markers were further evaluated using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) adjusting for age, sex, and body mass index. When the overall ANCOVA was significant, post-hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted with Bonferroni correction. Within each diabetic group, patients with and without vertebral fractures were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables.

Factors associated with vertebral fractures were examined using logistic regression analysis. Separate regression models were constructed for each diabetes phenotype. Variables with p < 0.25 in univariate analysis were considered candidates for inclusion in the multivariable logistic regression model. Multicollinearity among candidate predictors was assessed using variance inflation factors. If no variable met this criterion, a multivariable model was not constructed for that group. Results are presented as odds ratios (OR) and adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Model calibration was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, and model explanatory performance was summarized using Nagelkerke R². A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The mean age was significantly higher in the T2DM group (55.9 ± 9.1 years) compared to T1DM (30.6 ± 8.9 years), LADA (47.0 ± 9.4 years), and controls (37.4 ± 10.0 years) (p < 0.001). Diabetes duration was longest in T1DM (15.9 ± 8.0 years) (p < 0.001). Significant differences were observed among the study groups.

All patients with T1DM and LADA were receiving insulin therapy, 67% of the T2DM group required insulin treatment. There were no significant differences between the groups in vitamin D supplementation or antidepressant use.

Although serum creatinine levels were comparable across the groups, the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was significantly reduced in type 2 diabetes (T2DM) and LADA compared with T1DM and controls (p < 0.001).

Vertebral fracture assessment

The prevalence of vertebral fractures on radiography was 14.4% in the T1DM group, 34.0% in the T2DM group, and 27.5% in LADA group, higher in T2DM group compared to T1DM and LADA group (p = 0.0048). Among participants with vertebral fractures, the mean number of fractures per affected patient was 1.39 ± 1.53 (median 1) in T1DM, 1.90 ± 1.90 (median 2) in T2DM, and 2.25 ± 1.93 (median 2) in LADA. However, after adjustment for age, sex, and BMI, the number of vertebral fractures and grade 2–3 vertebral fracture count did not differ significantly across diabetes phenotypes (Table 4).

Association of fracture presence with demographic and clinical parameters

Within-phenotype fracture-stratified analyses showed that in the T2DM group, participants with vertebral fractures were older than those without fractures (58.1 ± 8.5 vs. 52.9 ± 9.2 years, p = 0.003). Among AGE-related biomarkers, CML levels were higher in T2DM participants with vertebral fractures than in those without fractures (350.63 ± 93.02 vs. 337.30 ± 167.62 ng/mL, p = 0.024), whereas pentosidine (p = 0.061) and sclerostin (p = 0.262) did not differ significantly by vertebral fracture status in T2DM .

Regarding bone turnover markers, β-CTX did not differ significantly between T2DM participants with and without vertebral fractures (0.337 ± 0.152 vs. 0.313 ± 0.187 ng/mL, p = 0.158). Thiazolidinedione use was also comparable between T2DM participants with and without vertebral fractures (5.7% vs. 7.9%, p = 0.999) (Table 5). In LADA, participants with vertebral fractures were older than those without fractures (49.9 ± 7.8 vs. 39.2 ± 8.9 years, p = 0.003) and had lower lumbar spine BMD (1.077 ± 0.138 vs. 1.188 ± 0.121 g/cm², p = 0.026), whereas no significant age difference was observed in the T1DM fracture-stratified comparison.

Correlation analyses

In the T1DM group, β-CTX showed a negative correlation with HbA1c (r = − 0.39, p < 0.05) and a strong positive correlation with osteocalcin (r = 0.75, p < 0.05). CML showed positive correlations with pentosidine (r = 0.70, p < 0.05) and sclerostin (r = 0.72, p < 0.05), while pentosidine also correlated positively with sclerostin (r = 0.82, p < 0.05). Osteocalcin was positively correlated only with β-CTX (r = 0.75, p < 0.05), supporting coupled changes in bone formation and resorption in T1DM.

In the T2DM group, carboxymethyllysine (CML) was positively correlated with pentosidine (r = 0.45, p < 0.05) and sclerostin (r = 0.28, p < 0.05), and showed a positive point-biserial correlation with vertebral fracture presence (r = 0.24, p < 0.05). Pentosidine was also positively correlated with sclerostin (r = 0.51, p < 0.05) and inversely correlated with osteocalcin (r = − 0.32, p < 0.05). In addition, HbA1c showed an inverse correlation with β-CTX (r = − 0.22, p < 0.05).

In the LADA group, osteocalcin demonstrated a strong positive correlation with β-CTX (r = 0.65, p < 0.05). CML was positively correlated with pentosidine (r = 0.36, p < 0.05) and sclerostin (r = 0.38, p < 0.05), and pentosidine showed a strong positive correlation with sclerostin (r = 0.76, p < 0.05). No significant correlations were observed between sclerostin and BMD parameters in this group.

Discussion

In this cross-sectional case-control study comparing adults with T1DM, T2DM, and LADA with non-diabetic controls, we identified a consistent biochemical profile suggestive of diabetes-related bone disease: circulating AGEs (carboxymethyllysine and pentosidine) and sclerostin were elevated, whereas the bone turnover markers osteocalcin and β-CTX were reduced across diabetes phenotypes. Vertebral fractures were common (14.4% in T1DM, 34.0% in T2DM, and 27.5% in LADA) and were observed in T2DM despite relatively preserved BMD, which is consistent with an association between AGE-related alterations in bone material properties and skeletal fragility beyond areal BMD measurements [17–19].

Areal BMD differed by diabetes phenotype. Patients with T1DM and LADA exhibited lower lumbar spine and femoral neck BMD than controls, whereas T2DM patients demonstrated relatively preserved or higher femoral neck BMD values. This pattern reinforces that areal BMD alone may not adequately reflect fracture risk in diabetes, particularly in T2DM where increased fracture susceptibility has been documented despite normal or increased bone mass [20–22]. Importantly, several BMD differences across diabetes phenotypes remained significant after adjustment for age, sex, and BMI, supporting that these patterns were not solely explained by demographic/body composition differences. In contrast, between-group differences in vertebral fracture burden measures were attenuated after adjustment, suggesting that baseline clinical heterogeneity may have influenced fracture-related comparisons.

Despite these BMD patterns, vertebral fractures were frequent: 14.4% in the T1DM group, 34.0% in the T2DM group, and 27.5% in the LADA group. These rates were markedly higher than the reported vertebral fracture prevalence in the general population [1] and are consistent with prior evidence that overall fracture risk is substantially increased in both T1DM and T2DM [23–26]. The highest fracture burden in T2DM despite relatively preserved BMD, together with the greater number of fractures per patient in LADA, supports the concept that diabetes-associated bone disease is predominantly a disorder of bone quality, including microarchitectural and material property alterations, rather than bone quantity [23, 24]. Although TZD use did not differ by vertebral fracture status in our T2DM subgroup, anti-diabetic medication exposure remains a potential source of residual confounding, and the limited number of TZD users precluded stable inclusion of this variable in the multivariable model.

Bone turnover markers indicated a generalized low-turnover state across diabetes phenotypes. Beta-CTX levels were significantly reduced in all diabetic groups compared with controls, and osteocalcin levels were lower —particularly in T2DM—, supporting impaired remodeling in diabetes [23, 24]. The strong positive correlations between osteocalcin and beta-CTX across T1DM and LADA suggest coupled suppression of bone formation and resorption. In T1DM, the negative correlation between beta-CTX and HbA1c further supports the adverse influence of poor glycemic control on skeletal remodeling.

A central biological signal in our cohort was the elevation of circulating AGE biomarkers, carboxymethyllysine (CML) and pentosidine, across all diabetic groups, with the most pronounced increases observed in T1DM. AGE accumulation may impair collagen cross-linking, reduce bone toughness, and compromise matrix integrity, thereby increasing fracture susceptibility independently of BMD [27, 28]. Clinical data further support the relevance of cumulative AGE burden to skeletal deficits in T2DM. In a cross-sectional case-control study, skin autofluorescence (SAF)—a validated non-invasive surrogate of tissue AGE accumulation—was higher in T2DM than in age- and sex-matched controls and was inversely associated with femoral neck and lumbar spine BMD/T-scores; SAF was also higher in individuals with densitometric osteoporosis, suggesting that greater long-term AGE exposure may be linked to lower BMD and osteoporosis in diabetes [29].

Sclerostin was also elevated in all diabetic groups, which is consistent with altered osteocyte-related signaling in diabetes. The positive correlations between CML, pentosidine, and sclerostin—particularly in T1DM and LADA—suggest that AGE accumulation and sclerostin may coexist within a broader diabetes-related metabolic milieu. However, these cross-sectional associations do not establish a direct mechanistic pathway, and the lack of a consistent independent association for sclerostin in adjusted models weakens support for a direct AGE–sclerostin–fracture link in the present dataset. In this context, elevated sclerostin levels may reflect overall disease-related metabolic disturbance rather than an independently confirmed mediator of skeletal fragility [30–32].

Importantly, several biomarker differences remained significant after adjustment for age, sex, and BMI. Specifically, the between-group differences in β-CTX, pentosidine, and sclerostin (and the lower osteocalcin level in T2DM vs. controls) persisted after covariate adjustment, indicating that these biomarker patterns were not explained solely by demographic or body composition differences. In contrast, the adjusted CML difference remained significant primarily for T1DM versus controls, suggesting heterogeneity in the AGE signal across diabetes phenotypes after accounting for key covariates. Nevertheless, residual confounding cannot be excluded—particularly given the age imbalance between the control and T2DM groups—and the adjusted between-group comparisons should therefore be interpreted with appropriate caution.

When associations with vertebral fracture presence were examined within each diabetes phenotype, no variable showed an independent association with fracture presence in the T1DM group. In T2DM, fracture-stratified analyses indicated that participants with vertebral fractures were older and had higher CML levels, whereas β-CTX and sclerostin did not differ significantly by fracture status. In multivariable analysis restricted to the T2DM group, age and pentosidine were independently associated with vertebral fracture presence, whereas CML did not retain significance after adjustment. These findings suggest that different AGE-related biomarkers may capture distinct facets of diabetes-related skeletal fragility, and that apparent unadjusted associations may be attenuated after accounting for clinical covariates. Similarly, no bone biomarker showed an independent association with vertebral fracture presence in T1DM or LADA, which may reflect the multifactorial nature of fracture susceptibility in autoimmune diabetes. Sclerostin was not independently associated with vertebral fracture presence in regression analyses, which may indicate that osteocyte-related pathways are more relevant to longer-term bone quality alterations than to cross-sectional fracture discrimination. Given the cross-sectional design, these findings should be interpreted as associative and do not establish temporal or mechanistic causality.

An additional point to consider is that the observed associations between AGEs, sclerostin, bone parameters, and vertebral fractures may partly reflect overall diabetes severity rather than a direct biological pathway. In particular, longer disease duration, poorer glycemic control, impaired renal function, and exposure to antidiabetic medications may influence AGE accumulation, bone turnover, and fracture susceptibility simultaneously. Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted within the broader context of diabetes-related metabolic burden rather than as evidence of a single confirmed mechanistic axis.

Collectively, these findings support the view that diabetes-related bone disease is a complex metabolic bone disorder associated with suppressed bone turnover, AGE accumulation, and altered osteocyte-related signaling. Standard BMD-based fracture risk assessment tools may underestimate fracture risk in diabetic patients, particularly in T2DM and LADA. Biomarkers reflecting bone quality and matrix alterations—especially AGE-related markers—may have potential relevance for risk stratification; however, given the cross-sectional design and potential residual confounding, these findings should be interpreted as associative and require confirmation in larger longitudinal studies.

Strengths and limitations

A major strength of this study is the integrated evaluation of multiple diabetes phenotypes (T1DM, T2DM, and LADA) together with BMD, vertebral fractures, bone turnover markers, AGEs, and sclerostin, allowing assessment of diabetes-related skeletal fragility from both structural and biochemical perspectives. Several limitations should be considered. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and residual confounding remains possible despite adjustment for age, sex, and BMI, given baseline differences between groups, including differences in age, BMI, and cardiometabolic comorbidity burden, in addition to the potential influence of unmeasured factors (e.g., comorbidities, medication exposure, physical activity, muscle function, and fall risk). In particular, diabetes duration, glycemic control, renal function, and antidiabetic medication exposure may have substantially influenced AGE accumulation, bone metabolism, and vertebral fracture risk. Accordingly, circulating biomarker levels, bone density measurements, and vertebral fracture prevalence may have been influenced not only by diabetes phenotype itself but also by broader differences in metabolic risk profiles and overall diabetes severity. In addition, categorical BMD classifications were not fully comparable across phenotypes because younger participants were classified using Z-score-based criteria, whereas older and/or postmenopausal participants were classified using WHO T-score thresholds, limiting direct comparison of categorical prevalence estimates. Vertebral fractures were assessed using plain radiographs, and more advanced imaging, bone biopsy, or HR-pQCT were not available for detailed microarchitectural characterization. Although TZD use did not differ by vertebral fracture status in T2DM, the limited number of exposed participants precluded stable inclusion in multivariable models. Finally, the modest sample size—particularly for subgroup and multivariable analyses—may have limited power to detect smaller effects and may also have reduced our ability to identify consistent independent associations, particularly for sclerostin. Larger longitudinal studies with more detailed skeletal phenotyping are needed to confirm and extend these findings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, diabetic patients—particularly those with T1DM—showed elevated circulating AGEs and sclerostin levels alongside suppressed bone turnover markers, findings consistent with altered bone metabolism across diabetes phenotypes. In T2DM, age and pentosidine were independently associated with prevalent vertebral fractures, highlighting the potential clinical relevance of AGE-related skeletal alterations beyond areal BMD. While these associations do not establish a direct mechanistic pathway and should be interpreted cautiously in light of the cross-sectional design and potential residual confounding, they are consistent with a potential link between diabetes-related biochemical alterations and skeletal fragility.
