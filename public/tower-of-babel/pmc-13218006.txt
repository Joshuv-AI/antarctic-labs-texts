Evaluation of the validity and reliability of the Meloq EasyBase force platform for assessing jump performance and balance parameters
Authors: Sena Gizem Arslan, Birgul Dingirdan Gultekinler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundForce platforms are widely used to assess postural stability and vertical jump performance, yet their cost and limited portability restrict routine clinical and field-based use. The Meloq EasyBase force platform offers a practical alternative, but its validity and reliability require investigation.ObjectiveThe aim of this study was to investigate the reliability and to provide evidence of concurrent validity of the Meloq EasyBase force platform for the evaluation of balance-related parameters and jumping in healthy young adults.MethodsSeventy-eight healthy young adults (mean age: 21.85 ± 1.68 years) participated in this cross-sectional study. Static balance was assessed during single-leg stance on dominant and non-dominant limbs, with center of pressure (CoP) displacement, velocity, directional sway, and ellipse area recorded. CMJ height was also measured. Assessments were repeated after 7–10 days. Reliability was evaluated using intrarater intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC), standard error of measurement (SEM), and minimum detectable change (MDC). Concurrent validity against a clinical balance test was examined using correlations with the Flamingo Balance Test (FBT).ResultsBalance parameters demonstrated moderate to high reliability (ICC = 0.58–0.76), with velocity-based and directional CoP measures showing higher consistency. CMJ height showed excellent reliability (ICC = 0.96; SEM = 1.53 cm; MDC95% = 4.24 cm). Significant low-to-moderate correlations were observed between FBT scores and most force platform balance parameters (r = 0.23–0.32, p < 0.05).ConclusionThe Meloq EasyBase force platform demonstrates high reliability for vertical jump performance and moderate reliability for balance parameters. The observed low-to-moderate correlations with the Flamingo Balance Test provide preliminary evidence of concurrent validity.

Introduction

Postural stability refers to an individual’s capacity to maintain the body’s center of mass within the base of support [1]. Effective postural stability depends on the precise integration of sensory information derived from the vestibular, visual, and proprioceptive systems, together with the generation of appropriate motor responses to shifts in the center of pressure (CoP). These sensory–motor components dynamically interact and self-organize to produce suitable postural adjustments in response to varying environmental and task-related demands [2].

The CoP reflects the neuromuscular strategies required to maintain balance and body orientation and is therefore widely used to investigate postural control mechanisms [3]. CoP-based measures provide objective information regarding postural adjustments during tasks that challenge stability and postural alignment [1, 4, 5]. In the evaluation of postural stability, two principal approaches are commonly adopted: static and dynamic assessments. Static postural stability refers to the ability to maintain a specified posture without movement and is dependent on a stable base of support whereas dynamic postural stability encompasses balance control during movement-based tasks in which the center of mass is continuously adjusted in response to external perturbations [6]. A variety of assessment approaches are employed to examine static postural stability. Among these, the single-leg stance test is widely used to evaluate an individual’s ability to maintain balance on a single limb and allows for the measurement of multiple balance-related parameters, particularly those derived from CoP metrics, throughout the testing period [7].

Previous studies have shown that individuals with health-related conditions exhibit more regular postural sway patterns, with greater differences observed in populations with sensorimotor impairments [1]. These findings indicate that regularity analysis of postural fluctuations is sensitive to changes in an individual’s health status. Consequently, more advanced analytical approaches have been increasingly applied in the assessment of postural oscillations [8].

Postural stability is most commonly assessed using data obtained from force platforms, which are often considered reference or “gold standard” tools in balance assessment. These systems allow evaluation of postural stability through CoP displacement measurements recorded during quiet standing tasks, including both bipedal and unipedal conditions [8, 9]. However, conventional force platforms are generally large, costly, and require complex installation procedures and specialized software. Among field-based balance assessments, the FBT (Flamingo Balance Test) from the Eurofit Physical Fitness Test Battery is one of the most widely used methods [9]. Although laboratory-grade force platforms are considered the gold standard for the assessment of postural stability, their use is often limited in field-based and clinical settings due to cost and accessibility constraints. Therefore, clinical balance tests are frequently used as practical alternatives. In this context, the Flamingo Balance Test (FBT) was selected as a reference measure due to its widespread use, simplicity, and feasibility in applied settings. Accordingly, the present study aimed to examine the concurrent validity of the Meloq EasyBase force platform against a clinical balance test rather than against a laboratory-based gold-standard device. This approach allows for the evaluation of the relationship between objective force platform–derived measures and commonly used clinical assessments of balance.

In addition to balance-related assessments, vertical jump performance, particularly countermovement jump (CMJ) height, is widely regarded as an important indicator of lower-extremity neuromuscular function and athletic performance [2]. Recent studies have further demonstrated that motor performance components such as technical execution and coordination are closely associated with functional performance outcomes in athletic populations, highlighting the importance of objective performance assessment tools [10]. Force platforms are commonly considered a reference measurement method for the assessment of vertical jump height due to their high temporal resolution and their ability to provide detailed characterization of force–time variables specific to jumping tasks [11]. Moreover, significant relationships have been reported between dynamic balance, jumping ability, and sprint performance, emphasizing the interdependence of neuromuscular and postural control mechanisms [2]. The widespread use of force plate–derived jump measurements in both clinical and performance-oriented applications underscores the need to examine the reliability of vertical jump measurements obtained from alternative force platform systems [12]. Accordingly, in addition to balance parameters, the present study aimed to evaluate the reliability of countermovement jump height measured using the Meloq EasyBase force platform.

The Meloq EasyBase force platform represents a novel and cost-effective alternative that enables the assessment of balance performance, jump-related variables, and isometric force parameters. Therefore, the primary aim of this study is to investigate the validity and reliability of the Meloq EasyBase force platform for the evaluation of balance-related parameters and jumping. Establishing the measurement properties of this device is essential to support its use in both clinical and research settings. This study hypothesized that the Meloq EasyBase force platform is a valid and reliable tool for assessing jump and balance parameters.

Methods

Participants

This prospective test–retest reliability study was conducted at Sakarya University of Applied Sciences Application and Research Center. A total of 78 healthy young adults, consisting of 20 males and 58 females, aged 18–25 years, with a mean age of 21.859 ± 1.680 years, were included in the study. The demographic characteristics of the participants are presented in Table 1. Healthy individuals were included in the study. Participants were recruited through advertisements disseminated via social media platforms, where detailed information about the study was provided. Individuals who expressed interest were screened for eligibility based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria prior to enrollment, and all participants provided informed consent before participation. This manuscript was prepared based on the STROBE Checklist guidelines. The sample size was calculated using the Walter et al. approach for a two-measurement ICC design, based on the ability to distinguish between an acceptable lower limit (ρ₀ = 0.70) and a target ICC representing good reliability (ρ₁ = 0.85) [13]. The calculation was performed in R using the powerICC package, with a significance level of α = 0.05, statistical power of 0.80, and k = 2 measurements. According to this analysis, the minimum required sample size was determined to be 53 participants. To account for potential data loss and incomplete measurements, the study was planned to be conducted with 60 participants. However, considering that increasing the sample size in reliability studies enhances measurement precision and generalizability, 85 participants were invited to participate in the study. Following the exclusion of six participants who did not attend the retest assessments and one participant who was excluded due to a knee injury, the study was completed with a total of 78 participants. The flow diagram is presented in Fig. 1. Participants aged 18–25 years who had no neurological or vestibular conditions that could affect balance were included in the study. Participants with a history of lower extremity injury or surgery within the previous six months were excluded. The participants’ dominant extremity was determined based on their preferred kicking foot.

Ethical approval and informed consent

The study protocol received ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of Sakarya University of Applied Sciences (Approval date: 17.10.2025, Approval number: 62/24). The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants were provided with detailed information regarding the study’s objectives, requirements, potential benefits, and risks, and written informed consent was obtained.

Equipment and procedures

All testing sessions were conducted at the same time of day (between 13:00 and 16:00). Participants performed a standardized warm-up consisting of 5 min of low-intensity aerobic activity, followed by dynamic stretching exercises targeting major lower extremity muscle groups (e.g., hamstrings, quadriceps, and gastrocnemius), and several submaximal familiarization trials specific to the testing procedures. Participants were instructed to refrain from vigorous physical activity and caffeine consumption for a specified period prior to testing. Participant positioning on the platform was carefully standardized across all trials. In addition, the order of tests was clearly defined and implemented using a (randomized/fixed) sequence to ensure methodological consistency. Data were collected during all tasks using a force platform (Meloq EasyBase, Sweden). The Meloq EasyBase force platform (Meloq EasyBase, Sweden), consisting of two independent force plates (45 × 32.5 cm), was used for data acquisition. Each plate has a measurement range of 0–12,000 N with an accuracy of ± 1%. Ground reaction force data were collected at a sampling frequency of up to 1600 Hz. The system communicates via Bluetooth 5.0 and provides more than 8 h of continuous operation. To determine inter-session reliability, participants completed two separate testing sessions spaced 7–10 days apart. All assessments were performed by the same evaluator, and the study was designed to examine intra-rater reliability. During the first testing session, assessments of static balance, CMJ, and the FBT were conducted using the Meloq EasyBase force platform. These specifications allow for high-resolution assessment of balance and jump performance. In the second testing session, static balance and vertical jump assessments were repeated using the same force platform (Fig. 2).

Static postural stability was assessed under the eyes-open condition in accordance with the protocol described by Goldie et al. [14]. This protocol has previously been demonstrated to be both valid and reliable [14]. During the static balance task, participants assumed a single-leg stance on both the dominant and nondominant lower extremities, with their hands placed on their hips. The non-stance limb was flexed at the hip and knee and elevated to the level of the contralateral ankle. If the non-stance foot contacted the force platform, participants were instructed to immediately return the limb to the test position. Trials were discarded and repeated if the non-stance limb contacted the stance limb or the ground surrounding the force platform [15]. All tests were performed in a single-leg stance position for 30 s on both the dominant and non-dominant lower extremities. Participants were allowed to rest while standing on both legs between trials, and two familiarization trials were provided for each testing condition [15]. The static postural stability assessment focused on several key parameters, including Total CoP Displacement [mm], Displacement Velocity [mm / s], CoP Ellipse Area [mm²], Anterior posterior (AP) CoP Displacement [mm], Mediolateral [16] CoP Displacement [mm], Mean Velocity [mm / s].

During the CMJ test, participants were instructed to keep their hands fixed on their hips in order to minimize the influence of the upper extremities on jump performance. The test began from an upright standing position with the knees fully extended; participants then performed a squat movement to approximately 90° of knee flexion, followed by an explosive vertical propulsion using both lower limbs [17]. Each participant completed three trials, with a 1-minute rest period between trials. The mean value of the three trials was used for statistical analysis [7].

Flamingo balance test

All participants were provided with a practical demonstration of the tests, and to minimize potential measurement errors, they were allowed one familiarization trial. Assessments were conducted barefoot and with eyes open for both the dominant and non-dominant lower extremities. For the administration of the FBT participants were instructed to stand barefoot on the tested limb on a wooden beam measuring 50 cm in length, 5 cm in height, and 3 cm in width. During the test, the free limb was flexed at the knee, with the heel drawn toward the buttocks, and participants were instructed to place both hands on the iliac crests, adopting a flamingo-like posture and to maintain balance for as long as possible [18]. The measurement was terminated if the participant lost balance by stepping off the beam, releasing the free foot, or removing the hands from the body. Each participant performed two trials for each limb. For the analysis, the number of balance losses (i.e., the number of attempts required to maintain balance) was used. The primary outcome variable for the FBT was defined as the number of balance loss events.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Descriptive statistics, including means and standard deviations, were calculated for all continuous variables. The assumption of normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov and all analyzed variables were found to be normally distributed. To determine the intra-rater test–retest reliability of the Meloq EasyBase force platform, intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) with 95% confidence intervals were calculated. Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) were calculated using a two-way mixed-effects model with absolute agreement (ICC [1, 3]) to assess intra-rater test–retest reliability. ICC values range from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating greater measurement reliability. Accordingly, ICC values of 0.90–0.99 were interpreted as very high reliability, 0.70–0.89 as high reliability, 0.50–0.69 as moderate reliability, and 0.26–0.49 as low reliability. ICC values below 0.25 were considered to indicate poor or no reliability [19]. The absolute reliability of the Meloq EasyBase force plates was evaluated by calculating the standard error of measurement (SEM) and the minimum detectable change at the 95% confidence interval (MDC95%). SEM was calculated as SEM = SD * √ (1 - ICC). Here, SD (standard deviation) is the standard deviation of for all scores MDC95% was calculated as MDC95% =SEM * 1.96 * √2. SEM% was calculated as (SEM / mean) x 100. MDC% was calculated as (MDC / mean) x 100. The statistical significance level was set at p < 0.05. Agreement between repeated measurements was assessed using Bland–Altman analysis. The mean difference (bias) and the 95% limits of agreement (mean difference ± 1.96 × SD) were calculated and visually inspected using Bland–Altman plots.

The concurrent validity of the Meloq EasyBase force platform was evaluated by examining the correlations between the measurements obtained from the force platform and the outcomes of the FBT. The Pearson correlation coefficient (r) parametric test was used to assess the possible relationships between CoP displacement–based static balance outcomes obtained from the Meloq EasyBase force platform and the FBT results. The r values were classified as weak (< 0.499), moderate (0.5–0.707), or strong (> 0.707) [19]. According to Cohen’s classification of effect sizes, values of d < 0.20 indicate a trivial (negligible) effect, 0.20 ≤ d < 0.50 a small effect, 0.50 ≤ d < 0.80 a moderate effect, and d ≥ 0.80 a large effect [20].

In the validity analysis, statistically significant correlations were identified between the parameters obtained from the force platform and the FBT for all measurements except two Table 7. However, no significant associations were observed between the Flamingo Balance Test and the CoP area measurements of the force platform for either the dominant extremity (p = 0.082) or the non-dominant extremity (p = 0.150). Agreement between test and retest measurements was evaluated using Bland–Altman analysis across all parameters (Figs. 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 and 8). For CoP displacement (dominant and non-dominant), a small positive bias was observed, with most values falling within the 95% limits of agreement; however, the relatively wide limits and increased dispersion at higher values indicated considerable variability and heteroscedasticity. In contrast, CoP displacement velocity demonstrated minimal bias and relatively narrow limits of agreement for both extremities, with a consistent distribution of differences across the measurement range, indicating good agreement and minimal heteroscedasticity. CoP ellipse area showed a small positive bias but markedly wide limits of agreement, with several outliers and pronounced heteroscedasticity, reflecting substantial variability between measurements. Anteroposterior and mediolateral CoP displacement parameters exhibited small positive bias and moderate limits of agreement, with most values within acceptable ranges and mild heteroscedasticity. Finally, CMJ height demonstrated negligible bias and narrow limits of agreement, with most values tightly clustered around zero, indicating high agreement and low measurement variability.

Discussion

This study investigated the validity and reliability of the Meloq EasyBase force platform for the assessment of jump and balance parameters. In the intra-rater reliability analysis of balance measures, five parameters demonstrated high reliability, while the remaining five showed moderate reliability. In the validity analysis, statistically significant associations were identified between the balance parameters obtained from the force platform and the FBT for all measurements except two. Additionally, vertical jump assessments exhibited very high reliability. Collectively, these findings suggest that the Meloq EasyBase force platform demonstrates acceptable reliability and provides preliminary evidence of validity for evaluating jump and balance parameters in healthy young adults; however, these results should be interpreted with caution, as no direct comparison with a laboratory gold-standard device was performed.

For postural stability, ICC values for CoP displacement, velocity, directional sway, and ellipse ranged from 0.58 to 0.76, indicating moderate to good reliability. These results support the consistency and stability of the platform for repeated balance assessments under standardized testing conditions. When comparing individual parameters, velocity-based CoP measures and directional sway components in the AP and ML directions demonstrated relatively higher reliability than global CoP displacement and elliptical area outcomes. This finding suggests that, particularly in young populations, velocity-sensitive metrics may more accurately capture subtle adjustments in postural control strategies and are less susceptible to trial-to-trial variability. In contrast, CoP ellipse area measures exhibited lower ICC values accompanied by substantially higher SEM% and MDC95% values, indicating a reduced capacity to detect change. Accordingly, ellipse area measures should be interpreted with greater caution when monitoring small or short-term balance adaptations in this population.

The SEM values, reflecting absolute measurement error, were approximately 12–14% for most displacement and velocity parameters, indicating an acceptable level of measurement precision. However, the MDC95% values, ranging from approximately 34% to 38%, suggest that only relatively large changes can be confidently interpreted as true performance adaptations beyond measurement error. Therefore, while the Meloq EasyBase force platform can be considered a reliable tool for monitoring of postural stability, caution is warranted when interpreting small pre–post changes at the individual level. In contrast to postural stability parameters, CMJ height demonstrated excellent reliability (ICC = 0.96). The low SEM (1.53 cm) and MDC95 (4.24 cm) values indicate high measurement precision and support the suitability of the Meloq EasyBase platform for the reliable assessment of CMJ performance in healthy young adults. Overall, these findings indicate that while the platform is appropriate for both balance and jump assessments, jump performance outcomes exhibit superior reliability and sensitivity to change compared with postural stability parameters.

A study reported in the literature demonstrated that the intra-rater reliability coefficients of the Quadrotics Poise Q10 force platform for postural sway parameters ranged between ICC = 0.60 and 0.87, indicating a high level of measurement consistency [21]. The ICC findings obtained in the present study are consistent with the results of previous studies that employed similar methods for the assessment of postural stability [22, 23]. From both clinical and research perspectives, the present data support the use of force platforms as reliable measurement tools for monitoring and evaluating postural stability in healthy young adults. In the current study, the observation of moderate-to-high intra-rater ICC values further demonstrates that the employed force platform provides consistent measurements in the assessment of postural stability. Agreement between test and retest measurements was evaluated using Bland–Altman analysis across all parameters. For CoP displacement (dominant and non-dominant), a small positive bias was observed, with most values falling within the 95% limits of agreement; however, the relatively wide limits and increased dispersion at higher values indicated considerable variability and heteroscedasticity. In contrast, CoP displacement velocity demonstrated minimal bias and relatively narrow limits of agreement for both extremities, with a consistent distribution of differences across the measurement range, indicating good agreement and minimal heteroscedasticity. CoP area showed a small positive bias but markedly wide limits of agreement, with several outliers and pronounced heteroscedasticity, reflecting substantial variability between measurements. Anteroposterior and mediolateral CoP displacement parameters exhibited small positive bias and moderate limits of agreement, with most values within acceptable ranges and mild heteroscedasticity. Finally, CMJ height demonstrated negligible bias and narrow limits of agreement, with most values tightly clustered around zero, indicating high agreement and low measurement variability.

The excellent reliability values obtained for countermovement jump height in the present study (ICC = 0.96, SEM = 1.53 cm, MDC95 = 4.24 cm) demonstrate strong agreement with previous research examining the force–time characteristics of vertical jump performance in healthy and athletic populations [12]. The study reported high to excellent reliability for jump height outcomes in both collegiate athletes and recreationally active individuals, despite differences in training status and neuromuscular proficiency, with ICC values generally exceeding 0.90 [12].

Consistent with these findings, the results of the present study indicate that vertical jump height can be assessed with high precision and repeatability using a force platform–based system even in healthy young adults who are not elite athletes. Moreover, the low SEM and MDC95 values observed in the current study suggest that jump height is not only resistant to measurement error but also represents a sensitive outcome capable of detecting meaningful changes in both clinical and performance-oriented research settings.

In another study, high to excellent reliability levels were reported for force plate–derived variables, including jump-related kinematic and kinetic measures, with ICC values largely exceeding 0.90 [24]. Although that study primarily focused on the reliability of variables such as countermovement depth and velocity, the consistently high reliability profile reported for force plate–based measurements supports the methodological robustness of these systems in the assessment of vertical jump performance. In line with this evidence, the findings of the present study demonstrate that jump height can be evaluated as a reliable and highly reproducible performance outcome using a force platform–based measurement system, even in healthy young adults who are not elite athletes. Overall, these results suggest that the Meloq EasyBase force platform provides acceptable measurement performance for field-based applications; however, these findings should be interpreted with caution, as no direct comparison with a laboratory gold-standard force platform was performed.

It should be acknowledged that the Flamingo Balance Test (FBT), used as the reference measure in this study, represents a clinical field-based assessment rather than a gold-standard laboratory measure of postural stability. As such, the strength of the concurrent validity findings may be inherently influenced by the methodological differences between the two assessment approaches. Concurrent validity analyses demonstrated low but statistically significant correlations between FBT performance and force plate–derived postural stability parameters obtained using the Meloq EasyBase system in healthy young adults (r = 0.23–0.32, p < 0.05). Therefore, these findings should be interpreted as preliminary evidence rather than strong support for the device’s validity. These findings indicate that while both assessment tools are related to balance performance, they reflect different components of postural control. The FBT provides a global, outcome-based clinical field assessment of static balance, whereas force plate–based measurements allow high-resolution and continuous quantification of postural control dynamics through variables such as CoP displacement, velocity, and directional sway [25–27]. Accordingly, the relatively modest magnitude of the observed correlation coefficients is an expected finding and is most likely attributable to fundamental methodological differences between the two approaches, including measurement sensitivity, scoring structure, and the specific postural control constructs being assessed.

Nevertheless, the presence of significant associations across both dominant and non-dominant limbs suggests that force plate–derived measures exhibit construct-related consistency with an established clinical balance test, thereby supporting the concurrent validity of the Meloq EasyBase platform. At the same time, the relatively low correlation coefficients indicate that force plate–based metrics may capture subtle postural control strategies that are not readily detected by simpler clinical tests, particularly in healthy young adult populations where ceiling effects are commonly observed.

From an applied perspective, while the FBT represents a practical and accessible tool for screening gross balance impairments, force plate–based assessments obtained using the Meloq EasyBase system provide complementary and more objective information regarding postural stability. In this context, the EasyBase force platform emerges as a particularly valuable assessment tool for research and performance monitoring settings in which the detection of subtle balance alterations beyond the resolution of clinical field tests is required.

One of the main strengths of the present study is that, to the best of our knowledge, it is among the limited number of studies examining the validity and reliability of the Meloq EasyBase force platform for the assessment of jump and balance performance. Furthermore, the EasyBase system represents a cost-effective and accessible alternative, which may facilitate its use in applied research and field-based performance monitoring settings.

Limitations

The use of the FBT as a clinical reference measure may be considered one of the main limitations of the present study. Although the test is widely employed as a practical and easy-to-administer field-based assessment of static balance, its clinical and outcome-based scoring structure may limit its ability to adequately reflect the fine components of postural control and subtle balance adaptations. This limitation is particularly relevant in healthy young adult populations, where ceiling effects are frequently observed, potentially attenuating the strength of associations with high-resolution force plate–derived measures. In addition, the inclusion of only healthy young adults may have influenced the observed outcomes, as this population typically exhibits higher baseline balance performance and lower variability, which may reduce the sensitivity of certain measures. Therefore, the generalizability of the present findings may be limited to healthy young adult populations, and caution should be exercised when extrapolating these results to other populations, such as older adults, clinical populations, or elite athletes. Furthermore, the reliance on a single field-based assessment tool may not fully capture the multidimensional nature of postural control. Future studies are needed to examine balance performance in populations with greater variability, such as older adults or clinical groups, and to evaluate vertical jump performance in athletic populations. Such investigations may help to further clarify the present findings, particularly in light of the relatively low correlations observed for the balance assessments. Another limitation of the present study is the relatively high proportion of female participants (74%), which may limit the generalizability of the findings across sexes. Given that sex-related differences in neuromuscular control, postural stability, and jump performance have been reported in the literature, the observed results may not fully represent male populations. In addition, the technical characteristics of the measurement device were not fully detailed, which may affect reproducibility. Finally, the use of the Flamingo Balance Test as the sole clinical reference measure represents a limitation, as it may not comprehensively reflect all aspects of balance performance.

Clinical applications

The Meloq EasyBase force platform may be considered a particularly useful tool for conducting baseline assessments and follow-up evaluations during rehabilitation and return-to-activity phases. Owing to its high reliability for vertical jump performance and acceptable reliability for postural stability measures, clinicians may confidently use the system to objectively monitor longitudinal performance changes and to distinguish true performance adaptations beyond measurement error.

Conclusion

The findings of the present study provide preliminary evidence regarding the validity and reliability of the Meloq EasyBase force platform for the assessment of jump performance and postural stability in healthy young adults. While vertical jump performance demonstrated high reliability, balance parameters showed moderate reliability, and the relatively high MDC values suggest limited sensitivity to detect small changes. The results related to validity indicate only a limited level of association between force platform measurements and the Flamingo Balance Test and should therefore be interpreted with caution. These findings should be considered preliminary, particularly given that the FBT represents a clinical field-based assessment rather than a gold-standard laboratory measure of postural stability. The results are specific to a sample of healthy young adults and should not be generalized to clinical populations. Although the EasyBase system may offer a practical option for field-based assessments, its applicability for detailed balance evaluation and clinical decision-making remains uncertain. Further research incorporating comparisons with laboratory-grade force platforms and more diverse populations is needed to better establish the validity and broader applicability of the device.
