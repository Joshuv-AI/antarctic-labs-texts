Young domination on Hamming rectangles
Janko Gravner, Matjaž Krnc, Martin Milanič, Jean-Florent Raymond

Abstract
We introduce a family of domination-type problems in Cartesian products of two graphs. The framework captures several well-studied topics, including variants of bootstrap percolation, line growth, distance domination, and target set selection. We focus on Cartesian products of two complete graphs and formulate the notion of Young domination number in terms of a growth rule determined by a Young diagram; this number is the smallest cardinality of an initial set that covers the entire vertex set in a prescribed number L L of iterations of the rule. We compute the Young domination number with L = 1 L=1 for several natural cases, including k k -domination for Cartesian products of two complete graphs of the same order, thereby proving a conjecture from 2009 due to Burchett, Lane, and Lachniet. We show that the case of L = 1 L=1 of Young domination is equivalent to computing bipartite Turán numbers for families of double stars, yielding implications of our results in extremal graph theory. For arbitrary fixed L L , we devise constant-factor approximation algorithms for the problem. Our approach is based on a variety of techniques, including duality between Young diagrams, algebraic formulations, explicit constructions, and dynamic programming.

Introduction

1.1
Motivation

Looking for the smallest set of vertices in a graph with a given property is a natural and fundamental problem in extremal combinatorics and algorithmic graph theory.
Variants of domination in graphs, in particular, have proved useful in many real-life contexts.
We introduce
Young domination
, a family of domination-type problems in Cartesian products of graphs.
This framework provides a common generalization of various well-studied problems, including
bootstrap percolation
[
GHPS
]
,
line growth
[
GSS
]
(also known as line percolation
[
BBLN
]
),
distance domination (see, e.g.,
[
Hen
]
),

k
-domination on rook’s graphs (see
[
BLL
]
, as well as
[
HH
, Section 8]
),
and variants of target set selection with constant thresholds (see, e.g.,
[
CHLWY
,
LYW
,
JW
]
).
Our problems are formulated in terms of a growth rule determined by a Young diagram, which ensures monotonicity; without this property,
related
questions become much less tractable (see, e.g.,
[
GG2
]
).
While we begin with a more general setup, we focus on growth dynamics on Cartesian products of two complete graphs, arguably the simplest nontrivial class.

1.2
Young domination

We now proceed with formal definitions, starting with the growth rule parametrized by a Young diagram.
The corresponding Young domination number will then be the smallest cardinality of an initial set that covers the entire vertex set in a prescribed number of iterations of the rule.

Consider two finite graphs
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
.
The
Cartesian product
of
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
is the graph
G_{1}\Box G_{2}
with vertex set
V(G_{1})\times V(G_{2})
in which two vertices
(u_{1},u_{2})
and
(v_{1},v_{2})
are adjacent if and only if
u_{1}=v_{1}
and
u_{2}v_{2}\in E(G_{2})
or
u_{2}=v_{2}
and
u_{1}v_{1}\in E(G_{1})
.
Each vertex
v=(v_{1},v_{2})\in V(G_{1}\Box G_{2})
has neighbors divided into two disjoint sets:
{\mathcal{N}}^{r}(v)
, the
row neighborhood
of
v
, defined as the set of all vertices
(v_{1},y)
such that
yv_{2}
is an edge of
G_{2}
, and
{\mathcal{N}}^{c}(v)
, the
column neighborhood
of
v
, defined as the set of all vertices
(x,v_{2})
such that
xv_{1}
is an edge of
G_{1}
.

For an integer
c\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}
, we denote by
[0,c]
the set
\{0,1,\ldots,c\}
.
For integers
a,b\geq 1
, we let
R_{a,b}=[0,a-1]\times[0,b-1]\subseteq{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}^{2}
be the discrete
a\times b
rectangle.
A set
{\mathcal{Z}}=\bigcup_{(a,b)\in I}R_{a,b}
, given by a union
of rectangles over some set
I\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{2}
, is called a (discrete)
zero-set
.
We allow the trivial case
{\mathcal{Z}}=\emptyset
and also infinite
{\mathcal{Z}}
.
Observe that zero-sets are exactly

downward-closed
sets in the sense that if
(x,y)\in{\mathcal{Z}}
and
0\leq x^{\prime}\leq x
,
0\leq y^{\prime}\leq y
then
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in{\mathcal{Z}}
.
Therefore, zero-sets are
Young diagrams
in the French notation
[
Rom
]
.
Each zero-set determines a type of domination
on Cartesian products of two graphs as follows.

Given a zero-set
{\mathcal{Z}}
, the
transformation

\mathcal{T}=\mathcal{T}({\mathcal{Z}})
, defined on sets of vertices of
G_{1}\Box G_{2}
, assigns to every set
A\subseteq V(G_{1}\Box G_{2})
a set
\mathcal{T}(A)\subseteq V(G_{1}\Box G_{2})
as follows:

•

the input set is enlarged, i.e., if
v\in A
then
v\in\mathcal{T}(A)
;

•

if
v\in V(G_{1}\Box G_{2})\setminus A
, then
v\in\mathcal{T}(A)
if and only if
(|{\mathcal{N}}^{r}(v)\cap A|,|{\mathcal{N}}^{c}(v)\cap A|)\notin{\mathcal{Z}}
.

Thus, the iteration
\mathcal{T}^{t}(A)
yields a growth dynamics with
initial set
A=\mathcal{T}^{0}(A)
, and we define

\mathcal{T}^{\infty}(A)=\bigcup_{t\geq 0}\mathcal{T}^{t}(A)
. Observe that

\mathcal{T}
is
monotone
:
A_{1}\subseteq A_{2}
implies

\mathcal{T}(A_{1})\subseteq\mathcal{T}(A_{2})
; in fact,
\mathcal{T}
is monotone if and only if
its zero-set
{\mathcal{Z}}
is downward closed. We call the transformation
\mathcal{T}
,
or equivalently, the resulting growth dynamics the
neighborhood growth
.

For
L=0,1,2,\ldots,\infty
, we say that
A
is a
{\mathcal{Z}}
-dominating set with latency
L
if
\mathcal{T}^{L}(A)=V(G_{1}\Box G_{2})
. Our focus is on smallest such sets,
so we define the
{\mathcal{Z}}
-domination number
of
({\mathcal{Z}},G_{1},G_{2})

with latency
L
as

\gamma^{L}=\gamma^{L}({\mathcal{Z}},G_{1},G_{2})=\min\{|A|\colon\mathcal{T}^{L}(A)=V(G_{1}\Box G_{2})\}.

It is trivially true that
\gamma^{0}({\mathcal{Z}},G_{1},G_{2})=|V(G_{1}\Box G_{2})|
.
When the latency is not specified, we assume the default choice
L=1
,
often omit it from the notation and call
\gamma=\gamma({\mathcal{Z}},G_{1},G_{2})=\gamma^{1}({\mathcal{Z}},G_{1},G_{2})
the
{\mathcal{Z}}
-domination number
of
({\mathcal{Z}},G_{1},G_{2})
.
In particular, if
a\in\mathbb{N}
and
{\mathcal{Z}}
is the triangle

(1.1)

T_{a}=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{2}\colon x+y\leq a-1\},

then
\gamma(T_{a},G_{1},G_{2})
is exactly the
a
-domination number of the product graph
G_{1}\Box G_{2}

[
HV
]
.
Furthermore,
\gamma^{L}(T_{1},G_{1},G_{2})
is exactly the distance-
L
domination number of the product graph
G_{1}\Box G_{2}
, for any finite
L
(see, e.g.,
[
Hen
]
).
In our general discussions, or if
{\mathcal{Z}}
and
L
are clear from the context, we refer to
{\mathcal{Z}}
-domination with latency
L
as
Young domination
.

Perhaps the most natural special case of Young domination is where the two factors are complete graphs,
G_{1}=K_{m}
and
G_{2}=K_{n}
on vertex sets
[0,m-1]
and
[0,n-1]
, in which case we write

\gamma^{L}=\gamma^{L}({\mathcal{Z}},m,n)=\gamma^{L}({\mathcal{Z}},K_{m},K_{n})\,.

The graph
K_{m}\Box K_{n}
is known as a
Hamming rectangle
, and neighborhood growth on this graph with general
{\mathcal{Z}}
was studied in
[
GSS
,
GPS
]
.
As in these references, we represent this graph as having vertex set
R_{n,m}
; see the beginning of Section
2
for more details.
We focus solely on the neighborhood growth dynamics on Hamming rectangles for the remainder of the paper.
See Figure
2.1
for an example.

1.3
Main results

We begin with three special cases where Young domination number is given by simple expressions.
The easiest are the
L
-shaped Young diagrams, given by zero-sets

V_{a,b}
, where
a,b\geq 0
and

(1.2)

(V_{a,b})^{c}=\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{2}\setminus V_{a,b}=[a,\infty)\times[b,\infty),

i.e., a point requires at least
a
occupied row neighbors
and
at least
b
occupied column neighbors to become occupied.

Proposition 1.1
.

Assume that
{\mathcal{Z}}=V_{a,b}
, where
0\leq a\leq n
,
0\leq b\leq m
.
Then
\gamma({\mathcal{Z}},m,n)=\max(am,bn)
.

Next we consider rectangular zero-sets
R_{a,b}
, whereby a point
with at least
a
occupied row neighbors
or
at least
b
occupied columns neighbors becomes occupied. In this case,

\gamma
is given by the following optimization procedure.

Theorem 1.2
.

Assume that
{\mathcal{Z}}=R_{a,b}
, where
1\leq a\leq n
,
1\leq b\leq m
.
Then

(1.3)

\gamma({\mathcal{Z}},m,n)=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}b\leq x\leq m\\
a\leq y\leq n\end{subarray}}\left((m-x)(n-y)+\max(ax,by)\right),

which can be computed in time
\mathcal{O}(\min(a,b)/\gcd(a,b))
.
In particular, for
a=b
and
m=n
,

(1.4)

\gamma({\mathcal{Z}},n,n)=\begin{cases}an-a^{2}/4\,,&\text{if $a\leq 2n/3$ and $a$ is even,}\\
an-(a^{2}-1)/4\,,&\text{if $a\leq(2n+1)/3$ and $a$ is odd,}\\
a^{2}+(n-a)^{2},&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Our most substantial exact result gives a complete solution to Young domination for triangular Young diagrams
T_{a}
from
(
1.1
) with
m=n
.
This case was considered in
[
BLL
]
, where various partial results were given.
In particular, the formula (
1.5
) for even
a
confirms Conjecture 1 in that paper.
Note that for
a\geq 2n-1
, the only Young dominating set is
[0,n-1]^{2}
, so we exclude this case from consideration.

Theorem 1.3
.

Assume that
n\geq 2
and let
{\mathcal{Z}}=T_{a}
be given by (
1.1
), where
1\leq a\leq 2n-2
. If
a
is even, then

(1.5)

\gamma({\mathcal{Z}},n,n)=\frac{an}{2}\,.

If
a
is odd and
a\leq n
, then

(1.6)

\gamma({\mathcal{Z}},n,n)=\frac{a+1}{2}\,n-\frac{a-1}{2}\,.

Finally, if
a
is odd and
a>n
, then

(1.7)

\gamma({\mathcal{Z}},n,n)=\frac{a-1}{2}\,n+\left\lceil\frac{n(2n-a+1)}{2(2n-a)}\right\rceil.

For general zero-sets, the Young domination number for
L=1
is connected to certain bipartite Turán numbers; see Section
3
.
However, the complexity of computing the Young domination number is open for any
L\geq 1
; see open problems
1
and
2
in Section
9
for further discussion.
We provide polynomial-time approximation algorithms, the most precise of which is for latency
L=1
.

Theorem 1.4
.

There exists an algorithm polynomial in
n
that takes as input
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
with
m\leq n
and a zero-set
{\mathcal{Z}}
with
{\mathcal{Z}}\subseteq R_{n,m}
, and returns a number in the interval
[\gamma,3\gamma]
where
\gamma=\gamma({\mathcal{Z}},m,n)
.

For an arbitrary fixed finite latency number, we give a constant factor approximation algorithm. In its statement, we allow
{\mathcal{Z}}

to be included in a rectangle much smaller than the universe

R_{n,m}
.

Theorem 1.5
.

For any fixed finite
L
,
there exist a constant
C\geq 1
and an algorithm that takes as input
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
and a zero-set
{\mathcal{Z}}
with
{\mathcal{Z}}\subseteq R_{a,b}\subseteq R_{n,m}
, returns a number in the interval
[\gamma^{L},C\gamma^{L}]
, where
\gamma^{L}=\gamma^{L}({\mathcal{Z}},m,n)
, and runs in time that is polynomial in
ab
.

In fact, the algorithms given by Theorems
1.4
and
1.5
can be easily adapted so that they return a
{\mathcal{Z}}
-dominating set
A
with
|A|\leq 3\gamma
and a
{\mathcal{Z}}
-dominating set
A
with latency
L
with
|A|\leq C\gamma^{L}
, respectively.
These results place the optimization problems of computing the Young domination numbers
\gamma^{L}({\mathcal{Z}},m,n)
in the complexity class
APX
(see, e.g.,
[
ACGKMP
]
).

Observe that
\gamma^{L+1}({\mathcal{Z}},m,n)\leq\gamma^{L}({\mathcal{Z}},m,n)
for all
L\geq 0
.
In light of this and Theorem
1.5
, it is natural to ask how much can
\gamma^{L}
differ for different
L
.
The next result addresses this to some extent, showing in particular that at least the first four are not within a constant factor of each other.

Theorem 1.6
.

Assume that
{\mathcal{Z}}\subseteq R_{a,b}
for some
a,b\geq 1
.
If
2\leq L\leq\infty
and
m,n>ab
, then
\gamma^{L}({\mathcal{Z}},m,n)=\gamma^{L}({\mathcal{Z}},\infty,\infty)
. If,
in addition,
L\geq 2ab+5
, then

\gamma^{L}({\mathcal{Z}},m,n)=\gamma^{\infty}({\mathcal{Z}},\infty,\infty)\in[|{\mathcal{Z}}|/4,|{\mathcal{Z}}|].

Moreover,

(1.8)

\sup_{{\mathcal{Z}},m,n}\frac{\gamma^{L}({\mathcal{Z}},m,n)}{\gamma^{L+1}({\mathcal{Z}},m,n)}=\infty,

for
L=0,1,2
.

1.4
Related work

The dynamics with
{\mathcal{Z}}=T_{a}
given by (
1.1
) is also known as

bootstrap percolation

[
GHPS
]
, while a rectangular zero-set
{\mathcal{Z}}=R_{a,b}
induces
line growth

[
GSS
]
, also known as
line percolation

[
BBLN
]
.
In these and related papers on
growth processes on graphs, points in the set
\mathcal{T}^{t}(A)
are called

occupied
(coded as 1s) and points outside of it
empty

(coded as 0s); a set
A
which makes every point eventually
occupied is precisely a Young dominating set with
L=\infty

and is also called
a
spanning

[
GSS
]
or
percolating

[
BBLN
]
set.
For
m=n
,
K_{n}\Box K_{n}
is often referred to as the

rook’s graph
.
The
a
-domination number for this case was considered in
[
BLL
]
(see also
[
HH
, Section 8]
), but domination and its variants on
Hamming graphs
, that is, Cartesian products of complete graphs, remain relatively unexplored.

Young domination is one way to extend
a
-domination
on graphs
[
HV
]
. Another is vector domination (see, e.g.,
[
CMV
,
HPV
]
), where every vertex has its own requirement for the number of neighbors in the dominating set.
In a further generalization in the same fashion as in the present paper,
Cicalese et al.
[
CCGMV
]
considered vector domination with latency.
In social network theory, designing small initial sets that yield large
final sets due to vector domination with infinite latency is referred
to as
target set selection
(see
[
Chen
]
).

Domination with infinite latency, that is, spanning or percolation, with random initial sets has played a crucial role in spatial growth processes, starting with the foundational paper
[
AL
]
.
By far the most common setting, with many deep and surprising results (see, e.g., the survey
[
Mor
]
), is the Cartesian product of
d
path graphs on
n
vertices, and thus with standard nearest neighbor lattice connectivity.
Associated extremal quantities have also received attention, most of all the minimal size of a spanning set, that is,
\gamma^{\infty}
in our notation.
For this graph, the
a
-domination
number with infinite latency equals
n^{d-1}
for
a=d

[
PS
]
and
\lceil d(n-1)/2\rceil+1
for
a=2

[
BBM
]
. For
other values of
a
, a sharp asymptotic formula for the hypercube (
n=2
)
as
d\to\infty
was given in
[
MN
]
, and an exact formula for
d=a=3
in
[
DNR
]
.
Smallest spanning sets have also been studied for trees
[
DR
,
Rie
,
BKN
]
, and strong products
[
BH
]
and direct products
[
BHH
]
of graphs.
Among many papers that study graph bootstrap percolation,
a dynamics akin to ours
when viewed as an edge-addition rule in bipartite graphs (see Section
3
), we mention
[
HHQ
]
as it also discusses
a method suited for handling product graphs.
For Hamming
graphs,
[
BBLN
]
and
[
BMT
]
study
\gamma^{\infty}

for line growth and bootstrap percolation, while

[
GSS
]
gives bounds on
\gamma^{\infty}

for general zero-sets. In this context, sets with

Preliminaries

Let
m,n\in\mathbb{N}=\{1,2,3,\ldots\}
.
The vertex set of our graph
K_{m}\Box K_{n}
is
R_{n,m}=[0,n-1]\times[0,m-1]
.
Also, we shorten
R_{n}=R_{n,n}
. Thus, two vertices
(j,i)
and
(\ell,k)
are adjacent if and only if they differ in precisely one coordinate.
For each
i\in[0,m-1]
the
i
-th row
of
R_{n,m}
is the set of all vertices of
R_{n,m}
with second coordinate
i
; similarly, for each
j\in[0,n-1]
the
j
-th column
is the set of all vertices of
R_{n,m}
with first coordinate
j
.
Given a vertex
(j,i)\in R_{n,m}
, its row neighborhood
{\mathcal{N}}^{r}(j,i)
then consists of all vertices distinct from
(j,i)
with second coordinate
i
,
and its column neighborhood
{\mathcal{N}}^{c}(j,i)
of all vertices distinct from
(j,i)

with first coordinate
j
.
Note that, in this setting, the first factor
K_{m}
, whose vertices are represented by the interval
[0,m-1]
, is vertical and the second factor
K_{n}
, represented by the interval
[0,n-1]
, is horizontal; also, each row neighborhood has
n-1
vertices and each column neighborhood
m-1
vertices.
We will also encounter infinite
m
and
n
:
R_{n,\infty}=[0,n-1]\times[0,\infty)
, and analogously,
R_{\infty,m}
and
R_{\infty,\infty}
.
Recall that every zero-set
{\mathcal{Z}}
defines the corresponding transformation
\mathcal{T}=\mathcal{T}({\mathcal{Z}})
on subsets of

; note that we may always replace

.

(i.e., the minimal neighborhood counts that induce occupation).

of

.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be an operation that, given a subset

, returns the subset of

obtained from

by permuting rows

Then, for any transformation

given by some zero-set,

. This also holds for operations permuting columns.

We now review a variant of the transformation
\mathcal{T}
, which adds fixed amounts to each row and column count
[
GSS
,
GPS
]
. The
advantage is that one can get a nontrivial growth dynamics even if the initial set is
A=\emptyset
, in which case the behavior of iterates is significantly more regular.
Our transformation
\mathcal{T}
is given additional two parameters
\vec{r}=(r_{0},\ldots,r_{m-1})\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}^{m}

and
\vec{c}=(c_{0},\ldots,c_{n-1})\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}^{n}
, which are sequences of nonnegative integers called
enhancements
. Then
the
enhanced neighborhood growth
is given by the triple
({\mathcal{Z}},\vec{r},\vec{c})
and is defined as follows:

\mathcal{T}(A)=A\cup\{(j,i)\in R_{n,m}\colon(|{\mathcal{N}}^{r}(j,i)\cap A|+r_{i},|{\mathcal{N}}^{c}(j,i)\cap A|+c_{j})\notin{\mathcal{Z}}\}.

We do not introduce a new notation as the usual neighborhood growth given by
{\mathcal{Z}}
is the same as its enhancement given by
({\mathcal{Z}},\vec{0},\vec{0})
, and we do not distinguish between the two.
We denote by
|\cdot|
the
\ell^{1}
-norm, thus

|\vec{r}|=r_{0}+\cdots+r_{m-1}
and
|\vec{c}|=c_{0}+\cdots+c_{n-1}
.

The next lemma, whose proof is a simple
verification (see
[
GPS
, Lemma 2.3]
), gives the key regularity property of the enhanced rule.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\mathcal{T}
be the dynamics defined by the triple
({\mathcal{Z}},\vec{r},\vec{c})
consisting of a zero-set
{\mathcal{Z}}
and nonincreasing enhancements
\vec{r}
and
\vec{c}
.
If
A\subseteq R_{n,m}
is a Young diagram, then so is
\mathcal{T}(A)
.
Consequently,
\mathcal{T}^{k}(\emptyset)
is a Young diagram for every
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
.

We conclude this section with two lemmas about the connection between regular and enhanced dynamics. For
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
,
A\subseteq R_{n,m}
, and
i\in[0,m-1]
,
j\in[0,n-1]
,
we denote by

and
\text{\tt col}_{j}(A)
the number of elements
in the
i
th row and
j
th column of

, respectively.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
and the zero-set
{\mathcal{Z}}
be fixed.
Assume
A\subseteq R_{n,m}
and the enhancements
\vec{r}
and
\vec{c}
are chosen so that: for each
i\in[0,m-1]
,
\text{\tt row}_{i}(A)\leq r_{i}
;
and for each
j\in[0,n-1]
,
\text{\tt col}_{j}(A)\leq c_{j}
.
Let
\mathcal{T}
be the regular dynamics (with zero enhancements) and let

\widehat{\mathcal{T}}
be the dynamics defined by
({\mathcal{Z}},\vec{r},\vec{c})
.
Then, for every
k
,

\mathcal{T}^{k}(A)\setminus\widehat{\mathcal{T}}^{k}(\emptyset)\subseteq A
.

Proof.

We prove the equivalent claim that
\mathcal{T}^{k}(A)\setminus A\subseteq\widehat{\mathcal{T}}^{k}(\emptyset)
by induction on
k
. For
k=0
, both sets in the claim are empty.
Assume now the claim is true for some
k
.
Then,
\mathcal{T}^{k}(A)\setminus A\subseteq\widehat{\mathcal{T}}^{k}(\emptyset)\subseteq\widehat{\mathcal{T}}^{k+1}(\emptyset)
.
Furthermore, for any point

z=(j,i)\in\mathcal{T}^{k+1}(A)\setminus\mathcal{T}^{k}(A)
,

|{\mathcal{N}}^{r}(z)\cap\mathcal{T}^{k}(A)|=|{\mathcal{N}}^{r}(z)\cap A|+|{\mathcal{N}}^{r}(z)\cap(\mathcal{T}^{k}(A)\setminus A)|\leq r_{i}+|{\mathcal{N}}^{r}(z)\cap\widehat{\mathcal{T}}^{k}(\emptyset)|,

by the assumption and by the inductive hypothesis. Analogously,

|{\mathcal{N}}^{c}(z)\cap\mathcal{T}^{k}(A)|\leq c_{j}+|{\mathcal{N}}^{c}(z)\cap\widehat{\mathcal{T}}^{k}(\emptyset)|.

Since
z\in\mathcal{T}^{k+1}(A)
, the definition of
\mathcal{T}
yields that
(|{\mathcal{N}}^{r}(z)\cap\mathcal{T}^{k}(A)|,|{\mathcal{N}}^{c}(z)\cap\mathcal{T}^{k}(A)|)\notin{\mathcal{Z}}
.
Hence, also

(r_{i}+|{\mathcal{N}}^{r}(z)\cap\widehat{\mathcal{T}}^{k}(\emptyset)|,c_{j}+|{\mathcal{N}}^{c}(z)\cap\widehat{\mathcal{T}}^{k}(\emptyset)|)\notin{\mathcal{Z}}

and consequently,
z\in\widehat{\mathcal{T}}^{k+1}(\emptyset)
.
∎

Fix
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
, and enhancement vectors
\vec{r}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}^{m}
,
\vec{c}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}^{n}
.
For every
A\subseteq R_{n,m}
, we define its

\widehat{A}
as an arbitrary but fixed subset of

R_{n,m}
such that:

A\subseteq\widehat{A}
; for every row

,

\text{\tt row}_{i}(\widehat{A})\geq\min(\text{\tt row}_{i}(A)+r_{i},n)
;
and for every column
j\in[0,n-1]
,

\text{\tt col}_{j}(\widehat{A})\geq\min(\text{\tt col}_{j}(A)+c_{j},m)
.
Thus, we obtain the enhancement by adding
r_{i}
elements in every row

, up to capacity, and analogously for columns.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
\mathcal{T}
be the standard dynamics given by
{\mathcal{Z}}
,
m
, and
n
, and let
\widehat{\mathcal{T}}
be the enhanced dynamics given by some

Then, each

Proof.

Since

, it suffices to show that

Consider an arbitrary

\text{\tt row}_{i}(\widehat{A})=n
or
\text{\tt col}_{j}(\widehat{A})=m
, then

. Otherwise, using the partial order
on

,

\displaystyle(|{\mathcal{N}}^{r}(z)\cap\widehat{A}|,|{\mathcal{N}}^{c}(z)\cap\widehat{A}|)

\displaystyle=(\text{\tt row}_{i}(\widehat{A}),\text{\tt col}_{j}(\widehat{A}))

\displaystyle\geq(\text{\tt row}_{i}(A)+r_{i},\text{\tt col}_{j}(A)+c_{j})

\displaystyle=(|{\mathcal{N}}^{r}(z)\cap A|+r_{i},|{\mathcal{N}}^{c}(z)\cap A|+c_{j})\notin{\mathcal{Z}}\,,

since

Therefore,
(|{\mathcal{N}}^{r}(z)\cap\widehat{A}|,|{\mathcal{N}}^{c}(z)\cap\widehat{A}|)\notin{\mathcal{Z}}

3
A connection with extremal graph theory

For a family of graphs
\mathcal{F}
, let
\mathrm{ex}(n,\mathcal{F})
denote the maximum number of edges in a graph on
n
vertices that does not contain any member of
\mathcal{F}
as a subgraph.
The numbers
\mathrm{ex}(n,\mathcal{F})
are called
Turán numbers
. Their determination is a classical problem in extremal graph theory, even in the special case when
\mathcal{F}
consists of a single graph
H
(see, e.g.,
[
Bol
,
AKSV
]
).

Similarly, the
bipartite Turán problem
considers two positive integers
m
and
n
and a family of graphs
{\mathcal{F}}
such that each
H\in{\mathcal{F}}
is a bipartite graph equipped with a bipartition
(X_{H},Y_{H})
.
The quantity of interest is then the
bipartite Turán number

\mathrm{ex}(m,n,{\mathcal{F}})
, the maximum number of edges in a bipartite graph
G
with bipartition
(X,Y)
with
|X|=m
and
|Y|=n
that does not contain any
H\in{\mathcal{F}}
as a subgraph that respects the given bipartitions (see, e.g.,
[
FS
]
, where the bipartite Turán number is denoted by
\mathbf{ex^{\ast}}(m,n,{\mathcal{F}})
).
Most research in this direction has addressed the case when
{\mathcal{F}}
contains a single graph
H
. When
H
is a complete bipartite graph
K_{s,t}
, the problem is known as the

Zarankiewicz problem
(see
[
Zar
]
).
While the determination of Zarankiewicz numbers
\mathrm{ex}(m,n,K_{s,t})

is still an open question (see, e.g.,
[
CHM
,
CHM2
,
Con
,
ST
]
for some recent developments), the bipartite Turán problem was completely solved for paths (see
[
GyRS
]
) and partially for cycles (see, e.g.,
[
LN
]
).
A variant of the problem was considered recently by Caro, Patkós, and Tuza (see
[
CPT
]
).

Our results lead to a resolution of the bipartite Turán problem for some families of double stars, including those containing a single double star.
Given two integers
p,q\geq 0
, a
double star

S_{p,q}
is the graph obtained from the disjoint union of complete bipartite graphs
K_{1,p}
and
K_{1,q}
by adding an edge between a vertex
u
of degree
p
in
K_{1,p}
and a vertex
v
of degree
q
in
K_{1,q}
(note that the vertex
u
is uniquely determined if
p\neq 1
and similarly for
v
).
We assume that
S_{p,q}
is equipped with a bipartition
(X_{p,q},Y_{p,q})
such that
u\in X_{p,q}
and
v\in Y_{p,q}
.
A family
\mathcal{F}
of double stars is
minimal
if, for all distinct
(p,q)
,
(p^{\prime},q^{\prime})
such that
S_{p,q},S_{p^{\prime},q^{\prime}}\in{\mathcal{F}}
, it is not true that
(p,q)\leq(p^{\prime},q^{\prime})
(using the partial order in
{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}^{2}
).
We will show that the problems of computing the bipartite Turán number for a minimal family of double stars and the Young domination number are dual to each other, in the sense specified below.

Assume that
m
and
n
are positive integers,
X
and
Y
disjoint
sets with
|X|=m
and
|Y|=n
, and that
\mathcal{F}
is a family of double stars, with fixed bipartitions.
Observe that, for a given
S_{p,q}\in{\mathcal{F}}
, a given bipartite graph
G
with parts
X
and
Y
contains
S_{p,q}
as a subgraph that respects the bipartitions if and only if there exist vertices
x\in X
,
y\in Y
, so that
xy
is an edge of
G
, and the degrees of
x
and
y
in
G
are at least
p+1
and
q+1
, respectively.
Recall that we denote by
\mathrm{ex}(m,n,\mathcal{F})
the corresponding bipartite Turán number, the maximum number of edges in a bipartite graph
G
with bipartition
(X,Y)
that does not contain any
S_{p,q}\in{\mathcal{F}}
as a subgraph that respects the bipartitions.
Note that we may replace
{\mathcal{F}}
by an equivalent minimal
family by keeping only those
S_{p,q}
for which
(p,q)
is minimal.

Let us now connect these notions to Young domination in Hamming rectangles. Recall that, if
{\mathcal{Z}}
is a zero-set, a point
(x,y)
belongs to
{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}^{2}\setminus{\mathcal{Z}}
if and only if
(a,b)\leq(x,y)
for some concave corner
(a,b)
of
{\mathcal{Z}}
.
To any minimal family
{\mathcal{F}}
of double stars, we associate a Young diagram
{\mathcal{Y}}_{\mathcal{F}}
whose concave corners are all points
(q,p)
such that
S_{p,q}\in{\mathcal{F}}
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Fix
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Assume that a zero-set
{\mathcal{Z}}
and a minimal family

{\mathcal{F}}
of double stars are such that

{\mathcal{Z}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}_{\mathcal{F}}
are both subsets of
R_{n,m}

and duals of each other, in the sense of Definition
2.1
.
Then,

\gamma({\mathcal{Z}},m,n)+\mathrm{ex}(m,n,\mathcal{F})=mn\,.

Proof.

By definition,
\gamma({\mathcal{Z}},m,n)
is the minimum cardinality of a set
A\subseteq V(K_{m}\Box K_{n})
such that every vertex
v\in V(K_{m}\Box K_{n})\setminus A
satisfies
(|{\mathcal{N}}^{r}(v)\cap A|,|{\mathcal{N}}^{c}(v)\cap A|)\not\in{\mathcal{Z}}
, that is, there exists a concave corner
z=(a,b)
of
{\mathcal{Z}}
such that
|{\mathcal{N}}^{r}(v)\cap A|\geq a
and
|{\mathcal{N}}^{c}(v)\cap A|\geq b
.
Let us identify the vertices of
K_{m}\Box K_{n}
with the edges of the complete bipartite graph
K_{m,n}
with bipartition
(X,Y)=(V(K_{m}),V(K_{n}))
.
Then,
A
corresponds to a set of edges of
K_{m,n}
such that for every edge
(i,j)
of
K_{m,n}
that is not in
A
there exists a concave corner
(a,b)\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Z}}
such that vertex
i\in X
is incident with at least
a
edges in
A
and vertex
j\in Y
is incident with at least
b
edges in
A
.
Let
A^{c}
denote the bipartite complement of
A
in
K_{m,n}
, that is, the bipartite graph with bipartition
(X,Y)
in which two vertices
i\in X
and
j\in Y
are adjacent if and only if
(i,j)\not\in A
.
The above condition on
A
can be equivalently phrased in terms of
A^{c}
, as follows: for every edge
(i,j)
of
A^{c}
there exists a concave corner
(a,b)\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Z}}
such that
i
is incident with at most
n-a
edges in
A^{c}
and
j
is incident with at most
m-b
edges in
A^{c}
.
This is in turn equivalent to the following condition: for all
p,q\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, if a double star
S_{p,q}
is a subgraph of
A^{c}
such that the vertex of degree
p+1
belongs to
X
and the vertex of degree
q+1
belongs to
Y
, then there exists a concave corner
(a,b)\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Z}}
such that
p\leq n-a-1
and
q\leq m-b-1
(note that since
p\geq 0
, we have that
a\leq n-1
and, similarly,
b\leq m-1
).
In other words, the double stars that can appear as subgraphs of
A^{c}
are precisely the double stars
S_{p,q}
such that
(p,q)
is an element of the dual

\textstyle\widehat{\vrule width=0.0pt,height=6.83331pt\vrule height=0.0pt,width=6.11111pt}

\textstyle{\mathcal{Z}}

of
{\mathcal{Z}}
.
Equivalently, the minimal family of double stars that are forbidden for
A^{c}
are precisely the stars in
\mathcal{F}
.
Hence, the maximum possible number of edges in
A^{c}
is given by the value of
\mathrm{ex}(m,n,\mathcal{F})
.
Since the number of edges in
A^{c}
equals
mn-|A|
, minimizing the cardinality of
A
is equivalent to maximizing the cardinality of
A^{c}
, which implies
\gamma({\mathcal{Z}},m,n)=mn-\mathrm{ex}(m,n,\mathcal{F})
, as claimed.
∎

Our results on exact evaluation of
\gamma
then immediately give the following results.
In particular, the next corollary is given by Proposition

.
Note that the double star
S_{a,0}

.
Therefore, for
{\mathcal{F}}=\{S_{a,0},S_{0,b}\}

counts the maximum number of edges in a bipartite graph with parts

and

of sizes

and

, respectively, such that each vertex in

.

Corollary 3.2

For
0\leq a\leq n
,

{\mathchoice{{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hbox{\raise 7.09259pt\hbox{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\lower 7.09259pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\widehat{\vrule width=0.0pt,height=6.83331pt\vrule height=0.0pt,width=7.67015pt}$}}}}\cr\hbox{$\displaystyle R$}\crcr}}}}{{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hbox{\raise 7.09259pt\hbox{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\lower 7.09259pt\hbox{$\textstyle\widehat{\vrule width=0.0pt,height=6.83331pt\vrule height=0.0pt,width=7.67015pt}$}}}}\cr\hbox{$\textstyle R$}\crcr}}}}{{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hbox{\raise 6.40926pt\hbox{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\lower 6.40926pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\widehat{\vrule width=0.0pt,height=4.78334pt\vrule height=0.0pt,width=6.06009pt}$}}}}\cr\hbox{$\scriptstyle R$}\crcr}}}}{{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hbox{\raise 5.9537pt\hbox{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\lower 5.9537pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\widehat{\vrule width=0.0pt,height=3.41667pt\vrule height=0.0pt,width=5.15805pt}$}}}}\cr\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle R$}\crcr}}}}}_{a,b}=V_{n-a,m-b}
.
Consequently, for the

{\mathcal{F}}=\{S_{a,0},S_{0,b}\}

\mathrm{ex}(m,n,\mathcal{F})=\min\{am,bn\}\,.

Proof.

Note that
{\mathcal{Y}}_{\mathcal{F}}=R_{a,b}

Lemma 4.1

Lemma 5.1
.

For all positive integers

, it holds that
\gamma\geq an/2
.

Proof.

To prove that
\gamma\geq an/2
, we begin with

,

, are such that for every

\sum_{k,k\neq j}(1-x_{ij})x_{ik}+\sum_{k,k\neq i}(1-x_{ij})x_{kj}\geq a(1-x_{ij}),

(5.2)

\sigma:=\sum_{i,j}x_{ij}\geq\frac{na}{2}.

Let

and
c_{j}=\sum_{i}x_{ij}

Summing (

) over all

(5.3)

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\sum_{j,k,k\neq j}(1-x_{ij})x_{ik}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}|\{(k,j)\colon x_{ik}=1\text{ and }x_{ij}=0\}|

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}r_{i}(n-r_{i})

\displaystyle=n\sigma-\sum_{i}r_{i}^{2}.

Similarly, the second terms on the left-hand side of (

) sum to

(5.4)

n\sigma-\sum_{j}c_{j}^{2}.

Note that, by the Cauchy–Bunyakovsky–Schwarz inequality applied to the vectors

\sigma^{2}=(\sum_{i}r_{i})^{2}\leq n\cdot\sum_{i}r_{i}^{2}

Therefore

so that (

Lemma 5.5

Theorem 6.2

There exists an algorithm polynomial in

In this proof, the enhancement vectors are always assumed to be nonincreasing.

We show how to compute

In order to explain it, we fix some notation and preliminary observations.

and

for all
(k^{\prime},\ell^{\prime},C^{\prime})\in{\mathcal{V}}_{q-1}
.
Then we compute all the values

for

.

The values of
k
,
\ell
,
C
,
q
are considered fixed throughout Step 2, so the dependency of various quantities on them is suppressed from the notation.
We partition the set

of feasible solutions

for

according to the values of the last coordinates,
x_{q}
and
y_{q}
: for
A\in[0,k]
and
B\in[0,\ell]
, we set
{\cal D}_{A,B}=\{(\vec{x},\vec{y})\in{\cal D}\colon x_{q}=A,y_{q}=B\}
.
Then

(6.4)

f_{q}(k,\ell,C)=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq A\leq k\\
0\leq B\leq\ell\end{subarray}}g(A,B),

where

g(A,B)=\min\left\{\sum_{i=0}^{q}(a_{i}+C)x_{i}+\sum_{j=0}^{q}b_{p-j}y_{j}+\sum_{0\leq i<j\leq q}x_{i}y_{j}\colon(\vec{x},\vec{y})\in{\cal D}_{A,B}\right\}\,.

By (
6.4
), it follows that in order to compute the value
f_{q}(k,\ell,C)
it suffices to compute the
\mathcal{O}(|k||\ell|)
values

.

Since every feasible solution

for

satisfies

and

, the objective function value simplifies to

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{q-1}(a_{i}+C)x_{i}+(a_{q}+C)\cdot A+\sum_{j=0}^{q-1}b_{p-j}y_{j}+b_{p-q}\cdot B+\sum_{0\leq i<j\leq q-1}x_{i}y_{j}+\left(\sum_{i=0}^{q-1}x_{i}\right)\cdot B

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{i=0}^{q-1}(a_{i}+B+C)x_{i}+\sum_{j=0}^{q-1}b_{p-j}y_{j}+\sum_{0\leq i<j\leq q-1}x_{i}y_{j}\right)+((a_{q}+C)\cdot A+b_{p-q}\cdot B)\,.

Therefore, by considering the vectors
\vec{x}\,^{\prime}
and
\vec{y}\,^{\prime}
obtained from
\vec{x}
and
\vec{y}
by removing the last coordinates,

\vec{x}\,^{\prime}=(x_{0},\ldots,x_{q-1})
and
\vec{y}\,^{\prime}=(y_{0},\ldots,y_{q-1})
, we infer that

\displaystyle g(A,B)

\displaystyle=\left((a_{q}+C)\cdot A+b_{p-q}\cdot B\right)

\displaystyle+\min\Bigg\{\sum_{i=0}^{q-1}(a_{i}+B+C)x^{\prime}_{i}+\sum_{j=0}^{q-1}b_{p-j}y^{\prime}_{j}+\sum_{0\leq i<j\leq q-1}x^{\prime}_{i}y^{\prime}_{j}\colon

\displaystyle\vec{x}\,^{\prime},\vec{y}\,^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{q-1}\,,\,|\vec{x}\,^{\prime}|=k-A\,,\,|\vec{y}\,^{\prime}|=\ell-B\Bigg\}\,,

which simplifies further to

(6.5)

g(A,B)=((a_{q}+C)\cdot A+b_{p-q}\cdot B)+f_{q-1}(k-A,\ell-B,B+C)\,.

Note that since

and
B\leq\ell\leq n

the value of

was already computed.
Using (
6.4
) and (

), this shows that the value
f_{q}(k,\ell,C)
can be computed in time
\mathcal{O}(|k||\ell|)
from the previously computed values.

The number of values

for all

and
(k,\ell,C)\in{\mathcal{V}}_{q}

is
\mathcal{O}(mn^{2}p^{2})
and they can
therefore all be computed in time
\mathcal{O}(m^{2}n^{3}p^{2})
.
Recalling that
\widehat{\gamma}({\mathcal{Z}},m,n)=f_{p}(m,n,0)
,
this completes the proof.
∎

.
