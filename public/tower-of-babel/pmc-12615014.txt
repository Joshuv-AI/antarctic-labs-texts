Rethinking SLIL classifications in the age of needle arthroscopy: do we need sub-grades?
Authors: Daniel Reiser, Sanjeev Kakar
Published: 2025

­

In the advent of needle arthroscopy, certain arthroscopic wrist classifications may need to be rethought. This article provides a rationale for why this may be important moving forward.

The natural history of untreated scapholunate interosseous ligament (SLIL) injuries remains incompletely understood. While early publications by Harrington et al1 and Watson et al2 asserted that scapholunate advanced collapse (SLAC) is an inevitable consequence of SLIL disruption, subsequent evidence has failed to consistently support this deterministic progression. Nonetheless, when left untreated, SLIL injuries may indeed lead to carpal instability and eventual degenerative changes.3,4

Wrist arthroscopy has become the gold standard for diagnosing traumatic injuries of the scapholunate interosseous ligament (SLIL) and triangular fibrocartilage complex (TFCC),5,6 surpassing MRI in sensitivity and specificity.7 Despite this, debate persists regarding the most appropriate classification and management strategies for these injuries. As emphasized by d’Ailly et al,5 standardized guidelines for arthroscopic procedures and interpretation are still lacking, despite widespread clinical reliance on two primary classification systems: the Geissler and the European Wrist Arthroscopy Society (EWAS) systems.8,9 The various treatment strategies are also tied to the arthroscopic classification,6,10 based on the fact that the various classification grades correspond to different ligament tears.4,9

The Geissler classification, introduced in the 1990s, remains one of the most widely used systems for grading SLIL injuries via wrist arthroscopy. In Geissler grade 3, a probe (typically around 2 mm) can pass through the scapholunate interval, whereas in grade 4, a standard 2.7 mm arthroscope can be passed through the gap, demonstrating gross instability and incongruity from both the radiocarpal and midcarpal portals. Similarly, the EWAS classification (Messina et al11) distinguishes between stages based on probe and arthroscope passage, with grade 3 C indicating complete but reducible diastasis with a probe, and grade 4 describing arthroscope passage across the SL gap without radiological abnormality.

A key feature of both classifications is this dichotomy: if only the probe fits through, the lesion is considered less severe (grade 3); if the arthroscope fits, the injury is considered more severe (grade 4). This distinction, while practical in the context of traditional arthroscopic equipment, maybe challenged in the context of evolving technology.

In the recent five to ten years, flexible needle arthroscopy has gained traction among hand and wrist surgeons due to its minimal invasiveness, reduced morbidity, and suitability for office-based procedures. These flexible systems can offer a 1.9 mm camera with a 2.2 mm sheath, significantly smaller than the standard 2.7 mm arthroscope with its 4.0 mm sheath. Importantly, this places the needle arthroscope in a size range much closer to a traditional probe than to a full-sized arthroscope.

This raises a fundamental question: are the current arthroscopic classifications still appropriate in needle arthroscopy, especially in determining grade 4 instabilities? This is an important consideration as treatments may be linked to correct staging, and there is evidence for the low or only fair interobserver reliability of the Geissler classification.8,12

In our practice, we have adopted needle arthroscopy for diagnosing and staging SLIL injuries. Like many colleagues, we continue to use the established Geissler and EWAS classifications. However, we are increasingly aware that these systems, rooted in the dimensions of traditional equipment, may lead to inadvertent misclassification of lesion severity when applied with smaller scopes.

Let us consider the Geissler system. If a 1.9 mm needle arthroscope, including the 2.2 mm sheath, passes through the scapholunate interval, does that truly represent a grade 4 lesion? According to Geissler’s original definition, this finding would require passage of a 2.7 mm (including the 4 mm outer sheath) arthroscope and visible incongruity. The needle arthroscope’s passage, though possible, does not necessarily signify the same degree of ligamentous disruption or instability. In fact, it may reflect a level of injury that lies between grades 3 and 4, a form of ‘intermediate instability’ not currently recognized in the classification.

A similar concern applies to the EWAS classification, which implicitly assumes the use of standard arthroscopic equipment. Passage of a needle arthroscope from the midcarpal to the radiocarpal joint through the SL interval may be mistakenly labelled as a stage 4 lesion, despite the fact that the scope’s diameter is more comparable to that of a probe.

Therefore, we encourage discussion on recognizing the limitations of current systems for needle arthroscopy. In particular, we propose discussing the possibility of intermediate classes (without formally naming new classes) that account for the smaller diameter of needle arthroscopes. For example, in the Geissler classification, passage of a 1.9 mm needle arthroscope within the 2.2 mm sheath without visible step-off could be designated as class 3b to distinguish it from the classic grade 4 based on a 2.7 mm arthroscope. Similarly, in the EWAS system, this finding could correspond to a new sub-group (class 4a) that accounts for the changed instrumentation. As minimally invasive wrist arthroscopy continues to evolve, our classification systems must evolve in parallel. The accuracy and clinical utility of our diagnoses depend on it.
