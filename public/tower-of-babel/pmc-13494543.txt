Optimizing microhomology-based genome editing by engineering DNA polymerase θ for improved efficiency and reduced on-target indels
Authors: Wanyi Wang, Meng Zhou, Tianshan Ji, Qin Jiang, Jinyi Zhao, Zimeng Xu, Ruofei Liu, Yining Luo, Feng Han, Le Cong, Lei Fan, Chen Chen, Chengkun Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryPrecise genome editing through targeted DNA insertion is critical for gene therapy and biomedical research. While existing methods rely on homology-directed repair (HDR), this process suffers from low efficiency in non-dividing cells. Microhomology-mediated repair provides an alternative but remains intrinsically inefficient. Here, we developed a DNA polymerase θ (Pol θ)-based editor (PET) to enhance kilobase-scale targeted DNA integration across diverse genomic loci and cell types. We demonstrate that polymerase domain (pPET) and helicase-like domain (hPET) independently improve microhomology-mediated editing, with pPET achieving a 3-fold increase in knock-in rates over conventional SpCas9-mediated editing. Next-generation sequencing reveals that pPET and hPET reduce on-target indel rates by ∼30%, while increasing precise insertions by up to 80%. Compared with other Pol θ domain configurations and MMEJ-enhancing factors, pPET exhibits superior performance. These findings establish Pol θ functional domains as effective tools for improving microhomology-driven genome editing and advancing therapeutic applications.Keywords: MMEJ, polymerase θ, gene edit, helicase-like domain, polymerase domain

Introduction

Precise genetic modification through targeted DNA insertion is fundamental for diverse applications ranging from biological research to gene therapy.1,2,3,4,5 Current approaches employ programmable nucleases like CRISPR-Cas9 to induce double-strand breaks (DSBs), relying on repair through either non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) or homology-directed repair (HDR).6,7 However, NHEJ can generate unwanted indels at the DSBs,8,9 while HDR has a low efficiency in non-dividing cells and requires extensive homology arms for donor DNA templates, limiting its utility for precise knock-in.1,10,11

Microhomology-mediated end joining (MMEJ) has emerged as an alternative, templated repair pathway that leverages short (5–25 bp) microhomology sequences to guide targeted integration with higher structural accuracy than NHEJ.12,13 MMEJ-based knock-in strategies have been demonstrated in mammalian cells14 and mouse tissues,15 showing that microhomology-flanked donors can support precise cassette insertion. However, these studies also revealed important limitations, including modest efficiency and flanking indel formation,16 which reduce the robustness of MMEJ as a practical precision-editing approach. Improving microhomology-driven repair thus remains a central goal for expanding high-precision genome engineering.

MMEJ partially overlaps with polymerase θ-mediated end joining (TMEJ), a polymerase θ (Pol θ)-dependent repair mechanism capable of acting on short microhomologies and particularly relevant when classical NHEJ is compromised.17,18,19 Although historically grouped together, MMEJ and TMEJ are now recognized as mechanistically related but genetically separable pathways: both rely on annealing of microhomology sequences, yet TMEJ strictly requires Pol θ, whereas canonical MMEJ can occur independently of Pol θ.20,21 TMEJ activity is enriched during mitosis, when Pol θ helps safeguard genome integrity by repairing resected DSBs that cannot be resolved by HDR.22 However, several studies demonstrate that Pol θ can also promote microhomology-guided repair in S/G2 phases,23,24,25 and may associate with chromatin in G1,26,27,28 suggesting dual roles: its well-established function in mitosis-enriched TMEJ, and a broader capacity to capture short microhomologies and initiate DNA synthesis across DSB ends, thereby supporting microhomology-mediated repair across diverse cell-cycle states.

Pol θ, encoded by POLQ, is a multi-domain protein composed of an N-terminal helicase-like domain (Pol θ-HEL), a central region, and a C-terminal polymerase domain (Pol θ-POL).29,30 The Pol θ-HEL displaces RPA and other proteins from DNA and aids in microhomology search,31,32 while Pol θ-POL facilitates microhomology annealing and performs DNA synthesis across the break site.33,34 In U2OS cells, deletion of the central region (POLQ Δcen) increased DNA joining efficiency compared to the wild-type protein, suggesting that this domain normally restricts repair efficiency. Conversely, removal of either the HEL or POL domains abolishes microhomology-mediated repair, highlighting the critical roles of microhomology search, stabilization, and synthesis in these processes.17 Although Pol θ is recognized as the key driver of TMEJ, whether its individual domains can be repurposed as compact, standalone genome editing effectors remains largely unexplored.

Here, we hypothesized that leveraging Pol θ or its individual functional domains could enhance microhomology-mediated kilobase-scale knock-in efficiency while minimizing unwanted indel formation. Our results demonstrated that both the polymerase (POL) and helicase-like (HEL) domains of Pol θ independently enhance microhomology-mediated genome editing, with the POL domain showing superior performance in facilitating a precise DNA knock-in. Importantly, these functional domains achieved higher editing efficiency than full-length Pol θ and outperformed other microhomology-enhancing strategies, including the overexpression (OE) of key MMEJ factors such as LIG3, and Exo1, across many genomic loci and cell types, including cells arrested in the G1 phase. These systems also demonstrated improved editing precision through reduced on-target indel rates, making them promising candidates for therapeutic applications. Our findings establish Pol θ functional domains as powerful enhancers of microhomology-driven genome editing. By addressing the key limitations of efficiency and precision in microhomology-mediated repair, these engineered CRISPR-Cas9 tools expand the possibilities for precise genome engineering and provide practical solutions for therapeutic gene editing.

Results

Pol θ functional domains outperform full-length protein in enhancing microhomology-mediated precision genome editing

Pol θ is a central mediator of microhomology-mediated end joining (MMEJ/TMEJ), an error-prone yet versatile pathway responsible for repairing DNA DSBs through the use of short microhomology sequences.17 Given its established role in stabilizing annealed microhomologies and synthesizing DNA across resected ends,30,31,35 we hypothesized that targeted recruitment of Pol θ or its individual functional domains to SpCas9-induces DSBs would bias repair toward microhomology-mediated repair and thereby improve the efficiency and precision of targeted gene editing.

Human Pol θ is a 2590-amino-acid protein comprising two major functional domains connected by an unstructured central region (Figure 1A). The helicase domain (Pol θ-HEL, 1–900 aa) at the N terminus facilitates RPA (replication protein A) displacement and promotes annealing of single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) with microhomologies in an ATP-dependent manner.31,36 The polymerase domain (Pol θ-POL, 1819–2590 aa) at the C terminus catalyzes DNA synthesis on substrates with 2–6 bp of microhomologies, mediating microhomology-mediated repair after DSBs.18,37

To test our hypothesis, we used the SAM recruiting system to evaluate Pol θ and its functional domains during the microhomology-mediated DNA repair process38,39 (Figures 1B and S1A). Pol θ, its POL domain, and its HEL domain were fused to the MS2 coat protein (MCP) through an EXTEN linker, while MS2 RNA loops were incorporated into the sgRNA scaffold. Using this system, we evaluated microhomology-mediated knock-in efficiency at endogenous loci (HSP90AA1, ACTB, RAB11A, and SEC61B) in HEK293FT cells, employing an microhomology donor template comprising a P2A-mKate expression cassette (∼1 kb) flanked by 25 bp microhomology arms (MHAs). Precise knock-in results in the correct insertion of the P2A-mKate expression cassette before the stop codon of the targeted gene, leading to mKate expression and enabling quantification of precise gene editing efficiency by measuring the percentage of mKate-positive cells (Figure S1B). FACS confirmed that microhomology-mediated integration yielded substantially higher knock-in efficiencies than HDR controls. Particularly, both fluorescence imaging and FACS analysis demonstrated that full-length Pol θ significantly increased mKate insertion efficiency across all tested loci up to 1.5-fold (Figures 1C–1G and S1C). Remarkably, both the POL and HEL domains outperformed full-length Pol θ at every locus tested, increasing knock-in efficiencies by ∼2- to 3-fold relative to control. Notably, the POL domain consistently exhibited the strongest enhancement, achieving approximately 15.2% knock-in efficiency.

To complement the recruitment strategy, we directly fused Pol θ or its domains to the C terminus of SpCas9 using an EXTEN linker (Figures 1H and 1I). As observed with SAM recruitment, POL and HEL domain fusions significantly increased microhomology-mediated knock-in at all tested loci, with POL domain again producing the largest improvement (∼2-fold over control). Full-length Pol θ fusion was the least effective, mirroring the trend seen with the SAM system.

To determine whether the catalytic activities of these domains were required during the microhomology-mediated gene editing process, we introduced established loss-of-function mutations into either HEL (F642A, L644A, N773A, and L777A) or POL (S1977P, K2181A, R2202A, R2254A, D2540A, and E2541A) domain and compare their effect on the gene editing34,40,41,42 (Figures 1J and S1D). All three inactivated variants failed to enhance microhomology-mediated integration relative to wild-type controls, confirming that the enzymatic functions of both domains are essential for promoting this process (Figures 1K and 1L).

We further engineered a POL-HEL dual-domain fusion protein (Figure S1D). When the two domains were linked by a short 3×GGGGS linker, the enhancement of mKate knock-in was minimal. Extending the linker to 35 amino acids markedly improved the editing efficiency, exceeding that of either domain expressed alone (Figures 1K and 1L). Moreover, co-expressing POL and HEL as separate proteins and simultaneously recruiting them to the Cas9-sgRNA complex via a dual-recruitment (DR) design further increased knock-in efficiency (Figures 1K and 1L; Figure S2A). In contrast, random OE of POLQ, POL, or HEL in the absence of recruitment substantially diminished their effects, suggesting proximity to the DSB is required for activity (Figures 1K and 1L; Figure S2B). Together, these results suggest that excessively short linkers impose steric constraints that limit domain cooperation at the break site, whereas longer linkers or recruiting the domains independently enhance their synergistic contribution to microhomology-mediated repair.

These findings collectively establish Pol θ and its functional domains as potent enhancers of microhomology-mediated genome editing, with isolated domains demonstrating superior performance across multiple experimental systems. Given the consistently higher efficiency observed with the POL domain-mediated CRISPR-Cas9 editor (pPET) and HEL domain-mediated CRISPR-Cas9 editor (hPET) compared to the full-length Pol θ editor (PET), we strategically prioritized these functional domains and utilized the SAM recruiting system to establish our pPET and hPET systems for subsequent optimization studies.

pPET and hPET demonstrate robust editing efficiency across various template designs and cell types

To further optimize the POL and HEL domains as enhancers of microhomology-mediated genome editing, we systematically evaluated template design parameters and assessed performance across multiple cell types. First, we examined the influence of MHA length on editing efficiency utilizing the P2A-mKate reporter template system (Figure 2A). Our results revealed that increasing MHA length generally improved editing efficiency, with longer MHAs enhancing precise editing at HSP90AA1, ACTB, and HIST1H2BK loci (Figures 2B–2D). Extending microhomology length from 0 to 25 bp substantially increased integration efficiency; however, further extension to 50 bp did not consistently provide additional benefit, with 25 bp frequently yielding the highest activity across conditions. Notably, pPET consistently outperformed hPET across all tested homology arm lengths in three test loci.

We next evaluated the impact of different donor template formats on the performance of pPET and hPET (Figure 2E). Compared with the double-stranded PCR template, the self-cleaving plasmid template, which incorporates sgRNA target sites outside of the MHAs, enables the release of the donor sequence through SpCas9/sgRNA-mediated cleavage. Both the double-stranded PCR template and the self-cleaving plasmid template effectively mediated mKate knock-in at multiple endogenous loci (Figure 2F). Consistent with previous findings, pPET and hPET outperformed the control group in editing efficiency.

To assess the capacity of these systems to support larger genomic insertions, we tested the integration of extended expression cassettes ranging from 1 to 3 kb (Figures 2G and 2H; Figure S2C). As expected, HDR donors with 100 bp homology arms exhibited reduced knock-in efficiency as insert size increased. By contrast, Pol θ, hPET, and pPET maintained strong performance, with insertion efficiencies showing minimal decline across cargo sizes and retaining a 2- to 3-fold improvement over MMEJ controls (Figures 2G and 2H). These results underscore the ability of Pol θ-based editors to support kilobase-scale gene insertion.

To further evaluate the versatility of our system, we applied pPET and hPET to additional mammalian cell lines (MCF7 and N2A) (Figures 2I and 2J; Figures S2D and S2E). In MCF7 breast cancer cells, both hPET and pPET achieved remarkable knock-in efficiencies of 12.6% and 13.3% at the HSP90AA1 locus, respectively, representing an approximately 2-fold increase over the SpCas9 control (∼6.5%). Similar improvements were observed at the ACTB locus. In N2A neuroblastoma cells, we observed knock-in efficiencies of 8.1% and 9.2% with hPET and pPET at the Actb locus, compared to 4.3% in controls, with comparable gains at Tubb3 locus.

Collectively, these results establish optimal template design parameters for pPET and hPET and demonstrate their versatility across different genomic loci and cell types, highlighting their potential as robust tools for precision genome engineering.

NGS analyses revealed enhanced precision and reduced on-target indel formation with pPET and hPET

To rigorously assess the precision of pPET- and hPET-mediated genome editing, we performed targeted deep sequencing to complement our FACS-based assays. Cells were transfected on day 2, followed by incubation and medium changes on days 3 and 4, and genomic DNA collection on day 5 for NGS analysis (Figure S3A). This workflow enabled quantitative, nucleotide-level characterization of repair outcomes, including precise insertion events and unintended indels.

We first evaluate editing precision using a minimal 34 bp (LoxP) donor sequence inserted at the HEK3 locus (Figure S3B; Figures 3A–3D). Both pPET and hPET substantially reduced indel formation compared with SpCas9 alone, lowering NHEJ-associated indels from ∼49.8% in controls to ∼43.3% and ∼42.4%, respectively. Correspondingly, microhomology-mediated precise insertion increased from 1.9% in controls to 3.15% with pPET and 4.36% with hPET. Notably, the ratio of precise knock-in to total on-target editing increased 1.8-fold for pPET and 2.5-fold for hPET relative to controls, indicating a significant shift toward high-fidelity repair outcomes.

We then extended our analysis to larger insertions at HSP90AA1 and HIST1H2BK loci by integrating a 2A-mKate expression cassette upstream of each endogenous stop codon (Figure S3C). Consistent with FACS-based results, both pPET and hPET markedly improved knock-in frequencies across all targets (Figure 3E). NGS profiling further revealed a reduction in on-target indels: pPET decreased indel formation by ∼32% and hPET by ∼36% at the HSP90AA1 locus relative to SpCas9 alone (Figures 3E–3G).

Detailed inspection of repair junctions showed that pPET and hPET generated precise, scar-free insertions, as evidenced by increased fractions of perfect 5′ and 3′ junctions (Figures 3H, 3I, and S3D). In contrast, SpCas9 alone produced a heterogeneous spectrum of deletions and indels, consistent with canonical NHEJ-dominated repair. These findings mirror our observations at the HEK3 locus and confirm that POL- and HEL-based editors bias repair toward microhomology-mediated precision outcomes.

Overall, these data demonstrate that pPET and hPET offer superior performance compared to SpCas9 alone in both safety and efficacy, simultaneously reducing on-target indels while enhancing microhomology-mediated precise knock-in efficiency across multiple genomic loci.

pPET outperforms alternative domain configurations and MMEJ-enhancing strategies

To establish the comparative effectiveness of our approach, we evaluated pPET and hPET against various domain configurations and other MMEJ-enhancing factors (Figures 4A and 4B). Quantitative analysis across HSP90AA1 and ACTB loci revealed that pPET consistently achieved the highest knock-in efficiency, reaching 12%–14% across both loci compared to control groups (4%–5%) (Figures 4C, 4D, and S4A). hPET also showed substantial enhancement, followed by full length Pol θ OE. As observed earlier, OE of a catalytically inactivated Pol θ (mPol θ) failed to promote editing, indicating that enzymatic activity is essential for improvement.

We next evaluated other components of the MMEJ pathway using the SAM recruitment system, including EXO1,15,43 LIG3,44,45 and POLD1.46,47 These factors provided only modest increases in editing efficiency relative to controls and produced levels comparable to full-length Pol θ OE, but all remained significantly less effective than pPET (Figures 4B–4D and S4A). Among these alternatives, EXO1 showed the strongest effect (6%–8% efficiency), followed by LIG3 and POLD1 (5%–7%), but underscoring the POL domain’s unique capacity to facilitate microhomology-mediated repair.

Given the central role of Pol θ in microhomology-mediated repair process, we hypothesized that endogenous Pol θ might influence pPET and hPET performance. To address this, we generated POLQ-knockout cell lines via lentiviral CRISPR-Cas9 delivery (Figure 4E). POLQ knockout markedly reduced knock-in efficiency in control samples and abolished the enhancements produced by EXO1, LIG3, and POLD1. By contrast, both pPET and hPET maintained strong editing activity at the HSP90AA1 locus, with comparable improvements observed at ACTB, confirming their robustness even in the absence of endogenous Pol θ (Figures 4F and 4G).

We further combined pPET and hPET with classical DNA repair pathway inhibitors, including the DNA-PKcs inhibitors NU744148 and the reported NHEJ inhibitor SCR7,49 as well as the HDR inhibitor B0250 (Figures 4H–4J and S4B). Inhibiting NHEJ or HDR enhanced microhomology-mediated mKate insertion in control groups, with NU7441 producing the largest boost. Notably, combining NU7441 or NHEJ inhibitors with pPET or hPET further increased knock-in efficiencies beyond either perturbation alone. These results indicate that the POL and HEL domains enhance microhomology-mediated repair through mechanisms distinct from classical NHEJ/HDR inhibition, and that these effects are additive when combined with pathway modulation.

Collectively, these findings establish pPET as the most effective microhomology-mediated precision editing enhancer among tested configurations, outperforming alternative MMEJ factors, Pol θ variants, and DNA repair pathway inhibitors. The strong performance of both pPET and hPET across diverse genomic loci, donor formats, and genetic backgrounds highlights their robustness and broad applicability. Moreover, the additive effects observed when combining pPET/hPET with classical NHEJ or HDR inhibitors underscore their distinct mechanistic contribution to microhomology-mediated repair. Together, these results position pPET and hPET as versatile, high-efficiency tools for precise genome engineering.

Cell-cycle independence of pPET and hPET

HDR is strictly confined to S/G2 phases, resulting in poor editing efficiency in non-dividing or cell cycle arrested cells. In contrast, microhomology-mediated repair pathways, including MMEJ and TMEJ, remain active when DNA replication is limited, suggesting that microhomology-driven editors may function independently of cell cycle position. To directly test whether pPET and hPET retain editing activity across different cell-cycle states, we synchronized HEK293T and MCF7 cells using two established strategies: serum starvation and double thymidine block (DTB) (Figures 5A and 5F).

To induce G0/G1 arrest, cells were serum-starved beginning on day 0, transfected on day 1, and collected for flow cytometry on day 4 (Figure 5A). Serum starvation efficiently arrested nearly all cells in G0/G1, as confirmed by cell-cycle profiling (Figure S4C). Under these conditions, HDR efficiency was markedly reduced at the HSP90AA1 and ACTB loci. In contrast, both pPET and hPET maintained robust knock-in activity comparable to unsynchronized controls (Figures 5B–5E and S4C).

We next synchronized cells at the G1/S boundary using DTB. Cells were exposed to thymidine for 18 h, released for 9 h, and re-treated for an additional 18 h before transfection on day 2. Samples were analyzed on day 6 (Figure 5F). DTB treatment substantially impaired HDR across both tested loci, consistent with its strict requirement for S/G2 entry. Notably, pPET and hPET again preserved strong editing activity despite the G1/S arrest, exhibiting minimal performance loss relative to untreated conditions (Figures 5G–5J and S4C).

Together, these data demonstrate that pPET and hPET support consistently high microhomology-mediated, kilobase-scale knock-in across diverse cell-cycle states, including in G0/G1-arrested and G1/S-blocked populations where HDR is severely compromised. These findings highlight pPET and hPET as powerful and versatile alternatives to HDR, with particular utility for genome editing in non-dividing or difficult-to-cycle cell types.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrate that our engineered pPET and hPET, utilizing the functional domains of DNA polymerase θ (Pol θ),17,51 significantly enhance microhomology-mediated DNA integration for precise genome editing. By systematically comparing full-length Pol θ with its isolated polymerase (POL)41 and helicase-like (HEL) domains,32,33 we established that these domain-based systems not only promote microhomology-mediated repair but also consistently outperform the full-length protein across multiple genomic loci.

Our results revealed that the POL domain exhibited the strongest enhancement of microhomology-mediated knock-in, achieving up to a 3-fold increase in editing efficiency compared to conventional SpCas9 alone. This is consistent with its established catalytic role in annealing short microhomologies and performing DNA synthesis across broken DNA ends during TMEJ/MMEJ. The HEL domain also improved DNA integration efficiency, though consistently less than the POL domain, reflecting their distinct mechanistic contributions to the repair process. The robust performance of both systems across diverse cell types (including HEK293FT, MCF7, and N2A) highlights their broad applicability. Furthermore, our analysis of donor template design, particularly homology arm length optimization, provides practical guidelines for maximizing microhomology-driven editing outcomes.

A notable advantage of pPET and hPET is their ability to simultaneously increase precise knock-in while reducing unwanted on-target indels, as quantified by NGS analysis of precise integration, NHEJ-induced mutations, and flanking junction integrity. This dual benefit addresses a significant limitation of conventional SpCas9-mediated genome editing, where DSB repair through NHEJ frequently results in unwanted mutations at the target site. By shifting the balance toward microhomology-mediated repair, pPET and hPET enhance both editing efficiency and fidelity.

When compared to alternative MMEJ-enhancing strategies, including OE of EXO1,15 LIG3,45 and POLD1,46,47 pPET demonstrated superior performance. Interestingly, combining the HEL and POL domains via long linker architectures or dual recruitment provided additive improvements, suggesting that the domains contribute complementary mechanistic functions during microhomology-mediated repair.

Importantly, pPET and hPET maintained high editing efficiency under cell-cycle synchronization using serum starvation or DTB, conditions under which HDR is severely compromised. This underscores the utility of microhomology-mediated kilobase-scale knock-in as a powerful alternative to HDR, particularly in non-dividing or G1-arrested cells, where classical homologous recombination is largely inactive. Our finding that pPET and hPET retain strong activity in G1-phase cells, where canonical TMEJ activity is thought to be reduced, suggests that the POL and HEL domains possess intrinsic biochemical capabilities that can operate independently of the full TMEJ machinery. These results raise the possibility that individual Pol θ domains can bypass cell-cycle constraints, although further mechanistic studies will be needed to define how these activities interface with endogenous repair pathways.

In conclusion, our study establishes Pol θ domains engineering as a powerful approach for enhancing microhomology-mediated genome editing. The pPET and hPET offer significant improvements in both editing efficiency and precision compared to conventional approaches, providing a foundation for developing safer and more effective gene editing technologies for both research and therapeutic applications.

Limitations of the study

Despite the advances, several challenges remain in this study. Efficient and tissue-specific delivery of pPET and hPET in vivo will be essential for therapeutic translation, and comprehensive profiling of off-target effects, including ectopic microhomology usage, will be required to establish safety. Additionally, while our data indicate that the enzymatic functions of the POL and HEL domains are critical for enhancing microhomology-mediated repair, the precise mechanistic interplay between these domains, endogenous repair factors, and cell-cycle regulators remains to be elucidated.

Experimental model and study participant details

Cell lines

HEK293T (CRL-3216), MCF7 (HTB-22) and N2A (CCL-131) were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). All the cell lines were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Homeland), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin (P/S, Biosharp) at 37°C in 5% CO2. N2A cells were further supplemented with 1% non-essential amino acids (NEAA; Gibco). Media was refreshed every 24 h. Short tandem repeat (STR) profiling was examined in all cell lines and all of the cell lines were contamination free. HEK293FT cells (HTB-30) used for lentivirus production were maintained under the same conditions.

Method details

Plasmid construction

The microhomology-mediated genome editing system was built upon an established SpCas9–sgRNA backbone (Addgene plasmid #64323). The parental plasmid was digested with NdeI (Thermo Fisher Scientific) to remove the native sgRNA scaffold, followed by purification using standard protocols. A modified MS2 stem-loop sgRNA scaffold was assembled via Gibson Assembly (2X MultiF Seamless Assembly Mix), incorporating BbsI sites to facilitate downstream guide RNA insertion. The lentivirus construction was built upon an established backbone (Addgene plasmid #98290), The parental plasmid was digested with Esp3I (Thermo Fisher Scientific) to remove the native sgRNA scaffold, followed by purification using standard protocols. Gene constructs encoding full-length or truncated Pol θ and other MMEJ factors were synthesized by Genscript. All of these effectors were fused to the MS2 coat protein (MCP) via an EXTEN linker or GS linker, and cloned into the expression vector using Gibson Assembly. Domain-specific configurations (e.g., POL, HEL) were engineered to investigate functional contributions to microhomology-mediated precise insertions. All plasmids were validated by restriction digest and Sanger sequencing in Tsingke. Constructs will be made available via Addgene. All the sequences of primer (Table S1), sgRNA (Table S2) and ORFs (Table S3) shown in the manuscript were listed in the Supplementary Tables.

RNA extraction and cDNA amplification

Total RNA was extracted using the RNA extraction and reverse transcription kit (Vazyme), and cDNA was synthesized for downstream PCR amplification of full-length open reading frames (ORFs), including EXO1, LIG3 and POLD1. Domain boundaries were annotated based on conserved domain databases and published references. The amplified genes were cloned into plasmid backbones for subsequent experiments.

Cell synchronization

Serum starvation (arrests the cell cycle at G0/G1) and Double Thymidine Block (DTB, induces cell arrest at the G1/S phase junction) were used for cell cycle synchronization respectively. HEK293T and MCF7 were digested with 0.05% trypsin, and these cells were collected, centrifuged at 200 g for 3 min, and the supernatant was discarded. The cells were washed twice with DPBS, and resuspended in the culture medium. For the serum starvation, the serum concentration in the medium was less than 0.5%. The cells were cultured in incubator at 37°C, 5% CO2 and saturated humidity for 36 h-72h.

For the cell-cycle arrest experiment via DTB, the cells were pre-treated with thymidine (Sigma) for 18h, the thymidine was removed, the cells were cultured in normal DMEM media with 10% FBS without thymidine for 9h and thymidine was added to the cells (final concentration of 2mM) for a second round of 18h. After the two-round thymidine treatment, the cells were transfected using Lipofectamine 3000 following the manufacturer’s instructions. The cells were analyzed on a BD FACSCanto II 3days later.

Cell cycle analysis

Cells were harvested by trypsinization and washed twice with ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). For fixation, cells were resuspended in 70% ice-cold ethanol and incubated at −20°C for overnight. Fixed cells were then washed twice with PBS to remove residual ethanol and resuspended in staining solution containing 50 μg/mL propidium iodide (PI; epizyme) and 100 μg/mL RNase A (epizyme) in PBS. Samples were incubated at 37°C for 30 min in the dark. Cell cycle distribution was analyzed using BD FACSCanto II, and data were acquired using FlowJo. A minimum of 50,000 events were collected per sample. Cell cycle phases (G0/G1, S, and G2/M) were determined using ModFit LT software.

Transfection

HEK293T cells were seeded in 48-well plates (Corning) at a density of 8 × 104 cells per well, 12–24 h prior to transfection. A total of 500 ng DNA (sgRNA-Cas9: MMEJ effector: donor DNA = 350ng: 125ng: 25ng. For different groups, equimolar amounts of effector proteins or donors were input) was transfected per well using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific), following the manufacturer’s protocol. MCF7 and N2A cells were seeded in 24-well plates at densities of 1 × 105 and 2 × 105 cells per well, respectively. For these cell types, 1 μg total plasmid DNA was transfected per well.

Lentivirus production and transduction

HEK293FT cells were seeded in 12-well plates (Corning) at a density of 5 × 105 cells per well, 12–24 h prior to transfection. A total of 2 μg plasmid DNA was transfected per well using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). In the cap of the tube, 1 μg lentiviral plasmid with 500 ng each 2nd generation viral packaging vectors (pMD2.G and pCMV-dR8.91 were mixed. The cells were placed back into the incubator and cultured for 48 h. The supernatant containing lentivirus was collected in a centrifuge tube. After centrifugation at 1000g for 5 min at 4°, the supernatant was transferred and passed through a 0.45um filter to remove impurities. Seed the 1 × 106 cells healthy, actively dividing HEK293T cells to each of the 6 wells. Add the virus to each well. Return cells to 37-degree incubator with 5% CO2 and allow infection to proceed for at least 24 h. After 24 h, the medium was replaced with fresh medium containing 2 μg/ml puromycin, and the culture was continued for 48–96 h.

Flow cytometry analysis of knock-in efficiency

Knockin efficiency was evaluated 72 h post-transfection by flow cytometry. Cells were washed twice with DPBS, dissociated with EDTA-Trypsin (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and transferred to 96-well U-bottom plates. After centrifugation at 200 × g for 3 min, cell pellets were resuspended in 120 μL PBS containing 2% FBS and analyzed within 30 min on a BD flow cytometer. mKate-positive cells were quantified as a percentage of total viable cells to determine knock-in efficiency.

Genomic DNA extraction and PCR amplification

Genomic DNA was extracted 72 h post-transfection using QuickExtract DNA Extraction Solution (TIANGEN) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Target loci were amplified by PCR using primers flanking the homology arms (Table S1). PCR products were purified using the Monarch PCR & DNA Cleanup Kit (Promega) and used for Sanger sequencing or NGS library preparation.

Sanger sequencing

Purified PCR products were submitted for Sanger sequencing using target-specific primers (Table S1) at Tsingke Biotechnology. Sequencing traces were analyzed to confirm editing outcomes and integration junction sequences.

NGS library preparation and sequencing

Genomic DNA (200 ng) was used as input for NGS library preparation. Target regions were first amplified using locus-specific primers (Table S4), and large-scale insertions were amplified by primers spanning homology arms in combination with cargo-specific primers, followed by a second PCR to append Illumina adapters and unique barcode indices. The second-round PCR products were size-selected on a 1.5% agarose gel and purified with the Monarch PCR & DNA Cleanup Kit (Promega). Libraries were quantified with the Qubit dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher) and sequenced on an Illumina MiSeq platform using paired-end 300 bp reads.

NGS data analysis

Sequencing reads were demultiplexed, trimmed, and merged using standard pipelines. Editing outcomes were analyzed with CRISPResso2 (https://crispresso2.com/), aligning reads to the reference amplicons and expected knock-in sequences. The quantification window was extended ±10 bp from the predicted cut site to capture precise insertions and indel events. Substitutions were excluded to avoid sequencing artifacts. Only reads with perfect matches to the knock-in template were counted as precise insertions.

Knock-in event analysis

Knockin events mediated by microhomology were quantified at endogenous loci constitutively expressed in human or mouse cells, including HSP90AA1 and ACTB. A double-strand break (DSB) was introduced immediately upstream of the stop codon by SpCas9, and repair was directed by Pol θ (or functional variants) using a donor template encoding a P2A-mKate cassette (Table S5). Precise in-frame integration places mKate expression under the control of the endogenous promoter, enabling quantification of knock-in efficiency by fluorescence microscopy and flow cytometry. Following puromycin selection, mKate-positive cells were quantified by flow cytometry. Integration junction fidelity was assessed by PCR amplification across the homology arms followed by NGS library preparation and sequencing as described above.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism. Data are presented as mean ± SEM. Statistical comparisons between two groups were performed using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test with equal variance. For experiments involving multiple comparisons, false discovery rate (FDR) was controlled at 1% using the Benjamini–Krieger–Yekutieli procedure. All experiments were performed with a minimum of three biologically independent replicates unless otherwise stated. Statistical significance thresholds: ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.005; ns, not significant.
