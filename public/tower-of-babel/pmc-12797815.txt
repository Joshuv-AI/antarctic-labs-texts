Core competencies and qualifications of health sciences school managers: a multi-method qualitative study
Authors: Leila Doshmangir, Masoumeh Jorjani, Alireza Khoshdel, Neda Kabiri, Abbas Haghparast, Saeed Semnanian, Jamil Sadeghifar, Mohammad Mahboubi, AliAkbar Haghdoost, Mohammad Hossein Somi, Saeid Aslanabadi, Ramin Rezapour
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe selection and appointment of qualified managers in health sciences schools are critically important due to their substantial impact on the performance of faculties and medical universities. Accordingly, the selection process should be based on criteria that effectively assess candidates’ competencies and experiences in alignment with the principles of meritocracy and academic excellence. Unlike previous studies that have mainly focused on general academic leadership traits, this study introduces a comprehensive, merit-based competency framework specifically tailored to health sciences school management. This study aims to identify and define the essential competencies and qualifications required for managers of health sciences schools, including educational department heads and school deans, with an emphasis on a merit-based approach.MethodsThis study employed a multi-method qualitative design, incorporating a scoping review, document analysis, semi-structured interviews, expert panel discussions, and the Delphi technique. The scoping review was conducted to identify key competencies and indicators relevant to the selection and appointment of medical school managers. We conducted a document analysis to examine national and governmental reports related to the Iranian medical universities. Also, we used semi-structured interviews and focus group discussions to explore stakeholders’ perspectives on existing processes, selection criteria, and influencing factors. A manager selection framework was developed based on the findings. The Delphi technique was subsequently employed to validate and finalize the proposed process for appointing Educational Department Heads (EDHs) and Health Sciences School Deans (HSSDs).ResultsWe identified a total of 18 criteria for the selection of EDHs and HSSDs which were categorized into five main domains of personal and character competencies, organizational and environmental knowledge, professionalism, educational background and expertise, and organizational role and engagement. In addition, the final checklist for selecting HSDs contains 16 specific criteria. This study also proposed a structured, seven-step process for the selection of EDHs.ConclusionThe findings of this study contribute to a paradigm shift in the traditional approach to appointing managers in health sciences schools. Our results indicated that EDHs and HSSDs should have expertise as well as a balanced combination of managerial competencies, human resource development capabilities, knowledge of organizational behavior, change management skills, and performance support strategies, which can significantly enhance their effectiveness and success in their leadership roles.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08295-0.Keywords: Health facility administrators, Health professions, Leadership, Professional competence

Introduction

Medical sciences universities represent one of the most critical elements of both higher education and national health system. These institutions are responsible for educating the next generation of healthcare professionals, advancing knowledge, and delivering healthcare services [1, 2]. Within this complex environment, leadership and management at different organizational levels including departments, faculties, and schools, play a decisive role in shaping institutional effectiveness and outcomes [3, 4]. Among academic leaders, Educational Department Heads (EDHs) and Health Sciences School Deans (HSSDs) hold critical positions, as they are responsible for steering educational, research, and developmental processes at both departmental and school levels. To fulfill their responsibilities, they must possess a set of core competencies and qualifications that go beyond general management skills [5, 6].

Research has shown that strong academic leadership enhances organizational learning, staff motivation, and institutional adaptability. However, the majority of research in this area has focused on hospital managers or general university leaders, rather than leaders of health sciences schools and departments. These academic units face unique challenges, such as bringing together different disciplines, balancing academic and clinical training, and keeping up with fast changes in health systems. Because of this, understanding managerial competencies required for these positions is an important issue that isn’t covered comprehensively in the literature [1].

Despite the importance of leadership at departmental and school levels, evidences revealed that medical sciences universities in developing countries such as Iran have faced substantial challenges in developing qualified educational leaders in recent years [7]. Managing large and diverse academic communities requires strong leadership and effective governance structures. However, most of the universities face with challenges such as limited managerial competencies, unclear role expectations, difficulties in faculty recruitment and retention, and insufficient professional development opportunities for academic leaders [2]. These challenges can lead to posing pressure on faculty deans and department heads. Also, there can appear inefficiencies that may have negative effect on the quality of education, research productivity, and the country’s health system.

It is obvious that, the current process of appointing educational leaders in Iranian universities is mainly based on the academic and scientific achievements, instead of considering managerial competencies such as change leadership, educational governance, communication skills, and strategic leadership abilities [8]. Both the experience of researchers and the existing literature suggest that no study has yet been conducted to identify managerial competencies required for EDHs and HSSDs in the Iran. To address this gap, this study aims to identify, categorize, and validate the managerial competencies required for EDHs and HSSDs in Iranian medical universities. By integrating scoping review, document review, qualitative study, Delphi technique, expert panel, and brainstorming, the study aims to develop a context-specific competency framework that can inform recruitment, promotion, and professional development policies for academic leaders in health sciences education.

The structure of this paper is organized as follows: Methods section outlines the research design, data collection, and analytical procedures. In the results section we have presented the key findings, including final criteria for selection of educational department heads with prioritized and weighted scores. The next section discusses the main findings with international evidence, and then we conclude with practical recommendations and insights for future research.

Methods

This study employed a multi-method qualitative approach, integrating literature and document reviews to provide formal criteria, semi-structured interviews and focus group discussions to capture experiences and insights from the key stakeholders, and expert panel discussions and the Delphi technique to ensure expert consensus (Fig. 1). The rationale for using multiple methods was to triangulate data sources and enhance the validity and depth of the findings and ensure that the framework is both theoretically sound and practically applicable in the context of Iranian medical universities.

A multi-method qualitative study to define the essential competencies and qualifications of medical sciences school managers

Phase 1: scoping review

We used instructions from the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) [9, 10] for conducting the scoping review, and followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension (PRISMA-ScR) [11]. The inclusion criteria were defined as follows: P (Population): medical schools’ deans, chairmen, and supervisors; C (Concept): recruitment criteria and competencies for these population; and C (Context): publications related to medical schools, colleges, faculties, and institutes worldwide. We conducted a systematic search using both free-text keywords and controlled vocabularies across the following databases without any limitations in language and publication date: PubMed, ISI Web of Knowledge, Scopus, and PsycINFO (via Ebsco). A PubMed search strategy is as below: (faculty[Title/Abstract]) OR (school[Title/Abstract]) OR (college[Title/Abstract]) OR (institute[Title/Abstract]) OR (department[Title/Abstract])) AND (select*[Title/Abstract]) OR (recruitment[Title/Abstract]) OR (appointment[Title/Abstract]) (dean[Title/Abstract]) OR (chair[Title/Abstract]) OR (chairperson[Title/Abstract]) OR (chief[Title/Abstract]) OR (director[Title/Abstract]) OR (executive[Title/Abstract]) OR (head[Title/Abstract]) OR (leader*[Title/Abstract]) OR (supervisor[Title/Abstract]) AND (competenc*[Title/Abstract]) OR (skill[Title/Abstract]) OR (criteri*[Title/Abstract]) OR (qualification*[Title/Abstract]) OR (factor[Title/Abstract]) OR (indicator[Title/Abstract]) AND (medical[Title/Abstract]) OR (health[Title/Abstract]).

Also, we searched Google Scholar for further relevant literature. The first 100 results were screened. Search was conducted in January 10, 2025. We used Systematic Review-Accelerator for developing search strategies in the other databases. The search results were imported into EndNote X.20 (Clarivate Analytics, PA, USA) for study selection, which was carried out by two independent reviewers based on the preliminary inclusion and exclusion criteria. Data extraction was also performed by two independent reviewers. A standardized data extraction tool was developed aligned with the recommendations from the JBI methodology group for scoping reviews. For summarizing data and categorizing the deans’ competencies, we employed qualitative content analysis [12, 13].

Phase 2: documents review, semi-structured interviews and focus group discussions

This study employed a multi-phase qualitative approach integrating document review, semi-structured interviews, a focus group discussion (FGD), brainstorming sessions, and a Delphi technique to identify and validate key criteria for selecting EDHs and HSSDs within medical sciences universities.

Document review

A comprehensive review of official and policy documents was conducted to extract formal selection criteria and procedural guidelines. Sources included Ministry of Health and Medical Education regulations, university management bylaws, guidelines for faculty leadership appointments, performance evaluation reports, and protocols from previous appointment interviews. These documents were analyzed to identify explicit requirements and implicit norms guiding leadership selection.

Semi-structured interviews

We conducted 19 semi-structured interviews using purposive sampling of experts. The inclusion criteria for these experts were involvement in leadership appointment processes or having a minimum of 10 years of academic leadership experience. The participants included a diverse group of key stakeholders such as senior faculty members, heads of clinical departments, directors of educational groups, and deans of medical schools. In addition, experts from the Ministry of Health and Medical Education (MoHME), especially from the Deputy for Education, participated in both interviews and FGD.

Interviews continued until thematic saturation was achieved. Each interview lasted between 40 and 90 min. Interviews were conducted face-to-face or in telephone or using virtual communications. The primary interviewer, a health policy researcher with extensive qualitative experience and no direct stake in the study outcomes, facilitated all interviews using a standardized interview guide (see Supplementary File 1).

Focus group discussion

We conducted one FGD with seven participants (two former faculty deans, two former university presidents, two senior faculty members with more than 15 years of experience, and one senior expert from Ministry of Health). The FGD session lasted about 110 min, moderated by a trained facilitator, and was audio-recorded and fully transcribed with participants’ informed consent.

Data analysis and trustworthiness

Data from interviews and the FGD were analyzed using thematic analysis in MAXQDA software version 2020. Two researchers coded transcripts using deductive and inductive approaches.

To ensure credibility, we shared findings with selected participants (member checking). We ensured dependability by a comprehensive documentation of the research process. Also, we ensured confirmability with peer debriefing and reflexivity. We triangulated our data collection sources to ensure the validity of the results.

Phase 3: brainstorming and expert panel

Outputs from Phases 1 and 2 helped us to develop a comprehensive matrix of selection criteria and responsibilities. This matrix was reviewed in four structured brainstorming sessions facilitated by a moderator. According to the results of the brainstorming sessions, we developed a questionnaire and distributed to 47 experts, 32 of whom including faculty members, previous department chairs, faculty deans, heads of clinical divisions, previous vice chancellors for education or research, and several graduate students responded and returned our questionnaires. Participants scored each criterion on a 5-point Likert scale, and criteria scoring less than 2 were excluded. The final list included 18 criteria with a scale of 1 to 10.

Phase 4: Delphi technique

A two-round Delphi process was conducted to validate and prioritize the criteria developed in the previous phase. Twenty seven experts independently rated each criterion on a 1 to 10 scale. Median scores and interquartile ranges were calculated after each Delphi round. We defined reaching consensus before the start of this phase to be as equal or more than 75% agreement within an interquartile range of 2 or less.

Theme 1: human resource management and development

This theme has three sub-themes including organizational structure and functions, human resource optimization, and performance evaluation. Participants highlighted existing challenges regarding this theme such as the discrepancies between the delegated authority and assigned responsibilities, the need to separate academic and administrative duties, and the prohibition of dual managerial positions. In addition, adhering to a participatory planning in selecting university level managers and the training of young professionals for future leadership roles were identified as strategies to enhance organizational performance.

Effective performance evaluation requires the development of valid, reliable, and measurable indicators as well as predetermined program objectives. Also, participants declared that performance assessments should be based on the implementation and achievement of operational plans.

“Human resource development hinges on the establishment of transparent evaluation criteria, a balanced distribution of authority and responsibility, and the creation of a dynamic organizational structure. These elements, when coupled with talent identification and development, serve as the foundation for sustainable organizational transformation” (P 21).

One interviewee criticized the Comprehensive Bylaw for the Management of Universities and Higher Education, Research, and Technology Institutes (P 4), stating:

“According to the bylaw, educational department heads are appointed for a two-year term upon the recommendation of the faculty dean and the approval of the university president, to carry out assigned duties. However, the bylaw fails to explicitly define the criteria required for such nominations by either the dean or the president. This lack of clarity has been overlooked in many universities to date” (P 11).

Theme 2: organizational behavior development

Sub-themes in the organizational behavior development included organizational culture enhancement, leadership styles, organizational communication, and organizational motivation. An effective organizational culture was defined in terms of having accountability, support and empathy, teamwork, adherence to regulations, and customer orientation. Organizational communication, plays a significant role in the implementation of major initiatives such as four-year strategic plans. According to participants, the success of these initiatives depends on vertical coordination across managerial levels (university, faculty, and department) and effective knowledge-sharing through technology. One study participant noted:

“Strengthening organizational behavior requires a combination of trust-based culture, flexible delegation of authority, staff participation, and transparent communication to align organizational interests” (P 21).

Theme 3: change management

Comparative management, transformational leadership, and commitment-based leadership were three sub-themes emerged in change management theme. Many experts emphasized that effective change leadership requires a context-specific approach based on available resources, organizational capacities, and environmental conditions. Due to the transformational leadership in medical universities, there is a need for clear visions, missions, and specific objectives based on the roles and differences of each department and faculty. The success of such leadership depends on activities being in line with the university’s roadmap as well as considering legal and financial capacities. Participants declared that for achieving these goals there is a need for autonomous departments as “mini-faculties”, which fosters the internal innovation. Programs should be supported by legal and financial mechanisms, and must align with higher-level strategic documents (such as the university’s four-year development plan). In this regard, expert panels were proposed to facilitate the distribution of responsibilities and to reinforce practical, mission-oriented approaches.

“Sustainable change in the educational system can only be achieved through a combination of structural flexibility, alignment with resource realities, and vertical coordination across managerial levels. A competent leader must possess the capacity to drive such transformation” (P 5).

Another participant commented:

“We do not fully utilize the full potential and responsibilities assigned to department chairs as outlined in the management bylaws for educational institutions. We must assess the existing infrastructure and actual needs to determine what organizational structure each department should have” (P 14).

Systemic thinking, alignment in planning, integration of human resource strategies with the organization’s overall strategy, and root cause analysis were also identified as critical factors in fostering effective change leadership.

Theme 4: performance monitoring and evaluation

Performance monitoring and evaluating for educational, administrative, and operational staff is an important component of managerial accountability. Personnel empowerment and the promotion of organizational learning were highlighted as essential goals, supported through skill development, and provision of educational resources. Additionally, non-instructional supports such as financial and physical resources, clarification of roles and responsibilities, and staff guidance were identified as facilitators of effective performance.

One interviewee noted:

“Behavioral and interpersonal skills, personality traits and attitudes, and domain-specific knowledge and expertise are among the core competencies required of faculty managers. While knowledge and technical skills can be acquired over time, changing attitudes and personality traits is more difficult and requires carefully planned interventions. These dimensions must be evaluated during the managerial selection process, and behavioral and cognitive skills, in particular, can often be enhanced through targeted training” (P 19).

Discussion

In this study we aimed to develop and validate a framework for selecting EDHs and HSSDs in Iranian medical universities. A multi-method qualitative approach of scoping review, document review, semi-structured interviews, FGD, brainstorming, expert panels, and the Delphi technique was used to identify key competencies, examine current appointment practices, and build consensus on proposed criteria. As a result, 16 final selection criteria were prioritized and grouped into five domains: personal and character competencies, organizational and environmental knowledge, professionalism, educational background and expertise, and organizational role and engagement. The outputs include a validated checklist and a seven-step selection process for EDHs, and offer a structured and competency-based model aligned with institutional priorities. This framework addresses the need for transparency and fairness in academic leadership appointments, which has long been a policy concern in Iranian higher education. Faculty deans, as integral members of the university system, play a critical role in institutional advancement [23].

Previous studies emphasize the importance of enhancing efficiency, strengthening leadership, reducing financial costs, and improving teaching and managerial skills in academic administrators [16, 22, 24]. Our findings are consistent with these studies in emphasizing leadership and managerial competences as major element for effective medical school deanship. However, this study extends the literature by embedding these general qualities within a structured and transparent selection framework that has measurable criteria rather than relying on informal or seniority-based criteria.

The selection of EDHs and medical faculty deans has often drawn the attention of policymakers because of its implications for academic governance and broader social systems [25, 26]. Having a transparent set of appointment criteria is essential for ensuring credibility and reducing political influence. In line with previous studies which emphasize the importance of structured human resource management in academic leadership positions, our findings reinforces human resource management as a core competency, including clear job descriptions, succession planning, and accountability mechanisms. However, this study differs the literature by arguing that such competencies must be demonstrated prior to appointment, rather than assuming they can develop during tenure. This difference in findings indicate development of an evidence-based leadership selection, which can fill the gap of the current selection process and make it with considering prior competency assessment.

In addition, while research by Buckley et al. [15] and Palmer et al. [22]., has viewed organizational culture as fundamental to managerial success, our findings integrate these behavioral competencies as core evaluative criteria within the selection framework. By considering competencies such as trust-based culture, participatory decision-making, and effective communication, as non-negotiable prerequisites for leadership selection, the current study emphasize on these as formal requirements for strengthening the selection process.

Davenport et al. [27] argue that without targeted leadership training, assigning faculty to managerial positions is counterproductive. Our findings support this view and operationalize it through a policy recommendation: mandatory leadership development programs for appointed heads within six months of selection, covering domains such as systems thinking, negotiation, and conflict management. This study adds to what others have found by linking fair, competency-based selection to university policies and giving a practical guide for training academic leaders.

Change management emerged as another critical leadership competency in our study. Its importance has been noted in prior studies, such as Kyamanywa [20] but these studies often describe it generally rather than linking it to formal selection processes. Our research advances the discourse by integrating change management capabilities into formal selection criteria for academic leaders. In a rapidly evolving academic environment shaped by technological advances, policy shifts, and resource constraints, leaders must possess adaptive and strategic thinking skills. Unlike studies situated in low-resource contexts [28], which focus on coping with the shortage of resources, this study addresses change management within highly competitive systems where innovation and rapid adaptation are essential. By considering change management as a major and assessable requirement for an academic leader, this study provides new insights for selection process of leaders who can effectively manage complex and dynamic academic setting.

The inclusion of professionalism and ethics as top-weighted criteria aligns with Jadhav’s findings [18] and Mohammadi et al. [28], reinforcing the global trend toward ethical governance. Our contribution lies in translating these values into actionable selection standards such as documented absence of conflicts of interest and adherence to ethical norms within a formalized checklist. Previous research has described a wide range of leadership competencies, including cultural, political, educational, and technological dimensions [29–31]. However, the present study goes further by synthesizing these elements into a consensus-driven, context-sensitive model and validating it through a rigorous Delphi process. The outcome is not merely descriptive but provides a standardized, scalable tool for academic leadership selection.

The findings suggest that replacing traditional, informal selection processes with structured, transparent models can increase candidate motivation, improve organizational performance, and enhance accountability. Compared to earlier studies [8, 20, 24], this research offers greater depth by combining international evidence with local insights and by linking identified competencies to a formalized selection process and evaluation mechanism.

Strengths and limitations of the study

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first comprehensive and multi-method study in Iran to develop and validate a framework for selecting EDHs and HSSDs. Other academic institutions can use this framework for their leadership selection. The study has some limitations. First, it was conducted in Iranian medical universities, which may limit generalizability to international higher education systems. While expert consensus strengthened the internal validity of our framework, some criteria reflect priorities which are specific for the Iranian context. Finally, the framework’s effectiveness in improving leadership performance should be examined in practice, so there is a need for future implementation studies [32].

Conclusion

Theoretical implications

The findings of this study underscore the necessity of moving beyond traditional, informal approaches to appointing medical school managers by adopting a structured, evidence-informed framework. This research contributes theoretically by highlighting that effective leadership in health sciences education cannot rely solely on academic or scientific credentials. Instead, it must incorporate a balanced set of managerial and behavioral competencies, including leadership, human resource development, organizational behavior, change management, and professionalism.

By identifying 16 key criteria across five core domains, the study enriches the theoretical understanding of academic leadership and bridges the gap between formal qualifications and behavioral competencies, an area previously underexplored in the literature. This integration offers a more comprehensive conceptualization of leadership effectiveness within medical universities.

Managerial or policy implications

From a managerial and policy perspective, the model presented here offers a pragmatic and unambiguous framework for selecting EDHs and HSSDs. The integration of such competencies into the hiring process has the potential to enhance the alignment between managerial positions and institutional goals, encourage ethical and responsible leadership, and increase the overall performance of academic health institutions. The seven-step selection process and empirically tested checklist developed through this research can serve as a guiding tool for university leaders and policymakers, enhancing merit-based appointment as well as consistent, competency-based decision-making in leadership recruitment and development.

Ideas for future research

A further investigation is necessary to determine the functionality of the proposed competency-based selection framework under various institutional and policy settings, and its impact on the governance quality, performance, and sustainability of leadership in academic health systems. In contemplating future prospects, a comprehensive evaluation of the implementation’s outcomes will elucidate the correlation between the structured undertaking and enhanced transparency, organizational efficacy, and sustained leadership stability. The execution of such studies would provide essential evidence regarding the framework and clarify the conceptual and practical dimensions of leadership and its evolution in health professional education on a global scale.
