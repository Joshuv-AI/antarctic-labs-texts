Polyethylene microplastics exposure reduces flesh quality and disturbs muscle fiber characteristics in freshwater fish (Paramisgurnus dabryanus): associations with gut microbiota alterations
Authors: Weiran Huo, Mengzhen Wang, Yunfan Han, Kaiqi Weng, Xiaohua Xia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Microplastics (MPs) pose a significant risk to fish health, yet their effects on meat quality in fish remain poorly understood. To investigate the effects of MPs on meat quality, large-scale loach (Paramisgurnus dabryanus) were exposed to polyethylene MPs (PE-MPs) at 0, 2.5, 5.0, and 10 mg/L for 30 days. The results showed that PE-MPs exposure significantly reduced condition factor (CF), weight gain rate (WGR), specific growth rate (SGR), hepatosomatic index (HSI), viscerosomatic index (VSI). Flesh quality measurements showed that PE-MP exposure impaired flesh quality, as evidenced by decreased intramuscular fat, essential amino acids content, and polyunsaturated fatty acids levels. Fluorescence spectroscopy combined with particle identification indicated the presence of PE-MPs in muscle, with particle counts increasing over exposure time and concentration. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and Real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) revealed that PE-MPs exposure increased muscle fiber cross-sectional area (CSA), diameter, and spacing, while decreasing type I fiber density. 16S rRNA sequencing analysis showed that PE-MPs ingestion was associated with gut microbiota alterations, which were strongly correlated with muscle fiber characteristics by Spearman analysis. Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) trial revealed that recipients receiving microbiota from PE-MP-exposed donors partially exhibited similar alterations in skeletal muscle fiber characteristics, growth performance, and flesh quality. Collectively, our findings suggest that PE-MPs exposure may induce gut microbiota alterations, and that these shifts in the gut microbiota were associated with reduced flesh quality and altered muscle fiber characteristics in large-scale loach. These findings provide new insights into the detrimental effects of PE-MPs on flesh quality.

Introduction

Plastics are extensively utilized in medicine, agriculture, industry, and daily life due to their high yield, versatility, and low cost. However, the relentless growth in global plastic production has led to a corresponding surge in plastic waste (Gallagher et al., 2016; Hou et al., 2021). Through physical, chemical, and biological degradation in the natural environment, plastic waste is broken down into increasingly smaller particles (Moore, 2008). Microplastics (MPs), defined as plastic particles with diameters smaller than 5 mm, are widely distributed in freshwater, marine, and terrestrial ecosystems (He et al., 2023; Senathirajah et al., 2021). Because of their small size, MPs could be mistakenly ingested by many organisms, including marine zooplankton (Brachionus koreanus and Diaphanosoma celebensis; Park et al., 2024), zebra mussel (Dreissena polymorpha; Magni et al., 2018), whiteleg shrimp (Litopenaeus vannamei; Chae et al., 2019), African catfish (Clarias gariepinus; Sayed et al., 2023), Nile Tilapia (Oreochromis niloticus; Hamed et al., 2021), Japanese anchovy (Engraulis japonicas; Tanaka and Takada, 2016), and human (Homo sapiens; Zhu et al., 2023). After ingestion, larger MPs tend to accumulate in the digestive tract and be excreted more quickly, whereas smaller ones can be transported through the digestive tract to the circulatory system and more likely to remain in the fish (Kaposi et al., 2014). It has been reported that most MPs tend to be accumulated in and act on the intestinal tract, but a small proportion of MPs can be transferred to peripheral tissues and the circulatory system, crossing biological membranes and resulting in a significant ecological and societal issue worldwide (Ding et al., 2018; Chen et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2021). For instance, aquatic organisms like fish, ingested MPs can accumulate in multiple internal organs, including the gut, gills, liver, and brain, subsequently inducing a spectrum of adverse effects such as gut microbiota dysbiosis, hypoactivity, molecular damage, growth inhibition, reproductive toxicity, neurotoxicity, and non-specific immunity response (Chen et al., 2017; Hamed et al., 2020; Huang et al., 2020; Wen et al., 2018; Xia et al., 2023).

Globally, fish is gaining momentum as a result of its high protein level and low fat level. Consequently, there has been a growing emphasis on fish flesh quality and safety by consumers. Prior studies have indicated that MPs exposure adversely affects meat quality traits in chickens (Gallus gallus) and pigs (Sus scrofa domestica) (Chen et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2024). However, it remains unclear whether MPs can produce adverse effects on flesh quality, although it is indisputable that MPs have been accumulated in the fish body. Meat quality is governed by a multitude of factors, with muscle fiber properties playing a central role (Listrat et al., 2016). Consequently, any factor that alters muscle fiber properties may impact the final quality of meat. Research on pigs has demonstrated that MPs exposure reduces the density of type I muscle fibers and impairs angiogenesis within muscle tissue, thus potentially compromising meat quality development (Yang et al., 2024). Consistently, the study performed by Wang et al., observed that MPs exposure decreased the average cross-sectional area (CSA) and diameter of muscle fibers in mice (Mus musculus), which are key determinants of meat yield and quality (Wang et al., 2021). Given the established link between physical and biochemical characteristics of muscle fibers and meat quality, these terrestrial-animal studies provide a conceptual basis that MPs pollution may impair meat quality by disrupting muscle fiber properties. However, the specific impact of MPs on the fish flesh quality through muscle fiber characteristics has never been studied, and the underlying mechanisms remain to be determined.

Acting as a second genome and a mediator of host-organ communication, the gut microbiota is implicated in the development of several chronic diseases, such as diabetes, obesity, autism, as well as schizophrenia (Huang et al., 2025a, Huang et al., 2025b). Relevant studies have reported that ingestion of MPs could reshape the community structure of fish gut microbiota, and that gut microbiota homeostasis is closely linked to skeletal muscle metabolism and fiber phenotype (Li et al., 2026; Huang et al., 2025a, Huang et al., 2025b). The hypothesis of the ‘gut–muscle axis’ has therefore been proposed by several authors (Grosicki et al., 2018; Liu et al., 2026; Zhou et al., 2025). Indeed, after fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) from pigs, skeletal muscle characteristics could be transferred from pig donors to germ-free (GF) mice recipients (Yan et al., 2016). Furthermore, the composition of muscle fiber types was regulated by the gut microbiota (Grosicki et al., 2018). These evidences support the contention that the gut microbiota can influence muscle fiber characteristics. Studies conducted under both laboratory and natural conditions have demonstrated that the ingestion of MPs adversely alters the gut microbiota of aquatic animals (Li et al., 2022). Based on these findings, we hypothesize that MPs exposure perturbs the gut microbiota, and that these microbial changes may be associated with alterations in muscle fiber characteristics and impaired flesh quality in freshwater fish.

Owing to their small particle size, persistence, and sedimentation, MPs frequently accumulate near the bottom of ponds (Yin et al., 2018). Moreover, omnivorous fish ingest more MPs than herbivorous or carnivorous fish, yet exhibit a weaker capacity to clear them (Zhang et al., 2021). Therefore, the present study aimed to investigate the effects of treatment with concentrations (0, 2.5, 5, 10 mg/L) of polyethylene microplastics (PE-MPs) on meat quality, growth performance, muscle fiber characteristics, and gut microbial community structure in large-scale loach (Paramisgurnus dabryanus), a typical benthic and omnivorous freshwater fish for 30 days. Specifically, this study investigates (i) whether PE-MP exposure impairs fish flesh quality and growth performance; ii) whether muscle fiber characteristics are involved in this process; and (iii) whether gut microbiota alterations are associated with, and may contribute to, this process. For the first time, our findings not only revealed a deeper impact of PE-MP on flesh quality but also provide critical insights for uncovering the underlying mechanisms from the perspective of the gut–muscle axis.

Materials and methods

PE-MPs

Pristine PE-MPs (8.0-12.0 μm diameter, density of 0.94 g/mL) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (USA). Nile red fluorescent dye was used to label the PE-MPs according to a prior study (Erni-Cassola et al., 2017). The properties of PE-MPs were characterized by scanning electric microscope (SEM, Hitachi TM3030 Plus, Japan) and fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR, Bruker Tensor II, Germany), as reported in our previous study (Xia et al., 2024).

Experimental fish and treatment

Healthy adult large-scale loaches (no signs of disease or injury) were purchased from the Freshwater Aquaculture Institute of Henan Province, China, with a mean body-weight of 3.5 ± 0.5 g and body-length of 7.5 ± 0.5 cm, respectively. Before the experiment, these large-scale loaches were temporarily raised in 250 L naturally dechlorinated tap water for 7 days. Large-scale loaches were kept in suitable conditions with the pH 7.2-7.5, 6.5 ± 0.5 mg/L dissolved oxygen and temperature 26 ± 1°C. Additionally, the large-scale loaches were fed with commercially formulated antibiotic-free diet at a rate of 2% of fish body weight each day. All animal procedures were reviewed and approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Henan Normal University.

The experimental design is illustrated in Fig. 1A. Following a 7-day acclimation period, 1800 large-scale loaches were randomly divided into four groups: a Control (Ctrl) group and three PE-MPs treatment groups designated as low-concentration microplastics (L-MP), medium-concentration microplastics (M-MP), and high-concentration microplastics (H-MP), respectively. The PE-MPs concentration in the current study was set as 2.5, 5.0, and 10.0 mg/L, equivalent to 3.4 × 107, 6.82 × 107, and 1.36 × 108 particles/m3, respectively. These particle-number equivalents overlap with the upper range of reported microplastic concentrations in aquatic settings (Koelmans et al., 2019), and the exposure design therefore represents a controlled high-end toxicological scenario rather than average environmental exposure levels. The Ctrl group without PE-MPs treatment, L-MP group at the concentration of 2.5 mg/L, M-MP group at the concentration of 5.0 mg/L, H-MP group at the concentration of 10.0 mg/L. In each group, a total of 450 large-scale loaches were equally reared in three replicate glass tanks (n = 150 per tank). During the experiment, the water in each tank was changed every 48 h, meanwhile, non-fluorescent PE-MPs and the corresponding stained PE-MPs were supplemented to make sure their concentrations unchanged. Prior to dissection, fish were rinsed twice (10 min each) with autoclaved tank water to remove any surface-adhered PE-MPs.Fig. 1Diagram of experimental design.

Diagram of experimental design.

Fecal microbiota transplantation

Prior to FMT, the large-scale loaches were allocated as either donors (Control donors, Ctrl-D and H-MP donors, HMP-D) or recipients (Control recipients, Ctrl-R and H-MP recipients, HMP-R). The recipients were kept free of PE-MPs during the FMT phase. The transplantation procedure is depicted in Fig. 1B. FMT was performed as previously described (Pham et al., 2008; Smith et al., 2017), with minor modifications. Briefly, recipient fish were treated 7 days with antibiotic cocktails containing Vancomycin (0.01 g/L), Metronidazole (0.5 g/L), Neomycin (0.5 g/L) and Ampicillin (0.5 g/L) before FMT. At the end of antibiotics intervention, the clearance efficiency of gut microbiota in recipients was presented in Fig. S1. Before every transplantation event, donors fish were rinsed twice (10 min each) with autoclaved tank water. Then, the whole intestines were excised aseptically and transferred to 10 cm petri dishes containing sterile PBS on ice. The isolated intestines were opened longitudinally, the intestinal contents scraped out and then cut into 0.5 cm pieces to enhance the release of bacteria. The collected intestinal contents were washed once with cold PBS, and then passed through a 5-μm filter. The resulting filtrate was introduced into the recipient fish tanks at a standardized dose equivalent to one donor intestine per two recipient fish. Meanwhile, FMT trial was conducted synchronously every 3 days in the whole experiment. Fecal samples were collected from the recipient fish at the end of the FMT experimental period. The samples were immediately cryopreserved for subsequent analysis.

Growth parameters measurements

The body weight and total length of each large-scale loach were measured with the average value at the beginning of experiment and after 30 days of PE-MPs exposure. After exposure for 30 days, 10 surviving large-scale loaches were randomly sampled from per replicate tank, and then anesthetized in an ice bath. The liver and gonads were excised and weighed. The hepatosomatic (HSI) and viscerosomatic index (VSI) were then calculated from these weights. The corresponding parameters including condition factor (CF), specific growth rate (SGR), weight gain rate (WGR), HSI and VSI were calculated according to the previous literature (Xu et al., 2023).

Flesh quality traits measurements

After 30 days of PE-MPs exposure, all large-scale loaches were sacrificed. After collection, muscle samples were placed on ice, packaged with plastic wrap, and transported to the laboratory at 4°C. Following a 24-h chill, samples were evaluated for both physical properties and proximate composition. The proximate composition of flesh was measured using a FoodScan Meat Analyzer (FOSS 78,800, Dedicated Analytical Solutions, Hillerod, Denmark). The surface color of muscle was determined using a Minolta colorimeter (Minolta CR400, Konica Minolta, Tokyo, Japan), recording the redness (a*), yellowness (b*), and brightness (L*) values according to the manufacturer's instructions. The pH of muscle was assessed with a pH meter (DELTA 320, Mettler Toledo, Columbus, OH, USA) by inserting the electrode into the muscle. To determine pressure loss, a dilatometer (C-LM3B, Tenovo, Beijing, China) was used. Fresh muscle samples were weighed (about 1 g), pressurized and reweighed after 5 min, with the pressure loss expressed as a percentage of the initial weight. For determination of the shear force, the Digital Meat Tenderness Instrument (C-LM3B, Tenovo, Beijing, China) was used (Baublits et al., 2005). The analysis of amino acid and fatty acid profiles was outsourced to Qingdao Kechuang Quality Testing Co., Ltd. (Qingdao, China) and performed using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) and gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS), respectively.

Analysis of PE-MPs accumulation

At the 0th, 5th 10th, 20th and 30th day of exposure, 10 surviving large-scale loaches were randomly sampled from per replicate tank for study. To detect PE-MPs, muscle samples were placed on glycerol-coated slides and examined under a fluorescence microscope (ZEISS, Axio Imager.D2, Germany). Images were captured at a magnification of 50× with a green filter (excitation wavelength: 510–560 nm). The concentration of PE-MPs in muscle samples was measured using a previous method (Li et al., 2015). To avoid contamination, all used in the procedure were pre-filtered through 5 μm filter paper prior to use. All containers and beakers were rinsed three times with filtered water. All experimental procedures were finished as soon as possible. One blank extraction group without tissue was performed simultaneously to correct for the potential procedural contamination. The dissected muscle samples were weighed and digested in H2O2 (30%, v/v, 2 mL) at 65°C for 12 h and then at room temperature for 24–48 h depending on the digestion effect of the soft tissue. Then, approximately 5 mL of filtered NaCl solution was added to separate the PE-MPs from dissolved liquid of the soft tissue. The suspension was filtered through a 5 μm pore size, 47 mm diameter cellulose ester filter membrane under the separate vacuum. Next, deionized water was used to rinse the residual particles and the MPs were resuspended in beakers, which were heated on hot plate at 70°C to complete water evaporation. Subsequently, Nile red fluorescent dye was added to the beaker, where it remained for 30 min at room temperature. Then, the beaker volume was added with purified water until it reached 25 mL and new vacuum filtration was performed. The filters were observed under a fluorescent microscope for photographic documentation and PE-MPs quantified using ImageJ software. The further identification was conducted out with Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR).

Histological analysis

After 30 days of exposure, three muscle samples from each group were separated and collected. For histological examination, the samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin wax, sectioned at 5 μm thickness, stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), and imaged under a light microscope (ZEISS, Germany). To assess muscle fiber characteristics, nine random fields containing a total of about 100 muscle fibers for measurement.

Real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) analysis

Total RNA was isolated from muscle tissues with TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen Corp, Carlsbad, CA). First-strand cDNA was synthesized using PrimeScript RT Master Mix kit (Takara Biotechnology Co. Ltd., China). The primer sequences were listed in Table S1 qRT-PCR was performed on a LightCycler 480 II system (Roche, Switzerland) using SYBR Premix ExTaq Kits (Takara Biotechnology Co. Ltd., China). Gene expression was quantified using the 2−ΔΔCT method, with β-actin as the reference gene.

16S rRNA sequencing and analysis

For microbial community analysis, intestinal contents were aseptically collected from 30 large-scale loaches per group (control and 10 mg/L PE-MPs), with samples pooled to form 3 biological replicates per group for subsequent 16S rRNA gene sequencing. Intestinal community genomic DNA was isolated from each replicate using the QIAamp DNA Stool Mini Kit (Qiagen) following the manufacturer's instructions. An aliquot of each extract was used for NanoDrop 2000 UV–vis spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, Wilmington, USA) to determine DNA quality and concentration. The V3-V4 hypervariable regions of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene were amplified using universal primers 338F/806R. The amplicons were purified, quantified, normalized, and pooled to construct the sequencing library. The library was sequenced on an Illumina HisSeq platform at Mayobio Biopharmaceutical Technology Company (Shanghai, China). The bioinformatics analysis of the sequences was performed following the previously described method (Huang et al., 2020).

Statistical analysis

Data were expressed as tank means ± standard deviation (SD). Differences among groups were assessed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) using SPSS 22.0 software (SPSS, Chicago, IL), followed by Tukey's test for post hoc comparisons when the ANOVA was significant (P < 0.05). Spearman correlation analysis was used to evaluate the relationships between muscle fiber characteristics and meat quality.

To understand if PE-MPs exposure leads to their accumulation in muscle, we exposed large-scale loaches with fluorescence PE-MPs of different concentrations. To identify the effects of PE-MPs accumulation on muscle fiber characteristics, we histologically studied the muscle at 30 days exposure. Quantitative analysis of H&E-stained sections revealed that PE-MPs exposure obviously increased average cross-sectional area (CSA), diameter, and spacing of the muscle fibers (Fig. 2C). The smallest muscle fiber CSA, diameter and spacing were observed in control group of large-scale loaches. Conversely, fiber density exhibited an opposite trend from the fiber diameter, with the highest density observed in the control group. In addition, compared to the control group, the mRNA levels of Myhc4 (type IIb, fast-twitch) and Myhc2 (type I, slow-twitch) in muscle were significantly reduced in the PE-MPs exposure group (Fig. 2D).

To investigate whether PE-MPs exposure alters muscle fiber characteristics and whether these changes are associated with impaired flesh quality and growth performance in large-scale loach, correlation coefficients (r) were evaluated (Fig. 2E). There were significant relationships between muscle fiber characteristics and VSI (P < 0.001), HSI (P < 0.001), WGR (P < 0.01) and SGR (P < 0.001) in large-scale loach. Consistent with these observations, significant correlations (P < 0.01) of flesh quality traits with muscle fiber characteristics were observed for pH, meat color, shear force value, intramuscular fat content and protein content.

Exposure to PE-MPs was associated with gut microbial perturbation in large-scale loach

To investigate PE-MPs-induced changes in the gut microbiota, we conducted 16S rRNA sequencing to profile the microbial communities in the intestines of control and 10.0 mg/L PE-MPs-treated large-scale loaches. Following quality filtering, a total of 520092 valid readings were obtained from 6 samples. The reliability of the data was supported by converging rank-abundance and rarefaction curves, indicating robust sampling for subsequent analysis (Fig. S2). Exposure to 10 mg/L PE-MPs significantly reduced the number of OTUs in the gut microbiota compared with the Ctrl-D (Fig. 3A). The HMP-D group exhibited a significant reduction in gut microbiota α-diversity, as indicated by lower Chao1 (richness) and Shannon (diversity) indices relative to the control (Fig. 3B and C). The principal co-ordinates analysis (PCoA), principal components analysis (PCA) and permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA), the analysis of β-diversity, showed a totally separation of gut microbial structure between the Ctrl-D and HMP-D groups (Fig. 3D, E, F). Further analysis of the microbial community structure suggested an altered gut microbial composition at different levels. Although Proteobacteria, Bacteroidota, and Firmicutes dominated the microbiota in both groups, PE-MPs exposure significantly altered the phylum-level profile. To test the hypothesis that the perturbed gut microbiota is associated with changes in muscle fiber properties, we assessed Spearman correlations between differentially abundant genera and muscle fiber characteristics (Fig. 4F). Discussion

Plastics, a broad category of synthetic or semi-synthetic polymers, have become indispensable to modern life. Their global production has grown exponentially, resulting in massive waste accumulation. Studies estimate that if current trends continue, around 12,000 tons of plastic litter will be discarded in landfills or natural ecosystems by 2050 (Geyer et al., 2017). The mass production and improper disposal of plastics pose a dual threat: not only do they lead to persistent environmental accumulation, but the subsequent fragmentation of this waste into MPs through light, heat, mechanical collision and other factors introduces widespread ecological risks (Li et al., 2023). Polypropylene (PP) and PE are the predominant polymers identified in freshwater MPs, a prevalence likely attributable to their high global production volumes and widespread use in consumer products (Li et al., 2020). The various confirmed and potential negative impacts of PE-MPs ingestion have been well documented in the literature, attracting global public concern (Wen et al., 2022). However, limited information is available on the effects of PE-MPs on fish flesh quality, despite fish representing a major animal protein source for 3.2 billion people globally. Hence, investigating the effects of PE-MPs on fish growth and flesh quality is warranted. In the present study, a typical benthic and omnivorous fish (large-scale loach), were exposed to PE-MPs at concentrations of 0, 2.5, 5.0, and 10 mg/L for 30 days. The results showed that PE-MPs exposure under the present experimental conditions may have contributed to a significant decrease in growth parameters. One possible explanation is that PE-MPs become enriched in the intestinal tract of loach, thereby reducing food intake efficiency and disrupted nutrient absorption (Wang et al., 2022; Xia et al., 2024). However, it should be acknowledged that direct measurements of zeta potential, suspension stability over time, and dye leaching were not available in the original experimental design, which limits the characterization of the actual exposure conditions. Future studies incorporating these physicochemical characterizations will be necessary to better understand the behavior and bioavailability of PE-MPs during exposure.

Evidence has shown that MPs adversely affect meat quality in terrestrial livestock such as chickens and pigs (Chen et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2024). However, their impact on the flesh quality of aquatic species, particularly fish, remains less understood. Extending the investigation to Paramisgurnus dabryanus, we found that 10.0 mg/L PE-MPs exposure significantly reduced flesh quality, notably intramuscular fat—a result consistent with the lipid-reducing effect of nanoplastics (PS-NPs) reported in another fish species, Larimichthys crocea (Lai et al., 2021). Given that amino acids are the building blocks of proteins and determine meat protein quality (Pereira and Vicente, 2013), the findings of this study are significant. Exposure to 10.0 mg/L PE-MPs significantly reduced the levels of essential amino acids (e.g., Leucine, Alanine, Lysine) and flavor-related amino acids (e.g., Asparagine, Glutamic acid, Glycine, Alanine) in muscle. Those findings indicate that 10.0 mg/L PE-MPs exposure could decrease the protein content and flavor of meat. Given the vital role of fatty acid composition in meat flavor development (Pereira and Vicente, 2013), we analyzed its response to PE-MPs. Our data indicate that the muscle fatty acid profile was significantly modified, highlighting a potential impact on flavor quality. After fish fed with 10.0 mg/L PE-MPs, the polyunsaturated fatty acids content (C18:2n6c, C18:3n3, C20:3n6, C20:2, C20:4n6, C20:5n3 and C22:6n3) significantly decreased. Corroborating this evidence, exposure to PS-NPs has similarly been shown to deplete amino acids and fatty acids in another aquatic species, the whiteleg shrimp Litopenaeus vannamei (Chae et al., 2019). In summary, PE-MPs exposure adversely affects key flesh quality components: it depletes intramuscular fat and essential amino acids, and shifts the polyunsaturated fatty acid profile, ultimately impairing the nutritional and flavor attributes of fish meat, with more PE particles potentially exerting a more significant impact. Despite relevant studies have reported that MPs can reshape the community structure of fish gut microbiota and thus alter host metabolism, especially amino acid metabolism and lipid metabolism (Huang et al., 2025a, Huang et al., 2025b; Lu et al., 2022), the gut microbiome-mediated effects still require further exploration.

Given that flesh texture depends on muscle fiber characteristics (Listrat et al., 2016), we therefore aimed to elucidate the mechanisms underlying PE-MPs-induced flesh quality deterioration by analyzing the physical and biochemical properties of fish muscle. In our study, we found that PE-MPs exposure obviously increased the average CSA, diameter and spacing of the muscle fibers. Conversely, fiber density decreased, with the highest density observed in the control group. It is generally accepted that muscles composed of larger fibers (with greater CSA) tend to have poorer meat quality than those with smaller fibers (Gentry et al., 2002). One proposed mechanism is that fiber size influences muscle growth potential and the size of the fiber bundle, which can translate into a coarser texture in the meat (Kokoszyński et al., 2019). In addition, muscle fiber type composition has been shown to influence technological meat quality traits, such as muscle pH (Zheng et al., 2018). Our research results show that PE-MPs exposure led to decrease the mRNA level of Myhc2 in muscle, which are crucial for the development of type I muscle fibers. This finding aligns with earlier research showing that PS-MPs exposure inhibited type I muscle fiber content (Yang et al., 2024). Given that a higher proportion of type I (slow-twitch) muscle fibers is associated with better meat quality (Nam et al., 2009), the reduction in type I fibers induced by PE-MPs exposure supports the conclusion that such exposure impairs flesh quality, at least in part, through this structural alteration. This relationship was investigated in the present study, as shown in Fig. 2E, which reveals multiple significant correlations between muscle fiber characteristics and flesh quality traits. These correlations support a strong link between fiber properties and flesh quality, suggesting that the PE-MPs-induced deterioration in flesh quality may be mediated by alterations in muscle fiber characteristics. Regarding how MP exposure affects muscle fibers, one possible mechanism involves the induction of oxidative stress during skeletal muscle regeneration, which may disrupt the balance of myoblast proliferation and differentiation, ultimately impairing muscle fiber formation and maturation (Wang et al., 2021).

The gut microbiota performs vital functions for the host. Beyond local effects, its interactions with other organs (e.g., liver, lung, brain) are an active research frontier. Consequently, a gut–muscle axis has been proposed, whereby skeletal muscle physiology and pathology are modulated by the diversity and functional state of the gut microbiota (Grosicki et al., 2018; Lustgarten, 2019; Xie and Huang, 2024). Indeed, accumulating evidence indicates that the composition and diversity of the gut microbiota can directly and indirectly influence muscle metabolism and function (Giron et al., 2022). Thus, we speculated whether PE-MP-associated flesh quality impairment may be linked to gut–muscle axis-related changes. For microbiota sequencing, we focused on the high-concentration group because it showed the most pronounced phenotype and was therefore selected to explore the potential microbiota-related mechanism. Supporting this hypothesis, we found that the H-MP group exhibited a markedly altered gut microbiota composition relative to the control. Notably, the relative abundance of Cetobacterium was significantly reduced in the H-MP group. This genus is known for its role in synthesizing vitamins and amino acids, which are crucial for host health and performance (Zhang et al., 2022). Furthermore, Spearman correlation analysis revealed strong associations between these microbial shifts and altered skeletal muscle characteristics, including a negative correlation between Prevotella and abundance and Myhc2 expression (Fig. 4F). Prevotella has been recently reported relative to body weight loss in overweight adolescents (Santacruz et al., 2009), which was illustrated to be associated with mRNA level of Myhc2. However, it should also be recognized that these correlations provide hypotheses for future mechanistic studies rather than direct mechanistic evidence. Regarding how MP-induced gut microbiota alterations affect skeletal muscle growth, emerging but limited evidence suggests that the microplastics impairs muscle growth via gut microbiota dysbiosis and hepatic oxidative stress along the gut-liver-muscle axis in Qihe gibel carp (Carassius gibelio var. Qihe, li et al.,. 2026). Future studies should include microbial metabolomics, targeted metabolite rescue, gnotobiotic models, and functional validation of key bacteria to provide direct mechanistic evidence in large-scale loach.

To further examine whether gut microbiota contributed to PE-MP-associated phenotypes, we conducted the FMT trial, a powerful method for linking microbial changes to host phenotypes (Lythgoe et al., 2022). Through FMT, we demonstrated that transferring the gut microbiota from PE-MPs-exposed donors to recipients partially reproduced the donors' skeletal muscle characteristics in the recipients. Extending previous observations that obesity-induced gut microbiota dysbiosis can impact muscle in mammalian models (Yan et al., 2016), our study provides novel and complementary evidence for a gut-muscle axis, demonstrating its disruption by an environmental pollutant (PE-MPs) in fish. Furthermore, transfer of gut microbiota from PE-MP-exposed donors partially reproduced several alterations in growth performance and flesh quality in recipients. Taken together, these data indicates that PE-MPs exposure may induce gut microbiota alterations, and that these shifts in the gut microbiota were associated with reduced flesh quality and altered muscle fiber characteristics in large-scale loach. However, the contribution of direct toxic effects of PE-MPs on muscle tissue and other indirect pathways remains to be determined. Future studies should investigate MPs accumulation, regulation, and function in fish skeletal muscle to elucidate the mechanisms by which MPs affect muscle development and growth.

Conclusion

In summary, our study demonstrates that PE-MPs exposure may alter the gut microbial composition, and that these shifts in the gut microbiota were associated with reduced flesh quality and altered muscle fiber characteristics in large-scale loaches. Theoretically, these findings enhance our understanding of how PE-MPs compromise fish flesh quality and provide insights into potential mechanisms involving gut microbiota alterations, although the causal mechanisms remain to be fully elucidated. Practically, our findings highlight the need for monitoring PE-MPs contamination in aquaculture systems and suggest that interventions targeting gut microbiota may help mitigate flesh quality losses.
