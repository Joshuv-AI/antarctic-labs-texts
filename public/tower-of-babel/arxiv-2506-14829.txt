The Hardness of Achieving Impact in AI for Social Impact Research: A Ground-Level View of Challenges & Opportunities
Aditya Majumdar, Wenbo Zhang, Kashvi Prawal, Amulya Yadav

Abstract
In an attempt to tackle the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (UN SDGs), AI for Social Impact (AI4SI) projects focus on harnessing AI to address societal issues in areas such as healthcare, social justice, etc. Unfortunately, despite growing interest in AI4SI, achieving tangible, on-the-ground impact remains a significant challenge. For example, identifying and engaging motivated collaborators who are willing to co-design and deploy AI based solutions in real-world settings is often difficult. Even when such partnerships are established, many AI4SI projects “fail” to progress beyond the proof-of-concept stage, and hence, are unable to transition to at-scale production-level solutions. Furthermore, the unique challenges faced by AI4SI researchers are not always fully recognized within the broader AI community, where such work is sometimes viewed as primarily applied and not aligning with the traditional criteria for novelty emphasized in core AI venues. This paper attempts to shine a light on the diverse challenges faced in AI4SI research by diagnosing a multitude of factors that prevent AI4SI partnerships from achieving real-world impact on the ground. Drawing on semi-structured interviews with six leading AI4SI researchers - complemented by the authors’ own lived experiences in conducting AI4SI research - this paper attempts to understand the day-to-day difficulties faced in developing and deploying socially impactful AI solutions. Through thematic analysis, we identify structural and organizational, communication, collaboration, and operational challenges as key barriers to deployment. While there are no easy fixes, we synthesize best practices and actionable strategies drawn from these interviews and our own work in this space. In doing so, we hope this paper serves as a practical reference guide for AI4SI researchers and partner organizations seeking to engage more effectively in socially impactful AI collaborations.

2
Related Work

Conceptual Foundations & Design Principles for AI4SG
: In their 2020 chapter,
Floridi et al. 2020
look across twenty-seven AI‐for‐Good projects, from disaster‐response chatbots to health‐monitoring tools, and extract seven guiding principles. They emphasize starting with a problem that truly matters, bringing stakeholders into the design from day one, and building in ethical checks at every turn. Each principle is grounded in concrete practices, such as community co‐design workshops and automated fairness audits, to help teams translate ideals into real-world impact. Around the same time,
Tomašev et al. 2020
surveyed efforts aligned with the UN’s 17 SDGs. By examining case studies in agriculture, public health and beyond, they distilled ten practical recommendations, everything from setting clear success metrics to embedding an ethics review board. Their work highlights how deep, ongoing collaboration with domain experts keeps projects on track and avoids the “tech for tech’s sake” trap. Building on these high‐level ideas,
Kshirsagar, Patel, and Rodriguez 2021
take a more hands‐on approach. Instead of broad principles, they break AI4SG collaborations into four daily workflows, namely, communication, data management, modeling, and impact assessment, and draw out eleven lessons. For example, they show why starting with a lightweight prototype can uncover hidden assumptions early, and how designing privacy protections by default saves headaches later.
Two case studies showcase how teams build lasting, trust-based partnerships. Unfortunately, while these studies offer useful frameworks, they often present ideal workflows that obscure how things unfold in real-world settings.

Deployment Challenges & Pipeline Planning
: Even strong AI ideas can falter in production.

Paleyes, Urma, and Lawrence 2020
reviewed over fifty industrial ML deployments in retail forecasting, manufacturing maintenance, finance fraud detection, and pinpointed four common failure modes: (1) fragile infrastructure (e.g., ad-hoc scripts cause version mismatches and runtime errors);
(2) drift without monitoring (models go stale as data distributions shift, with no automated alerts); (3) silos and handoffs (data scientists pass off models without context);
(4) Missing end‐to‐end tests (lab performance breaks under real-world load or security limits).
They argue that solving these issues requires more than better tools. It calls for containerized CI/CD (Continuous Integration and Continuous Delivery) pipelines, continuous drift detection, and cross‐functional ownership of the entire ML lifecycle. Next,
Saxena et al. 2025
propose a framework to identify “AI Mismatches”, which occur when model performance falls short of real-world task requirements and leads to harm. Based on an analysis of 774 AI cases, they develop seven matrices that help teams evaluate feasibility, risk, and equity early in development. They argue that many failures arise from flawed problem formulation rather than technical mistakes, and that early design stages are the best time to surface ethical concerns. Case studies show how the framework helps prevent harmful AI deployments.
Finally,
Perrault, Fang, and Tambe 2021
propose a four‐stage “data‐to‐deployment” pipeline for AI4SI: (1) problem identification and immersion (engaging stakeholders to define real-world context); (2) prediction (developing accurate models); (3) prescription (translating models into actionable decisions); (4) field testing and deployment (evaluating solutions through real-world pilots and feedback). Examples in flood prediction and maternal health show how this approach keeps prototypes from failing in practice.
In our paper, we build on top of these studies by providing grounded insights from researchers navigating day-to-day challenges of deployment in AI4SI.

3
Methods

To answer our research question, we conducted semi-structured interviews with experienced AI4SI researchers who have worked or are currently working in the field. Research communities have varying definitions of the phrase AI4SI, hence we use the following normative definition of AI4SI in this paper:
AI for Social Impact refers to the development and deployment of AI systems that are co-designed with impacted communities, prioritize equity and inclusion, and aim for measurable, long-term improvements in societal well-being for historically neglected social challenges, particularly within the low-resource, underserved, or marginalized contexts.

Thus, while some may argue that much AI/ML research leads to long-term social impact (e.g., ChatGPT), this paper focuses specifically on AI4SI technologies developed to address challenges faced by low-resource communities.
We believe this focus allows us to center often-overlooked beneficiaries of AI technologies, including but not limited to homeless individuals
(
Vajiac et al. 2024b
)
, subsistence low-literate farmers
(
Singh et al. 2024
)
, malnourished children
(
Tadesse et al. 2025
)
, individuals at risk of eviction
(
Tabar et al. 2022
)
, and expectant or new mothers in rural areas
(
Katebi et al. 2025
)
.

3.1 Recruitment and Data Collection
:
The study was conducted at the main campus of an R1 university in the Mid-Atlantic region of the United States. In order to recruit participants for this study, the authors utilized a combination of purposive and snowball sampling. We employed a purposive sampling strategy to find our initial ‘seed’ participants as follows: (1) We initially selected all corresponding authors of papers published in the past five years at the AAAI Workshop on AI for Social Good, AAMAS Workshop on Agents for Social Good, and KDD Workshop on Data Science for Social Good.
(2) Next, we ranked participants by seniority, based on CVs from their websites, and excluded those with less than ten years of academic research experience.
(3) After filtering, all remaining prospective participants were invited to be a part of our study via email. Subsequently, we extended our recruitment by implementing a researcher-led (citation-network informed) snowball sampling strategy. Specifically, we analyzed the citations and the co-authors mentioned in the works of the ‘seed’ participants, and if any of the co-authors had also published their work in AI4SI venues (including the three AI4SI workshops above, as well as venues like the AAAI AI for Social Impact Track, IJCAI AI for Society Track, and KDD Applied Data Science Track),
an invitation was extended to them via email.

Prospective participants’ emails were sourced from the directories of their host institutions. The recruitment email included a hyperlink to a published AI4SI paper authored by the recipient and a hyperlink to schedule an interview time based on the prospective participant’s availability. Up to four weekly reminder emails were sent, and participants who responded were selected for the study.
The data collection began in early March 2025 and ended in early April 2025.

The interviews were conducted and recorded virtually via the video-conferencing platform Zoom. Before the interview, participants were informed of the study’s motivation and interview overview, then asked to provide verbal consent for participation and recording.
The interview transcripts were transcribed verbatim and anonymized for analysis. Participants were also informed about the voluntary nature of their participation and that they could withdraw from the study at any time without penalty or consequences. In the initial phase of the interview, participants were asked to elaborate on the AI4SI project highlighted in their recruitment email and to specify the current state of the technology introduced in that paper. They were then asked to share experiences from other AI4SI projects, especially those that failed to deploy or had unexpected outcomes.
The interview concluded with a request for suggestions to improve collaboration models in the AI4SI space. Afterwards, participants were emailed a demographics survey and asked to complete it.
Our interview protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board and conducted in accordance with the ethical guidelines of the host institution.

3.2 Data Analysis
:
We adopted a thematic analysis approach to analyze the interview transcripts
(
Auerbach and Silverstein 2003
;
Braun et al. 2019
)
. Initially, the first author did multiple passes of the transcribed text to understand the breadth of topics covered by the participants. Following this, we used collaborative qualitative analysis
(
Richards and Hemphill 2017
)
. The first and the second authors independently analyzed
\sim
250 minutes of audio recordings by performing open coding on the transcripts. Using an inductive approach, they let the themes emerge from the text. A total of 26 preliminary codes were generated in this phase. The two authors then met to compare and refine their codes, discussed interpretations, and resolved any differences. Building on this, we moved to the second phase of analysis, using theoretical coding
(
Hennink, Hutter, and Bailey 2019
)
to organize the initial codes into higher-order themes, presented in Section
4
.
This process was iterative, involving ongoing refinement of codes and themes over multiple weeks to establish validity and reduce bias in our coding process. Any disagreements were resolved through rigorous discussions and a consensus among the authors. Throughout this process, we maintained an audit trail documenting coding decisions and memos to ensure the validity of the generated codes
(
Creswell and Miller 2000
)
. All of the generated codes were analyzed using Taguette
(
Rampin and Rampin 2021
)
, a qualitative data analysis software.

4.1
Structural and Organizational Challenges

Through our analysis, we identified four types of structural and organizational challenges: misaligned incentives in (1) academia, (2) partner organizations, (3) funding issues, and (4) institutional inertia and disciplinary gaps.

4.1.1 Misaligned Incentives in Academia.

A) Pervasive “Publish or Perish” culture:
Due to a pervasive “publish or perish” culture in academia, AI researchers interested in AI4SI projects often find themselves at conflicting crossroads: the need to produce meaningful, context-aware, deployable solutions with measurable real-world impact v/s publishing a high volume of papers
at well-reputed, competitive AI venues. For researchers working in low-resource settings, it often requires long-term partnerships with community stakeholders, extensive engineering, and field work to design systems with real-world relevance and impact. These realities of field-driven work, which rarely lead to high-volume publications, often misalign with academic success metrics.
As a result, the lack of appropriate incentive structures in academia often leads AI4SI projects to be deprioritized in favor of “lower hanging fruits” or more “easily publishable” work.

P_{1}
candidly reflected on this tension:

I think that one problem […] is the culture, not just the culture, but the incentives of CS academia have become really high number of publications focused. And I think that’s a very bad thing for the field and for the depth of work, and for the ability to do the kind of work that I would like to do, and would like to see people doing in the [AI4SI] space. […] That’s one thing that we really need to try to figure out, because the incentives are bad in some ways.

–
P_{1}

In addition, participants also mentioned how the time-intensive nature of these collaborations clashed with the rapid and competitive nature of academic publishing.
P_{5}
noted how sustained time commitments are often at odds with short publication cycles that define success in AI/ML:

As a PhD student in AI/ML, you probably know how competitive it is nowadays in this field, and how fast the publication cycle [is] […], and the peer pressure would indicate that “oh! other people can publish like 3 papers a year,” and if you work on this project […] you can work on it for 6 months, just with a lot of engineering work and trying to get it deployed, and then, still in the last minute, your nonprofit collaborator says, “no, we don’t want to push this further”. These are the things that are the true blocking factors from the AI researcher’s perspective.
–
P_{5}

B) Conventional Expectations of Novelty:
P_{4}
further illustrated how these structural incentives shaped their early-career publishing strategy, where they had to mask their AI4SI field work as part of the methodology section, to increase the chances of their work being accepted.

The way [it] used to be was that since there was no such venue to publish these kinds of papers in [AI4SI], the only way to do this was to smuggle it in by writing a methods paper and then putting in these sources of applications. But they wouldn’t occupy as much importance in terms of review. And so, there’s a lot more effort trying to add, possibly even unnecessary methodological improvements, to things that really were not that useful.
–
P_{4}

Author
A_{4}
shared similar experiences, noting that the need to accommodate real-world constraints in AI4SI projects often led to highly complicated problem formalizations, making it difficult to develop meaningful theoretical insights in this realm. Nonetheless, in an effort to meet expectations of traditionally minded CS/AI reviewers at top-tier venues,
A_{4}
felt compelled to include trivial theorems on the computational hardness of formalized problems, primarily to align with prevailing norms of academic rigor rather than to advance core goals of AI4SI research.

In addition, many AI4SI projects require high levels of engineering work to reach deployment, which rarely falls within conventional definitions of novel research, disincentivising researchers from pursuing such projects.
P_{5}
stated:
“PhD students are here to solve research problems. And the engineering work is additional work that they need to do beyond their research work. It’s time-consuming, and requires a huge amount of engineering work.”

C) A Careful Balancing Act:
Navigating this tension requires researchers to balance a difficult trade-off between technical novelty and ensuring real-world utility and impact.
P_{6}
reflected that focusing solely on novelty (cared for by the broader AI community) can undermine the practical utility of the system:

I feel that as academics we need to publish and let people know what we have done. So it’s a trade-off and a tricky line to figure out a novelty, but also make sure that it gets deployed, and it’s useful without worrying about novelty too much, because if I had just worried about novelty, then [PO] may not have even used our methods.
–
P_{6}

As a result of these structural disincentives, academics are less likely to pursue or maintain projects that do not align with conventional expectations in the broader CS/AI community. Even though an AI4SI project may have the potential to mitigate a pressing social issue, it is deprioritized since the extensive time, engineering, and deployment efforts aren’t recognized at an institutional level. As a result, valuable work is set aside not due to lack of relevance, but because it does not fit into prevailing academic incentive structures. As
P_{4}
mentioned:

All of us work on incentives, and we don’t want to be in a situation where we’re doing something for which we will not get rewarded, not [get] recognized.[…] As a community the incentives have to be aligned with what we want to accomplish in AI4SI.
–
P_{4}

4.1.2

Misaligned Incentives in Partner Organizations.

A) Extra-Role Expectations:
While misalignment of academic incentives poses a challenge to deploying AI4SI projects, POs have their own set of barriers. These POs come from various backgrounds, such as non-profits, hospitals, and government agencies operating on limited budgets, with small teams or volunteers juggling multiple responsibilities. Collaborating on a long-term AI research project is rarely a part of their formal responsibilities. As
P_{6}
mentioned: “
This is a new thing they have to do. Their current job does not have this, they aren’t getting paid for this. […] This isn’t part of their job description. So, they need to be interested in it.
”

Author
A_{1}
echoed this sentiment, recalling how coordinating with volunteer staff at a non-profit was especially challenging due to a 12-hour time difference. Volunteers contributed outside regular hours and without compensation, especially early on, which led to slower response times throughout the project.
Eventually, the delays became unsustainable, and the project had to be discontinued.

B) Divergent Priorities:
In some cases, the lack of sustained engagement stems from the misalignment between the research focus and the PO’s actual priorities, a finding that supplements
(
Saxena et al. 2025
)
. As
P_{5}
observed:

At the very beginning, we were not able to identify a problem that is truly crucial to the organization that can be solved by AI. We found a middle ground, [where] they are interested, but they don’t really care that much, and it can be solved by AI. […] And because of this reason, the nonprofit organization don’t have the incentive to invest time, energy, and human resources to this project to get it deployed.
–
P_{5}

Even when it seems like a relevant problem is identified, this disconnect between what constitutes a core problem manifests across various levels in the PO. While leadership may express enthusiasm about integrating AI in their systems, the front-line workers may view such tools as irrelevant or even burdensome.
P_{4}
shared their experience from a wildlife conservation experience:

We went on a trip with [PO for] wildlife conservation. And there’s a country where the minister said, “yeah, I foresaw wildlife conservation, awesome!”. And the secretary said, “sounds like an awesome idea, let’s take photos!” But then, when we go to the rangers on ground, they said, “we just need better shoes. We just need better guns. We don’t need AI. […] we have these old guns, and the poachers come with better guns”. And they’re just completely opposed to AI.
–
P_{4}

A similar dynamic emerged in a US-based deployment:

This was also true in Los Angeles at some other organization where the higher-ups are like, “yeah, we need AI-based modelling and monitoring of threats, and there’s so much to protect.” And then we went on patrols with officers, and the officers were just saying, “We just need better pay. We need better hours”. And you see in some of these organizations, that the “bank and file” have completely different needs.
–
P_{4}

Participants emphasized that without establishing a clear vision and strong early buy-in, these collaborations are unlikely to result in deployment. As
P_{2}
noted
“you sort of need that [clear vision] otherwise […] at some point, things will falter”
. Hence, it is imperative to establish alignment in initial phases of the work, like
P_{6}
shared:

In the first few parts of the project, it’s very important to ask the hard questions, so people are clear about what it takes to be successful, and are they really serious about it? Because if there is no buy-in from the stakeholders, these projects cannot succeed.
–
P_{6}

In summary, deployment is not just a technical task, but is a process which requires deep mutual buy-in from the beginning, since without it, even well-intentioned AI4SI projects cannot move beyond the prototyping phase.

4.1.3

Funding.

A) Insufficient Funding for AI4SI:
In cases where researchers and the PO can move past the previous challenges and start working on a mutually beneficial problem, the lack of funds can pose a serious issue for the project’s progress. According to
P_{5}
, often both parties are financially constrained, and to move beyond the prototyping stage, non-profit POs often expect the research team to bear the project costs:

It’s not that easy to get a lot of funding for this kind of work. And also sometimes to push for deployment in collaboration with the nonprofit, the nonprofit they are also resource-constrained, so they would expect some funding support to support their human efforts to work on this AI project.
–
P_{5}

Similar sentiment was shared by
P_{3}
, who mentioned that POs ability to fund or maintain these implementations is limited,
“It’s not so easy to deploy. Infrastructure is not there; capital is not there”
. Even when the PO wants to continue the project, this constraint prevents meaningful long-term integration into their systems.

P_{2}
shared a scenario where they developed an intervention but the PO couldn’t deploy it as they didn’t have the necessary funds:

Even aside from the AI part […] the object-level intervention itself was being provided by the research team. If we could have gone to them and said, like, “Hey, we have the capacity to continue delivering this intervention”, I think they would have loved for us to do that, and they would have worked with us to bring that into their space. But it’s not something that they could have provided themselves that time.
–
P_{2}

Participants also mentioned how this lack of funding often places a significant burden on AI4SI researchers during early stages of collaborations, when they have no option but to volunteer their time (or their students’ time)
for their respective POs, which can be an unsustainable model for long-term collaborations.

P_{6}
recalled working on a project when COVID-19 had just begun:

“Obviously there was no money immediately.[…] So you had to do pro bono or volunteering work. But you need to quickly figure out that this is not sustainable long term. You need to have a funding stream.”

Surprisingly, even in the case where the research group can provide funding to maintain the intervention, they are rarely sufficient for long-term deployment. As
P_{4}
reflected:

We gave grants to these wildlife conservation organizations to hire people to try to do this [deployment and maintenance work], but they couldn’t, because I think this just needs a level of support and organization, that our students cannot [provide], […] they have to do their own research and publish papers, and they can’t do this.
–
P_{4}

Ultimately, without a long-term funding model, even high-potential project deployments can run out of momentum. As
P_{6}
precisely stated
“If funding is not figured out, it may not fail immediately, but it will fail eventually, because at the end of the day somebody has to pay for it.”

4.1.4

Institutional Inertia and Disciplinary Gaps.

A) Red-Tape at Institutional Organizations:
Another hurdle that AI4SI projects often encounter are institutional processes that are misaligned with the timely nature of these collaborations, causing them to slow-down the field-driven deployment process.
P_{5}
noted that both researchers and POs can be under-prepared when navigating procedural red-tape at their host institutions:

There are a lot of steps that we need to go through, and this could scare both the nonprofit organization, and the researchers. For the nonprofit organizations, maybe they’re not familiar with this, so they don’t even know they would need to spend time on this and the same thing on our end for the students.
–
P_{5}

This view was echoed by
P_{2}
, who mentioned administrative processes such as data use agreements often operate on timelines which cannot keep up with research teams’ pace:

Data use agreements can be a real pain […] it all has to go through the lawyer’s office, it can take forever. We just try to start them as soon as possible, and then keep asking the lawyers until they’re done.
–
P_{2}

These institutional processes are particularly damaging for students in the field, who are caught in-between the demands of ethical review processes and the pressure to publish frequently. As
P_{5}
explained:

If you need to spend so much time like going through the data usage agreement and IRB for any human evaluation of your tool, then if that process takes half a year, you look at your peers realizing that they can get more than one paper done within the half a year, and then the student is under great pressure.
–
P_{5}

B) Improper Interdisciplinary Training:
In addition to these organizational delays, many research teams are structurally unprepared to carry out field-related work due to gaps in their disciplinary training. The majority of students have either CS
or ML as their foundation. However, AI4SI projects require additional interdisciplinary competencies ranging from econometrics and statistical inference to human-centred design and software engineering. As
P_{4}
noted:

People ask me, why don’t people [who] do AI, [just] know how to do [AI4SG]? I feel like, not exactly, because there’s a lot of econometrics training and a lot of statistical analysis, and a lot of field experiments, and what social workers do, and all sorts of things that we need to do to interface AI with society.
–
P_{4}

On a similar note,
P_{3}
mentioned how people working in ML may not have abilities to develop software applications:

“Someone might need an Android app, someone might need an iPhone app[…]. People doing ML might not have the skills to build websites or apps.”

Overall, the inertia created by the institutional process, coupled with a lack of interdisciplinary training, result in potential
AI4SI projects being stuck in initial phases or not being implemented, not because of a lack of will, but because surrounding structures were not created to support them.

4.2
Communication and Understanding Gaps

AI4SI projects span domains like computer science (CS), medicine, agriculture, public policy, economics, and social work, and sectors such as academia, NGOs, government, and healthcare. Due to their interdisciplinary nature, these projects often face communication breakdowns and misunderstandings that collaborators must learn to navigate.

A) Improper Perceptions of AI:
With the recent rise of AI, misunderstandings regarding these technologies have become prevalent due to media narratives,
resulting in inflated expectations and concerns. As
P_{1}
aptly put it:

I think it’s a little bit unfortunate in some ways, because I think AI gets these days especially painted with a very broad brush, and the kind of AI that I’m talking about is often not the kind of AI that they are worried about.
–
P_{1}

This misunderstanding further manifests in contradictory perceptions.
P_{1}
highlights this and further states the importance of demystifying these assumptions:

There’s fairly deep skepticism about AI, while at the same time, there’s also this sense of like, “Oh, wow! Can it do great things for us?” […] Educating people about what AI is and what are the kinds of things that we can do is a big thing that we need to do.
–
P_{1}

Hence, without a grounded understanding of what these systems can do for the PO, the initial conversations can be quite tricky and time-consuming. As
P_{2}
stated: “
It takes a while because they do not know anything about what AI could do, so they don’t know what to ask for, and you don’t know enough about their domain to tell them what they should be asking
”.
Due to this disconnect between collaborators’ domains, challenges in communicating across disciplinary boundaries can stall the progress of these projects. As
P_{5}
mentioned
“Figuring out how to communicate across these interdisciplinary boundaries is super difficult”
, particularly when foundational concepts and jargon differ. This is when participants mentioned the importance of long-term sustained collaborations, for example,
P_{1}
shared
“that’s why having relationships that go on for a long time are good, because then you come to a shared understanding of what’s happening and what you can do.”

B) Importance of Internal “Champions”:
A strategy which several participants employed was to find a collaborator at their PO with a background in their expertise.
P_{5}
explained:

Our sense was that if we had somebody on the inside who was trained in economics or game theory, or some probabilities and things like that, then we found it very easy to talk to them, and then they would be the ones who champion the system internally.
–
P_{5}

In summary, clear and consistent communication is a key factor for such collaborations, as without having a shared understanding, even well-intentioned projects can risk falling apart. As
P_{6}
concluded,
“it’s important at every point of time that you keep communicating. Keep telling what you have in mind, understand their issues and constraints as well, otherwise it can quickly break down”.

4.3
Collaboration Hurdles

Through our analysis, we identified two types of collaboration hurdles: forming strategic partnerships and trust issues.

4.3.1

Strategic Partnerships.

A) Buy-in from PO Leadership:
Successful deployments in AI4SI rely on not only the technical feasibility, but also fostering relationships with collaborators that have the authority and commitment to allow the integration of AI-based interventions in the PO’s operational pipeline. Participants mentioned the importance of having institutional buy-in from the PO’s side and someone from the organization with actual decision-making power.
P_{2}
explained:

And the sort of the deeper thing is like a lack of institutional capacity and buy-in to get to implementation. Because at the end of the day, deploying something requires time, commitment, and someone with sufficient decision-making power being on board right in the partner organization
–
P_{2}

In situations where the commitment from leadership was missing, the participants found themselves waiting for extended periods of time, and ultimately being forced to restart with another PO.
P_{6}
reflected on their experience:

I remember one hospital we were working with, […] we waited for one and a half years, and that guy was not even responding to our emails then, and we were stuck. And then we found out another right partner in another hospital and within 3 months we had applied for an NIH grant.
–
P_{6}

B) Collaboration with Intermediaries:
One way some researchers tackled this issue is by leveraging collaborations with “intermediaries”. These intermediaries are often researchers from another discipline (usually social work or medicine in this study) who have long-standing relationships with a PO, therefore, working with them can expedite the logistical process and facilitate trust between the new researcher and the PO.
P_{2}
, when discussing their work using AI for HIV prevention, mentioned how they worked on creating the algorithm and a separate social-work research team worked on implementing the interventions in HIV prevention drop-in centers:

The social work research team had like a long-running partnership with the drop-in centers, and so they would be able to come to the center and recruit people for the study and use space at the center to offer the interventions themselves, […] like the labor itself is all being supplied by the social work research team under their own sources of funding.
–
P_{2}

Similarly,
P_{1}
mentioned the advantage of working with an intermediary, who could directly work with the decision makers in the PO:

I’m a computer scientist, but my partner in crime [partner’s name], his work’s in public health, and he sits on a bunch of advisory boards and committees, and he brings the result of this research to the people who are making decisions right? And so he talks to them and […] helps to inform decision makers.
–
P_{1}

However, such collaborations can bring their own sets of challenges, as working in an interdisciplinary setting requires the members to mutually agree on shared incentives such as negotiating where and how to publish their work.
P_{1}
acknowledged this by stating:

How does one negotiate like, where are we going to try to publish this? What are the kinds of things that we can do in terms of publication? […] I’m grateful that [partner’s name] is very happy with publishing a whole bunch of stuff in AI and CS venues. There are many other disciplines where that would not be okay, or where people would care about that.
–
P_{1}

In addition, even though these partnerships have multiple advantages, such as streamlining processes, they can also serve as a bottleneck for new researchers entering the field of AI4SI, as they may not know who these intermediaries are and how to access their networks. They could therefore find themselves excluded from conversations that rely heavily on pre-existing trust. Overall, strategic collaborations require not just interdisciplinary understanding, but also shared priorities, mutual trust, and access to PO’s key decision makers which enable integration of interventions in their workflows.

4.3.2

Trust Issues.

A) Skepticism Toward Researcher Motives:
A recurring theme from the previous subsection was the difficulty in cultivating trust with a PO, especially in the early stages of collaboration. Participants mentioned the fragile nature of garnering trust from a PO, specifically when they express skepticism about the researcher’s true motives.
P_{5}
pointed out
“they [POs] don’t trust you because they feel like you just come and get a paper, and then you’re gone.”
To address these concerns, participants emphasized creating explainable models and communicating long-term commitment intent early in the collaboration.

P_{5}
further explained:

Try to use some interpretable models in the beginning, so that it’s easier to convey, what exactly is the AI doing […] then they would be willing to accept some models that they are less familiar with. […] Trust on AI can be mitigated through trust in the research [and] in the people they’re collaborating. […] The good side of it is that the PhD student will be here for 5 years.[…] And the nonprofit will feel like, “Oh, this is the long term thing. This is not like they come and go after one year.”
–
P_{5}

Previous negative experiences may also account for an organization’s skepticism towards collaborating with AI researchers.
P_{1}
explained that schools and local governments may be especially cautious due to reputational risks:

It is difficult to build trust, because they have repeatedly had experiences where they’ve worked with academia, and it hasn’t gone well, or there’s been some kind of information spillage […] then they get bad press because something happens in your local school division – it’s a big story.
–
P_{1}

B) Territorial Disputes:
Author
A_{4}
also encountered issues of mistrust from practitioner-oriented stakeholders. In one AI4SI project focused on locust swarm forecasting, their work was abruptly shelved after presenting a proof-of-concept model to a UN official. The official (recognized as the UN’s leading expert on locust forecasting) felt the need to protect their operational turf and domain expertise, and as a result, expressed deep skepticism about the AI model’s value (when none of this skepticism was communicated in prior communication with that expert), which ultimately stalled the project’s progress.

C) Discomfort with Lack of Agency:
Another method that was used to navigate trust issues was to provide more control to the PO early on, such as offering agency over model outputs and parameters.
P_{4}
recalled an experience where they created an elaborate interface for a software to provide more agency to the PO upon their request:

Initially, because they didn’t know what would happen with the system, they wanted more control. But once they used it and trusted output, they really didn’t need the complicated interface. […] Had we not given them the initial control, they would have not deployed the system […] so that initial step was probably still useful to get it accepted.
–
P_{4}

In another case, the potential PO is skeptical of the technology and what it promises to deliver itself.
P_{6}
recalled an experience working with epidemiologists who were convinced that AI isn’t viable in their domain:

Some epidemiologists were not even convinced that this can be done […] so you need to go and convince them that this is a possibility and this will help them do their job better.
–
P_{6}

Overall, trust was defined as a function of time and credibility. Once a project was successfully deployed, interest to collaborate from other organizations in the same domain are piqued.
P_{4}
shared their experience:

Once we got one success with wildlife conservation society, then it seemed like it was easier for others to have that trust in us that okay, they are good people, they deliver results and so forth.
–
P_{4}

These reflections, therefore, highlight the nature of building trust in AI4SI research: slow-building, iterative, and earned. Hence, it is vital for AI4SI practitioners to invest in long-term engagement, transparency, and prioritize relation-building just as much as technical development.

4.4
Operational Hurdles

Through our analysis, we identified three types of operational hurdles: (1) dynamic environments, (2) data issues, and (3) scalability and maintenance issues.

4.4.1

Dynamic Environment.

AI4SI projects are developed for POs, which are often characterized by high amounts of variability in both their staff and project priorities. Participants mentioned how quickly the landscape changes in these settings,
P_{1}
mentioned:

The landscape also just keeps changing continuously in this space, […] so there’s a question of how does one keep up with a moving target? Especially when you’re doing this in a small academic lab, where the policies on what you’re supposed to do and how you’re supposed to do it are regularly changing.
–
P_{1}

Some participants highlighted the tensions in rapidly evolving domains such as crisis response where they had to balance the tension between the trade-off between technical novelty and time commitment:

Because you’re dealing with a disaster, you need to be able to respond, and response sometimes doesn’t mean having the optimal answer[…] because sometimes, if you find best answers too late, then it’s meaningless. So there is a timeliness perspective.
–
P_{6}

Interviewees also shared the sentiment where due to sudden changes in the policy environment, stakeholder needs or organizational structure, researchers had to reallocate significant time and effort to re-align their solutions to the PO’s needs. As
P_{3}
emphasized:

The biggest problem is, it [environment] doesn’t stay the same. But the software stays the same. So the challenge is that after a while the reason why you don’t see wide adoption of a software is because there’s lots of variability in deployment space.
–
P_{3}

The dynamic nature wasn’t constrained to just the problem environment or organizational structure, participants also mentioned dealing with sudden change in priorities of their collaborators.
P_{6}
reflected on their experience:

We started [working] with a big company […] but suddenly, their focus changed with the coming of ChatGPT and generative AI. So the their managers focus all their efforts on other problems […] so naturally [AI4SG] projects will get deprioritized.
–
P_{6}

In addition, most nonprofit, public, and humanitarian POs face quick staff turnover, creating barriers for the long-term deployment of AI4SI projects. Some of these organizations rely on volunteer efforts or short-term staff. This churn of staff leads to constant retraining efforts, which are often unsupported by the project’s grants. As
P_{3}
put it:

If you build it, can any of their volunteers even use it? […] You will provide training to some of the people, and it’s a very volatile space where people come and go because a lot of them are volunteers. So just because you teach a couple of people doesn’t mean it’s going to stick.
–
P_{3}

Collectively, these examples highlight the uncertainty that characterizes many AI4SI environments. From staff churn at POs to shifting institutional priorities and evolving problem definitions, researchers must adapt their systems midway or risk obsolescence.
Navigating these uncertainties requires more than technical robustness, but also the ability to build sustainable long-term partnerships.

4.4.2

Data Issues.

Another recurring issue was the challenge of accessing good-quality data. Unlike typical AI/ML settings, where one can usually find an abundance of data to develop their models, AI4SI collaborations often unfold in scenarios where the data is messy, incomplete, or not collected with the needs of the AI/ML task. Participants shared that some projects never advance since the PO does not have the necessary data. Like
P_{4}
stated
“there are other places where people had ideas, but didn’t have data”
. Similarly,
P_{2}
noted:

It turns out that the partner organization does not actually have data that allows you to kind of do the thing you hope to do. They’re just not collecting data that would let you […] predict something. […] Or they’re not collecting the labels.
–
P_{2}

Throughout interviews, participants emphasized that poor data quality made it challenging to build reliable models that address the PO’s needs,
as
P_{3}
said
“there were a lot of accuracy challenges as well, primarily, because the data set was quite dirty”
.
P_{3}
further noted that POs might not be willing to share their data due to its highly sensitive nature:
“the challenge is that a lot of the data is sensitive. so you can’t really share files with someone who’s a 3rd party”
.
This sentiment was echoed by
P_{6}
, who shared that they spent a year convincing the PO to share data by having students intern there, noting that even when data exists, accessing and organizing it can require significant time and in-person effort:

Where do we get the data from? It’s a long fuse. I mean, I had to send a student, and they were not ready to share the data. So the 1st year was very hard. I had to literally send a student for an internship there.
–
P_{6}

In summary, the data collection pipeline is rarely straightforward in AI4SI. Before researchers can begin modeling a solution, they often spend months navigating institutional barriers, anonymization constraints, and data cleaning just to make data analysis possible.

4.4.3

Scalability and Maintenance.

55% of AI4SI projects receive less than $250K in funding
(
Bankhwal et al. 2024
)
, so even with promising pilots or field deployments, scaling and long-term maintenance remain major challenges. Since majority of the projects are narrowly scoped in the initial phases, when prompted to extend beyond their original use case, these solutions can turn brittle. As
P_{3}
mentioned:

You are building most of these solutions for a very niche, small scope. It’s going to work for “this city” or for “these many kids”, […] but doesn’t scale because your system is not built on a large enough data set. So when you try to scale it, it collapses.
–
P_{3}

However, once an intervention has been proven effective, maintaining and supporting it is its own resource-intensive task which requires substantial engineering efforts.
P_{4}
reflected on this by stating:
“If you needed a very sophisticated deployment, then you get into serious software engineering. And now you’re talking [about] millions of dollars of software engineering contracts, which is obviously not something a university can do or is interested in.”

P_{4}
further shared their experience of successful deployment scenarios, where they mentioned the importance of industry partnerships that are capable of sustaining large-scale software architectures.
P_{4}
elaborated: “If you have a [company partner], collaboration with [that company is] supporting it [deployment]. […] So there’s a scale of projects for deployment that can be handled at universities, beyond which we are not equipped to deal with.”

Another participant,
P_{5}
, shared their concern regarding why maintaining successfully deployed work can go against interests of an academic lab.
According to
P_{5}
,
“maintenance is even harder, because you cannot get publications out of it”
. This lack of academic incentive, therefore, discourages research teams in the long-term maintenance of the work. Hence, researchers often assume their purpose as highlighting the effectiveness of proposed interventions, and then hoping the PO would invest in maintaining that work. As
P_{6}
stated
“our interest in many things is to deploy something, show them the work of it, and then obviously, they have to get convinced enough that they maintain it.”

Another challenge in sustaining interventions long term is the departure of students working on the project.
P_{1}
shared
“maybe you couldn’t find students, or like students who was working on it graduated, and nobody else like kept going”
.

These reflections highlight that scaling and maintaining interventions post-deployment can limit the overall impact of AI4SI projects, as small academic labs often lack the resources, funding, external recognition, and industry partnerships needed to sustain and scale their work.

5
Discussion/Possible Solutions

A recurring theme across interviews was misalignment of academic incentives and the long-term nature of AI4SI work. Most academic systems still value technical novelty and publication count over long-term real-world deployment. Participants shared that the definition of valuable research should expand to include both algorithmic novelty and more applied work.
This might include recognizing deployed systems, open-source tools, or public impact as equally important contributions to the field.

That being said, participants also acknowledged that the landscape for AI4SI work is improving. Compared to a few years ago, several conferences now offer special tracks for AI4SI projects (e.g., AAMAS
(
Danassis 2025
)
, NeurIPS
(
Luck et al. 2018
)
, and AAAI
(
AAAI 2024
)
)recognizing their significance and deployment challenges, and helping bring greater visibility and legitimacy to this work.
Importantly, there seems to be a welcome shift in how this work is being evaluated: expectations for submissions to these tracks are gradually evolving to reflect the trade-offs between novelty and real-world relevance that participants consistently highlighted. More of this shift is needed to ensure that socially grounded work is not sidelined due to conventional academic norms.

Another suggestion from participants was to implement institutional reforms that would aid researchers in this field. There is a need for stronger support systems, such as university consortia focused on early steps in AI4SI collaborations, including connecting collaborators with researchers, managing data use agreements, and aligning goals, thereby institutionalizing these services.
An example is Carnegie Mellon University’s ML for Social Good initiative
(
CMU 2025
)
, which provides opportunities for public & social sector organizations to collaborate with their researchers. These initiatives take the burden of conducting outreach and navigating procedural red tape off the shoulders of individual researchers.

Training of students interested in joining the field was another recurring suggestion. Aspiring students in this field often have strong technical foundations but lack training in areas like statistical inference, econometrics, and field experiments in social contexts. Participants emphasized the need for better interdisciplinary training which involves public policy, qualitative research, or ethics. Some even called for separate dedicated programs for AI4SI in CS departments.

Participants also provided some practical suggestions they utilize currently to ensure sustained long-term collaborations. “Quick wins” was the most stated approach taken by researchers during the initial phases of their AI4SI projects. In this approach, rather than immediately aiming for technical novelty, researchers prioritized delivering small, tangible forms of value to their PO during the initial phase of the project. This could include simple data analysis or running regression models to help answer pressing questions from the PO. These early contributions helped researchers with gaining trust, understanding organizations domain, and establishing credibility with their POs.

Researchers also suggested steps to sustain the project after the initial phases. Some participants paired PhD students who conducted the core research of the project with master’s and undergraduate students to assist with the engineering efforts, such as software development. This delegation of tasks helps make the work more sustainable over time.

In addition, there may be other possible solutions that can help with some of the issues highlighted in the paper. Tools such as models cards
(
Mitchell et al. 2019
)
and data cards
(
Pushkarna, Zaldivar, and Kjartansson 2022
)
can be utilized to help reduce miscommunication issues between researchers and POs. Model cards can help explain what a model is doing and what its limitations are in a clear and accessible manner. Data cards help researchers understand a dataset’s origins, structure, and limitations, fostering shared understanding with POs.

Finally, “matching market” style funding opportunities tailored towards the creation of early collaboration opportunities between AI researchers and POs can help. These funding opportunities can provide sufficient incentives to both parties to brainstorm mutually beneficial problems, which can then be submitted as grant proposals. These grants allow researchers and POs to test ideas, align motivations, and build trust without committing resources or funding upfront.
Google India AI for Social Good program
(
Google 2021
)
, NSF ReDDDot
(
NSF 2024b
)
, and NSF CIVIC program
(
NSF 2024a
)
are good examples of such programs.

6
Concluding Thoughts

AI for Social Impact (AI4SI) represents a growing area of research that seeks to apply AI to real-world problems affecting underserved and marginalized populations. In a world where BigTech continues to dominate the AI landscape by often creating for-profit solutions for first-world problems,
it is imperative that the AI4SI community flourishes to serve as a counterbalance by focusing on the needs of marginalized and underserved populations (who will rarely get attention from BigTech because of the for-profit nature of these communities). The work facilitated by this field may be slower, more complex, and less rewarded, but it remains vital for ensuring that the benefits of AI are more equitably distributed.

Unfortunately, research in this space is challenging for a variety of reasons, as highlighted in this paper. In general, affecting positive real-world change through research interventions is difficult in any domain, but these challenges are compounded by the structural disincentives in academia.
Research that prioritizes socially relevant deployment over algorithmic novelty is often undervalued by peers, and limited funding further exacerbates the challenges of sustaining long-term, impact-driven work. As showcased in this paper, researchers face roadblocks not just during technical development, but also in navigating red tape, collaboration and communication hurdles, aligning incentives with POs, and ensuring their work is sustainable beyond initial pilots.

The reflections shared in this paper, drawn from the interviews and authors’ lived experiences, showcase the myriad challenges faced by AI4SI researchers. At the same time, the suggestions and workarounds they provided point towards possible paths to advance the field. By recognizing these challenges and documenting lessons learned, we hope this work raises awareness of the issues AI4SI researchers face and serves as a reference for aspiring practitioners.
Strengthening this field will require collective effort, but this would ensure that the fruits of AI technologies are shared equitably across various communities that need them most.

7
Limitations

Due to the non-trivial efforts involved in finding participants for this study, the final sample size is relatively small and skewed in its demographic configuration. Only one participant identified as a woman in our study, which may misrepresent or limit the challenges faced by researchers from that demographic. Along with that, the sampling strategy relied on citation networks, which could bias the results.

Nevertheless, our sample size aligns with established principles in qualitative research, where the purpose is not to “count” opinions or people
(
O’reilly and Parker 2013
)
, instead, the aim is to focus on “richness of data rather than frequencies”
(
Tracy 2010
)
, and to explore a range of opinions and different representations of an issue. All of these were sufficiently achieved with our sample size.

8
Ethical Considerations

As highlighted in the previous section, our study only has one participant who identified as a woman, even though in our recruiting efforts, we tried to recruit a diverse set of participants. This, however, might reflect the current state of the field, where certain perspectives remain underrepresented. We acknowledge this limitation and the way it may have shaped the narratives of the paper.

Additionally, all our participants were primarily comprised of researchers. Though they mentioned some perspectives from their PO’s side, these reflections do not entail the entirety of challenges that are faced by the PO while collaborating with AI researchers. We recognize that including these voices from various organizations such as non-profits, governmental agencies, hospitals, etc, is essential to capturing the full complexity of AI4SI collaborations.

We also acknowledge the recent critiques of the AI4SI space
(
Green 2019
;
Berendt 2018
;
Moore 2019
)
, specifically those concerned with techno-solutionism: the belief that technological innovations can be used to solve all complex social problems
(
Sætra 2023
)
. While this paper presents the practical challenges to deploying solutions in a real-world context, we do not assume that AI interventions are appropriate or sufficient in all contexts. Structural changes such as implementing informed policies, and sustained investments in public institutions are required to uplift these marginalized and underserved groups. We view AI4SI as one potential tool in this broader ecosystem for change, and emphasize the long-term sustainability and alignment with the community’s needs of these systems.

Finally, in this study, we have ensured to maintain the participants’ privacy. The only identifying information that was kept was their emails to send them the demographics survey and updates regarding the study. No personally identifiable information has been disclosed, and all the quotes were anonymized. Any identifying information was removed from the audio transcripts before starting the thematic coding process. To ensure participant’s well-being, they were informed that they are allowed to withdraw from the study whenever they pleased to without any consequences. The participants were provided with the goals of the study, and all provided voluntary verbal consent to partake in this study.

9
Positionality Statement

This paper is grounded in the authors’ belief that the value of AI4SI work is not defined just by algorithmic novelty, but the successful integration of these systems in real-world contexts that are underserved, resource-constrained, or systemically overlooked. Our normative stance (defined in Section
3
) prioritizes deployment, sustainability, and alignment with the affected community’s needs. We acknowledge that not everyone shares this view, especially in the broader AI community, where the value of a work is often determined via algorithmic novelty and theoretical innovations.

The authors are researchers working in an academic institution where academic incentives are still largely dependent on publication volume and theoretical novelty. We understand the slow and iterative nature of the work in this field and the tensions that this creates while working in this space. Our normative stance was developed through our lived experiences, trying to deploy our work on the ground. These experiences shape the barriers to deployment presented in this paper.

By explicitly mentioning our positional stance, we wish to clarify the assumptions that informed our analysis. We do not claim that this is the only way to approach AI4SI, but we believe that the responsible integration of these AI technologies is vital to serve the communities which are often left behind.

AI for Pallet Planning in

AI for Food Rescue.
This line of work addresses volunteer coordination challenges in food rescue operations through successive AI-based interventions. The first project developed a predictive model to assess whether a food rescue would be claimed, helping dispatchers plan interventions more efficiently and reduce unnecessary notifications. Building on this, the second project created a recommender system that selects and diversifies which volunteers receive notifications, significantly improving task claim rates and reducing volunteer fatigue. The most recent project goes a step further by predicting the difficulty of rescue tasks using both structured and text data, offering interpretable insights to better match tasks with volunteers’ experience levels and support long-term engagement.

A.3
Social Justice

AI for Homelessness Services.

AI has been used to improve how limited homelessness resources are allocated. Most recently, machine learning has been used to analyze caseworker discretion, revealing that while many decisions follow consistent patterns, discretionary choices often improve outcomes for less obvious cases, highlighting the value of expert judgment alongside algorithms.

AI for Eviction Prevention.

This project addresses the question of how useful the eviction predictions are in targeting outreach efforts. They create risk scores by completing an eviction prediction assignment, and then utilize these risk ratings to develop targeted outreach strategies. Experiment results demonstrate that the risk scores are effective, allowing a hypothetical caseworker team to reach more properties that are at risk of eviction.

A.4
Safety & Security

AI for Airport Security.

This project discusses the Los Angeles airport security protection problem and applies a game-theoretic model for this patrolling/monitoring problem. The research question was focused on where and when you set up checkpoints, and do canine patrols and do so in a principled fashion, knowing that the adversaries conduct surveillance; and was solved using the concept of randomization.

AI for Flight Security.

This project focuses on security issues at airport terminals and air marshals on international flights. They propose two applications to solve these issues accordingly. These applications are also deployed in different real-world scenarios. This study looks at the information, design decisions, and assessment standards that are essential to creating these applications.

AI for Maritime Security.

This project focuses on the security allocation problem for the United States Coast Guard and provides a game-theoretic system, as the solution. They provide findings from an actual experiment that validates the suggested model and, more significantly, quantifies the advantages of Stackelberg security games for allocating security resources.

AI for Public Transit Safety.

This project focuses on a real transit system and introduces a new general Security resource allocation in dynamic, unpredictable settings using the Bayesian Stackelberg game model. By incorporating a Markov Decision Process, it generates adaptive schedules that handle real-world disruptions. Field deployment on the LA Metro showed improved fare evasion outcomes over static methods.

AI for Infrastructure Resilience.

This project focuses on the Critical Infrastructure Systems (CIS) protection problem. They creates the CIS analysis tool, which employs heterogeneous network analysis to let users: (a) see the country’s vital infrastructures and their intricate interdependencies; (b) find network vulnerabilities; and (c) run quick simulations and other analyses to find answers to ”what-if” questions.
