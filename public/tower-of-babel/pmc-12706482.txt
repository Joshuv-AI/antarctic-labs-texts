Anastomotic pseudoaneurysm presenting 50 years after common carotid artery–subclavian artery bypass: illustrative case

Common carotid artery (CCA)–subclavian artery bypass is typically performed for CCA occlusive disease.1 The procedure involves grafting, most commonly with a saphenous vein or vascular prosthesis.2 Reported complications include infection, occlusion, anastomotic leakage, and nerve injury, most of which occur within the first 2 years.1 Pseudoaneurysm at the anastomotic site is rare.1 We report the first documented case of an anastomotic pseudoaneurysm occurring 50 years after bypass surgery.

Illustrative Case

History and Presentation

A 76-year-old woman presented to our institution with a progressively enlarging mass in the neck. She had undergone a left CCA–subclavian artery bypass 50 years earlier, with no records of the primary disease available. Given her history of right lower limb paresis as a subsequent complication, we suspected a prior CCA dissection. She showed no signs of local or systemic infection and reported no history of major trauma. She had sustained an ischemic stroke in the left middle cerebral artery territory at the time of the left CCA dissection, which prompted the subsequent carotid artery–subclavian artery bypass. The stroke resulted in persistent right lower limb paresis.

Presurgical Evaluation

CT imaging demonstrated a mass on the left side of the neck at the level of C5–6, which had gradually increased in size over the past 2 years (Fig. 1A and B). MRI with a fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) sequence revealed an ischemic stroke in the middle cerebral artery territory (Fig. 1D). The mass showed partial enhancement on gadolinium-enhanced T1-weighted imaging. For clarity, a schematic illustration was prepared to depict the presumed carotid artery–subclavian artery bypass anastomosis performed 50 years earlier and the site of pseudoaneurysm formation (Fig. 2A). CT angiography demonstrated complete occlusion of the left CCA from its bifurcation, with neither the graft nor mass visualized. The left internal carotid artery (ICA), external carotid artery (ECA), and CCA were maintained via cross-flow through the anterior communicating artery, right ICA, and superior thyroid artery (Fig. 2B). The left CCA was visualized by retrograde filling of its distal portion. Angiography revealed occlusion of both the left CCA and the graft, with the carotid artery bifurcation delineated by retrograde flow via the right ICA and the posterior circulation (Fig. 2C– E). We concluded that the mass was most likely a pseudoaneurysm arising at the bypass anastomotic site. The aneurysm was supplied solely by retrograde blood flow, as both the left CCA and the bypass were occluded at their respective origins. Our surgical strategy was to ligate the ICA-ECA bifurcation to eliminate retrograde blood flow to the anastomotic pseudoaneurysm.

Surgical Findings

Ligation and division of the ICA-ECA bifurcation proved insufficient. In addition, resection of the superior thyroid artery did not eliminate blood supply to the aneurysm (Fig. 3A). An artificial vessel and the proximal portion of the left CCA were identified just proximal to the aneurysm, but the exact anastomosis site could not be detected (Fig. 3B). The pseudoaneurysm received blood flow from vasa vasorum–like microvessels (Fig. 3C). The aneurysm was trapped and removed en bloc. Postoperative CT revealed that the swelling mass had been completely removed (Fig. 1C). The patient was discharged with no neurological deficit after surgery.

Pathological Analysis

On pathological examination, the graft was displaced toward the periphery of the aneurysm (Fig. 4A). The wall consisted of fibrous tissue, confirming the diagnosis of a pseudoaneurysm. The aneurysm lumen was largely filled with both fresh and organized thrombi (Fig. 4B). Foreign materials, such as fibers from the artificial vessel used for the graft and suture threads, were identified within the wall (Fig. 4C). Notably, lymphocytes and neutrophils had migrated toward these materials, eliciting immune responses, including phagocytosis. Microvessels were observed surrounding these immune cells and the artificial materials.

Discussion

Observations

The present report describes the case of a 76-year-old patient who developed an exceptionally delayed pseudoaneurysm at the anastomotic site 50 years after CCA–subclavian artery bypass surgery. CCA–subclavian artery bypass is typically performed for CCA occlusive disease,1 most commonly with a saphenous vein or vascular prosthesis.2 Reported complications include infection, occlusion, anastomotic leakage, and nerve injury,1 most of which occur within the first 2 years.1 Pseudoaneurysm at the anastomotic site is relatively rare.3 To the best of our knowledge, no pseudoaneurysm has been reported beyond 10 years after CCA–subclavian artery bypass.4 A limitation of our report is the absence of detailed records regarding the original surgery and its indication, which reflects the historical nature of an operation performed 5 decades ago. The present case, occurring 50 years postoperatively, thus represents the longest interval ever reported to date. Previous reports have described shorter latency periods with etiologies such as infection, graft degeneration, and mechanical stress.1,5,6 Reviewing these mechanisms may help explain the exceptionally delayed presentation observed in our patient.

Pseudoaneurysms generally arise from minor anastomotic leakage.3,7 Long-term degeneration of artificial prosthetic graft material has been implicated in late leakage and rupture, sometimes decades after surgery.8–9 In the present case, gradual deterioration of the artificial graft over half a century likely resulted in minor leakage, ultimately leading to pseudoaneurysm formation. This case underscores the need for awareness of extremely delayed vascular graft complications, even 50 years after the initial procedure.

Pseudoaneurysm may also develop secondary to infection6 or trauma,5 both of which can provoke chronic inflammation.10 Although our patient exhibited no clinical or laboratory evidence of infection, histopathological analysis revealed immune cell infiltration and phagocytosis within the prosthesis. Moreover, the use of silk sutures—common in vascular surgery several decades ago—has been associated with higher rates of surgical site infection compared with synthetic materials,11 potentially predisposing one to subclinical infection and chronic inflammatory changes. The interplay between graft degeneration and occult infection may have synergistically promoted aneurysm formation in this case. Notably, the lesion was located at the C5–6 level, a region subjected to the greatest cervical mobility.12,13 The patient’s history included no significant trauma; however, repetitive physiological neck motion over decades may have imposed mechanical stress equivalent to repetitive microtrauma, further contributing to vessel wall compromise.

It should also be noted that surgical techniques for carotid artery–subclavian artery bypass have evolved substantially over the past several decades, including the transition from silk to synthetic sutures and improvements in prosthetic graft design. These advances may have reduced the risk of late anastomotic complications in more contemporary practice, underscoring the importance of considering historical context when interpreting this case.

Taken together, this case illustrates that pseudoaneurysm formation can occur even many decades after CCA–subclavian artery bypass surgery. The findings underscore the importance of long-term surveillance in patients with vascular grafts, particularly in older patients, and suggest that mechanical stress, chronic inflammation, and graft material degradation should all be considered in the pathogenesis of extremely delayed vascular complications.

Lessons

A pseudoaneurysm arising 50 years after CCA–subclavian artery bypass represents an unprecedented latency. This case highlights that prosthetic grafts require lifelong clinical surveillance, because extremely delayed complications may result from prosthetic degeneration, chronic inflammation, or occult infection.
