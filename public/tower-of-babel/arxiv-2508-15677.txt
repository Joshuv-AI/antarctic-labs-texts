Understanding ramification of branched ℤ p \mathbb{Z}_{p} -covers
Debanjana Kundu, Katharina Müller

Abstract
. We provide a combinatorial approach to counting the number of spanning trees at the n n -th layer of a branched ℤ p \mathbb{Z}_{p} -cover of a finite connected graph 𝖷 \mathsf{X} . Our method achieves in explaining how the position of the ramified vertices affects the count and hence the Iwasawa invariants. We do so by introducing the notion of segments, segmental decomposition of a graph, and number of segmental t t -tree spanning forests.

1.
Introduction

Let
F=F_{0}
be a number field and fix a prime
p
.
Consider a tower of fields

F=F_{0}\subset F_{1}\subset F_{2}\subset F_{3}\subset\dots\subset\dots F_{\infty}

such that
F_{n}/F
is Galois with
\operatorname{Gal}(F_{n}/F)\simeq\mathbb{Z}/p^{n}\mathbb{Z}
and
\operatorname{Gal}(F_{\infty}/F)\simeq\varprojlim\mathbb{Z}/p^{n}\mathbb{Z}=\mathbb{Z}_{p}
.
K. Iwasawa proved an asymptotic formula for the growth of the
p
-part of the class group in such a tower.
More precisely, he showed that for
n\gg 0
,

\operatorname{ord}_{p}(|\textup{Cl}(K_{n})|)=\mu p^{n}+\lambda n+\nu,

where
\mu
,
\lambda
are non-negative integers and
\nu
is an integer, all of which are independent of
n
.
Here, we write
\operatorname{ord}_{p}(\cdot)
to mean the
p
-adic order (or the
p
-adic valuation).

In recent years this theory was generalized to unramified coverings of finite connected graphs; see for example
[
Gon22
,
Val21
,
DV23
,
Kat23
,
KM23
,
MV23
,
MV24
,
DLRV24
,
KM24a
]
.
Even more recently R. Gambheera–D. Vallierés
[
GV24
]
considered
branched

\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-coverings of finite connected graphs, i.e. coverings where at least one of the vertices is ramified.
Let

\mathsf{X}\longleftarrow\mathsf{X}_{1}\longleftarrow\mathsf{X}_{2}\longleftarrow\dots\longleftarrow\mathsf{X}_{n}\longleftarrow\cdots

be a tower of branched coverings such that the group
\mathbb{Z}/p^{n}\mathbb{Z}
acts without inversion on
\mathsf{X}_{n}/\mathsf{X}
.
Assume that all graphs
\mathsf{X}_{n}
are connected and denote the number of spanning trees of
\mathsf{X}_{n}
by
\kappa(\mathsf{X}_{n})
, then

\operatorname{ord}_{p}(\kappa(\mathsf{X}_{n}))=\mu p^{n}+\lambda n+\nu\text{ for }n\gg 0.

These results have been extended to the case of
\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{d}
-extensions in
[
KM24b
]
.
We will recall all the relevant definitions in Section
2
.

For a finite connected graph, the Kirchhoff Matrix Theorem asserts that the number of spanning trees coincides with the size of the Jacobian of the graph; see
[
BS13
, Theorem 6.2]
.
If a group
G
acts on
\mathsf{X}_{n}/\mathsf{X}
it also acts naturally on the Jacobian, denoted by
\operatorname{Jac}(\mathsf{X}_{n})
turning it into a module over
\mathbb{Z}_{p}[G]
; taking appropriate limits the Jacobian of
\mathsf{X}_{\infty}
is viewed as an Iwasawa module.The Iwasawa invariants in
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-extensions (or
\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{d}
-extensions) are then computed from the determinant of certain matrices associated to the base graph.
During preliminary investigations, we observed that Iwasawa invariants of branched
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-coverings crucially depended on the position of the ramified vertices; see Section
3
.
This raised the following question

Question:
How do the Iwasawa invariants of a branched
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-covering of a finite connected graph
\mathsf{X}
depend on the position of the ramified vertices?
Is there a combinatorial formula to calculate
\kappa(\mathsf{X}_{n})
and hence Iwasawa invariants, which accounts for the position of the ramified vertices?

Main Results

In this article, we study the above questions in reasonable amount of generality.
However, this required us to introduce new notions, definitions, and algorithms.
We now highlight our main results.

Segment

Our first contribution, is to define the notion of a
segment
of a graph; see Section
4.2
.
Given ramified vertices
v,v^{\prime}
in
\mathsf{X}
, naïvely one may think of a segment
\mathsf{S}=\mathsf{S}(v,v^{\prime})
to be the collection of all possible paths between ramified vertices that traverse through unramified vertices
only
; our definition allows the case
v=v^{\prime}
.
If
v=v^{\prime}
, we often refer to the segment as
1
-segment, else we refer to the segment as
2
-segment.
We say that a graph
\mathsf{X}
has a segment decomposition (or is
segment-able
) if we can decompose the graph into disjoint segments (i.e, no two segments can have common edges or common unramified vertices).

There are some natural questions to ask:

Question 1:
Given a graph
\mathsf{X}
, does it always have a segment-decomposition?

Question 2:
How does one find a segment decomposition of
\mathsf{X}
?

To answer the first question, we show that any graph with at most two ramified vertices is always segment-able.
However in general, a graph may not have a segment decomposition.
Regarding the second question, we propose an algorithm called
Segment Algorithm
(SeAl) to find the segment decomposition (when it exists); see Section
4.1
.
It is apparent from the algorithm that if a segment decomposition exists, it is unique.

Counting spanning trees in towers of graphs

For our main results, we define another notion generalizing the concept of spanning trees.
If
\mathsf{S}
is a segment of a graph with
1\leq t\leq 2
ramified vertices we define the notion of
segmental
t
-tree spanning forests
.
These are the ways in which
\mathsf{S}
can be decomposed into
t
trees such that each tree has
exactly one
ramified vertex.
The number of segmental
t
-tree spanning forests will be denoted by
F_{t}(\mathsf{S})
.
Note that if
t=1
, then
F_{1}(\mathsf{S})=\kappa(\mathsf{S})
is the number of spanning trees of
\mathsf{S}
.

Our first main theorem is Theorem
5.12
.
This result succeeds in giving a count of the number of spanning trees at the
n
-th layer of a branched
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-cover of
\mathsf{X}
just by looking at the base graph.
Moreover, this count is combinatorial in nature rather than an ad hoc determinant of the difference of two matrices.
More precisely,

Theorem A
.

Let
\mathsf{X}
be a connected graph with
l
many ramified vertices.
Suppose that
\mathsf{X}
has a segment decomposition with segments
\mathsf{S}^{1},\dots,\mathsf{S}^{k}
.
Arrange the segments such that
\mathsf{S}^{1},\ldots,\mathsf{S}^{k^{\prime}}
are 2-segments and
\mathsf{S}^{k^{\prime}+1},\ldots,\mathsf{S}^{k}
are 1-segments.
Let
\mathsf{X}_{n}
be the
n
-th layer the corresponding
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-tower with trivial voltage assignment.
Assume that all ramified vertices are totally ramified.
Then the number of spanning trees of
\mathsf{X}_{n}
is given by

\kappa(\mathsf{X}_{n})=\kappa(\mathsf{X})\cdot p^{n(l-1)}\prod_{i=1}^{k^{\prime}}F_{2}(\mathsf{S}^{i})^{p^{n}-1}\prod_{j=k^{\prime}+1}^{k}\kappa(\mathsf{S}^{j})^{p^{n}-1}.

Recall that
\operatorname{ord}_{p}(\kappa(\mathsf{X}_{n}))
allows us to determine the Iwasawa invariants.
We are also able to prove some variations of the above theorem.
In Theorem
5.18
we relax the hypothesis that all ramified vertices are totally ramified.
We prove a more general result in Theorem
5.23
where we allow the base graph
\mathsf{X}
to have non-trivial voltage assignment.
In this last case, the calculation of
\kappa(\mathsf{X}_{n})
is more involved; unfortunately it requires knowing the number of segmental spanning forests at the
n
-th layer.

Note that M. Vetluzhskikh and D. Zakharov
[
VZ24
]
prove a theorem for
\kappa(\widetilde{\mathsf{X}})
in terms of
\kappa(\mathsf{X})
and graph matroid for general abelian Galois covers
f\colon\widetilde{\mathsf{X}}\to\mathsf{X}
.
From an Iwasawa theoretic perspective it seems more accessible to use the number of segmental
t
-tree spanning forests instead of matroids.
We expect that some of our results should easily extend to the non-abelian Galois cover setting; especially in the case of a trivial voltage assignment only
|G|
plays a role and not the group itself.

Counting number of segmental spanning forests

It is pertinent from Theorem
A
that we learn how to count the number of segmental
t
-tree spanning forests of the base graph
\mathsf{X}
and also study its growth in
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-towers.
This can become particularly daunting when the base graph has non-trivial voltage assignment; see Example
5.22
.

In Proposition
6.1
we prove a formula for the number of segmental
t
-tree spanning forests of a base graph
\mathsf{X}
using the determinant of a modified Laplacian matrix.
This reinforces the intuition that our notion of segmental
t
-tree spanning forest generalizes that of the spanning tree.
What is even more striking is that the proof of the result requires studying its behaviour in a
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-tower (and then varying over all
p
).

Proposition B
.

Fix
1\leq t\leq 2
distinct (ramified) vertices
\{v_{1},v_{t}\}
of the connected graph
\mathsf{X}
.
Let
\textup{Val}(\mathsf{X})
be the valency matrix and
A(\mathsf{X})
be the adjacency matrix.
Let
M=M(\mathsf{X})
be the matrix obtained from
\textup{Val}(\mathsf{X})-A(\mathsf{X})
by deleting the rows and columns corresponding to
v_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq t
.
Then

F_{t}(\mathsf{X})=\det(M).

In
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-towers we first show that the
p
-part of the number of segmental
t
-tree spanning forests satisfies an Iwasawa-type formula (indeed, a segment of a graph is also a graph).
Moreover, we show the relationship between the Iwasawa invariants of graphs and the segments arising in the segment decomposition.
The following result summarizes the content of Theorem
6.8
and Corollary
6.13
in-text.

Theorem C
.

Let
\mathsf{X}
be a finite connected graph with
l
many ramified vertices such that the ramified vertices are totally ramified.
Suppose that
\mathsf{X}
has a segment decomposition with segments
\mathsf{S}^{1},\dots,\mathsf{S}^{k}
.
For
1\leq i\leq k
, let
\mathsf{S}^{i}
be a segment with
1\leq t_{i}\leq 2
number of ramified vertices.
Writing
\mathsf{S}^{i}_{n}
to denote the
n
-th layer of a
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-tower, there exist constants
\mu_{i},\lambda_{i},\nu_{i}
such that

\operatorname{ord}_{p}(F_{t_{i}}(\mathsf{S}^{i}_{n}))=\mu_{i}p^{n}+\lambda_{i}n+\nu_{i}\text{ for }n\gg 0,

where
\mu_{i},\lambda_{i}
can be made explicit.
Furthermore, if
\mu(\mathsf{X}),\lambda(\mathsf{X})
are the Iwasawa invariants associated with the the graph
\mathsf{X}
then

\mu(\mathsf{X})=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mu_{i}\quad\text{ and }\quad\lambda(\mathsf{X})=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}+l-1.

In short, this paper achieves to show that to study branched
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-covers of a finite connected graph
\mathsf{X}
, it suffices to study simpler pieces called
segments
often just at the base.

Outlook

There are several possible directions in which our results can be extended.
As a first step, a student of the second named author will be implementing the Segmental Algorithm (SeAl).
All of our results require that
\mathsf{X}
has a segment decomposition; we intend to investigate necessary and sufficient conditions for a given graph to have such a decomposition.
We expect that it should be fairly straight forward to extend the results in this paper to the case of more general
p
-adic Lie extensions (at least
\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{d}
-extensions).
Another direction of investigation is to see if our new combinatorial method of counting provides an easier alternative way to study the variation of Iwasawa invariants in branched
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-covers.

Abstractly, we may define the number of tree forest
F_{t}(\mathsf{X})
for a graph
\mathsf{X}
with
t

marked
vertices as the number of decompositions of
\mathsf{X}
into
t
trees such that each tree has exactly one marked vertex.
It seems that an analogue of Proposition
B
can be proven for
\mathsf{X}
with
t
marked vertices even when
t>2
; i.e., it might be possible to count
F_{t}(\mathsf{X})
in terms of the determinant of a modified Laplacian matrix (by removing
t
many rows and columns).
Then the question of interest is what happens to the expression of
\kappa(\mathsf{X}_{n})
in Theorem
A
if
\mathsf{X}
does not have a segment decomposition.
Is there a refined definition of a segment which accommodates
t
many ramified vertices and such that
\mathsf{X}
has a ‘refined segment decomposition’?
Will such a definition allow us to prove a combinatorial expression for
\kappa(\mathsf{X}_{n})
involving
F_{1}(\cdot)
,
F_{2}(\cdot)
, and
F_{t}(\cdot)
when
t>2
?

In
[
KM24b
, Section 5]
, we had studied finite connected planar graphs and their dual in
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-towers.
More precisely, we had considered the following setting: let
\mathsf{X}_{\infty}/\mathsf{X}
be a
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-tower of planar connected graphs with intermediate layers denoted by
\mathsf{X}_{n}
.
Fix a planar embedding for each
\mathsf{X}_{n}
and consider the dual graph
\mathsf{X}^{\vee}_{n}
at each layer.
Suppose further that
\mathsf{X}_{n}^{\vee}/\mathsf{X}^{\vee}
is a branched covering.
Then
\mathsf{X}_{\infty}^{\vee}/\mathsf{X}^{\vee}
is also a
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-tower of planar graphs with intermediate layers
\mathsf{X}_{n}^{\vee}
and
\operatorname{Jac}(\mathsf{X}_{n})\simeq\operatorname{Jac}(\mathsf{X}_{n}^{\vee})
.
It would be interesting to investigate how the segmental decomposition of
\mathsf{X}^{\vee}
is related to that of
\mathsf{X}
, when a dual branched covering exists.

Organization

Including this introduction, the article has seven sections.
Section
2
is preliminary in nature; we define the basic notions pertaining to graph theory and Iwasawa theory.
In this section, we also introduce the matrices that are useful in the study of Iwasawa theory of graphs.
In Section
3
we introduce the motivating example which led to this study and discuss potential questions which are worth investigating.
In Section
4
we introduce the notion of ‘segments’ which is crucial for our study and propose a Segment Algorithm (SeAl) which helps us to find a segment decomposition of a graph, when possible.
Section
5
has the main result(s) of this paper; in particular, we provide a formula for counting the number of spanning trees at the
n
-th layer of a
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-extension in terms of the number of
segmental spanning forests
.
We further show that when the base graph has trivial voltage assignment, then the number of segmental spanning forests
only
depends on the base graph.
In Section
6
we discuss how to extract information about these numbers (and hence about Iwasawa invariants) from determinants of well-understood matrices.
Moreover, we provide a formula for the Iwasawa invariants of a finite connected base graph (which has a segment decomposition) in terms of the Iwasawa invariants of the individual segments.
Finally, in Section
7
we provided examples of classes of graphs where the number of segmental spanning forests can be easily computed.

2.1.
Basic Definitions in Graph Theory

A graph
\mathsf{X}
consists of a vertex set
\mathbf{V}(\mathsf{X})
and a set of
directed edges

\mathbf{E}(\mathsf{X})
along with an
incidence function

\displaystyle\operatorname{inc}\colon\mathbf{E}(\mathsf{X})

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathbf{V}(\mathsf{X})\times\mathbf{V}(\mathsf{X})

\displaystyle e

\displaystyle\mapsto(o(e),t(e))

and an
inversion function

\displaystyle\operatorname{inv}\colon\mathbf{E}(\mathsf{X})

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathbf{E}(\mathsf{X})

\displaystyle e

\displaystyle\mapsto\overline{e}

satisfying the following conditions for all
e\in\mathbf{E}(\mathsf{X})

(i)

\overline{e}\neq e

(ii)

\overline{\overline{e}}=e

(iii)

o(\overline{e})=t(e)
and
t(\overline{e})=o(e)
.

The set of
undirected edges
is obtained by identifying
e
with
\overline{e}
and is denoted by
E(\mathsf{X})
.
We write
E_{v}(\mathsf{X})
to denote the set of all undirected edges adjacent to
v
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\mathsf{X}
and
\mathsf{Y}
be directed graphs.
A
morphism

f\colon\mathsf{Y}\to\mathsf{X}
of graphs consists of two functions
f_{\mathbf{V}}\colon\mathbf{V}(\mathsf{Y})\to\mathbf{V}(\mathsf{X})
and
f_{\mathbf{E}}\colon\mathbf{E}(\mathsf{Y})\to\mathbf{E}(\mathsf{X})
which satisfy the following properties for all
e\in\mathbf{E}(\mathsf{Y})

(i)

f_{\mathbf{V}}(o(e))=o(f_{\mathbf{E}}(e))
,

(ii)

f_{\mathbf{V}}(t(e))=t(f_{\mathbf{E}}(e))
,

(iii)

\overline{f_{\mathbf{E}}(e)}=f_{\mathbf{E}}(\overline{e})
.

We write
f
to denote both
f_{\mathbf{V}}
and
f_{\mathbf{E}}
when there is no chance of confusion.

2.3.
Group action on graphs, Galois theory, and coverings of graphs

Write
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathsf{X})
to denote the group of automorphisms of a graph
\mathsf{X}

Definition 2.4
.

Let
G

\mathsf{X}
be a graph.
The group
G
is said to

\mathsf{X}
if
G

When a group

If

The converse however is not true.

An

Set

The natural morphism of graphs

The notion of branched covering relaxes the condition that the action of

2.4.
Iwasawa theory of
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
tower of graphs obtained from a voltage assignment

2.4.1.

Let
\Gamma
be a multiplicative topological group isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
and we fix a topological generator
\gamma
. It has a filtration

\Gamma\supseteq\Gamma^{p}\supseteq\Gamma^{p^{2}}\supseteq\cdots\supseteq\Gamma^{p^{n}}\supseteq\cdots

such that
\Gamma^{p^{n+1}}\trianglelefteq\Gamma^{p^{n}}
and each subsequent quotient is isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
.

The
Iwasawa algebra

\Lambda=\Lambda(\Gamma)
is the completed group algebra
\mathbb{Z}_{p}\llbracket\Gamma\rrbracket\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\varprojlim_{n}\mathbb{Z}_{p}[\Gamma/\Gamma^{p^{n}}]
.
There exists an isomorphism of rings

\displaystyle\Lambda

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathbb{Z}_{p}\llbracket T\rrbracket

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle\mapsto 1+T.

Let
M
be a finitely generated torsion
\Lambda
-module.
The
Structure Theorem of
\Lambda
-modules
asserts
[
Was97
, Theorem 13.12]
that
M
is pseudo-isomorphic to a finite direct sum of cyclic
\Lambda
-modules.
In other words, there is a homomorphism of
\Lambda
-modules

M\longrightarrow\left(\bigoplus_{i=1}^{s}\Lambda/(p^{m_{i}})\right)\oplus\left(\bigoplus_{j=1}^{t}\Lambda/(f_{j}(x))\right)

with finite kernel and cokernel.
Here,
m_{i}>0
and
f_{j}(x)
is a distinguished polynomial (i.e. a monic polynomial with non-leading coefficients divisible by
p
).
The characteristic ideal of
M
denoted by
\operatorname{char}_{\Lambda}(M)
is (up to a unit) generated by the characteristic element,

f_{M}(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=p^{\sum_{i}m_{i}}\prod_{j}f_{j}(x).

There are two invariants which are important in the study of Iwasawa theory, which are defined as follows:

\mu(M)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\begin{cases}0&\textrm{ if }s=0\\
\sum_{i=1}^{s}m_{i}&\textrm{ if }s>0\end{cases}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\lambda(M)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sum_{j=1}^{t}\deg f_{j}(x).

2.4.2.

Let
\mathsf{X}
be a finite connected graph with vertex set
\mathbf{V}(\mathsf{X})
and set of edges
\mathbf{E}(\mathsf{X})
.
We define a
voltage assignment
function as follows

\alpha\colon\mathbf{E}(\mathsf{X})\longrightarrow\Gamma\textrm{ satisfying }\alpha(\overline{e})=\alpha(e)^{-1}.

For each vertex
v\in\mathbf{V}(\mathsf{X})
, choose a closed subgroup
I_{v}
of
\Gamma
, and define the set

\mathcal{I}=\{(v,I_{v})\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}v\in\mathbf{V}(\mathsf{X})\}.

This gives a graph
\mathsf{X}_{\infty}=\mathsf{X}(\Gamma,\mathcal{I},\alpha)
with vertex set equal to the disjoint union

\mathbf{V}(\mathsf{X}_{\infty})=\bigsqcup_{v\in\mathbf{V}(\mathsf{X})}\{v\}\times\Gamma/I_{v}

and the collection of directed edges is given by

\mathbf{E}(\mathsf{X}_{\infty})=\mathbf{E}(\mathsf{X})\times\Gamma.

Let
g\in\Gamma
.
The directed edge
(e,g)
connects the vertex
(o(e),gI_{o(e)})
to the vertex
(t(e),g\alpha(e)I_{t(e)})
.
Finally, let
\overline{(e,g)}=(\overline{e},g\alpha(e))
.
This is a branched cover
\mathsf{X}_{\infty}\to\mathsf{X}
.
Moreover, the graph
\mathsf{X}_{\infty}
is infinite.

In order to get finite graphs, for each positive integer
n
, consider the natural surjective group morphism
\pi_{n}\colon\Gamma\twoheadrightarrow\Gamma_{n}
, where
\Gamma_{n}=\Gamma/\Gamma^{p^{n}}
.
Set
\alpha_{n}=\pi_{n}\circ\alpha\colon\mathbf{E}(\mathsf{X})\to\Gamma_{n}
and define subgroups

\mathcal{I}_{n}=\{(v,\pi_{n}(I_{v}))\ \mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\ v\in\mathbf{V}(\mathsf{X})\}

of
\Gamma_{n}
indexed by the vertices of
\mathsf{X}
.
This gives a family of graphs
\mathsf{X}_{n}=\mathsf{X}(\Gamma_{n},I_{n},\alpha_{n})
which are finite.
The natural surjective group morphisms
\Gamma_{n+1}\twoheadrightarrow\Gamma_{n}
induce branched covers
\mathsf{X}_{n+1}\to\mathsf{X}_{n}
for each non-negative integer
n
.
Therefore, we obtain a (Galois) tower of graphs

\mathsf{X}=\mathsf{X}_{0}\longleftarrow\mathsf{X}_{1}\longleftarrow\mathsf{X}_{2}\longleftarrow\cdots\longleftarrow\mathsf{X}_{n}\longleftarrow\cdots\longleftarrow\mathsf{X}_{\infty},

where each map
\mathsf{X}_{n+1}\to\mathsf{X}_{n}
is a branched cover satisfying
[\mathsf{X}_{n+1}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathsf{X}_{n}]=p
.
Such a tower will be referred to as a
branched
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-tower of finite graphs
provided that all
\mathsf{X}_{n}
are connected.
When the closed subgroups
I_{v}
are all trivial, the graph
\mathsf{X}_{\infty}=\mathsf{X}(\Gamma,\alpha)
is the
unramified
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-tower
.
When considering the covering map
\pi_{n}\colon\mathsf{X}_{n}\to\mathsf{X}
, we assume that
\pi_{n}\circ g=\pi_{n}
for all
g\in\mathbb{Z}/p^{n}\mathbb{Z}
.
For simplicity, we write
\mathbb{Z}/p^{n}\mathbb{Z}
acts on
\mathsf{X}_{n}/\mathsf{X}
.

Definition 2.7
.

Let
\mathsf{X}
be a finite connected graph with

Let

3.3.
Further directions

The motivating example raises a couple of questions:

Q1:

How does the characteristic ideal change when we introduce a
non-trivial
voltage assignment?

Q2:

Does there exist a formula to explain characteristic ideals for general cycle graphs and an arbitrary number of ramified points?

The first question has a relatively easy answer.
Even if the voltage assignment were non-trivial, the number of spanning trees would not change.
Let us take the example of the graph with ramified vertices
v_{4}
and
v_{5}
.
Observe that no matter what the voltage assignment is, at the
n
-th layer we always have
p^{n}
edges between
v_{4}
and
v_{5}
.
And, for a fixed
\tau
we have
p^{n}
paths of the form

v_{1,\tau}-v_{2,\tau\alpha(e_{1,2})}-v_{3,\tau\alpha(e_{1,2})\alpha(e_{2,3})}-v_{4,\tau\alpha(e_{1,2})\alpha(e_{2,3})\alpha(e_{3,4})}.

As there are no further edges, we obtain the same graph as in the case of a trivial voltage assignment.

Remark 3.1
.

For non-cyclic graphs the voltage assignment may play a role as we see in Section
5.3
.

The answer to the second question is more involved and will be answered in the next section.
But the simple answer is yes, our motivating example can be generalized.

A segment
\mathsf{S}^{\bar{v}}
is defined as above.
A
segment decomposition
of
\mathsf{X}
is the collection of segments

\{\mathsf{S}^{1},\dots,\mathsf{S}^{u},\mathsf{S}^{\bar{v}}\mid\bar{v}\in\bar{\mathbf{V}}(\mathsf{X})\}.

Notation:
Going forward, we will often let

be a graph with segment decomposition

.
This means we have included both the
t
-segments for
t=1,2
.

Remark 4.13
.

(i)

A graph has a segment decomposition in the above sense if and only if it is segment-able, i.e if and only if SeAl returns a coloured graph.
From now on, we will only use the terminology that
\mathsf{X}
has a segment decomposition.

(ii)

Let
\mathsf{X}
be a graph with
l
many ramified vertices.
Suppose that
\mathsf{X}
has a segment decomposition.
Then there are always at least
l-1
many segments.

(iii)

The obstruction for
\mathsf{X}
having a segment decomposition is the presence of an edge in an admissible path between two different pairs of ramified vertices.

Definition 5.1
.

Proposition 5.5
.

Let

First, consider the case that

.

.

into two trees such that

and

Every admissible path from

and
v_{2}

5.2.
Graphs with segment decomposition and trivial voltage assignment

Let
\mathsf{X}
be a connected graph with
l
many ramified vertices.
Suppose that
\mathsf{X}
has a segment decomposition with segments
\mathsf{S}^{1},\dots\mathsf{S}^{k}
.
Let
\mathsf{T}
be a spanning tree of
\mathsf{X}
; set
\mathsf{T}^{i}=\mathsf{T}\cap\mathsf{S}^{i}
.
Without loss of generality, we arrange the segments such that
\mathsf{S}^{1},\ldots,\mathsf{S}^{k^{\prime}}
are 2-segments and
\mathsf{S}^{k^{\prime}+1},\ldots,\mathsf{S}^{k}
are 1-segments.

The following lemma tells us how many of the segments
\mathsf{S}^{i}
can have
\mathsf{T}\cap\mathsf{S}^{i}
as a spanning tree, where
\mathsf{T}
is a fixed spanning tree of
\mathsf{X}
.

Lemma 5.10
.

With notation as before,
\mathsf{T}^{i}
is a spanning tree of the 2-segment
\mathsf{S}^{i}
for
l-1
many indices.

Proof.

For every pair of distinct ramified vertices
(v,v^{\prime})
in
\mathsf{X}
, we mark the unique path
\mathsf{P}(v,v^{\prime})
between them in
\mathsf{T}
.
Now, delete any unmarked edge in
\mathsf{T}
and all those (unramified) vertices that are connected to the ramified ones only through unmarked edges.
Next, if there is a path
\mathsf{P}(v,v^{\prime})
which passes through unramified vertices only, contract the path into one edge between
v
and
v^{\prime}
.
Denote this resulting graph by
\widetilde{\mathsf{T}}
.
By definition, this is a tree with
l
vertices which are precisely the ramified vertices of
\mathsf{X}
.
There is an edge between two ramified vertices
v
and
v^{\prime}
in
\widetilde{\mathsf{T}}
if and only if there exists an index
i
such that

•

v
and
v^{\prime}
belong to a segment
\mathsf{S}^{i}

•

\mathsf{T}^{i}
is a tree.

In particular, the number of edges in
\widetilde{\mathsf{T}}
is the number of indices
i
such that
\mathsf{T}^{i}
is a spanning tree.
But recall that any tree on
l
vertices has
l-1
edges.
The claim follows.
∎

We now prove the main result of this section.
It allows us to count the number of spanning trees at the
n
-th layer of a
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-tower with trivial voltage assignment in terms of combinatorial quantities of the base graph
\mathsf{X}
.
We need to introduce a definition.

Definition 5.11
.

Consider a subset
I\subseteq\{1\leq i\leq k^{\prime}\}
with
\#I=l-1
.
For each set
I
consider the vector
(\mathsf{T}^{i})_{i\in I}
, where each
\mathsf{T}^{i}
is a spanning tree of
\mathsf{S}^{i}
.
Such a vector is called
admissible
if there is a spanning tree
\mathsf{T}
of
\mathsf{X}
such that
\mathsf{T}^{i}=\mathsf{S}^{i}\cap\mathsf{T}
for all
i\in I
.
A set
I
is called
admissible
if there is an admissible vector
(\mathsf{T}^{i})_{i\in I}
.

Theorem 5.12
.

Let
\mathsf{X}
be a connected graph with
l
many ramified vertices.
Suppose that
\mathsf{X}
has a segment decomposition with segments
\mathsf{S}^{1},\dots,\mathsf{S}^{k}
.
Arrange the segments such that
\mathsf{S}^{1},\ldots,\mathsf{S}^{k^{\prime}}
are 2-segments and
\mathsf{S}^{k^{\prime}+1},\ldots,\mathsf{S}^{k}
are 1-segments.
For any segment
\mathsf{S}^{i}
write
t_{i}
to denote the number of ramified vertices.
Let
\mathsf{X}_{n}
be the
n
-th layer the corresponding
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-tower with trivial voltage assignment.
Assume that all ramified vertices are totally ramified.
Then the number of spanning trees of
\mathsf{X}_{n}
is given by

\kappa(\mathsf{X}_{n})=\kappa(\mathsf{X})\cdot p^{n(l-1)}\prod_{i=1}^{k}F_{t_{i}}(\mathsf{S}^{i})^{p^{n}-1}.

Proof.

Fix a spanning tree
\mathsf{T}
of
\mathsf{X}
.
If
t_{i}=2
for a segment

with

Observation:

When

from

\mathsf{T}^{i}
is a spanning tree of
\mathsf{S}^{i}
.

Let

be admissible.
The graph
\mathsf{X}_{n}
consists of

copies of each segment
\mathsf{S}^{i}
.
Thus, for each
i\in I

p^{n}
many possibilities to choose a pre-image of
\mathsf{T}^{i}
that is again connected.
To obtain a

\mathsf{S}^{i}

\mathsf{S}^{i}

Thus for all

\mathsf{T}\cap\mathsf{S}^{i}
is a spanning tree of
\mathsf{S}^{i}
.

The number of spanning trees of
\mathsf{X}_{n}
can now be computed as

\displaystyle\kappa(\mathsf{X}_{n})=

\displaystyle\ \sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subseteq\{1,\dots,k^{\prime}\}\\
|I|=l-1\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(\mathsf{T}^{i})_{i\in I}\\
\text{admissible}\end{subarray}}p^{n(l-1)}\prod_{i\in I}F_{2}(\mathsf{S}^{i})^{p^{n}-1}\prod_{i\notin I}F_{t_{i}}(\mathsf{S}^{i})^{p^{n}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ p^{n(l-1)}\prod_{i=1}^{k}F_{t_{i}}(\mathsf{S}^{i})^{p^{n}-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subseteq\{1,\dots,k^{\prime}\}\\
|I|=l-1\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(\mathsf{T}^{i})_{i\in I}\\
\text{admissible}\end{subarray}}\prod_{i\notin I}F_{t_{i}}(\mathsf{S}^{i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ p^{n(l-1)}\prod_{i=1}^{k}F_{t_{i}}(\mathsf{S}^{i})^{p^{n}-1}\kappa(\mathsf{X}).\qed

.

Let
\mathsf{X}
be a connected graph with
l

\mathsf{X}
has a segment decomposition with segments

and let

be ramified vertices in

A set

Suppose that

As each

,

In particular, there exist spanning trees of
\mathsf{S}^{i}_{n}
.

Proposition 6.1
.

Fix
1\leq t\leq 2
distinct (ramified) vertices

Let

Proof.

Fix a prime
p

Even though this claim concerns the base level we will use a ramified
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-tower to prove it.
Let
\alpha
be a trivial voltage assignment.

Since
\mathsf{X}
has at most two ramified vertices, we know that
\mathsf{X}
has a segment decomposition, say
\mathsf{S}^{1},\ldots,\mathsf{S}^{k}
.
In fact, the segments are
glued
together to obtain
\mathsf{X}
and it follows from Proposition
5.5
that
F_{t}(\mathsf{X})=\prod_{i}F_{t_{i}}(\mathsf{S}^{i})
where each segment has
t_{i}
many ramified vertices.
Let
\mathsf{X}_{n}
be the
n

\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-tower with totally ramified vertices

and
v_{2}
and unramified at all other vertices.
Then by Theorem

\kappa(\mathsf{X}_{n})=p^{n}F_{t}(\mathsf{X})^{p^{n}-1}\kappa(\mathsf{X}).

In particular,

\operatorname{ord}_{p}(\kappa(\mathsf{X}_{n}))=n+\operatorname{ord}_{p}(F_{t}(\mathsf{X}))(p^{n}-1)+\operatorname{ord}_{p}(\kappa(\mathsf{X})).

Let
D^{\prime}
and
B
be the matrices defined in Definition
2.7
(iv–v). As the voltage assignment was taken to be trivial we obtain

\det(D^{\prime}-B)=\det(M)T^{2}.

Furthermore,

\operatorname{ord}_{p}(\kappa(\mathsf{X}_{n}))=n+\operatorname{ord}_{p}(\det(M))p^{n}+\nu\text{ for }n\gg 0.

Comparing with the above formula we obtain
\operatorname{ord}_{p}(\det(M))=\operatorname{ord}_{p}(F_{t}(\mathsf{X}))
.
Varying

proves that

F_{t}(\mathsf{X})=|\det(M)|.

To complete the proof, we need to show that
\det(M)
is in fact non-negative.
Write
M=(\textup{Val}(\mathsf{X})-A)^{\prime}
where the
′
means we delete the two rows/columns corresponding to

.
Note that the Laplacian matrix

is positive semi-definite.
Equivalently by Sylvestor’s Criterion all its principal minors are non-negative.
Recall that a principal minor of a square matrix is one where the indices of the deleted rows are the same as the indices of the deleted columns.
In particular, we have that

.
∎

Let

\mathsf{X}

\det(D^{\prime}-B)=9T^{2}
and therefore,
\mu(\mathsf{X})=2
and
\lambda(\mathsf{X})=1
.
We have calculated
\mu^{(2)}_{i}=1
and
\lambda^{(2)}_{i}=0
for the segments
\mathsf{L}_{i}
with
i=1,2
in Example
6.9
.
We can separately calculate the Iwasawa invariants associated with the Jacobian of the graphs
\mathsf{L}_{i}
.
In particular, a simple calculation shows that
\det(D^{\prime}(\mathsf{L}_{i})-B(\mathsf{L}_{i}))=3T
for
i=1,2
.
Thus,
\mu_{i}=\mu(\mathsf{L}_{i})=1
and
\lambda_{i}=\lambda(\mathsf{L}_{i})=0
for
i=1,2
.

7.
Examples for computing number of segmental
t
-tree spanning forests

7.1.
Line graph with multiple edges

Definition 7.1
.

A
line graph
is a finite undirected graph where
exactly two
vertices have only one neighbor and all other vertices have two neighbors.
Multiple edges between vertices are allowed but there are no loops.

For a line graph
\mathsf{X}
with
k
vertices we always denote the two vertices with only one neighbor by
v_{1}
and
v_{k}
.
We refer to these vertices as the
end-vertices
.

7.1.1.
Multiple edges between adjacent vertices

We start with a graph
\mathsf{X}
as above and arrange all other vertices such that there are only edges between
v_{i}
and
v_{i+1}
.
Set
\mathsf{n}_{i}
to denote the number of edges between
v_{i}
and
v_{i+1}
.

Proposition 7.2
.

Let
\mathsf{X}
be a line graph with
k
vertices with end-vertices
v_{1}
,
v_{k}
.
Suppose that the only edges in this graph are between vertices
v_{i}
and
v_{i+1}

Then

F_{2}(\mathsf{X})=\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}\mathsf{n}_{i}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\frac{1}{\mathsf{n}_{i}}\right)=\kappa(\mathsf{X})\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\frac{1}{\mathsf{n}_{i}}.

Proof.

By definition, every decomposition of
\mathsf{X}
into two trees forces
v_{1}
and
v_{k}
to lie in different trees.
Thus, it suffices to count the number of decompositions of
\mathsf{X}
into two trees.

Note that for every decomposition of
\mathsf{X}
into two trees, there exists a unique index
i
such that
v_{i}
and
v_{i+1}
do not lie in the same tree.
Fix such an
i
; the number of decompositions
equal to

\prod_{j\neq i}\mathsf{n}_{j}=\frac{\kappa(\mathsf{X})}{\mathsf{n}_{i}}.

Indeed, we have to choose one of the
\mathsf{n}_{j}
edges between
v_{j}
and
v_{j+1}
for all
j\neq i
. Thus,

F_{2}(\mathsf{X})=\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\frac{\kappa(\mathsf{X})}{\mathsf{n}_{i}}=\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}\mathsf{n}_{i}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\frac{1}{\mathsf{n}_{i}}\right).\qed

7.1.2.
Multiple edges between non-adjacent vertices

We now consider modified line graphs
\mathsf{X}=\mathsf{L}(k;n,m)
with
k
vertices such that the end vertices are named
v_{1}
and
v_{k}
.
Suppose that every other vertex
v_{j}
with
2\leq j\leq k-1
has exactly one edge with
v_{j-1}
and another edge with
v_{j+1}
.
Choose a vertex
v_{n}
such that
2\leq n\leq k-2
and
v_{m}
such that
m\geq n+2
and draw an edge
e_{v_{n},v_{m}}
.

Lemma 7.3
.

Let
\mathsf{X}=\mathsf{L}(k;n,m)
be a graph as described above.
Then

F_{2}(\mathsf{X})=(m-n)(k-m+n)+(k-m+n-1)=(k-m+n)(m-n+1)-1.

Proof.

If
\mathsf{X}
is divided into two trees
\mathsf{T}_{1}
and
\mathsf{T}_{2}
the following combinations of vertex distribution are possible:

(1)

v_{1}\in\mathbf{V}(\mathsf{T}_{1})
and
v_{n},v_{m},v_{k}\in\mathbf{V}(\mathsf{T}_{2})
.
There are
n-1
many ways of choosing the vertex sets of
\mathbf{V}(\mathsf{T}_{1})
and
\mathbf{V}(\mathsf{T}_{2})
satisfying this condition.

(2)

v_{1},v_{n}\in\mathbf{V}(\mathsf{T}_{1})
and
v_{m},v_{k}\in\mathbf{V}(\mathsf{T}_{2})
.
There are
m-n
many ways of choosing the vertex sets of
\mathbf{V}(\mathsf{T}_{1})
and
\mathbf{V}(\mathsf{T}_{2})
satisfying this condition.

(3)

v_{1},v_{n},v_{m}\in\mathbf{V}(\mathsf{T}_{1})
and
v_{k}\in\mathbf{V}(\mathsf{T}_{2})
.
There are
k-m
many ways of choosing the vertex sets of
\mathbf{V}(\mathsf{T}_{1})
and
\mathbf{V}(\mathsf{T}_{2})
satisfying this condition.

If we are in the first case, for each choice of vertex set there is exactly one way to choose
\mathsf{T}_{1}
but there are
m-n+1
many ways to choose
\mathsf{T}_{2}
.
By symmetry, if we are in the third case, for each choice of vertex set there is exactly one way to choose
\mathsf{T}_{2}
but
m-n+1
many ways to choose
\mathsf{T}_{1}
.
In the second case, once the vertex sets are decided, there is exactly one way to choose both
\mathsf{T}_{1}
and
\mathsf{T}_{2}
.
The total count is

\displaystyle F_{2}(\mathsf{X})

\displaystyle=(n-1)(m-n+1)+(m-n)+(k-m)(m-n+1)

\displaystyle=(m-n)(k-m+n)+(k-m+n-1).\qed

Remark 7.4
.

We label the vertices in a way to avoid both
v_{n}
and
v_{m}
to coincide with the end vertices.
This is because for our purposes, we will later require
v_{1}
and
v_{k}
to be ramified; if
v_{n}=v_{1}
and
v_{m}=v_{k}
then the graph has a segment decomposition and we can use Proposition
5.5
for counting purposes.
Note that, it suffices to consider the case when
v_{1}\neq v_{n}
but
v_{m}=v_{k}
because we can always relabel the vertices.

7.2.
Modified cycle graphs

In the next class of examples, we will count the number of segmental spanning forests of cycle graphs with exactly two ramified vertices and one additional edge.
The purpose of restricting to
two ramified
vertices is because when a cycle graph with an additional edge has three ramified vertices, it quite possible that the graph does not have a segment decomposition.

Let
\mathsf{X}
be an
n
-cycle graph with ramified edges at
v_{1}
and
v_{t}
.
Without loss of generality, we may assume that
2\leq t\leq\lceil\frac{n}{2}\rceil
.
We suppose that there is an edge between the vertices
v_{i}
and
v_{j}
and again without loss of generality we suppose that
i<j
.
We denote such a cycle graph by
\mathsf{C}_{n}(t;i,j)

There are different possibilities for the location of
i,j
(with respect to the ramified vertices
v_{1}
and
v_{t}
) which are as follows (up to relabeling).

(1)

1\leq i,j\leq t
where
j=i+1
.

(2)

1\leq i,j\leq t
where
j>i+1
.

(3)

2\leq i\leq t-1
and
t+1\leq j\leq n
.

Lemma 7.5
.

Consider the graph
\mathsf{X}=\mathsf{C}_{n}(t;i,i+1)
where
1\leq i<t
.
Then

F_{2}(\mathsf{X})=\begin{cases}n-1&\text{ if }i=1\text{ and }t=2\\
(2t-3)(n-t+1)&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Proof.

Note that such a graph
\mathsf{X}
decomposes into three segments if
t=2
and
i=1
.
Else, the graph decomposes into two segments.
In each case, the segments are line graphs in the sense of Definition
7.1
.

We have to divide the proof into few cases:

•

If
t=2
and
i=1
, say
\mathsf{L}_{1}
and
\mathsf{L}_{2}
are the two segments with one edge each and
\mathsf{L}_{3}
has
n-1
edges (and
n
vertices).
Observe that in view of Proposition
5.5

\displaystyle F_{2}(\mathsf{X})

\displaystyle=F_{2}(\mathsf{L}_{1})F_{2}(\mathsf{L}_{2})F_{2}(\mathsf{L}_{3})

\displaystyle=1\times 1\times(n-1).

The count for
F_{2}(\mathsf{L}_{i})
follows from Proposition
7.2
(or just brute force).

•

If the graph decomposes into two segments (say
\mathsf{L}_{1}
and
\mathsf{L}_{2}
) we may assume without loss of generality that
\mathsf{L}_{1}
has
t
many vertices and edges, and
\mathsf{L}_{2}
has
n-t+1
many edges.
Once again using Propositions
5.5
and
7.2
we conclude that

\displaystyle F_{2}(\mathsf{X})

\displaystyle=F_{2}(\mathsf{L}_{1})F_{2}(\mathsf{L}_{2})

\displaystyle=\kappa(\mathsf{L}_{1})\left(t-2+\frac{1}{2}\right)\kappa(\mathsf{L}_{2})(n-t+1)

\displaystyle=(2t-3)(n-t+1).

∎

Proposition 7.6
.

Consider the graph
\mathsf{X}=\mathsf{C}_{n}(t;i,j)
where
1\leq i,j\leq t
and
j\neq i+1
.
Then

F_{2}(\mathsf{X})=\begin{cases}(t-1)(n-t+1)&\text{ if }i=1\text{ and }t=j\\
(n-t+1)[(t-j+i)(j-i+1)-1]&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Proof.

Once again observe that the graph
\mathsf{X}
decomposes into three segments if
i=1
and
j=t
.
Else, the graph decomposes into two segments.
In each case, the segments are line graphs with multiple edges between non-adjacent vertices.
We have to divide the proof into few cases:

•

If
t=j
and
i=1
, we have the segments
\mathsf{L}_{1}
with exactly one edge,
\mathsf{L}_{2}
has
t
many vertices and
t-1
edges, and finally
\mathsf{L}_{3}
has
n-t+1
edges (and
n-t+2
vertices).
Observe that in view of Proposition
5.5

\displaystyle F_{2}(\mathsf{X})

\displaystyle=F_{2}(\mathsf{L}_{1})F_{2}(\mathsf{L}_{2})F_{2}(\mathsf{L}_{3})

\displaystyle=1\times(t-1)\times(n-t+1).

The count for
F_{2}(\mathsf{X})
follows from Proposition
7.2
(or just brute force).

•

If the graph decomposes into two segments (say
\mathsf{L}_{1}
and
\mathsf{L}_{2}
) we may assume without loss of generality that
\mathsf{L}_{1}
has
t
many vertices and edges, and
\mathsf{L}_{2}
has
n-t+1
many edges.
Once again using Propositions
5.5
and
7.2
we conclude that

\displaystyle F_{2}(\mathsf{X})

\displaystyle=F_{2}(\mathsf{L}_{1})F_{2}(\mathsf{L}_{2})

\displaystyle=F_{2}(\mathsf{L}_{1})\kappa(\mathsf{L}_{2})(n-t+1)

\displaystyle=F_{2}(\mathsf{L}_{1})(n-t+1).

But the count for
F_{2}(\mathsf{L}_{1})
is a little more delicate because
\mathsf{L}_{1}
is no longer a line graph but is a modified line graph.
Appealing to Lemma
7.3
we obtain that

F_{2}(\mathsf{X})=(n-t+1)[(t-j+i)(j-i+1)-1].

∎

Remark 7.7
.

We could have provided a uniform proof of Lemma
7.5
and Proposition
7.6
.
In fact, setting
j=i+1
in Proposition
7.6
recovers the earlier result.
For clarity of exposition, we separated the proofs.

Proposition 7.8
.

Consider the graph
\mathsf{X}=\mathsf{C}_{n}(t;i,j)
where
2\leq i\leq t-1
and
t+1\leq j\leq n
.
Then

F_{2}(\mathsf{X})=(n-j+1)[(i-1)(j-i+1)+(t-i)]+(j-t)[(t-i)(n-j+i+2)+(i-1)]

Proof.

In this case
\mathsf{X}
is a segment by itself.
The counting argument is similar to Lemma
7.3
.
If
\mathsf{X}
is divided into two trees
\mathsf{T}_{1}
and
\mathsf{T}_{2}
the following combinations of vertex distribution are possible:

(1)

v_{1}\in\mathbf{V}(\mathsf{T}_{1})
and
v_{i},v_{t},v_{j}\in\mathbf{V}(\mathsf{T}_{2})
.
There are
(n-j+1)(i-1)
many ways of choosing the vertex sets of
\mathbf{V}(\mathsf{T}_{1})
and
\mathbf{V}(\mathsf{T}_{2})
satisfying this condition.

(2)

v_{1},v_{i}\in\mathbf{V}(\mathsf{T}_{1})
and
v_{t},v_{j}\in\mathbf{V}(\mathsf{T}_{2})
.
There are
(t-i)(n-j+1)
many ways of choosing the vertex sets of
\mathbf{V}(\mathsf{T}_{1})
and
\mathbf{V}(\mathsf{T}_{2})
satisfying this condition.

(3)

v_{1},v_{j}\in\mathbf{V}(\mathsf{T}_{1})
and
v_{i},v_{t}\in\mathbf{V}(\mathsf{T}_{2})
.
There are
(j-t)(i-1)
many ways of choosing the vertex sets of
\mathbf{V}(\mathsf{T}_{1})
and
\mathbf{V}(\mathsf{T}_{2})
satisfying this condition.

(4)

v_{1},v_{i},v_{j}\in\mathbf{V}(\mathsf{T}_{1})
and
v_{t}\in\mathbf{V}(\mathsf{T}_{2})
.
There are
(t-i)(j-t)
many ways of choosing the vertex sets of
\mathbf{V}(\mathsf{T}_{1})
and
\mathbf{V}(\mathsf{T}_{2})
satisfying this condition.

If we are in the first case, for each choice of vertex set there is exactly one way to choose
\mathsf{T}_{1}
but there are
j-i+1
many ways to choose
\mathsf{T}_{2}
.
By symmetry, if we are in the fourth case, for each choice of vertex set there is exactly one way to choose
\mathsf{T}_{2}
but
i+1+(n-j+1)=n-j+i+2
many ways to choose
\mathsf{T}_{1}
.
In the second and third cases, once the vertex sets are decided, there is exactly one way to choose both
\mathsf{T}_{1}
and
\mathsf{T}_{2}
.
The total count is

\displaystyle F_{2}(\mathsf{X})

\displaystyle=(n-j+1)(i-1)(j-i+1)+(t-i)(j-t)(n-j+i+2)+(t-i)(n-j+1)+(j-t)(i-1)

\displaystyle=(n-j+1)[(i-1)(j-i+1)+(t-i)]+(j-t)[(t-i)(n-j+i+2)+(i-1)].\qed

7.3.
Complete graphs

Let us denote the complete graph on
n
-vertices by
\mathsf{K}(n)
.
Suppose that there are two ramified vertices, namely
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
.
Then
\mathsf{X}
has a segment decomposition and we can compute
F_{2}(\mathsf{K}(n))
using an iterative formula which depends on the number of spanning trees of
\mathsf{K}(i)
for
1\leq i<n
.
We denote this number by
\kappa_{i}=i^{i-2}
.
As will be clear from the proof, the position of the ramified vertices does not matter because every vertex is connected to every other vertex and we can relabel the vertices as desired.

Proposition 7.9
.

Let
n\geq 2
.
Let
\mathsf{X}=\mathsf{K}(n)
with ramified vertices at
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
.
Then

F_{2}(\mathsf{K}(n))=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\binom{n-2}{i-1}\cdot\kappa_{i}\cdot\kappa_{n-i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\binom{n-2}{i-1}\cdot i^{i-2}\cdot{(n-i)}^{n-i-2}.

Proof.

The way to prove this is similar to some of our previous counts, so we only provide a brief sketch.
If
\mathsf{X}=\mathsf{K}(n)
is divided into two trees
\mathsf{T}_{1}
and
\mathsf{T}_{2}
such that
v_{i}\in\mathsf{T}_{i}
, the following combinations of vertex distribution are possible:

(1)

\mathbf{V}(\mathsf{T}_{1})=v_{1}
and
|\mathbf{V}(\mathsf{T}_{2})|=n-1
.
There is exactly one way to choose these vertex sets.
Notice that the number of spanning trees of
\mathsf{T}_{1}
is clearly
\kappa_{1}=1
.
On the other hand, the graph that remains after removing
v_{1}
from
\mathsf{X}
is a complete graph on
n-1
vertices.
So the contribution to the count is

1\cdot\kappa_{1}\cdot\kappa_{n-1}.

(2)

|\mathbf{V}(\mathsf{T}_{1})|=2
and
|\mathbf{V}(\mathsf{T}_{2})|=n-2
.
There are exactly
\binom{n-2}{1}
many ways of choosing the vertex sets.
Then the number of spanning trees of
\mathsf{T}_{1}
is clearly
\kappa_{2}=1
.
On the other hand, the graph that remains after removing
\mathbf{V}(\mathsf{T}_{1})
from
\mathsf{X}
is a complete graph on
n-2
vertices.
So the contribution to the count is

\binom{n-2}{1}\cdot\kappa_{2}\cdot\kappa_{n-2}.

(3)

We continue this way till we obtain the vertex distribution
\mathbf{V}(\mathsf{T}_{2})=v_{2}
and
|\mathbf{V}(\mathsf{T}_{1})|=n-1
.

The result is then immediate.
∎
