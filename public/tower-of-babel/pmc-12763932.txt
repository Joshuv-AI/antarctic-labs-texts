Chronic kidney disease associated pruritus and healthcare resource utilization in European hemodialysis patients: results from an electronic patient-reported outcome survey
Authors: James Fotheringham, Charlotte Tu, Ronald L. Pisoni, Sammantha Fuller, Thilo Schaufler, Despina Ruessmann, Marc G. Vervloet, Roberto Pecoits-Filho, Angelo Karaboyas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChronic kidney disease-associated pruritus (CKD-aP) is highly prevalent among hemodialysis (HD) patients, yet frequently underreported and underdiagnosed. While CKD-aP is associated with adverse patient-reported and clinical outcomes, the relationship between incident, persistent, and resolving CKD-aP rather than only severity and healthcare resource utilization (HCRU) has not been well explored.MethodsWe analyzed 2,631 electronic patient-reported outcome (ePRO) surveys from 747 HD patients in six European countries participating in the Dialysis Outcomes and Practice Patterns Study (DOPPS) phase 7 (2021–2023). Patients completed surveys every three months for up to 18 months. Logistic regression estimated associations of pruritus severity and evolution with outcomes including self-reported pruritus treatment, non-nephrologist physician visits, and a composite of hospitalization and emergency room (ER) visit within three months.ResultsWithin three months of pruritus assessment, patients moderately bothered by pruritus had ER visit, hospitalization, and skin infection rates of 4%, 12%, and 11%, respectively; those very much or extremely bothered had rates of 10%, 15%, and 6%, compared to 5%, 8%, and 4% in patients not at all bothered. Patients developing incident pruritus over three months were more likely to report a skin infection than those without pruritus (OR = 2.34, 95% CI: 1.11, 4.90). Patients very much or extremely bothered were most likely to visit a dermatologist (13%), especially those persistently bothered three months later. Use of over-the-counter oral medications (e.g., antihistamines) was highest among patients very much or extremely bothered (21%) and those persistently bothered.ConclusionDespite high symptom burden, most patients with bothersome CKD-aP remained untreated. High prevalences of ER visits, hospitalization, and skin infection among patients bothered by itch in cross-sectional and longitudinal analyses suggest identifying incident and persistent CKD-aP may be as important as quantifying severity, arguing for serial measurement and intervention at lower severity to prevent downstream HCRU.

Background

Chronic kidney disease-associated pruritus (CKD-aP) – itching related to chronic kidney disease (CKD) – has been extensively studied in the CKD and hemodialysis (HD) population. It has most recently been shown to affect 50% of patients with CKD [1] and 67% of patients on HD [2]. CKD-aP has been associated with adverse patient-reported outcomes (PROs) such as depressive symptoms, poor sleep quality, and decreased physical and mental measures of health-related quality of life (HRQoL) [2–4], and a potential causal relation with these outcomes is acknowledged. CKD-aP is also associated with adverse clinical outcomes such as higher rates of mortality and hospitalization, prolonged recovery time after a dialysis session, and a higher likelihood of skipping dialysis sessions or withdrawing from dialysis [2].

Most studies assessing CKD-aP have relied on a single assessment, focusing on prevalence and CKD-aP’s associations with patient-reported and clinical outcomes over time [5]. Because CKD-aP is frequently underreported by patients, underdiagnosed by physicians, and under-recognized in dialysis units [4], treatment, if offered at all, is often delayed. Historically 18% of patients who were always or nearly always bothered by itchy skin reported taking no treatment for pruritus [4]. While approved therapies now exist, the lack of a standard process of assessing and reassessing pruritus may mean these statistics are still valid.

Beyond the well-documented effects of CKD-aP on patient-reported outcomes including quality of life and sleep quality—both of which have been shown to improve with treatment for CKD-aP [6, 7], the downstream impacts of CKD-aP on health-care resource utilization (HCRU) present an opportunity to demonstrate the value of timely and effective intervention.

In this study, we use frequent electronic assessments of pruritus to evaluate the association between its evolution (incident development, persistence and resolution), severity, and subsequent HCRU using several measures, including physician visits for CKD-aP, depression or poor sleep, and hospitalization or emergency-room visits. Greater understanding of these longitudinal relationships could aid healthcare professionals in deciding at what timepoint or severity to initiate effective, evidence-based treatments for CKD-aP [8, 9].

Methods

We analyzed data from an electronic patient-reported outcome (ePRO) prospective ancillary study nested within the Dialysis Outcomes and Practice Patterns Study (DOPPS) phase 7 (2021–2023), a multicenter, international study of adult chronic in-center HD patients [10, 11]. The ancillary ePRO study was conducted at European DOPPS study sites in 6 countries: France, Germany, Italy, Spain, Sweden, and the UK. DOPPS participants – including incident and prevalent HD patients – were invited to provide additional informed consent to participate in the ePRO study, which involved completing quarterly questionnaires over an 18-month period. Recruitment and data collection began in September 2021 and remained ongoing until administrative study end in August 2023. The ePRO survey assessed multiple patient-reported symptoms and health-related quality of life (HRQOL), focused primarily on CKD-aP, with CKD-aP instruments described previously [12].

Among the 1,611 participants enrolled in DOPPS phase 7, 1,288 were actively enrolled at the time of ePRO launch and thus approached for ePRO participation, and 981 (80%) consented and enrolled. Participants agreed to complete a brief ePRO questionnaire each quarter during the 18-month data collection period. The data collected from the ePRO questionnaires were integrated into the larger DOPPS database, which includes detailed patient demographics, comorbidities, laboratory measures, and prospective follow-up for clinical outcomes.

The exposure of interest for this analysis was self-reported pruritus severity. At each 3-month interval, patients were asked to rate the extent to which they were bothered by itchy skin during the past four weeks (1. Not at all, 2. Somewhat, 3. Moderately, 4. Very much, 5. Extremely), based on a question extracted from the Kidney Disease Quality of Life 36-item short form survey (KDQOL-36) [13], though the entirety of the KDQOL-36 was not included in this pruritus-focused questionnaire. The Very Much and Extremely groups were combined to avoid increased variability due to sparse cells. Changes in pruritus severity between 3-month intervals were defined based on whether patients were at least moderately bothered by itchy skin at one ePRO and another ePRO 3 months later; patients were grouped into 4 categories: Persistent (Yes/Yes), Resolved (Yes/No), Incident (No/Yes), and Absent (No/No) pruritus. These categories are time-varying, with data from all 3-month intervals utilized for analyses – not just the first 2 available measurements but between all 3-month periods.

Self-reported outcomes related to physician visits included questions asking whether the patient had (1) Visited a dermatologist or other non-nephrologist physician (e.g., general practitioner) for itchy skin in the past 3 months; and (2) Visited a psychiatrist, other physician, or therapist for symptoms of depression or poor sleep quality in the past 3 months. Response options were 0, 1, or 2 + visits, but these were consolidated to 0 and 1 + for the purposes of this analysis.

Patients were also asked whether they had a skin infection in the past 3 months. In contrast, emergency room (ER) visits and inpatient hospitalizations were captured by linking ePRO data with the larger DOPPS database. All-cause, cardiovascular-related, infection-related, and skin infection related events were defined. A composite outcome of all-cause ER visit and all-cause hospitalization was defined and considered a key outcome.

Patient characteristics at ePRO study enrollment were summarized by baseline pruritus severity. All subsequent analyses utilized all available ePRO surveys, multiple per patient, up to 7 surveys (month 0, 3, 6, 9, 12, 15, 18). Missing data were excluded, and complete case analyses were conducted. Self-reported HCRU (asking about the prior 3 months) was summarized by country and by pruritus severity captured on the prior ePRO survey. Self-reported HCRU (between the two ePRO surveys) was also summarized by change in pruritus severity between the two ePRO surveys.

Logistic regression was used to estimate the association between pruritus severity—and change in pruritus severity— and a composite outcome of all-cause ER visit and all-cause hospitalization over the following 3 months. All models were adjusted for country, age, sex, dialysis vintage, and the presence of each of the 12 comorbidities listed in Table 1. All analyses were conducted using SAS software, version 9.4 (SAS institute, Cary, NC).

Results

Patient characteristics

This analysis included 2428 total quarterly ePRO surveys across 747 unique HD patients. The median (interquartile range [IQR]) number of surveys completed per patient was 3 (1, 4). Over one-quarter (26%) of patients were at least moderately bothered by itchy skin at baseline, including 10% who were very much or extremely bothered. Patients with more severe pruritus tended to have longer dialysis vintage; most other patient characteristics, including serum phosphorus, varied minimally across pruritus severity (Table 1).

Hospitalization was observed within 3 months after survey completion in 10% of cases, and an ER visit was observed in 5% of cases. Among patients very much or extremely bothered by itchy skin, these numbers increased to 15% and 10%, respectively (Table 5). Compared to the reference group of not at all bothered, the adjusted OR (95% CI) for the composite outcome of ER and hospitalization was 1.27 (0.95, 1.71) for somewhat bothered, 1.34 (0.93, 1.93) for moderately bothered, and 1.74 (1.17, 2.57) for very much or extremely bothered by itchy skin. Using longitudinal data from consecutive surveys, the risk of hospitalization in the 3 months following any assessment of pruritus severity was 8% for respondents where pruritus remained absent compared to 14% for respondents who developed incident pruritus; hospitalization risk was 15% for those whose pruritus persisted and 11% for those whose pruritus resolved. A similar trend was observed with patient-reported skin infection, with 3-month risks of 3%, 7%, 11% and 7% for absence, incident, persistent and resolved respondents, respectively. Discussion

This international cohort study provides valuable insights into the evolution of pruritus and its impact on treatment patterns and HCRU. By assessing pruritus at frequent intervals, this study identified an unmet need in pruritus management, highlighted gaps in treatment efficacy, and demonstrated how changes in symptom severity influence medication use. Notably, patients who developed pruritus showed similar HCRU patterns to those with persistent pruritus, underscoring the need for earlier intervention and regular symptom assessment. Despite the availability of approved therapies, our findings suggest that pruritus remains under-recognized and undertreated, with limited specialist referrals and persistently high symptom burden among affected patients.

The majority of patients, even those with the most severe pruritus symptoms, remained untreated. Specialist referrals were infrequent, and even among those who sought care, pruritus often persisted. Patients generally initiated antihistamines and emollients promptly when pruritus developed but discontinued them at high rates once symptoms resolved. Notably, patients who were at least moderately bothered by itchy skin were more likely to have a skin infection, ER visit, or hospitalization within 3 months of survey completion. However, patients with incident or persistent pruritus—regardless of severity—experienced similar HCRU rates to those with the most extreme baseline severity.

Topical therapy such as emollients or creams are often recommended as a first-line treatment for CKD-aP [14], given their minimal risk of side effects due to lack of systemic absorption. In our study, nearly 50% of patients very much/extremely bothered by itchy skin used topical creams – increasing to 57% among those whose pruritus was persistent (Table 4). Since dry skin affects more than 70% of those reporting pruritus [4], and there is no formal guidance on when to discontinue emollients, it is likely that patients independently decide whether to continue or stop usage based on symptom progression.

Despite the potential benefits of specialist evaluation, only 13% of patients with severe pruritus sought care from a dermatologist or other non-nephrologist physician. While pruritus has a broad differential diagnosis—including infestations, systemic conditions (hypercalcemia and cholestasis), and dermatologic conditions (psoriasis, dermatophytosis, and bullous pemphigoid, among others) [15]—these conditions are relatively rare in dialysis patients (< 5%). Improved education on the link between pruritus and CKD may enhance patient reporting and encourage timely intervention. However, it remains unclear whether the 87% of patients with severe pruritus who did not seek specialist care had discussed their symptoms with their nephrologist or if they had refrained from reporting them altogether.

The longitudinal relationship between Persistent, Incident, Resolved, and Absent pruritus and PROs and hospitalization has been demonstrated in studies using annual assessments [5]: The prevalence of depression increases with incident pruritus and decreases upon resolution, while patients with Persistent pruritus exhibit the highest rates, mirroring our findings on access to healthcare professionals for depressive symptoms. A similar pattern exists for other PROs, such as restless sleep, prolonged recovery from a dialysis session, and physical and mental quality of life measures [5]. While prior studies have linked severe pruritus with higher rates of hospitalization [2], and particularly high hospitalization rates among patients with persistent pruritus [5], our study extends these findings by also evaluating ER visits.

There are a few limitations worth noting. First, recall or misclassification bias may have influenced the results, particularly in relation to self-reported pruritus symptoms and HCRU. Second, the association between pruritus and skin infection is bidirectional—while infections may contribute to pruritus, persistent scratching may also increase the risk of skin infections. Third, as an observational study, our findings are limited to associations rather than causal relationships. While pruritus severity is clearly linked with increased healthcare utilization, it is important to recognize that this association may be influenced by multiple confounding factors. Patients with more severe pruritus could also experience higher overall morbidity related to progressive kidney disease, such as cardiovascular issues, infections, or frailty. These factors, in addition to the psychological burden of pruritus, may contribute to increased healthcare utilization independent of the itching itself. Furthermore, symptoms such as depression, sleep disturbances, and fatigue often co-occur with pruritus in CKD patients, and these overlapping factors may amplify the observed associations with healthcare visits, hospitalizations, or emergency room use. For example, depressive symptoms are frequently observed in patients with CKD, and depression itself is a well-documented risk factor for higher healthcare utilization [16]. While other PROs (e.g., sleep quality, depression, fatigue) were included in the questionnaire, we did not account for these factors in the analysis because it would not be epidemiologically appropriate to adjust for factors likely downstream of itch. Adjustment for these factors more likely to be mediators than confounders would introduce bias into the models, and thus while these additional symptoms are important and relevant to itch, a more comprehensive exploration of them is beyond the scope of this manuscript. Additionally, our study period (2021–2023) preceded the routine availability of difelikefalin in most European markets, and as such, the current treatment landscape and awareness may have evolved.

Despite these limitations, our study has notable strengths. Unlike many prior studies, we captured data not only on hospitalizations but also on emergency department visits. Additionally, our study design allowed for direct patient-reported data on treatment use—including OTC medications (not captured by most data sources), topical creams, skin infections, and visits to specialists for itchy skin and sleep quality—filling gaps often left by administrative data sources. This potentially mitigates issues around treatment cessation not being captured by the supervising facility. Furthermore, the frequent 3-month survey intervals provide a more precise assessment of pruritus progression and its relationship with healthcare utilization, in contrast to prior studies relying on baseline severity assessments with follow-ups spanning a year or longer. The shorter assessment intervals in our study strengthen the observed link between pruritus evolution and HRCU, highlighting the potential benefits of more frequent symptom monitoring. More regular surveillance and earlier intervention, even in patients with lower initial severity, may improve patient reported outcomes while also mitigating downstream HCRU.

Beyond the scope of our current study, broader symptom monitoring tools like the IPOS-Renal (Integrated Palliative care Outcome Scale for Renal patients) may provide a more comprehensive, integrated approach to symptom monitoring, capturing fatigue, pain, and sleep disturbances, within a singular, validated tool. The use of such tools, particularly with more frequent assessments (quarterly vs. annual), could enhance patient care by offering a holistic view of symptom clusters and informing clinical and research strategies in both dialysis and non-dialysis populations.

Importantly, while our study focused on dialysis patients, pruritus is also a common and significant symptom in patients with CKD who have not yet initiated dialysis. Severe pruritus in non-dialysis CKD patients can impact quality of life and may be a contributing factor in the decision to initiate dialysis. Extending pruritus monitoring and intervention strategies to patients not on dialysis could provide benefits to a wider CKD population and may ultimately reduce HCRU across different stages of CKD. Future studies should investigate how symptom management strategies for pruritus could impact both dialysis and non-dialysis populations, potentially influencing treatment decisions and patient outcomes across a broader spectrum of CKD stages. Additionally, prospective studies examining the assumed benefit of prompt intervention for chronic pruritus in ESKD patients could determine if this strategy improves patient outcomes and reduces downstream healthcare utilization compared to patients who do not receive prompt treatment.

In conclusion, our findings demonstrate that beyond baseline severity, multiple domains of HRCU vary with longitudinal changes in pruritus when measured at 3-month intervals. This emphasizes that pruritus is not only a key patient-reported outcome closely tied with other PROs but also a condition with cascading clinical and economic consequences. Further research should explore the impact of CKD-aP treatment and symptom resolution on these downstream healthcare outcomes and resource utilization.
