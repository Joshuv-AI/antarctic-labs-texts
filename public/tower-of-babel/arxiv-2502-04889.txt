Any-stepsize Gradient Descent for Separable Data under Fenchel–Young Losses
Han Bao, Shinsaku Sakaue, Yuki Takezawa

Abstract
The gradient descent (GD) has been one of the most common optimizer in machine learning. In particular, the loss landscape of a neural network is typically sharpened during the initial phase of training, making the training dynamics hover on the edge of stability. This is beyond our standard understanding of GD convergence in the stable regime where stepsize is chosen sufficiently smaller. Recently, Wu et al. [63] have shown that GD converges with much larger stepsize under linearly separable logistic regression. Although their analysis hinges on the self-bounding property of the logistic loss, which seems to be a cornerstone to establish a modified descent lemma, our pilot study shows that other loss functions without the self-bounding property can make GD attain arbitrarily small loss with large stepsize. To further understand what property of a loss function matters in GD, we aim to show large-stepsize GD convergence for a general loss function based on the framework of Fenchel–Young losses . We essentially leverage the classical perceptron argument to derive the iteration complexity for achieving ε \varepsilon -optimal loss, which is possible for a majority of Fenchel–Young losses. This convergence result highlights that the self-bounding property may not be necessary for GD to attain arbitrarily small loss. Moreover, when a loss function entails separation margin , a notion relevant to the margin in support vector machines, GD often yields faster convergence than typical GD rate T = Ω ⁡ ( ε − 1 ) T=\Omega(\varepsilon^{-1}) for convex smooth objectives. Specifically, GD with the Tsallis entropy attains ε \varepsilon -optimal loss with the rate T = Ω ( ε − 1 / 2 ) T=\Omega(\varepsilon^{-1/2}) , and the Rényi entropy achieves the far better rate T = Ω ( ε − 1 / 3 ) T=\Omega(\varepsilon^{-1/3}) .

1.1
Related work

Gradient descent with large stepsize has attracted attention recently.
Specifically, non-monotonic behaviors of loss functions
[
65
]
and the sharpness adaptivity to loss landscapes
[
34
,
18
]
have been observed empirically.
It was argued that the sharpness tends to initially increases until the classical stable regime breaks down, and hovers on this boundary, termed as the edge of stability
[
18
]
.
This observation mainly sparks two questions: why the loss landscape hovers on the EoS, and why converging.
Answering either question must go beyond the classical optimization theory under the stable regime.

On why hovering on the EoS, let us make a brief review, though it is not a central focus of this paper:
Ahn et al. [2]
is a seminal work to empirically investigate the homogeneity of loss functions contributes to maintain the EoS.
Later, it was showed that normalized GD (represented by scale-invariant losses) adaptively leads their intrinsic stepsize toward sharpness reduction
[
36
]
.
The sharpness fluctuation is often attributed to the non-negligible third-order Taylor remainder of the loss landscape
[
37
,
20
]
.

We rather focus on why GD attains arbitrarily small loss with much larger stepsize.
In this line, previous studies show convergence based on specific models such as multi-scale loss function
[
32
]
, quadratic functions
[
5
]
, matrix factorization
[
59
,
17
]
, a scalar multiplicative model
[
68
,
33
]
, a sparse coding model
[
3
]
, and linear logistic regression
[
62
]
.
Among them, we advocate the logistic regression setup proposed by
Wu et al. [62]
because it is relevant to implicit bias of GD
[
54
,
29
,
46
]
, and moreover,
Wu et al. [63]
corroborates the benefit of large stepsize in GD convergence rate.
Our work is provoked by
Wu et al. [63]
, questioning what structure in a loss function leads GD to arbitrarily small loss.
Indeed, we observe in
Figure

1
that loss functions without the self-bounding property (
1
) can make GD attain arbitrarily small loss, though the self-bounding property seems essential to calm the EoS down to the stable phase
[
63
]
as well as to establish the max-margin directional convergence
[
29
,
46
]
.
A similar question to ours is raised by
Tyurin [58]
, who argues that the stable convergence of large-stepsize logistic regression might be an artifact due to the functional form of the logistic loss—eventually
Tyurin [58]
argued that large-stepsize logistic regression behaves like the classical perceptron.
To this end, we show in

that arbitrary-stepsize GD can converge under a wide range of losses even without the self-bounding property (
1
),
and moreover, occasionally yielding a better rate than the classical stable convergence rate.
We discuss it more in
Section

5
.
Note that one work attempts to extend the separable logistic regression setup to the non-separable one
[
41
]
; yet, we still do not have satisfactory results beyond the one-dimensional case.
Due to its intricateness, we follow the separable case.

Lastly, our work benefits the study of regret bounds of surrogate losses
[
9
,
1
,
23
,
6
,
38
,
8

A surrogate regret bound connect a surrogate loss to a downstream task loss, while the optimization error of the surrogate loss is usually ignored.

Preliminary on Fenchel–Young losses

Fenchel–Young losses have been introduced by
Blondel et al. [11]
as a general class of surrogate losses for structured prediction,
which are classification-calibrated
[
60
]
.
This can be seen as a Bregman divergence comparing primal and dual points
[
25
,
4
]
.
Despite that the logistic and hinge losses are widely prevailing in practice, we can improve the performance of some prediction tasks by changing specific Fenchel–Young losses, as reported by
Roulet et al. [50]

We choose Fenchel–Young losses because a vast majority of convex, Lipschitz, and classification-calibrated losses are included in this class—otherwise, GD convergence is hardly obtained beyond the edge of stability.
Moreover, the separation margin property, one of the key features of Fenchel–Young losses, controls GD behaviors significantly.

Definition 2
.

Let
\Omega\colon\mathbb{R}^{K}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{{\infty}\right\}
be a potential function.
The
Fenchel–Young loss

\ell_{\Omega}\colon\domain(\Omega^{*})\times\domain(\Omega)\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}

is defined as

\ell_{\Omega}(\bm{\theta};\bm{\mu})\coloneqq\Omega^{*}(\bm{\theta})+\Omega(\bm{\mu})-\left\langle{\bm{\theta}},{\bm{\mu}}\right\rangle.

In multiclass classification,
\ell_{\Omega}(\bm{\theta};\bm{\mu})
measures the proximity between a score
\bm{\theta}
and a target label
\bm{\mu}=\mathbf{e}_{i}
(for a class
i\in[K]
).
By definition,
\ell_{\Omega}(\cdot,\bm{\mu})
is convex for any
\bm{\mu}\in\domain(\Omega)
.
Moreover,
\ell_{\Omega}(\bm{\theta};\bm{\mu})=0
holds if and only if
\bm{\mu}\in\partial\Omega^{*}(\bm{\theta})
due to the equality condition of the Fenchel–Young inequality.

We follow
[
11
, Section 4.4]
to consider binary (
K=2
) loss functions.
The following set of assumptions is imposed on a potential function
\Omega
.
The asymmetric generalization is possible, but we choose to keep the analysis simpler so that we can focus more on the essence of GD convergence.

Assumption 1
.

For a potential function

3.1
Proof outline

The proof of

essentially relies on the perceptron convergence analysis

and the asymptotical order evaluation of rate functions

.
We sketch the proof in this section to highlight the structure of the GD convergence in our setup and complete the proof in

When we show the convergence of perceptron, we leverage an inequality of the following type:

\underbrace{C_{\text{L}}t\leq\left\langle{\mathbf{w}_{t}},{\mathbf{w}_{*}}\right\rangle}_{(\clubsuit)}\leq\underbrace{\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|\leq C_{\text{U}}(t)}_{(\diamondsuit)}\quad\text{for $t\geq 1$},

(4)

where
C_{\text{L}}>0
is a non-degenerate constant independent of
t
.
The inequality
(\clubsuit)
holds only while perceptron misclassifies some examples.
Thus, perceptron correctly classifies all examples after at most
T
iterations such that
C_{\text{L}}T>C_{\text{U}}(T)
.
Such
T
exists as long as
C_{\text{U}}(t)
is sublinear in
T
.

When it comes to our setup, an inequality
(\clubsuit)
is obtained by recursively expanding the update (
GD
)
3
3

3

This expansion relies on
\left\langle{\mathbf{w}_{*}},{\mathbf{z}_{i_{t-1}}}\right\rangle\geq\gamma
, namely, the linear separability in
Assumption

2
.
To lift the linear separability assumption, we may require to introduce additional distributional assumptions here.

\left\langle{\mathbf{w}_{t}},{\mathbf{w}_{*}}\right\rangle\geq\left\langle{\mathbf{w}_{t-1}},{\mathbf{w}_{*}}\right\rangle+\frac{\gamma\eta}{n}g(\left\langle{\mathbf{w}_{t-1}},{\mathbf{z}_{i_{t-1}}}\right\rangle)\geq\dots\geq\frac{\gamma\eta}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{t-1}g(\left\langle{\mathbf{w}_{k}},{\mathbf{z}_{i_{k}}}\right\rangle)\eqqcolon\gamma\eta\widetilde{G}(\mathbf{w}_{k}),

where
\mathbf{z}_{i_{k}}
is a misclassified example by
\mathbf{w}_{k}
.
Perceptron enjoys an inequality of
(\clubsuit)
-type immediately because it optimizes the loss function

When considering a Fenchel–Young loss satisfying

To obtain an inequality of

5
Discussion and open problems

Comparison with
Wu et al. [63]
.

The large-stepsize logistic regression has been shown to exhibit the following phase transition
[
63
]
:
the GD sequence initially stays in the EoS phase such that the risk
L(\mathbf{w}_{t})
fluctuates initially with its average
t^{-1}\sum_{k}L(\mathbf{w}_{k})
controlled.
Once we experience
L(\mathbf{w}_{t})\lesssim\min\left\{{1/\eta,\ell(0)/n}\right\}
, which is possible within
\mathcal{O}(\eta)
steps at most,
GD leaves the EoS and the loss converges in the rate
L(\mathbf{w}_{t})=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(1/(\eta t))
.
The stepsize
\eta
trades off the phase transition time for the stable convergence rate.
If we know the maximum number of steps
T
in advance, the choice
\eta=\Theta(T)
balances them, achieving the acceleration to
L(\mathbf{w}_{t})=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(1/T^{2})
.
We detail them in
Appendix

D
.
This is arguably interesting to demonstrate how GD benefits from large stepsize.

Nevertheless, we would like to highlight two caveats.
First, we must undergo
L(\mathbf{w}_{t})\leq\ell(0)/n
before exiting the EoS phase.
This means that
our linear model has already classified all points correctly during the EoS phase
since any single point
\mathbf{z}_{i}
incurs loss at most
\ell(\left\langle{\mathbf{w}_{s}},{\mathbf{z}_{i}}\right\rangle)\leq\ell(0)\implies\left\langle{\mathbf{w}_{s}},{\mathbf{z}_{i}}\right\rangle\geq 0
(cf.
Lemma

22
in
Appendix

D
).
4
4

4

Cai et al. [16]
extends
Wu et al. [62]
for two-layer near-homogeneous NNs, where it is not explicit that the model correctly classifies all points after the EoS phase.
Taking a closer look, we can see that their Lemma A.7 leverages the well-controlled risk, which is an alternative expression to “
L(\mathbf{w}_{s})\leq\ell(0)/n
” under their setup.

GD keeps improving the logistic loss after the stable phase just because the logistic loss does not enjoy separation margin and never touches strict zero.
We refer interested readers to the relevant discussion in
Tyurin [58]
, who argues that the faster convergence in the stable phase is attributed to the choice of the logistic loss.

Second, the EoS termination condition
L(\mathbf{w}_{t})\lesssim 1/\eta
suggests that the risk must be once
\mathcal{O}(1/T)
-optimal (with the optimally balancing choice
\eta=\Theta(T)
) before benefitting from the super-fast rate
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(1/T^{2})
.
Yet, GD under some loss functions including the Tsallis
q
-loss (
q>1
) and the Rényi
q
-loss (
q>1
) achieves better risk with the same GD steps.
If our goal is simply to classify all training points, these alternative losses might do better jobs in terms of optimization solely.

Self-bounding property and implicit bias.

Having said that, the phase transition may play an important role in implicit bias.
It was shown under the linearly separable case that logistic regression optimized with GD enlarges the norm
\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|
toward the max-margin direction in rate
\Omega(\ln(t))

[
54
,
62
,
16
]
.
Thus, we may argue that
\mathbf{w}_{t}
gradually comes to classify all data points correctly during the EoS phase and evolves toward the max-margin direction in the stable phase.

We reported how
\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|
evolves under the pilot setup in
Figure

2
with different loss functions.
As seen, the logistic and Tsallis
0.5
losses inflate
\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|
endlessly, which do not have separation margin.
In stark contrast, the Tsallis
2
-loss prevents
\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|
from growing endlessly just because of its separation margin—recall the norm upper bounds of the norm
\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|
in (
11
) and the growth rate
\rho
in
Lemma

9
.
This raises two open questions:
(1) Do we have similar implicit bias aligning toward the max-margin direction under a loss function with separation margin?
(2) What are benefits and caveats of endless growing of
\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|
?
The latter is particularly relevant to the overconfidence issue due to excessively large
\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|

[
61
]
and worse generalization due to prohibitively large within-class variance
[
26
]
.

Wu et al. [64]
argues that excessively large
\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|
leads to an inconsistent estimator.

The study on implicit bias for loss functions with the self-bounding property has been very scarce.
To our knowledge,
[
35
]
crafted the complete hinge loss,
which behaves like the hinge loss before GD converges to the zero risk yet incurs an extra penalty to artificially align the parameter toward the max-margin direction.
Together with the benefits and caveats of the max-margin implicit bias, we believe this is an interesting open topic.

Dependency on
n
.

Our main result (
Theorem

5
) provides the rate depending on the factor
n
.
This extra factor with respect to
n
arises due to the worst-case analysis such that we have
at least
one “bad” direction
\mathbf{z}_{i}
before the convergence, corresponding to the inequality (
9
) in the proof of
Theorem

5
(see
Appendix

B
).
This worst-case scenario supposes that all data points are nearly equidistant, which is unlikely since most data points tend to cluster in similar directions.
We conjecture that this
n
-dependency is not essential with additional mild data assumptions.

The stochastic case.

Our result can be extended for the stochastic gradient descent (SGD).
Consider the scenario where we sample one fresh data point at each
t
and update the linear parameter with the loss function computed on this sample.
Under the similar setup to
Theorem

5
, the population risk is
\varepsilon
-optimal with high probability after
T=\Omega(\varepsilon^{-(\alpha+2)})
.
The formal statement and proof are shown in
Appendix

C
.
While this rate apparently looks significantly slower than the GD rate
T=\Omega(\varepsilon^{-\alpha})
,
the extra iterations
\varepsilon^{-2}
is necessary for collecting sufficient samples to estimate the population risk.
By noting that the GD/SGD updates consume
n
/one samples, the GD/SGD rates are comparable in terms of the number of consumed samples.

Finite-time convergence.

Last but not least, we may potentially have another benefit of loss functions with separation margin.
Take a look at
Figure

1
again.
Loss functions without separation margin, such as the Tsallis
1.5
- and
2
-losses, converge to exact zero within finite time when sufficiently large stepsize is used.
Such finite-time convergence under the linearly separable case can be shown without significant challenges if we use perceptron, or even the hinge loss, while becoming highly non-trivial in the case of twice-differentiable loss functions.
This is because the perceptron argument requires a non-degenerate lower bound on
\left\langle{\mathbf{w}_{t}},{\mathbf{w}_{*}}\right\rangle
(see (
4
)), which is not straightforward therein as the loss gradient can be arbitrarily small positive (due to the twice differentiability of the loss).
We conjecture that an additional data assumption is necessary because the loss gradient could be adversarially vanishing against GD convergence, and leave this as future work.

\displaystyle\text{($\ell$: nonincreasing)}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell^{\prime}(\left\langle{\mathbf{w}_{k}},{\mathbf{z}_{i}}\right\rangle)\left\langle{\mathbf{z}_{i}},{\mathbf{u}_{2}}\right\rangle+\eta\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell^{\prime}(\left\langle{\mathbf{w}_{k}},{\mathbf{z}_{i}}\right\rangle)\mathbf{z}_{i}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell^{\prime}(\left\langle{\mathbf{w}_{k}},{\mathbf{z}_{i}}\right\rangle)\left\langle{\mathbf{z}_{i}},{\mathbf{u}_{2}}\right\rangle+\eta\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell^{\prime}(\left\langle{\mathbf{w}_{k}},{\mathbf{z}_{i}}\right\rangle)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\|\mathbf{u}_{2}\|}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell^{\prime}(\left\langle{\mathbf{w}_{k}},{\mathbf{z}_{i}}\right\rangle)\left\langle{\mathbf{z}_{i}},{\mathbf{w}_{*}}\right\rangle+\eta C_{g}\cdot G(\mathbf{w}_{k})

\displaystyle\text{(\lx@cref{creftype~refnum}{assumption:lipschitz_loss} and $G(\mathbf{w}_{k})\geq 0$)}

\displaystyle\leq-2\gamma\|\mathbf{u}_{2}\|G(\mathbf{w}_{k})+\eta C_{g}\cdot G(\mathbf{w}_{k})

\displaystyle\text{(\lx@cref{creftype~refnum}{assumption:data} and $G(\mathbf{w}_{k})\geq 0$)}

\displaystyle=0,

where the last equality is by the choice of
\mathbf{u}_{2}
and
G(\mathbf{w})
is defined in (
6
).

By combining them altogether, we have for

Telescoping the sum from

which completes the proof.

\displaystyle\E[\ell(\left\langle{\mathbf{w}_{k}},{Z_{k}}\right\rangle)|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}]

\displaystyle\leq\E[\ell(-\|\mathbf{w}_{k}\|)|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}]

\displaystyle\text{($\ell$ is nonincreasing and $\|Z_{k}\|\leq 1$)}

\displaystyle\leq\ell\left(-\frac{4m+\eta C_{g}}{\gamma}\right)

\displaystyle\text{(\lx@cref{creftypeplural~refnum}{lemma:w_lb_sgd} and\lx@nobreakspace\lx@cref{refnum}{lemma:rho_separation_margin})}

\displaystyle\eqqcolon M_{\eta,\gamma}.

For the variance
\mathrm{Var}(H_{k}|\mathcal{F}_{k-1})
, we similarly have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(H_{k}|\mathcal{F}_{k-1})

\displaystyle=\E[H_{k}^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}]-\E[H_{k}|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}]^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\E[H_{k}^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}]

\displaystyle\leq\ell\left(-\frac{4m+\eta C_{g}}{\gamma}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=M_{\eta,\gamma}^{2}.

Note that these moment bounds hold uniformly for any
k
.
By plugging
M=M_{\eta,\gamma}
and
\sigma^{2}=M_{\eta,\gamma}^{2}
into
Lemma

17
, if we have

\frac{1}{t^{\circ}}\sum_{k=t_{0}}^{t_{0}+t^{\circ}-1}\E\left[L_{k}(\mathbf{w}_{k})\right]>\varepsilon\text{~~~and~~~}t^{\circ}\geq\max\left\{{\frac{32M_{\eta,\gamma}^{2}\ln(1/\delta)}{\varepsilon^{2}},\frac{8M_{\eta,\gamma}\ln(1/\delta)}{\varepsilon}}\right\},

(11)

then we have

\frac{1}{t^{\circ}}\sum_{k=t_{0}}^{t_{0}+t^{\circ}-1}L_{k}(\mathbf{w}_{k})>\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\text{~~~with probability at least $1-\delta$.}

Let us use this concentration argument.
Split the interval
[0,T]
into length-
t^{\circ}
sub-intervals (for
t^{\circ}
satisfying (
11
)) such that

[0,T]=\underbrace{[0,t^{\circ}-1]}_{\eqqcolon\mathcal{I}_{1}}\sqcup\underbrace{[t^{\circ},2t^{\circ}-1]}_{\eqqcolon\mathcal{I}_{2}}\sqcup\underbrace{[2t^{\circ},3t^{\circ}-1]}_{\eqqcolon\mathcal{I}_{3}}\dots\sqcup\underbrace{[(N-1)t^{\circ},Nt^{\circ}-1]}_{\eqqcolon\mathcal{I}_{N}}\sqcup[Nt^{\circ},T].

(12)

Consider the scenario where
\E[L_{k}(\mathbf{w}_{k})]>\varepsilon
holds for all
k\in[0,T]
,
and focus on an arbitrary sub-interval
\mathcal{I}_{l}
.
Since we have
\frac{1}{t^{\circ}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{I}_{l}}\E[L_{k}(\mathbf{w}_{k})]>\varepsilon
,
the concentration argument implies that
\frac{1}{t^{\circ}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{I}_{l}}L_{k}(\mathbf{w}_{k})>\varepsilon/2
with probability at least
1-\delta
.
This further indicates the high-probability existence of

such that

.
In this case, we have

for this specific
k_{l}\in\mathcal{I}_{l}
,
which can be seen in the same manner as the proof of
Theorem

5
.
Since this concentration argument does not depend on the sub-interval choice
\mathcal{I}_{l}
,
there exists a set of indices
\left\{{k_{l}}\right\}_{l\in[N]}
such that each of
g(\left\langle{\mathbf{w}_{k_{l}}},{\mathbf{z}_{k_{l}}}\right\rangle)\geq g(\ell^{-1}(\varepsilon/2))
holds with probability at least
1-\delta
.

Next, we invoke the perceptron argument.
By combining
Lemmas

15
and
16
with the choice

, we have

\gamma\eta g\left(\ell^{-1}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)\right)\cdot N\leq\left\langle{\mathbf{w}_{T}},{\mathbf{w}_{*}}\right\rangle\leq\|\mathbf{w}_{T}\|\leq\frac{4\sqrt{\rho(\gamma^{2}\eta T)}+\eta C_{g}}{\gamma}\leq\frac{4m+\eta C_{g}}{\gamma},

with probability at least
(1-\delta)^{N}
,
where we additionally used
Lemma

9
at the last inequality.
By applying
Lemma

13
at the left-most side,
we have

which implies

Therefore, if

(or

), with probability at least

, we have
\E[L_{k}(\mathbf{w}_{k})]\leq\varepsilon
for some

.

Finally, we need verify that

defined in

matches (
3
),
but we skip it because it can be confirmed in the same manner as in the proof of

.
∎

C.1
Comparison between GD and SGD

Whereas the iteration complexity for GD given by
Corollary

6
is
T=\Omega(\varepsilon^{-\alpha})
,
the iteration complexity for SGD given by
Proposition

14
is
T=\Omega(\varepsilon^{-(\alpha+2)})
.
Hence, the SGD rate is significantly slower than the GD case.
This deterioration is because we can observe a non-trivial lower bound
g(\left\langle{\mathbf{w}_{k}},{\mathbf{z}_{k}}\right\rangle)
after every
t^{\circ}=\Omega(\varepsilon^{-2})
steps.
As in the GD case, we need
N=\Omega(\varepsilon^{-\alpha})
such non-trivial lower bounds, and hence the total iteration number amounts to
Nt^{\circ}=\Omega(\varepsilon^{-(\alpha+2)})
.
While this apparently looks a big bottleneck, note that the GD rate is
T\gtrsim n\varepsilon^{-\alpha}
if we explicitly write the
n
-dependency.
Since (
SGD
) consumes only one fresh sample at every update (while (
GD
) consumes
n
samples),
the extra complexity
N=\Omega(\varepsilon^{-2})
appearing in the SGD case compensates for this gap of sample sizes.
The GD/SGD rates are comparable in this sense.

Lemma 19

(EoS phase)
.

Suppose
Assumption

2

and consider a convex and nonincreasing loss
\ell
satisfying

3B
and

For every

Proof..

By invoking
Lemma

10
with the choice of
\theta

\theta=\frac{\sqrt{\rho(\gamma^{2}\eta t)}}{\gamma},

\displaystyle\frac{\|\mathbf{w}_{t}-\mathbf{u}\|^{2}}{2\eta t}+\frac{1}{t}\sum_{k=0}^{t-1}L(\mathbf{w}_{k})

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathbf{w}_{t}-\mathbf{u}\|+\|\mathbf{u}\|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{2\rho(\gamma^{2}\eta t)}{\gamma^{2}}+\left(\theta+\frac{\eta C_{g}}{2\gamma}\right)^{2}}+\left(\theta+\frac{\eta C_{g}}{2\gamma}\right)

Lemma 21

(Modified descent lemma)
.

Consider a loss satisfying
5A
.
Suppose there exists
s<T
such that

L(\mathbf{w}_{s})\leq\frac{1}{4C_{\beta}^{2}\eta},

then for every

we have

1.

L(\mathbf{w}_{t})\leq 1/(4C_{\beta}^{2}\eta)
and

L(\mathbf{w}_{t+1})\leq L(\mathbf{w}_{t})-\frac{3\eta}{4}\|\nabla L(\mathbf{w}_{t})\|^{2}\leq L(\mathbf{w}_{t})
,

where
G(\mathbf{w})
is defined in (
6
).

Proof..

We first show that Claim 1 implies Claim 2.
By
Assumption

5A
, we have

\displaystyle\ell(\left\langle{\mathbf{w}_{t+1}},{\mathbf{z}_{i}}\right\rangle)

\displaystyle\leq\ell(\left\langle{\mathbf{w}_{t}},{\mathbf{z}_{i}}\right\rangle)+\ell^{\prime}(\left\langle{\mathbf{w}_{t}},{\mathbf{z}_{i}}\right\rangle)\left\langle{\mathbf{w}_{t+1}-\mathbf{w}_{t}},{\mathbf{z}_{i}}\right\rangle+C_{\beta}g(\left\langle{\mathbf{w}_{t}},{\mathbf{z}_{i}}\right\rangle)\left\langle{\mathbf{w}_{t+1}-\mathbf{w}_{t}},{\mathbf{z}_{i}}\right\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\ell(\left\langle{\mathbf{w}_{t}},{\mathbf{z}_{i}}\right\rangle)+\ell^{\prime}(\left\langle{\mathbf{w}_{t}},{\mathbf{z}_{i}}\right\rangle)\left\langle{\mathbf{w}_{t+1}-\mathbf{w}_{t}},{\mathbf{z}_{i}}\right\rangle+C_{\beta}g(\left\langle{\mathbf{w}_{t}},{\mathbf{z}_{i}}\right\rangle)\|\mathbf{w}_{t+1}-\mathbf{w}_{t}\|^{2}.

Taking average over
i\in[n]
, we get

\displaystyle L(\mathbf{w}_{t+1})

\displaystyle\leq L(\mathbf{w}_{t})+\left\langle{\nabla L(\mathbf{w}_{t})},{\mathbf{w}_{t+1}-\mathbf{w}_{t}}\right\rangle+C_{\beta}G(\mathbf{w}_{t})\|\mathbf{w}_{t+1}-\mathbf{w}_{t}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=L(\mathbf{w}_{t})-\eta\|\nabla L(\mathbf{w}_{t})\|^{2}+C_{\beta}\eta^{2}G(\mathbf{w}_{t})\|\nabla L(\mathbf{w}_{t})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq L(\mathbf{w}_{t})-\eta\|\nabla L(\mathbf{w}_{t})\|^{2}+\frac{\eta}{4}\|\nabla L(\mathbf{w}_{t})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\text{(Claim 1)}

\displaystyle=L(\mathbf{w}_{t})-\frac{3\eta}{4}\|\nabla L(\mathbf{w}_{t})\|^{2},

which verifies Claim 2.

The base case
t=s
holds by

5A
as follows:

G(\mathbf{w}_{s})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(\left\langle{\mathbf{w}_{s}},{\mathbf{z}_{i}}\right\rangle)\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}C_{\beta}\ell(\left\langle{\mathbf{w}_{s}},{\mathbf{z}_{i}}\right\rangle)=C_{\beta}L(\mathbf{w}_{s})\leq\frac{1}{4C_{\beta}\eta}.

.

Since

Lemma 23

(Stable phase)
.

Consider a nonincreasing loss
\ell
satisfying
Assumptions

3B
and
5A
.

By the lemma assumption, we can apply

21

Therefore, we have for

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{w}_{s+k+1}-\mathbf{u}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\mathbf{w}_{s+k}-\mathbf{u}\|^{2}+2\eta\left\langle{\nabla L(\mathbf{w}_{s+k})},{\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{w}_{s+k}}\right\rangle+\eta^{2}\|\nabla L(\mathbf{w}_{s+k})\|^{2}

\frac{\sqrt{2}}{4}\frac{\eta C_{g}}{\sqrt{\gamma^{2}\eta\tau}}\leq\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{\sqrt{4C_{\beta}^{2}\eta+n/\ell(0)}}.

These two inequalities together imply that

\frac{1}{\tau}\sum_{k=0}^{\tau-1}L(\mathbf{w}_{k})\leq\frac{1}{4C_{\beta}^{2}\eta+n/\ell(0)}\leq\min\left\{{\frac{1}{4C_{\beta}^{2}\eta},\frac{\ell(0)}{n}}\right\},

which implies that there exists
s\leq\tau
for
L(\mathbf{w}_{s})
satisfies the right-hand side bound.
∎

Lemma 25

(Phase transition time under exponential tail)
.

Suppose
Assumption

2

and consider a nonincreasing loss
\ell
satisfying
Assumptions

3B
and
3C
, and
5B
.
Furthermore, assume
\eta\geq 1
.
Then, there exists
C>0
depending on
C_{e}
,
C_{g}
,
C_{\beta}
,
\ell(0)
, and
n
such that the following holds.
Let

\tau\coloneqq\frac{C}{\gamma^{2}}\max\left\{{\eta,n\ln n}\right\}.

If
\tau\leq T
, then there exists
s\in[0,\tau]
such that

L(\mathbf{w}_{s})\leq\min\left\{{\frac{1}{4C_{\beta}^{2}\eta},\frac{\ell(0)}{n}}\right\}.

Proof..

Under
Assumption

5B
, we have

\ell(z)\leq C_{e}g(z)=-C_{e}\ell^{\prime}(z),\quad\text{for $z\geq 0$,}

which implies

\frac{\ell^{\prime}(z)}{\ell(z)}\leq-C_{e}^{-1},\quad\text{for $z\geq 0$.}

Integrating both sides, we get

\ln\ell(z)\leq\ln\ell(0)+\int_{0}^{z}\frac{\ell^{\prime}(\zeta)}{\ell(\zeta)}\mathrm{d}{\zeta}\leq\ln\ell(0)-C_{e}^{-1}z,\quad\text{for $z\geq 0$,}

which implies

\ell(z)\leq\ell(0)\exp(-C_{e}^{-1}z),\quad\text{for $z\geq 0$.}

Using the exponential tail property, we have

\rho(\lambda)=\min_{z\in\mathbb{R}}\lambda\ell(z)+z^{2}\leq\lambda\ell(C_{e}\ln(\lambda))+C_{e}^{2}\ln^{2}(\lambda)\leq\ell(0)+C_{e}^{2}\ln^{2}(\lambda).

Applying
Lemma

20
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau}\sum_{k=0}^{\tau-1}G(\mathbf{w}_{k})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4\sqrt{\rho(\gamma^{2}\eta\tau)}+\eta C_{g}}{\gamma^{2}\eta\tau}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4\sqrt{\ell(0)+C_{e}^{2}\ln^{2}(\gamma^{2}\eta\tau)}+\eta C_{g}}{\gamma^{2}\eta\tau}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4\sqrt{\ell(0)}+4C_{e}\ln(\gamma^{2}\eta\tau)+\eta C_{g}}{\gamma^{2}\eta\tau}

\displaystyle(\sqrt{a+b}\leq\sqrt{a}+\sqrt{b})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4C_{e}}{\eta}\frac{\ln(\gamma^{2}\tau)}{\gamma^{2}\tau}+\frac{C_{g}+4C_{e}}{\gamma^{2}\tau}+\frac{4\sqrt{\ell(0)}}{\eta}\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}\tau}.

Here, we take
C>0
depending on
C_{e}
,
C_{g}
,
C_{\beta}
,
\ell(0)
, and additionally
n
such that

\gamma^{2}\tau\geq C\max\left\{{\eta,n}\right\}

and

\frac{\ln C}{C}\leq\frac{\min\left\{{\frac{1}{4C_{e}C_{\beta}^{2}},\frac{\ell(0)}{C_{e}}}\right\}}{4C_{e}(1+\ln n)+C_{g}+4C_{e}+4\sqrt{\ell(0)}}.

This choice is possible with sufficiently large
C\geq e
because
(\ln C)/C
is strictly decreasing in
C\geq e
toward zero.
Such
C
enables us to have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau}

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\tau-1}G(\mathbf{w}_{k})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{C\max\left\{{\eta,n}\right\}}\left[\frac{4C_{e}}{\eta}(\ln C+\ln\max\left\{{\eta,n}\right\})+C_{g}+4C_{e}+\frac{4\sqrt{\ell(0)}}{\eta}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4C_{e}(\ln C+\ln n)+C_{g}+4C_{e}+4\sqrt{\ell(0)}}{C\max\left\{{\eta,n}\right\}}

\displaystyle(\eta\geq 1)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\ln C}{C}\frac{4C_{e}(1+\ln n)+C_{g}+4C_{e}+4\sqrt{\ell(0)}}{\max\left\{{\eta,n}\right\}}

\displaystyle(\ln C\geq 1)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\min\left\{{\frac{1}{4C_{e}C_{\beta}^{2}},\frac{\ell(0)}{C_{e}}}\right\}}{\max\left\{{\eta,n}\right\}}

\displaystyle\leq\min\left\{{\frac{1}{4C_{e}C_{\beta}^{2}\eta},\frac{\ell(0)}{C_{e}n}}\right\}.

From this we have some
s\leq\tau
such that

G(\mathbf{w}_{s})\leq\min\left\{{\frac{1}{4C_{e}C_{\beta}^{2}\eta},\frac{\ell(0)}{C_{e}n}}\right\}.

This ensures that for every
i\in[n]
,

\frac{1}{n}g(\left\langle{\mathbf{w}_{s}},{\mathbf{z}_{i}}\right\rangle)\leq G(\mathbf{w}_{s})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(\left\langle{\mathbf{w}_{s}},{\mathbf{z}_{i}}\right\rangle)\leq\frac{\ell(0)}{C_{e}n}\leq\frac{g(0)}{n},

where the last inequality is due to
Assumption

5B
.
The above implies
\left\langle{\mathbf{w}_{s}},{\mathbf{z}_{i}}\right\rangle\geq 0
since
g(\cdot)
is nonincreasing.
Thus, we can apply
Assumption

5B
for
\left\langle{\mathbf{w}_{s}},{\mathbf{z}_{i}}\right\rangle
and get

\ell(\left\langle{\mathbf{w}_{s}},{\mathbf{z}_{i}}\right\rangle)\leq C_{e}g(\left\langle{\mathbf{w}_{s}},{\mathbf{z}_{i}}\right\rangle).

Taking an average over
i\in[n]
, we have

L(\mathbf{w}_{s})\leq C_{e}G(\mathbf{w}_{s}).

We complete the proof by plugging in the upper bound on
G(\mathbf{w}_{s})
.
∎

Appendix E
Separation margin and self-bounding property

In this section, we discuss the relationship between separation margin and the self-bounding property.
First, we show that a loss function does not have separation margin if it satisfies the self-bounding property.

Proposition 26
.

Consider a loss
\ell\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
that is continuously differentiable and nonincreasing, and satisfies
\ell(z_{0})>0
for some
z_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
.
If
\ell
satisfies
Assumption

5A
, then
\ell
does not have separation margin.

Proof..

Choose any
\varepsilon\in(0,1/C_{\beta})
.
The convexity of
\ell
implies that

g(z)=-\ell^{\prime}(z)\geq\frac{\ell(z)-\ell(z+\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon}\quad\text{for any $z\in\mathbb{R}$.}

By the self-bounding property (
Assumption

5A
), we further have

\frac{\ell(z)-\ell(z+\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon}\leq g(z)\leq C_{\beta}\ell(z).

Solving this, we have

\ell(z+\varepsilon)\geq(1-C_{\beta}\varepsilon)\ell(z).

Thus, if
\ell(z)>0
holds, we additionally have
\ell(z+\varepsilon)>0
for
\varepsilon\in(0,1/C_{\beta})
,
and we conclude that
\ell
cannot have separation margin because
\ell>0
holds on the entire
\mathbb{R}
.
∎

Next, we argue that the converse of
Proposition

26
does not hold,
that is, even if a loss
\ell
does not have separation margin, it does not always imply that
\ell
satisfies the self-bounding property.
A counterexample is a Fenchel–Young loss generated by the following potential function:

\phi(\mu)=\int_{0}^{\mu}\Phi^{-1}(p)\mathrm{d}{p},\quad\text{where $\Phi$ is the standard normal CDF}\;\;\Phi(x)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\left[1+\mathrm{erf}\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\right]

and
\mathrm{erf}
is the error function.
The generated Fenchel–Young loss is relevant to the probit model
[
40
]
because
\phi^{\prime}
is nothing else but the probit link function prevailing in generalized linear models.
Hence, we call the generated Fenchel–Young loss the
probit Fenchel–Young loss
for convenience.
We can have a concise form of the probit Fenchel-Young loss:

\displaystyle\ell(z)

\displaystyle=\phi^{*}(-z)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{-z}(\phi^{\prime})^{-1}(\zeta)\mathrm{d}{\zeta}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{-z}\Phi(\zeta)\mathrm{d}{\zeta}

\displaystyle=[\zeta\Phi(\zeta)+\Phi^{\prime}(\zeta)]_{-\infty}^{-z}

\displaystyle=-z\Phi(-z)+\Phi^{\prime}(-z).

The probit Fenchel–Young loss does not have separation margin because
\phi^{\prime}(\mu)=\Phi^{-1}(\mu)\to-\infty
as
\mu\downarrow 0
(see
Proposition

4
).
However, it does not satisfy the self-bounding property.
To see this, we have

\displaystyle\frac{g(z)}{\ell(z)}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\ell^{\prime}(z)}{\ell(z)}

\displaystyle=-\frac{-\Phi(-z)}{-z\Phi(-z)+\Phi^{\prime}(-z)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Phi(\bar{z})}{\bar{z}\Phi(\bar{z})+\Phi^{\prime}(\bar{z})},

\displaystyle(\bar{z}\equiv-z)

which implies

\displaystyle\lim_{z\to\infty}\frac{g(z)}{\ell(z)}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\bar{z}\to-\infty}\frac{\Phi^{\prime}(\bar{z})}{\Phi(\bar{z})+\bar{z}\Phi^{\prime}(\bar{z})+\Phi^{\prime\prime}(\bar{z})}

(L’Hôpital’s rule)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\bar{z}\to-\infty}\frac{\Phi^{\prime}(\bar{z})}{\Phi(\bar{z})}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\bar{z}\to-\infty}\frac{\Phi^{\prime\prime}(\bar{z})}{\Phi^{\prime}(\bar{z})}

(L’Hôpital’s rule)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\bar{z}\to-\infty}\frac{-\bar{z}\Phi^{\prime}(\bar{z})}{\Phi^{\prime}(\bar{z})}

\displaystyle=\infty.

Hence,
g(z)
cannot always be bounded from above by
\ell(z)
, that is, the self-bounding property is not satisfied.

Appendix F
Omitted calculation for examples

Here, we compute for each
\phi
,

\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\frac{\phi^{\prime}(\mu)}{\mu\phi^{\prime\prime}(\mu)}\left[1-\frac{\phi(\mu)}{\mu\phi^{\prime}(\mu)}\right]

to estimate the power
\alpha
of the convergence rate provided in
Theorem

5
, by making the error parameter
\bar{\varepsilon}>0
in (
3
) arbitrarily small.
Correspondingly, we compute

\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\frac{\mu}{[\mu\phi^{\prime}(\mu)-\phi(\mu)]^{\alpha}}

to estimate the constant
C_{\phi}
in the convergence rate, verifying that
C_{\phi}
neither degenerates nor diverges for arbitrarily small error parameter
\bar{\varepsilon}>0
.

Before proceeding with each example, we provide a rough estimate of
\rho
for loss functions without separation margin.

Lemma 27
.

Consider a loss
\ell
satisfying
Assumptions

3A
and
3B
that does not have separation margin.
Then,

\rho(\lambda)\leq-\phi\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)\lambda.

Proof..

First, we rewrite
\rho
as a dual form.
By introducing the dual variable
\mu
of
z
by

z=\phi^{\prime}(\mu)\quad\text{and}\quad\mu=(\phi^{*})^{\prime}(z),

we have

\displaystyle\rho(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\min_{z\in\mathbb{R}}\lambda\ell(z)+z^{2}

\displaystyle=\min_{z\in\mathbb{R}}\lambda\phi^{*}(z)+z^{2}

\displaystyle=\min_{\mu\in[0,1]}\lambda[\mu\phi^{\prime}(\mu)-\phi(\mu)]+[\phi^{\prime}(\mu)]^{2},

where we use the definition of the convex conjugate
\phi^{*}(z)=\mu z-\phi(\mu)
at the last identity.
Now, we write the objective as
R(\mu)
:

R(\mu)\coloneqq\lambda[\mu\phi^{\prime}(\mu)-\phi(\mu)]+[\phi^{\prime}(\mu)]^{2}.

Differentiating
R
, we have

R^{\prime}(\mu_{\star})=[\lambda\mu_{\star}+2\phi^{\prime}(\mu_{\star})]\phi^{\prime\prime}(\mu_{\star})=0\quad\overset{\phi^{\prime\prime}>0}{\implies}\quad\phi^{\prime}(\mu_{\star})=-\frac{\lambda}{2}\mu_{\star}

at the minimizer
\mu_{\star}
of
R
.
Plugging this back to
R
, we have

\rho(\lambda)=R(\mu_{\star})=\lambda\left[\mu_{\star}\left(-\frac{\lambda}{2}\mu_{\star}\right)-\phi(\mu_{\star})\right]+\left(-\frac{\lambda}{2}\mu_{\star}\right)^{2}=-\lambda\phi(\mu_{\star})\leq-\phi\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)\lambda,

where the last inequality owes to that a convex potential satisfying
Assumption

1
is minimized at the uniform distribution
\mu_{\star}=1/2

[
11
, Proposition 4]
.
∎

By using
Lemma

27
, we can simplify the convergence rate of (
GD
) given by
Theorem

5
for a loss that does not have separation margin.
Note that the following convergence rate is not sufficiently tight due to overestimation of
\rho
by
Lemma

27
;
nevertheless, the provided convergence rate is convenient when we do not have an access to
\rho
analytically.

Corollary 28
.

Under the same setup with
Theorem

5
, we additionally assume that
\ell
does not have separation margin.
If
(\alpha,C_{\phi})
with (
3
) satisfies
\alpha,C_{\phi}\in(0,\infty)
and

T>\frac{2C_{g}n}{C_{\phi}\gamma^{2}}\varepsilon^{-\alpha}+\frac{16[-\phi(1/2)]n^{2}}{C_{\phi}^{2}\gamma^{2}\eta}\varepsilon^{-2\alpha}\quad\text{for $\varepsilon\in(0,\bar{\varepsilon})$,}

then we have
L(\mathbf{w}_{T})\leq\varepsilon
.

Proof..

Combining
Theorem

5
and
Lemma

27
, we have the following convergence rate:

T>\frac{4n\sqrt{-\phi(1/2)}\varepsilon^{-\alpha}}{C_{\phi}\gamma\sqrt{\eta}}\sqrt{T}+\frac{C_{g}n\varepsilon^{-\alpha}}{C_{\phi}\gamma^{2}}.

Defining

a\coloneqq\frac{4n\sqrt{-\phi(1/2)}\varepsilon^{-\alpha}}{C_{\phi}\gamma\sqrt{\eta}}\quad\text{and}\quad b\coloneqq\frac{C_{g}n\varepsilon^{-\alpha}}{C_{\phi}\gamma^{2}},

we have the following inequality in
T
:

T^{2}-(a^{2}+2b)T+b^{2}>0.

This can be solved for
T\geq 1
:

T>\frac{a^{2}+2b}{2}\Biggl[1+\underbrace{\sqrt{1-\left(\frac{2b}{a^{2}+2b}\right)^{2}}}_{\leq 1}\,\Biggr],

for which
T>a^{2}+2b
is sufficient.
Thus, we have shown the statement.
∎

Throughout this section, we repeatedly use L’Hôpital’s rule.
When it is used, we notate by
(\ddagger)
.

F.1
Shannon entropy

For the Shannon entropy
\phi(\mu)=\mu\ln\mu+(1-\mu)\ln(1-\mu)
, we have

\phi^{\prime}(\mu)=\ln\mu-\ln(1-\mu)\quad\text{and}\quad\phi^{\prime\prime}(\mu)=\frac{1}{\mu}+\frac{1}{1-\mu},

which imply

\displaystyle\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\frac{\phi^{\prime}(\mu)}{\mu\phi^{\prime\prime}(\mu)}\left[1-\frac{\phi(\mu)}{\mu\phi^{\prime}(\mu)}\right]

\displaystyle=\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\frac{\ln\frac{\mu}{1-\mu}}{1-\frac{\mu}{1-\mu}}\left[1-\frac{\mu\ln\mu+(1-\mu)\ln(1-\mu)}{\mu\ln\mu-\mu\ln(1-\mu)}\right]

\displaystyle=\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\ln\frac{\mu}{1-\mu}\cdot\frac{\mu\ln\mu-\mu\ln(1-\mu)-\mu\ln\mu-(1-\mu)\ln(1-\mu)}{\mu\ln\frac{\mu}{1-\mu}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\frac{-\ln(1-\mu)}{\mu}

\displaystyle\overset{(\ddagger)}{=}\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{1-\mu}

\displaystyle=1,

and

\displaystyle\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\frac{\mu}{\mu\phi^{\prime}(\mu)-\phi(\mu)}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\frac{\mu}{\mu\ln\mu-\mu\ln(1-\mu)-\mu\ln\mu-(1-\mu)\ln(1-\mu)}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\frac{\mu}{-\ln(1-\mu)}\cdot\frac{1}{2\mu-1}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\frac{\mu}{\ln(1-\mu)}

\displaystyle\overset{(\ddagger)}{=}\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}(1-\mu)

\displaystyle=1.

Finally, we derive the convergence rate for the logistic loss.
Plugging
\alpha=1
,
C_{\phi}=1
,
C_{g}=1
, and
\rho(\lambda)\leq 1+\ln^{2}(\lambda)\leq 2\ln^{2}(\lambda)
to
Theorem

5
, we have

T>\frac{n}{\gamma^{2}}\left(\frac{4\sqrt{2}\ln(\gamma^{2}\eta T)}{\eta}+1\right)\varepsilon^{-1}=\left[\frac{4\sqrt{2}\ln(\gamma^{2}\eta)}{\eta}+1+\frac{4\sqrt{2}}{\eta}\ln T\right]\frac{n\varepsilon^{-1}}{\gamma^{2}}.

Dividing both ends by
\ln T
, we have

\frac{T}{\ln T}>\left[\left(\frac{4\sqrt{2}\ln(\gamma^{2}\eta)}{\eta}+1\right)\frac{1}{\ln T}+\frac{4\sqrt{2}}{\eta}\right]\frac{n\varepsilon^{-1}}{\gamma^{2}},

for which the following is sufficient when
T\geq 2
:

\displaystyle\frac{T}{\ln T}

\displaystyle>\left[\left(\frac{4\sqrt{2}\ln(\gamma^{2}\eta)}{\eta}+1\right)\frac{1}{\ln 2}+\frac{4\sqrt{2}}{\eta}\right]\frac{n\varepsilon^{-1}}{\gamma^{2}}

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{4\sqrt{2}\log_{2}(\gamma^{2}\eta)}{\eta}+\frac{1}{\ln 2}+\frac{4\sqrt{2}}{\eta}\right]\frac{n\varepsilon^{-1}}{\gamma^{2}}.

By ignoring the logarithmic factor, we have

T\gtrsim\left[\frac{4\sqrt{2}\log_{2}(\gamma^{2}\eta)}{\eta}+\frac{1}{\ln 2}+\frac{4\sqrt{2}}{\eta}\right]\frac{n\varepsilon^{-1}}{\gamma^{2}}.

F.2
Semi-circle entropy

For the semi-circle entropy
\phi(\mu)=-2\sqrt{\mu(1-\mu)}
, we first derive the analytical form of the corresponding Fenchel–Young loss.
We have

\phi^{\prime}(\mu)=\frac{2\mu-1}{\sqrt{\mu(1-\mu)}}\quad\text{and}\quad\phi^{\prime\prime}(\mu)=\frac{1}{2[\mu(1-\mu)]^{3/2}}.

The dual transform
(\phi^{*})^{\prime}
is given by

(\phi^{*})^{\prime}(z)=(\phi^{\prime})^{-1}(z)=\frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{\frac{z}{2}}{\sqrt{\left(\frac{z}{2}\right)^{2}+1}}+1\right],

thanks to the Danskin’s theorem
[
21
]
.
Then, we can derive the Fenchel–Young loss by the definition of the convex conjugate:

\ell(z)=\phi^{*}(-z)=-z(\phi^{*})^{\prime}(z)-\phi\left((\phi^{*})^{\prime}(-z)\right)=\frac{-z+\sqrt{z^{2}+4}}{2}.

Next, we compute the loss parameters
\alpha
and
C_{\phi}
respectively as follows:

\displaystyle\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\frac{\phi^{\prime}(\mu)}{\mu\phi^{\prime\prime}(\mu)}\left[1-\frac{\phi(\mu)}{\mu\phi^{\prime}(\mu)}\right]

\displaystyle=\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\frac{\frac{2\mu-1}{\sqrt{\mu(1-\mu)}}}{\frac{\mu}{2[\mu(1-\mu)]^{3/2}}}\left[1+\frac{2\sqrt{\mu(1-\mu)}}{\frac{\mu(2\mu-1)}{\sqrt{\mu(1-\mu)}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}2(2\mu-1)(1-\mu)\left[1+\frac{2(1-\mu)}{2\mu-1}\right]

\displaystyle=2,

and

\displaystyle\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\frac{\mu}{[\mu\phi^{\prime}(\mu)-\phi(\mu)]^{2}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\frac{\mu}{\left[\mu\frac{2\mu-1}{\sqrt{\mu(1-\mu)}}+2\sqrt{\mu(1-\mu)}\right]^{2}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}(1-\mu)

\displaystyle=1.

To estimate
\rho(\lambda)
,

\displaystyle\rho(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\min_{z\in\mathbb{R}}\lambda\ell(z)+z^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\lambda\frac{-\ln\lambda+\sqrt{\ln^{2}\lambda+4}}{2}+\ln^{2}\lambda

\displaystyle(z=\ln\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\lambda}{\ln\lambda+\sqrt{\ln^{2}\lambda+4}}+\ln^{2}\lambda

\displaystyle\leq\frac{5\lambda}{2\ln\lambda},

where we used

\frac{\lambda}{\ln\lambda}\geq\frac{2\lambda}{\ln\lambda+\sqrt{\ln^{2}\lambda+4}}\geq\frac{2}{3}\cdot\ln^{2}\lambda\quad\text{for $\lambda\geq 1$.}

Finally, we derive the convergence rate for the semi-circle loss.
Plugging
\alpha=2
,
C_{\phi}=1
,
C_{g}=1
, and
\rho(\lambda)\leq 5\lambda/(2\ln\lambda)
to
Theorem

5
, we have

T>\frac{n}{\gamma^{2}}\left(\frac{4\sqrt{\frac{5}{2}\frac{\gamma^{2}\eta T}{\ln(\gamma^{2}\eta T)}}}{\eta}+1\right)\varepsilon^{-2}=\left[\frac{2\sqrt{10}}{\eta}\sqrt{\frac{\gamma^{2}\eta T}{\ln(\gamma^{2}\eta T)}}+1\right]\frac{n\varepsilon^{-2}}{\gamma^{2}},

for which the following is sufficient when
T\geq 2
:

T>\left[\frac{2\sqrt{10}}{\eta}\sqrt{\frac{\gamma^{2}\eta T}{\ln(2\gamma^{2}\eta)}}+1\right]\frac{n\varepsilon^{-2}}{\gamma^{2}}.

Subsequently, we follow the same flow as in the proof of
Corollary

28
.
Defining

a\coloneqq\frac{2\sqrt{10}n\varepsilon^{-2}}{\gamma^{2}\eta}\sqrt{\frac{\gamma^{2}\eta}{\ln(2\gamma^{2}\eta)}}\quad\text{and}\quad b\coloneqq\frac{n\varepsilon^{-2}}{\gamma^{2}},

we have the following inequality in
T
:

T^{2}-(a^{2}+2b)T+b^{2}>0.

This can be solved for
T\geq 1
:

T>\frac{a^{2}+2b}{2}\Biggl[1+\underbrace{\sqrt{1-\left(\frac{2b}{a^{2}+2b}\right)^{2}}}_{\leq 1}\Biggr],

for which
T>a^{2}+2b
is sufficient, namely,

T>\frac{40n^{2}}{\gamma^{2}\eta\ln(2\gamma^{2}\eta)}\varepsilon^{-4}+\frac{2n}{\gamma^{2}}\varepsilon^{-2}

is sufficient.
Thus, the convergence rate is
T=\Omega(\varepsilon^{-4})
.

F.3
Tsallis entropy

For the Tsallis entropy

\phi(\mu)=\frac{\mu^{q}+(1-\mu)^{q}-1}{q-1},

define

\displaystyle\phi_{0}(\mu)

\displaystyle=\mu^{q}+(1-\mu)^{q}-1,

\displaystyle\phi_{1}(\mu)

\displaystyle=\mu^{q-1}-(1-\mu)^{q-1},

\displaystyle\phi_{2}(\mu)

\displaystyle=\mu^{q-2}+(1-\mu)^{q-2}.

When
0<q<2
(
q\neq 1
),

\displaystyle\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\frac{\phi^{\prime}(\mu)}{\mu\phi^{\prime\prime}(\mu)}\left[1-\frac{\phi(\mu)}{\mu\phi^{\prime}(\mu)}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q(q-1)}\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{\phi_{2}(\mu)}\cdot\frac{q\mu\phi_{1}(\mu)-\phi_{0}(\mu)}{\mu^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q(q-1)}\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{1+\left(\frac{\mu}{1-\mu}\right)^{2-q}}\cdot\frac{q\mu\phi_{1}(\mu)-\phi_{0}(\mu)}{\mu^{q}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q(q-1)}\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\frac{q\mu\phi_{1}(\mu)-\phi_{0}(\mu)}{\mu^{q}}

\displaystyle\overset{(\ddagger)}{=}\frac{1}{q(q-1)}\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\frac{q\phi_{1}(\mu)+q(q-1)\mu\phi_{2}(\mu)-q\phi_{1}(\mu)}{q\mu^{q-1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q}\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\frac{\mu^{q-1}+(1-\mu)^{q-2}\mu}{\mu^{q-1}}

\displaystyle\overset{(\ddagger)}{=}\frac{1}{q}\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\frac{(q-1)\mu^{q-2}+(1-\mu)^{q-2}-(q-2)(1-\mu)^{q-3}\mu}{(q-1)\mu^{q-2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q}\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\left[1+\frac{1}{q-1}\left(\frac{\mu}{1-\mu}\right)^{2-q}-(q-2)\left(\frac{\mu}{1-\mu}\right)^{3-q}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q}.

In addition, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\frac{\mu}{[\mu\phi^{\prime}(\mu)-\phi(\mu)]^{1/q}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\frac{(q-1)^{1/q}\mu}{[q\mu\phi_{1}(\mu)-\phi_{0}(\mu)]^{1/q}}

\displaystyle=(q-1)^{1/q}\left\{\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\frac{q\mu\phi_{1}(\mu)-\phi_{0}(\mu)}{\mu^{q}}\right\}^{-1/q}

\displaystyle=(q-1)^{1/q}\left\{q-1-\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\frac{q\mu(1-\mu)^{q-1}+(1-\mu)^{q}-1}{\mu^{q}}\right\}^{-1/q}

\displaystyle\overset{(\ddagger)}{=}(q-1)^{1/q}\left\{q-1-\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\frac{q(1-\mu)^{q-1}-q(q-1)\mu(1-\mu)^{q-2}-q(1-\mu)^{q-1}}{q\mu^{q-1}}\right\}^{-1/q}

\displaystyle=(q-1)^{1/q}\left\{q-1-(q-1)\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\left(\frac{\mu}{1-\mu}\right)^{2-q}\right\}^{-1/q}

\displaystyle=(q-1)^{1/q}\cdot(q-1+0)^{-1/q}

\displaystyle=1.

When
q\geq 2
,

\displaystyle\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\frac{\phi^{\prime}(\mu)}{\mu\phi^{\prime\prime}(\mu)}\left[1-\frac{\phi(\mu)}{\mu\phi^{\prime}(\mu)}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q(q-1)}\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{\phi_{2}(\mu)}\cdot\frac{q\mu\phi_{1}(\mu)-\phi_{0}(\mu)}{\mu^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q(q-1)}\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\frac{q\mu\phi_{1}(\mu)-\phi_{0}(\mu)}{\mu^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q(q-1)}\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\frac{q[\mu^{q}-(1-\mu)^{q-1}\mu]-\mu^{q}-(1-\mu)^{q}}{\mu^{2}}

\displaystyle\overset{(\ddagger)}{=}\frac{1}{q(q-1)}\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\frac{q[q\mu^{q-1}-(1-\mu)^{q-1}+(q-1)(1-\mu)^{q-2}\mu]-q\mu^{q-1}+q(1-\mu)^{q-1}}{2\mu}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\frac{\mu^{q-2}+(1-\mu)^{q-2}}{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}.

In addition, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\frac{\mu}{[\mu\phi^{\prime}(\mu)-\phi(\mu)]^{1/2}}

\displaystyle=(q-1)^{1/2}\left\{\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\frac{q\mu\phi_{1}(\mu)-\phi_{0}(\mu)}{\mu^{2}}\right\}^{-1/2}

\displaystyle\overset{(\ddagger)}{=}(q-1)^{1/2}\left\{\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\frac{q\phi_{1}(\mu)+q(q-1)\mu\phi_{2}(\mu)-q\phi_{1}(\mu)}{2\mu}\right\}^{-1/2}

\displaystyle=(q-1)^{1/2}\left\{\frac{q(q-1)}{2}\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}[\mu^{q-2}+(1-\mu)^{q-2}]\right\}^{-1/2}

\displaystyle=(q-1)^{1/2}\cdot\left[\frac{q(q-1)}{2}\right]^{-1/2}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2}{q}}.

F.4
Rényi entropy

For the Rényi entropy

\phi(\mu)=\frac{1}{q-1}\ln\left[\mu^{q}+(1-\mu)^{q}\right],

define

\displaystyle\phi_{0}(\mu)

\displaystyle=\mu^{q}+(1-\mu)^{q},

\displaystyle\phi_{1}(\mu)

\displaystyle=\mu^{q-1}-(1-\mu)^{q-1},

\displaystyle\phi_{2}(\mu)

\displaystyle=\mu^{q-2}+(1-\mu)^{q-2},

\displaystyle\phi_{3}(\mu)

\displaystyle=\mu^{q-3}-(1-\mu)^{q-3}.

When
0<q<2
with
q\neq 1
,

\displaystyle\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\frac{\phi^{\prime}(\mu)}{\mu\phi^{\prime\prime}(\mu)}\left[1-\frac{\phi(\mu)}{\mu\phi^{\prime}(\mu)}\right]

\displaystyle=\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\frac{\frac{\phi_{1}(\mu)}{\phi_{0}(\mu)}}{(q-1)\mu\frac{\phi_{2}(\mu)}{\phi_{0}(\mu)}-q\mu\frac{\phi_{1}(\mu)^{2}}{\phi_{0}(\mu)^{2}}}\left[1-\frac{\frac{1}{q-1}\ln\phi_{0}(\mu)}{\frac{q}{q-1}\mu\frac{\phi_{1}(\mu)}{\phi_{0}(\mu)}}\right]

\displaystyle=\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{(q-1)\frac{\mu\phi_{2}(\mu)}{\phi_{1}(\mu)}-q\frac{\mu\phi_{1}(\mu)}{\phi_{0}(\mu)}}\cdot\left[1-\frac{\phi_{0}(\mu)\ln\phi_{0}(\mu)}{q\mu\phi_{1}(\mu)}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(q-1)\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\frac{\mu\phi_{2}(\mu)}{\phi_{1}(\mu)}-q\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\frac{\mu\phi_{1}(\mu)}{\phi_{0}(\mu)}}\cdot\left[1-\frac{\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\phi_{0}(\mu)}{q}\cdot\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\frac{\ln\phi_{0}(\mu)}{\mu\phi_{1}(\mu)}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(q-1)\cdot 1-q\cdot 0}\cdot\left[1-\frac{1}{q}\cdot 1\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q},

where we use

\phi_{0}(\mu)\to 1,\quad\frac{\mu\phi_{2}(\mu)}{\phi_{1}(\mu)}=\frac{1+\left(\frac{\mu}{1-\mu}\right)^{2-q}}{1-\left(\frac{\mu}{1-\mu}\right)^{2-q}}\to 1,\quad\mu\phi_{1}(\mu)=\mu^{q}-\frac{\mu}{(1-\mu)^{1-q}}\to 0,

and

\displaystyle\frac{\ln\phi_{0}(\mu)}{\mu\phi_{1}(\mu)}

\displaystyle\overset{(\ddagger)}{\to}\frac{1}{\phi_{0}(\mu)}\cdot\frac{\phi_{0}^{\prime}(\mu)}{\mu\phi_{1}^{\prime}(\mu)+\phi_{1}(\mu)}

\displaystyle\to\frac{\phi_{0}^{\prime}(\mu)}{\mu\phi_{1}^{\prime}(\mu)+\phi_{1}(\mu)}

\displaystyle=\frac{q\phi_{1}(\mu)}{(q-1)\mu\phi_{2}(\mu)+\phi_{1}(\mu)}

\displaystyle=\frac{q}{(q-1)\frac{\mu\phi_{2}(\mu)}{\phi_{1}(\mu)}+1}

\displaystyle\to\frac{q}{(q-1)\cdot 1+1}

\displaystyle=1.

In addition, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\frac{\mu}{[\mu\phi^{\prime}(\mu)-\phi(\mu)]^{1/q}}

\displaystyle=\left\{\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\frac{\mu^{q}}{\mu\phi^{\prime}(\mu)-\phi(\mu)}\right\}^{1/q}

\displaystyle=\left\{\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\frac{(q-1)\mu^{q}\phi_{0}(\mu)}{q\mu\phi_{1}(\mu)-\phi_{0}(\mu)\ln\phi_{0}(\mu)}\right\}^{1/q}

\displaystyle=\left\{\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\frac{(q-1)\mu^{q}}{q\mu\phi_{1}(\mu)-\phi_{0}(\mu)\ln\phi_{0}(\mu)}\right\}^{1/q}\qquad\qquad(\phi_{0}(\mu)\to 1)

\displaystyle\overset{(\ddagger)}{=}\left\{(q-1)\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\frac{q\mu^{q-1}}{q\phi_{1}(\mu)+q(q-1)\mu\phi_{2}(\mu)-q\phi_{1}(\mu)\ln\phi_{0}(\mu)-q\phi_{1}(\mu)}\right\}^{1/q}

\displaystyle=\left\{(q-1)\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\frac{\mu^{q-1}}{(q-1)\mu\phi_{2}(\mu)-\phi_{1}(\mu)\ln\phi_{0}(\mu)}\right\}^{1/q}

\displaystyle\overset{(\ddagger)}{=}\left\{(q-1)\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\frac{(q-1)\mu^{q-2}}{(q-1)\phi_{2}(\mu)+(q-1)(q-2)\mu\phi_{3}(\mu)-\frac{q\phi_{1}(\mu)^{2}}{\phi_{0}(\mu)}-(q-1)\phi_{2}(\mu)\ln\phi_{0}(\mu)}\right\}^{1/q}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\!\bigg\{\!\frac{\big[1+\big(\frac{\mu}{1-\mu}\big)^{2-q}\big]\!\!+\!(q-2)\big[1-\big(\frac{\mu}{1-\mu}\big)^{3-q}\big]\!\!-\!\frac{q\phi_{1}(\mu)^{2}}{(q-1)\mu^{q-2}\phi_{0}(\mu)}\!-\!\big[1+\big(\frac{\mu}{1-\mu}\big)^{2-q}\big]\ln\phi_{0}(\mu)}{q-1}\!\bigg\}^{\!\!-\frac{1}{q}}

\displaystyle\overset{\text{(A)}}{=}\left\{\frac{1+(q-2)\cdot 1-0-1\cdot 0}{q-1}\right\}^{-1/q}

\displaystyle=1,

where at (A) we used

\displaystyle\frac{\phi_{1}(\mu)^{2}}{\mu^{q-2}\phi_{0}(\mu)}

\displaystyle\to\mu^{2-q}\phi_{1}(\mu)^{2}

\displaystyle=\mu^{q}-2(1-\mu)^{q-1}\mu+(1-\mu)^{2q-2}\mu^{2-q}

\displaystyle\to-2(1-\mu)^{q-1}\mu+(1-\mu)^{2q-2}\mu^{2-q}

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-\mu)^{2q-2}-2(1-\mu)^{q-1}\mu^{q-1}}{\mu^{q-2}}

\displaystyle\overset{(\ddagger)}{\to}\frac{(2q-2)(1-\mu)^{2q-3}+2(q-1)(1-\mu)^{q-2}\mu^{q-1}-2(q-1)(1-\mu)^{q-1}\mu^{q-2}}{(q-2)\mu^{q-3}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2(q-1)}{q-2}\cdot\frac{1}{(1-\mu)^{2-q}}\cdot\frac{(1-\mu)^{q-1}+\mu^{q-1}-(1-\mu)\mu^{q-2}}{\mu^{q-3}}

\displaystyle\to\frac{2(q-1)}{q-2}\cdot 1\cdot\frac{(1-\mu)^{q-1}+\mu^{q-1}-(1-\mu)\mu^{q-2}}{\mu^{q-3}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2(q-1)}{q-2}\cdot\left[\left(\frac{\mu}{1-\mu}\right)^{1-q}\mu^{2}+\mu^{2}-(1-\mu)\mu\right]

\displaystyle\to 0.

When
q=2
, we leverage

\phi_{0}(\mu)=2\mu^{2}-2\mu+1,\quad\phi_{1}(\mu)=2\mu-1,\quad\phi_{2}(\mu)=2,\quad\phi_{0}^{\prime}(\mu)=2\phi_{1}(\mu),\quad\phi_{1}^{\prime}(\mu)=2

to have

\displaystyle\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\frac{\phi^{\prime}(\mu)}{\mu\phi^{\prime\prime}(\mu)}\left[1-\frac{\phi(\mu)}{\mu\phi^{\prime}(\mu)}\right]

\displaystyle=\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\phi_{0}(\mu)\cdot\frac{2\mu\phi_{1}(\mu)-\phi_{0}(\mu)\ln\phi_{0}(\mu)}{4\mu^{2}[\phi_{0}(\mu)-\phi_{1}(\mu)^{2}]}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\frac{2\mu\phi_{1}(\mu)-\phi_{0}(\mu)\ln\phi_{0}(\mu)}{4\mu^{2}[\phi_{0}(\mu)-\phi_{1}(\mu)^{2}]}

\displaystyle(\phi_{0}(\mu)\to 1)

\displaystyle\overset{(\ddagger)}{=}\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\frac{2[\phi_{1}(\mu)+2\mu]-2\phi_{1}(\mu)\ln\phi_{0}(\mu)-2\phi_{1}(\mu)}{4\{2\mu[\phi_{0}(\mu)-\phi_{1}(\mu)^{2}]+\mu^{2}[2\phi_{1}(\mu)-4\phi_{1}(\mu)]\}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\frac{2\mu-\phi_{1}(\mu)\ln\phi_{0}(\mu)}{4\mu[\phi_{0}(\mu)-\phi_{1}(\mu)^{2}-\mu\phi_{1}(\mu)]}

\displaystyle\overset{(\ddagger)}{=}\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\frac{2-2\ln\phi_{0}(\mu)-\frac{2\phi_{1}(\mu)^{2}}{\phi_{0}(\mu)}}{4[\phi_{0}(\mu)-\phi_{1}(\mu)^{2}-\mu\phi_{1}(\mu)]+4\mu[2\phi_{1}(\mu)-4\phi_{1}(\mu)-\phi_{1}(\mu)-2\mu]}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\frac{1-\ln\phi_{0}(\mu)-\frac{\phi_{1}(\mu)^{2}}{\phi_{0}(\mu)}}{2[\phi_{0}(\mu)-\phi_{1}(\mu)^{2}-4\mu\phi_{1}(\mu)-2\mu^{2}]}

\displaystyle\overset{(\ddagger)}{=}\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\frac{-\frac{2\phi_{1}(\mu)}{\phi_{0}(\mu)}-\frac{4\phi_{0}(\mu)\phi_{1}(\mu)-2\phi_{1}(\mu)^{3}}{\phi_{0}(\mu)^{2}}}{2[2\phi_{1}(\mu)-4\phi_{1}(\mu)-4\phi_{1}(\mu)-8\mu-4\mu]}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\frac{\phi_{1}(\mu)}{\phi_{0}(\mu)^{2}}\cdot\frac{3\phi_{0}(\mu)-\phi_{1}(\mu)^{2}}{6[\phi_{1}(\mu)+2\mu]}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}.

In addition, defining

\zeta\coloneqq\frac{2\mu\phi_{1}(\mu)-\phi_{0}(\mu)\ln\phi_{0}(\mu)}{\phi_{0}(\mu)},

we have

\displaystyle(\xi

\displaystyle\coloneqq)\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\frac{\mu}{[\mu\phi^{\prime}(\mu)-\phi(\mu)]^{1/3}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\frac{\mu}{\left[\frac{2\mu\phi_{1}(\mu)-\phi_{0}(\mu)\ln\phi_{0}(\mu)}{\phi_{0}(\mu)}\right]^{1/3}}\quad\left(\text{implies~~}\xi=\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\mu\zeta^{-1/3}\text{;~we will use this below at (\$)}\right)

\displaystyle\overset{(\ddagger)}{=}\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\frac{3\zeta^{2/3}}{\frac{2\phi_{1}(\mu)}{\phi_{0}(\mu)}+2\mu\frac{2\phi_{0}(\mu)-2\phi_{1}(\mu)^{2}}{\phi_{0}(\mu)^{2}}-\frac{2\phi_{1}(\mu)}{\phi_{0}(\mu)}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\frac{3\phi_{0}(\mu)^{2}}{4}\frac{\zeta^{2/3}}{\mu[\phi_{0}(\mu)-\phi_{1}(\mu)^{2}]}

\displaystyle\overset{(\ddagger)}{=}\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\frac{3}{4}\frac{\frac{2}{3}\cdot 2\mu\frac{2\phi_{0}(\mu)-2\phi_{1}(\mu)^{2}}{\phi_{0}(\mu)^{2}}}{\left\{\phi_{0}(\mu)-\phi_{1}(\mu)^{2}+\mu[2\phi_{1}(\mu)-4\phi_{1}(\mu)]\right\}\zeta^{1/3}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\frac{2\mu[\phi_{0}(\mu)-\phi_{1}(\mu)^{2}]}{[\phi_{0}(\mu)-\phi_{1}(\mu)^{2}-2\mu\phi_{1}(\mu)]\zeta^{1/3}}

\displaystyle\overset{(\ddagger)}{=}\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\frac{3\zeta^{2/3}}{2}\frac{1}{\mu\phi_{0}(\mu)[\phi_{0}(\mu)-\phi_{1}(\mu)^{2}]-3\frac{\phi_{0}(\mu)^{3}[\phi_{1}(\mu)+\mu]\zeta}{\phi_{0}(\mu)-\phi_{1}(\mu)^{2}-2\mu\phi_{1}(\mu)}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\frac{3\zeta^{2/3}}{2}\frac{1}{\mu[\phi_{0}(\mu)-\phi_{1}(\mu)^{2}]+\frac{3\zeta}{\phi_{0}(\mu)-\phi_{1}(\mu)^{2}-2\mu\phi_{1}(\mu)}}

\displaystyle=\left\{\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\frac{2}{3}\frac{\phi_{0}(\mu)-\phi_{1}(\mu)^{2}}{\mu}\cdot(\mu\zeta^{-1/3})^{2}+\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\frac{2\mu}{\phi_{0}(\mu)-\phi_{1}(\mu)^{2}-2\mu\phi_{1}(\mu)}\frac{1}{\mu\zeta^{-1/3}}\right\}^{-1}

\displaystyle\overset{\text{(\$)}}{=}\left\{\frac{2}{3}\xi^{2}\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}(2-2\mu)+\frac{1}{\xi}\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{2-3\mu}\right\}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\left\{\frac{4}{3}\xi^{2}+\frac{1}{2\xi}\right\}^{-1},

which implies

\xi=\frac{1}{\frac{4}{3}\xi^{2}+\frac{1}{2\xi}}.

By solving this, we have

\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\frac{\mu}{[\mu\phi^{\prime}(\mu)-\phi(\mu)]^{1/3}}=\xi=\left(\frac{3}{8}\right)^{1/3}.
