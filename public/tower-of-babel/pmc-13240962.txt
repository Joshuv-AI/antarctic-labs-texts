Novel non-aqueous rotavirus vaccine: Formulated for thermostability and easy administration
Authors: John W. Langston, Melanie Carroll, Mary M. Whalen, Timothy M. Jaw, Leonardo V. Rojas, Carmen C. Hertel, Dmitry Serbzhinskiy, Vrinda Gote, Winston Balasundaram, Subhash Kapre, Ivan A. Olave
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRotavirus vaccination has greatly reduced the number of deaths caused by the virus, lowering childhood mortality by an estimated 37% globally. Although low- and middle-income countries (LMIC) demonstrate reduced efficacy than high-income countries, the vaccines still make a significant impact in these areas and continued efforts to support vaccination are needed. Liquid rotavirus vaccines are desirable for their ease of administration and reduced packing footprint; however, current ready-to-use rotavirus vaccines require refrigerated transport and storage. Here, we describe production methods and stability data for a ready-to use non-aqueous formulation that is suitable for ambient shipping and is shelf-stable. In-line sonication and filtration methods were employed during viral vaccine production to increase viral yields and streamline viral harvest steps. Lyophilized vaccine was formulated with medium-chain triglyceride (MCT) oil and excipients to form a suspension. Stability studies were performed for up to 36 months, and predictive regression analysis was carried out to estimate shelf-life. These results suggest a ready-to-use vaccine candidate with comparable thermostability to lyophilized commercially available products, formulated to a low dosage volume, amenable to ambient shipping with a reduced storage footprint.

Introduction

Diarrheal disease is the third leading cause of pediatric morbidity and mortality globally and the leading cause in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs).1 The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates 1.7 billion cases annually, resulting in the deaths of almost 500,000 children aged 9 and under, with approximately 90% being under age 5.2 Rotavirus infection is the most common etiological agent, accounting for approximately 37% of childhood diarrhea mortality.3,4 Several factors, including the availability of oral rotavirus vaccines, have lowered disease burden and associated mortality in developed countries, reducing the substantive prevalence to LMICs.5 One barrier to improving the availability of rotavirus vaccines in these areas is insufficient cold-chain capacity.5 Current oral liquid rotavirus vaccines (RotaTeqⓇ, ROTARIXⓇ, ROTAVACⓇ, and ROTASIIL-LiquidⓇ) must be stored at 2–8°C to preserve long-term stability with minimal loss of titer.6–10 While there are commercially available lyophilized formulations with robust stability profiles at 25°C (ROTASIIL-ThermoⓇ), end-point use requires solubilization in aqueous diluent prior to administration. Once reconstituted, these solutions have a 6-hour shelf life at 2–8°C.11,12 While addressing cold chain challenges, these formulations introduce complexity at the point of administration, and their packaging footprint must encompass a separate active pharmaceutical ingredient (API), diluent, and delivery device.

The detrimental effects of water on the stability of live attenuated vaccines (LAV) are well recognized and attributed to a myriad of physicochemical mechanisms.13 These effects provide the impetus for the development of freeze-dried and spray-dried formulations. In addition to proper containment, the formulation itself can serve to prevent moisture accumulation in dried LAV drug substance, thereby enhancing thermostability. The work presented here examines the use of medium-chain triglyceride (MCT) oil as a non-aqueous suspension medium for lyophilized live attenuated rotavirus vaccine (LARV). MCT oil has several benefits beyond acting as a carrier and moisture barrier. It is readily transported to and metabolized in the liver, providing an immediately available energy source with little to no storage in adipose tissue and does not require the enzymatic and energetic input necessary for metabolism of long-chain triglycerides.14,15 MCT oil has demonstrated therapeutic benefit in numerous enteric pathologies including celiac disease, Crohn’s disease, and food allergies.16 It is also used to supplement the formula of premature infants, providing a substantial portion of total fat-derived energy.16 Additionally, MCT oil is available commercially in food and pharmaceutical grades (i.e. NEOBEEⓇ M-5, Stepan, Maywood, NJ) and has been given Generally Recognized As Safe (GRAS) status by the United States Food and Drug Administration (US FDA).

LARV stability during gastric transit is also a concern and is addressed in commercial preparations using acid-neutralizing excipients. Calcium carbonate is one such excipient that can neutralize gastric contents and allow the vaccine to migrate through the stomach to the small intestine. To assess suitability of vaccine formulations, several in vitro assays have been employed as cost-effective surrogates for testing stability of oral medications at gastric pH and the acid neutralizing capacity (ANC) of formulation excipients. Here, we utilize the baby Rossett–Rice assay to demonstrate the ANC of antacid excipients delivered in MCT oil.

For these studies, we utilized human-bovine reassortant rotavirus strains developed by National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases (NIAID) and National Institutes of Health (NIH), USA. These rotavirus strains were derived from the United Kingdom (UK) (Compton) bovine rotavirus strain which belongs to the same VP7 serotype as the NCDV strain and the same VP4 serotype as the WC3 strain. The reassortants (D × UK, DS-1 × UK, P × UK, ST3 × UK, and AU32 × UK) contain the VP7 gene from the human serotypes 1, 2, 3, 4, and 9 and the remaining genes from the UK bovine strain and are referred to as G1, G2, G3, G4, and G9, respectively. These strains are safe, immunogenic, and less reactogenic than human equivalents17 and are currently used in approved rotavirus vaccines.

Here, we demonstrate a non-aqueous ready-to-use live attenuated rotavirus vaccine, formulated with MCT oil, that is shelf-stable for over 24 months at both 2–8°C and 23–27°C. Shelf-life predictive analysis shows the vaccine to be stable for at least 28 months at 23–27°C and 57 months at 2–8°C, making it a highly suitable candidate for LMIC markets. The thermostable, ready-to-use formulation minimizes need for sophisticated cold-chain requirements and additional preparations before administration.

Materials and methods

Vero cells

African Green Monkey Kidney cells (VERO-WHO cells) were sourced from Ministry of Food and Drug Safety, South Korea (Catalog No.: 88020401).

Human reassortant rotavirus strains G1, G2, G3, G4, and G9 were developed by National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases (NIAID) and National Institutes of Health (NIH), USA.

Monovalent rotavirus manufacture

Vero cell culture

Vero cells were revived and sequentially expanded from T-175 to Cell Factory 10 (CF10) in Opti-Pro serum-free medium (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA) in a humidified incubator at 37°C and 5% CO2. Once CF10 monolayers were confluent, the cells were utilized for viral production.

Viral production

Rotavirus working viral seed (WVS) was activated by incubating with recombinant Trypsin (Roche Custom Biotech, Indianapolis, IN) in Opti-Pro at 37°C for 60 minutes, prior to adsorption on confluent Vero monolayers at 37°C for 60 minutes. Following adsorption, harvest media (Opti-Pro containing 5 mM calcium chloride and rTrypsin) was added to the CF10 to a final total volume of 3.5 L. Vessels were incubated at 37°C and 5.0% CO2 for 72 hours.

Monovalent harvest, sonication, clarification, and stabilization

After 72 hours, the crude bulk was pumped from the CF10 into a 5 L storage bottle. The remaining adherent cellular material was lifted via trypsinization and transferred to the crude bulk. The harvested bulk was then homogenized using a Hielscher UP400St ultrasonicator (Hielscher Ultrasonics, Teltow, Germany) followed by clarification through 1.2 µm and 0.8/0.45 µm capsule filters (Sartorius Stedim Biotech, Edgewood, NY). The clarified bulk was stabilized by addition of sucrose, 4-(2-hydroxyethyl)-1-piperazineethanesulfonic acid (HEPES), and monosodium glutamate (Millipore Sigma, Burlington, MA) to final concentrations of 7.5%, 25.0 mM, and 4.9 mM, respectively.

Pentavalent rotavirus formulation

Freeze drying

Rotavirus serotypes G1, G2, G3, G4, and G9 were combined to form a pentavalent mixture of 6.5 Log10 Infectivity Units (IU)/mL/serotype. Additional buffers and stabilizers were mixed to the following final concentrations: 9.5% sucrose, 40 mM HEPES, 2.94 mM glutamate, 4.0% L-arginine, 30 mM NaCl. The bulk mixture was evenly aliquoted into GoreⓇ LyoguardⓇ lyophilization trays (GORE; Newark, DE) and transferred to a Virtis Ultra 50 L freeze dryer (SP Scientific, Irvine, CA). Thermal treatment of the material consisted of freezing at a shelf temperature of −40°C for 2 hours, followed by annealing at −25°C for 5 hours. Primary drying was performed at a shelf temperature of −40°C and a vacuum pressure of 38 millitorr (mT) for 30 hours, then warmed to −30°C at 38 mT for 10 hours, then −25°C at 38 mT for 19 hours, and finally at −14°C and 15 mT for 10 hours. Secondary drying was performed at a shelf temperature of 35°C and 15 mT vacuum pressure for 15 hours.

Jet milling and final formulation

Freeze-dried material was transferred to a glove box isolator and micronized inside a 2-inch Micron Master Jet Pulverizer (The Jet Pulverizer Co., Moorestown, NJ) utilizing nitrogen as the motive gas. The isolator was also flushed with nitrogen to maintain a relative humidity (RH) ≤6% during the entire process. A Schenck AccuRate model 106 volumetric feeder (Schenck Process GmbH, Darmstadt, Germany) was used to control delivery of material to the jet mill. The lyophilized product was milled, collected, and weighed, and the weight of the milled material was used to calculate the addition of final excipients. Half of the calculated amounts of trisodium citrate (Millipore Sigma, Burlington, MA), calcium carbonate (Specialty Minerals, Bethlehem, PA), and MCT oil (Stepan, Maywood, NJ) were added to the milled formulation, and the mixture was homogenized using a Silverson L5M-A Laboratory Mixer (Silverson Machines, East Longmeadow, MA). The remaining half of the excipients were then added, and homogenization was repeated. Mixing was performed under nitrogen to maintain dryness. The final vaccine formulation was then dispensed into standard 1.0 mL glass syringes (BD, Franklin Lakes, NJ).

Baby Rossett–Rice (BRR) assay for buffering capacity evaluation

A mixture of 0.5 mL of formulated pentavalent rotavirus vaccine and 9.5 mL of water was brought to 37°C in a 50 mL beaker under constant stirring. A 4.0 mL bolus of 0.1 N hydrochloric acid (HCl) was added and then delivered at a constant rate of 0.5 mL/min. The pH was recorded prior to, and then for a 20 min duration of, HCl addition.

Gas chromatography mass spectrometry (GCMS) analysis of MCT oil stability

Samples of NEOBEEⓇ M-5 MCT oil were diluted 1:100 or 1:1000 in hexane and analyzed on a GCMS-QP2010 (Shimadzu Scientific Instruments, Columbia, MD). Samples were vaporized at 325°C prior to injection onto an RtxⓇ-5MS column (Restek, Bellefonte, PA). The column oven was held at 130°C for 1 minute, then ramped at 30°C/minute up to 325°C and held constant for 7 minutes (14.5 minutes/sample). Helium gas was maintained at a constant linear velocity of 47.2 cm/sec.

Monovalent rotavirus titer determination by quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

MA104 cells were seeded into 96-well culture plates with complete DMEM (DMEM, 10% HI-FBS, 1 × Antibiotic/Antimycotic, 1 × GlutaMax) (Thermo Fisher) and grown to confluency. Rotavirus samples and serotype matched reference standards were activated with rTrypsin, subject to 10-fold serial dilution, then adsorbed on MA104 cell monolayers for 1-hour in infection medium (DMEM + GlutaMax). Additional medium was added to wells and incubated at 37°C for 18–19 hours. Cells were lysed with 4.5% Triton X-100 and stored at <−70°C. Once frozen, culture plates were thawed, and cells disrupted by pipetting. Cellular debris was removed by centrifugation and supernatant diluted for qRT-PCR.

Infection supernatant was diluted and denatured. 2× Platinum™ Hot Start PCR Master Mix, Superscipt III/Platinum Taq DNA polymerase, RNase Out, ROX reference Dye (Thermo Fisher), and serotype-specific primers and TaqMan probes (Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT)) were combined and mixed with denatured supernatant. qRT-PCR was run on Quantstudio 7 Pro (Applied Biosystems). A standard curve of C(t) values vs reference standard titer was plotted and unknown samples interpolated.

Pentavalent rotavirus titer determination by qRT-PCR

The infection procedure was carried out as described above, adapted from published methods,18 using matrix matched pentavalent rotavirus reference standard with formulated pentavalent vaccine. Standards and samples were subject to 2-fold serial dilutions before adsorption to cells. Pentavalent rotavirus qRT-PCR was achieved by mixing TaqPath 1-Step Multiplex Mastermix (No ROX), Superscipt III/Platinum Taq DNA polymerase, RNase Out (Thermo Fisher), and serotype-specific primers and TaqMan probes with denatured diluted supernatant. Each serotype-specific probe was coupled with a distinct fluorophore possessing wavelength emission corresponding to distinct channels of the instrument, allowing simultaneous detection and quantification of all five serotypes.

Karl Fischer moisture content analysis

Pentavalent rotavirus formulation was dispensed, sealed, and crimped in 2 mL glass vials flushed with dry nitrogen. Extra dry 99.9% methanol (Thermo Fisher) was added to the vial at 2× total dose volume. Vials were vortexed to mix and left to phase separation. The aqueous phase was removed using a 1 mL glass syringe and 21G needle, weighed on an analytical balance, then added to a Mettler Toledo C20 Coulometric Titrator (Thermo Fisher). The volume of methanol added was back-calculated and total water content was adjusted according to the total volume added to the machine. Water content in a 0.1% water standard and methanol was determined to account for any fluctuations in total water content between testing using Hydranol Coulomat AG (Thermo Fisher).

Statistics

Rotavirus titers in downstream processing steps were assessed for statistical significance by one-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) with Dunnett’s multiple comparisons to Monovalent Pool (α = 0.05) using GraphPad Prism version 10.1.2. Shelf-life predictive analysis was carried out using Minitab Stability Study function, with the Lower Specification limit set to 5.4 Log10 IU/mL, and a confidence interval of 95%.

Results

Production of monovalent bulk material

Preliminary rotavirus bulk production was based on established methods19 and consisted of cell growth, viral infection, freeze–thaws to facilitate cell lysis, followed by clarification and stabilization of the harvested bulk material. All steps were performed manually, which was time consuming, and introduced risks to aseptic processing associated with human intervention. To address these concerns, the process was iteratively optimized, ultimately combining in-line sonication and clarification, using a peristaltic pump to transfer the bulk material. To assess the impact of sonication and subsequent clarification on viral titers, rotavirus monovalent pools for serotypes G1, G2, G3, G4, and G9 underwent sonication, clarification, and stabilization. Titer analysis was performed after each process step and showed that sonication significantly increased viral titers for all serotypes compared to monovalent pools (Figure 2). Clarification led to some loss as anticipated; however, titers in final bulk were significantly greater than monovalent pools, except for G1, where there was no net change to monovalent pool (Table 1). The combination of sonication and clarification directly from the culture vessel saves considerable time during downstream processing, reduces consumption of single-use containers necessary for bulk storage at intermediate points prior to process completion, and significantly restricts manual intervention during processing. Using this methodology, we were consistently capable of producing 3–5 L batches of monovalent bulk material at titers ~≥7.0 Log10 IU/mL for all serotypes. Formulation development

In considering MCT oil for use in vaccine formulation, an assessment of the material’s stability in high-temperature environments for prolonged periods was performed. Fresh MCT oil was tested by GCMS and was subsequently tested after 6-months storage at 2–8°C, 25°C/60% Relative Humidity (RH) or 40°C/75% RH (Table 2). The MCT oil used in formulation was NEOBEEⓇ M-5 from Stepan Specialty Products and is a mixture of triglycerides containing caprylic (C8) and capric (C10) fatty acids at a 2:1 ratio, respectively.20 Total ion mass chromatograms of MCT oil showed 4 distinct peaks within retention times between 7 and 10 minutes (Table 2). Peaks 1 and 4 were identified as tricaprylic triglyceride and tricapric triglyceride, respectively, with 97% probability. Peaks 2 and 3 are presumably the dicaprylic monocapric triglyceride and the dicapric monocaprylic triglyceride, respectively, but could not be identified with high confidence as their spectra are not available in the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) mass spectrometry databases. For these reasons, direct comparison of peak areas was avoided in favor of peak area ratios. Overall, peak area ratios (Figure 3) and peak retention times (Table 2) were unchanged over 6 months at all conditions tested, indicating robust stability of the MCT oil and supporting its use in the vaccine formulation. Rotaviruses are known to be acid-labile and are rapidly inactivated in the stomach gastric acid with a half-life of seconds at pH 2.0, about 12 minutes at pH 3.0, and stable at pH 4.0.21 Since rotavirus vaccines are orally administered, to increase their effectiveness, they require a buffering system before or during vaccination to counteract their inactivation in an infant stomach acidic environment (pH 1.8–2.0). Multiple assays have been developed to study the effectiveness of antacids in an acidic environment.22 Among them, the Baby Rossett–Rice (BRR) assay is regarded as the best in vitro assay that closely mimics the stomach and its acid secretion in a 6-month-old infant.22–24 Commercially available freeze-dried rotavirus vaccines contain citrate and bicarbonate buffers in aqueous diluent that serve to neutralize gastric acidity with oral administration.6,7 To determine the buffering capacity of these antacids when suspended in MCT oil, we employed a modified version of the Baby Rossett–Rice25 assay with the objective of measuring the length of time these buffer systems were able to maintain pH above 4.0. We tested the acid neutralizing capacity (ANC) of 0.5 mL aliquots of dried, milled placebo, and buffering salts suspended in MCT oil (Table 3) in multiple independent experiments. At concentrations of 51 g/L and 120 g/L in MCT oil, respectively, sodium citrate and calcium carbonate were able to maintain pH at or above 4.0 for a 20-minute duration, the estimated time for gastric emptying in an infant’s stomach (Figure 4). Based on these results, a formulation consisting of lyophilized live oral rotavirus formulated with sodium citrate and calcium carbonate, suspended in MCT oil was considered suitable for further pursuit. Pentavalent rotavirus formulation

Rotavirus bulks were mixed with excipients and subject to lyophilization as described in materials and methods. The lyophilized bulk material and buffering components were milled separately using a 2-inch Micron Master Jet Pulverizer to obtain micronized particles <5 µm in size. Material was delivered to the jet mill using a volumetric feeder to accurately control the feed rate during micronization. The feeder and mill were enclosed inside a custom containment system which maintained a high purity nitrogen gas environment to ensure milling was performed under low moisture conditions.

Trays of freeze-dried material were transferred into the isolator under low moisture conditions, and the cakes were broken up and passed to the feeder through an 18-mesh screen to ensure the material was suitably sized for milling. The motive force for micronization was achieved using high purity compressed nitrogen gas delivered from a custom manifold at 20 standard cubic feet per minute (scfm) and 100 psi of pressure to a pressure header designed to generate 3–15 pound per square inch (psi) of pressure inside the grinding chamber. Micronized material was collected in a 2 L stainless steel bottle attached directly to the mill. The feeder and jet mill assembly are shown in Figure 5. Early proof-of-concept studies with monovalent rotavirus lyophilized suspensions in MCT oil indicated that rotavirus vaccines in this formulation would exhibit robust stability profile in a ready-to-use suspension (Supplementary Figure 1). To evaluate this further, formulated pentavalent rotavirus vaccine was dispensed into glass syringes and subject to long-term stability studies at 2–8°C, 23–27°C with 60% RH, and 28–32°C with 65% RH (Figure 7). At each time point vaccines were assessed for titer by qRT-PCR, moisture content, appearance, and extractable volume. Results for titer are displayed in Figure 7, while moisture content is displayed in Table 4. Appearance was consistently a white to pink suspension free from visible particulates throughout the study, and extractable volume was greater than the dose volume of 0.5 mL at each time point. Potency values above the lower limit were observed for 24 months when stored at 23–27°C/60% RH, and to 36 months when stored at 2–8°C, measured by titer remaining above the target dose of 5.4 Log10 IU/mL, and moisture content remaining below 1%. Accelerated studies for 6-months at 28–32°C/65% RH showed no significant changes in titer or moisture content of the drug product. Unsurprisingly, viral titer decreased faster at 23–27°C than at 2–8°C, and inherent variability of biological assays contributed to unexpected fluctuations in titer values throughout the study. Nevertheless, robust stability was observed at each condition. Discussion

To improve the prevention of diarrheal disease produced by rotavirus infection in infants of low- and medium-income countries, it is imperative to develop a second generation of a live attenuated oral rotavirus vaccine that is potent, low volume, cost-effective, thermo-stable at ambient temperature, and does not require reconstitution or external buffering systems.

In this manuscript, we described the development of a novel non-aqueous thermo-stable rotavirus vaccine formulation with a reduced dosing volume (0.5 mL/dose) predicted to maintain rotavirus stability at 23–27°C for more than 2 years and at 2–8°C for over 4 years. The vaccine is composed of a lyophilized mixture of pentavalent rotavirus drug substance, cryopreservatives, stabilizers, and buffering excipients micronized to particle sizes no larger than 5 µm which are suspended in MCT oil, thus eliminating a reconstitution step at the point of administration. Current WHO-prequalified LARVs are presented as either buffered, liquid formulations which require refrigerated (or frozen) storage6–10 or as lyophilized preparations that have a better thermostability profile but must be resuspended in buffered diluent prior to administration.11 Single dosage volumes range from 0.5 to 2.5 mL and can be challenging to deliver orally to infants at higher volumes. While lyophilized formulations enhance thermostability and shelf life, that shelf life is reduced to 6 hours at 2–8°C once reconstituted.

The formulation presented here attempts to address several current issues inflating the cost of delivery. The enhanced thermostability profile would allow storage completely outside of the cold chain, significantly decreasing storage and transport costs. This could translate to increased coverage into more rural areas with little- or no cold-chain infrastructure. Another factor affecting the cost of delivery is accidental freezing, which occurs across all segments of the cold chain.26,27 This is particularly a problem with liquid vaccines, several of which cannot be administered after freezing and thawing. Stakeholders from a number of nations have expressed that addressing just this issue alone could significantly improve their vaccination programs.28 Storage outside of the cold chain is arguably the most practical and cost-effective solution to this problem.

Lyophilized drug substances are extremely hygroscopic, and its stability is directly related to its dryness. Here, we show that MCT oil can provide an effective moisture barrier, completely preventing water accumulation in the suspension at 23–27°C and 60% RH for 36 months, and at 28–32°C and 65% RH for 6 months. Not only does suspension in MCT oil keep the lyophilized API dry and potent, it also allows for direct filling into ready-to-use syringes. MCT oil reacts with most common pharmaceutical plastics (i.e. polyethylene, polycarbonate, PET, PETG) to some degree, which necessitates filling into glass. Furthermore, the extractables and leachables (E&L) profile of MCT oil with most pharmaceutical rubbers is not well characterized. Nevertheless, this presentation is advantageous in that it eliminates reconstitution in diluent at the point of administration, shrinking the storage footprint and administration time significantly. It also minimizes wastage associated with multiple dose containers.

In addition to being an effective moisture barrier, MCT oil possesses other qualities that make it a suitable carrier for orally administered pharmaceuticals, particularly for infants. Infant formulas containing MCT oil as the primary fat-derived energy source have been commercially available for several decades for infants with poor metabolism of longer chain fatty acids. Medium-chain fatty acids and their triglycerides, including capric and caprylic triglycerides, have demonstrated in vitro and in vivo antimicrobial activity.29,30 Furthermore, MCT oil supplementation has also been reported to reduce the enteric Candida burden in premature infants.31 Initial studies presented here indicate robust stability at 30°C and 65% relative humidity for 6 months, but stability over longer time frames and in mixture with lyophilized rotavirus vaccine needs to be characterized.

As part of the downstream processing of monovalent bulk material, we have incorporated a continuous in-line sonication step which improves the final bulk titer, presumably by improving recovery of viral particles from intact cells and cellular debris. Large volume sonication solutions capable of processing more than 1000 L/hour are available commercially for industrial-scale applications including cellular extraction. The equipment allows for temperature control, is clean-in-place (CIP) and sterilization-in-place (SIP) compatible, and can be run continuously. While equipment is available, whether the effects of ultrasonication on rotavirus recovery presented here are observed at commercial scale still needs to be determined.

Whereas lyophilized formulations currently on the market are filled as liquid prior to freeze drying, the process presented here involves micronization of the lyophilized bulk and acid-neutralizing excipients prior to filling. The ability to generate uniformly sized particles less than 5 µm in diameter has several important benefits. It increases the surface area that interacts with MCT oil, facilitating mixing and homogeneity of the suspension, which supports uniform filling and dosing consistency. It also increases particle solubility, aiding pH neutralization of gastric content, enteric bioavailability, and thus potency.32 Another important factor is the influence of particle size in relation to its organoleptic properties; particles above a certain size are detected in the mouth and give a coarse texture.33 A suspension of particles 5 µm in diameter should avoid sensory coarseness which, together with a minimal dosing volume of 0.5 mL, should aid oral administration to infants.

We acknowledge that this study is confounded by the following limitations. Firstly, only a single batch of live oral pentavalent rotavirus vaccine was available for stability studies of drug product (DP), limiting the data for shelf-life estimation. Proof-of-concept studies were performed with monovalent rotavirus formulated by the same method and showed robust stability to 6 months (Supplementary data); however, no amount of predictive analysis can replace long-term stability studies on multiple batches of DP.

Mathematical models for shelf-life predictive analysis are historically based on linear regression methods. Sophisticated modeling methods are now available that may be more suitable for biologicals such as live attenuated vaccines,34–36 however, for this work we employed methods outlined in ICH Q1E Evaluation for Stability data since it is the current guideline for shelf life evaluation.

An additional limitation is the lack of immunogenicity data to assess the efficacy of the vaccine. A major limitation of rotavirus vaccine development is the lack of appropriate pre-clinical small animal models that are predictive of clinical efficacy in humans.37,38 Larger animal models may provide more insight but are beyond the scope of this study. Without suitable animal models, the impact of MCT oil on vaccine efficacy cannot be properly evaluated. Without this information, the safety of this vaccine can only be approximated by the safety of its constituents. At minimum, an evaluation of the safety of this MCT-based rotavirus vaccine is warranted and ideally paired with immunogenicity evaluation.

Rotavirus vaccine stability can be broadly addressed via the cold-chain infrastructure or modifications to the formulation. From a time and cost perspective, the choice is self-evident. This work demonstrates the feasibility of preparation of lyophilized rotavirus vaccine, suspended and kept dry in MCT oil, dispensed as a single dose into 1 mL glass syringes ready to administer. This formulation retains a robust thermostability profile characteristic of freeze-dried formulations, it optimizes the storage footprint by eliminating diluent and delivery device, and minimizes dosing volume and administration time. These features, together with the stability profile, are ideal for a vaccine candidate in LMIC.
