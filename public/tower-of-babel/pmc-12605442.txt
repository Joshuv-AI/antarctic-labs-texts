Uranium concentration in children’s hair samples and residential soil samples near mine tailings facilities, in Johannesburg, South Africa
Authors: Busisiwe Shezi, Renée Street, Vusumuzi Nkosi, Frank Winde, Helga Nuernberger, Joachim Schüz, Susanne Sachs, Ljubica Zupunski, Evgenia Ostroumova, Jana Seibt, Rahel Bertheau, Richard Husar, Uta Czeslik, Angela Mathee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCommunities near gold mine tailings are at risk of exposure to toxic substances, with children being particularly vulnerable. This comparative cross-sectional study evaluated uranium concentrations in hair samples collected from children and in soil samples from their residential areas near gold mine tailings in Johannesburg(exposed group) and compared them with those from Durban reference sites (unexposed group). A total of 406 participants aged 0–18 years were included. Out of those participants 208 were from Johannesburg (exposed group) and 198 were from Durban (unexposed group). Data collection involved the collection of hair and soil samples, administration of questionnaires, and anthropometric measurements. Most participants were female (54.2%). The mean age was 10 years (SD = 9.6). The majority of participants families (60.3%) relied solely on social grants. The median uranium concentration was significantly higher in the exposed group (17.07 µg/kg; range: 0.73–323) than in the unexposed group (8.12 µg/kg; range: 0.47–1131, p < 0.001). Girls (18.83 µg/kg) had higher uranium levels than boys (7.92 µg/kg) (p < 0.001), and younger children (not yet attending school) had the highest concentrations (18.67 µg/kg) (p < 0.001). Among the exposed group, girls (23.50 µg/kg) had higher uranium levels than boys (11.77 µg/kg), and children not yet attending school (21.31 µg/kg) and those attending preschool (25.37 µg/kg) had the highest concentrations. Targeted public health interventions and environmental mitigation strategies are necessary to reduce exposure risks in affected communities.Keywords: Mining, Uranium, Mine tailings facilities, Children, Hair

Introduction

Uranium (U) is a naturally occurring radioactive heavy metal that occurs as three isotopes: 234U, 235U, and 238U. 235U is widely used in the energy and military sectors, and the extraction of U-ore by the mining industry has led to persistent environmental contamination, particularly near mining operations. Mine tailings, a byproduct of mining activities, often contain elevated levels of uranium and other heavy metals. The dust that blows away from mine tailings may contain long-lived radionuclides (e.g., 238U and decay products like radium-226, radon, etc.) that are disseminated into the environment (Yong et al., 2021). Populations living near these mining areas are at risk of exposure, with children being particularly vulnerable.

Human exposure to uranium can occur via air, water, soil, and food, particularly vegetables and cereals grown in contaminated environments (Keith et al., 2013). While most ingested uranium is excreted, some is absorbed and accumulates in the bones, liver, and kidneys (National Research Council, 2012; The Royal Society, 2001). The biological half-life of uranium in bones is 70–200 days (ATSDR, 2013). Uranium exposure mainly causes chemical toxicity, particularly nephrotoxicity, by damaging the kidneys. Some studies suggest links to lung and kidney cancers, but evidence is inconclusive due to confounding factors like radon exposure, a radioactive gas produced as a decay product of uranium (ATSDR, 2013). Animal studies confirm uranium’s renal and neurological effects, with limited data on reproductive or developmental impacts (NRC, 2006). Emerging evidence points to broader toxicity, including endocrine disruption, teratogenicity, and genetic damage (Brugge & Buchner, 2011). Although naturally occurring uranium is both chemically and radiologically toxic, its chemical toxicity is generally considered to predominate due to its long half-life and consequently low specific activity. Nevertheless, the potential radiotoxic effects associated with uranium’s radioactive decay products should not be overlooked in exposure assessments. For example, studies of German (Brüske-Hohlfeld et al., 2006), French (Rage et al., 2015), Czech (Řeřicha et al., 2006), Canadian, and US-American (Schubauer-Berigan et al., 2009) uranium miners found increased risks of lung cancer, emphysema, and reduced lung function, mainly due to radon exposure. A pooled cohort study of 57,873 miners across five countries reported 1,217 lung cancer deaths, with a linear rise in risk linked to cumulative radon exposure (Richardson et al., 2022).

Children are more vulnerable to uranium exposure due to their developing nervous systems, higher absorption rates, lower body weight, and hand-to-mouth activities (Zhang et al., 2025). Although research on uranium exposure in children is limited, evidence shows that even low-level exposure to toxic pollutants can cause harm. Studies in children aged 3–6 years found higher uranium levels in hair among highly exposed children (40 μg/kg) compared to those less exposed (20 μg/kg) (Park et al., 2007), with adolescents (12–18 years old) situated in unexposed communities having lower uranium levels (3 μg/kg) (Carneiro et al., 2011).

For more than a century, mining has been a cornerstone of the South African economy (Wright et al., 2017). The 1886 gold discovery on the Highveld led to rapid urbanization and the rise of Johannesburg. Today, large gold mine tailings near residential areas pose a continued risk of exposure to toxic substances like lead, arsenic, and uranium, especially for nearby low-income communities. Many gold ores also contain uranium, which is unintentionally brought to the surface during extraction. Mine tailings facilities in the Witwatersrand Basin cover around 400 km2, often in regions that are densely populated. The gold mine tailings in Johannesburg have an average uranium concentration of about 100 000 μg/kg U3O8 (ranging from about 10 000 μg/kg to several hundred mg/kg), which equals or exceeds the concentrations found in tailings of dedicated uranium mines, for example, those in Germany or Namibia (Schonfeld et al., 2014; Winde & Sandham, 2004; Zupunski et al., 2023). In Gauteng Province alone, approximately 1.6 million people live close to, or on top of mine tailings facilities (Njinga & Tshivhase, 2016). Studies undertaken in Johannesburg showed that local tailings deposits are characterized by elevated levels of uranium, which may have the potential to cause cancer in local populations, particularly from uranium decay products such as radon (Winde & Sandham, 2004; Zupunski et al., 2023). The pathways through which local residents are exposed include inhalation of windblown tailings dust and radon, consumption of polluted river water and groundwater, ingestion of food produced with contaminated water (including home-grown vegetables), meat and milk from domestic livestock, fish captured from polluted water bodies, geophagia (intentional ingestion of soil) and unintentional (hand-to-mouth) consumption of contaminated soil, sediment and tailings material (Mphaga et al., 2024; Winde & Sandham, 2004).

Exposure during high wind seasons (typically late winter to early summer in many parts of South Africa) is a particular concern since toxic substances attached to dust particles may be carried over long distances via wind, soil or water (Csavina et al., 2012). Exposure assessment studies undertaken in Johannesburg have shown that air pollution levels often exceed national guideline levels (Dudu et al., 2018). Many Johannesburg settlements lie within 100 m of mining land. A 2013 expert workshop reviewing uranium surveys in the area recommended direct human biomonitoring to accurately assess exposure levels (Schonfeld et al., 2014). A subsequent study of hair collected from barber shops around gold tailings deposits in Johannesburg found uranium concentrations ranging from 31 to 2524 μg/kg (mean = 192 μg/kg) (Winde et al., 2019). A further study undertaken in Soweto found that communities living close to mine tailings facilities had similarly elevated 238U concentrations in hair samples, ranging from 22 to 1809 μg/kg (mean = 142 μg/kg), with children having statistically significantly higher uranium concentrations than adults (Zupunski et al., 2023). However, there continues to be a paucity of data on human exposure to metals near mine tailings facilities, particularly regarding children’s exposure to uranium. This study aims to characterize uranium concentrations by analyzing hair samples from children living near mine tailing facilities in Johannesburg and comparing them to children living in Durban, where no gold mining activities ever took place.

Methods

Study design, setting and population

This cross-sectional study was conducted over eleven months, commencing in January 2022. The study sites included the suburbs of Riverlea, Tshepisong, and Snake Park, which are situated in close proximity to mine tailings facilities in Johannesburg, with distances ranging from 0 to 2.84 km. Newlands East and Newlands West, located in the north of Durban, were selected as reference sites without U-related mining activities. According to Statistics South Africa, the population size of Riverlea, Snake Park, and Tshepisong is approximately 16,000, 40,000, and 72,000 people, respectively, with children under 18 years of age comprising 35, 39, and 27%. The reference sites, Newlands West and Newlands East, have estimated populations of 50,000 and 51,000 people, with children under 18 years accounting for 34 and 36%, respectively (Statistics South Africa, 2011). While the reference areas exhibit socio-economic conditions comparable to those of the Johannesburg study sites, these suburbs are situated in less industrialized areas than the Johannesburg study sites. Throughout this article, the sites in Johannesburg are referred to as “exposed group” and those near Durban as “unexposed group”.

Sampling strategy, selection of homes and participants

Participants in both the exposed and unexposed groups were categorized into predefined age categories: 0–4, 5–9, 10–14, and 15–18 years. The exposed group comprised 55 participants aged 0–4 years, 50 aged 5–9 years, 52 aged 10–14 years, and 51 aged 15–18 years. Similarly, the unexposed group included 46 participants aged 0–4 years, 51 aged 5–9 years, 50 aged 10-14 years, 51 aged 15–18 years. In each home, after seeking parental consent, one child was selected to participate in the study. We aimed to recruit a predefined, equal number of participants from each age group. The study was approved by the SAMRC Human Research Ethics Committee (EC042-10/2021).

Data collection techniques and instruments

Hair: Hair samples were cut from the nape of the neck, as close to the skin as possible, using stainless steel scissors, and placed in sealed polyethylene bags to prevent contamination before laboratory analysis. Researchers collected hair from each child, aiming for at least 1 cm in length as per the protocol. During participant selection, we assessed the hair length of potential participants. If a child’s hair was too short, we deferred sample collection and scheduled a follow-up visit after 3–4 weeks. Hair samples were collected during this second visit. However, in some cases, the available hair remained shorter than 1 cm, and a few participants had only a small amount of hair available for collection. Each sample bag was labeled with a unique identifier for the participant, and a field data log was used to record the exact time during which the hair was sampled.

Soil: Soil samples were collected from all the homes. If the area was paved, dust was swept off the sampling area. At each sampling point, five subsamples were collected using stainless steel shovels, following the guidelines set by the US EPA (US EPA, 2023). A global positioning system (Garmin, Olathe, KS, USA) was used to record the exact latitude and longitude of each sampling location. The locations of the soil samples were recorded on the sampling form.

Questionnaire: A questionnaire was administered by trained interviewers to gather essential information about the child, including age, sex, occupation of the parents, race/ethnicity, period of residence in the current dwelling, health status, source of income, presence of pets/animals, water source, and whether the participants grew their own food in the garden.

Weight and Height in Children: We measured the weight and height of children. Research assistants received training to perform anthropometric measurements following standard procedures recommended by the Centre for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC, 2024). Weight was recorded in kilograms using a calibrated digital scale (D-Quip Glass Bathroom Scale (Maximum: 150 kg, Minimum: 2 kg)). Standing height was measured using a stadiometer (Scale2000, Model: 213 1,721,009). For infants, weight was measured using the Charder Infant Scale (capacity: 20 kg, precision: 0.01 kg; model: MS 3500, max load: 10/20 kg), and length was measured using the Charder HM 8 M (graduation: 1 mm; measurement range: 8–35 cm).

Hair sample preparation and analysis

To determine uranium concentration in human hair, each hair sample was first milled, homogenized, and washed according to the following procedure. Using stainless steel tweezers, individual hair samples were manually transferred from the plastic bags into 2 mL reinforced plastic tubes (Biolabproducts GmbH) for milling/ homogenization, and, if needed, were coarsely chopped with stainless steel scissors before being deposited into the tube. Metal beads (NiRosta steel, W 1.4301; 21-25-NS-30.1, Biolabproducts GmbH) recommended for hair processing, were added to the plastic tubes as milling agents. The samples were then homogenized through milling using a bead mill homogenizer (Bead Ruptor 24, Omni International Inc.). The milled hair was then transferred into pre-weighed 15 mL polypropylene tubes (CELLSTAR, Greiner Bio-One). For larger hair samples, the process was repeated with individual aliquots and the portions were combined into one polypropylene tube. The total mass of the homogenized hair was measured. In the next step, the milled hair was washed in the 15 mL polypropylene tubes to eliminate possible external contaminants, such as dust. The washing procedure included the following steps: (1) washing with 10 mL ultra-pure water (PURELAB Ultra Genetic, Elga), (2) washing with 10 mL 0.1% Triton-X-100 solution (purest, Carl Roth GmbH), (3) rinsing with 10 mL ultra-pure water, (4) rinsing with 10 mL ultra-pure water. For each washing or rinsing step, the samples were placed into an ultrasonic bath (Ultrasonic Cleaner USC-TH, VWR) for 5 min. After that, the washing or rinsing solutions were separated from the hair by centrifugation at 3500 g for 5 min (Centrifuge 5810R, Eppendorf) and the resulting supernatants were removed with a pipette. Finally, the milled, and washed hair was rinsed with acetone (for residue analysis, Fisher Scientific). After removing the acetone with a pipette, the samples were air-dried for at least 72 h under a fume hood at room temperature and the final weight of each individual homogenized, washed, and air-dried hair sample was determined.

The digestion of the homogenized hair samples was performed by weighing aliquots of the homogenized hair into 50 mL DigiTubes (SCP Science) that were previously heated to 105 °C for 2 h to remove any volatile substances from the tubes and subsequently cooled down to room temperature in a desiccator. When available, approximately 100 mg of hair was used. For samples with less material, the entire sample was utilized. The latter affected about 40% of the samples. The hair was then dried in DigiTubes at 105 °C for 16 h in a drying oven. After that, the tubes were cooled down to room temperature in a desiccator and the dry weight of the hair sample was determined. Independently, the dry substance of 38 hair samples of which sufficient sample material was available (> 200 mg) was determined in glass vials. For hair digestion, a mixture of 4 mL HNO3 (69%, ARISTAR, VWR) and 1 mL H2O2 (30%, traceSelect, Sigma-Aldrich) was added to the hair in the DigiTubes. The resulting suspensions were treated for 20 min in an ultrasonic bath. Subsequently, the hair was digested at 120 °C for 5 h under reflux. After cooling, the samples were filled up to a final volume of 25 mL with Milli-Q water (Milli-Q®Reference, Merck MilliPore). In parallel with the hair samples, blank samples consisting of 4 mL HNO3 and 1 mL H2O2 were included in each digestion cycle. In addition, digestions of two certified hair standards (ERM-DB001 human hair, JRC, Institute for Reference Materials and Measurements; QM-H-1818, biological reference material, Centre de toxicology/INSPQ) (Santoro et al. 2013; Centre der toxicology, 2023) were performed periodically following the same digestion procedure to determine the method recovery rate.

The 238U content of the digestion solutions was measured by inductively coupled plasma-mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) as the sum of 238U and 235U according to DIN EN ISO 17294-2:2017-01. Based on the ICP-MS data, the uranium concentration in the hair was calculated related to the weight of the washed and air-dried hair sample.

Soil sample preparation and analysis

Each sample was thoroughly mixed by shaking until homogenized and then placed in a sample jar. Prior to analysis, rocks, pebbles, and twigs were removed from the soil. The soil samples were transferred into labelled glass petri dishes and baked in an oven at 40 °C for 48 h to reduce moisture content. The dried soil sample was grounded using a mortar and pestle, or a portable electric grinder, to enable it to pass through various sizes of metal sieves to obtain a finer soil sample. The sieving process used 2 mm mesh, 250 μm mesh, and 125 μm mesh. Between samples, a set of sieves were wiped with a wet cloth and dried with a cloth to avoid soil contamination, and also washed and dried in the oven. For the analysis of soils approximately 0.1 g of dried, homogenized sample was weighed directly into Teflon vessels for microwave digestion. The microwave digestion was conducted at a power level of 1600 W (100%) with two stages. In stage 1, the temperature was ramped for 25 min to 160 °C and held for 5 min under a pressure of 800 psi. In stage 2, the temperature was ramped for 15 min to 210 °C and held for 10 min. To the sample, 6 ml of ultrapure nitric acid (HNO₃) and 2 ml of supra-pure hydrochloric acid (HCl) were added. After digestion, the vessels were cooled, and the samples were diluted to a final volume of 50 ml. The analysis of the samples, was conducted using an Agilent 7900 ICPMS under the following conditions: RF power at 1600 W, carrier gas (Ar) flow at 0.83 L/min, sample depth at 10 mm, HMI dilution gas (Ar) at 0.15 L/min, helium flow at 5 mL/min, hydrogen flow at 6 mL/min, and nebulizer flow at 0.2 mL/min (Micro mist).

Data analysis

All data were cleaned to correct errors and remove duplicates using Microsoft Excel before being exported to Stata IC version 18 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA) for subsequent analysis. Descriptive statistics, scatterplots, and histograms were employed to characterize the distributions of uranium concentrations. We assessed differences in median uranium concentrations for the exposed versus unexposed group using Wilcoxon Rank-Sum test or Kruskal–Wallis test. Sex (boy/girl), body mass index [BMI] (underweight/normal/overweight/obese), duration of stay at their current residence (< 10 years/ ≥ 10 years), education (None (not yet attending school)/ creche/preschool/primary school/ high school), population group (coloured/African Black), child spend most of their time in another house/suburb (yes/no), household income solely from social grant (yes/no), number of smokers in the household (0/ 1–2/3–6), dust inside the dwelling a major problem (yes/no), presence of pets (yes/no), grow own food in the garden (yes/no) and overall state of the child’s health reported by parent or guardian (excellent/very good/good/fair/poor) were also analysed using Wilcoxon Rank-Sum test or Kruskal–Wallis test, but stratified into exposed and unexposed group. BMI was determined by dividing body weight (in kilograms) by the square of height (in meters), using weight and height measurements taken on the day of assessment/ sampling. The results were then classified according to sex- and age-specific reference cut-off values, as defined by the World Health Organization (WHO) growth curves (WHO, 2025). Spearman’s rank correlation was calculated to examine the relationship between continuous variables, including uranium concentrations in hair, uranium concentrations in soil, and age. The Shapiro–Wilk test was conducted to assess the normality of the data. Due to skewed distributions of uranium concentrations, data were log-transformed (natural log) to normalize the distribution prior to fitting linear regression models. Standard linear regression was then applied to evaluate the association between uranium concentrations and exposure status, while adjusting for potential covariates. Because the data were log-transformed, geometric means were calculated.

Results

Hair analysis

The dry substance of the air-dried samples was analyzed (Table 1). Under consideration of the indicated total uncertainties, the mean values of dry substance determined by both methods agree very well. For verification of the applied analytical method as well as for determination of the recovery rate, two certified human hair standards were analyzed using the same procedure for weighing, determination of the dry substance, digestion and uranium analysis as used for the hair samples. The standards were analyzed several times over the entire analysis period in parallel with the samples. The results of these analyses are shown in Table 1 in comparison to the certified values. The uranium contents determined for the standards closely match the values specified in the respective certificates, thus, very good recovery rates were achieved. These results therefore demonstrate the suitability of the analytical method for determining the uranium content in the hair samples. The relative total uncertainty of the method, which includes uncertainty components for sample weighing, digestion, filling up the final sample volume, several dilution steps, calibration and ICP-MS measurements amount to 12%. The inhomogeneity of the hair samples are not taken into account in this value.

Participant and household characteristics

The study comprised 406 participants: 208 individuals in the exposed group and 198 in the unexposed group. Most participants were female (54.2%) and Black African (81.0%). Almost one-third had resided in the area for more than 10 years. The majority of participants (77.3%) reported spending most of their time within their respective suburbs. Approximately one-quarter of the participants were not yet attending school (24.9%), 4.2% were enrolled in crèche or preschool, 42.1% were attending primary school, and 28.1% were in high school. Additionally, 56.2% of participants relied on social grants, and 55.9% lived in households with no smokers. A majority (75.6%) reported that dust was a significant issue in their homes, 26.1% had pets, and 21.9% grew food in their gardens. Most children were reported to be in excellent (39.2%) (unexposed 9.3% and exposed 19.1%) or good (37.4%) (unexposed 5.4% and exposed 15.4%) health (Table 3).

Uranium in hair concentrations by exposure status, participant characteristics, and uranium in soil

For the total sample of 406 participants, the median uranium concentration was 8.12 µg/kg for the unexposed group (N = 198) and 17.07 µg/kg for the exposed group (n = 208), with a statistically significant difference (p < 0.001). The median uranium concentration was higher in females than in males across all groups. In the total sample, females had a median of 18.83 µg/kg and males 7.92 µg/kg (p < 0.001). Among the exposed group, females had a median of 23.50 µg/kg and males 11.77 µg/kg (p < 0.001). In the unexposed group, females had a median of 12.94 µg/kg and males 6.07 µg/kg (p < 0.001). Younger children had the highest median uranium concentration across all groups. In the total sample, the median was 18.67 µg/kg (p < 0.001). Among the exposed group, the median was 21.31 µg/kg, and in the unexposed group, 14.64 µg/kg. In the exposed group, children attending preschool also had elevated concentrations 25.37 µg/kg. No significant differences in uranium concentrations were observed for the following variables: Population group, duration of stay in the area, water source, whether the child spent most of their time in another house or suburb, household income solely from social grants, number of smokers in the household, growing food in the garden, and overall state of the child’s health (Table 3). Spearman’s correlation analyses showed a weak but statistically significant negative correlation between uranium concentration in hair and age for the total sample (ρ = − 0.19, p = 0.0001), the unexposed group (ρ = − 0.27, p = 0.0002), and a non-significant negative correlation for the exposed group (ρ = − 0.11, p = 0.1168). A weak, non-significant positive correlation between uranium in hair and uranium in soil was observed in the total sample (ρ = 0.08, p = 0.0975) and unexposed group (ρ = 0.07, p = 0.337), while no correlation was found in the exposed group (ρ = 0.01, p = 0.8658).

Figure 2 presents the results from the multiple linear regression analysis. Due to missing data in the covariates, thirty-seven participants were excluded from the analysis: 5 from the unexposed group (median: 9.7 μg/kg) and 32 from the exposed group (median: 18.0 μg/kg). Children in the exposed group had geometric mean uranium concentrations 1.8 times higher compared to those in the unexposed group (P < 0.001). For each additional month of age, the geometric mean uranium concentration was 0.99 times lower (P < 0.001). Boys had geometric mean uranium concentrations that were about 2 times significantly lower than girls. No statistically significant associations were observed for the following variables: BMI, child level of education, household receiving a social grant, number of household smokers, duration of stay in the area (Fig. 2).

Discussion

This study assessed uranium concentrations in hair samples from children aged 0 to under 18 years living near mine tailings facilities in Johannesburg, compared to reference sites in Durban, South Africa. Multivariate regression models revealed that living near mine tailings facilities was associated with geometric mean uranium concentrations 1.8 times higher than those at the reference site. For each additional month of age, the geometric mean uranium concentration was 0.99 times lower (P < 0.001). These findings are comparable with other studies conducted among communities situated near mining sites and mine tailings facilities (Haakonde et al., 2021; Winde et al., 2019; Zupunski et al., 2023).

Uranium exposure, primarily from environmental sources such as mining, has been associated with a range of serious health issues. The cumulative effects of long-term exposure may have significant public health implications, particularly in areas where mining activities release uranium and its byproducts into the environment (Patocka, 2014). Currently, there are no established reference or recommended standards for uranium concentrations in human hair. In this study, the median uranium concentration in the scalp hair of children was 12.25 μg/kg, with a range from 0.47 to 1131.18 μg/kg. We found significant differences in uranium concentrations between children living near mine tailings facilities and those at reference sites, with the former showing a median uranium concentration of 17.07 μg/kg compared to 8.12 μg/kg in the unexposed group (p < 0.001). The study’s findings are consistent with other research indicating that uranium exposure, particularly near mine tailings facilities, results in elevated uranium concentrations in biological samples, such as hair (Winde & Sandham, 2004; Zlobina et al., 2024; Zupunski et al., 2023). For example, our results fall within the range reported in studies conducted among children (aged 7–15 years), with uranium concentrations ranging from 22 to 1809 μg/kg (median = 92 μg/kg) (Zupunski et al., 2023), as well as those conducted among adults, with concentrations ranging from 31 to 2524 μg/kg (median = 122) (Winde et al., 2019) in Johannesburg, and studies conducted near mining operations, such as in Russia (range: 1–179 μg/kg) (Zlobina et al., 2024). In comparison to established reference values, the uranium concentrations detected in this study’s soil samples were higher than those reported for residential soils by Health Canada (23 µg/g) and the National Environmental Protection Council (NEPC) guideline value of 20 mg/kg.

When comparing uranium concentrations by studied characteristics, uranium concentrations were higher in underweight individuals: 36.65 µg/kg in the total sample, 64.81 µg/kg in the exposed group, and 11.02 µg/kg in the unexposed group. These findings are consistent with a study conducted in Johannesburg, which reported higher uranium concentrations in underweight children (142.2 µg/kg) (Zupunski et al., 2023). Younger children, especially those not yet in school, had the highest uranium concentrations in the total sample (18.67 µg/kg) and in the unexposed group (14.64 µg/kg). In the exposed group, the highest levels were observed in children not yet attending school (21.31 µg/kg) and preschool children (25.37 µg/kg). These findings are in line with a study conducted in Johannesburg, which reported the highest concentrations in younger children (Zupunski et al., 2023). This highlight potential differences in exposure pathways, indicating the need for further assessments to better understand the sources and determinants of uranium exposure in different age groups. We also observed a statistically significant negative correlation between uranium concentration in hair and age (Spearman’s ρ = − 0.1923, p = 0.0001), as well as a 0.99-fold reduction in geometric mean uranium concentration (p < 0.001) for each additional month of age in our multiple linear regression analysis. The observed trend in this study is in line with the WHO report, which suggests that Tolerable Daily Intake (TDI) values for uranium are body weight-adjusted, implying that at similar intake levels, children due to their lower body mass are at higher risk of adverse effects compared to adults, and may show elevated biomarker levels such as uranium in hair (WHO, 2011). Studies also show increased vulnerability of younger children to environmental pollutants given their higher hand-to-mouth behavior (Wilson et al., 2021), and elevated respiration rates (Fleming et al., 2011). These findings highlight the need for age-specific risk assessments and protective measures to minimize uranium exposure among children, particularly in vulnerable communities.

Further, in our multivariate regression models, children living near mine tailings facilities had geometric mean uranium concentrations 1.8 times higher than those in the unexposed group (P < 0.001). Girls had uranium concentrations approximately two times significantly higher than boys (18.83 µg/kg vs. 7.92 µg/kg). These findings contrast with similar studies, which showed boys having higher levels compared to girls (Torrente et al., 2013; Zupunski et al., 2023). Research on hair growth parameters shows gender differences, which could affect the accumulation of uranium. For example, in a study conducted by Loussouarn et al (2016), total hair density varied by gender and scalp area, with men having significantly lower hair density than women in the vertex region by about 19 hairs/cm2 (p < 0.001). Similarly, hair growth rates differed by gender and scalp site, with men exhibiting slower growth rates at the nape compared to women (p < 0.001) (Loussouarn et al., 2016). This highlights the need for further investigation into how biological sex influences the uptake and storage of toxic substances such as uranium. Research has also shown an association between elements in biological and environmental samples among children, partly due to behaviors such as hand-to-mouth activity, which can increase the ingestion of contaminated soil or dust. In this study, a weak, non-significant positive correlation between uranium concentrations in hair and soil was observed in the total sample (ρ = 0.08, p = 0.0975). These findings highlight the need for further investigation into behavioral and environmental contributors to uranium exposure. While several factors, including household income, number of smokers in the household, water source, and presence of pets, were considered, no significant associations were found between these variables and uranium concentrations in the hair.

This study was not without limitations. As a cross-sectional design, it captures exposure data at a single point in time. Without longitudinal data or outcome measurements, it is not possible to determine whether uranium exposure leads to specific health outcomes or whether other factors may be influencing the exposure levels. Since exposure was measured at a single time point, it remains unclear whether the uranium concentration in hair reflects long-term cumulative exposure or more recent exposure. For example, hair growth occurs in distinct phases: the anagen phase (active growth, lasting approximately 2–7 years), the catagen phase (a transitional phase lasting 2–4 weeks), and the telogen phase (a resting phase that lasts around 3 months) (Kesika et al., 2023). The accumulation of uranium in hair may thus be influenced by the hair growth rate, which varies among populations and can affect the length and timing of exposure captured in hair segments. For example, in a study conducted by Loussouarn et al (2005), the rates of growth varied from 129 to 436 µm/day in the African group, from 165 to 506 µm/day in the Caucasian group, and from 244 to 611 µm/day in the Asian group. Despite this high interindividual variability, African hair grew more slowly than Caucasian hair, which in turn grew more slowly than Asian hair (mean ± SD: 280 ± 50, 367 ± 56, and 411 ± 43 µm/day, respectively) (Loussouarn, et al., 2005). Each hair strand sampled at a minimum length of 1 cm represents the individual’s cumulative uranium intake over a period of 2–4 weeks. However, it is not possible to simply subtract this time from the sampling date to pinpoint the exact period of uranium uptake, because hair actually forms beneath the scalp during the anagen phase. It is likely that the actual U-uptake period reflected in a 1-cm-long strand of hair cut directly above the scalp did not start 1 month before the sampling date but as much as 2–7 years earlier (depending on age, genetics, ethnicity etc.). Since we generally sample more than a single hair but tens or even hundreds of them, which all are in a slightly different state of the hair cycle before eventually falling out, the actual U-uptake period reflected in a 1-cm-long bundle of hair is generally larger than just a month. This is further influenced by the density, diameter, color, and curliness. For example, in a study conducted among young adults from 24 different ethnic groups across five continents, average hair density ranged from 153 hairs/cm2 in South African participants to 233 hairs/cm2 in French participants (Loussouarn et al., 2016). Some of the samples were collected in paved areas. We acknowledge that the frequency of sweeping of outdoor areas may influence uranium concentrations in surface soil, particularly in paved or regularly maintained spaces. However, data on household sweeping practices were not collected in this study, which may have introduced variability in surface contamination levels. Future studies should consider incorporating structured observations or questionnaires to better contextualize uranium concentrations in residential soil.

Additionally, research indicates that factors such as diet (Patterson et al., 2024), climatic conditions etc. (Zhang et al., 2024) may influence uranium exposure levels. In a study conducted in the UK, which studied hair growth patterns every month for 18 months in 14 healthy white men, aged 18–39, who worked indoors, the hair follicles on the scalp grew the most in spring with over 90% of hair follicles actively growing (Randall & Ebling, 1991). From there, growth was reported to slow down, reaching its lowest point in Autumn. In the present study, the potential influence of seasonal factors on exposure levels (Adam et al., 2025) cannot be entirely ruled out, and future studies may benefit from a more systematic evaluation of seasonal variation.

Compromised health is known to slow hair growth, potentially leading to higher uranium accumulation in hair. However, in this study, no consistent association was observed between general health status and uranium levels. Children rated as having poorer health did not show markedly higher concentrations compared to those reported in excellent or very good health. Future research should explore more precise measures of health status, including clinical assessments and biomarkers of physiological illness, to better understand their relationship with uranium accumulation in hair.

The Agency for Toxic Substances and Disease Registry (ATSDR) has identified that radon exposure, one of the decay products of uranium can result in various respiratory effects in both humans and animals, including chronic lung disease, pulmonary fibrosis, pneumonia, cancer, and decreased lung function (Maheswari & Rajeswari, 2018). Radon exposure has also been associated with issues such as impaired eyesight, skin allergies, various cancers, fertility problems, and other non-communicable diseases (Maheswari & Rajeswari, 2018). A study conducted in the same area found that residential dwellings near gold mine tailings had significantly higher average indoor radon concentrations (103.30 Bq/m3) compared to those in the control group (65.19 Bq/m3) (Mphaga et al., 2024). The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends an action level of 100 Bq/m3 for indoor radon concentrations, while Germany adopts a higher reference level of 300 Bq/m3 (WHO, 2009). Given these findings, future research should assess the cumulative health effects of multiple uranium decay products especially in vulnerable populations such as children.

The choice of sample testing for uranium is crucial, with hair samples providing more accurate and extended results compared to blood and urine (Haakonde et al., 2021). A strength of this study is the assessment of uranium in hair, which reflects longer-term exposure rather than short-term exposure-like blood or urine. An additional strength is the focus on a vulnerable population residing in close proximity to known sources of environmental contamination, which enhances the study’s relevance for informing public health interventions.

The findings of this study highlight the urgent need for stronger policies and regulations to mitigate the health risks associated with uranium exposure, particularly in mining regions. Particularly because there is no safe level of toxic substances among children. While international frameworks like the WHO’s radon guidelines (WHO, 2024) emphasize the need for stringent control of uranium exposure, many African countries with significant mining activities, such as Zambia and South Africa, still lack comprehensive environmental protection measures. The study’s results, particularly the significant differences in uranium levels between exposed and unexposed groups, highlight the importance of targeting high-risk populations, including children, and taking proactive measures to reduce environmental contamination (Matshusa & Makgae, 2017). In addition, there is a need for increased public health initiatives to raise awareness about the risks of uranium exposure. This should be accompanied by a more robust health surveillance system to track long-term health outcomes related to uranium exposure. Policymakers must also consider socioeconomic factors and ensure that vulnerable communities, particularly those near mining operations, have access to healthcare resources and preventive measures.

Conclusion

Our findings align with other studies suggesting that uranium exposure in various environmental contexts, such as mining areas, can lead to detectable levels of uranium in biological samples such as hair. These results suggest that additional measures should be taken to monitor and mitigate uranium exposure, particularly in areas known for higher environmental contamination. The study confirms that exposure status, sex, and age are significant determinants of uranium concentrations in hair. The higher concentrations in the exposed group, particularly among females and younger children, highlight the need for targeted interventions to reduce uranium exposure, especially in vulnerable populations. In addition, future research should focus on exploring the biological mechanisms underlying the sex-based differences observed in uranium concentrations and further investigating the environmental pathways of uranium exposure, particularly in residential areas. Given the potential for an increase in uranium mining activities in South Africa, it becomes crucial to conduct comprehensive environmental studies in communities affected by mining operations.
