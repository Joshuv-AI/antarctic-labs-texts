Cognitive and affective links between childhood adversity and psychological adjustment in emerging adults with chronic medical conditions
Authors: Alexandra M DeLone, Nathan L Basile, John M Chaney, Larry L Mullins, Christina M Sharkey
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveChildhood adversity has been linked to poor psychological adjustment and decreased emotion regulation (ER) abilities. The extended process model of ER outlines the pivotal role of cognitive appraisals in the generation and expression of emotion as well as the pursuit of ER. The aim of the current study is to examine whether illness-related cognitive appraisals (i.e., illness uncertainty and illness intrusiveness) and emotion dysregulation serially mediate the relationship between childhood adversity and psychological adjustment for emerging adults with chronic medical conditions (CMCs).MethodsParticipants included 557 undergraduate college students (Mage= 19.53 years, SD = 1.34) enrolled in a Midwestern public university with self-reported CMCs. Participants completed measures of adverse childhood experiences (ACEs), illness uncertainty, illness intrusiveness, emotion dysregulation, anxious symptoms, and depressive symptoms. A path analysis was conducted examining ACEs → cognitive appraisals (i.e., illness uncertainty, illness intrusiveness) → emotion dysregulation → depressive and anxious symptoms.ResultsThe overall model was significant and predicted 63.5% of the variability in anxious symptoms and 60.2% of the variability in depressive symptoms. All anticipated direct and indirect paths were significant.ConclusionsThe current study indicates that greater childhood adversity is indeed associated with negative appraisals of one’s CMC, such as greater perceived uncertainty and intrusiveness. Negative illness-related cognitive appraisals thus may limit one’s ability to effectively employ adaptive strategies to regulate emotions, which could contribute to greater depressive and anxious symptoms.

Emerging adulthood is a distinct period of development typically characterized from 18 through 25 years of age during which individuals explore opportunities and experience significant change as they gradually transition to assuming new family, occupational, and community responsibilities (Arnett, 2000). During this developmental period, emerging adults often move away from their families of origin, experience changes to their daily routines and sources of social support, as well as experience increased employment and/or academic expectations (Brougham et al., 2009). The developmental tasks of this period are even more complicated for those with chronic medical conditions (CMCs).

CMCs include both nonlife-limiting, and progressive, potentially life-limiting diseases or conditions lasting longer than 3 months or requiring a hospitalization over 1 month, and they often involve periods of acute exacerbation requiring medical attention resulting in physical and mental impairment (Mattsson, 1972; Pless & Pinkerton, 1975). While the onset of CMCs can occur at any age, self-management responsibilities for pediatric diseases increase during emerging adulthood (Ferro et al., 2015; Reed-Knight et al., 2014). This may include taking on primary responsibility for tasks such as making medical appointments, adhering to treatment regimens, and managing symptoms and side effects. As such, previous work from our lab has demonstrated that having a CMC increases the risk for psychological adjustment difficulties (e.g., anxious, depressive, and post-traumatic stress symptoms) in emerging adults (Espeleta et al., 2019; Sharkey et al., 2020).

Adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) are a defined constellation of early traumatic events, including abuse, neglect, and household challenges, which have also been linked with poor psychological adjustment in emerging adulthood (Boullier & Blair, 2018; Felitti et al., 1998). In fact, ACEs have been consistently identified as a significant risk factor for multiple negative physical and mental health outcomes (Boullier & Blair, 2018; Kalmakis & Chandler, 2015). Our previous work found that both ACEs and CMCs independently predict anxious and depressive symptoms in emerging adults (Espeleta et al., 2019; Sharkey et al., 2020). Additionally, emerging adults with CMCs are more likely to have experienced at least one ACE, and overall, report more cumulative ACEs than their healthy peers (Scott et al., 2021; Sharkey et al., 2020; Thompson et al., 2015). Furthermore, emerging adults with CMCs and a history of ACEs have demonstrated significantly worse psychological adjustment compared to those with either ACEs or CMCs (Sharkey et al., 2020). Thus, ACEs and CMCs, individually and in combination, appear to negatively impact adjustment for emerging adults.

Substantial evidence suggests childhood adversity also negatively impacts emotion regulation (ER), the cognitive process in which people utilize control strategies to determine their experience, timing, and expression of emotion (McRae & Gross, 2020; Pruessner et al., 2020). Early positive developmental influences, including predictable caregiving, attachment, and parenting style, have been highlighted as important mechanisms through which children develop ER skills (Blandon et al., 2008; Piotrowski et al., 2013; Wu & Feng, 2020). ER matures during late adolescence, with increased use of more adaptive strategies across development. However, childhood adversity results in developmental changes to ER-related neural circuitry (Jenness et al, 2021; Theurel & Gentaz, 2018; Zimmer-Gembeck & Skinner, 2011). These changes to neural circuitry often increase emotional salience and decrease cognitive control, which may lead to impaired ER, and subsequently poorer psychological adjustment (Jenness et al., 2021).

The cognitive process of ER has been outlined by the extended process model (Gross, 2015) and is described as a dynamic, iterative, and cyclical multistage process (Koval et al., 2023) that is greatly influenced by an individual’s experiences and environment. According to this model, emotions are generated and expressed across 4 stages: (1) reaction triggered by an internal or external situation; (2) attention is directed to the situation; (3) appraisal of the reaction is experienced as an emotional response; (4) behavioral response based on the emotional experience (Gross, 2015; Weissman et al., 2019). To regulate emotions, an individual must (1) appraise their current emotions and goals to identify a discrepancy and need to regulate; (2) choose appropriate ER strategies; and (3) successfully implement and maintain effective strategies (Gross, 2015; Koval et al., 2023). Difficulties regulating emotions interfere with meeting situational goals due to the presence of maladaptive strategies and has been termed emotion dysregulation (Weissman et al., 2019). Emotion dysregulation has been shown to mediate the relationship between childhood adversity and psychological adjustment throughout childhood, adolescence, and emerging adulthood (Weissman et al., 2019). Thus, emotion dysregulation appears to be an important transdiagnostic mechanism connecting childhood adversity to psychological adjustment (Weissman et al., 2019).

As outlined above, the extended process model of ER specifically outlines processes for how emotions are generated, expressed, and regulated with recognition of the central role of cognitive appraisals to the generation and expression of emotion as well as in the pursuit of ER (Gross, 2015; McRae & Gross, 2020). Early social and environmental factors during childhood influence cognitive appraisals more strongly than genetic or stable personality dimensions (McRae et al., 2017). It is, therefore, plausible that an individual’s early life circumstances, including childhood adversity, may impact how individuals appraise a situation, and subsequent success regulating their emotions.

Illness-related cognitive appraisals have been identified as key factors of psychological adjustment for emerging adults with CMCs by our research group and others (Bakula et al., 2019; Dattilo et al., 2021; Ramsey et al., 2013). The prominent role of cognitive appraisals in the generation and experience of emotions as well as the successful use of ER strategies suggest that it is important to consider the potential mediating effects of specific illness-related cognitive appraisals for emerging adults with CMCs. Two specific appraisals to consider in the context of CMCs are illness uncertainty and illness intrusiveness (Devins et al., 1990; Mishel, 1981). Illness uncertainty refers to the appraisal of a medical condition in which the outcomes are perceived as unclear to an individual due to the ambiguity, complexity, and unpredictability of illness-related events (Mishel, 1981; Sharkey et al., 2019). Illness intrusiveness refers to the perceived disruption to interests, daily activities, and lifestyle of an individual that results from both the direct effects of the medical condition (e.g., symptoms) and its treatment (e.g., medications, diet management, procedures, devices; Devins et al., 1990; Mullins et al., 2000). Among populations of emerging adults with various CMCs (e.g., asthma, allergies, inflammatory bowel disease, juvenile rheumatic diseases, type 1 diabetes), greater perceived illness uncertainty and illness intrusiveness have been found to negatively impact psychological adjustment by our lab and others (Mullins et al., 2017; Ramsey et al., 2013; Roberts et al., 2019). Previous work from our lab reported that these cognitive appraisals have additionally been found to mediate the relationship between ACEs and health anxiety for emerging adults (Traino et al., 2023). Consistent with the extended process model of ER, illness-related cognitive appraisals may mediate the serial relationship between childhood adversity, emotion dysregulation, and subsequent psychological adjustment among emerging adults with CMCs.

Although emotion dysregulation is an established mediator between childhood adversity and psychological adjustment for emerging adults with and without CMCs (Espeleta et al., 2019; Jenness et al., 2021; Sharkey et al., 2020), the literature to date has not examined the role of illness-related cognitive appraisals in this relationship. Thus, the aim of the current study is to evaluate whether illness-related cognitive appraisals (i.e., illness uncertainty and illness intrusiveness) and emotion dysregulation serially mediate the relationship between childhood adversity and psychological adjustment for emerging adults with CMCs. We hypothesize that there will be a significant direct effect of childhood adversity on psychological adjustment for emerging adults with CMCs. Additionally, we hypothesize that both illness uncertainty and illness intrusiveness will significantly mediate the relationship between childhood adversity and emotion dysregulation, which will serially mediate the relationship between childhood adversity and anxious and depressive symptoms in emerging adults with CMCs.

Methods

Participants and procedures

This study included 557 undergraduate students at a Midwestern Public University who self-identified as having CMCs. Participants were first asked to identify if they have a chronic illness by checking “yes” or “no.” Those who answered “yes” were then asked to provide their specific diagnoses across 20 broad illness types (e.g., sickle cell disease, chronic migraine, inflammatory bowel disease) and were prompted to provide “other” diagnoses not previously captured. For example, participants clicked the box for allergies and used free text responses to list specific food, environmental, and medication allergies. Participants were asked to endorse and provide diagnoses for all illness types for which they had previously been diagnosed with additional space to delineate other medical and mental health diagnoses. Any diagnoses listed as “other” were individually reviewed to assess for necessary ongoing disease management and medical care. The majority of participants were female (n = 445; 79.9%) and identified as White non-Hispanic (n = 435; 78.1%). The mean age of participants was 19.53 years (SD = 1.34). Median combined family household income was $80,000–$89,999. The most prevalent self-reported CMCs were asthma (n = 257; 46.1%) and allergies (n = 167; 30.0%). Across all participants, disease severity for the last 12 months averaged 3.46 (SD = 1.57), which indicated perceptions of moderate to severe disease. One hundred and forty-four participants (25.8%) rated their disease as severe to very severe. Four hundred and 25 (76.3%) participants reported currently taking medication to manage their CMC. The study protocol was approved by the university’s Institutional Review Board. Recruitment and data collection occurred between August 2017 and January 2023 through an online research system consisting of undergraduate students enrolled in psychology courses. Within this system, participants individually and voluntarily select which studies to participate in through an online database listing the institution’s open research protocols. Data utilized for this study were collected via online administration of self-report questionnaires as part of a larger project assessing the influence of chronic illnesses on college student adjustment. This project is ongoing and has continuously collected data since 2017. Thus, the current sample includes portions of participant data previously reported elsewhere (e.g., Bakula et al., 2019; Dattilo et al., 2021; Espeleta et al., 2019; Sharkey et al., 2018, 2019, 2020; Traino et al., 2023). Participation was voluntary, and those who participated were compensated with course credit. Participants completed consent forms and the larger battery of self-report measures online. Inclusion criteria included: (1) self-report of a CMC, (2) active enrollment status at the university, (3) between 18 and 23 years of age, (4) fluent in English. Data were included in analyses if the participant spent at least 15 min completing the battery of questionnaires. This time limit was determined through a review of patterns of missing data and represents 25% of the expected time (∼60 min) to complete the entire battery. It was determined that 15 min was a reasonable cut-off for excluding invalid data without introducing bias. Data are available on request to the corresponding author.

Measures

Demographic characteristics

Information on each participant’s sex, age, race, family income, and self-reported CMC diagnoses was collected via a demographic questionnaire.

Adverse childhood experiences: short form

The ACEs (Felitti et al., 1998) is a self-report questionnaire used to assess childhood adversity experiences. The current study used an eight-item version, including prompts related to childhood sexual, physical, and emotional abuse, emotional neglect, witnessing domestic violence, caregiver incarceration, caregiver mental illness, and caregiver substance abuse (Felitti et al., 1998). Participants indicated the presence of each of the adverse experiences in their childhood. A sum total of all adverse experiences endorsed is calculated for each participant, such that higher scores represent greater childhood adversity. Previous studies indicate that the ACEs measure has acceptable to good reliability (Espeleta et al., 2018, 2020; Solberg et al., 2024), and in the current study, reliability was acceptable (α = .75).

Difficulties in emotion regulation scale

The DERS (Gratz & Roemer, 2004) measures emotion dysregulation, defined as difficulties identifying and regulating one’s own emotions (Espeleta et al., 2019; Gratz & Roemer, 2004). This 36-item self-report measure utilizes a 5-point Likert scale from “almost never” to “almost always.” Example items include, “When I am upset, I have difficulty getting work done” and “When I’m upset, I take time to figure out what I’m really feeling.” All items are summed for a total score, with higher scores indicating greater emotion dysregulation. Prior studies have shown that the DERS has excellent reliability with emerging adults (Espeleta et al., 2019; Sörman et al., 2022), and in the current study, reliability was excellent (α = .96).

Mishel uncertainty in illness scale: community form

The MUIS-C (Mishel, 1981) measures illness-related uncertainty, or the perceptions of ambiguity, unpredictability, or lack of clarity in the efficacy or outcome of an illness and related treatment (Mishel, 1981; Sharkey et al., 2019). This 23-item self-report measure utilizes a 5-point Likert scale with responses from “Strongly agree” to “Strongly disagree.” Example items include, “My physical distress is predictable. I know when it is going to get better or worse.” and “The doctors say things to me that could have many meanings.” Total scores are calculated by summing the item scores, with higher scores representing greater illness uncertainty. Prior research reports good to excellent reliability for the MUIS-C with emerging adults across CMCs (Kurta-Nowicka & Jaracz, 2019; Mishel, 1981; Mullins et al., 2017; Sharkey et al., 2018), and reliability was excellent (α = .90) in the present study.

Illness intrusiveness rating scale

The IIRS (Devins, 2010) measures the perceived degree of disruptions to interests, valued daily life activities, and lifestyle that result from illness-related symptoms and effects of treatments (Devins et al., 1990; Mullins et al., 2000). This 13-item self-report questionnaire utilizes a 7-point Likert scale with responses ranging from “Not very much” to “Very much.” Total scores are derived by summing all items, with higher scores reflecting a higher degree of intrusiveness. Example items include “How much does your illness and/or its treatment interfere with your work?” and “How much does your illness and/or its treatment interfere with your financial situation?” The IIRS has demonstrated good to excellent reliability in previous studies in emerging adults (Bakula et al., 2019; Devins, 2010). In the present study, reliability was excellent (α = .91).

Center for epidemiological studies: depression scale

The CES-D (Radloff, 1977) measures depressive symptoms over the past week. This 20-item self-report survey utilizes a 4-point Likert scale ranging from “Rarely or none of the time (less than 1 day)” to “Most or all of the time (5–7 days).” The total score is derived by summing all items, such that higher scores represent greater depressive symptoms. Example items include “I felt that I could not shake off the blues even with help from my family and friends.” and, “I enjoyed life.” In previous studies with emerging adults, the CES-D demonstrated good to excellent internal consistency (Bakula et al., 2019; Radloff, 1977; Solberg et al., 2024). In the present study, reliability was excellent (α = .94).

Zung self-rating anxiety scale

The SAS (Zung, 1971) measures current anxious symptoms, including nervousness, physiological sensations, and worry. This 20-item self-report measure utilizes a 4-point Likert scale with responses ranging from “a little of the time” to “most of the time.” Example items include, “I feel afraid for no reason at all.” and “I feel calm and can sit still easily.” Total scores are derived by summing all items, such that higher scores indicate greater anxious symptoms. The SAS has been found to be a valid tool to screen for anxious symptoms (Dunstan et al., 2017) and demonstrated good reliability in previous studies with emerging adults (Bakula et al., 2019; Olatunji et al., 2006). Internal consistency was good (α = .87) in the present study.

Data analytic plan

Bivariate correlations were obtained between all primary variables in the model of interest as well as with demographic variables to assess for covariates. Race was dichotomized into White and Black, Indigenous, and people of color (BIPOC), due to the small percentage of our sample who identified as BIPOC. Participants were also dichotomized by the timing of data collection. The first university-wide announcement related to the COVID-19 pandemic, which occurred on March 14, 2020, was used to identify those who participated prior to the COVID-19 pandemic and those who participated during the COVID-19 pandemic. Therefore, we could statistically control for changes in psychological adjustment unrelated to our model that resulted from the COVID-19 pandemic.

To test the direct and indirect effects of childhood adversity, a path analysis was conducted in Mplus version 8.1. In the model, ACEs were entered as the independent variable, with cognitive appraisals (i.e., illness intrusiveness, illness uncertainty) and emotion dysregulation as mediators, and depressive and anxious symptoms entered as the dependent variables. The model controlled for participant age, sex, BIPOC identity, and participation prior to or during the COVID-19 pandemic. Full information maximum likelihood was employed to handle missing data. The model was tested using 5,000 bias-corrected bootstrapped samples. All possible paths were estimated; thus, the model was saturated with artificially perfect model fit. Of note, due to the cross-sectional nature of the data, these analyses reflect statistical rather than temporal mediation and the estimated associations are atemporal.

Results

Approximately 24% (n = 133) of the sample participated in the study during the COVID-19 pandemic. Emerging adults with CMCs who participated during the COVID-19 pandemic reported significantly higher levels of anxious (M = 45.36, SD = 10.66) and depressive (M = 23.88, SD = 13.17) symptoms compared to those who participated before the COVID-19 pandemic (M = 42.58, SD = 11.58; t(551)= 2.45, p = .007; M = 20.52, SD = 13.84; t(551)= 2.47, p = .007).

Primary analyses

Anxious symptoms

The overall model was significant and predicted 63.5% of the variability in anxious symptoms. All anticipated direct paths were significant, with ACEs (ß = 0.08, SE = 0.03, p = .006), illness uncertainty (ß = 0.20, SE = 0.04, p < .001), illness intrusiveness (ß = 0.26, SE = 0.04, p < .001), and emotion dysregulation (ß = 0.44, SE = 0.03, p < .001) predicting anxious symptoms. Sex significantly predicted anxious symptoms (ß = 0.13, SE = 0.02, p < .001), but no other covariates had significant effects.

All expected indirect effects were significant. The specific indirect path from ACEs through illness uncertainty through emotion dysregulation predicted anxious symptoms (ß = 0.03, SE = 0.01, p < .001, 95% BC [0.02, 0.05]). The indirect path from ACEs through illness intrusiveness through emotion dysregulation also predicted anxious symptoms (ß = 0.03, SE = 0.01, p = .001, 95% BC [0.01, 0.04]). Discussion

Overall, our findings are consistent with prior research that identified early childhood adversity, illness-related cognitive appraisals, and emotion dysregulation as risk factors for psychological difficulties among emerging adults with CMCs. However, our study extends the literature by analyzing specific indirect effects on depressive and anxious symptoms, which aligns with the extended process model of ER. All indirect effects in the hypothesized model were significant, suggesting that early childhood adversity may impact individuals’ appraisal of their CMC, which in turn could be associated with increased emotion dysregulation and higher levels of depressive and anxious symptoms. Research from our lab has previously shown that having a CMC in emerging adulthood is linked with additional early adversity, which can compound the risk for poor psychological health (Sharkey et al., 2020). The current study further indicates that greater childhood adversity may be associated with negative appraisals of one’s CMC, such as greater perceived uncertainty and intrusiveness during emerging adulthood. Furthermore, negative illness-related cognitive appraisals appear to limit one’s ability to effectively employ adaptive strategies to regulate emotions, contributing to greater depressive and anxious symptoms.

Anticipated success has been identified as an important mechanism in the decision to engage in ER (Milyavsky et al., 2019). ER requires significant cognitive resources, and therefore, an individual’s explicit or implicit decision to engage in ER is determined by their own cost benefit analysis (McRae & Gross, 2020). For emerging adults, it is plausible that increased expectations and responsibilities coupled with frequent and significant changes in their daily routine and sources of support may mean that individuals have fewer cognitive resources to devote to regulating their emotions. Furthermore, emerging adults who have experienced greater childhood adversity and who perceive their illness to be more uncontrollable and intrusive might then anticipate less success in modifying their circumstances. These emerging adults may possibly decide, explicitly or implicitly, not to engage in ER strategies, broadly, and may experience resulting decreased psychological adjustment. Although this hypothesis has a strong theoretical and empirical foundation, the cross-sectional nature of our study precludes causal conclusions and necessitates the consideration of alternative pathways. Thus, it may also be feasible that emerging adults who experience greater anxious or depressive symptoms have fewer resources to regulate their emotions, and in turn may interpret their illness as more uncertain or intrusive.

Based on the results of the current study and the underlying theory, cognitively focused treatment approaches targeting both illness-related cognitive appraisals and ER skills appear warranted. Notably, cognitive therapies that aim to reduce negative appraisals of one’s CMC have been shown to be effective (Guan et al., 2021). These interventions often utilize psychoeducation, cognitive restructuring, and other techniques from cognitive-behavior therapy (CBT), to effectively promote adaptive thinking patterns and reduce negative appraisals (Guan et al., 2021). ER interventions based on principles from dialectical behavior therapy (DBT) have also been shown to effectively manage the long-term psychological effects of ACEs (Cameron et al., 2018; Linehan et al., 1993). These interventions aim to enhance emotional understanding, distress tolerance, and adaptive behavioral responses (Cameron et al., 2018). However, these interventions have yet to target emerging adults with a CMC, who may need distinct supports that are illness-specific and developmentally tailored (Sharkey et al., 2019). Thus, integrating evidence-based approaches to managing illness-related cognitive appraisals and emotion dysregulation in the context of a CMC could be one beneficial approach to supporting this population. Both CBT and DBT interventions are also known to effectively reduce depressive and anxious symptoms in pediatric populations (Petsagourakis et al., 2024), suggesting that there is a clinical utility for this population regardless of the temporal relationship between risk factors presently analyzed.

The co-occurrence of childhood adversity and CMCs is well known, with one study indicating a 21% increased risk of having a CMC for each additional ACE (Kerker et al., 2015). Long-term health effects of ACEs have been demonstrated to begin as early as adolescence (Thompson et al., 2015). Therefore, adversity in childhood has the potential to alter the body’s biological responses in adolescence, potentially exacerbating the acuity of a CMC, and indirectly contributing to the number of emerging adults with a serious CMC. The effects of childhood adversity are known to carry forward throughout adulthood, and living with a CMC provides additional complexity to managing one’s own needs and responsibilities. Therefore, in addition to systemic-level interventions aimed at reducing experiences of childhood adversity to help prevent poor psychological health, screening for early childhood adversity may be an important metric of risk for those with CMCs. Childhood adversity screening tools can be used to identify adolescents and emerging adults who could benefit from interventions targeting illness-related cognitive appraisals and emotion dysregulation in hopes of improving psychological functioning.

It is important to note that the current study included data collected prior to and during the COVID-19 pandemic. Those who completed the study during the pandemic had higher depressive and anxiety symptoms than those who completed the study prior to the pandemic. Regarding our results, it could be that the health-related threat of the COVID-19 virus may have been perceived as a complicating factor for these emerging adults when managing their own CMC due to the increased risk of severe complications from the COVID-19 virus for those with certain health conditions. This could have contributed to appraisals of their CMC as less controllable and more intrusive during the pandemic, which may have strengthened the associations between anxious and depressive symptoms. However, within the larger model, study completion during the COVID-19 pandemic did not explain a significant proportion of variance, suggesting that childhood adversity and illness-related cognitive appraisals contributed to symptoms above and beyond individual’s experience during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Although the present study highlights important factors that increase risk for poor psychological health among emerging adults with CMCs, several limitations must be addressed in future research. First, additional theoretical models should be considered, as alternative path analyses were not examined in the present study. As the current study was cross-sectional in nature, there is the potential for bidirectional relationships between illness-related cognitive appraisals, emotion dysregulation, and anxious and depressive symptoms. A longitudinal investigation is needed to determine temporal associations and could provide further evidence to support the presently hypothesized model. The current study was also conducted at a single institution and the sample is predominantly female, white, and middle-upper income. Due to limited diversity and the statistical need to collapse BIPOC into a single group, we may have lost important differences between individuals with distinct racial and ethnic backgrounds. Recent research has outlined the distinct experiences and necessary considerations for people of ethnic and racial minorities within the context of ACEs. Researchers have developed a culturally informed model for understanding the role of racism as a distinct ACE, risk factor for experiencing subsequent ACEs, and predictor of mental health outcomes for Black youth (Bernard et al., 2020). Additional research has examined disparities in experiencing ACEs and the role of various intersecting identities, including race, ethnicity, gender, and sexuality (Giano et al., 2020, 2023; Maguire-Jack et al., 2020). As this area of study continues to expand, it is likely that we will better understand the unique risk and resiliency factors related to ACEs for various racial and ethnic populations (Wang et al., 2019). Replication of this study with a more diverse sample is thereby crucial for understanding the generalizability of these results, as individuals from marginalized backgrounds may face additional systemic challenges and barriers when managing healthcare systems. Future research should examine a more diverse population from multiple institutions as well as those not enrolled in college in an effort to increase the generalizability of study findings. Additionally, this sample included emerging adults with a median family income of $80–$89,000 with approximately 40% of the sample originating from families making over $100,000 annually. Our sample likely has had access to more resources, which has likely served as a protective factor that may not be generalizable to emerging adults from lower income households. It is also important to note that participants provided self-report of all CMC diagnoses, which were not independently verified via medical record review. Age at diagnosis was not assessed, providing a significant limitation on our ability to understand developmental influences of age at CMC onset or the impact of duration of managing a CMC. Greater variability in medical diagnoses, time since diagnosis, and disease severity may also contribute to a clearer picture of the role of early adversity, illness-related cognitive appraisals, and ER on psychological health for this population.

Importantly, qualitative and person-centered research is also needed to identify salient treatment targets and comprehensively assess the extended process model of ER in the context of emerging adults with CMCs. Investigations that provide qualitative opportunities to highlight the perspectives of emerging adults will certainly enhance the current conceptualization and support the development of a tailored intervention that effectively reduces negative illness-related cognitive appraisals and strengthens ER skills, in turn, reducing the effects of child adversity and facilitating better psychological adjustment.

While longitudinal studies are needed to better elucidate the temporal nature of these associations, our findings align with the extended process model of ER and suggest that greater childhood adversity may increase negative illness-related appraisals (i.e., intrusiveness and uncertainty), which in turn may contribute to difficulties regulating emotions and increase anxiety and depressive symptoms for emerging adults managing CMCs.
