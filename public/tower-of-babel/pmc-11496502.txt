Construction of a quantitative model for ski resort water demand and preliminary exploration of drainage irrigation pathways
Authors: Jialin Liu, Xue Bai, Yubo Zhang, Yan Bai, Rong Cai
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn China, natural snowfall is insufficient, and ski resorts often require artificial snowmaking in winter and turf management in summer, which results in high overall water consumption and considered as one of the high water-consuming service industries, with an urgent need for theoretical foundations related to water management. Based on life cycle theory, we examined the characteristics of the water systems of ski resorts in winter and summer. A Monte Carlo simulation was used to construct a stochastic model to quantify the theoretical water consumption for snowmaking at ski resorts and to investigate the mechanisms by which various factors influence this consumption. We summarize the necessity of summer turf management and irrigation requirements at ski resorts, explore the interaction between ski resorts and agricultural irrigation. The results show that the theoretical water demand of ski resorts is basically consistent with the actual water consumption of ski resorts, that the constructed model is feasible, and that snowmaking water is the most important water use of ski resorts. Exploring the collection and reuse of meltwater or rainwater from ski resorts for agricultural irrigation in the spring and summer could provide more possibilities for the sustainable management of regional water resources. The research findings can provide a theoretical basis for scientifically and reasonably setting the norm of water intake for ski resorts and for developing comprehensive water resource management plans for ski resorts and agricultural irrigation.Keywords: Ski resort, Water demand, Snowmaking, Irrigation, Monte CarloSubject terms: Agroecology, Grassland ecology, Sustainability

Introduction

With the rapid development of the economy, skiing, a sport combining fitness and tourism, has gained popularity in China, with the 2022 Beijing Winter Olympics serving as a crucial opportunity for the development of China’s ski industry. According to the “China Ski Industry White Paper (2022–2023),“, the number of ski resorts nationwide has reached 697, distributed across 29 provinces, autonomous regions, and municipalities directly under the central government. Water consumption in ski resorts has always been subject to strict state control. The “Water-Saving Society Construction Plan for the 14th Five-Year Plan Period” issued by the National Development and Reform Commission in 2021 explicitly states the need for strict control over water consumption in high-water-consuming service industries such as artificial ski resorts, implementing rigorous water intake management, and a progressive pricing system beyond the norm of water intake. In October 2022, China issued the “Yellow River Protection Law of the People’s Republic of China,” which stipulates in Article 52 that “The state shall implement a mandatory water use quota management system in the Yellow River Basin.” In this context, water conservation management of ski resorts in China is very important and urgent.

Of course, in the context of climate change, solving the balance between water supply and demand in ski resorts is a global challenge1,2.Current research has focussed on exploring the impacts of the local hydrology and environment and downstream water supply of artificial snowmaking3–6. Development of a ski area can negatively impact the soil7,8, vegetation9, and wildlife inhabiting the area10,11. Ski resorts can also cause a number of listed water quality issues that affect the surrounding and downstream water supplies12–14. Wang et al. measured the water carrying capacity of a ski tourism area with the help of an ecological footprint evaluation model15. A few scientific studies have quantitatively addressed the impacts of artificial snowmaking management. Vorkauf et al. found that he total water consumption of the resort Andermatt-Sedrun-Disentis(Switzerland) will rise by 79% by the end of the century16. Gerbaux et al. compared water demand and water availability for artificial snowmaking at several ski resorts in the northern French Alps17. Additionally, research by Qu Tao explored the coupled replenishment of agricultural water using rainwater collected from ski slopes18. However, no study has yet analysed the year-round water requirement of ski resorts and assessed a reasonable theoretical value of water requirement for ski resorts, and there is a need for technical support on the relationship between water resources in mountain areas and the operation of ski resorts, taking into account the impacts of climate change and related adaptation options, in the midst of an intense debate on the transition of the mountain tourism industry towards a more sustainable development.

The water systems of ski resorts mainly include water for artificial snowmaking and auxiliary functions such as vegetation irrigation, catering, and accommodation, with snowmaking water as the primary water consumption segment19. Differences in water usage arise primarily from the operational models of ski resorts with little relation to geographical regions. Irrigation of slope vegetation and greening are often overlooked, with limited research. This study focused on the water systems of ski resorts, systematically analyzing the water consumption characteristics of different formats in winter and summer, building a theoretical water demand models for snowmaking in winter based on behavioral analytical simulation, summarizing the experience of summer grassland management, and exploring the feasibility and implementation pathways of irrigating farmland using water collected from ski slope runoff. The research results will provide guarantees for scientific, advanced, and feasible norms of water intake for ski resorts, as well as a scientific basis and reference for regional water resource management and rational utilization, enhancing the level of water resource intensity and conservation at ski resorts.

Data collection and analysis

Data collection

A “Survey Form on Water Consumption at Outdoor Artificial Ski Resorts” was compiled, including information, such as the area of ski slopes, annual water consumption, and location of the ski resort. Surveys and consultations were conducted with enterprises and experts through online meetings, questionnaires, etc. To date, 77 sets of water-usage data have been collected from outdoor artificial ski resorts in Beijing, Shanghai, Shandong, Liaoning, Chongqing, Gansu, and other regions.

Norm of water intake for ski resorts

The norm of water intake refers to the amount of water required per unit time, per unit of product, per unit area, or per capita for daily life. As an essential quantitative benchmark for water conservation in China, the norm of water intake is widely used in water-related planning, water resource demonstration, water withdrawal permits, water planning, water conservation evaluation, water conservation carrier construction, supervision, and assessment. The norm of the water intake series of standards is an important part of China’s water conservation standards system and covers various industries such as agriculture, industry, and services20. The principle of its formulation is based on actual water consumption survey data, referring to standards such as GB/T 32,716 − 2016 “Technical guideline of stipulation for norm of water intake” and determining the advanced and common values of norm of water intake based on 20% and 80% pass rates21, respectively22. Similar concepts exist in the UK, where estimates of water consumption have been made to develop guidelines for enhanced water management23.

The norm of water intake standards for ski resorts typically focuses on the water supply range for snowmaking on ski trails and other areas designated for snow production, excluding water usage for dining, accommodation, and landscaping purposes. Monte Carlo simulation

The Monte Carlo Simulation, also known as stochastic sampling, is a research methodology based on probability statistics that primarily employs random sampling to simulate and solve practical problems involving randomness. Its essence lies in establishing a probabilistic model based on the actual problem, performing random simulations by sampling specific mathematical distributions for the input parameters, obtaining multiple sample values, and conducting statistical analyses. The results are interpreted as solutions to the problem, effectively enabling randomized hypothetical experiments on specific events conforming to the given mathematical distribution. This method provides a realistic simulation of random phenomena in real systems24,25, and of course also involves aspects such as water resource management26,27. The advantage of Monte Carlo Simulation over other models is its extreme flexibility and adaptability. It is able to deal with high-dimensional data and uncertainty in complex systems and is suitable for the simulation of various probability distributions without the need for analytic solutions. In addition, The Monte Carlo Simulation can accelerate the process through parallel computation and is particularly effective for complex, dynamic demand models.

Results and discussion

Winter snowmaking theoretical water demand

With global warming, ski resorts have increased their use of artificial snow to ensure skier seasons. Ski slopes must be compacted after snow coverage before skiing can commence28, with snow density typically ranging from 300 to 400 kg/m3 for ordinary ski resorts and approaching 600 kg/m3 for some high-altitude ski resorts29. According to the “Management Regulations for Ski Resorts in China (Revised in 2017)” approved by the Winter Sports Management Center of the General Administration of Sport of China and the Chinese Ski Association, as well as the national standard “Operation conditions and technical requirements for gymnasium and playground - Part 6: Ski area” (GB 19079.6–2013)30, it is explicitly stipulated that the thickness of the snow layer after compaction on ski trails should not be less than 30 cm. Ski resort operators adjust the thickness of the snow layer based on ski comfort considerations, with the normal thickness of snow on ski slopes being approximately 40 cm. In high-altitude skiing areas, the average thickness of the ski trails is required to be 1.2–1.5 m, allowing only 8–10 cm of sunlight penetration to ensure the preservation of snow. Considering the evaporation rate of 40 mm during the operation period of the ski resort, snowmaking is required during the ski season, with an annual cumulative compacted snow thickness of approximately 1.0 m. The efficiency of water utilization during snowmaking was approximately 60%, with high water-saving levels reaching 70%. There were no significant differences in the snowmaking efficiency or snow thickness of snowmaking machines between different regions, whether comparing the north and south or various regions in the north.

Water consumption per unit area of ski trails was estimated based on the water efficiency of artificial snowmaking. Through a literature review and on-site investigation, a probability model for three parameters—snow density after compaction, cumulative compacted snow thickness over the year, and efficiency of water utilization during snowmaking—was constructed. Based on a Monte Carlo simulation with 10,000 random samples, a simulation experiment on snowmaking water behavior was conducted and the theoretical water consumption for snowmaking under different scenarios was calculated. The probability distribution is shown in Fig. 1. The results indicate that the water consumption for artificial snowmaking ranges from 0.02 to 4.01 m3/(m3·a), with 20% and 80% distribution probabilities corresponding to 0.34 m3/(m3·a) and 0.59 m3/(m3·a), respectively. These values can be defined as advanced and general values for the theoretical water consumption per unit area of ski trails in outdoor ski resorts. In addition, we also compared the simulated values with the actual data, and it was found that the actual data were lower than the simulated data, which may be due to the fact that the sources of water intake in the actual process include non-conventional water sources and so on.

Probability distribution of theoretical and actual snowmaking water consumption.

The sensitivity analysis reflects the degree to which each parameter affects theoretical water consumption for snowmaking. A higher sensitivity value indicates a greater impact on water efficiency. A positive sensitivity value indicates a positive correlation with the water efficiency and vice versa. The sensitivity analysis results for artificial snowmaking water consumption based on Monte Carlo simulations are shown in Fig. 2. The sensitivity to snow density after compaction is the highest (63.9%). The cumulative compacted snow thickness over the years accounted for 18.1%. Additionally, the sensitivity of the efficiency of water utilization during snowmaking is -18.0%, indicating a negative correlation between the efficiency of water utilization during snowmaking and snowmaking water consumption. Therefore, rational planning of snow thickness is the focus of water conservation in ski resorts.

Summer grassland management water demand

Ski resorts are generally located in areas with favorable natural conditions, often aiming for year-round development, emphasizing marketing and product diversification, and seeking alternatives to skiing31. They present various business formats, such as winter skiing and summer cooling, as exemplified by the Wanlong Ski Resort in Zhangjiakou. After the ski season (November–April), the snowmelt period (April–May) is dedicated to ecological restoration, preparation for site greening, and operational testing of summer business projects. During the non-ski season (June–September), efforts have been focused on developing green ecological tourism32. Moreover, in summer, the bare land around ski resorts is prone to wind erosion, leading to soil erosion and slope collapse. Planting grass can reduce the risks, protect soil quality, and maintain terrain stability33. Additionally, grasslands can transport moisture into the atmosphere through transpiration, thereby reducing the rate of water loss from the soil. Therefore, summer grassland management can protect the terrain and landscape of ski resorts, maintain the ecological balance, and enhance landscape quality. Through proper grassland management, ski resorts can achieve development goals while providing visitors a better experience.

The construction of ski trails and artificial snowmaking have had significant effects on mountain grassland ecosystems, leading to a decrease in the biomass and species richness of mountain grassland plant communities34. Extensive research has been conducted on common vegetation during the restoration of ski resorts. Kubota et al. found that under natural conditions in Japanese ski resorts, the maximum height of summer vegetation growth was 0.8 m, with an average height of 0.41 ± 0.3 m. Common herbaceous vegetation included Rubus plicatus, annual grass35, Plantago asiatica, dandelion, Plantago lanceolata, Plantago asiatica, and Echinochloa crusgalli. Bjedov et al. found that during the self-restoration process of ski resorts, the main tree species in the self-grown vegetation were Pinus sylvestris and Picea abies, whereas the main herbaceous species included Polygonatum odoratum, Dactylis glomerata, Poa abbreviata, Galium mollugo, Arctostaphylos uva-ursi, and Festuca ovina36.

The use of seeds from local natural populations is recommended to establish a stable plant community37. However, non-native species are often used for vegetation restoration on ski slopes because they are cheaper than local plants and can quickly establish sufficient vegetation cover to control soil erosion. Meng et al. introduced a method for establishing turf on ski slopes that optimized the seeding rates of Festuca arundinacea, Lolium perenne, and Lolium multiflorum to enhance the wear resistance and recovery ability of ski slope turf, effectively extending the lifespan of ski slope turf38. The “Technical specification for water-saving of outdoor artificial ski resort” GB/T 30,683 − 2014 specifies the types of vegetation for ski slopes: in the Northeast region39, Lolium perenne, Festuca arundinacea, and Poa pratensis are recommended; in the North China region, Poa pratensis, Festuca arundinacea, Elymus nutans, and Medicago sativa are recommended; in the Northwest region, Elymus nutans, Leymus chinensis, and Agrostis gigantea are recommended; and in the Central China region, Poa pratensis, Festuca arundinacea, and Cynodon dactylon are recommended.

Water management for lawns can be divided into two stages. (1) Early Turf Management: The principle is to water small amounts frequently, ensuring that the turf remains moist but not waterlogged. From sowing to emergence, water should be applied at least once a day on sunny days to moisten the surface soil each time. Seeds begin to germinate after four days, and turf is established in approximately 3 weeks. (2) Maintenance management: The frequency and amount of irrigation should be determined based on the water demands of the lawn, weather conditions, and growth rate. The goal is to moisten the soil to a depth of 5–10 cm40. For example, lawns in Beijing are typically irrigated once every 1–2 weeks. Lawn water consumption follows a bimodal pattern over time, with the first peak occurring from mid-May to late June and the second peak from mid-July to mid-September. Lawn water consumption varies with climatic factors such as rainfall [24]. Sprinkler irrigation systems should comply with the requirements of the “Technical Code for Sprinkler Engineering GB/T 50085 − 2007 41. Moridi used the models to assess optimal water allocation scenarios42–44.

Regarding water demand calculation for summer turf management in ski resorts, water demand of m3/(m2·a) can be applied18. Local standards in Beijing specify that the water intake range for green spaces in ecological conservation areas is 0.12–0.29 m3/(m2·a)45. The ecological water deficit for lawns is 0.14 m3/(m2·a) annually, with significant seasonal variation, with the highest deficit in May and June and lower deficits from July to October46. Therefore, the water demand for ecological restoration in summer ski resorts is substantial.

Validation of water demand model

The national standards “Technical Specifications for Water Conservation in Outdoor Artificial Ski Resorts” (GB/T 30683 − 2014)39 and “Water Quotas Part 34: Artificial Ski Resorts” (DB11/T 1764.34–2021)47 specify the supply range of water intake for snowmaking on ski slopes and other areas, excluding water for catering, accommodation, and landscaping. Therefore, these standards can serve as a basis for validating the water demand model based on snowmaking theory. Among them, GB/T 20,683 − 2014 sets norm of water intake of snowmaking for the Northeast, North China, Northwest, and Central China regions at 0.40, 0.45, 0.50, and 0.55 m3/(m3·a), respectively; DB11/T 1764.34–2021 specifies a norm of water intake of 0.39–0.42 m3/(m3·a). The results show that the upper limits of these values are similar to the theoretical values of water demand for snowmaking on ski slopes, indicating the reliability of the water demand quantification model for ski resorts and providing theoretical support for calculating water demand during the snowmaking process.

Field survey data provide comprehensive annual water consumption for ski resorts, including water for snowmaking, irrigation of slope vegetation, landscaping, and domestic use, covering a wide range of areas. Therefore, these values are slightly higher than the norm of the water intake indicators and the theoretical values of water demand for snowmaking at winter ski resorts. In summary, the theoretical water demand for winter snowmaking ranges from approximately 0.34 to 0.59 m3/(m3·a), with national and local norm of water intake values ranging from approximately 0.39 to 0.55 m3/(m3·a), showing similar numerical ranges. The water demand for lawns is approximately 0.12–0.30 m3/(m3·a). The combined water demand for winter and summer lawn irrigation was slightly lower than the actual survey results for ski resorts, demonstrating that most ski resorts are primarily concerned with providing lodging, food and beverage, and other services that require water.

Interaction between ski resorts and agricultural irrigation

Ski resorts and agricultural irrigation may compete for water resources when they share the same water source, particularly in mountainous or highland areas where water resources are limited. Sharing water sources also means that ski resorts and agriculture may be affected by each other’s water quality. Ski resorts use chemicals for snowmaking or facility maintenance48, whereas agricultural irrigation involves the use of pesticides and fertilizers49. These chemicals may enter the water source and affect the water quality, necessitating attention from the relevant authorities.

However, ski resorts and agricultural irrigation can form an interactive relationship. Ski resorts require large amounts of water to produce snow in winter, whereas agriculture requires large amounts of water for crop irrigation in spring and summer. Reusing snowmelt and drainage water from ski resorts for agricultural irrigation in the spring and summer is a sustainable water resource management method. Ski resorts use their slopes to collect drainage water, including snowmelt and rainfall, with well-designed slope water collection systems. This water can be reused for agriculture, effectively alleviating the pressure on local water resources, promoting water resource recycling, and improving farmland irrigation efficiency50,51. However, normal operation of ski resort drainage systems can prevent waterlogging and ground collapse caused by water accumulation.

Collecting and reusing ski resort slope drainage for agriculture can be achieved through the following steps. First, it is necessary to establish appropriate drainage systems to collect ski resort drainage. Drainage may contain various impurities and chemical substances; therefore, proper treatment is needed to ensure that the water quality meets irrigation water standards52. Treatment methods include filtration, sedimentation, and disinfection to avoid negative impacts on the soil and crops. The treated drainage can be stored in reservoirs, ditches, and dams for subsequent irrigation use. The irrigation system should be designed reasonably following relevant standards53 to ensure that water can be effectively transported to fields requiring irrigation while maintaining uniformity and efficiency.

Therefore, to address the competition and management issues of water resources, ski resorts and agricultural irrigation can cooperate to develop comprehensive water resource management plans that consider the needs of ski resorts, agriculture, and other water users throughout the water supply chain, including water distribution, utilization, recovery, and reuse. Alternatively, water resource market mechanisms can be established to achieve rational allocation and optimal utilization of water resources through water resource trading and other means, incentivizing improvements in water resource utilization efficiency across different industries while also providing more water resource options for ski resorts and agriculture.

Conclusion

The sustainable utilization of water resources in high water-consuming service industries such as ski resorts is one of the key directions of water resources management in China at present. This study provides a scientific basis for sustainable water resource management by developing a quantitative model to estimate the water demand for snowmaking in ski resorts, analyzing the summer turf management, and exploring the potential for water competition and cooperation between ski resorts and irrigated agriculture. The results showed that the advanced and common values calculated by the quantitative model of snowmaking water demand in ski resorts were 0.34 m3/(m2·yr) and 0.59 m3/(m2·yr) respectively, which can be used to scientifically evaluate the water consumption of snowmaking in winter. The compaction of snow density had the greatest impact on water demand at ski resorts. turf management at ski resorts in summer is crucial for preserving the terrain and landscape, and also opens possibilities for developing green ecotourism at ski resorts during summer. The water demand for turf management is estimated to be approximately 0.12–0.30 m3/(m3·yr). There is both competition and potential cooperation for water resources between ski resorts and agricultural irrigation. Utilizing the sloped terrain of ski resorts to collect and reuse water for agricultural irrigation can help achieve sustainable water utilization and improve farmland irrigation efficiency. The research results can strengthen the methodological foundation for norm of water intake estimation at ski resorts, providing important guidance for analyzing water demand at ski resorts and implementing sustainable water management practices.

In addition, based on our results, it can be determined that future research directions should focus on the following areas: First, exploring and developing more efficient snowmaking techniques and turf management methods to reduce water consumption and improve water utilisation efficiency. Second, assessing the impact of climate change on water demand for ski resorts and turf management, and developing appropriate strategies to deal with them. In addition, a great deal of research should be done on water recycling and reuse technologies to enhance water recycling in ski resorts and agricultural irrigation systems, and integrated water management models should be constructed to optimise the allocation of all types of water demand in the region.
