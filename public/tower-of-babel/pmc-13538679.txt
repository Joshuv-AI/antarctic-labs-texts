Solvation-engineering-enabled quasi-solid positive electrode for four-electron aqueous Zn||Br batteries
Authors: Zejun Chen, Mengzi Geng, Shizhen Li, Hangqi Yang, Wanru Chen, Ning Li, Xianbo Jin, Chuang Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiffusion, crossover, and hydrolysis of bromine species in aqueous electrolytes impede the application of Zn||Br batteries with four-electron 2Br+/Br2/2Br− conversion. Here, we study a ligand-assisted sparingly solvating electrolyte using ZnSO4·7H2O and urea. The high solubility of urea suppresses water activity, while its stronger affinity toward bromine enables preferential coordination over water molecules. This synergistic effect creates a quasi-solvent-free microenvironment that limits bromine solvation and diffusion, resulting in a robust quasi-solid cathode. Moreover, the compact solvation structure favors the formation and stability of Br+ species by strengthening nucleophilic urea coordination and inhibiting hydrolysis. Consequently, the fabricated Zn||Br battery exhibits reversible 4e bromine chemistry with a capacity of 417.5 mAh g−1 at 1 A g−1 (based on KBr). A zinc battery coupling the 4e iodine positive electrode and 4e bromine (from the electrolyte) is constructed, yielding a total 8e transfer with a capacity of 897.8 mAh g−1 and a specific energy of 1,344 Wh kg−1 at 2 A g−1 (based on KI).Subject terms: Batteries, Batteries

Introduction

Rechargeable aqueous zinc batteries (AZBs) are considered a promising energy storage technology owing to their intrinsic safety, environmental friendliness and low cost1–5. Halide-based conversion-type positive electrodes, particularly those utilizing bromine and iodine, endow AZBs with multi-electron redox reaction, high discharge voltage (1.08 V for Br2/2Br− and 0.53 V for I2/2I− vs. standard hydrogen electrode), flat voltage plateau and rapid reaction kinetics6–11. For example, the recently reported four-electron (4e) zinc−iodine batteries (2I+ ↔ I2↔2I−) possess a high theoretical capacity of 422 mAh g–1 based on I2, positioning AZBs as potential candidates even for power battery applications12–15. Although bromine can provide a higher theoretical capacity, it (Br2 or Brn−, n = 3, 5, 7) exhibits high solubility in conventional strongly solvating aqueous electrolytes. As a result, the positive electrode behaves as a liquid-phase environment (liquid-phase positive electrode, Fig. S1a), where bromine species migrate freely. This extensive mobility causes active material loss, Zn negative electrode corrosion and a decrease in Coulombic efficiency, ultimately hampering further advancement of bromine chemistry in static AZBs16–20.

To address this challenge, current studies predominantly focus on the design of polar hosts or bromine complexing agents (BCAs) with higher affinity to bromine species than the electrolyte, thus suppressing the solvation and diffusion of these species18,21–24. However, the difficulty in precisely controlling the adsorption capacity and strength may result in locally insufficient binding, forming a semi-solid positive electrode with coexisting condensed and dissolved bromine species. Increasing the dosage of polar hosts or BCAs can partially mitigate this issue but inevitably compromises the overall energy density. In particular, BCA-type positive electrodes based on quaternary ammonium bromides exhibit high chemical stability and reversible bromine redox behavior characteristic of solid-state positive electrodes24–28. However, the high molecular weight of these BCAs limits the overall capacity, with a typical value of 215 mAh g–1 despite undergoing the 4e 2Br+/Br2/2Br− conversion28.

Alternatively, electrolyte engineering provides a molecular-level strategy to achieve a similar confined state without additional complexing agents. Recently, hydrated eutectic electrolytes or weakly solvating media (sparingly solvating electrolytes) have been explored to regulate solvation environments and stabilize multi-electron reactions in aqueous zinc−iodine batteries12,13,15,29,30. These strategies suppress the solvation and hydrolysis of iodine species by reducing the activity of free water through hydrogen-bond rearrangement, forming a solid-like iodine positive electrode. However, for bromine chemistry, the higher solubility and stronger hydrolysis tendency make solvation control difficult31–34, often resulting in a semi-solid positive electrode with poor reversibility (Fig. S1b). Furthermore, the 4e 2Br+/Br2/2Br− conversion requires a coordination environment with restricted water activity and abundant nucleophilic ligands, because electrophilic Br+ is thermodynamically unstable and easily hydrolyzed in water, similar to I+. Existing 2Br+/Br2/2Br− chemistries rely on a strongly acidic electrolyte (0.5 M HCl + 1 M HBr)35, complex electrode design28 or costly concentrated electrolyte36, limiting their large-scale applications in AZBs.

Herein, we propose a cost-effective, mild ligand-assisted sparingly solvating electrolyte that combines suppressed water activity with preferential coordination (Fig. S1c). This electrolyte enables a quasi-solid KBr positive electrode with high reversibility and promotes the formation and stabilization of Br+ species. The resulting Zn||Br battery exhibits a stable 4e redox reaction (2Br+ ↔ Br2↔2Br−), even at a high KBr loading of 113 mg. In addition, the electrolyte supports reversible 4e iodine and 4e bromine redox reactions within a single battery system, further enhancing the overall energy density.

Results

Bromine diffusion suppression and 2Br+/Br2/2Br− conversion

The electrolytes were prepared by mixing ZnSO4·7H2O, urea and H2O in varying molar ratios (denoted as xZyU-z, Fig. S2), among which 1Z3U-3 possesses the lowest water content. An electrochemically inert carbon-coated glass fiber (GF) separator was employed to reduce the internal impedance of the battery (Fig. S3−S5, details in the experimental section). The cyclic voltammograms (CVs, Fig. 1a) of the coin-type zinc||bromine battery (KBr as the positive electrode, Zn||KBr) with 1 M ZnSO4 exhibit a single pair of redox peaks at 1.87/1.79 V, corresponding to the 2e Br2/2Br− redox transition21,25. In contrast, CVs of the cell with 1Z3U-3 show two pairs of redox peaks at 1.95/1.85 and 1.81/1.74 V, which are assigned to the 4e 2Br+/Br2/2Br− conversion28,35. The three nearly overlapped CV curves demonstrate the reversible and stable 2Br+ ↔ Br2↔2Br− reaction in 1Z3U-3.

Consistent with the CV curves, the galvanostatic discharge curve (Fig. 1b) of the cell with 1 M ZnSO4 exhibits a voltage plateau at 1.81 V and an initial discharge capacity of 110.7 mAh g–1 (based on KBr) at a specific current of 1 A g–1. After cycling, orange-yellow bromine species are observed on the separator surface facing the zinc negative electrode (Fig. S6), attributed to bromine migration caused by excess free water. The ZnSO4 concentration is further increased to its upper limit of 3 M, but no significant improvement in battery performance is observed (Fig. S7). Although higher salt concentrations partially reduce water activity, the strong polarity and high mobility of free water molecules still promote severe bromine solvation and diffusion in 3 M ZnSO4. In comparison, the cell with 1Z3U-3 delivers a high initial discharge capacity of 417.5 mAh g–1 (theoretical capacity of 450 mAh g–1), with two discharge plateaus starting at 1.90 V. The corresponding separator retains its original white color after cycling (Fig. S8), revealing suppression of bromine species migration. Therefore, 1Z3U-3 not only enables highly reversible 4e bromine redox chemistry but also prevents bromine species diffusion and crossover, as illustrated in Fig. 1c. This 4e bromine redox reaction delivers both competitive discharge voltage and specific capacity28,37–50, corresponding to a specific energy of 656.5 Wh kg–1 (based on KBr, Fig. 1d). The source of the literature data shown in this figure can be found in Table S1. The three-electrode CV further confirms the high-voltage bromine chemistry in 1Z3U-3, with a reduction peak at 0.98 V vs Ag/AgCl (1.94 V vs Zn2+/Zn, Figure. S9). 1Z3U-3 also allows for highly reversible 4e iodine chemistry (2I+/I2/2I−) and co-conversion of 4e iodine and 4e bromine redox reactions (Fig. 1e, details in the following section), demonstrating its applicability in multi-electron zinc||halide batteries.

Bromine species solvation chemistry

Upon addition of Br3− or Br2, both pure water and 1 M ZnSO4 immediately turn yellow, which is attributed to the strong solvating power of bulk free water (Fig. 2a). In contrast, 1Z3U-3 remains colorless and transparent, with the pale-yellow Br3− solution floating on the upper layer, or oily Br2 droplets accumulating at the bottom. The ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) spectra (Fig. 2b) further support this observation, showing strong absorbance peaks of bromine species in H2O and 1 M ZnSO4, but no detectable peak in 1Z3U-3. The absence of such signals suggests poor solvation of Br3− and Br2 in 1Z3U-3. In situ UV-Vis spectra reveal that in the quartz-type Zn||KBr cell with 1 M ZnSO4 (Fig. S10), the absorbance intensity of Br3− at 264 nm increases continuously during the first charging process at 1 A g–1 (Fig. 2c), indicating substantial dissolution and accumulation of bromine species8,18. In comparison, the cell with 1Z3U-3 exhibits consistently minimal Br3− absorbance throughout the charging process (Fig. 2d), implying that the dissolution and migration of bromine species are effectively suppressed. Accordingly, after the first charging, the 1 M ZnSO4 electrolyte turns visibly yellow (inset in Figs. 2c and S11), whereas 1Z3U-3 remains colorless (inset in Fig. 2d). All comparisons confirm that the weak solvation ability of 1Z3U-3 effectively suppresses the dissolution, diffusion, and crossover of bromine species.

Compared with pristine urea (1012 cm–1), the C−N stretching vibration shifts to 1007 cm–1 in 1 M ZnSO4 containing 3 M (1Z + 3U) and 10 M (1Z + 10U) urea (Fig. 3e). This red shift is attributed to the weakening of intramolecular hydrogen bonding (H-bonding) within urea, induced by strong intermolecular H-bonding with H2O51,52. In 1Z3U-3, the C − N stretching vibration shifts to its original peak position at 1012 cm−1, resulting from the enhanced urea−urea interactions under low water content. Therefore, 1Z3U-3 exhibits a water-deficient structure that is unable to fully solvate urea. The SO42− peak at 983 cm–1 remains unchanged across different electrolyte formulations, indicating negligible interaction between urea and SO42−53,54. The blue shift observed in the C=O (1541 cm–1) and NH2 (1649 cm–1) peaks of urea (Fig. S12), along with the weakened and slightly blue-shifted O−H stretching vibration (3200−3500 cm–1) of water in urea-containing solutions (Fig. S13), verifying the strong affinity of urea for water51. The same phenomenon is also observed in the Fourier transform infrared spectra (FTIR, Fig. S14). In addition, the decreasing contact angle with higher urea content (Fig. S15) confirms the reduced water activity55,56.

The density functional theory (DFT) calculations (Fig. 2f) show that urea−water (U−H2O) interactions are stronger than water−water interactions (H2O−H2O), which accounts for the high solubility of urea and the formation of a water-deficient environment. This strong hydrogen-bonding ability of urea enables it to effectively compete with water molecules, reducing the number of free water molecules available for solvating bromine species. Furthermore, bromine species (Br3−/Br2) preferentially coordinate with urea rather than water, forming Br3−/Br2−U complexes. This preference is driven by the low electrostatic potential of the carbonyl group and the high density of polarized hydrogens, which facilitate strong dipole−ion interactions (Fig. 2g). The competitive coordination between urea and bromine, together with suppressed water activity, creates a quasi-solvent-free microenvironment (Fig. 2h), shifting bromine species from a solvent-solvated state to a ligand-coordinated state. As a result, this ligand-assisted sparingly solvating electrolyte (LA-SSE) suppresses bromine solvation and diffusion, which confines bromine within the positive electrode and promotes quasi-solid behavior.

The solvation behavior of bromine species is associated with the hydration strength of the accompanying inorganic anions, following the Hoffmeister series in order of SO42− > ClO4− > CF3SO3− (Fig. S16)28,57. Even in urea-enriched Zn(ClO4)2 and Zn(CF3SO3)2 solutions with minimal water content, bromine species still undergo diffusion (Fig. S17), owning to the weak hydration and poor water-structuring ability of the chaotropic ClO4− and CF3SO3−. These results underscore the importance of lowering water activity for restricting bromine solvation. Two electrolytes with low water content were further prepared for comparison: a high concentrated electrolyte (HCE) containing 20 m potassium acetate and 10 m zinc acetate (20 m KAc + 10 m Zn(Ac)2) and a hydrated eutectic electrolyte (HEE) based on ZnSO4·7H2O and 1-Butyl-3-methylimidazolium chloride. With Br3− or Br2 introduced, both electrolytes exhibit bromine species diffusion (Figs. S18 and S19). In HCE, it is challenging to prevent bromine species from interacting with water31. In HEE, the polar and ionized environment likely facilitates the solubility of bromine species34,58,59.

When the urea ligand is added to the prepared HCE (5 m urea + 15 m KAc + 10 m Zn(Ac)2), Br3− migration is further suppressed compared with 20 m KAc + 10 m Zn(Ac)2) (Fig. S20). Moreover, 5 m urea + 15 m KAc + 10 m Zn(Ac)2 shows a lower viscosity (84.2 mPa S) than 20 m KAc + 10 m Zn(Ac)2 (126 mPa S, Fig. S21). This observation suggests that bromine stabilization in LA-SSE arises from ligand-assisted coordination rather than low water content alone. These findings collectively highlight the importance of regulating water activity and the preferential coordination of ligand molecules with bromine species in establishing LA-SSE, which is crucial for suppressing bromine solvation and diffusion.

Acetamide (Ace, Fig. S22a) and nicotinamide (Nico), two amide-based ligands with similar physicochemical properties to urea, were further selected as control components. For each system, the electrolyte composition was adjusted to its minimum achievable water content. The CVs of Zn||KBr cell using Ace− or Nico−ZnSO4 electrolytes show only a single Br2/2Br− redox pair (Fig. S22b). When Br2 is added to the two electrolytes, reddish-brown precipitates form (Fig. S22c), in contrast to the homogeneous liquid complexes observed in the urea-based system. Such complexation−precipitation behavior may convert bromine species into stable, poorly conductive solids, leading to sluggish reaction kinetics and suppressing further oxidation toward high-valence Br+-type species. Therefore, urea introduces a ligand-coordination-based electrolyte design, which balances bromine confinement and activation beyond simple water activity reduction.

Formation and stabilization mechanism of Br+-type species

The in situ Raman spectra (Fig. 3a) capture the full evolution of bromine species in the Zn||KBr cell with 1Z3U-3 during the first cycle at 1 A g–1. Initially, Br3− forms during charging and is subsequently oxidized to Br+-type species, with peaks appearing around 272 cm−1 in the high-voltage range of 1.8−2 V28,36,60,61. As the discharge progresses, these Br+-type species peaks gradually disappear, reflecting the reduction of bromine species. The correlation between the Raman-identified reaction interval and the cumulative discharge capacity indicates that Br+-related redox processes account for approximately 30% of the total capacity. The extended CVs (Fig. S23) show two well-defined reduction peaks corresponding to the Br+/Br0/Br− conversion over 20 cycles at 0.5 and 1 mV s–1. This persistent visibility suggests that Br+-type species are a stable and reversible redox species, rather than a transient intermediate. Redox-selective chemical titrations were conducted using well-characterized reference bromine compounds. N-bromosuccinimide (NBS, Br+-type species, Fig. S24a) and tetrabutylammonium tribromide (TBABr3, representing Br3−) were used only as reference compounds in separate ex-situ titration experiments, not as additives in the battery electrolyte. Two cells were assembled using NBS or TBABr3 as the positive electrodes, respectively, paired with Zn electrodes. A slight excess of KI was added to the positive electrode region as a chemical indicator (Fig. S24b). Then, the two cells were allowed to rest for 30 min to ensure complete chemical conversion of the reference bromine species and to avoid interference from their inherent reactivity. Upon direct discharge, the NBS-based cell (NBS + KI) shows two distinct plateaus (Fig. S24c), whereas the TBABr3-based cell (TBABr3 + KI) exhibits only a single plateau (Fig. S24d). This difference reflects the higher oxidative capability of Br+-type species, which can oxidize I− to higher-valent I+, while Br3− only converts I− to I2. After charging Zn||KBr cells to different voltages, the same KI indicator was applied (Fig. S25a). Charging to 2.0 V (2.0 V + KI, Fig. S25b) produces two plateaus, similar to the NBS system, while charging to 1.8 V (only Br2 generation, 1.8 V + KI, Fig. S25c) results in a single discharge plateau, consistent with TBABr3. These results further confirm the formation of high-valent, Br+-type bromine species under higher voltage polarization.

The Gibbs free energy change (ΔG) for the transition from Br0 to Br+ is calculated to be 14.36 eV, which is higher than 1.04 eV for the Br0−Urea to Br+−Urea complexes (Fig. 3b). The low ΔG indicates that the urea coordination promotes the thermodynamic favorability of the further oxidation of Br2 to 2Br+62,63. The Br0 to [BrCl2]− conversion displays a higher ΔG of 4.87 eV compared to the Br0/Br+−Urea couple, which corresponds to a higher redox potential, consistent with previous reports28. The lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) and highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) energy levels (Fig. 3c) reveal that Br+−Urea shows a much larger energy gap (4.38 eV) than free Br+ (0.75 eV), suggesting higher resistance to self-disproportionation37,63. Compared to free Br+, the more uniform electron density of Br+ in Br+−Urea complex further suggests a more stabilized electronic structure and higher thermodynamic stability (Fig. 3d)37,64. Linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) curves reveal the onset potential of oxygen evolution reaction (OER) of 1.75 V in 1 M ZnSO4 (vs. Zn2+/Zn, Fig. 3e). However, 1Z3U-3, with water-deficient structure, shows a higher OER onset potential of 2.26 V, enabling the high-voltage 2Br+/Br2 redox couple.

To clarify the respective roles of nucleophilic coordination and water activity in Br+ generation and stabilization, bromine electrochemistry was examined in different electrolytes. The Zn||KBr cell using 20 m KAc + 10 m Zn(Ac)2, a salt-rich but weakly coordinating system, shows only a single Br2/2Br− redox couple (Fig. 3f). In comparison, the Cl−-enriched HEE provides both nucleophilic coordination and a water-deficient environment, enabling a 4e 2Br+/Br2/2Br− conversion (Fig. 3g). Besides, excessive water content severely suppresses Br+ formation. Although 1Z3U-15 possesses the same urea coordination environment, it exhibits only the Br2/2Br− conversion, with a low discharge voltage of 1.8 V and an initial capacity of 154 mAh g–1, due to pronounced Br+ hydrolysis at high water content. By comparison, the cell with 1Z3U-3 achieves full 4e 2Br+/Br2/2Br− conversion, with a discharge voltage of 1.9 V and a retained capacity of 330.8 mAh g–1 after 35 cycles, demonstrating the stabilizing effect of complete urea coordination (Fig. 3h, i). Moreover, the Coulombic efficiency (CE) gradually increases during 35 cycles (Fig. S26), indicating progressive stabilization of the electrochemical system. The low initial CEs can be attributed bromine redistribution within the positive electrode and interfacial stabilization processes at the zinc negative electrode. Such behavior has also been observed in Zn||halogen systems and Li||S batteries18,65,66.

The cell was further charged potentiostatically at 2.0 V to maintain the presence of Br+-type species. Over a 300 min holding period, the electrolyte pH near the positive electrode is almost unchanged, staying close to its initial value of approximately 5.1 (Fig. S27a). NBS was further employed as a Br+ reference compound for comparison. Upon addition of NBS, the pH of 1 M ZnSO4 gradually decreases from approximately 4.31 to 3.79 within 180 min (Fig. S27b), attributed to Br+ hydrolysis producing acidic bromine-containing species (e.g., HOBr)67,68. In contrast, the introduction of NBS into 1Z3U-3 results in the pH maintaining close to 5.0 (5.14 to 4.99) over the same period, demonstrating the contribution of restricted water activity to Br+-type species stabilization. These results demonstrate that both effective coordination and restricted water activity are essential for the generation and stabilization of Br+ species.

The characteristic 4e bromine redox chemistry (2Br−↔Br2↔2Br+) is also detected in urea-enriched Zn(ClO4)2 and Zn(CF3SO3)2 electrolytes with minimal water content (sulfate-free systems, Fig. S28a). This result suggests that anions are not required as a primary coordination species controlling bromine redox speciation. Instead, the 4e pathway arises from the urea-dominated solvation structure. However, the sulfate-free electrolytes exhibit faster capacity decay than the sulfate-based system (1Z3U-3, Fig. S28b), which is consistent with the increased bromine migration observed in the visual diffusion experiment. Therefore, it can be concluded that urea governs bromine redox chemistry, while sulfate mainly enhances the suppression of bromine transport and crossover.

Electrochemical performances of Zn||KBr cell

The Tafel curves (Fig. 4a) reveal that zinc foil is prone to corrosion in 1 M ZnSO4, exhibiting a higher corrosion current density (Icorr) of 1.1 mA cm–2 and a lower corrosion potential (Ecorr) of −0.93 V, compared to 0.1 mA cm–2 and −0.90 V in 1Z3U-3. The improved corrosion resistance of zinc foil in 1Z3U-3 is ascribed to the limited water activity. At a current density of 0.25 mA cm–2 and an areal capacity of 0.25 mAh cm–2, the Zn||Cu cell with 1 M ZnSO4 maintains stable CEs during Zn plating/stripping for only 50 cycles. Subsequently, fluctuating CEs and galvanostatic charge-discharge (GCD) curves are observed (Figs. 4b and 5c), indicating severe parasitic reactions and poor reversibility. In contrast, the cell with 1Z3U-3 delivers a stable average CE of 98.0% over 650 cycles. Accordingly, after plating/stripping in 1 M ZnSO4, the zinc metal exhibits a corroded and rough surface covered with Zn4SO4(OH)6·xH2O (ZSH, PDF#39-0690) (Fig. 4d, f). ZSH originates from electrochemical corrosion caused by the hydrogen evolution reaction in dilute electrolytes69,70. Conversely, the zinc plating/stripping in 1Z3U-3 retains a compact and smooth surface with no obvious ZSH (Fig. 4e, f). Zn plating/stripping behavior was further evaluated using Zn||Cu cells at an areal capacity of 1 mAh cm–2 in 1Z3U-3 (Fig. S29a). At current densities of 1−5 mA cm–2, the cells achieve average CEs ranging from 98.3% to 98.9% during 100 cycles, indicating reversible Zn plating/stripping behavior.

Moreover, at a current density of 0.5 mA cm–2 and an areal capacity of 0.5 mAh cm–2, the Zn||Zn symmetric cell with 1Z3U-3 shows stable voltage with small variations during Zn plating/stripping for over 850 hours (h), whereas a short-circuit is observed in the cell with 1 M ZnSO4 after only 160 h (Fig. 4g). At a current density of 1 mA cm–2 and an areal capacity of 1 mAh cm–2, the Zn||Zn cell with 1 M ZnSO4 (Fig. S29b) shows pronounced voltage hysteresis (ΔE) fluctuations, with ΔE increasing to approximately 510 mV (about 7 times the initial value) after 260 h, reflecting an unstable deposition interface. In contrast, the cell with 1Z3U-3 maintains a lower and stable ΔE of 61 mV even after 500 h. These results indicate that 1Z3U-3 plays a positive role in stabilizing zinc plating/stripping.

In situ electrochemical gas chromatography (EC-GC) quantifies hydrogen evolution during Zn plating at 1 mA cm–2. Compared with 1 M ZnSO4 (Fig. S29c), detectable H2 is delayed by approximately 2.1 h in 1Z3U-3 (Fig. S29d). After 8 h of plating, the cumulative H2 generation is 6.77 μmol L–1 in 1Z3U-3 (Fig. S28e), approximately 70% lower than that in 1 M ZnSO4 (24 μmol L–1). Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations (Figs. 4h and S30) and radial distribution functions (RDFs, Figure. S31) reveal that Zn2+ in 1Z3U-3 primarily exists as Zn2+(urea)(H2O)4SO42−, which contains fewer coordinated water molecules than the fully hydrated Zn(H2O)62+ species in dilute aqueous electrolytes. In general, H2O molecules within the solvation shell of Zn2+ are susceptible to decomposition during Zn deposition, as the Zn−H2O interaction weakens the H−O bonds71–73. Therefore, the reduced number of solvated water molecules in 1Z3U-3 corresponds to lower water activity, which allows highly reversible Zn plating/stripping.

The CV curves (Fig. 5a) of the Zn||KBr full cell with 1Z3U-3 display two pairs of redox peaks at different sweep rates from 0.1 to 0.5 mV s−1, confirming the 4e 2Br+/Br2/2Br− conversion. The GCD performances at 2 A g–1 (Fig. 5b, c) reveal that the cell with 1 M ZnSO4 delivers a low initial capacity of 106 mAh g–1, corresponding to a 2e transfer. The cell with HEE shows a high discharge voltage staring 2.0 V, indicating a 4e transfer, but exhibits a low initial discharge capacity of 202.8 mAh g−1, mainly due to the high solubility of bromine species. In contrast, the cell with 1Z3U-3 achieves an initial discharge capacity of 341.2 mAh g−1, with a capacity decay rate of 0.08% per cycle upon 400 cycles. The observed cycling stability of 1 M ZnSO4- or HEE-based cells arises largely from the capacitive contribution of active carbon, rather than from genuine reversible bromine redox. Even at a high specific current of 7 A g−1, the full cell maintains a capacity of 212.7 mAh g−1 with distinct discharge voltage plateau (Figs. 5d and S32). The fast reaction kinetics is attributed to the liquid-phase reactants involved in the redox reaction and the reduced internal impedance enabled by the carbon-coated separator (Fig. S33).

To further investigate the role of carbon-coated separator, both carbon-coated and uncoated separators were immersed in 10 mM Br3− solutions for 12 h in the dark. No noticeable color difference is observed between the two solutions (inset in Fig. S34). UV-Vis spectra (Fig. S34) show only a slight decrease in peak intensity for the solution with the carbon-coated separator, which can be attributed to weak physical adsorption by the carbon coating. The cycle performance of the cells with coated and uncoated separators was compared at 1 A g−1. The coated cell delivers a higher initial discharge capacity (381.3 mAh g−1) than the uncoated cell (340 mAh g−1, Fig. S35a). However, both cells exhibit similar cycle stability (Fig. S35b). After 70 cycles, the uncoated cell retains 258.9 mAh g−1 with a decay rate of 0.34% per cycle. The coated cell maintains 281.4 mAh g−1, exhibiting a decay rate of 0.37% per cycle. Additionally, the average CEs are nearly identical (89.8% for the uncoated cell vs. 91.8% for the coated cell), suggesting that the carbon coating has a negligible influence on overall reaction reversibility. Post-cycling analysis reveals the presence of bromine species in the carbon coating (Fig. S35c), which are attributed to interfacial deposition during cycling. The conductive carbon layer in close contact with the positive electrode acts as a secondary current collector, improving interfacial charge transfer and active material utilization74,75. Overall, these results suggest that the carbon coating plays a limited role in bromine adsorption. The carbon coating primarily enhances interfacial conductivity, resulting in higher initial capacity without affecting long-term cycling stability.

Long-term GCD test at 5 A g−1 reveals that the full cell delivers an initial capacity of 278.4 mAh g–1, with a capacity decay rate of 0.014% per cycle and an average CE of 98.0% over the subsequent 2000 cycles (Fig. 5e). This long-cycle performance further confirms the stability of both the positive electrode and negative electrode chemistries in 1Z3U-3. H-type cell was assembled to further evaluate bromine crossover (inset in Fig. S36a). After 100 cycles at 1 A g–1, UV-Vis spectra (Fig. S36a) show a weak Br3− peak on the positive electrode side. No absorption peak appears on the Zn negative electrode side, indicating minimal bromine migration. Self-discharge performance was explored with fully charged cells, which were allowed to stand at open-circuit voltage (2.0 V) over a given period. After 300 and 480 min, the cells retain 90.5% and 88.4% of their initial capacity (Fig. S36b), respectively. These results further demonstrate that the quasi-solid positive electrode suppresses bromine migration.

To evaluate the practicability of 1Z3U-3, a pouch cell with 4 × 4 cm2 positive electrode was prepared (inset in Fig. 5f). With a KBr loading of 2.98 mg cm−2 (total mass of 47.6 mg), 10 μL of electrolyte per mg of KBr and a negative to positive (N/P) ratio of 20, the pouch cell exhibits a discharge capacity of 11.4 mAh (0.71 mAh cm−2) and retains 9.4 mAh (0.59 mAh cm−2) after 200 cycles at 2 A g−1 (Figs. 5f and S37). Increasing the KBr loading to 7.06 mg cm−2 (total KBr mass of 113 mg) with 10 μL of electrolyte per mg of KBr and an N/P ratio of 10, the pouch cell delivers a discharge capacity of 33.7 mAh (2.1 mAh cm–2) at 1 A g−1, showing two distinct voltage plateaus (Fig. 5g). After 200 cycles at 2 A g−1, the pouch cell retains 17.8 mAh (1.1 mAh cm−2) with an average CE of 98.1% (Figs. 5h and S38, S39). The corresponding initial specific energy is 445 Wh kg−1 based on KBr or 31.6 Wh kg−1 at the device level (based on the total mass of the cell excluding the aluminum-plastic film). Future efforts will aim to further stabilize the deposition interface and explore electrode-free Zn||KBr cell, improving the overall energy density of battery76,77. At 25 °C and 0 °C, 1Z3U-3 shows no observable precipitation or phase separation after one month of static storage (Fig. S40a). However, at lower temperatures (−10 °C and −15 °C), the solution becomes turbid, with a small amount of crystallization detected. The high urea concentration also leads to increased viscosity (58 vs 2.7 mPa S, Fig. S40b) and reduced ionic conductivity (2.76 vs 23.9 mS cm−1) compared with the 1 M ZnSO4 electrolyte. Despite these changes, the stable long-term cycling performance demonstrates the overall stability of 1Z3U-3 under the tested conditions. Moreover, compared to some recently reported zinc||halide electrolytes, 1Z3U is more cost-effective, i.e., 0.39 USD kg−1, making it a commercially viable choice for large-scale applications (Tables S3 and S4).

Combined 4e iodine and 4e bromine redox chemistry

To verify the applicability of 1Z3U-3, a coin-type Zn||iodine battery using KI positive electrode (Zn||KI) was prepared and evaluated. The in situ Raman spectra (Fig. 6a) show the I+ peaks at 185 cm−1 during the first GCD process in the voltage range of 1.6−2.0 V13,15. These peaks reach maximum intensity at the fully charged state (2.0 V), then gradually diminish and disappear during the subsequent discharge. Consistently, the I 3d X-ray photoelectron spectrum (XPS, Fig. S41) reveals a high-valence I+ signal at 632.1/620.5 eV at the fully charged state, further confirming the reversible formation of I+ species in 1Z3U-314. At 2 A g−1, the first GCD curves (Fig. 6b) deliver a discharge capacity of 298.3 mAh g−1, with two distinct voltage plateaus at approximately 1.77 V and 1.23 V. These plateaus correspond to the 2I+ ↔ I2 and I2↔2I− redox conversions, which are consistent with the CV profiles (Fig. 2e). After 500 cycles, the cell retains a capacity of 246.9 mAh g−1 (Figs. 6b and S42).

The mass (31.2 mg) refers to the total loading of KI in the pouch cell. f GCD curves at 2 A g−1 and g cycle performance of the Zn||KI−Br cell at 5 A g−1. Testing temperature: 25 ± 1 °C.

The long-term stability of the 4e Zn||KI−Br cell is further explored at 10 A g−1 (Fig. 6c). After 10,000 cycles, a capacity of 160.4 mAh g−1 is maintained with a decay rate of 0.0028% per cycle. At specific currents of 2, 4, 6, 8, 10 A g−1, the cell shows reversible capacities of 280.8, 254.4, 238.8, 219.5 and 200.6 mAh g−1 (Figs. 6d and S43). Moreover, with a positive electrode dimension of 4 × 4 cm2 (inset in Fig. 6e), KI loading of 31.2 mg, 10 μL of electrolyte per mg of KI and an N/P ratio of 30, the Zn||KI pouch cell achieves an initial capacity of 299.8 mAh g−1 and retains 222.1 mAh g−1 after 400 cycles at 1 A g−1 (Fig. 6e). During cycling, the GCD curves (Fig. S44a) exhibit slight voltage decay, indicating stable and reversible of both the positive electrode and negative electrode. The common stable cell performances of Zn||KBr and Zn||KI cells reveal the strong capabilities of 1Z3U-3 in modulating both the bromine and iodine chemistries.

Upon further incorporation of the highly soluble LiBr into the electrolyte (1Z3U-4 with 0.2 M LiBr), the Zn||KI cell (Zn||KI−Br cell) delivers four distinct discharge voltage plateaus (Fig. 6f), corresponding to sequential 4e redox reactions of bromine (2Br+/Br2/2Br−) and iodine (2I+/I2/2I−). Each plateau contributes capacity of approximately 200 mAh g−1, giving rise to a specific capacity of 897.8 mAh g−1 and specific energy of 1344 Wh kg−1 at 2 A g−1 (based on KI). The nearly identical capacity contributions from bromine and iodine indicate that the molar masses of bromine and iodine are almost the same. When bromine is also included, the calculated specific capacity decreases to approximately 606.6 mAh g−1 and the specific energy to 908 Wh kg−1 (based on KI and Br). Long-term GCD test at 5 A g−1 reveals that the cell maintains a capacity of 452.7 mAh g−1 (based on KI) after 1200 cycles, with a capacity decay rate of 0.026% per cycle (Fig. 6g). The stable voltage profiles (Fig. S44b) indicate the reversibility and robustness of iodine−bromine co-conversion.

Discussion

In summary, the urea-enriched sparingly solvating electrolyte 1Z3U-3 was developed for reversible multi-electron Zn||KBr cells. The low-ESP carbonyl group and polarized hydrogen atoms of urea promote preferential binding with bromine species and contribute to the formation of a solvent-free environment, suppressing bromine solvation and migration. The urea coordination also lowers the ΔG of Br0 to Br+ conversion and stabilizes the Br+ electronic structure, while the low water content inhibits Br+ hydrolysis and electrolyte degradation. The constructed Zn||KBr cell demonstrates reversible 4e 2Br+ ↔ Br2↔Br− conversion, achieving stable discharge voltage of 1.74 V at 1 A g−1. At 5 A g−1, the cell retains a capacity of 197.5 mAh g−1 (based on KBr), with an average CE of 98.0% after 2000 cycles. 1Z3U-3 also enables a reversible 2I+/I2/2I− redox couple in Zn||KBr cell, which demonstrates a high initial capacity of 224.2 mAh g−1 (based on KI), with a low capacity decay rate of 0.0028% per cycle after 10,000 cycles at 10 A g−1. In addition, the use of 1Z3U-4 with 0.2 M LiBr, coupled with a KI positive electrode, enables combined 4e iodine and 4e bromine redox chemistry, achieving a total 8e transfer. This configuration results in a high capacity of 452.7 mAh g−1 (based on KI) after 1200 cycles at 5 A g−1, further highlighting the effectiveness of urea-enriched electrolyte.

Methods

Chemicals and materials

Urea (≥99.5%, Macklin), KBrO3 (≥99.8%, Macklin), ZnSO₄·7H₂O (≥99.7%, Aladdin), KI (≥99%, Aladdin), KBr (≥99%, Aladdin), and LiBr (≥99.9%, Aladdin); Super P (≥99.9%, Guangdong Canrd New Energy Technology Co., Ltd.), activated carbon (YP-50, ≥99.9%, Guangdong Canrd New Energy Technology Co., Ltd.), carbon nanotubes (CNTs, ≥99.9%, Suzhou Slnero Technology Co., Ltd.), and Ketjen Black (KB, ≥99.9%, Suzhou Slnero Technology Co., Ltd.); glass fiber separator (Whatman GF/D, 675 μm thickness, 80% porosity, average pore size 2.7 μm; Sigma-Aldrich); copper foil, titanium foil, and zinc foil (all 100 μm thickness, ≥99.99%, Shenzhen Kejing Star Technology Co., Ltd.), carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC, viscosity ≥1900 mPa s, Shenzhen Kejing Star Technology Co., Ltd.); and graphite paper (50 μm thickness, ≥99.9%, Tan Qianlang Electronic Materials Department). All chemicals and materials used in this work were commercially available and used as received.

Preparation of the electrolytes

A series of electrolytes were synthesized by mixing ZnSO4·7H2O with various hydrogen bond donors and deionized water, followed by stirring at 60 °C for 12 h and cooling to room temperature. The xZyU-z electrolytes were prepared using ZnSO4·7H2O, urea, and water at a molar ratio of x:y:z; for instance, 1Z3U-3 was obtained by mixing 0.01 mol ZnSO4·7H2O, 0.03 mol urea, and 0.03 mol water. Similarly, 1Z5A-4 was synthesized using 0.01 mol ZnSO4·7H2O, 0.05 mol acetamide and 0.04 mol water. In addition, the HEE electrolyte was prepared with ZnSO4·7H2O, 1-butyl-3-methylimidazolium chloride, and water at a molar ratio of 1:2:5 under the same conditions.

Preparation of carbon-coated GF separator

Carbon nanotubes (CNTs, ≥99.9%, Suzhou Slnero Technology Co., Ltd.), YP-50 activated carbon (≥99.9%, Guangdong Canrd New Energy Technology Co., Ltd.), Super P (≥99.9%, Guangdong Canrd New Energy Technology Co., Ltd.), and carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC, viscosity ≥1900 mPa s, Shenzhen Kejing Star Technology Co., Ltd.) were mixed at a mass ratio of 4:4:1:1 in deionized water to form a homogeneous slurry. The slurry was cast onto one side of a glass fiber separator (Whatman GF/D, 675 μm thickness, 80% porosity, average pore size 2.7 μm; Sigma-Aldrich) using a doctor blade, followed by vacuum drying at 60 °C for 12 h. The electrode was then punched into circular disks with a diameter of 19 mm. The loading of the coated carbon material was approximately 0.5 mg cm−2.

Preparation of the positive electrodes

The KBr positive electrode was prepared by mixing KBr (≥99%, Macklin), YP-50 activated carbon (≥99.9%, Guangdong Canrd New Energy Technology Co., Ltd.), KB (≥99.9%, Suzhou Slnero Technology Co., Ltd.), and carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC, viscosity ≥1900 mPa s, Shenzhen Kejing Star Technology Co., Ltd.) at a weight ratio of 4:4:1:1 in deionized water to form a homogeneous slurry. The slurry was coated onto graphite paper (50 μm thickness, ≥99.9%, Tan Qianlang Electronic Materials Department) using a doctor blade and then vacuum-dried at 60 °C for 12 h. The graphite paper was used as received without any additional surface treatment. The dried electrodes were punched into circular disks (diameter 16 mm) using a precision electrode punch (Shenzhen Kejing Star Technology Co., Ltd.) and directly used for cell assembly. The KI positive electrode was fabricated following the same procedure, replacing KBr with KI ( ≥ 99%, Aladdin). The active material loading was controlled at approximately 1.5 mg cm−2 for both electrodes.

Electrochemical measurements

Coin-type (CR2032) Zn||KBr cells were assembled using a KBr positive electrode, a carbon-coated glass fiber separator (Whatman GF/D, 675 μm thickness, 80% porosity, average pore size 2.7 μm; Sigma-Aldrich, 19 mm diameter), and a zinc metal negative electrode (100 μm thickness, 16 mm diameter), together with electrolyte (150 μL per cell). The coin cell cases and springs were made of stainless steel. Zn||KI cells were fabricated following the same procedure, except that the KBr positive electrode was replaced with a KI electrode. Both the KBr and KI electrodes were prepared in a single-side coated configuration unless otherwise specified. Zn||Cu, Zn||Zn, and Zn||Ti cells were assembled in the same configuration, where the positive electrode was replaced with Cu, Zn, or Ti foil (all 100 μm thickness), respectively.

Zn||KBr pouch cells were assembled in a single-layer configuration consisting of one cathode and one anode without multilayer stacking. The electrodes and separator were cut into 4 × 4 cm pieces for all pouch cell configurations. The mass loading of the active material was adjusted by controlling the coating thickness during electrode fabrication, while the superficial area was kept constant to ensure comparability across different cells. A controlled amount of electrolyte was injected into each pouch cell prior to vacuum degassing to remove trapped gases, followed by hermetic sealing of the pouch package. During electrochemical testing, the pouch cells were mechanically compressed using clips to maintain stable interfacial contact between electrodes. The applied mechanical constraint was kept identical for all measurements; however, the pressure was not quantitatively measured. Zn||KI pouch cells were fabricated following the same procedure, with the KBr positive electrode replaced by a KI positive electrode. Both the KBr and KI electrodes were prepared in a single-side coated configuration unless otherwise specified. The Cu, Ti, and Zn foils were mechanically polished using 1000-grit SiC abrasive paper and were cleaned with ethanol prior to use.

Cyclic voltammetry (CV), linear sweep voltammetry (LSV), and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) were performed using an Autolab electrochemical workstation. CV tests were conducted in the voltage range of 0.8–2.0 or 2.1 V, while LSV was performed from 0 to 3 V (vs Zn2+/Zn) at 5 mV s−1. Characterization

UV−vis spectra were recorded on a Hitachi U4100 UV spectrometer in the wavelength range of 200−600 nm. Contact angle measurements were performed using an FTA200 contact angle goniometer. Raman spectra were collected using a HORIBA XploRA Plus dispersive Raman micro-spectrometer equipped with a 532 nm laser over the range of 100−4000 cm⁻¹. Ex situ Raman measurements were performed on electrolyte samples, while in situ Raman measurements were conducted using a modified CR2032 coin cell with a quartz-window-sealed optical port. FTIR spectra were obtained in reflection mode using a NICOLET iS50 spectrometer. Ex situ FTIR measurements were performed on electrolyte samples. Surface morphologies of Zn deposits were characterized using SEM (GeminiSEM-450, Zeiss). The samples were rinsed with deionized water and ethanol sequentially prior to measurements. XRD patterns were recorded using a Bruker D8 Advance diffractometer operating at 40 kV and 30 mA with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.15405 nm).

XPS measurements were performed using a Thermo ESCALAB 250Xi system equipped with an Al Kα X-ray source. After cell disassembly, the electrodes were immediately transferred to a vacuum oven and dried at 60 °C for 12 h. The samples were then rapidly transferred into an Ar-filled glovebox (O2 < 0.1 ppm, H2O < 0.1 ppm), sealed in brown glass vials, and transported for XPS characterization. The binding energies were calibrated using the C 1 s peak at 284.8 eV. Background subtraction was performed using a Shirley method, and peak deconvolution was carried out using Gaussian–Lorentzian functions. Electrochemical gas chromatography (EC-GC) measurements were conducted using a sealed gas collection system coupled with a gas chromatograph (Agilent 8860 or equivalent). Gas evolution was quantified under galvanostatic conditions. Calibration was performed using standard H₂ gas prior to measurements. In situ pH measurements were conducted using a calibrated micro pH electrode immersed in the electrolyte during electrochemical testing. Calibration was performed using standard buffer solutions prior to measurements. All experiments were conducted at 25 ± 1 °C. GCD data and dQ/dV curves were directly obtained from raw outputs of a LAND battery testing system without any additional smoothing or filtering.

Calculation methods

Thermodynamic simulation

All quantum chemical calculations were performed using the Gaussian 09 software package78. The Becke three-parameter hybrid exchange-correlation functional (B3LYP) combined with the 6–311 + G(d) basis set for C, H, O, N, Cl, and Br atoms79 was employed to locate all stationary points (referred to as the B3LYP/6-311 + G(d) method). Grimme’s D3 dispersion correction was applied to account for van der Waals interactions80. Geometry optimizations were carried out until the residual forces were less than 10−5 hartree/bohr and the energy change was below 10−7 hartree. The self-consistent field (SCF) convergence criterion for energy was set to 10−8 hartree.

MD simulations

All molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were carried out using the Forcite module implemented in Materials Studio (Accelrys Software Inc., USA). The COMPASS force field was adopted to model both intra- and intermolecular interactions. Prior to the production simulations, each system was subjected to a two-step equilibration procedure. An initial 50 ps simulation was performed in the microcanonical (NVE) ensemble, followed by a second 50 ps equilibration in the isothermal–isobaric (NPT) ensemble at 300 K to stabilize the system and obtain an equilibrated simulation cell. The equilibrated models were subsequently simulated in the canonical (NVT) ensemble at 300 K for 1 ns. Structural characteristics of the systems were evaluated from the resulting trajectories by calculating radial distribution functions (RDFs) and solvation coordination numbers.

Binding energy

The calculation was executed in Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP) based on the projector-augmented wave (PAW) method. Exchange-correlation functional of generalized-gradient approximation (GGA) and Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) was used to describe electron interactions. In the calculation, the bottom layer was fixed to simulate the bulk, and top two layers were allowed to relax. A smearing width of 0.05 was chosen, and Monkhorst–Pack mesh was set to be 1 × 1× 1 to sample Brillouin zone. The cutoff energy was 420 eV, with convergence criteria of 10−5 eV and 0.02 eV/Å for electronic and ionic relaxation. DFT-D3 was employed for vdW dispersion correction. The binding energy was calculated as Eb = EA+B − EA − EB, where Eb is the binding energy between molecule A and B, EA+B, EA, EB are the energies of the complex of A and B, molecule A, and B, respectively81.
