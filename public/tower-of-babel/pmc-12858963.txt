New candidate gene mutations in astrocytoma with seizures
Authors: Pongsakorn Choochuen, Natthapon Khongcharoen, Surasak Sangkhathat, Anukoon Kaewborisutsakul, Thara Tunthanathip, Kanitpong Phabphal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSeizures are a common presenting symptom in astrocytomas, yet the underlying genomic drivers remain incompletely understood. We performed whole-exome sequencing on tumor tissue from 40 patients with astrocytoma (25 with preoperative seizures, 15 without) to explore somatic mutation profiles associated with seizure occurrence. Clinical characteristics, including age, tumor location, volume, and IDH mutation status, did not significantly differ between seizure and non-seizure groups. In the overall cohort, nine genes showed nominal associations with seizures, but none remained significant following correction for multiple comparisons. Stratification by IDH status revealed that CELSR1 mutations were significantly enriched in the seizure group among IDH-wildtype astrocytomas/glioblastomas (6/19 [31.6%] vs. 0/10 [0%], p = 0.028, OR = ∞), whereas GSTT4 and other candidate genes lost association in subgroup analyses. CELSR1, a core component of the planar cell polarity pathway, represents a novel candidate gene potentially contributing to tumor-associated epilepsy in IDH-wildtype disease. These findings highlight the importance of molecular subgrouping and warrant validation in larger cohorts and functional studies of CELSR1 in epileptogenesis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-34357-w.Keywords: Astrocytoma, Seizure, GSTT4, CELSR1Subject terms: Epilepsy, Tumour biomarkers, Genetic association study

Introduction

Astrocytoma is one of the most common brain tumors, accounting for approximately 75% of all gliomas1. Seizures may promote tumor growth, malignant transformation, and antiseizure drug resistance1. Patients with glioma who had preoperative epilepsy had significantly longer survival than those without seizures2, and their 5-year survival rate is approximately 70%–90%2. Therefore, it is important to achieve better seizure control and improve patients’ quality of life5. The mechanisms underlying brain tumor-associated epilepsy are complex and remain unclear.

Previous studies have reported that tumor type, grade, location, size and peritumoral edema are associated with seizure2,3. Disturbances at the cellular level, including alterations in synaptic and neuronal functions, connectivity, excitotoxicity, and alterations in the expression of specific genes and proteins related to intracellular communication, are proposed mechanism4. Recent studies have suggested that epileptogenesis in gliomas are more related to tumor molecular gene mutations than to tumor grade location, or peritumoral edema1,3. In the past decade, mutations in more than 10 genes have been associated with glioma associated epilepsy5,6. Advances in next-generation sequencing have facilitated comprehensive tumor genome profiling, providing new insights into the somatic mutations and genomic alterations involved in tumorigenesis. These technologies have also begun to uncover the genetic basis of clinical manifestations such as tumor-associated seizures. For instance, BRAF variants have been implicated in enhancing epileptogenicity in gliomas7, while pathways, such as RAS/MAPK and PI3K/AKT/mTOR, play significant roles in both neuronal excitability and tumor growth8. Additionally, alterations in neurotransmitter systems, particularly glutamate and GABA, contribute to network hyperexcitability and increased seizure activity. Up to 78% of cases with IDH-mutations are associated with episodes of epilepsy. In our cohort, 24% of IDH-wildtype cases presented with seizures. According to the WHO 2021 classification, the presence of IDH mutations (either IDH1 or IDH2) is a primary diagnostic criterion for astrocytomas, essential for tumor classification, prognostication, and treatment planning. IDH refers to isocitrate dehydrogenase, a key metabolic enzyme in the citric acid cycle. Mutations in IDH1 result in a neomorphic enzyme activity that produces the oncometabolite D-2-hydroxyglutarate (D2-HG)4–6. D2-HG has a chemical structure similar to glutamate and can enhance neuronal excitability by acting at the N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor5–7. Thus, D2-HG is considered to enhance neuronal activity by mimicking glutamate activity at the N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor4–6. Furthermore, the aberrant activity of the cystine-glutamate transporter by tumor cells can result in excessive glutamate activity around neurons3,5,8. Despite these advancements, the interplay between tumor biology and seizure activity remains complex and unclear, underscoring the need for further research to develop effective therapeutic strategies. Additionally, limited studies have been conducted using tumor tissue whole-exome sequencing to comprehensively analyze the role of gene mutations in patients with astrocytomas who present with seizures.

This study aimed to investigate the somatic genomic landscape of astrocytomas and its association with seizure occurrence. Analysis of somatic mutations and identification of driver genes in a cohort of patients with astrocytomas presenting with or without seizures provide insight into genetic markers that may contribute to seizure pathophysiology.

Materials and methods

This prospective, observational study was conducted between January 2021 and January 2023. This study included patients with astrocytoma who presented with seizures and underwent craniotomy with tumor removal. Data on patient characteristics, including demographics and neuroimaging findings, were collected. Clinical information was obtained from the patients’ electronic medical records. The radiological features that were evaluated included tumor location and volume. Tumor volume was calculated based on the T2/FLAIR hyperintense areas. Pathological diagnoses were made in accordance with the 2021 World Health Organization guidelines9.

Epileptic seizures were diagnosed by neurologists based on the criteria established by the 2017 International League Against Epilepsy10. Epilepsy onset was defined as seizure episodes occurring before tumor resection, confirmed by clinical history and video-EEG. All patients with preoperative seizures received standard antiepileptic drug therapy before surgery. No antiseizure medication (ASM) was administered to patients in the non-seizure group prior to surgery, including for prophylactic purposes. ASM therapy (levetiracetam or phenytoin) was administered only after documented seizure onset in patients who experienced preoperative seizures. The seizure types were classified as focal aware seizure, focal impaired awareness seizure, and focal to bilateral tonic-clonic seizure according to the ILAE 2017 Classification11.

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethical Committee of the Faculty of Medicine, Prince of Songkla University (EC. 65-168-14-1). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Biological samples and sequencing library preparation

Brain astrocytoma tissue samples were obtained from the Biobank of the Songklanagarind Hospital between 1997 and 2023. Figure 1 shows an overview of the workflow. DNA was extracted from the samples using a DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), and the quantity and quality of the resulting DNA were evaluated using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc., Waltham, MA, USA) and a TapeStation system (Agilent Technologies, Inc., Santa Clara, CA, USA).

Overview of the workflow.

Whole exome sequencing

Whole-exome sequencing was performed to comprehensively analyze somatic variants that affect protein function. The library was prepared using the Agilent SureSelect XT Human All Exon v6 kit (Agilent Technologies, Inc.) following the manufacturer’s protocol and was quantified using a Qubit dsDNA High Sensitivity Assay Kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Fragment size was assessed using the Agilent D1000 ScreenTape assay. Sequencing was conducted on an Illumina NovaSeq-6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, California, USA) with 150 bp paired-end reads, achieving an average target coverage depth of 200×.

The quality of the paired-end sequence files was evaluated and trimmed using fastqp (version 0.3.4)12. Sequences were aligned using the BWA program (version 0.7.17) by mapping the processed FASTQ files to the human reference genome (GRCh38.13)13. The resulting Sequence Alignment Map (SAM) files were converted into a Binary Alignment Map (BAM) format, sorted using SAMtools (version 1.17)14, and subsequently regrouped. Duplicate sequences were marked using Picard (version 3.0.0). Base quality score recalibration was conducted on deduplicated BAM files using the Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK; version 4.4.0) to refine base quality scores. Variant calling was performed using Mutect2 in tumor-only mode, leveraging a publicly available Panel of Normals obtained from the GATK repository (https://storage.googleapis.com/gatk-best-practices/somatic-hg38/1000g_pon.hg38.vcf.gz). We used GATK4 tools, including GetPileupSummaries, CalculateContamination, and FilterMutectCalls, with the default parameter settings to filter the variants identified by Mutect2. The identified variants were annotated using Funcotator, and the annotated mutation data were saved as MAF files. The ‘maftools’ package in R was used to summarize and visualize the mutational data15.

Identification of cancer driver gene for astrocytoma

A driver mutation is a genetic alteration that provides cancer cells with a growth advantage, thereby facilitating cancer initiation and progression16,17. To identify potential driver mutations, we prioritized annotated somatic mutations from the full patient cohort using two web-based machine learning tools available through the Cancer Genome Interpreter (CGI, https://www.cancergenomeinterpreter.org), BoostDM, and OncodriveMUT18. The CGI database also provides information on potential targeted therapies effective against the identified driver mutations. Additionally, we examined chemotherapy data, clinical trial findings, and levels of evidence from the Precision Oncology Knowledge Base (https://www.oncokb.org) and Clinical Interpretation of Variants in Cancer (https://civicdb.org).

Identification of seizure-associated mutations

We analyzed two groups of patients with astrocytoma: those who experienced seizures (n = 25) and those who did not (n = 15). Somatic mutations were identified in both groups, and their mutational landscapes were visualized using ‘maftools.’ To identify the mutation markers associated with seizures, we compared the frequency of somatic mutations in each gene between the two groups using Fisher’s exact test. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All p-values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using RStudio software (R version 4.3.0). Fisher’s exact test was used to assess the association between somatic mutations in specific genes and the occurrence of seizures. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All p-values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method.

Results

A total of 114 patients with astrocytoma were treated at our institution during the study period, of whom 59 (51.8%) had preoperative seizures. We excluded 74 patients from the analysis: 57 did not have a specimen in the biobank, 15 samples were tentatively classified as astrocytomas because the tissue quality was insufficient for a definitive diagnosis, and 2 did not undergo MRI. Therefore, 40 patients were included in this study, of whom 24 experienced seizures and 16 did not. Of these patients, the seizure and nonseizure groups had mean ages (SD) of 28.0 (9.7) and 32.6 (9.1) years, respectively. Of the 25 patients who presented with seizures, histological diagnoses included 19 patients with glioblastoma, IDH-wildtype, 6 patients with astrocytoma, IDH-mutant (2 astrocytoma, IDH-mutant, grade 2; 2 astrocytoma, IDH-mutant, grade 3; 2 astrocytoma, IDH-mutant, grade 4). The characteristics of the seizures were focal awareness in 5 patients, focal impairment awareness, and 12 had focal-to-bilateral tonic-clonic seizures. The temporal and extratemporal locations of patients who presented with seizures were nearly equal.

The clinical characteristics of the patients with astrocytomas are summarized in Table 1. Univariate analysis of the clinical characteristics showed no significant differences in age, sex, Karnofsky Performance Scale score, and tumor volume, tumor, or size between the seizure and nonseizure groups. We examined the intergroup differences in astrocytomas WHO grades according to the 2021 classification and found no significant differences between the two groups.

Somatic mutation profiling of WT and clinical relevance

Analysis of exome sequences from 40 samples revealed 22,891 single-nucleotide variants and 1,801 insertions or deletions. These variants were exclusively exonic, nonsynonymous changes with a high or moderate impact and a minimum coverage of 200×. The top five cancer-related genes were TP53, IDH1, ATRX, NOTCH1, and EGFR, detected in 48%, 28%, 25%, 25%, and 25% of cases in our cohort, respectively. IDH1 mutations were present in 28% of cases, with no significant difference between seizure and non-seizure groups when classified according to the WHO 2021 criteria. Notably, ATRX, IDH1, and TP53 were among the top five mutated genes in brain astrocytoma samples listed in the COSMIC database, with mutation frequencies comparable to those observed in our cohort (Fig. 2)19. In contrast, four of the top 10 mutated genes, including EGFR, PTEN, CIC, and APC, were not among the top 10 frequently mutated genes in astrocytoma according to the COSMIC database. These genes were identified in 22%, 20%, 20%, and 18% of cases, respectively. EGFR mutations are particularly common in glioblastomas, occurring in approximately 50% of all cases20. PTEN is a key tumor suppressor gene whose mutations are frequently observed in various cancer types, including astrocytomas. The loss of PTEN function is commonly associated with high-grade astrocytomas and promotes glioblastoma progression21,22. The CIC gene, which functions as a tumor suppressor, is linked to the activation of the MAPK pathway, a key driver of tumor progression. Although CIC mutations occur in approximately 40% of oligodendrogliomas, they are far less frequent in astrocytomas, with an incidence of only 1.9%23. Meanwhile, APC mutations disrupt the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, which is critical for cell proliferation and differentiation, and can contribute to tumorigenesis in multiple cancers24. APC mutations in astrocytomas have been identified in 14% of rapidly progressing IDH-mutant cases, suggesting a link to chromosomal instability, which may drive poor prognosis and rapid disease progression25. Although EGFR, PTEN, CIC, and APC are not among the most frequently mutated astrocytoma genes in the COSMIC database, previous studies have demonstrated their significant association with astrocytoma tumorigenesis, supporting their relevance to the tumor genomic landscape.

Furthermore, we identified cancer driver mutations in our patients. We identified 22 recurrent driver genes, each of which was detected in at least three cases. The top five most common driver genes were MUC16, TP53, MUC4, HLA-A, and IDH1, with mutation frequencies of 79.2%, 75.0%, 66.7%, 45.8%, and 41.7%, respectively. The roles of MUC16, TP53, MUC4, and IDH1 in tumorigenesis are well established26–30, whereas that of HLA-A remains unclear.

Comparison between IDH-mutant and IDH-wildtype astrocytomas

To evaluate whether somatic mutation profiles differ according to IDH mutation status, we stratified patients into IDH-mutant and IDH-wildtype groups and compared gene-level mutation frequencies. Seventy-nine genes showed nominal p-values < 0.05 in the unadjusted analysis; however, none remained significant after multiple-testing correction (Supplementary Table S1). The overall mutation patterns were similar between the two groups, as visualized in Supplementary Fig. S1A–B. These findings suggest that, within our cohort, IDH mutation status was not associated with distinct genome-wide somatic mutation profiles.

IDH-stratified analysis of seizure-associated mutations

To evaluate whether the association between somatic mutations and seizure occurrence differed by IDH mutation status, we performed subgroup analyses within IDH-mutant (n = 11; 8 with seizures, 3 without) and IDH-wildtype astrocytomas (n = 29; 17 with seizures, 12 without).

In the IDH-mutant subgroup, no genes demonstrated statistically significant differences in mutation frequency between seizure and non-seizure patients after false discovery rate (FDR) correction (Supplementary Table S2). Several genes showed nominal p-values < 0.05 (e.g., POTEH, SEMA4B), but none remained significant following multiple-testing adjustment, consistent with the small sample size and limited statistical power in this subgroup.

Similarly, in the IDH-wildtype subgroup, none of the observed differences in mutation frequency remained significant after FDR adjustment (Supplementary Table S3). Although a number of genes exhibited nominal associations with seizures (e.g., MUC5B, DST, CELSR1), these findings did not withstand correction for multiple comparisons.

Taken together, these results indicate that seizure-associated somatic mutations did not differ significantly when stratified by IDH mutation status. This suggests that the genetic contributors to seizure susceptibility in astrocytoma may be IDH-independent, and supports the need for validation in larger, IDH-balanced cohorts.

Identification of seizure-associated mutations

The association between somatic variants and seizure occurrence was evaluated by comparing the proportions of gene mutations between the seizure and nonseizure groups using Fisher’s exact test. In our cohort, 25 patients experienced seizures, and 15 did not. Seizure events in patients with astrocytoma were significantly associated with variants in nine genes (Fig. 3), with mutations in GSTT4 and CELSR1 being significantly more prevalent in the seizure group. GSTT4 variants were detected in 52% of patients with astrocytoma presenting with seizures compared with 13% in the nonseizure group (p = 0.01). Similarly, CELSR1 variants were present in 52% of patients with seizures but were not detected in the non-seizure group (p = 0.02) (Table 2).

Discussion

We collected clinically relevant information and performed whole exome sequencing of patients with astrocytoma who presented with and without seizures to identify diagnostically relevant genes associated with astrocytoma and seizures before presentation. Age, sex, astrocytoma grade, Karnofsky Performance Scale score, tumor volume, and tumor location were not associated with seizures. Results from whole exome sequencing showed that only mutations in GSTT4 and CELSR1 were statistically significant in patients with astrocytoma who presented with seizures.

In our cohort, mutations in IDH1, ATRX, and TP53 were identified, consistent with previous genomic reports in astrocytomas31. IDH1/2 mutations are reported in approximately 70%–80% of low-grade gliomas32,33 and are associated with better prognosis compared to high-grade gliomas34. In our study, IDH1 mutations were present in 28% of cases, with no significant difference between seizure and non-seizure groups. This is lower than in some previous studies35, possibly reflecting differences in patient population or tumor grade distribution. Prior studies suggest that altered glutamate concentration and transporter expression may contribute to seizure pathogenesis in IDH-mutant gliomas1, but this remains speculative and requires further experimental validation. Furthermore, our statistical comparison of somatic mutation profiles revealed no significant differences in other mutated genes between IDH-mutant and IDH-wildtype seizure cases, a finding that warrants confirmation in a larger study. In addition, ATRX and TP53 mutations are also frequent in astrocytomas and have been linked to tumorigenesis36–39. ATRX mutations, often co-occurring with TP53 mutations but mutually exclusive with 1p/19q codeletion40–42, are common in IDH-mutant astrocytomas. However, in our cohort, ATRX and TP53 alterations did not significantly differ between patients with and without seizures, and their direct effects on neuronal excitability remain unclear43,44.

Mechanistically, GSTT4 encodes a glutathione S-transferase involved in detoxification and the regulation of oxidative stress. Oxidative stress and impaired detoxification capacity have been implicated in neuronal hyperexcitability through the disruption of redox-sensitive ion channels and neurotransmitter systems, particularly glutamate and GABA pathways. Variants in GSTT4 may therefore contribute to an imbalance between excitatory and inhibitory signaling, predisposing to seizure activity45,46. CELSR1 encodes a member of the cadherin superfamily that regulates planar cell polarity, axonal guidance, and synaptic connectivity during neural development. Alterations in CELSR1 may disrupt the organization and connectivity of neuronal networks, leading to aberrant synaptic signaling and increased epileptogenic potential47,48. These proposed mechanisms are consistent with prior studies linking oxidative stress and abnormal neuronal connectivity to glioma-associated epilepsy. Further studies are also needed to examine survival outcomes associated with genetic mutations.

Despite the findings, this study had some limitations. A major limitation of our study is the absence of experimental validation to directly confirm the functional impact of the identified GSTT4 and CELSR1 variants on neuronal excitability and seizure generation. Future research involving in vitro and in vivo models will be necessary to validate these proposed mechanisms. Importantly, this study had a relatively small sample size and a lack of randomization to select a nonseizure group for the paired study. Larger prospective multicenter studies including all grades of astrocytomas, with and without seizures, are needed. Additionally, postoperative seizure recurrence and detailed AED exposure were not assessed in this study and should be addressed in future research. Furthermore, the statistical power of the IDH-stratified analyses was limited, particularly within the IDH-mutant subgroup (n = 11). The absence of significant seizure-associated mutations in this subgroup should therefore be interpreted with caution, as the small sample size may have reduced the ability to detect true biological differences. Larger, IDH-balanced cohorts will be essential to validate whether seizure-associated genetic patterns differ meaningfully between IDH-mutant and IDH-wildtype astrocytomas.

Conclusion

In conclusion, although several genes showed nominal associations with preoperative seizures in the overall cohort, subgroup analysis by IDH status revealed that CELSR1 emerged as the most promising candidate gene associated with preoperative seizures, particularly in IDH-wildtype astrocytomas (glioblastoma), whereas the association with GSTT4 was no longer observed in either subgroup, suggesting it may have been confounded by the mixed cohort. Larger cohorts and functional studies are now warranted to validate the role of CELSR1 in tumor-associated epilepsy and to explore its potential as a therapeutic target.
