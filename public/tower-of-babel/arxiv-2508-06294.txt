Secure Hybrid Key Growing via Coherence Witnessing and Bipartite Encoding
Pol Julià Farré ∗ , Chris Aaron Schneider & Christian Deppe

Abstract
We propose a novel Hybrid Key Growing (HKG) protocol based on quantum principles and a classical physical-layer assumption. We simultaneously exploit the quantum photon-number and photon-time-bin Degrees of Freedom (DoFs), effectively doubling the bit-per-pulse rate compared to conventional Quantum Key Growing (QKG) schemes. Our protocol integrates entity authentication, and is designed for practical implementation by avoiding reliance on single-photon sources or detectors. By incorporating prior knowledge about the quantum channel, the scheme actively mitigates noise effects, making it suitable for real-world conditions. Under certain assumptions on experimental efficiencies, our approach also promises an increased key generation rate in bits per second. Our simulation results display, first, expected outcomes to gain assurance about the correctness of our implementation and, second, relevant dependencies that showcase desirable properties of our scheme in regimes of low photon loss and dephasing. In particular, within such regimes, our encoding scheme reduces the Quantum Bit Error Rate (QBER) while preserving the ability to detect eavesdropping and identity-forgery attempts.

Our contributions

We introduce a novel method for Hybrid Key Growing (HKG) by combining quantum principles and one classical assumption on the physical layer—namely, that eavesdropping the quantum channel involved introduces a signal delay. Our protocol incorporates the main strength of one of the most practically robust variants of QKG to date: decoy-state QKG
[
10
,
31
,
32
]
. Like the decoy-state approach, our protocol does not rely on ideal single-photon sources or detectors, thereby improving resilience to moderate photon loss and enhancing practical security. A central advantage of our method is that it eliminates the basis reconciliation (key-sifting) step found in all conventional QKG protocols, effectively doubling the bit-per-pulse key generation rate.
Existing schemes that also utilize multiple photonic Degrees of Freedom (DoFs)
[
33
]
can achieve similar benefits, but typically incur a higher classical communication overhead. Our protocol, by contrast, adds complexity primarily at the transmitter and receiver hardware level, leaving its impact on the overall bit-per-second rate uncertain. Nevertheless, if this complexity does not significantly hinder throughput, the resulting gains could be highly valuable in latency-sensitive applications such as Digital Twin (DT) systems
[

35

Our HKG protocol introduces a novel approach to handling quantum noise by leveraging full knowledge of the underlying two-qubit quantum channel—a reasonable assumption in many practical scenarios

37

L

III
A new HKG protocol

In this section, we describe, in five phases, a Hybrid Key Growing (HKG) protocol between two parties: Alice and Bob. The first run of the protocol is exactly prescribed within the five shown phases, while further rounds require slight modifications that are detailed in the 5th phase.

1)  Quantum-bipartite key encoding

In the first phase, Alice, the sender, samples a bitstring
\mathbf{K}
of length
\beta=\nu\omega
, with
\omega,

\nu\in\mathbb{N}
and with
\nu>\omega
. Subsequently, she encodes each bit of
\mathbf{K}
as indicated by another bitstring
\mathbf{F}
, of the same length and preshared with Bob:

If a certain bit of
\mathbf{F}
is a ”
0
” Alice encodes the corresponding bit
b_{0}
of
\mathbf{K}
as

\ket{ b_0}_{N}\otimes\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{0}_{\mathrm{\mathrm{time}\text{-}\mathrm{bin}}}+(-1)^{\xi}\ket{1}_{\mathrm{\mathrm{time}\text{-}\mathrm{bin}}}),

(11)

where kets with the subscript

indicate qubit states belonging to the Hilbert space spanned by two distinct and orthogonal pulse time bins, and
\xi
is a uniformly random bit Alice needs to sample at each preparation. Instead, if a certain bit of
\mathbf{F}
is a ”
1
”, Alice encodes the corresponding bit
b_{1}
of
\mathbf{K}
as

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{0}_{N}+(-1)^{\xi}\mid 1\rangle_{N})\otimes\ket{b_1}_{\mathrm{\mathrm{time}\text{-}\mathrm{bin}}}.

(12)

2)  Decoding and coherence-witness estimation

Bob receives the sent pulses, and performs different operations on them, depending on their type. He measures those prepared as in Eq. (
11
) in the Fock basis to extract, for each of them, a secret bit
\hat{b}_{0}
. Given low quantum noise, the bit
\hat{b}_{0}
likely coincides with the bit
b_{0}
encoded by Alice. Moreover, the time-bin Degree of Freedom (DoF) of such incoming pulses is coupled with another reference system prepared as

\ket{\phi}\equiv\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{0}_{\mathrm{\mathrm{time}\text{-}\mathrm{bin}}}+\ket{1}_{\mathrm{time}\text{-}\mathrm{bin}})

(13)

and an ancillary qubit used to perform the swap test between the received pulse and
\ket{\phi}
. These swap tests are later used to average the coherence witness

W_{\mathrm{time\text{-}bin}}\equiv W_{\ket{\phi}\bra{\phi}}

(14)

over certain input states. On the other hand, Bob measures the time-bin of the incoming pulses prepared as in Eq. (
12
) to obtain, for each pulse, an estimated bit
\hat{b}_{1}
. Being
L
a natural parameter chosen after channel calibration (see Section
V-A

\ket{\psi}(L)\equiv\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{L + N}+\ket{L})

(15)

and an ancillary qubit used to perform the swap test between the state of the received pulse and
\ket{\psi}(L)

W_{\mathrm{Fock}}(L)\equiv W_{\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi}(L)}

(16)

over certain input states.

3)  Authentication decisions and eavesdropping check

Alice and Bob publicly compare a small random fraction
\kappa
, discarded for later use, of the obtained raw key. A Quantum Bit Error Rate (QBER) smaller than a threshold
\mu<\frac{1}{4}
, defined during channel calibration, leads Alice to reckon the receiver as legitimate, i.e., as Bob. Otherwise, the protocol is aborted.

Under no protocol abortion, Alice publicly shares the choice of
\xi
made in each pulse preparation. Bob estimates, only selecting those pulses sent with
\xi=0
, the mean value of the two coherence witnesses presented, defined as

\langle W_{\mathrm{time\text{-}bin}}\rangle_{\mathrm{selection_{1}}}\hskip 5.69046pt\mathrm{and}\hskip 5.69046pt\langle W_{\mathrm{Fock}}(L)\rangle_{\mathrm{selection_{2}}},

(17)

and computes the average
\hat{W}(L)
between them. An obtained value below a threshold
-\frac{1}{2}<\tau<0
, defined during channel calibration, ensures both no eavesdropping and sender’s legitimacy. Otherwise, the protocol is aborted.

4)  Key distillation

Under no protocol abortion, the two parties proceed with the information reconciliation
[
46
]
and privacy amplification
[
47
]
schemes, yielding a sifted, yet correct and secret, shared key.

Remark 3
.

Our protocol does not require the commonly used bases-reconciliation step, thus reducing the classical communication overhead and approximately doubling, due to no bases mismatching, the bit-per-pulse key rate with respect to conventional QKG schemes.

5)

\mathbf{F}

IV
Security analysis

Correctness
[
48
]
of the final shared key trivially follows from positive authentication decisions together with a negative eavesdropping check. We focus on entity-authentication and key-secrecy constraints, relying, for the former, on the completeness-and-soundness framework
[
49
]
.

IV-A

Authentication completeness

With regard to completeness, Bob is trivially accepted given no malicious parties and no quantum noise. In such case, and setting
L=N
, Alice is also accepted, because Bob performs the swap test against a state that flags each coherence witness in all instances. Namely, idealistically
[
50
,
51
]
,

\langle W_{\mathrm{time\text{-}bin}}\rangle_{\mathrm{selection_{1}}}=-\frac{1}{2}\hskip 5.69046pt\mathrm{and}

(18)

\small\langle W_{\mathrm{Fock}}(N)\rangle_{\mathrm{selection_{2}}}=-\frac{1}{2}.

(19)

Moreover, given the continuity of both the expected QBER and
\hat{W}
with respect to state fidelity, an adequate choice of
\mu
and
\tau
ensures completeness in setups with low-noise conditions.

IV-B

Authentication soundness

With regard to soundness, we divide our analysis into two parts, depending on the party that is aimed to be forged.

IV-B
1
Resistance against Bob-impersonation attacks

Firstly, let us consider an adversary not knowing
\mathbf{F}
, and performing a straightforward man-in-the-middle attack
[
52
]
. For each incoming pulse, the probability of measuring the right DoF is
\frac{1}{2}
, thus leading to an expected QBER lower bounded by
\frac{1}{4}>\mu
. Secondly, notice that only eavesdropping the used quantum channel provides no information about
\mathbf{F}
. However, this combined with the disclosure of
\xi
-choices and the information reconciliation scheme–both of which are public by design–may compromise the secrecy of
\mathbf{F}
. Desirably, the 5th phase of our scheme ensures that, even with full knowledge of
\mathbf{F}
, the probability of correctly forging Bob is roughly lower than
\frac{1}{2^{\omega}}
. That is, an attacker knowing
\mathbf{F}
, prior to its update, and aiming to impersonate Bob, measures with a high probability
p>1-\frac{1}{2^{\omega}}
the wrong DoF for, at least, one complete subset of
\nu
pulses. Hence, the expected QBER for the corresponding segment is
\frac{1}{2}>\mu
.

IV-B
2
Resistance against Alice-impersonation attacks

Two conditions are needed to impersonate Alice: after sampling
\beta
bits, encoding each of them in a light pulse and sending them to Bob, both the QBER and
\hat{W}
obtained must be low enough. This renders the attacker’s encoding choices nontrivial. If only one condition was to be fulfilled, an adversary could double-encode a bitstring (to avoid a high QBER) or double-encode superposition states (to avoid a high value of
\hat{W}
). We again first consider an adversary with no knowledge of
\mathbf{F}
. Each of the adversary’s encoding guesses is successful with a probability
\frac{1}{2}
. Therefore, on average, half of the times in which Bob would perform the swap test against a state flagging a coherence witness, he does so with non-superposition states, thus substantially increasing
\hat{W}
. Moreover, such attack would induce an expected QBER of
\frac{1}{4}
. Finally, an attacker knowing
\mathbf{F}
, and making a guess on the key update, encodes with high probability
p>1-\frac{1}{2^{\omega}}
all
\nu
bits of at least one of the
\omega
underlying subsets in the wrong DoF. In such case, the expected QBER for at least one of the bit-set compared rises to
\frac{1}{2}
. Moreover, the value of
\hat{W}
would vanish in those sets of bits corresponding to a wrong
F
-segment update.

IV-C

Key secrecy

As stated above,
\mathbf{F}
may be leaked to an eavesdropper harnessing public discussions and measurements on the sent stream of light pulses. This can compromise key secrecy against the well-known intercept-resend
[
53
]
attack. However, we make an assumption on the physical layer: an intercept-resend attack would introduce substantial time delays on the light pulses, thereby perturbing their time-bin DoF and thus increasing both the QBER and the value of
\hat{W}
. This assumption, which does not rely on the laws of quantum mechanics, but rather on the specifics of the channel engineering, is what renders our protocol hybrid.

VI
Conclusions and further research

Quantum Key Growing (QKG) is of critical importance in the current landscape of quantum communications, both due to its theoretical and experimental progress and its essential role in countering emerging quantum threats
[
54
,
55
]
. In this work, we have proposed a novel Hybrid Key Growing (HKG) protocol based on bipartite encoding, coherence witnessing, and a classical assumption on the physical layer. As shown in Sections
IV
and
V-B
, the protocol is compatible with real-world implementation under moderate noise conditions.

Although mature solutions such as decoy-state QKG remain highly effective and widely adopted, our approach explores alternative strategies with distinct operational principles. We believe that such diversity is vital to advancing in the field. Future research will examine whether the adversarial model used in our analysis poses challenges to existing protocols and whether our method improves scalability in networked settings, e.g., in Hybrid Conference Key Growing (HCKG).
