Learning Modular Exponentiation with Transformers
David Demitri Africa Sara M. Kapoor Theo Simon Sorg

Abstract
Modular exponentiation is crucial to number theory and cryptography, yet remains largely unexplored from a mechanistic interpretability standpoint. We train a 4-layer encoder-decoder Transformer model to perform this operation and investigate the emergence of numerical reasoning during training. Utilizing principled sampling strategies, PCA-based embedding analysis, and activation patching, we examine how number-theoretic properties are encoded within the model. We find that reciprocal operand training leads to strong performance gains, with sudden generalization across related moduli. These synchronized accuracy surges reflect grokking-like dynamics, suggesting the model internalizes shared arithmetic structure. We also find a subgraph consisting entirely of attention heads in the final layer sufficient to achieve full performance on the task of regular exponentiation. These results suggest that transformer models learn modular arithmetic through specialized computational circuits, paving the way for more interpretable and efficient neural approaches to modular exponentiation.



1
Introduction

Modular exponentiation is fundamental in numerous areas of mathematics and computer science, notably in cryptographic algorithms such as RSA
[
Rivest et al. 1978
]
and Diffie-Hellman key exchange
[
Diffie and Hellman 1976
]
. Despite its mathematical simplicity, modular exponentiation presents significant challenges for machine learning models, given the non-linear and cyclic nature of the operation and the complexity of generalizing it effectively. Prior work by
Charton 2024
explored how transformers can learn arithmetic functions such as the greatest common divisor (GCD), demonstrating that transformers implicitly uncover algorithmic structures in arithmetic tasks, laying important groundwork in understanding numerical reasoning in neural networks.

However, modular exponentiation introduces new layers of complexity, notably the interaction between exponentiation and modulo reduction, which can lead to nontrivial number-theoretic patterns not present in simpler tasks such as computing the greatest common divisor. To the best of our knowledge, transformer-based models have yet to be systematically investigated in the context of modular exponentiation, particularly from a mechanistic interpretability perspective. Understanding how these models represent and manipulate numerical information internally when performing modular arithmetic is crucial, both from the viewpoint of theoretical curiosity and practical applicability, as modular exponentiation serves as a foundational primitive in secure computation.

In this work, we extend the research initiated by Charton by training transformer models to perform modular exponentiation. We design novel sampling methods to capture the statistical characteristics of modular arithmetic and analyze how these sampling strategies influence model learning dynamics and generalization. Furthermore, we use mechanistic interpretability methods to provide insight into how models represent and manipulate number-theoretic information internally. We also observe the learning dynamics of the model, as it trains on the task, finding evidence of grokking as the model learns individual multiples.

With this work, we hope not only to advance the understanding of transformer arithmetic capabilities but also to open new avenues for explainable neural computation in number theory and cryptographic reasoning.

2
Related Work

2.1
Mechanistic Interpretability and Circuits

Mechanistic interpretability seeks to uncover the computational strategies and representational structures internalized by neural networks, aiming at transparency at the level of individual components such as neurons, attention heads, and weight matrices. The early foundational work by
Olah et al. 2020
introduced the concept of
circuits
, coherent subgraphs within neural networks that correspond to meaningful algorithmic operations. Subsequent studies by
Elhage et al. 2021
and
Wang et al. 2022
expanded this framework, dissecting the internal structures of transformer models and providing interpretive insights into attention mechanisms, feed-forward layers, and embedding spaces. A comprehensive practical review of these advances in mechanistic interpretability, specifically tailored for transformer-based language models, is provided by
Rai et al. 2024
.

2.2
Modular Exponentiation

Modular exponentiation, despite its mathematical simplicity, plays a crucial role in cryptography, number theory, and computational complexity
[
Rivest et al. 1978
,
Diffie and Hellman 1976
]
. Classical algorithms such as fast exponentiation use the cyclic nature of modulo arithmetic to efficiently compute large exponentials modulo a number. Machine learning approaches to modular arithmetic have been explored, but remain limited to simpler operations such as modular addition and multiplication
[
Gromov 2023
,
Saxena et al. 2024
]
. Recent studies such as the work of
Gromov 2023
demonstrate the ability of neural networks to learn modular addition through
grokking
, a sudden generalization phenomenon. Our work significantly extends this line of research by systematically investigating modular exponentiation, an inherently more complex and non-linear task, from a deep learning perspective.

2.3
Transformers and Mathematics

Mechanistic interpretability has recently been applied specifically to arithmetic reasoning within transformer architectures. For instance,
Quirke and Barez 2024
examined transformers performing addition tasks, discovering dedicated attention heads whose functions mirror human-engineered arithmetic algorithms. Similarly,
Stolfo et al. 2023
used causal mediation analysis to rigorously demonstrate interpretable arithmetic pathways within transformer architectures, revealing explicit algorithmic structures encoded in model activations. Extending this line of research,
Sharkey et al. 2022
used sparse autoencoders to disentangle complex arithmetic features hidden within transformer activations, improving the clarity and interpretability of arithmetic computations in neural models.

A notable phenomenon driving recent mechanistic interpretability studies in arithmetic contexts is grokking — characterized by delayed and sudden generalization from memorization to genuine algorithmic understanding. Initially documented by
Power et al. 2022
in the context of modular arithmetic tasks, grokking has provided valuable insights into how neural networks transition abruptly from simple memorization strategies to meaningful generalization. Subsequent analysis, such as that of
Nanda et al. 2023
, introduced internal “progress measures” to identify specific circuits responsible for this emergent arithmetic capability. More recently,
Doshi et al. 2024
studied grokking specifically within modular polynomial arithmetic, systematically uncovering specialized internal representations that transformers leverage during modular operations.

Our work builds directly upon these foundations by extending explainability techniques — including learning dynamics analysis, activation patching, and PCA on embedding representations — to the previously unexplored domain of modular exponentiation.

Training Approach

3.1
Sample generation

Following
Charton 2024
, we experiment with various methods of sampling integers, in our case for modular exponentiation. Unlike GCD, where two integers
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}
and their respective
\mathrm{gcd}(a,b)
are sampled, modular exponentiation requires three operands. The below description focuses on sampling strategies for integers
a,b,c\in\mathbb{Z}
and their outcome
d\in\mathbb{Z}
such that

a^{b}\equiv d\mod c

(1)

We try to sample
a,b,c
, and sometimes
d
, such that the tuples
(a,b,c,d)
follow a certain underlying distribution, with the maximum integer to sample set to
M=10^{6}
. Similarly to
Charton 2024
, we find that the different distributions lead to varying learning dynamics and absolute accuracies. We observe a clear correlation for the distribution of samples and the accuracy achieved on these numbers, with greater imbalance leading to lower overall accuracy. We detail the sampling generation strategies below.

Uniform operands

The simplest strategy involves uniformly at random sampling
a,b,c
and computing
d
. This initial approach samples
c\in[1,100]
and
a,b\in[0,M]
independently and uniformly at random.
d
is then computed according to
1
. This results in
d\in[0,99]
, which is heavily skewed towards small values, as can be seen in the histogram of the generated samples
Figure 1
.
The decoder might never learn to predict larger outcomes due to the large class imbalance, which we verify in our experiments.

d
values lie in
[0,40)
.

Uniform outcomes

To mitigate this high probability mass for small outcomes, we experiment with sampling the outcome
d
uniformly at random. For a given
d
, we proceed to rejection sample
(a,b,c)
independently and uniformly at random. This yields a uniform distribution on all possible triples
(a,b,c)
such that
1
holds. Note that
c>d
, so
c\in(d,100]
, reducing the search space. The overall distribution obtained is uniform for
d
and
(a,b,c)
uniform conditioned on
1
.

Reciprocal Operands

As in
Charton 2024
, we experiment with sampling operands according to a reciprocal distribution defined on
[0,M]
. Firstly, we start by sampling
\ln(a),\ln(b)
according to
\mathcal{U}(1,ln(M+2))
and computing
a=e^{\ln(a)},b=e^{\ln(b)}
, resulting in log-uniform values on
[1,M+2)
. The values are floored to obtain integers in
[1,M+1]
. Note that with this procedure,
a
and
b
are enforced to be strictly greater than 0, a constraint that is not enforced with uniform operands.
The reason for this lies in the reciprocal distribution that requires the lower bound to be greater than 0. Since
a,b=0
is a well-defined modular exponentiation, these values should be included in our sampling. We address this by shifting the reciprocal distribution by 1. We sample
a^{\prime},b^{\prime}
log-uniformly and set
a=a^{\prime}-1,b=b^{\prime}-1
. A histogram for reciprocal operands with uniform outcomes is displayed in
Figure 2
. The resulting individual distributions are uniform
d
, roughly log-uniform
a,b
and
c
skewed towards larger values, as expected due to the constraint
c>d
.

Figure 2
:
Histogram for generated samples in the uniform outcomes, reciprocal operands setting. Note that
a,b
are given on a log-uniform scale, while the bins for
c,d
are spaced uniformly.

The resulting discrete probability distribution
\mathcal{P}
that we get for sampling for sampling an integer
n
is given as

\mathcal{P}(n,0,M)=\begin{cases}0&n<0\mathrm{~or~}n\geq+1\\
\frac{\ln(n+2)-\ln(n+1)}{\ln(M+2)}&0\leq n\leq M\end{cases}

d
are performed both for uniform and reciprocal operands, resulting in four different combinations tested. Note that uniform outcomes can be combined with reciprocal operands by rejection sampling
c
uniformly and
a,b

3.2
Model training

For comparability with
Charton 2024
, we train four-layer encoder-decoder transformers with an embedding dimension of 256. Multi-head attention with eight attention heads is used. The batch size is set to 256 and a static learning rate of
10^{-4}
is given to the Adam optimizer
[
Kingma and Ba 2014
]
.
Each epoch consists of
300,000
generated samples, using seed 42.

Integer representations

Since transformers operate on discrete tokens, and the range of integers up to
M
is too large to use as a vocabulary for the small transformers trained here, a different representation of integers is needed. Again, this work follows
Charton 2024
in using the following string template, adapted to modular exponentiation

\mathrm{V3~}+a_{1}\ldots a_{n}+b_{1}\ldots b_{n}+c_{1}\ldots c_{n}+d_{1}\ldots d_{n}

where
a_{1},a_{2},\ldots
are the individual digits of the number
a
for a given base
B
. This setting requires
B+2
tokens, one for the
+
sign and an additional one for
\mathrm{V3}
.
For example, the equation

750178^{996884}\equiv 1\mod 95

is represented in base 1000 as

\mathrm{V3}+750~178~+996~884~+95~+1.

Similarly, for base 1013 the resulting string would be

\mathrm{V3}+740~558~+984~92~+95~+1.

This string is constructed using the samples generated during training.

Sampling

Subsection
3.1
proposed four different sample generation methods. All four methods were tested for 2500 epochs with base 1000. Test and validation sets are also generated. Contrary to most works, test accuracy is also measured during training. This is possible as the samples are generated rather than stemming from an existing finite set. Specifically, we generate the two sets

validation

using uniform operands and computed outcomes.

test

using uniform operands and uniform outcomes.

In this way, we can test the overall model performance for different underlying distributions simultaneously. We report accuracy by comparing the decoder output with the correct value
d
.

Bases

Since the choice of the base influences the string representation for the transformer, presenting a starting bias, various bases are tested to see whether the choice of the base impacts overall performance. Specifically, four bases are tested on the uniform outcome reciprocal operand setting of Subsection
3.1
, as the model performance is strongest on this setting. The four bases are:

•

1000, as the final result used by
Charton 2024
.

•

1013, as a prime number close to 1000, ideally isolating the effect of a prime number without skewing the overall results.

•

1279, as the nearest Mersenne prime number.

•

999, since the prime factorisation
999=3^{3}\cdot 37
has four prime factors as opposed to the 6 by 1000, but both numbers have only 2 distinct prime factors.

4.2
Deterministic predictions

Motivated by the observation of
Charton 2024
that models predict the same incorrect number deterministically, we compare predictions and targets for various model checkpoints. The model trained on reciprocal operands, checkpointed upon reaching validation accuracy 30%, reliably predicts the number 19 instead of the correct 91. For all 10012 generated samples with target 91, the model predicted 19. These samples contain duplicates — the 10012 samples are made up of 512 distinct ones.
Upon isolating this effect by rejection sampling
a,b,c
with fixed
d=91
and duplicate elimination, and scaling up the overall sample count, prediction 1 becomes the most common for target 91. The 19 is still present for common inputs, while the 1 is used for infrequent
a,b,c
. We hypothesize that the 1 might serve as a fall-back mechanism for our model, especially relevant for unseen and rare data.

4.3
Learning Dynamics Analysis

During training, we observe a significant surge in performance between epochs 1725 and 1750, as seen in
Figure 5
. When decomposed number-wise (by modulus), we observe individual increases from 20% to 100% accuracy for the moduli 92, 69, 46, 23 — the multiples of 23 in the range of the parameter
c\in[1,100]
. The overall increase in accuracy can therefore be explained by the simultaneous stepwise increases in accuracy for certain moduli. A similar effect is observed with the multiples of 31, 39, and 47. Some further visualizations of this phenomenon can be found in appendix
A.3
.

Figure 5
:
Sudden increase in performance for all four multiples of 23 in the allowed range of the modulus. The increase starts at epoch 1725 and stops once the moduli have reached 100% accuracy at epoch 1750.

A few small moduli, namely 1, 2, 4, 10, 12, 14, 15, and 18, are learned within the first 100 epochs to an accuracy of 100%. The rest exhibits a period of low accuracy, before increasing rapidly for a few epochs and then leveling off near perfect accuracy. This leads to an overall stepwise increase in accuracy, whenever a new modulus is solved. This is similar to the learning dynamics described by
Charton 2024
on the GCD task.
The surges are not observed for the other operands
a,b
or the outcome
d
. This numberwise grokking, especially for multiples at the same time, suggests that the model learns a mathematically meaningful function. Since multiples increase at the same time, we hypothesize that the numerical representations must capture this effect. Therefore, we explore whether the underlying representations encapsulate relations between integers.

We analyzed embeddings initially by numeric value. Prior to grokking, the model forms 6 spatially distinct clusters with no clear linear mapping of token value. After grokking, embeddings become more centered and compressed, especially for lower numbers.

We applied PCA on numeric token embeddings colored by their lowest prime factor, drawing inspiration from the analysis by

Visualizing numeric token embeddings by parity is insightful because even numbers inherently share the lowest prime factor of 2, creating a natural partition in modular arithmetic. Before grokking, our PCA analysis shows little clear separation between even and odd numbers, with post-grokking tokens appearing more centralized.

We visualized the PCA embeddings by prime numbers versus composites and did not observe any clearly distinct clusters before grokking and one larger cluster after grokking.

We colored each numeric token by its total number of divisors to capture a more fine-grained view of compositeness. Before grokking, tokens with low divisor counts are weakly distinguishable, but after grokking they seem to compress towards a central region.

We visualized multiplicative order a modulo k (the smallest positive integer k such that

We used Euler’s totient function
\phi(n)

Primitive Root

In modular exponentiation, a primitive root modulo a prime p is a number whose successive powers generate all non-zero residues mod p. We label tokens by whether they are primitive roots (1) or not (0) for a fixed prime (11) and apply PCA to see if those “generators” form distinct embedding patterns. Before grokking, embeddings appear loosely scattered, while after grokking they seem to converge inwards.

Residue

Finally, we color tokens by their residue class modulo a small integer (i.e., 5) to check if the embedding space captures simple mod-N structure. While residue appears somewhat mixed across pre-grokking clusters, residues appear more centralized post grokking.

4.5
Activation Patching

Activation patching is a mechanistic interpretability method that overwrites activation values in some component of the model
[
Heimersheim and Nanda 2024
]
. This is done to isolate which parts of the model have a causal effect when removed. We conduct two patching experiments to identify minimal circuits within our trained model, replacing activations of a given input with the activations from a counterfactual prompt. We follow the methodology of
Conmy et al. 2023
to select our circuit. For each decoder layer, we run the counterfactual prompt through the network and capture the self-attention context (the input to the output projection) using forward hooks. For each attention head, we run the full input through the network, while replacing the head’s activations with the corresponding counterfactual context. We then compute the KL divergence between the logits of the patched and unpatched network outputs. Heads for which the KL divergence exceeds a fixed threshold (
\tau
) are considered critical and are included in the final circuit. When evaluating the circuit’s effect, we simultaneously patch all the selected heads.

Regular Exponentiation

The most natural capability that one would expect from a model trained on modular exponentiation is the ability to perform regular exponentiation. In our experiments, when the model is prompted with inputs for which
c>a^{b}
he target output is simply
a^{b}
(i.e. no modulo reduction occurs). We guarantee nontrivial modular exponentiation by selecting
c
smaller than
a^{b}
. We generate 100 such prompt-counterfactual pairs.

Figure 7
:
KL divergence heatmap and of attention heads across generated samples (left) and comparison of accuracy between full and patched model (right).

After circuit search, we find a circuit composed of all heads in the latest layer with the same accuracy as the full model. We vary
\tau\in(0.01,0.05,0.1,0.5,1,2)
, finding that many early layers’ heads are not selected — suggesting that their activations have little causal impact on the final computation.

The final circuit is able to achieve high accuracy on the task, suggesting that the model meaningfully learns an internal representation of a task that is not modular exponentiation. In addition, the circuit is a substantially smaller fraction of the original model, which confirms our hypothesis that exponentiation mainly relies on the latest decoder layers. These findings align with the intuition that higher-layer heads encode abstract, task-specific transformations necessary for exponentiation, whereas lower-layer heads capture more generic or redundant information.

5
Discussion

Our study extends prior work on arithmetic learning in transformers by exploring modular exponentiation. We observe that transformer models trained with reciprocal operand sampling can achieve high accuracy on modular exponentiation tasks, echoing the findings of
Charton 2024
that distributional choices significantly impact both learning speed and generalization.

As in Charton’s paper, we find that uniform sampling of operands leads to poor coverage of the output space, especially when computing outcomes directly. In our case, this manifests itself as a strong bias towards small values for d in the computed outcome setting, making it difficult for the model to generalize to rarer and larger outputs. Reciprocal sampling balances this skew, enabling the model to achieve over 80% test accuracy. The introduction of uniform outcomes further reduces bias but, similar to Charton’s observation on uniform GCD outcomes, it is not strictly necessary for strong performance.

The learning dynamics reveal notable, moduli-specific grokking behavior. Accuracy surges coincide with the model solving sets of related moduli, such as multiples of 23, supporting the intuition that the transformer incrementally learns interpretable mathematical functions. This stepwise improvement mirrors the sieve-like learning in Charton’s GCD models, where powers of small primes were learned sequentially.

PCA of the embedding space reveals some structure change post-grokking, though not always in an easily interpretable or consistent form. While we observe a general centralization of embeddings after the model learns, clear clustering by number-theoretic properties such as parity, primality, or multiplicative order is limited. In particular, patterns in lowest prime factor appear only weakly or not at all before grokking, and although some features seem to become more centralized afterward, it is unclear whether this reflects meaningful internal organization or simply compression.

Separately, we observe deterministic mispredictions during training, such as consistent errors on
d=91
, which the model frequently maps to 19 or 1. These behaviors mirror the kinds of fallback mechanisms noted in Charton’s GCD models, where unseen or infrequent inputs were assigned to a small fixed set of high-frequency outputs. This suggests that the model relies on memorized shortcuts in the absence of generalizable patterns.

Finally, activation patching shows that the model’s internals can be decomposed into subgraphs that are selectively responsible for key computational steps in modular exponentiation. We find that one of these computational steps, the regular exponentiation, can be implemented using a circuit composed entirely of attention heads in the final layer, rendering earlier layers almost superfluous. This functional specialization suggests that the transformer learns to compartmentalize arithmetic operations into distinct circuits and that full capability is possible on certain tasks with only a fraction (one-fourth) of the attention heads being used.

6
Conclusion

In this work, we demonstrate that transformer models, when trained with carefully designed sampling strategies, can learn the complex task of modular exponentiation with high accuracy. Our results show that reciprocal operand sampling works best to resolve output imbalance, and uniform outcomes can help reduce bias in the output distribution. We observe stepwise grokking behavior, where accuracy surges as the model sequentially internalizes modular relationships. Decomposing this by moduli shows that the model learns several moduli simultaneously, suggesting some sensible arithmetic capabilities discovered in training.

We investigate this further using mechanistic interpretability techniques such as PCA and activation patching to show that while the embedding space evolves toward a more centralized structure post-grokking, key subcomponents in the network become specialized in processing modular arithmetic.

Future work should explore scaling these methods to larger models and more complex architectures, as well as assessing the robustness of emergent circuits under varied training regimes. We also suggest the investigation of circuits in other components of the model such as in the feedforward layers as well as on other tasks such as performance on certain moduli or regular arithmetic. Finally, future work might compare models with classical algorithms for modular exponentiation, looking at similar learned heuristics once accuracy has reached a certain threshold.

A.1
Reciprocal operand distribution

We would like to compute the probability distribution
G(n,0,M)
for given
n\in\mathbb{Z}
and
M
using the reciprocal distribution
F(x,1,M+1)
. Note that the flooring simply implies squashing the probability mass of all values
n\leq x<n+1
. Given the cumulative probability distribution
F(x,a,b)
and the probability density function
f(x,a,b)
of a reciprocal distribution defined on all reals, we have a distribution on the integers

H(n,a,b)=\int_{x\in[n,n+1]}f(x,a,b)~dx=F(n+1,a,b)-F(n,a,b)

This can be case-split into

H(n,a,b)=\begin{cases}0&n<a\mathrm{~or~}n\geq b\\
\frac{\ln(n+1)-\ln(n)}{\ln(b)-\ln(a)}&a\leq n\leq b-1\\
\end{cases}

With
Y:=X-1
,
X\sim H
as our shifted integer for our final distribution
P
, we substitute and simplify bounds and get for
Y=n^{\prime}

\displaystyle P(Y=n^{\prime},a,b)=H(n^{\prime}+1,a,b)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0&n^{\prime}<a-1\mathrm{~or~}n^{\prime}\geq b-1\\
\frac{\ln(n^{\prime}+2)-\ln(n^{\prime}+1)}{\ln(b)-\ln(a)}&a-1\leq n^{\prime}\leq b-2\\
\end{cases}

Note that this still relies on the old bounds. To get the formulation used in the main part of the paper, set
a=1,b=M+2
.
