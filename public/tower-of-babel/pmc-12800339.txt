Defect-induced optical and thermoelectric properties of cobalt doped ZnO nanostructures prepared through hydrothermal route
Authors: Danish Arif, Sayyam Saleem Kiani, Rajwali Khan, Adeel Younas Abid, Kashif Safeen, Khalid M Alotaibi, Wiqar H Shah, Adnan Ali, Sufaid Shah, Wubshet Mekonnen Girma, Atta Ullah Shah, Akif Safeen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this paper, we studied the thermoelectric properties of hydrothermally produced cobalt (Co) doped zinc oxide (ZnO) nanoparticles with varying Co concentrations by correlating with optical defects. X-ray diffraction results verify the generation of ZnO with a well-defined hexagonal structure. whereas the existence of Zn, Co, and O elements is verified through energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy for all under-inspected samples. Photoluminescence spectroscopy reveals a strong emission peak around 380 nm in the ultraviolet region and a second peak at ~ 506 nm which exhibits a broad emission in the green band ascribed to oxygen vacancies. As the doping concentration increases, the UV near-band emissions are observed to be red-shifted with decreased PL intensity in comparison to pure ZnO. We found that electric conductivity and Seebeck coefficient are increased by increasing the cobalt concentration in ZnO. Our findings reveal that incorporating Co into ZnO enhances the Seebeck coefficient to an extreme value of ~ 160 µV/K and ZT of 0.1, which is directly associated with thermoelectric proficiency and oxygen vacancies are found to be primary factor for this enhancement consistent with photoluminescence analysis. The study, on the one hand, depicted that the incorporation of defects represents a good opportunity for improving thermoelectric properties and on the other hand could be useful in the context of atomic scale defect engineering for improving the performance of thermoelectric materials.Keywords: ZnO, Defects, Thermoelectric, Sustainable energySubject terms: Nanoscience and technology, Physics

Introduction

Owing to its prospective to improve waste heat management by directly converting the waste heat into valuable electrical power, thermoelectric (TE) technology has garnered reasonable scholarly consideration in the recent past1. Reportedly, the thermoelectric performance is evaluated by using the Figure of Merit ZT= (S2σ)T/κ, a dimensionless parameter dependent on temperature, wherein S is the Seebeck coefficient while σ and κ are the electrical and thermal conductivities respectively2. In this regard, the prime objective is the simultaneous attainment of enhanced electrical conductivity and Seebeck coefficient along with reduced thermal conductivity which consequently are desirable for improved ZT and hence the optimal thermoelectric efficiency3. Due to the interdependence of these parameters, the independent quantification of these parameters is somewhat tedious. In this background, a semiconductor like ZnO could be an attractive alternative. Because of its reduced toxicity, improved high-temperature stability and eco-friendliness, ZnO comes out to be a favorable candidate when compared with the traditional TE materials, for instance, PbTe-based materials in the middle-temperature regime as well as SiGe-based alloys in the high-temperature range4.

Having better electron mobility, resistance to corrosion, thermal stability, cost-effectiveness and minimal health and environmental risks, ZnO is considered to be a reasonable choice, particularly for thermoelectric applications5,6. Nevertheless, the comparatively high thermal conductivity of pure ZnO hinders its thermoelectric capabilities. Therefore, reducing the material’s thermal conductivity and at the same time maintaining increased values of Seebeck coefficient and electrical conductivity is indeed an essential concern in thermoelectric applications7. Besides, tailoring its thermoelectric properties is often essential to achieve high thermoelectric efficiency. To achieved improved thermoelectric behavior of ZnO various approaches including foreign elemental doping and nanostructuring are utilized8. By modifying both the electrical conductivity and Seebeck coefficient an improvement in the power factor of ZnO could be mainly achieved. This phenomenon is facilitated by the energy filtering effect, where materials with a high carrier density exhibit enhanced electrical conductivity. Simultaneously, the selective filtration of low-energy carriers contributes to an improved Seebeck coefficient. Studies on aluminium-doped ZnO and aluminium-gallium-co-doped ZnO have demonstrated prominent improvements in power factors9. Aluminum single-doped ZnO and aluminium-gallium co-doped ZnO showed enhanced power factors and were extensively inspected systems in the context of achieving better thermoelectric properties. Even by tuning the pore structure in Al-doped ZnO an enhancement of thermoelectric properties was also demonstrated10. Besides, magnesium-doped zinc oxide films demonstrated power factors in the range of 100–200 µW/mK²,11 while gallium-doped ZnO films achieved a remarkable power factor of 449 µW/mK² at 623 K12. Additionally, tantalum-doped ZnO exhibited an impressive power factor of 1.52 × 10⁻³ W/mK² at 1002 K, and indium-doped ZnO materials recorded a power factor of 1.23 × 10⁻³ W/mK², along with a high ZT of 0.4513,14. Recently ZnO has also deliberated by incorporating multiple dopants in anticipation of attaining a higher doping level having the purpose that the combination of different transition metals possibly exhibits better thermoelectric properties15,16. Research indicates that doping in ZnO with different transition metals significantly improves its thermoelectric performance, establishing it as a promising material for high-temperature applications. However, systematic research focusing specifically on the thermoelectric behaviour of singly doped ZnO remains limited. Beside thermoelectric properties transition metal doped ZnO can also be used in high-temperature sensors17, spintronic devices18, UV optoelectronics19, photocatalysis20, and transparent electronics21. Recent studies have witnessed that cobalt-doped ZnO (Co-ZnO) was widely inspected for optical, magnetic, electrical and antibacterial properties but scarcely investigated particularly in the context of defect-triggered thermoelectric properties for a detailed range of doping concentration as well20,22–25. Because according to previous reports zero and three-dimensional defects exhibit an impact on both band structure and transport behavior of charge carriers while studying thermoelectric properties. For instance, the ionic radius difference is very slight perhaps due to the identical valence state of Co and Zn ions and apparently, imperfection formation for holding charge impartiality could be restrained. In addition, while compared with pure ZnO the Co-doped ZnO nanostructures are thought to retain more thermal stability. In addition, Co as a dopant for ZnO is considered a potential candidate in enhancing TE properties because of its large solubility in the ZnO matrix and its abundant electron states required carrier concentration26. Another aspect is related to the influence on the band gap, particularly, it is attributed to near band edge (NBE) emissions in Co-doped doped ZnO, which remains uncertain because of conflicting findings reported in the literature27. This has inspired us to study the consequence of Co doping on the structural, optical and thermoelectric performance of ZnO nanostructures. Optical defects, such as zinc interstitials and oxygen vacancies, disrupt the periodic potential field of the crystal lattice, causing local lattice distortions that deviate from the ideal structure28–30. These distortions serve as scattering centers for both electrons and phonons, thereby influencing charge carrier mobility and thermal transport. Moreover, these defects introduce charge imbalances, hence generating extrinsic carriers that would affect the electrical transport of the material. It is thus important that optical defects be correlated with thermoelectric transport properties to understand how such defects may influence thermoelectric performance. Clarifying their impact will provide an in-depth insight into defect-driven mechanisms that could help in optimizing material properties towards better thermoelectric efficiency.

The novelty of this work lies in showing, in a straightforward way, how defects created through controlled doping directly influence and improve the thermoelectric performance of the material. Through photoluminescence (PL) analysis, we link these dopant-induced defects to the formation of localized energy states that help boost electrical conductivity and the Seebeck coefficient, while minimizing thermal conductivity. The PL results clearly highlight the presence and impact of these defect states, explaining how they enhance charge transport and increase phonon scattering. By connecting defect formation, PL behavior, and thermoelectric performance. This type of study offers a significant perspective into the best doping approaches for improving the thermoelectric efficiency of ZnO as well as could be possibly a gateway in bridging the missing link of transition metal oxides doped ZnO research for the quest of enhanced TE properties.

Experiential details

Materials

Sigma-Aldrich supplied zinc nitrate hexahydrate [Zn(NO3)2·6 H₂O], and cobalt nitrate hexahydrate [Co(NO3)2·6 H₂O], each with a purity of 99.99%. Additionally, sodium hydroxide (NaOH), used as the precipitation agent, was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich. The chemicals were used as provided, without undergoing any further purification.

Sample preparation

Both pure and Co-doped ZnO nanoparticles were synthesized via hydrothermal technique. The first step involved dissolving a 0.22 M solution of Zn(NO3)2·6 H₂O in 70 ml of DI water while stirring continuously at room temperature until the solution was completely dissolved. Meanwhile, a certain amount of NaOH (0.78 M) was dissolved in deionized water and added dropwise to the Zn(NO3)2·6 H₂O solution until the pH of the mixture extended to 10. The final mixture was then put in an autoclave and heated to 120 °C for ten hours. The reaction produced nanoparticles, which were then repeatedly cleaned in a centrifuge for ten minutes at a time using distilled water to get rid of impurities. A 12-hour drying process was then applied to the white precipitates that resulted. The precipitates were then annealed for two hours at 600 °C. For the cobalt-doped specimens, a 70 ml solution of zinc nitrate hexahydrate and cobalt nitrate hexahydrate was made. The pH was raised to 10 by carefully adding a 0.78 M aqueous solution of NaOH drop by drop and rapidly stirring. Prior to being dried in an oven at 120 °C and then annealed for two hours at 600 °C in a furnace, the precipitates were collected and centrifuged clear. The prepared samples were categorized as undoped ZnO (pure ZnO), 0.5% Co, 1% and Co 1.5% Co-doped ZnO. The selected Co doping concentration (0–1.5%) were chosen because to maintain structural stability without introducing impurity phases while still producing measurable and meaningful changes in the defect states.

Characterization

The structural properties of pristine and Co-doped ZnO nanoparticles with different doping level of cobalt (Co) were studied by using the X-ray diffraction technique. Using copper kα (λ = 1.54060 Å) radiation, an XRD examination was accomplished with a BRUKER D-8 ADVANCE X-ray diffractometer. The synthesized samples were inserted in the diffractometer chamber and scanned between 20 and 80 degrees in an angular range. Using a scanning electron microscope (SEM), MODEL MAIA3 TESCAN, the morphology of synthesized ZnO nanoparticles is examined. A Hitachi F-7000 fluorescence spectrophotometer (λex = 325 nm) was utilized to study the optical characteristics. To evaluate the thermoelectric characteristics, the nanopowder was first pressed into pellets by applying a pressure of 10 tons for one minute. To decrease porosity, the pellets were subsequently thermally treated at 400 °C for the duration of two hours. To ensure consistent electrical contact, each pellet was coated with silver paste. Electrical conductivity and Seebeck coefficient were measured instantaneously over the temperature range of 300–900 K using a NETZSCH SBA 458 Nemesis system. Thermal conductivity measurements for the same pellets were also conducted within the same temperature range.

The optical, electron density and thermoelectric studies indicate that the addition of Co ions to the host lattice influences the physical parameters of the Co doping levels39. It is observed that with the doping of Co crystallinity of the samples is enhanced. Figure 2a and b display the Rietveld refinement analysis for both ZnO and Co-doped ZnO samples, which were modelled based on a single-phase wurtzite ZnO structure. The refinement yielded reliability factors for the Bragg intensity (RI) and structure factor (RF) of less than 1.9% and 1.73% for both pure ZnO and 1.5% Co-doped ZnO, respectively. The refinement showed a steady decrease in lattice constants with higher Co doping levels. It is known that the formation of interstitial zinc increases lattice constants, while the creation of oxygen vacancies decreases them41. The reduction in internal defects and lattice constants with increased Co doping indicates that oxygen vacancies were the main defects in the ZnO sample, and their density reduced as the concentration of Co dopant increased.

It was anticipated that the existence of oxygen vacancies in ZnO and Co-doped ZnO would influence the electron density distributions around the oxygen atoms. Therefore, the electronic structure of pure and 1.5% Co-doped ZnO was analyzed using the maximum-entropy method (MEM) based on structure factors obtained from X-ray measurements. MEM calculations were performed for pure and 1.5% Co-doped ZnO. The experimentally derived MEM electron densities were used to visualize the three-dimensional (3D) electron density using Visualization for Electronic Structural Analysis (VESTA) software (Version 3.5.0). The charge densities within the pure and doped ZnO unit cell are represented by the 3D isosurfaces shown in Fig. 2c and d. Figure 2c and d display electron density maps of the pristine and 1.5% Co-doped ZnO samples in the (110) plane, respectively. There was no significant difference in electron density between the Zn sites in ZnO and the Zn(Co) sites in Co-doped ZnO, which is accredited to the low dopant concentration and the similar electron distributions of Zn²⁺ and Co²⁺, as both have comparable total electron counts42. This suggests that Co doping led to greater occupancy of oxygen sites in the wurtzite ZnO structure, resulting in a reduction in the oxygen vacancy content43.

The atomic positions and their displacements remained stable, showing no significant variation with Co-doping. This indicates that 1.5% Co-doping in ZnO does not affect the atomic locality in the structure. As shown in Table 1, the bond length (L) in pure and 1.5% Co-doped ZnO structures do not have any significant difference.

Photoluminescence spectroscopy

Photoluminescence (PL) spectroscopy is used for exploring the electronic structure and quantized energy levels resulting from doping in order to better comprehend the kinds of defects and electron-hole recombination processes. The PL spectra of synthesized nanoparticles, recorded using a helium-cadmium (He-Cd) laser with an excitation wavelength of 325 nm, are presented in Fig. 5a, spanning a wavelength range from 350 nm to 650 nm. Figure 5b presents the PL spectra of all prepared samples, each showing two emission bands: a prominent UV peak and a broad deep-level emission. In the PL spectra of pure ZnO, two distinctive peaks are observed. The first peak, a strong emission around 380 nm in the ultraviolet region, stems from the recombination of free excitons near the band-edge (NBE) of ZnO. These peaks are ascribed to the characteristics peaks of ZnO, which result from electron-hole recombination during the near band-edge transition47,48. Knowingly, the observation of visible emissions is related to a multiple defect states like Zn interstitials, Zn and O vacancies. In the present case, the next peak observed at around 506 nm exhibits a broad emission in the green band ascribed to oxygen vacancies. The peak at 506 nm arises from the transition of an electron from the ionized oxygen vacancy state to the valence band and is associated with the creation of surface defects49.

(a) PL spectra of Pure and Co-doped ZnO (b) Energy band diagram.

For Co-doped ZnO at doping levels of 0.5%, 1%, and 1.5%, UV emission peaks observed at 380 nm, 383 nm, 387 nm, and 388 nm respectively are indicative of band-to-band transitions. The graph shows that as Co doping increases; PL intensity gradually decreases. This suggests that the recombination of photogenerated electron-hole pairs slows down and the separation proficiency of e-h pairs is enhanced with decreasing PL intensity. As the doping percentage increases, the UV near-band emissions exhibit a redshift compared to those of pure ZnO. Identical investigation has been performed in the literature for Co-doped ZnO nanostructures and thin films wherein a blue shift, red shift and even disappearance of peaks are entailed for UV emission due to different Co content in ZnO50. The intensity of the green emission peaks is reduced as the Co doping concentration increases, and also a red shift is observed compared to pure ZnO. It was proposed that the UV emission peak position in a Co-doped ZnO system is affected by more than one physical phenomenon in one or another way. For instance, the shift of the peak to shorter wavelengths (blue shift) is predictable to the Burstein-Moss effect, while the shift to longer wavelengths (redshift) observed in the emission peak could be attributed to either the exchange interaction or band tailing effect as well as the defects in the crystals. In our works, observed red-shift which reportedly results in the reduction of the band gap is possibly due to either the strong s–d and p–d exchange interactions between the band electrons of the host and d electrons of incorporated/substituted Co2+ ions51, or effect of the band tail52. Previous literature also confirms that the obtained red shift of UV emission for Co-doped ZnO and Mn-doped ZnO may be because of the sp-d exchange interaction between the substituted and host bands53,54. It was found that with the increasing Fe doping in ZnO the exchange interaction becomes more dominant55. Besides, in the light of hitherto reported studies the redshift of UV emission and the subsequent band gap reduction could also be attributed to band tailing effects means that when donor density escalates it leads to band tailing below the conduction band, a phenomenon which is converse to band filling effect found to originate in Burstein-Moss effect56. The band gap and crystallite size have an inverse relationship due to quantum confinement effects in nanoparticles57. With the increase in crystallite size, the influence of quantum confinement reduces, resulting in a decrement in the band gap. In ZnO, smaller crystallites exhibit more quantum confinement effects, which restrict the movement of charge carriers and lead to a wide band gap. However, the Co doping facilitates grain growth, leading to an increase in crystallite size. This development in crystallite size reduces the confinement effect, thereby causing a narrowing of the band gap58,59. In addition, the decrement in the band gap of doped ZnO can be ascribed to an increase in structural defects compared to pure ZnO. When Co2+ ions are introduced within ZnO crystal structure, defect states are introduced near the valence band, leading to band gap narrowing. As a result, photons in the visible light range can provide sufficient energy to excite electrons from the altered valence band to the conduction band. Furthermore, the decrease in band gap with doping may be influenced by exchange interactions between the localized d-electrons of Co2+ and the s and p electrons of Zn in the host lattice60. As a result of these interactions, the conduction and valence bands shift in opposite directions, downward and upward, respectively, further contributing to the band gap narrowing. In the context of defects in crystals, as impurity effects become prominent in the photoluminescence of pure materials, excitons interacting with the material encounter defects and tend to decay near these sites. Consequently, the intensity of UV emission diminishes as the Co content increases61. These defects are identified as critical factors in the thermoelectric effect, in the context of enhancing its performance, which is explained in the next section.

Thermoelectric properties

The change in electrical conductivity with temperature for pure and Co-doped ZnO is depicted in Fig. 6a. The conductivity is found to increase nonlinearly with rising temperature, entailing the semiconducting behavior for an under-investigated series of samples62. This effect is because of a greater number of carrier concentrations, and a diffusion mechanism. Reportedly, Electrical conductivity is influenced by two factors carrier mobility and the charge associated with each carrier and the premier may be largely influenced by grain defects and boundaries, whereas the latter could be related to the sample’s composition63. The electrical conductivity at 650 K increases from 259 Sm− 1 for the pure ZnO to 1198 Sm− 1 for the 0.5% Co-doped sample, 1780 Sm− 1 for the 1% sample, and further increases to 1800 Sm− 1 for the 1.5% Co-doped ZnO. This suggests that by adding more amount of Co dopant, an increase in carrier concentration occurs, a phenomenon mostly observed in non-degenerate semiconductors. The augmentation of carrier concentration could be mainly due to the substitution of Co3+ for Zn2+. We argue that the incorporation of Co for Zn may alter the ZnO band structure and add an impurity state in the band gap. The enhancement in carrier concentration could also be moderately attributed to the lowering of the band gap as well as the slowing down of electron-hole recombination with increasing doping content. Note that as temperature increases, the conductivity of the highest doped Co content (1.5%) is found to be marginally decreased. This trend could be attributed to a decrease in mobility at large doping and relatively high temperatures.

(a) Variation of electrical conductivity vs. temperature for all samples (b) Seebeck coefficient (c) power factor of pure and Co-doped ZnO (d) Thermal conductivity of pure and Co-doped ZnO.

The temperature-dependent Seebeck coefficient, shown in Fig. 6b, provides an understanding of the performance of undoped and Co-doped ZnO across different Co concentrations. Negative Seebeck coefficients were observed for entire samples, confirming n-type conductivity64. For pure ZnO, the absolute value of the Seebeck coefficient increases with temperature. Consistently, increasing the Co doping concentration from 0.5% to 1.5% enhances the Seebeck coefficient, rising from 118 to 160 µV/K. The sudden rise of the Seebeck coefficient within 500–900 K for the 1.5% Co-doped ZnO sample can be attributed to a comprehensive effect from energy filtering, resonant level formation, and thermally activated carrier dynamics. PL investigation presents a green emission peak located around 506 nm, implying oxygen vacancies that introduce localized states near the conduction band. These states selectively impede the motion of low-energy carriers, thus significantly enhancing the Seebeck coefficient via the energy filtering effect9. Additionally, Co doping introduces resonant levels that assist carrier excitation at high temperatures. The redshift and intensity suppression in PL spectra confirm increased defect density65. These mechanisms, taken together, contribute to the enhancement in the thermopower of Co-ZnO. Further, these defects may be due to oxygen vacancies in the lattice structure. The increase in the optical absorption and the reduced band gap in Co-doped ZnO is associated with a higher defect level as compared to pristine ZnO66. These defects are vital to the thermoelectric effect, expressively enhancing its performance. The defects introduced by Co-doping strongly affect the crystal structure, thereby impelling the material’s thermoelectric performance. The increment in the Seebeck coefficient and changes in the band gap of Co-doped ZnO can be attributed to the formation of defects, which are essential for the enhancement of thermoelectric efficiency67. One possible explanation for the observed rise in both electrical conductivity and Seebeck coefficient in Co-doped samples is the effect of energy-dependent scattering centers or carrier filtering, which scatter lower-energy carriers more effectively. This specifically lets higher-energy carriers, which are more important to the Seebeck effect, conduct electricity. This raises the Seebeck coefficient while maintaining good electrical conductivity68. Reportedly, point defects, including oxygen vacancies and bulk defects such as secondary phases affect the band structure as well as the transport behavior of the material69–71. The augmentation of S with respect to both concentration and temperature could be comprehended by invoking the direct interdependence of Seebeck coefficient and scattering factor72. In the presence of oxygen vacancies related zero dimensional defects as deduced in our PL findings, there is a possibility of creating more scattering centers which in turns enhances the scattering factor and thus the Seebeck coefficient. These oxygen vacancies might possess the tendency to promote localized states in electronic structure which acts as scattering centers and can filter charge carriers based on their energy. Seebeck coefficient ma improved by this energy filtering and according to literature, for instance, the existence of other bulk defects is also found to retard the activity of low energy carriers68. The identical effects of enhanced Seebeck coefficient due to the presence of three dimensional bulk-type defects such as secondary phases are previously reported in case of Co-doped ZnO, Sb-doped ZnO and Ca-doped CuAlO2 73,74. As well as when cobalt is added, it may cause impurity states or resonant levels to appear near the conduction or valence bands. These states are highly sensitive to impurity valence and electronegativity. Resonances induced by other transition metal dopants can further enhance thermopower, especially at moderate and high temperatures75.These resonant states may be enhanced the number of states near the Fermi level, which raises the Seebeck coefficient76, and lets charge carriers move around easily, keeping good electrical conductivity. By simultaneously considering the john teller effect and crystal field theory, it can be argued that impurities levels are generated due to the splitting of d-orbitals. Although, due to completely filled d-orbitals the john teller effect is not possible for ZnO nanoparticles but this effect comes in to play in case of Co ion as a dopant due to the presence of seven electrons. Consequently, the d-state is found to split into higher t2g state and lower Eg state possibly due to the field induced by oxygen atoms77. The t2g state further splits into bonding and antibonding states and new energy levels are formed just below the conduction band. As temperature increases the electrons jumps from these new energy states to conduction band thus participate in electrical conduction78,79.

The power factor (σS2) plot of pure and Co-doped ZnO nanoparticles at various measurement temperatures is also depicted in Fig. 6c. As a result of development of both Seebeck coefficient and electrical conductivity the power factor is also observed to rise with dopant concentration.

The thermal conductivity (K) of synthesized ZnO nanoparticles from a temperature range of 300–900 K is shown in Fig. 6d. At lower temperatures, the thermal conductivity of all samples declines sharply, beyond a certain temperature, it varies inversely with temperature. At low-temperature, scattering is mainly caused by induced and static charge carriers. As the temperature rises, phonons possessing extended mean free paths play a more significant role in thermal transport. Phonon scattering arises from several lattice imperfections, including mass fluctuations, structural disorder, dislocations, and grain boundaries, thereby affecting the overall thermal conductivity80,81. The incorporation of Co2+ ions in the ZnO host lattice introduces point defects by the difference of ionic radius. These defects act as phonon scattering centers, enhancing lattice disorder and decreasing the phonon mean free path. At higher temperatures, phonon-phonon scattering dominates, leading to a more pronounced reduction in thermal conductivity82. By tuning carrier concentration and mobility, Co doping strengthens electron–phonon interactions, which in turn reduces the electronic contribution to thermal conductivity83. Furthermore, defect-induced modifications, including oxygen vacancies and Zn interstitials may likewise play a role in reducing the thermal conductivity. These defects can soften the lattice, decreasing phonon group velocity and thereby reducing thermal conductivity84. Additionally, with Co doping grain boundaries increased, phonon scattering at these boundaries may further reduce thermal conductivity. The thermal conductivity reduced from 25 W/mK to ~ 5 W/mK with 1.0 wt% Co-doping at 900 K. This reduction in thermal conductivity is reasonable for the development of thermoelectric efficiency. Conclusion

In summary, this study explored how Co doping influences the structural, optical, and thermoelectric behavior of ZnO nanostructures. Our structural analysis confirmed the wurtzite hexagonal phase of ZnO, and the introduction of Co led to noticeable shifts in the XRD peaks along with an increase in crystallite size. The optical results showed that Co doping enhances visible-light absorption and gradually narrows the band gap as the Co concentration rises. Thermoelectric measurements revealed that Co-doped ZnO exhibits a marked improvement in the Seebeck coefficient at high temperatures. Additionally, both electrical conductivity and the power factor increased with higher Co content, demonstrating an overall enhancement in thermoelectric performance. Overall, these findings highlight the potential of Co-doped ZnO as a promising material for next-generation energy-conversion technologies, especially as the demand for sustainable alternatives grows amid the depletion of conventional energy sources.
