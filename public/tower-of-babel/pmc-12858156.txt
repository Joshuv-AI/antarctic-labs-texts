Migration of a copper T intrauterine device: A clinical Insight into complications and diagnostic approaches
Authors: Sangam Jha, Jafeesha B. Singh, Vikas Chandra Jha, Raveena Bhagat
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntrauterine devices (IUCDs), such as the multi-load copper T, are widely used long-acting reversible contraceptive methods due to their effectiveness and convenience. However, complications can arise in rare instances, such as device migration. Case Series Report Case 1: A 26-year-old, G5 P2 A2 L2, presented with 2 months of amenorrhoea and inability to feel CuT thread. USG done revealed intra-uterine pregnancy of approx. 10 weeks gestational age, however intrauterine CuT was not visualized. So, we presumed it a case of expelled CuT. Regular antenatal care continued till term and LSCS was performed at 39 weeks. Intraoperatively a thread was seen embedded in the omentum lying adjacent to the uterus. Further exploration revealed multi-load-copper 375 with pus collection around it. Case 2: A 36-year-old female, Para 2, Live 2, presented with complaints of lower abdominal pain and multiple episodes of haematuria since last 6 months. An initial pelvic ultrasound showed a hyperechogenic intravesical structure of 3.3 cm, suggesting a urinary bladder calculus. A pelvic CT scan revealed a metallic T-shaped structure within the urinary bladder suggestive of a misplaced copper T device. Patient underwent cystoscopic removal of calcified IUCD. Patient had no further urinary symptoms following removal. These cases highlight the importance of monitoring patients with IUCDs for potential complications, especially when threads are missing. They also emphasize considering intravesical migration of CuT as a cause of haematuria in women. Additionally, pelvic cavity exploration during LSCS is vital to locate any migrated IUCDs when such a history exists.

Introduction

Intrauterine copper T device (IUCD) is a widely used long-acting reversible contraceptive (LARC). In Eastern and South-Eastern Asia, IUCD usage stands at 18.6%.[1] Literature suggest that it is highly effective with less than 1% failure rate.[2] In addition, it has been associated with minimal complication rate.[3] Common complications include cramping, pain, spotting, or irregular bleeding and expulsion of the device.[4] Rare but more serious complications include uterine perforation which can occur in 1 of 1,000 women.[4] After perforation it can migrate to adjacent organs like bladder, rectum, omentum, mesentery or pouch of Douglas.[34] This can result in more severe complications like infection, adhesions, bowel perforation, or injury to nearby organs. Herein we present two cases of migrated CuT device one in the urinary bladder and other in the omentum, likely due to the secondary perforation.

Case 1

A 26-year-old woman, Para 2, with a history of two abortions and two live births, presented with concerns regarding a missing Copper T IUCD thread that had been inserted 2 years earlier, along with a delayed menstrual cycle. Past obstetric history was significant with previous 2 LSCS and two surgical abortions. On examination, the IUCD thread could not be located. A pelvic ultrasound was performed which revealed a single live intrauterine pregnancy of 10 weeks gestational age but no evidence of the Copper T within the uterus [Figure 1a].

(a) Gray scale image of uterus in sagittal section showing intrauterine pregnancy. (b) Intraoperative picture showing ectopic location of IUCD in the omentum (white arrow)

The patient subsequently received regular antenatal care till term. Her antenatal period was uneventful. At 39 weeks of gestation, she underwent LSCS, during which thread was visualized adhered to the omentum lying adjacent to the uterus. On further exploration, a multi-load copper 375 was found embedded in the omentum surrounded by a pus pocket [Figure 1b]. The Copper T, along with the adhered portion of the omentum, was surgically excised. A sample of the pus was sent for culture, which later returned sterile. She received routine postoperative care as per the institute protocol. She had uneventful postoperative recovery. She came for postnatal follow up after 6 weeks with no complaints and doing well.

Case 2

A 36-year-old female, Para 2, Live 2, presented with complaints of lower abdominal pain and multiple episodes of haematuria since last 6 months. There was no history of dysuria, vaginal discharge, or abnormal menstrual cycles. Her medical history was notable only for the insertion of a copper T IUCD 8 years prior. The patient mentioned that the copper T was removed, along with bilateral tubal ligation, 2 months after its insertion. Physical examination results were normal. Urine analysis revealed a trace of sugar but was otherwise normal. An initial pelvic ultrasound showed a hyperechogenic intravesical structure of 3.3 cm, suggesting a urinary bladder calculus. A pelvic CT scan revealed a metallic T-shaped structure within the urinary bladder, with one limb extending into the extravesical compartment along the right anterolateral aspect, suggestive of a misplaced copper T device [Figure 2a, b]. She was planned for cystoscopic removal of IUCD. On cystoscopy, a copper T 380A was found embedded in the bladder mucosa at the right anterolateral aspect, with calcification around the vertical limb of the copper T device [Figure 2c]. The copper IUD was safely removed through the urethra using a cystoscopy grasper [Figure 2d]. No major defect was noted at the site of removal. Postoperative period was uneventful. After catheter removal, she reported no further urinary symptoms. The patient was discharged on the following day.

Discussion

Intrauterine Copper containing devices are highly effective and safe method of contraception. However, despite their general safety, complications can arise. One of the serious complications among them is uterine perforation. It can be either primary (during insertion) or secondary (post-insertion). Primary perforation occurs during the insertion of the IUCD, often due to improper technique, undetected uterine abnormalities, or uterine contractions that push the device into or through the uterine wall. In the second case, migration would have been caused by primary perforation as patient presented within 2 months of insertion with missing thread. Secondary perforation occurs after insertion, typically caused by the gradual erosion of the IUCD through the uterine wall over time. This can be triggered by factors like uterine contractions, anatomical changes, previous uterine surgeries, or trauma, making the musculature weak and allowing the device to eventually migrate into the abdominal cavity.[4] Exact incidence of primary and secondary perforation is not yet clear.[5] In the first case, multiple caesarean sections and previous uterine procedures (Dilatation and Curettage) were contributing risk factors for secondary perforation.

Most commonly, women are asymptomatic and present with missing thread or rarely there can be severe abdominal pain, infection, and issues like diarrhoea, haematuria, dysuria, pelvic pressure, and suprapubic pain depending on the IUCD’s final location.[6] In the current series, case 2 presented with haematuria 8 years after insertion and case 1 presented with delayed menses and a missing thread, she mistakenly attributed her amenorrhea, caused by pregnancy, to a simple delay in her menstrual cycle.

Ultrasonography (USG) or the X-ray is the first modality to localize the missing IUCD. CT scan helps in locating the IUCD that has migrated outside the uterus.[7] In our first case we performed the USG, but could not visualize the IUCD in the uterus, furthermore intra-uterine pregnancy was confirmed. So, we did not proceed further with CECT for localization of IUCD. In case 2, USG suggested intravesical calculi, however, CECT confirmed the diagnosis of migrated CopperT in the urinary bladder.

Routine follow-up of the patient with IUCD insertion is not done, and hence migration is only suspected when patient present with symptoms. Migrated IUCD can be removed through laparoscopy, laparotomy or cystoscopy depending on the location. In the first case, it was removed during LSCS. Aljohani et al.[3] reported a similar case where a pregnant patient had a missing intrauterine contraceptive device, despite undergoing LSCS at term, the IUCD remained unretrieved. Postnatally, the patient presented with abdominal pain and bowel symptoms, and it was removed from POD through laparoscopy.[8] Benchaou et al.[9] reported case of omental migration of IUCD on Day two of insertion wherein patient presented with severe pain abdomen, so it was due to primary uterine perforation. Şam et al.[10] reported two cases of intravesical migration of IUCD, wherein patient presented with bladder stone and CuT was removed during cyst lithotripsy. In the current series, second case presented with haematuria and we suspected CuT pre-operatively on CECT. It was removed using cystoscopy by simply pulling it off with grasper.

Considering the risk of migration of IUCD and associated complications, regular follow-up to ensure its proper location should be encouraged. In the first case, the IUD’s displacement resulted in the failure of contraception, and the patient chose to continue with the pregnancy. So, we suggest that, women should be counselled to report immediately in case of missing period even on IUCD. In the second case, the primary physician assumed that the IUD thread was missing due to the expulsion and did not conduct further investigation, proceeding with a tubal ligation. Despite the lack of symptoms, it’s crucial to remove a perforated IUD to prevent potential harm to other organs, as demonstrated by our second case.

Conclusion

These cases highlight the importance of monitoring patients with IUCDs for potential complications, particularly in instances where the threads are missing. Although ultrasound is a useful initial tool for detecting misplaced IUDs, it may occasionally fail to locate them. In cases of high suspicion, CECT should be performed to ensure accurate detection. A high level of suspicion is essential for symptomatic patients. During a LSCS, it is important to explore the pelvic cavity to locate any migrated IUCDs if there is history of missing thread.
