Male Sapap3 knockout mice show threat bias under conflict during platform-mediated avoidance task: effects of extinction with response prevention and implications for obsessive compulsive disorder
Authors: Elizabeth E. Manning, Elizabeth A. Crummy, Jamie L. Pierson, Zoe LaPalombara, Xiaojun Li, Samyuktha Manikandan, Susanne E. Ahmari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Obsessive compulsive disorder (OCD) typically involves cycling between symptoms of intrusive thoughts (obsessions) and repetitive rituals aimed at avoiding aversive outcomes (compulsions), which interferes with patients’ lives. This relationship between obsessions and compulsions highlights a potential role of impaired threat processing and avoidance in OCD symptoms. Platform mediated avoidance (PMA) has proven to be a useful rodent paradigm to examine avoidance neurobiology and OCD-relevant constructs, such as exposure with response prevention (ERP) and persistent avoidance. However, the ERP protocol has only been used in rats, and studies in OCD-relevant transgenic mouse models may shed further light on neural mechanisms relevant to the disorder. To address this gap, we tested male Sapap3 knockout (KO) mice, a leading preclinical model in OCD research, using PMA with ERP. We examined avoidance acquisition, expression, and extinction, as well as reward-seeking under motivational conflict, in separate cohorts conditioned using high or low intensity shock. Sapap3-KOs exhibited heightened suppression of lever pressing for rewards and elevated freezing during a warning tone signaling impending footshocks, suggesting greater threat sensitivity under motivational conflict. Avoidance responding was acquired more slowly and extinguished more robustly following ERP in Sapap3-KOs trained with lower-intensity shock only. c-Fos expression showed preliminary evidence for decreased activity of medial orbitofrontal cortex (mOFC) in KOs, which may mediate post-ERP reduction of threat responses. Together these findings suggest that mOFC changes may contribute to therapeutic effects in OCD, and that dysfunction in mOFC may contribute to increased influence of threats (vs rewards) over action selection in OCD.

Introduction

Obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) is a chronic, debilitating disorder affecting 1–3% of the population. It is characterized by recurring distressing thoughts (obsessions) and ritualized actions (i.e. compulsions) that are often performed to avoid the perceived harmful outcomes of obsessions and obtain relief from obsession-related distress [1]. The link between obsessions, compulsions, and avoidance is further highlighted by studies showing that impairments in threat processing and avoidance are associated with compulsion severity and poorer treatment outcomes in OCD [2–4]. Additionally, OCD patients exhibit biases to attend to and learn to avoid negative stimuli [5, 6], habitually avoid devalued threats [7], have devaluation impairments on a rule-based avoidance task with longer illness duration [8], and inappropriately perform avoidant behaviors in response to safety cues [9]. Together, these studies suggest that persistent active avoidance could contribute to the development and severity of OCD, but little is known about the mechanisms underlying this dysregulation.

Active avoidance is a defensive behavioral response in which an action is performed to prevent exposure to a threat. Common preclinical models include shuttle avoidance, in which rodents are conditioned to run across a chamber to avoid shocks administered on one half of a shuttlebox, and platform avoidance, in which rodents can avoid shocks by stepping onto a platform to prevent contact with a grid floor delivering a foot shock. The platform-mediated avoidance task (PMA) specifically involves rodents choosing between lever pressing for rewards or making an avoidance response by mounting a platform during a cue that predicts a foot shock. This design allows modeling of conflict-based active avoidance, a critical feature of maladaptive avoidant behaviors [10, 11]. In addition, extinction of platform avoidance can be performed using exposure with response prevention (ERP) [12] to model the gold-standard behavioral therapy for OCD [13], which involves patients refraining from completing rituals in the presence of obsession-related stimuli, making this a powerful translational model for understanding how avoidant behaviors are extinguished or persist following ERP [14]. Studies in rodents have identified circuits involved in PMA conditioning and extinction whose homologues are also implicated in neuroimaging studies of avoidance in OCD patients, including medial prefrontal cortex and striatal regions relevant to threat appraisal and motivated behaviors altered in OCD [14–17]. Testing of OCD-relevant transgenic mice on this task may therefore provide important opportunities for identifying disease relevant mechanisms.

Sapap3 knockout mice (KOs) - a transgenic line with deletion of the postsynaptic scaffolding protein Sapap3 - are a common model for OCD-related behaviors, exhibiting compulsive grooming [18–22], elevated anxiety [20, 21], and deficits in behavioral flexibility [20, 23–25] relevant to symptoms observed in clinical populations [26, 27]. Recent studies also demonstrate enhanced fear conditioning and impaired fear extinction in KOs [[6, 28], (Ahmari lab, unpublished observations)], suggesting heightened sensitivity to negative stimuli and threat appraisal. Therefore, identifying behavioral and neurobiological changes associated with avoidance acquisition, extinction, or conflict-based reward seeking in Sapap3-KOs using the PMA task could provide valuable information for understanding alterations in avoidance behavior and associated symptoms in OCD. While one study has investigated Sapap3-KO performance in PMA conditioning and extinction [28], no studies have examined how KOs extinguish or persist in platform avoidance following ERP. Furthermore, the previous study from St. Laurent et al. [28] did not investigate changes in reward-seeking under motivational conflict across platform avoidance conditioning and extinction. Potential differences in reward-seeking vs avoidance conflict in Sapap3-KOs, and mechanisms underlying these decision-making processes, remain unknown.

To address these gaps, we examined PMA acquisition and extinction using an ERP model in male Sapap3-KOs and wild-type (WT) littermate controls. We then analyzed the expression of immediate early gene c-Fos to begin to examine potential neural mechanisms involved in reinstatement of active avoidance following ERP. We hypothesized that KOs would show excessive avoidance responding that would persist even following ERP exposure, similar to effects previously observed in rats with overtraining or orbitofrontal cortex disruptions [12, 14].

Methods

Animals

Male Sapap3-KOs and WT littermates were generated through heterozygous x heterozygous breeding and maintained on C57BL/6 background [21]. Mice were group-housed with 2–4 mice per cage, with ad libitum access to food and water until operant training commenced at approximately 4 months of age, when they subsequently had restricted daily food access and ad libitum access to water. Mice were housed in a reverse light-cycle room starting at least 1 week prior to the start of operant testing (12:12, lights on at 7:00 pm). All procedures were carried out in accordance with the guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals from the National Institutes of Health (NIH) and with approval from the University of Pittsburgh Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC).

Behavior testing

Grooming test

Approximately one week prior to the beginning of operant training, mice were tested in acrylic testing chambers (8 × 8 × 12 inches) during their light cycle similar to as previously described [19]. Videos were recorded using a front-on view (Canon Vixia; HF R800) of the testing arena and manually scored offline for time spent grooming. Mice were an average of 3.7 months of age (range 3.1–4.1) at the time of the grooming test. One mouse was excluded due to missing video data.

Operant acquisition

Throughout operant training, mice were food restricted to 85–90% of their free feeding weight (see Supplement for detailed methods). They were 4.1 months of age on average (range 3.5–4.5) when operant training commenced. Mice initially trained on a fixed ratio 1 (FR1) schedule for food reward (20-mg chocolate-flavored grain-based pellets; BioServ, Flemington, NJ) in operant chambers (MED-307W, Med Associates, Fairfax, VT) prior to progressing to variable ratio 2 (VR2) sessions and variable interval 30 (VI-30) sessions for reward training prior to beginning platform avoidance conditioning (Fig.1A,C). Platform avoidance conditioning

Following acquisition of high-rate lever pressing on a VI-30 schedule, mice underwent 14 days of platform avoidance conditioning similar to the paradigm previously described in rats [29]. During daily conditioning, mice were trained in 36 min sessions, during which 9 tones (30 s duration, average inter-tone interval 3 min (ITI range of 2’40”–3’42”, first tone starting at 5’12”) co-terminated in a 2 s foot shock. Mice had access to the same lever on VI-30 schedule throughout testing and could also move onto the platform at the end of the tone period to avoid the footshock. One cohort of animals was tested using a foot shock intensity of 0.40 mA (n = 7 WT and 7 KO), and a follow-up second cohort was tested using 0.24 mA (n = 8 WT and 8 KO). Mice were excluded from analyses if they failed to acquire platform avoidance by the final conditioning session (0.24 mA cohort: n = 1 WT, n = 1 KO; 0.4 mA cohort: n = 2 KOs).

Lever press suppression during the tone was calculated using the press rate in the 60 s prior to the tone (pretone press rate) as in previous studies[29]:

Suppression of lever pressing = (pretone press rate – tone press rate)/(pretone press rate + tone press rate) × 100.

Video recordings (Genious Wide Cam F-100, 15 fps) from above the operant chamber were used to quantify time spent avoiding (i.e. time on the platform). Time spent on the platform was quantified automatically using ANY-maze software (Wood Dale, IL), with zones set up for the “platform” and “whole chamber”. To determine average latencies to mount the platform, the difference between the timestamp for the first platform entry of each trial and the start of the tone was used. Platform entries were manually scored (Boris), and classified as the time when both front paws were positioned on the platform and the mouse remained on the platform for a minimum of 5 frames. A minimum of 3 trials with platform entries during the tone were required to obtain average latencies, with the exception of the latency to the first tone of day 1 conditioning.

Platform avoidance extinction with response prevention

Following 14 days of platform avoidance training, mice were trained for 3 days in an extinction with response prevention paradigm, in which access to the platform was blocked by a Perspex barrier to prevent avoidance actions [14], to attempt to mimic clinical ERP procedures which coach patients to resist compulsive actions in the context of obsession-provoking stimuli [13]. On each of these 3 days, mice were exposed to 15 tones (average 3 min ITI (ITI range of 2’40”–3’42”, with tone 1 starting 5’12” into the session), and no foot shock outcome was delivered. Mice were able to lever press on VI-30 schedule as per conditioning sessions.

Platform avoidance extinction test

Following extinction with response prevention, mice were tested in a final extinction test session, in which access to the platform was no longer blocked. As in conditioning, mice were exposed to 9 tones; however, these no longer co-terminated with a foot shock.

Freezing analysis

Freezing during tone presentations (30 s; [30]) was manually scored using videos from conditioning sessions (1, 2, 8, and 14), ERP sessions, and the extinction test. Freezing was counted if bouts occurred within the tone period (30 s). If freezing continued beyond the end of the tone or started prior to the tone, bouts were truncated to the end or start of the tone period, respectively. Any freezing occurring on the platform and freezing bouts off the platform <2 s were excluded from analyses based on previous work [31]. Percent of time spent freezing was examined (i.e. per 3-trial block) and over the total session (sum of freezing bout durations over total number of 30 s trials). The same analysis protocols were undertaken for freezing on the platform.

Immediate early gene analysis: tissue collection and processing

Mice from the 0.24 mA cohort were deeply anesthetized with ketamine 60 min after the first tone in the extinction test [as per previous PMA studies [32]], and transcardially perfused with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA; Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) in 0.1 M phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Brains were post-fixed in PFA overnight and subsequently transferred to a 30% sucrose solution with 0.1% sodium azide (Sigma) for a minimum of 48 h. Brains were frozen on dry ice and sliced into 35 µm sections on a Leica cryostat. Free-floating sections were stored in PBS with 0.1% sodium azide at 4 °C until used for immunohistochemistry.

Immunohistochemistry

Tissue was stained to detect c-Fos (rabbit anti c-Fos, 1:5000; Millipore, Burlington, MA) using methods similar to previous studies [23]; see Supplementary Methods for full details.

Microscopy and analysis

Slides were imaged at 20x using a slide-scanning scope (VS120, Olympus). c-Fos was automatically detected and quantified using CellSense in the regions of interest (ROIs): lateral orbitofrontal cortex (lOFC), medial orbitofrontal cortex (mOFC), prelimbic cortex (PrL), infralimbic cortex (IL), ventral striatum (VMS), and nucleus accumbens core (NAC C) and shell (NAC S). ROIs were selected a priori based on previous studies showing evidence of their dysregulation in patients with OCD during avoidance [15, 33] and in rat models of persistent avoidance [12, 32]. c-Fos density was calculated using the average c-Fos count within a 300 × 300 µm area in each hemisphere for 2–3 sections per animal, and slice thickness was incorporated into calculations for cells/mm3.

Statistical analysis

As KOs are well-documented to have increased grooming compared to WTs [18–21], a one-tailed t-test was used to detect genotype differences in time spent grooming. Lever suppression was calculated across the session or within three-trial bins (ERP and extinction test). Three-way ANOVAs [genotype x shock conditioning x time] were used for initial analyses of platform-mediated avoidance conditioning (i.e. lever suppression, percent of time spent on the platform, and freezing), ERP (lever suppression and freezing), and extinction test sessions (pre-post ERP platform avoidance). Geisser-Greenhouse corrections for sphericity (ε) were applied where applicable. Two-way repeated measures ANOVA or restricted maximum likelihood (REML) with Geisser-Greenhouse corrections were used to detect differences in press rate in VI-30 training. Genotype differences in overall lever suppression, lever suppression following the first tone, platform avoidance, and freezing during the extinction test session were analyzed with two-way ANOVAs. Differences in c-Fos density across regions of interest by genotype were analyzed with a two-way ANOVA. Bonferroni post hoc tests for multiple comparisons were performed when significant interactions were detected. Pearson’s correlations were performed for regional c-Fos in each ROI to determine if there were associations between c-Fos expression and lever suppression to the first tone in the extinction test. 4 mice that did not acquire avoidance in 14 days of conditioning were excluded from analyses (0.4 mA cohort: 2 KOs; 0.24 mA cohort: 1 WT and 1 KO). Grubb’s test was performed to identify outliers for each region (α = 0.05). One subject (KO) was excluded from c-Fos analyses in mOFC because its value was a statistical outlier (>2 standard deviations above the mean). GraphPad Prism 10.6 was used for all analyses.

Results

Sapap3-KOs displayed an excessive grooming phenotype, exhibiting significantly longer time spent grooming than WT littermate controls (Fig. 1B one-tailed t-test t(22) = 3.5, p = 0.0011). Mice were trained to lever press for reward pellets, acquiring significantly higher press rates over a two-week training period at VI-30 with similar performance between genotypes (Supplementary Fig. 1A, B).

Increased suppression of reward-seeking in Sapap3-KOs during platform-avoidance conditioning

Following reward acquisition, mice were trained to mount a platform during a 30 s tone period to avoid either a 0.4 mA or 0.24 mA foot shock that co-terminated with the tone (Fig. 1A). During platform avoidance, rewards remained accessible throughout the session; however, rewards could not be accessed while the mouse was on the platform, producing a motivational conflict. Mice demonstrated shock-conditioning dependent changes in reward seeking during platform avoidance conditioning, with greater suppression in reward seeking during the aversive tone (i.e. lever suppression) maintained in the 0.4 mA conditioned cohort vs. the 0.24 mA cohort (Fig. 1C: three-way ANOVA: session F (4.663, 102.6) = 16.24, p < 0.0001 ε = 0.36; session x shock level F (4.663, 102.6) = 2.476, p = 0.0404), ε = 0.36). In addition, Sapap3-KOs exhibited greater lever suppression throughout conditioning compared to WTs (Fig. 1C: three-way ANOVA: genotype F (1, 22) = 12.41, p = 0.0019). Despite these differences in reward-seeking during the tone, no differences were observed between groups in acquisition of platform avoidance across the conditioning period, as measured by time spent on the platform (Fig. 1D: three-way ANOVA : session F (3.514, 77.32) = 23.26, p < 0.0001 ε = 0.70; genotype: F (1, 22) = 0.698, ns; shock level: F (1, 22) = 2.816, ns, no significant interactions) and latency to mount the platform (Supplementary Fig. 2).

To determine if other threat-related behaviors could account for the genotype differences in lever suppression, freezing off the platform (Fig. 1E) was measured during conditioning. Freezing off the platform decreased with platform avoidance acquisition (Fig. 1E: three-way ANOVA: session F (1.155, 25.41) = 29.11, p < 0.0001 ε = 0.58; shock level F (1, 22) = 0.6110, ns) and was greater in Sapap3-KOs (Fig. 1E: three-way ANOVA: genotype F (1, 22) = 15.91, p = 0.0006; session x genotype: F (1.155, 25.41) = 10.44, p = 0.0024). Follow-up analyses of freezing collapsed between cohorts confirmed that elevated freezing in KOs was significant, particularly during early platform avoidance conditioning (Fig. 1E: two-way ANOVA: session F (1.148, 27.54) = 31.79, p < 0.0001; genotype F (1, 24) = 17.49, p = 0.003; interaction session x genotype F (1.148, 27.54) = 11.81, p = 0.0013; Bonferroni post hoc: day 2 KO vs. WT p = 0.005, day 8 KO vs. WT p = 0.089). No differences in freezing were observed prior to conditioning, suggesting that elevated KO freezing was not due to differences in baseline freezing behaviors between genotypes.

In addition to freezing behaviors occurring off the platform, freezing while on the platform was quantified during conditioning. On-platform freezing behavior was elevated in KOs throughout conditioning, and was also higher in the 0.4 mA conditioned cohort (Fig. 1F: three-way ANOVA: session F (1.367, 30.07) = 0.08933, ns, ε = 0.68; genotype F (1, 22) = 15.67, p = 0.0007; shock-level F (1, 22) = 16.64, p = 0.0005; genotype x shock level F (1, 22) = 10.54, p = 0.0037). These effects were driven by the KOs in the 0.4 mA conditioning cohort, with no differences in freezing found between WTs based on shock level (Fig. 1F: two-way ANOVA: genotype F (1, 22) = 15.67, p = 0.0007; shock level F (1, 22) = 16.64, p = 0.0005; interaction of genotype and shock level F (1, 22) = 10.54, p = 0.0037; Bonferroni post hoc: KO vs WT: 0.4 mA p = 0.0003, 0.24 mA ns; 0.4 mA vs. 0.24 mA: KOs p = 0.0002, WTs ns).

Continued suppression of lever pressing in KOs following ERP

Finally, mice were tested in an extinction test session following ERP, in which the platform was available, but no foot shocks were delivered (Fig. 3A). However, when examining reward-seeking during the tone, lever suppression trended towards differences between KOs and WTs, and was greater with conditioning at the higher shock amplitude (Fig. 3C: two-way ANOVA: genotype F (1, 22) = 3.931, p = 0.06; shock level F (1, 22) = 6.199, p = 0.021; genotype x shock level F (1, 22) = 2.157, ns). Reduced mOFC c-Fos activation in Sapap3-KOs following ERP

To identify whether any cortical or striatal regions that are relevant to OCD pathophysiology and avoidance learning were differentially activated in KOs during platform avoidance, tissue from the 0.24 mA conditioning cohort was collected to quantify expression of the immediate early gene c-Fos (an indirect static measure of neuronal activation) associated with exposure to the first tone during the extinction test (Fig. 4A, S3A-C). Discussion

The current study adapted the PMA task with ERP for use in mice to examine: 1) changes in avoidance behavior and threat processing in the Sapap3-KO OCD-relevant mouse model, and 2) the impact of these changes on reward seeking behavior during motivational conflict. In addition, we examined expression of the immediate early gene c-Fos to identify candidate neural structures that may contribute to altered behavior in platform avoidance. We found robust increases in suppression of reward seeking in KOs during the shock-predictive cue, suggesting increased sensitivity of threat processing systems. Surprisingly, no differences were found in platform avoidance acquisition or extinction in Sapap3-KOs. However, there was evidence for distinct changes in extinction in KOs that were conditioning-shock-intensity and behavior-dependent. While KOs tested with a higher intensity shock (0.4 mA) showed elevated suppression of reward-seeking during the first tone of the extinction test session (suggestive of impaired extinction) and elevated freezing through the extinction test, KOs tested with a lower intensity shock (0.24 mA) had similar performance to WT mice. We also provide early evidence for the role of altered mOFC activity (as measured by c-Fos expression) in changes to threat processing and avoidance following ERP, which warrants further investigation in vivo.

OCD patients are biased towards appraising negative emotional stimuli [6], have faster acquisition from negative versus positive outcomes [5], and show more intolerance and distress during uncertainty, even in situations with low uncertainty or known risk [34]. We therefore wanted to examine avoidance learning in an OCD-relevant mouse model to begin to address mechanisms underlying threat- and risk-assessment. We found that platform avoidance acquisition is similar in WTs and Sapap3-KOs, with both genotypes acquiring platform avoidance responses within two weeks of training. This suggests that constitutive deletion of the Sapap3 gene does not impact active avoidance learning These effects were surprising, given that a recent study found increased platform avoidance in KOs trained with mild (0.20 mA) footshocks [28]. However, WT mice in that study showed poor acquisition of platform avoidance with no significant difference in time on the platform on the first versus the last day of conditioning. In contrast, both genotypes learned the task within 14 days in our study, which could in part explain our observed differences.

Despite the similarities in platform avoidance behavior between genotypes, our results clearly demonstrate that KOs rapidly reached near-complete suppression of lever pressing during the aversive tone in both cohorts, regardless of shock magnitude. In contrast, WT mice tended to take more risks to press the lever for rewards during the 30 s tone, pressing as much as 50% of their baseline press rate in the low intensity cohort for much of conditioning. This suggests that KOs are more sensitive to threats and suppress reward-seeking for lower-intensity shocks, while WTs balance reward/threat conflict. Critically, KOs were well-trained in reward acquisition prior to PMA conditioning, reaching press rates comparable to WTs, ruling out the possibility that deficits in baseline reward seeking explain our results [as has been seen in other studies [6, 35]]. This observation of increased suppression of lever pressing in KO mice, even when other threat behaviors like freezing and avoidance were similar between the genotypes in late conditioning, raises the question of what behaviors KOs were doing when they were not pressing during the tone. Anecdotally there were signs of ‘indecisive’ behavior– i.e., mice weren’t freezing, but were orienting or moving between the lever and platform and seeming ‘stuck’ between the choices to lever press and avoid during the tone. Consistent with this finding, patients with OCD are generally more risk averse [[36], but see [37]], and risk aversion is associated with reduced neural activity to rewards in cortico-striatal regions [38, 39]. This risk aversion and avoidance behavior is thought to significantly contribute to the reduced quality of life seen in OCD by interfering with normal daily activities [40]. These findings highlight the value of testing reward conflict in the PMA task using suppression of lever pressing during the tone, and build on recent publications in mice that 1) developed a platform avoidance task without reward conflict [41], and 2) did not report on suppression of lever pressing during platform avoidance conditioning in Sapap3-KOs [28].

Following PMA acquisition and expression, KOs and WTs were trained on ERP to determine if KOs would have impaired extinction learning, consistent with phenotypes observed in fear conditioning paradigms and avoidance extinction with access to the avoidance response [6, 28]. In contrast to another recent study in Sapap3-KOs that performed extinction with platform access, we opted to use an extinction with response prevention procedure that blocked platform access, as has been used in several recent studies from the Quirk lab [12, 14, 16]. This extinction approach attempts to model key features of ERP, a first line therapy for OCD; it also resembles variants of “flooding” procedures used by Baum and colleagues to enhance avoidance extinction by blocking or removing the avoidance area [in this case, a shelf in the operant chamber to jump onto [42]]. Importantly, in both “flooding” and platform barrier extinction protocols, the subject is forced to experience the change in contingency (i.e. that the tone no longer ends in footshock) by extinguishing the Pavlovian associations between the threat cue and punishment (i.e. shock). In contrast, if the platform is kept in the chamber as in more classic extinction procedures, avoidance actions can still be performed, which can interfere with opportunities for new learning and lead to greater variability across subjects. Implementing ERP, rather than classic extinction procedures with access to the platform, ensures that mice have similar levels of exposure to the new contingency (i.e. cue does not lead to threat). In turn, this enforced exposure increases the likelihood of extinguishing fear responses [10], enabling distinction between heightened threat sensitivity and an inability to suppress inappropriate avoidance [10, 32]. In line with our predictions, during ERP, KOs were slower to reengage in reward-seeking during tone periods, suggesting delayed extinction. Specifically, conditioning with stronger foot shocks (0.4 mA) produced persistent suppression of lever pressing in KOs, as evidenced by genotype differences in suppression to the first tone in the extinction test session. In contrast, the similar reduction of platform avoidance observed in KOs and controls following ERP (when comparing the end of conditioning and the extinction test sessions) indicates that conditioned avoidance responses were successfully extinguished in both groups following ERP. Our results differ from a previous study in Sapap3-KOs that performed extinction with continued platform access and found impaired extinction of platform avoidance [28]. As mentioned above, this is notable as ERP ensures the extinction contingency (removal of shock) is experienced by the subject [43], whereas extinction without response prevention (i.e. with platform access) can interfere with extinction learning, because the updated action-outcome contingencies will not be experienced if subjects continue to engage in avoid responses. The current study observed greater extinction of avoidance responses during the extinction test in both groups compared to the previous study in Sapap3-KOs, supporting the theory that ERP is more effective than traditional extinction procedures at producing avoidance extinction learning. Our findings of robust avoidance extinction but persistent fear-related behaviors (i.e. freezing, lever suppression) in Sapap3-KOs support earlier work suggesting that “blocking” methods of extinction (i.e. forced prevention of avoidance responses) can reduce conditioned avoidance actions without extinguishing fear-related behaviors [44], thus reinforcing the need for future exploration of therapeutic mechanisms of ERP in OCD. Future work should also investigate the effects of OCD-relevant pharmacological treatments, such as selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, on Sapap3-KO behaviors during platform mediated avoidance to see if these interventions facilitate successful extinction learning.

Deletion of Sapap3 may produce a phenotype with heightened passive, rather than active, defensive behaviors, due to an inability to flexibly switch defensive strategies, as seen by elevated suppression of reward-seeking in Sapap3-KOs. Consistent with this model and prior fear conditioning studies in the Sapap3-KO model, we saw heightened freezing responses during avoidance conditioning [6, 28]. Specifically, we found increased freezing in KOs during early conditioning prior to learning platform avoidance and maintained freezing behaviors to threat cues in KOs, suggesting continued bias towards passive threat responses in this model. However, freezing responses did decrease as mice learned to mount the platform in both KOs and WTs, consistent with extensive literature showing that active avoidance requires a process of fear reduction to switch from passive (i.e. freezing) to active (i.e. flight, escape) responses [45, 46]. In our studies, KOs reverted to freezing during ERP when platform entries were prevented. Following conditioning at higher shock amplitudes, KOs froze more frequently during tones throughout ERP and extinction test sessions, demonstrating impaired extinction of passive threat responses as in previous studies [6, 28], and suggesting that general fear responses to the warning tone had not fully extinguished during ERP. In contrast, following conditioning with lower shock amplitudes, KOs exhibited increased freezing responses in early ERP which extinguished by the end of ERP - although they displayed slightly delayed extinction relative to controls. Interestingly, changes in freezing and suppression of lever pressing in KOs showed distinct profiles across conditioning, ERP, and extinction, suggesting that heightened freezing does not explain persistent elevation of suppression of lever pressing during late conditioning. This gradual reduction in freezing across conditioning contrasts to the recent PMA study in Sapap3-KOs, which found elevated freezing to tones in KOs throughout avoidance conditioning [28]. That study included freezing while on the platform, which could contribute to the discrepancy; freezing behaviors we observe on the platform are more in line with the St. Laurent study, with conditioning at higher shock producing persistent platform freezing in KOs. In addition, excessive grooming behavior is a key phenotype of Sapap3-KOs, and greater amounts of time spent grooming, rather than engaging in reward-seeking or active avoidance, could explain the increased lever suppression we see throughout the task despite the similar platform avoidance behaviors exhibited by WTs and KOs. However, because grooming could not be measured during our operant sessions due to camera angle and video quality, future experiments will be needed to test this possibility. Together, the propensity for persistent passive threat responses and reduced reward-seeking supports our conclusion that KOs are more biased towards avoiding negative stimuli at the cost of obtaining rewards, even following ERP when active threat responses have been extinguished.

To identify cortical and striatal regions that may be both involved in regulating conflict-based avoidance and differentially active in Sapap3-KOs, we quantified c-Fos expression following the extinction test session in mice trained on the low intensity 0.24 mA shock; this group was selected because they displayed the most pronounced genotype differences in lever press suppression during conditioning, but successfully extinguished platform avoidance following ERP. We found region-specific genotype differences in c-Fos expression, providing preliminary evidence for decreased c-Fos in Sapap3-KOs vs WTs after the barrier was removed following ERP in mOFC – a region important for integrating environmental cues and value in decision-making. Given Sapap3-KOs in the 0.24 mA cohort successfully extinguished following ERP, this may reflect that decreased engagement of mOFC supports their successful extinction. This may be consistent with observations of mOFC hyperactivity during initial avoidance acquisition in patients with OCD [33] that decreases during avoidance overtraining [15]; an additional study has similarly reported decreased OFC activity following ERP in OCD patients [47]. In addition to genotype differences, we identified associations between lower c-Fos density in the mOFC and decreased lever suppression during the first tone after ERP training, as well as a trend towards IL c-Fos positively correlating with lever suppression. These findings may reflect a potential role of disengagement of these regions to facilitate expression of reward seeking during the tone following successful extinction. Preclinical studies have identified an important role of mOFC and IL in regulating freezing behaviors in fear conditioning and fear extinction [11, 48, 49]. mOFC also plays an important role in risk/reward decision making, with mOFC inactivation biasing towards risky choices in a reward-based probabilistic discounting task [50], as well as reducing response perseveration [51]; this may be relevant to our findings of lower suppression of lever pressing in animals with reduced mOFC c-Fos. However, in contrast to our findings, studies in human participants have found positive associations between successful fear extinction and vmPFC activity (encompassing mOFC) [52], and IL inactivation increases lever suppression in PMA in rats [29]. Therefore, more work is needed to determine the role of these and other structures in flexible avoidance behavior under motivational conflict [14, 16, 53]. Our lack of significant effects in the IL could be due to differences in extinction procedures in our study. Previous studies show IL activity is negatively correlated with reinstated platform avoidance following extinction training with the platform present and accessible, while no correlations with IL c-Fos and reinstated platform avoidance were observed when the platform was removed from the chamber for extinction training [32]. Our procedure of preventing platform mounting with a barrier is more consistent with the procedure in the latter example, which may explain why we did not observe significant correlations in the IL. Thus, subtle differences in extinction learning protocols may differentially activate specific cortical regions. Furthermore, given the 0.4 mA cohort’s differences in freezing and lever suppression at the extinction test, it will be important to examine neural differences in threat encoding in Sapap3-KOs conditioned with higher shock intensities; lack of these data is a limitation of our findings. Future investigations examining neural activity in vivo across avoidance acquisition, ERP, and extinction tests will be valuable to help address these discrepancies. Taken together, our findings and those in the literature support the idea that mOFC and IL are capable of influencing distinct aspects of post ERP behavior in the platform-mediated avoidance task and OCD relevant models.

Together, these studies lend further support to a growing body of evidence reporting dysfunctional and heightened threat related behaviors in Sapap3-KO mice [6, 28], a leading preclinical mouse model in OCD research. Building on these studies, we report heightened suppression of reward seeking in KO mice when they are tested under motivational conflict, which may be relevant to imbalances between threat and reward or goal-directed behaviors in people with OCD [7, 54, 55]. We also show that ERP procedures previously developed for PMA in rats to model OCD exposure therapy procedures can 1) also be implemented in mice, and 2) lead to extinction of passive threat and suppression of reward-seeking in mice conditioned with a 0.24 mA shock. However, a limitation of these findings is that Sapap3-KO mice did not exhibit excessive or persistent avoidance, contrary to our predictions. It is possible that the Sapap3-KO line does not have a maladaptive active avoidance phenotype, though excessive avoidance in Sapap3-KOs is reported in other studies [28]. It is notable that our study did not address changes during PMA acquisition and extinction in the predominant compulsive phenotype of Sapap3-KOs - excessive grooming behaviors [21], which can be exacerbated under stress [56]. It is therefore possible that differences in grooming behaviors that better represent KO compulsivity and threat responses emerged in KOs during platform avoidance conditioning, ERP, and/or reinstatement of the platform. This possibility should be directly investigated in future studies.

When considering the translational relevance of PMA ERP, it is important to note that response prevention in clinical ERP is typically voluntary; the involuntary response prevention used in our PMA ERP procedure may therefore involve distinct neural processes. Despite this limitation, the successful extinction of platform avoidance with ERP protocols versus other extinction methods is notable and warrants further investigation. ERP is inherently a Pavlovian extinction procedure, as instrumental avoidance responses cannot be performed, and thus extinction recall (in the extinction test) may involve Pavlovian-Instrumental transfer effects to maintain suppression of avoidance responses. In agreement with this model, a previous study has shown that Pavlovian extinction of tone-shock pairings prior to pairing the tone with shuttle avoidance responses is sufficient to reduce shuttle responses in rats [57]. The extinction of platform avoidance in the Sapap3-KO line suggests that Pavlovian extinction of fear responses transfers to expression of instrumental avoidance responses, consistent with clinical observations of intact aversive Pavlovian-instrumental transfer in OCD [58]. However, while we overall found significant reductions in platform avoidance, the maintained expression of fear related behaviors in Sapap3-KOs suggests that there are differences in threat encoding and fear extinction which should be investigated in this model, as has been reported in previous studies [6]. It will therefore be important to replicate our findings with sample sizes powered to detect if subsets of Sapap3-KOs are resistant to ERP extinction learning, and whether this correlates with other behaviors (i.e. grooming and freezing).

Sapap3-KO mice that showed successful extinction with ERP also showed preliminary evidence for reduced engagement of mOFC, which may support extinction following ERP. Furthermore, our findings and those of St Laurent et al., who used a PMA paradigm followed by extinction via platform access in Sapap3-KO mice, highlight that differences in extinction procedures may produce distinct effects on expression of avoidance, freezing, and reward seeking under motivational conflict. Importantly, only male mice were used in the current studies, so generalizability of these results to females must be tested in future studies [However, note that prior work from our lab and others has found no evidence for sex differences across any phenotype tested in Sapap3-KOs [19, 23, 59]]. Consistent with our findings, both preclinical and neuroimaging studies of fear conditioning and active avoidance suggest that medial prefrontal cortices are critical for gating the selection of passive and active defensive responses [11, 17, 48, 60]. In summary, our work provides further rationale for monitoring and parsing the role of medial prefrontal cortices in regulating active avoidance, acquisition, and extinction, and identifies the mOFC as a potential target for treatment for maladaptive, conflict-based avoidance in a transgenic model relevant to OCD.
