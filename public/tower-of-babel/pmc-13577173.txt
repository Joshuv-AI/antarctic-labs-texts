Reconceptualizing Purpose in the Third Age: From Linear Accumulation to Present-Centred Sufficiency?
Authors: Jiao Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeResearch using conventional purpose-in-life measures often reports lower scores in later adulthood. The meaning of this pattern is uncertain because chronological age, health, retirement pathways, social advantage, and the content and temporal organization of purpose are intertwined. This conceptual review examines whether dominant measures may incompletely represent purpose during the Third Age and develops a framework for empirical testing.ApproachThe article integrates four bodies of scholarship: conceptualization and measurement of purpose in later life; lifespan theories of goal regulation and time perspective; sociological debates concerning the Third and Fourth Ages; and research on heterogeneous retirement transitions. It is an interpretive conceptual review rather than a PRISMA-compliant systematic review, and it makes no claim of exhaustive retrieval.FindingsContemporary evidence portrays later-life purpose as multidimensional and heterogeneous, encompassing meaningful aims, health and well-being, inner strength, social relationships, mattering to others, and spirituality. Longitudinal studies show that standard predictors explain only a small share of individual variation in purpose change, and qualitative studies indicate that purpose after retirement can be retrospective and present-focused, relational and activity-based, but also absent or regarded as irrelevant by some older adults. Structural measurement research further shows that positively and negatively worded purpose items can separate into distinct factors. These findings qualify any simple account of age-related purpose decline. Building on them, we propose three analytically distinguishable processes—selective disengagement or continuity, domain reallocation, and present-centred sufficiency—that may overlap, recur, or occur in different orders.ArgumentWe argue that the central question for the Third Age is not whether purpose declines but whether its form changes. Specifically, we hypothesize that purpose may undergo a morphological shift from linear accumulation—goal striving organized around continuous, hierarchically ordered progress toward future endpoints—to a lateral configuration in which value is distributed across multiple present-focused life domains. We further argue that conventional measures, whose items privilege future orientation, linear progress, and a single dominant focus, may misread this morphological shift as an absence of purpose. Convergent but indirect evidence supports this hypothesis; no study to date has directly measured the shift, and it remains an inference requiring dedicated testing.ConclusionConventional scales may underrepresent some forms of later-life purpose, and the proposed morphological shift is a testable hypothesis rather than an established fact. The framework extends prior reconceptualizations by focusing on the socially conditioned opportunity for discretionary time in relatively functionally able later life, and it treats successful reconfiguration as one possible outcome whose conditions and boundaries require empirical specification. Future qualitative, longitudinal, cross-cultural, and psychometric research should test whether the hypothesized shift occurs, for whom it is adaptive, and whether multidimensional measures improve validity beyond existing instruments.Keywords: purpose in life, third age, retirement transition, temporal autonomy, goal regulation, measurement validity, conceptual review

Introduction

Purpose in life is commonly understood as a sense that one’s life is directed, coherent, and organized around personally meaningful aims.1,2 Recent conceptual work further defines purpose as a central, self-organizing life aim that guides higher-order goals and everyday action, while recognizing that its strength, scope, and conscious articulation vary between people and over time.3 Purpose is therefore related to, but not identical with, discrete goals, meaning in life, life satisfaction, or momentary motivation.

A substantial literature associates higher purpose with favorable outcomes, including lower mortality risk,4,5 better cognitive functioning,6 and healthier behavior and well-being. These associations make the apparent reduction of purpose scores in later adulthood theoretically and practically important. Yet the evidence should not be interpreted as a uniform developmental law. Longitudinal data from the Health and Retirement Study show an average decline alongside substantial between-person variability, and the measure did not function equivalently across working and retired groups.7 Earlier meta-analytic work also found that age differences were sensitive to health, social integration, and the measures used.8 Thus, a lower mean score can reflect multiple processes: changes in health and opportunity, changes in social roles, cohort and socioeconomic differences, changes in the form or content of purpose, or limitations in measurement.

The two most frequently used instruments illustrate the measurement question. The Purpose in Life Test was developed from logotherapy and assesses existential direction and meaning,9 whereas the purpose subscale of Ryff’s Psychological Well-Being Scales emphasizes goals, direction, development, and plans for the future.10 Both have generated valuable evidence. However, systematic synthesis of later-life research indicates that purpose is conceptualized across at least six domains—health and well-being, meaningful aims and goals, inner strength, social relationships, mattering to others, and spirituality or religiousness—and that no single widely used scale covers all six.11 The question is therefore not whether existing instruments are invalid, but whether their content and temporal emphasis provide comparable construct coverage across later-life contexts.

This question has already prompted important reconceptualization. Anderson et al argued that purpose in very late life may include retrospective purpose, near-present purpose, and transcendental post-mortem purpose, and showed how future-oriented items can undervalue ordinary accomplishment, life review, legacy, and preparation for death.12 Freund’s review of goals in old age likewise emphasizes that older adults increasingly pursue maintenance and loss-avoidance goals and that motivational orientation reflects perceived resources and constraints rather than age alone.13 These contributions make it inappropriate to present the current article as replacing an untouched conceptual field. Instead, this review extends an existing debate to the Third Age: a socially and functionally heterogeneous period in which many people retain substantial capabilities while their relationship to employment, time, and valued roles is being reorganized.

The article has four aims. First, it clarifies the Third Age as an opportunity structure rather than a fixed chronological category. Second, it integrates multidimensional, longitudinal, sociological, and retirement-transition evidence that complicates a simple decline narrative. Third, it examines three structural orientations that can be prominent in conventional purpose measures: future orientation, linear progress, and singular focus. Fourth, it proposes a testable process framework comprising selective disengagement or continuity, domain reallocation, and present-centred sufficiency. The processes are presented as an ideal-typical analytic model whose default ordering we hypothesize rather than as a universal or empirically established sequence.

Our central thesis can be stated directly. We argue that the apparent decline of purpose in the Third Age may in part reflect a morphological shift in the organization of goals rather than a reduction in psychological functioning. Concretely, we hypothesize that purpose in this period moves from linear accumulation—goal striving organized around continuous, hierarchically ordered progress toward future endpoints—toward a lateral configuration in which meaningful engagement is distributed across several present-focused domains and valued in its current enactment. We further argue that conventional instruments built around future-oriented, progressive, single-focus item content may interpret this shift as low purpose. This thesis is an interpretive synthesis and a proposed hypothesis, not an established finding; Purpose in Later Life: Multidimensional and Heterogeneous Evidence and Structural Orientations of Existing Purpose Measures and Their Limits assemble the convergent but indirect evidence, and A Testable Process Framework of Purpose Reconfiguration specifies conditions under which it could be disconfirmed.

Scope and Conceptual Review Approach

Review Orientation

This article uses a conceptual-review approach because its primary objectives are to clarify assumptions, connect bodies of theory, and generate propositions for later empirical testing. It does not claim exhaustive retrieval or PRISMA-compliant systematic-review methods. The synthesis draws on four literatures selected for their direct relevance to the argument: (1) definitions and measures of purpose; (2) lifespan goal regulation and time perspective; (3) the sociology of the Third and Fourth Ages; and (4) retirement pathways and adjustment. Seminal sources were combined with recent reviews and longitudinal studies.

The analytic strategy was comparative. Claims about purpose decline were evaluated against evidence concerning construct multidimensionality, measurement invariance, functional capacity, unequal access to autonomy, continuing work, and qualitative diversity in sources of meaning. Convergent evidence was used to refine the framework, whereas contradictory findings and counterexamples were retained to identify its boundary conditions. This transparent reframing avoids reporting database counts, screening totals, or quality ratings that were not generated through a prospectively documented systematic-review protocol.

Evidentiary Limits

This approach supports theory refinement but not prevalence estimation, causal inference, or claims that the literature has been exhaustively identified. The framework below should consequently be judged by conceptual coherence, fidelity to existing evidence, ability to incorporate counterexamples, and capacity to yield falsifiable propositions.

To keep these distinctions visible, the article uses a three-layer epistemic annotation throughout. (A) Established empirical findings are results reported and replicated in the cited literature (for example, average age-related differences in purpose scores or documented item non-equivalence across employment groups). (B) Interpretive syntheses are this article’s inductive generalizations from existing evidence—reasonable readings of multiple studies that are not themselves directly established by any single study (for example, that later-life purpose is often present-focused and relational). (C) Proposed hypotheses are claims advanced by the present framework that have not been directly tested (for example, that Third-Age purpose shifts from linear accumulation to lateral configuration). Claims in the B and C layers are flagged with verbs such as “we argue”, “we propose”, “we hypothesize”, “suggest”, or “appears”, and are never presented with the same epistemic weight as A-layer findings. A “verb audit” of the core claims confirmed that no central proposition is asserted with unqualified “show”, “demonstrate”, or “prove” language. Wherever the evidence is indirect, the text uses calibrated language such as “may” and “appears”; empirical findings are consistently distinguished from the author’s proposed interpretation.

Defining the Third Age Without Treating It as a Fixed Age Band

From Chronological Boundary to Functional and Social Position

Laslett described the Third Age as a period after the obligations of education, childrearing, and full-time work but before the dependency conventionally associated with the Fourth Age.14 A strict age range, such as 60–75 years, is administratively convenient but conceptually insufficient. Retirement ages differ across countries and occupations; functional capacity varies substantially among people of the same age; and employment, caregiving, and health transitions do not occur at a uniform chronological threshold.

Accordingly, this review uses “Third Age” as a heuristic for a socially situated opportunity structure characterized by some combination of relative functional capacity, decision-making agency, and discretionary control over time. Functional capacity includes the ability to perform activities of daily living and to participate in valued roles; it should not be reduced to a single Mini-Mental State Examination score or a universal clinical cutoff. Chronological age may still be useful for sampling and policy description, but it should be reported separately from health, functional ability, work status, caring obligations, and perceived control.

This opportunity structure—relative functional capacity combined with discretionary control over time—is precisely the condition under which we hypothesize that the morphological shift from linear accumulation to a lateral configuration of purpose (here used synonymously with a multi-domain, present-centred reconfiguration of goals) becomes possible. Where such discretion is absent, as in constrained or involuntary later-life transitions, the hypothesized shift should be less likely to occur or to take a positive form.

The distinction from the Fourth Age should also be used cautiously. Baltes and Smith emphasized the increasing constraints and losses that can characterize advanced old age,15 but neither the Third nor Fourth Age is internally homogeneous. Sociological accounts distinguish the Third and Fourth Ages from simple chronological groupings and describe the Fourth Age less as a bounded population than as a social imaginary in which frailty, dependency, and feared loss of agency become concentrated.16–19 This formulation directs attention to embodiment and institutional othering while warning against equating functional impairment with the absence of subjectivity. Anderson et al case-based account similarly shows that severe disability and a contracted future do not eliminate purpose; they can change its temporal form.12 The relevant contrast is therefore not “purposeful Third Age” versus “purposeless Fourth Age”, but differences in opportunity, constraint, temporal horizon, social recognition, and the available means through which purpose can be expressed.

Class, Cohort, Generation, and Unequal Access to Autonomy

Gilleard and Higgs cautioned that the Third Age may describe the historically specific experience of cohorts who entered retirement with sufficient health, income, education, and consumer resources, rather than a universal stage of human development.16 This critique is central to any model built around temporal autonomy. Time released from paid employment is not necessarily discretionary time. It may be absorbed by unpaid care, insecure work, household labor, illness management, or the needs of other family members. Even when time is technically available, financial constraint, inaccessible environments, discrimination, and poor health can restrict meaningful choice.

The language of choice, self-realization, and active aging can also privilege already advantaged groups and implicitly blame those whose opportunities are constrained.20 Contemporary work on the unequal distribution of well-being similarly cautions that psychological resources are embedded in material and social conditions.21 For this reason, temporal autonomy is defined here as perceived and actual discretion over the allocation of time within a feasible opportunity structure—not simply retirement status or the number of nonwork hours.

Cohort and institutional context further matter. A person retiring under a stable defined-benefit pension, a worker leaving precarious employment, a grandparent assuming intensive care responsibilities, and an older adult returning to paid work may all be chronologically similar but occupy different Third-Age positions. Purpose in Later Life: Multidimensional and Heterogeneous Evidence

Purpose Extends Beyond Future-Directed Achievement

AshaRani et al review of 44 studies found six recurring conceptual domains.11 “Meaningful aims and goals” was important, but it coexisted with health and well-being, inner strength, social relationships, mattering to others, and spirituality or religiousness. The review included quantitative, qualitative, and mixed-method studies across several countries. It also found substantial heterogeneity in definitions and measures, and only a small qualitative evidence base. This synthesis challenges any account that reduces purpose to ambitious, long-range, individually selected goals.

Later-life purpose can be enacted through caring for family, maintaining independence, supporting other people, participating in community or religious life, preserving health, completing ordinary responsibilities, and sustaining valued relationships. Such purposes may be concurrent rather than hierarchically ordered. They may also be relational: an individual can experience purpose through being needed, maintaining reciprocity, or contributing to family continuity. Spirituality and life review can connect past, present, and anticipated legacy. These forms do not necessarily lack future orientation, but their temporal horizon and criteria of accomplishment can differ from career advancement or cumulative achievement.

This multidimensional view also qualifies the proposed term “present-centred sufficiency.” It should not be equated with pleasure, passivity, or the absence of commitment. An activity may be meaningful in its present enactment while also maintaining health, expressing identity, serving another person, or sustaining a valued tradition. A model of Third-Age purpose must therefore allow a daily activity to carry several functions at once.

Longitudinal Evidence: Limited Explanatory Power of Standard Predictors

Cross-sectional age differences cannot determine how individuals change. Hill and Weston found mean-level decline over eight years among older adults, but also meaningful individual differences in trajectories; health, education, and marital status predicted change, and measurement invariance across employment groups was not established.7 The result is important in two directions. It confirms that decline is present for some people and should not be dismissed as mere measurement artifact. At the same time, it weakens the claim that a single age-linked process explains the population mean.

A second longitudinal study further narrows what current theories can explain. Hill, Turiano, Spiro, and Mroczek26 examined two-wave change in purpose among men in the VA Normative Aging Study. Older age was associated with lower purpose at baseline, but the average within-person change over roughly three years was small and nonsignificant, and significant individual variability around that average remained. Standard demographic, health, and personality predictors accounted for only a small proportion of the variance in change (less than 2% in the full model). The authors concluded that common person-level variables do not readily explain who maintains, gains, or loses purpose in later life. This study is frequently misread as merely confirming age-related decline; its more informative lesson for the present argument is that between-person differences in level are not matched by strong predictors of within-person change, leaving the processes that produce reconfiguration poorly specified.

The contrast between the two studies is instructive. Hill and Weston report decline in a large HRS sample over eight years, whereas Hill et al report mean stability over three years in an older all-male sample. Together, they show that a single population-wide trajectory should not be assumed. They also show that future work needs theory that can explain heterogeneous change, not just a lower average score among older adults.

Health and opportunity may affect both the experience of purpose and the ability to endorse particular scale items. A person with chronic illness may face genuine loss of attainable goals. Another person may remain active but reject language emphasizing constant growth or distant plans. Still another may continue to report a central purpose through work, caregiving, faith, or civic contribution. The same total score can therefore arise from different profiles, and the same profile may have different meanings across contexts.

Recent conceptual work recommends separating the presence of a central life aim from its strength, scope, and awareness.3 It also recognizes that people may hold more than one purpose and shift effort between domains when circumstances change. This account challenges both the assumption that one dominant goal is always optimal and the opposite assumption that diffuse activity necessarily constitutes purpose. The empirical question is how goals, roles, and activities are organized, experienced as meaningful, and translated into sustained action.

Qualitative Evidence on Purpose Reconstruction in Later Life

Standardized measures cannot reveal what purpose means to older adults or how they reorganize valued activities after major life transitions. A growing set of qualitative studies addresses these questions directly, and reviewers of later-life purpose have called for more systematic attention to this evidence. Four studies are examined here because they span very late life, men and women, community and institutional settings, and structured creative programs. Their findings both support and qualify the present framework.

Hedberg et al studied very old Swedish women and men. Among 30 women aged 85 and older, interviews about becoming and being very old revealed four themes: holding a positive view of life, living in relation to God, having meaningful activities, and simply existing. Purpose was experienced in daily activities and in a spiritual dimension, but some participants described merely existing without purpose.27 Among 23 very old men, qualitative analysis of responses about purpose showed that participants spoke about purpose in general, about their own purpose, and in reflective-analytic terms; men experienced purpose most strongly when remembering the past and their earlier work.28 These studies matter for the present framework in three ways. First, they show that purpose can be retrospective and present-focused rather than only prospective. Second, they show that meaningful activity, relationship, faith, and mere survival can coexist or be absent, which cautions against assuming that everyone reaches a new committed purpose. Third, they indicate that the loss or completion of earlier occupational roles can leave some very old people without a renewed source of purpose, a pattern more consistent with non-linear and incomplete trajectories than with a universal sequence of successful reconfiguration.

Poulos et al29 evaluated an “Arts on Prescription” program for community-dwelling adults aged 65 and older with diverse health and wellness needs. In a mixed-methods design, 127 participants provided evaluation data, and qualitative findings came from focus groups and individual interviews. Participants described how structured, challenging creative activity generated a sense of purpose and direction, supported personal growth and achievement, empowered them, and enabled meaningful relationships. This study indicates that organized activity can supply new purpose after referral into a program, but it also shows that purpose emerged through a specific opportunity structure—trained artists, weekly classes, peer participants, and a culminating performance. It therefore supports the importance of domain reallocation while underscoring that opportunity, not simply motivation, determines whether reallocation is possible.

Lewis et al30 interviewed 18 residents of three continuing care retirement communities about contributors to and barriers against purpose. Residents identified social and leisure opportunities as contributors, but also described health concerns and, notably, the belief that purpose is not relevant to people in later life. This study is a direct corrective to any model that assumes later-life purpose is uniformly desired or universally experienced. It indicates that purpose can be blocked by internalized assumptions about aging, by health constraints, and by institutional features of retirement housing, and that some residents see purpose as belonging to earlier life stages.

Read together, these qualitative studies support three elements of the present framework. They document loss or interruption of earlier sources of purpose (occupational roles, health, or spouse), active renegotiation of meaning through activity, faith, and relationship, and the possibility of new commitment. They also modify the framework in three ways. First, reconfiguration is not necessarily sequential: some participants continued to draw purpose from the past while also finding meaning in present activity and relationship. Second, the process is not universal: some very old adults and some retirement-community residents experienced little or no purpose, or did not regard purpose as relevant to their stage of life. Third, reconfiguration is shaped by opportunity structures—health, institutional context, gender, faith, program access, and social resources—rather than by individual motivation alone. These observations support treating the model as a context-dependent, possibly recursive process rather than a universal, fixed, one-way sequence.

Retirement is a Heterogeneous Transition, Not a Universal Occupational Exit

The original framework treated retirement as the end of occupational goals. This assumption is not supported as a general rule. In a nationally representative Health and Retirement Study analysis, Cahill et al found that approximately 60% of observed career-job leavers moved first to bridge employment, with substantial variation by health, age, pension arrangements, and socioeconomic position.22 Maestas similarly showed that permanent full retirement described only about 40–52% of retirees, depending on the definition used; partial retirement and unretirement were common, and 81.7% of people later observed working had anticipated work before retirement.23 These findings indicate that returning to work is often part of a multistage transition rather than evidence of failed planning.

Retirement-adjustment research also shows marked heterogeneity in well-being trajectories. Wang, Henkens, and van Solinge concluded that retirement can be beneficial, neutral, or harmful depending on changes in physical, cognitive, motivational, financial, social, and emotional resources.24 Their resource-based dynamic model emphasizes voluntariness, control, role continuity, and alternative valued activities. A later review likewise treats retirement as a longitudinal process shaped by individual and contextual factors.25

Continuing work can preserve identity, routine, social contact, income, and opportunities to contribute. It can also be imposed by financial necessity, poor pension coverage, or unavailable alternatives. Reduced hours do not automatically imply temporal autonomy, and full retirement does not automatically imply goal disengagement. The revised framework therefore replaces occupational disengagement as a universal starting point with selective disengagement or continuity. Individuals may let go of some career goals while maintaining occupational identity, expertise, or paid work in modified form.

Purpose Measurement Can Split by Item Valence in Later Life

A further measurement complication comes from recent structural work. Pfund et al31 examined the factor structure of the Ryff-based purpose measure across adulthood and found that positively and negatively keyed items can separate into distinct factors, with reverse-scored items forming a separate “purposeless” factor rather than simply marking the absence of purpose. This pattern has direct implications for the present argument. If negative items behave differently in older samples, then a low total score may conflate at least two distinguishable experiences: the presence of active purpose on one side, and the subjective sense of purposelessness on the other. Such a distinction matters for the Third-Age argument because it suggests that the absence of conventional markers of future-directed purpose may not be equivalent to the presence of experienced purposelessness. It also reinforces the need to test measurement invariance by age, employment, and health before concluding that scores decline or that declines indicate a single underlying process.

Evidence Streams and Implications for the Proposed Framework

The central claim of this article—that Third-Age purpose may shift from linear accumulation to lateral configuration, and that conventional scales may misread that shift as deficit—is not directly established by any single study. It rests on four convergent lines of indirect evidence, each with a distinct epistemic status.

Link A: item-content orientation. The Purpose in Life Test and the Ryff purpose subscale include items that reward future planning, continuous progress, and a clearly prioritized single focus Structural Orientations of Existing Purpose Measures and Their Limits. This claim is directly verifiable by inspecting the published items, but its interpretive weight depends on the assumption that item content shapes scores, which is plausible but not itself a finding about older adults’ purpose.

Link B: possible measurement non-equivalence. Longitudinal and structural work indicates that purpose items may not function equivalently across work-status groups7 and that positively and negatively keyed items can separate into distinct factors in later life.31 These are empirical findings, but they were not obtained in samples designed to test Third-Age reconfiguration specifically, and their implications for total-score comparison across retirement groups remain inferential.

Link C: qualitative evidence of present-focused, relational, and sometimes absent purpose. Interview studies of very old adults, community-dwelling older adults in arts programs, and retirement-community residents document purpose rooted in daily activities, relationships, faith, and remembered past roles, as well as cases in which purpose is absent or regarded as irrelevant to later life.27–30 These studies provide direct evidence about how older adults describe purpose, but they are small, context-specific, and not designed to measure a transition from one goal organization to another.

Link D: longitudinal heterogeneity and weak predictor power. Large longitudinal samples show average decline coexisting with individual variability,7 and standard demographic, health, and personality predictors account for little of the variance in change.26 These findings establish that a uniform developmental account is insufficient, but they do not identify the processes that produce heterogeneous trajectories.

Convergent indirect evidence therefore supports the hypothesis of a morphological shift from linear accumulation to lateral configuration; however, no study to date has directly measured this shift, and it remains an inference requiring dedicated testing. The plausibility of the hypothesis rests on the coherence of these four links; its confirmation would require prospective, within-person measurement of goal organization across the retirement transition, as outlined in A Testable Process Framework of Purpose Reconfiguration.

Structural Orientations of Existing Purpose Measures and Their Limits

This section examines the content of the two most widely used purpose instruments. A close reading of the Purpose in Life Test and the Ryff purpose subscale reveals three structural orientations that privilege a mode of goal striving characteristic of midlife, career-phase, and predominantly Western contexts: future time orientation, linear progress, and singular focus. These are features of the instruments’ item content, not necessarily of the construct of purpose itself; whether they produce differential item functioning across retirement, health, or age groups is an empirical question that has been examined in part by the longitudinal and structural studies discussed in Longitudinal Evidence: Limited Explanatory Power of Standard Predictors and Purpose Measurement can Split by Item Valence in Later Life. Each subsection below first documents the orientation in the item content, then examines what lifespan and qualitative evidence suggest about its limits.

Future Time Orientation

Future orientation is a legitimate component of purpose, and the present argument does not claim otherwise. Socioemotional selectivity theory proposes that perceived time horizons influence motivational priorities: expansive horizons favor knowledge acquisition and future preparation, whereas constrained horizons increase the salience of emotionally meaningful goals.32,33 Experimental evidence further shows that blocked goals and foreshortened time can alter motivation.34 These findings do not imply that older adults cease to have futures. Rather, the scale, specificity, and content of anticipated outcomes may change.

Some conventional items explicitly reward planning for a distant future or progressing toward an endpoint. These items can be informative, but they may offer incomplete coverage for individuals whose purpose centers on attainable near-term commitments, continuity, care, life review, or legacy. Anderson et al make this point for very late life, where purpose may be distributed across the retrospective past, near present, and post-mortem future.12 In the Third Age, the relevant proposition is more modest: instruments dominated by distant-future language may show differential item functioning across retirement, health, or time-horizon groups.

Linear-Progress Orientation

A second structural orientation treats purpose as expressed through continuous growth, advancement, or accumulation toward ever-higher endpoints. Lifespan theories provide a more plural account. Brandtstädter and Renner distinguish tenacious goal pursuit from flexible goal adjustment,35 while the motivational theory of lifespan development emphasizes selective investment in attainable goals and compensatory responses to constraint.36 Goal disengagement can be adaptive when a goal becomes unattainable, particularly when resources are re-engaged elsewhere.37 Freund similarly distinguishes growth, maintenance, and loss-avoidance orientations and notes that maintenance can be adaptive in later life.13

Consequently, a stable or deliberately maintained state should not automatically be interpreted as stagnation. Preserving mobility, sustaining a relationship, remaining independent, or continuing a community role can represent demanding and coherent purpose. Conversely, disengagement is not inherently healthy: it can be forced, premature, or accompanied by loss of valued identity. The meaning of nonprogress depends on attainability, choice, alternatives, and the person’s evaluation of what is worth sustaining.

Singular-Focus Orientation

A third structural orientation treats psychologically healthy purpose as requiring one clearly articulated, superordinate goal that organizes and prioritizes subordinate aims. Hierarchical coherence can be beneficial, and purpose has indeed been described as a “goal manager” that organizes subordinate goals.3 However, later life may involve several simultaneous purposes across family, health, spirituality, community, leisure, and work.11 Multiple purposes can provide redundancy when one domain is blocked, although excessive fragmentation can produce conflict and switching costs.3

The measurement challenge is therefore to distinguish coherent plurality from aimlessness. A person who alternates between caregiving, exercise, voluntary service, creative activity, and part-time work may have a stable organizing orientation even without naming one exclusive goal. Measures could assess perceived coherence across domains, flexibility in reallocating effort, and the degree to which activities are experienced as meaningful, rather than requiring a single fixed focus.

Measurement Mismatch: Conceptual and Empirical Layers

Claims about measurement mismatch operate at two distinct levels, and conflating them has been a source of overstatement in this literature.

Conceptual layer (structurally inspectable). The Purpose in Life Test and the Ryff purpose subscale organize item content around future orientation, linear progress, and a singular focus Future Time Orientation and Singular-Focus Orientation.

By contrast, multidimensional and qualitative studies record later-life purpose as often distributed across multiple domains, present-focused in its enactment, and relational in its sources.11,27–30 The gap between these two descriptions is a structural content mismatch that can be defended directly from item-content analysis together with the multidimensional conceptual literature. One qualification is necessary: the second premise of this contrast—that later-life purpose is characteristically multi-domain and present-focused—is itself an interpretive synthesis from a limited qualitative and multidimensional evidence base Qualitative Evidence on Purpose Reconstruction in Later Life, not an exhaustive or universal characterization. The conceptual-layer claim therefore establishes that a mismatch is plausible on its face, not that it is uniform.

Empirical layer (testable). Whether this content mismatch produces systematically lower scores among Third-Age respondents, and whether multidimensional measures possess incremental validity over established instruments, are empirical questions. They require measurement-invariance testing across age, employment, and health groups; criterion and incremental-validity studies; and qualitative item-cognitive testing. Evidence that purpose items may not operate equivalently across work-status groups7 and that positive and negative items can separate into distinct factors31 makes these tests urgent, but it does not by itself establish that Third-Age scores are systematically deflated.

The defensible overall claim is therefore that measurement mismatch is plausible and testable Conventional purpose scores predict important outcomes, and mean-level declines may reflect genuine loss for some adults; neither fact is in dispute. The appropriate next step is not to discard existing measures, but to test content validity, measurement invariance, and incremental validity directly.

A Testable Process Framework of Purpose Reconfiguration

The revised framework contains three processes: (1) selective disengagement or continuity, (2) domain reallocation, and (3) present-centred sufficiency. They are analytically distinct but not necessarily chronological. They can overlap, recur, reverse, or remain absent. Table 3 states the processes, competing interpretations, and empirical tests.

Process 1: Selective Disengagement or Continuity

Goal-regulation research shows that abandoning an unattainable goal can protect well-being when people can reinvest in alternatives.37 Retirement may make promotion, organizational status, or long-term career projects less attainable or less desirable. For some individuals, releasing these goals can reduce strain and free motivational resources. Yet retirement research shows that many people preserve occupational involvement through bridge work, partial retirement, consulting, entrepreneurship, or later return to work.22–25

The first process is therefore selective rather than total. It can involve disengaging from employer-controlled advancement while retaining craft identity, professional contribution, mentoring, income-generating activity, or the social meaning of work. It may also involve continuity without disengagement when a person chooses to keep working. The central empirical variables are voluntariness, goal attainability, role centrality, perceived control, and congruence between expected and realized retirement pathways.

Proposition 1: When career-linked goals become unattainable or unwanted, voluntary disengagement accompanied by access to valued alternatives will predict better adjustment than involuntary disengagement or persistent investment without feasible progress.

Proposition 2: Continued work will be associated with purpose only when its meaning, conditions, and degree of choice are considered; bridge employment undertaken for identity continuity or contribution may differ from work undertaken under severe financial constraint.

Process 2: Domain Reallocation

Domain reallocation refers to changes in the distribution of attention, time, and identity across paid work, health, family, friendship, community, spirituality, leisure, and learning. Super’s life-span, life-space perspective described development as the changing configuration of multiple roles,38 and retirement-adjustment models emphasize the preservation or replacement of valued resources across domains.24 AshaRani et al six-domain synthesis provides a broad content map for this process.11

Reallocation need not follow occupational exit, and it need not move from one dominant domain to many weaker ones. Some people deepen one long-standing commitment; others add new roles; others reduce the number of commitments to protect health. The defining feature is not breadth itself but a revised configuration that the individual experiences as coherent and feasible. This is also where inequality becomes decisive: only some adults possess the money, health, transport, social networks, or freedom from care obligations needed to choose among domains.

The outcome of successful reallocation is what we term a lateral configuration of purpose. We define it by three operational features: (a) simultaneous investment across multiple life domains rather than hierarchical concentration in a single domain; (b) flexible reallocation of effort across domains in response to context rather than a fixed priority ordering; and (c) valuation of activities for their present enactment rather than exclusively as means to a future endpoint. We hypothesize that these three features tend to co-occur in the Third Age, but they may vary independently, and they are testable through time-use diaries, domain-specific goal-commitment measures, and experience-sampling designs. This is a proposed conceptual definition to be tested, not an already-validated scale; measurement development for it is described in Measurement Development.

Proposition 3: Changes in purpose after retirement will be better explained by profiles of domain investment and opportunity than by retirement status alone.

Proposition 4: Perceived temporal autonomy is hypothesized to be one central mechanism explaining why the organization of purpose in the Third Age differs from the career phase: discretionary control over time is expected to relax the demand for a single, hierarchically prioritized, progress-oriented goal and to permit a more lateral, present-focused configuration. This formulation is consistent with a mediating role for temporal autonomy in the pathway from retirement transition to goal organization. Because identification is a serious concern—retirees are selected, and autonomy is not randomly assigned—rigorous testing requires designs that address selection and within-person change across the retirement transition, such as pension-eligibility natural experiments or longitudinal within-person designs, rather than cross-sectional mediation. Health decline, loss of social roles, and financial constraint may operate as competing or countervailing mechanisms and should be modeled explicitly.

Process 3: Present-Centred Sufficiency

Present-centred sufficiency denotes the experience that an activity, relationship, or way of organizing daily life has value in its current enactment, without requiring constant escalation or a distant endpoint. It is related to, but distinct from, hedonic pleasure and mindfulness. The distinction between hedonic and eudaimonic well-being shows that present experience can involve excellence, authenticity, and valued functioning rather than mere comfort.39 Self-determination theory similarly suggests that activities can be intrinsically motivated when they support autonomy, competence, and relatedness.40

Sufficiency also differs from resignation. Maintaining health, caring for another person, practicing a skill, or sustaining a community can require effort and future coordination. What changes is the criterion of “enough”: accomplishment is not evaluated only by upward movement. Anderson’s near-present domain and Freund’s maintenance orientation support this possibility,12,13 but direct evidence in the Third Age remains limited.

Proposition 5: Present-centred sufficiency will be positively associated with well-being when it coexists with agency, valued engagement, and adequate resources, but not when it reflects constrained withdrawal or depressive loss of aspiration.

Proposition 6: Measures that add present value, maintenance, relational contribution, and domain-coherence items will show incremental validity for some Third-Age groups, particularly where conventional future-progress items display differential functioning.

Nonlinearity and Co-Occurrence

A strict sequence—disengagement, then reallocation, then sufficiency—is not supported by the current evidence. Stage theories of retirement have received limited direct support, and the duration and order of phases are uncertain.24 A worker may develop present-centred sufficiency before retirement, retain work while reallocating other domains, later disengage after a health change, and then return to work. Anderson’s three temporal domains can coexist,12 and Freund describes selection, optimization, and compensation as distinct but interrelated processes rather than invariant stages.13

The framework should therefore be modeled as a state-and-process system. Intensive longitudinal designs can examine daily or weekly fluctuations, while longer panel studies can identify profiles and transitions. Latent transition analysis, dynamic structural equation models, and person-specific time-series methods would be more appropriate than assuming one population-wide pathway.

At the same time, we hypothesize a default ordering under specifiable conditions. Under voluntary retirement, adequate health and economic resources, and the absence of intensive care obligations, we hypothesize that a default pathway of selective disengagement or continuity, then domain reallocation, then present-centred sufficiency is more likely than its alternatives. These conditions are not defined circularly: each refers to an independently measurable state (voluntariness of the retirement transition, functional health and financial adequacy, and caregiving burden), not to “whatever produces the default pathway.” The hypothesized ordering is expected to be disrupted or reversed under health shock, involuntary retirement, intensive caregiving, or sustained employment, and the qualitative evidence reviewed in Qualitative Evidence on Purpose Reconstruction in Later Life documents that many older adults do not traverse the pathway at all.

This hypothesis is falsifiable. The hypothesized default ordering would be disconfirmed if, among voluntary retirees with adequate resources, a substantial share showed present-centred sufficiency before domain reallocation, or progressed in the reverse direction. It would also be disconfirmed if sustained employment or major caregiving burden were found to be as strongly associated with lateral configuration as voluntary, resourced retirement.

Discussion

Theoretical Contribution and Relation to Prior Work

Anderson et al reconceptualized the temporal domains of purpose in very late life;12 AshaRani et al mapped the content domains of later-life purpose;11 and Freund analysed age-related shifts in goal orientations.13 The distinctive contribution of the present framework is to connect the formal organization of purpose to socially conditioned control over time during a relatively functionally able but institutionally heterogeneous period of later life. This is an incremental contribution, not a claim to have discovered that purpose can change in old age, and it is presented as an interpretive synthesis and a set of testable hypotheses rather than an established result.

Relationship Between Prior Reconceptualizations and the Present Framework

The framework differs from Anderson et al in population, temporal structure, and mechanism.12 Anderson et al focus on very late life and organize purpose across retrospective past, near present, and transcendental post-mortem domains under a contracted future horizon. The current framework focuses on later-life transitions that often occur while substantial functional capacity remains. It examines selective continuity, redistribution across life domains, and the possibility that current activity can be sufficient without continual escalation. The models are complementary rather than competing: Third-Age reconfiguration may precede, coexist with, or differ from very-late-life temporal weighting.

AshaRani and colleagues provide a taxonomy of what later-life purpose contains.11 The present model instead asks how investment and organization may change across those domains. Their six domains prevent the current model from becoming overly goal-centric and suggest content areas for measurement. Kashdan and colleagues clarify purpose as a central organizing life aim and distinguish purpose from discrete goals.3 Their account also cautions that domain plurality is not automatically purposeful; coherence, strength, scope, and awareness remain important.

Freund’s lifespan analysis supplies both support and a corrective.13 It supports attention to maintenance and loss avoidance and shows that goal orientation is responsive to resources and perceived constraints. It also undermines any age-essentialist sequence: younger adults can adopt maintenance orientations when resource loss is made salient, and older adults can pursue growth. The proposed processes should therefore be treated as context-sensitive possibilities rather than age-graded necessities.

Alternative Interpretations and Contradictory Evidence

Several alternative explanations must remain visible. First, lower purpose may be a valid indicator of real loss for adults facing illness, bereavement, involuntary retirement, social isolation, or blocked opportunities. Second, cohort and socioeconomic selection may account for apparent Third-Age autonomy. Third, present-focused responses may reflect adaptive reorientation, but they may also reflect constrained horizons, fatigue, or depression. Fourth, plurality across domains may be flexible and resilient, or it may indicate conflict and lack of commitment. Fifth, the qualitative and structural evidence reviewed above adds two further cautions: some older adults report little or no purpose and do not treat purpose as relevant to later life,30 and a low total score may reflect the presence of a distinct sense of purposelessness rather than the mere absence of purpose.31 Any claim that lower scores in the Third Age represent successful reconfiguration must therefore explain why similar response patterns are also found among adults who explicitly deny having purpose and among those for whom the scale itself appears to function differently.

Evidence linking purpose with mortality and cognition supports the continued value of conventional measures,4–6 but observational associations do not by themselves establish that increasing purpose will cause longer life or better health. Reverse causation and residual confounding remain possible. Similarly, the positive associations of bridge employment with adjustment do not prove that work is universally beneficial. Job quality, necessity, schedule control, discrimination, and health selection can all shape outcomes.

These contradictions are not peripheral; they define the empirical agenda. A useful model must predict when reconfiguration differs from decline and must permit the conclusion that some individuals experience both at once—for example, a loss of occupational purpose alongside growth in relational purpose.

Measurement Development

Measurement development should begin with diverse qualitative sampling rather than immediately naming new scales. Interviews and diary studies should include adults across income, education, gender, family structure, ethnicity, rurality, health, disability, retirement pathway, and national policy context. Questions should elicit what makes daily life worth organizing, what responsibilities feel meaningful, which aims have been relinquished or retained, and how participants evaluate maintenance, contribution, and “enough.”

Item generation can then cover the six content domains identified by AshaRani et al,11 the temporal domains proposed by Anderson et al,12 and the organizational processes proposed here. Cognitive interviewing should test whether “future”, “goal”, “growth”, “contribution”, and “sufficiency” carry equivalent meanings across groups. Psychometric evaluation should include exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses, item-response theory, and measurement invariance across employment status, functional ability, age, culture, and time.

Criterion validity should not be prespecified as a desired correlation. Instead, hypotheses should distinguish convergence and incremental prediction. A new measure should correlate with established purpose measures while demonstrating added explanatory value for theoretically relevant outcomes and groups. Longitudinal designs should test whether scores predict later engagement and well-being after accounting for baseline health, depressive symptoms, personality, and socioeconomic conditions.

Practical Implications

Retirement counseling should avoid assuming that successful adjustment requires either complete occupational withdrawal or immediate replacement with a single major goal. Assessment can identify which career goals remain valued, which have become unattainable, what work continuity is desired, and whether nonwork time is genuinely discretionary. Programs can support experimentation across volunteering, family, learning, health, creativity, spirituality, leisure, and flexible work without treating one pathway as universally superior.

Practitioners should also distinguish lack of purpose from lack of opportunity. Recommending more goals to a person constrained by poor health, caregiving, poverty, or inaccessible environments can individualize a structural problem. Interventions may need to increase transportation, social access, respite care, flexible employment, or community opportunities before asking individuals to reorganize motivation.

Assessment feedback requires particular care. Strong claims that a retiree is “without purpose” may reinforce stereotypes of retirement as devaluation. Ageism interventions show that attitudes toward older adults can be modified,41 but the present review does not establish that receiving a low purpose score directly causes internalized ageism. The practical recommendation is therefore precautionary: interpret scores contextually, discuss item relevance, and avoid converting a screening result into an identity judgment.

Limitations and Future Research

This article is a conceptual, not systematic, review. It does not provide exhaustive retrieval, formal study-quality ratings, or pooled effect estimates. Its proposed processes are derived from interdisciplinary synthesis and have not been validated as a scale or causal model. The terminology of “Third Age” remains culturally and historically situated, and the emphasis on temporal autonomy may fit advantaged retirement regimes better than contexts characterized by informal work, delayed retirement, or intensive family obligation.

The evidence base is also uneven. Later-life purpose research is dominated by cross-sectional studies and standardized measures, while qualitative and intervention studies remain limited.11 The four qualitative studies synthesized in Qualitative Evidence on Purpose Reconstruction in Later Life are instructive but concentrate in Northern Europe, Australia, and the United States and include small, mostly community or care-residence samples.27–30 The VA Normative Aging Study is limited to men.26 Pfund and colleagues’ structural finding requires replication across instruments and cultural contexts before it is treated as a general property of purpose measurement.31 Retirement studies provide strong evidence of pathway heterogeneity but usually measure purpose, identity, meaningfulness, and schedule control only indirectly. The model’s applicability to people in continuous career employment, bridge employment, involuntary retirement, severe disability, or the Fourth Age requires separate testing.

Four research priorities follow. First, qualitative and mixed-method studies should test whether the three processes are recognizable in lived accounts and whether important processes are missing. Second, longitudinal studies spanning pre-retirement, retirement, bridge work, and later life should test co-occurrence, reversal, subgroup differences, and the hypothesized default ordering specified in Nonlinearity and Co-occurrence. Third, cross-cultural research should compare policy regimes and unequal opportunity structures rather than treating nationality as a simple covariate. Fourth, measurement studies should test item functioning and incremental validity before claiming that established measures misclassify later-life purpose. Overarching all four is the need to test the morphological-shift hypothesis directly: no existing study measures whether goal organization changes from linear accumulation to lateral configuration across the retirement transition, and the instruments proposed in Measurement Development should be used in prospective within-person designs to do so.

Conclusion

Purpose in the Third Age should not be assumed either to decline uniformly or to undergo one universal transformation. Existing research demonstrates multidimensional content, heterogeneous trajectories, unequal opportunity structures, and diverse retirement pathways. Together, these literatures suggest that conventional future- and progress-oriented measures may incompletely capture purpose for some older adults, while remaining valid and predictive for others.

We argue that the central question for the Third Age is not simply whether purpose declines but whether its form changes. Our core hypothesis is that purpose may shift from linear accumulation—goal striving organized around continuous, hierarchically ordered progress toward future endpoints—to a lateral configuration in which value is distributed across multiple present-focused domains. We further argue that conventional instruments built around future-oriented, progressive, single-focus item content may misread this morphological shift as an absence of purpose. This thesis is an interpretive synthesis grounded in convergent but indirect evidence: item-content analysis, possible measurement non-equivalence, qualitative accounts of present-focused and relational purpose, and longitudinal heterogeneity with weak predictor power. We have labeled its epistemic status explicitly: it is a proposed hypothesis, not an established finding, and no study to date has directly measured the shift.

The proposed framework describes three processes—selective disengagement or continuity, domain reallocation, and present-centred sufficiency—that may overlap, recur, reverse, or fail to occur. Under specifiable conditions of voluntary retirement, adequate resources, and the absence of intensive care obligations, we hypothesize a default ordering of these processes; that ordering is expected to be disrupted under health shock, involuntary retirement, intensive caregiving, or sustained employment, and it is falsifiable in the ways described in Nonlinearity and Co-occurrence. The framework extends earlier reconceptualizations by focusing on the organization of purpose under changing control over time, while recognizing that time autonomy is socially distributed and cannot be inferred from chronological age or retirement status.

A balanced research agenda should therefore combine respect for established measures with openness to construct expansion. The practical objective is not to label all later-life change as successful reconstruction, but to distinguish adaptive reorganization from genuine loss and structural constraint—and to test when the hypothesized morphological shift occurs, for whom it is adaptive, and whether new measures can capture it. Such work could support more accurate assessment and more individualized, equitable approaches to retirement and healthy aging.
