Impact of SLCO1B1 (rs2306283) polymorphism on personalized atorvastatin dosing in a genetically distinct South Asian cohort
Authors: Tayaba Farooq, Uzma Naeem, Afifa Siddique, Samia Kausar, Akbar Waheed, Sidra Mumal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeDyslipidemia is a prevalent condition in the Pakistani population, significantly contributing to coronary heart disease (CHD). Atorvastatin, a widely used hypolipidemic drug, exhibits variable efficacy and safety influenced by genetic factors. This study aimed to assess the impact of SLCO1B1 rs2306283 (A > G) polymorphism on the efficacy and safety profile of atorvastatin in the Pakistani population.Materials/methodsOne hundred dyslipidemic patients, aged 40–75, received atorvastatin 10 mg daily for one month. Blood samples were collected on days 0 and 28 to measure lipid profiles and creatinine kinase (CK). Genotyping for rs2306283 (388 A> G) polymorphism was performed, and myopathy was evaluated using the Statin Associated Muscle Symptoms (SAMS) clinical index tool and CK levels.ResultsThe minor allele frequency of rs2306283 was 12%. Only the AA (76%) and AG (24%) genotypes were observed; the GG genotype was absent. Atorvastatin significantly improved lipid parameters; however, no association was observed between rs2306283 polymorphism and lipid-lowering efficacy. A significant association was found with myopathy, with an incidence of 18.4% in patients with the AA genotype and 0% in those with the AG genotype. The G allele demonstrated a protective effect, as it was completely absent in the myopathy group. No significant elevation in CK levels was associated with myopathy.ConclusionsThe SLCO1B1 rs2306283 polymorphism does not affect atorvastatin’s lipid-lowering efficacy; however, individuals with the AG genotype exhibit a significantly reduced risk of statin-induced myopathy in the Pakistani population. These findings support the potential role of pharmacogenetic screening in enhancing the safety of statin therapy.ClinicalTrials.gov IdentifierNCT06674044, Date: 03/09/2024.

Introduction

Dyslipidemia refers to a disruption in the normal balance of blood lipids, a condition marked by elevated concentrations of total cholesterol (TC) and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), as well as high triglycerides (TG), often accompanied by low levels of high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) [1, 2].

The Global Burden of Disease 2019 (GBD) study highlights the prevalence of cardiovascular disease (CVD) within Pakistan, where the age-standardized incidence rate of CVD is significantly higher than the global average of 918.18 per 100,000, compared to 684.33 per 100,000. Furthermore, Pakistan’s age-standardized mortality rate due to CVD stands at 357.88 per 100,000, surpassing the global rate of 239.85 per 100,000 [3]. This upward trend is expected to persist, largely due to the widespread prevalence of CVD risk factors, including diabetes (30.8%), hypertension (37%), and smoking, which affects about 25% of adult men [4].

Dyslipidemia is recognized as a significant, standalone risk factor for coronary heart disease (CHD) that can be modified [5]. The risk factors of dyslipidemia include high blood pressure, smoking, and higher body mass index, increased intake of unhealthy foods, reduced physical activity, urbanization, hypothyroidism, and uncontrolled diabetes [4–6].

Initial treatment of dyslipidemia primarily involves dietary and lifestyle changes, followed by drug therapy. Aerobic activities and maintaining a healthy diet significantly lower LDL levels by following the American College of Cardiology/ American Heart Association (ACC/AHA) 2018 cholesterol treatment guidelines. Statin therapy is initiated after assessing the 10-year atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) risk, alongside one or more additional cardiovascular risk factors like dyslipidemia, diabetes, hypertension, or smoking. The 2018 guidelines emphasize a personalized approach, using risk-enhancing factors and coronary artery calcium scoring to guide shared decision-making between clinicians and patients for primary prevention [7].

Statins, which are inhibitors of 3-hydroxy-3-methyl-glutaryl-coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase, form the foundation of dyslipidemia management. Their proven ability to reduce low-density lipoprotein (LDL) and total cholesterol levels has established them as a first-line therapy for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease [8, 9]. Statins have additional benefits beyond lowering cholesterol. These effects include reducing inflammation, enhancing endothelial function, and preventing platelet aggregation at sites of atherosclerotic plaque [10].

Clinical research has revealed that atorvastatin is an effective treatment for lowering TC, LDL, and TG levels in individuals with hypercholesterolemia and hypertriglyceridemia. Early initiation has shown a decrease in cardiovascular events and mortality, even in individuals with normal cholesterol levels [11]. So, in the context of prescribing statin therapy, it is a common practice to start atorvastatin promptly after an acute coronary syndrome, irrespective of lipid levels [12]. Compared to other statins, atorvastatin at moderate to high-intensity doses has demonstrated greater efficacy in reducing TC, LDL, and TG levels. However, these effects are dose-dependent, and the lipid-lowering potential varies across different statins and therapeutic intensities [13, 14].

Atorvastatin is typically administered at daily doses ranging from 10 to 80 mg and possesses a half-life of approximately 13 to 16 h [8]. However, interindividual variation in treatment response can compromise its therapeutic benefits and predispose patients to adverse effects, notably atorvastatin-induced myopathy (AIM) [15]. These adverse events (AEs) are a major concern as they significantly hinder treatment adherence [16].

SLCO1B1, also known as LST1, OATP2, OATPC, OATP-C, and SLC21A6, is a solute organic anion transporter family member 1 B1 that encodes a transmembrane protein specific to the liver [17]. The SLCO1B1 gene encodes the OATP1B1 transporter, a protein located on the basolateral membrane of hepatocytes that facilitates the hepatic uptake of drugs such as atorvastatin [18]. It is demonstrated that genetic variations in SLCO1B1 alter its transporter activity, impacting drug pharmacokinetics and influencing therapeutic efficacy and the risk of adverse effects [19].

Polymorphisms in the SLCO1B1 gene (chromosome 12p12.2) are a key source of pharmacokinetic variability, as they can alter the transporter’s function to either enhance or diminish the uptake of its drug substrates [19]. Over 141 single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) have been characterized within the SLCO1B1 gene [20]. Among these, the rs2306283 SNP has been the subject of intense research due to its prevalence and potential functional influence on the OATP1B1 transporter. This specific SNP results in a non-synonymous missense mutation located on chromosome 12 [17] that changes the amino acid at position 130 from asparagine to aspartic acid [21].

The International Transporter Consortium (ITC), consisting of researchers from academic, industrial, and regulatory sectors worldwide, has recorded a significant level of variability in SLC transporter activities among individuals, which is associated with genetic variations [22].

There is conflicting evidence on the impact of the rs2306283 polymorphism on transporter function. The rs2306283 SNP is linked to increased OATP1B1 transporter activity, resulting in reduced plasma concentrations of statins and potentially enhanced lipid-lowering efficacy, and decreases the risk of myopathy by lowering statin levels [23, 24]. However, some studies suggest the G allele may elevate statin concentrations, increasing the potential for myotoxicity [25]. The exact role of this SNP in modulating transport function remains uncertain. Further studies are needed to confirm these findings.

There is limited information on the impact of the rs2306283 polymorphism on the effectiveness and safety profile of atorvastatin in the Pakistani population. While it is well-established that statin response and toxicity are polygenic traits, influenced by variants in genes such as SLCO1B1 (rs4149056), APOE, CYP3A4, and ABCG2, data on the prevalence and effect of these polymorphisms within the Pakistani demographic remain scarce [26].

A primary focus on SLCO1B1 rs2306283 (388 A > G) is justified as it is a common and functionally significant polymorphism in South Asian populations. It significantly alters the function of the OATP1B1 transporter, a critical determinant of atorvastatin hepatic uptake. A better insight into this information might reflect different genetic and environmental factors that influence the transport of atorvastatin and its clinical outcomes. This represents a potential research gap in our country. This study aims to provide foundational data on this major pharmacogenetic contributor, upon which future multi-gene and genome-wide studies can be built.

Understanding these genetic influences could enhance treatment strategies, reduce adverse drug reactions, and improve therapeutic outcomes. Therefore, the results of this study may offer fresh perspectives on the pharmacogenetics of atorvastatin and its implications in lipid control management of various clinical scenarios. Hence, this study aims to identify the presence and effect of genetic polymorphism of SLCO1B1 (rs2306283 388 A>G) on the clinical efficacy and safety profile of atorvastatin in the Pakistani population.

Materials and methods

This prospective cohort study was conducted over a two-year period (October 2022 to October 2024) at the Multidisciplinary Laboratory of the Islamic International Medical College in collaboration with the Pakistan Railway Hospital (PRH), Rawalpindi. A cohort of 100 participants was recruited via non-probability convenience sampling. A sample size of 100 was calculated using the GAS power calculator for genetic association studies, with a minor allele frequency (MAF) of 0.36 [27], a significance level of 0.05%, and a study power of 90% [28–30]. The Ethical Review Committee approved the study protocol (Reference No: Riphah/IIMC/IRC/23/30104), and all procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Participant selection: Newly diagnosed 100 patients with dyslipidemia who were prescribed atorvastatin (Lipiget®) 10 mg/day at night for 1 month [23, 31, 32].Inclusion criteria: Pakistani individuals aged 40–75 years, with a deranged lipid profile (TC ≥ 200 mg/dL, TG ≥ 150 mg/dL, LDL-C ≥ 130 mg/dL, HDL-C ≤ 40 mg/dL) [33, 34] and have normal hepatic and renal function.Exclusion criteria included patients on other lipid-lowering medications, those with moderate or severe systemic diseases [35], pregnancies, hypo/hyperthyroidism [36], recent surgical procedures or trauma, and those using medications or diets that interact with atorvastatin.

Statistical methods

We performed the statistical analysis using SPSS software (version 27). Descriptive statistics were calculated for quantitative variables, while frequencies and percentages were determined for qualitative variables such as gender, ethnicity, and myopathy. A paired t-test assessed differences between pre-treatment and post-treatment values. We assessed associations of the SLCO1B1 variant with clinical and demographic factors using Chi-Square tests for genotype comparisons and allele-based analyses (Chi-Square or Fisher’s Exact Test). A p-value below 0.05 defines statistical significance. The methodology described in this paper follows the approved protocol of our registered clinical trial (NCT06674044).

Clinical efficacy of atorvastatin

Both AA and AG genotypes showed significant within-group improvements in serum lipid levels after atorvastatin therapy. However, no significant differences were observed between genotypes in follow-up lipid levels or mean changes. Furthermore, a factorial ANOVA revealed no significant interaction between gender and genotype on the change in LDL-C levels (F(3, 96) = 0.68, p = 0.569), indicating that the Pharmacogenetic effect was consistent across sexes. The effect sizes (Cohen’s d) ranged from small to moderate (0.07–0.38), indicating no association between the SLCO1B1 rs2306283 polymorphism and the clinical efficacy of atorvastatin (Table 4).

To further investigate the relationship using an additive genetic model, linear regression analyses were performed with the number of SLCO1B1 rs2306283 G alleles as the predictor for changes in each lipid parameter. This allele-based analysis confirmed the lack of a significant association, as the number of G alleles was not a significant predictor of the change in LDL-C (B = -7.41, p = 0.245), HDL-C (B = -1.00, p = 0.490), triglycerides (B = 39.08, p = 0.171), or total cholesterol (B = -2.55, p = 0.778).

Safety profile of atorvastatin

Atorvastatin-Induced myopathy (AIM)

The rate of myopathy in the AA group is 18.42% (14/76), whereas in the AG group, the rate of myopathy is 0% (0/24). The AA genotype has 19.4 times higher odds of developing myopathy compared to the AG genotype, which represents a large effect size (p = 0.02) as shown in Table 5.

Critically, an allele-based analysis was performed to directly assess the impact of the G allele. The minor allele (G) frequency was 0% (0/28 alleles) in patients who developed AIM, compared to 14.5% (25/172 alleles) in those who did not. This complete absence of the G allele in the myopathy group was statistically significant (p = 0.019, Fisher’s Exact Test). A binary logistic regression was performed to ascertain the effects of gender, SLCO1B1 genotype, and their interaction on the likelihood of participants developing atorvastatin-induced myopathy. The logistic regression model was statistically significant, p = 0.018. Most importantly, the gender-by-genotype interaction term was not statistically significant (p = 1.000), indicating that the strong protective effect of the AG genotype was consistent across both male and female patients.

Discussion

Dyslipidemias often go unnoticed because their symptoms are hard to detect, making early diagnosis difficult. Despite this, the daily administration of 10 mg atorvastatin for one month significantly improved all lipid parameters. Importantly, this lipid-lowering effect was consistent across SLCO1B1 rs2306283 genotypes, indicating that this polymorphism does not influence the drug’s efficacy in our population.

Our cohort demonstrated a female predominance (69%), consistent with Darroudi S. et al., ‘s report of higher dyslipidemia prevalence in females [41], and the WHO global data [42], though contrasting with Zhang et al.‘s findings in a younger cohort [43]. This gender imbalance, a recognized study limitation due to non-population-based recruitment, precludes definitive conclusions about gender-specific prevalence. While hormonal and lifestyle factors may influence dyslipidemia risk [44, 45], which were not assessed in our study and merit future research.

Our study, with a mean participant age of 49.2 ± 9.08 years per ACC/AHA guidelines [34], found a low frequency of the G allele (MAF = 12%), with AA (76%) and AG (24%) genotypes, and no GG. This supports Rajput T. et al.‘s observation of a less common GG genotype in a Punjabi Pakistani population [37]. Conversely, our results differ from Jada S. et al., who reported the G allele as highly polymorphic and common in other Asian populations (79.5% in Chinese, 87% in Malay, 57% in Indian) [46]. These inter-population differences may stem from environmental, evolutionary, and historical factors.

The SLCO1B1 rs2306283 genotype distribution was similar across genders and ethnicities in our cohort, consistent with reports in Greek and Macedonian populations [21, 47] and another Pakistani study conducted by Rajput T. et al. [37]. We found no evidence of a gender-specific effect on either efficacy or myopathy risk, indicating consistent pharmacogenetic relationships in this population. This contrasts with a report of an enhanced statin response in males with the AA genotype [48], a discrepancy potentially due to differences in the statin used, clinical endpoints, genetic background, or statistical power.

Consistent with its known efficacy, atorvastatin significantly improved all lipid parameters. However, analysis revealed no significant association with the SLCO1B1 rs2306283 genotype. Our analysis found no significant association between the SLCO1B1 rs2306283 polymorphism and the lipid-lowering efficacy of atorvastatin. This finding is consistent with a study from Macedonia that also reported no significant association for this variant [21] and is further supported by a 2022 meta-analysis of 16 studies confirming no significant genotype-dependent effect on LDL reduction [49]. In contrast to our overall finding, Ferrari M. et al., reported that LDL reduction was 14% less in individuals with the AA genotype [50]. A similar numerical trend was observed in our cohort, where the AG group showed a greater LDL reduction, though this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.185). The lack of significance may be due to the limited sample size of the AG group, which reduced the statistical power to detect a true effect [50]. However, in contrast, Wu X. et al., concluded that average HDL levels were significantly higher in patients with the AA genotype [51], while the Chilean population has reported significant reductions in triglycerides and increases in HDL in AG carriers following atorvastatin therapy [31]. Similarly, Ferrari M. et al., further quantified a genotype-specific difference in LDL reduction [50]. The divergence in results across studies is likely attributable to distinct genetic backgrounds, dietary habits, environmental factors, and variations in study design.

The incidence of atorvastatin-induced myopathy (AIM) was 14% in our cohort, aligning with a Jordanian study (12.5%) [52] but lower than a pilot report (35.5%) [53]. We observed a higher incidence in females (17.3%) than males (6.45%), a finding consistent with Sadeeqa S. et al., in a Pakistani population and Skiliving. et al., in a Swedish cohort [54, 55], but contrasting with the report by Abed W. et al., from Jordan [52]. These inconsistent gender disparities may be explained by differences in body composition, hormones, and pharmacokinetic differences [56].

A significant association was found between the SLCO1B1 rs2306283 genotype and AIM incidence, with an 18.4% incidence in AA individuals and a complete absence of myopathy in AG carriers. Allele-based analysis confirmed the G allele’s protective role (0% frequency in myopathy group, p = 0.019). This aligns with studies by Shahrure Z. et al. [24], Donnelly L. et al. [57], and Stewart A. et al. [58], but contrasts with a meta-analysis [59] and reports by Santos P. et al., and Turongkaravee S. et al. [60, 61], highlighting the impact of population-specific genetics on this Pharmacogenetic relationship.

Statin-associated muscle symptoms often present with normal or minimally elevated CK levels [62]. In our cohort, 14% of patients who developed AIM had a non-significantly higher mean CK (104.29 U/L) than those without symptoms (71.56 U/L), a finding consistent with the results reported by Santos P. et al. [61, 63]. We found no association between the SLCO1B1 rs2306283 genotype and CK levels, suggesting the reported myopathic symptoms occurred without significant muscle damage. This aligns with Santos P. et al., who found no CK elevation with atorvastatin [61], and Wu X. et al., who, despite observing a simvastatin-induced CK increase, found no association with this genotype [51]. This collective evidence suggests the polymorphism’s role is linked to the subjective experience of myopathy rather than being a primary driver of measurable muscle damage.

By bridging pharmacogenetics and clinical outcomes, this study provides preliminary insights into the potential role of SLCO1B1 rs2306283 polymorphism in predicting AIM risk in the Pakistani population. While no significant association was observed between this polymorphism and atorvastatin’s lipid-lowering efficacy, the observed difference in myopathy incidence between genotypes suggests that SLCO1B1 genotyping may contribute to enhancing the safety of statin therapy by identifying individuals at a higher risk for adverse effects. It emphasizes the utility of SLCO1B1 genotyping in tailoring atorvastatin therapy, particularly in populations where genetic data may be limited.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting our findings. First, the analysis was restricted to a single SLCO1B1 SNP, while statin pharmacokinetics and response are polygenic traits. A more comprehensive genetic profile was not feasible due to resource constraints. Second, the cohort’s small sample size resulted in a limited number of minor allele carriers, which likely reduced the statistical power to detect significant associations with lipid-lowering efficacy. Finally, the brief 4-week follow-up period prevented the assessment of long-term therapeutic outcomes and late-emerging adverse effects.

Recommendations

For future recommendations, the impact of the SLCO1B1 rs2306283 polymorphism on the risk of developing adverse effects during therapy with different types of statins can be investigated. The findings of the study can stimulate researchers for further evaluation of the genetic makeup of the Pakistani population.

Conclusion

Our findings characterize the pharmacogenetics of atorvastatin in a Pakistani cohort. The SLCO1B1 rs2306283 (A > G) G allele had a frequency of 12%, in contrast to the 88% frequency of the major A allele. Most notably, the polymorphism demonstrated a distinct dissociation between its influence on safety and efficacy. Genotype and allele-based analyses consistently revealed no significant impact on atorvastatin’s lipid-lowering efficacy. However, the A allele was a significant risk factor for atorvastatin-induced myopathy (AIM), while the G allele exhibited a protective effect. This suggests that pre-emptive screening for this variant could help mitigate adverse effects and personalize statin therapy in this population.
