Intersection problems for linear codes and polynomials over finite fields
Sam Adriaensen

Abstract
This paper proves a stability result for a variation of the Erdős-Ko-Rado theorem in the context of polynomials over finite fields. Let ℱ \mathcal{F} be a family of polynomials of degree at most k ⩾ 3 k\geqslant 3 in 𝔽 q ​ [ X ] \mathbb{F}_{q}[X] . Call ℱ \mathcal{F} intersecting if for any two polynomials f , g f,g in ℱ \mathcal{F} , there exists a point x ∈ 𝔽 q x\in\mathbb{F}_{q} for which f ⁡ ( x ) = g ⁡ ( x ) f(x)=g(x) . An intersecting family is called a star if it consists of all polynomials f f with deg ⁡ f ⩽ k \deg f\leqslant k such that f ⁡ ( x ) = y f(x)=y for some fixed points x , y ∈ 𝔽 q x,y\in\mathbb{F}_{q} . In this paper we prove that if ℱ \mathcal{F} is an intersecting family with | ℱ | ⩾ 1 2 ​ q k + 𝒪 ⁡ ( q k − 1 ) |\mathcal{F}|\geqslant\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}q^{k}+\mathcal{O}(q^{k-1}) , then ℱ \mathcal{F} is contained in a star. In fact, we prove that this is still true if we also evaluate the polynomials “at infinity”, which is equivalent to studying the problem for homogeneous bivariate polynomials. The proof technique extends to a general framework for intersection problems of linear codes C C . One has to investigate the geometry of the projective system 𝒮 \mathcal{S} associated to C C . If the hyperplanes that don’t intersect 𝒮 \mathcal{S} are well spread out with respect to the points not on 𝒮 \mathcal{S} , then one obtains stability results, showing that any intersecting family of reasonably large size is contained in a star.



Keywords:

Intersection problems, Erdős-Ko-Rado theorem, Finite geometry, Linear codes, Projective systems, Normal rational curves, Polynomials, Spectral graph theory.

1.1
General intersection problems

In their seminal paper, Erdős, Ko and Rado
[
EKR61
]
proved that if
\mathcal{F}
is a family of subsets of size
k
of an
n
-element set
\mathcal{P}
, with
n>2k
, then
|\mathcal{F}|\leqslant\binom{n-1}{k-1}
, with equality if and only
\mathcal{F}
consists of all
k
-element subsets through a fixed point of
\mathcal{P}
.
This consequently inspired its own branch in combinatorics, which we could denominate
intersection problems
.
Much research is still being done.
See e.g.
[
Ell22
,
KZ24
,
TT25
,
FHI+25
,
BW25
,
ST25
]
for some recent progress.

In this paper, we will study intersection problems in the following framework.
We start with an
incidence structure

(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{B},\textnormal{I})
.
This means that
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{B}
are sets (in our case always finite), and
\textnormal{I}\subseteq\mathcal{P}\times\mathcal{B}
is a relation.
We call the elements of
\mathcal{P}

points
, the elements of
\mathcal{B}

blocks
, and we call
I
the
incidence relation
.
We write
P\textnormal{I}B
as a shorthand for
(P,B)\in\textnormal{I}
, and we say that
P
is on
B
, or
B
goes through
P
, or
P
is incident with
B
.

We call two blocks
B_{1}
and
B_{2}

t
-intersecting
if there are at least
t
points incident with both
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
.
A family
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{B}
of blocks is said to be
t
-intersecting
if any two blocks contained in
\mathcal{F}
are
t
-intersecting.
If
t=1
, we simply say
intersecting
.
A
t
-star
(also called a
canonical
or
trivially
t
-intersecting family
by other authors) is a non-empty
t
-intersecting family of the form
\left\{B\in\mathcal{B}\,\,||\,\,P_{1},\dots,P_{t}\textnormal{I}B\right\}
,
with
P_{1},\dots,P_{t}
distinct points.
A
1
-star is simply called a
star
.
The question arises whether the largest
t
-intersecting families are
t
-stars, and if so, how large can a
t
-intersecting family be that is not contained in a
t
-star?

1.2
Intersection problems for polynomials

We will investigate intersecting families in the context of polynomials over finite fields of bounded degree.
Let
q
be a prime power,
\mathbb{F}_{q}
the finite field of order
q
, let
k<q
be an integer, and let
\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]_{\leqslant k}
denote the set of polynomials in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
of degree at most
k
.

One possible definition for two polynomials
f
and
g
to be
t
-intersecting is that
\deg(\gcd(f,g))\geqslant t
.
This approach was taken in
[
ST25
]
, but we will not expand on it here.

Instead, we say that polynomials
f
and
g
are
t
-intersecting if
f(X)=g(X)
has at least
t
solutions in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
Note that this implies that if
f\neq g
, then
\deg(f-g)\geqslant t
.
We easily obtain the following result.

Result 1.1

(
[
Adr22b
, Lemma 6.1]
)
.

Suppose that
t\leqslant k<q
.
A
t
-intersecting family in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]_{\leqslant k}
contains at most
q^{k+1-t}
polynomials.

Note that the above bound is attained by the
t
-stars.
Moreover, some stability results are known for large intersecting families in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]_{\leqslant k}
.

Result 1.2
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{F}
is an intersecting family in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]_{\leqslant k}
with
2\leqslant k<q
.

(1)

[
Adr22b
]
Then
|\mathcal{F}|\leqslant q^{k}
, with equality if and only if
\mathcal{F}
is a star.

(2)

[
Adr22a
]
If
k=2
, and
|\mathcal{F}|\geqslant\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}q^{2}+2\sqrt{2}q+8
, then
\mathcal{F}
is contained in a star.

(3)

[
ACW24
]
Suppose that
q\geqslant 8
if
q
is even, and
q\geqslant 53
if
q
is odd. If
|\mathcal{F}|\geqslant q^{k}-q^{k-1}
, then
\mathcal{F}
is contained in a star.

The proofs of
Section

1.2
(
1
,
3
) rely on proving the result for the case
k=2
, and then applying induction.
However, the problem becomes more intricate if we also evaluate the polynomials “at infinity”.

Definition 1.3
.

We define for a polynomial
f(X)=\sum_{i=0}^{k}a_{i}X^{i}
of degree at most
k
that
f(\infty_{k})=a_{k}
.
If
k
is clear from context, we denote
\infty_{k}
as
\infty
.

One can think of evaluating
f
on
\mathbb{F}_{q}\cup\left\{\infty\right\}
as evaluating
f
on the projective line
\PG(1,q)
.
Indeed, if we define
F(X,Y)=Y^{k}f(X/Y)=\sum_{i=0}^{k}a_{i}X^{i}Y^{k-i}
, then
F(X,1)=f(X)
and
F(1,0)=f(\infty)
.
Hence, two polynomials
f,g\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]_{\leqslant k}
are
t
-intersecting on
\mathbb{F}_{q}\cup\left\{\infty\right\}
if and only if there are
t
points
\left\langle(x_{1},y_{1})\right\rangle,\dots,\left\langle(x_{t},y_{t})\right\rangle
of
\PG(1,q)
where
Y^{k}f(X/Y)
and
Y^{k}g(X/Y)
agree.

We denote by
\mathbb{F}_{q}[X,Y]_{k}
the set of homogeneous polynomials in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[X,Y]
of degree
k
(including the zero polynomial), and make the following convention.

Definition 1.4
.

(1)

When we say that
f,g\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]_{\leqslant k}
are
t
-intersecting
if
f(X)=g(X)
has at least
t
solutions in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
This corresponds to the incidence structure
(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2},\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]_{\leqslant k},\textnormal{I})
, with
I
the natural incidence relation:

\textnormal{I}=\left\{((x,y),f)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2}\times\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]_{\leqslant k}\,\,||\,\,f(x)=y\right\}.

(2)

We say that
F,G\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X,Y]_{k}
are
t
-intersecting
if
F(X,Y)=G(X,Y)
has at least
t
pairwise linearly independent solutions on
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2}\setminus\left\{\mathbf{0}\right\}
.
Equivalently, if
f(X)=F(X,1)
and
g(X)=G(X,1)
, then
f(X)=g(X)
has at least
t
solutions on
\mathbb{F}_{q}\cup\left\{\infty\right\}
.
Hence, this corresponds to the incidence structure
((\mathbb{F}_{q}\cup\{\infty_{k}\})\times\mathbb{F}_{q},\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]_{\leqslant k},\textnormal{I})
, with natural incidence.

A
t
-intersecting family in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]_{\leqslant k}
can be interpreted as a
t
-intersecting family in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[X,Y]_{k}
, but not vice versa.
We can thus carry characterisation results in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[X,Y]_{k}
over to
\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]_{\leqslant k}
.

The crucial difference between the incidence structures is that for any
k^{\prime}<k
,
(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2},\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]_{\leqslant k},\textnormal{I})
has
(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2},\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]_{\leqslant k^{\prime}},\textnormal{I})
as a substructure.
This no longer holds for
\mathbb{F}_{q}[X,Y]_{k}
, since altering
k
means that we alter
f(\infty)
.
We can no longer apply induction.

Not much is known in the homogeneous setting.
It is easy to extend
Section

1.2
to this setting if
t<k
.
However, if
t=k
, we find a quadratic upper bound on
k
-intersecting families in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[X,Y]_{k}
, while we would expect a linear upper bound.

Proposition 1.5
.

Suppose that
t\leqslant k<q
, and
k\geqslant 2
. Let
\mathcal{F}
be a
t
-intersecting family in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[X,Y]_{k}
.

(1)

If
t<k
, then
|\mathcal{F}|\leqslant q^{k+1-t}
.

(2)

If
t=k
, then
|\mathcal{F}|<\frac{q^{2}-1}{k}+1
.

We note that
Section

1.2
(2) extends
[
Adr24
, Proposition 4.6.2]
, which in turn uses an argument by Blokhuis and Bruen
[
BB89
]
.
Moreover, there is a good reason why the case
t=k
does not give the upper bound
|\mathcal{F}|\leqslant q
.
Namely, if
q
is odd, then there are
2
-intersecting families in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[X,Y]_{2}
of size
\frac{3q-1}{2}

[
Adr24
, Proposition 4.6.1]
.
This construction is based on
[
BB89
]
.

Let us now focus on the case
t=1
.
A similar stability result to
Section

1.2
(
2
) holds.

Result 1.6

(
[
Adr22a
, Corollary 6.7]
)
.

If
\mathcal{F}
is an intersecting family in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[X,Y]_{2}
with
|\mathcal{F}|\geqslant\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}q^{2}+2\sqrt{2}q+8
, then either
\mathcal{F}
is contained in a star, or
q
is even, and
\mathcal{F}
is contained in an intersecting family of the form
\left\{aX^{2}+bXY+cY^{2}\,\,||\,\,a,c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\right\}
for some fixed
b\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
.

In this paper, we extend the result to polynomials of higher degree.
We will prove the following theorem, which is the main theorem of this paper.

Theorem 1.7
.

For every integer
k\geqslant 3
, there exists a constant
\sigma_{k}
, such that for all prime powers
q
, every intersecting family
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}[X,Y]_{k}
with
|\mathcal{F}|\geqslant\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}q^{k}+\sigma_{k}q^{k-1}
is contained in a star.

Note that since an intersecting family in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]_{\leqslant k}
represents an intersecting family in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[X,Y]_{k}
,
1.7
also holds for
\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]_{\leqslant k}
.
We remark that we do not believe the constant
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
to be optimal, but it is the optimum of the current proof techniques.

1.3
Intersection problems for linear codes

1.7
can be interpreted to be dealing with the intersection problem for
extended Reed-Solomon codes
.
More generally, we can discuss intersection problems for linear codes.
Given a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, a
k
-dimensional subspace
C
of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
is called a
linear code
with parameters
[n,k]_{q}
, or more briefly a linear
[n,k]_{q}
code.
Throughout the paper, we always assume that the linear codes under consideration are
projective
, which means that there do
not
exist coordinate positions
i
and
j
, and a scalar
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
, such that
c(i)=\alpha c(j)
for all
c\in C
.

Definition 1.8
.

Given a linear code
C
, we can define the incidence structure
(\left\{1,\dots,n\right\}\times\mathbb{F}_{q},C,\textnormal{I})
, where
(i,\alpha)
is incident with
c\in C
if and only if
c(i)=\alpha
.
Then
\mathcal{F}\subseteq C
is a
t
-intersecting family
if and only if for any two codewords
c,c^{\prime}\in C
, there exist at least
t
coordinate positions
i
where
c(i)=c^{\prime}(i)
.
Equivalently, no two elements of
\mathcal{F}
are at Hamming distance more than
n-t
.

Let us introduce some general terminology, but before doing so introduce a useful convention.
Throughout the paper, we will work with real vector spaces whose coordinate positions are labelled by a finite set
X
.
It is then easier to interpret this vector space as the set
\mathbb{R}^{X}
of functions
X\to\mathbb{R}
.
These functions will still be referred to as vectors.
Similarly, if
M
is a matrix whose rows and columns are labelled by finite sets
X
and
Y
respectively, we will represent it as a function
M:X\times Y\to\mathbb{R}
.
The same convention holds for complex vector spaces.

Definition 1.9
.

Given a set
S
, define its
characteristic vector

\chi_{S}
to be the function that takes the value 1 on the elements of
S
and the value 0 anywhere else.

Typically when we discuss the characteristic vector of
S
, we restrict its domain to a natural superset of
S
.
In particular, for a family
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{B}
of blocks, we let the domain of
\chi_{\mathcal{F}}
be
\mathcal{B}
, so that
\chi_{\mathcal{F}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{B}}
.

Based on the terminology from
[
Mea19
]
, we say that
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{B},\textnormal{I})
has the

•

weak EKR property
if the size of the largest star is the size of the largest intersecting family (typically we study settings where all stars have the same size),

•

EKR module property
if the characteristic vector of any intersecting family of maximum size is a linear combination of characteristic vectors of stars,

•

strict EKR property
if the only intersecting families of maximum size are stars,

•

HM property
(named after Hilton and Milner
[
HM67
]
) if the strict EKR property holds, and the largest intersecting families that are not contained in a star are of the following form for some point
P\in\mathcal{P}
, and some block
B\in\mathcal{B}
that is not incident with
P
:

\mathcal{F}=\left\{B^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}\,\,||\,\,P\textnormal{I}B^{\prime},\,(\exists Q\textnormal{I}B^{\prime})(Q\textnormal{I}B)\right\}\cup\left\{B\right\}.

In other words,
\mathcal{F}
consists of the block
B
, and all blocks through
P
that intersect
B
.
This family is easily checked to be intersecting, and any intersecting family of this form is said to be of
Hilton-Milner type
.

We will investigate for which linear codes
C
the above properties hold.
This is intimately tied to the geometry of the
projective system
of
C
(see
Section

2.4
for the definition).
We will prove the following theorem.

Theorem 1.10
.

Let
C
be a linear
[n,k]_{q}
code with projective system
\mathcal{S}
in
\PG(k-1,q)
.
Let
\mathcal{M}
denote the set of hyperplanes that don’t intersect
\mathcal{S}
.

(1)

C
has the weak EKR property if and only if
\mathcal{M}
is not empty.

(2)

C
has the EKR module property if and only if every point
P\notin\mathcal{S}
is incident with at least one hyperplane of
\mathcal{M}
.

(3)

If no 3 points of
\mathcal{S}
are collinear, and for every point
P\notin\mathcal{S}
,

\left||\left\{\Pi\in\mathcal{M}\,\,||\,\,P\in\Pi\right\}|-\frac{|\mathcal{M}|}{q}\right|<\frac{|\mathcal{M}|}{q\min\{\sqrt{n},q-1\}},

then
C
has the strict EKR property.

In addition, in order to prove
1.7
, we provide a set of sufficient conditions for a family of linear codes to ensure a stability result, putting us one step closer towards establishing the HM property.

Theorem 1.11
.

Let
\mathcal{Q}
be an infinite set of prime powers, and suppose that for each
q\in\mathcal{Q}
, there exists a linear code
C_{q}
with parameters
[n_{q},k]_{q}
(where
n_{q}
can vary, but
k
is fixed). Denote by
\mathcal{S}_{q}
the projective system associated with
C_{q}
, and by
\mathcal{M}_{q}
the set of hyperplanes that don’t intersect
\mathcal{S}_{q}
. Suppose that there exist constants
a,b,\delta,\mu,\tau
with
0<\mu<1
such that the following properties hold for all of
q\in\mathcal{Q}
:

(1)

no 3 points of
\mathcal{S}_{q}
are collinear,

(2)

n_{q}\leqslant aq+b
,

(3)

\big||\mathcal{M}_{q}|-\mu q^{k-1}\big|\leqslant\tau q^{k-2}
,

(4)

for each point
P
of
\PG(k-1,q)
outside of
\mathcal{S}_{q}
,
\left|\left\{\Pi\in\mathcal{M}_{q}\,\,||\,\,P\in\Pi\right\}-\frac{|\mathcal{M}_{q}|}{q}\right|\leqslant\delta q^{k-3}
.

Then there exists a constant
\sigma
such that in each of the codes
C_{q}
, every intersecting family
\mathcal{F}
with

|\mathcal{F}|\geqslant\max\left\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+a^{-1}}},\,1-\mu\right\}q^{k-1}+\sigma q^{k-2}

is contained in a star.

1.4
Overview of the proof strategy and the paper

A crucial tool in the proof strategy are certain Cayley graphs, sometimes called
linear graphs
, see e.g.
[
God10
, §6.4]
.
These are Cayley graphs
\Gamma
over a finite vector space
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}
, where the connection set
S
is closed with respect to non-zero scalar multiplication.
Associate with
S
the set
\mathcal{M}
of hyperplanes in
\PG(k-1,q)
which are of the form
v^{\perp}
for some
v\in S
.
Then the eigenvalues of
\Gamma
are determined by the distribution of the hyperplanes of
\mathcal{M}
over the points of
\PG(k-1,q)
.
In the context of a linear code
C
, we take
\mathcal{M}
to be the set of hyperplanes that don’t intersect the projective system associated with
C
.
The corresponding Cayley graph
\Gamma_{0}(C)
is then the Cayley graph on
C
where adjacency is given by not being intersecting.
Note that cocliques in this graph coincide with intersecting families.

Using this geometric framework for studying eigenvalues of linear graphs, we can prove
1.10
(
1
,
2
).
This will be the main focus of
Section

3

The proofs of
1.10
(
3
) and
1.11
require a more advanced strategy.
We build on the tools developed by the author in
[
Adr22a
]
, and vastly expand their applicability.
The strategy is as follows.
Consider a linear code
C
, a large intersecting family
\mathcal{F}
in
C
, and let
\mathcal{F}_{i,\alpha}
denote the star
\left\{c\in C\,\,||\,\,c(i)=\alpha\right\}
.

(1)

We prove that for any star
\mathcal{F}_{i,\alpha}
,
|\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{F}_{i,\alpha}|
is either small or large, i.e. we prove that
|\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{F}_{i,\alpha}|\notin[s,t]
for some integers
s<t
.
In order to prove this, we construct a graph
B(C,i,\alpha)
.
This graph is bipartite, with one part being the star
\mathcal{F}_{i,\alpha}
, the other part the remainder of
C
, and adjacency is given by not being intersecting.
Then
\mathcal{F}
is still a coclique in this graph, and the bipartite expander mixing lemma gives a bound on
|\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{F}_{i,\alpha}|\cdot|\mathcal{F}\setminus\mathcal{F}_{i,\alpha}|
.
This will be done in
Section

4.1
.

(2)

We then prove that there is a star
\mathcal{F}_{i,\alpha}
that contains a sizeable portion of
\mathcal{F}
.
To start, we need a good lower bound on the second smallest eigenvalue of
\Gamma_{0}(C)
.
We can then use this to prove that the characteristic vector
\chi_{\mathcal{F}}
of
\mathcal{F}
, when orthogonally projected onto the space spanned by the characteristic vectors of the stars, is large.
By an averaging argument, it follows that
\mathcal{F}
has a large intersection with some star
\mathcal{F}_{i,\alpha}
.
This is done in
Section

4.2
.

(3)

To finish the proof, we use Step (
2
) to prove that some star
\mathcal{F}_{i,\alpha}
intersects
\mathcal{F}
in at least
s
points (with
s
as in Step (
1
).
By Step
1
, this implies that
\mathcal{F}_{i,\alpha}
intersects
\mathcal{F}
in more than
t
points.
If
t
is large enough, this suffices to conclude that
\mathcal{F}
is a subset of
\mathcal{F}_{i,\alpha}
.

We use this strategy to prove
1.10
(
3
) in
Section

4.3
and
1.11
in
Section

4.4
.

In
Section

5
, we first prove
Section

1.2
, and then apply
1.11
to extended Reed-Solomon codes to prove
1.7
.

We conclude the paper with some open problems in
Section

6
.

In
Appendix

A
, we delve into the theory of association (and specifically translation) schemes.
This allows us to determine the exact eigenvalues of some graphs defined on homogeneous polynomials over finite fields.
These eigenvalues can be useful for further investigation of intersection problems.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Projective geometry

We start by recalling some basic notions from finite projective geometry.
We refer the reader to
[
Hir79
]
.
The projective space
\PG(k-1,q)
is defined to be the collection of all vector subspaces of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}
, equipped with the natural incidence relation, i.e. two elements
\pi
and
\rho
of
\PG(k-1,q)
are incident if and only if one is contained in the other.
We will use terminology from projective geometry.
In particular, if
U
is a subspace of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}
and its bases have
t
elements, then we say that it has (projective) dimension
t-1
.
Subspaces of dimension
0
,
1
, and
2
are called points, lines, and planes respectively.
Subspaces of codimension
1
are called hyperplanes.
Given a non-zero vector
(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})^{\top}

Definition 2.1
.

Consider a subspace
\rho
in
\PG(k-1,q)
of dimension

.
We define the

.

2.2
Eigenvalue bounds

Eigenvalue bounds from spectral graph theory form a versatile framework to tackle intersection problems.
Suppose that
R\subseteq X\times X
is a symmetric binary relation on a finite set
X
.
We can interpret
R
as an undirected graph
\Gamma
(possibly containing loops) on
X
.
We define the
adjacency matrix
of
R
(or of
\Gamma
) as

A_{R}=A_{\Gamma}:X\times X\to\mathbb{R}:(x,y)\mapsto\begin{cases}1&\text{if }(x,y)\in R,\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Since
R
is symmetric,
A_{R}
is a symmetric matrix, and thus admits an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors, and has only real eigenvalues.
We let
\lambda_{1}\geqslant\dots\geqslant\lambda_{|X|}
denote the eigenvalues of
A_{R}
, counted by multiplicity.
If we want to emphasize the relation
R
or the graph
\Gamma
, we might write
\lambda_{i}(R)
or
\lambda_{i}(\Gamma)
instead of
\lambda_{i}
.

The
expander mixing lemma
provides a ubiquitous framework for graph eigenvalue bounds.
We state two such bounds, which play an important role in this paper.
First some definitions and conventions:

•

A
coclique
in a graph is a set of vertices which are pairwise not adjacent.
A
clique
is a set of vertices which are pairwise adjacent.

•

If
S
is a subset of vertices of
\Gamma
, the domain of the characteristic vector
\chi_{S}
of
S
is understood to be the vertex set of
\Gamma
.

•

We denote the all-one vector (or the constant function taking value 1) by
\mathbf{1}
, and the zero vector by
\mathbf{0}
.

Result 2.2

(Hoffman ratio bound
[
GM16
, Theorem 2.4.1]
)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a
k
-regular graph on
n
vertices.
Suppose that
S
is a coclique in
\Gamma
.
Then

|S|\leqslant\frac{n}{\frac{k}{|\lambda_{n}|}+1}.

Equality holds if and only if
\chi_{S}
is in the span of
\mathbf{1}
and the
\lambda_{n}
-eigenspace of
A_{\Gamma}
.

The Hoffman ratio bound has proved itself to be a versatile tool to establish the EKR module property in many different settings.
We refer the reader to
[
GM16
]
for a detailed account of this approach.

The second eigenvalue bound that we discuss, concerns bipartite graphs.
Recall that a graph
\Gamma
is called
bipartite
if its vertices can be partitioned into two cocliques
L
and
R
.
We call
\Gamma

(d_{L},d_{R})
-biregular
if the degree of any vertex in
L
is
d_{L}
and the degree of any vertex in
R
is
d_{R}
.
Given a pair of sets
S\subseteq L
and
T\subseteq R
of vertices, we denote by

the number of edges in
\Gamma
with endpoints in
S
and
T
.

Result 2.3

(Bipartite expander mixing lemma
[
Hae95
, Theorem 5.1]
)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a
(d_{L},d_{R})
-biregular bipartite graph on
L\cup R
.
Take
S\subseteq L
and
T\subseteq R
.
Then

\left(e(S,T)-\frac{d_{R}}{|L|}|S||T|\right)^{2}\leqslant\lambda_{2}^{2}|S|\frac{|L|-|S|}{|L|}|T|\frac{|R|-|T|}{|T|}.

2.3
Eigenvalues of abelian Cayley (multi)graphs

Suppose that
G
is an abelian group.
Let
M
be a multiset with
G
as ground set.
Formally, this means that
M
is defined by a multiplicity function
\mu:G\to\mathbb{N}
.
Then
M
defines a directed multigraph
\Gamma
with vertex set
G
, where the arc
(g,h)
has multiplicity
\mu(h-g)
.
We call
\Gamma
a directed
Cayley multigraph
, and denote it as
\Gamma=\Cay(G,M)
or
\Cay(G,\mu)
.
Note that
\Cay(G,M)
is a simple, loopless, undirected graph if and only if
M
is an ordinary set that is closed under taking inverses, and
M
does not contain the identity element.
In this case, we call
\Cay(G,M)
a

.

The adjacency matrix of a directed Cayley multigraph
\Gamma=\Cay(G,M)
is defined as the matrix

A_{\Gamma}:G\times G\to\mathbb{N}:(g,h)\mapsto\mu(g-h).

The characters of
G
constitute an orthogonal basis of eigenvectors of
A_{\Gamma}
, see e.g.
[
LZ22
, Theorem 1]
.
Indeed, if
\chi:G\to\mathbb{C}
is a character of
G

(A_{\Gamma}\chi)(g)=\sum_{h\in G}A_{\Gamma}(g,h)\chi(h)=\sum_{h\in G}\mu(h-g)\chi(h)=\sum_{h^{\prime}\in G}\mu(h^{\prime})\chi(g+h^{\prime})=\left(\sum_{h^{\prime}\in G}\mu(h^{\prime})\chi(h^{\prime})\right)\chi(g).

It follows that
\chi
is an eigenvector of
A_{\Gamma}
with eigenvalue
\sum_{h\in G}\mu(h)\chi(h)
, which we denote by
\chi(\Gamma)
.
It is also well known that the group of characters
\hat{G}
is isomorphic to
G
, and there is a way to write
\hat{G}=\left\{\chi_{g}\,\,||\,\,g\in G\right\}
such that
G\to\hat{G}:g\mapsto\chi_{g}
is a group isomorphism.

In case
G=\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}
with
q=p^{h}
and

Let

Then we can take
\chi_{v}:\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}\to\mathbb{C}:w\mapsto\zeta^{T(v^{\top}w)}

Now consider the special case where

\mu(\alpha v)=\mu(v)
for all non-zero scalars

and vectors

Let

\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}

\PG(k-1,q)
.
Then

\chi_{v}(\Gamma)=\mu(\mathbf{0})+\sum_{w\in G^{\prime}}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}}\mu(\alpha w)\chi_{v}(\alpha w)=\mu(\mathbf{0})+\sum_{w\in G^{\prime}}\mu(w)\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}}\zeta^{T(\alpha v^{\top}w)}.

(1)

If we consider
\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\zeta^{T(\alpha v^{\top}w)}

In that case, the sum sees every power of

times, which makes the sum equal to zero.
Considering that (

\chi_{v}(\Gamma)=\mu(\mathbf{0})+(q-1)\sum_{w\in G^{\prime}\cap v^{\perp}}\mu(w)-\sum_{w\in G^{\prime}\setminus v^{\perp}}\mu(w)=\mu(\mathbf{0})+q\sum_{w\in G^{\prime}\cap v^{\perp}}\mu(w)-\sum_{w\in G^{\prime}}\mu(w).

(2)

If
G
is a Cayley graph, then

is an ordinary set, not containing
\mathbf{0}
.
Define the set
M^{\prime}=G^{\prime}\cap M
containing a unique scalar multiple of each element of
M
.
Then the above equation simplifies to

\displaystyle\chi_{v}(\Gamma)=q|M^{\prime}\cap v^{\perp}|-|M^{\prime}|.

2.4
Linear codes and projective systems

We recall some notions from coding theory.
The reader is referred to
[
Bal20
]
or
[
MS77
]
for more background.
Let
q
denote a prime power, and
\mathbb{F}_{q}
the finite field of order
q
.
A
linear
[n,k]_{q}
-code
is a
k
-dimensional subspace
C
of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
.
Recall that
C
is called
projective
if no two coordinates of
C
are linearly dependent, i.e. for every
i
and
j
,
\left\{(c(i),c(j))\,\,||\,\,c\in C\right\}=\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2}
.
We assume all codes to be projective.
If
C^{\prime}\leqslant C
is a linear
[n,k^{\prime}]_{q}
code, we call
C^{\prime}
a
subcode
of
C
.
The
support
of a vector
v\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
is the set

\supp(v)=\left\{i\in\{1,\dots,n\}\,\,||\,\,v(i)\neq 0\right\}

of coordinate positions where
v
has a non-zero entry, and the
(Hamming) weight
of
v
is defined as
\wt(v)=|\supp(v)|
, i.e. the number of non-zero entries in
v
.
The
minimum weight
of a linear code
C
is defined as

d=\min\left\{\wt(c)\,\,||\,\,c\in C\setminus\left\{\mathbf{0}\right\}\right\}.

If we want to emphasize the minimum weight
d
of a linear
[n,k]_{q}
code
C
, we may write that
C
is a linear
[n,k,d]_{q}
code.
The
Singleton bound
states that if a linear
[n,k,d]_{q}
code exists, then
d\leqslant n-k+1
.
In case of equality, we say that
C
is an
MDS code
, which stands for maximum distance separable.
The
weight distribution
of
C
is defined as the sequence
(W_{0},\dots,W_{n})
, where
W_{t}=|\left\{c\in C\,\,||\,\,\wt(c)=t\right\}|
equals the number of codewords of weight
t
.
The weight distribution of MDS codes is known.

Result 2.4

(
[
MS77
, Theorem 11.6 (page 321)]
)
.

Suppose that
C
is a linear
[n,k,d=n-k+1]_{q}
MDS code.
Then its weight distribution is given by
(1,0,\dots,0,W_{d},\dots,W_{n})
where for
t\geqslant d
,

W_{t}=(q-1)\binom{n}{t}\sum_{j=0}^{t-d}(-1)^{j}\binom{t-1}{j}q^{t-d-j}.

The prototypical example of MDS codes is given by the extended Reed-Solomon codes.
Recall that if we fix a degree
k
, then for all polynomials
f(X)=\sum_{i=0}^{k}a_{i}X^{i}
of degree at most
k
, we define
f(\infty)=a_{k}
.

Definition 2.5
.

Consider the finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, and choose an integer
k<q
.
Label the elements of the field so that
\mathbb{F}_{q}=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{q}\}
.
Consider the map

Ev:\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]_{\leqslant k}\to\mathbb{F}_{q}^{q+1}:f\mapsto(f(x_{1}),\dots,f(x_{q},1),f(\infty)).

Then the image of
Ev
is a linear
[q+1,k+1]_{q}
code, called the
extended Reed-Solomon code
, and denoted
ERS(q,k)
.

We refer the reader to
[
Bal20
, §6.2]
for more on (extended) Reed-Solomon codes.
The most important property is that they are MDS codes.

Result 2.6

(see e.g.
[
Bal20
, Lemma 6.5]
)
.

For each
k<q
, the extended Reed-Solomon code
ERS(q,k)
has minimum weight
q+2-k
, and hence is an MDS code.

We call a matrix
G\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k\times n}
a
generator matrix
for the linear
[n,k]_{q}
code
C
if the rows of
G
form a basis for
C
.
Let
P_{i}
denote the 1-dimensional subspace of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}
spanned by the
i
th
column of
G
.
Then
(P_{1},\dots,P_{n})
can be viewed as a collection of points in the projective space
\PG(k-1,q)
.
We call
(P_{1},\dots,P_{n})
a
projective system
associated with
C
.
Note that all points
P_{i}
are distinct because
C
is assumed to be projective.
The set of generator matrices of
C
equals
\left\{MG\,\,||\,\,M\in\GL(k,q)\right\}
.
It readily follows that the set of projective systems associated with
C
forms an orbit of the action of
\PGL(k,q)
on ordered
n
-tuples of points of
\PG(k-1,q)
.
We typically do not care about the order of the points of the projective system:
Two linear codes
C
and
C^{\prime}
are said to be
equivalent
if
C
can be transformed into
C^{\prime}
by coordinate transformations and scaling in each coordinate position, i.e.

C^{\prime}=\left\{\big(\alpha_{1}c(\sigma(1)),\ldots,\alpha_{n}c(\sigma(n))\big)\,\,||\,\,c\in C\right\}

for some permutation
\sigma
on
\left\{1,\dots,n\right\}
, and some non-zero scalars
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}
.
It is easy to check that two codes are equivalent if and only if they correspond to the same unordered projective system.

The projective systems of the extended Reed-Solomon codes will play a central role in this paper.

Definition 2.7
.

Let
\mathcal{P}_{1}
and
\mathcal{P}_{k}
denote the sets of points of
\PG(1,q)
and
\PG(k,q)
respectively.
Define the map

\nu_{k}:\mathcal{P}_{1}\to\mathcal{P}_{k}:(x:y)\mapsto(x^{k}:x^{k-1}y:\ldots:xy^{k-1}:y^{k}).

The image
\mathcal{N}
of
\nu_{k}
is called the
canonical normal rational curve
in
\PG(k,q)
.
Any image of
\mathcal{N}
under the group of collineations
\PGL(k+1,q)
is called a
normal rational curve
.

More information on normal rational curves can be found in
[
Hir85
, §21.1]
and
[
HT16
, §6.5]
.
Using the fact that
Ev(X^{k}),\dots,Ev(X),Ev(1)
forms a basis for
ERS(q,k)
, the link between extended Reed-Solomon codes and normal rational curves follows easily.

Lemma 2.8
.

The projective systems associated to the extended Reed-Solomon code
ERS(q,k)
are the normal rational curves in
\PG(k,q)
.

The fact that
ERS(q,k)
is an MDS code is equivalent to normal rational curves being so-called
arcs
in
\PG(k,q)
.
This means the following.

Result 2.9

(
[
Hir85
, Theorem 21.1.1 (iv)]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{N}
be a normal rational curve in
\PG(k,q)
.
Any subspace of dimension
r<k
of
\PG(k,q)
contains at most
r+1
points of
\mathcal{N}
.

Lastly, we remark an important connection between a linear code
C
and the geometry of its projective system
\mathcal{S}
arising from a generator matrix
G
of
C
.
For a vector
v\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}
, let
v^{\perp}
denote the subspace
\left\{w\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}\,\,||\,\,v^{\top}w=0\right\}
.
We can interpret
v^{\perp}
as a hyperplane of
\PG(k-1,q)
(or the whole space if
v=\mathbf{0}
).
A codeword
c\in C
can uniquely be written as
v^{\top}G
for some
v\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}
.
Note that
\wt(c)
equals the number of points
P_{i}
not contained in
v^{\perp}
.
Hence, the distribution of the points
P_{1},\dots,P_{n}
over the hyperplanes of
\PG(k-1,q)
tells us the weight distribution of the code
C
.

3
The EKR module property for linear codes

In this section we start the investigation of intersection problems for linear codes
C
.
The relation between
C
and its projective system
\mathcal{S}
will be crucial.
As an example, the reader can keep the case where
C=ERS(q,k)
is an extended Reed-Solomon code, and
\mathcal{S}
is a normal rational curve in mind.

We start with a simple observation.

Proposition 3.1
.

Suppose that
C
is a linear
[n,k]_{q}
code which has a linear
[n,t]_{q}
MDS subcode.
Then the largest
t
-intersecting families of
C
have size
q^{k-t}
.

Proof.

Suppose that
C^{\prime}\leqslant C
is a linear
[n,t,n-t+1]_{q}
MDS subcode.
Then no two elements of any additive coset of
C^{\prime}
are
t
-intersecting.
Therefore, a
t
-intersecting family
\mathcal{F}
in
C
contains at most 1 element from each of the
q^{k-t}
additive cosets of
C^{\prime}
in
C
.
This proves that
|\mathcal{F}|\leqslant q^{k-t}
.

Now pick
t
distinct coordinate positions
i_{1},\dots,i_{t}
, and
t
(not necessarily distinct) scalars
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{t}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
Define the
t
-star

\mathcal{F}=\left\{c\in C\,\,||\,\,(\forall j\in\{1,\dots,t\})(c(i_{j})=\alpha_{j})\right\}.

Then
\mathcal{F}
is a subspace of
C
of co-dimension at most
t
, hence contains at least
q^{k-t}
elements.
Since it is
t
-intersecting, we must have
|\mathcal{F}|=q^{k-t}
.
∎

This establishes the weak EKR property.

Corollary 3.2
.

If
C
is a linear
[n,k]_{q}
code, then either
C
is an intersecting family, or the largest intersecting families have size
q^{k-1}
.

Proof.

Either
C
has no codewords of weight
n
, in which case it is an intersecting family, or it does have a codeword
c
of weight
n
, in which case
\left\langle c\right\rangle
is a
[n,1]_{q}
MDS subcode of
C
, and
Section

3
finishes the proof.
∎

Next, we want to investigate whether the EKR module property holds.
In order to do so, we first determine the eigenvalues of relevant graphs.

Definition 3.3
.

(1)

Let
C
be a linear
[n,k]_{q}
code, and
T\subseteq\{0,\dots,n-1\}
. Define the graph

\Gamma_{T}(C)=\Cay(C,\left\{c\in C\,\,||\,\,n-\wt(c)\in T\right\}),

on
C
, where
c
and
c^{\prime}
are adjacent if and only they coincide in exactly
t
coordinates for some

.

(2)

Suppose that
G
is a generator matrix of
C
, and
H
is a right inverse.
For each
v\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}\setminus\left\{\mathbf{0}\right\}
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
, define the vector

\xi_{v,\alpha}:C\to\mathbb{R}:c\mapsto\begin{cases}1&\text{if }cHv=\alpha,\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Note that
\xi_{v,\alpha}=\xi_{\beta v,\beta\alpha}
for each non-zero scalar
\beta
.

Note that if
c
is a codeword of
C
, then
cHv
is a linear condition on
c
in the form
\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Hv)(i)c(i)=\alpha
.
In particular, if
Hv
is a standard basis vector, then the condition becomes
c(i)=\alpha
for a certain

.
This means that
\xi_{v,\alpha}
is the characteristic vector of a star.
Since
H
is a right inverse of
G
, this happens if and only if
v
is a column of
G
, in which case
\left\langle v\right\rangle
is in the projective system of
C

G
.
Note also that since
H
is a right inverse of
G
,
\chi_{v,\alpha}
and
\chi_{w,\beta}
will be linearly independent if
v
and
w
are linearly independent.

We will be interested in the component of
\xi_{v,\alpha}
orthogonal to
\mathbf{1}
, which equals
\xi_{v,\alpha}-\frac{1}{q}\mathbf{1}
.
Note that
\left\{\xi_{v,\alpha}\,\,||\,\,\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\right\}
is linearly independent, since the vectors have disjoint support.
However, their projections onto
\mathbf{1}^{\perp}
are no longer linearly independent since

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\left(\xi_{v,\alpha}-\frac{1}{q}\mathbf{1}\right)=\left(\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\xi_{v,\alpha}\right)-\left(\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\frac{1}{q}\mathbf{1}\right)=\mathbf{1}-\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{0}.

(4)

However, since we project
q
linearly independent vectors onto a hyperplane, their projections must still span a
(q-1)

\dim\left\langle\xi_{v,\alpha}-\frac{1}{q}\mathbf{1}\,\|\,\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\right\rangle=q-1.

Therefore, (
4
) describes the only linear dependence between the vectors, and any subset
q-1
of the vectors
\xi_{v,\alpha}-\frac{1}{q}\mathbf{1}
must be linearly independent.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
C
be a linear
[n,k]_{q}
code with generator matrix
G
, and projective system
\mathcal{S}
.
Choose
T\subseteq\{0,\dots,n-1\}
.
Then the set
\left\{\mathbf{1}\right\}\cup\left\{\xi_{v,\alpha}-\frac{1}{q}\mathbf{1}\,\,||\,\,v\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}\setminus\mathbf{0},\,\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}\right\}
is a basis of eigenvectors for
A_{\Gamma_{T}(C)}
.
Let
\mathcal{M}
be the set of hyperplanes
\Pi
with
|\Pi\cap\mathcal{S}|\in T
.
Then the eigenvalue of
\mathbf{1}
equals
(q-1)|\mathcal{M}|
, and the eigenvalue of
\xi_{v,\alpha}-\frac{1}{q}\mathbf{1}
equals
q|\left\{\Pi\in\mathcal{M}\,\,||\,\,\left\langle v\right\rangle\in\Pi\right\}|-|\mathcal{M}|
.

Proof.

The group
(C,+)
is isomorphic to
(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k},+)
.
An explicit isomorphism is given by
f:\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}\to C:v\mapsto v^{\top}G
.
Note that if
H
is a right inverse for
G
, then
f^{-1}(c)=(cH)^{\top}
.
Therefore,
\Gamma_{T}(C)
is isomorphic to the Cayley graph
\Gamma^{\prime}=\Cay(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k},S_{T})
with

S_{T}=\left\{v\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}\,\,||\,\,n-\wt(v^{\top}G)\in T\right\}.

It is easy to check that
S_{T}
is closed under taking non-zero scalar multiples.
Hence, we can use (
2
) to determine the eigenvalues of
\Gamma^{\prime}
.
Construct the set
S^{\prime}_{T}
by choosing a unique scalar multiple of every vector in
S_{T}
.
Note that there is a one-to-one correspondence between the elements of
S^{\prime}_{T}
and the elements of
\mathcal{M}
.
Indeed,
v\in S_{T}
if and only if

.
Since

contains a unique scalar multiple of every vector of

, for every hyperplane
\Pi\in\mathcal{M}
, there is a unique

with
\Pi=v^{\perp}
.

Take a non-trivial character
\chi_{v}
of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}
, as defined in

.
Then by (

\chi_{v}(S_{T})=q|S^{\prime}_{T}\cap v^{\perp}|-|S_{T}^{\prime}|=q|\left\{\Pi\in\mathcal{M}\,\,||\,\,v\in\Pi\right\}-|\mathcal{M}|.

It follows that
\chi_{v}\circ f^{-1}
must form a basis of eigenvectors of
A_{\Gamma_{T}(C)}
.
To finish the proof, it suffices to show that for every non-zero vector
v\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}
,

\left\langle\chi_{\alpha v}\circ f^{-1}\,\|\,\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}\right\rangle=\left\langle\xi_{v,\alpha}-\frac{1}{q}\mathbf{1}\,\|\,\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}\right\rangle,

or equivalently that

\left\langle\chi_{\alpha v}\,\|\,\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}\right\rangle=\left\langle\left(\xi_{v,\alpha}-\frac{1}{q}\mathbf{1}\right)\circ f\,\|\,\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}\right\rangle

Since all vectors listed above are orthogonal to
\mathbf{1}
, it suffices to show that

\left\langle\mathbf{1},\left\langle\chi_{\alpha v}\,\|\,\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}\right\rangle\right\rangle=\left\langle\mathbf{1},\left\langle\left(\xi_{v,\alpha}-\frac{1}{q}\mathbf{1}\right)\circ f\,\|\,\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}\right\rangle\right\rangle.

This means that

\left\langle\chi_{\alpha v}\,\|\,\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\right\rangle=\left\langle\xi_{v,\alpha}\circ f\,\|\,\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\right\rangle.

Since both spaces have dimension
q
, it suffices to check that the left one is a subspace of the right one.
Note that
\xi_{v,\alpha}\circ f(w)
equals 1 if
w^{\top}GHv=w^{\top}v=\alpha
, and 0 otherwise.
It follows that
\left\langle\xi_{v,\alpha}\circ f\,\|\,\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\right\rangle
is the space of functions

for which

only depends on
v^{\top}w
.
The fact that

only depends on
v^{\top}w
finishes the proof.
∎

Now we are ready to describe how the EKR module property holds, possibly after a slight alteration.

Definition 3.5

Suppose that

Denote the

Consider the graph

Let

Problem 3.9
.

Is there a geometric property of

The answer is not obvious.
In the next section, we give a sufficient condition for the strict EKR property to hold, and in fact to even prove stability results.

4
The strict EKR property and beyond for linear codes

As discussed in

We consider a linear code

in

4.1
Intersecting every star in few or many codewords

In this section, we will prove the following theorem.

Theorem 4.1
.

Consider a linear
[n,k]_{q}
code
C
with projective system
\mathcal{S}
.
Suppose that
\mathcal{F}
is an intersecting family in
C
.
Choose
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
, and let
\mathcal{F}_{i,\alpha}
be the star
\left\{c\in C\,\,||\,\,c(i)=\alpha\right\}
.
Let
Q\in\mathcal{S}
be the point corresponding to coordinate position
i
.
Define
\mathcal{M}
to be the set of hyperplanes of
\PG(k-1,q)
that don’t intersect
\mathcal{S}
.
For each line
\ell
through
Q
, define

\lambda_{\ell}=q\sum_{P\in\ell\setminus\left\{Q\right\}}\left(|\left\{\Pi\in\mathcal{M}\,\,||\,\,P\in\Pi\right\}|-\frac{|\mathcal{M}|}{q}\right)^{2},

and let
\lambda
be the maximum value of
\lambda_{\ell}
over all lines

through

.
Then

|\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{F}_{i,\alpha}|\cdot|\mathcal{F}\setminus\mathcal{F}_{i,\alpha}|\leqslant\lambda\left(\frac{q^{k-1}}{|\mathcal{M}|}\right)^{2}

We prove

by an application of the bipartite expander mixing lemma to the following graph.

Definition 4.2
.

Given a linear
[n,k]_{q}
code
C
, an integer

, and a scalar
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
, define the bipartite graph
B(C,i,\alpha)
as having vertex sets
L=\left\{c\in C\,\,||\,\,c(i)=\alpha\right\}
and
R=C\setminus L
, where
c\in L
is adjacent to

if and only if
c
and
c^{\prime}
don’t intersect, i.e.
\wt(c-c^{\prime})=n
.

As a first step, let us check that

is biregular, which is a necessary condition for applying the bipartite expander mixing lemma.

Lemma 4.3
.

Use the notation of
Section

4.1
.

(1)

For
\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
, the graphs
B(C,i,\alpha)
and
B(C,i,\alpha^{\prime})
are isomorphic.

(2)

The automorphism group of
B(C,i,\alpha)
has subgroups
G_{L}
and
G_{R}
that act sharply transitively on
L
and
R
respectively.
Therefore,
B(C,i,\alpha)
is biregular.

Proof.

(1) It is easy to check that if
c\in C
with
c(i)=\alpha^{\prime}-\alpha
, then the map

f:C\to C:c^{\prime}\mapsto c^{\prime}+c

is an isomorphism from
B(C,i,\alpha)
to
B(C,i,\alpha^{\prime})
.

(2) Using (1), it suffices to prove the statement for
\alpha=0
.
Define
G_{L}=\left\{c\in C\,\,||\,\,c(i)=0\right\}
and
define the group
(G_{R},*)=(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*},\cdot)\ltimes(G_{L},+)
, with operation

(\beta,c^{\prime})*(\gamma,c^{\prime\prime})=(\beta\gamma,c^{\prime}+\beta c^{\prime\prime}).

Then
G_{R}
acts on
C
as follows:

(\beta,c):C\to C:c^{\prime}\mapsto\beta c^{\prime}+c.

It is easy to see that this action stabilises
L
and
R
, and preserves adjacency.
If
c,c^{\prime}\in R
, then
\left(\frac{c^{\prime}(i)}{c(i)},c^{\prime}-\frac{c^{\prime}(i)}{c(i)}c\right)
is the unique element of
G_{R}
sending
c
to
c^{\prime}
, so
G_{R}
acts sharply transitively on
R
.
Moreover, the subgroup
G_{L}
of
G_{R}
acts sharply transitively on
L
.
∎

We can use
B(C,i,\alpha)
to define a distance 2 multigraph
B^{2}
, where the multiplicity of edge
\{c,c^{\prime}\}
equals the number of walks of length two from

to
c^{\prime}
.
The previous lemma implies that

is the disjoint union of two Cayley multigraphs, one of which is abelian.
This allows us to determine the eigenvalues of the adjacency matrix of

using characters, as described in
Section

2.3
.
This in turn reveals the eigenvalues of the adjacency matrix of

.

Proposition 4.4
.

Suppose that
C
is a linear
[n,k]_{q}
code with projective system
\mathcal{S}
.
Suppose that

corresponds to coordinate

of

.
Let
\mathcal{M}
denote the set of hyperplanes that don’t intersect
\mathcal{S}
.
Then the spectrum of the adjacency matrix of

•

Add the eigenvalues
\pm\sqrt{q-1}|\mathcal{M}|
with multiplicity 1,

•

for each line
\ell
through

, add the eigenvalues

\pm\left(q\sum_{P\in\ell\setminus\left\{Q\right\}}\left(|\left\{\Pi\in\mathcal{M}\,\,||\,\,P\in\mathcal{M}\right\}|-\frac{|\mathcal{M}|}{q}\right)^{2}\right)^{1/2}

,

•

Let
B

.

(5)

|B(\mathbf{0})|-\sum_{w\in V^{\prime}}|B(\mathbf{0},w^{\top}G)|=(q-1)d_{R}-\frac{1}{q-1}(q-1)d_{R}(d_{R}-1)=-d_{R}(d_{R}-q).

(6)

.
We can rewrite it as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sum_{w\in V^{\prime}\cap v^{\perp}}&|B(\mathbf{0},w^{\top}G)|=|\left\{(w,u)\in(V^{\prime}\cap v^{\perp})\times\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}\,\,||\,\,u^{\top}G\in B(\mathbf{0},w^{\top}G)\right\}|\\

(7)

Note that

if and only if

is a hyperplane in
\mathcal{M}
.
Suppose now that we take two hyperplanes
\Pi_{1},\Pi_{2}\in\mathcal{M}

How many pairs

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sum_{w\in V^{\prime}\cap v^{\perp}}|B(\mathbf{0},w^{\top}G)|&=|\left\{(\Pi_{1},\Pi_{2})\in\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{M}\,\,||\,\,\Pi_{1}\neq\Pi_{2},\,\Pi_{1}\cap\ell=\Pi_{2}\cap\ell\right\}\\

(8)

Now we plug (

\displaystyle\chi_{v}(\Gamma)

\displaystyle=q\left(\left(\sum_{P\in\ell\setminus\left\{Q\right\}}|\left\{\Pi\in\mathcal{M}\,\,||\,\,P\in\Pi\right\}|^{2}\right)-d_{R}\right)-d_{R}(d_{R}-q)

\displaystyle=q\left(\sum_{P\in\ell\setminus\left\{Q\right\}}|\left\{\Pi\in\mathcal{M}\,\,||\,\,P\in\Pi\right\}|^{2}\right)-d_{R}^{2}

Using

4.2
Intersecting some star in more than few codewords

In this subsection, we explain, given a large intersecting family
\mathcal{F}
, how to find a star
\mathcal{F}_{i,\alpha}
that has a sizeable intersection with
\mathcal{F}
.

Theorem 4.7
.

Let
C
be a linear
[n,k]_{q}
code with projective system
\mathcal{S}
.
Suppose that through every point
P\notin\mathcal{S}
there are at least
t>0
hyperplanes that don’t intersect
\mathcal{S}
.
If
\mathcal{F}\subset C
is an intersecting family, then there exists a star
\mathcal{F}_{i,\alpha}=\left\{c\in C\,\,||\,\,c(i)=\alpha\right\}
such that

|\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{F}_{i,\alpha}|\geqslant\frac{|\mathcal{F}|}{q}+\frac{q^{k-1}}{n}\left(\frac{|\mathcal{F}|}{q^{k}}\left(\frac{|\mathcal{M}|}{t}-1\right)+1-\frac{|\mathcal{M}|}{qt}\right)

Proof.

Let
\Gamma_{0}(C)=\Cay(C,\left\{c\in C\,\,||\,\,\wt(c)=n\right\})
denote the graph on
C
where codewords are adjacent if and only if they don’t intersect.
Let
A=A_{\Gamma_{0}(C)}
denote its adjacency matrix.
Let
\mathcal{M}
denote the set of hyperplanes that don’t intersect
\mathcal{S}
.
We know from
Section

3
that the smallest eigenvalue of
A
is
-|\mathcal{M}|
, and the corresponding eigenspace
E_{-|\mathcal{M}|}
is spanned by the vectors
\xi_{v,\alpha}-\frac{1}{q}\mathbf{1}
with
\left\langle v\right\rangle
the points of
\mathcal{S}
.
Note that this means that
\xi_{v,\alpha}
are the characteristic vectors of the stars.
The second smallest eigenvalue (not counted with multiplicity) must be
\lambda=qt-|\mathcal{M}|
.

Consider the characteristic vector
\chi_{\mathcal{F}}
of
\mathcal{F}
.
We decompose it according to the eigenspaces of
A
.
This yields that

\chi_{\mathcal{F}}=\frac{|\mathcal{F}|}{q^{k}}\mathbf{1}+u_{1}+u_{2},

with
u_{1}\in E_{-|\mathcal{M}|}
and
u_{2}\in\left\langle\mathbf{1},E_{-|\mathcal{M}|}\right\rangle^{\perp}
.
Note that
\mathbf{1}
is an eigenvector of
A
with as eigenvalue the degree of
\Gamma_{0}(C)
, which equals
(q-1)|\mathcal{M}|
.
We have

|\mathcal{F}|=\chi_{\mathcal{F}}^{\top}\chi_{\mathcal{F}}=\frac{|\mathcal{F}|^{2}}{q^{2k}}\|\mathbf{1}\|^{2}+\|u_{1}\|^{2}+\|u_{2}\|^{2}=\frac{|\mathcal{F}|^{2}}{q^{k}}+\|u_{1}\|^{2}+\|u_{2}\|^{2}

We also know that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\chi_{\mathcal{F}}^{\top}A\chi_{\mathcal{F}}=\left(\frac{|\mathcal{F}|}{q^{k}}\mathbf{1}+u_{1}+u_{2}\right)^{\top}A\left(\frac{|\mathcal{F}|}{q^{k}}\mathbf{1}+u_{1}+u_{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{|\mathcal{F}|^{2}}{q^{2k}}(q-1)|\mathcal{M}|\|\mathbf{1}\|^{2}-|\mathcal{M}|\|u_{1}\|^{2}+u_{2}^{\top}Au_{2}.

In addition,

u_{2}^{\top}Au_{2}\geqslant\lambda\|u_{2}\|^{2}=(qt-|\mathcal{M}|)\left(|\mathcal{F}|-\frac{|\mathcal{F}|^{2}}{q^{k}}-\|u_{1}\|^{2}\right).

Hence,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{|\mathcal{F}|^{2}}{q^{k}}(q-1)|\mathcal{M}|-|\mathcal{M}|\|u_{1}\|^{2}+(qt-|\mathcal{M}|)\left(|\mathcal{F}|-\frac{|\mathcal{F}|^{2}}{q^{k}}-\|u_{1}\|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=|\mathcal{F}|\left(\frac{|\mathcal{F}|}{q^{k-1}}(|\mathcal{M}|-t)+qt-|\mathcal{M}|\right)-qt\|u_{1}\|^{2},

which implies that

\|u_{1}\|^{2}\geqslant|\mathcal{F}|\left(\frac{|\mathcal{F}|}{q^{k}}\left(\frac{|\mathcal{M}|}{t}-1\right)+1-\frac{|\mathcal{M}|}{qt}\right).

Now make a matrix
M
whose rows are labelled by
C
, whose columns are labelled by
\{1,\dots,n\}\times\mathbb{F}_{q}
, and where
M(c,(i,\alpha))
equals 1 if
c(i)=\alpha
and 0 otherwise.
In other words, the columns of
M
are the characteristic vectors of the stars.
We determine the eigenvalues and eigenspace of
MM^{\top}
.
First, note that

MM^{\top}\mathbf{1}=M(q^{k-1}\mathbf{1})=nq^{k-1}\mathbf{1}.

Next, consider the generator matrix
G
of
C
corresponding to
\mathcal{S}
, and let
v
be the
i
th
column of
G
, so that
\xi_{v,\alpha}
is the characteristic vector of
\mathcal{F}_{i,\alpha}
.
Then

M^{\top}\xi_{v,\alpha}(j,\beta)=\begin{cases}q^{k-1}&\text{if }(i,\alpha)=(j,\beta),\\
0&\text{if }i=j,\,\alpha\neq\beta,\\
q^{k-2}&\text{if }i\neq j.\end{cases}

Therefore,

MM^{\top}\xi_{v,\alpha}(c)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}M^{\top}\xi_{v,\alpha}(j,c(j))=(n-1)q^{k-2}+\begin{cases}q^{k-1}&\text{if }c(i)=\alpha,\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

In other words,
MM^{\top}\xi_{v,\alpha}=q^{k-1}\xi_{v,\alpha}+(n-1)q^{k-2}\mathbf{1}
.
It follows that

MM^{\top}\left(\xi_{v,\alpha}-\frac{1}{q}\mathbf{1}\right)=q^{k-1}\xi_{v,\alpha}+(n-1)q^{k-2}\mathbf{1}-\frac{1}{q}nq^{k-1}\mathbf{1}=q^{k-1}\left(\xi_{v,\alpha}-\frac{1}{q}\mathbf{1}\right).

Since the vectors
\xi_{v,\alpha}-\frac{1}{q}\mathbf{1}
span
E_{-|\mathcal{M}|}
, every vector in
E_{-|\mathcal{M}|}
is an eigenvector of
MM^{\top}
with eigenvalue
q^{k-1}
, and this applies in particular to
u_{1}
.
Lastly, since the column space of
M
spans
\left\langle\mathbf{1},E_{-|\mathcal{M}|}\right\rangle
, every vector orthogonal to
\left\langle\mathbf{1},E_{-|\mathcal{M}|}\right\rangle
is in the kernel of
MM^{\top}
, and this applies in particular to
u_{2}
.
We conclude that

\displaystyle\chi_{\mathcal{F}}^{\top}MM^{\top}\chi_{\mathcal{F}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{|\mathcal{F}|}{q^{k}}\mathbf{1}+u_{1}+u_{2}\right)^{\top}MM^{\top}\left(\frac{|\mathcal{F}|}{q^{k}}\mathbf{1}+u_{1}+u_{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{|\mathcal{F}|}{q^{k}}\mathbf{1}+u_{1}+u_{2}\right)^{\top}\left(nq^{k-1}\frac{|\mathcal{F}|}{q^{k}}\mathbf{1}+q^{k-1}u_{1}\right)=nq^{k-1}\frac{|\mathcal{F}|^{2}}{q^{2k}}\|\mathbf{1}\|^{2}+q^{k-1}\|u_{1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\geqslant n\frac{|\mathcal{F}|^{2}}{q}+q^{k-1}|\mathcal{F}|\left(\frac{|\mathcal{F}|}{q^{k}}\left(\frac{|\mathcal{M}|}{t}-1\right)+1-\frac{|\mathcal{M}|}{qt}\right)

On the other hand, if we denote the star
\left\{c\in C\,\,||\,\,c(i)=\alpha\right\}
by
\mathcal{F}_{i,\alpha}
, then
\chi_{\mathcal{F}}^{\top}M(i,\alpha)=|\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{F}_{i,\alpha}|
.
Thus,

\chi_{\mathcal{F}}^{\top}MM^{\top}\chi_{\mathcal{F}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}|\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{F}_{i,\alpha}|^{2}=|\left\{(i,\alpha,c_{1},c_{2})\in\{1,\dots,n\}\times\mathbb{F}_{q}\times C\times C\,\,||\,\,c_{1}(i)=c_{2}(i)=\alpha\right\}|.

By a simple averaging argument, there must exist a
c_{1}\in C
such that

\displaystyle|\left\{(i,c_{2})\in\{1,\dots,n\}\times C\,\,||\,\,c_{2}(i)=c_{1}(i)\right\}|

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{1}{|\mathcal{F}|}\chi_{\mathcal{F}}^{\top}MM^{\top}\chi_{\mathcal{F}}

\displaystyle\geqslant n\frac{|\mathcal{F}|}{q}+q^{k-1}\left(\frac{|\mathcal{F}|}{q^{k}}\left(\frac{|\mathcal{M}|}{t}-1\right)+1-\frac{|\mathcal{M}|}{qt}\right)

Again by a simple averaging argument, there must exist an
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
such that

\displaystyle|\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{F}_{i,c_{1}(i)}|

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{1}{n}|\left\{(i,c_{2})\in\{1,\dots,n\}\times C\,\,||\,\,c_{2}(i)=c_{1}(i)\right\}|

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{|\mathcal{F}|}{q}+\frac{q^{k-1}}{n}\left(\frac{|\mathcal{F}|}{q^{k}}\left(\frac{|\mathcal{M}|}{t}-1\right)+1-\frac{|\mathcal{M}|}{qt}\right).\qed

If
|\mathcal{F}|=q^{k-1}
, then we can deduce from the EKR module property that
u_{2}=\mathbf{0}
, and the bound of
4.7
does not depend on the value of
t
.

Corollary 4.8
.

Let
C
be a linear
[n,k]_{q}
code that has the EKR module property.
If
\mathcal{F}
is an intersecting family in
C
of size
q^{k-1}
, then there exists a star
\mathcal{F}_{i,\alpha}
such that

|\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{F}_{i,\alpha}|\geqslant q^{k-2}\left(1+\frac{q-1}{n}\right).

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be the set of hyperplanes that don’t intersect the projective system
\mathcal{S}
associated to
C
.
Since the EKR module property holds, there exists an integer
t>0
such that every point
P\notin\mathcal{S}
is incident with at least
t
hyperplanes of
\mathcal{M}
.
By
4.7
, there exists a star
\mathcal{F}_{i,\alpha}
such that

\displaystyle|\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{F}_{i,\alpha}|

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{|\mathcal{F}|}{q}+\frac{q^{k-1}}{n}\left(\frac{|\mathcal{F}|}{q^{k}}\left(\frac{|\mathcal{M}|}{t}-1\right)+1-\frac{|\mathcal{M}|}{qt}\right)

\displaystyle=q^{k-2}+\frac{q^{k-1}}{n}\left(\frac{1}{q}\left(\frac{|\mathcal{M}|}{t}-1\right)+1-\frac{|\mathcal{M}|}{qt}\right)=q^{k-2}+\frac{q^{k-1}}{n}\left(1-\frac{1}{q}\right),

which is equivalent with the corollary statement.
∎

4.3

In this section, we will prove

Let
C
be a linear

\mathcal{F}

.

, there exists a star

(10)

On the other, hand we can apply

Instead of applying the statement of

Let

Note that

Then (

\left(\frac{|\mathcal{M}|}{q^{k-1}}|S|(q^{k-1}-|S|)\right)^{2}\leqslant\lambda|S|\frac{q^{k-1}-|S|}{q^{k-1}}(q^{k-1}-|S|)\frac{(q-2)q^{k-1}+|S|}{(q-1)q^{k-1}}.

\lambda\geqslant|\mathcal{M}|^{2}\frac{q-1}{\frac{q(q-2)}{1+\frac{q-1}{n}}+1}.

(11)

On the other hand, define

.

\lambda\leqslant q(q-1)r^{2}+\frac{|\mathcal{M}|^{2}}{q}.

Combined with (
11
), this implies that

q(q-1)r^{2}+\frac{|\mathcal{M}|^{2}}{q}\geqslant|\mathcal{M}|^{2}\frac{q-1}{\frac{q(q-2)}{1+\frac{q-1}{n}}+1}

This is equivalent to the following inequality (we omit some tedious arithmetic)

r^{2}\geqslant\frac{|\mathcal{M}|^{2}}{q(q-1)}\left(\frac{q-1}{\frac{q(q-2)}{1+\frac{q-1}{n}}+1}-\frac{1}{q}\right)=\frac{|\mathcal{M}|^{2}}{q^{2}(q-1)}\frac{n+q^{2}-q-1}{n(q-1)+1}

On the other hand, by the assumptions from the theorem,

r^{2}<\frac{|\mathcal{M}|^{2}}{q^{2}}\frac{1}{\min\{n,(q-1)^{2}\}}.

This implies that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{q-1}\frac{n+q^{2}-q-1}{n(q-1)+1}

\displaystyle\leqslant r^{2}\frac{q^{2}}{|\mathcal{M}|^{2}}<\frac{1}{\min\{n,(q-1)^{2}\}}

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\min\{n,(q-1)^{2}\}(n+q^{2}-q-1)

\displaystyle<(q-1)(n(q-1)+1).

(12)

We will prove that this leads to a contradiction.
First, suppose that
n<(q-1)^{2}
.
Then (
4.3
) becomes

n\left(n+(q^{2}-q-1)\right)<n(q-1)^{2}+q-1,

which simplifies to

n^{2}+(q-2)n<q-1.

This is a contradiction for
n>1
and
q\geqslant 2
.

Next, suppose that
n\geqslant(q-1)^{2}
.
Then (
4.3
) becomes

(q-1)^{2}(n+q^{2}-q-1)<(q-1)(n(q-1)+1),

which after dividing by
q-1
becomes

(q-1)(q^{2}-q-1)<1.

Using that
q\geqslant 2
, this also yields a contradiction.
∎

4.4
A stability result

In this subsection, we go beyond the strict EKR property, and use the developed tools to prove
1.11
, which describes a stability result.

1.11
.

Let
\mathcal{Q}
be an infinite set of prime powers, and suppose that for each
q\in\mathcal{Q}
, there exists a linear code
C_{q}
with parameters
[n_{q},k]_{q}
(where
n_{q}
can vary, but
k
is fixed). Denote by
\mathcal{S}_{q}
the projective system associated with
C_{q}
, and by
\mathcal{M}_{q}
the set of hyperplanes that don’t intersect
\mathcal{S}_{q}
. Suppose that there exist constants
a,b,\delta,\mu,\tau
with
0<\mu<1
such that the following properties hold for all of
q\in\mathcal{Q}
:

(1)

no 3 points of
\mathcal{S}_{q}
are collinear,

(2)

n_{q}\leqslant aq+b
,

(3)

\big||\mathcal{M}_{q}|-\mu q^{k-1}\big|\leqslant\tau q^{k-2}
,

(4)

for each point
P
of
\PG(k-1,q)
outside of
\mathcal{S}_{q}
,
\left|\left\{\Pi\in\mathcal{M}_{q}\,\,||\,\,P\in\Pi\right\}-\frac{|\mathcal{M}_{q}|}{q}\right|\leqslant\delta q^{k-3}
.

Then there exists a constant
\sigma
such that in each of the codes
C_{q}
, every intersecting family
\mathcal{F}
with

|\mathcal{F}|\geqslant\max\left\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+a^{-1}}},\,1-\mu\right\}q^{k-1}+\sigma q^{k-2}

is contained in a star.

Proof.

Suppose that the family of linear codes
(C_{q})_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}
satisfies the hypotheses of the theorem, and let
r
denote
\max\Big\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+a^{-1}}},\,1-\mu\Big\}
.
We will prove that the theorem holds for sufficiently large values of
q
, which suffices, since we can take
\sigma
large enough so that
rq^{k-1}+\sigma q^{k-2}>q^{k}
for small values of
q
.
Let
\mathcal{F}_{i,\alpha}
be the star that has the biggest intersection with
\mathcal{F}
, and write
x=|\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{F}_{i,\alpha}|
.

Claim 1: There exists a constant
\tau_{1}
(independent of
q
) such that for
q
sufficiently large,

x\geqslant\frac{1+a^{-1}}{q}|\mathcal{F}|-\tau_{1}q^{k-3}.

We apply
4.7
.
Let
t
be the minimum number of hyperplanes of
\mathcal{M}_{q}
incident with a point
P\notin\mathcal{S}_{q}
.
Then
t\geqslant\frac{|\mathcal{M}_{q}|}{q}-\delta q^{k-3}\geqslant\mu q^{k-2}-(\tau+\delta)q^{k-3}
.
Hence,
t>0
for
q
sufficiently large.
By
4.7
,

x\geqslant\frac{|\mathcal{F}|}{q}+\frac{q^{k-1}}{aq+b}\left(\frac{|\mathcal{F}|}{q^{k}}\left(\frac{|\mathcal{M}_{q}|}{t}-1\right)+1-\frac{|\mathcal{M}_{q}|}{qt}\right).

(13)

Since
t>0
, the weak EKR property holds, hence
|\mathcal{F}|\leqslant q^{k-1}
.
Thus, the right hand side is decreasing in
\frac{|\mathcal{M}_{q}|}{t}
.
It holds that

\frac{|\mathcal{M}_{q}|}{t}\leqslant\frac{|\mathcal{M}_{q}|}{\frac{|\mathcal{M}_{q}|}{q}-\delta q^{k-3}}=\frac{q}{1-\frac{\delta q^{k-2}}{|\mathcal{M}_{q}|}}\leqslant\frac{q}{1-\frac{\delta}{\mu q-\tau}}

Therefore, there exists a constant
\tau_{1}^{\prime}
such that
\frac{|\mathcal{M}_{q}|}{t}\leqslant q+\tau_{1}^{\prime}
for
q
sufficiently large.
Plugging this into (
13
) tells us that

x\geqslant\frac{|\mathcal{F}|}{q}\left(1+\frac{q+\tau_{1}^{\prime}-1}{aq+b}\right)-\frac{q^{k-1}}{aq+b}\frac{\tau_{1}^{\prime}}{q}.

Since the right hand side is decreasing in
\tau^{\prime}_{1}
, we may suppose that
\tau_{1}^{\prime}\geqslant\frac{b-1}{a}
, so that
\frac{q+\tau_{1}^{\prime}-1}{aq+b}\geqslant\frac{1}{a}
.
It is clear that there exists a constant
\tau_{1}
such that
\frac{q^{k-1}}{aq+b}\frac{\tau_{1}^{\prime}}{q}\leqslant\tau_{1}q^{k-3}
for
q
sufficiently large.
This proves the claim.

Claim 2: There exists a constant
\tau_{2}
(independent of
q
) such that for
q
sufficiently large,

x(|\mathcal{F}|-x)\leqslant q^{2k-3}+\tau_{2}q^{2k-4}.

(14)

We apply
4.1
.
Let
Q\in\mathcal{S}_{q}
be the point corresponding to the star
\mathcal{F}_{i,\alpha}
, and for each line
\ell
through
Q
, define

\lambda_{\ell}=q\sum_{P\in\ell\setminus\left\{Q\right\}}\left(|\left\{\Pi\in\mathcal{M}_{q}\,\,||\,\,P\in\Pi\right\}-\frac{|\mathcal{M}_{q}|}{q}\right)^{2}.

Then we have that
\lambda_{\ell}\leqslant q\cdot q\left(\delta q^{k-3}\right)^{2}
if
\ell
contains no second point of
\mathcal{S}_{q}
, and

\lambda_{\ell}\leqslant q\cdot\left(\left(\frac{|\mathcal{M}_{q}|}{q}\right)^{2}+(q-1)\left(\delta q^{k-3}\right)^{2}\right)

if
\ell
does contain a second point of
\mathcal{S}_{q}
.
For
q
sufficiently large, the second upper bound dominates.
By
4.1
,

x(|\mathcal{F}|-x)\leqslant q\cdot\left(\left(\frac{|\mathcal{M}_{q}|}{q}\right)^{2}+(q-1)\left(\delta q^{k-3}\right)^{2}\right)\left(\frac{q^{k-1}}{|\mathcal{M}_{q}|}\right)^{2}=q^{2k-3}+(q-1)\delta^{2}q^{2k-5}\left(\frac{q^{k-1}}{|\mathcal{M}_{q}|}\right)^{2}.

Since
|\mathcal{M}_{q}|\geqslant\mu q^{k-1}-\tau q^{k-2}
, and
\mu>0
, there exists a constant
\tau_{2}^{\prime}
such that
\frac{q^{k-1}}{|\mathcal{M}_{q}|}\leqslant\tau_{2}^{\prime}
for
q
sufficiently large.
Then the claim follows by taking
\tau_{2}=\tau_{2}^{\prime 2}\delta^{2}
.

We can now finish the proof.
By
Section

4.1
, it suffices to prove that
x>q^{k-1}-|\mathcal{M}|
, hence it is enough to prove that
x>x_{2}=(1-\mu)q^{k-1}+\tau q^{k-2}
.
Define
x_{1}=\frac{1+a^{-1}}{q}|\mathcal{F}|-\tau_{1}q^{k-3}
.
If we can prove that the inequality (
14
) does not hold for
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
, then by the theory of quadratic polynomials over the reals, it does not hold on the interval
[x_{1},x_{2}]
.
Since
x\geqslant x_{1}
by Claim 1, this then implies the desired inequality
x>x_{2}
.

First consider
x=x_{1}
.
Plugging in
|\mathcal{F}|\geqslant\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+a^{-1}}}q^{k-1}+\sigma q^{k-2}
, we find

\displaystyle x_{1}(|\mathcal{F}|-x_{1})\geqslant

\displaystyle\left(\sqrt{1+a^{-1}}q^{k-2}+(1+a^{-1})(\sigma-\tau_{1})q^{k-3}\right)

\displaystyle\cdot\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+a^{-1}}}q^{k-1}+\left(\sigma-\sqrt{1+a^{-1}}\right)q^{k-2}-(1+a^{-1})(\sigma-\tau_{1})q^{k-3}\right)

The leading term on the right hand side is
q^{2k-3}
, and the coefficient of
q^{2k-4}
can be made arbitrarily large by enlarging
\sigma
.
Hence, for some choice of the constant
\sigma
, this will ensure that inequality (
14
) fails for
x_{1}
if
q
is sufficiently large.

Next, consider
x=x_{2}
.
Then using that
|\mathcal{F}|\geqslant(1-\mu)q^{k-1}+\sigma q^{k-2}
, we find that

x_{2}(|\mathcal{F}|-x_{2})\geqslant\left((1-\mu)q^{k-1}+\tau q^{k-2}\right)(\sigma-\tau)q^{k-2}>(1-\mu)(\sigma-\tau)q^{2k-3},

where the last inequality holds under the assumption
\sigma\geqslant\tau
, which we may indeed assume.
We can choose
\sigma
sufficiently large such that
(1-\mu)(\sigma-\tau)>1
.
Then it immediately follows that
x_{2}(|\mathcal{F}|-x_{2})>q^{2k-3}+\tau_{2}q^{2k-4}
for
q
sufficiently large.
This finishes the proof.
∎

Remark 4.10
.

Note that if
\max\left\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+a^{-1}}},\,1-\mu\right\}=1-\mu
, then this constant is optimal in the above theorem due to the existence of Hilton-Milner type families, whose size equals
q^{k-1}-|\mathcal{M}|+1=(1-\mu)q^{k-1}+\mathcal{O}_{q}(q^{k-2})
by
Section

4.1
.
It is not clear to the author whether there exists a family of linear codes for which
1-\mu>\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+a^{-1}}}
.

Intersection problems for polynomials

In this section, we prove the theorems concerning (

-)intersecting families of polynomials stated in the introduction.
We translate the problem to the setting of linear codes, so that we can apply the previously developed tools.
This makes the following observation crucial, despite its trivial nature.

Lemma 5.1
.

Suppose that
q
is a prime power, and

.
Recall the map
Ev
from
Section

2.4
.
Consider a family
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}[X,Y]_{k}
.
Define
\mathcal{E}=\left\{Ev(f(X,1))\,\,||\,\,f\in\mathcal{F}\right\}
.

(1)

\mathcal{F}

\mathcal{E}
is a

-intersecting family in

.

(2)

\mathcal{F}
is a

-star in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[X,Y]_{k}

\mathcal{E}
is a

-star in

.

Proof.

This follows immediately from the definition of
t
-intersecting families in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[X,Y]_{k}
(
Section

1.2
),
t
-intersecting families in linear codes (
Section

1.3
), the implied definition of

-stars in both settings, and the definition of

(

2.4
).
∎

5.1
A general bound for
t
-intersecting families

Section

1.2
.

Suppose that
t\leqslant k<q
, and
k\geqslant 2
. Let
\mathcal{F}
be a
t
-intersecting family in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[X,Y]_{k}
.

(1)

If
t<k
, then
|\mathcal{F}|\leqslant q^{k+1-t}
.

(2)

If
t=k
, then
|\mathcal{F}|<\frac{q^{2}-1}{k}+1
.

Proof.

(1) We will prove (1) for
t
-intersecting families
\mathcal{F}
in
ERS(q,k)
, which is equivalent by
Section

5
.
Using
Section

3
, it suffices to show that if
t<k
, then
ERS(q,k)
has a linear
[q+1,t]_{q}
MDS subcode
C^{\prime}
.
It is well known that
\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
contains irreducible polynomials of every degree (this follows e.g. from the fact that
\mathbb{F}_{q}
has a field extension of every finite order).
Take an irreducible polynomial
f\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
of degree
k+1-t\geqslant 2
.
Define
C^{\prime}
to be the image of
\left\{f\cdot g\,\,||\,\,g\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]_{\leqslant t-1}\right\}
under
Ev
.
Then

Ev(f\cdot g)=(f(x_{1})g(x_{1}),\dots,f(x_{q})g(x_{q}),f(\infty_{k+1-t})g(\infty_{t-1})).

Since
f
has no roots on
\mathbb{F}_{q}\cup\{\infty_{k+1-t}\}
,
C^{\prime}
is equivalent to
ERS(q,t-1)
, hence it is a linear
[q+1,t]_{q}
MDS code.

(2) The notation will be easier if we represent the polynomials of
\mathbb{F}_{q}[X,Y]_{k}
as the polynomials
\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]_{\leqslant k}
evaluated on
\mathbb{F}_{q}\cup\left\{\infty\right\}
.
Recall that this transforms a polynomial
F\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X,Y]_{k}
into the polynomial
f(X)=F(X,1)
, and we define
f(\infty)=F(1,0)
.

Take a polynomial
f(X)\in\mathcal{F}
, and consider the set

V=\left\{(x,g)\in(\mathbb{F}_{q}\cup\left\{\infty\right\})\times\mathcal{F}\,\,||\,\,g(x)=f(x)\right\}.

We perform a double count on
V
.
Given a polynomial
g\in\mathcal{F}
, the number of values
x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\cup\left\{\infty\right\}
with
f(x)=g(x)
equals
q+1
if
f=g
and
k
otherwise.
Therefore,

\displaystyle|V|=(q+1)+k(|\mathcal{F}|-1).

(15)

On the other hand, we can take a point
x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\cup\left\{\infty\right\}
, and let
y
be
f(x)
.
Without loss of generality, we may assume that
x=\infty
and
y=0
.
Then
\left\{g\in\mathcal{F}\,\,||\,\,g(\infty)=0\right\}
constitutes a
(k-1)
-intersecting family in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]_{\leqslant k-1}
(ignoring the evaluation at
\infty
).
Therefore, by
Section

1.2
, this set contains at most
q
polynomials.
We conclude that

\displaystyle|V|\leqslant(q+1)q.

(16)

If we combine (
15
) and (
16
), we find that

|\mathcal{F}|=\frac{|V|-(q+1)}{k}+1\leqslant\frac{q^{2}-1}{k}+1.

If equality holds, then every point
(x,y)\in(\mathbb{F}_{q}\cup\left\{\infty\right\})\times\mathbb{F}_{q}
is incident with either
0
or
q
polynomials of
\mathcal{F}
.
Since each polynomial of
\mathcal{F}
is incident with exactly
q+1
points,
q
must then divide
(q+1)\left(\frac{q^{2}-1}{k}+1\right)
.
This implies that
k\equiv 1\pmod{q}
.
Since
2\leqslant k<q
, this yields a contradiction.
∎

Remark 5.2
.

If we take
t=k=1
, then
\mathcal{F}=\mathbb{F}_{q}[X,Y]_{1}
is an intersecting family, and the equality

|\mathcal{F}|=q^{2}=\frac{q^{2}-1}{k}+1

holds.

5.2
A stability result for intersecting families

We are ready to prove
1.7
.
We prove it by applying
1.11
to the extended Reed-Solomon codes.
In order to do so, we need some properties concerning their projective systems, the normal rational curves.
The reader should recall the function
\nu
defined in
Section

2.4
.

We start by reviewing some properties of normal rational curves.
For starters, every projectivity of
\PG(1,q)
can be lifted to a projectivity of
\PG(k,q)
that stabilises
\mathcal{N}
.

Result 5.3

(
[
HT16
, Theorem 6.30 (vi)]
)
.

For every map
\varphi\in\PGL(2,q)
, there exists a map
\Phi\in\PGL(k+1,q)
such that for all points
P
of
\PG(1,q)
,
\nu_{k}(\varphi(P))=\Phi(\nu_{k}(P))
.
As a corollary, the stabiliser in
\PGL(k+1,q)
of a normal rational curve
\mathcal{N}
in
\PG(k,q)
acts triply transitively on the points of
\mathcal{N}
.

We want to use the tools developed in the previous section to characterise large intersecting families in extended Reed-Solomon codes.
In order to do so, we need to prove that if
\mathcal{N}
is a normal rational curve in
\PG(k,q)
and
P
is a point outside of
\mathcal{N}
, then the number of hyperplanes through
P
that don’t intersect
\mathcal{N}
cannot deviate too much from a proportion
1/q
of these hyperplanes.
We start by establishing the number of hyperplanes intersecting
\mathcal{N}
in a given number of points.

Definition 5.4
.

For integers
t
and
k
with
0\leqslant t\leqslant k
, define the constants

\mu_{k,t}=\frac{1}{t!}\sum_{j=0}^{k-t}\frac{(-1)^{j}}{j!}.

Lemma 5.5
.

Consider a normal rational curve
\mathcal{N}
in
\PG(k,q)
,
k<q
.
Suppose that
t\leqslant k
.
Then there are

\binom{q+1}{t}\sum_{j=0}^{k-t}(-1)^{j}\binom{q+2-t}{j}q^{k-t-j}=\mu_{k,t}q^{k}+\mathcal{O}_{q}(q^{k-1})

hyperplanes that intersect
\mathcal{N}
in exactly
t
points.

Proof.

Recall that every non-zero codeword
c
of
ERS(q,k)
of weight
n-t
corresponds to a unique hyperplane of
\PG(k,q)
intersecting
\mathcal{N}
in
t
points, and that vice versa, each hyperplane corresponds to
q-1
codewords.
The statement of the lemma then directly follows from
Section

2.4
.
∎

Next, we check that the constants
\mu_{k,t}
sum to one.
This can be checked directly by computation, but we can also avoid having to do any arithmetic.

Lemma 5.6
.

For every positive integer
k
,

\sum_{t=0}^{k}\mu_{k,t}=1.

Proof.

Fix
k
.
Consider a normal rational curve
\mathcal{N}
in
\PG(k,q)
and
q>k
.
If we fix
t
, the number of hyperplanes that intersect
\mathcal{N}
in exactly
t
points is a polynomial
F_{k,t}(q)
in
q
of degree at most
k
by
Section

5.2
, and its
q^{k}
coefficient equals
\mu_{k,t}
.
The total number of hyperplanes equals
G_{k}(q)=q^{k}+q^{k-1}+\ldots+1
.
We find that
\sum_{t=0}^{k}F_{k,t}(q)=G_{k}(q)
for all prime powers
q>k
, hence the polynomials must coincide.
Looking at the coefficient of
q^{k}
, this proves exactly the statement of the lemma.
∎

Consider a normal rational curve
\mathcal{N}
in
\PG(k,q)
, and a point
Q\in\mathcal{N}
.
We call
\mathcal{N}^{\circ}=\mathcal{N}\setminus\{Q\}
a
punctured normal rational curve
, and
Q
the

of
\mathcal{N}^{\circ}
.

Result 5.7

(
[
HT16
, Lemma 6.31 (i)]
)
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{N}
is a normal rational curve in
\PG(k,q)
and take a point
Q\in\mathcal{N}
.
Then
Q
projects the remaining points of
\mathcal{N}
onto a punctured normal rational curve in
\PG(k-1,q)
.

Given a point
Q
on the normal rational curve
\mathcal{N}
, let
\mathcal{N}^{\circ}
denote the punctured normal rational curve equal to the projection of
\mathcal{N}\setminus\{Q\}
from
Q
, and let
Q^{\prime}
be the punctured point.
Then
\proj_{Q}^{-1}(Q^{\prime})
is a line
\ell
through
Q
in
\PG(k,q)
.
This line is called the

of
Q
to
\mathcal{N}
.
Call a line intersecting
\mathcal{N}
in two points

to
\mathcal{N}
.

Result 5.8

(
[
HT16
, Lemma 6.31]
)
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{N}
is a normal rational curve in
\PG(k,q)
with

and let
P
be a point not on
\mathcal{N}
.
Then there is at most one line through

that is either a tangent or bisecant line to
\mathcal{N}
.

For the next proposition, we will use the constants
\mu_{k,t}
defined in
Section

5.2
.

Proposition 5.9
.

For each integer
k\geqslant 3
, there exists a constant
\delta_{k}
(independent of
q
) such that the following holds for every

.
Let
\mathcal{N}
denote a normal rational curve in
\PG(k,q)
, and let
P
be a point not on
\mathcal{N}
.
For

, let

\left|s_{t}(P)-\mu_{k,t}q^{k-1}\right|\leqslant\delta_{k}q^{k-2}.

For

[

, Tables 1 and 2]
.
We prove that it holds for bigger values of

Take a point

of
\PG(k,q)
, not on
\mathcal{N}
.
For each point

, let

denote the number of hyperplanes incident with both

and

that intersect
\mathcal{N}
in exactly

points.
Then
\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{N}}s_{t}(P,Q)=ts_{t}(P)
.

First consider the case where

, and take some point
Q\in\mathcal{N}
.
Consider the projection from
Q
, let

be
\proj_{Q}(P)
, and let
\mathcal{N}^{\circ}
be the image of
\mathcal{N}\setminus\{Q\}
under

Let

It follows from

In the other cases,

equals the number of hyperplanes in

By the induction hypothesis,

\left|s_{t}(P,Q)-\mu_{k-1,t-1}q^{k-2}\right|\leqslant\delta_{k-1}q^{k-3}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{N}}s_{t}(P,Q)

\displaystyle\geqslant(q-1)\left(\mu_{k-1,t-1}q^{k-2}-\delta_{k-1}q^{k-3}\right),\text{ and}

\displaystyle\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{N}}s_{t}(P,Q)

\displaystyle\leqslant(q-1)\left(\mu_{k-1,t-1}q^{k-2}+\delta_{k-1}q^{k-3}\right)+2(q^{k-2}+\ldots+q+1).

Write
\delta^{\prime}=\delta_{k-1}+4
.
Note that
\frac{1}{t}\mu_{k-1,t-1}=\mu_{k,t}
.
Then using
s_{t}(P)=\frac{1}{t}\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{N}}s_{t}(P,Q)
, we find that

\left|s_{t}(P)-\mu_{k,t}q^{k-1}\right|\leqslant\frac{\delta^{\prime}}{t}q^{k-2}.

Lastly, consider the case
t=0
.
We have that

s_{0}(P)=q^{k-1}+\ldots+q+1-\sum_{t=1}^{k}s_{t}(P).

Using
Section

5.2
, it easily follows that

\mu_{k,0}q^{k-1}-\delta^{\prime}\sum_{t=1}^{k}\frac{1}{t}q^{k-2}\leqslant s_{0}(P)\leqslant\mu_{k,0}q^{k-1}+\left(2+\delta^{\prime}\sum_{t=1}^{k}\frac{1}{t}\right)q^{k-2}.

Therefore, we can take

\delta_{k}=2+(\delta_{k-1}+4)\sum_{t=1}^{k}\frac{1}{t}.

This finishes the proof.
∎

We are now ready to prove
1.7
.
We repeat the theorem statement for the reader’s convenience.

1.7
.

For every integer
k\geqslant 3
, there exists a constant
\sigma_{k}
, such that for all prime powers
q
, every intersecting family
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}[X,Y]_{k}
with
|\mathcal{F}|\geqslant\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}q^{k}+\sigma_{k}q^{k-1}
is contained in a star.

Proof.

By
Section

5
, it suffices to prove the theorem for intersecting families
\mathcal{F}
in
ERS(q,k)
.
Let
\mathcal{Q}
be the set of prime powers.
We check that if we fix
k\geqslant 3
, the family of codes
(ERS(k,q))_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}
satisfies the hypotheses of
1.11
.
Let us check this using the notation
\mathcal{M}_{q}
,
\mathcal{S}_{q}
,
a,b,\delta,\mu,\tau
from
1.11
.
We do point out that the code
ERS(q,k)
has dimension
k+1
, so we should replace
k
by
k+1
in the statement of
1.11
.

(1)

Since
\mathcal{S}_{q}
is a normal rational curve in
\PG(k,q)
, no 3 points of
\mathcal{S}_{q}
are collinear by
section

2.4
.

(2)

The length of the code is given by
n_{q}=q+1
, hence we can take
a=b=1
.

(3)

Write

as defined in
Section

5.2
. Then
|\mathcal{M}_{q}|
is a polynomial in

with leading term

, hence
\big||\mathcal{M}_{q}|-\mu q^{k}\big|\leqslant\tau q^{k-1}
for some constant

.

(4)

Let
\delta_{k}
be as in
Section

5.2
. Then by
Section

5.2
and the previous point, for each point

,

\displaystyle\left|\left\{\Pi\in\mathcal{M}_{q}\,\,||\,\,P\in\Pi\right\}-\frac{|\mathcal{M}_{q}|}{q}\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\left|\left\{\Pi\in\mathcal{M}_{q}\,\,||\,\,P\in\Pi\right\}-\mu_{k,0}q^{k-1}\right|+\left|\mu_{k,0}q^{k-1}-\frac{|\mathcal{M}_{q}|}{q}\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\delta_{k}q^{k-2}+\tau q^{k-2}.

Hence, we can take

Consider

.
This is a partial sum of

Since it is an alternating series whose terms have decreasing absolute value,

(17)

Remark 5.10
.

One naturally wonders how close the value

in

As discussed before, an intersecting family of Hilton-Milner type would have size

, so it is not unreasonable to expect

to be the optimal constant.
For comparison, note that

, and we have that

by (

Moreover,

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we established a general framework for investigating intersection problems in linear codes.
We used it to prove a stability result concerning intersecting families of polynomials.
Some questions remain.

Problem 6.1
.

We proved that an intersecting family in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[X,Y]_{k}
with
k\geqslant 3
of size
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}q^{k}+\mathcal{O}(q^{k-1})
must be contained in a star?
Can we improve the constant to
\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{(-1)^{j+1}}{j!}
?
Can we prove that
\mathbb{F}_{q}[X,Y]_{k}
satisfies the HM property?

The constant
\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{(-1)^{j+1}}{j!}
is minimal for
k=2
, and a Hilton-Milner type family in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[X,Y]_{2}
would have size
\frac{1}{2}(q^{2}+q)
.
It could hence be the case the establishing the HM property is hardest for
\mathbb{F}_{q}[X,Y]_{2}
.
Moreover, there is an even more concrete difficulty.
If
\mathcal{F}
is a star or a Hilton-Milner type family, then there exists a special point incident with all or all but one block of
\mathcal{F}
.
However, there are intersecting families of size
\left\lceil\frac{q^{2}}{2}\right\rceil
, with a vastly different structure, see
[
ACW24
, Example 4.2]
and
[
Adr24
, Construction 4.4.3]
, where each point is incident with at most
q
blocks of such a family.
An interesting open problem is to find similar constructions of higher degree.

Problem 6.2
.

Can we construct large intersecting families
\mathcal{F}
in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[X,Y]_{k}
where every point is incident with a small number of blocks of
\mathcal{F}
?

The characterisation of maximum
t
-intersecting families in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[X,Y]_{k}
(and even in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]_{\leqslant k}
) is still open for
t>1
.

Problem 6.3
.

We proved that if
t<k<q
, a
t
-intersecting family in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[X,Y]_{k}
contains at most
q^{k+1-t}
elements.
Can we prove that only stars achieve equality?
For certain ranges of the parameters
t
,
k
,
q
, this might be feasible using the
spread approximations method

[
KZ24
]
.
Moreover, can we improve the bound
\frac{q^{2}-1}{k}+1
on the size of
k
-intersecting families to a linear bound?

Finally, it would be interesting to find other settings than linear codes where the techniques of this paper can yield stability results of intersecting families.
In
[
Adr22a
]
, the author used the technique to prove stability results for intersecting families in
circle geometries
.
As a special case, this yields a stability result of intersecting families in
\PGL(2,q)
.

Problem 6.4
.

Given a permutation group
G
acting on a set
X
, call
g,h\in G

intersecting
if there exists an
x\in X
with
x^{g}=x^{h}
.
Use the techniques of the paper to prove stability results of intersecting families in certain classes of permutation groups.

Of course, other settings than permutation groups would also be interesting.

Appendix A
Tactical decompositions and translation schemes

In this section, we review the theory of association schemes, and in particular translation schemes.
We refer the reader to
[
BCN89
, Chapter 2]
or
[
God10
, Chapter 6]
for more background.
Ultimately, we apply the theory determine the eigenvalues of certain graphs defined on the vector space of homogeneous polynomials over finite fields.

A.1
Association schemes

An association scheme admits several definitions, and can be seen as a partition
\mathcal{A}=\{R_{0},\dots,R_{d}\}
of the cartesian product
X\times X
of some finite set
X
into some symmetric relations satisfying certain regularity conditions.
Each such relation induces a graph on
X
.
Let
A_{i}
denote the adjacency matrix of
R_{i}
.

Definition A.1
.

Given a finite set
X
, a partition
\mathcal{A}=\{R_{0},\dots,R_{d}\}
of
X\times X
is called a
d
-class (symmetric) association scheme
if
R_{0}
is the identity relation (i.e.
A_{0}
equals the identity matrix
I
), every adjacency matrix
A_{i}
is symmetric, and the linear subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{X\times X}
generated by
A_{0},\dots,A_{d}
is a commutative algebra under the ordinary matrix product.

We denote by
\mathbb{R}[\mathcal{A}]
the linear subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{X\times X}
spanned by
\mathcal{A}
, called the
Bose-Mesner algebra
of
\mathcal{A}
.
A crucial property of association schemes is that
\mathbb{R}[\mathcal{A}]
admits another important basis
E_{0}=\frac{1}{|X|}J,E_{1},\dots,E_{d}
, where each
E_{j}
is an orthogonal projection, and the column spaces of the
E_{j}
’s constitute an orthogonal decomposition of
\mathbb{R}^{X}
.
This basis is unique up to ordering.
We call it the
dual basis
of
\mathbb{R}[\mathcal{A}]
.

The transition matrices between the two aforementioned bases of
\mathbb{R}[\mathcal{A}]
capture crucial information about the association scheme.
Define the matrix
\boldsymbol{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{\{0,\dots,d\}\times\{0,\dots,d\}}
by
A_{j}=\sum_{i=0}^{d}\boldsymbol{P}(i,j)E_{i}
, and similarly the matrix
\boldsymbol{Q}
by
E_{j}=\frac{1}{|X|}\sum_{i=0}^{d}\boldsymbol{Q}(i,j)A_{i}
.
We call
\boldsymbol{P}
and
\boldsymbol{Q}
the
matrices of eigenvalues
or
character tables
of the association scheme.

Definition A.2
.

Suppose that
(G,+)
is a finite abelian group with identity element
0
, and that
S_{0}=\left\{0\right\},S_{1},\dots,S_{d}
is a partition of
G
into inverse-closed subsets.
For each
S_{i}
, let
R_{i}
be the relation corresponding to the Cayley graph
\Cay(G,S_{i})
.
If
\{R_{0},\dots,R_{d}\}
is an association scheme, it is called a
translation scheme
.

Each translation scheme
\mathcal{A}
has a dual association scheme.
This works as follows.
The group
\hat{G}
of characters of
G
is isomorphic to
G
.
The characters of
G
constitute an orthogonal basis of
\mathbb{C}^{G}
that diagonalises all the adjacency matrices
A_{i}
of
\mathcal{A}
.
Each matrix
E_{j}
of the dual basis, when viewed as a complex matrix, is the orthogonal projection onto a subspace of
\mathbb{C}^{G}
that is spanned by some subset
T_{j}\subset\hat{G}
of characters of
G
.
This yields a partition
T_{0}=\left\{0\right\},T_{1},\dots,T_{d}
of the elements of
\hat{G}
, and this partition again gives rise to a translation scheme
\mathcal{A}^{*}
on
\hat{G}
.
Since
G\cong\hat{G}
,
\mathcal{A}^{*}
is isomorphic to a translation scheme on
G
.
If
\boldsymbol{P}
and
\boldsymbol{Q}
are the matrices of eigenvalues of
\mathcal{A}
, then
\boldsymbol{Q}
and
\boldsymbol{P}
(in that order) are the matrices of eigenvalues of
\mathcal{A}^{*}
.
Moreover, the dual translation scheme
\mathcal{A}^{**}
of
\mathcal{A}^{*}
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{A}
.
If
\boldsymbol{P}=\boldsymbol{Q}
, then
\mathcal{A}
is said to be
formally self-dual
.
If in addition
\mathcal{A}
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{A}^{*}
, then
\mathcal{A}
is called
self-dual
.

A.2
Tactical decompositions

Consider the projective space
\PG(k-1,q)
.
Let
\mathfrak{S}=\{S_{1},\dots,S_{d}\}
be a partition of the hyperplanes and
\mathfrak{T}=\{T_{1},\dots,T_{d}\}
a partition of the points.
Then
(\mathfrak{S},\mathfrak{T})
is called a
tactical decomposition
of
\PG(k-1,q)
if there exist integers
s_{ij}
and
t_{ij}
such that

•

every hyperplane in
S_{i}
is incident with exactly
s_{ij}
points of
T_{j}
,

•

every point in
T_{i}
is incident with exactly
t_{ij}
hyperplanes of
S_{j}
.

We note that the condition that
\mathfrak{S}
and
\mathfrak{T}
have the same number of parts (in our case
d
) is necessary for a tactical decomposition to exist, as proven by Block
[
Blo67
, Corollary 2.1]
.
For every group
G\leqslant\PGamL(k,q)
of collineations, we can define
\mathfrak{S}
and
\mathfrak{T}
to be the sets of orbits of
G
on the hyperplanes and points respectively.
Then
(\mathfrak{S},\mathfrak{T})
is always a tactical decomposition.

Given a tactical decomposition
(\mathfrak{S},\mathfrak{T})
, define
S^{*}_{0}=T^{*}_{0}=\{\mathbf{0}\}
, and for each
i=1,\dots,d
define

\displaystyle S^{*}_{i}

\displaystyle=\left\{v\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}\setminus\left\{\mathbf{0}\right\}\,\,||\,\,v^{\perp}\in S_{i}\right\},

\displaystyle T^{*}_{i}

\displaystyle=\left\{w\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}\setminus\left\{\mathbf{0}\right\}\,\,||\,\,\left\langle w\right\rangle\in T_{i}\right\}.

Then the Cayley graphs given by
\Cay(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k},S_{i})
determine a translation scheme
\mathcal{A}
on
(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k},+)
.
Likewise, the Cayley graphs given by
\Cay(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k},T_{i})
determine a translation scheme
\mathcal{A}^{*}
on
(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k},+)
, and these schemes are (isomorphic to) each others duals.

Using the the discussion of
Section

2.3
(or see
[
Adr24
, Theorem 3.1.3]
), it is straightforward to determine that the matrices of eigenvalues of
\mathcal{A}
are given by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{P}=\begin{pmatrix}1&(q-1)|S_{1}|&\dots&(q-1)|S_{d}|\\
1&qt_{11}-|S_{1}|&\dots&qt_{1d}-|S_{d}|\\
\vdots&&&\vdots\\
1&qt_{d1}-|S_{1}|&\dots&qt_{dd}-|S_{d}|\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{Q}=\begin{pmatrix}1&(q-1)|T_{1}|&\dots&(q-1)|T_{d}|\\
1&qs_{11}-|T_{1}|&\dots&qs_{1d}-|T_{d}|\\
\vdots&&&\vdots\\
1&qs_{d1}-|T_{1}|&\dots&qs_{dd}-|T_{d}|\end{pmatrix}.

A
polarity
of
\PG(k-1,q)
is an involution of the subspaces of
\PG(k-1,q)
that reverses inclusion.
A polarity maps points to hyperplanes and vice versa.
If
\zeta
is a polarity that maps the set of hyperplanes
S_{i}
to the set of points
T_{i}
(and vice versa since
\zeta
is a polarity) for each
i
, then
\zeta
induces an isomorphism between
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{A}^{*}
.
Hence, in this case,
\mathcal{A}
is a self-dual translation scheme, and
\boldsymbol{P}=\boldsymbol{Q}
.

A.3
Bivariate homogeneous polynomials of small degree

Recall the map
\nu_{k}
defined in
Section

2.4
, and let
\mathcal{N}
denote its image, i.e.
\mathcal{N}
is the canonical normal rational curve in
\PG(k,q)
.
As discussed in
Section

5.2
, there is a subgroup
G
of
\PGL(k+1,q)
isomorphic to
\PGL(2,q)
that stabilises
\mathcal{N}
.
Note that the order of
\PGL(2,q)
equals
q(q^{2}-1)
.
Since
\PG(k,q)

.
In particular, if

, then the number of orbits grows with

.
However, if
k\leqslant 3

This yields translation schemes defined on
(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k+1},+)

(\mathbb{F}_{q}[X,Y]_{k},+)
.

Degree 2

\mathbb{F}_{q}[X,Y]_{2}

R_{0}

.
Then
|\mathcal{F}|\leqslant\frac{1}{3}q^{2}-\frac{2}{9}q
.

Proof.

The Delsarte clique bound (see e.g.
[
GM16
, Corollary 3.7.2]
) says that if
R
is a single relation in an association scheme, then a clique has it size bounded by
\frac{k}{|\tau|}+1
, if
R
is
k
-regular, and its adjacency matrix has smallest eigenvalue
\tau
.
For
3
-intersecting families in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[X,Y]_{3}
with
q>3
a power of 3, this yields the bound

|\mathcal{F}|\leqslant\left\lfloor\frac{\frac{1}{6}(q-1)^{2}q(q+1)}{\frac{1}{6}q(3q-1)}+1\right\rfloor=\frac{1}{3}q^{2}-\frac{2}{9}q.\qed

A.4
Trivariate homogeneous polynomials of degree 2

We can also define a translation scheme on
\mathbb{F}_{q}[X,Y,Z]_{2}
.
Say that
f,g\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X,Y,Z]_{2}
are in relation

•

R_{0}
if
f=g
,

•

R_{1}
if
f-g
has a double linear factor,

•

R_{2}
if
f-g
has two distinct linear factors,

•

R_{3}
if
f-g
splits into two (conjugate) linear factors over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
(but not over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
),

•

R_{4}
if
f-g
is irreducible over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
.

The corresponding tactical decomposition is given by the point- and hyperplane-orbits of the stabiliser of the
quadric Veronesean
in
\PG(5,q)
.
We refer the reader to
[
HT16
, Chapter 4]
for more details.
The parameters
s_{ij}
and
t_{ij}
were determined in
[
AL25
, Theorems 3.2 and 3.3]
.

For
q
odd, the translation scheme is self-dual because of the existence of a polarity that swaps the point- and hyperplane-orbits constituting the tactical decomposition, see e.g.
[
HT16
, Theorem 4.25]
.
We find that the matrices of eigenvalues
\boldsymbol{P}=\boldsymbol{Q}
are given by:

\begin{pmatrix}1&q^{3}-1&\frac{1}{2}q(q^{2}-1)(q^{2}+q+1)&\frac{1}{2}q(q-1)^{2}(q^{2}+q+1)&q^{2}(q-1)^{2}(q^{2}+q+1)\\
1&-1&\frac{1}{2}q(q+1)^{2}(q-1)&-\frac{1}{2}q(q-1)(q^{2}+1)&-q^{2}(q-1)\\
1&q^{2}-1&\frac{1}{2}q(q^{2}-2q-1)&\frac{1}{2}q(q-1)^{2}&-q^{2}(q-1)\\
1&-(q^{2}+1)&\frac{1}{2}q(q^{2}-1)&\frac{1}{2}q(q^{2}+1)&-q^{2}(q-1)\\
1&-1&-\frac{1}{2}q(q+1)&-\frac{1}{2}q(q-1)&q^{2}\end{pmatrix}.

In case that
q
is even, the scheme is no longer self-dual, and we find the following matrices of eigenvalues.

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{P}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}1&q^{3}-1&\frac{1}{2}q(q^{2}-1)(q^{2}+q+1)&\frac{1}{2}q(q-1)^{2}(q^{2}+q+1)&q^{2}(q-1)^{2}(q^{2}+q+1)\\
1&-1&\frac{1}{2}q(q+1)^{2}(q-1)&-\frac{1}{2}q(q-1)(q^{2}+1)&-q^{2}(q-1)\\
1&q^{3}-1&-\frac{1}{2}q(q+1)&-\frac{1}{2}q(q-1)&-q^{2}(q-1)\\
1&-1&\frac{1}{2}q(q^{2}-q-1)&\frac{1}{2}q(q^{2}-q+1)&-q^{2}(q-1)\\
1&-1&-\frac{1}{2}q(q+1)&-\frac{1}{2}q(q-1)&q^{2}\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{Q}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}1&q^{3}-1&q^{3}-1&(q+1)(q-1)^{2}(q^{2}+q+1)&q^{2}(q-1)^{2}(q^{2}+q+1)\\
1&-1&q^{3}-1&-(q^{2}-1)&-q^{2}(q-1)\\
1&q^{2}-1&-1&(q-1)(q^{2}-q-1)&-q^{2}(q-1)\\
1&-(q^{2}+1)&-1&q^{3}+1&-q^{2}(q-1)\\
1&-1&-1&-(q^{2}-1)&q^{2}\end{pmatrix}.

Remark A.5
.

As explained in
[
AL25
]
, we can naturally represent the vector space underlying
\PG(5,q)
as the space of symmetric
3\times 3
matrices over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
The point orbits consist of the projective points corresponding to rank 1 matrices, 2 orbits corresponding to rank 2 matrices, and an orbit corresponding to rank 3 matrices.
As such, we can fuse relations 2 and 3 in the above dual translation schemes (both for
q
odd and
q
even) to obtain the
symmetric bilinear forms
scheme over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, see e.g.
[
BCN89
, §9.5 D]
.
