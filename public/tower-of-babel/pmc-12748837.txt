Impact of drain and source engineering on dual metal InAs-GaSb VTFETs with high-K gate stack design
Authors: M. Saravanan, Eswaran Parthasarathy, Shiromani Balmukund Rahi, Ramkumar Natarajan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The performance of a Dual-Metal-InAs-GaSb Vertical Tunnel Field Effect Transistor (DM-InAs-GaSb VTFET) with an InAs source pocket was investigated in relation to the gate dielectric materials. This research chose gate dielectric materials such as SiO2, Al2O3, HfO2, and ZrO2. The simulation is performed using the Silvaco Technology Computer-Aided Design (TCAD) software. The drain current (ION) of the DM-InAs-GaSb VTFET, which includes an InAs source pocket and an extended drain, is assessed across several dielectric materials; still, ZrO2 (7.92 × 10− 5 A/µm) and HfO2 (8.15 × 10− 5 A/µm) demonstrate enhanced performance. The transconductance (gm) values were 606 µS/µm for HfO2 and 589 µS/µm for ZrO2. A comparison is performed between the Ge-Si VTFET and the suggested configuration. The proposed DM-InAs-GaSb VTFET demonstrates a 1.5-times increase in ON current (ION) and a three-time boost in transconductance (gm). The frequency response of the proposed device was evaluated by employing its SPICE characteristics to construct the common source amplifier in the SPICE circuit simulator. This amplifier comparison reveals that ZrO2 and HfO2 insulators provide significant gain, with HfO2 displaying a cut-off frequency of 1.808 GHz.

Introduction

The Tunnel field-effect transistors (TFETs) are a distinct class of transistors that operate according to quantum mechanical tunneling principles, as opposed to the conventional operation observed in standard transistors. In TFET, the phenomenon of tunneling occurs when particles traverse a narrow barrier, such as a heterojunction or an exceedingly thin oxide layer. TFETs can achieve reduced power consumption and enhanced subthreshold oscillation compared to conventional transistors. The primary goal of the dielectric material in TFET is to enhance the efficiency of the tunneling phenomenon. This characteristic is affected by several variables, such as the material’s bandgap, effective mass, and tunneling duration. These factors can be altered to enhance the efficiency and tunneling performance of the TFET. Low-power analog and digital applications benefit greatly from the use of TFETs1,2 owing to their lower subthreshold voltage swings and very low leakage currents. TFETs are also useful for neuromorphic computing, inverters, Static Random Access Memory (SRAM), and sensors3–6. Furthermore, the TFET also outperforms CMOS technology for low-power applications7. In the 0.6–1.2 V operating range, the FinFET performed well. However, the TFET further reduced the working voltage range from 0.2 to 0.6 V6,7 which is a desirable feature for SRAM. The performance of TFET based on the requirements of analog and digital applications, tremendous advancements in TFET, including vertical cladding layers8, germanium sources9, barrier widening10, dielectric modulation11, double pockets12, vertically extended drain gates13, diagonal tunneling5, and voltage scaling14, have been achieved in the last decade. The drain facilitated the widening of the energy barrier10, and a reverse short-channel effect was obtained which resulted in a reduction in the leakage current to 10–16 A/µm and a subthreshold voltage swing of 35 mV/dec10. The vertical extension of the drain region demonstrates ultra-sensitivity13. Diagonal tunneling is used to improve the sensitivity of bio-sensing applications5. The sensitivity of diagonal tunneling is superior to that of vertical and lateral tunneling mechanisms. A dual-pocket scheme was used for achieving three stable voltages in a ternary inverter12. TFET has seen significant improvements, but there are still problems, such as the ambipolar effect15, degradation of performance due to defects16, and fluctuation in work function17. The TFET also had a lower drive current than the MOSFET18. The recombinant method works much better for a direct-band semiconductor than an indirect-band semiconductor. Consequently, TFETs have made use of III-V semiconductors.

The GaSb/InAs TFET is a heterojunction tunneling field-effect transistor that employs Gallium Antimonide (GaSb) as the p-type source and Indium Arsenide (InAs) as the n-type channel/drain. It functions using band-to-band tunneling (BTBT) rather than thermionic emission, as seen in traditional MOSFETs. The broken-gap (type-III) band alignment between GaSb and InAs promotes efficient band-to-band tunneling (BTBT). This facilitates a steep subthreshold swing (SS < 60 mV/dec) and a low off-current (IOFF). Provides elevated ON-current (ION) owing to robust tunneling efficiency and precise bandgap alignment.

Device structure

The vertical structure TFET allows for fast passage of charge carriers (such as electrons or holes) via a thin barrier separating the source and drain areas. Since the tunnelling process can be more easily controlled due to the vertical structure, it is suitable for low-power applications where energy conservation is crucial. Improving the ON/OFF Current Ratio: For digital applications, a superior ON/OFF current ratio can be obtained by a vertical TFET structure than by a lateral TFET6. Configuration is essential to attain the desired performance characteristics, such as a pronounced subthreshold slope and a substantial ON/OFF current ratio. TFETs possess unique characteristics due to their vertical structure, rendering them advantageous for specific applications such as low-power, high-performance computing, and sensor technologies. Energy band profile

The electric field is the primary factor preventing charge carriers from propagating along the path. TFETs primarily operate through band-to-band tunneling. This occurs when electrons cross a barrier from the source to channel region. This tunneling process is made easier and is kept under control by the electric field. The Conduction Band (CB) and Valence Band (VB) energy profiles are useful for quantifying the ION, IOFF, and ambipolar currents. The energy-band diagram for the Device-C is shown in Fig. 3. The VB and CB energy levels are significantly close at the boundary between the source and channel. When the VB and CB energy gap is narrow, carriers start flowing between them25–28.

Fermi level (EFL) is a horizontal line from source to drain through the channel throughout the structure as shown in Fig. 3 (a) and 3 (c). When just Drain-Source Bias is applied (VGS = 0, VDS ≠ 0), the source and drain have distinct Fermi levels due to their different potentials. Uniform Fermi level is gone. Minimal current flows due to tunneling energy band mismatch. Applying Gate and Drain Bias (VGS ≠ 0, VDS ≠ 0), the Energy Fermi Level (EFL) at the Source (InAs, P⁺) remains near the Valence Band Edge (EVB), indicating abundant holes and a reservoir of initially occupied valence states for tunneling. Under positive voltage, the gate (GaSb) lowers the channel’s valence band edge (EVB) as shown in Fig. 3 (b) and 3 (d). Fermi energy (EFL) at Drain (GaSb, N⁺) is lower than at the source, controlled by q·VDS. The energy gradient displaces the drain conduction band, directing tunneled electrons into it.

In TFET modeling, the drain current needs to be calculated using a suitable BTBT model based on the device surface potential. For low gate voltages, the surface potential increases linearly with VGS. However, when the gate voltage is sufficiently high, the surface potential saturates and no longer responds to the gate bias. In this bias regime, which is similar to that of a MOSFET driven into strong inversion, the inversion charge in the channel prevents the surface potential from being bent anymore. The BTBT is distinguished by the sensitivity of the tunnelling current to the orientation of the bands at the tunnel junction. Implementing a precise surface potential model is essential for the use of TFET.

Electric field distribution

A high electric field is provided over the channel to increase the possibility of tunneling. The qualities of the TFET’s electric field are significantly influenced by how it is doped. The doping concentration and profile in the source, channel, and drain regions can be changed to improve device performance by altering the electric field distribution.

Electric field spread along the channel of the DM-GaSb-InAs VTFET.

The electric field for several dielectric materials used in Device-C is illustrated in Fig. 5. The electric field was observed to be highest at the tunnel intersection, due to substantial variations in doping concentration between the source and the channel. The channel region is a semiconductor, the band bends where the electric field is strongest, at the tunnel junction. Also, the TFET with ZrO2 and HfO2 was found to exhibit a higher peak electric field of 6.61 × 105 V/cm, which was due to higher dielectric constants 25 and 32 respectively. Conversely, the Device-C with SiO2 showed a lower peak electric field of 5.05 × 105 V/cm, which was due to its lower dielectric constant of 3.9. Hence high- k materials enable better control over the charge transport, reduce cross-talk, and improve device reliability.

The work-function difference generates a controlled potential step that relaxes the field where tunnelling is unwanted and intensifies it when tunnelling is desired.

Conclusion

The suggested DM-InAs-GaSb VTFET device, incorporating an InAs source pocket, was evaluated for its analog and RF properties using various dielectric materials, such as SiO2, Al2O3, HfO2, and ZrO2. The drain current (ION) of the device is assessed for several dielectric materials, with ZrO2 (7.92 × 10− 5 A/m) and HfO2 (8.15 × 10− 5 A/m) exhibiting higher performance relative to SiO2 and Al2O3. DM-InAs-GaSb VTFET has reduced hole mobility and increased electron mobility. The surface potential, electric field distribution, electron current density and hole current density were analyzed. The gm value for the proposed HfO2 based device is 606 µS/µm, while for ZrO2 it is 589 µS/µm. The cut-off frequency (fT) and transit time (τ) of devices doped with HfO2 and ZrO2 have been improved. A comparison is also performed with Ge-Si VTFET. The common source amplifier, constructed using the proposed DM-InAs-GaSb VTFET SPICE model, provides significant gain for the high-k dielectric material.
