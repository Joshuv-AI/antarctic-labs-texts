Two simultaneous cases of exertional heat stroke during a trail run in Guadeloupe (French West Indies): exceptional presentations or emerging trend?
Authors: Mathilde Monpierre, Delphine Delta, Patrick Portecop, Marc Valette, Frederic Martino, Olivier Hue
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundExertional heat stroke is a potentially fatal condition that can affect individuals performing intensive physical activity, particularly in hot environments. We report two simultaneous cases of EHS that occurred during the same outdoor sporting event in Guadeloupe (French West Indies) in April, in two unrelated young athletes with similar profiles but different clinical pictures. At the time of the event, the apparent temperature was 29.6 °C with a relative humidity of 85%.Cases presentationPatient 1: A 22-year-old man with no known medical history collapsed during an endurance test after running for one hour and 40 minutes. On admission, he presented with multi-organ dysfunction syndrome affecting neurological, hemodynamic, renal, and hepatic functions. Unlike the first case, no pre-hospital medical care was provided. The patient was evacuated to an emergency department. This risk is likely to increase in future years given the current context of global warming. Background

Exertional heat stroke (EHS), also known as exercise-induced heat stroke, is a medical emergency characterized by a rise in core body temperature, exceeding 40 °C, and initial neurological disorders during intense and prolonged muscular effort [1]. Hyperthermia may occur during exercise due to the inability of the cardiovascular system to thermoregulate. The three main pathophysiological mechanisms involved in EHS are inflammation, abnormal thermoregulation, and coagulation. The complex interplay between these mechanisms can lead to multi-organ failure, which can be fatal even when medically treated [1–3]. External body cooling by immersion in ice-cold water as soon as possible is the gold standard treatment for EHS [2, 4, 5]. Several risk factors for EHS have been reported in the literature, including environmental factors such as high air temperature and humidity. However, the epidemiology of EHS remains largely unknown worldwide since most of our knowledge relies on isolated case reports [6–10] and case series [11–15].

Our report provides a unique analysis of two EHS cases that occurred simultaneously during the same sporting event in Guadeloupe, a tropical environment, in two unrelated young student athletes. Both had similar acclimatized profile yet differed in their clinical presentations and severity levels, and luckily both had a good prognosis, which is particularly remarkable given the exceptional circumstances, i.e. two simultaneous EHS cases during the same event. As for the other case series of EHS that have been documented, they typically cover different events and times, and few of the reported simultaneous cases happened in the same conditions [16, 17], yet none in tropical environments.

Cases presentation

At the University of the French West Indies, two cases of EHS were observed among the participants of a practical outdoor endurance running test that was conducted in April to satisfy the curriculum of their sports science diploma. The students had to run a distance of 9 to 12 km between 7:30 and 10:15 a.m., in a sunny day where the average air temperature was 26.1 °C, the apparent temperature was 29.6 °C, wind speed 14.5 km/h, and the relative humidity was 85%. These weather conditions corresponded to a Wet Bulb Globe Temperature (WBGT) of 24.75 °C, calculated using the climate CHIP (Climate Change Health Impact Pathways) platform [18]. Participants were incited to take a shower at 2.5 km and 6.5 km. Six refreshment points were set up along the racecourse, at regular distances of 3.5 km at maximum.

Patient 1

A 22-year-old student, with no known past medical history, collapsed at 9:25 a.m. after running 7.7 km in one hour and 40 min during an outdoor running test. Pre-hospital care was initiated by the Fire and Rescue Service (FRS) and the Emergency Medical Service (EMS). Onsite clinical assessment revealed deep coma (Glasgow coma scale 3) and severe hyperthermia (41.7 °C). The patient was rehydrated, cooled by applying ice packs to the neck, groins, and axilla, and intubated onsite before being medically transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU) of Guadeloupe University Hospital (GUH) at 11:31 a.m. On hospital admission, his blood work showed hyperlactatemia-associated metabolic acidosis, acute renal failure, rhabdomyolysis, and moderately decreased PT level. The brain CT-scan was normal. The patient’s condition deteriorated rapidly and progressed to multi-organ dysfunction syndrome affecting neurological, hemodynamic, renal and hepatic functions with signs of fulminant hepatitis. ICU management focused on external cooling, vasopressor support using noradrenaline, extrarenal purification, antibiotic therapy − initiated to face a potential pre-existing sepsis or an infection triggered by the disruption of the intestinal barrier due to hyperthermia − and continuous administration of N-acetylcysteine. He was transferred to a specialized liver transplantation center (on D1) and kept under observation. His biological analyses showed gradual improvement in liver function, with a decrease in cytolysis and an increase in PT (> 10%) from D3, leaving no indication for transplantation in the absence of persistent severity criteria. Nonetheless, extrarenal purification was continued until D11 and he was successfully extubated at D15. His ICU stay was complicated by bilateral infectious pneumonitis, which was effectively treated with antibiotics. Overall, Patient 1 was hospitalized for 16 days in the specialized ICU and five days in the Step-Down Unit (SDU), discharged home on D22, with clinical follow-up examination on D27, and blood investigations on D27, D33, D44, and D55.

Patient 2

A 19-year-old student, with no known medical history, collapsed at around 9:22 a.m. after running one hour and 35 min over a distance of 8.2 km during the same sport test. Unlike patient 1, no pre-hospital medical care was provided and his temperature was not measured onsite. The patient was evacuated by FRS to the emergency department of GUH, where he arrived at 11:51 a.m. On admission, clinical examination revealed confusion (Glasgow coma scale 14) and hyperthermia (39.2 °C). No abnormalities were detected on his brain CT-scan. The initial blood work revealed signs of acute renal failure and rhabdomyolysis, with moderately decreased PT level. Arterial blood gases and liver function test were not ordered. He was treated with paracetamol and intravenous fluid therapy, without being hospitalized, and then was discharged home with instruction to a care provider to continue the intravenous rehydration at home for another 48 h. Blood work investigations were carried out at D2 and D4, and all parameters returned to normal.

Both patients recovered completely without physical sequelae and resumed their normal daily activities. There was no long-term follow-up and no investigations for their genetic predisposition. Both claimed that they had hydrated themselves during the exercise and had appropriately been dressed for it. To date, no recurrence of EHS has been reported in both.

Discussion and conclusions

Our report highlights the circumstance in which EHS occurred. The two patients, young athletes of similar profile participating in the same running test in a tropical environment, simultaneously presented with clinical manifestations of EHS of different severity levels, and both recovered.

Notably, the reported EHS cases involved young, athletic, healthy men, a profile well documented in the scientific literature [14, 15, 19]. The tropical weather conditions during the event were typically hot and very humid, a combination that has long been known as a risk factor for EHS [20, 21]. A WBGT of 24.75 °C, as calculated at the time of the event, indicates risk for moderate heat stress likely to alter thermoregulation during sustained physical activity. Numerous preventive measures were set up beforehand: (1) a 2-hour lecture on performance and risks in a tropical environment, prior to the test; (2) raising participants awareness to the need to wear light clothing and a head cover; (3) inciting them to sufficiently drink water before and during the running; (4) an early start time at 7:30 a.m.; (5) choosing a vastly shaded course to limit sunrays exposure; (6) the availability of showers at kilometers 2.5 and 6.5 where students were highly encouraged to pass through them during the course; (7) and finally the distribution of water, ice, and sugar at six refreshment points along the course. Despite all these measures, two cases of EHS occurred, which should drive attention to how to prevent such cases in the future.

The two patients, of comparable profiles (acclimatized young men with similar anthropometric characteristics, regular sporting activity, and no notable past medical history), were exposed to identical tropical environmental and physical stress, yet developed clinical pictures of different severity and intensity. This discrepancy might have been generated by intrinsic factors, such as hydration status, a possible individual genetic predisposition, or psychophysiological determinants such as their level of motivation. Hydration levels are always difficult to assess, however the two youngsters had reported drinking water before and during the event, which might have been insufficient. In addition to the environmental conditions, the challenging activity, the over-motivation, and the peer pressure may have precipitated EHS in them [3, 20, 22]. Patient 1 wanted to be the race winner to guarantee entrance to the paramedical school he was aiming at, and Patient 2 wanted to shine in front of his peers and to trumpethis capabilities and good performance. The presence of hemoglobin S (sickle cell disease) was not investigated, nor were genetic analyses performed in these cases, which might have explained the difference in the clinical severity they presented. Nonetheless, research in this concern is ongoing by other teams to better understand the role of genetic predispositions in EHS [23–26].

From a therapeutic standpoint, immediate external cooling using an ice-water bath is the gold standard treatment for EHS [1, 3, 27–29]. Pre-hospital healthcare was rapidly provided (within 30 min) by the emergency services to both athletes. For Patient 1, a conduction cooling technique (ice packs on the neck, groins, and axilla) started only after the EMS arrival and continued in the intensive care unit. He was intubated before effective cooling. Regarding patient 2, no cooling measures were implemented. In a prehospital setting, where the gold standard treatment for EHS is rarely available, it might be necessary to use more accessible cooling methods and to combine more than one to improve cooling effectiveness.These methods exploit different heat transfer mechanisms, including conduction (e.g., cold showers, ice packs, and cooling vests), evaporation (e.g., misting combined with ventilation), and convection (e.g., Tarp-Assisted Cooling with Oscillation, TACO). TACO is less effective than immersion in cold water and does not outperform other alternative strategies [30]. Both patients were still hyperthermic on admission to the hospital, i.e., more than two hours after the onset of symptoms. A known fact that the severity of EHS is directly related to the duration of hyperthermia, and yet neither patient was effectively cooled using the gold standard method. This raises questions on the well-known technical difficulties inherent to the implementation of external cooling using ice water in pre-hospital settings, outside an event safety plan.

The management of these two cases highlights opportunities for improvement through more training and preparedness. For patient 2, the diagnosis of EHS was made retrospectively, several months after hospital discharge. His medical records showed that on admission (more than two hours after stopping exercise), his body temperature was 39.2 °C, without any external cooling measures, which meant it was probably higher, of at least 40 °C at the time he collapsed. Moreover, his body temperature was measured only tympanically, which underestimates core temperature. Rectal temperature is the recommended measurement and remains the most accurate reflection of core temperature in such conditions [31]. The diagnosis of EHS was not suspected during the prehospital phase, when body temperature was not measured, nor during the in-hospital phase. Additionally, he was given paracetamol to treat hyperthermia and muscle pain, which is contraindicated in the context of EHS, as it may increase the risk of liver failure. Overall, his blood work was incomplete, with no blood gases and no liver function tests; he was not hospitalized for clinical and biological monitoring .This highlights the need to raise awareness on EHS early detection and management. Taken together, these observations underscore the need for prevention campaigns targeting the general public, sports professionals, first-aiders, and healthcare professionals, all the more in tropical environments where climatic conditions make people more prone to heat stroke. Training should be focused on how to recognize EHS clinical features and how to effectively manage it.

Despite the similar profiles of the two patients, their clinical presentations greatly varied in severity, illustrating the clinical diversity of this illness. The tropical climate is a major extrinsic risk factor, the impact of which could increase with global warming. Hence the need to train healthcare professionals and to raise public awareness on the prevention, recognition, and rapid management of this medical emergency, especially in tropical environments.
