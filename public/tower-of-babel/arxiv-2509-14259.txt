Persuasive or Neutral? A Field Experiment on Generative AI in Online Travel Planning CCS: Information systems Web services CCS: Information systems Expert systems CCS: Human-centered computing Field studies CCS: Human-centered computing Empirical studies in interaction design
Lynna Jirpongopas, Bernhard Lutz, Jörg Ebner, Rustam Vahidov, Dirk Neumann

Abstract
. Generative AI (GenAI) offers new opportunities for customer support in online travel agencies, yet little is known about how its design influences user engagement, purchase behavior, and user experience. We report results from a randomized field experiment in online travel itinerary planning, comparing GenAI that expressed (A) positive enthusiasm, (B) neutral expression, and (C) no tone instructions (control). Users in group A wrote significantly longer prompts than those in groups B and C. At the same time, users in groups A and B were more likely to purchase subscriptions of the webservice. We further analyze linguistic cues across experimental groups to explore differences in user experience and explain subscription purchases and affiliate link clicks based on these cues. Our findings provide implications for the design of persuasive and engaging GenAI interfaces in consumer-facing contexts and contribute to understanding how linguistic framing shapes user behavior in AI-mediated decision support.



1.
Introduction

Over the past few years, online travel agencies (OTAs) have been continuously replacing traditional travel agencies. In 2023, the global online travel industry generated $512.5 billion of revenue and is expected to reach 1.26 trillion by 2032. In particular, the vast majority of travelers (72%) said they preferred to book their trips online, compared to only 12% that preferred to use a travel agency.

Travel itinerary planning refers to organizing a holiday trip through various cities or towns, including the preferred activities such as visits to national parks, sights, museums, or beaches
(
Roy et al. 2011
;
Zhang et al. 2016
)
. Given a fixed time period and budget, travelers aim to maximize the overall experience of their trip
(
Zhang and Sun 2016
)
. Planning an optimal itinerary through various cities with little knowledge about the country or region while accounting for time and budget constraints can be challenging
(
Lim et al. 2019
)
. At the same time, it is emotionally charged, driven by excitement, anticipation, and fear of missing out.

This complexity makes travel itinerary planning a promising domain for GenAI, a natural candidate for integration into OTAs. While GenAI can easily assist customers with simple bookings such as individual flights, train tickets, or rental cars
(
He et al. 2025
)
, its potential should be even more pronounced when booking a complex itinerary through various destinations, including the desired activities of the traveler. GenAI not only provides knowledge about most places in the world, including points of interest, sights and possible activities, it can also guide users through the entire booking process.
Prior research has acknowledged the potential of GenAI in many areas, including planning, marketing, customer-relationship management, and customer support
(
Adam et al. 2021
;
Feuerriegel et al. 2024
;
Han et al. 2023
;
Lee et al. 2025
;
Seymour et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
. Despite rapid deployment of AI assistants across domains, there is little evidence on how GenAI should be designed for consumer-facing contexts. Benchmark studies on GenAI in itinerary planning demonstrate technical challenges such as hallucinations and constraint handling
(
Xie et al. 2024a
;
Wang et al. 2025
)
, while prototype systems show that GenAI can in principle generate itineraries
(
JU et al. 2024
;
Udandarao et al. 2025
)
. However, it remains unclear how GenAI should communicate with users in real-world planning scenarios to foster engagement and purchase behavior.

A growing body of HCI and service research shows that the tone of AI communication plays a central role in shaping user experience. Warm and enthusiastic messages can increase perceptions of social presence and engagement
(
Kull et al. 2021
)
, while neutral or competence-oriented tones may be more effective in tasks that demand accuracy and credibility
(
Packard and Berger 2021
;
Li et al. 2019
)
. Language expectancy theory suggests that humans have different normative expectations towards the language that is used in different contexts and social roles. Deviations from these norms can positively or negatively influence the communicator’s persuasiveness. For instance, humans generally expect communication in professional working environments to be more objective, whereas communication with friends and family are considered more personal and emotional
(
Burgoon and Miller 1985
;
Burgoon 1995
)
. Contextualized to itinerary planning with GenAI, emotionally expressive AI responses may constitute a positive expectancy violation, increasing persuasiveness of the AI, leading to greater user engagement and more frequent purchases. Conversely, users might also expect a more neutral tone, reflecting the rational and objective nature of AI. In fact, prior studies have found limited results regarding how (Gen)AI in customer service should be designed
(
Gnewuch et al. 2017
;
Han et al. 2023
;
Zhang et al. 2024
)
. Recent results by
Han et al. 2023
suggest that chatbots in customer support should communicate using more neutral and objective rather than emotional language. Against this background, online itinerary planning with GenAI represents a distinct use case where users may seek not only efficiency but also excitement and personalization–needs that require effective communication strategies.

The tone used by GenAI in customer service influences user experience
(
Hu et al. 2018
;
Okoso et al. 2025
)
. Different user experiences are then reflected in the way humans communicate
(
Fang et al. 2025
;
Ferland et al. 2025
;
He et al. 2024
)
.
Jang and Yi 2017
demonstrated that UX elements can be extracted from user-generated text using “Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count”
(
Pennebaker et al. 2015
, LIWC,)
and that these linguistic cues significantly affect user satisfaction. Therefore, we also perform an explorative analysis of the usage of linguistic cues in users’ input prompts. In particular, we aim to explain the subsequent purchase behavior across the experimental groups based on these cues.

We present results from a randomized field experiment, conducted at a US startup that offers a travel itinerary planning service based on OpenAI GPT models. A total of 282 users participated in the A/B/C test from November 3, 2024 to April 14, 2025. We added tone instructions in the base prompt of the chatbot to be either (A) positive enthusiastic, using emoji when appropriate, (B) neutral, expressionless, or (C) no tone instruction given.
Our findings suggest that users in Group A engaged more with GenAI, measured as the length of input prompts, than users in Groups B and C. In addition, we find that users of Group A and B purchased more subscriptions of the service than users in Group C. Furthermore, we identify several significant differences in the usage of linguistic cues across the experimental groups. We finally establish significant links between the usage of linguistic cues and users’ purchase behavior.

Our
main contributions
are as follows:

•

We provide what is, to the best of our knowledge, the first field experiment on GenAI in travel itinerary planning, examining how users respond to AI-generated itineraries in a real-world, consumer-facing context.

•

We demonstrate how the communication style of the AI agent (enthusiastic vs. neutral) shapes user engagement, experience, and purchase behavior.

•

We derive design implications for the development of GenAI systems that support complex planning tasks in customer service contexts.

The following section provides an overview of prior work on GenAI for travel itinerary planning and AI in customer service, which motivates our research questions. After describing the field experiment and the online travel planning platform, we present the results and conclude with a discussion of implications, limitations, and directions for future research.

2.
Background

2.1.
Travel Itinerary Planning with Generative AI

The topic of travel itinerary planning has attracted considerable attention in prior work.
Early research approached the task primarily as a mathematical optimization problem
(
Bolzoni et al. 2014
;
Roy et al. 2011
, e.g.,)
.
While these studies proposed powerful solvers and approximations, they typically relied on large structured databases containing detailed information about points of interest, cities, and regions.
Moreover, formulating the optimization problem is a demanding task that requires technical expertise, making it inaccessible for most end users.
Even once solved, itineraries need to be recomputed from scratch whenever users request changes, which results in long waiting times and limits the practicality of such methods in real-world customer-facing settings.

Given the challenges of traditional travel itinerary planning, researchers analyzed the usage of GenAI in itinerary planning tasks
(
Banerjee et al. 2025
;
He et al. 2025
;
Xie et al. 2023
;
Zhang et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
. In fact, GenAI appears as a natural one-fits-all-solution, which should account for all of the aforementioned problems. LLMs like OpenAI’s GPT models, can generate and access knowledge about countries, regions, cities, and points of interests, while supporting natural and convenient user interaction. However,
Banerjee et al. 2025
identify hallucination as a major issue, which might result in non-sensical itinerary suggestions. As a remedy,
Banerjee et al. 2025
ground each user query in a knowledge basis to ensure feasibility of the proposed trips. Similarly,
He et al. 2025
find that GenAI struggles to propose itineraries that match the user’s preferences.

Furthermore, there are studies that focus on AI travel agent benchmarking and evaluation methodologies, employing meticulously curated and labeled datasets
(
Wang et al. 2025
;
Xie et al. 2024b
)
.
Wang et al. 2025
introduces TripTailor, a large-scale benchmark for personalized travel planning based on real-world data from 40 Chinese cities, 500k+ POIs, and nearly 4,000 itineraries. Their experiments show that even state-of-the-art models like GPT-4o generate itineraries that rarely match human plans: only 21% satisfy feasibility and rationality constraints, and fewer than 10% surpass human itineraries in personalization. The main reasons are that current GenAI agents hallucinate or confuse information, struggle with spatial–geographic reasoning (leading to inefficient routes), and fail to capture personalized user preferences—making them unsuitable for fully autonomous, high-quality travel planning at present. In a similar vein,
Xie et al. 2024b
introduces TravelPlanner, a benchmark for testing whether LLM–based agents can handle realistic travel itinerary planning. Their experiments show that even advanced models like GPT-4 achieve only a 0.6% success rate, because current GenAI agents fail to manage multiple constraints simultaneously, often hallucinate, and struggle with long-horizon planning such as keeping track of budgets, routes, and accommodations. In short, the authors argue that GenAI is currently unsuitable for complex real-world planning tasks because it cannot reliably integrate information, respect constraints, or reason comprehensively across multi-step decision processes.

However, there are also preprint studies by Google
(
Udandarao et al. 2025
)
and Meta
(
JU et al. 2024
)
that demonstrate the potential feasibility of GenAI in itinerary planning based on prototypes. Roamify
(
Udandarao et al. 2025
)
is a Chrome-extension travel assistant that uses LLMs (e.g., Llama) to generate personalized itineraries based on user preferences, integrating up-to-date blog/web scraped content and recommendation systems. In user surveys and interviews, Roamify was generally preferred across age groups over traditional travel planning methods, especially when itineraries adapt dynamically to what users like. To the Globe
(
JU et al. 2024
)
is a real-time system that takes natural language requests and translates them into symbolic constraints, which are then solved via Mixed Integer Linear Programming to produce travel itineraries that satisfy interdependent constraints such as flights, hotels, attractions.

The study by
Zhang et al. 2024
is similar to ours. The authors analyzed the effects of chatbots expressing emotional concerns (e.g., “The museum is crowded and not very large. I’m worried it will affect your viewing experience”) and using emoji in the context of personalized tourist attraction recommendations. The chatbot interacted with users in three steps: first, it asked about the city the user wants to visit; second, it elicited information about travel companions and budgets; and third, it proposed a tourist attraction. Their results based on three online experiments suggest that the expression of concern creates an illusion of warmth, increasing trust and satisfaction.

2.2.
Design of AI in Customer Service

Prior research has extensively studied language in customer service when service agents are represented by humans
(
Gremler and Gwinner 2000
;
Kranzbühler et al. 2020
;
Packard and Berger 2021
;
Pugh 2001
, e.g.,)
or human-AI hybrids
(
Gnewuch et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
. While
Gremler and Gwinner 2000
and
Kranzbühler et al. 2020
highlight the importance of positive emotions and rapport in customer–agent communication,
Packard and Berger 2021
show that concrete rather than abstract language increases customer satisfaction. These findings are not contradictory but complementary, suggesting that effective customer service communication should ideally combine both warmth and concreteness. However, there is also evidence that, depending on the context, emotionally expressive language may also decrease the service employee’s perceived competence
(
Li et al. 2019
)
.

While AI systems are widely adopted in customer service for their efficiency and scalability
(
Adam et al. 2021
)
, much less is known about how design choices in their communication style (e.g., positive emotional vs neutral tone) shape user experience and engagement. In this regard, the review study by
Diederich et al. 2022
identifies several gaps in research on chatbots, including the need to account for user characteristics, explore adaptive design features, and investigate the long-term effects of different communication styles. They further emphasize that verbal design elements, such as emotional tone, should be studied in light of user perceptions and context.

Several prior works investigated the design of AI in customer service
(
Diederich et al. 2022
;
Gnewuch et al. 2017
;
Xie et al. 2024a
)
.
Similar to human service agents, there also seems to be a trade-off between perceived warmth and competence of the AI
(
Gray and Wegner 2012
;
Kull et al. 2021
;
Li et al. 2019
;
Han et al. 2023
)
.
Hu et al. 2018
emphasize that tone is a crucial yet undersexplored factor in customer care chatbots. They provide empirical evidence that tones used by agent in their responses could significantly affect user experience in customer care.
Similarly,
Kull et al. 2021
show that the tone of a chatbot’s initial message plays a crucial role in shaping brand engagement. Across three experiments, they find that a warmly phrased introduction (friendly, welcoming) reduces perceived brand–self distance and thereby fosters stronger consumer engagement compared to competent or neutral messages. This effect is particularly pronounced when consumers already have a neutral or positive relationship with the brand.

Li et al. 2019
examine how the use of emoticons by service employees shapes user perceptions in online customer–service interactions. Through controlled experiments and a field study, they find that emoticons increase perceived warmth but decrease perceived competence. The work highlights how subtle paralinguistic cues in text-based interfaces can affect satisfaction and behavioral responses, underscoring the need for context-sensitive design of social signals in service technologies.

Han et al. 2023
hypothesize that customers have prior experiences regarding AI’s capability of feeling and expressing emotions. Given that AI is substantially different from humans because it is not perceived to possess experience (i.e., the capacity to feel emotions), humans should be more likely to expect neutral instead of emotional language
(
Gray and Wegner 2012
)
. Although AI is increasingly becoming more sophisticated in recognizing human emotions
(
D’Mello and Calvo 2013
;
Wang et al. 2022
)
, it remains unclear how humans respond when communicating with emotionally-charged AI in different contexts.

Setting the chatbot’s language to meet the user’s expectation for particular contexts is considered a high design priority
(
Chaves 2020
)
.
Chaves 2020
proposed a framework for register-adaptive utterance that align with users’ expectations, which could enhance perceived credibility, appropriateness, and social role attribution. Collectively, these findings suggest that tailoring conversational tone to users’ contextual expectations is a key determinant of chatbot effectiveness as an assistant. So far, the investigated contexts include, among others, tourist attraction recommendation
(
Zhang et al. 2024
)
, psychotherapy
(
Song et al. 2025
)
, customer care
(
Hu et al. 2018
)
, movie recommendations
(
Lee and Choi 2017
)
, and feedback collection
(
Adam et al. 2021
)
.

2.3.
Research Questions

In summary, prior research has identified major challenges to using GenAI for travel itinerary planning
(
He et al. 2025
;
Wang et al. 2025
;
Xie et al. 2024a
)
, but prototypes suggest potential feasibility
(
JU et al. 2024
;
Udandarao et al. 2025
)
. Studies on AI in customer service have produced mixed results regarding the role of language
(
Gray and Wegner 2012
;
Kull et al. 2021
;
Han et al. 2023
)
.

Following
Chaves 2020
, we argue that users’ expectations about AI language are a critical design choice that depends on context. Travel itinerary planning involves high cognitive effort (optimizing time and budget) but also emotional aspects (excitement and fear of missing out).

We therefore examine whether GenAI should adopt an
enthusiastic
versus a
neutral
tone when communicating with users. In a field experiment with paying participants, we analyze how tone affects (1) linguistic features of user prompts, (2) user experience, and (3) purchase behavior. Our research questions are:

•

RQ1:
How does the communication tone of GenAI (enthusiastic vs. neutral) influence user engagement and purchase behavior in travel itinerary planning?

•

RQ2:
How does GenAI’s tone shape the linguistic characteristics of users’ input prompts?

•

RQ3:
How are linguistic cues in user prompts related to subsequent purchase decisions?

Method

Our research framework is illustrated in
Figure

1
. We performed a randomized field experiment in an existing webservice about online travel itinerary planning. Specifically, we manipulated the tone instructions added to the base prompt, which guides the AI to adjusting the itinerary based on the user’s preferences. We included three experimental groups, namely, Group (A) positive enthuaiastic tone, Group (B) neutral expressionless, and Group (C) with no tone manipulation as control group. We measure the length and number user input prompts, as well as linguistic cues used by users. In addition, we store data about users’ purchase behavior in terms of subscriptions of the webservice and clicks on affiliated links. Finally, we estimate mixed-effects regression models to analyze differences in user engagement, purchase behavior, and how users’ usage of linguistic cues is linked to subscriptions and clicks on affiliated links.

Tern.app
travel planning platform. Using OpenAI GPT models, Tern generates highly personalized and customized travel plans. The user interface and the chatbot allow easy editing and collaboration, allowing users to easily adjust their itineraries and share them with others.

3.2.
Participants

The experiment commenced on November 3, 2024, and concluded on April 14, 2025, spanning approximately 4.5 months of data collection. During this period, the platform attracted 5,212 unique visitors. Of these users, 1,774 (34%) proceeded to create a user profile and 1,617 (31% of total visitors) successfully generated at least one travel itinerary. There were 670 users that clicked on the “Ask AI” button, but not all proceeded to chat.

A total of 282 users chatted with the AI. The majority of these users were based in the United States (94 users, 34%), followed by users from Europe (43), India (20), the United Kingdom (12), Australia (11), Canada (9), Mexico (2), Brazil (2), and Singapore (1). For 58 users, the country was not identified. In terms of age distribution, 102 users (36%) were between the ages of 26–40, 49 users (17%) were aged 41 and above, 43 users (15%) were between 18–25, and 4 users (1%) were under the age of 18. Age data was unavailable for 49 users (18%). No gender information is available as it was not asked by the user interface. Regarding the devices, 124 used laptops or desktop computers (62 Windows, 58 Mac OS X, 4 Linux), 99 users had mobile devices (35 Android, 64 iOS) and 59 users had unknown devices. We collected 1301 input queries from the users who chatted with the AI Agent.

We ensured anonymity of the data by removing any personal information. For this purpose, we implemented Personally Identifiable Information (PII) filtering using the presidio-analyzer Python library, which is consistent with the methodology proposed by
Zhu et al. 2025
for post-processing to detect and remove PII. Unintentionally leaked PII was subsequently filtered manually.

3.3.
Manipulation: Tone Instructions in System Prompt

The “Ask AI” feature is powered by OpenAI’s GPT-4o model and guided by a system-level prompt that instructs it to emulate the role of a knowledgeable and helpful travel agent, providing personalized recommendations, itinerary adjustments, and destination-specific insights in response to user queries.

All users were randomly assigned to one of three experimental conditions, each defined by a variation in the tone of the AI assistant’s prompt.

•

Group A (Positive enthusiastic)
:
You have a positive enthusiastic tone. Your persona is a charming hotel concierge. Use emojis when appropriate.

•

Group B (Neutral expressionless)
:
You have a neutral expressionless tone.

•

Group C (Control)
:
(No tone-instruction augmentation to the base prompt.)

The underlying model and the base prompt content remained constant across conditions, with only the tone instructions augmented. The base prompt is the startup’s business secret, but in essence, it consists of the following instructions:

Act as a travel agent […] and incorporate user’s travel preferences.

All other system parameters, including model configuration and interface design, were held constant. Of the 282 users that chatted with the AI, 86 were randomly assigned to Group A, 105 to Group B, and 91 to Group C.

3.5.
Measurements

We store all user input prompts to the “Ask AI” feature. The number and length of the prompts serve as a measure of user engagement. To capture aspects of user experience, we extract linguistic cues from the prompts, which reflect users’ emotional, cognitive, and social orientation during interaction
(
Jang and Yi 2017
)
. Finally, we count the number of annual subscriptions and clicks on affiliate links as behavioral measures of purchases.

3.6.
Ethical Considerations

The field experiment analyzed anonymized behavioral data from users of an itinerary planning platform.
Participants were not explicitly informed that they were part of an experiment, as the interventions were
implemented as part of the regular functionality of the platform.
All data were collected in anonymized form, contained no personally identifiable information,
and were analyzed in aggregate.
According to institutional and legal regulations, no formal ethics approval was required for this field study.
At the same time, we plan to replicate the study under controlled laboratory conditions including neurophysiological measurements of cognitive and affective processes.
For these settings, our research group has obtained ethics approval from the University of (anonymized), Approval No. 24-1533-S2,
which underscores our commitment to conducting research in line with established ethical standards.

4.
Results

4.1.
User Engagement

We start by analyzing user engagement across the experimental groups regarding the length and number of input prompts. Our data contains 282 users that chatted with the AI. Out of these, 86 were randomly assigned to Group A, 105 to Group B, and 91 to Group C. The number of input prompts is 404 in Group A, 506 in Group B, and 391 in Group C.

Length of Input Prompts

We use mixed-effects regression models with random-effects at the user-level (equivalent to one-way ANOVA with repeated measurements) to analyze the length and number of input prompts.
2
2

2

All analyses were performed in Python v3.12. We used the
mixedlm
function with Wald z-tests for fixed effects from
statsmodels
.
Thereby, we model the user-specific repeated measurements across input prompts while allowing sufficient power to identify significant differences
(
Baayen et al. 2008
)
. Different from fixed effects, including a random effect still allows us to analyze the between-group variation
(

5.
Discussion

5.1.
Summary of Findings

Our field experiment demonstrates that the communication tone of GenAI significantly influences user engagement and purchase behavior in online travel planning. Specifically, users interacting with an enthusiastic chatbot (Group A) produced longer prompts, while both enthusiastic (Group A) and neutral tones (Group B) increased subscription likelihood compared to the control group (C) with no tone instructions. Although prompt quantity did not vary across conditions, linguistic analyses revealed systematic differences in how users expressed themselves, with positive emotion, interrogatives, and motivational language more prevalent in the neutral condition, and punctuation, spatial references, and prepositions more frequent in the enthusiastic condition. Importantly, several linguistic cues were found to predict subscription and click behaviors, suggesting that user communication style mediates the persuasive effects of AI tone.

Here, we found interesting interactions between the tone of GenAI and linguistic cues in user prompts. In Group A, a more casual and emotional communication style reduced the likelihood of subscriptions to the service. Similarly, users in Group B who wrote in a more analytical and structured style, and who referenced goals or biological needs, were more likely to subscribe. By contrast, users in Group C who wrote in a more fragmented way, using
quantifiers, and lifestyle references were more inclined to subscribe. Regarding clicks on affiliated links, linguistic cues had no significant associations for users in Group A. Users in Group B communicating in a more fragmented sentence structure and more goal-oriented language were associated with stronger click behavior. Higher click rates were observed for Group C users who expressed themselves in a more descriptive, socially anchored, and temporally specific manner.

5.2.
Theoretical Implications

To the best of our knowledge, our study provides the first field experimental evidence on the use of GenAI for travel itinerary planning in a consumer-facing context. Prior work has primarily focused on prototypes
(
JU et al. 2024
;
Udandarao et al. 2025
)
or benchmark studies
(
Xie et al. 2024a
;
Wang et al. 2025
)
that highlight technical feasibility and inherent challenges such as hallucinations. By contrast, we demonstrate in a real-world deployment with paying users that GenAI can indeed be applied in itinerary planning. In particular, we find that success does not depend solely on technical performance but is strongly shaped by design choices such as the tone instructions of the GenAI. These findings suggest that theories of GenAI use in complex planning tasks should not only focus on technical dimensions (e.g., accuracy, feasibility, constraint handling) but also integrate social and communicative aspects of design to fully explain user acceptance and impact.

In addition, our study extends prior research that has used linguistic cues to capture aspects of user experience
(
Jang and Yi 2017
;
Fang et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
. We measure such cues in user prompts through LIWC and show that they are linked to concrete purchase behavior. While these results are exploratory in nature and should be interpreted with caution, they nonetheless indicate systematic patterns that link linguistic expression to consumer decisions. Taken together, these findings suggest that linguistic signals can serve as a bridge between the experiential dimension of user interaction and actual consumer decisions, highlighting the importance of integrating linguistic cues into theories of GenAI persuasion and adoption. For example, future systems could dynamically adapt their tone when users employ highly motivational or emotionally positive language, or provide additional informational support when user prompts contain frequent interrogatives. More broadly, linguistic analysis could help detect moments when users are still exploring versus ready to commit, enabling GenAI to calibrate its communication style in real time.

Our findings also contribute to the broader discussion of AI in customer service by highlighting the trade-off between warmth and neutrality in GenAI communication. In our study, an enthusiastic (warm) tone proved effective in stimulating engagement and shaping linguistic expression, consistent with prior work emphasizing the relational benefits of warmth in service interactions
(
Kull et al. 2021
)
. At the same time, a neutral tone was equally effective in increasing subscription likelihood, suggesting that neutrality can enhance perceptions of competence and credibility, especially in cognitively demanding planning tasks. These results support the notion that the expected language of AI agents is context-dependent
(
Chaves 2020
)
: while warmth may be advantageous in affect-rich and exploratory settings, neutrality may be better suited for tasks that require focus and rational evaluation. Future research should further examine this contextual dependency and explore to what extent our findings generalize beyond pleasurable tasks such as holiday planning to more critical or stressful customer service situations.

5.3.
Practical Implications

The findings offer several implications for the design of consumer-facing GenAI systems in online travel planning. Incorporating explicit tone instructions is preferable to leaving the model unguided, as both enthusiastic and neutral tones increase subscription likelihood compared to the control. While enthusiastic framing stimulated longer and more detailed user prompts, neutral framing encouraged more structured and goal-oriented communication, both of which were linked to higher subscription probability.

Notably, industry guidelines such as Microsoft Azure’s documentation
(
Azure 2024
)
recommend specifying tone of voice as a best practice when prompting GPT models, yet provide little clarity on how to define or select an appropriate tone in practice; identifying effective styles thus requires deliberate design choices and empirical testing.
From a design perspective, chatbot agent tone should be calibrated to foster engagement without undermining perceived competence. Tone can be leveraged as a design lever not only to enhance user experience but also to guide economic outcomes in GenAI-enabled travel platforms.

5.4.
Limitations and Future Research

Our study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, the field experiment was conducted within a single commercial platform with users largely from North America, which may limit the generalizability of findings to other domains of GenAI use. Second, the manipulation of tone was restricted to enthusiastic versus neutral framing, leaving open whether alternative tones (e.g., formal, empathetic) would yield different effects. Third, outcomes such as subscription and affiliate clicks capture short-term behaviors, but longer-term impacts of tone on sustained engagement (e.g., actual booking of the planned itinerary) remain unexplored.

In the near future, we plan to replicate and extend the findings from the field experiment in a controlled lab experiment. While the field study achieves greater ecological validity
(
Lew et al. 2011
)
, a lab study will allow us to measure cognitive and emotional activity using EEG to better understand the driving factors that guide user behavior
(
Dimoka et al. 2012
)
.
