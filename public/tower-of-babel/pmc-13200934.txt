Placental transcriptome profiling and immune signature differences in pregnancies with and without gestational diabetes mellitus: An observational study
Authors: Yea Eun Kang, Seong Eun Lee, Joung Youl Lim, Ok Soon Kim, Jiyeon Yoon, Yewon Jung, Ju Hee Lee, Bon Jeong Ku, Mina Lee, Hyun Jin Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is associated with placental dysfunction, which contributes to adverse pregnancy outcomes. This study aimed to compare transcriptomic profiles and immune signatures in placentas from pregnancies with and without GDM. Placental tissues from 22 participants (12 GDM and 10 controls) were analyzed using ribonucleic acid sequencing. Differentially expressed genes were identified and subjected to gene ontology and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathway analyses. X-cell analysis was used to assess immune cell composition. Western blotting validated the expression of selected genes. A total of 1045 differentially expressed genes were identified. Among these, COL22A1, COL8A2, ACTC1, PAX6, PPP2R2C, G6PC were significantly upregulated. In contrast, LEP and ERBB2 were downregulated in GDM placentas. Gene ontology and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes analyses showed that extracellular matrix organization, muscle contraction, and calcium signaling pathways were upregulated. Pathways involved in glucose import, amino acid transport, and the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress response were downregulated. X-cell analysis suggested alterations in stromal and immune cell composition, with increased myocytes and fibroblasts and reduced M2 macrophages in GDM placentas. We found that various molecular signatures, including composition, metabolism, ER stress, and immune cells, were altered in GDM placentas compared to controls. This study demonstrates that the GDM placentas exhibit significant transcriptomic alterations, including changes in metabolic, ER stress-related, and immune pathways. These findings highlight the role of placental remodeling in the pathophysiology of GDM and may provide insights for future research and potential therapeutic strategies.

1. Introduction

The prevalence of gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is increasing with the growing prevalence of obesity and advanced maternal age worldwide.[1,2] This upward trend is a significant public health concern, as numerous studies have shown that GDM can lead to a spectrum of perinatal and fetal complications, including preeclampsia, macrosomia, and fetal distress. Furthermore, GDM poses long-term health risks to mothers, elevating the likelihood of developing type 2 diabetes and other metabolic diseases even after childbirth.[3–5] These multifaceted consequences underscore the critical importance of understanding the mechanisms underlying GDM.

At its core, GDM is characterized by hyperglycemia resulting from an inadequate compensatory response of pancreatic beta cells to heightened insulin resistance during pregnancy.[2] However, GDM is not a uniform condition; it encompasses a diverse range of underlying pathophysiological mechanisms. For some women, the predominant issue lies in pancreatic beta-cell dysfunction, which limits their ability to meet the increased insulin demands of pregnancy.[6] This dysfunction may result from genetic predisposition, environmental influences, or chronic metabolic stress. Conversely, in other cases, insulin resistance itself plays a more prominent role, often driven by obesity, systemic inflammation, or altered adipokine signaling.[6] Importantly, these distinct pathophysiological profiles are linked to different pregnancy outcomes. For example, pregnancies complicated by insulin resistance are more likely to result in large-for-gestational-age infants, while beta-cell dysfunction is more frequently associated with small-for-gestational-age births.[7,8]

Despite the heterogeneity of GDM, the placenta remains a unifying focus of study, given its pivotal role in the disorder’s pathogenesis and progression during pregnancy.[9] During pregnancy, the placenta secretes hormones such as human placental lactogen and placental growth hormone, which increase insulin resistance in maternal tissues to ensure adequate glucose availability for the fetus.[10] This physiological adaptation often leads to progressive insulin resistance as gestation advances, peaking in the third trimester.[11] In the context of GDM, these changes are exaggerated, contributing to dysregulated glucose levels. Dysfunctions in these processes have been closely associated with complications observed in GDM, including impaired vascularization, heightened inflammation, and disrupted metabolic homeostasis.[9] These disruptions highlight the placenta as a key focus for understanding the molecular underpinnings of GDM.

Building on this understanding, our previous study provided valuable insights into the placental immune environment in GDM, identifying roles for pro-inflammatory T cells and immune-escape signaling using ribonucleic acid (RNA) sequencing and fluorescence-activated cell sorting analysis.[12] However, these findings were derived from a limited dataset of only 9 participants, restricting the generalizability of our results. Recognizing this limitation, the current study seeks to expand upon our earlier work by performing a more comprehensive transcriptomic analysis using placental samples from a larger cohort of 22 participants (10 controls and 12 GDM patients). By employing gene ontology (GO) pathway analysis and X-cell analysis, we aimed to uncover altered molecular pathways and cellular mechanisms in GDM. Through this approach, we seek to deepen our understanding of the complex interplay between the placenta and the pathophysiology of GDM, paving the way for targeted interventions to mitigate its impact on maternal and fetal health.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study participants and enrollment criteria

A total of 22 participants were recruited from Chungnam National University Hospital, consisting of 12 patients diagnosed with GDM and 10 healthy pregnant women as controls. Most participants were high-risk pregnant women referred to this hospital. The pool of participants comprised all women who delivered at Chungnam National University Hospital and agreed to provide placental samples and consented to their genetic analysis for research. Participants were consecutively recruited from the outpatient clinic to minimize potential selection bias and ensure representativeness of the study cohort. The final number of participants (n = 22) was determined by the availability of eligible cases during the recruitment period and was similar to that of prior transcriptomic studies of GDM placentas, which typically included 10 to 20 participants per group.[13,14] Given that our analysis was based on bulk RNA sequencing, which allows robust detection of molecular differences even in modest cohort size, this sample size was considered sufficient to achieve the study objectives. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Chungnam National University Hospital (approval number: 2020-08-025). GDM was diagnosed using the 2-step approach recommended by the American Diabetes Association. In the first step, participants underwent a 50-g glucose challenge test, and those with a 1-hour plasma glucose level ≥140 mg/dL proceeded to the diagnostic 100-g oral glucose tolerance test. GDM was confirmed if at least 2 of the following criteria were met during the 100-g oral glucose tolerance test: fasting ≥95 mg/dL, 1-hour ≥180 mg/dL, 2-hour ≥155 mg/dL, or 3-hour ≥140 mg/dL.[15] Participants were required to meet the following inclusion criteria: singleton pregnancies, maternal age <40 years. Exclusion criteria included prepregnancy body mass index (BMI) exceeding 30 kg/m2, current insulin therapy for glycemic control, gestational hypertension, preeclampsia, proteinuria, or a medical history of polycystic ovary syndrome. None of the participants exhibited any obstetric or systemic complications, ensuring the focus remained on the metabolic differences related to GDM.

2.2. Placental tissue collection and processing

Placental tissues were collected immediately after cesarean delivery. To minimize variability, samples were taken from the central region of the maternal side of the placenta, where metabolic and immunological activities are most consistent. Tissue samples were dissected into 1 cm × 1 cm sections and immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen to preserve RNA integrity. Samples were stored at −80°C until further analysis. Homogenization of placental tissues was conducted under sterile conditions using a prechilled mortar and pestle. TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen) was employed for RNA extraction following the manufacturer’s protocol. All equipment and reagents were autoclaved or sterilized to prevent contamination, and RNA concentrations were measured using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). RNA integrity was assessed with the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer RNA Nano Chip (Agilent Technologies). Samples with RNA integrity numbers >7.5 were included for downstream transcriptomic analysis.

2.3. RNA library preparation and sequencing

RNA libraries were constructed using the SureSelectXT RNA Direct System (Agilent Technologies) according to the manufacturer’s guidelines. Libraries were prepared to ensure optimal coverage of transcriptomic data, targeting both coding and noncoding RNAs. RNA sequencing was conducted on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina Inc.) to generate high-quality paired-end reads (100 nucleotides in length). Each sample produced an average of 38 million reads, ensuring sufficient depth for robust gene expression analysis. The resulting sequencing data were deposited in the Gene Expression Omnibus database under accession number GSE237061, and additional information regarding sequencing metrics is provided in Table S1.

2.4. Transcriptomic and functional analysis

Transcript abundance was quantified as transcripts per million, and log10-transformed transcripts per million values were used for comparative visualization with the “ComplexHeatmap” package in R software (www.r-project.org). Differential expression analysis was conducted using the DESeq2 package, which identifies genes with statistically significant differences in expression between the GDM and control groups. Genes with an adjusted P value <.05 and fold change >2 were considered significantly differentially expressed. Enrichment analysis was performed using the fgsea R package to identify dysregulated pathways and biological processes. GO terms and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses were applied to further interpret the biological implications of the transcriptomic findings.

2.5. Protein extraction and Western blot analysis

Human placental tissues were lysed in radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer containing 30 mM Tris (pH = 7.5), 150 mM sodium chloride, 1% Nonidet P-40, 10% glycerol, and protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Roche). Protein concentration was determined using the bicinchoninic acid assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

For Western blot analysis, 30 to 50 μg of total protein was separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes. Primary antibodies used included COL8A2 (1:2000; Invitrogen), ACTC1 (1:5000; Invitrogen), LEPTIN (1:500; Abcam), and β-actin (1:1000; Cell signaling, Technology). Membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies and subsequently with alkaline phosphate-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:2000; Abcam) for 1 hour at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using the LI-COR Odyssey infrared imaging system (LI-COR Biosciences), and relative protein expression was quantified with ImageJ software. All experiments were independently repeated at least 3 times to ensure consistency and reproducibility.

2.6. Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software (version 26; IBM). Continuous variables were reported as means ± standard deviations and compared between groups using independent sample t tests. Categorical variables were analyzed using chi-square tests or Fisher exact tests, as appropriate. The significance threshold was set at P < .05, and all tests were two-sided.

To confirm the robustness of the findings, variability within groups was assessed using one-way analysis of variance for multiple comparisons. Data visualization was performed using GraphPad Prism (version 10; GraphPad Software).

A formal a priori power calculation was not performed due to the exploratory nature of this transcriptomic study and the lack of well-established effect size estimates for placental gene expression changes in GDM. In addition, sample availability was limited by strict clinical inclusion and tissue quality criteria.

3. Results

3.1. Clinical characteristics of participants

Clinical information of the participants enrolled in this study is summarized in Figures 1A and S1. The mean age was similar between the GDM group (32.5 ± 3.6 years) and the control group (32.6 ± 2.6 years, P = .940). Prepregnancy BMI was significantly higher in the GDM group (24.1 ± 3.3 kg/m2) compared with controls (20.7 ± 2.8 kg/m2, P = .015). BMI at delivery also tended to be higher in GDM (28.1 ± 2.5 kg/m2) than in controls (25.6 ± 3.7 kg/m2), although this difference did not reach statistical significance (P = .081). Weight gain during pregnancy did not differ significantly between groups (GDM: 10.2 ± 6.3 kg vs. Control: 12.3 ± 4.4 kg, P = .373). No significant differences were observed in gestational duration (GDM: 266.7 ± 4.2 days vs. Control: 267.5 ± 4.1 days, P = .644) or infant birth weight (GDM: 3275 ± 447 g vs. Control: 3250 ± 195 g, P = .864). Cesarean section rate, parity distribution, and other baseline clinical variables were also comparable between groups.

When comparing clinical factors between participants with and without GDM (Fig. 1B), we found no significant differences in most measured variables, including maternal age, gestational duration, and infant birth weight. However, prepregnancy BMI was significantly higher in the GDM group (24.1 ± 3.1 kg/m2) compared to controls (20.7 ± 2.7 kg/m2, P = .01). This finding aligns with previous studies highlighting BMI as a major risk factor for GDM.[4] Interestingly, while weight gain during pregnancy tended to be higher in GDM participants (12.0 ± 5.1 kg vs. 10.4 ± 5.7 kg), the difference did not reach statistical significance (P = .18). This suggests that while prepregnancy BMI may be a stronger predictor of GDM, pregnancy weight gain could still contribute to metabolic stress during gestation.

3.2. Differential gene expression and heatmap analysis

Differential expression analysis identified 1046 genes that were significantly differentially expressed between GDM and control placentas (P < .05) in Table S2. Of these, 610 genes were upregulated, and 436 genes were downregulated in GDM placentas. A heatmap of the differentially expressed genes (DEGs; Fig. 2A) revealed distinct expression profiles between the 2 groups, with clusters of genes associated with structural and metabolic processes being highly upregulated in GDM. Notable upregulated genes included COL22A1, COL8A2, ACTC1, PAX6, PPP2R2C, and G6PC. Conversely, LEPTIN and ERBB2 were significantly downregulated (Fig. 2B).

To validate our transcriptomic data, we performed Western blot analyses focusing on COL8A2, LEPTIN, and ACTC1, which were selected based on their roles in GDM-associated pathways. Western blot results confirmed that COL8A2 expression was significantly increased in GDM placentas, while LEPTIN expression was markedly reduced compared to controls (P < .05 for both; Fig. 2C). ACTC1 showed only a slight increase, which was not statistically significant. In addition, to account for the potential confounding effect of maternal BMI, we performed a subgroup analysis restricted to participants with BMI < 25. The expression patterns of key DEGs, including COL22A1, PAX6, G6PC, ACTA1, LEP, and ERBB2, showed trends consistent with those observed in the overall cohort, supporting the robustness of our findings (Fig. S1).

3.3. Functional pathway analysis

To better understand the biological implications of the observed DEGs, we conducted unsupervised GO analysis. Upregulated pathways in GDM placentas were largely associated with vascular structure development, fibrosis, and muscle contraction. Specific pathways included regulation of vasculogenesis, striated muscle contraction, collagen fibril organization, and smooth muscle contraction (Fig. 3A). Conversely, pathways related to amino acid metabolism and cellular stress responses were significantly downregulated. Key downregulated pathways included the endoplasmic reticulum (ER)-nucleus signaling pathway, L-serine transport, cellular response to unfolded proteins, and glucose import (Fig. 3A). KEGG analysis corroborated the GO findings, highlighting significant disruptions in metabolic and signaling pathways in GDM placentas. Notable pathways included oxidative phosphorylation, glycolysis, lysosome function, and insulin signaling (Fig. 3B). Interestingly, despite significant alterations in many pathways, processes related to placental development and differentiation remained relatively stable between the 2 groups.

3.4. Cellular composition analysis

To further investigate cellular changes, we conducted X-cell analysis to assess inferred cell-type enrichment scores in the placental transcriptomes. GDM placentas showed higher enrichment scores for myocyte signatures and trends toward differences in stromal and immune-related signatures, including fibroblasts, M2 macrophages, and NKT cells, although most changes did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 4A). Similarly, no statistically significant differences were observed in T cell subtype signatures, although minor variations were noted (Fig. 4B). Overall, these findings suggest potential alterations in transcriptomic signatures associated with stromal and immune-related cell types, characterized by a significant increase in myocyte-related enrichment scores, while other observed differences remained modest and largely nonsignificant.

(B) T cell subtype proportions showed no significant differences, although minor alterations were noted. Data are presented as mean ± SD. 4. Discussion

In the present study, 689 upregulated and 356 downregulated DEGs were identified between patients with GDM and controls. These DEGs were strongly associated with key biologicalprocesses, including blood vessel morphogenesis, collagen-containing extracellular matrix organization, muscle development, and cellular component remodeling. Functional analyses highlighted that cell adhesion, muscle contraction, and collagen fibril organization pathways were significantly upregulated, while pathways related to glucose import, insulin response, and amino acid metabolism were downregulated in GDM placentas. Such findings provide important insights into how GDM alters placental structure and function, which may underlie the risk of placenta-associated complications.

GDM is well-documented to increase the risks of macrosomia, prematurity, and preeclampsia through placental dysfunction.[16] Various studies have also linked fibrosis to GDM pathophysiology, further implicating structural abnormalities in the placenta.[17,18] While current GDM management guidelines primarily focus on early diagnosis and glycemic control, recent systemic reviews reveal nuanced outcomes associated with this approach. For instance, while glycemic control reduces the risk of large-for-gestational-age infants, it has little impact on the incidence of small-for-gestational-age births or preeclampsia.[19] Furthermore, early intervention targeting blood glucose levels has also not been shown to reduce pregnancy-related hypertension.[18] These discrepancies underscore the complex interplay between metabolic and vascular changes in GDM, suggesting that glucose dysregulation alone may not fully explain the observed alterations. Our findings indicate that both metabolic and vascular pathways are involved in GDM-related placental changes, but future studies are required to determine whether therapeutic strategies targeting these pathways could translate into improved pregnancy outcomes.

GDM and preexisting diabetes during pregnancy change the structure of the placenta, including increased calcium and fibrin deposits, and are associated with higher incidences of placental infarction, hematoma, and fibrosis.[20,21] Moreover, the GDM placenta decreases blood flow to the fetus, alters vascular development, and impairs arterial remodeling, resulting in decreased placental blood flow.[22] Consistent with a previous study on GDM, we provided evidence in the present study for the link between placental structural variation and GDM through the increased expression of various genes related to blood vessels (COL22A1, COL8A2, PAX6), cytoskeleton (PPP2R2C), and collagen-containing extracellular matrix (COL22A1, COL8A2, G6PC). Moreover, we found that various muscle-related pathways, such as calcium signaling, smooth muscle contraction, muscle tissue development, and calcium-dependent cell-to-cell adhesion signaling, were significantly increased in patients with GDM compared to controls based on GO and KEGG analyses using whole DEG results. Endothelial dysfunction and changes in endothelium-derived factors from the placenta target the vascular smooth muscle and lead to increased vasoconstriction in the placenta during hypertensive pregnancy and preeclampsia.[23] Moreover, changes in uteroplacental remodeling and vascularization decrease placental blood flow and lead to intrauterine growth retardation.[24] GDM is a risk factor for placenta-associated pregnancy complications[20]; however, the vascular mechanisms underlying the detection and management of GDM in pregnancy are not fully understood. Our data suggested that inadequate vascular remodeling and increased collagen deposition could be altered in the placenta of patients with GDM, which may provide potential mechanistic links to placenta-associated complications, although direct outcome data were not evaluated here.

We also found that GDM placentas exhibited downregulation of metabolic or ER stress-related pathways, such as branched-chain amino acid transport, glutamine transport, L-serine transport, ER fold protein response, and response to ER stress, as well as reduced insulin signaling, glucose import, and response to insulin. Pregnancy induces various changes in the endocrine regulation of maternal carbohydrate and lipid metabolism that are essential for elevating circulating blood glucose levels to meet fetal demands.[25] In GDM, placental metabolic function is disrupted, and changes in bioactivity induce alterations in the normal balance of maternal metabolism.[11] The availability of glucose, lipids, and amino acids through cellular organelle communications and their impact on the placenta are known to be altered in maternal obesity or diabetes; however, there are few reports identifying their importance in the GDM placenta by unsupervised analysis based on transcriptomic profiles. In this study, although the participants were not obese and controlled blood glucose well during pregnancy, the metabolic pathways of key substrates, ER stress response signaling, and related genes (LEP and ERBB2) in the placenta were significantly downregulated, suggesting changes in nutrient transport and cellular metabolism in GDM placentas.

Immune regulation is a central component of healthy pregnancy, with maternal–fetal immune tolerance being critical for placental function. Increased numbers of NK cells have been reported to cause cytotrophoblast apoptosis, increased collagen deposition, and maternal intolerance, leading to defective placentation and placenta related complication.[26] Consistent with these findings, our study observed a tendency for increased NK cell and M2 macrophage populations in GDM placentas, as revealed by X-cell analysis. These immune alterations likely contribute to the pro-inflammatory and fibrotic environment in GDM placentas, further compounding structural and functional disruptions. We also examined the proportions of T cell subtypes and observed a trend of reduced naive T cells in GDM placentas. Additionally, myocyte proportions were significantly increased, and fibroblast proportions showed an upward trend (P = .18), while epithelial cell proportions showed a downward trend (P = .086). These cellular changes are consistent with the fibrosis-related signals identified in this study within the GDM placental environment.

This study has several limitations. First, the RNA-seq-based deconvolution method used to assess immune cell proportions may not fully capture the complexity of the placental immune microenvironment, necessitating future validation through flow cytometry or other advanced techniques. Second, while transcriptomic analyses identified significant molecular and cellular alterations, functional validation of these pathways was not performed and remains an area for further study. Third, the relatively small sample size may limit the generalizability of the findings, particularly to diverse populations with varying clinical characteristics, such as those with obesity or glycemic status. However, it was not possible to obtain baseline glycemic information for all participants or to directly compare glucose levels between the groups, and additional meaningful associations with placental changes might have been revealed if such data were available.

Despite neonatal birth weights showing no significant differences between GDM and control groups, molecular-level differences remain profound. These findings emphasize the importance of understanding GDM as a heterogeneous disease, especially given the ongoing controversies surrounding its diagnostic criteria.[27] Our findings underscore the multifaceted nature of GDM, highlighting its impact on molecular signatures associated with placental composition, metabolism, ER stress, and immune cell dynamics. Importantly, while we demonstrate extensive vascular and metabolic remodeling in GDM placentas, further studies incorporating clinical outcomes are required to establish their relevance for pregnancy complications.

5. Conclusion

This study emphasizes the importance of a holistic approach to understanding GDM, focusing not only on glycemic control but also on addressing vascular and metabolic disruptions. Our findings suggest that these pathways may represent potential targets for further investigation, rather than definitive therapeutic strategies. Further research with larger, well-characterized cohorts is warranted to validate these transcriptomic signatures and to clarify their clinical significance in GDM. Such studies will be essential for developing interventions that could ultimately improve maternal and fetal outcomes in pregnancies complicated by GDM.
