Optimized Construction of a Yeast SICLOPPS Library for Unbiased In Vivo Selection of Cyclic Peptides
Authors: Nanna Birkmose, Emilie U. Frydendahl, Charlotte R. Knudsen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
DNA-encoded libraries hold great potential for discovering small, cyclized peptides with drug potential. Split-intein circular ligation of peptides and proteins (SICLOPPS) is a well-established method for in vivo selection of cyclic peptides targeting specific intracellular components. However, the method has mainly been used in prokaryotic cells. In contrast, selection studies performed directly in eukaryotic cells allow for the identification of cyclic peptides promoting a functional outcome, without the need to define a specific cellular target. Here, we report the construction of a Saccharomyces cerevisiae-specific SICLOPPS library of 80 million members, via careful optimization of several steps to increase the size of the library. Individual library members were shown to be correctly expressed and processed in yeast. High-throughput sequencing was conducted on the randomized primer used for library construction and the pure yeast SICLOPPS library isolated from Escherichia coli. A distinct guanine insertion bias was observed in the peptide-encoding, randomized sequence, which was primarily attributed to the degenerate primer used to introduce the randomized sequence. Moreover, high-throughput sequencing was performed on the library before and after the induction of cyclic peptide expression in yeast. Importantly, expression of the SICLOPPS library in S. cerevisiae caused only a marginal further sequence bias. Our work paves the way for selection studies using a large and diverse library to identify cyclic peptides of therapeutic interest that promote a specific phenotypic outcome in eukaryotic organisms, with yeast representing a beneficial model system due to its high transformation efficiency.

Introduction

Small, circularized peptides hold great therapeutic potential.1,2 Given their large size relative to small-molecule drugs, cyclic peptides are especially suited to target macromolecular structures such as protein–protein or protein–DNA/RNA interactions within a cellular complex.1,3 Moreover, cyclic peptides typically display low cellular toxicity, are more resistant to proteolysis than linear peptides and are relatively easy to synthesize and modify.1,2 While almost 60 cyclic peptides are approved for clinical use by the FDA,2 focus on the development of cyclic peptide therapeutics has mainly been on derivatives of naturally occurring products.1,2,4,5 However, several technologies for de novo cyclic peptide drug development have been employed in recent years to identify novel candidate drugs using very large (typically up to 109), genetically encoded cyclic peptide libraries.2,6 Examples of these technologies include phage display and mRNA display, where candidate cyclic peptides from the generated DNA/RNA-encoded library are discovered through several rounds of affinity-based selection rounds.3,7−9 For instance, the mRNA display-based Random nonstandard Peptides Integrated Discovery (RaPID) technology has led to the identification of cyclic peptide binders of therapeutically relevant targets, with the identified peptides consisting of eight to 15 variable residues and being cyclized through a thioether bond.7,10,11

Split-intein circular ligation of peptides and proteins (SICLOPPS)12 is an alternative strategy to produce genetically encoded, cyclic peptide libraries. The intracellular nature of SICLOPPS paves the way for in vivo selection of cyclic peptides aiming at cellular targets based on function, as opposed to affinity-based selections in vitro.6,7,9 SICLOPPS is based on the natural phenomenon of protein splicing facilitated by split intein domains.13,14 By reversing the order of the C-terminal and N-terminal inteins flanking the target sequence, a head-to-tail cyclization reaction occurs spontaneously after transcription and translation.12,15 Thus, SICLOPPS-derived peptides are cyclized through a peptide bond.3 A SICLOPPS library is a collection of plasmids, each containing a nucleotide sequence encoding a unique cyclic peptide. SICLOPPS libraries are constructed by molecular cloning techniques, using PCR amplification to introduce the randomized cyclic peptide-encoding sequence between the inteins with a degenerate library primer followed by restriction enzyme digestion, ligation and amplification in Escherichia coli. The library is obtained by purifying plasmid DNA from the pooled colonies of transformed cells, each expressing a library member,16 and can then be introduced in any given host cell.17−20 Since the randomized sequence is introduced by a primer, the exact composition of the cyclic peptide library is controlled at the DNA level. While SICLOPPS allows for the cyclization of peptides or proteins of almost any size,21 most SICLOPPS libraries encode cyclic peptides containing six to eight variable amino acids.20,22,23

SICLOPPS has been widely used for generating cyclic peptide libraries for early stage drug discovery.6 Selection studies using SICLOPPS libraries are often performed in E. coli, searching for cyclic peptides that disrupt the formation of a specific homo- or heterodimeric protein complex.17,19,23−25 The system can be used for selecting inhibitors of human protein complexes,23−25 and several studies have further validated and investigated the effect of the identified cyclic peptides in human cell lines26−28 and mice.27 However, few examples exist of selection studies on SICLOPPS libraries performed directly in eukaryotic cells,18,20,29−31 a strategy highly relevant if searching for cyclic peptides promoting a certain functional outcome in eukaryotic cells without specifying the cellular target. Saccharomyces cerevisiae holds great advantages as a model for in vivo selection studies due to their ease of handling in the laboratory, the ability to routinely and robustly achieve a very high number of cells transformed with plasmid DNA and well-established methodologies for genomic manipulation. Of note, Kritzer et al. used a SICLOPPS library to identify eight-residue cyclic peptides that could reduce α-synuclein toxicity in an S. cerevisiae Parkinson’s disease model.20 Two lead cyclic peptides were further investigated and validated to prevent neurodegeneration in a Caenorhabditis elegans Parkinson’s disease animal model.20

Genetically encoded libraries should be as large and complex as possible to increase the chance of identifying members with the desired binding properties or functionality3,32−34 and are typically the result of a highly optimized library construction process. As for SICLOPPS libraries, phage display libraries2 are constructed with the introduction of a randomized peptide-encoding sequence by a degenerate primer in PCR reactions, and a number of reports have described efficient construction protocols of phage display libraries.32−34 Specifically, the intermolecular ligation of two DNA fragments can be challenging to achieve with high efficiency,16,33−35 and an optimized transformation protocol using highly competent cells is needed.16,34,36 Another important factor is to ensure that the library has high complexity and encodes a diverse and balanced pool of library members. This goal can be compromised, if the encoding DNA sequences within the library carry a base composition bias, thereby leading to a reduction in the pool of available library members as well as an intrinsic overrepresentation of certain library members during selection.37−43 Several studies have investigated propagation biases concerning the construction and selection of phage display libraries. Here, phages displaying certain peptides lead to growth advantages of the individual E. coli cells upon propagation, thus leading to an overrepresentation of some library members.38,39,41,44−46 With high-throughput sequencing methods being readily available today, compositional bias in genetically encoded libraries is easily investigated.33,37,41−43,47 Base composition bias has been reported and described in several systems, including phage display,39,41,42 systemic evolution of ligands by exponential enrichment (SELEX),47 and adeno-associated viral display libraries.37 Yet, a similar analysis has not been reported for SICLOPPS libraries to the best of our knowledge.

Here, we report the optimized construction and characterization of a large, yeast-compatible SICLOPPS library encoding nearly 80 million cyclic peptides. Various factors were found to greatly increase library size during construction. The library was shown to be correctly encoded, expressed and processed to produce cyclic peptides in S. cerevisiae. High-throughput sequencing of the SICLOPPS library revealed a diverse library despite a distinct guanine insertion bias, which was found to be mainly attributed to the used library primer that inserts the randomized sequence during library construction. Moreover, sequencing of the library after introduction in S. cerevisiae and induction of cyclic peptide expression for potential selection studies revealed only minor changes in library composition, allowing for unbiased selection of the initially generated SICLOPPS library.

Materials and Methods

Preparation of a Yeast Library Vector by Cloning of Inteins

The yeast SICLOPPS library was constructed using the pESC-LEU yeast epitope tagging vector (Agilent Technologies #217452). The unique NheI restriction enzyme cleavage site present in pESC-LEU was removed using the Quikchange Lightning Site-Directed Mutagenesis Kit (Agilent Technologies) and mutagenic primers (Table S1). The gene fragments encoding the DnaE C-terminal and N-terminal split inteins derived from Synechocystis sp. PCC680314 were amplified from an existing SICLOPPS library16 and cloned into the vector using standard cloning procedures (see Table S1 for primers used). The N-terminal split intein was inserted in-frame with the c-myc epitope tag (EQKLISEEDL), already present in pESC-LEU, resulting in the C-terminal tagging of intein N. The integrity of the intein-containing plasmid, termed pESC-LEU-SICLOPPS, was verified by Sanger sequencing (Eurofins Genomics TubeSeq Service).

Preparation of Electrocompetent Escherichia coli SIG10 Cells

Electrocompetent E. coli SIG10 cells (F- mcrA Δ(mrr-hsdRMS-mcrBC) endA1 recA1 Φ80dlacZΔM15 ΔlacX74 araD139 Δ(ara,leu)7697galU galK rpsL nupG λ- tonA (StrR)) (Sigma-Aldrich) were prepared by inoculating a bacterial colony grown on LB agar added 100 μg/mL streptomycin in 10 mL LB and incubating at 37 °C with shaking overnight. The overnight culture was diluted 1:100 in LB and incubated at 37 °C with shaking until reaching an OD600 of 0.7–0.8, then chilled for 20 min in an ice water bath. The cells were kept on ice for the remainder of the procedure, and all containers, solutions and centrifuges were prechilled. Cells were centrifuged for 20 min at 3500g and 4 °C, and the supernatant was removed by pouring. The cell pellet was carefully resuspended in 900 mL sterile H2O. Centrifugation, removal of supernatant and resuspension were repeated twice, the first time using 500 mL and the second time using 50 mL of sterile H2O. The cells were transferred to a 50 mL tube, centrifuged for 20 min at 2900g and 4 °C, the supernatant was removed, and cells were carefully resuspended in 15 mL sterile 10% glycerol. Following repeated centrifugation and removal of the supernatant, cells were finally resuspended in 2 mL sterile 10% glycerol. 1.5 mL tubes containing 50 μL aliquots of the cells were frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −70 °C. The transformation efficiency of the prepared electrocompetent SIG10 cells was determined to be 4.12 × 109 colony-forming units (cfu)/μg DNA using 1 ng of supercoiled pESC-LEU-SICLOPPS plasmid DNA and 25 μL competent cells.

Yeast SICLOPPS Library Construction

The following describes our optimized protocol for the construction of a SICLOPPS library in an S. cerevisiae expression vector. The observations and developments leading to the protocol are described in the Results section. A graphical representation of the library construction is shown in Figure S1. The degenerate library primer used for library construction was synthesized by standard phosphoramidite chemistry by Eurofins. The constructed pESC-LEU-SICLOPPS vector was used as a DNA template in five separate PCR reactions (1 × (95 °C 5 min, 65 °C 2 min, 72 °C 1.5 min), 30 × (95 °C 1 min, 65 °C 1 min, 72 °C 1.5 min), 1 × (72 °C 10 min)). The library primer and a reverse primer (Table S1) were used to amplify DNA fragments containing part of the intein segments while incorporating the cyclic peptide-encoding sequences TGC-(NNS)5 between the inteins. The PCR reactions were pooled and purified (QIAquick PCR and Gel Cleanup Kit (Qiagen)). The resulting DNA fragment was used as a template for setting up five separate “zipper” PCR reactions to minimize mismatches in the randomized region,16 using the same program (except applying 25 instead of 30 cycles), a zipper primer, and a reverse primer (Table S1). DNA from the five reactions was purified, pooled, and the correctly sized fragment was extracted from an agarose gel and purified (QIAquick PCR and Gel Cleanup Kit (Qiagen)). Both vector (8.2 kb) and insert (431 bp) were digested with restriction enzymes NheI (restriction site naturally present in C-terminal intein) and SalI (both from Thermo Scientific) in several reactions according to the manufacturer’s instructions, except extending the incubation times to 2 h for the PCR fragment and overnight for the vector. Subsequently, the enzymes were heat inactivated. The vector DNA, extracted from an agarose gel, and the PCR fragment were both purified using the QIAquick kit. A ligation reaction was made using 1 U T4 DNA ligase (Thermo Scientific), 300 ng digested pESC-LEU-SICLOPPS, and a four times molar excess of the PCR fragment (64 ng) in a 20 μL reaction. A vector-only negative control ligation was included. Both reactions were incubated overnight at room temperature, followed by heat inactivation of the ligase and purification of the reactions by drop dialysis16 using a 0.025 μm pore size membrane filter (Millipore). Four separate library transformations were conducted using electroporation with 3 μL ligation reaction and 50 μL competent E. coli SIG10 cells. After electroporation, 950 μL SOC medium was quickly added to each reaction, and the cells recovered for 1 h at 37 °C with shaking. Each transformation was plated on large (245 × 245 mm) bioassay dishes of LB agar added 100 μg/mL ampicillin, with 100 μL cells being removed for 10-fold serial dilutions on smaller plates. The plates were incubated for 16–20 h at 37 °C, and library size was determined by counting colonies on dilution plates. The fraction of self-ligated and nondigested vectors without insert in the library was estimated by counting colonies resulting from a transformation of the negative control ligation.16 Colonies on library plates were collected by scraping them off into LB medium, and library plasmids were separately purified from each pool of cells by a Nucleobond midiprep kit (Macherey-Nagel) and combined to obtain the final SICLOPPS library. Colonies picked at random from dilution plates were used to purify individual library members, and sequences were determined by Sanger sequencing.

Validation of Intein-Mediated Peptide Splicing by Western Blotting

Random individual library members from the constructed SICLOPPS library were transformed into S. cerevisiae IS110-18A cells ([PSI]−MATa aro7-1 leu2-2 ilv1-2 his4-166 lys2-101 met8-1 trp5-48 ura4-1)48 using standard lithium acetate heat shock.49 Picked colonies of transformed cells were grown to stationary phase at 30 °C with shaking in synthetic defined (SD) minimal medium lacking leucine (−Leu) to select for transformants and supplemented with 2% galactose to induce expression of cyclic peptides. Cultures were chilled in an ice water bath, pelleted by centrifugation, and washed in ice-cold H2O. Cell pellets were frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −70 °C until lysed. Cells were lysed by resuspension in a lysis buffer followed by rapid freeze–thawing.50 Aliquots of lysed cells were used for Western blotting with primary antibody (mouse anti-c-myc (9E10) (Sigma-Aldrich #M4439)) diluted 1:1000 and secondary antibody (HRP-goat antimouse (Dako)) diluted 1:5000 using standard procedures.

High-Throughput Sequencing of Library Primer and SICLOPPS Library

Amplicons containing the randomized cyclic peptide-encoding sequences were generated in PCR reactions. Primers amplifying the degenerate library primer (Table S1) were used to generate a 104 bp fragment, with the primers adding 20 extra bases in both the 5′- and 3′-end to make the fragment sufficiently long for sequencing. Amplicons from the SICLOPPS plasmid library were generated using a different set of primers (Table S1) amplifying a 199 bp region likewise containing the randomized sequence. The following PCR program was applied: 1 × (95 °C 3 min, 58 °C 2 min, 72 °C 1.5 min), 30 × (95 °C 1 min, 58 °C 1 min, 72 °C 1.5 min), 1 × (72 °C 10 min). The reactions were purified (QIAquick PCR and Gel Cleanup Kit (Qiagen)) and verified with respect to size and specificity on an agarose gel. Samples were sent for the NGSelect Amplicon Sequencing service performed by Eurofins, with the inclusion of a preliminary adapter ligation step and merging of read pairs. Data analysis on sequencing results was performed in R using RStudio51,52 and using R packages Biostrings53 and ShortRead.54 Briefly, reads were filtered to obtain reads that (1) contain a sequence corresponding to the 3′-end of the C-terminal intein and a cysteine codon (CACAATTGC), (2) contain a sequence corresponding to the 5′-end of the N-terminal intein (TGCCTCAGT), (3) contain a sequence corresponding to the expected length of the cyclic peptide-encoding sequence (15 bp) between the two intein-defining sequences and (4) that this sequence between the inteins is correctly encoded ((NNS)5; N = A, T, G, C and S = G, C). Filtered reads were used to obtain randomized cyclic peptide-encoding sequences for downstream analysis, while the identity of reads not passing filtering criteria was also investigated.

Introduction and Characterization of the SICLOPPS Library in Saccharomyces cerevisiae

S. cerevisiae IS110-18A cells were transformed with the generated SICLOPPS library using an upscaled lithium acetate heat shock protocol based on20,55. The transformation was conducted using 330 mL of exponential-phase culture in YPD (OD600 0.4–0.6) and 20 μg of SICLOPPS library plasmid DNA. Moreover, after heat shocking, the cells were allowed to recover in 10 mL YPD for 1 h at 30 °C with shaking and washed in sterile TE buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, 1 mM EDTA, pH 7.5). The transformed cells were resuspended in 2 mL TE buffer and plated on a large bioassay dish of SD/–Leu supplemented with 2% glucose, with 100 μL being removed for 10-fold serial dilutions on smaller plates. The plates were incubated for 3 days at 30 °C. Colonies on the large plate were collected by scraping them off into TE buffer, and the number of transformants was estimated by counting colonies on dilution plates. The collected, transformed cells were washed in TE buffer, pelleted, resuspended in YPD, mixed with an equal volume of 50% glycerol and frozen in aliquots at −70 °C. To obtain transformants for DNA extraction and subsequent sequencing, a frozen aliquot of transformed cells was washed in sterile H2O and plated on a large bioassay dish of SD/–Leu supplemented with 2% glucose. A volume of cells calculated to yield approximately 3-fold more colonies than the expected number of transformed cells was used to ensure a representation of all transformants. Cells were collected, washed in H2O and replated on a large bioassay dish of SD/–Leu supplemented with 2% galactose to obtain approximately the same number of colonies as on the glucose plate. The remaining cells were pelleted and frozen at −20 °C (cells containing uninduced library). After 3 days of incubation at 30 °C, replated cells were collected, washed in H2O, pelleted and frozen at −20 °C (cells containing induced library). A fraction of both uninduced and induced cells were lysed and subjected to Western blotting as described above, to examine the leakiness of the GAL1 promoter. Total DNA from both samples (uninduced and induced) was extracted using a yeast DNA extraction kit (Thermo Scientific) and used as DNA templates in PCR reactions to obtain amplicons from the SICLOPPS library containing the randomized, cyclic peptide-encoding sequences as described in the section above. Sample preparation, sequencing, and data analysis were performed as described above.

Results

Optimized Construction of a Large Yeast SICLOPPS Library

We sought to construct a DNA-encoded SICLOPPS library of small, cyclic peptides with the prospect of performing large-scale selection studies in S. cerevisiae. The library was constructed in the pESC-LEU yeast expression vector, allowing for control of intein-peptide expression by a strong galactose-inducible GAL1 promotor and selection for transformed cells in yeast using the LEU2 marker. The DnaE intein domains derived from Synechocystis sp. PCC680314 were cloned into pESC-LEU to add an in-frame c-myc tag to the C-terminus of intein N to allow library expression and splicing to be followed by Western blotting (Figure S1). Upon expression of the intein-peptide fusion proteins and intein-mediated protein splicing,14 the library consists of six-residue cyclic peptides containing a cysteine to enable splicing12 and five randomized amino acids encoded as NNS (Figure S1). N denotes any of the four nucleotides, while S denotes the occurrence of a G or C at the third codon position. NNS codons encode all 20 canonical amino acids with a more even representation of amino acids and elimination of two of the three stop codons (TAA and TGA) from the randomized sequence.6,34,56 Five randomized positions result in a library size representing a large number of possible cyclic peptide variants. Yet, the library will still be of a size allowing all possible combinations to be included within a typical SICLOPPS library.3 Specifically, each of the five NNS codons can encode one of 32 codons, yielding a total number of possible nucleotide sequences in the SICLOPPS library of 325 = 3.36 × 107 variants. Moreover, keeping the cyclic peptides relatively small makes them easier to develop into more drug-like molecules with improved pharmacokinetic and -dynamic properties, while still being large enough to carry structural motifs for target binding.5

The library was constructed based on a published protocol, where the ligated plasmid library is amplified by transformation into E. coli and followed by plasmid DNA purification from bacteria.16 However, initial attempts resulted in very small libraries, with only a few hundred library members as estimated by counting colony-forming units (cfu) of transformed bacterial cells from serial dilutions. This initial failure may be attributed to the use of a relatively large eukaryotic vector for library construction (see Discussion).

Early attempts to increase library size included the improvement of the conditions for restriction enzyme digestion, testing DNA purification methods, and refining a protocol to generate highly competent E. coli cells for electroporation of ligation reactions (see Materials and Methods). Moreover, after ligation of the intein-containing vector and the insert containing the randomized sequence, the reactions need to be purified as the conductivities of the solutions of ligated DNA must be low for electroporation. Remarkably, switching from purification by silica spin columns to drop dialysis16 resulted in an almost 100-fold increase in library size (Figure S2A).

During the process of testing reaction conditions for restriction enzyme digestion and ligation, the DNA insert containing the randomized sequence showed a tendency to ligate with itself in ligation reactions (Figure 1A), thus preventing correct ligation with the vector. Before that observation, the linearized vector was treated with alkaline phosphatase to minimize self-ligation of the vector, as is common in cloning-based library construction protocols.16,32,57 In an alternative approach, the insert was treated with alkaline phosphatase in an attempt to increase correctly ligated plasmids by preventing multimerization of the insert. As a control, a ligation reaction with DNA where neither vector nor insert was dephosphorylated was included. Surprisingly, complete omission of the phosphatase treatment increased the library size by almost 40-fold (Figure 1B). At the same time, the background of self-ligated library plasmids was increased by about 40%, as estimated by counting transformants from a negative control vector-only ligation reaction. However, this was judged to be acceptable given the very large increase in total library size. Note that this approach is only feasible, since two different restriction enzymes with incompatible overhangs are used in our protocol. Furthermore, the DNA fragment that is excised between the two restriction sites in the vector is removed by agarose gel purification to prevent reinsertion. Further work to increase library size included investigating the importance of the total DNA amounts used as well as the molar ratio between the linearized intein-containing vector and the insert containing the randomized sequence in ligation reactions. The library size increased linearly with increasing vector DNA amount (Figure S2B), and a four times molar excess of the insert in ligation reactions was found to produce the largest libraries (Figure 1C,D).

During library construction, random colonies from agar plates of serially diluted E. coli library transformants were picked to investigate the composition of the library. Plasmid DNA was extracted and Sanger sequenced to obtain the DNA sequences ((NNS)5) encoding the randomized amino acids in the cyclic peptides. This revealed that up to 25% of library members initially encoded large detrimental deletions in the intein-peptide fusion proteins (data not shown). This problem was resolved by including an extra agarose gel purification step of the insert carrying the randomized sequence to obtain correctly sized DNA inserts for ligation reactions.

Finally, all factors found to increase library size were combined in a single experiment while also increasing DNA amounts and volumes of ligation reactions used for transformation as well as the volume of competent E. coli cells. As a result, ≈20 million transformants were obtained in a single transformation, which was higher than anticipated considering the relative contributions from optimizations and up-scaling. Purified plasmid DNA from four individual transformations was combined (Table S2) to produce a SICLOPPS library of 8.04 × 107 members. 1.00% of this library is estimated to be vectors without a cyclic peptide-encoding insert (Table S2), and thus, the final SICLOPPS library consists of 7.96 × 107 members each encoding a randomized cyclic peptide. This number exceeds the theoretical number of nucleotide sequences encoded by the library (3.36 × 107), ensuring good coverage of all potential library members.

The Constructed SICLOPPS Library is Encoded as Expected and Correctly Processed in S. cerevisiae

To investigate the coding potential of the final constructed yeast SICLOPPS library, 20 cyclic peptide-encoding randomized sequences of library members from randomly picked bacterial colonies were obtained by Sanger sequencing (Table 1). Initially, 44% of the sequenced plasmids had two peaks in the sequencing chromatogram at several positions of the randomized region (Figure S3A). Repeated transformation of the isolated library plasmid followed by Sanger sequencing of plasmids derived from multiple transformants resulted in the successful isolation of two distinct randomized sequences for all investigated plasmids with double peaks (Figure S3B). Since 44% of the picked colonies in the final SICLOPPS library expressed two distinct library members, the final SICLOPPS library is likely even larger than estimated, as cfu counts were used to estimate the library size. It is likely that this observation arises due to multiple transformants expanding in very close proximity on the growth medium, giving them the appearance of a single colony. Of the 20 randomly picked library members (Table 1), one contained a large deletion and did therefore not encode any peptide. Another picked library member was a self-ligated or nondigested vector without any insertion of randomized sequence. Three library members contained a TAG stop codon in the cyclic peptide sequence resulting in nonsplicing proteins (Table 1).

The randomized cyclic peptide-encoding sequences were determined by the isolation of library member plasmid DNA followed by Sanger sequencing. The corresponding peptide sequences were deduced from the nucleotide sequences. Functionality assessments are based solely on the sequences presented in the table.

The sequences obtained by Sanger sequencing were analyzed to determine, if the randomized sequence was encoded as expected (Figure 2A). Assuming complete randomization, each NNS codon should have 25% of each nucleotide at the first and second positions of the codon, while the third codon position is expected to have 50% of either C or G. A base-insertion frequency was obtained for each of the three codon positions based on the 18 library members encoding a cyclic peptide (Table 1). Overall, the sequences were encoded as predicted (Figure 2B). However, a distinct preference for G insertion was observed. This was especially pronounced at the third codon position, where G was present in 67.8% of codons instead of the expected 50% (Figure 2B). During library generation, inserts containing randomized sequences are amplified in PCR reactions using the intein-containing vector as the DNA template and a randomized library primer.16 Initially, the DNA sequence between the inteins (GGGCCC), originating from the pESC-LEU vector backbone, was suspected to cause preferential annealing of specific sequences contained in the randomized library primer during the construction of the library, thereby causing the G insertion bias. However, the construction of a similar SICLOPPS library employing the same library primer, but using the S. cerevisiae pSF-TEFI-LEU2 expression vector containing a different backbone sequence (ATAGCCTCGAG), resulted in a similar bias (Figure S4).

Next, the expression and splicing of the intein peptide-encoding constructs in S. cerevisiae were evaluated, as the generated SICLOPPS library is intended for in vivo selection studies in yeast. The expression of the cyclic peptides can be inferred by detecting the expression and processing of the ≈20 kDa intein-peptide,16,20,58 as visualized by Western blotting using an antibody against the C-terminal c-myc tag on intein N. (Figure 2A). Splicing of the fusion protein to produce a cyclized peptide releases the ≈4 kDa intein C from the peptide-intein N portion, thereby resulting in a size reduction (Figure 2A). The S. cerevisiae IS110–18A strain48 was transformed with individual library members (Table 1), cultured in a galactose-containing medium to induce expression and lysates of cell cultures grown to stationary phase were studied by Western blotting using an anti-c-myc antibody (Figure 2C; unprocessed blots in Figure S5). As anticipated, five library members (no. 2, 3, 7, 15 and 19) expressed no detectable protein due to either deletions, lack of insert, or stop codons in the cyclic peptide-encoding sequence (Table 1 and Figure 2C). For most of the remaining library members, the unprocessed intein-peptide was highly expressed (Figure 2C, upper band). Moreover, most library members also displayed splicing activity, although at varying efficiencies (Figure 2C, lower band). Only a single library member, number 18, exhibited no detectable splicing (Figure 2C). In contrast, no expression of the library could be detected in lysates of E. coli cells transformed with the entire library (Figure S6), despite recent indications of yeast GAL1 promoter-driven expression in E. coli.59

High-Throughput Sequencing Reveals a Base Composition Bias in the SICLOPPS Library Mainly Originating from the Degenerate Library Primer

Given the large size of the constructed yeast SICLOPPS library (7.96 × 107), the composition deduced by Sanger sequencing of a few randomly selected library members (Figure 2B) may not be representative of the entire library.38,60 Therefore, the distribution of randomized sequences in the library was studied by Illumina-based high-throughput sequencing of small PCR amplicons derived from library-member plasmid DNA, each containing a cyclic peptide-encoding sequence. Moreover, amplicons derived from the small, partially degenerate oligonucleotide used as the library primer inserting the randomized sequences during library construction were included in the sequencing analysis. This was done to investigate the origin of the observed nucleotide bias (Figure 2B) that could potentially arise from the used degenerate primer.41,42

Sequencing resulted in 104 bp (library primer) or 199 bp (SICLOPPS library) reads all spanning the randomized cyclic peptide-encoding sequence flanked by parts of the intein domains (Figure S7). A total of 4.65 × 106 reads for the library primer and 6.04 × 106 reads for the SICLOPPS library was obtained (Table 2). For the library, 1.75% of these represented reads were derived from vectors with no insertion of the randomized sequence (Table 2). This value is in line with, albeit slightly higher than, the value estimated by counting cfu obtained after the transformation of a self-ligation control reaction (1.00%). Filtering for reads derived from correctly encoded library members was performed, thereby identifying reads with randomized cyclic peptide-encoding sequences placed correctly between the inteins, and of the correct length (15 bp) and composition ((NNS)5). This filtering resulted in 94.1% correct sequences for the oligonucleotide primer and 91.1% correct sequences for the SICLOPPS library (Table 2). For the library, almost two-thirds of the ≈430,000 incorrect sequences (64.0%) were the result of discrepancies of the sequences defining the ends of the inteins from the expected sequence, mostly due to single-base mismatches. The remaining incorrect sequences in the library included library members with randomized sequences of a wrong length (22.8%) or not matching the NNS coding scheme (9.76%), while a small number (3.44%) were of low sequencing quality and contained widespread mismatches and indels (data not shown).

Sequencing was performed on four different samples: the randomized library primer used to construct the library, the constructed SICLOPPS library purified from Escherichia coli, the library after introduction in Saccharomyces cerevisiae (uninduced) and the library after introduction and cyclic peptide expression in S. cerevisiae (induced). Listed are the total number of obtained reads for each sequencing reaction and the number of these reads corresponding to library members without any cyclic peptide-encoding insert. Filtering for correctly encoded reads was conducted. The frequency among filtered, correct sequences of library members with one or several stop codons in the cyclic peptide-encoding sequences, as well as the number of filtered sequences being unique, are listed. The nucleotide sequences were translated, and the percentage of all possible cyclic peptide combinations being present in each sequencing reaction was calculated.

Next, an in-depth analysis of cyclic peptide-encoding sequences from the filtered reads was performed. For the constructed SICLOPPS library, 15.1% of library members contained a stop codon in the cyclic peptide-encoding sequence and are therefore not predicted to be functional, as supported by the Western analysis of splicing efficiency (Figure 2C). This frequency is similar to results obtained by Sanger sequencing (Table 1) and is close to the predicted frequency of 14.7%, if the NNS codons were completely randomized.56 The percentage of unique cyclic peptide-encoding sequences constituted 81.7% of reads for the library primer, whereas this number was lowered to 64.8% in the constructed library. In line with this, the most frequently occurring randomized sequences were present 11 times for the oligonucleotide and 29 times for the library. This indicates that a loss of library complexity occurs during the SICLOPPS library construction—most likely as a result of the amplification of certain sequences during PCR and replication in E. coli.41 Translation of the randomized nucleotide sequences resulted in 1.69 and 1.56 million unique peptides encoded by the oligonucleotide primer and the constructed library, respectively. Since each of the five NNS codons can encode one of the 20 canonical amino acids or a TAG stop codon, the theoretical number of peptide sequences within the library is 215 = 4.08 × 106 peptides. Thus, around 40% of all possible peptide combinations are contained in these sequencing results (Table 2). Given the sequencing depth of the library (∼6 × 106) and the estimated total number of library members (∼8 × 107), all or nearly all cyclic peptides are likely present in the library.

The filtered, cyclic peptide-encoding sequences were also used to further investigate the G insertion bias already observed by Sanger sequencing (Figure 2B). Strikingly, a distinct base composition bias was evident already in the library primer (Figure 3A). For the first and second bases of randomized codons, G and T were slightly overrepresented, whereas a G was located at the third position for 59.9% of sequences. The frequencies of nucleotides in the constructed library were very similar, however with an additional increase in G at all positions (Figure 3B). This was once more especially pronounced at the third codon position with 63.7% guanine. Thus, the primary source of the observed bias in the constructed yeast SICLOPPS library (Figures 2B and Figure 3B) is the used degenerate primer, which turned out not to be completely randomized (Figure 3A), while a small additional bias arises during library construction.

Not surprisingly, guanine-rich codons such as GGG and GTG were overrepresented both in the library primer and in the constructed library (Figure 3C,D). Codon biases were slightly more pronounced in the constructed library (compare Figure 3D,C), with a pattern of the first, third and fifth codon positions being similar, and the second and fourth codon positions being similar in the randomized cyclic peptide. Of note, codons are represented almost evenly across all five positions in the constructed library (Figure 3D) despite a bias in their overall frequency (Figure 3D).

Likewise, amino acid frequencies deviated from the expected, with the predicted frequencies varying for the amino acids due to a different number of codons encoding each amino acid (Figure 3E,F). Frequencies of encoded amino acids in the degenerate primer and in the constructed library followed similar patterns (compare Figure 3E,F), again underlining the contribution of the randomized primer to the observed bias in the constructed library. Glycine, tryptophan and valine are examples of overrepresented amino acids, whereas proline, threonine and serine are examples of underrepresented amino acids (Figure 3F). No major differences were observed for the amino acid distribution across the five positions in the cyclic peptides (Figure S8C,D).

Induction of SICLOPPS Library in S. cerevisiae Causes Minor Biases in the Representation of the Initial, Unselected Library

In phage display, it is well described how the propagation of the library in E. coli can lead to an over- or underrepresentation of certain library members, respectively, due to specific peptides causing growth advantages or disadvantages unrelated to the selection performed.39,40,44,45 To investigate if a similar propagation-related bias was present for the constructed library upon introduction in yeast, high-throughput sequencing of the library purified from S. cerevisiae cells before and after galactose induction was performed. The SICLOPPS library was introduced in yeast IS110-18A48 cells by large-scale transformation.20,55 The number of individual transformants each encoding a library member, as determined by cfu counts, was estimated to be 4.30 × 106. The cells were harvested and frozen.

To obtain amplicons containing the cyclic peptide-encoding sequences, a frozen aliquot of yeast cells transformed with the constructed SICLOPPS library was plated on SD/–Leu supplemented with glucose. To ensure the representation of all transformants, a calculated number of cells were plated to obtain approximately 3-fold as many colonies as the estimated number of transformants. After pooling of cells from the resulting colonies, total DNA was extracted to represent the uninduced state, while a fraction of the cells were replated and further incubated on SD/–Leu with galactose, to obtain a similar number of colonies as on the glucose agar plate. Cells were harvested, and total DNA was extracted to represent the induced state. Western blotting using lysates of harvested cells confirmed no detectable expression of the SICLOPPS library before induction (Figure S9). The two DNA preparations were used as templates in PCR reactions to produce amplicons analogous to those used to sequence the pure SICLOPPS library. Illumina-based high-throughput sequencing resulted in a similar total number of reads as compared to sequencing of the pure SICLOPPS library (Table 2). Filtering for correct reads identical to the filtering applied to reads obtained upon sequencing of the library primer and library purified from E. coli (see previous Results section) resulted in a slightly lower fraction of correct reads (Table 2). The fraction of self-ligated and nondigested vectors without insert in the library remained largely the same for the uninduced library (1.75% vs 1.59%), whereas the fraction was increased to 2.30% in the induced library (Table 2). Furthermore, the fraction of cyclic peptides containing stop codons was nearly identical without induction, but increased from 15.1% to 21.3% upon induction (Table 2).

Whereas 64.8% of filtered sequences in the pure SICLOPPS library were unique, this percentage decreased to 10.1% and 11.4% for the sequencing data sets derived from the uninduced and induced libraries in yeast, respectively. (Table 2). This low number of identified unique library members represented after introduction in yeast is likely due to challenges in yeast DNA extraction (see Discussion). Despite this decrease, over half a million unique sequences were represented in each data set (Table 2), thus ensuring sufficient statistical coverage of the library to investigate potential sequence biases.

Filtered randomized sequences encoding the cyclic peptide library members expressed in yeast were used to evaluate the representation of the library. Notably, sequences derived from the uninduced library (Figure 4A) were almost identical in nucleotide composition to the pure SICLOPPS library (Figure 3B). As an example, the third “S” position of the NNS codons contained 63.666% G in the pure library and 63.665% G in the uninduced library. For the induced library, the G content at the third codon position was marginally increased to 64.1% (Figure 4B), indicating a minor propagation-related bias arising upon the expression of cyclic peptides. As for the pure library (Figure S8B), nucleotide frequencies were nearly constant across the five NNS codons (Figure S10A,B). Also, the amino acid frequencies across all five randomized positions in the uninduced (Figure 4C) and induced (Figure 4D) library were very similar, except for the previously mentioned increase in stop codon frequency. Amino acids frequencies of the library introduced in yeast (Figure 4C,D; repressed and induced, respectively) were also comparable to frequencies observed in the original library (Figure S8D). A direct comparison of changes in amino acid representation observed upon induction was conducted (Figure 4E) by subtracting deviations from expected frequencies for the uninduced library (Figure 4C) from the same deviations in the induced library (Figure 4D). Very small changes were observed upon induction of the library, with individual amino acids at the different positions changing by 1.01% at most. The occurrence of leucine was particularly lowered at the second randomized position upon induction (Figure 4E). Cysteine and glycine were slightly higher in frequency at most positions, whereas tryptophan was specifically enriched in the last position (Figure 4E). In general, subtle patterns of differential frequencies were observed at the codon level, with the only prominent change being an increase in the presence of the TAG stop codon at all five positions in the library extracted from induced cells (Figure S10C–E). Thus, the composition of the generated SICLOPPS library remains largely the same upon expression in S. cerevisiae, meaning that nearly unbiased selections of the initial cyclic peptide library can be performed.

Discussion

There is an increasing interest in the use of small, cyclic peptides as therapeutics to treat a broad range of human diseases.1,2,7 Here, large DNA-encoded libraries can be used for the de novo discovery of cyclic peptides targeting a specific cellular component or pathway.2,5,6 In this study, we describe the construction of a large S. cerevisiae SICLOPPS library, allowing for in vivo selection of cyclic peptides within a eukaryotic cellular context. We expect that this strategy will broaden the scope of using SICLOPPS libraries for drug discovery since selection can be performed directly for a particular phenotypic outcome without prior knowledge of the specific cellular target.20 Moreover, using an inducible GAL1 promoter instead of a constitutive promoter20 will allow us to determine if any observable effect is dependent on cyclic peptide expression, by comparing to cells grown under noninducible conditions.

Initial attempts to construct a yeast SICLOPPS library resulted in suboptimal library size. These initial difficulties may be due to the use of a relatively large eukaryotic vector (∼8 kb) for library construction as compared to using a smaller bacterial vector, which is the most common practice for SICLOPPS libraries.16,19,22,24,25 It is most likely more difficult to efficiently ligate a large plasmid with the relatively small (∼430 bp) DNA insert containing the randomized sequence, due to the decreasing probability of the vector ends participating in the ligation reaction. Surprisingly, the use of phosphatase to prevent plasmid self-ligation greatly decreased library size (Figure 1B), even when considering the subsequent increase in self-ligated plasmid vectors, upon omission of vector dephosphorylation. To our knowledge, this phenomenon has previously gone unreported despite DNA dephosphorylation being a common step in library generation protocols.16,32,57 We believe that our reported optimization steps (Figures 1 and S2) could be useful in the endeavor by other researchers to construct large SICLOPPS DNA libraries, as well as other methods such as phage display that rely on ligation reactions and amplification of a DNA library in E. coli.3,32 Overall, our library generation strategy is comparable to or more efficient than other related studies, considering the relatively small amounts of DNA used.18,20,32,33,37 Moreover, while a eukaryotic SICLOPPS library has been reported previously,20 our optimization work has led to the construction of a larger library (8 × 107 versus 5 × 107) using significantly smaller amounts of plasmid DNA, thus allowing for easier upscaling of library size if necessary. An alternative approach for even greater library sizes could be the use of whole-plasmid PCR to eliminate the need for vector-insert ligation reactions.33,35

An initial inspection of the SICLOPPS library composition was performed through the isolation of individual library members followed by Sanger sequencing. In total, 15 of the 20 random library members were correctly encoded to produce functional intein-peptides (Table 1), and these were all validated to be expressed in S. cerevisiae (Figure 2C). All, except one of the library members, also displayed processing to release the peptide in a cyclized form, although at varying efficiencies. Altogether, 14 of 20 = 70% of randomly picked library members were shown to be functional, which is in accordance with results obtained by others.20

The split intein domains used to catalyze cyclization in the constructed SICLOPPS library are derived from Synechocystis sp. PCC6803,14 and were chosen based on their verified ability to drive cyclization in S. cerevisiae(20) as well as their minimal cellular toxicity.58 The intein-mediated reaction has previously been found to enable cyclization of a large range of target sequences, except for some preferences or constraints for certain residues near the splice sites.21,61 One study found that the amino acid at the position corresponding to the first randomized position in the present study influences the intein processing efficiency, with tryptophan, phenylalanine, and tyrosine yielding high levels of processing.61 Indeed, the cyclic peptide encoded by library member number 17 contains a tryptophan at this position (Table 1) and shows very high levels of processing (Figure 2C). Yet, given the relatively low number of investigated library members and their highly variable sequences (Table 1), it is challenging to discern any distinct patterns between members that are processed well and members that are not (Figure 2C). Future studies will determine if any over- or underrepresentation of amino acids upon expression (Figure 4D) will affect the overall splicing capability of the SICLOPPS library.

Sequences of the randomly picked library members were further used to evaluate the base composition of the library (Figure 2B), as a highly diverse and unbiased library will increase the chance of successful selection by allowing more library members to be assayed.37,38,40,42 These findings were later expanded by high-throughput sequencing of the constructed SICLOPPS library, an approach that, to our knowledge, has not been reported previously for SICLOPPS libraries. Despite the cyclic peptide library being overall encoded as expected, a distinct overrepresentation of guanine was observed (Figure 3B). This guanine bias could potentially decrease the diversity of cyclic peptides that are present during selection. Some of the biases observed in our high-throughput sequencing data sets could arise during PCR reactions on the library to produce amplicons for sequencing, but this seems unlikely as similar nucleotide frequencies were observed in a Sanger sequencing of individual library members (compare Figures 2B and Figure 3B).

High-throughput sequencing of the degenerate library primer, used to insert the randomized sequences in the library, revealed the composition of the primer to be the main cause of the observed G insertion bias (Figure 3A). Only a slight, further increase in G content was observed after the construction of the library (Figure 3B). Importantly, the library is not expressed during library construction in E. coli (Figure S6), thus limiting growth-related biases as observed during construction of phage display libraries.39,44,45 Interestingly, a similar G insertion bias in genetically encoded nonselected libraries has previously been reported by others.42,47 In both studies, libraries were constructed by randomized oligos produced through standard phosphoramidite chemistry, which was also used to synthesize the degenerate library primer in the present study. The chemical synthesis of DNA oligos was found to likely introduce the observed nucleotide bias in the libraries, through preferential incorporation of G during synthesis related to the chemical reaction mechanism.42,47 Therefore, the observed guanine bias could likely be corrected by reconstruction of the reported SICLOPPS library using a different library primer synthesized with revised phosphoramidite ratios.42 The observed guanine overrepresentation in the library described here gave rise to a skewered distribution of codons, thereby causing the frequencies of several amino acids to deviate from the expected frequencies (Figure 3F). However, all amino acids were represented in a positionally unbiased manner across all five randomized positions of the cyclic peptides (Figure S8D), thus ensuring a representation of all amino acids at all positions. Hence, a correction of the observed guanine insertion bias was deemed unnecessary.

High-throughput sequencing of the constructed SICLOPPS library after introduction in S. cerevisiae resulted in the detection of around half a million unique library members for both the uninduced and induced library (Table 2), which contrasts with the estimated 4 million yeast transformants each theoretically representing a unique library member. However, plasmid DNA isolation from yeast cells is more challenging than purification from E. coli, among other things due to a greatly reduced plasmid copy number per cell and the copurification of genomic DNA. Moreover, only around half of all sequences in either data set were also found in the other data set, despite the cells originating from the same pool of transformants. Thus, it is plausible that only a fraction of library members present could be extracted for sequencing, explaining the substantial decrease in library members represented in the sequencing data. The overall composition of the constructed SICLOPPS library after introduction in S. cerevisiae was almost indistinguishable from the pure library (compare Figures 4A and 3B), indicating that no bias occurs upon colony formation of transformed cells before library induction. Remarkably, inducing the expression of library members resulted in only very minor changes in base frequencies (Figure 4B), with the most prominent difference being a slight enrichment of library members either containing no randomized sequence or a stop codon (Table 2). An explanation for these increases in nonfunctional library members could be that certain cyclic peptides in the library are intrinsically toxic to the yeast cells, thereby resulting in an enrichment of library members with no phenotypic effect.40 This demonstrates the opportunity for conducting unbiased selections of the initial library in yeast to identify target-specific cyclic peptides. Additionally, our findings contrast propagation-related biases described for phage display libraries in E. coli.39,44,45 An overrepresentation of cells expressing growth-enhancing library members is probably less pronounced in our experimental setup due to the yeast transformants being amplified by colony formation on agar plates without competition from other transformants. In contrast, phage display libraries are typically amplified in liquid, fast-dividing bacterial cultures where transformants compete for the available nutrients, thus allowing cells expressing advantageous library members to expand rapidly. A potential limitation of our method is the relatively low transformation efficiency in yeast when compared to E. coli, which in most cases only allows for a partial representation of the library during selection.6,56 In the present study, 4.3 million yeast transformants were obtained, thereby including only a fraction of the 80 million library members. Yet, multiple library transformations in parallel could readily be achieved for a greater representation of library members in a selection experiment. Moreover, one of the few examples of applying SICLOPPS libraries in eukaryotic cells successfully selected several cyclic peptide hits despite assaying only a very small portion of the entire library.20

The constructed yeast SICLOPPS library can be used for a wide range of phenotypic selections of cyclic peptides targeting a specific cellular component or pathway. As one of the only requirements, our library carries a LEU2 selection marker and therefore requires the use of a leu2 null mutant strain for selection. We expect that the work presented here can be applied to the construction of S. cerevisiae SICLOPPS libraries in other yeast expression vectors, thus, broadening the scope that this type of library can be used for in vivo selections. De novo-developed cyclic peptide compounds hold great therapeutic potential, thereby necessitating large and diverse genetically encoded DNA libraries as a tool for initial discovery. Of note, work by Prof. Ali Tavassoli’s group demonstrates how cyclic peptides, initially identified in SICLOPPS selections, can be modified into more drug-like compounds of potential use as therapeutics against human diseases.26,27

Conclusion

In conclusion, we have generated a large S. cerevisiae SICLOPPS library for in vivo selection of cyclic peptides. Productive efforts to improve library construction resulted in a library of 80 million members, which was shown to be expressed and correctly processed to produce cyclic peptides in vivo in S. cerevisiae. Sequencing of the library primer and the pure SICLOPPS library, as well as sequencing of the library after introduction in yeast, showed that the library is encoded as intended but carries a guanine insertion bias mainly attributed to the oligo used to insert the randomized sequence. Upon expression in yeast, a very small additional bias is observed. These findings hold promise for performing unbiased selections from the initial cyclic peptide library in S. cerevisiae and indicate the potential of identifying distinct cyclic peptides of great target specificity.
