Paraneoplastic progressive Supranuclear palsy: a case report and literature review
Authors: Kaoutar Simma, Asmae Sikkal, Hajar Khattab, Kamal Haddouali, Salma Bellakhdar, Bouchra EL Moutawakil, Mohamed Abdoh Rafai, Hicham EL Otmani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractProgressive supranuclear palsy-Richardson syndrome (PSP-RS) is a sporadic atypical parkinsonian disorder typically resistant to levodopa. While most cases are neurodegenerative, various conditions (genetic, vascular, infectious, or paraneoplastic) can mimic this phenotype, termed PSP-like or PSP mimics. Although paraneoplastic PSP is extremely rare, it is important to recognize because of its potential reversibility. The diagnosis can be particularly challenging, especially when classical tumor markers or imaging fail to identify a clear neoplastic origin. Here, we describe a 39-year-old man with an Anti-Ri–mediated PSP-like syndrome in whom an extensive evaluation, including surgical removal of two suspicious sites (thymus and testis), failed to identify an underlying tumor. The patient presented with rapidly progressive symptoms characteristic of PSP-RS, raising clinical suspicion for a paraneoplastic cause. We also review the literature on paraneoplastic PSP.Keywords: progressive supranuclear palsy, paraneoplastic syndrome, atypical parkinsonism, anti Ri, PSP like syndromes, autoimmune movement disorders

Introduction

Progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP) is a classical neurodegenerative tauopathy characterized by the deposition of 4-repeat tau in various cell types and brain regions. Its classical form, Richardson’s syndrome (PSP-RS), manifests with early postural instability and falls, vertical more than horizontal oculomotor dysfunction evolving into supranuclear gaze palsy, Parkinsonism, behavioral changes, executive dysfunction, and pseudobulbar palsy [1]. Once regarded as a rare disorder with a distinct presentation, PSP is now recognized as a spectrum of clinical variants [2]. Although they differ initially, these variants tend to converge on PSP-RS over time [3]. Numerous conditions can mimic PSP-RS—collectively termed PSP-like syndromes—including genetic, vascular, infectious, drug-induced, autoimmune, and paraneoplastic etiologies [3]. This article focuses on paraneoplastic PSP, a rare entity whose recognition is of particular importance [4].

Case report

We report the case of a 39-year-old man referred for a rapidly progressive gait disturbance of 18 months’ duration. He experienced stiffness, a short-stepped gait, foot dragging, and intermittent freezing. Within a few months, he required support for ambulation and became nearly bed-bound. He also reported binocular diplopia and increasing difficulty opening his mouth, chewing, swallowing, and speaking, which evolved into slow, strained speech.

Clinical examination revealed severe parkinsonism without tremor, impaired postural reflexes with a spontaneous tendency to fall (see video), and an initially vertical gaze palsy that rapidly progressed to complete external ophthalmoplegia with preserved oculocephalic reflexes (supranuclear palsy) (Fig. 1). A mild static-kinetic cerebellar syndrome was also observed, together with pseudobulbar features, eye-opening apraxia, and jaw-closure dystonia. Brain MRI showed periaqueductal T2-weighted hyperintensity without gadolinium enhancement and no midbrain atrophy (Fig. 2). A PSP-like syndrome was considered, given the rapid progression, young age, and MRI findings. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis revealed elevated protein concentration (0.7 g/l) with normal cell count (0/mm3). Oligoclonal bands were present, restricted to the CSF, and the IgG index was elevated at 0.8. Autoimmune and viral investigations were negative, including anti-AQP4 (NMO) and anti-MOG antibodies. Testing for onconeuronal antibodies detected Anti-Ri (ANNA-2). Anti-Ri antibodies were tested in serum. The laboratory did not provide semiquantitative results (e.g. ± or 2+); however, the clinical biologist indicated that the result was significantly positive, without specifying the exact titer. Other antineuronal antibodies, including anti-Ma2 and the remainder of the standard onconeuronal panel, were investigated and found to be negative. Whole-body CT revealed an anterior mediastinal mass and an ectopic testis with calcifications. A subsequent 18F-FDG PET-CT showed FDG uptake in the ectopic testis (Fig. 3).

The patient underwent orchiectomy, but pathological examination—reassessed multiple times—showed no malignancy. Thoracic surgery was then performed to explore the thymic mass, which was completely resected and showed no malignant features on histopathology. He received 1 g/day of intravenous methylprednisolone for five days without clinical improvement and died one month later.

Discussion

Several disorders can exhibit the core features of PSP, particularly the Richardson syndrome variant (PSP-RS), while not strictly fulfilling the clinical exclusion criteria. These conditions—sometimes referred to as PSP mimics, phenocopies, or PSP-like syndromes—may have genetic, vascular, infectious, drug-induced, autoimmune, or paraneoplastic etiologies [3, 5]. PSP-RS mimics are defined by a PSP-RS–like syndrome plus at least one red flag: positive family history, onset before age 45, rapid or stepwise progression, acute/subacute onset, atypical clinical findings, normal/atypical brain MRI, or history of HIV, syphilis, aortic surgery, or recent use of dopamine-blocking agents [3, 6].

Autoimmune or antibody-mediated movement disorders (paraneoplastic or non-paraneoplastic) encompass a broad spectrum of neurological conditions [3]. Paraneoplastic syndromes (PNS) result from an aberrant immune response triggered by a remote malignancy; onconeural antibodies, which target intracellular antigens, are highly specific for paraneoplastic etiology and may precede or follow the cancer diagnosis [5]. PNS affects only 1–15% of patients with cancer, and PSP as a paraneoplastic manifestation is exceedingly rare, and reported tumors include breast and lung carcinoma, as well as lymphoma [5]. Recent work by Yamahara et al outlines several features suggestive of paraneoplastic PSP, including younger onset (<40 years), acute or subacute progression, concurrent neoplasms, abnormal CSF findings (elevated protein, pleocytosis, increased IgG index, or CSF-specific oligoclonal bands) and absence of typical PSP MRI changes [6]. Additional atypical signs, such as pronounced weight loss or leg ataxia, may further indicate a paraneoplastic origin. Among the antibodies most commonly linked to paraneoplastic PSP phenotypes are anti-IgLON5, anti-Ri, and anti-Ma2 [6].

Our patient met all suggestive features consistent with a paraneoplastic PSP-like syndrome rather than classic neurodegenerative PSP. Anti-Ri antibodies were detected, while anti-Ma2 was negative, and anti-IgLON5 was not tested. Based on these findings, the PNS-Care score was 7, consistent with a probable paraneoplastic neurological syndrome.

Anti-Ri/ANNA-2 classically presents with ataxia and oculomotor dysfunction, including opsoclonus-myoclonus and vertical gaze paresis. Movement disorders can feature myoclonus (in about one-third of cases), Parkinsonism, and dystonia of the jaw or neck. Parkinsonism with supranuclear gaze palsy and cognitive impairment has been described in multiple cases, suggesting that Anti-Ri–mediated brainstem encephalitis can mimic neurodegenerative syndromes involving Parkinsonism or dementia and underscore the variability of Anti-Ri syndromes supporting that our patient’s clinical picture, although atypical, falls within the recognized spectrum of Anti-Ri encephalitis [5].

Our patient presented with a PSP-like paraneoplastic syndrome characterized by Anti-Ri onconeural antibodies, yet no malignancy was identified despite an exhaustive diagnostic workup and surgical exploration of two suspicious sites (an ectopic testis and an anterior mediastinal mass). In many paraneoplastic neurological syndromes and in the few documented cases of paraneoplastic PSP, no tumor is ultimately found. In our patient, despite prompt management, the clinical course was fatal, underscoring the severity of paraneoplastic neurological syndromes.

Since the early 2000s, several cases of autoimmune encephalitis (AE) or paraneoplastic neurological syndromes (PNS) presenting with progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP)-like phenotypes have been documented [6]. Yamahara et al. identified 24 confirmed PSP-like cases across 15 publications, comprising one case series and fourteen individual case reports [6]. The most frequently discussed condition resembling PSP is anti-IgLON5 disease, which is associated with the presence of anti-IgLON5 antibodies, followed by conditions associated with anti-Ma2 and anti-Ri antibodies. In addition to these antibodies, one case report presented a patient mimicking PSP with each of the following antibodies: anti-CV2/collapsin response mediator protein 5 (CV2/CRMP5), anti-Hu, anti-dipeptidyl-peptidase-like protein 6 (DPPX), anti-kelch-like protein 11 (KLHL11), anti-leucine-rich glioma-inactivated 1 protein (LGI1), and anti-Sez1l6 antibodies. Uncharacterized antibodies and anti-NH2 terminus of alpha-enolase (NAE) antibodies have also been detected in PSP-mimicking AE/PNS. Among the 24 patients, ages ranged from 36 to 79 years, with a male predominance (16 men vs. 8 women) [6]. Disease progression was subacute in 10 of 22 cases and chronic in 12. Among patients with available oncological data, associated malignancies included small cell lung cancer (2/14), breast cancer (2/14), and testicular cancer (1/14), while no tumors were found in 9 cases [6].

Core PSP-like features reported included vertical gaze palsy (17/23), postural instability (19/23), and freezing of gait (3/23). Additional findings not typical of PSP included CSF abnormalities—such as elevated protein or cell counts and CSF-specific oligoclonal bands (OCBs) (8/12)—absence of midbrain tegmental atrophy on MRI (6/24), weight loss (3/24, each associated with anti-DPPX, anti-CV2/CRMP5, and anti-KLHL11 antibodies, respectively), as well as sleep disturbances and limb ataxia (3/24, all linked to anti-IgLON5 antibodies) [6].

Regarding treatment, immunotherapy regimens—including corticosteroids (8/12), intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG, 7/12), rituximab (3/12), and cyclophosphamide (1/12)—as well as oncological therapies (chemotherapy in 4 cases, radiotherapy in 1), led to clinical improvement in 7 of 12 cases, temporary benefit in 1, and no response in 4 [6].
