Prevalence of quorum-sensing genes (lasI, lasR, RhlI and rhlR) in biofilm-producing Pseudomonas aeruginosa clinical isolates in Northern Iran
Authors: Robabeh Gholami Aghamahali, Mahya Pahlavanian, Hamid Reza Goli
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe quorum sensing (QS) systems are involved in biofilm formation ability of Pseudomonas aeruginosa, as key factors in the development of infections. Therefore, the aim of this study was to determine the frequency of significant QS encoding genes, including lasI, lasR, rhlI, and rhlR, in clinical isolates of P. aeruginosa.Materials and methodsThis study was performed on 100 clinical isolates of P. aeruginosa that were identified by biochemical, microbiological, and PCR tests. The biofilm formation assay was done by microtiter plate method. Alkaline Lysis was used to extract the genomic DNAs. Then, the PCR method was used to identify the 16 S rRNA gene and the frequency of the lasR, lasI, rhlR, and rhlI genes.ResultsAmong 89 biofilm-producer isolates, 48 (53.93%), 17 (19.1%), and 24 (26.96%) showed a strong, moderate, and weak biofilm formation ability, respectively. The lasI gene was identified in 94% of the isolates and the rhlR gene was presented in 99% of the isolates, while 100% of the isolates carried the lasR and rhlI genes. Among the 6 isolates lacking the lasI gene, 3, 1, 1, and 1 isolates were collected from the ICU, emergency, burn, and surgical departments, respectively. In addition, 3, 1, 1, and 1 isolates lacking the lasI gene were collected from sputum, urine, wound, and catheter samples, respectively. One strain lacking the rhlR gene was also isolated from a wound sample in burn department. In this study, 94% of the isolates had all 4 genes tested, while 100% of the isolates carried at least 3 QS genes.ConclusionConsidering that all biofilm-producing isolates were collected from hospitalized patients with active infections, it can be concluded that the presence of QS genes in the development of acute infections caused by this organism has been proven in this study.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-12406-z.

Introduction

Pseudomonas aeruginosa is a common environmental organism in hospitals and is recognized as an opportunistic pathogen for humans, causing bacteremia, sepsis, and severe respiratory, urinary, ear, and eye infections [1]. Infections caused by P. aeruginosa are often life-threatening and the treatment can be challenging because the bacterium is inherently resistant to multiple antibiotics and can easily acquire new resistances [2]. Due to the increasing antibiotic resistance and the prevalence of multidrug-resistant (MDR) isolates of P. aeruginosa worldwide over the past three decades, the World Health Organization (WHO) has declared this bacterium a priority pathogen and called for research and development of new strategies to eradicate it [2]. The presence of underlying diseases, such as cystic fibrosis, diabetes, and Acquired Immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS), and procedures that weaken the immune system, such as chemotherapy or organ transplantation, increase the risk of mortality in patients infected with this organism, especially in cases of respiratory infections and extensive burns [3]. One of the most important reasons for the increased infectivity and mortality caused by this organism is its inherent and acquired drug resistance, which is achieved through mechanisms, such as the production of degrading enzymes, chromosomal mutations, reduced influx and increased efflux, and most importantly, the formation of biofilm and its regulation through QS systems [4]. The production of biofilm along with the innate and acquired antibiotic resistance mechanisms in P. aeruginosa has led to an increased incidence of multidrug-resistant (MDR) strains in recent years [5, 6].

The ability of P. aeruginosa to form biofilm is a key factor in the development of chronic infections, and the main components of P. aeruginosa biofilms include exopolysaccharides (EPSs), such as alginate, Pellicle polysaccharide (Pel) and Polysaccharide synthesis locus (Psl), and extracellular DNA (eDNA) [7, 8]. Rhamnolipids also participate in cell-to-surface interactions, maintenance, and bacterial dispersal during the stages of P. aeruginosa biofilm development, and mediate cell dispersal from biofilms, thereby enhancing the ability to colonize new sites [2]. Cyclic di-guanosine monophosphate (c-di-GMP) also plays a critical role in regulating biofilm formation, motility, adhesion, virulence, and morphogenesis, and its high cellular concentration increases the production of matrix components, including exopolysaccharides [9]. Biofilm adhesion and overall structural stability are hallmarks of the contribution of polysaccharides to biofilm function, and polysaccharides can also influence the expression of QS and ultimately the release of eDNA mediated by the quinolone-Pseudomonas-quinolone (PQS) signal [10]. The regulatory mechanism of biofilm formation has always been a popular area of ​​research. Active participation of microbial cells and EPS production are essential for the adhesion process and biofilm production. However, this process requires significant changes in cellular physiology and the mechanisms controlling these changes are mainly chemical signaling (quorum sensing), while P. aeruginosa has three interconnected QS systems [11]. Two systems use acyl homoserine lactones (AHLs) and the third uses quinolone signaling molecules [11]. It has been shown that a two-component regulatory system, quorum sensing, c-di-GMP signaling, and the functional extracellular plasma membrane signaling pathway can play a role in controlling biofilm processes [12]. The QS regulatory mechanism is essential in bacterial biofilm formation. Signaling molecules are synthesized, secreted, and accumulated extracellularly by bacteria, and when bacterial concentration and signal levels reach a critical threshold, the signaling elements combine with the receptor to stimulate the expression of genes associated with biofilm formation [13].

P. aeruginosa has 4 QS systems, named Las, Rhl, Iqs, and Pqs, depending on the N-acyl homoserine lactone (AHL) signal molecules [14]. The signaling molecules of these 4 systems are: N-(3-oxododecanoyl) -l-HSL (OdDHL), N-butanoyl-L-homoserine lactone (C4-HSL), 2-(2-hydroxyphenyl) -thiazole-4-carbaldehyde (IQS), and 2-heptyl-3-hydroxy-4(1 H)-quinolone (PQS) [14]. The Las system is composed of the transcriptional regulatory protein (LasR), and the N-3-oxododecanoyl homoserine lactone synthase (LasI), while the Rhl system is composed of the transcriptional regulatory protein (RhlR), and the N-butyryl homoserine lactone (C4-HSL) synthase (RhlI) [15]. When the concentration of autoinducers reaches a critical threshold, the transcriptional regulatory protein binds and activates the expression of many target genes, along with AHL synthase, leading to the formation of biofilm and other virulence features. The Las and Rhl are two distinct hierarchically linked QS systems, while the Las system dominates the Rhl system [15]. The Las system regulates the expression of elastases, proteases, bacterial motility factors, and RhlR, while the Rhl system regulates the expression of RhlI, RhlAB (rhamnolipid biosynthesis), elastase B, alkaline protease, motility, expression of sigma factor in stationary growth phase, and production of pyocyanin and hydrogen cyanide [16]. The presence of genes related to the QS system plays an important role in biofilm production, and by investigating their presence, we can hope to find suitable candidates for vaccine design. Such prevalence study has not been reported in this region of Iran and was considered as a gap in this region. So, we aimed to determine the prevalence of lasI, lasR, rhlI, and rhlR genes among clinical biofilm-forming isolates of P. aeruginosa and to evaluate their association with biofilm production.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval declarations and consent to participation

This study was done according to the Declarations of Helsinki. A written informed agreement form was delivered by the participants or close relatives. The classifying patientsʹ data was kept private. The non-repeated clinical samples were collected from hospitalized patients with a nosocomial infection after 48 hours’ hospitalization. Moreover, this study was approved by the Iran National Committee for Ethics in Biomedical Research. The National Ethical Code for this study was IR.MAZUMS.REC.1397.368.

Bacterial isolation and identification

One hundred P. aeruginosa clinical isolates were collected from patients hospitalized in educational-therapeutic hospitals, including Zare (a burn center), Imam Khomeini (a general center), Bu-Ali Sina (a pediatric center), Fatemeh Al-Zahra (a heart center), and Razi (an infectious center) from 2023 to 2024. Based on the statistical parameters considering a 95% confidence interval and a 0.097% error rate to determine the sample size, 100 clinical strains were selected for this study [8]. P. aeruginosa isolates were identified by standard microbiological and biochemical methods, such as gram staining, pigment production on Mueller-Hinton agar (Condalab, Spain), positive oxidase test, bacterial growth at 42 °C, the formation of Alk/Alk state in Triple Sugar Iron (TSI) agar (Condalab), citrate utilization as alone carbon source on Simon Citrate agar (Condalab), oxidative utilization of glucose but not lactose on Oxidation/Fermentation (OF) medium (Condalab), ability to grow on Cetrimide agar (Condalab), along with shape, size, and odor of colonies were initially identified [17]. Also, identification of the 16 S rRNA gene by PCR using specific primers (Table 1) was used to final confirmation of the isolates [18].

Biofilm production assay

Biofilm formation capacity of the P. aeruginosa clinical isolates was evaluated using the micro titer plate (MTP) method [19, 20]. A 96-well flat-bottomed polystyrene tissue culture plate (three wells for each strain) was used for this experiment. First, 180 µL of Trypticase Soy Broth (TSB) (Merck, Germany) containing 1% glucose and 20 µL of a bacterial suspension with a turbidity equal to 0.5 McFarland were added to each well, and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Then, to exclude planktonic bacteria, the wells were washed three times with Phosphate Buffer Saline (PBS) (pH = 7.2). Next, to fix the adhered cells, absolute methanol (Merck) was added to each well for 15 min. Then, the methanol discarded and the wells were air-dried. Next, the wells were stained with 0.2 mL of 0.1% (v/v) Hucker crystal violet for 15 min and washed three times with tap water. After air-drying the wells, to solubilize the crystal violet, 200 µl of a 33% glacial acetic acid (Merck) was added to wells for 15 min and its optical density (OD) was measured at 590 nm using a microplate reader (Stat Fax 2100). Three uninoculated wells comprising sterile TSB were considered as negative controls. An average of negative control OD was considered as the optical density cut-off (ODc). Considering the OD results, the isolates were separated into four groups. The isolates with an OD ≤ ODc were detected as biofilm-negative, while the isolates with an ODc < OD ≤ 2×ODc were considered as weakly biofilm producers. Also, the isolates that exhibited a 2×ODc < OD ≤ 4×ODc were grouped as moderate biofilm-producer, and strong biofilm producers had a 4×ODc < OD [21]. The P. aeruginosa PAO1 strain was used as a positive control strain in this test [19].

Extraction of bacterial genomic DNA

In this study, the alkaline lysis method was used to extract the genomic DNAs of P. aeruginosa clinical isolates. A membrane and wall-destructive buffer with detergent properties was used for this purpose. So, 0.4 g of sodium hydroxide (NaOH) was combined with 0.5 g of sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) (Sigma, Germany) and dissolved in 200 mL of sterile distilled water. Then, 20 µl of this buffer was combined with several pure colonies of bacteria in a 1.5 cc microtube and stirred with a sterile plastic applicator until the membrane was completely destroyed. In the next step, to more completely lyse the bacterial cells and kill viable bacteria, the microtubes were incubated in a hot water bath for 10 min at 95 °C. Then, to precipitate the bacterial residues, lipids, and proteins, the microtubes were centrifuged at 13,000 g for 3 min. In this case, the DNA was placed in the supernatant, and the other components were precipitated. Then, to increase the volume of extracted DNA and to reduce its concentration, 180 µL of sterile distilled water was added to the microtubes [22]. To confirm the quality and quantity of the extracted DNAs, their optical density was measured using a Nano Drop device (ND1000, USA). Meanwhile, extracted DNA fragments or smear formation were examined using 1% agarose gel electrophoresis (Sigma, Germany). The extracted DNAs were stored at -20 °C until use.

PCR test for confirmation of the organisms and detection of QS-related genes

To perform the PCR test, the desired primers were first selected from previous studies. Then, the quality of the primers was checked on the NCBI BLAST website, and sent for synthesis (Metabion, Germany). Then, the lyophilized primers were diluted according to the manufacturer’s instructions to obtain a concentration of 100 pmol per milliliter. In the next step, the primers were diluted to 10 pmol per milliliter with sterile distilled water and stored at -20 °C as a working solution. PCR was performed in a thermocycler with temperature gradient conditions (BioRad, USA). The final reaction volume for all PCR tests in this study was 15 µL, from which 7.5 µL of PCR Master mix (Ampliqon, Denmark), 5 pmol of each forward and reverse primers (0.5 µL), 450 ng of DNA (1.5 µL), and 5 µL of DNase and RNase free sterile deionized distilled water were used in each PCR reaction to identify all genes studied. Also, control strains carrying the genes of interest were used as positive controls and distilled water with Master Mix as negative controls in this study.

The PCR test conditions for the amplification of the QS-related genes were as follows: for all genes, an initial denaturation step was performed for 5 min at 95 °C. Subsequently, 35 PCR cycles were considered for the amplification of all genes. In these cycles, three steps were used, including a denaturation at 95 °C for 45 s, a primer annealing step at different temperatures for each gene (56 °C for lasR and lasI genes, 65 °C for rhlR gene, and 54 °C for rhlI gene) for 60 s, and an extension step at 72 °C for 75 s. Lastly, a 72 °C for 10 min was used for the final amplification of all genes. Then, the PCR products were electrophoresed on a 1.5% agarose gel (Sigma, Germany) prepared with 1X TBE (Tris-Boric acid-EDTA) buffer by adding 1.5 µl of Safe Stain (Sinaclone, Iran). Also, the control strains containing desired genes were used as positive control in this study.

Statistical analysis

The data were entered into SPSS 22 software and evaluated. After investigating the data and variables for normality, the frequency of the variables was determined using descriptive tests. Also, to compare the variables and determine the relationship between them, the Chi-square statistical test was used and a P-value˂0.05 was considered as statistically significant.

Results

Bacterial isolates and clinical data of the studied patients

A total of 100 non-repeated clinical isolates of P. aeruginosa were included in this study. These isolates were obtained from hospitalized patients (60 males and 40 females) in teaching and therapeutic hospitals affiliated to the Mazandaran University of Medical Sciences. The average age of the patients was 46 years. The results of molecular identification of the isolates are shown in Fig. 1.

P. aeruginosa isolates were obtained from different clinical specimens, including respiratory (37%), urine (26%), wound (20%), catheter (8%), blood (5%), stool (2%), and ocular secretion (2%). In addition, based on the hospital department of isolate collection, the most isolates (53%) were collected from the intensive care units (ICUs), followed by the emergency department (13%) (Fig. 2.) Among respiratory P. aeruginosa isolates, 33 (89.18%) were obtained from patients hospitalized in ICUs, which were critically ill patients (P-value ˂0.05). In addition, both isolates collected from ocular secretions were from children who had severe eye infections. Also, 8 (30.76%), 8 (30.76%), and 3 (11.53%) urine isolates were obtained from patients hospitalized in ICUs, emergency, and pediatric wards, respectively. On the other hand, 7 (35%) and 4 (20%) wound isolates were collected from ICUs and burn wards, respectively. Also, out of 8 catheter isolates, 3 (37.5%) were obtained from the surgical ward.

Biofilm formation assay

Among 89 biofilm-producer isolates, 48 (53.93%), 17 (19.1%), and 24 (26.96%) showed a strong, moderate, and weak biofilm formation ability. Remarkably, 90.0% of isolates obtained from burn center were strong biofilm-producer, and 48.27%, 81.81%, 50%, and 40% of isolates collected from ICU, burn, CCU, and pediatric wards displayed a strong capacity to formation of the biofilm. Also, 41.02%, 77.27%, 34.61%, 60%, and 37.5% of respiratory, wound, urine, blood, and catheter isolates were strong biofilm-producer, respectively (Fig. 3).

Discussion

The success of P. aeruginosa in infecting host cells and evading from the host immune system is due to the presence of a vast arsenal of pathogenic factors, especially the production of biofilm [24]. The widespread presence of this bacterium in nature and the existence of various pathogenic mechanisms and antibiotic resistance increase mortality rate by this opportunistic nosocomial organism (18 to 61%) [25]. The QS is dependent on cell density and plays a significant role in all stages of P. aeruginosa pathogenicity, bacterial escape from the host immune system, and the development of drug resistance [25]. The social behavior of this pathogen, as well as its biological responses to the surrounding community, is dependent to the QS system and ensure the stability and survival of the bacteria. The dispersible signaling molecules produced by this system must cross a critical threshold (quorum) to induce the expression of pathogenic genes and other biological responses of the bacteria [24]. Considering this information, knowledge about the presence and expression status of QS related genes, and designing or finding compounds against this system can be important in controlling the pathogenicity of P. aeruginosa and reducing the severity of clinical symptoms in patients [26]. The QS system in P. aeruginosa induces the expression of genes regulating virulence factors by producing a diffusible autoinducer (AI) and forming a complex between AI and other proteins [27]. Three central systems, Las, Rhl, and Pqs, in the QS system in P. aeruginosa form a cascade network that regulates intercellular signaling [28]. Although Las and Pqs are regulators of the Rhl pathway, the RhlR-C4-HSL complex generates most of the QS-dependent pathogenicity factors in P. aeruginosa, and all these systems stimulate each other in a cascade and activate the pathogenicity factors of this organism, such as elastases, proteases, exopolysaccharides, rhamnolipid, and biofilm production [25]. Given the significant role of the QS in biofilm production, it is important to investigate the prevalence of relevant genes in clinical isolates. By obtaining this information, it is possible to be informed about the genetic status of clinical isolates in each region and design a more appropriate plan to deal with them.

Al-Rabia et al. demonstrated that by disrupting the QS systems in P. aeruginosa, the expression of pathogenic factors can be reduced, and more effective treatment of drug-resistant infections can be promised [29]. They showed that thymoquinone, isolated from the essential oil of black cumin seed, can have antibiofilm and antivirulence activity against the P. aeruginosa PAO1 strain, reducing the expression of QS genes, reducing the PAO1 pathogenesis in mice, and increasing their survival (from 20% to 100%). Thymoquinone was able to inhibit biofilm production, pyocyanin and protease activity, and bacterial swarming motility, and to reduce the expression of QS-related genes including lasI, lasR, rhlI, and rhlR [29]. Abou Elez et al. investigated 276 P. aeruginosa isolates collected from fish and fish farmers in Egypt [30]. The isolates were fully resistant (100%) to tobramycin, gentamicin, and colistin, while 61.5% were multidrug resistant (MDR) and 23.1% were extensively drug resistant (XDR). Most isolates had at least four virulence-related genes including lasB, toxA, exoU, and oprL and three biofilm genes, including pslA (80.76%), pelA (80.76%), and lasR (76.92%). The QS genes including lasI and rhlR were identified in 84.6% and 61.5% of P. aeruginosa isolates, respectively. They showed that the emergence of drug-resistant P. aeruginosa isolates in fish poses a high risk to consumers [30]. However, the presence of lasI, lasR and rhlR genes in our study was 94%, 100%, and 99%, respectively. These differences were related to the source of bacterial isolation, although 16.3% of the isolates identified by Abou Elez et al. were P. aeruginosa and 34.4% were P. fluorescens, and 100% of their isolates were biofilm producer, but 89% of our isolates were biofilm former [30]. However, 95.5% and 98.87% of the biofilm-producing isolates in our study carried the lasI and rhlR genes, and the other two genes were present in all biofilm-producing isolates. Hemmati et al. reported that 95.8% of their P. aeruginosa isolates were biofilm-producer, of which 42.5%, 33.3%, and 20% had a strong, moderate, and weak biofilm pattern, respectively [31]. The lasI, lasR, rhlI, and rhlR genes were detected in 89.1%, 78.3%, 81.6%, and 90.8% of isolates, respectively. The most common QS genes among MDR isolates were rhlR and lasI genes at 94.3% [31]. In addition, rhlI, rhlR, and lasI genes were positive for all XDR isolates. However, the lasR gene was the least abundant among MDR (83%) and XDR (90.6%) isolates. Also, rhlR (94.7%) and lasR (81.7%) genes had the highest and lowest prevalence among biofilm-forming isolates, respectively [31]. The prevalence of these four biofilm producing genes in our study was higher than in the abovementioned study, while the types of clinical samples and hospitalization wards were almost similar. This variance could be due to the diverse antibiotic consumption policies in two regions, because biofilm production is one of the important ways of antibiotic resistance in P. aeruginosa. Another study by Altan et al. in Turkey on 47 carbapenem-resistant P. aeruginosa (CRPA) isolates showed that 90% of the isolates carried the lasR and rhlR genes [32]. Most CRPA isolates had the potential to form biofilms and were able to transfer resistance genes related to the ceftazidime, cefepime, ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, and piperacillin-tazobactam simultaneously using transferable resistance plasmids [32]. These data indicate the important role of biofilm production in carbapenem resistance in clinical isolates of P. aeruginosa. Rojo-Bezares et al. isolated 52 P. aeruginosa strains from 15 river surface water samples in the La Rioja (Spain), while all isolates were susceptible to all antibiotics tested, except one isolate that was resistant to carbapenems [33]. Also, 59% of their isolates carried the exoS, exoY, exoT, exoA, lasA, lasB, lasI, lasR, rhlAB, rhlI, rhlR, and aprA genes, and 27% produced a higher biofilm biomass compared to the PAO1 strain [33]. These results highlight the importance of rivers as temporary reservoirs and sources of virulent P. aeruginosa transmission. The prevalence of biofilm genes in the aforementioned study was much lower than in our study, indicating a dissimilarity in the biofilm production ability of clinical and environmental isolates. Also, in their study, almost all strains were antibiotic susceptible, which explains the low prevalence of biofilm-producing genes.

Ghanem et al. studied 125 clinical isolates of P. aeruginosa and showed that 93.6% had at least one virulence gene [34]. Also, 94.4%, 69.6%, 80%, 81.6%, and 89.6% of their isolates were positive for rhlR, rhlI, lasR, lasI, and lasB, respectively. The distribution of QS-related genes among isolates based on sample type did not show any significant correlation, except for the lasI gene. Also, there was a significant correlation between the distribution of rhlI, rhlR, and lasR genes, and emergence of multidrug resistant (MDR) phenotype in P. aeruginosa isolates [34]. However, in our study, no isolate was found to be negative for the presence of all tested genes, and the presence of these genes was higher than in the study by Ghanem et al. Mohamed et al. at Tanta University, Egypt, reported that 38.2% of 55 P. aeruginosa isolates associated with ocular infections were MDR [35]. The frequencies of exopolysaccharide, biofilm, protease, and pyocyanin production were positive in 94.5%, 89.1%, 81.8%, and 78.2% of the isolates, respectively. The QS genes lasR and rhlR were detected in 89% and 81.8% of isolates, respectively. This study highlighted the importance of the QS system in regulating the formation of virulence factors and antimicrobial resistance in P. aeruginosa. Consequently, anti-QS and anti-virulence therapies could be promising alternatives to currently available antibiotic therapies [35]. However, in our study, two isolates were prepared from ocular secretions, and both isolates carried all of the studied genes. Dolatshah et al. investigated 100 P. aeruginosa clinical isolates, while 69% were biofilm- producer, and 9%, 13%, and 47% of the isolates were strong, moderate, and weak biofilm producers, respectively [36]. The important point in their study was that all isolates carried all the genes involved in quorum sensing (rhlI, rhlR, rhlAB, lasB, lasI, and lasR) as well as the pathogenicity gene cupA, while the pslA gene was identified in 92% of the isolates [36]. The prevalence of biofilm genes in our study was almost similar to the aforementioned study, while 89% of our isolates had the ability to produce biofilm, which was slightly higher than the aforementioned study. Another study on 50 isolates of P. aeruginosa collected from burn patients in Egypt was conducted by Elnegery et al., where 86% had a proteolytic activity, and 76% were rhamnolipid-producer [37]. Biofilm formation was observed in 96% of their isolates. The lasR and rhlR genes were present in almost all isolates, except for three isolates that were negative for both genes and two isolates that were positive for only the lasR gene [37]. They found that QS affects the production of virulence factors and biofilm of P. aeruginosa in burn wounds, while the lasR gene positively regulates biofilm formation, proteolytic activity, pyocyanin production, and synthesis of the rhamnolipid, and the RhlR gene positively affects the production of protease and rhamnolipid [37]. However, only one burn isolate in our study lacked the lasI gene and another isolate lacked the rhlR gene, while the rest of the isolates had all the genes. These results indicate the significant role of biofilm production for bacterial pathogenicity on burned surfaces and the development of antibiotic resistance in these patients. Cotar et al. also investigated 49 clinical isolates of P. aeruginosa collected from wound and respiratory tract secretions of 21 hospitalized cardiovascular patients undergoing surgery [16]. They showed that all isolates had the rhlR, rhlAB, lasI, lasR, and rhlI genes. Other virulence genes also had different frequencies between respiratory and wound isolates [16] The presence of biofilm genes in our study was almost similar to the aforementioned study, except for 3 (8.1%) respiratory isolates lacking the lasI gene, 1 (26.5%) wound isolate lacking this gene, and 1 (26.5%) wound isolate lacking the rhlR gene. The remaining respiratory and wound isolates in our study carried all tested genes.

Conclusion

The increased pathogenicity and mortality caused by P. aeruginosa are due to the production of multiple virulence factors, while biofilm production plays a significant role in the pathogenicity and antibiotic resistance. The presence of QS genes in the production of biofilm and pathogenicity of this bacterium is critical, so many recent studies have focused on the inhibition of QS. The inhibition of QS in P. aeruginosa can lead to the inhibition of bacterial aggregation and the reduction of the severity of the pathogenicity, although the pathogenicity of this organism is multifactorial. High prevalence of las and rhl genes among strong biofilm-producing isolates suggests their role in persistence and antimicrobial resistance of P. aeruginosa in clinical settings, indicating the significant role of these genes in intercellular communications and ultimately the increase in the pathogenicity of this bacterium.

Recommendation

In these circumstances, more appropriate strategies should be taken to control intercellular communications in this bacterium and inhibit its pathogenicity. Inhibition of QS genes, especially las and rhl ones, may inhibit the intercellular communications and can consider as an infection control or therapeutic targeting option.
