An mTOR-Stat3-Stathmin pathway controls centrosome and microtubule dynamics for oocyte polarization
Authors: Amal Shawahny, Yoel Bogoch, Neta Hart, Yaniv M Elkouby
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryIn animals and plants, egg production is essential for fertility, reproduction, and embryonic development. However, our understanding of the regulatory mechanisms underlying the cellular and developmental programs of oogenesis is lacking. Here, we aimed to identify overlooked regulators of early oogenesis in zebrafish. First, we established a long-term ovary culture system that enables physiological oocyte development from oogonia to primordial follicles, providing an effective ex vivo platform for rapid investigation. Next, we utilized this system for functional screening of candidates from stage-specific oocyte transcriptomic data. We identified mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR), signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (Stat3), and the microtubule-destabilizing protein Stathmin as novel regulators of oocyte polarity. In zebrafish and most species, oocyte polarity is essential for oogenesis and embryonic development. In polarization, microtubules control the localization of the polarity regulator Buc to the centrosome, as well as its condensation into the Balbiani body, an oocyte membraneless compartment. Through a combination of genetics and pharmacological manipulations in vivo and ex vivo, we show that loss of mTOR or Stat3 functions, as well as overactivation of Stathmin, disrupted centrosome regulation and destabilized microtubules, leading to dispersed Buc condensates and loss of polarity. We demonstrate that mTOR acts upstream of Stat3 in oocytes, and inhibition of Stathmin in either stat3−/− or mTOR-deficient ovaries rescued both cytoskeletal and polarity defects. Thus, we established an unpredicted but essential mTOR-Stat3-Stathmin pathway that controls centrosome and microtubule dynamics to drive oocyte polarity. We propose that through cytoskeletal regulation, this pathway provides oocytes with polarization competence, a likely common step in cell polarity.Keywords: cell polarity, aebrafish oogenesis and ovary development, microtubules, centrosome MTOC, Balbiani body, mTOR, Stat3, Stathmin, Bucky ball molecular condensation, Buc molecular condensation

Introduction

Early oogenesis requires dynamic and precisely coordinated cellular and developmental programs that are critical for egg production and reproduction. In humans, early oogenesis occurs during fetal development and determines the ovarian reserve for the entire lifespan. Deficiencies in these early processes are the leading cause of infertility, miscarriages, and gonadal tumors,1 yet the underlying mechanistic defects remain unclear due to the lack of a complete understanding of these natural processes.

To bridge the gap in our understanding of early oogenesis, we aimed to identify novel regulators of early oocyte development in zebrafish, an excellent model for early oogenesis,2,3,4,5 as stepping stones for uncovering essential mechanisms. To identify regulators, we developed a novel long-term ovarian culture system as an effective ex vivo platform for investigation and used it to functionally screen for candidates from our oocyte transcriptomic database.6 This led us to uncover an unpredicted pathway that controls zebrafish oocyte polarity.

In vertebrates, including zebrafish and Xenopus, the oocyte is polarized along an animal-vegetal axis, with the vegetal pole harboring key embryonic regulators.7 Embryonic dorsal- and germline fate determinants are organized as RNA-protein (RNP) complexes in specialized germ granules during oogenesis.7 Those determinants must localize to the oocyte vegetal pole, from which they later establish the global embryonic body axes and the germline lineage.7 The formation, integrity, and polarized localization of these determinant-containing germ granules require the Bucky ball (Buc) protein in fish8,9 and its homolog XVelo in frogs.10,11

Buc is an intrinsically disordered protein,12 which undergoes phase separation to drive the formation of a membraneless compartment called the Balbiani body (Bb),13 through molecular condensation.9,14 During symmetry breaking at the onset of oocyte differentiation, Buc becomes polarized by localizing in dynamic liquid-like condensates around the centrosome9,12,14 and subsequently organizes granules in a nuclear cleft,9,12,14 assembling the mature solid-like Bb.9,13,14 The mature Bb translocates to the cytoplasmic membrane, docks to the cortex, disassembles, and unloads its passenger RNP complexes, specifying this region of the cortex as the oocyte’s vegetal pole.15,16 Loss of Buc functions in buc−/− oocytes results in radially symmetrical eggs and early embryonic lethality.17,18

Buc dynamics and Bb formation are orchestrated by microtubules.9,12 At symmetry breaking, Buc polarization around the centrosome requires microtubules of the bouquet centrosome microtubule organizing center (MTOC) machinery.4,9,12,19 The bouquet centrosome MTOC machinery is a cellular organizer that simultaneously controls symmetry-breaking and essential meiotic chromosome pairing dynamics, forming a conserved chromosomal configuration called the chromosomal bouquet.4,12 The localization of small Buc granules into the main condensate in the cleft requires dynein-mediated transport along microtubules.9 Later, microtubules cage the mature Bb and are essential for maintaining its integrity, size, and shape.9

These mechanisms are widely conserved. The Bb is conserved in oocytes from insects to humans, exhibiting similar morphology and cellular and developmental dynamics.13,20,21,22,23,24 In mice, oocyte polarity is not detected, but the Bb is associated with the proper formation of the primordial follicle,22 suggesting a critical role in oogenesis, and Bb components localize around the centrosome and require microtubule trafficking.22,25 In the insect Thermobia, Bb components polarize and align with the bouquet configuration,26 likely via the conserved bouquet MTOC machinery. In Drosophila oocyte polarity, the Oskar protein organizes RNP complexes in pole granules through molecular condensation27,28 in an analogous manner to Buc10 and is trafficked over microtubules.27,29,30 However, despite the essential roles of microtubules in these processes, how their organization is regulated in vertebrate oocytes to facilitate polarization remains unknown.

Results

A novel ex vivo system to study early oogenesis and ovarian development in zebrafish

Early oogenesis in zebrafish occurs during post-embryonic development in juvenile fish. Investigations of zebrafish ovaries require demanding genetic approaches, such as four-generation maternal-effect forward genetic screens (e.g., Dosch et al.,18 Fuentes et al.,31 and Wagner et al.32) or reverse genetics with sophisticated conditional approaches to avoid early lethality. Furthermore, severe defects in zebrafish oogenesis often result in sex conversion, leading to the development of sterile males33,34,35,36 and precluding investigation of ovaries. To circumvent these issues, we aimed to develop an ex vivo system for the study of early oogenesis as a robust assay complementary to genetic studies.

We expanded current protocols for short-term (hours) culture of zebrafish juvenile ovaries9,19,37,38 to develop a mounting-media-free system, along with conditions for culturing ovaries for several days (Figure S1A). We optimized the mounting-media-free culture, which maintains ovary shape while allowing improved passage of material from the media. To preserve the ovaries in their physiological morphology, we placed them in poly-D-lysine (PDL)-treated glass-bottom dishes and covered them with a cell strainer mesh to hold them in a straightened and flattened position like in the fish body, interfacing with the glass bottom of the dish (Figure S1A). We then optimized the culture media by using DMEM and adding supplements, including ovary extract, fetal bovine serum (FBS), and fish serum, as sources of physiologically derived growth factors (basic ovary culture media [BOCMs]; STAR Methods), and cultured the ovaries at 28°C for 10 days.

Ovaries cultured in BOCM remained viable for 7 days post-culture (dpc), as shown by the vital mitochondrial dye MitoTracker, which selectively labels active mitochondria as a proxy for cell viability. However, ovaries were necrotic at 10 dpc (Figures 1A and 1B). To evaluate oocyte development in culture, we pulsed ovaries with 5-ethynyl-2'deoxuridine (EdU) at 1 dpc and chased for labeled cells at 7 dpc. EdU labeling was detected in the nuclei of cells in pulsed ovaries but not in EdU-non-treated ovaries (Figure 1C), confirming signal specificity. Both Ddx4-positive germ cells and Ddx4-negative somatic cells were labeled with EdU, demonstrating that both somatic and germ cells divide in culture (Figure 1D). However, based on morphological staging criteria,2 all EdU-labeled Ddx4-positive germ cells were at the oogonia stage, and none were detected at meiotic stages (Figures 1D and 1H). While these conditions allow for ovary viability and cell division, they are insufficient to initiate meiosis and oocyte differentiation.

We hypothesized that oocyte differentiation would require female hormones and added estradiol-17 beta (E2) and 17alpha, 20beta-dihydroxy-4-pregnen-3-one (DHP) to our culture media (complete ovary culture media [COCM]1; STAR Methods). E2 induces oogonial proliferation,39 and DHP acts directly on the initiation of the first meiotic division later in oogenesis.39 Under these conditions, EdU-labeled Ddx4-positive oocytes were also detected at leptotene and zygotene stages of meiosis I, with few continuing to pachytene and diplotene stages in primordial follicles (Figures 1E and 1H). In addition, ovaries remained vital through 10 dpc (Figure S1B).

To support more efficient oocyte development, we optimized the time and duration of exposure to E2 and DHP throughout culture. E2 can inhibit germline cyst breakdown,40 which might explain the low frequency of primordial follicles and oocytes at meiotic stages in ovaries cultured in COCM1. Attempting to mimic physiological regulation, we supplemented with both E2 and DHP (COCM1; STAR Methods) during 1–3 dpc to promote early development and only with DHP (COCM2; STAR Methods) during 4–10 dpc (Figure 1F) to allow further differentiation through meiosis. Indeed, we detected a significantly higher number of EdU-labeled Ddx4-positive oocytes at leptotene and zygotene stages, as well as an increased number of pachytene-stage oocytes in primordial follicles (Figures 1F–1H). The number of diplotene-stage oocytes was slightly increased but remained low (Figure 1H).

Progression in culture reflected the normal developmental pace of oogenesis in vivo. Pulsing 4 weeks post-fertilization (wpf) fish with EdU for 16 h in vivo, as we did ex vivo, and raising them in normal conditions for 7 days, equivalent to 7 days in culture, we found similar numbers of EdU+ oogonia, as well as differentiating oocytes at leptotene to diplotene stages in vivo, to those we detected ex vivo (Figures 1H and S2A). Furthermore, apoptosis is rare in vivo, with 0–5 cells per ovary showing cleaved-caspase-3 signals,19,33 and this was not altered in cultured ovaries at 7 dpc, side by side to non-cultured ovaries in vivo (Figure S2B), supporting that our culture conditions ex vivo closely mimic physiological conditions. As further confirmation, upon pulsing with EdU at 1 dpc, detection of EdU+foxl2l+ (oogonia),41,42 EdU+rec8+ (meiotic entry),42 and EdU+dmc1+ (meiotic prophase)42,43,44 cells at 7 dpc (Figure S2C) confirmed oogonia initiate oocyte differentiation and progress through prophase in culture.

Thus, we conclude that our new long-term ovarian culture reliably recapitulates early oogenesis, from oogonia in germline cysts through pachytene stages in primordial follicles, and can be used as an effective ex vivo system for investigation.

Transcriptomic-based ex vivo screen identifies Stat3 as a regulator of oocyte polarity

To identify novel candidate regulators of zebrafish oogenesis, we mined our reported oogenesis transcriptomics database,6 which includes the transcriptome of five major stages in early oogenesis, isolated by size: symmetry breaking (oogonia-zygotene stages), nuclear cleft (primordial and primary follicles at pachytene and diplotene stages), mature Bb (primary follicle at dictyate—meiotic arrest in diplotene), stage (St.) II, and St. III.2,6 Identification of distinct clusters of specific gene expression dynamics (Figure 2A) provided a molecular characterization of oogenesis in zebrafish.6 We mined this data in search of novel candidate regulators of zebrafish oogenesis, using three criteria for candidate selection: (1) genes with unknown functions in zebrafish oogenesis; (2) genes with distinct timing of activity, indicating developmental regulation; and (3) genes with available small molecule inhibitors, allowing for experimental manipulations. We narrowed down a short list of selected candidates for a small pilot functional screen.

We screened for candidate functions by applying their small molecule inhibitors in the ex vivo ovary culture system. We cultured wild-type (WT) developing ovaries from juvenile fish at 4 wpf for 7 days in the presence of the inhibitor of interest or vehicle as control. As a straightforward assay for general ovarian morphology, oocyte staging, and development, we labeled post-culture ovaries with DAPI and the lipid dye DiOC6, which is routinely used to visualize major cellular and morphological features of developing oocytes and ovaries.2,9,12,19 DiOC6 detects the enriched endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and mitochondria in the Bb, demarcating its stages of formation.2,9,12 Scoring for DiOC6 phenotypes identified positive hits. In this work, we focus on uncovered functions of the signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (Stat3) pathway.

In the canonical Stat3 pathway, Stat3 is activated through phosphorylation in a Janus kinase (Jak)-dependent or -independent manner (reviewed in Herrera and Bach,45 Hillmer et al.,46 Johnson et al.,47 and Yu et al.48) and translocates to the nucleus to transcriptionally regulate target genes’ expression.45,46,47,48 Transcripts for genes associated with Stat3 and Jak/Stat pathways were enriched in cluster #1 in our transcriptomic data (Figures 2A and 2B). Cluster #1 includes genes active during symmetry breaking and early Buc nucleation in the nuclear cleft at oogonia through pachytene stages, suggesting Stat3 activity at the appropriate developmental time window to control oocyte polarization. We detected phosphorylated Stat3 (p-Stat3) (active Stat3) signals in juvenile ovaries, where signals appeared enriched in germ cells with undetected signals in somatic ovarian cells (Figure S3A), suggesting direct role in oocytes. Specifically, p-Stat3 was detected in nuclei of oocytes from oogonia through diplotene (Figure 2C).

We tested the function of Stat3 in oogenesis using its small molecule inhibitor, 2,4-diamino-5-p-chlorophenyl-6-ethyl-pyrimidine (pyrimethamine)49 in the ex vivo culture system, compared with DMSO as a control (Figure 2D). Ovaries were isolated from fish of a consistent standard length (SL) (Figure S3B), a standardized measure for zebrafish post-embryonic and ovarian development.2,50 Ovaries in pyrimethamine- and DMSO-treated groups remained vital through culture, as indicated by MitoTracker signals (Figure S3C). Ruling out toxic effects, apoptosis was very slightly increased by pyrimethamine treatment compared with DMSO but remained within the normal physiological range as in WT ovaries in vivo (Figure S3D).19 Treated ovaries exhibited normal oocyte and ovarian morphology, with a typical distribution of oocyte stages, suggesting that Stat3 inhibition did not alter gross ovarian and oocyte development (Figure S3E).

We observed specific polarity phenotypes upon Stat3 inhibition. We previously defined distinct steps in Bb formation12: symmetry breaking (oocytes at 10–15 μm in diameter), assembly in early-nuclear-cleft stages (oocytes at 17–25 μm in diameter), progression in mid-cleft stages (oocytes at 25–40 μm in diameter), and mature Bb (oocytes at 50–70 μm in diameter). We analyzed these defined steps in Bb formation in Stat3-deficient ovaries. In normally polarized oocytes at symmetry breaking, as well as cleft and mature Bb stages, Buc granules are found as polarized single aggregates, or multiple tightly adjacent aggregates, in a region that is smaller than approximately ¼ of the nuclear perimeter2,5,9,12 (Figure 2E top, single arrowheads). Oocytes where Buc granules were scattered and expanded around the nucleus to regions spanning more than ¼ of the nuclear perimeter were defined as non-polarized oocytes with “dispersed” Buc (Figure 2E bottom, multiple arrowheads). In contrast to normally polarized Buc granules in DMSO-treated control ovaries (Figure 2E), in pyrimethamine-treated ovaries, Buc condensates were dispersed in oocytes at symmetry-breaking (42.6%), early-cleft (70.1%), late-cleft (75.8%), and mature Bb (57.1%) stages (Figure 2E).

Similar phenotypes were detected for DiOC6. In normally polarized oocytes, the lipid dye DiOC6 detects the enriched mitochondria and ER in the forming and mature Bb, as well as additional mitochondria normally found around the nucleus in the remaining cytoplasm2,12 (Figure 2F top, single arrowheads point to enrichment in the Bb). When polarity is defective, DiOC6 signals are no longer enriched in the forming and mature Bb but exhibit ectopic aggregates of enriched signals or are found around the nucleus2,12 (Figure 2F bottom, multiple arrowheads). DiOC6 signals lost their enriched aggregate arrangement and were not polarized in 32.5%–38.8% of oocytes throughout these stages (Figure 2F). These results suggest polarity defects resulting from Stat3 inhibition.

We confirmed these results in physiological conditions by inhibiting Stat3 in whole living fish, adding pyrimethamine or DMSO to the fish water (Figure S4A). To achieve efficient inhibition while minimizing potential stress and ensuring fish viability, we treated the fish with alternating cycles of treatment and rest between 4 and 6 wpf, under standard husbandry conditions (STAR Methods). The SL of all fish in both groups was consistent before treatment at 4 wpf, and all fish reached similar and normal SL at 6 wpf (Figure S4B), confirming normal general fish physiology and growth.

Ovaries from DMSO-treated fish appeared normal, with typical distribution of oocyte stages and properly polarized Buc and DiOC6 (Figures S4C–S4E). By contrast, 53.8% of ovaries from pyrimethamine-treated fish were underdeveloped (Figure S4C), being smaller and containing fewer oocytes at primordial follicle stages. However, oocytes that reached these stages in either underdeveloped or properly developed ovaries exhibited polarity defects, with dispersed Buc granules and DiOC6 signals (Figures S4D and S4E), confirming the results ex vivo. Ovarian underdevelopment was specific and not seen in control-treated fish, nor was it observed in treatments ex vivo, implying that this phenotype arose from indirect systemic effects of Stat3 inhibition in whole fish. Together, these in vivo and ex vivo results suggest Stat3 is required for zebrafish oocyte polarity.

Stat3 mutant fish confirm that Stat3 is essential for oocyte polarity

To further test Stat3 function, we analyzed ovaries of fish carrying the stat3 loss-of-function mutant allele stat3stl27.51 Loss of Stat3 in stat3stl27/stl27 fish (hereby referred to as stat3−/−) leads to lethality during juvenile post-embryonic development; however, mutant fish remain viable through 4 wpf51 (Figure S5), which allowed us to examine ovarian development in vivo. The absence of nuclear p-Stat3 in oocytes in stat3−/− ovaries (Figure 3A) confirmed the lack of Stat3 activity in mutant ovaries, consistent with reports in embryonic development51 and regeneration.52 As reported, stat3 mutants developed scoliosis (Figure S5A), and that their SL was slightly shorter than the SL of WT fish (Figure S5B), likely because of their scoliosis phenotype.51

Ovaries of stat3 mutant fish appeared to develop normally. Certain mutations in zebrafish that induce vast induction of oocyte apoptosis can cause sex reversal.34,35,36 However, in stat3−/− gonads, apoptosis was very slightly increased compared with WT but was within the normally low physiological levels (Figure S5D). Gonad morphology was consistent with that of developing ovaries and not converting gonads, containing a typical distribution of oocyte stages (Figure 3B; Video S1), as we previously confirmed with expression of a set of sex-specific marker genes.33 Evidently, the number of germline cysts per gonad that expressed the female-specific marker gene foxl2l41,42 was unaltered (Figure S5E), determining the female state of stat3−/− gonads.

stat3 mutant oocytes at all stages of Bb formation exhibited polarity defects (Figures 3C and 3D). In contrast to WT, in the majority of oocytes in stat3 mutant ovaries, the Bb appeared disassembled, with non-polarized but dispersed Buc and DiOC6 signals (Figures 3C and 3D). Oocytes at mature Bb stages (50–70 μm) are scarce in ovaries at 4 wpf. Nevertheless, we observed few oocytes at mature Bb stages, and in stat3−/− ovaries they all showed a clear loss of Buc polarity, compared with WT (Figure S6A).

Overall, based on pharmacological manipulations ex vivo and in vivo, as well as genetic analysis in stat3 mutant fish, we conclude that Stat3 is essential for Buc localization, Bb formation, and oocyte polarity. We next addressed the mechanisms by which Stat3 controls Buc polarization.

Stat3 is required for microtubule stabilization and regulation of the centrosome in oocytes

Stat3 is well known for its regulation of transcription45,46,47,48 and could regulate the expression of an intermediate factor, which in turn controls oocyte polarity. However, the specific polarity and Buc localization defects we observe upon loss of Stat3 functions are challenging to explain by direct transcriptional regulation alone. Evidence for non-nuclear functions of Stat3 exists from cell culture models53,54,55 and in mice.56 Stat3 was shown to control microtubule stability independently of transcriptional control by regulating the Stathmin protein.53,54 Stathmin depolymerizes microtubules by destabilizing their +end tips and prevents microtubule polymerization by sequestering free tubulin molecules.57 It has been established in cell culture models that Stat3 controls Stathmin activity by binding Stathmin’s tubulin-interacting domain and thereby antagonizing Stathmin microtubule destabilization activity.53,57 Considering the essential roles of microtubules in oocyte polarization and Bb formation,9,12 we investigated whether Stat3 controls microtubules through Stathmin regulation in oocytes.

First, we detected puncta of p-Stat3 in oocyte cytoplasm along with nuclear signals (Figures 2C and 3A), supporting potential microtubule regulation in the oocyte cytoplasm. Cytoplasmic p-Stat3 puncta were abolished together with the nuclear signals in stat3−/− ovaries (Figure 3A), demonstrating that they very likely represent p-Stat3 molecules.

We tested whether loss of Stat3 function leads to microtubule instability. In WT ovaries, oocytes displayed a network of microtubule cables and bundles, as detected by α-tubulin (αTub) labeling, including microtubules of the bouquet MTOC machinery at symmetry breaking9,12,19 and those of the cleft meshwork in cleft stage oocytes9,12,19 (Figure 4A). By contrast, in stat3−/− ovaries, microtubules of the bouquet MTOC machinery and of the cleft meshwork were absent, and we detected much fewer cables in oocytes’ cytoplasm overall, as validated by quantification of signal intensity (STAR Methods; Figure 4A; Video S2), suggesting their instability. Microtubules were further lacking in the few mature Bb stage oocytes detected at 4 wpf in stat3−/− ovaries compared with WT (Figure S6B). Labeling stabilized microtubules by acetylated tubulin confirmed this observation. In contrast to a network of acetylated microtubules present throughout the oocyte cytoplasm at various stages of Bb formation in WT ovaries (Figure 4B), acetylated microtubule signals were completely abolished in most oocytes in stat3 ovaries (Figure 4B). Thus, Stat3 is essential for microtubule stabilization and organization in the oocyte.

Stat3 is also known for its regulation of centrosomes in cancer, where it is essential for clustering abnormally duplicated centrosomes.54 Stat3 was proposed to control centrosome regulation in a transcriptional-independent manner, at least partly through Stathmin regulation.54 We therefore investigated whether Stat3 is required for normal centrosome and MTOC regulation in zebrafish oogenesis.

The centrosome is composed of a pair of centrioles, which are surrounded by the proteinaceous pericentriolar material (PCM).58,59 In centrosome maturation, PCM modifications lead to the recruitment of γTub, which in turn nucleates microtubules.58,59 We analyzed the localization of γTub in symmetry breaking and early-cleft stage oocytes in stat3−/− ovaries. In WT oocytes, γTub localizes to the single centrosome at both symmetry-breaking and early-cleft stages (Figure 4C). By contrast, γTub localization was abnormal in stat3−/− ovaries. In most oocytes at both symmetry-breaking stages and cleft stages, γTub either formed two foci resembling two centrosomes or was disorganized and appeared like a cloud (Figure 4C). Both phenotypes were confirmed by three-dimensional (3D) segmentation through the entire oocyte volume (Figure 4D; Video S3).

Video S3. Centrosome aberrations in stat3 ovaries, related to Figure 4

Videos of the centrosome phenotypes in the representative oocytes from Figure 4D. γTub is in maroon and DPAI in gray.

The observed γTub phenotypes could reflect defects in PCM and/or centriole regulation. To address potential centriole phenotypes, we labeled ovaries for the centriole marker εTub.60 In contrast to a single focus of εTub signals in WT oocytes at both symmetry-breaking and cleft stages, most oocytes at both stages in stat3−/− ovaries exhibited two εTub foci (Figure 4E), similar to γTub. Thus, Stat3 is required for proper centriole (εTub) and PCM (γTub) regulation in zebrafish oogenesis, likely by controlling centrosome duplication and/or centriole regulation.

We confirmed the concomitant loss of polarity alongside abnormal centrosome regulation by co-labeling ovaries for γTub and Buc. 3D segmentation of entire oocytes revealed that 48.8% of oocytes with either two foci of γTub or its cloud-like disorganization also exhibit dispersed Buc condensates (Figure 4F; Video S4). Thus, Stat3 is essential for microtubule stabilization and proper organization of the centrosome MTOC machinery in early oogenesis, suggesting Stat3 may control oocyte polarity through the regulation of Stathmin.

Stat3 acts upstream of Stathmin to control the centrosome MTOC machinery and polarity

Supporting our hypothesis that Stathmin is involved in oocyte polarity, a “Stathmin in breast cancer” pathway was enriched in our transcriptomic data with the Stat3 and Jak/Stat pathways (Figure 5A). We detected p-Stathmin signals in oocytes (Figure 5B), which were prominent at symmetry-breaking and early-cleft stages and reduced at mid-cleft stages (Figure 5B), indicating dynamic Stathmin regulation in oocytes. Notably, the Stathmin protein specifically localized near microtubules (Figure S6C), supporting a relevant role in their regulation.

We hypothesize that normally in oocytes Stat3 antagonizes Stathmin, and upon Stat3 loss-of-function, Stathmin becomes overactive, destabilizing microtubules and leading to mis-localization of Buc condensates and loss of polarity. If this hypothesis is correct, inhibiting Stathmin in stat3−/− ovaries should prevent microtubule destabilization and rescue polarity. To test this, we used the small molecule Stathmin inhibitor GDP36661 to inhibit Stathmin in stat3−/− ovaries. We treated WT and stat3−/− ovaries ex vivo with GDP366 or DMSO as control and monitored Buc localization (Figure 5C). As expected, WT ovaries treated with GDP366 or DMSO exhibited normally polarized Buc condensates at symmetry breaking through cleft stages (Figure 5C). Since microtubules are normal in WT ovaries, potential over-stabilization by Stathmin inhibition is unlikely to perturb polarity. Similar to stat3−/− ovaries in vivo, ex vivo stat3−/− ovaries treated with DMSO showed mislocalized Buc in all or most oocytes throughout these stages (Figure 5C). However, inhibition of Stathmin in stat3−/− ovaries treated with GDP366 restored Buc localization, with normally polarized condensates similar to WT (Figure 5C). Levels of rescued polarity were observed from 50% ± 7% of oocytes at symmetry breaking to 85% ± 12.8% of oocytes at early-cleft stages and nearly complete rescue of 97% ± 5.9% of oocytes at mid-cleft stages (Figure 5C), suggesting Stathmin acts downstream of Stat3 in Buc polarization.

GDP366 and commercially available Stathmin inhibitors might inhibit survivin as an off-target. Survivin is a known regulator of mitosis progression, which could affect microtubules in this context.62 However, several lines of evidence rule out significant functional manipulation of survivin in our experimental setting. First, the survivin protein was specifically detected in mitotic oogonia cysts and not in later stages during Bb formation (Figure S7A). Second, neither the number of cysts expressing survivin nor its cellular distribution was altered by GDP366 treatments on either WT or stat3 mutant backgrounds (Figure S7A). Finally, survivin loss-of-function induces prometaphase defects, cytokinesis failure, and mitotic catastrophe, as well as increased apoptosis,62 and these are not apparent in our settings. GDP366 treatments did not significantly induce apoptosis on either WT or stat3 mutant backgrounds (Figure S7B). Consistently, GDP366 treatments did not alter the number of mitotic germ cells (oogonia) and their direct, meiosis-initiating progeny (leptotene) cells per ovary on either background (Figure S7C), arguing for normal completion of mitosis and transition to differentiation and against mitotic defects and/or delays. While we cannot formally exclude survivin inhibition, the phenotypes we report very likely arise specifically from Stathmin loss-of-function.

In the rescue experiments above, Stathmin inhibition likely prevents microtubule destabilization in the absence of Stat3 activity. To determine if this is the case, we examined microtubule organization in the same experimental setting, treating WT and stat3−/− ovaries ex vivo with GDP366 or DMSO as control, and monitored αTub. As expected, we detected overall normal microtubule organization in WT ovaries treated with either GDP366 or DMSO and a lack of clear microtubule cables in stat3−/− ovaries treated with DMSO (Figure 6A). However, in stat3−/− ovaries treated with GDP366, detection of clear microtubule cables and bundles was restored (Figure 6A). Analyses of these phenotypes per stage during polarization, at symmetry breaking through cleft stages, confirmed rescue of microtubule organization in stat3−/− oocytes by Stathmin inhibition (Figure 6B). Rescue levels ranged between 60% of oocytes at symmetry breaking, 79% at early-cleft, and 93.5% in mid-cleft oocytes (Figure 6B). Quantification of signal intensity validated these results (Figure 6B).

We tested whether the centrosome phenotypes in stat3−/− ovaries can also be rescued by inhibition of Stathmin by labeling for γTub in similar experiments. Normal single-focus γTub-positive centrosomes were detected in oocytes at symmetry-breaking and early-cleft stages in WT ovaries treated with either GDP366 or DMSO (Figure 6C). In stat3−/− ovaries treated with DMSO, most oocytes at either stage exhibited either two foci or the cloud-like disorganized signals of γTub, as expected (Figure 6C). Like microtubules, Stathmin inhibition in stat3−/− ovaries treated with GDP366 exhibited a normal single centrosome labeled with γTub, with rescue in 92.1% of oocytes at symmetry breaking and 88.2% of oocytes at early-cleft stages (Figure 6C).

Our results suggest that Stat3 is required to antagonize Stathmin and that this regulation is crucial for microtubule organization, centrosome MTOC formation, and oocyte polarization. The Stathmin protein is detected in stat3−/− oocytes wherein microtubules are lost as internal control (Figure S6C), and Stat3 is not required for expression of the Stathmin gene (Figure S6D), ruling out contribution by transcriptional control. While we could not directly test protein-protein interactions due to lack of available reagents, these results support regulation of Stathmin by Stat3 via protein-protein interactions.

Stathmin likely fine-tunes the balance of microtubule stability and organization in oocytes. Therefore, Stat3 plays a key role in maintaining a functional microtubule network, essential for the localization of Buc. We propose that by ensuring correct microtubule organization, Stat3 provides the oocyte with the potential to polarize and thus functions as a polarity competence factor.

mTOR is an upstream regulator of Stat3

We next explored potential upstream regulators of Stat3. Two known activators of Stat3, mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) and interleukin (IL)-6,46,47,48,63 are enriched alongside Stat3 in our transcriptomic data (Figures 7A, S7D, and S7E). mTOR pathway components play a role in sex determination in juvenile bipotential gonads in zebrafish,64 but its potential role in oocyte polarity had not been examined. The mTOR pathway is part of an intricate network of signaling pathways, making functional studies of mTOR specifically in oocyte polarity by genetics in vivo challenging. To address this, we tested the role of mTOR by inhibiting its activity in ovaries ex vivo.

We treated ovaries with the established small molecule inhibitor of mTOR, rapamycin, or DMSO as a control ex vivo and assayed for Buc localization (Figure 7B). In contrast to control-treated ovaries, inhibition of mTOR activity resulted in a loss of polarity, with Buc condensates mislocalized and dispersed in the majority of oocytes at symmetry breaking through cleft stages (Figure 7B). As a positive control for mTOR inhibition and concomitantly with Buc polarity loss, we detected reduced nuclear and cytoplasmic signals of p-Stat3 upon rapamycin treatment, as expected65 (Figure 7C). Thus, mTOR is required for Stat3 activation in oocytes and for proper Buc polarity, with mTOR inhibition phenocopying the effects observed in stat3 mutant ovaries.

These results suggest that mTOR functions upstream of Stat3-Stathmin in regulating oocyte polarity. We hypothesized that inhibition of Stathmin activity should rescue Buc polarity upon loss of mTOR function. To test this, we treated WT ovaries ex vivo with either the Stathmin inhibitor GDP366, rapamycin, or a combination of both, side by side with DMSO as a control (Figures 7D and 7E). As expected, treatment with DMSO or GDP366 alone resulted in normal Buc polarity at symmetry-breaking and cleft stages (Figure 7E). Treatment with rapamycin alone led to a loss of Buc polarity in oocytes throughout symmetry-breaking (56.6% ± 2.8%), early-cleft (44% ± 2.8%), and mid-cleft (46.1% ± 4.2%) stages (Figure 7E). However, when GDP366 was added to rapamycin-treated ovaries, a near-complete rescue of Buc polarity was observed throughout these stages (87.1% ± 1.4% at symmetry-breaking, 92.3% ± 1.1% at early-cleft, and 98.2% ± 0% at mid-cleft stages; Figure 7E). These results demonstrate Stathmin activity is likely downstream of mTOR signaling.

Discussion

Cell polarity is a fundamental biological process that drives developmental events. Mechanisms of cellular polarization can be generalized into three primary steps: (1) the symmetry-breaking step, which establishes the initial polarized localization of polarity factor(s) from a symmetric or random distribution (e.g., Campanale et al.66 and Ierushalmi and Keren67); (2) the polarization step, where a cascade of activities originating from localized polarity factors leads to a polarized state67; and (3) the functional output step on the cell cortex, where the localization of specific components to distinct cortical regions facilitates polarized cellular functions.67 In oocyte polarity, the symmetry-breaking step, driven by the bouquet MTOC machinery,4,19 initiates the polarization of the Buc protein.12 The polarization step is mediated by Buc dependent molecular condensation of Buc RNPs, which is regulated and positioned in the oocyte by microtubules.9 At the functional output step on the cell cortex, Macf1 and actin-mediated docking of Buc RNPs to the cortex15,68 ensures localization of germplasm and dorsal determinants to the vegetal pole for future embryonic axis formation and germline specification.16

Here, we uncover a previously unrecognized feature of oocyte polarity, which operates in parallel to the primary steps above: the acquisition of polarization competence. This concept refers to the oocyte’s ability to develop the potential to polarize, which includes actively maintaining an optimal centrosome MTOC and microtubule network that are essential for polarity. Our findings establish a novel mTOR-Stat3-Stathmin pathway that controls the optimal centrosome MTOC and microtubule organization in the oocyte, regulating polarization (Figure 7F). We propose that this pathway confers polarization competence to the oocyte. The acquisition of polarization competence is likely a common step in cell polarity.

Evidence suggests Stat3 may play non-transcriptional roles in other polarity contexts. In murine embryonic fibroblast migration, Stat3 and Stathmin regulate microtubules independently of transcription, likely contributing to the establishment of the cell leading edge.53,69 In sperm, p-Stat3 localizes to the acrosome, a polarized organelle that forms opposite the flagellum.70 This suggests that Stat3’s regulation of microtubules and its roles in acquiring polarization competence may be widely conserved.

Stat3 and Stathmin regulate centrosome clustering, MTOC formation, and microtubule stability in cancer contexts,53,54,57,71,72,73 operating through intricate interactions with master centrosome regulators aurora A (AurA) and Plk1.54,72 In the oocyte, Stat3-Stathmin regulation of the centrosome MTOC likely involves similar interactions with key centrosome regulators like AurA and Plk1, but this requires further studies. Extending from the centrosome MTOC, the microtubule network in the oocyte is also highly dynamic, changing organization to facilitate polarity.9,12,19 The dynamic spatial organization of microtubule cables can be controlled by Stathmin. Opposite gradients of phosphorylated and non-phosphorylated Stathmin were reported by fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) in cell culture, with denser microtubule organization correlating with phosphorylated Stathmin.69 Stathmin down-regulation in another cell line increased looping of microtubules, suggesting the control of the spatial form of cables. It is tempting to speculate that localized Stathmin activity in the oocyte can influence microtubule cable dynamics locally to craft their changing organization.

Such regulation of centrosome and microtubule organization by the mTOR-Stat3-Stathmin pathway likely extends to other organisms. For instance, p-Stat3 was detected in mouse oocytes between embryonic day 12.5 and post-natal day 4,70 which overlaps with Bb formation. Further, p-Stat3 localized to microtubules and meiotic spindle poles in mouse oocyte maturation.74 p-Stat3 was also detected in human fetal and adult ovaries, in both oocytes and granulosa cells,75 indicating potential functions in human oogenesis.

Stat3 functions in oocyte polarization open exciting possibilities for understanding cell-cell communication in early oogenesis. Stat3’s established roles in broadly regulating cell-cell communication46,47,56,63 suggest it may be involved in oocyte polarization. Communication between the oocyte and granulosa cells is a hallmark of follicular development in oogenesis,73,76,77 but whether granulosa or other somatic cells potentially regulate oocyte polarity is unknown. Identifying Stat3 activating signal(s) and their sources, including potentially by IL-6 (Figure S7E), offers an interesting area for future investigation.

Transcriptional regulation of germline development by Stat3 is established. In Drosophila, Stat3 controls primordial germ-cell migration to the gonad, as well as germline- and follicle stem cell maintenance.45 In turtles, Stat3 mediates temperature-dependent sex determination, upregulating expression of the oocyte transcription factor foxl2 and promoting female fate.78 Stat3 transcriptional and non-transcriptional regulations likely cooperate in zebrafish oocytes, and future studies, including by generation of germline-specific versus somatic loss-of-function stat3 mutants, will resolve Stat3 transcriptional regulation in oogenesis.

Our work presents an effective ex vivo system for studying oogenesis. Our long-term culture enables the development of follicles from oogonia in ovaries ex vivo. This process reliably recapitulates physiological early oogenesis in highly accessible settings for ex vivo analyses. As demonstrated here, this system provides an invaluable platform for rapid functional studies and robust candidate screening and can be combined with genetics to investigate processes that would otherwise be difficult to address. In the future, when combined with live imaging techniques, it will enable real-time investigation of dynamic processes over extended time periods, which holds great promise for advancing oogenesis research.

Method details

Gonad collection

Juvenile ovaries were collected from 4-6 week post-fertilization (wpf) juvenile fish. Fish had a standard length (SL) measured according to Parichy et al.79 and were consistently ∼8-12mm. Ovary collection was done as in Elkouby et al.12 Briefly, to fix the ovaries for immunostaining, fish were cut along the ventral midline and the lateral body wall was removed. The head and tail were removed and the trunk pieces, with the exposed abdomen containing the ovaries, were fixed in 4% PFA at 4°C overnight with nutation. Trunks were then washed in PBS and ovaries were finely dissected in cold PBS. Ovaries were washed in PBS and then either stored in PBS at 4°C in the dark, or dehydrated and stored in 100% MeOH at -20°C in the dark.

Genotyping

Genotyping was performed at 3 wpf, after which fish were rested and raised in the system until the collection of their ovaries at 4-6 wpf. Fish were anaesthetized in 0.02% Tricaine (Sigma Aldrich, #A5040) in system water, and a piece of their fin tail was clipped for DNA extraction. DNA was extracted using the standard HotSHOT DNA preparation method. stat3stl27 mutant fish were genotyped by KASP genotyping (LGC, Teddington, UK). KASP-PCR was performed on an Applied Biosystems StepOnePlus machine and following the manufacturer instructions, using the following SNP sequence:

ATGCATGTTATAACAATCTGAAAACTGAAAATACTAAAACTGAAACTAATATTTAGCTATTGCTTGGGTATAACCTCTTACTCATCCTCCACAGGACA[TGGAGCA/-]GAAGATGAAAATGTTGGA GAATCTGCAGGATGATTTTGATTTCAACTACAAAACTCTTAAGAGTGCTGGA

Fluorescence immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Fluorescence immunohistochemistry (IHC) was performed as in Elkouby et al.12 Briefly, ovaries were washed 4 x 20 minutes in PBT (0.3% Triton X-100 in 1xPBS. If stored in MeOH, ovaries were gradually rehydrated before washes. Ovaries were blocked for 1.5-2 hours in blocking solution (10% FBS in PBT) at room temperature, and then incubated with primary antibodies in blocking solution at 4°C overnight. Ovaries were washed 4 x 20 minutes in PBT and incubated with secondary antibodies in fresh blocking solution for 2 hours, and were light protected from this step onward. Ovaries were then washed 4 x 20 minutes in PBT and incubated in PBT containing DAPI (1:1000, Molecular Probes), with or without DiOC6 (1:5000, Molecular Probes) for 50 minutes and washed 2 x 5 minutes in PBT and 2 x 5 minutes in PBS. Ovaries were mounted in Vectashield (with DAPI, Vector Labs) between two #1.5 coverslips using a 120μm spacer (Molecular Probes).

Primary antibodies used were Buc6 (1:500; Yenzym Antibodies LLC), αTubulin (1:1000, Merck), γTubulin (1:400, Sigma-Aldrich), εTub (1:200, Sigma-Aldrich), Phospho-Stat3 (Tyr705) (1:200; cell signaling), Phospho-Stathmin (Ser16) (1:200; cell signaling). Secondary antibodies used were Alexa Fluor 488 and 594 (1:500; Molecular Probes). Vital dyes used were: DiOC6 (1:5000, Molecular Probes), Mitotracker (1:2000, Molecular Probes).

HCR-fluorescent in situ hybridization

HCR-FISH was performed according to the manufacturer protocol (Molecular Instruments) and was adjusted for ovaries. Briefly, cultured ovaries were rehydrated and washed 5 x 5 min and 3 x 20 min with PBST. Ovaries were then washed 5 min with probe hybridization buffer at RT and pre-hybridized with 100 μl probe hybridization buffer for 30 min at 37⁰, followed by hybridization with probes of interest for O.N at 37⁰C. Ovaries were washed 4 x 15 min with probe wash buffer at 37⁰C and then 3 x 15 min with 5X SSCT at RT. Ovaries were incubated in pre-amplification buffer for 30 min at RT, and then for O.N (in the dark) at RT with amplifying solution containing hairpins. Ovaries were washed 5 x SSCT for 2 x 5 min, 2 x 30 min, and 1 x min, and counterstained for DAPI and DIOC6 and mounted in ProLong Gold antifade mounting media. Probes used: foxl2l, rec8a, dmc1.

Ex vivo ovary culture

Preparation of ovary extract

Ovary extracts were prepared as described in Bontems et al.,8 with modifications. Briefly, ovary extracts were prepared from whole zebrafish ovaries obtained 8 months post-fertilization. The ovaries were disrupted in a medium composed of 60% Leibovitz's L-15 and 40% Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS) by performing three freeze-thaw cycles using liquid nitrogen. Samples were centrifuged at maximum speed (20,000 x g) for 30 minutes at 4°C. The clear supernatant was collected and stored at -80°C at a concentration of one ovary per milliliter in the same 60% L-15/40% HBSS medium.

Preparation of the culture dishes

Culture dishes were prepared under sterile tissue culture conditions. High glass-bottom 35-mm dishes were coated with poly-D-lysine (PDL) and incubated overnight at room temperature. Dishes were then washed sequentially with 1× PBS, followed by three washes with double-distilled water (ddH₂O). Dishes were further incubated for 2 hours before use. Microseal spacers were cut to size and placed on the prepared plates.

Preparation of the culture medium

Basic ovary culture medium (BOCM) contained ovary extract (see preparation of ovary extract), fetal bovine serum (FBS, 10%; Sigma), HEPES buffer solution (20 μM; Gibco), L-alanyl-L-glutamine (2 mM; Sigma), penicillin-streptomycin-amphotericin B solution (1%; Sigma), fish serum (1%; Oncorhynchus mykiss serum, purchased from Kibbutz Dan), and insulin-transferrin-selenium (ITS, 1%; Sigma), in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) high-glucose (Sartorius). Complete ovary culture medium 1 (COCM1) is identical to BOCM, except it also contains 17α,20β-dihydroxy-4-pregnen-3-one (DHP; Cayman Chemical, #16146) at final concentrations of 1 ng ng/mL, and estradiol-17β (E2; Sigma-Aldrich, #E2758) at final concentrations of 1 ng ng/mL. Complete ovary culture medium 2 (COCM2) is identical to BOCM, except it also contains DHP at final concentrations of 1 ng ng/mL.

Ovaries were cultured under a 5% CO₂ atmosphere at 28 °C, and culture medium was refreshed every 2 days over the course of culture. At the end of experiments cultured ovaries were fixed for analyses as above.

EdU pulse-chase and click chemistry

Ovaries were cultured and pulse-labeled with EdU overnight, washed by replacing culture medium, cultured for desired period of times per experiment, and were fixed post-culture. Detection of EdU incorporation into DNA was performed using the iClick EdU Andy Fluor 594 Imaging Kit (ABP Biosciences, #A005). Ovaries were washed in 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS (pH 7.4) for 10 minutes, then washed 4 × 20 minutes in PBT (0.5% Triton® X-100 in PBS). An additional 10-minute wash in 3% BSA was performed before adding the iClick reaction cocktail. The reaction cocktail consisted of 430 μL of iClick EdU Reaction Buffer, 20 μL of CuSO₄, 1.5 μL of Andy Fluor 594 azide, and 50 μL of 1× Reaction Buffer Additive, for a total volume of 500 μL per sample. The cocktail was added to each plate, and ovaries were incubated for 1 hour. After incubation, ovaries were washed sequentially: 1 × 10 minutes in BSA, 2 × 5 minutes in PBT, and 2 × 5 minutes in PBS. For combination with HCR-FISH, the iClick reaction was performed, followed by 20 minutes fixation in 4% PFA at room temperature with rotation, and then the HCR protocol as described above.

Drug treatments in ex vivo zebrafish ovary culture

Ovaries in culture were treated with COCM1+2 containing 100 μM Pyrimethamine, or 50 nM Rapamycin, or 75 μM GDP366 or their combinations as described in the text, for 7 days. Ovaries were cultured and collected post-culture as described above.

Drug treatments in whole live zebrafish

Juvenile zebrafish at 4 wpf were used for drug treatments in whole living fish. The fish, measuring between 5 and 11 mm in standard length (SL) were kept in fish system water containing one of the following treatments: DMSO, or 100 μM Pyrimethamine (Sigma-Aldrich #46706). The treatments were administered in alternating 2-days cycles of treatment and rest (fish water without DMSO or Pyrimethamine), over the course of two weeks. Fish were normally fed as per our routine husbandry conditions and their water were manually replaced after each feeding with fish water containing either DMSO or Pyrimethamine in a treatment cycle or plane fish water in a rest cycle. At the end of experiments fish SL was measured, and ovaries were collected as described above.

Confocal microscopy

Images were acquired on a Zeiss LSM 880 confocal microscope using a 40X lense. The acquisition setting was set across samples and experiments to: XY resolution=1104x1104 pixels, 12-bit, 2x sampling averaging, pixel dwell time=0.59sec, zoom=0.8X, pinhole adjusted to 1.1μm of Z thickness, increments between images in stacks were 0.53μm, laser power and gain were set in an antibody-dependent manner to 4-9% and 400-650, respectively, and below saturation condition. Unless otherwise noted, shown images are partial Sum Z-projection. Acquired images were not manipulated in a non-linear manner, and only contrast/brightness and region of interest (ROI) were adjusted in Fiji. All Figures were made using Adobe Photoshop CC 2014 and BioRender.com, in compliance with BioRender's terms of use.

Three-dimensional segmentation

Confocal raw data images of entire z-stacks of oocytes were extracted from whole ovary images and imported to IMARIS. Nuclei, centrosomes and Buc condensates were segmented in 3D using blend volume rendering mode, and signal brightness and object transparency were slightly and linearly adjusted in all channels to optimize visualization. Animation frames were made using the key frame animation tool.

Quantification of microtubule phenotypes

Quantification of microtubule phenotypes was done by measuring their signal intensities in confocal images of oocytes per stage under the different experimental conditions. A measurement line was drawn in the cytoplasm. To standardize measurements between oocyte stages and sizes, the line length was set as a 1/3 of the oocyte diameter. Signal intensities were measured along the line, normalized by R studio using the min-max normalization plug-in, and plotted in R studio. Peaks in the plots of normalized signals along the measurement line correspond to microtubule cables/bundles crossing the line, as expected.

Bioinformatic analysis of oocyte transcriptomic data

Analysis of the RNAseq data was performed as in Bogoch et al.6 The canonical pathway analysis for genes enriched in different clusters was performed using the Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA) suite (QIAGEN Inc.). Heatmaps of the expression levels of the genes associated with the different pathways were created using Clustvis.80

RNA extraction and RT-qPCT

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR were performed as in Ahmad et al.33 In brief, RNA was extracted from 50 juvenile ovaries per genotype using TRI-reagent®, followed by phenol;chloroform extraction and precipitation, DNAaseI reaction and additional phenol;chloroform extraction and precipitation. cDNA was generated using random hexamer primers and the Superscript IV Reverse Transcriptase kit as per the manufacturer instructions. cDNA was then used in PCR amplification followed by qPCR, using a StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR machine (Applied Biosystems) and SYBRgreen. Results were normalized to β-actin as loading control and to ddx4 as marker of germ cells as in Ahmad et al.,33 and fold-change to wt was calculated as ΔΔCt. All reactions were performed in triplicates.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All statistical analysis and data plotting were performed using the GraphPad Prism 7 software. Datasets were tested with two-way ANOVA or two-tailed unpaired t-test. In the figures, P values are indicated by asterisks as follows: ∗P < 0.05, ∗∗P < 0.01, ∗∗∗P < 0.001, and ∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001. ns, not significant at P > 0.05).
