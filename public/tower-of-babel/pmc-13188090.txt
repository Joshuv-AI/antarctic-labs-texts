Metal-free interconnected knotless anchor fixation for arthroscopic posterior glenoid bone block augmentation with subscapularis transfer in a professional soccer goalkeeper: a case report
Authors: Shorash Kawa Shaheen, Ralf Doyscher, Markus Scheibel
Published: 2026

Introduction

Posterior glenohumeral instability with posterior glenoid bone loss is uncommon and remains challenging in high-demand athletes, particularly when bipolar pathology is present.26,29 Arthroscopic posterior bone block augmentation traditionally relied on screw fixation, which can be associated with implant prominence, irritation, chondral risk, and reoperation for hardware removal.11,17,25 Metal-free fixation strategies using interconnected sutures with knotless polyetheretherketone (PEEK) anchors have been developed to provide controlled reduction and compression while avoiding screw-related morbidity.4,18 Recently, Scheibel et al described an all-arthroscopic posterior glenoid bone block stabilization using a tricortical ipsilateral scapular spine autograft and an interconnected knotless PEEK anchor construct.24 Building on this concept, the present case report describes the application of the same interconnected, metal-free, screwless fixation principle using an iliac crest autograft in an elite professional soccer goalkeeper. In contrast to the previously reported technique, concomitant humeral-sided bipolar pathology was additionally treated with an arthroscopic subscapularis transfer for an engaging reverse Hill–Sachs lesion.

Case report

A professional male soccer goalkeeper presented with recurrent posterior instability of the nondominant shoulder, including 3 traumatic posterior dislocations, the most recent occurring one month before surgery. He reported pain, apprehension in flexion and internal rotation, and inability to train or compete at his preinjury level. Pre-operative range of motion was forward flexion 170°, abduction 170°, internal rotation to Th8, and external rotation 70°. On examination, posterior apprehension was present, and the Jerk and Kim tests reproduced symptoms.26,35 Neurovascular status was normal, and there was no generalized joint hyperlaxity. Plain radiographs excluded acute fracture. Computed tomography (CT) with 3-dimensional reconstruction using the best-fit-circle method demonstrated posterior glenoid bone loss of 17.4% with 22° posterior glenoid tilt (Fig. 1A).5,27,34 Magnetic resonance imaging showed a posterior capsulolabral lesion and a concomitant ventral reverse Hill-Sachs defect (Fig. 2). Technique description

The patient was positioned in right lateral decubitus, and the ipsilateral iliac crest was prepared and draped. A standard posterior portal was used for diagnostic arthroscopy. Arthroscopy demonstrated a pronounced posterior erosive glenoid defect with a posterior capsulolabral lesion and a markedly thin and insufficient posterior capsule (Fig. 3A). A ventral reverse Hill–Sachs lesion was present. The rotator cuff was intact, the biceps tendon was unremarkable, and no superior labrum anterior-to-posterior lesion was identified. An anteroinferior and an anterosuperior portal were established. The posterior capsulolabral complex was mobilized from the posterior glenoid rim, and the posterior glenoid circumference as well as the scapular neck were prepared to a flat bleeding surface (Fig. 3B). The donor-site defect was filled with a structural iliac crest allograft and closed in layers. A deep posterior portal was created, and the infraspinatus was bluntly spread. Two 4.5-mm knotless anchors were inserted into the posterior glenoid neck and scapular neck with approximately 7 mm medial offset from the glenoid rim and 10 mm spacing (Fig. 3C and D). The graft was prepared with 2 drill holes using a 2.0-mm Kirschner wire (K-wire), and the suture limbs were passed through the graft (Fig. 3E). The deep posterior portal was slightly enlarged for graft insertion after a positive index finger test, as previously described for arthroscopic anterior glenoid bone grafting.1 The graft was then introduced arthroscopically, and the sutures were interconnected and tensioned using a tensioner to achieve stable compression of the graft against the posterior glenoid and scapular neck (Fig. 3F). The capsule was then pierced deep posteroinferiorly, a FiberWire loop was placed, and the labrum was reinserted deep anteroinferiorly at approximately the 5 o'clock position using a 2.9-mm PushLock anchor placed in the native glenoid rim. To further cover the graft, an ICONIX anchor was placed in a similar position, and the posterior labrum was secured with mattress sutures (Fig. 3G). More cranially, the labrum was secured again to the native glenoid with a further 2.9-mm anchor and a FiberWire loop posterosuperiorly, achieving a stable posterior reconstruction with capsulolabral advancement over the graft (Fig. 3H). Because slight residual posterior translation persisted, a ventral remplissage was performed. After freshening the ventral defect, 2 double-loaded BioComposite Corkscrew anchors were inserted into the defect, and 4 mattress sutures were passed transtendinously through the subscapularis tendon and tied, resulting in complete defect coverage (Fig. 3I-L). The arthroscopic portals were closed in accordance with standard practice.

Outcomes and follow-up

Follow-ups at 6 weeks, 3 months, 6 months, and beyond 2 years showed clinical improvement without recurrent instability. Post-operative CT obtained within the first post-operative week confirmed anatomic graft placement flush with the posterior glenoid rim (Fig. 1B). The graft measured approximately 24 × 15 × 10 mm. The shoulder was immobilized in a neutral-position brace for 6 weeks, with immediate wrist and elbow motion and passive shoulder mobilization initiated at 3 weeks post-operatively, followed by progressive shoulder rehabilitation. Strengthening and sport-specific training were initiated after 3 months. At approximately 3 months post-operatively, the athlete reported minimal pain (visual analog scale 0–1/10), no subjective instability, and had resumed structured rehabilitation. He returned to full team training at 4 months and competitive match play at 6 months without relevant limitations and at his preinjury performance level. At the latest follow-up beyond 2 years, physical examination demonstrated symmetric stability without apprehension. Range of motion of the operated left shoulder was 120° of forward elevation and 120° of abduction, with 45° of external rotation and 50° of internal rotation at the side. At 90° of abduction, external and internal rotation were 45° and 40°, respectively. Strength testing was symmetric compared with the contralateral side, and there were no iliac crest donor-site complaints. At the latest follow-up, the athlete remained free of recurrent instability and continued playing professional soccer. Patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) included a Subjective Shoulder Value of 80%, a Western Ontario Shoulder Instability Index of 83.3% (35/210) , a Rowe score of 85/100, and a Constant Murley score of 87 points.6,33 Advanced imaging demonstrated graft consolidation with favorable remodeling and maintained graft position, without implant-related complications and without magnetic resonance imaging signs of clinically relevant cartilage damage or osteoarthritis (Fig. 1C).12,31

Discussion

This case demonstrates that an all-arthroscopic, metal-free, screwless “interconnected” knotless PEEK anchor construct can provide reliable fixation for posterior glenoid bone-block augmentation in an elite overhead and contact athlete, while simultaneously enabling a comprehensive bipolar reconstruction strategy. Posterior instability with relevant posterior glenoid bone loss remains uncommon and diagnostically and therapeutically demanding, particularly in high-demand athletes in whom subtle residual translation may be clinically unacceptable.8,15 In this setting, the primary surgical goal is maximal stability to allow unrestricted performance at the professional level, even if this entails a small, clinically tolerable reduction in range of motion.26

Arthroscopic posterior bone-block augmentation has traditionally relied on screw-based fixation.30 Although screws can generate strong compression, multiple clinical series and reviews have highlighted implant-related concerns such as prominence and irritation, chondral risk, and reoperations for hardware removal.24 These problems may be amplified if graft remodeling or partial resorption changes implant position over time.7 These concerns have driven the development of alternative fixation concepts. Interconnected suture constructs with knotless anchors aim to combine controlled graft reduction, compression, and reproducible seating with the potential benefit of avoiding metal-related complications.3 In the present case, early post-operative CT confirmed flush anatomic positioning of the bone block, and advanced imaging beyond two years demonstrated consolidation and favorable remodeling without implant-related complications, supporting the biological compatibility of a screwless, metal-free construct in a high-load environment. Graft selection for posterior augmentation remains debated.21 A recent technical note described the same interconnected anchor concept using a tricortical scapular spine autograft.24 The present report extends that concept by demonstrating feasibility with a bicortical iliac crest autograft in a professional goalkeeper. For this specific athlete profile, preservation of periscapular muscle function and scapular kinematics is critical for repetitive overhead and collision mechanisms inherent to goalkeeping.10,13 Therefore, an iliac crest graft was selected to avoid any sport-specific compromise of scapular spine integrity or periscapular attachments while providing predictable bone stock and reliable biology.14 Autograft was preferred over allograft because it provides immediate structural bone and avoids concerns regarding graft incorporation in a high-demand athlete.28 Although iliac crest harvest is associated with donor-site morbidity in some patients, no donor-site complaints were reported at the latest follow-up in this case, and the donor-site defect was managed with structural allograft.2,9

A second key aspect of this case is the management of bipolar pathology. Posterior glenoid bone loss and posterior capsulolabral insufficiency were accompanied by an engaging reverse Hill–Sachs lesion. Treating only the glenoid-sided deficiency risks leaving a residual engagement mechanism, particularly in high-demand athletes with combined translational and rotational stresses.15,32 Consistent with the principle of addressing both sides of the joint when instability is multifactorial, a concomitant subscapularis transfer (modified McLaughlin concept) was performed to fill the humeral defect and reduce engagement risk.16,29 This combined approach allowed the surgeon to pursue maximal stability through a fully arthroscopic approach without introducing metallic implants.

Clinically, the post-operative course reflects the expected stability–motion trade-off after bone augmentation and capsular advancement.33 At beyond two years, the patient demonstrated stable, symmetric apprehension-free shoulders but retained a mild motion deficit. This reduction primarily affected forward elevation, abduction, and rotational range of motion, particularly external rotation in abduction. Importantly, functional performance was preserved.

This residual motion loss was considered clinically acceptable in the present case, given the primary goal of maximizing stability in a professional goalkeeper with relevant bipolar pathology. It was likely multifactorial and may have been related to the combination of posterior bone block augmentation, capsulolabral advancement, and subscapularis transfer. In particular, some loss of external rotation may have been expected after remplissage-type defect coverage. The athlete returned to full team training at approximately 4 months and to competitive match play at approximately 6 months and continued to play professional soccer without recurrent instability. PROMs beyond two years (Subjective Shoulder Value 80%, Western Ontario Shoulder Instability Index 83.3% (35/210), Rowe 85/100, and Constant Murley 87) support that the residual motion loss did not translate into relevant functional limitation for elite sport participation.6,19,23

Generalizability may be limited by the elite-athlete setting with high rehabilitation compliance and specialized care, and durability beyond two years remains uncertain, including the risk of late graft remodeling, partial resorption, and progressive degenerative changes.

This report has limitations inherent to a single-case design and lacks comparative data against screw fixation or alternative graft sources. In addition, the contribution of each procedure component (bone block fixation, capsulolabral shift, and subscapularis transfer) cannot be isolated. Nevertheless, the combination of detailed operative documentation, return-to-sport timeline in an elite athlete, objective clinical examination, PROMs, and cross-sectional imaging beyond two years provides clinically relevant evidence that the interconnected knotless, metal-free fixation strategy can be successfully applied to posterior bone-block augmentation with concomitant humeral-sided defect management in a high-demand setting.

Conclusion

Posterior shoulder instability with relevant posterior glenoid bone loss and concomitant reverse Hill–Sachs pathology is uncommon and surgically demanding in elite athletes. In this professional soccer goalkeeper, an all-arthroscopic posterior glenoid bone-block augmentation using a bicortical iliac crest autograft fixed with a metal-free “interconnected” knotless PEEK anchor construct, combined with posterior capsulolabral repair, capsular shift, and arthroscopic subscapularis transfer, restored stability and enabled return to professional match play within 6 months. Beyond 2 years, the patient remained recurrence-free with graft consolidation and favorable remodeling, while only mild motion restriction persisted.
