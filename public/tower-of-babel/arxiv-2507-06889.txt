Free-surface Euler equations with density variations, and shallow-water limit
Théo Fradin

Abstract
. In this paper we study the well-posedness in Sobolev spaces of the incompressible Euler equations in an infinite strip delimited from below by a non-flat bottom and from above by a free-surface. We allow the presence of vorticity and density variations, and in these regards the present system is an extension of the well-studied water waves equations. When the bottom is flat and with no density variations (but when the flow is not necessarily irrotational), our study provides an alternative proof of the already known large-time well-posedness results for the water waves equations. Our main contribution is that we allow for the presence of density variations, while also keeping track of the dependency in the shallow water parameter. This allows us to justify the convergence from the free-surface Euler equations towards the non-linear shallow water equations in this setting. Using an already established large time existence result for these latter equations, we also prove the existence of the free-surface Euler equations on a logarithmic time-scale, in a suitable regime.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Physical setting

Let
\Omega_{t}
be the domain of the fluid, delimited from below by an non-dimensional non-flat bottom
-1+\beta b
where
b:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is a smooth function such that
-1+\beta b
is negative and bounded from below, and
\beta\geq 0
. Here,
d\in\{1,2\}
denotes the horizontal dimension. The domain of the fluid is also delimited from above by a free surface
\epsilon\eta_{0}
of size
\epsilon
not necessarily small, namely

(1.1)

\immediate\immediate\Omega_{t}:=\{(x,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R},-1+\beta b(x)<z<\epsilon\eta_{0}\}.

We denote by
\epsilon{\bf U}:=(\epsilon V,\epsilon w)^{T}
the total velocity of the fluid, with
V
its horizontal component and
w
its vertical one, both of size
\epsilon
. We consider a fluid with a density given by
\rho_{\textrm{total}}(t,x,z):=\underline{\rho}+\epsilon\delta\rho(t,x,z)
with
\underline{\rho}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
a constant reference density and
\delta\geq 0
. Although the size of the velocity and free-surface perturbations is
\epsilon
, we consider a density perturbation of size
\epsilon\delta
. Both
\epsilon
and
\delta
can be taken large in our main result (see however Remark
1.1
). The incompressible Euler equations are completed with the mass conservation equation, and in non-dimensional form read

(1.2)

\immediate\immediate\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}V+\epsilon{\bf U}\cdot\nabla_{x,z}V+\frac{1}{\underline{\rho}+\epsilon\delta\rho}\nabla P+\frac{g\underline{\rho}}{\underline{\rho}+\epsilon\delta\rho}\nabla\eta_{0}&=0,\\
\mu\left(\partial_{t}w+\epsilon{\bf U}\cdot\nabla_{x,z}w\right)+\frac{1}{\underline{\rho}+\epsilon\delta\rho}\partial_{z}P+g\delta\frac{\rho}{\underline{\rho}+\epsilon\delta\rho}&=0,\\
\partial_{t}\rho+\epsilon{\bf U}\cdot\nabla_{x,z}\rho&=0,\\
\nabla\cdot V+\partial_{z}w&=0,\end{aligned}\right.\qquad\text{ in }\Omega_{t};

here,
P
denotes the perturbation of the hydrostatic pressure at equilibrium, given by
g\underline{\rho}(\epsilon\eta_{0}-z)
. Namely, if
P_{\textrm{total}}
denotes the total pressure of the fluid,
P_{\textrm{total}}=g(z-\epsilon\eta_{0})+\epsilon P
. See Subsection
3.1
for the precise definition of
P
from the unknowns
V,w,\rho
and
\eta_{0}
. The parameter
\mu\in(0,1]
is the shallow water parameter, and is defined as the square of the ratio between characteristic vertical and horizontal scales. In many applications, it is small with respect to
1
, therefore in this paper we first conduct a study on (
1.2
) that holds uniformly in
\mu
, so that we can then examine the limit
\mu\to 0
; see for instance
[
Val17
,
Duc22
,
Lan20
]
for an account on numerous shallow water models, widely used to study oceanic flows.

The set of equations (
1.2
) is completed with boundary conditions

(1.3)

\immediate\immediate\left\{\begin{aligned} P_{|z=\epsilon\eta_{0}}&=0,\\
\partial_{t}\eta_{0}+\epsilon V_{|z=\epsilon\eta_{0}}\cdot\nabla\eta_{0}-w_{|z=\epsilon\eta_{0}}&=0,\\
{\bf N_{b}}\cdot{\bf U}_{|z=-1+\beta b}&=0,\end{aligned}\right.\qquad\text{in }[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d},

where
{\bf N_{b}}:=(-\beta\nabla^{T}b,1)^{T}
is the (upward) normal direction at the bottom. The first boundary condition in (
1.3
) is the continuity of the pressure at the surface with the atmospheric pressure, assumed to be constant and renormalized at
0
. The second boundary condition is the kinematic equation on the free-surface and the last one is the impermeability of the bottom.

In view of studying the initial value problem associated with these equations, we add the following initial data, that must satisfy the incompressibility condition in (
1.2
) and the first and third boundary conditions in (
1.3
):

(1.4)

\immediate\immediate\left\{\begin{aligned} {\bf U}_{|t=0}&={\bf U}_{\mathrm{in}}\qquad&\text{in }\Omega_{t},\\
\rho_{|t=0}&=\rho_{\mathrm{in}}\qquad&\text{in }\Omega_{t},\\
(\eta_{0})_{|t=0}&=(\eta_{0})_{\mathrm{in}}\qquad&\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{d}.\end{aligned}\right.

The aim of this paper is to study the initial boundary value problem on the system (
1.2
) with boundary conditions (
1.3
) and initial data (
1.4
) and obtain results that are uniform in
\mu
(see the statement of Theorem
A
in Subsection
1.2
below). This result then allows us to justify the persistence of the non-linear shallow water equation (
1.9
) in this setting, see the statement of Theorem
B
in Subsection
1.3
.

Let us now define the non-dimensionalized vorticity

\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\mu}:=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu}}\partial_{z}V^{\perp}-\sqrt{\mu}\nabla^{\perp}w\\
\nabla^{\perp}\cdot V\end{pmatrix}.

Taking the
(d+1)
-dimensional curl of the equations on
V
and
w
in (
1.2
) and dividing the first component by
\sqrt{\mu}
, we get

(1.5)

\immediate\immediate\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\mu}+\epsilon{\bf U}\cdot\nabla_{x,z}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\mu}+\epsilon\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\mu}^{[x]}\cdot\nabla\begin{pmatrix}V\\
\sqrt{\mu}w\end{pmatrix}+\epsilon\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\mu}^{[z]}\partial_{z}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu}}V\\
w\end{pmatrix}=\frac{\delta}{\sqrt{\mu}}{\bf F}\qquad\text{in }\Omega_{t}.

Here,
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\mu}^{[x]}
and
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\mu}^{[z]}
denote the horizontal and vertical components of the vorticity
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\mu}
. When
d=1
, (
1.5
) classically boils down to a scalar transport equation, although we do not use this particular structure in this study so that our result holds both when
d=1
and
d=2
. The source term
{\bf F}
stems from the density variations, namely

(1.6)

\immediate\immediate{\bf F}:=\begin{pmatrix}\nabla^{\perp}\left(\frac{\rho}{\underline{\rho}+\epsilon\delta\rho}\right)+\epsilon\partial_{z}\left(\frac{\rho}{\underline{\rho}+\epsilon\delta\rho}\right)\nabla^{\perp}\left(P-g\eta_{0}\right)-\epsilon\nabla_{x}^{\perp}\left(\frac{\rho}{\underline{\rho}+\epsilon\rho}\right)\partial_{z}P\\
\epsilon\sqrt{\mu}\nabla^{\perp}\left(\frac{\rho}{\underline{\rho}+\epsilon\delta\rho}\right)\cdot\nabla(P-g\eta_{0})\end{pmatrix}.

Setting in Eulerian coordinates.

In the presence of vorticity but without density variations, see the works
[
KT18
]
and
[
WZZZ21
]
, where the authors study the free-surface Euler equations with minimal regularity assumptions on the initial data, although without tracking the dependency in
\mu
. The author in
[

]
derives energy estimates for the Euler equations (

) with a non-flat bottom. He even treats the case of emerging bottom, that is enabling

for some

, improving the results in
[
SZ08a
,
SZ08b
,
SZ11
]
. See also
[
ZZ08
]
. However, these works do not keep track of the dependency of their estimates with respect to
\mu
.

In
[
CL15
]
, the authors derive a generalization of the Zakharov/Craig-Sulem formulation in the presence of vorticity variations, alongside an equation (namely, (
1.5
) with
\delta=0
) on the evolution of the vorticity, in the flat-bottom case. This formulation is extended to the case of a non-flat bottom and with Coriolis forcing in
[
Mel17
]
, where the energy estimates on the subsequent system are performed. These two results keep track of the dependency in
\mu
, and even perform the shallow water limit
\mu\to 0
. See also the survey
[
Lan20
]
. In
[
MRS23
]
, the incompressible limit for the compressible free-surface Navier-Stokes equations is considered. The authors in
[
MR17
]
study the free-surface Navier-Stokes equations (that is, adding viscosity in (

)), with no bottom boundary. Although the presence of viscosity changes several parts of the analysis, the limit of vanishing viscosity is studied.

In the presence of density variations, several studies have been conducted in the whole space
[
Dan10
]
or in more general domains
[
IT99
]
. In the case of a strip with a density stratification, with or without a free-surface, see
[
DLS20
]
,
[
BD24
]
and
[
Fra24
]
for the well-posedness theory. To the best of our knowledge, the present study is the first on the free-surface case with a non-flat bottom and density variations, keeping track of the dependency of the shallow water parameter
\mu
. To the best of our knowledge, it is also the first result on the persistence of the non-linear shallow water equations as an approximate model for the free-surface Euler equations with weak density variations.

Our motivation for performing the analysis directly on the free-surface Euler equations rather than on a Zakharov/Craig-Sulem type formulation as in
[
CL15
]
is three-fold. First, and most importantly, extending the Zakharov/Craig-Sulem formulation from
[
CL15
]
in the presence of density variations is not straightforward. Then, we believe that some parts of the analysis are more standard, namely the scheme of existence (see Subsection
3.5
) as well as the quasilinear structure (see Subsections
3.2
and
3.3
). Eventually, this allows us to compare the analysis with the one performed on similar systems: namely the Navier-Stokes equations (see for instance
[
MR17
]
) or the stratified Euler equations (see
[
DLS20
]
,
[
BD24
]
and
[
Fra24
]
).

1.2.
First result: well-posedness of (
1.2
)

The main result of this paper is a local well-posedness result on the system (
1.2
), stated in Theorem
A
. We state here a shorter version, for the sake of clarity. Let us first state the main assumptions sufficient for the following theorem to hold. Let
d\in\{1,2\}
be the horizontal dimension,
s_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
with
s_{0}>\frac{d+1}{2}
, and
s\in\mathbb{N}
with
s\geq s_{0}+2
be the regularity of the solution considered. Let
M>0
, we assume

(H.1)

\immediate\immediate\|b\|_{H^{s+\frac{3}{2}}}\leq M.

Let
M_{\mathrm{in}}>0
and
(V_{\mathrm{in}},w_{\mathrm{in}},\rho_{\mathrm{in}},(\eta_{0})_{\mathrm{in}})\in(H^{s})^{d+2}(\Omega_{0})\times H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfying the following assumptions. First, we make the non-cavitation assumption, namely

(H.2)

\immediate\immediate\underline{\rho}+\epsilon\delta\rho_{\mathrm{in}}\geq c_{*}>0.

We also assume that the depth of the fluid is bounded from below

(H.3)

\immediate\immediate 1-\beta b+\epsilon(\eta_{0})_{\mathrm{in}}\geq c_{*},

for some
c_{*}>0
, as well as the positivity of the Rayleigh-Taylor coefficient

(H.4)

\immediate\immediate\mathrm{a}\geq c_{*}>0,

defined as

(1.7)

\immediate\immediate\mathrm{a}:=g\underline{\rho}-\epsilon(\partial_{z}P)_{|z=\epsilon\eta_{0}}.

Here,
P
is defined from the initial data through (
3.1
).
These assumptions are classical in this setting, see for instance
[
CL15
]
. Note however the results in
[
dP19
]
which hold without (
H.3
), and a discussion about (
H.4
) for the Navier-Stokes equation in the vanishing viscosity limit in
[
MR17
]
.

We furthermore assume

(H.5)

\immediate\immediate\|(V_{\mathrm{in}},\sqrt{\mu}w_{\mathrm{in}},\sqrt{\mu}\rho_{\mathrm{in}})\|_{H^{s}(\Omega_{0})}+|(\eta_{0})_{\mathrm{in}}|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq M_{\mathrm{in}};

the Sobolev spaces
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
as well as
H^{s}(\Omega_{t})
are defined in (
1.11
) and (
1.12
). We also assume that the initial (non-dimensional) vorticity
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\mu}{}_{\mathrm{in}}
defined from
(V_{\mathrm{in}},w_{\mathrm{in}})
through (
2.8
) satisfies

(H.6)

\immediate\immediate\|\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\mu}{}_{\mathrm{in}}\|_{H^{s}(\Omega_{0})}\leq M_{\mathrm{in}}.

Recall the notation
a\vee b:=\max(a,b)
. We can now make a rough statement of Theorem
A
.

Theorem
A

(Rough statement)
.

Let
\mu\in(0,1],

\epsilon,\beta,\delta\in[0,1]
. Let
(V_{\mathrm{in}},w_{\mathrm{in}},\rho_{\mathrm{in}}(\eta_{0})_{\mathrm{in}})\in(H^{s})^{d+2}(\Omega_{0})\times H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfying (
H.1
), (
H.2
), (
H.3
), (
H.4
), (
H.5
), (
H.6
), the first and third boundary conditions in (
1.3
) and the incompressibility condition in (
1.2
). Then there exists
T>0
independent of
\beta
,
\epsilon
,
\mu
and
\delta
such that there exists a unique solution
(V,w,\rho,\eta_{0})\in C^{0}([0,\frac{T}{\epsilon\vee\beta\vee\frac{\delta}{\mu}}],H^{s}(\Omega_{t})^{d+2}\times H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
to (
1.2
) satisfying the boundary conditions (
1.3
) and the initial condition (
1.4
).

Remark 1.1
.

The constant
T
in the previous theorem is independent of
\beta,\epsilon,\mu,\delta
. However, it may depend on the constants defined in the assumptions, namely
M,c_{*},M_{\mathrm{in}}
, as well as
d,s_{0}
and
s
.

Note that the time of existence in the previous theorem can be made independent of
\mu
by assuming small density variations, i.e.
\delta\leq\mu
.

Remark 1.2
.

The minimal Sobolev regularity for Theorem
A
to hold is
s_{0}+2
. In the case of the Euler equations without a free boundary, the minimal regularity obtained from the energy method is
s_{0}+1
. In the present study, the non-linear interactions between the free surface and the other unknowns yield the minimal regularity
s_{0}+2
. See however the works
[
SZ08a
]
,
[
SZ08b
]
,
[
SZ11
]
,
[
WZZZ21
]
,
[
KT18
]
,
[
dP19
]
that manage to lower the minimal regularity, though with a time of existence that is not independent of the shallow-water parameter
\mu
.

Remark 1.3
.

In the irrotational case
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\mu}=0
, in the shallow-water limit
\mu=0
, one gets the columnar motion property
\partial_{z}V=0
. Then, the non-linear shallow water equations are an approximate model of the water waves equations, with precision
O(\mu)
, see
[
Lan13
]
and references within.

The setting in which the initial non-dimensional vorticity is of size
1
(see (
H.6
)) is the setting studied in
[
CL15
]
and
[
Mel17
]
. It roughly states that
\partial_{z}V\sim\sqrt{\mu}
, and as such, it is a relaxation on the columnar motion assumption
\partial_{z}V=0
in the shallow water setting (i.e.
\mu=0
). We use the vorticity equation (
1.5
) in this study to show that the assumption (
H.6
) is propagated for for positive times, at least in the regime
\delta\leq\mu
of small density variations, and on the time-scale considered in Theorem
A
. This point is strongly used to control some terms in the analysis: namely, the vertical advection term in (
1.2
) is

(1.8)

\immediate\immediate\epsilon\sqrt{\mu}w\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu}}\partial_{z}V,

where we have a uniform control on
\sqrt{\mu}w
, but not on
w
(see (
3.22
)). The singularity
1/\sqrt{\mu}
that appears is dealt with in the following analysis by using
\partial_{z}V\sim\sqrt{\mu}
from (
H.6
), see more precisely the proof of Proposition
3.5
.

In the setting
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\mu}{}_{\mathrm{in}}=O(1)
, the non-linear shallow water equations are an approximate model of the free-surface Euler equations with precision
O(\sqrt{\mu})
. This slower rate of convergence was already observed in
[
CL15
]
and is due to the presence of vorticity. Theorem
B
shows that it extends to the case of small density variations.

In the context of stratified fluids (that is, when the reference density
\underline{\rho}
is not assumed to be constant but strictly decreasing with respect to
z
), the equation on the vorticity does not appear to help propagate the condition
\partial_{z}V\sim\sqrt{\mu}
from (
H.6
). This is because the density variations at equilibrium cannot be taken small with respect to
\mu
, as opposed to the present case with
\delta\leq\mu
. Because the condition
\partial_{z}V\sim\sqrt{\mu}
cannot be propagated in the stratified case,
[
DLS20
]
and
[
Fra24
]
do not assume this condition to hold, and the singularity in (
1.8
) is dealt with the additional assumption
\epsilon\leq\sqrt{\mu}
. Although the present analysis and the one for the stratified case share a lot of similarities, this is a major discrepancy.

Remark 1.4
.

Solving the water waves equations in the Zakharov/Craig-Sulem formulation is an initial value problem on the horizontal space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(see
[
Lan13
]
and references within). On the other hand, both the present study on (
1.2
) or
[
CL15
]
on the free-surface euler equations in the Zakharov/Craig-Sulem formulation treat initial boundary value problems, in the free-surface domain
\Omega_{t}
, as the evolution of the vorticity does not reduce to an equation defined on the free surface. The approximation scheme to construct a solution is then more involved, see for instance
[
BGS06
]
for an global picture on hyperbolic initial boundary value problems. The strategy used in
[
CL15
]
relies on a suitable vertical regularization, despite the boundaries. The proof of Theorem
A
is slightly different, as we use semi-Lagrangian coordinates to write a reformulation of the Euler equations without vertical advection, see (
3.66
). Both strategies are adapted to the case where the flow is tangent to the boundaries.

Remark 1.5
.

In the absence of topographic variations (
\beta=0
) and no density variations (
\delta=0
), the time of existence in Theorem
A
becomes
\frac{T}{\epsilon}
, which is sometimes called a large time, because it grows as the size
\epsilon
of the deviation from the equilibrium decreases. With large topographic variations, the same time of existence
\frac{T}{\epsilon\vee\beta}
as in Theorem
A
holds for the water waves equations, see for instance
[
Lan13
]
. Whether a large-time existence result (i.e. on
[0,\frac{T}{\epsilon}]
) holds in the presence of large topographic variations is an open question, both for the water waves equations or for the present system (
1.2
), see also Remark
3.9
. However, in the case of the water waves equations with surface tension and large topographic variations, the large time existence holds, see
[
MG17
]
. Such a large time existence result also holds for the non-linear shallow water equations with large topographic variations, see
[
BM10
]
.

1.3.
Second result: shallow water limit

As the time of existence in Theorem
A
is bounded from below uniformly with respect to
\mu
in the case of small density variations (
\delta\leq\mu
), it defines a family of solutions
(V,w,\rho,\eta_{0})_{\mu>0}
in
C^{0}([0,\frac{T}{\epsilon\vee\beta\vee\frac{\delta}{\mu}}[,H^{s}(\Omega_{t})^{d+2}\times H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
, parameterized by
\mu>0
. A natural question is then to study its limit as
\mu\to 0
. The candidate for the limit is the solution of the non-linear shallow water (or Saint-Venant) equations, that read

(1.9)

\immediate\immediate\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}\eta_{\mathrm{sw}}+\nabla\cdot\left((1-\beta b+\epsilon\eta_{\mathrm{sw}})V_{\mathrm{sw}}\right)&=0,\\
\partial_{t}V_{\mathrm{sw}}+\epsilon V_{\mathrm{sw}}\cdot\nabla V_{\mathrm{sw}}+g\nabla\eta_{\mathrm{sw}}&=0,\\
\end{aligned}\right.\qquad\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{d};

here,
V_{\mathrm{sw}}
actually denotes the vertically averaged horizontal velocity, and
\eta_{\mathrm{sw}}
is the function whose graph is the free-surface. In particular, (
1.9
) does not take into account density variations. See
[
Lan13
]
and
[
Duc22
]
and references within for an account on this system, and
[
Lan20
]
where the vorticity is considered. We now make a rough statement of the convergence result, as the precise statement of Theorem
B
requires the introduction of some notations.

Theorem
B
(Rough statement)
.

If the solution
(V,w,\rho,\eta_{0})
of (
1.2
) for
\mu>0
given by Theorem
A
and the solution
(V_{\mathrm{sw}},\eta_{\mathrm{sw}})
are close initially (more precisely, the norm of their difference in some functional space is small with respect to
\mu
; see (
H.17
)), then they remain close on the time interval
[0,\frac{T}{\epsilon\vee\beta\vee\frac{\delta}{\mu}}]
given by Theorem
A
.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first justification of the non-linear shallow water equations, in the presence of topography and density variations, from the free-surface Euler equations.

Remark 1.6
.

The convergence from the water waves equation towards the non-linear shallow water equations was already established in the irrotational case with topography (see
[
Lan13
]
and references within), in the flat-bottom case with vorticity (see
[
CL15
]
) and in the case with vorticity and topographic variations (as well as Coriolis forcing) in
[
Mel17
]
.

Remark 1.7
.

The present result proves that the non-linear shallow water equations (
1.9
) are an approximate model for the free-surface Euler equations (
1.2
) with precision
O(\sqrt{\mu})
; see Remark
1.3
for a note on the precision. It takes place in the regime where the initial non-dimensional vorticity is of size
O(1)
, which can be seen as a relaxation of the columnar motion property (
\partial_{z}V\neq 0
). It would be of interest to prove a well-posedness result in the same fashion as Theorem
A
outside of the case
\partial_{z}V\sim\sqrt{\mu}
. Then the solution of (
1.2
) is expected to be described at first order in
\mu
by the solution of the hydrostatic Euler equations (see for instance
[
MW12
]
for the flat-surface, flat-bottom case in dimension 2, and
[
Lan20
]
for a different viewpoint).

Remark 1.8
.

With large topographic variations (
\beta=1
) and small density variations (
\delta\leq\mu
), Theorem
A
only proves the existence of the solution to (
1.2
) on
[0,T]
, and on this time interval, it remains close to the solution of the non-linear shallow water system (
1.9
). However, it was shown in
[
BM10
]
that, despite large topographic variations, the solution of (
1.9
) exists on the time interval
[0,\frac{T}{\epsilon}]
. This might suggest that it is possible to prove the large time existence (i.e. on a time interval
[0,\frac{T}{\epsilon}]
) of the solution of the water waves equations and of (
1.2
). Nonetheless, these questions remain, to the best of our knowledge, open.

Set
\beta=1
and
\delta=0
for the sake of clarity. As stated in Remark
1.8
, the existence time for the solution of (
1.2
) is
T
, whereas it is
\frac{T}{\epsilon}
for (

) according to
[
BM10
]
, and thus is much larger in the shallow water case. We use the latter result in subsection

to improve the former, in the regime
\mu\leq\epsilon
and with well-prepared data, to get an existence time of order
\log(\frac{1}{\epsilon})
; we give a rough statement of this result, that is a corollary of Theorem

. See Corollary

for the precise statement.

Corollary
C
(Rough statement)
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
A
and moreover if the initial data (

) is close (with respect to
\mu
) to an admissible initial data for the non-linear shallow water equations (
1.9
) and in the regime
\mu\leq\epsilon
, then the solution
(V,w,\rho,\eta_{0})

Plan of the paper

\immediate\immediate\text{or }\quad\begin{aligned} &\limsup\limits_{t\to T_{\max}}\|(V,\sqrt{\mu}w,\sqrt{\mu}\rho)(t,\cdot)\|_{H^{s}(S_{r})}+|\eta_{0}(t,\cdot)|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+\|\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\mu}(t,\cdot)\|_{H^{s-1}(S)}=+\infty\\
&\liminf\limits_{t\to T_{\max}}\inf\limits_{(x,r)\in S}\mathrm{a}=0,\\
&\liminf\limits_{t\to T_{\max}}\inf\limits_{(x,r)\in S}(\underline{\rho}+\epsilon\delta\rho)=0,\\
&\liminf\limits_{t\to T_{\max}}\inf\limits_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}1-\beta b(x)+\epsilon\eta_{0}(t,x)=0.\\
\end{aligned}

We now show that the last two conditions cannot happen. The condition on the density cannot happen as
\rho
is transported by the fluid, therefore if (
H.2
) holds initially, it holds as long as
V
and
w
remain Lipschitz continuous. for the last alternative in (
3.83
), we can compute, thanks to the kinematic equation (
2.6
) on
\eta_{0}
and the divergence-free condition in (
2.5
):

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}(1-\beta b(x)+\epsilon\eta_{0}(t,x))

\displaystyle=\epsilon\partial_{t}\eta_{0}(t,x)=-V(t,x,0)\cdot\nabla\eta_{0}(t,x)+w(t,x,0)

\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{0}\partial_{r}(-V(t,x,r)\cdot\nabla(\underline{\eta}(x,r)+\epsilon\eta(t,x,r))+w(t,x,r))dr

\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{0}\left(-\nabla(\underline{\eta}+\epsilon\eta)\partial_{r}V+\partial_{r}w-V\nabla(\underline{h}+\epsilon h)\right)

\displaystyle=-\int_{-1}^{0}\nabla\cdot((\underline{h}+\epsilon h)V)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(1-\beta b+\epsilon\eta_{0})

\displaystyle=-\nabla\cdot((1-\beta b+\epsilon\eta_{0})\overline{V}),

where

\overline{V}(t,x):=\frac{1}{1-\beta b(x)+\epsilon\eta_{0}(t,x)}\int_{-1}^{0}(\underline{h}(x,r)+\epsilon h(t,x,r))V(t,x,r)dr

is the vertically averaged horizontal velocity. This is the conservation of the fluid height, and so if initially the fluid height is bounded from below (see Assumption (
H.3

remains Lipschitz continuous, by the characteristics method, and the blow-up criterion (

3.61

∎
