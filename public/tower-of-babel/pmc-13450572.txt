Chemical space visualization at scale: a survey of end-to-end pipelines and dataset-size archetypes
Authors: Maha M AlShammari, Moayad Alnammi, Moataz Ahmed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChemical space visualization supports exploration of high-dimensional molecular data by revealing patterns of similarity, diversity, and structure–property relationships. As chemical libraries expand from thousands to billions of compounds, practical visualization increasingly depends on pipelines that balance chemical meaning with computational and memory constraints. In this survey, we review 56 studies published between 2000 and 2025 and synthesize the end-to-end workflow of chemical space visualization across five core stages: dataset choice, molecular featurization, dimensionality reduction (DR), clustering, and evaluation. We quantify usage trends over time and relate method selection to dataset scale. Across the literature, fingerprints remain the dominant representation for large libraries due to their scalability, while recent studies increasingly incorporate fragments, SMILES-based encodings, and learned embeddings when richer signals are needed. DR practice shows a shift from PCA-centric baselines to neighborhood-preserving methods such as t-SNE and UMAP, with graph layout approaches like TMAP enabling visualization at extreme scale. Clustering shows the weakest convergence to a single standard: hierarchical methods, K-means, and SOM remain frequent choices, complemented by scalable summarization and domain-driven strategies (e.g., BIRCH/BitBIRCH and scaffold-based partitioning) when all-pairs similarity becomes prohibitive. Finally, we propose dataset-size-aware pipeline archetypes and identify open challenges, including inconsistent structure-aware evaluation, limited reproducibility reporting, and the need for scalable, chemically grounded methods for ultra-large libraries.Keywords: Chemical space visualization, Molecular representation, Fingerprints, Dimensionality reduction, Clustering, Scalability, Pipeline, Cheminformatics

Introduction

Chemical space denotes the set of all theoretically possible molecular structures and is vast even under conservative constraints; for example, the number of small organic molecules with up to 30 atoms has been estimated to lie between 1060 and 10100 [1]. The scale of this space makes systematic exploration a central challenge in drug discovery and materials design, where the practical goal is to search large collections of molecules, identify promising regions, and prioritize candidates for downstream validation [2]. This need is amplified by the rapid growth of modern chemical libraries that now reach tens of billions of compounds (e.g., ZINC22), increasing the demand for scalable computational methods that can organize and interpret high-dimensional molecular representations [3].

Chemical space visualization provides a practical way to support this exploration by constructing low-dimensional maps that reveal structure, similarity, and diversity patterns. Most visualization workflows follow three core steps. First, featurization converts molecular structures into numerical representations, including descriptors, fingerprints, graph-based encodings, Simplified Molecular Input Line Entry System (SMILES)-derived features [4], and learned embeddings [5]. Second, dimensionality reduction (DR) maps these high-dimensional representations into 2D or 3D spaces to enable visual inspection and comparative analysis of molecular neighborhoods [6–8]. Third, clustering groups molecules into chemically meaningful regions of the map, supporting tasks such as library summarization, series identification, and diversity analysis; common choices include K-means, hierarchical clustering, and self-organizing maps (SOM), with scalable alternatives (e.g., BIRCH/BitBIRCH) used when dataset size and compute constraints require it [9]. The quality of these groupings and projections is assessed using complementary evaluation signals, including structural metrics (e.g., Tanimoto similarity and scaffold analysis), statistical validity indices (e.g., Silhouette, Davies-Bouldin Index (DBI), Calinski-Harabasz Index (CHI), Adjusted Rand Index (ARI)), and projection-fidelity metrics that quantify neighborhood preservation in the low-dimensional map [10].

A number of reviews have discussed parts of this workflow, including early surveys of classical projection methods [1, 11], computational mapping tools for drug discovery [12], visual representations of chemical space spanning multiple method families [13], navigation of natural product chemical space [14], open-source visualization and chemical space analysis tools [15, 16], and a recent survey of DR algorithms and software for chemical space navigation [17]. Table 1 summarizes these representative reviews. However, these existing reviews typically focus on specific methods or application domains rather than examining the full visualization pipeline from featurization through DR and clustering. In particular, they rarely connect method selection to dataset scale and computational constraints in a systematic way.

In this work, we address these gaps by providing an integrative review of chemical space visualization workflows spanning datasets, featurization, DR, clustering, and evaluation. We analyze 56 studies published between 2000 and 2025 to (i) quantify usage trends over time, (ii) relate method choices to scalability constraints using computational and memory complexity summaries, and (iii) propose dataset-scale pipeline archetypes that translate literature trends into practical workflow templates. Together, these contributions provide a consolidated reference for selecting and justifying chemical space visualization methods under realistic data and compute regimes.

Review methodology

Our review methodology follows a structured approach consisting of four main stages: research questions, search strategy design, study selection, and data synthesis, and is guided by the Modular Chemical Space Visualization Pipeline illustrated in Fig. 1. The main goal of this study is to provide a comprehensive review of state-of-the-art approaches for chemical space visualization, with a particular focus on how DR and clustering techniques are integrated within modern computational pipelines.

Modular pipeline for chemical space visualization: an overview of the five core stages reviewed in this survey

The illustrated pipeline comprises five consecutive components: dataset selection, molecular featurization, DR, clustering, and evaluation metrics. Although the pipeline is presented as a sequence of conceptually distinct stages, some techniques span multiple stages. In particular, methods such as Self-Organizing Maps (SOM) and Generative Topographic Mapping (GTM) perform DR and implicit clustering simultaneously. In such methods, latent nodes or map units identify regions that can be interpreted as clusters, and these regions can be analyzed in terms of chemical property distributions or aggregated into higher-level superclusters. Consequently, the separation of DR and clustering in this review is organizational rather than methodological. Together, these components provide a basis for methodological comparison across studies. Search strategy

The search process was conducted in Scopus, Google Scholar, and ACS Publications for studies published between 2000 and the beginning of 2025, using the following search string:

("Chemical Space" OR "Large Chemical Space" OR "Compounds Database") AND ("Visualization" OR "Dimensionality Reduction" OR "Clustering" OR "Cheminformatics")

This period was selected due to the rise of public molecular databases (e.g., ChEMBL, PubChem, ZINC) and modern DR methods (e.g., t-SNE and Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP)) that emerged and were employed in the field after 2000. Therefore, the selected period might capture the evolution of computationally driven and large-scale chemical space visualization.

Study selection

We intentionally applied broad search queries to ensure comprehensive coverage, which retrieved a large number of studies requiring further filtering. To align these studies with our research questions, we identified the most relevant ones via a multi-stage selection process. This process started with screening the title, abstract, and content to ensure compatibility with the goals of the study. Studies were included if they explicitly addressed chemical space visualization or related workflows involving molecular representation, DR, or clustering. Preference was given to studies providing methodological descriptions, dataset information, and quantitative analysis related to chemical space exploration. Studies focused on unrelated applications (e.g., predictive QSAR modeling without visualization, or purely synthetic chemistry reports) were excluded. This filtering process ensured that the final set of selected studies directly contributed to the understanding of method choices and trends in chemical space visualization. These studies emphasize methodological clarity regarding datasets, featurization, and evaluation metrics. To account for both established and new trends, recent works and preprints introducing new approaches were also included. Overall, 56 studies met the inclusion criteria for detailed analysis.

Employed datasets in chemical space visualization

Chemical space visualization relies on datasets that differ widely in source and scale, which strongly influence the analysis and methods that are feasible. From the reviewed studies, four sources dominate: public repositories that pair structures with assay annotations (e.g., ChEMBL [69], PubChem [70], DrugBank [71]), very large on-demand purchasable libraries used to chart the breadth of accessible chemical space (e.g., ZINC [72] and make-on-demand collections), in-house high-throughput screening collections, and domain-specific collections (e.g., natural-product, metabolomics, and materials datasets). Across these sources, visualizations are used to characterize structural diversity and scaffold coverage, compare libraries, guide library design and prioritization, and benchmark DR and clustering pipelines on datasets ranging from thousands to billions of molecules. This section addresses RQ1 by summarizing the sources and scales of datasets used in the 56 reviewed studies.

Sources of datasets

Chemical space analyses commonly draw on well-established public resources such as ChEMBL [30, 35, 44, 46, 47, 51, 52, 56, 57, 66, 67], ZINC [3, 9, 24, 37, 46], PubChem [9, 35], and DrugBank [29, 63]. The dataset sizes reported in this subsection reflect those used in the cited studies at the time of their analysis and may differ from current database releases. ZINC, one of the largest virtual-screening resources, includes billions of purchasable molecules, with ZINC22 providing one billion compounds for large-scale interaction and diversity studies [9]. ChEMBL (1.56 million compounds) is central to structure-activity modeling and target-centric analyses [35]. PubChem hosts millions of molecular structures and annotations for structural and property-based analyses [9], while DrugBank offers curated data on approved and investigational drugs essential for visualizing therapeutic chemical space [29]. Specialized datasets frequently used for benchmarking include SMU (8.9 million compounds) [25], the NCI AIDS dataset (29,184 compounds) [20], and disease-focused collections such as the Chemical Genomics of Tuberculosis (47,217 molecules) [50].

Recently, extremely large chemical spaces have also been explored using on-demand combinatorial libraries such as Enamine’s REAL Space (tens of billions of compounds) and its extended variant xREAL Space (trillion-scale) [73]. Unlike conventional datasets composed of explicitly enumerated compounds, these libraries are typically defined by combinatorial rules and building blocks, and therefore require specialized algorithms for efficient exploration and screening [74]. In this review, we focus on studies that operate on explicitly enumerated molecular libraries. While combinatorial design spaces are of growing importance in drug discovery, their exploration benefits from algorithms specifically designed to exploit the building-block and reaction-rule structure of these libraries, an approach that differs fundamentally from the visualization and clustering workflows reviewed here.

Dataset scale and implications

Dataset scale constrains visualization choices. The sizes cited here correspond to those reported in the reviewed studies. Small collections, such as Heteroaromatics (45 compounds) and Amino Acids (87 compounds) [19], enable focused structural studies and permit more computationally intensive DR methods and interactive exploration. Medium-sized sets ranging from thousands to low millions of compounds, such as ZINC-DrugBank (1,490 compounds) [24], DrugBank (1,900 drugs) [29], and ChEMBL (1.56 million compounds) [35] support broader structure-activity analyses and comparative library mapping. Large to ultra-large libraries containing over one billion compounds (e.g., ZINC20 [37] and ZINC22 2D [9]) enable industrial-scale screening and diversity mapping but impose strong computational and memory demands, motivating scalable featurizations, linear or approximate DR, and clustering algorithms designed for big data. In practice, integrating datasets across these varying scales provides complementary views of diversity and coverage, and supplies realistic benchmarks for evaluating visualization pipelines.

In the next section, we describe how molecules from these sources can be featurized, since representation choices directly influence the scalability of downstream visualizations.

Employed featurization techniques and their impact

Featurization converts molecular structures into computationally usable formats, enabling DR, clustering, and predictive modeling. In chemical space visualization, featurization methods are typically categorized as molecular descriptors and graph-based representations. Molecular descriptors comprise both continuous physicochemical and topological descriptors and discrete structural encodings such as molecular fingerprints. Each of these captures distinct views of chemical characteristics used in similarity analysis and property prediction. Figure 2 illustrates examples of these featurization techniques. In this review, we present fingerprints separately from other types of descriptors for practical clarity, as fingerprints are often treated as an independent family of representations in the literature due to their binary or count-based encoding and their prevalence in similarity search and large-scale clustering. In the following subsections, we provide a general description of these featurization techniques and the studies that utilized them.

Molecular descriptors

Molecular descriptors quantify chemical properties related to molecular size, shape, and electrostatics, translating physicochemical information into numerical form. They range from simple descriptors (e.g., molecular weight) to complex topological and 3D features. Comprehensive treatments of molecular descriptors for cheminformatics applications can be found in foundational references [76] as well as more recent work covering descriptor design and the role of descriptors in chemical space representation and computational modeling [77]. These descriptors allow for the biological interpretation of molecular interactions and play a central role in property prediction. For instance, the Chemplot study analyzing datasets such as FreeSolv, Huuskonen, and BBBP used 200 physicochemical descriptors for DR and clustering tasks [48].

Fingerprint-based molecular descriptors

Molecular fingerprints are a subtype of molecular descriptors that transform structural characteristics into binary or integer vectors, thus indicating the presence or absence of particular molecular fragments. Among them, Morgan fingerprints provide a circular encoding framework based on atom-centered neighborhoods. They have been utilized to explore the chemical space of the ChEMBL dataset [8, 47, 67], providing adaptable encoding radii that capture both local and global features. Additionally, ECFPs [78] are widely used as an instantiation of this framework. In particular, ECFP4 has been frequently applied to datasets such as NuBBE [32] and ChEMBL [35, 66] because it effectively captures local molecular environments and structural diversity. MACCS keys (166-bit) remain popular for drug and nutraceutical datasets [29, 58], providing interpretable substructure-level representations. Such fingerprints have played a vital role in similarity search, clustering, and virtual screening.

Graph-based representations

Graph-based techniques conceptualize molecules as graphs in which atoms are nodes and bonds are edges. Such a representation encodes the structural connectivity between atoms. Molecular graphs have been used to represent molecules from ZINC and other datasets [3, 21, 24, 34, 37, 53]. To assess molecular similarities in terms of topological patterns, graph-based similarity methods like NAMS have been used on the SIGMAR1 dataset [34]. Thus, they provide another avenue for successful structure-based learning and prediction in cheminformatics.

Other molecular representations

Some of the molecular representations that have been employed in the literature do not belong to the above-mentioned categories. These alternative approaches comprise string-based encodings and learned embeddings. SMILES strings provide a one-dimensional textual notation of chemical structures and have been used for dataset preprocessing, visualization, and deep learning inputs [2, 3, 25, 37, 41, 42, 49, 59, 65]. Although molecular graphs can be reconstructed from SMILES, the encoding itself is sequential rather than graph-structured. Representation-learning methods also produce dense continuous embeddings of molecules or reactions, such as rxnfp and transformer-derived vector encodings [8, 42, 48].

These alternative representations go beyond the classical cheminformatics types to allow flexible, model-driven featurization of molecules that can be used to complement fingerprints, descriptors, and graph-based methods.

Combination of featurization techniques

Several studies have employed hybrid featurization techniques that combine the strengths of diverse methods to complement each other. For example, a combination of ECFP, MACCS, and descriptor features enhanced clustering performance and diversity analysis in Approved Drugs, Nutraceuticals, and Mtb inhibitor datasets [58]. Similarly, the combination of ECFP fingerprints and ISIDA descriptors has been applied to ChEMBL28 and DEL datasets, enabling multi-scale structural mapping [57]. Another study incorporated SMILES strings, Morgan fingerprints, and RDKit descriptors into the PPDB and ChEMBL datasets, thus generating detailed molecular representations, which later enhanced predictive modeling and similarity evaluation [47]. These hybrid methods produce more substantial representations, thereby improving the interpretability and robustness of chemical space analysis.

Impact of featurization on downstream tasks

The performance of DR, clustering, and predictive modeling is directly affected by the quality of featurization. Combining various techniques such as ECFP and MACCS with PubChem fingerprints yields superior clustering and visualization, as reported by Lin et al. [79], thereby enhancing molecular mapping. The combination of descriptors and fingerprints enhances virtual screening and similarity searching by capturing complementary structural information [80]. In addition, predictive models gain an advantage from having hybrid feature sets consisting of descriptors, fingerprints, and graph-based encodings, which together improve the prediction of bioactivity and toxicity [81]. In line with this, the incorporation of multiple featurization methods can have significant effects on achieving high-performance molecular analytics in chemical space studies.

The quality of the reduction and corresponding visualization in capturing structural relationships between molecules provides promising assistance for computational chemists in drug discovery and molecular modeling efforts. Most DR techniques discussed in this review, including PCA, t-SNE, UMAP, SOM, and GTM, are generally applied in an unsupervised manner for chemical space visualization, where the primary objective is to discover patterns of structural similarity without labeled outcomes. However, several of these techniques have supervised or semi-supervised variants (e.g., supervised PCA, supervised UMAP), and others can be integrated into supervised workflows in which class labels influence the structure of the latent space [82–85].

Across the reviewed studies, DR is predominantly used to visualize high-dimensional molecular data by projecting it into 2D or 3D spaces, which facilitates analysis of chemical similarity, structural diversity, and clustering relationships [32, 34, 35, 45, 48, 53, 56–58, 62, 66, 68]. Additionally, several studies have used visualization techniques as secondary analysis tools to identify activity cliffs, structure-activity relationships, or interesting clusters of active molecules for drug development and material synthesis [52, 53, 55, 62, 66, 68]. Some studies have also explored the use of several DR techniques (e.g., PCA, t-SNE, UMAP, GTM) comparatively for mapping quality and interpretability [8, 35, 59].

In the earliest period (2000–2010), PCA dominates, accounting for roughly 38% of reported uses, with GTM and MDS appearing sporadically and the remainder spread across various other methods. These include linear projection techniques such as PCA; non-linear techniques encompassing manifold learning methods (t-SNE and its parametric variant pt-SNE, UMAP), distance-preserving methods (MDS), and graph-based layouts (TMAP); and latent variable models such as GTM and VAE. Overview of key reviewed DR techniques

PCA projects data into lower dimensions while retaining maximum variance [87]. It is fast, interpretable, and effective for large datasets like ChEMBL and ZINC [88]. However, its linear assumption limits performance with complex non-linear molecular features, and PCA depends heavily on descriptor quality [89–92].

t-SNE, UMAP, TMAP, and MDS are better suited for non-linear molecular manifolds. t-SNE excels at preserving local similarities and identifying clusters in datasets like FreeSolv [93, 94], though its complexity makes it slow for ultra-large libraries [95, 96]. Parametric t-SNE (pt-SNE) utilizes deep learning to approximate t-SNE projections, offering a scalable, out-of-sample extension for new compounds while retaining local structural fidelity [97–99]. UMAP offers a more scalable alternative, maintaining both local and global structures [84, 100], but requires careful hyperparameter tuning to avoid misleading visualizations [101, 102]. TMAP represents the chemical space as a graph-based layout based on approximate nearest-neighbor graphs and minimum spanning trees, providing exceptional scalability and preserving the local and global structures of ultra-large datasets, improving upon the visualization clarity of previous methods while lacking a reusable parametric mapping [38]. MDS preserves global distances for faithful similarity mapping [103] but is computationally prohibitive and highly sensitive to the chosen distance metric [104, 105].

GTM provides a probabilistic mapping that reveals latent structures. Beyond projection, the latent grid nodes of GTM may also be interpreted as soft cluster centers using responsibility vectors: each latent point identifies a region of descriptor space, allowing the map to be used for both dimensionality reduction and clustering simultaneously. Latent nodes may further be grouped into superclusters on the basis of chemical property distributions or density patterns [106–108]. SOM similarly combines DR and clustering in a single framework and is discussed in subsection Partitional, soft, and hierarchical clustering. VAEs learn non-linear latent spaces optimized for de novo molecular generation [109, 110], but require extensive training iterations T and suffer from high parameter-tuning overhead [111, 112].

In addition to the widely adopted DR techniques discussed above, domain-specific approaches designed for cheminformatics have also been proposed. HCASE maps molecular structures into a low-dimensional space using Hilbert space-filling curves and scaffold-key representations, offering deterministic embedding with interpretable neighborhood preservation [67]. As a recently introduced method, its broader adoption remains to be seen across diverse chemical datasets.

Scalable DR for large datasets

PCA remains a baseline for efficiency; by utilizing truncated implementations for 2D projections, it achieves O(nd) complexity, facilitating the analysis of million-scale molecular datasets on standard hardware. While t-SNE effectively captures local structures, its iterative phase is computationally expensive even with Barnes-Hut approximations, as it requires O(nlogn) operations per iteration [118, 119]. Notably, parametric t-SNE does not require a one-time O(n2) similarity matrix over the full dataset; instead, it computes pairwise probabilities within mini-batches, giving quadratic cost in terms of the batch size. Accordingly, its training complexity is commonly expressed as O(Tn(Bd+LH2+Hd)) with working memory O(LH2+B2+B(H+d)), where B is the batch size, while out-of-sample inference is a single forward pass through the network [126]. In contrast, UMAP improves scalability by separating approximate k-NN graph construction, typically O(nlogn), from graph-based stochastic optimization with per-iteration cost O(nk), giving an overall complexity of O(nlogn+Tnk) [102]. This allows UMAP to maintain both local and global structural integrity while handling large-scale chemical spaces more efficiently.

Latent variable models such as GTM and VAE provide expressive non-linear mappings, but their training cost can be substantial due to iterative optimization and model size. For GTM, training scales as O(T(ngd+b3)) with memory O(ng+bd), where g is the number of latent grid points and b is the number of basis functions [129]. For VAEs, training is typically linear in the number of samples, O(Tn(LH2+Hd)), with working memory dominated by model parameters and batch activations (rather than pairwise structures) [131].

TMAP targets extreme-scale visualization by avoiding iterative embedding optimization. It constructs a c-approximate k-NN graph using an augmented LSH forest query, with overall time complexity O(n(kkc+logn)) (where kc is the query augmentation factor) and memory O(nk), and then performs a minimum spanning tree construction whose cost is negligible relative to graph construction in practice [38]. Benchmarks further report empirical sub-linear scaling of approximately O(n0.931) on large chemical databases [38]. However, TMAP does not learn a reusable parametric mapping, since it produces a static tree layout optimized for visualization.

Overall, for million-scale molecular datasets, truncated PCA provides a fast linear baseline, while UMAP and TMAP offer near-linear-to-linearithmic O(nlogn) scaling that supports high-throughput exploratory analysis. By contrast, methods with quadratic (or worse) dependence on n are typically confined to smaller samples or require approximations. Parametric models such as VAEs and pt-SNE are preferred when a reusable embedding function is required for out-of-sample prediction, but their training overhead and constant factors can be limiting at ultra-large scale [126, 131].

Clustering algorithms

From 2021 to the present, method choice further diversified with the growth of large and complex molecular datasets. Hierarchical clustering remains the most frequent named family, while K-means, Bemis-Murcko scaffold grouping, and responsibility-vector-based methods appear with comparable frequency. Domain-specific similarity-threshold techniques such as Taylor-Butina can yield structurally coherent clusters when the cutoff is well tuned, but their reliance on pairwise similarities makes them difficult to apply at million-to-billion scale without approximations or batching. Bemis-Murcko scaffolds provide a standard abstraction that retains ring systems and connecting linkers while removing peripheral substituents, and such scaffold summaries have been applied at very large scale (e.g., the 4.5B subset of ZINC-22) [3]. Other algorithms such as responsibility-vector-based, BIRCH/BitBIRCH, and probabilistic clustering schemes are less common but show promising results. Overall, the temporal development reflects a shift from early reliance on a few standard algorithms toward more diverse and comparative evaluations aimed at improving interpretability and robustness of chemical space groupings.

We can categorize these clustering algorithms into partitional, including K-means, K-medoids, SOM, BIRCH, Taylor-Butina, and Bemis-Murcko scaffold; hierarchical, including agglomerative hierarchical clustering; and soft clustering, including fuzzy and responsibility-vector-based clustering (Fig. 8). Partitional, soft, and hierarchical clustering

Clustering methods used in chemical space analysis can be grouped into partitional (hard assignment), soft (probabilistic/fuzzy assignment), and hierarchical (multi-resolution) families. Across these categories, the choice of molecular representation (e.g., continuous descriptors vs. binary fingerprints) and the associated dissimilarity measure (e.g., Euclidean vs. Tanimoto) strongly influence the resulting clusters and their interpretability [136, 137].

Partitional clustering assigns each molecule to exactly one cluster based on an optimization objective or a rule-based partition. Centroid-based approaches such as K-means minimize within-cluster dispersion in a chosen feature space [138, 139], whereas K-medoids uses representative data points (medoids) as cluster centers, which can improve robustness to atypical observations depending on the dissimilarity definition [140, 141]. In chemical space visualization, such methods are frequently used with low-dimensional embeddings in order to detect cluster boundaries and allow their visual interpretation.

Prototype-based neural approaches such as SOM assign each molecule to a best-matching unit on a 2D lattice of reference vectors. Although SOM is commonly applied as a clustering method, it also functions as a topology-preserving DR technique by mapping high-dimensional data onto a structured 2D grid. Each unit of the map can be considered a cluster, and neighboring units maintain structural continuity, which enables DR and clustering to be conducted in a single framework [11, 20, 22, 25, 142]. In this case, clustering is closely integrated with the visualization, since the map is composed of discrete organized units representing prototypical regions of the projected space. BIRCH introduces an incremental summarization strategy via clustering features (CF) and a CF-tree, enabling hierarchical compression of the dataset into compact micro-clusters that can be further refined by downstream procedures [143]. Similarity-threshold clustering is common in cheminformatics: the Taylor-Butina procedure forms clusters by selecting representative centroids in a fingerprint similarity graph induced by a user-defined cutoff (rather than specifying a target number of clusters a priori) [144–146].

Other methods perform deterministic structural partitioning based on predefined chemical abstractions rather than on distances in descriptor or latent space [147]. Bemis-Murcko scaffold grouping assigns each molecule to a structural class by extracting its molecular framework: all atoms participating in ring systems and the linker atoms connecting them are retained, while peripheral substituents are removed. The resulting framework can be further abstracted to a generic scaffold by converting all atoms to carbon and all bonds to single bonds, yielding an unlabeled graph that defines groups solely on the basis of core topology. Because this grouping criterion is derived directly from the molecular graph rather than from any descriptor or latent representation, scaffold-based partitioning is independent of the featurization and DR choices made elsewhere in the pipeline, and is therefore better understood as structural classification than as distance-based clustering. This property makes it particularly useful for scaffold diversity analysis and large-scale library characterization [37, 148, 149]. This approach offers structure-driven grouping that directly facilitates the interpretation of chemical space through the visual organization of chemotypes.

Several scaffold-based visualization approaches incorporate structural grouping directly into the visualization process. For example, Scaffold Hunter organizes chemical space using hierarchical scaffold trees, in which molecules are grouped according to shared structural frameworks and visualized through an interactive tree representation annotated with bioactivity information [150]. In this approach, clustering is built into the visualization through scaffold-based partitioning rather than applied as a separate step. Similarly, constellation plots combine scaffold-based grouping with coordinate-based chemical space representations. Molecules are first grouped into analog series defined by shared core structures, and these cores are then displayed in a coordinate-based low-dimensional representation [151]. Each point in the visualization represents a core rather than an individual molecule, and connections between points reflect shared compounds across analog series. This integration of structural grouping and visualization enables the identification of structure-activity relationships directly within the projected chemical space.

Soft clustering assigns partial memberships, allowing a molecule to belong to multiple clusters with different degrees of affiliation. Fuzzy c-means is a canonical example, producing a membership matrix that can capture overlapping chemical series or ambiguous boundaries between chemotypes [152–154]. Responsibility-vector approaches (including GTM-like responsibility assignments) similarly represent membership through probabilistic responsibilities over latent nodes or components, which can be advantageous when uncertainty quantification or mixed-membership structure is of interest [155, 156].

Hierarchical clustering organizes molecules into a nested structure (dendrogram) through iterative merges (agglomerative) under a chosen linkage criterion and dissimilarity measure [157–159]. This representation supports multi-resolution inspection of chemical series by choosing a cut level post hoc, and it is often used to interrogate relationships between clusters, scaffolds, or representative compounds [160, 161].

Scalable clustering techniques for large datasets

Building on subsection Scalable DR for large datasets, this subsection continues to answer RQ5 by focusing on scalable computational strategies for clustering large-scale chemical datasets, highlighting algorithms and implementation-level optimizations that enable efficient grouping of millions to billions of molecules.

Scalability and memory efficiency are critical issues when clustering multi-millions or billion-scale molecular libraries. A key limitation is the quadratic barrier induced by pairwise comparisons: agglomerative hierarchical clustering typically requires O(n2) memory (e.g., storing a dense pairwise distance matrix) and ranges from O(n2) to O(n3) time depending on linkage and implementation, making direct application to ultra-large libraries prohibitive. Similarly, Taylor-Butina clustering relies on computing all-pairs fingerprint similarities, incurring O(n2) time and space, which quickly becomes infeasible at very large n [9, 144]. This limitation extends to K-medoids, which is typically quadratic in memory (distance matrix) and approximately O(Tkn2) in time (after an O(dn2) distance-computation phase), and is therefore rarely used beyond moderate n without approximation or sampling [173].

To overcome these scaling constraints, BIRCH-style approaches avoid explicit all-pairs storage by streaming the data into a compact summary structure (the CF-tree), yielding near-linear construction time (often expressed as O(nd) for fixed tree parameters) and overhead proportional to the number of retained leaf entries rather than n2 [143]. BitBIRCH is a BIRCH-inspired algorithm that combines a CF-tree with the instant similarity (iSIM) mechanism to cluster binary fingerprints using Tanimoto similarity with near-linear scaling and a substantially smaller memory footprint. In benchmarks, BitBIRCH was more than three orders of magnitude faster than the RDKit implementation of Taylor-Butina on a library of around 1.5 million molecules, and an approximation variant was demonstrated to cluster on the order of one billion molecules in under five hours on modern hardware without degrading cluster quality [9]. Bemis-Murcko scaffold grouping provides an extreme-scale alternative by reducing each molecule to its core framework and performing deterministic scaffold-based partitioning, enabling efficient structural classification and diversity analysis across ultra-large datasets while avoiding pairwise similarity computations [3, 37].

Beyond specialized billion-scale algorithms, general-purpose iterative methods can remain practical when their per-iteration costs are linear in n and when k (or other latent sizes) is controlled. K-means has average time complexity O(Tnkd) and modest memory overhead beyond storing the input features, which makes it amenable to mini-batch, distributed, and GPU-accelerated implementations for large chemical libraries. SOMs provide a related prototype-based alternative that also scales linearly in n for a fixed map size, as the per-epoch training cost O(nmd) is dominated by best-matching-unit (BMU) search and neighborhood updates [85].

Soft clustering methods introduce additional memory overhead: fuzzy c-means (FCM) typically requires storing an n×k membership matrix (overhead O(nk)) and has per-iteration cost on the order of O(Tnkd), while responsibility-vector-based schemes (e.g., GTM-style responsibilities) store an n×L responsibility matrix (overhead O(nL)) with typical per-iteration cost O(TnLd). Consequently, such soft methods can scale well in n for moderate k or L, but become memory-bound as the number of clusters and latent nodes grows.

In practice, large-scale clustering often combines algorithmic choices with implementation-level optimizations. To reduce per-comparison cost and memory, long fingerprints may be folded to shorter bit vectors (reducing memory at the cost of potential bit collisions), and fast bit operations (e.g., popcount-based Tanimoto) are used to accelerate similarity evaluation. To reduce the total number of comparisons and wall-clock time, batch, streaming, and distributed strategies split very large libraries into manageable shards that can be processed in parallel and then merged or reconciled via representative-based postprocessing [9, 27]. Finally, although hierarchical clustering remains valuable for interpretability, it is most realistically applied at scale to subsamples or to reduced representations (e.g., CF-tree leaf summaries, scaffold representatives, or cluster centroids), rather than directly to the full billion-molecule library due to its O(n2) memory requirement [143].

Discussion and recommendations

This section outlines the key findings from the reviewed studies, emphasizing dataset-size-dependent patterns in chemical space visualization pipelines. The analysis also outlines persistent challenges and future research directions throughout the field.

Implications of dataset scale

In the 56 reviewed studies, molecular fingerprints remain the dominant representation choice, reflecting their favorable trade-off between computational efficiency and chemical relevance, particularly for large libraries where similarity search and clustering must be executed at scale. Over time, the representation landscape shows a clear shift: early studies relied more heavily on hand-crafted descriptors and domain-specific representations, whereas the 2011–2020 period marks a strong consolidation around fingerprint-based pipelines, consistent with the growing availability of million-scale public and vendor libraries. In the most recent period (2021–present), representation choices diversify again, with increased use of fragment descriptors, SMILES-based encodings, and learned embeddings, suggesting renewed emphasis on richer structure–property representations, while fingerprints continue to function as a scalable default baseline. Overall, the trend indicates that large dataset sizes push studies toward compact, index-based representations, while smaller datasets can accommodate more expressive and costlier featurizations.

DR shows a clear shift from scalable linear baselines toward neighborhood-preserving non-linear methods, constrained by computational and memory costs. PCA remains widely used across the reviewed studies, consistent with its efficient O(nd) profile for fixed low-dimensional projections (i.e., small c) and its amenability to parallelization, making it practical for million-scale chemical libraries. As visualization goals increasingly prioritize local structure, t-SNE has gained prominence; however, its naive quadratic requirements (driven by pairwise similarities) and Barnes-Hut approximations that scale as O(nlogn) per iteration limit its applicability to ultra-large datasets. More recently, UMAP has emerged as a more scalable non-linear alternative, with overall time O(nlogn+Tnk) and working memory dominated by the sparse k-NN graph O(nk), supporting its growing adoption in large-scale studies. At the extreme scale, TMAP reflects a shift toward graph-based layouts that enable visualization of ultra-large libraries where classical manifold learning becomes computationally prohibitive. These trends indicate a size-dependent trade-off: PCA persists as a robust baseline, t-SNE/UMAP are preferred when local neighborhood fidelity is prioritized and dataset size permits, and TMAP is utilized when extreme-scale visualization (multi-million and beyond) is required. It should be noted that DR techniques such as GTM can be applied to very large chemical libraries if they are implemented using hierarchical mapping strategies and precomputed map infrastructures, as has been demonstrated by recent work enabling navigation of chemical spaces that contain hundreds of millions of compounds [46].

Compared with featurization and DR, clustering shows the weakest convergence to a single dominant strategy: a small set of methods (hierarchical clustering, the K-means family, and SOM) appears repeatedly, but many studies use a wide range of other approaches. Early work relied mainly on SOM and K-means, while later studies show sustained use of hierarchical clustering together with a broader mix of methods, including scaffold-based clustering and other domain-driven variants, as well as responsibility-vector-based strategies. This diversity is expected because clustering is often selected based on the analysis goal (e.g., scaffold coherence, neighborhood density, or activity-driven grouping) and is frequently used to compare alternative views of chemical space rather than as a single final answer. Dataset size further constrains feasible choices, as reflected by the computational and memory costs in our clustering Table 4: K-means scales as O(Tnkd) time and O(kd+n) space, whereas K-medoids scales as O(dn2+Tkn2) time and O(n2) space and becomes impractical for large libraries. For million-scale workflows, more scalable tree-based approaches such as BIRCH/BitBIRCH avoid explicit all-pairs storage and achieve near-linear construction time under fixed parameters, with memory proportional to the CF-tree size (number of retained leaf entries) rather than n2. Taylor-Butina clustering can yield structurally coherent clusters with an appropriately tuned similarity threshold, but because it relies on pairwise fingerprint similarities and scales quadratically in time and space, it is rarely feasible at million-to-billion scale without approximations, batching, or specialized acceleration.

Taken together, the trends point to dataset size as the key constraint shaping practical combinations of featurization, DR, and clustering because it directly sets the feasible computation and memory budget. At small-to-medium scales, studies can afford richer representations (descriptors, fragments, hybrids, and in some cases learned embeddings) and more expensive methods (e.g., t-SNE, MDS variants, K-medoids, and some hierarchical variants) to improve interpretability and local structure discovery. As scale increases, practice converges toward representations and algorithms with sub-quadratic scaling and sparse non-O(n2) memory use: fingerprints (often compressed or folded), truncated PCA and increasingly UMAP and TMAP-style graph layouts, and clustering choices that avoid global all-pairs similarity. Consequently, pipelines favor centroid and prototype-based methods such as K-means and SOM, BIRCH-like summarization, and scaffold-based partitioning, or apply clustering to reduced representatives primarily to support visualization.

This review has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the analysis is based on the set of studies found through a structured but non-systematic search strategy, and so may not cover all relevant publications. Second, the trends in this work are based on the methodologies and dataset sizes used in the reviewed studies and not the full capabilities of the underlying algorithms. Third, the evaluation of DR and clustering techniques is dependent on their application in individual studies, which may differ with respect to their implementation, parameter settings, and data characteristics. Finally, rapidly evolving approaches, especially ones that rely on deep learning, might not be fully reflected in the existing literature. In spite of these limitations, the observed trends give a consistent basis for analyzing the computational trade-offs of different methods, which we formalize in the following paragraph.

Pipeline archetypes by dataset size

The archetypes are organized into five dataset-size regimes: small (<5K), medium (5K–100K), large (100K–1 M), very large (1 M–1B), and ultra-large (>1B). For small datasets, studies can afford more expensive geometric objectives and interactive analysis, and therefore commonly pair chemically interpretable descriptors and fingerprints with DR methods that emphasize faithful geometry or map-based interpretation (PCA, MDS, GTM, SOM, etc.). Clustering in this regime is correspondingly flexible, including fuzzy and hierarchical variants as well as similarity-threshold procedures (e.g., Taylor-Butina), reflecting an emphasis on interpretability and exploratory structure discovery.

For medium datasets, the archetype shifts toward neighborhood-preserving non-linear DR (UMAP and t-SNE) while retaining PCA as a baseline, and it introduces learned representations or latent models (e.g., VAE) in a subset of studies. Clustering remains diverse in this regime, with centroid and prototype-based methods (K-means, SOM) frequently used alongside fuzzy and hierarchical approaches. When higher-resolution structure is desired, quadratic or near-quadratic procedures (e.g., K-medoids, similarity-threshold clustering) may still be feasible.

For large datasets (100K–1 M), scalability becomes a primary constraint, and pipelines increasingly adopt graph- and index-based visualization (TMAP) and efficient baselines (truncated PCA, UMAP), with t-SNE constrained to subsamples when used. Clustering choices likewise prioritize near-linear memory and compute: scaffold-based partitioning (Bemis-Murcko) and BIRCH-style summarization (BitBIRCH) are common, with K-means used when an explicit partition is needed. More expensive clustering procedures (e.g., HAC or Taylor-Butina) are constrained to representatives rather than applied to all molecules. By representatives, we mean applying a scalable clustering procedure first, then selecting a subset of representative molecules (e.g., CF-tree summaries, scaffold representatives, or centroids).

For very large libraries (1 M–1B), the archetype is explicitly scalability-driven: representations favor compact or index-friendly fingerprints (e.g., folded fingerprints or MinHash-style schemes), and DR favors TMAP and truncated PCA, with UMAP applied to subsamples when needed for local neighborhood fidelity. When neural or parametric DR appears at this scale, it is most naturally used as a pretrained projection model whereby it is trained on a manageable subset and then used for scalable out-of-sample inference. Clustering at this scale prioritizes scaffold-based partitioning and summarization (Bemis-Murcko, BitBIRCH), with any quadratic procedures restricted to representative sets.

Finally, for ultra-large datasets (>1B), pipelines avoid full all-pairs operations and rely on highly scalable representations (compact fingerprints, fragments, or latent embeddings), combined with TMAP, truncated PCA, and pretrained projections for out-of-sample mapping. Clustering is typically limited to extreme-scale partitioning and summarization (Bemis-Murcko, BitBIRCH), reflecting the practical need to keep both compute and memory sub-quadratic in n.

Gaps and future research directions

Despite substantial progress in DR, clustering, and large-scale chemical space mapping, several gaps remain that limit the reliability, coverage, and practical utility of chemical space visualizations. Across the reviewed literature, these gaps are tied to five recurring issues: how visualizations are evaluated, how molecules are represented, how methods scale to very large datasets, how reproducible analyses are, and how well current benchmarks cover diverse chemical domains. By examining the gaps discussed below and outlining future research directions aimed at improving evaluation consistency, representation richness, scalability, reproducibility, and dataset diversity in chemical space visualization, this subsection answers the final research question RQ6.

A major gap is the lack of consistent, structure-aware quantitative evaluation. Current practices frequently prioritize visual inspection and generic embedding metrics, but these approaches often lack a systematic assessment of how well low-dimensional proximity preserves intrinsic chemical relationships, such as scaffold hierarchies or Tanimoto similarity. This creates a risk of over-interpreting visually separated regions as chemically meaningful. Future work should more consistently report structure-based measures alongside visualization outputs, using chemical similarity as the reference notion of neighborhood. Examples include neighborhood-preservation metrics derived from a co-ranking matrix (e.g., PNN, QNN, LCMC), which compare k-NN rank structure in the embedding against k-NN rank structure under a reference distance in the original representation space [8]. These neighborhood-agreement scores can be made structure-aware by defining the reference neighborhoods using fingerprint Tanimoto similarity or scaffold-based similarity, rather than a generic metric in descriptor space. For clustering, complementary structure-aware diagnostics include within-cluster Tanimoto distributions, scaffold diversity, and enrichment of known chemical series within clusters or map regions. Adopting a standard set of such structure-aware metrics would reduce reliance on visual inspection, improve comparability across studies and datasets, and support more reliable benchmarking of both DR and clustering pipelines [38].

A second gap is the representation trade-off: fingerprints are efficient and scalable but can miss stereochemistry and 3D effects, while learned embeddings can be richer yet harder to interpret and sometimes computationally heavier to train and validate. Future work should benchmark hybrid representations that combine complementary signals (fingerprints, fragments, physicochemical descriptors, and learned embeddings) and clarify when the added complexity measurably improves neighborhood preservation, cluster coherence, or chemical series separation.

At million-to-billion scale, many DR and clustering methods become impractical without approximation or specialized algorithms. Priorities for future work include scalable neighborhood-based DR (e.g., approximate UMAP), clustering that avoids dense pairwise similarity matrices (e.g., BIRCH/BitBIRCH-style summarization), and workflows that favor coarse projections or graph layouts over exhaustive clustering at ultra-large scale. There is also an opportunity to develop more chemical-domain-specific DR and clustering methods that exploit scaffold structure or fingerprint similarity while remaining scalable and interpretable.

Reproducibility and transparency also remain inconsistent. Hyperparameters (e.g., DR settings, distance metrics, fingerprint length, sampling strategies), preprocessing steps, and filtering choices can strongly affect visual outcomes, yet reporting varies widely and code availability is mixed. Future work should adopt clearer reporting standards for chemical space visualization studies, including complete specification of representations, preprocessing, hyperparameters, and evaluation criteria, and should prioritize open-source implementations and reusable benchmark datasets so that results can be replicated and compared.

Finally, there are opportunities to broaden chemical space coverage beyond common drug-like and commercially curated libraries. Many visualizations focus on bioactivity-rich datasets and vendor-accessible collections, while natural products, metal-containing compounds, environmental chemicals, and other underrepresented domains receive less attention. Expanding benchmark datasets and validation tasks to better cover these regions would improve generality and reduce domain bias in both representations and visualization methods.

Conclusion

Chemical space visualization has moved from small, interactive studies to workflows that must handle million- to multi-billion-compound libraries under tight compute and memory limits. By reviewing 56 studies published between 2000 and 2025, we treat chemical space visualization as an end-to-end pipeline that includes dataset selection, featurization, dimensionality reduction (DR), clustering, and evaluation. Several consistent patterns emerge. Fingerprints remain the most common representation at scale, while fragments, SMILES-derived encodings, and learned embeddings are increasingly used when richer structure–property signals are needed and the added cost is justified. DR has expanded beyond PCA baselines toward neighborhood-preserving methods such as t-SNE and UMAP, and toward extreme-scale layout approaches such as TMAP when dense pairwise computations become too expensive. Clustering remains diverse and strongly driven by the analysis goal: hierarchical clustering, K-means, and SOM appear frequently, while large-library settings increasingly motivate scalable alternatives such as BIRCH/BitBIRCH and scaffold-based partitioning that avoid explicit O(n2) similarity construction.

A central contribution of this review is the set of dataset-scale pipeline archetypes distilled from these observed patterns. Rather than presenting methods in isolation, the archetypes summarize compatible choices for representation, DR, and clustering across practical size regimes as scale increases, and clarify the key trade-offs that constrain method selection. These templates are intended to support new studies by indicating when simple, interpretable baselines are sufficient, when neighborhood-preserving DR is worth the added cost, and when ultra-large settings require layouts and grouping strategies that prioritize scalable organization over exhaustive partitioning.

This review also highlights gaps that limit how comparable and reliable chemical space visualizations are across studies. Many papers still rely heavily on visual inspection without consistent structure-aware quantitative checks. Reporting of preprocessing steps and key hyperparameters varies widely, even though these choices can change the map and the resulting groupings substantially. Benchmarking is also uneven across chemical domains, with many studies concentrating on drug-like and commercially curated libraries. Progress will require clearer reporting standards, more diverse benchmark datasets and tasks, and scalable methods and evaluation protocols that remain chemically meaningful and interpretable.
