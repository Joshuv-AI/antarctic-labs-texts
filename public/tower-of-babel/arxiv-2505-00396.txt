Temporal coupled mode theory for high- Q Q resonances in dielectric metasurfaces
Dmitrii N. Maksimov, Pavel S. Pankin, Dong-Wook Kim, Mingzhao Song, Chao Peng, Andrey A. Bogdanov

Abstract
In this work, we propose a coupled mode theory for resonant response from quasi-guided modes in periodic dielectric metasurfaces. First, we derived a generic set of constraints imposed onto the parameters of the temporal coupled mode theory by energy conservation and time-reversal symmetry in an invariant form that allows for asymmetry between the coupling and decoupling coefficients. The proposed approach is applied to the problem of Fano resonances induced by isolated quasi-guided modes in the regime of specular reflection. Our central result is a generic formula for the line-shape of the Fano resonance in transmittance for the lossless metasurfaces in the framework of 2D electrodynamics. We consider all possible symmetries of the metasurface elementary cell and uncover the effects that the symmetry incurs on the profile of the Fano resonance induced by an isolated high- Q Q mode. It is shown that the proposed approach correctly describes the presence of robust reflection and transmission zeros in the spectra as well as the spectral signatures of bound states in the continuum. The approach is applied to uniderictionally guided resonant modes in metasurfaces with an asymmetric elementary cell. It is found that the existence of such modes and the transmittance in their spectral vicinity are consistent with the theoretical predictions. Furthermore, the theory predicts that a uniderictionally guided resonant mode is dual to a counter-propagating mode of a peculiar type which is coupled with the outgoing wave on both sides of the metasurface but, nonetheless, exhibits only a single-sided coupling with incident waves.



I
Introduction

In recent years dielectric metasurfaces have become an indispensable tool in the fields of photonics and radiophysics since their application paves the way to shaping optical response induced by high-quality (high-
Q
) modes
Kuznetsov et al. 2016
;
Campione et al. 2016
;
Genevet et al. 2017
;
Algorri et al. 2019
;
Koshelev and Kivshar 2020
. The propagating modes in periodic dielectric metasurfaces are Bloch waves traveling across the plane of the metasurface. If the frequency of such waves at a given in-plane Bloch wavenumber occurs above the line of light, the modes radiate electromagnetic energy into outer space. Thus, the modes become leaky and, as such, can be termed
quasi-guided
or
radiative
modes
Tikhodeev et al. 2002
;
Huang et al. 2023
. On the other hand, leaky modes are
resonant
, that is, can be coupled with incident light to induce a resonant response, which is accompanied by the enhancement of the electromagnetic field in the host structure
Mocella and Romano 2015
;
Yuan et al. 2017
. This phenomenon has been instrumental in the design of various devices that employ a strong light-matter interaction, even with low-loss dielectrics
Baranov et al. 2017
;
Kivshar 2018
. Nowadays, such devices include lasers
Kodigala et al. 2017
;
Shankhwar et al. 2017
;
Huang et al. 2020
;
Yang et al. 2021
;
Azzam et al. 2021
;
Hwang et al. 2022
, sensors
Romano et al. 2018
;
Ollanik et al. 2018
;
Yesilkoy et al. 2019
;
Tseng et al. 2020
;
Wang et al. 2021
;
Conteduca et al. 2021
;
Liu et al. 2023
, absorbers
Liu et al. 2017
;
Fan et al. 2017
;
Saadabad et al. 2021
;
Tian et al. 2018
;
Chen et al. 2019
, polaritonic devices
Al-Ani et al. 2022
;
Wu et al. 2024
;
Sortino et al. 2024
;
Masharin et al. 2024
, and nonlinear optical components
Yang et al. 2015
;
Schiattarella et al. 2024
;
Liu et al. 2019
;
Kravtsov et al. 2020
;
Koshelev et al. 2019a
.
Formally, various types of resonances in metasurfaces, including Wood anomalies
Sarrazin et al. 2003
;
Wood 1935
, lattice resonances
Kravets et al. 2018
;
Castellanos et al. 2019
, quasi-bound states in the continuum
Rybin et al. 2017
, and high-

Mie resonances

can be associated with quasi-guided resonant modes also know as
resonant states

;

quasi-normal modes

Lalanne et al. 2018a
;
Alpeggiani et al. 2017a
.

In the far field the high-
Q
resonant modes manifest themselves as sharp Fano resonances in the transmittance spectrum
Campione et al. 2016
;
Zhou et al. 2014
;
Limonov et al. 2017
;
Krasnok et al. 2019
. The Fano resonances are typically understood as the product of interference between waves following two optical paths. The above interpretation can be applied to dielectric metasurfaces via decomposing the electromagnetic field into a resonant field, which is induced by coupling to a resonant eigenmode, and a background field, which is independent of the incident frequency on the scale of the high-
Q
resonance width
Fan et al. 2003
. The Fano resonances, therefore, are the primary signature of the resonant modes in a dielectric metasurface. The intricate behavior of Fano resonances makes it possible to apply dielectric metasurfaces to shape the transmittance spectrum for filtering
Foley and Phillips 2015
;
Monti et al. 2020
, polarization conversion
Guo et al. 2017
;
Hu et al. 2020
, and circular dichroism
Gorkunov et al. 2020
;
Toftul et al. 2024
.

One remarkable feature of the subwavelength dielectric metasurfaces is the opportunity to control the life-time of the resonant modes. In the ideal case of a lossless dielectric an infinite life-time can be achieved even above the line of light with non-radiating modes embedded in the continuum of scattering solutions. Such modes are known as bound states in the continuum (BICs) and are representative of the utmost case of light localization
Hsu et al. 2016
;
Koshelev et al. 2019b
;
Joseph et al. 2021
;
Kang et al. 2023
. By definition, the BICs are not coupled with incident radiation, and therefore are seemingly useless in applications. However, BICs can be activated by breaking the system’s symmetry, either structurally
Koshelev et al. 2018
, or by incident field
Maksimov et al. 2020a
. As a consequence, the BIC can be transformed into a quasi-BIC with extremely high quality factor (
Q
-factor) which diverges to infinity as the symmetry is recovered under variation of a certain control parameter, e.g. the angle of incidence. The
Q
-factor divergence manifests itself in the transmittance spectrum as a collapsing Fano resonance with the line-width vanishing on approach to the BIC point in the parametric space
Blanchard et al. 2016
;
Bogdanov et al. 2019
;
Pankin et al. 2020
;
Bulgakov and Maksimov 2018
. The collapse of the Fano resonance is complemented by the critical field enhancement
Mocella and Romano 2015
;
Yoon et al. 2015
, the latter effect being limited only by the material losses in the host dielectric.

Another interesting type of high-
Q
resonant modes to have recently attracted a lot of attention in the literature is a unidirectional guided resonant mode (UGR)
Yin et al. 2020
. This type of resonant mode can be viewed as a semi-BIC since the UGRs do not radiate only to a single direction, i.e., either to the substrate or to the superstrate of the metasurface. In addition to intriguing topological properties
Jiang et al. 2023
;
Yin et al. 2023
;
Zhuang et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2024a
, UGRs are applicable to optical devices that utilize asymmetric coupling between the resonant mode and the outgoing waves
Zhang et al. 2021
;
Zhang et al. 2022
;
Li et al. 2023
;
Xu et al. 2023a
;
Xu et al. 2023b
;
Wang et al. 2024b
.

Theoretical description of the Fano resonances induced by various types of quasi-guided modes in dielectric metasurfaces is hindered by the complexity of the host structures. The aforementioned phenomena occur only in dielectric systems, which are at least two-dimensional (2D) structures with the permittivity modulated in one spatial dimension. This does not allow for analytic solutions of Maxwell’s equations
to be written in a closed form. Nonetheless, it is possible to derive a generic expression for the transmittance as a function of the incident frequency by using temporal coupled mode theory (TCMT). The TCMT is a phenomenological instrument
that relies on symmetries and conservation laws to establish constraints on the number of parameters that describe the Fano line-shape
Fan et al. 2003
;
Suh et al. 2004
. The TCMT approach has proved to be efficient for analyzing the scattering and absorption spectra of dielectric metasurfaces
Zhou et al. 2016
;
Alpeggiani et al. 2017b
;
Ming et al. 2017
;
Maksimov et al. 2020b
;
Bikbaev et al. 2021
;
Zhang et al. 2023
;
Wu et al. 2022
;
Huang et al. 2024
.

In this work, we revisit the TCMT in application to the Fano response from dielectric metasurfaces hosting high-
Q
resonant modes. Our goal is to develop a TCMT approach that both accommodates various kinds of high-
Q
resonances, including BICs and UGRs, and reflects the role of the symmetry of the metasurface elementary cell.
We derive a generic formula for the line-shape of the Fano resonance in transmittance for the lossless metasurfaces in the framework of 2D electrodynamics. We discuss all possible symmetries of the metasruface elementary cell and uncover the effects that the symmetry incurs on the profile of the Fano resonance induced by an isolated high-
Q
mode. The article is organized as follows. In Sec.
II
we introduce the system under scrutiny and discuss the generic properties of the scattering matrix (
S
-matrix) in the regime of specular reflection. In Sec.
III
we overview and revisit the TCMT to derive the generic form of the constraints imposed on the resonant mode coupling and decoupling coefficients by energy conservation and time-reversal symmetry.
In Sec.
IV
we apply the results of the previous section to dielectric metasurfaces and derive the generic TCMT solution for the Fano resonance line-shape. In Sec.
V
we verify our findings numerically in comparison with finite-difference frequency domain (FDFD)
Rumpf 2022
simulations for all types of point group symmetries of the elementary cell including in-plane mirror symmetry, out-of-plane mirror symmetry, and inversion symmetry. In Sec.
VI
we apply our approach to UGRs. It is shown for the asymmetric metasurfaces that the UGR is dual to a counter-propagating mode of a peculiar type. Unlike the UGR which is not coupled with one of the outgoing channels whereas being coupled with both incident ones, the dual mode, which we term unidirectional coupled resonant mode (UCR), is not coupled with one of the incident channels whereas is still coupled with both outgoing ones. Finally, we summarize and conclude in Sec.
VII
.

II
System description

The system to be considered is shown in Fig.
1
. One can see in Fig.
1
(a) that the system is a ruled grating that possesses a translation symmetry in the
x
-direction with period
\Lambda
. The body of the grating is made of lossless dielectric with permittivity
n_{1}=3.5
. As it has been already mentioned before, here we focus on 2D electrodynamics which means that all fields are independent of
y
. This can be achieved by choosing the
x0z
-plane as the plane of incidence. Further on we focus on the transverse electric wave (s-waves) with the electric vector perpendicular to the plane of incidence. Albeit the scattering of light is controlled by vectorial Maxwell’s equations the problem can be reduced to a single scalar equation for the
y
-component of the electric field while the other components are equal to zero

\left(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}+\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial z^{2}}\right)E_{y}(x,z)+\epsilon(x,z)\frac{\omega^{2}}{c^{2}}E_{y}(x,z)=0,

(1)

where
\epsilon(x,z)
is the dielectric function,
\omega
is the angular frequency of the incident light, and
c
is the speed of light. In the far-field the dielectric function is constant, thus, the monochromatic solutions are plane waves

E_{y}(x,z)\propto e^{\pm ik_{x}x\pm ik_{z}z},\ \ \frac{n_{0}^{2}\omega^{2}}{c^{2}}=k_{x}^{2}+k_{z}^{2},

(2)

where
n_{0}=1.5
is the refractive index of the ambient medium, and
k_{x,z}
are the corresponding components of the wave vector. Since the grating is periodic the solution has to comply with the Bloch theorem

\displaystyle E_{y}(x,z)=E_{y}^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}(x,z)e^{i\beta x},\ \ \beta\Lambda\in(-\pi,\pi],

(3)

where
E^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}_{y}(x,z)
is a periodic function of
x
with period
\Lambda
, and
\beta
is the Bloch propagation constant. The conservation of quasi-momentum requires

k_{x}=\beta+{2\pi m}/{\Lambda}
where index
m
is known as the diffraction order. By setting
k_{z}=0
one finds the cut-off frequencies for the diffraction scattering channels as follows

n_{0}\omega=\pm c\left(\beta+\frac{2\pi m}{\Lambda}\right),\ \ m=-\infty,\ldots,-1,0,1,\ldots,\infty.

(4)

The diagram of the diffraction channels is shown in Fig.
1
(b).
The specular reflection occurs when only the zeroth order diffraction channel is open for propagation of light, i.e.

|k_{x}|<\frac{n_{0}\omega}{c}<\frac{2\pi}{\Lambda}-|k_{x}|.

(5)

We assume that the metasurface supports an isolated right-going
\beta>0
resonant mode that does not overlap with the other high-
Q
resonant modes on a frequency scale of its resonant width
\gamma
for any
\beta
. Since the mode is radiative it is characterized by a complex eigenfrequency

\omega_{0}-i\gamma
with
\omega_{0}
being the resonance center-frequency.
The resonant mode is two-fold degenerate, so there is another resonant mode with the same complex frequency but opposite Bloch wavenumber
-\beta
. The amplitudes of these two counter-propagating modes will be further denoted by
a_{\scriptscriptstyle{\rightarrow}}
and
a_{\scriptscriptstyle{\leftarrow}}
with the index arrow indicating the propagation direction.

We consider scattering of plane monochromatic waves in the spectral domain where only specular reflection is possible, see Eq. (

This assumption, together with the specified incident frequency
\omega
and the angle of incidence
\theta
, drastically simplify our analysis, since only four scattering channels are open
for incident/outgoing light. The scattering channels are defined in Fig.

, where the superscript
{}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\!+\!)}
is used for the incident channels and
{}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\!-\!)}

We also assume that the outgoing channels are linked to the incident ones via the time-reversal operation.
The
4\!\times\!4

S
-matrix is implicitly defined through the following formula that relates the vectors of incident and outgoing (scattered) amplitudes

{\bf s}_{\scriptscriptstyle(\!-\!)}=\widehat{S}_{\scriptscriptstyle{4\!\times\!4}}{\bf s}_{\scriptscriptstyle(\!+\!)},

(6)

where the vectors of outgoing
{\bf s}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\!-\!)}
and incident
{\bf s}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\!+\!)}
amplitudes are given by

\bf{s}_{\scriptscriptstyle{(\pm)}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}s_{1}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\pm)}}\\
s_{2}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\pm)}}\\
s_{3}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\pm)}}\\
s_{4}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\pm)}}\end{array}\right).

(7)

Note that in case
\theta\neq 0
the time reversal changes the propagation direction of all waves with respect to the
x
-axis. Since both the energy conservation

and the time reversal

\widehat{S}_{\scriptscriptstyle{4\!\times\!4}}=\widehat{S}_{\scriptscriptstyle{4\!\times\!4}}^{\intercal},

(8)

and unitary

\widehat{S}_{\scriptscriptstyle{4\!\times\!4}}^{-1}=\widehat{S}_{\scriptscriptstyle{4\!\times\!4}}^{\dagger}.

(9)

In addition the

Note that the matrix

where

\widehat{U}={\rm diag}(e^{i\psi_{1}},e^{i\psi_{2}},e^{i\psi_{3}},e^{i\psi_{4}})

\displaystyle\psi_{1}=\frac{1}{2}(\psi+\eta+\xi),

\displaystyle\psi_{2}=\frac{1}{2}(\psi-\eta-\xi),

\displaystyle\psi_{4}=\frac{1}{2}(\psi-\eta+\xi)

III
TCMT revisited

The key idea behind the TCMT is to cast the scattering problem in the form of a system of coupled driven oscillators. Each oscillator in the system represents an optical resonant mode which can be excited by incident light.
Mathematically, in the TCMT framework the dynamics is controlled by a system of linear ordinary differential equations.
The credit of the modern TCMT formulation is to Fan, Suh, and Joannopoulos who were the first to have put forward a number of important relationships for the system’s coefficients rendering the TCMT tractable for describing Fano resonances in a single mode approach

Fan et al. 2003
. Later, in
Suh et al. 2004
the TCMT was generalized to the multi-modal case. Generalizations for arbitrary-shaped objects with an infinite number of scattering channels are also possible
Ruan and Fan 2012
;
Bulgakov et al. 2023
.
More recently, the effects of the time-reversal symmetry, energy conservation, and reciprocity on the TCMT constraints were thoroughly investigated in
Zhao et al. 2019
.

The TCMT is akin to the plethora of resonant state expansion methods which have recently gained popularity in the photonics community
Muljarov and Weiss 2018
;
Lalanne et al. 2018b
;
Sauvan et al. 2022
. These methods also rely on series expansions into eigenmodes of Maxwell’s equation with radiation boundary conditions. The difference is, though, that the TCMT, being a phenomenological approach, does not utilize the explicit form of the eigenmodes, but rather establishes the generic form of the optical response relying on physical constraints such as symmetries and conservation laws.
Practically, the TCMT solutions always contain unknown coefficients which are to be fitted to comply with exact full-wave solutions obtained by numerically exact methods. In this work we are going to apply the FDFD method
Rumpf 2022
.
Yet, the choice of simulation method is, of course, always at the discretion of the researcher, so other methods such as the increasingly popular finite-difference time-domain and finite-element methods could have been equally applied.
Finally, it is worth mentioning that recently some attempts have been made to put the TCMT on more rigorous grounds by deriving the TCMT equations from resonant mode expansions

Alpeggiani et al. 2017b
;
Zhang and Miller 2020
;
Wu and Lalanne 2024
.

According to
Suh et al. 2004
the TCMT the relationship between
{\bf s}_{\scriptscriptstyle(\!+\!)}
and
{\bf s}_{\scriptscriptstyle(\!-\!)}
can be written in the following form

\displaystyle\frac{d{\bf a}(t)}{dt}=[-i\widehat{\Omega}-\widehat{\Gamma}]{\bf a}(t)+\widehat{K}^{\intercal}{\bf s}_{\scriptscriptstyle(\!+\!)}(t),

\displaystyle{\bf s}_{\scriptscriptstyle(\!-\!)}(t)=\widehat{C}{\bf s}_{\scriptscriptstyle(\!+\!)}(t)+\widehat{D}{\bf a}(t),

(17)

where
{\bf a}(t)
is a column vector composed of the amplitudes of the resonant eigenmodes supported by the photonic structure,

\widehat{C}
– the matrix of direct (non-resonant) process,
\widehat{\Omega}
– the Hermitian matrix containing the natural frequencies of the resonant modes,
\widehat{\Gamma}
– the Hermitian matrix of decay rates,
\widehat{K}
is the coupling matrix, and
\widehat{D}
is the decoupling matrix. In the frequency domain, i.e. for systems illuminated by monochromatic light, we apply a time-harmonic substitution for all
time-dependent quantities, e.g.

{\bf a}(t)={\bf a}e^{-i\omega t},

(18)

which leads to

\displaystyle[i(\widehat{\Omega}-\omega\widehat{\mathbb{I}})+\widehat{\Gamma}]{\bf a}=\widehat{K}^{\intercal}{\bf s}_{\scriptscriptstyle(\!+\!)},

\displaystyle{\bf s}_{\scriptscriptstyle(\!-\!)}=\widehat{C}{\bf s}_{\scriptscriptstyle(\!+\!)}+\widehat{D}{\bf a}.

(19)

Equation (
III
) are the basic equations
to be used for finding the
S
-matrix.

Now, for the sake of generality, assume that there are
M
resonant modes coupled with
N
scattering channels.
Then the quantities in Eq. (
III
) are as follows. The vector
{\bf a}
is a
M\!\times\!1
column vector,
\widehat{D}
and
\widehat{K}
are
N\!\times\!M
matrices, and
\widehat{C}
is a
N\!\times\!N
matrix. In the same manner as in
Suh et al. 2004
, by taking into account the photon number conservation in radiation loss from an unpumped system one can show that Eq. (
III
) leads to

2\widehat{\Gamma}=\widehat{D}^{\dagger}\widehat{D}.

(20)

At the same time the general solution for the
S
-matrix can be obtained from Eq. (
III
) as follows

\widehat{S}(\omega)=\widehat{C}+\widehat{D}\frac{1}{i(\widehat{\Omega}-\omega\widehat{\mathbb{I}})+\widehat{\Gamma}}\widehat{K}^{\intercal}.

(21)

The unitarity of the
S
-matrix given by Eq. (
21
) leads to a rather awkward equation

\displaystyle\widehat{K}^{*}\frac{1}{-i(\widehat{\Omega}-\omega\widehat{\mathbb{I}})+\widehat{\Gamma}}\widehat{D}^{\dagger}\widehat{C}+\widehat{C}^{*}\widehat{D}\frac{1}{i(\widehat{\Omega}-\omega\widehat{\mathbb{I}})+\widehat{\Gamma}}\widehat{K}^{\intercal}

\displaystyle+2\widehat{K}^{*}\frac{1}{-i(\widehat{\Omega}-\omega\widehat{\mathbb{I}})+\widehat{\Gamma}}\widehat{\Gamma}\frac{1}{i(\widehat{\Omega}-\omega\widehat{\mathbb{I}})+\widehat{\Gamma}}\widehat{K}^{\intercal}=0.

(22)

To make use out of Eq. (
III
) we consider the eigenvalue problem

(i\widehat{\Omega}+\widehat{\Gamma}){\bf a}_{m}=i\omega_{m}{\bf a}_{m}.

(23)

In what follows we focus on the generic case when there exist
M
linearly independent, though not necessarily orthogonal, eigenvectors. In the other words it is assumed that there are no exceptional points

in the spectrum. The vectors
{\bf a}_{m}
will be termed the right eigenvectors of
i\widehat{\Omega}+\widehat{\Gamma}
. The right eigenvectors can be complemented with the left eigenvectors
{\bf b}_{m}
which solve the eigenvalue problem

{\bf b}_{m}^{\intercal}(i\widehat{\Omega}+\widehat{\Gamma})=i\omega_{m}{\bf b}_{m}^{\intercal}.

(24)

It is possible to show that the right and left eigenvectors can be normalized such that the following orthonormalization condition holds true

{\bf b}_{m}^{\intercal}{\bf a}_{m^{\prime}}=\delta_{m,m^{\prime}}.

(25)

Note that
{\bf a}_{m}
are not orthogonal in terms of the skew-symmetric inner product
{\bf a}_{m}^{\dagger}{\bf a}_{m^{\prime}}\neq\delta_{m,m^{\prime}}
. Thus, one would rather
say that
{\bf a}_{m}
and
{\bf b}_{m}
form a bi-orthogonal basis

. Since the vector space dimensionality is finite one can show
that the biorthoganal vectors resolve the identity operator as follows

\widehat{\mathbb{I}}=\sum_{m=1}^{M}{\bf a}_{m}{\bf b}_{m}^{\intercal}.

(26)

The matrix in the TCMT equations Eq. (
III
) can be written in terms of the left and right eigenvectors

i(\widehat{\Omega}-\omega\widehat{\mathbb{I}})+\widehat{\Gamma}=i\sum_{m=1}^{M}(\omega_{m}-\omega){\bf a}_{m}{\bf b}_{m}^{\intercal}.

(27)

The following useful relationships can be derived from Eq. (
26
) and Eq. (
27
)

\frac{1}{i(\widehat{\Omega}-\omega\widehat{\mathbb{I}})+\widehat{\Gamma}}=-i\sum_{m=1}^{M}\frac{1}{\omega_{m}-\omega}{\bf a}_{m}{\bf b}_{m}^{\intercal},

(28)

and

2\widehat{\Gamma}=i\sum_{m=1}^{M}(\omega_{m}-\omega){\bf a}_{m}{\bf b}_{m}^{\intercal}-i\sum_{m=1}^{M}(\omega_{m}^{*}-\omega){\bf b}^{*}_{m}{\bf a}_{m}^{\dagger}.

(29)

Now we are well equipped for analyzing Eq. (
III
). By plugging Eq. (
28
) into Eq. (
III
) one finds

\displaystyle i\sum_{m=1}^{M}\widehat{C}^{*}\widehat{D}\frac{{\bf a}_{m}{\bf b}_{m}^{\intercal}}{\omega_{m}-\omega}\widehat{K}^{\intercal}-i\sum_{m=1}^{M}\widehat{K}^{*}\frac{{\bf b}_{m}^{*}{\bf a}_{m}^{\dagger}}{\omega_{m}^{*}-\omega}\widehat{D}^{\dagger}\widehat{C}=

\displaystyle 2\sum_{m,m^{\prime}=1}^{M}\widehat{K}^{*}\frac{{\bf b}_{m}^{*}{\bf a}_{m}^{\dagger}}{\omega_{m}^{*}-\omega}\widehat{\Gamma}\frac{{\bf a}_{m^{\prime}}{\bf b}_{m^{\prime}}^{\intercal}}{\omega_{m^{\prime}}-\omega}\widehat{K}^{\intercal},

(30)

Substituting Eq. (
29

III

\widehat{K}^{*}{\bf b}_{m}^{*}{\bf a}_{m}^{\dagger}(\widehat{D}^{\dagger}\widehat{C}+\widehat{K}^{\intercal})=0.

(31)

After summation over

and application of Eq. (
26

\widehat{K}^{*}(\widehat{D}^{\dagger}\widehat{C}+\widehat{K}^{\intercal})=0.

(32)

In the same manner it can be shown that the symmetry of the
S
-matrix leads to

\widehat{D}\widehat{K}^{\intercal}=\widehat{K}\widehat{D}^{\intercal}.

(33)

It is worth mentioning that the above equation may also hold for lossy systems, see the supplemental to
Zhou et al. 2016
, since it can be derived solely relying on the Lorentz reciprocity.

Let us now analyze our findings. First of all we recall that according to
Suh et al. 2004
the TMCT matching condition in the presence of energy conservation and time-reversal symmetry have the following form

\displaystyle\widehat{C}\widehat{D}^{*}+\widehat{D}=0,

\displaystyle\widehat{D}=\widehat{K}.

(34)

One can easily see that if the matrices
\widehat{D}
and
\widehat{K}
satisfy Eq. (
III
) then they also solve the new found Eq. (
32
) and Eq. (
33
).
One can argue, however, that there are solutions of Eq. (
32
) and Eq. (
33
) not accommodated in Eq. (
III
). To see this, consider a unitary transformation in space of the resonator eigenmodes
{\bf a}\rightarrow{\bf a}^{\prime}
, bearing in mind that unitarity is necessary for maintaining Eq. (
20
). This unitary transfromation can be written as

{\bf a}=\widehat{U}{\bf a}^{\prime},

(35)

where
\widehat{U}
is a unitary matrix.
By plugging Eq. (
35
) into the TCMT equations Eq. (
III
) we find that the matrix quantities are transformed as follows

\displaystyle i\widehat{\Omega}^{\prime}+\widehat{\Gamma}^{\prime}=\widehat{U}^{\dagger}(i\widehat{\Omega}+\widehat{\Gamma})\widehat{U},

\displaystyle\widehat{D}^{\prime}=\widehat{D}\widehat{U},

\displaystyle\widehat{K}^{\prime}=\widehat{K}\widehat{U}^{*}.

(36)

One can easily check that the TCMT matching conditions given by Eq. (
20
), Eq. (
32
), and Eq. (
33
) are invariant to any unitary transformation whereas Eq. (
III
) is not. This is a consequence of that the matrix
\widehat{K}
is covariant with
{\bf a}
while
\widehat{D}
is contra-variant. The
\mathrm{U}(M)
invariant matching conditions that we have derived in this section obviously lead to new solutions not accommodated in Eq. (
III
) after some unitary transformations.

TCMT for a 2D metasurface

Let us now apply the results of the previous section to the system introduced in Sec.
II
.
The
4\!\times\!4

S
-matrix, which was defined in Eq. (

) can be rewritten in a more convenient form

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}{\bf s}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\leftarrow}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\!-\!)}\\
{\bf s}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\rightarrow}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\!-\!)}\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&\widehat{S}\\
\widehat{S}^{\intercal}&0\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}{\bf s}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\rightarrow}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\!+\!)}\\
{\bf s}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\leftarrow}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\!+\!)}\end{array}\right),

where

{\bf s}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\rightarrow}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\!+\!)},{\bf s}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\leftarrow}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\!-\!)}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}{s}_{1}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\!\pm\!)}\\
{s}_{2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\!\pm\!)}\end{array}\right),\ {\bf s}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\leftarrow}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\!+\!)},{\bf s}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\rightarrow}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\!-\!)}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}{s}_{3}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\!\pm\!)}\\
{s}_{4}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\!\pm\!)}\end{array}\right).

(38)

Note that the orientation of the subscript arrows in the above equations corresponds to the wave propagation direction with respect to the
x
-axis according to Fig.
2
. Thus, taking into account the momentum conservation
Zhou et al. 2016
the coupling and decoupling matrices can also be written in a simplified form as follows

\widehat{K}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&{\bf\kappa}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\rightarrow}}\\
{\bf\kappa}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\leftarrow}}&0\end{array}\right),\ {\bf\kappa}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\rightarrow}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\varkappa_{1}\\
\varkappa_{2}\end{array}\right),\ {\bf\kappa}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\leftarrow}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\varkappa_{3}\\
\varkappa_{4}\end{array}\right),

(39)

and

\widehat{D}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}{\bf d}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\leftarrow}}&0\\
0&{\bf d}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\rightarrow}}\end{array}\right),\ {\bf d}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\rightarrow}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}d_{3}\\
d_{4}\end{array}\right),\ {\bf d}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\leftarrow}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}d_{1}\\
d_{2}\end{array}\right),

(40)

where the subscript enumerates the scattering channels according to Fig.
2
.
The coupling and decoupling matrices are of a block form in compliance with the momentum conservation. In accordance with our definition of
\widehat{D}
and
\widehat{K}
the time domain TCMT equations (
III
) take the following form

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}a_{\scriptscriptstyle{\leftarrow}}\\
a_{\scriptscriptstyle{\rightarrow}}\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}-i\omega_{0}-\gamma&0\\
0&-i\omega_{0}-\gamma\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}a_{\scriptscriptstyle{\leftarrow}}\\
a_{\scriptscriptstyle{\rightarrow}}\end{array}\right)+\widehat{K}^{\intercal}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}{\bf s}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\rightarrow}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\!+\!)}\\
{\bf s}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\leftarrow}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\!+\!)}\end{array}\right),

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{c}{\bf s}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\leftarrow}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\!-\!)}\\
{\bf s}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\rightarrow}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\!-\!)}\end{array}\right)=\widehat{C}_{\scriptscriptstyle{4\!\times\!4}}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}{\bf s}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\rightarrow}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\!+\!)}\\
{\bf s}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\leftarrow}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\!+\!)}\end{array}\right)+\widehat{D}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}a_{\scriptscriptstyle{\leftarrow}}\\
a_{\scriptscriptstyle{\rightarrow}}\end{array}\right).

In the next step we apply the TCMT matching equations Eq. (
20
), Eq. (
32
), and Eq. (
33
). Equation (
33
) yields

\widehat{D}=A\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}{\bf\kappa}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\rightarrow}}&0\\
0&{\bf\kappa}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\leftarrow}}\end{array}\right),

(55)

where
A
is an arbitrary complex number. Substituting the above into

\widehat{D}^{\dagger}\widehat{D}=2\widehat{\Gamma}
gives

A=e^{i\zeta}\sqrt{\frac{2\gamma}{\kappa^{2}}},

(56)

where
\zeta\in[0,~2\pi)
is a real number and

\kappa^{2}={\bf\kappa}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\leftarrow}}^{\dagger}{\bf\kappa}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\leftarrow}}={\bf\kappa}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\rightarrow}}^{\dagger}{\bf\kappa}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\rightarrow}}

(57)

so the vectors
{\bf\kappa}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\leftarrow}}
and
{\bf\kappa}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\rightarrow}}
are equal in the norm. Before applying Eq. (
32
) we recall that according to Eq. (
10
) and Eq. (
16
) the
4\!\times\!4

S
-matrix can be written in a block form with a symmetric unitary matrix off the main diagonal at any given frequency. Here we apply this result to the matrix of the direct process which, being independent of frequency, can be written as follows

\widehat{C}_{\scriptscriptstyle{4\!\times\!4}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&\widehat{C}\\
\widehat{C}&0\end{array}\right),

(58)

where

\widehat{C}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\rho&i\tau\\
i\tau&\rho\end{array}\right).

(59)

After using Eq. (
32
) and assuming in accordance with
\gamma>0
that both vectors
{\bf\kappa}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\leftarrow}}
and
{\bf\kappa}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\rightarrow}}
have at least one non-zero element we arrive at

\displaystyle e^{-i\zeta}\sqrt{\frac{2\gamma}{\kappa^{2}}}\widehat{C}{\bf\kappa}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\rightarrow}}^{*}+{\bf\kappa}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\leftarrow}}=0,

\displaystyle e^{-i\zeta}\sqrt{\frac{2\gamma}{\kappa^{2}}}\widehat{C}{\bf\kappa}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\leftarrow}}^{*}+{\bf\kappa}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\rightarrow}}=0.

(60)

The solution of the above equation is any vector
\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{\rightarrow}}(\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{\leftarrow}})
that satisfies the normalization condition

\kappa^{2}=2\gamma.

(61)

Now, our goal is to write such a solution in a form symmetric with respect to both
\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{\rightarrow}}
and
\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{\leftarrow}}
. To do this
we introduce matrix
\widehat{B}
that is a symmetric unitary square root of
-\widehat{C}
so that

\displaystyle\widehat{B}\widehat{B}=-\widehat{C},

\displaystyle\widehat{B}^{\intercal}=\widehat{B},

\displaystyle\widehat{B}\widehat{B}^{\dagger}=\widehat{\mathbb{I}}.

(62)

Matrix
\widehat{B}
can be explicitly written as

\widehat{B}={\frac{1}{\sqrt{2(1+\rho)}}}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\tau&-i(1+\rho)\\
-i(1+\rho)&\tau\end{array}\right).

(63)

Having in mind Eq. (
IV
) the solution of Eq. (
IV
) can be written as

\displaystyle{\bf\kappa}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\rightarrow}}=\sqrt{2\gamma}e^{-i\zeta/2}\widehat{B}{\bf n}^{*},

\displaystyle{\bf\kappa}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\leftarrow}}=\sqrt{2\gamma}e^{-i\zeta/2}\widehat{B}{\bf n},

(64)

where
\bf n
is any
2\times 1
vector such as
{\bf n}^{\dagger}{\bf n}=1
.
The
S
-matrix for the right going waves is then written as

\widehat{S}=\widehat{C}+\frac{2\gamma}{i(\omega_{0}-\omega)+\gamma}\widehat{B}{\bf n}{\bf n}^{\dagger}\widehat{B}.

(65)

For the left-going waves the
S
-matrix is the transpose of Eq. (
65
) according to Eq. (
10
).
Using Eq. (
IV
) it is possible to demonstrate that
\widehat{S}
is a unitary matrix.

The unit vector
\bf n
can be parametrized by three variables

\displaystyle{\bf n}=e^{i\chi}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\cos\alpha~e^{+i\delta/2}\\
\sin\alpha~e^{-i\delta/2}\end{array}\right),

\displaystyle\chi\in[0,2\pi),\ \alpha\in[-\pi/2,\pi/2],\ \delta\in[0,\pi),

(68)

which leads to the following solution
for the transmittance

\displaystyle T=\frac{[\tau(\omega_{0}-\omega)+\rho\gamma\sin(2\alpha)\cos\delta]^{2}+\gamma^{2}\sin^{2}(2\alpha)\sin^{2}\delta}{(\omega_{0}-\omega)^{2}+\gamma^{2}}.

(69)

Note that the above result is independent of the choice of the incidence channel. In contrast, the analytic expressions for the squared resonant mode amplitude are different depending on the direction of incidence. When the metasurface is illuminated through channel
s_{1}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\!+\!)}
the solution reads

|a_{\scriptscriptstyle{\rightarrow}}|^{2}=\frac{\gamma[1-\rho\cos(2\alpha)+\tau\sin(2\alpha)\sin\delta]}{(\omega_{0}-\omega)^{2}+\gamma^{2}},

(70)

whereas with channel
s_{3}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\!+\!)}
the solution is

|a_{\scriptscriptstyle{\leftarrow}}|^{2}=\frac{\gamma[1-\rho\cos(2\alpha)-\tau\sin(2\alpha)\sin\delta]}{(\omega_{0}-\omega)^{2}+\gamma^{2}}.

(71)

The solution for the
S
-matrix Eq. (
65
) does not depend on the phases
\chi
and
\zeta
. Further on we set

\chi=0,\ \zeta=0,

which gives

{\bf d}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\leftarrow}}={\bf\kappa}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\rightarrow}},\ {\bf d}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\rightarrow}}={\bf\kappa}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\leftarrow}}.

(72)

The expression for the coupling vectors can be written as

\displaystyle{\bf\kappa}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\rightarrow},\scriptscriptstyle{\leftarrow}}=\!\!\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{1+\rho}}\!\left(\!\begin{array}[]{c}\tau\cos\alpha~e^{\mp i\delta/2}-i(1+\rho)\sin\alpha~e^{\pm i\delta/2}\\
-i(1+\rho)\cos\alpha~e^{\mp i\delta/2}+\tau\sin\alpha~e^{\pm i\delta/2}\end{array}\!\right)\!.

Since the Fano resonance is a product of interference between a resonant and a non-resonant pathways, the
following generic expression holds for the transmittance coefficients

T=\left|A_{1}+\frac{A_{2}e^{i\Delta}}{\Omega+i}\right|^{2},

(75)

where
A_{1},A_{2},\Delta_{2}
are real numbers, and
\Omega=(\omega-\omega_{0})/\gamma
.
Equation (
75
), besides
\Omega
, depends on three real-valued parameters, same as Eq. (
69
). It is possible to
show after some algebra that the parameters in Eq. (
69
) and Eq. (
75
) are linked by the following formulas

\displaystyle A_{1}

\displaystyle=|\tau|,

(76)

\displaystyle A_{2}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{(\tau+\sin(2\alpha)\sin\delta)^{2}+\rho^{2}\sin^{2}(2\alpha)\cos^{2}\delta},

(77)

\displaystyle\Delta

\displaystyle=-\arctan{\left(\frac{\rho\sin(2\alpha)\cos\delta}{\tau+\sin(2\alpha)\sin\delta}\right)}-\operatorname{sign}\left(\tau\right)\frac{\pi}{2}.

(78)

Alternatively, according to
Limonov et al. 2017
, the Fano formula (
75
) can be also expressed as

T=\left[\frac{(q+\Omega)^{2}}{1+\Omega^{2}}p+(1-p)\right]A_{1}^{2},

(79)

where
p\in[0,1]
is the interaction coefficient
Rybin et al. 2013

p=\frac{2F\cos^{2}\Delta}{F+2\sin\Delta+\sqrt{F^{2}+4F\sin\Delta+4}},

(80)

F=A_{2}/A_{1}
is the relative intensity, and
q
is the Fano asymmetry parameter,

q=\frac{F\cos\Delta}{p}.

(81)

Thus, we have demonstred that Eq. (
69
) complies with the generic theories of the Fano responce.

The symmetry operations can be described with the use of the first Pauli matrix

\hat{\sigma}_{x}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\

VI
Uniguided and unicoupled resonant modes

By definition an UGR is decoupled from outgoing radiation in one of the two outgoing channels. Without a loss of generality we consider the situation when there is no outgoing radiation in channel
s_{3}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\!-\!)}
, see Fig.
2
. Then, according to the definitions introduced in Eq. (
40
) for the right-going waves we should have
d_{3}=0
. By using Eq. (
IV
) and Eq. (
72
) one finds that
d_{3}=0
and
\gamma\neq 0
result in two possible sets of conditions

\displaystyle\tau=0,\ \ \alpha=0,

(97)

and

\displaystyle\delta=\frac{\pi}{2},

\displaystyle\tan\alpha=\frac{\tau}{1+\rho}.

(98)

The first set of conditions, Eq. (
97
), can be satisfied only in asymmetric metasurfaces and metasurfaces with in-plane mirror symmetry. The second set of conditions, Eq. (
VI
), can be satisfied in both asymmetric metasurfaces and metasurfaces with inversion symmetry. Note that in metasurfaces with in-plane mirror symmetry a UGR can only occur on the zero-transmittance background,
\tau=0
, which is consistent with
Zhang et al. 2022
. At the same time, since for metasurfaces with inversion symmetry
\alpha=\pm\pi/4
the second line in Eq. (
VI
) dictates that a UGR is only possible with zero-reflectance background,
\rho=0
, which is again consistent with
Zhang et al. 2022
. Either of the two sets of conditions, Eq. (
97
) and Eq. (
VI
), can be met by varying two geometric parameters. It is obviously a time-consuming computational task since two equations have to be satisfied simultaneously or, in the other words, UGRs are non-robust to variation of geometry.

In a metasruface with in-plane mirror symmetry
{\bf d}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\rightarrow}}={\bf\kappa}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\rightarrow}}
, so
\varkappa_{1}=0
, which means that the UGR is not only decoupled from outgoing light in a certain channel, but also can not be excited by incident light on the same side. The same conclusion holds true for the mode counter-propagating to the UGR. Thus, UGRs occur in pairs
d_{3}=0,\ \varkappa_{1}=0
and
d_{1}=0,\ \varkappa_{3}=0
.
In the case of the inversion symmetry the situation is similar, but, according to Eq. (
IV
), the zero coupling occurs on the other side of the metasurface
\varkappa_{2}=0
. The UGRs again emerge in pairs but now the zero-coupling and the zero-decoupling directions are swapped between the counter-propagating modes, i.e. we have
d_{3}=0,\ \varkappa_{2}=0
and
d_{2}=0,\ \varkappa_{3}=0
. The picture becomes more interesting when all symmetries are lifted and Eq. (
VI
) is satisfied. One can easily see that if
d_{3}=0
and
\alpha\neq\pm\pi/4
, then
\varkappa_{1,2}\neq 0
. Thus, the UGR is coupled with incident waves on both sides of the metasurface. We, however, remember that the coupling and decoupling vectors are swapped when the propagating direction is reversed. Therefore, we always have
\varkappa_{3}=d_{3}
. Thus, in an asymmetric metasurface an UGR is dual to a counter-propagating mode decoupled from incident radiation from one of the sides of the metasurfaces. We suggest to term this type of mode
unidirectional coupled resonant mode
(UCR). Notice, that, unlike UGR, the UCR can radiate into both half-spaces.

VII
Summary and conclusions

We proposed a temporal coupled mode theory (TCMT) for resonant response from quasi-guided modes in periodic dielectric metasurfaces. A generic set of constraints imposed onto the parameters of the temporal coupled mode theory by energy conservation and time-reversal symmetry is derived. It is demonstrated that the proposed approach allows for asymmetry between the coupling and decoupling coefficients. In particular, the TCMT approach is applied to the problem of Fano resonances induced by isolated quasi-guided modes in the regime of specular reflection. We derived a generic formula, Eq. (
69
), for the line-shape of the Fano resonance in transmittance for the lossless metasurfaces in the framework of 2D electrodynamics. An analytic expression for the Fano asymmetry parameter, Eq. (
81
), is found. It is demonstrated that our approach is applicable to all possible symmetries of the metasurface elementary cell. In particular, the approach explains the effects of the symmetry on the profile of the Fano resonance induced by an isolated high-
Q
mode. It is shown that the proposed approach correctly describes the presence of robust reflection and transmission zeros in the spectra as well as the spectral signatures of bound states in the continuum (BICs).

Thus far, there have been many attempts to derive the line shapes of the Fano resonances relying on the spectra of radiative modes
Blanchard et al. 2016
;
Alpeggiani et al. 2017b
;
Popov et al. 1986
;
Fehrembach et al. 2002
;
Gippius et al. 2005
;
Wang et al. 2013
;
Bykov and Doskolovich 2015
;
Wu et al. 2022
;
Yuan et al. 2022
including systems with reflectionless scattering modes
Zhou et al. 2016
;
Sweeney et al. 2020
.
In particular, the TCMT was applied for analyzing the fundamental bounds on decay rates in asymmetric single-mode optical resonators with two scattering channels in
Wang et al. 2013
, where it was shown that full reflection is always achievable at a Fano resonance, even for structures lacking mirror symmetries, whereas full transmission can only be seen in a symmetric configuration where the two decay rates are equal. In the context of the present work, the above result corresponds to normal incidence at which transmittance is always zero at the minimum of the resonant line if the high-
Q
mode is excited. At the same time reflectance zeros are only possible if either inversion or in-plane mirror symmetries are preserved. Our approach also complies with the earlier work on photonic crystal slabs
Gippius et al. 2005
where the authors reported that reflectivity peaks to unity near the quasiguided mode resonance for normal light incidence in the absence of diffraction, depolarisation, and
absorptive losses, while for the oblique incidence the full reflectivity is reached only in symmetrical systems. Interestingly, only in-plane mirror symmetry does not guaranty the absence of reflectance zeros which can be obtained by fine-tuning the system’s parameters
Yuan et al. 2022
, such reflectance zeros are, however, non-robust.

For oblique incidence it was demonstrated many times
Popov et al. 1986
;
Shipman and Tu 2012
;
Bykov and Doskolovich 2015
that both reflectance and transmittance zeros simultaneously occur in symmetric metasurfaces. More recently, Zhou and co-workers proposed a TCMT model that can take into account all possible symmetries of the elementary cell
Zhou et al. 2016
. Similarly to the model presented, their approach also implies asymmetry between the coupling and the decoupling matrices that manifests itself in the relationship
\widehat{D}=\widehat{K}\hat{\sigma}_{x}
. Moreover, the authors came to the same conclusion that the inversion symmetry only allows for robust reflectance zeros at oblique incidence, meanwhile the in-plane mirror symmetry only permits robust zeros in transmittance.
Nethertheless, the TCMT solution for reflectance derived in
Zhou et al. 2016
, is not generic as it does not reproduce the frequency independent transmittance/reflectance in the point unidirectional guided resonance (UGR)
com
. The approach presented here is
found to be consistent with the existence of UGRs. It is shown to correctly describe the transmittance and resonant field enhancement in the spectral vicinity of an UGR in metasurfaces with an asymmetric elementary cell. The analytic expression for the
S
-matrix in the UGR point is found to be consistent with the previously reported effect of intensity flattened phase shifting
Zhang et al. 2021
;
Zhang et al. 2022
;
Li et al. 2023
.
Furthermore, in the case of asymmetric metasurfaces the theory predicts that a UGR is dual to a counter-propagating mode of a peculiar type which is coupled with the outgoing wave on both sides of the metasurface but, nonetheless, exhibits only a single-sided coupling with incident waves. This type of mode, which we termed a unidirectional coupled resonance (UCR) is confirmed numerically.

The discussion is summarized in TABLE I. We believe that the results presented can be of use in engineering Fano response from dielectric metasurfaces as well as pave a way for application of TCMT in nanophotonics. In particular, generalizations for lossy systems are required for describing enhanced light-matter interaction in the critical coupling regime.
