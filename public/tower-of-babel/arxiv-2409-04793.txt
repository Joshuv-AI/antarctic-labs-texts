Action is the primary key: a categorical framework for episode description and logical reasoning
Yoshiki FUKADA

Abstract
This research presents a computational framework for describing and recognizing episodes and for logical reasoning. This framework, named cognitive-logs, consists of a set of relational and graph databases. Cognitive-logs record knowledge, particularly in episodes that consist of “ actions ” represented by verbs in natural languages and “ participants ” who perform the actions. These objects are connected by arrows (morphisms) that link each action to its participant and link cause to effect. Operations based on category theory enable comparisons between episodes and deductive inferences, including abstractions of stories. One of the goals of this study is to develop a database-driven artificial intelligence. This artificial intelligence thinks like a human but possesses the accuracy and rigour of a machine. The vast capacities of databases (up to petabyte scales in current technologies) enable the artificial intelligence to store a greater volume of knowledge than neural-network based artificial intelligences. Cognitive-logs serve as a model of human cognition and designed with references to cognitive linguistics. Cognitive-logs also have the potential to model various human mind activities.

Implementation of logical reasoning on computer systems has been an important research topic

Logical reasoning processes like “

A notable achievement in this area is the development of the logic programming language Prolog

The Prolog language has been successfully applied to various systems, such as expert systems

Representations of knowledge in graphical network has also been a long-standing area of study.
Semantic networks were introduced as directed labelled graphs

Later, a more restricted concept—knowledge graphs—was defined
[

By applying category theory, ontology logs, or ologs, were introduced

While logic programming has a long research history and numerous successful outcomes,
its application to real-world scenarios has been limited.
The challenge in such real-world applications lies in episode description.
In a logic depicted by the “
if A then B
” formula,

A
and
B
are not merely objects but scenarios,
necessitating comparisons of real episodes to these scenarios.
No model has yet been developed for making such comparisons between episodes or scenarios.

The developments in neural network-based artificial intelligence show significant progress.
The invention of the transformer
[
6
]
is a prominent milestone.
However, current neural network-based artificial intelligences do not yet seem
to replicate human thinking.
Services that use large-scale language models
provide “wonderful” responses, giving the impression that
“logical reasonings in artificial intelligence has been realized.”
Nevertheless, their learning processes require an enormous amount of data.
In contrast, humans are capable of learning from even a single experience.
Moreover, these large-scale language models are “black boxes,”
hence they lack explainability.

Some arthropods exhibit amazing intelligences.
Even common spiders demonstrate impressive abilities in constructing their webs.
Human brains have extremely large frontal lobes,
with the number of neurons being
10^{6}
times greater than that of arthropods.
It is questionable what accounts for the difference in brain size.
Logical reasoning is something these small creatures do not poses.
Perhaps neural-network architecture is not efficient for logical reasoning.

This research aims to develop a database-driven artificial intelligence that thinks like a human.
There are several other projects also aiming to develop human-like artificial intelligences.
One ambitious project
[
7
]
seeks to reproduce a brain
as an assembly of neural-network based machine-learning.
In contrast, this research constructs
a mathematical model suitable for von Neumann architectures.

This paper illustrates the design of a new cognitive model
using category theory—cognitive-logs.
It enables efficient episode description and knowledge representation.
A functor among cognitive-logs represents similarities between an episode and a scenario,
replicating rule-based logical reasoning.
The basic idea of the model draws from cognitive linguistics.
Discussions from the viewpoint of cognitive science are also presented.
It will be shown that various inference processes—abstractions, deductions, inductions, analogies, and metaphors—can be modelled using cognitive-logs and operations on them.

2.2
Action is primary entity

The motivation of this study is to recreate the thought process of humans.
Human cognitions are classified into things-like elements and process-like elements,
which correspond to nouns and verbs, respectively
[
10
]
.
Their boundaries between nouns and verbs are vague
[
11
]
.
In most natural languages, verbs can be converted into nouns.
A verb represents an action, and the action represents the process.
Although actions do not exist as real objects,
our brains perceive actions as entities.
Perhaps, our brains have “action cells” similar to the grandmother cells.
In Japanese spoken language, subjects are often omitted, but verbs are not.
As Tomasello
[
12
]
pointed out,
actions are the primary entities in an episode.

Most of these grammars are combinations of subject–verb,

The unit of subject–verb combination is thus an essential unit of episode description.

Causal relationships in the real world are complex,

An effect occurs under multiple necessary conditions,

(In other words, the sufficient condition is “the last piece” of the necessary conditions.)
Here, an action represents an event.

Arrows of “

A pair of arrows “

Similarity, association, classification, and description of characteristics

4.1
Be-logs

Our basic cognition involves classification, such as “a pigeon is a bird.”
Such classifications are based on similarity recognition,
which is implemented through associations.
These cognitions are mostly expressed using a
be
-verb
and hold a special (more essential) position.
Be-logs are categorical networks for
similarity, association, classification, and description of characteristics.
The name “be” represents
be
-verbs.

Similarity is an essential part of cognition.
Our similarity recognition has the following two characteristics.

•

Asymmetry: The similarity of
A
to
B

is not necessarily equal to the similarity of
B
to
A
[

]
.

•

Composition is not guaranteed:
A
being similar to
B
, and
B
being similar to
C
,

A

C

The following e-log satisfies these characteristics:

\begin{array}[]{ccccccc}&who&&cause&&who&\\
\ulcorner\text{A}\urcorner&\longleftarrow&\ulcorner\text{resembles}\urcorner&\longleftarrow&\ulcorner\text{is resembled}\urcorner&\longrightarrow&\ulcorner\text{B}\urcorner\text{.}\\
\end{array}

(4)

However, this form is redundant.
Since similarities and classifications are not events,
“

”–“

Another category seems more appropriate:

\begin{array}[]{cccccc}&source&&target&\\
\ulcorner\text{A}\urcorner&\longleftarrow&\ulcorner\text{resembles}\urcorner&\longrightarrow&\ulcorner\text{B}\urcorner\text{.}\\
\end{array}

(5)

(6)

4.2
Theory of classification

This subsection introduces two theories of classification.
One is quite rigorous and deductive,
while the other is intuitive but well-models our cognition.
Be-logs effectively depict both theories.

4.2.1
Classification based on characteristics

A be-log records the characteristics of participants.
A set of characteristics defines a class.
For example, the class “birds” is defined as:
having a beak, being covered with feathers, having wings, and laying eggs.

Classification is hierarchical.
The class “Aves” (birds) is classified into orders (in biological terminology)
such as Galliformes (turkeys, chickens, or other heavy-bodied ground-feeding birds),
Columbiformes (pigeons and doves), and others.
Birds in each order retain the characteristics of Aves;
namely, the set of Aves’ characteristics is a subset of the set of characteristics for each order.
These relationships correspond to a tree of classification.

Consider classes
A
and
B
,
and their set of characteristics,
Ch(A)
and
Ch(B)
.
If
A
belongs to
B
(
A\subseteq B
),
then
Ch(B)
is a subset of
Ch(A)
(
Ch(A)\supseteq Ch(B)
).

This relationship induces a definition of similarity based on characteristics as follows.

S_{A\to B}\equiv\frac{|Ch(A)\cap Ch(B)|}{|Ch(B)|}

(8)

where
S_{A\to B}
is the similarity indicating that
A
resembles
B
,
and
|\>|
denotes the number of elements in a set.
Using this definition,
S_{A\to B}=1
means
that “
A
belongs to
B
” and there is a preorder
(A\leqslant B)
.
This similarity definition clarifies the applicability of a syllogism.
Since a preorder relation is a category,

S_{A\to B}=1
means
A\to B
.
A syllogism such as “
A is B. B is C. Therefore, A is C.
”
is only applicable when
S_{A\to B}=1
and
S_{B\to C}=1
.
This syllogism corresponds to the composition of

A\to B
and
B\to C
.
This similarity definition can be extended to other kinds of feature matchings,
such as point clouds or images.

Recent advanced DNA analysis has replaced
classical characteristics-based Linnaean taxonomy with
evolutionary-tree-based classification.
However, such characteristics-based classifications seem essential in our cognition.

4.2.2
Classification based on prototypes

An infant may recognize similarities between pigeons, crows, and some other birds,
and create a group of similar members (birds).
Such a process is considered to be an important part of our knowledge construction.
In cognitive linguistics
[
15
]
,
a set of such similar members forms a class,
and representative members (real world examples) of the class are called “prototypes”.

Between prototypes, the “degree of similarity” can be evaluated.
Since similarities are asymmetric,
similarities in a class form a directed and weighted graph.
An appropriate graph-analysis can define a “centre” of the class
and the “distance” from the centre to a prototype.
These centre and distance concepts are important in the theory of cognitive linguistics.
For example, a “pigeon” is close to the centre of “birds,” while a “penguin” is far.

It is possible to extract common features (characteristics) from the prototypes of a class
and create a class based on these characteristics.
Classification based on prototypes is more primitive
than that based on characteristics.

5.1
S-logs or scenario-logs as references

One of the simplest logical inference might be
1+1=2
.
However, this formula is invalid in Boolean algebra.
This simple example shows that “absolute logic” does not exist;
instead we “refer to rules.”

We perform various complex logical inferences:
future prediction based on physical laws,
judgement applying ethical rules,
and constructing a new idea by joining multiple laws and rules.
All of these inferences are based on “comparison with laws and rules.”
In a broader sense, laws and rules are “reference scenarios.”
Logical reasoning is implemented as a comparison between an episode and reference scenarios.
These episodes and scenarios are represented with e-logs;
hence, the comparison between them is a functor.
Completeness of a functor represents the similarity
between an episode and a scenario.

Considering the above,
s-logs or scenario-logs are presented as categories that have the same structure as e-logs.
This definition is not strict.
Just as we refer to our own experiences,
an e-log that depicts a concrete episode can be used as a reference.

5.8
Generation of s-logs

An e-log is a record of episodes,
whereas s-logs represent knowledge.
Manual installation of s-logs for an artificial intelligence,
similar to how humans read books, is possible.
However, automated knowledge acquisition from experiences, i.e.,
the conversion of an e-log into an s-log is an important process in learning.
Basically, s-logs are variants of e-logs,
so, their structures are the same.
The difference between s-logs and e-logs is
that an s-log depicts a generalized scenario,
whereas an e-log depicts an individual episode.
Hence, the description of s-logs must be as follows:

•

Participants are indicated using classes.

•

Timestamps are indicated as relative temporal orders.

The process to generate an s-log can be as follows:
extract a partial e-log from the e-log that contains the specific scenario,
perform appropriate abstraction,
and overwrite participants and timestamps.

5.9
Planning and invention

Planning and invention are the ultimate goals of applications of cognitive-logs.
Such a process can be modelled as an inverse process of story comprehension.
It consists of the following steps:

•

Choose some s-logs from the storage of knowledge (s-logs).

•

Assemble an s-log so that the scenario ends with a preferable result.

•

Convert the s-log into an e-log of the plan
by assigning each participant in the s-log to a participant (existing object) in the e-log.

Since the number of possible combinations of s-logs will be huge,
the computational cost seems high.
This is why such creative thinking is difficult.

6
Functor search and its evaluation

Cognitive-log is a cognition model.
Its realistic manipulation accepts incompleteness of functors.
When we observe an episode, we try to understand it.
Such an understanding is modelled as searching for a functor from the e-log into an s-log,
where the s-log represents some knowledge.
The feeling of “understanding” is modelled as obtaining high completeness of the functor.
Thus, such evaluation of functor represents an important process of cognition.

6.1
Functor evaluation based on structure

A functor is a structural matching between categories.
Mathematical completeness is the basis of functor evaluation.
A homomorphism in a category can be represented using a logical matrix
whose entries and operations are Boolean algebra.
Here, we define
E
as the arrows of “
who,
”

S
as the arrows of “
cause-S,
”
and
N
as the arrows of “
cause-N.
”
Basically, a functor does not differentiate the type of arrows.
However, the rule of causality restricts the conversion,
namely, an effect occurs after the cause.
This relationship can be satisfied by separating the conversions for
S
and
N
.
The conversions of
S
and
N
satisfy the following equations:

\sum^{\infty}_{\acute{n}=1}(\acute{S}_{s}+\acute{N}_{s}^{Tri})^{\acute{n}}+I_{Ss}=P_{S}\left(\sum^{\infty}_{n=1}(S_{e}+N_{e}^{Tri})^{n}\right)P_{S}^{T}+I_{Ss}.

(15)

\sum^{\infty}_{\acute{n}=1}(\acute{N}_{s}+\acute{S}_{s}^{Tri})^{\acute{n}}+I_{Ss}=P_{S}\left(\sum^{\infty}_{n=1}(N_{e}+S_{e}^{Tri})^{n}\right)P_{S}^{T}+I_{Ss}.

(16)

Where, the suffix
e
denotes the e-log and
s
denotes the s-log respectively,

P_{S}
is the conversion matrix that indicates the mappings between actions.
The computation must use Boolean algebra; namely,
1+1=1
.

I_{Ss}
is an identity matrix that represents the identity morphisms.
Since these computations are Boolean, the sums with the identity matrix cannot be removed.

N_{e}^{Tri}
and
S_{e}^{Tri}
are “
cause
” arrows that belong to trivial causal-relationships.
Since arrows of “
cause-S
” and “
cause-N
”
in a trivial causal relationship are exchangeable,
these exceptional handling are required.
The sums of the power series of
S_{s}
and
S_{e}
represent composites of “

” arrows.
Under the rule of causality,
the “

” matrices
S
and
N
are strictly triangular matrix.
Hence,
\acute{S}_{s}^{\acute{n}}
and
S_{e}^{n}
become zero for finite
\acute{n}
and
n
.
The power series may rapidly decay.

The conversion of
E
in a functor from an e-log into an s-log is as follow:

\acute{E}_{s}=P_{E}E_{e}P_{S}^{T}

(17)

P_{E}
is the conversion matrix that indicates the mappings between participants.

If the functor is complete,

\acute{E}_{s}=E_{s}
,
\acute{S}_{s}=S_{s}
.
Because the conversions are functions,
there is one and only one entry of
P_{E}
and
P_{S}
in each column whose value is
1
,
while the others are zero.
Moreover, the converted e-log must satisfy its functional relationship from actions to participants.
Namely, if a participant does not have its morphism function into the s-log,
its action(s) cannot have a morphism function into the s-log.
This requirement falls under the following mathematical rule:
if
P_{E}E_{e}
has a column with all entries being zero,
then all entries in the corresponding column
(the column number is the same as
P_{E}E_{e}
) of
P_{S}
must be zero.

S-logs are built so that all their objects are essential.
That means the functor must be surjective.
A surjective functor satisfies the following requirement:
at least one entry in each row of
P_{E}
and
P_{S}
has to be non-zero.

If all objects in the e-log are essential,
the functor must be injective.
An injective functor satisfies the following requirement:
at least one entry in each column of
P_{E}
and
P_{S}
has to be non-zero.

A complete functor satisfies the above rules.
Incompleteness of the functor may indicate incompatibility between the e-log and s-log;
perhaps the s-log was inadequate.
However, the incompleteness may indicate things that are going to happen
or hidden events that occurred in the past.
An inference according to the laws (§
5.6
) applies to such a non-surjective functor.

7.1.1
Deductions

Deduction, or deductive inference, is a type of logical inference process
which draw conclusion from a premise.
The aforementioned process of inferences according to laws is a type of deduction.
An s-log depicts basic logics such as “
if A then B
” including mathematical theorems.
And a functor search finds matchings.
Composition of multiple s-logs and modification using functors
is also a form of deduction that creates new theorems.

7.1.2
Inductions

Induction is a process of finding a law from experiences.

Conversion of an e-log or e-logs into an s-log may represents
the most typical form of induction, known as enumerative induction.
The most reasonable process involves gathering “similar” experiences
and storing their “average” in an s-log.
Functor operations implement these similarity evaluations and averaging processes.
If an experience was “reasonable,” even a single experience can create an s-log.
Deductive inferences evaluate its “reasonableness.”
Unlike reinforcement learning,
humans do not require thousands of experiences.
Such generation of an s-log may resemble such human learning.

Abduction or retroduction is also a kind of induction;
It generates a hypothesis from experiences and other laws.
This process may appear to fill in incomplete parts of an s-log or an e-log
rather than generating a new one.
This process may involve many deductions.

7.1.3
Analogy and metaphor

As Lakoff and Johnson demonstrated in their famous literature
[
25
]
,
metaphors are not only rhetoric in literary works
but also important parts of cognitive processes.
According to them, most of the human conceptual system is made up of metaphors.
In the “ARGUMENT IS WAR” example,
many elements in an argument are expressed using elements of war:

attack a position
,
indefensible
,
strategy
,

new line of attack
,
win
, and
gain ground
[
25
]
.
This metaphor ignores the difference between the qualitative properties of war and that of argument,
such as the existence of violent factors of war:
death of humans, blood, pain, and others.
However, this metaphor preserves the logical structures of the concept of war,
i.e., the causal relationships between the elements of war.
A metaphor “maps” one episode or concept onto another while
preserving the structures of the episode/concept.
Such a metaphor systematically ignores some of these properties.
Functors are structure preserving maps.
A functor between cognitive-logs with modifications in similarity evaluation may resemble a metaphor.

7.1.4
Psychological application

In the functors shown in Fig.
11

the dolly is mapped to the worker.
This mapping describes the episode so that the dolly is considered a part of the worker.
In the same way, various episodes where workers use tools
can be abstracted as the tools being parts of the worker.
In the real world, it is quite common for expert technicians to feel like their tools are parts of their body.
It is also well-known phenomenon that some drivers have the illusion
that the power of the car is their own power.
Functors between cognitive-logs may represent such illusions.

7.2
Theory of understanding

Humans are creatures who aspire to understand.
Understanding brings the abilities to predict the future and manipulate the environment.
The purpose of science is understanding and prediction.
However, in recent scientific developments, significant estrangements are seen
between the science of understanding and the science of prediction
[
26
]
.
Recent neural-network-based artificial-intelligences offer significant predictive abilities;
however, their predictions do not come with understanding
and hence lack explainability and reliability.
Cognitive-log is fully explainable and offers a framework for artificial intelligence
that includes understanding.
This subsection discusses human thinking and understanding.

7.2.1

According to a research

The film “Tron” released in 1982 and famous for being one of the first to adopt computer graphics,

The film introduced many advanced and innovative concepts for computers and networks.
Unfortunately, the film was not successful in the business,

As declared in §
5.7
,
comprehension is finding a functor from the e-logs of the story to s-logs
that depict known scenarios.
Comprehension implies establishing an abstraction and finding reasons why it goes so.
Finding a functor into an s-log represents such a cognitive process of comprehension.
Perhaps humans are creatures who feel good when such comprehension—finding structural matching between a known scenario and an episode—is achieved.

7.2.2
Meaning is structure and pre-installation of initial knowledge

The hypothetical neuron cell, the grandmother cell, does not store information about the grandmother,
but the network structure around the cell does.

As discussed above (§
6.3
),
comparison between the episodes “Bob loves Alice” and “A worker carried a cargo”
requires comparison between “carried” and “love.”
Information concerning similarities and classifications stored in be-logs forms a network.
Any process of inference eventually falls on comparisons of network structures.
Thus, meaning is structure.
However, it is unclear whether a network solely illustrates a concept.
A brain consists of many types of neurons.
Concepts of “good” and “bad”
may be implemented by using connections to specific neurons,
such as sympathetic nerves and parasympathetic nerves.
In the same way, an artificial intelligence using cognitive-logs may require
some specific IDs that denote specific meanings and are given in a top-down manner.

7.2.4
Two systems

“When you have eliminated the impossible, whatever remains,
however improbable, must be the truth.”—Arthur Conan Doyle, The Case-Book of Sherlock Holmes.
This short quote shows that
rigorous logic sometimes seems improbable in our intuitive reasoning.

Kahneman presented the idea
that our thinking operates through two distinct systems.
System 1 is fast and intuitive,
while System 2 is slow but accurate and logical
[
28
]
.
Neural-network-based artificial intelligences resemble System 1,
and reasoning based on cognitive-logs may resemble System 2.

Here is an idea to use neural-network-based large language models (LLM)
for processing cognitive-logs.
Since cognitive-logs are relational databases that form a chain of symbols,
they are similar to languages.
A trained LLM that answers corresponding functors into s-logs for an input e-log
may resemble logical reasoning under System 1.
However, answers from such a System 1 are limited to learned knowledge,
and its accuracy may be compromised.
On the other hand, operations using category theory on cognitive-logs
can provide rigorously correct answers, much like Sherlock Holmes.

8
Concluding remarks

This study presents
a design for a categorical framework for episode description and logical reasoning.
It has been seen that
cognitive-logs and the processes within them provide mathematical models for various inferences.
A cognitive-log is a cluster of relational and graph databases which are categories.
An episode is recorded in an e-log that consists of “Actions” and “Participants.”
An arrow of “who” connects an “Action” to the “Participant” who performs the action.
An arrow of “
cause
” depicts a causal relationship between “
Actions.
”
It is remarkable that actions are explicit entities.
Actions are the main components of an episode,
thus action is the primary key.
Knowledge concerning classification, similarities, and characteristics is recorded in be-logs,
and knowledge of laws and rules is recorded in s-logs.
Functor searches among cognitive-logs
represent knowledge-based logical inferences.
Abstraction of an episode and comprehension of a story are also modelled.
Implementing these operations in a computer system enables
a database-driven artificial intelligence that thinks like a human.
Thus, cognitive-logs show promising potential for a new framework of artificial intelligence.

This research extensively adopts findings from cognitive linguistics.
Operations within cognitive-logs offer models of linguistic phenomena.
Analogy and metaphor are modelled as functors under broader similarity evaluation.
Prototype theory was re-organized as graph structures in be-logs.
There is also a possibility that cognitive-logs can model psychological activities.
Further research may clarify their effectiveness.
