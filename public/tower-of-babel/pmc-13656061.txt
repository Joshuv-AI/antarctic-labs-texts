Parallel-bar versus cross-bar configurations in the Nuss procedure: addressing cohort overlap and morphology-based selection criteria
Authors: Paula Duarte D’Ambrosio, Lucas Monteiro Delgado, Rachid Eduardo Noleto da Nóbrega Oliveira, Enzo Campedelli Borges, Mathias de Souza Ribeiro, Giulia Moraes Piazzi, Gustavo Campos Sabino, Patrícia Viana, Rafael Rodrigues Lourencini, Bernardo Mazzini Ketzer, José Ribas Milanez de Campos
Published: 2026

We thank Dr. Tedde for his interest in our systematic review and meta-analysis comparing cross-bar (CB) and parallel-bar (PB) configurations in the minimally invasive repair of pectus excavatum (MIRPE) (1). We note that five of the ten references cited in the commentary (2) are self-citations to the author’s own technical notes, case series, and opinion pieces; we address the substantive points below.

The preference for “MIRPE” over “Nuss procedure”, and the “Cup” versus “Grand Canyon” classification, reflect editorial and conceptual positions already discussed at length in our article, citing the same body of work by Park HJ (3,4)—two of whose own primary studies are, in fact, included in our meta-analysis. Only one of the four included studies applied this classification in a form that could be harmonized across cohorts, which is precisely why we recommend individualized, morphology-tailored technique selection rather than a uniform preference—a position consistent with, not contradicted by, the point raised. Regarding bridge-plate stabilization, we already identify inconsistent reporting of fixation method as a source of heterogeneity in our original Discussion. We note that the claim of “no instances of bar displacement, a finding consistent with global literature” rests in part on a single-patient case report of the sandwich technique for pectus carinatum—a different deformity from the excavatum cohorts discussed here, and case-level evidence (n=1) that cannot substantiate a population-level claim.

The one clinically material point concerns possible cohort linkage: Sayan et al. (5) and Ersöz et al. (6) share a co-author (Yüksel) and overlapping Turkish enrollment [2012–2018]; Moon et al. (7) and Hyun et al. (8) share a corresponding author (Park HJ) at the same institution, with overlapping enrollment [2016–2018]. Shared authorship and overlapping windows are circumstantial: they show overlap cannot be formally excluded from aggregate data, not that it occurred. This is precisely the class of limitation our ROBINS-I and GRADE assessments were designed to capture, and did capture—risk of bias for all four studies was already rated moderate to serious, and certainty of evidence for every pooled outcome, including postoperative Haller index (HI), was already rated low to very low. The HI outcome, our only significant finding [mean difference =−0.10; 95% confidence interval (CI): −0.17 to −0.03], was pooled from only Moon et al. (7) and Hyun et al. (8), as stated plainly in our manuscript. Should the original investigators confirm patient-level overlap, a sensitivity re-analysis excluding the smaller cohort would be warranted, and we would welcome that clarification; absent confirmation, shared institution remains a hypothesis, not a demonstrated duplication. Even discounting the HI result entirely, our central conclusion—equivalence between CB and PB, with technique individualized to morphology and surgeon expertise—is unchanged, since we already characterized this difference as being of uncertain clinical relevance. A comparable, lower-stakes observation applies to Sayan/Ersöz, whose shared outcomes (operative time, length of stay, pleural effusion) were already non-significant and already flagged for substantial heterogeneity in our sensitivity analysis.

We confirm that 51 of 157 CB patients (33%) in Hyun et al. (8) received a third “XI” bar, a detail we ourselves reported transparently in our original Tab. 2, not one uncovered by the commentary. Hyun et al. (8) reported outcomes only for the pooled CB group, so bar-count stratification was unavailable from published data—an inherent constraint of aggregate-data synthesis, not specific to our analysis; notably, their own subgroup data show no significant complication difference between two- and three-bar configurations.

We would also caution against the inference that increasing CB use among “the most experienced” surgeons can be read from publication dates alone; this is not a validated proxy for experience or practice patterns and is not supported by data within the pooled studies.

In summary, most of the points raised reiterate the authors’ own prior publications rather than engage with our methodology, and the one material observation describes a limitation our risk-of-bias and certainty-of-evidence framework had already identified and rated. It does not alter our central conclusion: current data do not support a routine preference for either configuration based on perioperative outcomes alone, and technique selection should remain individualized to deformity morphology, surgeon expertise, and institutional resources.
