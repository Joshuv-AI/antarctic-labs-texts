The Seasons of a Career in Physical Chemistry: Olivia Harper Wilkins
Authors: Olivia H. Wilkins, Joan-Emma Shea, Gregory D. Scholes, Gemma C. Solomon
Published: 2026

“The Seasons of a Career in Physical Chemistry” is an interview series published across ACS Physical Chemistry Au, The Journal of Physical Chemistry A/B/C, and The Journal of Physical Chemistry Letters. The series celebrates the people behind physical chemistry, giving researchers at all career stages the opportunity to tell their own stories: how they came to be where they are today, the joys and challenges they have encountered along the way, and what they imagine as they look to the future. Whether reflecting on a lifetime of work or navigating the uncertainties of an earlier career stage, each interviewee offers a window into research environments and trajectories as varied as the science itself, with the hope of providing inspiration for the broader physical chemistry community. In this interview, we feature Olivia Harper Wilkins, Assistant Professor of Chemistry at Dickinson College.

Biographical History

Early Influences

What early experiences, people, or interests first led you toward science, and ultimately to physical chemistry?

I am grateful to have parents who have always fostered my curiosity. When I was little, my mom would find me on the floor carefully inspecting the vacuum cleaner to try to figure out how it worked. I also enjoyed digging in the yard in search of cool rocks and fossils (the latter of which I never found). I enjoyed going to the library and checking out books about natural phenomena, especially the explosion of Mount St. Helens. Family vacations or day trips usually took us to some kind of historic site or a scenic railroadplaces where I learned about the evolution of industry and science. I wanted to be any and every type of scientist I could. When I first took chemistry in high school, I was in awe of it being “the central science” and saw the field as a way to not have to decide what kind of a scientist I wanted to be when I grew up. If I became a chemist, I figured I could be whatever kind of scientist I wanted. I also loved math, so I was fascinated with how algebraic balancing chemical equations was. I think calculus is so much fun, so it is no surprise I ultimately became a physical chemist.

Path into the Field

Was your path into physical chemistry a straight one or a winding one? What were some key transitions along the way?

My path into physical chemistry probably began when my family and I visited Cass Scenic Railroad in West Virginia when I was seven. As we drove through the mountains of Pocahontas County, giant structures resembling satellite dishes grew out of the trees. I vividly remember drawing some of the dishes in my sketchpad. When I was 18, my family returned to the area to learn about the giant structures, and we found Green Bank Observatory (then part of the National Radio Astronomy Observatory, or NRAO). There, I learned about how radio telescopes explore the invisible universe, and I became enamored with the Green Bank Telescope, which is taller than the Statue of Liberty and has a dish that could fit two football fields on its surface. I could not stop thinking about radio telescopes, and it became my dream to one day work at the NRAO.

Without that passion for radio telescopes, I’m not sure I would have become a physical chemist. I took Thermodynamics and Kinetics in my second year of college, and there were some circumstances that made the lecture aspect of the course incredibly difficult for me and made me question whether I was actually cut out to be a chemist. The following summer, I ended up working on my dream: I was a research assistant at the NRAO in Green Bank, West Viriginia. While there, I first heard the term “astrochemistry” and was enraptured. I learned I could combine my obsession with radio telescopes with my degree in chemistry to study the wider chemical universe. That ultimately led me to pursue a PhD in Chemistry at Caltech. I think that is when I first realized I was a physical chemist.

Identity as a Scientist

How do you describe your scientific identity today, and how has that description changed over time?

I would describe my scientific identity as “astrochemist”, and while I have held that identity for over a decade now, its meaning has certainly changed. Throughout much of graduate school, being a physical chemist sometimes felt like being a miscellaneous chemist; physical chemist graduate scholars at Caltech seemed like they were more dispersed, especially in terms of their physical space on campus, than other subdisciplines, and many of us did not work in laboratories. There were many days when I felt like I was more of an astronomer than a chemist. Now, though, I firmly identify as a chemist.

My scientific identity has also included “educator”, something that has taken many forms. As a graduate scholar, I taught scientific writing and was heavily involved in the effective teaching pedagogy community at Caltech, and I also did a lot of public outreach. As a postdoctoral fellow, I was no longer in the classroom as an instructor, but I continued to give public talks at observatories and libraries, and I went to K-12 classrooms for career days. Now, I’m back to teaching in the college classroom in addition to building a research program at Dickinson.

“Artist” is also a key part of my identity. I illustrate summaries of conference presentations, and I did many of the illustrations in Astrochemistry, the volume I wrote for the ACS In Focus series. I also enjoy painting, drawing, woodburning, and now ceramics, and much of the art I create is inspired by science

Career Development and Scientific Focus Areas

Evolution of Research Focus

How have your research interests evolved throughout your career, and what factorsscientific, institutional, or personaldrove those changes?

I have gone back and forth between telescope observations and laboratory work throughout my career so far. My undergraduate research experiences in astrochemistry were observational and driven by a passion for large radio telescopes. During a summer at the Harvard-Smithsonian Center for Astrophysics, I learned about spectral line catalogues because I was using them to identify molecules in space. I became curious about what it looked like to work on the other side of the database, in the lab, which led me to a Fulbright Research Fellowship in Germany to conduct laboratory work and data analysis at the Universität zu Köln (University of Cologne) for the Cologne Database for Molecular Spectroscopy, the premier molecular line catalogue used for astrochemical observations.

At that point, I really liked working with both telescope data and in the lab, so I went to Caltech intent on doing both during my PhD. I managed to have a telescope proposal selected around the end of my first year of my PhD, which ended up giving me a big data set to work with and an even bigger desire to get more telescope data. I had plenty to keep me busy with observations, and observational research seemed to afford much greater flexibility, which was great for me as a young parent. I abandoned my goal of doing experimental work in my PhD. When I got near the end of writing my dissertation, however, my PI forwarded a call for applications to do cosmic ice experiments at NASA. Without any graduate laboratory experience, I figured the opportunity was a long shot, but I applied anyway, trying to sell myself on my experience with observations (since astrochemical experiments aim to explain observations of extraterrestrial, cosmic laboratories) and rotational spectroscopy (the technique used in radio astronomy and in the lab to which I was applying to work). I was awarded a NASA Postdoctoral Program (NPP) Fellowship, and I embarked on an incredibly challenging and rewarding journey learning about and conducting cosmic ice experiments at NASA Goddard Space Flight Center.

Now, as a faculty member myself, I am in a situation where I can merge my passions for observational and experimental work into one research program.

Signature Contributions

What do you consider your most meaningful scientific contributions, and what makes them significant to you or the field?

The first thing that comes to mind is that I like to infuse science and artwork. One way that I do that is to illustrate summaries of talks I see, especially at conferences. People seem to really enjoy seeing their work transformed into art, even if just a small sketch. The smiles people have on their faces when they see how I interpreted the key points of their work with pen and colored pencil brings me so much joy. We cannot have science without the people who do it, and bringing a personal touch to how I present and understand science makes me feel more grounded in that reality, especially when it often feels like we’re supposed to forget that the scientific work we do is done by people.

I’m also really proud of the work I’ve done to map molecules in star-forming regions at high resolution. So far, not many people have done this kind of work, and I’m excited about how looking at the changes in molecule abundances or temperature across a nebula can provide insights into the intimate connection between chemistry and astrophysical environment that is not always feasible with characteristics averaged over an entire nebula. I started this work during my PhD and spent a lot of time writing and revising computer code written in Python, and now I get to continue that work with my own students. I feel like I have a lot of ownership in this type of work, and that is a good feeling to have about something you have worked really hard on.

Navigating Career Decisions

What were some of the most consequential career decisions you have made, and how did you approach those choices?

One of the most consequential career decisions I’ve made was applying for a NASA Postdoctoral Program (NPP) fellowship to do laboratory astrochemistry research at NASA’s Goddard Space Flight Center. I had an interest in lab work, but I almost did not apply since I had no graduate experience in experimental work. My graduate advisor, Dr. Geoff Blake, had forwarded me the call for proposals, however, so I felt like that was a sign that I should apply. I’m so glad I did because I learned so much as an NPP Fellow working in the SubLIME lab (Sublimation of Laboratory Ices, Millimeter/submillimeter Experiment, PI: Dr. Stefanie Milam) at NASA. I learned how to make vacuum connections and troubleshoot instruments not working, and I got my first experience writing a proposal (albeit an unsuccessful one) for federal funding. These experiences helped prepare me for my current position as a faculty member setting up my own research lab. Perhaps more importantly, I built a network of amazing colleagues, collaborators, and mentors.

Scientific Challenges

What do you see as the most important open scientific questions or technical challenges in physical chemistry today?

I think the most important scientific questions are ones related to energy and energy storage. This is beyond my expertise, but I appreciate that there are other physical chemists working to answer such questions.

The Intersection of Personal Life and Work

Life beyond the Lab

What interests, responsibilities, or commitments outside of science have been important to you, and how have they interacted with your career?

The biggest commitment and source of joy outside of science has been my family: my spouse, Alex, and our son, Günther. They have been with me at every stage of my career. Alex and I got married after I graduated from college, 2 weeks before we moved to Germany for my Fulbright. While there, we became a family of three. I started my PhD with a four-month-old, so Güni is a good timekeeper for my career.

One of my favorite ways that family has interacted with my career is that I frequently have travel companions. Alex and Güni often travel with me when I go to conferences or workshops, and it is nice having their company after a long day. I’m also happy that they can travel to so many different places they might not otherwise experience!

Beyond my family, I really enjoy creating art and crafting in different capacities. I love drawing, painting, wood burning, knitting, andmost recentlyceramics. Much of my art is inspired by science, especially space and chemistry, and in grad school, I started selling my artwork to help offset the costs (e.g., gas mileage, materials) of doing outreach activities. Something I’m especially proud of is that I wrote a volume for ACS In Focus called Astrochemistry, and I did many of the illustrations myself!

Balance, Boundaries, and Trade-offs

How have you managed the balanceor tensionbetween professional demands and personal well-being over time?

Much of the tension between professional demands and personal well-being has manifested in family life. Alex has been my biggest supporter in so many ways: cheering me on in triumph, comforting me in failure, and being willing to follow me on every adventure. His patience has been critical for when professional demands win in the tug-of-war over my time and energy. For much of our marriage, he’s been a stay-at-home parent, which has made it easier to travel to conferences, put in extra time at work before proposal deadlines, and not have to worry about sick days or school bus pick-ups.

Having a small child in Germany was one of the best things I could have done for myself. Germany has excellent parental leave, and living there helped give me a mindset that puts my well-being over my work (although I’m not always good at practicing that). In grad school, having a small child meant I had no choice but to step away from my work a lot of the time to provide food, comfort, or playtime. I’ve lost some of that as I’ve progressed in my career and as Güni grows older and has fewer immediate needs, but I think the time I had when he was small helped me be better about setting boundaries for myself. Depending on the demands of a given day, I refrain from doing work at home when I can, and I’m trying to move toward that more and more. On days when the professional demands are too great, I compromise. For example, I will grade lab notebooks on the sofa while Güni snuggles up against me and watches a movie. I feel a little bit guilty when this happens, but Alex always reminds me that when I avoid work that has piled up, I’m not mentally present anyway. Alex is great at seeing the positives of these situations. “Mommy could be working at her office all night but instead she chose to work at home so she could be with us,” he often says. While I do not like that work-life integration, I appreciate that when it is fairly unavoidable, I have the support of my partner.

Barriers and Support Systems

Have you faced structural, cultural, or personal barriers in your career, and what sources of support helped you navigate them?

I’ve long struggled with mental health, which has presented barriers through panic attacks, depressive episodes that made it difficult to focus or even get out of bed in the morning, and adjusting to medication. The greatest thing I did for myself in that regard was that I started seeing a therapist during graduate school (with encouragement from my partner, my parents, and my friends). I learned a lot about myself through that experience, and I am now better equipped to navigate barriers introduced by mental health more on my own.

Mentorship and Community

How have mentorship, collaboration, or professional communities influenced your experience, and how do you try to contribute to these spaces for others?

One of the most meaningful mentorship experiences I had as a mentee was during the first year of my PhD. Brandon Carroll was in his last year at Caltech, and he taught me so much about coding, data analysis, and proposal writing. But I think the most impactful thing about his mentorship was that he was vulnerable and recalled how overwhelmed he felt as a grad student early on. He often commented, “It’s like drinking from a fire hose,” perhaps sensing my frustration and sometimes fear that I was not good enough for the work I was starting. That understanding, the fact that Brandon did not pretend like he had it all figured out all the time, was so comforting and encouraging. I try to emulate that as much as possible.

Looking Backward and Looking Forward

Lessons from Experience

Looking back, what lessonsscientific or personalstand out most from your career so far?

I’ve learned that if you do not ask, you do not get. My graduate advisor, Geoff, stated this regularly, and it helped give me the confidence to apply for things that I was certain I would be rejected from. There have been plenty of rejections in my career, but there have also been successes, some of which have been surprising. Even now, I feel encouraged to pursue things that seem hopeless. For example, the Director of Sponsored Projects at Dickinson, Ben Sweger, always makes me feel encouraged to submit proposals, even when we both know they are a long shot. I’m grateful that I’ve had people around me to encourage trying for things that interest me.

Advice to the Next Generation

What advice would you offer to early career physical chemists today?

I think the best advice I can offer is to try out whatever interests you, even if you do not think you are qualified. As a physical chemist, you have so many skills that are translatable to other things. If you are curious about something, submit that application, write that proposal, introduce yourself to someone working on a given project. You are capable of so much; do not assume you are not just because you feel like you do not have the “right” experience. Even if you fail in those endeavors, you will learn so much by being curious.

The Future of the Field

When you look ahead, what challenges and opportunities do you foresee for physical chemistry over the next decade, and what gives you hope?

One of the biggest challenges I foresee is changes in funding or adapting to recent (and sometimes abrupt) changes in how scientific research is funded (particularly in the US). I think this will especially affect how we train early career and student physical chemists who may not have as much access to funded research opportunities. If we move to unpaid research experiences, for example, we risk excluding talented physical chemists who lack the financial means to participate. However, I have hope because there are so many people, scientists and nonscientists alike, who are passionate about science and about funding science.
