Jacob L. Block, Mehryar Mohri, Aryan Mokhtari, Sanjay Shakkottai

Abstract
We study machine unlearning in large generative models by framing the task as density ratio estimation to a target distribution rather than supervised fine-tuning. While classifier guidance is a standard approach for approximating this ratio and can succeed in general, we show it can fail to faithfully unlearn with finite samples when the forget set represents a sharp, concentrated data distribution. To address this, we introduce Temper-Then-Tilt Unlearning (T3-Unlearning) , which freezes the base model and applies a two-step inference procedure: (i) tempering the base distribution to flatten high-confidence spikes, and (ii) tilting the tempered distribution using a lightweight classifier trained to distinguish retain from forget samples. Our theoretical analysis provides finite-sample guarantees linking the surrogate classifier’s risk to unlearning error, proving that tempering is necessary to successfully unlearn for concentrated distributions. Empirical evaluations on the TOFU benchmark show that T3-Unlearning improves forget quality and generative utility over existing baselines, while training only a fraction of the parameters with a minimal runtime.



1
Introduction

Modern generative models are often trained on large datasets comprising dynamic information, such as private or copyrighted data with revocable permissions
[
Coo+25
]
. As a result, efficient mechanisms are needed to remove the influence of specific samples from trained models, both to comply with privacy regulations
[
Eur16
,
Cal18
]
and to prevent the extraction of unwanted information
[
CTWJHLRBSE+21
]
.

Machine unlearning
addresses this by modifying a model trained on a dataset
\mathcal{D}
to approximate the outcome of retraining from scratch on a “retain set”
\mathcal{D}_{r}\subset\mathcal{D}
, removing the influence of the “forget set”
\mathcal{D}_{f}=\mathcal{D}\setminus\mathcal{D}_{r}
. While there are many ways to formalize and address this problem, we focus on generative models and frame unlearning as a distribution correction task rather than a parameter update task. Specifically, we assume the original model has learned the ground truth distribution
p
over the full dataset
\mathcal{D}
. Since
\mathcal{D}
is a disjoint union of the retain and forget sets,
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{D}_{r}\sqcup\mathcal{D}_{f}
, we model
p
as a mixture of two component distributions: the target retain distribution
p_{r}
and the forget distribution
p_{f}
, corresponding to
\mathcal{D}_{r}
and
\mathcal{D}_{f}
, respectively. This formulation resembles the Huber contamination model
[
Hub64
]
, where an underlying distribution is corrupted by an adversarial contamination component. In the context of unlearning, our aim is to learn to sample from
p_{r}
given access to the mixture
p
and samples from
p_{r}
and
p_{f}
respectively.

A principled approach to recover the target distribution
p_{r}
is to
tilt
the original distribution
p
, shifting probability via multiplicative reweighting. This arises in KL-constrained reinforcement learning
[
SLAJM15
,
SWDRK17
,
RSMMEF23
]
, importance sampling
[
SSNKVK08
,
GSHSBS+09
]
, and posterior sampling for generative models
[
DN21
,
CKMKY23
,
YK21
,
Mud+24
,
RWGKP25
]
. Rather than explicitly parameterizing an estimator for the optimal tilt, given by the density ratio
p_{r}/p
,
[
SSNKVK08
,
GSHSBS+09
,
KHS09
]
, we adopt an implicit classifier-guided approach, leveraging the fact that this ratio is proportional to the optimal probabilistic classifier distinguishing retain from forget samples
[
BBS07
,
RPN24
]
.

Contributions.

This paper develops a principled framework for unlearning in generative models based on distributional tilting. We characterize the statistical limits of classifier-guided unlearning, propose a method that overcomes these limitations, and validate its effectiveness both theoretically and empirically. We detail these contributions below.

We first study the connection between unlearning and classification in the finite-sample case. We consider unconditional generative models over a continuous domain and analyze how the error of a classifier-guided estimator of the retain distribution
p_{r}
depends on the excess risk
\delta
of the learned classifier on the surrogate classification task. For an estimator
\hat{p}_{r}
, we distinguish two notions of unlearning error: the
Retain Error

\mathcal{E}_{r}
, measured by the
p_{r}
-weighted discrepancy
\mathcal{E}_{r}(\hat{p}_{r})\coloneq{\mathrm{KL}}\left(p_{r}\,\|\,\hat{p}_{r}\right)
, and the
Forget Error

\mathcal{E}_{f}
, measured by the
p_{f}
-weighted
\ell_{1}
-norm
\mathcal{E}_{f}(\hat{p}_{r})\coloneq\left\|p_{r}-\hat{p}_{r}\right\|_{1,p_{f}}
.

Under standard classifier-guided tilting, the estimator is
\hat{p}_{r}\propto p\cdot\hat{f}
, where the learned classifier
\hat{f}
approximates
p_{r}/p
. We show that while this approach reliably recovers the modes of
p_{r}
, with Retain Error scaling as
\mathcal{O}(\delta)
(Theorem
3.3
), it can leak forget set information. In particular, the Forget Error scales as
\mathcal{O}(\left\|p_{f}\right\|_{\infty}\sqrt{\delta})
(Theorem
3.4
), which becomes vacuous when
p_{f}
is highly concentrated.
We further show the dependence on the sharpness of
p_{f}
is unavoidable. There exists a problem instance and a classifier with excess risk at most
\delta
such that the estimator incurs Forget Error on the order of
\Omega\left(\left\|p_{f}\right\|_{\infty}\!\!\cdot\delta\right)
(Theorem
3.5
). Thus, strong classification alone does not prevent information leakage when the forget distribution is sharply peaked.

This limitation parallels known hardness results in importance sampling, reinforcement learning, and inference time alignment, which show that it is statistically and computationally difficult to add a sharp mode in a region assigned negligible probability by a base distribution
[
CMM10
,
VSGYG24
,
XFKRAR25
,
FMR25
,
HBLJKF25
,
RSSLMRF25
]
. Crucially, our dual task of
removing
sharp modes is central to unlearning, as it captures settings in which the forget set consists of highly specific or atypical information relative to the retain set that is memorized by the model with high confidence.

To address this issue, we propose
Temper-Then-Tilt Unlearning (T3-Unlearning)
. The method first tempers the base distribution, flattening sharp probability spikes, and then applies classifier-guided tilting to the tempered distribution. Concretely, for a learned classifier
\hat{f}
and temperature
T\geq 1
, the estimator takes the form
\hat{p}_{r}^{(T)}\propto p^{1/T}\cdot\hat{f}
. Tempering amplifies the influence of the classifier and enables stronger suppression of concentrated forget-set modes.

We prove that the Forget Error of
\smash{\hat{p}_{r}^{\,(T)}}
is bounded by
\mathcal{O}(\left\|p_{f}\right\|_{\infty}^{1/T}\cdot\delta^{1/2k})
, up to a tempering-induced bias term that vanishes when
T=1
, where
k\geq T
reflects a mild integrability condition (Theorem
3.7
). This improved scaling with
\left\|p_{f}\right\|_{\infty}
comes at the cost of slower dependence on the classifier excess risk. Finally, we bound the additional Retain Error induced by tempering, showing that this bias worsens as the entropy
H(p_{r})
decreases (Theorem
3.8
).
Together, these results establish a principled tradeoff between tempering-induced bias and robustness to sharply concentrated forget distributions, improving the dependence of unlearning error on the classifier excess risk.

LLM Experiments.
We apply T3-Unlearning to empirical unlearning tasks for large language models (LLMs). For a given context
\bm{x}
, the classifier scores each candidate completion
(\bm{x},y)
and is used to tilt the tempered base distribution
p^{(T)}(y\!\mid\!\bm{x})\propto p(y\!\mid\!\bm{x})^{1/T}
prior to sampling (Figure
1
). We implement the T3-Unlearning classifier as a small linear head on top of the fixed hidden states of the base model, efficiently unlearning while avoiding costly updates to the underlying pretrained network. Experiments on the TOFU benchmark
[
MFSLK24
]
show that T3-Unlearning consistently outperforms existing baselines while incurring only a fraction of their computational cost.

1.1
Related Work

Theoretical studies of machine unlearning have primarily focused on recovering model parameters that match retraining from scratch on
\mathcal{D}_{r}
or achieve approximate statistical indistinguishability, analyzing convex losses
[
GGHV20
,
SAKS21
,
NRS21
]
, linear classification
[
LSS25
]
, and overparameterized models
[
BMS25
]
. In contrast to these works, which formulate unlearning as a parameter recovery problem, we treat unlearning as a distribution correction problem and operate directly on the learned data distribution. This avoids reliance on the original parameter space, which is prohibitively large for modern generative models such as LLMs.

Other works proposed data partitioning approaches which achieve exact unlearning through model checkpointing
[
BCCJTZLP21
,
GKKMSZ23
]
. While effective in their intended settings, they rely on specialized training pipelines and are not applicable to arbitrarily trained models.

A separate line of work addresses unlearning in LLMs through supervised fine-tuning objectives that explicitly degrade performance on the forget set while preserving performance on the retain set
[
MFSLK24
,
WZZSHW25
,
YWHLZH25
,
DLBHV25
,
LPGYBGLDGM+24
,
ZLBM24
,
FLLJZML25
]
. These schemes require fine-tuning the base model, which is computationally costly and can induce unintended degradation on behavior unrelated to the unlearning task. In contrast, our method freezes the base model and instead learns a distributional reweighting via a lightweight parameterization, yielding substantially lower computational cost. Further, unlike fine-tuning-based methods that suppress forget-set generations, our method admits theoretical guarantees for minimizing specific Retain and Forget Errors measuring key notions of discrepancy to the ground-truth retain distribution.

Recent work, closer in spirit to our framework, has proposed an alternative unlearning paradigm for LLMs where the base model is kept frozen and unlearning is performed by learning a perturbation of the model’s output distribution
[
ER23
,
JLZLKLC24
,
SSW25
]
. For a pretrained model
p_{\bm{\theta}^{\ast}}(y\mid\bm{x})
parameterized by
\bm{\theta}^{\ast}\in\bm{\Theta}
, these methods introduce a tilt function
f_{\bm{\phi}}(\bm{x},y)
, parameterized separately by
\bm{\phi}
, and define the updated next-token distribution as
\hat{p}(y\mid\bm{x})\propto p_{\bm{\theta}^{\ast}}(y\mid\bm{x})\cdot f_{\bm{\phi}}(\bm{x},y)
.
This formulation preserves the original model
p_{\bm{\theta}^{\ast}}
and decouples unlearning from
\bm{\Theta}
, enabling updates over a lower-dimensional auxiliary parameter space. However, these approaches parameterize
f_{\bm{\phi}}
using an auxiliary LLM or a LoRA-style adaptation
[
HWALWWC+22
]
, requiring forward and backward passes through deep networks and large amounts of data to train a highly expressive tilting function. While these methods aim to up-weight retain set sequences and suppress forget set sequences, they incur substantial computational cost and lack theoretical guarantees for recovering the target retain distribution.
In contrast, building on the same tilting scheme, we propose a computationally efficient unlearning method with provable finite-sample guarantees for recovering the target distribution
p_{r}
.

Notation.
We abuse notation by using the symbols
p
,
p_{r}
, and
p_{f}
interchangeably to denote a measure over samples (e.g.,
\bm{z}\sim p
), its corresponding density, or probability mass (e.g.,
p(\bm{z})
or
p(y\mid\bm{x})
).
H(p)=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbin{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{\scalebox{0.6}{$-$}}}\ln p\right]
denotes entropy, and
\operatorname{Std}_{p}[\,\cdot\,]
denotes standard deviation under
p
. For a function
g
and
k\geq 1
,
\left\|g\right\|_{k,p}:=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}\sim p}\left[|g(\bm{z})|^{k}\right]^{1/k}
.

2
Proposed Method: T3-Unlearning

We formalize the generative model unlearning task. We consider a generative model with query access
p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{z})
parameterized by
\bm{\theta}
. For LLMs, we write
\bm{z}=(\bm{x},y)
, where
\bm{x}
denotes the context and
y
the next token, with the autoregressive factorization
p_{\bm{\theta}}(y\mid\bm{x})
. For generality, we present the formulation in terms of a generic variable
\bm{z}\sim p(\bm{z})
where
p
is either a density or probability mass, and specialize as needed.
As mentioned earlier, we are given the original pretrained model
p_{\bm{\theta}^{\ast}}
, which we assume approximates the data distribution
p
over the full set
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{D}_{r}\sqcup\mathcal{D}_{f}
, together with sample access to both
\mathcal{D}_{r}
and
\mathcal{D}_{f}
. The goal is to produce a model that generates samples from the ground-truth retain distribution
p_{r}
, corresponding solely to the information contained in
\mathcal{D}_{r}
.

2.1
Probabilistic Formulation

Under the distribution tilting framework, where the updated distribution takes the form
\hat{p}_{r}\propto p\cdot\hat{f}
, the optimal tilt
f^{\ast}
that exactly recovers
p_{r}
is given by the
density ratio
of the target
p_{r}
and original distribution
p
. For autoregressive models with
\bm{z}=(\bm{x},y)
, this reduces to
f^{\ast}(\bm{x},y)=p_{r}(y\mid\bm{x})/p(y\mid\bm{x})
, and more generally to
f^{\ast}(\bm{z})=p_{r}(\bm{z})/p(\bm{z})
.

We aim to recover the unknown target distribution
p_{r}
by estimating this ratio. Formally, we label each sample in the full dataset
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{D}_{r}\,\sqcup\,\mathcal{D}_{f}
by its membership in the retain set, yielding the labeled dataset
\mathcal{S}=\{(\bm{z},s)\mid\bm{z}\in\mathcal{D}\}
with
s=\mathds{1}\{\bm{z}\in\mathcal{D}_{r}\}
. We assume
(\bm{z},s)
are drawn from a measure
\mathbb{P}
over
\Omega=\mathcal{Z}\times\{0,1\}
. Let
\gamma\in(0,1)
denote the population proportion of the forget set. Then
s\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(1-\gamma)
indicates whether
\bm{z}
is drawn from the retain or forget component, with
p_{r}(\bm{z})\coloneq\mathbb{P}\left(\bm{z}\mid s=1\right)
and
p_{f}(\bm{z})\coloneq\mathbb{P}\left(\bm{z}\mid s=0\right)
.
The marginal
p
is thus the
\gamma
-weighted mixture

p(\bm{z})=(1-\gamma)p_{r}(\bm{z})+\gamma p_{f}(\bm{z}).

(1)

By Bayes’ rule, the retain distribution is a tilt of the original model
p
, i.e.,

p_{r}(\bm{z})\propto p(\bm{z})\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(s=1\mid\bm{z}\right)
,
which under the auto-regressive factorization of LLMs simplifies to
p_{r}(y\mid\bm{x})\propto p(y\mid\bm{x})\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(s=1\mid\bm{x},y\right)
.
This recovers the standard classifier-guidance formulation: the density ratio
f^{\ast}
can be interpreted as the scaled
posterior probability

f^{\ast}(\bm{z})\propto\mathbb{P}\left(s=1\mid\bm{z}\right).

(2)

Thus, we can estimate
f^{\ast}
in practice by training a probabilistic classifier to infer
s
from
\bm{z}
. This reframes the unlearning problem, which is often characterized by ill-posed objectives, into a well-defined supervised learning problem.

2.2
T3-Unlearning Procedure

Standard classifier guidance implicitly assumes access to an accurate estimate of the optimal classifier
f^{*}
which yields an accurate estimate of the desired posterior. However, as detailed in Section
3
, this assumption breaks down in the finite-sample regime when the forget distribution
p_{f}
is highly concentrated. In such settings, even small classification errors can be amplified by sharp modes in
p_{f}
, leading to information leakage. To address this, we propose the T3-Unlearning algorithm which applies a two-stage process:

Classifier Training.
Given a dataset
\mathcal{S}_{n}=\{(\bm{z}_{i},s_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
drawn from the data model in Section
2.1
, we learn a classifier
\hat{f}(\bm{z})\approx\mathbb{P}(s=1\mid\bm{z})
. For a specific parameterization
f_{\bm{\phi}}(\bm{z})
, we minimize the regularized cross-entropy loss

L_{n}^{\lambda}(\bm{\phi})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(f_{\bm{\phi}}(\bm{z}_{i}),s_{i})+\lambda\left\|\bm{\phi}\right\|_{2}^{2},

(3)

where
\ell(f(\bm{z}),s)=-s\ln f(\bm{z})-(1-s)\ln(1-f(\bm{z}))
and
\lambda\geq 0
is the regularization coefficient.

Tempered Inference.
We apply
base model tempering
, first smoothing the base distribution
p
via a temperature
T\geq 1
and then tilting using the learned classifier
\hat{f}
, yielding:

\hat{p}^{(T)}_{r}(\bm{z})\propto p(\bm{z})^{1/T}\hat{f}(\bm{z}).

(4)

For LLMs, where
\hat{\bm{\phi}}
are the learned parameters which minimize (
3
) and
p_{\bm{\theta}^{\ast}}
is the frozen original model, this translates to the autoregressive update rule:

\hat{p}^{(T)}_{r}(y\mid\bm{x})\propto p_{\bm{\theta}^{\ast}}(y\mid\bm{x})^{1/T}f_{\hat{\bm{\phi}}}(\bm{x},y).

(5)

While standard classifier guidance sharpens the classifier to control guidance strength
[
DN21
]
, we instead temper the base model, reducing the magnitude of the correction needed by the classifier. See Remark
3.6
for the limitations of classifier sharpening for unlearning.

Figure 1
:
T3-Unlearning for LLMs. We freeze the base model and train a linear head (shaded) on pooled hidden states to predict the vector
\bm{g}_{\bm{\phi}}(\bm{x})
of class posteriors for all possible next tokens. In training, we apply the loss to the entry
[\bm{g}_{\bm{\phi}}(\bm{x})]_{y}
corresponding to the estimator of the class posterior
\mathbb{P}\left(s=1\mid\bm{x},y\right)
, while the entire vector
\bm{g}_{\bm{\phi}}(\bm{x})
tilts the base model’s tempered logits for inference.

2.3
LLM Implementation

We propose the following procedure for applying T3-Unlearning to LLMs (Figure
1
). We define the classifier
f_{\bm{\phi}}
as a low-rank linear head over pre-computed features from the frozen base model
\bm{\Psi}
. For vocabulary
\mathcal{V}
and an
L
-length context
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{V}^{L}
, we obtain a fixed-size representation by mean-pooling over the sequence dimension of the final hidden states
(\bm{H}_{1},\dots,\bm{H}_{L})=\bm{\Psi}(\bm{x})\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times L}
. This is passed through the low-rank classifier factorized by
\bm{A}_{\bm{\phi}}\in\mathbb{R}^{h\times d}
,
\bm{B}_{\bm{\phi}}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{V}|\times h}
(
h\ll d
) to produce per-token probabilities:

\bm{g}_{\bm{\phi}}(\bm{x})=\sigma\big(\bm{B}_{\bm{\phi}}\bm{A}_{\bm{\phi}}\operatorname{MeanPool}(\bm{\Psi}(\bm{x}))\big)\in[0,1]^{|\mathcal{V}|}.

We then define
f_{\bm{\phi}}(\bm{x},y)
as the
y^{\text{th}}
entry of
\bm{g}_{\bm{\phi}}(\bm{x})
:

f_{\bm{\phi}}(\bm{x},y)=\left[\bm{g}_{\bm{\phi}}(\bm{x})\right]_{y}\approx\mathbb{P}\left(s=1\mid\bm{x},y\right).

During training (Figure
1
, Top), we compute the loss
\ell(f_{\bm{\phi}}(\bm{x},y),s)
for each pair
(\bm{x},y)
and label
s
using only the classifier output. For inference (Figure
1
, Bottom), we apply the classifier guidance to the tempered base model distribution, sampling from
\hat{p}_{r}^{\,(T)}(y\mid\bm{x})
:

\hat{p}_{r}^{\,(T)}(y\mid\bm{x})=\operatornamewithlimits{softmax}_{y}\Biggl(\frac{\ln p_{\bm{\theta}^{\ast}}(y\mid\bm{x})}{T}+\ln[\bm{g}_{\phi}(\bm{x})]_{y}\Biggr).

Our design offers two key advantages: hidden states in training can be cached, eliminating repeated forward passes through the base model, and the number of trainable parameters is minimal, enabling fast, memory-efficient unlearning.

3
Theoretical Guarantees

3.1
Unlearning Metrics

Before establishing our theoretical guarantees, we first formalize the criteria used to evaluate an unlearning procedure. Recall from (
1
) that we observe samples from the mixture
p(\bm{z})=(1-\gamma)\,p_{r}(\bm{z})+\gamma\,p_{f}(\bm{z})
,
and our goal is to generate samples according to the retain distribution
p_{r}(\bm{z})
. For any candidate estimate
\hat{p}_{r}
of
p_{r}
, we evaluate unlearning along two axes:
Retain Error
, which measures how accurately the estimate recovers the target retain distribution, and
Forget Error
, which measures how effectively it avoids leaking information in regions associated with the forget distribution.

The first notion of unlearning error, termed the
Retain Error
, evaluates the accuracy of
\hat{p}_{r}
in regions where the target distribution itself places probability mass. We formally quantify this via the forward KL divergence

\mathcal{E}_{r}(\hat{p}_{r}):=\ {\mathrm{KL}}\left(p_{r}\,\|\,\hat{p}_{r}\right)=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}\sim p_{r}}\left[\ln\frac{p_{r}(\bm{z})}{\hat{p}_{r}(\bm{z})}\right]

(6)

As this is an expectation under
p_{r}
, it is insensitive to discrepancies on sets where
p_{r}
assigns negligible probability. While such insensitivity is often benign in standard estimation settings, it is problematic in unlearning: an estimate can achieve small
{\mathrm{KL}}\left(p_{r}\,\|\,\hat{p}_{r}\right)
while assigning nontrivial probability to regions where the forget distribution
p_{f}
concentrates, thereby leaking information about the forget set.

To capture this failure mode, we define a complementary notion of error, the
Forget Error
, that focuses explicitly on the behavior of
\hat{p}_{r}
over regions emphasized by
p_{f}
. Concretely, we measure this error as the
p_{f}
-weighted
\ell_{1}
-norm

\mathcal{E}_{f}(\hat{p}_{r}):=\|p_{r}-\hat{p}_{r}\|_{1,p_{f}}=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}\sim p_{f}}\left[\left|p_{r}(\bm{z})\mathbin{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{\scalebox{0.6}{$-$}}}\hat{p}_{r}(\bm{z})\right|\right].

(7)

Taken together, the Retain and Forget Errors provide complementary control: the former ensures faithful recovery of
p_{r}
on its typical set, while the latter detects residual mismatch in regions where
p_{f}
places weight, which is precisely where unlearning failures manifest.

Remark 3.1
.

While direct control of distributional discrepancies (
\mathcal{E}_{r}
and
\mathcal{E}_{f}
) gives a definitive notion of unlearning, these quantities are intractable to compute for LLMs, as they require expectations over a combinatorial input space. Consequently, empirical evaluations rely on hypothesis tests
[
MFSLK24
]
and membership inference attacks
[
SLHMZHLZSZ25
]
to assess distinguishability from a retrained reference. Our theory targets these fundamental discrepancies directly, since minimizing them implies indistinguishability under any test function, and in Section
4
we show strong performance under the empirical proxies used in practice.

3.2
Surrogate Analysis Framework

We work in a general nonparametric setting where the retain and forget distributions admit densities
p_{r}
and
p_{f}
over a continuous domain
\mathcal{Z}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We approximate
p_{r}
via the T3-Unlearning estimator
\hat{p}_{r}^{\,(T)}
defined as

\hat{p}_{r}^{\,(T)}(\bm{z})=\frac{p(\bm{z})^{1/T}\hat{f}(\bm{z})}{\int_{\bm{u}}p(\bm{u})^{1/T}\hat{f}(\bm{u})},

(8)

where
\hat{f}\in\mathcal{F}
is a learned surrogate classifier intended to approximate the Bayes posterior
f^{\ast}(\bm{z})\coloneqq\mathbb{P}\left(s=1\mid\bm{z}\right)
.

Remark 3.2
.

We assume
T
is chosen so that
\int p^{1/\tau}<\infty
for all
1\leq\tau\leq T
, ensuring
\hat{p}_{r}^{\,(T)}
is well-defined. This always holds for sub-exponential distributions; for power-law tails
p\propto\left\|\bm{z}\right\|_{2}^{-\alpha}
, it requires
\alpha>dT
, for ambient dimension
d
.

Our analysis directly relates the unlearning error of
\hat{p}_{r}^{\,(T)}
to the statistical quality of
\hat{f}
. Let
L(f)\coloneq\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z},s}\left[\ell(f(\bm{z}),s)\right]
denote the population risk under the cross-entropy loss
\ell
. We assume
\hat{f}
achieves excess risk at most
\delta
, i.e.,
L(\hat{f})-L(f^{\ast})\leq\delta
, and derive bounds on the Retain and Forget Errors of
\hat{p}_{r}^{\,(T)}
as functions of
\delta
and the mixture parameters.

While particular function classes admit standard excess risk rates when the true posterior lies in the hypothesis space (e.g., logistic regression achieving
\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2})
rates; see Appendix
C
), our theory is stated in terms of the surrogate excess risk
\delta
. This abstraction allows it to apply across learning settings with favorable risk guarantees, agnostic to any function class. Consequently, regardless of how the surrogate classifier
\hat{f}
is obtained, we characterize unlearning performance solely through its surrogate risk.

3.3
Unlearning Guarantees: Untempered Estimator

To motivate the role of tempering, we first analyze the baseline that
directly
tilts the observed mixture using standard classifier-guidance. Concretely, for a classifier
\hat{f}
, we define the
untempered
estimator as
\hat{p}_{r}^{\,(1)}
, the special case of the T3-Unlearning estimator (
8
) with fixed temperature
T=1
.

We first establish in Theorem
3.3
that this standard approach strictly controls Retain Error, and then subsequently show that it can fail to bound Forget Error when the forget distribution
p_{f}
is sharply concentrated.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
\hat{f}\in\mathcal{F}
satisfy the excess risk bound
L(\hat{f})-L(f^{\ast})\leq\delta
. Then the Retain Error (
6
) of the untempered estimate
\hat{p}_{r}^{\,(1)}
in (
8
) satisfies

\mathcal{E}_{r}(\hat{p}_{r}^{\,(1)})\leq\frac{\delta}{1-\gamma}.

Theorem
3.3
shows that the Retain Error of the untempered estimator is controlled by the classifier’s excess risk, up to the factor
(1-\gamma)^{-1}
. The bound scales linearly in
\delta
, as improved classification performance leads to a more accurate reconstruction of the retain distribution. The factor
(1-\gamma)^{-1}
reflects the inverse proportion of the retain component in the original mixture. As
\gamma\to 1
, the signal from
p_{r}
vanishes, rendering recovery ill-conditioned. However, in standard unlearning scenarios where the forget set is small (
\gamma\ll 1
), this factor is negligible. In this regime, the bound in Theorem
3.3
remains sharp, effectively equating the Retain Error with the classifier’s excess risk.
While Theorem
3.3
shows the untempered estimator attains small Retain Error when the classifier has low excess risk (and
\gamma
is not too large), this alone does not ensure effective unlearning. As discussed in Section
3.1
, controlling the Forget Error (
7
) is essential to prevent information leakage from the forget set. The next theorem makes this explicit by upper-bounding the Forget Error of the untempered estimator, showing that it may degrade even when the Retain Error is small.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
\hat{f}\in\mathcal{F}
satisfy the excess risk bound
L(\hat{f})-L(f^{\ast})\leq\delta
. Then the Forget Error (
7
) of the untempered estimate
\hat{p}_{r}^{\,(1)}
in (
8
) satisfies

\mathcal{E}_{f}(\hat{p}_{r}^{\,(1)})\leq\left\|p_{f}\right\|_{\infty}\sqrt{\frac{2\delta}{1-\gamma}}.

The bound in Theorem
3.4
depends on the surrogate excess risk
\delta
and the mixture weight
\gamma
in the expected way: it improves as
\delta
decreases and deteriorates as
\gamma
increases, with the same
(1-\gamma)^{-1}
amplification as in Theorem
3.3
. Unlike the Retain Error guarantee, this bound scales with
\sqrt{\delta}
and
\sqrt{(1-\gamma)^{-1}}
. This reflects that Retain Error and excess risk are both KL-based metrics, while Forget Error is
\ell_{1}
-based; applying Pinsker’s inequality relates the two quantities, degrading the bound from linear to square-root.

Crucially, the bound also carries a multiplicative dependence on
\left\|p_{f}\right\|_{\infty}
. This captures the sharpness of the forget distribution and can be very large when
p_{f}
is highly concentrated on a small region, in which case the bound may become uninformative even for small
\delta
. In the next theorem, we show that this sensitivity to the concentration of
p_{f}
is not an artifact of the analysis by constructing an instance where the Forget Error is lower bounded in terms of
\left\|p_{f}\right\|_{\infty}
.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
p_{r}
and
p_{f}
have disjoint supports, with
p_{f}
uniform on its support. Then for any mixture weight
\gamma\in(0,1)
and any
\delta>0
, there exists a classifier
\hat{f}
achieving excess risk
L(\hat{f})-L(f^{*})\leq\delta
such that the Forget Error (
7
) of the untempered estimate
\hat{p}_{r}^{\,(1)}
in (
8
) satisfies

\mathcal{E}_{f}(\hat{p}_{r}^{\,(1)})\geq\left\|p_{f}\right\|_{\infty}\cdot\frac{\gamma\left(1-\exp\left(-\frac{\delta}{\gamma}\right)\right)}{1-\gamma\exp\left(-\frac{\delta}{\gamma}\right)}.

In particular, as
\delta\to 0
, we have
\mathcal{E}_{f}(\hat{p}_{r}^{\,(1)})=\Omega\left(\left\|p_{f}\right\|_{\infty}\!\!\cdot\delta\right)
.

Theorem
3.5
shows that for a broad class of component distributions
p_{r}
and
p_{f}
, the Forget Error of the untempered estimator necessarily scales with the peak density. This confirms that the dependence on
\left\|p_{f}\right\|_{\infty}
in Theorem
3.4
is intrinsic rather than an artifact of the analysis. The proof constructs a worst-case classifier
\hat{f}
which incurs error only on the forget set. In this case, the Forget Error is lower bounded by the sharpness of
p_{f}
, quantified by its squared
\ell_{2}
-norm
\left\|p_{f}\right\|_{2}^{2}
. When
p_{f}
is uniform, this coincides with
\left\|p_{f}\right\|_{\infty}
yielding the stated bound. While the lower bound scales linearly in
\delta
, compared to the
\sqrt{\delta}
rate in the upper bound in Theorem
3.4
, it establishes the unavoidable dependence on the peak density of the forget distribution.

Remark 3.6

(Limitations of Classifier Sharpening)
.

Prior work
[
DN21
,
JLZLKLC24
]
controls guidance strength by sharpening the classifier
\hat{f}^{\,w}
for
w>0
. While this can be interpreted as a strategy for improving the surrogate task excess risk to some
\delta_{w}
, Theorems
3.4
and
3.5
show that such sharpening cannot eliminate the fundamental dependence of the Forget Error on the sharpness of
p_{f}
.

3.4
Unlearning Guarantees: The Tempered Estimator

Theorems
3.4
and
3.5
show that the untempered estimator is highly sensitive to the sharpness of
p_{f}
: its Forget Error scales linearly with
\left\|p_{f}\right\|_{\infty}
, and the corresponding lower bound confirms this dependence is intrinsic in general. We show that tempering overcomes this limitation. Under a mild integrability condition on the mixture density
p
, the
T
-tempered estimator
\hat{p}_{r}^{\,(T)}
admits a Forget Error bound which depends on
\left\|p_{f}\right\|_{\infty}
sublinearly as
\left\|p_{f}\right\|_{\infty}^{1/T}
, substantially improving robustness when
p_{f}
is sharply concentrated.

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
\hat{f}\in\mathcal{F}
satisfy the excess risk bound
L(\hat{f})-L(f^{\ast})\leq\delta
. Define the
\tau
-tempered oracle estimate
p_{r}^{(\tau)}\propto p^{1/\tau}\cdot f^{\ast}
for
\tau\in[1,T]
. Consider
k\geq T
such that
\int p^{\frac{k-T}{T(k-1)}}<\infty
. Then for some
\tau\in[1,T]
, the Forget Error (
7
) of the
T
-tempered estimate
\hat{p}_{r}^{\,(T)}
in (
8
) satisfies

\mathcal{E}_{f}(\hat{p}_{r}^{\,(T)})\leq{\left(1-\tfrac{1}{T}\right)\left\|p_{f}\right\|_{2,p_{r}^{(\tau)}}\!\cdot\operatorname{Std}_{p_{r}^{(\tau)}}[\ln p]}\,+\,\mathcal{O}(\left\|p_{f}\right\|_{\infty}^{1/T}\cdot\,\delta^{1/2k}).

Theorem
3.7
bounds the Forget Error of the
T
-tempered estimator by a sum of two terms: a
tempering bias
term and a
\delta
-dependent
estimation error
term. The second term exhibits the key benefit of tempering: the dependence on the sharpness of the forget distribution is sublinear, scaling as
\left\|p_{f}\right\|_{\infty}^{1/T}
rather than
\left\|p_{f}\right\|_{\infty}
as in the untempered bound of Theorem
3.4
. While the dependence on the classifier excess risk worsens from
\delta^{1/2}
to
\delta^{1/2k}
, for fixed
\delta
and large
\left\|p_{f}\right\|_{\infty}
tempering can greatly improve the Forget Error guarantee.

The first term quantifies the price of tempering. It depends on the intermediate “oracle” tempered distribution
p_{r}^{(\tau)}
, for some
\tau\in[1,T]
, and measures the distortion caused by raising
p
to the power
1/T
. When
p_{r}
and
p_{f}
are well separated, this bias is small, and it vanishes entirely in the idealized case of disjoint supports, since then
\left\|p_{f}\right\|_{2,p_{r}^{(\tau)}}=0
. In this regime, the overall error is dominated by the estimation term, directly addressing the failure mode of the untempered estimator, whose Forget Error provably exhibits linear dependence on
\left\|p_{f}\right\|_{\infty}
in the disjoint-support setting (cf. Theorem
3.5
). However, taking
T
too large causes the bias term to dominate, as overaggressive tempering flattens
p
and induces an unavoidable mismatch even for the Bayes-optimal classifier
f^{\ast}
. This indicates that minimizing Forget Error requires balancing these competing effects.

While tempering is crucial for controlling Forget Error when
p_{f}
is sharply concentrated, it globally distorts the base distribution. We next quantify the induced Retain Error bias.

Theorem 3.8
.

Let
\hat{f}\in\mathcal{F}
satisfy the excess risk bound
L(\hat{f})-L(f^{\ast})\leq\delta
. Define the
\tau
-tempered density
p^{(\tau)}\propto p^{1/\tau}
for
\tau\in[1,T]
. Then for some
\tau\in[1,T]
, the Retain Error (
6
) of the
T
-tempered estimate
\hat{p}_{r}^{\,(T)}
in (
8
) satisfies

\mathcal{E}_{r}(\hat{p}_{r}^{\,(T)})\leq\frac{\delta}{1-\gamma}+\left(1-\frac{1}{T}\right)\left(\frac{\left(\int p^{1/\tau}\right)\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}\sim p^{(\tau)}}[\left|\ln p(\bm{z})\right|]}{(1-\gamma)^{\frac{\tau+1}{\tau}}\exp\left(\frac{\tau\mathbin{\raisebox{0.3014pt}{\scalebox{0.6}{$-$}}}1}{\tau}H(p_{r})-\tfrac{\delta\mathbin{\raisebox{0.3014pt}{\scalebox{0.6}{$-$}}}\gamma\ln\gamma}{1\mathbin{\raisebox{0.3014pt}{\scalebox{0.6}{$-$}}}\gamma}\right)}-H(p_{r})\right)

Theorem
3.8
decomposes the Retain Error of the tempered estimator into two terms. The first term,
\delta/(1-\gamma)
, matches the untempered guarantee (Theorem
3.3
) and reflects the expected error caused by imperfect classification. The second term is a
tempering-induced bias
that arises even with the Bayes-optimal classifier
f^{\ast}
, as tempering
p
to
p^{(T)}\propto p^{1/T}
introduces a global distortion. As expected, the bias term vanishes to
0
when
T=1
.

This clarifies when tempering inflates Retain Error: the bias is amplified when the retain distribution
p_{r}
is concentrated (small
H(p_{r})
), as tempering flattens high-density regions and increases mismatch. Combined with Theorem
3.7
, this forms a fundamental tradeoff:
T
should be large enough to tame
\left\|p_{f}\right\|_{\infty}
, yet not so large that tempering distortion overwhelms the gains. We empirically observe this tradeoff both in continuous-domain synthetic experiments (Appendix
E
) and in LLM benchmarks (Appendix
F.3.3
). We next present the LLM experiments in detail.

4
Experiments

We evaluate our method in the practical LLM setting using the Task of Fictitious Unlearning (TOFU) benchmark
[
MFSLK24
]
. TOFU consists of a fine-tuned LLM and a dataset of question-answer pairs regarding 200 fictitious authors generated by GPT-4
[
Ope23
]
. The unlearning task requires forgetting all question-answer pairs from a subset of the authors (the forget set) while preserving knowledge of the remaining authors (retain set) as well as heldout real-world authors (RA) and general world facts (WF) datasets.

4.1
Unlearning Metrics

TOFU evaluates unlearning using several metrics built around the
Truth Ratio
(
R_{\text{truth}}
), an intermediate statistic comparing the model’s relative probability of incorrect “perturbed” answers
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{pert}}
to that of the paraphrased true answer
\bm{a}^{\dagger}
for a given question
\bm{q}
:

R_{\text{truth}}(p_{\bm{\theta}},\bm{q})=\frac{\frac{1}{\left|\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{pert}}\right|}\sum_{\bm{\tilde{a}}\in\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{pert}}}p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{\tilde{a}}\mid\bm{q})^{1/\left|\bm{\tilde{a}}\right|}}{p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{a}^{\dagger}\mid\bm{q})^{1/\left|\bm{a}^{\dagger}\right|}}.

From this statistic, TOFU defines two summary metrics.

Forget Quality (FQ)
quantifies statistical indistinguishability from a retrained reference model. It is computed as the p-value of a two-sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov test comparing the Truth Ratio distributions generated by the unlearned model and the reference model over the forget set.

Model Utility (MU)
measures retained performance on tasks unrelated to the forget set. It is defined as the harmonic mean, over the retain, RA, and WF datasets, of three components: (i)
Probability
, the length-normalized probability of the true answer; (ii)
ROUGE
, the ROUGE-L recall
[
Lin04
]
; and (iii)
TR
+
, an inverted confidence score given by
\text{TR{+}}(p_{\bm{\theta}},\bm{q})=\max\{0,1-R_{\text{truth}}(p_{\bm{\theta}},\bm{q})\}
.
While prior work often conflates
R_{\text{truth}}
and TR
+
under the term “Truth Ratio,” we distinguish them explicitly for clarity. See Appendix
F.2
for detailed definitions for all metrics.

MU-ROUGE (Generative Utility).

While Model Utility provides a broad summary of retained performance, it aggregates heterogeneous quantities with distinct semantic meanings (Probability, ROUGE, and TR
+
) into a single harmonic mean. In particular, increases in token-level probability or confidence can raise the overall score even when the quality of generated text does not improve. To isolate generative performance, we propose
MU-ROUGE
, defined as the harmonic mean of ROUGE-L scores across the retain, RA, and WF datasets. By focusing on generation quality, MU-ROUGE provides a more direct and interpretable measure of retained utility. We report both Model Utility and MU-ROUGE but emphasize MU-ROUGE as the primary utility metric.

5
Conclusion

We introduced the
Temper-Then-Tilt Unlearning
framework (T3-Unlearning), which framed unlearning as density-ratio estimation via probabilistic classification. Our theoretical analysis established finite-sample guarantees and showed that base model tempering was essential for unlearning highly concentrated forget-set distributions. Empirically, T3-Unlearning outperformed existing baselines on the TOFU benchmark; by training only a lightweight linear classifier over frozen representations, it achieved superior forget quality and generative utility with minimal computational cost.

Proof of Theorem
3.3
.

While we focus on estimating
p_{r}
as
\hat{p}_{r}^{\,(1)}\propto\hat{f}\cdot p
, we can similarly estimate
p_{f}
as
\hat{p}_{f}^{\,(1)}\propto(1-\hat{f})\cdot p
as a tool for our analysis. Thus, we define the following estimators for
p_{r}
and
p_{f}
respectively:

\hat{p}_{r}^{\,(1)}(\bm{z})=\frac{p(\bm{z})\hat{f}(\bm{z})}{\int_{\bm{u}}p(\bm{u})\hat{f}(\bm{u})}\quad\text{and}\quad\hat{p}_{f}^{\,(1)}(\bm{z})=\frac{p(\bm{z})(1-\hat{f}(\bm{z}))}{\int_{\bm{u}}p(\bm{u})(1-\hat{f}(\bm{u}))}

By construction,
\hat{f}(\bm{z})
estimates the class posterior
f^{\ast}(\bm{z})=\mathbb{P}\left(s=1\mid\bm{z}\right)=(1-\gamma)\frac{p_{r}(\bm{z})}{p(\bm{z})}
. Define the Bernoulli PMF over the class label
s
induced by
\hat{f}
as

\hat{\pi}(s\mid\bm{z})=\begin{cases}\hat{f}(\bm{z})&\text{if }s=1\\
1-\hat{f}(\bm{z})&\text{else },\end{cases}

and let
\pi^{\ast}(s\mid\bm{z})
denote the distribution induced by the true class posterior
f^{\ast}
. Recall that
\mathbb{P}
is the base measure over pairs
(\bm{z},s)
defined in Section
2.1
. We can then translate the excess risk of
\hat{f}
directly into a bound on the KL-divergence between the target distribution
p_{r}
and our estimator
\hat{p}_{r}
.

\displaystyle L(\hat{f})-L(f^{\ast})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{(\bm{z},s)\sim\mathbb{P}}\biggl[\ln\frac{\pi^{\ast}(s\mid\bm{z})}{\hat{\pi}(s\mid\bm{z})}\biggr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}\mid s=1}\biggl[\ln\frac{\pi^{\ast}(s\mid\bm{z})}{\hat{\pi}(s\mid\bm{z})}\bigm|s=1\biggr]\mathbb{P}\left(s=1\right)+\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}\mid s=0}\biggl[\ln\frac{\pi^{\ast}(s\mid\bm{z})}{\hat{\pi}(s\mid\bm{z})}\bigm|s=0\biggr]\mathbb{P}\left(s=0\right)

\displaystyle=(1-\gamma)\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}\sim p_{r}}\biggl[\ln\frac{\pi^{\ast}(1\mid\bm{z})}{\hat{\pi}(1\mid\bm{z})}\biggr]+\gamma\,\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}\sim p_{f}}\biggl[\ln\frac{\pi^{\ast}(0\mid\bm{z})}{\hat{\pi}(0\mid\bm{z})}\biggr]

\displaystyle=(1-\gamma)\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}\sim p_{r}}\biggl[\ln\left(\frac{f^{\ast}(\bm{z})}{\hat{f}(\bm{z})}\right)\biggr]+\gamma\,\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}\sim p_{f}}\biggl[\ln\left(\frac{1-f^{\ast}(\bm{z})}{1-\hat{f}(\bm{z})}\right)\biggr]

\displaystyle=(1\!\mathbin{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{\scalebox{0.6}{$-$}}}\!\gamma)\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}\sim p_{r}}\biggl[\ln\left(\frac{(1-\gamma)\frac{p_{r}(\bm{z})}{p(\bm{z})}}{\frac{\hat{p}_{r}^{\,(1)}(\bm{z})}{p(\bm{z})}\int_{\bm{u}}p(\bm{u})\hat{f}(\bm{u})}\right)\biggr]+\gamma\,\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}\sim p_{f}}\biggl[\ln\left(\frac{\gamma\frac{p_{f}(\bm{z})}{p(\bm{z})}}{\frac{\hat{p}_{f}^{\,(1)}(\bm{z})}{p(\bm{z})}\int_{\bm{u}}p(\bm{u})(1\!\mathbin{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{\scalebox{0.6}{$-$}}}\!\hat{f}(\bm{u}))}\right)\biggr]

\displaystyle=(1\!\mathbin{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{\scalebox{0.6}{$-$}}}\!\gamma){\mathrm{KL}}\left(p_{r}\,\|\,\hat{p}_{r}^{\,(1)}\!\right)\!+\gamma{\mathrm{KL}}\left(p_{f}\,\|\,\hat{p}_{f}^{\,(1)}\!\right)\mathbin{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{\scalebox{0.6}{$-$}}}(1\!\mathbin{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{\scalebox{0.6}{$-$}}}\!\gamma)\ln\!\int_{\bm{u}}\frac{p(\bm{u})\hat{f}(\bm{u})}{1\!\mathbin{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{\scalebox{0.6}{$-$}}}\!\gamma}\mathbin{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{\scalebox{0.6}{$-$}}}\gamma\ln\!\int_{\bm{u}}\frac{p(\bm{u})(1\!\mathbin{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{\scalebox{0.6}{$-$}}}\!\hat{f}(\bm{u}))}{\gamma}

\displaystyle\geq(1-\gamma){\mathrm{KL}}\left(p_{r}\,\|\,\hat{p}_{r}^{\,(1)}\right)+\gamma{\mathrm{KL}}\left(p_{f}\,\|\,\hat{p}_{f}^{\,(1)}\right)-\ln\left(\int_{\bm{u}}p(\bm{u})\hat{f}(\bm{u})+p(\bm{u})(1-\hat{f}(\bm{u})\right)

\displaystyle=(1-\gamma){\mathrm{KL}}\left(p_{r}\,\|\,\hat{p}_{r}^{\,(1)}\right)+\gamma{\mathrm{KL}}\left(p_{f}\,\|\,\hat{p}_{f}^{\,(1)}\right)-\ln\int_{\bm{u}}p(\bm{u})

\displaystyle=(1-\gamma){\mathrm{KL}}\left(p_{r}\,\|\,\hat{p}_{r}^{\,(1)}\right)+\gamma{\mathrm{KL}}\left(p_{f}\,\|\,\hat{p}_{f}^{\,(1)}\right)

Note that the sole inequality above follows from the convexity of
\mathbin{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{\scalebox{0.6}{$-$}}}\!\ln(\,\cdot\,)
. Thus,

(1-\gamma){\mathrm{KL}}\left(p_{r}\,\|\,\hat{p}_{r}^{\,(1)}\right)+\gamma{\mathrm{KL}}\left(p_{f}\,\|\,\hat{p}_{f}^{\,(1)}\right)\leq L(\hat{f})-L(f^{\ast})\leq\delta

Since KL divergence is non-negative, we have that

\mathcal{E}_{r}(\hat{p}_{r}^{\,(1)})\coloneq{\mathrm{KL}}\left(p_{r}\,\|\,\hat{p}_{r}^{\,(1)}\right)\leq\frac{\delta}{1-\gamma}.

∎

A.2
Proof of Theorem
3.4

Theorem
.

Let
\hat{f}\in\mathcal{F}
satisfy the excess risk bound
L(\hat{f})-L(f^{\ast})\leq\delta
. Then the Forget Error (
7
) of the untempered estimate
\hat{p}_{r}^{\,(1)}
in (
8
) satisfies

\mathcal{E}_{f}(\hat{p}_{r}^{\,(1)})\leq\left\|p_{f}\right\|_{\infty}\sqrt{\frac{2\delta}{1-\gamma}}.

Proof of Theorem
3.4
.

We can immediately relate the
\ell_{1}
error to the KL divergence bound in Theorem
3.3
using Pinsker’s inequality.

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{f}(\hat{p}_{r}^{\,(1)})\coloneq\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}\sim p_{f}}\biggl[\left|p_{r}(\bm{z})-\hat{p}_{r}^{\,(1)}(\bm{z})\right|\biggr]

\displaystyle=\int_{\bm{z}}p_{f}(\bm{z})\left|p_{r}(\bm{z})-\hat{p}_{r}^{\,(1)}(\bm{z})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|p_{f}\right\|_{\infty}\left\|p_{r}-\hat{p}_{r}^{\,(1)}\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|p_{f}\right\|_{\infty}\sqrt{2\cdot{\mathrm{KL}}\left(p_{r}\,\|\,\hat{p}_{r}^{\,(1)}\right)}\qquad\text{(Pinsker's)}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|p_{f}\right\|_{\infty}\sqrt{\frac{2\delta}{(1-\gamma)}}\qquad\text{(Theorem~\ref{th:risk-to-retain-err-untempered})}.

A.3
Proof of Theorem
3.5

Theorem
.

Let
p_{r}
and
p_{f}
have disjoint supports, with
p_{f}
uniform on its support. Then for any mixture weight
\gamma\in(0,1)
and any
\delta>0
, there exists a classifier
\hat{f}
achieving excess risk
L(\hat{f})-L(f^{*})\leq\delta
such that the Forget Error (
7
) of the untempered estimate
\hat{p}_{r}^{\,(1)}
in (
8
) satisfies

\mathcal{E}_{f}(\hat{p}_{r}^{\,(1)})\geq\left\|p_{f}\right\|_{\infty}\cdot\frac{\gamma\left(1-\exp\left(-\frac{\delta}{\gamma}\right)\right)}{1-\gamma\exp\left(-\frac{\delta}{\gamma}\right)}.

In particular, as
\delta\to 0
, we have
\mathcal{E}_{f}(\hat{p}_{r}^{\,(1)})=\Omega\left(\left\|p_{f}\right\|_{\infty}\!\!\cdot\delta\right)
.

Proof of Theorem
3.5
.

Let
\mathcal{Z}_{r}=\operatorname{supp}(p_{r})
and
\mathcal{Z}_{f}=\operatorname{supp}(p_{f})
where
\mathcal{Z}_{r}\cap\mathcal{Z}_{f}=\emptyset
. Define
p_{f}
as the uniform distribution over
\mathcal{Z}_{f}
, while
p_{r}
can be any arbitrary density supported on
\mathcal{Z}_{f}
.

We first construct a witness classifier
\hat{f}
that satisfies the risk bound
L(\hat{f})-L(f^{*})\leq\delta
but maximizes the unlearning error. Define
\hat{f}
as:

\hat{f}(\bm{z})=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\bm{z}\in\mathcal{Z}_{r}\\
\epsilon&\text{if }\bm{z}\in\mathcal{Z}_{f}\end{cases}

(9)

where
\epsilon\in(0,1)
is a constant scalar. Thus,
\hat{f}
achieves perfect performance over the retain component support
\mathcal{Z}_{r}
but has
\epsilon
error over
\mathcal{Z}_{f}
. Note that the optimal classifier
f^{\ast}(\bm{z})
is the indicator function:

f^{\ast}(\bm{z})=\mathds{1}\{\bm{z}\in\mathcal{Z}_{r}\}.

Thus, we compute the excess risk of
\hat{f}
using the decomposition in the proof of Theorem
3.3
in Appendix
A.1
:

\displaystyle L(\hat{f})-L(f^{\ast})

\displaystyle=(1-\gamma)\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}\sim p_{r}}\biggl[\ln\left(\frac{f^{\ast}(\bm{z})}{\hat{f}(\bm{z})}\right)\biggr]+\gamma\,\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}\sim p_{f}}\biggl[\ln\left(\frac{1-f^{\ast}(\bm{z})}{1-\hat{f}(\bm{z})}\right)\biggr]

\displaystyle=\gamma\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}\sim p_{f}}[-\ln(1-\hat{f}(\bm{z}))]

\displaystyle=-\gamma\ln(1-\epsilon).

We saturate the risk bound by setting
-\gamma\ln(1-\epsilon)=\delta
, implying that
\epsilon=1-\exp\left(-\frac{\delta}{\gamma}\right)
.

For our estimator
\hat{p}_{r}
, let
N
denote the partition function:

N=\int_{\bm{u}}p(\bm{u})\,\hat{f}(\bm{u}).

Note that for any
\bm{z}_{f}\in\mathcal{Z}_{f}
we have that
p(\bm{z}_{f})=\gamma p_{f}(\bm{z}_{f})
and
p_{r}(\bm{z}_{f})=0
. The Forget Error is then

\mathcal{E}_{f}(\hat{p}_{r}^{\,(1)})\coloneq\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}\sim p_{f}}\left[\left|p_{r}(\bm{z})-\hat{p}_{r}^{\,(1)}(\bm{z})\right|\right]=\int_{\bm{z}\in\mathcal{Z}_{f}}p_{f}(\bm{z})\left|0-\frac{\gamma p_{f}(\bm{z})\epsilon}{N}\right|=\left\|p_{f}\right\|_{2}^{2}\cdot\frac{\gamma\epsilon}{N}.

Since
p_{f}
is uniform over
\mathcal{Z}_{f}
, its squared
\ell_{2}
-norm coincides with its peak value, which is the inverse of the support volume:

\left\|p_{f}\right\|_{2}^{2}=\left\|p_{f}\right\|_{\infty}=\operatorname{Vol}(\mathcal{Z}_{f})^{-1}.

Thus,

\mathcal{E}_{f}(\hat{p}_{r})=\left\|p_{f}\right\|_{\infty}\cdot\frac{\gamma\epsilon}{N}.

(10)

We lastly compute
N
exactly using the definition of
\hat{f}
in (
9
):

\displaystyle N

\displaystyle=\int_{\bm{z}}p(\bm{z})\hat{f}(\bm{z})

\displaystyle=\int_{\bm{z}\in\mathcal{Z}_{f}}(1-\gamma)p_{r}(\bm{z})\hat{f}(\bm{z})+\int_{\bm{z}\in\mathcal{Z}_{f}}\gamma p_{f}(\bm{z})\hat{f}(\bm{z})

\displaystyle=\int_{\bm{z}\in\mathcal{Z}_{f}}(1-\gamma)p_{r}(\bm{z})+\int_{\bm{z}\in\mathcal{Z}_{f}}\gamma p_{f}(\bm{z})\epsilon

\displaystyle=(1-\gamma)+\gamma\epsilon

Substituting
N(1-\gamma)+\gamma\epsilon
and
\epsilon=1-\exp\left(-\frac{\delta}{\gamma}\right)
into the expression in (
10
):

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{f}(\hat{p}_{r}^{\,(1)})

\displaystyle\geq\left\|p_{f}\right\|_{\infty}\cdot\frac{\gamma\left(1-\exp\left(-\frac{\delta}{\gamma}\right)\right)}{(1-\gamma)+\gamma\left(1-\exp\left(-\frac{\delta}{\gamma}\right)\right)}

\displaystyle=\left\|p_{f}\right\|_{\infty}\cdot\frac{\gamma\left(1-\exp\left(-\frac{\delta}{\gamma}\right)\right)}{1-\gamma\exp\left(-\frac{\delta}{\gamma}\right)}

This establishes the main theorem statement. We now analyze how this lower bound scales as
\delta\to 0
. Using the Taylor expansion
e^{x}=1+x+\mathcal{O}(x^{2})
and substituting
x=-\delta/\gamma
, we get:

\exp\left(-\frac{\delta}{\gamma}\right)=1-\frac{\delta}{\gamma}+\mathcal{O}(\delta^{2}).

Substituting this expansion:

\displaystyle\left\|p_{f}\right\|_{\infty}\cdot\frac{\gamma\left(1-\exp\left(-\frac{\delta}{\gamma}\right)\right)}{1-\gamma\exp\left(-\frac{\delta}{\gamma}\right)}

\displaystyle=\left\|p_{f}\right\|_{\infty}\cdot\frac{\gamma\left(1-\left(1-\frac{\delta}{\gamma}+\mathcal{O}(\delta^{2})\right)\right)}{1-\gamma\left(1-\frac{\delta}{\gamma}+\mathcal{O}(\delta^{2})\right)}

\displaystyle=\left\|p_{f}\right\|_{\infty}\cdot\frac{\gamma\left(\frac{\delta}{\gamma}-\mathcal{O}(\delta^{2})\right)}{1-\gamma+\delta-\mathcal{O}(\delta^{2})}

\displaystyle=\left\|p_{f}\right\|_{\infty}\cdot\frac{\delta+\mathcal{O}(\delta^{2})}{(1-\gamma)+\delta+\mathcal{O}(\delta^{2})}.

The term
(1-\gamma)
is a non-zero constant, so as
\delta\to 0
, the higher-order terms and the
\delta
in the denominator become negligible compared to
(1-\gamma)
. Further since
(1-\gamma)>0
is fixed and independent of
\delta
, we obtain:

\mathcal{E}_{f}(\hat{p}_{r}^{\,(1)})=\Omega\left(\left\|p_{f}\right\|_{\infty}\!\!\cdot\delta\right).

∎

A.4
Supporting Lemmas

Before proving the remaining theorem statements, we prove two key lemmas.

Lemma A.1

(Classifier
\ell_{1}
Error Bound)
.

Let
\hat{f}\in\mathcal{F}
satisfy the population risk bound
L(\hat{f})-L(f^{\ast})\leq\delta
. Then,

\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}\sim p}\left[\left|f^{\ast}(\bm{z})-\hat{f}(\bm{z})\right|\right]\leq\sqrt{\frac{\delta}{2}}.

Proof of Lemma
A.1
.

We identify
\left|\hat{f}(\bm{z})-f^{\ast}(\bm{z})\right|
as the total variation distance between the Bernoulli random variables over the conditional class label
s\mid\bm{z}
induced by
\hat{f}
and
f^{\ast}
. Define the Bernoulli PMF over the class label
s
induced by
\hat{f}
as

\hat{\pi}(s\mid\bm{z})=\begin{cases}\hat{f}(\bm{z})&\text{if }s=1\\
1-\hat{f}(\bm{z})&\text{else,}\end{cases}

and let
\pi^{\ast}(s\mid\bm{z})
denote the distribution induced by the true class posterior
f^{\ast}
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}\sim p}\left[\left|\hat{f}(\bm{z})-f^{\ast}(\bm{z})\right|\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}\sim p}\left[D_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(\pi^{\ast}(\,\cdot\mid\bm{z}),\hat{\pi}(\,\cdot\mid\bm{z})\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}\sim p}\left[\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\pi^{\ast}(\,\cdot\mid\bm{z})\,\|\,\hat{\pi}(\,\cdot\mid\bm{z})\right)}\right]\qquad\text{(Pinsker's)}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}\sim p}\left[\frac{1}{2}{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\pi^{\ast}(\,\cdot\mid\bm{z})\,\|\,\hat{\pi}(\,\cdot\mid\bm{z})\right)\right]}\qquad\text{(Jensen's)}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\left(L(\hat{f})-L(f^{\ast})\right)}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{\delta}{2}}

∎

Lemma A.2

(Partition Function Lower Bound)
.

Let
\hat{f}\in\mathcal{F}
satisfy the population risk bound
L(\hat{f})-L(f^{\ast})\leq\delta
. Then,

\int_{\bm{z}}p(\bm{z})^{1/T}\hat{f}(\bm{z})\geq(1-\gamma)^{\frac{T+1}{T}}\exp\left(\frac{T-1}{T}H(p_{r})-\frac{\delta-\gamma\ln\gamma}{1-\gamma}\right)

Proof of Lemma
A.2
.

We first lower bound the mixture density
p=(1-\gamma)p_{r}+\gamma p_{f}
by just the retain component
(1-\gamma)p_{r}
:

\displaystyle\int_{\bm{z}}p(\bm{z})^{1/T}\hat{f}(\bm{z})

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\bm{z}}((1-\gamma)p_{r}(\bm{z}))^{1/T}\hat{f}(\bm{z})

\displaystyle=(1-\gamma)^{1/T}\int_{\bm{z}}p_{r}(\bm{z})^{1/T}\hat{f}(z)

\displaystyle=(1-\gamma)^{1/T}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}\sim p_{r}}\left[p_{r}(\bm{z})^{\frac{1-T}{T}}\hat{f}(\bm{z})\right].

(11)

Since the logarithm is concave, by Jensen’s Inequality:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}\sim p_{r}}\left[p_{r}(\bm{z})^{\frac{1-T}{T}}\hat{f}(\bm{z})\right]

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\ln\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}\sim p_{r}}\left[p_{r}(\bm{z})^{\frac{1-T}{T}}\hat{f}(\bm{z})\right]\right)

\displaystyle\geq\exp\left(\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}\sim p_{r}}\left[\ln\left(p_{r}(\bm{z})^{\frac{1-T}{T}}\hat{f}(\bm{z})\right)\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\frac{T-1}{T}H(p_{r})+\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}\sim p_{r}}\left[\ln\hat{f}(\bm{z})\right]\right).

(12)

Then we can control the final term
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}\sim p_{r}}\left[\ln\hat{f}(\bm{z})\right]
using the excess risk bound
L(\hat{f})-L(f^{\ast})\leq\delta
. From the total risk bound, we have the intermediate bound (See Appendix
A.1
):

(1-\gamma)\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}\sim p_{r}}\biggl[\ln\left(\frac{f^{\ast}(\bm{z})}{\hat{f}(\bm{z})}\right)\biggr]+\gamma\,\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}\sim p_{f}}\biggl[\ln\left(\frac{1-f^{\ast}(\bm{z})}{1-\hat{f}(\bm{z})}\right)\biggr]\leq\delta

We lower bound the second term as follows:

\displaystyle\gamma\,\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}\sim p_{f}}\biggl[\ln\left(\frac{1-f^{\ast}(\bm{z})}{1-\hat{f}(\bm{z})}\right)\biggr]

\displaystyle=\gamma\,\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}\sim p_{f}}\biggl[\ln\left(\frac{\gamma\frac{p_{f}(\bm{z})}{p(\bm{z})}}{\frac{\hat{p}_{f}(\bm{z})}{p(\bm{z})}\int_{\bm{u}}p(\bm{u})(1-\hat{f}(\bm{u}))}\right)\biggr]

\displaystyle=\gamma\left({\mathrm{KL}}\left(p_{f}\,\|\,\hat{p}_{f}\right)-\ln\int_{\bm{u}}\frac{p(\bm{u})(1-\hat{f}(\bm{u}))}{\gamma}\right)

\displaystyle\geq-\gamma\ln\int_{\bm{u}}\frac{p(\bm{u})(1-\hat{f}(\bm{u}))}{\gamma}

\displaystyle\geq-\gamma\ln\int_{\bm{u}}\frac{p(\bm{u})}{\gamma}

\displaystyle=\gamma\ln\gamma.

The first inequality follows from the non-negativity of KL divergence, and the second comes from the fact that
\hat{f}(\bm{u})\in[0,1]
. Thus,

(1-\gamma)\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}\sim p_{r}}\left[\ln\frac{f^{\ast}(\bm{z})}{\hat{f}(\bm{z})}\right]\leq\delta-\gamma\ln\gamma

Rearranging terms,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}\sim p_{r}}\left[\ln\hat{f}(\bm{z})\right]

\displaystyle\geq-\frac{\delta-\gamma\ln\gamma}{1-\gamma}+\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}\sim p_{r}}\left[\ln f^{\ast}(\bm{z})\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{\delta-\gamma\ln\gamma}{1-\gamma}+\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}\sim p_{r}}\left[\ln\left((1-\gamma)\frac{p_{r}(\bm{z})}{p(\bm{z})}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{\delta-\gamma\ln\gamma}{1-\gamma}+\ln(1-\gamma)+{\mathrm{KL}}\left(p_{r}\,\|\,p\right)

\displaystyle\geq-\frac{\delta-\gamma\ln\gamma}{1-\gamma}+\ln(1-\gamma).

(13)

Substituting the bound in (
13
) for (
12
) gives that

\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}\sim p_{r}}\left[p_{r}(\bm{z})^{\frac{1-T}{T}}\hat{f}(\bm{z})\right]\geq(1-\gamma)\exp\left(\frac{T-1}{T}H(p_{r})-\frac{\delta-\gamma\ln\gamma}{1-\gamma}\right)

Then by (
11
),

\int_{\bm{z}}p(\bm{z})^{1/T}\hat{f}(\bm{z})\geq(1-\gamma)^{\frac{T+1}{T}}\exp\left(\frac{T-1}{T}H(p_{r})-\frac{\delta-\gamma\ln\gamma}{1-\gamma}\right)

∎

A.5
Proof of Theorem
3.7

Theorem
.

Let
\hat{f}\in\mathcal{F}
satisfy the excess risk bound
L(\hat{f})-L(f^{\ast})\leq\delta
. Define the
\tau
-tempered oracle estimate
p_{r}^{(\tau)}\propto p^{1/\tau}\cdot f^{\ast}
for
\tau\in[1,T]
. Consider
k\geq T
such that

\int p^{\frac{k-T}{T(k-1)}}<\infty.

Define
A(T,\gamma)
as

A(T,\gamma)\coloneq(1-\gamma)^{\frac{T+1}{T}}\exp\left(\frac{T-1}{T}H(p_{r})+\frac{\gamma\ln\gamma}{1-\gamma}\right).

Then for some
\tau\in[1,T]
, the Forget Error (
7
) of the
T
-tempered estimate
\hat{p}_{r}^{\,(T)}
in (
8
) satisfies

\mathcal{E}_{f}(\hat{p}_{r}^{\,(T)})\leq\left(1-\tfrac{1}{T}\right)\left\|p_{f}\right\|_{2,p_{r}^{(\tau)}}\!\cdot\operatorname{Std}_{p_{r}^{(\tau)}}[\ln p]+\frac{\left\|p_{f}\right\|_{\infty}^{1/T}\left(\delta/2\right)^{\frac{1}{2T}}}{A(T,\gamma)}+\frac{\left\|p_{f}\right\|_{\infty}^{1/T}\left(\int_{\bm{z}}p(\bm{z})^{\frac{k-T}{T(k-1)}}\right)^{\frac{k-1}{k}}\left(\delta/2\right)^{\frac{1}{2k}}}{A^{2}(T,\gamma)\exp\left(\mathbin{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{\scalebox{0.6}{$-$}}}\frac{\delta}{1-\gamma}\right)}

Proof of Theorem
3.7
.

Define the ground truth
T
-tempered density
p_{r}^{(T)}(\bm{z})\propto p(\bm{z})^{1/T}\cdot f^{\ast}(\bm{z})
. We then decompose the Forget Error into a tempering bias and estimation error.

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{f}(\hat{p}_{r}^{\,(T)})\coloneq\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}\sim p_{f}}\left[\left|p_{r}(\bm{z})-\hat{p}_{r}^{\,(T)}(\bm{z})\right|\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}\sim p_{f}}\left[\left|p_{r}(\bm{z})-p_{r}^{(T)}(\bm{z})+p_{r}^{(T)}(\bm{z})-\hat{p}_{r}^{\,(T)}(\bm{z})\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}\sim p_{f}}\left[\left|p_{r}(\bm{z})-p_{r}^{(T)}(\bm{z})\right|\right]}_{\text{Tempering Bias}}+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}\sim p_{f}}\left[\left|p_{r}^{(T)}(\bm{z})-\hat{p}_{r}^{\,(T)}(\bm{z})\right|\right]}_{\text{Estimation Error}}

(14)

We first bound the tempering bias term. Using the fact that
f^{\ast}\propto p_{r}/p
, we have that

p_{r}^{(T)}(\bm{z})\propto p_{r}(\bm{z})p(\bm{z})^{\frac{1}{T}-1}.

Rather than working with the temperature indexed family
p_{r}^{\,(\,\cdot\,)}
directly, we define a family of distributions
q_{\alpha}(\bm{z})
indexed by the inverse temperature
\alpha\in[1/T,1]
, which interpolates between the tempered estimator and the target distribution:

q_{\alpha}(\bm{z})\coloneq p_{r}^{\,(1/\alpha)}=\frac{p_{r}(\bm{z})p(\bm{z})^{\alpha-1}}{N(\alpha)},\quad\text{where }N(\alpha)=\int_{\bm{u}}p_{r}(\bm{u})p(\bm{u})^{\alpha-1}.

Note that At the endpoints of our interval, we recover our distributions of interest:

\displaystyle q_{1/T}(\bm{z})

\displaystyle=p_{r}^{(T)}(\bm{z})\quad\text{and}\quad q_{1}(\bm{z})=p_{r}(\bm{z}).

We first compute the derivative of the density
q_{\alpha}(\bm{z})
with respect to
\alpha
:

\frac{\partial q_{\alpha}(\bm{z})}{\partial\alpha}=q_{\alpha}(\bm{z})\frac{\partial\ln q_{\alpha}(\bm{z})}{\partial\alpha}=q_{\alpha}(\bm{z})\left(\ln p(\bm{z})-\mathbb{E}_{\bm{u}\sim q_{\alpha}}[\ln p(\bm{u})]\right).

By the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, we can express the difference between the target and the estimator as the integral of this derivative:

p_{r}(\bm{z})-p_{r}^{(T)}(\bm{z})=\int_{1/T}^{1}\frac{\partial q_{\alpha}(\bm{z})}{\partial\alpha}d\alpha.

We substitute this into our error metric
\mathcal{E}_{f}(\hat{p}_{r}^{\,(T)})
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{f}(\hat{p}_{r}^{\,(T)})\coloneq\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}\sim p_{f}}[|p_{r}(\bm{z})-p_{r}^{(T)}(\bm{z})|]

\displaystyle=\int_{\bm{z}}\left(p_{f}(\bm{z})\left|\int_{1/T}^{1}\frac{\partial q_{\alpha}(\bm{z})}{\partial\alpha}d\alpha\right|\right)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\bm{z}}\left(p_{f}(\bm{z})\int_{1/T}^{1}\left|\frac{\partial q_{\alpha}(\bm{z})}{\partial\alpha}\right|d\alpha\right).

We apply Fubini’s Theorem to swap the order of integration:

\mathcal{E}_{f}(\hat{p}_{r}^{\,(T)})\leq\int_{1/T}^{1}\underbrace{\left(\int_{\bm{z}}p_{f}(\bm{z})\left|\frac{\partial q_{\alpha}(\bm{z})}{\partial\alpha}\right|\right)}_{M(\alpha)}d\alpha.

Here,
M(\alpha)
is a scalar function representing the instantaneous error rate at temperature
\alpha
. By the Mean Value Theorem for Integrals, there exists a fixed
\alpha^{\ast}\in[1/T,1]
such that:

\int_{1/T}^{1}M(\alpha)d\alpha=\left(1-\frac{1}{T}\right)M(\alpha^{*}).

Substituting the definition of the derivative back into
M(\alpha^{*})
, we obtain:

\mathcal{E}_{f}(\hat{p}_{r}^{\,(T)})\leq\left(1-\frac{1}{T}\right)\int_{\bm{z}}p_{f}(\bm{z})q_{\alpha^{*}}(\bm{z})\left|\ln p(\bm{z})-\mathbb{E}_{q_{\alpha^{*}}}[\ln p]\right|.

Let
\tau=1/\alpha^{\ast}
be the corresponding temperature. To interpret the resulting bound, we apply Hölder’s inequality:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{f}(\hat{p}_{r}^{\,(T)})

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-\frac{1}{T}\right)\int_{\bm{z}}p_{f}(\bm{z})p_{r}^{(\tau)}(\bm{z})\left|\ln p(\bm{z})-\mathbb{E}_{\bm{u}\sim p_{r}^{(\tau)}}\left[\ln p(\bm{u})\right]\right|

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{1}{T}\right)\int_{\bm{z}}\left(p_{f}(\bm{z})\sqrt{p_{r}^{(\tau)}(\bm{z})}\right)\left(\sqrt{p_{r}^{(\tau)}(\bm{z})}\left|\ln p(\bm{z})-\mathbb{E}_{\bm{u}\sim p_{r}^{(\tau)}}\left[\ln p(\bm{u})\right]\right|\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-\frac{1}{T}\right)\left\|p_{f}\right\|_{2,p_{r}^{(\tau)}}\cdot\operatorname{Std}_{\bm{z}\sim p_{r}^{(\tau)}}[\ln p(\bm{z})],

where
\left\|p_{f}\right\|_{2,p_{r}^{(\tau)}}=(\int p_{r}^{(\tau)}p_{f}^{2})^{1/2}
.

We now bound the estimation error. Define the partition functions:

N_{T}=\int_{\bm{z}}p(\bm{z})^{1/T}\hat{f}(\bm{z})\qquad\text{and}\qquad N_{T}^{\ast}=\int_{\bm{z}}p(\bm{z})^{1/T}f^{\ast}(\bm{z})

We then expand the estimation error term using the triangle inequality:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p_{f}(\bm{z})}

\displaystyle\left[\left|p_{r}^{(T)}(\bm{z})-\hat{p}_{r}^{\,(T)}(\bm{z})\right|\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{\bm{z}}p_{f}(\bm{z})\left|p_{r}^{(T)}(\bm{z})-\hat{p}_{r}^{\,(T)}(\bm{z})\right|

\displaystyle=\int_{\bm{z}}p_{f}(\bm{z})\left|\frac{p(\bm{z})^{1/T}f^{\ast}(\bm{z})}{N_{T}^{\ast}}-\frac{p(\bm{z})^{1/T}\hat{f}(\bm{z})}{N_{T}}\right|

\displaystyle=\int_{\bm{z}}p_{f}(\bm{z})\left|\frac{p(\bm{z})^{1/T}f^{\ast}(\bm{z})}{N_{T}^{\ast}}-\frac{p(\bm{z})^{1/T}\hat{f}(\bm{z})}{N_{T}^{\ast}}+\frac{p(\bm{z})^{1/T}\hat{f}(\bm{z})}{N_{T}^{\ast}}-\frac{p(\bm{z})^{1/T}\hat{f}(\bm{z})}{N_{T}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{N_{T}^{\ast}}\int_{\bm{z}}p_{f}(\bm{z})\left|p(\bm{z})^{1/T}f^{\ast}(\bm{z})-p(\bm{z})^{1/T}\hat{f}(\bm{z})\right|+\int_{\bm{z}}p_{f}(\bm{z})p(\bm{z})^{1/T}\hat{f}(\bm{z})\left|\frac{1}{N_{T}^{\ast}}-\frac{1}{N_{T}}\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{T}^{\ast}}\int_{\bm{z}}p_{f}(\bm{z})\left|p(\bm{z})^{1/T}f^{\ast}(\bm{z})-p(\bm{z})^{1/T}\hat{f}(\bm{z})\right|+\int_{\bm{z}}p_{f}(\bm{z})p(\bm{z})^{1/T}\hat{f}(\bm{z})\frac{\left|N_{T}^{\ast}-N_{T}\right|}{N_{T}^{\ast}N_{T}}.

(15)

Using Lemma
A.2
, we can immediately lower bound the partition functions:

\displaystyle N_{T}^{\ast}

\displaystyle\geq(1-\gamma)^{\frac{T+1}{T}}\exp\left(\frac{T-1}{T}H(p_{r})+\frac{\gamma\ln\gamma}{1-\gamma}\right)

\displaystyle N_{T}

\displaystyle\geq(1-\gamma)^{\frac{T+1}{T}}\exp\left(\frac{T-1}{T}H(p_{r})-\frac{\delta-\gamma\ln\gamma}{1-\gamma}\right).

Defining
A(T,\gamma)
as
A(T,\gamma)\coloneq(1-\gamma)^{\frac{T+1}{T}}\exp\left(\frac{T-1}{T}H(p_{r})+\frac{\gamma\ln\gamma}{1-\gamma}\right)
, we can then express the bounds as

\displaystyle N_{T}^{\ast}

\displaystyle\geq A(T,\gamma)

\displaystyle N_{T}

\displaystyle\geq A(T,\gamma)\exp\left(-\frac{\delta}{1-\gamma}\right)

Thus, we must control the remaining two integrals as well as the partition function difference
N_{T}^{\ast}-N_{T}
. Define the integrals:

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle=\int_{\bm{z}}p_{f}(\bm{z})\left|p(\bm{z})^{1/T}f^{\ast}(\bm{z})-p(\bm{z})^{1/T}\hat{f}(\bm{z})\right|

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\bm{z}}p_{f}(\bm{z})p(\bm{z})^{1/T}\hat{f}(\bm{z}).

We first upper bound
I_{1}
by applying Hölder’s inequality using conjugate pairs
\frac{T}{T-1}
and
T

\displaystyle I_{1}=\int_{\bm{z}}p_{f}(\bm{z})p(\bm{z})^{1/T}\left|f^{\ast}(\bm{z})-\hat{f}(\bm{z})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|p_{f}(\bm{z})\right\|_{\frac{T}{T-1}}\cdot\left\|p^{1/T}\cdot|f^{\ast}-\hat{f}|\right\|_{T}

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{\bm{z}}p_{f}(\bm{z})^{\frac{T}{T-1}}\right)^{\frac{T-1}{T}}\left(\int_{\bm{z}}p(\bm{z})\left|f^{\ast}(\bm{z})-\hat{f}(\bm{z})\right|^{T}\right)^{\frac{1}{T}}

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{\bm{z}}p_{f}(\bm{z})\cdot p_{f}(\bm{z})^{\frac{1}{T-1}}\right)^{\frac{T-1}{T}}\left(\int_{\bm{z}}p(\bm{z})\left|f^{\ast}(\bm{z})-\hat{f}(\bm{z})\right|^{T}\right)^{\frac{1}{T}}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\left\|p_{f}\right\|_{\infty}^{\frac{1}{T-1}}\int_{\bm{z}}p_{f}(\bm{z})\right)^{\frac{T-1}{T}}\left(\int_{\bm{z}}p(\bm{z})\left|f^{\ast}(\bm{z})-\hat{f}(\bm{z})\right|\right)^{\frac{1}{T}}

\displaystyle=\left\|p_{f}\right\|_{\infty}^{1/T}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}\sim p}\left[\left|f^{\ast}(\bm{z})-\hat{f}(\bm{z})\right|\right]^{\frac{1}{T}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|p_{f}\right\|_{\infty}^{1/T}\left(\frac{\delta}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2T}}\qquad\text{(Lemma \ref{lm:EV-f-minus-fstar-app})}

The second inequality follows from that fact that
f^{\ast}(\bm{z}),\hat{f}(\bm{z})\in[0,1]
, so
|f^{\ast}(\bm{z})-\hat{f}(\bm{z})|\in[0,1]
. We bound

\displaystyle I_{2}=\int_{\bm{z}}p_{f}(\bm{z})p(\bm{z})^{1/T}\hat{f}(\bm{z})

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\bm{z}}p_{f}(\bm{z})p(\bm{z})^{1/T}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|p_{f}\right\|_{\frac{T}{T-1}}\cdot\|p^{1/T}\|_{T}

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{\bm{z}}p_{f}^{\frac{T}{T-1}}(\bm{z})\right)^{\frac{T-1}{T}}\left(\int_{\bm{z}}p(\bm{z})\right)^{\frac{1}{T}}

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{\bm{z}}p_{f}(\bm{z})\,p_{f}(\bm{z})^{\frac{1}{T-1}}\right)^{\frac{T-1}{T}}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\left\|p_{f}\right\|_{\infty}^{\frac{1}{T-1}}\int_{\bm{z}}p_{f}(\bm{z})\right)^{\frac{T-1}{T}}

\displaystyle=\left\|p_{f}\right\|_{\infty}^{1/T}

We lastly bound the difference in partition functions
\left|N_{T}^{\ast}-N_{T}\right|
. Expanding their definitions:

\displaystyle\left|N_{T}^{\ast}-N_{T}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{\bm{z}}p(\bm{z})^{1/T}f^{\ast}(\bm{z})-\int_{\bm{z}}p(\bm{z})^{1/T}\hat{f}(\bm{z})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\bm{z}}p(\bm{z})^{1/T}\left|\hat{f}(\bm{z})-f^{\ast}(\bm{z})\right|

Applying Hölder’s inequality with conjugate exponents
q=\frac{k}{k-1}
and
q^{\prime}=k
for some
k\geq T

\displaystyle\int_{\bm{z}}p(\bm{z})^{1/T}\left|\hat{f}(\bm{z})-f^{\ast}(\bm{z})\right|

\displaystyle=\int_{\bm{z}}p(\bm{z})^{\frac{k-T}{kT}}\left(p(\bm{z})^{\frac{1}{k}}\left|\hat{f}(\bm{z})-f^{\ast}(\bm{z})\right|\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left\|p^{\frac{k-T}{kT}}\right\|_{\frac{k}{k-1}}\cdot\left\|p^{1/k}\cdot|\hat{f}-f^{\ast}|\right\|_{k}

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{\bm{z}}p(\bm{z})^{\frac{k-T}{kT}\cdot\frac{k}{k-1}}\right)^{\frac{k-1}{k}}\left(\int_{\bm{z}}p(\bm{z})\,\left|\hat{f}(\bm{z})-f^{\ast}(\bm{z})\right|^{k}\right)^{1/k}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\int_{\bm{z}}p(\bm{z})^{\frac{k-T}{T(k-1)}}\right)^{\frac{k-1}{k}}\mkern-15.0mu\cdot\,\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}\sim p}\left[\left|\hat{f}(\bm{z})-f^{\ast}(\bm{z})\right|\right]^{1/k},

where the last inequality again follows from the fact that
|f^{\ast}(\bm{z})-\hat{f}(\bm{z})|\in[0,1]
.

By Lemma
A.1
, we have that
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}\sim p}\left[\left|\hat{f}(\bm{z})-f^{\ast}(\bm{z})\right|\right]^{1/k}\leq\left(\frac{\delta}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2k}}
. Combining the above relationships gives that

\left|N_{T}^{\ast}-N_{T}\right|\leq\left(\int_{\bm{z}}p(\bm{z})^{\frac{k-T}{T(k-1)}}\right)^{\frac{k-1}{k}}\left(\frac{\delta}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2k}}

Combining all results:

\mathcal{E}_{f}(\hat{p}_{r}^{\,(T)})\leq\left(1-\tfrac{1}{T}\right)\left\|p_{f}\right\|_{2,p_{r}^{(\tau)}}\!\cdot\operatorname{Std}_{p_{r}^{(\tau)}}[\ln p]+\frac{\left\|p_{f}\right\|_{\infty}^{1/T}\left(\delta/2\right)^{\frac{1}{2T}}}{A(T,\gamma)}+\frac{\left\|p_{f}\right\|_{\infty}^{1/T}\left(\int_{\bm{z}}p(\bm{z})^{\frac{k-T}{T(k-1)}}\right)^{\frac{k-1}{k}}\left(\delta/2\right)^{\frac{1}{2k}}}{A^{2}(T,\gamma)\exp\left(\mathbin{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{\scalebox{0.6}{$-$}}}\frac{\delta}{1-\gamma}\right)}

∎

where the inequality follows from Theorem

. To bound the remaining term, we define the partition functions

. Substituting this into (

Appendix B
Tempering as Smoothing under a KL Constraint

We briefly describe how tempering a distribution can be interpreted as maximizing entropy under a KL divergence constraint. Consider the following optimization problem, where
p
is some fixed distribution and
C>0
is a constant:

\mathring{p}=\argmax_{\mu}H(\mu)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\mu\,\|\,p\right)\leq C.

(22)

This is a convex program in
\mu
. Solving the Lagrangian of (
22
) shows that

\mathring{p}\propto p^{1/T(C)},

where
T(C)\geq 1
is a function of the KL constraint parameter
C
. Thus, tempering can be seen as finding the distribution with maximum entropy within a KL ball of radius
C
around
p
, i.e., the “smoothest” distribution that stays close to
p
in KL divergence.

\bm{\phi}
,
\bm{\phi}^{\prime}
:

\left|\ell\left(f_{\bm{\phi}}(\bm{z}),s\right)-\ell\left(f_{\bm{\phi}^{\prime}}(\bm{z}),s\right)\right|\leq\left\|\bm{\phi}-\bm{\phi}^{\prime}\right\|_{2}\left\|\bm{z}\right\|_{2}.

Applying this inequality to (
27
) gives that

L_{n}^{\lambda}(\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{\lambda}^{(j)}\,;\mathcal{S}_{n})-L_{n}^{\lambda}(\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{\lambda}\,;\mathcal{S}_{n})\leq\frac{1}{n}\left\|\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{\lambda}^{(j)}-\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{\lambda}\right\|_{2}\left(\left\|\bm{z}_{j}\right\|_{2}+\left\|\bm{z}_{j}^{\prime}\right\|_{2}\right).

(28)

Combining the bounds in (
25
) and (
28
) yields a bound on the distance to the “swapped” estimator:

\left\|\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{\lambda}^{(j)}-\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{\lambda}\right\|_{2}\leq\frac{1}{n\lambda}\left(\left\|\bm{z}_{j}\right\|_{2}+\left\|\bm{z}_{j}^{\prime}\right\|_{2}\right).

(29)

Thus, on the sample
(\bm{z}_{j},s_{j})
, we can bound the additional loss incurred by the swapped estimator again using Lipschitzness:

\displaystyle\ell\left(f_{\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{\lambda}^{(j)}}(\bm{z}_{j}),s_{j}\right)-\ell\left(f_{\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{\lambda}}(\bm{z}_{j}),s_{j}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{\lambda}^{(j)}-\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{\lambda}\right\|_{2}\left\|\bm{z}_{j}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n\lambda}\left(\left\|\bm{z}_{j}\right\|_{2}+\left\|\bm{z}_{j}^{\prime}\right\|_{2}\right)\left\|\bm{z}_{j}\right\|_{2}

(30)

We now use the fact that the expected excess risk when learning from the finite sample dataset
\mathcal{S}_{n}
is equal to the expected additional loss incurred by the swapped estimator on the replaced sample (Theorem 13.2 of
[
SB14
]
). Formally,

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{S}_{n}}\left[L(\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{\lambda})-L_{n}(\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{\lambda})\right]=\mathbb{E}_{(\mathcal{S}_{n},(\bm{z}_{j}^{\prime},s_{j}^{\prime}))}\left[\ell\left(f_{\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{\lambda}^{(j)}}(\bm{z}_{j}),s_{j}\right)-\ell\left(f_{\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{\lambda}}(\bm{z}_{j}),s_{j}\right)\right]

(31)

Applying (
31
) to the bound in (
30
):

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{S}_{n}}\left[L(\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{\lambda})-L_{n}(\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{\lambda})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{n\lambda}\left(\left\|\bm{z}_{j}\right\|_{2}+\left\|\bm{z}_{j}^{\prime}\right\|_{2}\right)\left\|\bm{z}_{j}\right\|_{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n\lambda}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\bm{z}_{j}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}^{2}\left[\left\|\bm{z}_{j}\right\|_{2}\right]\right)\qquad\text{(since $\bm{z}_{j}$ and $\bm{z}_{j}^{\prime}$ are i.i.d.)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{n\lambda}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\bm{z}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right],

(32)

where
\bm{z}\sim p
is a generic input variable drawn from the marginal
p
. Since the finite-sample regularized loss is larger than the unregularized loss and minimized by
\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{\lambda}
, we have that for any
\bm{\phi}
:

L_{n}(\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{\lambda})\leq L_{n}^{\lambda}(\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{\lambda})\leq L_{n}^{\lambda}(\bm{\phi}).

Taking the expectation over
\mathcal{S}_{n}
:

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{S}_{n}}\left[L_{n}(\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{\lambda})\right]\leq L(\bm{\phi})+\lambda\left\|\bm{\phi}\right\|_{2}^{2}

(33)

Applying (
33
) to
\bm{\phi}=\bm{\phi}^{\ast}
and using the bound in (
32
):

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{S}_{n}}\left[L(\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{\lambda})-L(\bm{\phi}^{\ast})\right]-\lambda\left\|\bm{\phi}^{\ast}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{S}_{n}}\left[L(\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{\lambda})-L_{n}(\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{\lambda})\right]\leq\frac{2}{n\lambda}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\bm{z}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]

(34)

Rearranging the outer inequality of (
34
) gives the desired expression.
∎

Appendix D
Density Ratio Estimation under Class Imbalance

The logistic regression excess risk bound assumes the samples in the unlearning dataset
\mathcal{S}_{n}=\{(\bm{z}_{i},s_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
reflect the true population proportion of forget set samples
\gamma
, meaning that

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{S}_{n}}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{1}\{s_{i}=0\}\right]=\gamma

However, as mentioned in Remark
C.2
, practical unlearning datasets are often constructed by subsampling
\mathcal{D}_{r}
along the full
\mathcal{D}_{f}
, resulting in an observed forget set proportion which is larger than the population value
\gamma
. In this section, we show how to modify our estimator in this case to account for this mismatch and maintain infinite-sample consistency.

Consider an
n
-sample unlearning dataset
\mathcal{S}_{n}^{\mu}=\{(\bm{z}_{i}^{\mu},s_{i}^{\mu})\}_{i=1}^{n}
with forget set proportion
\mu
:

\mu=\frac{1}{\left|\mathcal{S}_{n}^{\mu}\right|}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{1}\{s_{i}^{\mu}=0\}

We still apply the probabilistic classification subproblem and recover an estimator
\hat{f}_{\mu}
for the true conditional distribution
f_{\mu}^{\ast}(\bm{z})
defined as

\hat{f}_{\mu}(\bm{z})\approx f_{\mu}^{\ast}(\bm{z})=\mathbb{P}_{\mu}(s=1\mid\bm{z})=\frac{(1-\mu)p_{r}(\bm{z})}{(1-\mu)p_{r}(\bm{z})+\mu p_{f}(\bm{z})},

where
\mathbb{P}_{\mu}
denotes the base measure for the analogous generative process to the one defined in Section
2.1
, except when
\gamma=\mu
.

Define
p_{\mu}(\bm{z})
as the marginal over
\bm{z}
induced by
\mathbb{P}_{\mu}
:

p_{\mu}(\bm{z})=(1-\mu)p_{r}(\bm{z})+\mu p_{f}(\bm{z}).

If we could access
p_{\mu}
, then we could employ the same estimator
\hat{p}_{r}
for
p_{r}
from our previous theoretical analysis as
\hat{p}_{r}(\bm{z})\propto p_{\mu}(\bm{z})\hat{f}_{\mu}(\bm{z})
. However, we can only access
p(\bm{z})
which includes mixture weight
\gamma\neq\mu
.

However, we can still recover an unbiased estimator of
p_{r}
by modifying our transformation of
\hat{f}_{\mu}
to extract the density ratio
\frac{p_{r}(\bm{z})}{p(\bm{z})}
using the following relationship:

\frac{p_{r}(\bm{z})}{p(\bm{z})}=\frac{\mu f_{\mu}^{\ast}(\bm{z})}{(\mu-\gamma)f_{\mu}^{\ast}(\bm{z})+\gamma(1-\mu)}

Thus, we can apply a different algebraic transformation of
p
and
\hat{f}_{\mu}
to recover an asymptotically consistent estimator
\bar{p}_{r}
:

\bar{p}_{r}(\bm{z})\propto\frac{\mu\hat{f}_{\mu}(\bm{z})p(\bm{z})}{(\mu-\gamma)\hat{f}_{\mu}(\bm{z})+\gamma(1-\mu)}

When
\gamma=\mu
, we recover the same estimator
\bar{p}_{r}=\hat{p}_{r}\propto p\cdot\hat{f}_{\mu}
as in the case where
\mathcal{S}_{n}
contains i.i.d. samples from the model in Section
2.1
. While this new estimator requires knowledge of
\gamma
, we assume this is known from the original training process. Finally, we note that the original (incomputable) estimator
\hat{p}_{r}\propto\hat{f}_{\mu}\,\cdot p_{\mu}
is equal to our new estimator
\bar{p}_{r}
if and only if
\hat{f}=f^{\ast}
, i.e., the two estimators are only equivalent in the limit where they achieve optimality.

Appendix E
Synthetic Data Experiments

We evaluate the proposed method on a synthetic benchmark generated from Gaussian component distributions to verify our theoretical insights. Specifically, we consider the setting where the retain and forget distributions follow univariate Gaussian distributions, denoted as
p_{r}=\mathcal{N}(\mu_{r},v_{r})
and
p_{f}=\mathcal{N}(\mu_{f},v_{f})
, with means
\mu_{r},\mu_{f}
and variances
v_{r},v_{f}
, respectively.

Data Generation and Classification.

We construct the mixture distribution
p(z)=\gamma p_{f}(z)+(1-\gamma)p_{r}(z)
. We generate an
n
-sample dataset
\mathcal{S}_{n}=\{(z_{i},s_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
by sampling the component label
s_{i}\sim\text{Bernoulli}(1-\gamma)
, where
s_{i}=1
indicates
z_{i}\sim p_{r}
and
s_{i}=0
indicates
z_{i}\sim p_{f}
. throughout these experiments, we set
\gamma=0.1
, fix the means as
\mu_{r}=1.0
and
\mu_{f}=0.0
, and fix the retain variance
v_{r}=1.0
.

In this experimental setup, we consider the full T3-Unlearning pipeline: we train a probabilistic classifier
\hat{f}
using the generated data and construct the retain set distribution estimator
\hat{p}_{r}^{\,(T)}\propto p^{1/T}\cdot\hat{f}
. In the univariate Gaussian setting, the Bayes optimal classifier
f^{\ast}(z)=\mathbb{P}(s=1\mid z)
takes the form of a sigmoid applied to a quadratic function of the input. Consequently, we train our surrogate classifier using a specific parameterization
\bm{\phi}
by computing a quadratic feature map of the inputs
z\in\mathbb{R}
.

Let
\bm{\varphi}(z)=[1,z,z^{2}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
denote the fixed quadratic feature map, and define the family of classifiers
f_{\bm{\phi}}(\bm{\varphi}(z))=\sigma\left(\bm{\phi}^{\top}\bm{\varphi}(z)\right)
parameterized by
\bm{\phi}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, where
\sigma
denotes the sigmoid function. For a regularization coefficient
\lambda\geq 0
, we minimize the regularized logistic regression objective
L_{n}^{\lambda}
over the
n
-sample dataset
\mathcal{S}_{n}
defined as

L_{n}^{\lambda}(\bm{\phi}\,;\mathcal{S}_{n})\coloneq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(f_{\bm{\phi}}(\bm{\varphi}(z_{i})),s_{i})+\lambda\left\|\bm{\phi}\right\|_{2}^{2},

where
\ell
denotes the binary cross-entropy loss. Let
\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{\lambda}
minimize
L_{n}^{\lambda}(\bm{\phi}\,;\mathcal{S}_{n})
, so our recovered classifier is
f_{\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{\lambda}}
and the corresponding density estimate is
\hat{p}_{r}^{\,(T)}(z)\propto p^{1/T}(z)\cdot f_{\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{\lambda}}(\bm{\varphi}(z))
.

Partition Function Estimation.

When applying non-trivial tempering (
T>1
), the normalized density estimate for recovered classifier parameters
\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{\lambda}
is given by:

\hat{p}_{r}^{\,(T)}(z)=\frac{p^{1/T}(z)\cdot f_{\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{\lambda}}(\bm{\varphi}(z))}{\int_{u}p^{1/T}(u)\cdot f_{\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{\lambda}}(\bm{\varphi}(u))}.

(35)

Computing the partition function (the denominator) requires numerical integration. Since tempering a Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2})
by temperature
T
effectively scales the variance to
T\sigma^{2}
, we employ an approximation that assumes negligible overlap between the tempered components. We approximate the base density term as
p(z)^{1/T}\approx(1-\gamma)^{1/T}p_{r}(z)^{1/T}+\gamma^{1/T}p_{f}(z)^{1/T}
and then estimate the integral via Monte Carlo sampling.

We similarly evaluate the Retain Error (
6
) and Forget Error (
7
) metrics via Monte Carlo sampling, as they both represent expectations under the respective ground truth densities
p_{r}
and
p_{f}
.

Connection to Theory.
The following experiments aim to validate the theoretical analysis in Section
3
, which relates Retain and Forget Errors (measures of unlearning quality) to the excess risk of the recovered classifier
f_{\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{\lambda}}
and underlying distribution parameters such as the peak forget density
\left\|p_{f}\right\|_{\infty}
and forget proportion
\gamma
. While the theory abstracts classifier performance using the excess risk
\delta
, here we provide a more concrete perspective, linking
\delta
to practical factors that govern its achievable value: the distribution parameters and the number of available samples
n
.

We report results in terms of the underlying quantities which directly control the key theoretical parameters
\left\|p_{f}\right\|_{\infty}
(peak forget component density) and
\delta
(classifier excess risk). To probe the effect of
\left\|p_{f}\right\|_{\infty}
, we vary the forget variance
v_{f}
, noting that for a Gaussian, the peak density is
\left\|p_{f}\right\|_{\infty}=(2\pi v_{f})^{-1/2}
. Thus, sending
v_{f}\to 0
allows us to study the impact of highly concentrated
p_{f}
on unlearning. Regarding classifier performance, the primary practical factor controlling excess risk is the number of samples
n
. In the notation of this specific parameterization, the excess risk associated with a candidate set of parameters
\bm{\phi}
is defined

\delta=\mathbb{E}_{(z,s)}\left[\ell(f_{\bm{\phi}}(\bm{\varphi}(z)),s)\right]-\mathbb{E}_{(z,s)}\left[\ell(f^{\ast}(z),s)\right],

where
f^{\ast}(z)\coloneq\mathbb{P}\left(s=1\mid z\right)
is the Bayes optimal classifier.

As shown in Appendix
C
, for regularized logistic regression with properly tuned
\lambda
, the expected excess risk decays as
\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2})
. While this rate also depends on the data distribution variance, in Experiment 2 we fix distribution parameters and vary
n
to isolate its effect.

v_{f}
. As
v_{f}\to 0
, the forget component’s peak density grows unbounded,

increases (
v_{f}
decreases), larger base model temperatures
T

p_{r}
and
p_{f}
have significant overlap.

v_{f}

\lambda
that minimizes the average population risk over 10 trials. Using the selected
\lambda

n
-sample datasets and evaluate unlearning performance across a range of temperatures
T\in[1.0,3.0]

F.1
Baseline Methods

We formally define each of the baseline methods and the notation for their hyperparameters. Let
\bm{z}_{r}=(\bm{q}_{r},\bm{a}_{r})\in\mathcal{D}_{r}
and
\bm{z}_{f}=(\bm{q}_{f},\bm{a}_{f})\in\mathcal{D}_{f}
denote representative samples from the retain and forget sets, respectively. We use the notation
\bm{q}
,
\bm{a}
since benchmarks like TOFU consist of question-answer pairs, although the methods apply to general text sequences.

We denote the unlearned model by
p_{\bm{\theta}}
, where
\bm{\theta}
are the parameters optimized during unlearning, and the original pretrained parameters by
\bm{\theta}^{\ast}
. We denote by
\mathrm{StopGrad}[\cdot]
the stop-gradient operator (equivalent to
.detach()
in automatic differentiation frameworks). Let
\mathrm{Unif}(\,\cdot\,)
denote the uniform distribution over its argument. For distributions
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
, the cross entropy is defined as

{\mathrm{CrossEnt}}\left(p_{1},p_{2}\right)\coloneqq-\mathbb{E}_{p_{1}}\left[\ln p_{2}\right].

We now write the loss function
\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta}\,;\,\bm{z}_{r},\bm{z}_{f})
for each method.

Gradient Ascent (GradAscent)

[
DMZMKLM25
]
maximizes the negative log-likelihood (NLL) loss on the forget set:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{GradAscent}}(\bm{\theta}\,;\,\bm{z}_{r},\bm{z}_{f})=\ln p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{a}_{f}\mid\bm{q}_{f})

Gradient Difference (GradDiff)

[
DMZMKLM25
]
minimizes the NLL on the retain set while maximizing the NLL on the forget set. For retain and forget loss weights
\alpha_{r},\alpha_{f}\geq 0
:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{GradDiff}}(\bm{\theta}\,;\,\bm{z}_{r},\bm{z}_{f})=\alpha_{f}\ln p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{a}_{f}\mid\bm{q}_{f})-\alpha_{r}\ln p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{a}_{r}\mid\bm{q}_{r})

Weighted Gradient Ascent (WGA)

[
WZZSHW25
]
modulates the forget-set unlearning signal using a confidence-based weight
w_{\mathrm{WGA}}
defined as

w_{\mathrm{WGA}}(\bm{z}_{f})=\mathrm{StopGrad}\!\left[p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{a}_{f}\mid\bm{q}_{f})^{\beta}\right].

The resulting objective then maximizes the weighted NLL on the forget set sample while minimizing the NLL on the retain set sample. For a retain and forget loss weight
\alpha_{r},\alpha_{f}\geq 0
:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{WGA}}(\bm{\theta}\,;\,\bm{z}_{r},\bm{z}_{f})=\alpha_{f}\,w_{\mathrm{WGA}}(\bm{z}_{f})\ln p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{a}_{f}\mid\bm{q}_{f})-\alpha_{r}\ln p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{a}_{r}\mid\bm{q}_{r}).

Saturation and Importance (SatImp)

[
YWHLZH25
]
performs a similar procedure to WGA, maximizing a weighted NLL over the forget set while minimizing the NLL over the retain set. The SatImp weight
w_{\mathrm{SatImp}}
is defined as

w_{\mathrm{SatImp}}(\bm{z}_{f})=\mathrm{StopGrad}\!\left[p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{a}_{f}\mid\bm{q}_{f})^{\beta_{1}}\bigl(1-p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{a}_{f}\mid\bm{q}_{f})\bigr)^{\beta_{2}}\right],

where
\beta_{1},\beta_{2}\geq 0
control the strength of the two weights. For retain and forget loss weights
\alpha_{r},\alpha_{f}\geq 0
, the resulting objective is then

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SatImp}}(\bm{\theta}\,;\,\bm{z}_{r},\bm{z}_{f})=\alpha_{f}\,w_{\mathrm{SatImp}}(\bm{z}_{f})\ln p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{a}_{f}\mid\bm{q}_{f})-\alpha_{r}\ln p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{a}_{r}\mid\bm{q}_{r}).

Unlearning via Self-Distillation on Adjusted Logits (UnDIAL)

[
DLBHV25
]
perturbs the original model’s distribution and distills this into the unlearned model. Consider a single next-token
y_{f}
which completes the context
\bm{x}_{f}
from the forget set, meaning that the sequence
(\bm{x}_{f},y_{f})
is a subsequence of the question-answer pair
(\bm{q}_{f},\bm{a}_{f})
. We denote the perturbed distribution conditioned on the forget set context
\bm{x}_{f}
as
\tilde{p}_{\bm{\theta}^{\ast}}(\cdot\mid\bm{x}_{f})
, defined by

\ln\tilde{p}_{\bm{\theta}^{\ast}}(y\mid\bm{x}_{f})=\mathds{1}\{y=y_{f}\}\left(\ln p_{\bm{\theta}^{\ast}}(y\mid\bm{x}_{f})-\beta\right)+\mathds{1}\{y\neq y_{f}\}\ln p_{\bm{\theta}^{\ast}}(y\mid\bm{x}_{f})+C(\bm{x}_{f}),

where
\beta\geq 0
subtracts from the original model’s logit assigned the true response and
C(\bm{x}_{f})
is a normalization constant. For retain and forget loss weights
\alpha_{r},\alpha_{f}\geq 0
, the resulting UnDIAL objective is then

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{UnDIAL}}(\bm{\theta}\,;\,\bm{z}_{r},\bm{z}_{f})=\alpha_{f}{\mathrm{CrossEnt}}\left(\tilde{p}_{\bm{\theta}^{\ast}},p_{\bm{\theta}}\right)-\alpha_{r}\ln p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{a}_{r}\mid\bm{q}_{r}).

Representation Misdirection for Unlearning (RMU)

[
LPGYBGLDGM+24
]
performs unlearning by steering internal representations of forget set inputs away from their original values, while preserving representations on the retain set.

Fix a layer index
k
and let
M_{\bm{\theta}}^{k}(\bm{q})\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times L}
denote the hidden representations at layer
k
of model
p_{\bm{\theta}}
for input question
\bm{q}
, where
d
is the hidden dimension and
L
is the sequence length. RMU samples a random “control vector”
\bm{u}\sim\mathrm{Unif}([0,1]^{d})
once at the start of unlearning and rescales it to have
\ell_{2}
norm equal to some
c>0
. Let
\bm{1}_{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{L}
denote the vector of all ones.

The RMU objective encourages forget set representations to align with this fixed random target, while constraining retain set representations to remain close to those of the original pretrained model. For a norm constraint
c>0
and loss weights
\alpha_{r},\alpha_{f}\geq 0
, the resulting RMU objective is then

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{RMU}}(\bm{\theta}\,;\,\bm{z}_{r},\bm{z}_{f})=\alpha_{f}\left\|M_{\bm{\theta}}^{k}(\bm{q}_{f})-\frac{c}{\left\|\bm{u}\right\|_{2}}\bm{u}\bm{1}_{L}^{\top}\right\|_{F}^{2}+\alpha_{r}\left\|M_{\bm{\theta}}^{k}(\bm{q}_{r})-M_{\bm{\theta}^{\ast}}^{k}(\bm{q}_{r})\right\|_{F}^{2}.

Unlearning from Logit Difference (ULD)

[
JLZLKLC24
]
introduces an auxiliary assistant LLM
f_{\bm{\phi}}(\bm{q},\bm{a})
that outputs a distribution over next tokens, so
f_{\bm{\phi}}(\bm{q},\bm{a})
assigns a probability to
\bm{a}\mid\bm{q}
. Formally,
f_{\bm{\phi}}(\bm{x},\cdot)\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{V})
, where
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{V})
denotes the probability simplex over the vocabulary. This assistant is used to tilt the frozen base model distribution
p_{\bm{\theta}^{\ast}}(\cdot\mid\bm{q})
, yielding the updated model

\hat{p}_{\bm{\theta}^{\ast}\!,\bm{\phi}}(\bm{a}\mid\bm{q})\propto\frac{p_{\bm{\theta}^{\ast}}(\bm{a}\mid\bm{q})}{f_{\bm{\phi}}(\bm{q},\bm{a})^{T}},

where
T>0
controls the strength of the tilt. The base model
p_{\bm{\theta}^{\ast}}
remains fixed, and only the assistant
f_{\bm{\phi}}
is trained. In ULD,
f_{\bm{\phi}}
is implemented as a rank-
r
LoRA
[
HWALWWC+22
]
adaptation of the first
k
layers of
p_{\bm{\theta}^{\ast}}
, with the remaining layers discarded. For retain and forget loss weights
\alpha_{r},\alpha_{f}\geq 0
, the assistant is trained using the ULD objective

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ULD}}(\bm{\phi}\,;\,\bm{z}_{r},\bm{z}_{f})=-\alpha_{f}\ln f_{\bm{\phi}}(\bm{q}_{f},\bm{a}_{f})+\alpha_{r}\,{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\mathrm{Unif}(\mathcal{V})\,\|\,f_{\bm{\phi}}(\bm{q}_{r},\,\cdot)\right).

I Don’t Know Direct Preference Optimization (IdkDPO)

[
MFSLK24
]
applies the DPO
[
RSMMEF23
]
objective to the forget set samples. It predefines a set of responses like “I don’t know” or “I can’t answer that” which are used as the preferred label
\tilde{\bm{a}}_{f}
, while the original forget set ground truth
\bm{a}_{f}
is used as the dispreferred label
\bm{a}_{f}
. The original model before unlearning
p_{\bm{\theta}^{\ast}}
is used as the reference model. Additionally, it minimizes the NLL loss on the retain set. For a regularization coefficient
\beta\geq 0
and loss weights
\alpha_{r},\alpha_{f}\geq 0
, the IdkDPO objective is then

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IdkDPO}}(\bm{\theta})=-\alpha_{f}\frac{2}{\beta}\ln\sigma\left(\beta\left(\ln\frac{p_{\bm{\theta}}(\tilde{\bm{a}}_{f}\mid\bm{q}_{f})}{p_{\bm{\theta}^{\ast}}(\tilde{\bm{a}}_{f}\mid\bm{q}_{f})}-\ln\frac{p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{a}_{f}\mid\bm{q}_{f})}{p_{\bm{\theta}^{\ast}}(\bm{a}_{f}\mid\bm{q}_{f})}\right)\right)-\alpha_{r}\ln p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{a}_{r}\mid\bm{q}_{r}).

Negative Preference Optimization (NPO)

[
ZLBM24
]
applies a DPO-like objective but without a preferred response. For a regularization coefficient
\beta\geq 0
and loss weights
\alpha_{r},\alpha_{f}\geq 0
, the NPO loss is defined as

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NPO}}(\bm{\theta})=-\alpha_{f}\frac{2}{\beta}\ln\sigma\left(-\beta\ln\frac{p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{a}_{f}\mid\bm{q}_{f})}{p_{\bm{\theta}^{\ast}}(\bm{a}_{f}\mid\bm{q}_{f})}\right)-\alpha_{r}\ln p_{\bm{\theta}}(y_{r}\mid\bm{x}_{r}).

Simple NPO (SimNPO)

[
FLLJZML25
]
modifies the NPO objective by applying length normalization and removing the reliance on the reference model
p_{\bm{\theta}^{\ast}}
. For a regularization coefficient
\beta\geq 0
, reward margin parameter
\Delta\geq 0
, and loss weights
\alpha_{r},\alpha_{f}\geq 0
, the SimNPO objective is defined as

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SimNPO}}(\bm{\theta})=-\alpha_{f}\frac{2}{\beta}\ln\sigma\left(-\frac{\beta}{\left|\bm{a}_{f}\right|}\ln p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{a}_{f}\mid\bm{q}_{f})-\Delta\right)-\alpha_{r}\ln p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{a}_{r}\mid\bm{q}_{r}).

F.2
TOFU Metrics

Recall that for a model
p_{\bm{\theta}}
and a question-answer pair
(\bm{q},\bm{a})
, the Truth Ratio, denoted
R_{\text{truth}}(p_{\bm{\theta}},\bm{q})
, measures the ratio of the model’s average probability on a set of incorrect “perturbed” answers
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{pert}}
to the paraphrased true answer
\bm{a}^{\dagger}
:

R_{\text{truth}}(p_{\bm{\theta}},\bm{q})=\frac{\frac{1}{\left|\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{pert}}\right|}\sum_{\bm{\tilde{a}}\in\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{pert}}}p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{\tilde{a}}\mid\bm{q})^{1/\left|\bm{\tilde{a}}\right|}}{p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{a}^{\dagger}\mid\bm{q})^{1/\left|\bm{a}^{\dagger}\right|}}.

Using the Truth Ratio, TOFU
[
MFSLK24
]
defines two summary metrics:
Forget Quality
and
Model Utility
. We define these metrics in detail.

Forget Quality.
Forget Quality quantifies whether the unlearned model’s behavior on the forget set is statistically indistinguishable from that of a gold-standard model retrained exclusively on retained data. Specifically, it is defined as the p-value of a two-sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) test comparing the distributions of Truth Ratios generated by the unlearned model versus the retrained model over the forget set questions.

Specifically, let
\bm{\theta}^{\ast}_{r}
denote the ground truth retained model parameters. We then define two empirical distributions:

\displaystyle\hat{R}_{\text{Truth}}

\displaystyle=R_{\text{truth}}(p_{\bm{\theta}},\bm{q}),\quad\bm{q}\sim\mathcal{D}_{f}

\displaystyle R^{\ast}_{\text{Truth}}

\displaystyle=R_{\text{truth}}(p_{\bm{\theta}^{\ast}_{r}},\bm{q}),\quad\bm{q}\sim\mathcal{D}_{f},

The forget quality is computed as the p-value of the two-sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov test between
\hat{R}_{\text{Truth}}
and
R^{\ast}_{\text{Truth}}
. The sufficient statistic of the KS-Test is the supremum absolute error between the CDFs of the two distributions
\hat{R}_{\text{Truth}}
and
R^{\ast}_{\text{Truth}}
. Define the two respective CDFs as
\hat{F}
and
F_{r}^{\ast}
. Then we compute
D_{\text{KS}}
as

D_{\text{KS}}=\sup_{t}\left|\hat{F}(t)-F_{r}^{\ast}(t)\right|

Then using an approximation for the p-value, which is exactly expressed as a series, we have that the Forget Quality is approximately expressed as
2\exp\left(-\left|\mathcal{D}_{f}\right|\cdot D_{\text{KS}}^{2}\right)
.

A high p-value indicates that the unlearned model successfully mimics the output statistics of a model trained without
\mathcal{D}_{f}
.

Model Utility.
Model Utility is calculated as the harmonic mean of the
Probability
,
ROUGE
, and
TR
+
metrics across the retain, Real Author (RA), and World Facts (WF) datasets. For a model
p_{\bm{\theta}}
and a question-answer pair
(\bm{q},\bm{a})
, we define these metrics in detail.

•

Probability
computes the probability assigned to the answer given the question
p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{a}\mid\bm{q})^{1/\left|\bm{a}\right|}
, normalized by answer length. For the retain set it is computed as

p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{a}\mid\bm{q})^{1/\left|\bm{a}\right|}.

For the multiple choice RA and WF datasets, the Probability metric is additionally normalized by sum of the length-normalized probabilities assigned to a set of incorrect answers
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{pert}}
, computed as

\frac{p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{a}\mid\bm{q})^{1/\left|\bm{a}\right|}}{p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{a}\mid\bm{q})^{1/\left|\bm{a}\right|}+\sum_{\tilde{\bm{a}}\in\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{pert}}}p_{\bm{\theta}}(\tilde{\bm{a}}\mid\bm{q})^{1/{\left|\tilde{\bm{a}}\right|}}+\epsilon},

where
\epsilon=10^{-10}
is added for numerical stability.

•

ROUGE
computes the ROUGE-L recall
[
Lin04
]
which measures the overlap between the model’s generated response
\hat{\bm{a}}\sim p_{\bm{\theta}}(\,\cdot\mid\bm{q})
and the ground truth answer
\bm{a}
. Let
\mathrm{LCS}(\hat{\bm{a}},\bm{a})
denote the longest common subsequence between the two sequences after word stemming. ROUGE-L recall is then computed as the length of the longest common subsequence divided by the reference text length:

\frac{\left|\mathrm{LCS}(\hat{\bm{a}},\bm{a})\right|}{\left|\bm{a}\right|}.

In our experiments, responses are generated using greedy decoding. As a result,
\hat{\bm{a}}
should be interpreted as a deterministic decode from
p_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot\mid\bm{q})
rather than a formal sample from the model distribution.

•

TR
+
computes the inverted, clipped Truth Ratio, where higher values indicate larger confidence in the correct answer:

\text{TR{+}}(p_{\bm{\theta}},\bm{q})=\max\{0,\,1-R_{\text{truth}}(p_{\bm{\theta}},\bm{q})\}.

F.3
TOFU Experiments

We explain our experimental setup in detail. To evaluate each unlearning method, we perform a grid search over hyperparameter configurations using random seeds 1 and 2, selecting the configuration that achieves the best performance. Since we aim to maximize multiple objectives, we select the parameter configuration that maximizes the Forget Quality, since a method optimized for a model utility metric could simply return the original model without any unlearning.

We used the OpenUnlearning
[
DMZMKLM25
]
implementation of TOFU and all baselines except for ULD which does not have an official OpenUnlearning implementation. For consistency, we implemented ULD within the OpenUnlearning codebase. We used the Llama 3.1 8B model.

All experiments were conducted on a single NVIDIA GH200, using the AdamW optimizer
[
LH19
]
and a linear learning rate scheduler with both warmup and decay for all methods. For the parameter-efficient methods ULD and T3-Unlearning, we use a batch size of 32 with no gradient accumulation and ZeRO stage 0
[
RRRH20
]
. For all other methods, we use a batch size of 8 with gradient accumulation over 4 steps (effective batch size 32) and ZeRO stage 3.
