Psychosocial and supportive care in radiation therapy: a qualitative comparison of Norway and Canada
Authors: Espen Aas, Sophie Bezanson, Christine Tørris, Julia Penney, Arlinda Ruco, Safora Johansen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeRadiation therapy technologists (RTTs) are well positioned to address psychosocial and supportive care (PSSC) needs through daily patient contact. However, PSSC remains inconsistently integrated in practice. Building on prior cross-national survey findings, this study aimed to deepen understanding of how Norwegian and Canadian RTTs’ perceptions and practices in providing PSSC, and identify barriers and opportunities to improve PSSC delivery.MethodsA qualitative comparative study using semi-structured interviews was conducted with RTTs practicing in Norway and Canada. Participants were recruited through convenience and snowball sampling. The interview guide explored four categories: 1) demographics; 2) perceptions and current practices related to PSSC; 3) barriers and enablers of PSSC; and 4) opportunities to improve PSSC delivery. Interviews were transcribed and analyzed using inductive thematic analysis to identify themes and compare patterns across both countries.ResultsA total of 26 RTTs were interviewed (Norway: n = 13; Canada: n = 13). RTTs in both countries recognized the importance of providing psychosocial support and care to patients undergoing radiation therapy. Norwegian RTTs described protecting relational care through scheduling flexibility and supportive environments. Canadian RTTs described delivering PSSC through structured assessments and integrating tasks within time- and staffing-constrained environment. Shared barriers included limited time and variable awareness of resources.ConclusionNorwegian and Canadian RTTs described a strong commitment to PSSC, but organizational conditions shaped how care was delivered. Norwegian RTTs used a flexible patient-centered approach, while Canadian RTTs relied on a structured approach. Effective PSSC delivery requires alignment between RTTs’ roles, targeted education, and institutional structures that support quality care.Keywords: Psychosocial care, Supportive care, Radiation therapy technologists, Qualitative study, Cross-national comparison, Patient-centered care

Introduction

Psychosocial and supportive care (PSSC) has gained recognition as a key aspect of clinical oncology practice, helping patients cope with their cancer diagnosis and treatment [1, 2]. PSSC supports patient well-being by addressing social, psychological, emotional, spiritual, and functional needs to enhance coping and quality of life [3–8]. In practice PSSC includes emotional support, symptom management, and practical guidance, and is central to patient-centered, holistic cancer care [4, 9, 10].

Radiation therapy technologists (RTTs) maintain daily interactions with patients undergoing treatment and are well positioned to provide PSSC [4, 11, 12]. Their frequent interactions with patients help develop therapeutic relationships, reducing patient anxiety and supporting psychosocial well-being [4, 12–14]. RTTs report varying engagement in PSSC as a result of discomfort and limited confidence with emotional interactions [4, 6, 9, 11, 12, 15]. Other barriers include time constraints, limited training, and unclear role expectations related to PSSC [4, 6, 9, 11]. Institutional support and departmental culture influence RTTs’ confidence and ability to manage patients’ psychosocial distress [4, 6, 9]. System-level factors may further force RTTs to prioritize technical tasks and limit the time available for emotional support [4, 6, 11, 14].

Prior quantitative research has described cross-national patterns in RTTs’ perceptions and practices related to PSSC in Norway and Canada [4, 9, 12]. Bezanson et al. [9] found differences between Norwegian and Canadian RTTs in their perceptions and provision of PSSC within their respective universal healthcare systems [9]. In both countries, RTTs recognized PSSC as an integral aspect of quality care [9]. Norwegian RTTs reported dedicating more time to providing PSSC and were more satisfied with their current capacity [9]. Canadian RTTs expressed a stronger desire for additional training and institutional support [9]. Common barriers across both countries included staffing shortages and time constraints [9].

Qualitative studies have additionally provided insight into RTTs’ practices and personal experiences related to PSSC [14, 16]. However, existing studies have focused on single institutions or single national contexts [14, 17, 18]. Additionally, previously published work describes tasks and time management, but does not focus on the mechanisms and workarounds that shape RTT-delivered PSSC under different organizational conditions. The cross-national differences observed in prior quantitative work indicate that deeper, context-sensitive exploration is warranted. The study addressed a knowledge gap by providing cross-national contextual explanations of how RTTs deliver PSSC, including perceived scope, barriers, and adaptive strategies.

This study aimed to extend prior survey findings by deepening understanding of how Norwegian and Canadian RTTs’ provide PSSC in practice. Using semi-structured interviews, the study examined RTTs’ PSSC perception and practices of addressing patients’ needs in Norway and Canada. The study further aimed to identify barriers experienced by RTTs in providing PSSC, as well as opportunities to improve the provision of PSSC in both countries. Norway and Canada were included because they participated in the prior survey and offer comparable publicly funded cancer care systems with differing healthcare contexts, enabling a meaningful cross-country comparison.

Methods

Study design

This qualitative descriptive study used a constructivist research paradigm, viewing knowledge and experience as socially and culturally constructed [19]. Semi-structured interviews enabled an exploration of how PSSC perspectives are shaped by the different social and cultural contexts in Norway and Canada. The reporting of this study followed the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research: Synthesis of Recommendations (SRQR) guideline [20].

This work extended an established Norway-Canada collaboration by following up on a prior survey of RTTs’ PSSC perceptions, practices, and barriers [9]. The research team included RTTs and public health researchers to interpret clinical practice and policy implications of the study findings in both countries. Assumptions of national differences were acknowledged, and reflexivity was maintained through systematic coding and collaborative interpretation [9].

Study participants

All practicing RTTs in Norway and Canada were eligible for this study. Norwegian RTTs were recruited by convenience sampling through managers at two radiation therapy departments of varying size within the same health region in Norway. Invitations were sent to all RTTs in those departments, and volunteers were scheduled for interviews. Canadian RTTs were recruited by convenience and snowball sampling through professional networks, social media, and the Canadian Association of Medical Radiation Technologists (CAMRT). For feasibility, participation was scheduled on a first-come first-served basis.

Interviews

A semi-structured interview guide was developed from targeted literature review and prior results reported by Bezanson et al. [9]. The interview guide explored four main categories: 1) demographics; 2) perceptions and current practices related to PSSC; 3) barriers and enablers of PSSC; and 4) opportunities to improve PSSC delivery (Table 1). The interview questions were developed in English and translated into Norwegian by professional translators. RTTs on the research team reviewed the guide for contextual accuracy and face validity before data collection.

Interview categories and related questions

The interviews were set to last approximately 30 min. Interviews were conducted online by study members using Zoom in Norway and Microsoft Teams in Canada. All interviews were audio-recorded with participant consent and transcribed verbatim. Interviews with Norwegian RTTs were professionally transcribed and translated into English, while interviews with Canadian RTTs were transcribed using Microsoft Teams. Interviews were conducted from March 25 to July 21, 2025 until data saturation, with a minimum of 10 participants per country.

Data analysis

Interview transcripts were reviewed for quality assurance and to remove identifying information before analysis. Data were analyzed using thematic analysis [21] to develop themes and compare patterns across both countries.

Researchers familiarized themselves with anonymized transcripts through repeated reading. Initial codes were generated inductively. Two researchers independently coded Norwegian and Canadian transcripts. The codes were collated into themes and subthemes. After independent coding, researchers compared codes and reviewed themes across both datasets. Coding discrepancies were discussed with the broader research team and addressed before finalizing the codebook. This process supported reflexivity and strengthened the credibility of the findings. The NVivo 14 (Lumivero, 2023) software was used for coding and data management.

Results

Demographics

A total of 26 RTTs were interviewed, 13 in Norway and 13 in Canada (Table 2). Participants were mostly women (61% in Norway; 85% in Canada) with similar mean ages (Table 2). Many participants worked as treatment therapists (69.2% in Norway; 53.8% in Canada). Most participants spent at least 50% of their work time in direct patient contact, which was more common in Norway (92.3%) than in Canada (61.5%) (Table 2).

Thematic analysis

Perceptions

This theme describes how RTTs understand and provide PSSC across scope, practice, training, and confidence. Most Norwegian and Canadian RTTs framed PSSC as an important aspect of patient care: “So for me personally, it’s like the bread and butter of the job.” (CAN9). Many RTTs described PSSC as routine work that is necessary for patient comfort and accurate treatment delivery: “If they don’t relax and if they’re very tense and stressed, we see that it affects the treatment itself. For example, when they have to lie completely still on the table during treatment, we can see that those who are very stressed can’t stay still. That affects the treatment.” (NOR12). Several participants also noted scope limitations indicating that complex psychosocial needs fall outside of RTTs’ practice and require referrals to allied health professionals, such as psychologists and social workers: “But as soon as it comes down to like side effects, symptom management, more complex psychological, social components, I’d say is out of our scope.” (CAN5).

Many Norwegian and Canadian RTTs reported limited focus on PSSC in education and professional training: “It wasn’t really emphasized in my education, but I’ve always been personally interested in psychology and sociology.” (NOR6). Participants mentioned spending more time on supportive care, including symptoms and side effect management, with less emphasis on psychosocial aspects as they were harder to address in practice: “Supportive care is something that has been a part of both our education and through ongoing courses.” (NOR11). Another participant shared: “I really feel like we we did get really good education on care of side effects and I felt well prepared to do that. But in terms of like spiritual care or emotional care, I don’t know.” (CAN8).

Norwegian RTTs reported being confident in providing PSSC: “I feel fairly confident. I have a sense of what to say and not to say.” (NOR10). Canadian RTTs described varied confidence levels, often limited to tasks emphasized in training: “When it comes to, you know, umm, anything that a social worker does, I don’t feel very confident about it ‘cause I’m not a professional, I’m not trained.” (CAN1). Several participants linked confidence to experience: “The longer you work with people, the better and more confident you get at reading them, seeing what they need, asking the right questions.” (NOR5).

Practices

This theme outlines how RTTs provide PSSC through education, ongoing assessment, and referrals. RTTs in Norway and Canada prioritized a first-day education session to establish rapport and assess psychosocial needs. As one Norwegian participant explained: “As radiation therapists, we usually start the patient’s treatment pathway with what we call an initial consultation. That’s about 10 to 20 min where we explain what’s going to happen, go through logistics, what to expect from radiotherapy, and discuss acute side effects. We try to identify any additional needs they may have” (NOR8). Similarly, a Canadian participant described a semi-structured first encounter: “We give them like an explanation about what to expect for treatment as as you go through the course and stuff like that. And we have, like on our checklist, something that we would have to fill out just describing like their psychosocial response to what we, we discuss.” (CAN10).

Daily check-ins varied in duration and approach, with RTTs adapting their methods based on time constraints and patient needs. Most RTTs worked within 15-min appointment slots that could be extended when needed. RTTs recognized their role as consistent points of contact throughout treatment: “So, on treatment, radiation therapists are the ones that’s seen that patient every day for 20 days, 25 days. So if there’s any changes, or, or having those points of contact, or having that, that relationship with the patient, that, yeah, is of such value to the team.” (CAN13). In addition, “(…) we do a formalized weekly check in assessment where we ask patients about certain side effects related to their treatment site, and we fill that in in our like electronic clinical system, and that’s tracked weekly” (CAN5). This captured a common view that protected time is important for improving PSSC.

Participants from both countries mentioned the important aspect of relationship building alongside technical tasks. One Norwegian RTT described their approach: “We respond as best we can. Usually, I think it’s enough to just listen, be empathetic, and validate the patient’s feelings. And if we realize they need more, we refer them to a professional.” (NOR8). Balancing formal assessment with informal conversation allowed RTTs to monitor symptoms and emotional wellbeing, and accommodate extended appointments when patients needed extra support. Norwegian and Canadian participants referred patients when needs exceeded scope or time, or when organizational barriers limited RTT follow-up: “I’m aware of my limitations. If the anxiety is very severe, or if it’s something else, like trauma or a very difficult social situation, then I discuss it with colleagues: What should we do? Should we contact a nurse? A doctor? A priest? So, I might recommend that the patient speak with professionals who are more skilled in providing the kind of support the patient needs.” (NOR6).

Barriers

This theme describes clinical and institutional factors that inhibit RTTs’ ability to provide PSSC. Norwegian RTTs identified limited time and systemic demands as primary barriers that cause practical challenges, such as delays in care: “There’s a lot of focus on making our work efficient. It has to be efficient, and there’s not much time left for supporting patients and providing good care. I feel like that’s disappearing a bit.” (NOR6). Canadian RTTs described similar barriers, including time constraints, understaffing, and insufficient education and training: “There’s so much demand for radiation therapy right now, and the supply is so limited that some places have even considered cutting their appointment times shorter. And then in those environments, we just don’t have the time to assess our patients properly.” (CAN9).

Norwegian and Canadian RTTs reported limited interdisciplinary collaboration and inconsistent awareness of available resources and services. Norwegian and Canadian RTTs further commented that appointments are often focused on delivering the technical aspects of care, which leaves limited time for RTTs to identify and address PSSC needs: “Like I know, like everything is getting so, in my field, I can say everything is getting very technical and high-tech and very detailed and very precise and in terms of technology. But I think we have to kind of shift back to our focus on like “How is the person in front of us doing? How are they dealing with all of this? How are they navigating?” (CAN4).

Norwegian RTTs expressed interest in expanding their skills and collaboration, although systemic staffing shortages limit their opportunities for professional development: “Time and opportunity are limited. We have to get through the patient list, and that often depends on staffing. But if I had the opportunity, if a concrete opportunity came up, I would absolutely take it.” (NOR8). Canadian RTTs described workarounds such as multitasking, extending appointment times, and referring patients to allied health professionals to mitigate system constraints.

Opportunities for improvement

This theme outlines concrete opportunities to strengthen RTTs’ delivery of PSSC. Participants in both countries suggested similar ideas to improve PSSC. Time was the most cited need: “We’re not given enough time for that social, psychosocial needs assessment for the patient, unfortunately” (CAN9). The need for improved education and training was mentioned by most participants in the study: “I’d say I guess the training would be a good support or like a skill refresher” (CAN12). Participants wanted clear, responsive referral pathways and routine collaboration with professionals such as nurses, psychologists, and social workers: “(…) to collaborate more closely with nurses, with psychologists, and have more theoretical training, especially in psychology, which I find very interesting” (NOR8). Some found it difficult to initiate PSSC conversations, and language barriers complicated needs assessments: “With language barriers, communication becomes difficult. We do have tools, digital tools that make it easier. But it’s still much harder to pick up on patient needs when communication isn’t smooth.” (NOR6).

In Norway, participants described having the flexibility to use additional time for patients who need it, by asking coworkers to step in. When asked if they felt adequately supported in offering high-quality, comprehensive PSSC, one participant responded: “No, I’d say there’s a lack of clear direction about how much we’re actually supposed to offer, and I feel it varies a lot between staff. How many are even able or willing to give that extra bit, really.” (NOR2). Canadian participants expressed a need for additional supports through staff in-services, enhancing institutional support, and prioritizing RTTs’ self-care to prevent burnout. Addressing organizational barriers, including time and resource constraints, was also cited as an opportunity for improvement: “But one of the issues for us right now, and across the country, is the shortage of radiation therapists. So we need, you know, frontline radiation therapists to treat patients. So it almost feels like a luxury right now to be able to do that, but that’s what I would like to see in the future is more roles and responsibilities, more active interdisciplinary care, more quality improvement projects and the time to do them.” (CAN8).

Discussion

Participants in both countries perceived PSSC as an important aspect of quality care. RTTs described embedding PSSC into first-encounter education and daily interactions, and using referrals when needs exceeded available time, training, or role expectations. Cross-national differences reflected a varied approach to providing care. The Norwegian approach included a patient-centered focus, while Canada had a more structured approach. The findings highlighted that improving RTT-delivered PSSC requires alignment between training, clear referral pathways, and institutional conditions that enable RTTs to provide PSSC.

Perceptions

Confidence in providing PSSC was described as a skill developed through workplace experience with patients and supportive practice, rather than a direct product of formal education. Participants described PSSC as including supportive care tasks and less defined psychosocial care. This requires improved education and communication skills to better support their patients. This extends quantitative findings demonstrating that RTTs recognize the importance of their psychosocial role [9], while clarifying how confidence and scope is formed in practice.

Despite similar educational gaps, Norwegian RTTs described more consistent opportunities to develop confidence through patient contact supported by the colleges and the institutions [12]. In this study, over 92% of Norwegian RTTs reported spending more than 50% of their time in patient contact, which may support confidence and practice in delivering PSSC. Hulley et al. [12] found that both personal factors such as culture and experience, together with organizational factors like time, space, and perceived support influence RTTs’ capacity to communicate with emotional patients. Among Canadian RTTs, confidence in delivering PSSC varied, largely due to inadequate institutional support, staffing shortages, and time constraints that restrict opportunities to strengthen psychosocial skills [9, 12].

Practices

RTTs in both countries described specific points where PSSC is most feasible, including first-encounter education and brief daily check-ins that function as ongoing PSSC need assessments. Egestad [14] described how attentive RTT interactions help patients feel seen, which can reduce existential anxiety. Norwegian RTTs described using a relational approach supported by flexibility in treatment schedules and teamwork. This includes motivational interviewing and building therapeutic relationships with patients, allowing for the ability to respond to patient distress when time permits [9]. However, participants also mentioned concerns of efficiency pressures, potentially leaving patient needs unaddressed despite professional commitment. Canadian RTTs described using structured approaches, including standardized checklists and scheduled meetings for assessments, to ensure patients’ needs are addressed in time-limited settings. Prior work has reported that screening tools require integration with adequate training and clear referral pathways to translate into improved patient support [13, 17].

Barriers

Across both countries, time pressures were described as a practical constraint that forced RTTs to prioritize technical tasks over PSSC. This aligns with previous studies identifying time, staff numbers, and workload as major obstacles to psychosocial care [6, 12]. Canadian RTTs mentioned understaffing and institutional resource deficits, with 48% reporting limited organizational support compared to only 6% of Norwegian RTTs [9]. This disparity suggests that barriers operate differently across healthcare systems. Canadian RTTs faced persistent resource constraints, while Norwegian RTTs navigated cultural expectations for efficiency within comparatively well-resourced settings. Limited interdisciplinary collaboration and unclear referral pathways created additional constraints in both contexts. Elsner et al. [13] and Gunn et al. [17] proposed that environments with limited resources and unclear referral pathways can cause hesitancy among RTTs with respect to addressing PSSC needs due to fear of being unable to manage the needs they identify. This creates a cycle where systemic constraints limit care provision and reduce opportunities for RTTs to develop psychosocial skills.

Opportunities for improvement

Both Norwegian and Canadian RTTs emphasized that improving PSSC delivery require both skill development, time, interdisciplinary collaboration and clearer role definitions. There are currently no clinical guidelines for PSSC delivery in Norway, and while such guidelines exist in Canada [22], participants appeared to be unaware of them, suggesting a need for improved knowledge translation.

Both countries reported a need for more education, consistent with Bezanson et al. [9]. Jones et al. [6] similarly indicated that RTTs felt a need for training in communication with depressed and angry patients and conflict management, skills they felt were underdeveloped in formal education. To improve education in PSSC delivery, it is important for the department leadership to acknowledge the importance of PSSC and support RTTs in developing the skills needed to facilitate such care. Larsen et al. [4] reported that when department leadership actively supports PSSC, this can lead to improvements in both clinical practice and patient experience. Meaningful change requires a dual approach: supporting RTTs to develop skills through training and education and building a supportive environment that enables RTTs to apply those skills [6, 12, 13].

Limitations

This study used convenience and snowball sampling, which may overrepresent RTTs dedicated to providing PSSC. Interviews relied on self-report data and may be influenced by recall or social desirability. Saturation was based on a minimum sample target, which may not capture all variation.

Conclusion

This qualitative descriptive study extends prior cross-nation quantitative study by deepening understanding of how Norwegian and Canadian RTTs provide PSSC in practice. RTTs in both countries described PSSC as integral to quality care. They reported embedding PSSC in first-encounter education and brief daily check-ins, while relying on referrals when needs exceeded scope, time, or training. Implementation varied between the countries. Norwegian RTTs emphasized flexibility and college support for relationship-based care, while Canadian RTTs focused on structured assessments. Both countries reported limited time, and staffing and appointment pressures. These findings underscore that effective PSSC delivery requires prioritized protected time, strengthened education and communication skills, clearer role expectations, and clear referral pathways to improve PSSC delivery. Future research should employ in-depth interviews of patients to understand perceived PSSC quality and to explore whether RTTs’ concerns about providing insufficient care are justified.
